 ABSTRACT

This report formally documents the completion of the Advanced Reactor Technologies Level 2
Milestone (M2AT-26OR0605058), “Complete initial wear/abrasion studies,” due June 1, 2026. The work
presented in this report expend upon our previously published manuscript titled Sliding friction and wear
behavior of nuclear graphite in high temperature inert environment: Influence of contact load, speed and
temperature. The sliding friction and wear of self-mated ET-10 nuclear graphite were characterized across
a range of elevated temperatures (650°C and 750°C), contact loads (20 N and 40 N), and sliding speeds (1
mm/s and 10 mm/s) within a controlled argon environment. Tribological data combined with advanced
morphological characterization were used to provide a detailed mechanistic framework for the frictional
and wear behavior of nuclear graphite in high-temperature inert conditions. Furthermore, the report
evaluates the inherent limitations of bench-scale characterization and addresses the critical disparities
between laboratory findings and the complex, in-service friction and wear behavior of fuel pebbles within
a reactor environment.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Advanced nuclear reactor concepts, such as high-temperature gas-cooled reactors (HTGRs) and fluoride
salt-cooled high-temperature reactors (FHRs), use a pebble-bed (PB) configuration in which thousands of
spherical fuel elements continuously circulate through the core multiple times before their final discharge
[1-4], as illustrated in Figure 1. These pebbles, typically 40–60 mm in diameter, consist of tri-structural
isotropic (TRISO) fuel particles (<1 mm diameter) homogeneously dispersed within a graphite matrix and
encapsulated by a fuel-free outer graphitic shell [1-4]. In PB-HTGR designs, these elements operate in a
helium atmosphere at temperatures ranging from 550°C to 950°C [4]. Conversely, FHRs use a molten
fluoride salt coolant, such as FLiBe (2LiF–BeF2), at temperatures between 550°C and 650°C [5],
although specific regions may be exposed to an argon cover gas [6]. During their circulation, graphitic
pebbles experience repeated mechanical interactions, including sliding and rolling as well as impact with
neighboring pebbles, the graphite reflector wall, and metallic containment components, as illustrated in
Figure 1b,c,d. These recurring contacts can induce surface abrasion and microfracture, progressively
compromising the structural integrity of the pebbles and the reactor wall [7, 8]. Furthermore, excessive
wear generates fine graphite debris that presents significant safety hazards and may alter coolant flow or
overall reactor performance [9, 10]. Because frictional behavior of these interfaces governs pebble
circulation and packing dynamics, characterizing the tribological behavior of nuclear graphite is essential
for core design, component longevity, and ensuring operational reliability.

This report discusses a systematic investigation into the self-mated sliding friction and wear performance
of ET-10 nuclear graphite, as illustrated in Figure 1e, across a range of contact loads, sliding speeds, and
temperatures within an argon environment. Morphological characterization via specialized microscopy
tools was used to investigate the microstructural changes of the sliding interfaces and to determine the
underlying wear mechanism. The integration of tribological testing and advanced morphological
characterization provides a mechanistic understanding of nuclear graphite behavior under high
temperatures and inert conditions. Additionally, this report presents an examination of the fundamental
limitations and challenges of bench-scale testing and addresses the gaps between experimental data and
pebble friction and wear behavior under actual reactor conditions. The resulting insights into friction and
wear behavior and wear mechanisms offer critical data for understanding tribological behavior and
supporting the design of more reliable and safer nuclear energy systems.

Friction and wear of graphite are very sensitive to the environment, particularly the surrounding gas
composition and temperature [6, 12]. This wear behavior is not an intrinsic material property but rather a
result of surface chemical interactions and the passivation of the carbon lattice. Under ambient conditions
(air and room temperature), the characteristic low friction of graphite is maintained by the adsorption of
water vapor, oxygen, and hydrogen [13-16]. These species saturate the dangling bonds at the edge sites of
graphite crystallites. This saturation effectively neutralizes interfacial adhesive forces and reduces the
shear resistance between basal planes, leading to a stable and low coefficient of friction (COF). In inert

 2

environments such as argon, helium, or vacuum, the lack of bond-saturating species leaves dangling
bonds exposed, significantly increasing the adhesion of the contacting surfaces [12, 17, 18]. This adhesion
often leads to a phenomenon known as “dusting,” a wear regime characterized by the rapid fragmentation
of graphite into fine, abrasive debris [19, 20]. The influence of temperature on the friction and wear
behavior of graphite depends on the gas composition. In the presence of air, an increase in temperature
causes adsorbed species begin to desorb. The subsequent exposure of unsaturated dangling bonds
increases the surface energy of the contact interface, promoting stronger interfacial adhesion and a
corresponding rise in the COF. At higher temperatures in the presence of reactive species, graphite
oxidation can further accelerate surface reactions, leading to increased friction, wear, and significant
surface degradation [21, 22]. However, within inert environments, rising temperatures have been
observed to reduce friction [6, 23], which was primarily attributed to the increase in the mechanical
strength of graphite at elevated temperatures [6]. Low friction in graphite is enabled by a self-lubricating
tribofilm [6, 24, 25]. Sliding interactions such as abrasion and microfracture of the crystallites generate
fine debris at the contact interface. Under the influence of normal and shear forces, this debris is
compacted and smeared across the sliding area to form a cohesive deposit layer. The tribofilm exhibits
self-lubricating characteristics, which diminish interfacial shear resistance and ultimately stabilize the
friction and wear behavior.

Although the tribological characterization of graphite has been documented extensively, research focusing
on self-mated nuclear grades under conditions strictly representative of reactor environments, specifically
high temperatures in inert atmospheres, remains relatively unexplored. Vergari et al. [6] characterized the
sliding behavior of ET-10 in an argon environment at temperatures ranging from 17°C to 600°C. Their
findings revealed a significant thermal influence, with the COF decreasing from 0.60 at ambient
temperature to 0.31 at 600°C. This reduction was attributed to two primary factors: the anomalous
increase in the mechanical strength of graphite at elevated temperatures and the evolution of a thicker,
more stable tribofilm that serves as a protective interfacial layer. This trend is further supported by
investigations of Koboi BG80, which demonstrated a consistent decrease in both friction and wear from
25°C to 1100°C in both helium and nitrogen environments [23].

molded, fine-grain synthetic graphite manufactured by IBIDEN Co., Ltd. (Japan) and supplied by Kairos

bulk density of 1.75 g/cm3, an average grain size of 15 µm, an open porosity of approximately 15%, and

Flat specimens. Square flat specimens, measuring 25.4 × 25.4 × 1.6 mm, were machined from a

Machined graphite specimens underwent ultrasonic cleaning in an ethanol bath for 30 min to remove
residual machining debris and surface contaminants. The samples were then thermally dried in an oven at
120°C for 4 h. Then an outgassing procedure was performed to eliminate adsorbed moisture and trapped
oxygen, which can significantly influence the lubricating properties of graphite [6]. The specimens were
heated to 1200°C and maintained for 8 h under high vacuum (<10−5 Torr). After cooling under vacuum,
the samples were transferred to a controlled environment before tribological testing.

2.2 SLIDING FRICTION AND WEAR EXPERIMENTS

2.2.1 Tribometer

Although graphitic fuel pebbles in an operational reactor environment experience complex dynamic
modes, including sliding, rolling, and impact interactions, this investigation specifically focuses on self-
mated sliding contact. Friction and wear experiments were conducted using a multifunctional high-
temperature tribometer (Rtec MFT-2000A, San Jose, California, USA) configured in a pin-on-flat
geometry, as shown in Figure 3a. This setup effectively replicates the localized sliding contact
experienced at the interface between a fuel pebble and the graphite reflector wall. In this configuration,
the graphite pin is mounted to a loading head equipped to monitor both normal force, Fn, and tangential
friction force, Ff. The pin is loaded against a graphite flat mounted to a rotary stage which provides a
unidirectional rotating motion, as shown in Figure 3b. In general, the tribological behavior of graphite is
highly sensitive to its surrounding gas composition. To ensure consistent inert environment during testing,
the experiments were performed in a glovebox with a controlled argon environment consisting of
O2 < 0.5 ppm and H2O < 0.5 ppm.

2.2.3 Surface Roughness Measurements

The surface roughness of the graphite samples was characterized by using a 3D laser confocal
microscope. The arithmetic mean roughness, Ra, was determined by averaging 20 individual line profiles
extracted from the measured topographical data using a 48 mm evaluation length and an 8 mm cutoff
length, according to ISO 21920 [27]. The Ra of the wear track region was determined using a 1.25 mm
evaluation length and a 0.25 mm cutoff length due to limited measurement region.

2.3 MICROSTRUCTURAL CHARACTERIZATION

2.3.1 Top-Down Microstructural Characterization of Sliding Surfaces

Top-down imaging of the worn graphite contact areas was conducted using scanning electron microscopy
(SEM; S-4800, Hitachi, Japan) and 3D laser confocal microscope. The SEM images were collected with
15 keV accelerating voltage in secondary electron mode. The confocal microscope images were acquired
with brightfield coaxial illumination.

2.3.2 Cross-Sectional Microstructural Characterization of Sliding Surfaces

The microstructural evolution of the sliding interfaces was characterized via a systematic multistage
workflow, as illustrated in Figure 4:

Subsurface cross-sectioning via SEM/FIB technique, Figure 4a,b: To investigate the subsurface
morphology of the graphite sliding surfaces, cross-sections were milled using a focused ion beam
(FIB)/SEM (NB-5000, Hitachi, Japan) system. Before milling, the region of interest was coated with a
dual protective platinum layer to preserve the integrity of the surface features. An initial layer of
platinum, 0.2 µm thick, was deposited via electron-beam-assisted deposition, followed by a secondary,
thicker platinum layer, 0.4 µm thick, applied using the ion beam. Subsequently, a roughly 20 µm wide
trench was milled parallel to the sliding direction to expose the underlying material structure. Once the
raw subsurface was exposed, the cross-sectional regions were further polished through subsequent
polishing steps using a progressively lower ion beam current to ensure a smooth, artifact-free finish.

Cross-sectional imaging via SEM, Figure 4c: The clean cross-sectional interfaces were imaged using
SEM with a 15 keV accelerating voltage. This step allowed for a detailed examination of the subsurface
microstructure of the sliding surface and the bulk graphite as well as the identification of any subsurface
cracking or porosity resulting from the sliding interaction.

 3. DETERMINATION OF TRIBOLOGICALLY-RELEVANT CONDITIONS AND DESIGN
 OF A TEST MATRIX

Understanding the appropriate parameters for tribological characterization of graphite components in a
PB-HTGR, such as reactor core temperature, pebble sliding speed and distance, pebble-on-pebble and
pebble-on wall contact loads, are essential for designing an experimental matrix; however, determination
of these conditions can be challenging. The test conditions for this study were determined by reviewing
existing literature and guidance from Kairos Power and X-energy while accounting for the inherent
limitations of the testing apparatus.

Temperature: Although PB-HTGR operational temperatures typically range from 550°C to 950°C [2-4],
this investigation used 650°C and 750°C as the primary experimental setpoints.

Sliding speeds: Pebble circulation speeds are extremely low, a couple of micrometers per second [9, 11,
28], depending on their location in the reactor; this speed falls below the controllable speed of the
tribometer. Moreover, testing at these extremely low sliding speeds would be very time-consuming.
Consequently, sliding speeds of 1 and 10 mm/s were chosen to ensure precise experimental control while
providing a sufficient range to evaluate speed-dependent behavior.

Sliding distance: Given that a pebble may travel a 9 m reactor core approximately six times, the
cumulative travel distance could reach 54 m. However, because pebbles undergo simultaneous sliding and
rolling that continuously refreshes contact patches, the actual sliding distance per individual contact is
reduced. A conservative total sliding distance of 20 m was adopted for this investigation.

Contact loads: Due to limited literature regarding in-core contact loads, pebble contact loads were
estimated using modeling data from Tang et al. [9], which suggest a normal load of approximately 28 N
near the reactor annulus. Based on this estimate, experimental loads of 20 N and 40 N were selected.
More details on the calculation of the contact loads can be found elsewhere [11, 28].

Atmospheric considerations: Although PB-HTGRs use helium, argon was chosen for this study because
of its cost-effectiveness and compatibility with existing equipment. The difference in thermal
conductivity between helium and argon (~0.15 vs. 0.017 W m−1 K−1) is assumed to have a negligible
impact on friction. Because the thermal conductivity of the graphite (~104 W m−1 K−1) is several orders of
magnitude greater, frictional heat is dissipated almost entirely through the graphite specimens.

The tribologically relevant conditions for PB-HTGRs suggest a large range of potential parameter
combinations. However, due to the time-intensive nature of high-temperature testing and limited
specimen availability, the friction and wear of graphite were characterized for four specific conditions, as
summarized in Table 1. A reference condition was defined as Fn = 20 N, v = 10 mm/s, and T = 650°C.
Three additional test sets implemented single-parameter variations relative to this reference: (i) v was
decreased to 1 mm/s, (ii) T was increased to 750°C, and (iii) Fn was increased to 40 N. In all experimental
sets, the total sliding distance, d, was maintained at a constant 20 m.

 4. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

4.1 EFFECT OF TEST CONDITIONS ON FRICTION BEHAVIOR OF NUCLEAR
 GRAPHITE

The frictional behavior of the self-mated ET-10 graphite shows a nonmonotonic trend across all
investigated test conditions, as shown in Figure 5 and summarized in Table 3. The evolution of the
friction behavior is characterized by an initial higher running-in period that transitions into a stable steady
state after approximately 2 m of sliding distance. Remarkably, although the transient running-in phase is
highly sensitive to experimental variables, the steady state COF consistently converges to a narrow range
of 0.30–0.35, regardless of the applied load, temperature, or sliding speed. The maximum COF during
running-in is significantly influenced by the test parameters; specifically, lower maximum COF values
were observed when either the sliding speed was reduced or the temperature was increased.

Influence of sliding speed. Reducing the speed from 10 mm/s to 1 mm/s resulted in a 25% reduction in the
maximum COF, from around 0.64 to 0.53 (average of the two tests), as shown in Figure 5b. This
sensitivity can be attributed to the energy associated with asperity interactions. At lower speeds, the
energy of collision between surface asperities of the graphite pin and flat is significantly reduced. This
reduced energy leads to less severe initial wear and a more moderate increase in roughness during the first
stage of sliding. Consequently, the graphite pin–flat interface experiences lower resistance during the
three-body abrasion phase.

Influence of temperature. The tests conducted at 750°C exhibited a maximum COF comparable to that of
the low-speed tests: approximately 0.53, as shown in Figure 5c. This behavior can be explained by an
increase of graphite strength at elevated temperatures. This increased strength at 750°C, as compared to
the 650°C reference, is hypothesized to enhance the resistance of the graphite to asperity microfracture
and surface degradation during the initial running-in. Reduction in COF with increased temperature has
also been observed by Vergari et al. [6] and was also contributed to increased graphite strength high
temperature.

Influence of contact load. In contrast to speed and temperature, the contact load demonstrated the least
significant influence on the frictional profile. Doubling the normal load from 20 N (reference test
conditions) to 40 N resulted in a very similar maximum COF of approximately 0.63, as shown in Figure
5d. This lack of sensitivity can be explained by Hertzian contact mechanics, which state that the mean
contact pressure scales with load as Fn1/3 [29]. Because the contact area increases more rapidly than the
pressure at these higher loads, the resulting mean contact pressure increases only modestly. This result
could explain why the running-in friction remains largely comparable even when the total applied force is
significantly increased.

4.2 WEAR BEHAVIOR OF NUCLEAR GRAPHITE

The wear behavior of the ET-10 graphite has been shown to be affected by the contact geometry and
contact load. Optical imaging and surface topography measurements revealed that both the pin and flat
sliding surfaces underwent significant topographical changes. The contact sliding surface in both graphite
pin and flat is smoother compared to the surrounding unworn region. Additionally, the contact surfaces
also appear brighter under coaxial illumination, indicating surface smoothing. The sliding surface wear
was less sensitive to the test condition, with exception of the contact load. However, the graphite pin and
flat exhibited fundamentally distinct wear behaviors.

Wear behavior of the graphite pin. Graphite pins experienced material loss under all test conditions, as is
evident from the topographical maps of the sliding surfaces and the corresponding line profiles,
comparing the initial unworn profile to the worn profile shown in Figure 6. The sliding surfaces are
smoothed compared to the surrounding unworn region with the Ra, decreasing from 4.3 µm to a range of
0.5–0.7 µm. This order-of-magnitude reduction in Ra demonstrates the transition from the initial abrasive
interaction to a more stable regime. The wear rates of the graphite pins remained consistent in magnitude
across all test conditions: all values were on the order of 10−5 mm3N−1m−1. This result is summarized in
Figure 7 and Table 3. However, the wear rates varied between repeated tests in nearly all conditions,
except for the tests conducted at the slowest v = 1 mm/s. The variability in wear behavior between
different test conditions is likely due to the relatively high initial Ra, the presence of microfracture
features on the sliding surfaces, and the wear debris accumulation around the worn surface, complicating
precise measurements of the wear rate. Although the wear rates remained relatively stable, the Fn = 40 N

 10

load condition resulted in significantly more surface damage at the leading edge of the pin and larger
worn surface area compared with the 20 N load condition.

4.3 WEAR MECHANISM AND MICROSTRUCTURAL ANALYSIS OF SLIDING SURFACE
 OF NUCLEAR GRAPHITE

4.3.1 Microstructural Analysis of Sliding Surface of Graphite

The sliding of graphite against graphite is characterized by the formation of a tribofilm: a thin, compacted
layer of graphitic wear debris known to reduce both friction and wear [6, 14]. The presence of this film
across all test conditions can be deduced by the smoothed contact regions observed in the optical and
topographical analyses. SEM analysis was performed characterize the interfacial morphology and
investigate the wear mechanisms across the sliding surfaces of the pin and flat.

Sliding surface of the graphite pin. Analysis of the SEM images of the graphite pins revealed a distinct
microstructural contrast between the contact zone and the surrounding unworn material, as shown in
Figure 10. The sliding surfaces exhibited a polished texture, indicative of abrasive wear, and were
populated by scattered wear debris particles. A higher concentration of debris was consistently observed
at the leading edge of the contact area. Furthermore, the size and volume of this debris were highly
sensitive to the applied load: the reference condition Fn = 20 N produced particles up to 10 µm in size,

 14

and increasing the load to Fn = 40 N resulted in significantly larger particles, reaching up to 50 µm in
size.

Sliding surface of the graphite flat. The SEM characterization of the graphite flats in Figure 11 revealed
morphological features similar to those found on the pins, including the development of a continuous,
smooth tribofilm across all experimental sets. Consistent with the pin observations, the Fn = 40 N load
condition on the flats resulted in a more substantial accumulation of debris and larger particle sizes up to
20 µm, compared to the 10 µm particles observed at Fn = 20 N. Moreover, the tribofilm on the flats also
displayed evidence of localized spallation and delamination, likely stemming from contact fatigue
induced by the intermittent loading cycle of the flat’s wear track. While general abrasion generated fine
debris throughout the track, the largest particles were primarily associated with these spallation-induced
damage zones. Specifically, Figure 11d illustrates that larger debris fragments are formed through the
delamination and subsequent fracture of the established tribofilm, highlighting a transition in the material-
removal mechanism under higher mechanical stress.

4.3.2 Microstructural Analysis of the Tribofilm

Electron microscopy was used to investigate the morphology of the tribofilm. FIB milling was used to

shows a notably smoother texture and brighter contrast compared to the bulk material. As expected for

4.3.3 Raman Spectroscopy of the Tribofilm

The crystalline structure of the tribofilm was investigated using Raman spectroscopy and compared to the
unworn region. As shown in Figure 14, all specimens retain the sharp 2D band characteristic of graphitic
materials. However, the transition from the unworn to the worn state is marked by a significant increase
in the D band (~1,350 cm−1) relative to the G band (~1,580 cm−1), signaling a substantial rise in structural
defects and grain boundaries within the sliding interface [31]. The analysis shows that the lateral
crystallite size drops from the highly ordered unworn state to approximately 15 nm in the worn tribofilm.
This threefold reduction in crystalline size confirms that the sliding process mechanically shears and
compacts the graphite into a refined nanocrystalline layer. The degree of this structural disorder is further
reflected in the widening of the G-band full width at half maximum, indicating a less ordered graphitic
lattice in the worn areas [31]. Ultimately, the Raman data confirm that the formation of a stable tribofilm
requires a fundamental transition from ordered bulk graphite to a refined, defect-rich nanocrystalline
interface.

4.4 GRAPHITE SELF-LUBRICATING MECHANISM

The lower-friction steady-state regime in graphite, as observed in this study, is fundamentally driven by
the formation of a tribofilm. To investigate the relationship between microstructural evolution and the
high friction observed during the running-in regime, interrupted sliding tests were performed at two
sliding distance intervals: 0.18 m, representing the peak COF, and 0.70 m, marking the transition toward
the steady-state regime, as shown in Figure 15, based on friction behavior observed in Figure 5. The first
test, stopped at 0.18 m, reached a maximum COF of 0.60. The second test, run to 0.70 m, showed the
friction had already begun its decrease toward the steady-state regime and reached a value of 0.36, as
shown in Figure 15a. Morphological analysis of the graphite pins and flats at these two distances revealed
that the shift in friction is directly related to the structural changes at the interface. After 0.18 m of sliding,
the pin surface exhibits a major abrasive wear mode characterized by localized, discontinuous regions of
tribofilm formation, as shown in Figure 15b. At this early stage, the flat surface remains rough with no
evidence of film development, and wear debris accumulation at the leading edge of the track is minimal.
By contrast, the surfaces at 0.70 m show more notable changes. The tribofilm on the pin is more
continuous and contains embedded wear debris particles, with a larger accumulation of debris observed at
the leading edge, as shown in Figure 15b. The sliding surface of the flat is smoother, shows localized
regions of tribofilm, and resembles the surface of the pin at the earlier 0.18 m interval. This

 20

morphological evolution indicates that the gradual reduction in friction results from the increasing
coverage of the tribofilm on both pin and flat.

distance of 0.18 m and 0.70 m(a) Friction behavior. (b) SEM images of pin and flat at a sliding distance of 0.18 m
 and (c) SEM images of pin and flat at a sliding distance of 0.70 m. Reproduced from previous work [11].

Based on the tribological and morphological investigation in this study, the proposed model of friction
behavior evolution due to microstructural changes of the sliding self-mated graphite interface is illustrated
in Figure 16. This model attempts to explain the characteristic transition from an initially higher running-
in friction to a stable steady state observed during sliding.

Stage 1: Running-in Part I. During the onset of sliding (0–0.3 m), the interface is dominated by two-body
abrasion driven by direct asperity interactions. Due to the quasi-brittle nature of nuclear graphite, these
initial contacts induce localized fracture and the delamination of crystallites. This stage is marked by
progressive surface damage and an increase in roughness on both the pin and flat; this roughness elevates
the frequency and severity of asperity collisions. Consequently, the COF increases nonmonotonically, and
most of total material removal occurs during this transient phase.

Stage 2: Running-in Part II. As sliding continues, the accumulation of wear debris shifts the dominant
mechanism to three-body abrasion. During this phase, the wear mode transitions to polishing off asperity
peaks rather than fracturing the bulk. Simultaneously, the open pores and topographical valleys of the
graphite are replenished by wear debris. Under the influence of repeated normal and shear stresses, these
loose particles undergo compaction, initiating the formation of a localized tribofilm. This initial tribofilm
begins to smooth the contact areas, effectively reducing the intensity of asperity collisions and triggering
the observed decline in friction.

Stage 3: Steady state. After approximately 2 m of sliding, the interface reaches a dynamic equilibrium
where the rates of tribofilm formation and abrasive removal are balanced, resulting in a stable COF and a
constant wear rate. The resulting tribofilm is a continuous, morphologically distinct layer with a thickness

 21

of 0.5–2.0 µm. The tribofilm possesses a nanocrystalline structure that provides a low-shear interface.
This refined layer effectively shields the underlying substrate from further damage, and any subsequent
wear originates from the microscale delamination of the film itself. The high stability of the friction signal
during this regime suggests a self-replenishing mechanism operating at a microscopic level in which
localized film failure is nearly instantaneously countered by the compaction of available wear debris,
maintaining the integrity of the protective interface.

 5. CHALLENGES AND OPPORTUNITES

Although this study provides fundamental mechanistic insights into the tribological behavior of ET-10
graphite, several critical factors must be addressed to bridge the gap between bench-scale characterization
and the complex operational environment of a PB-HTGR.

5.1 COMPLEX DYNAMIC CONTACT MODES

The simplified pin-on-flat sliding geometry used in this work does not fully replicate the mechanical
interactions of graphitic components within a PB-HTGR. In reality, the pebbles will experience a
combination of (i) sliding wear, which is governed by both hardness and fracture toughness, (ii) rolling
contact fatigue, and (iii) impact-induced microfracture, which largely depend on the fracture toughness.
The bench-scale testing primarily characterizes sliding wear and underestimates the structural degradation
caused by other wear modes. Moreover, the continuous sliding over a fixed track promotes the
development of a mature, uniform tribofilm, as has been demonstrated in this work. However, in an actual
reactor, the constant circulation and random orientation of pebbles mean that sustained interactions at a
single contact point are unlikely. Instead, sliding and rolling interactions occur continually on fresh
interfaces; therefore, formation of a self-lubricating tribofilm may be dynamic, localized, and less
frequent. Consequently, the pebbles and graphite walls likely operate in a running-in state in which COF
and wear rates are dominated by evolving surfaces rather than the steady values reported in this work.

 22

5.2 MATERIAL DISPARITY: NUCLEAR GRAPHITE VS. MATRIX MATERIAL

In PB-HTGRs, the permanent reflector walls are typically constructed from nuclear-grade graphite;
however, the actual fuel pebbles are composite materials. The outer fuel-free zone of a pebble is
composed of a graphite matrix, which is a composite formed by pressing a mixture of natural graphite,
synthetic graphite, and carbonaceous binder [1, 2, 4]. This matrix material is inherently more
heterogeneous and typically exhibits distinct mechanical properties, such as lower density and different
fracture toughness, compared to structural nuclear graphite. The distinct microstructural evolution of the
matrix material, particularly the role of the binder in debris formation, can lead to different wear modes,
altered tribofilm adhesion kinetics, and varying friction stabilization behaviors that are not fully captured
by self-mated nuclear graphite experiments. These differences suggest that, although the trends observed
in this study provide a robust baseline for reflector-on-reflector interactions, the specific wear coefficients
for pebble-to-wall and pebble-to-pebble contacts may be significantly influenced by the unique
morphology of the matrix material.

5.3 GAS DISPARITY: ARGON VS. HELIUM

Although argon serves as a robust inert baseline for isolating the effects of oxygen and moisture, HTGRs
use high-pressure helium as the primary coolant [2, 4]. The distinct physical properties of helium, such as
its lower density and significantly higher thermal conductivity relative to argon, can introduce subtle but
important variables. Higher thermal conductivity can more effectively dissipate heat from asperity
contacts, potentially altering the localized flash temperatures that govern the kinetics of tribofilm
formation. These environmental differences may influence the mechanical stability of the interface,
suggesting that, while the fundamental trends observed in argon are representative, the absolute rates of
wear and film stabilization may shift under helium-cooled conditions.

5.4 IRRADIATION EFFECTS

A primary challenge in predicting long-term component life is the evolution of graphite properties under
neutron irradiation. In a reactor core, graphite undergoes significant displacement damage, leading to the
accumulation of defects that alter its thermophysical and mechanical properties [32, 33]. The resulting
embrittlement, characterized by increased hardness and a simultaneous reduction in fracture toughness, is
expected to fundamentally shift wear mechanisms. Specifically, the transition from a coupled abrasion-
microfracture regime to a microfracture-dominant mode may accelerate material removal rates beyond
what is observed in unirradiated specimens.

 6. FUTURE WORK

To advance the predictive accuracy of graphite wear models, future research will prioritize bridging the
gap between laboratory-scale characterization and reactor environments by investigating fuel matrix
interactions, transient running-in friction behavior, and a broader range of dynamic contact modes. A
primary focus will be the characterization of the fuel element matrix material, which constitutes the outer
contact surface of circulating fuel pebbles and presents a more complex tribological profile than structural
nuclear graphite [2, 4]. Future experiments will investigate the following interactions: (1) matrix-on-
matrix configuration to simulate pebble-on-pebble interactions, and (2) matrix-on-graphite configuration
to simulate pebble-on-wall (reflector) interactions. A key observation from the current study is the distinct
behavioral contrast between the transient and steady-state phases of sliding: whereas the steady-state COF
remains relatively independent of test parameters, the running-in behavior is highly sensitive to initial
contact conditions. This result suggests that the mechanisms governing the formation of a tribofilm are
potentially more complex than those explained in this study. Future efforts will also focus on an
investigation of microstructural evolution of the interface in the transient period to understand the

 23

mechanism and its dependence on the test conditions, with a particular emphasis on the role of wear
debris. Although the current study establishes a fundamental baseline for sliding-dominated wear, the
mechanical duty cycle of fuel elements in a PB-HTGR is more complex. The graphitic components are
subjected to a combination of sliding, rolling, and impacts. Future investigations will expand the
experimental scope to include these contact modes to characterize the friction and wear behavior of the
graphitic components in the reactor core more realistically.
