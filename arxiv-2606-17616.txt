Towards Speech Impairment Prediction in German-Speaking Individuals with Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis
Monica, Ricarda, Justin, Finja, Alexandra-Iulia, Anne, Isabell, Björn

Abstract
Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) is a neurodegenerative disease, often affecting speech due to bulbar dysfunction. In this study, we predict speech impairment in people with ALS (pwALS) using two clinical speech-related scores. We evaluate cross-sectional (across speakers) and personalised (within-speaker) modelling paradigms and analyse the utility of common speech tasks to contribute to the standardisation of speech data collection for pwALS. Experiments on a German-speaking cohort of 66 pwALS show that repetition tasks (/da/-/da/, /da/-/ba/) achieved the best cross-sectional performance (Concordance Correlation Coefficient (CCC) = 0.62) for predicting the Quality of Life in the Dysarthric Speaker questionnaire, while the within-speaker setting reached a CCC of 0.86. This study represents an initial step towards speech impairment prediction in German-speaking pwALS and highlights the potential of automated speech analysis as a supportive tool for speech impairment assessment.

Introduction

als
(
als
) is a severe progressive
mnd
(
mnd
), with an upper and lower motor neuron pathology leading to reduced mobility, loss of motor control, respiratory failure, and bulbar dysfunction problems
[
1
]
. Median survival of
pwals
(
pwals
) is 2-4 years, with respiratory failure as a major cause of mortality
[
2
]
. Since
als
progression is highly heterogeneous, non-linear, and ultimately irreversible
[
3
]
, there is a strong need for reliable biomarkers to monitor the disease over time and facilitate intervention
[
4
]
.

The
alsfrsr
(
alsfrsr
) is the most commonly used clinical measurement for assessing functional impairment in
als

[
5
,
6
]
. It consists of 12 items with a maximum score of 48 points. It has demonstrated high consistency, reliability, and responsiveness to change
[
5
]
, and serves as a primary outcome measure in clinical trials on disease progression
[
5
,
7
]
. One of its items,
alsfrsr
-speech, evaluates speech and is rated using ordinal values from 0 (loss of useful speech) to 4 (normal speech). Since speech and motor impairments strongly affect
qol
(
qol
)
[
5
]
, a commonly used complementary measure in
pwals
is the subscore
Speech Characteristics
of the
qoldys
(
qoldys
). This self-administered questionnaire provides a subjective assessment of how speech difficulties impact daily life
[
8
]
.
It contains 10 items rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (all of the time), assessing the frequency of impaired speech characteristics. The maximum score is 40 points (highest impairment).
Measuring this information is useful for capturing the impact of speech-related therapeutic interventions in the patient’s
qol

[
9
]
.

In recent years, automatic speech analysis has been explored for the recognition and monitoring of
als

[
10
]
, as speech-based
ml
(
ml
) methods offer a non-invasive and sensitive way to identify and track speech impairments
[
10
,
11
]
.
Previous works
have reported accuracy scores up to 98% for distinguishing
hc
(
hc
) from
pwals
with a bulbar onset
[
10
,
12
]
. Beyond detection, studies have also targeted speech severity: Vieira et al. achieved a multiclass
auc
(
auc
) of 86% for predicting the
alsfrsr
-speech score
[
13
]
, and Mallol-Ragolta et al. reached an
uar
(
uar
) of 88% when identifying
pwals
with detectable speech impairments
[
14
]
. Kothare et al. further modelled individual
alsfrsr
trajectories using acoustic, linguistic, and orofacial cues
[
15
]
. The recent SAND challenge
[
16
]
used an Italian dataset, to tackle (1) multi-class
als
classification and (2)
alsfrsr
score prediction. Top participants reached about 60% F1 for task 1 and 57% for task 2, the latter not surpassing the 58% baseline
[
16
]
.

However, current studies show considerably variability in data collection protocols
[
10
,
17
]
. Although some initial efforts in harmonising data collection for atypical speech have been made
[
18
]
, they are not specific for
als
and still at an early stage. In the case of
als
, standardising speech protocols is particularly important given the burden of the disease.
Combined with the clinical heterogeneity of
als

[
3
]
, these challenges hinder comparison across studies.
In this work, we address these gaps by evaluating: (i) differences in prediction performance between cross-sectional and within-speaker speech impairment models, (ii) differences in predictability between two speech-related clinical scores–the established
alsfrsr
-speech and the more subjective
qoldys
scores– and (iii) the utility of common speech tasks for predicting speech impairment.
By analysing both scores, multiple speech tasks, and two modelling settings within the same
als
cohort, our study provides a
comparison for speech tasks, modelling settings and clinical scores for speech impairment prediction.
Our research questions are:

•

RQ1:

Prediction of speech impairment scores for
pwals
:
Can acoustic features predict speech impairment both across speakers (cross-sectional) and within individuals during clinical assessments?

•

RQ2:

Comparison of speech impairment scores
: Which of the two speech‑related clinical scores (
alsfrsr
-speech or
qoldys
) is more predictable from acoustic information?

•

RQ3:

Speech task utility
: Which speech task(s)
is/are most informative for predicting these scores?

2
presents the dataset and describes the methodology. Sections
3
and

report and discuss the obtained

5
concludes the paper and section
6

Material and methods

2.1
Dataset

The AIMnd 2.0 dataset is an extension of the dataset
introduced in
[
19
]
, collected at the outpatient clinic for
mnd

in Germany. The data collection received ethical approval (nr.2023-325-S-NP).
Recording details are available in
[

.

The dataset contains audiovisual recordings of
hc

pwmnd

pwals

We analyse five speech tasks: sustained
\textipa

[
20

[
10

21

Other non-speech tasks were also recorded but are out of the scope in this work.

2.2
Data processing

Audio-visual recordings are converted to WAV, downsampled to 16 kHz, and segmented using a
vad
(
vad
) model
[
22
]
1
1

1

https://huggingface.co/pyannote/voice-activity-detection
with default settings; segments shorter than 300 ms were discarded. To reduce background noise–from a laboratory freezer–we apply spectral-gating noise reduction, removing approximately 70% of stationary noise.

To address RQ1 (
prediction of speech impairment scores for
pwals
), we define two split strategies: a speaker‑independent split for cross‑sectional modelling and a speaker‑dependent split for within‑speaker setting.
To create the speaker-independent splits (cross-sectional setting), we create three severity bins from the
alsfrsr
-speech score using quantile binning. We perform a speaker-level, stratified split, keeping the distribution of severity bins across training and testing. All sessions from each speaker are kept within the same split to prevent speaker leakage. The test size corresponds to 20% of the speakers. The resulting cohort, clinical scores, and metadata for each split are reported in Table
1
.
For the within-speaker setting, we define a temporal split. The training set contains all first sessions from each speaker (speakers=66,
alsfrsr
-speech=3.11
\pm
0.99,
qoldys
=14.53
\pm
10.89). The testing set consists of the speakers’ second and third sessions, if available (speakers=17, sessions=20,
alsfrsr
-speech=3.10
\pm
0.79,
qoldys
=14.00
\pm
8.78).
This split ensures that evaluations are performed on future sessions, preventing temporal leakage and mimicking a monitoring scenario in which a clinician uses knowledge from each patient to assess their follow‑up progression.

Feature extraction

We extract one hand-crafted and two deep-learnt feature sets:
1) The
egemaps
(

)
[

]
from the openSMILE

]

,

. Concretely, we use the 88 functionals and summary statistics from the 25 Low-Level Descriptors (LLDs). These features are also easily reproducible.
2) Embeddings from the transformer‑based Whisper Large v3 model

Regression.

We employ
svm
,
xgb
(
xgb
), and
rf
for predicting two speech-related clinical scores: the
alsfrsr
-speech and
qoldys
scores.
According to a systematic review by Bowden et al. no single model consistently outperforms others in speech-based
als
research; however,
svm
and
rf
generally achieved the strongest performance across prior studies
[
10
]
. To address RQ3 (
speech task utility
), each model is trained separately on each speech task.
To obtain a final combined prediction across tasks, we apply mean fusion to the task-specific predictions for each model and feature set.

Model parameters are optimised through a randomised search combined with GroupKFold
cv
(
cv
), using five folds defined at the speaker-level to prevent speaker leakage between training and validation splits. Experiments are implemented in scikit-learn. Further reproducibility details are available in Section
6
. For the
svm
models, we explore the following parameter space:
C
\in{logspace(-2,2,20)}
,
epsilon
\in{0.05,0.1,0.2,0.5}
,
kernel
\in{linear,rbf}
,
gamma
\in{scale,auto}
.
For the
xgb
models, the parameters are:
n_estimators
\in{200,400,600}
,
learning_rate
\in{0.03,0.05,0.1}
,
max_depth
\in{3,4,5,6}
,
subsample
\in{0.6,0.8}
,
colsample_bytree
\in{0.6,0.8}
.
For the
rf
models, we consider:
n_estimators
\in{100,200,400}
,
max_depth
\in{None,10,20,30}
,
min_samples_split
\in{2,5,10}
.
Optimal parameters are selected for each model, speech task and feature set. The best-performing configurations are used to retrain the final model, which is then evaluated on the testing set.

Evaluation.
Models are evaluated in terms of
ccc
(
ccc
) at a speaker- and session-level, meaning we have one inference per session and per speaker.
We also compute the 95% Confidence Interval (CI) for
ccc
.
The CIs are calculated using 1,000 bootstrapping
iterations
[
31
]
.
To further explore agreement, we calculate Bias (difference between predicted values and ground truth values) and upper and lower
loa
(
loa
), calculated as

\text{LoA}_{\text{lower}}=\text{Bias}-1.96\,s_{d}

\text{LoA}_{\text{upper}}=\text{Bias}+1.96\,s_{d},

where
s_{d}
is the standard deviation of the differences, computed as the square root of the sum of squared deviations of each individual difference (
\hat{y}_{i}-y_{i}
) from the Bias, divided by
n-1
.

4
Discussion

Regarding RQ1 (
prediction of speech impairment scores for
pwals
), results on the testing set are promising for most speech tasks and for both settings, with exceptions found mostly for the speech task
\textipa
/a:/. Across tasks and modelling settings, Whisper embeddings combined with
svm
outperform other feature and model combinations in most cases.
As expected, within-speaker predictions (Table
3
) outperform cross-speaker results (Table
2
) for both speech impairment scores and for almost all speech tasks, with the exception of picture description and the fused model for
alsfrsr
-speech. In the within-speaker setting, data from the same speakers appear in both training and testing, using a temporal split in which earlier sessions correspond to the training set. The aim of the setup is to account for the heterogeneous progression of
als
. Although simple, our approach demonstrates potential for
detecting speech impairment in both newly diagnosed German-speaking patients and those undergoing follow-up visits.
Task fusion shows promising results for
qoldys
in the within-speaker setting. In contrast, fusion-based prediction of the
alsfrsr
-speech score shows slightly higher results for cross-sectional setting over the within-speaker setting, the difference is marginal (0.02) to support solid conclusions.

Addressing RQ2 (
comparison of speech impairment scores
),
prediction performance was higher for
qoldys
than for
alsfrsr
-speech in both cross-speaker and within-speaker settings. This likely highlights differences in scale design:
qoldys
is a continuous scale from 0 to 40, whereas
alsfrsr
-speech is a coarse 0–4 ordinal scale and is more susceptible to class imbalance with the highest score being overrepresented.
Further, regression tends to perform better on continuous targets than on ordinal labels and future work should investigate other modelling approaches for ordinal labels
[
32
]
.
qoldys
focuses on speech instability and subtle perceptual changes, which may be earlier reflected in acoustic features, while
alsfrsr
-speech assesses overall functional communication, which tends to be more stable across time and only major changes are then reflected. Also, as the
alsfrsr
-speech was rated by different physicians, there may be inter-rater variability in the
alsfrsr
-speech ratings.
To the best of our knowledge,
qoldys
has not previously been used as a speech-based prediction target. We believe future work should combine both measures, as they provide a more comprehensive assessment of speech impairment in
pwals
and may support the development of more effective speech-based
ml
systems for
als
.

Concerning RQ3 (
speech task utility
), the read passage task achieved the highest
ccc
for predicting
alsfrsr
-speech, while /da/-/ba/ and /da/-/da/ performed best for
qoldys
in the cross-sectional setting. Although /da/-/da/ reached a comparatively high
ccc
, it showed underestimation in the predictions (negative mean bias), whereas /da/-/ba/ exhibited minimal bias. Across speech tasks, variability in
loa
shows task-dependent prediction stability. For example, the picture description and the read passage tasks show wider
loa
when predicting
qoldys
, indicating high variation of prediction errors despite moderate
ccc
values.
A similar pattern is observed in the within-speaker setting. For predicting the
alsfrsr
-speech score, the read passage showed the highest agreement, whereas /da/-da/ performed best for
qoldys
.

The sustained
\textipa
/a:/ task exhibited the lowest
ccc
values in both cross-sectional and within-speaker analyses.
Mallol-Ragolta et al. recently identified
\textipa
/a:/ as a highly informative task for distinguishing between
pwals
and
hc

[
19
]
compared to other commonly used speech tasks. This finding is also supported by other work classifying between
als
and
hc

[
33
]
.
Since our results are inconclusive mostly due to the small sample size, further work is needed to clarify the utility of sustained
\textipa
/a:/ in speech impairment prediction in
pwals
.

Diadochokinetic and reading tasks seem more informative for detecting subtle changes in speech impairment, likely because they place greater demands on oral motor control and coordination. This findings is supported by Mallol-Ragolta et al.  who found that reading and syllable repetition tasks are among the most salient speech tasks for distinguishing between
hc
and
pwals
with normal speech (
alsfrsr
-speech equal to 4)
[
14
]
.
Given that RQ2 suggests that
qoldys
is more sensitive to changes in speech impairment, diadochokinetic tasks, the tasks with the highest
ccc
for this score, may better capture subtle changes in articulation and speech motor coordination.
In contrast, read speech may reflect better functional speech intelligibility, which aligns more to how the
alsfrsr
-speech score is measured
[
5
]
.
In general, our findings suggest that model performance is task-dependent. This highlights the importance of understanding speech task utility for speech impairment prediction in
pwals
.

Current results prevent us from drawing solid conclusion.
The dataset used in this study is derived from a single clinical centre and includes a small cohort. Follow-up data are particularly limited, as only 17 of 66
pwals
contributed with more than one session.
This highlights the challenges of collecting speech data from
pwals
,
as disease progression leads to increased physical impairment and
potentially
reduced capacity or willingness to participate.
Consequently, within-individuals’ findings remain preliminary.

Methodologically, the present analyses are restricted to commonly used and easily reproducible acoustic feature sets. Next steps consist of expanding these to include less frequently explored acoustic features, such as articulatory precision and sentence intelligibility
[
34
,
35
]
.
In addition, incorporating linguistic and orofacial features, as proposed in
[
15
]
, may further enhance prediction performance of speech impairment. Integrating feature interpretability will also be a key objective in subsequent work.

Future work should also investigate more advanced personalised frameworks, as our current within-speaker approach primarily addresses temporal modelling and does not employ individualised strategies, mostly due to the limited availability of longitudinal samples.
Further research should also evaluate the generalisability of the proposed approach to related conditions such as other
mnd
, while accounting for potential confounding factors (e.g., medication, weight,
als
onset).
Next steps will also address methodological guidelines specific for the application of
ml
in healthcare such as the CLARITY AI
[
36
]
as well as the clinical usability of speech analysis in healthcare settings.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we explored speech impairment prediction in
pwals
. We addressed three research questions: (i) whether acoustic features can predict speech‑related
als
clinical scores both across speakers and within individuals over time; (ii) differences in performance between
alsfrsr
-speech and
qoldys
scores; and (iii) how informative commonly used speech tasks are for this prediction.
Results indicate promising performance
for predicting the two speech-related
als
clinical scores in both a cross-section and within-speaker settings, showing slightly higher performances for predicting the
qoldys
score, suggesting that acoustic features may be more suitable for granular speech changes assessed in this score. Among the five speech tasks, diadochokinetic and the reading tasks yielded the most promising results, whereas the sustained vowel
\textipa
/a:/ showed the weakest performance. Future work is needed to draw more solid conclusions.
A system capable of automatically predicting speech impairment in
pwals
could speed up evaluation during clinical assessments.
Patients could perform speech tasks during waiting times at the clinic or from home. Such system could serve as a support tool for monitoring disease progression.
