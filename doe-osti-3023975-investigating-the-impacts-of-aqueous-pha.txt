Title: Investigating the Impacts of Aqueous-Phase Processing on Organic
 Aerosol Chemical Climatology Using ARM and ASR Observations
SC Division: SC-23.1
Report/Product Number: DOE-UCDavis-DE-SC0022140
Program Manager: Dr. Shaima Nasiri
 Atmospheric aerosols influence climate through both direct radiative effects and cloud-
mediated processes. Organic aerosol (OA), a major component of aerosol particles, plays a key
role in these processes by affecting aerosol optical properties, hygroscopicity, and cloud
condensation nuclei (CCN) activity. A large fraction of OA consists of secondary organic aerosol
(SOA) formed from oxidation of volatile organic compounds (VOCs). However, the processes
controlling SOA formation and evolution remain incompletely understood, contributing to
significant uncertainties in atmospheric models.
 In addition to gas-phase pathways, aqueous-phase chemistry in cloud, fog, and aerosol liquid
water is increasingly recognized as a major source of SOA. These processes can rapidly generate
highly oxidized, low-volatility compounds (aqSOA), modify aerosol composition and
microphysical properties, and produce light-absorbing brown carbon (BrC), which influences
both aerosol absorptivity and condensed-phase oxidant formation. Field observations frequently
show enhanced SOA under high-humidity conditions, often accompanied by strong correlations
with secondary inorganic species such as sulfate, highlighting the importance of aqueous
processing in atmospheric aerosol evolution.
 This project aims to quantify the role of aqueous-phase chemistry in the formation, evolution,
and properties of aqSOA to support improved model representation of atmospheric aerosols. To
achieve this, we performed integrated analyses of high-resolution aerosol mass spectrometry data
from DOE ASR/ARM field campaigns and long-term observations, complemented by targeted
laboratory studies.
 Building on this integrated approach, this project was organized around several key focus
areas: 1) investigating the role of aqueous-phase chemistry in ambient SOA formation and
evolution across diverse environments; 2) advancing UAS-based aerosol measurement
techniques to enable high-resolution characterization of aerosol vertical distributions and
aerosol-cloud interactions; 3) identifying diagnostic mass spectral signatures of aqSOA
associated with cloud and fog processing and elevated aerosol liquid water conditions; 4)
developing a multilinear engine (ME-2) – based framework to improve characterization of
organic aerosol composition and chemical climatology using long-term aerosol measurements;
and 5) synthesizing results from this research with global AMS and ACSM datasets and expand
collaboration with modeling communities.
 Together, these efforts advance understanding of the chemical and physical properties of
aqSOA, including its oxidation state, molecular composition, and links to aerosol size
distributions and cloud-relevant properties. They also provide observational constraints and data
products that support model evaluation and improvement. In doing so, the project addresses key
scientific questions regarding the importance of aqueous-phase chemistry in SOA formation, the
 1

identification of aqSOA signatures in ambient data, and the role of aqSOA in aerosol–cloud
interactions, thereby contributing to reduced uncertainties in aerosol–climate assessments.
 This work has so far resulted in 16 peer-reviewed publications, one submitted manuscript,
and numerous conference presentations. The key accomplishments for each focus area are
summarized below.

Focus Area #1: Investigating the role of aqueous-phase chemistry in ambient SOA
 formation and evolution across diverse environments
 Through integrated analyses of high-resolution aerosol mass spectrometer (HR-AMS) dataset
from multiple DOE-supported field campaigns (HI-SCALE, BBOP, TRACER, CARES, and
BNL Aerosol Lifecycle studies), along with wintertime measurements in California’s San
Joaquin Valley and observations of long-range transported biomass burning plumes at Mt.
Bachelor, we examined aerosol evolution across a wide range of atmospheric conditions,
including cloud-influenced environments and high-humidity settings with persistent fog and
elevated aerosol liquid water conducive to aqueous-phase processing (Farley et al., 2022; Farley
et al., 2025; Farley et al., 2023; Farley et al., 2024; Trousdell et al., 2026).
 Results consistently show that aqueous-phase chemistry plays a major role in SOA formation
and atmospheric aging. In biomass burning plumes, aqueous processing drives continued
chemical evolution during long-range transport, producing more oxygenated and less volatile
organic aerosol and substantially altering aerosol properties under relatively clean background
conditions (Farley et al., 2022; Farley et al., 2025). This evolution is accompanied by changes in
particle mixing state and the formation of secondary coatings, indicating that aqueous processing
plays an important role in modifying both OA and black carbon (BC)-containing particles. In
urban and industrial environments, including Houston during the TRACER campaign and the
San Joaquin Valley of California, aqueous-phase processing also contributes to the formation
and transformation of soot coatings, influencing particle composition, mixing state, and
potentially optical properties (Farley et al., 2023; Farley et al., 2024).
 Analyses of cloud- and humidity-influenced environments further demonstrate that aqueous-
phase reactions are closely linked to aerosol liquid water and cloud–boundary layer interactions,
which facilitate rapid SOA production and chemical transformation (Trousdell et al., 2026).
These processes are associated with enhanced SOA mass, increased oxidation state, and the
formation of highly functionalized organic species characteristic of aqSOA. The Trousdell et al
(2026) study provides direct field evidence that aqueous-phase chemistry significantly influences
aerosol composition and evolution at Southern Great Plains (SGP) and demonstrates an
integrated chemical-microphysical framework for diagnosing aqSOA processing in ambient
AMS datasets.
 Overall, these results provide strong and consistent evidence that aqueous-phase chemistry is
a major pathway governing SOA formation and aging across a wide range of atmospheric
conditions. By driving increases in aerosol mass, oxidation state, and chemical complexity,
aqueous processing exerts a strong influence on aerosol composition, volatility, mixing state, and
climate-relevant properties. These findings provide critical observational constraints and a
unified framework for interpreting aqSOA in ambient datasets, underscoring the importance of
explicitly representing multiphase processes in atmospheric models to improve predictions of
aerosol–radiative forcing interactions.

Focus Area #2: Advancing UAS-based aerosol measurement techniques to enable high-
 resolution characterization of aerosol vertical distributions and their interactions
 with clouds
 This project advanced observational capabilities for characterizing atmospheric aerosols by
integrating uncrewed aerial systems (UAS) with novel offline aerosol mass spectrometry to
enable vertically resolved measurements of aerosol chemical composition and properties. A key
innovation was the development of analytical methods for quantitative chemical analysis of
nanogram-level particulate matter, allowing detailed characterization of aerosols collected from
UAS platforms operating across the boundary layer and lower troposphere (Niedek et al., 2025;
Niedek et al., 2023).
 Building on these capabilities, UAS platforms such as ArcticShark provide flexible, high-
resolution sampling in regions that are difficult to access using traditional ground-based or
aircraft measurements, particularly within and near clouds and across sharp vertical gradients.
Observations over the SGP reveal pronounced vertical variability in aerosol composition and
properties, reflecting the combined influences of emissions, boundary layer dynamics, cloud
processing, and secondary formation (Mei et al., 2025). These vertically resolved datasets offer
critical insights into how aerosols are transported, transformed, and coupled to cloud processes.
 Importantly, the integration of UAS observations with process-level modeling demonstrates
how vertically resolved measurements can directly constrain simulations of aerosol–cloud
interactions and aerosol lifecycle processes (Mei et al., 2024). The ability to resolve vertical
structure is particularly important for understanding aerosol impacts on cloud formation, cloud
microphysics, and radiative effects, which are highly sensitive to aerosol properties at cloud-
relevant altitudes. In addition, measurements of UAS samples collected under cloud processing
conditions provide direct insights into how clouds modify aerosol composition and properties
(Niedek et al., 2025)
 Overall, this work establishes UAS-based aerosol sampling as a powerful tool for advancing
atmospheric science. The combination of vertically resolved observations and high-sensitivity
chemical analysis provides new constraints on aerosol sources, transformations, and aerosol–
cloud interactions, supporting improved representation of these processes in Earth system
models.

Focus Area #3: Identifying diagnostic mass spectral signatures of aqSOA associated
 with cloud and fog processing and elevated aerosol liquid water conditions
 Complementing Focus Areas #1 and #2, this effort focused on identifying diagnostic mass
spectral signatures of aqSOA through integrated laboratory experiments, field observations, and
HR-AMS analysis. We established molecular- and ion-level indicators that distinguish aqSOA
from gas-phase SOA, with emphasis on precursors that readily undergo aqueous reactions in the
atmosphere, including phenolic compounds from biomass burning and nitrogen-containing
organics.
 Laboratory studies show that aqueous processing produces more highly oxidized and
functionally diverse SOA compared to gas-phase pathways, resulting in distinct mass spectral
features characterized by enhanced oxygenation, fragmentation, and oligomerization (Jiang et al.,
2024). Photoaging experiments further reveal that aqueous processing drives characteristic
 3

changes in spectral profiles, including increased signals from oxidized fragments and light-
absorbing BrC components, linking aqueous chemistry to both composition and optical
properties (Jiang et al., 2023a).
 Consistent with these findings, analyses of water-soluble organic aerosol and fog water
demonstrate that cloud and high-humidity conditions promote aqSOA formation with identifiable
spectral markers, including highly oxygenated species, organic acids, and nitrogen-containing
compounds (Jiang et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2023b). Advances in HR-AMS analysis improve
detection of these features and highlight organic nitrogen as an important tracer of aqueous
processing in environments with elevated aerosol liquid water (Ge et al., 2024). Complementary
studies of fog water demonstrate that photochemical processing of dissolved organic matter
drives oxidation and functionalization pathways reflected in both molecular composition and
bulk mass spectra of aqSOA (Jiang et al., 2025).
 Together, this work establishes a robust framework for identifying aqSOA in ambient
datasets using HR-AMS, linking specific mass spectral features to aqueous-phase formation
pathways under cloud/fog conditions and high aerosol liquid water. These diagnostic signatures
provide critical tools for interpreting field observations, quantifying aqSOA contributions, and
improving representation of multiphase SOA processes in atmospheric models.

Focus Area #4: Developing a multilinear engine (ME-2) – based framework to improve
 characterization of organic aerosol composition and chemical climatology using
 long-term aerosol measurements
 Building on prior DOE ASR–funded efforts, we refined rolling-window ME-2 source
apportionment approaches to improve the resolution and interpretation of OA components in
long-term datasets acquired with aerosol chemical speciation monitors (ACSMs). By integrating
ME-2 analysis with laboratory constraints, tracer ions, reference spectra, and atmospheric
conditions, we quantified the contribution of aqueous-phase processes to SOA formation and
atmospheric aging. Complementary single-particle measurements further highlight the chemical
diversity and mixing state of atmospheric aerosols, underscoring the importance of resolving
heterogeneous aerosol populations when interpreting bulk OA composition (Wu et al., 2024).
 This framework enables more robust separation of OA sources and atmospheric processes,
improving characterization of temporal variability and aerosol chemical climatology, and
providing critical observational constraints for evaluating and improving model representations
of multiphase SOA processes.

Focus Area #5: Synthesizing results from this research with global AMS and ACSM
 datasets and expand collaboration with modeling communities
 This focus area includes two main tasks: 1) expanding and integrating a global database of
aerosol mass spectrometry observations, incorporating DOE datasets and results from Focus
Areas 1–4 alongside AMS and ACSM measurements contributed by collaborators and the
broader AMS community; and 2) strengthening collaborations with modelers to apply these
datasets for improving representation of aerosol processes and chemical climatology in global
models. The resulting observational and mechanistic insights have been increasingly
incorporated into modeling frameworks, including recent efforts to simulate aqueous-phase

 4

processing of biomass burning phenols and their contribution to SOA formation (Zhang et al.,
2024). In addition, these datasets have supported large-scale assessments of particulate matter,
including improved estimates of submicron aerosol (PM1) concentrations across the United
States (Li et al., 2025).
 In parallel, we expanded an online global database of aerosol measurements acquired with
AMS and ACSM (https://sites.google.com/site/amsglobaldatabase/), which currently includes
over 100 datasets from a wide range of environments and measurement platforms. Each dataset
is accompanied by detailed metadata and includes key parameters such as mass concentrations,
mass spectra, tracer ions, elemental ratios, size distributions, and factor analysis results.
Together, this database provides a comprehensive resource for examining PM sources and
evolution on a global scale. It enables cross-site comparisons, supports improved understanding
of OA properties and processes, and provides critical observational constraints for evaluating and
improving atmospheric models. These efforts facilitate the translation of field observations into
data products and parameterizations that can be directly applied in model development and
assessment.
