Recovery After Surgery for Insertional Achilles Tendinopathy With and Without Bioinductive Scaffold Augmentation: A Retrospective Comparative Cohort Study
Authors: Gil Genuth, Peter Stavrou, Lukas D Iselin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Insertional Achilles tendinopathy (IAT), often associated with Haglund deformity, is a common cause of posterior heel pain, with up to 40% of patients requiring surgical intervention after unsuccessful conservative treatment. Bioinductive collagen scaffolds have been proposed as a potential adjunct for tendon healing.Purpose:To evaluate clinical and structural outcomes after open IAT surgery with and without scaffold augmentation.Study Design:Cohort study; Level of evidence, 3.Methods:A retrospective comparative cohort study was conducted between 2021 and 2024, including 30 consecutive patients (30 ankles) who underwent open IAT surgery with Haglund resection and double-row tendon reattachment performed by a single surgeon. Fifteen patients underwent standard repair, and 15 received additional augmentation with a bioinductive collagen scaffold. Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Physical Function (PF) and Pain Interference (PI) scores were collected preoperatively and at 6, 12, and 18 months postoperatively. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was obtained at study enrolment (baseline) and repeated at 12 months after surgery. Secondary outcomes included time to return to sport (RTS), and all postoperative complications were recorded. Minimal clinically important difference thresholds of 5 to 8 points for PF and 8 to 10 points for PI were applied. Statistical analysis included between-group comparisons at each time point and evaluation of changes over time, with significance set at P < .05.Results:Preoperative PROMIS scores were similar between groups (PF: 39.8 ± 5.7 vs 40.4 ± 6.1; PI: 64.1 ± 6.4 vs 63.7 ± 5.9). At 6 months, both groups improved, with greater change in the scaffold group (PF: 54 ± 5.3 vs 49.5 ± 6; P = .03; PI: 50.6 ± 6.1 vs 55.3 ± 6.4; P = .04). At 12 months, PROMIS scores were similar (PF: 58.1 ± 4.9 vs 57 ± 5.1; P = 0.41; PI: 47.2 ± 5.4 vs 48.5 ± 5; P = 0.38), and this was maintained at 18 months (PF: 59.4 ± 4.7 vs 58.7 ± 4.9; P = 0.67; PI: 45.9 ± 5.1 vs 46.7 ± 4.8; P = .59). Time to RTS was shorter in the scaffold group (8 ± 1.7 vs 9.7 ± 2.1 months; P = .02). MRI at 12 months showed tendon integrity in all cases, and complication rates were low and comparable between groups.Conclusion:Our study shows that bioinductive scaffold augmentation in open IAT surgery is safe and may be associated with earlier improvements in pain, function, and RTS, while longer-term outcomes are similar to standard repair. These findings suggest a potential role for this approach in active individuals seeking a faster recovery.

Insertional Achilles tendinopathy (IAT) is a common and often debilitating condition encountered in foot and ankle practice, frequently associated with a Haglund deformity (HD) and forming part of the Haglund syndrome triad. 13 Up to 6% to 8% of the general population experience Achilles tendon pain, and approximately one-third of these cases are attributed to IAT.11,12 Among patients presenting with IAT, as many as 25% exhibit concomitant HD, where mechanical irritation and retrocalcaneal bursitis contribute to chronic pain and functional decline.12,16 While nonoperative management remains the initial treatment, failure rates of up to 40% have been reported, and persistent symptoms after 3 to 6 months of conservative therapy often lead to surgical intervention.4,12 There are several operative options for treating IAT, including both open and minimally invasive techniques. Although minimally invasive approaches have gained popularity in recent years, the most commonly performed procedure remains an open Achilles debridement, which permits direct access for debridement of diseased tendon tissue and resection of associated HD before reattachment to the calcaneus.7,8,12,15

In recent years, bioinductive collagen scaffolds composed of highly porous bovine type I collagen have emerged as a biologically active adjunct to enhance tendon healing.17,19,20 These implants promote cellular infiltration, fibroblast activity, and deposition of new collagen fibers, ultimately integrating into the native tendon as the scaffold resorbs. Their successful application in rotator cuff surgery has been well documented, with multiple studies demonstrating safety, increased tendon thickness, and significant improvements in pain and functional outcomes.20,21 Translational interest has expanded to include lower-extremity tendon pathology, with early evidence suggesting potential benefit in degenerative Achilles conditions. 19

The use of a bioinductive scaffold as an adjunct in the surgical treatment of IAT and Haglund syndrome is therefore of growing interest but remains sparsely reported in the literature. Genuth and Iselin 7 published the first series of 8 patients treated with open IAT surgery augmented with a bioinductive scaffold, demonstrating improvements in pain and function, alongside encouraging magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) evidence of tendon remodeling and low complication rates at short-term follow-up. However, comparative data assessing whether scaffold augmentation provides measurable clinical or structural advantages over standard IAT surgery remain lacking.

The present study aims to address this gap by comparing outcomes of open IAT surgery performed with and without augmentation using a bioinductive scaffold (REGENETEN, Smith & Nephew), with 18-month follow-up. The purpose of this study was to evaluate, through combined clinical and radiographic outcomes, whether bioinductive scaffold augmentation provides a benefit in the surgical management of insertional Achilles tendinopathy. We hypothesized that scaffold augmentation would improve clinical outcomes and enhance radiographic tendon healing compared with standard surgical treatment alone.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

Patients were diagnosed with IAT associated with an HD based on clinical evaluation and confirmatory radiographic imaging. All included patients were physically active individuals, defined a priori as participation in sport or recreational physical activity at least twice per week for a minimum of 60 minutes per session before symptom onset and study enrolment. This retrospective comparative cohort study was conducted at a single tertiary foot and ankle center. Inclusion criteria comprised adults aged ≥18 years who remained symptomatic despite at least 3 months of structured nonoperative management, such as physical therapy, inserts, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and subsequently underwent primary open surgical treatment. Exclusion criteria included previous Achilles tendon surgery on the affected side, acute Achilles tendon rupture, inflammatory or systemic tendon disorders, active infection, or insufficient clinical follow-up. Between 2021 and 2024, a total of 30 patients (30 ankles) underwent open surgical treatment for IAT, all of which were performed by the senior author (G.G).

Fifteen patients underwent standard open Achilles debridement with Haglund resection and tendon reattachment using a double row technique (control group), while 15 patients underwent the same procedure augmented with a bioinductive collagen scaffold (scaffold group). The bioinductive implant is a highly porous, resorbable type I bovine collagen scaffold engineered to facilitate biologically mediated tendon regeneration. The implant is applied to the surface of the diseased tendon and secured with bioabsorbable fixation, serving as a bioinductive matrix that promotes host cell migration, neovascularization, and deposition of an organized extracellular matrix. The scaffold undergoes gradual enzymatic resorption and is replaced by native tissue, supporting structural restoration.

The primary surgeon (G.G.) adopted bioinductive scaffold augmentation as part of the operative technique in 2023, and implant use was determined on a case-by-case basis based on the surgeon's clinical judgment. Prospectively collected clinical and imaging data were analyzed retrospectively as part of routine clinical care. The study was approved as a service evaluation by the institutional ethics committee. Variables recorded included age, sex, comorbidities, complications, and functional outcomes.

Outcome Measures

Patient-reported outcomes included Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Physical Function (PF) and PROMIS Pain Interference (PI) scores collected preoperatively and at 6, 12, and 18 months postoperatively. Additional outcomes included time to return to sport (RTS) and postoperative complications, with specific attention to infection, wound dehiscence, and tendon-related complications.

In this study, we applied established minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds to interpret PROMIS outcomes. For PROMIS PI, an MCID of approximately 8 to 10 points was used, reflecting the degree of improvement patients reliably perceive as meaningful in foot and ankle conditions. For PROMIS PF, we used an MCID of 5 to 8 points, consistent with published values for lower-extremity musculoskeletal disorders. 10 These thresholds were used both to inform our a priori sample-size calculation and to evaluate whether postoperative improvements represented clinically significant change in addition to statistical significance (A detailed description of the a priori sample size calculation used to determine the study cohort is provided in the statistical analysis section below.)

MRI was obtained at baseline upon enrolment into the study and repeated at 12 months postoperatively to assess structural outcomes. Imaging evaluation included assessment of Achilles tendon thickness, fiber continuity, residual tendinopathic signal, insertional integrity at the calcaneal footprint, and the presence of retrocalcaneal bursitis or calcaneal bone marrow edema. Particular attention was given to intra-tendinous signal intensity on fluid-sensitive sequences (T2-weighted and short tau inversion recovery [STIR]) as well as tendon morphology and fiber organization on T1-weighted and PD sequences. In the scaffold cohort, MRI was also evaluated for features consistent with scaffold integration or resorption and restoration of normal tendon architecture. All imaging studies were reviewed independently by a musculoskeletal radiologist who was blinded to treatment allocation.

Surgical Technique

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with the patient in the prone position. A midline posterior incision was made extending to the Achilles insertion. The tendon was split full-thickness along the midline and debrided of all macroscopically diseased tissue. The Haglund prominence was resected using a micro sagittal saw and osteotomes under fluoroscopic guidance to ensure complete decompression.

Tendon reattachment was performed using a standardized double-row configuration (Healicoil anchors, suture tapes, and knotless footprint anchors; Smith & Nephew). In the scaffold group, a bioinductive type I collagen scaffold (REGENETEN) was applied over the repaired distal Achilles tendon according to the manufacturer's surgical technique (Figure 1). The control group underwent the same procedure without scaffold augmentation. Soft-tissue closure was performed in layers, and all patients were placed in a walking boot without heel lifts.

Postoperative Protocol

Both cohorts followed an identical rehabilitation pathway. Patients remained nonweightbearing for 4 weeks in a walking boot, followed by protected weightbearing for an additional 4 weeks in the same boot. Transition to athletic footwear occurred at 8 weeks. Physical therapy commenced at 4 weeks, focusing on gradual strengthening and functional progression. Importantly, all patients underwent physical therapy according to a standardized, predefined rehabilitation protocol shared by all treating physical therapists, who were blinded to patients’ treatment group allocation. The primary operating surgeon (G.G.) maintained close communication with the treating physical therapists and was actively involved in the decision-making process regarding progression through rehabilitation milestones. RTS was defined as the patient's ability to return to their preoperative level of sport participation, including the same type, frequency, and intensity of athletic activity performed before the onset of symptoms. Clearance for RTS was based on functional readiness and was determined jointly by the treating physical therapist and the primary surgeon after a comprehensive clinical and functional assessment.

A Priori Sample Size Calculation

A formal a priori sample-size calculation was performed using PROMIS PF and PROMIS PI as the primary endpoints. Previous literature in foot and ankle surgery indicates a minimal MCID of approximately 5 to 8 points for PROMIS PF and 8–10 points for PROMIS PI, with reported standard deviations ranging from 8 to 12 points. Based on these values, and assuming an expected effect size of approximately 0.9, a power of 80%, and an alpha level of .05, a minimum of 13 patients per group was required to detect a clinically meaningful difference. To accommodate potential loss to follow-up, the final target sample size was increased to 15 patients per group. This sample size is consistent with comparable Achilles studies and provides adequate power to detect meaningful differences in patient-reported outcomes.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported as means with standard deviations and ranges for continuous variables, and as frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Between-group comparisons of PROMIS PF, PROMIS PI, and RTS timelines were performed using independent t tests or Mann-Whitney U tests, depending on data distribution. Within-group changes from pre- to postoperative timepoints were assessed using paired t tests. Given that the MCID for PROMIS PF is generally 5 to 8 points and for PROMIS PI is 8 to 10 points, postoperative improvements exceeding these thresholds were considered clinically meaningful in addition to being statistically significant. Categorical variables, including complication rates, were compared using the chi-square test or the Fisher exact test. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 29 (IBM).

Results

Between 2021 and 2024, a total of 30 patients met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled in the study: 15 in the bioinductive scaffold group (8 men, 7 women) and 15 in the nonscaffold control group (7 men, 8 women).

The mean age was similar (bioinductive scaffold: 52.4 ± 7.1 years; control: 51.9 ± 6.8 years; P = .84). Comorbidities were evenly distributed and overall low in frequency: hypertension was present in 3 patients in the bioinductive scaffold group and 2 in the control group, and type 2 diabetes mellitus in 1 patient in each group. No patients in either cohort had inflammatory arthropathies, connective-tissue disorders, chronic steroid use, or immunosuppressive therapy (Table 1).

All patients had clinically and radiographically confirmed IAT with HD and had completed a mean of 5 ± 1.6 months of unsuccessful conservative treatment. Preoperative MRI in all cases demonstrated findings consistent with IAT, including distal Achilles tendinosis and associated retrocalcaneal bursitis. On sagittal and axial T2-weighted and PD fat-suppressed sequences, increased intratendinous signal intensity and thickening of the distal Achilles tendon at its calcaneal insertion were observed, reflecting degenerative changes and tendinosis. Fluid-sensitive sequences (T2 FS/STIR) demonstrated hyperintense fluid distension of the retrocalcaneal bursa, consistent with bursitis. In several cases, subcortical marrow edema within the posterior superior calcaneus was also visible on STIR sequences, suggesting reactive bone changes related to chronic impingement at the Achilles insertion. No full-thickness tendon tears were identified (Figure 2).

Preoperative PROMIS PF scores were comparable (bioinductive scaffold: 39.8 ± 5.7; control: 40.4 ± 6.1; P = .74), as were PROMIS PI scores (bioinductive scaffold: 64.1 ± 6.4; control: 63.7 ± 5.9; P = .85). At 6 months, the bioinductive scaffold cohort demonstrated better outcomes, with PROMIS PF improving to 54 ± 5.3 versus 49.5 ± 6 in controls (P = .03) and PROMIS PI improving to 50.6 ± 6.1 versus 55.3 ± 6.4 in controls (P = .04). By 12 months (PF: 58.1 ± 4.9 versus 57 ± 5.1; P = 0.41; PI: 47.2 ± 5.4 versus 48.5 ± 5; P = .38) and by 18 months (PF: 59.4 ± 4.7 versus 58.7 ± 4.9; P = .67; PI: 45.9 ± 5.1 versus 46.7 ± 4.8; P = .59) scores converged, with both groups surpassing MCID thresholds for meaningful clinical improvement (Table 1, Figure 3) All patients underwent MRI at 12 months, which demonstrated restoration of tendon continuity and preserved Achilles tendon integrity. On sagittal and axial PD and T1-weighted sequences, the Achilles tendon fibers appeared continuous and well aligned at the calcaneal insertion, without evidence of partial or full-thickness tearing. Fluid-sensitive sequences (T2-weighted and STIR) showed resolution of the previously noted intratendinous hyperintensity and retrocalcaneal bursal fluid, with no persistent edema or inflammatory signal within the distal tendon or surrounding soft tissues. Tendon thickness and morphology were comparable across all cases, and no abnormal signal suggesting recurrent tendinopathy was identified. Additionally, STIR sequences showed no evidence of bone marrow edema in the posterior calcaneus, supporting the resolution of reactive inflammatory changes. (Figure 2B).

All patients were physically active preoperatively (≥2 sports sessions per week, each lasting >60 minutes) (Table 2), and the bioinductive scaffold group returned to sport earlier (8 ± 1.7 months) than controls (9.7 ± 2.1 months; P = 002) (Figure 4). RTS was achieved in 14 of 15 patients (93.3%) in the bioinductive scaffold group and in 13 of 15 patients (86.7%) in the control group. Although a slightly higher proportion of patients in the scaffold cohort returned to their preoperative level of sport participation, this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.54). Complications were minimal: 1 deep vein thrombosis in each group and 1 transient sural neuropathy in each, all resolving without intervention. No Achilles ruptures or deep infections were diagnosed. A lower rate of superficial wound complications was observed in the scaffold augmentation group compared with controls (1 vs 3); however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .19) (Table 3).

Discussion

The key findings of this study demonstrate that bioinductive scaffold augmentation provides an early clinical advantage in the surgical management of insertional Achilles tendinopathy. At 6 months, PROMIS PF improved from 39.8 ± 5.7 to 54 ± 5.3 in the scaffold group compared with 40.4 ± 6.1 to 49.5 ± 6 in controls (P = .03). Similarly, PROMIS PI improved from 64.1 ± 6.4 to 50.6 ± 6.1 with scaffold augmentation versus 63.7 ± 5.9 to 55.3 ± 6.4 in the control group (P = .04). These between-group differences exceeded established MCID thresholds, indicating clinically early benefit. In addition, RTS occurred earlier in the scaffold group (8 ± 1.7 months) compared with controls (9.7 ± 2.1 months; P = .02), a finding of particular relevance in this physically active cohort. However, by 12 and 18 months, outcomes converged, with no statistically significant differences between groups (PROMIS PF at 18 months: 59.4 ± 4.7 vs 58.7 ± 4.9; P = .67; PROMIS PI at 18 months: 45.9 ± 5.1 vs 46.7 ± 4.8; P = .59), indicating comparable long-term results.

IAT, with or without an HD, remains a common and challenging source of posterior heel pain for foot and ankle surgeons. Although 3 to 6 months of conservative treatment is typically recommended before surgery, many patients ultimately require operative intervention. Traditional open debridement with Haglund resection and tendon reattachment has demonstrated reliable outcomes. Yet, prolonged recovery, wound-healing issues, and soft tissue complications continue to drive efforts toward improving postoperative recovery and tendon healing.1,2,3,4 Bioinductive collagen scaffolds have emerged as a biologically active adjunct aimed at enhancing tendon remodeling by stimulating cellular infiltration and promoting organized collagen deposition, thereby potentially accelerating the healing environment.5,6,19 Early reports in the Achilles literature, including those by Valk et al 20 and Ng et al 17 have demonstrated encouraging clinical and structural improvements when bioinductive scaffolds were used to augment repairs in acute Achilles tendon rupture. More recently, Genuth and Iselin 7 published the first series involving IAT surgery augmented with a bioinductive scaffold, reporting promising short-term outcomes, including early MRI evidence of tendon regeneration. 6 However, no previous study has directly compared standard open IAT surgery with and without scaffold augmentation. The present comparative cohort study is, to our knowledge, the first to evaluate clinical, functional, and radiologic outcomes between these 2 strategies.

MRI evaluation at 12 months demonstrated restored tendon integrity in all patients, with no structural differences between groups. The bioinductive scaffold was incorporated in all augmented cases, consistent with its intended biological mechanism. Importantly, the use of a scaffold did not increase complication rates. The complication rate was comparable between groups. Each cohort experienced 1 venous thromboembolism (P = .999) and 1 transient sural nerve neuropathy (P≥ .999), both of which resolved spontaneously. Importantly, no Achilles tendon ruptures and no deep infections were observed. There was a clinically meaningful, although not statistically significant, trend toward fewer superficial wound issues in the bioinductive group (1 vs 3; P = .19), supporting the hypothesis that scaffold augmentation does not increase and may modestly reduce soft-tissue irritation during early healing.

This study has several strengths, including a homogeneous surgical technique performed by a single surgeon, consistent postoperative protocols, and the use of validated PROMIS domains, which provide a more sensitive and patient-centered assessment of outcomes than traditional clinician-reported measures. Although statistically significant early postoperative differences were observed between groups, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, given the observational study design and predefined time-point comparisons. Nevertheless, limitations must be acknowledged. The sample size is modest, and while it is adequately powered to detect differences in PROMIS outcomes, larger multicenter studies are needed to confirm these findings. Although the follow-up duration extends to 18 months, it may not capture late structural failures or long-term differences in sport performance and durability. Additionally, while PROMIS offers advantages, discipline-specific tools such as the Victorian Institute of Sport Assessment-Achilles could complement future assessments.9,14,18 An additional limitation of this study is that the decision to use a bioinductive scaffold was made at the discretion of the operating surgeon rather than through randomization. This selective adoption reflects real-world clinical practice, in which emerging biologic technologies are often incorporated gradually and based on perceived patient suitability, intraoperative findings, and surgeon preference. While this approach enhances the external validity of the study, it introduces the potential for selection bias that may influence early differences in outcomes. Similar surgeon-directed treatment allocation has been described in observational studies evaluating biologic augmentation and new surgical implants, particularly during early adoption phases. 18 Future prospective randomized trials are needed to eliminate this source of bias and better determine which patient subgroups benefit most from scaffold augmentation. As minimally invasive techniques for insertional Achilles tendinopathy continue to evolve and gain popularity, 5 an important future direction will be determining how bioinductive scaffolds can be integrated into these less invasive approaches. The biological benefits demonstrated in our study suggest that scaffold augmentation may complement minimally invasive decompression or limited debridement procedures, potentially enhancing early tendon healing while preserving the advantages of smaller incisions. Future research should therefore explore technical adaptations, feasibility, and outcomes of combining bioinductive technology with emerging minimally invasive IAT techniques.

Conclusion

Our study shows that bioinductive scaffold augmentation in open IAT surgery is safe and may be associated with earlier improvements in pain, function, and RTS, while longer-term outcomes are similar to standard repair. These findings suggest a potential role for this approach in active individuals seeking a faster recovery.
