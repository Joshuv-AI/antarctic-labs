ABSTRACT
Power Spectrum Analysis (PSA) is a powerful technique for detecting subtle differences in electronic
components by analyzing frequency responses to dynamic stimuli. However, current PSA
implementations depend heavily on expert judgment to select stimulus conditions, limiting
standardization and comprehensive data interpretation. This report addresses the challenge of
optimizing stimulus selection and enhancing visualization of PSA data by introducing Power
Spectrum Imaging (PSI), a novel method that converts multiple PSA spectra acquired over a range
of frequencies and voltages into greyscale images. PSI enables systematic exploration of stimulus
conditions and facilitates direct comparison across devices. We demonstrate PSI’s effectiveness
through experiments on discrete Zener diodes, microcontrollers, and U.S. Government test parts,
revealing previously unrecognized spectral features and device-specific signatures. Our results show
that PSI provides richer, multidimensional insights than traditional single-frequency PSA, improving
detection sensitivity and characterization capabilities. This work establishes PSI as a valuable tool for
supply chain verification, counterfeit detection, and aging studies, offering a path toward
standardized, automated PSA workflows.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Power Spectrum Analysis (PSA) detects differences between components by measuring the
frequency response of power fluctuations in biased devices subjected to dynamic stimuli [1]. PSA
signatures associated with individual devices under test are found to be sensitive to subtle changes
between devices. This method has been effective in detecting subtle differences in electrical
components, yielding critical insights for applications in supply chain management, defect detection,
failure analysis, and aging studies [2]. Current implementation of PSA relies heavily on the
experience of Subject Matter Experts (SMEs) to determine the stimulus conditions to collect data.
Different analysis methods have been developed to extract information from PSA signatures [3].
The key to successful PSA implementation is to select and optimize the dynamic stimuli for the
spectral results obtained from the device under test. To readily acquire and analyze a wider range of
input conditions, a new method has been developed to generate an image of multiple spectra, Power
Spectrum Imaging (PSI). PSI displays a range of stimulus frequencies instead of a specific frequency.
The developed software generates a PSI with user defined pixel density from line scan spectra.
Parameters are customizable enabling applications for specific devices allowing for the selection of
an input voltage, frequency, resolution bandwidth, step size and frequency span. PSI is then used for
visualizing and analyzing multiple device frequency responses in a single image. The resulting PSI
data has some similarities to spectrograms used in acoustics [4].

1. INTRODUCTION
 1.1 Power Spectrum Analysis (PSA) Basics
 Power Spectrum Analysis (PSA) is a technique that detects differences between
 electronic components by measuring the frequency response of power fluctuations in
 biased devices subjected to dynamic stimuli. PSA has proven effective in identifying
 subtle variations in electrical components, providing critical insights for applications
 such as supply chain management, counterfeit detection, defect identification, failure
 analysis, and aging studies.
 1.2 Motivation and Purpose
 Current PSA implementations rely heavily on the expertise of Subject Matter Experts
 (SMEs) to select stimulus conditions and interpret results. The success of PSA
 depends on optimizing dynamic stimuli and data collection patterns that influence
 the device under test (DUT). To address these challenges, we developed Power
 Spectrum Imaging (PSI), a method that generates images from multiple PSA spectra,
 enabling comprehensive visualization and comparison of stimulus conditions.

2. SETUP AND APPROACH
 2.1 Distinguishing PSI from PSA
 PSI extends traditional PSA by allowing customizable parameters including input
 voltage, frequency, resolution bandwidth, step size, and frequency span. Unlike PSA,
 which typically uses a single stimulus frequency, PSI employs a range of input
 frequencies to generate a greyscale image from line scan spectra. This approach
 provides an enhanced data set for analysis and visualization.

 Our experiments were performed on both Zener Diodes and U.S. Government
 ASIC devices. But during the first few months of conducting experiments, it was
 determined this approach was not the best way to go about collecting data. Our
 results indicated no single stimulus was universally optimal. This motivated the use
 of multiple stimuli across a frequency range to collect a 2D grayscale image. Spectral
 data in a line scan in the x-axis and the equipment variation in the y-axis. Again,
 utilizing discrete Zener diodes and a range of stimulus frequencies, PSA spectra
 (normalized to a standard) were acquired at multiple frequencies spanning a range of
 stimulus frequency values (5kHz to 10MHz) and an input voltage of 0.95V with a
 square wave applied between VDD and VSS. The individual spectra were then
 displayed as intensity lines in an image. The resulting images produced both the
 differences in normalized PSA spectra between devices but also how those
 differences change with applied frequency. Figure 3a is an example of a single
 normalized line spectra collected on discrete devices and Figure 3b is the PSI taken.
 Note the bright to dark phase shifts along the ‘rays’ (lines from upper right to lower
 left corner in the image). These were not observed in earlier PSA results. Each
 discrete device under test is biased using the input voltage from the function

3.2 Microcontrollers
 Microcontroller devices of varying memory sizes were examined using a similar
 approach to the discrete Zener diodes. Each experiment applied a square wave
 between VDD and VSS with multiple frequencies spanning a range of stimulus frequency
 values (5kHz to 1MHz) and an input voltage of 2.5V, 3V, 4V and 5V. The images
 displayed differences as expected, but for one set of data the applied square wave was
 set to swing from 2.5V to 5V with an input frequency range of 5kHz to 1MHz. The
 selected voltage range did not completely turn off the device. In one device, the PSI

3.3 U.S. Government ASIC Devices
 Using the knowledge gained from the discrete diodes and the microcontrollers, we set
 up to run the third set of devices. These ASIC devices were supplied to us by the
 United States Government (USG) for test purposes. These USG devices are a
 complex 48 pin integrated circuit. The devices were configured using DC bias on the
 analog power and control pins. The core voltage was varied 0-1.5V and two external
 power supplies independent of the PSI setup supplied the other power inputs; analog
 (2.25V) and I/0 (3.3V). A total of 50 parts of multiple variations were analyzed. An
 applied square wave 5kHz to 100kHz and a spectral span of 5kHz to 1MHz were
 used. The resolution bandwidth chosen (4kHz) broadened the image rays, but a
 complete image was acquired in ~2 minutes for a 201 by 801-pixel PSI. A lower
 bandwidth would have produced finer lines, but the data acquisition time increases
 dramatically. The current research goal was to get information in a reasonable amount
 of time. As the images were generated, we noticed differences in intensities along the
 rays of normalized images. The intensities down the rays were used for device
 differentiation. From all 50 devices a grayscale image was generated then subtracted
 from a device selected for reference. Intensity graphs plotted for both an odd and
 even harmonic rays. Since odd/even harmonics had periodicity, we chose one odd
 and one even nearest the image diagonal. These were rays 9 and 10 counting from the
 left. Fig. 5a is a raw PSI grayscale image collected from a single USG device. Fig. 5b is
 a grayscale image created after subtracting from a reference. Fig. 5c indicates location
 of rays 9 and 10 whose intensities were acquired and compared.

4. RESULTS
 4.1 Key Findings
 • Application of Power Spectrum Imaging (PSI):
 PSI was applied to discrete Zener diodes, microcontrollers, and U.S. Government
 ASIC devices to evaluate its effectiveness in detecting subtle variations among
 electronic components.
 • Discrete Zener Diodes:
 o A total of 42 Zener diodes from two manufacturers were tested.
 o PSI generated two-dimensional grayscale images from spectral data collected
 over a frequency range of 5 kHz to 10 MHz
 o The images revealed frequency-dependent phase shifts and intensity
 variations that were not observed using conventional single-frequency Power
 Spectrum Analysis (PSA).
 • Microcontrollers:
 o Devices with varying memory sizes (16 kB, 32 kB, and 64 kB) were
 examined.
 o PSI successfully differentiated between memory sizes based on spectral
 signatures.
 o A unique resonant-like spectral feature was identified in one microcontroller
 under specific voltage and frequency stimulus conditions.
 • U.S. Government ASIC Devices:
 o Fifty ASIC devices were analyzed using PSI with a spectral span of 5 kHz to
 1 MHz and a resolution bandwidth of 4 kHz.
 o Spectral images were acquired rapidly, with each image generated in
 approximately two minutes.
 o Intensity variations along harmonic spectral rays enabled differentiation of 48
 stable devices; two devices were excluded due to instability.
 o Odd harmonic rays provided greater differentiation capability compared to
 even harmonics.
 o Attempts to classify devices into six predefined groups based on PSI data did
 not yield consistent groupings.
 4.2 Future Research Directions
 • Development of more sophisticated electrical stimuli combining varying voltage and
 frequency to create hyperspectral PSI images.
 • Construction of three-dimensional data cubes representing PSI images across voltage
 and frequency planes.
 • Advanced image and data extraction techniques to enhance feature detection and
 classification.

CONCLUSION
This work introduces Power Spectrum Imaging (PSI) as an advancement over traditional Power
Spectrum Analysis (PSA) by enabling multi-frequency stimulus visualization through greyscale
imaging. PSI enhances the ability to detect subtle differences and anomalies in electronic
components, supporting applications in supply chain verification, counterfeit detection, and aging
studies. The method’s flexibility and rich data output promise significant improvements in
component characterization and verification workflows.
The implementation of Power Spectrum Imaging (PSI) was demonstrated on multiple devices.
Experiments conducted on discrete Zener diodes, microcontrollers, and U.S. Government ASIC
devices confirmed that PSI’s multi-frequency spectral imaging approach captures nuanced
frequency-dependent behaviors that are not discernible through single-frequency PSA. The
generation of two-dimensional grayscale images from line scan spectra enabled rapid visualization
and differentiation of device-specific spectral signatures across a broad frequency range, with
acquisition times on the order of minutes. Notably, PSI revealed unique resonant-like features in
microcontrollers under specific stimulus conditions and provided repeatable differentiation among
48 of 50 tested ASIC devices based on intensity variations along harmonic spectral rays. While
classification of ASIC devices into predefined groups was inconclusive, the overall results validate
PSI as a robust, customizable technique for enhanced device characterization. These findings
support PSI’s potential to improve supply chain assurance, defect detection, and failure analysis
workflows at Sandia, offering a scalable method for comprehensive spectral assessment of complex
electronic components.
