Introduction: Emergence of a New Mode of Quantitative Extreme Materials Mechanics

Over the past few years, the laser-induced particle impact test (LIPIT) has emerged as a new
quantitative tool for extreme-conditions materials mechanics. The principle of the approach is
shown in Fig. 1a, where a short green laser burst is used to ablate a target, and that ablation in turn
is used to launch a particle (typically 5-50 μm) at high velocities. Depending on the power of the
laser and the properties of the particle, the velocities attained can be as low as ~10 m/s and as high
as ~1500 m/s. Importantly, the LIPIT also involves a second laser used for imaging of the particle
in flight and during its collision with a substrate target.

The ability to record microparticle impacts at high velocity has produced some remarkable
observational science giving insights on a variety of phenomena. These include: crater formation
caused by plasticity [1–6] or impact melting [1,7–10], fracture and puncturing events on more
friable materials [11–14], hydrodynamic instabilities or ‘jetting’ [3,5,6,8,9,15,15–19], and
viscoplastic distortion of polymers at high rates [12,13,20–27].

2. Progress Report

 1

This section reviews the highlights of DOE BES (Grant No. DE-SC0018091). That prior DOE
BES project was focused on the role of temperature effects in extreme materials mechanics using
LIPIT. Temperature is a critical variable in determining active deformation mechanisms, and
plastic strain produces heat as well; in short, temperature is a dimension of inquiry essential to a
full understanding of extreme materials mechanics. Two of the main thrusts of this work are
reviewed below, as they not only break new ground in methodology and understanding, but also
point to gaps in understanding that motivate future work.

2.1 Dynamic Recrystallization in Extreme Conditions

At lower rates and/or at elevated temperatures, metals deform primarily through dislocation
processes. As the strains increase and the dislocation densities rise, there are additional processes
of structure evolution that lead to dynamic recrystallization. Fig. 2 shows a map of deformation
and recrystallization mechanisms as they are often encountered in FCC metals, with the y-axis
representing the Zener-Holloman parameter to combine rate and temperature together. In this
figure, the upper red region is the zone in which dislocation behavior accumulates and
conventional mechanisms of dynamic recrystallization occur at higher strains.

In this map, LIPIT occupies a very unique position: it can achieve strains that range from low to
high (across the full map), at low temperatures and at high rates that place it in the lower (blue)
regions of the map. In this range of rates, many metals transition from dislocation-based plasticity
to twinning, but at even higher strains the development of recrystallization remains mysterious
under such extreme conditions. Our work systematically probed across this range: using LIPIT
experiments on copper, we explored impacts of increasing velocity to span the range shown by the
data points in Fig. 2. Individual impacts were then cross-sectioned by FIB and subjected to
scanning and scanning transmission electron microscopy to study the evolution of deformation
and recrystallization mechanisms. Importantly, as strain rises in these experiments, so too does the
adiabatic temperature rise that prevails in the contact zone. We calibrated the temperature of the
experiments with finite element modeling, ultimately allowing us to design experiments that
crossed the blue regions of this map as well as penetrating into the more conventional “red” regions
at the top, owing to temperature rise at the impact site.

These studies successfully tracked the transition from dislocation plasticity to profuse nanoscale
twinning (“nanotwinning”) at higher strains. Beyond that, nanotwinning gave rise to a unique
mechanism of recrystallization that these experiments captured in detail for the first time:
nanotwinning-assisted dynamic recrystallization (ntDRX), which is schematized in Fig. 2b. The
adjacent TEM micrographs capture the impacted particle (Fig. 2c) and a close view near the
interface (Fig. 2d) at a critical moment where nanotwins fragment into fine grains at the impact
site. At even higher strains, the temperature rise associated with the impact moves upward out of
the blue regions of the map, and into a range where “conventional” DRX mechanisms occurred;
these also were confirmed with detailed microscopy. Our publications on these results in Refs.
[16] and [36] provided what we think is the first complete survey of dynamic recrystallization
mechanisms at the highest strain rates.

2.2 Instrument Development for High Temperature LIPIT Studies

At the outset of our project, LIPIT had exclusively been used as a room temperature methodology.
We had therefore identified a large opportunity to develop new approaches to LIPIT to achieve
test conditions under controlled elevated temperatures. This required innovation on both halves
(launch and impact) of the method:

 • We developed the first LIPIT hot stage apparatus in which the substrate target is mounted
 to a heated stage, properly insulated from the surroundings so as to create a hot zone for
 the test, without causing thermal distortions or errors in the optics and electronics. This
 permitted the first non-ambient experiments on aluminum substrates, at temperatures up to
 100̊ C as a first step [37]. While the launch side of the apparatus is not heated, it is important
 to note that the gap between launchpad and target is typically well below 1 mm; it is a
 narrow gap between two large parallel plate structures. Heat transfer analysis suggests a
 uniform temperature for the particle and substrate (and surrounding vapor).
 • The success of the heating stage that we developed revealed a weakness on the launch side
 of the apparatus. Because the standard LIPIT launchpad used widely for the past ~10 years
