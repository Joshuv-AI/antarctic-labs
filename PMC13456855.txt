Acute lower gastrointestinal bleeding from Meckel’s diverticulum in a young adult: a case report on surgical management

Introduction

Meckel’s diverticulum (MD), first described by J. F. Meckel in 1809, results from the incomplete obliteration of the vitelline duct during embryogenesis[1]. MD represents the most common congenital anomaly of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, accounting for 90% of all vitelline duct anomalies[2,3]. Its prevalence in the general population ranges from 0.3% to 2.9%, with a higher incidence reported in males[2,4]. Although most MDs remain asymptomatic throughout life, complications may include GI bleeding, inflammation (diverticulitis), intestinal obstruction, and, rarely, perforation or neoplasm[5]. Bleeding is the most common presentation in children and is typically due to ulceration from ectopic gastric mucosa[6]. In adults, however, obstruction and diverticulitis tend to predominate, and bleeding is rare[7]. The variability in presentation makes preoperative diagnosis challenging; MD is still frequently diagnosed intraoperatively despite the availability of advanced imaging[8].

Here, we report the case of a young adult who presented with acute-onset hematochezia and abdominal pain at a tertiary care center. This case contributes to the limited adult literature on MD-related bleeding, highlighting the diagnostic challenges and the mechanism of bleeding in the absence of frank ulceration. This case report is presented in line with the SCARE checklist[9].

Case presentation

A 20-year-old previously healthy man presented to the emergency department after 2 days of hematochezia. He denied fever, diarrhea, constipation, or urinary symptoms. He had no regular medications, including no nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug or anticoagulants, and no relevant surgical, family, or social history. Physical examination revealed normal bowel sounds and a tender lower abdomen without guarding or rebound. Murphy’s, McBurney’s, and psoas signs were negative. Initial laboratory tests showed a hemoglobin level of 138 g/L (normal: 140–180) and a normal coagulation profile (PT 81%, INR 1.1, aPTT 26 s). A digital rectal examination was performed, and the patient was discharged with outpatient follow-up. Two days later, he returned to the emergency department with persistent symptoms and worsening lower abdominal pain. Laboratory tests showed a drop in hemoglobin to 97 g/L, prompting hospital admission. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed a blind-ended diverticular structure in the right iliac fossa measuring 7 × 3 cm, consistent with MD, showing wall thickening and hyperemia without active bleeding, diverticulitis, or pneumoperitoneum. On the following day, an esophagogastroduodenoscopy revealed no abnormalities or upper GI sources of bleeding. During colonoscopy, the terminal ileum appeared normal aside from residual hematin, and no diverticulum or clear source of bleeding was identified. At this point, the main differential diagnoses considered included Meckel’s diverticulum, angiodysplasia, Crohn’s disease, and an infectious or ischemic colitis. The absence of mucosal inflammatory changes on colonoscopy, the normal terminal ileum, the patient’s age, and the CT appearance of a blind-ending ileal structure collectively made MD the most likely diagnosis.

Later that day, hemoglobin dropped further to 72 g/L, prompting transfusion of two units of packed red blood cells. The patient exhibited hemodynamic instability with a blood pressure nadir of 95/47 mmHg and two episodes of syncope. Resuscitation was initiated with 500 mL of Ringer’s lactate as a rapid infusion. Given persistent hypotension and only a modest hemoglobin increase to 79 g/L following the first two units, an additional two units of packed red blood cells were administered the same day. Hemodynamic stability was achieved on the surgical ward without vasopressor support or intermediate care admission.

The patient was subsequently taken to the operating room for exploratory laparoscopy. Antibiotic prophylaxis consisted of intravenous cefuroxime. The procedure was performed by a board-certified general surgeon with 15 years of experience at a tertiary care hospital. No free fluid or signs of peritonitis were present. The small bowel was examined, and at approximately 140 cm proximal to the ileocecal valve, an MD was confirmed. It showed signs of inflammation and adhesions to the mesenteric side. After adhesiolysis, the affected segment was mobilized and exteriorized through a Pfannenstiel incision. A 10-cm segment of ileum, including the diverticulum, was resected using a stapler. A hand-sewn isoperistaltic side-to-side anastomosis was performed in two layers. Thromboprophylaxis with enoxaparin was initiated 6 hours postoperatively.

The resected specimen measured 7.5 × 2 cm (Fig. 1). Upon opening, an intraluminal hematoma was evacuated, and a mucosal transition was observed 5 cm from the ileal junction (Fig. 2). Histopathology confirmed a transition from intestinal to gastric fundic-type mucosa, with no ulcerations (Fig. 3). The patient developed a paralytic ileus on postoperative day 2 and was managed with nasogastric tube decompression until the passage of stool on postoperative day 5, representing a Clavien–Dindo grade II complication. He was discharged on postoperative day 6. The recovery was otherwise uneventful. At the 4-week follow-up, the patient was asymptomatic and resumed normal bowel habits.

Discussion

Embryologically, MD may contain heterotopic tissue due to the pluripotency of the involved cells. Ectopic gastric mucosa is the most frequent type, present in up to 50% of symptomatic cases, and is the primary cause of bleeding. Notably, the absence of a macroscopic ulcer on histopathology does not exclude ectopic gastric mucosa as the bleeding source. Intermittent acid exposure from ectopic mucosa may cause micro-ulcerations or erosions in the adjacent ileal epithelium that bleed acutely but show minimal residual changes, a mechanism consistent with the absence of ulceration on histopathological examination and the permanent resolution of bleeding following resection in this case[10]. Other heterotopic tissues include pancreatic (5%) and, rarely, hepatobiliary, endometrial, or colonic tissue. In children, bleeding is the most common symptom, while in adults, obstruction, diverticulitis, and perforation predominate, the latter frequently mimicking acute appendicitis[11,12].

Bleeding due to MD in adults is relatively uncommon and may present insidiously or as an acute hemorrhage. Hematochezia most commonly originates from a lower GI source, though upper GI bleeding should always be excluded. Upper GI causes include peptic ulcer disease, esophageal varices, Mallory–Weiss tears, and erosive gastritis, typically presenting with melena. Lower GI causes are more varied and include hemorrhoids, colorectal polyps and malignancy, angiodysplasia, infectious or ischemic colitis, inflammatory bowel disease, diverticular disease, and small bowel sources such as MD. In young patients without known colorectal disease, small bowel etiologies, including MD and Crohn’s disease, deserve particular consideration when standard upper and lower endoscopy fails to identify a source[13].

Diagnosis is challenging, standard endoscopy often fails to identify the source, and only 5.7% of MD cases are diagnosed pre-operatively[8]. While technetium-99m pertechnetate scans are highly sensitive in children, their utility in adults is significantly lower[13]. CT can suggest MD when a blind-ending structure is seen in continuity with the distal ileum[14]. In this case, CT findings were sufficiently suggestive to proceed directly to surgical exploration, and hemodynamic instability precluded further diagnostic workup.

Management of symptomatic MD is surgical. The choice between simple diverticulectomy and segmental small bowel resection depends on (1) the integrity of the diverticulum base and (2) the presence and location of ectopic tissue[15]. The diverticulum’s height-to-diameter ratio provides guidance. Long diverticula (height-to-diameter ratio > 2) typically harbor ectopic mucosa at the body or tip, whereas shorter diverticula are more likely to contain ectopic tissue near the base[16]. When bleeding occurs, segmental or wedge resection is recommended to ensure complete removal of ectopic mucosa, particularly in short diverticula[15]. In contrast, diverticulectomy may be adequate for long diverticula when the base appears unaffected.

The optimal management of incidentally discovered MD remains debated. Early studies discouraged resection, citing a low lifetime risk of complications (4.2%) and higher surgical morbidity in asymptomatic patients[17]. A large study from the Mayo Clinic proposed that resection of incidentally discovered MD should be considered when certain risk factors are present and predict a symptomatic MD, namely: (1) age under 50, (2) male sex, (3) diverticulum length greater than 2 cm, and (4) the presence of ectopic or abnormal tissue. The authors thus recommended resection when one criterion was met and reported the proportion of symptomatic diverticula to be 17%, 25%, 42%, and 70% when 1, 2, 3, or all 4 criteria are met, respectively[18].

Although rare, the risk of malignancy in MD is an important concern. A SEER database analysis found the adjusted cancer risk in MD to be 70 times higher than elsewhere in the ileum, with most cancers being neuroendocrine tumors (NETs)[19]. In a 2018 case series, neoplasia was identified in 4.5% of MD, all of which were NETs [20] Van Malderen et al. similarly found malignancy in 5.1% of 373 surgically resected MD, based on a 14-year retrospective pathology review[21]. NETs accounted for the majority (63.2%), followed by GI stromal tumors (10.5%), adenocarcinoma (5.3%), and pancreatic epithelial neoplasia (5.3%). Metastatic disease was present at diagnosis in 33.3% of patients. Notably, 17 of the 19 malignancies were incidental findings[20]. These findings highlight a non-negligible risk of malignancy in incidentally discovered MD.

Conclusion

MD should be included in the differential diagnosis of unexplained lower GI bleeding in adults, even when standard endoscopy is unremarkable. CT imaging plays a key role in guiding surgical decision-making. Segmental resection is the procedure of choice when ectopic mucosa is suspected. For incidentally discovered MD, prophylactic resection should be considered in selected patients with multiple risk factors, weighed against their surgical risk.
