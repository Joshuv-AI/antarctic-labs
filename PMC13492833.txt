Evaluation of educational environment at different academic levels using the Dundee Ready Educational Environment Measure
Authors: Mehran Falahchai, Elham Jafari, Yasamin Babaee Hemmati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:The role of the educational environment in learning and performance of students is undeniable, particularly in clinical fields. This study aimed to assess the perception of dental students at the basic science, preclinical, and clinical levels toward the educational environment of Guilan Dental School by using the Dundee Ready Educational Environment Measure (DREEM).MATERIALS AND METHODS:This cross-sectional study was conducted on 1st-year–6th-year dental students in the 2025 year. Data were collected using the standard DREEM, with 50 close-ended questions with 5-point Likert-scale answer choices. Perception of students was evaluated in five domains: student perception of learning (SPL), student perception of teacher (SPT), student academic self-perception (SASP), student perception of the atmosphere (SPA), and student social self-perception (SSSP). Data were analyzed at 0.05 level of significance.RESULTS:Totally, 168 dental students participated in this study, out of which 92.9% were aged 18–25 years and 57.1% were males. The mean educational environment quality score was 101.77 ± 17.43. “Moving in the right direction” in the SPT domain had the highest frequency. The perceived quality of educational environment and its domains had no significant association with age or gender of students (P < 0.05). The mean score of the SASP domain significantly decreased with an increase in academic level of students (r = −0.18, P < 0.05).CONCLUSIONS:Dental students rated some domains of their educational environment positively; however, areas such as SSSP require improvement to align with the supportive academic environments emphasized in health education policies.

Introduction

Learning environment has a definition beyond only the physical classroom environment and encompasses all educational experiences of the learners.[1] This environment includes not only the physical space but also the social, cultural, and psychological components, the dominant culture, student interactions, instruction methods, and even the management structures that affect the educational environment.[2] Learning depends on several factors; however, interactions and involvement in the environment may be the most important parameters in this respect. Professional development of individuals in basic sciences highly depends on the educational environment and student interactions with this environment.[3]

Currently, educational environment has a critical role in high-quality medical education as it affects the students’ satisfaction with their educational course, well-being, and educational achievements.[45] Investment in high-quality education in medical universities is a fundamental step toward public health promotion, which is implemented through training of expert healthcare workers. Continuous feedback and fixing the defects play a vital role in the educational environment quality promotion.[36] A deep understanding of this environment and the influential factors is imperative for strategy planning and efficient management of medical educational programs.[7] As an active element in this environment, students express their perception and understanding of this environment through their behavior and performance. Therefore, precise assessment of the educational environment of universities, with special emphasis on the perception and experiences of students, plays a key role in educational quality promotion and achieving the educational system goals.[89]

A positive association has been reported between the learning environment and quality of patient care.[10] On the contrary, an unsuitable learning environment is associated with higher levels of stress, depression, and occupational burnout among medical interns.[10] The results of studies on dental education have been comparable with those on medical education, highlighting the significance of the quality of educational environment in the development of professional identity of dental students, their educational progress, and their psychosocial well-being.[4911121314]

The significant role of a positive educational environment in optimal quality of education, especially medical education, led to the development of tools for assessment of the quality of educational environments.[15] Student-centered questionnaires are widely recognized as effective tools for evaluation of educational environments and identifying areas for improvement. Their implementation can guide improvements in various dimensions of the learning environment, particularly in clinical settings.[416] Several questionnaires have been used for evaluation of clinical educational environments, including the Medical Education Environment Measure, Postgraduate Hospital Educational Environment Measure, Surgical Theatre Educational Environment Measure, Anesthetic Theatre Educational Environment Measure, and Dundee Ready Educational Environment Measure (DREEM), which are mostly suitable for evaluation of medical education.[14] The DREEM questionnaire, designed in 1997, is a well-known and reliable tool for the evaluation of educational environments, particularly medical education.[15] This questionnaire has questions in five domains: student perception of learning (SPL), student perception of teacher (SPT), student academic self-perception (SASP), student perception of the atmosphere (SPA), and student social self-perception (SSSP).[17] Each domain has questions that assess different aspects of an educational environment and are scored according to a 5-point Likert scale.[13]

The DREEM is a general international tool that is not specific for a certain culture. Interpretation of the results of this questionnaire can help in strategy planning for the improvement of educational curricula and enable the medical schools to assess their educational environment.[416] The DREEM has been used in a high number of dental schools worldwide.[111416181920] Therefore, it appears suitable for assessment of dental schools. In addition, its Farsi version has shown optimal validity and reliability.[3] Studies conducted in Iran evaluated the quality of educational environments of Mashhad, Shahed, and Rafsanjan Dental Schools by using the DREEM. The results showed optimal educational environment quality of Shahed Dental School, while the educational environment of Mashhad and Rafsanjan Dental Schools was far from optimal.[212223]

To date, only a limited number of studies worldwide have assessed students’ perceptions of the educational environment using the DREEM across all three phases of dental education—basic science, preclinical, and clinical—in a structured, comparative manner. Most published studies have focused either on a single stage of dental training or have compared only two levels, such as preclinical versus clinical years.[1124] This study is among the few that provide a comprehensive, level-wise evaluation of the educational environment throughout the full trajectory of dental education. Moreover, as students’ perceptions are influenced by sociocultural and institutional contexts, such evaluations must be contextualized within each unique educational setting.[918] In this regard, no previous study in Iran has conducted a full-phase comparative analysis using the DREEM to evaluate perceptions across all academic levels in a dental school. The results of the present study offer both internationally comparable data and locally relevant insights for improving educational environments and informing the curriculum developers in Iranian dental education. Therefore, this study was undertaken to assess the quality of the educational environment at Guilan Dental School by using the DREEM questionnaire, with a focus on capturing student perceptions across the basic science, preclinical, and clinical levels.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted in Guilan Dental School in 2025.

Study participants and sampling

The minimum sample size was calculated to be 139 by using the sample size calculation formula and the mean estimation, assuming the standard deviation of educational environment quality score to be 31.5 according to a previous study,[18] alpha = 0.05, study power of 0.80, and d = 7.5. All 1st-year–6th-year dental students of Guilan Dental School (basic science, preclinical, and clinical academic levels) who willingly filled out the questionnaire completely after signing informed consent forms were enrolled. Dental students who filled out the questionnaire incompletely were excluded. The students were selected by stratified sampling, such that each of the basic science, preclinical, and clinical levels was considered as one stratum. According to the total sample size and number of students in each stratum, the share of each stratum from the total sample size was determined. Next, a list of students in each stratum was used for systematic sampling of students from each stratum. After informing the students about the study objectives and data processing specifications, the paper print of the questionnaire was distributed among the students of each stratum simultaneously. The students were assured about the confidentiality of their information.

Data collection tool and technique

The questionnaire had two sections: a demographic section and the DREEM. The demographic section asked for the age group, university admission year, and gender of the students. The DREEM standard questionnaire comprised 50 close-ended questions to assess the perception and expectations of students from the educational environment in five domains of SPL, SPT, SASP, SPA, and SSSP. The DREEM questionnaire has been previously utilized in a study conducted in Iran, where its reliability and validity were confirmed. The overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the questionnaire was reported as 0.88, with subscale values of 0.75 for learning, 0.72 for perception of teachers, 0.71 for academic self-perception, 0.73 for atmosphere, and 0.71 for social self-perception.[18]

The questions were scored using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = totally disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = no opinion, 4 = agree, and 5 = totally agree). Filling out the questionnaire required approximately 10 minutes, and it was then collected.

The SPL domain had 12 questions with a maximum achievable score of 48. Scores 0–12 indicated very weak learning, 13–24 indicated relatively weak learning, 25–36 indicated relatively positive learning, and 37–48 indicated very positive learning.

The SPT domain had 11 questions with a maximum score of 44, with scores 0–11 indicating bad, 12–22 indicating in need of revision, 23–33 indicating moving in the right direction, and 34–44 indicating model teachers.

The SASP domain had eight questions with a maximum achievable score of 32, which was interpreted as follows: scores 0–8: total failure, 9–16: many negative aspects, 17–24: feeling more on the positive side, and 25–32: confident.

The SPA domain had 12 questions with a maximum achievable score of 48, which was interpreted as follows: 0–12: terrible environment, 13–24: huge modifications are required, 25–36: a more positive atmosphere, and 37–48: a very positive atmosphere.

The SSSP domain included 7 questions with a maximum achievable score of 28. Scores 0–7 indicated miserable, 8–14: not a nice place, 15–21: not a bad place, and 22–28: very good.

The maximum total score of the questionnaire was 200. Total scores of 0–50 indicated very poor, 51–100 indicated many problems, 101–150 indicated more positive rather than negative, and 151–200 indicated a good environment.

Considering the different number of questions in each domain, and for the purpose of correct comparison of the mean domain scores with each other, the mean domain scores were reported as relative frequency (percentage) to enable precise comparisons. To do so, the mean score of each domain was divided by the maximum achievable score of the respective domain to obtain a comparable percentage. Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guilan University of Medical Sciences (IR.GUMS.REC.1403.473).

Statistical analysis method

The data were reported as mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage. Normal distribution of data was evaluated by the Shapiro-Wilk test and skewness and kurtosis. Homogeneity of the variances was analyzed by Levene’s test. To examine the simultaneous effects of age, academic year, and gender on the total DREEM score and on each DREEM subscale (SPL, SPT, SASP, SPA, and SSSP), separate multivariable linear regression models were applied. For each model, unstandardized coefficients (B), standard errors (SE), standardized coefficients (β), t-statistics, and two-tailed P values were reported. Model assumptions—normality of residuals, linearity, and absence of multicollinearity—were verified before interpretation. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 28 at 0.05 level of significance.

Discussion

The educational environment of Guilan Dental School, evaluated via the DREEM questionnaire, was categorized as “more positive than negative,” with notable strengths in SPT and SPA domains, but clear weaknesses in SPL and especially SSSP. This pattern places Guilan within the moderate quality range, similar to other regional studies, yet trailing more advanced educational systems in specific domains.

Regionally, comparable DREEM profiles have been reported. In Syria, Alfakhry et al.[11] found an overall score slightly above 108, matching Guilan’s interpretive category. Both studies identified SSSP as the weakest domain, suggesting shared regional challenges regarding social support and peer interaction. Similarly, Aliabad et al.[22] at Rafsanjan reported a total score in the “rather positive than negative” range, with weaknesses in SASP and SPL paralleling those observed in Guilan. Eslami et al.[23] in Mashhad echoed these findings, noting concerns about teaching effectiveness and academic self-confidence, particularly during transitions into clinical training. Collectively, these Iranian studies highlight systemic weaknesses in active learning strategies, clinical integration, and psychosocial support.

In contrast, studies from Sri Lanka, South Korea, and Saudi Arabia reported more favorable environments. Ellawala et al.[25] in Sri Lanka documented a total DREEM score of 124.4, higher than Guilan’s, although SSSP remained the weakest domain. Unlike Guilan, Sri Lankan students perceived SPL more positively, likely reflecting better instructional design following curricular reforms. Gil et al.[18] in South Korea reported one of the highest overall performances, with all domain scores exceeding 50% of the maximum, and no domain classified as weak. While SPL and SSSP were relatively lower compared to other domains, they remained within acceptable ranges, reflecting strong curricular integration and student-centered educational models.

Similarly, studies from Saudi Arabia revealed a more positive trend. At Mustaqbal University, Arora et al.[16] found overall DREEM scores around 130–131, with strong performances in SPL and SASP, despite relative weaknesses in SSSP. Similarly, Aldowsari et al.[20] at Jazan University reported SPT as the strongest domain, suggesting the critical role of faculty engagement. Both institutions demonstrated higher satisfaction rates than Guilan, emphasizing the benefits of effective teaching practices and supportive academic climates. Higher-income settings further illustrated this contrast. Pritchard et al.[4] in Australia and New Zealand reported a mean DREEM score of 141, reflecting strong institutional support, structured mentoring, and integrated learning approaches. No domain was considered weak, although mild declines in emotional well-being and academic self-confidence were noted among senior students. In Hungary, Dávidovics et al.[19] reported “rather positive” overall DREEM scores, with international students rating SPT and SPA slightly lower than local students, possibly due to cultural differences, but without any domain falling into the weak category.

Regarding demographic influences, the present study found no significant association between gender or age with the DREEM scores. This finding aligns with most contemporary studies.[1118192223] These studies consistently reported no significant gender differences, suggesting a trend toward parity in academic environments. However, an exception was noted in a study by Sabbagh et al.[24] in Saudi Arabia, where female students scored significantly higher, particularly in SPL and SSSP domains, possibly reflecting cultural and motivational factors.

Academic seniority, however, revealed a consistent influence across multiple contexts. In the present study, higher academic years were associated with lower SASP scores. Gil et al.[18] in Korea and Aliabad et al.[22] in Rafsanjan similarly reported lower DREEM scores among more senior students, indicating the impact of increasing clinical workload and stress. Eslami et al.[23] in Mashhad found that clinical students had lower SPL and SASP scores than preclinical peers. In contrast, Ellawala et al.[25] in Sri Lanka observed improved perceptions among final-year students, attributed to curricular reforms. Similarly, Pritchard et al.[4] reported that third-year students achieved the highest DREEM scores, highlighting how supportive curriculum structures can counteract academic fatigue.

In summary, while Guilan Dental School’s educational environment aligns with many middle-income dental schools in presenting moderate strengths and persistent weaknesses, particularly in psychosocial domains, evidence suggests that structural reforms, student-centered learning, and enhanced mentoring could significantly improve overall student perceptions and outcomes.

Based on the findings of this study, several policy-relevant implications emerge for dental education stakeholders. While the overall educational environment of Guilan Dental School was perceived as acceptable, the persistent challenges in social self-perception and the decline in academic self-confidence among senior students call for structured reforms. Academic administrators are encouraged to implement phase-specific mentoring and wellness programs, enhance peer interaction platforms, and provide continuous pedagogical training for clinical instructors. Furthermore, routine evaluation of the educational environment by using validated instruments such as DREEM can guide data-driven quality improvements and should be integrated into national dental education policies.

Limitation and recommendation

Limitations of the present study such as a relatively small sample size may affect the generalizability of the findings to the entire population of dental students. In addition, the single-center design of this study affects direct generalizability of the findings to all dental schools. Although the DREEM is a well-known instrument for the evaluation of educational environments, it may not cover all the intended aspects. Furthermore, some confounding factors such as the financial status of students and their previous learning experiences might have affected the results, which were not controlled for. Therefore, future studies are recommended with greater control over the role of confounders. In addition, the association of precise age and grade point average of students with the perceived quality of educational environment should be investigated in future studies. Future multicenter studies on other dental schools with a larger sample size would enable a more precise comparison of the quality of educational environments at the national level. Nonetheless, the use of a validated instrument (DREEM), a high response rate (90.3%), and inclusion of students from all academic years enhanced the internal validity and relevance of the findings.

Conclusion

The present study revealed that dental students of Guilan Dental School were relatively satisfied with the overall educational environment, particularly in their perception of teaching (SPT). However, low scores in the SSSP domain and the declining academic self-perception (SASP) with advancement in academic level highlight areas of concern. These findings, in alignment with international evidence, underscore the importance of designing supportive academic policies aimed at enhancing students’ well-being, self-efficacy, and preparedness for clinical practice. Addressing these gaps is essential not only for improving dental education outcomes but also for aligning with broader health policy benchmarks that emphasize learner-centered environments, reduced academic stress, and better-trained future healthcare professionals.
