Optimal Decoding for Measurement-Based GHZ State Preparation: The Maximum-Utility Decoder
Misha Yutushui, Theo Haas, Simon Trebst

Abstract
The meticulous preparation of macroscopic Greenberger-Horne-Zeilinger (GHZ) states provides a foundational resource for quantum technologies such as metrology, cryptography, and fault-tolerant codes. While state-of-the-art measurement-based protocols offer efficient low-depth execution, their performance can be bottlenecked by conventional decoders, such as minimum weight perfect matching (MWPM) or even maximum-likelihood decoding (MLD), which optimize for binary logical recovery and fail to maximize the continuous long-range order characteristic of a GHZ state for two-dimensional geometries. Here we overcome this limitation by framing the decoding problem as minimum Bayesian risk inference, introducing a general paradigm that maximizes the expected utility of the decoded state. Implementing this maximum-utility approach, we construct an algorithm that achieves the highest possible per-shot decoded quantum order and thereby establish an optimal decoding strategy for measurement-based GHZ state preparation. To improve its computational efficiency, we design a scalable two-stage decoder, which first encodes the syndromes into the edge weights of MWPM and then refines the result with a convolutional neural network trained to maximize the expected utility, at a fraction of the cost of the optimal decoder. Remarkably, we find that the first stage alone—which makes the matching aware of the gauge choice at no cost beyond bare MWPM—already performs near-optimally up to the largest sizes we study, N = 256 × 256 N=256\times 256 , closing up to 87 % 87\% of the gap between the bare-MWPM and optimal decoding thresholds. Generalizing MWPM and MLD, the maximum-utility decoder (MUD) establishes a versatile framework that can be explicitly tailored to the operational demands of specific experiments by redefining the utility function.

The objective of decoding a GHZ state fundamentally differs from decoding standard topological codes
[
14
,
16
,
13
]
. For the toric code, the precise microscopic path of an applied correction is unimportant, provided it achieves the correct logical operation. By contrast, when preparing a GHZ state, local deviations directly impact the physical long-range order. Any discrepancy between the true error and the applied correction leaves behind an uncorrected domain that reduces macroscopic correlations; see Fig.
1
.

Minimum weight perfect matching (MWPM)
[
28
,
23
]
is a natural heuristic for this task, producing the shortest, most likely error configuration. However, MWPM is fundamentally suboptimal because it cannot differentiate between highly degenerate shortest paths that yield different amounts of long-range order, see Fig.
1
. In more technical terms, MWPM infers the correction solely from
gauge-invariant
data—the endpoints of the syndrome chains. This is justified only when the decoding problem maps to a Nishimori line, where the system indeed exhibits a local
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge symmetry
[
40
]
. Away from the Nishimori line, the gauge symmetry is broken, and the precise configuration of syndromes becomes actionable information ignored by the MWPM decoder, making it suboptimal.

To overcome this limitation, we introduce a decoding paradigm where success is measured by a continuous
utility function
and
which guides the implementation of an optimal decoder for a specific task.
Rooted in minimum Bayesian risk inference
[
2
]
, the decoder maximizing the expected utility—maximum-utility decoder (MUD)—presents a generalized architecture that naturally reduces to MWPM or maximum likelihood decoding (MLD) when the utility function is tailored for standard surface-code decoding
[
14
,
6
]
. For GHZ state preparation, we define the utility as the squared magnetization—a direct measure of long-range order. Specifically, the decoder searches the landscape of possible corrections and selects the configuration that maximizes the expected macroscopic correlations. By natively processing the full geometric information of the syndromes rather than just their endpoints, the MUD systematically extracts the highest-fidelity GHZ state from the noisy hardware, establishing an optimal decoding paradigm for measurement-based state preparation.

II
Surface-code decoding

To establish MUD as a generalized decoding paradigm, we first frame standard topological code decoding within a Bayesian decision-making framework. Consider the toric code subjected to bit-flip noise. The error configuration
\bm{e}
occurs on the edge qubits with probability
P(\bm{e})
. Measuring the plaquette syndromes
\bm{f}=\partial\bm{e}
identifies parity violations, where
\partial
denotes the dual boundary operator that maps the dual edge chain
\bm{e}
to its endpoints. Based on the observed syndromes
\bm{f}
, a decoder must suggest a correction chain
\bm{c}
such that
\partial\bm{c}=\bm{f}
.

Within decision theory, a decoder’s objective can be universally defined by a utility function
\mathcal{U}(\bm{c},\bm{e})
that quantifies the success of a correction
\bm{c}
given a true error
\bm{e}
. The optimal inference problem is equivalent to maximizing the expected utility given the syndrome information

\displaystyle\text{MUD: }\bm{f}\to\bm{c}=\mathop{\operatorname{argmax}}\limits_{\bm{c}}\sum_{\bm{e}}\mathcal{U}(\bm{c},\bm{e})P(\bm{e}|\bm{f})\,,

(1)

where
P(\bm{e}|\bm{f})=P(\bm{e})\delta(\bm{f}-\partial\bm{e})/P(\bm{f})
is the
conditional
error probability. Standard decoding algorithms merely correspond to different choices of the utility function

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\bm{c},\bm{e})=\begin{cases}\delta(\bm{e}-\bm{c})\,,&\text{for MWPM}\,,\\
\delta([\bm{e}]-[\bm{c}])\,,&\text{for MLD}\,,\\
\end{cases}

(2)

where
[\cdot]
denotes the homology class of the chain. By penalizing any microscopic mismatch, MWPM finds the most likely error configuration given the syndrome. In contrast, MLD prioritizes logical fidelity instead of microscopic accuracy by recognizing that any error in the same homology class as the correction forms a trivial loop that does not change the logical information. Because all errors within this class yield identical logical outcomes, the MLD utility function treats the entire class
uniformly
, maximizing the aggregate probability of a successful recovery.

In the topological cases, the utility landscape is
binary
,
\mathcal{U}(\bm{c},\bm{e})=0
or
1
, and the utility function merely defines certain sets of errors. Conversely, for non-topological states, a utility function is needed as the quality of the decoded state is characterized by a
continuous
order parameter, e.g., the decoded magnetization squared
\mathcal{U}_{\text{GHZ}}(\bm{c},\bm{e})=\langle\hat{M}^{2}\rangle
for the GHZ state, which is closely related to the fidelity
1
1

1

A shot fidelity is
{\cal F}=1
for a perfectly decoded shot (
\langle\hat{M}^{2}\rangle=1
) and
{\cal F}=0
otherwise. It corresponds to the utility function
\mathcal{U}_{\cal F}(\bm{\tau},\bm{\sigma})=\theta\!\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\tau_{i}-1\right)
, whose Heaviside nonlinearity renders it analytically intractable.
.
We now demonstrate how MUD improves the performance of the measurement-based GHZ-state preparation protocol.

III
Measurement-based protocol

To prepare a macroscopic GHZ state, we employ a constant-depth measurement protocol on a square Lieb lattice geometry; see Fig.
2
(a). The procedure first entangles data qubits on the vertices with auxiliary stabilizer qubits on the lattice edges. Measuring these auxiliary qubits implements a joint parity check of the two adjacent data qubits that yields a classical outcome
s_{ij}=\pm 1
, which we treat as a syndrome configuration. The latter can be viewed as a chain of dual edges; see the middle panel of Fig.
2
(a). Even in a noiseless scenario, this measurement projects the data qubits into a “glassy” GHZ state, where domains of opposite orientation are separated by closed dual loops of
s_{ij}=-1
, corresponding to “antiferromagnetic” domain walls.
Such closed-loop configurations can be decoded deterministically by flipping one type of domains to produce a pristine GHZ state;
this is the classical decoding step that every measurement-based state preparation protocol requires.

However, realistic hardware suffers from imprecise operations, which we model here by replacing ideal entangling gates with asymmetric coherent rotations
R_{ZZ}(2t_{A/B})=e^{-it_{A/B}ZZ}
applied between sub-lattices
A
and
B
; see Fig.
2
. With these imperfections,
open
antiferromagnetic chains are produced
\partial\bm{s}=\bm{f}
with endpoints at gauge-invariant plaquette fluxes
f_{\square}=-1
that cannot be eliminated by bit-flip corrections,
see Fig.
1
. Observing the syndrome chain
\bm{s}
implies that the data qubits are weakly measured and their wavefunction is partially projected with the Kraus operator

\displaystyle|\Psi(\bm{s})\rangle\propto e^{\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}J_{s_{ij}}Z_{i}Z_{j}}|+\rangle^{\otimes N}\,,

(3)

where the summation is over neighboring vertices and the effective bond couplings satisfy

\tanh\frac{\beta J_{s}}{2}=s[\tan t_{B}][\tan t_{A}]^{-s}
for
t_{A}\geq t_{B}

[
53
]
.
(The case of
t_{A}<t_{B}
follows from symmetry by swapping
t_{A}\leftrightarrow t_{B}
.)
The probability of measuring a syndrome configuration
\bm{s}
is given by the partition function of the 2D random-bond Ising model (RBIM)
P(\bm{s})=Z_{\text{RBIM}}[\bm{s}]\propto\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}e^{-\beta H(\bm{s})}
with Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H(\bm{s})=-\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\left(J_{s_{ij}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+hs_{ij}\right)\,,

(4)

where
\beta h=\frac{1}{2}\log|\tan(t_{A}+t_{B})\tan(t_{A}-t_{B})|
and
\sigma_{i}=\pm 1
can be viewed as the
Z_{i}
eigenvalue of the vertex qubits.

III.1
Domain walls and open loops

Prior to syndrome measurements, the protocol prepares a quantum state with a joint spin-syndrome probability distribution

\displaystyle P(\bm{\sigma},\bm{s})=\frac{1}{2^{N}}\prod_{\langle ij\rangle}\frac{1-s_{ij}\cos 2(\sigma_{i}t_{A}+\sigma_{j}t_{B})}{2}\,.

(5)

If we marginalize over the spin configurations
\bm{\sigma}
, the distribution reduces to the RBIM partition function
\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}P(\bm{\sigma},\bm{s})=Z_{\text{RBIM}}[\bm{s}]
with the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
4
). Conversely, marginalizing over
\bm{s}
yields a uniform distribution of vertex spins,
P(\bm{\sigma})=\frac{1}{2^{N}}
. Hence, prior to the correction stage, the data qubits exhibit no long-range order.

The conditional probability
P(\bm{s}|\bm{\sigma})
implies that the relative alignment of spins
\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=\pm 1
biases the syndrome measurement toward
s_{ij}=\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}
. Consequently, a typical configuration
\bm{s}
contains a large number of loops enclosing domains of opposite orientation.

Concurrently, the gate imperfections
t_{A},t_{B}<\pi/4
introduce
open syndrome chains
, as illustrated in Fig.
1
. By analogy with the Ising model, we say that a plaquette contains a flux if it is the endpoint of the dual syndrome chain, i.e., plaquettes with an odd number of antiferromagnetic bonds
f_{\square}=\prod_{\langle ij\rangle\in\square}s_{ij}=-1
. These fluxes are gauge-invariant objects in the sense that they cannot be eliminated by bit flips. In contrast to domain walls that reduce long-range order but do not affect long-range entanglement, fluxes are detrimental to both. The structure of the fluxes simplifies along the symmetric Nishimori line, where the syndrome paths become gauge-redundant.

III.2
Nishimori line

On the Nishimori line (
t_{A}=\pi/4
or
t_{B}=\pi/4
), the couplings become symmetric
\beta J_{+}=-\beta J_{-}
and
\beta h=0
. A bit-flip
X_{i_{0}}
reverses the signs of all edge syndromes
s_{i_{0}j}
adjacent to vertex
i_{0}

\displaystyle X_{i_{0}}e^{\frac{\beta J}{2}s_{i_{0}j}Z_{i_{0}}Z_{j}}=e^{\frac{\beta J}{2}(-s_{i_{0}j})Z_{i_{0}}Z_{j}}X_{i_{0}}\,,

(6)

where we used
J_{s_{ij}}=s_{ij}J
on the Nishimori line. The bit-flip correction
X(\bm{\tau})=\prod_{i\in\bm{\tau}}X_{i}
parametrized by
\tau_{i}=\pm 1
, with
i\in\bm{\tau}
if
\tau_{i}=-1
, deforms the syndrome chain

\displaystyle X(\bm{\tau})\;|\Psi(\bm{s})\rangle~=~|\Psi(\bm{s}\partial\bm{\tau})\rangle=~|\Psi(\bm{c})\rangle\,,

(7)

where
c_{ij}=s_{ij}\tau_{i}\tau_{j}
is the edge chain deformed from the syndrome chain
\bm{s}
by the boundary
\partial\bm{\tau}
. This gauge transformation effectively erases the information about the microscopic path of
\bm{s}
from the corrected wavefunction. Consequently, the position of the fluxes
\bm{f}=\partial\bm{s}
represents the only non-trivial information available to a decoder.

III.3
Away from the Nishimori line

For arbitrary gate parameters
t_{A},t_{B}<\pi/4
, the local
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge symmetry is explicitly broken. Ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic bonds have asymmetric contributions (
\beta J_{+}\neq-\beta J_{-}
), and Eqs. (
6
) and (
7
) no longer hold. The bit-flip correction

\displaystyle X(\bm{\tau})|\Psi(\bm{s})\rangle~\propto~|\Psi(\bm{s},\bm{\tau})\rangle=\prod_{\langle ij\rangle\in\partial\bm{\tau}}e^{-\beta\Delta JZ_{i}Z_{j}}|\Psi(\bm{s}\partial\bm{\tau})\rangle

(8)

does not yield a wavefunction of the form in Eq. (
3
) due to a non-trivial contribution for
\Delta J\equiv\frac{J_{+}+J_{-}}{2}\neq 0
. The operator acting on the boundary
\partial\bm{\tau}
reflects this explicit symmetry breaking. Consequently, the corrected quantum state depends on both the original syndrome chain
\bm{s}
and the deformed chain
\bm{c}=\bm{s}\partial\bm{\tau}
. Identifying the optimal correction, therefore, requires a decoding procedure that accounts for the complete microscopic syndrome path rather than just its boundary
\bm{f}=\partial\bm{s}
.

IV
Decoding

To restore macroscopic order, the decoder processes the observed syndromes
\bm{s}
and outputs a correction on a set of vertices
\bm{\tau}
. Applying the bit-flip correction
X(\bm{\tau})
results in the decoded state Eq. (
8
), whose squared magnetization is

\displaystyle\langle\hat{M}^{2}\rangle_{\bm{s},\bm{\tau}}=\frac{\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}M^{2}e^{-\beta H(\bm{s},\bm{\tau})}}{\tilde{Z}[\bm{s},\bm{\tau}]}\,,

(9)

with the magnetization
M=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}/N
and the transformed Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H(\bm{s},\bm{\tau})=-\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}J_{s_{ij}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\tau_{i}\tau_{j}\,.

(10)

This quantum expectation value can be written as a classical a posteriori expectation value,

\displaystyle\langle\hat{M}^{2}\rangle_{\bm{s},\bm{\tau}}=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}\mathcal{U}(\bm{\tau},\bm{\sigma})P(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{s})\,,

(11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\bm{\tau},\bm{\sigma})=\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\tau_{i}\right)^{2}\,,

(12)

derived explicitly in Appendix
A.1
. Thus, the value
\langle\hat{M}^{2}\rangle_{\bm{s},\bm{\tau}}
is precisely the expected utility of the correction
\bm{\tau}
given the underlying spins
\bm{\sigma}
that the optimal decoder seeks to maximize. Notice that compared to the surface code, the syndromes
\bm{s}
here live on edges and the ‘noise’ induces vertex flips
\bm{\sigma}
; thus, the utility function is a function of vertex variables
\bm{\sigma}
and
\bm{\tau}
, cf. Eq. (
2
) for the surface code.

IV.1
Optimal decoder

The expected utility
\langle\hat{M}^{2}\rangle_{\bm{s},\bm{\tau}}
can be expressed in terms of the spin-spin correlation function
C_{ij}(\bm{s})=\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\rangle_{\bm{s}}
as

\displaystyle\langle\hat{M}^{2}\rangle_{\bm{s},\bm{\tau}}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j}C_{ij}(\bm{s})\tau_{i}\tau_{j}\,.

(13)

Therefore, maximizing the expected utility amounts to a standard
quadratic unconstrained binary optimization problem

[
33
]
of the real-symmetric matrix
C_{ij}(\bm{s})
. In general, this problem is strongly NP-hard
[
33
]
. In the present case, we find that the matrix
C_{ij}(\bm{s})
has, in the physically relevant regime, a large spectral gap and in practice admits a polynomial-time solution; see Appendix
A.2
. Nevertheless, the problem requires computation of
all

C_{ij}(\bm{s})
correlation functions and can be done, at reasonable computational cost, for lattice sizes up to
L=20
only. In the following, we introduce two practical,
scalable
decoding schemes that approach an almost optimal decoder for lattice sizes up to
L=256
and
L=64
, respectively.

IV.2
Minimum-energy heuristic

On the Nishimori line, the Hamiltonian of Eq. (
10
) reduces to Eq. (
4
), i.e.,
H(\bm{s},\bm{\tau})=H(\bm{s}\partial\bm{\tau})=H(\bm{c})
. Then, the energy of the fully polarized spin configuration (
\sigma_{i}=\pm 1
for all
i
) is proportional to the length of the correction chain,
H(\bm{c})=-J\sum c_{ij}\propto-|\bm{c}|
. Hence, the MWPM can be interpreted as the decoder minimizing the energy of this spin configuration.

To adapt this heuristic argument away from the Nishimori line, we note that the energy of the fully polarized spin configuration can be written as

\displaystyle\left.H(\bm{s},\bm{\tau})\right|_{\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=1}=-\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}c_{ij}w_{ij}\,,

(14)

where
\bm{c}=\bm{s}\partial\bm{\tau}
and we defined positive weights
w_{ij}=[\bm{s}J_{\bm{s}}]_{ij}
. To minimize the energy Eq. (
14
) with respect to
\bm{c}
, we deploy an MWPM algorithm on a
weighted graph
, where each edge is assigned a weight that explicitly depends on the measured syndrome value

\displaystyle w_{ij}=\begin{cases}J_{+},\quad&s_{ij}=1\\
|J_{-}|,\quad&s_{ij}=-1\end{cases}\,.

(15)

This syndrome-weighted MWPM (SW-MWPM) algorithm serves as a direct, physically motivated generalization of MWPM away from the Nishimori line. (Note that in the extreme case
t_{A}=t_{B}
,
J_{+}=\infty
, and the corrections only pass through antiferromagnetic bonds.) Crucially, SW-MWPM breaks the degeneracy between multiple shortest paths down to a much smaller set of lowest-
weight
paths; see Fig.
8
in Appendix
B
. Even for moderate lattice sizes, the degeneracy is frequently broken down to a single path, whereas unweighted MWPM must arbitrarily choose one out of 10-100 degenerate shortest paths, often resulting in suboptimal macroscopic order.

IV.3
Neural-network decoder

To resolve the issue of the remaining degeneracy—especially on the Nishimori line where
J_{+}=|J_{-}|
and thus SW-MWPM is equivalent to unweighted MWPM—we deploy a convolutional neural network (CNN). Specifically, we devise a two-stage
[
38
]
hybrid-CNN decoder (Fig.
3
): First, the bare observed syndromes
\bm{s}
are decoded using (SW-)MWPM to produce a baseline chain
\bm{c}
and the correction
\bm{\tau}^{(1)}
inside
\bm{s}\bm{c}
. Then, taking both the initial syndromes
\bm{s}
and the baseline chain
\bm{c}
as inputs, the CNN refines the correction to yield a higher-utility correction
\bm{\tau}^{(2)}
. Finally, the bit-flip correction is applied to the region
\bm{\tau}=\bm{\tau}^{(1)}\bm{\tau}^{(2)}
. The CNN is trained with the loss function
\mathcal{L}_{M}=-\langle\hat{M}^{2}\rangle_{\bm{s},\bm{\tau}}
and thus designed to maximize the utility. The specific network architecture and training details are given in Appendix
B
.

The latter is an analytically tractable quantity that is closely related to the fidelity, which in the preparation of a GHZ state on a given device is the

V.1
Sampling the protocol

Both the finite-size magnetization moments and the threshold extraction require simulation of the protocol over many shots and system sizes. Sampling the syndromes directly from
P(\bm{s})\propto Z_{\text{RBIM}}[\bm{s}]
requires evaluation of the RBIM partition function and is prohibitively expensive. The joint distribution Eq. (
5
), however, is easy to sample in the opposite order. First, we draw an unbiased spin configuration,
\sigma_{i}=\pm 1
with equal probability. Second, we draw each syndrome independently, conditioned on the relative alignment
\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}
of its two adjacent spins,

\displaystyle P(s_{ij}\mid\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j})=\frac{1-s_{ij}\cos 2(\sigma_{i}t_{A}+\sigma_{j}t_{B})}{2}\,,

(16)

that is,
s_{ij}=1
with probability
\sin^{2}(\sigma_{i}t_{A}+\sigma_{j}t_{B})
. By construction, this reproduces Eq. (
5
) exactly, while avoiding the partition function altogether.

We pass the sampled syndromes
\bm{s}
to the decoder, which returns a correction
\bm{\tau}
, and record the decoded magnetization of the shot,
M=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\tau_{i}
. Averaging over shots yields
\langle\hat{M}^{2}\rangle
and, analogously, the higher moment
\langle\hat{M}^{4}\rangle
entering the Binder cumulant. With moderate computational resources we collect
N_{\text{shots}}=10^{6}
–
10^{7}
configurations per point in the
(t_{A},t_{B})
plane. The accessible system size is set by the decoders’ complexity. For SW-MWPM, which is computationally equivalent to regular MWPM, we study systems with up to
256\times 256
data qubits. The subsequent CNN stage reduces accessible systems to
L=64
(Fig.
4
).
The exact MUD requires evaluating all-to-all spin correlations and is feasible for the linear system sizes of up to
L=20
.

Numerical results: Decodability threshold

We extract the threshold by evaluating the decoded magnetization squared for multiple system sizes, from the smallest

V.3
Numerical results: Decoded magnetization squared

In a practical context, a more important figure of merit than the threshold might be the average decoded magnetization squared (which is connected to the fidelity of the final state). To quantify the performance of a new decoder (dec-1) relative to the baseline decoder (dec-2), we define the relative improvement

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{dec-1, dec-2}}=\frac{\langle\hat{M}^{2}\rangle_{\text{dec-1}}-\langle\hat{M}^{2}\rangle_{\text{dec-2}}}{\langle\hat{M}^{2}\rangle_{\text{dec-1}}+\langle\hat{M}^{2}\rangle_{\text{dec-2}}}\,.

(18)

\langle\hat{M}^{2}\rangle_{\text{dec-1}}=1
and
\langle\hat{M}^{2}\rangle_{\text{dec-2}}=0
(which is realized for
L\to\infty
), the relative improvement reaches
\Delta_{\text{dec-1, dec-2}}=100\%
.

VI
Discussion

Our results recast decoding as a Bayesian decision problem in which the decoder is fixed by the utility function encoding the operational goal. Viewed this way, MUD is not specific to GHZ-state preparation: any task whose success admits a
quantitative
figure of merit can be decoded optimally by maximizing the corresponding expected utility.

This directly connects to conventional
quantum error correction
. For the 2D repetition code, we show that MUD reproduces the standard decoders as special cases; see Appendix
F
. The utility
\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{MLD}}(\bm{\tau},\bm{\sigma})=\theta\!\left(\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\tau_{i}\right)
recovers maximum-likelihood decoding, whereas the simpler magnetization utility
\mathcal{U}_{M}(\bm{\tau},\bm{\sigma})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\tau_{i}
yields a per-qubit decoder with optimum
\tau_{i}=\operatorname{sign}\langle\sigma_{i}\rangle_{\bm{s}}
—the blockwise and bitwise maximum a posteriori estimators, respectively. Because the bitwise decoder avoids the nonlinearity of the
\theta
-function, we expect it to deliver competitive logical fidelity at a fraction of the cost; a quantitative investigation of MUD applied to the 2D repetition code is left to future work.

A second consequence is
hardware-aware
decoding. On NISQ hardware, the feedforward correction is applied with imperfect gates, so a recovery that is optimal under ideal application may cease to be optimal once the errors introduced by its own execution are taken into account. Whereas standard decoders use device-characterization data to build a noise model, MUD can leverage the same data one step further, encoding the faults of the recovery operation itself into the utility function and returning the correction that is optimal for the actual noisy hardware.

A further direction opens up when decoding logical operations rather than a static memory. For a logical gate acting jointly on several code blocks—as in transversal entangling gates
[
15
,
3
]
or lattice surgery
[
25
,
34
]
—the fidelity of the executed gate is a natural utility. The MUD then optimizes the success of the operation itself rather than the recovery of each logical qubit in isolation. By promoting the objective of decoding to an explicit design choice, MUD provides a template for such operation-tailored decoders.

Appendix A
Maximum-utility decoder

In this appendix, we provide additional details on the maximum-utility decoder (MUD) and derive certain properties cited in the main text.

A.1
The expected utility as a quantum expectation value

We first explicitly derive the relation between the quantum expectation value of the magnetization squared, Eq. (
9
), and the expected utility in Eq. (
12
). Using the gauge symmetry, the partition function
\tilde{Z}[\bm{s},\bm{\tau}]
of the Hamiltonian Eq. (
10
) can be explicitly rewritten as

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}[\bm{s},\bm{\tau}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}e^{\beta\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}J_{s_{ij}}\tau_{i}\sigma_{i}\tau_{j}\sigma_{j}}

(19)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}}e^{\beta\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}J_{s_{ij}}\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\sigma^{\prime}_{j}}=Z_{\text{RBIM}}[\bm{s}],

(20)

where
Z_{\text{RBIM}}[\bm{s}]
is a standard RBIM partition function with Eq. (
4
), and we changed variables as
\sigma_{i}^{\prime}=\sigma_{i}\tau_{i}
. Then, we express the conditional probability in terms of this partition function

\displaystyle P(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{s})\equiv\frac{P(\bm{\sigma},\bm{s})}{\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}P(\bm{\sigma},\bm{s})}=\frac{e^{\beta\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}J_{s_{ij}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}}}{Z_{\text{RBIM}}[\bm{s}]}.

(21)

Finally, for the expected utility, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\cal U}]\equiv\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}\mathcal{U}(\bm{\tau},\bm{\sigma})P(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{s})

(22)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\tau_{i}\right]^{2}\frac{e^{\beta\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}J_{s_{ij}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}}}{Z_{\text{RBIM}}[\bm{s}]}

(23)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\right]^{2}e^{\beta\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}J_{s_{ij}}\tau_{i}\tau_{j}\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\sigma^{\prime}_{j}}}{Z_{\text{RBIM}}[\bm{s}]}\equiv\langle\hat{M}^{2}\rangle_{\bm{s},\bm{\tau}}.

(24)

This equivalence proves that maximizing the long-range order of the decoded state is achieved via a decision based on the maximum expected utility.

A.2
Exact MUD

The equivalence of quantum and classical expectation values allows us to reformulate the exact MUD decision in terms of a standard computational problem—the quadratic unconstrained binary optimization
[
33
]
, which in general is strongly NP-hard. The expected utility in the form Eq. (
23
) can be expressed in terms of the spin-spin correlation functions as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\cal U}]=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\tau_{i}\right]^{2}\frac{e^{\beta\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}J_{s_{ij}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}}}{Z_{\text{RBIM}}[\bm{s}]}

(25)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\tau_{i}\tau_{j}\frac{\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}e^{\beta\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}J_{s_{ij}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}}}{Z_{\text{RBIM}}[\bm{s}]}

(26)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\tau_{i}C_{ij}(\bm{s})\tau_{j}\equiv\frac{1}{N^{2}}M_{\hat{C}}(\bm{\tau})

(27)

where
C_{ij}(\bm{s})=\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\rangle_{\bm{s}}
, which we compute via the Kac-Ward formula
[
27
,
35
,
49
,
26
,
7
]
. Then, the optimal correction
\tau_{i}=\pm 1
maximizes the binary quadratic form
M_{\hat{C}}
of the real-symmetric matrix
C_{ij}(\bm{s})
.

For an arbitrary matrix
C_{ij}(\bm{s})
, the problem is intractable even for moderate linear system sizes, since the number of corrections
\{\tau_{i}\}
is
2^{L^{2}}
. However, in the ordered phase, the matrix
\hat{C}
is rank-1 dominant and has a large spectral gap; see Fig.
6
. Moreover, we empirically find that this holds even in the disordered phase not too far from the transition. In this case, we can typically find the solution in polynomial time following the program described below.

Local iterative optimization.—

The spectrum of
\hat{C}
is dominated by a single large eigenvalue; see Fig.
6
. Therefore, the eigenvector
\bm{v}
with the largest eigenvalue provides a good starting point
\bm{\tau}^{(0)}=\operatorname{sign}(\bm{v})
for an iterative optimization procedure. We iteratively flip a random spin
\tau_{i}
if
\Delta M_{i}\equiv-4\tau_{i}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}C_{ij}\tau_{j}\right)>0
until no spin can be flipped. We restart the optimization process multiple times and select the best-performing solution
\bm{\tau}^{*}
.

Certifying the solution.—

The output of the optimization procedure
\bm{\tau}^{*}
is not proven to be optimal, because there may be a global maximum that local updates do not reach even after multiple restarts. However, the solution
\bm{\tau}^{*}
can be easily certified, i.e., proven to be optimal if it is a global maximum. To this end, we define a vector
d_{i}=\tau^{*}_{i}[\hat{C}\bm{\tau}^{*}]_{i}
and a matrix
S_{ij}=\delta_{ij}d_{i}-C_{ij}
. If the matrix
\hat{S}
is positive semidefinite, then the candidate
\bm{\tau}^{*}
is a global maximum of the quadratic form
M(\bm{\tau}^{*})
.

We now show that for a positive-semidefinite
\hat{S}
, the quadratic form is bounded by
M(\bm{\tau}^{*})
, i.e.,
\bm{\tau}^{*}
is a global maximum. In other words, for any candidate solution
\tau_{i}=\pm 1
, we show that
M(\bm{\tau})\leq M(\bm{\tau}^{*})
. Expressing
\hat{C}=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d})-\hat{S}
, we find that

\displaystyle\bm{\tau}^{T}\hat{C}\bm{\tau}=\bm{\tau}^{T}\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d})\bm{\tau}-\bm{\tau}^{T}\hat{S}\bm{\tau}\leq\sum_{i}d_{i}\equiv M(\bm{\tau}^{*})

(28)

for positive-semidefinite
\hat{S}
, which satisfies
\bm{\tau}^{T}\hat{S}\bm{\tau}\geq 0
. Here, we used

\displaystyle\bm{\tau}^{T}\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d})\bm{\tau}=\sum_{i,j}\tau_{i}\delta_{ij}d_{i}\tau_{j}=\sum_{i}\tau_{i}^{2}d_{i}=\sum_{i}d_{i},

(29)

for any
\tau_{i}=\pm 1
, and by definition

\displaystyle\sum_{i}d_{i}=\sum_{i,j}\tau^{*}_{i}C_{ij}\tau^{*}_{j}\equiv M(\tau^{*}).

(30)

Note that positive semidefiniteness of
\hat{S}
proves that
\bm{\tau}^{*}
is optimal; however, the converse is not true, and an indefinite
\hat{S}
does not prove that
\bm{\tau}^{*}
is not optimal.

Branch and bound.—

When the certificate is inconclusive, we prove or improve
\bm{\tau}^{*}
with an exact branch-and-bound search
[
30
,
29
]
. Since
\bm{\tau}^{*}
is almost always already optimal and
\hat{C}
is rank-1 dominant, the search tree typically collapses, and the proof costs far less than assembling the correlation matrix
\hat{C}
. If the search exceeds a
predefined node budget, the algorithm returns
\bm{\tau}^{*}
without a
proof of optimality; the returned correction remains valid and at least of
the heuristic quality.

Practice.—

In the regime of sufficiently strong measurements where the GHZ state can be recovered, the local iterative procedure almost always finds the optimal solution. The certificate and branch-and-bound stages are primarily used to prove that the solution is optimal.

To test this, we perform a benchmark simulation of the algorithm. We randomly choose
10^{4}
,
10^{3}
, and
10^{2}
points
t_{A},t_{B}\in[0.4\,\pi/4,\pi/4]
for
L=8,16
and
20
, respectively. For each point and system size, we draw an uncorrelated sample from the distribution Eq. (
5
). We then decode the obtained syndromes with the optimal MUD algorithm and collect the statistics. We find that the certification fails in
19.8\%,28.7\%
, and
27.0\%
of the samples, respectively. Nevertheless, the local iterative solution
\bm{\tau}^{*}
was proven to be a global maximum in
99.5\%,82.9\%
, and
79\%
of cases, respectively. For all three systems, we find that all of the cases where the iterative solution is either not optimal or where the branch-and-bound exceeds the node budget occur below or near the threshold, i.e., in the paramagnetic phase. We expect that in this regime, the search terminates without improving
\bm{\tau^{*}}
due to the presence of near-degenerate optima; thus, the deviation from the true optimum is negligible.

Correlation-function truncation.—

The runtime of the decoder is dominated by computing the all-to-all correlation-function matrix
C_{ij}
. This cost can be greatly reduced by truncating the matrix
\hat{C}
by setting
C_{ij}=0
for
|i-j|>r_{\text{max}}
without computing the correlation function. For
r_{\text{max}}=4
we find that the algorithm produces comparable results with a much shorter runtime for large systems; see Fig.
11
. This approximation is justified as away from the strong measurement limit, the spin-spin correlation function decays with the distance between spins.

Appendix B
Hybrid-CNN decoder

In this appendix, we provide additional details on the hybrid-CNN decoder as a practical implementation of the ersatz MUD decoder. To make a scalable, nearly optimal decoder, we use the approach that leverages neural networks for decoding. We first note that the joint probability Eq. (
5
) yields a non-trivial dependence on
\bm{s}
of the conditional distribution

\displaystyle P(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{s})=\frac{P(\bm{\sigma},\bm{s})}{\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}P(\bm{\sigma},\bm{s})}.

(31)

This implies that
\bm{s}
contains a learnable signal for predicting
\bm{\sigma}
. (Note that the distribution of the gauge-invariant vortices
\bm{f}=\partial s
does not contain any information about
\bm{\sigma}
since
P(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{f})=\frac{1}{2^{N}}
.) The problem possesses translational invariance (up to the boundary of the sample); hence, we use a convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture.

A practical challenge in training the model is the presence of a large number of antiferromagnetic bonds. (Recall that Eq. (

) implies that any given syndrome has a marginal probability
P(s_{ij})=\frac{1+s_{ij}\cos 2t_{A}\cos 2t_{B}}{2}

\bm{\tau}^{(1)}
. The deformed chain
\bm{c}=\bm{s}\partial\bm{\tau}^{(1)}
now contains only antiferromagnetic bonds that belong to open chains spanning between the fluxes. To focus the model on these non-trivial chains, we pass the original syndromes
\bm{s}
and the smallest-weight deformed chain
\bm{c}
to the CNN that outputs predictions for the correction
\bm{\tau}
that maximizes

Note that
\bm{c}
is chosen arbitrarily by the algorithm from a large pool of chains with the same (weight) length; see Fig.

\bm{c}
(and thus in
\bm{\tau}^{(1)}

\bm{s}\bm{c}=\partial\bm{\tau}^{(1)}

\bm{\tau}^{(2)}
to obtain the largest expected magnetization squared.

B.1
Model and training details

Model.—

Specifically, we use the residual CNN with Feature-Wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) to encode the information of the physical parameters
t_{A}
and
t_{B}
. As a compromise between efficiency and runtime, we chose a
3\times 3
kernel with 12 blocks and 64 channels (normalized over 8 groups). The total number of learnable parameters is 990,401. We find that the number of parameters needed is significantly larger than typically required for toric code decoding, and reducing the number deteriorates the performance of our architecture.

Features.—

To embed
2L(L-1)
edges into a
L\times L
square lattice, we separate edges into vertical and horizontal, constituting two
L\times(L-1)
channels. Further, we map the edge
L\times(L-1)
to the vertex
L\times L
configurations by assigning to each vertex in horizontal/vertical channels the sum of adjacent horizontal/vertical edges. The resulting input of the model is zero-padded for
L\times L
channels for
\bm{s}
and
\bm{c}
edge configurations in addition to
t_{A},t_{B}
passed through the FiLM layers.

Loss function.—

For each shot of the experiment
(s_{ij},\sigma_{i})
, the objective of the model is to predict the correction
\tau^{(2)}_{i}
that would result in the largest
averaged
decoded magnetization squared

\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}_{M}=\left(\sum_{i}\tau^{(1)}_{i}\tau^{(2)}_{i}\sigma_{i}\right)^{2},

(32)

where
\tau^{(1)}_{i}
is the first-stage corrections inside the loops
\bm{s}\bm{c}
. Perfectly predicting
\tau^{(2)}_{i}=\tau^{(1)}_{i}\sigma_{i}
in each shot is not mathematically possible since away from the strong measurement point
t_{A}=t_{B}=\pi/4
, the probability distribution
P(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{s})
is
not
a delta function
\delta(\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}-s_{ij})
. Hence, the ultimate goal of training is to minimize the loss function
\mathcal{L}_{M}
averaged over a sufficient number of shots.

To make training more efficient in the initial stages, we introduce additional terms

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}=\operatorname{BCE}(\tau^{(2)}_{i},\sigma_{i}\tau^{(1)}_{i}),

(33)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}=-\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\tau^{(2)}_{i}\tau^{(2)}_{j}[c]_{ij}.

(34)

The binary cross-entropy term
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}
produces a large number of gradients that facilitate fast learning. The edge-alignment term
\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}
penalizes the model for flipping domains that are not aligned with the remaining antiferromagnetic frustrations
c
. This term ensures that the model does not introduce new domains away from the frustrations.

We train the model by scheduling the loss function by smoothly changing the weights of the loss functions starting from

Appendix C
Entanglement threshold

We compute the optimal threshold as the location of the
entanglement transition
using the domain-wall entropy
[
43
]
, which is equivalent to the coherent mutual information. We augment the square
L\times L
lattice by coupling the left (right) boundary spins
\sigma_{x,1}
(
\sigma_{x,L}
) to an additional spin
\sigma_{L}
(
\sigma_{R}
); see Fig.
9
. The couplings between these new spins and the boundaries are the same as for the bulk spins. For a fixed syndrome configuration
\bm{s}
, we compute the correlation function
C_{\bm{s}}=\langle\sigma_{L}\sigma_{R}\rangle_{\bm{s}}
. The domain-wall entropy of this syndrome configuration is computed as

\displaystyle I_{\bm{s}}=-\frac{1+C_{\bm{s}}}{2}\log_{2}\frac{1+C_{\bm{s}}}{2}-\frac{1-C_{\bm{s}}}{2}\log_{2}\frac{1-C_{\bm{s}}}{2}.

(35)

We then average the domain-wall entropy and squared correlation function over the syndrome configurations:

\displaystyle I_{c}=\sum_{\bm{s}}I_{\bm{s}}P(\bm{s}),\qquad C^{(2)}_{c}=\sum_{\bm{s}}[C_{\bm{s}}]^{2}P(\bm{s}).

(36)

C.1
Numerical technique

Since we are interested in the nonlinear moments of
C_{\bm{s}}
, we cannot use the procedure defined in the main text, which relies on computing averages of the joint distribution
P(\bm{\sigma},\bm{s})
. Therefore, for a specific configuration
\bm{s}
, we compute
C_{\bm{s}}
as a contraction of the tensor network closely following Ref.
[
53
]
. We define the tensor network on a
(L+2)\times L
lattice. We then force the left and right boundary spins to be aligned by inserting the identity bond matrix corresponding to an infinite ferromagnetic coupling strength. This ensures that
\sigma_{0,y}\equiv\sigma_{L}
and
\sigma_{L+1,y}\equiv\sigma_{R}
for all
y
. Contracting the tensor network on this augmented lattice results precisely in
C_{\bm{s}}
.

The efficient numerical sampling procedure does not allow us to compute the nonlinear expectation value. However, it is well suited to sampling independent syndrome configurations
\bm{s}
from
P(\bm{s})
, where we simply discard the latent variable
\sigma_{i}
used for conditioning in Eq. (
16
). (To obey the boundary conditions at
x=0
and
x=L+1
, we sample only one spin,
\sigma_{L}
and
\sigma_{R}
, per boundary.) We compute
I_{c}
and
C^{(2)}_{c}
by averaging over
N_{\text{shots}}=50,000
independent syndrome configurations for
L=4
–
20
.

Appendix E
Analytic properties of gauge-oblivious decoding

In this Appendix, we show that the leading contribution to the thresholds of the gauge-oblivious MWPM decoder with high accuracy depends only on
A\equiv\sin 2t_{A}\sin 2t_{B}
. To this end, we compute the decoded expectation value of the two-point correlation function
C_{ij}=\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\rangle_{\text{dec}}
to analyze its parametric dependence on
t_{A/B}
parameters. Since odd moments
\langle\prod_{i=1}^{2n+1}\sigma_{i}\rangle
vanish and the analysis is the same for any even moment
\langle\prod_{i=1}^{2n}\sigma_{i}\rangle
, the results hold for any observable of
\bm{\sigma}
.

Given the observed syndromes
\bm{s}
, the MWPM decoder outputs the chain
\bm{c}(\bm{f})
, which depends only on the endpoint
\bm{f}=\partial\bm{s}
. Then, the vertex spins inside the closed dual loops
\bm{s}\bm{c}(\bm{f})=\partial\bm{D}(\bm{s})
are corrected. The decoded correlation function can be computed as

\displaystyle C_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}\sum_{\bm{s}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}D_{i}(\bm{s})D_{j}(\bm{s})P(\bm{\sigma},\bm{s})

(37)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}\sum_{\bm{s}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\left(\prod_{l\in{\cal S}_{ij}}s_{l}c_{l}(\bm{f})\right)P(\bm{\sigma},\bm{s}),

(38)

where we expressed the region
\bm{D}(\bm{s})
inside the loop
\bm{s}\bm{c}
through
D_{i}(\bm{s})D_{j}(\bm{s})=\prod_{l\in{\cal S}_{ij}}s_{l}c_{l}(\bm{f})
the product over an edge chain
{\cal S}_{ij}
connecting vertices
i
and
j
. (Recall that
\bm{b}=\partial\bm{D}
implies that
b_{ij}=D_{i}D_{j}
for any edge
\langle ij\rangle
.) We now exchange the sum over all 1D chains
\bm{s}
for sums over the endpoints
\bm{f}=\partial\bm{s}
and all vertex configurations
\bm{r}
. Any chain
\bm{s}
can be expressed in terms of
\bm{f}
and
\bm{r}
as
s_{ij}=[s_{f}]_{ij}r_{i}r_{j}
, where
\bm{s}_{f}
is a chain with the same endpoints
\bm{f}=\partial\bm{s}=\partial\bm{s}_{f}

3
3

3

This parametrization is unique modulo total sign flip
\bm{r}\to-\bm{r}
.
. Without loss of generality, we choose
\bm{s}_{f}=\bm{c}(\bm{f})
and obtain

\displaystyle C_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}\sum_{\bm{f},\bm{r}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}r_{i}r_{j}P(\bm{\sigma},\bm{c}(\bm{f})\partial\bm{r})

(39)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}}\sum_{\bm{f}}\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\sigma^{\prime}_{j}\sum_{\bm{r}}P(\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}\bm{r},\bm{c}(\bm{f})\partial\bm{r}),

(40)

where we defined
\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}=\bm{\sigma}\bm{r}
. Now, we express the joint probability distribution Eq. (
5

\displaystyle P(\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}\bm{r},\bm{c}\partial\bm{r})\propto\prod_{\langle ij\rangle}(1+Ac_{ij}\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\sigma^{\prime}_{j}+Bc_{ij}r_{i}r_{j}),

(41)

where
A=\sin 2t_{A}\sin 2t_{B}
and

. The

We then use the high-temperature loop expansion

\displaystyle\prod_{\langle ij\rangle}(1+K_{ij})=\sum_{E^{\prime}\subseteq E}\prod_{\langle ij\rangle\in E^{\prime}}K_{ij},

(42)

where the sum is over all subsets of edges

\displaystyle P(\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}\bm{r},\bm{c}\partial\bm{r})\propto\prod_{\langle ij\rangle}(1+Ac_{ij}\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\sigma^{\prime}_{j})\prod_{\langle ij\rangle}\left(1+r_{i}r_{j}K_{ij}\right)

(43)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\langle ij\rangle}(1+Ac_{ij}\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\sigma^{\prime}_{j})\sum_{E^{\prime}\subseteq E}\prod_{\langle ij\rangle\in E^{\prime}}r_{i}r_{j}\;K_{ij},

(44)

K_{ij}=\frac{Bc_{ij}}{1+Ac_{ij}\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\sigma^{\prime}_{j}}

Notice that Eq. (

\displaystyle P_{\text{eff}}(\bm{\sigma}^{\prime},\bm{c})=\sum_{\bm{r}}P(\bm{\sigma}^{\prime}\bm{r},\bm{c}\partial\bm{r}).

(45)

We insert Eq. (
44

45

Appendix F
2D repetition code

The MUD decoder is designed to maximize the long-range order of the GHZ state parametrized by the magnetization squared. In the context of the repetition code, the GHZ state is the
|+\rangle_{\text{L}}
eigenstate of the logical
X_{\text{L}}
operator. As in measurement-based state preparation, bit-flip noise introduces domain walls that are identified by measuring syndromes—parity checks on the edges between two data qubits.

When the syndrome measurements are perfect
t_{A}=t_{B}=\pi/4
, the domain walls can always be eliminated, and the logical information is preserved if fewer than half of the data qubits are flipped by the noise, i.e., the bit-flip error rate is
p_{\text{x}}<0.5
. However, away from the fine-tuned strong-measurement point
t_{A},t_{B}<\pi/4
, two distinct situations arise: the 1D repetition chain and the 2D repetition lattice. In the former case, the syndromes are not redundant. Any syndrome error propagates uncontrollably, so there is no finite threshold,
t^{\text{c}}_{A}=t^{\text{c}}_{B}=\pi/4
. In the 2D case, any parity check
s_{ij}
between qubits
i
and
j
can be independently verified by any chain of syndromes
{\cal S}_{ij}
with endpoints
i
and
j
. This redundancy results in a finite threshold
t^{\text{c}}_{A},t^{\text{c}}_{B}<\pi/4
.

As in the GHZ-state preparation, the data and syndrome qubits in the weak-measurement 2D repetition code are governed by the RBIM-like distribution

\displaystyle P(\bm{\sigma},\bm{s})\propto e^{\beta\left[\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\left(J_{s_{ij}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+hs_{ij}\right)+\sum_{i}h_{x}\sigma_{i}\right]},

(49)

where
\beta h_{x}
controls the strength of the bit-flip noise. The statistics of the measurement-based protocol are recovered when
p_{x}=0.5
and
h_{x}=0
.

F.1
Maximum likelihood decoder

In the repetition code, the logical information is preserved if, after the correction, the majority of the data qubits point in the same direction as the original logical state. For concreteness, we assume that the logical state is
|1\rangle_{L}
, and the logical information is restored by the decoder if
\langle\hat{M}_{z}\rangle_{\text{dec}}>0
. Given the observed syndromes
\bm{s}
, the MLD decoder chooses the correction
\bm{c}
that is most likely (with respect to
P(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{s})
) to result in
\langle\hat{M}_{z}\rangle_{\text{dec}}>0
. The probability that the correction
\bm{c}
recovers the logical information given
\bm{s}
is

\displaystyle P_{\text{L}}(\bm{c}|\bm{s})=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}:\left(\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\tau_{i}\right)>0}P(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{s}),

(50)

where
\tau_{i}=\tau_{i}(\bm{c}\bm{s})
is the
smallest
region whose boundary is a loop
\bm{c}\bm{s}
, i.e.,
\tau_{i}\tau_{j}=c_{ij}s_{ij}
. The maximum-likelihood decoder then chooses the most probable correction

\displaystyle\text{MLD: }\bm{s}\to\bm{c}=\mathop{\operatorname{argmax}}\limits_{\bm{c}}P_{\text{L}}(\bm{c}|\bm{s}).

(51)

F.2
Maximum-utility decoder

The MLD for the 2D repetition code can be formulated as MUD with expected utility given by the logical recovery probability

\displaystyle P_{\text{L}}(\bm{\tau}|\bm{s})=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}}\mathcal{U}(\bm{\tau},\bm{\sigma})P(\bm{\sigma}|\bm{s}),

(52)

and the utility function

\displaystyle{\cal U}_{\text{MLD}}(\bm{\tau},\bm{\sigma})=\theta\left(\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\tau_{i}\right),

(53)

where
\theta(x)
is a Heaviside step function. This choice of utility function is analytically intractable due to the nonlinearity of the
\theta
-function.

Another natural choice of the utility function corresponds to magnetization

\displaystyle{\cal U}_{M}(\bm{\tau},\bm{\sigma})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\tau_{i}.

(54)

In this case, the expected utility is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\cal U}_{M}]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\tau_{i}\langle\sigma_{i}\rangle_{\bm{s}}.

(55)

The solution for
\bm{\tau}
that maximizes the expected utility is then
\tau_{i}=\operatorname{sign}(\langle\sigma_{i}\rangle_{\bm{s}})
. This choice of utility function corresponds to correcting the most-likely error on each qubit individually. In Bayesian statistics, it is known as a (qu-)bitwise maximum a posteriori (MAP) decoder. By contrast, MLD is a blockwise MAP decoder that maximizes the probability of correcting all qubits simultaneously.
