 ABSTRACT

Rapid wide-area assessments of vegetation encroachment on transmission and distribution line rights-of-
way (ROW) is a highly desirable capability for understanding risks to the power grid during severe
weather and wildfire events. Conventional assessments are time-consuming and expensive due to the need
for in-situ inspections and the use of aerial assets. Performing conventional assessments on a wide area
would require immense resources and time that might not be available within the horizon of an expected
adverse event. Developing a capability to accurately assess vegetation encroachment into ROWs will
enable faster analysis of potential grid vulnerabilities in NAERM. This project sought to develop a
prototype capability for rapid ROW vegetation encroachment assessments by using Puerto Rico as a test
case. Puerto Rico is a heavily forested island territory frequently impacted by tropical cyclones that
threaten the electric grid by downing trees across transmission and distribution lines. Multispectral
satellite imagery (MSI) enable very high resolution (i.e., 0.5 - 2 meter) assessment of vegetation
conditions at scale and with revisit times appropriate for regular monitoring (e.g., weekly to quarterly,
depending on cloud cover) of the entire island. Synthetic aperture radar (SAR) data from satellites was
also investigated as solution to the cloud-cover issue as they are active sensors that emit and receive a
microwave signal rather than relying on solar illumination, and are therefore unaffected by cloud cover
and can collect data during day or night. Finally, MSI-derived digital surface models (DSMs) map the
height of objects relative to sea level, and were assessed for their ability to estimate the height of forest
canopies relative to coincident transmission lines. The results of the mapping investigation are described
in this report.

 SPACE-BASED VEGETATION MAPPING INVESTIGATION

While mapping vegetation from satellite imagery has been performed routinely, challenges remain for at-
scale adoption of high-resolution commercial data for regular monitoring. During this proposed proof-of-
concept effort, we adapted an existing data processing pipeline capability that ingests, orthorectifies, and
pansharpens WorldView multispectral imagery to further classify vegetation and assess proximity to
known transmission lines. Our objectives included: (1) establishing a baseline map classifying images
containing transmission powerlines as cloud, forest, and non-forest; (2) validating the results for
accuracy; (3) comparing the results against areas claimed to have been cleared; and (4) developing a
pipeline to automate the repeat mapping of vegetation for continual monitoring. Additionally, we
leveraged existing capabilities to create DSMs, and built a new pipeline to process SAR data for
complimentary data points that could help determine vegetation threats to the power grid (Table 1).

1.1 MULTISPECTRAL IMAGE MAPPING

Very-high resolution WorldView (WV) satellite multispectral imagery was used to assess vegetation
encroachment on transmission lines in Puerto Rico. With a spatial resolution of 1-2 meters and repeat
acquisitions possible every other day, WorldView-2 and WorldView-3 satellite sensors capture photons in

the visible to near-infrared range across eight spectral bands (i.e., coastal blue, blue, green, yellow, red,
red edge, near-infrared 1, and near-infrared 2). Cloud cover is a persistent issue in tropical and subtropical
regions of the world, and results in fewer available images than the near-daily repeat time would
otherwise permit. Our use of these images to achieve our goals of mapping vegetation adjacent to power
lines consisted of several steps: (1) automate the download and mapping of WV imagery for operational
application; (2) develop algorithm to map WV imagery for forest, non-forest, and clouds, and evaluate for
accuracy; (3) use LUMA reports of vegetation-clearing dates and locations to identify and acquire WV
imagery before and after the clearing events; and (4) map those images and evaluate for capacity to detect
cleared vegetation as compared with manually digitized polygons of apparent clearance.

1.1.1 Pipeline

To automate the process of mapping vegetation, we have implemented a protocol that detects when WV
imagery has been collected over a region of interest, automatically download the image to an Amazon
Web Services (AWS) S3 bucket, transfer the image to local processing servers, apply the mapping
algorithm, and output the resulting map to either EAGLE-I or other repositories for end-user access
(Figure 1). Maintenance for operational use will require an AWS subscription and fee paid based on the
magnitude of image acquisitions used.

Figure 1. Workflow of pipeline for image download, mapping, and dissemination. User directs imagery service to
order imagery from Maxar (i.e., specify ROI, time range, sensor type, other optional filtering like max cloud cover
pct, etc.). Maxar delivers imagery to an S3 bucket in the cloud. After delivery, the ordering service syncs the
imagery from our AWS S3 bucket to our on-site Vast S3 bucket. The ordering service tells the STAC API to
ingest/index the newly delivered imagery. The STAC API tracks imagery metadata, including the S3 path of each
image. The imagery can then be searched for via the STAC API by either downstream applications/services or users
to pull into downstream applications.

1.1.2 Algorithm Development

We acquired three WV images on which to develop and test a new algorithm. First, two cloud-detection
algorithms were applied and compared against the images for accuracy in masking out as much cloud as
possible while minimizing false positives (e.g., bright buildings). One algorithm uses artificial
intelligence (AI) to detect clouds based on training from previous images, and the other uses spectral
information to isolate pixels with a typical cloud signature. The latter was found to produce substantially
better results, and was selected for further use in this project. Specifically, the AI algorithm performed
well, but was more conservative than the spectral algorithm such that many cloudy pixels were not

masked and ultimately mapped as ‘non-vegetation’, which would result in false negatives where those
clouds covered forest. We determined that the more conservative spectral algorithm, which masked more
of the visible clouds in each scene, would produce more appropriate results for detecting where forest was
or was not cleared.
Vegetation-mapping algorithm development included 10 vegetation indices (e.g., normalized difference
vegetation index [NDVI]), and three texture metrics (i.e., mean, entropy, and variance. The former use
spectral information from multiple bands to isolate vegetation pixels from non-vegetation based on
spectral signatures common to most vegetation. Texture metrics use a moving-window to compare
adjacent pixels for similarity or difference, and are useful in distinguishing between types of vegetation
(e.g., grass versus forest) because forest canopies tend to contain more structure than scrub and grass,
which are more homogeneous. To determine the best combination of indices and metrics, a validation
dataset was downloaded from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) that
mapped forest canopy and scrub vegetation throughout the main island of Puerto Rico from 2020 imagery
acquired at 1 meter resolution with manual digitization. The results of the indices and metrics were
compared against these maps and the combination that best reproduced the validation data for our three
test images used the following steps:
 a) Ingest and preprocess imagery for radiometric calibration and atmospheric correction
 b) Mask out water and cloud
 c) Apply NDVI and select all pixels with value greater than 0.6 (i.e., vegetation pixels)
 d) Apply mean and entropy texture metrics to vegetation pixels only
 e) Calculate Otsu thresholds for mean and entropy maps, respectively
 f) Assign ‘forest’ (‘non-forest’) class to those vegetation pixels with values less (greater) than Otsu
 threshold for mean, and greater (less) than Otsu threshold for entropy
 g) Combine mean and entropy class maps and assign forest as those pixels where both texture
 metrics agreed on forest class, with remaining pixels assigned as non-forest
 h) Output raster map with cloud, non-forest, and forest classes (as values 0, 1, and 2, respectively).

1.1.3    Results

The algorithm processes both GeoTIFF and NITF formatted images, and completes the fully automated
algorithm from ingestion of image to output of georeferenced raster map (Figures 2-3) in an average of
four minutes per image (i.e., ~64 km2 per minute) using a 4-core CPU and 30 GB of RAM, similar to
standard laptops.

One of the three maps contained too much cloud cover for statistically valid representation and was
excluded from further analysis. The remaining two output maps were then evaluated for accuracy against
the NOAA validation maps (Figure 3) by generating random points distributed across the extent of each
image in ArcGIS Pro and extracting the value of each map and the validation to each point for
comparison. Results are shown in Table 1.

Evaluation of the false positives and false negatives found substantial inconsistencies between the
validation maps and WV images, such that areas mapped as ‘non-forest’ in the 2020 validation data were
very clearly forested in the more recent 2025 WV images. This was likely the result of tropical cyclones
impacting the island in the 2018-2020 period and denuding and degrading forest such that it would appear
to analysts as non-forest scrub or grassland. We chose not to manually adjust the validation data as doing
so could introduce investigator bias, but contend that more recent validation data would better represent
the accuracy of our product.

1.1.4 Vegetation Clearance Verification

To verify that vegetation was cleared as indicated by LUMA, weekly reports of clearance location were
reviewed to identify regions where substantial clearance was indicated (i.e., distance cleared) and low-
cloud-cover WV imagery was available. Two locations were identified, and a total of 76 WV images were
acquired and mapped before and after the purported clearing events. Verifying clearance, however, is
made challenging by the nature of the reports in that LUMA reports the latitude and longitude of start and
stop ‘clearing event’ locations as well as the distance in miles of vegetation cleared. This requires us to
make assumptions about which transmission lines were cleared (i.e., transmission lines follow varied
paths that do not adhere to a linear interpolation between start and stop locations; Figure 3), as well as
where along the line clearance was supposedly conducted (i.e., exact location of clearing is not indicated).
Therefore, it is not possible to verify explicitly if stated clearance corresponds to apparent clearance, but
rather we can implicitly determine if any clearance appears to have been conducted adjacent to
transmission lines between start and stop locations, assuming entire trees or stretches of forest were
cleared (i.e., trimming of branches would not be resolvable with existing satellite technology).

Figure 3. Map of LUMA transmission lines (yellow) relative to interpolated lines (orange) drawn between
LUMA-reported start/stop clearance event locations. Interpolated lines were used to assume which
transmission line was purportedly cleared, which is occasionally challenging.

Based on these methods for the two reported clearance events that we evaluated, no clearing was
identified. While cloud cover obscured sections of each event, the majority was visible and indicated no
clearing by either algorithmic mapping of images, or by manual digitization by an analyst. Our
assumption is that clearing involved substantial changes in vegetation that 2-meter pixels would detect,
but it is possible that individual clearing activities displayed a smaller footprint that is resolvable with
these methods (e.g., trimming branches).

1.2 SYNTHETIC APERTURE RADAR MAPPING

Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) is an active microwave imaging system capable of acquiring data
regardless of illumination or atmospheric conditions, making it especially valuable in persistently cloudy
regions such as Puerto Rico. Modern SAR missions, from early systems like RADARSAT-1 and ERS to
current platforms such as Sentinel-1 and ALOS-2, have expanded land-cover and vegetation monitoring
through improved spatial resolution and dual- or quad-polarization measurements. Polarimetric SAR
exploits amplitude and phase information across multiple channels to characterize surface, double-
bounce, and volume scattering, providing a much richer description of land cover than single-polarization
imagery, which cannot resolve key scattering mechanisms. SAR pre-processing can be standardized
easily and quickly applied using SNAP (Sentinel Application Platform) a free, open-source software suite
from the European Space Agency (ESA) used for visualizing, analyzing, and processing remote sensing
imagery like SAR. Algorithmically, there are a multitude of approaches suggested in the literature for
mapping vegetation where many studies have noted the value of radar vegetation index (RVI) and Dual-
polarization RVI (DPRVI) as effective methods. In this study we evaluate how polarimetric methods can
improve land-cover classification in environments where optical imagery is limited or unavailable and
where single-polarization SAR provides insufficient discrimination.

1.2.1 Algorithm Development

This study evaluated land cover classification performance using dual-polarization Sentinel-1 data and
quad-polarization ALOS-2 data processed through parallel polarimetric workflows. Sentinel-1A and
Sentinel-1C Interferometric Wide products were radiometrically calibrated, debursted, speckle-filtered,
terrain-flattened, and orthorectified, after which dual-pol covariance matrices were generated for entropy-
based Cloude–Pottier decomposition and classification in the H-α domain. Quad-pol ALOS-2 PALSAR-2
scenes underwent full polarimetric preprocessing, including noise and Faraday rotation correction, refined
Lee filtering, orientation-angle correction, and terrain flattening, enabling construction of the 3×3
coherency matrix required for model-based Freeman–Durden decomposition. The resulting surface,
double-bounce, and volume scattering powers were classified using a Wishart clustering scheme. These
two processing pathways allow a direct comparison between full- and dual-polarimetric information
content and their respective impacts on land-cover separability in complex urban and vegetated terrain.

1.2.2   Results

A comparison of Sentinel-1 Interferometric Wide (Swath type) acquisitions over Puerto Rico on 2025-10-
04 (pre-LUMA clearing) and 2025-10-16 (post-LUMA clearing) revealed that dual-polarization SAR
does not provide sufficient sensitivity to reliably detect or monitor vegetation clearing activities. Despite
the temporal change between the two scenes, the VV and VH channels exhibit limited separability
between cleared and uncleared vegetation, indicating that dual-polalrization Sentinel-1 data lack the
polarimetric detail needed for operational monitoring of easement maintenance.

Furthermore, a comparison of ALOS-2 quad-polarimetric data and Sentinel-1 dual-polarimetric data over
San Francisco illustrates the substantially higher quality and class separability provided by full-pol
acquisitions. San Francisco was used as an analog site because ALOS-2 data were not available over
Puerto Rico, yet its mix of dense urban structures and vegetated hillsides provides a representative test
environment for evaluating the performance gap between quad-pol and dual-pol SAR for vegetation-
mapping applications (see Figure 4).

Figure 4: Comparison of ALOS-2 (left) and Sentinel-1 (right) over San Francisco, CA. Quad-polarization ALOS-2
data capture fine structural detail and clearly separate surface, double-bounce, and volume scattering, producing
well-defined land-cover classes, while dual-polarization Sentinel-1 shows reduced contrast and greater confusion
between vegetation and urban surfaces. This difference demonstrates the higher information content of quad-pol
acquisitions and its advantage for vegetation classification in complex terrain.

1.2.3 Future SAR Work

Vegetation mapping of transmission line easements would benefit significantly from the use of
polarimetric, satellite-based SAR data. SAR vegetation mapping classification capabilities through
polarimetric measurements, combined with its all-weather imaging capability, frequent revisit times, and
suitable spatial resolution, makes it particularly well-suited for this application. The preferred approach
for SAR-based monitoring includes the use of quad-polarization NISAR data (recently launched and data
soon to be acquirable in February 2026) and the integration of SAR-derived vegetation information with
multispectral vegetation mapping products.

Recommended follow-on work includes rigorous verification and validation of vegetation mapping
performance across diverse U.S. land cover types using reference products such as the National Land
Cover Database (NLCD) and Multi-Resolution Land Characteristics datasets; testing and operational
adoption of NISAR as the primary quad-pol platform; and further evaluation of the quad-polarization
Radar Vegetation Index for characterizing vegetation in transmission easements across U.S. lands and
territories.

1.3 DIGITAL SURFACE MODEL MAPPING

In addition to mapping two-dimensional forest location relative to transmission lines, it may be critical in
areas of variable terrain to map forest canopy height if transmission lines are either of known height or
span sections of high relief that would situate the lines substantially higher than adjacent ground and
canopy. Puerto Rico corresponds to the latter with transmission lines traversing ridges and ravines.
Therefore, to map the height of forest canopy, we applied recent technical advances of photogrammetric
stereo reconstruction to generate a high-resolution digital surface models (DSMs) from WorldView
imagery. Stereo image pairs were used in this study. All images contained rational polynomial
coefficients (RPCs) metadata that provide a compact mathematical representation of ground-to-image
geometry. The stereo image pairs were input to a high-performance surface reconstruction pipeline that
output a single raster map containing surface height per pixel in meters. Pixels containing forest
represented canopy height. While the stereo pairs used for this study did not coincide with clearance event
locations, Figure 5 demonstrates the potential utility of this line of evidence to identify or rule out areas of
clearance priority by identifying clearly where forest lies substantially below apparent transmission line
height.

Figure 5. Digital surface model overlaid atop aerial imagery basemap intersecting transmission line (yellow) and
traversing a ravine. Pixel values from inset maps indicate DSM-mapped canopy height within the ravine is
approximately 50 meters above sea level, and canopy height along the adjacent ridge is approximately 75 meters
above sea level, suggesting transmission lines that span the ravine would not likely require clearance.

1.4 CONCLUSIONS

This project combined commercial high-resolution multispectral and SAR satellite data to investigate and
develop a multimodal forest-mapping protocol to verify vegetation clearance and, more broadly, map the
location of vegetation adjacent to transmission power lines. We combined MSI-derived 2-meter
resolution maps of forest with photogrammetric digital surface models and cloud-agnostic SAR-derived
forest maps to identify pixel-by-pixel where forest exists. Accuracy metrics based on independent
validation data indicate strong results for land-cover mapping from MSI products. SAR products were
determined to be insufficient due to the specifications of existing sensors. However, the recently launched
NISAR mission is expected to rectify that issue by providing freely available quad-pol data at high
resolution that will be ideally suited to mapping forest. DSMs clearly indicate elevation discrepancies and
would be useful in determining if forest canopies approach transmission line height when the latter is
known.

The combination of these data products demonstrated strong capacity to map forest for the purpose of
verifying utility company records, and is adaptable to larger scale mapping throughout the continental
United States and elsewhere. However, no forest clearance was detected with either mapping or
manual/visual efforts. This may indicate that clearing events are too small to resolve with 2-meter
resolution imagery (e.g., branches trimmed rather than trees removed), that the sparse nature of the
clearance event reports led to our misinterpretation of which transmission lines were cleared (unlikely), or
otherwise. Future efforts may merge these products with wildfire models to feed fuel maps and improve
predictions of fire location and movement.
