 Executive Summary
 Industrial process heat (IPH) represents one of the largest energy demands in U.S.
manufacturing that is primarily supplied by greenhouse gas (GHG) emitting natural gas
(NG) and other fossil fuel. Roughly 60% of US IPH demand (about 6,500 TBtu annually)
falls in the medium-temperature range of 100–250 °C [1]. While concentrated solar
thermal (CST) technologies can provide a renewable, cost-effective source of heat in this
temperature range, solar intermittency limits their adoption in industries that operate 24/7.
Element 16 Technologies developed a low-cost sulfur thermal energy storage (TES)
technology to bridge this gap by capturing excess solar heat during the day and
dispatching it reliably during non-solar hours. The core innovation is the use of sulfur, an
abundant, industrial waste byproduct that costs 10 times cheaper than molten salt used
in commercial TES systems.
 The overall goal of the project was to advance the design and development of molten
sulfur TES to a manufacturing-relevant prototype stage for solar industrial process heat
applications, while establishing and validating a realistic pathway to commercial success.
Key tasks included corrosion and mechanical durability testing to identify cost-effective
materials, design investigations using physics-based simulation tools, techno-economic
evaluations of system lifetime costs, and pilot-scale testing for performance verification.
Corrosion testing of steel alloys under cyclic molten sulfur conditions showed that
austenitic stainless steels in the 300 series performed particularly well, with corrosion
rates below 35 microns per year and no structural degradation of welds or joints. Thermal
cyclic testing of pilot sulfur TES units up to 1.5 MWh quantified charge/discharge rates,
heat losses, round-trip efficiency and validated the system's capability to operate
effectively under intermittent charging conditions. Results demonstrated stable operation
and round-trip thermal efficiencies of 85%, which will improve further at larger scales due
to favorable surface-to-volume ratios. A techno-economic model, informed by sulfur TES
performance model validated using pilot test data, showed that hybrid solar+sulfur
TES+NG boiler systems are economically competitive with incumbent natural gas boilers
for multiple locations in the southwest US. The project also generated the first
unsubsidized, paid purchase orders for engineering design of a sulfur TES pilot integrated
with solar thermal for enhanced oil recovery. In addition, Element 16 was awarded funding
from the California Energy Commission to deploy a sulfur TES pilot at a large industrial
facility to support transition from coal cogeneration to solar energy.
 This project expands understanding of molten sulfur as a cost-effective, durable, and
scalable thermal storage medium. Results demonstrate both technical effectiveness and
economic feasibility, validating sulfur TES as a practical solution for decarbonizing
medium-temperature IPH. The broader public benefits include: Environmental: lower
GHG emissions, Economic: more stable and lower IPH costs that enhance U.S.
manufacturing competitiveness, Energy security: reduced reliance on imported natural
gas, Health and safety: reduced nitrous oxide and other combustion emissions that pose
public health risks.
 In summary, this project established molten sulfur TES as a technically viable and
economically competitive pathway to accelerate the decarbonization of industrial process
heat in the United States.

 Background
 Process heating accounts for most of the overall energy footprint of the industrial
manufacturing sector. Industrial process heat (IPH) is primarily supplied by combustion
of greenhouse gas (GHG) emitting natural gas and other fossil fuels. Up to 60% of the
process heat demand (~6500 TBtu) in US manufacturing industries falls in the medium
temperature range of 100-250 oC [1,2]. Industries that require heat in this temperature
range include chemical production, ethyl alcohol manufacturing, non-metallic mineral
production (e.g. soda ash, potash, and borax mining), and food processing. In addition,
the heat demand for thermal enhanced oil recovery (TEOR) operations [3] in US that
requires steam in this temperature range is ~200 TBtu, often supplied by natural gas
fueled once-through steam generators.
 To meet this demand, adoption of cost effective and commercially proven line-
focusing concentrating solar thermal (CST) technologies such as parabolic trough and
linear Fresnel offer promising solutions. These systems can operate at temperatures up
to 310 °C using safer, non-corrosive, food-grade mineral oil as heat transfer fluids, making
them attractive for adoption in the risk-averse manufacturing sector [4]. However, energy
supply from solar is intermittent and only available during the day, while the operation of
most industrial processes requires continuous energy supply to meet the 24/7 load
requirement. Hence, integration of low-cost thermal energy storage (TES) is critical to
make solar energy dispatchable by storing excess heat during peak solar periods and
meet the process heat demand when solar is unavailable. This increases system
reliability and reduces the dependency on fossil fuel backup and can enable lower
levelized cost of heat (LCOH) than that of natural gas boiler.
 The most common hot TES uses high-cost solar salt (between $1000-$1200/ton), in
two-tank configurations. The widespread implementation of molten nitrate salt TES is in
concentrating solar power plants [5]. Typically, in a two-tank system, molten nitrate salt
flows through heat exchanger to transfer heat process fluid as it flows between hot tank
and cold tank. The strength of molten salt TES is that the salts are liquid in the operating
temperature range for power generation, allowing for it to have good charge/discharge
efficiency and heat transfer rate through heat exchangers. However, molten salt TES is
a poor technology fit for integration with parabolic trough and linear Fresnel technologies
that deliver heat at 310 oC due to its high cost, high freezing point limiting its temperature
cycling range, and parasitic heating energy losses to avoid solidification in pipes and
tanks. Large molten salt TES systems have also suffered from reliability challenges in the
Crescent Dunes and Noor Ouarzazate III projects causing significant economic loss [6,7].
The leaks have mostly occurred in the floors of hot-salt tanks associated with improper
design and fabrication procedures leading to high residual stress after welding fabrication,
high stress during operation, stress relaxation cracking (SRC), buckling, and creep [6].
 A potentially competing technology in the temperature range of interest is two-tank oil
storage concept. However, the high cost of thermal oil ($2500 to $5000 per ton) makes
the storage cost prohibitively expensive in that case, too [8] and could potentially suffer
from similar failure issues as the two-tank molten salt TES. Other sensible-based solid-
state thermal storage media (concrete, rocks) are being investigated (e.g., EnergyNest,
Brenmiller, Siemens Gamesa, Polar Night Energy), but suffer from inherent challenges
related to high cost, poor thermal responsiveness, thermal cyclic stability concerns, and
 Page 6 of 87

 large footprint. The main challenge associated with concrete based TES systems is heat
 transfer. Heat transfer is problematic due to the conduction dominated thermal transport
 from HTF to concrete, which has poor thermal conductivity. The limitations of direct (no
 pipes) rock-based TES systems are low thermal conductivity, oil degradation in contact
 with solid media, thermal ratcheting due to difference in thermal expansion of the tank
 wall and solid filler material causing undesirable mechanical stress and potential tank
 failure, and high pumping costs associated with the high pressure drop through porous
 bed of rocks resulting in lower round-trip efficiency.
 Element 16 is developing sulfur TES to solve the two main challenges preventing
 state-of-the-art molten salt’s use for low-to-medium temperature industrial process heat.
 First is cost. Most of the cost of the molten salt heat storage system is the salt itself, which
 is generally about $1,200 per ton. Sulfur is about $60 per ton, changing TES economics
 on an order-of-magnitude scale. Second is operation temperature range. Since molten
 nitrate salts solidify around 220-240 °C, they primarily operate only above 265°C with
 extensive electrical heat tracing of tanks, equipment and pipes to prevent solidification (or
 risk breaking the heat storage, heat exchanger equipment, pumps, piping and valves).
 The operational expenses associated with electrical heat tracing can result in high
 levelized cost of storage.
 Element 16’s sulfur TES employs a single-tank design with heat transfer fluid (HTF)
 tubes located within molten sulfur stored in a pressure vessel (Figure 1). This
 configuration provides improved reliability by avoiding sulfur pumping, enabling sulfur to
 safely solidify, such as during maintenance, without damaging storage or heat transfer
 equipment. Since sulfur solidifies at ~110 °C, it remains well above its solidification point
 under normal operation. The system operates above 125 °C and is thermally cycled
 entirely within the liquid phase.

 the tank shell and heat transfer fluid flows through the pipes. The inset plot shows representative
 temperature contour and natural convection current streamlines in molten sulfur.

 Figure 2 illustrates its implementation with concentrated solar thermal (CST)
 technology for industrial process heat applications. During peak solar hours, heat in the

form of hot HTF from CST will be circulated through the internal TES-HX to charge the
molten sulfur TES. During discharge, cold HTF is circulated through the internal TES-HX
to retrieve stored heat from molten sulfur. The now hot HTF from the sulfur thermal
storage will be used in commercially available shell and tube HTF heat exchanger to
generate process steam for industrial facilities (Figure 2). The arrangement of pipes and
the flow of HTF in the pipe circuit were optimized to maximize system efficiency based on
experimental and computational understanding of molten sulfur natural convection
dynamics.

 Project Objectives
 The overall goal of the project is to advance the design and development of molten
sulfur thermal energy storage (TES) product to a manufacturing-relevant prototype stage
for solar industrial process heat applications, while establishing and validating a realistic
pathway to commercial success. The primary challenge with adoption of solar thermal is
solar intermittency that reduces the capacity factor of solar thermal systems, decreases
its reliability to supply continuous and on-demand heat, and increases levelized cost of
heat (LCOH). The proposed development of low-cost molten sulfur TES for integration
with solar thermal will increase system resiliency to provide on-demand/continuous heat
supply and reduce levelized cost of heat from solar thermal to the point that it is
competitive with natural gas-based thermal equipment.
 The project will have the following positive impacts: Economic development and
Environmental benefits: The successful development of technology will improve US
manufacturing economic competitiveness by reducing natural gas purchases for process
heat. Reducing natural gas usage will reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and
benefit the environment. Replacing natural gas boiler that has a best-case efficiency of
81 percent and emits 53 kg of CO2 per 1 MMBtu of heat produced, the annual emissions
reduction with concentrated solar thermal + sulfur TES for the 100-250oC US process
heat market will be approximately, 425 million metric tons of CO2. Health and Safety:
Reduction in natural gas usage will reduce nitrous oxide emissions and benefit the health
of people. Energy Security: Sulfur TES enables increased penetration of domestic
renewable solar resources and reduces domestic consumption of natural gas for
industrial heat, thus strengthening the nation's energy security.
 The planned product development activities include corrosion and mechanical
durability testing to identify feasible materials that are compatible with molten sulfur,
thermo-fluidic characterization and design investigation of molten sulfur TES using
physics-based simulation tools validated using data from lab prototype testing, techno-
economic evaluation to establish the system lifetime cost, and pilot-scale testing of the
molten sulfur TES for performance verification.
 The objectives of the project are:
 Budget Period 1
1. Identify, recruit and engage with thermal energy storage, cost modeling and corrosion
 experts and solar thermal providers for independent review of the research and
 development activities.
2. Develop an economic decision-making modeling and preliminary molten sulfur TES
 cost modeling tool informed by fabrication partner and reviewed by experts to
 determine the cost targets for thermal energy storage development.
3. Characterize the corrosion rates and mechanical durability of various alloys
 compatible with molten sulfur through thermal cyclic tests to identify the suitable
 containment material(s) that meets the cost target for sulfur containment vessel.
4. Identify and engage with potential host site partners for pilot prototype testing, and
 interact with solar thermal providers, stakeholders to evaluate and understand the

 critical parameters to be considered in the design of molten sulfur TES that will feed
 into the activities planned for budget period 2.

 Budget Period 2
5. Characterize the performance of the molten sulfur TES for various operation modes
 through experimental testing of the lab prototype unit.
6. Develop a computational model that captures the sulfur thermo-fluidic behavior
 validated using the data obtained from lab prototype testing and utilize it to design the
 molten sulfur TES for pilot testing.
7. Develop a reduced order performance model of the molten sulfur TES and cost model
 reviewed by experts for robust design optimization to identify design configuration that
 meets the cost target for internal heat exchanger geometry.
8. Finalize the pilot design and drawings of molten sulfur TES reviewed by experts for
 fabrication and obtain a signed agreement with one of the host sites for pilot testing.

 Budget Period 3
9. Pilot scale fabrication, testing and characterization of the molten sulfur TES
 performance for various operation modes pertinent to integration with concentrated
 solar thermal for industrial process heat applications.
10. Validate the performance modeling and design tools based on measurements from
 the pilot-scale unit testing and use it in conjunction with cost modeling tool to identify
 a realistic pathway to achieve the storage cost target at scale for commercial success.
11. Customer engagement and solar thermal technology company engagement to collect
 feedback on the molten sulfur TES technology viability and development activities and
 obtain signed agreement from at least one of the stakeholders committing to fund
 and/or host a pilot of the technology.

Design of Experiment Matrix
 The Element 16 team conducted a literature survey of past studies on sulfur corrosion
to inform material selection for testing. The studies in the literature on the long duration
corrosion behavior of metals and alloys exposed to molten sulfur in our temperature range
of interest (150 oC – 300 oC) are sparse. Most of the studies are focused on corrosion
behavior of metals and alloys in sulfur vapor at high temperatures [14-18] (700-1200 oC),
hydrogen sulfide gas [18-21], and low temperature sulfur environments with interference
from hydrolysis [22,23]. Nevertheless, the studies were informative to understand the
expected corrosion reaction mechanism with respect to materials and inform the choice
of materials to be considered for corrosion testing. In general, the corrosion of metals and
metallic alloys in sulfur is characterized by sulfide scale formation, termed as sulfidation.
 The corrosion of steel at elevated temperatures in molten sulfur has been described
by Dravnieks [24] who measured the corrosion rates from both electrical conductance
and weight loss measurements of the remaining cross-section of thin strips of steel
corroding in molten sulfur for a duration of 200 hours. The results showed that the metal
sulfide scales grow according to parabolic kinetics (metal loss ∝ time1/2) consistent with
a rate of attack limited by diffusion through an adherent scale. Similar kinetic behavior
was observed from studies conducted in sulfur vapor and hydrogen sulfide gas at higher
temperatures [14-21]. The sulfidation behavior is akin to oxidation mechanism in that
resistance to sulfidation is enhanced when alloyed with chromium [15,16,20,25] (typically
greater than 10% by weight). In addition, it was shown that the addition of aluminum (Al),
or manganese (Mn) or refractory metals such as molybdenum (Mo), tantalum (Ta),
niobium (Nb) to iron decreases sulfidation rate [26].

                                          Page 23 of 87

 Hence, the strategy employed was to investigate commonly available practical alloys
with reasonably high chromium (Cr) content such as austenitic 300 series stainless steel.
To mitigate the susceptibility of austenitic stainless steel (SS) to intergranular corrosion
due to sensitization — lowered chromium content due to chromium carbide precipitation
at the grain boundaries— that is likely to occur near the weld area during welding and
stress relief annealing, stainless steel with carbon content less than a maximum of 0.03%
(denoted by suffix ‘L’) [27] are preferred.
 Based on the above information and with feedback from the experts identified in Task
1, the following samples listed in Table 2 were selected for the corrosion and mechanical
durability studies. Despite aluminum’s low strength at high temperatures, aluminum alloy
was also investigated because its high sulfidation resistance and high thermal
conductivity provides compelling argument to use them as heat transfer enhancement
structures in the form of fins, for which strength bearing properties is not a major concern.
A total of 40 flat coupons, 8 each for SS-316L, SS-317L, SS-347, Corten B and Al 5052
were first tested. Multiple samples of the same material were investigated as necessary
so that they can be removed at different intervals during the testing process to estimate
the corrosion rates and kinetics. Based on the results, SS-347 and Corten-B were
eliminated due to high cost and high corrosion rate, respectively, for the next set of
corrosion studies with welded and stressed-welded (U-bend) coupons. A total of 40
coupons 7 straight-welded and 3 stressed-welded coupons each for SS-316L, SS-317L,
SS-304 and SA516-70 were tested in the second round.

Corrosion and Mechanical Durability Characterization (300oC molten sulfur)
 The samples subjected to isothermal testing in molten sulfur were cleaned using a
multi-step procedure to remove corrosion products. First, loose deposits were brushed
off, and the surfaces were glass bead blasted. The samples were then immersed in an
inhibited sulfuric acid pickling solution in an ultrasonic bath, followed by ultrasonic
cleaning in alcohol and acetone, and drying. After drying, the post-exposure weights of
the specimens were recorded.
 An effective cleaning process should remove only corrosion products without stripping
base metal. To quantify any cleaning-induced metal loss, replicate unexposed control
specimens were subjected to the same cleaning procedure. The weights of the control
specimens before and after cleaning were compared, and this correction factor was
applied to adjust corrosion mass-loss values for the exposed specimens.
 The applicability of weight-loss measurements to quantify corrosion rate depends on
how effectively corrosion products are removed. To verify the efficacy of the cleaning

                                               Page 26 of 87

process, corrosion buildup on flat-welded coupons was analyzed by scanning electron
microscopy (SEM) and energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). Figure 1 shows elemental
analysis at three stages: before exposure, after corrosion testing, and post-cleaning.
Across all materials, corrosion product buildup after exposure significantly increased the
apparent sulfur signal and suppressed peaks from underlying elements such as iron.
 Post-cleaning EDS confirmed that bead blasting and chemical cleaning removed the
majority of corrosion products. Residual sulfur concentrations ranged from 0.37–1.27
wt.%, with most coupons below 1 wt.% and only a single 316L coupon showing slightly
higher levels. These results indicate that the cleaning procedure was effective in
eliminating corrosion buildup and ensuring the accuracy of mass-loss measurements.

 Overall, the results from corrosion and mechanical durability testing demonstrated that
the commonly available and low-cost SS316 and SS304 stainless steel alloys are highly

stressed components indicates that the system can achieve lifetimes of ≥20 years under
expected operating conditions.

program as an add-on to this SETO project. As discussed in the previous section,

technology with thermal oil heat transfer fluid and decrease the sulfur TES cost for IPH
applications between 120-200 °C due to increase in energy density since molten sulfur
can be thermally cycled over a wider temperature range.
 Corrosion and mechanical testing of A516-Gr70 carbon steel with the amorphous Si
coating, SS 304L, SS 316L, and SS 347L showed that SS 304L and SS 347L exhibited
the best corrosion resistance, with corrosion rates of approximately 100 microns per year
in molten sulfur at 400°C after 1000 hours of exposure. Considering material cost
difference between SS 304L and SS 347L, SS 304L was identified as the more cost-
effective option. From the mechanical strength perspective, 1000 hours of exposure to
molten sulfur at 400°C did not substantially affect the ultimate strength of any of the four
materials, likely due to the corrosion effect being limited only to the first tens of microns
at the material’s surface. Detailed results and analyses are presented in Ref. [28].

 Element 16 was unable to secure a signed agreement with a host site for pilot
prototype testing (GNG-2F). The original solar thermal partner indicated that their solar

 Element 16 team established a wide set of numerical simulations to resolve the

schemes.

transient stability studies. Computational simulations using the 3D and 2D cross-sectional
domain for the lab prototype were then conducted, which showed that three-dimensional
effects along the span of the sulfur TES tank were minimal, and the system dynamics can
be characterized using a two-dimensional approach.

 The final computational setup was based on the 2D-cross section shown in Figure 35
and incorporated the complex temperature-dependent thermophysical properties of sulfur
[36-38], as presented in Figure 36. The melting point of sulfur is 118.8°C and the boiling
point is 446.6°C at 1 atm [36]. The operational range for the sulfur in the current study is
between 120°C to 300°C such that sulfur remains in liquid phase. The viscosity of liquid
sulfur is a function of both temperature and the amount of hydrogen sulfide impurities
(H2S) present in the system [38]. Sulfur forms polymerized chains that rapidly increase
the sulfur viscosity by multiple orders of magnitude as temperatures approach 161°C.
Above this temperature these chains begin to break apart, leading to orders of magnitude
reduction in viscosity. The presence of hydrogen sulfide hinders polymerization, and thus
the magnitude of the viscosity variation is strongly dependent on the concentration of
impurities. These properties are shown in Figure 36a for pure sulfur along with various
concentrations of H2S impurities. A major source of uncertainty in the numerical
framework is the H2S impurity concentration and its impact on the performance due to the
change in sulfur viscosity. Hence, the sensitivity of different H2S impurity concentrations
on model predictions were studied and the results compared against experimental data
to identify the sulfur viscosity curve that provides the best agreement with experimental
observations. In addition to the viscosity variation presented in Figure 36(a), the effect of
sulfur viscosity variation at high impurity concentration reported by Palm [39] was also
investigated.

Sulfur TES Performance Evaluation:
 A transient computational analysis of sulfur TES using the validated ROM was
performed for repeated charge and discharge cycles to investigate its dynamic response.
An industrial process heat load of 45 MWt was considered. For process steam output
temperature, two different scenarios were explored: (1) 12 bar, 188 oC that corresponds
to heat requirement at US Borax mining facility listed in Table 5), (2) 3 bar, 140 oC that
corresponds to IPH requirement at AB InBev as listed in Table 5. Figure 2 shows an
illustration of the system implemented for IPH application. The influence of the design
parameters — sulfur mass, internal heat exchanger pipe diameter and pitch ratio or
specific surface area — on the dynamic charge and discharge performance of the system,
and system cost (using cost model discussed in Task 5.0) was analyzed to identify
feasible design window that meets the following performance and cost targets of G/NG-
2D - Performance targets: Peak charge rate – 2×discharge rate (corresponding to solar
multiple of 3.0), charge time ≤ 8 h, Effective discharge time (𝑡𝐷∗ ) = (Energy discharged
during cyclic operation /𝑄̇𝐼𝑃𝐻 ) ≥ 10 h; Storage cost target: CTES < 45 (±2.5) $/kWh where
CTES is based on the energy discharged during the daily cyclic operation.
 Initially, the system is in a fully discharged state at temperature of 140 oC and charging
takes place as hot HTF corresponding to charge rate of 2×𝑄̇𝐼𝑃𝐻 flows through the internal
heat exchanger to transfer heat to sulfur. The maximum HTF flow rate through the system
based on allowable pressure drop and pumping constraints were imposed. The charging
process was simulated for a maximum target duration of 8 hours. Following the charge
process, the discharge process was simulated for a total duration of 16 hours to complete
daily cycling. During the discharge process, as HTF exit temperature decreases, the HTF
flow ramps up — controlled by adjusting the speed of the pump using a variable frequency
drive — to meet the desired constant heat rate. Once the HTF exit temperature reached
a certain minimum discharge cut-off temperature (𝑇𝐷′ ), part heat load was provided by the
sulfur TES with the rest supplied by the back-up boiler (Figure 2). The discharge cut-off
temperature is dictated by the maximum HTF flow rate allowable due to pressure drop
considerations and the design surface area of the oil to steam heat exchanger. In this
analysis, 𝑇𝐷′ was taken to be 20 oC above the required steam saturation temperature
based on the trade-off between cost and performance. During the cyclic operation, the
charging temperature profile of the first process becomes the initial condition of the
subsequent discharge process which in turn becomes the initial condition of the next
charge process and so on. Cyclic charge and discharge simulations were conducted until
a periodic quasi-steady state is reached, when the variations in performance metrics and
model output from one cycle to another are less than 0.5%.

Task 8.0: Pilot Prototype Development (GNG-2E, M-2.8.1)
 The objective was to finalize the engineering drawings for the pilot prototype reviewed
by experts and fabrication partners. The sulfur TES vessel was designed and fabricated
in accordance with ASME Section VIII for pressure vessels, while the internal heat
exchanger piping was designed per ASME B31.1, consistent with established industry
practices for high-temperature steam generators and boiler systems. Figure 1 shows the
engineering drawing of the pilot prototype unit that went through multiple iterations
between Element 16 and PCL (fabrication firm) engineering team to ensure that the
design of the sulfur TES was ASME code compliant.
 The final design of the single-tank sulfur TES was informed by the thermophysical
properties, corrosion behavior and safety considerations associated with molten sulfur.
As discussed earlier, the selected single-tank configuration retains sulfur entirely within
the thermal energy storage vessel, rather than pumping it between tanks as in
conventional two-tank molten salt systems. This design choice significantly reduces
failure modes associated with molten sulfur handling, including wear, leakage, valve
failure, and solidification in piping, thereby improving system safety and reliability. From
a process safety standpoint, the sulfur inside the vessel is blanketed with inert nitrogen
gas and as result there is no oxidizing atmosphere in the vessel. Material selection for the
storage vessel and internal components was guided by the corrosion and mechanical
durability testing conducted in Task 3. Based on these results, stainless steel 316 was
selected as the primary material of construction. The measured corrosion rate in Task 3
was used to determine the corrosion allowance.
 For the targeted discharge heat rate of 30 (±5) kW and a discharge duration of 14
hours (EOP-B), the required storage capacity was determined to be approximately 420–
490 kWh. For molten sulfur cycled between 210°C and 290°C, a 490-kWh storage
capacity requires approximately 21 tons of sulfur. Incorporating a 10% design margin, the
                                                 Page 61 of 87

TES vessel was designed to accommodate up to 23 tons of sulfur. The resulting vessel
diameter and length are 72 inches and ~265 inches, respectively with a wall thickness of
roughly 0.25 inch, inclusive of corrosion allowance. The vessel is designed with sufficient
head space to accommodate the volumetric expansion of sulfur as it transitions from solid
to liquid state.
 The internal TES-HX configuration was designed using the validated CFD model
discussed in Task 7 to meet the charge and discharge performance requirements defined
under EOP-B and EOP-C. Thermal-hydraulic insights from the CFD analysis led to a
design consisting of two serpentine flow paths installed symmetrically about the vertical
diametral axis of the tank. This configuration ensures uniform heat transfer fluid flow
distribution, promotes stable thermal stratification, and achieves the desired charge and
discharge rates. The serpentine internal TES-HX design facilitates selective melting of
sulfur from the top of the vessel to the bottom during initial melting and remelting after
maintenance, allowing liquid sulfur to expand safely into the empty head space.
 A valve switching scheme was implemented to control HTF flow direction during
charge and discharge operations, as schematically illustrated in Figure 2. During
charging, hot HTF enters the internal TES-HX from the top of the vessel and exits from
the bottom after transferring heat to sulfur. During discharge, the HTF flow direction is
reversed such that cold HTF flows through the serpentine internal TES-HX from the
bottom and exits as hot HTF at the top. This flow arrangement preserves thermocline
formation within the vessel and maximizes exergetic efficiency. Comparative CFD
simulations with reversed HTF flow directions showed a reduction in exergetic efficiency
from 83.5% for the selected configuration to 75.1%, confirming the importance of HTF
flow direction in system performance.
 The piping and instrumentation (P&ID) diagram of the system along with the
instrumentation and sensors is shown in Figure 42. The system has three main loops: (a)
thermal oil loop (shown in red) that is heated by the electric oil heater (E04) and stores
thermal energy in sulfur heat storage vessel (E01) during charge mode, and retrieves
heat from sulfur heat storage vessel during discharge mode, (b) pressurized hot water
loop (shown in purple) that is heated by the hot thermal oil in a discharge heat exchanger
(E06), which is then cooled by cooling water in a plate and frame heat exchanger sink
(E05) to simulate industrial process heat load, and (c) cooling water loop (shown in blue)
that is connected to the cooling tower (CT01) and operates during discharge mode. As
illustrated in Figure 42, the system configuration includes temperature, pressure and flow
sensors to make necessary measurements for performance measurement and
verification.

Task 12.0: Sulfur TES Design Review (M-3.12.1, M-3.12.2)
 Element 16 received an independent review of sulfur TES design from Bill Huffman,
Director of Quality and Engineering at Ward Vessel and Exchanger. He is also a chairman
of TEMA technical committee. The review report did not identify any major issues. One
key recommendation, echoing a suggestion from Shane Powers at the DOE, was the
non-destructive examination of the internal heat exchanger coil. This examination was
successfully carried out using a helium leak test, as discussed in Task 12. Other
recommendations, such as spot x-ray of welds on the vessel and the installation of
manway/inspection openings, will be incorporated into future systems that Element 16
develops for commercial installations.

Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:
 Element 16 successfully completed a comprehensive set of research and product
development activities, including corrosion and mechanical durability testing to identify
materials compatible with molten sulfur, thermo-fluidic characterization and design
studies of sulfur TES using simulation tools validated with lab prototype data to optimize
molten sulfur TES design operating up to 300 oC.
 Building on these activities, Element 16 team successfully set up, commissioned, and
demonstrated pilot sulfur thermal energy storage system at its facility. Although the
original plan called for pilot prototype testing at a host site, Element 16 was unable to
secure a signed agreement. The original solar thermal partner’s outlet temperature (<200
°C) was insufficient to meet project goals, and other prospective site partners were unable
to commit due to scheduling uncertainties. Additionally, rising steel prices since project
initiation introduced budget pressures that required renegotiation of performance metrics.
To address these challenges, Element 16 proposed modifying the pilot scope to complete
the testing activities in-house using simulated solar thermal profiles, while still meeting
the originally negotiated performance metrics. This pivot enabled the team to maximize
technical progress despite site and cost constraints, and the plan was formally reviewed
and approved by DOE.
 The pilot sulfur TES system was fully characterized in terms of average charge and
discharge rates and round-trip efficiency. The unit underwent multiple charge-discharge
cycles and was operated at a maximum heat transfer fluid inlet temperature of 295 oC
and a bulk sulfur temperature of 285 oC, without any safety issues throughout the testing
campaign. The thermal-to-thermal round-trip efficiency was calculated to be 85%. As
system size increases (larger tank diameter), higher thermal-to-thermal round-trip
efficiency can be achieved due to a decrease in surface area to volume ratio, with a
resulting reduction in heat losses per unit storage capacity. The experimental results
collectively validate the system's capability to operate effectively under intermittent
charging conditions, a key requirement for integrating with variable renewable energy
sources like solar thermal.
 A physics-informed reduced order model that can predict the spatial and transient
temperature distribution of sulfur and heat transfer fluid (HTF) was developed and
validated using data collected from the pilot prototype testing. The validated sulfur TES
                                         Page 81 of 87

performance characterization tool was integrated with a cost model and a concentrated
solar thermal (CST) performance model to investigate the technoeconomic of the system
for process heat application. The technoeconomic results showed that the integration of
sulfur TES significantly improves solar utilization and technoeconomic benefits of CST for
industrial process heat. The hybrid CST + sulfur TES + backup boiler configuration
achieved a lower levelized cost of heat (LCOH) than a stand-alone natural gas boiler for
a range of carbon emission price scenarios and multiple locations, indicating that large-
scale sulfur TES integrated with CST offers a sustainable and economically viable
solution for accelerating the decarbonization of industrial process heat. At an industrial
facility located in Trona, California the lower LCOH of the hybrid system was 3.2
cents/kWh compared with 3.8 cents/kWh for process heat supplied solely by the natural
gas boiler. This integrated modeling framework enables Element 16 to conduct location-
specific annual simulations and technoeconomic analysis to identify the optimal
configuration for various industrial process heat application requirements and market
conditions.
 The project also generated the first unsubsidized, paid purchase orders for
engineering design of a sulfur TES pilot integrated with solar thermal for enhanced oil
recovery. In addition, Element 16 was awarded funding from the California Energy
Commission to deploy a sulfur TES pilot at a large industrial facility to support transition
from coal cogeneration to solar energy.
 Overall, the project validated material compatibility, system performance, and techno-
economic feasibility of molten sulfur TES operating up to 300 oC for decarbonizing
industrial process heat applications in the temperature range of 120-250 oC that accounts
for roughly 60% of US IPH demand (about 6,500 TBtu annually).

Path Forward:
 As molten sulfur TES transitions from pilot scale demonstration toward industrial
deployments, it is important to address the engineering and operational challenges
associated with scaling the technology. Based on discussions with fabrication partners
and freight regulations, a horizontal single-tank design that can be factory-fabricated and
shipped limits the practical capacity of sulfur TES operating up to 300 oC to approximately
10-15 MWh for process steam applications in the 120 – 200 oC temperature range.
 While multiple units can be fabricated and shipped to achieve larger capacities, a
single large vessel offers greater economies of scale benefits. These benefits can
potentially be realized though on-site construction. Accordingly, future work must
investigate the logistics of on-site fabrication including the feasibility of designing and
constructing vertical sulfur TES vessels to reduce system footprint and cost. Vertical
sulfur TES vessel configuration introduces additional design considerations that require
detailed thermo-mechanical investigation, particularly with respect to thermal stress
analysis at the vessel floor due to temperature gradients and frictional force interactions
between the vessel floor and foundation during transient thermal cyclic operation.
Addressing these effects is critical to ensure a fail-safe design and reliable long-term
operation.

                                         Page 82 of 87

 Although this project did not complete a CST integrated pilot demonstration, a
petroleum refinery customer conducted a successful hazard and operability (HAZOP) and
control strategies review for a scaled-up 250-ton molten sulfur TES system integrated
with their on-site solar thermal facility. This independent review provides confidence in
the feasibility of integrating sulfur TES with commercial CST systems at industrial scale.
In addition, Element 16 is working on a California Energy Commission funded project
focused on integrating a pilot sulfur TES unit with an operational mining facility in
California. This project will provide valuable experience in system integration, permitting,
and safety validation under real-world industrial conditions. Future development efforts
will focus on implementing advanced control strategies, such as model predictive control,
that incorporate system state feedback and predictive forecasts to enable reliable
operation of sulfur TES integrated with dynamically varying solar thermal outputs while
meeting industrial process heat requirements.
 Element 16 is also pursuing the integration of sulfur TES tied to the electric grid, which
is increasingly decarbonized with renewable generation. The testing of the pilot sulfur
TES unit with an electric heater conducted in this project directly informs the design and
operation of such grid-tied systems. Integration of sulfur thermal storage can significantly
improve the economics of grid-tied electrotechnology for industrial process heat by storing
energy during periods of low electricity prices and delivering the stored heat when
electricity prices are high. This approach aligns with the broader goals of improving
energy efficiency and accelerating industrial decarbonization. Element 16 has validated
the market potential for this model in European countries such as Finland, where
regulatory frameworks allow thermal energy storage systems to access wholesale
electricity markets for time-of-use heat arbitrage. Building strong partnerships with key
stakeholders in the renewable energy sector and collaborating on joint research initiatives
like this one will be crucial to those efforts. Element 16 will also continue to engage with
customers to provide tailored solutions and comprehensive support for their unique
energy requirements.
 Element 16 has begun early commercialization steps, including its first unsubsidized
purchase orders for front-end engineering design (FEED) and detailed engineering of a
sulfur TES pilot with a solar steam field. In parallel, the team is executing a CEC-funded
pilot-scale sulfur TES unit at Searles Valley Minerals’ Mojave Desert facility, directly
supporting their Zero Net Energy and industrial decarbonization goals. These milestones
demonstrate meaningful market validation and customer traction, key steps toward
commercialization.
 Building on the successful development of sulfur TES up to 300 °C, Element 16’s next
R&D milestone is extending the operating temperature range to 400 °C and above. The
goal is to evaluate feasibility, select materials and components based on corrosion testing
at these higher operating temperatures, and design and test a prototype TES system
capable of charging to 400 °C or above for integration with existing parabolic trough
systems. This system will ultimately provide heat across a broad range of industrial
applications (100–350 °C).
