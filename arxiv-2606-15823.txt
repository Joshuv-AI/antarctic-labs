Minimization of the inradius of convex bodies for prescribed diameter and circumradius in Minkowski spaces Thanks: 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification. 52A40 ; 52A10, 52A15, 52A20 . This research has been funded by the PID2022-136320NB-I00 project / AEI/10.13039/501100011033/ FEDER, UE
René Brandenberg, Bernardo González Merino, Mia Runge

Abstract




Abstract

We study Blaschke-Santaló diagrams for the inradius, circumradius, and diameter in arbitrary-dimensional Minkowski spaces. Their boundary parts are regularly described by four linear inequalities and one more complex inequality, and we analyze which bodies fill the
boundaries and for which (types of) norms that boundaries collapse to a single point. In this context, the diagram with respect to the
1
-norm in 3-space plays an exceptional role. We complete the description of the according diagram by tightening Bohnenblust’s inequality through involvement of the inradius.

1.
Introduction

For
n\geq 2
let
\mathcal{K}^{n}
be the set of
convex bodies
( i. e. non-empty, convex, and compact sets) in
\mathds{R}^{n}
and
\mathds{M}^{n}=(\mathds{R}^{n},\left\|\cdot\right\|)
an
n
-dimensional Minkowski space
with the according unit ball
\mathds{B}
. The
Euclidean
space is denoted by
\mathds{E}^{n}:=(\mathds{R}^{n},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{2})
. For any
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
, let
R(K)
be the
circumradius
,
r(K)
the
inradius
, and
D(K)
the
diameter
of
K
.

The mapping
f_{\mathds{M}^{n}}:\mathcal{K}^{n}\to[0,1]^{2}
is defined by

f_{\mathds{M}^{n}}(K)=\left(\frac{r(K)}{R(K)},\frac{D(K)}{2R(K)}\right).

Its image
f_{\mathds{M}^{n}}(\mathcal{K}^{n})
is called the
Blaschke-Santaló diagram
for the inradius, diameter, and circumradius or simply the
(
r,D,R
)-diagram
( w. r. t. the Minkowski space
\mathds{M}^{n}
). For the sake of brevity, we write
f
instead of
f_{\mathds{M}^{n}}
if we talk about a general Minkowski space
\mathds{M}^{n}
or if
\mathds{M}^{n}
is clear from the context.

Our goal is to understand the general structure of
f(\mathcal{K}^{n})
. It is well known that
f(\mathcal{K}^{n})
does not have holes
[
4
, Lemma 2.3]
, thus it suffices to describe its boundaries. This is obtained by providing a
complete system of inequalities
. Here, we say that a system of valid inequalities is complete if, for every tuple
(r,D,R)
that fulfills all inequalities, the existence of a
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
with
r(K)=r
,
D(K)=D
, and
R(K)=R
is guaranteed. A complete system of inequalities for the inradius, diameter, and circumradius for Euclidean 2-space is presented in
[
16
]
and for Euclidean 3-space in
[
BGR_euclidean
]
. General (even non-symmetric) norms for
(r,D,R)
-diagrams are considered (mostly for the planar case) in
[
4
]
.

It turns out that the boundary of
f(\mathcal{K}^{n})
can regularly be described by five geometric inequalities ( cf.
Figure

2
).
The first boundary is the
symmetry boundary
(upper boundary), induced by the inequality

(1)

D(K)\leq 2R(K).

All symmetric
K
(and all those who possess an antipodal pair of points in their circumsphere) reach equality in (
1
).
Furthermore, it has been shown in
[
4
, Theorem 1.5]
that all
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
fulfill
D(K)=2R(K)
if and only if
\mathds{B}
is a parallelotope.

The second part of the boundary is the
completeness boundary
(right boundary), induced by the
concentricity inequality

[
7
]

(2)

r(K)+R(K)\leq D(K).

All (diametrically) complete sets reach equality in (
2
).

The lower bound on the diameter-circumradius ratio depends on the chosen Minkowski space. The
Jung constant
is defined as
j(\mathds{M}^{n}):=\max\{R(K)/D(K),K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}\}
.
Thus, the diameter-circumradius ratio lies in the interval
[1/j(\mathds{M}^{n}),2]
and the
Jung inequality

(3)

R(K)\leq j(\mathds{M}^{n})D(K)

induces the
Jung boundary
(lower boundary).

The left-hand side of the diagram is usually split into two parts. The first is induced by the trivial inequality
r(K)\geq 0
. It is called the
lower dimensional sets inequality
, or
LDSI
for brevity, since it is fulfilled with equality if and only if
K
is subdimensional. The other part is again described by an inequality that depends on the chosen Minkowski space and an exact expression cannot be given in general. It is called the
isosceles simplices inequality
or
ISI
since in Euclidean 2- and 3-space exactly isosceles simplices reach equality
[
BGR_euclidean
,
16
]
. Here, we call a simplex
isosceles
if at most one edge is not of diametrical length and if all edges are of diametrical length, the simplex is called
diametric
. Since needed in our proves, even for general Minkowski spaces, diametric simplices in Euclidean spaces are called
regular
and regular
n
-simplices
centered
, if the Euclidean distance of the vertices to the origin is constant.

In many cases, such as in
\mathds{E}^{n}
for
n\geq 4
, the
ISI
is the only unknown inequality. Therefore, the main focus of the paper lies in the understanding of the left boundary of these diagrams, or, in other words in the behavior of the
minimization
of the inradius for prescribed values of the circumradius and the diameter.

Obviously, segments are always mapped to
(0,1)
. The first of our main results concerns in which Minkowski spaces all subdimensional bodies are mapped to
(0,1)
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathds{M}^{n}
be a Minkowski space. Every lower dimensional set
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
fulfills
D(K)=2R(K)

if and only if

i)

n=2
or
\mathds{B}
is a parallelotope, or

ii)

n=3
and
\mathds{B}
is a cross-polytope.

While
i)
is obvious or well-known
[
4
]
,
ii)
states that the 3-dimensional cross-polytope plays a very unique role in this question. Even more, the proof of the theorem reveals an interesting connection to Hanner-polytopes
[
10
,
11
]
. In order to prove
ii)
, we show in
Lemma

4.3
that
\mathds{B}
is a Hanner-polytope (which is always symmetric) if and only if all planar triangles have diameter-circumradius ratio 2 within
\mathds{M}^{n}
.

The 3-dimensional cross-polytope is special not only because of
Theorem

1.1
. It also belongs to the exceptional cases of possible unit balls of a Minkowski space where the corresponding Jung inequality becomes redundant ( cf.
Proposition

3.1
).
This implies that only three inequalities suffice to completely describe the diagram
f(\mathcal{K}^{3})
in this setting (c.f. Figure

). Moreover, the
ISI
turns out to be linear, which seems to be quite unique, too.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathds{M}^{3}
be a Minkowski space whose unit ball
\mathds{B}
is a
3
-dimensional cross-polytope and let
K\in\mathcal{K}^{3}
. Then,

\displaystyle D(K)\leq 2R(K),\quad r(K)+R(K)\leq D(K),\quad\text{and}\quad 2(R(K)-r(K))\leq D(K).

Moreover, the three inequalities build a complete system for
f(\mathcal{K}^{3})
.

For every
D\in[1/j(\mathds{M}^{n}),2]
let
r_{D}:=\min\{r(K):K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}\text{ with }R(K)=1,D(K)=D\}
. We call a point
(r_{D},D/2)
,
D\in[1/j(\mathds{M}^{n}),2]
,
left-most
and we say that
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
is mapped to a left-most point if
(r(K)/R(K),D(K)/2R(K))=(r_{D},D/2)
for some
D\in[1/j(\mathds{M}^{n}),2]
. The left-most points might not completely describe the
ISI
but since every point in the diagram can be horizontally connected to the completeness boundary, the
ISI
is fully characterized by the left-most points ( cf.
Remark

5.3
).

One may notice that the set of points
(r(K)/R(K),D(K)/2R(K))
given by the convex bodies attaining equality in the
ISI
describes a non-increasing curve and the induced boundary is filled by isosceles triangles or simplices in all known
(r,D,R)
-diagrams
[
4
,
BGR_euclidean
,
16
]
. Our third result shows that this part of the boundary is always non-increasing and that every left-most point on the
ISI
is, at the very least, the image of a simplex.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathds{M}^{n}
be a Minkowski space.

i)

For every left-most
(r_{D},D/2)
,
D\in[1/j(\mathds{M}^{n}),2]
, there exists a subdimensional set or a simplex
S
such that
R(S)=1
,
D(S)=D
, and
r(S)=r_{D}
.

ii)

The set of left-most points
\{(r_{D},D/2):D\in[1/j(\mathds{M}^{n}),2]\}
is non-increasing, i. e., if
1/j(\mathds{M}^{n})\leq D_{1}<D_{2}\leq 2
, then
r_{D_{2}}\leq r_{D_{1}}
.

In the planar setting, we additionally show in
Theorem

5.5
that every left-most point is in fact already the image of an isosceles triangle, explaining the naming
ISI
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we collect technical and preliminary results. In Sections 3 and 4, we consider the
LDSI
and
ISI
in general, and prove
Theorem

1.1
and
Theorem

1.3
. We conclude the paper by proving
Theorem

1.2
.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
\left\{u^{1},\dots,u^{n}\right\}
denote the canonical basis of
\mathds{R}^{n}
.
For any
X\subset\mathds{R}^{n}
, let
\lin(X)
,
\aff(X)
, and
\conv(X)
be the
linear
,
affine
, and
convex hull
of
X
, respectively. The convex hull of two points
x
and
y
is called a
segment
and is abbreviated by
[x,y]
. The
boundary
of
X
is denoted by
\bd(X)
, the
interior
by
\inte(X)
, the
closure
by
\cl(X)
, the
relative boundary
by
\relbd(X)
, and the
relative interior
by
\relint(X)
.
For any
X,Y\subset\mathds{R}^{n}
and
\rho\in\mathds{R}
, let
X+Y:=\{x+y:x\in X,y\in Y\}
be the
Minkowski sum
of
X
and
Y
and
\rho X:=\{\rho x:x\in X\}
be the
\rho
-dilatation
of
X
. We abbreviate
x+Y:=\left\{x\right\}+Y
and
-X:=(-1)X
.
If
X=-X
, then
X
is called
0-symmetric
.

Let
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
.
A point
p
in the boundary of
K
is
extreme
if
p\notin\conv(K\setminus\left\{p\right\})
. The set of extreme points is denoted by
\ext(K)
.
The
support function
of
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
is defined as
h_{K}(\cdot):\mathds{R}^{n}\to\mathds{R}
,
h_{K}(a):=\max_{x\in K}a^{\top}s
and the

outer normal cone
of
K
in
v\in\bd(K)
is denoted by
N_{K}(v):=\left\{a\in\mathds{R}^{n}:a^{\top}v=h_{K}(a)\right\}
.
The
polar
of
K
is the set
K^{\circ}:=\left\{a\in\mathds{R}^{n}:h_{K}(a)\leq 1\right\}
.

The
circumradius
of
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
is defined as

R(K):=\inf_{c\in\mathds{R}^{n}}\sup_{x\in K}\left\|x-c\right\|=\inf\left\{\rho:\penalty\ K\subset c+\rho\mathds{B},\penalty\ c\in\mathds{R}^{n}\right\}

and the
inradius
as

r(K):=\sup\left\{\rho:\penalty\ c+\rho\mathds{B}\subset K,\penalty\ c\in\mathds{R}^{n}\right\}.

The
diameter
is the length of the maximal distance of two points in
K
:

D(K):=\max_{x,y\in K}\left\|x-y\right\|=2\max_{x,y\in K}R([x,y]).

For
s\in\mathds{R}^{n}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
, the
s
-breadth

b_{s}
is defined as

b_{s}(K):=\frac{h_{K}(s)+h_{K}(-s)}{h_{\mathds{B}}(s)}.

The following proposition collects well-known properties of the diameter
[
9
]
, including the fact that the diameter can also be described as the maximal breadth of a convex body.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
.

D(K)=\max_{s\in\mathds{R}^{n}\setminus\left\{0\right\}}b_{s}(K)=\max_{s\in\ext(\mathds{B}^{\circ})}b_{s}(K)=D\left(\frac{K-K}{2}\right)=2R\left(\frac{K-K}{2}\right).

The three functionals
r,D,R
are all monotonically increasing with respect to set inclusion and homogeneous of degree
1
.
Moreover, they fulfill a certain affine invariance, namely: let
A:\mathds{R}^{n}\to\mathds{R}^{n}
be a non-singular affine transformation and
L_{A}
be the linear transformation corresponding to
A
. Then, denoting with
R^{\prime},r^{\prime},D^{\prime}
the functionals with respect to the Minkowski space with unit ball
L_{A}(\mathds{B})
, we have

R^{\prime}(A(K))=R(K),\quad r^{\prime}(A(K))=r(K),\quad\text{ and }\quad D^{\prime}(A(K))=D(K).

We use the following notation for
hyperplanes
: for
a\in\mathds{R}^{n}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
and
\beta\in\mathds{R}
, we define
H^{=}_{(a,\beta)}:=\left\{x\in\mathds{R}^{n}:a^{\top}x=\beta\right\}
. (Closed)
half-spaces
are denoted analogously using ”
\leq
” and ”
\geq
”.

For
K,C\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
we say that
K
is
optimally contained
in
C
, and denote it by
K\subset^{\operatorname{opt}}C
, if
K\subset C
and there exist no
c\in\mathds{R}^{n}
and
\rho\in(0,1)
such that
K\subset c+\rho C
. The following proposition characterizes optimal containment
[
5
, Theorem 2.3]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
K,C\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
. Then
K\subset^{\operatorname{opt}}C
if and only if

i)

K\subset C
and

ii)

for some
k\in\{2,\ldots,dim(C)+1\}
, there exist
p^{1},\ldots,p^{k}\in\bd(K)\cap\bd(C)
and half-spaces
H^{\leq}_{(a^{i},(a^{i})^{\top}p^{i})}
supporting
C
at
p^{i}
with
a^{1},\ldots,a^{k}\in\ext(C^{\circ})\setminus\{0\}
, affinely independent, such that
0\in\relint(\conv(\{a^{1},\ldots,a^{k}\}))
.

To be precise, in
[
5
]
, the condition on the outer normals is written as
0\in\conv(\{a^{1},\ldots,a^{k}\})
. However, the case
0\in\relbd(\conv(\{a^{1},\ldots,a^{k}\}))
would imply that
0
is already in the convex hull of a proper subset of
\{a^{1},\ldots,a^{k}\}
. It is therefore appropriate to state this here in this form.

In
[
4
, Lemma 2.3]
it is shown that
f(\mathcal{K}^{n})
is star-shaped with respect to
f(\mathds{B})=(1,1)
:

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
with
K\subset^{\operatorname{opt}}\mathds{B}
. Then,

f((1-\lambda)K+\lambda\mathds{B})=(1-\lambda)f(K)+\lambda f(\mathds{B}),

for every
\lambda\in[0,1]
.

By
Proposition

2.3
, it is sufficient to describe the boundaries of
f(\mathcal{K}^{n})
entirely in order to obtain the complete diagram.

There is a special case in which the diagram collapses into the single segment
[(0,1),(1,1)]

[
4
]
.

Proposition 2.4
.

D(K)=2R(K)
for all
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
if and only if
\mathds{B}
is a parallelotope.

We recall a recently proven property showing quasiconcavity of the inradius of simplices sharing a common facet
[
BGR_euclidean
, Theorem 1.2]
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\mathds{M}^{n}
be a Minkowski space,
p^{2},\ldots,p^{n+1}\in\mathds{R}^{n}
affinely independent, and
P
a convex set contained in the open half-space bounded by the affine hull of
p^{2},\ldots,p^{n+1}
. Define
S_{p}
,
p\in P
,
to be the simplex with vertices
\left\{p,p^{2},\ldots,p^{n+1}\right\}
. Then there exists
p^{*}\in\ext(P)
such that
S_{p^{*}}
has minimal inradius
r(S_{p^{*}})
over all simplices
S_{p},p\in P
.

We use this property to compare inradii of simplices, whose vertices all belong to the boundary of
\mathds{B}
. Here, the convex hull
P
of points on
\bd(\mathds{B})
, our choices for the changing vertex, may not belong to
\bd(\mathds{B})
itself.
Corollary

2.6
below shows that we may finally choose the last vertex on a subset of
\bd(\mathds{B})
which belong to the projection of
P
onto
\bd(\mathds{B})
with a center of projection being a vertex of the shared facet
\conv\{p^{2},\dots,p^{n+1}\}
of the simplices.

For
\left\{q^{1},\ldots,q^{k}\right\}\subset\bd(C)
and
P:=\left\{p^{1},\ldots,p^{m}\right\}\subset\bd(C)
we say that
q
belongs to the
C,P
-convex hull
of
\left\{q^{1},\ldots,q^{k}\right\}
, which we denote by
\conv_{C,P}\left(\left\{q^{1},\ldots,q^{k}\right\}\right)
, if

q\in\conv\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}\left\{p^{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}(q^{i}-p^{j}):\alpha_{i}\geq 0,i\in[k],\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\geq 1\right\}\right)\cap\bd(C).

If
P
consists of a single point
p
, we abbreviate
\conv_{C,\left\{p\right\}}=:\conv_{C,p}
.

Using this notation, the quasiconcavity property can be applied to points on the boundary of a general convex body
[
BGR_euclidean
, Corollary 3.5]
.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
\mathds{M}^{n}
be a Minkowski space and
\left\{p^{2},\ldots,p^{n+1}\right\}\subset\bd(\mathds{B})
be affinely independent. Furthermore, let
\left\{q^{1},\ldots,q^{k}\right\}\subset\bd(\mathds{B})
be contained in the same open half-space bounded by the hyperplane
\aff\left(\left\{p^{2},\ldots,p^{n+1}\right\}\right)
. Then, for
S:=\conv\left(\left\{p^{1},p^{2},\ldots,p^{n+1}\right\}\right)
with
p^{1}\in\conv_{\mathds{B},\left\{p^{2},\ldots,p^{n+1}\right\}}

and
S_{i}:=\conv\left(\left\{q^{i},p^{2},\ldots,p^{n+1}\right\}\right)

we have

r(S)\geq\min_{i\in[k]}r(S_{i}).

3.
Well-known parts of the diagram

We say that one of the five boundaries of the diagram
f(\mathcal{K}^{n})
(symmetry boundary, completeness boundary, Jung boundary,
LDSI
, and
ISI
)
collapses
if
the system of inequalities is complete even without this (type of) inequality.

The upper bound (
1
) never collapses since segments are mapped to
(0,1)
, the unit ball to
(1,1)
and the connecting line can be filled by convex combinations of segments and
\mathds{B}
by
Proposition

2.3
.

The right boundary, induced by (
2
), is a linear boundary of
f(\mathcal{K}^{n})
, too. A convex body
K
is called (diametrically)
complete
if
D(K\cup\left\{p\right\})>D(K)
for every
p\notin K
. A complete set
K^{*}
with
K^{*}\supset K
and
D(K^{*})=D(K)
is called a
completion
of
K
. A completion of
K
with
R(K^{*})=R(K)
is called a
Scott-completion
. There exists a Scott-completion for every
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
independently of the chosen Minkowski space
[
17
,
20
]
. In
[
15
]
it is shown that every complete set is mapped to the completeness boundary (which is the reason for our naming), and obviously, for every
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
, every one of its Scott-completions is mapped to the rightmost point of the diagram with the same
y
-coordinate as
K
. Using
Proposition

2.4
, we easily conclude that this boundary collapses if and only if
\mathds{B}
is a parallelotope.

and the

The Jung boundary (

[

, who proved that
j(\mathds{E}^{n})=\sqrt{n/2(n+1)}
. In
[

with
R(S_{J})/D(S_{J})=j(\mathds{M}^{n})

such that it has minimum inradius amongst all sets attaining equality in (

S_{J}^{*}
be a Scott-completion of

, then
S_{J}^{*}
clearly fulfills (

(1-t)S_{J}+tS_{J}^{*}

fills the Jung boundary of
f(\mathcal{K}^{n})
.

Bohnenblust
[
1
]
showed that in general

(4)

j(\mathds{M}^{n})\leq\frac{n}{n+1}

and the the Minkowski spaces in which this inequality is attained with equality are exactly the cases in which the Jung boundary collapses. This collapse is characterised by the following equivalent conditions
[
3
, Corollary 2.9]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mathds{M}^{n}
be a Minkowski space. Then, the following are equivalent.

i)

The Jung-boundary collapses.

ii)

j(\mathds{M}^{n})=\frac{n}{n+1}
, i. e.
\mathds{M}^{n}
is Bohnenblust-extreme.

iii)

There exists a complete simplex in
\mathds{M}^{n}
.

iv)

S-S\subset D(S)\mathds{B}\subset(n+1)(S\cap(-S))
,
where
S
is a non-singular linear transformation of a centered regular
n
-simplex.

Examples of unit balls where the Jung boundary collapses are regular hexagons in the planar case and cross-polytopes or cube-octahedrons in 3-space.

In the following, we study the types of triangles mapped to the Jung boundary in the planar setting. We call those triangles
Jung-triangles
. In
\mathds{E}^{n}
, the regular
n
-simplex is always mapped to the Jung boundary. Let us mention that it is not known if diametric simplices always exist in all Minkowski spaces for
n\geq 5
. Existence is shown in Minkowski spaces with a unit ball sufficiently close to the Euclidean ball
[
8
]
or the cube
[
18
]
.
Diametric triangles always have a unique completion
[
6
]
. Again, this is different in higher dimensions. In
\mathds{E}^{3}
, e. g., the regular tetrahedron is obviously diametric, but has (not only) the two famous Meissner bodies
[
14
]
as different completions.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\mathds{M}^{2}
be a Minkowski plane. If the unit ball
\mathds{B}
is strictly convex and smooth, every Jung-triangle is diametric.

Proof.

Let
S:=\conv(\left\{p^{1},p^{2},p^{3}\right\})\subset^{\operatorname{opt}}\mathds{B}
be a Jung-triangle. Since
\mathds{B}
is not a parallelogram we have
D:=D(S)<2
and, by
Proposition

2.2
, there exist outer normals
a^{i}
supporting
S
at
p^{i}
,
i=1,2,3
, with
0\in\inte(\conv(\left\{a^{1},a^{2},a^{3}\right\}))
.
Hence, moving one vertex slightly does not change the optimal containment.

The diameter of a triangle is attained by one of its edges. Assume that
D=D([p^{1},p^{2}])>D([p^{2},p^{3}])
. Now, consider
p^{1}+D\mathds{B}
. Assume the boundary of
p^{1}+D\mathds{B}
does not cross the boundary of
\mathds{B}
at
p^{2}
. Then, the hyperplane with outer normal
a_{2}
also supports
p^{1}+D\mathds{B}
at
p^{2}
. But since
\mathds{B}
is strictly convex and smooth, it needs to support
p^{1}+D\mathds{B}
at
p^{1}+Dp^{2}
, which implies
p^{1}=(1-D)p^{2}
and therefore
D=2
, a contradiction.
Thus, the boundary of
p^{1}+D\mathds{B}
crosses the boundary of
\mathds{B}
at
p^{2}
and we can move
p^{2}
slightly such that
p^{2}\in\inte(p^{1}+D\mathds{B})
but still
D>D([p^{2},p^{3}])
. Then the diameter of
[p^{1},p^{2}]
becomes smaller.
If we still have
D=D([p^{1},p^{3}])
, we can apply the same idea and move
p^{3}
slightly as well and decrease the diameter. Unless
S
was diametric, we can decrease the diameter while maintaining optimal containment, which contradicts
S
being a Jung-triangle.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

In every Minkowski plane there exists a Jung-extreme diametric triangle.

Proof.

Let
\mathds{B}
denote the unit ball of the considered Minkowski plane
\mathds{M}^{2}
. Then there exists a sequence
(B_{k})_{k\in\mathds{N}}
of smooth and strictly convex sets
B_{k}
being unit balls of Minkowski planes such that
B_{k}\to\mathds{B}

[
13
]
. By
Theorem

3.2
for every
B_{k}
there exists a diametric Jung-triangle
S_{k}\subset^{\operatorname{opt}}B_{k}
.
(B_{k})_{k\in\mathds{N}}
is bounded as it is converging and therefore
(S_{k})_{k\in\mathds{N}}
is bounded as well. By the Blaschke-Selection theorem, there exists a converging subsequence of
(S_{k})_{k\in\mathds{N}}
, so we may assume
S_{k}\to S
for some triangle
S
which is diametric w. r. t.
\mathds{B}
. Let
j_{k}
be the Jung constant of the Minkowski plane with unit ball
B_{k}
. Then,

j_{k}\to j(\mathds{M}^{2})
and
j_{k}=\frac{R(S_{k})}{D(S_{k})}\to\frac{R(S)}{D(S)}
. Thus,
j(\mathds{M}^{2})=\frac{R(S)}{D(S)}
, which shows that
S
is a diametric Jung-triangle for
\mathds{M}^{2}
.
∎

4.
The collapse of the
LDSI

To understand when the
LDSI
collapses, we need to study intersection properties of the gauge. The
Helly dimension

\him(K)
of
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
is the smallest integer
k\in[n]
, with the property that
whenever
(K_{i})_{i\in I}
is a family of translations of
K
, s. t.
\bigcap_{i\in I^{\prime}}K_{i}=\emptyset
for all
I^{\prime}\subset I
with
|I^{\prime}|\leq k+1
, then
\bigcap_{i\in I}K_{i}=\emptyset
. It is well-known that
\him(K)\in\left\{1,\ldots,\dim(K)\right\}
with
\him(K)=1
if and only if
K
is a parallelotope
[
19
]
.

A set
\left\{a^{1},\ldots,a^{k+1}\right\}
is
minimally dependent
, if
S:=\conv\left(\left\{a^{1},\ldots,a^{k+1}\right\}\right)
is a
k
-simplex with
0\in\relint(S)
. For a convex body
K
, let
\md(K)
denote the largest integer
k
such that there exists a minimally dependent set
\left\{a^{1},\ldots,a^{k+1}\right\}\subset\ext(K^{\circ})
. The following proposition is given in
[
2
]
.

Proposition 4.1
.

For every convex body
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
, we have
\him(K)=\md(K)
.

A generalization to the Helly dimension is that of the
(m.k)
-intersection property.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
m,k\in\mathds{N}
with
2\leq k<m
. A convex body
C\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
has the
(m.k)
-intersection property
(
(m.k)
-IP
) if the following applies: For every family of
m
translations of
C
such that every
k
of them intersect, all
m
translations intersect. Bodies that possess the
(3.2)
-IP are called
Hanner-polytopes
(
Proposition

4.4
shows why ”polytopes”).

Notice that
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
always has the
(\infty,\him(K))
-IP. It is well-known that the
(m.2)
-IP for
m>4
implies the
(4.2)
-IP.

The following lemma shows a connection between intersection properties and the diameter-circumradius ratio.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathds{M}^{n}
be a Minkowski space. Then, the following are equivalent.

i)

\mathds{B}
has the
(m.2)
-IP.

ii)

Any convex hull
S
of
m
points fulfills
D(S)=2R(S)
.

Proof.

ii)\Rightarrow i)
: Let
x^{1},\ldots,x^{m}\in\mathds{R}^{n}
and
B_{i}:=x^{i}+\mathds{B}
,
i\in[m]
, such that
B_{i}\cap B_{j}\neq\emptyset
,
1\leq i<j\leq m
. Then,
\left\|x^{i}-x^{j}\right\|\leq 2
,
1\leq i<j\leq m
, and for
S:=\conv(\left\{x^{1},\ldots,x^{m}\right\})
it follows that
D(S)\leq 2
. Now,
ii)
implies
R(S)\leq 1
, i. e. there exists a center
c\in\mathds{R}^{n}
s.t.
S\subset c+\mathds{B}
. Hence,
\left\|x^{i}-c\right\|\leq 1
, and therefore
c\in B_{i}
,
i\in[m]
. Thus,
B_{1}\cap\ldots\cap B_{m}\neq\emptyset
, which shows that
\mathds{B}
has the
(m.2)
-IP.

i)\Rightarrow ii)
: Let
S=\conv(\left\{x^{1},\ldots,x^{m}\right\})
fulfill
D(S)=2
, i. e.
\left\|x^{i}-x^{j}\right\|\leq 2
and
(x^{i}+\mathds{B})\cap(x^{j}+\mathds{B})\neq\emptyset
,
1\leq i<j\leq m
. By
i)
there exists
c\in(x^{1}+\mathds{B})\cap\ldots\cap(x^{m}+\mathds{B})
and therefore
x^{i}\in c+\mathds{B}
,
i\in[m]
. Thus,
S\subset c+\mathds{B}
, which implies
R(S)\leq 1
and, because of (
1
), therefore
R(S)=1
.
∎

5.
Properties of the
ISI

The
ISI
collapses if and only if there exists a subdimensional set
K
with
R(K)=1
and
D(K)=1/j(\mathds{M}^{n})
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathds{M}^{n}
be a Minkowski space.
If
\him(\mathds{B})\leq n-1
, the
ISI
collapses.

Proof.

If
\him(\mathds{B})\leq n-1
, then by
Proposition

4.1
, we have
\md(\mathds{B})\leq n-1
. Then, for any
K\subset^{\operatorname{opt}}\mathds{B}
, we can choose at most
n
affinely independent outer normals as in
Proposition

2.2
. Thus, the diameter of
K
is larger than the one of the convex hull of at most
n
touching points, which is a subdimensional set. Thus, there exists a subdimensional set
K
with
R(K)=1
and
D(K)=1/j(\mathds{M}^{n})
and the set of points provided by the equality case of the
ISI
collapses.
∎

Obviously, there are Minkowski spaces with
\him(\mathds{B})=n
(like the Euclidean spaces) such that the

does not collapse. However, one cannot replace the
“if”
in Theorem
5.1
by an

.

The following lemma shows that we may connect any point in the diagram, which is induced by a simplex optimally contained in the unit ball, to a point induced by an optimally contained set

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
S:=\conv(\left\{p^{1},p^{2},\ldots,p^{n+1}\right\})\subset^{\operatorname{opt}}\mathds{B}
be a full-dimensional simplex with
D(S)<1/j_{n-1}
.
Then, for every

there exists a simplex

with

,

, and

.

Proof.

We show that there is a continuous path
\gamma:[0,1]\mapsto\bd(\mathds{B})
with
\gamma(0)=p^{1}
such that

\displaystyle S_{t}\subset^{\operatorname{opt}}\mathds{B},

\displaystyle r(S_{t})\leq r(S),\text{ and}

\displaystyle D(S_{1})\geq\frac{1}{j_{n-1}},

where
S_{t}:=\conv(\left\{\gamma(t),p^{2},\ldots,p^{n+1}\right\})
,

.
The claim then follows by continuity of the diameter.

By the assumptions on
S
we have
\left\{p^{1},p^{2},\ldots,p^{n+1}\right\}\subset\bd(\mathds{B})
and outer normals
a^{i}
to
\mathds{B}

,

, with

.
We define

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle:=\left\{p\in\bd(\mathds{B}):\pos(\left\{-a^{2},\ldots,-a^{n+1}\right\})\cap N_{\mathds{B}}(p)\neq\emptyset\right\},

\displaystyle Q_{\bd}

\displaystyle:=\left\{p\in Q:\bd(\pos(\left\{-a^{2},\ldots,-a^{n+1}\right\}))\cap N_{\mathds{B}}(p)\neq\emptyset\right\},

\displaystyle Q^{>}

\displaystyle:=\left\{p\in Q:r(\conv(\left\{p,p^{2},\ldots,p^{n+1}\right\})>r(S)\right\},\text{ and}

\displaystyle Q^{\leq}

\displaystyle:=\left\{p\in Q:r(\conv(\left\{p,p^{2},\ldots,p^{n+1}\right\})\leq r(S)\right\}.

Then,
Q
is closed and path-connected and
for every

the set
\conv(\left\{p,p^{2},\ldots,p^{n+1}\right\})
is optimally contained in
\mathds{B}
. If

, there exists an outer normal at

\bd(\pos(\left\{-a^{2},\ldots,-a^{n+1}\right\}))
. As explained in the remark after

, this implies that we may remove at least one of the vertices while preserving optimal containment. In that case the diameter is at most the diameter of a subdimensional set, i. e.

Hence,
p^{1}\notin Q_{\bd}
, i. e.

, and obviously

.
Thus, it suffices to show that there exists a

\gamma:[0,1]\mapsto Q^{\leq}
with
\gamma(0)=p^{1}
and
\gamma(1)\in Q_{\bd}
.

Assume this is not the case. Denoting

X:=\left\{p\in Q^{\leq}:p\text{ and }p^{1}\text{ are path-connected in $Q^{\leq}$}\right\},

the assumption may be rewritten as
X\cap Q_{\bd}=\emptyset
.

Let

. There is a sequence of points in
\bd(\mathds{B})
converging to
q
with no outer normals in
\pos(\left\{-a^{1},\ldots,-a^{n+1}\right\})
. Then,
q
has to have an outer normal in
\bd(\pos(\left\{-a^{2},\ldots,-a^{n+1}\right\}))
.
Hence,
\bd(Q)\subset Q_{\bd}
, which implies
X\subset\inte(Q)
.

The path-connected components of
Q^{\leq}
are closed because
Q^{\leq}
is closed. Thus,
X
and

are closed and disjoint. Furthermore,
Q_{\bd}
is closed. Together, we obtain that
Q_{\bd}\cup(Q^{\leq}\setminus X)
and

are two disjoint compact sets. Thus, there is an

such that

and

are disjoint and since

we may choose

small enough such that

.
Now, any point in

obviously does not belong to

either, which implies

and therefore

.
However, this means that for any two point

such that
p^{1}\in\conv_{\mathds{B},p^{i}}(\left\{q^{1},q^{2}\right\})
, for some

(which one may obtain, e.g., by choosing

,
