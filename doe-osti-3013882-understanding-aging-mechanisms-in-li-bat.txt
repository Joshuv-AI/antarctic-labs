ABSTRACT
This report explores aging mechanisms and cycling performance of Li batteries, with special
consideration of both the passive and active materials within the cell. Specifically, we find that
metal dissolution, caused by material incompatibilities in cells, is common across many battery
types and is highly detrimental. To enable these studies, we developed in situ coin cell and pouch
cell NMR methods, as well as HR-MAS NMR protocols, to achieve chemical resolution and gain
mechanistic insights into Li battery degradation with minimal modification. Additionally, we
explore metal spiking experiments and complementary detection methods for metal dissolution in
batteries, including XRF and XPS.

1. INTRODUCTION

Higher energy density Li-ion batteries, likely with silicon (Si) or lithium (Li) anode materials,
are needed to meet the Department of Energy’s (DOE’s) ambitious electric vehicle (EV) battery
targets. Cycling and, especially, calendar aging have been identified as substantial barriers for silicon
[27, 7, 40] and lithium metal batteries [21, 24, 18]. Our studies suggest that battery packaging
substantially impacts aging, challenging the conventional paradigm of focusing studies on a single
component and attempting to engineer batteries from a bottom-up approach. Specifically, we
show that steel-cased coin cell batteries, common in research studies, age differently and more
significantly than polymer-coated aluminum pouch cells, which are more relevant because they
can be scaled for applications like EVs. Steel corrosion has been demonstrated in the literature
[46, 8, 25, 36], so we studied aging in alternative corrosion-resistant titanium (Ti) coin cases.
Unfortunately, our data suggest that Ti cases cause more degradation in some Si anode cells but
no extra degradation in other Li anode cells relative to steel. Each case is case-dependent. What
is clear from these studies is that, to correlate battery performance to specific aging mechanisms,
it is essential to account for the passive materials. In this report, we test various hypotheses
under the paradigm that battery aging is a system-level problem and that both active and passive
materials affect the aging mechanisms. We introduce various tools to enable further research into
this problem.
Mechanistic studies with ex situ techniques require battery disassembly, which can damage inter-
faces [16], introduce artifacts, and make it impossible to study the same cell at various aging times.
Batteries need to be studied at a system level because active and passive components can experience
cross-talk, where degradation mechanisms of one component interact with degradation products of
another [39]. To account for these system-level effects, either advanced in situ or carefully designed
and controlled ex situ experiments are needed. In this report, we explore both approaches.
Modern in situ nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) affords rich materials characterization on the
same cell without disassembly over time [11, 28, 33, 17, 22], but cells have conventionally been
modified extensively due to metal introducing many difficulties with NMR, rendering studies into
the mechanism of aging questionable (especially if metal components impact aging). Most of these
modified cells used for in situ NMR studies do not have the same performance as more conventional
coin or pouch cells.
To overcome the limitations associated with unrealistic cell constructions required by previous in
situ NMR hardware, our Sandia team partnered with ABQMR to develop and patent an in situ NMR
probe capable of investigating coin cells with metal cases [44, 42]. This hairpin coil probe produces
a radiofrequency (rf) field that oscillates perpendicular to the long axis of the coil and parallel to
its width. Our finite element simulations show that a “skimming” orientation of the coin cell in
the coil allows the rf field to enter the cell via the sealing gap between the case parts, as opposed
to the “damming” orientation where the rf signal cannot penetrate the metal case. High-frequency

 17

rf fields used for NMR do not penetrate deeply into bulk metals. The rf fields only penetrate the
"skin" of the metal, for most metals < 100 𝜇𝑚 at modern high-field NMR frequencies. See more
on this in Chapter 2 and Chapter 3. We have previously shown that a coin cell NMR probe enables
NMR studies of metal-cased cells, including a commercial cell [44].
Ti cases enable easier in situ coin cell NMR studies due to their lower magnetism. However, we
have now shown that some cells with LiPF6 -based electrolytes degrade differently when in stainless
steel or Ti coin cell cases. Studying chemistry in stainless steel cells is non-trivial. Doing so
required us to develop coin cell NMR experiments that overcome the large static magnetic field
inhomogeneity caused by the stainless steel casing. In particular, the stainless steel casings cause
the liquid to broaden substantially. The solid materials are limited to cylindrical disks in the center
of the coin cell battery, but the liquids tend to squeeze throughout the case and therefore experience
the widest range of magnetic fields. We have previously reported several pulsed NMR techniques
that we used to overcome the broadening and obtain chemical information. This NMR methods
development was partially funded by this project and partially funded by parallel projects.
We have observed large differences in performance for some chemistries between coin cell and
pouch cell batteries. Pouch cell batteries usually outperform coin cell batteries. Motivated by these
observations, we sought to perform in situ NMR on pouch cells. NMR of "mini-pouch" cells has
been shown before. Here, we built, optimized, and used three pouch cell probes that can be used
on full-size (47 × 64 mm) pouch cells. These Pouch cells are constructed using commercially
available jigs and sealing equipment and are essentially constructed as is typical, see Chapter 2 for
details. Chapter 2 and Chapter 3 provides, experimental, analytical and numerical evidence that the
rf penetrate the pouch cell NMR probe travels through the narrow 27 𝜇𝑚 seal. Additionally Chapter
2 and 3 outline the considerations for optimizing NMR signal of materials in Pouch cells.
These in situ NMR experiments on coin cell and pouch cells are powerful but are also experimentally
demanding. To achieve high chemical resolution, often new data collection strategies will be needed,
such as tailored pulse sequences to the molecules of interest. Therefore, we think that these in
situ experiments are best used to test specific hypotheses. As a complement, we have developed
high-resolution magic-angle-spinning (HRMAS) NMR protocols for studying changes in cycled
or aged coin or pouch electrolytes, see Chapter 4. Different from most other ex situ protocols,
our HRMAS strategy requires no dilution step. We place the whole separator from the cycled cell
into a sealed sample holder and use MAS to remove the magnetic susceptibility broadening of the
solid separator. We are able to achieve high-resolution NMR spectra of the liquid components. We
are also highly sensitive to the paramagnetic metal salts that dissolve into the electrolyte solution
during aging. This has enabled us to discover several material incompatibilities within cells and
correlate the chemistry to electrochemical measurements.
We have attempted to reproduce some of the effects of material incompatibilities through various
metal spiking experiments. These metal spiking experiments are complicated by cycling because the
various metal additives get plated out of solution. It is also difficult to reproduce the concentration
of the various metal ions, and one has to be careful with anion selection. An overview of these
studies is presented in Chapter 5.
Various methods were used to confirm our NMR results. In Chapter 6, we discuss some complica-
tions about interpreting XPS data and point out the potential of confounding an F peak with a Ni

peak.
In Chapter 7, we revisit in situ experiments and discuss the possibility of comparing spin counting
to Coulomb counting to track Li and charge in batteries.

2. POUCH CELL FABRICATION AND TESTING TO INFORM PROBE

2.1. Pouch Cell Fabrication and Testing to Inform Probe Design

In order to design a nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) probe capable of characterizing pouch
cell batteries, we first needed to understand how the electromagnetic field enters and leaves pouch
cell battery packaging. Metal castings generally block electromagnetic fields, making it difficult to
perform NMR experiments on coin or pouch cell batteries. In a previous project, we showed that
in-situ NMR was possible using a coin cell probe because of a gap between metal case materials
[?, 44] allowed by a polymer gasket that prevents the cell sides from shorting together. This polymer
gap region provides space for the electromagnetic field to enter and leave coin cells if the cells are
placed in a strategic orientation to allow the field to skim over the electrodes after entering in the
gasket region.

We hypothesized that the polymer spacing between the two aluminum sheets in pouch cell batteries
would be a critical parameter impacting the ability of the electromagnetic field to enter and leave
pouch cell seals. Therefore, we began by measuring the polymer spacing between Al bag layers
using a laser plasma focused ion beam (PFIB) to cross-section a pouch cell and scanning electron
microscope (SEM) to image thicknesses. Figure 2-1 shows a photograph and cartoon of empty
pouch cells where the case and leads are the same as they would be in pouch cell batteries, but the
middle part of the cell, which normally contains a stack of electrodes, is empty. We sealed these
using Sandia’s pouch cell fabrication instruments and procedures, which have been described in
our previous work [?, ?].

We cross sectioned the cell in the middle area and on one of the sealed sides marked by the red
boxes. An SEM cross section of the pouch cell material in the unsealed region is shown in Figure
2(a) with the polymer that would be inside the cell on the top (presumed to be a polypropylene-like
material) and the polymer that would be outside the cell on the bottom (presumed to be oriented
nylon). There is also a dry lamination layer (DL) that is present between the aluminum and the nylon
. The SEM cross section of the pouch cell in the sealed region is shown in Figure 2(b). It includes
two Al and four polymer layers because it consists of two pieces of the bag material sealed together
in the edge region. The polymer inside the cell shrinks from two 41 𝜇m thick polymer layers in
an unsealed region to a 27𝜇m thick region melted together after sealing. Therefore, the polymer
gap between the two Al sides of the pouch cell through which the electromagnetic field must enter
and leave the cell is about 27𝜇m. This compares to a much larger gap in coin cell batteries with a
polypropylene gasket crimp seals. This means the skimming-enabled NMR interrogation of coin
cell batteries may be more difficult in pouch cell batteries with a thinner gap between the two metal
sides of the case.

2.2. Model Pouch Cell Fabrication

Next, we fabricated a series of model pouch cells and delivered them to ABQMR so that they could
use the pouch cells to investigate which parameters (i.e., orientation, geometry, etc.) impact the
ability to measure NMR signals from inside pouch cells. The geometry of these model pouch cells
was also replicated in models and the experimental results were compared to modeling results to
ensure that we understood the mechanism of rf interaction with the pouch cells. These pouch cells
did not contain electrode stacks or electrolyte like would be present in a pouch cell battery. Instead,
the pouch cells were constructed with model materials containing NMR-active nuclei species but
the model materials were sealed in pouch cell packaging to mimic sealing of electrode stacks
in pouch cell batteries. The model materials were butyl rubber (latex), viton, and fluorosilicone
rubber (McMaster-Carr product numbers 8633K52, 86075K22, 2183T3). The latex rubber provides
plentiful hydrogen signals for understanding rf signal penetration and collection. The viton and
fluorosilicone contain F nuclei in addition to H, which are advantageous because there are a lot of
materials that have H signals that can contribute stray H signals to the latex when testing the model
materials. There are not as many stray F signals expected in other materials during NMR testing
that could contribute to the F signal from the model material. Also, materials with both H and F
provide the ability to run correlation experiments. The model pouch cells were constructed to be
approximately the size of pouch cells that can be produced in Sandia’s dry room pouch cell line.
The dimensions of the model pouch cells are given in Fig. 2-4.
The first round of model pouch cells consisted of 16 cells and 3 additional control samples, which are
described in Table 1 and photographs are provided in Fig. 2-5. Pouch cells were constructed in two
sizes (see Fig .2-4) and cells 1 and 2 as examples in Fig. 2-5) to understand whether size impacted
the ability to probe the contents of the cell. We were concerned that the larger size pouch cells
would be difficult to fit in the NMR chamber and that they may experience signal non-uniformity
due to the large dimensions, so it was helpful to also examine a smaller size to understand the
sensitivity of the dimensions on signal strength and because the smaller size may have been needed
as a backup geometry if the large size was too difficult to probe. Three materials were used to
examine various NMR-active nuclei (see cells 1, 5, and 7 for example in Fig. 2-5). The packaging
was varied between a polymer-coated Al bag material (Hosen) that is used as standard pouch
cell packaging for batteries (see cell 1 in Fig. 2-5) and a polyethylene bag material (see cell 3 in
Fig. 2-5). The Al packaging consists of Al metal sheets with polymer coatings on each side. The Al
blocks rf penetration so this packaging was anticipated to require a specialized probe, careful pouch
orientation within the electric field, and understanding of electromagnetic field interactions with
the pouch to enable NMR. To serve as a control, we also tested model materials in the polyethylene
bag material because it does not block the electromagnetic field. We tested empty cells (see cells
10 and 12 in Fig. 2-5 to understand the background from the packaging and we tested the model
materials with no packaging as another control (see samples 17-19). Most of these pouch cells

3. MAGNETIC FIELD PENETRATION INTO POUCH CELLS

3.0.1. Heuristic Model

For sinusoidally varying fields, the boundary condition on the magnetic field H on a perfect electric
conductor (PEC—an idealization of high-conductivity metals) is n̂ · H = 0, where n̂ is the unit
vector normal to the PEC surface. That is, the normal component of H is zero on the surface of the
PEC body. At the boundary, a tangential H will induce a current density J = n̂ × H (both J and H
have units of A/m); this latter relation is illustrated in Figure 3-1.

Figure 3-1. An incident tangential magnetic field H induces a current density J on the surface of a perfect
 electric conductor.

Magnetostatics dictates that an induced current density J must “wrap around” the edges of a finite
PEC body (because buildup of electric charge is precluded by the magnetostatic approximation,
i.e., ∇ · J = 0). For example, if a thin conducting plate (such as the metal foil used in a lithium
pouch cell) is immersed in a uniform tangential magnetic field, the same J flows on both the top and
bottom surfaces of the plate as shown in Figure 3-2. Note that the boundary condition J = n̂ × H
is satisfied on both the top and bottom surfaces.

If a second plate is introduced, as seen in Figure 3-3, the essential qualities of the problem remain
unchanged, and a current density J, equal to the incident tangential field H, is induced on both
surfaces of both plates, irrespective of the distance between the plates. A similar condition applies
to a single “C-shaped” plate oriented as shown in Figure 3-2 with respect to the incident magnetic
field vector.

Thus, we see that neither a folded nor non-folded pouch cell geometry will inherently shield a suit-
ably oriented, sinusoidally varying magnetic field from their interior regions. The exact field levels
in the interior regions will, however, depend on some details of the pouch and electrode/separator
stack geometry as described in the next section.

 45

Figure 3-2. The same current density J is induced on both sides of a thin perfect electric conductor plate
 subject to an incident tangential magnetic field H.

  Figure 3-3. Multiple conducting plates, or folding of a single plate as shown, do not alter the essential
 qualities of the problem; a current J is induced which conveys the magnetic field to the interior regions
 as it wraps around the edges of the plate(s).

4. METAL DISSOLUTION IN LITHIUM BATTERIES WITH A
 LOCALIZED HIGH-CONCENTRATION ELECTROLYTE: A CASE
 STUDY

4.1. Introduction

Differences in form factor (e.g., cylindrical cell, pouch cell, coin cell), casing material, and other
passive components (non-active components such as current collectors) affect cycling performance
of secondary batteries. Some effects are attributed to mechanical factors such as applied pressure[2],
and some are attributed to material incompatibilities. A common example of material incompati-
bilities includes the choice of common current collectors for the anode (Cu) and cathode materials
(Al) due to the side redox reactions that occur at the respective potentials of the electrodes. Other
examples of material incompatibility include the stainless-steel cap on the cathode side of coin
cell batteries and the aluminum current collector backing on the cathode with LiTFSI- and LiFSI-
based electrolytes (Li bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide and Li bis(fluoromethanesulfonyl)imide,
respectively.[5, 8, 9, 26, 41, 46, 48, 49] LiFSI-based electrolytes are typically used with next-
generation, high energy density Li metal batteries (LMBs). Material incompatibilities are exac-
erbated at the extremes of voltage windows (0 > voltage > 4.3 V vs. Li/Li+ ), and typically lead
to poor capacity retention. Many of these material incompatibilities are observed only in fully
assembled batteries and are challenging to study using most standard chemical analysis tools due
to variation between samples, sampling location, and additional sample preparation steps like
washing, which may dilute or alter the sample.

A mechanistic understanding of materials incompatibilities would aid in designing mitigations to
eliminate material incompatibilities of all battery components, including passive components, to
develop next-generation, high energy density LMBs. To achieve a high energy density battery
system, high-capacity Li metal anodes are ideally paired with high voltage and high-capacity
cathodes, such as Ni-rich LiNixMnyCo1−x−y O2 (NMC, where 𝑥 ≥ 0.6). Here, we develop an
understanding of materials incompatibilities between LiFSI-based electrolytes and case materials
in coin cells, as indicated by cycling performance and chemical analysis.

A well-documented mitigation strategy to reduce steel corrosion in coin cells is to use Al-coated
stainless-steel cathode caps (Al-clad). [41] The efficacy of the Al coating has been reported to be
dependent on the coating density and thickness. [49] The coating’s effectiveness is also dependent
on the oxide that forms on the Al surface (mostly Al2 O3 and Al(OH)3 ) and the reactivity of the
oxide in the electrolyte. The oxide might also be altered or refreshed through reactions with
the electrolyte during cycling and aging. [49] Aside from Al-clad cathode caps, most Li-based
batteries use Al foil for the cathode current collector. Typical carbonate electrolytes with LiPF6
salts passivate Al, preventing uncontrolled reactions; however, other common salts that efficiently

 53

cycle beyond-graphite anodes (e.g., Li) do not passivate Al. LiFSI improves Li metal cycling
performance but is known to be incompatible with Al current collectors in high voltage cells. [49]
Understanding the interactions between the casing, current collector, and the electrolytes is needed
for development of high energy density LMBs.
High-concentrated electrolytes (HCEs) based on LiFSI salts are reported to enable high Coulombic
efficiency (CE) Li cycling. [30, 37, 47] CE is the ratio of discharge capacity, 𝑄 𝑑 , to charge capacity,
𝑄 𝑐 , such that:  
 𝑄𝑑
                                       CE (%) =           × 100.
 𝑄𝑐
The CE describes the reversibility of plating (charge) and stripping (discharge) when cycling Li
metal, and a CE greater than 99.95% is required for next-generation Li metal anodes to reach
long cycle lives (1000+ cycles). [13] LiFSI-based electrolytes generate LiF-rich solid electrolyte
interphases (SEIs) on the Li metal surface, which are correlated to increased CE. [4, 32] Despite the
promise of these electrolytes, they are reported to have poor electrochemical oxidative stabilities [26,
45] and corrode Al current collectors. [1, 19] These processes lead to capacity fade with cycling,
likely due to parasitic reactions such as electrolyte loss or transition metal dissolution. [1, 19, 26]
Localized high-concentration electrolytes (LHCEs) incorporate an additional solvent that acts as a
diluent, typically a fluorinated ether (e.g., 2,2,2-trifluoroethyl-1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl ether, TTE),
which improves cycling stability when using high voltage cathodes (e.g., Ni-rich LiNixMnyCoyO2 ,
𝑥 ≥ 0.6). LHCEs have decreased viscosity, improved low-temperature performance, and require
lesser quantities of expensive salts (e.g., LiFSI), but can introduce other materials incompatibil-
ities. [34, 38] Reports of sustained cycling at > 4.5 V with TTE electrolytes indicate that the
fluorinated ether helps passivate Al current collectors. [34] These improvements have resulted in
cells with LHCE that cycle Li metal with an average CE of 99.3% over 300 cycles. [38] To achieve
sustained cycling in a Li/NMC cell with LHCE, Al-clad cathode caps are required in place of the
typical stainless steel cathode caps. We aim to understand the incompatibilities between electrolytes
that enable highly efficient Li cycling and stainless steel.
NMC cathodes are typically limited by the oxidative stability of the electrolyte and the tendency
of the transition metals to dissolve, especially when charging to high voltages. Dissolution of the
transition metals results in active material (cathode) loss, which directly causes capacity fade; the
dissolved transition metal ions may also cause a cascade of reactions that lead to further capacity
loss and low CEs. For example, the transition metal ions may deposit/react at the Li anode, creating
the opportunity for electronic pathways that cause further electrolyte decomposition and irregular
Li electrodeposit morphologies. [14]
Several recent studies have investigated transition metal dissolution with various LHCE chemistries.
One study found that TTE diluents lessened transition metal (Ni, Mn, Co) dissolution in an LiTFSI,
LiDFOB, LiBF4 mixed salt electrolyte. [14] The addition of the TTE promoted an Al-rich layer on
the cathode surface, suggesting some amount of corrosion of the Al current collector, which then
later aided in passivation of the NMC. [14] A variety of other solvents have been investigated to
mitigate transition metal dissolution. Chlorinated ethers used as the solvating species are reported
to suppress cathode transition metal dissolution. [43] Additionally, LiDFOB can be used as a
sacrificial additive to help passivate the cathode, suppressing transition metal dissolution. [10,

 54

12, 23] Conversely, certain fluorinated diluents (e.g., tris(2,2,2-trifluoroethyl)borate, TFEB) can
increase transition metal dissolution. [3] Many of these studies correlate a LiF- and/or Li2 O-rich
cathode electrolyte interphase (CEI) with decreased transition metal dissolution. [3, 35, 43]
Many studies focusing on next-generation Li/NMC batteries with LiFSI- or LiTFSI-based elec-
trolytes specify the use of Al-clad cathode caps, but not all studies specify the case material. This
makes understanding capacity fade mechanisms in next-generation battery chemistries difficult to
compare. There are few studies that make direct comparisons between cell casing materials or
investigate mechanisms of case-dependent failure. The casing effects on cycling performance can
be broken down into three main classes: (1) chemical reactivity, (2) dissolution effects, and (3)
effects due to electrochemical potentials caused by dissimilar metals. Additionally, the value of
cutoff voltages, as well as the formation of passivating interphase layers that form and change with
cycling, further complicate the understanding of system component interactions.
Here, we investigate Li metal/NMC batteries with HCE (1.4 mol LiFSI:1 mol DME) and LHCE
(1 mol LiFSI:1.2 mol DME:3 mol TTE). We study the difference in electrochemical cycling
performance in CR2032 coin cells made with stainless steel 316L cathode caps or Al-coated stainless
steel 316L cathode caps (“Al-clad”) through ex situ characterization of cycled cells. Only cathode
caps are coated with Al due to the reactivity of Li metal and Al at low voltages (characteristic of
the anode). We use high-resolution magic-angle-spinning (HR-MAS) nuclear magnetic resonance
(NMR) and X-ray fluorescence (XRF) spectroscopy to investigate changes in cycling performance
as a function of battery components (casing material, electrolyte composition).
To study these casing effects, one must study batteries as a complete system, where there are
many parameters that are difficult to control. Many experimental characterization techniques with
high enough chemical specificity to contribute to a mechanistic understanding are not compatible
with studying batteries in the same form factor where performance is evaluated. Even ex situ
characterization is not trivial due to the complex mixtures, often small sample volumes, and
inevitable perturbation upon battery disassembly.
We use HR-MAS NMR and XRF on separators extracted from cells to study the solution phase
electrolyte at different points during cell cycling. HR-MAS is well-suited to observe the formation
of degradation products and the dissolution of metal ions. HR-MAS measurements can be made
on separators extracted from coin cells. These separators contain less than 20 𝜇L of electrolyte.
To perform similar experiments with standard high-resolution solution-state NMR, the coin cell
components would need to be diluted or washed. Dilution or washing steps necessarily lower the
concentration of degradation products, making them difficult to measure. The XRF complements
the HR-MAS metal measurements by providing element-specific information. We correlate bat-
tery performance in different case materials (stainless steel and Al-clad) with changes in metal
concentration within the electrolyte.
To investigate transition metal dissolution, many reports rely on surface analysis (e.g., X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy, Time-of-Flight Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry, or Inductively
Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-OES) of the cell components). [3, 12, 43]
Although surface analysis and techniques like Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry
(ICP-MS) or ICP-OES provide element-specific information and better detection sensitivities than
the HR-MAS and XRF technique used here, the former relies on the assumption that the transition

 55

metals deposit homogeneously on the electrode surface, and the latter can result in variable transition
metal concentrations. Additional washing steps make it difficult to control the amount of electrolyte
present in the separator and within the pores of the electrolyte; if the volume or mass of the
electrolyte is not known, the precision of the measured concentration is not known, making the
measurement unreliable. The HR-MAS method used here provides a measurement of the bulk
electrolyte/separator material and does not require a washing step, which can introduce error.
Although XRF is location-specific like other surface analysis techniques, we use XRF to supplement
the NMR findings by providing element-specific information and report averages of at least three
points taken on each separator. These methods can be performed with no dilution of the electrolyte
and no change to the cell format. Using these techniques, we monitor degradation processes during
cycling to investigate the effect of casing. We also evaluate the chemical compatibility with different
materials without applied electrochemistry and determine that metal dissolution can occur without
a voltage.

4.2. Methods

4.2.1. Coin Cell Preparation and Testing

All samples were prepared in an argon-filled glovebox (< 0.2 ppm moisture and oxygen). Local-
ized high-concentration electrolytes (LHCE) and high-concentration electrolytes (HCE) were each
prepared to specific molar ratios as defined in the literature (1 mol LiFSI:1.2 mol DME:3 mol TTE
for LHCE and 1 mol LiFSI:1.4 mol DME for HCE). The LiFSI (Oakwood Chemical) was dried for
48 h at 100 ◦ C under vacuum prior to use. The DME (Sigma Aldrich) and the TTE (Synquest Labs)
were each dried over activated alumina for 48 h prior to use. NMC cathodes (LiNi0.6 Mn0.2 Co0.2 O2 ,
NMC622) were purchased from NEI Corporation (NANOMYTE® BE-54E) and dried at 100 ◦ C
under vacuum prior to use. Lithium on copper (50 µm Li laminated on 9 µm Cu) was purchased
from Albermarle and used as received.
Coin cells were prepared using Hohsen coin cell parts (stainless steel 316L, see Table ?? for
316L composition) with the following configuration: two 0.2 mm thick spacers, one 1 mm thick
spacer, one 16 mm diameter Li on copper anode, two Celgard 2325 separators, and one 16 mm
diameter cathode. Al-clad cathode caps (Hohsen) were typical stainless steel 316L cathode caps
with an Al coating, purchased commercially. The Celgard 2325 separators used are trilayer
polypropylene/polyethylene/polypropylene microporous membranes. A total of 80 µL of electrolyte
was added to the cell, which is enough to flood the cell. From this 80 µL, a drop of electrolyte was
added between the Li anode and the 1 mm spacer, and the remaining electrolyte was added to the
separators.
Prior to cycling, cells were rested for 24 h. Cells were cycled at C/10, using the nominal capacity
reported by NEI Corporation (175 mA h g−1 ) between 3 V and 4.3 V. Cells were cycled on Arbin
battery cyclers. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) measurements were conducted in coin cells
using a Biologic VMP-300 potentiostat at a scan rate of 1 mV s−1 .
Cell separators were extracted from disassembled cells after a 24 h rest (with no cycling) and after
1, 5, and 15 complete cycles (after discharge) for NMR and XRF characterization. Cells were

 56

disassembled using a Hohsen de-crimping die for use with the Hohsen automatic crimper in an
argon-filled glovebox (< 0.2 ppm moisture and oxygen).

4.2.2. NMR

NMR samples were prepared by removing Celgard separators from coin cell batteries and inserting
them into Bruker 22 µL Kel-F inserts. The inserts were then put in Bruker 4 mm rotors with
Kel-F drive caps. NMR experiments were performed at 14.1 T using a Bruker Avance II 600 MHz
spectrometer. All experiments were performed using a Bruker 4 mm HR-MAS 1H- 31P- 13C probe.

  F, 7Li, and 27Al were performed on the 1H, 31P, and 13C channels, respectively. Samples were
spun at 5 kHz.

Each NMR sample consisted of extracted separators containing < 10 µL of electrolyte; we used
two Celgard 2325 separators per 2032 coin cell. Our cells were constructed with 80 µL, and the
remainder of the electrolyte was within the pores of the cathode or anode, turned into passivating
layers (SEI or CEI), or squeezed out during assembly. The cells with more extensive cycling

4.2.3. XRF

The separators were characterized with XRF using a Bruker M4 Tornado Plus with x-ray tube,
ixtube-Rh-30-3. The sealed inserts used for NMR, containing the separators, were transferred to a
dry room with dew point < −50 ◦ C, and the separators were removed from the inserts. Data were
acquired with settings of 40 kV tube voltage, 300 µA tube current, and 20 µm spot size. Data at
three different points on each sample were collected, and the XRF data shown are the average.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied to the XRF data to reduce the dimensionality
of the dataset and identify patterns in metal concentrations across different coin cell builds and
cycles. The analysis began with standardizing the metal concentration data to ensure each variable
contributed equally to the PCA. A covariance matrix was then computed to capture the relationships
between the different metals. From this matrix, eigenvalues and eigenvectors were extracted to
identify the principal components. The standardized data was subsequently projected onto the
eigenvectors to form the principal components. The variance explained by each principal component
was calculated to determine the amount of information retained. PCA was implemented using the
‘scikit-learn‘ library in Python, and the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) were plotted
to visualize the data.

4.3. Results

4.3.1. Electrochemical Performance and Characterization

Figure 4-1a-b shows the cycling performance of Li/NMC cells as a function of electrolyte (LHCE
vs. HCE) and case material (Al-clad vs. steel). The cell performance is dependent on the casing
material. Cells made with steel cathode caps show poor capacity retention and sporadic Coulombic
efficiency (CE). Cells with Al-clad caps show stable cycling with little capacity fade over the first
50 cycles. The cycling data shown in Figure 4-1 is the average of 3 cells. Cells with Al-clad cases
cycle with high CE, i.e., the discharge capacity (𝑄 𝑑 ) nears the charge capacity (𝑄 𝑐 ). The Al-clad
casing enables stable cycling in both HCE and LHCE, reaching CEs of 99.9 % and 99.8 % at cycle
50, respectively. The cells with steel cathode caps show capacity fade within the first 5 cycles,
with an average CE value of 88.5(55) % for HCE and 95.4(24) % for LHCE already at cycle 5.
Photographs of cathode caps of cells opened for NMR characterization after cycling 5 or 15 cycles
and the steel caps show evidence of corrosion, in particular with HCE.

Cells made with steel cases have lower CEs than cells made with Al-clad cases; this indicates
the presence of parasitic reactions. Causes of low CE and capacity fade may include electrolyte
decomposition, unstable SEI formation, formation of electronically isolated Li (often referred to
as “dead” Li), loss of active material at the cathode (Ni, Mn, Co dissolution), and case material
dissolution. The steel cells, in particular HCE/steel cells, show a large degree of cell-to-cell
variation as shown in Figure 4-1b. The LHCE/steel cells show capacity fade, suggesting loss of
active material. Conversely, HCE/steel cells exhibit signs of overcharging, with charge capacities
above the theoretical capacity of NMC. For cycling tests, both time (20 h) and voltage (4.3 V)
limits were used, restricting the maximum capacity to values around twice the vendor-reported
capacity. The HCE/steel cells reach the time limit during charge by cycle 37, as evidenced by
the constant, abnormally high charge capacity. High charge capacities could be caused by a short
circuit within the cell, reconnection of dead lithium during cycling, or parasitic reactions due
to materials incompatibilities. Here, the charge voltage stays between 2.9 V and 3.3 V without
reaching the cutoff voltage of 4.3 V. No discharge capacity is delivered following the charge step.
The charge capacity in these later cycles is not due to the expected electrochemical reactions (i.e.,
Li deintercalation from NMC and Li plating at the anode). Instead, the capacity is likely from the
corrosion of the stainless-steel parts within the cell. There is some variability in the cycled cells.
For example, we observe overcharging in some of the LHCE/steel cells that were used for NMR
measurements. The difference in performance between cells used for baseline cycling studies and
those used for NMR measurements highlights the sporadic nature of metal dissolution, corrosion
mechanisms, and the need to study the battery holistically.

There are only a few differences in cycling performance between Al-clad casing with LHCE and
HCE, i.e., with or without TTE, during the first 50 cycles. The reproducibility error is greater with
LHCE/Al-clad than with HCE/Al-clad cells. The positive effect of TTE in Al-clad cell cycling
performance is expected to become apparent only after extended cycling; in one report, deviation
between the HCE and LHCE cycling performance occurred after 120 cycles (for cells cycled
between 2.8 V and 4.4 V).[38]

4.3.2. Chemical Characterization of Extracted Separators

In addition to electrochemical evaluation, we correlate the differences in performance between
cells with changes in the electrolytes from the extracted separators. First, we use ex situ HR-MAS
NMR to track the electrolytes with chemical resolution. This technique is ideal for this study
because of its ability to detect solutions (electrolyte) within a porous solid (separator). [6] Regular
high-resolution solution-state NMR would not work well for these separator samples because the
solid matrix would cause undesired magnetic susceptibility broadening and residual anisotropies
(chemical shift and dipolar) that would severely limit resolution. The MAS reduces the magnetic
susceptibility broadening and reduces the residual effects of anisotropies caused by the separator
material. Liquid-state NMR measurements could be used if the separators and cell components
were washed in a solvent first, but this step would considerably perturb the sample and make
low-concentration impurities difficult to observe. In these experiments, we assume the electrolyte
in the separator is representative of the electrolyte throughout the cell.

 60

Electrolyte control samples consist of separators that were saturated in electrolyte but not assembled
into batteries. The control spectra collected with HR-MAS NMR are shown on the top row of
Figure 4-2, and the spectra show narrow, well-resolved lines. We resolve and assign all major
electrolyte components in the 1H, 19F, and 7Li spectra. The solid separator material is mostly
invisible in our HR-MAS experiments compared to the mobile electrolyte components.
NMR allows us to monitor the chemical composition of the electrolyte in the separator from various
cell configurations. In the 1H and 19F spectral data, we have the sensitivity and dynamic range to
observe impurities with concentrations as low as 100 mmol and 10 mmol, respectively. Through
HR-MAS NMR, we can also indirectly track the accumulation of paramagnetic species in the elec-
trolyte solutions. Paramagnetic ions result in spectral broadening and changes in nuclear relaxation
rates. By measuring spin-lattice (𝑇1 ) and spin-spin (𝑇2 ) relaxation times, we qualitatively track
the paramagnetic metal ion concentrations and collect information concerning the identities of the
metals. [31] Metals have varied electron 𝑔-tensors and therefore affect the nuclear spins differently
through the electron-nuclear hyperfine coupling. The sensitivity to different paramagnetic metals
is variable; however, µmol sensitivity to many of the metals in steel and NMC is expected.
The ex situ HR-MAS separator measurements do have some limitations. While the relaxation rate
analysis qualitatively reports on paramagnetic metal ion dissolution, making accurate quantitative
claims when there are multiple metal ion types is difficult. We are only sensitive to the degradation
products and metal dissolution species that are present in the separator, where we would expect
additional degradation products to be present at the interfaces of each electrode. Regardless,
tracking degradation products within the separator allows us to evaluate the cell performance as a
function of electrolyte and case material.

4.3.2.1. Cycled Cell Measurements

The NMR data of cycled cells has trends that are consistent with the electrochemical data (Figure 4-
2). We cycled cells 1, 5, or 15 times, and then extracted separators within 24 h of final discharge. In
the Al-clad samples, we see only small shifts and minimal broadening as compared to the control
samples. This is consistent with electrochemical data, which shows stable cycling in Al-clad casing.
These results indicate that there is little metal dissolution or electrochemical breakdown. At later
cycles, we see more broadening in LHCE/Al-clad than HCE/Al-clad. This result is also supported
by the NMR relaxation data, where we see faster relaxation in the LHCE samples than the HCE
samples. In contrast, the steel samples show considerable broadening from paramagnetic metal
species with increased cycling; this is also reflected in the relaxation rates.
Here, we do not show the TTE peaks in the presented NMR spectra; the volatility of TTE makes
quantification ex situ difficult (vapor pressure is 4.8(11) kPa at 295 K). [20] We conducted experi-

we also see TTE degradation. We see evidence of TTE degradation even after immediate transfer,
with degradation peaks appearing in both the 1H and 19F spectra. The degradation products are
most prevalent after 2 min, and the peaks (both TTE and degradation peaks) are nearly gone after

The analysis of metal concentrations from XRF reveals the variable dissolution of metals in different
case materials and electrolytes. Here, we use Principal Component Analysis (PCA), a statistical
method that simplifies multivariate datasets by identifying variability structures and grouping
similar samples. This allows us to better understand how metal concentrations vary across different
case materials and electrolytes as the batteries cycle. PCA indicates a clear separation of LHCE/steel
cells, particularly at higher cycle numbers, suggesting substantial variation in metal concentrations
compared to other cell types and electrolytes. The LHCE/steel cell shows higher concentrations
of Ni, Fe, and Cr after 0 cycles than other cell constructions (Figure 4-3). After one cycle, Fe,
Cr, and Ni concentrations decrease, indicating that the initial steel dissolution that occurred before
cycling was followed by the integration of dissolved steel ions into electrode interphases or other

 63

components, as shown by XRF of the lithium metal. After later cycles (5–15 cycles), there is an
increase in Fe, Ni, and Cr, but not a sustained increase (for both 5 and 15 cycles) in Co or Mn,
suggesting the formation and disruption of a passivating layer on the steel.

HCE/steel cells exhibit less metal dissolution compared to LHCE/steel cells, although the levels
generally increase with cycling. This indicates that the HCE may facilitate the formation of a more
robust passivating layer, at least initially, on the steel surface. With both electrolytes, the Al-clad
cells cluster together in the PCA plot. The clustering of the Al-clad cells from the PCA analysis
indicates that the LHCE and HCE have similar amounts of metal dissolution at low cycle numbers.
Fe is detected in all sample types, despite being present only in the steel and not expected in the
NMC cathode material. This Fe ion dissolution likely comes from steel sources from the anode
side or from incomplete passivation by the Al coating on the cathode cap.

Pairwise correlation analysis of the XRF data reveals correlations between metals, suggesting their
sources of dissolution. Pairwise correlation analysis is used to identify potential relationships
between different variables to help in identifying the potential source of the metal dissolution; here,
these variables are the different metals measured in the XRF spectra. Since Mn and Ni are present
in both the steel casing (2.0 % and 11.3 % mol, respectively) and the NMC cathode material, it
is difficult to determine the source of either element. If NMC was the source of the Mn and Ni
dissolution, we may expect a slope of 6/2 = 3 for Mn vs. Ni; however, if steel was the source of the
Mn and Ni, then we may expect a slope of 11.3/2 = 5.6 for Ni vs. Mn. If the slopes in the pairwise
correlation analysis do not agree with the expected value calculated for a given metal source, it
could be because (1) more than one component is dissolving and/or (2) the elements within the
steel or cathode do not dissolve at the same rate. For either of these cases, we would expect a slope
that is some other value that does not match 3 or 5.6. We note that this estimate for determining the
source of metal dissolution is imperfect as certain transition metals are typically more susceptible
to dissolution (e.g., Ni from NMC); however, there are some instances where NMC is reported to
dissolve in its stoichiometric ratios. [15, 29]

In LHCE/steel samples, Mn and Ni have slopes near the stoichiometric ratios if NMC dissolution
was uniform, as seen in the table assuming NMC dissolution. Cr and Fe are also strongly correlated
in LHCE/steel samples with the expected slope for stoichiometric 316L steel dissolution. This
suggests that LHCE dissolves both steel and NMC. The data indicates that both LHCE and the
steel cathode cap are necessary for dissolution of NMC. The LHCE/Al-clad cells do not show large
amounts of NMC dissolution, which is in agreement with the cycling results. Steel dissolution is
evident in all samples, suggesting incomplete Al-cladding of the cathode cap or dissolution from
other steel components in the coin cell.

XRF was performed on the Li metal anodes (50 µm Li on Cu) after 5 cycles and 15 cycles to identify
the presence of metals on the electrode. At 15 cycles, the LHCE/steel sample had the highest levels
of metals (Cr, Ni, Mn, and Fe) on the Li metal surface, likely sourced from the steel. At 5 cycles,
the LHCE/Al-clad sample had the greatest amount of Cr, and all samples had comparable amounts
of Fe. This suggests that all samples are corrosive to the steel, even with the Al-cladding present
on the sample. All values are relative to Cu since the Cu was relatively consistent, whereas sulfur
varied greatly, likely depending on how much salt remained on the surface. XPS was measured on
Li anodes after 5 cycles for HCE and LHCE with both case types and after 15 cycles for HCE with

both case types. No transition metals from the NMC cathode or the steel case were observed in
XPS measurements, despite small quantities of Fe and Cr measured in the XRF.

The XRF data aligns with both the cycling and NMR data, showing that Mn, the most paramagnetic
metal ion, is present in solution, with pairwise correlation analysis suggesting that NMC is the
source of the Mn. These results highlight the different dissolution behaviors in various coin cell
builds and emphasize the role of both electrolyte type and cell case material in influencing metal
dissolution and passivation.

4.3.4. Chemical Incompatibility and System-Level Effects

Next, we evaluate whether the observed metal ion dissolution is caused by cell cycling or due to
chemical instabilities between the electrolytes and the cathode or case material by tracking metal
dissolution in non-cycled systems. We test whether the chemical dissolution is dependent on the
presence of other system components, for example: does the presence of steel increase the rate of
NMC dissolution?

In separators taken from cells that were assembled and then disassembled after 24 h without
cycling (0-cycles), we observe small differences in chemical shift positions and line broadening in
the NMR spectra. The observed peak broadening is due to the introduction of paramagnetic metal
ions (which are only present in the NMC and the steel). The observed changes at rest could be due
to the chemical incompatibilities between the metal and the electrolytes, causing metal dissolution
(corrosion), or because of the electrochemical potential across the cell as-built (nominally 3 V). To
distinguish between these mechanisms, we measured electrolyte-filled separator samples that were
in contact with either only the NMC cathode (NMC-only) or a stainless-steel spacer (steel-only)
for 24 h in a sealed polypropylene container, without any other cell components.

We observe line broadening for the LHCE/NMC-only and LHCE/steel-only samples, suggesting
that both steel and NMC are dissolved by the LHCE while at rest. In contrast, for the HCE, we
see more broadening with the samples from built cells that were rested for 24 h (HCE/Al-clad
and HCE/steel) than in the HCE/NMC-only and HCE/steel-only samples. For the HCE/steel-
only sample, we observe almost no broadening or changes in relaxation times, which suggests
minimal paramagnetic metal dissolution. Conversely, we observe peak broadening in spectra of
the HCE/NMC-only sample, likely due to dissolution of the paramagnetic transition metals within
the NMC. The HCE cells (HCE/Al-clad and HCE/steel) exhibit more broadening compared to the
HCE/NMC-only sample, which is attributed to the combination of the steel cell parts with the NMC
cathode and the potential across the cell. In the LHCE cells (LHCE/Al-clad and LHCE/steel), the
effect of the cell on metal dissolution is convoluted by the chemical instability observed with the
LHCE/steel-only and LHCE/NMC-only samples.

We see some shifts in the spectra caused by paramagnetic ion dissolution, for example in the
LHCE/steel cell (Figure 4-4a). The observed DME and LiFSI shifts are associated with unexpected
𝑇1 and 𝑇2 relaxation times. We also see shifts that correlate with TTE breakdown/evaporation for the
LHCE samples, for example as shown in LHCE/NMC-only and LHCE/steel-only in Figure 4-4b.

4.4. Discussion

The NMR data shows little-to-no electrolyte (LiFSI, DME) degradation, but we do see paramagnetic
metal dissolution, as evidenced by line broadening, shifts, and relaxation measurements. These
NMR samples are separators and not electrodes; therefore, any electrolyte degradation products that
are trapped at the interface, or that make up the CEI or SEI, are not detected. We observe breakdown
of TTE in cycled samples, but it is difficult to unequivocally distinguish between TTE evaporation
and degradation, considering the identified decomposition product, 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropanol,
will further decompose to form a gas (tetrafluoroethylene). The dissolution of metals increases
with cycling and is roughly correlated to electrochemical performance. Even in cells made with Al-
clad cathode caps, we see characteristics of paramagnetic metals that can be attributed to other steel
parts within the cell and the NMC. XRF reveals the paramagnetic metal identities in the separators
and generally corroborates the NMR data (except for HCE/Al-clad cycle 5, which deviates from the
NMR and electrochemistry data); however, deconvoluting the source of the paramagnetic metals
due to the overlap of Ni and Mn in both steel and NMC is difficult.

Overall, the XRF data supports more steel dissolution in LHCE/steel than HCE/steel. PCA of the
XRF data clearly shows that LHCE/steel cells have substantial variance in metal concentrations,
consistent with the variable cycling data. We also see more NMC dissolution with LHCE/steel
cells compared to HCE/steel cells. This NMC dissolution is most clear in the correlation analysis.
The XRF correlation analysis reveals Mn, Ni, and Co levels in the electrolyte that match their
ratios in the NMC cathode, suggesting NMC dissolution with cycling. LHCE cells made with steel
cathode caps exhibit higher metal concentrations compared to cells made with Al-clad cathode
caps, with elevated Fe, Cr, and Ni suggesting these metals are sourced from the steel. From the
NMR measurements, cells made with steel cathode caps have greater amounts of paramagnetic
metals compared to those made with Al-clad cathode caps. The pronounced dissolution of NMC
components (Ni, Mn, Co) in LHCE/steel samples points to instability between the steel and LHCE
and possible galvanic corrosion. This underscores the critical role of casing, cathode materials,
and electrolyte on battery stability and performance. Furthermore, the presence of Fe, Cr, and Ni
in the XRF spectra of the LHCE/Al-clad cells indicates the general instability between LHCE and
steel. This suggests that steel dissolution comes from steel components on the anode side of the
cell or imperfections on the Al-clad cathode cap. [49] Additional protective barriers may further
reduce dissolution of steel when in the presence of electrolytes with TTE additives and/or LiFSI.

Though many studies incorporating LiFSI-based chemistries with high-voltage cathodes employ
Al-clad cathode caps, not all studies specify the use of Al-clad versus steel casing. This incon-
sistent reporting may contribute to irreproducibility in results from different studies—particularly
regarding capacity fade and metal dissolution. For example, Li et al. observed overcharging during
cycling and substantial Ni, Mn, and Co dissolution via inductively coupled plasma atomic emission
spectroscopy (ICP-AES) with LHCE when cycled in Li/NMC811 cells, [23] but the case material
used is not mentioned in the report. In a separate study by Ren et al., Li/NMC811 cells cycled in
Al-clad cases with the same LHCE did not observe overcharging (though the voltage ranges studied
are slightly different in this study). [13] Our results show a chemical instability between LHCE and
steel, even without an applied potential in a cell. We also observe overcharging and large quantities

 68

of transition metal dissolution in LHCE/steel cells. Understanding the interactions between case
material and electrolyte is necessary to interpret results.
Although Al-clad cases decrease transition metal dissolution, they are not a one-size-fits-all solution
to case material incompatibilities. For example, graphite/NMC811 cells built with Al-clad cathode
caps show worse cycling performance with 1.2 LiFSI EC:EMC 3:7 (wt:wt) compared to cells with
1.0 LiPF6 EC:EMC 3:7 (wt:wt). [49] The poor performance with the LiFSI-based electrolyte was
attributed to galvanic corrosion between the steel cathode cap and its Al-coating. This suggests that
galvanic couples can form if there are imperfections in the Al-coating (either native to the coating
itself or due to corrosion of the coating) on Al-clad cases. Galvanic couples may also form between
Al current collectors and steel cases if the Al current collector is not adequately passivated.
From our data, increased steel dissolution is correlated with increased NMC dissolution. We
hypothesize that transition metal dissolution due to materials incompatibilities between the LHCE
and steel casing disrupts the CEI and leads to additional metal ion dissolution from the NMC
itself.

4.5. Conclusion

We analyzed metal dissolution in Li/NMC batteries using electrochemical data, XRF, and NMR
to explain the stability and performance of different cell builds and electrolytes. The results reveal
significant metal dissolution in steel cells, particularly in LHCE/steel samples, where NMC cathode
dissolution leads to substantial Mn, Ni, and Co in solution. Results suggest that both steel casing
and LHCE contribute to the degradation process. The XRF data explicitly shows dissolution of
steel in both HCE and LHCE.
Our ex situ NMR method is useful for analyzing extracted separators without dilution or changing
the cell form factor. The HR-MAS method is practical and, to our knowledge, has not been used for
this application. This method may be widely useful for studying electrolyte degradation in cells.
These findings challenge the perceived benefits of TTE in the LHCE. TTE has been identified as a
major factor in the dissolution of metal components within the coin cells. Our data indicate that TTE
exacerbates metal dissolution and may lead to parasitic reactions that affect battery performance.
This includes both electrochemical incompatibilities that have been considered previously, as well
as newly identified chemical instabilities with stainless steel. We also see rapid TTE loss from
opened cells (potentially due to evaporation, degradation, or a combination of both), which may
influence variability in performance. This highlights the need to reconsider the use of TTE in
certain cell builds and to explore alternative diluents or additives. Incompatibilities with metal
components are important not just for coin cells but also for larger format cells with metal casings
or current collectors.
This study emphasizes the importance of considering all cell components—casing materials, cath-
ode materials, and electrolytes—in the design of batteries. The integration of these components
has an impact on the overall stability and performance of the cells. The findings highlight the
critical role of material selection and analytical techniques in optimizing the performance of bat-
teries. Future research should focus on identifying and testing new electrolyte formulations that

 69

minimize both steel and cathode material dissolution, thereby improving the overall performance
and longevity of batteries.

5. ADDITION OF TRANSITION METALS TO LI BATTERY
 ELECTROLYTES

5.1. Introduction

Metal dissolution can occur in batteries due to corrosion of the inactive materials (e.g., casing
materials or current collectors) or due to degradation of the cathode materials. As discussed
previously, these degradation processes can lead to significant performance losses, most notably
poor capacity retention and deviations in discharge voltage. Understanding the mechanisms and
impacts of metal dissolution is crucial for improving battery performance and longevity.
The different metal dissolution processes can adversely affect battery performance through the
following ways:
 1. Structural changes to the cathode causing voltage fade
 2. Accelerated growth of SEI at the anode and irreversible Li+ consumption
 3. Electrolyte decomposition
 4. Pitting corrosion
 5. Binder destabilization and delamination
 6. Dissolution of electrode active materials
All of these processes can lead to capacity loss. Current collector (Cu) dissolution can also
create a risk of forming short circuits due to redeposition. In the case of binder destabilization,
this process can also lead to disconnection between the electrodes and the current collectors.
Recently, significant efforts have been focused on crosstalk, where the degradation process at one
electrode (transition metal dissolution at the cathode) leads to the transition metal species causing
a secondary degradation process at the other electrode (transition metal redeposition at the anode).
The capacity loss resulting from these processes can degrade the performance and reliability of
batteries in practical applications.
To simulate these processes, we conducted metal spiking experiments to simulate the dissolution
of metal ions in the electrolyte. The metal ions chosen were based on materials within the cell and
within the cathode to simulate the dissolution and include Fe, Mn, Ni, Cu, and Al. Similar studies
have been previously conducted with Li-ion-based systems, where a drop in capacity was observed
with the addition of transition metal-based salts into the electrolyte. These studies suggested that
the added metals were taking Li+ insertion sites within the anode, which led to decreased capacity
and increased SEI (electrolyte degradation and resistance increase).
The following goals drove this work:

 77

 1. Identify the impact of trace metal contaminants on battery performance
 2. Identify which dissolved metals have the greatest impact on battery performance
 3. Determine NMR signatures associated with dissolved metals
Goals 1 and 2 were to supplement the work discussed in the previous section concerning the
dissolution of transition metals as a result of casing incompatibilities. Goal 3 was intended to be
used as a diagnosing technique where the NMR signal could be used to diagnose transition metal
dissolution in situ.

5.2. Methods

5.2.1. Electrolyte Spiking

Electrolytes were based on the LHCE electrolyte described in previous chapters, 1 mol LiFSI:1.2 mol
DME:3 mol TTE (mol:mol:mol). Initial experiments incorporated triflate-based metal spiked salts
at varying concentrations. These experiments were replaced later with metal TFSI-based salts,
which are more similar to the FSI anion that the base electrolyte uses. To streamline this sec-
tion, the triflate-based metal spiking experiments are excluded from the electrochemical results
section.
Two types of metal-spiked electrolytes were made. First, electrolytes were made by mixing the
desired quantity of the metal salt in order to reach a desired concentration of the metal ion (e.g.,
5 mmol, 10 mmol, 50 mmol of the salt). Second, electrolytes were made by controlling the mole
ratios such that the metal salt concentration was constant; however, given the variation in oxidation
state of the metals, this resulted in different concentrations of TFSI anion in the electrolyte. These
were made at the following ratios: 0.97 mol LiFSI:0.03 mol M TFSI:1.2 mol DME:3 mol TTE
(mol:mol:mol:mol). These electrolytes are estimated to have a concentration of 50 mmol of the
spiked metal.
Non-Li metal salts were used as received due to initial drying causing the salts to become discolored.
We note that many of the salts exhibited poor solubility in the electrolyte, so these experiments
are imperfect. Additional experiments, not shown here, where additional DME was added to the
electrolyte did not fully solubilize the electrolytes. To simplify these nuances, the experiments
were conducted keeping the target concentrations or mole ratios the same. The metal salts used for
spiking experiments are outlined in Table 5-1.

5.3. Electrochemistry Results and Discussion

5.3.1. Metal-Spiked Experiments

Metal spiking electrochemical experiments started with assembling cells with the metal TFSI salt
added to LHCE at predefined concentrations. A control experiment with LiTFSI was conducted to
determine whether the TFSI anion negatively impacted the cycling performance of the Li/NMC622
cells. This is shown in Figure 5-1, where control cells containing only LHCE were cycled and
compared to the Li-spiked sample. For this experiment, the LiTFSI was added based on mole ratios
such that the resulting mole ratios of the electrolyte were 0.97 mol LiFSI:0.03 mol LiTFSI:1.2 mol
DME:3 mol TTE, or about 50 mmol LiTFSI.
As shown in Figure 5-1, the LHCE control sample does have a slightly lower discharge capacity
compared to the Li-spiked sample. This result is not intuitive but is likely representative of
differences in how the electrolyte was made. We also note that the initial capacities are slightly
off, so this may also be attributed to some error with weighing one of the samples. Regardless, the
Li-spiked sample does not appear to experience any significant deviations in cycling performance as
compared to the control. There are some slight drops in discharge capacity, which could suggest a
small amount of parasitic side reactions as a result of the addition of the TFSI anion, but otherwise,
the TFSI-based metal salts perform with adequate behavior to investigate the effect of the metal
addition to the electrolyte.
Low concentrations of metal salts expected to be present in solution were investigated in Li/NMC622
cells. The first experiments were conducted using Al, Mn, and Fe. Al is commonly used as a current
collector for the cathode and is reported to corrode with LiFSI- and LiTFSI-based electrolytes
(though the addition of TTE is believed to mitigate Al corrosion by forming an improved passivation
layer on the current collector surface). Fe and Mn are both present in stainless steel, and Mn is
present in the cathode. These cycling results are shown in Figure 5-2 and are compared to the same
baseline, or control, data shown in Figure 5-1.
From these results, there is not an obvious trend or deviation observed. The Fe-spiked cycling
appears around as stable as the control cycling. The 1 mmol Mn-spiked data shows a deviation in
early performance, but the later cycles are stable. The low capacities observed in early cycling are
not observed when the Mn concentration is increased to around 10 mmol. The Al-spiked cycling
shows the greatest deviation, but as before, this is only early on during cycling, and the cells cycle
stably through the extended cycling. These results suggest the possibility of the metals depositing
on the anode during the first few charge cycles, which could potentially mask any adverse effects of
the spiked metal sample. The other potential issue is that the concentrations were fairly low in these

5.3.3. Frankenstein Experiments

The cells built with the metal-spiked electrolytes all started on a charging step in which Li+ ions
would be reduced at the anode, plating Li metal. Because Li metal has a more negative reduction
potential than the other metals investigated, it was hypothesized that one flaw with the above
study was that the metal ions would be reduced on the first cycle and that, because there is an
excess of Li metal at the anode, any capacity losses as a result of this parasitic process would be
obscured. In cells where the effects of metal dissolution were observed, each charge step caused
the dissolution of metal ions as a result of corrosion processes. This hypothesis was tested through
testing “Frankenstein” cells.

The Frankenstein cells were made by cycling a Li/NMC622 cell 2.5 times (ending on a charge

With the 10 mmol experiments, a small initial drop was observed with the Fe-spiked electrolytes
immediately after rebuilding the cell, but the Frankenstein cells were able to cycle stably for the
remaining cycles. One of the cells with the 50 mmol experiments experienced a significant drop in
capacity for the six cycles following the cell rebuild. The other cell did experience a slight drop in
capacity, though not as dramatic. Both cells recovered from the initial capacity loss, with a small
amount of capacity fade observed with later cycling.

The Frankenstein experiments were promising: a capacity loss was observed with the metal-spiked
electrolytes after the cells were rebuilt, and cycling did recover (again, likely in part due to the
excess Li metal in the cell). However, additional analysis could not verify our hypothesis that
the metal ions were reduced during the first charge step (e.g., first cycle dQ/dV analysis, the CV
analysis shown previously), and the cells were able to cycle stably over time.

5.5. Conclusions

Li/NMC622 cells were cycled with electrolytes containing added metal salts to investigate which
dissolved metals were the most detrimental to cell performance. Ultimately, the metal-spiking
experiments were not successful. The metal salt solubility limitations within the electrolytes made
analysis of the data difficult and inconclusive. The cycling experiments generally resulted in
relatively stable cycling.
With these experiments, metals were intentionally added into the electrolyte, which is not repre-
sentative of processes within the cell. When metal dissolution occurs in the cell, the metals are
dissolving with each cycle, so there can be a continuous generation of metal ions. Beyond this, we
wanted to determine the effect of the addition of the metals on the cell cycling performance. The
cause of metal dissolution during cycling is due to ongoing parasitic redox reactions, which will
already be represented within the charge/discharge profiles.
While the results were largely inconclusive, cycling experiments did suggest that cells with Fe and
Cu added to the electrolytes at relatively high concentrations resulted in instabilities later on in
cycling that were not observed with the control experiments. Mn- and Al-spiked electrolytes had a
more detrimental effect, but in some cases, this was as extreme as near-zero capacities throughout
all cycles, which may be more likely related to the salt’s solubility versus a parasitic process that
causes cell failure over time.

6. DETECTION OF NI DISSOLUTION USING XPS

6.1. Introduction

Transition metal dissolution, whether due to materials incompatibilities or cathode degradation, is
a parasitic process that can lead to premature battery failure. In studying casing effects, described
in earlier sections, we found that it was difficult to determine the source of the transition metal
dissolution. Battery researchers commonly rely on techniques like XPS for surface characterization
of electrodes, including for diagnosing transition metal dissolution.
In studying transition metal dissolution ourselves, we found that it was easy to misdiagnose Ni
metal dissolution when the signal was due to the presence of F. Considering the widespread
use of F-containing species in batteries—from salts, to solvents, to binders—we anticipate this
overlap could inflate the reports of Ni metal dissolution. This is particularly pertinent for high-
Ni content cathodes for high-voltage batteries, which are of particular interest to next-generation
battery chemistries. Work focused on the overlap between F Auger lines and the Ni 2p peak has
been studied before in the context of battery research, and other researchers have reported using
different peaks instead of the Ni 2p peak to quantify Ni metal dissolution for this reason. The
work reported here further highlights these previously discussed points with the aim of serving as
a cautionary tale when evaluating transition metal dissolution in battery chemistries.

6.2. Methods

6.2.1.      Materials

Solvents (NMP, DOL, DME, all purchased from Sigma Aldrich) were dried over activated alumina
for at least 48 h prior to use. LiTFSI salts were purchased from Ossilla and dried at 100 ◦ C under
vacuum for at least 24 h prior to use. Ni(TFSI)2 (Alfa Aesar) was dried under vacuum in a glovebox
antechamber (not heated) prior to use. NMC622 powder was purchased from NEI Corporation and
used as received. Solvay 5130 PVDF was purchased and used as received. SuperP carbon black
was purchased from Alfa Aesar and used as received. Commercial pre-cast NMC622 electrodes
were purchased from NEI Corporation and dried at 100 ◦ C under vacuum overnight prior to use.
Materials were stored and handled in an Ar-filled glovebox unless otherwise noted.
Electrolytes were prepared by measuring out the calculated amount of salt to reach the specified
molarity (0.5 for Ni(TFSI)2 and 1 for LiTFSI, to keep the TFSI content constant). The solvents
were added in equal volumes to achieve a 50:50 (v:v) mixture of DOL:DME. The solutions did not
account for the volume change associated with the salt dissolving, so the molarities reported here
are estimations.

 87

The cathode samples were made by mixing an 80:10:10 mixture of NMC622:PVDF:SuperP (w:w:w)
in NMP. The solution was 25 % solids by mass. The target loading was 1.3 mAh/cm2 . The active-
free samples were made by making a 50:50 (w:w) mixture of PVDF:SuperP in NMP. The resulting
solution was 6.3 % solids by mass. Films were cast onto Al foils using a doctor blade coater under
ambient conditions. The cathode and active-free samples were then dried at 75 ◦ C to remove the
remaining NMP and then dried at 100 ◦ C under vacuum overnight.

6.2.2. Coin Cells and Electrodeposition Experiments

Coin cells were built in an Ar-filled glovebox using Hohsen coin cell parts (stainless steel 316L)
and an automatic Hohsen coin cell crimper. All cells were built with one wave spring, two 0.2 mm
spacers, one 1 mm spacer, two Celgard 2325 separators, and approximately 80 µL of electrolyte.
The Li used in cells was 50 µm Li laminated on a 10 µm Cu current collector. The Cu substrates
were 16 mm in diameter and 9 µm thick. The substrates were etched in 1.2 HCl, followed by rinsing
with 18 MΩ water and acetone.

Electrodeposition was conducted galvanostatically using a Biologic VMP3 Multichannel potentio-
stat. The electrodeposition experiments were conducted at 0.5 mA/cm2 for 1 mAh/cm2 . Unfor-
tunately, during these experiments, the current range was too high for appropriate current control,
and the actual current and capacity deviated from 1 mAh/cm2 by about 10 %. The voltage during
electrodeposition was limited to −1 V.

6.2.3. X-Ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)

XPS measurements were taken using a Kratos Axis Supra XPS system with a monochromatic Al
K𝛼 X-ray source operating at a base pressure of 2 × 10−9 or lower. Samples were transferred to the
SEM using an inert transfer arm to prevent ambient air exposure. Data was analyzed with CASA
XPS by aligning carbon spectra to 284.8 eV and using the relative sensitivity factors for the Kratos
system, where F1s is set to 1.

6.2.4. Scanning Electron Microscopy/Energy Dispersive X-Ray Spectroscopy
 (SEM/EDS)

Samples were loaded onto an SEM stub inside an Ar-filled glovebox, sealed in a mason jar, and then
transferred from the glovebox to an inert glovebag that was sealed around an SEM (Zeiss Supra
55VP). The glovebag was purged with Ar, and then the jar was opened, and the stub was loaded
into the SEM, which was then pumped down. Samples were imaged between 2 keV and 5 keV at
a working distance of 4 mm. An Oxford X-Max 80 EDS detector provided elemental information
for the samples.

7. SPIN COUNTING VS. COULOMB COUNTING FOR CHARGE
 ANALYSIS IN LITHIUM BATTERIES

7.1. Introduction

In operando Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy of Li secondary batteries can be
used to quantify the Li content in different environments as a function of cycling. The quantity of Li
in the cathode or anode can then be related to the charge of the battery. In this work, we use NMR to
estimate battery charge in a anode-free 2032 coin cell batteries. We explore the conjecture that the
difference in charge determined by potentiostatic measurements and by the NMR measurements
provides information about both reversible and parasitic processes that are otherwise difficult to
disentangle.
We show that this is a promising approach.
