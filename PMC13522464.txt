Multi-omic analysis of androgen-deprived human testes reveals Sertoli cell regulation and niche reprogramming
Authors: Yifei Guo, Kongrong Xu, Qianyin Zhou, Lin Du, Chenxin Dang, Guangmin Liu, Xingyuan Yi, Dai Zhou, Jian Cao, Zhizhong Liu, Liqing Fan, Xiaoyan Wang, Jingtao Guo, Hao Bo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Testis development and male fertility depend on androgens, yet the mechanisms underlying their regulation of adult human testicular function remain poorly defined due to ethical and technical constraints. To address this, we leverage androgen-deprived adult human testes as a spontaneous model and construct a comprehensive multi-omics atlas integrating single-nucleus RNA sequencing, single-cell ATAC-seq, and spatial transcriptomics. Our analyses highlight androgen-regulated transcription factors critical for Sertoli cell function and identify Neuregulin 2 (NRG2) as an androgen-dependent niche factor secreted by Sertoli cells that promotes spermatogonial proliferation both in vivo and in vitro. We further observe that androgen deprivation triggers ectopic expression of the Sertoli cell marker WT1 in the testicular interstitium, reprogramming a subset of interstitial cells toward a Sertoli-like transcriptomic state. Together, these findings uncover molecular mechanisms of androgen regulation in the adult human testes and provide a valuable resource for advancing therapies for androgen-related infertility and reproductive disorders.

Introduction

Androgens are essential to establish and maintain the normal function of the testes in adult human1,2. These hormones are produced by Leydig cells in the testicular interstitium and act through the androgen receptor (AR), which is expressed in most testicular somatic cells, including Sertoli cells, peritubular myoid cells, endothelial cells, and Leydig cells3–5. Upon binding androgens, AR regulates target gene transcription through classical and non-classical pathways to sustain cell function6–9. AR activation regulates transcriptional programs that sustain cellular function, whereas disruption of androgen signaling through AR mutations or androgen deficiency impairs Sertoli cell maturation and leads to the loss of spermatocytes and spermatids10,11. Clinically, this underlies reproductive disorders such as androgen insensitivity syndrome and late-onset hypogonadism1,2,12–14. Understanding how androgen deficiency alters testicular regulation is critical for developing effective therapeutic strategies.

Spermatogenesis relies on a specialized regulatory niche formed by somatic cells, particularly Sertoli cells, which maintain the spermatogonial stem cell (SSC) pool through paracrine signaling. These signals direct spermatogonia to either self-renew15,16 or enter meiosis17,18, ultimately producing haploid sperm19. Androgen signaling is essential for sustaining this niche, yet its effects on testicular somatic cells appear to differ across species20–24. This species-specific variation limits the translational relevance of animal studies and highlights the need for human-specific models to investigate androgen regulation in the testis.

Recent advances in multi-omics technologies have expanded our knowledge of the human testis25–29, but key barriers remain. Mature Sertoli cells are exceptionally large and are often underrepresented in single-cell RNA sequencing datasets, leaving their regulatory programs incompletely defined. In addition, ethical and technical constraints prevent direct experimental manipulation of androgen signaling in the adult human testis, making it difficult to define androgen-dependent mechanisms in vivo. Patients with prostate cancer taking GnRH agonists to receive pharmacological androgen deprivation therapy (ADT). GnRH agonists bind to GnRH receptors, inhibiting the HPG axis and reducing androgen levels to near zero, thus creating a physiological androgen deficiency model. This model offers a unique opportunity to investigate how androgens regulate testicular somatic cells and the spermatogonial niche while overcoming the translational limitations of animal studies and the technical constraints of existing datasets.

Here, we applied single-nucleus RNA sequencing, single-cell ATAC-seq, and spatial transcriptomics to generate a comprehensive multi-omics atlas of androgen-deprived human testes. This approach allowed robust capture of Sertoli cell nuclei and revealed androgen-regulated transcription factors that control downstream processes, including endocytosis. These functionally impaired Sertoli cells also diminished secretion of NRG2, a factor that can promote spermatogonial proliferation both in vivo and in vitro. Furthermore, we discovered a subset of testicular interstitial cells in ADT-treated testes that began expressing the Sertoli cell marker WT1 and acquired a Sertoli-like transcriptomic state. In summary, our study provides insights into androgen-dependent regulation in the adult human testis and offers a multi-omics resource that may support future research on androgen-related reproductive disorders.

Results

Androgen deprivation alters the composition of adult human testes

To investigate the impact of androgen deprivation on human testes, we obtained testicular samples from four prostate cancer patients undergoing ADT with luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone (LHRH) agonists. Samples from six individuals without ADT treatment served as controls (CTL). Histological examination revealed that ADT-treated seminiferous tubules exhibited thicker walls and vacuolated lumens compared to CTL, suggesting their incomplete spermatogenesis (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1a). To define the underlying cellular and molecular changes, we performed snRNA-seq and scATAC-seq. Specifically, snRNA-seq was conducted on samples from four ADT-treated and six control individuals, while scATAC-seq included three ADT-treated and five control samples, with one additional control dataset from a previously published study30. To preserve spatial context, we also applied spatial transcriptomic profiling to three control and two ADT samples using the 10x Visium HD platform (Fig. 1b). Following quality control to remove low-quality nuclei and multiplets, our final dataset comprised 80,866 high-quality nuclei (51,753 control; 29,113 ADT) for snRNA-seq and 49,929 nuclei (29,308 control; 20,621 ADT) for scATAC-seq. We began by integrating the snRNA-seq datasets and applying batch correction, followed by dimensionality reduction using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) (Fig. 1c). The robustness of the dataset was validated by the specific expression of established marker genes for major testicular cell types (Fig. 1d). The sequential expression of UCHL1 (spermatogonia), SYCE1 (spermatocytes), and PRM3 (spermatids) confirmed the successful capture of the complete spermatogenic lineage within the data. Somatic cell populations encompassed immune cells (expressing CD163 or PTPRC), endothelial cells (expressing PECAM1 and CDH5), and PDGFRA+ testicular interstitial cells, which comprised ACTA2 + /MYH11+ peritubular myoid cells and a distinct cluster of IGF1+ cells (Fig. 1d). Furthermore, we clearly captured a population of steroidogenic CYP17A1 + /STAR+ Leydig cells and a substantial population of WT1 + /SOX9+ Sertoli cells (Fig. 1c, d), which have been difficult to identify in previous single-cell RNA-seq studies25,29,31.

We observed a distinct reconstitution of testicular cell populations following ADT (Fig. 1e). Spermatogenesis was severely disrupted, and somatic cell composition shifted with an expansion of immune cells and mesenchymal cells, alongside the loss of Leydig cells (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Spatial transcriptomics corroborated these findings, showing well-organized seminiferous tubules in the CTL sample with strong expression of TNP1 (spermatids) within tubules and CYP17A1 (Leydig cells) in the interstitium, both of which were disrupted in the ADT sample (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 1d). Instead, the ADT testicular interstitium was populated by aggregated immune cells. Notably, we detected focal areas of high IGHG1 expression, indicating recruitment of plasma cells following ADT (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 1e). In summary, we captured abundant spermatogenic niche cells, particularly Sertoli cells, and delineated the reorganization of testicular cells after androgen deprivation, including impaired spermatogenesis, an increase in immune and mesenchymal cells, accompanied by a decrease in Leydig cells.

Transcriptional state of human Sertoli cells following androgen deprivation

We first focused on the transcriptomic changes in Sertoli cells, the only somatic cell type in seminiferous tubules31. After ADT, Sertoli cells showed a marked decrease in both the number of detected transcripts and expressed genes (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Principal components analysis (PCA) revealed significant changes in gene expression profiles in ADT-treated Sertoli cells (Fig. 2a). To assess the functional status of these cells, we evaluated the expression of established adult Sertoli cell marker genes27, which were significantly downregulated in the ADT group (Supplementary Fig. 2b), suggesting a potential loss of mature Sertoli cell function. In contrast, genes enriched in immature Sertoli cells, such as BMPR2, GATA4, and AMH, were upregulated following ADT (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 2b). To further explore these changes, we performed pseudotime trajectory analysis on Sertoli cells from both groups. ADT-treated cells were positioned at an earlier point along the pseudotime axis, indicating a less mature transcriptional state that gradually progressed toward the more developed profile seen in the CTL group (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 2c). Along this trajectory, expression of genes associated with ‘tissue remodeling’ and ‘embryonic organ development’ declined, whereas genes involved in ‘phagocytosis’, ‘regulation of reproductive processes’, and ‘metabolic activity’ increased (Fig. 2e). We also quantified Sertoli cell maturation using previously defined gene sets associated with Sertoli cell development and differentiation. Cells from the ADT group exhibited significantly lower maturation scores than those in the control group (Supplementary Fig. 2d). In addition, we compared the gene expression patterns observed along the ADT-to-control pseudotime trajectory with published scRNA-seq data spanning human testis development from infancy through adulthood25–27 (Supplementary Fig. 2e). The trajectories displayed similar trends: genes associated with immature Sertoli cells, such as BMPR2 and GATA4, decreased in expression over time, while markers of mature Sertoli cells, including CITED1 and DEFB123, progressively increased (Fig. 2f).

Finally, we observed a significant reduction in the expression of known AR target genes32 in the ADT group (Fig. 2g), suggesting that the observed transcriptional changes were likely driven by disrupted AR signaling. In brief, these findings indicate that androgen deprivation may partially reverse the developmental state of adult Sertoli cells, shifting them toward a more immature phenotype.

Identification of androgen-related transcription factors in human Sertoli cells

To clarify androgen-related transcriptomic regulation, we analyzed chromatin accessibility in testicular tissue using scATAC-seq. Samples were obtained from three ADT-treated patients and five control patients, yielding a total of 49,929 high-quality cells for downstream analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Dimensionality reduction and clustering revealed distinct cell populations that closely matched those identified in our snRNA-seq dataset. Cell type annotations were confirmed by the predicted expression of known marker genes based on chromatin accessibility at promoter regions33. We then identified cell type–specific regulatory regions using MACS2. We identified TFs with differential motif accessibility between the ADT and control groups. Most TF motifs showed reduced accessibility following ADT, with only a few, such as WT1, showing increased accessibility (Fig. 3b). Notably, the motif of AR exhibited a significant decrease in accessibility in ADT-treated Sertoli cells (Supplementary Fig. 3c), implicating impaired AR signaling as a key driver of the observed transcriptional changes. Other key TF motifs, including NR3C2, SOX4, SOX9, and NFE2L1, also showed marked reduced accessibility following ADT (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 3c).

Further, we integrated scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq datasets and applied SCENICplus34 to jointly analyze gene expression and chromatin accessibility in Sertoli cells. Regulons governed by NR3C2, SOX4, SOX9 and NFE2L1 were specifically enriched in control cells, but not in those from ADT group (Supplementary Fig. 3d), indicating that these regulons are likely regulated by androgen signaling. Based on these findings, we constructed Sertoli cell-specific regulatory networks driven by androgen-responsive TFs (Fig. 3c). Each TF controlled a distinct set of target genes, which showed significantly reduced expression in the ADT group (Supplementary Fig. 3e).

To further validate the function of the identified TFs, we used the snRNA-seq/scATAC-seq data to simulate the knockdown effects of each key TF in Sertoli cells separately. After 10 iterations, the transcriptomic state of Sertoli cells in the CTL group shifted towards a profile similar to that of the ADT group (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 3f). As NR3C2 had the highest score calculated by SCENICplus (Supplementary Fig. 3d), we performed siRNA-mediated knockdown of NR3C2 in human primary Sertoli cells (Fig. 3e). The primary Sertoli cells were isolated and purified from control testicular tissue35,36. After purification, we employed PCR and immunofluorescence to assess the expression of Sertoli cell markers (Supplementary Fig. 3g, h). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that over 95% of the cells were positive for Sertoli cell markers WT1 and SOX9, as well as the somatic cell marker VIM. In contrast, markers associated with myoid cells (ACTA2), Leydig cells (CYP11A1), and germ cells (DDX4) showed minimal positivity, confirming the high purity of the Sertoli cell population. Following knockdown, the expression of genes associated with mature Sertoli cell identity and function—such as CITED1, PRND, and DEFB123, which are also downregulated in the ADT group—was significantly reduced (Fig. 3f). These results highlight the critical role of NR3C2 in human Sertoli cell function.

Androgen-related TFs regulate phagocytic function of human Sertoli cells

We then explored the biological functions of genes regulated by androgen-related transcription factors. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed significant enrichment in pathways related to organic compound metabolism, membrane transport, positive regulation of endocytosis, and cell-cell adhesion (Fig. 3g). These results indicate that androgen-responsive TFs play a central role in regulating key Sertoli cell functions, including metabolic activity37, maintenance of the blood–testis barrier (BTB) through intercellular junctions38,39, and phagocytosis of apoptotic germ cells40. Supporting this, the expression of endocytic genes such as AP2B1 and APOA1 were significantly reduced following NR3C2 knockdown in the cultured Sertoli cells, as well as in ADT transcriptomic datasets (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 3i).

For further validation, we treated the cultured primary Sertoli cells with 1 nM testosterone for 48 hours and measured their phagocytic capacity using fluorescently labeled latex beads (Fig. 3h and Supplementary Fig. 3j). Testosterone-treated group showed a significant increase in the number of beads uptake by Sertoli cells, indicating enhanced phagocytic activity41,42 (Fig. 3i). In summary, our results demonstrate that the activity of TFs such as NR3C2, SOX4, SOX9, and NFE2L1 is reduced in human Sertoli cells following ADT. Androgens support essential Sertoli cell function, such as phagocytosis40, by regulating the expression of downstream target genes through these TFs.

Impaired function of spermatogonia in androgen-deprived human testes

As the only somatic cell type in direct contact with germ cells, Sertoli cells play a central role in regulating spermatogonial stem cell (SSC) fate through paracrine signaling, balancing differentiation into sperm or self-renewal to maintain the stem cell pool15. To investigate the role of androgens in this regulatory process, we conducted a focused analysis of germ cell populations. Using established marker genes, we refined the annotation of germ cell subtypes (Fig. 4a, b). Spermatogonia were divided into two subpopulations: undifferentiated spermatogonia, marked by the expression of undifferentiated marker genes (PIWIL4 and GFRA1), and differentiating spermatogonia, characterized by the expression of differentiation marker KIT and proliferation marker MKI67. Spermatocytes were further classified into early and late stages based on the gradient expression of meiotic genes (SYCP3, SPO11) and zona pellucida binding proteins (ZPBP, ZPBP2), and by the absence of the round spermatid marker ACRV1. In the ADT group, an adequate number of undifferentiated spermatogonia and a few differentiating spermatogonia were preserved, while both early and late spermatocytes were nearly absent (Fig. 4a). This suggests that androgen deprivation can disrupt the progression of spermatogonia into meiosis. Notably, a few spermatocytes and even some spermatids were still detectable in the ADT group (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Spatial transcriptomic analysis further supported these observations. The spermatogonial marker ID4 was highly expressed in seminiferous tubules from both the control and ADT groups, while the meiotic marker SYCP3 was only present in a small fraction of tubules in the ADT group (Supplementary Fig. 4b). This suggests that meiotic activity is restricted to a few isolated tubules rather than being broadly maintained after ADT.

We next assessed the proliferative capacity of spermatogonia. Cell cycle analysis revealed that ADT-treated spermatogonia had a higher proportion of cells in the G1 phase and a reduced proportion in mitosis, indicating a decline in proliferation (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 4c). A similar pattern was observed in the spatial transcriptomic data, where G1-phase spermatogonia were more prevalent in the ADT sample (Supplementary Fig. 4d). In brief, androgen deprivation impairs the differentiation of spermatogonia into spermatocytes and diminishes the proliferative activity of the remaining spermatogonia.

Androgen-dependent signals promote spermatogonia proliferation in vivo and in vitro

To determine whether the impaired spermatogenesis in ADT samples is driven by Sertoli cell dysfunction, we performed CellChat analysis43 to examine somatic–germline communication. In the ADT group, the overall number of intercellular interactions was significantly reduced, particularly those between Sertoli cells and germ cells (Supplementary Fig. 4e). Specifically, PTN, ACTIVIN, and NRG signaling pathways originating from Sertoli cells and targeting spermatogonia were absent following ADT (Fig. 4d). Previous studies have shown that ACTIVIN signaling from Sertoli cells promotes spermatogonial differentiation in rodents44, while PTN, secreted by Leydig and endothelial cells, also supports SSC function in mice45. However, the role of NRG2 in spermatogonial regulation remains unclear. In our dataset, NRG2 expression was strongly reduced in Sertoli cells following ADT (Supplementary Fig. 4f). Immunohistochemistry confirmed that, in control human testes, NRG2 was predominantly localized within seminiferous tubules, whereas ADT samples showed a pronounced reduction (Fig. 4e). This decrease was further supported by western blot analysis, which demonstrated lower NRG2 protein levels in ADT testes (Fig. 4f). Together, these findings indicate that androgen deprivation disrupts Sertoli-to-germ cell signaling and identify loss of NRG2 as a candidate contributor to spermatogonial dysfunction.

We next used the mouse spermatogonial cell line GC-1 as an in vitro model to evaluate the role of NRG2 in regulating spermatogonial function (Fig. 4g). Treatment with 10 ng/mL recombinant NRG2 significantly increased the number of DNA-synthesizing cells (Fig. 4h, i). Consistently, CCK-8 assays showed enhanced cell viability after 48 hours of NRG2 treatment (Fig. 4j). We then assessed apoptosis levels in NRG2-treated and control cells and found no significant difference between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 4g, h). Taken together, these results indicate that Sertoli cells support spermatogonial proliferation through secretion of factors such as NRG2 in vivo and in vitro. This regulatory mechanism may be androgen-dependent, as androgen deprivation leads to reduced NRG2 expression in Sertoli cells, ultimately impairing spermatogonial function and disrupting spermatogenesis.

Androgen deprivation triggers ectopic WT1 expression in the testicular interstitium

The testicular interstitium contains various cell types that constitute an important part of the spermatogenic niche46. To understand how these cells respond to androgen deprivation, we performed a focused analysis of testicular interstitial populations. We identified four distinct sub-clusters (Fig. 5a), including ACTA2+ myoid cells and CYP17A1+ Leydig cells (Fig. 5b). Following ADT, the proportion of myoid cells increased, while Leydig cells were significantly depleted (Supplementary Fig. 5a). IF for ACTA2 confirmed that seminiferous tubules in the ADT group were surrounded by a greater number of myoid cells (Fig. 5c), which may contribute to the thickening of the tubule wall. Despite this increase in cell number, myoid cells exhibited downregulation of genes associated with muscle contraction (Fig. 5d), indicating a loss of functional competency. Similarly, in Leydig cells, the expression of androgen biosynthesis genes (STAR, CYP11A1, CYP17A1) was significantly reduced after ADT, despite an increase in LH receptor expression (Supplementary Fig. 5b). This suggests that LHRH agonist treatment disrupts androgen biosynthesis, uncoupling hormonal stimulation from steroid production. We also identified a cluster of mesenchymal cells. The expression of PDGFRA, DCN, and SFRP1 confirm their mesenchymal identity (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 5c). These cells additionally expressed genes characteristic of embryonic testicular interstitial cells26 (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Spatial transcriptomic analysis revealed the expansion of this population following ADT. While genes marking these cells were restricted to peritubular regions in CTL, became broadly expressed throughout the interstitium, filling the space vacated by lost Leydig cells in ADT testis (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Fig. 5d).

Unexpected, we identified a cluster of cells co-expressing the testicular interstitial marker PDGFRA and the supporting cell marker WT1 in ADT-treated testes, which was negligible in the CTL group (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Given their dual-lineage marker expression, we first sought to clarify their spatial localization. CellTrek47 predicted that these PDGFRA + /WT1+ cells reside in the interstitium (Fig. 5f), which was further confirmed by IF. The PDGFRA was expressed on the cell membrane, while WT1 was localized in the nucleus. The corresponding bright-field images illustrate their co-expression within individual cells (Fig. 5g). We then performed a joint analysis integrating these PDGFRA + /WT1+ cells with interstitial and Sertoli cells. The PDGFRA + /WT1+ cells were casted between Sertoli cells and mesenchymal cells on the diffusion map (Fig. 5h), suggesting a hybrid transcriptomic state. This was supported by correlation analysis, which revealed a higher transcriptional similarity to Sertoli cells than to other interstitial populations (Supplementary Fig. 5e). GO analysis indicated that genes upregulated in these cells were associated with processes including ‘macromolecule methylation’, ‘developmental cell growth’, and ‘JNK cascade’ (Supplementary Fig. 5f). The expression of genes encoding AP-1 proteins within the JNK signaling pathway, such as FOS and JUN, were also significantly higher in ADT group (Supplementary Fig. 5g)

For further validation, we identified the mesenchymal cells in the scATAC-seq datasets based on chromatin accessibility at promoters of key genes (e.g., DCN, NR2F2, IGF1) and low predicted expression of the myoid cell gene MYH11 (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Fig. 5h). The chromVAR revealed that AR motif accessibility was generally lower in ADT mesenchymal cells. Intriguingly, a portion of ADT mesenchymal cells exhibited elevated accessibility of WT1 motif (Fig. 5j). These results suggest that androgen deprivation induces ectopic expression of the supporting lineage marker WT1 in the testicular interstitium, leading to a transcriptomic shift of a subset of interstitial cells towards a Sertoli-like identity.

Discussion

Testosterone deficiency can cause male hypogonadism1. Due to ethical and technical constraints, the mechanisms of androgen acting on adult human testes remain poorly understood. Here, we profiled testicular nuclei from patients undergoing ADT using single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq). This approach successfully captured a robust representation of the testicular somatic cell compartment, establishing a critical platform to dissect the regulatory roles of androgen in the human testes. Furthermore, by integrating snRNA-seq with scATAC-seq and spatial transcriptomics, we have constructed a comprehensive multi-omic atlas of the human testes under androgen deprivation, serving as a valuable resource for unveiling novel therapies for androgen-related reproductive disorders.

Sertoli cells, the only somatic cell type in direct contact with germ cells, regulate nearly every step of spermatogenesis15,48. During puberty, androgens drive the maturation of Sertoli cells from a proliferative, immature state into differentiated adult state49. Mature Sertoli cells execute vital functions within the seminiferous tubules, such as establishing the blood-testis barrier to create an immunoprivileged niche, metabolic supporting for germ cells, and phagocytosing apoptotic germ cells24. Our results showed that upon androgen deprivation, mature Sertoli cells exhibit a transcriptional regression toward an immature state, demonstrating that androgens are essential not only for driving Sertoli cell maturation but also for maintaining their mature state and biological functions.

The most important role of Sertoli cells is to support spermatogenesis through paracrine signaling50. However, the role of androgens in regulating this process remains incompletely characterized. Our findings identified several Sertoli cell-derived signaling pathways, including PTN, ACTIVIN, and NRG, are received by spermatogonia and are likely mediated by androgens. Deprivation of androgens leads to loss of these signals and impairment of spermatogonial proliferation. Specifically, we demonstrate that NRG2 promotes spermatogonial proliferation in vitro without compromising cell viability, offering a promising strategy for supporting in vitro gametogenesis51. Previous murine studies indicate that PTN is secreted mainly by endothelial and Leydig cells and signals through SDC receptors to regulate spermatogonial stem cell functions45. In contrast, our data from human testes showed that PTN originates from Sertoli cells and act via NCL receptor, suggesting the species-specific difference in the regulation of spermatogonial fate. Spermatogonial proliferation is a complex process likely regulated by multiple signaling pathways. Optimization of culture conditions for long-term expansion of human spermatogonia will require additional studies and more physiologically relevant human models. Furthermore, although meiosis is largely absent in ADT-treated testes, we still detected several tubules expressing meiotic markers. The mechanisms enabling these tubules to resist systemic androgen deprivation remains to be investigated.

The expression of WT1 is known to direct testicular somatic progenitors differentiate toward the supporting lineage, while its absence promotes differentiation into interstitial cell types52–54. In ADT-treated testes, we identified a population of PDGFRA + /WT1+ cells in the interstitium, suggesting that some testicular interstitial cells may acquire a supporting cell-like identity following androgen deprivation. A growing body of evidence indicates that the adult testicular interstitium exhibits greater heterogeneity and plasticity than previously recognized. For example, proliferating myoid progenitors have been identified in adult porcine testes55, as well as a subpopulation of mesenchymal cells in the interstitium of adult mice testes has been shown to contribute to somatic cell development56. In our data, we discovered a distinct population of PDGFRA+ mesenchymal cells that express genes characteristic of embryonic mesenchyme and exhibit increased chromatin accessibility of WT1 motif following ADT. These findings suggest that the PDGFRA + /WT1+ cells observed in ADT-treated testicular interstitium may originate from these mesenchymal cells. Previous studies have highlighted a regulatory interaction between WT1 and AR57–59, with WT1 expression found to be higher in androgen-insensitive cell lines compared to androgen-sensitive ones60. Additionally, co-activation of WT1 and AP-1 proteins has been shown to induce WT1 gene expression in leukemic K562 cells61. These findings provide indirect proposes of potential mechanisms driving WT1 ectopic expression. More precise understanding needs to be further investigated.

All ADT samples were obtained from patients treated with common clinical androgen-suppressing drugs such as Goserelin, Leuprorelin, or Degarelix Acetate. These medications work by competitively binding to GnRH receptors, inhibiting the excitatory effects of endogenous GnRH on the pituitary gland, which reduces the production of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH), and directly lowers testosterone levels. While this represents an inherent limitation of our clinical model, it remains the most widely accepted method for androgen suppression. Dissecting the direct effects of androgen loss from indirect consequences of reduced gonadotropins will require future studies using more refined experimental models.

We also identified additional components of the spermatogenic niche such as immune and endothelial cells. Their responses to androgen warrant further investigation. Collectively, our results offer mechanistic insights into androgen regulation in human testis and establish a foundational model for uncovering the spermatogenic niche function and cellular reprogramming under hormonal deficiency.

Methods

Sample collection and ethics statement

Testicular samples in the control group were obtained from patients undergoing partial scrotectomy for scrotal Paget’s disease, epidermal cysts, or testicular tumors, as well as from patients with prostate cancer undergoing bilateral orchiectomy for androgen deprivation. Informed consent was obtained from each patient before surgery.

Control testicular samples have normal testicular pathology, complete spermatogenesis, and no evidence of pathological conditions (such as testicular tumors) affecting the testicular tissue. Participants had no history of exposure to reproductive toxins, normal testosterone levels, and testicular volume within the normal range. Furthermore, they had not received treatments known to affect spermatogenesis, such as radiation, chemotherapy, or ADT, prior to surgery. Their BMI was under 30.

Testicular samples in the ADT group were collected from prostate cancer patients who underwent orchiectomy following long-term androgen deprivation therapy using gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonists. Their testicular tissue showed no pathological signs related to prostate cancer. These participants had no history of exposure to reproductive toxins. Their testicular volume was significantly reduced, and testosterone levels were markedly below the normal range. Testicular pathology revealed abnormalities in spermatogenesis. These individuals had undergone only ADT treatment prior to surgery and had a normal reproductive history. Their BMI was also under 30. The detail information for samples see Supplementary Data 1.

These human samples were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Cancer Hospital of Xiangya School of Medicine, Central South University (NO. SBQLL-2020-138) and the Ethics Committee of Reproductive and Genetic Hospital of CITIC-Xiangya (NO. LL-SC-2021-025). All of these samples were in-house. The ethics committees approved the study protocols and informed consent was obtained for sharing clinical data that may allow identification of the patients. No payments were made to donors and the donors knowingly and willingly consented to provide research materials.

Sample fixation for immunostainings

The testicular samples were fixed in Bouin’s solution for 24 h and subsequently washed three times with 0.01 M PBS (10 min per wash) to thoroughly remove residual Bouin’s solution. Following fixation, the tissues were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series and gradually transitioned into xylene. Specifically, the samples were sequentially immersed in 50, 75, 80, 95, 100%, a 1:1 mixture of absolute ethanol and xylene, xylene I, and xylene II. Each solution incubation lasted between 30 and 50 min, with the exact duration adjusted based on ambient temperature to ensure optimal dehydration and clearing. Finally, the cleared tissues were embedded in paraffin blocks.

Immunohistochemical staining

The tissue embedded in paraffin was sliced, and the sections were then placed onto slides and heated at 65 °C for 1 h. Tissue sections were processed using an HRP/DAB Detection IHC Kit (Abcam, #ab64264) with minor modifications to the manufacturer’s protocol. The paraffin sections were deparaffinized sequentially with xylene I, xylene II, a 1:1 mixture of xylene and ethanol, 100% ethanol, 100% ethanol, 95% ethanol, 85% ethanol, 70% ethanol, and 50% ethanol, each for 5 min, followed by three 5-min washes with PBS under shaking. Antigen retrieval was performed in Tris-EDTA buffer using microwave heating: 3 min at high power until boiling, followed by 15 min at low power, and then natural cooling. After three additional 5-min PBS washes, the tissue areas were circled with a hydrophobic pen. Endogenous peroxidase was blocked using the kit’s hydrogen peroxide block for 30 min at room temperature, followed by PBS washes. The kit’s protein block was applied for 30 min and then gently removed without washing. After blocking, the sections were incubated with the primary antibody anti-NRG2 (AiFang Biological, #AF06657, 1:100) at 4 °C overnight. The slides were rewarmed for half an hour, washed with PBST three times for 5 min each, and then incubated with the kit’s biotinylated goat anti-polyvalent secondary antibody for 15 min. After PBST washes, streptavidin peroxidase (from the kit) was applied for 10 min. Following PBS washes, DAB development was performed using the kit’s chromogen/substrate at a 1:50 dilution; staining was monitored microscopically and stopped by immersion in distilled water. Counterstaining was performed with hematoxylin, followed by differentiation in 1% acid alcohol and bluing in tap water. Finally, the sections were dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol and xylene, and mounted with neutral resin.

Immunofluorescence staining

The paraffin sections were deparaffinized with xylene, respectively xylene 1, xylene 2, xylene and ethanol 1:1 mixture, 100% ethanol, 100% ethanol, 95% ethanol, 85% ethanol, 70% ethanol, 50% ethanol each for 5 min, followed by 5 min of PBS washing on a shaker. After that it was used for about 300 ml of 1×Tris-EDTA (pH 9.0, supplemented with Tween 20 to 0.05%) antigen repair solution, heated in a plastic dye box in a microwave oven on high for about 5 min, and then stopped heating after boiling, put the slices into the microwave oven, and repaired on low fire for 5 min, and then took out the slices and examined and opened the lid to dissipate the heat for a few seconds and then continued to be repaired on low fire for 10 min. When closing, use a histochemical pen to draw a circle slightly larger than the boundary of the tissue, and then add 60 μl of 5% BSA/PBS to the surface of the tissue, and close the wet box at room temperature for 30 min. Dilute the primary antibody to the corresponding concentration with the closure solution and then add 60 μL of the primary antibody anti-ACTA2 (#ab7817, Abcam, 1:800) to the surface of the tissue, and hybridize in the wet box at 4 °C overnight. Remove and rewarm for half an hour, wash with PBST (0.1% Tween 20) on a shaking table for 3 × 5 min, then dilute the secondary antibody with PBST to the corresponding concentration and add 60 μl drops to the surface of the tissue, and hybridize in the wet box at room temperature for 1 h. Wash with PBST (0.1% Tween 20) on a shaking table for 3 × 5 min. After washing, the liquid on the slide surface was blotted out, and 15 μl of sealer (containing DAPI) was added to the surface of the tissue, coverslip was applied, and the slides were sealed with nail polish.

For multiplex immunohistochemistry, staining for WT1 (#ab89901, 1:100) and PDGFRA (#ab203491, 1:100) was performed using a 4-color multiple fluorescent immunohistochemical staining kit (Absin, #abs50028) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

For cell staining, cultures in confocal dishes were fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min, and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 hour. Cells were then incubated with primary antibodies: anti-SOX9 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #14-9765-82, 1:300), anti-CYP11A1 (Cell Signaling Technology, #14217, 1:300), anti-DDX4 (Abcam, #ab27591, 1:100), anti-VIM (ProMab BiotechnologiesInc., # P20777, 1:200), anti-SCF (ProMab BiotechnologiesInc., #32239, 1:500) at 4 °C overnight, and corresponding Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (CoWin Biosciences, #CW0103/#CW0102, 1:2000) for 1 hour with DAPI nuclear counterstaining (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #D1306, 1:1000). All samples were washed with PBS (3 × 5 min) between steps, and antibody incubations were followed by PBST washes.

Western blot

Human testicular tissues stored at −80 °C were placed in ice‑precooled RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors at a 1:50 ratio. After adding the lysis buffer, the tissues were homogenized using a pre‑cooled low‑temperature oscillatory disruptor for 300 s. The homogenate was briefly centrifuged to assess the extent of tissue lysis, and the disruption step was repeated 3–5 times until complete lysis was achieved. Subsequently, the lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay. Total protein was mixed with 5X instant protein loading buffer (Yeasen, LT101S) at a 1:4 ratio and denatured at 100 °C for 10 min. Proteins were separated by SDS‑PAGE at 90 V for 20 min followed by 130 V for 45 min, and then transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore) at a constant current of 200 mA for 90 min. The membrane was blocked with 5% skimmed milk in TBST at room temperature for 1 h, followed by incubation with rabbit anti‑human NRG2 primary antibody (proteintech, 20690‑1‑AP) overnight at 4 °C. After washing three times with TBST (10 min each), the membrane was incubated with HRP‑conjugated goat anti‑rabbit IgG at room temperature for 1 h and washed again three times with TBST (10 min each). The blot was visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence reagent, and images were captured with a ChemiDoc MP system. Band intensities were quantified with ImageJ software and normalized to Vinculin as the loading control.

Isolation and culture of primary human Sertoli cells

Primary human Sertoli cells were isolated from adult testicular tissues using a two-step enzymatic digestion protocol followed by differential plating. Briefly, minced testicular fragments were washed multiple times in DMEM to remove blood cells. The tissue was first digested in DMEM containing 2 mg/ml Collagenase Type IV (Gibco, #17104019) and 10 µg/ml DNase I (Sigma, #DN25-1G) at 34 °C with gentle shaking at 100 rpm for 10–15 min until seminiferous tubules were released. Tubules were washed extensively via natural sedimentation to remove interstitial cells. A second digestion was performed using a solution containing 4 mg/ml Collagenase Type IV, 2 mg/ml Hyaluronidase (Sigma, #H3506-5G), 2 mg/ml Trypsin, and 10 µg/ml DNase I at 34 °C for 15–20 min until the tubules became translucent and dissociated. Digestion was terminated with 10% FBS, and the cell suspension was mechanically dissociated, filtered through a 40-µm strainer, and centrifuged. The pellet was resuspended in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS. Cells were seeded onto 0.2% gelatin-coated dishes and cultured at 34 °C overnight. Non-adherent germ cells were removed by medium change the next day. Adherent Sertoli cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin and passaged at 80% confluence at a 1:3 ratio.

Cell phagocytic capacity detection

The phagocytic capacity of Sertoli cells was evaluated using fluorescently labeled latex beads. After 48 h of treatment with 1 nM testosterone (Selleck, #S4757), cells cultured in 24-well plates were gently washed and incubated with 6 µL of fluorescently labeled latex beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #F8825) per well in 500 µL fresh medium for 24 h at 34 °C under 5% CO₂. Following incubation, cells were washed twice with PBS (10 minutes per wash) to remove non-internalized beads. Cells were then fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 2 min, and blocked with 1% BSA in PBS for 30 min. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #H1399) for 10 min. After three 5-minute washes with PBS, images were acquired using fluorescence microscope. The method for enumerating phagocytic activity involved counting the number of fluorescently labeled beads in each field of view and matching them with the corresponding bright-field images to eliminate any microspheres outside the cells. The total number of beads was then divided by the number of Sertoli cells in each bright-field. The average number of beads per Sertoli cell in each field was used to quantify their relative phagocytic capacity.

GC-1 cell culture

The GC-1 cell line was obtained from Procell (Wuhan, China). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. The medium was refreshed every 48 h. Upon reaching approximately 85% confluence, cells were either passaged or subjected to subsequent experimental procedures.

Detection of cell proliferation and apoptosis

EdU assay was performed using the Click-iT EdU Imaging Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, #C0071S). After 48 hours of treatment with recombinant NRG2 (MCE, #HY-P74682), GC-1 cells were incubated with 300 µL of pre-warmed 2× EdU working solution for 2 h at 37 °C. Cells were then fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min at room temperature and washed three times with washing buffer. Permeabilization was carried out using 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 minutes, followed by two additional washes. The Click reaction was performed with 70 µL of Click Additive Solution per well under light-protected conditions for 30 min. After three washes, nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 for 10 min. GC-1 cell proliferation was assessed by calculating the ratio of EDU-positive cells to total cells in the field of view.

Cell viability was detected by Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) Assay. Following 48-hour NRG2 treatment, GC-1 cells were trypsinized using 0.25% EDTA-trypsin, counted, and seeded into a 96-well plate at 1000 cells per well with five replicates per group. After overnight culture, the medium was replaced with 120 µL of CCK-8 working solution per well and incubated for 90 min at 37 °C. Then, 110 µL of the reaction mixture from each well was transferred to a new 96-well plate, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Measurements were taken daily for three consecutive days. The average optical density (OD) values were used to generate proliferation curves relative to the control group.

Apoptosis was detected using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (APE x Bio, #K200311133A132). After 48 h of NRG2 treatment, cells were digested with EDTA-free trypsin, centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 mins, and washed once with cold PBS. A total of 2 × 10⁵ cells were resuspended in 500 µL of 1× binding buffer containing 5 µL Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL propidium iodide (PI), followed by incubation in the dark for 15 min at room temperature. Samples were analyzed immediately using a flow cytometer (FongCyte™-Challenbio) with 530 nm and 620 nm channels for FITC and PI detection, respectively. Data were processed with FlowJo software to quantify apoptotic populations.

siRNA transfection

Primary Sertoli cells were first detached using trypsin digestion. Subsequently, 2 × 10⁵ cells were seeded into each well of a 6‑well plate. When the cell confluence reached approximately 70%, the culture medium was replaced with fresh DMEM/F12 complete medium (Thermo, United States, #C11330500BT). Lipofectamine™ 3000 transfection reagent (Thermo, United States, #L3000015) was then mixed with either NR3C2‑targeting siRNA (target sequence: CAGCUAAGAUUUAUCAGAA) or non‑targeting negative control siRNA (NC) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, incubated at room temperature for 20 min, and added to each well. After 48 h of incubation in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator at 37 °C, cells were harvested for subsequent experiments. All siRNAs were designed and synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China).

RNA extraction and qRT‑PCR

Total RNA was extracted following the instructions of the Trizol LS Reagent (Thermo, United States, #10296028CN). This procedure employed a combination of chemical lysis, RNase inhibition, phase separation, and alcohol precipitation. The quality and quantity of total RNA were assessed using an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent, United States). One microgram of total RNA was reverse‑transcribed into cDNA using the First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Roche, Switzerland, #04897030001). The cDNA was diluted by adding four volumes of nuclease‑free water. Subsequently, 2 µL of the diluted cDNA template was mixed with 10 µL of SYBR Green qPCR Mix (ABclonal, China, #RK21203), 0.4 µL each of forward and reverse primers, and 7.2 µL of nuclease‑free water. The qPCR was performed on a LightCycler480 Real‑Time PCR Detection System (Roche, United States). The 2 − ∆∆CT approach was used to quantify the PCR products and GAPDH was used as an internal control. Primer sequences were listed in Supplementary Data 2.

Nuclei isolation for sequencing

The collected tissues were washed by 1× PBS, then quickly frozen and stored in liquid nitrogen. Nuclei extraction was separated by mechanical extraction method62. Firstly, put the tissues into 2 ml Dounce homogenizer set and thawed in homogenization buffer (containing 20 mM Tris pH8.0, 500 mM sucrose, 0.1% NP-40, 0.2U/μL RNase inhibitor, 1× protease inhibitor cocktail, 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA), and 0.1 mM DTT. Use Dounce pestle A to grind the tissue 10 times, filter with 70 μm cell filter, and then grind with Dounce pestle B 10 times, filter with 30 um cell filter. Centrifuge at 500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to pellet the nuclei, and resuspend in the blocking buffer containing 1% BSA and 0.2U/μL RNase inhibitor in 1× PBS. Centrifuge again at 500 × g for 5 min and resuspend with Cell Resuspension Buffer (MGI).

snRNA-seq library preparation and sequencing

The mRNA capture was operated on DNBelab C4 device (MGI). Complete cDNA amplification and library construction according to the MGI DNBelab C series reagent Kit (MGI, 940-000047-00). In brief, the single-cell suspensions were converted to barcoded scRNA-seq libraries through steps including droplet encapsulation, emulsion breakage, mRNA captured bead collection, reverse transcription, cDNA amplification, and purification. cDNA production was sheared to 250-400 bp, and indexed sequencing libraries were constructed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Qualification was performed using Qubit ssDNA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. All libraries were further sequenced by the DIPSEQ T1 sequencing platform generating reads containing 30-bp read 1 (including the 10-bp cell barcode 1, 10-bp cell barcode 2, and 10-bp unique molecular identifier (UMI)), 100-bp read 2 and 10-bp barcodes (sample index).

scATAC–seq library preparation and sequencing

Single-nucleus ATAC-seq libraries was performed by using DNBelab C Series Single-Cell ATAC Library Prep Set (MGI, #1000021878). Briefly, transposed single-nucleus suspensions were converted to barcoded snATAC-seq libraries, through procedures including droplet encapsulation, pre-amplification, emulsion breakage, capture beads collection, DNA amplification, and purification. Indexed sequencing libraries were prepared according to the user guide. Concentrations of sequencing libraries were measured with Qubit ssDNA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All libraries were sequenced using a paired-end 50 sequencing scheme by the ultra-high-throughput DIPSEQ T1 sequencer platform.

Spatial transcriptomic library generation and sequencing

Spatial transcriptomic libraries were prepared using the 10X Visium HD platform for FFPE samples according to the manufacturer’s protocol (10x Genomics, Document CG000684). Briefly, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks were sectioned at 5μm thickness using a cryostat (Leica CM1950), mounted onto slides, and subjected to deparaffinization, H&E staining, and brightfield imaging for morphological evaluation. Total RNA was extracted from adjacent sections, and samples with DV200 values greater than 30% were deemed suitable for library generation.

Probe hybridization was performed using the Visium HD Hybridase Reagent Kit (10x Genomics, PN-1000466/PN-1000667), enabling specific binding of mRNA targets, followed by ligation using the Visium HD Ligation Kit (PN-2000425). Tissue sections were aligned with the capture area (6.5 × 6.5 mm) of Visium HD slides, and probe transfer was facilitated using the CytAssist instrument. Released probes were captured by surface-bound poly-T oligonucleotides and extended to incorporate UMI, spatial barcodes, and sequencing adapters.

The barcoded cDNA was amplified, purified, and appended with dual-indexes to construct sequencing-ready libraries. Final libraries were quantified using a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher, Q33216) and assessed for size distribution with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, G2939AA). Qualified libraries were sequenced on an Illumina platform under 150 bp paired-end mode to generate high-quality spatial gene expression data.

Raw data processing

SnRNA-seq data filtered and gene expression matrix was obtained using DNBelab C Series scRNA analysis software (https://github.com/MGI-tech-bioinformatics/DNBelab_C_Series_HT_scRNAanalysis-software). Briefly, all samples were performed sample de-multiplexing, barcode processing, and single-cell 3’ unique molecular identifier (UMI) counting with default parameters. Processed reads were then aligned to GRCh38 genome reference using STAR (v2.5.3). Valid cells were automatically identified based on the UMI number distribution of each cell by using the “barcodeRanks()” function of the DropletUtils tool to remove background beads and the beads that had UMI counts less than the threshold value. Finally, we used PISA to calculate the gene expression of cells and create a gene x cell matrix for each library.

The raw data of spatial transcriptomic sequencing were processed using Space Ranger software (v3.0.0) and aligned to the GRCh38 reference genome. The analytical workflow included raw data processing and quality control, registration of tissue images with sequencing data, sequence alignment, and expression matrix construction. To balance spatial resolution and transcript detection sensitivity, an 8 × 8 μm bin size was selected as the fundamental resolution unit, which enables optimal performance in both cell subtype identification and spatial tissue architecture interpretation.

SnRNA-seq data analysis

Using the Read10X function in Seurat63, UMI count matrix for each sample were loaded into R and then generated into independent Seurat objects. These objects were integrated by the merge and JoinLayers functions. After quality control (expressed genes > 1000 and reads mapped to the mitochondrial genome < 30), mitochondrial genes were removed from merged counts matrix. The batch effect between samples were removed by harmony64. After normalization, the top 2500 highly variable genes and 1–25 PCs were used for UMAP and clustering.

When conducting sub-cluster analysis on Sertoli cells, germ cells and interstitial cells, the cell barcodes of the designated cells were extracted and the corresponding UMI count table from the total matrix was utilized to create a new Seurat object.

Differentially expressed genes between groups were identified by the FindMarkers function in Seurat. All GO enrichment analysis were performed using enrichGO function of clusterProfiler and org.Hs.eg.db65.

To compare ADT-treated Sertoli cells with immature Sertoli cells, we used MapQuery from Seurat to project previous data26,27 on the PCA plot. The Monocle2 package66 was used for trajectory construction and pseudotime inference following the default settings. The Seurat object of particular cells was converted to a CellDataSet object for importing into orderCells function to construct the trajectory.

The average expression of gene sets in specific cell types were calculated by the AddModuleScore function from Seurat. Genes from GO:0060009 and GO:0060008 were used to quantify Sertoli cell maturation; genes from GO:0006939 were used for calculating myoid cell function while genes in GO:0006694 were chosen for quantifying Leydig cell function. The quantification of AR signaling was achieved using known AR target genes32.

To infer the cell cycle stage of individual spematogonial cells, the CellCycleScoring function from Seurat was utilized with default parameters, utilizing marker genes for G2/M and S phase. Cells were subsequently categorized into distinct cell cycle phases, namely G1, S, and G2M.

The ligand-receptor pairs were defined based on the UMI count matrix using CellChat43. The CellChatDB.human was employed as a ligand-receptor interaction database for subsequent cell-cell communication analysis using default configurations. “Secreted Signaling” database was used to calculate ligand-receptor interactions between Sertoli cells and spermatogonia.

To value the transcriptomic similarity between PDGFRA + /WT1+ cells and Sertoli cells, the diffusion map was achieved by URD67. Merged gene counts matrix with its annotations were used to create an URD object. About 1000 variable features among developmental stages were used for diffusion map calculation. For correlation analysis, averaged expression of differentially expressed genes in given clusters were first calculated by AverageExpression from Seurat. The Pearson correlation coefficient between each cluster were then computed by corr in R.

ScATAC-seq data analysis

The scATAC-seq data were processed and analyzed using ArchR33. The reference genome was set to hg38 using the addArchRGenome function. Arrow files were generated and subsequently integrated into an ArchRProject containing both control and ADT-treated testicular samples. Dimensionality reduction was performed via iterative latent semantic indexing (addIterativeLSI) using 1–30 principal components, followed by clustering and gene score calculation using default ArchR functions. Batch effects were corrected using addHarmony function. Cell type annotations were refined by integrating snRNA-seq data with the scATAC-seq dataset through the addGeneIntegrationMatrix function. Peak calling was conducted with MACS3 (v2.2.7.1) to identify cell type-specific accessible regions.

Subsets corresponding to Sertoli cells and mesenchymal cells were extracted using subsetArchRProject. Transcription factor motif enrichment and annotation were performed with addMotifAnnotations to identify candidate regulatory factors.

To identify the androgen-dependent transcription factors and modules governing the Sertoli cell functions. We integrated the snRNA-seq and scATAC-seq datasets by SCENIC+34. The raw gene counts matrix and MACS2 peak matrix of Sertoli cells were used to generate annodata or cisTopic objects by scanpy or pycisTopic for further analysis. Topic modeling was performed using MALLET, using 500 iterations and models with 2 topics and from 5–50 by an increase of 5, selecting the model with 35 topics based on the model selection metrics. Next, pycisTarget was run, using a custom database with the consensus regions, on DARs and binarized topics (with Otsu thresholding), with and without promoters and using pycisTarget and DEM. Finally, SCENIC+ was run with default parameters. High-quality regulons were selected based on the correlation between gene-based regulon AUCs and region-based regulon AUCs and on the number of target genes (>10). The TF regulatory network was drawn by igraph.

Spatial transcriptomic data analysis

The raw expression matrix was imported into Seurat for downstream analyses, including cell clustering, differential expression analysis, and spatial pattern recognition. Top 1500 variable genes and 1–25 PCs were used for clustering. The spatial gene expression plots were drawn by SpatialFeaturePlot function from Seurat.

Spatial projection of PDGFRA + /WT1+ cells were performed by CellTrek R package47 with default parameters. In brief, processed snRNA-seq datasets of interstitial cells were co-embed with spatial transcriptomic datasets using the traint function. The spatial coordination of each cell was then predicted by the celltrek function. The spatial coordination results were plotted by SpatialDimPlot function from Seurat.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size. Based on existing datasets and effect size estimates, this sample size is sufficient to yield meaningful insights into spermatogenic niche function. All criteria for data exclusion were pre-established. Cells in snRNA-seq dataset with less than 1000 genes, or greater than 30% of mitochondrial RNA counts were filtered. The experiments were not randomized and the Investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment because this is an observational study.
