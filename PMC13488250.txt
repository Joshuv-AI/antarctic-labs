Psychological outcomes and intention to use following an 8-week virtual reality tennis training programme: a randomised controlled trial with exploratory mediation analysis
Authors: Rui Liu, Hazwani Ahmad Yusof, ZiMei Zhong, Wen Jie Wu, Rohayu Hami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVirtual reality (VR) provides an immersive format for sports training, but psychological responses associated with continued use of the technology remain incompletely understood. This study compared changes in self-efficacy, flow experience, embodied cognition, and intention to use following VR versus traditional tennis training and explored indirect associations through the three psychological variables.MethodsIn a parallel-group randomised controlled trial (NCT07356427), 180 Physical Education and Sports Science students with at least two semesters of tennis training experience were assigned to VR training (n = 90) or traditional training (n = 90). The intervention comprised 16 sessions over 8 weeks using PICO 4 headsets. Primary analyses used complete cases with pre- and post-intervention data (n = 153). Group × Time interactions were assessed using mixed ANOVA, and exploratory parallel multiple mediation was examined using PROCESS Model 4 with 5,000 bootstrap resamples and adjustment for baseline values.ResultsGroup × Time interactions were statistically significant for all four outcomes (p ≤ .006). Compared with traditional training, VR training was associated with greater pre-to-post changes in self-efficacy (g = 0.93, 95% CI [0.59, 1.26]), embodied cognition (g = 0.67, 95% CI [0.35, 1.00]), intention to use (g = 0.61, 95% CI [0.29, 0.94]), and flow experience (g = 0.45, 95% CI [0.13, 0.77]). Indirect associations through self-efficacy (indirect effect = 0.100, 95% CI [0.033, 0.183]) and flow experience (indirect effect = 0.078, 95% CI [0.018, 0.164]) were statistically distinguishable from zero; the indirect association through embodied cognition was not.ConclusionsAmong participants with complete data, VR tennis training was associated with greater improvements in self-reported psychological outcomes and intention to use than traditional training. The concurrent mediation findings are exploratory and do not establish temporal or causal pathways. Introduction

Virtual reality (VR) technology is increasingly used in sports training because it can create immersive, controllable simulations of competitive situations [1–3]. Compared with conventional practice, VR may permit standardised stimulus presentation, immediate feedback, adaptive difficulty, and repeated exposure to scenarios that are difficult to reproduce consistently in physical settings [4–7]. Interest in VR applications for racket sports, including tennis, has expanded over the past decade, with prior studies examining technical, tactical, anticipatory, and psychological outcomes [8–10].

Tennis is well suited to VR-based investigation because it requires rapid visual tracking, anticipation, time-constrained decision-making, and coordinated motor responses [11, 12]. These perceptual-cognitive demands can be represented in controlled virtual tasks [13, 14]. Advances in display resolution, motion-to-photon latency, and six-degree-of-freedom tracking have improved the fidelity of tennis simulations and their ability to represent salient spatiotemporal features of play [15, 16].

Despite growing interest, the psychological responses associated with VR-based sports training and their relevance to continued technology use remain incompletely understood. Self-efficacy refers to confidence in one’s capability to perform the actions required to achieve desired outcomes and is associated with sport participation, persistence, and technology adoption [17, 18]. Bandura identified mastery experiences, vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, and interpretations of physiological states as major sources of self-efficacy [19, 20]. In VR settings, repeated practice, immediate feedback, and progressive task difficulty may provide mastery-oriented experiences without some of the social evaluation pressures present in conventional training [21, 22].

Flow experience is characterised by intense task absorption, reduced self-consciousness, intrinsic enjoyment, and perceived control [23, 24]. Flow theory proposes that flow is more likely when task challenge and perceived skill are balanced, goals are clear, and feedback is immediate [25, 26]. VR environments may support these conditions by reducing external distraction and providing explicit goals, real-time feedback, and adaptive task difficulty [27, 28]. Research in gaming, education, and rehabilitation has reported associations between immersive environments and flow, but evidence in sports training remains limited [29, 30].

Embodied cognition emphasises that cognitive processes are grounded in bodily interactions with the environment [31, 32]. In VR, relevant experiences include presence, agency, and body ownership [33, 34]. These perceptions may influence how users interpret the realism, usefulness, and acceptability of a virtual training environment [35, 36]. However, whether embodied cognition is independently associated with intention to continue using VR training, beyond self-efficacy and flow experience, remains uncertain.

The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and related frameworks treat intention to use as a proximal indicator of technology adoption [37, 38]. Identifying factors associated with continued use is important when considering VR integration into educational sports programmes [39, 40]. Although psychological variables have been examined in technology acceptance across multiple domains, evidence concerning their role in VR-based sports training remains limited.

This study used a parallel-group randomised controlled trial to compare pre-to-post changes in self-efficacy, flow experience, embodied cognition, and intention to use following an 8-week VR tennis training programme versus traditional tennis training among Physical Education and Sports Science students with tennis experience. Based on social cognitive theory [19, 20] and flow theory [25, 26], we hypothesised that: (H1) Group × Time interactions would favour VR training for the four measured outcomes; and (H2) post-intervention self-efficacy and flow experience would show statistically significant indirect associations between group assignment and intention to use after adjustment for baseline values. Embodied cognition was included as an exploratory third mediator because of its theoretical relevance to immersive interaction [31, 32]. Because the mediators and intention to use were assessed concurrently at post-intervention, the mediation analysis was considered exploratory and cannot establish temporal precedence.

Methods

Study design

This study used a parallel-group randomised controlled trial design with 1:1 allocation. The trial was conducted at Hezhou University in Hezhou City, Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, China, from September to November 2025. The 8-week intervention provided 16 repeated training sessions within the university semester. Questionnaire distributors at baseline and post-intervention were blinded to group assignment; participants and training instructors could not be blinded because the two training conditions were visibly different. Because all primary outcomes were self-reported, participant expectancy effects cannot be excluded. The Human Research Ethics Committee (Jawatankuasa Etika Penyelidikan Manusia) at Universiti Sains Malaysia approved the study protocol on 3 July 2025 (JEPeM Code: USM/JEPeM/PP/25030255). Following completion of participant enrolment and the intervention, the trial was retrospectively registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07356427) on 20 January 2026, before completion of data analysis and manuscript preparation. Retrospective registration does not fulfil prospective registration requirements. The trial was reported in accordance with the CONSORT statement [41], and a completed CONSORT checklist is provided as an additional file.

Participants

Eligible participants were undergraduate students aged 18–26 years who were enrolled in Physical Education or Sports Science programmes at Hezhou University, had completed at least two semesters of tennis instruction, and demonstrated basic tennis proficiency as assessed by course instructors. Exclusion criteria were: (1) visual impairment not correctable with glasses or contact lenses compatible with the headset; (2) a history of photosensitive epilepsy or seizure disorder; (3) a vestibular disorder or history of severe motion sickness that could be aggravated by VR use [42]; (4) current participation in provincial-level or higher competitive tennis programmes; and (5) previous experience with VR-based sports training. All eligible participants provided written informed consent after receiving information about the study procedures, potential risks, and their right to withdraw without penalty.

The sample size was determined a priori using G*Power 3.1 [43]. Previous VR sports training studies reported medium-to-large standardised differences (d = 0.6–0.8) for psychological outcomes [44, 45]. A medium interaction effect (f = 0.25) was therefore assumed for the primary outcome of self-efficacy. With α = 0.05, power = 0.80, two groups, two measurement occasions, and an assumed correlation of 0.50 between repeated measures, the minimum required sample was 158 participants (79 per group). The final complete-case sample of 153 was marginally below this threshold, which may have reduced power to detect smaller effects. To allow for an anticipated attrition rate of 15%, the enrolment target was increased to 180 participants (90 per group).

Randomisation and blinding

Participants were assigned to the VR training group (n = 90) or traditional training control group (n = 90) using a computer-generated sequence and a 1:1 allocation ratio. Stratified block randomisation used variable block sizes of 4 and 6, with strata defined by year of study (first–second year/third–fourth year) and sex (male/female). An independent statistician who was not involved in recruitment, intervention delivery, or outcome assessment generated the allocation sequence. Allocation was concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes that were opened only after enrolment was confirmed. This procedure followed established randomisation guidance [46]. Questionnaire distributors at baseline and post-intervention were unaware of group assignment and did not participate in intervention delivery.

Intervention

VR Training Group: Participants completed 8 weeks of VR tennis training using PICO 4 head-mounted displays (ByteDance; resolution: 2160 × 2160 pixels per eye; refresh rate: 90 Hz; six-degree-of-freedom tracking). The commercially available First-Person Tennis application included an adaptive artificial-intelligence opponent, a physics-based ball-trajectory model, and progressive difficulty settings. Participants completed 16 supervised 60-minute sessions, delivered twice weekly.

Each session followed a standardised protocol: (1) a 10-minute warm-up and familiarisation period, including controller calibration and basic movement orientation; (2) a 40-minute core VR training period comprising forehand and backhand groundstrokes, volleys, serves, reaction-based return tasks, and simulated rallies with progressively increasing opponent difficulty; and (3) a 10-minute cool-down, review of the performance summary, and instructor-led debriefing. The application provided real-time visual and auditory feedback on stroke mechanics, ball trajectory, and court positioning. Difficulty was adjusted according to individual performance to maintain an approximate 70–80% success rate and support challenge-skill balance [47]. Trained instructors supervised all sessions, monitored safety, provided guidance, and managed discomfort or cybersickness symptoms in accordance with established procedures [42].

Traditional Training Control Group: Participants completed 8 weeks of conventional tennis training matched to the VR condition for frequency (twice weekly), duration (60 min per session), general content structure, and progression. Each session comprised: (1) a 10-minute physical warm-up with dynamic stretching and footwork drills; (2) 25 min of technical drills using ball-feeding machines and partner practice, covering the same stroke categories as the VR group; (3) 20 min of point play and match simulation focused on tactical decision-making; and (4) a 5-minute cool-down and performance review. The same qualified tennis coaches supervised both conditions and followed standardised coaching protocols aligned with national physical education curricula [48].

Outcome Measures

All outcomes were assessed at baseline, within one week before training began, and immediately after the 8-week intervention, within one week after the final session. Adapted Chinese-language scales were administered in paper-and-pencil format. Self-efficacy (SE) was assessed using an 8-item scale developed with reference to Bandura’s guidance for constructing self-efficacy measures [18]. Participants rated confidence in performing tennis skills and managing competitive situations on a 5-point scale (1 = not at all confident to 5 = completely confident; Cronbach’s α = 0.89). Flow experience (FL) was assessed using a 10-item scale based on Csikszentmihalyi’s flow theory [25] and adapted to the sports training context; items covered challenge-skill balance, concentration, perceived control, and intrinsic enjoyment (α = 0.87). Embodied cognition (EC) was assessed using an 8-item scale informed by the Avatar Embodiment Questionnaire [33] and the Slater-Usoh-Steed Presence Questionnaire [35]; items addressed body ownership, presence, and agency (α = 0.85). Intention to use (IU) was assessed using a 6-item scale adapted from the Technology Acceptance Model [37], covering willingness to continue using the training method, recommend it to others, and prefer it to alternatives (α = 0.91). All four outcome scales used 5-point Likert responses. Except for the independently validated VR Tennis KAP Questionnaire [49], the adapted scales had not undergone full psychometric validation in this specific population; the reported α coefficients represent sample-specific internal consistency. Baseline knowledge, attitudes, and practices concerning VR tennis training were assessed using the published VR Tennis KAP Questionnaire [49] to describe participants’ initial technology orientation.

Sport motivation was assessed at baseline using the Sport Motivation Scale-II (SMS-II) [50], adapted for Chinese university students. Because no single published Chinese validation study was identified when the study was designed, the English instrument was translated using a forward-backward procedure [51]. Two bilingual researchers independently completed the forward translations, discrepancies were resolved by consensus, and a third bilingual expert back-translated the agreed Chinese version for semantic comparison. Internal consistency across the subscales was acceptable in the present sample (α = 0.81). The SMS-II assesses intrinsic motivation, identified regulation, introjected regulation, external regulation, and amotivation using a 7-point response scale (1 = does not correspond at all to 7 = corresponds completely). Sport motivation was used to characterise the sample and assess baseline comparability between groups.

Statistical analysis

Independent-samples t tests and chi-square tests were used to describe baseline comparability for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Distributional assumptions and potential outliers were examined before inferential analyses. For each outcome, a 2 (Group: VR vs. traditional training) × 2 (Time: pre- vs. post-intervention) mixed-design analysis of variance tested the Group × Time interaction. Hedges’ g and 95% confidence intervals were calculated from the between-group difference in pre-to-post change scores [52]. Partial eta squared (ηp²) was reported for the ANOVA interaction to provide a variance-based effect-size estimate alongside Hedges’ g. The primary analyses were restricted to participants with complete pre- and post-intervention data (n = 153); no intention-to-treat analysis or missing-data sensitivity analysis was performed. Missingness was evaluated by comparing observed baseline characteristics between completers and non-completers, testing differential attrition by group, and conducting Little’s test of missing completely at random (MCAR). Exploratory parallel multiple mediation was conducted using PROCESS Model 4 [53] with 5,000 bootstrap resamples and bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals. Group assignment was the exposure; post-intervention self-efficacy, flow experience, and embodied cognition were entered as parallel mediators; and post-intervention intention to use was the outcome. Baseline values of each mediator and baseline intention to use were included as covariates. An indirect estimate was considered statistically distinguishable from zero when its bootstrap confidence interval excluded zero. Because mediators and outcome were measured concurrently at post-intervention, the indirect estimates were interpreted as exploratory statistical associations rather than causal mechanisms. All tests were two-sided with α = 0.05. Analyses were conducted in SPSS version 27.0.

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

A total of 195 students were screened for eligibility, of whom 180 met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled (Fig. 1). Fifteen were excluded: eight did not meet eligibility criteria (four had insufficient tennis experience, three had previous VR training experience, and one had uncorrectable visual impairment), five declined because of the time commitment, and two had scheduling conflicts. Eligible participants were randomised to VR training (n = 90) or traditional training (n = 90) using the stratified block-randomisation procedure described above.

Of the 180 randomised participants, 153 (85.0%) provided complete baseline and post-intervention data and were included in the primary analysis (VR: n = 74, 82.2% retention; traditional training: n = 79, 87.8% retention). Post-intervention data were missing for 27 participants because of incomplete questionnaires (n = 18), withdrawal for reasons unrelated to training intolerance or VR-related discomfort (n = 6), or absence from the post-intervention assessment because of scheduling conflicts (n = 3). Completers and non-completers did not differ significantly on observed baseline demographic or outcome variables (all p > .10), and attrition did not differ by group, χ²(1) = 1.09, p = .297. Little’s MCAR test was also non-significant (p = .267). These findings are compatible with the MCAR assumption for the observed variables but do not establish that missingness was unrelated to unmeasured factors. Session attendance was high in both groups (VR: 95.8%; traditional training: 94.6%) and did not differ significantly.

The complete-case sample (N = 153) included 88 males (57.5%) and 65 females (42.5%), with a mean age of 21.04 years (SD = 1.90; range, 18–26). Participants had a mean of 2.03 years of tennis training experience (SD = 0.78) and were distributed across all four undergraduate years. No statistically significant baseline differences were detected between groups for age, sex, year of study, tennis experience, self-efficacy, flow experience, embodied cognition, intention to use, or sport motivation (all p > .05).

Main intervention effects

Mixed ANOVA identified statistically significant Group × Time interactions for all four outcomes (Table 1), consistent with Hypothesis 1. For self-efficacy, the interaction was F(1, 151) = 32.83, p < .001, ηp² = 0.179. Self-efficacy increased in the VR group (mean change = + 0.52 on the 5-point scale, p < .001) and showed little change in the traditional training group (mean change = − 0.04, p = .672). For flow experience, the interaction was F(1, 151) = 7.90, p = .006, ηp² = 0.050. Flow experience increased in the VR group (mean change = + 0.49, p < .001), whereas the change in the traditional training group was not statistically significant (mean change = + 0.13, p = .142).

For embodied cognition, the Group × Time interaction was F(1, 151) = 17.72, p < .001, ηp² = 0.105. Scores increased in the VR group (mean change = + 0.42, p < .001) and showed little change in the traditional training group (mean change = − 0.03, p = .758). For intention to use, the interaction was F(1, 151) = 14.52, p < .001, ηp² = 0.088. Intention-to-use scores increased in the VR group (mean change = + 0.48, p < .001); the change in the traditional training group was not statistically significant (mean change = + 0.07, p = .398). Effect sizes

Hedges’ g estimates based on between-group differences in change ranged from small-to-medium to large, and all 95% confidence intervals excluded zero (Table 1; Fig. 4). The largest standardised difference was observed for self-efficacy (g = 0.93, 95% CI [0.59, 1.26]), followed by embodied cognition (g = 0.67, 95% CI [0.35, 1.00]), intention to use (g = 0.61, 95% CI [0.29, 0.94]), and flow experience (g = 0.45, 95% CI [0.13, 0.77]).

Mediation analysis

Exploratory parallel mediation analysis examined the indirect statistical associations of group assignment with intention to use through post-intervention self-efficacy, flow experience, and embodied cognition, while adjusting for baseline mediator values and baseline intention to use (Table 2; Fig. 5). The total association between group assignment and post-intervention intention to use was B = 0.421 (SE = 0.095, p < .001). After the three mediators were included, the direct association remained statistically significant but was smaller, B = 0.308 (SE = 0.089, p = .001). Because the mediators and outcome were assessed concurrently, temporal precedence and causal mediation were not established.

The indirect association through self-efficacy was 0.100 (95% CI [0.033, 0.183]), corresponding to 23.7% of the total association. The indirect association through flow experience was 0.078 (95% CI [0.018, 0.164]), corresponding to 18.6% of the total association. The indirect association through embodied cognition was 0.044 (95% CI [− 0.013, 0.125]) and was not statistically distinguishable from zero. The combined indirect estimate through self-efficacy and flow experience was 0.178 (95% CI [0.099, 0.281]), corresponding to 42.3% of the total association. These findings were consistent with Hypothesis 2 but should be interpreted as exploratory because the mediators and outcome were assessed at the same post-intervention time point.

Discussion

In this randomised controlled trial, complete-case analyses showed greater pre-to-post changes in self-efficacy, flow experience, embodied cognition, and intention to use in the VR training group than in the traditional training group. These outcomes were self-reported psychological and technology-acceptance variables; the study did not assess objective tennis skill, motor learning, or competitive performance. The exploratory mediation analysis identified statistically distinguishable indirect associations through self-efficacy and flow experience; their combined estimate was 0.178 (95% CI [0.099, 0.281]). Because the mediators and intention to use were assessed concurrently after the intervention, these findings do not establish temporal or causal pathways.

The largest standardised between-group difference in change was observed for self-efficacy (g = 0.93). This pattern is consistent with social cognitive theory, in which mastery experiences are a central source of self-efficacy [19, 20]. The VR condition incorporated repeated practice, immediate feedback, and progressive difficulty, all of which could support mastery-oriented experiences [21, 22]. However, the study did not isolate the contribution of these design components or assess objective competence. The finding should therefore be interpreted as a difference in self-reported confidence rather than evidence of improved tennis performance.

The standardised difference in change for flow experience was smaller (g = 0.45). Clear goals, immediate feedback, and adjustment of task difficulty are theoretically compatible with the conditions proposed to support flow [25–28]. The observed result is also broadly consistent with reports from other immersive applications [29, 54, 55]. Nevertheless, the present design cannot determine whether the PICO 4 hardware, the First-Person Tennis application, novelty, expectancy, or other features accounted for the difference. Repeated assessment of flow across sessions and comparison with an active technology control would help clarify the stability and specificity of this association.

Embodied cognition also showed a between-group difference in change (g = 0.67), indicating that participants in the VR condition reported stronger experiences of presence, agency, and body ownership. Six-degree-of-freedom tracking and physics-based simulation may have contributed to these perceptions, but component-level effects were not tested. Accordingly, the findings generate hypotheses for VR interface design rather than demonstrating that embodiment features improve skill acquisition or training performance. Future studies could directly compare tracking, avatar, and feedback configurations and include behavioural measures of motor learning and transfer.

The indirect associations through self-efficacy and flow experience suggest that these psychological variables may be relevant to intention to continue using VR training. These findings may inform hypotheses for the design of educational VR programmes aimed at engagement and technology acceptance. They do not demonstrate improvements in technical skill, motor learning, athletic performance, or sustained real-world adoption. The published VR Tennis KAP Questionnaire [49] may be useful for describing baseline knowledge, attitudes, and behavioural readiness, but the present trial did not test whether KAP-guided implementation improves outcomes. Future studies should prospectively evaluate whether mastery-oriented and flow-supportive design features are associated with psychological outcomes and subsequent use over longer follow-up periods.

Embodied cognition did not show a statistically significant indirect association with intention to use (indirect effect = 0.044, 95% CI [− 0.013, 0.125]). Although group assignment was associated with post-intervention embodied cognition (B = 0.447, p < .001), embodied cognition was not independently associated with intention to use after adjustment for self-efficacy and flow experience (B = 0.099, p = .178). This pattern indicates that the estimated indirect association was not distinguishable from zero in the parallel model. Possible explanations, including overlap between embodiment and flow or a longer time course for embodiment-related responses, remain speculative. Multi-time-point designs that measure candidate mediators before the outcome are required to evaluate temporal ordering and support stronger causal interpretation.

Novelty, expectancy, and enthusiasm for new technology remain plausible alternative explanations for some of the observed differences, particularly because participants were aware of their allocation and all primary outcomes were self-reported [56, 57]. The 8-week duration, repeated exposure, and matched instructor contact do not eliminate these sources of bias. The study did not include an active technology control condition or a baseline measure of expected benefit or perceived novelty. Future trials should compare immersive VR with a non-immersive technology condition matched for time, attention, and feedback, and should assess expectancy and novelty prospectively.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the single-university sample limits generalisability across institutions, cultures, competitive levels, age groups, and sports. Second, outcomes were measured only immediately after the intervention; durability and subsequent technology use were not assessed. Third, participants and instructors could not be blinded, and the reliance on self-report leaves the estimates vulnerable to expectancy and response biases. Fourth, 15.0% of randomised participants lacked post-intervention data, and the primary analyses used complete cases only. No intention-to-treat or missing-data sensitivity analysis was performed. Although observed baseline comparisons, differential-attrition testing, and a non-significant Little’s MCAR test were compatible with MCAR, they cannot rule out missingness related to unmeasured factors. Complete-case estimates may therefore be biased and should be interpreted cautiously. Future trials should pre-specify intention-to-treat-compatible methods, such as mixed-effects models or multiple imputation, and report sensitivity analyses. Fifth, the complete-case sample of 153 was slightly below the planned minimum of 158, which may have reduced power for smaller effects. Sixth, except for the published KAP instrument, the adapted outcome measures had not undergone full psychometric validation in this population. Seventh, no objective measures of technical skill, motor learning, physical workload, or competitive performance were included; consequently, educational and performance implications remain hypotheses for future testing. Finally, the absence of an active technology control and direct measures of novelty or expectancy prevents attribution of the observed psychological differences specifically to immersive VR.

Conclusions

Among the 153 participants with complete data, assignment to an 8-week VR tennis training programme was associated with greater improvements in self-reported self-efficacy, flow experience, embodied cognition, and intention to use than traditional training. Exploratory concurrent mediation identified indirect statistical associations through self-efficacy and flow experience. These findings are limited to psychological and technology-acceptance outcomes and should not be interpreted as evidence of improved tennis skill, motor learning, or competitive performance. The absence of intention-to-treat and sensitivity analyses, together with concurrent measurement of the mediators and outcome, requires cautious interpretation. Future trials should include objective performance measures, active technology controls, validated outcome instruments, multiple follow-up assessments, and pre-specified intention-to-treat-compatible methods for missing data.
