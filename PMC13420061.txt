Coexistence of PYCR1-related cutis laxa and bilateral grade V vesicoureteral reflux with reflux nephropathy: A case report
Authors: Dorna Derakhshan, Faizan Bashir, Ali Derakhshan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PYCR1-related cutis laxa (autosomal recessive cutis laxa type IIB) is a rare inherited connective tissue disorder characterized by loose, inelastic skin, and variable multisystem involvement. While systemic and neurological manifestations have been well described in the literature, data on renal and urinary tract findings remain sparse. We report a 6-year-old girl with genetically confirmed PYCR1-related cutis laxa who presented with recurrent febrile urinary tract infections. Workup revealed bilateral grade V vesicoureteral reflux alongside a small, atrophic left kidney with cortical defects on dimercaptosuccinic acid scintigraphy, in keeping with established reflux nephropathy. Differential renal function was markedly asymmetric, with the left kidney contributing 24% and the right kidney 76% of total function. Despite the degree of unilateral parenchymal loss, estimated glomerular filtration rate and blood pressure remained within age-appropriate limits, though mild proteinuria was noted on further evaluation. Given the burden of recurrent febrile infections in the setting of high-grade bilateral reflux, she was initially managed with prophylactic nitrofurantoin. Following urology consultation surgical correction was therefore pursued, and she underwent bilateral antireflux surgery. She remained free of breakthrough urinary tract infections throughout early postoperative follow-up. The concurrence of severe vesicoureteral reflux, reflux nephropathy, and PYCR1-related cutis laxa has rarely been documented. Whether this association reflects a pathophysiological link or represents coincidental findings in a single patient is not yet clear; nonetheless, this case highlights the importance of considering underlying urological pathology in children with complex connective tissue disorders who present with recurrent urinary tract infections.

Introduction

Congenital cutis laxa is a rare, genetically heterogeneous connective tissue disorder characterized by impaired elastic fiber integrity. Although the diagnosis is usually prompted by its distinctive dermatologic phenotype—generalized loose, inelastic skin with diminished recoil, and a prematurely aged appearance, the disease is intrinsically multisystemic.1,2 At a molecular level, these disorders converge on disruption of extracellular matrix (ECM) architecture, and in forms associated with the PYCR1 gene, impaired proline metabolism may have downstream effects on collagen and elastin stability across multiple organ systems.3 –7 Elastic tissue abnormalities may involve the respiratory, gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, and genitourinary systems, with the latter remaining comparatively underreported in the pediatric literature.8,9 In PYCR1-related disease, disruption of proline metabolism has been linked to abnormalities of connective tissue architecture, contributing to a broad and variable clinical spectrum.

Renal and urinary tract manifestations have received relatively little attention in the literature. Given the structural role of elastin within the urinary tract and renal vasculature, 10 consideration of potential renal involvement is biologically reasonable, although the spectrum of such findings in cutis laxa remains incompletely defined. Importantly, the severity and pattern of organ involvement are highly variable, 11 and clinically significant findings may not always be apparent from the cutaneous phenotype alone. Early recognition of potential urinary tract involvement is therefore critical to prevent long-term renal sequelae.

We report a 6-year-old girl with PYCR1-related cutis laxa who underwent evaluation for recurrent febrile urinary tract infections (UTIs) and was found to have bilateral grade V vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) with established reflux nephropathy. Her clinical course is described with particular attention to the renal and urological findings.

Case presentation

A 6-year-old girl with a known diagnosis of congenital cutis laxa was admitted in the department of Pediatric Nephrology at Namazi Hospital, Shiraz, Iran, for evaluation of recurrent UTIs in mid-2025. The diagnosis of cutis laxa had been originally established based on the typical clinical features and later confirmed by genetic testing. She was born to consanguineous parents, with both siblings (she and her brother) affected by cutaneous laxity. Apart from developmental delay and strabismus, the male sibling had no reported history of urological symptoms or other systemic complications commonly associated with cutis laxa, such as pulmonary emphysema, hernias, gastrointestinal diverticula, or cardiovascular involvement.

On re-examination, the patient continued to exhibit generalized loose, wrinkled, and inelastic skin with markedly diminished recoil, most prominent over the limbs and trunk, imparting a prematurely aged appearance (Figure 1). No associated skin hyperextensibility, abnormal scarring, thin or translucent skin, finger contractures, or hip dislocation were observed. Ophthalmologic evaluation did not reveal cataracts or corneal clouding, although strabismus was present. Mildly large and posteriorly positioned ears were also noted on physical examination. At the time of assessment, there were no clinically evident cardiopulmonary abnormalities, and no hernias were identified. The patient additionally demonstrated developmental delay with delayed acquisition of milestones. Over the subsequent course, she experienced recurrent UTIs, which prompted further urological evaluation.

Clinical features of cutis laxa in the patient. (a) Excess, inelastic skin involving the lower extremities and feet. (b) Prominent wrinkling and aged appearance of the dorsal hand. (c) Loose, wrinkled skin over the abdomen.

A voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG) demonstrated that the patient had bilateral VUR (Figure 2). A further renal evaluation by a Tc 99m dimercaptosuccinic acid (DMSA) scan revealed a compromised cortical function of a small, atrophied left kidney with cortical defects mainly at mid and lower poles, indicating chronic renal parenchymal damage, whereas the right kidney was normal in size and cortical function was preserved. The percentage of differential renal function was assessed as 24% and 76% for the left and right kidney, respectively. The reflux was classified as grade V bilaterally. The patient had a history of multiple febrile UTIs prior to presentation. Despite the marked asymmetry in renal function, serum creatinine-based estimated glomerular filtration rate was within the normal range for age, and blood pressure measurements were normal. Urinary evaluation revealed proteinuria of approximately 200 mg/day.

Voiding cystourethrogram demonstrating bilateral (grade V) vesicoureteral reflux with contrast extending into both ureters and pelvicalyceal systems.

In view of the recurrent febrile UTIs and severe bilateral reflux, the patient was initially started on nitrofurantoin prophylaxis (2 mg/kg nightly). Because VCUG demonstrated bilateral grade V VUR and DMSA scintigraphy had already shown significant damage to the left kidney, bilateral anti-reflux surgery was later performed. At the latest follow-up visit, approximately 3 months after surgery, she remained on prophylactic antibiotics and had not experienced any further UTIs. Blood pressure and renal function were unchanged and remained within the normal range.

Genetic testing performed earlier by whole exome sequencing (WES) revealed a homozygous pathogenic variant in the PYCR1 gene (NM_001282280.1, c.535G>A; p.Ala179Thr), thus confirming the diagnosis of autosomal recessive cutis laxa type IIB (ARCL2B).

Discussion

The present patient had genetically confirmed PYCR1-related cutis laxa and was found to have bilateral grade V VUR with established reflux nephropathy following evaluation for recurrent febrile UTIs. Although multisystem involvement is well recognized in cutis laxa, urinary tract manifestations have received relatively limited attention in the literature.

The underlying molecular defects span several pathways including elastic fiber biogenesis and TGF-β (transforming growth factor) signaling to vesicular trafficking and mitochondrial metabolism and the specific genetic subtype largely dictates the pattern and severity of extracutaneous involvement. 8 Among the autosomal recessive subtypes, ARCL2B (Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man #612940) is caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in PYCR1, which encodes pyrroline-5-carboxylate reductase 1, a mitochondrial enzyme catalyzing the final step in de novo proline biosynthesis. 4 Proline itself is the predominant amino acid in collagen and elastin; it is therefore intuitive that impairment of its endogenous production would ultimately destabilize the ECM. The seminal work by Reversade et al. established the mechanistic link between loss-of-function PYCR1 mutations and cutis laxa by demonstrating altered mitochondrial morphology, membrane depolarization, and heightened apoptosis under oxidative stress in fibroblasts from affected individuals. 4 This mitochondrial vulnerability is particularly pronounced in cells of connective tissue lineage, leading to fragmented elastic fibers and structurally abnormal collagen fibrils on electron microscopy.5,12

The patient carried a homozygous PYCR1 missense variant identified by WES, and her overall clinical phenotype was consistent with previously reported cases of PYCR1-related cutis laxa. In addition to the characteristic skin findings, she had developmental delay and strabismus, features that have been described in affected individuals. 13 An interesting aspect of this family was the difference in clinical findings between the two affected siblings. Although her brother carried the same genetic variant, he had neurodevelopmental impairment and strabismus but no history of significant urinary tract disease. Differences in the clinical presentation of individuals carrying the same PYCR1 variant have been described previously.13,14 The contrasting clinical features observed in these siblings further illustrate this variability. However, whether the urinary tract findings in our patient are related to the underlying disorder or simply represent a coincidental occurrence cannot be determined from a single case.

The most notable finding in our patient was the presence of bilateral grade V VUR accompanied by established reflux nephropathy. Although VUR is a common pediatric urological disorder, its occurrence in the setting of PYCR1-related cutis laxa has been only rarely discussed. The relationship between these findings remains uncertain; however, normal function of the ureterovesical junction depends not only on its anatomical configuration but also on the integrity of the surrounding connective tissue. Given the underlying connective tissue abnormalities that characterize cutis laxa, it is plausible that alterations in ECM structure could contribute to impaired antireflux mechanisms in some patients. Similar associations between VUR and inherited connective tissue disorders have been described previously, supporting the concept that connective tissue integrity may play a role in maintaining normal urinary tract function.15,16

Not all forms of cutis laxa appear to carry the same risk of urological involvement. Urinary tract abnormalities, including bladder diverticula and VUR, are well recognized in some of the more severe elastin-related forms of the disease, particularly those associated with LTBP4 and FBLN5 variants. 17 In contrast, urinary manifestations have been reported far less frequently in PYCR1-related cutis laxa. Although PYCR1 does not directly participate in elastic fiber assembly, its deficiency has been linked to abnormalities of connective tissue architecture, including disrupted collagen and elastic fiber organization.5,18,19 Whether these alterations contribute to urinary tract dysfunction in affected individuals remains uncertain, but the finding of severe bilateral reflux in our patient raises an interesting possibility that warrants further clinical observation.

The renal findings in this patient are clinically significant. DMSA scintigraphy demonstrated a small, atrophic left kidney with cortical defects consistent with established reflux nephropathy, and differential renal function was markedly reduced on the affected side (24% vs 76% for the right kidney). Despite this asymmetry, overall renal function remained preserved, blood pressure was normal, and serum creatinine-based estimated glomerular filtration rate was within the normal range for age. Mild proteinuria (approximately 200 mg/day) was also present. While these findings are reassuring in the short term, children with reflux nephropathy remain at increased risk of hypertension, proteinuria, and chronic kidney disease later in life, underscoring the importance of long-term nephrological follow-up.15,20 –26

The bilateral nature of the reflux, together with the presence of established renal scarring, warrants continued long-term follow-up. Although overall kidney function remains preserved at present, the combination of reflux nephropathy and proteinuria places the patient at risk for future renal complications. Regular monitoring of blood pressure, urinary protein excretion, and renal function will therefore be important as she grows older, particularly given the marked asymmetry in renal function between the two kidneys.

The management approach in this patient was influenced by the combination of recurrent febrile UTIs, bilateral grade V reflux, and established reflux nephropathy. Continuous antibiotic prophylaxis with nitrofurantoin was initiated in an effort to reduce the risk of further infections. The use of prophylactic antibiotics in children with VUR remains supported by evidence demonstrating a reduction in recurrent UTIs, although concerns regarding antimicrobial resistance and the magnitude of benefit in different patient groups continue to be debated.27,28 Nitrofurantoin was selected because of its established role in prophylaxis and favorable activity against common urinary pathogens. 29 Due to the severity of reflux and the presence of preexisting renal damage, bilateral antireflux surgery was subsequently performed. At 3-month follow-up, the patient had remained free of breakthrough UTIs while continuing prophylactic antibiotic therapy.

The decision to proceed with surgery in this patient was influenced by several factors, including recurrent febrile UTIs, bilateral grade V reflux, and evidence of established reflux nephropathy. Although surgical correction cannot reverse existing renal scarring, it may reduce the risk of further pyelonephritic injury and help preserve the remaining functional renal parenchyma. 30 The absence of breakthrough UTIs during the early postoperative period is encouraging, although continued follow-up will be necessary to assess long-term renal and urological outcomes.

One aspect of this case that deserves mention is how little is known about urinary tract involvement in PYCR1-related cutis laxa. To our knowledge, urinary tract abnormalities have been only infrequently described in individuals with molecularly confirmed PYCR1-related cutis laxa.4,31 Among the reported findings are bladder diverticula, 31 although the significance of these observations within the broader clinical spectrum remains uncertain. Whether the severe reflux observed in our patient was related to the underlying connective tissue disorder or simply represented a coexisting condition cannot be determined. Nevertheless, the recurrent febrile UTIs in this child ultimately led to the identification of bilateral grade V reflux and established renal damage, findings with important implications for long-term care. The case therefore serves as a reminder that clinically significant renal pathology may occasionally come to light during the evaluation of symptoms that might otherwise be viewed in isolation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we report a child with genetically confirmed PYCR1-related cutis laxa who was found to have bilateral grade V VUR and established reflux nephropathy during evaluation for recurrent febrile UTIs. Whether these findings are related to the underlying connective tissue disorder or represent a coincidental association cannot be determined from a single case. Nevertheless, the severity of the renal involvement in this patient and the differing clinical presentations observed within the affected family highlight the clinical heterogeneity of PYCR1-related disease. Continued follow-up will be important to monitor long-term renal outcomes, while additional reports may help clarify the spectrum of urological findings encountered in affected individuals.
