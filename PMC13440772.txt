Needle-free gauze knot-tying training using human hair: A low-cost, accessible method for basic microsurgical skill acquisition
Authors: Hiroshi Inagaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Knot-tying practice on surgical gauze using 10-0 nylon suture is widely used as an introductory exercise in microsurgical training. The conventional approach, however, entails concerns regarding cost, supply, needle loss, and needlestick injury. To describe a needle-free gauze knot-tying training method using human hair, and to discuss its educational implications and limitations.Methods: Using forceps alone, a single strand of human hair was passed manually through the mesh of commercially available surgical gauze, and loop formation and knot-tying were performed. The procedure and its position relative to previously reported gauze-based training methods were examined.Results: Because human hair is slightly thicker and more elastic than 10-0 nylon suture, it could be passed through the gauze mesh without a needle. The same elasticity introduced characteristic difficulties in loop maintenance, knot advancement, and knot stabilization. These difficulties were considered relevant to the acquisition of tension control, balanced tightening, and avoidance of excessive force.Conclusion: Needle-free gauze knot-tying training using human hair preserves the framework of established gauze-based training while removing barriers related to cost, supply, and needle handling. The method is best positioned as a preparatory, bridge-type exercise preceding more formal microsurgical practice.

INTRODUCTION

Acquisition of microsurgical skill is generally pursued by repeating basic patterns on nonbiological models in the earliest phase of training.[4,6] Knot-tying practice on surgical gauze is one of the most basic and accessible methods and has long been reported in the literature,[4,7] but it presupposes the use of 10-0 nylon suture with an attached needle. Although 10-0 nylon is well suited to microvascular anastomosis training, it is too expensive to be consumed casually on a daily basis, and its use carries the risk of needle loss, needlestick injury, and the burden of needle management.

In contrast, human hair is essentially free of cost and universally available. Despite its small diameter, it retains a degree of rigidity and elasticity[10] that allow it to be passed through the gauze mesh without an attached needle. This makes preparation substantially simpler than in the conventional method. Although human hair has been described as a suture material in dermatology[1] and as a do-it-yourself microsuture for basic microsurgical training,[8] its needle-free application within an established gauze knot-tying exercise has not been reported.

The present article describes a needle-free gauze knot-tying training method using human hair and discusses its specific procedure, educational significance, and limitations.

METHODS

Materials

The required materials are human hair, commercially available surgical gauze, two pairs of forceps, and scissors [Figure 1]. There is no strict restriction on hair length, but a short strand provides little free end for manipulation and increases difficulty. Training environment

The procedure can be performed under an operating microscope, a tabletop microscope, or magnifying loupes; it can also be initiated at a desktop.[2]

Procedure

A piece of gauze is placed on a flat surface and stabilized so that its mesh structure is clearly visible. One end of the hair is grasped with forceps and passed through the gauze mesh. The passed hair is then used to form a loop, and the free end is drawn through to create a knot. Several additional throws are applied to stabilize the knot, and the ends are trimmed with scissors [Figure 3 and Video 1].

RESULTS

Simplicity of setup

Hair could be used immediately whenever gauze and forceps were available; no needle preparation was required.

Passability of hair through the gauze mesh

Hair could be passed through the gauze mesh without a needle. Because hair does not possess the clear penetrating property of a needle, adjustment of the mesh orientation, the grasping position of the forceps, and the angle of advancement was required.

Procedural difficulties

Owing to the elasticity of hair, a formed loop sometimes retracted, or the free end slipped back through the mesh. Balanced tightening was required for stable knot formation.

Preservation of the gauze mesh structure

In the course of forming a continuous series of knots, the gauze mesh structure was preserved without major distortion, and the knots could be arranged in an orderly row [Figure 4].

DISCUSSION

Position of the method: not a substitute for 10-0 nylon, but a simplification of the entry to gauze training The primary intent of the present method is not to present human hair as a complete substitute for 10-0 nylon suture. Rather, hair is used as a setup that allows the already established gauze knot-tying exercise[4,7] to be initiated more cheaply, more easily, and more safely. The method can therefore be interpreted as preserving the educational framework of gauze-based training[2,4,7] while further lowering its cost and simplifying its entry point.Elasticity of hair as an educational load rather than a drawback The elasticity of hair, at first glance, makes the material more difficult to handle than 10-0 nylon suture. In the present method, however, this property is not merely a drawback. Loop retraction demands tension maintenance and procedural timing; knot slippage demands symmetric tightening and an understanding of knot construction; and hair breakage teaches the avoidance of excessive force. The difficulties encountered when working with hair can therefore be exploited as an educational load.Educational value of preserving the gauze mesh structure In our observations, the gauze mesh structure could be preserved when the knots were arranged in an orderly row, whereas inappropriate traction or rough grasping readily disturbed the weave. This suggests that the training may encompass not only the act of tying but also the preservation of the underlying fine structure during manipulation. Atraumatic handling of microinstruments is recognized as a basic component of experimental microsurgical training,[6,9] and although gauze is not biological tissue, operating without unnecessary disruption of the mesh may help cultivate an awareness of avoiding excessive traction and asymmetric force. Educational value of preserving the gauze mesh structure In our observations, the gauze mesh structure could be preserved when the knots were arranged in an orderly row, whereas inappropriate traction or rough grasping readily disturbed the weave. This suggests that the training may encompass not only the act of tying but also the preservation of the underlying fine structure during manipulation. Atraumatic handling of microinstruments is recognized as a basic component of experimental microsurgical training,[6,9] and although gauze is not biological tissue, operating without unnecessary disruption of the mesh may help cultivate an awareness of avoiding excessive traction and asymmetric force. The method therefore offers a dimension of training in which the supporting structure is protected during manipulation.

Limitations

This method has several limitations. The physical properties of human hair are not identical to those of clinical suture material, and the method does not reproduce clinical suture itself. The method does not involve needle handling, and skills related to needle use must be acquired separately. Finally, this article is a descriptive technical note focused on procedural presentation, not a quantitative comparative study; it does not demonstrate that the present method is superior to conventional methods in learning efficiency or skill retention.

CONCLUSION

Needle-free gauze knot-tying training using human hair is a method by which the already established gauze knot-tying exercise can be initiated more cheaply, more easily, and more safely. The method is best positioned as a preparatory, bridge-type exercise preceding more formal microsurgical practice.
