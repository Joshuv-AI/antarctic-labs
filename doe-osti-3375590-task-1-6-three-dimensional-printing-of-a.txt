Task 1.6 – Three-Dimensional Printing of All-Solid-State Lithium Batteries
(Jianchao Ye, Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory)

Objectives. The project has two primary objectives: (1) down select three-dimensional (3D) printing and
post-processing approaches for solid-state electrolyte (SSE) / cathode integration, and (2) understand battery
failure mechanisms via ex situ and in situ characterization.
Impact. The adoption of a thin separator layer, thick cathode structure, and metallic lithium anode will lead to
electric vehicle batteries with > 350 Wh/kg energy density for increased mileage per charge. The higher ionic
conductivity with suppression of lithium dendrite growth will allow high critical current densities for
fast-charging applications. The improved electrode/electrolyte contact will increase battery cycle life for
long-term service.
Approach. The technical approaches include advanced manufacturing based on 3D printing and related
techniques, ex situ / in situ characterizations, and battery testing. Direct-ink writing and projection micro
stereolithography (PµSL) 3D-printing techniques will be employed to fabricate thin-film SSEs (< 100 µm),
gradient SSEs, and 3D interfaces for battery performance evaluation. Three approaches, including sintering-
free, hybrid, and co-sintering, will be investigated. The knowledge obtained from studying these approaches is
transferable and complementary to each technique.
Out-Year Goals. The long-term vision of the team is to 3D-print all components of the all-solid-state lithium
battery (ASSLB) to facilitate the scale-up of ASSLB manufacturing. In this project, the team will tackle the
issues emerging from integrating solid electrolyte with electrodes. The project goal is to demonstrate a
successful 3D-printing approach to integrate SSE into electrodes and show reasonable capacity retention
(> 80%) after 300 cycles at current density ≥ 1 mA/cm2.
Collaborations. The team will work closely with a computational partner (Task 3.6 led by B. Wood) to better
understand battery failure mechanisms and design new battery architectures and chemistries for
performance improvement.

 Milestones

1. Successful printing of a 3D porous separator for 250 Wh/kg gravimetric energy density design, with
 satisfying porosity and mechanical/thermal stabilities. (Q1, FY 2025; Completed)
2. Achieving conductivity > 10-4 S/cm at room temperature along with good stability with lithium anode and
 LiFePO4 (LFP) or Ni-Mn-Co cathode for the polymer electrolyte (PE). (Q2, FY 2025; Completed)
3. Successful printing of a 3D porous separator to further increase the gravimetric energy density to
 300 Wh/kg, with improved chemical/mechanical/thermal stabilities. (Q3, FY 2025; On schedule)
4. Integrate the PE into the printed porous separator while maintaining > 10-4 S/cm at room-temperature
 conductivity. (Q4, FY 2025; On schedule)

 Task 1.6 – Ye, LLNL

 Progress Report

In FY25 Q2, the team evaluated a highly conductive polymer electrolyte for the assembly of 3D interdigitated
batteries, optimized both cathode and anode infilling procedures to enable complete channel infilling, and
scrutinized the defect evolution in the 3D 1,6-hexanediol diacrylate (HDDA) membrane fabrication to further
improve the membrane quality to avoid short circuit.

Development of polymer electrolyte infilled porous HDDA for 3D battery assembly.

To achieve enhanced battery performance, the ionic conductivity of the solid polymer electrolyte (SPE) must
be high enough. Here, the team adopted a thermal curable SPE with ethylene acrylate as monomer, ethylene
glycol dimethylacrylate as cross-linker, succinonitrile as plasticizer, azobisicobutyronitrile as initiator, as well
as fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC) as interface stabilizer. A projection micro stereolithography (PµSL) printed
porous HDDA separator was used as scaffold to further improve the mechanical properties of the SPE while
maintaining its high ionic conductivity. The obtained HDDA-SPE composite shows an ionic conductivity of
0.62 ± 0.02 mS/cm at room temperature. The total resistance of a Li symmetric cell is around 400 ohm cm2 for
a 250-µm-thick HDDA-SPE sample. The composite shows good electrochemical stability from -0.3V to 4.6V.
The properties of the SPE fulfill our needs for 3D battery assembly.

Cathode infilling.

The team developed an infilling protocol utilizing a 3D printed sample holder integrated with a vacuum pump.
The process involves applying a thin layer of cathode ink onto the membrane, which is then drawn to the bottom
of the cathode channel by vacuum. The ink is dried while the process is monitored using a camera, and the
procedure is repeated until the channels are completely infilled. Figure 2 shows an example of a cathode infilled
3DM. The method allows complete cathode infilling without damaging the 3D porous membrane. However,
pre-existing cracks could cause the leak of cathode slurry to the anode side, leading to the issue of short circuit
in the next anode integration step.

Li metal anode infilling.

After demonstrating successful infilling with the cathode ink, the next step is to infill metallic Li into the anode
channel of the membrane. Several approaches were explored, including using pressure, heat, or both to facilitate
Li channel infilling. These methods involved assembling coin cells with cathode-infilled membranes and Li
chips, then applying heat to soften Li by reducing its yield strength and thereby improving Li infilling (Figure
3). Our findings indicate that pre-heating the Li chip and allowing it to rest in the coin cell at temperatures
below its melting point over time is an effective approach to infill lithium to the anode channels.

Figure 3. Anode infilling. (a) A drawing of the method used to infill metallic Li into the anode channel after cathode infilling. A coin cell was
used to apply the compressive stress at elevated temperature (e.g., 150 °C). (b) A photo of a cross-sectional sample prepared by razor blade
cutting of a 3DM with both cathode and anode infilled.

Quality control for 3D printed porous HDDA membranes.

 Task 1.6 – Ye, LLNL
To avoid short circuit originated from the defects in 3D membranes (3DMs), it is of critical importance to
understand the origin of the defects. The team examined the 3DMs at different stages of processing, such as
after printing, removal from Indium Tin Oxide (ITO) glass slides, solvent exchange, and drying. It was found
that besides potential mechanical damages during sample handling, some defects can be traced back to the
printing process caused by the solid impurities (debris, particles, fibers, etc.) in the resin. This implies that it is
necessary to properly filter the resin before printing, maintain a clean working environment, use fiber-less/dust-
free wipes, and replace the scratched plastic sheet used in the PµSL printer. Figure 4 shows the commonly
found defects. After changing a fresh photoresist bath, the defect population dropped dramatically. No apparent
slurry leakage was observed except a couple of black particles sticking to the anode side surface that need
further attention.

Figure 4. Defect analysis in 3D HDDA membranes. (a) As printed 3D HDDA membranes using old photoresist that contains impurities like
fibers and dust, leading to several observable defects (cracks, holes, fractures, scratches) in the printed membrane. (b) As printed 3D HDDA
membranes and cathode infilled membranes using fresh photoresist with much less impurities. Resulted membrane shows much less
defects.
