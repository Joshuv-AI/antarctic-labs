Inconsistent reporting of adolescent and young adult (AYA) status in non-interventional cancer research: a critical barrier to evidence usability
Authors: Katja Reuter, David R Freyer, Joel Milam, Erin M Mobley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInconsistent reporting of age at cancer diagnosis limits the utility of non-interventional studies focused on adolescents and young adults (AYAs), defined by the National Cancer Institute as individuals diagnosed between ages 15 and 39. Standardised reporting is essential to synthesise evidence and develop targeted interventions to address this population’s unique needs. While initiatives have emphasised reporting age at study enrolment in reporting results, there is a lack of guidance on specifying age at diagnosis—an essential detail for accurately identifying AYA status.To estimate the scope of this problem, we reviewed 734 non-interventional cancer studies published between 2018 and 2022 and found that 33% did not report age at diagnosis sufficiently to determine AYA inclusion, with no improvement over time. We recommend that future reporting guidelines explicitly require age-at-diagnosis metrics, including mean, median, range and count of participants diagnosed between ages 15 and 39, to improve data comparability of non-interventional AYA cancer research and accelerate targeted interventions. We further recommend that studies with mixed-age samples report outcomes stratified by AYA status wherever sample size permits.Keywords: ONCOLOGY, STATISTICS & RESEARCH METHODS, Adolescent

Brief communication

More adolescents and young adults (AYAs) are surviving cancer than ever before, with 5-year survival now exceeding 86%.1 As this population of survivors, diagnosed between ages 15 and 39 and now estimated at more than 2.1 million in the USA alone,2 continues to grow, so does the urgency of addressing persistent gaps in understanding how cancer and its treatments affect this population from diagnosis through treatment and survivorship.1 3–6

Inconsistent data reporting of AYAs with cancer in research studies limits progress—even as the volume of AYA-focused studies increases (figure 1). The National Cancer Institute (NCI) has emphasised the need to improve data collection for this population. In 2020, the AYA Patient-Reported Outcomes Task Force was launched to standardise the collection of quality-of-life and other patient-reported measures in clinical trials conducted through the NCI Community Oncology Research Program and the National Clinical Trials Network.7 8 Standardised reporting is vital for data aggregation across studies. However, many publications inconsistently report age at cancer diagnosis, impeding evidence synthesis and the development of targeted interventions for this group.9

While conducting a systematic review of non-interventional cancer studies to synthesise barriers to AYA-specific survivorship care and health-related quality of life,10 we observed substantial variability in how AYAs were defined and categorised, along with frequent omission of age-at-diagnosis information. To determine whether a study population includes AYAs, as defined by the NCI and affirmed in current clinical practice guidelines11 as individuals diagnosed with cancer between the ages 15 and 39, researchers must report participants’ age at diagnosis clearly. For our review, we aimed to include only studies in which at least 50% of participants were AYAs by this definition. However, many otherwise relevant studies had to be excluded due to insufficient reporting of age-at-diagnosis data. These inconsistencies prompted us to further examine the scope of the problem.

To assess the extent of inconsistencies in AYA status reporting in the non-interventional oncology literature, we conducted a retrospective analysis of a convenience sample of 734 non-interventional cancer studies published between 2018 and 2022 from the systematic review described previously. These studies were drawn from the Covidence-facilitated screening phase of the systematic review mentioned above10 and analysed sequentially in the order presented, without additional selection. The sample consisted predominantly of quantitative studies (eg, cross-sectional surveys, cohort studies, registry-based analyses). Qualitative studies, which were not excluded during this phase, represented a small minority. For each study record, we extracted information on whether age-at-diagnosis data as subgroup-level results permitted identification of AYAs, defined as individuals diagnosed between the ages of 15 and 39 years. We recorded whether studies reported three key descriptive statistics (mean, median and range) for both age at diagnosis and age at the time of study enrolment. These statistics were selected because, in combination, they offer the most direct basis for determining whether a sample includes AYAs as defined by the NCI (ages 15–39). Mean and median alone are insufficient: for example, a mean of 32 and median of 30 could reflect either a purely AYA sample or a mixed-age sample that includes older adults, with both scenarios producing central tendency values that would allow one to assume a study should be categorised as an AYA sample. The range resolves this ambiguity, for example, a range of 20–38 confirms AYA inclusion, while a range of 20–65 signals a mixed sample. When the range falls entirely within or entirely outside the AYA window, classification is straightforward; the combination of all three statistics therefore provides a more complete picture. Additionally, we note that while categorical age group reporting (eg, predefined age groups with numeric boundaries) informed study classification where boundaries fell entirely within or outside the AYA window, it was not tracked as a separate variable. Of the 734 studies evaluated, we classified 160 studies (22%) as studies involving AYAs, 334 (46%) as non-AYA studies and 240 (33%) as unclassifiable based on the reported data (table 1). This approach allowed us to examine reporting patterns and pinpoint gaps in standardisation of age-related metrics across a wide range of non-interventional cancer research publications.

Studies coded as unclassifiable (n=240) lacked sufficient age-at-diagnosis data to determine AYA status reliably. Of these, 22% reported an age range at diagnosis and 3% reported a median age and only one reported a mean age (table 1). For instance, some studies listed only a broad age range (eg, ‘26–60 years’) without specifying the age at diagnosis. Others grouped participants by interview age (eg, ‘18–39 years (16%), 40–49 years (20%), 50–64 years (65%)’) in conjunction with time since treatment (eg, ‘<5 years (46%)’, ‘years post-cancer treatment ranged from 5 to 39 years’), making it impossible to determine whether participants were AYAs.

Even when age at diagnosis or time since treatment was reported, the use of broad spans (eg, ‘18 months to 75 years’) obscured AYA representation within the sample. Similarly, reporting a median with a broad range—such as ‘median age 60.9 years (range: 22.7–82.9)’—did not allow for confirmation of AYA representation in the sample.

One study that reported mean age at diagnosis (table 1) was still categorised as unclassifiable due to incomplete information. Although it provided mean years since diagnosis by sex (‘females 10 years, males 12 years’) and age-at-study ranges for subgroups (‘20–40 years’ for females (5%) and males (12%)), it did not report explicit age-at-diagnosis data. As a result, it remained unclear whether the sample included at least 50% AYAs, despite the inclusion of some descriptive statistics.

Reporting of age-at-diagnosis metrics showed modest fluctuation over the 5-year period of this sample frame but no consistent improvement (figure 2). The proportion of studies reporting mean age at diagnosis ranged from 29% (lowest in 2020) to 45% (highest in 2022), median from 7% (2019) to 16% (2022) and range from 57% (2020) to 62% (2022). When considering any form of age-at-diagnosis data (mean, median or range), reporting ranged from 69% in 2020 to 75% in 2022. Similarly, reporting of age at the time of study varied annually over the 5 years of this sample frame: mean (53%–61%), median (11%–18%), range (79%–86%) and any related data (91%–98%). These trends highlight continued inconsistency and a lack of progress towards standardised reporting.

Overall, reporting participants’ age at study enrolment was common among unclassifiable studies, with 95% providing at least one descriptive statistic: 46% reported a mean, 15% a median and 87% a range. In contrast, only 22% reported any age-at-diagnosis metric, with 0% reporting a mean, 3% a median and 21% a range. These findings are consistent with those of Wayant et al,9 who reviewed 182 phase II/III oncology trials published in 2021 and found that none disaggregated adverse event data for AYAs within the overall sample. By failing to separate AYA outcomes, these trials precluded any comparative assessment of treatment toxicity, discontinuation rates or dose modifications between AYAs and older adults—thereby making it impossible to include the evidence in future AYA research.

Federal research policies in the USA explicitly require reporting participants’ age at enrolment (eg, National Institutes of Health’s ‘Inclusion Across the Lifespan’ policy).12 Cancer-specific initiatives similarly recognise age as an essential data element. For example, the NCI’s Genomic Data Commons designates both age at enrolment and age at diagnosis as core clinical data variables.13 Likewise, national cancer registry standards enforced by the Centers for Disease Control Prevention and NCI mandate that registries capture age at diagnosis, allowing no more than 3% missing data.14 These standards underscore that age at diagnosis should be treated as a mandatory variable in cancer datasets.

Despite these policies and recognised importance of accurate age reporting, publication guidelines often fail to uphold equivalent standards. For example, the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology checklist encourages reporting of demographic characteristics but does not explicitly require reporting age at enrolment or age at diagnosis.15 16 The NCI’s AYA Progress Review Group has emphasised the importance of defining the AYA age range and urged researchers to justify age-related inclusion criteria.17 However, it provides no specific guidance on reporting participants’ age at diagnosis—an essential metric for accurately identifying AYAs in study samples. As a result, AYAs may be included in studies, but their data are frequently obscured within summary measures for the overall population. In contrast, international efforts such as the European Society for Medical Oncology’s GROW (Global Registry of Oncology in Women) framework (2023) explicitly recommend distinguishing between age at diagnosis and age at treatment initiation.18

We also acknowledge that in jurisdictions with small cancer populations (eg, at the zip code or county level) or for uncommon cancer subtypes, local privacy regulations (eg, suppression of cells with fewer than 10 individuals) may limit the researcher’s ability to report age-at-diagnosis at the level of granularity desired publicly. In such cases, we recommend that authors explicitly state these constraints rather than omitting age-at-diagnosis information entirely. Alternatively, if aggregate results can be provided for an entire subgroup of AYAs without identifying their small area location (eg, zip code or county), this could provide an alternative route to share the AYA-pertinent results. Aggregate disclosures, such as ‘age-at-diagnosis data cannot be reported at the subgroup level in this jurisdiction due to privacy requirements’, preserve transparency about the study’s AYA composition even when detailed metrics are not feasible. Emerging data sharing techniques such as privacy-preserving record linkages or federated data systems that allow researchers to analyse data remotely without compromising patient privacy may also help address this challenge over time.19

In conclusion, the lack of standardised age-specific reporting, particularly age at cancer diagnosis, contributes to ongoing inconsistencies in identifying and characterising AYAs in cancer research. We recommend that future reporting guidelines explicitly require both age-at-enrollment and age-at-diagnosis metrics, including mean, median, range and the number of participants diagnosed between ages 15 and 39. Beyond reporting age-at-diagnosis metrics at the study level, we further recommend that studies with mixed-age samples report outcomes stratified by AYA status (ages 15–39 vs other age groups) when the sample size permits. Without such results stratification, even studies that accurately characterise their sample’s age composition may remain unusable for AYA evidence synthesis—this was demonstrated by Wayant et al,9 who found that none of the oncology trials they reviewed reported outcomes separately for AYAs.9 Expanding access to age-at-diagnosis data from national health surveys (eg, National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, National Health Interview Survey, Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System) in a privacy-preserving manner would enhance data comparability and enable more rigorous analyses.20 Authors, journal editors and peer reviewers, who are positioned to enforce reporting standards at the point of publication, also have a critical role to play in advancing more accurate and comprehensive reporting for AYA populations. Greater consistency in age-related reporting will support efforts to synthesise research findings and the development of tailored, evidence-based interventions to improve disparities among this vulnerable population.
