Efficacy of a Dairy‐Free Diet in Adult Patients With Eosinophilic Esophagitis: A Pilot Prospective Multicenter Study
Authors: Beatrice Marinoni, Giovanni Aldinio, Federica Mascaretti, Roberto Penagini, Elisa Marabotto, Francesco Calabrese, Andrea Pasta, Elena Formisano, Pierfrancesco Visaggi, Nicola Debortoli, Marialuisa Guarciariello, Domenico Gargano, Paola Iovino, Matteo Ghisa, Daria Maniero, Edoardo Vincenzo Savarino, Marina Coletta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) is a chronic, immune‐mediated disease in which elimination diets represent an effective therapeutic option. Although six‐food elimination diets (6‐FED) achieve high remission rates, their complexity often compromises adherence. Dairy‐free diet (DFD) has emerged as a simpler alternative, yet evidence in adult populations remains limited.AimsTo evaluate the clinical, endoscopic, and histological outcomes of a 12‐week DFD in adult patients with EoE and to explore potential predictors of histological remission and dietary adherence.MethodsWe conducted a prospective, multicenter, open label, single‐arm interventional study across five Italian referral centers, enrolling 31 adults with active EoE. Participants followed a 12‐week DFD, after which clinical symptoms (EEsAI), disease severity (I‐SEE), endoscopic findings (EREFS), quality of life (EoE‐QOL‐A), and adherence (VAS scale) were assessed.ResultsTwenty‐eight patients completed the 12‐week protocol. Histological remission was achieved in 53.6% of patients (intention‐to‐treat: 48.4%); among these, 86.7% also reached clinical remission. The median EEsAI improved from 35 to 18.5 (p = 0.009) and the median I‐SEE score from 6.5 to 4 (p < 0.0001). Quality of life improved (median EoE‐QoL‐A from 36.5 to 52, p = 0.02), and adherence was high (median VAS 8 [IQR 7–9.5]). Predictors of lower histological response included previous esophageal dilations and higher baseline exudate and eosinophil counts. Male sex and higher BMI predicted lower adherence.ConclusionsIn this exploratory multicenter prospective study, DFD induced histological remission in more than half of patients and clinically meaningful improvements in symptoms, disease severity, endoscopy, and quality of life.Keywords: dairy‐free diet, empiric elimination diets, eosinophilic esophagitis, eosinophils, esophagus, one‐food elimination diet

Introduction

Eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) is a chronic, immune‐mediated disease characterized by eosinophilic inflammation of the esophagus and symptoms of esophageal dysfunction (i.e., dysphagia, chest pain, and food bolus impaction) [1]. Over the past two decades, EoE has been increasingly recognized as a distinct clinical entity, with a significant rise in prevalence worldwide [2, 3]. It is now considered a leading cause of dysphagia and food bolus impaction in young adults [4, 5, 6] and is strongly associated with atopic conditions, including asthma and allergic rhinitis [3]. However, the symptoms of EoE are often subtle and insidious, leading to significant diagnostic delays as patients adopt coping strategies [7, 8, 9]. For these reasons, EoE profoundly impacts patients' quality of life (QoL) and often requires multiple strategies to achieve symptom control and histological remission [10, 11].

Elimination diets have been established in the latest clinical guidelines as an effective first‐line therapies for EoE [12, 13]. These diets work by systematically removing potential food triggers of esophageal inflammation and subsequently reintroducing them to identify specific culprits. Among the available strategies, the six‐food elimination diet (6‐FED), which excludes dairy, wheat, soy, eggs, nuts, and seafood, has been shown to achieve the highest rates of clinical and histological remission [14, 15]. However, its benefits come with notable drawbacks. The restrictive nature of 6‐FED poses significant challenges for patient adherence, often leading to poor compliance over time [15, 16]. Furthermore, the exclusion of multiple food groups increases the risk of nutritional deficiencies, necessitating careful monitoring and dietary counseling. Indeed, in the recent guidelines, a step‐up approach (i.e., starting with least restrictive diets first) has been recommended, in order to reduce unnecessary dietary burden and endoscopies [12, 13].

Because dairy products are among the most common triggers of EoE, an approach starting with a one‐food elimination diet (1‐FED) targeting animal milk represents a reasonable strategy, as a more practical and patient‐friendly alternative [12, 17].

Despite the promise of this strategy, there is a paucity of prospective data evaluating the clinical, endoscopic, and histological outcomes of elimination diets, especially in adults. Current evidence on dietary interventions is largely derived from pediatric cohorts or generalized elimination protocols [14, 18, 19], leaving a critical gap in understanding the efficacy of targeted dietary exclusions for adults.

To address this gap, our study explores the clinical, endoscopic, and histological responses to a 12‐week course of a dairy‐free diet (DFD) and predictive factors of adherence in adult patients diagnosed with EoE.

Materials and Methods

Patient Recruitment

From April 2023 to February 2025, patients aged 18–85 years with a confirmed diagnosis of EoE and evidence of histologically active disease were prospectively enrolled at five Italian referral centers for EoE (Milan, Salerno, Padova, Genoa, and Pisa). The diagnosis was established according to international consensus criteria, requiring both the presence of symptoms of esophageal dysfunction and a peak eosinophil count of ≥ 15 eosinophils per high‐power field (Eos/HPF) in at least one esophageal biopsy [20]. Patients with critical esophageal strictures were excluded, defined as severe rings (R3) or strictures (S1) according to the Endoscopic Reference Score (EREFS) [21].

The DFD was prescribed to eligible patients with one of the following indications: (1) as a first‐line therapy for newly diagnosed EoE; (2) as an add‐on therapy for EoE patients on proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) with symptom improvement but persistent histological activity; (3) as second‐line therapy in patients with neither clinical nor histological response to PPI therapy; and (4) in PPI responders (clinical and histological) who wanted to stop pharmacological therapy. Patients who received DFD as add‐on therapy to PPI and were on PPI at study enrollment continued the same dosage and regimen throughout the study.

Study Design

This was a prospective, multicenter, open label, single‐arm interventional study evaluating the effects of a 12‐week DFD in adult patients with EoE. All participants were assigned to the same dietary intervention without randomization or a control group.

All participants underwent a baseline clinical assessment that included symptoms evaluation with the Eosinophilic Esophagitis Activity Index (EEsAI) [22], disease severity assessment through the International Symptoms Severity and Endoscopic Evaluation (I‐SEE) score [23] and QoL assessment using the EoE‐QOL‐A questionnaire [24]. No control group was included, as the study aimed to prospectively evaluate real‐world effectiveness and adherence to a DFD.

Considering histological remission as primary endpoint, the sample size was determined using a one‐sided exact binomial test (H0: p ≤ 0.05; H1: p > 0.05), with α = 0.05 and a power of 0.90, based on a historical 1‐year spontaneous remission rate of 5%. Assuming an expected histological remission rate of 25% and a dropout rate of 10%, at least 27 patients were considered necessary, as a relatively lower histological response rate was foreseen due to possible suboptimal compliance.

Study Procedure

An esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) was performed at baseline to assess endoscopic and histological activity; if the patient was already in histological remission, the latest EGD was considered the baseline examination. Endoscopic disease activity was evaluated according to the EREFS [21]. Histological activity was evaluated by collecting at least six samples from at least two sites among proximal, mid‐, and distal esophagus.

Dietary counseling was delivered by a nutritional biologist specialized in clinical nutrition and gastrointestinal diseases. All participants received a structured baseline counseling session focused on the complete elimination of milk‐containing products derived from cow, goat, or sheep. During this session, patients received detailed instructions on how to identify and avoid dairy products, including hidden sources of milk proteins in processed foods through careful ingredient label reading. Counseling also included practical guidance on nutritionally appropriate substitutions to maintain adequate dietary intake, particularly with regard to calcium and protein sources, as well as recommendations for balanced meal planning. Strategies to prevent cross‐contamination at home and when eating outside were also discussed. Educational materials summarizing dietary recommendations were provided to support adherence. Participants were instructed to maintain their usual dietary habits apart from the elimination of dairy products. No specific dietary patterns (e.g., vegan, gluten‐free, or Mediterranean diets) were required for study participation. A follow‐up phone call was conducted during the intervention period to assess adherence, reinforce dietary recommendations, and address any questions or concerns that arose during the intervention.

Endoscopic evaluation with esophageal biopsies was rescheduled after the 12‐week (±1 week) DFD to reevaluate both endoscopic and histological activity. At follow‐up, patients repeated all the assessments and completed a visual analog scale (VAS) to self‐report adherence to diet.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were clinical and histological remission after 12 weeks of DFD. Clinical activity was assessed using the EEsAI, a validated patient‐reported outcome measure ranging from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms [22]. Clinical remission was defined as an EEsAI score ≤ 20, according to validated thresholds [25, 26, 27]. Histological remission was defined as a peak eosinophil count < 15 Eos/HPF in all evaluated biopsies [1].

Secondary outcomes included improvement in endoscopic findings, QoL, and disease severity after 12 weeks of DFD. Endoscopic findings were assessed using the EREFS score, a validated scoring system specifically developed for EoE. The EREFS score evaluates and grades five key endoscopic features: edema, rings, exudates, furrows, and strictures [21]. Disease severity was assessed using the Index of Severity for Eosinophilic Esophagitis (I‐SEE), a composite validated score including symptoms, inflammatory features, and fibrostenotic findings [23]. Disease‐specific QoL was assessed using the validated EoE‐QoL‐A questionnaire, with higher scores indicating better QoL [24]. Dietary adherence was assessed using a VAS (range 0–10), where higher scores indicated greater perceived adherence to the DFD, and analyzed as a continuous variable. In addition, we planned an exploratory secondary analysis comparing patients who achieved histological remission after 12 weeks of DFD with those who did not in order to better characterize treatment response and explore baseline predictors of remission and dietary adherence.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the study population. Continuous variables, including EEsAI score, I‐SEE score, EREFS score, peak eosinophil count, EoE‐QoL‐A score, and adherence VAS score, were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical variables, including the presence of symptoms (e.g., dysphagia, food impaction, chest pain, heartburn, vomiting/regurgitation, and odynophagia) and remission status, were presented as counts and percentages. Changes after treatment were assessed using Wilcoxon signed‐rank test for continuous variables and McNemar's test for categorical variables. Comparisons for continuous variables were conducted using Mann–Whitney U test. For categorical variables, associations were evaluated using chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate. ANCOVA models were employed to compare outcomes between histological responders and nonresponders at 12 weeks, adjusting for baseline values to control for potential confounding due to initial differences.

Multivariable logistic and linear regression models were constructed to explore baseline factors potentially associated with histological remission and adherence, respectively. Baseline variables considered included age, sex, BMI, smoking status, previous therapies, prior esophageal dilation, baseline symptoms and severity (EEsAI), disease severity (I‐SEE), endoscopic findings (EREFS), and peak eosinophil count.

Candidate variables for these models were preselected based on their clinical relevance or statistical significance in preliminary bivariate analyses. Results were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) for the logistic regression and β coefficients for linear regression. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. Data management and analysis were performed using JMP Version 18 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee (ethics approval number 81_2023) and performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study. The data will be available upon reasonable request. The study was partially supported by the Italian Ministry of Health, Current Research IRCCS.

Results

A total of 31 adult patients were initially enrolled in the study. However, three patients did not complete the 12‐week DFD because they chose not to continue the dietary intervention, leaving 28 patients available for final analysis. Before starting the DFD, dysphagia and food impaction were the predominant symptoms, affecting 75.0% and 42.3% of patients, respectively. Overall Cohort Results

After 12 weeks of the DFD, prevalence of dysphagia significantly decreased from 75.0% to 28.6% (p = 0.0008), as food impaction from 42.3% to 17.9% (p = 0.008). Reductions were also observed in chest pain (14.3%–7.1%, p = 0.32), epigastric discomfort (28.6%–14.3%, p = 0.10), heartburn (21.4%–14.3%, p = 0.41), vomiting/regurgitation (17.9%–10.7%, p = 0.32), and odynophagia (14.3%–3.6%, p = 0.08). The I‐SEE score significantly reduced from a median of 6.5 (IQR 6–8) to 4 (IQR 2–6) (Wilcoxon signed‐rank test S = −154.5, p = < 0.0001) after DFD regimen. Similarly, the median EEsAI score decreased from 35 (IQR 27–66) to 18.5 (IQR 0–37) (Wilcoxon signed‐rank test S = −98.00, p = 0.009). In addition, the endoscopic EREFS score and the peak eosinophilic count decreased to 2 (IQR 1–3) and 14 Eos/HPF (IQR 0–48) after DFD regimen (p = < 0.0001 and p = 0.005, respectively).

QoL, assessed by the EoE‐QoL‐A, significantly improved from a median of 36.5 (IQR 24.5–58) to 52 (IQR 30.5–71) after the intervention (Wilcoxon signed‐rank test S = 99.5, p = 0.02). Adherence to the DFD was generally high, with a median VAS adherence score of 8 (IQR 7–9.5) after 12 weeks. At 24 weeks, 68% of patients continued the DFD, and the median VAS adherence score was 8 (IQR 8–9).

Histological Remission vs. Non‐Remission

Fifteen out of 28 patients (53.6%) were on histological remission after the DFD, among which 13 were also on clinical remission (86.7%; Figure 3). In an intention‐to‐treat analysis including all 31 enrolled patients, assuming nonresponse for the three patients who discontinued the study before follow‐up endoscopy, the histological remission rate was 48.4% (15/31). Among those 13 patients without proven histological remission, 4 (30.8%) were only on clinical remission after the DFD. A comparison between patients with and without histological remission is presented in Table 2. Patients on histological remission after 12 weeks of diet had significantly lower EEsAI (12 [0–18.5] vs. 36 [15–5‐56.5], p = 0.01) and I‐SEE scores (2 [1–3.5] vs. 6 [4–8], p = 0.0006). Moreover, they also improved endoscopic items: EREFS (1 [1, 2] vs. 2.5 [1–5], p = 0.03) and median peak eosinophilic count (0 [0–3] vs. 50 [28–121.5], p < 0.0001) significantly decreased compared with patients who did not reach histological response. In the ANCOVA models, adjusting for baseline values, histological response remained significantly associated with lower EEsAI scores (F 1,23 = 10.9, p = 0.003) and lower I‐SEE scores (F 1,24 = 18.4, p = 0.0003) at 12 weeks. Predictors of Histological Remission

Among the variables assessed at baseline, those included in the final logistic regression model were any previous dilations, baseline exudate score, and peak eosinophil count. Specifically, patients with previous dilations were less likely to have histological remission compared to those without previous dilations (adjusted OR = 0.06, 95% CI 0.001–0.87, p = 0.04). Regarding the presence of exudate at baseline, the analysis showed that, compared to patients with an exudate score of 0, those with a score of 1 had an 89% reduction in the odds of histological remission (adjusted OR = 0.11, 95% CI 0.004–1.00, p = 0.05), whereas those with a score of 2 had a 96% reduction in the odds of histological remission (adjusted OR = 0.04, 95% CI 0.001–0.79, p = 0.03). For peak esophageal count at baseline, each unit increase was associated with a 2% reduction in the odds of histological remission (adjusted OR = 0.98, 95% CI 0.94–1.00, p = 0.12).

The model explained 31% of the variability in the histological remission (R 2 = 0.31, p = 0.02). Despite the small sample size, the model demonstrated high numerical stability and robustness, achieving successful convergence in only five iterations with well‐controlled standard errors (maximum 1.47). Furthermore, an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.83 and a nonsignificant lack‐of‐fit test (p = 0.40) confirmed the reliability of the maximum likelihood estimates without evidence of data separation.

Predictors of Adherence to Diet

Among the variables assessed at baseline for adherence, those included in the final linear regression were sex and BMI. Male sex was associated with lower adherence compared to female sex (β = −1.10, 95% CI −1.82 to −0.39, p = 0.004), whereas a higher BMI was associated with lower adherence scores (β = −0.14, 95% CI = −0.30–0.02, p = 0.08). The model explained 41% of the variability in the DFD adherence VAS score (R 2 = 0.41, p = 0.003). The reliability of these estimates was confirmed through residual analysis, showing a random distribution of errors.

Discussion

Our real‐life, multicenter prospective study provides exploratory evidence suggesting that a DFD may be an effective initial dietary intervention for adult patients with EoE. Symptom relief was substantial across the entire cohort, with significant reductions in dysphagia and food impaction. Objective measures improved in parallel: The median EEsAI score decreased from 35 to 18.5, whereas disease severity, assessed via the I‐SEE composite score, declined from 6.5 to 4. Similarly, endoscopic and histological outcomes improved, as both the median EREFS score and the peak eosinophil count decreased significantly. Importantly, after 12 weeks of DFD, more than half of patients (53.6%) achieved histological remission.

Cow's milk protein (CMP) is consistently identified as the most common dietary trigger in EoE [18]. In Mediterranean populations such as Italy, dairy products are commonly consumed as part of the habitual diet, which may increase exposure to milk proteins and potentially contribute to their role as a major dietary trigger [28]. Evidence on dietary interventions is largely derived from pediatric cohorts. A prospective pediatric study of a single‐food milk elimination diet, including 41 children (mean age of 9 years), reported histological remission in 51% of cases, with significant improvement in symptoms and endoscopic abnormalities, regardless of whether histological remission was achieved or not [18, 29]. In addition, when 1‐FED was compared with a four‐food elimination diet (4‐FED), histological, endoscopic, and QoL outcomes were similar, suggesting that 1‐FED is a reasonable first‐choice therapy in pediatric EoE due to its efficacy, tolerability, and relative simplicity [30].

In adults, the first multicenter randomized trial was published in 2023. In this study, 129 PPI‐refractory EoE patients were randomized to either milk‐only elimination (1‐FED) or 6‐FED. After 6 weeks, both groups showed improvements in clinical and endoscopic outcomes. Histological remission was achieved in 34% of patients on 1‐FED and 40% on 6‐FED, with no significant difference between groups [31]. Our data corroborate and reinforce the efficacy of the milk‐only elimination, further extending the period of observation and follow‐up at 12 weeks (vs. 6 weeks) and evaluating compliance to diet after 6 months of treatment. Overall, these results suggest that eliminating animal milk alone may represent a feasible first‐line dietary strategy for EoE patients, although confirmatory studies are needed.

In our cohort, more than half of patients (53.6%) achieved histological remission after 12 weeks of the DFD, and the majority of them (86.7%) also attained clinical remission. As expected, histological responders showed lower EEsAI, I‐SEE, and EREFS scores, indicating not only a histological response but also meaningful clinical improvement. Regression analyses identified predictors of lower likelihood of histological remission, including prior dilations, higher baseline exudate scores, and elevated distal eosinophilic counts. These analyses should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size, which limited statistical power; thus, the results should be considered exploratory and hypothesis‐generating. Nonetheless, these findings suggest that patients with a more severe phenotype are less likely to benefit from dietary therapy alone and may require alternative strategies [31, 32].

QoL improved significantly in our cohort following the DFD, reflecting reduced disease burden and acceptable dietary constraints. This is consistent with previous studies reporting QoL improvement in both pediatric and adult populations [17, 18, 30, 33]. Interestingly, QoL improvement was independent of clinical and histological remission. This suggests that benefits may stem not only from symptomatic and endoscopic improvements but also from the psychological reassurance of initiating therapy and feeling supported by the medical team.

The acceptability of this dietary regimen was confirmed by the high adherence scores. Furthermore, 68% of patients continued the DFD at 6 months, suggesting medium‐term sustainability. This contrasts with the high dropout rates and poor long‐term adherence typically observed with 6‐FEDs [18]. The simplicity of excluding a single food group likely explains this improved adherence. Nonetheless, involvement of a nutrition specialist remains essential to ensure nutritional adequacy and provide patient support, as recommended by both pediatric and adult guidelines [12, 13]. In our study, male sex and higher BMI predicted poorer adherence. Males were more severe patients, with a higher prevalence of prior dilations, whereas higher BMI may pose challenges in maintaining restrictive dietary regimens in this subgroup.

The strengths of our study include its prospective design in a real‐life setting, exclusive inclusion of adults, and multicenter recruitment across five Italian referral centers, enhancing generalizability. We used validated clinical and endoscopic scores and incorporated an adherence score to objectively assess compliance. Moreover, the evaluation of adherence at 6 months provides preliminary evidence for medium‐term sustainability of DFD. However, several limitations should be acknowledged, as the relatively small sample size and the absence of a control group. Moreover, the open‐label design could have introduced expectation bias, and continuation of PPI therapy in a subgroup of patients may have confounded histological outcomes. In addition, the self‐administered questionnaires used in this study have not been validated in Italian. Long‐term durability of remission, the effects of dairy reintroduction, and detailed nutritional assessment were not addressed, limiting broader conclusions. Also, dietary adherence was assessed using a self‐reported VAS, which, although practical and commonly used in clinical research, has not been formally validated for adherence assessment in EoE elimination diets, and we cannot exclude the possibility that some participants may have independently modified other dietary components during the intervention period, which could potentially influence treatment response.

Future research should explore long‐term outcomes of DFD, including sustainability of remission, nutritional adequacy, and the impact of intermittent dairy exposure. Comparative trials of DFD plus PPI versus DFD alone would clarify the additive role of acid suppression. Biomarker‐driven studies, integrating transcriptomic and serologic predictors [17], could help identify patients most likely to respond to dietary therapy. Furthermore, evaluation of processed or baked dairy products and goat‐derived proteins may broaden dietary options for responders [18]. Finally, larger, prospective adult cohorts assessing step‐up strategies will help determine the optimal sequencing of elimination diets to maximize efficacy while minimizing burden.

In conclusion, DFD achieved clinically meaningful improvements in adult EoE, inducing histological remission in half of patients while improving symptoms, endoscopic appearance, disease severity, and QoL. Together with pediatric and adult trial data [17, 18, 29], our findings support dairy elimination as an effective and practical first‐line dietary strategy. Given its simplicity, favorable adherence profile, and positive impact on patient‐reported outcomes, DFD may represent a valuable therapeutic option. However, these findings require confirmation in larger, randomized controlled trials involving broader patient populations. Further studies are needed to confirm these results, refine patient selection, optimize long‐term outcomes, and integrate biomarkers into personalized dietary management.
