 ABSTRACT
The US nuclear industry is investigating a transition to the use of a fuel performance–based licensing
strategy known as “time-at-temperature” over the traditional complete avoidance of critical heat flux
(CHF), which has been shown to be overly conservative for certain classes of transients. This switch would
improve the flexibility of plant operation, save on fuel costs, and allow for power uprates, thus improving
the efficiency and output of the current fleet of light-water reactors (LWRs). It presents new challenges
that will require a better understanding of the phenomena that impact fuel performance and plant safety.
The Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS) program has been working to improve
the validation and applicability of its tools for this application space to support the nuclear industry. This
report documents recent efforts during fiscal year 2026 to improve the LWR thermal hydraulics subchannel
code, CTF, that is developed by the NEAMS program. This work has resulted in the addition of two new
validation datasets: one focused on CHF prediction and one focused on post-CHF heat transfer prediction.
These activities, coupled with efforts in previous years, have improved our understanding of the CTF
modeling approach and have resulted in the discovery of opportunities for improvement in the modeling
approach. Additionally, significant effort has been devoted to the application of Bayesian methods for
calibration and uncertainty quantification (UQ) of the CTF models that impact post-CHF figures of merit,
including occurrence of dryout and peak temperatures in post-CHF regions. This study has found that
applying this method to the full range of pre- and post-CHF conditions simultaneously is challenging due
to the complexity of the model and the large number of interacting closure models. Initial estimates of
uncertainty distributions have been determined for models of interest, but future work is needed to confirm
their applicability for a variety of cases and to explore more efficient UQ methods.

 1. INTRODUCTION
The US nuclear industry is investigating a transition from the traditional critical heat flux (CHF)-avoidance
licensing methodology toward a materials-based licensing framework for fuel used in the current
fleet of light-water reactors (LWRs). Under the existing approach, operating limits are established
such that critical heat flux (CHF) is not reached during anticipated operational occurrences, including
moderate-frequency events such as turbine trips, thereby preventing significant changes in fuel performance.
However, reactor operating experience has demonstrated that brief excursions into post-CHF conditions
do not necessarily result in fuel damage. Plant observations involving transient dryout followed by rewet
have found no measurable degradation in fuel performance for sufficiently mild events [17]. Likewise,
controlled experiments conducted at the Halden reactor have confirmed that temporary dryout under
appropriate conditions does not adversely affect fuel integrity [14, 15].
A materials-based licensing philosophy, often referred to as a time-at-temperature (t@T) approach, would
replace the strict avoidance of CHF with acceptance criteria based on cladding temperature and exposure
duration. Rather than prohibiting the onset of dryout, this methodology would demonstrate that the fuel
cladding remains below an allowable temperature for an allowable period during limiting transients.
Adoption of this strategy has the potential to increase operational flexibility, improve fuel utilization,
enable reactor power uprates, and reduce operating costs across the existing LWR fleet. It is anticipated
that the boiling water reactor (BWR) fleet will benefit more significantly from this approach, as the
temperature excursion of a dryout is less severe than in departure from nucleate boiling (DNB) conditions,
which is why Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS) has been focusing more on
BWR conditions in the near term; however, it is expected that the pressurized water reactor (PWR) fleet
will also benefit from improved dose consequence analysis, so future plans will cover PWR conditions as
well.

 1

Implementing this licensing paradigm may benefit from a corresponding shift toward best estimate plus
uncertainty (BEPU) analysis methods to prevent excess conservatisms that may make a t@T approach
infeasible. Accurate prediction of CHF, post-CHF heat transfer regimes—including transition boiling, film
boiling, and inverted annular film boiling—and the subsequent rewet process becomes essential. These
physical models must be rigorously validated and their associated uncertainties quantified so that cladding
t@T can be predicted with sufficient confidence for use in licensing evaluations.
The NEAMS program has been supporting the US nuclear industry over the past few years toward the t@T
initiative by developing, demonstrating, and validating its advanced modeling and simulation tools for this
particular application. In FY24, the NEAMS Thermal Fluids (TF) focus area led an initial assessment of
the thermal hydraulics (T/H) subchannel code CTF for the t@T application; in that work, a validation of
CTF with the Harwell tests was performed, focusing on post-CHF heat transfer and the BWR full bundle
tests (BFBT) turbine trip and pump trip tests [18]. That report also documented the implementation of
a new post-CHF model—heat transfer regime selection and heat transfer models for transition boiling
(TB), inverted annular film boiling (IAFB), and dispersed flow film boiling (DFFB) regimes—which
significantly improved the prediction of dryout in the Harwell cases. In FY25, the NEAMS TF focus area
expanded this assessment to add the Halden IFA613 tests to the validation basis and performed a sweeping
sensitivity analysis using the Harwell cases to identify high-impact models affecting t@T [12]. This
milestone continues to build on past work by further expanding the validation basis for these applications,
expanding the sensitivity analysis, and beginning to investigate uncertainty quantification (UQ) methods
for better understanding the uncertainty of the models in CTF.
The goal of this milestone is to: 1) continue to improve on the validation of high impact models for
t@T, 2) use Bayesian Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) to determine the uncertainty of models
affecting prediction of t@T figure of merit (FOM)s. Two new validation datasets have been added in this
report: Stewart and Groeneveld [23] and the Korea Atomic Energy Research Institute (KAERI) CHF
database [3]. The former focuses on post-CHF heat transfer models used for predicting clad surface
temperatures, and the latter focuses on the prediction of CHF. Both datasets include both BWR and PWR
conditions. These models have been identified as high-impact models for t@T in previous work [12] by
NEAMS. Furthermore, the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) published the results of a phenomena
identification ranking table (PIRT) that was conducted for t@T; it also ranks post-CHF models as among
the highest sources of importance and uncertainty with regard to t@T. The results of this validation are
documented in Section 2.
The sensitivity analysis utilizing the Harwell tests (originally performed in Kumar et al. [12]) was
performed again after making revisions and improvements to the Harwell models and filtering the cases
to more closely target BWR operating conditions specifically. This analysis is presented in Section 3 and
ensures that the high-impact models discovered in FY25 are still relevant for the current analysis.
Finally, a comprehensive study of the use of Bayesian Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) calibration
was performed targeting the models expected to impact the t@T application. The goal of this analysis
was to develop a formal statement of the uncertainty of all of the models affecting t@T so that those
distributions may later be used in feed-forward UQ studies that will allow us to place bounds on predicted
temperatures. This study is presented in Section 4.

 2. VALIDATION
Validation data for t@T applications was the focus of prior work (see [18] and [12]) with the addition of
the following tests: IFA613, the Harwell tests, and BFBT. In the current study, two additional datasets
have been included for validating the t@T licensing strategy—Stewart and Groeneveld and the KAERI
database—and are documented in the following sub-sections.

2.1 STEWART AND GROENEVELD
Stewart and Groeneveld [22, 23] conducted a series of low-quality and subcooled film boiling tests at
elevated pressures over a range of mass fluxes using the hot patch technique. The hot patch technique is
used to transition from nucleate boiling to film boiling in a short distance, and the dataset is useful for
validating post-CHF models in CTF. The test section has nominal inner and outer diameters of 8.9 mm
and 11.1 mm, respectively, and was uniformly heated by a DC power supply. In addition to the DC power
supply, a hot patch made of copper was soldered to the Inconel 600 tubing and separately powered by
twelve 500 W cartridge heaters. The axial temperature profile was measured during the transient using
thermocouples spot-welded to the test section. The transient data were recorded by setting up a stable
film boiling condition (steady-state value) for a given test section power, followed by a reduction in test
section power for further measurements until the minimum film boiling temperature was reached. The
minimum film boiling temperature was determined as the minimum temperature at which film boiling
could be sustained. Below this temperature, quench fronts propagated upstream and downstream of the
quench point. Of the multiple tests available across pressures and mass fluxes, only tests at nominal system
pressures of 60 bar and 80 bar were chosen for the current validation study. A total of 17 tests were chosen
at the first recorded condition to be modeled in CTF and are summarized in table 1.
The CTF models for the 17 cases were built using a templated input file and a legend file containing the
experimental operating conditions, and the SubKit preprocessor was used to write CTF input files for each
condition. A uniform axial mesh was used for all the cases, with a cell size of 5 cm (∼ 2 in.), resulting
in a total mesh count of 53 cells. This includes an initial 58 cm of unheated length, followed by a 17 cm
heated length before the hot patch, a hot patch length of 10 cm, followed by a 180 cm heated test section.
This is consistent with the heated section length of 197 cm without the hot patch, as specified in Stewart’s
report [22]. If the 10 cm hot patch is taken into account, the total heated length comes out to be 207 cm.
The linear heat rate in the hot patch alone is 2–3 times that in the remaining heated section. The initial
unheated length was modeled to span the inlet thermocouple measurement. Furthermore, the unheated test
section succeeding the heated length was not modeled because only the outlet thermocouple measurement
was available beyond the heated length. Finally, the mesh size was chosen to ensure that the hot patch has
at least two mesh cells.
Some of the model options for setting up the CTF models include the following:
 • Entrainment model enabled
 • Direct solver used for pressure matrix solution
 • IAPWS97 fluid property tables used
 • GE non-proprietary flow regime map [19]
 • Legacy interfacial drag models used
 • Thom boiling model

The model option to force CHF to occur at a specified user location and the option to skip transition
boiling were recently implemented in CTF and will be merged into the master branch of the CTF code
repository. The CHF location is forced as CHF is expected to occur somewhere in the hot patch. In the
current study, the CHF location was set as the end of the hot patch. This keeps the focus of this validation
study solely on the post-CHF heat transfer models. The second newly implemented option allows for
bypassing the transition boiling heat transfer regime and directly setting the code to the film boiling
regime. As a more systematic alternative, minimum film boiling models are being explored to provide a
more physics-based approach to move the code rapidly into the film boiling regime. Datasets such as the
present one could be used to validate the new minimum film boiling models.
The steady-state results for two of the 17 cases—#6531.01 and #8221.01—are shown in Figures 1 and 2,
respectively. The other 15 cases are shown in the appendix. The ‘.01’ in the case numbers denotes the first
experimental measurements once a stable film boiling regime had been established. The cases starting with
‘6’ have a nominal system pressure of 60 bar, and those with ‘8’ have a nominal system pressure of 80
bar. The two cases are at opposite ends of the relative subcooling and relative mass flux range—#8221 is
a low subcooling, low mass flux case, and #6531 is a high subcooling, high mass flux case. Furthermore,
the mass flux of #8221 is closer to prototypical BWR mass fluxes. The main concern in modeling a hot
patch case is the difficulty in predicting the hydraulic and thermal conditions at the end of the hot patch.
Nonetheless, forcing the CHF location and skipping the transition boiling regime were useful in providing

reasonable wall temperature predictions, as seen in Figures 1 and 2. The peak temperature within the hot
patch itself is very high, but the focus is on the area downstream of the hot patch. The main difference in
both the cases is that the high subcooling case has a liquid rich core throughout the post-CHF regime, as
is evident in the mass flow rate plots. For #8221, the continuous liquid phase eventually dries out because
most of the liquid film has been entrained as droplets and the remaining liquid has evaporated.
Examining the results of #6531 more closely, this case is more similar to a DNB than to a high-quality
dryout event, given that the core flow is highly subcooled and the code predicts the liquid to be saturated
only at the end of the tube. Therefore, the majority of the post-CHF heat transfer regime would be
expected to reside in the liquid-rich IAFB regime. The switch to DFFB, especially at such a low void
fraction, needs further examination. Apart from a void fraction criterion, the code uses the Bromley heat
flux to decide on the switch to DFFB [10]. The decrease in the vapor temperature beyond 1.5 m can be
attributed to the interfacial heat transfer between the subcooled liquid and the vapor. This occurs even as
the vapor heat transfer coefficient increases—driven by the increase in vapor velocity—and tends to bring
the vapor temperature closer to the wall temperature at a constant wall heat flux and assuming negligible
boiling and a negligible liquid heat transfer coefficient. The final wall and vapor temperatures are a balance
of all these competing mechanisms; accurately predicting the upstream conditions is therefore necessary to
improve agreement with the experimental data.
Looking at the results of #8221 closely, the experimental temperature profile in the post-CHF regime
shows an approximately linear increase with axial distance. Previous sensitivity studies highlight that
the vapor heat transfer coefficient is predominantly important in the prediction of the wall temperature
[12]. Therefore, it is most likely that radiative heat transfer and droplet impingement are less significant
in the prediction of the wall temperature. Nonetheless, given that the droplet mass flow rate is relatively
high, cases such as this one can be used to perturb the droplet impingement heat transfer model. Like
the previous case, the interfacial heat transfer would also need to be analyzed, especially after liquid film
dryout, to confirm the evaporation of the droplets to vapor. The loss of the continuous liquid film also led
to a spike in the void fraction, which again points to the evaporation of liquid droplets to vapor. The spike
in wall temperature as the code switches from IAFB to DFFB needs to be examined as it is expected to
be a smooth transition between the two heat transfer regimes. The decrease in the vapor convective heat
transfer coefficient after the dryout of the continuous liquid phase, and its subsequent increase, would also
need to be further analyzed to improve the temperature prediction.
A comparison of the wall temperature predictions with the experimental measurements in the post-CHF
region from all 16 cases is shown in Figure 3. The errors at the tail end of the distributions can be better
understood from the temperature difference comparison plots shown in Figure 4. Three of the four plots

A trend can be observed in the comparison plots: the high-quality cases, in which the temperature is

in the IAFB regime and a brief increase in wall temperature when switching to the DFFB regime and/or
dryout of the continuous liquid phase, which leads to a decrease in wall temperature. This requires further
examination.

2.2 KAERI DATABASE
An extensive experimental CHF database has been digitized and published online by Bourisaw [3].
Overall, 1539 CHF test cases were collected/digitized from the KAERI report by Park [16], which
comprised 651 uniform heating measurements and 888 non-uniform heating cases. The experimental
setups used water coolant in round tubes with inner diameters of 5.44–28.3 mm and heated lengths of
0.061–7.0 m. These tests, in addition to expanding the range of conditions used to validate the CTF model,
uniquely add a variety of axial power shapes, which are known to impact the CHF and are, therefore, a
valuable addition to the CTF CHF database. The database also includes data from a variety of different
authors, lessening the chance that experimental biases and uncertainties impact the analysis. Version 2 of
the database was used in this validation exercise.
The database provides the diameter of the tube, which is used to build a tube in CTF with flow on the
inside. The tube thickness is arbitrarily set to 3 mm in CTF, which should not affect results because all
cases are steady-state. The axial mesh size is uniform and targets a mesh cell height of 75 mm; however,
actual mesh cell height will vary slightly because the tube heights are not perfectly divisible by the targeted
height. Additionally, different experiments have different tube heights.
Some options used for setting up the CTF models include:
 • Entrainment model enabled
 • Direct solver used for pressure matrix solution
 • IAPWS97 fluid property tables used
 • Legacy flow regime map used
 • Thom boiling model
 • Legacy interfacial drag models used
Each case was run using the steady-state check feature in CTF. The relative ℓ − ∞ norm criteria were set to
1e − 2 and the relative ℓ − 2 norm criteria were set to 1e − 3. The energy and mass balance criteria were set
to 1e − 3. The maximum allowable timestep size was set to 1 × 10−3 s, but CTF will dynamically pick the
timestep size based on the Courant limit. Both a mesh refinement study and a timestep refinement study
were conducted to confirm these as adequate selections. A variety of CHF modeling options were tested
during this validation.
The database covered a wide range of operating conditions, some of which are far outside of nominal PWR
and BWR operating conditions. To keep the analysis more focused on these conditions, the database cases
were filtered according to the following criteria:
    • q′ <100 kW m−1
    • 200 kg m−2 s−1 < G < 3500 kg m−2 s−1
    • −0.4 < x < 0.0
    • 60 bar < p < 181 bar
The first criterion is for linear heat rate, the second is for inlet mass flux, the third is for inlet equilibrium
quality, and the fourth is for outlet pressure. After filtering, there were 230 uniform cases modeled
and 349 nonuniform cases modeled. The distribution of the experiment operating conditions that were
modeled is shown in Figure 5 for the uniform power distributions and Figure 6 for the nonuniform power
distributions.

The next set of CHF models that were evaluated are the ML-based models that were recently developed
and implemented by Furlong [8]. The first one is the pure ML-based model, meaning there is no existing
model used to bound the model results, and instead a neural network is used to correlate input features
to output features. Note that this model was trained on the Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) CHF
database (the same database used to develop the Groeneveld lookup table), which consisted entirely of
uniform power cases. The first set of results for this model use the purely local formulation, known as
DPGX, which represents the input features of the model (tube diameter, local pressure, mass flux, and
local quality). Results are shown in Figure 13. Note that results are colored by tube diameter, as a trend
was noted for that particular parameter. The model begins to significantly under-predict CHF at larger
tube diameters. Note that the training set for this model included tube diameters up to 16 mm, but the
KAERI database includes tube diameters above 20 mm. A well-known issue with ML techniques is that
they struggle with extrapolation outside of their training database. Still, the ML-based model outperforms
the Groeneveld model for uniform power conditions. Nonuniform power predictive ability degrades
significantly due to the lack of a nonuniform power profile treatment in the ML model.
The other formulation of the pure ML model is the DLPGX formulation, which adds a heated length input
feature, “L”, making the model semi-local. Similar to what was observed when testing this model on the
NRC database, the error improves for the uniform heated cases. The MRE drops to essentially zero, and
the performance for large tube cases improves significantly. However, nonuniform power profile case
predictive behavior did not improve at all.
The hybrid form of the ML CHF model, which uses the Groeneveld correlation to inform the neural
network predictions, is shown in Figure 15. Again, results are sorted by tube diameter. Large tube
diameters are still more difficult for the model to predict; however, the error has decreased significantly
due to the inclusion of the Groeneveld model, which does not have any issue in predicting large tubes. For
both uniform and nonuniform power, the hybrid version of the model is slightly more accurate than the
pure ML version, but nonuniform power profile CHF is still over-predicted.
Results for the semi-local, DLPGX formulation of the hybrid model are shown in Figure 16. The uniform
power profile case is the best predicted of the models tested, and, although nonuniform power profile case
prediction has improved, CHF is still overpredicted compared to Case 3.
As part of this work, a simple correction was made to the ML-based models by applying the Groeneveld
K5 factor to the result, which seeks to capture the effect of non-uniform power profiles on CHF. This was
tested for the pure ML model; however, the K5 factor was applied only to the DPGX formulation, as it is
not clear how it would mix with the semi-local model, which tries to capture the impact of heating length.
Results are shown in Figure 17. As expected, there is no impact on the uniform power cases; however, the
non-uniform power profile cases are significantly more accurately predicted. This version of the model is
now more accurate than any of the other models for the non-uniform power profile cases, indicating that
including a simple correction factor may be a suitable first step to extending the application space of the
ML-based models.

The dataset used for the Sobol’ analysis is the Harwell dataset, which was one of the two datasets covered

Before proceeding with the updated Sobol’ analysis, it was found that the maximum time-step size
used in the Harwell tests was too large to ensure energy balance in some of the tests. Therefore, the
following changes were made to the Harwell template file, which is used to write test-specific CTF input
files: a reduction of the maximum time step to 1E-2s, an increase in the overall solution time to ensure
steady-state is reached, and a correction to the heated wall geometry. The error histogram comparing
the old solution with the corrected input deck is shown in Figure 18. Overall, the tails of a fitted normal
distribution shifted from an overprediction in experimental data to an underprediction, and there was an
accompanying ∼10% increase in the overall error statistics.
For the initial set of sensitivity tests, the force CHF model option discussed in Section 2.1 was also
enabled. The axial locations to force CHF were obtained from the experimental database and rounded off
to the closest CTF node. Following on from the prior study, the selected set of multipliers were chosen
for their impact on the dryout prediction and can be broadly categorized into three buckets: interfacial
and wall drag multipliers, heat transfer coefficient (HTC) multipliers, and entrainment/de-entrainment
rate multipliers. The multipliers are tabulated in Table 2. The range of variation for the majority of the
multipliers found to have little influence in the prior study was considerably expanded to an order of
magnitude. Such a high perturbation value could be justified given the lack of robust benchmarking for
some of these closures, such as the hot wall radiative model, which models the heat transfer between
the dry wall and the vapor and dispersed droplet phases. In the next FY, different post-CHF models will
be tested, which will help in correcting model form errors. Another such model with large uncertainty
is the minimum film boiling model, whose perturbation range was increased to ±300F from ±100F. As
mentioned in Section 2.1, alternative minimum film boiling models are currently being tested to help bring
down the uncertainty in this model. Any new model would need to ensure both transition to film boiling
as well as rewet. In fixing the CHF location, the k_chf parameter is irrelevant in determining the dryout
location, but the CHF is also used in deciding between DFFB and IAFB.

 4. UNCERTAINTY QUANTIFICATION
With the validation basis of the post-CHF models expanded and their sensitivities better understood,
focus turned to their uncertainty for nominal BWR conditions. A well-known method for simultaneously
calibrating a model to data as well as rigorously defining the model uncertainty is the Bayesian approach.
This method has been tested in the past for calibration of two-phase closure models in CTF [13]. In that
study, the focus was mainly on recalibration of the model parameters rather than their uncertainties. In
this study, the method was expanded to post-CHF models, and the discovered uncertainties were then
propagated in the code. First, a demonstration for a simple problem is provided in Section 4.1 to outline
the objectives of the method and to show how it can improve a simple model to better predict synthetic
data as well as capture the uncertainty of the prediction. A parallel is drawn to how the approach is being
used in CTF in this demonstration. Sections 4.2–4.4 document how this approach was extended to CTF.
The Bayesian MCMC approach requires experimental data for calibrating the models and understanding
uncertainty. For all cases in this study, the Harwell cases are used as the data source. This study was
performed in steps, starting with the simplest models and progressing to the full model suite. Section 4.2
shows how the calibration was performed using only the pre-CHF region of the experiments. Section
4.3 shows how the calibration was then performed using only the post-CHF region of the experiments.
And Section 4.4 shows how the calibration was performed for the entire tube in two ways: first, the CHF
location is fixed and, second, the CHF location is solved by CTF. The solved-CHF location is the most
complex case, as it has the most degrees of freedom and active models to be considered in the calibration.
After completing the calibration, the discovered model uncertainties were propagated through the system
to predict unseen Harwell cases, as documented in Section 4.5.

independent, symmetric, and identically distributed around the model predictions, and we further assumed
that these data can be modeled by a Gaussian distribution that takes the following form:

σ2 represents the variance of the data distributed about the model predictions. This variance is composed
of two components, σ2 = θd2 + θσ2 :

 32

 • θd – This is the variance associated with each data sample according to experimental or equipment
 uncertainty. This is often called aleatoric uncertainty. It is irreducible because it is a property of the
 measurement system.
 • θσ – In this work, this is treated as an unknown calibratable parameter that is discovered as part
 of the Bayesian inference computation. This value partially reflects the model form uncertainty;
 however, if θd is mis-specified or is not given as part of the experimental data set, then θσ may take
 on a larger value to reflect unknown uncertainties in the data. This is known as epistemic model
 uncertainty.
We make the further simplification that Equation (8) is an acceptable approximation to the likelihood, and
this likelihood form is used in all cases that follow, meaning the variance associated with each data point is
constant across all inputs. This is known as homoscedastic data uncertainty, which the authors recognize is
a simplification for treating this data variance.
The prior distribution, p(θ), is a free choice in the Bayesian approach. In this work, a flat, uniform prior
with set upper and lower bounds was used in all the calibration tasks.
The integral appearing in the denominator of Equation (6) is normally intractable to compute quickly,
or analytically in most cases, particularly if θ is high-dimensional. This difficulty is avoided by utilizing
a suitable MCMC sampling strategy that draws samples from the posterior, Eq. (6), without directly
computing this integral. In this work, we utilized a Python MCMC sampler package, emcee [7], for this
task. We used a mixture of differential evolution moves and Snooker moves with this sampler, which are
reported to show improved efficiency in high dimensions and robustness to multi-modal distributions [24].
The emcee package utilizes an ensemble of Markov chains, and these chains can be evolved in parallel via
MPI to significantly speed up the overall MCMC calculation.

Examining the Impact of Model Form Error on the MCMC Calibration
The next example considers fitting a linear model to data that exhibit a fundamentally quadratic nature.
This is intended to showcase the difficulties of calibrating a model form that is not consistent with the
true observed physics. This commonly occurs when calibrating closure models because closure model
relationships often lack a complete representation of the physics and are only simplifications of complex
underlying physical processes. Calibrating a simple linear model to non-linear data can lead to greater
uncertainties in the resulting calibrated parameters. In cases where the model form is incorrect, the MCMC
calibration cannot fix this model form error; instead, the reported epistemic model uncertainty can be
abnormally high, and model biases can potentially be absorbed into θσ .
In this example, we generated synthetic data from a nonlinear, quadratic process. Data were generated by
drawing 30 random samples from a normal distribution centered at 0 with a standard deviation of 10 and
then adding this noise to the base quadratic process according to

                                         yd = θ0 + θ1 x + θ2 x2 + ε,                                    (11)

where ε ∼ N(0, 10) with true model parameters of θ0 = 1.0, θ1 = 2.5 and θ2 = 1.5. As before, we assumed
that the reported measurement error is only θd = 1.0 and thus, similar to the previous linear example,
is significantly under-reported relative to the true data noise. The data drawn from this noisy quadratic
process are shown in Figure 27.

In the following CTF closure model calibration sections, the situation is similar to that discussed in
this simplified setup: model form issues can create difficulties in obtaining reliable model parameter
uncertainty estimates. This known difficulty offers a direction for future CTF closure model calibration
studies, which would involve, in addition to the standard Bayesian calibration work presented here, a
model selection procedure or a more complex reversible jump MCMC analysis that considers multiple
model forms simultaneously and reports unified posterior distributions over all considered models [9].
It should also be noted that although this simple example directly calibrated model parameters (i.e.,
coefficients in the linear model), the approach used for CTF model calibration operated at a higher level
by calibrating single multipliers on each exposed model (e.g., multiplier for boiling heat transfer instead
of the coefficients in the boiling heat transfer model). This decision was made based on findings from
a previous activity, in which Bayesian MCMC methods were applied to two-phase closure models in
CTF [13]. In that study, it was found that the calibration complexity rapidly expands when directly
calibrating model coefficients for multiple models. In the current study, it was anticipated that the number
of parameters to consider in the calibration would be significantly larger than in that study due to there
being more models. To make the problem more tractable, the multiplier approach was used. Although
this will not lead to a calibration of the same quality as directly calibrating the model coefficients, it
is anticipated that it will still determine model uncertainty by determining the distribution of model
multipliers, which can be used in future UQ studies.

4.2 CALIBRATION OF PRE-CHF MODELS
Before performing the MCMC study for the post-CHF regime, the pre-CHF region was the focus. Given
the relatively simpler flow physics in this regime, this basically served as a test case for the emcee MCMC
sampler with a weighted mixture of a conventional differential evolution move (weight = 0.7) and a
snooker move (weight = 0.3) [6]. The total number of samples for this study was 30,000, generated using
10 chains and 3000 samples per chain. The first 1000 samples were burn-in generations to allow the
chains to properly mix and converge to a stationary distribution. The datasets used for this study were the
following: Walt, Rohsenow, Thom, and the 50 selected tests from the entire Harwell dataset ([20]). The
residuals for the MCMC analysis for each test were equally weighted by the inverse of their respective
number of total data points to ensure that a single dataset did not dominate the analysis. A uniform prior
was assumed for all parameters. The ranges of all the parameters were fixed at ±50% ([12]), except for the
nucleate boiling heat transfer coefficient, whose upper bound was increased to 2.0 based on initial testing,
and the minimum film boiling temperature multiplier was fixed at ±10◦ F.
The converged posterior distributions for the pre-CHF study are shown in Figure 30, and the MCMC
chains for select parameters are shown in Figure 31. The chains look well mixed, and most variables
show smooth distributions, though some are cut off as the upper bounds are not large enough. Given that
those parameters are not important in this regime, the study was not repeated with increased bounds.
Only two parameters are consequential from the Sobol’ analysis ([12])—the nucleate boiling heat transfer
coefficient and the annular film drag multiplier. The median annular film drag multiplier was ∼0.9 with
a standard deviation σ of ∼0.19, and the median nucleate boiling heat transfer coefficient was ∼1.55
with a σ of ∼0.13. This suggests that the boiling heat transfer should be increased to improve the code’s
over-prediction of wall temperature. This requires a more extensive calibration encompassing additional
tests before propagating this distribution for, say, a multi-physics rod bundle application. Propagating the
median calibrated multiplier of the nucleate boiling heat transfer model confirms the improved prediction
of wall temperature, as shown in Figure 32.

4.3 CALIBRATION OF POST-CHF MODELS
The second stage of the MCMC calibration was to extend it to the post-CHF regime. This was done in
three steps given the difficulties in ensuring that the chains mix properly if the problem is too complex for
the MCMC with the nonlinearities that can arise in the post-CHF regime. Firstly, the parameter space was
reduced to make the problem more tractable. Secondly, alongside the parameter reduction, it was decided
to split the analysis into a post-CHF-only study by forcing the dryout location, covered in this section. This
takes the dryout model and, consequently, the dryout location out of the equation.
The two parameters of consequence identified from the Sobol’ analysis for the post-CHF were the
single-phase vapor heat transfer coefficient and the minimum film boiling temperature. The droplet
deposition heat transfer was added, which was found to be slightly sensitive to the change in the post-CHF
wall temperature. Two more parameters were added to this list because of their perceived importance in
the post-CHF heat transfer regime—the hot wall radiative heat transfer coefficient and the multiplier on the
droplet entrainment in annular flow. It must be noted that the same multiplier is automatically applied to
the de-entrainment model. The final calibratable parameter is the uncertainty in the temperature prediction
given the measurement data, σ, which is covered in the previous section (Section 4.1). The total σ can be
broken into a known component representing the thermocouple measurement uncertainty, taken to be equal
to 1◦ C, and an unknown calibratable component. Expanded bounds were used for four of the five closure
model parameters: ranges of [0.5, 15] were used for the hot wall radiative heat transfer, droplet deposition
heat transfer, and the entrainment model in annular flow; the minimum film boiling heat transfer was
varied over the range of [−300F, 300F]. The single-phase vapor heat transfer coefficient was varied over
the range of [0.5, 1.5], and the unknown sigma was varied over the [1e-8◦ C, 100◦ C] range. The MCMC
analysis was conducted on 50 BWR-type Harwell tests out of 67 potential tests, with the remaining tests
kept in the testing dataset.
The posterior distribution obtained from the converged chains with ∼10,000 effective samples (after
burn-in) is shown in Figure 33. The most significant parameter, the single-phase vapor heat transfer
coefficient, has a median value of ∼0.618. This points to an under-prediction of the wall temperature,
as seen in Figure 19, for example. The hot wall radiative heat transfer and the droplet deposition heat
transfer have median multipliers of ∼10.6 and ∼4.4, respectively, with large sigma values, reflecting
their considerable uncertainties. The entrainment model multiplier returns a uniform distribution, which
can be interpreted as a lack of importance of this parameter in the determination of the post-CHF wall
temperature. This is consistent with the sensitivity analysis result, where the entrainment of liquid droplets
was not found to be sensitive to the wall temperature. Inclusion of a liquid thinning–based dryout model
could reveal the sensitivity of the entrainment model. The minimum film boiling temperature is closer to
the lower perturbation boundary with a mean of ∼238◦ F. This makes it closer to the CHF temperature,
shortening the transition boiling region. Finally, the total sigma value has a median of ∼42.5◦ C, which
indicates that an assumed σ value of 1◦ C significantly underestimates the uncertainty in the exp. data,
given the model. A combination of more experimental data and better closure models and/or finer model
parameters could bring this uncertainty down. The propagation of the posterior distribution multipliers is
covered in Section 4.5.

4.4 CALIBRATION OF PRE- AND POST-CHF MODELS
The second stage of the MCMC analysis involves both the pre- and post-CHF regimes but keeps the dryout
location fixed, with the goal of relaxing this assumption in the final step. The MCMC analysis follows on
from the post-CHF-only calibration with the addition of three pre-CHF sensitive parameters—the nucleate
boiling heat transfer coefficient and the drag coefficients on the liquid film and liquid droplets in the
annular flow regime. Similar to the post-CHF-only analysis, the sigma value is a calibratable parameter.
The ranges for the new parameters also follow from Section 4.3, with extended bounds of [0.5, 10.0] for
the drag coefficients, and more conservative bounds of [0.5, 2.5] for the nucleate boiling heat transfer
coefficient. Similar to the calibration in Section 4.3, this calibration was performed with 50 BWR-type
Harwell tests. The effective number of samples was 10000, with more than 40000 samples discarded as
burn-in. A large number of samples had to be discarded as the chains took time to mix, which points to
poor initial starting values.
The results of the posterior distribution of the MCMC analysis shown in Figure 34 show that the single-phase
vapor heat transfer coefficient has a median value of ∼0.71, which is close to the value obtained in
Section 4.3. The nucleate boiling heat transfer coefficient has a median value of ∼2.1 with a large
uncertainty. The most likely cause for the large uncertainty is the nodalization used in the Harwell cases;
this nodalization results in errors in specifying the exact dryout location, leading to a misclassification
of points at the boundary of the pre- and post- CHF region. The large median value is also most likely
due to the error in the dryout location. A separate pre-CHF-only calibration (not shown) gave a median
value of 1.0, while a pre-CHF analysis of 50 cases from the entire Harwell dataset (similar to Section 4.2
and not shown) gave a median value of 1.15. Small sigma values were observed in both analyses. The
large uncertainties in the droplet drag coefficient and the annular film drag coefficient could be from their
lack of importance in the pre-CHF wall temperature change. The pre-CHF regime has an impact on the
entrainment model, which in turn affects the droplet deposition heat transfer, pushing it to a very low
median value. The hot wall radiative heat transfer coefficient has a median value of ∼13.0, which is more
than 2× the value obtained in Section 4.3. This could be because of the reduced droplet impingement heat
transfer pushing the radiative heat transfer higher to balance it out. Whereas the σ value is 0.5× the value
obtained in Section 4.3, the minimum film boiling heat transfer coefficient is close to the nominal value,
which requires further investigation. The posterior distribution values were not propagated as this was an
intermediate analysis.
The final MCMC analysis with the forced CHF restriction is still ongoing work. The CHF heat transfer
model multiplier was added to the list. However, it was difficult to achieve an adequate mixing of the
chains. Therefore, the FOM was modified from the temperature residual to two independent residuals: the
PCT residual and a dryout location residual. There are two calibratable σ values, one for PCT and one for
dryout location. Preliminary results are promising, as the chains mix better than when only the temperature
residual is used, but more work is required before the analysis can be finalized and the uncertainties can be
propagated.

 5. SUMMARY AND FUTURE WORK
The work presented in this report significantly advances the pedigree of CTF for t@T applications by
incorporating additional validation cases targeted at high-impact models for t@T as well as testing
well-established UQ methods for determination of their uncertainty. A summary of high-level findings
and conclusions of this report is as follows.
 • The form of the current post-CHF model allows for CTF to linger in the TB heat transfer regime for
 large spatial and temporal regions of the solution (found during Stewart and Groeneveld validation).
 The TB regime should be short-lived, with the code quickly entering the DFFB or IAFB regime
 after the CHF event. This was overcome for this study by forcing the code out of that regime with a
 switch implemented in the code; however, a model improvement will be needed to fix this. Another
 method being investigated is to implement a new minimum film boiling temperature (MFB) model
 that reduces the size of the TB region through more accurate prediction of the MFB temperature.
 • The transition logic from IAFB to DFFB can lead to switching to DFFB at relatively low quality and
 can lead to non-physical jumps in the heat transfer behavior that were observed in the Stewart and
 Groeneveld validation cases. The transition criteria will require further review following additional
 validation.
 • The Stewart and Groeneveld validation considered cases only at BWR pressures. This validation
 should be expanded in the future to include PWR pressures as NEAMS begins to shift its focus to
 PWR t@T applications.
 • The Stewart and Groeneveld validation discovered a mean underprediction of post-CHF temperature
 of 22.5 K with a standard deviation of 83.8 K, whereas the Harwell validation (filtered to consider
 only those cases relevant to BWR conditions) found a mean underprediction of post-CHF temperature
 of 35.6 K with a standard deviation of 44.3 K.
 • The KAERI CHF validation study significantly expands the CHF model assessment of CTF,
 particularly by adding non-uniform power profile cases. Additionally, it tested the new ML-based
 CHF models that have recently been implemented in CTF and discovered that they generally
 outperform all existing modeling choices in CTF for uniform power profile cases. A test was
 performed in which the Groeneveld K5 factor was applied to the ML CHF models to capture the
 impact of nonuniform power profiles on CHF, and this resulted in the ML CHF model performing
 the best for nonuniform power cases as well.
 • The updated sensitivity analysis using the Harwell data confirms that the single-phase vapor HTC
 is still the most influential model affecting surface temperature in post-CHF regimes, with other
 models (radiative heat transfer, droplet deposition, IAFB HTC model) having little impact. The
 MFB model impact has increased with the increased sensitivity bounds. This analysis will need to
 be re-run once the issue causing the code to be stuck in the TB regime is resolved.
 • Implementation of Bayesian MCMC for complex and highly nonlinear problems has been challenging.
 The pre-CHF assessment was straightforward, whereas the post-CHF assessment required a
 reduction in the number of parameters considered to achieve convergence. The combined pre- and
 post-CHF assessment worked with a frozen CHF location, but results are inconclusive for the solved
 CHF location. Extending the approach to full calibration of model parameters is likely to be more
 challenging and will have a lower likelihood of success. Extension to more complex models, such
 as rod bundles, will also be intractable, as the run time for the analyses is already on the order of

 51

 one week using 150 processors. Further parallelization is not possible due to the limited number of
 chains that can be used in the approach. Future UQ work should look at the possibility of splitting
 the model assessment (i.e., pre-CHF, CHF, post-CHF) to determine model uncertainties individually
 and then demonstrating their combined propagation for integral tests. This approach will be more
 computationally feasible if shown to work and may be able to focus on model parameter calibration.
Future work that will allow NEAMS to continue to support the US nuclear industry in understanding and
adoption of t@T includes:
 • Expanding validation to include high-pressure Stewart and Groeneveld tests; Iguchi transient
 dryout and re-wet tests [11]; the University of Wisconsin transient dryout and re-wet tests based on
 Duarte [4], which are currently being performed; the Becker post-CHF tube tests, including both
 uniform and non-uniform power profiles [2, 1]; and the EPRI rod bundle CHF tests [5].
 • Exploring alternative approaches to applying Bayesian MCMC for the full range of pre- and
 post-CHF conditions.
 • Performing a more rigorous feed-forward UQ study by utilizing the discovered model uncertainty
 distributions for a wider range of experiments in the CTF validation database.
 • Implementing and testing alternative models for MFB, and implementing and testing alternate DFFB
 and IAFB transition criteria.
 • Performing a Harwell sensitivity analysis and expanding that analysis to other tests, including
 Stewart and Groeneveld and Becker, after implementing additional models to confirm sensitivities.
 • Exploring the application of ML techniques to develop improved post-CHF models by utilizing the
 validation that has been built.

 6. CONCLUSION
This milestone reports on recent efforts by the NEAMS TF technical area to continue to improve the
assessment and applicability of CTF for t@T applications. These efforts were carried out to support the
US nuclear industry in its pursuit of a t@T licensing strategy, which will allow for more efficient use of
nuclear energy but will also require better understanding of phenomena that will impact fuel performance
and plant safety during dryout and rewet events. A recent PIRT that was organized by EPRI found that
post-CHF heat transfer models are among the highest-impact phenomena with the largest uncertainty,
and previous NEAMS sensitivity analysis work has confirmed this as well. The work documented in this
report addresses that finding by increasing the validation basis for both CHF and post-CHF conditions as
modeled by the NEAMS T/H solution code for LWRs, CTF. Additionally, Bayesian MCMC techniques
were deployed to more formally state the uncertainty of the models that will play a role in the prediction
of FOMs during the t@T event. A summary of the major findings of this work is presented in Section 5,
and consideration is given to additional work that can be undertaken to continue to improve on NEAMS’
ability to support the industry in adopting a t@T strategy.
