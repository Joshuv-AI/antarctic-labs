Project summary

Molecule species consume a substantial amount of plasma power (often more than 95%) for their
vibrational quantum (N2 0.29 eV, O2 0.2 eV) is much less than electron energy and makes them
especially important in plasma chemical kinetics. Different channels of relaxation and chemical
reactions rely on these vibrational excited molecules to participate in [1]. Within the air, the oxygen
molecules feature two very low energy metastable singlet states with long lifetimes (Tab 3.1), and
their electronic excitation energy is close to the typical energy of vibrational excitation and
therefore the relative concentration of such metastable particles in the discharges can be very high
and important.

 Oxygen can also form anions as the electronegative gas which consists of molecules with a
high electron affinity and attract electrons. In this case, the additional electron loss term, electrons

attachment to electronegative neutrals, can heavily affect the electron balance. The negative ions
formed as a result of electron attachment can be neutralized quite easily and extremely fast by
recombination of negative ions and positive ions. This process is volumetric and could drive the
plasma into the attachment-controlled regime rather than the diffusion-controlled regime which
requires a higher electron temperature and reduced electric field to sustain the discharge [1]. At
atmospheric pressure, a noble gas feed glow discharge would have a larger electron density gradient
near its periphery where negative ions would form. Near the electrode or dielectric, negative ions
can affect the sheath by its low mobility and enhance the local electric field radially [2]. The
importance of negative ions in the weakly ionized molecular gas discharge instability has been
analyzed and determined to stimulate an attachment instability wave mode [3]. Interestingly, the
pattern dynamics have been proven to be sensitive to oxygen which is an electronegative gas [4].
Acidic electrolytes H2SO4, HCl, HF were found to lower the pattern complexity as the author
speculated the electrolysis products O2, Cl2, F2 of increasing electron affinity restricts the pattern
formation [5]. It is then of interest to investigate the role of oxygen negative ions in the mechanism
of SOP.
 Diagnostics of negative ions usually utilize a mass spectrometer [6] or the laser-induced pho-
todetachment [7] and both of them are well-established methods in low pressure plasma. At at-
mospheric pressure, the thin sheath and less diffusive plasma cause the challenge for mass spec-
trometry to sample the ion species. The laser photodetachment method involves a tunable laser
illuminating a plasma within its beam area and the photon energy exceeds the negative ion’s elec-
tron affinity. Free electrons would be detached from the process and detected as the increase of
electron density, thus a Langmuir probe is generally used to measure the electron saturation current.
However, the atmospheric plasma’s thin and collisional sheath does not fulfill the requirements of
the Langmuir probe measurement. In this case, the current increase can be measured within the
circuit while the electron density measurement is needed by other means. Sasaki et al recently
measured the negative ion species in an atmospheric glow discharge over metallic and liquid cath-
ode [8]. Results showed that O− was the dominant negative ion from oxygen and OH− became
dominant from the dissociative electron attachment of H2O2. On the other hand, the negative ion
species and density have not been conducted for the glow discharge with liquid anode.
 In this chapter, a laser photodetachment of the negative ions in the atmospheric glow discharge
plasma with a liquid anode is performed and the density and dominant species of negative ions are
analyzed. The impact of negative ions on the SOP dynamics is also examined.

 Fig.3.1 shows the schematics of the laser photodetachment experiment. A tunable Nd:YAG
pulsed laser (wavelength of 355 nm, photon energy 3.49 eV) outputting a maximum 100 mJ beam
energy at 4 ns pulse width is used to perform the detachment. Tab 3.2 lists the major negative ions
that would be present in a plasma running in humid air. The energy of photons at 355 nm is higher
than all the listed negative ions and produces photodetachment. The other possible negative ions
such as H−, O− 3are negligible in the experiment. The theoretical ratio of the detached negative ions
versus all negative ions α can be calculated by:

 where n− represents the negative ion density, σ is the photodetachment cross-section, E, S are
the laser pulse energy and beam area, respectively; h, c are Planck constant and speed of light. The
increased current ratio ∆I/I is the ratio of detached negative ions versus free electrons ∆n−/ne
if we assume the discharge current is mainly carried by them. Hence, a normalization factor
 A = n−/ne would be added to transform the current ratio ∆I/I into detachment ratio ∆n−/n−
  when the experimental data is fitted with Eq 3.1. The normalization factor is the density ratio of
all negative ions over free electrons and can be used to estimate the negative ion density. Note that
this detachment ratio depends on the laser fluence at constant photon energy (wavelength) which is
the energy per area and will be saturated at enough high fluence. A focusing lens is put in between
 the plasma column and laser output port to increase the laser fluence. The effective beam area is
 measured by a laser viewing card. The laser beam energy can be adjusted by a delay generator
which triggers the laser pulse and the value is measured by a laser power meter. A small portion of
 the laser beam is diverted by a beamsplitter towards a photodiode to acknowledge the laser pulse
on the oscilloscope. A beam dump is set at the back of the plasma to trap the laser beam for safety.
 The discharge part is very similar to the setup illustrated in section 2.2.1 except for the current
measurement. The detachment current signal would happen in a nanosecond time scale that corre-
sponds to the laser pulse and such current change would only be detectable on the anode where free
electrons drift. Therefore, a shunt non-inductive resistor is put between the liquid and earth ground
whose voltage is measured directly by an oscilloscope. An intensified CCD camera (PIMAX 4) is
implemented and synchronized with the pulsed laser to observe the SOP dynamics when negative
 ions are destroyed by the photodetachment. A 300 ns gate width is used to allow enough emission
of the pattern to be registered.

3.2 Results and Discussion

3.2.1 Current Change at Photodetachment

 Figure 3.2: Photodetachment current temporal change at a 97 mJ laser pulse (discharge current
 32 mA, beam diameter 8.6 mm)

The temporal variation of discharge current as photodetachment laser reached is shown in Fig
3.2. The laser energy is 97 mJ and the beam is unfocused with 8.6 mm diameter. The beam
can cover the majority of the plasma column and shall detach all the negative ions if possible. The
black curve represents the laser pulse that starts 8 ns earlier than the current signal due to the
position difference of the photodiode and plasma column. In principle, the photodetachment
happens almost immediately after the laser. The current pulse has a rise time ∼20 ns and then
decays exponentially at a time constant of 58 ns. The fast decay implies that the free electrons are
reattached with electronegative gas. Note there is also a slower-falling part in the curve whose
origin is unknown. The current signal is also not smooth but coupled with several peaks. It is
plausible the signal transmission is interfered with the impedance in the circuit. Another possible
source is the reflected laser rays shine the plasma column later than the major beam and generate
these secondary peaks which was discussed in [12]. Some curtains and masks are put around the

plasma to reduce this effect as far as possible.

3.2.2 Photodetachment Current Ratio

 Figure 3.3: Normalized photodetachment ratio at an 8.6 mm diameter laser beam (discharge
 current 32 mA, laser pulse 97 mJ, normalized factor 0.104, effective cross section
                                       3.25 × 10−22m2)

According to Eq 3.1, the percentage of detached negative ions would increase and saturate if the
laser pulse energy is high enough. This percentage corresponds to the change of detachment cur-
rent ∆I/I where the largest ∆I/I is achieved when the detachment ratio ∆n−/n− is saturated to
unity. Fig 3.3 plots the change of normalized detachment ratio as a function of pulse energy. Sur-
prisingly, the detachment ratio only reaches 62 % at the highest pulse energy and fails to saturate.
The pulsed laser is expected to provide enough energy for saturation if the major negative species
is O− as [8] showed. Since the laser beam area is known, so is the photon energy. The overall
detachment cross section can be deduced from fitting the data with Eq 3.1 where the normalization
factor and effective photodetachment cross section are set as unknown parameters. Based on the

measured electron density of 1013cm−3 [13], normalization factor 0.104 is calculated which means
∼ 1012cm−3 negative ion density present in the plasma column. It was also reported in [14] that
the negative ion density shall be two orders of magnitude smaller than the electron density. The
fitted effective cross section is in between O2 − and O2− · (H2O) and far below the O− and OH−
meaning O−2 and is the major negative ions. Normally, the negative ions in a low pressure plasma
with oxygen shall be dominated by O−, although research shows the percentage of O2− increases
with the chamber pressure since the charge transfer between O− and O2 (a1∆g) become more im-
portant at higher pressure (>10 mTorr)[15]. Another research also pointed out the importance of
this reaction if a larger reduced electric field and small current density (<10 mA/cm2) exists, O−2
can become dominant even at low pressure [16].

 Figure 3.4: Normalized photodetachment ratio at a 3.7 mm diameter laser beam (discharge
 current 34 mA, normalized factor 0.0434, effective cross section 3.28 × 10−22m2)

 Although the laser pulse energy is limited, the detachment ratio is correlated with the laser
fluence which is energy per area. Therefore, more negative ions can be detached if the laser beam
is focused into a smaller beam. On the other hand, a smaller beam would not be able to cover the
whole plasma and the detachment current only represents the negative ions detached from the

plasma volume within the beam coverage. Therefore, a smaller normalization factor is expected.
Fig 3.4 plots the detachment ratio at a beam diameter of 3.7 mm. As anticipated, the data is closer
to saturation, yet the detachment current abnormally increases and causes a deviation from the
theoretical trend. This anomaly was also found in similar research and is blamed on the laser
ablation on probe [12, 17, 18]. Indeed, a very strong current signal can be found when the laser
beam covers the cathode surface. It also becomes extremely serious when a focused beam is used
and photoelectrons may be involved in this case. Except for the anomaly, the data at smaller pulse
energy do follow the trend and the fitted effective cross section is very close to the unfocused beam
result.
 Fig 3.5 shows the detachment ratio as a function of laser fluence from four different beam sizes.
By plotting the data as a function of laser energy per area, different beam sizes can be compared
with the theoretical curve together where the detailed fitting results can be found in Tab
3.3. The smaller the beam area, the higher the laser fluence and the higher the chance to reach
saturation of photodetachment. Although the anomaly exists, the fitted effective photodetachment
cross sections across these beam sizes are narrowly dispersed. A smaller beam also limits the
volume of plasma having photodetachment and thus a smaller normalization factor is found. The
effective cross sections from four different beam sizes are all close to the O2−’s. It is convincing
that the experiment shows a glow discharge at atmospheric pressure is dominated by O2− negative
ions whereas the exact mechanism requires a kinetic model to be understood. Note that the data is
prone to deviate from the theoretical curve at smaller laser energy when a smaller laser beam is
used. Although the precise reason for the deviation is unclear, a focused beam shall be avoided in
the photodetachment experiment in future work.

3.2.3 Impact at Self-organized Pattern

Identifying the dominant negative ions O2− again shows the importance of oxygen gas in atmo-
spheric glow discharge. However, SOP exhibits no impact when a photodetachment is performed
in the plasma as shown in Fig 3.6. The original images are 16-bit grayscale which is transformed
into a color one for better contrast. Due to the intense reflection of laser rays toward the camera, the
earliest image is taken at 23 ns after the laser pulse whereas no prominent distinction is observed.
Given that the rise time of the photodetachment current is 20 ns in Fig 3.2, it is believed that at
least 62% of negative ions have been detached at this moment. The right side shows the pattern
image at 1 µs after the laser pulse and no observable change exists. In fact, different orders of delay
time scale from nanoseconds to a few hundred microseconds were examined, yet no impact from
photodetachment was found. From these results, the plasma self-organization is possibly not cor-
related with the negative ions mechanism, or not all of them are affected in enough time. A pulsed
DC glow discharge experiment was performed and results show that the pattern is established in
∼100 µs which is a rather long time scale compared with the kinetic processes [19]. Negative ions
are also known to exist around the periphery of the plasma column where the ambient oxygen
attaches with runaway electrons, thus it is plausible that the photodetachment experiment is not
fully convincing due to the limitations of laser energy and pulse frequency.

 3.3 Summary
 A laser photodetachment of negative ions is performed in this chapter to investigate its role in the
 mechanics of SOP. A prominent current increase can be detected when photodetachment is
 engaged and shall increase with laser energy until becomes saturated. However, the laser energy
 is not enough to reach saturation and it is found that the dominant species is O− according to the
 2
 fitted effective photodetachment cross section. The factor between the current ratio and the de-
 tachment ratio can be used to estimate the negative ion density which is about ∼ 1012cm−3. A
 focused laser beam increases the laser fluence and leads to a more intense photodetachment while
 an abnormal current spike also happened probably due to the ablation on the exposed electrode.
 An examination of the SOP dynamics from the ICCD camera reveals no change when the photode-
 tachment happened. This means the self-organization at atmospheric glow discharge with liquid is
 not dependent on the 62% negative ions that are detached by the laser. Nevertheless, the current ex-
 periment cannot fully ascertain the mechanism of negative ions in SOP unless the photodetachment
 is performed thoroughly at a larger energy and length scale.
