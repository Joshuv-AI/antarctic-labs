Dorsal raphe tau pathology disrupts sleep‐wake orchestration and sleep architecture in a sex‐specific manner
Authors: Louis J. Kolling, Ruixiang Wang, Gabriel S. Gaudencio, Jamie S. Fishbeyn, Thomas D. James, Suzanne M. Mason, Riley O. McCoy, Zhimin Song, Ian Pascal McConnell, Kylie Ziemke, Kasia M. Janik, Yu Xu, Gabrielle Bierlein‐De La Rosa, Shafa Ismail, Catherine Anne Marcinkiewcz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONSleep disturbances are frequent symptoms of Alzheimer's disease (AD), mirrored by early pathology in the dorsal raphe nucleus (DRN). Whether early DRN pathology can drive sleep disturbances, and the relative contributions of serotonin (5‐HT) and sex, has not been directly tested.METHODSWe isolate tau pathology to whole DRN (DRNTau), serotonin neurons of the DRN (5‐HTTau), or non‐serotonergic neurons of the DRN (non5HTTau). We use longitudinal EEG recordings, in male and female mice, to determine the contributions of DRN neuronal subtypes to sleep disruption within the context of prodromal AD.RESULTSDRNTau, 5‐HTTau, and non5HTTau disrupt sleep architecture. 5‐HTTau and non5HTTau further disrupt sleep‐wake orchestration and exhibit sex‐dependent alterations that coincide with changes in delta wave power.DISCUSSIONTau‐mediated sleep dysfunction may be driven by various cellular subtypes of the DRN, are primarily sexually divergent, and may resultant in changes in 5‐HT activity and relative spectral brain wave contributions.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder resulting in cognitive, affective, and sleep deficits. Daytime sleepiness, frequent nighttime awakenings, and overall poor sleep quality have long been reported in persons with AD. 1 The development of sleep deficits prior to cognitive impairment is mirrored by the accumulation of a hyper‐phosphorylated pathological form of the protein tau (pTau) in brainstem regions prior to the development of cortical and hippocampal pathology. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Generally, pTau burden corresponds to the severity of associated symptoms. However, the relationship between disease progression and sleep orchestration is much more complex; recent studies found a non‐linear U‐shaped relationship between cognitive impairment and sleep orchestration. 5 As a result, how this early brainstem pathology disrupts sleep and which cell types are most relevant have yet to be fully characterized.

To date, studies in rodent models of AD or other tauopathies have yielded inconsistent sleep phenotypes. Some studies show decreased non‐rapid eye movement (nREM) sleep or changes in electroencephalogram (EEG) spectra, while other groups report no changes in sleep parameters. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 The models used in these studies have shown utility in investigating the mechanisms underlying cognitive deficits; however, the levels and effects of pTau in brain regions relevant to sleep might not share the same time course in each model; indeed, our previous work showed that changes in depressive and anxiety‐like behaviors were not apparent at each timepoint. 4 , 11 Because the progression of tau pathology may affect various neuronal types differently, sleep disruptions might not be apparent in each model at the times tested.

The dorsal raphe nucleus (DRN) is the largest of the brain's serotonin (5‐HT)‐producing nuclei and a common site of early pTau accumulation. 12 , 13 Critically, the DRN plays a major role in both sleep‐wake orchestration and the transition between sleep stages, metrics known to be affected during the prodromal phase of AD (also known as the preclinical phase). 14 , 15 , 16 We elect to use the term prodromal phase, as patients suffering from these symptoms do report clinically, though they are not diagnostically associated with AD as there remains a paucity of differential diagnostic criteria at this stage.

The function of 5‐HT neurons in the DRN is regulated by sex hormones in a sex‐dependent manner whose dimorphism is present in both rodents and humans. 17 , 18 , 19 Therefore, the goal of this study was to elucidate the role of the DRN in sleep disruption, within the context of the prodromal phase of AD. To accomplish this, we used viral‐vector‐mediated over‐expression of Tau[P301L] in the DRN of adult male and female mice, with the goal of mirroring a more‐native initiation of pTau in the brainstem as is commonly observed in human AD. The point mutation P301L is associated with severe forms of frontotemporal dementia and results in the accumulation of pTau in human subjects and transgenic mice. 20 , 21 We combine multiple parallel approaches in both wild‐type and transgenic mice to isolate the impact of pTau pathogenesis on the DRN as a whole (DRNTau), the 5‐HT neurons of the DRN (5‐HTTau) or the non5HT neurons of the DRN (non5HTTau). Of these models, the 5‐HTTau model most closely recapitulates human pathology and is subject to the greatest innate sexual dimorphism. 13 , 17 , 18 , 22 , 23 To reduce the impact of normal aging on sleep and capture the progression of tau pathology, our study includes longitudinal EEG recordings at 4, 8, 12, and 16 weeks post‐injection (w.p.i.) of adeno‐associated virus (AAV)‐Tau[P301L]. Our previous work showed that DRNTau increased 5‐HT excitability and modulated anxiety‐ and depressive‐like behaviors on this time scale. 13 Here, find differential effects of Tau[P301L] on sleep regulation across models and between sexes that mirror the sleep disruptions characteristic of AD in a composite manner and that correspond to antagonistic shifts in spectral power density during the respective disrupted stages. EEG spectral analysis may represent a critical diagnostic tool in the differentiation of AD‐related sleep disorders during the tau‐based prodromal phase of AD.

METHODS

Ethics approval

All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the University of Iowa (UI) Office of Animal Resources (Protocol 4032080) and/or the University of Florida (UF) Animal Care Services (Protocol IACUC202500000146) that abided by the American Veterinary Medical Association and National Institutes of Health guidelines.

Animals

Male C57 mice (C57BL/6J mice; Catalog No.: 000664; Jackson Labs, Bar Harbor, ME, USA; RRID:IMSR_JAX:000664) and Sert‐cre homozygous mice (Catalog No.: 014554; Jackson Labs; RRID:IMSR_JAX:0014554) were purchased for use. Sert‐cre homozygous mice were out‐crossed to C57 mice to generate Sert‐cre heterozygous mice for experimental use. C57 (male n = 24) mice were aged to 8 weeks before AAV injection and EEG/electromyogram (EMG) implantation for longitudinal DRNTau EEG recordings. Sert‐cre mice were aged to 8 weeks before AAV injection, then underwent either EEG/EMG implantation for longitudinal 5‐HTTau EEG recordings (male n = 33, female n = 25), electrophysiology (male n = 25, female n = 24), behavioral assessment (male n = 29, female n = 25), or EEG/EMG implantation for longitudinal non5HTTau EEG recordings (male n = 16, female n = 18). A total of 24 C57 and 195 Sert‐cre mice were used for experiments (219 mice in total).

All mice were group‐housed (two to five mice per cage, except for temporary single housing during EEG/EMG recordings) in a temperature‐ and humidity‐controlled Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care‐approved vivarium on a standard 12 h/12 h dark/light cycle in accordance with institutional requirements. Mice were housed in conventional‐style rodent cages with shredded paper bedding, containing separate food and water that could be obtained ad libitum. Mice were maintained on chow that was composed of 14% kcal fat, 60% kcal carbohydrate, and 26% kcal protein (Catalog No.: 5P76; Land O'Lakes, Arden Hills, MN, USA).

Viral Infusion

All mice underwent surgery at 8 weeks old, and surgeries were performed using an Angle Two stereotactic frame (Leica Biosystems), as described previously. 13 Briefly, mice were anesthetized using an inhalation anesthetic (isoflurane) for induction and maintenance of the anesthetic plane. Mice were administered peri‐operative analgesics (Ethiqa XR and Meloxicam) and continuously monitored for toe‐pinch reflex and pallor. All stereotactic infusions targeted the DRN using the following coordinates (relative to the bregma, in millimeters): Anteroposterior: −4.6, Mediolateral: 0, Dorsoventral: −3.3, with a tilting angle of 23.58°. Virus titers were approximately 8.9 × 1012 VG/mL, and each mouse received a single 500‐nL infusion at a rate of 100 nL/min. DRNTau surgeries: 12 “control” mice received AAV8‐CAG‐eGFP (University of Iowa Viral Vector Core), and 12 mice received AAV8‐CBA‐Tau[P301L]. 5‐HTTau surgeries: 100 Sert‐cre “control” mice received AAV8‐DIO‐hSyn‐mCherry (Catalog No.: 50459‐AAV8; Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA), and 95 mice received AAV8‐FLEX‐ef1a‐Tau[P301L] (University of Iowa Viral Vector Core). Non5HTTau surgeries: 17 “control” mice received AAV8‐DIO‐hSyn‐mCherry (Catalog No.: 50459‐AAV8; Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA), and 17 mice received AAV8‐FLEX(off)‐ef1a‐Tau[P301L] (VectorBuilder, Chicago, IL, USA). All surgeries for a given experiment were completed within a 5‐day period.

EEG Recordings

Electroencephalography and electromyography

Pinnacle three‐channel EEG/EMG headsets (two EEG channels, one EMG channel; Catalog No.: 8201‐SS; Pinnacle, Nashville, TN, USA) were affixed to the skull in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, the front of the head mount was aligned 3 mm anterior to the bregma. Screw holes were drilled through the headset using a 23‐gauge needle, electrode screws were advanced using a hand‐held screwdriver, and epoxy was used to secure the screws in place and facilitate electrical insulation. One‐tenth inch screws (Catalog No.: 8209) were used for the two anterior screws, and 0.12 in. screws (Catalog No.: 8212) were used for the two posterior screws to compensate for the curvature of the skull away from the flat headset. This procedure allows all four lead screws to rest in the cerebral cortex region. The terminal ends of the EEG electrode screws correspond roughly to the following coordinates (with respect to the bregma): anterior screws: AP +2.0, ML ± 1.25, DV −2.0; posterior screws: AP −3.5, ML ± 1.25, DV −2.3. In this configuration, the anterior left and posterior left EEG electrode screws function as “signal” electrodes (corresponding to EEG channels 2 and 1, respectively), with the posterior right screw functioning as a shared “reference” electrode. The fourth anterior right EEG electrode screw functions as a “ground” electrode. EMG leads were embedded in the nuchal muscles of the posterior neck. The head mount was then embedded in acrylic, and the incision was sutured to cover the edges of the headset while leaving the recording interface exposed.

EEG and EMG were recorded at 4, 8, 12, and 16 weeks following surgery using the Pinnacle wireless digital telemetry system in conjunction with Sirenia Acquisition software version 2.2.7. Continuous EEG/EMG recordings were conducted in mouse home cages. The wireless system enabled simultaneous data acquisition from up to 10 mice across distinct Bluetooth channels. Each mouse head mount was fitted with a wireless potentiostat (Catalog No.: 8274‐HR‐SL) powered by a 1.45‐V zinc‐air battery. Recordings were initiated using a supplementary “jump‐start” battery (Catalog No.: 8276) to preserve the primary cell's capacity. Mice maintained unrestricted access to food and water during recordings. EEG and EMG were acquired at 1000 Hz and filtered with a 300‐Hz low‐pass filter. While each continuous recording lasted 72 h, a 24‐h stable window (the middle 24‐h period) was selected for analysis. Records were scored offline in 10‐s epochs by a trained researcher blinded to experimental conditions.

Power spectra and sleep stage scoring

Spectral power was computed using Serenia Sleep Pro. For EEG power, spectra were designated as delta 1 to 4 Hz, theta 4 to 8 Hz, alpha 8 to 12 Hz, beta 12 to 30 Hz, and gamma 30 to 100 Hz. Prior to analyses, spectral bands were deconvoluted using Sirenia Sleep software and normalized to total power. The final power values of spectral percent composition were then computed using the average power of both EEG electrodes. Assignment of sleep stages was performed in the following manner: nREM sleep was identified as being characterized by high amplitude and low frequency in the EEG channels, with relatively low amplitude in the EMG channel. High delta power was additionally used to categorize a 10‐s epoch as nREM. In contrast, REM sleep was identified as being characterized by a low‐amplitude and highly repeatable signal in the EEG channels (i.e., a “sawtooth” pattern), with low amplitude in the EMG channel. High theta power was additionally used to categorize a 10‐s epoch as REM sleep. Wake states were identified as being characterized by low‐amplitude, high‐frequency, highly irregular EEG channels, with relatively high and irregular EMG amplitude as compared to sleep states. High alpha power was additionally used to categorize a 10‐s epoch as awake.

5‐HTTau electrophysiology

Brain slice preparation

This procedure was described in detail previously. 4 , 13 Briefly, deeply anesthetized mice were transcardially perfused with ice‐cold oxygenated modified artificial cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Brains were quickly dissected, and coronal slices containing the DRN (300 µm) were obtained using a vibratome (VT1200S; Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany). All slices were isolated approximately 1 to 3 h after the beginning of the light phase, corresponding to the sleeping phase of the mice, and all recordings were performed within the same light‐phase period.

Ex vivo electrophysiological recordings

Neurons were visualized using an upright microscope (BX51WI; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) accompanied by a differential interference contrast imaging system. Membrane currents were amplified with a Multiclamp 700 B amplifier (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA), filtered at 3 kHz, and sampled at 20 kHz with a Digidata 1550B digitizer (Molecular Devices). Data were acquired via pClamp 11 software (Molecular Devices). Access resistance was monitored, and changes greater than 20% would lead to discontinuation of the recordings. Whole‐cell recordings were performed using an intracellular solution containing (in mM): 135 K‐gluconate, 5 NaCl, 2 MgCl2, 10 HEPES, 0.6 EGTA, 4 Na2ATP, and 0.4 Na2GTP (pH 7.3, ∼290 mOsmol/kg). After establishing a whole‐cell configuration, cells were first sampled for proper access resistance (less than 40 MΩ) prior to initiating a series of current‐clamp recordings. Cells were held at −70 mV between pulses to compare data across cells. Rheobase was measured using a linearly increasing continuous current injection, and the first‐evoked action potential was used for the measure of action‐potential kinetics after flattening the baseline with a slope‐correction operation. Action‐potential characteristics (frequency, instantaneous frequency, inter‐spike interval) and current‐dependence data were determined by incrementally injecting a family of current pulses lasting 250 ms, injected every second, and stepped in 10 pA increments from −100 pA to 200 pA. Input resistance was calculated using the voltage deflection elicited by a current injection of −10 pA.

Dual‐electrode recordings of evoked post‐synaptic currents

Cesium methanesulfonate‐based intracellular solution was used (in mM) to facilitate voltage‐clamp recordings of electrically evoked excitatory or inhibitory post‐synaptic currents (EPSCs or IPSCs) in 5‐HTTau neurons: 135 cesium methanesulfonate, 10 KCl, 1 MgCl2, 10 HEPES, 0.2 EGTA, 4 MgATP, 0.3 Na2GTPa, 20 Na2phosphocreatine (pH 7.3, ∼290 mOsmol/kg) with 1 mg/mL QX‐314. After acquiring whole‐cell access to the recorded cell, a second (stimulating) electrode was placed 50 µm lateral to the recorded neuron. EPSCs were evoked by square pulses (duration 100 µs; 0.1 Hz) in increasing intensities (10, 20, 40, 80, 160, and 320 µA), while the recorded neuron was clamped at −55 mV. Similarly, IPSCs were evoked while the neuron was clamped at +10 mV. To assess the probability of presynaptic neuroplasticity and neurotransmitter release (paired‐pulse facilitation or inhibition), two stimuli with an inter‐stimulus interval of 30 ms were delivered at 0.1 Hz, so EPSCs and IPSCs were evoked in pairs, respectively. The ratio of the peak transient currents of the two paired EPSCs or IPSCs was used to calculate the paired‐pulse ratio (PPR = EPSC2/EPSC1 or IPSC2/IPSC1), computed based on the average of 10 replicate trials at each stimulating intensity.

5‐HTTau behavioral assessment

Following viral infusion at 8 weeks old, male and female 5‐HTTau mice were assessed broadly for changes to depressive‐like, anxiety‐like, or cognitive behaviors at 8 and 16 w.p.i. Behavioral tests were performed in the order presented below, and mice received at least two “rest days” between behavioral assessments.

Elevated plus maze

Anxiety‐like behavior was assessed using the elevated plus maze (EPM) following our previously described protocol. 24 Each mouse was placed in the center of a plus‐shaped maze elevated above the floor, consisting of two white open arms and two dark closed arms, and allowed to explore for 5 min. Sessions were recorded using a Basler GenICam camera and Media Recorder software (Noldus, VA, USA). The percentage of time spent in open and closed arms and the central zone, number of arm entries, total distance moved, and mean velocity were analyzed using EthoVision XT15 software (Noldus).

Novelty‐induced suppression of feeding

Forty‐eight hours before testing, mice were each given a single piece of Froot Loops cereal (Kellogg's) in their home cage, and 24 h before testing, all chow was removed from the home cage, and mice remained fasted until testing was complete. On test day, mice were transferred to a testing room and allowed to acclimate for 1 h. Mice were then individually placed in an open arena (50 × 50 × 25 cm; 20 to 25 lux) that contained a single Froot Loop in the center of the arena. Mice were allowed to explore the arena for 10 min while the tester was absent from the room, and latency to feed on the Froot Loop was measured post hoc using recorded video. Mice were then returned to their home cage and allowed to feed freely on the Froot Loop. Any mouse that did not feed on the Froot Loop in its home cage after the test was excluded from data collection.

Social interaction test

Social behavior was assessed using the three‐chamber social interaction test as described previously by us. 4 , 25 , 26 The apparatus consisted of a Plexiglass box divided into three chambers with small openings between them. During habituation, the test mouse was allowed to explore all chambers freely for 10 min. Following habituation, the mouse was confined to the center chamber while a conspecific stranger mouse of the same sex and age was placed inside a metal holding cage in one of the side chambers, and an identical empty cage was placed in the opposite chamber. The doors were then opened, allowing the test mouse to freely explore all chambers for 10 min. Behavior was recorded using a camera and Media Recorder software (Noldus). The total time spent interacting with the stranger mouse or empty cage was scored by an observer blinded to the experimental groups.

Sucrose preference test

The sucrose preference test was performed using PhenoTyper observation cages (Noldus) in tandem with sipper bottles fitted with lickometers (to measure the number of licks made by an animal at a particular bottle), as we previously performed. 4 Animals were provided ad libitum access to tap water or a 5% sucrose solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h. Mice underwent training for three consecutive days, wherein the placement of the sucrose and water bottles was alternated each day to control for any side preference. On the fourth assessment day (testing day), the number of interactions to each sipper bottle was measured via Ethovision XT14 software, and the number of sucrose “licks” was represented as a percentage of the total number of licks for both bottles to calculate the percent sucrose preference.

Open field test

Mice were placed in the corner of an open arena (50 × 50 × 25 cm; 20 to 25 lux) and allowed to freely explore the arena for 30 min. The assay was recorded using an overhead camera, and the total distance traveled (cm), time spent in the center 15% of the arena, and time spent in the corners of the arena were quantified using Ethovision XT14 software (Noldus, Leesburg, VA, USA).

Novel‐object recognition

Short‐term memory was assessed using the novel object recognition (NOR) test, as described previously. 27 The test consisted of three phases: habituation, acquisition, and testing phase. During habituation (day 1), mice were allowed to freely explore the empty arena for 10 min. After 24 h, in the acquisition (training) phase, each mouse was placed in the same arena containing two identical objects positioned equidistantly and allowed to explore for 10 min. Two hours later, during the test phase, one of the familiar objects was replaced with a novel object, and the mouse was allowed to explore both objects for 5 min. Exploration was defined as the mouse directing its nose toward, sniffing, or touching the object. Exploration time was recorded by an observer blinded to the experimental groups. The discrimination index (DI) was calculated as (N − F)/(N + F), where N represents the time spent exploring the novel object and F represents the time spent exploring the familiar object. A positive DI indicates preference for the novel object, whereas a negative DI indicates preference for the familiar object. Percent novel preference was calculated as (N/(N + F)) × 100. Reduced discrimination and diminished novel object preference during the test session indicate impairments in recognition memory.

Forced alteration Y‐maze

Spatial working and reference memory was assessed using the forced‐alteration Y‐maze test, as described previously. 28 Mice were acclimated to the room and tester at least 7 days prior to the experiment, were again acclimated to the room for 1 h the day prior to the experiment, and were acclimated to the room for 1 h before data collection. Each of the three arms of the Y‐maze apparatus were labeled A, B, and C, respectively. Symbols were placed at the ends of each of the three arms (star, polka dots, or vertical stripes), and one arm was blocked to prevent exploration of the arm by the subject mouse. The mouse was placed in the distal end of one of the two open arms facing the center of the maze, allowed to freely explore the open arms of the maze for 15 min, then returned to its home cage for 1 h. The blocked arm was then unblocked, and the mouse was allowed to explore the entire maze for 5 min. Percent preference for the novel (previously closed) arm was calculated as (Timenovel / Timetotal) × 100, where Timenovel represents the total duration of the mouse's center point within the novel arm, and Timetotal represents the total duration of the test. Timenovel and total locomotion were calculated using Ethovision XT 14 software.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Following the conclusion of EEG recordings, electrophysiology, or behavioral assessments, all mice were assessed for pTau in the DRN. Mice were perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde, and brains were harvested before being sectioned on a sliding‐type freezing microtome serially at 35 µm and stored in anti‐freeze medium until IF. Coronal sections containing the DRN were blocked for 60 min, and incubated in primary antibodies targeting Tph2 (goat, Catalog No.: EB11012; Everest Biotech, Oxfordshire, UK; 1:375) and phosphorylated human tau (rabbit, AH36; Catalog No.: SMC‐601; StressMarq Biosciences, Victoria, BC, Canada; 1:500) at 4°C overnight. Species‐specific secondary antibodies were incubated for 2 h at room temperature. Blocking buffer consisted of 10% normal donkey serum, 0.5% Triton X‐100, and 0.2% Tween‐20 in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS). Incubation buffer consisted of 2% NDS and 0.1% Tween‐20 in PBS. Confocal z‐stacks (1 µm) were captured using an Olympus FV3000 laser scanning confocal microscope (six optical sections/z‐stack) and converted to maximum‐intensity projection images using FIJI (ImageJ) software.

Experimental design and statistical analyses

Data analyses and statistical tests were performed using Prism version 10 (GraphPad, Prism Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA; RRID:SCR_002798). Two‐way comparisons use a two‐way mixed model with repeated measures (2w ANOVA), and three‐way comparisons use a three‐way mixed model with repeated measures (3w ANOVA). Pairwise comparisons and one‐way ANOVA for all other experiments were performed as follows: Outliers were first determined using a ROUT test (Q = 1%). Data were then checked for normal distribution using a Shapiro‐Wilk test and homogeneity of variance using the Fmax test. Where data violated homogeneity of variance, a non‐parametric analysis was used. Bar charts annotate means, with plungers representing standard deviation. Two‐way plots represent means, with plungers representing standard error. The full statistical details can be found in Table S1, a statistical summary table.

RESULTS

DRNTau induces deficits in REM sleep and increases REM delta wave power

AAV‐mediated over‐expression of Tau[P301L] resulted in significant accumulation of pTau in male C57 mouse brains, which also recapitulate cognitive and behavioral deficits associated with AD. 13 , 20 The DRN contains the cell bodies of most of the brain's 5‐HT neurons, which project throughout the brain to impact affect, cognition, sleep, and other behaviors. 29 As these previous studies were performed in males only, and given the known effects of sex on 5‐HT production and turnover, we first sought to determine any effect of pTau in the DRN on the orchestration of sleep. 17 , 18 , 19 We dually performed EEG electrode implantation and injected either AAV8‐eGFP or AAV8‐Tau[P301L] into the DRN of adult male C57 mice at 8 weeks of age, then assessed EEG‐defined sleep at 4, 8, 12, and 16 w.p.i. (Figure 1A,B). This strategy over‐expresses Tau[P301L] broadly across the DRN, generating what we refer to herein as DRNTau mice. We assessed sleep stages broadly as REM and nREM, separated by dark/light phase, to determine any sleep‐wake cycle reversal that may mirror the circadian disruptions characteristic of AD. 30 We primarily found that DRNTau mice experienced a reduction in REM sleep during the light phase (sleeping phase of the mouse) (2w ANOVA; virus **p = 0.0098; Figure 1D, left), with no other changes in sleep state within either dark/light phase.

Separate from circadian disruptions, we further assessed sleep architecture within the context of each sleep/wake state, as 5‐HT neurons are known to strongly participate in transitions to and from REM and nREM stages. 14 , 31 , 32 DRNTau mice undergo fewer REM bouts (2w ANOVA; virus *p = 0.0444; Figure 2B, left) and have a longer average nREM bout duration throughout a 24‐h period (2w ANOVA; virus *p = 0.0146; Figure 2E, left). Interestingly, DRNTau mice have increased percent delta composition during REM sleep (2w ANOVA; virus **p = 0.0069; Figure 2H, left) and during wake periods (2w ANOVA; virus ***p = 0.0005; Figure 2J, left) as compared to control mice, which is typically representative of nREM slow‐wave sleep.

5‐HTTau disrupts sleep‐wake orchestration and reduces nREM delta power in a sex‐dependent manner

As we observed disruptions to sleep architecture in DRNTau mice, we next sought to dissect the contributions of 5‐HT neurons and non‐5‐HT neurons in the context of pTau challenge, and also to determine any differential effects of sex on sleep outcomes. We dually performed EEG electrode implantation and injected either cre‐dependent AAV8‐DIO‐mCherry or cre‐dependent AAV8‐FLEX‐Tau[P301L] into the DRN of adult male and female Sert‐cre mice. This strategy over‐expresses Tau[P301L] in the 5‐HT neurons of the DRN, generating what we refer to herein as 5‐HTTau mice. As before, we assessed EEG‐defined sleep at 4, 8, 12, and 16 w.p.i. 5‐HTTau have distinct and complementary electrophysiological and behavioral phenotypes compared to DRNTau mice

We previously observed enhanced excitability and electrical activity of 5‐HT neurons in DRNTau mice, which was accompanied by an increase in evoked EPSC amplitude. 13 We asked whether those effects were primarily driven by pTau in 5‐HT neurons, so we then used slice electrophysiology to assess the properties of 5‐HT neurons in male and female 5‐HTTau mice. All brain slices were isolated and recorded during the light phase, corresponding to the sleeping period of the mice. We first performed dual‐electrode, voltage‐clamp, paired‐pulse facilitation/inhibition recordings to dually assess excitatory neurotransmission and 5‐HT synaptic plasticity (Figure 5A,B). In male 5‐HTTau mice, we observed an increase in evoked EPSC amplitude (2w ANOVA; virus *p = 0.0259; Figure 5C), which coincided with a decrease in EPSC PPR, indicating augmented presynaptic glutamate release (2w ANOVA; virus *p = 0.0396; Figure 5D). We further observed an increase in the magnitude of evoked‐EPSC current density following the first pulse (2w ANOVA; virus *p = 0.0494; Figure 5E). Interestingly, we found no difference in the current density of the second pulse (Figure 5F). We observed no difference in inhibitory currents or inhibitory paired‐pulse ratio for male 5‐HTTau mice (Figure 5G–J). We next assessed 5‐HT neurons of female 5‐HTTau mice in the same fashion and again observed an increase in the amplitude of evoked EPSCs (2w ANOVA; stimulus × virus interaction *p = 0.0381; Figure 5K). We observed no further differences in female 5‐HTTau mice for evoked EPSCs or IPSCs (Figure 5L–R). In separate mice, we lastly performed current‐clamp recordings to assess innate excitability and action‐potential characteristics. 5‐HT neurons of male 5‐HTTau mice demonstrate a large increase in inter‐spike interval (2w ANOVA; virus ****p < 0.0001; Figure 5U), with no other changes in innate excitability or electrical activity for male (Figure 5S,T) or female mice (Figure 5V–X). Additionally, we found no differences in the innate‐excitability parameters of rheobase or input resistance (data not shown).

Previous reports suggested that the introduction of pTau to hippocampal pyramidal neurons altered action‐potential amplitude, width, and rise and decay kinetics. 33 , 34 As such, we wanted to identify any changes in the action‐potential kinetics of 5‐HTTau neurons that might accompany our observed changes in paired‐pulse facilitation and inter‐spike interval. We assessed these measures in the first‐evoked action potential of each recorded cell. We found an interactive effect among sex and virus for peak amplitude (1w ANOVA; *p = 0.0118), rise time to half amplitude (1w ANOVA; *p = 0.0436), and decay tau (1w ANOVA; *p = 0.0257), and also a main effect of virus on the time to max decay slope (1w ANOVA; ***p = 0.0007) and decay tau (1w ANOVA; *p = 0.0350; Table S2). Overall, these subtle changes trended toward smaller peak amplitude, faster rise kinetics, and faster decay kinetics in male 5‐HTTau mice and larger peak amplitude, slower rise kinetics, and off‐set decay kinetics in female 5‐HTTau mice.

We previously observed depressive‐like behaviors in DRNTau mice, particularly deficits in social interaction and sucrose preference. 13 As our current electrophysiology findings in 5‐HTTau mice did not directly mirror our previous electrophysiology findings in DRNTau mice, we now asked whether 5‐HTTau mice would develop any anxiety‐ or depressive‐like phenotype. While we observed differences between sex for most of the behavioral tasks that we assessed, we primarily found a strong increase in latency to feed during the novelty‐induced suppression of feeding task for male 5‐HTTau mice at 8 and 16 w.p.i. (2w ANOVA; Tukey's post hoc **p = 0.0069, Figure 2, 6 ANOVA; Tukey's post hoc ****p < 0.0001, Figure 6I). Interestingly, female 5‐HTTau mice exhibited a reduction in locomotor distance during the Y‐maze task at 16 w.p.i. (2w ANOVA; Tukey's post hoc *p = 0.0129; Figure 6U), signifying a reduction in overall exploratory drive, with no other effects of 5‐HTTau on behavioral outcomes.

We previously found that an adolescent‐isolation challenge was sufficient to induce changes to neural 5‐HT function, which concomitantly affected various metabolic parameters in wild‐type mice. 26 We therefore asked whether 5‐HTTau could alter whole‐animal metabolic parameters and utilized Promethion metabolic phenotyping chambers to assess male and female 5‐HTTau mice at 16 w.p.i. DISCUSSION

Using viral‐mediated strategies and longitudinal EEG recordings, we characterized for the first time the effect of DRN tau pathology on sleep. We further delineated the contributions of serotonergic and non‐serotonergic populations to these effects and identified several differential effects due to sex. The DRN is commonly affected early in the prodromal phase of AD, which coincides with disruptions to sleep. We provide the first evidence that tau pathology in the DRN may directly contribute to this classical symptom and that 5‐HTTau mice recapitulate the disruptions to sleep characteristic of human AD, enabling the continued interrogation of AD etiology in the 5‐HTTau model. We found that changes in delta wave power present in a sex‐ and cell type‐specific manner, suggesting that longitudinal EEG assessment may represent a critical clinical measure in the differentiation of AD‐based sleep disruptions. Given the known human sexual dimorphism in both 5‐HT function and AD prevalence, our findings suggest that sexually divergent sleep‐wake orchestration and sleep architecture may also underscore this complex relationship.

A common feature of prodromal AD is the presence of pTau in the DRN, suggesting some relative susceptibility of this region to the sporadic initiation of tau pathology. 12 , 13 , 35 The DRN is the largest of the brain's serotonergic nuclei – in addition to housing a variety of 5‐HT neuron subtypes that are dually glutamatergic, peptidergic or GABAergic, it also contains non‐serotonergic GABAergic, glutamatergic, dopaminergic, and peptidergic neurons. 36 , 37 , 38 5‐HT neurons of the DRN are known to play a major role in both sleep‐wake orchestration and sleep architecture, promoting wakefulness and coordinating transitions between REM‐nREM stages via interactions with cholinergic neurons in the pons. 14 , 15 , 16 Given the complex interplay between estrogens and 5‐HT neurons of the DRN, it is unsurprising that we observed near‐ubiquitous sexual dimorphism in sleep‐wake orchestration and sleep architecture. This sexual dimorphism extends beyond rodent models and is mirrored in human populations. In addition to effects on 5‐HT synthesis and turnover, estrogen depletion following menopause accelerates the progression of AD in women, likely due to impaired 5‐HT homeostasis. 39 , 40 Women suffer insomnia at significantly higher rates than men, which largely coincides with the menopausal transition. 41 , 42 , 43 Though some of this disparity may be explained by hormonally driven vasomotor symptoms (e.g., “hot flashes”), this disruption to sleep may further accelerate underlying brain pathologies via reductions in the durations of restorative sleep stages.

We observed an increase in excitatory synaptic drive after Tau[P301L] expression exclusively in 5‐HTTau neurons of both sexes, consistent with our previous findings in male DRNTau mice and the findings of other groups using a similar model. 13 , 44 This indicates that pTau dysregulates synaptic homeostasis and increases 5‐HTTau signaling, which may explain the outcome of our behavioral experiments. Although we performed a battery of behavioral tests for anxiety‐ and depressive‐like behaviors, the primary behavioral phenotype that we observed was during the novelty‐induced suppression of feeding (NSF) test in male 5‐HTTau mice. In an analogous behavioral test, Bechtholt and colleagues found that depletion of 5‐HT was sufficient to override the anorectic stressor of a novel environment on feeding. 45 They further found that repletion of 5‐HT restored the ability of a novel environment to suppress feeding activity. Given these findings, we speculate that our male‐specific increase in EPSC current density (Figure 5E) may underlie an increase in glutamate‐mediated 5‐HT release, which then magnifies 5‐HT's anxiogenic effects in the context of the NSF test. Mechanistically, this increase in glutamatergic transmission may stem from an impairment of the tonic retrograde endocannabinoid (eCB) “brake” system – pTau may disrupt the scaffolding or synthesis of eCBs (e.g., via mis‐localization of diacylglycerol lipase) within the 5‐HTDRN neurons, thereby weakening eCB inhibition and increasing the probability of presynaptic glutamate release onto 5‐HTDRN neurons. 46 Alternatively, pTau may alter post‐synaptic glutamate receptor function, such as aberrant recruitment of calcium‐permeable AMPA receptors to augment excitatory synaptic strength. 47 , 48 Future studies are needed to delineate the role of the eCB system in the prodromal stage of AD. 49

By initiating Tau[P301L] over‐expression in adulthood, we have avoided many of the potential developmental and compensatory effects that confound numerous preclinical models. To date, sleep data from these genetic mouse models, such as Tg2576, 5xFAD, and rTg4510, have been inconsistent, with some reporting no changes in sleep‐wake orchestration, sleep architecture, or spectral power density. 10 , 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 Though these models benefit from their broad utility, they do not model the initiation of pTau in the brainstem as is commonly observed in human AD. 12 Indeed, even among the literature on human populations with AD, the effects of healthy aging on sleep are not entirely parsed from sleep‐disruption outcomes. A 2021 study by Lucey et al. exemplified the relationship between sleep disruption and cognitive outcomes in the context of AD, incorporating measures for APOE genotype and CSF biomarkers while controlling for age, sex, and years of education. 5 They reported inverse U‐shaped distributions for the relationship between cognitive outcomes and the time spent in various sleep stages and sleep times overall, respectively. This finding, in tandem with our data presented herein, reinforces the need for longitudinal within‐patient sleep studies. A primary limitation of our study is that we limited our manipulation and assessments to the DRN, though this also represents a strength, as it allows for a clearer picture of the DRN's role within this context. It is important to acknowledge that sleep is initiated, orchestrated, and modulated by many brain regions that are relevant in early AD, particularly including the locus coeruleus. Future studies that characterize the longitudinal sleep‐disruptive effects of tau pathology in the locus coeruleus are both warranted and necessary to advance our understanding of AD etiology. In addition, DRN dopamine neurons are also involved in sleep‐wake modulation. 54 As such, it is worth investigating the unique contribution of pTau in DRN dopamine neurons to sleep disturbances in early AD.

The most restorative and deepest sleep occurs in nREM periods with high delta wave power, particularly “slow wave sleep.” Neuropsychiatric conditions that are associated with interrupted or fragmented sleep, as in the prodromal phase of AD, reduce nREM duration and delta wave power. 55 , 56 , 57 , 58 , 59 , 60 Delta wave power is implicated in synaptic homeostasis and neuronal plasticity, which suggests some complementarity between our electrophysiological findings of compromised presynaptic plasticity and reduced nREM delta power in 5‐HTTau mice. 61 , 62 , 63 , 64 The importance of delta power in the context of AD is further elevated by its role in the clearance of toxic proteins from the brain. The glymphatic system, which circulates CSF to “wash” the brain, uses delta waves to coordinate large fluidic pulses during nREM sleep, which have been shown to enhance the clearance AD‐associated proteins. 65 , 66 , 67 , 68 , 69 Our current and previous electrophysiological data support the longstanding notion that pTau dysregulates neuronal function, including 5‐HT neurons. 4 , 13 , 37 , 70 , 71 5‐HT neurons are a powerful modulator of thalamocortical relay neurons, which act as a “pacemaker” to stabilize and coordinate delta waves. 55 , 72 , 73 It is tempting to speculate that 5‐HTTau directly disrupts delta wave orchestration, which concomitantly reduces the clearance of amyloid beta and tau, reinforcing the progression of AD proteinopathy. To address this, we assessed the longitudinal effects of Tau[P301L] on 5‐HT biosynthesis and neuron survival in the DRN (Figure S1A–K). We observed no main effect of time on these metrics within the DRN. We next considered the nuanced opposing electrophysiology findings in male 5‐HTTau mice – 5‐HTTau neurons exhibit a strong increase in EPSC current density, suggesting an increase in glutamatergic responsivity, but also seemingly lose their paired‐pulse facilitation response (Figure 5D,E). To directly assess the net impact of these changes on 5‐HT signaling to the thalamus, we measured 5‐HT percent‐immunoreactive area in the mid‐thalamus (containing the paraventricular, ventral anterior‐lateral, and medio‐dorsal thalamic regions) and the caudal thalamus (containing the ventral anterior‐lateral and medio‐dorsal thalamic regions), which contain the majority of thalamocortical relay neurons (Figure S1L–O). We observed a decrease in 5‐HT labeling within both the mid and caudal thalamic areas in male 5‐HTTau mice at 16 w.p.i., with no difference in female mice for either region (Figure S1P–S). This suggests that 5‐HTTau indeed disrupts 5‐HT signaling to the thalamus, possibly affecting axonal 5‐HT biosynthesis, release, reuptake, or turnover, which may then mediate our observed differences in sleep architecture and orchestration. To increase the translatability of these findings, future studies should investigate the direct role of 5‐HT activity on thalamocortical relay neurons and the concomitant clearance of Aβ from the CSF, in the contexts of both healthy and diseased aging.

This study underscores the potential for longitudinal EEG recordings to advance our understanding of how sleep‐associated neural circuitry is affected by AD progression. By targeting pTau to the 5‐HT neurons of the DRN, we were able to model pathology that is characteristic of the prodromal phase of AD and concomitantly promote sex‐dependent disruptions to sleep‐wake orchestration and sleep architecture. These disruptions strongly correlate with alterations to delta wave power, which may be directly modulated by 5‐HT function, elevating the importance of the relationship between 5‐HT neurodegeneration and sleep disruption.
