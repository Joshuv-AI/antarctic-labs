 Evaluation of RMI Ejecta Sourcing from Reconstructed Interfaces

 in 2D FLAG Flyer-Plate Simulations

 Work performed December 2025-January 2026

 E. A. Hinrichs, UNM LANL XCP-6

 Abstract

This study investigates the performance of RMI ejecta sourcing from a reconstructed interface in
two-dimensional FLAG ejecta flyer-plate simulations. Enabling the RMI ejecta model to operate
on reconstructed interfaces has been a longstanding challenge in FLAG, limiting its applicability
  to experiments in which interface reconstruction is not required. Addressing this limitation is
therefore an important step toward expanding the model’s predictive capability. The first phase
 of this effort focused on identifying a flyer-plate/target configuration that produces large ejecta
 clouds from two distinct shocks using the standard RMI ejecta model. A series of simulations
 examined the effects of flyer composition, layer thickness, and mesh resolution on shock
 structure and ejecta formation. VISAR traces were used to evaluate shock timing and identify
configurations that produced a clear double-shock response. A copper middle-layer flyer with a
 doubled tin layer yielded the most favorable shock characteristics and was selected as the
baseline configuration. After confirming this configuration performed as expected when running
 a reference FLAG version, it became the basis for three simulations: (1) a standard two-
 dimensional ejecta flyer-plate calculation using a reference FLAG version, (2) the same
calculation with the reconstructed interface option enabled, and (3) a calculation employing ALE
strategies to force the ejecta model to operate on the reconstructed interface. While the baseline
 simulation produced the expected ejecta response, the reconstructed interface approach did not
  result in measurable ejecta, indicating that this capability remains under development. These
  results highlight both the progress made and the remaining challenges in extending the RMI
 ejecta model to reconstructed interfaces.

1. Introduction

Accurate modeling of ejecta production is essential for interpreting a wide range of shock-driven
experiments. In FLAG, ejecta generation is commonly modeled using the Richtmyer–Meshkov
instability (RMI) ejecta source model, which has been successfully applied to planar interfaces in
both two- and three-dimensional simulations. However, many experimentally relevant
configurations evolve away from planarity at late times, necessitating the use of arbitrary
Lagrangian–Eulerian (ALE) methods and the introduction of reconstructed material interfaces.
At present, the production version of FLAG does not robustly support sourcing ejecta from
reconstructed interfaces, limiting the applicability of the RMI ejecta model for these
experiments.

Enabling ejecta sourcing from reconstructed interfaces has therefore been a longstanding goal of
the FLAG ejecta development effort. Significant progress toward this capability has been made
by the FLAG ejecta team, particularly through the work of Brayden Wagoner and Alan Harrison,
who have focused on extending the RMI ejecta source model to operate on reconstructed
interfaces. While this functionality has been implemented in development versions of FLAG, it
has not yet undergone comprehensive testing under conditions representative of experimental
simulations. Such testing is a necessary prerequisite before the capability can be incorporated
into the production code used by the broader FLAG community.

The need to rigorously evaluate this reconstructed-interface ejecta capability motivated the
present short turn-around project. The primary objective was to assess the behavior and
performance of the RMI ejecta model when operating on reconstructed interfaces in two-
dimensional FLAG flyer-plate simulations. To accomplish this, a systematic approach was
employed involving flyer-plate configuration testing, refinement of input decks, and iterative
analysis of VISAR diagnostics. Particular attention was given to how flyer layer composition,
thickness, mesh resolution, and numerical strategy influence shock propagation and ejecta
production. Together, these efforts aim to provide an initial verification framework for
reconstructed-interface ejecta sourcing and to identify remaining challenges prior to broader
deployment.

2. Approach and Strategy

The overall strategy of this study was to rigorously evaluate FLAG’s emerging capability to
source RMI ejecta from reconstructed material interfaces under conditions representative of
experimental simulations. To do this, the work proceeded in two main stages.

First, a two-dimensional, two-shock flyer-plate configuration was identified that produces robust,
well-resolved ejecta when run with the standard RMI ejecta model in the production version of
FLAG (v3.26.1). This configuration was required to be physically realistic, based on designs
reported in the literature, computationally tractable on a modest number of nodes, and
sufficiently “beefy” to produce large ejecta clouds from two distinct shocks.

Second, this baseline configuration was used to perform three closely related simulations on a
private development branch of FLAG provided by Brayden Wagoner:

 • Simulation (a): a standard two-dimensional ejecta calculation with no reconstructed-
 interface capability enabled;
 • Simulation (b): the same calculation with reconstructed-interface ejecta sourcing
 enabled;
 • Simulation (c): the reconstructed-interface calculation augmented with ALE strategies to
 force the ejecta model to operate on reconstructed interfaces.

This progression allows for controlled comparisons. The (a)–(b) comparison tests whether
enabling reconstructed-interface logic alters results when it should not, while the (b)–(c) and (a)–
(c) comparisons probe whether ejecta production can be recovered when reconstructed interfaces
are unavoidable. The primary figure of merit for these comparisons is the total ejecta mass
produced, supplemented by VISAR diagnostics of the shock structure.

3. Mini-Study: Selection of a Flyer-Plate Configuration

3.1 Baseline Modeling Framework
The mini-study to identify a suitable baseline configuration was conducted on the Rocinante
supercomputer during December 2025 and January 2026 using the production version of FLAG
(v3.26.1). Several existing FLAG input decks served as starting points, including one-
dimensional and two-dimensional flyer-plate calculations developed within the Common
Modeling Framework (CMF). While a two-dimensional CMF flyer-plate deck was available, it
did not include ejecta and relied on external mesh files, motivating the development of a self-
contained two-dimensional configuration with ejecta enabled.

Using these decks as a foundation, a two-dimensional flyer-plate configuration with ejecta was
constructed and modified to produce a two-shock drive using a multilayer flyer.

3.2 Flyer-Plate Design and Variations
The flyer-plate design was initially based on the multilayer flyer configuration reported by
Maskaly et al. (J. Appl. Phys. 133, 025901, 2023). Although that study focused on shallow
bubble collapse phenomena, which are outside the scope of the present work, we adopted the
flyer-plate architecture described therein. In this design, the flyer consisted of tantalum, a low-
impedance intermediate layer, and tin, impacting a tin target.

FIG. 1. Flyer-plate/target configuration diagram and table for ejecta experiments published by Maskaly et
                                  al, J. Appl. Phys 133:025901 (2023)

To improve shock separation and ejecta production, the original PMMA middle layer was
replaced with alternative materials, and the layer thicknesses were systematically varied.
Several configurations were examined, including:
 • a tantalum–lithium fluoride–tin flyer,
 • reduced lithium fluoride thickness,
 • reduced flyer velocity,
 • replacement of the lithium fluoride middle layer with copper.
For each variation, the configuration file was run, and the resulting VISAR traces were
coplotted.

Analysis of the VISAR coplots indicates that the copper middle-layer configuration produced the
most favorable response. The VISAR for this case exhibited a distinct and sharp initial shock
with the largest initial velocity among the four configurations. In addition, the copper
configuration showed two clearly distinguishable shocks separated by approximate 0.75 µs, with
a nearly constant velocity between them. In contrast, the other configurations displayed negative
velocity slopes between shocks, suggesting that the tin may not have reached a sufficiently high
temperature to fully melt and therefore retained residual strength.

 FIG. 2. 25 µm 1D VISAR coplot demonstrating effects of a tantalum–lithium fluoride–tin flyer
 (Lithium_Fluoride), reduced lithium fluoride thickness (LiF_Thin), reduced flyer velocity (LiF_Slow),
and a replacement of the lithium fluoride middle layer with copper (Copper). Refer to text above plot for
 further details.

3.3 Selected Baseline Configuration
Based on this analysis, the copper middle-layer configuration was selected as the baseline
design. The flyer consisted of tantalum, copper, and tin layers impacting a tin target. The
respective layer and target thicknesses were 0.3238 cm, 0.1693 cm, 0.1208 cm, and 0.2761 cm,
with a flyer velocity of 0.2359 cm/μs.

The final configuration employed a mesh zone size of 10 μm, which provided a clear and stable
two-shock VISAR response while remaining computationally tractable. Simulations were
typically run on a small number of nodes for durations sufficient to capture both shocks and the
associated ejecta production (four nodes with a wall time of one hour typically did the trick).

4. Two-Dimensional Ejecta Simulations and Parameter Refinement

4.1 Baseline Two-Dimensional Ejecta Calculation
A two-dimensional FLAG input deck incorporating the selected flyer configuration and ejecta
model was constructed and executed. Initial two-dimensional results showed a less well-defined
double-shock structure compared to the corresponding one-dimensional calculations, motivating
further refinement.

 FIG. 3. VISAR of 2D 25 µm flyer with selected configuration

Mesh resolution was increased from 25 μm to 10 μm, and flyer layer thicknesses were adjusted
to extend the shock separation time. Figure 4 shows the result of reducing the zone size from 25
µm to 10 µm.

 FIG. 4. VISAR of 2D 10 µm flyer with selected configuration

Additionally, the copper and tin layers were individually increased by factors of 2, 2.5, and 3.
VISAR analysis demonstrated that doubling the tin layer thickness most effectively elongated the
first shock and produced a clean, well-separated double-shock structure.

 FIG. 5. VISAR of 2D 10 µm flyer with doubled tin layer thickness, FLAG v3.26.1

At this stage, mass-versus-time data from the ejecta ultra files confirmed ejecta production. The
resulting velocity and mass histories define the baseline calculation using the production FLAG
code (v3.26.1). This baseline configuration consists of:
A flyer of tantalum, copper, and tin layers impacting a tin target. The respective layer and
   target thicknesses were 0.3238 cm, 0.1693 cm, 0.1208 cm, and 0.2761 cm, with a flyer
velocity of 0.2359 cm/μs. The input deck for this calculation is 2D with a zone size of 10 μm.

 FIG. 6. Mass vs Time plot of 2D 10 µm flyer with doubled tin layer thickness, FLAG v3.26.1

The RMI ejecta source model parameters for this baseline configuration included prescribed
perturbation wavelength and amplitude, with ejecta triggering based on a velocity jump criterion.
Following this mass output, the velocity threshold was set to 0.039 cm/µs to ensure ejecta
production from both shocks observed in the VISAR diagnostics. Full details of the FLAG input
deck for this baseline configuration are provided in Appendix A.

5. Execution Using Brayden’s Private FLAG Branch

To evaluate reconstructed-interface ejecta sourcing, the baseline configuration was run using a
private development build of FLAG provided by Brayden Wagoner. This required manually
copying the appropriate flag.000 binary and adjusting the execution environment to ensure
compatibility with the private build.

Several practical issues were encountered during this process, including runtime errors related to
environment variables and processor allocation. These were resolved by explicitly controlling the
execution environment and running with multiple ranks, which distributed the ejecta source sites
across processors and avoided the earlier failures. These steps are documented here to assist
future users of the private build.

Three simulations were performed using this branch: the standard calculation (a), the
reconstructed-interface calculation (b), and the reconstructed-interface calculation with ALE
strategies enabled (c).

5.1 Brayden’s FLAG Branch Preparation
The input deck was altered to optimize ejecta production and capture of the double-shock
structure. Specifically, the parameter duration_time (line 775), which defines the duration of
ejecta production, was increased from 0.5 µs to 1.0 µs to ensure that ejecta generation lasted the
full duration of the initial shock, which was approximately 0.5 µs. Additionally, the parameter
puthresh (line 764), which sets the velocity threshold for ejecta production, was uncommented

and reduced from 0.07 cm/µs to 0.039 cm/µs. This change ensured that the second shock, with a
velocity difference of approximately 0.0417 cm/µs, would trigger ejecta production, as the
previous threshold was too high.

5.3 Scratch Execution Using sbatch
The simulation was executed on Brayden’s version using eight processors with a wall time of
one hour. Then the reconstructed interface option was executed, enabled by uncommenting line
758 in the input deck. The job was submitted using a modified submission script via the sbatch
command.

For simulation (a), outputs included Sim1_flyer.visar.visar and Sim1_Ejecta_mass.ult.00001:

These files did not complete within the allotted time, so the wall time was increased from one
hour to four hours on eight nodes to ensure full completion.

For simulation (b), the outputs included Sim2_flyer.visar.visar and Sim2_Ejecta_mass.ult.00001,
corresponding to the simulation with the reconstructed interface enabled:

Unfortunately, enabling the reconstructed interface by changing a single line in the input deck
did not produce ejecta. Simulation (b) ran until approximately 1.2 µs, which is past the
occurrence of both shock breakouts. Therefore, under these conditions, ejecta production was
expected, and a measurable ejecta mass should have been recorded, but none was observed.

6. Results & Discussion

VISAR traces from simulation (a), coplotted for both FLAG versions, show strong agreement in
the timing and structure of the shock features, indicating that the baseline configuration produces
consistent results across code versions.

 FIG. VISAR traces of simulation (a) coplotted for both FLAG versions

 Similarly, the mass–time comparisons demonstrate that, when identical physics options are
 enabled, the simulations reproduce comparable shock breakout behavior.

A key and somewhat unexpected result emerged when the reconstructed interface option was
enabled in simulation (b). The simulation ran to approximately 1.2 μs, which was beyond the
occurrence of both shock breakouts, yet the output file Sim2_Ejecta_mass.ult recorded zero

ejecta mass, suggesting that ejecta production was not captured under the current configuration.
Importantly, this simulation did not use ALE and therefore never actually constructed a
reconstructed interface. Nevertheless, simply setting the reconstructed interface line altered the
outcome.

This is a subtle but significant point. Enabling the reconstructed interface option directs FLAG to
use a different section of the code path that is intended, in principle, to behave identically when
no reconstructed interfaces are present. The disappearance of ejecta under these conditions
suggests that this assumption may not hold in practice. A current hypothesis involves the
employment of pszoner, the method used to construct the mesh. The zone connectivity was not
initially established properly along the crucial boundary. This hypothesis is still under
investigation.

 Worth noting: Increasing NPP and running just past the first shock breakout results in a
 noticeable difference in ejecta mass.

5. Conclusion

The results presented here are based on a carefully tuned simulation configuration. Considerable
effort was devoted to identifying an appropriate flyer design, layer thicknesses, and mesh
resolution capable of producing a clean and well-resolved double-shock response and
implementing this information in a 2D FLAG input deck with ejecta.

The overarching goal of this project was to test the reconstructed interface ejecta sourcing in
FLAG. The central finding is that enabling the reconstructed interface option suppressed ejecta
production entirely in this simulation, even in the case that no reconstructed interface was
formed. This indicates that activating the reconstructed interface line in the input deck alone can
change the ejecta behavior, pointing to a potential issue or limitation in the current
implementation. As previously mentioned, the current interpretation of this behavior is that the
reconstructed interface capability may be sensitive to how the mesh is defined.

APPENDIX

 A. Achieving a clearly resolved double-shock structure required several targeted
 adjustments. Introducing a copper middle layer, reducing the zone size from 25 μm to 10
 μm, and doubling the tin flyer thickness significantly improved both the visibility and
 separation of the two shocks. The copper layer proved especially effective, likely due to
 its higher shock transmissivity relative to lithium fluoride, while the increased tin
 thickness elongated the first shock, allowing sufficient separation between the two shocks
 and producing a well-resolved VISAR signature. Fine zoning was also critical,
 underscoring the importance of mesh resolution for accurately capturing closely spaced
 shock features in two-dimensional simulations.
