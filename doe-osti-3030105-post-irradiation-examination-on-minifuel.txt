 ABSTRACT

Post-irradiation examination (PIE) of MiniFuel compacts was conducted at Oak Ridge National Laboratory
(ORNL) under the Nuclear Science User Facilities project in collaboration with Kairos Power (KP) to
evaluate the performance of tristructural-isotropic (TRISO) particles under high particle power and
fluoride-salt-cooled high-temperature reactor (FHR)-relevant conditions. MiniFuel compacts containing
low-enriched uranium oxide-uranium carbide (LEUCO), low-enriched uranium dioxide (LEUO2), and
natural UCO (NUCO) kernels were irradiated for four cycles at ORNL’s High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR)
at target temperatures between 500°C and 900°C. Post irradiation, the experiment was disassembled at
ORNL to recover the MiniFuel subcapsules, which were subsequently punctured to measure fission gas
release. Subcapsule disassembly allowed the recovery of components of interest, such as silicon carbide
(SiC) thermometry, fuel specimens, fission product sinks, and SiC spacers. The experimental irradiation
temperature was confirmed by analyzing the SiC thermometry via dilatometry. PIE on the fuel specimens
included gamma spectrometry and deconsolidation leach burn leach, which were complemented by imaging
techniques such as x-ray computed tomography, optical microscopy, and electron microscopy. The PIE
results provide insight into TRISO particle integrity, fission product retention, coating performance, and
kernel migration, informing fuel qualification for application in KP’s FHR concept.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Tristructural-isotropic (TRISO) fuel has demonstrated exceptional performance in high-temperature gas-
cooled reactor conditions, but Kairos Power’s (KP’s) fluoride-salt-cooled high-temperature reactor (FHR)
will operate the fuel at lower operating temperatures (500°C–700°C) and higher particle powers than in
previous tests [1]. KP’s FHR design leverages TRISO fuel in pebble form; the salt coolant enables higher
power densities and altered fission product (FP) transport behavior in comparison with helium-cooled
systems. Enhanced heat transfer from liquid salt, relative to helium, enables higher particle powers and
lower fuel operating temperatures. The higher particle powers could increase the probability of kernel
migration, although the lower operating temperatures may mitigate this phenomenon due to reduced CO
production [2]. Finally, the lower-temperature operation could challenge the integrity of the TRISO silicon
carbide (SiC) layer due to increased SiC swelling at lower temperatures [3].

To support the development of KP’s FHR fuel concept, KP and Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL),
under the Nuclear Science User Facilities (NSUF) program, initiated an irradiation testing experiment using
ORNL’s High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) and Irradiated Fuels Examination Laboratory (IFEL). The test
matrix was constructed to allow for the separation of the effects of temperature and particle power (by
varying the fuel enrichment). Additionally, multiple kernel compositions were considered, including
uranium carbide – uranium oxide (UCO) and uranium dioxide (UO2) kernels; the latter generally exhibited
more significant kernel migration in previous studies. The fuel specimens included in this experiment were
fabricated at ORNL under the strategic partnership project NFE-19-07790[4] as cylindrical compacts, each
containing 20 TRISO particles with either low-enriched UCO (LEUCO), natural UCO (NUCO), or low-
enriched UO2 (LEUO2) kernels embedded in a graphite matrix [4]. The TRISO particles were sourced from
the Advanced Gas Reactor (AGR) program, providing a well-characterized pedigree that enables direct
comparison of the results from this irradiation testing with past AGR experiments. Pre-irradiation
characterization of these fuel compacts included dimensional inspection, x-ray computed tomography
(XCT) to verify particle count and placement, and as-fabricated defect analysis that confirmed very low
defect fractions [1]. The MiniFuel design [5][6] was adapted to perform irradiation testing of these
compacts at target SiC layer temperatures of 500°C, 700°C, and 900°C [6][6][7]. Five MiniFuel targets
(KP01–KP05) were assembled and inserted into HFIR for a four-cycle irradiation campaign
[7][8][9][8][10][9].

Following irradiation, the MiniFuel targets were transferred to IFEL for disassembly and post-irradiation

 3. EXPERIMENT DISASSEMBLY

After target disassembly at ORNL’s IFEL [9], each subcapsule was punctured to collect fission gas release
(FGR) measurements prior to subcapsule disassembly [10]. The FGR results are discussed in Section 5. All
30 subcapsules were disassembled to recover internal components of interest, such as the passive SiC TM,
fuel compacts, spacers, and sinks. The subcapsule cutting was intended to be performed below the FGR
puncture hole and above the sink, to ensure the sink recovery and the subcapsule disassembly. However,
some subcapsules presented a larger FGR puncture hole and required re-cutting to disassemble the
experiment, sacrificing the sink. APPENDIX A provides images of the recovered fuel specimens and
APPENDIX B details the components recovered for each subcapsule. All fuel compacts from the high-
temperature target (KP01) were self-deconsolidated upon disassembly, and most of the 20 particles from
each of these compacts were recovered. Self-deconsolidation was observed only in KP01 (the highest-
temperature target) compacts. Given the lack of correlation with disassembly sequence, number of
disassembly cuts, or personnel performing the work, the underlying cause remains unresolved and may

 4

reflect temperature and/or target-specific design factors; additional work is needed. In addition, the
conservative approach taken when pressing the compacts to avoid damaging the TRISO particles
contributes to the KP01 experiment characteristics. This pressing approach resulted in a relatively low
matrix density though within the matrix densities explored in the first AGR program irradiation (AGR-1).
A subset of fuel compacts irradiated at lower temperatures were recovered broken (compacts from KP323,
KP336, and KP232, and a subset of compacts appeared to be attached to the SiC spacer (compacts from
KP122, KP322, and KP336). Two fuel compacts, KP321 and KP326, were lost during the hot cell
disassembly process, and one fuel specimen (KP124) was recovered upon disassembly but subsequently
lost during the PIE process. All but two SiC TM were recovered upon disassembly and were shipped to the
Low Activation Materials Development and Analysis (LAMDA) laboratory. PIE work subsequent to this
disassembly is detailed in the following sections.

 5. FISSION GAS RELEASE MEASUREMENTS

Each subcapsule was punctured at IFEL to measure FGR [10]. FGR results are presented in Figure 4 as a
function of experimental temperature. Most of the FGR measurements were below the minimum detectable
activity (MDA). The recoil release from a single failed particle calculated from Lewis [13] was estimated
around 0.15% [10], which provides a lower bound for the minimum release that might be expected from a
single particle failure. Failed particles are not expected to release their entire fission gas inventory, as release
is an integral response requiring diffusive transport from the kernel through intact coating layers and the
surrounding matrix. Therefore, the fractional release from a single particle is expected to be less than unity
and strongly temperature dependent.

 6. ANALYSIS OF PARTICLE FAILURE

A subset of compacts were selected to perform particle failure analysis using the deconsolidation leach burn
leach (DLBL) process, re-established at ORNL under the AGR program [14], as well as imaging via XCT
and optical microscopy (OM).

6.1 DLBL PROCESS

The purpose of the DLBL process is to determine whether any TRISO fuel particles failed during irradiation
and, if so, whether it was a full TRISO failure or partial failure. Failures may be complete, resulting in the
release of uranium, transuranics, and FPs into the leaching solution, or partial, meaning that breaches are
limited to failure of the SiC layer with one or more pyrolytic carbon layers intact coating layers (e.g., inner
pyrolytic carbon [IPyC] or outer pyrolytic carbon [OPyC]). The process, which follows an ORNL procedure
[15] [15] adapted for MiniFuel compacts, is summarized in the flowchart provided in Figure 5.

For recovered compacts, the process begins with deconsolidation, in which an electrical current is applied
across the compact via electrodes while the bottom of the specimen is immersed in concentrated nitric acid.
This step separates the graphite matrix from the fuel particles. An aliquot of the deconsolidation acid is

 6

collected and sent to the Radioactive Materials Analytical Laboratory (RMAL) for radiochemical analysis
(see Section 6.1.1). Fuel compacts that are self-deconsolidated upon disassembly bypass this step. The
resulting particles and residual matrix then undergo two 24 h pre-burn acid leaches in a Soxhlet extraction
system. Particles and debris are placed in a boiling flask containing concentrated nitric acid. The acid is
continuously distilled into the Soxhlet chamber using a heating mantle and condenser. Acid samples from
each pre-burn leach are collected and analyzed at RMAL. These data determine the effective number of
failed particles in the compact.

After the pre-burn leaches, the particles are analyzed in the Irradiated Microsphere Gamma Analyzer
(IMGA) (see Section 6.1.2) to identify particles with abnormal gamma signatures that may indicate failure.
A subset of particles (typically two) is selected for XCT and OM (see Sections 6.1.3 and 6.1.4, respectively),
and the remaining particles continue in the DLBL sequence.

Next, the particles undergo the burn step, in which the graphite matrix, OPyC, and any exposed IPyC
coatings are oxidized in air. This is performed in a specialized quartz flask with an inlet cap that controls
airflow and oxidation rate. The flask is placed in a muffle furnace, which is ramped to 750°C; this
temperature is held for ~72 h. Finally, the particles are subjected to two post-burn leaches, which are
performed in the same Soxhlet setup as the pre-burn stage. Each leach is followed by a collection of acid
samples for RMAL analysis. These post-burn leaches reveal whether actinides or FPs were released due to
SiC layer failure, providing a measure of SiC coating performance under irradiation. Before each set of
leaches (pre- and post-burn), the flask is cleaned with nitric acid to minimize background contamination.

 Figure 5. Flowchart of the DLBL process.

6.1.1 Radiochemical Analysis of the DLBL Solutions

Analyses of the nitric acid solutions collected during the DLBL process were performed at ORNL’s RMAL
and included inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) and gamma spectroscopy.

Isotopes of uranium and plutonium were analyzed via single quadrupole ICP-MS (iCAP RQ, Thermo Fisher
Scientific) and Qtegra Intelligent Scientific Data Solution (ISDS) software. The sample introduction system
consisted of an ESI Teflon ST-style high-flow nebulizer with cyclonic spray chamber and nickel cones with
a 2.8 mm skimmer cone insert. The samples were prepared for analysis using Optima grade nitric acid
(Fisher Scientific) and Type I ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ). The linear regressed calibration curves were
verified using independent lots of standards with traceability to the National Institute of Standards and
Technology (NIST) and certified reference materials (CRMs). Calibration and verification standards were
obtained from Inorganic Ventures (Christiansburg, VA) and High-Purity Standards (Charleston, SC).

 7

Gamma analysis was performed using five high-purity germanium (HPGe) detector gamma spectroscopy
systems (Ametek models GMX-35190-P-S, GEM-25195-S, and GMX3094-70-CW, and two Mirion model
GC2518). Each detector was coupled with a Mirion Lynx multichannel analyzer (model LYNX-MCA) and
cooled using Mirion’s cryostat systems (model ICC-VD). The gamma data were collected and analyzed
using Mirion’s Apex-Gamma/Guard data acquisition software. Each detector was calibrated with a NIST
traceable standard covering an energy range of approximately 0.059 to 2 MeV. A 0.1 mL aliquot of each
sample was collected into a calibrated geometry, counted for 30 min, and analyzed following RMAL
procedures.

6.1.2 IMGA Gamma Counting

The IMGA is used to automate the process of gamma scanning individual irradiated fuel particles. It is
stationed within IFEL in a small hot cell on the second floor of the laboratory. It is connected to the main
hot cells on the ground floor with a small elevator, and material can be added to the hot cell through a 4 in.
throw-in port.

The IMGA device itself, shown in Figure 6, consists of a Parker Daedal X-Y-Theta motion control system
and an SMC vacuum generator. The vacuum generator is connected to a swappable tool on the X slide,
which is used to pick up individual particles with a vacuum needle in particle handling mode or vials with
a vacuum cup in vial handling mode. The particles are held in either open vials or closed individual counting
vials in a carousel on the rotary turntable. After the particles are picked up with the vacuum generator, they
are positioned in front of an ORTEC HPGe gamma detector outside the hot cell and separated by a thin
beryllium window. The gamma detector is on rails and can be slid closer to or farther from the hot cell,
allowing the counting geometry to be adjusted since the particle counting position is fixed. The detector
signal is digitally processed by an ORTEC DSPEC-50 and analyzed with ORTEC GammaVision 8.10
paired with custom handling and sorting software.

 Figure 6. Photograph of the IMGA in particle mode outside the hot cell.

 8

Particle mode is used to automatically count thousands of particles, primarily in support of the AGR
program. It uses a vacuum needle to handle individual particles and counts them on the timescale of
minutes. Particles are then sorted into bins on the carousel depending on their retention of key radionuclides,
such as 144Ce and 137Cs. This process allows for greater throughput and counting of thousands of particles
over nights and weekends but does not maintain particle identity, except for particles with abnormal gamma
signatures which are automatically isolated. Vial mode is used to count individual particles stored in
counting vials. These are handled with a vacuum cup and typically counted on the timescale of hours. This
allows particle identity to be maintained and allows for greater count times to measure low-yield
radioisotopes such as 154Eu. The particles counted in this report were handled in vial mode.

After particles are deconsolidated in the main hot cells, as described in Section 6.1, they are transferred in
small aluminum cans to the IMGA cell using the elevator. A stereoscope and micromanipulator stage are
used to image the particles, clean any debris, and place the particles in counting vials. These prepared
particles are placed in the counting carousel, and a short count is taken to determine a counting geometry
that yields an acceptable detector dead time. Two particles from each compact were counted for long
enough to capture high-confidence 154Eu data (typically 2–6 h), whereas the rest were counted on a shorter
timescale to allow for greater throughput while still capturing high-confidence data for the other
radionuclides of interest. After gamma scanning with the IMGA system, the particles were unloaded into
individually labeled vials to track identity and allow for mounting of selected particles in metallography
and XCT sample mounts.

Gamma energies in gammas per second were first calculated with a background subtraction and geometry
detection efficiency correction for the gamma energy and geometry, as previously determined when
performing detector calibration. Next, the total gamma decay activity for each particle was determined by
dividing the gammas per second by the product of the self-shielding factor and the branching ratio for that
gamma energy. Finally, the resulting activities were decay-corrected to one day after EOI, which was
February 5, 2022. This allows for direct activity comparison between particles. Resulting activities in
becquerels were used to determine two different unitless ratios. To account for fabrication variations such
as kernel size, the resulting activities for each gamma energy peak were normalized to the average values
and ratioed against normalized 137Cs. This is referred to as the measured to adjusted average (M/AA) value.
Activity values were also normalized to average to allow for kernel size comparison. This is referred to as
the measured to average (M/A) value. M/A and M/AA values were used to identify normal and potentially
failed particles to be used in XCT (Section 6.1.3) and OM (Section 6.1.4). Activity summary values are
shown in Appendix C. Additional information of the method using the M/A value and M/AA values can be
found in Hunn, et al [16].

6.1.3 X-ray Computed Tomography

Particles were selected for XCT analysis based on their activity values calculated using the IMGA.
Summary activity values are shown in APPENDIX C. One average particle was mounted from each
compact, and a second average particle was reserved at the IMGA for future analysis. If there was a particle
that showed FP release, such as KP123-06, it was mounted as well. The XCT mounts, shown in Figure 7,
were loaded into the IMGA cell with a bead of Struers EpoFix epoxy in the v-cup in the stem. The
micromanipulator stage and stereoscope were used to pick up an individual particle and place it into the
XCT mount with a mark drawn on the side to have a reference point for maintaining orientation when
transferring to a metallography mount after XCT analysis (if an internal feature of interest was identified).
The completed XCT mounts with a captured particle were then loaded out of the hot cell.

XCT was performed using a Versa 620 system operating with an accelerating voltage of 160 kV at 25 W

software with a beam hardening constant of 0.25 to reduce uneven brightness from the sample edge to
center due to hardening of the x-ray spectrum.

6.1.4 Optical Microscopy

Particles were selected for OM based on their activity values calculated using the IMGA, as shown in
APPENDIX C. Two average particles from each compact were selected for OM. The 0.110 in. aluminum
MiniMet mounts, as shown in Figure 8, were placed on a piece of double-sided tape that was placed on a
small plastic disk. The mount on the tape was loaded into the IMGA cell using the 4 in. throw-in port, and
the two selected particles were placed in the mount using the micromanipulator stage and stereoscope. The
first particle was placed so that it was touching an edge of the mount, and the second particle was placed
centrally in the mount so that it touched the first particle. This was done to assist later polishing, as particle
midplane could be easily determined as when both particles were visibly touching each other and the first
particle was visibly touching the edge of the mount. After the particles were captured on the double-sided
tape in the mount, the mount was transferred to the MET2 cell. The MET2 cell is also located on the second
floor of IFEL and is accessible via an elevator connected to the main hot cells on the ground floor and
through a 4 in. throw-in port. The samples were then potted with Struers EpoFix epoxy impregnated with

 10

15% by weight graphite powder to prevent charging when later examined with the Tescan Vega scanning
electron microscope (SEM). A vacuum bell jar was used to remove air bubbles from the mount.

Silicon carbide spacers from certain compacts were also selected for OM. To mount these, the ¼ in. mount
shown in Figure 8 was used to accommodate the 5.70 mm diameter of the spacer. The mount was affixed
to a plastic disc and filled with graphite epoxy, prepared as described in this section. A plastic dummy was
used to create a slot to drop in the spacer after the epoxy cured. The dummy was removed, and the prepared
mount was loaded into the MET2 hot cell, where tweezers were used in-cell to drop the spacer into the
cutout slot and backfill with more carbon epoxy.

Figure 8. Left: Image of a 0.110 in. aluminum MiniMet mount. Center: Image of a ¼ in. aluminum MiniMet
 mount. Right: Image of an X-mount.

If, after XCT analysis, a particle was selected for further investigation, it was transferred to a hood in IFEL
and loaded into a variation of the 0.110 in. aluminum MiniMet mounts. This variation, called an X-mount,
is shown in Figure 8. The marking on the side of the XCT mount can be used to load the particle into the
X-mount at any desired rotational angle in order to expose features of interest when grinding and polishing.
The stem of the XCT mount was loaded horizontally into the side opening of the X-mount, and the opening
was filled with Struers EpoFix epoxy. After the epoxy cured, the stem of the XCT mount was snapped off,
leaving the particle and the top of the XCT mount captured in the X-mount. This X-mount was loaded into
the MET2 cell and polished in the same manner as the regular MiniMet mounts, as described below.

A Buehler MiniMet 1000 grinder polisher was used to thin the samples once the epoxy cured. A diamond
lapping disk with 15 µm grit was first used with water and isopropyl alcohol to grind about 40% of the
sample away. Next, a 6 µm grit diamond lapping disk was used with a water and isopropyl alcohol mixture
to remove the 15 µm scratches and reach about 45% of the depth of the sample. Diamond lapping films (3
µm, 1 µm, and 0.5 µm) were subsequently used with Buehler IsoCut fluid to polish to 50% of sample depth,
remove scratches from previous grinding steps, and prepare a smooth, flat surface for OM and later electron
microscopy.

Cleaned and polished samples were placed on a Leica DMI 5000 M inverted optical microscope and imaged
using a Leica DFC 490 color digital camera. Micrographs of each particle were captured using the 10x
objective to obtain a wide-angle image of the particle. Micrographs of each particle were then captured
using the 20x objective with emphasis on the kernel and north, south, east, and west hemispheres of the
particle. A total of six micrographs per particle were captured. This was repeated for the other particle in
the mount. The imaged mount was transferred to a plastic vial with cotton padding and loaded out of the
hot cell for analysis with the SEM, as described in Section 6.1.5.

6.1.5 Scanning Electron Microscopy

Electron microscopy and elemental analysis were performed using a Tescan Vega SEM equipped with an
Oxford Ultim Max 65 silicon drift detector for energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS) using the
Oxford Aztec analysis software suite. After OM, the samples were transferred out of the IFEL hot cell to a
charging area for initial decontamination and were then transferred to a hood near the SEM for final
decontamination and surface cleaning. Once the samples were sufficiently cleaned, they were mounted onto
an aluminum SEM stub using conductive carbon tape and colloidal graphite. The samples were then coated
with a 20 nm thick layer of carbon using a Cressington Series 108 sputter coater to ensure a conductive
surface to mitigate charging effects.

Both secondary electron (SE) imaging and backscattered electron (BSE) imaging were utilized to
characterize the particle cross sections. The SE images provide surface-sensitive information, enabling the
identification of surface features and the differentiation of surface debris from embedded FP features. The
BSE images provide atomic number contrast, enabling the identification of high-atomic-number actinides
and FPs that are embedded in the relatively low-atomic-number TRISO layers. Image montages of entire
particles, with emphasis on the OPyC, SiC, IPyC, and buffer layers, were obtained using both imaging
modes to ensure that surface features (such as loose debris) were not misinterpreted as embedded features.
A general image set was taken for each particle cross section, consisting of a montage of more than 150, 80
× 80 μm field-of-view micrographs stitched together to form a high-resolution panoramic image of the
entire particle. Additional images of areas of interest were also acquired when necessary. The SE
micrographs were obtained using a 5 keV accelerating voltage, a probe current of 500 pA, a 19 µs dwell
time, and a working distance of 5 mm. The BSE micrographs were obtained using a 20 kV accelerating
voltage, a probe current of 3 nA, a 19 µs dwell time, and a working distance of 10 mm.

Locations were then chosen on each particle cross section for EDS analysis to examine the distribution of
actinides and FPs in the TRISO layers after irradiation. Point-ID analysis was used to identify local
compositions in high-Z features. The compositions acquired via Point-ID analysis are only qualitative due
to the submicrometer size of the analyzed features, which include significant contributions from the
surrounding matrix. Additionally, minor constituents present in the high-Z actinide and FP features may
not be observed due to sensitivity limitations of the analysis. The Point-ID analysis was acquired under
similar beam conditions to BSE imaging (20 kV accelerating voltage, 3 nA probe current, 15 mm working
distance), and the general acquisition parameters used for Point-ID analysis were to collect spectra within
an energy range of 0–20 keV with 10 eV/channel, 4,096 channels, a unitless process time of 2, and a live
time of 30 s.

 7. GAMMA COUNTING OF SUBCAPSULE COMPONENTS

Selected compacts, graphite disks, and spacers were gamma counted using either the IMGA or the Vertical
Counting System (VCS) at IFEL.

The IMGA is typically used to obtain gamma emission spectra and counting data of individual particles in
either loose particles or in counting vials, as described in greater detail in Section 6.1.2. On occasion, the
IMGA was also used to gamma count other components from subcapsules, such as the sinks, spacers, or
intact fuel compacts. These other components were counted in small aluminum cans (pellet cans) because
they were too large to fit in the IMGA counting carousel. The pellet cans were manually placed in front of
the detector from within the hot cell, but a major limitation of this technique is that they are too large to fit
within the IMGA counting cup (shown on the bottom right in Figure 6), so they were placed on top of a
tungsten shield in front of the counting cup. Additionally, the interior diameter of the pellet cans is 18 mm
with no way to center the contents. The combined uncertainties of the manual placement and interior
diameter represent an estimated 2 cm of uncertainty in the counting geometry. Furthermore, the efficiency
for the counting geometry was calculated using a bare point source unshielded by aluminum. This adds
more uncertainty to the gamma measurement data. Therefore, the gamma measurement data for components
counted in pellet cans should be treated as qualitative due to geometry and efficiency uncertainties.

The VCS is an ORTEC HPGe detector set inside a leaded enclosure in a low-background area. The output
signal is fed to a multichannel analyzer, DSPEC-50, which is paired with the spectrum analysis software,
GammaVision. Inside the leaded enclosure is a custom rack, which allows sample holder plates to be staged
at desired distances from the face of the detector. This distance is determined based on the activity of the
sample and the dead time of the detector. Greater than 10,000 counts are required for each peak of interest
to have confidence quantifying activities, and this determines the total amount of time that each sample is
counted. Point sources (137Cs, 152Eu, and 241Am) are used to perform energy and efficiency calibrations. A

 15

calibration is created for each counting geometry needed. This information is controlled by making official
log entries that mark the sample name, time, geometry, system, and qualified user.

Figure 11 shows the gamma measurement results for selected FP sinks and spacers, expressed as the fraction
of 106Ru, 134Cs, 137Cs, and 154Eu activities calculated for the fuel compact. Most sinks and spacers show an
activity less than 1% of the fuel compact inventory.

Passive SiC TM analysis confirmed that experimental irradiation temperatures were within 5% of design
values for the 500°C targets, with deviations up to 13% at 900°C, thereby validating the thermal conditions
of the campaign. FGR measurements indicated that most subcapsules were below minimum detectable
activity; only a limited number of specimens (e.g., KP124, KP224, KP324) showed measurable release
consistent with the recoil equivalent of one or a few failed particles. Gamma counting of compacts and
structural components with IMGA or the VCS further supported the conclusion of overall strong FP
retention.

The DLBL process indicated limited particle failures, predominantly during the pre-burn leach stage. For
cases with pre-burn particle failure observed, no additional failed equivalent particles were detected in the
post-burn leaches, indicating no SiC layer failure obscured by an intact OPyC layer. All fuel samples from
target KP01 (planned for the highest temperatures) were recovered self-deconsolidated, whereas all lower-
temperature target specimens required deconsolidation and were recovered as intact or broken. The
disassembly sequence, number of cuts, and personnel varied and do not correlate with the self-
deconsolidation outcome. Additional work is warranted to examine the KP01 target design and temperature
influences. Comparison with FGR data shows only partial correlation: compacts with detected failed
equivalent particles (KP123 and KP126) had either very low or unavailable FGR values, whereas NUCO
specimens (KP224 and KP324) showed measurable FGR despite no failed equivalent particles being
identified by DLBL. Complementary XCT and OM analyses confirmed coating integrity in most particles,
with selected specimens showing expected irradiation-induced phenomena such as buffer densification, no
sign of kernel migration, and localized cracking of pyrolytic carbon layers. SEM and EDS data were
collected but not analyzed to date.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that the TRISO fuel particles maintained their structural and FP
retention capability under the demanding conditions of high particle power and extended irradiation in
HFIR. The performance observed in both LEUCO and LEUO2 kernels at elevated temperatures, as well as
the consistency of NUCO behavior at lower powers, provide confidence in the robustness of TRISO
coatings for application in KP’s FHR concept. The data generated from this campaign can also serve as a
first set of benchmark data for fuel performance modeling to ultimately support the future deployment of
TRISO-based fuels in advanced reactor systems.

APPENDIX C. IMGA SUMMARY DATA

 APPENDIX C. IMGA SUMMARY DATA

The tables in this appendix document the data for key gamma-emitting fission products captured using the
IMGA. The process for capturing and correcting the data is described in Section 6.1.2. Potentially failed
particles, if identified, are listed at the tops of the tables. M/A values indicate particle-to-particle variations
that can be related to factors such as variation in quantity of fissionable material and localized burnup. The
M/AA values are adjusted using normalized 137Cs activity (137Cs is normally a well-retained nuclide) to
account for the variations present in the M/A value. This makes the M/AA values more indicative of fission
product retention. Summary values are at the bottoms of the tables and provide an indication of the variation
of values within the tables.

Several particles have less-than values reported for 110mAg. These are based on the quantifiable limit at the
time the particles were counted. In KP125, none of the particles had measurable 110mAg.
