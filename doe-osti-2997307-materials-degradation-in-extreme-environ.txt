LDRD PROJECT NUMBER: 237207
LDRD PROJECT TITLE: Materials Degradation in Extreme Environments:
Novel In-situ Measurements of Cracking in Molten Salt
PROJECT TEAM MEMBERS: Rebecca Schaller, Ryan Katona, Kenneth
Armijo, Jason Taylor, Dimitri Madden

ABSTRACT:

The safety and reliability of next-gen reactors, i.e. molten salt reactors (MSR), depends on
materials performance and longevity in extreme environments, yet no in-situ, validated
measurement techniques for environmentally assisted cracking (EAC), one potential degradation
mechanism, exist for these conditions. This project combined the use of in-situ direct current
potential drop (DCPD) crack growth rate determination applied to a horizontal load frame with
high temperature fittings and novel sample geometries to enable evaluation in MSR
environments and develop a full in-situ SCC measurement capability for extreme environments
(high conductivity and elevated T), necessary to predict materials reliability. A successful
demonstration of DCPD measurement in molten NaNO3/KNO3 salts at 340 oC was accomplished
and validated through post-test fractography imaging. This is the first ever public example, to
the authors knowledge, of an in-situ crack growth rate measurement in a molten salt
environment.

INTRODUCTION AND EXECUTIVE SUMMARY OF RESULTS:

Next generation 4 nuclear energy (NE) molten salt reactors (MSR) operate under conditions that
push the limits of current engineering alloys in terms of corrosion, ion mobilization, and
subsequent thermal mechanical derated strength at temperature. Understanding materials
reliability is key to MSR performance, both for safety/reliability and reducing overall cost.
Corrosion susceptibility and subsequent time-dependent crack growth under MSR relevant
operational conditions, have been identified as degradation modes that are necessary for
operational insight and control, enabling materials resistance when exposed to these extreme
environmental conditions. While there has been significant research into both areas, tools are
still lacking for in-situ/real time measurements in high temperature and high conductivity salt
chemistries. Prior to this project, no in-situ crack growth rate measurement capabilities have
been publicly demonstrated for MSR conditions. The purpose of this research is to develop and
demonstrate an in-situ crack growth measurement capability in an extreme environment
condition (high conductivity and elevated temperature (T)), pertinent to understanding materials
degradation for advanced nuclear power.

Sandia National Laboratories is a multimission laboratory managed and operated by National Technology and
Engineering Solutions of Sandia, LLC, a wholly owned subsidiary of Honeywell International, Inc., for the U.S.
Department of Energy’s National Nuclear Security Administration under contract DE-NA-0003525.

Existing technologies for assessing overall materials reliability with respect to corrosion and
environmentally assisted cracking often take the form of a “cook and look” approach. Some
examples include the exposure of coupons of interest in simulated or surrogate molten salt
environments and post-test analysis, i.e. mass loss or gain for corrosion measurements or slow
strain rate testing in air (post exposure) to observe environmental exposure influences on
mechanical properties [1-4]. More recent studies have been successful in obtaining in-situ
corrosion rate measurements in MSR environments through potentiodynamic polarization
capabilities [5]. However, in the case of crack growth, no in-situ detection techniques have been
developed to date. Additionally, discrepancies exist between the current methods of crack
growth rate determination with either post exposure slow strain rate testing or exposure of pre-
stressed samples (i.e. c-rings, u-bends, or 4-point bends) and field observations. Several factors
can contribute to this, including the incubation time (time to initiate a pit or crack), secondary
crack formation, strain rate, lack of a corrosive environment during crack testing, corrosion
potential, etc. In-situ cracking measurements can resolve many of these inconsistencies
providing a more relevant measurement of the crack growth rates (CGR). While in-situ CGR
measurement techniques still remain unvalidated in MSR environments, they have been
calibrated for high conductivity solutions as in the case of a liquid gallium solution with a
conductivity of 106 S/m [6]. Additionally, other studies have demonstrated the viability of in-
situ measurements at elevated temperature, > 600 oC [7].

To determine in-situ CGR in extreme environments relevant to MSR, this project utilized direct
current potential drop (DCPD) techniques and a horizontal load frame, where Hastealloy C276
fixtures and fittings accommodated higher temperature operation (500 oC). Additionally, crack
samples were partially submerged, such that the pre-crack, and subsequently growing crack tip
would be submerged in the molten salt. All connections and fittings, as well as the DCPD wires,
were kept under an inert gaseous environment (Nitrogen) to reduce any potential noise, salt
purity contamination issues, galvanic coupling influences, and degradation of the load
frame/measurement system itself. An elongated crack sample was designed specifically for these
experiments to further enable separation between the submerged and inert regions. Initial
experiments were performed using sodium nitrate/potassium nitrate salts; these replicate similar
conditions to FLiNaK or FLiBe salts, which are commonly of interest for MSR environments
(they still exhibit the high conductivity and high melting temperatures), but with significantly
less hazards for laboratory testing.

The redesign and testing of the load frame electrochemical cell was carried out to enable
measurements under these extreme MSR environmental conditions. The modifications to the
system included: 1) the aforementioned modification to the sample to elongate the sample
allowing for separation between the crack tip and connections, 2) sealing of the Hastelloy C276
cell with high temperature gaskets and Ceramabond™ 571 Magnesium Adhesive (heat resistant
paint) on the exterior to prevent/reduce molten salt leakage, 3) sealing of all ports with

Ceramabond™ 571 Magnesium Adhesive to prevent leakage, 4) flow of nitrogen to create an
inert space above the sample and reduce potential reactions, and 5) application of heat tape and
insulation to cell exterior to achieve desired temperatures sufficient to melt the introduced salts
in the chamber. Each of these modifications will be discussed in detail along with any
iterations/lessons learned to help inform any potential future MSR experimental designs.
This research demonstrated the first in-situ measurement of crack growth rate in a molten salt
environment validated with post test fractography images. An average CGR of 6.5 x 10 -6 mm/s
with a crack extension of 0.31 mm was measured for fatigue crack growth of a compact tension
stainless steel sample in molten NaNO3/KNO3 salt at 340 oC. This not only established the first
publicly known in-situ CGR measurement in these molten salt environments, but also provides a
method for determining valuable details regarding materials behavior for extreme environments.

DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT AND
METHODOLOGY:

To enable in-situ CGR measurement in MSR extreme environments a horizontal load frame with
a DCPD system was used. This system was initially set up and validated to measure CGR using
DCPD in corrosive (concentrated saltwater brines) and lower T (< 90 °C) environments [8, 9].
However, to operate under MSR relevant conditions, multiple modifications had to be made.
The corrosion cell (shown in Figure 1) was originally acrylic with a volume of 3L and was
insufficient for the desired temperatures and environments. It was replaced with a C276 cell
(Hastelloy, a Ni-Cr-Mo alloy with known corrosion resistance). Additionally, the clevises and
pull rods (shown in the inset of Figure 1) were also C276 parts. While C276 alloys may not be
ideal for fixtures/cells for all MSR environments, especially those that operate at higher
temperatures (>>> 500 oC), as their mechanical properties begin to degrade, these parts were
used initially to achieve relevant measurements while reducing the overall cost of the system
(they were on-hand due to the initial design/intent of use for the load frame). C276 is stable
under the conditions intended for this study and insights gained from the use of the C276
hardware and fittings combined with additional fixtures and materials (i.e. the gaskets and
epoxies, originally developed for the high temperature molten salt pot studies) can inform on
future system redesign with more robust alloys.

As previously mentioned, the typical compact tension (CT) samples were redesigned and
machined with an elongated configuration (Figure 2) to help alleviate concerns of cell leakage,
galvanic influences/corrosion of connections for the DCPD system, and to maintain the pull
rods/clevises in an inert environment (above the molten salt, as shown in Figure 2-b). The
samples were an elongated version of CT specimens based on ASTM E1681 and ASTM E647
[10, 11]. Samples were fabricated from an austenitic stainless steel 304 grade L (SS304L) plate
(12” x 12” x 1.5”) with the mechanical properties and composition provided in Table 1 and
Table 2, respectively. For DCPD measurements, Ni wires (1mm diameter) were spot welded to
the samples (example setup shown in inset in Figure 1). The wires were not coated as the design
of the cell eliminated contact with the MSR environment. Prior to all testing, the sample was
subjected to a pre-cracking procedure to ensure an atomically sharp crack tip for crack growth
rate testing. This was performed within laboratory ambient air before exposure to heat/solution
on the modified molten salt load frame. Samples were fatigued using a frequency of 1 Hz, Kmax
(peak stress intensity factor) = 30 MPam, and an R-ratio (minimum cyclic stress to maximum
cyclic stress) of 0.1. This was continued until the pre-cracks reached a length of at least 1 in to
ensure that the crack tip would later be submerged in molten salts during testing.

After pre-cracking and prior to use on the modified load frame, samples were loaded into the cell
and load train using C-276 clevises. The samples were electrically isolated with ceramic coated
pins. Once the cell was oriented on the load frame, the CT samples were aligned with the
clevises to allow for free rotation and comply with the K solution boundary conditions [12].
Ports on the cell were attached to a gas line to maintain an inert atmosphere in the void space of
the cell above the molten salt once introduced (Figure 3-A). The cover gas used was nitrogen.
DCPD wires that were connected to the sample were oriented on the specimen facing up, such
that they would remain in the inert portion of the cell. The NaNO3/KNO3 salts were introduced
into the cell in the solid state through a funnel in one of the end ports and allowed to settle
gravitationally. Once heated above the eutectic melting point, these salts will melt to form the
NaNO3/KNO3 solution in the base of the chamber. Additional ports on the cell endcaps allowed
for introduction of the potential probe, the DCPD wires, and a thermocouple (TC). All ports
were sealed prior to testing with Ceramabond™ 571 Magnesium Adhesive. The cell endcaps
were sealed with gaskets hand machined from sheets of Flexitallic © Thermiculite (60 x 60 x
1/8”). The C276 cell itself was wrapped in heat trace (controlled by an external thermal
controller) and subsequently insulation for melting and maintaining the salt mixture in the
molten state within the cell during exposure and testing.

An initial study with this cell (Figure 3-a) was carried out to ensure heater viability, cell design,
and if the desired temperatures could be achieved to maintain the salts in the molten state.
However, once the salt melting temperatures were achieved (> 260 oC on the external
thermalcouple), leakage at the cell endcaps was observed (Figure 3-b). The cell was then taken
apart, the gaskets were re-cut and sealed and in the second variation, heat-resistant epoxy was
painted on the exterior of the endcaps to further seal the cell and alleviate future concerns of
leaking.

 Figure 3. Images of a) horizontal load frame with modifications for molten salt testing and b) initial thermal test with molten
 salts showing molten salt leak at failed thermiculite gasket.

Two additional heater tests were conducted prior to the actual in-situ cracking tests. In both,
additional tests, heater failures were noted. Air gaps within the configuration due to the
necessity to wrap heat trace around the cell, but with space limitations from the screws that
sealed the endcaps on the cell, led to multiple heat trace failures. In these iterations, as the heat
trace was wrapped on the exterior of the screws, variances or hotspots occurred across the heat
trace. Additionally, in both cases, the heat trace had been ramped from room temperature to 500
oC in one attempt, which led to burn out. In the final test, a simple solution was applied to

alleviate some of the issues of hotspots/burnout as a copper shim was created to go over the
exterior screws, allowing for even distributed heating of the heat trace. Additionally, the
temperature was ramped in a two-step process, from room temperature to 250 oC (external TC)
and holding overnight to ensure the cell equilibrates T while maintaining the salts in the solid
state. After the overnight hold, the cell temperature was then ramped from 250 to 450 oC, above
melting, and allowed to stabilize again. Initial tests were conducted at these elevated
temperatures to benchmark DCPD in the extreme environmental conditions. The air gap between
the copper shim and the C276 cell had a significant influence on the internal temperature, thus,
while the external temperature was 450 oC, the internal temperature was only ~340 oC.

For the fatigue CGR test, as mentioned, sodium nitrate/potassium nitrate salts were introduced
into the cell in the solid state. A mass of 467 g of NaNO3 and 311 g of KNO3 salts were
introduced and it was calculated that once melted, they would fill a volume below the clevises
and wires (to ensure the crack tip was submerged but the DCPD wire connections were not).
Nitrogen was introduced as a cover gas to maintain the rest of the cell in an inert environment of
approximately 1/3 PSIG. Once the temperature within was stabilized at 340 oC, the following

parameters were applied: an R ratio of 0.3 at a frequency of 1 Hz. The Kmax as calculated from
the ASTM E1681 was 26.33 MPam. However, as discussed, the CT specimen was elongated
for these experiments, thus the change in geometry influences the calculation of K. Therefore,
the sample geometry was modeled in COMSOLTM Multiphysics modeling software. K values
were calculated utilizing J-integral which is a path-independent integral used for characterizing
the severity of the loading at a crack tip. Considering linear elastic fracture mechanics (LEFM),
the value of the J-integral can be shown to be identical to the strain energy release rate (G), and
also to be related to K. Plasticity was not considered in the calculations. The solution for K at the
crack tip resulted in a value of 30.3 MPam (as shown in Figure 4).

 Figure 4. Schematic from COMSOLTM Multiphysics modeling software of Kmax for elongated CT specimen.

For DCPD measurements, a current of 3 A was applied to the sample (and reversed every 1 s) to
reduce potential thermoelectric effects. The measured potentials associated with the reversing
current were averaged to obtain a single data point for each measurement pair. 10 current
reversals were then averaged together to yield a potential utilized in determining crack
length. The CGR was calculated from the DCPD-measured crack length vs. time trace using the
polynomial (n = 7) method described in ASTM E647[10].

Sample crack extension was recorded over time for a significant time-period to confirm growth
(roughly 1.5 h of active crack growth). Post test, the sample and salt solution were allowed to
cool (salts solidified). The load frame cell was taken apart and the CT sample removed from the
solid salts and further rinsed with DI water to remove any remaining salts. Once cleaned, the
sample was imaged optically (on a Keyence digital microscope) to record the crack extension
along the side grooves on the CT sample as well as any potential corrosion damage. The sample
was then reloaded into the load frame and fatigued apart in air at a maximum load of 1500 lbs.,
an R-ratio of 0.1, and a frequency of 1 Hz for at least 10 mm (1 cm) of crack extension. After the
fatigue cycle, the sample was subjected to ductile fracture and torn apart to expose the crack tip
for both optical and Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) imaging. All optical imaging was
carried out on a Keyence VHX-5000 Digital Microscope. SEM imaging was carried out on a
Zeiss Supra 55VP SEM. The microscope parameters used were 20 kV accelerating voltage with
a 60 µm aperture and a working distance of 8.5 mm. The images were collected using Zeiss’
standard secondary and backscatter electron detectors.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION:
Prior to full immersion testing in the molten NaNO3/KNO3 salt solutions, the samples were pre-
cracked in air to produce an “atomically sharp” crack tip from which to initiate stress corrosion
cracking. The pre-cracking was extended somewhat longer than prior Sandia tests for SS304 [9]
to ensure that the crack tip would be fully submerged in the salt solution during testing and
maintain sufficient room on the sample above the molten salt solution for the DCPD wire
connections (as previously discussed in the experimental section and shown in Figure 2-b).
Therefore, the sample was pre-cracked to a total length of 25.4 mm (1 in) as compared to prior
Sandia testing where sample pre-cracks were only 0.5 mm long. It is noted that the pre-crack
itself was only 12.7 mm as the notch in the sample was already 12.7 mm long yielding a total
crack length of 25.4 mm. This extension of the pre-crack in air was measured with DCPD
(Figure 5) and later verified with optical imaging (Figure 9 and Figure 10).

Also, prior to full immersion in-situ CGR testing, it was important to both verify the modified
load frame setup and test the heat control within the system. As mentioned in the experimental
section of this document, and shown in Figure 6-a, the initial heater tests resulted in failures due

“hot-spots” that led to heat trace degradation as the cell was heated. The two-step ramp process
allowed for further heat equilibration, possibly reducing the influences of the variance across the
cell in heating and enabling higher temperatures (significant enough to melt the nitrate salts) to
be achieved as shown in Figure 6-b.

observations. Additionally, some cracking is observed on multiple planes, indicating some
potential crack branching/irregularities in the crack front.

This work presents the first known in-situ CGR data collected in a molten salt environment. For
an SS304L CT specimen, the CGR was determined by DCPD to be 6.5 x 106 mm/s under fatigue
loading with a Kmax of 30.3 MPam in a molten 60:40 NaNO3:KNO3 salt solution. This CGR
was validated through post-test optical and SEM fractography images which correlated both the
crack extension observed and the presence of fatigue bands indicating crack growth during
measurement. This data not only corroborates a novel measurement technique, but provides the
foundation for developing an understanding of materials behavior in extreme environments.

ANTICIPATED OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS:

The successful completion of this EELDRD engaged Sandia’s Missions through “empowering
our workforce” with both the leadership that was demonstrated by an early career staff member
and the training and knowledge gained by the technologist working on the project. Additionally,
this project “transformed our facilities and infrastructure” through the development and
successful application of the in-situ CGR measurement technique which will enable us to

“accelerate the evaluation and qualification of components and systems” as it provides
measurements of important materials properties and insight into potential degradation
mechanisms. As a result of this project, new collaborations within Sandia, between 1852 and
8925 have been established. The development of this capability positively impacts Sandia’s
mission of accelerating innovation to lead in modern engineering and allows Sandia to ensure
reliability of next-Gen reactors, seed funding in multiple application areas, and enable novel
materials’ development.

However, as with any fruitful scientific project, multiple lessons and suggestions for future work
were gained throughout this program. To further expand the use of this in-situ testing setup for
more streamlined and efficient materials’ behavior quantification, this would likely require
system/cell redesign and upgrades. A summary of the lessons learned and potential suggested
system improvements is listed here to provide insight for future development of this in-situ CGR
measurement technique in extreme environments.

Lessons Learned and Suggested Improvements/Mitigations:

 • Heat trace needs to be snug against the surface to prevent hot spots. The copper shim
 allowed for the heat trace to maintain uniform contact however still had an airgap. A
 potential solution to issues with heat trace would be to switch the heating element of the
 setup to a clam-shell type heater. Slower ramping profiles and/or step wise ramping that
 allow for thermal equilibration are also suggested to prevent burn out.
 • Sealing the cell is a challenge. It would be desirable in future iterations to redesign the
 corrosion cell and have a solid interface at the top and the bottom of the tube. This would
 allow for easier loading of the sample as well as mitigate any potential leakages from the
 molten salt. Additionally, cell fabrication from materials with higher strength properties
 at elevated temperature, i.e. Haynes 230 or Inconel 740, would also be desirable to
 achieve testing in relevant MSR conditions that often reach 700-800 oC.
 • The resistance of the sample significantly increases with the increase in temperature and
 influences the measurement of DCPD. A larger voltage range is needed to measure the
 crack extension and should be considered when testing at even higher temperatures.
 • The cover gas (in this case nitrogen) can potentially influence the overall temperature if
 the flow rate is significantly high. It is suggested to have a low flow rate in future testing.
 • The sample needs to have a fully characterized K profile to enable full K-control during
 testing in future iterations.

While there are many lessons learned and suggestions produced from this work, they provide a
stepping-stone to enhance and accelerate future proposals and work in this area.

Efforts and dissemination of work on this project have led to multiple areas of collaboration,
including submission of an LOI in August 2025 for an inter-laboratory with Lawrence Livermore
National Laboratory (LLNL) on “Predictive Control of Impurity-driven Molten Salt Corrosion
through Multiscale Modeling and Targeted Experiments” as well as continued interaction

the expansion and further use of this capability to more realistic MSR environments (for example

to accelerate their path to success.

CONCLUSION:
This research demonstrated the first successful known in-situ CGR measurements in molten salt
validated with fractography images. Fatigue crack growth CGR was measured in-situ through
direct current potential drop (DCPD) on a stainless-steel compact tension specimen in molten
NaNO3/KNO3 salt solution at 340 oC and was validated through post-test fractography. This not
only demonstrated that in-situ CGR measurement techniques can be successfully applied in
molten salt solutions, but also provides a technique that can measure properties necessary for the
knowledge of materials behavior in extreme environments. This has major implications for
materials development, safety, and degradation, specifically for next generation nuclear reactors.
Development of this in-situ measurement technique also buys down risk as it demonstrates that
in-situ CGR measurement is viable under extreme conditions but could be expanded to further
extreme environments (i.e. even higher T, SCO2, etc.).
