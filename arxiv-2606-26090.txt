Fast mixing of all-to-all quantum systems at high temperatures
Thiago Bergamaschi

Abstract
It is shown that arbitrary quantum k k -local Hamiltonians with bounded strength interactions admit a quantum Gibbs sampler [ CKG23 ] with a system-size independent spectral gap, at sufficiently high temperatures. This generalizes the existing quantum fast-mixing results beyond the geometrically-local setting. As a consequence, such systems admit fully-polynomial time quantum approximation algorithms for partition functions and global expectation values.



1
Introduction

A central theme in quantum many-body physics is that locality constrains dynamics.
In
geometrically local
systems (that is, lattices) such constraints are typically captured by Lieb-Robinson bounds
[
LR72
,
HK06
,
NS10
]
.
These bounds quantify the speed at which information percolates throughout the system, confining the time-evolution of local operators, to an effective
lightcone
around the operator.
Lieb-Robinson bounds have played a foundational role in our understanding of locality in quantum systems, underlying results on tensor network descriptions and area laws
[
Has06
,
HW05
,
Has07
,
AC21
]
, quantum and classical simulation algorithms
[
Osb06
,
HHKL23
,
WA23
,
MYLZ25
]
, and correlation-decay and stability properties
[
HK06
,
NS06
,
BHV06
,
BHM10
]
.

At the same time, many quantum systems of interest are not naturally described by lattice geometry. Examples include electronic-structure Hamiltonians
[
WLA13
,
OIWF21
]
, strongly correlated systems
[
SY93
,
Kit15
,
MS16
]
, mean-field models
[
Tót90
,
Bjö13
,
Bjö16
]
, expander-based qLDPC codes
[
TZ14
,
HHO21
,
PK22
,
DLV24
]
, all of which exhibit long-range or non-geometric interactions. Understanding how such
all-to-all systems
scramble quantum information under time-evolution can be significantly more challenging, since
the usual notion of a lightcone is no longer the correct measure of operator growth
[
LSH+13
,
Luc20
,
CL21
]
.

In this paper, we consider such families of all-to-all interacting Hamiltonians, and study the complexity of their thermal (or ‘Gibbs’) state-preparation algorithms. Following the modern quantum Gibbs sampling paradigm, we study their dissipative state preparation under Lindbladian evolution (or, a continuous-time quantum Markov chain)
[
RWW23
,
CKBG23
,
CKG23
,
DLL25
,
JI24
]
. Recently, there has been significant interest in proofs of efficient convergence, or
mixing times
for these algorithms, albeit, existing arguments for non-commuting systems are all limited to lattice Hamiltonians
[
RFA25
,
RSA26
,
BLMT25
,
BC26
,
ŠMBB26
,
TZ25
]
. In part, this is since the analysis in prior work fundamentally relies on Lieb-Robinson bounds.

The main result in this paper is a proof that arbitrary all-to-all
\mathsf{k}
-local Hamiltonians with bounded strength interactions (see
Section

1.1
) admit a Gibbs sampler
[
CKG23
]
with a system-size independent spectral gap at sufficiently high temperatures. Thus, such systems are
fast-mixing
, and consequently admit fully-polynomial time quantum approximation schemes for partition functions and expectation values.
Our argument is based on revisiting the recently introduced non-commutative versions of the Dobrushin conditions, sufficient conditions for rapid-mixing in quantum systems
[
BLMT25
,
MOZ98
,
TK15
,
RSA26
]
, under the quantum cluster expansion of Netočný
and Redig
[
NR04
,
MH21
]
. In the process, we hope that our techniques shed some light on the challenges in studying mixing times in all-to-all quantum systems, and serve as a stepping stone towards more complex disordered systems; see
Section

1.2
for further discussion.

We consider generic

Definition 1.1

(All-to-all Hamiltonians)

Let

The

u\in[n]:\quad\big|\{v:\exists e\in\Gamma\text{ s.t. }\{u,v\}\subseteq e\}\big|\leq\mathsf{d}

(1)

The

u\neq v:\sum_{e\supseteq\{u,v\}}\|\mathbf{h}_{e}\|\leq\mathsf{J}

We focus on the regime where

Remark 1.2

Our goal is to study the rate of (open-system) thermalization

\boldsymbol{\rho}:=\frac{e^{-\beta\mathbf{H}}}{\tr[e^{-\beta\mathbf{H}}]}.

(2)

1.2
Discussion and related work

Cluster expansions.
As we discuss shortly, the starting point behind
Theorem

1.3
is a cluster expansion for the short (complex) time evolution of all-to-all Hamiltonians. While Lieb-Robinson bounds for lattice Hamiltonians quantify the locality of the time-evolution of local observables as a function of
distance
on the lattice
[
LR72
,
HK06
,
NS10
,
ALY23
]
, such cluster expansions will express the time evolution as linear combinations of connected operators
[
LSH+13
,
Luc20
,
CL21
]
.

Cluster expansions have a rich history in classical and quantum many-body physics, including e.g. establishing uniqueness and analyticity of the Gibbs measure
[
DS85
,
DS85a
,
KP86
,
HMS20
]
, correlation decay properties
[
Uel05
,
KGK+14
,
FU15
]
,
algorithms for partition functions and local expectation values at high temperatures
[
MH21
,
YL23
,
WA23
,
MM24
,
CRC+25
]
and low temperatures
[
HPR19
,
CGG+21
,
Ber23
,
HM23
]
, concentration inequalities
[
NR04
,
KS20
,
CBG+26
]
. The specific quantum cluster expansion we use is based on the approach in
[
NR04
]
and its algorithmic version in
[
MH21
]
; which in turn is based on the seminal classical Kotecký and Preiss condition
[
KP86
]
.

Discussion on classical simulation.

Perhaps the most direct applications of our techniques would be to design new or improve existing classical simulation algorithms for all-to-all systems. Although we do not attempt this effort here for conciseness, we believe the complex-time operator growth bounds of
Section

3
could be leveraged for several different styles of classical algorithms:

1.

To prove
separability
(unentanglement) of such systems at high temperatures, under the “quasi-local perturbations of identity” approach of
[
BLMT24
]
(see also
[
Sca26
,
BCP26
]
).

2.

To prove a zero-free region in
\beta
for the complex-valued partition function, in turn implying classical quasi-polynomial time algorithms under Barvinok’s method
[
Bar16
,
Bar14
,
Bar14a
,
HMS20
]
. See also
[
ZK26
,
Zlo26
]
for a recent application of this method to the Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev (SYK) model
[
SY93
,
Kit15
,
MS16
]
, similarly based on Kotecký and Preiss criteria.

3.

The spectral gap for
\mathcal{L}
implies, following the quasi-adiabatic framework
[
CKG23
,
BC26
]
, that the purified Gibbs state can be prepared by short-time evolution of a time-dependent quasi-local all-to-all Hamiltonian. It is conceivable that real-time L.R. bounds for such systems also give quasi-polynomial time simulation algorithms e.g.
[
ALY23
, Section 4]
,
[
MYLZ25
]
.

Quantum mixing times.

There is a longstanding literature on the design and analysis of quantum Markov chains, largely focused on lattice Hamiltonians.
[
KB16
,
BCL+21
,
BCG+24
,
CRF21
,
KACR25
,
CGKR25
]
studied commuting lattice systems, and proved such systems are fast (and rapid) mixing by relying on certain decay-of-correlation assumptions.
[
TK15
,
Tem17
,
DLL+24
]
studied bounded degree stabilizer models, and proved rapid/fast-mixing by relying on the absence of “free energy barriers” in such systems. A variety of special cases can also be analyzed under certain quantum-to-classical mappings
[
HJ25
,
BCL24
,
BGL25
,
PSS+25
,
BGS+25
]
.

Proofs of mixing times in non-commuting (lattice) systems began with the work of
[
RFA25
]
, who proved a spectral gap for the
[
CKG23
]
Lindbladian at high temperatures by relying on techniques from the study of gapped ground states (namely, the stability of the gap
[
MZ13
]
). This was later strengthened by
[
RSA26
,
BLMT25
]
to rapid-mixing on lattices by means of quantum analogues of the Dobrushin condition, see also
[
TZ25
,
ŠMBB25
,
ŠMBB26
,
BT26
]
for applications to related systems. This framework, based on the decay of the oscillator norm (and its dual), was largely introduced in
[
MZ95
,
MZ96
,
MOZ98
]
as a quantum analog of the results of Aizenman and Holley
[
AH87
]
. We note that classically, these Dobrushin-style conditions
[
DS85
,
DS87
]
suffice to establish rapid-mixing for all-to-all classical systems at high-temperatures, and it is this gap that we attempt to close in this work. However, as mentioned, we fall short of proving
rapid
mixing.

Recently
[
BC26
]
studied one-dimensional quantum systems, and proved such systems are fast-mixing at all temperatures. At the heart of their analysis was the introduction of a “pseudo-Lindbladian” generator
\mathcal{K}
, which admits locality properties absent in the generator
\mathcal{L}

[
CKG23
]
, at the cost of not generating a CPTP map (indeed, this will later be the reason why our results are limited to fast-mixing). In this paper, we revisit this pseudo-Lindbladian in the context of all-to-all systems, see
Section

1.3
.

Outlook on disordered systems.

As mentioned, the most interesting future direction lies in the study of mixing times for random quantum Hamiltonians, in particular quantum spin glasses
[
BM80
]
and the Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev Hamiltonian
[
SY93
,
Kit15
,
MS16
]
. The results in this paper, much like the Dobrushin condition
[
DS85
,
DS87
]
applied to classical spin glasses, are far too weak to address such systems. Indeed, in the classical Markov chain literature, proofs of mixing times for spin glasses came about over
20
years after their counterparts for lattice systems; developed through a prominent framework known as
spectral independence

[
ALG24
,
AJK+21
,
AJK+22
,
EKZ21
]
and variants.

We hope that the techniques developed here for deterministic systems can nevertheless serve as a stepping stone, shedding some light on the relevant challenges in all-to-all quantum systems, as well as their connections to operator growth theory and classical simulation algorithms.

1.3.1
A central challenge

Central to existing proofs of fast or rapid mixing for the
[
CKG23
]
Lindbladian dynamics
[

,
RSA26
,
ŠMBB26
,
ŠMBB25
,

is to establish basic quasi-locality properties for the operator Fourier transform of a local operator

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{A}}(\omega):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{\sigma}(t)\cdot e^{-i\omega t}\cdot e^{i\mathbf{H}t}\mathbf{A}e^{-i\mathbf{H}t}\mathrm{d}t

(4)

Question 1.5

Can one establish such a “shell decomposition” for

; here, even allowing the Gaussian filter width

1.3.2
Quasi-locality of complex-time evolution.

In

Lemma 1.6

(Cluster expansion for
\mathbf{A}(z)
, informal)
.

The complex-time evolution of a local operator

e^{iz\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{A}e^{-iz\mathbf{H}}-\mathbf{A}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathsf{F}\subseteq\Gamma\\
\mathsf{F}\textsf{ connected to }j\end{subarray}}\mathbf{A}_{\mathsf{F}}(z),\quad\|\mathbf{A}_{\mathsf{F}}(z)\|\leq\prod_{e\in\mathsf{F}}\big(e^{2|z|\|\mathbf{h}_{e}\|}-1\big),

(5)

which, roughly speaking, expresses the contribution to
\mathbf{A}(z)
associated to a connected cluster of interactions

\mathsf{F}
.

In some intuitive sense, (

. To make this quantitative, we introduce two quasi-locality parameters which later play a central role in our mixing-time analysis.

Definition 1.7
.

We refer to the

\mathsf{conv}_{\beta}

\mathsf{conv}_{\beta}:=\max_{a\in\mathcal{S}_{[n]}^{1}}\sup_{|z|\leq\beta}\|\mathbf{A}^{a}(z)-\mathbf{A}^{a}\|

(6)

We remark that the reason this constant will play such a central role in the latter stages of this paper, is due to liberal use of KMS Hölder’s inequality, see

Definition 1.8
.

We refer to the

\mathsf{corr}_{\beta}(i,j):=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathcal{S}_{i}\\

Intuitively,

1.3.3
Dobrushin conditions for the pseudo-Lindbladian
\mathcal{K}

[
BC26
]
.

The convergence of complex-time evolution enables us to turn to the “pseudo-Lindbladian” of
[
BC26
]
, who designed a superoperator
\mathcal{K}=\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathcal{K}_{i}
resembling the
[
CKG23
]
Lindbladian, but with significantly sharper quasi-locality properties instrumental in proving spectral gaps.

Definition 1.10
.

For every single-site Pauli operator
\mathbf{A}^{a}\in\mathcal{S}_{[n]}^{1}
, we introduce the superoperator:

\mathcal{K}_{a}[\mathbf{X}]:=[\mathbf{A}^{a},\mathbf{X}]\,\bigl(\boldsymbol{\rho}^{1/2}\mathbf{A}^{a\dagger}\boldsymbol{\rho}^{-1/2}\bigr)\;-\;\bigl(\boldsymbol{\rho}^{-1/2}\mathbf{A}^{a\dagger}\boldsymbol{\rho}^{1/2}\bigr)[\mathbf{A}^{a},\mathbf{X}],

(8)

and further let
\mathcal{K}_{j}:=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{S}_{j}^{1}}\mathcal{K}_{a}
be the generator defined by all single-site Pauli jumps on
j\in[n]
.

This generator satisfies a series of nice properties:

Lemma 1.11

(Properties of
\mathcal{K}

[
BC26
, Lemma III.5]
)
.

Each
\mathcal{K}_{j}
is
\boldsymbol{\rho}
-detailed-balanced and is negative semi-definite. Furthermore, the kernel of
\mathcal{K}_{j}
is exactly the set of locally trivial operators:

\mathcal{K}_{j}[\mathbf{X}]=0\iff\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{X}_{[n]\setminus j}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{j}

(9)

We emphasize that this explicit, local characterization of the kernel of the single-site generators
\mathcal{K}_{i}
(
9
), is not true for the
[
CKG23
]
generator
\mathcal{L}_{i}
. We refer to this superoperator as a
pseudo-Lindbladian
as it admits non-positive spectra but does not generate a CP map, and thus doesn’t define a physical channel one could implement on a quantum computer. Nevertheless, shortly we show why a spectral gap for
\mathcal{K}
implies a spectral gap for
\mathcal{L}
(
Section

1.3.4

Dobrushin conditions for
\mathcal{K}
.
We now establish a set of sufficient conditions for
\mathcal{K}
to admit a spectral gap. Akin to the previous Dobrushin-style conditions in the quantum Gibbs sampling literature
[
RSA26
,
BLMT25
,
MOZ98
]
, these conditions intuitively capture two locality properties of the local generators
\mathcal{K}
: locality in
temperature
, and locality in
space
.

Locality in temperature.
We assume that for each site
j\in[n]
the single-site generator
\mathcal{K}_{j}^{(\beta)}:=\mathcal{K}_{j}
can be approximated by that at infinite-temperature (the depolarizing semigroup):

\forall j\in[n]:\quad\|\mathcal{K}_{j}^{(\beta)}-\mathcal{K}_{j}^{(0)}\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\leq\eta_{j}.

(10)

Pairwise influence.
We assume that for each site
j\in[n]
, the generator
\mathcal{K}_{j}
approximately commutes with the depolarizing semigroup on another site
\ell\neq j
:

\forall j\neq\ell:\quad\|[\mathcal{K}^{(0)}_{\ell},\mathcal{K}_{j}^{(\beta)}]\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\leq\kappa_{j\ell}.

(11)

Remark 1.12
.

The intuition behind (
11
) is that at high-temperatures, a single update
\mathcal{K}_{j}
on
j\in[n]
cannot simultaneously influence all the other
\ell\neq j;
resembling a mean-field statement.

(and scale roughly linearly in

(A Dobrushin condition for

.

There exists a universal constant

),(

) hold for a choice of constants

, as well as the convergence of complex-time evolution (

Then, if said constants satisfy the inequality:

(12)

then
\mathcal{K}
admits a system-size independent spectral gap:

.

1.

1.3.4
Dirichlet form comparison arguments

Now equipped with the Poincaré inequality for
\mathcal{K}
, it remains to argue why this implies a Poincaré inequality for
\mathcal{L}
. In the recent work of
[
BC26
]
for one-dimensional systems, such a comparison was done by relating their Dirichlet forms:

\forall\mathbf{X}:\quad\langle\mathbf{X},-\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}[\mathbf{X}]\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\geq\eta_{\beta}\cdot\langle\mathbf{X},-\mathcal{K}[\mathbf{X}]\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}

(14)

up to a temperature dependent constant
\eta_{\beta}
. Although this is broadly the strategy we employ in this paper as well, their argument made liberal use of the unconditional convergence of complex time-evolution and sharp bounds on the convergence of real-time evolution in 1D; which we currently do not know how to implement in all-to-all systems (
Section

1.3.1
).

Remark 1.15
.

In particular, their comparison argument relied on a shell decomposition for the operator Fourier transform
\hat{\mathbf{A}}(\omega)
which decays both in space and in
\omega
, see
[
BC26
, Lemma VI.2]
.

Arguably the most technical part of our work is a proof of a Dirichlet form comparison akin to (
14
) at sufficiently high temperatures, see
Section

5
. Very roughly speaking, the proof strategy is to understand
\mathcal{L}
as a Markov process with updates given by the operator Fourier transform
\hat{\mathbf{A}}(\omega,t)
, and to sequentially compare Dirichlet forms with the analogous Markov processes with updates
\mathbf{A}(t)
and
\mathbf{A}
respectively (i.e.
\omega,t
have been “peeled off”).

The key step behind (
14
).
To give concrete intuition, we highlight a key step behind how we relate the Dirichlet forms of generators with jump operators
\mathbf{A}
(single-site operators) and
\mathbf{A}(t)
(time-evolved single-site operators). We begin with Duhamel’s formula:

\mathbf{A}(t)-\mathbf{A}=i\int_{0}^{t}e^{i\mathbf{H}s}[\mathbf{H},\mathbf{A}]e^{-i\mathbf{H}s}\mathrm{d}s.

(15)

By the reverse triangle inequality, for any operator
\mathbf{X}
, we can then (very roughly speaking) understand the norms of commutators of
\mathbf{A}(t)
with
\mathbf{X}
, as a function of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{X}
, up to an error term which depends on commutators of 2-site operators:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\|[\mathbf{A}(t),\mathbf{X}]\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}_{\textsf{``Dirichlet form'' of }\mathcal{L}_{a}}\geq\underbrace{\|[\mathbf{A},\mathbf{X}]\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}_{\textsf{``Dirichlet form'' of }\mathcal{K}_{a}}-\int_{0}^{t}\underbrace{\bigg\|\big[[\mathbf{H},\mathbf{A}](s),\mathbf{X}\big]\bigg\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textsf{``Dirichlet form'' of }\mathcal{L}\\
\textsf{under 2-site updates}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{d}s

(16)

Indeed,
[\mathbf{H},\mathbf{A}]
is a linear combination of
2
-local Paulis, and thus the second term on the RHS above can be interpreted as the Dirichlet form of a generator with 2-site updates (as opposed to single-site).

Remark 1.16
.

We use the quotation marks as the Dirichlet form of such generators are technically integrals over the
squared
commutator-norm; here we simply mean to convey the intuition.

The key idea, stemming from recent work
[
CR25
,
CAN25
,
BC26
,
BCV25
]
, is to perform a
path comparison argument
akin to those in the classical literature
[
DS93
]
: any Markov chain with pairwise updates should be simulable by a Markov chain with single-site updates (up to a
\beta
-dependent constant loss). Here we implement an analogous strategy by relying on the convergence of complex-time evolution (
Section

1.3.2
) at high-temperatures, to relate their Dirichlet forms.

To fully prove (
14
) we need to similarly argue how to “peel off”
\omega
in order to relate
\hat{\mathbf{A}}(\omega,t)
and
\mathbf{A}(t)
. This step is technical but ultimately based on the same intuition above. We refer the reader to
Section

5
for further discussion.

The KMS inner product and detailed-balance

We dedicate this section to the relevant non-commutative weighted inner product. We assume

Definition 2.1

(KMS inner product)

Given a full-rank state

Associated to a weighted inner product, we can define a notion of detailed-balance:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{\rho}^{1/4}\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\rho}^{-1/4}\|\cdot\|\mathbf{Y}\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}

Of course, the efficacy of the above is contingent on the convergence of complex-time evolution of
\mathbf{X}
. We also consider the following induced norm on superoperators:

Definition 2.4

For any superoperator

\displaystyle\norm{\CN}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}:=\sup_{\mathbf{O}}\frac{\norm{\CN[\vO]}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}{\norm{\vO}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}.

3.1
Cluster expansions for the complex-time evolution

To state the cluster expansion, we require some basic graph-theoretic definitions. First, the local Hamiltonian
\mathbf{H}
naturally induces a graph on the Hamiltonian terms.

Definition 3.1

(Interaction Graph)
.

We refer the
interaction graph

G_{\mathsf{I}}
of
\mathbf{H}
the graph whose vertex-set is
\Gamma
, and two terms
e_{1},e_{2}\in\Gamma
are connected in
G_{\mathsf{I}}
if
\mathsf{supp}(e_{1})\cap\mathsf{supp}(e_{2})\neq\emptyset
.

The cluster expansion will group terms by connectivity. We refer to a connected cluster in the interaction graph
G_{\mathsf{I}}
as a set of edges / Hamiltonian terms (without multiplicities) which is strongly connected in
G_{\mathsf{I}}
(meaning, no isolated connected components).

Definition 3.2

(Connected Clusters)
.

A subset of the edges
\mathsf{F}\subseteq\Gamma
is said to be a
connected cluster

\mathsf{F}\in\mathsf{CC}
if
\mathsf{F}
is strongly-connected in
G_{\mathsf{I}}
.

Remark 3.3
.

In an abuse of notation, we say a site
i\in[n]
is in
\mathsf{F}\subseteq\Gamma
if
\exists e\in\mathsf{F}
s.t.
i\in\mathsf{supp}(\mathbf{h}_{e})
.

We are now in a position to state the cluster expansion for the complex-time evolution. The below formulation is largely inspired by shell or annulus decompositions from the literature of Lieb-Robinson bounds in lattice Hamiltonians.

Lemma 3.4

(Cluster expansions for the complex-time evolution)
.

Fix
z\in\mathbb{C}
, and let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{S}_{i}
be a single-site Pauli operator supported on site
i\in[n]
. The complex time evolution
\mathbf{A}(z):=e^{iz\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{A}e^{-iz\mathbf{H}}
of
\mathbf{A}
admits the following cluster expansion:

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(z):=\mathbf{A}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathsf{F}\in\mathsf{CC}\\
i\in\mathsf{F}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{A}_{\mathsf{F}}(z),\quad\text{where}\quad\|\mathbf{A}_{\mathsf{F}}(z)\|\leq\prod_{e\in\mathsf{F}}\big(e^{2|z|\|\mathbf{h}_{e}\|}-1\big)

(32)

where
\mathsf{supp}(\mathbf{A}_{\mathsf{F}})=\mathsf{supp}(\mathsf{F})\subseteq[n]
.

Namely,
\mathbf{A}(z)
is decomposed into a linear combination of operators supported only on the connected clusters; in such a manner that the strength / norm of the contribution of each cluster
\mathsf{F}
roughly decays with the product of interaction strengths within
\mathsf{F}
.

3.1.1
Convergence of complex-time evolution and correlations

Before we present the proof of
Section

3.1
, we present two simple corollaries. These corollaries express bounds on the convergence of complex-time evolution, as a function of properties of the underlying induced graph
G_{\mathsf{I}}
and its weights. We later leverage these definitions directly in our proof of the Dobrushin condition. These definitions are restatements of those presented in our technical overview in
Section

Definition 3.5
.

We refer to the
complex-time evolution constant

\mathsf{conv}_{\beta}
as the maximum deviation of any single-site Pauli operator from itself under complex-time evolution:

\mathsf{conv}_{\beta}:=\max_{a\in\mathcal{S}_{[n]}^{1}}\sup_{|z|\leq\beta}\|\mathbf{A}^{a}(z)-\mathbf{A}\|

(33)

A bound on this constant is immediately offered by applying the triangle inequality to

3.1

Corollary 3.6

(Convergence of complex-time evolution)
.

In the context of
Section

3.1

\displaystyle\mathsf{conv}_{\beta}\leq\max_{i\in[n]}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathsf{F}\in\mathsf{CC}\\
i\in\mathsf{F}\end{subarray}}\prod_{e\in\mathsf{F}}\big(e^{2|z|\|\mathbf{h}_{e}\|}-1\big).

(34)

We will shortly discuss conditions under which this sum converges. Next, we introduce a measure of pairwise correlations under complex-time evolution.

Definition 3.7
.

For any pair of sites
i\neq j\in[n]
, we refer to the

\mathsf{corr}_{\beta}(i,j)
as the maximum commutator-norm of any pair of single-site Pauli operators under complex-time evolution:

\mathsf{corr}_{\beta}(i,j):=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathcal{S}_{i}\\

(35)

Observe the above is
0
when

and depends only on
\mathsf{Im}(z)

Corollary 3.8

(Convergence of total pairwise correlations)
.

In the context of
Section

3.1

\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq i}\mathsf{corr}_{\beta}(i,j)\leq 2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathsf{F}\in\mathsf{CC}\\
i\in\mathsf{F}\end{subarray}}|\mathsf{supp}(\mathsf{F})|\cdot\prod_{e\in\mathsf{F}}\big(e^{4|\beta|\|\mathbf{h}_{e}\|}-1\big)

(36)

with
|\mathsf{supp}(\mathsf{F})|
the number of distinct sites incident on
\mathsf{F}
.

Proof.

We restrict our attention to
z=\mathsf{Im}(z)
and a pair of Pauli operators
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
on sites
i,j
respectively. Let us shorthand
w_{e}:=e^{2|z|\|\mathbf{h}_{e}\|}-1
for simplicity. The cluster expansion in

3.1
then guarantees:

\displaystyle\|[\mathbf{A}(z)^{\dagger},\mathbf{B}(z)]\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathsf{T}\in\mathsf{CC}\\
j\in\mathsf{T}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathsf{F}\in\mathsf{CC}\\
i\in\mathsf{F}\end{subarray}}\|\mathbf{A}_{\mathsf{F}}(z)^{\dagger}\|\cdot\|\mathbf{B}_{\mathsf{T}}(z)\|\cdot\mathbb{I}[\mathsf{F}\cap\mathsf{T}\neq\emptyset]

(37)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathsf{F}\in\mathsf{CC}\\
i,j\in\mathsf{F}\end{subarray}}\prod_{e\in\mathsf{F}}(2w_{e}+w_{e}^{2})=2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathsf{F}\in\mathsf{CC}\\
i,j\in\mathsf{F}\end{subarray}}\prod_{e\in\mathsf{F}}\big(e^{4|z|\|\mathbf{h}_{e}\|}-1\big)

(38)

where we note that the clusters
\mathsf{F},\mathsf{T}
from
\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B}

or in both. Finally, we sum over all

\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq i}\mathsf{corr}_{\beta}(i,j)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathsf{F}\in\mathsf{CC}\\
i,j\in\mathsf{F}\end{subarray}}\prod_{e\in\mathsf{F}}\big(e^{4|\beta|\|\mathbf{h}_{e}\|}-1\big)

(39)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathsf{F}\in\mathsf{CC}\\
i\in\mathsf{F}\end{subarray}}|\mathsf{supp}(\mathsf{F})|\cdot\prod_{e\in\mathsf{F}}\big(e^{4|\beta|\|\mathbf{h}_{e}\|}-1\big)

(40)

where in the last line we observe that each
j\in|\mathsf{supp}(\mathsf{F})|
contributes the same factor to the sum.

\mathsf{F}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{\mathsf{F}}(z):=\sum_{m}\frac{(iz)^{m}}{m!}\sum_{\mathsf{U}(\mathbf{e})=\mathsf{F}}\prod_{i}^{m}\mathsf{adj}_{e_{i}}[\mathbf{A}]

(42)

we note that if
\mathsf{F}\notin\mathsf{CC}
is not connected, then
\mathbf{A}_{\mathsf{F}}(z)=0
. Otherwise, following
[
NR04
, Lemma 6.10, Lemma 6.21]
, we can control the norm of
\mathbf{A}_{\mathsf{F}}(z)
by counting the number of such ordered sequences
\mathbf{e}
satisfying
\mathsf{U}(\mathbf{e})=\mathsf{F}
, by enumerating over the multiplicity
m_{e}\geq 1
of each edge
e\in\mathsf{F}
:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{A}_{\mathsf{F}}(z)\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathsf{U}(\mathbf{e})=\mathsf{F}}\frac{|2z|^{|\mathbf{e}|}}{|\mathbf{e}|!}\prod_{i}^{|\mathbf{e}|}\|\mathbf{h}_{e_{i}}\|

(43)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\{m_{e}\geq 1:e\in\mathsf{F}\}}\frac{|2z|^{\sum_{e\in\mathsf{F}}m_{e}}}{(\sum_{e\in\mathsf{F}}m_{e})!}\bigg(\prod_{e\in\mathsf{F}}\|\mathbf{h}_{e}\|^{m_{e}}\bigg)\cdot\bigg(\begin{aligned} &\quad\text{\# sequences of length $\sum_{e}m_{e}$}\\
&\quad\text{with multiplicities $\{m_{e}\}_{e\in\mathsf{F}}$}\end{aligned}\quad\bigg)

(44)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\{m_{e}\geq 1:e\in\mathsf{F}\}}\frac{|2z|^{\sum_{e\in\mathsf{F}}m_{e}}}{(\sum_{e\in\mathsf{F}}m_{e})!}\bigg(\prod_{e\in\mathsf{F}}\|\mathbf{h}_{e}\|^{m_{e}}\bigg)\cdot\frac{(\sum_{e\in\mathsf{F}}m_{e})!}{\prod_{e\in\mathsf{F}}m_{e}!}

(45)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{e\in\mathsf{F}}\sum_{m_{e}\geq 1}\frac{|2z\|\mathbf{h}_{e}\||^{m_{e}}}{m_{e}!}

(46)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{e\in\mathsf{F}}\big(e^{2|z|\|\mathbf{h}_{e}\|}-1\big).

(47)

In the first line, we simply applied the norm bound
\|\mathsf{adj}_{e}\|_{\infty\rightarrow\infty}\leq 2\|\mathbf{h}_{e}\|
. In the second, we used
Section

3.1.2
to count the number of ordered sequences of total length
\sum_{e\in\mathsf{F}}m_{e}
with multiplicities
\{m_{e}\}_{e\in\mathsf{F}}
. At this point the summation factorizes over the various edges.

∎

We make use of the following trivial combinatorial counting fact.

Fact 3.9

(Multinomial coefficients)
.

The number of ways to partition a set of
n
distinct elements into
k
subsets where the number of elements per subset is
n_{1},n_{2}\cdots n_{k}
is

\frac{n!}{\prod_{i}^{k}n_{i}!}.

(48)

3.2
Sufficient conditions for the convergence of the cluster expansion

In this section, we consider the special case of local Hamiltonians as defined in

1.1
. We remind the reader:

1.

The
locality

\mathsf{k}
of
\mathbf{H}
is the maximum support size
|\mathsf{supp}(\mathbf{h}_{e})|\leq\mathsf{k}.

2.

The

\mathsf{d}
is the number of qudits any other qudit interacts with

u\in[n]:\quad\big|\{v:\exists e\in\Gamma\text{ s.t. }\{u,v\}\subseteq e\}\big|\leq\mathsf{d}

3.

The

u\neq v:\sum_{e\supseteq\{u,v\}}\|\mathbf{h}_{e}\|\leq\mathsf{J}

(50)

Lemma 3.10
.

In the context of
Section

3.1
, let
\mathbf{H}
be any
\mathsf{k}
-local Hamiltonian satisfying
Section

1.1
. Then, there exists a universal constant
\mathsf{c}
s.t.
\forall|z|<z_{\mathsf{c}}:=\mathsf{c}\cdot[k\mathsf{d}\mathsf{J}]^{-1}
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathsf{F}\in\mathsf{CC}\\
i\in\mathsf{F}\end{subarray}}|\mathsf{F}|\cdot\prod_{e\in\mathsf{F}}\big(e^{4|z|\|\mathbf{h}_{e}\|}-1\big)\leq 100\cdot\mathsf{k}\cdot\frac{|z|}{z_{\mathsf{c}}}

Remark 3.11
.

We note that this entails bounds on

via

We note there is a long history of sufficient conditions for convergence of cluster expansions. Here we use an basic tree counting argument
[

]
; more sophisticated recursive arguments are possible à la Kotecky-Preiss
[

Proof.

Observe
|\mathsf{supp}(\mathsf{F})|\leq\mathsf{k}\cdot|\mathsf{F}|
, and thus it suffices to evaluate the sum conditioned on
|\mathsf{F}|=m:

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathsf{F}\in\mathsf{CC}\\
i\in\mathsf{F},|\mathsf{F}|=m\end{subarray}}\prod_{e\in\mathsf{F}}\big(e^{4|z|\|\mathbf{h}_{e}\|}-1\big),\quad\text{and let}\quad w_{e}=e^{4|z|\|\mathbf{h}_{e}\|}-1.

(52)

For this purpose, we overcount the sets
\mathsf{F}\in\mathsf{CC}
such that
i\in\mathsf{F}
and
|\mathsf{F}|=m
using a tree counting argument.

Let
\mathbb{T}
be the set of unlabeled trees on
m+1
vertices. For each
T\in\mathbb{T}

v\in[m]
by a Hamiltonian term
\sigma_{T}:[m]\rightarrow\Gamma
and (in abuse of notation) label the root by the site

\sigma_{T}
as

\mathsf{supp}(\sigma_{T}(u))\cap\mathsf{supp}(\sigma_{T}(v))\neq\emptyset.
Then:

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathsf{F}\in\mathsf{CC}\\
i\in\mathsf{F},|\mathsf{F}|=m\end{subarray}}\prod_{e\in\mathsf{F}}w_{e}\leq\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}}\sum_{\textsf{valid }\sigma_{T}}\prod_{i}^{m}w_{\sigma_{T}(i)}

(53)

Let us now focus attention to a single
T\in\mathbb{T}
. Suppose WLOG the
m
th vertex is a leaf. Suppose we condition on a partial assignment
\sigma_{T\setminus m}
, and let us denote as
e_{p}\in\Gamma
the assignment to the parent of this leaf. Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{\textsf{valid }\sigma_{T}}\prod_{i}^{m}w_{\sigma_{T}(i)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\textsf{valid }\sigma_{T\setminus m}}\prod_{i}^{m-1}w_{\sigma_{T}(i)}\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma_{m}\in\Gamma\\
\text{s.t. }\sigma_{T}\textsf{ valid}\end{subarray}}w_{\sigma_{m}}

(54)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\textsf{valid }\sigma_{T\setminus m}}\prod_{i}^{m-1}w_{\sigma_{T}(i)}\cdot\sum_{e:e\cap e_{p}\neq\emptyset}w_{e}\leq\Delta\cdot\sum_{\textsf{valid }\sigma_{T\setminus m}}\prod_{i}^{m-1}w_{\sigma_{T}(i)}\leq\Delta^{m}

(55)

where we overcount the valid assignments to the leaf by simply summing over all edges adjacent to
e_{p}
. In the above, we defined the parameter
\Delta\leq\mathsf{k}\mathsf{d}\big(e^{4|z|\mathsf{J}}-1\big)
(
Section

3.2.1

3.2.1
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathsf{F}\in\mathsf{CC}\\
i\in\mathsf{F}\end{subarray}}|\mathsf{F}|\cdot\prod_{e\in\mathsf{F}}\big(e^{4|z|\|\mathbf{h}_{e}\|}-1\big)\leq 4\mathsf{k}\cdot\sum_{m}m\cdot(4\Delta)^{m}\leq\frac{16\mathsf{k}\Delta}{(1-4\Delta)^{2}}.

(56)

To adequate to the lemma statement, we simply pick a suitable constant
\mathsf{c}
such that
\mathsf{k}\mathsf{d}(\exp[4|z|\mathsf{J}]-1)\leq 8\mathsf{k}\mathsf{d}|z|\mathsf{J}=8|z|/(\mathsf{c}z_{\mathsf{c}})
.

∎

4
Dobrushin conditions for the pseudo-Lindbladian

The “Dobrushin condition” and related “Dobrushin-Shlosman conditions” were developed as criteria on classical Gibbs measures to ensure a property known as
uniqueness
of the measure in the thermodynamic limit
[
DS85
,
DS85a
,
DS87
]
. A prolific subsequent line of work studied the implications of these conditions to rapid-mixing of spin systems
[
HS87
,
HS89
,
AH87
,
SZ92
,
SZ92a
]
.

Recently,
[
RSA26
,
BLMT25
]
studied extensions of these conditions to non-commuting quantum spin systems, in order to prove rapid-mixing of such systems at high temperatures. Following their techniques, here we similarly develop sufficient conditions for the “pseudo-Lindbladian”
\mathcal{K}
of (
8
) to admit a system-size independent spectral gap. We shall see that the structure of
\mathcal{K}
1) significantly simplifies the presentation, due to its explicit locality; however, 2) at the cost of only obtaining fast-mixing (not to be confused with rapid-mixing), since
\mathcal{K}
does not generate a CP map.

We organize this section as follows.

1.

We begin in
Section

4.1
by introducing a modest variant of the oscillator norm, which we coin the KMS oscillator norm, and prove that its contraction entails a Poincaré inequality.

2.

In
Section

4.2
, we establish a Dobrushin condition for the generator
\mathcal{K}

[
BC26
]
which implies contraction of the KMS-oscillator-norm, and in turn, a spectral gap for
\mathcal{K}
.

3.

In
Section

4.3
we prove that said Dobrushin conditions reduce cleanly to properties of the complex-time evolution of local Pauli operators, previously derived in
Section

3
.

We then conclude this section with
Section

4.4
, where we put all these ingredients together to prove the main result of this section:

Theorem 4.1

(
\mathcal{K}
is gapped)
.

Let
\mathbf{H}
be a
\mathsf{k}
-local Hamiltonian of local dimension
2^{\mathsf{q}}
, degree
\mathsf{d}
, pairwise strength
\mathsf{J}
, as in
Section

1.1
. Then, there exist universal constants
\mathsf{c,\gamma}
such that for any
\beta<[\mathsf{c}\mathsf{k}^{2}\mathsf{J}\mathsf{d}]^{-1}
, the generator
\mathcal{K}
(
8
) defined by all single-site Pauli jumps satisfies
\mathsf{gap}(\mathcal{K})\geq\gamma
.

4.1
The KMS oscillator norm

We dedicate this subsection to a discussion on a modification to the oscillator norm, which we coin the KMS oscillator norm.

Definition 4.2

(KMS oscillator norm)
.

For any operator
\mathbf{X}
and full rank density matrix
\boldsymbol{\rho}
, we refer to
|\!|\!|\mathbf{X}|\!|\!|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
as the KMS-oscillator-norm of
\mathbf{X}
, defined by:

|\!|\!|\mathbf{X}|\!|\!|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}:=\sum_{i\in[n]}\|\delta_{i}[\mathbf{X}]\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\quad\text{with}\quad\delta_{i}[\mathbf{X}]:=\mathbf{X}-\frac{1}{2^{\mathsf{q}}}\tr_{i}[\mathbf{X}].

(62)

We note that
-\delta_{i}\propto\mathcal{K}_{i}^{0}
is proportional to the generator of the depolarizing channel. The following lemma proves that if any (pseudo) Lindbladian
\mathcal{F}
generates an exponential decay in KMS oscillator norm, then it must be gapped.

Lemma 4.3

(KMS-oscillator-norm decay implies a Poincaré inequality)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be
\boldsymbol{\rho}
-detailed-balanced for a full rank state
\boldsymbol{\rho}
. Then, if there exists
\alpha>0
such that

\forall\mathbf{X}:\quad|\!|\!|e^{t\mathcal{F}^{\dagger}}[\mathbf{X}]|\!|\!|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\leq|\!|\!|\mathbf{X}|\!|\!|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\cdot e^{-\alpha\cdot t}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\forall\mathbf{X}:\quad\frac{\langle\mathbf{X},-\mathcal{F}^{\dagger}[\mathbf{X}]\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}{\|\mathbf{X}-\tr[\boldsymbol{\rho}\mathbf{X}]\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{2}}\geq\alpha.

(63)

Proof.

Since
\boldsymbol{\rho}
is full rank and
\mathcal{F}
is detailed-balanced,
\mathcal{F}^{\dagger}
admits a KMS orthonormal set of eigenoperators
\{\mathbf{B}_{i}\}_{i}
:

\mathcal{F}^{\dagger}[\mathbf{B}_{i}]=-\lambda_{i}\mathbf{B}_{i},\quad i\neq j:\quad\langle\mathbf{B}_{i},\mathbf{B}_{j}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=0

(64)

where
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and without loss of generality,
\mathbf{B}_{0}=\mathbf{I}
,
\lambda_{0}=0
. Then, for each
i>0:

\displaystyle e^{-\lambda_{i}t}\cdot|\!|\!|\mathbf{B}_{i}|\!|\!|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=|\!|\!|e^{t\mathcal{F}}[\mathbf{B}_{i}]|\!|\!|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\leq|\!|\!|\mathbf{B}_{i}|\!|\!|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\cdot e^{-\alpha\cdot t}\Rightarrow\lambda_{i}\geq\alpha.

(65)

where the first inequality is the assumption of KMS oscillator norm decay, and the implication
(\Rightarrow)
follows since
|\!|\!|\mathbf{B}_{i}|\!|\!|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
is non-zero if
\mathbf{B}_{i}\neq\mathbf{I}
.
∎

4.2
Dobrushin conditions for
\mathcal{K}

We proceed by providing a set of sufficient conditions on the generator
\mathcal{K}
, to ensure the decay in the KMS oscillator norm. Our approach is based on
[
RSA26
,
MOZ98
]
, with minor modifications to address the fact that the generator
\mathcal{K}
is not completely positive. We remind the reader:

Observation 4.4
.

For the purposes of showing the decay in the oscillator norm, what makes
\mathcal{K}
special is the following identity regarding the kernel of a single local term
\mathcal{K}_{i}
:

\mathcal{K}_{i}[\mathbf{X}]=\mathcal{K}_{i}\circ\delta_{i}[\mathbf{X}],\quad\delta_{i}[\mathbf{X}]:=\mathbf{X}-\frac{1}{2^{\mathsf{q}}}\tr_{i}[\mathbf{X}]

(66)

Indeed,
\delta_{i}
has the effect of removing the identity term on qubit
i
, which is stationary under
\mathcal{K}_{i}
. This suggests that if
\mathbf{X}
is already “in thermal equilibrium” on
i
, then updates on
i
cannot further “spread disagreements” outside
i
. We emphasize this is categorically not true for the
[
CKG23
]
Lindbladian.

Assumption 4.5
.

We introduce the following assumptions on the locality of complex-time evolution and the pseudo-Lindbladian generator
\mathcal{K}=\sum_{i}\mathcal{K}_{i}
.

1.

Locality in temperature.
For each
i\in[n]
, there exist constants
\lambda,\eta_{i}\geq 0
s.t.:

\|\mathcal{K}_{i}+\lambda\cdot\delta_{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\leq\eta_{i}.

(67)

2.

Pairwise influence.
For each
i\neq j\in[n]
, there exists a constant
\kappa_{ij}\geq 0
such that:

\|[\delta_{j},\mathcal{K}_{i}]\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\leq\kappa_{ij}.

(68)

We will prove shortly that these constants
\eta,\kappa
exist, and scale linearly in
\beta
at sufficiently small
\beta
, for our systems of interest. A trivial calculation below shows
\lambda=2\cdot 4^{\mathsf{q}}.

Remark 4.6
.

(
67
) simply imposes that
\mathcal{K}_{i}
, as a function of
\beta
, is a perturbation of the
\beta=0
case. In turn, (
68
) resembles a mean-field approximation, in that “on average” updates on
i\in[n]
shouldn’t affect all the other sites
j\in[n].

We are now in a position to state the main result of this section.

Lemma 4.7

(A Dobrushin condition for
\mathcal{K}
)
.

Assume that the generator
\mathcal{K}
(
8
) satisfies
Section

4.2
, and furthermore:

\gamma:=\max_{i}\bigg[\|\delta_{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\cdot\eta_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\kappa_{ij}\bigg]<\lambda

(69)

Then for any
\mathbf{X}
, the KMS oscillator norm of
\mathbf{X}_{t}:=e^{t\mathcal{K}}[\mathbf{X}]
decays as
|\!|\!|\mathbf{X}_{t}|\!|\!|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\leq e^{-t(\lambda-\gamma)}\cdot|\!|\!|\mathbf{X}|\!|\!|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}.

A consequence of the discussion on the KMS oscillator norm (
Section

4.1
) from the previous section, is then that the Dobrushin condition above implies a spectral gap for
\mathcal{K}
.

Remark 4.8
.

In the above,
\|\delta_{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
is the induced superoperator
\boldsymbol{\rho}
norm of
\delta_{i}
. As we discuss shortly, the convergence of complex-time evolution will imply it is bounded by a constant.

Proof of
Section

4.2
.

For any fixed
i\in[n]
, the time-derivative of
\mathbf{X}_{t}
satisfies the expansion:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbf{X}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\mathcal{K}_{j}[\mathbf{X}_{t}]=-\lambda\delta_{i}[\mathbf{X}_{t}]+(\mathcal{K}_{i}+\lambda\delta_{i})[\mathbf{X}_{t}]+\sum_{j\neq i}\mathcal{K}_{j}[\mathbf{X}_{t}]

(70)

where we recall that
-\delta_{i}
is the generator for the depolarizing channel.
Following
[
RSA26
]
, we claim (and justify shortly) that
\delta_{i}[\mathbf{X}_{t}]
satisfies the bound:

\displaystyle\|\delta_{i}[\mathbf{X}_{t}]\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\leq e^{-\lambda t}\cdot\|\delta_{i}[\mathbf{X}]\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda(z-t)}\cdot\bigg(\|\delta_{i}\circ(\mathcal{K}_{i}+\lambda\delta_{i})[\mathbf{X}_{z}]\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}+\sum_{j\neq i}\|[\delta_{i},\mathcal{K}_{j}][\mathbf{X}_{z}]\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\bigg)\mathrm{d}z.

(71)

We can now apply the influence assumptions in the lemma statement. Assumption (
67
) and the kernel condition (
66
), applied to
\mathbf{Y}=\mathcal{P}_{z}[\mathbf{X}]
, implies a bound on the first term in the RHS above.

\displaystyle\|\delta_{i}\circ(\mathcal{K}_{i}+\lambda\delta_{i})[\mathbf{Y}]\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\delta_{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\cdot\|(\mathcal{K}_{i}+\lambda\delta_{i})[\mathbf{Y}]\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\leq\eta_{i}\cdot\|\delta_{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\cdot\|\delta_{i}[\mathbf{Y}]\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}

(72)

where we used that
\delta_{i}^{2}=\delta_{i}
, and
\|\delta_{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
is the induced superoperator
\boldsymbol{\rho}
norm. In turn, for the second term we apply assumption (
68
) and the kernel condition (
66
):

\displaystyle\|[\delta_{i},\mathcal{K}_{j}][\mathbf{Y}]\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{ji}\cdot\|\delta_{j}[\mathbf{Y}]\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}

(73)

Summing (
71
) over
i\in[n]
then exposes the desired KMS-oscillator norm:

\displaystyle|\!|\!|\mathbf{X}_{t}|\!|\!|

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\lambda t}|\!|\!|\mathbf{X}|\!|\!|+\max_{i}\bigg(\eta_{i}\|\delta_{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}+\sum_{j\neq i}\kappa_{ij}\bigg)\int^{t}_{0}e^{\lambda(z-t)}\cdot|\!|\!|\mathbf{X}_{z}|\!|\!|\mathrm{d}z

(74)

which results in the advertised bound.

It remains to then justify (
71
). Let us denote
\mathcal{P}_{t}=e^{t\mathcal{K}}
, and
\mathcal{P}_{t}^{(i)}=e^{t(\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{K}_{i})}
to be the generator with the updates on
i
removed. The following calculation,
[
RSA26
, Equation (A5)]
controls the deviation between the two time-evolutions via:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\bigg(e^{\lambda z}\cdot\mathcal{P}^{(i)}_{t-z}\circ\delta_{i}\circ\mathcal{P}_{z}[\mathbf{X}]\bigg)=e^{\lambda z}\cdot\mathcal{P}_{t-z}^{(i)}\circ\bigg(\sum_{j\neq i}[\delta_{i},\mathcal{K}_{j}]\circ\mathcal{P}_{z}[\mathbf{X}]+\delta_{i}\circ(\mathcal{K}_{i}+\lambda\delta_{i})\circ\mathcal{P}_{z}[\mathbf{X}]\bigg).

(75)

Integrating on both sides and using the contractivity of
\mathcal{P}_{t},\mathcal{P}_{t}^{(i)}
in the
\boldsymbol{\rho}
norm then gives the desired statement.
∎

Remark 4.9
.

We remark the time-evolution
\mathcal{P}_{t}
in (
75
) need not be contractive in the
\infty
norm, since
\mathcal{K}
is not CP. Nevertheless, the negative semi-definite spectra implies it is contractive in (KMS)
\boldsymbol{\rho}
norm (
Section

1.3.3
), which is why we required the modification to the oscillator norm.

4.3
Dobrushin conditions from the quasi-locality of complex-time evolution

We dedicate this subsection to simplifying the Dobrushin conditions above (the approximations in time and space for the generator
\mathcal{K}
), to certain operator-growth bounds. As we shall see, each of the parameters in
Section

4.2
admits a clean reduction to some property of the complex-time evolution of local Pauli operators.

We refer the reader back to
Section

3.1.1
and
Section

3.1.1
for the definitions of the complex-time evolution and pairwise-correlations constants
\mathsf{conv}_{\beta},\mathsf{corr}_{\beta}(i,j)
. Equipped with these definitions, we can now simplify each of the parameters in the Dobrushin condition. The proofs are deferred to
Section

4.3.1
below. We begin with the simplest of the three: the superoperator norm of the partial trace.

Lemma 4.10
.

The induced superoperator
\boldsymbol{\rho}
norm of
\delta_{i}
is bounded by
\|\delta_{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\leq 2(1+\mathsf{conv}_{\beta/4})^{2}
.

Next, we turn to the locality in temperature condition.

Lemma 4.11

(Locality in temperature)
.

For each site
i\in[n]
, the generator
\mathcal{K}_{i}
at inverse-temperature
\beta
satisfies the approximation:

\|\mathcal{K}_{i}^{(\beta)}-\mathcal{K}_{i}^{(0)}\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\leq 2^{2\mathsf{q}+3}\cdot\mathsf{conv}_{\beta/4}\cdot(1+\mathsf{conv}_{\beta/4})

(76)

Finally, we turn to the mean-field approximation.

Lemma 4.12

(Mean-field approximation for
\mathcal{K}
)
.

For any pair of sites
i,j\in[n]
, the generator
\mathcal{K}_{i}
at inverse-temperature
\beta
satisfies the approximation

\|[\delta_{j},\mathcal{K}_{i}]\|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\leq 2^{2\mathsf{q}+2}\cdot(1+\mathsf{conv}_{\beta/4})^{2}\cdot\mathsf{corr}_{\beta/4}(i,j)

(77)

Put together, we can now completely recast the Dobrushin condition of
Section

4.2
in terms of operator-growth conditions.

Corollary 4.13
.

Assume that the parameters
\mathsf{conv}_{\beta},\mathsf{corr}_{\beta}
from
Sections

3.1.1
and
3.1.1
satisfy the inequality

2^{4}\cdot\mathsf{conv}_{\beta/4}+2^{2}\cdot\mathsf{corr}_{\beta/4}<1.

(78)

Then,
\mathcal{K}
is gapped.

Proof.

Substitution of the bounds above in
Section

4.2
gives:

2^{3}\cdot\mathsf{conv}_{\beta/4}\cdot(1+\mathsf{conv}_{\beta/4})^{3}+2\cdot(1+\mathsf{conv}_{\beta/4})^{2}\cdot\mathsf{corr}_{\beta/4}<1.

(79)

The observation that this bound is trivial unless
\mathsf{conv}_{\beta/4}\leq 8^{-1}
and arithmetic simplifications gives the desired result.
∎

4.3.1
Deferred proofs

Proof.

[Proof of
Section

4.3
