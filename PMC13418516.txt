Magnetic dummy-template molecularly imprinted polymers as selective sorbents for geosmin enrichment from water samples
Authors: Miaomiao Yu, Lirong Li, Yufeng Wu, Sai Wang, Naili Wang, Xueqiang Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In the present work, core–shell Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs were prepared using a dummy-template imprinting strategy and applied as extraction sorbents for the selective enrichment of geosmin (GSM) from water samples. The resulting Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs showed an adsorption capacity of 6.85 mg g−1, reached adsorption equilibrium within 75 min, and could be magnetically separated within 30 s. Compared with the corresponding non-imprinted polymers, the imprinted sorbents showed enhanced adsorption toward GSM and preferential recognition of GSM over 2-methylisoborneol. After six adsorption–desorption cycles, the adsorption performance decreased by only 9.36%, indicating acceptable reusability. When applied to magnetic dispersive solid-phase extraction coupled with GC-MS, the Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs achieved recoveries of 83.1–90.2% for spiked water samples, with relative standard deviations lower than 7.4%, and provided limits of detection and quantitation of 0.034 and 0.115 mg L−1, respectively. These results suggest that Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs could be used as selective pretreatment sorbents for GSM enrichment and may provide a useful reference for developing imprinting-based extraction materials.

Introduction

Taste and odor episodes in source and drinking water remain a persistent challenge for water quality management because they directly affect sensory acceptability and consumer confidence.1 Among these odorants, GSM is of particular concern because of its frequent occurrence and extremely low odor threshold.2,3 Due to their low odor thresholds, these compounds may cause noticeable odor problems even at very low levels.4,5 Notably, during sudden algal blooms or unexpected contamination events, the concentration of GSM in localized source waters or pollution hotspots can escalate dramatically. This necessitates the development of rapid, convenient, and reliable screening techniques to achieve timely risk assessment and source tracking. Therefore, effective pretreatment and selective enrichment of odorants from aqueous matrices are important for improving the reliability of subsequent instrumental analysis. Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) has been widely used for the determination of volatile and semivolatile odorants in environmental samples.6,7 However, the direct analysis of GSM in water is often limited by its low abundance and the presence of matrix interferences. Conventional pretreatment techniques, including liquid–liquid extraction, solid-phase extraction, and microextraction-based methods, have been applied for odorant enrichment.8–10 Although these approaches are useful, they may suffer from limited selectivity, relatively complicated operation, or matrix-dependent performance. Thus, developing selective and easily recoverable sorbents remains desirable for water sample pretreatment.

Molecularly imprinted polymers (MIPs) offer specific recognition sites that are complementary to target molecules in size, shape, and functional groups, making them attractive sorbents for selective extraction and separation.11–17 In trace analysis, however, the use of the target molecule as the template may introduce potential template leakage and interfere with subsequent quantification. Dummy-template imprinting provides a practical solution by using a structurally related substitute to generate recognition sites while reducing the risk of target-analyte contamination.18,19 For GSM analysis, this strategy is particularly relevant because reliable pretreatment should not introduce additional background interference. Meanwhile, the development of molecularly imprinted materials is increasingly emphasizing greener preparation and application strategies, including the reduction of hazardous solvent consumption, the use of environmentally benign components, and improved material regeneration and reusability.20

Surface imprinting on magnetic supports further improves the accessibility of recognition sites and simplifies sorbent recovery. Compared with bulk MIPs, surface-imprinted materials provide shorter diffusion pathways and faster mass transfer.21,22 Meanwhile, Fe3O4-based magnetic particles enable rapid solid–liquid separation, and SiO2 coating can enhance particle stability, improve dispersibility, and provide active sites for further modification.23–25 Despite these advantages, the use of dummy-template magnetic surface-imprinted polymers as selective pretreatment sorbents for GSM remains relatively limited, especially in combination with chromatographic analysis for water sample application.26

In this work, cis-decahydro-1-naphthol was selected as a dummy template, because it possesses a rigid saturated bicyclic skeleton and a hydroxyl group similar to GSM (Fig. 1). Previous computational studies on GSM molecular imprinting from our group demonstrated that the selective recognition of GSM is closely associated with the hydrogen-bonding interaction between the hydroxyl functionality and functional monomers, together with the geometric complementarity between the target molecule and the imprinted cavity.27 Based on these findings, cis-decahydro-1-naphthol was considered a suitable substitute for generating GSM-recognition sites. Although structurally similar to GSM, cis-decahydro-1-naphthol is chemically distinct from the target analyte, which helps minimize potential interference caused by template leakage during GC-MS analysis. Fe3O4@SiO2 was employed as the magnetic support to prepare Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs for selective GSM enrichment. The preparation conditions were optimized, and the resulting sorbent was systematically characterized in terms of morphology, adsorption performance, selectivity, magnetic response, and reusability. The material was subsequently applied as a magnetic dispersive solid-phase extraction sorbent coupled with GC-MS for GSM determination in spiked water samples. The primary objective was to demonstrate the feasibility of dummy-template magnetic surface imprinting for rapid and selective GSM pretreatment. At its current sensitivity, the method is intended as a convenient preliminary screening tool for identifying relatively high-concentration pollution sources or contamination hot spots during unexpected water-odor incidents, rather than for routine ultra-trace monitoring of background GSM levels.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and instrument

Ferric chloride (FeCl3·6H2O), sodium acetate (NaAc), ethylene glycol (EG), polyethylene glycol (PEG, Mw = 2000), tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), 3-(methacryloxy) propyltrimethoxysilane (KH-570), methacrylic acid (MAA), methacrylamide (MAD), 4-vinylpyridine (4-VP), ethylene glycol dimethacrylate (EGDMA), and 2,2-azoisobutyronitrile (AIBN) were purchased from Aladdin Bio-chemical Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai China). Cis-decahydro-1-naphthol, (±) geosmin (GSM), and 2-methylisoborneol (2-MIB) were obtained from Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA). Acetonitrile (ACN), methanol, ethanol and acetic acid were obtained from Concord Technology Co., Ltd (Tianjin, China). Toluene and acetone were purchased from Tianjin Bohua Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. These chemicals were used as received without further purification and were of at least analytical grade unless otherwise specified. The deionized water used in the experiment was obtained using a Milli-Q water purification system (Millipore, USA).

The morphology and particle size distribution of particles were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images with models Zeiss Sigma 500 and JEOL JEM-2100 F. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) was determined using the potassium bromide compression method in the wavelength range of 400–4000 cm−1 using the PerkinElmer Frontier infrared spectrophotometer to confirm changes in the chemical structure of the material. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was detected by Thermo SCIENTIFIC ESCALAB 250Xi1. Using a Netzsch STA449F5 thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA) in a nitrogen environment, heat the sample from room temperature at a rate of 10 °C min−1 for thermal gravimetric testing. The magnetic properties of the samples were evaluated at room temperature using a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM, Lake Shore 8600).

Synthesis of core–shell Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs

Synthesis of Fe3O4 nanoparticles

Superparamagnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles (Fe3O4-NPs) were synthesized by a hydrothermal method as described in the SI.

Synthesis of Fe3O4@SiO2 and modification of Fe3O4@SiO2

50 mg of Fe3O4-NPs were dispersed in 30 mL of an ethanol/water mixture (5 : 1, v/v) by ultrasonication, followed by mechanical stirring. Ammonium hydroxide (400 µL) and TEOS (250 µL) were then added, and the mixture was stirred at 25 °C for 6 h. The resulting Fe3O4@SiO2 particles were washed several times with deionized water and dried at 60 °C for 12 h.

Fe3O4@SiO2 (0.5 g) was dispersed in 50 mL of toluene by sonication for 20 min. KH-570 (2.0 mL) was then added dropwise under continuous stirring. After nitrogen purging, the reaction was allowed to proceed for 12 h. The modified Fe3O4@SiO2 particles were collected by magnetic separation, washed several times with anhydrous ethanol, and dried overnight in a vacuum oven.

Synthesis of Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs

Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs were prepared by surface polymerization, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Briefly, cis-decahydro-1-naphthol, used as the dummy template, and MAA were mixed at a molar ratio of 1 : 3 and dissolved in 60 mL of acetonitrile to allow the formation of a pre-polymerization complex. Subsequently, modified Fe3O4@SiO2 particles (0.1 g), EGDMA, and AIBN (0.01 g) were added to the solution, and the mixture was dispersed by sonication. The optimized molar ratio of dummy template : MAA : EGDMA was 1 : 3 : 7.5. After purging with nitrogen for 15 min, the mixture was sealed and reacted at 50 °C for 6 h in a water-bath oscillator, followed by further polymerization at 60 °C for 24 h. The resulting particles were collected by magnetic separation. The template was removed by Soxhlet extraction with methanol/acetic acid (9 : 1, v/v) until no template residue was detected in the eluate by GC-MS. The polymer was then washed with methanol and deionized water until a neutral pH was reached. Non-imprinted polymers (NIPs) were prepared using the same procedure but without the addition of cis-decahydro-1-naphthol.

Scheme diagram of the synthetic procedures for Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs.

Kinetic experiments

Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs or Fe3O4@SiO2@NIPs (10 mg) were separately added to 5 mL of GSM methanol solution (300 mg L−1). Aliquots were collected at 10, 20, 30, 45, 60, 75, 90, and 120 min. After magnetic separation, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane filter, and the residual GSM concentration was determined by GC-MS.

Isothermal experiments

Adsorption isotherm experiments were carried out by adding 10 mg of Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs or Fe3O4@SiO2@NIPs to 5 mL of GSM methanol solutions at different initial concentrations. After shaking at room temperature for 12 h, the sorbents were magnetically separated. The equilibrium adsorption capacity of the analyte on the sorbent was calculated according to eqn (1). The adsorption data were fitted using Langmuir and Freundlich isotherm models.1Q = (C0 − Ce)V/mwhere C0 and Ce (mg L−1) are the initial and equilibrium concentrations of GSM, respectively; V (L) is the volume of the solution; and m (g) is the mass of the adsorbent.

Evaluation of selectivity and stability

Briefly, 10 mg of Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs was added to 5 mL of methanol solution containing 250 mg L−1 of GSM or 2-MIB under the same conditions as those used in the adsorption isotherm experiments. The same procedure was applied to Fe3O4@SiO2@NIPs for comparison.

To evaluate reusability and batch-to-batch stability, adsorption experiments were conducted using both repeated adsorption–desorption cycles of the same batch and different batches of Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs. The residual GSM concentration in the supernatant after magnetic separation was determined by GC-MS.

Application to environmental water samples

Lake water samples collected in Tianjin, China, were filtered through a 0.45 µm membrane filter and stored at 4 °C before analysis. Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs were added to 30 mL water samples spiked with GSM standard solution and extracted for 24 min under the optimized conditions. The sorbent was then separated using an external magnet, and the adsorbed GSM was desorbed with methanol/acetic acid (9 : 1, v/v). The eluate was evaporated to dryness under a gentle nitrogen stream, and the residue was reconstituted in 1.0 mL methanol prior to GC-MS analysis.

Statistical analysis

All optimization experiments and selectivity experiments were independently performed in triplicate (n = 3), and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance among different experimental groups was evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Different lowercase letters in the figures indicate significant differences among groups.

The adsorption kinetic, adsorption isotherm, pH optimization, reusability and reproducibility experiments were primarily conducted to investigate adsorption behavior, equilibrium characteristics, and operational stability. Therefore, these experiments were analyzed by kinetic/isotherm model fitting or trend evaluation rather than statistical comparison among groups.

Results and discussion

Optimization of the synthesis of Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs

The adsorption performance of surface-imprinted polymers is strongly influenced by the composition of the imprinting layer, particularly the type of functional monomer and the ratio between monomer and crosslinker. These parameters affect the formation of recognition sites, the thickness of the imprinted shell, and the accessibility of adsorption sites. Therefore, optimization of the polymerization conditions was necessary to obtain Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs with satisfactory adsorption performance toward GSM.

Selection of functional monomers

Functional monomers play a key role in the formation of pre-polymerization complexes and in the generation of effective recognition sites in molecularly imprinted polymers.28 In this study, MAA, 4-VP, and MAD were selected as representative acidic, basic, and neutral functional monomers, respectively, and evaluated under the same reaction conditions in acetonitrile.29 For all three functional monomers, the same cross-linker, initiator, polymerization solvent, reaction temperature, and polymerization time were used, and only the type of functional monomer was varied.

As shown in Table 1, the polymer prepared with MAA exhibited the highest adsorption capacity (5.64 mg g−1) and imprinting factor (IF = 1.81), indicating that MAA was the most suitable functional monomer among those tested. This result may be attributed to the favorable interaction between the carboxyl groups of MAA and the dummy template during pre-polymerization, which facilitates the formation of well-defined recognition sites after polymerization and template removal. Therefore, MAA was selected for subsequent synthesis.

Effect of different monomer ratios

To optimize the composition of the imprinted layer, a series of polymers were synthesized using different template-to-monomer ratios (1 : 1, 1 : 2, 1 : 3, and 1 : 4) and monomer-to-crosslinker ratios (3 : 5, 3 : 10, 3 : 15, and 3 : 20). These variations were expected to influence the thickness, compactness, and accessibility of the imprinted shell.

As shown in Fig. 3A, the adsorption capacity of DMIPs increased significantly with increasing monomer content and reached the maximum value at a template-to-monomer ratio of 1 : 3 (5.52 mg g−1, p < 0.05). A further increase in monomer content did not improve adsorption performance, possibly because excessive monomer may increase non-specific interactions or result in a thicker polymer layer that hinders access to recognition sites. This phenomenon may be attributed to the formation of an excessively thick polymer layer or increased non-specific adsorption sites, which could hinder analyte diffusion and reduce the accessibility of imprinted cavities.

Optimization of the dummy template-to-monomer ratio (A) and monomer-to-cross-linker ratio (B); TEM images of Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs prepared under different imprinting conditions (C). DMIPs-1, DMIPs-2, DMIPs-3, DMIPs-4, and DMIPs-5 represent Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs prepared with different molar ratios of dummy template : monomer : cross-linker (1 : 3 : 5, 1 : 3 : 7.5, 1 : 3 : 10, 1 : 3 : 15, and 1 : 3 : 20, respectively).

Similarly, increasing the amount of crosslinker initially enhanced the adsorption capacity, but excessive crosslinking led to a decline in performance (Fig. 3B). The highest adsorption capacity was obtained at a monomer-to-crosslinker ratio of 3 : 10 and 3 : 15, with no significant difference between these two conditions (p > 0.05), whereas both conditions showed significantly higher adsorption capacity than 3 : 5 and 3 : 20 (p < 0.05). This behavior may be associated with over-rigidification of the polymer network and reduced accessibility of the imprinted cavities. Based on these results, a template : functional monomer : cross-linker molar ratio of 1 : 3 : 7.5 was selected and used for the subsequent preparation of Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs.

TEM characterization further confirmed the influence of polymerization conditions on the imprinted shell thickness (Fig. 3C). The shell thickness increased gradually from approximately 16 nm (DMIPs-1) to 80 nm (DMIPs-5) with increasing crosslinker content, indicating that excessive polymer growth could result in a thicker shell structure. Considering both adsorption performance and structural characteristics, a template : functional monomer : cross-linker molar ratio of 1 : 3 : 7.5 was selected as the optimal condition and used for subsequent preparation of Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs.

Characterization

The morphology and structure of the prepared materials were characterized by TEM, FT-IR, XPS, TGA, and VSM. As shown in Fig. 4A, the Fe3O4 nanoparticles synthesized by the solvothermal method exhibited a relatively uniform spherical morphology with good dispersibility and an average diameter of approximately 200–300 nm (Fig. 4D). After hydrolysis of TEOS, a SiO2 shell with a thickness of about 50–100 nm was formed on the surface of Fe3O4 (Fig. 4B), indicating successful coating of the magnetic core. The SiO2 layer is expected to improve the dispersion stability of Fe3O4 and provide a suitable surface for subsequent polymer growth.

TEM images and corresponding particle size histograms of Fe3O4 (A and D), Fe3O4@SiO2 (B and E), and Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs (C and F).

After surface imprinting polymerization, Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs showed a clear core-shell-shell structure and an increased particle size relative to Fe3O4@SiO2 (Fig. 4C and F). The outer imprinted layer appeared relatively uniform, indicating successful formation of the surface-imprinted polymer shell. Although slight aggregation of some particles was observed, the overall morphology suggests that the prepared DMIPs possessed an accessible imprinted layer favorable for adsorption. The relatively uniform imprinted shell and acceptable particle dispersion may facilitate adsorption by improving the accessibility of recognition sites.30,31

The surface elemental composition and chemical state of Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs were further investigated by XPS. As shown in Fig. 5B, the survey spectrum displayed signals corresponding to C 1s, O 1s, Si 2p, and Fe 2p,36,37 consistent with the expected composition of the magnetic core–shell imprinted polymer. In the high-resolution C 1s spectrum (Fig. 5C), the peaks at 284.1, 284.5, 286.1, and 288.1 eV were assigned to C–C/C–H, CC, C–O, and CO species, respectively.38 The O 1s spectrum (Fig. 5D) showed two major components at 529.4 and 530.9 eV, corresponding to O2− and OH− species.39 These XPS results further support the successful construction of Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs. The detected C–O, CO, and hydroxyl-related species further confirm the availability of oxygen-containing functionalities that may participate in GSM recognition through non-covalent interactions.

The thermal stability of the materials was evaluated by TGA (Fig. S1). Fe3O4 and Fe3O4@SiO2 showed relatively low weight loss over the tested temperature range, indicating good thermal stability. In contrast, Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs exhibited a more pronounced weight loss, particularly between 150 and 400 °C, which was mainly attributed to decomposition of the organic imprinted polymer layer. The total mass loss of Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs was approximately 32.31%, further confirming successful coating of the organic shell.

The magnetic properties of the prepared materials were characterized by VSM at room temperature (Fig. S2). The saturation magnetization values of Fe3O4, Fe3O4@SiO2, and Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs were 69.79, 27.54, and 20.34 emu g−1, respectively. The decrease in magnetization after silica coating and polymer shell formation is expected because of the introduction of non-magnetic layers. Nevertheless, Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs retained sufficient magnetic responsiveness for rapid solid–liquid separation, which is advantageous for practical sample pretreatment.

Influence of pH on adsorption of the imprinted polymers

The adsorption of GSM by Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs was evaluated under different pH conditions, and the results are shown in Fig. 6. The adsorption capacity was strongly influenced by solution pH. Both acidic and alkaline conditions reduced the binding capacity of the polymer, whereas the highest adsorption was observed in the pH range of 6–7. This behavior may be related to the effect of pH on the hydrogen-bonding interactions and recognition-site matching between GSM and the imprinted cavities. Under excessively acidic or alkaline conditions, these interactions may be weakened, resulting in lower adsorption capacity. Because the initial pH of the water samples used in this study was close to 6.5–7.0, subsequent adsorption and extraction experiments were carried out without further pH adjustment.

Adsorption kinetics

The adsorption kinetics of GSM on Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs were investigated, and the results are shown in Fig. 7A. The adsorption capacity increased rapidly during the initial stage and gradually approached equilibrium within approximately 75 min. The fast initial uptake can be attributed to the presence of abundant accessible binding sites on the polymer surface, while the subsequent slower adsorption stage likely reflects diffusion toward less accessible recognition sites within the polymer layer. Compared with some previously reported molecularly imprinted adsorbents,40,41 the equilibrium time obtained in this study was relatively short, which may be associated with the surface-imprinted structure and the accessible recognition sites of Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs.

Adsorption kinetics (A), isotherms (B) and regressions (Langmuir (C), Freundlich (D), Tekin (E) and Scatchard models (F)) of Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs and Fe3O4@SiO2@NIPs for GSM.

To further analyze the adsorption behavior, the experimental data were fitted with pseudo-first-order, pseudo-second-order, Weber–Morris intraparticle diffusion, and Elovich models.42 As shown in Fig. S4, the pseudo-second-order and Elovich models provided better fits than the pseudo-first-order model, as indicated by the higher correlation coefficients. In addition, the adsorption capacity predicted by the pseudo-second-order model was close to the experimental value. These results suggest that the adsorption process involved heterogeneous surface interactions and that chemisorption-related effects may contribute to the overall adsorption behavior.43–46

Adsorption isotherm and Scatchard analysis

The equilibrium adsorption behavior of Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs and Fe3O4@SiO2@NIPs toward GSM was investigated by adsorption isotherm experiments. As shown in Fig. 7B, the adsorption capacity of Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs increased with increasing initial concentration of GSM and was consistently higher than that of Fe3O4@SiO2@NIPs over the tested concentration range. The equilibrium adsorption capacities of Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs and Fe3O4@SiO2@NIPs were 5.58 and 3.22 mg g−1, respectively, giving an imprinting factor of 1.73. This result demonstrates that dummy-template imprinting significantly enhanced the adsorption performance toward GSM.

To further analyze the adsorption behavior, the experimental data were fitted using the Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin isotherm models.47–49 The corresponding fitting parameters are summarized in Table 2. Among the tested models, the Freundlich model showed the best fit (R2 = 0.973), suggesting that GSM adsorption on Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs occurred on a heterogeneous surface with non-uniform binding energies. In addition, the obtained 1/n value was within the range favorable for adsorption,50 indicating that the adsorption process was feasible under the studied conditions.

Adsorption selectivity

The selectivity of Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs was evaluated using GSM as the target compound and 2-MIB as a structurally related competing compound, with both compounds tested at an initial concentration of 250 mg L−1. As shown in Fig. 8A, the adsorption capacity of Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs toward GSM (5.43 ± 0.26 mg g−1) was significantly higher than that toward 2-MIB (1.31 ± 0.28 mg g−1) (p < 0.05), demonstrating the preferential recognition ability of the imprinted sorbent toward GSM.

In contrast, Fe3O4@SiO2@NIPs exhibited comparable adsorption capacities toward GSM (2.15 ± 0.19 mg g−1) and 2-MIB (2.05 ± 0.32 mg g−1), with no significant difference observed between the two compounds (p > 0.05). This result indicates that adsorption by NIPs was mainly governed by non-specific interactions rather than selective recognition.

The significantly enhanced adsorption of GSM by Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs can be attributed to the formation of template-induced recognition cavities during polymerization. Specifically, the hydroxyl group of GSM may interact with oxygen-containing functional groups within the imprinted cavities through hydrogen bonding, while the rigid bicyclic structure facilitates spatial matching with the template-induced cavities. In contrast, although NIPs possess similar chemical functionalities, the absence of template-directed cavities limits their ability to provide specific recognition, resulting in adsorption mainly dominated by non-specific interactions.

Reusability and reproducibility

The reusability of Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs was evaluated through six consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles using the same batch of polymer (Fig. 8B). In Fig. 8B, the dark-blue bars represent the adsorption capacity during the reuse cycles, while the black line represents the corresponding recovery rate. The adsorption capacity and recovery rate decreased only slightly after repeated use, indicating acceptable reusability of the prepared material. In addition, TEM observations after repeated cycles (Fig. S5) showed that the morphology of the polymer remained largely intact, suggesting that the magnetic core–shell–shell structure was sufficiently stable under the experimental conditions.

Batch-to-batch reproducibility was also evaluated using six independently prepared batches of Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs (Fig. 8B). In Fig. 8B, the light-blue bars represent the adsorption capacities of the six independently prepared batches. The comparable adsorption capacities obtained for the different batches indicated satisfactory reproducibility of the adsorption performance. These findings suggest that the prepared Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs have potential as reusable adsorbents for practical sample pretreatment.

Overall, the results of the reuse-cycle and batch-to-batch reproducibility experiments demonstrate that the prepared Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs possess satisfactory reusability, structural stability, and reproducibility, supporting their potential application as reusable adsorbents for practical sample pretreatment.

Optimization of MDSPE conditions

Optimization of extraction conditions

Response surface methodology (RSM) was employed to optimize the main factors affecting the extraction efficiency of the MDSPE procedure, including sorbent amount (X1), pH (X2), and extraction time (X3). Although the adsorption equilibrium time determined from kinetic experiments was approximately 72 min, the extraction time in the MDSPE procedure was optimized based on GSM recovery and practical extraction efficiency rather than complete adsorption equilibrium. Based on preliminary experiments, the investigated ranges were 20–50 mg for sorbent amount, pH 4–9, and 10–30 min for extraction time. The extraction recovery of GSM was used as the response value (Y), and 17 experiments were designed according to the Box–Behnken design using Design-Expert 12 software. The experimental conditions and corresponding responses are listed in Table S1.

Optimization of desorption conditions

The effects of desorption solvent type, solvent volume, and desorption time on the recovery of GSM were investigated. After adsorption, the Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs sorbent was magnetically separated from the sample solution, and the collected sorbent was treated with the desorption solvent. The desorption process was performed by mechanically shaking the sorbent with the desorption solvent at room temperature. After desorption, the sorbent was separated using an external magnet, and the obtained supernatant was collected for subsequent GC-MS analysis. As shown in Fig. S6A, methanol/acetic acid (9 : 1, v/v) provided higher desorption efficiency than methanol, ethanol, acetonitrile, or acetone alone, indicating that the acidic mixed solvent was more effective for disrupting the interactions between GSM and the imprinted sites.

The effect of eluent volume is shown in Fig. S6B. Quantitative desorption could be achieved when the volume of methanol/acetic acid (9 : 1, v/v) reached 3.0 mL. Therefore, 3.0 mL was selected as the optimal desorption volume. In addition, the recovery increased with desorption time and reached a satisfactory level at 9 min (Fig. S6C). Therefore, mechanical shaking at room temperature for 9 min was selected as the optimal desorption condition and was used in subsequent experiments.

Effect of sample volume

To evaluate the applicability of the proposed extraction system to real water samples, the effect of sample volume on extraction recovery was investigated over the range of 10–100 mL. As shown in Fig. S7, the recovery initially increased as the sample volume increased from 10 to 20 mL, then remained acceptable within the range of 20–40 mL, and finally decreased when the sample volume exceeded 60 mL. The decrease at larger sample volumes may be related to insufficient sorbent capacity or incomplete extraction under the selected conditions. Based on these results, 30 mL was selected as the sample volume for subsequent environmental water analysis.

Feasibility evaluation in spiked water samples

The analytical performance of the proposed MDSPE-GC-MS method was evaluated in terms of linearity, precision, and accuracy. The method showed good linearity over the GSM concentration range of 0.15–3.0 mg L−1, with a correlation coefficient (R2) of 0.9987. The selected concentration range was intended primarily for method validation and sorbent performance evaluation under controlled conditions. Furthermore, the limit of detection (LOD, 3σ) and limit of quantitation (LOQ, 10σ) were estimated to be approximately 0.034 mg L−1 and 0.115 mg L−1, respectively. Although the proposed sorbent shows good feasibility for GSM enrichment and GC-MS determination, the current LOD remains insufficient for routine ultra-trace monitoring of background GSM in many real-water environments. Therefore, the method is more appropriately positioned as a rapid preliminary screening tool for relatively elevated GSM contamination, such as pollution hot spots or unexpected water-odor incidents. Future sensitivity improvements may be achieved by increasing the sample volume, optimizing the sorbent-to-sample ratio, reducing the final desorption volume, and introducing solvent evaporation and reconstitution to enhance the enrichment factor. In addition, coupling the procedure with GC-MS/MS or optimized selected-ion-monitoring conditions could further enhance instrumental sensitivity while maintaining acceptable recovery, extraction time, and matrix tolerance.

The intra-day and inter-day precision values, expressed as relative standard deviations (RSDs), ranged from 0.75% to 7.64% at three concentration levels (0.15, 1.0, and 3.0 mg L−1). To further assess the applicability of the method, lake water samples were spiked with GSM at three levels (0.3, 1.0, and 2.5 mg L−1) and analyzed using the proposed procedure. The recoveries ranged from 83.1% to 90.2%, with RSDs below 7.4%. These results indicate that the proposed method provides acceptable accuracy and precision for the selective enrichment and determination of GSM in spiked lake water samples within the investigated concentration range. However, the present validation was mainly conducted using spiked lake-water samples, and systematic evaluation of potential matrix effects from dissolved organic matter, inorganic ions, and other co-existing odor compounds was not performed in this study. Therefore, although the competitive adsorption experiment with MIB demonstrated the selective recognition capability of Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs toward GSM, further investigations involving more complex environmental matrices and controlled interference experiments are required to comprehensively assess the matrix tolerance and practical applicability of the proposed method.

Furthermore, compared with HS-SPME, the use of a dummy-template imprinted sorbent provides enhanced selective extraction toward GSM. Although HS-SPME also exhibits good sensitivity, the proposed method integrates molecular recognition and magnetic separation, avoiding the limitations associated with fiber-based extraction, such as limited fiber lifetime and single-use characteristics. The incorporation of a magnetic core also enables rapid phase separation without centrifugation, significantly simplifying the pretreatment procedure.

It should be noted that the methods compared in Table 5 were developed using different sample matrices, extraction principles, and analytical configurations. Therefore, direct ranking based solely on individual parameters such as LOD, recovery, or precision may not be appropriate. Instead, the proposed method provides a balanced analytical platform that combines selective enrichment, low solvent consumption, rapid magnetic separation, and reusable sorbent characteristics. Benefiting from the rapid phase separation (within 30 s) and eliminating the need for centrifugation, this method holds promising potential for the rapid emergency screening of sudden taste and odor incidents, particularly for tracking high-concentration pollution sources or algal bloom hotspots.

Nevertheless, certain limitations remain. From a green chemistry perspective, the preparation of the imprinted polymer, template-removal process, and analyte desorption procedure still rely on organic solvents, which may increase environmental burden due to solvent consumption and waste generation. Although the proposed magnetic separation strategy reduces the need for centrifugation and simplifies the pretreatment procedure, further improvement is required to develop a more sustainable extraction platform. Future work will focus on replacing conventional organic solvents with greener alternatives, such as ethanol–water mixtures, aqueous-compatible solvents, bio-based solvents, or deep eutectic solvents. In addition, reducing solvent consumption through miniaturized synthesis, simplified washing procedures, solvent recycling, and the development of water-compatible functional monomers and cross-linkers will be important for improving the overall sustainability of magnetic molecularly imprinted materials. Furthermore, the selection of suitable dummy templates remains largely empirical, and computational chemistry approaches may provide a rational strategy for designing more efficient imprinting systems. The applicability of the proposed method in more complex environmental matrices and ultra-trace GSM monitoring scenarios also requires further systematic validation.

Conclusions

In summary, a dummy-template magnetic surface-imprinted polymer, Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs, was successfully prepared using cis-decahydro-1-naphthol as the dummy template. The obtained material was comprehensively characterized by TEM, FT-IR, XPS, TGA, and VSM, confirming successful formation of the magnetic core–shell–shell structure. Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs showed satisfactory adsorption capacity, selective recognition toward GSM, rapid magnetic separation, and acceptable reusability.

When used as an adsorbent in magnetic dispersive solid-phase extraction coupled with GC-MS, Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs enabled the selective enrichment and determination of GSM in water samples with satisfactory recovery and precision. The optimized method simplified the pretreatment procedure and reduced extraction time compared with conventional approaches. These results indicate that the proposed material is a promising adsorbent for selective pretreatment of GSM and related odorants in aqueous samples, and they highlight the potential of dummy-template magnetic surface imprinting for further analytical development.

However, some limitations of the current method should be acknowledged. Although Fe3O4@SiO2@DMIPs exhibited satisfactory selectivity and reusability, the current procedure still involves organic solvents during polymer preparation, template removal, and analyte desorption, which limits its environmental sustainability. Moreover, the present method was mainly validated using spiked water samples, and further evaluation using diverse real water matrices is necessary. Future studies should systematically investigate the influence of dissolved organic matter, inorganic ions, and co-existing odor compounds to better understand matrix effects and improve the reliability of the method for practical environmental applications.

Future studies will focus on developing greener synthesis and regeneration strategies, improving enrichment efficiency and instrumental sensitivity for ultra-trace GSM detection, rationally designing dummy templates through computational approaches, and expanding magnetic imprinting technology toward other odor-active contaminants.
