Clinical Spectrum of Nodoventricular Accessory Pathways
Authors: Chhayroud Heng, Chandara Rith, Nilar Aung, Sorkhan Sok, Namgung June, Sokha Chan, Jinsun Park, Jae-Sun Uhm, Gi-Byoung Nam, Hui-Nam Pak, Melvin M. Scheinman, Chun Hwang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNodoventricular accessory pathways (NV-APs) are rare embryonic remnants connecting the atrioventricular node to the ventricle. We describe their electrophysiological characteristics and ablation outcomes across a multicenter Asian cohort, highlighting a developmental link to Ebstein anomaly.Case SeriesFive patients presented with supraventricular tachycardia due to NV-APs (4 concealed, 1 manifest). Programmed stimulation confirmed ventricular participation and excluded the atrium from the circuit. Two patients had Ebstein anomaly, clinically extending histological data regarding structural atrioventricular junctional abnormalities in the postnatal heart. Ablation targets varied significantly given diverse anatomical insertions. Mapping efforts were made to record AP potentials; a successful ablation site was achieved at the right infero-paraseptum in 3 patients guided by AP potentials, the left infero-paraseptum in 1 patient, and the supero-paraseptal region via the noncoronary aortic sinus in 1 patient.ConclusionThis series illustrates the clinical heterogeneity of NV-APs. Their presence in Ebstein anomaly represents a developmental association driven by tricuspid hinge and atrioventricular node axis displacement, requiring targeted mapping of discrete potentials.Take-Home MessagesNodoventricular pathways exhibit significant anatomical heterogeneity, including a developmental link to Ebstein anomaly. Successful ablation requires precise mapping of discrete pathway potentials rather than generic regional ablation.

Nodoventricular accessory pathways (NV-APs) are rare variant of AP that connects the atrioventricular node (AVN) or its extensions to the ventricular myocardium or Purkinje system. Their infrequency and atypical conduction properties make diagnosis challenging, particularly when they participate in tachycardia circuits. Electrophysiological maneuvers are essential to differentiate NV-AP from other APs or atypical atrioventricular nodal re-entrant tachycardia (AVNRT) and to define their conduction pattern.1, 2, 3 Previous reports have emphasized diagnostic criteria and ablation targets, but presentations varied widely. This case report included NV-APs patients from Asian countries to highlight the diverse clinical spectrum of NV-APs, including their occurrence in patients with Ebstein anomaly, which may create further challenges.Take-Home Messages•Nodoventricular pathways exhibit significant anatomical heterogeneity, including a developmental link to Ebstein anomaly.•Successful ablation requires precise mapping of discrete pathway potentials rather than generic regional ablation.

An electrophysiology (EP) study was performed, and NCT was readily induced during catheter positioning. The ventricular pacing showed a concentric retrograde activation pattern with decremental conduction. The tachycardia terminated promptly with a single ventricular extrastimulus (VES) delivered during His bundle activation accompanied by ventriculoatrial (VA) block (Figure 1B). Ventricular overdrive pacing (VOP) during tachycardia demonstrated a postpacing interval–tachycardia cycle length (PPI-TCL) of 34 ms (Figure 1C). Furthermore, VOP at a shorter cycle length during tachycardia resulted in progressive ventricular fusion before tachycardia termination (Supplemental Figure 1). A spontaneous premature ventricular complex (PVC) coinciding with His activation advanced the subsequent atrial signal (Figure 1D), indicating ventricular involvement in the re-entrant circuit. During tachycardia, atrial extrastimulus (S2) and atrial overdrive pacing (AOP) from the coronary sinus catheter showed atrial dissociation, while the tachycardia cycle length remained unchanged at the ventricular recording site (Figure 1E, Supplemental Figure 2), confirming that the atrium was not part of the circuit. These EP findings supported the presence of an accessory pathway, excluding atrial tachycardia and orthodromic re-entrant tachycardia via atrioventricular accessory pathways (AV-APs) as well an AVNRT, in view of very short PPI-TCL.

A concealed NV-APs was the most likely diagnosis. Mapping and ablation were performed during VOP and during tachycardia. The mapping was guided by the earliest atrial activation, and the ablation was performed at the site with a small discrete potential between A and V (Figure 1F). Radiofrequency energy was delivered at that site and resulted in successful termination of the tachycardia without junctional beats; VA was dissociated after ablation. The patient remained symptom free during more than 6 months of follow-up.

The EP study revealed multiple concealed AV-APs, including ones at the right inferior and right free wall, each with differing retrograde refractory periods. Initial ablation at the right inferior and free wall sites was performed; however, tachycardia remained inducible with a shift in the retrograde atrial activation pattern. VOP during tachycardia demonstrated a short PPI-TCL of 75 ms (Figure 2B), and a spontaneous PVC occurred during tachycardia was able to advance the A signal and reset the tachycardia (Figure 2C), confirming ventricular involvement in the re-entrant circuit. Right ventricular (RV) pacing 20 ms shorter than the tachycardia cycle length resulted in progressive fusion with the termination of tachycardia (Figure 2D). Upon spontaneous termination of the tachycardia, a paradoxical AH response was observed (Figure 2E), with a 19-ms difference in AH intervals between tachycardia and sinus rhythm. This finding indicated parallel activation of the atrium and His bundle, supporting the diagnosis of a concealed NV-AP. An ablation was performed at the right infero-paraseptal region, targeting a site with a sharp retrograde A signal and a small potential between the A and V signals (Figure 2F), suggestive of an AP potential. Ablation at this site rendered the tachycardia noninducible; no junctional beat was observed during ablation. We concluded that the patient had a right inferior free wall, right anterior free wall, and an NV-AP with insertion to the AVN at the right infero-paraseptal area but could not conclude involvement of right inferior extensions of the AVN as part of the tachycardia circuit. The patient remained symptom free during 6 months follow-up.

Case 3

A 37-year-old man with a failed prior ablation presented with recurrent palpitations. Transthoracic echocardiography was normal. Surface ECG showed sinus rhythm with episodes of WCT with left bundle branch block morphology (Figure 3A). He underwent an EP study; during VES, the VA interval progressively prolonged (Figure 3B), indicating decremental retrograde conduction properties. VES easily induced both WCT and NCT, with the earliest atrial signal recorded on the His catheter. During sustained NCT, VOP produced progressive fusion and further VA prolongation without changing the H-H cycle length (Figure 3C). A PVC delivered during the His-refractory period advanced the next atrial electrogram and reset the tachycardia (Figure 3D), confirming that the ventricle was part of the re-entrant circuit. AOP from the right atrium failed to reset the tachycardia (Figure 3E). Simultaneous right atrial and RV pacing was performed (Supplemental Figure 3). Despite successful RV capture (evidenced by H-H shortening), the VA interval prolonged and the subsequent P-P interval lengthened. This paradoxical response—where ventricular manipulation failed to advance or reset the atrium—confirmed that the atrium was not a necessary component of the re-entrant circuit and was dissociated from the ventricle, establishing the diagnosis of a concealed NV-AP, excluding the atrium from the circuit. Case 4

A 49-year-old woman presented with recurrent palpitations over several years, characterized by sudden onset and offset episodes lasting approximately 5 minutes. A 3-day Holter monitoring did not capture any tachycardia. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed Ebstein anomaly with 11 mm of mild displacement of the septal leaflet responsible for the malcoaptation with the severe tricuspid regurgitation warranting surgical intervention. An EP study was performed preoperatively. Baseline ECG showed a positive delta wave in lead V1 and the inferior leads without a right bundle branch block pattern (Figure 4A). During sinus rhythm, a positive HV interval of 37 ms was noted. AOP and atrial extrastimulation demonstrated decremental properties of the accessory pathway, characterized by progressive augmentation of the delta wave and prolongation of the stimulus-to-delta interval at shorter pacing cycle lengths. Simultaneously, the AH interval prolonged until the HV interval became negative, eventually resulting in the His signal being obscured within the ventricular electrogram (Figure 4B). RV pacing demonstrated earliest atrial activation at the His catheter with evidence of VA decremental conduction (Figure 4C). During tachycardia, VES advanced the atrial signal and reset the tachycardia (Figure 4D), AOP demonstrated atrial capture without altering the tachycardia cycle length at the ventricular level. This observed atrioventricular dissociation proves that the re-entry circuit is independent of atrial involvement (Figure 4E). VOP showed progressive fusion and VA prolongation (Figure 4F), with a PPI-TCL of 71 ms. With the impression of NV-APs, mapping during RV pacing localized early A in the right infero-paraseptal, where in the sinus an A and AP potential preceded the delta wave (Figure 4G). Tachycardia was easily induced with AOP and atrial extrastimulation; in that instance, critical prolongation of AH interval including fast pathway block with an AH jump followed by conduction through the slow pathway with the earliest A in His, initiated tachycardia. Discussion

NV-APs are characterized by a connection between the AVN or its extensions to the ventricular myocardium or Purkinje system. Unlike typical AV-APs, NV-APs bypass the atrium entirely in the re-entrant circuit. An EP study is essential for diagnosis, using pacing maneuvers to demonstrate decremental conduction properties, confirm ventricular participation in the tachycardia circuit, and exclude atrial involvement.1,2 Previous case series have focused on EP criteria for differentiating NV pathways from other atypical pathways or atypical AVNRT.4, 5, 6 Standard ablation at the typical slow pathway region or left infero-paraseptal region (left posterior extension) have also been described, but recent publications have emphasized that these sites may be insufficient in some cases given the diverse anatomical connections of these pathways.7, 8, 9

Our case series broadens the clinical spectrum of NV-AP presentations. Notably, 2 patients in our series presented with Ebstein anomaly; this clinical observation concurs with and extends the histological findings of Sánchez-Quintana et al,10 who identified structural atrioventricular junctional abnormalities in postnatal hearts that may facilitate NV-AP formation. Pathological studies by Sánchez-Quintana et al,10, 11, 12, 13, 14 Ho et al,10, 11, 12, 13, 14 Anderson et al,10, 11, 12, 13, 14 and others13,14 from the postmortem fetal and early neonate Ebstein's hearts have shown that the majority of specimens had abnormal direct muscle bundle connections from the AVN region to the superior crest of the ventricular septum that could become NV-APs. However, previous clinical studies and 1 large multicenter Ebstein cardiac arrhythmia study that included all AP cases in the teenage or older population did not support these histologic findings.10,11 However, the relatively frequent occurrence of Ebstein anomaly in our case series suggests a potential anatomical or histological predisposition rather than a coincidental finding. This novel observation raises the possibility that the abnormal atrioventricular junctional anatomy characteristic of Ebstein anomaly may predispose to the development of atypical pathways, highlighting the need for further clinical studies with careful evaluation of the tricuspid annular region during mapping and ablation.

Concealed NV-APs with retrograde conduction only were identified in 4 patients (cases 1, 2, 3, and 5). All patients presented with inducible tachycardia during EP study, and ablation was successful in all cases. Two patients (cases 1 and 2) were ablated at the right infero-paraseptal region, guided by the earliest sharp atrial potential accompanied by a small fractionated signal during tachycardia, interpreted as the accessory pathway potential. One patient (case 3) underwent ablation at the left infero-paraseptum, where an accessory pathway potential was recorded; these rare forms of NV-APs have been reported.7, 8, 9 One patient (case 4) demonstrated both anterograde and retrograde conduction, and successful ablation at the right infero-paraseptum based on identification of an accessory pathway potential between the atrial and ventricular signals eliminated both conductions.

Another patient (case 5) was successfully ablated at the supero-paraseptal region from the noncoronary aortic sinus, guided by a small fractionated potential. This unique form of concealed atrioventricular pathway was ablated by successfully targeting a small fragmented sharp potential from the supero-paraseptal region of the noncoronary aortic sinus, which eliminated tachycardia. The EP study findings could not rule out an atypical form of AVNRT involving the superior slow pathway as reported by Kaneko et al15 with bystander concealed NV-AP. The participating of NV-AP as the retrograde limb of tachycardia suggested by the tachycardia initiation without dual nodal conduction, decremental VA conduction, with the earliest A in His rather than proximal coronary sinus and extrastimulus V reset during tachycardia. The finding also suggests NV-AP that inserted into the superior slow pathway with supero-paraseptal or retroaortic AVN.

An important observation in our series is the variability in successful ablation signals. While the majority of concealed NV-APs were mapping guided by the earliest retrograde A during tachycardia or V pacing at the right infero-paraseptum close to the slow pathway area, as shown by several past investigators, other cases included our required identification of discrete AP potentials—either at the right infero-paraseptum (cases 1, 2, and 4), left infero-paraseptum (case 3), or supero-paraseptum (case 5). This highlights the need for tailored mapping strategies that often require prolonged procedure time and carry a higher risk of complications. Clinical presentations in our series support tailoring EP strategies to each individual patient AP potential–guided ablation in NV-AP patients. Moreover, the identification of NV-APs in patients with Ebstein anomaly underscores the importance of comprehensive preoperative EP evaluation in this population.

Conclusions

This case series broadens the knowledge of NV-APs with varied presentations and ablation sites. We describe a novel clinical association between NV-APs and Ebstein anomaly, suggesting that structural atrioventricular junction abnormalities may predispose to pathway formation. Notably, we report the successful ablation of an NV-AP from the supero-paraseptal region that may involve superior slow pathway using a retrograde aortic approach. These findings highlight the importance of individualized mapping and careful anatomical evaluation. Further studies are needed to confirm these associations and optimize ablation strategies.
