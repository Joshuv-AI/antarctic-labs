Clinical Features and Outcomes of Pediatric Lyme Arthritis in Europe: A Large Swiss Cohort Study
Authors: Beat M Greiter, Semjon Sidorov, Elvira Cannizzaro, Michelle Seiler, Ester Osuna, Franca Baggi Menozzi, Stefanie von Felten, Adrian Egli, Christoph Berger, Patrick M Meyer Sauteur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLyme arthritis (LA) in children typically manifests as monoarthritis of a large joint. Although the prognosis is generally good, persistent arthritis occurs in 10%–23% of patients despite antibiotic treatment. Most described pediatric LA cohorts are from the United States, with limited European data available. This study aimed to describe clinical features and outcomes of pediatric LA in Europe using a large Swiss cohort.MethodsChildren diagnosed with LA at the University Children's Hospital Zurich, Switzerland between 1 January 2006 and 31 December 2020 were included. Data on clinical disease presentation, laboratory findings, and treatment courses were compared between outcome groups using descriptive statistics and multivariable logistic regression models.ResultsA total of 107 LA patients were included. Persistent arthritis, defined as symptoms lasting ≥2 months after antibiotics, occurred in 26 patients (24.3%; 95% CI, 16.8%–33.7%). Characteristics associated with persistent arthritis included older age, symptoms >14 days before presentation, delayed LA-specific antibiotic treatment >30 days, absence of empirical antibiotics, and lower blood monocyte counts. Among patients with persistent arthritis, 10 (38.5%) recovered (9 after intra-articular corticosteroid injections), whereas 16 (61.5%) were eventually diagnosed with juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA).ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive characterization of pediatric LA in the largest European cohort to date, complementing existing data from the United States. Early clinical and laboratory features did not reliably differentiate children who achieved resolution from those who developed persistent arthritis. The finding that a substantial proportion of children with persistent arthritis were later diagnosed with JIA highlights the urgent need to identify predictors of disease persistence and to optimize management strategies.

Lyme arthritis (LA) is a manifestation of Lyme disease (LD) caused by infection with several geographically distinct genospecies of Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato (B. burgdorferi) [1]. LD is one of the most common vector-borne infections in Europe and the United States, with an estimated annual incidence of approximately 128 000 cases in Europe [2] and 476 000 cases in the United States [3]. LA develops in a minority of infected individuals, accounting for roughly 3%–7% of LD cases in Europe and 10%–28% in the United States [4, 5].

Lyme arthritis typically emerges weeks to months after a tick bite and may occur during any season of the year [6]. It predominantly affects large joints, particularly the knee [5–7]. In children, persistent or recurrent joint swelling and pain are the most frequent clinical features [7–9]. Compared with adults, children more often present with arthritis as their sole manifestation of LD [10], which may complicate differentiation from septic arthritis or early-onset juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) [7]. According to current diagnostic guidelines, LA is diagnosed based on clinical findings and the detection of B. burgdorferi-specific antibodies in serum using two-tier serological testing [11, 12]. Although serum antibody testing is strongly recommended over B. burgdorferi-specific polymerase chain reaction (PCR) of synovial fluid/tissue when assessing LA, PCR can be a useful additional diagnostic tool to provide more definitive information [11].

Overall, outcomes for pediatric LA are generally good, though about 10%–23% of affected children develop persistent arthritis despite appropriate antibiotic treatment [11, 13, 14]. This condition, termed “postinfectious,” “persistent,” or “antibiotic-refractory” arthritis [1, 15] is currently thought to result from immune dysregulation rather than ongoing infection [16]. Management recommendations for persistent LA generally include a maximum of two antibiotic courses, supported by anti-inflammatory agents, intra-articular corticosteroids, and, in selected cases, disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) and biologic agents [9, 11, 15, 17, 18].

Most pediatric LA cohorts described to date originate from the United States, and European data remain limited [13, 14, 19–22]. Therefore, our understanding of pediatric LA is largely based on United States-derived populations, which may not fully represent European clinical presentations, in part due to differences in B. burgdorferi genospecies circulating in the United States (B. burgdorferi sensu stricto) and Europe (predominantly B. afzelii and B. garinii) [1, 23]. Although B. burgdorferi sensu stricto is still considered the predominant genospecies in clinically manifest LA in Europe, B. afzelii and B. garinii could be detected in up to 30% of European patients with LA [16, 20, 24]. Further, B. burgdorferi sensu stricto isolates from the United States and Europe differ genetically, suggesting that strain-level variation may contribute to regional differences in clinical presentation [25, 26]. In addition, definition of persistent LA and antibiotic treatment approaches vary among studies, and treatment strategies for persistent LA in children lack standardization and systematic validation.

The objective of this study was to describe the clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and outcomes of LA in Europe using a large Swiss cohort.

METHODS

Ethical Statement

This study was approved by the ethics committee of the Canton of Zurich (BASEC-Nr. 2022-02226) as a further use of health-related personal data for research. The study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines (https://www.strobe-statement.org/).

Study Design and Eligibility Criteria

This retrospective observational study was performed as an electronic hospital database review, as previously described [6]. The inclusion criteria were children and adolescents aged ≤18 years who were diagnosed with LA at the University Children's Hospital Zurich between 1 January 2006 and 31 December 2020.

Case and Outcome Definitions

Definition of Lyme Arthritis

A diagnosis of LA was defined according to current guidelines [11, 12]: (1) clinical diagnosis of arthritis, and (2) positive B. burgdorferi-specific two-tier serology.

Clinical Outcome Classification

LA patients were categorized into 2 groups based on clinical outcome: (1) children with clinically resolved arthritis following LA-specific antibiotic treatment within <2 months after treatment completion (ie, no signs and symptoms of arthritis), and (2) children with clinically persistent arthritis for ≥2 months after the completion of one course of LA-specific antibiotic treatment (ie, ongoing joint swelling [effusion and/or synovial thickening on ultrasound] and/or limitation of range of motion with pain-related functional impairment).

Standard LA-specific antibiotic treatment included one course of primarily oral amoxicillin (<8 years of age) or doxycycline (≥8 years of age), or ceftriaxone intravenous (IV) each for 28 days, according to current LD treatment recommendations at the respective time during the 15-year study period [6]. All patients received one course of LA-specific antibiotic treatment. In general, a second course of LA-specific antibiotic treatment was not initiated, reflecting the current understanding and established clinical practice in our institution. Patients with persistent arthritis were managed through an interdisciplinary approach in close collaboration with pediatric rheumatologists.

The 2-month threshold after treatment completion was selected as a standardized and clinically meaningful time point for outcome assessment, consistent with previous literature describing persistent arthritis after standard antibiotic treatment [1]. In the present study, this 2-month threshold was used as a standardized outcome measure following the initial antibiotic course, reflecting the institutional management strategy at our center, and should not be interpreted as implying that completion of two antibiotic courses was required. Further, this outcome definition does not take into account the use of anti-inflammatory drugs (ie, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [NSAIDs]), which are usually prescribed continuously in cases of persistent arthritis.

Final Clinical Status

The final clinical status was assessed descriptively based on follow-up data available at our institution and included either sustained resolution of arthritis or the assignment of an alternative rheumatologic diagnosis. The diagnosis of JIA was not a predefined study outcome but reflects the final clinical diagnosis assigned by pediatric rheumatologists during follow-up. JIA was diagnosed according to the International League of Associations for Rheumatology classification criteria, defined as arthritis of unknown origin persisting for at least 6 weeks with onset before 16 years of age after exclusion of other causes [27, 28].

Clinical and Laboratory Data

Epidemiological, demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were extracted from medical records for each patient, as described previously [6]. Routine laboratory parameters presented were available only at the time of initial presentation.

Borrelia burgdorferi-specific Testing

Two-tier serology for the detection of B. burgdorferi-specific antibodies (IgM and IgG) in serum was conducted at the ISO-accredited Institute of Medical Microbiology, University of Zurich, following standardized manufacturer instructions and using identical assays throughout the 15-year study period, as previously described in detail [6].

The screening test was an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (recomWell Borrelia IgM and IgG, MIKROGEN Diagnostik, MIKROGEN GmbH, Neuried, Germany), and an immunoblot (Western blot; recomLine Borrelia IgM and IgG, MIKROGEN Diagnostik) was used as the confirmatory assay.

In brief, diagnostic bands for B. burgdorferi antigens were evaluated for color reaction and intensity after addition of 1:100 diluted patient serum. The intensity was graded using a Dynablot plus instrument with recomScan software (MIKROGEN Diagnostik) as follows: 0 (no reaction, equal to the intensity of the cutoff for the control), 1+, 2+, 3+, and 4+. Intensities of at least 1+ were considered positive. When positive, each band received a certain point value (IgG: p100, 5 points; VlsE, 5 points; p58, 4 points; p41, 1 point; p39, 5 points; OspA, 5 points; OspC, 5 points; and p18, 5 points; for IgM: same point values, except for p39, 4 points; and OspC, 8 points). The immunoblot was considered positive if the sum value of points was >7. Point values and point values thresholds were provided by the manufacturer [29]. Final interpretation of the serologic assay was based on immunoblot results for both IgM and IgG isotypes.

Statistical Analysis

Median and interquartile range (IQR, first and third quartile) were reported for continuous variables, frequency and percentage for categorical variables. For the variables “time from symptom onset to hospital presentation” and “time from symptom onset to LA-specific antibiotic treatment,” both the raw continuous values and clinically relevant categories (>14 days [time of symptom onset to emergency department (ED) presentation] and >30 days [time from symptom onset to LA-specific antibiotic treatment], respectively, as previously applied by Hirsch et al. [30]) were reported. Descriptive statistics were tabulated by LA clinical outcome (from now on referred to as “clinical outcome”) as defined above, ie (1) resolved arthritis, and (2) persistent arthritis. Differences between the 2 groups were assessed using Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. The prevalence of resolved and persistent arthritis was reported with a Wilson 95% CI with continuity correction. Variables showing significant differences (P value <.05) in univariate analyses were further adjusted for age and sex using logistic regression. To assess linearity of continuous predictors in logistic regression, models with linear terms were compared with models using natural splines (3 degrees of freedom) as described by Harrell [31]. Variables showing evidence of nonlinearity were included as categorical predictors in the regression model.

Analyses were performed using the R software, version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

RESULTS

Study Population

A total of 108 patients with LA were included in the study. Clinical outcome data at 2 months following the completion of LA-specific antibiotic treatment were unavailable for 1 patient, precluding assignment to a clinical outcome group; this patient was therefore excluded from further analyses. The remaining 107 patients were categorized into clinical outcome groups: 81 (75.7%; 95% CI, 66.3%–83.2%) had resolution of arthritis, and 26 (24.3%; 95% CI, 16.8%–33.7%) had persistent arthritis for ≥2 months after completion of a single course of LA-specific antibiotic treatment.

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the 107 LA patients with outcome data at 2-month follow-up are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 10.1 years (IQR, 8.1–12.5). Patients with persistent arthritis were significantly older than those with resolved arthritis (P < .0001). Overall, 63 (58.9%) of patients were male. Underlying diseases were reported in 6 (5.6%) patients; none of these patients had rheumatological conditions, and musculoskeletal system disease had only previously been diagnosed in 1 (0.9%) patient (ie, Osgood-Schlatter disease). A history of tick bite related to the current episode was documented in 29 (27.1%) patients, and previous symptoms and signs suggestive of LD (other than LA) were reported only in 1 (0.9%) patient, who had a prior rash compatible with erythema migrans.

There was no significant difference between the 2 clinical outcome groups in the synovial WBC counts of patients who underwent joint puncture (for clinically suspected septic arthritis); ie, among those with resolved arthritis, n = 41, 50.6%; and persistent arthritis, n = 9, 34.6% (Table 2).

Borrelia burgdorferi-specific Testing

According to the inclusion criteria, specific antibodies against B. burgdorferi were detected by IgM and/or IgG ELISA, and positivity was confirmed by immunoblot in all LA patients (Supplementary Table 1). The predominant isotype detected by immunoblot confirmation was B. burgdorferi-specific IgG, mainly alone (n = 63, 58.9%) or in combination with IgM (n = 24, 22.4%). Test results by ELISA or immunoblot did not significantly differ between clinical outcome groups.

The serum IgG response among LA patients, as determined by band positivity (band intensity of at least 1+; Supplementary Table 2), was predominantly directed against the B. burgdorferi antigens p100, VlsE, p58, p41, p39, and p18. However, IgG antibodies against OspC were found more frequently in patients with persistent arthritis (n = 11, 61.1%) than in those with resolved arthritis (n = 21, 30.9%) (Supplementary Table 2). Further, IgG concentrations based on the band intensity score (from 0 to 4; Supplementary Table 3) were significantly higher against OspC and lower against p41 in patients with persistent arthritis than in those with resolved arthritis.

A positive B. burgdorferi-specific PCR in synovial fluid following arthrocentesis, performed initially to rule out septic arthritis, was found in 3 out of 7 (42.9%) patients with resolved arthritis and in 4 out of 8 (50.0%) patients with persistent arthritis (Supplementary Table 1).

Treatment and Outcome

Treatment regimens and clinical outcomes are detailed in Table 3. Prior to the diagnosis of LA, 41 (38.3%) patients were empirically treated for suspected septic arthritis with antibiotics, including flucloxacillin in all patients. Patients with resolved arthritis were more likely to receive initial empirical treatment (n = 37, 45.7%) than those with persistent arthritis (n = 4, 15.4%) (P = .0057). Hospitalization occurred more often in patients with resolved arthritis than persistent arthritis (n = 38, 46.9% vs n = 6, 23.1%; P = .04). However, duration of hospitalization did not differ between the outcome groups (Table 3).

The median time between arthritis-related symptom onset and start of LA-specific antibiotic treatment was 13 days (IQR, 9.0–26.0) (Table 3). A treatment delay of >30 days from symptom onset to start of LA-specific antibiotic treatment was observed in 21 (19.6%) patients, with such a treatment delay being significantly less frequent in patients with resolved arthritis (n = 8, 9.9%) compared with those with persistent arthritis (n = 13, 50.0%) (P < .0001). Lyme arthritis-specific antibiotic treatment regimens were comparable between groups, with doxycycline being the most frequently prescribed agent. The median treatment duration was 28 days in both groups, consistent with current treatment guidelines at this time [1, 6, 11, 32]. Four (15.4%) patients with persistent arthritis received ceftriaxone IV as a second antibiotic course (median duration, 15.5 days, IQR, 14.0–19.0) (Table 3). Further, additional treatment in patients with persistent arthritis included intra-articular corticosteroids (n = 23, 88.5%) and systemic corticosteroids (n = 2, 7.7%).

Follow-up beyond 2 months was available for 107 patients, allowing assessment of final clinical status (Table 3). Of those with resolved arthritis within 2 months after completion of a single course of antibiotics (n = 81), 80 (98.8%) patients achieved sustained full recovery. Thereof, 1 (1.2%) patient initially met the criteria for resolution but developed recurrent arthritis in the same joint after an asymptomatic period of 6 months. This patient was subsequently diagnosed with JIA by a rheumatologist. Among patients with persistent arthritis (n = 26), 10 (38.5%) patients achieved complete remission, of which 9 patients received additional intra-articular corticosteroid injection and 1 patient no further treatment (Figure 1). The remaining 16 (61.5%) patients with persistent arthritis, including all 4 patients who received a second course of antibiotics, had ongoing arthritis symptoms. They were referred to the pediatric rheumatologists, and all 16 (100%) patients were later diagnosed with JIA (Table 3 and Supplementary Table 4). Among those patients, 2 (11.5%) were HLA-B27 positive, 1 (3.8%) was tested positive for rheumatoid factor, and 2 (11.5%) demonstrated positive antinuclear antibodies (ANAs). Five (19.2%) patients were treated with DMARDs and 4 (15.4%) patients received biologic agents following nonresponse to intra-articular or systemic corticosteroid treatment (Figure 1). Characteristics of patients subsequently diagnosed with JIA are summarized in Supplementary Table 4, including medical history and clinical findings suggestive of JIA.

DISCUSSION

This study represents, to our knowledge, the largest European pediatric cohort describing the clinical characteristics and outcomes of LA in children. At our tertiary center, children with LA receive a standardized treatment course of antibiotics and are followed by pediatric infectious disease specialists. In our cohort, 24.3% of patients developed persistent arthritis after a single course of standard antibiotic treatment, which is comparable to arthritis persistence rates reported by United States studies following two courses of antibiotic therapy [7, 13, 14]. While current European and United States guidelines suggest considering a second antibiotic course in such cases (weak recommendation) [11, 17], our practice is to refer these patients to pediatric rheumatologists rather than extend antimicrobial treatment. This approach reflects growing evidence that persistent arthritis after standard antibiotic treatment is predominantly immune-mediated rather than due to ongoing B. burgdorferi infection, although residual infection cannot be definitively excluded with current diagnostic methods [16, 32, 33]. In our cohort, all 4 children who received a second course of antibiotic treatment (ceftriaxone IV) in accordance with the mentioned guideline recommendations [11, 17] developed persistent arthritis. Although based on a small number of patients, this finding further supports the current practice at our institution.

In this study, children with persistent arthritis were older at presentation than those with complete resolution, consistent with other pediatric studies indicating that increasing age may be associated with a more prolonged inflammatory response [13, 14, 22, 34, 35]. The most common clinical signs in both groups were joint swelling and pain. Some studies have reported that children with persistent symptoms exhibit less severe pain or a milder clinical presentation than those with rapid resolution [13, 22]. We could not directly confirm this finding in our cohort, as severity of pain and joint swelling were not systematically assessed. Interestingly, children with rapid resolution were more frequently hospitalized and received empirical IV antibiotic therapy because of an initial suspicion of septic arthritis, which may indirectly reflect a more severe initial presentation. Based on the present data, we cannot distinguish between a potential beneficial effect of the empirical treatment modality itself and confounding by indication related to hospital-based management.

Age- and sex-adjusted logistic regression revealed that delays in hospital presentation after symptom onset and LA-specific antibiotic treatment were associated with persistent arthritis. Previous studies have outlined the potential association between delayed antibiotic treatment and persistent or recurrent symptoms in LA and LD overall [30, 36]. Horton et al. [13] described a relationship between longer symptom duration before ED presentation and persistent arthritis after antibiotic treatment. This aspect was not observed in several smaller studies [14, 15, 22, 37]. The same study group led by Horton et al. [13] reported that children with higher levels of inflammatory parameters at clinical presentation had better responses to antibiotic treatment. In our cohort, only lower peripheral monocyte counts were associated with persistent arthritis, after adjustment for age and sex. Animal and in vitro studies suggest that monocytes play a key role in limiting B. burgdorferi dissemination during early infection [38–40]. But the relevance of circulating monocytes in human LA remains unclear due to insufficient prospective clinical data.

Although the differences in B. burgdorferi-specific antibody responses between outcome groups were no longer significant after adjusting for age and sex, the observed pattern remains noteworthy: Children with persistent arthritis more often exhibited OspC-specific IgG antibodies and higher OspC concentrations, alongside lower responses to late antigens, such as p100 and p18. In United States cohorts, where B. burgdorferi sensu stricto is the main genospecies causing LD, strong OspA responses have been associated with antibiotic-refractory arthritis [41]. Considering that B. burgdorferi modulates its surface-lipoprotein expression during infection and that OspC is highly diverse/heterogeneous in European B. afzelii and B. garinii strains [23, 42–44], our findings may potentially reflect strain-specific immunogenicity or differences in the timing and maturation of humoral responses. These interpretations are limited by single serum samples available for this study and its retrospective design, but they underscore the need for longitudinal serological characterization and genotyping of strains to better understand these host-pathogen interactions.

Overall, 84.1% of total patients had recovered, reflecting a generally good prognosis for pediatric LA in this study. Nine patients with persistent arthritis were successfully treated with intra-articular corticosteroid injection, which has been described previously as effective and safe in children with persistent LA [45]. However, 61.5% of children with persistent arthritis did not respond to further courses of antibiotic or anti-inflammatory treatment and were subsequently diagnosed with JIA. Importantly, persistent arthritis in this context may encompass a spectrum of entities, including postinfectious immune-mediated arthritis, ongoing infection-related inflammation, B. burgdorferi-triggered immunopathology or the unmasking of an underlying rheumatic disease such as JIA. Given the overlap in clinical presentation and the absence of definitive biomarkers distinguishing these conditions, potential misclassification cannot be fully excluded in routine clinical practice, and management strategies are often similar [46].

Persistent LA may represent a transitional state between infection-driven inflammation and dysregulated immune responses. In line with this hypothesis, previous studies have shown that persistent LA exhibits similar immune signatures to JIA, suggesting overlapping pathomechanisms including an imbalance between regulatory T cells, and interferon-gamma- and interleukin 17-producing CD4+ effector T-cell subsets (Th1 and Th17 cells, respectively) [16, 47–50]. Further, Dirks et al. [51] identified a disease-associated T-cell receptor motif on clonally expanded synovial effector T cells in adolescents with persistent LA that was absent in B. burgdorferi seronegative JIA and rheumatoid arthritis. This suggests a distinct localized T-cell response in the joints, with potentially different antigen targets compared with other rheumatic diseases.

Although we detected pronounced IgG responses against multiple B. burgdorferi antigens in serum, and also B. burgdorferi DNA in the synovial fluid of some patients—findings altogether strongly indicative of B. burgdorferi infection [6]—distinguishing LA from early JIA remains challenging. This difficulty is further compounded by the relatively high seroprevalence of B. burgdorferi-specific antibodies in endemic regions, which has recently been reported at 11% among healthy children in Switzerland [52]. The observed association between infection-triggered inflammation and the later development of chronic autoimmune arthritis warrants further investigation on more diverse study populations, including children with JIA and other forms of mono- and oligoarthritis.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective, monocentric design with regionally confined patients limits standardization of clinical and laboratory assessments and may introduce selection bias. In addition, no a priori sample size calculation was performed, and the study was therefore not designed to estimate the prevalence of persistent LA with a defined level of precision. Accordingly, the observed proportion should be interpreted as a descriptive estimate within this cohort. Further, comparisons with current literature are complicated by the lack of pediatric-specific treatment guidelines and heterogeneous definitions of persistent arthritis ranging from 2 to 6 months after antibiotic treatment among other studies on children with LA [13, 14, 19, 20, 22, 35].

In conclusion, our study found that over 80% of children with LA showed full resolution after diagnosis and one course of LA-specific antibiotic treatment. It is not possible to reliably predict, based on initial clinical or laboratory findings alone, which children will experience resolution, develop persistent arthritis, or eventually be diagnosed with JIA, in line with conclusions reported in United States pediatric LA cohorts [13, 19, 46]. These results underscore the complexity of persistent (postinfectious) arthritis in children with LA and highlight the need for prospective, longitudinal studies to better identify predictors of persistent arthritis and optimize follow-up and management strategies.
