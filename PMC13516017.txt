Authors: Yew Woon Chia, Sohil Pothiawala, Julian Kenrick Loh, Carrie Kah-Lai Leong, Shir Lynn Lim, Ruth Weixian Chen, Ying Hao Christopher Seet, Zhi-Yan Valerie Ng, Benjamin Sieu-Hon Leong, for the National Post-Cardiac Arrest and Survivorship (PCAS) Workgroup

ABSTRACT
A robust chain of survival is important for achieving positive outcomes following out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, which continues to pose a substantial public health challenge in Singapore. Although improvements have been made in prehospital resuscitation and survival to hospital admission, further progress is required for survival to hospital discharge and neurological recovery. This underscores the urgent need to strengthen post-cardiac arrest care—the sixth link in the chain of survival—to optimise outcomes for patients admitted to the intensive care unit after return of spontaneous circulation. This review builds on earlier recommendations of the National Targeted Temperature Management Workgroup (2017) and the National Post-Cardiac Arrest and Survivorship Workgroup (2021), providing an updated summary of post-cardiac arrest management and practical guidance for clinicians treating resuscitated cardiac arrest patients in the intensive care unit.

INTRODUCTION

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is of significant public health concern, as it occurs abruptly without warning and can result in high mortality and severe disabilities in survivors. According to the Singapore OHCA Registry, the local incidence of OHCA has risen from 26.5 cases per 100,000 population in 2011 to 66.7 cases per 100,000 population in 2021.[1] Although there has been an increase in the rates of bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) from 22.0% to 59.4% and automated external defibrillator application from 1.8% to 9.5% over the same period, survival to hospital admission remains poor, peaking at 19.9%, with a decline observed during the COVID-19 pandemic. Furthermore, among survivors admitted to the hospital, only one-third are discharged alive; within this group, the proportion achieving a favourable neurological outcome has increased—from 52.1% in 2011 to 79.3% in 2021.

Every link in the chain of survival is crucial, and the chain is only as strong as its weakest link. This paper examines the sixth link—post-cardiac arrest care—reviewing the latest evidence for managing patients following return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). This review builds on earlier recommendations of the National Targeted Temperature Management (TTM) Workgroup in 2017, the National Post-Cardiac Arrest and Survivorship (PCAS) Workgroup in 2021, and the recent American Heart Association (AHA), European Resuscitation Council (ERC)/European Society of Intensive Care Medicine (ESICM) 2025 guidelines and International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) Consensus on Science With Treatment Recommendations, to provide an update on post-cardiac arrest management and propose a framework (the ‘A to I’ approach) for implementing a comprehensive bundle of care for resuscitated cardiac arrest patients in the intensive care unit (ICU) [Table 1].[23456]

POST-CARDIAC ARREST SYNDROME

Achieving ROSC is only the first step towards the goal of complete recovery after cardiac arrest. Following a period of global ischaemia during cardiac arrest and reperfusion that occurs during and after resuscitation, there is a series of complex pathophysiological changes, known as the post-cardiac arrest syndrome (PCAS).[7] It comprises four major components: post-cardiac arrest brain injury, post-cardiac arrest myocardial dysfunction, systemic ischaemia–reperfusion response and persistent precipitating pathology. The optimal care of PCAS requires a coordinated management of multiple organ systems and aims to mitigate the severity of hypoxic-ischaemic brain injury (HIBI), which is the leading cause of death and severe disability in patients who survive an initial cardiac arrest. Treatments to optimise ventilatory, haemodynamic and metabolic variables, together with temperature control, should be initiated as soon as possible.[38]

Once ROSC is achieved, factors contributing to the cardiac arrest should be identified early to enable appropriate intervention and to resolve the persistent precipitating pathology. A good history of the events surrounding the collapse, a detailed physical examination, and investigations such as a 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG), point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS), transthoracic echocardiography and computed tomography (CT) are used to rapidly determine the cause.

Cardiac disease, particularly ischaemic heart disease, is the most common cause of OHCA in the adult population.[9] A 12-lead ECG should be performed as soon as possible after ROSC to diagnose ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), and an immediate coronary angiography should be arranged for patients with confirmed STEMI, or haemodynamic and/or electrical instability, suspicious of a coronary artery occlusion.[10] Echocardiography or POCUS are useful for identifying both cardiac and extra-cardiac aetiologies, including cardiac regional wall motion abnormalities suggestive of myocardial ischaemia/infarction, other cardiomyopathies, pericardial effusion/tamponade, right ventricular dilation suggestive of pulmonary embolism, aortic root dilation suggestive of aortic dissection and hypovolaemia.

The ERC/ESICM 2025 guidelines suggest that a full-body head-to-pelvis CT including a brain CT and CT pulmonary angiogram, should be considered when there is no evidence of a cardiac cause.[5] Computed tomography can help to diagnose extra-cardiac causes of cardiac arrest, including pulmonary embolism, intracranial haemorrhage, thromboembolic stroke, aortic dissection and pneumothorax. Additionally, CT can help identify complications of resuscitation, such as rib or sternal fractures, aspiration, pulmonary contusions, pneumothorax and haemorrhage, to guide subsequent medical interventions.

Some causes of cardiac arrest, particularly metabolic disturbances such as hyperkalaemia, hypokalaemia and hypercalcaemia, may become apparent during resuscitation of the patient, especially while evaluating the 5Hs and 5Ts of cardiac arrest, or during ECG or point-of-care blood gas analysis. Appropriate treatment to correct these metabolic abnormalities must be initiated in tandem with other supportive measures. Sepsis is also one of the common causes of cardiovascular collapse, and once suspected, protocolised resuscitation using sepsis-care bundles and administration of appropriate antibiotics have been shown to reduce mortality. Cardiotoxic drugs such as tricyclic antidepressants, cardiac glycosides and recreational drugs are the main pharmacologic agents that can cause cardiac arrest. Identifying the drug involved is usually the main challenge, but if the cardiotoxic agent is known, targeted treatment such as antidotes or the use of haemodialysis for rapid removal, may be administered.[8]

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest is most commonly caused by acute coronary syndrome in patients with sustained ROSC who survive to ICU admission.[9] The importance of prioritising time to intervention is established in STEMI and acute myocardial infarction-related cardiogenic shock. In the presence of persistent ST-segment elevation (STE) post-cardiac arrest, emergent coronary angiography is recommended, mirroring global recommendations for all patients with STEMI.[45610]

The proportion of OHCA patients without STE is much larger and includes extra-cardiac causes of cardiac arrest. Around 60% of patients in this cohort still have significant coronary artery disease, but importantly, only 15%–40% are estimated to have a culprit occlusion requiring revascularisation.[1112] A trial-level meta-analysis comprising 1590 patients across six randomised trials with OHCA without STE suggests no benefit in an early invasive approach over delayed or selective angiography with respect to all-cause mortality.[12] Importantly, the trials excluded patients with haemodynamic and/or electrical instability, where early coronary angiography is likely beneficial and remains the default approach.

A significant confounder in assessing cardiac arrest patients is the competing mortality from neurological causes, for which HIBI is the most common cause of death in randomised control trials (RCTs).[13] Poor outcome due to brain injury may mitigate and confound the potential benefit of coronary revascularisation in this population.[14] This may be addressed in the upcoming DISCO-no-COMA (Direct or Subacute Coronary Angiography in Patients with Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest without Coma) trial. Major guidelines recommend that early or delayed coronary angiography is reasonable in OHCA patients in the absence of STE, haemodynamic and/or electrical instability.[456]

After ROSC, airway and ventilatory support should be guided by neurological status and adequacy of spontaneous breathing. Patients with brief arrests who promptly recover and breathe adequately may not require advanced airway support or mechanical ventilation. Those who remain comatose or require sedation and controlled ventilation should be intubated by experienced operators with waveform capnography confirmation, using a supraglottic airway or basic airway measures as a bridge if expertise is unavailable, while employing a physiologically difficult airway approach.[5]

Immediately after ROSC, 100% or the highest available fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) should be administered until oxygenation can be reliably assessed by peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) or partial pressure of arterial oxygen (PaO2). Thereafter, in line with the AHA and ERC/ESICM guidelines, FiO2 should be promptly titrated to maintain SpO2 94%–98% or PaO2 75–100 mmHg, given the potential harm associated with both hypoxaemia and hyperoxaemia.[45]

The Reduction of Oxygen After Cardiac Arrest (EXACT) trial demonstrated more hypoxaemic episodes with a lower early oxygen saturation target of SpO2 90%–94%, whereas the Blood Pressure and Oxygenation Targets after Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest (BOX) trial did not show clinical benefit from a lower PaO2 target of 68–75 mmHg in comatose post-cardiac arrest patients.[1516] On the other hand, secondary analysis of the Targeted Temperature Management-2 (TTM-2) trial demonstrated an association between early severe hyperoxaemia (PaO2 >245 mmHg) and poor functional outcome in patients with OHCA.[17] When pulse oximetry is used, clinicians should recognise the risk of occult hypoxaemia in patients with darker skin pigmentation and the reduced reliability of pulse oximetry in a low cardiac output state.[18]

Carbon dioxide is a well-known regulator of cerebrovascular tone and consequently cerebral blood flow. Increased partial pressure of arterial carbon dioxide (PaCO2), that is, hypercapnia, increases cerebral blood flow and cerebral oxygenation but at a risk of increasing cerebral blood volume and intracranial pressure (ICP).[192021] Hypocapnia, by contrast, induces cerebral vasoconstriction, which may reduce cerebral blood flow and cause cerebral ischaemia and secondary brain injury.[22]

Although observational and phase II studies had shown promising results for targeted therapeutic mild hypercapnia in improving cerebral oxygenation, the TAME (Targeted Therapeutic Mild Hypercapnia after Resuscitated Cardiac Arrest) trial found no improvement in neurological outcome with mild hypercapnia compared with normocapnia, and protocol discontinuation was more frequent in the hypercapnia group because of protocol-defined acidaemia.[2324] Therefore, ventilation after ROSC should target normocapnia with a PaCO2 of 35–45 mmHg.[456]

Ventilatory adjustment in mechanically ventilated patients should be guided by PaCO2. End-tidal CO2 (ETCO2) is useful for confirming airway device function and for trend monitoring, but should not be used in isolation to infer PaCO2 because the arterial to ETCO2 gradient may be substantial following cardiac arrest.[25] In patients with spontaneous hypothermia or on hypothermic temperature control, PaCO2 should be checked frequently, and local practice should apply either temperature-corrected or uncorrected blood gas analysis consistently.[26]

Mechanical ventilation after ROSC should follow a lung-protective strategy, with the ERC/ESICM guidelines specifying a tidal volume of 6–8 mL/kg ideal body weight.[5] This approach is supported by limited post-cardiac arrest observational data, in which lower tidal volume ventilation was associated with better neurocognitive outcome in an OHCA cohort, although a separate in-hospital cardiac arrest cohort did not demonstrate a clear association between tidal volume and neurological outcome.[2728] Taken together, these data support a conservative lung-protective approach, whereas further post-cardiac arrest evidence remains limited. As per general neurointensive care principles, positive end expiratory pressure should be limited to <10 cmH2O and patients should be nursed 30° head-up to avoid an increase in ICP after cardiac arrest.[29]

C (CIRCULATION)

Haemodynamic monitoring

We suggest cardiac output monitoring if the patient is in shock and resources are available.

Post-cardiac arrest myocardial dysfunction and low cardiac output may occur in up to two-thirds of post-cardiac arrest patients.[3031] All patients should have an intra-arterial catheter for continuous blood pressure monitoring. In haemodynamically unstable patients, cardiac output monitoring—using pulmonary artery catheter, transpulmonary thermodilution or non-invasive methods—is reasonable to guide therapy. Echocardiography should be performed as early as possible to detect underlying cardiac pathology, quantify myocardial dysfunction and guide fluid and vasopressor management. Fluid responsiveness should be assessed with dynamic indices (e.g. pulse pressure variation, passive leg raise) before fluid administration.[3] Additional monitoring of central venous oxygen saturation (ScvO2), central venous-arterial CO2 gap (Pcv–aCO2 gap) and lactate clearance provide useful insights into the adequacy of cardiac output and tissue oxygen delivery.

Hypotension should be actively avoided after ROSC. A minimum mean arterial pressure (MAP) of 65 mmHg is recommended, aligning post-cardiac arrest target with those used in cardiogenic shock and sepsis.

Four RCTs enrolling more than 1000 patients compared lower MAP targets (ranging from 63 mmHg to 75 mmHg across trials) with higher targets (ranging from 72 mmHg to 100 mmHg); none demonstrated benefit for survival or neurological outcome with higher MAP targets, but higher targets were also not associated with significant risks such as recurrent cardiac arrest or cardiac arrhythmias.[32]

A recent scientific statement from the AHA and the Neurocritical Care Society recommends targeting a MAP higher than 80 mmHg in post-cardiac arrest patients.[33] This was based on the physiological rationale that cerebral blood flow (CBF) may be inadequate after cardiac arrest. If the ICP is elevated, cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) will be compromised because MAP is the primary determinant of CPP. Additionally, CBF autoregulation is impaired, or the lower limit may be right-shifted.[343536] This means that at lower MAP values, CBF may be MAP-dependent, and the patient is at increased risk of cerebral hypoperfusion.

Therefore, a higher MAP target may be appropriate in patients with known chronic hypertension, raised ICP (e.g. optic nerve sheath diameter >5 mm on ocular ultrasound or on neuroimaging), or signs of tissue hypoperfusion, supporting the concept of individualised haemodynamic optimisation.[3637]

The use of near-infrared spectroscopy-derived cerebral oxygen saturation or ICP to examine the presence of cerebral autoregulation and determine an optimal MAP may also enable a more individualised approach.[38] Although these are observational findings, they indicate that optimal MAP targets may need to be individualised and support further research to identify optimal targets for individual cardiac arrest survivors receiving intensive care.

During hypothermic temperature control, the normal physiological response is bradycardia, which is associated with good neurological outcome.[3940] A similar association between bradycardia and improved long-term outcome has been shown in patients not treated with hypothermic temperature control.[41] Therefore, ERC/ESICM recommends that bradycardia (heart rate <30–40/min) be left untreated if there are no signs of tissue hypoperfusion.[5]

In post-cardiac arrest patients, systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) is a frequent complication, often resulting in severe vasodilation and vasoplegia. In these situations, inotropic support should be initiated only after MAP has been optimised with fluids and vasopressors.[30]

There is insufficient evidence to recommend any specific vasopressor after cardiac arrest. The only relevant RCT (n = 40, noradrenaline compared with adrenaline) showed identical 30-day mortality of 90% in both groups, and observational studies have yielded inconsistent results.[42] Although noradrenaline is the preferred first-line vasopressor by expert consensus, supported by extrapolation from cardiogenic shock and sepsis data, ILCOR did not issue a vasopressor-specific recommendation because of insufficient direct evidence. Observational evidence suggests that dopamine combined with noradrenaline or adrenaline may increase mortality, and dopamine alone is associated with higher 28-day mortality in cardiogenic shock compared with noradrenaline.

Post-resuscitation myocardial dysfunction (as confirmed by echocardiography and/or haemodynamic monitoring) frequently co-exists with vasoplegia; if low cardiac output predominates (low ScvO2, elevated Pcv-aCO2 gap or rising lactate), low-dose dobutamine (3–5 mcg/kg/min) could be added.[3043]

Routine mechanical circulatory support (MCS) is not recommended for cardiogenic shock after cardiac arrest. A pooled analysis of 14 randomised trials found no survival benefit from early routine MCS compared with standard care in cardiogenic shock patients, regardless of whether cardiac arrest had occurred.[6] This is consistent across international guidelines.

Mechanical circulatory support (intra-aortic balloon pump, microaxial flow pump or veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation) may be considered in highly selected patients, particularly those with Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) >8 (i.e. conscious on arrival), STEMI-related cardiogenic shock, and short arrest duration (<10 min)—features suggesting limited HIBI. An individual patient data meta-analysis of nine RCTs showed that this subgroup (without high risk of brain injury) derived the greatest potential benefit from MCS.[44] For patients at high risk of brain injury, the potential benefits of MCS may be diminished.

The decision to initiate MCS should be made collectively by a multidisciplinary cardiogenic shock team comprising a cardiac intensivist, interventional cardiologist, heart failure specialist and cardiothoracic surgeon. Neurological prognosis is the dominant determinant of overall outcome; in patients at high risk of severe HIBI, the cardiac benefit of MCS is outweighed by the burden of device complications. However, early GCS should not be used in isolation to exclude patients from MCS consideration, as some comatose patients may achieve meaningful neurological recovery.

Seizures and epileptiform activity occur in 10%–35% of comatose post-cardiac arrest patients and contribute to secondary brain injury through neuronal hypermetabolism. Both AHA and ERC/ESICM recommend a prompt electroencephalogram (EEG) in all patients who do not follow commands after ROSC, to detect electrographic seizures—including non-convulsive status epilepticus—and monitor effects of treatment. Myoclonus is the most common motor manifestation of HIBI (18%–34% of patients) and requires an EEG to distinguish cortical from subcortical origin before treatment decisions are made. Suppressing myoclonus in the absence of an EEG correlate is not recommended.[45] When myoclonus occurs with a benign EEG background (e.g. continuous, reactive pattern), a wake-up trial should be attempted within days of arrest.

Shivering during hypothermic temperature control closely mimics seizure activity clinically. Any new or worsening motor activity in a cooled patient should prompt urgent EEG assessment before antiseizure treatment is started.

Temperature target

We recommend targeting a temperature between 32°C and 37.5°C for patients who are unresponsive to verbal commands after ROSC from cardiac arrest. Whether subpopulations of cardiac arrest patients may benefit from hypothermic temperature control at 32–34°C remains uncertain. Comatose patients with spontaneous mild hypothermia after ROSC should not be actively warmed to achieve normothermia.

Many animal models and earlier clinical studies have suggested a benefit for hypothermic temperature control after resuscitation from cardiac arrest. A large meta-analysis of 45 experimental studies showed a strong and consistent beneficial effect of hypothermia as compared with normothermia on neurological outcome after cardiac arrest, and that faster cooling rates were associated with increasing effect size.[51] However, in these studies, the target temperature had generally been achieved in less than 1 h.[5152] The failure to translate these findings into positive outcomes in clinical trials may reflect the 5–8 h generally taken to achieve target temperature in the clinical setting.[5354] This delay in cooling could have lowered the beneficial effects of hypothermia.

It is also important to note that patients who suffer cardiac arrest in Singapore may have differences in important characteristics such as arrest aetiology, initial rhythm, witness status, bystander CPR and downtime compared with the landmark studies. For example, in the TTM-2 trial, 91% of the cardiac arrests were witnessed (~50% in Singapore), 80% of patients had bystander CPR (~60% in Singapore), 74% of patients had a shockable rhythm (~15% in Singapore), and the median duration from cardiac arrest to sustained ROSC was 25 min.[55] These differences suggest that patients enrolled in the trial might have had less HIBI and, hence, experienced less benefit with a hypothermic temperature control strategy.

Differing opinions remain regarding the role for hypothermic temperature control in comatose post-cardiac arrest patients and whether there may be subpopulations that might benefit from mild hypothermia, such as those who are more brain-injured. Some observational studies suggest a benefit with temperature control to lower targets for patients with higher illness and brain injury severity.[565758] Regardless, the rapid infusion of large amounts of cold fluids immediately after ROSC in the prehospital setting is harmful and should not be done.[59]

Temperature control devices

It is recommended to continuously monitor core body temperature using either an oesophageal or urinary bladder temperature probe, and use a temperature control device with thermoregulatory feedback based on continuous temperature monitoring to maintain core body temperature within the target range during temperature control. It is worth noting that in the TTM-2 trial, 46% of patients randomised to normothermic temperature control required a device to maintain normothermia.[55] Although intravascular cooling has been shown to achieve target temperature faster during induction, result in less temperature fluctuations during maintenance and have a lower incidence of post-temperature control fever than surface cooling, there are no clear differences in survival or survival with good neurological outcome between the two techniques. Therefore, reviews are mixed on whether intravascular or surface cooling is preferable.[6061626364]

Duration of temperature control

There is a range of temperature control durations (from 36 h to 72 h), which is supported by data and expert opinions. One trial showed no difference between 24 h and 48 h of hypothermia, although the study may have been underpowered and effect estimates favoured longer temperature control duration.[65] Another trial found no difference between 12–24 h and 36 h of hypothermia.[66] In a recent RCT, there was no difference in outcomes comparing a period of device-based fever prevention of 12 h vs. 48 h after an initial 24-h period of normothermic temperature control (i.e. total 36 h vs.72 h of temperature control).[67] Currently, guidelines recommend a total duration of temperature control and fever prevention of at least 36 h after ROSC. Importantly, if hypothermic temperature control is used, rewarming should be delayed if patient is haemodynamically unstable, has raised ICP, severe hyperkalaemia or ongoing seizures.

Balanced crystalloids (e.g. lactated Ringer’s solution, Plasma-Lyte A) are the preferred fluids in post-cardiac arrest patients. They are associated with faster correction of base deficit and lower rates of hyperchloraemia compared to 0.9% sodium chloride.[7475] However, in the subset of patients with confirmed severe cerebral oedema, 0.9% sodium chloride may be preferred. This is extrapolated from the traumatic brain injury subgroup of the BEST-Living (Balanced Versus Saline Trialists: Living Systematic Review and Meta-analysis) meta-analysis, in which balanced crystalloids were associated with higher in-hospital mortality.[76] Hypotonic fluids are contraindicated in post-cardiac arrest patients, given their propensity to worsen cerebral oedema.[77]

Fluid administration should be guided by the adequacy of haemodynamic optimisation, as reflected by improvements in lactate and other markers of tissue perfusion. Restrictive fluid administration guided by assessment of fluid responsiveness, measurement of filling pressures, and cardiac output may optimise clinical outcomes. Rapid prehospital infusion of large volumes of cold fluids is harmful and not recommended.[59]

Post-cardiac arrest brain injury is the leading cause of death among patients who achieve ROSC following cardiac arrest.[92] In most of the cases, these deaths occur as a direct consequence of HIBI, which results in an irreversible loss of brain function, that is, brain death.[93] Most neurological deaths following resuscitated cardiac arrest result from active withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy (WLST) in patients for whom the severity of HIBI indicates that survival with a poor neurological outcome is very likely.[949596] Accurate prognostication is, therefore, essential to avoid inappropriate WLST in patients who may still have a chance of a neurologically meaningful recovery and to avoid futile treatment in patients with a severe and irreversible brain injury.

Local and international guidelines, including the AHA and ERC/ESICM, emphasise that neuroprognostication should be timely, structured, and multimodal, integrating clinical examination with electrophysiology, biomarkers and neuroimaging.[3456] No single predictor is 100% accurate, and a single test should not be used in isolation because of the risk of falsely pessimistic prediction. We recommend using a multimodal neuroprognostication strategy to reduce false-positive rates of poor outcome and avoid the risk of inappropriate WLST.

Prognostic assessment should be deferred until at least 72 h after ROSC, or 72 h after completion of rewarming in patients treated with hypothermic temperature control. Before prognostic evaluation is undertaken, clinicians should ensure that major confounders such as sedative and paralytic medications, electrolyte abnormalities, hypoglycaemia and severe acid-base disorders, are corrected. Haemodynamic instability and ongoing hypoxia may also impair neurological assessment and should be addressed.

Formal neuroprognostication should be undertaken in post-cardiac arrest patients who have not awakened and regained the ability to obey commands (i.e. motor score <6) at ≥72 h after ROSC (or after rewarming from hypothermic temperature control) and confounders excluded.[97] The absence of pupillary and corneal reflexes at ≥72 h after ROSC; bilaterally absent N20 short-latency somatosensory evoked potentials at ≥24 h; highly malignant EEG (defined as suppression or burst-suppression with or without superimposed periodic discharges) at ≥24 h; neuron-specific enolase (NSE) >60 mcg/L at 48 h and/or 72 h; status myoclonus (defined as continuous and generalised myoclonus persisting for ≥30 min) within 72 h; diffuse and extensive hypoxic-ischaemic injury on brain CT at any time or on MRI at 2–7 days after ROSC, are all predictors of poor neurological outcome, defined as Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) 3–5.[9899100101102103104] To fulfil the criteria for poor outcome, two or more of the above predictors need to be present.

If no unfavourable signs are noted and features of favourable signs are present, such as GCS motor score of 4 or 5 at 72–96 h; continuous normal voltage EEG background within 72 h; normal NSE values (≤17 mcg/L) at 24–72 h after ROSC; or no diffusion changes on MRI at 2–7 days, good neurological outcome is likely.[105] A recent analysis of four prospective multicentre studies showed that in patients with a GCS motor score ≤5, with the presence of at least one favourable and no unfavourable signs, more than 60% had a good functional outcome (CPC 1–2) at 6 months.[105] In situations where neither favourable nor unfavourable signs are present, the outcome is likely to be indeterminate. Further observation and serial assessments must be carried out to detect recovery.[106]

Emerging biomarkers such as neurofilament light (NfL), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and S100B have also been evaluated as biomarkers for neuroprognostication. In the TTM-2 sub-study (n = 819), NfL achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) curve of 0.92–0.93 at 24–48 h, with 98% specificity for poor outcome and 93–96% negative predictive value for good outcome at 24 h—demonstrating that accurate neuroprognostication is feasible as early as 24 h post-ROSC, well before the current 72-h standard.[107] The AHA 2025 guidelines recognise NfL as a potential supplementary biomarker within a multimodal framework, whereas the ERC/ESICM 2025 guidelines have not yet endorsed routine clinical use pending validated assays and standardised thresholds. The GFAP, a marker of astrocytic injury, also shows early prognostic utility (AUROC of 0.87 at 24–48 h) and may further enhance accuracy when combined with NfL.[107]

In summary, predicting neurological outcome after cardiac arrest requires a multimodal approach involving serial clinical examination (including automated pupillometry, which shows high specificity for predicting poor neurological outcome), electrophysiology, serum biomarkers and imaging to achieve improved prognostic certainty for both good and poor outcomes.[108] In situations where indeterminate outcomes are obtained, both clinicians and caregivers should have further discussions regarding goals and trajectory of care to avoid premature WLST.

Post-cardiac arrest rehabilitation (PCAR) has been recently included as the final link in the chain of survival, and is essential to quality survivorship and meaningful recovery.[109110] It requires a structured, individualised, multidisciplinary approach that focuses on four key aspects of recovery: restoration of physical function, addressing associated cognitive impairment, managing psycho-emotional trauma and reintegration back into community living. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Joshi et al.[111] suggested that there are improvements in physical endurance and cognitive response with the implementation of rehabilitative measures after cardiac arrest. These findings are consistent with those reported in two other studies.[112113]

There is limited evidence on how PCAR should be carried out, although Salwa et al. have suggested four phases of rehabilitation: acute, early, functional and long-term.[110] In the acute phase (first 72 h post-event), recommendations are to incorporate low-intensity therapy such as passive range-of-motion exercises and upright positioning. Between 72 h and up to a week post-event is classified as the early phase, and they recommend introducing transfers from bed to chair, core strengthening, maintaining an upright posture, and low-resistance strengthening to reduce the risk of critical illness myopathy.[111]

In the functional phase of PCAR (>1 week post-event), the emphasis shifts to motor strength recovery, early mobilisation, gait retraining, delirium management and continued documentation by the care team and family members of the ongoing events to assist with areas of cognitive impairment. Assessments using the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU or the Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist are recommended by AHA and ERC/ESICM to better manage delirium through early detection and recognition. Extrapolated from rehabilitation issues and outcomes in post-intensive care syndrome, as well as the SCARF (Survivors of Cardiac Arrest Focused on Fatigue) study by Joshi et al., it is believed that early mobilisation, coupled with functional or resistance exercise within 72 h of ICU admission, may reduce the duration on ventilatory support, hospital length of stay, loss of muscle strength and assist with delirium prophylaxis.[112113]

As for the long-term outpatient phase, the ROCK (Rehabilitation Intervention for Sudden Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest Survivors Focusing on Return to Work) trial by Christensen et al. identified issues with cognitive fatigue, cognitive impairment and psycho-emotional issues, hindering progress and return to work.[114] Therefore, in this phase, rehabilitation strategies prioritise relieving cognitive fatigue, overcoming cognitive impairments through neurocognitive training and tackling emotional resilience through support networks for both patients and caregivers.[115]

Both AHA and ERC/ESICM recommend structured assessments in four aspects: physical deficits, cognitive deficits, fatigue and emotional distress. In the local context, we commonly use the EuroQoL (EQ-5D-5L) to assess physical impairment, the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) to assess the degree of cognitive impairment, the Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) to assess fatigue, and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) to assess the severity of anxiety or depression.[116117118119120] Recommendations from AHA and ERC/ESICM suggest the use of additional measures in each of the domains to assess the depth of deficits and impact on patients. In addition to the EQ-5D-5L, they have recommended structured questions about physical activity to further assess physical impairments. For cognitive impairment, they have recommended including the Symbol Digit Modalities Test with MoCA.[118] In the fatigue assessment category, they have recommended performing FSS and the Modified Impact Scale. In terms of emotional and psychosocial impact, they have recommended the use of either the Impact of Events Scale-revised or the Post-Traumatic Stress Disorders Checklist for DSM-5 on top of HADS.[121]

Specific domains during PCAR would include cardiac and neurocognitive rehabilitation, as well as routine physical rehabilitation for musculoskeletal strengthening. Multiple studies have confirmed that the majority of cardiac arrest survivors would benefit from cardiac rehabilitation, and exercise-based rehabilitation is safe for survivors. Even with moderate-to-high-intensity exercise training, it has not been associated with any serious adverse events in patients with cardiovascular diseases.[111112122] Cardiac rehabilitation has been shown to reduce mortality from cardiovascular causes, readmission rates and recurrence of cardiac events.[122]

Cardiac arrest survivors also tend to have more prevalent losses in memory, attention and executive functioning.[123124125] Both AHA and ERC/ESICM advocate for a stint of inpatient rehabilitation for cardiac arrest survivors, and any small improvements gained from a specialised inpatient brain injury rehabilitation unit would be beneficial and reduce care burden for both the family and society.[111126]

In short, the addition of ‘recovery and survivorship’ as the final link in the chain of survival reflects an important evolution in post-cardiac arrest care. Success is no longer measured solely by survival to hospital discharge, but by the quality of survival. We need to focus on holistic rehabilitation across physical, cognitive and psychological domains, with the ultimate aim of enabling cardiac arrest survivors to regain independence and return to their previous level of functioning.

CONCLUSION

Post-cardiac arrest care constitutes a vital element in the chain of survival and requires a comprehensive, systematic, multidisciplinary approach to optimise neurologically intact survival. The quality of ICU management following resuscitation significantly affects overall outcomes. Patients who achieve successful resuscitation from cardiac arrest should be provided with the highest standard of care to enhance their prospects for a favourable long-term recovery.
