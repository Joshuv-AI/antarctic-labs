Suicide and Self-Harm Risk in Young People at Clinical High Risk of Psychosis: Exploring Comorbid Risk Factors and Potential Points for Intervention
Authors: Samuel McKay, Jo Robinson, Patrick McGorry, Cassandra Wannan, Melissa Kerr, Paul Amminger, Hok Pan Yuen, Jessica Spark, Nicky Wallis, Andrea Polari, Barnaby Nelson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuicide is a leading cause of death among young people, and those at clinical high risk (CHR) of psychosis face increased suicide risk. Despite recommendations for routine suicide monitoring in CHR individuals, identifying effective intervention points remains challenging. This study investigates clinical correlates of suicidality in CHR youth, with particular focus on depression severity and DSM-5 diagnoses.HypothesesWe expected suicidality to be positively associated with depressive symptoms, psychiatric symptoms, and common comorbid DSM-5 diagnoses, particularly depression and substance use disorders, and negatively associated with social and occupational functioning.Study DesignCross-sectional data were drawn from the baseline assessment in the Staged Treatment in Early Psychosis (STEP) trial. Participants were 342 CHR young people (age M = 17.7, SD = 3.1, 57.9% female and 42.1% male). Measures included psychosis risk, suicidal ideation, and self-harm, psychiatric symptoms, functioning, depression, and DSM-5 disorders (SCID).Study ResultsSeventy-seven percent of participants reported lifetime thoughts of suicide or engaging in self-harm. While there were positive relationships between suicidality and severity of attenuated psychiatric symptoms, multivariable analyses showed that only depression, particularly moderate to severe depression, was associated with suicidality when controlling for other clinical factors and diagnoses. This pattern remained consistent across sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsSuicidality is highly prevalent among CHR young people. This study shows that depression severity, particularly severe and ongoing depression, should be a key consideration in monitoring and treating suicide risk within CHR services.

Introduction

Individuals at clinical high risk (CHR) of psychosis, characterized by subthreshold psychotic symptoms or other indicators of elevated risk for psychotic disorder, are at greater risk of self-harm and suicide than the general population.1 Meta-analytic evidence indicates that suicidality is highly prevalent among young people at CHR for psychosis, with more than half reporting lifetime suicidal ideation and substantial proportions reporting self-harm and suicide attempts.2,3 Recent evidence suggests that CHR children and adolescents present specific profiles of suicidal thoughts and behaviors and, in some cases, greater suicidality than their peers experiencing first-episode psychosis.4–6 Given the high rates of suicidality in CHR young people and findings suggesting that self-harm is a risk marker for later psychotic disorders,7 there is an increasing focus on the predictors and correlates of suicidality that could be the target of intervention.

Spanning all ages, existing evidence shows that a lifetime history of suicide attempts,8,9 greater psychiatric comorbidity,8,10 increased psychotic symptoms,11 low self-esteem,11 substance use,9 impaired functioning,8,9,12 and some specific disorders and associated symptoms, including depression,13–15 anhedonia,16 and suspiciousness,1 are related to suicidality in CHR populations. A growing number of studies have examined suicidality specifically in adolescents and young people at CHR for psychosis, reporting elevated rates of suicidal ideation, self-harm, and suicide attempts.3,8–14,16–19 While recent larger studies have identified associations between suicidality and a range of clinical features including depressive symptoms, comorbid diagnoses, trauma and impaired functioning,18,20 and depression severity has emerged as a key psychopathological correlate,21 many studies have relied on relatively small samples and have typically focused on single predictors or broad symptom domains rather than the relative contribution of multiple clinical factors simultaneously.3,17,19 This remains an important limitation, as suicide is a leading cause of death among young people, a group characterized by distinct, age-specific risk factors for suicide.22 Additionally, while one study evaluated the full spectrum of potential comorbid diagnoses,8 it focused on a composite count of comorbid conditions instead of examining the individual diagnoses that might be linked to heightened suicidality. As a result, it remains unclear whether the severity of depressive symptoms or specific DSM-5 diagnoses is differentially associated with suicidality in CHR youth when considered alongside other clinical factors. This is particularly important given that comorbid mental health conditions are common in CHR populations,23 and specific disorders, such as affective conditions, are the strongest predictors of suicide risk in young people.24 Examining depression severity and specific diagnostic categories within multivariable models may therefore provide important insights into the clinical drivers of suicidality in CHR youth.

Identifying the mental health disorders most strongly related to suicidality in CHR youth is crucial for pinpointing intervention opportunities to lower suicide risk in this vulnerable group.11,16 This is particularly relevant since the risk of suicide can be more immediate than the risk of psychosis at first contact with psychiatric services1,15 and early intervention may lead to reduced risks of mortality25 and transition to psychosis,26 along with supporting better mental health outcomes generally.27 A comprehensive understanding of the primary risk factors for suicide in this population could inform both the assessment of suicide risk and the development of effective short- and long-term intervention strategies.

Considering this background, the aim of this study was to examine the clinical correlates of lifetime suicidality in a large CHR sample of young people. Specifically, we investigated whether depressive symptoms, psychotic symptoms, and common comorbid DSM-5 diagnoses were associated with suicidality and whether social and occupational functioning was inversely related to suicide risk. Based on previous research, we expected suicidality to be positively associated with depressive symptoms and psychiatric symptoms, and negatively associated with social and occupational functioning. We also anticipated that diagnoses of depression and substance use would be positively associated with suicidality.

Methods

Procedure

The data from this study were drawn from a larger randomized trial registered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12616000098437) and clinicaltrials.gov (NCT02751632). The trial methods, baseline demographic data,28 and outcomes26 have also been published previously. The study was approved by the Melbourne Health and Human Research Ethics Committee. The present study analysed baseline data from the cohort. Baseline CAARMS assessments capture lifetime suicidal thoughts and behaviors up to study entry, whereas follow-up assessments capture events occurring since the previous assessment. The current analysis therefore focuses on lifetime suicidality at study entry.

Participants

Participants were 342 CHR young people (aged 12-25 M = 17.7 SD = 3.1, 57.9% female; 85.4% APS, 0.3% BLIPS, 2.6% TRAIT, or combination 11.8%) recruited through the PACE clinic and 4 headspace centres (Craigieburn, Glenroy, Sunshine, Werribee) located in Melbourne, Australia. Help-seeking young people attending the recruitment clinics were eligible to participate if all of the following inclusion criteria were met: (1) aged between 12 and 25; (2) ability to speak adequate English; (3) ability to provide informed consent; and (4) meeting CHR criteria. Exclusion criteria were: (1) past history of a psychotic episode of 1 week or longer; (2) attenuated psychotic symptoms only present during acute intoxication; (3) organic brain disease known to cause psychotic symptoms; (4) any metabolic, endocrine, or other physical illness; (5) diagnosis of a serious developmental disorder; and (6) documented history of developmental delay or intellectual disability. Participants taking antidepressant or antipsychotic medication at screening were evaluated for the clinical rationale of the prescription. Individuals were excluded if the medication could not be safely discontinued at study entry.

Measures

This was part of a larger trial, and the full list of study measures can be found here28; only the relevant measures for this study are reported below.

Structured Clinical Interview for the DSM-5

The Structured Clinical Interview for the DSM-5 (SCID-5) is a semi-structured interview designed to identify major DSM-5 disorder diagnoses, aligning with the classification and diagnostic criteria set by the American Psychiatric Association (2013).29

Comprehensive Assessment of At-Risk Mental States

The Comprehensive Assessment of At-Risk Mental States (CAARMS) is a semi-structured assessment tool to identify young people at ultra-high risk of developing psychosis.30 CAARMS has subscales for disorders of thought content, non-bizarre ideas, perceptual abnormalities, and disorganized speech, including a measure of lifetime suicidal ideation and self-harm (7.3), which receive a global “severity” rating and a frequency rating. The severity score ranges from 0 (“never, absent”) to 6 (‘psychotic and severe); the frequency score ranges from 0 (absent) to 6 (continuous). The CAARMS was used to identify CHR young people. Lifetime suicidality in the form of suicidal ideation and self-harm from the CAARMS was calculated by multiplying the severity and frequency score (range: 0-36) with higher scores representing greater suicidality.

Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale

The Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS) is a scale utilized by clinicians for assessing psychiatric symptoms.31 It encompasses 24 items, each scored on a scale ranging from 1 (not present) to 7 (extremely severe), with the highest possible total score being 168.

Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale

The Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) is a 10-item (scored 0-6) questionnaire used to measure the severity of depressive episodes.32 A higher score indicates more severe depression, with a maximum score of 60. The following cut-off points have been established: symptoms absent (0-6); mild depression (7-19); moderate depression (20-34); and severe depression (>34).33

Social and Occupational Functioning Assessment Scale

The Social and Occupational Functioning Assessment Scale (SOFAS), a scale ranging from 1 to 100, is employed to subjectively assess an individual’s social, occupational, and psychological functioning.34 It specifically concentrates on the person’s social and occupational performance levels, independent of the severity of their clinical symptoms. A greater score on this scale denotes higher functioning.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed in the R for Mac, version 4.5.2. There was a small amount of missing data (3.8% CAARMS suicidality intensity, 4.4% CAARMS suicidality frequency, 1.8% MADRS, and 0.6% BPRS). Multiple imputation (MI)35 using the MICE package with random forest estimation was used to model the missing data with 100 MI datasets, which is appropriate to manage biased estimates.36 All subsequent analyses were conducted on the MI dataset, and results were compared to those produced by the non-imputed data to confirm no differences in interpretation (eg, changes to significant relationships) between the raw and imputed data. Upon verifying that all necessary assumptions were satisfied, linear regression analysis was used to assess whether gender, age, depression, or psychotic symptoms predicted suicidality scores. A second linear regression assessed the relationship between suicidality and the 10 most common DSM-5 diagnoses in the sample (≥5% prevalence), allowing examination of specific diagnoses while limiting the number of predictors in the model. The remaining 46 diagnostic categories, which were excluded from the analysis, typically comprised a very small proportion of diagnosed individuals, with an average of 1.53% (n = ~5) of the sample falling within each category. Due to the low number of cases in these categories, they were considered underpowered and also posed a risk of both multicollinearity and model overfitting in the regression analysis.37 Finally, a one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) using the clinical thresholds (0-6 indicate an absence of symptoms; 7-19 represent mild depression; 20-34 moderate; 35-60 indicate severe depression) for the MADRS scale was undertaken to further assess the impact of depression severity on suicidality.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the robustness of the findings. First, to address potential overlap between depression severity and suicidal ideation, analyses were repeated using depression scores excluding the suicidal ideation items from the MADRS and BPRS. Second, additional models examined recent suicidal ideation using the MADRS suicidal ideation item (item 10) and the BPRS suicidality item (item 4) as outcome variables. Third, to assess whether psychiatric complexity influenced the observed associations between diagnoses and suicidality, regression models were repeated, including the total number of DSM-5 diagnoses as an additional predictor. Finally, analyses examining psychotic symptoms were repeated with positive symptoms disaggregated into hallucinations, unusual thought content, and conceptual disorganization rather than modeled as a single domain. Results from these analyses are reported in the supplementary tables.

Discussion

While previous studies have reported associations between depression and suicidality in CHR populations, few have examined the relative contribution of multiple clinical predictors simultaneously in youth samples. The present study contributes to a growing literature demonstrating high levels of suicidality among young people at CHR for psychosis. Consistent with previous research, rates of suicidality were high, with 77% of CHR young people reporting lifetime suicidal ideation or self-harm behaviors, reinforcing findings from earlier CHR studies and recent meta-analyses indicating that suicidality is common in this population.3 The association between depressive symptoms and suicidality observed in the current study also aligns with prior work identifying depression as one of the most consistent correlates of suicide risk in CHR samples.3 By modelling multiple clinical factors simultaneously in a large CHR youth sample, the present study helps clarify which indicators are most strongly associated with suicidality.

When these variables were considered simultaneously, depression severity emerged as the most robust predictor of suicidality, whereas psychotic symptoms and social and occupational functioning were not significantly associated. Consistent with this pattern, analyses examining the 10 most common DSM-5 diagnoses in the sample showed that only recurrent severe major depression was associated with increased suicidality in the multiple regression model. Further analyses of depression severity showed that only moderate and severe depression were predictive of increased suicidality, with severe depression having a larger effect. Unexpectedly, substance use disorders (eg, severe cannabis use disorder) were not associated with suicidality.

The current findings suggest that depression, particularly more severe and ongoing depression, is a primary risk factor for suicidality in CHR young people. While previous studies and reviews have highlighted that depression and related constructs such as anhedonia are important predictors of suicidality in CHR populations,3,13–16 depression severity has been of limited interest and even less so with young people.3,17 However, recent evidence is converging on this finding. Pelizza et al.21 reported that CHR individuals with suicidal ideation showed significantly greater depression severity than those without, even when other symptom dimensions were considered, and Trujillo et al.18 found that suicidality in a large CHR sample was broadly associated with depressive symptoms and comorbid major depression alongside other clinical features. The current study found that only severe, ongoing depression, defined by DSM-5 diagnoses, was significantly linked to lifetime suicidality, unlike less severe or temporary forms of depression. Similarly, analyses using the MADRS scale showed that only the moderate and severe depression levels were linked to suicidality, with severe depression showing the strongest relationship. In the context of the broader literature, our findings align with evidence that affective disorders, and particularly recurrent depressive disorders, are the strongest predictors of suicidal thoughts and behaviors in young people.24 Other mental health conditions may influence suicidal behaviors but seem to have a less direct effect compared to affective disorders.24

We found no significant relationship between social and occupational functioning and suicidality. This supports prior research using the SOFAS measure5 and contradicts studies using other assessment tools.8,9,12 For example, some earlier studies used the Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF),9,12 which incorporates psychological symptoms such as self-harm and suicide at higher score ranges and may therefore inflate associations with suicidal thoughts and behaviors. Similarly, studies using the Global Functioning: Social and Role Scales have reported associations between poor social functioning and suicidal ideation, while role functioning was unrelated.8 In contrast, the SOFAS provides a global measure of functioning that does not distinguish between role or social functioning, nor does it incorporate clinical symptomatology. These differences in measurement may help explain inconsistencies across studies. Further research using consistent and clearly differentiated measures of functioning is needed to clarify how functioning relates to suicidality in CHR populations.

We also did not find a relationship between substance use and suicidality, although the only diagnosis in the 10 most common disorders was severe cannabis use disorder, which was present in only 6.4% of the population. The low diagnosis rate limited the power to detect potential effects. Research with a larger cohort of CHR young people with comorbid substance use disorders may present different findings. Similarly, we did not find relationships with commonly reported anxiety-related disorder diagnoses in our cohort. This may be because they have a less direct effect on suicidal thoughts and behaviors.24 Future studies using more nuanced measures of symptom severity may help clarify the combined effects of depression, anxiety, and substance use in CHR youth.

Clinical Implications

The current study findings support routine monitoring of suicide risk in CHR populations11 but more specifically suggest that, within CHR youth services, depression severity may be a more important clinical indicator of suicidality than attenuated psychotic symptoms or most other common comorbid diagnoses. Moderate to severe and recurrent depressive presentations may warrant heightened suicide risk monitoring, even when psychosis-risk symptoms are the primary reason for service contact.38

Given the specific association of recurrent severe major depression with increased suicidality, clinicians working with CHR young people should prioritize the identification and treatment of severe and persistent depression. This may include closer monitoring, more targeted risk assessment, and timely delivery of evidence-based treatments for severe depression.39 Similarly, evidence suggests that treatment approaches such as cognitive behavioral therapy can be effective for treating depression,40 suicidal thoughts and behaviors,41 and the risk of transition to psychosis.42 Utilizing such treatment strategies that are effective across multiple mental health conditions may provide greater benefit than treatments that are targeted only at a single condition.

An increased focus on incorporating suicide prevention strategies into staging models for CHR youth may also provide another avenue for reducing suicide risk in this vulnerable group, while also potentially decreasing the risk of transition to psychosis.7 Clinical staging frameworks conceptualize mental illness progression based on symptom severity, specificity, persistence, and functional impact, allowing treatment intensity to be adjusted according to illness stage.43–45 Within this framework, depression severity and suicidality may serve as indicators guiding intervention intensity in CHR services. For instance, lower levels of depressive symptoms or suicidality may warrant monitoring and psychosocial support, whereas more severe presentations may require more intensive psychological or pharmacological interventions alongside suicide-specific strategies such as safety planning and crisis intervention.41,46

Limitations

The current study was cross-sectional and thus causal conclusions cannot be drawn about the directionality of effects. Similarly, we did not capture suicide attempts or suicides directly. As a result, it remains unclear whether severe depression is predictive of actual suicidal behaviors in CHR young people. Our design may have also led to the exclusion of severely depressed individuals, as those on antidepressants were ineligible to participate in this study. Lastly, the primary outcome measure of suicidality was taken from the clinician-rated CAARMS, which does not clearly distinguish self-harm from suicidal thoughts or behaviors. This limited our capacity to determine whether associations between depression, suicidality, and self-harm apply uniformly across both or are specific to 1. However, the consistency of findings across sensitivity analyses using recent suicidal ideation items from the MADRS and BPRS provides some reassurance that the central role of depression was not specific to the CAARMS-derived outcome. Further research using validated measures of suicidal thoughts and behaviors is needed to clarify the impact of depression on suicide risk in CHR young people.

Conclusion

Suicidality is highly prevalent among CHR young people, and early identification and intervention can help reduce mortality risk.25 This study shows that depression severity, and particularly severe and ongoing depression, should be a key consideration in monitoring and treating suicide risk for CHR youth. As suicide related behaviors in youth may predict later psychosis risk independent of the CHR criteria,7 the monitoring of suicidality may also be beneficial for guiding early intervention efforts targeting both suicide and psychosis. Further research examining the mechanisms linking depression and suicidality in CHR populations is needed to better explain the differences observed across studies.
