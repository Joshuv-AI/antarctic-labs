Disentangling coherent structures and the origin of swirl-switching
Eman Bagheri \aff 1, Riccardo Casali \aff 1, Stefan Becker \aff 1, Philipp Schlatter \aff 1

Abstract
Modal decomposition of turbulent flows using classical proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) often suffers from mode mixing, in which a distinct coherent structure may be distributed over several POD modes. We propose a decomposition method based on the Hilbert transform and band-pass filtering to address this issue (filtered Hilbert POD – FHPOD). We apply this approach to the turbulent flow through a 180 bent pipe at R ​ e D = 10,000 Re_{D}=10,000 (based on bulk velocity ( U b U_{b} ) and pipe diameter ( D D )) and curvature γ = 0.2 \gamma=0.2 , simulated using direct numerical simulation. The FHPOD results in four distinct mode families, including a swirl-switching mode at Strouhal number of 0.13 localised in the curved section. Our novel modal decomposition shows that the modes observed in the bend and downstream correspond to distinct physical mechanisms rather than to a single universal swirl-switching instability throughout the pipe, as previous work implied. To further examine the origin of the swirl-switching mode, we perform a local stability analysis of the cross-sectional mean flow along the bend. We find unstable eigenmodes at the same streamwise wavenumber and within the same range of Strouhal numbers as the swirl-switching mode found in the modal decomposition. The result supports the interpretation that the swirl-switching phenomenon is an intrinsic instability of the curved-pipe flow that can be excited and potentially enhanced by incoming turbulent structures, but is ultimately not caused by them. Finally, we also establish a link of the downstream modes to the local shear layers of the modified base flow, highlighting the different nature of these modes.

Introduction

Flows in curved pipes are found in a wide range of industrial and physiological systems and have been the subject of research for more than a century
(
3

52

(
32

Re_{D}=\frac{U_{b}D}{\nu}

(1)

\gamma=\frac{D}{2R_{c}}\ .

(2)

Here,

U_{b}

\nu

R_{c}
the radius of curvature of the bend. As a result of the curvature and the centrifugal force, a Prandtl’s secondary flow of the first type forms inside the curved pipe. This cross-stream secondary motion is termed Dean vortices in recognition of the contributions of

At sufficiently high Reynolds numbers, the Dean vortices become unstable and significantly affect the turbulent flow

A key feature of the unstable Dean vortices is the so-called

phenomenon.

They studied turbulent flows in mitred
90^{\circ}

St=\frac{fD}{U_{b}},\qquad 2\times 10^{-4}\lesssim St\lesssim 4.5\times 10^{-3},

(3)

Motivation of the current work

Despite the extensive body of work, several key aspects of swirl-switching remain unresolved. The characteristic Strouhal number of the motion is far from universal. The reported values span more than two orders of magnitude (see table
1
). Excluding the studies that utilise recycling inflow conditions, part of this scatter can be attributed to differences in geometry, Reynolds number, and the location of the instabilities. An equally important reason for this scatter is the method employed to identify the large-scale coherent structure. As
54
showed, the identified characteristic frequencies depend on the choice of the measured quantity of interest. Furthermore, dimensionality reduction can also lead to a misinterpretation of the characteristic frequency
(
18
)
. In particular, when only a single observable (e.g. streamwise vorticity at a given cross-section) is analysed, coherent structures dominated by other velocity components may be overlooked or misidentified.

On the other hand,
19
showed that the relative contributions of different frequencies depend strongly on the streamwise measurement location. This implies the coexistence of multiple instability mechanisms that dominate at different streamwise positions. Consequently, the phenomenon reported as “swirl-switching” in different studies may not correspond to the same universal mechanism, but instead may point to distinct instabilities with similar footprint occurring at different locations within the flow. Part of the confusion in the literature may therefore be due to the use of the same term for different coherent structures with distinct underlying physical mechanisms. To address this issue, we propose the following requirements for the clean separation and the accurate energy quantification of distinct modes:

(1)

The data must be free of spurious periodicity or artificial forcing that could contaminate the intrinsic instabilities.

(2)

The analysis must be fully three-dimensional and span the entire domain of interest.

(3)

Degenerate POD modes represent the same convecting structures, and they must have identical energy and identical spectra of the time-coefficients.

(4)

The temporal spectrum associated with each mode should be unimodal. Multiple peaks in the spectra indicate mode mixing and inadequate separation.

(5)

The interpretation of mode shapes must consider both rotational (cross-stream) and axial (streamwise) motions to avoid misattribution of mechanisms.

The use of synthetic-eddy inflow conditions together with 3D-POD in
18
;
28
addressed requirements
(1)
and
(2)
and clarified some of these earlier discrepancies. However, classical POD does not guarantee a clean separation of distinct instabilities. Thus, requirements
(3)
and
(4)
remain critical challenges and are crucial to accurately classify different coherent structures.

The first objective of this work is to satisfy all five requirements outlined above in our modal decompositions, thereby isolating different instabilities of different origins as distinct modes. In this context, a central goal is to identify the swirl-switching phenomenon as a single mode and understand its physical origin. More specifically, through spatio-temporal separation of the underlying mechanisms, we first isolate the swirl-switching mode from other coexisting structures. This enables us to determine its characteristic frequency range, wavelength, spatial support, and dominant velocity components. Subsequently, we investigate whether the swirl-switching mode is linked to Dean-vortex dynamics, the downstream shear-layer dynamics, or is an inherent instability of the bent-pipe flow. In this sense, the present work is not only aimed at improving modal decompositions of turbulent flows, but also at establishing a clearer connection between the observed coherent structures and the mechanisms responsible for them.

In spatially developing flows, travelling structures naturally appear as degenerate POD pairs with a 90-degree phase shift due to their convective nature. In the limit of an infinite number of snapshots, POD satisfies requirement
(3)
for statistically stationary flows. In practice, however, particularly in turbulent flows where coherent structures are embedded within a broad range of scales, finite sampling often leads to imperfect pairing of degenerate modes. Consequently, a single convecting structure often appears distributed across multiple modes with unequal energy contents.

Moreover, the classical POD does not satisfy requirement
(4)
by construction. In this context, the unimodality of a mode refers to a single dominant frequency band in its time-coefficient rather than a perfectly harmonic response. In turbulent flows, nonlinear interactions and amplitude modulation can generate spectrally broadened peaks. As a result of violations of requirements
(3)
and
(4)
, the energy-based ranking of distinct instabilities becomes an ill-posed problem. In other words, the energy of a single physical instability may be distributed across several POD modes. This is partially the reason why many studies rely on the reduced-order reconstructions of the flow field using multiple modes to recover an instability of interest
(
24
;
33
)
. For instance,
33
noted that no single “swirl mode” appears among the most energetic POD modes, but the flow reconstruction using multiple modes reproduces a swirl-switching structure. This indicates that mode mixing distributed the relevant swirl-switching structure across multiple modes in their analysis. This issue is particularly more severe in fully turbulent flows, where modal energy levels of the modes are closely spaced. Therefore, the truncation up to a limited set of leading modes may discard a portion of a physically coherent instability of interest that is distributed across multiple POD modes with lower energy contents. Moreover, the energy of the modes highly depends on the spatial extent of the computational domain and the duration of the sampled time series. Consequently, a mode that contains multiple frequency bands indicates mixing of multiple different instabilities, and its energy is ill-defined from a physical point of view. While DMD and SPOD yield single-frequency modes, such a strict monochromaticity is often too restrictive for turbulent flows, given the multiscale and nonlinear nature of turbulence. Here, we instead seek a finite-band representation that captures each instability as a single coherent mode.

To enforce requirement
(3)
, we perform POD on the analytic signal of the data, following the Hilbert Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (HPOD) framework introduced by
26
and subsequently applied in
38
;
39
. To address requirement
(4)
, we extract the dominant HPOD modes and analyse their spectral content. A Butterworth filter with sufficiently broad support is then designed and applied to the snapshots to suppress secondary frequency bands contaminating the dominant mode. The purpose of this step is not to enforce an artificially narrow spectral band, but to reduce spectral mixing while preserving the physically relevant frequency content. As a result, the novel
filtered HPOD (FHPOD) method
can mitigate mode mixing and limit contamination from structures of different physical origins that are correlated with the mode of interest.

Lastly, requirement
(5)
is crucial to correctly identify the underlying physical mechanisms. As shown in Section
4
, some modes carry most of their energy in the streamwise velocity component, whereas others are dominated by cross-stream rotational motion. Thus, we visualise the former using isosurfaces of the streamwise velocity and the latter using isosurfaces of the streamfunction.

Besides establishing a consistent modal decomposition framework, the present study also aims at identifying the low-frequency instability at
St\approx 0.03-0.04
as a distinct mode. Although this low-frequency mode has been observed in multiple experimental studies
(
4
;
44
;
24
;
23
;
19
;
20
)
, the associated instability has not previously been extracted as a separate mode in numerical simulations.
18
reported that a similar low-frequency peak at
St\approx 0.045
for
\gamma=0.1
was present in the PSD of the time-coefficients, but noted that resolving its large wavelength would require an extended outlet section that was computationally prohibitive at the time. In the following, we demonstrate that, by extending the straight section downstream of the bend, this low-frequency mode can be adequately isolated and characterised.

3
Computational methodology

The methodology consists of the following main steps. First, we generate the dataset required for the modal decomposition using DNS. Second, we formulate the filtered Hilbert POD (FHPOD) method, which is later applied to the DNS data to extract coherent structures with distinct spectral support. Finally, we introduce the local stability analysis used to assess whether the swirl-switching FHPOD mode is consistent with unstable modes of the bent-pipe mean flow.

3.2
Filtered Hilbert Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (FHPOD)

The FHPOD method is designed to address two limitations of classical POD, namely, the imperfect pairing of degenerate modes associated with a convecting structure and the mixing of multiple frequency bands within a single mode. For this analysis, we use a sequence of
N_{s}=5374
three-dimensional DNS velocity snapshots
\bm{u}(\bm{x},t_{n})
sampled at a constant temporal interval of
\Delta t=0.15\,D/U_{b}
. This results in a total simulated time of
T=N_{s}\Delta t\approx 806\,D/U_{b}
corresponding to approximately
24
periods of the low-frequency mode expected at
St\approx 0.03
, ensuring that this instability is sufficiently resolved in the dataset.

Let
\bm{q}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the SEM degrees of freedom for the three velocity components concatenated as

\bm{q}_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{u}_{n}\\
\bm{v}_{n}\\
\bm{w}_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

(4)

To enforce the symmetry about the equatorial plane of the bent pipe (
y=0
), each snapshot is mirrored (reflected) and added to the matrix of snapshots as follows

\bm{Q}=\big[\bm{q}_{1}^{(o)},\dots,\bm{q}_{N_{s}}^{(o)},\bm{q}_{1}^{(m)},\dots,\bm{q}_{N_{s}}^{(m)}\big]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N},\qquad N=2N_{s}

(5)

in which
q_{j}^{(m)}
denotes the reflected velocity field of the
j^{th}
snapshot with respect to the
y=0
plane. The reflection operator
\mathcal{R}_{y}
is defined as

\mathcal{R}_{y}:\begin{cases}(s,r,y)\mapsto(s,r,-y),\\[5.69054pt]
(u_{s},u_{r},u_{y})(s,r,y)\mapsto(u_{s},u_{r},-u_{y})(s,r,-y).\end{cases}

(6)

In the next step, we apply a temporal band-pass filter to each row of
\bm{Q}
. The filtering is performed on the original and the mirrored snapshot sequences separately in order to avoid cross-contamination at the concatenation point. We use a second-order Butterworth filter for this purpose with a forward–backward strategy to achieve zero-phase filtering. This results in a filtered snapshot matrix

\bm{Q}_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}.

(7)

The filter bands are selected based on an initial inspection of the POD time-coefficient spectra, with the aim of isolating physically distinct finite-band structures. To pair the degenerate convecting structures into a single complex mode, we first form the analytic signal associated with the filtered snapshots. For a real-valued signal
x(t)
, the analytic signal is defined as

z(t)=x(t)+i\,\mathcal{H}_{t}\{x(t)\},

(8)

where
\mathcal{H}_{t}\{\cdot\}
is the Hilbert transform in time. Applying this to
\bm{Q}_{f}
with the original and mirrored halves treated separately, yields the complex analytic snapshot matrix

\bm{Q}_{f}^{(\mathcal{H})}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}.

(9)

The POD is defined with respect to the inner product.

\langle\bm{a},\bm{b}\rangle_{M}=\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{M}\bm{b},

(10)

in which
\bm{M}
is the diagonal mass matrix induced by Gauss–Lobatto–Legendre quadrature and
(\cdot)^{\dagger}
denotes the Hermitian transpose. By introducing the mass-weighted analytic snapshot matrix

\bm{A}_{f}=\bm{M}^{1/2}\bm{Q}_{f}^{(\mathcal{H})}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times N},

(11)

the complex singular value decomposition (SVD) problem

\bm{A}_{f}=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^{\dagger},

(12)

is solved using a Lanczos-based method in distributed memory
(
35
)
. Finally, FHPOD modes in physical units are recovered from the left singular vectors by

\bm{\phi}_{j}=\bm{M}^{-1/2}\bm{u}_{j}.

(13)

3.3
Quantifying the energy

The squared singular values
\sigma_{j}^{2}
of
\bm{A}_{f}
quantify the energy of mode
j
within the filtered analytic dataset
\bm{Q}_{f}^{(\mathcal{H})}
. To compute the energy of each mode with respect to the unfiltered snapshots
\bm{Q}
, we first introduce the real, mass-weighted, and unfiltered snapshot matrix

\bm{A}_{0}:=\bm{M}^{1/2}\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}.

(14)

The corresponding mass-weighted analytic snapshot matrix is then

\bm{A}:=\bm{A}_{0}+i\,\mathcal{H}_{t}\{\bm{A}_{0}\}=\bm{M}^{1/2}\bm{Q}^{(\mathcal{H})}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times N},

(15)

where
\mathcal{H}_{t}
is applied row-wise in time. Since the mass-weighted left singular vector
\bm{u}_{j}
corresponding to the physical FHPOD mode
\bm{\phi}_{j}
was extracted from the filtered matrix
\bm{A}_{f}
in (
11
), it is not in general a left singular vector of the unfiltered matrix
\bm{A}
. Therefore, we decompose
\bm{A}
as

\bm{A}=\bm{A}_{f}+\bm{R},

(16)

where
\bm{R}=\bm{A}-\bm{A}_{f}
is the mass-weighted analytic remainder matrix containing the out-of-band contributions excluded by the filter. For the mass-weighted left singular vector
\bm{u}_{j}
, we define the projection coefficients onto the unfiltered analytic snapshots

\bm{p}_{j}:=\bm{u}_{j}^{\dagger}\bm{A}.

(17)

Substituting the decomposition (
16
) into (
17
) yields

\bm{p}_{j}=\bm{u}_{j}^{\dagger}\bm{A}_{f}+\bm{u}_{j}^{\dagger}\bm{R}=\bm{p}_{j,\mathrm{in}}+\bm{p}_{j,\mathrm{out}}.

(18)

The first term is the in-band projection,

\bm{p}_{j,\mathrm{in}}=\bm{u}_{j}^{\dagger}\bm{A}_{f}=\sigma_{j}\bm{v}_{j}^{\dagger},

(19)

where
\sigma_{j}
and
\bm{v}_{j}
are the singular value and right singular vector of the filtered matrix
\bm{A}_{f}
already obtained by solving the SVD problem (
12
). The second term is the out-of-band projection,

\bm{p}_{j,\mathrm{out}}=\bm{u}_{j}^{\dagger}\bm{R}.

(20)

The projected energy
\tilde{\sigma}_{j}^{2}
is then defined by the
\ell^{2}
-norm of the projection coefficient,

\tilde{\sigma}_{j}:=\|\bm{p}_{j}\|_{2}=\left(\bm{p}_{j}\bm{p}_{j}^{\dagger}\right)^{1/2}.

(21)

Thus,
\tilde{\sigma}_{j}^{2}
represents the projected modal energy of FHPOD spatial mode
\bm{\phi}_{j}
.
In practice,
\bm{p}_{j}
is computed without explicitly forming
\bm{Q}^{(\mathcal{H})}
or
\bm{A}
. Substituting (
15
) in (
17
) yields

\bm{p}_{j}=\bm{u}_{j}^{\dagger}\bm{A}=\bm{u}_{j}^{\dagger}\bm{A}_{0}+i\,\bm{u}_{j}^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}_{t}\{\bm{A}_{0}\}.

(22)

We first project the real and unfiltered snapshots onto the complex mass-weighted left singular vector
\bm{u}_{j}

\bm{s}_{j}:=\bm{u}_{j}^{\dagger}\bm{A}_{0},

(23)

which is evaluated in a streaming manner without the need to form the full matrix
\bm{A}_{0}
. As the Hilbert transform is a linear operation performed only in time, and the projection is a spatial linear operation, the two operations commute:

\bm{u}_{j}^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}_{t}\{\bm{A}_{0}\}=\mathcal{H}_{t}\left\{\bm{u}_{j}^{\dagger}\bm{A}_{0}\right\}=\mathcal{H}_{t}\{\bm{s}_{j}\}.

(24)

Substituting (
23
) and (
24
) into (
22
) yields

\bm{p}_{j}=\bm{s}_{j}+i\,\mathcal{H}_{t}\{\bm{s}_{j}\},

(25)

obtained by applying a one-dimensional Hilbert transform to the scalar time-coefficient sequence
\bm{s}_{j}
for each mode
j
.

3.4
Cross-sectional streamfunction
\Psi

For each streamwise location
s
, let
\mathcal{A}(s)
denote the pipe cross-section in the
(r,y)
-plane with the normal in
\hat{\bm{s}}
. We define the streamwise vorticity as

\omega_{s}(r,y,s):=\bigl(\nabla\times\bm{U}\bigr)\cdot\hat{\bm{s}}(s).

(26)

The cross-sectional streamfunction
\Psi(r,y,s)
is then obtained for each streamwise location
s
by solving the Poisson problem

\Delta_{(r,y)}\,\Psi(r,y,s)=\,-\omega_{s}(r,y,s)

(27)

with Dirichlet boundary condition at the pipe wall,

\Psi(r,y,s)=0.

(28)

Here,
\Delta_{(r,y)}
denotes the in-plane Laplacian operator. In a spatially developing three-dimensional flow, the in-plane velocity on
\mathcal{A}(s)
is not necessarily two-dimensionally divergence-free. Using the Helmholtz decomposition on each cross-section, we obtain:

\bm{U}_{\perp}(r,y,s)=\bm{U}_{\perp}^{\mathrm{sol}}(r,y,s)+\nabla_{(r,y)}\Phi(r,y,s),\qquad\nabla_{(r,y)}\!\cdot\bm{U}_{\perp}^{\mathrm{sol}}(r,y,s)=0,

(29)

where
\bm{U}_{\perp}^{\mathrm{sol}}
is the in-plane solenoidal component and
\nabla_{(r,y)}\Phi
contains the non-solenoidal part. Solving the Poisson problem above yields the streamfunction associated with
\bm{U}_{\perp}^{\mathrm{sol}}
.

3.5
Local stability analysis

The stability of the cross-sectional mean turbulent flow is investigated through modal stability analysis
(
45
;
10
)
,
i.e.
by studying the evolution of infinitesimal perturbations
\widetilde{\bm{b}}=\{\widetilde{\bm{u}},\widetilde{p}\}
applied to the cross-sectional mean turbulent base flow
\bm{B}=\{\bm{U},P\}
, with
\bm{U}
being the mean velocity vector and
P
the mean pressure. Similar mean flow stability approaches have been used to identify instability mechanisms in both unsteady laminar flows
(
47
)
and turbulent mean flows
(
15
;
31
;
40
;
37
;
50
;
5
)
. For this purpose, the two-dimensional (2D) stability code described in
7
, capable of performing modal stability analysis for toroidal and helical pipe geometries, is used.
Therefore, here the stability of the mean turbulent flow, at different pipe cross-sections along
S
, as if it were the mean flow in a toroidal pipe with the same curvature, is computed. Due to this assumption, the streamwise invariance allows a 2D analysis with periodic modes in the streamwise direction (see below), also called BiGlobal
(
29
)
. The code is written in helical coordinates
\left(r,\theta,s\right)
of
14
, using a spectral collocation method for the spatial discretisation and employing Fourier and Chebyshev basis functions in the azimuthal and radial directions, respectively. No-slip boundary conditions are applied at the wall.
The system is augmented with equations to cancel out the spurious pressure modes
(
2
)
, which affect the eigenmodes.

The evolution of the aforementioned
\widetilde{\bm{b}}
is governed by the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations linearised around the basic state,

\begin{split}&\partialderivative{{\uvecp}}{t}+(\bm{U}\dotproduct\gradient)\widetilde{\bm{u}}+(\widetilde{\bm{u}}\dotproduct\gradient)\bm{U}+\gradient\widetilde{p}-\frac{1}{Re_{D}}\laplacian\widetilde{\bm{u}}=0,\\
&\divergence\widetilde{\bm{u}}=0\ .\end{split}

(30)

Here, we neglect the influence of the perturbations on the Reynolds stresses arising in the mean flow equations. The perturbations, through a Fourier transform, assume the form (normal modes ansatz)

\widetilde{\bm{b}}(s,r,\theta,t)=\sum_{\alpha=-\infty}^{+\infty}\widehat{\bm{b}}_{\alpha}(r,\theta)e^{\ \mu t+i\alpha s}\ ,

(31)

where
\alpha=2\pi R_{p}/l_{s}
is the streamwise wavenumber, being
l_{s}
the wavelength, and
\mu=\sigma+i\omega
the complex eigenvalue.

For each streamwise wavenumber, one obtains the following eigenvalue problem

-\mu\mathcal{M}\widehat{\bm{b}}_{\alpha}=\mathcal{L}\widehat{\bm{b}}_{\alpha}\ ,

(32)

where
\mathcal{M}
is the mass matrix and
\mathcal{L}
the linearised Navier–Stokes operator, defined as

\mathcal{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{I}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\ \ \mathcal{L}=\begin{pmatrix}\gradient_{0}U+U\dotproduct\gradient_{\alpha}-\frac{1}{Re_{D}}\laplacian_{\alpha}&\ \ \gradient_{\alpha}\\
\divergence_{\alpha}&\ \ 0\end{pmatrix}\ ,

(33)

where
\mathcal{I}
is the
3\crossproduct 3
identity matrix and
\nabla_{\alpha}
is used to emphasise that the differential operators depend on the wavenumber
\alpha
, since the term
e^{i\alpha s}
contributes to
\partialderivative*{s}
, as described in
7
.
Solving the eigenvalue problem (
32
), we find the modes that would locally lead to an instability in the case of a laminar flow, i.e., the ones that are characterised by the real part of the eigenvalue being positive (
\sigma>0
).
It should also be noted that the present analysis adopts a quasi-laminar approximation, in which the contribution of the Reynolds stresses is neglected in equation (
30
). A fully consistent formulation would instead require linearisation of the Reynolds–averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) equations, together with a closure for the perturbation of Reynolds stresses. Nevertheless, similar approximations have been used in stability analyses of turbulent jets, swirling jets, swirl injectors and bluff-body wakes, where large-scale coherent structures are treated as perturbations of the turbulent mean flow and the effect of turbulence is assumed to enter primarily through the mean flow itself
(
15
;
31
;
40
)
. In some studies, turbulence has also been represented using a frozen eddy viscosity, and comparisons with quasi-laminar analyses have shown that such models can improve quantitative predictions of frequencies or mode shapes, while introducing additional modelling uncertainty such as overestimating turbulent dissipation and thereby stabilising modes that would otherwise be unstable
(
37
;
50
;
5
)
. The present analysis, therefore, adopts a quasi-laminar stability approach, in which the turbulent mean flow is used as the base flow while perturbation of Reynolds stresses is neglected. This choice is intended to help identify the underlying instability mechanisms, while avoiding the additional damping due to turbulent dissipation that may suppress the unstable modes of interest.

Identification of modes

After establishing the mean flow structures, we proceed with performing a three-dimensional POD without applying the Hilbert transform, following the approach described in

Figure 6:
PSD of time-coefficients for the first four POD modes

None of the first four modes forms clean degenerate pairs, and requirement
(3)
is not satisfied. Despite the long sampling duration and large number of snapshots, the modes do not appear as well-defined pairs with equal energy. The breakdown of degenerate pairing is partly due to the existence of the energetic low-frequency instability at

When the POD is repeated on a domain with a shortened downstream extent and fewer snapshots, the influence of this instability is reduced, and the pairing improves, with modes 1 and 2 appearing nearly degenerate. A similar imperfect pairing of degenerate POD modes is also visible in previous POD studies
(
18

Mode 2 and 3 exhibit similar spectral content with shared peaks at

To address the issue of degenerate pairing, we apply the Hilbert transform in time. By constructing the analytic signal at each point and assembling the resulting complex fields into the snapshot matrix, we obtain complex POD modes. By construction, the real and imaginary parts of each mode are enforced to be

(3)

St\approx 0.03
. Figure
8
shows the corresponding mode shapes for POD modes 2 and 3 and HPOD mode 1. Note that the energy-based ordering of the modes also changes, and the same instability that appears in POD modes 2 and 3 emerges as the most energetic complex mode in the HPOD decomposition. As the real and imaginary parts of the HPOD modes are only phase-shifted representations of the same structures, we only show the real part in the rest of this article unless stated otherwise.

Especially when classical POD is used, even small mixing with other modes that contain much stronger in-plane velocity components can further obscure this mode. 4.3
Perturbation of the vorticity equation

The physical origin of the swirl-switching phenomenon remains the subject of ongoing debate. One line of investigation attributes this behaviour to upstream turbulence, suggesting that streaks or VLSMs entering the bend cause or influence the unsteady motion of the Dean vortices
(
43
;
23
;
6
)
. Similarly,
55
linked the swirl-switching phenomenon to VLSM-like streamwise fluctuations generated within the toroidal pipe, arguing that low-frequency switching is associated with the temporal variation of the VLSMs, whereas higher-frequency switching is related to its streamwise spatial scale. In contrast, another body of work interprets swirl-switching as an intrinsic feature of the bent pipe flow itself rather than as a response imposed solely by upstream forcing. Within this perspective, the phenomenon has been associated with bimodal vortex dynamics, inner-wall or shear-layer interactions, and the emergence of a three-dimensional wave-like structure generated within the curved section
(
4
;
17
;
33
;
18
)
. Swirl-switching has also been interpreted as a symmetry-breaking process or global instability of the bend shear layer associated with the transition dynamics of the flow, rather than as a response driven solely by upstream forcing
(
18
;
28
)
.

Recently,
1
derived the mean vorticity transport equation in the Frenet–Serret coordinate system for toroidal flows. They showed that the strength of the Dean vortices is related to the cross-sectional profile of the pipe governed by the centrifugal production term (see (
35
)).

\displaystyle\frac{D\overline{\omega}_{s}}{Dt}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\partial_{y}\left(\frac{\kappa}{h_{s}}\overline{U}_{s}^{2}\right)}_{\text{centrifugal production}}+\underbrace{\frac{\kappa}{h_{s}}\overline{U}_{r}\,\overline{\omega}_{s}}_{\text{radial redistribution}}+\nu\,(\nabla^{2}\overline{\bm{\omega}})_{s}-\bigl[\nabla\times(\nabla\cdot\overline{\mathbf{R}})\bigr]_{s}

(35)

Here,
\kappa=1/R_{c}
is the dimensional centreline curvature,
h_{s}=1+\kappa r
is the scale factor, and
\overline{\mathbf{R}}
denotes the Reynolds stress tensor.
\overline{\mathbf{U}}
and
\overline{\bm{\omega}}
represent mean velocity and mean vorticity fields, respectively. We use this equation to investigate the effect of streamwise velocity perturbations on the secondary flow. For this purpose, we derive a perturbation equation for the vorticity transport equation. To describe a short-lived coherent perturbation embedded in turbulence, we employ a triple decomposition as follows.

\mathbf{u}=\overline{\mathbf{U}}+\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}+\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime},\qquad\bm{\omega}=\overline{\bm{\omega}}+\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}+\bm{\omega}^{\prime\prime}.

(36)

Here,
\overline{{(\cdot)}}
represent the long-time mean fields,
\widetilde{(\cdot)}
denotes the coherent phase-averaged perturbation, and
(\cdot)^{\prime\prime}
denotes the remaining turbulent fluctuations, with
\overline{\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}}=\overline{\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime}}=\mathbf{0}
. Assuming the perturbation is sufficiently short-lived that it does not modify the Reynolds stress tensor
\overline{\mathbf{R}}
, the perturbation satisfies the following linearised equation for the streamwise
s
–component

\left(\partial_{t}+\overline{\mathbf{U}}\cdot\nabla\right)\widetilde{\omega}_{s}=\big[(\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}\cdot\nabla)\overline{\mathbf{U}}\big]_{s}+\big[(\overline{\bm{\omega}}\cdot\nabla)\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\big]_{s}-\big[(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla)\overline{\bm{\omega}}\big]_{s}+\nu(\nabla^{2}\widetilde{\bm{\omega}})_{s}.

(37)

Considering a streamwise-only initial perturbation, a fully developed mean flow, and keeping the leading order terms, yields

\frac{D\widetilde{\omega}_{s}}{Dt}=\frac{\overline{\omega}_{s}}{h_{s}}\,\partial_{s}\widetilde{u}_{s}+\partial_{y}\!\left(\frac{2\kappa}{h_{s}}\,\overline{U}_{s}\,\widetilde{u}_{s}\right)+\nu\left(\nabla^{2}\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}\right)_{s}.

(38)

Note that although
\widetilde{\omega}_{s}
is kinematically zero when velocity perturbations are only streamwise (
\widetilde{u}_{y}=\widetilde{u}_{r}=0
), it can be generated dynamically by the source terms on the right-hand side. Finally, we restrict the flow to a 2D cross-section model by neglecting the streamwise variation of the mean flow and the perturbation,

\partial_{s}(\cdot)=0.

(39)

Under this assumption, equation (
38
) reduces to

\frac{D\widetilde{\omega}_{s}}{Dt}=\partial_{y}\!\left(\frac{2\kappa}{h_{s}}\,\overline{U}_{s}\,\widetilde{u}_{s}\right)+\nu\left(\nabla^{2}\widetilde{\bm{\omega}}\right)_{s}.

(40)

Equation (
40
) is a simplified and linearised form of the vorticity perturbation equation in which coherent streamwise velocity perturbations appear as a source term on the right-hand side of the equation. In other words, large-scale and short-lived structures with significant streamwise momentum, such as streaks or VLSMs, can indeed linearly modulate the secondary flow. This is consistent with the observations of
43
, who stated that upstream low and high-speed streaks are correlated with the downstream displacement of the stagnation point and the switching of the symmetry plane of the Dean vortices. Although equation (
40
) shows a linear coupling mechanism through which streamwise coherent structures can force the secondary flow modulations, it does not by itself establish whether such structures are the primary cause of swirl-switching or instead excite an existing instability intrinsic to bent pipe flows. In other words, the linear operator in (
40
) admits the possibility that streamwise velocity perturbations can trigger secondary flow modulations. However, determining whether swirl-switching instead originates from an intrinsic instability depends on the spectral properties of the corresponding operator, specifically whether or not it admits unstable or weakly damped eigenmodes of the bent-pipe base flow associated with swirl-switching. Despite variations of Reynolds number, bend angle, curvature, and upstream conditions, the reported peak at
St=0.10
–
0.13
and qualitative characteristics of this phenomenon remain fairly consistent in both experimental and numerical studies. This suggests that swirl-switching is unlikely to arise solely as a convective response to inflow structures. It instead points to the presence of an intrinsically unstable or weakly damped eigenmode of the bent-pipe flow that may be amplified by external streamwise perturbations. To examine this hypothesis, we perform a local linear stability analysis of the flow. Mean cross-sectional base flows are extracted from the DNS data at several angular positions along the
180^{\circ}
bend, and their local stability is analysed in the following section.

4.4
Local stability analysis of the mean flow within the bend

In this section, we analyse the local linear stability of mean cross-sectional base flows extracted from the DNS at several angular positions
\theta
along the
180^{\circ}

3.5
), and their growth rates and frequencies are tracked to determine whether the bent-pipe mean flow supports an intrinsic instability and, if so, how this instability evolves through the bend. At each angular position
\theta

\alpha\approx 0.6

5
Summary and conclusions

In this study, we revisit the swirl-switching phenomenon for the case of turbulence in a
180^{\circ}
bent pipe flow. We address the issue of mode mixing in the classical POD by first establishing five requirements for a physically meaningful modal decomposition of turbulent flows. As these requirements are often not satisfied in POD studies, we show how distinct instabilities are distributed across multiple modes, their energy is artificially split, and their physical origin becomes ambiguous and difficult to identify. We therefore propose an alternative decomposition method based on the Hilbert transform combined with temporal band-pass filtering before the SVD step, termed filtered Hilbert POD (FHPOD). The current approach allows the decomposition of the flow into separate energetically and spectrally distinct mode families. This mode disentangling approach shows that the different spectral peaks reported in the literature do not necessarily correspond to a single physical phenomenon, but reflect distinct coherent structures with varying strengths along the streamwise direction. More specifically, the method separates entangled distinct structures that have often been grouped under the same “swirl-switching” label in the literature.

The resulting decomposition reveals four families of large-scale coherent structures that have not previously been clearly identified using classical POD as single distinct modes. The FHPOD results in a low-frequency axial wave (
AX
) at
St\approx 0.03
; a curvature-driven swirl-switching mode (
SS
) inside the bend at
St\approx 0.13
, a swirl-breathing mode (
SB
) at
St\approx 0.26
, and a downstream shear-layer instability (
DS1
) at
St\approx 0.15
together with its second harmonic (
DS2
) localised in the shear layer of the four-vortex mean flow structure.

In particular, the separation of
SS
from the
DS
family is central to understanding the swirl-switching phenomenon. Since swirl-switching is identified as a distinct mode and is separated from the downstream shear-layer instabilities, we are able to investigate its origin using local stability analysis. The swirl-switching mode
SS
is found to be consistent with the intrinsic instabilities of the mean flow inside the bent section of the pipe identified using the local stability analysis. The local stability analysis shows two branches of unstable modes whose spatial structure, wall localisation, and frequency range closely match the FHPOD mode
SS
. The associated unstable frequencies vary along the bend rather than collapsing to a single monochromatic value, indicating that swirl-switching is better interpreted as a finite-band response of a spatially evolving instability. To the authors’ knowledge, this agreement provides the clearest evidence so far that swirl-switching originates from an intrinsic instability of the bent pipe mean flow, rather than being imposed directly by upstream turbulent structures. Although the presence of streamwise perturbations such as streaks and VLSMs can excite the swirl-switching mode, they are ultimately not the underlying physical cause of this phenomenon. Therefore, streamwise perturbations should be interpreted as an excitation mechanism for an instability that is already present in the bent-pipe mean flow. This interpretation is consistent with
43
, who found upstream streaks correlated with the downstream displacement of the inner-side stagnation point, and with
23
, who showed that modifying the incoming flow with a honeycomb altered and delayed the appearance of the swirl-switching mode but did not eliminate it. This result also supports the idea that identifying the origin of large-scale coherent structures can be facilitated by disentangling these structures as distinct modes in the modal decomposition as a first step.

The present approach has the following limitations. The FHPOD procedure is a posteriori decomposition technique and therefore requires an initial inspection of POD results and their spectra. As a result, the method is most effective when the underlying coherent structures have distinct spectral support with sufficient spatial localisations. If multiple modes strongly overlap in both space and frequency, they may not be fully separable using this procedure. A promising direction for future work is to formulate the temporal filtering step of the FHPOD using wavelets or wavelet packets, enabling separation of distinct coherent structures using adaptive time-frequency bands that can vary locally along the streamwise direction rather than prescribed global band-pass filters. In addition to reducing the reliance on manual band selections, such an approach could be particularly well-suited for spatially developing turbulent flows, where the characteristic frequency of the convecting instabilities may vary along the axial direction. Further studies are also needed to shed light on the effect of curvature, Reynolds number, and bend angle on the mode families identified in this work.
