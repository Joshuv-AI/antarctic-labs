Evaluation of cytotoxicity, setting time, and mineralization potential of mineral trioxide aggregate with Nigella sativa aqueous extract: An in vitro study
Authors: Shanmugapriya Karunakaran, K. E. Selvendran, Hafeela Farhana, Ashok Leburu, Madhuram Krishnamurthy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aim:To evaluate the effect of different concentrations of Nigella sativa (NS) aqueous extract on the cytotoxicity, setting time, and mineralization potential of mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA).Materials and Methods:In this in vitro study, three groups were evaluated: MTA + 30% NS (Group A), MTA + 50% NS (Group B), and MTA alone (Group C). Cytotoxicity was assessed using the MTT assay on MG-63 osteoblast-like cells. Setting time was measured using the Gillmore needle test according to the ISO 6876 standards. Mineralization was evaluated using Alizarin Red S staining. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance and Tukey’s post hoc test (P < 0.05).Results:The addition of NS improved cell viability in a concentration-dependent manner, with Group B showing the highest viability. Both NS groups demonstrated reduced initial and final setting times compared to MTA alone. Mineralization was significantly enhanced in NS groups, particularly in Group B.Conclusion:Incorporation of NS, especially at 50%, enhances the biocompatibility, accelerates the setting time, and improves the mineralization potential of MTA, suggesting its potential as a beneficial modification for endodontic applications.

INTRODUCTION

Mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) is a widely used bioactive material in restorative and regenerative endodontics owing to its superior sealing ability, biocompatibility, and capacity to promote hard-tissue formation.[12] It is commonly employed in apexification, root-end fillings, perforation repair, and pulp capping. MTA sets through a hydration reaction that forms a durable matrix, preventing bacterial leakage. It demonstrates minimal cytotoxicity and enhances osteogenic, cementogenic, and odontogenic differentiation by upregulating mineralization-related genes, thereby confirming its bioactivity. However, its prolonged setting time remains a significant limitation, potentially causing washout in the presence of moisture and compromising the sealing efficiency and treatment outcome.[345678]

Efforts to improve the properties of MTA have led researchers to investigate various additives that might enhance its performance. Among these, “Nigella sativa (NS),” also known as “black seed” or “black cumin,” has garnered attention due to its extensive medicinal history and bioactive components, including thymoquinone, thymohydroquinone, and thymol.[9] Thymoquinone, in particular, demonstrates notable anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and bone-healing properties. Research suggests that it may stimulate osteoblast activity while inhibiting osteoclast function, creating an environment that could accelerate bone formation and enhance mineralization, an essential factor in dental regeneration and repair.[10]

The combination of NS with MTA could potentially yield a dental material with improved physicochemical properties, particularly in reducing setting time.[11121314] This modification may not only streamline clinical applications but also promote osteogenesis, supporting the healing and integration of dental materials in a patient’s tissue. In addition, bioactive compounds of NS are believed to contribute to a more favorable healing environment by mitigating inflammation and oxidative stress, factors commonly involved in tissue repair.[15]

To evaluate the efficacy of this combination, the present in vitro study employs three primary methods: cytotoxicity testing using MTT assay, the Gillmore needle test to measure initial and final setting time, and mineralization potential using Alizarin Red staining test. Biocompatibility is a fundamental prerequisite for any dental material intended for direct contact with vital tissues. Among the various parameters used to evaluate biocompatibility, cell viability is one of the most critical, as it provides insight into how living cells respond to a given material at the cellular and metabolic levels. The MTT assay is a widely accepted method for assessing cell viability. It measures the metabolic activity of cells via mitochondrial dehydrogenase activity, which reduces the yellow tetrazolium salt (MTT) into insoluble purple formazan crystals. The intensity of the resulting color, quantified by spectrophotometry, correlates directly with the number of viable cells. This method provides a sensitive and reliable means of evaluating the cytotoxic effects of dental materials, particularly during early stages of material-tissue interaction. The Gillmore needle test provides a standardized assessment of setting time based on needle penetration resistance, offering a quantifiable measure of material hardening. By potentially reducing the setting time, the modified MTA may improve treatment outcomes, benefiting both clinicians and patients.

This study aimed to compare the Initial and final setting time and cytotoxicity of “MTA” with and without the addition of NS aqueous extract, as indicators of improved material performance. The findings of this research may open new avenues for enhancing the application of MTA in dental procedures, contributing to the development of more efficient and biologically compatible materials for dental restoration and regeneration.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The research protocol received ethical clearance from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Karpaga Vinayaga Institute of Dental Sciences (Approval No.: KIDS/IEC/2024/III/009).

Aqueous extract preparation

Fresh NS seeds were collected and thoroughly cleaned to eliminate dust and other impurities. The seeds were finely ground into a uniform powder using a mechanical grinder. Following the procedure adapted from Mawlood (1996), 100 g of the powdered sample was suspended in distilled water and heated in a water bath until boiling to extract the active components. The mixture was then passed through a filter to remove undissolved residues, and the resulting clear filtrate was incubated at 37°C to allow moisture to evaporate, yielding the dried crude extract. For the preparation of the working solution, 1 g of the dried extract was dissolved in 3 mL of distilled water, and the final volume was made up to 10 mL to produce a 100 mg/mL (100%) stock solution. From this stock, 30% and 50% aqueous extract concentrations were obtained through serial dilution.

Study groups

This in vitro investigation was conducted to assess the influence of NS aqueous extract on the physicochemical and biological properties of MTA through multiple experimental groups. For determining the initial and final setting time and mineralization potential, Group A contained MTA combined with 30% NS aqueous extract, Group B included MTA blended with 50% NS aqueous extract, and Group C comprised unmodified MTA. For the cytotoxicity assessment, Group A and B were formulated in the same way with 30% and 50% NS extracts, respectively, while Group C consisted of MTA alone, and Group D served as the untreated cells. This grouping design enabled a comparative evaluation of how varying concentrations of NS extract influence the performance characteristics of MTA.

Osteoblast cell culture, seeding, and viability testing (MTT assay)

The cytotoxic response of the test materials was evaluated using the MTT assay. Osteoblast-like cells (MG-63) were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. The cultures were incubated at 37°C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2 and 95% humidity until the cells reached approximately 70%–80% confluence. Once the desired growth was achieved, the cells were seeded into 96-well culture plates at a density of about 1 × 104 cells per well. Extracts of the test materials were prepared in fresh culture medium and incubated for 24 h following ISO 10993-5 standards for the biological evaluation of medical devices.

After exposure to the material extracts for 24 h, 20 μL of MTT reagent (5 mg/mL prepared in phosphate-buffered saline) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated again for 4 h at 37°C. During this incubation, mitochondrial dehydrogenase enzymes in viable cells converted the yellow MTT dye into purple formazan crystals. The medium was then removed carefully, and 150 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide was added to dissolve the crystals completely. The optical density (OD) of the resulting solution was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. The percentage of viable cells was calculated relative to the untreated control group, which was considered 100% viable. All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Physicochemical testing (initial and final setting time)

The “setting time” of each group (Group A comprised MTA mixed with 30% NS aqueous extract, Group B included MTA mixed with 50% NS aqueous extract, and Group C consisted of MTA without any NS extract) was assessed following ISO 6876 standards for dental materials. Both “initial and final setting times” were measured to determine the impact of NS extract on the hardening process of the material. For each group, MTA samples were prepared by thoroughly blending the “MTA powder” with the corresponding concentrations of NS extract to obtain a uniform mixture.

The “initial setting time” was defined as the duration from the “initiation of mixing” until the material could resist indentation by a 300 g Gillmore needle with a “flat-ended diameter of 1 mm.” This measurement provided insight into the time required for the material to reach a preliminary hard state. The “final setting time” was recorded as the time from mixing until the material resisted indentation from a 400 g Gillmore needle, indicating complete hardening.

Each setting time measurement was conducted in triplicate to ensure accuracy and reproducibility. All tests were performed under standardized conditions – 37°C temperature and 95% relative humidity to mimic the conditions of the human oral environment. These tests allowed for a thorough comparison of the setting characteristics of MTA modified with NS extract, potentially highlighting improvements in clinical applicability due to reduced setting time.

Alizarin Red staining

“Alizarin Red S staining” was performed to assess mineralization and osteogenesis, as calcium deposition indicates osteogenic activity. Following 14 days of incubation, the DPS cells were preserved by fixing them with “10% formalin” for 15 min at “room temperature,” ensuring the retention of cellular and mineral structures. The fixed cells were subsequently washed thoroughly with distilled water to remove residual formalin, preparing the samples for staining.

The cells were stained with a “2% Alizarin Red S solution,” adjusted to a “pH of 4.2,” for 20 min to highlight areas of calcium deposition. After staining, the cells were rinsed with “distilled water” to eliminate any excess dye, leaving behind calcium-bound stain that appeared as red nodules when observed under an inverted microscope. To perform quantitative analysis, the dye was extracted with “10% acetic acid,” and its “absorbance” was measured at “405 nm” using a spectrophotometer. This quantification provided a comparative measure of mineralization across the different groups.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS software, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, USA). The mean and SD were calculated for each group – Group A (MTA with 30% NS), Group B (MTA with 50% NS), and Group C (MTA alone) – to compare the initial and final setting time and mineralization potential. Differences among the groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When significant differences were detected, Tukey’s honestly significant difference post hoc test was employed to identify specific pairwise variations. The MTT assay results for cell viability were also examined using one-way ANOVA followed by post hoc testing to determine the concentration-dependent influence of NS extract. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Cell viability (MTT assay)

After 24 h, all experimental groups exhibited comparable levels of cell viability. The control group (Group D) showed the highest viability (100.0 ± 2.5%), while the MTA-only group (Group C) maintained moderate viability (82.5 ± 2.8%). The incorporation of NS extract resulted in a concentration-dependent increase in cell viability. Specifically, the MTA + 30% NS group (Group A) demonstrated a viability of 84.1 ± 3.1%, while the MTA + 50% NS group (Group B) showed the highest viability among the experimental groups at 87.6 ± 3.4% [Table 1 and Figure 1]. Notably, the increase in osteoblast viability in Group B was statistically significant compared to the control MTA group (P < 0.005), as determined by one-way ANOVA followed by a post hoc test. These findings are visually supported by phase contrast microscopy images, which revealed denser cellular populations and enhanced proliferation in Group B [Figure 2].

Setting time evaluation

Setting time evaluation revealed that the MTA group (Group C) exhibited the longest setting times, with an initial setting time of 38 ± 1.2 min and a final setting time of 158 ± 2.0 min. The addition of 30% NS (Group A) reduced the setting times to 36 ± 0.8 min (initial) and 155 ± 1.5 min (final). The most significant reduction was observed in Group B (MTA + 50% NS), which showed an initial setting time of 35 ± 1.0 min and a final setting time of 150 ± 2.5 min. These results suggested that NS extract slightly accelerates the setting reaction of MTA, particularly at higher concentrations, offering potential clinical advantages by reducing chairside working time [Table 2 and Figure 3].

Mineralization potential (Alizarin Red staining)

Mineralization potential, assessed via Alizarin Red staining, indicated enhanced calcium deposition in groups treated with NS. Group B (MTA + 50% NS) demonstrated the highest level of mineralization, evidenced by intense red staining and an OD of 0.79 ± 0.04 at 562 nm. Group A (MTA + 30% NS) exhibited moderate mineralization with an OD of 0.68 ± 0.03. In contrast, the MTA-only group (Group C) showed the least mineralization, with fewer and smaller mineralized nodules and a significantly lower OD value of 0.54 ± 0.02 [Table 3 and Figure 4d]. The differences in OD values among the groups were statistically significant (P < 0.05), highlighting a dose-dependent enhancement of osteogenic activity with increasing concentrations of NS extract. Microscopic images further confirmed the presence of larger and more numerous calcified nodules in Group B compared to Group A and C [Figure 4a-c].

DISCUSSION

“MTA” has become widely recognized in dental applications for its “biocompatibility” and its capacity to promote dentinogenic and osteogenic responses, aiding in the formation of cementum and bone.[161718] MTA’s efficacy stems from its ability to form “a calcium silicate hydrate-based colloidal gel” upon hydration, which solidifies to create an “impermeable, high-alkalinity barrier.” This barrier not only prevents bacterial infiltration but also provides an ideal environment for hard tissue formation through the release of “calcium hydroxide” and is followed by a rise in pH.[19]

Incorporation of NS extract into MTA has been shown to improve its biological and physical properties. The bioactive compound thymoquinone provides antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial benefits that help control inflammation and bacterial activity at repair sites. NS enhances MTA’s cytocompatibility, promotes cell proliferation and mineralization, and may accelerate hydration and calcium ion release. Achieving faster setting and greater hardness before moisture exposure reduces washout risk, ensuring better sealing and clinical performance in retrograde fillings.[2021]

The present study revealed that adding NS extract to MTA improved the viability of osteoblast-like cells, with the effect increasing in proportion to the concentration used. Osteoblast cells were used in the MTT assay instead of dental pulp cells because both MTA and NS are primarily involved in bone and periapical tissue regeneration. MTA exhibits osteoinductive properties that enhance osteoblast proliferation and mineralized tissue formation, making osteoblasts the most appropriate cell type for assessing cytocompatibility.[171920] Furthermore, osteoblast-like cell lines provide stable and reproducible results compared to primary dental pulp cells.[2021] The MTA + 50% NS group exhibited the highest cell viability, significantly greater than the MTA group, while the 30% NS group also showed a moderate increase. These results suggest that NS possesses biologically active compounds capable of promoting cellular viability and proliferation, thereby enhancing the biocompatibility of MTA.

This observation is consistent with previous studies that have reported the cytoprotective, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties of NS (black seed) and its active components, particularly thymoquinone.[11] For instance, Ardiana et al. demonstrated that NS extracts supported fibroblast proliferation and reduced oxidative stress, creating a favorable environment for tissue healing.[22] Similarly, a study by Wirries et al. showed enhanced osteoblast proliferation and differentiation when exposed to herbal compounds containing thymoquinone.[23]

The baseline viability of MTA alone observed in this study aligns with established literature citing its favorable biocompatibility.[24] However, unmodified MTA is also known to release calcium hydroxide during setting, which may transiently elevate pH and affect early cell survival.[25] The addition of NS appears to modulate this response, possibly by buffering the alkaline environment and promoting a more stable and conducive surface for cell adherence and proliferation.

Previous research has demonstrated that incorporating hydration accelerators can enhance the properties of “MTA,” such as its “setting time,” thereby improving its efficiency for clinical use.[26] In this in vitro study, the addition of “NS aqueous extract” to MTA showed a promising enhancement in both biocompatibility and setting time. Specifically, Group B (MTA with 50% NS extract) shows faster setting time, compared to both Group A (MTA with 30% NS) and Group C (MTA alone). This is likely due to the bioactive components in NS, such as flavonoids, which act as a catalyst and speed up the hydration process of MTA.[917] The faster setting time may offer a clinical advantage, particularly in procedures where minimizing the waiting period is crucial for efficient workflow and patient comfort. A shorter setting time also reduces the risk of contamination and improves the handling properties of the material, which are important factors in achieving successful clinical outcomes.

This study provides a novel perspective on the potential benefits of combining NS with MTA, contributing to the limited body of research on bioactive modifications of dental materials. The use of standardized methods, such as ISO 6876 for setting time, adds rigor and ensures that findings are clinically relevant. Furthermore, the inclusion of two different concentrations of NS allowed for a comparative analysis, highlighting the dose-dependent effects of the extract on MTA’s properties.

A key limitation of this study is its in vitro design, which might not accurately reflect the complexities of “in vivo conditions.” Factors like “immune responses,” “blood flow,” and interactions with “other cellular components” in the body may alter the behavior of NS-supplemented MTA in clinical settings. In addition, only two concentrations of NS extract were tested; the ideal concentration for clinical use may vary and would require further investigation. Another limitation is the absence of long-term assessment of degradation rates, as well as potential effects on mechanical properties, such as compressive strength, which are critical for Clinical implementation.

Further studies are required to validate these results through in vivo experiments and to assess the long-term effects of NS extract on the setting behavior of MTA under physiological conditions. Exploring varied concentrations or combining NS with other bioactive agents may further enhance MTA’s performance. Molecular investigations into its influence on osteogenic signaling could clarify its role in hard tissue regeneration. In addition, evaluating the mechanical properties and clinical applicability of NS-modified MTA in procedures such as retrograde filling and pulp capping is essential. Future research should also examine patient outcomes and immunological responses to establish its clinical safety and effectiveness in restorative and endodontic regenerative procedures.

CONCLUSION

The incorporation of NS extract into MTA demonstrated a notable improvement in cytocompatibility, with significantly higher cell viability observed in the modified groups compared to unmodified MTA. The 50% NS group, in particular, showed the most favorable cellular response, indicating that the bioactive components of the extract support a more hospitable environment for osteoblast-like cells. This suggests enhanced biocompatibility, which is critical for successful outcomes in regenerative endodontic applications.

Furthermore, the addition of NS extract resulted in a modest reduction in both initial and final setting times. This improved setting behavior enhances the handling characteristics of MTA, making it more efficient for time-sensitive clinical procedures such as pulp capping or root-end fillings by minimizing chairside time and reducing the risk of contamination. Overall, this study supports the potential of NS-modified MTA as a multifunctional material with superior biocompatibility, osteogenic potential, and practical usability.
