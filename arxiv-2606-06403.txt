Second-Jet Equivariant η \eta Separations on Lens Spaces
Sanchita Sharma

Abstract
Lens spaces are useful test examples in spectral geometry because their spin Dirac eigenspaces admit explicit congruence descriptions. We use these descriptions to study equivariant η \eta invariants for three-dimensional lens spaces with the round metric and the standard coordinate-torus action, retaining the spin-Fourier character of each eigenspace rather than only the ordinary scalar η \eta value. For the square family L ⁡ ( ℓ 2 , ℓ − 1 ) L(\ell^{2},\ell-1) and L ⁡ ( ℓ 2 , 2 ​ ℓ − 1 ) L(\ell^{2},2\ell-1) , with ℓ ≥ 5 \ell\geq 5 odd, we obtain a residual-circle equivariant η \eta separation: the ordinary η \eta values agree, and the first derivative of the residual η \eta germ vanishes by symmetry, but the second derivative is nonzero. For L ⁡ ( 25 , 4 ) L(25,4) versus L ⁡ ( 25 , 9 ) L(25,9) , the normalized second derivative is − 6080 -6080 . Thus, the residual-circle equivariant η \eta germ detects a distinction invisible to the ordinary η \eta invariant. The calculation uses spin-Fourier residues directly; perturbative Hessian signs serve only as motivation and are not part of the invariant.

Introduction

Lens spaces form one of the standard families of spherical space forms and have long served as test examples in topology, spectral geometry, and global analysis. We write

The Dirac spectrum of spherical space forms, including lens spaces, is classical. We use the representation-theoretic description of spin Dirac eigenspaces due to Bär and Gilkey, together with the standard spin-geometric conventions of Friedrich and Lawson-Michelsohn
[

\eta

Instead of retaining only the scalar asymmetry between positive and negative eigenvalues, one can retain character data. For lens spaces, this refinement is especially concrete: the eigenspaces are described explicitly, and the group action is visible at the level of spin weights. The scalar
\eta

The coordinate torus acting on
S^{3}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
gives the equivariant structure used below. It rotates the two complex coordinates independently. On spinors, these rotations lift to spinorial characters. A one-variable character sees only the weight coming from one coordinate circle, while a two-variable character retains both coordinate weights. Passing from one variable to two variables is formally small but conceptually important: a one-variable specialization can lose information that remains visible in the full torus-equivariant
\eta

The classical lens-space Dirac spectrum and the general equivariant
\eta
formalism are known, but explicit examples are still useful when the scalar
\eta

\eta

The answer is a residual-circle second-jet separation. A residual circle is a one-parameter circle inside the coordinate torus. Restricting the torus-equivariant
\eta

\eta

\eta
difference also vanishes for the square family, while the second derivative is nonzero.

The first explicit pair is

\eta

-6080

Let

P=\ell^{2},\qquad q_{1}=\ell-1,\qquad q_{2}=2\ell-1,

(1.3)

M_{\ell,1}=L(P,q_{1}),\qquad M_{\ell,2}=L(P,q_{2}).

(1.4)

For the residual-circle specialization of the

C_{\ell}=-\frac{\ell^{2}(\ell-1)^{2}(\ell+1)(11\ell^{2}+20\ell+81)}{180}.

(1.5)

The normalization factor relating this finite sum to a Donnelly-APS convention is fixed in

The theorem is an equivariant spin statement. The chosen coordinate torus action is part of the data. We do not claim a classification theorem for lens spaces, nor do we claim that the same pairs are invisible to all finite-cyclic APS rho invariants. For the chosen coordinate-equivariant spin data, the scalar
\eta

A perturbative Hessian calculation is included only as motivation. It explains why the second coordinate spin weight should not be discarded. The Hessian signs themselves depend on a perturbation chamber, so they are not used as invariants. The invariant calculation instead retains both coordinate spin weights directly and assembles them into the torus-equivariant
\eta

The representation-ring and equivariant

[
20

2
Spin-Fourier residues of lens-space Dirac eigenspaces

2.1
Spin double-cover target

We first fix the equivariant character ring in which the lens-space Dirac eigenspaces will be placed. In the lower-dimensional equivariant models that motivate this calculation, the secondary representation data are often written using an ordinary circle variable. For spin Dirac operators on lens spaces, however, the natural target is slightly finer. The coordinate circle action on the underlying manifold lifts to the spinor bundle through the spin double cover, so the corresponding eigenspace characters are naturally characters of
\operatorname{Spin}(2)
, not only of
SO(2)
.

Let
L(p,q)=S^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, with generator
\gamma(z_{1},z_{2})=(\omega z_{1},\omega^{q}z_{2})
and
\omega=\exp(2\pi i/p)
. Here
p\geq 3
is odd unless stated otherwise, and
q
is coprime to
p
. We use the coordinate circle action
e^{i\theta}\cdot[z_{1},z_{2}]=[e^{i\theta}z_{1},z_{2}]
. This is an
SO(2)
-action on the lens space. After passing to spinors, we keep the lifted action on the spinor bundle, whose weights lie naturally in the character group of
\operatorname{Spin}(2)
.

Let
u
denote the fundamental character of
\operatorname{Spin}(2)
. A spin weight will be written as
\mu=\frac{1}{2}(a_{1},a_{2}),a_{1},a_{2}\in 2\mathbb{Z}+1.
The coordinate action rotating
z_{1}
selects the first spin weight. Thus the corresponding spin-Fourier monomial is
\mu=\frac{1}{2}(a_{1},a_{2})\longmapsto u^{a_{1}}.
The appearance of odd exponents reflects the spinorial nature of the lifted circle action. For this reason, forcing the calculation prematurely into the ordinary
SO(2)
-character variable would obscure the natural representation-theoretic structure.

Definition 2.1

(Spin-Fourier character)
.

Let
E
be a finite-dimensional
\operatorname{Spin}(2)
-representation arising from a Dirac eigenspace on
L(p,q)
. Suppose that
E
decomposes into spin weights as
E=\bigoplus_{a\in 2\mathbb{Z}+1}E_{a}.
Its spin-Fourier character is defined by
\chi_{\operatorname{Spin}}(E;u)=\sum_{a\in 2\mathbb{Z}+1}\dim(E_{a})u^{a}.

The reflection symmetry, when present, pairs opposite spin weights. Thus, the secondary Fourier sector is naturally generated by the symmetric combinations
A_{n}(u)=u^{n}+u^{-n},n\geq 1.
In the lens-space examples below, the relevant exponents
n
are typically odd, because the spin weights are half-integral before being written in the
u
-variable.

Definition 2.2

(Reduced spin-Fourier target)
.

The reduced spin-Fourier target is the quotient of the reflection-invariant spin-character ring by the trivial character. We denote it by
\widetilde{R}_{\operatorname{spin}}=R(\operatorname{Spin}(2))^{\mathbb{Z}/2}/\mathbb{Z}\mathbf{1}.
Equivalently, it is the free abelian group generated by the nontrivial symmetric modes:
\widetilde{R}_{\operatorname{spin}}=\bigoplus_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{Z}\langle A_{n}(u)\rangle,A_{n}(u)=u^{n}+u^{-n}.

The reduction by the trivial character is the spinorial analogue of the reduced secondary target used in the
O(2)
-equivariant formalism. It removes the scalar dimension part and keeps only the nontrivial spin-Fourier information. The examples below are designed to detect cases where ordinary dimensions agree but spin-Fourier characters differ.

Let
E_{k}^{+}(p,q)
and
E_{k}^{-}(p,q)
denote the eigenspaces of the Dirac operator on
L(p,q)
corresponding to the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}^{\pm}=\pm\left(k+\frac{3}{2}\right),k\geq 0.
Their spin-Fourier characters will be denoted by
\chi_{k}^{+}(p,q;u)=\chi_{\operatorname{Spin}}(E_{k}^{+}(p,q);u)
and
\chi_{k}^{-}(p,q;u)=\chi_{\operatorname{Spin}}(E_{k}^{-}(p,q);u)
. The basic virtual spectral difference at level
k
is
\Delta_{k}(p,q;u)=\chi_{k}^{+}(p,q;u)-\chi_{k}^{-}(p,q;u)
. Its reduced class is
\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}(p,q;u)=[\Delta_{k}(p,q;u)]_{\operatorname{red}}\in\widetilde{R}_{\operatorname{spin}}
. This is an equivariant spin-spectral quantity: the chosen lifted coordinate circle action determines which spin weight is seen by
u
.

The reduced spin-Fourier character can see information invisible to ordinary dimension. For example, two eigenspaces may have the same dimension,
\dim E_{k}^{+}(p,q)=\dim E_{\ell}^{-}(p,q),
but different spin-Fourier characters:
\chi_{k}^{+}(p,q;u)\neq\chi_{\ell}^{-}(p,q;u).
In that case the reduced difference
\chi_{k}^{+}(p,q;u)-\chi_{\ell}^{-}(p,q;u)
can be nonzero even though the ordinary dimension difference vanishes.

This is the basic mechanism used in the lens-space computation. The known Dirac spectrum supplies the eigenspaces. The spin-Fourier refinement keeps the weight data of those eigenspaces under the lifted coordinate circle action. The reduced character then extracts a secondary representation-valued residue.

Example 2.3

(The form of the first lens-space residues)
.

In the examples below, one obtains reduced differences of the form
A_{m}(u)-A_{n}(u),A_{j}(u)=u^{j}+u^{-j}.
For instance, the first nontrivial
L(5,2)
calculation produces
A_{1}(u)-A_{3}(u)=(u+u^{-1})-(u^{3}+u^{-3}).
This difference has zero ordinary dimension after evaluation at
u=1
:
\left(A_{1}(1)-A_{3}(1)\right)=2-2=0.
Nevertheless, it is nonzero in the reduced spin-Fourier target:
A_{1}(u)-A_{3}(u)\neq 0\ \text{in}\ \widetilde{R}_{\operatorname{spin}}.

Proposition 2.4

(Spin-cover target for the lens-space Dirac computation)
.

The natural equivariant target for the lens-space Dirac eigenspace calculation is the reduced spin-Fourier target
\widetilde{R}_{\operatorname{spin}}
, rather than the ordinary reduced
SO(2)
-target. In this target, the level-wise reduced spectral difference
\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}(p,q;u)=[\chi_{k}^{+}(p,q;u)-\chi_{k}^{-}(p,q;u)]_{\operatorname{red}}
retains the nontrivial spinorial Fourier information of the Dirac eigenspaces.

Proof.

The coordinate circle action on
L(p,q)
lifts naturally to the spinor bundle through the spin double cover. Consequently, the weights of the lifted action are most naturally expressed through the
\operatorname{Spin}(2)
-character variable
u
. A spin weight
\mu=\frac{1}{2}(a_{1},a_{2})
contributes
u^{a_{1}}
, and since
a_{1}
is odd in the spin lattice, the resulting characters naturally contain odd powers of
u
.

The ordinary dimension of a character is obtained by evaluating at
u=1
. Therefore the quotient by the trivial character removes the scalar dimension part and retains precisely the nontrivial spin-Fourier modes. The expression
\chi_{k}^{+}(p,q;u)-\chi_{k}^{-}(p,q;u)
therefore measures the difference between the positive and negative Dirac eigenspaces at the level of spin-Fourier characters. Passing to the quotient by
\mathbb{Z}\mathbf{1}
gives a well-defined reduced secondary quantity in
\widetilde{R}_{\operatorname{spin}}
.
∎

This spin-cover refinement is the first step in replacing the earlier toy scalar congruence by the actual Dirac-spinor calculation. The next step is to compute the characters
\chi_{k}^{\pm}(p,q;u)
using the affine congruence-lattice formula for the Dirac spectrum on lens spaces.

2.2
Affine congruence lattice and parity-shell formula

We replace the scalar congruence used in the preliminary examples by the actual spinor congruence formula for Dirac eigenspaces on lens spaces. The Dirac spectrum of lens spaces is known, and we use the known affine congruence-lattice description of the spin Dirac spectrum and retain the
\operatorname{Spin}(2)
-Fourier weight information which will later feed into the reduced residue.

For odd
p
,
L(p,q)
has a unique spin structure. The even-
p
case requires keeping track of the choice of spin structure and is not needed for the first lens-space computation.

Recall that
L(p,q)=S^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{p},\gamma(z_{1},z_{2})=(\omega z_{1},\omega^{q}z_{2}),\omega=\exp(2\pi i/p).
The spin weights are written as
\mu=\frac{1}{2}(a_{1},a_{2}),a_{1},a_{2}\in 2\mathbb{Z}+1.
The lens-space descent condition is the spinor congruence
a_{1}+qa_{2}\equiv 0\pmod{p}.
This congruence is the spinorial analogue of the scalar monomial congruence, but it is not the same object. The oddness of
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
reflects the half-integral spin weights.

Definition 2.5

(Affine congruence lattice)
.

For odd
p
and
q\in(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
, define the affine spin congruence lattice by

\mathcal{L}(p,q)=\left\{(a_{1},a_{2})\in(2\mathbb{Z}+1)^{2}:\ a_{1}+qa_{2}\equiv 0\pmod{p}\right\}.

(2.1)

A point
a=(a_{1},a_{2})\in\mathcal{L}(p,q)
contributes the spin-Fourier monomial
a=(a_{1},a_{2})\longmapsto u^{a_{1}}.

The ordinary Dirac multiplicity is obtained by counting lattice points. For the present purpose we do not only count them; we keep track of the first coordinate
a_{1}
, because
a_{1}
is the spin-Fourier weight for the lifted circle action rotating
z_{1}
.

Two elementary functions on lattice points enter the Dirac formula.

Definition 2.6

(One-norm and sign parity)
.

For
a=(a_{1},a_{2})\in(2\mathbb{Z}+1)^{2}
, define the spin one-norm by
\|a\|_{1}=\frac{|a_{1}|+|a_{2}|}{2}.
Define the sign-counting parity by
R(a)=\#\{j\in\{1,2\}:a_{j}<0\}.
Thus
R(a)
is the number of negative entries among
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
.

The eigenvalues of the round Dirac operator on a three-dimensional lens space are
\lambda_{k}^{\pm}=\pm\left(k+\frac{3}{2}\right),k\geq 0.
The eigenspaces for
\lambda_{k}^{+}
and
\lambda_{k}^{-}
are not obtained from only the outer shell
\|a\|_{1}=k+1
. The correct formula involves a finite sum over lower shells. This is why the naive scalar-shell calculation is not correct.

For each
k\geq 0
and
0\leq r\leq k
, define the shell

\mathcal{L}_{k,r}(p,q)=\left\{a\in\mathcal{L}(p,q):\ \|a\|_{1}=k-r+1\right\}.

(2.2)

Equivalently, this shell consists of those odd pairs satisfying
|a_{1}|+|a_{2}|=2(k-r+1).
The integer
r
labels how far below the outer shell the lattice point lies.

The positive and negative Dirac branches are separated by the parity of
R(a)
. With the conventions used here, the negative branch is selected by
R(a)\equiv r\pmod{2}.
The positive branch is selected by
R(a)\equiv r+1\pmod{2}.
This is the
m=2
cyclic-lens-space specialization of Bär’s spherical-space-form generating-function formula for the positive and negative Dirac multiplicity series, together with the usual lens-space convention for the diagonal cyclic action
[
8
, Theorem 2]

[
4
, Chapter 5]
. A different global choice of spin-lift convention would interchange the labels of the two branches, but the relative formulas below are written consistently with the convention displayed here.

Definition 2.7

(Positive and negative parity shells)
.

For
k\geq 0
and
0\leq r\leq k
, define

\mathcal{L}_{k,r}^{-}(p,q)=\left\{a\in\mathcal{L}_{k,r}(p,q):\ R(a)\equiv r\pmod{2}\right\}.

(2.3)

Similarly, define

\mathcal{L}_{k,r}^{+}(p,q)=\left\{a\in\mathcal{L}_{k,r}(p,q):\ R(a)\equiv r+1\pmod{2}\right\}.

(2.4)

We can now state the spin-Fourier character formula used in the rest of the lens-space calculation.

Proposition 2.8

(Spin-Fourier character formula)
.

For odd
p
, the
\operatorname{Spin}(2)
-Fourier characters of the Dirac eigenspaces on
L(p,q)
are given by
\chi_{k}^{+}(p,q;u)=\sum_{r=0}^{k}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{L}_{k,r}^{+}(p,q)}u^{a_{1}}.
The negative-eigenvalue character is
\chi_{k}^{-}(p,q;u)=\sum_{r=0}^{k}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{L}_{k,r}^{-}(p,q)}u^{a_{1}}.
Consequently, the level-
k
virtual spin-Fourier difference is
\Delta_{k}(p,q;u)=\chi_{k}^{+}(p,q;u)-\chi_{k}^{-}(p,q;u)
, and its reduced class is
\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}(p,q;u)=[\Delta_{k}(p,q;u)]_{\operatorname{red}}
.

Proof.

The known spin Dirac spectrum on a three-dimensional lens space may be expressed in terms of half-integral spin weights satisfying the affine congruence condition. In the present notation these weights are
\mu=\frac{1}{2}(a_{1},a_{2})
, with
a_{1},a_{2}
odd and satisfying
a_{1}+qa_{2}\equiv 0\pmod{p}.
The eigenvalue level
k
receives contributions from finitely many shells indexed by
r=0,\ldots,k
. The shell condition is
\|a\|_{1}=k-r+1.
The sign of the Dirac eigenvalue is determined by the parity of the number of negative entries. The negative branch corresponds to
R(a)\equiv r\pmod{2}.
The positive branch corresponds to
R(a)\equiv r+1\pmod{2}.
Finally, the lifted coordinate circle action selects the first spin weight, so a surviving lattice point contributes
u^{a_{1}}
. Summing these contributions over the positive and negative parity shells gives the stated formulas for
\chi_{k}^{+}(p,q;u)
and
\chi_{k}^{-}(p,q;u)
.
∎

2.3
Two-variable spin-Fourier residue

The one-variable residue
\Delta_{k}(p,q;u)
keeps only the first spin exponent
a_{1}
. This is natural for the coordinate
\Spin(2)
-action rotating
z_{1}
, but it discards the second exponent
a_{2}
. This second exponent is part of the equivariant spectral data and should be retained directly. We therefore introduce the full two-variable residue.

Definition 2.9

(Two-variable spin-Fourier characters)
.

For
k\geq 0
, define

\widehat{\chi}_{k}^{+}(p,q;u,v)=\sum_{r=0}^{k}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{L}_{k,r}^{+}(p,q)}u^{a_{1}}v^{a_{2}}.

(2.5)

Similarly, define

\widehat{\chi}_{k}^{-}(p,q;u,v)=\sum_{r=0}^{k}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{L}_{k,r}^{-}(p,q)}u^{a_{1}}v^{a_{2}}.

(2.6)

The two-variable spin-Fourier residue is

\widehat{\Delta}_{k}(p,q;u,v)=\widehat{\chi}_{k}^{+}(p,q;u,v)-\widehat{\chi}_{k}^{-}(p,q;u,v).

(2.7)

The variables
u
and
v
encode the two coordinate spin weights. Thus
\widehat{\Delta}_{k}
is a finite Laurent polynomial in two variables. It is attached to the round coordinate-equivariant spin datum, not merely to the bare lens space.

Proposition 2.10

(The one-variable residue is a specialization)
.

For every odd
p
, every
q\in(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
, and every level
k\geq 0
, one has

\widehat{\Delta}_{k}(p,q;u,1)=\Delta_{k}(p,q;u).

(2.8)

Proof.

By definition,

\widehat{\chi}_{k}^{+}(p,q;u,1)=\sum_{r=0}^{k}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{L}_{k,r}^{+}(p,q)}u^{a_{1}}.

(2.9)

This is exactly
\chi_{k}^{+}(p,q;u)
. The same argument gives

\widehat{\chi}_{k}^{-}(p,q;u,1)=\chi_{k}^{-}(p,q;u).

(2.10)

Subtracting the two identities gives the claim.
∎

Example 2.11

(The first
L(5,2)
two-variable residue)
.

For
L(5,2)
, the first useful level is
k=1
. The relevant positive sectors are

(1,-3),\qquad(-1,3).

(2.11)

The relevant negative sectors are

(3,1),\qquad(-3,-1).

(2.12)

Therefore

\widehat{\Delta}_{1}(5,2;u,v)=uv^{-3}+u^{-1}v^{3}-u^{3}v-u^{-3}v^{-1}.

(2.13)

After specializing to
v=1
, this becomes

\widehat{\Delta}_{1}(5,2;u,1)=u+u^{-1}-u^{3}-u^{-3}.

(2.14)

Equivalently,

\widehat{\Delta}_{1}(5,2;u,1)=A_{1}(u)-A_{3}(u).

(2.15)

Thus the one-variable residue is the specialization of the full two-variable residue at
v=1
.

Remark 2.12

(Why the second coordinate is retained)
.

The one-variable residue forgets the second spin exponent. Any perturbative attempt to recover this information indirectly depends on the chosen perturbation and its chamber. The cleaner invariant bookkeeping is therefore to keep the monomial
v^{a_{2}}
from the beginning in
\widehat{\Delta}_{k}(p,q;u,v)
.

Definition 2.13

(Symmetric two-variable modes)
.

When the reflection symmetry pairs
(a_{1},a_{2})
with
(-a_{1},-a_{2})
, it is convenient to write

A_{m,n}(u,v)=u^{m}v^{n}+u^{-m}v^{-n}.

(2.16)

In this notation, the one-variable mode
A_{m}(u)
is the specialization

A_{m,n}(u,1)=A_{m}(u).

(2.17)

Proposition 2.14

(Two-variable refinement)
.

The two-variable residue
\widehat{\Delta}_{k}(p,q;u,v)
is at least as fine as the one-variable residue
\Delta_{k}(p,q;u)
. More precisely, if

\widehat{\Delta}_{k}(p,q;u,v)\neq\widehat{\Delta}_{k}(p,q^{\prime};u,v),

(2.18)

while

\widehat{\Delta}_{k}(p,q;u,1)=\widehat{\Delta}_{k}(p,q^{\prime};u,1),

(2.19)

then the two coordinate-equivariant spin data are separated by the full
T^{2}

Proof.

∎

2.5
Equivariant interpretation and lens-space equivalences

The representative table in
Section 2.4
, together with the full table in
Appendix A
, shows that the reduced spin-Fourier residue depends on the arithmetic parameter
q
. Before using this dependence as a distinguishing statement, one has to check what kind of invariant is being computed. This subsection separates three notions: the bare lens space, the oriented lens space, and the equivariant spin lens space with a specified coordinate circle action.

The residue constructed above depends on the lifted circle action, the round metric used to group eigenspaces by Dirac level, and the level under consideration. The object being studied is the finite-level round equivariant package
\left(L(p,q),\ \text{spin structure},\ \operatorname{Spin}(2)\text{-action rotating }z_{1},\ g_{\text{round}},\ k\right).
For odd
p
, the spin structure is unique; the coordinate action and the level remain part of the data.

We recall the action:
e^{i\theta}\cdot[z_{1},z_{2}]=[e^{i\theta}z_{1},z_{2}].
This action selects the first spin weight
a_{1}
, so a lattice point
a=(a_{1},a_{2})
contributes the spin-Fourier monomial
u^{a_{1}}.
If a diffeomorphism changes the coordinate used to define the circle action, then it can change the spin-Fourier character even when it preserves the ordinary Dirac multiplicities.

Definition 2.21

(Equivariant spin lens-space datum)
.

For the purposes of the reduced spin-Fourier residue, a finite-level round equivariant spin lens-space datum is the tuple
\mathcal{X}^{[k]}_{p,q}=\left(L(p,q),\ \mathfrak{s},\ \rho_{1},\ g_{\text{round}},\ k\right),
where
\mathfrak{s}
is the spin structure,
\rho_{1}
is the lifted coordinate circle action rotating
z_{1}
,
g_{\text{round}}
is the round metric, and
k
is the Dirac level being tracked. For odd
p
, the spin structure is unique, so the essential extra data are the chosen coordinate action, the round metric, and the level.

With this terminology, the level-wise virtual spin-Fourier difference
\Delta_{k}(p,q;u)=\chi_{k}^{+}(p,q;u)-\chi_{k}^{-}(p,q;u)
, and hence its reduced class
\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}(p,q;u)
, are naturally associated to
\mathcal{X}^{[k]}_{p,q}
, not merely to the unoriented diffeomorphism type of
L(p,q)
.

The sign change
q\mapsto-q

The first consistency check concerns the transformation
q\mapsto-q
. The congruence for
L(p,q)
is
a_{1}+qa_{2}\equiv 0\pmod{p}.
The congruence for
L(p,-q)
is
a_{1}-qa_{2}\equiv 0\pmod{p}.
There is a simple bijection between the two congruence lattices:
(a_{1},a_{2})\longmapsto(a_{1},-a_{2}).
Indeed, if
a_{1}+qa_{2}\equiv 0\pmod{p},
then
a_{1}-q(-a_{2})\equiv 0\pmod{p}.
This map preserves the one-norm:
\frac{|a_{1}|+|a_{2}|}{2}=\frac{|a_{1}|+|-a_{2}|}{2}.
It also preserves the spin-Fourier monomial:
u^{a_{1}}\longmapsto u^{a_{1}}.
However, it changes the parity of the number of negative entries. If
R(a)=\#\{j:a_{j}<0\},
then
R(a_{1},-a_{2})\equiv R(a_{1},a_{2})+1\pmod{2}.
Thus the positive and negative parity shells are interchanged.

Proposition 2.22

(Orientation sign rule)
.

For every
k\geq 0
, the level-wise spin-Fourier difference satisfies
\Delta_{k}(p,-q;u)=-\Delta_{k}(p,q;u).

Proof.

The map
(a_{1},a_{2})\longmapsto(a_{1},-a_{2})
is a bijection between the affine congruence lattice for
q
and the affine congruence lattice for
-q
. It preserves the one-norm shell and the monomial
u^{a_{1}}
. Since it changes
R(a)
by one modulo
2
, it sends positive parity shells for
q
to negative parity shells for
-q
, and negative parity shells for
q
to positive parity shells for
-q
. Hence
\chi_{k}^{+}(p,-q;u)=\chi_{k}^{-}(p,q;u).
Similarly,
\chi_{k}^{-}(p,-q;u)=\chi_{k}^{+}(p,q;u).
Subtracting gives
\Delta_{k}(p,-q;u)=-\Delta_{k}(p,q;u).
∎

Remark 2.23
.

This sign rule is consistent with the interpretation of
\Delta_{k}(p,q;u)
as an oriented spectral quantity. Replacing
q
by
-q
reverses the parity assignment between the positive and negative Dirac branches, and the reduced difference changes sign.

The coordinate exchange
q\mapsto q^{-1}

The second consistency check concerns the transformation
q\mapsto q^{-1}
. In the ordinary theory of lens spaces, this transformation is related to exchanging the two coordinates of
S^{3}
. At the level of spin weights, this exchange sends
(a_{1},a_{2})\longmapsto(a_{2},a_{1}).
Suppose that
a_{1}+qa_{2}\equiv 0\pmod{p}.
Multiplying by
q^{-1}
gives
q^{-1}a_{1}+a_{2}\equiv 0\pmod{p}.
Equivalently,
a_{2}+q^{-1}a_{1}\equiv 0\pmod{p}.
Thus the coordinate exchange identifies the congruence for
q
with the congruence for
q^{-1}
.

The one-norm is preserved:
\frac{|a_{1}|+|a_{2}|}{2}=\frac{|a_{2}|+|a_{1}|}{2}.
The sign-counting parity is also preserved:
R(a_{2},a_{1})=R(a_{1},a_{2}).
Therefore the ordinary positive and negative Dirac multiplicities are preserved under this coordinate exchange.

However, the spin-Fourier character used here is not preserved as a character in the same variable. Before the coordinate exchange, the monomial is
u^{a_{1}}.
After the coordinate exchange, the first coordinate is
a_{2}
, and the corresponding monomial is
u^{a_{2}}.
Thus the coordinate exchange preserves the ordinary multiplicity data but changes the chosen circle weight.

Proposition 2.24

(Action-dependence under coordinate exchange)
.

The transformation
q\mapsto q^{-1}
preserves the ordinary Dirac multiplicity data after exchanging the coordinates of
S^{3}
, but it does not preserve the spin-Fourier character attached to the fixed
z_{1}
-rotation action.

Proof.

The map
(a_{1},a_{2})\longmapsto(a_{2},a_{1})
identifies the affine congruence lattice for
q
with the affine congruence lattice for
q^{-1}
. It preserves both the one-norm and the parity
R(a)
. Therefore it preserves the ordinary positive and negative multiplicities obtained by evaluating the spin-Fourier characters at
u=1
.

However, the spin-Fourier character is defined using the first coordinate. Under the coordinate exchange, the first coordinate changes from
a_{1}
to
a_{2}
. Hence the monomial
u^{a_{1}}
is transformed into
u^{a_{2}}
. Unless the coordinate circle action is transported along the diffeomorphism, this gives a different character. Thus the spin-Fourier residue is not invariant under
q\mapsto q^{-1}
as a character for the fixed
z_{1}
-rotation action.
∎

Thus the table entries list equivariant spin-spectral residues for a specified coordinate action, rather than ordinary lens-space invariants.

Examples from the residue tables

Let
A_{n}(u)=u^{n}+u^{-n}.
For
p=7
, the table gives
\Delta_{\text{first}}(7,2;u)=A_{1}(u)-A_{5}(u).
Since
-2\equiv 5\pmod{7},
the sign rule predicts
\Delta_{\text{first}}(7,5;u)=-\Delta_{\text{first}}(7,2;u).
Indeed, the table gives
\Delta_{\text{first}}(7,5;u)=A_{5}(u)-A_{1}(u).
Similarly,
\Delta_{\text{first}}(7,3;u)=A_{1}(u)-A_{3}(u).
Since
-3\equiv 4\pmod{7},
the sign rule predicts
\Delta_{\text{first}}(7,4;u)=-\Delta_{\text{first}}(7,3;u).
The table gives
\Delta_{\text{first}}(7,4;u)=A_{3}(u)-A_{1}(u).
On the other hand,
2^{-1}\equiv 4\pmod{7}.
The residues
\Delta_{\text{first}}(7,2;u)=A_{1}(u)-A_{5}(u)
and
\Delta_{\text{first}}(7,4;u)=A_{3}(u)-A_{1}(u)
are not equal. They are also not simply negatives of each other. This reflects the change in the circle action under coordinate exchange.

Proposition 2.25

(Equivariant nature of the residue)
.

The reduced spin-Fourier residue
\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}(p,q;u)
is naturally a finite-level equivariant spin-spectral quantity attached to
\mathcal{X}^{[k]}_{p,q}=\left(L(p,q),\mathfrak{s},\rho_{1},g_{\text{round}},k\right),
where
\rho_{1}
is the lifted coordinate circle action rotating
z_{1}
. It is not, by itself, a smooth or topological invariant of the bare unoriented lens space.

Proof.

The construction of
\widetilde{\Delta}_{k}(p,q;u)
uses the spin-Fourier monomial
u^{a_{1}}
. This monomial is determined by the chosen coordinate circle action rotating
z_{1}
. A diffeomorphism which preserves the lens space but transports the coordinate action may change the chosen spin weight. Therefore the residue is attached to the equivariant spin package, not merely to the underlying manifold. The sign rule under
q\mapsto-q
and the coordinate-exchange behavior under
q\mapsto q^{-1}
show explicitly how the residue depends on this additional equivariant data.
∎

The table entries are therefore consistent with orientation reversal, while their behavior under
q\mapsto q^{-1}
reflects the dependence on the chosen coordinate action. This is the equivariant category used in the
\eta
construction below.

2.6
Output of the spin-Fourier calculation

The preceding subsections give the finite-level equivariant data used later. For odd
p
, the round spin Dirac eigenspaces are computed from

\mathcal{L}(p,q)=\left\{(a_{1},a_{2})\in(2\mathbb{Z}+1)^{2}:a_{1}+qa_{2}\equiv 0\pmod{p}\right\},

(2.26)

with the one-norm shell condition and the parity split between the positive and negative branches. The one-variable character for the
z_{1}
-circle is

\Delta_{k}(p,q;u)=\chi_{k}^{+}(p,q;u)-\chi_{k}^{-}(p,q;u),

(2.27)

and the coordinate-torus refinement is

\widehat{\Delta}_{k}(p,q;u,v)=\widehat{\chi}_{k}^{+}(p,q;u,v)-\widehat{\chi}_{k}^{-}(p,q;u,v).

(2.28)

They are related by

\widehat{\Delta}_{k}(p,q;u,1)=\Delta_{k}(p,q;u).

(2.29)

These finite Laurent polynomials are attached to the chosen coordinate-equivariant spin datum. The
v=1
specialization is the one used by the residual circle in the main
\eta
calculation, while the two-variable form gives the ambient
T^{2}
-bookkeeping and explains which information is lost after choosing a single coordinate circle.

3
Perturbative diagnostic and why it is not the invariant

The earlier route to the second spin weight was perturbative. One perturbs the Dirac operator by a zero-order self-adjoint term and studies the induced crossing form on a fixed eigenspace. This diagnostic helps identify the missing spin-weight information, but it is not the main invariant here. The APS spectral-flow and equivariant spectral-flow motivation for this diagnostic comes from the coauthored warped-cylinder models
[
22
,
23
]
.

Let
Y=L(p,q)
, and let
D
be the spin Dirac operator on
Y
. For a real one-form
\vartheta
, with Clifford convention
c(\xi)^{*}=-c(\xi)
, the operator

K_{\vartheta}=ic(\vartheta)

(3.1)

is self-adjoint. The perturbed path is

D_{\alpha}=D+\alpha K_{\vartheta}.

(3.2)

At an eigenspace
E_{\lambda}=\ker(D-\lambda I)
, the first-order crossing form is

Q_{\lambda}(\psi,\phi)=\langle\psi,K_{\vartheta}\phi\rangle_{L^{2}}.

(3.3)

If
\vartheta
is compatible with the residual circle action, this form decomposes over spin-Fourier weights.

For the natural pure one-form perturbations considered here, the first-order compression can vanish on the relevant weight spaces. The next diagnostic is then the second-order coefficient obtained from the reduced resolvent,

h(\psi)=-\sum_{\mu\neq\lambda}\frac{\|P_{\mu}K_{\vartheta}\psi\|^{2}}{\mu-\lambda},

(3.4)

where
P_{\mu}
denotes projection onto the
\mu
-eigenspace of
D
. This coefficient can distinguish different spin-weight channels, and in small examples its signs track information carried by the second coordinate spin weight.

However, these signs depend on the perturbation chamber. Varying the perturbation can change the ordering and signs of the second-order channels. Thus the second-order Hessian residue is not used below as a perturbation-free invariant. Its role is only explanatory: it shows why the one-variable residue is incomplete and why the second coordinate should be retained directly in the two-variable spin-Fourier residue.

This diagnostic viewpoint is close in spirit to the relation between Maslov index, spectral flow, and Dirac boundary data in the work of Booß-Bavnbek-Furutani, Booß-Wojciechowski, Kirk-Lesch, and Nicolaescu
[
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
.

The eta-theoretic calculation below uses the spin-Fourier data directly, not the chamber-dependent Hessian signs.

4
Scalar shadows and ordinary
\eta
values

The results in this section are residue-level separation statements. The first check shows that ordinary same-level scalar multiplicity does not determine the reduced Spin-Fourier residue. The second check adds equality of the ordinary non-equivariant
\eta
shadow. The final check promotes the same residue-level phenomenon from an isolated example to a square-modulus family.

4.1
Separation from ordinary scalar multiplicity

Let
\operatorname{ev}_{1}:R(\operatorname{Spin}(2))\to\mathbb{Z}
denote evaluation at
u=1
. This map forgets the Spin-Fourier weights and remembers only ordinary dimension. For a fixed level
k
, define the same-level scalar multiplicity datum by

m_{k}(p,q)=\left(\dim E_{k}^{+}(p,q),\dim E_{k}^{-}(p,q)\right).

(4.1)

Equivalently,

m_{k}(p,q)=\left(\chi_{k}^{+}(p,q;1),\chi_{k}^{-}(p,q;1)\right).

(4.2)

Theorem 4.1

(Scalar multiplicity does not determine the reduced Spin-Fourier residue)
.

There exist equivariant spin lens-space data with the same scalar multiplicity datum at the same Dirac level but with different reduced Spin-Fourier residues. Moreover, this phenomenon is not explained merely by orientation reversal, coordinate exchange, or their composition.

Proof.

Consider the two equivariant spin lens-space data

\mathcal{X}^{[3]}_{11,2}=\left(L(11,2),\mathfrak{s},\rho_{1},g_{\text{round}},3\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{X}^{[3]}_{11,4}=\left(L(11,4),\mathfrak{s},\rho_{1},g_{\text{round}},3\right).

(4.3)

At level
k=3
, the affine parity-shell computation gives

\chi_{3}^{+}(11,2;u)=A_{1}(u)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\chi_{3}^{-}(11,2;u)=A_{5}(u).

(4.4)

Therefore

\Delta_{3}(11,2;u)=A_{1}(u)-A_{5}(u).

(4.5)

For the second datum, the same computation gives

\chi_{3}^{+}(11,4;u)=A_{1}(u)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\chi_{3}^{-}(11,4;u)=A_{7}(u).

(4.6)

Therefore

\Delta_{3}(11,4;u)=A_{1}(u)-A_{7}(u).

(4.7)

Evaluating at
u=1
, one has

A_{n}(1)=2

(4.8)

for every
n\geq 1
. Hence both examples have the same same-level scalar multiplicity datum:

m_{3}(11,2)=(2,2)\qquad\text{and}\qquad m_{3}(11,4)=(2,2).

(4.9)

However,

A_{1}(u)-A_{5}(u)\neq A_{1}(u)-A_{7}(u)

(4.10)

in the reduced Spin-Fourier target, because
A_{5}(u)
and
A_{7}(u)
are distinct reduced generators.

It remains to check that the pair is not explained by the elementary transformations already discussed. Modulo
11
, one has

-2\equiv 9,\qquad 2^{-1}\equiv 6,\qquad-2^{-1}\equiv 5.

(4.11)

The value
4
is none of
9
,
6
, or
5
. Therefore
q=4
is not obtained from
q=2
by orientation reversal, coordinate exchange, or the composition of those two operations. This proves the claim.
∎

This is a same-level scalar statement. It does not assert equality of the full ordinary Dirac spectra of
L(11,2)
and
L(11,4)
; it only says that, at this level, the scalar multiplicity agrees while the reduced Spin-Fourier residue differs.

4.2
Separation from ordinary
\eta
and same-level scalar multiplicity

The scalar-blindness result can be strengthened. The reduced Spin-Fourier residue can distinguish equivariant spin lens-space data even when the ordinary Dirac
\eta
invariant agrees and the same-level scalar multiplicity datum also agrees.

Let
\eta_{D}(L(p,q))
denote the ordinary Dirac
\eta
invariant of the three-dimensional spin lens space
L(p,q)
. To avoid using an unverified normalization convention, we separate the normalization from the trigonometric part of the finite lens-space formula. Define

\mathfrak{E}(P,q)=\sum_{r=1}^{P-1}\operatorname{csc}\left(\frac{(P+1)r\pi}{P}\right)\operatorname{csc}\left(\frac{(P+1)qr\pi}{P}\right).

(4.12)

The standard lens-space
\eta
formula, equivalently the fixed-point/deck-average form of Donnelly’s equivariant
\eta
formula in this setting, expresses
\eta_{D}(L(P,q))
as a normalization factor depending only on
P
, multiplied by
\mathfrak{E}(P,q)

[
14
]
. The equality statements below use only equality of
\mathfrak{E}(P,q)
at fixed
P
, and therefore do not require the explicit numerical normalization.

Theorem 4.2

(Ordinary eta-shadow blindness of the reduced Spin-Fourier residue)
.

There exist equivariant spin lens-space data

\mathcal{X}^{[4]}_{25,4}=\left(L(25,4),\mathfrak{s},\rho_{1},g_{\text{round}},4\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{X}^{[4]}_{25,9}=\left(L(25,9),\mathfrak{s},\rho_{1},g_{\text{round}},4\right)

(4.13)

such that the ordinary Dirac
\eta
invariants agree and the same-level scalar multiplicity data agree at a Dirac level, but the reduced Spin-Fourier residues differ.

Proof.

The trigonometric
\eta
sums agree:

\mathfrak{E}(25,4)=\mathfrak{E}(25,9).

(4.14)

Since the omitted eta-normalization factor depends only on
P=25
, this implies

\eta_{D}(L(25,4))=\eta_{D}(L(25,9)).

(4.15)

We do not use the common numerical value here.

Now consider the Dirac level
k=4
. The same-level scalar multiplicity data are

m_{4}(25,4)=(2,2)\qquad\text{and}\qquad m_{4}(25,9)=(2,2).

(4.16)

Thus the ordinary positive and negative scalar multiplicities agree at this level. However, the reduced Spin-Fourier residues are different. The computation gives

\Delta_{4}(25,4;u)=A_{3}(u)-A_{5}(u).

(4.17)

For the second datum, the computation gives

\Delta_{4}(25,9;u)=-A_{5}(u)+A_{9}(u).

(4.18)

Since
A_{3}(u)
and
A_{9}(u)
are distinct generators in the reduced Spin-Fourier target, one has

A_{3}(u)-A_{5}(u)\neq-A_{5}(u)+A_{9}(u).

(4.19)

Therefore

\Delta_{4}(25,4;u)\neq\Delta_{4}(25,9;u).

(4.20)

This proves that the reduced Spin-Fourier residue distinguishes the two equivariant spin lens-space data even though the ordinary
\eta
invariant agrees and the scalar multiplicity data at the chosen level agree.
∎

Remark 4.3

(Nontriviality of the
p=25
pair)
.

The pair
q=4
and
q=9
modulo
25
is not explained by the elementary transformations used above. Indeed,

-4\equiv 21,\qquad 4^{-1}\equiv 19,\qquad-4^{-1}\equiv 6\pmod{25}.

(4.21)

The value
9
is none of
21
,
19
, or
6
. Hence this is not merely orientation reversal, coordinate exchange, or their composition within the fixed
z_{1}
-rotation convention.

The theorem proves a specific computable statement: ordinary
\eta
and same-level scalar multiplicity do not determine the reduced Spin-Fourier residue attached to the fixed equivariant spin datum.

4.3
An infinite square-modulus family

The previous theorem is an isolated ordinary-eta-blind example. An infinite square-modulus family explains the pattern behind the isolated example. Let
\ell\geq 5
be odd, and let

p=\ell^{2},\qquad q_{1}=\ell-1,\qquad q_{2}=2\ell-1,\qquad k=\ell-1.

(4.22)

For
\ell=5
, this recovers

p=25,\qquad q_{1}=4,\qquad q_{2}=9,\qquad k=4.

(4.23)

Proposition 4.4

(Spin-Fourier residues in the square family)
.

Let
\ell\geq 5
be odd. For

p=\ell^{2},\qquad q_{1}=\ell-1,\qquad q_{2}=2\ell-1,\qquad k=\ell-1,

(4.24)

one has

m_{k}(p,q_{1})=m_{k}(p,q_{2})=(2,2).

(4.25)

However,

\Delta_{k}(p,q_{1};u)=A_{\ell-2}(u)-A_{\ell}(u),

(4.26)

whereas

\Delta_{k}(p,q_{2};u)=A_{2\ell-1}(u)-A_{\ell}(u).

(4.27)

In particular,

\Delta_{k}(p,q_{1};u)\neq\Delta_{k}(p,q_{2};u).

(4.28)

Proof.

At level
k=\ell-1
, the contributing affine spin-lattice points satisfy

|a|+|b|\leq 2\ell

(4.29)

and

a+qb\equiv 0\pmod{\ell^{2}}.

(4.30)

First take
q=q_{1}=\ell-1
. The congruence becomes

a+(\ell-1)b\equiv 0\pmod{\ell^{2}}.

(4.31)

Modulo
\ell
, this implies

a-b\equiv 0\pmod{\ell}.

(4.32)

Since
a
and
b
are odd, the difference
a-b
is even. Under the bound
|a|+|b|\leq 2\ell
, the only possible cases are

a=b,\qquad a=b+2\ell,\qquad a=b-2\ell.

(4.33)

If
a=b
, then

a+(\ell-1)b=\ell a.

(4.34)

Divisibility by
\ell^{2}
forces
a
to be a multiple of
\ell
. Since
|a|\leq\ell
and
a
is odd, this gives

(a,b)=(\ell,\ell)\qquad\text{or}\qquad(a,b)=(-\ell,-\ell).

(4.35)

If
a=b+2\ell
, then

a+(\ell-1)b=\ell(b+2).

(4.36)

Divisibility by
\ell^{2}
gives

b+2\equiv 0\pmod{\ell}.

(4.37)

The bound gives

b=-(\ell+2)\qquad\text{and}\qquad a=\ell-2.

(4.38)

Thus one obtains

(a,b)=(\ell-2,-\ell-2).

(4.39)

The case
a=b-2\ell
similarly gives

(a,b)=(-\ell+2,\ell+2).

(4.40)

Therefore the contributing first Spin-Fourier weights for
q_{1}
are

\pm\ell\qquad\text{and}\qquad\pm(\ell-2).

(4.41)

The points
(\ell,\ell)
and
(-\ell,-\ell)
have even sign-counting parity and contribute to the negative branch at this level. The points
(\ell-2,-\ell-2)
and
(-\ell+2,\ell+2)
have odd sign-counting parity and contribute to the positive branch. Hence

\Delta_{k}(p,q_{1};u)=A_{\ell-2}(u)-A_{\ell}(u).

(4.42)

Now take
q=q_{2}=2\ell-1
. The congruence is

a+(2\ell-1)b\equiv 0\pmod{\ell^{2}}.

(4.43)

Again, modulo
\ell
, this implies

a-b\equiv 0\pmod{\ell}.

(4.44)

The same three cases occur. If
a=b
, then

a+(2\ell-1)b=2\ell a.

(4.45)

Since
\ell
is odd, divisibility by
\ell^{2}
forces
a
to be a multiple of
\ell
. Hence

(a,b)=(\ell,\ell)\qquad\text{or}\qquad(a,b)=(-\ell,-\ell).

(4.46)

If
a=b+2\ell
, then

a+(2\ell-1)b=2\ell(b+1).

(4.47)

Divisibility by
\ell^{2}
gives

b+1\equiv 0\pmod{\ell}.

(4.48)

The bound gives

b=-1\qquad\text{and}\qquad a=2\ell-1.

(4.49)

Thus one obtains

(a,b)=(2\ell-1,-1).

(4.50)

The case
a=b-2\ell
similarly gives

(a,b)=(-(2\ell-1),1).

(4.51)

Therefore the contributing first Spin-Fourier weights for
q_{2}
are

\pm\ell\qquad\text{and}\qquad\pm(2\ell-1).

(4.52)

The
\pm\ell
pair contributes to the negative branch, while the
\pm(2\ell-1)
pair contributes to the positive branch. Hence

\Delta_{k}(p,q_{2};u)=A_{2\ell-1}(u)-A_{\ell}(u).

(4.53)

Both examples have one positive reduced weight pair and one negative reduced weight pair, so their scalar data are both
(2,2)
. Since
A_{\ell-2}(u)
and
A_{2\ell-1}(u)
are distinct reduced Spin-Fourier generators, the two residues are different.
∎

Lemma 4.5

(A cotangent sum identity)
.

For every integer
\ell\geq 2
and every
x
away from the poles, one has

\sum_{m=0}^{\ell-1}\cot\left(x+\frac{m\pi}{\ell}\right)=\ell\cot(\ell x).

(4.54)

Proof.

Use the product identity

\sin(\ell x)=K_{\ell}\prod_{m=0}^{\ell-1}\sin\left(x+\frac{m\pi}{\ell}\right)

(4.55)

for a nonzero constant
K_{\ell}
. Taking the logarithmic derivative gives the stated identity.
∎

Theorem 4.6

(Ordinary
\eta
equality in the square family)
.

Let
\ell\geq 5
be odd. Then

\eta_{D}(L(\ell^{2},\ell-1))=\eta_{D}(L(\ell^{2},2\ell-1)).

(4.56)

More precisely, the unnormalised trigonometric
\eta
sums satisfy

\mathfrak{E}(\ell^{2},\ell-1)=\mathfrak{E}(\ell^{2},2\ell-1).

(4.57)

The common numerical value of
\eta_{D}
depends on the standard normalization factor in the Dirac
\eta
formula and is not used in this argument.

Proof.

Let
P=\ell^{2}
and
q_{a}=a\ell-1
, where
a=1
or
a=2
. It is enough to compare the unnormalised trigonometric
\eta
sums

\mathfrak{E}(P,q_{a})=\sum_{r=1}^{P-1}\operatorname{csc}\left(\frac{(P+1)r\pi}{P}\right)\operatorname{csc}\left(\frac{(P+1)q_{a}r\pi}{P}\right).

(4.58)

Since
P=\ell^{2}
, one has

\sin\left(\frac{(P+1)r\pi}{P}\right)=(-1)^{r}\sin\left(\frac{r\pi}{P}\right).

(4.59)

Similarly,

\sin\left(\frac{(P+1)q_{a}r\pi}{P}\right)=(-1)^{q_{a}r}\sin\left(\frac{q_{a}r\pi}{P}\right).

(4.60)

Because
q_{a}+1=a\ell
and
\ell
is odd, the unnormalised trigonometric sum becomes

\mathcal{S}_{a}=\sum_{r=1}^{\ell^{2}-1}\frac{(-1)^{ar}}{\sin\left(\frac{r\pi}{\ell^{2}}\right)\sin\left(\frac{(a\ell-1)r\pi}{\ell^{2}}\right)}.

(4.61)

It remains to compute the auxiliary sum
\mathcal{S}_{a}
.

Write

r=m\ell+s,\qquad 0\leq m\leq\ell-1,\qquad 0\leq s\leq\ell-1.

(4.62)

The pair
(m,s)=(0,0)
is omitted. First assume
s\neq 0
. Introduce
\alpha=\pi s/\ell^{2}
,
\beta=\pi/\ell
, and
Y=\pi as/\ell
.
For the fixed value of
s
, the corresponding part of
\mathcal{S}_{a}
is

(-1)^{as}\sum_{m=0}^{\ell-1}\frac{1}{\sin(\alpha+m\beta)\sin(Y-\alpha-m\beta)}.

(4.63)

Since
a=1,2
,
1\leq s\leq\ell-1
, and
\ell
is odd, one has

\sin Y\neq 0.

(4.64)

Using

\frac{1}{\sin x\sin(Y-x)}=\frac{\cot x+\cot(Y-x)}{\sin Y},

(4.65)

the fixed-
s
contribution is proportional to

\sum_{m=0}^{\ell-1}\cot(\alpha+m\beta)+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell-1}\cot(Y-\alpha-m\beta).

(4.66)

By Lemma
4.5
, the first sum is

\ell\cot(\ell\alpha)=\ell\cot\left(\frac{\pi s}{\ell}\right).

(4.67)

The second sum is

\ell\cot(\ell(Y-\alpha))=\ell\cot\left(as\pi-\frac{\pi s}{\ell}\right)=-\ell\cot\left(\frac{\pi s}{\ell}\right).

(4.68)

The two sums cancel. Therefore every contribution with
s\neq 0
vanishes.

It remains to compute the terms with
s=0
. Then
r=m\ell
, where
1\leq m\leq\ell-1
. We have

\sin\left(\frac{m\ell\pi}{\ell^{2}}\right)=\sin\left(\frac{m\pi}{\ell}\right).

(4.69)

Also,

\sin\left(\frac{(a\ell-1)m\ell\pi}{\ell^{2}}\right)=\sin\left(am\pi-\frac{m\pi}{\ell}\right)=(-1)^{am+1}\sin\left(\frac{m\pi}{\ell}\right).

(4.70)

Since

(-1)^{am\ell}=(-1)^{am},

(4.71)

the
s=0
contribution is

\mathcal{S}_{a}=-\sum_{m=1}^{\ell-1}\operatorname{csc}^{2}\left(\frac{m\pi}{\ell}\right).

(4.72)

Using the standard identity

\sum_{m=1}^{\ell-1}\operatorname{csc}^{2}\left(\frac{m\pi}{\ell}\right)=\frac{\ell^{2}-1}{3},

(4.73)

we obtain

\mathcal{S}_{a}=-\frac{\ell^{2}-1}{3}.

(4.74)

Thus the unnormalised trigonometric
\eta
sum satisfies

\mathfrak{E}(\ell^{2},a\ell-1)=-\frac{\ell^{2}-1}{3}.

(4.75)

This value is independent of
a
for
a=1,2
. Since the standard eta-normalization factor depends only on
P=\ell^{2}
, the ordinary
\eta
invariants are equal:

\eta_{D}(L(\ell^{2},\ell-1))=\eta_{D}(L(\ell^{2},2\ell-1)).

(4.76)

∎

Theorem 4.7

(Infinite ordinary-eta-blind Spin-Fourier separation)
.

For every odd
\ell\geq 5
, let

p=\ell^{2},\qquad q_{1}=\ell-1,\qquad q_{2}=2\ell-1,\qquad k=\ell-1.

(4.77)

Then

\eta_{D}(L(p,q_{1}))=\eta_{D}(L(p,q_{2})),

(4.78)

and

m_{k}(p,q_{1})=m_{k}(p,q_{2})=(2,2),

(4.79)

but

\Delta_{k}(p,q_{1};u)\neq\Delta_{k}(p,q_{2};u)

(4.80)

in the reduced Spin-Fourier target. Thus the reduced Spin-Fourier residue gives infinitely many same-level separations invisible to the ordinary
\eta
value and ordinary scalar multiplicity.

Proof.

The ordinary
\eta
equality follows from the previous theorem. The scalar-multiplicity equality and the two explicit formulas for the reduced Spin-Fourier residues follow from the square-family Spin-Fourier proposition. Since
A_{\ell-2}(u)
and
A_{2\ell-1}(u)
are distinct reduced generators for
\ell\geq 5
, the two residues are different.
∎

Remark 4.8

(Nontriviality of the square-family pairs)
.

The pair
q_{1}=\ell-1
and
q_{2}=2\ell-1
is not an elementary orientation-reversal or coordinate-exchange pair. Indeed,

-q_{1}\equiv\ell^{2}-\ell+1\pmod{\ell^{2}},

(4.81)

while

q_{1}^{-1}\equiv-\ell-1\pmod{\ell^{2}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad-q_{1}^{-1}\equiv\ell+1\pmod{\ell^{2}}.

(4.82)

For
\ell\geq 5
, none of these is equal to
2\ell-1
modulo
\ell^{2}
.

The square-family result upgrades the
p=25
ordinary-
\eta
example to an infinite square-modulus family. It remains a reduced Spin-Fourier residue statement, but it is no longer an isolated finite computation.

5
T^{2}
-equivariant
\eta
and finite two-variable shells

The two-variable residue is the finite-shell coefficient of the
T^{2}
-equivariant
\eta
character of the spin Dirac operator. The invariant-level result uses the residual-circle
\eta
/rho germ obtained from these shell coefficients, rather than a single isolated shell.

In the square-family separation proved below, the residual circle used is the one-parameter subgroup which rotates the first coordinate. Thus, the resulting
\eta
germ is a one-variable specialization of the full
T^{2}
-equivariant character. The two-variable residue gives the larger coordinate-torus bookkeeping, but the nonvanishing theorem below uses this particular residual-circle specialization.

Let
g=(u,v)\in T^{2}
. For
\operatorname{Re}(s)
sufficiently large, define the equivariant
\eta
function by

\eta_{g}(D_{p,q},s)=\sum_{\lambda\neq 0}\operatorname{sign}(\lambda)|\lambda|^{-s}\operatorname{Tr}\left(g\mid E_{\lambda}\right),

(5.1)

where
E_{\lambda}
denotes the
\lambda
-eigenspace of the spin Dirac operator on
L(p,q)
. Since the round Dirac eigenvalues are
\lambda_{k}^{\pm}=\pm\left(k+\frac{3}{2}\right)
, inserting the two-variable characters gives

\eta^{T^{2}}_{p,q}(s;u,v):=\eta_{g}(D_{p,q},s)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(k+\frac{3}{2}\right)^{-s}\widehat{\Delta}_{k}(p,q;u,v).

(5.2)

Thus
\widehat{\Delta}_{k}(p,q;u,v)
is the
k
-th eta-shell coefficient. The one-variable coefficient is recovered by the specialization
\widehat{\Delta}_{k}(p,q;u,1)=\Delta_{k}(p,q;u)
.

5.1
First-shell
\eta
separation

The following elementary observation turns finite residue computations into eta-character separation statements.

Theorem 5.1

(First differing shell separates
\eta
characters)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
be two round coordinate-equivariant spin lens-space data. Suppose that

\widehat{\Delta}_{j}(\mathcal{X};u,v)=\widehat{\Delta}_{j}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime};u,v)

(5.3)

for all
j<K
, but

\widehat{\Delta}_{K}(\mathcal{X};u,v)\neq\widehat{\Delta}_{K}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime};u,v).

(5.4)

Then the
T^{2}
-equivariant
\eta
characters are different:

\eta^{T^{2}}_{\mathcal{X}}(s;u,v)\neq\eta^{T^{2}}_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}(s;u,v).

(5.5)

Moreover, for
\operatorname{Re}(s)\to+\infty
, their difference has leading term

\left(K+\frac{3}{2}\right)^{-s}\left(\widehat{\Delta}_{K}(\mathcal{X};u,v)-\widehat{\Delta}_{K}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime};u,v)\right).

(5.6)

Proof.

Subtracting the two eta-character expansions gives

\eta^{T^{2}}_{\mathcal{X}}(s;u,v)-\eta^{T^{2}}_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}(s;u,v)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(k+\frac{3}{2}\right)^{-s}\left(\widehat{\Delta}_{k}(\mathcal{X};u,v)-\widehat{\Delta}_{k}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime};u,v)\right).

(5.7)

By assumption, all terms with
k<K
vanish. The first nonzero term is therefore the
K
-th term. As
\operatorname{Re}(s)\to+\infty
, every later term is smaller by a factor of the form

\left(\frac{K+\frac{3}{2}}{k+\frac{3}{2}}\right)^{s},\qquad k>K.

(5.8)

Hence the displayed
K
-th term is the leading term. Since its coefficient is nonzero, the
\eta
characters are different.
∎

A first-differing shell separates the equivariant
\eta
characters as spectral functions, for example in the large-
\operatorname{Re}(s)
regime where the first nonzero shell controls the expansion. The
s=0
statement used below is stronger: it is the residual-circle computation of
\Phi(\delta)
, especially the nonvanishing of
\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)
.

Corollary 5.2

(Two-variable refinement of the one-variable
\eta
shadow)
.

Suppose two data collections satisfy

\Delta_{K}(\mathcal{X};u)=\Delta_{K}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime};u),

(5.9)

but

\widehat{\Delta}_{K}(\mathcal{X};u,v)\neq\widehat{\Delta}_{K}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime};u,v).

(5.10)

Then the old one-variable shell does not separate the data at level
K
, but the full
T^{2}
-equivariant
\eta
shell does.

Proof.

The equality of the one-variable residues is the equality after specializing to
v=1
. The inequality of the two-variable residues says that the distinction lies in the
a_{2}
-weight data. By Theorem
5.1
, a first such difference gives a nonzero difference of
T^{2}
-equivariant
\eta
characters.
∎

5.2
Normalization of the residual-circle
\eta
germ

We fix the residual-circle convention used in the rest of the section. Let

L(P,q)=S^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{P},\qquad\gamma(z_{1},z_{2})=(\omega z_{1},\omega^{q}z_{2}),\qquad\omega=e^{2\pi i/P},

(5.11)

where
P
is odd and
q\in(\mathbb{Z}/P\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. For
1\leq j\leq P-1
, put

x_{j}=\frac{\pi j}{P}.

(5.12)

The residual-circle parameter is the half-angle
\delta
. Thus the residual-circle element is

g_{\delta}=(e^{2i\delta},1)\in T^{2}.

(5.13)

In the deck-sum expression, this shifts the first half-angle by
x_{j}\mapsto x_{j}+\delta
, while the second half-angle is kept fixed.

Definition 5.3

(Odd spin-lift representative)
.

Let
P
be odd and let
q\in(\mathbb{Z}/P\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. We denote by
Q(q)
the unique odd representative modulo
2P
satisfying

Q(q)\equiv q\pmod{P},\qquad 1\leq Q(q)\leq 2P-1.

(5.14)

Equivalently,

Q(q)=\begin{cases}q,&q\text{ odd},\\
q+P,&q\text{ even}.\end{cases}

(5.15)

For example, when
P=25
, one has
Q(4)=29
and
Q(9)=9
.

The following elementary identity explains the sign appearing in the even-
q
case.

Lemma 5.4

(Odd representative identity)
.

For
1\leq j\leq P-1
, one has

\csc(Q(q)x_{j})=\begin{cases}\csc(qx_{j}),&q\text{ odd},\\
(-1)^{j}\csc(qx_{j}),&q\text{ even}.\end{cases}

(5.16)

Proof.

If
q
is odd, then
Q(q)=q
. If
q
is even, then
Q(q)=q+P
, and

\sin(Q(q)x_{j})=\sin(qx_{j}+\pi j)=(-1)^{j}\sin(qx_{j}).

(5.17)

Taking reciprocals gives the formula.
∎

Definition 5.5

(Normalized residual-circle
\eta
germ)
.

For
|\delta|<\pi/P
, define the normalized residual-circle
\eta
germ by

\mathcal{E}^{\text{norm}}_{P,q}(\delta)=\sum_{j=1}^{P-1}\csc\bigl(Q(q)x_{j}\bigr)\csc(x_{j}+\delta).

(5.18)

For two parameters
q_{1},q_{2}\in(\mathbb{Z}/P\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
, define the normalized relative residual-circle
\eta
germ by

\Phi_{q_{1},q_{2}}(\delta)=\mathcal{E}^{\text{norm}}_{P,q_{1}}(\delta)-\mathcal{E}^{\text{norm}}_{P,q_{2}}(\delta).

(5.19)

Equivalently,

\Phi_{q_{1},q_{2}}(\delta)=\sum_{j=1}^{P-1}\left[\csc\bigl(Q(q_{1})x_{j}\bigr)-\csc\bigl(Q(q_{2})x_{j}\bigr)\right]\csc(x_{j}+\delta).

(5.20)

Lemma 5.6

(Analyticity near the identity)
.

The functions
\mathcal{E}^{\text{norm}}_{P,q}(\delta)
and
\Phi_{q_{1},q_{2}}(\delta)
are real-analytic for
|\delta|<\pi/P
.

Proof.

Since
1\leq j\leq P-1
, one has
0<x_{j}<\pi
. If
|\delta|<\pi/P
, then
x_{j}+\delta\notin\pi\mathbb{Z}
. Hence
\csc(x_{j}+\delta)
is analytic on this interval. Also,
\gcd(Q(q),P)=1
, so
Q(q)j\not\equiv 0\pmod{P}
for
1\leq j\leq P-1
. Therefore
\sin(Q(q)x_{j})\neq 0
. Each summand is analytic, and the sum is finite.
∎

Thus no extra regularization at
\delta=0
is being used in the normalized germ. The possible fixed-point singularity of a local expression is not present in this finite deck-average expression, where the summation is over
1\leq j\leq P-1
.

Lemma 5.7

(Deck-sum normalization for the relative residual germ)
.

Fix an odd value of
P
and a spin-lift convention. Up to a summand independent of
q
, the Donnelly-APS residual-circle equivariant
\eta
germ has the normalized deck-sum form

\eta^{\text{res}}_{P,q}(\delta)=\kappa_{P}\mathcal{E}^{\text{norm}}_{P,q}(\delta)+\text{a term independent of }q,

(5.21)

where
\kappa_{P}\neq 0
depends only on
P
and on the global eta-normalization convention. Consequently, for any two parameters
q_{1},q_{2}\in(\mathbb{Z}/P\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
,

\eta^{\text{res}}_{P,q_{1}}(\delta)-\eta^{\text{res}}_{P,q_{2}}(\delta)=\kappa_{P}\Phi_{q_{1},q_{2}}(\delta).

(5.22)

Proof.

Use the standard heat-kernel averaging description of equivariant
\eta
invariants on the quotient
S^{3}/\mathbb{Z}_{P}
. Since the residual-circle element
g_{\delta}=(e^{2i\delta},1)
commutes with the deck transformation
\gamma
, the equivariant trace on the quotient is obtained by summing the corresponding traces of
g_{\delta}\gamma^{j}
on the covering sphere. The summand with
j=0
is independent of
q
, so it cancels in every relative difference between
L(P,q_{1})
and
L(P,q_{2})
. Thus only the terms
1\leq j\leq P-1
enter the relative formula.

For
1\leq j\leq P-1
, the element
g_{\delta}\gamma^{j}
has rotation half-angles
x_{j}+\delta
and
Q(q)x_{j}
, where
x_{j}=\pi j/P
and
Q(q)
is the odd spin-lift representative. Donnelly’s formula for the spin Dirac
\eta
invariant of a spherical space form gives, for these nontrivial deck terms, a product of the corresponding cosecant factors, multiplied by a global nonzero constant depending only on the Dirac normalization, the quotient order
P
, and the fixed spin-lift convention
[
14
]
. This gives the displayed relative identity. The interval
|\delta|<\pi/P
keeps the finite deck sum away from the poles described in Lemma
5.6
, so the resulting relative germ is analytic near
\delta=0
.
∎

In the sequel we work with the normalized relative germ
\Phi_{q_{1},q_{2}}
. By the preceding lemma, vanishing and nonvanishing statements for
\Phi_{q_{1},q_{2}}
are exactly the corresponding relative Donnelly-APS statements, up to a common nonzero factor.

For the pair
L(25,4)
and
L(25,9)
, we write
\Phi(\delta)=\Phi_{4,9}(\delta)
. Since
Q(4)=29
and
Q(9)=9
, this means

\Phi(\delta)=\sum_{j=1}^{24}\left[\csc(29x_{j})-\csc(9x_{j})\right]\csc(x_{j}+\delta),\qquad x_{j}=\frac{\pi j}{25}.

(5.23)

Equivalently, using
\sin(29x_{j})=(-1)^{j}\sin(4x_{j})
, one may write

\Phi(\delta)=\sum_{j=1}^{24}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{\sin(x_{j}+\delta)\sin(4x_{j})}-\sum_{j=1}^{24}\frac{1}{\sin(x_{j}+\delta)\sin(9x_{j})}.

(5.24)

5.3
The

The first explicit residual-circle specialization is the pair
L(25,4)
and
L(25,9)

\eta

\eta

Let
x_{j}=\pi j/25
for

. By the normalization convention in

, the normalized relative residual-circle
\eta

L(25,4)
and

\Phi(\delta)=\sum_{j=1}^{24}\left[\csc(29x_{j})-\csc(9x_{j})\right]\csc(x_{j}+\delta).

(5.25)

Define

C_{j}=\csc(29x_{j})-\csc(9x_{j}).

(5.26)

Thus

(5.27)

Lemma 5.8

(Pairing symmetry)
.

For
1\leq j\leq 24
, one has

C_{25-j}=C_{j}.

(5.28)

Consequently,

\Phi(\delta)=\sum_{j=1}^{12}C_{j}\left[\csc(x_{j}+\delta)+\csc(x_{j}-\delta)\right].

(5.29)

In particular,
\Phi
is an even function of
\delta
.

Proof.

Since
x_{25-j}=\pi-x_{j}
, we have

\sin(29x_{25-j})=\sin(29\pi-29x_{j})=\sin(29x_{j}),

(5.30)

and similarly

\sin(9x_{25-j})=\sin(9\pi-9x_{j})=\sin(9x_{j}).

(5.31)

Therefore
C_{25-j}=C_{j}
. Also,

\csc(\delta+x_{25-j})=\csc(\delta+\pi-x_{j})=\csc(x_{j}-\delta).

(5.32)

Pairing the
j
-term with the
(25-j)
-term gives the displayed formula. The bracket is invariant under

, so
\Phi
is even.
∎

Proposition 5.9

(Ordinary

.

\Phi(0)=0.

(5.33)

Proof.

At
\delta=0
,

(5.34)

(5.35)

Hence
\Phi(0)=0
.
∎

Proposition 5.10

(First-order blindness)
.

One has

\Phi^{\prime}(0)=0.

(5.36)

Proof.

By the pairing symmetry lemma,
\Phi
is even in
\delta
. Therefore its first derivative at the origin vanishes:

(5.37)

∎

Lemma 5.11

(Exact second derivative)
.

The second derivative of
\Phi
at the origin is

\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)=-6080.

(5.38)

Proof.

We use

\frac{d}{d\delta}\csc(x+\delta)=-\csc(x+\delta)\cot(x+\delta),

(5.39)

and

\frac{d^{2}}{d\delta^{2}}\csc(x+\delta)=\csc(x+\delta)\cot^{2}(x+\delta)+\csc^{3}(x+\delta).

(5.40)

Therefore

\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)=\sum_{j=1}^{24}C_{j}\left[\csc(x_{j})\cot^{2}(x_{j})+\csc^{3}(x_{j})\right].

(5.41)

Using
C_{25-j}=C_{j}
,
\csc(x_{25-j})=\csc(x_{j})
, and
\cot^{2}(x_{25-j})=\cot^{2}(x_{j})
, this becomes

\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)=2\sum_{j=1}^{12}C_{j}\left[\csc(x_{j})\cot^{2}(x_{j})+\csc^{3}(x_{j})\right].

(5.42)

Substituting
C_{j}=\csc(29x_{j})-\csc(9x_{j})
gives the exact finite trigonometric identity

2\sum_{j=1}^{12}\left[\csc(29x_{j})-\csc(9x_{j})\right]\left[\csc(x_{j})\cot^{2}(x_{j})+\csc^{3}(x_{j})\right]=-6080.

(5.43)

This is a finite algebraic identity in the cyclotomic field generated by

\zeta=e^{\pi i/25}.

(5.44)

Indeed, after writing each sine and cosine in terms of powers of
\zeta
, multiplying by the common denominator, and reducing modulo the cyclotomic polynomial
\Phi_{50}(\zeta)
, the numerator reduces to zero after moving the right-hand side to the left. Hence the identity is exact, and
\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)=-6080
.
∎

Theorem 5.12

(Residual-circle equivariant
\eta
separation)
.

\eta

and

(5.46)

and hence
\Phi(\delta)\neq 0
for all sufficiently small nonzero

5.7
.

Proof.

The first identity is the ordinary
\eta
cancellation proposition. The second is the first-order blindness proposition. The third is the exact second derivative lemma. Taylor expansion at

gives

\Phi(\delta)=\Phi(0)+\Phi^{\prime}(0)\delta+\frac{1}{2}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)\delta^{2}+O(\delta^{4}).

(5.47)

Substituting the three identities gives

\Phi(\delta)=-3040\delta^{2}+O(\delta^{4}).

(5.48)

Therefore
\Phi(\delta)\neq 0
for all sufficiently small nonzero
\delta
.
∎

5.4
Residual-circle equivariant rho number

Terminology.

Throughout the rest of this section, a residual-circle rho germ means the numerical relative equivariant
\eta
germ for the fixed residual circle and the fixed coordinate-equivariant spin data. Thus the word “rho” is used in the APS/Donnelly numerical sense. We do not construct a Higson-Roe analytic-surgery rho class, nor do we claim an invariant of the bare unoriented lens space. The chosen spin lift, residual circle, and coordinate torus action are part of the datum.

The preceding residual-circle
\eta
separation can be written as a relative equivariant
\eta
, or rho-number, statement.

Let
\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{25,4}=(L(25,4),\mathfrak{s},\alpha_{\text{res}},g_{\text{round}})
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{25,9}=(L(25,9),\mathfrak{s},\alpha_{\text{res}},g_{\text{round}})
.
Here
\mathfrak{s}
is the unique spin structure,
\alpha_{\text{res}}
is the residual coordinate-circle action, and
g_{\text{round}}
is the round metric. For a residual-circle element
g_{\delta}
, define the normalized residual-circle rho number by

\rho^{\text{norm}}_{\delta}(\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{25,4},\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{25,9})=\kappa_{25}^{-1}\left(\eta_{g_{\delta}}(D_{\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{25,4}})-\eta_{g_{\delta}}(D_{\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{25,9}})\right).

(5.52)

With the normalized convention fixed above, this relative rho number is the same function
\Phi(\delta)
.

Theorem 5.14

(Residual-circle rho-jet separation)
.

For the pair
\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{25,4}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{25,9}
, one has

\rho^{\text{norm}}_{0}(\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{25,4},\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{25,9})=0,

(5.53)

and

\left.\frac{d}{d\delta}\rho^{\text{norm}}_{\delta}(\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{25,4},\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{25,9})\right|_{\delta=0}=0.

(5.54)

However,

\left.\frac{d^{2}}{d\delta^{2}}\rho^{\text{norm}}_{\delta}(\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{25,4},\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{25,9})\right|_{\delta=0}=-6080.

(5.55)

Thus the ordinary
\eta
/rho shadow is blind to this pair, but the residual-circle equivariant rho germ is nonzero.

Proof.

This is exactly the content of Theorem
5.12
, rewritten in relative
\eta
notation. The identity
\rho^{\text{norm}}_{\delta}(\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{25,4},\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{25,9})=\Phi(\delta)
gives the three displayed formulas from
\Phi(0)=0
,
\Phi^{\prime}(0)=0
, and
\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)=-6080
.
∎

Corollary 5.15

(Finite-order residual-circle rho-number separation)
.

There exists a finite-order residual-circle element
g
such that

\rho^{\text{norm}}_{g}(\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{25,4},\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{25,9})\neq 0.

(5.56)

Proof.

By Theorem
5.14
, one has

\rho^{\text{norm}}_{\delta}(\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{25,4},\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{25,9})=-3040\delta^{2}+O(\delta^{4}).

(5.57)

Therefore
\rho^{\text{norm}}_{\delta}(\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{25,4},\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{25,9})\neq 0
for all sufficiently small nonzero
\delta
. Choose such a
\delta
with
\delta/\pi\in\mathbb{Q}
. Then
g_{\delta}=e^{2i\delta}
has finite order and gives the desired nonzero normalized relative
\eta
value.
∎

5.5
The square-family residual-circle rho germ

The same residual-circle construction extends from the explicit pair
L(25,4)
and
L(25,9)
to the square-modulus family. The exact rho-germ reduction is proved here; the nonvanishing of the resulting coefficient is proved in the following subsection.

Let
\ell\geq 5
be odd, with
P=\ell^{2}
,
q_{1}=\ell-1
, and
q_{2}=2\ell-1
. Let
\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{\ell,1}=(L(P,q_{1}),\mathfrak{s},\alpha_{\text{res}},g_{\text{round}})
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{\ell,2}=(L(P,q_{2}),\mathfrak{s},\alpha_{\text{res}},g_{\text{round}})
.
For

x_{j}=\frac{\pi j}{P},\qquad 1\leq j\leq P-1,

(5.58)

define

\Phi_{\ell}(\delta)=\Phi_{q_{1},q_{2}}(\delta).

(5.59)

Equivalently, since
Q(q_{1})=P+\ell-1
and
Q(q_{2})=2\ell-1
, one has

\Phi_{\ell}(\delta)=\sum_{j=1}^{P-1}\left[\csc((P+\ell-1)x_{j})-\csc((2\ell-1)x_{j})\right]\csc(x_{j}+\delta).

(5.60)

Using
\sin((P+\ell-1)x_{j})=(-1)^{j}\sin((\ell-1)x_{j})
, this can also be written as

\Phi_{\ell}(\delta)=\sum_{j=1}^{P-1}\frac{(-1)^{j}}{\sin(x_{j}+\delta)\sin(q_{1}x_{j})}-\sum_{j=1}^{P-1}\frac{1}{\sin(x_{j}+\delta)\sin(q_{2}x_{j})}.

(5.61)

This is the normalized relative residual-circle
\eta
germ for
\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{\ell,1}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{\ell,2}
; Lemma
5.7
gives the corresponding Donnelly-APS normalization.

Lemma 5.16

(No fixed denominators vanish)
.

For
1\leq j\leq P-1
, one has

\sin(q_{1}x_{j})\neq 0,\qquad\sin(q_{2}x_{j})\neq 0.

(5.62)

Consequently,
\Phi_{\ell}(\delta)
is well-defined for
|\delta|<\pi/P
.

Proof.

Since
\gcd(\ell-1,\ell)=1
, one has
\gcd(q_{1},P)=1
. Since
\gcd(2\ell-1,\ell)=1
, one also has
\gcd(q_{2},P)=1
. If
\sin(q_{a}x_{j})=0
, then
q_{a}j\equiv 0\pmod{P}
. Because
q_{a}
is coprime to
P
, this forces
j\equiv 0\pmod{P}
, impossible for
1\leq j\leq P-1
. If
|\delta|<\pi/P
, then
0<x_{j}+\delta<\pi
, so
\sin(x_{j}+\delta)\neq 0
.
∎

Since

\sin((P+q_{1})x_{j})=\sin(\pi j+q_{1}x_{j})=(-1)^{j}\sin(q_{1}x_{j}),

(5.63)

we can write

\Phi_{\ell}(\delta)=\sum_{j=1}^{P-1}B_{\ell,j}\csc(x_{j}+\delta),

(5.64)

where

B_{\ell,j}=\csc((P+q_{1})x_{j})-\csc(q_{2}x_{j}).

(5.65)

Equivalently,

B_{\ell,j}=\csc((\ell^{2}+\ell-1)x_{j})-\csc((2\ell-1)x_{j}).

(5.66)

Lemma 5.17

(Pairing symmetry and evenness of the residual germ)
.

For
1\leq j\leq P-1
, one has

B_{\ell,P-j}=B_{\ell,j}.

(5.67)

Consequently,

\Phi_{\ell}(\delta)=\sum_{j=1}^{(P-1)/2}B_{\ell,j}\left[\csc(x_{j}+\delta)+\csc(x_{j}-\delta)\right].

(5.68)

In particular,
\Phi_{\ell}
is even in
\delta
, and therefore

\Phi_{\ell}^{\prime}(0)=0.

(5.69)

Proof.

Since
x_{P-j}=\pi-x_{j}
, and since both
P+q_{1}=\ell^{2}+\ell-1
and
q_{2}=2\ell-1
are odd, one has

\sin((P+q_{1})x_{P-j})=\sin((P+q_{1})x_{j}),\qquad\sin(q_{2}x_{P-j})=\sin(q_{2}x_{j}).

(5.70)

Thus
B_{\ell,P-j}=B_{\ell,j}
. Also,

\csc(x_{P-j}+\delta)=\csc(\pi-x_{j}+\delta)=\csc(x_{j}-\delta).

(5.71)

Pairing the
j
-term with the
(P-j)
-term gives the displayed formula. The bracket is even in
\delta
, so the full finite sum is even. Differentiating at
\delta=0
gives
\Phi_{\ell}^{\prime}(0)=0
.
∎

Lemma 5.18

(Ordinary eta-shadow cancellation)
.

For every odd
\ell\geq 5
, one has

\Phi_{\ell}(0)=0.

(5.72)

Proof.

At
\delta=0
, the residual-circle expression reduces to the ordinary eta-shadow difference for the square pair

L(\ell^{2},\ell-1)\qquad\text{and}\qquad L(\ell^{2},2\ell-1).

(5.73)

The square-family ordinary
\eta
cancellation gives

\eta_{D}(L(\ell^{2},\ell-1))=\eta_{D}(L(\ell^{2},2\ell-1)).

(5.74)

The common normalization factor depends only on
P=\ell^{2}
, so the relative expression vanishes.
∎

Theorem 5.19

(Square-family residual-circle rho-germ reduction)
.

For every odd
\ell\geq 5
, the normalized square-family residual-circle rho germ satisfies

\Phi_{\ell}(0)=0,\qquad\Phi_{\ell}^{\prime}(0)=0,\qquad\Phi_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}(0)=C_{\ell},

(5.75)

where

(5.76)

Consequently, if
C_{\ell}\neq 0
, then the ordinary
\eta
/rho shadow vanishes but the residual-circle equivariant rho germ is nontrivial. The Donnelly-APS second derivative is obtained from this number by the common nonzero factor of Lemma
5.7
.

Proof.

The identity
\Phi_{\ell}(0)=0
is Lemma
5.18
. The identity
\Phi_{\ell}^{\prime}(0)=0
follows from the evenness in Lemma
5.17
. Differentiating

\frac{d}{d\delta}\csc(x+\delta)=-\csc(x+\delta)\cot(x+\delta)

(5.77)

once more gives

\frac{d^{2}}{d\delta^{2}}\csc(x+\delta)=\csc(x+\delta)\cot^{2}(x+\delta)+\csc^{3}(x+\delta).

(5.78)

Substituting
\delta=0
into the paired expression for
\Phi_{\ell}
gives the displayed formula for
C_{\ell}
. If
C_{\ell}\neq 0
, then

\Phi_{\ell}(\delta)=\frac{C_{\ell}}{2}\delta^{2}+O(\delta^{4}),

(5.79)

so
\Phi_{\ell}(\delta)\neq 0
for all sufficiently small nonzero
\delta
. Choosing such a

with

value.
∎

5.6
Nonvanishing of the square-family coefficient

It remains to prove the nonvanishing of
C_{\ell}
. We use a finite cyclotomic trace calculation, which removes the last computational condition from Theorem
5.19
.

Let
P=\ell^{2}
and
x_{j}=\pi j/P
. For an odd integer
a
coprime to
P
, define

S_{P}(a)=\sum_{j=1}^{P-1}\csc(ax_{j})\left[2\csc^{3}(x_{j})-\csc(x_{j})\right].

(5.80)

Lemma 5.20

(Square-family coefficient as a difference of trace sums)
.

For odd
\ell\geq 5
, one has

C_{\ell}=S_{P}(P+\ell-1)-S_{P}(2\ell-1).

(5.81)

Proof.

Use

\csc(x)\cot^{2}(x)+\csc^{3}(x)=2\csc^{3}(x)-\csc(x).

(5.82)

Substituting this identity into the definition of
C_{\ell}
, and using the full unpaired sum, gives the claim.
∎

Lemma 5.21

(Cyclotomic trace form of
S_{P}(a)
)
.

Let
P
be odd and let
a
be odd with
\gcd(a,P)=1
. Then

S_{P}(a)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z^{P}=1\\
z\neq 1\end{subarray}}\left[\frac{32z^{(a+3)/2}}{(z^{a}-1)(z-1)^{3}}+\frac{4z^{(a+1)/2}}{(z^{a}-1)(z-1)}\right].

(5.83)

Proof.

Let
z_{j}=\exp(2\pi ij/P)
. A direct calculation gives

\csc(ax_{j})\csc(x_{j})=-\frac{4z_{j}^{(a+1)/2}}{(z_{j}^{a}-1)(z_{j}-1)},

(5.84)

and

\csc(ax_{j})\csc^{3}(x_{j})=\frac{16z_{j}^{(a+3)/2}}{(z_{j}^{a}-1)(z_{j}-1)^{3}}.

(5.85)

Multiplying by the coefficients in
2\csc^{3}(x_{j})-\csc(x_{j})
and summing over
1\leq j\leq P-1
proves the formula.
∎

Let
\mathcal{A}_{P}=\mathbb{Q}[z]/(1+z+\cdots+z^{P-1})
, and define the trace functional

\tau_{P}(F)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z^{P}=1\\
z\neq 1\end{subarray}}F(z).

(5.86)

For monomials,

\tau_{P}(z^{m})=\begin{cases}P-1,&P\mid m,\\
-1,&P\nmid m.\end{cases}

(5.87)

Lemma 5.22

(Inverse formula in the cyclotomic quotient)
.

If
\gcd(a,P)=1
, then in
\mathcal{A}_{P}
one has

(z^{a}-1)^{-1}=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{r=1}^{P-1}rz^{ar}.

(5.88)

Proof.

Multiplying by
z^{a}-1
, the finite sum telescopes in the quotient
\mathcal{A}_{P}
:

(z^{a}-1)\sum_{r=1}^{P-1}rz^{ar}=P.

(5.89)

Dividing by
P
gives the inverse formula.
∎

Define

I_{a}(z)=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{r=1}^{P-1}rz^{ar}.

(5.90)

Let
\alpha_{1}=P+\ell-1
and
\alpha_{2}=2\ell-1
, and define
\mathfrak{e}(a)=(a+1)/2
and
\mathfrak{f}(a)=(a+3)/2
for odd
a
.
The preceding lemmas give

C_{\ell}=32D_{3}(\ell)+4D_{1}(\ell),

(5.91)

where

D_{3}(\ell)=\tau_{P}\left(z^{\mathfrak{f}(\alpha_{1})}I_{\alpha_{1}}(z)I_{1}(z)^{3}-z^{\mathfrak{f}(\alpha_{2})}I_{\alpha_{2}}(z)I_{1}(z)^{3}\right),

(5.92)

and

D_{1}(\ell)=\tau_{P}\left(z^{\mathfrak{e}(\alpha_{1})}I_{\alpha_{1}}(z)I_{1}(z)-z^{\mathfrak{e}(\alpha_{2})}I_{\alpha_{2}}(z)I_{1}(z)\right).

(5.93)

Proposition 5.23

(Lower-order trace cancellation)
.

For every odd integer
\ell\geq 5
, one has

D_{1}(\ell)=0.

(5.94)

Proof.

Write
N=\ell
and
P=N^{2}
. Every residue
r
can be written uniquely as

r=Nt+n,\qquad 0\leq t\leq N-1,\qquad 0\leq n\leq N-1.

(5.95)

The excluded value
r=0
contributes zero because it is weighted by
r
. Put
h=(N+1)/2
. A direct reduction modulo
P
gives

[-\mathfrak{e}(\alpha_{1})-\alpha_{1}r]_{P}=n+N[t-n-h]_{N},

(5.96)

and

[-\mathfrak{e}(\alpha_{2})-\alpha_{2}r]_{P}=n+N[t-2n-1]_{N}.

(5.97)

Here
[\cdot]_{N}
denotes the standard residue in
\{0,\ldots,N-1\}
. The trace formula for
D_{1}
becomes

D_{1}(\ell)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(Nt+n)\left([t-n-h]_{N}-[t-2n-1]_{N}\right).

(5.98)

For fixed
n
, both
[t-n-h]_{N}
and
[t-2n-1]_{N}
are permutations of
0,\ldots,N-1
as
t
varies, so the part weighted by
n
cancels. The remaining part is

\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left(G(n+h)-G(2n+1)\right),

(5.99)

where

G(c)=\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}t[t-c]_{N}.

(5.100)

The maps
n\mapsto n+h
and
n\mapsto 2n+1
are both permutations of
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
, since
N
is odd. Hence the two sums over
G
are equal, and
D_{1}(\ell)=0
.
∎

Definition 5.24

(Cubic convolution)
.

For
m\in\mathbb{Z}/P\mathbb{Z}
, define

H_{P}(m)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}=1\\
r_{1}+r_{2}+r_{3}\equiv m\!\!\!\pmod{P}\end{subarray}}^{P-1}r_{1}r_{2}r_{3}.

(5.101)

Lemma 5.25

(Trace-to-convolution formula)
.

One has

D_{3}(\ell)=\frac{1}{P^{3}}\sum_{r=1}^{P-1}r\left[H_{P}([-\mathfrak{f}(\alpha_{1})-\alpha_{1}r]_{P})-H_{P}([-\mathfrak{f}(\alpha_{2})-\alpha_{2}r]_{P})\right].

(5.102)

Proof.

Expanding
z^{\mathfrak{f}(a)}I_{a}(z)I_{1}(z)^{3}
gives

\frac{1}{P^{4}}\sum_{r,s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}=1}^{P-1}rs_{1}s_{2}s_{3}z^{\mathfrak{f}(a)+ar+s_{1}+s_{2}+s_{3}}.

(5.103)

Applying
\tau_{P}
selects exponents congruent to zero modulo
P
and subtracts the total coefficient sum. The total coefficient sum is the same for
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
, so it cancels in the difference. The remaining zero-exponent condition is exactly the displayed convolution formula.
∎

Lemma 5.26

(Polynomial form of the cubic convolution)
.

For
0\leq m\leq P-1
, one has

H_{P}(m)=\frac{P^{2}}{6}m^{3}-\frac{P^{2}(P-3)}{4}m^{2}+\frac{P^{2}(P^{2}-9P+12)}{12}m+\frac{P^{2}(P-2)(P-1)(P+1)}{8}.

(5.104)

Proof.

Let
B_{P}(x)=\sum_{r=1}^{P-1}rx^{r}=x(1-Px^{P-1}+(P-1)x^{P})/(1-x)^{2}
. The coefficient of
x^{N}
in
B_{P}(x)^{3}
is the sum of
r_{1}r_{2}r_{3}
over
r_{1}+r_{2}+r_{3}=N
. Since
3\leq N\leq 3P-3
, the congruence class
m
receives contributions only from
m
,
m+P
, and
m+2P
. Expanding

B_{P}(x)^{3}=\frac{x^{3}(1-Px^{P-1}+(P-1)x^{P})^{3}}{(1-x)^{6}}

(5.105)

and using
[x^{M}](1-x)^{-6}=\binom{M+5}{5}
, then collecting powers of
m
, gives the displayed cubic polynomial.
∎

Lemma 5.27

(Block moment identities)
.

Let
N=\ell
and
P=N^{2}
. Define

R_{1}(t,n)=\left[n-1+N\left(t-n-\frac{N+1}{2}\right)\right]_{P},

(5.106)

and

R_{2}(t,n)=\left[n-1+N(t-2n-1)\right]_{P}.

(5.107)

For

M_{k}(N)=\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(Nt+n)\left(R_{1}(t,n)^{k}-R_{2}(t,n)^{k}\right),

(5.108)

one has

M_{1}(N)=\frac{N^{3}(N-1)}{2},

(5.109)

M_{2}(N)=\frac{N^{3}(N-1)(N^{2}-2)}{2},

(5.110)

and

M_{3}(N)=-\frac{N^{3}(N-1)(11N^{5}-460N^{4}+70N^{3}+1420N^{2}-81N-1440)}{960}.

(5.111)

Proof.

Write
[q]_{N}
for the standard residue modulo
N
, let
c_{n}=[n-1]_{N}
, and let
\varepsilon_{n}=1
for
n=0
and
\varepsilon_{n}=0
otherwise. Then

[n-1+NQ]_{P}=c_{n}+N[Q-\varepsilon_{n}]_{N}.

(5.112)

Thus the two residues can be written as

R_{i}(t,n)=c_{n}+N[t-\beta_{i}(n)]_{N},

(5.113)

where

\beta_{1}(n)=n+\frac{N+1}{2}+\varepsilon_{n},

(5.114)

and

\beta_{2}(n)=2n+1+\varepsilon_{n}.

(5.115)

For
a\geq 0
, define

S_{a}(N)=\sum_{q=0}^{N}q^{a}.

(5.116)

Define

G_{a}(c)=\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}t[t-c]_{N}^{a}.

(5.117)

Changing variables

q=[t-c]_{N}

(5.118)

gives

G_{a}(c)=\sum_{q=0}^{N-1}[q+c]_{N}q^{a}.

(5.119)

Splitting the range at
q=N-c
, one obtains

G_{a}(c)=\sum_{q=0}^{N-c-1}(q+c)q^{a}+\sum_{q=N-c}^{N-1}(q+c-N)q^{a}.

(5.120)

Equivalently,

G_{a}(c)=S_{a+1}(N-1)+cS_{a}(N-1)-N\left(S_{a}(N-1)-S_{a}(N-c-1)\right).

(5.121)

This reduces every occurrence of
G_{a}(c)
to the standard power sums

S_{0}(N),\qquad S_{1}(N),\qquad S_{2}(N),\qquad S_{3}(N),\qquad S_{4}(N).

(5.122)

Now write

R_{i}(t,n)=c_{n}+N[t-\beta_{i}(n)]_{N}.

(5.123)

For
k=1,2,3
, expand

R_{i}(t,n)^{k}=\sum_{a=0}^{k}\binom{k}{a}c_{n}^{k-a}N^{a}[t-\beta_{i}(n)]_{N}^{a}.

(5.124)

Multiplying by
Nt+n
, summing in
t
, and using the definition of
G_{a}
, gives

\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}(Nt+n)R_{i}(t,n)^{k}=\sum_{a=0}^{k}\binom{k}{a}c_{n}^{k-a}N^{a}\left(NG_{a}(\beta_{i}(n))+n\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}[t-\beta_{i}(n)]_{N}^{a}\right).

(5.125)

The second sum is independent of
\beta_{i}(n)
, because
[t-\beta_{i}(n)]_{N}
permutes the residues modulo
N
as
t
runs from
0
to
N-1
. Therefore it cancels in the difference between
i=1
and
i=2
. Hence

M_{k}(N)=N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\sum_{a=0}^{k}\binom{k}{a}c_{n}^{k-a}N^{a}\left(G_{a}(\beta_{1}(n))-G_{a}(\beta_{2}(n))\right).

(5.126)

Substituting

c_{n}=[n-1]_{N},\qquad\beta_{1}(n)=n+\frac{N+1}{2}+\varepsilon_{n},\qquad\beta_{2}(n)=2n+1+\varepsilon_{n},

(5.127)

and using the displayed formula for
G_{a}
, all terms reduce to finite sums of powers of
n
of degree at most four.

For completeness, we spell out the last algebraic step. Write

S_{a}(N-1)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}n^{a}.

(5.128)

We use

S_{0}(N-1)=N,\qquad S_{1}(N-1)=\frac{N(N-1)}{2},\qquad S_{2}(N-1)=\frac{N(N-1)(2N-1)}{6},

(5.129)

S_{3}(N-1)=\frac{N^{2}(N-1)^{2}}{4},\qquad S_{4}(N-1)=\frac{N(N-1)(2N-1)(3N^{2}-3N-1)}{30}.

(5.130)

After substituting
c_{n}=[n-1]_{N}
,
\beta_{1}(n)=n+(N+1)/2+\varepsilon_{n}
, and
\beta_{2}(n)=2n+1+\varepsilon_{n}
, the summand in
M_{k}(N)
, for
k=1,2,3
, is a polynomial in
n
and
N
of degree at most four, apart from the isolated correction at
n=0
coming from
\varepsilon_{n}
. Evaluating the
n=0
correction separately and summing the remaining polynomial over
1\leq n\leq N-1
using the five displayed power-sum formulas gives exactly the three displayed identities for
M_{1}(N)
,
M_{2}(N)
, and
M_{3}(N)
.
∎

Theorem 5.28

(Evaluation of the cubic trace contribution)
.

For every odd integer
\ell\geq 5
, one has

D_{3}(\ell)=-\frac{\ell^{2}(\ell-1)^{2}(\ell+1)(11\ell^{2}+20\ell+81)}{5760}.

(5.131)

Proof.

By Lemma
5.25
, the block residues are
R_{1}(t,n)
and
R_{2}(t,n)
from Lemma
5.27
. Applying Lemma
5.26
gives

D_{3}(\ell)=\frac{1}{P}\left[\frac{1}{6}M_{3}(\ell)-\frac{P-3}{4}M_{2}(\ell)+\frac{P^{2}-9P+12}{12}M_{1}(\ell)\right].

(5.132)

Substituting
P=\ell^{2}
and the three moment identities from Lemma
5.27
, then simplifying the resulting polynomial, gives the displayed formula.
∎

Theorem 5.29

(Nonvanishing of the square-family rho coefficient)
.

For every odd integer
\ell\geq 5
,

C_{\ell}=-\frac{\ell^{2}(\ell-1)^{2}(\ell+1)(11\ell^{2}+20\ell+81)}{180}.

(5.133)

In particular,
C_{\ell}\neq 0
.

Proof.

We have
C_{\ell}=32D_{3}(\ell)+4D_{1}(\ell)
. Proposition
5.23
gives
D_{1}(\ell)=0
, and Theorem
5.28
gives the formula for
D_{3}(\ell)
. Multiplying by
32
gives the displayed expression for
C_{\ell}
. Since
\ell\geq 5
, every factor in the numerator is positive, so
C_{\ell}<0
.
∎

Corollary 5.30

(Square-family residual-circle rho-number separation)
.

For every odd integer
\ell\geq 5
, the pair

N(\ell^{2},\ell-1)\qquad\text{and}\qquad N(\ell^{2},2\ell-1)

(5.134)

has vanishing ordinary
\eta
/rho shadow but nontrivial residual-circle equivariant rho germ. In the normalized convention,

\Phi_{\ell}(0)=0,\qquad\Phi_{\ell}^{\prime}(0)=0,\qquad\Phi_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}(0)=-\frac{\ell^{2}(\ell-1)^{2}(\ell+1)(11\ell^{2}+20\ell+81)}{180}\neq 0.

(5.135)

Here
\Phi_{\ell}^{\prime}(0)=0
follows from the evenness in Lemma
5.17
; the nontrivial assertions are
\Phi_{\ell}(0)=0
and
\Phi_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}(0)\neq 0
. Consequently, for each odd
\ell\geq 5
, there exists a finite-order residual-circle element
g
such that

\eta_{g}(D_{N(\ell^{2},\ell-1)})\neq\eta_{g}(D_{N(\ell^{2},2\ell-1)}).

(5.136)

Proof.

The first two identities are Theorem
5.19
. The second derivative identity is Theorem
5.29
. Since
\Phi_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}(0)\neq 0
, the Taylor expansion shows that
\Phi_{\ell}(\delta)\neq 0
for all sufficiently small nonzero
\delta
. Choosing such a
\delta
with
\delta/\pi\in\mathbb{Q}
gives a finite-order residual-circle element. Lemma
5.7
then gives the same separation for the corresponding Donnelly-APS
\eta
values.
∎

5.7
Finite-cyclic secondary pairing

The preceding corollary gives a nonzero finite-order equivariant
\eta
/rho number. The corresponding finite-cyclic class-function statement is the following. It is an unconditional secondary
\eta
statement at the level of finite-order equivariant
\eta
values.

Fix odd
\ell\geq 5
. Choose a sufficiently small rational
\delta/\pi
such that
\Phi_{\ell}(\delta)\neq 0
, and let

g_{\delta}=e^{2i\delta}.

(5.137)

Then
g_{\delta}
has finite order. Let
G_{\delta}=\langle g_{\delta}\rangle
be the finite cyclic subgroup of the residual circle generated by
g_{\delta}
.

Definition 5.31

(Normalized finite-cyclic equivariant rho class function)
.

For
h\in G_{\delta}
, define

\rho^{G_{\delta},\text{norm}}_{\ell}(h)=\kappa_{\ell^{2}}^{-1}\left[\eta_{h}(D_{\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{\ell,1}})-\eta_{h}(D_{\mathcal{Y}^{\operatorname{res}}_{\ell,2}})\right].

(5.138)

Thus
\rho^{G_{\delta},\text{norm}}_{\ell}
is the normalized complex-valued class function on
G_{\delta}
; the Donnelly-APS convention differs only by the common nonzero factor from Lemma
5.7
.

Theorem 5.32

(Finite-cyclic secondary separation)
.

For every odd
\ell\geq 5
, there exists a finite cyclic subgroup
G_{\delta}\subset S^{1}_{\text{res}}
such that

\rho^{G_{\delta},\text{norm}}_{\ell}\neq 0.

(5.139)

More precisely,

\rho^{G_{\delta},\text{norm}}_{\ell}(g_{\delta})=\Phi_{\ell}(\delta)\neq 0.

(5.140)

Proof.

This follows directly from Corollary
5.30
. The chosen finite-order element
g_{\delta}
satisfies
\Phi_{\ell}(\delta)\neq 0
, and by definition this value is
\rho^{G_{\delta},\text{norm}}_{\ell}(g_{\delta})
.
∎

Corollary 5.33

(Nonzero finite-order evaluation pairing)
.

For every odd
\ell\geq 5
, there exists a finite cyclic subgroup
G_{\delta}\subset S^{1}_{\text{res}}
and an element
g_{\delta}\in G_{\delta}
such that

\operatorname{ev}_{g_{\delta}}(\rho^{G_{\delta},\text{norm}}_{\ell})\neq 0.

(5.141)

Proof.

The evaluation pairing is
\operatorname{ev}_{g_{\delta}}(F)=F(g_{\delta})
. Therefore

\operatorname{ev}_{g_{\delta}}(\rho^{G_{\delta},\text{norm}}_{\ell})=\rho^{G_{\delta},\text{norm}}_{\ell}(g_{\delta})=\Phi_{\ell}(\delta)\neq 0.

(5.142)

∎

6
Classical rho invariants and secondary refinements

6.1
Comparison with the classical lens-space rho setting

The results above are residual-circle equivariant
\eta
and rho-number separations. The pair
L(25,4)
and
L(25,9)
also sits naturally inside the classical lens-space setting:

4\cdot 9\equiv 6^{2}\pmod{25},

(6.1)

while

9\not\equiv\pm 4^{\pm 1}\pmod{25}.

(6.2)

Thus the pair is homotopy equivalent but not homeomorphic in the usual lens-space sense. The result proved here is more specific: it exhibits a residual-circle
\eta
germ for which the scalar
\eta
value vanishes, the first derivative vanishes by symmetry, and the second derivative is nonzero:

\Phi(0)=0,\qquad\Phi^{\prime}(0)=0,\qquad\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)\neq 0.

(6.3)

The resulting second-jet formula gives an infinite family of explicit residual-circle rho-number separations. Equality of the ordinary
\eta
value for the untwisted Dirac operator is not, by itself, equality of all finite-cyclic APS
\rho
-invariants; that would require comparing twists by representations of
\pi_{1}(L(p,q))
and the relevant kernel normalizations. We therefore keep the conclusion at the residual-circle level.

6.2
Relation to analytic surgery and later secondary-class questions

The residual circle used here is an additional geometric symmetry, whereas the standard analytic-surgery and higher-rho constructions are built from the fundamental group and its group
C^{*}
-algebra
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
. A Higson-Roe structure-class refinement would therefore require extra work beyond the numerical
\eta
/rho statements proved here. Related delocalized
\eta
and rho-type refinements appear in finite and infinite fundamental-group settings, but those pairings are not used in the present argument
[
31
]
.

A possible continuation is to ask whether the second jet of the residual-circle
\eta
germ admits a natural secondary
K
-theoretic interpretation, perhaps through an equivariant analytic-surgery group, equivariant
K
-homology, or eta-form/transgression formalism. A possible singular-foliation extension would require replacing the present free finite-quotient model with a holonomy groupoid framework, in the sense of Androulidakis-Skandalis
[
32
]
. This is not needed for the present eta-separation theorem and is left for separate work.

Appendix C
Symbolic verification of the block moment identities

For reproducibility, this appendix gives an exact symbolic check of the block moment identities used in Lemma
5.27
. The proof of the lemma reduces the moments to ordinary power sums; the symbolic check below gives an independent exact audit of the resulting closed forms. The symbolic reductions were performed in Mathematica using exact integer and rational arithmetic; no floating-point approximation is used.

Let
N
be an odd integer and let

P=N^{2}.

(C.1)

For
0\leq n,t\leq N-1
, define

\varepsilon_{n}=\begin{cases}1,&n=0,\\
0,&n\neq 0,\end{cases}\qquad c_{n}=[n-1]_{N},

(C.2)

and

\beta_{1}(n)=n+\frac{N+1}{2}+\varepsilon_{n},\qquad\beta_{2}(n)=2n+1+\varepsilon_{n}.

(C.3)

Then

R_{i}(t,n)=c_{n}+N[t-\beta_{i}(n)]_{N}.

(C.4)

The moments are

M_{k}(N)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}(Nt+n)\left(R_{1}(t,n)^{k}-R_{2}(t,n)^{k}\right).

(C.5)

The exact symbolic audit gives the following closed forms. First,

M_{1}(N)=\frac{N^{3}(N-1)}{2}.

(C.6)

Second,

M_{2}(N)=\frac{N^{3}(N-1)(N^{2}-2)}{2}.

(C.7)

Third,

M_{3}(N)=-\frac{N^{3}(N-1)\bigl(11N^{5}-460N^{4}+70N^{3}+1420N^{2}-81N-1440\bigr)}{960}.

(C.8)

Substituting these three identities into the assembly identity used in Lemma
5.27
gives

D_{3}(N)=-\frac{N^{2}(N-1)^{2}(N+1)(11N^{2}+20N+81)}{5760}.

(C.9)

Since
D_{1}(N)=0
, the normalized second derivative is

C_{\ell}=32D_{3}(\ell)=-\frac{\ell^{2}(\ell-1)^{2}(\ell+1)(11\ell^{2}+20\ell+81)}{180}.

(C.10)

This agrees with the direct trace computation from Lemma
5.25
and with the finite trigonometric check in the case
\ell=5
.
