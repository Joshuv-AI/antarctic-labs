Stimuli-responsive metal-organic polyhedra–based hydrogel membrane for switchable separations toward CO2/H2
Authors: Kun Li, Yuan Peng, Wentai Hu, Xinyu Wang, Weishen Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carbon capture is essential for producing blue hydrogen and syngas. However, designing a single membrane with dual CO2-selective and H2-selective separations has long been considered impractical because these modes rely on fundamentally different mechanisms. We report a stimuli-responsive membrane that overcomes this limitation by integrating two functional materials. A zirconium metal-organic polyhedron (Zr-MOP) polymerized hydrogel membrane was fabricated via a stepwise molecular-level blending and polymerization strategy, achieving excellent filler dispersion, strong interfacial compatibility, and good solution processability. The Zr-MOP cages reinforce the hydrogel hydrogen-bond network, enabling high CO2-philic performance with a CO2/H2 separation factor of 44 and CO2 permeability of 7025 Barrer. Upon dehydration, membrane shrinkage induces cage rearrangement and exposure of molecular sieving windows, switching the separation behavior to a highly H2-selective mode with an H2/CO2 separation factor of 798. This transition is reversible and can be further regulated by pH stimuli. This work introduces a strategy for membranes with switchable and inverted separation functions.

INTRODUCTION

Separating CO2 from H2 is critical for achieving important environmental, energy, and chemical engineering goals such as decarbonization and the downstream utilization of syngas (1, 2). Membrane-based gas separation is an advanced separation technology due to its low energy consumption, continuous operation, and environmental adaptability (3–6). In these processes, the membrane material itself is the key component (analogous to the “chip” in electronics). Polymer membranes bearing polar groups currently dominate CO2/H2 separation because of their strong affinity for CO2. However, these membranes face a trade-off between gas permeability and selectivity, stemming from the competition between CO2 preferential adsorption and rapid diffusion of small H2 molecules (7, 8). Polycrystalline molecular sieve membranes [e.g., metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)] have attracted attention toward H2 separation from CO2 for a high-purity H2 due to their high-density periodic pores and excellent molecular sieving properties (9–13). However, practical issues such as scale-up manufacturing and structural defects continue to impede industrial implementation of these membrane solutions (4). To summarize, developing CO2-philic membranes to capture CO2 from CO2-lean streams remains challenging (14). Even more difficult is to create a single membrane that can be switched between CO2-selective and H2-selective modes to satisfy multiple industrial scenarios. New membrane materials are urgently needed, ones that provide precise CO2-recognition or exclusion within their transport channels to achieve superior CO2/H2 and H2/CO2 separations while also being amenable to large-scale production.

Mixed-matrix membranes (MMMs), which combine polymer matrixes with porous fillers, can leverage the advantages of both polymer and molecular sieve membrane types (15–18). However, MMMs often suffer from incompatibility between the filler and matrix and filler agglomeration. These issues critically limit their ultimate separation performance. MOFs, as prototypical three-dimensional (3D) and 2D porous fillers, have been widely used in MMMs for gas separation (19–21). Yet, few studies have directly tackled the compatibility and dispersion challenges (22). In parallel with MOFs, metal-organic polyhedra (MOPs) are discrete 0D porous cages typically 1 to 3 nm in size. Each MOP consists of metal nodes or clusters bridged by organic ligands via internal coordination bonds, and MOP cages can orderly aggregate into bulky crystalline grains through weak external interactions (van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonds, π-π interactions, etc.) (23). MOPs exhibit excellent solution processability, which is highly advantageous for MMM fabrication. In addition, their easily tuned surface functionalities, window apertures, and cage cavities allow the transport environment to be tailored for enhancing gas separation performance. Zirconium MOPs (Zr-MOPs), in particular, offer superior stability due to strong Zr─O coordination bonds. Among them, an amine-functionalized Zr-MOP, [Cp3Zr3O(OH)3]4[BDC-NH2]6[(C2H5)2NH2]2Cl6 (also known as UMOP-1-NH2, denoted hereafter as Zr-MOP-NH2) (24, 25), has attracted attention due to its basic pendant amino group. We envisioned that these active amino groups could be postsynthetically modified into polymerizable groups to enable covalent linking with polymers at molecular level, addressing filler aggregation and matrix incompatibility once and for all. Moreover, MOP fillers have a strong affinity for CO2 due to higher polarizability and quadrupole moment of CO2, which should boost CO2-selective transport. Meanwhile, recent studies indicate that analogous triangular window apertures (e.g., those in UiO-66 3D frameworks) can exhibit H2-selective sieving (26–28). Therefore, incorporating Zr-MOP cages into a polymeric membrane is an appealing strategy to tailor switchable dual-functional CO2/H2 separations.

Hydrogels were chosen as the polymeric matrix due to their adjustable hydrophilic networks (29, 30). Unlike conventional polymers, hydrogel matrixes are formed from short monomers, which suffice to covalently graft on the Zr-MOP cages. The abundant continuous water-based hydrogen bond network can facilitate CO2-selective transport. In addition, hydrogels have a stimuli-responsive dehydration behavior that causes a shrinkage of (free) volume (31, 32). By exploiting this property, we conceived that the window apertures of MOPs embedded in the membrane could be controllably exposed, thereby providing flexible H2-selective transport pathways to adapt the membrane for hydrogen purification cascades.

Here, we developed a self-standing, integrated MOP-hydrogel membrane by polymerizing Zr-MOP cages with hydrogel monomers, achieving a material with switchable CO2/H2 separation capabilities. A double-network poly(vinyl alcohol)/poly(acrylic acid-co-acrylamide) [PVA/p(AA-co-AM)] hydrogel was deployed as the matrix because the intertwined double network provided very accessible reactive monomers, high hydroscopicity, pH responsiveness, and good mechanical strength. Zr-MOP-NH2 was postsynthetically modified with methacrylic anhydride, in order to replace the amino groups with methacrylamide groups (Fig. 1A and fig. S1). The ultimate MOP was depicted as Zr-MOP-Met. The introduced Met groups allowed the MOP cages to copolymerize with AA and AM monomers or N,N′-methylene-bis-acrylamide (MBA) crosslinker under ultraviolet (UV) light irradiation, forming a covalently crosslinked Zr-MOP-Met@hydrogel MMM (Fig. 1, B and C). This two-step strategy, (i) molecular-level mixing to create a homogeneous, solution-processable precursor and (ii) UV-induced polymerization to form the solid membrane, enabled excellent control over membrane formation without limitation on size. The obtained MMMs with an optimal loading of 15 weight % (wt %) Zr-MOP-Met achieved exceptional CO2/H2 separation performance, combining high selectivity and high CO2 permeability. Furthermore, when the membrane lost water and shrunk in volume as triggered by a dry ambiance or exposing to an acidic environment, its separation behavior switched to an outstanding H2-selective separation performance (Fig. 1C). Benefiting from the inheriting flexibility of hydrogel, the membrane could be bent or curled at will, and it also exhibited a remarkable self-healing ability.

RESULTS

Synthesis and characterization of Zr-MOP-Met powders

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern (Fig. 2A) confirmed the successful synthesis of Zr-MOP-NH2, consistent with the previous result (24). After postsynthetic modification, Zr-MOP-Met remained the same crystalline structure as the parent MOP. We characterized the morphology and structural chemistry of Zr-MOP-NH2 and Zr-MOP-Met using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, and high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS). Notably, the Zr-MOP-NH2 and Zr-MOP-Met powders showed different colors (Fig. 2, B and C, insets). The Zr-MOP-NH2 displayed a well-defined cubic morphology with a particle size of ∼10 μm, whereas Zr-MOP-Met formed irregular plate-like aggregates (Fig. 2, B and C), showing a change attributed to the increased steric hindrance of the grafted Met groups. The FTIR spectra showed a new C═O stretching peak at 1677 cm−1 in Zr-MOP-Met (Fig. 2D), verifying successful grafting of Met groups onto the MOP benzene rings (33). The 1H NMR spectra of the organic ligands after MOP digestion further confirmed the presence of Met groups in Zr-MOP-Met (Fig. 2E). New proton signals at 2.03, 5.63, 5.93, 7.69, 8.11, and 9.21 parts per million (ppm) were assigned to the methacrylamide ligand, while the signals from the original amino ligand were still present (34). HRMS provided insight into the degree of functionalization (fig. S2A). For Zr-MOP-NH2, major mass/charge ratio (m/z) peaks at 804.63, 1072.84, and 1607.78 corresponded to the expected [M]4+, [M-H]3+, and [M-2H]2+ ions (where M is a single MOP cage without Cl− ions) (24). Focusing on the [M-2H]2+ ion, the Zr-MOP-Met sample displayed new peaks at m/z ∼ 1642.28, 1675.80, 1709.82, 1743.83, 1783.55, and 1811.86, corresponding to cages with one through six Met groups attached (fig. S2, B and C). This result indicated two key points: (i) Most of the MOP cages were functionalized with two Met groups, and (b) the structural integrity of the MOP cages was preserved after modification.

In general, the porosity of these MOP materials arises from the internal cage cavities and the packing voids between the cage arrays (24, 25, 35). The porosity of the obtained cages was analyzed by N2 sorption at 77 K. Both samples showed type II sorption isotherms (Fig. 2F), indicating a microporous character (36). The pore size distribution (PSD) of Zr-MOP-NH2 centered at 0.68, 1.09, and 1.69 nm, corresponding well with the theoretical pore sizes of tetrahedral cages (0.6 nm), cubic packing gaps (1 nm), and rhombicuboctahedral voids (1.8 nm) in the crystal structure (24). Owing to large probe molecule size, no window size was identified according to the N2 sorption. Zr-MOP-Met showed a much lower Brunauer-Emmett-Teller surface area (20.63 m2 g−1) than that of Zr-MOP-NH2 (110.43 m2 g−1), mainly due to the loss of intercage voids. This was consistent with the more randomly aggregated morphology of Zr-MOP-Met interfered by the bulky Met groups (Fig. 2, B and C). N2 sorption could also not resolve pores below 1 nm in Zr-MOP-Met, presumably because the Met groups partially blocked the cage windows, a phenomenon also noted in literature (37). We therefore used CO2 sorption to probe the small apertures (fig. S3, A and B). Both Zr-MOP-NH2 and Zr-MOP-Met showed pronounced PSD peaks centered at ∼0.358 nm (fig. S3C), corresponding to the crystalline triangular windows of the cages. This confirmed again that the intrinsic microporosity of the cages was preserved after functionalization. Overall, we successfully synthesized methacrylamide-functionalized Zr-MOP cages ready for membrane fabrication.

Structure of the MMMs

An in situ free-radical polymerization was initiated to integrate the Zr-MOP-Met filler with the hydrogel matrix at various loadings (see Materials and Methods for details). High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) revealed that Zr-MOP-Met cages dispersed well in water, their tetrahedral morphology was visible (Fig. 3A). Also, its dispersion was better than that of unmodified Zr-MOP-NH2 under the same condition (fig. S4A). After mixing Zr-MOP-Met into the hydrogel precursor solution, the cages remained excellently dispersed (Fig. 3B) due to favorable interactions between the active Met groups on the cages and the hydrogel monomers. The presence of monomers in the imaging field did obscure the contrast somewhat, but the uniform distribution of Zr-MOP-Met cages was still conformed. By contrast, the precursor containing Zr-MOP-NH2 revealed large agglomerates (fig. S4B), highlighting the poor compatibility of the unmodified cages.

Self-standing Zr-MOP-Met@hydrogel-x membranes (where x is the mass fraction of Zr-MOP-Met filler) were achieved by casting the precursor solution into an 18-mm mold and UV-curing for 30 min. Figure 3C showed photograph of a pure hydrogel membrane and MMMs with various Zr-MOP-Met loadings. As the cage cargo increased, the membranes developed a progressively darker color. Notably, membranes with low MOP loadings (x < 10 wt %) shrank in size during curing, likely due to hydrogel dehydration stemming from the exothermic polymerization process. In contrast, MMMs with higher loadings (≥10 wt % Zr-MOP-Met) retained the original size. This phenomenon suggested that sufficient Zr-MOP-Met cages could notably improve water retention of hydrogel through an optimized structure, namely, the well-dispersed porous MOP cages and strong MOP-polymer crosslinking, together with the conversion of free water to bound water by the polar amino and Met groups, all helped strengthen the water-based hydrogen bond network. This could greatly benefit CO2 transport. The thicknesses of as-synthesized membranes were measured under an optical microscope, all revealing a few hundred microns (fig. S5 and table S1). The MMMs also inherited excellent hydrogel flexibility. They could be bent or rolled without damage (fig. S6), aided by the dual-network hydrogel and the cage “pinning” effect reinforcing the polymer matrix. However, when the Zr-MOP-Met loading exceeded 20 wt %, the precursor solution failed to cure into a membrane.

FTIR analysis of the freeze-dried membranes (water obscured IR signals) confirmed the formation of the hybrid network (fig. S7). The spectra showed the characteristic vibrations of the polymer matrix (Fig. 3D), including C─H stretching (2940 cm−1), C═O (1654 cm−1) and N─H (1596 cm−1) stretching, and amide C─N bending (1163 cm−1), as well as the peaks assigned to MOP cages including the Zr─O bonds at 766 cm−1 and the out-of-plane C─H deformation at 812 cm−1 (38, 39). The result well demonstrated that Zr-MOP-Met was integrated into the hydrogel network. XRD patterns of the MMMs (Fig. 3E) showed that the sharp diffraction peaks of bulky Zr-MOP-Met (Fig. 2A) disappeared upon the incorporation into the membrane, further verifying the cages were well-dispersed, i.e., no large/bulky crystalline aggregates/grains remained. SEM revealed uniform, smooth membrane surfaces for the MMM, similar to the pure hydrogel membrane (Fig. 3, F and G, and figs. S8 and S9). No obvious particles/aggregates were observed. Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping for Zr in a high-loading Zr-MOP-Met@hydrogel-15 membrane likewise showed a homogeneous distribution of Zr (Fig. 3H), confirming that the Zr-MOP-Met cages were uniformly distributed throughout the hydrogel matrix led by the molecular-level mixing and polymerization. By contrast, an MMM containing unmodified Zr-MOP-NH2 exhibited large MOP aggregates within the matrix (fig. S10), reflecting the poor phase compatibility often seen in traditional MMMs (40–42).

Fluorescence microscopy further verified the good dispersion of MOP cages within the hydrogel membrane. Zr-MOP-Met crystals emitted a blue fluorescence under 405-nm excitation (Fig. 3, I and J), whereas the PVA/p(AA-co-AM) hydrogel itself showed no fluorescence (Fig. 3K). Taking advantage of this, we imaged a Zr-MOP-Met@hydrogel-15 membrane under 405-nm excitation, and a uniform fluorescence signal was observed (fig. S11). The corresponding confocal 3D reconstruction over a 400 μm–by–400 μm–by–200 μm volume of the sample displayed a normalized homogeneous dispersion of these high-density MOP cages throughout the membrane (Fig. 3L). We also examined the membrane surface roughness by atomic force microscopy (AFM) analysis for dried samples. The Zr-MOP-Met@hydrogel membranes displayed surface roughness comparable to the pure hydrogel membrane (fig. S12). Some protrusions originating from the hydrogel matrix upon drying were present. Overall, the above results demonstrated that the Zr-MOP-Met cages were highly dispersed in the hydrogel matrix, and they simultaneously strengthened the hydrogel network by harnessing the molecular-level blending and in situ polymerization.

Separation performance of the membranes

The membrane gas separation performance was evaluated by using an equimolar CO2/H2 feed in a Wicke-Kallenbach cell at room temperature. Figure 4A summarized the CO2/H2 separation factor (SF) and permeability of membranes with varying Zr-MOP-Met loadings. Harnessing the continuous water-based hydrogen bond network in the hydrogel (43), CO2 permeated preferentially through the membrane. The pure hydrogel membrane exhibited a moderate CO2/H2 separation, with an average SF of 18 ± 1 and a CO2 permeability of 1561 ± 118 Barrer. As the Zr-MOP-Met loading increased, both SF and CO2 permeability showed volcanic growth trends. Among the MMMs, the membrane with 15 wt % Zr-MOP-Met loading achieved the best performance with a CO2/H2 SF of 44 ± 6 and a CO2 permeability of 7025 ± 1228 Barrer, which were 144 and 350% higher than those of the pure hydrogel membranes, respectively (Fig. 4B). The CO2/H2 ideal adsorbed solution theory (IAST) selectivity at 1 bar for Zr-MOP-Met was around 35 (fig. S13). Hence, this exceptional performance was attributed to the abundant amino groups and intrinsic microporosity of the Zr-MOP-Met filler, along with its uniform dispersion at high loading, which synergistically enhanced CO2-selective adsorption and reduced CO2 diffusion resistance in the membrane. In addition, the Zr-MOP-Met@hydrogel-15 membrane was ∼57% more selective than the MMM containing 15 wt % of Zr-MOP-NH2 (Fig. 4B), underscoring the importance of using microporous, discrete, and polymerizable MOP cages and integrating them via molecular-level polymerization to achieve superior separation performance. As shown in Fig. 4C (detailed in table S2), our Zr-MOP-Met@hydrogel membranes surpassed all the previously reported membranes in CO2/H2 separation performance, representing the state-of-the-art record for this separation application.

Single-gas permeation tests on the Zr-MOP-Met@hydrogel-15 membrane revealed a sharp cutoff between CO2 and other gases (Fig. 4D). The ideal selectivities were 35.4 for CO2/H2, 9.9 for CO2/N2, and 61.7 for CO2/CH4, in good agreement with the selectivities measured using equimolar gas mixtures (Fig. 4E). Moreover, the MMM exhibited excellent performance for separating CO2 from CH4 (mixed gas SF: 51.8) and from N2 (mixed gas SF: 13.4), highlighting its versatility for applications such as natural gas sweetening and CO2 capture from flue gas. In fact, the membrane’s CO2/CH4 separation performance ranked among the top tier of the reported membranes (44–47).

The remarkable membrane performance was further highlighted under varying feed compositions. The Zr-MOP-Met@hydrogel-15 membrane maintained a high separation performance across a broad range of CO2 concentrations (Fig. 4F), indicating its robustness and ability to remove low-percentage CO2 from H2 streams. We also examined the effect of transmembrane pressure (fig. S14). As pressure increased, the permeabilities of both CO2 and H2 decreased, consistent with a facilitated transport mechanism (43). The Zr-MOP-Met@hydrogel-15 membrane demonstrated an excellent separation durability. Its permeability and CO2/H2 mixture SF remained stable over 260 hours of continuous operation in a humid feed (Fig. 4G), and the performance fully recovered even after a 33-hour exposure to dry air. We weighted both the wet and dry membranes and calculated the water contents of the membranes. It was concluded that a high-water content of wet membrane was capable of ensuring a good CO2/H2 separation performance (fig. S15 and table S3) via a humidity-dependent facilitated transport mechanism. By contrast, a pure hydrogel membrane executing the separation task suffered severe degradation (fig. S16), emphasizing the stabilizing “pinning” effect of the Zr-MOP-Met cages in the hydrogel network. In addition, bending or curling the MMM did not alter its separation performance (fig. S17), illustrating its mechanical robustness.

Switching the feed to a completely dry gas caused the membrane’s separation behavior to invert. H2 became the preferentially permeating species, harvesting an extraordinary H2/CO2 SF of 798 ± 150 (Fig. 4H and table S3). That was because the p(AA-co-AM)–based hydrogel is stimuli-responsive according to the ambient conditions, reducing water retention and giving rise to volume shrinkage when exposed to dry atmosphere. SEM image confirmed a notable thickness reduction to ∼5% of its original (21 μm versus 417 μm; fig. S18), the dried MMM remained intact with no cracks or pinholes. This sharp volume shrinkage brought the Zr-MOP-Met cages to rearrange into a more compact structure. Therefore, the densely packed Zr-MOP-Met cages with their 0.358-nm windows, rather than the dry gel network, dominated the transport pathway, enforcing a strict molecular sieving mechanism that favored the smaller H2 over CO2 (Fig. 4I and fig. S3C). Even polycrystalline MOF membranes with slightly larger pores have been shown to achieve H2/CO2 separation via size exclusion (28, 48–50), so the ultramicroporous MOP cages are highly effective sieves once the hydrogel is desiccated.

In practice, fully dehydrating or rehydrating the membrane can take several hours as observed during the long-term durability test. To accelerate this switching, we exploited the pH-responsive nature of the AA-based hydrogel network (51). Under acidic conditions, the hydrogel’s carboxyl groups are quickly protonated, causing the network to shrink (dehydrate); while under neutral/basic conditions, deprotonation causes the network to swell (rehydrate). Harnessing this distinctive feature, we could reversibly manipulate membrane separation mode by simply soaking the membrane in solutions of different pH for only 30 min (Fig. 5A). In pH < 3 (strongly acidic), the membrane (dehydrated state) exhibited H2-selective separation. Starting from pH = 3, a dehydrated H2-selective membrane could thus be “switched on” to CO2-selective operation (hydrated state). The gas separation capability switch threshold could be determined by the relative water content, namely, with ≥ cal. 79 wt % of water in the Zr-MOP-Met@hydrogel-15 MMM, the H2-selective molecular sieving mechanism would switch to a CO2-facilitated transport mechanism (tables S3 and S4). This behavior was driven by the disruption (at low pH) or reformation (at higher pH) of the water-based hydrogen bond network (figs. S19 and S20). Correspondingly, FTIR spectra showed changes in the intensity of the free carboxylate peak with pH variation (fig. S21), confirming the protonation/deprotonation of the carboxyl groups.

Notably, the Zr-MOP-Met@hydrogel membrane retained intact through repeated pH cycles, whereas a pure hydrogel membrane overswelled and disintegrated at high pH (fig. S22 and table S5). We attributed this resilience to the Zr-MOP-Met cages acting as molecular anchors in the polymer matrix during dehydration and rehydration. The dispersed MOP, on the one hand, prevented the dehydrated membrane from overcompacting and preserved H2 transport channels and, on the other hand, prevented the fully hydrated membrane from suffering irreversible polymer chain relaxation. Thus, by alternating the membrane between acidic and neutral solutions, we could repeatedly switch its separation behavior on demand (Fig. 5B and fig. S23). Each transition (H2-selective to CO2-selective or vice versa) required only ∼30 min of treatment. To our knowledge, this is the first membrane capable of switchable CO2/H2 dual-function separation, able to satisfy the purification needs of both H2-rich and CO2-rich gas streams, for example, methanol-based or coal-based syngas products.

Last, we demonstrated the scalability and self-healing capability of the Zr-MOP-Met@hydrogel membrane. All raw materials are inexpensive, and the solution-casting fabrication routine is easily scalable. As a proof of concept, we prepared a large-area Zr-MOP-Met@hydrogel membrane of 19 cm by 19 cm (Fig. 5C). This membrane showed good mechanical strength and could be folded or rolled (Fig. 5, D to F; fig. S24; and movie S1) while maintaining the same pH-responsive CO2/H2 (H2/CO2) separation performances (Fig. 5G). Notably, even if the membrane was cut or fractured, the damaged parts could be healed with the assistance of crosslinker and initiator at the interface and a quick UV irradiation for 5 min, effectively “welding” the pieces back together (fig. S25). This distinctive self-healing ability was attributed to the dynamically reversible crosslinks in the hydrogel network and the presence of the well-dispersed 0D Zr-MOP-Met cages that acted as reinforcing junctions. Leveraging this self-healing capability, we could even assemble multiple membrane pieces into a larger membrane (Fig. 5, H and I), indicating excellent potential for scalable fabrication and repairability.

DISCUSSION

In summary, by taking full advantage of the 0D MOP cage functionality and a molecular-level polymerization strategy, we have established an environmentally adaptable MMM concept that offers switchable CO2/H2 or H2/CO2 separation, along with a suite of desirable features (solution processability, structural flexibility, self-healing, modular assembly, and scalability). In the Zr-MOP-Met@hydrogel membrane, the MOP cages were uniformly incorporated at high loading and fulfilled multiple functions: (i) They copolymerized with the hydrogel monomers to construct a robust composite structure; (ii) they acted as polymer network “rivets”, reinforcing the hydrogen bonded network to sustain CO2-selective transport and preventing polymer chain relaxation to maintain mechanical strength; and (iii) their intrinsic microporous windows served as precise molecular sieves for highly selective H2 transport when the membrane dehydrated. As a result, the hydrated Zr-MOP-Met@hydrogel membrane exhibited an unprecedented CO2/H2 separation performance (mixed gas SF of 44 with CO2 permeability of 7025 Barrer), while the dehydrated membrane exploited the ubiquitous Zr-MOP-Met cage apertures to achieve exceptional H2/CO2 separation (mixed gas SF of 798). By leveraging the hydrogel’s inherent pH responsiveness, we could accelerate and finely control the switching between these two modes. Furthermore, the two-step fabrication (molecular-level filler blending followed by in situ polymerization) imposed no fundamental restrictions on the membrane’s size, shape, or thickness. Coupled with the material’s intriguing self-healing capability, which enabled fractured membrane pieces to be rapidly mended or reassembled same as a jigsaw puzzle, our membrane platform provided a versatile and scalable solution. This work exemplifies the powerful synergy between MOP-based porous fillers and hydrogel matrixes, and we believe that the principles demonstrated here will inspire the design of next-generation membranes for customized molecule or ion separations.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Chemicals

Bis(cyclopentadienyl)zirconium (IV) dichloride (Cp2ZrCl2) and 2-aminoterephthalic acid were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Methacrylic anhydride (Met), acrylic acid (AA) and 2-hydroxy-4′-(2-hydroxyethoxy)-2-methylpropiophenone (Irgacure 2959) were purchased from TCI. Acrylamide (AM) was purchased from J&K Scientific. N,N′-methylene-bis-acrylamide (MBA) and PVA-1788 were purchased from Aladdin. Dichloromethane (DCM), N,N′-diethylformamide (DEF), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), acetonitrile and ethyl acetate were supplied by Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. All the commercial chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Synthesis of Zr-MOP-NH2 and Zr-MOP-Met powders

The Zr-MOP-Met powders were synthesized according to the previous reports with some modifications (24, 34, 52). Cp2ZrCl2 and 2-aminoterephthalic acid were dissolved in a mixture of DEF and H2O with a molar ratio of 1: 0.55: 120: 110. After sonicating for 5 min, the yellow solution was heated in a preheated oven at 338 K for 5 hours. The yellow Zr-MOP-NH2 powders were washed with DMF for five times by centrifugation, soaked in DCM for 48 hours for solvent exchange, and lastly dried at 323 K overnight. After that, 0.18 g of Zr-MOP-NH2 and 0.45 ml of Metwere added into 20 ml of DCM and stirred for 96 hours at room temperature. The Zr-MOP-Met products were washed with DCM for three times by centrifugation to remove any unreacted methacrylic anhydride and then dried overnight at 323 K.

Fabrication of Zr-MOP-Met@hydrogel membrane

First, 3 g of PVA was dissolved in 100 ml of deionized water and then heated to 363 K until fully dissolution. Then, AA (1.5 g), AM (0.5 g), MBA (0.02 g), Irgacure 2959 (0.02 g), and a certain amount of Zr-MOP-Met were added to the PVA aqueous solution (10 ml). After stirring for 30 min in N2 environment, the precursor solution was poured into a glass mold, which was covered by a commercial polydimethylsiloxane film frame with a center hole of 18 mm to control the ultimate area and thickness of MMM. Subsequently, UV light with a wavelength of 365 nm was used to irradiate the solution to trigger a complete polymerization for 30 min. As a result, the membrane was cured and removed onto a stainless-steel net substrate for subsequent tests.

Gas permeation test

Separation performance of Zr-MOP-Met@hydrogel membranes were evaluated by a home-made Wicke-Kallenbach device according to our previous work (22, 53). For the single-gas test, the feed gas was humidified H2, CO2, N2, and CH4, respectively, regulated by mass flow controllers with a flow rate of 50 ml/min. Humidified argon was chosen as the sweep gas. The permeate gas was evaluated by an on-line gas chromatography (Agilent, 7890b). Also, in this work, gas permeability is the average value of three different membranes under the same preparation conditions.

Characterizations

The XRD patterns of MOP powders and hydrogel membranes were collected using powder XRD (Rigaku Smartlab, at 40 kV and 200 mA) at a scan speed of 10°/min with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å). The simulated XRD pattern of Zr-MOP-NH2 was obtained from Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre (reference no. 1520038). The powders for XRD test were soaked in DMF, respectively, for 48 hours and dried overnight. FTIR spectra were measured on a Bruker VERTEX 70v spectrometer over the wavelength range of 400 to 4000 cm−1. The spectra of backgrounds and samples were recorded for 32 times. The tested membranes for FTIR were immersed in liquid nitrogen and then freeze-dried (Labconco, Freezone 2.5 L) for 48 hours. 1H NMR spectra were recorded on an AVANCE III HD 700-MHz NMR spectrometer. MOP powders were decomposed with 1 M NaOH aqueous solution, and the solution was then centrifuged to remove undissolved solid. The clear solution was acidified with 1 M HCl aqueous solution and then extracted by ethyl acetate. The ligands were obtained by solvent removal. Last, the dried product was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide–d6 with tetramethylsilane (TMS) for NMR measurement. HRMS data were recorded on SolariX XR 15-T FTMS using an electrospray ionization mode, and the MOP powders were dispersed in acetonitrile/H2O (v:v = 1:1) at a concentration of 1 mg/ml for the test. The morphologies of MOP powders and membranes were observed using scanning electron microscopes (Quanta 200 FEG and JMS-7900F). The CO2-selective membranes dried in air for at least 1 week before tests. H2-selective membranes were obtained after being blow-dried online. HAADF-STEM images were taken by a JEM-F200 device. Fluorescence confocal microscopy tests were conducted on laser scanning confocal microscope (Andor iQ 3.2) with 10×/20× dry objective lens and coupled with a Nikon Ti-E microscope (light source: CoolLED pE-100; laser, 405 nm). AFM (NanoWizard, JPK, Germany) was used to estimate the membrane surface roughness. The samples were fixed on a glass slide, and dried at 308 K for 20 hours. N2 sorption measurements at 77 K were performed on a Micromeritics Physisorption Analyzer (ASAP 2020 Plus), and the samples were activated by DCM to remove residual DMF. H2 and CO2 adsorption isotherms were recorded at 77 K, 273 K, and 298 K. The sample was activated under a dynamic vacuum at 333 K for 12 hours before the test. The water contact angle was tested on a contact angle analyzer (Kruss DSA100). The thickness of membranes was determined by optical microscope (Motic, BA310met).
