Anti-CD20 associated enterovirus meningoencephalitis: A narrative review with two illustrative cases
Authors: Kedar R Mahajan, Frank Bittner, Mary Alissa Willis, Carlos Isada, Robert Bermel, Robert J Fox, Jonathan Lee, Amy Kunchok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundB-cell depleting therapies are used for MS, autoimmune disorders, renal disease and hematological malignancies but increase infection susceptibility.ObjectiveTo describe two cases of enterovirus meningoencephalitis in patients receiving B-cell depleting therapy and review anti-CD20-associated cases, detailing diagnosis, treatment, and outcomes.MethodsWe present two cases, one with MS and one with minimal change disease, and review 40 additional anti-CD20 cases. Demographic, clinical, laboratory, neuroimaging, treatment, and outcomes were abstracted. Neuro-QoL measures were analyzed.ResultsOur cases were diagnosed by CSF multiplex PCR and treated with IVIg; one also received methylprednisolone and pocapavir. Persistent sequelae included dysarthria, cognitive impairment, nystagmus, pain, and reduced Neuro-QoL scores. Of 42 cases, 34 were associated with rituximab and eight with ocrelizumab. CSF PCR or molecular testing detected enterovirus in 39 cases; three required alternative specimens/sites. IVIg or subcutaneous immunoglobulin (SCIg) was used in 36 cases, steroids in seven, fluoxetine in six, pocapavir in two, pleconaril in two, and ribavirin/favipiravir in one. Outcomes included recovery in 11, sequelae in 19, and death in 12.ConclusionsEnterovirus infection in B-cell depleted patients may cause severe CNS involvement. CSF PCR aids diagnosis, though alternative specimens may be required. Early IVIg and investigational antivirals may be considered.Keywords: B-cell depleting therapy, enterovirus meningoencephalitis, Neuro-QoL, multiple sclerosis, review article

Introduction

B-cell depleting therapies, including rituximab, ocrelizumab, ofatumumab, and related anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies, are widely used to treat multiple sclerosis (MS), autoimmune diseases, renal disease, and hematological malignancies. While effective, complications due to impaired humoral immunity, reduced antigen presentation, and impaired T-cell co-stimulation can impede pathogen recognition and clearance. While upper respiratory and gastrointestinal viral infections are more common, central nervous system (CNS) involvement is associated with higher morbidity, particularly in the setting of weakened humoral immunity.

Non-polio enteroviruses are the leading cause of viral meningitis/encephalitis hospitalizations (∼52%) in the U.S. 1 Progression to encephalitis is uncommon but is more likely to occur in young children and immunocompromised hosts, and is associated with greater mortality and morbidity. 2 Enteroviral meningoencephalitis has been reported with B-cell depleting therapies and is associated with meningitis, meningoencephalitis, myelitis, brainstem encephalitis, cranial neuropathies, and persistent neurological disability.3–30 Rituximab has been the most commonly reported anti-CD20 therapy, but more recent reports have included cases associated with ocrelizumab in people with MS.3,5,10 Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System infection-related adverse events showed strong disproportionality signals with ocrelizumab and enteroviral encephalitis/meningitis despite small absolute case numbers, suggesting that enterovirus-related CNS infections are a noteworthy pharmacovigilance signal in ocrelizumab-associated infections. 31 Another anti-CD20 therapy, obinutuzumab, used for follicular and non-Hodgkin lymphoma and chronic lymphocytic leukemia, has also been associated with multiple cases of enterovirus meningoencephalitis with CNS, liver, and cardiac involvement with outcomes ranging from recovery, residual symptoms, and death.32–35

Other conditions associated with hypogammaglobulinemia that have been associated with enteroviral meningoencephalitis include congenital and X-linked hypogammaglobulinemia, transient hypogammaglobulinemia of infancy, chronic variable immunodeficiency, human immunodeficiency virus, and T-cell depletion.36–45

The clinical spectrum, imaging findings, and long-term outcomes for enterovirus meningoencephalitis are limited. Collectively, published reports have described fever, headache, cognitive impairment, focal symptoms, tremor, dysarthria, cranial impairments, fatigue, gait impairment, and ataxia, as well as one case of limbic syndrome on ocrelizumab.3–5 We highlight diagnostic challenges and sequelae of two cases who developed enterovirus meningoencephalitis during B-cell depleting therapy, one with MS treated with ocrelizumab and another with minimal change disease (MCD) treated with rituximab. We also summarize published cases of anti-CD20 associated enterovirus meningoencephalitis, describing clinical manifestations, diagnostic approaches, MRI findings, treatments, and outcomes. Our cases highlight persistent neurologic and quality-of-life sequelae, the potential role of longitudinal Neuro-QoL assessment, and the use of pocapavir in severe anti-CD20 associated enterovirus CNS infection in a subject with MS.

Methods

Two adults on B-cell depleting therapy who developed enterovirus meningoencephalitis between 2023 and 2024 at our institution were identified through direct clinical care by the treating authors and details were abstracted from the electronic medical record. Enterovirus meningoencephalitis was defined by compatible clinical presentation and positivity on a CSF polymerase chain reaction (PCR) multiplex assay. Demographic, clinical, laboratory, neuroimaging, and treatment data were abstracted from the electronic medical record. Quality of life in neurological disorders (Neuro-QoL) T-scores (mean 50, SD 10) were available with repeated measures pre-/post-encephalitis for the case with MS and only post-encephalitis for the case with MCD. Neuro-QoL domains included functional domains, in which higher scores indicate better function, and symptom domains, in which higher scores indicate greater symptom burden. 46 Scores were analyzed relative to the month of encephalitis (from onset of initial symptoms coded as month 0) using linear regression with η2 and Cohen's f2 quantifying variance attributable to time (Figure 2). Because Neuro-QoL data were available for only two cases and repeated pre-/post-event measures were available for only Case 1, analyses were considered exploratory. For Case 1, a linear regression model was fit to characterize longitudinal change relative to the encephalitis date: Scorei=β0+β1(Monthsfromencephalitis)i+εi . Scorei represents the Neuro-QoL T-score at each timepoint, β0 the intercept (predicted T-score at the encephalitis date, time = 0), β1 the slope coefficient (average monthly change in the domain T-score), (Monthsfromencephalitis)i the corresponding number of months before (negative) or after the encephalitis onset (positive), and εi the residual error for each observation (variability not explained by the model). To quantify the magnitude of the longitudinal association, eta-squared ( η2 ) was calculated for the proportion of variance in domain T-scores attributable to time. Cohen's f2 was derived using the standard transformation f2=η2/(1−η2) to determine the magnitude of the longitudinal change in each domain T-score relative to residual (unexplained) variance. R/RStudio 2026.04.0 build 526 was used for statistical analysis. 47

PubMed was searched for publications containing the following query: (enterovirus*[Title/Abstract] OR enteroviral[Title/Abstract] OR echovirus[Title/Abstract] OR coxsackievirus[Title/Abstract]) AND (encephalitis[Title/Abstract] OR meningoencephalitis[Title/Abstract] OR meningitis[Title/Abstract]) AND (ocrelizumab[Title/Abstract] OR rituximab[Title/Abstract] OR ofatumumab[Title/Abstract] OR ublituximab[Title/Abstract] OR “anti-CD20"[Title/Abstract] OR “B-cell depletion"[Title/Abstract] OR “B cell depletion"[Title/Abstract] OR hypogammaglobulinemia[Title/Abstract]). Reports were included if they described enterovirus-associated meningitis, meningoencephalitis, encephalitis, myelitis, or encephalomyelitis in a patient receiving anti-CD20 therapies that are also used for treatment of MS. Non-enterovirus cases, duplicate reports, and aggregate reviews without extractable patient-level data were excluded.

Two cases and review of published cases

Two White adults in their 30s developed enterovirus meningoencephalitis while receiving B-cell depleting therapies: one with MS on ocrelizumab and one with MCD on rituximab (Supplementary Table 1). Neither had a family history of immunodeficiencies. Symptom onset occurred 49.4 (Case 1) and 8 (Case 2) months after initiating B-cell depleting therapy. CSF enterovirus PCR was positive, and neuroimaging findings ranged from midbrain lesion (Case 1) to cerebellar and leptomeningeal abnormalities (Case 2). Both received IVIg and corticosteroids; Case 1 also received pocapavir. Outcomes included neurocognitive symptoms, binocular diplopia, pain, and dysautonomia.

Forty additional published cases were summarized in Table 1 with respect to clinical symptoms and MRI findings, interventions, and outcomes for all cases; Supplementary Table 2 provides details for each published report. Numeric IgG values were available for 33 cases and IgM values for 18 cases. Median IgG was numerically higher in MS/MOG-AD cases than non-MS/MOG-AD cases, but this did not reach statistical significance (594.5 mg/dL [IQR 492–709.3] vs 431 mg/dL [IQR 360–541]; Mann–Whitney p = 0.051). IgM levels were similar between groups.

Case 1

A 33-year-old man with MS on ocrelizumab for 4 years initially developed fever, cough, myalgia, rhinorrhea, and nasal congestion for 3 days prior to being diagnosed with COVID-19. He was treated with nirmatrelvir/ritonavir (Paxlovid). Three weeks later, he developed headache, fever, neck stiffness, nausea, nystagmus, and diplopia. He had a lumbar puncture notable for lymphocytic pleocytosis and tested positive for enterovirus meningitis on a CSF PCR multiplex for meningitis/encephalitis. Six days after the meningeal symptoms, he became encephalopathic (expressive aphasia; nonsensical speech). MRI brain showed a left cerebral peduncle area of diffusion restriction with later faint enhancement. During hospitalization, he had myoclonus, dysautonomia requiring admission to the intensive care unit, generalized weakness, and transaminitis. He received IVIg 0.4 g/kg/day overlapping with methylprednisolone 1 g/day for 5 days. Complications included methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia, neuropathic pain, poor appetite, and dysphagia requiring a nasogastric tube. The FDA authorized emergency investigational new drug (E-IND) use of pocapavir (ViroDefense Inc.) 1.6 g/day for 14 days during which his communication progressed from gestures to a communication board, followed bya few words, and then simple conversations. Following acute rehabilitation for 3 weeks, he continued to exhibit word-finding difficulties, poor appetite, gastroparesis, anhedonia, and dysautonomia (including convulsive syncope and postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome). He switched to diroximel fumarate. Repeat MRI brain at 7 months showed evolution of the midbrain lesion (Figure 1). Neuro-QoL measures (from 20 months before to 10.2 months after meningoencephalitis) showed significantly worse anxiety ( η2 = 0.59) and upper extremity function ( η2 = 0.66) (Figure 2). Combined IgG and IgM hypogammaglobulinemia was noted around the onset of initial symptoms (Figure 3A); no evidence of peripheral immunosuppression outside of B-cell counts was observed (Figure 3B). Nineteen months after his initial symptoms, he had mild cognitive impairment, word-finding difficulties, and postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome with resolution of his poor appetite, anhedonia, and gastroparesis.

Case 2

A 36-year-old woman with MCD received a year of prednisone followed by a cycle of rituximab (1 g given twice, 2 weeks apart). Three months later, she developed symptoms of viral gastroenteritis, upper respiratory illness, and intermittent fevers and malaise lasting for two months. She then developed vertigo for weeks, which resolved with residual mild fatigue. Eight months after her rituximab cycle, she developed severe vertigo and received a dose of methylprednisolone 1 g IV followed by a prednisone taper for presumed vestibular migraine. MRI brain with contrast was normal. One month of severe vertigo prompted further evaluation; CSF meningitis/encephalitis multiplex PCR was positive for enterovirus, and a 7-Tesla MRI brain found cerebellar hyperintensities and leptomeningeal enhancement of the internal auditory canals (IACs) (Figure 1). Symptoms were unchanged following IVIg for 4 days followed by monthly IVIg. Baclofen and prism glasses improved periodic alternating horizontal (mostly gaze-evoked) nystagmus and esotropia. Repeat MRI brain two months later showed subtle mild leptomeningeal enhancement of the IACs, which completely resolved 4 months later. She received no further treatment for MCD. Post-encephalitis Neuro-QoL scores resembled those seen with Case 1 (Figure 2).

Discussion

Our two cases and review of 40 published cases highlight enterovirus meningoencephalitis as a rare but potentially devastating complication of B-cell depleting therapies, associated with diagnostic challenges, frequent residual neurological deficits and substantial mortality. Across 42 cases, rituximab was implicated in 34 cases and ocrelizumab in 8. CSF PCR or molecular testing detected enterovirus in 39 cases, while three required brain tissue, blood, or respiratory specimens. IVIg or SCIg was used in 36 cases, corticosteroids in 7, fluoxetine in 6, pocapavir in 2, pleconaril in 2, and ribavirin/favipiravir in 1. Outcomes included recovery in 11, residual sequelae in 19, and death in 12.

While our two cases demonstrated a positive CSF enterovirus PCR, negative CSF PCR has been reported in a biopsy-confirmed case and in cases where enterovirus was detectable only in respiratory or intestinal reservoirs; CSF PCR may be positive only transiently early in the disease course.13,23 This highlights the need for broad testing, including metagenomic next-generation sequencing, if clinical suspicion is high. 30

There is no FDA-approved treatment for enteroviral infections. Treatment has typically been with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg), steroids, fluoxetine, and occasionally antivirals which are off-label, adjunctive, investigational, compassionate-use, or empiric approaches. Hypogammaglobulinemia has been associated with the development of enterovirus meningoencephalitis, and low IgG levels have sometimes correlated with worse outcomes.3,4,14,23,32,49 In our MS case, combined IgG and IgM hypogammaglobulinemia was present around symptom onset, while other peripheral immune cell counts were normal, supporting impaired humoral immunity as a risk factor. Intraventricular immunoglobulin has also been used in echoviral encephalitis associated with X-linked hypogammaglobulinemia with an Ommaya reservoir with clinical improvement and CSF viral clearance.50,51 Among cases with reported immunoglobulin levels, IgG and IgM were generally reduced. IgG levels were numerically lower in non-MS/MOG-AD cases than in MS/MOG-AD cases, although this comparison was limited by missing data, small sample size, and publication bias. IVIg may have provided passive immunity to improve viral clearance but its role in management remains unproven. Schilthuizen et al. used IVIg to target IgG > 10 g/L enterovirus-specific antibodies rose, fecal PCR was negative and the subject improved clinically. 49 Palacios et al. found IVIg treatment ineffective in a subject who had fatal Coxsackie B3 meningoencephalitis – suggesting salvage therapy once established disease occurred may be inadequate. 23

The case described by Luciani et al. of Coxsackie B4 demonstrated multiple point mutations, some of which changed encoded amino acids, accrued over the course of an 8-month clinically silent period between a self-limiting meningitis and fulminant myocarditis. 52 A viral 2C-encoding motif suggested fluoxetine could have been effective in this strain based on cell-based assays.52,53

Pocapavir under compassionate use has been used for enterovirus meningoencephalitis in a patient with X-linked agammaglobulinemia and in another patient with rheumatoid arthritis on rituximab.54,55 Pocapavir was used in the first case with MS to our knowledge and temporally was associated with clinical improvement; its independent contribution cannot be determined. Pocapavir had also been used in a patient with rheumatoid arthritis and severe enteroviral meningoencephalitis, with clinical stabilization for 4 months; however, the patient died of pneumonia 8 months later. 54 Another antiviral pleconaril with similar properties has been used in a few cases of enteroviral meningoencephalitis in patients on rituximab, but the drug is no longer manufactured.24,49,56

Overall, early IVIg is biologically plausible and commonly used; investigational antivirals may be considered, although evidence is limited to isolated cases and causality cannot be inferred as they were often used concurrently with other therapies (steroids, fluoxetine), rehabilitation, and supportive care.

Additional immunologic stressors may have contributed to risk of neuroinvasive enteroviral infection in our two cases. Case 1 developed enteroviral meningoencephalitis approximately three weeks after COVID-19; recent SARS-CoV-2 infection may have contributed to susceptibility, but B-cell depletion and hypogammaglobulinemia remained the most evident risk factors. Case 2 received steroids for presumed vestibular migraine before enterovirus infection was diagnosed, which may have contributed to viral persistence or progression, although systemic and vestibular symptoms preceded corticosteroid exposure and rituximab-associated B-cell depletion likely represented the major predisposing factor.

Post-infection disease-modifying therapy in MS is unclear. In our MS case, we elected to de-escalate to diroximel fumarate to provide moderate control of disease activity with a low observed rate of serious infection. 57 In a recently published case series of five subjects with MS who developed enterovirus meningoencephalitis, two patients resumed ocrelizumab as scheduled, one switched to glatiramer acetate, one stopped therapy because of nonactive secondary progressive MS and age, and one was undecided. 3 Subsequent disease-modifying therapy management was not noted in other published MS cases.5,10

MRI findings were heterogeneous and included multifocal or diffuse T2w hyperintensities, infratentorial involvement, supratentorial deep gray matter involvement, myelitis, leptomeningeal enhancement, restricted diffusion, and atrophy or encephalomalacia. Our cases demonstrate a focal midbrain/cerebral peduncle lesion with diffusion restriction and later enhancement in one case and subtle leptomeningeal/internal auditory canal enhancement in the other. Normal MRI has also been reported, particularly early in the disease course, so neuroimaging should not be used to exclude enterovirus CNS infection.

Prior reports generally describe outcomes qualitatively with recovery, residual deficits, or death. Longitudinal Neuro-QoL assessment in our MS case provided a quantitative measure of patient-centered morbidity, demonstrating a substantial long-term impact on anxiety and upper extremity function in a previously high-functioning individual;more than half the variance was attributable to time following encephalitis ( η2 between 0.59 and 0.66). Neuro-QoL in routine neurological care provides a helpful framework to monitor recovery, determine whether therapeutic advancements have a measurable impact on daily living, and capture psychosocial and functional outcomes missed with conventional neurological examination.

Mortality was substantial, with death in 12 of 42 cases (29%). In our review, deaths occurred in rituximab-associated cases and were predominantly among patients with hematologic malignancy or other systemic autoimmune/hematologic conditions rather than MS. When reported, the time from symptom onset to death ranged from several days to approximately 1 year, with causes including progressive meningoencephalitis, severe neurologic decline, systemic complications such as pneumonia, stroke after biopsy, and other systemic enteroviral complications including myocarditis.6,12–14,18,19,21–23,52,54 This mortality is higher than that reported for treated herpes simplex virus (HSV) encephalitis in general populations but falls within the severe range described in immunocompromised hosts, including HSV encephalitis in immunocompromised hosts, varicella zoster virus following solid organ transplant, and viral encephalitis after hematopoietic cell transplantation.58–62

Limitations of our cases include referral bias, absence of B-cell counts at presentation, variable follow-up, and lack of matched B-cell replete controls. The literature review limitations include publication bias, inconsistent reporting of MRI findings, variable treatment timing with stage of illness and follow-up duration.

New or rapidly evolving headaches, focal deficits, or cognitive changes in B-cell depleted patients should prompt evaluation for infectious etiologies. Distinguishing between infection, autoimmune disease activity, and pseudorelapses requires correlation with clinical course, imaging, and CSF/serum testing. Early recognition is essential because IVIg, reduction or de-escalation of immunosuppression, and investigational antivirals may be considered, although optimal therapy remains undefined.
