Airway tracers and biomarkers for aspiration detection and VAP prediction in ICU patients with tracheal tubes: a systematic review and diagnostic accuracy meta-analysis
Authors: Suwen Li, Chenxin Ye, Luying Chen, Yuting Wu, Gendi Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVentilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) remains an important complication in mechanically ventilated patients in intensive care units (ICUs). Early subglottic microleakage may precede overt infection but remains difficult to detect at the bedside. Airway tracers and biochemical biomarkers have been proposed as bedside tools for detecting upstream microleakage and predicting downstream VAP, but their diagnostic value remains uncertain because studies differ in sampling matrices, thresholds, reference standards, and endpoints.MethodsSix databases were systematically searched from inception to 1 March 2026. Studies evaluating airway tracers or biomarkers in critically ill adults receiving invasive mechanical ventilation or with artificial airways were included under a dual-endpoint framework, comprising upstream microleakage or aspiration-related event detection and downstream VAP prediction. Study data were extracted, and risk of bias was assessed using the QUADAS-2 tool. For markers with sufficient clinical and methodological comparability, quantitative analyses included bivariate random-effects pooling, variance decomposition, stepwise sensitivity analyses, and Bayes’ theorem–based post-test probability projection.ResultsSixteen studies were included. Blue-dye–based visual methods were summarized descriptively because the available studies represented distinct aspiration paradigms and did not meet the criteria for quantitative pooling. FEES-validated tracheostomy studies reported relatively high sensitivity for swallowing-related transglottic aspiration, whereas enteral-tracer or reflux-aspiration monitoring studies were limited by surrogate reference standards and/or low visual detectability for continuous, low-volume microleakage. One mechanically ventilated enteral-feeding cohort was retained as narrative evidence only because it did not provide a direct 2 × 2 diagnostic accuracy comparison of visual blue-dye detection against the microsphere reference standard. For VAP prediction, airway α-amylase showed a pooled sensitivity of 78.1%, specificity of 70.3%, and an area under the curve of 0.811.ConclusionsCurrent airway tracers and biomarkers do not appear suitable as standalone diagnostic triggers for VAP. However, airway α-amylase may have potential as a negative-predictive screening tool. Future research should standardize volumetric airway sampling and prospectively validate clinically actionable rule-out algorithms to support early antimicrobial de-escalation.

Introduction

Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) remains a common and clinically important healthcare-associated infection in mechanically ventilated patients in intensive care units (ICU). Despite preventive bundles, it has been reported in approximately 10%–25% of patients receiving mechanical ventilation for more than 48 h [1], may prolong mechanical ventilation by 5–7 days and ICU stay by up to 9 days, and is associated with substantial additional costs [2]. VAP is also associated with increased mortality, with attributable mortality ranging from 13% to 30%, and higher rates reported in multidrug-resistant infections [3].

Timely, targeted antimicrobial therapy is therefore important for improving outcomes. However, clinicians face a difficult trade-off: delayed treatment pending definitive confirmation may increase mortality, whereas empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic use based on early suspicion may lead to unnecessary exposure, antimicrobial resistance, and additional costs [4,5]. Inappropriate broad-spectrum use has been reported to increase per-patient hospital costs by up to $3,000 [5]. Current diagnostic strategies remain limited. Conventional clinical criteria are delayed indicators, and a meta-analysis using histopathology as the reference standard showed that a Clinical Pulmonary Infection Score (CPIS) greater than 6 provides only moderate diagnostic performance [6]. These criteria also have limited ability to distinguish bacterial VAP from sterile inflammatory conditions such as chemical pneumonitis secondary to gastric aspiration [7]. As a result, clinicians remain vulnerable to both overtreatment and delayed pathogen-directed therapy [8].

In response to these limitations, numerous airway biomarkers have been investigated for early VAP prediction. Major US and European guidelines suggest that diagnostic biomarkers should ideally achieve at least 90% sensitivity and specificity [3] to reduce missed diagnoses, avoid unnecessary investigations, and limit the costs associated with inaccurate testing [9]. However, a recent systematic review of 29 lower-airway biomarkers [10] reported substantial diagnostic heterogeneity and concluded that no single candidate appears sufficiently reliable for routine clinical use. Importantly, that review primarily evaluated downstream host immune-response markers that generally reflect established or evolving infection rather than upstream exposure events. Although α-amylase and pepsin were briefly mentioned as potential surrogates of early subglottic microleakage, no systematic review has comprehensively synthesized available microleakage-related tracers and airway biomarkers or their predictive value for subsequent VAP. One possible source of inconsistency is the conflation of delayed immune responses with early markers of physical exposure to colonized secretions.

In mechanically ventilated patients, early microbial exposure is thought to be closely related to silent subglottic microleakage. Standard endotracheal cuffs may form longitudinal folds that act as capillary channels, allowing the continuous seepage of colonized oropharyngeal secretions [11,12]. Microbiome studies are consistent with this mechanism, describing rapid post-intubation associations between the upper and lower airways [13,14]. Within the lung microbiome island model [15], this microleakage can be conceptualized as continuous microbial immigration, whereas clinical VAP may reflect downstream failure of host elimination [16]. Accordingly, capturing this early exposure may be relevant for early intervention.

However, direct detection of occult microleakage remains methodologically challenging. Mechanically ventilated patients are at particularly high risk for microleakage, with reported incidence rates ranging from 60% to 88% [11], owing to artificial airways, continuous sedation, and supine positioning. Fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES) is useful for assessing swallowing-related penetration or aspiration across the vocal cords in extubated or tracheostomized patients. However, it does not directly measure passive cuff-related subglottic microleakage during ongoing invasive mechanical ventilation and is not designed for continuous bedside monitoring in sedated, intubated patients. Because cuff leakage is continuous rather than discrete, intermittent visual assessment cannot reliably capture cumulative exposure [17]. This creates a reference-standard challenge in microleakage research: in the absence of a reliable, non-invasive bedside standard for continuous validation, assessment of new biomarkers remains constrained [11]. As a result, many studies have relied on surrogate markers, particularly airway pepsin and α-amylase, and have implicitly treated their detection as evidence of subglottic microleakage [18,19]. However, without an independent standard to confirm silent leakage events, the validity of this approach remains uncertain.

Driven by the need to move beyond delayed VAP criteria and constrained by the absence of a universally accepted reference standard, the past two decades have seen the development of diverse bedside tools and airway biomarkers for upstream microleakage detection. These include non-invasive acoustic methods [20] and physical tracer techniques such as the modified blue-dye test (MBDT) or other blue-dye–based visual methods. However, while visual dyes are practical, their macroscopic detection limits may fail to capture trace, low-volume subglottic microleakage. Consequently, alternative biochemical and cytological markers such as pH monitoring, glucose oxidase, and the Lipid-Laden Macrophage Index [21,22], as well as molecular assays targeting leaked material [23], have increasingly been investigated in diagnostic research.

The objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to comprehensively evaluate the diagnostic and predictive accuracy of available bedside tracers and airway biomarkers for detecting subglottic microleakage, microaspiration, or related aspiration events and for predicting subsequent VAP in critically ill adults with invasive mechanical ventilation or artificial airways.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses of Diagnostic Test Accuracy Studies (PRISMA-DTA) guidelines [24]. The protocol was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF; https://osf.io/yaf6p/).

Search strategy and selection criteria

We searched MEDLINE, EMBASE, Web of Science, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, CINAHL, and ClinicalTrials.gov from inception to 1 March 2026, without language or publication date restrictions. Full search strategies for each database are provided in Supplementary Appendix 1. Reference lists of included studies and relevant systematic reviews were also screened for additional records.

Eligible studies enrolled adults aged ≥18 years in critical care settings, including ICUs and specialized long-term acute care hospitals (LTACHs), who were receiving invasive mechanical ventilation or had artificial airways, including endotracheal intubation or tracheostomy. We included studies evaluating endogenous biomarkers or exogenous tracers detected in airway or lower respiratory tract specimens when they had a plausible role in detecting microaspiration (MA), aspiration-related events, or predicting VAP, including early aspiration pneumonia where relevant.

For the primary question of cuff-related or enteral microleakage during ongoing invasive mechanical ventilation, studies evaluating enteral-tracer monitoring, reflux-aspiration monitoring, or airway biomarkers in mechanically ventilated patients were considered directly applicable. Tracheostomy studies using MBDT and FEES to evaluate swallowing-related transglottic aspiration or dysphagia were retained as contextual evidence for blue-dye–based visual methods, but were not considered direct evidence for passive cuff-related subglottic microleakage around an inflated cuff.

For MA or aspiration-related events, acceptable reference standards included direct optical or imaging visualization, validated isotope tracing, or indirect surrogate markers. These standards were retained to maximize data capture and allow assessment of incorporation bias [25]. For VAP and early aspiration pneumonia, acceptable reference standards included guideline-endorsed criteria from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the American Thoracic Society, and the Infectious Diseases Society of America; the CPIS, with or without quantitative lower respiratory tract culture; or investigator-defined clinical diagnoses. Eligible designs included prospective or retrospective DTA studies and observational prognostic cohort studies, provided sufficient data were available to construct a 2 × 2 table or to report sensitivity, specificity, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve with 95% confidence intervals. Studies without a direct 2 × 2 diagnostic accuracy comparison were retained only for narrative synthesis when they provided clinically relevant evidence on tracer detectability.

We excluded studies involving only patients younger than 18 years without extractable adult subgroup data, studies conducted exclusively in general wards or outpatient settings, and case-control DTA studies because they may overestimate diagnostic performance. Reviews, case reports, editorials, letters, duplicate publications, and conference abstracts without full-text data were also excluded.

Study selection, data extraction, and risk-of-bias assessment

Two independent reviewers screened records after deduplication. Titles and abstracts were screened first, followed by independent full-text assessment. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or by consultation with a third senior reviewer. Data extraction and risk-of-bias assessment were performed independently by two reviewers using standardized forms based on the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS-2) tool [26].

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

For each study with sufficient diagnostic accuracy data, 2 × 2 contingency tables were constructed to calculate sensitivity, specificity, and corresponding 95% confidence intervals. To avoid analytical cross-contamination, we used two separate conceptual pipelines: one for upstream tracers or biomarkers detecting MA or aspiration-related events and another for downstream biochemical markers predicting incident VAP. Analyses were performed in R4.5.1 using the mada and meta packages, and all outputs were independently cross-checked by two analysts against the prespecified protocol.

Because biomarkers, aspiration mechanisms, reference standards, and clinical targets differed substantially across studies, we used a staged synthesis approach. All eligible studies were included in the qualitative synthesis, but quantitative pooling was restricted to markers with sufficient homogeneity and at least three comparable cohorts. Studies with major data gaps, non-comparable target conditions, or absence of a direct 2 × 2 diagnostic accuracy comparison were not pooled.

Based on clinical applicability, blue-dye–based studies were summarized using structured descriptive synthesis, given that they represented distinct aspiration paradigms, including FEES-validated swallowing assessment in tracheostomized patients and enteral-tracer or reflux-aspiration monitoring during invasive mechanical ventilation. Study-level sensitivity and specificity estimates were displayed descriptively when extractable, but no pooled sensitivity, pooled specificity, SROC curve, or publication-bias test was calculated for MBDT.

A bivariate random-effects model was used for airway α-amylase in VAP prediction, for which sufficient comparable cohorts were available. Pooled sensitivity and specificity with 95% confidence intervals were estimated [27]. A continuity correction of 0.5 was applied to zero-cell counts [28], and summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curves were generated, with area under the curve (AUC) calculated. Heterogeneity was assessed after pooling. Threshold effects were examined by the correlation between logit sensitivity and logit false-positive rate, and the Zhou-Dendukuri algorithm was used to separate threshold-related from non-threshold heterogeneity [29]. Because few studies were available, structured narrative synthesis [30] was also used to explore heterogeneity across prespecified QUADAS-2 domains. For airway α-amylase, sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding cohorts with marked clinical heterogeneity or high risk of incorporation bias.

Finally, to assess potential translational utility, we modeled negative-predictive screening for airway α-amylase. Using pooled sensitivity and specificity, we calculated the negative likelihood ratio (NLR) and applied Bayes’ theorem to estimate the post-test probability of VAP after a negative result across baseline prevalences ranging from 1% to 50% [30]. Publication bias and small-study effects were assessed using Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test based on the diagnostic odds ratio [31], with P < 0.10 considered significant, only for quantitatively pooled diagnostic meta-analyses.

Results

General description of included studies

The structured database search identified 30,328 records, of which 16 independent studies met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Eight studies evaluated upstream MA or aspiration-related events using the modified blue-dye test (MBDT) or blue-dye–based visual methods, airway α-amylase, glucose oxidase test strips, and exogenous plant DNA [21,[32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37],45]. Modified Blue-Dye Test and Blue-Dye–based visual methods

Six prospective studies evaluated blue-dye–based visual methods for detecting aspiration-related events [21,[32], [33], [34], [35],37]. Because these studies encompassed mechanistically distinct aspiration paradigms, including discrete swallowing evaluation and continuous enteral or reflux-aspiration monitoring, and used heterogeneous reference standards, the data were not suitable for quantitative pooling. Consequently, no pooled sensitivity, pooled specificity, SROC curve, or publication-bias test was calculated for MBDT. Three studies evaluated MBDT in tracheostomized patients using FEES as the reference standard [[32], [33], [34]]. These cohorts primarily assessed swallowing-related transglottic aspiration or dysphagia after oral or pharyngeal administration of dyed material, rather than passive cuff-related subglottic microleakage during ongoing invasive mechanical ventilation. Their applicability to cuff-related microleakage was limited because testing involved cuff deflation in some studies and post-weaning or mixed ventilator status in others. In this specific context, reported patient-level sensitivity was relatively high, ranging from 79.0% to 100%, with specificity ranging from 40.0% to 100% (Fig. 2). These findings therefore serve as contextual evidence for MBDT in macroscopic swallowing dysfunction, rather than primary evidence for subglottic microleakage.

Two studies evaluated blue-dye–based methods in an enteral-tracer or gastric reflux–aspiration monitoring context during invasive mechanical ventilation [21,35]. One study compared visual blue-dye inspection with glucose oxidase testing in tracheally intubated adults receiving enteral feeding and reported low patient-level sensitivity but high specificity [21]. Another study evaluated methylene blue added to enteral nutrition in mechanically ventilated patients, using tracheal pepsin detection as the comparator, and reported high apparent sensitivity and specificity [35]. However, both studies relied on indirect surrogate reference standards, limiting their applicability as definitive diagnostic accuracy evidence for cuff-related subglottic microleakage.

One mechanically ventilated enteral-feeding cohort evaluated continuous enteral tracer monitoring using both blue food coloring and yellow microscopic beads [37]. Aspiration or regurgitation events were defined by fluorometric detection of microscopic beads, whereas blue-dye detection was based on gross visual inspection. Because the study did not provide a direct 2 × 2 comparison between visual blue-dye detection and the microsphere reference standard, it was not included in the study-level diagnostic accuracy display or quantitative synthesis. Blue food coloring was visually detected in only 3 of 1,118 tracheal or oropharyngeal samples; this finding was therefore retained as narrative evidence regarding the limited visual detectability of dye-based tracers during continuous, diluted enteral monitoring in mechanically ventilated patients.

Overall, the available evidence suggests that visual dye methods may be more applicable to discrete swallowing-related macroaspiration after oral bolus administration than to continuous, trace cuff-related or enteral microleakage during invasive mechanical ventilation. In the latter setting, visual detection appears particularly vulnerable to tracer dilution, low aspirated volume, intermittent suctioning, and reference-standard heterogeneity.

Airway α-amylase

Airway α-amylase, representing total enzymatic activity without salivary or pancreatic isoform differentiation, was evaluated for both upstream MA detection and downstream VAP prediction. For upstream MA, one retrospective study involving 109 patients used tracheal pepsin >200 ng/mL as the reference standard [36]. Based on patient mean concentrations, the unadjusted AUC was 0.72, with a cut-off of 1685 U/L, sensitivity of 87.0%, and specificity of 29.0%. After statistical correction for repeated measurements across serially diluted samples using N-acetylcysteine, the AUC decreased to 0.566. This study was rated at high risk of bias in the patient selection and reference standard domains.

For downstream VAP prediction, six independent studies, including three prospective and three retrospective cohorts, representing 971 patients and 309 VAP cases, were included. The bivariate random-effects model yielded a pooled sensitivity of 78.1% (95% CI: 70.1%–84.4%) and specificity of 70.3% (95% CI: 59.2%–79.4%). The corresponding SROC curve showed an AUC of 0.811 (Fig. 3), and study-level sensitivity and specificity estimates are shown in the forest plots (Fig. 4). Threshold-related heterogeneity was minimal, whereas variance decomposition indicated substantial non-threshold between-study heterogeneity (sensitivity τ2 = 0.144; false-positive rate τ2 = 0.309), which appeared to be related to three factors: sample dilution protocol, patient phenotype, and reference standard stringency.Fig. 3SROC curve for airway α-amylase in predicting VAP.Fig. 3Fig. 4α-amylase sensitivity/specificity forest plots.Fig. 4

Reported diagnostic cut-offs varied widely, ranging from 8.1 to 4681.5 U/L, and generally decreased as the volume of normal saline used for sample dilution increased. Studies evaluating undiluted tracheal aspirates reported cut-offs above 4300 U/L, specifically 4321.5 U/L [38] and 4681.5 U/L [40]. Standardized lavage samples yielded lower thresholds: >200 U/L with 10 mL saline instillation, 163 U/L with 20 mL saline in mini-bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), ≥125 U/L with 60 mL saline in BAL fluid, and 8.1 U/L with 40 mL saline in mini-BAL. Despite variable specificity, negative or low α-amylase results under study-specific thresholds were associated with high negative predictive values, ranging from 85% for standalone BAL [42] to 100% when combined with bedside thoracic ultrasound [41].

Variation in diagnostic performance was also associated with patient phenotype and sampling timeline. Specificity was lower in populations characterized by acute MA events, such as out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, where specificity was 61.0% [19], or early generalized bacterial pneumonia, where specificity decreased to 55.0% [42]. In addition, continuous serial sampling over two weeks suggested that the cumulative number of α-amylase exceedances was positively associated with VAP incidence [40].

A stepwise sensitivity analysis assessed the influence of these methodological differences. Excluding cohorts with acute MA or early generalized pneumonia yielded a core classic VAP cohort of four studies, which showed a pooled sensitivity of 80.7%, specificity of 76.8%, and AUC of 0.856. This suggests that airway α-amylase may perform more favorably in the classic VAP population commonly encountered in clinical practice. Further restricting the analysis to the single cohort using strict quantitative microbiological cultures yielded a sensitivity of 73.0% and specificity of 68.6% [39]. Notably, a combined detection model incorporating α-amylase, pepsin, and the lipid-laden macrophage index yielded an AUC of 0.905 [38]. Overall, although diagnostic thresholds for airway α-amylase were strongly influenced by sample dilution, predictive accuracy also appeared to vary according to patient phenotype and reference standard stringency.

To assess the translational utility of airway α-amylase, Bayes’ theorem–based post-test probability projection was applied to estimate the post-test probability of VAP after a negative test result across a continuous range of baseline prevalences (Fig. 5). Based on the pooled sensitivity (78.1%) and specificity (70.3%) derived from the α-amylase subset, the negative likelihood ratio (NLR) was 0.31 (95% CI: 0.20–0.49). This NLR was used to project post-test probabilities via Bayes’ theorem. To reflect real-world clinical settings, outcome projections were anchored at baseline VAP prevalences of 15% and 20%, which approximate the disease incidence reported in modern preventive-bundle-adherent ICUs and standard critical care settings, respectively. Assuming a 20% pre-test probability, a negative airway α-amylase test yielded a post-test probability of 7.2%, corresponding to an NPV of 92.8% at this prevalence. At a 15% pre-test probability, the post-test probability was 5.2%, corresponding to an NPV of 94.8% at this prevalence. Pepsin

Airway pepsin was evaluated exclusively for downstream prediction of VAP, as no eligible independent cohorts assessing direct upstream MA met the inclusion criteria. Two independent prospective studies evaluated airway pepsin. One study involving 262 elderly mechanically ventilated patients assessed tracheal aspirates processed with equal-volume NAC without saline lavage [38]. Using clinical VAP criteria based on new or progressive radiographic abnormalities plus clinical signs, with biomarker results blinded to the diagnostic assessment, this cohort identified an optimal cut-off of 126.61 ng/mL, with a sensitivity of 76.3%, specificity of 86.0%, and AUC of 0.802. In contrast, a second study evaluating bronchial secretions in 68 general critically ill patients used clinical, radiological, and laboratory diagnostic criteria, including quantitative culture thresholds where applicable [44]. This cohort reported a cut-off of ≥4.87 ng/mL; the extracted diagnostic classification yielded a sensitivity of 60.0%, specificity of 52.1%, and no reported AUC. Methodological assessment suggested a high risk of index test bias in both studies, as diagnostic cut-offs were derived post hoc through dataset-specific optimization rather than prespecified thresholds.

Accordingly, airway pepsin performance appeared to depend on sampling depth and reference-standard definition, and no pooled meta-analysis was performed because of the limited number of studies.

Glucose oxidase test strip

Evidence on glucose oxidase test strips was limited and methodologically indirect. In one enteral-feeding aspiration study, glucose oxidase testing served as the objective comparator against which blue-dye visualization was evaluated, rather than as an index test validated against an independent reference standard [21]. An extracted patient-level estimate also indicated low sensitivity of blue-dye visualization (20.0%). In the sample-level analysis reported by the original study, its relative sensitivity and specificity compared with glucose oxidase testing were 13.0% and 97.0%, respectively, in all experimental-group patients [21]. A second prospective study evaluated twice-daily undiluted tracheal aspirate glucose testing in 24 mechanically ventilated patients [43]. Hospital-acquired pneumonia developed in 8.3% of patients with persistently negative airway glucose compared with 75.0% of patients with any positive reading, yielding an overall sensitivity of 50.0% and specificity of 100%.

Methodological assessment suggested important risk-of-bias and applicability concerns. The available studies reported substantial matrix interference, requiring exclusion of bloody tracheal aspirates to reduce the risk of false-positive results from circulating blood glucose. In addition, applicability was restricted to patients actively receiving glucose-containing enteral nutrition. Accordingly, glucose oxidase test strips appear to have limited clinical utility because of limited and indirect validation evidence, modest sensitivity in pneumonia prediction, matrix interference, and restrictive applicability.

Exogenous Plant DNA

One independent study met the inclusion criteria by retrospectively evaluating plant DNA detection in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid among a restricted cohort of 106 ICU patients with confirmed VAP [45]. Using clinically documented MA as the reference standard, plant DNA was detected in only 6.0% of cases. The diagnostic distribution included four true positives and two false positives among DNA-positive patients, along with three false negatives and 97 true negatives among DNA-negative patients. This corresponds to a sensitivity of 57.1% and specificity of 98.0%.

Methodological assessment suggested a high risk of bias in the patient selection and reference standard domains. The study enrolled only confirmed VAP cases rather than a prospective screening cohort, and relied on retrospectively inferred clinical history rather than objective instrumental validation to define aspiration status. Accordingly, exogenous plant DNA testing appears to have limited clinical utility for routine aspiration screening because of low sensitivity and a restrictive study design.

Publication bias

For airway α-amylase, Deeks’ test did not indicate significant funnel plot asymmetry, with a P value of 0.218. Given the limited number of studies and substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity, the observed funnel plot dispersion should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect variation in sampling matrices, reference standard criteria, and dilution protocols, in addition to or instead of conventional publication bias.

Discussion

In mechanically ventilated patients, VAP pathogenesis is thought to often involve silent subglottic microleakage, rather than being explained solely by macroscopic aspiration [11,17,46]. Unlike prior reviews focused on established downstream VAP [10], we adopted a dual-endpoint framework to evaluate physical tracers for upstream microleakage detection and biochemical markers for downstream VAP prediction. Consistent with ATS/IDSA principles, biomarkers would ideally achieve >90% sensitivity and specificity to support safe antibiotic initiation. However, substantial methodological heterogeneity, unstandardized sampling, and imperfect reference standards meant that no standalone marker could be confidently shown to meet this threshold, which limits their use as affirmative diagnostic triggers. In this context, our synthesis suggests a different and potentially underrecognized clinical role: these markers may be more useful as negative-predictive tools to contribute to risk stratification.

Although an inflated cuff may reduce large-volume MA, it may still permit pooled subglottic secretions to leak gradually into the lower respiratory tract along longitudinal folds [11,17,47]. This continuous process is not well captured by intermittent visual methods such as MBDT or other blue-dye–based visual approaches. In the descriptive synthesis, blue-dye evidence was separated according to clinical context. FEES-validated tracheostomy studies assessed discrete swallowing-related transglottic aspiration after oral or pharyngeal dye administration, whereas enteral-tracer or reflux-aspiration studies evaluated continuous, low-volume leakage during invasive mechanical ventilation. This distinction helps explain why visual dye methods appeared more informative in macroscopic swallowing-related aspiration but showed more limited applicability and detectability for continuous diluted enteral or cuff-related microleakage. The mechanically ventilated enteral-feeding microsphere cohort [37] further illustrates this detectability limitation, because visual blue-dye detection was rare despite repeated tracheal and oropharyngeal sampling.

This divergence suggests an important limitation of visual dye detectability rather than a formal pooled diagnostic finding. In the mechanically ventilated enteral-feeding microsphere cohort, microscopic beads detected by fluorometry were used to define aspiration or regurgitation events, whereas blue food coloring was assessed only by gross visual inspection [37]. The rare visual detection of blue dye despite repeated tracheal and oropharyngeal sampling supports the concern that highly diluted enteral tracers may fall below the threshold of naked-eye detection. Conversely, studies reporting very high MBDT sensitivity generally evaluated larger oral or pharyngeal boluses during FEES-validated swallowing assessment, a clinical paradigm different from continuous cuff-related or enteral microleakage [[32], [33], [34]]. Accordingly, the available blue-dye evidence is better interpreted as descriptive evidence of context-dependent detectability rather than as a pooled diagnostic accuracy estimate. In addition, historical FDA safety warnings regarding systemic toxicity associated with enteral dyes further caution against routine continuous dye-based visual screening in the ICU.

Beyond visual tracers, upstream MA detection with biochemical markers is also constrained by pathophysiological and methodological limitations. A key issue is reliance on imperfect surrogate reference standards. For example, validating airway α-amylase against tracheal pepsin may yield relatively high sensitivity but very low specificity [36], in part because the reference standard itself is biologically limited. Pepsin is particularly challenging in the ICU for two main reasons. First, stressed pulmonary tissue may express pepsinogen C endogenously, which commercial assays may not reliably distinguish from gastric-derived pepsin A, thereby increasing the risk of false-positive results [48,49]. Second, pepsin requires acidic conditions (pH < 4.0) for stability [50]. Widespread prophylactic use of PPIs and continuous enteral nutrition increases gastric pH, while refluxed pepsin may rapidly denature after entering the neutral airway [18,50]. As a result, using pepsin to validate salivary markers may introduce substantial misclassification bias. Moreover, when pepsin is used as a direct VAP predictor, optimal cut-offs vary markedly with sampling depth and dilution (proximal trachea versus deep bronchi) [38,44]. Thus, pepsin may have limited suitability as a standalone marker of gastric aspiration or as a reference standard for validating salivary markers in the ICU.

Other enteral-derived markers face similar constraints. Evidence on glucose oxidase testing was limited and methodologically indirect; available studies suggested modest sensitivity in pneumonia prediction and substantial matrix interference. Even minor mucosal bleeding during routine suctioning may introduce circulating blood glucose and contribute to false-positive results, whereas highly diluted enteral formulas may fall below the strip’s visual detection threshold [21,43]. Given these biological and rheological constraints, the ability of gastric markers to capture upstream events remains uncertain. Molecular tracing is also consistent with this interpretation: in one targeted diagnostic study, exogenous plant DNA was detected in only 6% of confirmed VAP cases, and the sequences were attributed to inhalation of contaminated oral debris rather than gastric reflux [45]. This low detection rate does not strongly support a predominant role for macroscopic gastric aspiration in that cohort and is compatible with the view that VAP may be substantially influenced by occult, continuous microleakage of colonized endogenous oropharyngeal secretions. From a pathophysiological perspective, the inflated cuff may limit large-volume regurgitation while allowing pooled saliva to pass gradually through longitudinal folds into the lower airways [11,17].

This paradigm is consistent with several clinical observations. Preventive strategies targeting the upper airway, including routine subglottic secretion drainage and selective oropharyngeal decontamination, have been associated with reduced VAP incidence, providing in vivo support for the upper airway as a major pathogenic reservoir [9,51]. In addition, cuff modifications designed to reduce microchannel leakage can measurably decrease subglottic leakage [47]. Clinical microbiome studies also show microbiological concordance or association between dysbiotic oral biofilms and tracheal pathogens in VAP [52]. In parallel, longitudinal studies of oropharyngeal, tracheal, and lower-respiratory microbiota have described time-dependent microbial shifts during mechanical ventilation and critical illness, supporting biological plausibility for upper-airway-derived microbial immigration into the lower airway [13,53,54].

Taken together, these findings support greater attention to markers of oropharyngeal microleakage. Because of its predominantly salivary origin, airway α-amylase appears to be a biologically plausible candidate marker of oropharyngeal secretion entry. This biological interpretation should, however, be separated from its use as a quantitative predictive test. The presence of α-amylase in airway secretions indicates exposure of the lower airway to oral secretions, but does not by itself define the timing, magnitude, or clinical consequence of aspiration. Serial studies have shown that α-amylase is frequently detectable in tracheal specimens of mechanically ventilated patients, supporting the concept that MA is often continuous or recurrent rather than a single discrete event [55]. At the same time, the exact pulmonary residence time and washout kinetics of α-amylase after an isolated aspiration event remain insufficiently defined [56]. Therefore, a single elevated airway α-amylase value may reflect recent aspiration, persistent residual signal, or ongoing repeated MA. In contrast, quantitative cut-offs are better viewed as protocol-specific risk-stratification thresholds, influenced by sampling site, dilution volume, assay procedure, use of single versus serial measurements, and the selected reference standard. This distinction also separates α-amylase from pepsin: pepsin mainly reflects gastric-content reflux or aspiration, whereas α-amylase more directly reflects oropharyngeal secretion entry. These mechanistic and methodological distinctions help explain why a biologically plausible exposure marker has not yet translated into consistently reliable VAP prediction across heterogeneous studies.

Although airway α-amylase showed moderate predictive accuracy, variance decomposition using the Zhou and Dendukuri algorithm indicated that threshold effects contributed minimally to the observed heterogeneity. This pattern of non-threshold variability may help explain several statistical features reported in prior meta-analyses. For example, an earlier systematic review of airway α-amylase reported relatively uniform pooled sensitivity but extremely high heterogeneity in the diagnostic odds ratio [4]. This discrepancy may reflect differences in how data were stratified. When data from biochemically distinct sampling matrices are pooled without stratification, anatomical dilution effects can produce substantial variation in diagnostic cut-offs. In addition, earlier syntheses combined cohorts evaluating classic VAP with ventilator-associated tracheobronchitis [4,57]. By applying strict classic VAP criteria and separating matrix-level differences, our review helps clarify this pattern and highlights three potential sources of methodological heterogeneity. First, patient phenotype appears to influence diagnostic estimates. Acute MA cohorts, including severe neurocritical trauma and out-of-hospital cardiac arrest [19,41], largely reflect large-volume or peri-intubation aspiration events and therefore differ from the continuous microleakage typical of ICU VAP pathogenesis. Second, the use of composite clinical reference standards introduces substantial misclassification. Validation against CPIS often yields higher specificity [7], whereas stricter quantitative microbiological thresholds reduce specificity [39]. Within a strict DTA framework, elevated α-amylase in the absence of overt pneumonia would be classified as a false positive. However, under the lung microbiome Island Model [15], these markers may reflect true upstream microbial immigration. Rather than indicating an immediate need for antibiotics, such signals may identify a higher-risk phenotype that could benefit from intensified preventive measures [11,17]. Third, unstandardized sampling matrices directly affect diagnostic cut-offs. Undiluted tracheal aspirates produce very high thresholds above 4,000 U/L [38,40], whereas standardized saline lavage yields much lower thresholds clustered between 125 U/L and 163 U/L [39,42]. In addition, severe systemic inflammation and alveolar-capillary barrier disruption in ARDS may allow serum pancreatic amylase (p-type) to enter the alveolar space, creating a biochemical signal that may be difficult to distinguish from true salivary MA (s-type). The clinical implications are meaningful. Even after restricting the sensitivity analysis to a core classic VAP cohort and applying the strictest microbiological standards, standalone airway α-amylase remained below the 90% sensitivity/specificity threshold considered desirable for affirmative antibiotic initiation. This suggests a narrower clinical role and shifts attention toward combinatorial models or negative-predictive screening. Although multi-marker panels combining α-amylase with gastric and cytological aspiration markers may capture complementary dimensions of aspirated material and host response [38], the most immediate clinical application may be in helping to exclude VAP.

Notably, the most promising role for airway α-amylase may be negative-predictive screening. Individual cohorts reported standalone NPV values of 85% [42] and up to 100% when combined with bedside thoracic ultrasound [41], but such metrics vary with baseline prevalence. To address this limitation and improve translational relevance, we applied Bayes’ theorem–based post-test probability projection to the pooled estimates. As shown in Fig. 5, in a representative ICU with 20% baseline VAP prevalence, a negative α-amylase test yielded a calculated NPV of 92.8%, leaving a residual risk of 7.2%. At 15% baseline prevalence, the NPV increased to 94.8%, corresponding to a residual risk of 5.2%.

For clinical interpretation of these projections, it is necessary to define the maximum acceptable residual risk of active VAP when considering antibiotic withdrawal. In this framework, the threshold does not reflect prevalence or mortality alone, but rather the level of residual infection risk at which the benefit of stopping antibiotics may outweigh the harms of continuing them, including acute kidney injury, Clostridioides difficile infection, and selection of multidrug-resistant pathogens [59]. We therefore modeled three illustrative residual-risk thresholds (Fig. 5): a primary 7% benchmark derived from an estimated VAP attributable mortality of about 20% and an acceptable additional mortality risk of 1.5%, consistent with ERS/ESICM de-escalation principles [9]; a stricter 5% benchmark reflecting a more conservative rule-out scenario [3]; and a more permissive 10% threshold for settings with a higher multidrug-resistant pathogen burden.

Accordingly, acceptable thresholds may need to be individualized according to immune status, comorbidity burden, and local resistance ecology. As shown in our Bayes’ theorem–based projection, a negative α-amylase test produced a residual VAP risk of 5.2% at 15% baseline prevalence, which was close to the stricter illustrative 5% benchmark, and 7.2% at 20% baseline prevalence, which closely approximated the illustrative 7% benchmark informed by ERS/ESICM de-escalation principles. Residual risk remained below the illustrative 7% benchmark up to a baseline prevalence of 19%. This may be clinically relevant because one of the main challenges in VAP management is safely discontinuing empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics once they have been started. The 2016 ATS/IDSA guidelines already support discontinuation based on negative invasive quantitative cultures [3], but this approach is invasive and may delay decisions by 48–72 h. Airway α-amylase may therefore serve as a candidate non-invasive, real-time rule-out adjunct that could support earlier de-escalation by identifying a low-risk phenotype with a lower probability of clinically significant ongoing oropharyngeal MA. As emphasized in the 2020 ATS/SCCM Joint Workshop Report, such targeted de-escalation is an important strategy to reduce antimicrobial resistance, and the lack of objective tools to guide early antibiotic discontinuation remains a major barrier to implementation [60].

However, these propositions remain theoretical, and no randomized controlled trials have yet evaluated airway α-amylase–guided de-escalation on hard clinical outcomes. Thus, although standalone airway α-amylase may not be suitable as an affirmative trigger for treatment, its most immediate clinical value may lie in negative-predictive rule-out, which warrants prospective validation. Future VAP prediction frameworks should therefore prioritize actionable antimicrobial stewardship. A key prerequisite is standardized sampling; because undiluted tracheal aspirates are highly variable, future studies should consider standardized volumetric sampling such as mini-BAL with dilution correction, while accounting for altered diffusion in severe barrier injury such as ARDS. These matrix-adjusted measurements should also be translated into rapid point-of-care platforms to preserve the time advantage of early screening. With standardized sampling, tiered algorithms combining bedside biomarkers with molecular and clinical tools could be developed. This is supported by proof-of-concept data showing that screening within 48–72 h after intubation may provide a clinically actionable window; in one cohort, α-amylase combined with bedside thoracic ultrasound achieved 100% NPV for VAP exclusion [41]. Although preliminary, these findings suggest that a robust negative-predictive algorithm could help rule out VAP and reduce unnecessary broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure [9].

Conversely, an elevated screening biomarker could prompt targeted investigation of the lower-airway microbiome. Molecular approaches, such as targeted PCR for oropharyngeal anaerobes like Veillonella parvula [23] or longitudinal metagenomic sequencing to track dysbiotic dynamics [13], may help clarify the downstream microbiological consequences of early exposure. Although these methods remain too resource-intensive for routine screening, a cost-effective bedside biomarker could enrich the population selected for molecular testing and support more selective deployment of molecular platforms.

Limitations

The external validity of our findings is limited by the inclusion of several highly specific patient cohorts, such as post-cardiac arrest or neurocritical populations [19,41], and by the limited applicability of FEES-based cohorts to continuous passive cuff-related or enteral microleakage assessment in sedated patients. Bedside diagnostic accuracy is further constrained by the substantial rheological and volumetric variability of unstandardized airway matrices (undiluted aspirates versus saline lavages). In addition, the use of post hoc, data-driven cut-offs in several included studies introduces the possibility of statistical overfitting in the absence of independent external validation.

The relatively small number of included studies limits meta-analytic stability, precludes robust meta-regression, and educes the statistical power of Deeks’ test for detecting small-study effects. Meta-epidemiological evidence suggests that meta-analyses based on only a few studies may sometimes approximate later, more mature pooled estimates; however, it remains difficult to predict which early syntheses will remain stable and which will change as additional evidence accumulates [58]. Therefore, the pooled estimates in the present review should be interpreted as preliminary and context-dependent estimates rather than definitive estimates of diagnostic or predictive performance. Misclassification bias is also possible when composite reference standards, such as CPIS, are used in the absence of a true diagnostic gold standard. Moreover, conventional cross-sectional DTA designs do not capture the temporal lag between early biomarker elevation and VAP onset, underscoring the need for future time-to-event analytic approaches. Finally, the current literature addresses diagnostic accuracy rather than clinical impact; the absence of randomized controlled trials evaluating whether biomarker-guided de-escalation improves outcomes such as mortality, antibiotic exposure, or duration of mechanical ventilation remains an important barrier to clinical implementation.

Conclusions

Current airway tracers and biomarkers do not appear suitable as standalone affirmative triggers for VAP diagnosis. However, our synthesis suggests that airway α-amylase may have potential clinical utility as a negative-predictive screening tool. Bayes’ theorem–based projection suggested that a negative airway α-amylase test corresponded to estimated NPVs of 94.8% and 92.8% at baseline VAP prevalences of 15% and 20%, respectively, with corresponding residual risks of 5.2% and 7.2%. These estimates should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory evidence for safe antibiotic discontinuation. Future research should prioritize standardized volumetric airway sampling and prospectively validate clinically actionable rule-out algorithms to support evidence-based early antibiotic de-escalation strategies.
