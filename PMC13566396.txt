Integrative genomic and transcriptomic analysis of hypertension in a Taiwanese population
Authors: Sheng-Nan Chang, Guan-Wei Lee, Jien-Jiun Chen, Chih-Hsien Wang, Fu-Chun Chiu, Pang-Shuo Huang, Eric Y Chuang, Chia-Ti Tsai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives:Hypertension is highly prevalent in Asian populations and represents a major cardiovascular risk factor. However, most genome-wide association studies (GWASs) and transcriptome-wide association studies (TWASs) have focused primarily on Caucasian cohorts. This study aimed to identify genetic loci and gene expression signatures associated with hypertension in an Asian population.Methods:We analyzed 10 739 hypertensive patients and 49 668 controls from the Taiwan Biobank, testing 4 512 191 genome-wide single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Integrated GWAS, TWAS, and expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) analyses were conducted to characterize genetic risk. Additionally, a polygenic risk score (PRS) was constructed using a split-sample design to evaluate genetic risk stratification.Results:We identified 14 loci significantly associated with hypertension, including a novel locus at 5p13.1. eQTL analysis linked this locus to DAB2 expression in whole blood. TWAS detected 55 hypertension-associated genes, with 20 (36%) overlapping GWAS loci. Several novel genes outside GWAS loci, including FBXL15, KCNIP2, and CRIP3, were highly significant and implicated in vascular biology and hypertension mechanisms. PRS analysis effectively differentiated hypertension risk, with individuals in the top 10% showing a > 3.5-fold increased risk compared to the bottom 10%.Conclusions:Our findings provide new insights into the genetic and transcriptomic landscape of hypertension in Asians. The identification of novel loci and genes advances understanding of disease biology and may guide precision medicine approaches for risk prediction and therapeutic development.Keywords: biobank, expression quantitative trait loci, genome-wide association study, hypertension, transcriptome-wide association study Taiwan

INTRODUCTION

Hypertension is a complex trait defined by sustained elevation of systemic blood pressure (SBP). Genetic studies have demonstrated a substantial genetic overlap between clinically diagnosed hypertension and continuous blood pressure (BP) traits, including SBP, diastolic BP (DBP), pulse pressure (PP), and mean arterial pressure (MAP) [1–3]. Consequently, loci identified in genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of quantitative BP traits are typically relevant to hypertension susceptibility.

Most recently, a single-stage meta-analysis comprising over one million individuals of European ancestry significantly expanded the catalog of known genetic variants [1]. This landmark study identified 13 novel loci and reported a total of 2103 independent genetic signals associated with BP traits at genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10–8) [1]. Collectively, these associations now explain >60% of single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-based BP heritability, marking a substantial advance in resolving the polygenic architecture of hypertension [1]. Furthermore, the application of improved polygenic risk scores (PRS), such as those using SBayesRC, has demonstrated the ability to capture up to 11.37% and 12.12% of the phenotypic variance for systolic and DBP, respectively [1].

Given the differences in environmental factors and genetic backgrounds between East Asian and European populations, the mechanisms influencing BP may vary. Therefore, conducting an in-depth study among East Asian populations is essential, both to validate previously identified regions and to uncover new genetic loci linked to hypertension. A recent meta-analysis of BP and hypertension GWASs in the Chinese population identified three novel loci (CACNA1D, CYP21A2, and MED13L) [4].

More recently, Lin et al. conducted a large-scale GWAS in a Han Taiwanese population, identifying 8 loci for hypertension and demonstrating causal links between elevated BP and unstable angina pectoris through Mendelian randomization and phenome-wide association studies (PheWAS) [5]. While this study provided critical insights into the genetic architecture of hypertension and its comorbidities in East Asians, it primarily focused on hypertension as a risk factor for other cardiovascular diseases and predicting cardiovascular mortality using Mendelian randomization. The functional mechanisms linking these genetic signals to specific gene expression changes remain largely unexplored.

To bridge this gap, our study integrates genomic data with transcriptomic evidence to provide a functional interpretation of hypertension susceptibility. We aimed to identify risk loci specific to the Taiwanese population using GWAS and compared them with European findings. Furthermore, we performed a transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) by integrating GWAS summary data with expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) to pinpoint specific genes functionally associated with hypertension, offering novel insights into the molecular etiology of the disease beyond statistical association.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population and data collection

The patients with hypertension and control subjects were from the Taiwan Biobank (TWB). TWB has gathered data from over 100 000 Taiwanese individuals from the general community. The primary goal of this recruitment was to advance research into how environmental and genetic influences contribute to disease susceptibility, while also offering a comprehensive health profile of the Taiwanese populace. To recruit patients with hypertension, we included participants who self-reported to have hypertension and/or were on antihypertensive medications. The Research Ethics Committee of the National Taiwan University Hospital approved the study protocol (201606128RINC).

We used self-reported hypertension as the standard for identifying hypertension subjects because according to previous studies, the specificity of self-reporting hypertension exceeded 90%, and the sensitivity 80% [6,7]. Additionally, previous related studies from the TWB pointed out that over 99% of participants are Han Chinese, including Taiwanese Minnan, Taiwanese Hakka, and those of various Chinese origins [8]. Moreover, when compared to 1000 Genomes Project samples from East Asia using principal component analysis (PCA), the TWB samples did not show obvious population stratification. Genome-wide genotyping

The patients were genotyped by a custom Axiom Genome-Wide Array Plate that contained 714 431 SNP sites, called the TWB chip 2, based on technology developed by Affymetrix. For genotypic quality control, SNPs with a missing call rate of >0.05, Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) P-value of <1×10−6 in controls, and a minor allele frequency (MAF) <0.05 were excluded (Fig. 1). For GWAS quality control, we excluded samples with a call rate <0.99 and heterozygosity rate >±3 std by PLINK (v1.9) [9].

Study overview and analytical workflow. The study incorporated 10 739 hypertensive cases and 49 668 controls from the Taiwan Biobank (TWB). Following genotyping using the TWB Chip 2 and imputation based on the 1000 Genomes Han Chinese reference panel, strict quality control (QC) criteria were applied (MAF > 0.05, call rate > 0.99). A genome-wide association study (GWAS) utilizing PLINK 1.9 and BOLT-LMM identified 14 risk loci (P < 5 × 10–8), including a novel signal at 5p13.1. Downstream analyses were divided into three parallel phases: (1) replication phase involving external validation in UK Biobank and BioBank Japan, alongside novelty assessment via the GWAS Catalog; (2) functional mapping which integrated MAGMA gene-based analysis, eQTL analysis (linking the novel locus to DAB2), and transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) using MetaXcan across 5 cardiovascular tissues to identify 17 high-confidence priority genes; and (3) polygenic risk score (PRS) analysis employing a split-sample design (90% discovery/10% validation) and PRSice-2 to evaluate genetic risk stratification.

Genotype imputation

Given that not all SNPs are designed in the chip stage, we employed the imputation system from the University of Michigan during the data processing stage to impute our raw data, expanding the original 600 000 loci to tens of millions of loci. To ensure uniformity across all datasets, we first transitioned the genomic coordinate system from hg38 to hg19 using the HRC-1000G-check-bim tool (https://www.well.ox.ac.uk/~wrayner/tools/). We then employed Minimac4 to conduct genotype imputation on the 22 chromosomes. This was facilitated by the Michigan Imputation Server, using the reference data from East Asian panel (EAS) that is part of the 1000 Genomes Project (Phase 3 Integrated Release Version 5 Haplotypes).

From the imputed SNPs, we only considered those that met the following quality standards: an imputation r2 value >0.8; a P-value for HWE >1×10−6 and a MAF of at least 5%; genotype data completeness in >99% of the control subjects. Following these criteria, 60 407 out of 81 155 TWB individuals (10 739 cases and 49 668 controls) after imputation were included in this study and a final set of 4 512 191 SNPs was retained for GWAS analysis.

Association analysis

An SNP-based association analysis was performed using PLINK (v1.9) (Figure S1, Supplemental Digital Content). Manhattan plots and quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots were generated with the Python packages (QMplot and Manhattan++) [10]. We used the BOLT-LMM (v2.3) [11] software to estimate the SNP-wide heritability in our data. Heritability is defined as the variance in gene expression divided by the variance in phenotypic expression. The association analysis was adjusted for the following covariates: sex; age; body mass index (BMI); two binary indicators for diabetes and hyperlipidemia; and the top 7 principal components to account for potential population stratification. The odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported [12]. To define independent loci from the genome-wide significant SNPs, we applied linkage disequilibrium (LD)-based clumping using an East Asian ancestry reference panel. Lead SNPs were identified as those with the lowest P-value within an LD block, defined by an r2 threshold of 0.1 and a window of ±500 kb. Signals separated by >500 kb or with r2 < 0.1 were classified as distinct loci.

Cross-validation of hypertension-associated variants

To ensure the novelty of our findings was assessed against the most current evidence, we queried the GWAS Catalog (available at https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/) in December 2025 to curate variants known to be associated with hypertension or BP phenotypes (including SBP, DBP, PP, and MAP). In this study, to strictly define novelty, any variant/locus previously reported to be associated with any of these BP-related traits (HTN, SBP, DBP, PP, or MAP) at a genome-wide significance level (P < 5 × 10−8) was considered a “known” locus. We also performed a reverse lookup by extracting all previously reported significant BP variants from a large European-based trans-ethnic BP trait GWAS [13] to evaluate their reproducibility in our cohort. Furthermore, to comprehensively investigate potential pleiotropy, we searched for cross-trait associations linked to these 14 identified loci, with a specific focus on metabolic traits and cardiovascular or renal diseases such as coronary artery disease, BMI and lipid levels.

We also used UK Biobank (UKB) as an external validation cohort (n = 437 262). The UKB validation was restricted to individuals of European ancestry. When exact lead SNPs were unavailable or nonsignificant in the UKB, we incorporated LD reference data to identify proxy SNPs. We searched for variants within 500 kb and within the same LD block to ensure they served as valid proxies. The distance and LD measures for these proxies are reported. To further assess the transferability of our findings across ancestries, we extended the validation to include publicly available summary statistics from the Japanese Biobank (BioBank Japan PheWeb) and a large-scale trans-ethnic meta-analysis of BP-related traits involving European and American ancestries [13].

Identification of genes and their roles using functional mapping and annotation

We utilized functional mapping and annotation (FUMA) (v1.3.7) [14] for post-GWAS examination. This tool amalgamates data from various biological databases to enable gene ranking, functional annotation. FUMA also provides gene-based analysis, multimarker analysis of genomic annotation (MAGMA). It is a gene analysis tool that can sequentially count the SNPs in a gene and then combine the P-values of the SNPs into the P-value of the gene. Using this method, we calculated the significance of the SNPs on the entire gene.

Y: patients’ phenotype (in this case, it's binary: control is indicated as 0, and the case is indicated as 1), α0g represents the intercept, Xg* represents the principal component matrix from SNPs in gene, W, and βg represent the effects of covariate variables (optional), αg represents the genetic effect, and εg represents the residual.

When SNPs were situated inside a gene's boundaries, the gene-based P-value for that particular protein-coding gene was determined by associating those SNPs directly to the phenotype.

Gene-based association analysis (MAGMA)

To complement single-variant GWAS and facilitate biological interpretation, we performed gene-based analysis using MAGMA (v1.08) via the FUMA platform. This approach aggregates SNP-level associations to evaluate overall gene significance. We mapped SNPs to 18 491 protein-coding genes based on the NCBI Build 37.3 gene definitions. To capture regulatory variants proximate to gene boundaries, we extended the gene window by 500 kb upstream and 500 kb downstream.

LD was calculated using the 1000 Genomes Project EAS reference panel to account for the correlation structure among SNPs within each gene. The gene-based P-value was computed using the MAGMA model, which performs a multiple linear principal component regression. Significance was defined using a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of P < 0.05/18 491 (2.7 × 10–6).

To assess the novelty of these findings, we cross-referenced significant genes with the GWAS Catalog, specifically filtering for BP and hypertension traits.

Expression quantitative trait locus and transcriptome-wide association study implementation

We performed eQTL analysis using the eQTLGen Consortium database, which includes blood samples from over 31 000 individuals to identify genetic variants associated with gene expression levels. We used MetaXcan (v0.7.1) for our TWAS. MetaXcan is a computational method that uses transcriptome prediction models to assess the association between predicted gene expression and a phenotype. It has been applied to over 100 phenotypes, with models trained in 49 different tissues from the genotype-tissue expression (GTEx) project. These results have been modified to account for sex and confounding factors associated with experimental design and population demographics. The adjusted results, which form a catalog of phenotypic association outcomes of gene expression variation, serve as an expanding resource for the scientific community [15]. They applied an elastic net prediction model trained on the GTEx v8 release to calculate expression weights for each SNP located within ±1 Mb of a gene. To ensure reproducibility, TWB GWAS summary statistics underwent stringent harmonization and filtering prior to analysis. Variants with missing rsIDs, strand ambiguity, low imputation quality, or MAF < 1% were removed. Alleles were aligned to the GTEx reference panel, and only SNPs present in both the GWAS summary statistics and prediction models were retained.

The analysis was conducted across 49 human tissues to capture broad biological context, with a specific focus on five cardiovascular-relevant tissues: heart atrial appendage, heart left ventricle, artery aorta, artery coronary, and artery tibial. Significance was defined using a stringent Bonferroni-corrected threshold adjusted for the number of genes tested per tissue (atrial appendage; P = 3.7 × 10–6, heart left ventricle; P = 3.9 × 10–6, artery aorta; P = 3.6 × 10–6, artery coronary; P = 3.8 × 10–6, artery tibial; P = 3.6 × 10–6).

Polygenic risk score analysis

PRSice-2 is a comprehensive software tool used for calculating PRS from large-scale biobank data [16]. PRS are metrics that quantify the cumulative risk for specific diseases based on a multitude of genetic variants. Developed to meet the demands of increasingly large datasets in genomics research, PRSice-2 is optimized to handle data from biobank-scale studies efficiently, involving hundreds of thousands to millions of individuals. It provides more precise and accurate PRS calculations than its predecessor, PRSice, by accommodating a broader range of genetic architectures. PRSice-2 accepts a wide range of input file formats, including those from popular software like PLINK and BGEN. Moreover, it offers flexible options for PRS calculation, including different p-value thresholds and opportunities to apply clumping and thresholding (C+T) or pruning and thresholding (P+T) to handle LD. Therefore, we utilized PRSice-2 as the primary tool for PRS analysis and implemented the Clumping and Thresholding (C+T) method. To generate the PRS, we utilized a split-sample design within the TWB to mitigate overfitting. The cohort was divided into a 90% discovery set for deriving summary statistics and an independent 10% validation set for performance evaluation. Association with hypertension was assessed in the validation set using logistic regression. We used the first seven principal components of the SNP data, BMI, sex, age, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia, for adjustments.

RESULTS

Genome-wide association study of hypertension

We utilized the axiom genome-wide array chip for a GWAS, which included 714 431 SNPs and whole genome genotype imputation. After applying stringent quality control filters to eliminate markers or subjects with call rates below 99%, a total of 4 512 191 whole genome SNPs were retained and utilized for GWAS analysis. We first performed PCA of SNP data using the pricipal components 1–4 to predict hypertension. The C statistic was 0.5, suggesting the absence of a discernible population stratification in our study (Figure S2, Supplemental Digital Content).

The Manhattan plot (Figure S3A, Supplemental Digital Content) and Q–Q plot (Figure S3B, Supplemental Digital Content) of GWAS analysis showed 1033 significant SNPs with a P-value threshold of <5 × 10–8 and genomic control inflation factors of 1.007. Among the significant SNPs, we identified 14 risk loci (CASZ1, WNT2B, KCNK3, AC092684.1, FGF5, DAB2, HOTTIP, ACTR1A, NHLRC2, LINC01153, LSP1, ARHGAP42, ATP2B1, and RP11–25E2.1), with one locus being novel (DAB2) (Table 2). Within these 14 risk loci, 46 genes reached genome-wide significance (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content).

A novel hypertension genetic locus in the Taiwanese population and replication analysis

The lead variant of the novel significant locus was rs4957500 (OR 0.91, 95% CI [0.90–0.93], P = 4.37 × 10–8), around which there are only two pseudogenes localize (CCDC11P1 and INTS6P1). The nearest gene is DAB2, with rs4957500 located 200 kb upstream (Figure S4, Supplemental Digital Content). It is possible that rs4957500 localizes within the distal regulatory region of DAB2 gene [17]. We then validated whether DAB2 was associated with hypertension in the UKB. We used a region plot to examine the significance level of the SNPs near the DAB2 gene in the UKB. We found this region did not reach genome-wide significance level (Figure S5, Supplemental Digital Content). This locus also has never been reported before to be associated with hypertension. Therefore, we speculate that this might be a risk locus unique to Taiwanese people. We also used the existing UKB data to validate the other 13 significant SNPs (Table 3). Among them, 12 were successfully replicated in the UKB dataset. The remaining locus on chromosome 10 (rs7085142) was associated with BMI.

We also used the existing Japanese Biobank data to validate the 14 significant SNPs. Of the 14 loci, 12 were successfully replicated in the Japanese Biobank dataset. The remaining two loci included the novel locus on chromosome 5 (rs4957500), which was associated with nonalbumin protein levels, and the locus on chromosome 10 (rs7085142) associated with BMI. In additional to Biobank data, we also cross-referenced our significant SNPs in a large European-based trans-ethnic analyses [13]. Similarly, 12 of the 14 loci were replicated in European-based trans-ethnic analyses, while the chromosome 5 locus remained novel (Table 2). We also performed a reverse lookup by extracting all previously reported significant BP variants from a large European-based trans-ethnic BP trait GWAS [13] to evaluate their reproducibility in our cohort (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content). Among the 337 SBP loci, 12 reach genome-wide significance and 21 reach sub-threshold significance (P < 10–5) (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content).

Because one of our loci was associated with BMI, we performed a systematic check for pleiotropic effects of hypertension loci using the GWAS Catalog, the European-based trans-ethnic analyses [13] and Japanese Biobank. The results are shown in Table 3. Many of hypertension loci are also associated with non-hypertension traits, indicating the pleiotropic effects of hypertension loci.

Gene-based association analysis

To more confirm our findings and facilitate a more intuitive recognition of significant genes associated with hypertension, we subsequently implemented the MAGMA gene-based method. This strategy amalgamates SNPs within a specific gene to determine its overall significance. We used the Bonferroni correction for simultaneous testing of 18 491 human genes (P < 0.05/18 491). This analysis revealed 37 significant genes (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content ), with the top four being NT5C2 (P = 8.8 × 10–13), AS3MT (P = 8.9 × 10–13), ARL3 (P = 1.1 × 10–12), and KCNK3 (P = 5.8 × 10–12). The most significant gene was different from that revealed in GWAS (FGF5 gene).

We performed a systematic evaluation using the GWAS Catalog to determine whether any of the 37 genes represented novel associations with hypertension or BP traits. All 37 genes had previously been reported in association with hypertension or BP traits. This is likely because the MAGMA-identified genes were located in proximity to our 14 identified hypertension loci, 12 of which had already been reported as associated with hypertension or BP traits.

Expression quantitative trait locus and transcriptome-wide association study analyses

We first performed eQTL analysis using the eQTLGen Consortium database to investigate whether our novel 5p13.1 locus was associated with DAB2 gene expression. We found rs4957500 itself showed a significant cis-eQTL effect on DAB2 expression in whole blood (P = 4.37 × 10−8). In addition, rs4957500 is located approximately 2 kb from the eQTL lead SNP rs4957210, which showed a stronger association with DAB2 expression (P = 2.79 × 10−10) and a signed Z score of −6.31, indicating reduced expression associated with the effect allele (Figure S4, Supplemental Digital Content). This indicates that our novel 5p13.1 locus was associated with DAB2 expression.

We further employed TWAS to associate the expression levels of gene to the phenotype of hypertension, which directly implicates the functional significance of the significant genes. We used MetaXcan to examine the association between gene expression and hypertension in 49 human tissues from GTEx v8. We concentrated on two heart tissues (the heart atrial appendage and the heart left ventricle) and three arterial tissues (coronary and tibial arteries and aorta) that are most related to the mechanism of hypertension.

We identified 55 genes the expression levels of which were significantly associated with hypertension phenotype in one of the selected tissues (Figure S6, Supplemental Digital Content) (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content ). Detailed gene-level association statistics for these findings, including effect sizes, standard errors, and P-values, are provided in Table 4. Among them, 20 genes (36%) colocalized with GWAS genes, and 35 genes (64%) were at least 500 kb away from the hypertension risk loci. Among these genes away from GWAS loci, 12 genes are novel that have never been reported to be related to BP or hypertension mechanism [18,19]. Fifteen genes were not associated with BP or hypertension but were associated with phenotypes related to hypertension or hypertension mechanisms, such as BMI, obesity, lipid level, renal function, insulin resistance and testosterone level.

Polygenic risk score analysis

In this study, we utilized the PRSice software to calculate PRS. To gauge the extent to which PRS can differentiate individual risks of hypertension, we first stratified individuals into deciles based on their PRS, and compared the ORs of hypertension across different groups. We observed that a higher decile corresponds to an increased OR. Stratification by deciles revealed that the top 10% had a significantly higher hypertension risk than the bottom 10% (OR = 3.53, 95% CI 2.04–6.84, P = 4.06 × 10−5). Using the middle decile (40–50%) as a population-representative reference, the top 10% remained significantly associated with increased risk (OR = 1.53, 95% CI 1.13–2.01, P = 3.35 × 10−3). Similarly, comparisons between the bottom 10% and the middle decile also showed a significant difference (OR = 2.27, 95% CI 1.23–4.18, P = 8.02 × 10−3), confirming effective risk stratification across the spectrum (Figure S8, Supplemental Digital Content) [20].

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first and largest study combining GWAS and TWAS for hypertension in Asia. Our findings provide critical insights into the genetic architecture and molecular mechanisms underlying this complex disorder, particularly within Asian populations. These results should be interpreted in the context of recent large-scale genomic studies that have refined the global landscape of BP genetics. Notably, Keaton et al. recently reported over 2000 independent genetic signals from a cohort exceeding one million participants [1]. Although primarily focused on European ancestry, that study emphasized the importance of trans-ethnic analyses, demonstrating significant concordance in allelic effects when comparing European discovery loci with the Japanese Biobank (n = 145 000) [1]. Specifically, they observed effect size correlations of r = 0.69 for known loci and r = 0.50 for novel loci between European and Japanese populations. This cross-population consistency reinforces the relevance of our current findings in the Taiwanese population, while simultaneously highlighting that further large-scale, ancestry-specific GWAS remain essential to fully capture population-specific genetic risk factors.

Novel locus of hypertension in Taiwan

In the whole-genome GWAS, we identified 14 significant loci, of which 9 were consistent with previous findings across multiple populations, 3 were specifically associated with hypertension in East Asian populations, one had been previously associated with BMI, and one was a novel locus, rs4957500, located between two pseudogenes (CCDC11P1 and INTS6P1) on chromosome 5p13.1. The lead, or sentinel, SNP rs4957500 is positioned 200 kb upstream of DAB2, suggesting that this region may also function as a potential distal regulatory element for DAB2 [21]. The localization of a lead SNP within a distal regulatory region of a candidate gene has been previously reported. For example, the most significant atrial fibrillation-associated region, validated across multiple populations and studies, is located 200 kb upstream of the 5’ regulatory region of the PITX2 gene, rather than within or directly adjacent to the PITX2 gene itself [22]. The eQTL analysis also shows that this novel 5p13.1 locus is associated with DAB2 expression, further supporting the possibility that DAB2 is the disease-causing gene.

This is also the first time that DAB2 gene is implicated as a possible susceptibility gene of hypertension. Prior research indicates that DAB2 plays a role in cardiovascular diseases, particularly in high low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol level and myocardial infarction [23]. Plausible explanations of association may be that when cholesterol cannot be effectively cleared from the bloodstream, excess cholesterol can accumulate along artery walls, leading to stiff arteries and increase in BP [24]. Elevated serum LDL level has also been reported to be associated with an increased risk of hypertension in Japanese men [25]. Finally, DAB2 has also been implicated in promoting inflammation [26], insulin resistance and diabetes that may also be associated with hypertension in the circumference of metabolic syndrome [27].

Prioritizing genome-wide association study hypertension genes

In the present study, we conducted whole-genome GWAS alongside downstream analyses to prioritize genes potentially contributing to the development of hypertension. GWAS frequently identifies numerous associated SNPs; therefore, we first grouped SNPs within the same LD block and reported significant loci or LD blocks, within which there may be multiple candidate genes associated with hypertension. Next, we employed gene-based analysis (MAGMA) to aggregate all SNPs associated with a specific gene, facilitating the identification of significant genes by allowing for gene-level significance even when individual SNPs do not display sufficiently significant p-values.

Finally, we performed TWAS to examine the correlation between gene expression and hypertension, identifying possible causal genes outside the identified risk loci or blocks. Through these methodologies, we aimed to identify genes with a high likelihood of causing hypertension. To provide a comprehensive overview of the genetic landscape, we compiled the union of all significant genes identified by any of the three approaches (GWAS, MAGMA, or TWAS) (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content ). From this union set, we further prioritized candidates by identifying those supported by multiple lines of evidence. We identified 17 overlapping genes that were statistically significant across all three analyses: GWAS, MAGMA, and TWAS.

Of these 17 genes, 14 have previously been associated with hypertension or other BP traits (including SBP, DBP, Map, and PP) [13,28–30]. Although our primary analysis used a binary hypertension phenotype derived from self-reported hypertension status and/or antihypertensive medication use, we identified multiple loci that have been previously reported in large-scale GWAS of continuous BP traits. We further evaluated all SBP loci identified in a large European-based transethnic GWAS [13] within the TWB cohort and compared their effect sizes. Specifically, we assessed the concordance in the direction of beta estimates derived from linear regression (continuous BP trait in the transethnic study) and logistic regression (binary hypertension trait in our cohort). Among the 337 loci reported in the transethnic analysis [13], 176 showed concordant directions of effect. The lack of full concordance may be partly explained by differences in the sentinel SNPs across studies, with some loci represented by variants located at considerable distances from each other (though still within 500 kb to be considered representing the same locus across different studies). This concordance supports the validity of using hypertension as a phenotype in our study and suggests that key BP-related genetic signals can be captured using a case–control design.

However, the exact biological mechanism underlying this association is still unclear and needs further investigation for many of these hypertension genes.

Three among these 17 genes, CALHM2, TAF5, and INA currently lack evidence of association with hypertension. Upregulation of CALHM2, which acts as a calcium and ATP channel, is implicated in the shift from a contractile to a proliferative phenotype in pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells [31]. This change might be crucial in the pathological vascular remodeling observed in pulmonary arterial hypertension. TAF5 has been reported to be linked to cerebral white matter disease [32] that may be a vascular complication of hypertension. INA has been linked to BMI and glomerular filtration rate, both of which may be related to hypertension mechanism [33].

Transcriptome-wide association studies and hypertension

To date, only one study has combined GWAS and TWAS analyses for hypertension in Asian populations [34]. They sought to identify susceptibility genes for salt sensitivity of BP by GWAS and TWAS. The case number is limited in this study. In our TWAS, we identified several novel genes that have never been reported to be related to hypertension or close to any hypertension risk loci. Among them, CRIP3, KCNIP2 and FBLX15 were highly significantly associated with hypertension in all tissues, marking the first time they have been linked to hypertension.

FBXL15 has been reported to regulate bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling by directing the degradation of the HECT-type ubiquitin ligase Smurf1 [35], and selective BMP-9 inhibition protects against experimental pulmonary hypertension [36], suggesting the role of FBXL15 in vascular remodeling mediated by BMP. The involvement of FBXL15 in hypertension is further supported by findings that circulating BMP-9 levels are associated with hypertension and insulin resistance in humans [37].

With regard to KCNIP2 and hypertension, studies have demonstrated that KCNIP2 is expressed in vascular smooth muscle cells [38]. As an accessory protein of the A-type potassium current channel, KCNIP2 plays a critical role in cardiac electrophysiology [36]. Additionally, high-threshold A-type potassium currents have been reported to suppress membrane excitability in vascular smooth muscle cells, potentially contributing to vascular relaxation and BP regulation [39].

CRIP3 encodes cystatin C, a well established serum biomarker for renal function and hypertension. In addition to its role as a biomarker, cystatin C functions in protease inhibition and contributes to vascular remodeling within blood vessels [40]. This suggests that targeting the protease pathway could potentially induce vascular remodeling and provide a therapeutic approach for hypertension. Furthermore, the observation that protease inhibitors used in HIV treatment can lead to hypertension underscores the role of CRIP3 in vascular biology and its relevance to hypertension [41].

LIMITATIONS

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, the predominance of European ancestry in the GTEx v8 database limits the generalizability of our MetaXcan results to non-European populations [42]. Second, while we focused on heart and arterial tissues to reduce complexity, hypertension is a systemic disease; thus, the identified genes may not be restricted to these tissues and could involve other organs. Third, data on continuous BP traits were not available for further analysis. Fourth, the lack of a completely independent external validation cohort remains a limitation. Finally, comparative analysis of Asian-specific vs. large-scale European-derived PRS remains a priority for future research to address ancestry transferability.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, this comprehensive research has unearthed potential genetic markers for hypertension within the Taiwan population, contributing novel insights into the genetic foundations of this complex disease. Our findings underscore the importance of understanding the genetic and molecular mechanisms underlying hypertension, highlighting potential targets for future research and intervention strategies. As our grasp of the genetic contributions to hypertension continues to grow, genetic screening might become an indispensable tool in diagnosing, predicting risk, and treating hypertension. These findings stand as a testament to the vast potential of genomics research in improving cardiovascular health and outcomes. Further functional validation studies, particularly in diverse populations, are imperative to expand and solidify our understanding of hypertension's genetic underpinnings.
