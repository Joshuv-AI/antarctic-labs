Establishing a nomogram model for predicting hospital length of stay in children with II–III degree scald burns based on admission assessment indicators
Authors: Ying Gao, Biyun He, Jing Huang, Yang Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExisting pediatric burn studies have primarily focused on risk factors or survival outcomes, while predictors of hospital length of stay (LOS) remain insufficiently explored. This study aimed to develop and validate a nomogram predicting LOS by incorporating the clinical characteristics of children with II–III degree scald burns.MethodsThis retrospective study collected cases of pediatric patients with II–III degree scald burns admitted to Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, National Clinical Research Center for Children and Adolescents’ Health and Diseases between July 2024 and August 2025. A generalized linear regression model for predicting LOS was established based on univariate Gamma regression, multicollinearity tests, and multivariate Gamma regression to screen variables, followed by construction of a nomogram. Model performance and calibration were internally validated using root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), cross-validation, and 1,000-times bootstrap resampling.ResultsA total of 191 children (male: 64.9%; female: 35.1%) with II–III degree scald burns were included, with the majority aged between 1 and 3 years. Multivariate analysis showed that burn time before admission, total body surface area, albumin, blood glucose, white blood cell count, hemoglobin and skin condition were independent predictors of LOS in children with II–III degree scald burns. The model established had the lowest Akaike information criterion value, indicating the best fit. The Gamma generalized linear model demonstrated moderate predictive performance with a cross-validated R2 of 0.31, along with acceptable calibration and mild optimism in bootstrap internal validation.ConclusionsWe constructed a nomogram to predict LOS in children with scald burns, which may help clinicians assess LOS early and optimize treatment.Keywords: Pediatrics, scald burns, length of stay (LOS), nomogram, generalized linear regression

Key findings

• We developed and internally validated a nomogram to predict hospital length of stay (LOS) in children with II–III degree scald burns.

• Burn time before admission, total body surface area, albumin, blood glucose, white blood cell count, hemoglobin and skin condition were identified as independent predictors of LOS.

• The model demonstrated moderate predictive performance with good calibration and internal validation stability.

What is known and what is new?

• Previous pediatric burn studies have mainly focused on risk factors or survival outcomes, while reliable tools for predicting LOS remain limited.

• This study establishes a generalized linear model-based nomogram specifically for pediatric II–III degree scald burns, integrating readily available clinical and laboratory indicators to enable individualized LOS prediction.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The proposed nomogram provides clinicians with an early, practical tool to estimate hospitalization duration and assess disease severity in pediatric scald burn patients.

• Its application may support risk stratification, optimize resource allocation, and guide personalized treatment planning.

• Future multicenter and prospective studies are recommended to further validate and refine the model before broader clinical implementation.

Introduction

Burn injuries pose a serious threat to children’s health worldwide. Among various burn types, II–III degree burns are the most frequently observed in pediatric patients (1). Burns include all forms of thermal injury, while scalds represent a specific subtype caused by hot liquids or steam. Epidemiological data consistently show that scalds account for the majority of pediatric burn cases (2). In China, scalds are the most common cause of burn injuries, with the majority occurring during childhood (3,4). Among these cases, hospitalized pediatric burn patients often present with deeper or more extensive injuries compared to outpatients, frequently requiring multiple skin grafts and prolonged treatment (5).

Burns typically trigger a wide range of systemic responses, including increased risk of inflammation and infection, which often results in longer hospital length of stay (LOS) in pediatric patients than in other types of trauma (6). International studies have identified a number of prognostic factors associated with LOS in burn patients (7,8). Studies have indicated that hospitalization duration in pediatric burn patients was positively correlated with age, total body surface area (TBSA), burn severity, burn type, and complications (7,9). In pediatric burns, scald burns prolong hospitalization compared to other burn types (10). Prolonged hospitalization for burn treatment leads to increased consumption of medical resources, thereby raising healthcare costs. Therefore, predicting LOS in pediatric patients based on clinical factors is crucial for optimizing treatment strategies.

Despite the existence of some predictive models, such as the one developed by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) for predicting LOS in burn patients, approximately 20% of pediatric burn patients still have longer-than-predicted hospital stays (4). Moreover, most existing burn prediction models focus on risk factors or survival outcomes rather than LOS in children (11,12). Therefore, it is necessary to develop LOS prediction models specifically for this population. By integrating multiple independent factors, a nomogram can provide an accessible tool for forecasting treatment outcomes and decision-making in pediatric burn care. This study aims to develop and validate a nomogram to predict LOS in children with II–III degree scald burns based on their clinical characteristics, improving the overall treatment of burn patients. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-0199/rc).

Methods

Study population

This retrospective study reviewed 202 pediatric burn patients admitted to Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, National Clinical Research Center for Children and Adolescents’ Health and Diseases between July 2024 and August 2025. Patient selection followed the inclusion criteria: (I) children aged 0–14 years; (II) burns of degree II–III; (III) data were substantially complete. The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with burns caused by non-routine hot fluids, including chemical burns, electrical burns, and scalds caused by other special causative factors. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 191 patients with typical hot-liquid scald injuries were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). All patient data have been anonymized for privacy. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, National Clinical Research Center for Children and Adolescents’ Health and Diseases (Approval No. 2026-IRB-0016-P-01). Informed consent was obtained from all participants’ legal guardians.

Flowchart of patient inclusion and exclusion. LOS, length of stay.

Data imputation

To minimize bias caused by excluding missing data while preserving the sample size, missing values were imputed using the nonparametric random forest-based method “missForest”. Among the patients, 5 had incomplete discharge time records, and the missingness of other variables is presented in Table S1. The missForest algorithm uses observed variables to iteratively estimate and impute missing values through random forest models.

Data collection

The dependent variable in this study is the LOS of scald burns patients. Since LOS is a continuous positive-valued variable exhibiting right-skewed distribution upon normality testing, ordinary linear regression models are unsuitable. Therefore, a generalized linear model was employed for analysis. All available samples were used for model development, and internal validation was performed using 10-fold cross-validation.

The collected variables include: demographic information such as age, gender, and urban/rural residence; guardian information such as kinship relationship, duration of guardianship, and guardian’s educational level; immediate treatment methods such as running water irrigation and other treatments (e.g., community-based povidone-iodine disinfection); Admission information such as the child’s mental state, presence of shock, infection, burn time before admission, burn depth, burn degree, TBSA, fever, conscious state, skin condition, and urine volume. Vital signs such as body mass index (BMI), body temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), oxygen saturation (SpO2), mean blood pressure (MBP), capillary refill time (CRT); clinical laboratory indicators such as pH, K+, Na+, Ca2+, glucose, lactate, HCO3-, actual base excess (ABE), white blood cell (WBC), hemoglobin, platelets, C-reactive protein (CRP), total protein, albumin. At admission, the attending physician assessed the “mental status” based on clinical evaluation. Children were categorized as normal or abnormal according to their behavior, including crying, lethargy, and overall responsiveness. Shock was defined according to Pediatric Advanced Life Support (PALS) criteria as inadequate tissue perfusion with clinical signs of poor end-organ perfusion and/or hypotension.

Variable selection and model development

In this study, we chose the Gamma regression model for modeling and analysis. Based on the data characteristics, the Gamma family was chosen as the error structure, and the log link function was used to connect the predictor variables with the response variable. The model form is: ln(µi) = β0 + β1X1 + β2X2 + ... + βpXp. Where µi represents the expected value of the number of hospitalization days for the i-th sample, X1, X2, ..., Xp are the independent variables, and β0, β1, ..., βp are the regression coefficients. The linear predictions can be restored to the original dimensions of the predicted LOS using the exponential logarithm (13).

The independent variable selection in this study followed these steps: First, a univariate Gamma regression model was established for each candidate independent variable, calculating its regression coefficients and P values. Variables achieving P<0.2 in univariate analysis were considered potentially statistically significant candidates. Selected continuous variables underwent multicollinearity and correlation analysis, with Pearson’s correlation coefficients calculated. When significant correlations existed between variables (|r| ≥0.7), the variable with greater clinical interpretability or statistical stability was retained for subsequent analysis. Include the screened variables in a multivariate Gamma regression model. Employ bidirectional stepwise regression for variable selection, using the Akaike information criterion (AIC) as the metric for comparing different models. This approach balances model fit quality and complexity to obtain the optimal model. The Beta coefficient and its 95% confidence interval (CI) for each covariate are reported within the model.

Model evaluation

A nomogram was generated based on the final model to visually demonstrate the relative contribution and predictive effect of each predictor variable on the LOS. Variable significance in the model was assessed through bias analysis and likelihood ratio tests. To evaluate the model’s predictive performance, a scatter plot first displayed the predicted LOS against the actual LOS. Subsequently, a residual plot visualizes the differences between predicted and actual LOS to further examine whether any systematic errors exist that the model failed to capture. In addition, the root mean square error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE) were calculated to evaluate model prediction error. Cross-validation was performed for internal validation of the Gamma generalized linear model, and RMSE and MAE derived from cross-validation were used to assess model performance. Bootstrap resampling was also used for internal validation with 1,000 repetitions. In each bootstrap sample, the generalized linear model with a Gamma distribution and log link was refitted, and optimism bias was estimated by comparing the apparent performance with the test performance. Optimism-corrected performance measures were subsequently obtained. Furthermore, calibration curves based on bootstrap resampling and cross-validation were generated to evaluate the agreement between predicted and observed outcomes and to assess model calibration performance.

Statistical analysis

Due to skewed data distributions, continuous variables were presented as median [interquartile range (IQR)], while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Independence between groups was assessed using chi-square tests for categorical variables and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables. Sample size adequacy for predictive modeling was evaluated according to the events per variable (EPV) principle, using a minimum threshold of 10 EPV (14). To minimize bias from excessive data deletion while preserving sample size. All statistical analyses were conducted in R software (version 4.5.0) using the packages: mice, tableone, corrplot, rms, car, and ggplot2. The significance level for two-tailed tests was set at P<0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 191 children with scald burns were included in the study. Males accounted for 64.9% and females for 35.1% of the cohort. The majority of burn victims were aged approximately 1 to 3 years (Table 1). The burn degree in these patients was predominantly moderate (59.7%) and severe (35.6%). The median LOS was 11.00 [8.00, 15.00] days, and the median TBSA (%) was 10.00 [8.00, 15.00].

Generalized linear model

Univariate Gamma regression (Table 2) identified live place, conscious state, skin condition, urine volume, shock, burn time before admission, flowing water time, TBSA, CRT, Na+, glucose, WBC, hemoglobin, platelet, total protein, albumin as candidate variables. A total of 191 patients were included, with 16 candidate predictor parameters. The ratio of sample size to the number of predictors was approximately 11.2:1, which met the commonly recommended rule of at least 10 observations per predictor variable for regression modeling. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated for continuous variables selected at P<0.2 (Figure 2A). Total protein was excluded due to multicollinearity with albumin. The remaining variables underwent bidirectional stepwise regression (Figure 2B) and were screened using a multivariate Gamma regression model (Table 3). Ultimately, the model including skin condition, burn time before admission, TBSA, glucose, WBC, hemoglobin, and albumin yielded the lowest AIC, indicating optimal model fit (Table 3). These variables were used to construct the nomogram (Figure 3). Each predictor value is plotted on the corresponding axis of the nomogram, and the total points are summed to obtain the predicted LOS. The bias analysis revealed the contribution of each variable to the model (Figure 4), ranked from highest to lowest: albumin, TBSA, hemoglobin, burn time before admission, glucose, WBC, and skin condition. Verification of the nomogram

The scatter plot showed good overall agreement between the predicted and observed values (Figure 5A). The residual plot further illustrated the distribution of errors between predicted and observed values, with most residuals clustered around zero and only a few cases showing relatively large deviations (Figure 5B). The model performance evaluation demonstrated that the Gamma generalized linear model showed a moderate level of fitting ability (Table 4). The deviance explained was 33.8%, indicating that the model could explain approximately one-third of the variability in LOS. In terms of prediction error, the RMSE was 6.56 days and the MAE was 4.89 days, suggesting that the predicted LOS differed from the observed LOS by approximately 5 days on average. The final AIC of the bidirectional stepwise regression model was 1,197.186.

Discussion

LOS has become an increasingly important indicator of quality care (15). However, existing LOS prediction models show considerable variability, and certain unexamined predictors may be important. Further exploration of LOS prediction models for scald burn patients is warranted. Our study predicted LOS in pediatric scald burn patients using generalized linear regression models, identifying albumin, TBSA, hemoglobin, burn time before admission, glucose, WBC, and skin condition as independent predictors of LOS in children with II–III degree scald burns. The regression coefficients further reflected the clinical implications of these predictors. Increased burn time before admission, TBSA, blood glucose, and hemoglobin were associated with longer hospital stays, while increased blood albumin levels were associated with shorter hospital stays. Among the variables included in these models, albumin was the primary predictor, followed by TBSA.

The present study demonstrated that the Gamma generalized linear model achieved moderate but stable predictive performance for LOS in pediatric patients with typical hot-liquid scald injuries. The model showed reasonable fitting ability, with a deviance explained of 33.8%, and maintained comparable performance after internal validation using cross-validation, where the cross-validated R2 remained at 0.31. In addition, the relatively small increases in RMSE and MAE after cross-validation and bootstrap optimism correction suggest that the model retained acceptable generalizability and was not severely overfitted. Both bootstrap- and cross-validation-based calibration curves demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed LOS. Together, these findings suggest that the model possesses acceptable internal stability and calibration performance, although its predictive ability remains moderate, likely reflecting the multifactorial nature of LOS in pediatric burn patients.

Burn-related factors significantly impact patient prognosis and LOS. Delayed treatment may make it difficult for patients with unstable conditions to receive a clear treatment plan in a timely manner, thereby increasing the risk of admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) and prolonging the LOS (16). Delayed presentation in burn patients may increase infection rates, exposing them to risks of sepsis, pneumonia, malnutrition, and poor wound healing (17). Our study found that longer time from burn injury to admission predicted longer LOS. Therefore, reducing this interval may significantly improve outcomes and shorten hospital stays. In addition to delays in seeking care, burn severity is another key determinant of patient prognosis and LOS. The size of TBSA burns has been reported in numerous studies to correlate with patient prognosis and LOS (18,19). A retrospective cohort study identified TBSA as a significant predictor of LOS in an adjusted model (20). This finding was further validated in a predictive model for prolonged hospitalization in burn patients (21). These findings align with our results, which show that TBSA was an independent predictor of LOS in pediatric scald burn patients. Mechanistically, larger burn areas can induce complex fluid shifts, including systemic leakage into non-burned tissues. This leads to intravascular volume overload, causing organ ischemia, lactic acidosis, and further prolonging of LOS (22).

Given the close relationship between burn extent and the proportion of unaffected skin, we further evaluated the impact of skin condition on LOS. In our study, the proportion of normal skin was high, with 83.2% of children having largely unaffected skin areas. Interestingly, our regression analysis showed that a higher percentage of normal skin was associated with longer hospital stays, which appears counterintuitive because smaller burn areas are generally expected to require shorter hospitalization. This apparent paradox may be explained by several factors: the high overall proportion of normal skin limits variability, a small number of children with a lower proportion of normal skin may have experienced extremely short or long hospital stays due to complications, and potential multicollinearity with other variables may have influenced the regression coefficient. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted with caution and does not imply that more normal skin directly prolongs hospitalization.

Routine laboratory tests are commonly performed in children with severe scald burns, but the relationship between specific laboratory indicators and LOS remains unclear. In our study, albumin, WBC count, glucose, and hemoglobin were identified as independent risk factors for prolonged LOS in burn children. These indicators are closely linked to core burn-related pathophysiological disturbances, particularly fluid imbalance and metabolic dysfunction (23,24). Fluid loss is a central hallmark of moderate-to-severe burns, and the resulting intravascular volume depletion leads to pronounced alterations in blood parameters. Under these conditions, both hemoconcentration and bone marrow activation contribute to leukocytosis. Reflecting these physiological responses, WBC count has been recognized as a significant predictor of burn mortality (25). Moreover, Frugoni’s predictive model for prolonged LOS in surgical burn patients identified abnormal WBC count also constituted an independent risk factor for extended hospitalization (21). These findings are in agreement with our results, further supporting the value of WBC count as a clinically relevant marker of burn severity and prolonged recovery.

In addition to inflammatory responses, nutritional and protein metabolic status may also substantially influence recovery in pediatric burn patients. Albumin plays a critical role in maintaining colloid osmotic pressure and fluid balance (26), and hypoalbuminemia is common in burn patients, particularly in those with extensive burns (27). Burn-induced physiological stress and endoplasmic reticulum stress may impair protein synthesis and promote protein degradation, contributing to reduced serum albumin levels (28). In addition, significant intravascular albumin loss following burn injury can further aggravate hypoalbuminemia (29), which has been associated with poorer clinical outcomes and increased mortality risk (30). Consistent with these findings, our study showed that higher albumin levels were associated with shorter LOS, emphasizing the clinical importance of monitoring albumin levels in pediatric burn patients.

Glucose metabolic disturbances following burn injury may also contribute to prolonged hospitalization. Our study found that increased blood glucose levels were associated with prolonged hospital stays in pediatric burn patients. While the potential benefits of strict glycemic control remain inconclusive (31), previous studies suggest that hyperglycemia in severely burned children may impair antimicrobial defenses, increase the risk of sepsis, reduce graft survival, and contribute to higher mortality (32). These findings indicate that maintaining normoglycemia through active interventions may be important for improving outcomes in critically injured burn patients.

In this study, higher hemoglobin levels at admission were significantly associated with prolonged LOS in children with moderate-to-severe (II–III degree) scald injuries. Elevated hemoglobin in the acute phase of burn injury may reflect hemoconcentration secondary to substantial plasma loss and increased capillary permeability, thereby indicating more severe fluid depletion and larger burn extent. Previous studies have shown that admission hemoglobin may serve as a reference marker for extensive burns and is positively associated with LOS (23). Although hospital LOS is affected by multiple clinical factors (33), hemoglobin remained an independent predictor after multivariable adjustment, suggesting its potential value in early risk assessment.

There are several potential limitations in this study. First, the clinical applicability of predicting LOS as a continuous outcome requires further validation, as no risk stratification categories or clinically meaningful decision thresholds were defined in the present study. Second, despite incorporating a relatively comprehensive set of clinical variables, the relatively small sample size may affect model robustness, the stability of estimated associations, and the statistical significance of certain predictors, and may also increase the risk of model overfitting. Third, this study was a retrospective single-cohort study and was not conducted under a blinded design; therefore, potential measurement bias may exist during data collection and assessment, which may have affected the accuracy of the findings. Finally, the study population consisted of children with II–III degree scald burns from a single region, whose demographic characteristics and medical conditions possess certain specificities, and the model has not yet been externally validated. Therefore, the model’s generalizability to other regions or different age groups requires further validation through prospective, multicenter studies.

Conclusions

We developed a model to predict the LOS for children with II–III degree scald burns. The model effectively forecasts hospitalization duration based on burn time before admission, TBSA, albumin, blood glucose, WBC count, hemoglobin and skin condition. This provides a valuable reference tool for early clinical assessment of the child’s condition and the formulation of individualized treatment strategies.
