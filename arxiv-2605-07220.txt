On the Robustness of Distribution Support under Diffusion Guidance
Ruijia Cao, Yuchen Wu, Nisha Chandramoorthy

Abstract
Diffusion guidance is a powerful technique that enables controllable and high-fidelity sample generation with diffusion models. At a high level, it modifies the score function by incorporating a guidance term that steers the generative process toward a desired condition. Despite its empirical success, the theoretical properties of diffusion guidance remain largely unexplored, and it is not well understood why it consistently produces high-quality samples. In this work, we explain the effectiveness of diffusion guidance by establishing a robustness of support property. Specifically, we show that, given exact access to the score functions, guided diffusion processes almost always generate samples that remain close to the target support. This property is particularly desirable, as samples that lie off the support are often structurally implausible and may adversely affect downstream tasks. Our analysis covers both Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models (DDIM) and Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM), and applies to a wide range of discretization schemes induced by exponential integrators. Our results provide a rigorous foundation for understanding why diffusion guidance produces physically meaningful and structurally plausible samples.

Introduction

Score-based diffusion models
, originally inspired by thermodynamic principles
(
50

, have emerged as a powerful class of generative models, achieving state-of-the-art performance across a wide range of applications
(

At a high level, diffusion models generate samples by progressively denoising a sequence of intermediate distributions
\{P_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, thereby transforming a simple, easy-to-sample distribution
P_{1}
(e.g., Gaussian noise) into a complex target distribution
P_{N}
.
This generative procedure is driven by a sequence of
score functions

\{\nabla\log P_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N-1}
, which are typically learned by training deep neural networks through a score matching procedure
(
27
)
.
Given the estimated score functions, diffusion models generate samples through an
N

\displaystyle P_{1}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{sample}}Y_{1}\xrightarrow{\nabla\log P_{1}}Y_{2}\xrightarrow{\nabla\log P_{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\nabla\log P_{N-1}}Y_{N}\,\overset{d}{\approx}\,P_{N}.

In practice, many applications require controllable data generation to ensure that the generated content aligns with specific objectives or user-defined preferences
(
13
;
24
;
64
;
6
)
.
A common approach to achieve this goal with diffusion models is
diffusion guidance

(
13
;
24
)
,
a technique that steers the generation process toward samples with the desired attributes.
Specifically, to generate samples associated with a target label
\eta_{0}
, diffusion guidance modifies the unconditional score
\nabla\log P_{i}(\cdot)
by incorporating an additional guidance term
\gamma\nabla\log P_{i}(\eta_{0}\mid\cdot)
for all
i\in[N-1]
,
where
\gamma\geq 0
controls the strength of the guidance.
In practice, this guidance term must be learned from data, and the two main approaches for doing so are
classifier guidance

(
13
)
and
classifier-free guidance

(
24
)
, depending on whether the term is approximated via classifier training or joint score estimation.
Notably, diffusion guidance not only enables controllable generation, but also often improves the quality of the generated samples.
An informal illustration of the sample generation process under guided diffusion models is provided below, and a more detailed description is deferred to Section
2.2

\displaystyle P_{1}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{sample}}Y_{1}\xrightarrow{\nabla\log P_{1}+{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}\mathrm{guidance}}}Y_{2}\xrightarrow{\nabla\log P_{2}+{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}\mathrm{guidance}}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\nabla\log P_{N-1}+{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}\mathrm{guidance}}}Y_{N}\,\overset{d}{\approx}\,{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}\mathrm{?}}\,.

Despite its empirical success, the theoretical properties of diffusion guidance remain largely unexplored.
In particular, the distribution produced by guided diffusion sampling has not yet been fully characterized.
To shed light on the effectiveness of diffusion guidance, a line of work analyzes simplified settings in which the target distribution admits tractable structure, for example, when the conditional distributions are Gaussian or supported on one-dimensional intervals
(
60
;
11
;
56
)
.
For more general distributions,
5
demonstrate that guidance does not, in general, sample from a powered distribution, and can be interpreted as a predictor-corrector method.
Recent work establishes an average-case result demonstrating that diffusion guidance reduces the expected reciprocal of the conditional label probability,
and thus increases classification confidence in an average-case sense
(
34
;
30
)
.
From a different perspective,
49
show that under mild smoothness conditions, classifiers trained by minimizing the empirical cross-entropy loss lead to an effective guidance mechanism.
For conditional sample generation with hard constraints,
21
propose a guidance framework based on Doob’s
h
-transform.

In this work, we adopt a different perspective, explaining the success of diffusion guidance through the
robustness of support
property
(
7
)
.
In particular, we ask the following question:

Does diffusion guidance generate samples that are close to the target support?

The ability to generate samples near the target support is a highly desirable property for generative models, since off-support samples are often structurally implausible and can adversely impact downstream tasks.
For example, a recent paper on video sampling empirically demonstrates that, in certain settings, generative models may fail to achieve the robustness of support property, leading to physically distorted videos
(
29
)
.
In the context of diffusion guidance, this means that although guidance may not generate samples exactly from the conditional distribution associated with the label of interest (and indeed should not when
\gamma\neq 1
),
we still expect it to produce physically meaningful and plausible samples that remain near the target support.
Support robustness of diffusion guidance has been established in
11
for one-dimensional mixture distributions.
However, it is unclear whether their analysis extends to high-dimensional settings.

1.1
Main contributions

In this paper, we study the behavior of diffusion guidance when the target distribution is a mixture of general distributions supported on compact convex sets.
Our findings are summarized below:

•

Support robustness with perfect score estimation:

We show that guided diffusion models with exact access to the score functions exhibit strong support robustness, in the sense that the generated sample paths almost surely converge to the correct support.
Our theory applies broadly to all guidance strength
\gamma\geq 1
and to a wide family of discretization schemes induced by an exponential integrator (including continuous-time processes with infinitesimal discretization), and covers both the deterministic diffusion sampler Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models (DDIM)
(
51
)

and the stochastic diffusion sampler Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM)
(
23
)
.
Our theory implies that, in the absence of score estimation error and with sufficiently fine discretization, diffusion guidance with arbitrary guidance strength produces physically plausible samples that remain close to the support of the target component.

•

Numerical evidence:

We provide numerical evidence supporting our theoretical findings.
Based on experimental outcomes, we further conjecture that, under mild regularity assumptions, convergence to the target support may continue to hold even in the absence of support convexity.
We leave a rigorous investigation of this point for future work.

In summary, our results provide insight into the qualitative behavior of the sample paths produced by diffusion guidance, revealing that the resulting dynamics inherently adapt to the intrinsic geometry of the target distribution.
In practice, however, diffusion guidance can still produce physically implausible samples, particularly when the guidance strength
\gamma
is large.
Our results suggest that this behavior does not originate from the diffusion guidance framework itself, but rather from either errors in score estimation or an insufficiently fine discretization scheme.
Note that diffusion models are generally expected to fail under inaccurate score estimation or coarse discretization.

Theoretical explanations for the success of diffusion guidance.

A recent line of work seeks to theoretically explain the empirical success of diffusion guidance.

2
Background

2.1
Score-based diffusion models

This section provides a brief overview of diffusion models.
At a high level, a diffusion model consists of a
forward process
and a
reverse process
.
In the forward process, the target distribution is progressively transformed into an easy-to-sample noise distribution (typically a standard Gaussian).
The reverse process, in turn, maps this noise back to the target distribution, enabling the generation of new samples.
Note that only the reverse process is used during the sample generation stage.

Diffusion models are often analyzed through their continuous-time limits, and are typically implemented as numerical approximations to these limits
(
53
)
.
For better understanding, we first introduce the continuous-time formulation of diffusion models, and then describe the samplers implemented in practice, which can be viewed as time-discretized approximations of these continuous-time processes.

Diffusion models through a continuous-time perspective.

A common choice for the forward process is based on the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process.
Specifically, let
p_{0}
denote the target distribution to be sampled. We then consider the following stochastic differential equation (SDE) defined on
0\leq t\leq T
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}y_{t}=-y_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\qquad y_{0}\sim p_{0},

(1)

where
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denotes a standard
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued Wiener process that is independent of
y_{0}
. For
0\leq t\leq T
, we denote by
p_{t}
the distribution of
y_{t}
.
Standard computation implies that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&p_{t}\overset{d}{=}\lambda_{t}\,y_{0}+\sigma_{t}\,w,\qquad(y_{0},w)\sim p_{0}\otimes\pi_{d},\\
&\lambda_{t}=e^{-t},\qquad\sigma_{t}=\sqrt{1-\lambda_{t}^{2}},\end{split}

(2)

where
\pi_{d}
denotes the distribution of a standard Gaussian random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
From
Eq.

2
, we see that process (
1
) begins with the target distribution, and progressively injects Gaussian noise until the resulting distribution approaches the standard Gaussian distribution.

For the reverse process, two commonly used approaches are DDIM and DDPM.
Their continuous-time formulations corresponding to the forward process (
1
) are given below:

•

DDIM sampler.

The continuous-time process underlying the deterministic DDIM sampler is formulated as an ordinary differential equation (ODE) on
0\leq t<T
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x_{t}=\left(x_{t}+\nabla\log p_{T-t}(x_{t})\right)\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad x_{0}\sim p_{T}.

(3)

The marginal distributions of process (
3
) match that of the forward diffusion process (
1
), in the sense that
x_{t}\overset{d}{=}y_{T-t}
for all
0\leq t<T
.

•

DDPM sampler.
The continuous-time process underlying the stochastic DDPM sampler is on the other hand formulated as an SDE defined on
0\leq t<T
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x_{t}=\left(x_{t}+2\nabla\log p_{T-t}(x_{t})\right)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t},\qquad x_{0}\sim p_{T},

(4)

where
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a standard
d
-dimensional Wiener process that is independent of
x_{0}
.
Process (
4
) also matches the marginal distributions of process (
1
) in a time-reversed sense:
x_{t}\overset{d}{=}y_{T-t}
for all
0\leq t<T

(
2
)
.

Processes (
3
) and (
4
) are both driven by a family of
score functions

\{\nabla\log p_{T-t}\}_{0\leq t<T}
, which in practice are unknown and are typically learned from training data drawn from
p_{0}
through a
score matching
procedure
(
27
)
.
In addition, the correct initial distribution
p_{T}
depends on
p_{0}
hence is also unknown.
On the positive side, from
Eq.

2
, we know that
p_{T}
can be well approximated by a standard Gaussian distribution for only moderately large
T
(See Proposition 4 in
3
for a rigorous justification of this point).
To sample from
p_{0}
, rather than tracking the processes in (
3
) and (
4
) all the way to
T
, practitioners typically stop early at
T-\delta
for a small positive
\delta
to avoid numerical instability.

Diffusion models via time-discretization.

Apart from score estimation and approximate initialization,
practical implementation of DDIM (DDPM) requires choosing a discretization scheme for the associated reverse-time ODE (SDE).
There are multiple ways to discretize the reverse dynamics, and the choice of discretization scheme can significantly affect the resulting diffusion model.
For instance, a popular approach is the exponential integrator, which has been empirically shown to produce high-fidelity samples
(
65
)
.

31
further improve the sampling efficiency of diffusion models by adaptively selecting discretization step sizes.
Another line of work investigates the use of high-order numerical solvers to accelerate sample generation with diffusion models
(
40
;
41
;
61
;
25
;
26
;
36
)
.
For a summary of other common discretization strategies, we refer the reader to
(
39
, Section 2)
.

2.2
Controllable generation with diffusion guidance

In many practical scenarios, instead of sampling unconditionally from
p_{0}
, one may wish to generate samples associated with a user-specified label
\eta_{0}
.
Diffusion guidance achieves this by augmenting the unconditional score
\nabla\log p_{T-t}(\cdot)
with an additional term
\gamma\nabla\log p_{T-t}(\eta_{0}\mid\cdot)
, where the hyperparameter
\gamma\geq 0
controls the guidance strength.
Here, with a slight abuse of notation, we denote by
p_{t}
the joint distribution of
(y_{t},\eta_{0})
, assuming that the Wiener process
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
in
Eq.

1
is independent of
(y_{0},\eta_{0})
.
From a continuous-time perspective, the guided ODE associated with the DDIM sampler takes the following form:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}x_{t}=&\left(x_{t}+\nabla\log p_{{T-t}}(x_{t})+\gamma\nabla\log p_{{T-t}}(\eta_{0}\mid x_{t})\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\\
=&\left(x_{t}+(1-\gamma)\nabla\log p_{{T-t}}(x_{t})+\gamma\nabla\log p_{{T-t}}(x_{t},\eta_{0})\right)\,\mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

(5)

where
p_{T-t}(\eta_{0}\mid x_{t})\in[0,1]
denotes the probability of label
\eta_{0}
given
x_{t}
under
p_{T-t}
.
Throughout this work, unless otherwise stated, all gradients are taken with respect to the
x
-space.
Similarly, the guided SDE associated with the DDPM sampler admits the following expression:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}x_{t}=&\left(x_{t}+2\nabla\log p_{{T-t}}(x_{t})+2\gamma\nabla\log p_{{T-t}}(\eta_{0}\mid x_{t})\right)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\\
=&\left(x_{t}+2(1-\gamma)\nabla\log p_{{T-t}}(x_{t})+2\gamma\nabla\log p_{{T-t}}(x_{t},\eta_{0})\right)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t}.\end{split}

(6)

In practice, the additional guidance term must also be learned from data. Depending on the training approach, it can be classified as either classifier guidance (where
p_{T-t}(\eta_{0}\mid\cdot)
is learned) or classifier-free guidance (where a joint score function over both the label and the sample space is learned).
We refer interested readers to
13
and
24
for further details.

Observe that when
\gamma=0
, processes (
5
) and (
6
) reduce to the unconditional sampling processes (
3
) and (
4
), respectively. On the other hand, when
\gamma=1
, they coincide with the reverse processes associated with the conditional distribution
p_{0}(\cdot\mid\eta_{0})
.
Notably, practitioners often choose
\gamma>1
to improve fidelity of the generated samples. With this choice, the goal is not to sample exactly from
p_{0}(\cdot\mid\eta_{0})
, but to generate samples that are more strongly aligned with the target label
\eta_{0}
.

3
Results for DDIM

In this section, we present our main results for DDIM.
Throughout this paper, we denote by
\mathcal{I}
a finite set of labels and,
without loss of generality, assume that diffusion guidance is always directed toward samples associated with
\eta_{0}\in\mathcal{I}
.

3.1
Summary of main results

We begin by stating the assumptions on the target distribution
p_{0}
required to establish our main theorem.

Assumption 3.1
.

We assume that
p_{0}=\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}w_{\eta}p_{\eta}
is a mixture distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
(w_{\eta})_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}
are non-negative mixture weights that satisfy
\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}w_{\eta}=1
, and
p_{\eta}
is a probability density function supported on a convex and compact set
K_{\eta}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We further impose the following assumptions:

1.

The set
K_{\eta}
contains at least one interior point for all
\eta\in\mathcal{I}
. In addition,
K_{\eta}\cap K_{\eta^{\prime}}=\emptyset
for all
\eta,\eta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}
with
\eta\neq\eta^{\prime}
.

2.

The weight associated with the target component is positive, i.e.,
w_{\eta_{0}}>0
.

3.

The density function associated with the target component is bounded from below on its support, i.e.,
\inf_{x\in K_{\eta_{0}}}p_{\eta_{0}}(x)>0
.

For simplicity, we first present our findings for the continuous-time guided ODE.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(x_{t})_{0\leq t<T}
be any sample path that represents a solution to the guided ODE (
5
), then under Assumption
3.1
, for any starting location
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and guidance strength
\gamma\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to T^{-}}\mathsf{dist}(x_{t},K_{\eta_{0}})=0.

We explain the intuition behind the theorem in the next section.
The crucial ingredient of our proof is a differential inequality controlling the rate of decay of the distance function along the trajectory, which roughly states that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathsf{dist}(x_{t},K_{\eta_{0}})\leq-\frac{1}{4\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\mathsf{dist}(x_{t},K_{\eta_{0}})

(7)

for all

\mathsf{dist}(x_{t},K_{\eta_{0}})

A rigorous statement of this inequality, along with its proof, is provided in Appendix

Remark 3.1
.

Theorem
3.1
establishes a path-wise convergence result that holds for any initial location, providing stronger guarantees than those based on average-case analysis.
Our result implies that, for a sufficiently small early-stopping parameter
\delta

5
) remain within the vicinity of the target support.
Furthermore, inequality (
7
) suggests that the distance contracts at rate
\mathcal{O}(-1/\sigma^{2}_{T-t})
whenever the trajectory is not too close to
K_{\eta_{0}}
.

We then extend the continuous-time analysis given in Theorem
3.1
to cover the discrete samplers used in practice.
While we focus on the sampling scheme based on the exponential integrator
(
65
)
, we expect that our results generalize to other discretization methods as well.

To describe the sampling scheme based on the exponential integrator, we consider a discretization with time steps
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{N}\leq T
.
On each interval
[t_{k},t_{k+1})
for
k=0,1,\ldots,N-1
, we perform the following update

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\widehat{x}_{t}=\big(\widehat{x}_{t}+\nabla\log p_{T-t_{k}}(\widehat{x}_{t_{k}})+\gamma\nabla\log p_{T-t_{k}}(\eta_{0}\mid\widehat{x}_{t_{k}})\big)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Note that this update equation can be expressed in the following closed form:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\widehat{x}_{t_{k+1}}=e^{\Delta_{k}}\widehat{x}_{t_{k}}+(e^{\Delta_{k}}-1)\cdot\big(\nabla\log p_{T-t_{k}}(\widehat{x}_{t_{k}})+\gamma\nabla\log p_{T-t_{k}}(\eta_{0}\mid\widehat{x}_{t_{k}})\big),\\
&\Delta_{k}=t_{k+1}-t_{k},\qquad k=0,1,\cdots,N-1.\end{split}

(9)

We next impose an assumption on the step sizes.
Note that our assumption is standard and aligns with the one adopted in

Assumption 3.2
.

We assume that there exists
\kappa\in(0,1)
, such that
\Delta_{k}\leq\kappa\min\{1,T-t_{k+1}\}

.

Under Assumptions

, and

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathsf{dist}(\widehat{x}_{t_{k}},K_{\eta_{0}})=0

for any initialization
\widehat{x}_{t_{0}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and guidance strength
\gamma\geq 1
.
Here, the sequence

Remark 3.2
.

So far, we have considered a simplified setting in which the score functions and guidance terms are known exactly.
Evidently, the support robustness property continues to hold under uniform bounds on the score and guidance term errors.
However, we do not yet know how to extend the result to the more realistic setting in which the errors are measured in
L_{2}
.
In this case, it is natural to assume that the errors are under the base measures
(p_{t})_{0<t\leq T}
from the forward process, as both the score and the guidance terms are trained using data drawn from these distributions.
However, in the guided diffusion model, the intermediate states in the reverse process do not approximately follow the distributions of the forward process. Instead, their distributions depend on the choice of the guidance parameter
\gamma
.
This distribution mismatch represents the primary obstacle to extending our results to account for score and guidance estimation errors.

Remark 3.3
.

Theorem
3.2
requires the discretization scheme to be sufficiently fine, as governed by
\kappa_{0}
.
Moreover, a closer inspection of our proof shows that the interval of
\kappa_{0}
for which the theorem holds may shrink as
\gamma
increases,
indicating that a large guidance strength combined with insufficiently fine discretization can lead to physically distorted samples.

Remark 3.4
.

Although we analyze a commonly used discretization scheme here, we do expect similar results to hold for other discretization schemes.
One reason is that the one-step displacement under different discretization schemes is
\kappa
under Assumption
3.2
.
The drift evaluation at
\widehat{z}_{t_{k}}
is therefore a close approximation of the continuous-time drift, which blows up as
\mathcal{O}(1/\sigma^{2}_{T-t})
.
Thus, the rate of the contraction of the distance function is unaffected.

Our main result
3.1
assumes that the support domains of the mixture components are convex.
It is worth noting that this convexity requirement may be somewhat restrictive, as practical mixture distributions do not necessarily exhibit convex support.
We make two remarks regarding this point.
First, our numerical experiments indicate that our theoretical findings continue to hold for mixture distributions with non-convex support.
Second, although many high-dimensional datasets in practice may not have convex support, their latent representations in a lower-dimensional subspace often form simpler, and sometimes even linearly separable clusters.
For instance, this effect is evident in the Principal Component Analysis (PCA) plots of human genomics data
15
.
On this note, we remark that Latent Diffusion Models introduced in
47

have been shown to yield highly competitive performance on various image-related tasks compared to diffusion models trained directly on pixel space while being more computationally efficient.

3.2
Intuition: reformulating DDIM with guidance

In this section, we present a change-of-variables reformulation of the process (
5
) that simplifies the analysis and offers intuition for the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
Observe that for
0<t\leq T
, we can express the density functions
p_{t}(\cdot)
and
p_{t}(\eta_{0}\mid\cdot)
using Gaussian convolutions:

\displaystyle p_{t}(x)=\int\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}w_{\eta}p_{\eta}(x_{0})\phi_{t}(x-\lambda_{t}x_{0})\mathrm{d}x_{0},

\displaystyle p_{t}(\eta_{0}\mid x)=\frac{w_{\eta_{0}}\int p_{\eta_{0}}(x_{0})\phi_{t}(x-\lambda_{t}x_{0})\mathrm{d}x_{0}}{\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}w_{\eta}\int p_{\eta}(x_{0})\phi_{t}(x-\lambda_{t}x_{0})\mathrm{d}x_{0}},

\displaystyle\phi_{t}(z)=\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{t})^{d}}\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\|z\|^{2}\Big).

Taking the gradients of
p_{t}(x)
and
p_{t}(\eta_{0}\mid x)
with respect to
x
, we obtain by Stein’s formula
(
54
)
the following expressions for the score function and the guidance term:

\displaystyle\nabla\log p_{t}(x)=\frac{\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}w_{\eta}\int p_{\eta}(x_{0})\phi_{t}(x-\lambda_{t}x_{0})(-\sigma_{t}^{-2}x+\lambda_{t}\sigma_{t}^{-2}x_{0})\mathrm{d}x_{0}}{\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}w_{\eta}\int p_{\eta}(x_{0})\phi_{t}(x-\lambda_{t}x_{0})\mathrm{d}x_{0}},

\displaystyle\nabla\log p_{t}(\eta_{0}\mid x)=\frac{\int p_{\eta_{0}}(x_{0})\phi_{t}(x-\lambda_{t}x_{0})(-\sigma_{t}^{-2}x+\lambda_{t}\sigma_{t}^{-2}x_{0})\mathrm{d}x_{0}}{\int p_{\eta_{0}}(x_{0})\phi_{t}(x-\lambda_{t}x_{0})\mathrm{d}x_{0}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\frac{\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}w_{\eta}\int p_{\eta}(x_{0})\phi_{t}(x-\lambda_{t}x_{0})(-\sigma_{t}^{-2}x+\lambda_{t}\sigma_{t}^{-2}x_{0})\mathrm{d}x_{0}}{\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}w_{\eta}\int p_{\eta}(x_{0})\phi_{t}(x-\lambda_{t}x_{0})\mathrm{d}x_{0}}.

For notational simplicity, for
\eta\in\mathcal{I}
and
0<t\leq T
, we define

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\overline{\zeta}_{\eta,t}(x)=\frac{w_{\eta}\int p_{\eta}(x_{0})\phi_{t}(x-\lambda_{t}x_{0})\mathrm{d}x_{0}}{\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}w_{\eta}\int p_{\eta}(x_{0})\phi_{t}(x-\lambda_{t}x_{0})\mathrm{d}x_{0}}=p_{t}(Y=\eta\mid X_{t}=x),\\
&\overline{m}_{\eta,t}(x)=\frac{\int x_{0}p_{\eta}(x_{0})\phi_{t}(x-\lambda_{t}x_{0})\mathrm{d}x_{0}}{\int p_{\eta}(x_{0})\phi_{t}(x-\lambda_{t}x_{0})\mathrm{d}x_{0}}=\mathbb{E}\big[X_{0}\mid X_{t}=x,\,Y=\eta\big],\end{split}

(10)

where
p_{t}(Y=\eta)=w_{\eta}
,
X_{0}\mid Y=\eta\sim p_{\eta}
,
X_{t}=\lambda_{t}X_{0}+\sigma_{t}Z
, and
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,{\rm I}_{d})
is independent of
(X_{0},Y)
.
Observe that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it holds that
\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}\overline{\zeta}_{\eta,t}(x)=1
,
\nabla\log p_{t}(x)=-\sigma_{t}^{-2}x+\lambda_{t}\sigma_{t}^{-2}\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}\overline{\zeta}_{\eta,t}(x)\overline{m}_{\eta,t}(x)
, and

\nabla\log p_{t}(\eta_{0}\mid x)=\lambda_{t}\sigma_{t}^{-2}\overline{m}_{\eta_{0},t}(x)-\lambda_{t}\sigma_{t}^{-2}\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}\overline{\zeta}_{\eta,t}(x)\overline{m}_{\eta,t}(x).

Plugging the above expressions into the guided ODE (
5
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathrm{d}x_{t}=\Big\{\frac{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}-1}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}x_{t}+\frac{\gamma\lambda_{T-t}}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\overline{m}_{\eta_{0},T-t}(x_{t})-\frac{(\gamma-1)\lambda_{T-t}}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}\overline{\zeta}_{\eta,T-t}(x_{t})\overline{m}_{\eta,T-t}(x_{t})\Big\}\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\Big\{x_{t}+\frac{\gamma\lambda_{T-t}}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\Big(\overline{m}_{\eta_{0},T-t}(x_{t})-\frac{x_{t}}{\lambda_{T-t}}\Big)-\frac{(\gamma-1)\lambda_{T-t}}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}\overline{\zeta}_{\eta,T-t}(x_{t})\Big(\overline{m}_{\eta,T-t}(x_{t})-\frac{x_{t}}{\lambda_{T-t}}\Big)\Big\}\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(11)

Define
z_{t}=\lambda_{T-t}^{-1}x_{t}
for
0\leq t<T
. By
Eq.

11
, we see that the resulting process
(z_{t})_{0\leq t<T}
is the solution to the following ODE:

\mathrm{d}z_{t}=\Big\{\frac{\gamma}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\left(m_{\eta_{0},T-t}(z_{t})-z_{t}\right)-\frac{\gamma-1}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}\zeta_{\eta,T-t}(z_{t})\left(m_{\eta,T-t}(z_{t})-z_{t}\right)\Big\}\mathrm{d}t,

(12)

where for
\eta\in\mathcal{I}
and
0\leq t<T
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\zeta_{\eta,t}(z)=\frac{w_{\eta}\int p_{\eta}(x_{0})\phi_{t}(\lambda_{t}(x_{0}-z))\mathrm{d}x_{0}}{\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}w_{\eta}\int p_{\eta}(x_{0})\phi_{t}(\lambda_{t}(x_{0}-z))\mathrm{d}x_{0}}=p_{t}(Y=\eta\mid X_{t}=\lambda_{t}z),\\
&m_{\eta,t}(z)=\frac{\int x_{0}p_{\eta}(x_{0})\phi_{t}(\lambda_{t}(x_{0}-z))\mathrm{d}x_{0}}{\int p_{\eta}(x_{0})\phi_{t}(\lambda_{t}(x_{0}-z))\mathrm{d}x_{0}}=\mathbb{E}\big[X_{0}\mid X_{t}=\lambda_{t}z,\,Y=\eta\big].\end{split}

(13)

By definition,
\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}\zeta_{\eta,t}(z)=1
for all
0\leq t<T
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Note that the vector-valued function
m_{\eta,t}(z)
can be interpreted as the mean of a tilted version of
p_{\eta}
, with the tilt determined by the input vector
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
When
K_{\eta}
is convex, we know that
m_{\eta,t}(z)\in K_{\eta}
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

For notational convenience, for
\eta\in\mathcal{I}
and
0<t\leq T
, we define

\displaystyle F_{\eta,t}(z)=m_{\eta,t}(z)-z.

(14)

With the above definition, we can re-write ODE (
12
) as

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}z_{t}=\Big\{\frac{\gamma}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}F_{\eta_{0},T-t}(z_{t})-\frac{\gamma-1}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}\zeta_{\eta,T-t}(z_{t})F_{\eta,T-t}(z_{t})\Big\}\mathrm{d}t.

(15)

Intuitively, one may interpret the reformulation of (
5
) that we derive here as a force-balance equation: each term
F_{\eta,t}(z_{t})
acts as a “force” that pulls
z_{t}
towards a point
m_{\eta,t}(z_{t})
in
K_{\eta}
.
When
\gamma=1
, the only acting force is
F_{\eta_{0},T-t}(z_{t})
, and one should therefore expect that the trajectory of
z_{t}
to move toward
K_{\eta_{0}}
as
t
increases.
When
\gamma>1
, the trajectory is subject to additional forces that repel it from other regions,
and it is not a priori clear that the trajectory will still move toward
K_{\eta_{0}}
.
Nevertheless, our analysis shows that the force directed toward
K_{\eta_{0}}
dominates the other forces, thereby ensuring that the trajectory of
z_{t}
ultimately converges to
K_{\eta_{0}}
.
See Figure
1
for an illustration of ODE (
15
).

) when

As in the derivations of Section

where we recall that
\overline{m}_{\eta,t}
and
\overline{\zeta}_{\eta,t}
are defined in
Eq.

10
.
Define
z_{t}=e^{T-t}x_{t}
for

.
With such a change of variables, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}z_{t}&=\Big\{\frac{2\gamma}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\big({m}_{\eta_{0},T-t}({z_{t}})-{z_{t}}\big)-\frac{2(\gamma-1)}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}{\zeta}_{\eta,T-t}(z_{t})\big({m}_{\eta,T-t}(z_{t})-z_{t}\big)\Big\}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}e^{T-t}\mathrm{d}B_{t}\\

(18)

The drift term above is exactly twice that of the ODE in (

we argue that as

dominates, thus ensuring that the trajectory moves towards the support of the target distribution.

6
Discussion

In this work, we show that diffusion models with guidance exhibit the support robustness property.
Our theory applies to both DDIM and DDPM,
and encompasses both continuous-time processes and discretizations induced by an exponential integrator.

Several interesting directions remain for future exploration:

•

Support robustness without convexity assumptions.

Our numerical experiments in Section
5
suggest that the support robustness property extends to mixture distributions with non-convex support.
We currently impose the convexity assumption due to technical limitations,
removing this restriction and providing a rigorous theoretical justification is an interesting direction for future work.

•

Accommodating score estimation error.

Our current theoretical results assume exact access to the score functions.
As noted in Remark
3.2
, extending these results to estimated scores requires controlling the score estimation error under a shifted distribution, which requires additional technical developments.

•

Further analysis of the guided distribution.

This work suggests that diffusion guidance generates samples that remain on the target support.
An interesting open problem is to further characterize the distribution produced by diffusion guidance.
In particular, while our results indicate that diffusion guidance produces physically plausible samples, they do not fully explain its success with respect to sample-quality metrics.
A deeper understanding would require a more refined analysis of the guided distribution.

We then define the Hausdorff distance.

Definition A.1

(Hausdorff distance)
.

Let
X,Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be two compact sets.
Then, their Hausdorff distance, denoted by
{d}_{\mathsf{H}}(X,Y)
, is given by

{d}_{\mathsf{H}}(X,Y)=\max\left\{\sup_{x\in X}\mathsf{dist}(x,Y),\,\sup_{y\in Y}\mathsf{dist}(y,X)\right\}.

Remark A.1
.

{d}_{\mathsf{H}}(\cdot,\cdot)
defines a metric on the space of non-empty compact subsets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Remark A.2
.

It is well-known that convex sets can be approximated arbitrarily well by convex sets with algebraic (and hence smooth) surfaces
(
18
)
.
More precisely, for any
\varepsilon>0
and any compact convex set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exists another compact convex set
\widetilde{K}
with smooth boundary, such that
{d}_{\mathsf{H}}(\widetilde{K},K)\leq\varepsilon
.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a compact, convex set with
C^{k}
-boundary, where
k\geq 2
.
Then, the function
x\mapsto\mathsf{dist}(x,K)
is
C^{k}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus K
.
Furthermore, the Laplacian of this function is given by

\Delta\mathsf{dist}(x,K)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{\kappa_{i}(\Proj_{K}(x))}{1+\kappa_{i}(\Proj_{K}(x))\mathsf{dist}(x,K)},

where
\left\{\kappa_{i}\right\}_{1\leq i\leq n-1}
denote the principal curvatures of
\partial K
.
In particular, since
\kappa_{i}\geq 0
and
\partial K
is compact,

\Delta\mathsf{dist}(x,K)\leq\mathcal{C}_{K}=n\sup_{y\in\partial K}\max_{1\leq i\leq n-1}\kappa_{i}(y).

Proof of Lemma
A.3
.

We prove Lemma
A.3
in Appendix
A.3
.

∎

\displaystyle F_{K}(x)=\inf_{0<r<r_{K}}\frac{\vol\big(\mathcal{B}(x,r)\cap K\big)}{r^{d}},

where
r_{K}=\inf_{x\in\partial K}\left\|v_{K}-x\right\|>0
.
To prove the lemma, it suffices to show
\inf_{x\in\partial K}F_{K}(x)>0
.

For all
x\in\partial K
, define

\displaystyle C_{x}

\displaystyle=\big\{\alpha x+(1-\alpha)z:z\in\mathcal{B}(v_{K},\delta_{K}),\,\alpha\in[0,1]\big\}

\displaystyle H_{x}

\displaystyle=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left\langle y-v_{K},x-v_{K}\right\rangle\geq 0\right\}

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{x}

\displaystyle=C_{x}\cap H_{x}.

By convexity, we have
C_{x}\subseteq K
for every
x\in\partial K
.
Note that for all
r\in(0,r_{K})
, it holds that

\displaystyle\vol\big(C_{x}\cap\mathcal{B}(x,r)\big)\geq\vol\big(\widetilde{C}_{x}\cap\mathcal{B}(x,r)\big)\geq\left(\frac{r}{\left\|x-v_{K}\right\|}\right)^{d}\cdot\vol\big(\widetilde{C}_{x}\big).

Note that
\widetilde{C}_{x}
is a cone with base being a
(d-1)
-dimensional ball that has radius
\delta_{K}
, hence

\displaystyle\vol\left(\widetilde{C}_{x}\right)=\frac{\left\|x-v_{K}\right\|\cdot\omega_{d-1}\cdot\delta_{K}^{d-1}}{d},

where
\omega_{d-1}
denotes the volume of a
(d-1)
-dimensional ball.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\vol\left(C_{x}\cap\mathcal{B}(x,r)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{r}{\left\|x-v_{K}\right\|}\right)^{d}\cdot\frac{\left\|x-v_{K}\right\|\cdot\omega_{d-1}\cdot\delta_{K}^{d-1}}{d}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{d-1}\cdot\delta_{K}^{d-1}}{d\left\|x-v_{K}\right\|^{d-1}}\cdot r^{d}.

Since
\left\|x-v_{K}\right\|\leq\mathsf{diam}(K)
, we see that for all
r\in(0,r_{K})
,

\displaystyle\vol\left(C_{x}\cap\mathcal{B}(x,r)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\omega_{d-1}\cdot\delta_{K}^{d-1}}{d\cdot\mathsf{diam}(K)^{d-1}}\cdot r^{d}.

The lemma follows by setting
\rho_{K}=\frac{\omega_{d-1}\cdot\delta_{K}^{d-1}}{d\cdot\mathsf{diam}(K)^{d-1}}
.

Using the above notations, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mbox{The last line of Eq. \eqref{eqn:dist_derive_II}}\\

\mathsf{dist}(\widehat{z}_{t_{k}},K_{\eta_{0}})>\varepsilon
for all
k\geq N
.
Write
\{Z_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
for

Z_{k+1}=\mathsf{dist}(\widehat{z}_{t_{k+1}},K_{\eta_{0}})^{2}.

By Lemmas
C.1
and
C.2
, we see that
\mathbb{E}[Z_{k+1}\mid Z_{k}]\leq(1-\frac{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}{5\sigma_{T-t_{k}}^{2}})\,Z_{k}
.
By the same reasoning as in the paragraph preceding
C.2
, we see that
Z_{k}\to 0
almost surely as
k\to\infty
.
A contradiction.
Therefore,
L\leq\varepsilon
almost surely, completing the proof.

C.7
Proof of Lemma
C.4

Since
\partial\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}}
is smooth,
Lemma
A.3
implies that the distance function
\mathsf{dist}(\cdot,\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}})
is at least
C^{2}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}}
.
Applying Itô’s formula, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\big(\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}})\big)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}}),\,\mathrm{d}z_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\nabla^{2}\mathsf{dist}({z}_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}})\,\mathrm{d}z_{t},\,\mathrm{d}z_{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\left\langle\nabla\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}}),\,b(t,z_{t})\right\rangle+e^{2(T-t)}\Delta\mathsf{dist}({z}_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}})\right)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+\sqrt{2}e^{T-t}\left\langle\nabla\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}}),\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\right\rangle.

Note that the process
{W}_{t}
defined by
\mathrm{d}{W}_{t}=\langle{\nabla\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}}),\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}}\rangle
is a one-dimensional Brownian motion.
Indeed,
Itô isometry implies the quadratic variation of this process is
[{W}]_{t}=t
and
\mathbb{E}[{W}_{t}]=0
.
Therefore, by Lévy’s characterization of Brownian motion
(
46
, Theorem 3.6 Chapter IV)

{W}_{t}
coincides in law with a standard one-dimensional Brownian motion.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\big(\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}})\big)=f(t,{z}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+e^{2(T-t)}\,\mathrm{d}{W}_{t},

completing the proof of the lemma.

C.8
Proof of Lemma
C.5

By our assumption that
{d}_{\mathsf{H}}(\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}},K_{\eta_{0}})\leq\varepsilon
, we have

\left\|\Proj_{K_{\eta_{0}}}({z}_{t})-\Proj_{\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}}}({z}_{t})\right\|\leq\varepsilon.

(71)

Since
\nabla\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}})=\frac{z_{t}-\Proj_{\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}}}({z}_{t})}{\left\|z_{t}-\Proj_{\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}}}({z}_{t})\right\|}
, we have
\left\|\nabla\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}})\right\|=1
. Note that for any
0\leq t<T
,

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}}),\,b(z_{t},t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}}),\,\frac{2\gamma}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}(m_{\eta_{0},T-t}(z_{t})-z_{t})-\frac{2(\gamma-1)}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}{\zeta}_{\eta,T-t}(z_{t})(m_{\eta,T-t}(z_{t})-z_{t})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left\langle\nabla\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}}),\,\frac{2\gamma}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}(\Proj_{\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}}}({z}_{t})-z_{t})-\frac{2(\gamma-1)}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}{\zeta}_{\eta,T-t}(z_{t})(\Proj_{{K}_{\eta}}({z}_{t})-z_{t})\right\rangle}_{\rm I}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\left\langle\nabla\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}}),\,\frac{2\gamma}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}(m_{\eta_{0},T-t}(z_{t})-\Proj_{{K}_{\eta_{0}}}({z}_{t}))-\frac{2(\gamma-1)}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}{\zeta}_{\eta,T-t}(z_{t})(m_{\eta,T-t}(z_{t})-\Proj_{{K}_{\eta}}({z}_{t}))\right\rangle}_{\rm II}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\left\langle\nabla\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}}),\,\frac{2\gamma}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}(\Proj_{{K}_{\eta_{0}}}({z}_{t})-\Proj_{\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}}}({z}_{t}))\right\rangle}_{\rm III}

To upper bound
\rm{III}
, note that
Eq.

71
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality imply that

\displaystyle{|\rm{III}|}\leq\frac{2\gamma\varepsilon}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}.

(72)

To upper bound
\rm{II}
, Lemma
A.2
implies that

\displaystyle{|\rm{II}|}\leq\frac{(4\gamma-2)\log\sigma_{T-t}^{-1}\sup_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}C_{K_{\eta}}}{\sigma_{T-t}},

(73)

where
C_{K_{\eta}}
is from Lemma
A.2
.
As for term
\rm{I}
, similar to the derivation of
Eq.

33
and using the upper bound
{d}_{\mathsf{H}}(\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}},K_{\eta_{0}})\leq\varepsilon
, we have

\displaystyle{\rm{I}}\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{2\gamma-2(\gamma-1)\zeta_{\eta_{0},T-t}(z_{t})}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\cdot\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}})+\frac{2\gamma-2}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\cdot\sum_{\eta\neq\eta_{0}}\zeta_{\eta,T-t}(z_{t})\cdot\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},K_{\eta})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{2\gamma-2(\gamma-1)\zeta_{\eta_{0},T-t}(z_{t})}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\cdot\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},K_{\eta_{0}})+\frac{2\gamma-2}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\cdot\sum_{\eta\neq\eta_{0}}\zeta_{\eta,T-t}(z_{t})\cdot\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},K_{\eta})+\frac{2\gamma\varepsilon}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}.

Following the same argument as in the derivation of
Eq.

45
, we conclude that

\displaystyle{\rm{I}}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2D_{0}(\gamma-1)\left|\mathcal{I}\right|}{w_{\eta_{0}}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{\eta_{0}}(x)\cdot\rho_{\eta_{0}}\Xi_{T-t}^{d}\sigma^{2}_{T-t}}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{3\Xi_{T-t}^{2}}{2\widetilde{\sigma}_{T-t}^{2}}\right)+\frac{4(\gamma-1)\Xi_{T-t}}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}-\frac{2}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},K_{\eta_{0}})+\frac{2\gamma\varepsilon}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}

(74)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2D_{0}(\gamma-1)\left|\mathcal{I}\right|}{w_{\eta_{0}}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{\eta_{0}}(x)\cdot\rho_{\eta_{0}}\Xi_{T-t}^{d}\sigma^{2}_{T-t}}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{3\Xi_{T-t}^{2}}{2\widetilde{\sigma}_{T-t}^{2}}\right)+\frac{4(\gamma-1)\Xi_{T-t}}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}-\frac{2}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}})+\frac{(2\gamma+2)\varepsilon}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}},

where we recall
D_{0}=\sup_{y,y^{\prime}\in\cup_{\eta\in\mathcal{I}}K_{\eta}}\left\|y-y^{\prime}\right\|
,
\Xi_{T-t}=\sqrt{\widetilde{\sigma}_{T-t}}
and
\widetilde{\sigma}_{T-t}^{2}=\lambda_{T-t}^{-2}\sigma_{T-t}^{2}
.
The above upper bounds apply to any
t\in[0,T)
.
Combining
Eqs.

72
,
73
and
74
, we see that there exists
t_{\varepsilon}>T-1/2
that depends only on
(p_{0},\varepsilon,\gamma)
,
such that for any
t\in[t_{\varepsilon},T)
and
\mathsf{dist}({z}_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}})>(2\gamma+3)\varepsilon
, it holds that

\displaystyle f(t,z_{t})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}}),\,b(t,z_{t})\right\rangle+e^{2(T-t)}\Delta\mathsf{dist}({z}_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}})

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\Big(2\,\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}})-(4\gamma+3)\varepsilon\Big)+e\,\mathcal{C}_{\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}}}\leq-\frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}},

where we recall that
\mathcal{C}_{\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}}}
is defined in Lemma
A.3
.
Consider the one-dimensional Itô process
(X_{t})_{\tau\leq t<T}
defined by

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-\frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t+e^{2(T-t)}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\qquad X_{\tau}=\mathsf{dist}(z_{\tau},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}}).

(75)

By a comparison theorem for SDEs
(
28
, Chapter VI, Theorem 1.1)
, we obtain that almost surely,

\displaystyle\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}})\leq X_{t},\quad\text{for all }t\in[\tau,\,\widetilde{T}),

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}=\min\big\{\inf\{s>\tau:\mathsf{dist}(z_{s},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}})=(2\gamma+3)\varepsilon\},\,T\big\}.

Therefore, it suffices to show that almost surely there exists
s\in[\tau,T)
, such that
X_{s}=(2\gamma+3)\varepsilon
.
This is equivalent to proving that almost surely
\widetilde{T}<T
.
Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\widetilde{T}=T\big)\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\inf_{\tau\leq t<T}X_{t}\geq(2\gamma+3)\varepsilon\Big)\leq\inf_{s_{i}\to T^{-}}\,\mathbb{P}\Big(X_{s_{i}}\geq(2\gamma+3)\varepsilon\Big),

where
\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence in
[\tau,T)
that converges to
T
as
i\to\infty
.
In addition,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(X_{s_{i}}\geq(2\gamma+3)\varepsilon\Big)=\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathsf{dist}(z_{\tau},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}})-\int_{\tau}^{s_{i}}\frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}+\int_{\tau}^{s_{i}}e^{2(T-t)}\mathrm{d}W_{t}\geq(2\gamma+3)\varepsilon\Big),

which converges to zero as
i\to\infty
.
This proves that
\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{T}=T)=0
, completing the proof.

C.9
Proof of Lemma
C.6

If there does not exist
t\geq t_{\varepsilon}
such that
\mathsf{dist}({z}_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}})=a
, then
U_{[t_{\varepsilon},T)}=0
.
In what follows, we condition on the event
\{\exists\,t\geq t_{\varepsilon}:\mathsf{dist}({z}_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}})=a\}
.

Define
\mathsf{Hit}(a)=\inf\{t\geq t_{\varepsilon}:\mathsf{dist}({z}_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}})=a\}
.
Define a sequence of intervals
(I_{m})_{m\geq 1}
by

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\left[\mathsf{Hit}(a),\,\mathsf{Hit}(a)+\frac{T-\mathsf{Hit}(a)}{2}\right],

\displaystyle I_{m}

\displaystyle=\left[I_{m-1}^{r},\,I_{m-1}^{r}+\frac{T-I_{m-1}^{r}}{2}\right]\quad\text{for }m>1,

where we write
I_{m}=[I_{m}^{\ell},I_{m}^{r}]
.
By construction, the length of
\left|I_{m}\right|
is
(T-\mathsf{Hit}(a))/2^{m}
.
For each
m\geq 1
, define the event
E_{m}=\left\{U_{I_{m}}\geq 1\right\}
, where
U_{I_{m}}
denotes the number of upcrossings of
\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}})
from
a
to
2a
over the interval
I_{m}
.
We next show that
\mathbb{P}(E_{m}\text{ i.o.})=0
(i.o. stands for infinitely often).
Consider the one-dimensional Itô process
(X_{t})_{\tau_{m}\leq t<I_{m}^{r}}
defined by

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-\frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t+e^{2(T-t)}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\qquad X_{\tau_{m}}=a.

(76)

where
\tau_{m}
denotes the first time that
\mathsf{dist}(z_{t},\widetilde{K}_{\eta_{0}})=a
within the interval
I_{m}
conditioned on the event
E_{m}
.
Applying the SDE comparison theorem again, we see that

\displaystyle E_{m}\subseteq

\displaystyle\Big\{\,\max_{\tau_{m}\leq t\leq I_{m}^{r}}X_{t}\geq 2a\Big\}\subseteq\Big\{\,\max_{\tau_{m}\leq t\leq I_{m}^{r}}\int_{\tau_{m}}^{t}e^{2(T-s)}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\geq a\Big\}

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\Big\{\max_{I_{m}^{l}\leq t\leq I_{m}^{r}}\int_{I_{m}^{l}}^{t}e^{2(T-s)}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\geq a/2\,\Big\}\cup\Big\{\inf_{I_{m}^{l}\leq t\leq I_{m}^{r}}\int_{I_{m}^{l}}^{t}e^{2(T-s)}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\leq-a/2\,\Big\}.

Note that the quadratic variation of the process
\int_{I_{m}^{l}}^{t}e^{T-s}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}
is upper bounded by

\int_{I_{m}^{l}}^{t}e^{2(T-s)}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq\int_{I_{m}^{l}}^{I_{m}^{r}}e^{2(T-s)}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq e\left|I_{m}\right|.

Applying Bernstein’s maximal inequality (see
(
42
, Section 2.3)
or
(
46
, Exercise 3.16 Chapter IV)
), we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(E_{m}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{\tau_{m}\leq t\leq I_{m}^{r}}X_{t}\geq 2a\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{\tau_{m}\leq t\leq I_{m}^{r}}\Big[\int_{\tau_{m}}^{t}-\frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma_{T-t}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\tau_{m}}^{t}e^{2(T-s)}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\Big]\geq a\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{\tau_{m}\leq t\leq I_{m}^{r}}\int_{\tau_{m}}^{t}e^{2(T-s)}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\geq a\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big\{\max_{I_{m}^{l}\leq t\leq I_{m}^{r}}\int_{I_{m}^{l}}^{t}e^{2(T-s)}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\geq a/2\,\Big\}\cup\Big\{\inf_{I_{m}^{l}\leq t\leq I_{m}^{r}}\int_{I_{m}^{l}}^{t}e^{2(T-s)}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\leq-a/2\,\Big\}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big\{\max_{I_{m}^{l}\leq t\leq I_{m}^{r}}\int_{I_{m}^{l}}^{t}e^{2(T-s)}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\geq a/2\,\Big\}\Big)+\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big\{\inf_{I_{m}^{l}\leq t\leq I_{m}^{r}}\int_{I_{m}^{l}}^{t}e^{2(T-s)}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\leq-a/2\,\Big\}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{a^{2}}{4e\left|I_{m}\right|}\right)=2\exp\left(-\frac{a^{2}\cdot 2^{m}}{4e(T-\mathsf{Hit}(a))}\right),

where the third inequality follows from the fact that
X_{t}
has a negative drift.
Therefore,

\sum_{m\geq 1}\mathbb{P}\left(E_{m}\right)\leq\sum_{m\geq 1}2\exp\left(-\frac{a^{2}\cdot 2^{m}}{4e(T-\mathsf{Hit}(a))}\right)<\infty.

The first Borel-Cantelli lemma implies that
\mathbb{P}\left(E_{m}\text{ i.o.}\right)=0
, completing the proof.
