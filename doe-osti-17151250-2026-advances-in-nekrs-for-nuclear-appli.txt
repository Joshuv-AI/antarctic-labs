 Summary

This report summarizes the principal fiscal year 2026 advances in NekRS for high-fidelity nu-
clear thermal-hydraulic simulation. The NekRS v26 release expands performance portability across
NVIDIA, AMD, and Intel GPU platforms; strengthens I/O, visualization, and analysis capabilities;
and adds features supporting production engineering and multiphysics applications. Major develop-
ments include geometric multigrid, enhanced NekNek multimesh coupling, improved coupling with
OpenMC through Cardinal/MOOSE, expanded turbulence and stabilization models, and improved
documentation and release testing.

 A central achievement is the optimization of the pressure Poisson solver, which typically
dominates the cost of incompressible-flow simulations and limits scalability at large GPU counts.
The improvements combine geometric h-multigrid levels, a matrix-free coarse-solver formulation, a
Schwarz smoother requiring one communication round instead of two, and a GPU implementation
of the direct XX T coarse solver. On the CEED BPS5 benchmark running on JUPITER, these
changes accelerate the pressure solve by 1.44× on 4,096 NVIDIA GH200 GPUs. At 4,096 GPUs,
the coarse-grid contribution is reduced from approximately 30% of the pressure-solve time to about
11%, corresponding to an estimated 25% reduction in overall Navier–Stokes solution time. These
results reiterate that reducing communication and host-side processing is essential to improving
performance at production scale, where data movement and synchronization increasingly outweigh
the cost of GPU computation.

1 Introduction

High-fidelity thermal-hydraulic simulation is an essential tool for improving the safety, performance,
and economic viability of current and advanced nuclear reactors. These systems involve tightly
coupled fluid-flow and heat-transfer phenomena across a wide range of spatial and temporal scales.
Accurately predicting localized conditions-, particularly hot spots, turbulent mixing, and thermal
loads, is critical for assessing safety margins, limiting material degradation, extending component
lifetimes, and supporting reactor designs that operate at higher temperatures.

 Because experiments cannot fully characterize reactor behavior across all relevant geometries
and operating conditions, predictive simulation plays a central role in the Nuclear Energy Advanced
Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS) program. High-fidelity calculations complement experiments
by resolving fine-scale physics and supplying data for reduced-order models, closure relations, and
system-level engineering tools. Realizing these benefits, however, requires more than numerical
accuracy. Production simulations must also use modern computing systems efficiently enough to
deliver results at the scales and turnaround times demanded by reactor analysis and design.

 NekRS is Argonne National Laboratory’s open-source, GPU-oriented computational fluid
dynamics code for incompressible and low-Mach-number flows, heat transfer, and related multiphysics
applications. Its high-order spectral-element discretization provides low numerical dissipation and
dispersion, while multilevel preconditioners, high-order time integration, adaptive communication
strategies, and performance-portable OCCA kernels enable execution across NVIDIA, AMD, and
Intel GPU platforms. These characteristics make NekRS well suited to the large, geometrically
complex turbulent-flow calculations required by nuclear energy applications.

 This report summarizes major advances incorporated into NekRS during fiscal year 2026. The
NekRS v26 release broadens performance portability, introduces a single-precision solver mode,
improves just-in-time compilation and large-scale I/O, and expands in situ visualization and analysis
capabilities. It also strengthens support for production engineering applications through new and
improved boundary conditions, stabilization methods, turbulence models, scalar transport options,
and multidomain coupling. Additional developments include geometric multigrid, expanded NekNek
multimesh functionality, conservative coupling with OpenMC through Cardinal/MOOSE, and more
comprehensive user documentation and release testing.

 A principal focus of this work is the performance of the pressure Poisson solver, which is
commonly the dominant computational cost in incompressible-flow simulations. In a previously
reported full-core pebble-bed reactor calculation, the pressure solve accounted for as much as
90% of the runtime before tuning. At large GPU counts, its coarse-grid component becomes
especially restrictive because it combines global communication with relatively little work per
process. Consequently, improving this solver directly affects the feasibility, cost, and turnaround
time of production-scale nuclear thermal-hydraulic simulations; faster individual kernels alone cannot
overcome the communication and host-side bottlenecks that emerge at these scales.

 The FY26 improvements address this limitation through four complementary changes: geometric
h-multigrid levels that reduce the size of the coarse problem, a matrix-free formulation that avoids
expensive coarse-level assembly and application, a Schwarz smoother requiring fewer communication

 1

rounds, and a GPU implementation of the direct XX T coarse solver. On the CEED BPS5 benchmark,
these changes accelerate the pressure solve by 1.44× on 4,096 NVIDIA GPUs. At 4,096 GPUs, the
coarse-grid contribution falls from approximately 30% of the pressure-solve time to about 11%. The
resulting improvement corresponds to an estimated 25% reduction in overall Navier–Stokes solution
time, demonstrating that communication-aware algorithm design and the removal of host-side work
can produce substantial application-level gains even when conventional GPU kernel optimization
offers limited benefit.

 Together, these developments make NekRS more capable, portable, and efficient for high-fidelity
nuclear reactor analysis. The following sections describe the v26 release and its testing, present the
additional multiphysics and multimesh capabilities developed during FY26, and provide a detailed
assessment of the pressure-solver optimizations and their performance on up to 4,096 GPUs.

2 NekRS v26 Release

The NekRS v26 release adds a broad set of capabilities that extend the code’s performance portability,
I/O and analysis infrastructure, and multi-domain coupling.

 A new FP32 solver mode enables single-precision execution of the solvers, which is important
for some of the new platforms on the DOE HPC horizon. A new DPCPP backend provides native
support for the Intel GPUs on ALCF’s Aurora and HIP support, for OLCF Frontier and other
AMD GPU-based platforms, has been added for the NekRS SEMFEM preconditioner [1]. JIT
compilation performance has been improved as well, which should reduce start-up time for NekRS.
A new iofld class manages reading and writing field files, including ADIOS2 support, which is a
possible pathway to overall improved I/O for large solution field files. Additional output options
include element filtering and interpolation onto uniform grids or different polynomial orders. An
Ascent plugin enables in situ visualization [2]. New SEM and mesh-surface operators, along with
surface shear-stress and pressure integration simplify common post-processing and analysis tasks.
The multidomain neknek framework has been substantially extended with multi-session (i.e.,
independent-executable) support, multi-rate time stepping, conjugate heat transfer (CHT) coupling,
and neknek handling in the nrsqsub job submission scripts.

 NekRS v26 provides new feature support for production engineering problems. The recycling
boundary condition for turbulent inflow conditions is now interpolation-based and supports arbitrary
fields. T-mesh support, which allows scalars to be solved on a combined fluid-solid domain, has been
extended to all scalars. Robin boundary conditions and linear implicit velocity source terms are now
available. Stabilization has been improved through a gradient jump penalty method [3] and better
regularization of the artificial viscosity method (AVM) [4]. Finally, the RANS k-τ model family
[5] has been expanded with SST, DDES, and IDDES variants, which enables hybrid RANS-LES
simulations within NekRS.

 The v26 release has been tested against the checklist below. Each item names a feature or
property the release supports and describes what the testing covers.

 • GPU backends. NekRS GPU kernels are written in the performance-portable OCCA

 2

  abstraction layer [6], which generates and compiles the kernels at runtime for the selected
  GPU hardware. V26 is built and run on all three GPU backends: CUDA (NVIDIA), HIP
  (AMD), and DPC++/SYCL (Intel). Kernel variants (addressing platform-specific performance
  optimization) are checked against generic OCCA versions for correctness. This testing avoids
  performance regression that otherwise shows up at runtime only.

• ASAN/Valgrind clean, including memcheck and GPU racecheck. NekRS host code is
  run under AddressSanitizer and Valgrind to detect out-of-bounds accesses, use of uninitialised
  memory and leaks. On the device side, the CUDA Compute Sanitizer memcheck tool checks
  for invalid global and shared memory accesses, and racecheck detects data races on shared
  memory within kernels.

• Performance testing. Performance is tracked with representative cases (e.g., time-to-
  solution for the turbPipe example) with strong and weak scaling runs. Each measurement is
  compared with the previous release, built and run in the same environment, in order to flag
  code-performance regressions.

• Checkpointing and restart. Restart is tested in all supported variants: restarting at
  a different polynomial order N , which requires interpolating the solution to the new order
  (prolongation to a higher order or coarsening to a lower one); output on a regular grid;
  checkpoints written in double precision (FP64) for bit-accurate restart; and restart of low-
  Mach cases, which must also restore the thermodynamic pressure pth 0 .

• Shared Dirichlet–Neumann edge across two elements. When an edge is shared by
  two elements, one with a Dirichlet boundary face and the other with a Neumann face, the
  nodes on that edge must be treated as Dirichlet nodes in both elements. This case exercises
  the construction of the boundary mask during gather–scatter, where an error would leave the
  edge nodes unconstrained in one of the elements. A dedicated test case with this configuration
  verifies that the boundary values are imposed consistently and that the solution converges at
  the expected rate.

• hpfRT. The high-pass filter relaxation term (hpfRT) [7] stabilizes marginally-resolved sim-
  ulations, such as large-eddy simulations, by damping the highest polynomial modes of the
  solution. The strength of the damping and the number of filtered modes are set in the .par
  file. Release testing checks that hpfRT reproduces reference results for the velocity and the
  scalars, and that it behaves correctly together with other features such as restart and variable
  properties.

• AVM (+ CHT). The artificial viscosity method (AVM) [4] adds localised numerical diffusion
  to scalar fields where the solution is under-resolved, for example near sharp fronts, while
  leaving smooth regions untouched. It is tested on its own and in conjugate heat transfer
  (CHT) cases. The CHT combination is tested explicitly because the artificial viscosity must be
  applied consistently across the fluid–solid interface without disturbing the heat flux between
  the two regions.

• In-situ visualization. In-situ visualization lets simulation data be rendered or analysed
  while the run is in progress, without writing full field files to disk. For production runs, this
  matters because the volume of output for a large simulation can impede subsequent data

 3

  interrogation. Release testing of the in-situ interface verifies that correctness and that the
  run-time overhead is acceptable.

• Runtime control .upd). NekRS simulations can be controlled at runtime by placing an
  update file (.upd) in the case directory. NekRS checks for the file during the run and carries
  out the requested actions, for example writing a checkpoint, updating runtime settings or
  ending the run cleanly. Each supported action is tested on a running case, including correct
  behaviour when several ranks read the file and the run continues or stops as requested.

• Time averaging (tavg). The tavg module accumulates run-time averages of the solution
  fields and of their products, which are needed for turbulence statistics such as mean velocity
  profiles and Reynolds stresses. Tests check the averages against reference values and verify that
  the accumulated statistics survive a checkpoint and restart, so that averaging can continue
  across several jobs.

• Mesh-solve-only capability. For moving-mesh cases, NekRS can be run so that only the
  mesh-motion problem is solved, without advancing the flow. This is used to check the mesh
  deformation on its own, for example to confirm that the moved mesh stays valid, before
  running the coupled simulation. Tests confirm that the mesh solution matches the coupled
  run and that the flow fields are left untouched.

• Different numbers of boundary conditions for the velocity and temperature meshes.
  In conjugate heat transfer cases the temperature mesh covers both the fluid and the solid,
  while the velocity mesh covers only the fluid, so the two meshes generally carry different sets
  of boundary IDs. Tests verify that boundary conditions are mapped to the correct boundaries
  of each mesh and that the setup is rejected with a clear error message when the mapping is
  inconsistent.

• Multiple scalars. NekRS supports several transported scalars, each with its own boundary
  conditions, properties and solver settings. The existing Ethier test-case exercises multiple
  scalars but only varies their boundary conditions. The v26 test suite is extended so that
  scalars also differ in their properties and solver settings, to catch errors in which settings from
  one scalar are applied to another.

• Low-Mach for closed domains. The low-Mach formulation handles flows with large density
  variations (e.g., due to thermal dilation). In closed domains, the thermodynamic pressure can
  change in time and must be tracked as an unknown. Tests check the time evolution of the
  thermodynamic pressure and the conservation of mass against reference results.

• Low-Mach with spatially varying viscosity. When viscosity depends on temperature, it
  varies in space and the viscous term takes its full, variable-coefficient form. This combination
  is tested in low-Mach cases to verify that the extra terms introduced by the varying viscosity
  are included correctly and that the solution matches reference results.

• Robin boundary conditions (bcType = Robin). Robin boundary conditions prescribe a
  linear combination of the field value and its normal derivative at the boundary, which models,
  for example, convective heat transfer to a surrounding medium. Tests compare the results
  with analytical solutions and check the convergence rate under polynomial refinement.

 4

• Surface forces. Surface forces (e.g, wall shear, pressure drop, lift, and drag), are computed
  by integrating the pressure and viscous stresses over selected boundaries. The computed forces
  and their coefficients are validated against reference values for standard benchmarks such as
  flow past a cylinder, including the split into pressure and viscous contributions.

• opSEM. opSEM collects spectral-element operators, such as gradient, divergence and curl,
  that users can call from their own case code (.udf). Each operator is tested against analytical
  results on curved and distorted meshes, so that post-processing and user-defined source terms
  can rely on them.

• Environment variables (NEKRS CACHE LOCAL, NEKRS CACHE BCAST, etc.). These environ-
  ment variables control how NekRS handles its cache of compiled kernels and case files, for
  example whether each node keeps a local copy and whether files are distributed from one rank
  to all nodes at startup. On large systems this avoids thousands of ranks reading the same files
  from the shared file system simultaneously. Tests check each variable and its combinations,
  including startup at large node counts, where file-system contention is most likely.

• neknek with solid overlap only and different numbers of boundary IDs. neknek
  couples several overlapping mesh domains that exchange boundary data during the run. This
  test covers the case where the domains overlap only in the solid, as in some conjugate heat
  transfer setups, and where the velocity and temperature meshes have different numbers of
  boundary IDs. The test checks that interpolation between the domains is set up only where it
  is needed and that the coupled solution matches a single-domain reference.

• coarseSolver = smoother. With this option, the coarsest multigrid level is treated with
  the smoother instead of a direct or algebraic coarse solver. It is cheap and avoids the global
  communication of an exact coarse solve, at the cost of a weaker preconditioner. Tests verify
  that the option converges and report its iteration counts and cost compared with the other
  coarse solvers.

• coarseSolver = jpcg. This option approximates the coarse-grid problem with a Jacobi-
  preconditioned conjugate gradient iteration. It runs entirely on the GPU and needs no setup of
  an algebraic hierarchy. Tests check convergence and cost across problem sizes, in particular in
  the range where the communication overhead of the algebraic coarse solver becomes significant.

• User elliptic objects. Users can create their own elliptic solvers in the case code to solve
  additional Poisson or Helmholtz problems, for example for post-processing or for extra physics
  models. These solvers use the same discretization and preconditioners as the built-in pressure
  and velocity solvers. Tests check the setup, the solution against analytical results, and the
  interaction with the built-in solvers, including memory use and the choice of preconditioner.

• Cardinal use cases. Cardinal couples NekRS with the MOOSE framework, mainly for
  multiphysics simulations of nuclear reactors. Relevant Cardinal use cases not yet covered by
  the items above are under development and will be added to the release tests so that the
  release does not break the coupling.

• One-way-coupled neknek. In one-way coupling, data is passed from one domain to another
  but not back, for example when an upstream simulation provides inflow conditions for a

 5

 downstream one. The receiving domain does not influence the sending domain, which makes
 the coupling cheaper and allows the domains to use different settings. Tests check that data
 flows in one direction only and that the receiving solution matches a reference in which the
 same inflow is prescribed directly.

3 Additional Capabilities

This section describes four capabilities added in FY26 subsequent to the v26.0 release: geometric (h-)
multigrid support (Section 3.1), coupling of overlapping domains with NekNek (Section 3.2), mapping
of Monte Carlo (MC) solutions onto the NekRS mesh (Section 3.3), and improved documentation
(Section 3.4).

3.1 Geometric (h-) Multigrid

The pressure solve in NekRS is preconditioned by p-multigrid, which builds its hierarchy by lowering
the polynomial degree on a fixed mesh [8, 9, 10]. Once the lowest degree is reached, the remaining
problem is handed to a coarse-grid solver, and at large GPU counts that coarse solve becomes
the part of the preconditioner that limits scaling, as noted early on in [11, 12]. This release
extends the multigrid hierarchy to hp-multigrid. For cases in which the mesh has been refined by
recently-supported global h-refinement in Nek5000/RS, it is possible to develop coarse solutions on
the originating mesh, which have 1/8th the number of unknowns in the case of an oct-refined mesh
(and still fewer for finer degrees of refinement). The coarse levels are obtained by reversing the
refinement schedule with which the mesh was built, so the transfer between levels is effected with the
same tensor-product efficiency of the local spectral element restriction/prolongation operators. The
resultant coarse problem is sufficiently small that it can be solved using the communication-minimal,
direct, XX T solver originally developed in [11, 12]. Combined with the GPU port of this coarse
solver, the overall pressure solution time is reduced by a factor of 1.44× on 2048 GPU of JSC’s
JUPITER. More details of this new development are given in Section ??.

3.2 NekNek Multimesh Capability

The multimesh variant of NekNek can solve a problem on several (≥ 2) overlapping meshes at
once, each handled by its own NekRS instance on its own set of MPI ranks. The solutions are
coupled through the overlapping regions: at every time step each domain interpolates the solution
of its neighbours onto its own interface boundaries and uses it as a boundary condition. Complex
geometries can thus be built from simpler, independently-generated meshes. Moreover, parts of
a domain can move relative to the rest, as is required for rotating components. The new release
allows domains to overlap only in solid domains (i.e., without a need to exchange interface velocity
variables). Moreover, the velocity and temperature meshes of a domain may have different numbers
of boundary IDs. One-way coupling is now supported, where data passes from an upstream domain
to a downstream one but not back.

 6

3.3 NekRS Coupling to OpenMC

For many NEAMS applications, it is desirable to support high-fidelity thermal hydraulics directly
coupled to neutron transport codes such as OpenMC. NekRS is coupled to the Monte Carlo neutron
transport code OpenMC to capture this feedback. OpenMC computes the heat source, which is
mapped onto the NekRS mesh; NekRS computes the temperature and density fields, which are
mapped back onto the OpenMC geometry. The two codes are iterated until the solution converges.
OpenMC tallies its results over geometric cells, which are generally much coarser than the spectral
elements of NekRS, and its results carry statistical noise that must not be amplified by the transfer.
The new mapping method, via Cardinal/MOOSE, conserves the total heat source during the transfer
and averages the NekRS fields over each OpenMC cell for the return path.

3.4 Improved Documentation

NekRS documentation has been extended to cover the features of this release and to lower the
entry barrier for new users. New material includes a getting-started guide, a reference of all .par
options, and descriptions of the multigrid and coarse-solver settings. Example cases in the release
are also documented with purpose, setup, and expected results. For performance-critical settings
(e.g., choice of coarse solver and the number of h-multigrid levels) the documentation explains
trade-offs and gives recommendations.

4 Optimization of the Pressure Solver for Production Runs

The compressible Navier-Stokes equations model a multiscale physics problem in which acoustic
(compressible) waves move much faster than the characteristic fluid speed, U and thus place an
acoustic CFL (Couran-Friedrichs-Lewy) constraint on the time step size, which must scale like
∆t ≲ ∆x/a, where a ≫ U is the local sound speed. To bypass this constraint, the incompressible
Navier-Stokes equations (INSE) impose a divergence-free constraint on the velocity field, which is
the mass-conservation equation in the case where the fluid density is constant. This model gives
rise to the pressure-Poisson equation as a substep in time-advancement of the INSE.

 Because it represents the action of the fastest time-scale in this physical situation, the pressure
solve is also invariably the most ill-conditioned system in the overall time advancement and therefore
the principal consumer of compute cycles. For example, [13] demonstrates that 90% of the runtime
was spent in the pressure solve for a full-core pebble-bed reactor with 352K pebbles and 50B
grid-points on P =27648 NVIDIA V100 GPUs of OLCF’s Summit. With tuning, it was possible
to cut the runtime and pressure time by roughly half, which means that 80% of the time was still
spent in the pressure solve. Having a fast, multilevel, pressure solver is therefore of paramount
importance when solving large scale thermal-hydrualics problems.

 In this section, we discuss recent developments for the NekRS coarse-grid solver, which is at
the bottom of a multigrid hierarchy, which has the fine, full, mesh (comprising billions of unknowns)
at the top level and relatively few (103 –105 ) unknowns at the coarsest level. It is important to

 7

recognize that the pressure solve involves all-to-all communication because every point in the domain
is susceptible to motion if there is (say) an expansion at one point in the domain. The coarse-grid
solve is communication intensive for this very reason; it’s all-to-all (i.e., has costly communication),
but there is relatively little work on each MPI rank.

 Here, we present the results of a comprehensive performance analysis of the NekRS Poisson
solver on the Julich Supercomputer Center (JSC) Jupiter system, using up to 4096 NVIDIA GH200
GPUs.

4.1 Methodology

All measurements use the CEED BPS5 benchmark [14], a Poisson solve on a Kershaw-distorted mesh,
with ∼16 000 spectral elements per MPI rank for each case.1 The Poisson solver configuration is the
production one: p-multigrid over polynomial degrees N =7, 3 and 1, additive Schwarz smoothing
with fourth-kind optimal Chebyshev acceleration, and algebraic multigrid (BoomerAMG) on the
coarse grid [10]. After a warm-start for each configuration, the benchmark repeats the solve 30
times internally. Only the minimum is evaluated, which removes system noise. The iteration count
is reported with its standard deviation. The original case and the variation are built identically and
run on the same nodes.

4.2 Work performed

4.2.1 Geometric (h-) multigrid levels

Geometric (h-) coarsening extends the existing p-multigrid hierarchy to hp-multigrid with the added
levels coarsened after reaching the N =1 mesh, which leads to at least an 8-fold reduction in the
coarse mesh size (e.g., ∼ 2000 degrees-of-freedom per GPU in this study).

4.2.2 Coarse-solver formulations

Matrix-free finest coarseSolver = boomerAMG+matrixFreeFinest avoids assembling and applying
the most expensive level of the AMG hierarchy: the coarse correction is evaluated on the reduced
operator, and the smoothing role of that level is taken over by the solver’s own matrix-free smoother,
which runs on the GPU on data already resident there. Two further options were implemented:
AMG coarse solve executed on the device, and aggregation of the coarse system onto one rank per
node.

4.2.3 Schwarz smoother

The additive Schwarz smoother required two communication rounds per application; it now performs
prolongation and assembly in a single round, verified against the original operator. The restriction
step additionally avoids one assembly per multigrid level. Since the smoother is applied on every
level of every cycle, this is the largest single saving that requires no change of solver configuration.

4.2.4 Direct coarse solver (XX T ): GPU port

Nek5000/RS contains a direct parallel sparse direct coarse solver, XX T [11, 12], which completes
in 2 log2 P message cycles, on par with the cost of a single all-reduce. XX T solves the coarse
system exactly and therefore needs the fewest iterations of all configurations examined, but in the
original implementation, the local work ran on the host, which made it too expensive to exploit
the communication savings. In the revised version the local work is moved to the GPU while the
communication was left unchanged. The coarse-grid time fell by 58% at 256 GPUs and by 80%
at 1024 GPUs, with identical iteration counts in every comparison. This is what makes the direct
coarse solver the fastest configuration at scale; without it the same configuration is slower than the
AMG alternative despite needing fewer iterations.

5 Conclusions

This report summarizes major advances delivered through the NekRS v26 release and subse-
quent FY26 development activities. These advances expand the code’s performance portability,
multiphysics and multidomain capabilities, production-oriented feature set, release testing, and
user documentation. Together, they strengthen NekRS as a reliable and accessible platform for
high-fidelity nuclear thermal-hydraulic analysis on current and emerging exascale computing systems.

 A principal achievement is the substantial improvement in the performance and scalability of the
pressure Poisson solver, which remains the dominant computational cost in many incompressible-flow
simulations. The optimized solver achieves speedups of 1.44× on 4,096 NVIDIA GH200 GPUs. At
4,096 GPUs, the coarse-grid contribution is reduced from approximately 30% of the pressure-solve
time to about 11%, contributing to an estimated 25% reduction in overall Navier–Stokes solution time.
These gains are enabled by geometric h-multigrid, a matrix-free coarse-level formulation, reduced
communication in the Schwarz smoother, and GPU acceleration of the communication-minimal
XX T direct coarse-grid solver.

 The results demonstrate that performance improvements at production scale depend increasingly
on reducing communication, synchronization, and host-side processing rather than solely accelerating
computational kernels. By addressing these system-level bottlenecks, the FY26 developments
make larger and more detailed simulations practical while reducing their computational cost and
turnaround time.

 Collectively, these advances represent an important step toward the efficient and accurate
simulation of complex nuclear energy systems, including transient and multiphysics phenomena.
They also complement broader efforts in high-fidelity reactor modeling and reinforce the central role
of NekRS in advancing scalable computational fluid dynamics for nuclear science and engineering.
