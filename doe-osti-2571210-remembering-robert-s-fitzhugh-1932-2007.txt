 Remembering Robert S. Fitzhugh
 1932-2007
 George Chandler, Jake Perea, Francis Honey, Lyle Kerstiens, Jay Wechsler, Roger Meade

Robert S. Fitzhugh, a Laboratory pioneer and mainstay of the Laboratory’s nuclear
testing program, died January 7, 2007, just two days after celebrating his 85th birthday.
An engineer dedicated to craft, Fitz was one of the longest serving Laboratory employees
and one of the most respected. His pension, because of his long tenure, was higher than
his salary. Born January 5, 1922, in Philadelphia, Fitz graduated high school in 1939 and
from Michigan State University with a BS in Electrical Engineering in June 1943. He
enlisted in the United States Army in May 1943 and, after completing basic training,
attended the University of Iowa as part of the Army Specialized Training Program.
When the ASTP program was disbanded in early 1944, Fitz was sent to Columbia
University as a laboratory technician and then on to Oak Ridge, where he worked on the
thermal diffusion program. Fitz did not like Oak Ridge, describing the Zeppelin-like
hanger he worked in as “a horrible place.”

Fitz came to Los Alamos in May 1945 as part of the Army’s Special Engineer
Detachment. The SEDs, enlisted personnel who had technical and scientific training,
were sent to Los Alamos to fill a manpower shortage in the technical ranks. Assigned
initially to work on timing and firing problems, Fitz was sent to tiny Bingham, New
Mexico, on July 16, 1945, to monitor fallout from the Trinity Test. Like many other
SEDs assigned to Los Alamos, Fitz continued working at the Lab after the war ended.
When finally mustered out of the Army in March 1946, he was immediately hired by the
Laboratory as a junior scientist in group M-4, where he worked on the development and
application of techniques for testing the hydrodynamic characteristic of bomb models. As
his group leader, Stanley Burris, noted, “He was largely responsible for improvements in
techniques by which the data obtained can be increased by a factor of ten to twelve.” As
Burris also noted, “The quantity and quality of his work is excellent.”

In 1951, Fitz joined J-15 to take over the high-speed camera work on weapon tests at the
Nevada Proving Ground. Less than a year later, he took his cameras to the Pacific
Proving Ground for the Mike shot, the first hydrogen bomb. His key contribution to the
entire atmospheric test program was the use of high-speed photography as an
indispensable tool for understanding nuclear detonations. With the move to underground
testing, Fitz became an expert on data acquisition and analysis of ground motion effects.
He also became a mentor for the engineers of Group J-8. His “Fitz Lists,” which detailed
upcoming J-8 Nevada Test Site events, became famous for their extraordinary clarity and
precision. He was, as one colleague recalled, “one of the best Instrumentation Engineers”
that he had been privileged to work with. As computers became a major part of
instrumentation, Fitz became more and more frustrated and never personally used them.
In his view they just got in the way of his work. However much he disliked computers, he
did realize their utility in analyzing data. Fitz found a young technician to act as his
“organic terminal” permitting him to use computers by talking to the tech. Fitz retired in

1991 with forty-five years of experience. However, he continued as a Lab Associate and
guest scientist for many years.

In addition to his great attention and dedication to his technical career, Fitz had another
passion – the outdoors. He spent lunch hours hiking the many trails around Los Alamos
and riding his horses through Northern New Mexico on the weekends, often coming to
work on Monday with bite and kick marks from one his horses. In July 1946, an Army
buddy and long-distance runner, Jay Wechsler, accepted the challenge of racing against
one of Fitz’s horses. Wechsler thought it was possible to beat a horse over a long
distance. On July 28, 1946, Fitz and his horse Thunder beat Wechsler quite handily.
Every summer thereafter, Fitz took great pride in sending a copy of the newspaper
account to Wechsler as a reminder of the race.

Fitz’s stable in La Puebla was a gathering spot for several generations of neighborhood
kids, whom he mentored as father and grandfather. Every Christmas he would take a
carload of these kids to Santa Fe to shop for gifts for their families. He was known
throughout the La Puebla Community for his generous spirit and helping hand.

Fitz will always be remembered by his J-8 colleagues for his humor, the use of a blue
lead pencil to take notes, and his wool pants, which he wore year-round. Most of all he
will be remembered for his appreciation of everything and everyone around him.
