 Uncertainty in Dynamic Compression of
 Materials: The Shock Hugoniot
 Philip Rae

Conclusions
It is demonstrated that even using the best current techniques for generating
a dynamic shock compression Hugoniot curve, the e↵ects from small changes
in alloying elements or compressibility are difficult to discriminate from each
other.

Explanation
The shock Hugoniot defines the compression end states reachable via a single
shock for a given material. It is therefore one measure of dynamic compressibil-
ity. At higher pressures of most interest for nuclear weapons it is not possible to
directly measure the pressure and volume (PV) reached during a shock process.
Instead, other state variables (usually particle velocity and shock velocity) are
measured and used to calculate the PV state. Although particle velocity can be
measured to a precision of ⇡0.1%, shock velocity is difficult to measure to better
than 0.5-1%. Even after fitting a suitable curve through the Hugoniot points
residual uncertainty of >0.5% remains. The changes to dynamic compressibility
resulting from small changes in alloy composition are sufficiently small that they
cannot reasonably be resolved from fitted curves. The situation is slightly better
for small changes in compressibility (⇡2%) at the highest pressures (>300 MPa
or 3MBar), but experimentally these are challenging pressures to reach using
precision apparatus. Thus, the shock Hugoniot is a poor method for resolving
small changes in compressibility between samples.

Discussion
The dynamic compression of materials, particularly metals, has obvious rele-
vance to nuclear weapons design. However, as will be demonstrated here, high
precision and accuracy measurements of the compressibility of even simple met-
als at high pressures (>100 GPa, 1 Mbar) is experimentally challenging.

 As an example, we will consider the shock Hugoniot of simple, chemically
unreactive, non-toxic, readily available and pedigreed metals (i.e., Ta, Cu and
Al). The Hugoniot is the name given to the locus of end states of compression
that can be reached by a single shock. An example in pressure/volume space
(PV space) is shown in figure 1. Additionally, these simple metals do not exhibit
phase transitions in this pressure regime (measuring Hugoniots for metals that
do adds considerable additional complexity). Importantly, the Hugoniot curve is
not a thermodynamic path unlike the isentrope which is also commonly plotted
in PV space.
 At higher pressures (>50 GPa, 0.5 Mbar) it is very hard to directly measure
pressure or volume accurately. Instead, other parameters are measured and the
PV curve calculated from the jump equations1 . Typically the shock velocity
(Us ) associated with a shock particle velocity (Up ) is what is actually measured
to define a Hugoniot. At lower pressures, embedded electrical pressure gauges
(either piezoelectric or piezoresistive) can be used, but these fail at higher pres-
sures both because the dielectric materials required to isolate the gauge become
conductive, and because the physical strains imposed by the large compressions
tear the electrical connections.
 Volume or density can be measured by flash X-ray di↵raction or transmission
methods, but these are seldom accurate to more than a few percent and are
challenging to field in many experimental facilities, particularly when make
short exposure (< 1µs) measurements of dense metals.
 Historically, high pressure shocks were imparted into test materials using
direct explosive drive. This is suboptimal because although the peak initial
pressure can be accurately found, the following Taylor flow releases the pres-

sure in a complex explosive- and geometry-dependent manner. Later, thin metal
plates were accelerated by explosives and these theoretically impart a flat-topped
pressure wave followed by a smooth isentropic release. In practice, significant
trade-o↵s are required. Principally, throwing very flat plates is difficult with
explosives. Curved (convex) plates striking flat target test materials impart
time-dependent pressure and shear waves invalidating the requirements for ac-
curate measurements of pure 1D plane strain conditions. The greater the throw
distance of the metal plate before striking the target the more explosive energy
is coupled to the plate, but also the greater the curvature. Alternatively, short
throws keep the plates flatter but the plate is still being accelerated by the
explosive, invalidating the flat topped pressure requirement.
 More recently, the ‘gold-standard’ tests have been flat plate gas-gun experi-
ments (aka. plate impact facilities). In these, a very flat and parallel impactor
is mounted onto a sabot and this strikes a flat and parallel instrumented target
at a precisely measured velocity. In this way a very flat-topped pure 1D plane
strain wave is imparted into the test material. In the highest precision tests
the impactor and target are made of the same material. In this case, symmetry
ensures that the particle velocity imparted into the target is exactly half that of
the impactor. In other tests, the impactor can be made of a di↵erent material to
either generate a greater or lesser pressure than achievable otherwise; however,
the uncertainties in the Hugoniot of the impactor then have to be factored into
the overall error budget for the new Hugoniot point being measured.
 The velocity of the projectile can relatively easily be measured to a precision
of ⇡0.1 %. In a symmetric impact this leads to a similar small uncertainty
in Up . Measuring the shock velocity is more challenging. There is no good
direct gauge for this parameter. Instead, the time it takes for the shock to
traverse a known distance is measured. For these calculations, knowing the
exact time di↵erence, target thickness and planarity of impact is vital. This is
hard with metals that can be lapped and glued on a laboratory bench. It is much
harder for materials where a controlled gas atmosphere must be maintained
(i.e., they are oxygen reactive) or are highly-toxic or -radioactive. Additionally,
for these difficult to work with materials, symmetric impacts are generally not
performed to minimize the mass of the difficult material. This imparts an
increased uncertainty into the overall accuracy of the results for metals that are
more challenging to work with.
 Some of the most precise measurements of the high pressure Hugoniot for
Cu, Al and Ta were undertaken by LLNL in the late 70s and early 80s [1](see
appendix A). These were mostly symmetric impacts performed in a 2-stage gas
gun2 and great care was taken to measure the shock velocity precisely. A careful
error analysis was also included and this demonstrated absolute Hugoniot point
accuracy values of between 0.5-1.2% and fit residuals to the full data set of 0.6-
0.9%. An example for Ta is shown in figure 2 together withe 95% confidence
bands for the least squares fit through the data. It is worth reiterating that

 Despite these high quality data, the following exercise will demonstrate a
problem with detecting small variations in response from subtly di↵erent alloys
using these methods. Consider a hypothetical alloy comprising 99% Ta and 1%
Cu (Ta99Cu1). Clearly both the density and shock Hugoniot for this alloy will
di↵er slightly from pure Ta. The question is, can we reliably distinguish the
behavior of Ta99Cu1 in the Hugoniot?
 Density can be measured relatively easily to four significant figures using
techniques such as immersion density or helium pycnometry. With great care,
precision can approach five significant figures. A number of spectroscopic or
solution chemistry techniques would be able to assay and quantify the di↵erences
between Ta and Ta99Cu1 with <0.1% precision. Therefore techniques exist that
can distinguish small changes in alloys, but these are not directly relevant to
dynamic compressibility. High quality shock Hugoniot data for Ta99Cu1 are
not available. Instead we shall create some synthetic data using the ratio of
masses rule of mixtures [2].
 Validation of this simplified approach is demonstrated using the following
example. Muntz metal is a brass alloy comprising 60% Cu and 40% Zn and
Hugoniot data are available. Data for pure Cu and pure Zn were taken from the
same source [3] and the rule of mixtures applied. For most metals the following
linear equation is found to fit the relationship between Us and Up ,

Using the values for pure Cu and Zn and the rule of mixtures by mass fraction,
the values for estimated Muntz metal C0 and s were found to be within 1.3% of
those fitted to the real Muntz metal measurements, see table 1. As explained
earlier, the absolute accuracy of each individual Cu, Zn and Muntz data point
were probably several percent.
 With this in mind, the linear fit from equation 1 for pure Ta is C0 =3.293
and s=1.307 and the estimated values for Ta99Cu1 are C0 =3.299 and s=1.309.
With the small adjustment for density, a curve for Ta99Cu1 is shown in figure 3
compared with the previous data for pure Ta. It will be seen that the curve for
the new alloy lies within the 95% confidence interval for the curve of best fit
for pure Ta. Assuming a similar uncertainty for the hypothetical alloy, it will
be difficult, if not impossible, to demonstrate a statistically valid discrepancy
between the two metals. This appears to be an inherent limitation of making
dynamic compression measurements in this way. However, to date a signifi-
cantly better method of generating high-pressure Hugoniot data has not been
demonstrated.
 Finally, a hypothetical case is considered where the Hugoniot for Ta is artifi-
cially sti↵ened by 2%. Figure 4 shows the e↵ect of increasing s by 2% compared
with the line of best fit for Ta from real data (i.e., s=1.333 rather than s=1.307).
Also included are the 95% confidence limits for the original data and reason-
able confidence limits for the sti↵ened data. It will be seen that measuring a
statistically meaningful di↵erence in the response of these two materials would
be challenging, even for this well behaved and easy to work with material. The
sti↵ening has greatest e↵ect at large pressures (>3Mbar) where discrimination
with enough data might be possible. The projectile velocity required for sym-
metric impacts to achieve these pressures is greater than 6 km/s. Two stage
guns capable of this performance are available for easy to handle metals, but
two stage guns capable of these velocities for more challenging materials are
exceptionally rare.

 6

 In summary, it is demonstrated that small variations in metal compressibility
are difficult to discriminate using even precise Hugoniot data.

 Hugoniot curves for Al (alloy 1100), Cu (type oxygen-free high-conductivity), and Ta have been
 measured in the shock pressure range 30-430 GPa (0.3-4.3 Mbar) with a two-stage light-gas gun.
 Impactor velocities were measured to 0.1 % by flash radiography. Shock velocities were measured
 to 0.5-1.2% with an electronic detection system with subnanosecond time resolution. Our data
 and those of other workers were fitted to a linear relation between shock and mass velocities. The
 fractional standard deviations of the data from the fits range from 0.6 to 0.9% for the three metals.
 Methods of data analysis and error analysis for individual data points and for the least-squares
 fitting to the data sets are presented. Bands of uncertainty about the fits, arising from
 experimental uncertainties in the data, are presented and are used to calculate the systematic error
 introduced by the method of shock-impedance matching. The accuracy of the data and of the fits
 qualifies these metals as equation-of-state standards for shock-wave experiments.
