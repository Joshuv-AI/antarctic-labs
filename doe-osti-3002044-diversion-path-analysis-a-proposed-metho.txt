 1. INTRODUCTION

Nuclear material control and accounting (MC&A) is a critical element of both the US Nuclear Regulatory
Commission (NRC) and US Department of Energy (DOE)’s 1 domestic safeguards and security
requirements. NRC licensees are required, under Title 10 of the Code of Federal Regulations (10 CFR)
Part 74 to establish and maintain an MC&A program that captures and records the quantities and
locations of special nuclear material (SNM) at the facility. Along with physical protection, MC&A is a
key element of domestic nuclear material safeguards that enables the NRC to ensure that SNM is
controlled and accounted for. SNM, per 10 CFR Part 74,refers to plutonium, 233U, and uranium enriched
in the isotope 233U or 235U, but does not include source material [1].

Periodic physical inventories, coupled with material balance evaluations, are effective and demonstrated
tools to account for and detect theft or diversion of SNM in facilities containing SNM in bulk material
form (i.e., not in discrete, countable items). Historically in the United States, these types of facilities have
included fuel fabrication, conversion, and enrichment facilities. In comparison, reactors have relied on
item counting of assemblies and control of SNM while in containment (e.g., a sealed reactor pressure
vessel) because, to date, reactor fuel has been in item form. In liquid-fueled molten salt reactors (MSRs),
unlike traditional light water reactors (LWRs) or bulk facilities, bulk SNM quantities can change
significantly during operation as a result of depletion and transmutation. This introduces challenges to the
use of traditional periodic physical inventories and material balance evaluations to detect theft or
diversion of SNM in reactors that use SNM in bulk material form.

Liquid-fueled (i.e., salt-fueled) MSR facilities are MSRs that use SNM within a salt eutectic as the fuel.
The SNM is in a bulk material form any time it is outside of fresh or spent fuel storage containers. Some
examples of when SNM will be in bulk form in the facility are during addition of fuel to the reactor
system, while fuel is circulating in operation, and while fuel is in a drain tank. Periodic physical
inventories and material balance evaluations can likely be effectively applied to many portions of an MSR
facility, including all areas where depletion and transmutation are not significantly changing the quantities
of SNM within the control area. Within an MSR facility, this would include fresh fuel receipt and
loading, waste streams that may contain SNM, irradiated fuel storage outside of the reactor core, and any
irradiated fuel processing that may happen after SNM has been removed from the reactor. All of these
process steps could rely on measurements of SNM quantities compared with documented inventories.
Any discrepancies from predicted (i.e., book) inventories and measured inventories could be quantified as
inventory differences, consistent with traditional MC&A guidance from the NRC (e.g., in NUREG-1065
Revision 2, NUREG-2159 Revision 1, and RG 5.29 Revision 2) [2,3,4].

Within the reactor system, additions and removals to the book inventory include depletion of the SNM
(e.g., fission of 235U), which complicates the use of physical inventories. SNM control, however, can also
likely be effectively applied to detect theft of SNM throughout a liquid-fueled MSR facility [5,6]. To
complement these approaches, prior technical reports have identified that a diversion path analysis may
be a useful, risk-informed, and performance-based tool to determine suitable elements of an MC&A
approach for the reactor system within a liquid-fueled MSR facility [7].

To effectively leverage SNM control measures, Hogue et al. have suggested using diversion path analysis
to determine where SNM could enter and/or leave the reactor system. Although there is no universal
definition of diversion path analysis, no NRC or DOE requirement, and no existing guidance to perform
diversion path analysis as a step in developing a MC&A program, both the NRC and the DOE have
referred to diversion path analysis. The NRC referenced diversion path analysis as a way to address
safeguards and security aspects during an analysis it conducted of 10 CFR as it relates to spent fuel
reprocessing. In an enclosure related to a gap analysis performed by the NRC, titled “Safeguards and
Security Issues”, five regulatory gaps related to the development of regulations for spent nuclear fuel
reprocessing facilities were identified [8,9]. One of those gaps was diversion path analysis, and the NRC
states that “currently, there are no existing regulations for a diversion path analysis requirement in 10
CFR Part 74. Establishing diversion path analysis requirements could make 10 CFR Part 74 more risk-
informed and would provide an effective detection and response program to mitigate potential safeguards
vulnerabilities and system weaknesses.”

There is also precedence for using a diversion path analysis as a tool for MC&A. A 1978 US DOE
handbook provides a methodology for a diversion path analysis, which it describes as “a safeguards
evaluation tool which is used to determine the vulnerability of the Material Control and Material
Accounting (MC&MA) Subsystems to the threat of theft of SNM by a knowledgeable Insider” [10]. The
handbook goes on to describe how the diversion path analysis should be executed: “The [diversion path
analysis] team should consist of two individuals who have technical backgrounds. The implementation of
[diversion path analysis] is divided into five basic steps: Information and Data Gathering, Process
Characterization, Analysis of Diversion Paths, Results and Findings, and Documentation.”

 2

A diversion path analysis could serve as a tool to identify MC&A elements in certain process areas of
nontraditional facility types, including within the internal control area 2 of a liquid-fueled MSR. It could
be useful in identifying compensatory measures to complement material control and, for example,
physical inventories that have higher than desired uncertainties due to challenging measurement
environments like those expected within reactor confinement in an MSR. Diversion path analysis is
consistent with a risk-informed performance-based regulatory approach. An MC&A approach developed
using a thorough diversion path analysis may give a regulator confidence that the goals of domestic
safeguards, and MC&A specifically, are met with the MC&A elements included in a proposed MC&A
approach.

 2. POTENTIAL USE OF A DIVERSION PATH ANALYSIS

The goal of domestic safeguards is to protect against the sabotage, theft, and diversion of SNM and to
ensure that SNM “within the United States is not stolen or otherwise diverted from civilian facilities for
possible use in clandestine fissile explosives and does not pose an unreasonable risk owing to radiological
sabotage” [11]. The NRC accomplishes this through physical protection and MC&A. A diversion path
analysis would only be useful for meeting objectives related to preventing or detecting theft or diversion.
Physical protection is more suitable to prevent sabotage of nuclear facilities and to investigate and recover
missing SNM. However, a diversion path analysis may help identify technical aspects (e.g., the level of
effort or technical difficulty) relevant to sabotage.

The goal of the diversion path analysis methodology is to determine plausible paths to remove SNM (e.g.,
through a sampling port) and identify MC&A and other process control elements, including
measurements, containment, or surveillance, to prevent or detect each plausible path. These MC&A
elements (i.e., weighing systems, level sensors, gamma spectrometers, electrochemical sensors, optical
spectroscopy instruments) could then be used to build an MC&A approach or to provide justification
(e.g., to the NRC) for how domestic safeguards objectives are being met. Such compensatory measures
likely are needed because current NRC guidance is not tailored to a reactor facility with SNM in bulk
form.

As recommended in [7] and depicted in Figure 2, the following steps are necessary to define a
comprehensive MC&A approach:

 i. Identify MC&A objectives across the facility—For example, quantify SNM in all material
 entering the reactor system confinement or ensure no SNM is removed from irradiated waste
 storage.

 ii. Conduct diversion path analysis—Diversion path analysis is performed to identify plausible
 paths where SNM could be removed from the process and their associated indicators.

 iii. Identify appropriate MC&A elements–MC&A elements are assigned to prevent or detect each
 diversion path. These elements can be combined in the MC&A program to ensure that each SNM
 theft through diversion path would be prevented or detected (ideally through multiple detection
 methods).

 3. DIVERSION PATH ANALYSIS METHODOLOGY

A diversion path analysis methodology needs to be thorough and repeatable to provide a regulator with a
technically credible MC&A approach that contains elements to prevent or detect all plausible diversion
pathways. This report describes an initial methodology that aims to develop a repeatable framework to
conduct an effective diversion path analysis for a liquid-fueled MSR. This methodology has six steps: (1)
establish the SNM process flow, (2) identify potential diversion paths, (3) assess diversion paths, (4)
prioritize diversion paths, (5) identify indicators and signatures of diversion for each path, and (6) assign
MC&A elements to cover all plausible diversion paths. Additional research is needed to further
standardize and proceduralize a process that provides assurance to a regulator that the diversion path
analysis was thorough and that further considers which diversion paths are plausible.

3.1 ESTABLISH SNM PROCESS FLOW

The proposed methodology for a diversion path analysis starts with defining the flow of SNM through the
facility or internal control area that is being evaluated. This should include all aspects of the process flow
that will, or could, include SNM within the facility or internal control area. A process flow for a liquid-
fueled MSR facility could include fresh fuel receipt, fresh fuel storage, fresh fuel synthesis (e.g., if any
chemical synthesis is happening at the facility), fresh fuel addition (including any salt purification or
preparation, holding or accounting tanks), the reactor system, chemical processing of irradiated fuel (e.g.,
if the design includes chemical purification processes), off-gas handling and storage, irradiated salt
storage, irradiated salt conditioning or stabilization, and any analytical capabilities that might handle salt
samples or SNM that is removed from the reactor system. The diversion path analysis could be completed
for the facility in its entirety or for a single internal control area within the facility, such as the reactor
system.

Note, a diversion path analysis can be an effective tool to incorporate MC&A into the design process
during any design phase; however, if using this tool earlier in the design process, more assumptions will
need to be made because some of the diversion path analysis methodology relies on detailed process flow
that may not have been determined in, for example, a preconceptual design.

 4

3.2 IDENTIFY POTENTIAL DIVERSION PATHS

For each of the process flow areas, a team of experts should rigorously identify all plausible pathways for
SNM theft. This includes identifying how the SNM might be removed from the process flow (e.g.,
removal of an entire fresh fuel container) and any concealment that is plausible. The goal is to document
all diversion scenarios and any concealment methods that may have distinct indicators to enable the
identification of MC&A elements that can detect each of the indicators. Therefore, a diversion category
could have multiple different diversion scenarios with distinct indicators. For example, if the diversion
category is to divert an entire fresh fuel container, each of the following scenarios should be included as
distinct paths in a thorough diversion path analysis:

 i. Removal of an entire fresh fuel container without replacement

  ii. Removal of an entire fresh fuel container and replacement with a container that does not look
 visually the same

 iii. Removal of an entire fresh fuel container and replacement with a container that looks visually the
 same

 iv. Removal of an entire fresh fuel container and replacement with a container that looks visually the
 same and has the same gross full mass

  v. Removal of an entire fresh fuel container and replacement with a container that looks visually the
 same and has the same gross full mass and emits a radiation signature

3.3 ASSESS EACH DIVERSION PATH AGAINST METRICS

For this methodology, three objective metrics were defined to assess the plausibility of each diversion
path, with the goal of identifying the highest priority diversion paths and providing a basis to further
apply policy or judgement decisions about which paths might be considered plausible. These metrics—
SNM quantities, the technical difficulty, and time to obtain SNM from the process stream—were clearly
defined such that the assessment process can be repeated, and similar results can be achieved across
analyses. Each diversion scenario was analyzed against these metrics, which provided the impetus for
selection of MC&A elements.

Note that the metrics and categories defined in Table 1, Table 2, and Table 3 for the proposed metrics are
meant to be a starting point for the MC&A community to further define diversion path analysis. For
example, additional categories, like the attractiveness of the material that could be obtained through
diversion, may be useful. DOE already defines material attractiveness categories, but the NRC does not
[12].

3.3.1 SNM quantities

The first metric captures the quantities of SNM that might be diverted through a specific diversion path.
These categories were developed leveraging INFCIRC/225, though they do not explicitly align with the
SNM categories. Because diversion path analysis aims to identify plausible diversion pathways, these
loosely defined categories without bins of specific masses were effective. In the future, more sensitive
diversion path analyses could be conducted with more specific mass limits for each category. Table 1
includes the proposed categories to assess the quantities of SNM that could be obtained through the
specific diversion path.

The quantity of SNM obtained through a specific diversion path could depend on the length of time the
diversion was occurring. In other words, an adversary will obtain a greater quantity of SNM if they divert
material for 1 month compared to 2 hours. To ensure all diversion paths were captured, in some instances,
a scenario was captured twice—one assuming the diversion occurred over a short length of time and one
assuming it occurred over a longer period of time.

3.3.2 Technical difficulty

The second metric captures the technical difficulty of diverting material through a specific path. Both the
level of effort and technical difficulty were considered. For international safeguards applications, where
resource constraints may not be as limiting a factor because the adversary being considered is a State
actor, the level of effort metric is likely most relevant. However, for MC&A applications in domestic
safeguards that instead consider theft by non-State actors and/or individuals who might have access to, or
even be employed at, the facility, a metric capturing the technical difficulty of removing SNM from the
process stream through various diversion paths is most relevant. Since this report focuses on domestic
safeguards, the technical difficulty metric is further defined and explored.

Table 2 lists the proposed categories to assess the technical difficulty of removing SNM from a process
stream. In these categories, the technical knowledge of the system refers to the process steps for which the
material is being diverted from. That is, the individual may not need to have technical knowledge of the
entire facility or entire process flow but may just need to be aware of the system or process step from
which the material is being removed. For example, when assessing this metric for the diversion path of
theft of an entire fresh fuel container from fresh fuel storage, the technical knowledge of the individual
considered should include knowledge of the fresh fuel storage process and area.

 Low Requires one person who may or may not have technical knowledge of the system
 Medium Requires one person with technical knowledge of the system
 High Requires two or more people with technical knowledge of the system

3.3.3 Time to obtain SNM from process stream

The third metric assesses the time it would take to remove the SNM from the process stream. This does
not include any separation of the SNM from the material it is within or the time it may take to get the
SNM out of the building. For example, when considering theft of an entire fresh fuel container from a
fresh fuel storage area, the time it would take to remove the SNM from the process stream would be short
(i.e., less than 24 hours). Table 3 includes the proposed categories to assess the time to obtain material
from the process stream.

3.4 PRIORITIZE DIVERSION PATHS BASED ON METRICS AND PLAUSIBILITY

Once the diversion paths are assessed based on the above metrics, they can be prioritized based on
plausibility (i.e., how believable is it that based on a subjective assessment a path would be used to divert
material). This prioritization helps (i) determine whether or not certain diversion paths that were
identified should be considered plausible and (ii) efficiently tailor the MC&A plan toward robust
coverage of highest priority diversion paths. Based on the above metrics and categories, diversion paths
that have been assessed to be the most concerning for MC&A are those in which obtaining large
quantities of SNM is possible, removing the SNM from the process stream is not technically difficult, and
obtaining SNM can be accomplished in a short time.

There is opportunity for additional guidance to determine which diversion paths are not technically
plausible. A regulatory body, like the NRC, could provide guidance so that the technical assessment of
certain metrics in certain categories are considered implausible. For example, a diversion path at the
lowest risk categories as defined in Section 3.3 (i.e., small quantities of SNM, high technical difficulty to
remove SNM, long time to obtain SNM) might be considered implausible (or an unreasonable way to
obtain SNM), and therefore an MC&A program may not need to have distinct MC&A elements to
prevent or detect such a diversion path but may rather rely on other physical protection or MC&A
elements applied at the facility. Metric categories could be defined through regulatory requirements or
guidance such that there are thresholds above which a path must be covered. These categories should be
derived based on risk assessments, similar to a design basis threat (DBT), which the NRC defines as “a
description of the type, composition, and capabilities of an adversary, against which a security system is
designed to protect. The NRC uses the DBT as a basis for designing safeguards systems to protect against
acts of radiological sabotage and to prevent the theft of [SNM]. Certain nuclear facility licensees are
required to defend against the DBT [13].” DOE has established a DBT policy, DOE Order 470.3C, which
represents goals for the planning and implementation of safeguards and security programs by defining
aspects of credible threats that must be addressed through safeguards and security programs.

Currently, DBTs are primarily tailored to define requirements for physical protection programs, but they
could be leveraged to help identify diversion paths as well as assess the diversion path in the context of
this methodology’s metrics. Alternatively, different standards of rigor could be applied to different
plausibility categories. For example, a diversion path at the highest risk categories as defined in Section
3.3 could each be covered by two independent and distinct MC&A elements (e.g., a gamma detector to

3.5 DETERMINE INDICATORS OR SIGNATURES OF EACH DIVERSION PATH

After path prioritization, the next step recommended in a diversion path analysis is to consider the
indicators, or signatures, of each diversion path. For example, the indicator of theft of a fresh fuel
container (without replacement) is a missing container. The indicators of theft of a fresh fuel container
replaced with a container that looks visually the same are (i) a potential discrepancy in the serial number
of the container from records, (ii) a potential discrepancy in the gross full weight of the container with
MC&A records, and (iii) a missing or broken TID or a discrepancy in the serial number on the TID with
MC&A records.

3.6 ASSIGN MC&A ELEMENTS TO COVER ALL PLAUSIBLE DIVERSION PATHS

Once indicators or signatures for each diversion path are identified, MC&A elements can be specifically
chosen to detect any of those signatures. For example, one way to detect removal of an entire fresh fuel
container would be to conduct a physical inventory, counting the number of fresh fuel containers as items
and comparing this to the number that should be present. One way to detect removal of an entire fresh
fuel container and replacement with a container that looks visually the same is to verify a serial number
and TID (i.e., seal or cable lock around the container). While identifying MC&A elements to cover each
diversion path, a more holistic look at the MC&A program should be considered to gain cost and resource
efficiencies and use MC&A elements and equipment to cover more than one path.

 4. CONCLUSIONS

Periodic physical inventories, coupled with material balance evaluations, are fundamental tools to meet
NRC and DOE requirements to maintain knowledge of the quantities and location of SNM within a
nuclear facility. However, reactors introduce challenges to performing periodic physical inventories
because of lack of physical access to quantify SNM. In liquid-fueled MSRs, bulk SNM quantities can also
change significantly during operation because of depletion and transmutation, introducing further
challenges for conducting traditional periodic physical inventories and material balance evaluations to
detect theft or diversion of SNM. Reactors have traditionally relied on control during operation to ensure
that SNM has not been removed from within a physical boundary (e.g., reactor confinement or a reactor
pressure vessel). Liquid-fueled MSRs may benefit from a similar approach in which a robust control
boundary, ideally coupled with a physical boundary limiting access, is established around the reactor
system where depletion and transmutation are significantly changing the SNM quantities. This would
allow periodic physical inventories and material balance evaluations to be leveraged in parts of the
facility where the SNM is in item form, while still ensuring MC&A objectives are met throughout the
facility.

Diversion path analysis is a useful tool that identifies all pathways where SNM may be removed from the
process stream for areas, such as the reactor system within a liquid-fueled MSR, where control boundaries
are heavily relied on. This technical report suggests a diversion path analysis methodology that is
consistent with a risk-informed, performance-based approach to meeting regulatory requirements.

This technical report proposed a general methodology, including assessment metrics and categories for
quantities of SNM, technical difficulty of getting SNM from the process stream, and the amount of time
to obtain SNM are provided for consideration during a diversion path assessment on a liquid-fueled MSR

 8

to identify and for prioritizing plausible diversion paths and assigning MC&A elements as a part of a
broader MC&A program. This approach could be used to justify compensatory measures taken at a
liquid-fueled MSR facility for specific internal control areas (i.e., within the reactor system while
operational) to meet the intent of periodic physical inventories to ensure SNM has not been removed from
that area; an approach that is consistent with SNM control and with MC&A programs at traditional
LWRs.

Although both the NRC and DOE have referenced diversion path analyses previously, neither entity
requires a diversion path analysis to be performed and neither has current guidance for how a diversion
path analysis might be performed to meet regulatory requirements. Further guidance is needed by
regulators to establish how plausible diversion paths might be defined and to associate assessment metrics
with these definitions. Further consideration of whether material attractiveness categories (i.e., as defined
by the DOE) should be incorporated into a diversion path analysis is needed. Additionally, further
guidance on coverage of each path should be considered. A graded approach to coverage of plausible
diversion paths could be established. For example, a diversion path assessed through the metrics to be a
higher risk categories (i.e., large SNM quantities, low technical difficulty to remove SNM from the
process, and short time to obtain SNM) or involving higher material attractiveness categories may have
two independent and distinct MC&A elements to prevent or detect the diversion, whereas a diversion path
assessed through the metrics to be a lower risk category may only have one MC&A element to prevent or
detect it.
