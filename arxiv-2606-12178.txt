On the maximum number of vectors in { 0 , ± 1 } n \{0,\pm 1\}^{n} with forbidden inner products
Ilya Lobatskii, Yakov Shubin

Abstract
Let M ⊂ { 0 , ± 1 } n M\subset\{0,\pm 1\}^{n} be a set such that ( m , m ) = 4 (m,m)=4 for every m ∈ M m\in M , and ( m 1 , m 2 ) ∈ { − 4 , − 3 , − 2 , − 1 , 0 , 3 } (m_{1},m_{2})\in\{-4,-3,-2,-1,0,3\} for any two distinct vectors m 1 , m 2 ∈ M m_{1},m_{2}\in M . We determine the maximum possible cardinality of such a set M M for all sufficiently large n n .



Keywords:
extremal set theory, vectors in
\{0,\pm 1\}^{n}
.

1
Introduction

In this paper, we consider the problem of finding the maximum number of vectors in
\{0,\pm 1\}^{n}
with a fixed number of nonzero coordinates and whose pairwise inner products take values in a set
L
. We first recall the relevant background.

A substantial body of work has been devoted to the analogous problem for vectors in
\{0,1\}^{n}
. In this setting, vectors can be identified with subsets of an
n
-element set, while the values of inner products can be identified with the cardinalities of intersections of the corresponding subsets. One of the fundamental results of this kind is the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem (see
[
9
]
), which determines the largest number of vectors in
\{0,1\}^{n}
with
k
nonzero coordinates and pairwise positive inner products, that is, with the zero inner product forbidden. This theorem has became the foundation of an entire area of extremal set theory.

A related problem, usually referred to as the Erdős–Sós problem, asks for the maximum size in the case where exactly one value of the inner product, denoted by
t
, is forbidden; see
[
10
]
. This problem remains open in its general form. In 1985, Frankl and Füredi proved a breakthrough result, solving it in the case
n>n_{0}(k,t)
,
k>2t
(see
[
11
]
). In recent years, a number of results have also been obtained for some special cases of the Erdős–Sós problem in
[
21
]
,
[
19
]
,
[
8
]
, and
[
5
]
.

All such problems can be formulated in terms of finding the independence number of a certain graph. Recall that a subset of vertices of a graph
G
is called independent if no two of its vertices are joined by an edge. The size of the largest independent subset in a graph
G
is called its
independence number
and is denoted by
\alpha(G)
. Define the graph
G(n,k,t)
whose vertices are all possible
k
-element subsets of the set
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, with an edge between two vertices whenever the intersection of the corresponding subsets has exactly
t
elements.

The graphs
G(n,k,t)
are important in extremal set theory, combinatorial geometry, and the study of codes with forbidden distances. We give several examples. In 1981, Frankl and Wilson used an estimate for the independence number of the graph
G(4p,2p,p)
to show that the chromatic number of Euclidean space grows exponentially with the dimension (see
[
15
]
). In 1991, Kahn and Kalai used the results of Frankl and Wilson to disprove the classical Borsuk conjecture that every bounded set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with more than one point can be partitioned into
n+1
parts of smaller diameter (see
[
18
]
,
[
30
]
,
[
29
]
). A more detailed survey can be found in
[
25
]
.

We now pass to a more general formulation in which more than one inner product may be forbidden. Deza, Erdős, and Frankl
[
6
]
initiated the study of
(n,k,L)
-systems, that is, families of
k
-element subsets of an
n
-element set in which all pairwise intersection sizes belong to the set
L
. It is easy to see that the Erdős–Sós problem is a special case of this problem for
L=\{0,1,\ldots,k\}\setminus\{t\}
. Let
m(n,k,L)
denote the maximum possible number of sets in an
(n,k,L)
-system (see
[
20
]
). The problem of determining
m(n,k,L)
in general appears to be extremely difficult. A number of papers address various special cases of this problem (for example, see
[
1
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
17
]
). For instance, in
[
16
]
, Frankl found the order of growth of
m(n,k,L)
for all
k\leqslant 7
and all values of
L
, except for the cases
k=7
,
L=\{0,2,3,5\}
and
L=\{0,2,3,5,6\}
.

We now turn to problems concerning sets of vectors in
\{0,\pm 1\}^{n}
with forbidden inner products. Several papers have been devoted to the study of such sets (for example, see
[
3
]
,
[
24
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
22
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
13
]
). For such sets, there is also an analogue of the Erdős–Sós problem: one seeks the maximum number of vectors in
\{0,\pm 1\}^{n}
with
k
nonzero coordinates in which one value
t
of the pairwise inner products is forbidden. This problem was solved in
[
3
]
for
t=0
, and also for odd negative
t
in the regime
n>n_{0}(k,t)
. For negative even
t
, the exact answer is unknown in general: Frankl and Kupavskii obtained
[
14
]
, an asymptotic result was obtained for all negative
t
.

Passing from vectors in
\{0,1\}^{n}
to vectors in
\{0,\pm 1\}^{n}
made it possible to improve the lower bound in the well-known Nelson–Erdős–Hadwiger problem (see
[
28
]
) and also in Borsuk’s problem (see
[
27
]
).

The following theorem was proved in
[
4
]
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. Define
c(n)
as follows:

c(n)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{ if }n\equiv 0\\
1&\text{ if }n\equiv 1\\
2&\text{ if }n\equiv 2\text{ or }3\end{array}\quad(\bmod\ 4).\right.

Let
\mathcal{V}\subset\{0,\pm 1\}^{n}
be a set such that
(v,v)=3
for every
v\in\mathcal{V}
, and
(v_{1},v_{2})\neq 0
for any two distinct vectors
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathcal{V}
. Then

|\mathcal{V}|\leqslant\max\{6n-28,4n-4c(n)\}.

Theorem
1
is analogous to a simpler theorem of Nagy
[
23
]
for vectors in
\{0,1\}^{n}
with inner product 1 forbidden. The proof of Theorem
1
in the case
n\geqslant 14
is based on the analysis of certain structures called snakes and cobras. For the cases
n<14
, Cherkashin, Kulikov, and Raigorodskii used a computer search, treating each value separately. The reason is that, for small values of
n
, the extremal example may be different, and there may also be several such examples. In the present paper we focus on the method of snakes, but for a more complicated problem; therefore, we assume
n
is sufficiently large.

We consider families of vectors in
\{0,\pm 1\}^{n}
with exactly 4 nonzero coordinates for which the inner products 1 and 2 are forbidden. It is worth noting that the forbidden values 1 and 2 are not arbitrary. Sets of vectors with 4 nonzero coordinates and these forbidden inner products were used in
[
26
]
as a counterexample to Borsuk’s conjecture for
\ell_{p}
metrics.

The main result of this paper is the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\subset\{0,\pm 1\}^{n}
be a set such that
(m,m)=4
for every
m\in\mathcal{M}
, and
(m_{1},m_{2})\in\{-4,-3,-2,-1,0,3\}
for any two distinct vectors
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathcal{M}
. Then there exists
n_{0}
such that for all
n\geqslant n_{0}
the maximum possible cardinality of
\mathcal{M}
is equal to
8n-48
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we introduce the definitions needed for the proof and formulate several simple statements. In Section 3 we prove the lower bound in Theorem
2
, that is, we present a suitable set of
8n-48
vectors. Section 4 is devoted entirely to the proof of the upper bound in Theorem
2
.

2
Notation and auxiliary statements

Recall that, for a positive integer
k
,
[k]
denotes the set
\{1,\ldots,k\}
. For a vector
v\in\{0,\pm 1\}^{n}
, we use the standard notation
\supp(v):=\{i\in[n]:v_{i}\neq 0\}
.

We introduce the following notation for the set of vectors with exactly one nonzero coordinate:

P=P_{n}\coloneqq\bigl\{v\in\{0,\pm 1\}^{n}\bigm|(v,v)=1\bigr\}.

We refer to the vectors in
P
as
positions
.

For a vector
v\in\{0,\pm 1\}^{n}
, define
p(v)\subset P
to be the set of positions that occur in this vector, that is,

p(v)\coloneqq\bigl\{a\in P\bigm|(v,a)=1\bigr\}.

For a set of vectors
F\subset\{0,\pm 1\}^{n}
, denote

\ p(F)\coloneqq\bigcup_{v\in F}p(v).

For a set of positions
P^{\prime}\subset P
, we define

u(P^{\prime})\coloneqq\sum_{a\in P^{\prime}}a,

where
u(P^{\prime})\in\{0,\pm 1\}^{n}
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a family of vectors satisfying the condition of Theorem
2
. For
u,v\in\mathcal{M}
, we write
u\sim v
if and only if
(u,v)\geqslant 3
. It is straightforward to check that
\sim
is an equivalence relation on
\mathcal{M}
, since the values 1 and 2 of the inner product are forbidden. Thus, all vectors in
\mathcal{M}
are partitioned into groups in which all pairwise inner products are equal to 3. Denote the set of equivalence classes by
\mathcal{M}/\sim
. Below we classify these classes according to their structure.

Definition 1
.

A set
S\subset\mathcal{M}
is called a snake if
|S|\geqslant 2
and there exists
h\in\{0,\pm 1\}^{n}
such that

(h,h)=3
and
(v,h)=3
for all
v\in S
.

The vector
h
in the definition is called the head of the snake
S
. All vectors in a snake have pairwise distinct nonzero coordinates outside the head.

Definition 2
.

A set
C\subset\mathcal{M}
is called a 5-cluster if
|C|\geqslant 3
and there exists
h\in\{0,\pm 1\}^{n}
such that

(h,h)=5
and
(v,h)=4
for all
v\in C
.

If an equivalence class consists of a single element, we call it a
single vector
. In Section
4.1
we prove that all equivalence classes in
\mathcal{M}/\sim
are either single vectors, snakes, or 5-clusters.

We also need several statements and definitions concerning configurations of snakes.

Claim 3
.

Let
S\in\mathcal{M}/\sim
be a snake with head
h
such that
|S|\geqslant 4
. Then
(v,h)\leqslant 0
for all
v\in\mathcal{M}\setminus S
.

Proof.

Suppose, to the contrary, that there exists a vector
v\in\mathcal{M}\setminus S
such that
(v,h)\geqslant 1
. Since
|S|\geqslant 4
, there exists
w\in S
such that
(\supp(w)\cap\supp(v))\subset\supp(h)
. In this case
(v,w)=(v,h)\geqslant 1
, which is impossible because
v\notin S
. This contradiction proves the claim.
∎

Definition 3
.

Let
S_{1},S_{2}
be snakes with heads
h_{1},h_{2}
. We say that the snakes
S_{1},S_{2}
are compatible if
(h_{1},h_{2})\leqslant-1
.

Definition 4
.

Let
S_{1},S_{2},S_{3},S_{4}\in\mathcal{M}/\sim
be four distinct snakes. We say that these snakes form a serpentarium if they are pairwise compatible and
|S_{i}|\geqslant 4
for every
i\in[4]
.

3
An example of an extremal configuration

In this section we give an example of a configuration for which the bound in Theorem
2
is attained. Let
n>6
, and let
P^{\prime}
be the set of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a 1 in exactly one of the last
n-6
coordinates and zeros in all other coordinates.

Denote

H_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}g_{1}\\
g_{2}\\
g_{3}\\
g_{4}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&0&\dots&0\\
1&-1&-1&0&\dots&0\\
-1&1&-1&0&\dots&0\\
-1&-1&1&0&\dots&0\end{pmatrix}\in M_{4\times n}(\mathbb{R}),

H_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}h_{1}\\
h_{2}\\
h_{3}\\
h_{4}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&1&1&1&0&\dots&0\\
0&0&0&1&-1&-1&0&\dots&0\\
0&0&0&-1&1&-1&0&\dots&0\\
0&0&0&-1&-1&1&0&\dots&0\end{pmatrix}\in M_{4\times n}(\mathbb{R}).

Note that
(h_{i},h_{j})=(g_{i},g_{j})=-1
for
i\not=j\in[4]
. The families
\mathcal{G}=\{g_{1},g_{2},g_{3},g_{4}\}
and
\mathcal{H}=\{h_{1},h_{2},h_{3},h_{4}\}
serve as two sets of heads of pairwise compatible snakes. Put

\mathcal{M}=\{g+a\bigm|g\in\mathcal{G},a\in P^{\prime}\}\cup\{h-a\bigm|h\in\mathcal{H},a\in P^{\prime}\}.

Clearly, every vector in
\mathcal{M}
has 4 nonzero coordinates from the set
\{-1,1\}
, and
|\mathcal{M}|=8|P^{\prime}|=8n-48
. It remains to verify that the vectors in
\mathcal{M}
satisfy the conditions on pairwise inner products.

We call the vectors in
\{g+a\bigm|g\in\mathcal{G},a\in P^{\prime}\}
vectors of the first type, and the vectors in
\{h-a\bigm|h\in\mathcal{H},a\in P^{\prime}\}
vectors of the second type. Choose arbitrary distinct vectors
u,v\in\mathcal{M}
. If
u
and
v
belong to the same snake, then
(u,v)=3
. If
u
and
v
belong to different snakes but are of the same type, then
(u,v)\in\{-1,0\}
. If
u
and
v
are of different types, then again
(u,v)\in\{-1,0\}
. Thus, the conditions on inner products are satisfied for all pairs of vectors in
\mathcal{M}
, and hence the example satisfies the required condition.

4
The upper bound in Theorem
2

4.1
Types of equivalence classes

We prove a lemma that shows that
\mathcal{M}/\sim
contains only the structures described in Section
2
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
F\subset\mathcal{M}
be an equivalence class in
\mathcal{M}
with respect to
\sim
. Then exactly one of the following three conditions holds:

(1)
|F|=1
;

(2)
F
is a snake;

(3)
F
is a 5-cluster.

Proof.

The case
|F|=1
is described in condition (1). If
|F|=2
, then
F=\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
is a snake with head
h=u(p(v_{1})\cap p(v_{2}))
. Now let
|F|\geqslant 3
. Consider arbitrary distinct vectors
v_{1},v_{2}\in F
. Put
h=u(p(v_{1})\cap p(v_{2}))
. Then
(h,h)=3
and
(v_{1},h)=(v_{2},h)=3
. For any
v\in F\setminus\{v_{1}\}
we have
(v,v_{1})=3
, and therefore
(v,h)\geqslant 2
. There are two cases.

Case 1.
Suppose that there exists
v_{3}\in F
such that
(v_{3},h)=3
. In this case
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}
agree on the nonzero coordinates in
\supp(h)
. If
(v,h)=3
for all other
v\in F\setminus\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
, then
F
is a snake. Suppose that for some
v\in F
we have
(v,h)=2
. Then, for each
i\in[3]
, the vector
v
coincides with
v_{i}
on exactly two coordinates of
\supp(h)
and on one coordinate outside
\supp(h)
. But the nonzero coordinates of
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}
outside
\supp(h)
are pairwise distinct, so
v
cannot have a common additional coordinate with all three vectors
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}
, a contradiction.

Case 2.
Now suppose that
(v,h)=2
for every
v\in F\setminus\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
. Choose an arbitrary vector
v_{3}\in F\setminus\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
. Then
v_{3}
has a zero at one coordinate of
\supp(h)
and shares one additional coordinate with
v_{1}
and one distinct additional coordinate with
v_{2}
. Put
g=u(p(v_{1})\cup p(v_{2})\cup p(v_{3}))
. Then
(g,g)=5
and
(v_{i},g)=4
for
i\in[3]
. For any
v\in F
we have
(v,g)\geqslant 3
, since
(v,v_{i})\geqslant 3
for every
i\in[3]
. Suppose that for some
v\in F
we have
(v,g)=3
. Denote
a_{i}=u(p(v_{i})\setminus p(h))
for
i\in[2]
. Then
g-h=a_{1}+a_{2}
. From
(v,g)=3
and
(v,h)=2
we get
(v,g-h)=1
. Without loss of generality, assume that
(v,a_{1})=1
and
(v,a_{2})=0
. Then
(v,v_{2})=(v,h)+(v,a_{2})=2
, a contradiction. Hence
(v,g)=4
for all
v\in F
, that is,
F
is a 5-cluster.

∎

We now have a classification of equivalence classes. Let

S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\subset\mathcal{M}
be all equivalence classes that are snakes, and let
C_{1},\ldots,C_{l}\subset\mathcal{M}
be all equivalence classes that are 5-clusters. Denote the set of single vectors by
\mathcal{T}=\{v\in\mathcal{M}\bigm|\nexists u\in\mathcal{M}:(u,v)=3\}
. Define
m=|\mathcal{T}|
. Thus, the total number of equivalence classes is equal to
k+m+l
.

For each such class
F
, we need to count the number of positions used by the vectors in
F
. We shall use the following simple observation.

Claim 5
.

Let
F\subset\mathcal{M}
be an equivalence class with respect to
\sim
. Then

(1)
if
F
is a snake or a single vector, then
|p(F)|=|F|+3
;

(2)
if
F
is a 5-cluster, then
|p(F)|\geqslant|F|
and
|p(F)|=5
.

In particular,
|p(F)|\geqslant|F|
for every equivalence class
F
.

4.2
Vectors with a fixed nonzero coordinate

For each position
a\in P
, let
f(a)
denote the number of equivalence classes in which the position
a
is used. Formally,

f(a)\coloneqq|\{K\in\mathcal{M}/\sim\bigm|a\in p(K)\}|.

By double counting the incidence of a position with an equivalence class, we have

\sum_{a\in P}f(a)=\sum_{K\in\mathcal{M}/\sim}|p(K)|.

(1)

In this section we prove upper bounds on the function
f
.

Lemma 6
.

For every
a\in P
we have
f(a)\leqslant 4
.

Proof.

Set
s=f(a)
. Let
w_{1},\dots,w_{s}
be vectors belonging to distinct equivalence classes such that
a\in p(w_{i})
for every
i\in[s]
.

Put
v_{i}=w_{i}-a
. For any distinct
i,j\in[s]
we have
(w_{i},w_{j})\leqslant 0
, hence
(v_{i},v_{j})\leqslant-1
.

We obtain the following chain:

0\leqslant\left(\sum_{j=1}^{s}v_{j},\sum_{j=1}^{s}v_{j}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{s}(v_{j},v_{j})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[s]\\
i\not=j\end{subarray}}(v_{i},v_{j})\leqslant 3s-s(s-1)=s(4-s).

(2)

The condition
s(4-s)\geqslant 0
implies
s\leqslant 4
, as required.

∎

Next we show that the bound
f(a)\leqslant 4
can be improved if at least two vectors using the position
a
occur in the same equivalence class.

Lemma 7
.

Let
a\in P
, and suppose that there exist
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathcal{M}
such that
(w_{1},w_{2})=3
and
a\in p(w_{1})\cap p(w_{2})
. Then
f(a)\leqslant 3
.

Proof.

We use the notation from the proof of Lemma
6
. We show that
s
cannot be
4
. If
s=4
, then all inequalities in the chain (
2
) become equalities. Thus, we obtain
\sum_{i=1}^{4}v_{i}=0
.

By assumption, one of the equivalence classes contains at least two vectors using the position
a
. Without loss of generality, there exists
w_{1}^{\prime}\neq w_{1}
in the same equivalence class as
w_{1}
such that
a\in p(w_{1}^{\prime})
. Denote
v_{1}^{\prime}=w_{1}^{\prime}-a
. Applying the same argument to this vector, we get
v_{1}^{\prime}+v_{2}+v_{3}+v_{4}=0
, whence
v_{1}=v_{1}^{\prime}
. Therefore
w_{1}
and
w_{1}^{\prime}
coincide, a contradiction.

∎

We record several consequences of Lemma
7
.

Claim 8
.

Let
C
be a 5-cluster. Then for every position
a\in p(C)
we have
f(a)\leqslant 3
.

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary position
a\in p(C)
. In a 5-cluster
C
, at most one vector does not contain the position
a
, since each vector in the 5-cluster uses 4 positions out of 5 fixed positions. By definition,
C
contains at least 3 vectors, so there exist two vectors
w_{1},w_{2}\in C
such that
a\in p(w_{1})\cap p(w_{2})
. Therefore Lemma
7
gives
f(a)\leqslant 3
.
∎

Lemma 9
.

Fix a position
a\in P
. Then

(1) Suppose that there exists a snake
S_{i}
with head
h
such that
a\in p(h)
. Then
f(a)\leqslant 3
.

(2) Suppose that snakes
S_{i},S_{j}
with heads
h_{i}
and
h_{j}
, respectively, are compatible and
|S_{i}|\geqslant 4
,
|S_{j}|\geqslant 4
. If their heads have a common position
a\in p(h_{i})\cap p(h_{j})
, then
f(a)=2
.

Proof.

(1) Consider distinct
w_{1},w_{2}\in S_{i}
. Clearly,

(w_{1},w_{2})=3
and
a\in p(h)=p(w_{1})\cap p(w_{2})
. Hence Lemma
7
gives
f(a)\leqslant 3
.

(2) By assumption,
S_{i}
and
S_{j}
are compatible, so
(h_{i},h_{j})\leqslant-1
. Since the heads
h_{i}
and
h_{j}
have the common position
a
, their other two positions must be opposite; that is,
h_{i}+h_{j}=2a
. By Claim
3
, for every vector
w\in\mathcal{M}\setminus(S_{i}\cup S_{j})
we have
(w,h_{i})\leqslant 0
and
(w,h_{j})\leqslant 0
. Hence
(w,2a)=(w,h_{i}+h_{j})\leqslant 0
, that is,
a\notin p(w)
for every
w\in\mathcal{M}\setminus(S_{i}\cup S_{j})
. Thus
f(a)=2
.

∎

4.3
The number of equivalence classes

In this section we proof upper bound for the number of equivalence classes of
\mathcal{M}
.

Lemma 10
.

If
|\mathcal{M}|\geqslant 8n-48
, then
k+l+m<30
.

Proof.

Let
P^{\prime}\coloneqq\bigcup_{i=1}^{l}p(C_{i})
be the set of positions that occur in 5-clusters.

By Claim
8
, for every
a\in P^{\prime}
we have
f(a)\leqslant 3
, and therefore

\sum_{i=1}^{l}|p(C_{i})|=5l\leqslant\sum_{a\in P^{\prime}}f(a)\leqslant 3|P^{\prime}|\implies|P^{\prime}|\geqslant\frac{5}{3}l.

Combining this bound with Lemma
6
, we get

\sum_{a\in P}f(a)=\sum_{a\in P^{\prime}}f(a)+\sum_{a\in P\setminus P^{\prime}}f(a)\leqslant 4|P|-|P^{\prime}|\leqslant 8n-\frac{5}{3}l.

Using Claim
5
, we obtain the following chain:

\displaystyle 8n-\frac{5}{3}l\geqslant\sum_{a\in P}f(a)=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{M}/\sim}|p(F)|=\sum_{i=1}^{k}|p(S_{i})|+\sum_{i=1}^{l}|p(C_{i})|+\sum_{v\in\mathcal{T}}|p(v)|

\displaystyle\geqslant|\mathcal{M}|+3(k+m).

Thus
|\mathcal{M}|+3(k+m)\leqslant 8n-\frac{5}{3}l
. Since
|\mathcal{M}|\geqslant 8n-48
, it follows that
k+m+\frac{5}{9}l\leqslant 16
, and hence
k+m+l\leqslant 16\cdot\frac{9}{5}<30
. Lemma
10
is proved.
∎

4.4
Configurations of snakes

In this section we show that, for sufficiently large
n
, two serpentaria can be found in an extremal example.

Lemma 11
.

There exists
n_{0}
such that, if
n>n_{0}
and
|\mathcal{M}|\geqslant 8n-48
, then there are two disjoint serpentaria.

Proof.

For a vector
v
we write
p_{pm}(v)=p(v)\cup\{-a:a\in p(v)\}
. Define
p_{pm}(F)
analogously for a set of vectors
F
.
Define the sets
H_{1},H_{2},H_{3},H_{4}\subset P
as follows:

H_{1}\coloneqq p_{pm}(\{h\bigm|\exists i\in[k],h\text{ is the head of }S_{i}\}),

H_{2}\coloneqq\bigcup_{i\in[l]}p_{pm}(C_{i}),

H_{3}\coloneqq\bigcup_{v\in\mathcal{T}}p_{pm}(v),

H_{4}\coloneqq\bigcup_{i\in[k],|S_{i}|\leqslant 3}p_{pm}(S_{i}),

H\coloneqq P\setminus(H_{1}\cup H_{2}\cup H_{3}\cup H_{4}).

From the definitions it follows that
|H_{1}|\leqslant 6k
,
|H_{2}|\leqslant 10l
,
|H_{3}|\leqslant 8m
, and
|H_{4}|\leqslant 12k
. By Lemma
10
, we have
k+l+m<30
. Therefore

|H_{1}|+|H_{2}|+|H_{3}|+|H_{4}|\leqslant 18(k+l+m)\leqslant 18\cdot 29=522.

Hence

|H|\geqslant|P|-|H_{1}|-|H_{2}|-|H_{3}|-|H_{4}|\geqslant 2n-522.

We prove that for sufficiently large
n
there exists a position
a\in H
such that
f(a)=f(-a)=4
. We argue by contradiction. Note that if
a\in H
, then
-a\in H
. Let
n>309
. Then
|H|>2\cdot 309-522=96
. We have

\sum_{a\in P}f(a)=\sum_{a\in H}f(a)+\sum_{a\in P\setminus H}f(a)\leqslant 4|P|-\frac{1}{2}|H|<8n-48\implies|\mathcal{M}|<8n-48.

This is a contradiction. Now fix
a\in H
for which
f(a)=f(-a)=4
. Since
a\notin H_{2}\cup H_{3}\cup H_{4}
, every class that uses
a
is a snake of cardinality at least 4. Since
a\notin H_{1}
, the position
a
does not lie in the head of such a snake. We show that the snakes containing the position
a
are pairwise compatible. Consider any two distinct such snakes
S_{1},S_{2}
with heads
h_{1},h_{2}
, respectively. Then

h_{1}+a\in S_{1},\quad h_{2}+a\in S_{2}\implies(h_{1}+a,h_{2}+a)\leqslant 0\implies(h_{1},h_{2})\leqslant-1,

as required. Thus, the snakes containing the position
a
form a serpentarium. The same argument applies to
-a
. It remains only to observe that these serpentaria cannot have common snakes, since a snake cannot use both positions
a
and
-a
at the same time. Hence we have found two disjoint serpentaria, and the lemma is proved.
∎

4.5
Completion of the proof

Lemma 12
.

Fix a serpentarium, and let
\mathcal{H}
be the set of heads of its snakes. For
a\in P
, denote
\mathcal{H}_{a}\coloneqq\{h\in\mathcal{H}:a\in p(h)\}
. Then the following properties hold.

(1) For any two distinct
h_{1}\not=h_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
, we have
(h_{1},h_{2})=-1
.

(2) For all
a\in P
, we have
|\mathcal{H}_{a}|=|\mathcal{H}_{-a}|
.

(3) For every
h\in\mathcal{H}
and every vector
v\in\mathcal{M}
not belonging to any snake in this serpentarium, we have
(v,h)=0
.

(4) Let
h_{i}
be the head of a snake
S_{i}
that does not belong to this serpentarium. If
|S_{i}|\geqslant 4
, then
(h_{i},h)=0
for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

(1)
By the definition of a serpentarium, for any
h_{1}\not=h_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
we can say that
(h_{1},h_{2})\leqslant-1
. We have

0\leqslant\left(\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}}h,\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}}h\right)=3\cdot 4+\sum_{h_{1}\not=h_{2}\in\mathcal{H}}(h_{1},h_{2})\leqslant 12-12=0.

(3)

It follows that

\sum_{h_{1}\not=h_{2}\in\mathcal{H}}(h_{1},h_{2})=-12.

This is possible only if
(h_{1},h_{2})=-1
for all
h_{1}\not=h_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
.

(2)
All inequalities in (
3
) must be equalities, and hence
\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}}h=0
. It follows that the positions
a
and
-a
occur in the same number of heads from
\mathcal{H}
, as required.

(3)
Consider an arbitrary vector
v\in\mathcal{M}
that does not belong to any snake in this serpentarium. By Claim
3
, we have
(v,h)\leqslant 0
for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
. Observe that

\left(v,\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}}h\right)=\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}}(v,h)=\sum_{a\in p(v)}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}}(a,h)=0.

Therefore
(v,h)
must be zero for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
.

(4)
Consider an arbitrary head
h\in\mathcal{H}
. Suppose that
(h_{i},h)\geqslant 1
. On the other hand, for every vector
w\in S_{i}
, Claim
3
gives
(w,h)\leqslant 0
. This means that
\supp(w)
must intersect
\supp(h)\setminus\supp(h_{i})
. This contradicts the assumption
|S_{i}|\geqslant 4
. Thus
(h_{i},h)\leqslant 0
.

As in part (3), we have

\left(h_{i},\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}}h\right)=\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}}(h_{i},h)=\sum_{a\in p(h_{i})}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}}(a,h)=0,

so
(h_{i},h)
must be zero for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
.
∎

By Lemma
11
, the set
\mathcal{M}
already contains two disjoint serpentaria; denote them by
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
. Without loss of generality, assume that
\mathcal{S}_{1}=\{S_{1},S_{2},S_{3},S_{4}\}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}=\{S_{5},S_{6},S_{7},S_{8}\}
. This already implies
k\geqslant 8
. Using equality (
1
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in P}f(a)=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{M}/\sim}|p(F)|\geqslant\sum_{F\in\mathcal{M}/\sim}|F|+3k\geqslant\sum_{F\in\mathcal{M}/\sim}|F|+24.

(4)

In the serpentaria
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
, denote the sets of heads by
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
, respectively, and also put
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\cup\mathcal{H}_{2}
.

Next we bound the sum
\sum_{a\in P}f(a)
. Let
P_{1}
be the set of positions that are used in exactly one head from
\mathcal{H}
, and let
P_{2}
be the set of positions that are used in at least two heads from
\mathcal{H}
.

Lemma 13
.

(1) For every position
a\in P_{1}
, inequality
f(a)\leqslant 3
holds.

(2) For every position
a\in P_{2}
, equality
f(a)=2
holds.

Proof.

(1)
This follows from part (1) of Lemma
9
.

(2)
If the position
a
is used in two heads from one serpentarium, then the statement follows from Lemma
9
, since any two snakes of the same serpentarium are compatible. Now suppose that in each serpentarium there is exactly one head using
a
. Without loss of generality,
a\in p(h_{1})
and
a\in p(h_{5})
, where
h_{1}
and
h_{5}
are the heads of the snakes
S_{1}
and
S_{5}
, respectively.

First note that no vector belonging to the other snakes of these two serpentaria can use the position
a
. Indeed, suppose, without loss of generality, that a vector
v
belongs to a snake of the first serpentarium with head
h\neq h_{1}
and
a\in p(v)
. Since
a\notin p(h)
, we have
v=h+a
. By Lemma
12
,
(v,h_{5})=0
and
(h,h_{5})=0
. Hence

(v,h_{5})=(h,h_{5})+(a,h_{5})=1,

a contradiction.

Now consider an arbitrary vector
v\in\mathcal{M}
that does not belong to the snakes from
\mathcal{S}_{1}\cup\mathcal{S}_{2}
. Our goal is to show that
a\notin p(v)
.

By part (2) of Lemma
12
, in the first serpentarium there is a head
h_{2}
such that
-a\in p(h_{2})
, and in the second serpentarium there is a head
h_{6}
such that
-a\in p(h_{6})
. Consider the vector
h_{1}+h_{5}-h_{2}-h_{6}
. By Lemma
12
, we know that heads from different serpentaria are orthogonal, and therefore

(h_{1}+h_{5}-h_{2}-h_{6},h_{1}+h_{5}-h_{2}-h_{6})=4\cdot 3-2(h_{1},h_{2})-2(h_{5},h_{6})=16.

On the other hand, this vector has coordinate value 4 in the coordinate corresponding to
a
, hence
h_{1}+h_{5}-h_{2}-h_{6}=4a
.

It remains to note that, by part (3) of Lemma
12
, the vector
v
is orthogonal to each of the vectors
h_{1},h_{2},h_{5},h_{6}
, so

(v,4a)=(v,h_{1}+h_{5}-h_{2}-h_{6})=0.

Hence
a\notin p(v)
. Thus, only the classes
S_{1}
and
S_{5}
use the position
a
. Therefore
f(a)=2
.

∎

Now we know that
p(\mathcal{H}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{H}_{2})=P_{1}\sqcup P_{2}
. By double counting,
|P_{1}|+2|P_{2}|=8\cdot 3=24
. Applying Lemma
13
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in P}f(a)=\sum_{a\in P\setminus(P_{1}\cup P_{2})}f(a)+\sum_{a\in P_{1}}f(a)+\sum_{a\in P_{2}}f(a)\leqslant 4|P|-|P_{1}|-2|P_{2}|=8n-24.

(5)

Combining estimates (
4
) and (
5
), we get

|\mathcal{M}|=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{M}/\sim}|F|\leqslant\sum_{a\in P}f(a)-24\leqslant 8n-48.

The upper bound in Theorem
2
is proved.
