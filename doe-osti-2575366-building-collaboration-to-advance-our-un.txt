Final Technical Report
Project Title: Building collaboration to advance our understanding of regional climate impacts of
dust in California's San Joaquin Valley
Principal Investigator: Adeyemi Adebiyi, Life and Environmental Sciences, University of
California - Merced.
Award Number: DE-SC0023033
Period of Performance: 09/01/2022 - 5/14/2025

Executive Summary:
This project successfully achieved its central objective of building collaborative research
capabilities at UC Merced, a Hispanic-Serving Institution, to advance understanding of the
regional climate impacts of dust in California's San Joaquin Valley. Through strategic partnerships
with three DOE national laboratories (PNNL, LLNL, and LBNL), we developed critical expertise
in the Energy Exascale Earth System Model (E3SM) and Atmospheric Radiation Measurement
(ARM) facilities. Among the project's scientific contributions, one key publication includes
demonstrating that fallowed agricultural lands are the primary source of anthropogenic dust in
California's Central Valley, with dust activities increasing substantially between 2008 and 2022,
in correlation with drought severity and expanded fallowed land coverage. This finding suggests
that current climate models, including E3SM, likely underestimate the dust burden due to
inadequate representation of agricultural land-use changes. Beyond the scientific contributions,
the project successfully trained a PhD student, established ongoing collaborations resulting in
multiple manuscripts in preparation, and positioned UC Merced to participate in the DUSTIEAIM
campaign for 2026-2027, thereby building sustainable research capacity while addressing climate
science questions directly relevant to the California Central Valley.

1) Project Objectives
The central objective of the project was to develop capabilities at UC Merced in dust-climate
research by building collaboration with researchers at the Department of Energy (DOE)'s national
laboratories. Because of UC Merced’s location, we are uniquely positioned to help advance our
understanding of dust impacts on regional air quality and climate in the San Joaquin Valley.
Through the project, we aimed to build collaboration between UC Merced and three DOE national
laboratories: the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL), the Lawrence Livermore
National Laboratory (LLNL), and the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL). The goal
of this collaboration was to achieve two specific objectives:
 • Become familiar with the Energy Exascale Earth System Model (E3SM) to better
 understand and improve dust impacts on regional climate in the San Joaquin Valley.
 • Become familiar with the instrumentation on the Atmospheric Radiation Measurement
 (ARM) Mobile Facilities (AMF) currently deployed and develop expertise to propose a
 future campaign that characterizes dust impacts in the San Joaquin Valley.

2) Activities Conducted and Key Results
 a) Develop capabilities in dust modeling with E3SM and collaborative projects at PNNL.
To achieve the project objectives, we conducted activities that helped UC Merced develop
capabilities in dust modeling using E3SM, including my graduate student and I visiting PNNL.
While I stayed between July 10 and July 21, 2023, my graduate student was at PNNL until August
10, 2023, and we were both hosted by Dr. Hailong Wang, one of the unfunded collaborators on the
project. Activities conducted at PNNL included installing the E3SM model and its components
and configuring it for a specific case example that was used as a test run. Other activities included
understanding the radiative impacts of dust in E3SM, particularly relating to the impacts of coarse
dust on precipitation. In addition, we also explore new research questions, particularly around
anthropogenic dust emission, especially from agricultural sources in California. Given that
parameterization of dust in models, including E3SM, often ignores changes in land-use practices,
such as land fallowing, that could lead to dust emission, our first goal was to quantify the
contribution of dust associated with land use over California. The first part of this project and
collaboration has resulted in a publication (Adeyemi A. Adebiyi, Kibria, M. M., Abatzoglou, J. T.,
Ginoux, P., Pandey, S., Heaney, A., Chen, S.-H., and Akinsanola, A. A.: Fallowed agricultural
lands dominate anthropogenic dust sources in California, Commun Earth Environ, 6, 1–14,
https://doi.org/10.1038/s43247-025-02306-0, 2025.). The paper explores the regional-scale
relationship between fallowed agricultural lands, anthropogenic dust sources, and windblown dust
burden over California. The key findings showed that substantial fractions of anthropogenic dust
sources, which are predominantly in California’s Central Valley, are fallowed lands, and these
sources are associated with dust burden typically downstream of their locations. In addition, the
paper also showed that these anthropogenic dust activities in the Central Valley increased
substantially between 2008 and 2022, consistent with the expansion of fallowed land areas, and
are sensitive to drought severity over that period. These fallowed lands are not directly represented
in the E3SM and may contribute to the underestimation of dust burden in the model, with potential
implications on air quality, agriculture, and regional climate, including precipitation clouds and so
on. We plan to expand this study to evaluate the representation of agricultural dust across the US
in E3SM.

Furthermore, to introduce myself and my research at PNNL, I presented my prior research on the
impacts of coarse dust – another dust property that is underestimated in E3SM - to the researchers
and scientists at PNNL in a 1-hour seminar. That opportunity allowed me to meet a number of the
scientists and leadership in the Atmospheric Sciences & Global Change Division, and as a result,
the collaboration that started during this period has resulted in a manuscript that is about to be
submitted for publication (Huang, Huilin, Adeyemi A. Adebiyi, Ye Liu, Ziming Chen, Jian Lu.
Spring Dust in Colorado Plateau and Its Interannual Variability – Implications from two decades
of the MERRA2 reanalysis). For this collaborative project, we took a data-driven approach to
establish a climatology of spring dust transport to the Colorado Plateau, leveraging Modern-Era
Retrospective analysis for Research and Applications v2 (MERRA-2) reanalysis data and an
unsupervised machine learning algorithm, Self-Organizing Maps (SOM), to categorize high-dust
events into a few representative transport patterns. The result shows that there are four distinct dust
transport pathways from surrounding deserts impacting the Colorado Plateau: one intense
southwesterly near-surface transport, one with weaker surface emissions but substantial remote
transport, one with moderate southwesterly flow but stronger upward motion and upper-level
transport, and one rare northwesterly pattern linked to the Great Basin sources, accounting for
38%, 14%, 37%, and 11% of total dust events, respectively. After establishing this benchmark, we
plan to extend this work to understand how E3SM represents dust transport to the Colorado
Plateau. Finally, I also visited Dr. Xue Zheng in LLNL to understand better the Single Column
Model (SCM) associated with E3SM. This visit was an extension of similar collaborative work
started at PNNL with Dr. Shuaiqi Tang. This work is still in progress and expected to result in a
submission in Spring 2026.
 b) Activities involving AMF
For the second objective, we proposed to visit the DOE's Surface Atmosphere Integrated Field
Laboratory (SAIL), a campaign that focused on the East River Watershed to understand mountain
hydrometeorology and made measurements using the second ARM Mobile Facility (AMF2) and
a scanning X-band dual polarimetric radar near Crested Butte, Colorado. However, due to the
difficulty of visiting the campaign site during the winter and spring period (because of road
closure), I could not visit the SAIL campaign between the time of the award and the end of the
campaign in May/June 2023. To still achieve the objective set out, I participated in online Zoom
meetings introducing the public to the capabilities of AMF and joining three teams in proposing
AMF campaigns. Among the three proposed campaigns that I was part of, one was selected, and
that was Desert-Urban SysTem IntegratEd AtmospherIc Monsoon (DUSTIEAIM), which
proposed to study interactions between the land surface and the atmosphere over a desert
megalopolis (Phoenix, Arizona) between April 2026 and September 2027. Specifically,
DUSTIEAIM will quantify the bimodal water cycle and energy budgets at the
urban/rural/agricultural interface of the southwestern U.S. as regional air masses and
meteorological patterns change throughout the year in response to the seasonal cycle over the
region.
3) Opportunities For Training And Professional Development
The project allowed one PhD student (Md Minhazul Kibria) to participate in data analysis,
computational modeling, and manuscript writing. The students gained a deep fundamental
understanding of how dust modelling works in an Earth system model (E3SM). The student also
gains an opportunity to network with researchers and scientists at PNNL as well as develop

research questions associated with the agricultural dust that will be part of his thesis upon
completion of his PhD.
