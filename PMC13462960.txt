Review of image segmentation techniques for biomedical micro-CT: from laboratory absorption imaging to synchrotron phase-contrast
Authors: Hao Song, Ning Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractX-ray micro-computed tomography (μCT) is widely used in biomedical research for non-destructive, high-resolution imaging. Synchrotron Radiation Phase-Contrast μCT (SR-PCI-μCT) further enhances image quality with higher Signal to Noise Ratio (SNR), improved contrast, and faster acquisition. However, segmenting SR-PCI-μCT images remains challenging due to their heterogeneous image property and limited training data. Additionally, the increasing throughput of synchrotron facilities demands robust, efficient segmentation methods. This paper reviews a list of recent segmentation approaches in biomedical μCT. Traditional methods remain simple and effective for simple, high-contrast structures but require extensive tuning and generalize poorly to complex, low-contrast soft tissues. Data-driven models provide higher accuracy and robustness yet rely heavily on large expert-annotated datasets, limiting reproducibility and cross-dataset adaptability. Recent advance on vision transformers have shifted the paradigm from task-specified to more domain-specified segmentation, though these techniques are still evolving and require adaptation for SR-PCI-μCT studies. This survey provides the first bi-modality review covering both laboratory and synchrotron biomedical μCT segmentation. It consolidates recent segmentation methodologies, identifies major trends in deep-learning techniques, and highlights current limitations across SR-PCI-μCT. Additionally, it outlines open challenges to guide future research and practical advancements in biomedical μCT segmentation.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

First discovered by Wilhelm Conrad Röntgen in 1895, X-ray is a form of high-energy electromagnetic radiation that is extensively applied across diverse disciplines, such as medical imaging, materials science, astronomy, and biomedical studies. Its ability to penetrate through objects and materials allows the non-invasive analysis of internal structure without slicing or breaking the specimens. Computed tomography (CT) is an imaging technique that uses a series of X-ray measurements acquired from multiple angles, processed by computer algorithms to generate detailed cross-sectional and three-dimensional (3D) images of an object’s internal architecture. X-ray microtomography, also known as micro-computed tomography (μCT), which was invented in the 1980s by Dover and Elliott1,2, is an advanced variation of traditional CT and is generally preferred for applications requiring high-resolution imaging. Unlike medical scanners, which usually have resolutions in the mm range, μCT systems typically achieve resolutions from 1 to 100 μm. The higher resolution, combining a lower voxel size, allows imaging of samples with micro-structures such as pores3, trabecular bone4, or even sub-cellular dimensions5.

Bench-top μCT versus synchrotron μCT

Bench-top μCT, also known as laboratory μCT (lab-μCT), is widely deployed in many biomedical studies because of its compact size, moderate device cost, and high spatial resolution6. In the past decades, synchrotron radiation micro-computed tomography (SR-μCT) has attracted increasing attention from researchers due to its superior resolution, monochromatic beam, high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), faster acquisition time, and sample versatility7,8.

Comparing Lab-μCT with synchrotron

The main distinctions between lab-μCT and SR-μCT arise from their X-ray sources, image quality, resolution, scan speed, and system scale9. Lab-μCT uses conventional polychromatic X-ray tubes, which produce lower-intensity, less coherent beams and are susceptible to beam-hardening artifacts10. In contrast, SR-μCT employs synchrotron-generated X-rays produced by relativistic electrons circulating in a storage ring, yielding highly collimated, intense, coherent, and optionally monochromatic beams that effectively suppress beam hardening11. Compared with lab-μCT, SR-μCT benefits from the narrow bandwidth of energy-selective X-rays, whose high intensity enables rapid scanning. Within the same time frame, SR-PCI-μCT can perform substantially more scans than lab-μCT, dramatically reducing image acquisition time.

Phase contrast imaging

A further strength of SR-μCT is its capability for phase contrast imaging. While lab-μCT primarily relies on absorption contrast, where pixel intensity depends on the degree of X-ray attenuation across different materials, synchrotron X-ray μCT can employ absorption contrast, Phase Contrast Imaging (SR-PCI-μCT), or a combination of both, depending on the beamline configuration. Using highly collimated X-ray beams and an appropriate propagation distance, phase shifts generate interference patterns that enhance edge definition and contrast without staining, making the technique particularly valuable for biomedical samples12,13. Propagation-based PCI is widely used due to its simplicity and robustness for low-density samples14,15. Although phase contrast has recently been introduced in lab-μCT systems16, it remains under active development. Advances in SR-PCI-μCT continue to inform improvements in lab-based imaging and may ultimately support future clinical applications.

In the past decades, SR-μCT has drawn increasing attention across multiple scientific domains, including the medical and biomedical fields. Image segmentation

Basis of image segmentation

Biomedical imaging experiments generate large volumes of complex, high-resolution data that require systematic processing. Image processing pipelines provide a structured framework for sample image acquisition, storage, visualization, and quantitative analysis, thereby transforming raw datasets into meaningful scientific insights. Image segmentation is one of the critical steps that partition an image into meaningful regions20,21. It enables the isolation of the Region of Interest (ROI) from the background or less informative areas. Image segmentation assigns unique identifiers to ROIs for downstream tasks and improves efficiency and accuracy by excluding irrelevant background.

Traditional image segmentation

The history of image segmentation can be traced back to the early 1970s and 1980s, when traditional thresholding22, region-based23, and edge-based methods24,25 were formally described. More segmentation techniques were then proposed from 1990s to 2000s, such as graph-based methods26–30, active contours30,31, and watershed32.

Deep-learning-based image segmentation

The 2010s represent a pivotal decade during which deep-learning-based image segmentation techniques emerged and rapidly gained substantial scientific attention due to their high accuracy, robustness, and efficiency. The first notable milestone in deep-learning-based image segmentation is the Fully Convolutional Network (FCN), proposed by Long et al. in 201533. In the same year, Ronneberger et al. proposed U-Net, which has rapidly become one of the most influential foundational models for more than 100 thousand image segmentation studies across various domains to date34. There are other widely adopted deep segmentation models, including SegNet35, DeepLab36,37, Mask R-CNN38, among others.

Designing a image segmentation method

To design a suitable image segmentation method, especially in high-resolution fields like SR-μCT studies, requires a systematic consideration that balances image properties, computational cost, and accuracy39. A well-defined segmentation objective, such as semantic or instance segmentation, determines the fundamental direction of algorithm design. The characteristics of the image are important and can influence the choice of algorithm. For example, traditional methods offer robust and efficient solutions for high-contrast data with moderate SNR, whereas low-contrast, noisy images require more sophisticated segmentation algorithms at the cost of increased computational complexity.

Comparative analysis of recent survey papers

A notable trend in 2024 and 2025 is the growing number of review articles examining deep-learning-based methodologies across diverse domains. These surveys span classical, deep-learning, and transformer-based approaches, with some addressing general imaging and others focusing on medical and biomedical applications. Reviewing these works helps contextualize the distinct contribution of μCT biomedical imaging. However, despite the breadth of existing surveys, none specifically address the increasing demand for segmenting bench-top and synchrotron μCT biomedical imaging, underscoring the need for a dedicated synthesis. This section introduces representative survey studies on image segmentation, spanning general-purpose applications to medical research, to outline the current domain landscape and provide a reference point for assessing recent advances in image segmentation.

Recent survey articles on image processing

Among recent literature, a major survey on general-purpose image segmentation by Brar40 synthesizes both traditional and modern approaches, outlining classical thresholding methods such as Otsu’s variance maximization22, Kapur’s entropy41, and Tsallis entropy42, as well as optimization-based thresholding using GA43,44, PSO45,46, and ABC47,48. It further reviews edge-based and morphological operators, watershed segmentation32,49, region-growing and region-merging methods50–53, and ML-based models including MRFs54,55, Bayesian Networks56, Neural Networks57, and DCNNs58. Brar also surveys modern deep-learning architectures–2D/3D CNNs, FCN, V-Net, Mask R-CNN38, GAN-based models, hybrid 2D/3D networks, and transformer-based designs such as UNETR–highlighting their consistent superiority across general imaging domains.

Complementing this general perspective, Rayed et al.59 review deep-learning-based medical and biomedical segmentation, covering datasets, preprocessing, evaluation metrics, and leading architectures such as U-Net, FCN, DeepLab, and Mask R-CNN, while outlining challenges and future directions in semantic and instance segmentation. Neha et al.60 further categorize U-Net variants by imaging modality, recommending specialized architectures for X-ray61–66, MRI67–73, and CT74–80, while Azad et al.81 classify U-Net-based methods into six architectural enhancement categories. Additional surveys examine ML-based segmentation82, transformer-driven models83–85, disease-specific applications such as lung tumors86 and stroke83, semi-supervised learning87, and attention-based architectures88, collectively emphasizing the rise of deep-learning foundation models and the need for explainability and robustness. However, none of these surveys treat μCT as a distinct imaging domain or address the segmentation challenges unique to laboratory and synchrotron μCT.

Research gaps and goals

μCT biomedical image segmentation studies require a dedicated review because their datasets fundamentally differ from those of other common imaging systems, such as MRI, ultrasound, and even clinical CT89. For example, μCT images generally have a resolution ranging from 1 to 100 μm, which is higher than clinical CT (around 500 μm), MRI (100 μm to 1 mm), and ultrasound (sub-mm)90,91. In addition, reconstruction geometry differences between μCT and other imaging techniques lead to unique artifact profiles such as ring artifacts, beam hardening, and Partial Volume Effects (PVE)91. The laboratory or synchrotron experimental setup for biomedical studies also introduces different noise sources92, which need to be considered separately from those in clinical environments. Moreover, segmentation is further challenged by low contrast and heterogeneous composition, particularly in soft tissues where attenuation differences are minimal93. Finally, data-driven methods require high-quality annotations, yet generating large labeled μCT biomedical datasets is labor-intensive, making hybrid strategies that combine deep-learning with imaging-domain knowledge increasingly important94.

Although clinical and medical research share substantial areas of overlap with biomedical science, independent investigations focused specifically on biomedical studies continue to constitute a significant portion of the field. As introduced in Section “Recent survey articles on image processing”, most recent survey studies have either focused on deep-learning-based segmentation methods themselves or on the medical/clinical domain. There remains a need to comparatively review and evaluate segmentation methods tailored specifically for laboratory and synchrotron μCT. Therefore, conducting a literature review centered primarily on biomedical research remains both necessary and valuable.

This survey paper represents the first review to focus exclusively on segmentation methods for biomedical μCT imaging, encompassing both laboratory absorption-based μCT and synchrotron μCT as a bi-modality framework. The goal of this study is to bridge the gap between general image segmentation and the μCT-specific challenges, helping researchers develop appropriate segmentation methods for μCT datasets. The paper also aims to identify open challenges and future research directions for biomedical μCT.

Segmentation techniques for absorption-based μCT

The majority of lab-μCT systems use absorption-based imaging, where pixel intensity depends on the degree of X-ray attenuation across different materials. Synchrotron μCT can employ absorption contrast, phase contrast, or a combination of both. While lab-μCT is widely used due to its accessibility and suitability for general research environments, synchrotron facilities provide absorption imaging with highly coherent, high-intensity radiation that enables enhanced spatial resolution, improved SNR, and faster imaging speed. For most biomedical studies, however, phase-contrast imaging is preferred due to its ability to enhance soft tissue contrast where attenuation differences are minimal95. Despite differences in scale and performance, both modalities share the same fundamental imaging principles and support a broad range of biomedical applications. This section introduces several commonly applied segmentation techniques in biomedical lab-μCT studies, specifically for absorption-based μCT.

Thresholding-based segmentation

Image thresholding is a fundamental segmentation technique that partitions an image into foreground and background regions based on whether their pixel intensity values fall into a chosen range. Thresholding applies one or more threshold values to classify pixels into one or multiple groups, producing binary or multi-class segmented labels. Thresholding-based segmentation methods have been widely adopted in biomedical studies, including cortical and trabecular compartment analysis96, bone structure characterization97,98, COVID-19-AR investigations99, and many other applications. The major challenge of applying thresholding to biomedical materials is that thin or weakly contrasted structures are often misclassified. One improvement to this limitation is the use of hybrid methods that combine global and local thresholding with morphological operations, which can enhance the connectivity and accuracy of the resulting segmentations97,100.

However, thresholding-based segmentation methods encounter specific challenges in biomedical micro-CT imaging systems. A well-known limitation is the partial volume effect (PVE), in which a single voxel contains multiple tissue types, resulting in intermediate grayscale values that blur or smear boundary information. Selecting a single threshold value for thin structures such as trabeculae is therefore highly prone to error. This challenge can be solved by designing a hybrid method combining edge detection and local thresholding101 or using iterative reconstruction102. In addition, thresholding is generally less effective for soft tissues such as the brain, where μCT images show low contrast, overlapping intensities, and spatially uneven intensity distribution. To overcome these limitations, Nadkarni et al. developed a hybrid approach that combines thresholding and morphological operations with atlas-based labeling via diffeomorphic registration to a PCD μCT atlas103, enabling detailed brain morphometry while addressing registration issues noted in earlier MRI atlas studies104,105.

Thresholding-based segmentation performs well for high-density materials with high absorption contrast, where its reliability decreases when applied to complex morphologies, low-density tissues, or noisy data. Even though thresholding is still widely used in the biomedical domain, it lacks the robustness and efficiency needed for high-throughput workflows in large-scale imaging systems.

Region-based segmentation

The general definition of region-based segmentation is to partition an image by grouping neighboring pixels with similar intensity, color, or texture21. Unlike thresholding or edge-based methods, these approaches emphasize region homogeneity, making them effective for images with gradual transitions or weak boundaries. Representative methods include seeded region growing23,50, split-and-merge106, region-based active contours107, and morphological watershed49. This section specifically focuses on region-based methods commonly applied to biomedical μCT imaging.

Region growing

The foundation of region growing involves initiating segmentation from a set of seed points and expanding regions by incorporating neighboring pixels that exhibit predefined similarity criteria, such as intensity or color ranges, relative to the seed. 2D and 3D Region growing and its variations are widely applied in the medical domain108,109. However, due to properties of μCT such as higher resolution, more heterogeneous noise patterns, and more complex sample structures, region-growing segmentation can become sensitive and unreliable for some biomedical samples110.

Some successful applications of region-growing in biomedical μCT include midfoot bone segmentation using seeded region-growing111, as well as automated region growing (ARG) for analyzing trabecular bone microstructure in wrist specimens4. Semi-automatic region growing can also be applied to pulmonary nodule segmentation112 and lung cancer volume segmentation113, as tumors typically form connected structures with relatively homogeneous regional properties.

For high-contrast structures like bones, region growing is effective, producing spatially coherent and topologically connected regions with simple implementation and low computational cost. However, its performance depends heavily on seed-point selection and similarity criteria, which can introduce variability and reduce reproducibility. The method also struggles with low-density materials like soft tissue, where weak or diffuse boundaries often lead to segmentation leakage.

Morphological watershed segmentation

Morphological watershed segmentation treats a grayscale image as a topographic landscape with valleys and peaks, where pixel intensities represent elevation114. By simulating flooding from local minima, watershed lines form at points where advancing fronts meet, effectively defining object boundaries. The method yields closed contours and excels at separating touching or overlapping structures, making it valuable in biomedical imaging and materials science.

Watershed segmentation has been successfully applied across both renal imaging and biomaterial analysis. In kidney studies, Mua et al.115 evaluated edge-based, threshold-based, and marker-controlled watershed methods on grayscale CT scans, finding the marker-controlled approach most effective for isolating kidney stones within complex anatomy. In biomaterials research, Rossberg et al.116 demonstrated that applying watershed algorithms to grayscale intensity gradients accurately delineates irregular pore boundaries in hydrogels and scaffolds, outperforming traditional thresholding (as shown in Fig. 2). Likewise, Mirghafari et al.117 combined high-resolution μCT with filtering, thresholding, distance transforms, and watershed segmentation to isolate pores in meniscal extracellular matrices, enabling subsequent skeletonization and quantification of connectivity, tortuosity, and permeability. Collectively, these studies highlight watershed segmentation’s versatility in resolving complex anatomical and porous structures for improved structural and functional analysis.

In conclusion, watershed segmentation is an effective tool in biomedical imaging for segmenting cellular structures, tissue boundaries, and morphological features. It has benefit of producing closed contours and separating touching or overlapping objects with high precision. However, its sensitivity to noise and intensity variations often causes over-segmentation, requiring careful preprocessing or hybrid approaches. These challenges, along with the complexity of dynamic in vivo environments, limit its use in live imaging and complicated experimental scenarios.

Clustering-based segmentation

Clustering is an unsupervised segmentation approach that groups pixels into regions based on feature similarity, such as intensity, color, or texture110. Methods like k-means, mean shift, and Gaussian mixture models use different strategies to form clusters whose internal similarity exceeds that between clusters. Clustering is especially convenient when no seed points are available, requiring only the desired number of output classes.

To perform accurate segmentation of lung tumor burden in genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs), Montgomery et al. developed the Mouse Lung Automated Segmentation Tool (MLAST), which employs clustering-based segmentation to automate tissue classification within the thoracic cavity118. As described in Fig. 3, MLAST applies a one-dimensional k-means clustering algorithm to voxel intensity values, segmenting the lung field into three density-based tissue categories, soft tissue, intermediate, and lung, without the need for contrast agents. Clustering is also proven efficient when combining with deep-encoder to segmenting intravascular OCT images119.

Clustering-based segmentation is computationally efficient and adaptable to heterogeneous biomedical images. Its drawbacks include sensitivity to noise, lack of spatial context, and difficulty with overlapping intensities. Also, due to the nature of lower contrast, clustering-based segmentation is rarely used in soft tissue μCT imaging.

Random-walk segmentation

Random-walk segmentation, first proposed by Grady in 2006120, is a graph-based image segmentation technique that models pixel connectivity using probabilistic paths. Random-walk algorithm treats the image as a weighted graph, where each pixel is a node and edges represent similarity between neighboring pixels. Given a set of labeled starter seed points, the algorithm computes the probability that a random walker starting from an unlabeled pixel will reach a particular seed, assigning the pixel to the label with the highest probability121. Random-walk segmentation is particularly advantageous for its robustness to noise and ability to produce smooth, coherent boundaries, making it suitable for complex and low-contrast images.

Random-walk has been applied in biomedical μCT segmentation. For example, Ruiz Pujadas et al.122,123 used it for cochlear implant planning, integrating random walks with a statistical shape model from non-rigid registration to incorporate both intensity and anatomical constraints. Their approach handles weak boundaries, preserves complex topology, and adapts to local gradients through a confidence map, though scalability may be limited by training data and computational cost. Another study shows that prior knowledge can enhance the performance of random walk segmentation for lung tumor124.

Furthermore, random-walk is also valuable for interpolating 3D segmentation from sparse annotations. Biomedisa125 generates high-quality volumetric labels from a few orthogonal annotated slices using adaptive weighted random walks and parameter-free interpolation, accelerating biomedical workflows and supporting training-data generation for deep-learning models. Engelkes et al. compared Biomedisa with several other segmentation methodologies, including thresholding, watershed, neural networks, and segment bone from synthetic and real CT volumes126. Their study shows that Biomedisa performs well for low-CNR volumes or in cases where local thresholding is too time-consuming.

Despite its theoretical appeal, random walk segmentation remains underutilized in biomedical studies due to challenges such as complex sample environments and difficulty in automatic seed selection for μCT images. Manual seed placement raises labor cost, and the algorithm is sensitive to signal noise and imaging artifacts. These limitations have prompted a shift toward machine learning-based segmentation methods, with random walk increasingly serving as a supporting component within hybrid frameworks.

Deep-learning-based image segmentation

While the previous sections focus on traditional algorithm-driven methodologies, this section shifts toward modern data-driven approaches, particularly deep-learning-based segmentation methods such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Vision Transformers. As noted in Section “Image segmentation”, CNNs have become central to biomedical image analysis, demonstrating strong performance in segmenting biomedical μCT images, which supports a wide range of downstream analytical tasks. By learning spatial hierarchies directly from raw data through stacked convolutional layers, CNNs overcome the limitations of traditional methods that rely on handcrafted features and rule-based algorithm design, thereby shifting the paradigm of image segmentation into a new era. Section “Comparative analysis of recent survey papers” has listed many recent survey studies that discuss about deep-learning-based methods for general purpose and medical/clinical purpose. Thus, this section will concentrate on reviewing deep-learning-based image segmentation applications specifically for absorption contrast biomedical μCT imaging.

Fully connected network and U-Net

Fully Convolutional Networks (FCNs) have become foundational tools in biomedical micro-CT analysis, enabling precise, voxel-level segmentation of complex anatomical structures even in low-contrast or pathology-altered imaging conditions. U-Net, as one of the most popular variation of FCN, adds a symmetric encoder-decoder structure with skip connections that pass high-resolution spatial features from the downsampling path to the upsampling path. Based on these architectural principles, U-Net has become one of the most commonly used method in biomedical micro-CT analysis, where its ability to recover fine spatial detail enables reliable segmentation of delicate anatomical structures even under challenging imaging conditions.

U-Net has been proven effective for segmenting bone microarchitecture in many studies. For example, Caron et al. introduced a two-stage pipeline using YOLOv4 for region localization followed by U-Net segmentation of trabecular microcracks, improving speed and reducing false positives despite sensitivity to contrast variations127. Leger et al. applied a 3D U-Net to segment mineralized cartilage at the Achilles tendon interface, enhancing spatial coherence and anatomical robustness while maintaining accuracy under downsampling128. Another study also shows the potential of U-Net segmenting the bone ingrowth through the biodegradable scaffold implants129.

In addition, U-Net is also often selected as a solution for lung segmentation challenges. For instance, Vincenzi et al. proposed a fully automated FCN pipeline to segment right and left lungs in murine μCT images. The model effectively handles the density changes caused by pulmonary fibrosis, which typically require manual segmentation130. Their work was later extended to integrate 3D μCT scans with 2D histological data to perform semi-automatic segmentation of lung fibrosis in a mouse model131. Similarly, Fer et al. developed a robust 3D U-Net for lung cancer segmentation132. Their model was trained on over 3000 scans to segment lungs in healthy mice and in those with lung cancer and fibrosis. The model achieved a Dice score of 0.92, matching human reader performance while significantly reducing manual processing time.

Furthermore, a recent architecture, TotalSegmentator, has achieved significant success in automatically delineating 104 anatomic structures on CT images, including organs, bones, muscles, and vessels133. TotalSegmentator introduces a deep-learning-based segmentation model built on the nnU-Net framework, trained on a large and diverse dataset of 1204 routine clinical CT examinations spanning multiple scanners, contrast phases, and pathologies. The model achieves high accuracy, with a Dice score of 0.943, and demonstrates strong robustness even in challenging or abnormal cases. By publicly releasing both the annotated dataset and the pretrained model, this study provides an accessible and comprehensive tool that streamlines radiologic research workflows and supports applications such as organ volumetry, disease characterization, and surgical or radiation therapy planning. However, TotalSegmentator was not built or trained to handle the μCT image modality, and therefore its performance on μCT data is not guaranteed.

In conclusion, U-Net and its variations have achieved significant success in biomedical μCT image segmentation tasks. Benefiting from to their robustness and high accuracy, they are widely deployed in many biomedical studies involving diverse sample types and imaging setups. However, it has also been observed that training a U-Net model requires substantial annotated training data, which can be labor- and time-intensive to obtain. In addition, training a specialized U-Net model demands considerable computational and memory resources, making it infeasible on low-end computers. Moreover, U-Net’s performance is sensitive to noise and artifacts, where a changes in noise distribution or artifact characteristics can significantly degrade model performance. Thus, careful preprocessing design is essential. Section “Potentially applicable methods for SR-PCI-μCT: image denoising and data-efficient learning” will discuss how preprocessing methods such as denoising can assist segmentation.

Vision transformer

FCN and its U-Net variants have demonstrated strong performance in biomedical image segmentation, especially for organs and bone. However, their reliance on local convolutions limits their ability to capture long-range dependencies and multi-scale contextual information134, which can reduce accuracy for complex structures with variable shapes and sizes. Modern architectures partially mitigate these limitations through mechanisms such as dilated convolutions, attention modules in hybrid CNN-Transformer designs, and multi-scale feature pyramids.

Transformers, introduced by Vaswani et al.135, shifted this paradigm by using self-attention to capture global relationships rather than local receptive fields. Self-attention is a mechanism that lets each element of an input sequence weigh and integrate information from all other elements to decide what matters most. Originally developed for machine translation, Transformers were later adapted to vision tasks; Dosovitskiy et al. introduced the Vision Transformer (ViT)134, which processes images as patch tokens and applies self-attention across them, enabling more effective modeling of long-range spatial interactions than traditional CNNs.

ViTs have been deployed in many medical and clinical studies, such as multi-organ CT and ACDC cardiac MRI segmentation136, fine-grained 3D vessel analysis in coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA)137, various segmentation tasks in retinal vessel studies138–141, segmenting strokes83, among others. However, their application specifically to biomedical μCT image segmentation remains under active development. Accordingly, the subsequent review synthesizes current applications of Vision Transformer-based segmentation techniques within biomedical laboratory μCT to elucidate their present capabilities and limitations.

Breakthrough transformer applications for biomedical μCT segmentation

Two notable applications for multi-organ segmentation are ViTBIS and EMSViT, proposed by Sagar et al., which combine multi-scale convolutions with attention-based tokenization to capture global context, achieving superior performance on datasets such as Synapse, BraTS 2019, and spleen CT compared with CNNs and earlier transformer models142,143. Another study proposed by Jiang et al. evaluates Swin UNETR for automated multi-organ mouse micro-CT segmentation, aiming to improve accuracy and robustness across varying imaging conditions144. In jiang’s study, Swin UNETR uses a hierarchical Swin Transformer encoder with a CNN-based decoder, and its performance consistently outperforms nnU-Net and AI-based mouse organ segmentation system (AIMOS) in terms of the average Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) and the Hausdorff distance (HD95p). In addition, Lagzouli et al. developed DBAHNet, a dual-branch hybrid network integrating CNNs and transformers for segmenting cortical and trabecular bone in high-resolution 3D μCT scans of mouse tibiae145. By leveraging attention mechanisms for both fine-detail extraction and global context modeling, DBAHNet achieved improved accuracy and smoother boundaries. Despite their strong performance, these transformer-based models remain computationally demanding and less suited to resource-limited environments.

Segment anything model and it medical and biomedical μCT variants

In 2023, Meta AI released the Segment Anything Model (SAM), which marked a shift in computer vision from task-specific models to a prompt-capable segmentation approach146. The SAM architecture consists of a ViT-based image encoder, a prompt encoder, and a powerful mask decoder to perform segmentation. The model was trained on billions of masked images, revolutionizing natural image processing.

The first and most influential medical adaptation model of SAM is MedSAM, proposed by Ma et al., first published in April 2023147. MedSAM follows SAM’s basic structure, but was specifically fine-tuned for medical images, addressing SAM’s limitations in processing low-contrast gray-scale images. It is trained on a large-scale dataset containing more than one million masked images across over 10 imaging modalities, including CT, MRI, endoscopy, ultrasound, X-ray, pathology, fundus, dermoscopy, mammography, and OCT. Overall, MedSAM outperforms SAM and specialized U-Net models, demonstrates strong generalization to unseen modalities. Another notable medical adaptation model of SAM is the Medical SAM Adapter (Med-SA), proposed by Wu et al. in December 2023148. Med-SA was designed to address the need for 3D segmentation in volumetric modalities such as CT and MRI, which SAM cannot natively handle. Med-SA modifies SAM by inserting lightweight adapter modules that inject medical-specific knowledge, and it adapts SAM to 3D modalities through its Space-Depth Transpose (SD-Trans) mechanism, which lets the 2D attention blocks jointly learn spatial and slice-wise depth correlations. Med-SA achieves state-of-the-art performance across 17 medical segmentation tasks by efficiently adapting SAM with only 2% tunable parameters, but its SD-Trans design assumes relatively thick, clinically oriented 3D slices and is therefore not directly compatible with biomedical μCT volumes where depth correlations behave very differently. Other medical adaptations models of SAM, such as SAM-Med3D149 and SAM2150, expanded SAM’s compatibility to 3D modality and even video segmentation. However, none of those variations were trained on micro-CT images, which limited their performance on biomedical μCT modality.

Since SAM and its variations are still under active development, there are no existing studies that directly adapt SAM models to biomedical μCT imaging. However, a few applications have adapted SAM models to microscopy imaging modalities, which point out the potential of future direction adopting SAM on μCT modality. For example, Segment Anything for Microscopy (μSAM) is introduced by Archit et al. as a domain-adapted SAM extension that enables accurate, unified segmentation and tracking across diverse light and electron microscopy modalities151. μSAM develops a full fine-tuning pipeline that retrains all components of the model on microscopy-specific datasets, yielding substantial improvements in both interactive and automatic segmentation. The resulting light microscopy model and electron microscopy models consistently outperform the default SAM and achieve performance comparable to specialized tools such as CellPose152 and MitoNet153 across a wide range of microscopy benchmarks. However, μSAM has not been applied to μCT, and therefore its performance on biomedical μCT data remains undetermined.

Conclusion

Overall, the methods reviewed in this section highlight common strategies for tackling segmentation challenges in biomedical-μCT, particularly absorption-based laboratory systems. Table 2 summarizes the techniques and their applications. Thresholding offers a simple and efficient solution but struggles with low-contrast samples such as soft tissues. Region-based methods better handle uneven brightness and maintain connectivity, yet their sensitivity to noise and weak boundaries limits performance in many biomedical cases. Random-walk algorithms are more robust to noise and produce smooth boundaries, but they rely on user-defined seeds and perform poorly on thin, elongated structures like vessels154, reducing their suitability for certain sample types. At last, traditional segmentation methods differ in how strongly they react to parameter choices: watershed is the most sensitive, followed by region growing, then clustering-based and graph-based approaches, while thresholding remains the least sensitive and generally the most stable.

As for modern deep-learning-based methodologies, FCN and U-Net have dominated image segmentation for nearly a decade due to their robustness and high accuracy, despite requiring large amounts of domain-specific annotated data and substantial computational resources. This transition from algorithm-driven to data-driven approaches reshaped the segmentation paradigm in the 2010s. Since the 2020s, vision transformers have further transformed the field by replacing convolutional inductive biases with global self-attention, enabling more effective modeling of long-range dependencies. More recently, the introduction of SAM and its variants has shifted the landscape again by enabling prompt-driven, general-purpose segmentation without task-specific training, moving the field toward flexible, foundation-model-based frameworks. Medical-domain adaptations of SAM further highlight the potential for developing general segmentation models capable of handling diverse imaging modalities without relying on domain-specific annotations.

Many other models similar to SAM and its variants, including the Mamba architecture and its derivatives155–157, have been developed for medical imaging tasks using common modalities such as CT and MRI, which limits their applicability to more specialized domains. Despite substantial progress in image analysis, no model has been designed specifically for biomedical μCT data, largely due to their ultra-high resolutions. A major challenge is that most models struggle with resolutions in the 2–8 K range, often requiring down-sampling or domain-specific adaptations158. This gap underscores the need for approaches that account for the unique resolution demands, structural characteristics, and domain-specific properties inherent to biomedical micro-computed tomography.

Segmentation techniques for phase contrast-based μCT

Section “Segmentation techniques for absorption-based μCT” provided an overview of image segmentation methodologies spanning traditional techniques and modern deep-learning approaches, including thresholding, region-based algorithms, random-walker methods, fully convolutional networks (FCNs), U-Net variants, and Vision Transformer architectures. When transitioning from absorption-based μCT to phase-contrast μCT in SR-PCI-μCT, the imaging context changes fundamentally. Absorption contrast is highly effective for visualizing high-density structures such as bone but offers limited soft-tissue differentiation. In contrast, SR-PCI-μCT leverages X-ray refraction and scattering to enhance soft-tissue visibility, thereby addressing one of the principal limitations of conventional μCT.

Because these two imaging modalities produce inherently different contrast mechanisms, artifact appearance and noise characteristics, segmentation strategies developed for absorption-based μCT do not always translate directly to phase-contrast data. Accordingly, this section reviews recent studies applying segmentation methods to SR-PCI-μCT datasets and examines how methodological choices evolve to meet the unique challenges of phase-contrast imaging. In parallel with the structure of the previous section, this section surveys image segmentation methodologies for SR-PCI-μCT, with the overarching goal of clarifying the distinctions between segmentation approaches in absorption-based versus phase-contrast μCT.

Thresholding-based segmentation for SR-PCI-μCT

Thresholding-based segmentation is generally more challenging in SR-PCI-μCT than in attenuation μCT due to fundamental differences in imaging physics and contrast mechanisms. In SR-PCI-μCT, the high spatial coherence of synchrotron X-ray beams enables phase-contrast imaging, which introduces edge-enhancement fringes and non-monotonic intensity transitions that violate the assumptions underlying thresholding. These effects arise because phase contrast produces oscillatory, non-step-like intensity variations at material boundaries, rather than the monotonic transitions typical of absorption-based imaging. As a result, thresholding-based methods often require specialized adaptations or must be combined with additional processing steps to remain effective.

The first case in which thresholding-based methods perform well is when samples are properly dyed, resulting in images with improved contrast. For example, Norvik et al. proposed an SR-PCI-μCT setup to visualize and analyze alveolar capillary dysplasia with misaligned pulmonary veins (ACD/MPV)159. The study employed a dual-mode segmentation strategy tailored to the contrast properties of the imaged tissue. In ACD/MPV samples, automatic segmentation was enabled by radiodense vascular dyes, which produced strong attenuation differences and allowed the software to isolate and render dye-filled vessels in three dimensions. In contrast, non-injected control tissue required manual segmentation. The dyeing process enhances the intensity difference between the ROI and the background, enabling straightforward thresholding. However, dyeing techniques may not be suitable for all sample types, as the process is non-reversible and cannot be applied to specimens preserved for later use.

In addition, in SR-PCI-μCT experiments, bone structures exhibit good contrast, similar to the absorption-contrast imaging setups discussed in Section “Thresholding-based segmentation”. Mölich et al. proposed an improved approach for segmenting osteocyte lacunae in human bone imaged with SRμCT, aiming to enhance the reliability of morphometric analysis for these small and variable structures160. Their method, Kangaroo, performs an initial over-segmentation, isolates each lacuna as an individual region of interest, applies a size-adaptive black top-hat transform, and computes a local Otsu threshold based on the surrounding gray-value distribution, followed by morphological filtering to remove false positives. Compared with global Otsu thresholding and fixed-size filtering, this adaptive strategy yields more consistent boundaries and reduces both under- and over-segmentation.

In conclusion, the inherent simplicity of thresholding-based algorithms limits their performance in complex sample structures or low-contrast images. However, they remain one of the most straightforward solutions when samples exhibit high contrast under SR-PCI-μCT setups. For samples with high density contrast or compatible with dye-based contrast enhancement, thresholding can still serve as a primary option.

Edge-based segmentation for SR-PCI-μCT

Edge-based segmentation identifies object boundaries by detecting gradient changes, assuming that strong gray-value discontinuities correspond to meaningful edges. In absorption-based X-ray μCT, boundaries in soft or low-density materials are often weak due to limited attenuation. However, SR-PCI-μCT phase contrast enhance boundary visibility by capturing refractive index variations, making edge-based segmentation more effective.

For example, Lee et al. used SR-PCI-μCT to visualize the human superior colliculus in 3D without contrast agents161. To address the low and uneven absorption of unstained soft tissue, they developed an edge-based segmentation strategy using the Deriche-Canny detector. As illustrated in Fig. 4, multi-scale median filtering was applied to suppress noise and enhance vessel boundaries, followed by logical edge combination and morphological closing to robustly delineate vascular spaces. This approach is particularly effective for identifying large connected structures such as blood vessels. By integrating this edge-based method with thresholding-better suited for high-atomic-weight components like neuromelanin with iron-Lee et al. further established a 3D visualization protocol for analyzing unstained brain anatomy162.

With the advent of phase contrast imaging, which enhances boundary visibility by capturing subtle refraction-induced intensity variations, edge-based methods have become more applicable to biomedical μCT. However, their performance declines in complex in situ or in vivo settings, where boundaries remain ambiguous and noise or structural heterogeneity is high. As a result, edge-based segmentation in SR-PCI-μCT often benefits from integration with region-based or machine learning approaches to achieve reliable and robust results.

Region-based segmentation for SR-PCI-μCT

Section “Region-based segmentation” provides a classification of region-based image segmentation techniques, outlining their underlying principles, operational mechanisms, and distinctive characteristics. It also reviews several absorption-based biomedical μCT studies that utilize region-based segmentation to investigate internal specimen structures, supporting tissue identification and contributing to advancements in disease modeling, drug development, and diagnostic research. In the context of SR-PCI-μCT imaging, however, conventional region-based methods often prove inadequate due to the increased complexity and resolution of the images110. Factors such as highly complex in vivo or in situ anatomical details can compromise segmentation accuracy. As a result, more sophisticated and adaptive approaches are required to address the challenges posed by SR-PCI-μCT applications. This section outlines the application of various region-based segmentation techniques in SR-PCI-μCT biomedical bone studies, along with a discussion of their advantages and limitations.

Region growing

As mentioned in Section “Thresholding-based segmentation for SR-PCI-μCT”, stained specimens provide higher sample contrast, which enables traditional region-growing segmentation on SR-PCI-μCT imaging. For example, a recent study by Tkachev et al. imaged iodine-stained patient-derived orthotopic xenograft (PDOX) specimens of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, enabling non-destructive visualization of intact tumor-tissue architecture163. A semi-automatic segmentation workflow based on gray-value interval selection, combining region-growing algorithms with manual refinement, was developed to reliably distinguish tumor tissue from surrounding organs. Although SR-PCI-μCT generally provides better contrast and higher SNR compared with absorption-contrast lab-μCT, segmenting soft tissue using region-growing methods remains challenging without staining techniques.

Watershed

Breast cancer-induced bone metastases involve abnormal vascularization and bone degradation. While SR-PCI-μCT allows high-resolution imaging without contrast agents, segmentation remains difficult due to poor bone-vessel contrast. To acquire high-accuracy segmentation of bone metastases, Xu et al. developed a method combines marker-controlled watershed with a monogenic signal phase-asymmetry control surface to accurately separate bone, vessels, and background in SR-uCT images164. Markers for each class are generated using hysteresis thresholding and morphological thinning, ensuring low false-positive seeds in all connected components. Since gradient magnitude fails at low-contrast bone-vessel interfaces, the method computes multi-scale phase asymmetry from the 3D monogenic signal, exploiting its intensity-invariant, edge-sensitive properties to produce a superior control surface for watershed flooding. This combination yields improved segmentation accuracy, achieving the higher Dice scores than hysteresis thresholding and gradient-based marker-controlled watershed.

Clustering + CNN

Clustering can be applied in SR-PCI-μCT segmentation of osteoporotic and COVID-19-affected bone tissues. To address the challenges faced by traditional segmentation methods in recognizing these fine-grained differences, Poles et al. proposed a convolutional neural network (CNN) combined with clustering techniques165. This approach extracts entropy-filtered image patches and applies K-means clustering to features derived from a pre-trained VGG16 model, enabling the identification of latent structural patterns and improving segmentation accuracy. This clustering strategy allows for more precise differentiation of bone conditions at the microscale.

Random walk segmentation

Random walk segmentation has been demonstrated effective in segmenting porous biomedical materials. For instance, Schotte et al. devised a semi-automated pipeline for segmenting the lacuno-canalicular system (LCN) in stingray tesserae, a porous network within mineralized cartilage166. As shown in Fig. 5, this pipeline integrates marker-based watershed segmentation, ambient occlusion fields, and contour-tree segmentation with a random-walk distance transform to isolate and distinguish complex structures. This multi-step methodology efficiently processes porous anatomical features, manages connectivity and morphological variability with minimal manual intervention, and facilitates high-throughput analysis of thousands of lacunae. Nevertheless, the dependence on commercial software and custom modules might impede reproducibility. Overall, this approach offers a robust framework for quantifying biological porosity.

Although this examples demonstrated that random walk segmentation has the potential to address segmentation challenges in SR-PCI-μCT studies, it still faces several limitations. First random walk algorithms require seed points as input, which are often difficult to obtain automatically. Manual input, on the other hand, is impractical for high-throughput data processing pipelines. Furthermore, random walk segmentation is highly sensitive to noise, complex texture, and uneven intensity distribution, which reduces its performance in low-dose imaging or in complex in vivo and in situ biomedical studies. Consequently, random walk segmentation is more often employed as an assisting tool rather than a primary method in biomedical SR-PCI-μCT applications.

Conclusion

Compared with laboratory absorption-contrast μCT, synchrotron phase-contrast images generally exhibit higher SNR, different noise distributions, and unique artifacts and contrast characteristics. Traditional image segmentation algorithms therefore often require adaptation to accommodate these new image features. Among all methodologies mentioned in this section, region-based methods demonstrate greater adaptability in SR-PCI-μCT biomedical studies due to the contrast enhancement provided by phase retrieval. With improved contrast and clearer boundaries, region-based segmentation methods offer higher compatibility while preserving simplicity and efficiency. However, these methods still encounter challenges similar to those in absorption-contrast μCT modalities, particularly when dealing with higher-resolution data and finer anatomical structures, especially in in vivo and in situ datasets.

Deep-learning-based segmentation for SR-PCI-μCT

Section “Deep-learning-based Image Segmentation” reviews deep-learning-based segmentation models for absorption-based μCT biomedical image segmentation tasks, demonstrating the power of deep-learning approaches and highlighting improvements in accuracy, robustness, throughput, and the overall versatility of data-driven techniques. However, when the focus shifts to SR-PCI-μCT, the substantial need for large volumes of annotated training data becomes a major limitation. Due to the lack of a widely recognized, publicly available SR-PCI-μCT dataset that serves as a community benchmark, training task-specific deep-learning models has become a significant roadblock for many biomedical studies. Annotating high-quality masked training data is time- and labor-intensive, typically requiring multiple specialized experts to label the same dataset. This dependence on expert annotation further increases the difficulty of producing sufficient training data, thereby limiting the deployment of deep-learning-based segmentation in SR-PCI-μCT biomedical research.

U-Net designed for SR-PCI-μCT imaging

Recent studies show that deep-learning-based models, especially U-Net, remain compatible with biomedical SR-PCI-μCT image segmentation tasks when sufficient training data are available.

For example, Xu et al. combined a 3D nnU-Net with marker-controlled watershed refinement to delineate chondrocyte and osteocyte lacunae in synchrotron phase-contrast μCT, reducing over-segmentation and false positives167. Extending these methods to nanoscale porosity, Silveira et al. trained U-Net and Sensor3D models on Zernike phase-contrast nano-CT of zebrafish vertebrae affected by halo and shade-off artifacts, demonstrating strong artifact resilience and cross-beamline transferability168. In bone studies, Baltruschat et al. employed a scaled two-dimensional U-Net architecture with systematically adjusted model width, depth, and input size to segment biodegradable magnesium-based bone implant images into bone, residual material, and degradation layer169. As shown in Fig. 6, using multi-axis slicing and soft voting to incorporate 3D context while maintaining computational feasibility, this method achieved a mean Intersection over Union of 0.906 and outperformed semi-automatic workflows. In related work by Xu et al., SR-PCI-μCT was used to quantitatively analyze bone, blood vessels, and metastases in murine tibiae affected by breast cancer170. Segmentation was performed using the self-configuring nnU-Net framework, which required minimal training data, improved accuracy over watershed-based methods, and reduced computation time, although metastases remained challenging to segment due to low contrast and the need for manual annotation during training. Furthermore, another bone study by Liu et al. adopted U-Net++ for SR-PCI-CT images to segment fracture callus in diabetic and non-diabetic mice171. In Liu’s study, U-Net++ was trained using 800 SR-PCI-CT images that were randomly selected as the training set. The model showed reliable segmentation capability, reaching accuracies of 0.69, 0.81, and 0.733 with corresponding Dice scores of 0.72, 0.83, and 0.76 for reserve/proliferative, hypertrophic, and mineralized cartilage, respectively.

In addition, segmentation of soft tissue structures in SR-PCI-μCT presents unique challenges due to subtle contrast variations and complex morphology. For example, Paiva et al. proposed a study evaluated semiautomatic and automatic methods for analyzing the lenses of the frog Thoropa miliaris, comparing Interpolation, Watershed, and U-Net algorithms on high-resolution volumetric datasets acquired at the IMX beamline of the Brazilian Synchrotron Light Laboratory172. Interpolation achieved the highest Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) of 97.02% but required extensive manual input, Watershed reached 95.41% with sensitivity to gradient variations, and U-Net, while less precise DSC score of 89.29%, reduced segmentation time to seconds and was robust with limited training data. In addition, Riberti et al. proposed a study on the microstructural organization of peri-implant collagen bundles during wound healing using SRμCT with phase-contrast at the SYRMEP beamline of the ELETTRA Synchrotron Facility173. A U-Net-based segmentation, trained on manually annotated slices with data augmentation, enabled orientation-specific classification of transversal and longitudinal fibers, outperforming Otsu thresholding in resolving fiber intertwinement and achieving higher connectivity density, though limited by small training datasets and the need for careful tuning to avoid overfitting.

Furthermore, Yadis et al. establish a study of vascular segmentation in human organs using Hierarchical Phase-Contrast Tomography (HiP-CT) for capturing multi-scale vascular structures174. The HiP-CT data were acquired ex vivo at the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility, producing high-resolution volumetric images with voxel sizes ranging from approximately 20 to 1 μm in targeted regions of interest. The segmentation task was performed using the nnU-Net framework, a self-configuring deep-learning architecture based on the U-Net family, trained on a kidney datasets annotated by two domain experts. The advantages of this approach include its adaptability to heterogeneous datasets, high Dice similarity scores indicating strong voxel-level agreement, and the ability to capture complex vascular hierarchies. However, limitations were observed in segmenting collapsed large vessels, reduced connectivity in fine vessels, and boundary inaccuracies, particularly for structures approaching the resolution limit, which can impact the continuity of the vascular tree and downstream quantitative analyses.

Vision transformer for SR-PCI-μCT imaging

Section “Vision Transformer” has discussed a broad range of U-Net-based and ViT-based applications for biomedical μCT, as well as the SAM architecture and its recently developed variants for medical image segmentation. Despite the power and success of vision transformer models in medical studies and absorption-contrast biomedical μCT, their application in SR-PCI-μCT remains limited due to the lack of suitable training datasets.

To address this challenge, vision transformers can be combined with other methods to create a hybrid training-testing network. For example, Vršnak et al. proposed a hybrid method for tissue segmentation in synchrotron X-ray images of endomyocardial biopsy samples175. This study introduces a two-stage segmentation pipeline designed to overcome the scarcity of expert-annotated data in synchrotron X-ray phase-contrast imaging (X-PCI) of endomyocardial biopsy (EMB) samples. First, experts manually annotate up to three slices per biopsy by providing point-based prompts to the SAM2 foundational segmentation model. SAM2 then propagates these annotations across the remaining slices using its memory bank and attention mechanisms, producing approximately 8000 automatically generated masks from only a small number of manually guided slices. In the second stage, a 2D U-Net architecture is trained slice-by-slice using the SAM2-generated masks as supervision. The U-Net trained on SAM2-generated annotations significantly outperforms both traditional image processing approaches (e.g., thresholding, edge detection) and U-Nets trained solely on the limited expert-annotated dataset.

Conclusion

As shown in Table 3, when sufficient training data are available, deep-learning-based segmentation of SR-PCI-μCT biomedical images continues to achieve high performance, often surpassing traditional methods. However, many biomedical SR-PCI-μCT studies remain constrained by the scarcity of annotated training data. Although recent advances in deep learning, such as U-Net, YOLO, and self-configuring frameworks such as nnU-Net, have demonstrated improvements in robustness, execution speed, and accuracy even under limited training conditions, data scarcity still poses a significant challenge. Continued progress in model design, data augmentation, and annotation strategies will be essential to ensure consistent performance across diverse tissue types and imaging conditions.

Potentially applicable methods for SR-PCI-μCT: image denoising and data-efficient learning

Section “Segmentation Techniques for Absorption-based µCT” and Section “Segmentation Techniques for Phase Contrast-based µCT” discussed a wide range of deep-learning-based models that address segmentation challenges in biomedical μCT studies. One standout conclusion from this survey is that most segmentation methodologies remain highly sensitive to image noise. Although SR-PCI-μCT imaging provides enhanced SNR and contrast, noise continues to be a major factor affecting segmentation accuracy. Moreover, Section “Segmentation Techniques for Phase Contrast-based µCT” specifically highlights that a critical limitation in developing deep-learning-based segmentation models is the lack of publicly available, expert-annotated, domain-specific training datasets. Most existing benchmark datasets for absorption-based μCT cannot be directly used for SR-PCI-μCT model training.

One potential solution to this problem is to pre-process the training and testing datasets so that they exhibit similar image properties. Such adjustments may include re-sizing, contrast alignment (adjusting the contrast of one dataset to match another), artifact removal, and denoising. Due to the nature of deep-learning-based models, their performance improves when the testing dataset shares similar image characteristics with the training data. This section serves as a side topic to Section “Segmentation Techniques for Phase Contrast-based µCT”, focusing on recent image denoising techniques and common methodologies that can mitigate the shortage of training data for SR-PCI-μCT. In addition, although the discussion includes approaches relevant to both lab-μCT and synchrotron imaging, the intent is to explore strategies particularly useful for addressing synchrotron-specific challenges under certain conditions, such as low-dose imaging or strong artifacts introduced by auxiliary equipment.

Image denoising in SR-PCI-μCT

Denoising is a critical step in general image processing. A well-designed denoising algorithm enhances visual image quality, facilitates subsequent processing steps, and improves the accuracy of quantitative analysis. Denoising can also help domain adaptation to reduce the amount of annotation task, which will be discussed in later sections. Traditional denoising techniques, such as Gaussian filtering, median filtering, and bilateral filtering, offer straightforward noise reduction by averaging pixel values based on predefined rules. However, these methods often introduce blurring, leading to the loss of edges and fine structural details that are essential in biomedical studies. Additionally, traditional approaches typically require dataset-specific algorithm design, which limits their generalizability and compatibility across diverse imaging datasets. Modern deep-learning models offers an alternative approach by data-driven denoising, which can learn the noise distribution directly from the dataset and performs accurate denoising with minimal distortion. The deep-learning-based denoising can be trained by two ways: fully supervised learning or self-learning.

Denoising with fully-supervised Learning

In the fully-supervised learning paradigm, the model is trained on a large set of input-output pairs, where an algorithm learns to map input data to the corresponding output based on these labeled examples. The term “fully supervised” indicates that the model is trained exclusively on labeled datasets, with the goal of learning the relationship between input and output pairs to accurately predict outcomes for new, unseen data. The primary advantage of full-supervised learning is its high accuracy, where the major drawback is the need for large volume of training data.

In denoising tasks, manually generating a clean reference image is often impractical. Instead, input and output image pairs can be acquired using different imaging modalities or devices applied to the same sample. For example, in the study proposed by Muller et al., training pairs are acquired by a low-dose (LD) and a high-dose (HD) ex vivo μCT scans of 38 mice using the X-CUBE benchtop system, employing two predefined protocols-General-Purpose (GP) for LD and High-Resolution (HR) for HD imaging176. The denoising methodology involved training a 2D U-Net and a 3D U-Net, both designed to restore high-quality images from noisy LD scans. Unlike many prior studies that simulate LD images, this work used real paired LD and HD images for supervised training, enhancing realism and applicability. Another study proposed by Mathew et al. explores deep-learning to improve fast polychromatic X-ray μCT imaging of multiphase flow in porous media, where slow scans are unsuitable due to fluid dynamics177. Mathew’s study proposed a cycle-consistent GAN (cycleGAN), trained on unpaired datasets to transform noisy fast scans into high-quality images. Using Bentheimer sandstone under partial brine saturation, the model outperformed traditional filters in noise reduction and structural preservation. Morphological and blind evaluations confirmed its effectiveness for real-time pore-scale analysis in geological imaging.

Furthermore, a Ding et al.178 develops an edge view enhanced phase retrieval(EVEPR), a deep-learning-based enhancement method that improves phase-contrast micro-CT images of low-density materials by combining the complementary information in denoised edge-enhanced contrast (EEC) images and phase-retrieved (PR) images. The method performs CNN-based denoising and phase-retrieval enhancement by training on paired denoised EEC and PR images, allowing the network to learn high-frequency edge details from EEC and consistent area contrast from PR images. The CNN prediction result showed enhanced area contrast beyond conventional PR algorithms while improving SNR and CNR. By producing images with higher SNR, higher CNR, and more consistent grey values, EVEPR greatly improves segmentation accuracy and efficiency, achieving Dice scores above 0.9 and reducing manual adjustments, which allowes for more accurate segmentation.

In conclusion, training a fully supervised denoising model requires paired images with independently distributed noise, enabling the network to learn a mapping that removes noise while preserving structural details. This design avoids manual labeling, which is normally impractical for denoising tasks. Deep denoising methods supports the generation of large, reproducible, and transferable training datasets across samples, datasets, and imaging conditions. However, in some experimental setups, such as SR-PCI-μCT inline systems, obtaining paired images is difficult due to limited space for dual detectors and the need for consistent device orientation, which can introduce alignment issues and hinder reliable pair acquisition.

Denoising with self-supervised learning

In many scientific scenarios-such as astronomical telescope imaging or ultra-fast imaging-acquiring paired clean and noisy images is difficult or entirely impractical. In such cases, fully supervised training becomes unsuitable, and self-supervised learning paradigms emerge as more viable solutions. One of the most prominent branches of self-supervised denoising is the Noise2Noise family, which demonstrates that a model can be trained to perform effective denoising using only pairs of independently noisy observations, without requiring clean ground truth images.

Noise2Noise (N2N), introduced by Lehtinen et al.179, demonstrated that CNNs can be trained for denoising using only corrupted input-target pairs that share underlying structure but contain independent, zero-mean noise. Under this assumption, the expected value of a noisy target equals the clean signal, enabling accurate denoising without access to clean ground truth. N2N models often match or even surpass fully supervised performance across diverse noise types and applications such as MRI reconstruction and Monte Carlo rendering. The success of N2N sparked extensive research in self-supervised denoising. Calvaron et al.180 enhanced the framework by generating synthetic noisy pairs through pixel or region swapping, thereby expanding the training set and reducing overfitting. Additionally, Zhou et al.181 introduced Slice Noise2Noise, which replaces paired noisy images with adjacent slices in a 3D volume, eliminating the need for explicitly paired training data and making the method well suited for volumetric modalities such as MRI and CT.

On top of N2N model, other variations have been proposed to denoise images without the need for clean target. For example, Noise2Void (N2V) trains denoising models using only single noisy images by masking central pixels and predicting them from their surroundings182. In addition, Noise2Inverse (N2I) adapts denoising to inverse problems like tomography, where spatially correlated noise violates Noise2Noise assumptions183. N2I trains CNNs using statistically independent reconstructions from split measurement data, enabling self-supervised learning without clean or paired images. Furthermore, EVEPR is a recent variation that applies the Noise2Image concept to self-supervised denoising of SR-PCI-μCT datasets178. The method splits a single dataset of 3000 projections into two equal, non-overlapping groups of 1500 projections; each group is independently reconstructed to produce two noisy EEC CT volumes. Corresponding slices from the two volumes serve as training pairs. This approach enables self-learning from a single SR-PCI-μCT acquisition without requiring clean reference images or dual imaging devices.

In laboratory and synchrotron micro-CT, image quality is degraded by a combination of random noise and systematic artifacts. Laboratory systems are primarily affected by Poisson photon noise, electronic readout noise, and scintillator-induced variability, whereas synchrotron systems also exhibit photon shot noise and detector-related noise. Both modalities also suffer from structured artifacts, including beam hardening, ring artifacts, and motion-induced distortions. In synchrotron imaging, additional coherence-related effects such as speckle and phase-contrast fringes may also occur. While the random noise components can often be approximated as zero-mean following logarithmic transformation of the projection data, the major artifacts are deterministic and spatially correlated, violating the assumptions underlying zero-mean noise models. As a result, self-supervised denoising approaches such as the Noise2Noise family are effective primarily for reducing random photon and electronic noise, but they are not suitable for correcting structured artifacts, which require dedicated preprocessing or model-based correction strategies.

Conclusion

In summary, for large-volume and diverse imaging modalities such as SR-PCI-μCT, traditional denoising methods are generally insufficient. Deep-learning-based denoising approaches have demonstrated strong robustness and high efficiency when handling large datasets, which would otherwise require extensive fine-tuning with conventional techniques. In particular, CNNs trained with image pairs are especially well suited for SR-PCI-μCT data. In the following section, we discuss strategies to mitigate the limitations posed by training dataset shortages commonly encountered in SR-PCI-μCT studies, and further examine the critical role of image denoising techniques in addressing this challenge.

Mitigating the limitations of training dataset shortage for SR-PCI-μCT

Recent advances in absorption- and phase-contrast biomedical μCT have been driven largely by supervised deep learning, which typically requires large amounts of expert-annotated training data. However, the shortage of annotated datasets for SR-PCI-μCT has become a major roadblock for many biomedical studies. As data volumes continue to grow, manual annotation of large synchrotron datasets has become increasingly impractical, motivating the rise of self-supervised learning (SSL), weakly supervised or prompt-based segmentation methods (such as SAM and its variants), and domain adaptation or generalization techniques.

Self-supervised learning with fine tuning

SSL leverages abundant unlabeled data and, when paired with fine-tuning (FT) on a small labeled subset, offers a practical alternative to fully supervised training. For example, Punn et al. introduced BT-Unet, a self-supervised segmentation framework that pre-trains U-Net encoders using the Barlow Twins redundancy-reduction objective, generating augmented views of unlabeled images to learn invariant and non-redundant features before fine-tuning with limited annotations184. This strategy improves performance under scarce labels and adapts well to complex U-Net variants. Similarly, BTSwin-Unet integrates Swin Transformer blocks with convolutional operations for 3D brain tumor segmentation, employing a masked autoencoder pre-training scheme to learn robust features without manual labels185. By combining global attention with local inductive biases, the method enhances feature extraction and reduces computational complexity, despite relying on a relatively simple pre-training setup.

Furthermore, a study shows that the self-supervised feature extraction models, when combined with a random forest classifier, enable semantic image segmentation using sparsely annotated labels. Seifi et al. proposed FeatureForest, a segmentation pipeline that combines per-pixel feature embeddings from large pre-trained vision models with a lightweight random forest classifier to enable fast, interactive, multi-class semantic segmentation of microscopy images186. FeatureForest extracts rich per-pixel feature vectors from models such as SAM2150 or DINOv2187, then uses a few user-provided scribble labels to train a random forest that classifies every pixel. As shown in Fig. 7, FeatureForest improves segmentation accuracy over classic filter-based random forests. Weakly supervised or prompt-based segmentation methods

The example in Section “Vision Transformer for SR-PCI-µCT imaging” introduced a new direction for generating training labels using weakly supervised or prompt-based models. In the study by Vršnak et al.175, training labels were produced by providing a small number of expert-annotated slices as prompts to the SAM2 model150. Using these limited annotations, SAM2 automatically propagated labels across the remaining slices, generating a large set of training masks. Using these AI-generated masks as supervision, Vršnak et al. subsequently trained a U-Net model to perform the final segmentation task, demonstrating that high-quality downstream performance can be achieved even when the initial human-labeled data is sparse.

This approach provides a great potential of adaptation to all Synchrotron beamlines when only sparse or partially annotated datasets are available, which is often the case due to the high cost and expertise required for manual labeling. Beamline experiments frequently generate large volumes of high-resolution data, yet only a fraction of these slices receive expert attention because annotating them is time-consuming and interrupts the scientific workflow. By integrating prompt-based models into the data-processing pipeline, facilities could dramatically reduce the annotation burden while still producing reliable training masks at scale.

Domain adaptation and generalization

Domain adaptation refers to the process of transferring a model trained on a labeled source dataset to a related but distribution-shifted target dataset, whose annotations are limited or unavailable. In synchrotron imaging, such shifts commonly arise from variations in beamline configuration, detector type, reconstruction algorithms, or sample preparation protocols. These differences can alter texture, contrast, or noise characteristics, causing a model trained on one dataset to underperform or even fail on another. To address this issue, domain adaptation techniques aim to align the feature distributions of the source and target domains. Approaches such as adversarial feature alignment, intensity normalization, contrast matching, and denoising can reduce discrepancies between datasets. When combined with prompt-based models like SAM2, segmentation performance can often be recovered quickly without requiring extensive new annotations. This hybrid strategy enables efficient transfer of segmentation capabilities across experiments, reducing the need for large-scale re-annotation.

In contrast, domain generalization aims to train models that remain robust across datasets without requiring adaptation to each new domain. Rather than correcting for distribution shifts after they occur, the model learns domain-invariant features by training on diverse datasets, employing strong augmentation strategies, or incorporating AI-generated labels from multiple sources. This produces segmentation models capable of handling new imaging conditions or sample variations out of the box, reducing the need for repeated fine-tuning and enabling more reliable deployment across synchrotron experiments.

Conclusion

In conclusion, denoising–one of the most important steps in the image processing pipeline–not only improves image quality and segmentation accuracy, but also serves as an effective form of domain adaptation. Deep-learning-based denoising methods have demonstrated strong capability in handling the high throughput and high diversity of SR-PCI-μCT datasets, while simultaneously assisting deep-learning-based segmentation models by mitigating the impact of limited training data. By reducing noise-induced variability, denoising can help harmonizing datasets acquired under different imaging conditions, thereby enhancing the robustness and transferability of downstream segmentation models. Additional strategies, such as AI-generated training labels, self-supervised learning with targeted fine-tuning, and cross-dataset domain transfer, may further help address the persistent challenge of training data scarcity.

Conclusion

Takeaway messages

This paper presents a comprehensive review of image segmentation techniques commonly applied to absorption-based μCT (Section “Segmentation Techniques for Absorption-based µCT”) and phase contrast-based μCT (Section “Segmentation Techniques for Phase Contrast-based µCT”) within biomedical imaging. As imaging technologies continue to advance rapidly across scientific disciplines, traditional segmentation methods are increasingly challenged by the demands of modern high-throughput, high-resolution datasets. deep-learning-based segmentation approaches have emerged as powerful alternatives due to their robustness, accuracy, and data-driven design. Nevertheless, their application to SR-PCI-μCT remains non-trivial. These methods typically require large annotated datasets, substantial computational resources, and long training times, and they often struggle to capture long-range dependencies within complex image structures. Recent advances in Vision Transformers have introduced more powerful deep-learning architectures capable of modeling long-range dependencies, improving segmentation generalizability, and achieving higher accuracy. However, their reliance on large, high-quality annotated training datasets poses a significant barrier to reproducibility and practical deployment in biomedical μCT studies, where annotated data are often scarce and costly to obtain.

Gaps and limitations to the state

Recent advances in deep learnin–especially the development of the Segment Anything Model (SAM) and its variants–have focused mainly on clinical imaging. Most current work targets common clinical modalities such as X-ray, clinical CT, and MRI, where large annotated datasets and well-established benchmarks already exist. In contrast, applying these models to biomedical μCT imaging is still challenging. A key limitation is the lack of widely accepted, publicly available SR-PCI-μCT datasets that could function as standard benchmarks. Biomedical SR-PCI-μCT images have fundamental difference from clinical CT and MRI in image characteristics, which makes existing open-source datasets unsuitable for training or evaluating segmentation models designed for SR-PCI-μCT. Consequently, progress in this field is slowed by scarce data resources and the absence of consistent evaluation frameworks.

Future directions of the field

After this survey study, several future directions emerge for the biomedical SR-PCI-μCT domain. The development of robust, high-accuracy segmentation techniques is likely to increasingly rely on hybrid methodologies and self-supervised learning. Hybrid models can leverage the strengths of self-supervised learning with fine-tuning (SSL + FT) or transfer learning (TL), while integrating the complementary capabilities of deep neural networks and vision transformers. Their performance can be further enhanced by exploiting large volumes of unlabeled data. Recent advances in self-supervised learning, AI-generated labeling, and domain adaptation offer promising solutions to the persistent challenge of annotation scarcity. In addition, incorporating domain-specific prior knowledge can provide critical support in the design of segmentation methodologies, enriching the input to segmentation algorithms and improving model compatibility, robustness, and reproducibility across diverse imaging conditions.
