Modification to the PICO Single Use Negative Pressure Wound Therapy System Application to Improve Patient Experience in Total Hip and Knee Arthroplasty
Authors: Abu Z Saeed, Mohammad U Attar, Sudheer L Karlakki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPICO single use negative pressure wound therapy (sNPWT) is increasinsingly being used in high‐risk wounds across many surgical specialities. Their use in orthopaedic arthroplasty surgery is associated with practical challenges limiting mobilisation including tube disconnection and pullouts. We present a description of a novel modification to the PICO sNPWT application using a Granuflex dressing to secure the pump to the patient. Our early patient experience showed 100% (24/24) patients reporting no issues with wound healing and 91.7% (22/24) reporting the dressing and pump staying on for the prescribed amount of time without detachment. This modification allows the dressing to be more conducive to early mobilisation without hindering use of walking aids and crutches. There is a role for the modification to be adopted by other surgical specialties.

Introduction

Negative pressure wound therapy to manage wound healing dates back to early civilisation [1] however, subatmospheric pressure vacuum assisted closure was first described in 1997 by Argenta and Morykwas [2]. Negative pressure wound therapy systems have shown improvement in surgical site complications across multiple surgical disciplines, including cardiac, vascular, gynaecological, breast and orthopaedic surgery in both elective and trauma settings [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. The use of negative pressure therapy in high‐risk wounds is recommended by the World Union of Wound Healing Societies Consensus document [10], World Health Organisation (WHO) [11] and National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) [12].

PICO (Smith & Nephew) Single Use Negative Pressure Wound Therapy System (sNPWT) is a single use negative pressure wound therapy system that first obtained FDA approval and CE mark in 2011 [13]. It consists of a single use cannister‐free pump which provides 80 mmHg of negative pressure, multi‐layer adhesive dressing and a hollow connecting extension tube [14]. In 2018 it was made available as PICO 7 (Smith & Nephew) in Europe. PICO 7 sNPWT is a seven day dressing which is reported to have an improved pump and an integrated belt clip to allow better transport of the pump for patients [12, 14]. NICE recommends the use of PICO 7 sNPWT for closed surgical incisions in patients that are at high risk of developing surgical site infections [12]. More recently, the PICO 14 sNPWT has a pump that is capable and programmed to deliver negative pressure for 14 days, developed for wounds which are slower to heal such as surgical wounds through a previous incision and complex surgical wounds [15].

Surgical site complications and peri‐prosthetic joint infections can have devastating consequences in orthopaedic arthroplasty surgery with mortality rates of up to 11% in patients with peri‐prosthetic hip joint infections [16, 17]. The use of incisional negative pressure dressings in reducing this is therefore emerging. A randomised control trial by Karlakki et al. showed a trend towards reduction in length of stay and signifcantly smaller chance of excess length of stay due to a leaky wound in the PICO group [3]. Helito et al. showed a reduction in surgical site complications and reintervention rates in patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty that were managed with PICO dressing as compared to standard dressings [18].

Earlier negative pressure devices were bulky and not conducive to ambulatory care or enhanced recovery following surgery [19]. Introduction of portable devices such as PICO 7 sNPWT [12] and 3M PrevenaTM [20] made this supportive of early mobilisation following surgery but yet retained the benefits of negative pressure therapy for compromised surgical wounds with high risk of delayed wound healing. However, they still pose some limitations in the design of the dressing and connection with the pump.

In the case of PICO sNPWT this is the long interconnecting tube that poses practical challenges such as mobilisation with crutches and change of clothing. Karlakki et al. reported 9.8% of patients in their study of primary hip and knee replacements experienced tube dissociation from either the pump or dressing as a result of being caught whilst changing clothes or ambulating and suggested that for these dressings to be truly beneficial to ambulatory care the pump needed to be an integral part of the dressing [3]. Disconnection of the tube can disrupt the dressing requiring unplanned dressing changes and exposure of the wound. It can also be distressing for patients when it occurs outside of a healthcare setting. Manufacturers subsequently introduced PICO 7 sNPWT with a belt clip which helped the functionality but not necessarily addressed the issues with long tubing. Being aware of the long tubing is not unique to orthopaedic surgery, Nyman et al. examined patient experience using PICO sNPWT dressings and some participants reported that the tube was too long and wished for a shorter tube [21]. Some participants also reported it was challenging to always be in control of the pump [21].

Therefore, we still do not have a satisfactory single use negative pressure incisional wound therapy dressing that is truly fit to support early rehabilitation in hip and knee arthroplasty that is satisfactory for patients. To address the issues with long tubing the senior author has been trialling a modified application of the PICO sNPWT in hip and knee arthroplasty over 18 months. We share our modification method and early patient experience of the PICO sNPWT. We hypothesise that our modification will reduce detachment of the pump and improve patient satisfaction.

The PICO sNPWT unit therefore is free of long interconnecting tubes, the pump is not in direct contact with the skin, free to breathe, can be lifted out of the plastic housing for change of batteries or showering if needed. Thus, the pump is almost integral to dressing, allowing mobilisation without worrying for the long tubing, negating accidental pull outs, but it does need one to wear loose clothing and some care whilst putting on underwear.

The recommended port position by Smith & Nephew is proximal to the wound to stop exudate pooling and port blockage [14]. This advice stems from larger cannister‐based NPWT dressings with open wounds where port blockage was experienced. The senior author has been using PICO dressings since 2011 and in his experience port blockage is rare with PICO sNPWT regardless of port position. The main aim and benefit of positioning the ports as described in this study is to allow the pump and port to be easily visible to the patient whilst dressing and undressing and therefore preventing accidental detachment.

We trialled this modification from April 2024 and assessed its suitability and patient experience in 25 consecutive patients that underwent a total hip or knee arthroplasty. We designed a questionnaire at the 6‐week stage specifically looking at:

Results

25 cases had either a PICO 7 or 14 sNPWT dressing applied with the modification described above. One patient did not complete the questionnaire and therefore 24 were included in the results. There were 19 total hip replacements and five total knee replacements. Mean age was 73.5 years (Range 52–87 years).

100% of patients reported at the 6‐week follow‐up that their wound had healed and had no problems with wound healing. 87.5% of patients reported an excellent post‐operative scar, 4.2% reported a raised wound and 8.3% did not complete this question. There were no objective measures of wound healing performed which is a limitation of this study.

The Granuflex dressing and pump stayed on for the desired amount of time in 91.7% (n = 22) of cases. There were two cases where the Granuflex dressing came off whilst dressing. 20.8% (n = 5) patients reported problems with clothing and the PICO dressing. There were no reported problems with skin at the site of Granuflex application.

Discussion

We present a novel modification to the PICO 7 and 14 sNPWT to help improve the use of PICO dressings in hip and knee arthroplasty. In the post‐operative period patients often require crutches and we need to minimise barriers to ambulation and activities of daily living which can include long tubing or holding pumps [3]. This modification allows the pump and dressing to function as a synergistic unit and our initial experience suggests there was a low rate of detachment of the pump from the Granuflex and tubing.

We report a high rate of successful Granuflex dressing attachment to the skin for the desired time and therefore this presents a secure method of fixation for the pump. This is critical as disturbance of the pump can lead to loss of negative pressure as well as risk of disruption to the wound dressing. Five patients reported problems with clothing which included a patient reporting difficulty with tight jeans. Patients are counselled on the use of loose clothing in the post‐operative period to accommodate for the expected swelling and this advice can be extended to PICO dressings to prevent the pump getting caught on clothing.

Our questionnaire identified no cases of skin problems with Granuflex applied for 7 or 14 days which is encouraging as any skin problems near the wound could be detrimental in hip and knee arthroplasty.

Although negative pressure wound dressings are unlikely to offer significant cost benefit in routine hip and knee arthroplasty in low risk patients, the dynamics of primary hip and knee arthroplasty are changing [24, 25, 26]. These procedures are performed in an increasingly aging population with higher ASA grades and therefore we can expect more patients to be at a higher risk of wound complications [27, 28]. In addition, enhanced recovery day case arthroplasty is gaining more popularity [29].

There is a need for a compact incisional negative pressure dressing system that can manage these more complex wounds whilst not being inhibitory to early mobilisation or rehabilitation and having minimal impact on patients daily life. In our study there were two cases of the Granuflex dressing coming off and once this occurs the same Granuflex dressing cannot be reapplied however the application of a new Granuflex dressing with a double‐sided disc is achievable. This modification can also be extended to other surgical wounds including breast, vascular and gynaecological surgery.

Conclusion

We present a simple modification to enhance the use of PICO 7 and 14 sNPWT dressings in elective hip and knee arthroplasty which has been well tolerated and accepted. If manufacturers can supply a purpose made adhesive patch with a pump housing that can be applied to the skin for an extended period of time, it will enhance the usability of the dressing and device. Until a more suitable alternative is available this may be a best fit solution not only for arthroplasty but also other surgical wounds.
