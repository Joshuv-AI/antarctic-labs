HydroWIRES
The U.S. electricity system is changing rapidly, and the flexible capabilities of hydropower
(including pumped storage hydropower) make it well positioned to aid in supporting grid
reliability and resilience. Recognizing these challenges and opportunities, H2O launched an
initiative known as HydroWIRES: Water Innovation for a Resilient Electricity System.
HydroWIRES is focused principally on understanding and supporting the changing role of
hydropower in the evolving U.S. electricity system. Through the HydroWIRES Initiative, H2O
seeks to understand and drive utilization of the full potential of hydropower resources to
contribute to electricity system reliability and resilience, now and into the future.

HydroWIRES is distinguished in its close engagement with the DOE’s national laboratories.
Five national laboratories—Argonne National Laboratory, Idaho National Laboratory, National
Laboratory of the Rockies, Oak Ridge National Laboratory, and Pacific Northwest National
Laboratory—work as a team to provide strategic insight and develop connections across the
U.S. Department of Energy portfolio that add significant value to the HydroWIRES Initiative.

HydroWIRES operates in conjunction with the Grid Modernization Initiative, which focuses on
the development of new architectural concepts, tools, and technologies that measure, analyze,
predict, protect, and control the grid of the future, and on enabling the institutional conditions
that allow for quicker development and widespread adoption of these tools and technologies.

Connections with the HydroWIRES Road Map
This report on electric utility modeling and asset valuation frameworks focuses primarily on
addressing HydroWIRES Objectives 1.2 and 1.3: Value Drivers and Valuation Methodologies. It
is informed by an extensive body of work among all participating national laboratories in
hydrological and grid modeling along with hydropower valuation. Results from this study will
inform subsequent PSH and other valuation studies for grid technologies. Other relevant DOE

Critical Materials and Energy Innovation, the U.S. Department of Energy Office of Science, and
the U.S. Department of Energy Office of Electricity, as all of these offices support model
development and analysis that utilizes comparable tools for a wide range of energy and water
research questions.

 Executive Summary

ES.1 Study Objectives
As an energy storage technology, pumped storage hydropower (PSH) supports various aspects of
power system operations. However, determining the value of PSH plants and their many services
and contributions to the system has long been a challenge. There is a general understanding that
PSH resources provide many services and benefits for the operation of power systems; however,
estimating the value of these services—especially the monetary value of some of those
services—is inherently difficult.

The overall objective of this study is to develop processes, models, and data that better value
PSH from a utility perspective; more specifically, a vertically integrated utility enabling optimal
PSH design and deployment. This study is designed to enhance the Tennessee Valley Authority’s
(TVA’s) toolset in making PSH investment decisions and contribute to the design of a more
affordable, reliable future grid. These techniques can be applied to a broader range of assets
and utilities.

There are several analytical goals supporting the overall project objectives: (1) quantify the
impacts and value of new PSH in the TVA system using an array of modeling and analysis tools
spanning a range of spatial and temporal scales and process detail, (2) expand and complement
the analytical methods conducted within TVA by exploring alternative power sector scenario
representations and leveraging research-oriented capabilities of the U.S. Department of Energy
(DOE) national laboratories, (3) present various valuation and impact metrics that include and go
beyond those typically considered in internal TVA analysis, (4) perform internally consistent
multi-model analysis that integrates tools and data across scales to allow a more comprehensive
exploration of PSH valuation, and (5) collaborate directly with TVA to produce analysis that
balances accuracy of the TVA system representation with the alternative approach and
perspectives of laboratory tools.

ES.2 TVA System and Interest in PSH
TVA is a vertically integrated utility that owns and operates energy (power and gas) and water
infrastructure for 10 million people across Tennessee and parts of Kentucky, Virginia, North
Carolina, Georgia, Alabama, and Mississippi. TVA’s power system has over 32 gigawatts (GW)
of generating capacity across a diverse set of resources including nuclear, coal, natural gas,
hydropower, and other renewables (solar and wind). This generation mix could change
considerably in the coming decades. TVA’s draft 2025 Integrated Resource Plan (IRP) presents a
wide range of possible future mixes, and a variety of internal decisions and external factors could
drive an even greater range of possibilities.1 Coal capacity could retire by 2035, with demand
being met largely by the nuclear and natural gas fleet and the potential for growth in solar,

TVA currently operates one PSH facility, Raccoon Mountain, a 1,700 MW facility online since
1978. The Nickajack Reservoir along the Tennessee River serves as the lower reservoir, which is
connected with an underground tunnel to the upper reservoir built on the bluffs above the lower
reservoir near Chattanooga, Tennessee. The facility is used primarily to balance electricity
supply and demand, but it also provides an array of ancillary services used to maintain grid
stability, reliability, and resiliency. It provides operators with an important resource to respond to
grid disturbances that would otherwise be more challenging to manage. The long-term
demonstrated value of the Raccoon Mountain PSH system combined with ongoing power sector
trends has created renewed interest in PSH for TVA.

ES.3 Rorex Creek PSH
The Rorex Creek PSH plant would be located near Tennessee River Mile 388 in Pisgah,
Alabama. The Guntersville Reservoir would serve as the lower pool and TVA would construct
an upper reservoir, power tunnel, underground powerhouse, switchyard, intake/outflow structure,
and a 500 kilovolt (kV) transmission line. TVA would also be required to relocate an existing
500-kV transmission line in the project area. The Rorex Creek project site encompasses roughly
4,850 acres and is primarily owned by private landowners with TVA property near the river and
along the mountainside. The upper reservoir on the Sand Mountain Plateau would have a usable
volume of 48,000 acre-feet. TVA would construct an underground powerhouse containing
4-6 variable speed pump-turbine/generator-motor units. While earlier studies considered a
1,600 MW design that was used for transmission reliability analysis herein, this study primarily
evaluates a 1,200 MW, 4-unit design that was ultimately identified as being more promising by
TVA.2 With a power capacity of 1,200 MW and 23,365 MWh of stored energy capacity, Rorex
Creek PSH could generate at nameplate capacity for nearly 20 hours. The estimated round-trip
efficiency (RTE) for the plant would be 80.1%.

ES.4 Results from Technoeconomic Studies
This study uses several tools and models developed and used by Argonne National Laboratory
and the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR), informed by input data and assumptions
provided by TVA. Figure ES-1 shows the key data flows, models, and metrics employed in this
analysis. Argonne’s Pumped Storage Hydro Valuation Tool (PSHVT) was used for a screening-
level valuation analysis for a range of potential future power sector scenarios (72 in total)
developed in partnership with TVA. NLR then employed several highly detailed grid models to
further examine PSH valuation. The Regional Energy Deployment System (ReEDS), NLR’s
flagship capacity expansion model, was used to simulate future power sector scenarios (48 in
total) that are aligned with Argonne's PSHVT but incorporate additional capabilities and data.
Results from ReEDS were then converted into inputs for the PLEXOS commercial production
cost model software. NLR used the PSS®E transmission simulation tool to explore how new
PSH operation can support grid strength and stability using higher time resolution nodal power
flow simulations. Finally, Argonne used the valuation results from both the PSHVT and the
NLR-led analysis to conduct detailed financial analysis for the new PSH facility. The financial
analysis was used to estimate benefit-cost ratios (BCRs) and other financial metrics over the
lifetime of the plant.

Figure ES-2 presents a breakdown of annual system cost reductions resulting from Rorex Creek
deployment for six resource portfolio scenarios considered in the PSHVT assessment. System-
perspective benefits are led by avoided system capital costs, complemented by notable
reductions in system contingency reserve and generation costs in most cases. The “constrained
gas” portfolio exhibits higher avoided capital costs than other scenarios, driven by the constraints
on gas turbine capacity. In this scenario, the candidate PSH substitutes for peaking capacity that
would otherwise be supplied by battery storage yielding greater capacity value because batteries
carry higher capital costs. High PSH values are also observed in the “high solar” and “high
nuclear” scenarios where system flexibility demands are greater.

While TVA is a vertically integrated utility without competitive markets, it can be useful to
quantify the operating value, effectively an avoided cost, that a PSH plant may accrue based on
modeled marginal energy and ancillary service value. Figure ES-3 shows the annual value from
hourly locational marginal values when pumping and generating along with ancillary service
prices. Note that PLEXOS does not attempt to maximize the value generated by any individual
plant, including the PSH plant. The PSH plant provides a lot of reserves (650 MW-h on average
in the TVA Base Case), but since the marginal value for reserves is typically low, the total
reserves value is also limited, although not zero in most cases. Lower operational values are
observed in the low solar cases, and the highest values occur in the three cases with high gas
prices (TVA Base Case, High Solar + Battery, and High Solar + Nuclear). This again results
from the complementarity of PSH with solar due to its temporal availability and zero-marginal
cost, as well as the value of PSH in systems with increasing fuel prices.

Because ReEDS is tightly coupled with the probabilistic resource adequacy suite (PRAS)
resource adequacy (RA) model, results can show how the Rorex Creek PSH operates during
single-day stress periods identified in PRAS. In addition, the optimization model structure allows
for a shadow price of capacity to be identified in each stress period. This price, when multiplied
by the power provided by Rorex Creek PSH, produces revenue, and the total revenue across all
stress periods in a year is then divided by the rated capacity of the plant to estimate an RA
capacity value for Rorex Creek PSH in that year. While TVA as a vertically integrated utility has
no capacity market, this metric reflects the implied RA capacity value as it relates to the value of
energy during periods of scarcity on the grid, which can change in timing and frequency across
years and scenarios. The RA value for Rorex Creek was estimated at $120/kW-year, and
additional value could be demonstrated if multi-day stress periods were considered.

The financial analysis using PSHVT results yield several important conclusions. Results vary
considerably by scenario, with high natural gas prices, high deployment of solar, and high load
corresponding to higher value for PSH operations. The constrained gas scenario generates the
highest returns for PSH. The reference case and retain coal case yield the lowest returns for PSH.
Scenarios with higher RPSs in surrounding states had limited effect on outcomes. All
72 scenarios explored for this study using the PSHVT yielded positive financial returns, as
measured in both net present values (NPVs), which ranged from $286 million to $1.7 billion, and
benefit-cost ratios ranging from 1.2 to 2.2.

Financial analysis using results from the ReEDS plus PLEXOS analysis yielded BCR results
ranging from 1.6 (NPV - $897 million) for the low solar plus battery case with an isolated spatial
scope and a $500 price cap to 4.1 ($4.71 billion) for the high solar plus nuclear case with an
isolated spatial scope and no price cap. The scenarios with lower solar penetration rates tend to
yield lower financial returns, as do the scenarios with price caps. The ReEDS plus PLEXOS runs
produced broadly higher financial returns when compared to those generated by the PSHVT,
though the differences tightened considerably when a price cap was imposed in PLEXOS. BCRs
ranged from 1.2–2.2 for the PSHVT and 1.6–2.5 for the ReEDS plus PLEXOS runs using the
$500 price cap.

While both the PSHVT and PLEXOS models were run for 2035, PLEXOS was additionally run
for 2044 and 2050. The PLEXOS results indicate significant growth in PSH value between 2035
and 2044, with value under the base case nearly doubling. In addition, the RA analysis suggests
that the cost of RA could increase significantly in the years following PSH deployment. These
results suggest that PSH would become more valuable to TVA over time.

Other significant findings include:

 • As adjustable-speed PSH enhances system strength overall, it also increases the system’s
 ability to accommodate additional inverter-based resources (IBRs), which can help
 reduce operational costs and improve energy diversity. Transmission reliability analysis
 was completed on a 1,600 MW plant design, which was the initial focus of this study
 before the decision to refocus on a 1,200 MW design, but findings are expected to scale
 proportionally to the 1,200 MW design. The 1,600 MW design increases IBR hosting
 capacity at selective buses under several cases. The area near the Rorex Creek plant
 (Buses 36995–36999) could host approximately 980–1,600 MW of new IBR capacity,

 x

 and nearby 500-kV and 161-kV buses could host an additional 85–190 MW of IBRs.
 This represents a notable local change in the ability of the local system to deploy new
 IBRs.

 • Even with a full suite of modeling tools, we still may not be able to fully quantify the
 operational value of PSH in a utility setting. For instance, one example would be black
 start capability, which is not considered here and is not often quantified as a source of
 value. While difficult to quantify, it is necessary for a utility to consider. In addition, we
 cannot capture all possible future conditions that could be observed on the grid. Thus, a
 key value might be a hedge under future uncertainty because times of excess and scarcity
 are always likely. Additional PSH value arises from other ancillary services that typically
 lack explicit valuation but provide system stability and transmission reliability.

The alternative approaches used in this study evaluated a total of 120 scenarios, varied by spatial
scope, generation portfolio, natural gas price, load shape, environmental policies, and the
presence of price caps. All scenarios evaluated in this study returned BCRs in excess of 1.0 and
positive NPVs suggesting exceptionally strong financial performance for Rorex Creek PSH.
With that noted, results are highly sensitive to a few key financial parameters. Results are
sensitive to changes in borrowing and discount rates. Changing the discount rate by +/- 2%
results in a $3.7 billion swing in NPV for the ReEDS plus PLEXOS Reference Integrated Case
with a $500 price cap. Results are also highly sensitive to the partial or complete removal of the
investment tax credit (ITC). Elimination of the ITC would increase costs by $1.3 billion in
NPV terms.

 1.0 Introduction

This study presents the methodology and results of a detailed evaluation of the proposed Rorex
Creek pumped storage hydro (PSH) plant proposed by the Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA).
This report is the second of several covering PSH technical assistance (TA) projects awarded by
the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Hydropower and Hydrokinetic Office (H2O), formerly
the Water Power Technologies Office, in 2023. In addition to assisting TVA in the evaluation of
the proposed facility, this study is designed to achieve the dual goals of improving the practices
used by vertically integrated utilities to evaluate the economic feasibility of PSH and to learn
what new elements and modifications to the Pumped Storage Hydro Valuation Tool (PSHVT)
and other power sector models used by national laboratories [1] are required to more effectively
provide analysis that developers, industry experts, and government personnel need to better
understand the value proposition for PSH in the United States. These TA projects leverage the
valuation methodologies and analyses presented in the Pumped Storage Hydropower Valuation
Guidebook [2].

1.1 State of PSH Technology
PSH is a mature energy storage technology that is the only widely deployed utility-scale long-
duration energy storage (LDES) technology, defined as having storage durations of 10 hours or
more. Storage duration relates to the maximum amount of time a storage facility can generate
electricity at its maximum rated capacity before needing to be recharged. PSH uses an elevation
difference (head) between two reservoirs to create a potential energy difference that can be
exploited to generate electricity when demand is high by letting water flow from the upper to
lower reservoir through a turbine, then energy can be used to pump water back to the upper
reservoir when demand is low. While the pump-turbine efficiency results in round-trip efficiency
(RTE) losses on the order of 20% due to pipe friction, pump-turbine flow inefficiencies, and
electrical heat losses, these losses are acceptable with sufficient opportunity for energy arbitrage
realized through pumping water during low-cost operating hours and generating during periods
with higher costs or value propositions for other capacity and ancillary services.

  1.1.1 PSH Technology Features and Challenges

PSH has many characteristics that create both challenges and opportunities for its deployment.
Because systems rely on head for power production potential, favorable topography must be
present to enable reservoirs to be built or used at different elevations without excessive costs to
construct embankment or other dams for the upper and lower reservoirs. Further, these reservoirs
should be sufficiently close together in horizontal distance, typically with a distance-to-head
ratio less than 12 [3] where lower is better until there are challenging flow conditions in the
water conveyance between the reservoirs or other geological constraints. Systems where both
reservoirs exist along a natural waterway are considered open-loop systems, whereas systems are
closed-loop when neither reservoir interacts with a natural waterway [4]. Systems are often
called add-on PSH when one new off-waterway reservoir (typically an upper reservoir) is
constructed and connected to an existing reservoir. Regardless of the configuration, sites are
selected based on favorable geology (e.g., avoiding faults and karst formations) and

 1

environmental, cultural, and other considerations relevant for licensing or other applicable
regulatory review processes that are required to allow site construction and operation.

PSH tends to have strong economies of scale, meaning that larger systems are typically more
economical on a cost-per-capacity ($/kilowatt [kW]) basis [5]. Thus, utility-scale systems are
typically on the order of billions of U.S. dollars in total capital cost for systems that are hundreds
of megawatts (MW) to over 1,000 MW in size. Combined with multi-year regulatory and
construction timeframes, PSH presents an investment risk that requires careful valuation to
justify financing such an investment. In a competitive electricity market setting, valuation is key
to securing offtaker agreements to purchase power and other services from the PSH operator that
can then enable capital investors to accept the risk when financing the facility. In a vertically
integrated setting, valuation must sufficiently justify the investment to a board of directors
and possibly a regulatory commission if any rate changes are proposed to accommodate
the investment.

PSH can provide a wide array of value streams due to its substantial operating flexibility [2].
Systems can typically switch between pump and generation mode in a matter of minutes, and
power output can be ramped quickly in generation mode in response to fluctuations in electricity
demand. Systems with fixed-speed pumps can only operate each unit at a fixed power input in
pumping mode, but plants typically have multiple units to provide some flexibility. Further,
adjustable-speed pumps or other advanced designs (e.g., ternary, quaternary) allow enhanced
flexibility in pumping mode. PSH can thus provide value through energy arbitrage as well as a
variety of ancillary services such as frequency regulation, voltage support, and operating
reserves. Its capacity is typically considered “firm” capacity that would be available to ensure
resource adequacy during strained grid conditions (e.g., peak load). The large rotating machinery
operating at PSH plants also provides inertia that supports grid reliability and resilience through
outages or disturbances of any type. A key challenge, however, is that many of these capacity
and ancillary services do not have substantial monetized value despite being recognized by grid
operators for their key contributions to system reliability and performance. PSH is also unique in
that its useful lifetime can reach 100 years or more with continued maintenance. Thus, PSH
value is accrued over time scales that can far exceed typical economic evaluations, creating
challenges in valuation compared to competing storage technologies or other investment
opportunities. While PSH is an established technology, its cost-value proposition has changed
over time, driving the need for technoeconomic valuation studies under current and future
market, policy, and technology conditions.

  1.1.2 PSH in the United States

PSH is among the most mature forms of energy storage in the world, with its earliest operations
in the United States dating back to the commissioning of the Rocky River PSH project in
Connecticut in 1929. PSH has since expanded across the United States, with 43 plants operating
with a total installed power capacity of 21.9 gigawatts. The 2023 Hydropower Market Report
notes that these plants collectively represented 96% of the U.S. energy storage capacity at the
end of 2022 [6]. Of these plants, all but one are open-loop facilities, and the vast majority were
developed during the 1960s through the 1990s. As the focus of plant operations has changed over
time and the risks associated with an evolving economic landscape and environmental challenges
have mounted, fewer PSH plants have been commissioned. The 40 MW Lake Hodges

 2

(California) PSH plant was the last commissioned in the United States in 2012. The last large-
scale PSH plant commissioned in the United States was the Rocky Mountain PSH plant near
Rome, Georgia, and it began operations in 1995. However, there have been no major
decommissionings of PSH in the United States, with a more common development being
upgrades to expand capacity at existing sites and extend plant lifetimes [7].

1.2 TVA Interest in PSH
The potential value provided by PSH has sparked particular interest in new PSH construction
with TVA [8]. This section provides an overview of the TVA system and its current motivation
for exploring new PSH development, followed by a description of characteristics that TVA is
considering for a site near Rorex Creek in Jackson County, Alabama.

  1.2.1 Overview of TVA System

TVA is a vertically integrated utility that owns and operates energy (power and gas) and water
infrastructure for 10 million people across Tennessee and parts of Kentucky, Virginia, North
Carolina, Georgia, Alabama, and Mississippi (Figure 1-1). Its power system has over 32 GW of
generating capacity across a diverse set of resources including nuclear, coal, natural gas,
hydropower, and other renewables (solar and wind) [9]. This generation mix could change
considerably in the coming decades, with TVA’s draft 2025 Integrated Resource Plan (IRP)
presenting a wide range of possible future mixes [10]. In IRP scenarios, coal capacity is
projected to retire by 2035, with demand being met largely by the nuclear and natural gas fleet
and the potential for growth in solar, storage, purchased power, and hydrogen or fossil fuels with
carbon capture and storage. By 2050, renewable energy and storage contributed a much larger
share to the generation mix in the IRP scenarios, demonstrating the potential for a substantial
change in technology portfolio and how the grid must be operated. Scenario differences are
driven by alternative assumptions about technology development, regulatory and other policies,
and demand growth, all of which are highly uncertain over multidecadal time frames. This
uncertainty in the TVA system’s evolution is a key driver of the work presented in this report.

  1.2.2 History with PSH

TVA currently operates one PSH facility, Raccoon Mountain, a 1,700 MW facility online since
1978. The Nickajack Reservoir along the Tennessee River serves as the lower reservoir, which is
connected with an underground tunnel to the upper reservoir built on the bluffs above the lower
reservoir near Chattanooga, Tennessee [12]. The facility is used primarily to balance electricity
supply and demand, more efficiently dispatch low-cost generation such as nuclear, and it also
provides an array of ancillary services used to maintain grid stability, reliability, and resiliency.
It provides operators with an important resource to respond to grid disturbances that would
otherwise be more challenging to manage.

  1.2.3 Motivation for New Investment in PSH

The long-term demonstrated value of the Raccoon Mountain PSH system combined with
ongoing power sector trends has created renewed interest in PSH for TVA. Potential growth in
solar energy creates an increasing need for PSH to shift energy from the middle of the day (when
there could be excess generation) to morning or evening hours when net demand is relatively
high. Batteries can perform diurnal energy shifting as well, but PSH does so for longer durations
and in larger quantities, potentially allowing TVA to shift energy across multiple days as
necessary to maintain reliable, low-cost system operation. More broadly, additional PSH could
provide greater overall system flexibility among TVA’s diverse generation portfolio however it
changes in the future. The new PSH system could also complement Raccoon Mountain in

 4

providing critical capacity and ancillary services, particularly with the loss of system inertia from
retiring coal units. New PSH investment interest is further bolstered by the federal investment
tax credit for utility-scale storage capacity investment, which could provide up to a 50% credit
toward the capital cost of new PSH. The cost-value proposition for new PSH in TVA is a moving
target with continual market and policy change, but current and projected conditions have
generated strong interest in near-term investment. This interest has led to TVA proposing several
alternative PSH expansion plans, including two new site alternatives and the expansion of
Raccoon Mountain [8]. Based on conversations with TVA and scope limitations, this study
focuses on new PSH proposed at Rorex Creek near Pisgah, Alabama.

  1.2.4 Overview of Rorex Creek PSH

The Rorex Creek PSH plant would be located near Tennessee River Mile 388 in Pisgah,
Alabama (Figure 1-2). The Guntersville Reservoir would serve as the lower pool, and TVA
would construct an upper reservoir, power tunnel, underground powerhouse, switchyard,
intake/outflow structure and a 500 kilovolt (kV) transmission line. TVA would also be required
to relocate an existing 500-kV transmission line in the project area. The Rorex Creek project site
encompasses roughly 4,850 acres and is primarily owned by private landowners with TVA
property near the river and along the mountainside. The upper reservoir on the Sand Mountain
Plateau would have a usable volume of 48,000 acre-feet. TVA would construct an underground
powerhouse containing 4-6 variable-speed pump-turbine/generator-motor units. This study
evaluates the 1,200 MW, 4-unit design in all sections except for Section 5 focused on
transmission reliability modeling [14]. All transmission reliability modeling was complete before
a decision was made to refocus on a 1,200 MW design.

1.3 PSH Valuation for TVA
The overall objective of this study is to develop processes, models, and data that better value
PSH from a utility perspective, enabling optimal PSH design and deployment. This study
effectively enhances TVA’s toolset in making PSH investment decisions and will contribute to
the design of a more cost-effective, stable future grid. These techniques can then be applied to a
broader range of assets and utilities.

  1.3.1 Analytical Goals

There are several analytical goals supporting the overall project objectives.

 1. Quantify the impacts and value of new PSH in the TVA system using an array of
 modeling and analysis tools spanning a range of spatial and temporal scales and process
 detail.

 2. Expand and complement the analytical methods conducted within TVA by exploring
 alternative power sector scenario representations and leveraging the research-oriented
 capabilities of the DOE national laboratories.

 3. Present various valuation and impact metrics that include and go beyond those typically
 considered in internal TVA analysis.

 6

 4. Perform internally consistent multi-model analysis that integrates tools and data across
 scales to allow a more comprehensive exploration of PSH valuation.

 5. Collaborate directly with TVA to produce analysis that balances accuracy of the TVA
 system representation with the alternative approach and perspectives of laboratory tools.

These goals are directed towards producing results and analysis that are informative and
actionable by TVA staff in making decisions about proposed PSH in the TVA service territory.
However, they also facilitate the creation of a broader multi-model analytical methodology for
PSH valuation that could be implemented for other proposed PSH sites. The tools, techniques,
and metrics used herein can also be applied in various combinations to other storage and
generation technologies, providing a versatile valuation toolkit for utilities and other analysts in
the public, private, academic, or non-profit sectors.

  1.3.2 Structure of the Analysis

The analysis utilizes several tools and models developed and used by Argonne National
Laboratory and the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR), informed by input data and
assumptions provided by TVA. This section introduces the flows of data between models and
institutions, then subsequent sections of the report describe each tool in detail. Figure 1-3 shows
the key data flows, models, and metrics employed in this analysis.

Figure 1-3 Key Data Components, Models, and Metrics Used for PSH Valuation in TVA.
Multiple line colors are used solely to clarify connections.

Argonne’s PSHVT was used for a screening-level valuation analysis for a range of potential
future power sector scenarios developed in partnership with TVA. The PSHVT uses PSH
technical and cost specifications provided by TVA along with power system data and constraints
to quantify energy and ancillary service values of the PSH. Insights from the PSHVT analysis
and data collection process enhanced the more comprehensive assessments later carried out using

 7

tools developed by NLR. The PSHVT was also used to explore a broad range of scenarios (72)
that both explored cases, including a retain coal case, that were not part of the full assessment
and evaluated the sensitivity of the results with respect to changes in several key factors
including natural gas prices, renewable portfolio standards (RPSs) in states surrounding the TVA
service territory, and different load growth scenarios.

NLR then employed several highly detailed grid models to further examine PSH valuation. The
Regional Energy Deployment System (ReEDS) [15, 16], NLR’s flagship capacity expansion
model (CEM), is used to simulate future power sector scenarios that are aligned with Argonne’s
PSHVT but incorporate additional capabilities and data. One example is that ReEDS is tightly
coupled with the Probabilistic Resource Adequacy Suite (PRAS) [17, 18], a stochastic resource
adequacy simulation tool that uses multiple years of hourly data and sampled plant outages to
characterize resource adequacy requirements and contributions across the fleet. Like the PSHVT,
ReEDS is populated with TVA system data and new PSH specifications where possible, and it is
used to quantify plant-level resource adequacy value and system-level cost and capacity
implications of the new PSH.

Results from ReEDS are then converted into inputs for the PLEXOS commercial production cost
model (PCM) software [19, 20]. While ReEDS simulates a reduced-order system dispatch over
many representative days and time slices, PLEXOS performs hourly operational simulations for
these projected future infrastructures with more detailed operating constraints to better calculate
the energy arbitrage and ancillary service values that can be estimated at that time resolution.

NLR uses the PSS®E transmission simulation tool [21] to explore how new PSH operation can
support grid strength and stability using higher time resolution nodal power flow simulations
during a 2031 summer peak load case when the new PSH is assumed to be pumping. This
particular scenario of grid stress helps demonstrate how the new PSH impacts transmission
performance metrics such as short-circuit ratio and the ability for nearby transmission nodes
to host additional inverter-based resources (IBRs), both under normal and contingency
(outage) conditions.

Finally, Argonne used the valuation results from both the PSHVT and the NLR-led analysis to
conduct detailed financial analysis for the new PSH facility. The financial analysis was used to
estimate cost-benefit ratios and other financial metrics over the lifetime of the plant. This
analysis is conducted across a broad range of scenarios and uses input data from the PSHVT,
ReEDS, and PLEXOS to examine alternative valuation approaches and what they mean for the
viability of new PSH in TVA.

 • The extremely broad set of scenarios evaluated by the PSHVT allowed us to evaluate the
 influence of alternative investment cases (e.g., retain coal cases) and key variables
 (e.g., natural gas prices, RPSs in surrounding states, and varying load growth scenarios)
 on the economic viability of PSH.

 • Applying the PSHVT to a planned project enabled learning that can result in future
 modifications to the model, which will improve its accuracy and expand its impact.

Other secondary objectives included the acceleration of the data collection process, the
refinement of a framework for conducting TA using the PSHVT, and a test of the PSHVT when
applied to evaluate PSH for a vertically integrated utility. Note that more information about the
PSHVT, along with several other models used to support this study, is contained in Appendix A.

2.2 Application of the PSHVT
The project team used the PSHVT to evaluate the Rorex Creek PSH system using the price-
influencer model. The PSHVT also has an embedded price-taker model. The price-taker model
relies on historic prices and takes those prices as fixed, implicitly assuming that the PSH plant
would have no impact on market prices. The price-influencer model in the PSHVT, on the other
hand, evaluates the dynamic effects of adding PSH into the region’s power grid and builds those
effects into system costs and prices. The price-influencer model can also be used to perform
either a system-focused or owner-operator focused analysis. Since TVA is both the owner and
system operator, the focus of this analysis was the system-focused models. Benefits therefore are
defined in terms of avoided system costs as opposed to market revenue.

The PSHVT is a systematic decision-tree-based tool that follows a 15-step benefit-cost analysis
(BCA) framework to define the value of PSH plants. The tool development was funded by DOE
H2O under the HydroWIRES Initiative. The PSHVT brings the Pumped Storage Hydropower
Valuation Guidebook to life. It enables the assessment of PSH value by providing valuation
approaches for multiple grid services while considering impacts from grid portfolio and policy
choices. The PSHVT provides a step-by-step and easy-to-follow way to advance the state of art
of PSH valuation studies. The PSHVT can evaluate the value that PSH plants provide for the
grid services listed in Table 2-1 [1].

2.6 Conclusions
Across nearly all cases the addition of PSH expands renewable portfolios while curbing
investments in peaking gas turbines and batteries. From a system perspective, PSH value is
driven primarily by avoided capital costs, with meaningful additional savings from reductions in
generation and contingency reserve costs. The system cost reduction with the candidate PSH
varies by scenario from roughly $112–$200 million annually. Higher natural gas prices and
higher load growth (in “reg + growth” load profile) correspond to higher PSH value, and the
constrained‑gas scenarios generate the highest returns for PSH.

3.0 Integrated Planning and Resource Adequacy Modeling

3.1 Introduction
Long-term planning models can be used to identify cost-optimal investment decisions across all
generation, transmission, and storage assets in an electricity system. In the context of PSH
valuation for TVA, it can show how a new PSH investment impacts other investment decisions
by comparing model results with and without the proposed new PSH plant. It can also assess the
value of new capacity for meeting resource adequacy (RA) requirements. The approach here uses
a long-term planning model, ReEDS, that is fully integrated with an RA model, PRAS, to
explore impacts of a new PSH plant on TVA system investments and its contributions to RA. It
does so with a more detailed representation of spatial, temporal, and technology considerations
than the PSHVT, offering a unique perspective on PSH system value even when metrics overlap.

3.2 Model Descriptions
ReEDS is the capacity expansion model used for long-term planning, whereas PRAS is the
resource adequacy model integrated into ReEDS.

  3.2.1 Capacity Expansion Modeling with ReEDS

ReEDS is NLR’s flagship capacity expansion model that simulates the multidecadal evolution
and operation of the bulk power system of the contiguous United States or its subregions. ReEDS
solves for a cost-optimal system in successive model solve years, typically through 2050. A
reduced-form operating representation uses chronological time slices for several representative
days to capture chronological differences and coincidence between load and variable renewable
resources. Starting from the existing fleet configuration, ReEDS performs a linear programming
optimization that minimizes the cost of future investments and operation for electricity
generation, storage, and transmission infrastructure that serves electricity load and additional
market, policy, and technological constraints. These constraints include state and local policies,
reliability requirements, and operating reserve requirements. Key inputs include projections for
future electricity demand, fuel prices, and technology cost and performance. ReEDS is an open-
source tool available on GitHub [1, 2], and additional model input data details can be found in
Appendix A.2.1 and A.2.2 of this report as well as its online documentation [3]. The ReEDS
version used in this work is adapted from the version used in the 2024 Standard Scenarios
analysis, version 2025.0.0 [4].

  3.2.2 Integrated Resource Adequacy Modeling with PRAS

To evaluate system reliability, ReEDS interfaces with PRAS, an open-source software package
developed by NLR to study power system RA and reliability by stochastically simulating power
system operations over many thousands of outage conditions [5, 6]. Between each model solve
year, PRAS conducts hourly operational simulations for the infrastructure in that year to identify
time periods when the system is at risk to meet demand, called “stress periods.” This is done by
calculating probabilistic resource metrics such as normalized expected unserved energy (NEUE)
and loss-of-load expectations (LOLE) that can be compared against user-defined targets to

 25

determine if the system is sufficiently reliable. If targets are not met, ReEDS is run again with
the stress periods added to the time periods used in the ReEDS optimization so that capacity is
built to meet demand in those periods. PRAS then tests the resulting infrastructure again, and the
two models iterate until reliability targets are met before moving to the next model year.

3.3 Model Configuration and Data Collection
NLR worked closely with TVA staff to incorporate input data and assumptions into ReEDS (and
PRAS by extension) to represent the TVA system more accurately. This involved configuring the
ReEDS spatial scope and resolution while modifying input data that represent the TVA generator
fleet, hydropower operating constraints, fuel prices, and load. It also required testing and
iteration with TVA staff to determine suitable rates of variable energy resource deployment and
inter-regional electricity trade with non-TVA regions.

  3.3.1 Spatial Scope and Resolution

ReEDS simulates the TVA model region over two different spatial definitions: (1) a county-level
representation of the TVA service territory as an islanded system, and (2) a coarser BA
resolution of the full Eastern Interconnect (EI) (Figure 3-1). The islanded county-level
representation offers a more accurate representation of service area boundaries along with the
TVA fleet and demand, but it ignores the bulk power transmission connections between TVA
and other regions and thus does not account for interregional trade when economic. It does,
however, represent bulk power transmission flows and capacity expansion between TVA
counties. Eastern Interconnect simulations use 92 BAs that roughly follow state and service
territory boundaries, four of which predominantly overlap with TVA. This spatial definition
allows for interregional trade while also representing bulk power transmission between the four
TVA regions, but the four TVA BAs less accurately match current service territory boundaries
and system characteristics. A county-level representation of the full EI or TVA and its
surroundings could capture both benefits but is not computationally tractable. Results are
presented for both spatial definitions to offer alternative possible outcomes given the limitations
of each.

Figure 3-1 Spatial Definitions Used in ReEDS Simulations

  3.3.2 Generation Fleet Data

The default ReEDS generator database is modified for this analysis using proprietary data from
TVA including retirement years, nameplate capacities, and heat rates. Some units in the ReEDS
database for TVA regions are not actually TVA-owned and -operated units, but these are
generally kept in the analysis to maintain consistency with ReEDS load data. Hydropower
capacity factors, minimum capacity, and maximum capacity are also updated for TVA plants
using operational data from TVA to better match utility expectations.

  3.3.3 Fuel Prices and Load

TVA provided natural gas fuel price data aligned with their existing fuel price contracts and
costs. The TVA-provided gas prices are high compared to default ReEDS assumptions, so they
are used for a high gas price sensitivity when simulating an isolated TVA system. However,
ReEDS reference gas prices are used for a high gas price sensitivity when simulating the full EI
to avoid discontinuities in gas prices across TVA/non-TVA boundaries that lead to erroneous
results. ReEDS default gas prices are generally used as a lower gas price sensitivity.

In all simulations, ReEDS load data is used to stay compatible with the generator database.
Several load projections are available in ReEDS, and for this study the Baseline scenario
provided by Evolved Energy Research is used throughout because the load growth rate most
closely matches load growth expected by TVA.

3.4 Scenario Definitions
ReEDS scenarios were chosen to align with PSHVT scenarios to the extent possible, providing a
range of potential future generation portfolios for TVA and the ability to compare outcomes
between alternative modeling methodologies. Eight scenarios were simulated for each spatial
configuration, and the alternative data and assumptions used across scenarios are described in
Table 3-1. Scenarios are named to indicate the intended emphasis on deployment of different
technologies—e.g., low solar = low solar deployment. Alternative outcomes are achieved in
several ways: (1) using alternative natural gas price projections from either TVA or the 2023
Annual Energy Outlook from the U.S. Energy Administration, (2) using alternative technology
cost projections from the 2024 Annual Technology Baseline, or (3) using firm limits on solar
growth. Solar growth limits along with the wind growth limit used for all scenarios were
developed in collaboration with TVA based on their market expectations for those technologies

  3.5.3 System Costs

Differences in generation, storage, and transmission capacity, along with associated changes to
system operation, correspond to changes in overall system cost. ReEDS calculates a system cost
metric that first annualizes all capital costs by spreading them evenly across years corresponding
to a model year (e.g., 2035 model year corresponds to 2033–2035) while operating costs in non-
modeled years are linearly interpolated. Annualized capital costs are also adjusted by a capital

 31

recovery factor to scale them appropriately for financing assumptions. These can then be
summed across years directly to calculate an undiscounted total system cost, or a selected
discount rate can be applied. To avoid confusion with the financial analysis in Section 6,
undiscounted results are presented here, but calculations could be easily reproduced for any
discount rate. While 2050 is the final simulated model year, system cost calculations extend to
2080 to include all years from 2050 to the end of the 30-year economic lifetime that is applied
universally in ReEDS. Fuel and operations and maintenance (O&M) costs are kept constant after
2050 as a representation of the costs associated with infrastructure built through 2050.

Figure 3-6 plots total undiscounted system cost impacts of Rorex Creek PSH, first showing the
full amortized costs for Rorex Creek PSH on the leftmost column of each panel (solid areas),
then the differences in other costs excluding Rorex Creek in other columns. Different hashed
area patterns represent different technologies, while colors correspond to different cost
categories. Capital cost differences follow closely from the capacity differences shown in
Figure 3-3 and Figure 3-5. A key difference is that these system costs also include the ReEDS
quantification of fuel and other O&M costs, most of which can be attributed to natural gas
capacity. Differences in ITCs are observed when the new PSH displaces battery capacity. While
substantial variability in cost impacts exists across the Isolated scenarios, there is always a net
savings before accounting for Rorex Creek PSH. Impacts are more consistent for Integrated
scenarios, with Rorex Creek typically resulting in lower natural gas capital, fuel, and O&M costs
with relatively small impacts on other costs. Despite the changes in transmission capacity across

  3.5.4 Resource Adequacy Contribution

Beyond system-wide metrics, ReEDS can also explore technoeconomic outcomes for the
proposed PSH facility in isolation. Subsequent sections of the report describe operational
behavior and economics for the Rorex Creek PSH along with detailed plant-level financial
analysis, but ReEDS has the capacity to examine plant-level RA capacity value towards ensuring
RA during periods of grid stress. Because ReEDS is tightly coupled with the PRAS RA model,
results can show how the Rorex Creek PSH operates during single-day stress periods identified
in PRAS. In addition, the optimization model structure allows for a shadow price of capacity to
be identified in each stress period. This price, when multiplied by the power provided by Rorex
Creek PSH, produces a revenue and the total revenue across all stress periods in a year is then
divided by the rated capacity of the plant to estimate an RA capacity value for Rorex Creek PSH
in that year. While TVA as a vertically integrated utility has no capacity market, this metric
reflects the implied RA capacity value as it relates to the value of energy during periods of
scarcity on the grid, which can change in timing and frequency across years and scenarios. It
does, however, consider only single-day stress periods, so it does not capture the RA value over
multi-day periods of grid stress such as during severe winter storms.

Figure 3-7 below plots the resulting RA capacity value of Rorex Creek for each year and
scenario for both the Isolated and Integrated spatial definitions. This metric is more variable
across time and scenarios for the Isolated spatial definition. The isolated system appears to have
sufficient reserve capacity in 2035 for relatively low RA capacity value, but capacity value
typically increases before stabilizing and sometimes falling in the late 2040s. Lower RA capacity
value in the Low Solar scenarios reflect increased availability of gas-fired capacity, with similar
results observed with high battery deployment. Conversely, RA capacity value is stable at around
$120/kW per year with the Integrated spatial definition. In these simulations, inter-regional trade
allows for a more stable generation mix in TVA, and even the Low Solar cases fall in line with
the others despite notably less solar and more gas in those scenarios. Given the reality of
allowable power flows between TVA and other regions along with the potential for more
substantive differences in stress period selection for Isolated scenarios, these results indicate that
a RA capacity value of $120/kW-year could be a reasonable approximation for TVA.

3.6 Conclusions
The ReEDS-PRAS integrated capacity expansion and resource adequacy model simulated
several future grid infrastructure scenarios for TVA with and without a new 1,200 MW PSH
plant at Rorex Creek, considering TVA in isolation as well as when integrated with the full
Eastern Interconnection. These scenarios provide a range of potential future TVA technology
mixes that are largely natural gas and solar by 2050 in addition to some nuclear and hydropower
capacity and some wind and batteries in certain cases. The share of capacity between natural gas
and solar technologies is sensitive to natural gas price and solar technology costs, especially
when TVA is viewed in isolation. Installing Rorex Creek PSH typically reduces the need for
natural gas-based capacity, both combined cycle and simple cycle plants. It sometimes reduces
economic deployment of batteries as well. New PSH often supports additional solar capacity
deployment, primarily through its ability to shift solar energy to different times of day, but its
relationship with both solar and wind capacity are less certain in an isolated planning context.
Impacts of new PSH on backbone transmission capacity are proportional to impacts on solar
capacity, meaning that this relationship is indirect. While it might not be intuitive that new PSH
would increase transmission needs, it does so by enabling economic solar-plus-transmission
deployment that is more cost effective than other alternatives.

System cost impacts of installing Rorex Creek PSH correspond to its effect on the generation and
transmission capacity mix along with the associated changes in system operation. Thus, system
cost savings are often dominated by reductions in natural gas fuel, O&M, and capital costs.
These cost impacts may be either amplified or offset by other technology costs depending on the
scenario and how PSH affects deployment of solar (and corresponding transmission), battery,
wind, or other capacity. More detailed operational and financial modeling approaches are more
appropriate to complete a full CBA on Rorex Creek PSH, but ReEDS CEM results show how its
construction is likely to reduce generation and transmission costs elsewhere.

ReEDS also enables a discussion of the RA capacity value of Rorex Creek PSH for providing
RA in times of grid stress. PSH is a key contributor to meeting demand in these stress periods. In
modeled scenarios, an RA capacity value of $120/kW-year is typical when representing TVA

 34

within the full EI, a setup more consistent with actual operation. RA capacity value is more
variable and often lower in an isolated system where other technologies must be built to ensure
resource adequacy that competes with PSH. This analysis, however, only considers single-day
stress periods, so RA capacity value could be higher when considering multi-day periods of grid
stress such as during winter storms.

These results show how Rorex Creek PSH affects bulk generation, transmission, and storage
infrastructure needs and the associated system-wide costs. Ultimate cost impacts are uncertain
and will be highly sensitive to realized costs of constructing the Rorex Creek facility and all
other technologies requiring investment over the next several decades. Costs and capacity value
estimates are just pieces of the overall discussion of the cost-value proposition of new PSH in
TVA, and the next section expands to a detailed examination of operating cost and value.

 4.0 Production Cost Modeling

4.1 Introduction
Production cost models, sometimes known as operational models or unit commitment and
economic dispatch models, optimize generator operations subject to physical and market
constraints of an electricity system. They can evaluate the role that PSH plays in an evolving
power system alongside other power system assets. The PCM offers a holistic look at the
operational value that a PSH asset may provide, including operational details that a CEM (in this
case, ReEDS) might ignore due to computational limits. For instance, ReEDS does a simplified
and linear approximation of generator dispatch and considers only representative operational
days (with “stressed” periods being considered for RA). PCMs, on the other hand, perform a full
annual hourly mixed integer and linear optimization that more accurately represents the
constraints of individual generating units. In addition, PCMs are better suited to consider
additional sources of value for the PSH to provide ancillary services, such as contingency
reserves, regulating reserves, and load-following reserves, which CEMs typically omit to
maintain tractability. In this way, they can provide a different perspective on the PSH system
value.

4.2 PCM Approach
For this study, we directly use outputs of ReEDS (see Section 3.0 for full detail) to create the
inputs for a PLEXOS database using open-source R2X utility [1]. This tool can convert a future
modeled infrastructure in any ReEDS solve year into a PLEXOS input database, converting and
supplementing data as necessary to fully characterize the system.

  4.2.1 Overview of PLEXOS Model

We use Energy Exemplar’s PLEXOS model [2] for assessing operational impacts of PSH in the
Integrated TVA system. PLEXOS is a commercially available PCM software that conducts a
daily unit-level cost-minimizing unit commitment and economic dispatch optimization with
hourly resolution. It contains unit-level information about the generation fleet (along with
transmission capacity) as determined for future years by ReEDS. The spatial definition is
consistent with ReEDS, meaning that we similarly consider both the Isolated and Integrated
spatial definitions. The isolated system has higher spatial detail but requires TVA to be fully self-
sufficient and is more similar to how vertically integrated utilities perform their internal analyses.
On the other hand, the integrated system is more closely aligned with how actual operations
occur since TVA is a part of a much larger, synchronous system (the EI).

All hourly profiles for load, wind, and solar are derived from NLR’s Renewable Energy Potential
model via the ReEDS tool. After ReEDS determines the buildout for a given scenario and year,
the open-source translation tool R2X is used to create a PLEXOS input dataset that mirrors the
ReEDS system [1]. We then make some changes to the dataset:

 36

 • Simplify transmission constraints in the Isolated spatial configuration. ReEDS aggregates
 transmission to the county level, where each county is assigned a node. In PCM, these are
 simplified into six nodes, with constrained flows between these nodes.

 • Replace generic hydropower data with TVA-specific data (this involves adding daily
 energy limits to TVA hydropower as well as adjusting daily capacity).

 • Require nuclear units to operate at their maximum capacity.

 • Use PSH specifications for Rorex Creek: Use 80.1% RTE to differentiate from 80% used
 for Raccoon Mountain PSH, 20-hour storage duration, allow 0–100% rated capacity in
 both pump and generation mode due to the adjustable speed pump).

 • Add hurdle rates ($5.25/MWh nominal) across TVA boundaries.

While it is possible to translate results from any ReEDS solve year, we examine PCM results for
2035, 2044, and 2050 to see the evolution of PSH system operations over time and across
scenarios. In addition to detailed operations, PLEXOS also represents generator outages
including planned maintenance outages (typically during low net load times) and forced outages
that occur randomly throughout the year in accordance with each generator’s forced outage rate
and mean time to repair. Reserve services are broken down into three types—spinning
contingency reserves, regulation reserves, and flexibility reserves. These three types of reserves
are considered to have three purposes: (1) spinning contingency reserves are deployed in the
event of an unexpected outage, (2) regulation reserves deal with expected frequency deviations,
and (3) flexibility reserves address forecast error and variability related to wind and solar
generation. The actual deployment of these reserves is not considered directly; however, they are
procured based on the required capacity and ramp. Table A-1 shows how reserve requirements
are calculated based on load, variable generation, and ramping requirement (cite ReEDS
documentation) and Table 4-1 shows the resulting requirement for the TVA Base Case. Any
other assumptions, such as fuel costs, demand forecast, and generator operating characteristics,
are consistent with the ReEDS methodology discussed in Section 3.0. Note that the reserves
requirement varies slightly across scenarios (+/- 10% for flexibility and regulation, whereas the
contingency requirement stays consistent) as it depends on load, solar, and wind generation
profiles.

  4.3.2 PSH Impacts on Operational Costs and Revenues

This section explores the impact that a potential PSH in the TVA footprint may have on system
operational costs and plant revenues as determined by the PCM. The total generation cost is the
sum of fuel costs (coal, natural gas, uranium), startup and shutdown costs, and variable operating
and maintenance (VO&M) costs. Differences in operating costs reflect not only differences in
how resources are dispatched, but also in the resources that make up the generation and
transmission mix as well.

Figure 4-5 shows the difference in annual generation costs with and without PSH in both Isolated
and Integrated spatial configurations for the solve years of 2035, 2044, and 2050. Negative y-
axis values indicate system savings (i.e., less expensive operations due to the presence of PSH)
whereas positive y-axis values indicate increased system costs. Typically, the reduced annual
operational cost due to PSH varies between around $130 million dollars per year to a slight cost
increase depending on scenario. The Isolated scenarios have more variable cost impacts, likely
because the infrastructures from ReEDS are more variable. In the Integrated cases, avoided
generation costs are primarily natural gas fuel with a significant component of avoided startup
and shutdown costs as well. Due to variations in dispatch and starting systems it’s difficult to
generalize the results, but we typically find higher avoided costs in the TVA Base Case, and
High Solar + Battery and High Solar + Nuclear cases, all three of which use the higher projected
natural gas prices. This makes sense because most of the avoided costs are fuel-related.
Typically, lower avoided costs occur in the Low Solar cases, which can be explained by the
lower availability of zero marginal cost energy to operate the pump.

Another value that Figure 4-5 captures indirectly is the value of PSH providing reserves because
it frees up another resource that had previously been providing that service, thus providing a
more efficient dispatch of the thermal fleet. For instance, in the TVA Base Case (Isolated
configuration) we find that the new PSH plant is providing some type of reserve (either
Contingency, Regulation, or Flexibility) in nearly two-thirds of hours of the year for 2035, 2044,
and 2050. In fact, in 2030 the new PSH plant is on average holding 650 MW-h of reserves at any
given time, which increases to nearly 700 MW-h on average in 2050. This allows more efficient
thermal plants to operate at higher power output, reducing overall operating costs.

While TVA is a vertically integrated utility driven by least-cost planning and operation instead of
competitive markets, it can be useful to quantify the marginal value that a PSH plant may
provide for energy and ancillary services. These values can be interpreted as avoided system
costs by the PSH plant. Figure 4-6 shows the marginal value using hourly locational marginal
values of energy when pumping and generating along with marginal ancillary service value. Note
that PLEXOS does not attempt to maximize this value for any individual plant, including the
new PSH plant. The PSH plant provides a lot of reserves (650 MW-h on average in the TVA
Base Case), but since the marginal value for reserves is typically low, the total reserves value is
also fairly low but not zero in most cases. We also found that a small number of very high-value
hours heavily influences the total reserve value of the PSH plant. In these hours, it becomes
difficult to provide the reserve requirement, and a violation of reserve requirements is observed.
However, this value, or penalty, is an input to the model ($5,000/MW-h of shortage) in place as a
“release valve” during hours of possible capacity shortage. In discussions with TVA, we set the
value of reserves during those hours to $500/MWh. We observed a handful (2-10) of hours in

most cases that violate the reserves penalty. Except for these few hours, the marginal value of
reserves is typically $0/MW-h, which reduces the reserves value of the plant. As with the

4.4 Conclusions
The role and value of PSH will vary based on the evolution of the power system. A full analysis
of hourly unit commitment and economic dispatch across various scenarios can provide key
insights into the potential role of PSH. We find that the PSH plant avoids system operational
costs primarily by displacing fuel from the most expensive marginal generators as well as
reducing the required generator start and stops on the system. The magnitude of this value varies
across scenarios, but we find that scenarios with high solar generation provide complementarity
with PSH, as it can use energy when it’s marginally very inexpensive, then generate to displace
expensive peaking plants. We also find that scenarios with the highest natural gas prices
correspond to higher system value for PSH, as the energy it generates can be used to avoid
the cost of energy from the most expensive plants. In other words, higher gas prices increase
the price spread between times of storage charge and discharge, increasing the potential
for arbitrage.

Even with a full suite of modeling tools, we still may not be able to fully quantify the full
operational value of PSH in a utility setting. For instance, one example would be black start
capability, which is not considered here. Black start is not often quantified as a source of value
but is necessary for a utility to consider. In addition, we cannot capture all possible future
conditions that could be observed on the grid. Thus, a key value might be a hedge against future
uncertainty, because there likely will always be times of excess energy and times of scarcity.
Additional PSH value arises from other ancillary services that typically lack explicit valuation
but provide system stability and transmission reliability; these are discussed in the next section.

 5.0 Transmission Reliability Modeling

5.1 Introduction
Beyond energy and capacity value for RA and reserves, PSH provides a range of ancillary
services that improve transmission reliability and overall grid strength and stability. Mechanical
inertia and controls allow PSH to provide voltage support and frequency stability, improving the
dynamic grid response to disturbances. There are some limitations of providing these ancillary
services for fixed-speed pumps, but the Rorex Creek PSH facility intends to use an adjustable-
speed pump to maximize flexibility and grid stability contributions. Such designs were first
proposed in the 1980s [1]. Adjustable-speed pumping is made possible with the use of power
electronics that vary the AC frequency at the pump motor. Whether it has a doubly fed excitation
on the rotor or a fully fed stator with salient poles rotor, it has the capability to vary the speed of
the pump, resulting in the capability to vary the load absorbed by the pump. Generally, the
continuous pump power absorption range will be in the 70%–100% range. In some cases, it
could be as much as 60%–100%. Figure 5-1 shows conventional and advanced pump storage
designs, including ternary and quaternary designs that are not discussed here.

While grid stability contributions do not have easily monetized value, it is important to consider
them in the overall value proposition for new PSH. This analysis section uses transmission
reliability modeling to quantitatively assess metrics such as short-circuit capacity and IBR
hosting capacity. The analysis seeks to assess the value of a new advanced PSH system to grid
stability in TVA including the following components:

 • Quantify the contributions to grid strength and stability of a potential new PSH facility in
 the TVA region.

 • Discuss how grid strength and stability contributions provide value to the TVA system.

 • Identify scenarios where the new PSH would be more or less valuable for contributing to
 grid strength and stability.

Doing so first requires the implementation of a dynamic model for adjustable-speed PSH
(AS-PSH). Pumped generation technology has evolved rapidly in recent years towards advanced
pump designs, and dynamic modeling of newer PSH designs is a challenge for the industry
because there might not be standardized dynamic models useful for technology evaluation in
transmission reliability models. The mathematical model for these systems—as well as model
improvements for control and new application areas—has been reported thoroughly [2, 3, 4].
Argonne conducted a series of comprehensive studies to research the value of AS-PSH in the
United States, and their reports provide a systematic summary of modeling and analysis [5].
These studies form the basis of the techniques used herein.

5.2 Modeling Approach
  5.2.1 Dynamic Simulation Tools

Transmission reliability analysis uses two software packages, PSS®E and GSET. PSS®E (Power
System Simulator for Engineering) is an industry-standard transmission-level power system
analysis tool widely used by utilities, ISOs/RTOs, and research institutions [7]. It provides
powerful scripting interfaces (Python API, IPLAN) and highly customizable functions that
enable user-defined analyses and automated studies for large-scale power systems, such as N-
1/N-2 contingency analysis and short-circuit studies.

Grid Strength Evaluation Tool (GSET) is an NLR-developed Python-based tool for online and
offline grid-strength assessment and visualization. It incorporates a suite of traditional and
advanced grid-strength metrics, including short-circuit ratio (SCR), normalized SCR (NSCR) [8,
9], and generalized (gSCR) [10] by leveraging PSS®E’s built-in short-circuit analysis module.
GSET offers a practical way for grid planners and operators to evaluate system strength in terms
of short-circuit capacity and system strength contribution. It can automatically compute user-
selected system strength metrics for each bus or system under normal or contingency conditions.

In this study, we focus on the traditional SCR metric and its underlying short-circuit capacity
(SCC), which requires a power-flow input file for TVA’s grid.

 48

 5.2.2 Short-Circuit Capacity and IBR Hosting Capacity

To evaluate how AS-PSH can enhance grid strength and stability in the TVA system under
different scenarios, we use two primary metrics: SCC and system strength contribution (SSC).

SCC, also called short-circuit MVA (SCMVA), represents the amount of fault current the grid
can deliver at a given bus. It is a direct indicator of how “strong” or “stiff” the local grid is. A
higher SCC corresponds to a stronger system with smaller voltage deviations following
disturbances, while a lower SCC indicates a weaker system where bus voltages are more
sensitive to faults or changes in power injections. SCC is typically defined as:

Because AC system strength significantly influences the stability of grid-connected IBRs, it is
often useful to compare the relative strength of different buses or operating conditions using the
SCR. SCR normalizes the SCC by the size of the connecting facility and serves as an effective
indicator of IBR stability risk. To further assess the impact on IBR hosting capacity, we evaluate
the maximum amount of additional IBR capacity that can be integrated at selected buses before
the SCR drops below a specified threshold. In this study, we use an SCR threshold of 5, as
values below 5 are commonly associated with weak-grid conditions and increased control-
stability challenges for grid-following IBRs. The hosting capacity is therefore determined by
gradually increasing the IBR capacity at each target bus until the SCR reaches this threshold.
This hosting capacity is calculated for the buses near the new PSH, so it is important to note that
any benefit to IBR hosting is specific to those bus locations.

 5.2.3 Model Assumptions

For this study, we use the 2031 summer peak load case (TVA–EI case) provided by TVA as the
baseline system model. The newly proposed AS-PSH plant is modeled as a 1,600 MW AS-PSH
facility located in Jackson County, Alabama, consistent with TVA’s initial development plans.
Note that this is different from the 1,200 MW system size used for other analysis components
due to the project timeline relative to system specifications provided by TVA and the decision
made by TVA to refocus on a 1,200 MW PSH system. Nevertheless, results are expected to be
consistent, with the magnitude of results scaling appropriately with system capacity.

Because TVA’s summer peak conditions are typically characterized by high solar output and
periods of over-generation, the AS-PSH unit is assumed to operate in pumping mode for all
scenarios. This operating assumption aligns with expected system dispatch patterns under high

renewable-generation conditions. Under these assumptions, we evaluate and compare a range of
grid-strength related metrics across the TVA system to quantify the benefits of the AS-PSH
installation.

5.4 Conclusions
This section evaluates the grid-strength benefits of installing a 1,600-MW AS-PSH facility in the
TVA region under summer peak load conditions with the PSH in pumping mode. The system
size differs from 1,200 MW used in other analysis components in this report because TVA
design specifications changed after transmission reliability modeling was completed, but results
are expected to scale proportionally with system capacity. Using TVA’s 2031 and 2035 summer
peak system models, we quantify how AS-PSH enhances SCC and local IBR hosting capability
under both current and future grid conditions. In the 2031 case, the 1,600-MW AS-PSH unit
provides a total of 58,635.2 MVA of additional SCC across the TVA system and enables
approximately 980–1,600 MW of new IBR hosting capability near its installation location. In
nearby regions, it contributes an additional 85–190 MVA of IBR hosting capacity. Impacts are
similar in the 2035 case, though the new PSH is too distant from POIs exhibiting weak grid
conditions to significantly improve SCR at those locations. Further, it is important to note that
most current solar proposals in TVA are relatively far from the Rorex Creek PSH site in the
western portions of the TVA service territory, so IBR hosting benefits could be limited without
more interest in IBR deployment in or near northeast Alabama. Nevertheless, AS-PSH can
significantly mitigate future weak-grid conditions in its surrounding area, and these results
demonstrate only a portion of the potential value new PSH can have to TVA system stability. An
important future analysis direction would be to evaluate the fast dynamic response and grid
support from AS-PSH during one or more transient events, which could highlight additional
benefits and scenarios where new PSH can help maintain a reliable, resilient TVA grid.

6.6 Conclusions
The results produced by the PSHVT yield several important conclusions. Results vary
considerably by scenario, with high natural gas prices, high deployments of solar, and high load
corresponding to higher value for PSH operations. The constrained gas scenario generates the
highest returns for PSH. The reference case and retain coal case yield the lowest returns for PSH.
Scenarios with higher RPSs in surrounding states had limited effect on outcomes. All
72 scenarios explored for this study using the PSHVT yielded positive financial returns, as
measured in both NPVs, which ranged from $286 million to $1.7 billion, and BCRs, ranging
from 1.2 to 2.1. Results are highly sensitive to key financial assumptions (e.g., borrowing rates,
ITCs, discount rates). Results are particularly sensitive with respect to assumptions governing the
ITC, with total elimination reducing NPVs by approximately $1.3 billion.

The results from the ReEDS plus PLEXOS runs are broadly higher than those generated by the
PSHVT, with BCR ranges from 1.6 (NPV of $897 million) for the low solar plus battery case
with an isolated spatial scope and a $500 price cap to 4.1 ($4.71 billion) for the high solar plus
nuclear case with an isolated spatial scope and no price cap. The scenarios with lower solar
deployment rates tend to yield lower financial returns, as do the scenarios with price caps. The
ReEDS plus PLEXOS runs produced broadly higher financial returns when compared to those
generated by the PSHVT, though the differences tighten considerably when a price cap is
imposed in PLEXOS. BCRs ranged from 1.2-2.2 for the PSHVT and 1.6-2.5 for the ReEDS plus
PLEXOS runs using the $500 price cap.

following PSH deployment. These results suggest that PSH could become more valuable to TVA
over time.

 7.0 Conclusions

This study evaluated the economic viability of the proposed 1,200 MW, 23,365 MWh Rorex
Creek PSH plant that would be located near Pisgah, Alabama. In addressing this question, this
study has developed processes, models, and data that better value PSH from a utility perspective,
more specifically a vertically integrated utility, enabling optimal PSH design and deployment.
This study is designed to enhance the TVA’s toolset in making PSH investment decisions and
contribute to the design of a more cost-effective, stable future grid. These techniques can then be
applied to a broader range of assets and utilities. The remainder of this section presents the
conclusions drawn from the technoeconomic studies and associated financial analysis.

7.1 Conclusions Drawn from the Technoeconomic Studies
This study uses several tools and models developed and used by Argonne and NLR, informed by
input data and assumptions provided by TVA. From these studies defined throughout this report,
we draw several important conclusions:

 • There are both benefits and costs to conducting valuation analysis with such a complex
 suite of models. Using multiple tools allows cross-model comparisons that can help
 demonstrate robust outcomes or reveal uncertainties in input assumptions and power
 system representations. Greater model consistency and integration allow for more
 internally consistent analysis of the full value stack for a given investment opportunity,
 such as PSH in this case. However, these benefits require increased effort to ensure
 consistency in data and methods and subsequently to examine any discrepant results.
 Careful study planning and early examination of key inputs and assumptions can help
 reduce repeated and iterative simulations. While integrated multi-model studies require
 additional cost and time, the additional effort could be justified to support large
 (i.e., multi-billion dollar) investment decisions with long-lasting implications.

 • Annual system cost reductions resulting from Rorex Creek deployment for the six base
 resource portfolio scenarios considered in the PSHVT assessment yielded annual
 operational savings in the $140–$190 million range. System-perspective benefits are led
 by avoided system capital costs, complemented by notable reductions in system
 contingency reserve and generation costs in most cases. The “constrained gas” portfolio
 exhibits higher avoided capital costs than other scenarios, driven by the constraints on gas
 turbine capacity. In this scenario, the candidate PSH substitutes for peaking capacity that
 would otherwise be supplied by battery storage yielding greater capacity value because
 batteries carry higher capital costs. High PSH values are also observed in the “high solar”
 and “high nuclear” scenarios where system flexibility demands are greater.

 • While TVA is a vertically integrated utility without competitive markets, it can be useful
 to quantify the plant-level value, or avoided cost, that a PSH plant may accrue based on
 modeled marginal energy and ancillary service value. For the ReEDS/PLEXOS cases,
 value associated with energy and reserve services averaged around $70 million annually.
 The PSH plant provides a lot of reserves (650 MW-h on average in the TVA Base Case),
 but since the marginal value for reserves is typically low, the total reserves value is fairly

 65

 low, although not zero in most cases. Lower operational values are observed in the low
 solar cases, and the highest values occur in the three cases with high gas prices (TVA
 Base Case, High Solar + Batter, and High Solar + Nuclear). This again results from the
 complementarity of PSH with solar due to its temporal availability and zero-marginal
 cost, as well as the value of PSH in systems with increasing fuel prices.

• Because ReEDS is tightly coupled with the PRAS RA model, results can show how the
 Rorex Creek PSH operates during single-day stress periods identified in PRAS. In
 addition, the optimization model structure allows for a shadow price of capacity to be
 identified in each stress period. This price, when multiplied by the power provided by
 Rorex Creek PSH, produces a value to TVA, and the total value across all stress periods
 in a year is then divided by the rated capacity of the plant estimates an RA capacity value
 for Rorex Creek PSH in that year. While TVA as a vertically integrated utility has no
 capacity market, this metric reflects the implied resource adequacy capacity value as it
 relates to the value of energy during periods of scarcity on the grid, which can change in
 timing and frequency across years and scenarios. The RA value for Rorex Creek was
 estimated at $120/kW-year, and additional value could be demonstrated if multi-day
 stress periods were considered.

• The financial results produced by the PSHVT yield several important conclusions.
 Results vary considerably by scenario, with high natural gas prices, high shares of solar,

 $134 million to $1.4 billion, and BCRs, ranging from 1.1 to 1.9.

• Financial analysis using results from the PLEXOS plus ReEDS analysis yielded BCR
 results ranging from 1.6 (NPV - $897 million) for the low solar plus battery case with an
 isolated spatial scope and a $500 price cap to 4.1 ($4.71 billion) for the high solar plus
 nuclear case with an isolated spatial scope and no price cap. The scenarios with lower
 solar penetration rates tend to yield lower financial returns, as do the scenarios with price
 caps. The ReEDS plus PLEXOS runs produced broadly higher financial returns when
 compared to those generated by the PSHVT, though the differences tightened
 considerably when a price cap was imposed in PLEXOS. BCRs ranged from 1.2–2.2 for
 the PSHVT and 1.6–2.5 for the ReEDS plus PLEXOS runs using the $500 price cap.

• While both the PSHVT and PLEXOS models were run for 2035, PLEXOS was
 additionally run for 2044 and 2050. The PLEXOS results indicate significant growth in
 PSH value between 2035 and 2044, with value under the base case nearly doubling. In
 addition, the RA analysis suggests that the cost of RA could increase significantly in the
 years following PSH deployment. These results suggest that PSH would become more
 valuable to TVA over time.

 • As adjustable-speed PSH enhances system strength overall, it also increases the system’s

A.1 PSHVT
The PSHVT is a step-by-step tool designed to assess the value of services provided by PSH
plants as defined in the voluminous Pumped Storage Hydropower Valuation Guidebook. The
PSHVT provides an easy-to-follow, step-by-step process to work through the Guidebook’s
15-step decision framework. This decision tree-based tool provides valuation guidance for PSH
developers, plant owners or operators, and other stakeholders such as regulators, policymakers,
and consultants to assess the value of existing or potential new PSH plants and their services.
The tool can be accessed at https://pshvt.egs.anl.gov/.

The tool is designed to advance the state of the art in assessing the value of a broad range of
services provided by PSH plants, including:

 • Bulk power capacity

 • Energy arbitrage

 • Production cost reductions

 • Ancillary services

 • Power system stability benefits

 • Transmission benefits

The tool enables the user to design multiple projects and several scenarios that fall under each
project where the user can modify inputs and evaluate the sensitivity of the results with respect to
changes in those key parameters. For example, a user could evaluate two PSH projects and then
save multiple cases under each where the scale of the plants or key policy assumptions or growth
rates are varied. The user can accumulate projects and cases and edit or remove them at their
discretion. This function enables the user to return to existing cases and update them as needed
over time without having to start from scratch. Note that the number of cases under each project
is identified in the repository and once an individual project is selected, the tool identifies the
number of steps completed under each case.

Features of the PSHVT include embedded tools: a back-end BCA tool, a price-taker valuation
tool for small-scale PSH, a price-influencer model that uses Argonne’s A-LEAF tool, and an
MCDA tool. When evaluating large-scale PSH that could influence market prices, one path of
the tool walks the user through a valuation process using capacity expansion planning and

 68

production cost models such as Aurora or PLEXOS. It does provide an overview of multiple
model types, segmented by primary application, but all calculations are conducted external to the
PSHVT. The second price-influencer path directs the user to the PSHVT module of the A-LEAF
tool, which enables the user to perform all required calculations to estimate the benefits of PSH
operations from a PSH owner-operator or system/grid operator perspective. A recent addition is a
downloadable version of the tool. For a more detailed description of the tool, see an article
describing the PSHVT at https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/10287701.

A.2 ReEDS
  A.2.1 Additional Information on Spatial Scope and Resolution

ReEDS simulates the TVA model region over two different spatial definitions to capture
different metrics used in this study. The first is a county-level spatial representation of the TVA
service territory; this spatial definition defines the TVA region as any U.S. county in which TVA
services over 50% of total electricity demand and only considers this TVA region in the ReEDS
model optimization. This resolution provides a more accurate representation of the TVA service
territory and its expected demand, initial generation and transmission technologies, and available
potential renewable energy sites, allowing for a more direct comparison between simulated
ReEDS results and projections from the TVA IRP. The higher resolution of this spatial definition
also allows for more detailed analysis of ReEDS investment decisions, particularly where
ReEDS decides to locate the various generation, storage, production, and transmission
technologies in optimization. This “isolated” spatial definition, however, is unable to represent
the effects of inter-regional trade between TVA and its neighboring regions’ service providers, as
the model does not simulate these “non-TVA” regions. Additionally, computational limitations
prevent the model from solving the entire EI at the county level, which otherwise would have
allowed for the representation of inter-regional trade.

A second spatial definition is used to step over these limitations and model inter-regional trade;
this spatial definition is at the ReEDS BA resolution and defines the TVA region using four BAs
that most closely resemble the county-level spatial boundaries of the county-level TVA spatial
definition. Along with the four “TVA BAs,” this spatial definition also considers all other BAs in
the Eastern Interconnect during ReEDS model simulation. This “integrated” spatial definition
thus allows the model to consider inter-regional activity between TVA and the rest of the EI
during optimization, and additional post-processing steps are conducted to filter results
specifically for the four BAs representing TVA. The BA-level resolution of this definition,
however, reduces the ability for direct results comparisons against either the county-level
definition or TVA IRP, as the BA boundaries are less aligned with the real TVA service
territories, resulting in an even less accurate representation of TVA expected demand, initial
technologies, and renewable energy sites. Additionally, the reduced resolution of the TVA region
leads to lower precision in the siting of generation and transmission model investments, since
ReEDS will have only four regions within TVA to site new capacity. As a result, model results
using the “integrated” spatial definition are almost exclusively used to analyze system-wide
inter-regional transmission trends for TVA.

  A.2.2 Additional Information on Input Data

This study uses a modified version of the default ReEDS generator database. The default ReEDS
generator database was developed from the National Energy Modeling System unit database
from AEO2023 and supplemented with information from the March 2024 EIA860M form.
Extensive effort was made to validate the data for each unit owned by TVA in the database. The
research team worked with members of TVA to validate each unit’s start and retirement years,
nameplate capacities, heat rates, and regional assignments and adjust metrics as required to be
more aligned with TVA-provided unit data.

When deciding which units to include in ReEDS, the research team elected to adhere to the
regional location of a unit over its ownership. Effectively, units from the database are included in
model simulations if they exist within the regional boundaries of the defined TVA region,
regardless of whether they are explicitly owned or operated by TVA. Likewise, units owned or
operated by TVA but located outside the TVA region were not included in model simulations.
This decision was made to ensure spatial consistency between generation resources and load as
defined in ReEDS because ReEDS load data is not readily divisible into TVA and non-TVA
components within a given region.

Hydropower capacity factors used in the study were derived from plant-level operational data
provided by TVA. For each hydropower plant owned and operated by the utility, observed total
generation as well as average minimum and maximum nameplate capacity data was provided at
6-hour intervals. Plant-level data was mapped and aggregated to their respective ReEDS
technology and BA, and capacity factor values were calculated at the monthly ReEDS BA
spatiotemporal resolution. For the county-level runs using the Isolated spatial definition, BA-
level hydropower capacity factors were uniformly disaggregated such that each county is
assigned the hydropower capacity factor of its corresponding BA. The calculated values
represent hydropower capacity factors for “future” ReEDS years (i.e., 2020–2050). ReEDS
simulations of historical model years use default ReEDS hydropower capacity factors, which
also consider realized net generation at the plant level, to maintain consistency with historic
energy demand.
