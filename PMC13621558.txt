How large language models judge and influence human cooperation

Alexandre S Pires, Laurens Samson, Sennay Ghebreab, Fernando P Santos, Frederick Chang

Abstract

Humans increasingly rely on large language models (LLMs) to support decisions in social settings. Previous work suggests that such tools can shape people’s moral judgments. However, how LLMs evaluate decisions in social dilemmas, and the long-term implications of LLM-based assessments on human cooperation—driven by indirect reciprocity, reputations, and the capacity to judge interactions of others—remain unclear. We evaluate the social norms of 21 state-of-the-art LLMs when judging more than 40,000 fictitious decisions in social dilemmas. Through an evolutionary game-theoretical model, we study prosociality in populations adhering to these norms, theoretically studying their potential impact on long-term human cooperation. We observe a remarkable agreement in LLMs when evaluating actions against opponents perceived as good. Yet, there is large within- and between-model variance when assessing actions toward ill-reputed individuals. Our theoretical analysis reveals that these differences can significantly impact the prevalence of cooperation within our model. Finally, we show that prompt-based interventions can steer LLM norms, particularly when these define objective goals. Our theoretical model links LLM-based advice to long-term social dynamics, highlighting the importance of designing LLM norms considering their potential effects on human cooperation.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have proliferated throughout society at a remarkable speed. These tools can offer significant benefits, improving productivity, access to information, routine tasks, and education (, ). At the same time, LLMs suggest renewed ethical and social challenges (, ). Humans behave differently when interacting with LLMs than when interacting with other people (). LLMs themselves can reveal biases (, ), based on cultural differences (), gender (, ), or identity (). Such biases also manifest when LLMs judge behaviors, potentially shaping societal norms and reinforcing existing inequalities and cultural stereotypes (). Evidence suggests that these systems may influence our political opinions (, ), moral judgments (), and expressed opinions (). In fact, analyses of chat messages on ChatGPT show that about 1.9% of messages specifically relate to seeking personal advice and making interpersonal decisions (). As a result, it is nowadays fundamental to understand how interactions with language models can possibly affect our very own social fabric, particularly our capacity to cooperate with each other on a large scale ().

Human cooperation is a fundamental aspect of well-functioning societies, and our ability to cooperate is known to depend on shared values, interaction observability and reputation spreading (). We assign reputations according to social norms, and these play a central role in deciding with whom to cooperate (). This mechanism is known as indirect reciprocity (IR) (). While norms and indirect reciprocity have been extensively studied in human societies (), their role in human-AI hybrid settings remains less understood (). Most importantly, as LLMs can influence beliefs and moral judgments, their impact in human reciprocal cooperation—and eventually human-AI prosociality (, )—remains unclear. Beyond influencing norms, humans may also delegate LLMs to judge others (, ). In these settings, the social norms that LLMs use to assess others’ actions are highly relevant to sustain cooperation, but little is known about them.

In this work, we aim to answer three questions: (i) What social norms do LLMs adopt, within an indirect reciprocity framework? (ii) Can the social norms used by LLMs sustain cooperation under indirect reciprocity? and (iii) Can we guide the social norms of LLMs using prompt-based interventions?

To address these questions, we introduce a framework and dataset of prompts to extract the social norms used by LLMs in indirect reciprocity settings. Our dataset includes 43,200 examples of interactions, generated by combining human hand-written templates and elements. This extensive prompt dataset results from combining 16 individuals (with given names portraying different gender and cultural backgrounds), playing a 2-action game on two roles, with two possible reputations, and interacting in nine different contexts (e.g. offering money or food); all of these interactions are prompted based on five template variations (see Methods for a detailed description of the prompt dataset creation and size). In these examples, cooperation is explicitly or implicitly framed as incurring a cost (c) to the helper and a benefit (b) to the individual being helped, with b>c. We provide these examples to a total of 21 LLMs (e.g. GPT-4o (), Deepseek R1 (), and Grok 2 ()) and ask them to assign a reputation to individuals that cooperate or refuse to cooperate. From the answers provided, we extract a social norm following the formalism typically considered in indirect reciprocity models.

In a second stage, we employ a traditional evolutionary game-theoretical model to evaluate the capacity of LLM-based norms to sustain human cooperation. Crucially, our model provides the flexibility to consider probabilistic norms, thereby allowing us to evaluate the average responses of LLMs and their eventual uncertainty. We study two regimes of reputation spreading: public reputations, where following an interaction, observers gossip about the assigned reputation until consensus is reached (); and private reputations, where each agent possesses an individual view of every other agent, maximizing possible disagreement (). Notably, cooperation under a social norm is highly dependent on gossip levels and is particularly difficult in private assessment settings (). While private reputation systems emulate settings where individuals hold private views and might disagree on how to assess others, public reputation systems might in reality result from a large number of gossip rounds, population-wide broadcasting, and/or centralized reputation systems.

Finally, we study the impact of prompt engineering on the social norms expressed by LLMs by including additional instructions in our prompt dataset. This method allows us to measure how LLMs interpret and adopt user-defined preferences and goals. A schematic view of our pipeline is presented in Fig. .

Results

Measuring social norms used by LLMs

We measure the social norms of multiple LLMs, following the formalism typically used in indirect reciprocity (, , ). We consider 21 LLMs with varying levels of accessibility (open- and closed-weights models), parameter sizes, and different price ranges, reflecting both models used by everyday consumers and enterprises (see Methods). To assess these social norms, we generate a dataset of 43,200 prompts that ask the model to assign a reputation (good or bad) to an individual (donor), after observing this donor either help or not help another individual (receiver). Importantly, the LLM is also informed about the prior reputation it assigned to the receiver, allowing (but not forcing) it to utilize reputational information, reflecting a second-order social norm ().

In Fig. , we present the average assigned reputation by each LLM when asked to assess an interaction. With the exception of Llama 2 7B, all tested models assess interactions with good individuals following the same principles of norms identified theoretically as the most cooperative (known as the leading eight social norms) (): cooperating with good individuals results in a good reputation to the donor, and defecting against good individuals is considered bad.

When assessing interactions with bad individuals, we observe a large variation in judgments, also echoing previous theoretical works indicating that ranking the most cooperative social norms depends on subtle details such as reputation observability and behavioral errors (, ). Most LLMs tested assign a good reputation to those who cooperate with bad recipients, but vary in how they judge defections. This corresponds to a mixture of two social norms known to sustain cooperation: Image Score (IS, cooperating is good, defection is bad ()) and Simple Standing (SS, cooperating is always good, defecting against bad individuals is also good (, )). Notably, IS is a low-complexity norm, as it considers solely the donor’s action and not the reputation assigned to the recipient (). There are also a variety of models that present different norms, such as Gemma 2 27B IT () and Llama 3.1 8B (), which use a mixture of IS and a different social norm known as Shunning (SH), where only cooperating with good people is considered good. SH is, importantly, a strict norm as it labels any individual interacting with a bad recipient as bad, which can lead to a spread of bad reputations (, , ). Llama 3.3 70B () employs a norm close to Stern-Judging (SJ), where cooperating with good individuals is good, but agents should defect when facing bad individuals, motivating punishments (). Finally, Gemini 1.5-Pro () and Grok 2 () both feature norms with no consistent rule for being considered good or bad when facing bad individuals.

With some exceptions, most LLM families we tested tend to move from IS toward SS as versions and parameter size increase. This indicates a shift toward higher complexity social norms () which make use of more context about donations’ recipients to assign a reputation. Moreover, different versions of the same family can have vastly distinct social norms, such as Claude 3.5 Haiku () and Claude 3.7 Sonnet (), despite their similar ethical goals (). The Llama family () presents an interesting example of version differences, as the norms of each model differ significantly. First, Llama 2 7B assigns a good reputation to donors almost independently of the recipient’s reputation, making it the simplest social norm. Its larger version, Llama 2 13B, instead uses a SS–IS mixture, while, as previously stated, Llama 3.1 8B and Llama 3.3 70B present entirely different norms. The only other family of models that evolves away from SS is Gemma, where the Gemma 2 9B model uses a SS–IS mixture, yet the larger 27-billion-parameter model adopts a SH–IS mixture, a similar trajectory to Llama 2 13B and Llama 3.1 8B.

We note that the interactions portrayed in the prompts, which LLMs are asked to evaluate, differ in phrasing, topics contextualizing the interactions, and actor names suggesting different gender and cultural backgrounds. This variability allows us to measure within-model variance and sensitivity to small variations in prompts. In Fig. , this uncertainty is represented by an ellipse indicating 1 SD of the calculated social norm (see Methods section). While some models, such as Claude 3.5 Haiku, showcase very low variation, indicating that their social norm is consistent across phrasings, actor names and interaction contexts, models such as Grok 2 display more volatile norms. We present an expanded analysis of these biases, and sensitivity to phrasing, in the . In general, we find that LLM-based assessments also depend on gender and perceived cultural background of the recipients (as inferred from their names), and more significantly, on the context of the interaction.

Cooperation under LLM norms

After extracting the social norm revealed by different LLMs, we pose the question: how are such norms likely to impact long-term cooperation dynamics? To this end, we applied a classic evolutionary game-theoretical model to study cooperation in an adaptive population where individuals repeatedly play donation games and assess each other following the social norms observed in LLMs. In the donation game, one agent (donor) decides to cooperate with another agent (receiver). To cooperate means incurring a cost c to provide a benefit b to the receiver (b>c); to defect means paying no cost and providing no benefit. This simple game captures the quintessential social dilemma of cooperation: to cooperate is socially desirable, as its benefits are higher than the costs (as b>c); however selfish individuals are likely to avoid cooperating as it is individually costly (as c>0). Donors decide to cooperate or defect depending on behavioral strategies: (i) always cooperate, (ii) always defect, or (iii) cooperate only with good individuals. Crucially, strategies with a greater payoff are more likely to be imitated and used by other players (). We assume that every nonparticipating agent will observe donation interactions and assign a reputation to the donor. Importantly, when assessing LLM norms, our model assumes a limit where all humans have access to the same LLM, and assign to every donor the reputation suggested by that LLM’s norm (see for results on limited LLM influence in human social norms). Following traditional models, cooperative actions have a probability to be incorrectly executed, resulting in a defection (with a probability ee), and social norms might be incorrectly applied (with a probability ea). Refer to the for an analysis of the effect of the different cooperation model parameters.

In Fig. , we present the prevalence of cooperation (cooperation index, I, see Methods) across the same norm space introduced in Fig. , for a public reputation setting. Following traditional results in IR (), we observe that the space around SJ presents the highest level of cooperation (I≃0.96), with cooperation decaying approaching SS (I≃0.75), more so around IS (I≃0.51), and becoming substantially lower closer to SH (I≃0.24). By overlaying the social norms extracted from LLMs on this cooperation map, we observe that most models adopt the SS-IS edge leading to I∈[0.5,0.75]. We also find that most of the benchmarked LLM families are evolving toward SS (Fig. , top-right corner), and the capacity to promote cooperation through the social norm is improving in relation to earlier models. Interestingly, of all tested models, only Llama 3.3 70B IT exhibits a norm capable of maximizing cooperation under this scenario. Furthermore, the models with unclear social norms, such as Grok 2 and Gemini 1.5 Pro, are still capable of promoting high levels of cooperation.

Figure provides an overview of the norms extracted and their connection with cooperation in a setting where reputations are assumed to perfectly spread in the population or be stored in a centralized reputation system. The introduction of LLMs is, however, also likely to impact how information spreads given within- and between-model variation and the existence of decentralized systems (e.g. fine-tuned and applied locally). This variation might lead to disagreements on reputations about the same individual, a phenomenon that is well known to affect cooperation under indirect reciprocity (, ). In Fig. , we study cooperation under a selection of LLM norms while varying (i) the benefit-cost ratios of cooperation (b/c) and (ii) the extent to which reputations perfectly spread in the population and are agreed by all; under public reputations, individuals are all assumed to share the same opinion about the same individual; under private reputations, individuals hold personal views about each other and might disagree about their opinion of others. We also show how different LLMs lead to different uncertainty regions regarding cooperation. Under public reputations, we again observe that Llama 3.3 70B IT is capable of maximizing cooperation. Despite GPT-4o being apparently close to Simple Standing, it presents a low level of cooperation as it sometimes assigns a good reputation to donors who defect against good individuals. Notably, Grok 2, which does not follow any of the well-defined social norms, still achieves high levels of cooperation. Models such as Claude 3.5 Haiku present fairly invariable norms that result in consistent effects in cooperation, while models like GPT-4o and Grok 2 present larger uncertainty regions, highlighting the importance of consistent norms.

We also observe how cooperation depends on reputation observability, with the best performing norm under public reputations (revealed by Llama 3.3 70B) presenting the worst level of cooperation under private reputations. In contrast, norms close to Image Score, common in earlier and smaller models such as Claude 3.5 Haiku, instead show a moderate level of cooperation, but a high resilience under decentralized reputation systems. Aligning social norms used by LLMs

After associating cooperation with social norms extracted from LLMs, a fundamental question follows: can we align the extracted norms to achieve cooperative states? This allows us to not only assess the flexibility of an LLM to other types of judgment rules, but the capacity of the LLM to translate user goals and expectations to changes in its social norm. In particular, we focus on four types of instructions: universalization (), that asks the LLM to consider what would happen to cooperation if everyone followed its judgment rule; empathizing (), which prompts the LLM to consider what it would have done if it were the donor; signaling (), which suggests to consider if the assigned reputation rewards cooperation while discouraging defection; and motivation (), where we instruct the LLM to consider that the reputation it assigns could affect the choices of others and that its goal is to maximize cooperation.

In Fig. , we present how the norms extracted from a subset of the LLMs change when using these additional instructions. Starting with empathizing, the change in norm is highly model dependent. While Llama 3.1 8B IT and, to a lower degree, Gemini 1.5 Pro shift in the direction of Shunning (SH), suggesting a low level of empathy toward the donor, Phi-4 instead shifts to Simple Standing (SS), indicating high empathy. On the other hand, Qwen 2.5 7B IT more closely follows Image Score (IS). When prompted to maximize cooperation via motivation, the tested models universally shift their social norm toward IS, indicating an association that punishing defectors but not cooperators, regardless of the receiver’s reputation, is more likely to lead to high cooperation. Under signaling, all models reduce the probability of assigning a good reputation to defectors (in Llama 3.1 8B IT this probability was already close to 0), thereby effectively signaling that defections are negative. Finally, universalization is again model-dependent. While Gemini 1.5 Pro and Llama 3.1 8B IT move toward SH (with the latter assigning “bad” in every scenario, including with good recipients), Phi-4 and Qwen 2.5 7B IT both approach IS.

In addition to the impact on the norm, the sensitivity of each model to additional instructions is also of interest. Gemini 1.5 Pro and Llama 3.1 8B IT are highly influenced by the prompt interventions, while Phi-4 and, in particular, Qwen 2.5 7B IT show a reduced effect. Importantly, however, we note that all the models tested stay within the same norm region, showcasing some level of consistency in their norms.

We conclude that, depending on the model, the interventions tested are effective in shifting the social norm used by an LLM. Notably, motivation and signaling are consistent across most models, and are particularly impactful in incentivizing IS, which disregards reputational information about the receiver and is most successful at promoting cooperation in private reputation environments (see Fig. ).

Discussion

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly accessible () and widely used as a source of information and advice (, ). These models are capable of influencing the opinions of users () regarding how to judge others, by providing advice or deciding on their behalf (). This raises questions regarding LLMs’ capacity to alter human reputation dynamics and affect human prosociality in the long-run.

We developed a framework and prompt dataset to assess the social norms used by LLMs in a format compatible with previous work on indirect reciprocity (IR), and studied how LLM social norms may affect human cooperation under an evolutionary game-theoretical model. We analyze multiple state-of-the-art LLMs, together with less powerful and more generally accessible models. We observe that, just as norms vary across human societies (), LLMs reveal a wide range of assessments when judging human cooperation. Importantly, we observe that across versions of the same LLM family, earlier and smaller models tend to adopt a low-complexity and objective norm similar to Image Score (IS, cooperation is good, defection is bad) (). In contrast, recent versions, with more parameters, tend to adopt more complex and reputation-dependent norms—often Simple Standing (SS, cooperation is always good, it is also good to defect against bad individuals) (). Importantly, the social norms used across models of the same family can change drastically, such as in the Llama () or Claude (, ) family.

We further show that LLM-inferred norms can be suboptimal at maintaining cooperation, contingent on the level of information sharing in the population, under a classic evolutionary game-theoretical model where a population of individuals repeatedly plays a donation game and attribute reputations using a given LLM’s social norm. Under public reputations, a common assumption where reputations are centralized, more complex norms closer to Stern-Judging (SJ, cooperating with good individuals is good, but one should defect against bad individuals) () maximizes cooperation in our model by promoting the stability of individuals channeling cooperation to those also cooperating. Of the LLMs tested, we found that only Llama 3.3 70B IT utilized such a norm. Nevertheless, most evaluated families of LLMs are moving toward Simple Standing, and achieve higher cooperation relative to earlier LLMs, in our model. On the other hand, when reputations are private and there is no mechanism for widespread agreement, only norms close to Image Score maintain moderate levels of cooperation within our model. Although this scenario is less common, norms used by earlier LLMs lead to better cooperation outcomes in our framework, as they disregard prior receivers’ reputations and thus minimize disagreements.

Finally, we examined whether the social norms expressed by LLMs can be guided through prompt interventions. We tested four types of instructions, based on previous mechanisms that have been shown to improve cooperation in either LLMs or humans: encouraging the model to consider what would happen if all agents judged similarly to it (universalization) (); prompting it to adopt the perspective of the donor (empathizing) (); instructing it to assign a reputation that clearly promotes cooperation and discourages defection (signaling) (); and providing the goal of maximizing cooperation (motivation) (). Among these, signaling and motivation produced the most consistent effects across models. Motivation shifted LLM norms toward Image Score, while signaling lead to models more often judging defection with bad individuals as bad. This consistency may be due to the explicit goal present in the prompt, helping models organize their reasoning more coherently, akin to chain-of-thought prompting (, ). In contrast, the empathizing and universalization interventions yielded model-dependent outcomes. Our results extend the discussion of reasoning, observation and information-spreading cooperation mechanisms, such as signaling () and empathy (, , ), by showing how LLMs interpret such mechanisms and consequently alter their social norms.

Although we focus our analysis on cooperation dynamics, we also identify biases in how LLMs assign reputations. Models differ in their social norms depending on the gender and region of the actors in the prompt (as inferred from their names), aligning with previous results on these types of biases (, , ). We find a high variation in the norms displayed depending on the context of the interactions observed by the LLMs, with a greater uncertainty in ambiguous prompts (e.g. “Liam needs help.”) than in more defined contexts (e.g. “Alice needs money to eat.”), again aligning with previous works (). While this variation may reflect contextual sensitivity, it also introduces unpredictability in reputation judgments, which can potentially lead to unintended social biases when LLMs are used in real-world decision-making processes. In our model, we quantify these variations in social norms as uncertainty regions in the space of possible social norms. We show how some models, such as Claude 3.5 Haiku () and Mistral Small () exhibit low uncertainty, and therefore a prompt-resilient and consistent social norm. On the other hand, models such as Grok 2 () and Llama 3.3 70B () use norms that are highly sensitive to actor names, phrasing and contexts for interactions.

Our results highlight an important concern: although LLMs are trained on vast amounts of human-generated data, the norms revealed by these tools vary significantly from each other and, according to our model, may fail to maintain human cooperation. Given the increasing integration of LLMs in decision-making processes, ranging from content moderation to AI-assisted governance (), it is crucial to consider not only their implicit social norms, but also their capacity to influence human cooperative behavior (), including through indirect means such as altering human reputation assignments or delegation. Previous work has shown that LLMs can persuade humans (, ), shaping their beliefs and behaviors. The risk that these effects may be augmented in a future where agentic AI, powered by LLMs, becomes highly customizable and acts autonomously on behalf of humans motivates the need to understand LLM-influenced human cooperation dynamics ().

The stylized model we develop presents limitations due to our simplifying assumptions. First, we assume that humans will be influenced to use the social norm suggested by the LLMs, which remains to be empirically verified. While prior work suggests that LLMs can, in fact, influence human judgments (), it remains under-explored whether these results extend to individuals assessing social dilemmas of cooperation. Furthermore, we considered scenarios where only one LLM is present and equally accessible to everyone. A deeper analysis could consider inequalities in LLM access and scenarios with multiple distinct LLMs. Finally, our analysis is theoretical and, for mathematical tractability, assumes a homogeneous population of individuals. These limitations suggest future work on theoretical modeling (e.g. allowing for different segments of the population to be influenced by different LLM versions) and experimental work (e.g. testing the external validity of our model and the extent to which LLM advice on judging cooperative behavior is followed by users).

Despite these challenges, our work offers a means of combining recent LLM tools and prior literature on human cooperation through reciprocity. This answers a call for better integration of (recent) LLM research within (traditional) multiagent systems literature (). Overall, by combining LLM-extracted behaviors and evolutionary dynamics modeling, our framework provides an example of long-term behavioral dynamics analysis in societies where LLM-based advice becomes prevalent. Our results and conclusions are constrained to current LLM models and our game-theoretical model. However, we hope that our framework can be used as a benchmark to audit the development of future LLMs, acknowledging their potential influence on human cooperation when used as decision-making advice tools—a critical task to address given reported effects such as human-AI feedback loops () and data poisoning (). Crucially, we already show that subtle differences in LLM model versions and prompting lead to variation in the social norms extracted, and such differences significantly affect long-term cooperation dynamics within our theoretical framework.

Methods

We study LLM norms and their effect on human cooperation following a two-step pipeline: First, determining the social norm used by the LLM; and second, studying the impact of this social norm on human cooperation via an evolutionary game-theoretical indirect reciprocity (IR) model.

The model, described in the Section Cooperation model, is used to determine the cooperation level of an adaptive population playing the donation game, using a given social norm. In our model, we make use of the social norms extracted from LLMs.

To assess the social norm employed by the LLM, a prompt dataset is generated from a template system, allowing many prompt variations of the same structure. The LLM is then individually prompted on each prompt of the dataset, and its answers are parsed and aggregated into a social norm. This process is described in Section LLM social norm assessment.

Cooperation model

Following traditional models of IR (, ), we consider a finite and well-mixed population of Z adaptive agents repeatedly playing a donation game. In this game, two agents are paired, with one being in the role of donor, and another as receiver. The donor can play one of two actions: C, cooperate, which gives a benefit b to the receiver at its own cost of c, (b>c>0); or D, defect, where no benefit is given, at no cost to the donor. Each individual has assigned a binary reputation, either good (G) or bad (B), to every other individual in the population. We further assume that all individuals not participating in a game observe the interaction, assigning a reputation to the donor. The details of the reputation assignment are explained in Section Reputation dynamics.

At each game, the action picked by the donor is dependent on its strategy. We consider strategies conditioned on the reputation of the receiver, which we encode as s=(sG,sB), where sG and sB are the probability of picking action C against an individual seen as G and B, respectively. We focus our analysis on three commonly studied pure strategies: ALLC (1,1), which always cooperates; ALLD (0,0), which always defects; and DISC (1,0), which only cooperates with G individuals and defects otherwise. Additionally, we consider errors in the execution of the strategy, where with a probability ee a donor intending to cooperate will instead defect (, ).

Reputation dynamics

The assignment of reputations is made individually by each agent, following a social norm common to the entire population. In particular, we consider second-order social norms (), where the assigned reputation of the donor is dependent on its action and the reputation of the receiver, in the view of the observer. We formalize a social norm as d=(dG,C,dG,D,dB,C,dB,D), d∈[0,1]4, where each entry corresponds to the probability of assigning a good reputation given the donor’s action and the receiver’s reputation. We include assessment errors, where with a probability ea an agent assigns the opposite reputation to what was intended (). In our figures we explicitly identify four key norms, previously discussed in the literature (, ): Image Score (IS) (), d=(1,0,1,0), where cooperation is good and defection is bad; Simple Standing (SS), d=(1,0,1,1), where only defection against good agents is bad, and any other action is good; Shunning (SH), d=(1,0,0,0), where it is only good to cooperate with good agents; and Stern-Judging (SJ) (), d=(1,0,0,1), where it is only good to cooperate with good agents and defect with bad agents. Importantly, this norm formulation accounts for continuous values, d∈[0,1]4, a detail that allows us to directly test average norms extracted from LLMs.

LLM norm adoption

In our model, LLMs always play the role of observers, and are accessible by all individuals. We consider the simplest scenario, where we assume any agent will consult an LLM and fully adopt its opinion. As such, if the LLM presents a social norm dL, a norm is extracted via the process described in Section LLM social norm assessment, the social norm used by the population will be d=dL.

Strategy dynamics

Having the average reputation at each strategy state, we next define how the population transitions between states. We employ a birth–death process using two replication mechanisms: mutations, where an agent adopts a random strategy with probability γ, and imitations, where an agent may adopt the strategy of another. Imitation is modeled using the pairwise comparison rule (), also known as the Fermi update rule: the probability that an agent using strategy s imitates another using strategy s′ is equal to Ps→s′(n)=(1+e−βΔFs,s′)−1, where ΔFs,s′(n)=F ̄s′(n)−F ̄s(n) is the difference between the average fitness of strategy s′ and strategy s, and β is the strength of selection. Strategies with higher fitness difference are more likely to be imitated. A higher strength of selection (β→∞) results in a deterministic evolutionary process, while lower values (β→0) tend toward a random selection process.

LLM social norm assessment

We next detail the assessment process of the social norm of an LLM. First, we outline the construction of the prompt dataset, followed by the method to process responses and aggregate them to define a social norm. Prompt dataset

The prompt dataset is constructed to assess what social norm an LLM is using. This is done by providing LLMs the context of a donation game interaction, and requesting them to output an assessment regarding the reputation of the donor.

As detailed in Section Cooperation model, we make use of second-order social norms, where the reputation of a donor depends on its action (C or D) and on the reputation of the receiver (G or B). As such, each prompt first presents the donor and receiver to the LLM, together with the prior reputation of the receiver, followed by a description of their interaction and the action chosen by the donor. Finally, the LLM is instructed to provide its new reputation, answering exclusively “good” or “bad.”

To ensure variety in the dataset, a template system was used with 5 template prompts, presenting the structure detailed above but varying in phrasing, containing fields (e.g. the donor’s action) to be filled. Various possible elements were then defined, containing actor names that suggest different genders and cultures to be used for the donor and receiver, the available actions for the donor, as well as different contexts for interactions, such as the donor asking for money or food. Importantly, we assume that gender and cultural backgrounds can be conveyed through the first names of the actors. With two names per gender per world region (four regions), for both the donor and receiver (which cannot be equal), two possible actions for the donor, two possible reputations for the recipient, nine contexts of interaction, and five variations in prompt template, a total of ((2×2×4)2−(2×2×4))×2×2×9×5=43,200 unique prompts were generated, resulting in 10,800 prompts per social norm entry.

Following work on prompting techniques to promote cooperation when using LLMs (), we also study 4 types of prompt interventions by adding specific instructions to the prompt, resulting in 4 additional datasets: “Universalization” ()—which suggests to the LLM to consider what would happen to cooperation if everyone assigned reputations like it did; “Empathizing” ()—which asks the LLM to consider what it would have done if it was in the same situation as the donor; “Signaling ()—which instructs the LLM to consider if the opinion it assigns clearly rewards cooperative behaviors and discourages non-cooperative behaviors; and “Motivation” ()—which prompts the LLM to consider that the assigned reputation can potentially affect the choices of others to help, and that its goal is to maximize cooperation. The full details of all the datasets are available in the .

Formally, we define D′ to be the dataset composed of all the prompts detailed above, for a given dataset variation. From there, a subset of the dataset is defined as Df=f(D′), where f is a filtering function that returns a subset of the dataset that matches some given criteria (e.g. the donor’s action). Our final set of datasets, T is composed of D′ as well as subsets corresponding to different pairs of donor and receiver genders, name regions, and contexts of interaction. Finally, we define DX,Y as the set of prompts in a dataset D where the donor executes action Y∈{C,D}, and the receiver has a previous reputation X∈{G,B}.

Norm aggregation

After prompting an LLM on the prompt dataset, we next parse each of its answers. Although our prompts present formatting instructions, not all models adhere to the required format, potentially presenting alternative answers, such as “neutral,” or appending justifications after the answer. For each response, a reputation value o∈[0,1] is assigned depending on the content of the reply, with 1 and 0 corresponding to assigning the donor a good and bad reputation, respectively.

We parse solely the first paragraph with content of each response, omitting any discussion or justification by the model. If the answer contains “good” and not “bad,” 1 is assigned. If it contains exclusively “bad,” 0 is assigned. An additional rule is used to account for formatting errors: answers with “neutral” assign 0.5. Any other answer is considered invalid, and the total invalid answer rate is recorded (presented in the ).
