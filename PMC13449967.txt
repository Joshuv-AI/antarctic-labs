Protocol for high-resolution mapping of RNA-associated chromatin using chromatin isolation by RNA purification-tag
Authors: Ruishuang Fu, Rongjie Zhao, Huimin Yang, Yujun Xia, Dewen Xu, Aiqin Shi, Huacheng Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMapping the genomic binding sites of long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) is essential for understanding their regulatory functions. Here, we present a protocol for integrating RNA hybridization with in situ tagmentation using chromatin isolation by RNA purification-tag (ChIRP-Tag). We describe steps for performing cell permeabilization, hybridization of biotinylated probes to target RNAs, and recruiting Protein A/G-Tn5 transposase via antibodies to catalyze in situ library construction. This protocol captures RNA-associated chromatin with high sensitivity and spatial resolution across various cell types.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Fu et al.1

Before you begin

Long non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) regulate gene expression by acting as scaffolds or guides to modulate chromatin accessibility and transcriptional activity.2 While traditional ChIRP-seq (Chromatin Isolation by RNA Purification) is a powerful tool for mapping lncRNA-chromatin interactions,3,4 it faces three major hurdles: ① High input requirement: Typically requiring 107 to 108 cells, making it unsuitable for rare or clinical samples; ② High background noise: Non-specific probe hybridization necessitates a complex “split-pool” strategy; ③ RNA integrity loss: The sonication required to shear chromatin often degrades the target lncRNA, reducing retrieval efficiency.

To overcome these limitations, we developed ChIRP-Tag (also called ChIRP–CUT&Tag), which leverages Tn5-mediated tagmentation (similar to CUT&Tag5) instead of sonication. This critical modification significantly reduces the required cell number input, minimizes RNA degradation, and streamlines library preparation. The following preparatory steps must be completed before initiating the main experimental workflow.

Innovation

To our knowledge, this protocol differs from previously described methods, including the original ChIRP protocol reported by Chu et al.4 Our ChIRP-Tag protocol integrates the specificity of antisense hybridization with the sensitivity of in-situ Tn5 tagmentation. By replacing traditional sonication and physical pull-downs with an antibody-mediated “spatial cascade positioning” strategy, this method preserves fragile RNA-chromatin complexes within intact nuclei. In the workflow, biotinylated probes precisely recruit the Protein A/G-Tn5 transposase via a primary-secondary antibody bridge, triggering simultaneous DNA cleavage and adapter ligation directly at lncRNA binding sites.

We systematically refined this protocol to maximize signal-to-noise ratios through fine-tuned permeabilization conditions and stringent hybridization-washing steps that balance nuclear access with structural integrity. The resulting workflow is highly efficient, requiring 10- to 100-fold fewer cells than conventional methods while significantly reducing background noise. This streamlined approach not only facilitates the mapping of high-abundance lncRNAs but also unlocks the potential to interrogate low-abundance transcripts and dynamic RNA-chromatin regulatory networks in scarce clinical samples or rare cell populations.

Materials and equipment

Probe design

Probe design is vital for ChIRP-Tag success. Probes are cornerstone of specificity and sensitivity in the entire protocol. They hybridize to the target lncRNA sequence amid a vast background of other cellular RNAs (rRNA, mRNA) and genomic DNA, serving as the docking site for the antibody-Tn5 complex by carrying a specific modification (e.g., biotin or digoxigenin). Moreover, lncRNAs are not linear but fold into complex secondary and tertiary structures and are coated with proteins. Therefore, we design multiple probes (usually 20 per lncRNA) covering the entire length of the transcript. This strategy ensures that even if some binding sites are blocked by protein complexes or RNA folding (steric hindrance), at least a subset of probes will successfully hybridize.

Antibodies

The antibody system in ChIRP-Tag bridges the oligonucleotide probes with the Tn5 transposase, which is fundamental for signal acquisition. The primary antibody provides specificity by recognizing the hapten label (e.g., biotin or digoxigenin) on hybridized probes, effectively linking the nucleic acid bait to the protein complex. The secondary antibody is then used to amplify this interaction; by binding to the primary antibody, it increases the recruitment of protein A/G-Tn5 transposomes, a step that is critical for the sensitive detection of low-abundance lncRNAs.

Next-generation sequencing

The protocol described here produces libraries compatible with Illumina sequencing platforms, though it can be adapted to generate libraries for MGI platforms with appropriate adapters. We typically use the NovaSeq 6000 System, sequencing in paired-end (PE100) mode. Paired-end sequencing is essential here to preserve the size information of the Tn5-tagmented DNA. Regarding sequencing depth, we recommend a minimum of 20 million usable reads for each probe pool. For lower-abundance lncRNAs, deeper sequencing (up to 30-40 million reads per pool) may be required to obtain a robust signal-to-noise ratio.

A successful ChIRP-Tag assay generates high-complexity sequencing libraries that enable genome-wide mapping of lncRNA-chromatin interactions with high sensitivity and specificity. When visualized in a genome browser, the data typically display distinct peaks at genomic loci where the target lncRNA is bound, often corresponding to regulatory regions such as promoters, enhancers, or other functional elements.

ChIRP-Tag is particularly effective for detecting interactions involving low-abundance lncRNAs, which are often challenging to profile using conventional methods. The approach enables the identification of both cis- and trans-acting LncRNA binding sites, providing insights into the spatial organization of chromatin and the regulatory roles of noncoding RNAs. The high signal-to-noise ratio achieved by this method facilitates the discovery of subtle or transient interactions that may be missed by other techniques.

Overall, ChIRP-Tag offers a powerful and straightforward strategy for profiling lncRNA-chromatin associations across the genome. The resulting data can be used to generate metagene plots, heatmaps, and other visualizations that reveal the distribution and functional relevance of lncRNA binding sites, supporting downstream analyses of gene regulation, epigenetic modification, and chromatin architecture.

Limitations

While ChIRP-Tag is highly efficient, several limitations should be considered. First, assay sensitivity depends entirely on probe design and specificity; off-target hybridization can lead to background noise. Therefore, careful probe design and the split-pool strategy are essential to distinguish true binding sites from false positives. Second, crosslinking and digitonin permeabilization conditions may require optimization for different cell types to balance RNA accessibility with chromatin stability. Over-crosslinking can inhibit Tn5 transposase activity, while under-crosslinking may fail to capture transient RNA-chromatin interactions. Third, low-abundance lncRNAs may still require higher cell input (e.g., >1×106 cells) or deeper sequencing (30-40 million reads per pool) to achieve robust signal-to-noise ratios. Fourth, ChIRP-Tag is not suitable for RNAs that are highly unstable (short half-life) or completely buried within compact, inaccessible chromatin, as these conditions prevent effective probe hybridization. Finally, successful execution requires Protein A/G-Tn5 fusion protein and strict temperature control during hybridization (37°C) and tagmentation (37°C), as deviations can drastically reduce efficiency.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

High non-specific binding to the lncRNA-specific probes and/or lacZ negative control probes.

Potential solution

High background can arise from either non-specific RNA probe binding or non-specific Tn5 recruitment. To address this, optimize the washing steps at two critical stages:•Post-hybridization washes (Steps 13–15): Increase the number and/or duration of the washes after RNA probe hybridization (before ConA bead binding). The salt and/or detergent concentration can also be increased in the wash buffers to improve stringency.•Post-ConA bead binding washes (Steps 24–26): Increase the number and/or duration of the washes after the cells are bound to ConA magnetic beads, specifically following the secondary antibody and pA/G-Tn5 transposase incubations, to remove unbound pA/G-Tn5 transposase.

Problem 2

Poor enrichment of the lncRNA of interest.

Potential solution

Poor enrichment may be due to several factors, including low expression of the target lncRNA in the chosen cell type or tissue. Increasing the amount of starting material for each pull-down (e.g., to ≥1×106 cells) and/or selecting a cell line or tissue type with higher lncRNA expression can improve results. Sample degradation can also reduce enrichment. Degradation is inevitable, especially when using RNase-rich samples, but can be minimized by working quickly, keeping buffers and samples on ice when required, and using appropriate RNase and protease inhibitors. Quantify RNA input after extraction using a Nanodrop to determine the amount of material for RT-qPCR. Taking sample “input” aliquots at different time points during the procedure can help identify when most degradation occurs. Inefficient probe hybridization can also reduce pull-down efficiency. Exclude regions with high secondary structure or protein binding when designing probes, as probe binding to these regions may block interactions between the lncRNA and other molecules. In step 12 (hybridization), the supernatant representing the “unbound” fraction can be collected and analyzed by RT-qPCR to assess RNA pull-down efficiency.
