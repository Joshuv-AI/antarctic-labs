DDIapp—A Web‐Based Application for Static Drug–Drug Interaction Assessment
Authors: Hirotaka Watase, Sylvie Klieber, Yorgos M. Psarellis, Nikhil Pillai, Panteleimon D. Mavroudis, Saroj Dhakal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn this work we describe the development of a web‐based application for static drug–drug interaction (DDI) risk assessment in accordance with the International Council for Harmonization (ICH) M12 guidance. The app was built using the Shiny for Python framework and it employs a modular mathematical modeling structure that incorporates models for the assessment of enzyme inhibition, enzyme induction, and transporter inhibition at intestinal, hepatic, and renal levels. In addition, a “net effect” model estimates the combined impact of inhibition and induction on victim drug exposure, expressed as the area under the curve ratio (AUCR). Matrix‐specific approaches for estimating unbound fractions in microsomes and hepatocytes (f u,mic and f u,hep), as indicated in regulatory alignment, are implemented. The application provides a dynamic interface that supports flexible parameter input, real‐time evaluation across multiple DDI scenarios, and automated risk categorization using predefined thresholds based on the ICH M12 guidelines, with visual cues to aid risk interpretation. Additional features include integrated equation display for transparency and interpretation, export capabilities to PDF and Excel formats and a glossary with links to guidance documents and resources from major regulatory authorities (FDA, EMA, PMDA and NMPA). This DDIapp is validated against Certara's drug–drug interaction calculator under ICH M12 evaluation conditions. DDIapp offers a user‐friendly platform for assessing the risk of pharmacokinetic drug interactions as a perpetrator involving metabolic enzymes and transporters, supporting both scientific research and regulatory decision‐making.

Introduction

Drug–drug interactions (DDIs) occur when the pharmacological effect of one drug is altered by the presence of another, potentially leading to reduced therapeutic benefit, enhanced toxicity, or unexpected adverse reactions [1, 2, 3]. In general, DDIs are classified into two main categories: Pharmacokinetic interactions, which affect drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, or excretion (ADME). For example, inhibition of cytochrome P450 enzymes can lead to elevated plasma levels of co‐administered drugs that are metabolized by the same enzyme, increasing the risk of toxicity. A well‐known example is Amiodarone (CYP3A4 inhibitor), leading to increased Simvastatin (CYP3A4 substrate) levels and a higher risk of myopathy or rhabdomyolysis when the two drugs are taken together. To reduce this risk, the Simvastatin dose should be limited to 20 mg/day or less when used together with Amiodarone [4, 5].Pharmacodynamic interactions, which alter the drug's effect at its target site, potentially leading to synergistic or antagonistic effects or unexpected clinical outcomes.

DDIs are a major concern in modern pharmacotherapy, as they can significantly impact both the efficacy and safety of therapeutic regimens. With the increasing prevalence of polypharmacy, particularly among elderly patients and those with chronic conditions, the risk of DDIs is growing [6, 7, 8]. Studies have indicated that DDIs contribute to a substantial proportion of hospital admissions and healthcare costs [9, 10]. Therefore, understanding and managing DDIs is essential for optimizing patient care, which involves the following approaches: Careful review of drug profiles during prescribingUse of clinical decision support tools and up‐to‐date interaction databases (e.g., Micromedex [11], Lexicomp [12]).Implementation of preventive strategies, such as dose adjustments, therapeutic monitoring, and alternative drug selection.

Regulatory agencies have issued comprehensive guidelines for evaluating DDIs during drug development, emphasizing the importance of in vitro and in vivo studies, as well as predictive modeling. The static modeling approach, which relates in vivo inhibitor concentrations to in vitro inhibition constants (K i) to assess DDI risk, has been established as a practical framework for prioritizing clinical DDI studies and classifying inhibitors as weak, moderate, or strong based on their impact on clinical probe substrate pharmacokinetics [13]. In 2024, The International Council for Harmonization of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH) M12 guidelines provided a standardized framework for the evaluation of DDIs [14]. These guidelines promote consistency in the design, conduct, and interpretation of enzyme‐ or transporter‐mediated interaction studies, which are crucial for assessing the potential impact of DDIs on drug metabolism and transport. The guidelines are endorsed by major regulatory agencies, including the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [15], the European Medicines Agency (EMA) [16], the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) in Japan [17], and the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) in China [18].

In this work, we present a web‐based application designed to evaluate DDIs in accordance with the ICH M12 guidelines. This application focuses on assessing a drug's impact (the drug is studied as a precipitant) on the pharmacokinetics of other drugs (object or victim drug), specifically addressing metabolic‐mediated interactions, enzyme inhibition or induction, as well as transporter inhibition. The evaluation is conducted using a modeling approach based on pre‐systemic intestinal (I gut) and maximal systemic concentration in the plasma (C max) as input exposure parameters, rather than time‐course simulations. It features a user‐friendly interface and covers various aspects of DDI assessment, including enzyme inhibition (competitive and irreversible) at intestinal and hepatic level, enzyme induction, net effect calculation (as a ratio of the area under the concentration versus time curve [AUC] of the object drug in the presence and absence of the precipitant drug [AUCR]), and transporter inhibition. All the equations and thresholds referenced in this paper and implemented in the application are based on the ICH M12 guidelines. The application also allows for the creation of summary tables and the export of data to Excel and PDF files, facilitating the organization and dissemination of findings. By using this application, researchers can work more efficiently, improve communication both within their teams and in regulatory contexts, streamline the DDI evaluation process, reduce the potential for errors, and provide a standardized approach to DDI assessment. Furthermore, this is the first open‐source and free DDI risk calculator that accounts for the ICH M12 guidelines. The availability of the Python‐based code on GitHub ensures transparency and encourages collaboration among researchers, fostering innovation and continuous improvement in the field of DDI evaluation.

Application Description

DDIapp is built on a Shiny for Python dashboard framework, featuring a sidebar panel and conditional pages [19]. The application implements both basic models for enzyme inhibition (competitive and time‐dependent), enzyme induction, and transporter inhibition, as well as a mechanistic static net effect model that integrates the combined impact of inhibition and induction on victim drug exposure, in accordance with ICH M12 guidelines. As shown in Figure 1, the top section displays parameters that apply to all DDI evaluations, while the sidebar provides navigation to individual evaluation modules, as well as summary and glossary pages. Each of these components is described in greater detail in the following sections.

DDIapp overview: The top section presents common parameters (compound name, molecular weight, C max, f u,p, dose, k a, F a, F g, Q h, and Rb.) applicable across all DDI evaluations, whereas the sidebar organizes the individual evaluation modules in addition to the summary and glossary pages.

Following this layout, users initiate their analysis by entering shared parameters applicable to all DDI evaluations, such as compound's name, molecular weight (MW, g/mol), maximal systemic concentration in plasma (C max, μmol/L; or convert from ng/mL using C max [μmol/L] = C max [ng/mL]/MW), unbound fraction in plasma (f u,p; input 0.01 if f u,p < 0.01), dose (mg), rate constant of absorption in humans (k a,/h; if unknown, use k a = 6/h as a worst‐case scenario), fraction of dose absorbed at the gut level (F a, set at 1 if unknown), fraction of victim dose escaping metabolism within the walls of the gastrointestinal tract (F g, intestinal wall availability, set at 1 if unknown), hepatic blood flow (Q h, L/h; set at 97 L/h as default), and blood‐to‐plasma ratio (Rb; if unknown, R b = 1 for neutrals/bases or Rb = 0.55 for acids/zwitterions) [20]. These values are used throughout the following sections and all concentration parameters (C max, K i, K I, IC 50, EC 50) are expressed in molar units (μmol/L) throughout this manuscript to ensure unit consistency across all calculations. Users can also enter text in the user memo cell for notes or reminders, which will be exported as‐is to both Excel and PDF output files.

Enzyme Inhibition

Enzyme inhibition can be classified into reversible (competitive) and irreversible (time‐dependent) inhibition, occurring both hepatically and intestinally. This application implements competitive inhibition for hepatic enzymes and intestinal CYP3A4, and time‐dependent inhibition for hepatic CYP enzymes. Each panel is presented in a collapsed state by default, and expands when clicked. Users can select an unbound fraction in the microsomes (f u,mic), which is used to convert dissociation constant of the enzyme‐substrate complex (K i, inhibition constant), the concentration of the drug that produces one‐half of maximal rate of enzyme inactivation (K I) and concentration of inhibitor producing 50% of the maximum inhibition (IC 50) values into corresponding unbound parameters. f u,mic as input parameter can be experimentally determined (i.e., experimental value) or calculated with either Austin [21] or Hallifax [22] methods. If users select methods of Austin or Hallifax, they need to input the logarithm of the partition coefficient between octanol and water for the neutral form of a molecule (LogP), or the distribution coefficient at a specific pH (LogD) and microsomal protein concentration (g/L) instead of the f u,mic experimental value directly. For LogP or LogD selection: use LogP when the acid dissociation constant (pKa) of the compound's is greater than 7.4, otherwise use LogD at pH 7.4. In addition to the common f u,mic parameter described above, users can optionally specify enzyme‐specific unbound fraction in the assay system (f u,inc) values for individual enzymes. When an enzyme‐specific f u,inc is provided, it will override the common f u,mic value for the particular enzyme's calculation, allowing for more refined assessments when experimental data are available for specific enzyme systems. Either an inputted f u,mic or a calculated f u,mic appears in the app (with enzyme‐specific f u,inc values taking precedence when provided) and is used for subsequent calculations as shown in Figure 2a.

Competitive Enzyme Inhibition

The basic static model for competitive enzyme inhibition relates the in vivo inhibitor concentration to the in vitro inhibition constant (K i) to assess DDI risk [23]. By clicking “Competitive inhibition” (Figure 2a), the enzymes to evaluate DDI appear in the panel. These are Cytochrome P450 (CYP) 1A2, CYP2B6, CYP2C8, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, CYP3A4, Uridine‐diphosphate‐Glucuronosyl‐Transferase (UGT) 1A1, UGT1A4, UGT1A9, UGT2B7 and UGT2B15. For CYP3A4, both hepatic and intestinal evaluations are automatically displayed. Users can add custom enzymes by clicking the “Add custom enzyme” button, enabling evaluation of inhibition for any enzyme of interest. For calculation of the effect, probe substrates corresponding to each enzyme are shown in the interface. Users select enzymes and then input K i at the input cell where it appears by checking each enzyme as shown in Figure 2b. Alternatively, users can input IC 50 values along with substrate concentration ([S]) and K m values, and the application automatically calculates K i using the Cheng‐Prusoff equation (K i = IC 50/(1 + [S]/K m)), assuming competitive inhibition [24]. Once inputting values is completed, then users can click “Calculate” button. By doing so, results appear with attribute, enzyme, substrate, risk (as R value), alert (displayed when the R value exceeds the criteria specified in the M12 guidelines), note as represent input values as shown in Figure 2c. R values are calculated as follows: (1) Rhepatic=Cmax×fu,pKi×fu,mic (2) Rintestinal=Dose×1000000MW×250×Ki×fu,mic These equations are shown in the panel for users to confirm how the R values are calculated. If the R value exceeds 0.02 for R hepatic and 10 for R intestinal, the number appears in red bold with an alert message [identical to K i,u > 50 × C max,u (i.e., Cmax,uKi,u<0.02) and Ki,u>0.1×maximum clinical dose250mL (i.e., Dose250mLKi,u<10)inICHM12].

Irreversible CYP Inhibition (Hepatic)

The time‐dependent inhibition model accounts for both the inactivation kinetics (K I, K inact) and the endogenous degradation rate constant of an enzyme in the liver (K deg,h) [25]. For this evaluation, combination of enzymes and probe substrates are implemented, namely phenacetin for CYP1A2, bupropion for CYP2B6, amodiaquine for CYP2C8, diclofenac for CYP2C9, S‐mephenytoin for CYP2C19, dextromethorphan for CYP2D6 and midazolam and testosterone for CYP3A. For either of the combinations, users select enzymes and then input maximal rate of enzyme inactivation (K inact), K I and endogenous degradation rate constant of an enzyme in the liver (K deg,h). For Kdeg,h, a typical value for each CYP isoform is set as the default according to the ICH M12 guidelines, with unit conversion from/min to/h (i.e., multiplied by 60) [14, 15, 16, 17] while users can change the values if needed.

Enzyme Induction

DDIapp implements the basic static model for enzyme induction as described in ICH M12 guidelines [14]. This model is based on established theoretical frameworks for predicting clinical drug–drug interactions from in vitro induction data [26, 27]. To evaluate enzyme induction, users first input a d factor value, which is an empirical calibration factor to enable in vitro‐to‐in vivo induction scaling. If the d factor is not estimated, a default value of 1 is assumed. Users can select an unbound fraction in hepatocytes (f u,hep) used to convert concentration of inducer producing 50% of the maximum effect (EC 50) values from induction assays into corresponding unbound parameters, as input (i.e., experimental value), methods of Austin [28] or Kilfold [29], respectively as shown in Figure 3a. Similar to enzyme inhibition, users can optionally specify enzyme‐specific f u,inc values for individual enzymes. When provided, an enzyme‐specific f u,inc will take precedence over the common f u,hep value for that enzyme's induction assessment, enabling more accurate predictions when enzyme‐specific unbound fraction data are available.

Transporter Inhibition

Membrane transporters can be major determinants of the pharmacokinetic, safety, and efficacy profiles of drugs [30]. The International Transporter Consortium has established frameworks for evaluating transporter‐mediated drug–drug interactions, including methodologies for in vitro assessment and criteria for triggering clinical studies [30].

Users first input a common f u,inc. While a common f u,inc is used across all transporter evaluations by default, users can also define transporter‐specific f u,inc values for individual transporters. When a transporter‐specific f u,inc is entered, it will override the common f u,inc for that transporter's calculation. The transporter inhibition panel is organized into four collapsible sections—intestinal efflux, hepatic uptake, renal uptake, and renal efflux as shown in Figure 4a. For each section, users can select transporters and input IC 50 or K i values in μmol/L as shown in Figure 4b. Once users click the ‘Calculate’ button, results appear like those of enzyme inhibition and enzyme induction as shown in Figure 4c.

Net Effect

The net effect model implements the mechanistic static model as described in ICH M12 Section 7.5.1.2 [31, 32], accounting for drug–drug interactions at both hepatic and intestinal levels. Users first look at a Q en input box, where the default value is set to 18 L/h according to the ICH M12 guidelines. Users can then select enzymes and clinical substrates (victim drugs) for evaluation. For each selected clinical substrate, users can input both the fraction of hepatic metabolic clearance mediated by enzyme of interest (f m) and the fraction of the dose escaping intestinal metabolism (F g) specific to that clinical substrate. The default f m and F g values for each enzyme were assigned based on literature‐informed and biologically plausible assumptions [33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41]. It should be noted that explicit f m values are rarely reported in the literature for most clinical substrates; instead, the available information typically indicates the primary metabolic pathway (e.g., “predominantly metabolized by CYP3A4”), from which f m values are commonly inferred to be in the range of 0.8–0.9. The default values provided in DDIapp represent reasonable estimates based on such literature information, but users are encouraged to review and adjust these values based on compound‐specific data when available. For time‐dependent (irreversible) inhibition at the intestinal level, users can optionally specify a K deg,g value (endogenous degradation rate constant of an enzyme in the intestine) that differs from the K deg,h value set in the irreversible hepatic CYP inhibition panel. When K deg,g is not specified, the same value as K deg,h is used for intestinal calculations, assuming similar enzyme degradation kinetics between hepatic and intestinal compartments.

Once users click the ‘Calculate’ button, three lines of results appear: the total net effect (AUCR), the net effect with induction only, and the net effect with inhibition only as shown in Figure 5b. If the AUCR value is greater than 1.25 or smaller than 0.80, the values become red, and alerts appear.

Summary Tables and Outputs

Summary tables are generated when users click the “Create Summary Tables” button as shown in Figure 6a. Separate tables are shown for Enzyme Inhibition, Enzyme Induction, Transporter Inhibition, and Net effect, respectively as shown in Figure 6b. If there is no applicable data, no table appears in the section. The “Attribute” column shows attribute information of each evaluation such as “Competitive enzyme inhibition”. Next to the “Attribute” column, an enzyme or a transporter for which the evaluation is intended appears (“Enzyme/Transporter”). For “Enzyme inhibition” and “Net effect” tables, applicable probe substrates or clinical substrates appear (“Probe/Clinical Substrate”). Next to the “Probe/Clinical Substrate” column, there are “Risk (R)” and “Alert” columns. The R values calculated based on the ICH M12 evaluation appear. If the R value exceeds the criteria specified in ICH M12 (e.g., R > 0.02 for competitive enzyme inhibition in the liver), the font becomes bold and red in color, and caution appears in the “Alert” column. Input values and/or some intermediate values calculated for the evaluation appear in the “Note” column. Then, the created tables are exported as either an excel or a pdf file by clicking either “Download excel file (Click after creating summary table)” or “Download PDF file (Click after creating summary table)”, respectively. As indicated in the buttons, users need to create summary tables before exporting an excel or a pdf file.

Summary

DDIapp is a validated, web‐based platform designed to streamline static drug–drug interaction (DDI) risk assessment in accordance with the International Council for Harmonization (ICH) M12 guideline. Developed using the Shiny for Python framework, the application features modular components for reversible enzyme inhibition, time‐dependent (irreversible) inhibition, enzyme induction, integrated “net effect” modeling for enzyme inhibition and enzyme induction, and transporter inhibition. The dynamic interface enables flexible parameterization, method‐specific estimation of unbound fractions (f u,mic and f u,hep), and transparent equation display to ensure regulatory compliance and interpretability. Automated alerts, color‐coded outputs, and consolidated summary tables allow rapid identification of potential risks, while export options (Excel/PDF) support efficient reporting. A glossary linked to regulatory resources and explicit calculation steps further enhance transparency and provide educational value for researchers and trainees. In addition, validation against industry‐standard software and independent internal testing confirms the robustness of the platform.

To maximize flexibility, users can add or remove enzymes and transporters. Each evaluation panel displays relevant formulas alongside input‐dependent values, ensuring clarity in calculations. For example, dosages entered in milligrams and/or for C max entered in ng/mL are automatically converted for calculations thereafter. Intermediate steps (e.g., A h, B h) in net effect calculations are shown in the “Note” column of the results table to reinforce transparency. Risk visibility is emphasized through systematic color coding and alerts. For enzyme and transporter inhibition, values exceeding guideline‐defined cutoffs appear in bold red with alerts. Color indicators are preserved in Excel exports but not in PDFs due to technical reasons; therefore, an alert column ensures clarity across all formats.

The use of Shiny for Python offers significant value in terms of flexibility and adaptability. With its open‐source framework and publicly available code, DDIapp can be customized to meet specific individual/organizational needs. Users can modify input/output formats, adjust the user interface, incorporate additional enzymes or transporters, and tailor output to specific workflows. Importantly, this adaptability will allow the tool to evolve alongside any scientific and regulatory changes without requiring a complete rebuild, making it a sustainable solution for long‐term use.

In summary, this application aims to make DDI risk assessment simpler and more understandable for researchers, improving workflow efficiency and supporting informed decision‐making in both scientific and regulatory contexts. By addressing the growing need for standardized, user‐friendly tools in modern drug development, DDIapp represents a practical solution for accelerating evaluation processes while promoting transparency and resource sharing within the scientific community.
