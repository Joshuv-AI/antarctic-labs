Minimizing Total Biharmonic Distance in Large Graphs via Link Recommendation CCS: Theory of computation Graph algorithms analysis
Xinna Zhou, Zhongzhi Zhang

Abstract
. The total biharmonic distance, which is the sum of the biharmonic distance between every pair of nodes in a network, is a key metric for evaluating network connectivity and robustness. In this paper, we study the problem of minimizing the total biharmonic distance by adding k k nonexistent edges for a given graph G G and budget k k . The problem is computationally challenging. We show that the objective function of the problem is monotone but not supermodular. To solve this problem, we propose simple greedy algorithms with cubic time complexity. To mitigate the high time complexity of these greedy algorithms, we apply several techniques, including the projection method, the Laplacian solver, and convex hull approximation. These techniques reduce the time complexity of our proposed algorithms from cubic to nearly linear while providing error guarantees. Finally, extensive experiments on real datasets demonstrate both the efficiency and effectiveness of our proposed algorithms. This paper has been published in Proceedings of the 32nd ACM SIGKDD Conference on Knowledge Discovery and Data Mining V.1 (KDD ’26). DOI: https://doi.org/10.1145/3770854.3780204 .



1.
Introduction

Graph distance metrics play a crucial role in the realm of network analysis. Notable distance metrics include geodesic distance
(
Newman 2018
)
and resistance distance
(
Klein and Randić 1993
)
. Resistance distance originates from electrical network theory and has been widely utilized in clustering
(
Shi and Malik 2000
)
, graph learning
(
Kreuzer et al. 2021
;
Black et al. 2023
;
Zhu et al. 2003
)
, and collaborative recommendation systems
(
Fouss et al. 2007
;
Kunegis and Schmidt 2007
)
. Additionally, it has been applied to ranking problems in large graphs
(
Sarkar et al. 2008
)
, detecting anomalous changes in graph structure over time
(
Sricharan and Das 2014
)
, assessing node significance
(
Lu et al. 2024
)
, and enhancing the efficiency of graph systems
(
Qiu et al. 2021
)
. Over the past few decades, considerable efforts have been dedicated to further understanding and exploring the properties of resistance distance
(
Dörfler and Bullo 2013
;
Dörfler et al. 2018
;
Thulasiraman et al. 2018
)
.

Resistance distance has several variants
(
Verma and Zhang 2017
)
, among which biharmonic distance
(
Lipman et al. 2010
)
stands out. It was introduced to capture more nuanced local and global structural information, offering advantages over resistance distance in many applications, such as maintaining graph connectivity and identifying critical cut edges
(
Black et al. 2024
)
. Biharmonic distance has been applied across various domains, including graph clustering
(
Black et al. 2024
)
, graph embedding techniques
(
Kreuzer et al. 2021
;
Black et al. 2023
)
, graph matching
(
Fan et al. 2020
)
, network coherence
(
Yi et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2020
)
, leader selection in noisy networks
(
Fitch and Leonard 2015
)
, identifying crucial edges
(
Yi et al. 2018a
)
, and other network science problems
(
Bamieh et al. 2012
;
Tyloo et al. 2019
)
.

Beyond individual metrics, the aggregate of a metric in a graph is often used to evaluate the overall network. For instance, the Kirchhoff index sums the resistance distances between all node pairs
(
Klein and Randić 1993
;
Ghosh et al. 2008
)
. Similarly, the total biharmonic distance, or biharmonic index
(
Yi et al. 2021
)
, is the sum of biharmonic distances across all node pairs in the graph. These aggregate metrics serve as powerful tools for evaluating various properties of a network and have attracted significant attention. The Kirchhoff index is key for measuring network connectedness
(
Tizghadam and Leon-Garcia 2010
)
and evaluating the robustness of consensus algorithms
(
Qi et al. 2018
)
. The total biharmonic distance can be expressed by the Kirchhoff index and the resistance distances between some node pairs
(
Tyloo et al. 2018
)
.
The total biharmonic distance has many application scenarios. It can effectively evaluate the robustness of second-order consensus algorithms in distributed network systems
(
Tyloo et al. 2018
;
Xu et al. 2022
)
and identify local vulnerabilities
(
Tyloo et al. 2019
)
. For example, in power grids and complex oscillator networks, total biharmonic distance can measure the fault tolerance of the system
(
Tyloo et al. 2019
)
. In communication networks, when lines are cut and information must be redirected, total biharmonic distance can evaluate a network’s vulnerability to changes
(
Tyloo et al. 2018
)
. In addition, it is an important tool for evaluating network connectivity
(
Black et al. 2024
)
and can also be used to define edge importance metrics
(
Siami and Motee 2017
)
.

Networks with smaller total biharmonic distance are known to exhibit enhanced robustness and stronger connectivity, leading to improved overall system performance
(
Bamieh et al. 2012
;
Yi et al. 2021
)
. Therefore, optimizing total biharmonic distance through different graph operations becomes essential. Motivated by practical scenarios such as link recommendation in online social networks, we study the problem of optimizing (minimizing) total biharmonic distance by adding nonexistent edges (links) to a graph. More precisely, we aim to devise effective and efficient algorithms for the following optimization problem: For a given connected undirected unweighted graph
G=(V,E)
with
n
nodes and
m
edges, a small positive integer
k
, how to add
k
nonexistent edges from a candidate edge set
Q=(V\times V)\backslash E
to graph
G
, so that the total biharmonic distance for the resulting graph is minimized.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized below.

\bullet
We show that the total biharmonic distance is monotonically decreasing but not supermodular. We then propose a simple greedy algorithm with cubic time complexity, providing an approximation guarantee based on the curvature and submodularity ratio of the objective function. We also present a gradient-based greedy algorithm as a novel approach to address this problem.

\bullet
We employ several techniques, including random projection, the Laplacian solver, and convex hull approximation, resulting in a scalable and efficient algorithm
ApproxFast
. This algorithm is gradient-based and achieves a time complexity of
\tilde{O}(k(nl+m)/\epsilon^{2})
, where
\tilde{O}(\cdot)
hides poly
(\log(n))
factors,
\epsilon>0
is the error parameter that balances performance and efficiency, and
l
represents the number of nodes on the boundary of the approximate convex hull, which is typically small in most real-world networks.

\bullet
We evaluate our algorithms by performing experiments on many real-world networks of varying scales, showing that our proposed fast algorithm
ApproxFast
achieves both good efficiency and effectiveness and is scalable to large networks with over four million nodes.

2.2.
Graph and Related Matrices

Let
G=(V,E)
be a connected undirected unweighted graph with
V=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and
E=\{e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{m}\}
. The adjacency matrix
\bm{\mathit{A}}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
satisfies
\bm{\mathit{A}}_{i,j}=1
if nodes
i
and
j
are adjacent, and
0
otherwise. The degree matrix is
\bm{\mathit{D}}=\text{diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
, where
d_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bm{\mathit{A}}_{i,j}
, and the Laplacian matrix is
\bm{\mathit{L}}=\bm{\mathit{D}}-\bm{\mathit{A}}
. If we fix an arbitrary orientation for all edges in
G
, then we define the signed edge-vertex incidence matrix
\bm{\mathit{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, whose entries are:
\bm{\mathit{B}}_{e,u}=1
if node
u
is the head of edge
e
,
\bm{\mathit{B}}_{e,u}=-1
if node
u
is the tail of edge
e
, and
\bm{\mathit{B}}_{e,u}=0
otherwise.
\bm{\mathit{L}}
can also be represented by
\bm{\mathit{L}}=\bm{\mathit{B}}^{T}\bm{\mathit{B}}
. For any pair of distinct nodes
u,v\in V
, we define
\bm{b}_{uv}=\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v}
. If an edge
e
connects
u
and
v
and we fix an arbitrary orientation such that
e
is directed from
u
to
v
, we define
\bm{b}_{e}=\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v}
. Since
\bm{\mathit{L}}
is symmetric, it has eigenvalues
0=\lambda_{1}(\bm{\mathit{L}})<\lambda_{2}(\bm{\mathit{L}})\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}(\bm{\mathit{L}})
with corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors
\bm{\mathit{u}}_{1},\bm{\mathit{u}}_{2},\dots,\bm{\mathit{u}}_{n}
, and can be written as
\bm{\mathit{L}}=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\lambda_{i}\bm{\mathit{u}}_{i}\bm{\mathit{u}}_{i}^{T}
. Its Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse is
\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}=(\bm{\mathit{L}}+\frac{1}{n}\bm{\mathit{J}})^{-1}-\frac{1}{n}\bm{\mathit{J}}
. We have
\frac{1}{2n^{4}}\bm{\mathit{L}}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\preceq\bm{\mathit{L}}\preceq n\bm{\mathit{I}}
and
\frac{1}{n}\bm{\mathit{L}}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\preceq\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\preceq 2n^{4}\bm{\mathit{I}}

(
Chung 1997
)
. The eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenvectors of
\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2}
are
\lambda_{i}^{2}
and
\bm{u}_{i},i=1,2,\ldots,n.
Thus,
\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2}
has the following spectral decomposition:
\bm{\bm{\mathit{L}}}^{2}=\sum\nolimits_{i=2}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}(\bm{\mathit{L}})\bm{u}_{i}\bm{u}_{i}^{\top}
, which indicates that
\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2}
is positive semi-definite with only one zero eigenvalue. Its Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse
\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2{\dagger}}
is positive semi-definite with only one zero eigenvalue. Similarly,
\bm{\mathit{L}}^{3}
and
\bm{\mathit{L}}^{3{\dagger}}
are positive semi-definite with only one zero eigenvalue. For any edge set
T\subseteq(V\times V)\setminus E
, let
G_{T}=(V,E\cup T)
be the graph after adding edges in
T
.

2.3.
Biharmonic Distances and Related Metrics

Definition 2.1 (
(
Yi et al. 2018a
)
).

For a graph
G=(V,E)
, the biharmonic distance
\bm{b}(u,v)
between any pair of distinct nodes
u,v
is defined by

\displaystyle\bm{b}(u,v)=\sqrt{\bm{b}_{uv}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2{\dagger}}\bm{b}_{uv}}=\|\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}\bm{b}_{uv}\|=\sqrt{\bm{\mathit{L}}_{u,u}^{2{\dagger}}+\bm{\mathit{L}}_{v,v}^{2{\dagger}}-2\bm{\mathit{L}}_{u,v}^{2{\dagger}}}.

We denote the square of the biharmonic distance as
\bm{b}^{2}(u,v)
. For brevity, we will sometimes refer to
\bm{b}^{2}(u,v)
as the biharmonic distance, when the context makes it clear that we mean its square. Based on the biharmonic distance, we can derive the following graph index.

Definition 2.2 (
(
Yi et al. 2018b
)
).

For a graph
G=(V,E)
, the total biharmonic distance
B(G)
is defined by

B(G)=\frac{1}{2}\sum\nolimits_{u\in V}\sum\nolimits_{\nu\in V}\bm{b}^{2}(u,v)=n\sum\nolimits_{i=2}^{n}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}^{2}}=n\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2\dagger}}\right).

The biharmonic distance can be further generalized to higher-order variants
(
Siami and Motee 2017
)
by considering arbitrary powers of
\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}
.

Definition 2.3 (
(
Black et al. 2024
)
).

For a graph
G=(V,E)
, the
k
-harmonic distance
\bm{\mathit{h}}_{k}(u,v)
between any pair of distinct nodes
u,v
is defined by

\bm{\mathit{h}}_{k}(u,v)=\sqrt{\bm{b}_{uv}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{k{\dagger}}\bm{b}_{uv}}.

Similarly, we will refer to
\bm{\mathit{h}}_{k}^{2}(u,v)
as the
k
-harmonic distance, rather than the square of the
k
-harmonic distance, when the context is clear. Notably, when
k=1
,
\bm{\mathit{h}}_{k}^{2}(u,v)=R_{uv}
which is the resistance distance between nodes
u
and
v
. When
k=2
,
\bm{\mathit{h}}_{k}(u,v)=\bm{b}(u,v)
which is the biharmonic distance between nodes
u
and
v
. We also introduce the total
k
-harmonic distance.

Definition 2.4 (
(
Tyloo et al. 2019
)
).

For a graph
G=(V,E)
, the total
k
-harmonic distance
K(G)
is defined by

K(G)=\frac{1}{2}\sum\nolimits_{u\in V}\sum\nolimits_{\nu\in V}\bm{\mathit{h}}_{k}^{2}(u,v)=n\sum\nolimits_{i=2}^{n}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}^{k}}=n\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{\bm{\mathit{L}}^{k\dagger}}\right).

3.
Problem Formulation

In this section, we begin by examining the monotonicity of the total biharmonic distance. Next, we present the problem statement. We then analyze the properties of the objective function before introducing two simple greedy algorithms.

3.1.
Monotonicity of Total Biharmonic Distance

The monotonically decreasing property of the total biharmonic distance was shown in
(
Wei et al. 2021
)
. However, the method in
(
Wei et al. 2021
)
does not give the marginal decrease, which is important for our algorithm. We take a different approach by first deriving the marginal decrease in total biharmonic distance when an edge is added.

Lemma 3.1.

For a connected graph
G=(V,E)
, the marginal decrease of the total biharmonic distance after adding an edge
e
is

\Delta(e)=B(G)-B(G_{\{e\}})=-n\frac{(\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{2\dagger}\bm{b}_{e})^{2}}{(1+\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e})^{2}}+n\frac{2\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{3\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}}{1+\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}}.

Proof.
By Definition
2.2
,
\Delta(e)=n\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{L^{2\dagger}}\right)-n\mathrm{Tr}\left((\bm{L}+\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{\top})^{2\dagger}\right).
Using the Sherman-Morrison formula
(
Meyer 1973
)
, we have

(1)

(\bm{\mathit{L}}+\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{T})^{{\dagger}}=\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}-\frac{\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}}{1+\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}\bm{b}_{e}}.

Squaring both sides, we obtain

(2)

\displaystyle(\bm{\mathit{L}}+\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{T})^{2{\dagger}}=\left(\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}-\frac{\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}}{1+\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}\bm{b}_{e}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2{\dagger}}-\frac{\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2{\dagger}}\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}}{1+\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}\bm{b}_{e}}-\frac{\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2{\dagger}}}{1+\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}\bm{b}_{e}}+\frac{(\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}})^{2}}{(1+\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}\bm{b}_{e})^{2}}.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left((\bm{L}+\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{\top})^{2\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{L^{2\dagger}}\right)-\frac{2\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{L^{2\dagger}}\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\right)}{1+\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L^{2\dagger}}\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\right)}{(1+\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e})^{2}}.

Therefore, one can conclude that

(3)

\displaystyle\Delta(e)

\displaystyle=-n\frac{\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L^{2\dagger}}\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\right)}{(1+\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e})^{2}}+n\frac{2\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{L^{2\dagger}}\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\right)}{1+\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}}

\displaystyle=-n\frac{\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{2\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\right)}{(1+\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e})^{2}}+n\frac{2\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{3\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}\right)}{1+\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}}

\displaystyle=-n\frac{(\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{2\dagger}\bm{b}_{e})^{2}}{(1+\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e})^{2}}+n\frac{2\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{3\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}}{1+\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}}.

This finishes the proof.

\Box

Building on Lemma
3.1
, we now prove the monotonicity of total biharmonic distance in Lemma
3.2
.

Lemma 3.2.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a connected graph with
n
nodes and
e\in(V\times V)\backslash E
. Then

B(G_{\{e\}})<B(G)
, that is,
B(G)
is monotonically decreasing.

Proof.
Suppose
\bm{\mathit{u}}=\bm{\mathit{B}}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}
,
\bm{\mathit{v}}=\bm{\mathit{B}}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e},
thus

\bm{\mathit{u}}^{T}\bm{\mathit{u}}=\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2\dagger}\bm{\mathit{B}}^{T}\bm{\mathit{B}}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}=\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2\dagger}\bm{\mathit{L}}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}=\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{3\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}.

Likewise,
\bm{\mathit{v}}^{T}\bm{\mathit{v}}=\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}
,
\bm{\mathit{u}}^{T}\bm{\mathit{v}}=\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}.
By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
(
Steele 2004
)
, we have
(\bm{\mathit{u}}^{T}\bm{\mathit{v}})^{2}\leq(\bm{\mathit{u}}^{T}\bm{\mathit{u}})(\bm{\mathit{v}}^{T}\bm{\mathit{v}})
, implying

(\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2\dagger}\bm{b}_{e})^{2}\leq\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{3\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}.

According to Lemma
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta(e)

\displaystyle\geq-n\frac{\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{3\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}}{(1+\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e})^{2}}+n\frac{2\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{3\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}(1+\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e})}{(1+\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e})^{2}}

\displaystyle=n\frac{2\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{3\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}+\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{3\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}}{(1+\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e})^{2}}>0.

This establishes that
\Delta(e)>0
, thereby completing the proof.

\Box

This lemma indicates that adding any nonexistent edge to
G
decreases the total biharmonic distance. Since a smaller biharmonic distance indicates better performance, it is useful in applications like assessing the robustness of second-order consensus algorithms in noisy networks
(
Bamieh et al. 2012
;
Yi et al. 2021
)
. This motivates our exploration of minimizing the total biharmonic distance by adding
k
new edges.

3.2.
Problem Statement

We now give a mathematical formulation of our problem.

Problem 1.

Given a connected graph
G=(V,E)
, a candidate edge set
Q=(V\times V)\backslash E
and an integer
k\leq|Q|
, find a subset
T\subseteq Q
of
k
edges such that the total biharmonic distance of the graph
G^{*}=(V,E\cup T^{*})
is minimized. More formally, the objective is to find

T^{*}=\argmin_{T\subseteq Q,|T|=k}B(G_{T}).

Problem
1
is inherently a combinatorial problem, which is computationally challenging.

3.3.
Properties of Objective Function

We have already proved that the objective function of Problem
1

B(\cdot)
is monotone in Lemma
3.2
. Next, we show that function
B(\cdot)
is not supermodular.

Lemma 3.3.

The objective function in Problem
1
, specifically the total biharmonic distance
B(\cdot)
, is not supermodular. This means that there exist two edge sets
S
and
T
such that
S\subset T\subseteq Q
, and an edge
e\in Q\backslash T
, for which the following inequality holds

B(G_{S\cup\{e\}})-B(G_{S})>B(G_{T\cup\{e\}})-B(G_{T}).

Proof.
To prove the non-supermodularity of the objective function, consider the graph shown in Figure
1
. The solid black lines denote the edges of the original graph, the solid blue line represents the edge in
T
, and the dashed red line indicates the edge
e
. We define
S=\emptyset
,
T=\{(1,3)\}
, and
e=(1,4)
. The computation yields
B(G_{S\cup\{e\}})-B(G_{S})=-0.89
and
B(G_{T\cup\{e\}})-B(G_{T})=-1.01
, violating the condition of supermodularity since
-0.89>-1.01
. Therefore, the objective function is non-supermodular.

\Box

Figure 1.
A counterexample with 5 nodes for Lemma
3.3
.

For traditional monotonically decreasing and supermodular functions, a simple greedy strategy can attain an approximation ratio of
(1-1/e)
solution in each iteration
(
Nemhauser et al. 1978
)
. Despite the objective function
B(\cdot)
being not supermodular, the greedy method can still remain a suitable strategy for solving Problem
1
with guaranteed performance. We define the non-negative, monotone-increasing function
f(T)=B(G)-B(G_{T})
, where
T\subseteq Q
. We introduce a new quantity
\Theta_{T}(S)=f(S\cup T)-f(S)
, which represents the marginal benefit of adding the set
T\subseteq Q
to the set
S\subseteq Q
. Utilizing this, we introduce the concepts of submodularity ratio and curvature.

Definition 3.4 (
(
Bian et al. 2017
)
).

For a non-negative set function
f
, the submodularity ratio is defined as the largest scalar
\gamma
such that for any two arbitrary sets
S\subseteq Q
and
T\subseteq Q
,

\sum\nolimits_{i\in T\setminus S}\Theta_{i}(S)\geq\gamma\Theta_{T}(S).\Omega(n^{2})

Definition 3.5 (
(
Bian et al. 2017
)
).

For a non-negative set function
f
, the curvature is defined as the smallest scalar
\alpha
such that for any two arbitrary sets
S\subseteq Q
and
T\subseteq Q
, and any element
j\in S\backslash T
,

\Theta_{j}(S\cup T\backslash j)\geq(1-\alpha)\Theta_{j}(S\backslash j).

We then establish the bounds for the submodularity ratio
\gamma
and curvature
\alpha
of the function
f(T)=B(G)-B(G_{T})
, which will be used in the next section.

Lemma 3.6.

The submodularity ratio
\gamma
of the function
f(T)=B(G)-B(G_{T})
,
T\subseteq Q
is bounded by

1>\gamma\geq\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}(\bm{L})}{n}\right)^{5},

and its curvature
\alpha
is constrained by

0<\alpha\leq 1-\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}(\bm{L})}{n}\right)^{5}.

Proof.
Let
Q=(V\times V)\backslash E
be the candidate edge set,
S
and
T
be two arbitrary subsets of
E
, and
u,v
be any two subsets of the candidate edge set
Q
. To begin with,
we first derive a lower and upper bound for the marginal benefit function
\Theta_{T}(S)=f(S\cup{T})-f(S)
, respectively. We use
\bm{L}(S)
to denote the Laplacian matrix of the resultant graph
G_{S}
. On the one hand,

\displaystyle\Theta_{T}(S)

\displaystyle=f(S\cup T)-f(S)=B(G_{S})-B(G_{S\cup T})

\displaystyle=n\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{L}(S)^{2\dagger}\right)-n\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{L}(S\cup T)^{2\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{i=2}^{n}\frac{n}{\lambda_{i}^{2}(\bm{L}(S))}-\frac{n}{\lambda_{i}^{2}(\bm{L}(S\cup T))}

\displaystyle=n\sum\nolimits_{i=2}^{n}\frac{(\lambda_{i}(\bm{L}(S\cup T))-\lambda_{i}(\bm{L}(S)))(\lambda_{i}(\bm{L}(S\cup T))+\lambda_{i}(\bm{L}(S)))}{\lambda_{i}^{2}(\bm{L}(S))\lambda_{i}^{2}(\bm{L}(S\cup T))}

\displaystyle\geq n\frac{(\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{L}(S\cup T)\right)-\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{L}(S)\right))(\lambda_{2}(\bm{L}(S\cup T))+\lambda_{2}(\bm{L}(S))}{\lambda_{n}^{2}(\bm{L}(S))\lambda_{n}^{2}(\bm{L}(S\cup T))}

\displaystyle=\frac{2n|T\backslash S|(\lambda_{2}(\bm{L}(S\cup T))+\lambda_{2}(\bm{L}(S))}{\lambda_{n}^{2}(\bm{L}(S))\lambda_{n}^{2}(\bm{L}(S\cup T))}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\Theta_{T}(S)

\displaystyle=n\sum\nolimits_{i=2}^{n}\frac{(\lambda_{i}(\bm{L}(S\cup T))-\lambda_{i}(\bm{L}(S)))(\lambda_{i}(\bm{L}(S\cup T))+\lambda_{i}(\bm{L}(S)))}{\lambda_{i}^{2}(\bm{L}(S))\lambda_{i}^{2}(\bm{L}(S\cup T))}

\displaystyle\leq n\frac{(\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{L}(S\cup T)\right)-\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{L}(S)\right))(\lambda_{n}(\bm{L}(S\cup T))+\lambda_{n}(\bm{L}(S))}{\lambda_{2}^{2}(\bm{L}(S))\lambda_{2}^{2}(\bm{L}(S\cup T))}

\displaystyle=\frac{2n|T\backslash S|(\lambda_{n}(\bm{L}(S\cup T))+\lambda_{n}(\bm{L}(S))}{\lambda_{2}^{2}(\bm{L}(S))\lambda_{2}^{2}(\bm{L}(S\cup T))}.

Then, we put the above two bounds together and derive the lower bound of the submodular ratio
\gamma.

\displaystyle\frac{\sum\nolimits_{e\in T\setminus S}\Theta_{e}(S)}{\Theta_{T}(S)}

\displaystyle\geq\sum\nolimits_{e\in T\setminus S}\frac{(\lambda_{2}(\bm{L}(S\cup{e}))+\lambda_{2}(\bm{L}(S))}{\lambda_{n}^{2}(\bm{L}(S))\lambda_{n}^{2}(\bm{L}(S\cup e))}\cdot\frac{\lambda_{2}^{2}(\bm{L}(S))\lambda_{2}^{2}(\bm{L}(S\cup T))}{(\lambda_{n}(\bm{L}(S\cup T))+\lambda_{n}(\bm{L}(S))}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}(\bm{L})}{\lambda_{n}(\bm{L}(Q))}\right)^{5}=\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}(\bm{L})}{n}\right)^{5}.

The last equality holds since the largest eigenvalue of the Laplacian matrix of a complete graph with

nodes is
n.

Similarly, we derive the upper bound of the curvature
\alpha.
Let
j
be any candidate edge in
S\backslash T.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\Theta_{j}(S\cup T\backslash j)}{\Theta_{j}(S\backslash j)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2n(\lambda_{2}(\bm{L}(S\cup T\backslash j))+\lambda_{2}(\bm{L}(S\cup T))}{\lambda_{n}^{2}(\bm{L}(S\cup T\backslash j))\lambda_{n}^{2}(\bm{L}(S\cup T))}\cdot\frac{\lambda_{2}^{2}(\bm{L}(S\backslash j))\lambda_{2}^{2}(\bm{L}(S))}{2n(\lambda_{n}(\bm{L}(S\backslash j))+\lambda_{n}(\bm{L}(S))}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}(\bm{L})}{\lambda_{n}(\bm{L}(Q))}\right)^{5}=\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}(\bm{L})}{n}\right)^{5},

which combing with Definition
3.5
completes the proof.

\Box

3.5.
Gradient-Based Greedy Approach

Traditional heuristic greedy algorithms select edges based on the maximum marginal decrease or gain in the objective function. While this approach is commonly employed, researchers have also explored using gradients or partial derivatives of the relevant function to assess the impact of edge modifications
(
Li and Zhang 2018
;
Black et al. 2023

. In this subsection, we explore the gradient of the total biharmonic distance to characterize the significance of an edge instead of the marginal decrease
\Delta(e)

Lemma 3.8.

For a connected graph
G=(V,E)
, the gradient of total biharmonic distance
c(e)
for an edge

Proof.
Let

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial B(G_{\{e\}})}{\partial w_{e}}=n\frac{\partial\mathrm{Tr}\left((\bm{L}+\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{T})^{2\dagger})\right)}{\partial w_{e}}

\displaystyle=n\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\left((\bm{L}+\epsilon\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{T})^{2\dagger}\right)-\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{L}^{2\dagger}\right)\right].

2

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left((\bm{L}+\epsilon\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{T})^{2\dagger}\right)-\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2{\dagger}}\right)

\displaystyle\epsilon\mathrm{Tr}\left(\frac{\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2{\dagger}}\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}}{1+\epsilon\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}\bm{b}_{e}}\right)+\epsilon\mathrm{Tr}\left(\frac{\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2\dagger}}{1+\epsilon\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}\bm{b}_{e}}\right)-\mathrm{Tr}\left(\frac{(\epsilon\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}\bm{b}_{e}\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}})^{2}}{(1+\epsilon\bm{b}_{e}^{T}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}\bm{b}_{e})^{2}}\right)

4.
Fast Approximation Algorithm

Both Algorithm
1
and Algorithm
DeterGrad
struggle with high time complexity, making them computationally prohibitive for large-scale networks. This challenge arises from several key factors. First, calculating
c(e)
requires inverting the Laplacian matrix, which has a time complexity of
\Theta(n^{3})
, presenting a significant computational burden. Second, each iteration involves examining all pairs of nodes to identify an edge to add and requires querying
|Q|
gradients, where
n^{2}-m-k\leq|Q|\leq n^{2}-m
. This leads to a computational bottleneck. Finally, even with the use of the Sherman-Morrison formula to update
\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}
,
\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2\dagger}
, and
\bm{\mathit{L}}^{3\dagger}
, the time complexity per iteration remains
O(n^{2})
. Our primary objective is to optimize three aspects for improved efficiency. We begin by addressing the highest time cost, which is our first challenge. To reduce the high computational cost associated with matrix inversion, we employ the projection technique and the Laplacian solver to estimate
c(e)
. This approach also eliminates the need to update
\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}
,
\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2\dagger}
, and
\bm{\mathit{L}}^{3\dagger}
, effectively addressing the third challenge simultaneously. For the second challenge, we introduce a pruning strategy based on geometric principles to approximate the convex hull, which helps to reduce the size of the candidate edge set. By integrating these methods, we propose an approximation algorithm, outlined in Algorithm
3
. This algorithm achieves an
\epsilon
-approximation of
c(e)
in each of the
k
iterations, enabling approximate solutions with nearly linear time complexity.

4.1.
Projection Technique and Laplacian Solver

We begin by reformulating
\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{3\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}
in terms of the
\ell_{2}
-norm.

\displaystyle\bm{b}_{e}^{\top}\bm{L}^{3\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e})^{\top}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}=(\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e})^{\top}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{L}}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e})^{\top}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{B}}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{B}}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{b}_{e}=\|\bm{\mathit{B}}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2\dagger}(\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v})\|^{2}.

In this way, computing
c(e)
reduces to computing
\|\bm{\mathit{B}}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2\dagger}(\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v})\|^{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. However, calculating this exact
\ell_{2}
-norm is computationally intensive because the dimension
m
of the vectors
\bm{\mathit{B}}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2\dagger}\bm{\mathit{e}}_{i}
(where
i=1,2,\ldots,n
) is high, and it also requires matrix inversion. To alleviate this issue and reduce the dimensionality of the vectors, we employ the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma for dimensionality reduction.

Lemma 4.1 (
(
Johnson and Lindenstrauss 1984
;
Achlioptas 2001
)
).

Given fixed vectors
\bm{\mathit{v}}_{1},\bm{\mathit{v}}_{2},\ldots,\bm{\mathit{v}}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\beta>0
, let
\bm{\mathit{Q}}_{t\times d}
be a random matrix, where each entry is either
1/\sqrt{t}
or
-1/\sqrt{t}
, with probability of 1/2, and
t\geq 24\log(n)/\beta^{2}
. Then, with probability at least
1-1/n
, the following holds for all pairs
i,j\leq n
:

(1-\beta)\|\bm{\mathit{v}}_{i}-\bm{\mathit{v}}_{j}\|^{2}_{2}\leq\|\bm{\mathit{Q}}\bm{\mathit{v}}_{i}-\bm{\mathit{Q}}\bm{\mathit{v}}_{j}\|^{2}_{2}\leq(1+\beta)\|\bm{\mathit{v}}_{i}-\bm{\mathit{v}}_{j}\|^{2}_{2}.

Let
\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{t\times m}
be a random matrix with entries
\pm 1/\sqrt{t}
, where
t=\lceil 24\log(n)/\beta^{2}\rceil
. By Lemma
4.1
, we have the approximation:

(5)

\|\bm{Q}\bm{\mathit{B}}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2\dagger}(\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v})\|^{2}\approx_{\beta}\|\bm{\mathit{B}}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2\dagger}(\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v})\|^{2}

for every pair
(u,v)
with probability at least
1-1/n
.

We have projected a set of
n

m
-dimensional vectors onto a low
t
-dimensional subspace spanned by the columns of the matrix
\bm{\mathit{Q}}
. However, the high computational cost of
\Omega(n^{3})
associated with matrix inversion still exists. To circumvent this expensive operation, we utilize a fast SDD linear system solver to solve the corresponding linear equations.

Lemma 4.2 (
(
Spielman and Teng 2014
;
Cohen et al. 2014
)
).

Let
\bm{\mathit{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a positive semi-definite matrix with
m
nonzero entries, and
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
a vector. For any error parameter
\delta>0
, there exists a solver, denoted by
\boldsymbol{\mathit{a}}=\textsc{Solve}(\bm{\mathit{X}},\bm{b},\delta)
, which returns a vector
\boldsymbol{\mathit{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying

\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathit{a}}-\bm{\mathit{X}}^{-1}\bm{b}\right\|_{\bm{\mathit{X}}}\leq\delta\left\|\bm{\mathit{X}}^{-1}\bm{b}\right\|_{\bm{\mathit{X}}}

with probability at least
1-1/n
. The expected runtime of this solver is
\tilde{O}(m)
, where
\tilde{O}(\cdot)
suppresses poly(
\log(n)
) factors.

To approximate
\|\bm{\mathit{B}}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2\dagger}(\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v})\|^{2}
, we apply the solver as follows. For any small
\beta>0
, we choose an appropriate
\delta
to ensure the approximation error remains within
\beta
with probability at least
1-1/n
. Let
\bm{\mathit{Z}}=\bm{\mathit{Q}}\bm{\mathit{B}}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2\dagger}
. We solve the system of equations
\bm{\mathit{L}}^{2}\bm{\mathit{z}}_{i}=\bm{\mathit{y}}_{i}
, with the constraint
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{z}}_{i}=0
, for
i=1,\dots,t
, where
\bm{\mathit{z}}^{\top}_{i}
and
\bm{\mathit{y}}^{\top}_{i}
represent the
i
-th rows of
\bm{\mathit{Z}}
and
\bm{\mathit{Q}}\bm{\mathit{B}}
, respectively. By using the solver
\textsc{Solve}(\bm{\mathit{L}},\bm{\mathit{y}}_{i},\delta)
twice, and carefully selecting
\delta
, we achieve a reliable approximation of
\bm{\mathit{z}}_{i}
. Before proving this, we first introduce some lemmas (all missing proofs are provided in Appendix
A
).

Lemma 4.3.

Given a Laplacian matrix
\bm{\mathit{L}}
and a vector
\bm{\mathit{z}}
orthogonal to
\mathbf{1}
, we have

(6)

\|\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{z}}\|_{\bm{\mathit{L}}}\leq 2n^{4}\|\bm{\mathit{z}}\|_{\bm{\mathit{L}}}.

Proof.

\|\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{z}}\|_{\bm{\mathit{L}}}=\sqrt{\bm{\mathit{z}}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{z}}}\leq\sqrt{2n^{4}}\|\bm{\mathit{z}}\|,

and
\|\bm{\mathit{z}}\|_{\bm{\mathit{L}}}=\sqrt{\bm{\mathit{z}}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{L}}\bm{\mathit{z}}}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n^{4}}}\|\bm{\mathit{z}}\|,
which further suggest that
\|\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{z}}\|_{\bm{\mathit{L}}}\leq 2n^{4}\|\bm{\mathit{z}}\|_{\bm{\mathit{L}}}.

\Box

Lemma 4.4.

Given two vector sequences
\bm{\mathit{z}}^{(1)},\bm{\mathit{z}}^{(2)}
and
\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{z}}}^{(1)},\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{z}}}^{(2)}
such that

\bm{\mathit{z}}^{(1)}=\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{y}},\quad\bm{\mathit{z}}^{(2)}=\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{z}}^{(1)},

and

\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{z}}}^{(1)}=\textsc{Solve}(\bm{\mathit{L}},\bm{\mathit{y}},\delta),\quad\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{z}}}^{(2)}=\textsc{Solve}(\bm{\mathit{L}},\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{z}}}^{(1)},\delta),

(7)

\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{z}}}^{(2)}-\bm{\mathit{z}}^{(2)}\|_{\bm{\mathit{L}}}\leq\delta^{\prime}\|\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{y}}\|_{\bm{\mathit{L}}},

where
\delta^{\prime}=2n^{4}\left(\delta^{2}+2\delta\right)

Proof.
First, according to Lemma
4.2
, we have

(8)

\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{z}}}^{(1)}-\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{y}}\|_{\bm{\mathit{L}}}\leq\delta\|\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{y}}\|_{\bm{\mathit{L}}},

(9)

\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{z}}}^{(2)}-\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{z}}}^{(1)}\|_{\bm{\mathit{L}}}\leq\delta\|\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{z}}}^{(1)}\|_{\bm{\mathit{L}}}.

Since
\|\cdot\|_{\bm{\mathit{L}}}
is a norm, (
8

(10)

(1-\delta)\|\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{y}}\|_{\bm{\mathit{L}}}\leq\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{z}}}^{(1)}\|_{\bm{\mathit{L}}}\leq(1+\delta)\|\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{y}}\|_{\bm{\mathit{L}}}.

Then, we apply the triangle inequality on the left-hand side of (
7

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{z}}}^{(2)}-\bm{\mathit{z}}^{(2)}\|_{\bm{\mathit{L}}}\leq

4.2.
Pruning Technique

In this subsection, we utilize a pruning technique based on the convex hull of a set of points
(
Rockafellar 1970
)
. This approach allows us to reduce the candidate edge set
Q
to a smaller subset
P
with
P\subset Q
and
|P|\ll|Q|
. This reduction improves the efficiency of our algorithm and provides approximate solutions for the optimization problem.

Based on (
13

can be reduced to calculating distances between
O(n^{2})
pairs of points
\tilde{\bm{\mathit{Z}}}\bm{\mathit{e}}_{i}
in
\mathbb{R}^{t}

i=1,2,\ldots,n

P=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}\}

p_{i}=\tilde{\bm{\mathit{Z}}}\bm{\mathit{e}}_{i}

Definition 4.6 (
(
Rockafellar 1970

Given a set of
n

P=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}\}

\bar{P}

Below, we explain how to efficiently identify the convex hull of a set of points. For

O(n^{\lfloor t/2\rfloor})

(

Awasthi et al. 2020

(

Huang et al. 2018

(
Awasthi et al. 2020

6.
Related Work

Resistance Distance Computation.

Various algorithms have been developed for estimating resistance distances in large graphs
(
Hayashi et al. 2016
;
Peng et al. 2021
;
Liao et al. 2023
;
Yang and Tang 2023
)
. Early methods used random projection
(
Spielman and Srivastava 2008
)
, while subsequent approaches improved efficiency through random walk sampling and random spanning tree formulations
(
Peng et al. 2021
)
. More recent work introduced Monte Carlo techniques for faster random walk-based estimations
(
Yang and Tang 2023
)
and landmark-based sampling methods
(
Liao et al. 2023
;
Liao et al. 2024
)
. Despite extensive studies on resistance distance, its variants, such as biharmonic distance, remain less understood.

Biharmonic Distance Computation.

Originally introduced by Lipman et al.
(
Lipman et al. 2010
)
, biharmonic distance captures global structural information and is more effective than resistance distance in network influence mining and graph embedding
(
Yi et al. 2018a
;
Kreuzer et al. 2021
;
Black et al. 2023
)
. Several efficient algorithms for biharmonic distance computation have been proposed
(
Yi et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2024
)
, but no methods have been developed to minimize total biharmonic distance through edge addition.

Optimization via Edge Addition.

Edge addition has been studied for optimizing various objectives
(
Xu and Zhang 2023
;
Then et al. 2014
;
Crescenzi et al. 2016
)
, such as increasing the number of spanning trees
(
Li et al. 2019
)
, reducing random walk hitting times
(
Adriaens et al. 2023
)
, improving centrality measures
(
Shan et al. 2018
;
Medya et al. 2018
;
D’Angelo et al. 2019
;
Li et al. 2021
)
, and minimizing total shortest path distances
(
Meyerson and Tagiku 2009
)
. Other objectives, which are more closely related to network connectivity and robustness, include reducing the network diameter
(
Frati et al. 2015
;
Adriaens and Gionis 2022
)
, bolstering algebraic connectivity
(
Ghosh and Boyd 2006
)
, decreasing the Kirchhoff index
(
Ghosh et al. 2008
;
Predari et al. 2022
;
Kooij and Achterberg 2023
)
, and increasing robustness
(
Chan and Akoglu 2016
)
.
We are the first to propose our problem. None of the existing approaches can be directly translated to the problem of minimizing total biharmonic distance through edge addition. Furthermore, existing approaches may face challenges when applied to large-scale networks. Our work addresses this gap by introducing novel algorithms specifically designed for this challenging optimization problem.

7.
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied the problem of minimizing the total biharmonic distance in a network through link recommendation. We first demonstrated the monotonicity of the total biharmonic distance, showing that adding new edges to the graph decreases the total biharmonic distance. Based on this property, we formulated a combinatorial optimization problem with broad applicability, including network robustness maximization. The problem is combinatorially complex. We established that while the objective function of the problem is monotone, it is not supermodular. To tackle the problem, we employed greedy heuristics. The first algorithm,
DeterDiff
, has a provable bound of approximation factor and cubic time complexity. The second algorithm,
DeterGrad
, uses the gradient rather than the marginal decrease, showing selecting the edge with the largest gradient is an effective strategy. We then leveraged the projection technique, the Laplacian solver, and the convex hull approximation to develop an algorithm with nearly linear time complexity and an error guarantee. Finally, we performed experiments on ten real networks, showing that
DeterDiff
,
DeterGrad
, and
ApproxFast
exhibit comparable effectiveness, with
ApproxFast
being the most efficient.
ApproxFast
demonstrates scalability in large graphs containing over 4 million nodes. Future work includes extending our algorithm for other network optimization problems.

\displaystyle\left|\|\bm{\mathit{Z}}(\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v})\|-\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{Z}}}(\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v})\|\right|\leq\|(\bm{\mathit{Z}}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{Z}}})(\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{a\sim b\in P}\|(\bm{\mathit{Z}}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{Z}}})\left(\bm{\mathit{e}}_{a}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{b}\right)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(n\sum\nolimits_{a\sim b\in P}\|(\bm{\mathit{Z}}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{Z}}})\left(\bm{\mathit{e}}_{a}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{b}\right)\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle n^{1/2}\left(\sum\nolimits_{a\sim b\in E}\|(\bm{\mathit{Z}}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{Z}}})\left(\bm{\mathit{e}}_{a}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{b}\right)\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{1/2}\|(\bm{\mathit{Z}}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{Z}}})\bm{\mathit{B}}^{\top}\|_{F},

where the third inequality is derived by Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality.

We now transform the above obtained Frobenius norm
n^{1/2}\|(\bm{\mathit{Z}}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{Z}}})\bm{\mathit{B}}^{\top}\|_{F}
into the
\bm{\mathit{L}}
-norm as

\displaystyle n^{1/2}\|(\bm{\mathit{Z}}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{Z}}})\bm{\mathit{B}}^{\top}\|_{F}=n^{1/2}\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}\left((\bm{\mathit{Z}}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{Z}}})\bm{\mathit{B}}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{B}}(\bm{\mathit{Z}}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{Z}}})^{\top}\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{1/2}\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}\left((\bm{\mathit{Z}}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{Z}}})\bm{\mathit{L}}(\bm{\mathit{Z}}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{Z}}})^{\top}\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{1/2}\sqrt{\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}(\bm{\mathit{z}}_{i}-\tilde{\bm{\mathit{z}}}_{i})^{\top}\bm{\mathit{L}}(\bm{\mathit{z}}_{i}-\tilde{\bm{\mathit{z}}}_{i})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle n^{1/2}\delta^{\prime}\|\bm{\mathit{Q}}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{B}}^{\top}\|_{F},

where the inequality is obtained according to (
11
) and
\delta^{\prime}
is given by (
12
). We continue to simplify
n^{1/2}\delta^{\prime}\|\bm{\mathit{Q}}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{B}}^{\top}\|_{F}
as

\displaystyle n^{1/2}\delta^{\prime}\|\bm{\mathit{Q}}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{B}}^{\top}\|_{F}=n^{1/2}\delta^{\prime}\left(\sum\nolimits_{a\sim b\in E}\|\bm{\mathit{Q}}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathit{e}}_{a}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{b})\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle n^{1/2}\delta^{\prime}\left((1+\beta)\sum\nolimits_{a\sim b\in E}R_{a,b}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{1/2}\delta^{\prime}\left((1+\beta)(n-1)\right)^{1/2}.

On the other hand, we provide a lower bound of
\|\bm{\mathit{Z}}(\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v})\|^{2}
as

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathit{Z}}(\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v}\|^{2}\geq(1-\beta)(\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v})^{\top}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{3\dagger}(\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v}).

The eigenvalues of the Laplacian matrix
\bm{\mathit{L}}
of
G
are at most
n
, so the non-zero eigenvalues of
\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}
are at least
\frac{1}{n}
and the non-zero eigenvalues of
\bm{\mathit{L}}^{3\dagger}
areat least
\frac{1}{n^{3}}
. Moreover, the vector
\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v}
is orthogonal to
\ker\bm{\mathit{L}}
, since
\ker\bm{\mathit{L}}
is spanned by the all-ones vector. By the Courant-Fischer theorem, which states that for a symmetric matrix
\bm{\mathit{A}}
with minimal non-zero eigenvalue
\lambda_{\min}
, and for any non-zero vector
\bm{\mathit{x}}\perp\ker\bm{\mathit{A}}
, we have
\lambda_{\min}\leq\frac{\bm{\mathit{x}}^{T}\bm{\mathit{A}}\bm{\mathit{x}}}{\bm{\mathit{x}}^{T}\bm{\mathit{x}}}
. Thus, we obtain

(\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v})^{\top}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{3\dagger}(\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v})\geq\frac{2}{n^{3}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathit{Z}}(\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v}\|^{2}\geq(1-\beta)\frac{2}{n^{3}}.

Combining the above-obtained results, it follows that

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\frac{\left|\|\bm{\mathit{Z}}(\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v})\|-\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathit{Z}}}(\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v})\|\right|}{\|\bm{\mathit{Z}}(\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v})\|}

\displaystyle\leq\delta^{\prime}n^{2}((1+\beta)(n-1))^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{2(1-\beta)}\right)^{1/2}\leq\frac{\beta}{3}.

This completes the proof.

\Box

A.2.
Proof of Theorem
4.9

Proof.
We begin by analyzing the time complexity of the algorithm. First, a random matrix
\bm{Q}
is generated, which takes
O(m\log n/\epsilon^{2})
time (Line 4). Next, we compute
\bm{\mathit{Q}}\bm{\mathit{B}}
, which requires
2m\times 24\log n\epsilon^{-2}=\tilde{O}(m\epsilon^{-2})
time, as
\bm{\mathit{B}}
contains
2m
entries (Lines 5-6). The algorithm proceeds through
k
iterative rounds (Lines 7-16), with each round selecting a single edge. During each iteration, the Laplacian solver is applied
2t
times, resulting in a total cost of
2\times 24\log n\epsilon^{-2}\times m=\tilde{O}(m\epsilon^{-2})
. Consequently, the points
\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}\}
are computed in
\tilde{O}(m\epsilon^{-2})
time (Lines 8-12).

Subsequently, the convex hull of the point set
P
is computed using
ApproxConv
, with a time complexity of
O(n\log n\epsilon^{-2}l)
(Line 13). The maximum distance between points on the convex hull is then determined in
O(l^{2}\log n\epsilon^{-2})
time (Line 14). Finally, the solution, the graph, and the Laplacian matrix are updated based on the identified results (Lines 15-16). Thus, the overall time complexity of the
ApproxFast
algorithm is
\tilde{O}(k(nl+m)/\epsilon^{2})
.
