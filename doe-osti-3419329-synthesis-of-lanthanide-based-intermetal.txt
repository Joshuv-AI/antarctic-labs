Abstract
Lanthanide-containing materials are of interest because of their useful magnetic and electronic
behavior. To study this intrinsic behavior, researchers need high-quality single crystals to avoid
interference from grain boundaries or defects commonly found in polycrystalline samples. One
method for growing single crystals is metal-flux synthesis, an approach that promotes crystal
growth at lower reaction temperatures thus providing access to complex intermetallic phases. My
project focuses on synthesizing novel lanthanide-containing intermetallic silicides related to
previously reported f-element compounds with the Gd₁₊ₓFe₄Si₁₀₋ᵧ structure type. I investigated
reactions targeting lanthanum, praseodymium, neodymium, samarium, europium, and
gadolinium-containing products, and used scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive
X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) to identify promising lanthanide-containing phases. I prepared
reactions by loading lanthanide oxides, Co, Si, Al, and Ga into alumina crucibles in a
0.2:1:1:10:10 Ln₂O₃/Co/Si/Al/Ga mmol ratio, where Ln = La, Nd, Pr, Sm, Eu, or Gd. The
reactions were sealed in quartz tubes, heated in a furnace, and centrifuged to separate crystals
from excess flux. Afterwards, I mechanically isolated single crystals and prepared them for
characterization. Pr-, Nd-, and Sm-containing reactions were confirmed through SEM-EDS to
have formed the targeted quaternary phase. Single crystal X-ray diffraction (SC-XRD) data
showed the Nd-containing crystals exhibited a hexagonal unit cell associated with the
Gd₁₊ₓFe₄Si₁₀₋ᵧ structure type. La- and Eu-containing reactions instead formed competing
Co(Al/Ga/Si)₃ phase crystals. Single-crystal XRD, powder XRD, and magnetic susceptibility and
heat-capacity measurements will be collected for the remaining lanthanide-containing crystals.
This data will help determine the complete crystal structure of the lanthanide analogues, their
phase purity and whether they exhibit localized magnetic behavior, magnetic ordering, or other
electronic signatures that can be compared with the previously reported actinide analogues. The
disordered structure of this intermetallic family is associated with unusual magnetic and
electronic behavior meaning lanthanide analogues may show potential as thermoelectric
materials. Developing new thermoelectric candidate materials is relevant to energy efficiency
and waste-heat recovery, which supports DOE’s broader goal of advancing science and
technology solutions for America’s energy needs.

Introduction
Background
Lanthanide-containing intermetallic materials are important because they can display useful
electronic, magnetic, thermal, and optical properties. To understand these intrinsic properties,
researchers often need high-quality single crystals, which have a continuous crystal lattice, to avoid
complications from grain boundaries that are present in polycrystalline samples [1]. One method
for growing single crystals is metal-flux synthesis, in which an excess low-melting metal or metal
mixture is used as the reaction medium. This approach can promote crystal growth at lower
reaction temperatures and can provide access to complex intermetallic phases [4].
My internship project focused on synthesizing lanthanide-containing intermetallic silicides related
to previously reported f-element compounds with the Gd₁₊ₓFe₄Si₁₀₋ᵧ structure type. Prior work
showed that uranium analogues, U₁.₃₃T₄Al₈Si₂ where T = nickel or cobalt, can be grown from
aluminum/gallium flux and contain disordered uranium/silicon layers. These disordered layers are
important because they are associated with unusual magnetic and electronic behavior, including
fragile magnetic ordering, spin-glass-like behavior, and strong disorder scattering [2]. From a
thermoelectric perspective, this structural disorder may also help reduce thermal conductivity by
increasing phonon scattering, while the transition-metal/aluminum/silicon framework may help
maintain electronic transport. Later work expanded this chemistry to additional f-elements,
including cerium, thorium, uranium, and neptunium, showing that metal-flux growth is a useful
approach for preparing structurally complex f-element intermetallics [3].
The goal of this work was to explore whether lanthanide analogues of these materials could be
synthesized using a similar aluminum/gallium flux method. Lanthanide analogues are valuable
because they can help determine how changing the f-element affects crystal formation,
composition, and future magnetic or thermoelectric properties. During the internship, reactions
targeting lanthanum, praseodymium, neodymium, samarium, europium, and gadolinium-
containing products were investigated, with the main focus on isolating single crystals and using
scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) to identify
promising lanthanide-containing phases.
Objectives
The objective of my project was to synthesize novel lanthanide-containing intermetallic silicides
using aluminum/gallium metal-flux growth. These materials are lanthanide analogues of
previously reported f-element compounds with the Gd₁₊ₓFe₄Si₁₀₋ᵧ structure type, but most
lanthanide members have not been reported, except for the cerium analogue [3]. Expanding this
family to other lanthanides is important because it provides new single-crystal materials for
studying how the identity of the f-element influences crystal formation, structural disorder, and
magnetic behavior. Since lanthanide 4f electrons are generally more localized than actinide 5f
electrons, these new phases can also be compared with previously synthesized actinide analogues
to better understand the role of f-electrons in this structure family. During the internship, the work
focused on setting up lanthanide flux reactions, isolating single crystals, analyzing products using
SEM-EDS, and confirming the neodymium analogue by single-crystal X-ray diffraction.
Progress
Technical Approach

 2

Lanthanide-containing intermetallic crystals were synthesized using an aluminum/gallium metal-
flux growth method adapted from previous work on actinide silicides [2,3]. Metal-flux growth was
selected because the molten metal acts as a reaction medium, allowing reactions to occur at lower
temperatures than many conventional solid-state methods and promoting the growth of single
crystals [4]. In this work, aluminum and gallium were used together as the flux. The addition of
gallium lowers the effective melting temperature of the flux mixture relative to pure aluminum,
which melts at 660°C.
I prepared reactions using lanthanide oxides, cobalt, silicon, aluminum, and gallium in a
0.2:1:1:10:10 Ln₂O₃/Co/Si/Al/Ga mmol ratio, where Ln = La, Nd, Pr, Sm, Eu, or Gd. Then, I
loaded the reagents into alumina crucibles using two different loading methods to evaluate whether
reagent placement affected product formation. In the first method, cobalt and silicon were mixed
before adding Ln₂O₃, aluminum, and gallium. In the second method, silicon, Ln₂O₃, aluminum,
gallium, and cobalt were added sequentially, and the crucible was gently tapped to allow the cobalt
to settle through the mixture.
Because cobalt and some reaction components can be sensitive to oxygen, I partially assembled
the reactions in an argon-filled glovebox. Each alumina crucible was placed inside a quartz tube
with quartz wool positioned above the crucible to act as a filter during centrifugation. The quartz
tubes were flame-sealed under vacuum. The sealed reaction tubes were heated to 1000°C over 12
h, held at 1000°C for 24 h, and then cooled to 800°C over 72 h. At 800°C, the tubes were removed
from the furnace, inverted, and centrifuged to separate the product crystals from the excess molten
flux.
After cooling, I opened the quartz tubes and mechanically isolated the crystals from the reaction
products. Then, I cleaned the crystals by sonication in ethanol to remove residual surface flux.
Afterwards, I mounted elected crystals on metal stubs using carbon tape for scanning electron
microscopy (SEM) analysis. The product crystals were examined using a Quanta FEG 650 SEM
equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) to determine crystal morphology and
obtain semi-quantitative elemental compositions.
Results
The lanthanide flux reactions produced several distinct crystal morphologies depending on the
lanthanide element used. In reactions targeting the La-containing analogue, faceted crystals were
observed; however, SEM-EDS analysis indicated that these crystals corresponded primarily to a
Co(Al/Ga/Si)₃ phase rather than the targeted lanthanide-containing quaternary phase. This result
suggests that, under the reaction conditions tested, La did not readily incorporate into the targeted
Gd₁₊ₓFe₄Si₁₀₋ᵧ-type structure.
In contrast, reactions containing Pr, Nd, Sm, and Gd produced pencil-shaped crystals, described
as faceted rods with hexagonal cross sections and pointed ends (Figure 1). This crystal habit is
similar to that previously observed for related Gd₁₊ₓFe₄Si₁₀₋ᵧ-type f-element intermetallics. As
shown in Figure 1, SEM images and EDS elemental maps confirmed the presence of lanthanide,
cobalt, aluminum, silicon, and minor gallium in these crystals. The SEM-EDS compositions for
the Pr-, Nd-, and Sm-containing products are summarized in Table 1. Although some binary
phases were also observed, the pencil-shaped quaternary phase appeared to be the main product in
these reactions.

For the Eu-containing reaction, only the binary phase was observed under the conditions tested.
This reaction will be repeated to confirm whether Eu can form the targeted quaternary phase.
Overall, these results show that Pr, Nd, and Sm are promising lanthanides for forming new
analogues of this intermetallic family.

 refinement and comparison with the previously reported actinide analogues will help determine
 the extent of structural disorder and site occupancies in the Nd-containing phase.

 Future Work
 Single-crystal X-ray diffraction data will be collected for the remaining lanthanide-containing
 crystals to determine whether they adopt the targeted Gd₁₊ₓFe₄Si₁₀₋ᵧ-type structure. Powder X-ray
 diffraction data will also be collected in the coming days to evaluate the phase purity of the
 synthesized products. These measurements will help determine whether the bulk crystalline
 products correspond to the targeted phases identified by single-crystal X-ray diffraction or whether
 additional secondary phases are present.
 After structural and phase-purity analysis, magnetic susceptibility and heat-capacity measurements
 will be collected to investigate the low-temperature physical properties of the crystals. These data
 will help determine whether the lanthanide analogues exhibit localized magnetic behavior,

Conclusions
The goal of this project was to synthesize novel lanthanide-containing intermetallic silicides
using the metal flux growth method. Over the course of my 10-week internship, I set up
lanthanide flux reactions, isolated single crystals, prepared samples for SEM, and assisted in
analyzing products using SEM-EDS. Pr-, Nd-, and Sm-containing reactions consistently
produced promising pencil-shaped crystals, while the Gd-containing reaction only produced a
few pointed crystals. SEM-EDS data confirmed the incorporation of Pr, Nd, or Sm together with
Si, Co, Al and minor Ga. Single-crystal X-ray diffraction data from the Colorado School of
Mines confirmed I was able to successfully synthesize Nd-containing crystals with a hexagonal
unit cell consistent with the Gd₁₊ₓFe₄Si₁₀₋ᵧ-type structure. We have yet to send Pr-, Sm-, and Gd-
containing crystals for single-crystal XRD, but the crystals I have synthesized are pencil-shaped,
which is characteristic of the Gd₁₊ₓFe₄Si₁₀₋ᵧ-type structure. La- and Eu-containing reactions
produced competing Co(Al/Ga/Si)₃ phase crystals. The Eu-containing reaction will be repeated
to confirm whether it can form the targeted quaternary phase. Magnetic susceptibility and heat-
capacity measurements will be collected to determine whether the lanthanide analogues exhibit
localized magnetic behavior, magnetic ordering, or other electronic signatures that can be
compared to previously reported actinide analogues. These results show that Nd, Pr, Sm and Gd
are capable of forming new analogues of this intermetallic family. Expanding this family to other
lanthanides is important because it provides new single-crystal materials for studying how the
