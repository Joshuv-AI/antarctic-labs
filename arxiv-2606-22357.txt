ORBIT: Training-Free Multi-Attribute Behavioral Steering via Orthogonal Subspace Rotation
Narges Ghasemi, Amir Ziashahabi, Salman Avestimehr, Jonathan May, [0.4em] Information Sciences Institute, University of Southern California

Abstract
Language models are widely used in assistant settings, where controlling behavioral attributes is often essential. Activation steering modifies hidden-state representations at inference time, providing a lightweight, training-free mechanism that can be toggled at runtime. Existing methods, however, have focused primarily on steering a single attribute at a time. When multiple attributes must be controlled simultaneously, naive summation of per-attribute steering vectors suffers from norm imbalance and directional cancellation, while classifier-based approaches require retraining whenever the attribute set changes. We introduce ORBIT (Orthogonal Rotation-Based Intervention Technique), a training-free extension of rotation-based steering to the multi-attribute setting. Our method constructs a joint subspace from per-attribute steering planes via singular value decomposition and applies a single norm-preserving rotation within that subspace toward a combined target direction. Adaptive per-token gating identifies which attributes need correction at each position, and an optional additive boost strengthens attributes with weak initial projection. We also introduce TraitFactory , a new multi-attribute benchmark that focuses on behavioral tendencies rather than surface-level style. We evaluate ORBIT on TraitFactory and ToneBank across three models (Llama-3.2-3B, Qwen-2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B) while steering multiple attributes simultaneously, showing that it achieves stronger and more balanced multi-attribute steering than existing training-free baselines while better preserving output coherence.



1
Introduction

Inference-time activation steering modifies hidden-state representations during a forward pass to shift model behavior along a target attribute, without updating any parameters
(
23
;
16
;
31
;
25
)
.
For a single attribute (e.g., making responses more concise, or more cautious), the problem is well studied and effective methods exist.
However, many practical scenarios require controlling
multiple
attributes simultaneously: a response that is both cautious and concise, or an argument that combines empirical grounding with moral framing.
Achieving such multi-attribute control at inference time, without retraining, remains a challenging problem.

The difficulty is not simply that there are more attributes to handle.
Moving from one attribute to several introduces a qualitatively different challenge:
geometric interference
between attribute directions in activation space.
Different attributes may induce steering directions that partially cancel, redundantly overlap, or differ by orders of magnitude in norm.
As the number of simultaneous attributes grows, the probability that at least two directions have substantial negative cosine similarity increases, and any composition scheme that ignores this geometry becomes increasingly fragile.

Why existing methods fall short.

Let
K
denote the number of attributes to be steered simultaneously.
The most natural multi-attribute extension of Contrastive Activation Addition
(
16
, CAA;)
sums per-attribute steering vectors:
\mathbf{h}^{\prime}=\mathbf{h}+\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}\mathbf{v}_{k}
.
This is straightforward for one attribute but problematic for
K>1
.
Different attributes produce vectors with vastly different norms; without normalization, high-magnitude attributes dominate.
With normalization, absolute scale is controlled, but choosing the
\alpha_{k}
values is still difficult: we do not know a priori how strongly each attribute should be pushed, and each coefficient interacts with the others. As
K
grows, tuning these coupled strengths becomes a high-dimensional search that scales poorly.

Training-based alternatives, such as gradient-guided classifiers
(
14
)
, learned gating functions
(
13
)
, and composition operators
(
18
)
, can sidestep linear summation, but they tie the model to a fixed attribute set: swapping or adding even a single attribute requires new labeled data and retraining, limiting flexible, on-the-fly behavioral composition.

Angular Steering
(
25
)
formulates single-attribute steering as a norm-preserving rotation in a 2D subspace, subsuming CAA and directional ablation
(
1
)
as special cases.
However, it is defined for a single feature direction, and its effect can be limited when the target attribute signal is initially weak but stronger induction is needed.
Naively composing
K
independent 2D rotations in potentially overlapping planes reintroduces the same cancellation and dominance problems, compounded by norm distortion from repeated projections (empirical evidence in Appendix
H
).

Our approach.

The central challenge in multi-attribute steering is that attribute directions are obtained separately, so applying independent rotations makes them interfere with one another.
ORBIT (Orthogonal Rotation-Based Intervention Technique) addresses this by first constructing one shared subspace that captures all target attributes, then applying a single norm-preserving rotation inside that shared subspace.
Components outside this subspace are left unchanged.
Adaptive per-token gating then applies correction only where needed, with stronger updates for more misaligned tokens.

Our contributions are:

1.

We introduce ORBIT, a training-free geometric method that constructs a joint trait subspace and applies a single norm-preserving rotation within it; components outside that subspace are left unchanged by construction (§
4.2
).

2.

We study naive multi-attribute composition strategies and characterize their common geometric failure modes, then validate them empirically (§
3.4
, Appendix
H
).

3.

We introduce
TraitFactory
, a multi-attribute benchmark of 13 personality traits across 22 topic categories, complementing
ToneBank
(5 conversational tones) (§
5
).

4.

To capture balanced multi-attribute control, we introduce
geometric gain
(positive only when all attributes improve) and
joint success rate
(the fraction of targeted attributes that improve), with
minimum per-attribute improvement
as a complementary metric.

5.

We evaluate ORBIT on both benchmarks across three model families (Llama-3.2-3B, Qwen-2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B), demonstrating it consistently outperforms baselines, while preserving output coherence. (§
6
).

2
Related work

Activation steering for single attributes.

Contrastive activation methods such as ActAdd
(
23
)
, CAA
(
16
)
, and RepE
(
31
)
, extract a steering direction from paired prompts and add it to hidden states at inference time.
Angular Steering
(
25
)
reformulates this as a norm-preserving rotation in a 2D subspace, preserving activation norm by construction.
These techniques are effective for a single attribute but do not address how to compose multiple attributes simultaneously.

Per-sample adaptive steering.

Rather than applying a fixed global vector, PIXEL
(
29
)
computes a sample-specific steering direction and scale at inference time, tailoring intervention intensity to each input.
This addresses a complementary challenge, input-dependent dosing, whereas our work targets the composition of multiple attributes without directional interference; the two mechanisms are compatible in principle.

Multi-attribute composition.

Steering multiple attributes at once remains relatively underexplored:
24
found that naive vector addition is “largely unsuccessful” as the number of attributes grows.
Existing remedies include heuristic vector summation
(
20
, CONFST;)
, gradient-guided classifiers
(
14
, K-Steering;)
, and learned composition operators
(
13
, MAT-STEER;)
,
(
8
, MSRS;)
,
(
18
, CST;)
.
Learned methods must be retrained whenever the attribute set changes, limiting flexibility.

ORBIT addresses multi-attribute composition as a closed-form geometric problem: it constructs a joint subspace via SVD and steers through norm-preserving rotation, requiring no training and generalizing to new attribute combinations without retraining.
We evaluate against CAA and K-Steering (§
6
): PIXEL is limited to a single attribute with sample-specific calibration, while MAT-STEER, MSRS, and CST each require training composition-specific parameters tied to a fixed attribute set.
An extended discussion is provided in Appendix
A
.

3
Background

This section focuses on the components directly used by ORBIT: CAA-style vector extraction and Angular Steering geometry.

3.1
Problem formulation

Let
f_{\theta}
be an autoregressive language model with hidden states
\mathbf{h}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
at layer
\ell
and sequence position
t
.
Given a target set of behavioral attributes
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{K}\}
, multi-attribute steering seeks to produce modified hidden states
\mathbf{h}^{\prime}_{\ell}
at inference time, without retraining, such that subsequent outputs simultaneously reflect all
K
attributes while remaining fluent and coherent.

3.2
Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA)

CAA
(
16
)
uses a contrastive mean vector per attribute and adds weighted vectors at inference time:

\mathbf{v}_{k}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\mathbf{h}^{(i)}_{\ell,+}-\mathbf{h}^{(i)}_{\ell,-}\right),\qquad\mathbf{h}^{\prime}_{\ell}=\mathbf{h}_{\ell}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\,\mathbf{v}_{k}.

(1)

For a single attribute, this reduces to
\mathbf{h}^{\prime}_{\ell}=\mathbf{h}_{\ell}+\alpha\,\mathbf{v}_{k}
.
Two problems emerge.
Without normalization, attributes with larger
\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\|
dominate; with normalization, the
\alpha_{k}
values become entangled because
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{i}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{j}\neq 0
in general, so the effective contribution of each attribute depends on all others, turning calibration into a combinatorial problem.

3.3
Angular Steering

Angular Steering
(
25
)
recasts single-attribute steering as a
rotation
within a 2D subspace spanned by the feature direction
\hat{\mathbf{v}}
and the activation’s orthogonal residual.
The rotation angle controls steering strength continuously, the activation norm is preserved exactly, and the framework subsumes CAA (small angle
\approx
additive perturbation) and directional ablation (
\theta=\pi
) as special cases.
It is training-free but limited to a single attribute.
The question we address is: how should
K
such rotations be composed without interference?

3.4
Geometric limitations of additive composition

A core assumption behind CAA-style multi-attribute steering is that independently extracted vectors can be summed,
\mathbf{v}_{\text{joint}}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\alpha_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}
, but Figure
1
shows why this is unstable in practice.
In
TraitFactory
(Figure
1
a), per-trait norm scales vary substantially across layers and the dominant trait shifts with depth, so larger vectors can dominate the sum.
At layer 14 on tones (Figure
1
b), pairwise angles show both antagonism (
concise
–
expert
,
\cos=-0.45
) and redundancy (
casual
–
empathetic
,
\cos=0.46
), so additive composition can either cancel or over-amplify directions.
Hence, as
K
grows, both the magnitude and orientation of the summed vector become hard to control, motivating the joint rotation formulation in §
4.2
(additional diagnostics is provided in Appendix
J
).

:

); norm ratios reach

at layer 7,

at layer 21, and the dominant trait changes with depth.

); pairs range from antagonistic to redundant.

4
Method: ORBIT

4.1
Motivation and core geometric insight

Angular Steering provides a principled, norm-preserving rotation for a single attribute, but extending this framework to multiple attributes is nontrivial: independently estimated directions can conflict, making naive composition increasingly unstable as
K
grows.

ORBIT resolves this by replacing separate local operations with a single rotation in a joint attribute subspace, preserving the orthogonal complement exactly while remaining fully training-free (no additional training or retraining when attributes change).
Empirically, this unified formulation yields more balanced control than naive composition baselines (Appendix
H
), with results in §
6
.

4.2
The ORBIT algorithm

At a high level, ORBIT has a setup stage and a decoding stage: it prepares per-attribute planes in setup, then during decoding computes per-attribute misalignment gates, forms a target direction from the active attributes, rotates within the resulting steering subspace (which may reduce to a single-attribute plane), and reconstructs the full activation.
Figure
2
illustrates this pipeline for the representative case
K=2
; the same sequence applies to any
K
.
Unless noted otherwise, we use one fixed configuration across tasks, and the additive boost is an optional post-rotation component.

Figure 2
:
K{=}2
steering pipeline in ORBIT.

A)
Trait planes are merged by SVD into a shared orthonormal basis
\mathbf{E}
.

B)
Continuous gates from signed projections determine pass-through, single-trait, or joint steering.

C)
In the joint case,
\mathbf{h}
is decomposed, rotated toward target
\mathbf{t}
in the subspace, optionally boosted, and reconstructed while preserving
\mathbf{h}_{\perp}
.

Attribute plane extraction.

For each attribute
a_{k}
, we construct a 2D steering plane following Angular Steering
(
25
)
.
The first axis
\mathbf{b}_{1}^{(k)}
reuses the contrastive mean direction from Eq.
1
(
\mathbf{b}_{1}^{(k)}=\mathbf{v}_{k}
) and represents attribute polarity (increasing vs. decreasing the trait).
The second axis
\mathbf{b}_{2}^{(k)}
is the first PCA direction of paired differences
(\mathbf{h}_{+}^{(i)}-\mathbf{h}_{-}^{(i)})
, orthogonalized and normalized against
\mathbf{b}_{1}^{(k)}
; it captures dominant residual variation beyond the mean shift.
Together,
(\mathbf{b}_{1}^{(k)},\mathbf{b}_{2}^{(k)})
defines the per-attribute plane used for steering.

Step 1: Joint subspace construction.

The goal of this step is to build one shared orthonormal subspace where all selected attributes can be steered together with a single rotation.
Because axes from different attributes can overlap, the collected directions are not generally orthogonal.
We therefore concatenate the normalized per-attribute axes, extract an orthonormal basis
\mathbf{E}
for their span, and drop directions that are numerically negligible.
This keeps the common steering geometry while automatically removing redundant directions.

Step 2: Per-token adaptive gating.

To avoid unnecessary edits, we apply steering only where a token opposes the target attributes.
For each attribute
k
, we measure token alignment via the signed projection onto its axis:

s_{k}=\mathbf{h}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{b}}_{1}^{(k)}.

(2)

We then use a zero-margin linear gate:

g_{k}=\max(0,-s_{k}).

(3)

Thus, aligned tokens (
s_{k}\geq 0
) receive no intervention, while opposing tokens (
s_{k}<0
) are steered with strength proportional to the magnitude of opposition.
This focuses intervention on misaligned regions and preserves tokens that already express the desired behavior.

Step 3: Combined target direction.

To resolve multi-attribute trade-offs at each token, we first form a gate-weighted aggregate direction:

\mathbf{r}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}g_{k}\,\hat{\mathbf{b}}_{1}^{(k)}.

(4)

If no attribute is active (equivalently,
\|\mathbf{r}\|=0
), the token is passed through unchanged.
Otherwise, we set
\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{r}/\|\mathbf{r}\|
and use it as the steering target.
This keeps control token-specific, emphasizing whichever attributes are currently most misaligned rather than enforcing fixed global weights.

The same construction supports suppression: negating an attribute’s polarity axis,
\hat{\mathbf{b}}_{1}^{(k)}\to-\hat{\mathbf{b}}_{1}^{(k)}
(a signed weight
w_{k}=-1
in Algorithm
1
), steers the target away from that attribute, so ORBIT can raise one attribute while suppressing another (Appendix
F.6
).

Step 4: Subspace decomposition and full-alignment update.

Our objective here is to modify only the attribute-relevant part of each token activation, while preserving non-attribute content.
For a token activation
\mathbf{h}
, we decompose:

\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{in}}+\mathbf{h}_{\perp},\quad\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{in}}=\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\top}\mathbf{h},\quad\mathbf{h}_{\perp}=\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{in}}.

(5)

Here,
\mathbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
is the orthonormal basis matrix from Step 1, while
\mathcal{E}=\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{E})
is the subspace itself.
Thus,
\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{in}}
is the component of
\mathbf{h}
inside
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathbf{h}_{\perp}
is the orthogonal remainder that we keep unchanged.
Since
\mathbf{t}
is already in the steering subspace in practice, we directly align
\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{in}}
to
\mathbf{t}
while preserving its magnitude:

\mathbf{h}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{in}}=\|\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{in}}\|\,\frac{\mathbf{t}}{\|\mathbf{t}\|}.

(6)

Step 5: Reconstruction.

We form the final steered activation by combining the updated in-subspace component with the preserved orthogonal component:

\mathbf{h}^{\prime}=\mathbf{h}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{in}}+\mathbf{h}_{\perp}.

(7)

Since
\mathbf{h}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{in}}
is norm-preserving and
\mathbf{h}_{\perp}
is unchanged from Step 4, this reconstruction preserves activation norm while steering only attribute-relevant directions.

Optional additive boost.

The rotation in Step 4 reorients only existing in-subspace mass.
When a token has weak or opposing trait projection, this can yield a small effective update.
To strengthen steering in this regime, we optionally apply a small additive in-plane shift after rotation.

For each attribute
k
, we construct a boost direction from contrastive data by taking the mean positive–negative activation gap and projecting it onto the attribute plane
P_{k}=\mathrm{span}(\hat{\mathbf{b}}_{1}^{(k)},\hat{\mathbf{b}}_{2}^{(k)})
:

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}=\text{proj}_{P_{k}}\!\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\mathbf{h}^{(i)}_{+}-\mathbf{h}^{(i)}_{-}\right)\right).

(8)

This gives a data-driven in-plane direction that separates positive and negative trait examples on average.

In our current setup, boost reuses the same adaptive token gate as rotation (Step 2): tokens that are already aligned receive no boost, while misaligned tokens receive larger updates.
The post-rotation update produces the boosted in-plane component:

\tilde{\mathbf{h}}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{in}}=\mathbf{h}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{in}}+\alpha_{\text{boost}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}g_{k}\,\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}.

(9)

Applying boost after rotation prevents one attribute’s additive shift from interfering with another’s rotation.
The final reconstruction becomes
\mathbf{h}^{\prime}=\tilde{\mathbf{h}}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{in}}+\mathbf{h}_{\perp}
.

Intervention points.

Following Angular Steering, we intervene at normalization outputs immediately before Attention and MLP in each transformer block (two intervention points per block), and apply steering at all such points across layers.
At each autoregressive decoding step, only the current response token (the last sequence position) is modified.

Theoretical properties and complexity analysis are deferred to Appendix
B
.

5
Experimental setup

Datasets and models.

We evaluate on two multi-attribute benchmarks.

ToneBank

(
14
)
contains 1,184 prompts across 18 categories, each answerable in 5 conversational tones (
Expert
,
Empathetic
,
Cautious
,
Casual
,
Concise
).

TraitFactory
(ours) targets deeper behavioral dispositions with 1,077 prompts across 22 categories and 13 personality traits; following
4
, each trait uses 5 positive and 5 negative prompt variants.
We experiment on a subset of 5 traits,
deception
,
evil
,
humorous
,
empathy
, and
sycophantic
, spanning diverse behavioral axes.
For both benchmarks we evaluate all
\binom{5}{K}
combinations for
K=1,2,3
using the same contrastive data across methods.
Dataset details, together with trait-separability and question-diversity analyses, appear in Appendix
C
.
We evaluate on Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct
(
22
)
, and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
(
6
)
, covering two model families and two parameter scales.

Baselines.

All methods share the same
\alpha
calibration and layer selection.
We compare against: Unsteered (no intervention), CAA (§
3.2
), K-Steering
(
14
)
, and four naive geometric composition variants (Appendix
H
).
We additionally compare against the trained-composition method
Mat-Steer

(
13
)
at a fixed attribute set (Appendix
F.2
).

Evaluation.

GPT-4o-Mini rates each steered response on a 0–10 scale per target attribute and assigns a coherence score (0–100); full prompts are presented in Appendix
K
.
We additionally cross-validate all scores with a second, independent judge in Appendix
I.1
.
To penalize attribute dominance, we report the
geometric gain
, which is the signed geometric mean of per-attribute improvements
\Delta_{k}=\text{score}^{\text{steered}}_{k}-\text{score}^{\text{unsteered}}_{k}
:

\text{GeoGain}=\operatorname{sign}\!\left(\textstyle\prod_{k}\Delta_{k}\right)\cdot\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\ln|\Delta_{k}|\right).

(10)

A positive value requires
all
attributes to improve; any single regression drives it toward zero.
We also report the
joint success rate
, the fraction of targeted attributes that improve, averaged across examples:

\text{SuccessRate}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{1}[\Delta_{k}^{(i)}>0].

(11)

\alpha
calibration.

For CAA and K-Steering,
\alpha
is calibrated per attribute combination via binary search: the largest value maintaining coherence is selected
(
14
)
.
ORBIT’s rotation has no steering coefficient and is parameter-free; the only calibrated quantity is a single scalar boost magnitude per combination, far smaller in scope than CAA’s per-attribute coefficient or K-Steering’s trained classifier, and the boost is itself optional (Figure
3
; Appendix
E
).
ORBIT’s results are robust to this magnitude and to the number of contrastive pairs used (Appendix
G
).

General capabilities.

To verify preservation of general model capabilities, we also evaluate on
TinyBench
, which includes ARC-C, GSM8k, HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA, and WinoGrande.

Steering effectiveness.

Table

summarizes judge scores, coherence, and geometric gain across all models, datasets, and values of

ORBIT achieves the highest judge score in the majority of configurations and maintains coherence within a few points of the unsteered baseline, whereas CAA consistently degrades coherence.
The advantage is most visible on

and the margin widens as

grows.
K-Steering scores near baseline on

at

The advantage also holds against the trained-composition method

at a fixed attribute set (Appendix

and

(Appendix

Balanced multi-attribute control.

The geometric gain columns expose a failure mode hidden by mean scores: attribute dominance, where one trait improves at the expense of others.
ORBIT is the only method that maintains positive geometric gain at every configuration, confirming that all targeted attributes improve simultaneously.
CAA and K-Steering frequently turn negative at
K{\geq}2
; K-Steering’s gain on
TraitFactory

K{=}3
is essentially zero across all models, while ORBIT remains between
+0.61
and
+1.22
.
Joint success rates (Appendix
F.1
) corroborate this: on
TraitFactory

K{=}2
, ORBIT achieves 81% on Llama-3.2-3B versus 23% for K-Steering.
The complementary minimum per-attribute improvement
\min_{k}\Delta_{k}
(Appendix
F.4
) confirms that even the weakest attribute improves under ORBIT, while it degrades under the baselines.
Naive geometric composition variants also fail to steer more than one attribute (Appendix
H
).

Role of additive boost.

We ablate three configurations:
rotation only
(no additive component),
fixed boost
(an in-plane shift with
\alpha_{\text{boost}}{=}1
applied uniformly after rotation), and
calibrated boost
(the shift is scaled by the adaptive gate
g_{k}
, so aligned tokens receive little boost while misaligned tokens receive more).
Rotation alone preserves coherence but produces limited steering when tokens have weak in-subspace projection.
Fixed boost increases scores but can degrade coherence at higher
K
.
Calibrated boost strikes the best balance, achieving the strongest scores at
K{=}1{-}2
on both datasets while preserving output quality (Figure
3
); a sweep over boost magnitude (Appendix
G.3
) shows ORBIT has a wide, stable operating range. For ablation on calibration effect, please refer to Appendix
G.2
.

Across all benchmarks and values of

).

.

7
Discussion

Why rotation outperforms addition on resistant traits.

Additive injections compound in magnitude across layers and degrade coherence, so in practice they are applied at only a few layers.
Rotation, being norm-preserving, can instead be applied at every layer.
We think this sustained, norm-stable intervention is why ORBIT steers resistant traits like deception and evil that CAA and K-Steering barely move.

Computational cost.

The cost of ORBIT is roughly twice the cost of CAA and an order of magnitude cheaper than K-Steering, which requires per-token backpropagation through its classifier (Appendix
B
).

Limitations.

Similar to prior contrastive methods, ORBIT represents each attribute with a linear steering direction, so attributes whose activation structure is strongly non-linear may not be fully captured by this approximation.
On the evaluation side, although we steer up to
K{=}5
attributes (§
6
, Appendix
F.3
), scaling to substantially larger attribute sets is left to future work.

8
Conclusion

We presented ORBIT, a training-free method for steering multiple behavioral attributes at inference time.
Rather than summing per-attribute vectors, ORBIT constructs a single joint subspace via SVD and applies one norm-preserving rotation within it, guided by per-token adaptive gating.
This design avoids the cancellation, norm inflation, and order dependence that destabilize naive composition as the number of attributes grows.
Across two benchmarks, three models, and up to
K{=}5
attributes, ORBIT consistently outperforms baselines on judge scores, geometric gain, and joint success rate while preserving coherence and general capabilities; through signed weights, it further supports antagonistic control, raising one attribute while suppressing another.
Because it requires no training and generalizes to new attribute combinations without retraining, ORBIT offers a simple, flexible, and powerful mechanism for multi-attribute behavioral control.

Appendix A
Extended related work

A.1
From alignment to inference-time control

The dominant approach to shaping LLM behavior, RLHF
[
15
,
2
]
, Constitutional AI
[
3
]
, and instruction tuning, bakes a fixed behavioral profile into model weights.
This is powerful but rigid: the resulting model cannot easily shift between behavioral modes at deployment time.
Worse, fine-tuning on new tasks can erode existing alignment
[
17
]
or cause catastrophic forgetting
[
11
]
.
These limitations motivate
inference-time
alternatives that modify behavior without updating parameters.

A.2
Single-attribute activation steering

ROME
[
12
]
demonstrated that rank-one weight modifications can edit factual associations, establishing that behavioral information is localized in activation space.
PPLM
[
5
]
introduced gradient-guided activation steering via an attribute classifier, but requires per-token gradient computation and a trained classifier.

Contrastive methods reduced the overhead.
Activation Addition
[
23
, ActAdd;]
extracts a steering vector from a single contrastive prompt pair and adds it at one layer.
Inference-Time Intervention
[
10
, ITI;]
identifies informative attention heads via logistic regression probes; Representation Engineering
[
31
, RepE;]
extracts feature directions through PCA over contrastive embeddings.
CAA
[
16
]
computes a difference-of-means vector over contrastive pairs, added to activations with a single scalar coefficient.
AXBench
[
27
]
showed that such linear baselines can match or outperform sparse autoencoders for single-attribute steering
[
26
,
19
]
; the reliability of contrastive directions has also been studied directly
[
21
]
.

Geometric methods formulate steering in terms of activation-space transformations.
Directional ablation
[
1
]
removes a behavioral direction via Householder reflection.
Angular Steering
[
25
]
generalizes this by formulating steering as a continuous, norm-preserving rotation in a 2D subspace, with CAA (small angle) and directional ablation (
\theta=\pi
) as special cases.
Spherical Steering
[
28
]
extends this with geodesic interpolation and confidence-based gating.
All of these methods operate on a single attribute.

A.3
Non-linear probing and gradient-based steering

Evidence that activation space has non-linear structure
[
9
,
7
]
suggests that linear methods may leave performance on the table.

30
showed that non-linear probe gradients can steer model behavior, a technique that K-Steering
[
14
]
formalized into a multi-label gradient-guided framework with an MLP classifier.
These approaches capture richer representational structure but require training, creating a tension between expressiveness and flexibility.

A.4
Multi-attribute composition

The multi-attribute problem has received growing attention.

24
conducted the first systematic study and concluded that naive vector addition for multiple attributes is “largely unsuccessful”, a finding that directly motivates our work.

Additive multi-attribute composition.

CAA
[
16
]
sums per-attribute mean-difference vectors with a scalar coefficient.
K-Steering
[
14
]
avoids linearity through gradient guidance but ties the classifier to a fixed attribute configuration and requires classifier training.
CONFST
[
20
]
selects per-attribute vectors via Bayesian confidence and averages them, again a linear combination.
These methods compose additively, making them susceptible to norm imbalance and directional cancellation as
K
grows.

Multi-attribute steering with learned composition.

MAT-STEER
[
13
]
addresses multi-attribute conflicts through learned per-token gating functions and orthogonality regularization, requiring a gradient-based training phase.
MSRS
[
8
]
decomposes multi-attribute steering into shared and attribute-specific subspaces via SVD, then learns projection and gating parameters through representation fine-tuning.
Compositional Steering Tokens
[
18
]
learns explicit composition operators via self-distillation, requiring training for each attribute combination.

Per-sample adaptive steering.

PIXEL
[
29
]
computes sample-specific steering vectors and adaptive scaling at inference time, achieving fine-grained per-input control at the cost of additional forward passes per sample.
This operates along a different axis, input-dependent intensity, whereas ORBIT addresses the orthogonal challenge of composing multiple attributes without directional interference.

Appendix B
Theoretical properties and complexity

Reduction to Angular Steering for
K=1
.

When
K=1
, the basis matrix
\mathbf{V}=[\hat{\mathbf{b}}_{1},\hat{\mathbf{b}}_{2}]
has rank 2, and the SVD produces
\mathbf{E}=[\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2}]
spanning the same 2D plane.
The target reduces to
\mathbf{t}=\hat{\mathbf{b}}_{1}
, and the rotation within
\mathcal{E}
is exactly the Angular Steering rotation.
ORBIT is thus a strict generalization: it adds multi-attribute capability without sacrificing single-attribute behavior.

Behavior across inter-attribute geometries.

The subspace dimension
m
provides a lens into how ORBIT handles different geometric configurations.
When attribute directions are mutually orthogonal,
m=2K
and the rotation simultaneously aligns with all attributes independently, the ideal case where no trade-offs are needed.
When directions are correlated (e.g., “expert” and “formal” share a high cosine similarity),
m<2K
and the SVD automatically merges their shared variance, effectively steering a reduced set of independent factors.
When directions oppose, the adaptive gating resolves the conflict: per-token gate strengths
g_{k}
weight each attribute by its actual misalignment, so the combined target
\mathbf{t}
naturally prioritizes whichever attributes are currently under-represented in the activation, in contrast to parallel sum, which blindly sums opposing deltas.

Complexity.

Table
2
reports the inference cost of each method
per generated token
.

CAA
adds a weighted sum of
K
steering vectors to the activation,
\mathbf{h}^{\prime}=\mathbf{h}+\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}\mathbf{v}_{k}
. Each
\mathbf{v}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, so scaling and accumulating one vector is
O(d)
and the full sum is
O(Kd)
.

K-Steering
takes
S
gradient steps per token, each a forward and backward pass through its three-layer MLP classifier. The pass cost is dominated by the layer multiplications:
dD
for the input layer mapping the
d
-dimensional activation into the
D
-dimensional hidden layer,
D^{2}
for the hidden-to-hidden layer, and
DK
for the output layer producing the
K
attribute logits. Multiplying by the
S
steps gives
O\!\big(S(dD+D^{2}+DK)\big)
. This per-token backpropagation, on top of a separately trained classifier, is what makes it roughly an order of magnitude costlier than the others.

ORBIT
has two per-token components. Gating computes a signed projection
s_{k}=\mathbf{h}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{b}}_{1}^{(k)}
for each of the
K
attributes, an
O(d)
dot product each, hence
O(Kd)
. The subspace projection
\mathbf{h}_{\text{in}}=\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\top}\mathbf{h}
with
\mathbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
costs
O(md)
(reconstruction is the same order). Together this is
O(Kd+md)
, with
m\leq 2K\ll d
, so ORBIT is CAA’s cost plus a small
O(md)
projection term, only a constant factor more and far below K-Steering since it uses matrix multiplications rather than gradient steps.

The SVD that builds the basis
\mathbf{E}
from
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 2K}
costs
O(K^{2}d)
, but it runs once per attribute set, so it does not appear in the per-token column. The
Setup
column records how each method prepares its steering directions: CAA and ORBIT compute their vectors and subspace directly from contrastive activations with no training, whereas K-Steering must train an MLP classifier by gradient descent before steering, and must repeat this training if the attribute set changes.

Appendix C
Dataset details

C.1
ToneBank

ToneBank

[
14
]
contains 1,184 tone-agnostic questions across 18 categories, designed so that each question can be meaningfully answered in any of the 5 target tones without prompt bias.
The generation prompt template (one per category):

“You are generating tone-agnostic questions for a dataset. Generate exactly [N] diverse, high-quality questions that fall under the category: [CATEGORY]. Create questions that could be meaningfully answered in multiple different tones, such as: helpful, expert, casual, cautious, empathetic, concise. Each question should be tone-neutral (able to be answered well in any of the tones). Return the result as a JSON object with a single field ‘questions’.”

Contrastive pair construction.

For each of the 5 tone labels, we define a positive and negative system prompt pair.
Table
3
gives abbreviated versions (full prompts in our code release).
These system prompts are prepended to each attribute-agnostic question and fed through the
target model itself
(e.g., Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct) to generate contrastive response pairs.
Specifically, the pipeline:

1.

Formats each question as a chat-template message with the positive (or negative) system prompt.

2.

Generates up to 96 tokens from the target model with sampling (temperature-based).

3.

Records the hidden-state activations at every LayerNorm output at the last prompt token position (before the first generated token).

4.

Computes
\mathbf{b}_{1}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}(\mathbf{h}^{(i)}_{+}-\mathbf{h}^{(i)}_{-})
per LayerNorm module.

5.

Computes
\mathbf{b}_{2}^{(k)}
as PC0 of the centered paired differences, orthogonalized against
\mathbf{b}_{1}^{(k)}
.

Up to 150 contrastive pairs per tone are used, stratified across prompt categories.
The same contrastive response texts are also used to train the K-Steering classifier and to compute CAA vectors (from decoder-layer outputs instead of LayerNorm outputs).

C.2

Appendix H
Naive composition and the role of norm preservation

ORBIT combines two ingredients: norm-preserving rotation and a jointly constructed subspace.
To isolate the contribution of the joint subspace, we compare ORBIT against naive strategies that use the
same
per-attribute angular steering planes but combine them in simple ways.
Each strategy applies single-attribute Angular Steering rotations and differs only in how the per-attribute updates are merged:

•

Parallel Sum / Mean
: Compute each attribute’s rotated activation independently, take the per-attribute delta
\Delta_{k}=\mathbf{h}^{\prime}_{k}-\mathbf{h}
, and add all deltas (sum) or their average (mean) to the original activation.

•

Sequential Full
: Apply full Angular Steering rotations one attribute at a time in cascade; the output of attribute
k
becomes the input for attribute
k{+}1
. The result depends on application order.

•

Sequential Perpendicular
: Like Sequential Full, but after rotating for attribute
k
, only the component of the update perpendicular to the previous attribute’s plane is kept, attempting to reduce cross-attribute interference.

Every variant is norm-preserving per rotation, exactly like ORBIT, but none constructs a joint subspace; they therefore isolate the effect of the subspace construction from that of norm preservation.

H.1

We first evaluate at
K{=}2
on Llama-3.2-3B with empathy and sycophantic as targets (Figure
8
, Table
15
).
All naive variants improve empathy modestly but fail entirely on sycophantic (scores indistinguishable from the baseline
{\approx}2.0
), and sequential methods exhibit clear order dependence: Sequential Full (E
{\to}
S) matches the baseline exactly while (S
{\to}
E) shifts toward empathy—the last attribute in the cascade dominates.
ORBIT is the only method that moves both attributes simultaneously (empathy 8.75, sycophantic 3.44) while maintaining coherence (89.7).
The baselines reach coherence comparable to ORBIT but much lower target scores: norm preservation explains the
coherence
they retain, but only joint-subspace steering explains ORBIT’s
