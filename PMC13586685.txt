Tamibarotene with venetoclax and azacitidine in RARA+ acute myeloid leukemia: results from the SY-1425-202 study
Authors: Thomas Cluzeau, Uma Borate, Christine McMahon, Pierre Fenaux, Mael Heiblig, Pierre Peterlin, Brian Ball, Sylvain Chantepie, Alireza Eghtedar, Suman Kambhampati, Melhem Solh, Jose Torregrosa-Diaz, Thorsten Braun, James Dugan, Jonathan Feld, Marie-Pierre Gourin, Arnaud Pigneux, Jean-Baptiste Robin, Gary Schiller, Eytan Stein, David Roth, Michael Kelly, Daniel Pollyea, Stephane de Botton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•TAMI added to VEN/AZA was well tolerated but did not improve efficacy in RARA+ AML, and the study was stopped early for futility.•RARA positivity alone may not predict benefit from RARα-targeted therapy in this setting.

Introduction

Venetoclax (VEN) with azacitidine (AZA) is the standard of care in older/unfit patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML).1, 2, 3 AZA plus VEN became the standard of care for patients with AML ineligible for intensive chemotherapy in 2020. Using this regimen, complete remission (CR)/CR with incomplete hematologic recovery (CRi) rate was 66.4%, and median overall survival (OS) was 14.7 months. Unfortunately, the majority of patients relapse, and specific biological subtypes, including monocytic AML and mutations in KRAS, NRAS, FLT3-ITD, and TP53, are associated with inferior outcomes after VEN-based therapy.4, 5, 6

Recently, a superenhancer at the retinoic acid receptor α (RARA) gene was found to be associated with RARA mRNA overexpression and was present in ∼30% of AML cases.7 Preclinical models demonstrated that RARA overexpression impaired differentiation and triggered immortalization.8,9 A high CR rate and the rapid onset of response were observed in unfit patients with RARA+ newly diagnosed AML treated with tamibarotene10 (TAMI)/VEN, with responses observed irrespective of cytogenetic risk groups or mutation profiles. Importantly, RARA is expressed at high levels in AML blasts with a monocytic phenotype that is associated with VEN resistance in AML. Furthermore, ∼80% of the unfit patients with RARA+ newly diagnosed AML included in the trial have a monocytic phenotype (which includes lower BCL2 and higher MCL1 expression) associated with VEN resistance or shorter duration of response.11

Therefore, the SY-1425-202 study evaluated whether adding TAMI to AZA plus VEN could further improve outcomes in RARA+ AML to overcome features associated with primary or secondary resistance to VEN. We hypothesized that the activation of RARA signaling with TAMI could overcome resistance to VEN-based therapy in RARA+ AML.

Methods

Patients

Key eligibility criteria included age ≥18 years and a confirmed diagnosis of previously untreated AML according to World Health Organization classification.12,13 Patients were considered to be ineligible for standard induction therapy if they were aged ≥75 years or if they had ≥1 of the following coexisting conditions precluding intensive chemotherapy: a history of congestive heart failure for which treatment was warranted or an ejection fraction of 50%, a diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide of 65%, an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status score of 3, creatinine clearance ≥30 mL/min to <45 mL/min based on the Cockcroft-Gault glomerular filtration rate estimation, hepatic impairment with total bilirubin >1.5 to ≤3.0 × upper limit of normal, and any other comorbidity that the investigator judged to be incompatible with intensive chemotherapy and reviewed and approved by the sponsor. Patients had to be positive for RARA gene overexpression based on a quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction investigational assay performed at a central laboratory. This test was positive or negative and not used quantitatively. Full eligibility criteria are in the supplemental Appendix (Protocol).

RARA assay

RARA expression was assessed using a centralized quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction assay. Although the assay generated quantitative results, it was used in a binary manner (positive vs negative) for eligibility. RARA positivity had to be confirmed by cycle 1, day 8 to allow continuation or randomization in the study. Detailed assay thresholds and the distribution of expression levels were not available for analysis.

Study design

SY-1425-202 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04905407) was a phase 2, open-label, 3-part, multicenter study evaluating TAMI/VEN/AZA combination therapy in previously untreated patients with RARA+ non–acute promyelocytic leukemia AML who were unlikely to tolerate standard intensive chemotherapy at the time of study entry (Figure 1). Patients could initiate VEN/AZA prior to confirmation of RARA status, and randomization occurred after confirmation of RARA positivity, up to cycle 1, day 8. The trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Good Clinical Practice guidelines of the International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use. The study was conducted at 33 sites. Trial safety was monitored by an independent data monitoring committee. Institutional review board approval was received at all clinical sites.

Study schematic. C1, cycle 1; D1, day 1.

This study evaluated the addition of TAMI, a targeted therapy for RARA+ patients with AML who were not candidates for treatment with intensive chemotherapy, to the established VEN/AZA regimen. The design of the study followed an accepted approach in initiating the study of a novel combination in that it began with a small cohort of participants in part 1 to identify any safety issues and determine a tolerable regimen of TAMI in combination with VEN/AZA before proceeding to part 2, in which a larger number of participants (∼80) were to be treated. Part 2 was structured to compare the clinical activity and characterize the safety of TAMI/VEN/AZA with that of VEN/AZA through a 1:1 randomization of ∼80 participants between the 2 treatment arms. Part 3 was designed to explore the activity of adding TAMI to VEN/AZA in participants who did not respond to VEN/AZA, with participants subsequently followed for response.

End points

Participants were assessed for the primary end point of CR/CRi from the time of the index date, defined as the earlier of the date of randomization (cycle 1, day 1) until the initiation of non–study drug therapy or until time of disease progression/relapse in part 2 of the study. Participants were also assessed for the secondary end points of overall response rate (ORR). For the part 1 Response-Evaluable (RE) analysis set and the part 2 modified intent-to-treat (mITT) analysis set, the number (%) of participants and the 95% exact binomial confidence interval (CIs) of the percentage of overall responders and non–overall responders (along with criteria for nonresponse), based on the Clopper-Pearson method, were summarized. The number (%) of participants and the 95% exact binomial CIs for the individual and combined rates of CR, CRi, CR with partial hematologic recovery (CRh), morphologically leukemia-free state (MLFS), and partial response were also summarized. The CR and CR/CRh rates were analyzed separately using the same statistical methodologies applied to the primary efficacy end point for the mITT analysis set. The duration of CR, CR/CRi, and CR/CRh was estimated using Kaplan-Meier product-limit estimates and presented in separate summaries by treatment group for the mITT analysis set and for participants who were responders. Time to CR, CR/CRi, and CR/CRh was summarized separately using descriptive statistics by treatment group and only for participants who were responders to the mITT analysis set. A sensitivity analysis based on the RE population was also conducted for time to CR/CRi.

Statistical analysis

A prespecified interim futility analysis based on CR/CRi rate was conducted. The study was terminated after meeting futility criteria, indicating a low probability of demonstrating superiority of the investigational arm. This interim Clinical Study Report (CSR) presents initial safety and efficacy data from a data cutoff based on a prespecified interim futility analysis of the CR/CRi rate. Aside from the futility analysis, no formal treatment comparisons or P values were generated, and limited efficacy analyses were performed. There is no formal statistical hypothesis for this prespecified interim analysis. In part 2, the null hypothesis to be tested was whether the CR/CRi rate after treatment with TAMI/VEN/AZA was the same as the CR/CRi rate after treatment with VEN/AZA in the RARA+ participants with previously untreated AML who were ineligible for standard induction therapy. The alternate hypothesis to be tested was whether the CR/CRi rate after treatment with TAMI/VEN/AZA was higher than the CR/CRi rate after treatment with VEN/AZA in RARA+ participants with previously untreated AML who were ineligible for standard induction therapy. In parts 1 and 3, there was no formal statistical hypothesis. The CR/CRi rate was evaluated in the intent-to-treat (ITT) population (patient populations are defined in the supplemental Appendix). CR/CRi rate and 95% exact binomial CIs were calculated by treatment group. The stratified Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test was applied to compare the CR rates between the 2 treatment groups. The point estimate of the difference of the proportions for CR rate between the 2 treatment groups was provided along with 95% CIs. OS was to be evaluated in the ITT population. Because CR differences were not significant, the planned interim analysis for OS was not performed; consequently, no statistical testing could be performed on the secondary end points, and the P values provided are descriptive only. The safety analysis set included all participants. The statistical analysis plan is provided in the supplemental Material.

Ethics statement

This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All adult participants provided written informed consent to participate in this study.

Results

Patients

A total of 160 participants were screened for this study, with an additional 180 participants who were considered prescreen failures (ie, prescreened participants for RARA evaluation who did not sign the main informed consent form). Randomization occurred after confirmation of RARA positivity within cycle 1. In part 1, 10 participants were enrolled to receive TAMI/VEN/AZA. At the time of the data cutoff, none of the participants were on study treatment. In part 2, 25 participants randomized to TAMI/VEN/AZA received study treatment. At the time of the data cutoff, 10 participants were on study treatment, and 15 discontinued the study treatment. Eight participants discontinued from the study. Twenty-six participants randomized to VEN/AZA received study treatment. At the time of the data cutoff, 8 participants were on study treatment, and 18 discontinued the study treatment. Nine participants discontinued from the study. There were 5 part 2 participants who received VEN/AZA who continued on to part 3 of the study to receive TAMI/VEN/AZA. In part 3, 5 participants were enrolled and received TAMI/VEN/AZA as salvage therapy after treatment failure with VEN/AZA in part 2. At the time of the data cutoff, 3 participants were on study treatment, and 2 discontinued both study treatment and study participation. Demographics and baseline characteristics of patients treated are listed in Table 1. A total of 340 patients were prescreened or screened, including 180 prescreen failures (RARA testing only) and 160 screened patients. Efficacy

All randomized patients who received treatment were included in the modified intent-to-treat population.

Part 1: TAMI/VEN/AZA safety lead-in

In part 1, an ORR of 77.8% was observed in response-evaluable participants who received TAMI/VEN/AZA treatment. This included 5 participants who achieved CR and 2 who achieved CRi.

Part 2: TAMI/VEN/AZA OR VEN/AZA randomized controlled trial

Based on the interim data (Table 2), there were no notable differences in demographic or baseline disease characteristics between the 2 treatment groups, with the exception of a higher percentage of patients in the TAMI/VEN/AZA treatment group with NPM1 mutation (28.0%) than in the VEN/AZA treatment group (3.9%).

The CR/CRi rates were similar in the 2 treatment groups at the time of the interim efficacy analysis in part 2. Futility criteria were met, and the study was then closed to further enrollment. Interim efficacy analyses were performed on the mITT analysis set and are summarized as follows.

The CR/CRi rate was 60.0% (95% CI, 38.7-78.9) in the TAMI/VEN/AZA treatment group and 69.2% (95% CI, 48.2-85.7) in the VEN/AZA treatment group. Among the CR/CRi responders, the median duration of CR/CRi was 293.0 days in the TAMI/VEN/AZA treatment group and 253.0 days in the VEN/AZA treatment group. Among the CR/CRi responders, the median time to CR/CRi was 21.0 days in the TAMI/VEN/AZA treatment group and 24.5 days in the VEN/AZA treatment group.

The CR rate was 48.0% in the TAMI/VEN/AZA treatment group and 57.7% in the VEN/AZA treatment group. Among CR responders, the median duration of CR was 293.0 days in the TAMI/VEN/AZA treatment group and 256.5 days in the VEN/AZA treatment group. Among CR responders, the median time to CR was 21.0 days in the TAMI/VEN/AZA treatment group and 25.0 days in the VEN/AZA treatment group. The ORR was 80.0% (95% CI, 59.3-93.2) in the TAMI/VEN/AZA treatment group and 73.1% (95% CI, 52.2-88.4) in the VEN/AZA treatment group.

Part 3: TAMI/VEN/AZA salvage therapy

The available efficacy data supporting the primary and secondary efficacy end points for this study are presented in this interim CSR. Because part 3 of this study uses exploratory efficacy end points only, limited efficacy results for part 3 are presented. Of the 5 participants enrolled, 2 were classified as overall responders, with the best overall responses of CR and MLFS in 1 participant each.

No clinically meaningful improvement in CR/CRi rate was observed with the addition of TAMI.

Part 1: TAMI/VEN/AZA safety lead-in

Safety data from part 1, safety lead-in, demonstrated acceptable tolerability of the 6 mg TAMI twice daily dosing regimen in combination with VEN/AZA. This dosing regimen was carried forward to parts 2 and 3 of the study. Part 1 safety events are summarized as follows: the most frequently reported treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs; >3 participants) were neutropenia/decreased neutrophil count, leukopenia/ decreased white blood cell count, thrombocytopenia/ decreased platelet count, pneumonia, constipation, decreased appetite, and cough. The most frequently reported grade 3 or 4 TEAEs (>2 participants) were neutropenia/decreased neutrophil count, leukopenia/decreased white blood cell count, thrombocytopenia/decreased platelet count, febrile neutropenia, and pneumonia. The most frequently reported TAMI treatment–related TEAEs (>1 participant) were neutropenia/decreased neutrophil count, decreased appetite, fatigue, myalgia, thrombocytopenia/decreased platelet count, pruritus, and leukopenia/decreased white blood cell count. The most frequently reported serious AEs (SAEs; >1 participant) were neutropenia, febrile neutropenia, and sepsis. Three on-treatment deaths were reported, all of which were attributable to AEs; none were considered by the investigator to be related to TAMI. Tolerability of TAMI/VEN/AZA was observed, with participants receiving up to 12.5 months of treatment and a median study treatment duration of 2.6 months. Five participants met the criteria for potentially clinically significant liver function tests. No participants met the criteria for Hy’s law.

The observed grade 4 adverse events were predominantly expected hematologic toxicities (neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, leukopenia) commonly associated with VEN/AZA. Regarding the 3 grade 5 TEAEs, all were considered related to complications of AML or background therapy and were not considered by investigators to be related to TAMI. The safety review conducted after part 1 concluded that the overall tolerability profile remained acceptable and consistent with expectations for this patient population and backbone regimen, supporting continuation into the randomized portion of the study.

Part 2: TAMI/VEN/AZA OR VEN/AZA randomized controlled trial

A similar proportion of participants in the TAMI/VEN/AZA and VEN/AZA treatment groups experienced TEAEs (Table 5). The rates and severity of hematologic TEAEs were comparable between the treatment groups, with the exception of a higher rate of any grade and grade 3 or 4 thrombocytopenia/decreased platelet count and a higher rate of grade 3 or 4 leukopenia/decreased white blood cell count in the TAMI/VEN/AZA treatment group. A higher number of nonhematologic AEs, including constipation, peripheral edema, diarrhea, weight loss, arthralgia, back pain, and pruritus, were observed in the TAMI/VEN/AZA treatment group. These AEs were primarily grade 1 or grade 2. Pruritus and arthralgia were not reported in participants treated in the VEN/AZA treatment group. Part 2 safety events are summarized as follows: the most frequently reported TEAEs (>25% of participants) were constipation, neutropenia/decreased neutrophil count, diarrhea, nausea, thrombocytopenia/decreased platelet count, peripheral edema, pruritus, and leukopenia/decreased white blood cell count in the TAMI/VEN/AZA treatment group and neutropenia/decreased neutrophil count, nausea, febrile neutropenia, decreased appetite, and dyspnea in the VEN/AZA treatment group. In the TAMI/VEN/AZA treatment group, the most frequently reported grade 3 or 4 TEAEs (>20% of participants) were neutropenia/decreased neutrophil count, thrombocytopenia/decreased platelet count, leukopenia/decreased white blood cell count, anemia/decreased hemoglobin, febrile neutropenia, and sepsis. In the VEN/AZA treatment group, the most frequently reported grade 3 or 4 TEAEs (>20% of participants) were neutropenia/decreased neutrophil count and febrile neutropenia. In the TAMI/VEN/AZA treatment group, the most frequently reported TAMI treatment–related TEAEs (>10% of participants) were leukopenia/decreased white blood cell count, thrombocytopenia/decreased platelet count, anemia/decreased hemoglobin, febrile neutropenia, nausea, and pruritus. The most frequently reported SAE (>2 participants in either treatment group) was febrile neutropenia in both the TAMI/VEN/AZA and VEN/AZA treatment groups. On-treatment deaths were reported in 8.0% of participants in the TAMI/VEN/AZA treatment group, attributable to an AE and progressive disease in 3.9% each. None were considered by the investigator to be related to TAMI. On-treatment deaths were reported in 11.5% of participants in the VEN/AZA treatment group, which were attributable to AEs in 7.7% and to progressive disease in 3.9%. Tolerability of TAMI/VEN/AZA was observed, with participants receiving up to 10.7 months of treatment and a median study treatment duration of 2.2 months in the TAMI/VEN/AZA treatment group. The median study treatment duration in the VEN/AZA treatment group was 2.4 months. Among participants in part 2 of the trial, 41.7% in the TAMI/VEN/AZA treatment group and 14.8% in the VEN/AZA treatment group met criteria for potentially clinically significant liver function tests. No participants met the criteria for Hy’s law.

Part 3: TAMI/VEN/AZA salvage therapy

Safety conclusions for part 3 are limited because of the small number of participants enrolled at the time of the data cutoff for this interim CSR. Part 3 safety events are summarized as follows: the most frequently reported TEAEs (>1 participant) were thrombocytopenia/decreased platelet count, febrile neutropenia, and neutropenia/decreased neutrophil count. the most frequently reported grade 3 or 4 TEAEs (>1 participant) were thrombocytopenia/decreased platelet count, febrile neutropenia, and neutropenia/decreased neutrophil count. TAMI treatment–related TEAEs included alopecia and hypertriglyceridemia/increased blood triglycerides in 1 participant. The only SAE that occurred in >1 participant was febrile neutropenia. Two on-treatment deaths were reported, which were attributable to an AE and progressive disease in 1 participant each. None were considered by the investigator to be related to TAMI. Two participants met criteria for potentially clinically significant liver function tests. No participants met the criteria for Hy’s law.

Discussion

SY-1425-202 was a phase 2, open-label, 3-part, multicenter study evaluating TAMI/VEN/AZA combination therapy in previously untreated patients with RARA+ non–acute promyelocytic leukemia AML who were unlikely to tolerate standard intensive chemotherapy at the time of study entry. This study included a part 1 safety lead-in to inform the dosing regimen of the combination for evaluation in parts 2 and 3 of the study. In part 2, participants were randomized 1:1 to receive either TAMI/VEN/AZA or VEN/AZA, and in part 3, participants who received VEN/AZA in part 2 and who experienced progressive disease, relapse after initial CR or CRi response, or treatment failure received TAMI/VEN/AZA.

This interim CSR presents initial safety and efficacy data from a 31 July 2024 database lock date based on a prespecified interim futility analysis of the CR/CRi rate from a 6 June 2024 data cutoff date. Enrollment in part 1 is complete, and parts 2 and 3 have been closed to further enrollment. A final CSR will be provided at a later date. A total of 61 participants were enrolled (part 1)/randomized (part 2) at the date of data cutoff. The available efficacy and safety data supporting the primary and secondary efficacy end points for this study are presented in this interim CSR.

In part 1, the incidence and types of AEs observed in AML participants who received TAMI/VEN/AZA were generally consistent with what has previously been reported in AML with VEN/AZA (Venclexta US Prescribing Information; Venclyxto Summary of Product Characteristics). The safety profile observed in part 1 confirmed the tolerability of administering 6 mg TAMI twice daily in combination with VEN/AZA in part 2 of the study.

In part 2, a similar proportion of participants in the TAMI/VEN/AZA and VEN/AZA treatment groups experienced TEAEs. The rate and severity of hematologic TEAEs were comparable between treatment groups, with the exception of a higher rate of any grade and grade 3 or 4 thrombocytopenia/decreased platelet count and a higher rate of grade 3 or 4 leukopenia/decreased white blood cell count in the TAMI/VEN/AZA treatment group (dose intensity, duration of cytopenias, and supportive care interventions were not available); however, when evaluated by hematology laboratory abnormalities, there were no notable differences between treatment groups, suggesting that the addition of TAMI to the VEN/AZA treatment regimen does not increase the incidence of hematologic toxicity. These findings are consistent with the mechanism of action of TAMI, which induces differentiation and is not cytotoxic.

A higher number of nonhematologic AEs, including constipation, peripheral edema, diarrhea, weight loss, arthralgia, back pain, and pruritus, were observed in the TAMI/VEN/AZA treatment group. These AEs were primarily grade 1 or 2 and generally consistent with AEs that have been previously observed with TAMI and other retinoids or VEN/AZA1. Pruritus and arthralgia were not reported in participants treated in the VEN/AZA treatment group. These AEs have been reported with use of TAMI and other retinoids (Amnolake package insert; Vesanoid US Prescribing Information). There were no grade 3 or 4 nonhematologic TEAEs that occurred at a higher rate (>10% difference between treatment groups) for participants in the TAMI/VEN/AZA treatment group than in the VEN/AZA treatment group.

Severe and life-threatening TEAEs, SAEs, and TEAEs leading to death occurred at similar rates in participants in the TAMI/VEN/AZA and in the VEN/AZA treatment groups. Grade 3 and 4 TEAEs were predominantly hematologic or infectious, as would be expected in older patients with AML unable to receive standard induction therapy who were treated with VEN/AZA.1 TEAEs leading to dose interruption and dose reduction of both VEN and AZA were comparable between treatment groups, suggesting that TAMI did not affect the tolerability of standard-of-care treatment with VEN/AZA. Across treatment groups, there were no TEAEs that led to the discontinuation of study treatment in >1 participant. Overall, these safety findings indicate that the addition of TAMI to the VEN/AZA treatment regimen was generally well tolerated, with no new safety signals identified.

Interim efficacy data showed a CR/CRi rate of 60.0% (95% CI, 38.7-78.9) for CR/CRi responders in the TAMI/VEN/AZA treatment group and 69.2% (95% CI, 48.2-85.7) for CR/CRi responders in the VEN/AZA treatment group, an ORR of 77.8% in part 1, and 2 of 5 participants achieving a response in part 3.

The results of the interim analysis demonstrated that efficacy was similar between the 2 treatment arms. The study met futility criteria. As such, TAMI/VEN/AZA treatment was unlikely to lead to a significantly higher CR/CRi rate compared to VEN/AZA treatment at the completion of the study, and the study was closed to further enrollment. Longer follow-up could potentially have allowed hematologic recovery in some patients initially classified as MLFS, but this information could not be captured based on the interim analysis. TAMI/VEN/AZA was generally manageable, with increased nonhematologic adverse events compared to VEN/AZA. Of note, the number of patients with features of primary or secondary resistance to VEN/AZA (ie, monocytic phenotype and/or genotype)6 is too low to conclude that the addition of TAMI to VEN/AZA could improve the outcome of these difficult-to-treat patients.

An additional consideration is that the control arm performed better than anticipated at the time of study design. The study was powered assuming a CR/CRi rate of 45% with VEN/AZA based on historical data available when the protocol was developed. However, subsequent studies have consistently reported CR/CRi rates approaching 60% to 70% in similar frontline AML populations. Consequently, the higher-than-expected efficacy of the backbone regimen may have reduced the ability to detect a modest incremental benefit associated with TAMI. This observation should be considered when designing future biomarker-selected studies evaluating novel agents in combination with highly effective VEN-based backbones.

Despite strong preclinical rationale, the activation of RARA signaling did not translate into improved response rates in combination with VEN/AZA. This suggests that RARA overexpression alone may not be sufficient to overcome mechanisms of resistance to VEN-based therapy. The use of a binary classification of RARA expression may have diluted a biologically relevant subset of patients with true dependency on RARA signaling. CR/CRi may not fully capture the effects of differentiation therapies, as suggested by shifts in response categories such as MLFS. The high efficacy of VEN and AZA in this population creates a ceiling effect, limiting the ability to demonstrate incremental benefit. The limitations of the study include the small sample size, early termination, and the absence of OS analysis, correlative studies, monocytic phenotype data, and cytopenia kinetics. Future studies should focus on refined biomarker strategies, including quantitative RARA assessment and integration with molecular and phenotypic features.

Conclusion

In this randomized study, the addition of TAMI to VEN/AZA did not improve response rates in RARA+ AML. Although the combination was generally manageable, these results do not support its use in this setting. The findings highlight the need for improved biomarker selection and a better understanding of RARA biology in AML.
