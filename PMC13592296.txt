White‐Light‐Mediated Imidazole Catalysis Toward Terpene‐Based Thermosets
Authors: Chenxi Zhang, Sandra Olivero, Alice Mija, Véronique Michelet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLight‐mediated curing of terpene‐derived polymers generally depends on UV irradiation and photoinitiators. We report herein that environmentally friendly limonene‐ and carvone‐derived diepoxides cure with glutaric anhydride under white light through imidazole‐mediated catalysis, without conventional photoinitiators, to afford fully bio‐based thermosets. 1H NMR reveals a switch in ring‐opening selectivity relative to thermal curing, from predominant exo‐epoxide opening under heating to preferential endo‐epoxide activation under white light, evidencing a distinct light‐responsive pathway. The resulting novel thermosets are colorless, transparent and exhibit enhanced thermomechanical properties over thermally cured counterparts, with a Tg of 110°C, tensile strength of 42 MPa, and strain at break of 15%. This strategy is underexplored in terpene‐derived epoxy–anhydride systems and may broaden the scope of terpene‐based thermoset networks.Keywords: catalysis, limonene, materials science, photoinitiator, polymer chemistry, polymer, thermosetting polymer, ultimate tensile strength, UV curing

Introduction

Thermosetting polymers are essential across a wide range of industrial and everyday applications, owing to their exceptional chemical resistance and multifunctional characteristics. Among them, epoxy resins stand out as highly adaptable materials, widely employed not only as adhesives but also in demanding sectors such as aerospace, automotive, electronics, and construction [1]. However, the production of these materials is heavily dependent on non‐renewable petroleum‐based resources, and their non‐recyclable nature contributes to severe environmental challenges. Consequently, the advancement of renewable and biodegradable polymers has become a critical priority for addressing these pressing issues [2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. In this context, the research community has shifted its focus toward novel materials derived from renewable feedstocks. Among these, bio‐based terpene‐derived monomers have demonstrated significant potential due to their multiple accessible olefinic groups, which can be readily functionalized to produce either thermoplastic or thermosetting polymers [7, 8, 9, 10, 11].

To enable the efficient conversion and practical application of these renewable monomers, selecting an appropriate polymerization method is essential. In this regard, photopolymerization has emerged as a promising, green, and efficient crosslinking strategy, particularly in the development of bio‐based thermosetting materials. Compared with conventional thermal curing, light‐induced polymerization offers several advantages, including low energy consumption, reduced volatile emissions, compatibility with heat‐sensitive substrates and biological systems, and precise spatial and temporal control over curing [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. In terpene‐based systems, reported photocuring strategies have predominantly relied on thiol–ene chemistry under UV or visible irradiation, enabling the preparation of crosslinked thermosets and photoresins for 3D/4D printing applications [20, 21, 22, 23]. Despite these advances, current terpene photopolymerization strategies remain centered on thiol–ene formulations and typically rely on conventional photoinitiator‐based activation, while direct photoinitiator‐free white‐light curing of terpene‐derived epoxy–anhydride networks remains largely unexplored.

To date, terpene‐based light‐mediated polymerizations have almost exclusively relied on UV irradiation and photoinitiators. White light offers a milder and more accessible irradiation source but has not previously been applied to the construction of terpene‐derived epoxy–anhydride networks. In this work, we report the first imidazole‐mediated white‐light curing route for limonene‐ and carvone‐derived diepoxides with glutaric anhydride, enabling the formation of fully bio‐based thermosets without conventional photoinitiators (Scheme 1). Importantly, white‐light curing follows a distinct light‐responsive pathway, as evidenced by the switched ring‐opening selectivity relative to thermal curing, while also affording thermosets with superior thermomechanical properties.

(a) Thiol–ene photocuring of terpene derivatives with PETMP [20]. (b) Radical copolymerization of limonene and myrcene followed by thiol–enenetwork formation [23]. Bottom: direct white‐light‐induced polymerization strategy developed in this work.

Results and Discussion

A terpene‐derived thermosetting monomer was synthesized from bio‐based feedstocks, providing access to renewable epoxy systems with competitive performance characteristics. Notably, imidazole was found to promote the polymerization of epoxy–anhydride formulations under white‐light irradiation in the absence of conventional photoinitiators, indicating an alternative light‐responsive catalytic pathway for epoxy curing. The resulting thermoset based on limonene diepoxide (LDO) exhibited a glass transition of 110°C together with balanced mechanical properties (tensile stress of 42 MPa and strain at break of 15%), supporting its potential utility in light‐curable coatings and structural materials.

Synthesis of Limonene‐ and Carvone‐Derived Diepoxides

As shown in Scheme 2, carvone was employed as a renewable terpene platform and functionalized in a stepwise manner. Low‐temperature reduction afforded carveol, introducing a hydroxyl handle while preserving the rigid terpene scaffold. The hydroxyl group, as a polar motif, may promote intermolecular hydrogen bonding and provide reversible interaction sites, thereby restricting chain mobility and potentially affecting stiffness and hardness in the cured network. Esterification then enabled incorporation of an aromatic unit, whose intrinsic rigidity is expected to impose additional conformational constraints and thus favor higher stiffness/hardness [24]. Finally, epoxidation of the olefin furnished well‐defined epoxy reactive sites for network formation. Based on these considerations, 4 and 5 were selected for polymerization; both bear epoxy groups but differ by hydroxyl versus aromatic functionalities, enabling assessment of hydrogen‐bonding interactions and aromatic rigidity on the mechanical response of the resulting networks.

Synthesis carvone‐derived diepoxides.

In the case of (R)‐(+)‐limonene, we envisaged to evaluate not only the activity of diepoxide derived from limonene (LDO) but also some derivatives, bearing aryl groups on the vinyl chain. This strategy was motivated by the renewable nature and ready availability of limonene as a bio‐based feedstock, as well as by our previous results showing that limonene‐derived diepoxides constitute an efficient platform for the preparation of high‐performance thermosetrs [25, 26, 27]. We have prepared LDO 7 starting from commercially available (R)‐(+)‐limonene 6, according to previously described protocol (Scheme 3(1)) [28]. Then we introduced various aryl groups on the vinyl chain via Pd‐catalyzed cross‐coupling Mizoroki–Heck reaction.

The reaction between commercially available (+)‐limonene monoepoxide 8 and iodobenzene (1:1.2) was selected as a model system. A screening of Pd catalysts, phosphine ligands, bases, solvents, temperature identified Pd(OAc)2/PPh3 as the best catalyst system, and Ag2CO3 to promote the process under milder conditions (Table S1).

Under the optimized conditions, the limonene‐derived substrate 8 underwent smooth Mizoroki–Heck arylation with various aryl iodides (Scheme 3(2)). Electron‐neutral as well as electron‐rich and electron‐poor aryl iodides afforded products 10–12 in moderate to good, isolated yields (61%–74%), whereas 4‐iodophenol proved to be unreactive and no formation of 13 was observed.

Photocuring of Limonene and Carvone Diepoxide Derivatives With Glutaric Anhydride

Following our previous study [25], the optimal formulation consists of mixing limonene diepoxide and carveol diepoxide derivatives with glutaric anhydride (GA) at a stoichiometric ratio of 1:1, using imidazole (IMI) as a catalyst at 2.5 wt%. Accordingly, the same experimental conditions were applied to all limonene and carveol diepoxide derivatives. DSC analysis (details and values in Table S2) revealed clear differences in curing behavior among the diepoxide monomers. LDO 7 underwent complete curing, affording a cross‐linked network (Tg = 110°C) with no detectable residual exotherm. In contrast, all other monomers displayed residual curing enthalpy in the DSC thermograms, indicating incomplete conversion. Increasing the imidazole loading up to 8.5 wt.% or adding Eosin‐Y did not improve curing efficiency. The lower curing efficiency of the other terpene‐derived diepoxides may be related to their structural differences from LDO 7. Bulky aromatic substituents may hinder epoxy group reaction, whereas in compound 5 the additional hydroxyl group may interact with the IMI/GA‐derived acidic species through hydrogen bonding, thereby reducing epoxide activation and curing efficiency. Oxidized geraniol was also examined as a linear terpene‐derived diepoxide. Under white‐light curing, this formulation afforded a flexible network and showed only a weak residual exotherm in DSC, indicating partial curing while also highlighting the strong influence of the flexible linear monomer architecture on the final material properties.

UV/Vis spectroscopy (Figure 1) showed strong absorption of IMI and the IMI/epoxy/anhydride mixture in the 230–280 nm region, whereas absorption above 300 nm was negligible. Accordingly, irradiation at 254 or 280 nm at room temperature resulted in gelation but not full curing, while complete solidification was obtained at 254 nm upon heating to 50°C (Table 1). Under white‐light irradiation, complete curing was obtained with a temperature increase to approximately 50°C. However, dark control conducted at the same temperature only produced a gel‐like material (Figure S7), demonstrating that thermal input alone is insufficient to account for the complete curing under white light. These results indicate that white‐light irradiation is essential for achieving complete network formation, while the accompanying photothermal heating facilitates the curing process.

Rewardingly, the polymer showed high optical transparency in the visible range, with transmittance increasing from ∼75% at 400 nm to ∼88% at 800 nm (Figures S1, S4, and S5), consistent with commonly used transparency criteria [29]. To further demonstrate the interest of this material, we conducted other analyses such as tensile test, swelling and gel content tests.

Tensile testing (Figure 2a) revealed pronounced differences between thermally cured and white‐light–cured LDO/GA/IMI networks. The thermally cured sample (110°C, 1 h; 160°C, 1 h; 200°C, 30 min) showed an ultimate stress of 34 MPa and a fracture strain of 5.4% [25]. In contrast, white‐light curing (overnight, ca. 50°C) increased the ultimate stress to 42 MPa and the fracture strain to 15%, indicating a substantially improved strength–ductility balance. This enhancement may be attributed to photothermally assisted curing under mild conditions, which may promote the formation of a tougher polymer network, having, in the same time, more elastic segments between the crosslinks. Consistently, DSC and DMA analyses further supported the increased network constraint under white‐light curing. DSC showed that the T g increased from 98°C for the thermally cured network to ca. 110°C for the white‐light‐cured network (Figure 2b). DMA analysis revealed a higher storage modulus in the rubbery region for the white‐light‐cured network, and the apparent crosslink density calculated from the rubbery storage modulus increased from 0.29 mol L− 1 for the thermally cured network to 0.36 mol L− 1 for the white‐light‐cured network (Figure S9).

Table 2 gives the comparison of the swelling ratio (S%) and gel content (GC%) of LDO networks prepared by thermal curing and photopolymerization (Figure S6). Both samples fragmented in dichloromethane (DCM) without dissolution, supporting the formation of crosslinked three‐dimensional networks. Notably, photopolymerization produced a markedly more solvent‐resistant network: the photopolymerized sample showed lower swelling in toluene (1.8%) than the thermally cured sample (8%) and remained intact in acetone and methanol with limited swelling (7.2% and 15%), whereas the thermally cured material disintegrated in these polar solvents.

Consistently, the white‐light‐cured network exhibited lower swelling ratios and a higher gel content than the thermally cured network. The gel content increased from 91.6% for the thermally cured sample to 98.2% for the white‐light‐cured sample, indicating a higher insoluble fraction and more complete network formation. Together with the improved integrity in polar solvents, these results suggest that white‐light‐mediated curing affords a network with higher integrity and improved solvent resistance.

Polymerization Mechanism Study

We then decided to thorough investigate the mechanism of the white‐light polymerization process. As shown in Figure 3, the conversion–time profiles obtained from ^1H NMR analysis revealed markedly different ring‐opening selectivities under the two curing conditions, with the corresponding time‐dependent ^1H NMR spectra provided in Figure S8.

Time‐dependent conversion of endo and exo epoxides in LDO determined by 1H NMR under white‐light irradiation and thermal curing at 120°C.

Under white‐light irradiation, the endo epoxide was consumed preferentially during the early stage of curing, reaching 47% and 59% conversion after 1 and 2 h, respectively, while the exo epoxide remained largely unreacted. In contrast, thermal curing at 120°C showed the opposite selectivity, with faster consumption of the exo epoxide than the endo epoxide during the early and intermediate stages of the reaction. After prolonged curing, both epoxide groups eventually reached high conversion under both conditions. These distinct kinetic trajectories indicate that white‐light and thermal curing do not simply differ in reaction rate but proceed through different ring‐opening pathways.

Tensile testing, DSC, DMA, swelling/gel fraction analyses, and NMR collectively demonstrate that LDO networks formed by photopolymerization differ markedly from those obtained thermally, consistent with distinct network architectures and reaction pathways. Motivated by the superior properties of materials obtained under irradiation, we next propose the photopolymerization pathway shown in Scheme 4. It should be noted that this scheme is intended to illustrate the preferential opening of the internal epoxide at the early stage of photo‐curing rather than the complete curing mechanism. As curing proceeds, both epoxide groups can participate in further ring‐opening reactions and contribute to the formation of the crosslinked network, although the internal epoxide appears to react faster and more efficiently under photo‐curing conditions.

Proposed pathway illustrating the preferential opening of the internal epoxide during the early stage of white‐light photopolymerization of LDO.

First, imidazole, as a nucleophile, would open the anhydride to give an acyl‐imidazolium/carboxylate pair A [30, 31], in equilibrium with the carboxylic acid, which, as a Brønsted acid, would pre‐activate the epoxide through hydrogen bonding and would enable under white light the formation of an oxonium‐like protonated epoxide (B) [32, 33]. The conjugate base (R′COO−) would then add to the activated epoxide to open the ring and form an ester linkage C [34], affording the propagating network segment E via D. Imidazole would be regenerated in the process, establishing a light‐gated, acid‐catalyzed cycle [35, 36].

We further examined the interaction between imidazole and glutaric anhydride to clarify the proposed imidazole–anhydride adduct‐mediated epoxide ring‐opening pathway. The existence of intermediates A was supported by 1H NMR analyses, by mixing both imidazole and anhydride and observing the proton shifts (Figure S2). In contrast, an equimolar imidazole/LDO mixture recorded in the dark showed no evidence for epoxide consumption: the characteristic LDO epoxide signals remain essentially unchanged in pattern and relative integration (consistent with retention of two epoxide units), and no evident new O–CH resonances attributable to ring‐opened products were observed. These results suggested that the anhydride–imidazole pre‐reaction, rather than direct imidazole–epoxide addition, is the primary origin of the acidic activator proposed in Scheme 4.

As shown in Figure 4, four control experiments supported a light‐gated ionic mechanism. (a) Addition of TEMPO did not affect the polymerization rate or conversion, disfavoring a radical pathway. (b) A strong base fully suppressed curing, which is consistent with a required Brønsted‐acid (protonic) activator under irradiation. (c) Replacing imidazole with N‐methylimidazole strongly inhibited polymerization, highlighting the critical role of the imidazole N–H in proton transfer/hydrogen‐bond‐assisted activation. (d) Substituting glutaric anhydride with glutaric acid resulted in negligible curing. This contrast may indicate that simple acid–base salt formation was insufficient and that the formation of the anhydride–imidazole activated A manifold (acylimidazole) was required to establish a self‐sustaining proton‐mediated catalytic cycle under irradiation.

Mechanistic experiments supporting the proposed reaction pathway.

Under acidic conditions, epoxide ring opening typically would occur at the more substituted carbon due to stabilization of the developing carbocation. However, since both the endocyclic and exocyclic epoxides contain tertiary carbons, this principle alone would not explain why the endocyclic epoxide reacts first. Insight into this selectivity could be rationalized from reported kinetic studies on epoxide opening in the presence of water [37].

Elrod's group reported kinetics on limonene dioxide derivatives, indicating a strong positional bias under aqueous conditions (Table 3): cis‐endo‐LDO undergoes acid‐catalyzed ring opening much faster (k″GA = 160 × 10− 6 s− 1) than trans‐endo (8.36 × 10− 6 s− 1) and the exo isomer (12.6 × 10− 6 s− 1), suggesting preferential endo epoxide activation upon protonation [36]. Although the absolute rate constants are condition‐dependent, combined with our control experiments, this is consistent with our proposed mechanism in which the imidazole–anhydride adduct provides the acidic species that initiates and sustains epoxide ring‐opening propagation.

Taken together, the improved solvent resistance and mechanical performance of the white‐light‐cured LDO network may arise from two complementary factors. First, NMR studies indicate that white‐light curing follows a distinct ring‐opening pathway from thermal curing, leading to different network formation processes. Second, the milder irradiation conditions may allow more gradual network development, whereas elevated‐temperature thermal curing can promote rapid local gelation and restricted molecular diffusion, potentially generating a more heterogeneous network. These pathway‐ and process‐dependent differences may contribute to the enhanced network integrity of the white‐light‐cured thermosets. In addition, preliminary proof‐of‐concept demonstrations showed that the white‐light‐cured LDO/GA network could be used as an adhesive and as a transparent coating, further highlighting its potential for practical applications (Figure 5).

Preliminary application demonstrations of the white‐light‐cured LDO/GA network. (a) Adhesive bonding of wooden substrates, with the bonded sample supporting a 100 g load. (b) Transparent coating on a patterned substrate, showing that the underlying pattern remains clearly visible after curing.

The white‐light‐cured LDO/GA network exhibited promising practical applicability in both adhesive and coating applications. Strong adhesion was demonstrated by bonding two wooden substrates, with the bonded assembly capable of supporting a 100 g load. In addition, the cured material formed a highly transparent coating on a patterned substrate, allowing the underlying pattern to remain clearly visible and highlighting its excellent optical transparency.

Conclusion

This work demonstrates a proof‐of‐concept strategy for the white‐light‐mediated curing of terpene‐derived epoxy–anhydride systems through imidazole‐mediated catalysis without conventional photoinitiators, enabling the formation of fully bio‐based thermosets. Relative to thermal curing, white‐light curing follows a distinct light‐responsive pathway, as evidenced by the switched ring‐opening selectivity, and affords colorless and transparent thermosets with improved thermomechanical performance. The resulting thermosets exhibit high gel content and strong solvent resistance, consistent with the formation of a uniform and stable crosslinked network. Beyond demonstrating a photoinitiator‐free curing strategy, this study also provides a basis for the further development of limonene‐derived monomers and sustainable high‐performance thermosetting polymers.
