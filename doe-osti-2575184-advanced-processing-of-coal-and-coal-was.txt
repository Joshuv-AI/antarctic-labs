 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

 The University of North Dakota (UND) Energy & Environmental Research Center (EERC),
 in collaboration with the UND Center for Process Engineering Research (CPER), conducted a
 project to validate two technologies capable of converting North Dakota lignite and lignite coal
 waste to high-quality graphite for fast-charging lithium-ion battery (LIB) anode. The project was
 conducted over about 3 years from April 7, 2022, to July 6, 2025. The two technological paths
 pursued in this project include path A – direct conversion of coal or coal waste to graphite by the
 upgraded carbon ores to products (UCOP) process being developed at the EERC and path B –
 lignite-derived coal tar pitch (CTP) conversion to graphite (CTP2G) process being developed at
 CPER. The results from this project validate the two technological approaches and are expected to
 be an integral part of a portfolio of emerging technologies for making high-quality graphite not
 only from North Dakota lignite, but from all ranks of U.S. domestic coal and coal waste resources.
 The quality of the graphite produced by these technologies is high enough for various applications,
 including batteries for the fast-growing electric vehicle industry, energy storage applications,
 electric arc furnace electrodes for steel production, and graphene production, among others.
 Although the two technologies can produce high-quality graphite, they are fundamentally different
 in that the UCOP technology provides a direct path to transform coal to graphite, while the CTP2G
 technology needs to go through a CTP intermediate and a coking process for the intermediate,
 which requires a special facility to accomplish. For application in the industry, the UCOP process
 is designed to be more flexible, with feedstock to include potentially any carbonaceous material
 such as all coal ranks and biochar, while the CTP2G process is designed to utilize CTP as the
 starting precursor.

 The key project accomplishments include the following:

 x Successful preparation of high-quality synthetic graphite from North Dakota lignite
 coal/coal wastes and lignite-derived CTP.

 x Patent application has been filed for the UCOP process and an internal invention
 disclosure has been filed for the CTP2G process.

 x The produced graphite performs better than a commercial battery-grade sample in LIB
 coin cells, especially fast-charging capability, stability, and long-duration cycling.

 x Coin-type Li-ion half-cells with CTP2G graphite showed excellent performance, with
 >370 mAh/g capacity, >90% initial coulombic efficiency, and 93%/67% retention at
 1C/2C rate, which outperforms commercial graphite in charging speed, stability, and
 cycling.

 x Results of fabricated 18650 cells were consistent with the observations in coin cells.

 INTRODUCTION AND BACKGROUND

 The University of North Dakota (UND) Energy & Environmental Research Center (EERC),
 in collaboration with the UND Center for Process Engineering Research (CPER), conducted a
 project to validate two technologies capable of converting North Dakota lignite and lignite coal
 waste to high-quality graphite for fast-charging lithium-ion battery (LIB) anode. The EERC began
 research into high-value carbon products in 2019 with sponsorship from the State Energy Research
 Center of North Dakota. This initial effort led to the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) awarding
 a 3-year project (DE-FE0031881) to the EERC a year later to develop a technology to upgrade and
 convert coals into high-value carbon products to maximize the coal value chain. The research team
 succeeded in converting coal feedstocks to graphite, which was used to make graphene oxide (GO),
 reduced GO, and graphene quantum dots from all coal ranks from lignite to anthracite (Azenkeng
 et al., 2023a). Results from the previous project showed potentially feasible technology for direct
 conversion of coal to high-quality graphite, which was successfully patented (Azenkeng and
 Laumb, 2023b, 2024). In this project (DE-FE0032139), the focus was to further develop the
 patented technology to enable production of high-quality graphite from coal and coal wastes for
 fast-charging LIB applications. UND’s CPER has also been working on various battery-related
 research projects, including development of lignite-derived carbon materials (DE-FE-0031984)
 for LIB anodes, advanced Si/graphite materials (DE-FE-0026825/S00045) for LIB anodes, and
 advanced LIB cathode materials from lignite, as well as development of LIB fast-charging
 technology. The CPER-led effort has focused primarily on CTP precursors made from North
 Dakota lignite feedstocks by a collaborating partner, AmeriCarbon Products. The parallel research
 efforts at UND EERC and CPER culminated in the joint collaborative investigation that was the
 subject of this project, with the aim to develop technologies that can enable production of high-
 quality graphite from North Dakota lignite and, thus, maximize the lignite-to-products value chain.
 The two technological paths pursued in this project (Figure 1) include path A – direct conversion
 of coal or coal waste to graphite by the upgraded carbon ores to products (UCOP) process being
 developed at the EERC and path B – lignite-derived coal tar pitch (CTP) conversion to graphite
 (CTP2G) process being developed at CPER.

 The project was divided into two budget periods (BPs). BP1 activities were conducted
 between April 7, 2022, and October 6, 2023, and included upgrading of coal and coal waste
 feedstocks, production of CTP, carbonization of upgraded coal/coal waste residues, conversion of
 CTP to needle coke, and graphitization. BP2 was carried out between October 7, 2023, and
 July 6, 2025, and focused on functionalization of the produced graphite, fabrication and testing of
 a LIB anode prototype, and a techno-economic feasibility analysis. The results from this project
 validate the two technological approaches and are expected to be an integral part of a portfolio of
 emerging technologies for making high-quality graphite not only from North Dakota lignite, but
 from all ranks of U.S. domestic coal and coal waste resources. The quality of the graphite produced
 by these technologies is expected to be high enough for various applications, including batteries
 for the fast-growing electric vehicle industry, energy storage applications, electric arc furnace
 electrodes for steel production, and graphene production, among others.

 UPGRADED CARBON ORES TO PRODUCTS TECHNOLOGY

 Technology Overview

 The UCOP technology comprises a series of simple wet chemistry and thermal treatments
 designed to upgrade and convert coal/coal wastes directly to synthetic graphite. The key technical
 steps include physical cleaning, chemical cleaning, heteroatom reduction, carbonization and heat
 treatment, and graphitization. Additional postgraphitization processing steps include particle
 engineering (size and shape), coating, and classification. During physical cleaning, a high-specific-
 gravity solution is used to separate high-density mineral grains from the feedstock. Additional
 acid-soluble minerals are further dissolved out of the coal matrix by treating it with strong acids
 such as hydrochloric acid in the chemical cleaning step. The physical and chemical cleaning steps
 combined significantly reduce the ash impurities in the feedstock. After cleaning, reduction of
 heteroatoms (nitrogen, sulfur, and oxygen) is performed using proprietary chemical reagents to
 produce an upgraded coal precursor with improved graphitizability. The upgraded precursor is
 carbonized and further heat-treated via a staged temperature ramping program to optimize the
 graphitic content of the resulting coke. The final coke material is then graphitized at high
 temperatures, up to 3000°C, to obtain synthetic graphite. A schematic representation of the UCOP
 technology is presented in Figure 2.

 Heteroatom Reduction

 The resultant clean coal/coal waste residue was reacted with proprietary chemical agents in
 an autoclave reactor at about 250°–300°C to reduce the concentration of heteroatoms to obtain an
 upgraded residue. The ultimate analysis results in Tables 2 and 4 indicate varying degrees of
 heteroatom reductions, presented in Table 5, where oxygen reduction ranges from 12% to 37%
 and sulfur reduction ranges from about 25% to 39%, depending on the concentration of each
 element in the starting material. The nitrogen content reduction was marginal and, in most cases,
 showed an increase that is attributed to low initial concentrations in the starting material that are
 close to the instrument’s detection limit. Unlike nitrogen, which is less impactful in the
 graphitization process, oxygen is a key heteroatom of interest that can affect the graphitizability
 of the coke product and sulfur is an impurity that can negatively affect the graphitized material or
 its end uses. Thus removal and/or reduction of the oxygen and sulfur concentrations are of greater
 interest than nitrogen.

 Thermal Transformations and Graphitization

 Carbonization and Heat Treatment

 The upgraded coal residues were carbonized using the system displayed in Figure 3 to
 remove the volatile organic matter content. The carbonization system consists of a tube furnace,
 an inert gas source to create an inert environment, and an optional condensate trap to collect
 condensed tars and/or condensable hydrocarbon gases. A piece of analytical equipment such as
 Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analyzer can be attached to analyze the effluent gases in real
 time. The samples were placed in the tube furnace and carbonized at about 1000°C and further
 heat-treated overnight at high temperatures, up to 1600°C. The resulting coke material obtained
 after carbonization and heat treatment was stored in airtight containers prior to graphitization.

 Figure 3. Photo of the carbonization and heat-treatment setup.

 Postprocessing of Graphite Particles

 Preliminary postprocessing of the synthesized graphite samples was done by milling the
 samples in a tabletop jet system to investigate optimal size fractions and morphologies that yield
 better performance in battery applications. The milled samples were subsequently sieved using a
 precession sonic sifter with cut sizes of 5-, 10-, 20-, and 45-plus-μm sieves. Representative SEM
 images (Figure 9) show that the particles in the –5-μm size fraction (Figure 9A) appear to have
 escaped out of the jet mill without significant changes to the morphology relative to the original
 particles in the −250-μm population. However, Figures 9B, C, and D suggest that the particles
 with larger diameters are slightly rounder because of the impact of the mill.

 Observations from SEM analysis also suggest that most of the novel graphite microstructures
 that were previously known to be present in the as-graphitized samples appear to have been
 shattered and/or destroyed during jet milling as much smaller numbers of these microstructures
 were observed in the milled sample compared to the corresponding unmilled sample. The milling
 and sieving process also faced challenges with particle agglomeration. Partially broken
 graphulerenite particles (Figure 10A), agglomerated particles formed from fines during milling
 and/or sieving (Figure 10B), and flakes and half pieces (Figures 10C and D) were seen in the
 matrix of the milled samples. Particle agglomeration was a major challenge during sieving and
 resulted in an inefficient and ineffective separation, where more fines remained in the matrix of
 the 10–20-μm size fraction of coal-derived graphite compared to a commercial battery-grade
 graphite reference (Figure 11). These fines will need to be removed to further improve the
 performance of the sample in a battery.

 Electrochemical Performance Testing

 The electrochemical performance of the produced coal-derived graphite was measured on
 CR2032 half-coin cells. Fast-charging or rate capability testing was conducted first and followed
 immediately by long-term cycling tests. All rate capability and cycling tests are based on a specific
 discharge capacity of 350 mAh/g in one cycle per hour, i.e., 1C. For rate capability testing, the
 samples were cycled at various rates, including 0.1C (10 hr per cycle) for the initial two cycles to
 measure the initial coulombic efficient (ICE) based on the first cycle, followed by 0.1C for
 10 cycles, 1/3C (3 hr per cycle) for 10 cycles, 1C (1 hr per cycle) for 10 cycles, 2C (30 min per
 cycle) for 10 cycles, and back to 0.1C for 10 cycles to conclude the rate capability testing protocol.
 After the rate capability testing, the cells were cycled at 1/3C for the remaining up to 1000 cycles.
 For each graphite sample, five coin cells were tested, and a representative dataset closest to the
 average of all five coin cells data sets was reported.

 Initial specific capacity (approximated by the initial first discharge capacity), ICE, and
 cycling life at 80% capacity retention were measured to assess the performance of the samples and
 compared to a commercial battery-grade SAG-R reference graphite from MTI Corporation (MTI
 Corporation, 2024). In addition, rate capability testing assessed the fast-charging capability at
 1/3C, 1C, and 2C rates relative to 0.1C. The results (Table 8 and Figures 16 and 17) show that the
 graphite samples made in this study perform better than the commercial battery-grade graphite
 sample in most of the properties measured even though the samples from this study are yet to be
 fully optimized with respect to coating, particle classification, and spheroidization. The ICE was
 lower than 90% for all samples, and this was found to be closely associated with coating of the
 graphite particles that was not optimal. Coin cells were prepared and tested from the commercial
 reference graphite sample using the same protocol as those made in this study so that a better
 comparison that minimizes instrument and coin cell preparation effects can be made.
 Consequently, the data in Table 8 and Figures 16 and 17 for the commercial reference graphite are
 measured using the same equipment and protocol as for the samples made in this study.

 Summary of Techno-Economic Feasibility Analysis

 A techno-economic feasibility analysis conducted on the UCOP technology was based on
 an advanced laboratory-scale batch process with equipment duplicated to produce a small pilot-
 scale capacity of about 50 metric tons a year of synthetic graphite from North Dakota lignite.
 Results of the analysis show that the estimated manufacturing cost per kilogram of graphite is
 approximately $39, which is high but reasonable for a technology that is still at an early stage of
 development. Informal conversations with other U.S. developers at the recent University of
 Kentucky roundtable discussion on domestic production of graphite suggest that the manufacturing
 cost of synthetic graphite can be up to $70/kg depending on the processing parameters and
 technology. Thus the estimates obtained in this study for the UCOP process are within the expected
 range for the prevailing commercial prices of approximately $7–$10/kg (Norley, 2024; Anovion,
 2025) and other developing technologies. The established methodology and preliminary dataset
 created in this study serve as important baselines upon which future technological progress can be
 measured or referenced.

 Conclusions and Future Work

 The results of this project have validated the UCOP technology and show that:

 x North Dakota lignite coal/coal wastes can be converted directly to high-quality synthetic
 graphite.

 x Preprocessing conditions are key to achieving high quality and to observing new graphite
 microstructures.

 x The produced graphite performs well in LIB coin cells and better than commercial
 battery-grade graphite for most parameters tested, especially high-rate capability,
 stability, and long-duration cycling.

 Anticipated work to continue to develop the UCOP technology includes:

 x Optimization of the heteroatom reduction process conditions and chemical
 concentrations.

 x Research into possible options for larger-capacity graphitization furnace systems.

 x Construction and testing of a pilot-scale demonstration facility for the UCOP process.

 CTP Production and Carbonization

 CTP Production

 North Dakota lignite, as the feedstock of graphite, was first converted to CTP through
 AmeriCarbon’s patented coal liquefaction process, Eco-Pitch™. CTP is reported to have higher
 polyaromatic hydrocarbon content and fewer sulfur-containing compounds than petroleum-based
 pitches (Andreikov et al., 2019). Therefore, cokes from CTP can have better carbon
 microcrystalline parameters and can be graphitized more easily than petroleum-based coke (Zhu
             et al., 2021).

 During the project, over a dozen lignite-derived CTPs were screened and tested for their
 suitability in graphite production. In this final report, six representative samples (Table 9) with
 varying compositions, softening points, optical textures, and quinoline-insoluble (QI) contents
 have been systematically analyzed as graphite feedstocks. The six samples were selected based on
 the following reasons:

 1) Softening point: This key parameter determines the mobility of aromatic compounds
 within CTP during thermal processing. It directly affects the development of anisotropic
 (liquid crystalline) structures during coking. To capture a broad processing window,
 CTPs with a low softening point (50℃), medium softening point (102℃), and high
 softening point (220℃) were selected and converted to coke.

 2) QI contents: QI refers to the insoluble residue after quinoline extraction of CTP, which
 may include pyrolytic carbon, carbon black, or mineral matter. High QI content can
 hinder the alignment and growth of anisotropic mesophase domains during coking,
 adversely affecting the microstructure and quality of the resulting coke. Four CTPs
 (CTP 1–4) with varying QI levels were selected and compared to evaluate these effects.

 3) CTP refinement: To isolate and understand the impact of QI, two refined CTPs
 (CTPs 5 and 6) were refined via solvent extraction and filtration to fully remove QI.

 Coking and Carbonization

 CTP to Needle Coke: Two-Stage Coking

 A sample of CTP was thermally treated in a pressure-controlled reactor under an inert gas
 atmosphere (argon or nitrogen) to produce green coke. The pitch was initially heated to a moderate
 temperature under elevated pressure for several hours to promote partial polymerization and phase
 development. This was followed by a higher-temperature treatment to complete the carbonization
 process. The resulting solid carbon material, known as green coke, was collected for further
 analysis and processing.

 Green Coke to Carbonized Coke: High-Temperature Carbonization

 The green coke was crushed into granules and thermally treated in a furnace at high
 temperature for several hours. This process removed the remaining volatile components and
 converted the material into carbonized coke. The overall CTP-to-graphite yields are 40%–70%,
 among which the coking yield varies drastically with the feedstock and coking conditions, while
 the carbonization and graphitization yields are consistent among all the samples. CTPs with high
 softening points (e.g., CTP 4) tend to exhibit greater coking yields, up to about 85%, due to their
 high contents of heavy aromatic compounds that form solid residues upon thermal treatment. In
 contrast, CTPs with lower softening points (CTPs 5 and 6) typically have higher content of light,
 volatile components that are lost during high-temperature processing, resulting in lower coking
 yields. The carbonization step, carried out after coking, effectively converts the green coke into
 needle coke by removing the remaining volatile matter. This step is essential not only for
 increasing fixed carbon content but also for preventing issues such as furnace corrosion or
 contamination during subsequent graphitization. The yields from the carbonization step and the
 graphitization step ranged from 80% to 97%. These values are consistent across all samples,
 indicating that the majority of volatile components were effectively removed during the previous
 carbonization step.

 Carbonized coke, as the precursor of graphite, exhibits several structural characteristics
 similar to graphite, such as layered anisotropic microstructures, which can be examined by
 polarized light microscopy, a key technique for evaluating the quality of needle coke (Zhu et al.,
 2019). The optical textures of needle coke are commonly categorized into three microstructural
 types: fine mosaic, fibrous, and leaflet. The fibrous and leaflet structures indicate well-aligned
 carbon layers, which are favorable for graphitization. In contrast, the fine mosaic structure reflects
 a disordered carbon layer, which is generally unfavorable for efficient graphitization (Zhu et al.,

 The carbonized cokes were further graphitized under a pure argon atmosphere, with a coke-

 well-stacked crystalline layered structure. While among the graphites (Graphites 1–4) produced
 from the unrefined pitches, Graphite 1 presents the most intensive peaks.

 Graphite 1 stands out among the graphites derived from unrefined pitches, while Graphites 5 and

 Functionalization

 Graphitization Temperatures

 The graphitization process accounts for the cost of graphite production. Lowering the
 graphitization temperature can significantly reduce energy consumption and furnace requirements.
 Moreover, a lower graphitization temperature is conducive to graphite with lower graphitization
 degrees, which favors fast-charging performance because of enlarged interlayer spacing (Fan et
 al., 2012). Cokes 1 and 6 were graphitized at the high temperature T1 for several hours.
 Electrochemical tests were conducted, and the results were compared with samples processed at
 the higher temperature T2 for the same duration.

 Particle Size

 Graphite particle size determines Li-ions’ transport distance during charging and discharging
 cycles, a critical contributor to graphite’s fast-charging performance. Thus, the particle sizes of
 synthetic graphite were tested and optimized. The synthetic graphites were pulverized into powder
 and then further classified into three particle sizes: large, medium, and small. After classification,
 nanosize ash and submicron particles were removed to minimize the coke’s surface area.

 Electrochemical Performance Testing

 The graphite samples were fabricated into electrodes and then assembled into coin cells for
 electrochemical tests. The graphite electrode consists of 94% graphite, 2% carbon black as the
 conducting agent, and 4% polyvinylidene fluoride as the binder. Coin cells were assembled in an
 Ar-filled glovebox using Li metal as the counter electrode and Celgard 2325 as the separator. The
 electrolyte for the coin cells was 1.2 M LiPF6 in ethylene carbonate, dimethyl carbonate, ethyl
 methyl carbonate (1:1:1, v/v/v) with 10% fluoroethylene carbonate. A Neware battery-testing
 apparatus evaluated the cycling and rate performance of the assembled cells. The charge-discharge
 tests ranged between 0.01 V and 1.5 V.

 Summary of Techno-Economic Feasibility Analysis

 A techno-economic analysis was conducted to evaluate the feasibility of converting coal tar
 pitch into graphite materials using Aspen Plus® for process simulation. The analysis incorporated
 detailed mass and energy balances based on lab-scale experimental data and scaled to an industrial
 capacity of approximately 22,000 tonnes per year. Capital and operating costs were estimated
 using factored methodologies benchmarked against our previous DOE-funded project DE-
 FE0031984, targeting AACE Class 5 accuracy (–50% to +100%). The estimated production cost
             is $7107 per tonne, with feedstock (37.9%), energy (16%), capital (13.4%), and labor (20%)
 representing the major cost drivers, as shown in Figure 31. This assessment provides a
 foundational economic baseline to support investment decisions and further development of
 advanced carbon material technologies.

 Conclusions and Future Work

 In summary, the CTP-derived graphite developed in this project meets or exceeds key
 performance requirements for next-generation LIB anodes, as shown in Table 15. With a flake size
 of 8–13 μm and a carbon purity greater than 99.9%, the optimal sample achieves an impressively
 low ash content (<0.03%), significantly better than typical commercial graphite. The material also
 exhibits high tap density (1.1–1.2 g/cm³) and competitive pressing density (1.55 g/cm³), supporting
 high volumetric capacity. Electrochemically, it delivers a strong initial specific capacity
 (>370 mAh/g) with >90% ICE. Notably, the CTP-derived graphite shows excellent rate
 performance, retaining 93.3% capacity at 1C and 67.1% at 2C—far surpassing commercial
 benchmarks, achieving our goal of making fast-charging graphite for LIBs’ anodes. Although the
 coin-type half-cell shows 600 cycles at 80% capacity retention due to the aggressive lithium
 plating/stripping inherent in half-cell configurations, we expect the material to meet the 1000-cycle
 target in 18650 full cells, consistent with industry practice. These promising results underscore the
 potential of CTP-derived synthetic graphite as a cost-effective and high-performance solution for
 fast-charging LIBs.

annual production capacity of 22,000 tonnes.

 UCOP Process

 UCOP Technology Description

 The UCOP process (Figure 2) aims to achieve direct conversion of lignite coal or any
carbonaceous feedstock to high-quality synthetic graphite. The technology is designed to use
simple wet chemistry processes to upgrade coal or coal wastes and then convert the upgraded
product using thermal treatment to synthetic graphite. The key technical steps include physical
cleaning and chemical cleaning to remove ash-producing inorganic impurities using mineral acids.
The clean coal is further upgraded by reducing the heteroatom content to improve the
graphitizability of the precursor. The resultant coal residue is carbonized to remove volatile
organic content and further thermally treated to promote growth of graphitic domains. The
carbonized coke is then graphitized at high temperatures up to about 3000°C to obtain high-quality
synthetic graphite. The products from UCOP technology include synthetic graphite, which can be
further converted to graphene, and additional useful by-products such as petrochemical
intermediates and extraction of rare earth elements (REEs) from wastewater streams generated
from the chemical cleaning step. Potential application areas for these materials span the fields of
electrical circuit boards, optical sensors, batteries, and other high-end uses in both civilian and
military sectors.

gases, and process chemicals, respectively.

 Energy Costs

 The total energy consumption for each equipment was estimated using the power rating on
the equipment and the time duration for which the equipment is run per batch or directly measured
by a Fluke multimeter during each batch. Total estimated power consumption per batch for each
equipment was summed over all equipment used and multiplied by the number of batches per year
to obtain the estimated yearly estimate for producing 50 MT of graphite. Based on these estimates,
the energy cost contribution to the manufacturing cost was estimated to be $6.21/kg-graphite.

 Utilities Costs

 The main item currently being considered under utilities is deionized (DI) water. For the up-
front wet chemistry processes (physical/chemical cleaning and heteroatom reduction), an
estimated 1,527,120 gallons of DI water would be used per year, which cost approximately
$18,448 at $0.012/gal. The contribution of DI water cost to the manufacturing cost was estimated
to be $0.36/kg-graphite.

 4

 Environmental Stewardship Costs

 The cost contribution for environmental remediation comes from wastewater treatment and
disposal, gaseous effluents from heteroatom reduction, gaseous effluents from carbonization,
gaseous effluents from graphitization, and coal tailings from physical cleaning. Because the
technology is still at the laboratory scale with relatively small amounts of gaseous effluents, no
estimate for gaseous effluents disposal was feasible. Assuming all the 1,527,120 gallons of
estimated DI water usage ends up as acidic wastewater, approximately $87,351 was estimated for
treatment/disposal per year. The DI water cost contribution to the manufacturing cost was
estimated to be $1.73/kg-graphite.

 Overhead Costs

 The total overhead cost includes contributions from facility maintenance, facility utilities,
administrative salaries, insurance, R&D activities, and marketing/outreach. Of these, the facilities
maintenance cost was estimated as 3% of total energy and labor costs and the facilities utilities
costs estimated as 5% of energy and labor costs, while no reasonable estimates for the remaining
items could be made at this stage of the technology. For the facility maintenance and utilities
estimated total of $55,060 per year, the contribution toward the manufacturing cost was estimated
to be $1.09/kg-graphite.

 Discussion

 A breakdown of the various factors and their contributions to the overall manufacturing
cost/kg-graphite by the UCOP process at 50 MT a year capacity is provided in Table 2. This
analysis shows that currently, the biggest contributors to the overall manufacturing cost are capital
equipment, labor, energy, and process chemicals. These are all factors that are expected to
significantly scale downward as the technology is scaled up. Process chemicals, which are
currently the most expensive contributing factor, can be further optimized to reduce the amount of
chemical usage and reduce costs. Additional cost savings are expected for the labor category
stemming from process integration, automation, and the resultant improved efficiency.

 The prevailing market selling prices for synthetic graphite of about $7–$10/kg-graphite
(Norley, 2024; Novonix, 2025) are based on supply chains sourced out of China, where energy,
labor, equipment, and every conceivable metric that contributes to the manufacturing costs is lower
than anywhere else in the world. Consequently, techno-economic feasibility analysis would
necessarily have to consider the socio-economic conditions under which the technology is
developed/implemented vis-à-vis the competitor or commercial benchmark. For example,
governmental policies and regulations regarding environmental stewardship compliance, labor
codes, energy policies, subsidies or lack thereof, etc., can significantly impact reported production
costs for commodities such as synthetic graphite. In addition, the $6–$7/kg-graphite market prices
are based on commercial-scale production of tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands metric
tons per year. Thus, the values presented in Table 2 cannot be fairly compared to reported market
prices for synthetic graphite but rather serve as important markers based upon which future

 Conclusions

 A techno-economic feasibility analysis has been conducted for the UCOP technology based

• Coking and Carbonization: CTP goes into a series of consecutive thermal treatments to make

 ENIRONMENTAL JUSTICE CONSIDERATIONS

 The University of North Dakota (UND) Energy & Environmental Research Center (EERC),

in collaboration with the UND Center for Process Engineering Research (CPER), conducted this

project with the aim to validate a technology capable of converting North Dakota lignite and lignite

coal waste to high-quality graphite for fast-charging lithium-ion battery (LIB) anode. Two

technological paths were pursued in this project, including Path A – direct conversion of coal or

coal waste to graphite by the upgraded carbon ores to products (UCOP) process being developed

at the EERC and Path B – the lignite-derived coal tar pitch (CTP) conversion to graphite (CTP2G)

process being developed at CPER.

 Small amounts of lignite and lignite coal waste, including ~10 lb of weathered overburden

coal waste, ~15 lb waste coal fines (CF), and ~15 lb of regular combustion-grade coal from

Freedom and Center mines in North Dakota, were upgraded and converted by the UCOP process

to high-quality synthetic graphite. The produced graphite, about 2 lb, was used to make several

CR2032 LIB half-coin cells and tested for electrochemical performance. Additionally, CTP made

from North Dakota lignite was acquired from AmeriCarbon Corporation and used to make

synthetic graphite by the CTP2G process. The project’s final environmental justice considerations

are presented below in the form of responses to pertinent questions recommended in the original

project solicitation.

(1) How does the technology rely on limited resources such as coal, biomass, freshwater, land,

 and/or low-carbon energy? What is the relationship between the amount of resources used

 versus the amount of product formed?

 The technologies being developed in this project are intended for conversion of coal or coal

waste to synthetic graphite. Coal is the primary feedstock, no biomass usage currently, normal

 1

usage of fresh water for general purpose cleaning, and there is required use of deionized water for

some processes. Potential future plants can be designed to use low-carbon-intensity energy

sources, and facility construction is anticipated to be possibly colocated with local coal mines with

normal land usage for the plant’s footprint.

 North Dakota has the largest lignite coal deposit in the world, with approximately 25 billion

tons that are economically mined through surface operations from within 1800 ft deep. This is

enough coal resources for potential graphite production by the technologies being developed for

hundreds of years. For the CTP2G pathway, the overall yield from lignite to synthetic graphite is

approximately 15%–25%, with the remainder converted into by-products such as ash, coal tar, and

hydrocarbons. As a result, a large amount of raw lignite is required to produce a relatively modest

amount of high-purity graphite. This underscores the need for careful feedstock selection,

optimized process efficiency, and valorization of by-products to enhance both the economic and

environmental sustainability of the process.

(2) How does the technology remediate legacy environmental impacts of the energy industry,

 including environmental impacts associated with the use of coal?

 The most significant impact is the reduction in legacy carbon dioxide or greenhouse gas

emissions by more than 50% as a result of conversion of coal to carbon products rather than

burning it to make electricity. Potential toxic or hazardous species are much less compared to the

traditional coal power plants and can be recovered in aqueous medium for easy and safe disposal.

 wastes used versus the amount of product formed?

technology. About one-third of the input stream is expected to be the desired graphite product and

waste effluents from projects during implementation. During this project, the amount of waste

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

 The University of North Dakota (UND) Energy & Environmental Research Center (EERC),

in collaboration with the UND Center for Process Engineering Research (CPER), conducted this

project with the aim to validate two technologies capable of converting North Dakota lignite and

lignite coal waste to high-quality graphite for fast-charging lithium-ion battery (LIB) anode. The

TECHNOLOGY READINESS LEVEL OF PROPOSED TECHNOLOGIES

UCOP Process

 The EERC began research into high-value carbon products in 2019 with initial funding from

the State Energy Research Center (SERC) of North Dakota. Through that initial effort, it was

shown that graphene quantum dots (GQDs) can be made from North Dakota lignite and led to a

larger DOE project entitled “Laboratory-Scale Coal-Derived Graphene Process” (DE-

 4

FE0031881), which focused on making high-value carbon products such as graphene from all coal

ranks. The research team successfully produced graphene oxide (GO), reduced graphene oxide

(rGO), and GQDs from lignite, subbituminous coal, bituminous coal, and anthracite coal. These

graphene products were made using a top-down approach starting from synthetic graphite that was

produced from the coals using the UCOP process. Analytical characterization of the produced

graphite showed good crystallinity and degree of graphitization of up to 90%.

 In 2022, this project (DE-FE0032139) was awarded by DOE to further develop the process

for graphite production from North Dakota lignite and lignite coal wastes with the aim of using

the produced graphite for LIB applications. The results obtained to date have helped to define

baseline properties of the graphite materials produced from North Dakota lignite. Significant

improvements have been observed, including a high degree of graphitization up to 95%, low

residual ash content of ~0.013 wt%, low moisture content of ~0.01%, and high carbon purity of

~99.98%. These metrics are very competitive with commercial battery-grade graphite (e.g., purity

≥99.95% and ash <0.2 wt%). In addition, preliminary electrochemical performance tests in half-

coin cells have shown remarkable material stability, better high-rate capability charging

characteristics, and better cycling performance when compared to a commercial battery-grade

graphite. The work performed so far has yielded results that validate the technological process

components in a laboratory environment in batch operations. Thus, the technology is currently

assessed to be at TRL 4.

CTP2G Process

 The status of the CTP2G technology at the beginning of the project was classified as TRL 3

with justification as follows:

 5

 CPER has successfully completed two supplementary projects aimed at developing

advanced anodes for LIBs from lignite-derived feedstock: 1) Si/graphite composite anode from

lignite-derived coal-tar-pitch (DE-FE0031984). The team successfully developed a bench-process

at the scale of approximately one (1) kilogram per batch producing the Si/graphite composite

anode materials from lignite CTP, and the electrochemical performance of the composite anodes

is comparable to the commercial references but at a significantly lower price. 2) Si/graphene

anodes from lignite-derived humic acids (DE-FE-0026825/S00045). The team successfully

developed a bench-scale process (1 kg/batch) for making a low-cost and high-performance porous

silicon/graphene composite anode for LIBs using coal as feedstock. The best anode produced by

this project exhibited remarkable overall battery performance, including a first-cycle reversible

capacity of 1800 mAh/g, first-cycle efficiency of 79%, and a cycling life of 500 cycles with an

85% capacity retention. This performance is one of the best results for similar anodes reported in

the literature and significantly outperforms the project’s objectives of a first-cycle reversible

capacity of 1000 mAh/g, a first-cycle efficiency of 80%, and a cycling life of 300 with an 85%

capacity retention. The outcomes of these projects show that without proper technologies, lignite

derivatives can be used to make battery-grade anode materials.

 The proposed technology in this project (DE-FE-0032139), to produce fast-charging

graphite anodes for LIBs, was a new process with a relatively high risk, but the proof of concept

for making fast-charging graphite has been validated in a low-fidelity experiment. Fast-charging

graphite anode was synthesized via the functionalization of a commercial graphite as feedstock.

The preliminary results show the capacity retention of the fast-charging graphite at 0.5C, 1C, 2C,

and 5C are 89%, 81%, 57%, and 16%, which is significantly higher than 69%, 29%, 9%, and 2%

of the commercial one. The specific capacity 366 mAh/g is above our target value of 345 mAh/g

 6

for a final product, and the initial coulombic efficiency (ICE) is 82%, slightly lower than the target

value of 90% and will be improved by optimizing the functionalization process. Overall, the

electrochemical performance, in particular the fast-charging capability of the fast-charging

graphite, set up a satisfactory baseline for the proposed project.

TECHNOLOGY MATURATION BASED ON THE CURRENT PROJECT

Current Project Activities to Mature UCOP Technology

 During this project, a simplified flow diagram of the key technical pieces of the UCOP

process (Figure 2) were mapped out, although particle engineering, coating/calcination, and

sieving/packaging are outside the scope of the current project. Activities that were carried out

during this project to further mature the technology include finetuning the optimum particle size

distribution (PSD) of lignite feedstock, better mixing ratios of coal and aqueous solutions for

physical and chemical cleaning, designing a control system for conducting heteroatom reactions,

designing new engineering controls for improved safety during carbonization and heat treatment,

and improved graphitization conditions. Research on appropriate equipment for integrating sample

recovery during physical cleaning, chemical cleaning, and heteroatom reduction as well as

clarification of the resulting aqueous fluids for recycling was conducted. Based on the information

that was assembled during this project, a preliminary conceptual drawing of a potential integrated

UCOP process was developed, which is anticipated to be implemented in a bench-scale system as

the next phase in UCOP maturation.

 Additional information about potential critical technological elements (CTEs) was gathered.

The key CTEs for the UCOP process include optimization of the heteroatom reaction chemicals

to improve process economics and designing a suitable graphitization furnace that can

accommodate not only the anticipated larger batches of sample but also attain the proper

temperature for graphitization. Because of the high temperatures needed for graphitization to

obtain high-quality graphite, integrated recovery of low-grade heat to be used for other upfront

processes like drying is being explored as an additional step to improve the overall process

economics. Thus, at the conclusion of this project, the UCOP process is ready for implementation

at TRL 5, where the system components will be integrated to begin the process of creating a

prototype system.

Current Project Activities to Mature CTP2G Technology

 CPER’s CTP2G technology involves producing synthetic graphite from lignite-derived CTP

through three key steps: coking, carbonization, and graphitization. The CTP feedstock was

produced by our partner’s, AmeriCarbon Products LLC’s, proprietary coal liquefaction technology

called “Eco-Pitch™,” a technology that has been demonstrated at a pilot-scale facility. Activities

that were performed during this project to mature the technology include:

 8

  1) The impact of the properties of CTP feedstock, including softening point, ash content, optical

 properties, and quinoline insoluble (QI) on the quality of synthetic graphite products were

 systematically studied, while the investigation of the liquefaction process was excluded from

 the scope of work of the proposed project. The key finding is that the QI value has a more

 profound impact on the quality of the final synthetic graphite than other properties.

  2) An optimal CTP coking and carbonization process in order to identify the optimal needle

 coke for graphitization was established. Key pitch coke performance metrics (morphology

 retention, residual carbon content, surface area, and porosity) were evaluated as a function

 of temperature, time, atmosphere, and pressure. Key findings are that the coking yield is

 sensitive to the coking temperature and pressure and can be increased by up to 15% through

 adjusting the coking conditions.

  3) A functionalization approach was developed to prepare fast-charging graphite from the

 graphite. The key elements include controlling the primary and secondary particle sizes,

 spheritic morphology, surface area, and d-spacing.

  4) A bench-scale (1lb/ batch) synthetic graphite sample was produced, and an LIB prototype

 including coin-cell and 18650 cylindrical cells was fabricated. By the end of the project, we

 have achieved all the key technical performance targets we defined at the beginning of the

 project. Specifically, specific capacity >345 mAh/g, ICE >90%, cycling life >1000 cycles,

 capacity retention >80 % at 1C, and >50% at 2C; the last two attributes are the critical

 measurements to fast charging.

 With the above activities conducted, the CTP2G technology is classified as TRL 5 at the

completion of the proposed project as justified below. Overall, the proposed R&D work was

conducted successfully with all the milestones and target technical performance met. The project

 9

objective of producing fast-charging synthetic graphite from lignite meets the TRL 5 description

as follows: The basic technological components (upgrading lignite and liquefication of lignite into

pitch, carbonization of the intermediate to produce the synthetic graphite, and functionalization of

the synthetic graphite anode to produce fast-charging graphite) are integrated so that the system

configuration is similar to (matches) the final application (LIB anode) in almost all respects. Some

of the requirements for TRL 5, such as the liquefication process, were already achieved prior to

the start of this project. The proposed bench-scale test has generated multiple batches of about 1 lb

of products, which is more than a high-fidelity laboratory-scale system, as required at TRL 5, but

one magnitude lower than a pilot-scale test required at TRL 6. However, the proposed work has

developed data over a larger breadth of testing conditions as well as more complete integration at

the bench scale to prepare for a smooth progression to TRL 6. In addition, our team successfully

fabricated 18650 cylindrical cells with a capacity over 2Ah. The battery performance testing on

large cells such as 18650 cylindrical cells meets DOE requirements for TRL 6 battery technology,

which has a minimal capacity of 200 mAh as a “prototypical system validation in relevant

environment.”

 Based on a discussion provided in the Technology Readiness of the Proposed Technologies

POSTPROJECT R&D TO FURTHER MATURE THE TECHNOLOGIES

 Future R&D to mature the UCOP technology will center around the identified CTEs and

developing a bench-scale system. Optimizing the heteroatom reaction chemicals and designing a

suitable graphitization furnace that can accommodate not only the anticipated larger batches of

 10

sample but also attain the proper temperature for graphitization will be conducted. Options for

integrated recovery of low-grade heat to be used for other upfront processes like drying will be

explored. Fabrication and testing of the integrated pieces in a bench-scale system will be

implemented to further demonstrate process scalability. For the integrated system, process controls

and automation systems that will further improve efficiency, lower costs, and facilitate large-scale

implementation will be developed.

 For CTP2G technology, to attain the next TRL level, the next phase of technology

development will include integration within a pilot-scale (100-kg level) facility, including a pilot-

scale furnace for carbonization and graphitization. A pilot-scale furnace with a carbonization

capacity of 10 kg/batch is already available at UND’s CPER, but up to 10–100 times higher

capacity will be needed to meet the requirement for TRL 6. In addition, a sample conveyor system

will be integrated between carbonization and graphitization by UND experienced engineers to

form a continuous production system. With the encouraging outcomes of this project, UND CPER

is making great efforts to move the CTP2G technology toward commercialization as follows.

McLean Plant: AmeriCarbon Products, UND, North American Coal Corporation, and Barr

Engineering have launched a collaborative initiative to design, build, and operate a commercial-

scale carbon product manufacturing facility in McLean County, North Dakota (“McLean Plant”).

This facility will utilize North Dakota lignite in AmeriCarbon’s patented Liquid Carbon Pitch

(LCP) process to produce Eco-Pitch, a 100% domestically sourced alternative to coal tar pitch—

an essential material for manufacturing synthetic graphite and other carbon-based products. The

project is being advanced under a $1.4 million grant from the North Dakota Industrial Commission

(FY23-102-251) to support initial engineering design. Once complete, the McLean Plant will have

the capacity to manufacture 15,000 tons of primary products annually—including Eco-Pitch,

 11

asphalt binder, and graphite—along with an additional 13,500 tons of by-products. The fully

operational facility, with a projected capital investment of $80 million, is scheduled for completion

following detailed engineering and construction by December 2026.

Collaboration with Packet Digital: UND is actively partnering with Packet Digital, a Fargo-

based company specializing in drone battery technologies. Through this collaboration, UND is

fabricating high-energy-density pouch cells samples for Packet Digital’s drone customers by

testing the fast-charging graphite developed in its research projects. These anodes will undergo

thorough testing and benchmarking against commercial counterparts to validate their enhanced

performance. Additionally, the team is also pursuing funding through the Defense Industrial Base

Consortium’s open announcement (OA-24-01) to support the development of high-capacity

lithium batteries. This multifaceted effort highlights UND’s dedication to advancing next-

generation battery technologies through innovative materials and strategic partnerships.

Considering Packet Digital’s annual demand for fast-charging graphite, the team is actively

seeking funding to establish a pilot-scale production line with two primary goals: 1) directly meet

Packet Digital’s anode material needs for LIBs, and 2) validate the technology at pilot scale in

preparation for the commercial-scale at the Mclean Plant.
