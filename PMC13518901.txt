β-cell FMO3-produced TMAO prevents NF-κB-mediated senescence and inflammation in ageing and diabetic conditions
Authors: Baomin Wang, Yumei Yang, Yuen Man Ho, Huige Lin, Juntao Yuan, Kekao Long, Kenneth King Yip Cheng, Xiaomu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman cells utilize gut microbiota-derived metabolites to control systemic metabolism. Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) is traditionally considered a hepatocyte-derived metabolite from microbial trimethylamine. Here we show that pancreatic β-cells also produce TMAO as an autocrine and intracellular metabolite to maintain β-cell function. β-cells synthesize TMAO via flavin-containing monooxygenase 3 (FMO3), but this machinery deteriorates in humans and rodents under diabetic and ageing conditions. β-cell-specific deletion of FMO3 depletes intracellular TMAO, leading to senescence, inflammation, and defective glucose-stimulated insulin secretion, causing age-dependent glucose intolerance in mice. Loss of FMO3 triggers nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) activation, promoting senescent and inflammatory responses. Mechanistically, TMAO binds to inhibitor of kappa B alpha (IκBα), which inhibits IκBα degradation and NF-κB nuclear translocation, thereby blocking NF-κB-mediated transcription of senescent and inflammatory programs. Replenishment of FMO3 reduces NF-κB activation and senescence in aged human islets. Our findings reveal a protective role of β-cell-derived TMAO against ageing-related β-cell dysfunction.Subject terms: Ageing, Type 2 diabetes, Mechanisms of disease

Introduction

Pancreatic β-cells synthesize and secrete insulin to maintain systemic glucose homeostasis. Overt diabetes occurs when β-cell insulin secretion fails to compensate for peripheral insulin resistance. Dietary glucose is the primary nutrient that triggers insulin synthesis and secretion. Additionally, glucose can induce the secretion of glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP1) in the gut, which further potentiates insulin secretion. Other dietary nutrients, such as amino acids and free fatty acids, can also directly or synergistically interact with glucose to exert insulinotropic effects and modulate β-cell functions1,2. Several dietary nutrients, such as fibers, control β-cell functions via production of short-chain fatty acids through the gut microbiota metabolism3,4. However, whether gut-derived metabolites can be further metabolized or modified by host cells, such as pancreatic β-cells, which in turn regulate β-cell function remains poorly investigated.

Flavin-containing monooxygenase 3 (FMO3) is a xenobiotic enzyme that metabolizes microbial trimethylamine (TMA) into trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO). TMA is generated by gut microbiota through degradation of nitrogen-rich nutrients such as choline, phosphatidylcholine, and L-carnitine5. Circulating TMAO can also be directly obtained from the diet such as fish6 and excreted by the kidneys7. Therefore, TMAO level is regulated by the interaction between host metabolism, gut microbiota, and diet composition.

In the host, hepatic FMO3 expression can be regulated by multiple factors, including the glucose regulatory hormones (including insulin and glucagon), bile acid receptor farnesoid X receptor (FXR), sex hormones, and environmental pollutants such as 2,3,7,8-tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin (TCDD) via aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) activation8–10. Recent studies highlight the important role of hepatic FMO3-TMAO axis in cardiometabolic diseases. Under the diabetic conditions, induction of hepatic FMO3 elevates TMAO that impairs insulin signaling and promotes endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress in the liver, thereby exacerbating glucose intolerance in mouse models8,11,12. Similarly, dietary choline supplementation increases circulating TMAO and induces insulin resistance and glucose intolerance in mice13–15. TMAO also impairs pancreatic β-cells function by inhibiting calcium signaling via NLPR3 inflammasome in mouse models16. In humans, elevated circulating TMAO levels have been observed in cardiometabolic diseases, including obesity, diabetes, atherosclerosis, thrombosis, and hypertension17–19. The TMAO reduction positively associates with improved insulin sensitivity in humans receiving dietary intervention and weight loss20. On the contrary, there are also evidence showing protective role of FMO3 and TMAO in metabolic health. Subcutaneous infusion of TMAO ameliorates high-fat-diet-induced glucose intolerance by enhancing insulin secretion. Treatment with TMAO alleviates glucolipotoxicity in pancreatic β-cells21. In obese humans who underwent bariatric surgery showed a 2-fold increase of circulating TMAO after 1 year22. In response to calorie restriction, hepatic FMO3 is upregulated to counteract ageing-induced inflammation, oxidative stress and steatosis in the liver23. These findings indicate that the FMO3-TMAO axis exerts both detrimental and beneficial effects on metabolic homeostasis, but the underlying reasons for these divergent effects remain unclear.

While FMO3 is predominantly expressed in the liver, it is also found in other tissues, such as the thyroid, adrenal glands, aorta, and adipose tissue. These extrahepatic FMO3 may contribute to TMAO production in the body24–28. Indeed, reduction of hepatic FMO3 expression by 90% using anti-sense oligonucleotide only lead a very modest decrease in circulating TMAO levels ( ~ 20–30%) in rodents8,29,30. This suggests that the liver may not be the sole site of TMAO production, or that other FMO protein family members compensate for TMAO production. Indeed, our recent study showed that adipose FMO3 also contributes to elevated TMAO level during ageing28. Interestingly, FMO3 mRNA has also been detected in the pancreas25. As mentioned above, previous studies have demonstrated that circulating or exogeneous TMAO exerts both detrimental or protective effects on pancreatic β-cell functions21,31. Given that FMO3 is traditionally recognized as a hepatic enzyme, the role of β-cell FMO3 and its derived TMAO on β-cell functions has not been adequately investigated. In this study, we investigate the role of β-cell FMO3 and its derived TMAO on β-cell functions using a β-cell specific FMO3 knockout mouse model and human pancreas/islets. Contrary to a recent study showing the detrimental effect of hepatic FMO3 derived TMAO on β-cell functions16, we here demonstrate that β-cell FMO3 and its derived intracellular TMAO prevents β-cell senescence, defective glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS), and inflammation in an age-dependent manner.

Results

Pancreatic β-cells produce TMAO via FMO3

To comprehensively understand TMAO metabolism in different tissues, we intraperitoneally injected deuterated trimethylamine (d9-TMA) into 12-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice. Metabolomics analysis revealed that circulating levels of d9-TMAO, the catalytic product of d9-TMA, increased sharply and then declined at 30- and 120-minute post-injection, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1a). d9-TMAO was also detectable in multiple tissues, including the liver, pancreas, various fat depots, kidney, and skeletal muscles (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1b). As expected, the highest concentrations of d9-TMAO were found in the liver and kidney (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1b), which is consistent with their roles in TMAO biosynthesis (liver) and excretion (kidney), as previously reported7,32. Surprisingly, at the 120-minute time point, d9-TMAO concentration in the pancreas was higher than that in the liver (Fig. 1a). Despite expressing lower FMO3 protein levels compared to the liver, the pancreas achieved comparable TMAO production from TMA as in the liver, suggesting that the pancreas may also play a crucial role in TMAO metabolism and/or TMAO plays a role in pancreas function (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1c). In middle-aged male humans, we confirmed that the rate of d9-TMAO biosynthesis from d9-TMA was significantly higher in pancreatic islets than in liver explants (Fig. 1k), while TMAO biosynthesis was diminished in pancreatic islets isolated from patients with type 2 diabetes (Fig. 1k). In contrast, the hepatic d9-TMAO biosynthetic ability was comparable between normal and diabetic subjects (Fig. 1k).

Hepatic FMO3 and TMAO levels are higher in female mice than that in male mice32. We next investigated whether (1) pancreatic islets/β-cells are equipped with the FMO3-TMAO production machinery; (2) if so, whether its capacity is higher than that in primary hepatocytes; and (3) the FMO3-TMAO machinery is sexually dimorphic. To this end, pancreatic islets and primary hepatocytes were isolated from 8-week-old male and female C57BL/6 J mice. In line with previous studies32–35, both mRNA and protein expressions of FMO3 were significantly higher in the hepatocytes from female mice than those from male mice (Fig. 1c, d). Although Fmo3 mRNA levels in pancreatic islets were 99% lower than those in primary hepatocytes from the same animal, their protein expressions in the islet reached 50–60% of the levels in primary hepatocytes from male mice (Fig. 1c, d). Interestingly, mRNA and protein levels of FMO3 in pancreatic islets were similar between male and female mice, indicating that islet FMO3 expression is not sexually dimorphic (Fig. 1c-d). Next, we evaluated TMAO production by incubating primary hepatocytes and islets with d9-TMA for 24 h. Consistent with the difference in FMO3 protein expression, hepatocytes exhibited a higher ability to generate d9-TMA-derived d9-TMAO compared to pancreatic islets in both sexes (Fig. 1f). However, when normalized to total protein concentration in cell lysate, intercellular production rate of d9-TMAO was higher in islets than in hepatocytes (Fig. 1e, f). Align with the comparable FMO3 expression, there was no difference in TMAO production and secretion ability in the islets between male and female (Fig. 1e). We confirmed that human islets also expressed FMO3 and produced TMAO, but TMAO production was reduced in the diabetic islets (Fig. 1h–k). Next, we determined which cell types in pancreatic islets express FMO3. Immunofluorescent staining revealed that FMO3 mainly co-localized with insulin but not glucagon in pancreatic islets of human and mice, suggesting that this TMAO-producing enzyme is abundantly expressed in β-cells (Fig. 1g, j). Previous studies suggest that TMA is produced by gut microbiota36. To confirm this, we treated C57BL/6 J mice with broad-spectrum antibiotics to deplete gut microbiota. As expected, LC-MS/MS analysis showed a significant reduction of serum TMA levels in the mice treated with antibiotics compared with those treated with PBS (Supplementary Fig. 1d, e). Furthermore, rat INS-1E and mouse MIN6 pancreatic β-cells also expressed FMO3 and were able to produce and secrete TMAO at similar capacity. (Supplementary Fig. 2a).

Next, we examined whether FMO3 is responsible for TMAO production in pancreatic β-cells. siRNA-mediated Fmo3 silencing resulted in a marked reduction of FMO3 protein as well as d9-TMAO production and secretion in INS-1E and MIN6 cells (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). To further confirm the role of FMO3 in β-cell TMAO biosynthesis, we generated a β-cell specific FMO3 knockout (β-FMO3 KO) mouse model by crossing Fmo3floxed/floxed mice with a transgenic mouse expressing Cre recombinase under the control of the rat insulin II promoter (RIP-Cre mice) (Supplementary Fig. 3a). β-FMO3 KO mice exhibited a marked reduction of FMO3 protein in insulin-positive β-cells compared to their wild-type (WT) littermates (Fig. 2a, b and Supplementary Fig. 3b). β-cell specific deletion of FMO3 abrogated intra-islet TMAO synthesis without altering circulating and hepatic TMAO levels (Fig. 2c, d and Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). The uptake of TMA into pancreatic islets isolated from β-FMO3 KO and WT controls were similar (Supplementary Fig. 3e). These data suggest that impaired TMAO production in FMO3 deficiency islets results from defective enzymatic conversion (Supplementary Fig. 3e). In addition, β-cell FMO3-derived TMAO perhaps functions locally within islets, given the unaltered circulating TMAO in β-FMO3 KO mice.

β-cell specific deletion of FMO3 induces β-cell dysfunction and progressive glucose intolerance during ageing

To investigate the physiological functions of FMO3 in β-cells, β-FMO3 KO mice and their WT littermates as well as RIP-Cre controls were subjected to phenotypic characterization for one year (48 weeks) (Supplementary Fig. 3f). At 8 weeks old, β-FMO3 KO mice exhibited normal glucose tolerance, insulin secretion and insulin sensitivity compared with WT and RIP-Cre controls (Supplementary Fig. 3g–i). However, starting at 12 weeks of age, β-FMO3 KO mice displayed mild glucose intolerance (Fig. 2e, f). This change was primarily due to defective GSIS (Fig. 2h–j) but not insulin resistance (Supplementary Fig. 3j–l). Of note, since RIP-Cre controls and WT controls exhibited similar glucose tolerance and/or insulin secretion at different ages, we only included WT littermates for subsequent comparison with β-FMO3 KO mice. Pancreatic islets from 16-week-old β-FMO3 KO mice also displayed defective GSIS compared to those isolated from WT controls (Fig. 2k). Likewise, siRNA-mediated Fmo3 silencing abolished GSIS in INS-1E cells (Supplementary Fig. 2d), and such a defect could be partially counteracted by 100 μM TMAO supplementation (Fig. 2k and Supplementary Fig. 2d). To further determine the concentration-dependent effect of TMAO on insulin secretion, INS-1E cells were treated with increasing doses of TMAO. Only 100 μM TMAO, but not the lower concentrations, significantly potentiated GSIS. (Supplementary Fig. 2e). Notably, TMAO supplementation restored total intracellular TMAO (unlabeled TMAO+d9-TMAO) levels in INS-1E cells with Fmo3 silencing to approximately 50% of the levels observed in siScramble-transfected cells (Supplementary Fig. 2f). On the other hand, overexpression of wild-type FMO3, potentiated both TMAO biosynthesis and GSIS in INS-1E cells (Supplementary Fig. 2g–i), whereas the FMO3 P153L mutant (without TMA to TMAO conversion ability) did not exhibit these potentiating effects. In contrast to a recent study showing the detrimental effects of liver-derived TMAO on β-cell functions16, our findings suggest that β-cell FMO3-derived TMAO potentiates GSIS.

β-cell FMO3-derived TMAO prevents senescent response and maintains GSIS in pancreatic β-cells during ageing

To understand the effect of β-cell FMO3 deletion on transcriptomic levels, we conducted RNA sequencing (RNAseq) analysis on pancreatic islets isolated from 48-week-old β-FMO3 KO mice and their WT controls. There were 1353 genes differentially expressed between two genotypes, with 990 genes upregulated and 363 downregulated (log2 fold change ≥1 or ≤−1.0; adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05). KEGG analysis indicated that the pathways related to TNF-α, NF-κB, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction and type 1 diabetes, were enriched by β-cell specific deletion of FMO3 (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Transcriptional Regulatory Relationships Unraveled by Sentence-based Text mining (TRRUST v2) indicated that NF-κB RELA (p65) is the top-ranked transcription factor responsible for the upregulated genes in FMO3-deficient islets (Supplementary Fig. 4c).

Activation of canonical NF-κB triggers inflammatory cytokine expression, apoptosis, and chemotaxis in β-cells under diabetic conditions37–40. Recent studies showed that NF-κB acts as a key transcriptional regulator of senescent-associated secretory proteins (SASP) in various cell types41–45, but whether and how its activation contributes to β-cell senescence is unknown. In addition to the well-established NF-κB-regulated cytokines/chemokines (such as Il-1β, Tnf-α, Ccl5, Cxcl10, etc.), our transcriptomics data also indicted that the conventional senescent markers, including p53 and Cdkn1a (known as p21) but not Cdkn2a (known as p16), were upregulated in FMO3 deficient islets (Fig. 3a, b). The β-gal positive signals (indicative of cellular senescence) within the islets, as well as p21 protein expression and RelA phosphorylation at serine 563 (pRelA; a surrogate marker of RelA activity) in insulin positive β-cells were increased in β-FMO3 KO mice (Fig. 3c-e). This was further confirmed by immunoblotting analysis which showed the same trend of p21 and pRelA induction in pancreatic islets isolated from β-FMO3 KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 4d).

Next, we examined whether FMO3 deficiency-induced NF-κB activation and senescence are due to defective TMAO biosynthesis. In the subsequent experiment, we mainly measured p53/p21 expression and β-gal signals to reflect cellular senescence, as well as pRelA and pIκBα expressions to reflect NF-κB activation, unless otherwise specified. Ex vivo, treatment with TMAO counteracted the upregulation of the senescent markers, and NF-κB activation in the FMO3-deficient islets (Fig. 3f, g). To confirm whether the effects of the FMO3-TMAO axis on the NF-κB pathway and senescence in β-cells are cell-autonomous, we transfected INS-1E cells with siRNA against Fmo3 or Scramble control, then treated them with or without TMAO. The three groups, including Scramble, siFmo3 and siFmo3 + TMAO, were subjected to RNAseq analysis. Transcriptomic data indicated that NF-κB, p53-p21 and TNF-α pathways were upregulated by Fmo3 silencing, which is consistent with the RNAseq data from the FMO3-deficient pancreatic islets (Fig. 4a, b). However, Fmo3 silencing or TMAO treatment exerted no obvious effect on mRNA expression of Cdkn2a, another senescent pathway known to control β-cell functions46. The enrichments in p53-associated senescence and NF-κB related inflammation in siFmo3-transfected INS-1E cells were reversed by TMAO supplementation (Fig. 4c, d). TRRUST analysis also indicated that RelA is the key transcriptional factor contributing to the changes induced by Fmo3 silencing (Fig. 4e). Consistently, immunoblotting also showed that pRelA and pIκBα were upregulated, while total IκBα was downregulated in INS-1E cells with Fmo3 silencing or FMO3-deficient islets. These changes were reinstated by TMAO treatment (Fig. 4f, g). TMAO has been shown to bind with PERK and modulate ER stress in hepatocytes12. However, our RNAseq data showed that Fmo3 silencing did not alter ER stress responses in INS-1E cells (Supplementary Fig. 5a). In addition, Fmo3 silencing had no obvious impact on total and phosphorylation of PERK, excluding the involvement of the PERK pathway (Fig. 4f). Although Foxo1 emerged as a potential regulator in TRRUST analysis (Fig. 4e), immunoblotting revealed that neither total FOXO1 nor phospho-FOXO1 levels were altered upon FMO3 silencing, suggesting that the Foxo1 pathway is not directly modulated by the FMO3–TMAO axis in β-cells (Supplementary Fig. 5c).

TNF-α-mediated NF-κB activation impairs β-cell functions in ageing-related diabetes40,47. Next, we examined whether FMO3-derived TMAO protects β-cells against TNF-α-induced NF-κB activation and senescence. Treatment with TNF-α induced NF-κB signaling activation, accompanied with an induction of senescence in INS-1E cells transfected with GFP control (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). Overexpression of WT-FMO3, but not its P153L mutant, partially abolished TNF-α-induced senescence and NF-κB activation (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). Likewise, treatment with TMAO also abrogated the effects of TNF-α on phosphorylation of RelA and IκBα, as well as IκB degradation, but had no impact on total and phosphorylated IKKα/β, the immediate upstream regulators of RelA and IκB-α in INS-1E cells (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d).

To confirm whether NF-κB activation is responsible for the β-cell dysfunction in β-FMO3 KO mice, we treated β-FMO3 KO mice and their WT controls with the NF- κB inhibitor BAY11-7082 for 4-weeks. The glucose intolerance and defective GSIS in β-FMO3 KO mice were reversed by pharmacological inhibition of NF-κB (Fig. 5a, b). Ex vivo assay also showed that BAY11-7082 treatment improved GSIS in FMO3-deficient islets (Fig. 5c). The BAY11-7082-mediated improvement was associated with a significant reduction of pRelA + and p21+ β-cells as well as mRNA levels of the SASP genes in β-FMO3 KO mice (Fig. 5d, f).

To ascertain whether β-cell FMO3-derived TMAO improves β-cell function in vivo, we administered the 12-week-old β-FMO3 KO and WT mice with adeno-associated virus (AAV) expressing FLAG-tagged WT-FMO3, FLAG-tagged FMO3 P153L mutant, or GFP control under the control of the rat insulin promoter to the pancreas via the bile duct. Four groups of animals were included: 1. WT + AAV-GFP; 2. β-FMO3 KO + AAV-GFP; 3. β-FMO3 KO + AAV-WT-FMO3, and 4. β-FMO3 KO + AAV-FMO3-P153L. We verified that exogenous FMO3 was expressed only in the pancreatic islets, but not in the exocrine cells of β-FMO3 KO mice (Fig. 6a). In addition, AAV-WT-FMO3 or AAV-FMO3-P153L did not alter circulating TMAO levels (Fig. 6b). AAV-mediated replenishment of WT FMO3 but not its P153L mutant restored GSIS and improved glucose tolerance in β-FMO3 KO mice (Fig. 6c–f). Pancreatic islets isolated from β-FMO3 KO + AAV-GFP exhibited defective GSIS, and elevated senescent gene expression compared to those from WT + AAV-GFP (Fig. 6g, h). Replenishment of WT-FMO3 but not the P153L mutant rectified the elevated senescent and inflammatory gene expression as well as GSIS in the FMO3 null islets (Fig. 6g, h). Taken together, these data demonstrate that β-cell autonomous FMO3-derived TMAO inhibits NF-κB activation and senescence, at least in part contributing to the maintenance of β-cell function.

FMO3-TMAO axis mediates the crosstalk between NF-κB and p53 in pancreatic β-cells

The crosstalk between NF-κB and p53 is known to control multiple cellular functions, including senescence, inflammation and etc48. We further investigated whether and how the FMO3-TMAO axis modulates the NF-κB and p53 crosstalk in β-cells. To this end, we first tested whether concomitant silencing of p53 reverses Fmo3 silencing-induced senescence and NF-κB activation in INS-1E cells. The promoting effects of Fmo3 silencing on p53 and p21 mRNA and protein expressions but not on pRelA, pIκBα, and IκBα downregulation and NF-κB-dependent cytokine expression were largely antagonized by concomitant p53 silencing in INS-1E cells (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). On the other hand, pharmacological inhibition of NF-κB using BAY11-7082 abolished both NF-κB activation and p53 and p21 mRNA and protein expressions in INS-1E cells transfected with siRNA against Fmo3 (Supplementary Fig. 7c, d). These data suggest that FMO3 suppresses p53-dependent senescence via inhibiting NF-κB activation. A previous study showed that RelA binds to the p53 promoter and enhances its transcriptional activity49. We therefore hypothesized that FMO3-derived TMAO inhibits NF-κB-induced p53 transcription via binding to the p53 promoter. To test this hypothesis, the p53 promoter region containing the NF-κB binding site or its mutant without NF-κB ability was cloned into a luciferase vector and transfected into INS-1E cells. The luciferase assay showed that treatment with TNF-α significantly induced p53 promoter activity, but such an effect was counteracted by TMAO treatment (Supplementary Fig. 7e). The luciferase activity of the p53 promoter without NF-κB binding ability was refractory to TNF-α or TMAO treatment (Supplementary Fig. 7e). In addition, siRNA-mediated Fmo3 silencing increased p53 promoter activity, but such an effect could be neutralized by TMAO or BAY11-7082 (Supplementary Fig. 7f). Taken together, these data suggest that FMO3-derived TMAO antagonizes NF-κB-mediated p53 transcriptional expression and subsequent senescence responses.

TMAO has been shown to modulate cellular signaling transduction via direct binding to its target protein12. We thus hypothesized that TMAO interacts with key proteins in the NF-κB and p53 pathways. Drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assay revealed that TMAO dose-dependently prevented protease-induced degradation of IκBα and RelA, but not IKKα/β, MDM2 (an E3 ligase targeting p53 degradation)50 or p53 in INS-1E cell lysate (Fig. 7a). Conversely, TMA did not show any effect on the proteinase-induced degradation of RelA, IκBα or IKKα/β in the cell lysate (Fig. 7b). To prove the effect is direct and not mediated by interacting proteins, we incubated recombinant RelA and IκBα proteins with proteinase in the presence or absence of TMAO in vitro. TMAO only prevented proteinase-induced degradation of IκBα but not RelA proteins (Fig. 7c, d).

Inhibition of the FMO3-TMAO axis activates NF-κB by regulation of RelA-IκBα interaction and IκBα stability

IκBα inhibits NF-κB activation by sequestering RelA in the cytoplasm. IκBα is an unstable protein and subjected to proteosome degradation upon TNF-α stimulation51,52. On the other hand, IκBα stability can be enhanced by the RelA binding53,54. We hypothesized that TMAO inhibits RelA activation by promoting IκBα stability via the RelA-IκBα interaction. Co-immunoprecipitation assay showed that TMAO enhanced the interaction between IκBα and RelA in INS-1E cells under both basal and TNF-α-stimulated conditions (Fig.7e, f). Consistent with our findings in β-cell lines, treatment with TMAO also significantly enhanced the interaction between endogenous IκBα and RelA in human islets (Fig. 7g). Subcellular fractionation experiment revealed that pretreatment with TMAO significantly blunted TNF-α-induced nuclear translocation of RelA, while concurrently elevating cytoplasmic IκBα levels, further supporting the role of TMAO in sequestering NF-κB in the cytoplasm (Supplementary Fig. 8a). On the contrary, knockdown of FMO3 diminished the IκBα and RelA interaction and IκBα protein abundance, but such effect of FMO3 knockdown was rescued by TMAO treatment (Fig. 7h). In vitro assay showed that the interaction between recombinant RelA and IκBα proteins was enhanced by TMAO (Fig. 7i). Cycloheximide (CHX) protein chase experiment showed that TMAO treatment decelerated TNF-α-induced IκBα degradation in INS-1E cells (Supplementary Fig. 8b). Treatment with the proteasomal inhibitor MG132 rescued the IκBα reduction in INS-1E cells with silencing of Fmo3 (Supplementary Fig. 8c).

Previous studies showed that the 20S core component of the multi-complex 26S proteasome is sufficient to degrade IκBα protein in vitro. The RelA -bound IκBα is more stable and more compact than free IκBα52,53. In addition, the binding of RelA mask the degradation signal of IκBα52,53. Next, we examined whether TMAO prevents proteosome-mediated IκBα degradation and, if so, whether it requires RelA interaction using the in vitro approach as previously described52,53. Incubation with TMAO abolished 20S proteosome-induced recombinant IκBα degradation (Supplementary Fig. 8d). The inhibitory ability of TMAO on 20S-induced IκBα degradation was similar in the presence or absence of recombinant RelA protein (Supplementary Fig. 8d). To definitively rule out the possibility that TMAO suppresses NF-κB signaling via direct inhibition of IKK kinase activity, we performed an in vitro kinase assay using recombinant IKKβ. TMAO, even at high concentrations up to 1 mM, had no effect on IKKβ kinase activity, whereas the specific IKK inhibitor MLN120B potently suppressed the activity (Supplementary Fig. 8e). In addition, pre-incubation of full-length IκBα or IκBα-RelA complex with TMAO did not affect the kinase activity of IKKβ either (Supplementary Fig. 8f). This data confirmed that the inhibition of NF-κB by TMAO is not mediated through IKK. Our findings suggest that on one hand, TMAO binds with IκBα and prevents its degradation by proteasome, thereby maintaining IκBα expression. This action of TMAO does not require the presence of RelA. On the other hand, TMAO binds IκBα and enhances its interaction with RelA, which in turn restricts nuclear translocation of RelA. In line with these observations, co-immunoprecipitation analysis showed that TMAO pretreatment did not alter the association of IκBα and RelA with IKK upon TNF-α stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 8g). Collectively, our findings suggest that TMAO stabilizes cytoplasmic IκBα and hence prevents nuclear translocation of RelA, thereby blocking the NF-κB dependent transcriptional senescent and pro-inflammatory program in β-cells.

Removal of senescent β-cells alleviates β-cell dysfunctions in β-FMO3 KO mice

β-cell senescence has been demonstrated to either positively or negatively regulate β-cell functions and glucose metabolism. These differential effects could be attributed to the activation of distinct senescent pathways (p53-p21 vs p16), the type and levels of stimuli, and/or the stages and types of diabetes55–57. Therefore, it remains unknown whether FMO3 deficiency-induced senescence contribute to β-cell dysfunction such as defective GSIS. Another notable characteristic of senescent cells is their resistance to apoptosis, which is mainly due to the activation of several anti-apoptotic pathways such as BCL2, Akt/PKB, HSP90 and HIF-1α58–61. ABT263 (navitoclax) is a senolytic drug designed to eliminate senescent cells by inhibiting the BCL2 pathway. Given that the gene expressions of BCL2 and BCL-xL were induced in FMO3 deficient islets (Fig. 3b), we examined whether elimination of senescent cells using ABT263 improves the β-cell functions of β-FMO3 KO mice. Ex vivo, ABT263 treatment significantly inhibited the senescent program in the islets of β-FMO3 KO mice, as evidenced by reduced β-gal staining (Supplementary Fig. 9a), decreased expression of p53 and p21, and SASP, compared to those treated with a vehicle for 48 h (Supplementary Fig. 9b, c). The reduction in senescence was accompanied by the restoration of GSIS in FMO3-deficient islets (Supplementary Fig. 9d). In vivo, a four-week treatment with ABT263 potentiated GSIS and improved glucose tolerance in 16-week-old β-FMO3 KO mice compared to those treated with vehicle (Supplementary Fig. 9e, f). Such changes were associated with reduced number and intensity of p21-positive β-cells in β-FMO3 KO mice treated with ABT263 (Supplementary Fig. 9g). In summary, these findings suggest that the accumulation of senescent β-cells contributes to defective GSIS and glucose dysmetabolism in β-FMO3 KO mice.

Expression of β-cell FMO3 and TMAO production decrease with age and diabetes and exert anti-senescent and NF-κB effects in mouse and human islets

To investigate whether FMO3 expression is influenced by age and diabetic conditions, we analyzed mRNA and protein expression of FMO3 in human islets. Immunofluorescent staining revealed that FMO3 protein expression was lower in aged individuals with normoglycemia when compared to young individuals with normoglycemia (Fig. 8a, b). This age-dependent decline in FMO3 expression was further confirmed by immunoblotting, (Fig. 8c). The FMO3 decline was accompanied with a concomitant increase of nuclear RelA expression (Fig. 8c). FMO3 protein expression was further reduced in aged individuals with diabetes (Fig. 8a–c). The FMO3 reduction was accompanied with an increase of a cluster of senescent and inflammatory markers including TP53, CDKN1A, IL-1β and MCP1 in the aged individuals with diabetes when compared with young or aged individuals with normoglycemia (Fig. 8d). The aged islets also displayed reduced TMAO biosynthetic ability when compared to those isolated from young individuals (Fig. 8e). On the contrary, overexpression of FMO3 reduced these senescent and inflammatory genes in the aged islets of humans with hyperglycemia (Fig. 8f-i). Consistent with our findings in β-cell lines, FMO3 overexpression only reduced phosphorylation of RelA and IκBα but not IKKα/β in human islets (Fig. 8f). We confirmed that β-cell FMO3 and TMAO biosynthesis was also reduced in pancreatic islets of 24-month-old male C57BL/6 J mice (aged; equivalent to 55–65-year-old in humans) compared to those in 3-month-old C57BL/6 J mice (young control; equivalent to 20-30 years old in humans) (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). On the contrary, hepatic FMO3 expression and its TMAO production ability were similar between young and aged mice (Supplementary Fig. 10b).

To mimic ageing in a cell model, we treated INS-1E cells with the DNA damage inducer doxorubicin as previously described62–64. As expected, doxorubicin provoked expression of the ageing markers including p53 and p21, but it significantly downregulated FMO3 expression and TMAO production (Supplementary Fig. 10c, d). Overexpression of FMO3 abolished doxorubicin-induced senescent program in INS-1E cells (Supplementary Fig. 10e). Taken together, these findings suggest that reduction of β-cell FMO3 and its derived TMAO contribute to β-cell dysfunction in ageing and diabetes.

Discussion

Type 2 diabetes is an age-related disease featured by gradual deterioration of insulin secretion and increased β-cell senescence and inflammation. Our study reveals that human and rodent β-cells are able to metabolize gut microbiota-derived TMA into TMAO via FMO3. This FMO3-TMAO axis is essential for preventing NF-κB activation and its associated β-cell dysfunctions, including senescence, inflammation, and defective GSIS, in an age-dependent manner. Our findings further reinforce the concept that the proper interaction between microbiota-derived metabolite and β-cells is important for metabolic homeostasis.

Circulating TMAO is elevated in cardiometabolic diseases, including type 2 diabetes, but the effect of this small metabolite can be context-dependent, exhibiting either detrimental or beneficial outcomes. While a recent study showed that high-choline diet-mediated systemic TMAO elevation impaired GSIS in C57BL/6 J mice, which was abrogated by global Fmo3 knockout16, and that treatment with 100 nM TMAO impaired GSIS in MIN6 β-cells by disrupting calcium influx and mitochondrial respiration16. Our results demonstrated that β-cell FMO3-derived TMAO exerts multiple beneficial effects, including potentiation of GSIS and inhibition of NF-κB-dependent senescence and inflammation. This discrepancy may be attributed to different approaches used, wherein we used β-cell specific knockout and overexpression of FMO3 mouse models, Fmo3 silencing β-cell line and adenoviral-mediated FMO3 overexpression in human islets models to examine the effect of altered β-cell FMO3 expression and functions of intracellular TMAO, while the aforementioned study focused on the effect of systemic TMAO on β-cell functions. On the other hand, our findings are consistent with two in vitro β-cell studies in which TMAO treatment at micromolar to millimolar concentrations enhances insulin secretion and inhibits ER stress in INS-1E cells under diabetogenic conditions31,65. The defective GSIS in FMO3 null islets or silenced β-cells might be due to the influence of the FMO3-TMAO axis on mitochondrial metabolism via a NF-κB-dependent manner, as our RNAseq data showed a significant downregulation of mitochondrial oxidation in INS-1E cells with FMO3 silencing (Supplementary Fig. 5b). Inhibition of NF-κB partially reversed FMO3 deficiency-induced defective GSIS in pancreatic islets. Of note, NF-κB has been recently shown to regulate multiple mitochondrial TCA cycle gene expression66. Taken in conjugation, these findings suggest the importance of β-cell FMO3-TMAO axis in β-cell functions (Supplementary Fig. 11).

The underlying mechanism(s) by which TMAO regulates cellular processes, such as inflammation and senescence remains poorly understood. Metabolites are able to bind to, and activate or inactivate protein functions67–69. For instance, glutamate dehydrogenase-derived alpha-ketoglutarate directly binds to and activates IKKβ, thereby promoting NF-κB-mediated glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) expression and glucose uptake in brain tumor70. Here, we showed that TMAO directly binds with IκBα and hence stabilizes IκBα as well as enhances the IκBα-RelA interaction and hence blocks nuclear translocation of RelA. On the other hand, our findings show that TMAO does not affect the kinase activity of IKKβ and its binding with IκBα and RelA. These TMAO actions are supposed to inhibit the transcriptional programs of NF-κB in pancreatic β-cells. Our data suggest a direct physical interaction between TMAO and IκBα, but not other NF-κB components such as IKKβ, IKKα and RelA. Knockdown of FMO3 and TMAO reduction diminished RelA-IκBα interaction and promoted their phosphorylation without affecting IKKα/β phosphorylation. Our in vitro assay demonstrated that TMAO prevents 20S proteosome-induced IκBα degradation in the absence or presence of RelA. TMAO also exerted no effect on IKKβ kinase activity. Notably, TMAO is known to restore misfolded proteins and control protein oligomerization71. However, the molecular basis of TMAO effects on IκBα folding and IκBα-RelA interaction requires further investigation. Collectively, we propose that TMAO binds to and inhibits IκBα degradation and promotes RelA-IκBα interaction, both of which restrict NF-κB-dependent inflammatory cytokine expression and p53-induced senescence.

Consistent with our study showing the anti-inflammatory/SASP effects of FMO3 in β-cells, knockdown of hepatic Fmo3 has been shown to promote inflammation in the liver. However, whether the anti-inflammatory effects of hepatic FMO3 are due to TMAO biosynthesis and NF-κB inhibition has not been explored9. In contrast, the majority of previous studies showed that exogeneous TMAO triggers NF-κB activation and subsequent inflammation in multiple cell types, including smooth muscle cells, endothelial cells, osteoclasts and adipocytes, in a reactive oxidative species (ROS) or inflammasome-dependent manner28,72,73. On the other hand, a recent study showed that short-term treatment with exogeneous TMAO downregulates the TNF-α pathway in endothelial cells, whereas long-term treatment exerts the opposite effect74. The differential effects of TMAO on NF-κB activation in various cell types might be attributed to differences in experimental design (such as the concentration and duration of TMAO treatment), the source of TMAO (endogenous versus exogenous), and cell-type-specific difference/threshold in ROS tolerance.

Senescence is one of the root causes of ageing-related diseases, including diabetes. β-cell senescence, often induced by peripheral insulin resistance, DNA damage and inflammatory microenvironment within the pancreatic islets, is commonly observed in type 1 and type 2 daibetes75. Removal of senescent β-cells using senolytic drugs improves ageing-dependent diabetes55. Here we showed that loss of β-cell FMO3-TMAO axis is a contributor to β-cell senescence. We observed a significant reduction of β-cell FMO3 expression and TMAO biosynthesises in both aged human and rodent islets. The p53-p21 and p16 axes are two major arms of the senescent responses that initiate cell cycle arrest and SASP in multiple cell types76–79. Activation of the p53-p21 axis diminishes GSIS and β-cell proliferation, whereas p16 overexpression augments GSIS and promotes β-cell maturation75,80–82. In our study, we observed that β-cell FMO3-derived TMAO mainly controls the p53-p21 but not the p16 axis, as silencing of p53 largely abolished the upregulation of p21 protein expression in FMO3-silenced INS-1E cells. Aged islets are infiltrated with macrophages expressing TNF-α, which can activate NF-κB in pancreatic β-cells83. In addition, acinar cells release TNF-α that induces β-cell apoptosis in ageing84. Our RNAseq data showed that TNF-α and NF-κB pathways are upregulated in islets/β-cells upon inactivation of FMO3, suggesting its involvement in ageing responses. Inhibition of NF-κB reversed FMO3 deletion/silencing-induced p53 and p21 upregulation. In contrast, p53 silencing is unable to reverse FMO3-silencing-induced NF-κB activation in INS-1E cells. These data suggest that NF-κB precedes p53 activation in regulating the p53-p21 axis. Further analysis showed that p53 promoter activity is induced by NF-κB activation but antagonized by FMO3-derived TMAO in β-cells. Consistent with our study, NF-κB activation induced by the loss of IκBα also triggers senescence in cancer cells, as reflected by higher expression of multiple SASP85. Apart from TNF-α, we also demonstrated that FMO3-derived TMAO prevents doxorubicin-induced senescence in INS-1E cells, and their deficiency promotes β-cell senescence in vivo. These findings support a key protective role of TMAO against β-cell senescence.

FMO3 is traditionally regarded as a hepatic enzyme, recent studies showed that it can also be detected in the aorta, adrenal gland, and thyroid in rodents8,86. However, its physiological functions and TMAO biosynthesis ability in the extrahepatic tissues remain poorly investigated. We demonstrated that pancreatic β-cells also express FMO3 protein and produce TMAO, yet β-cell FMO3 does not influence circulating TMAO level. Although the FMO3 mRNA and protein levels in pancreatic islets/β-cells are significantly lower than those in hepatocytes, their TMAO production rate is similar in the presence of the same amount of TMA substrate. This finding suggests that the TMAO-producing ability of FMO3 might be controlled by a post-translational modification. Hepatic FMO3 is known to be controlled by sex hormones, but our finding showed that FMO3 expression in islets is comparable between males and females. On the other hand, we showed that TNF-α treatment reduced FMO3 expression in INS-1E cells. As mentioned above, TNF-α from macrophages/acinar cells is increased in pancreas during ageing83,84. Taken in conjunction, we propose that age-related increase in TNF-α downregulates FMO3 and hence TMAO production in pancreatic β-cells, which causes NF-κB-dependent SASP and p53-dependent cell cycle arrest. The SASP further recruit inflammatory macrophages infiltration in the islets, thereby forming a viscous cycle and resulting β-cell dysfunction in ageing.

β-cell-specific deletion of FMO3 or its reconstitution did not alter circulating TMAO levels, despite significant depletion or restoration of intracellular TMAO within islets, respectively. The dissociation of β-cell FMO3 expression and circulating TMAO suggests three non-exclusive possibilities: (1) The contribution of β-cell FMO3 to systemic TMAO is negligible compared to hepatic FMO3, given the greater liver mass and its higher FMO3 expression; (2) TMAO produced by β-cells is compartmentalized and metabolically sequestered within pancreatic tissue, potentially through local degradation or limited diffusion; (3) β-cell-derived TMAO is excreted directly into the gastrointestinal tract via pancreatic exocrine secretions, bypassing systemic circulation. Our study demonstrates that β-cell FMO3-derived TMAO functions as an autocrine/paracrine protector against senescence and inflammation. A key limitation of our work is the unresolved mechanism of TMA uptake into β-cells. While organic cation transporters (OCT1/2) mediate TMA/TMAO flux in hepatocytes87, their role in β-cells remains untested. Elucidating transporters governing TMA import and TMAO export in β-cells represents a vital future direction to fully understand the spatiotemporal regulation of this protective pathway.

Although current study indicates a protective role of the FMO3-TMAO axis in pancreatic β cells, so-far there is only one case report showing diabetes in a patient with trimethylaminuria88. The discrepancy between this clinical observation and preclinical findings stems from the systemic pathological features of trimethylaminuria and multiple compensatory mechanisms, which are inherently distinct from β-cell-specific FMO3 deficiency models. First, as an autosomal recessive disorder, trimethylaminuria is characterized by global FMO3 inactivation rather than cell-type-restricted deficiency. This systemic impairment leads to elevated TMA levels, which have been shown to robustly suppress metabolic inflammation and improve glycaemic control by inhibiting interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase 4 (IRAK4)89, thereby might be counteracting the β-cell dysfunction induced by reduced endogenous TMAO production. Second, humans acquire substantial amounts of TMAO and its precursors (e.g., choline, L-carnitine) through dietary intake, which effectively compensates for impaired endogenous synthesis—a regulatory pathway that is not fully recapitulated in the experimental diets of preclinical mouse models. Furthermore, as a rare disease, trimethylaminuria has historically been the focus of clinical attention due to its hallmark fishy body odor phenotype, resulting in a lack of systematic, longitudinal monitoring of glucose metabolism-related indices that may lead to the under detection of subclinical β-cell dysfunction. Finally, the FMO3-TMAO axis exerts highly tissue-specific and context-dependent effects, and glucose metabolism is governed by multiple redundant signaling pathways. These factors collectively form a buffering mechanism against β-cell impairment, ultimately reconciling the consistency between preclinical conclusions and clinical phenotypes.

In summary, our study demonstrates that β-cell FMO3-derived TMAO prevents multiple β-cell dysfunctions, including defective GSIS, senescence and inflammation using animal models, β-cell lines, and human islets. In addition, we also uncovered how TMAO modulates NF-κB activity by binding with and modulating the IκBα and RelA interaction and hence IκBα protein stability, and its crosstalk with the p53-dependent senescent pathway. Our findings suggest that manipulating FMO3-TMAO biosynthesis in β-cell represents a potential senotherapeutics against ageing-related β-cell dysfunction in diabetes.

Methods

Human studies

Human pancreatic tissues were procured from non-T2D and T2D donors with different ages from the Department of Liver Surgery and Transplantation at Zhongshan Hospital Affiliated to Fudan University between 2022-2024. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital Affiliated to Fudan University (Ethics approval number: B2021-853R). Participants were sex- and age-matched. Sex was determined by self-report (male or female). Written informed consent was obtained from the participants (and from parents or legal guardians for participants under 18 years of age), and all procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Clinical and metabolic characteristics of human subjects are listed in Table S1. T2D was diagnosed as HbA1C ≥ 6.5%90. The human pancreatic tissues were stored in cold University of Wisconsin (UW) solution before weighting and pancreatic duct perfusion with cold collagenase P (0.4 mg/mL, Roche, Cat# 11213865001) prepared in Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS, Gibco™, Cat# 14025076). The perfused pancreas was further digested at 37 °C for 20 min and subjected to gradient centrifugation using density gradient reagents histopaque 1119 (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 1119-1) and 1077 (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 1077-1) at 4 °C, 339 g for 30 min. The purified human islets were collected between the 2nd and 3rd layers of the gradient and then picked manually under a microscope. Captured islets were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco, Cat#11879020,) supplemented with 5.5 mM glucose and 10% FBS. The isolated islets with similar sizes were subjected to assessment for insulin secretory ability. The isolated islets were fully consumed during analysis. The residual pancreas paraffin blocks are stored at the Department of Endocrinology and Metabolism, Zhongshan Hospital, and are available for sharing upon reasonable request (contact corresponding author).

Animal studies

Fmo3floxed/floxed mice (C57BL/6 J) were generated by Shanghai Model Organisms Center (Cat# NM-KO-200734) and described in our previous study28. To create β-cell-specific Fmo3 knockout mice and their wild-type controls (so-called β-FMO3 KO and WT mice), the homozygous Fmo3floxed/floxed mice were crossed with RIP-Cre mice, which express the Cre recombinase under the control of the rat insulin II promoter (obtained from The Jackson Laboratory, Cat#003573). Primer sequences for genotyping are provided in Table S2. All mice were housed in the Centralized Animal Facilities of Fudan University, maintained on a 12-hour light/dark cycle, with controlled humidity (50–60%) and temperature (20 °C–25 °C). Unless otherwise specified, the animals were fed a normal chow diet (24.7 kcal% protein, 13.2 kcal% fat, and 62.1 kcal% carbohydrates; Cat# 5053, PicoLab Rodent Diet 2.0) and given free access to water. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of Fudan University (Permit Number: 202011017S) or Hong Kong Polytechnic University (Permit Number: 24-25/1244-HTI-R-GRF). Sex- and age-matched animals were used in each experiment. Animal numbers are specified in the respective figure legends.

For the d9-TMA injected model, d9-TMA (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Cat# DLM-603-5) was dissolved in PBS. 12-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice were intraperitoneally injected with d9-TMA (20 mg/kg). Tissue samples including pancreas, liver, various fat depots, kidney, gastrocnemius, and blood were collected at 0 min (before injection), 30 min post injection, and 120 min post-injection.

For the ABT263 treatment, 12-week-old male β-FMO3 KO and WT mice were treated with PBS or ABT263 (Selleck Chemicals, Cat# S1001 in ethanol: polyethylene glycol 400: Phosal 50 PG) via oral gavage at a dosage of 50 mg per kg body weight 4 times a week for 4 weeks.

For NF-κB inhibitor treatment in mice, Bay11-7082 (MCE, Cat# HY-13453) was dissolved in 10% (v/v) dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and 10% DMSO/PBS was used as vehicle control. 12-week-old male β-FMO3 KO and WT mice were intraperitoneally injected with Bay11-7082 (5 mg/kg) or vehicle thrice a week for 4 weeks as previously described91.

For the adeno-associated virus injection model, AAV-gfp, AAV-Fmo3 and its P153L mutant (AAV-P153L) were intra-ductally injected into 12-week-old male β-FMO3 KO and WT mice92. The expression of transgene is driven by the insulin promoter. Briefly, the mice were anesthetized with isoflurane. A midline incision was made to reveal the abdominal cavity. A micro-clamp was placed on the bile duct above the branching of the pancreatic duct to prevent perfusion of the liver. Then a 30g-gauge needle was inserted through the anti-mesenteric aspect of the duodenum to cannulate the common bile duct and the tip of the catheter was positioned at the origin of the pancreatic duct branch in the biliary-pancreatic duct. 1 × 1012 genomic copy number of AAV resuspended in 0.15 ml saline was infused per mouse. Mice were allowed to recover on a heating pad (37 °C) for 2 h. They were given free access to food and water after the recovery. After 4 weeks of AAV injection, the mice were subjected to metabolic characterization, followed by the collection of serum from the mice under feeding and pancreas and islets for further analysis.

For antibiotic treatment experiment, 12-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice were administrated with an antibiotic’s cocktails (0.5 g/L ampicillin [Cayman Chemical Company, Cat# 14417], 0.5 g/L neomycin [Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 21810031], 0.5 g/L metronidazole [Cayman Chemical Company, Cat# 9002409] and 0.5 g/L vancomycin [Cayman Chemical Company, Cat# 15327]) in drinking water for the 3 or 4 weeks to remove intestinal microbiota as we and others previously described36,93,94.

Glucose tolerance test (GTT), insulin tolerance test (ITT), and glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) in mice

For GTT, mice were fasted for 16 h, followed by an intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of 2 g/kg D-glucose (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# G7021). Blood glucose was measured at 0, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min after glucose injection. Insulin secretion in response to i.p. injection of glucose was tested at 0, 15, 30, and 60 min. ITT was performed by i.p. injection of 0.75 U/kg body weight of insulin in 6-h fast mice, with subsequent measurement of blood glucose at 0, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, and 120 min. Blood samples were taken from the tail vein for the measurement of glucose using an ACCU-Chek glucose meter (Roche, USA). Serum was separated for measurement of insulin concentration using mouse insulin ELISA kit (Immunodiagnostics, Cat# 32270) following manufacturer’s instructions.

Isolation of primary pancreatic islets from mice

The mice were fasted for 4 h and sacrificed by cervical dislocation. Briefly, the common bile duct was clamped off at the duodenum. The pancreas was perfused with 3 ml collagenase P (0.4 mg/mL, Roche, Cat# 11213865001) via bile common ducts. The pancreas was then separated and incubated with 7 ml of collagenase P (0.4 mg/mL) at 37 °C for 20 min for digestion. The digested pancreas was subjected to gradient centrifugation using density gradient reagents histopaque 1119 (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 11191) and 1077 (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 10771) at 339 g in 4 °C for 30 min. The purified islets were collected between the 2nd and 3rd layers of the gradient and then picked manually under a microscope and cultured in RPMI 1640 with 10% FBS overnight. The islets were manually picked under a microscope for insulin secretion assay or LC-MS targeted metabolomic study.

Cell culture and insulin secretion assay

Rat insulinoma cells INS-1E (AddexBio Technologies, Cat# C0018009) and moue pancreatic β-cell line MIN6 (AddexBio Technologies, Cat# C0018008) were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco, Cat# 31800105) or DMEM (Gibco, Cat# 12800082) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, Cat# A5256701), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Cat# 15140-122), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco, Cat# 11360-070), 1×GlutaMAX (Gibco, Cat# 35050-61) and 50 μM beta-mercaptoethanol (MCE, Cat# HY-Y0326). The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The β-cells or isolated islets were treated with different concentrations of TMAO (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 317594), TMA (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 243205), d9-TMA (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Cat# DLM-603-5), doxorubicin (MCE, Cat# HY-15142A), TNF-α (Abcam, Cat# ab259410), ABT263 (Selleck Chemicals, Cat# S1001), thapsigargin (MCE, Cat# HY-13433), CHX (MCE Cat# HY-12320), and Bay11-7082 (MCE, Cat# HY-13453), for 15 minutes to 48 hours as indicated in each figure legend. The FBS-containing medium was found to contain TMA, thereby providing the substrate for FMO3-mediated TMAO production,

For insulin secretion assay, the cells or isolated islets were washed twice with Krebs buffer containing low glucose (2 mM) and 0.1% fatty acid-free BSA (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# A8806) and then maintained for 120 min. The cells were stimulated with high glucose (20 mM) or low glucose (2 mM as basal) for 30 min. Insulin concentration in the collected conditional medium was measured using an insulin ELISA kit (Immunodiagnostics, Cat# 32100) and normalized with protein concentration, islet size, or total insulin content. For insulin content measurement, cells or islets were extracted with acid-ethanol (75% ethanol, 1.5% HCl) overnight at 4 °C. The extracts were neutralized with Tris-buffer (pH7.5) and insulin content was quantified using the insulin ELISA kit as above.

Measurement of TMA and TMAO by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)

Intracellular TMAO was quantified using targeted metabolomics using the Agilent 6460 Liquid Chromatography-Electrospray Ionization Triple Quadrupole Mass Spectrometer (University Life Science, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University) following a previous reported method95 with some modifications. Briefly, calibration curves for labeled or unlabeled TMAO standards were prepared in 80% methanol containing 0.5 µM d9-TMAO (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Cat# DLM-4779-1) as the internal standard for TMAO, and 0.5 µM d8-valine (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Cat# DLM-311-PK) as the internal standard for d9-TMAO. For sample preparation, 10 μL of standard mixture, culture medium, or mouse serum was extracted using 100 μL of chilled absolute methanol containing the internal standards. Cultured cells (human islets, mouse islets, mouse hepatocytes, MIN6, or INS-1E β-cells) were seeded onto 60-mm dishes. After the indicated experimental treatments, cells were washed twice with cold PBS, extracted with 400 μL of cold 80% methanol containing internal standards, and scraped into Eppendorf tubes. For tissue samples, approximately 50 mg of tissue was homogenized with 400 μL of cold 80% methanol. The metabolite extracts were vortexed or sonicated, and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 15 min under 4 °C. To quantify TMAO, 8 μL of supernatant was injected into an Acquity UPLC BEH Amide Column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm, Waters, Cat# 186004801) connected to the mass spectrometer.

For TMA quantification, the samples were first derivatized. Briefly, 25 μL of methanol-extracted samples containing 0.5 µM d9-TMA (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Cat# DLM-603-PK; as internal standard for TMA) and 0.5 µM d8-valine (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Cat# DLM-311-PK; as internal standard for and d9-TMA) were incubated with 25 μL 100 mM TBB tert-butyl bromoacetate (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 124230) and 5 μL 30% ammonium hydroxide in room temperature for 30 min. Then, 20 μL of 1% formic acid was added and the derivatized samples were subjected to centrifugation at 12,000 g for 15 min under 4 °C. An 8 μL aliquot of the supernatant was injected into the Acquity UPLC BEH Amide Column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm, Waters, Cat# 186004801) connected to the mass spectrometer for TMA quantification.

The target analytes (TMAO and TMA) were monitored by multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) in positive electrospray ionization (ESI) mode, using the following characteristic precursor-to-product ion transitions: TMAO (76.0 → 58.0), d9-TMAO (85.0 → 66.0), TMA (174.0 → 59.0), d9-TMA (183.0 → 68.0), d8-valine (126.0 → 80.0). The mobile phase gradient consisted of 5 mM ammonium formate (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 09735) and 0.1% formic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 695076) in mobile phase A (water: acetonitrile, 90:10, v/v) and mobile phase B (water: acetonitrile, 10:90, v/v) at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min.

The mass spectrometer was operated with the voltage set to 3.5 kV and source temperature held at 300 °C. The target analytes were monitored by MRM with the specific precursor ion to product ion transitions determined for each metabolite. The fragmentor voltage (70–100 V) and collision energy (10–30 psi) were also optimized for each metabolite to facilitate their detection and quantification. Blank controls were injected every 6 sample injections. The number of samples in each group is specified in the respective figure legends.

The acquired mass spectra data were analyzed using Agilent MassHunter Quantitative Analysis Software (VB.06.00). Enrichment of metabolites was corrected by internal standards and normalized to the protein concentration of the samples.

Adenovirus infection and siRNA transfection

For the transfection assay, INS-1E cells were transfected with either siRNA against Fmo3 (siFmo3), p53 or Scramble controls (GenePharma) using Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX reagent (Invitrogen, Cat# 13778150) for 48 h according to the manufacturer’s manual. The sequences of siRNA are listed in Table S3. For the viral infection experiment, INS-1E cells were incubated with adenovirus overexpressing human FLAG-tagged FMO3 or human FLAG-tagged P153L (WZ Biosciences Inc.) or GFP at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100 for 48 h. Polybrene (MCE, Cat# HY-112735) was added to the media with the final concentration of 10 μg/ml to enhance infection efficiency.

Construction of the luciferase reporter vector and luciferase assays

A reporter plasmid encoding luciferase under the control of the human p53 gene promoter or its derived mutant without RelA binding sites was cloned into pGL3-Basic vector (Qingke Biosciences Inc.). INS-1E cells were co-transfected with the reporter vector and pRL-TK renilla luciferase vector (Qingke Biosciences Inc.) as transfection efficiency control using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thremo Fisher, Cat# L3000075) for 6 h, followed by treatment with TNF-α (20 ng/ml) or TMAO (100 μM) for 24 h. The luciferase activities were measured using a dual luciferase report assay system (Beyotime RG027). Briefly, the transfected cells were lysed using 250 μl of the provided passive lysis buffer. The cell lysates were collected after centrifugation at 12,000 g for 4 min at 4 °C. Then, 100 μl of the cell lysate supernatant from each sample was added to a 96-well plate and mixed with 50 μl of the firefly luciferase substrate. The firefly luciferase activity was measured using a Varioskan LUX multimode microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Subsequently, 50 μl of the Renilla luciferase substrate was added to each sample and mixed well, and the Renilla luciferase activity was measured as an internal control. The firefly luciferase activity driven by the promoter construct was then normalized to the Renilla luciferase activity to account for variation in transfection efficiency and cell lysate preparation.

Co-immunoprecipitation

The INS-1E cells or isolated human islets were solubilized in a lysis buffer containing 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-Cl (pH7.4), 2 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40 and proteinase inhibitor, followed by centrifugation at 13,000 g at 4 °C for 10 min. To immunoprecipitate RelA-IκBα protein complex, the lysate was incubated with RelA or IκBα antibody or rabbit IgG at 4 °C overnight as indicated in each figure. The immunocomplexes were then incubated with Protein A/G agarose (Millipore, Cat# IP10) at 4 °C for 1 h, followed by washing with cold lysis buffer for three times and elution with 2X SDS sample loading buffer by heating at 99 °C for 5 min. The eluted immunocomplex and total cell lysate were loaded onto SDS-PAGE gel and subjected to electrophoresis, followed by immunoblotting analysis of IκBα and RelA. For co-immunoprecipitation of the endogenous IKKβ complex, INS-1E cells were pretreated with or without TMAO (100 µM, 24 h). Where indicated, the cells were subsequently treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (10 µM) for 1 h, followed by stimulation with TNF-α (20 ng/mL) for 30 min. The lysates were incubated with anti-IKKβ antibody (or control IgG) overnight at 4 °C, followed by capture with Protein A/G agarose. The immunoprecipitants were washed, eluted, and subjected to immunoblotting analysis for IκBα, RelA, and IKKβ as indicated in the corresponding figure.

The interaction between recombinant IκBα (Origene, Cat# TP300711) and RelA (Origene, Cat# TP320780) proteins was studied using an in vitro co-immunoprecipitation assay. 500 ng of purified IκBα and RelA proteins were mixed at a 1:1 molar ratio and incubated with or without TMAO (100 μM) for 1 h on ice. Anti-IκBα antibody was then added to capture the IκBα-containing complexes using protein A/G agarose (Millipore, Cat# IP10), followed by washing with 1% NP-40 lysis buffer for 5 times. The bound proteins were eluted by boiling and analyzed by immunoblotting analysis of RelA.

Drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS)

The binding between TMAO and NF-κB proteins was assessed using, DARTS as previously described with some minor modifications96. INS-1E β-cells were washed with PBS and harvested using a lysis buffer (0.2% Triton X-100, 200 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4; protease inhibitor cocktail). After sonication and centrifugation, protein concentration was determined by BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 23225). 500 μg total protein of cell lysate was prepared per reaction in chilled TNC reaction buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 50 mM NaCl, 10 mM CaCl2, pH 8.0), followed by incubation with different concentrations of TMAO for 1 h on ice. After warming the reaction mixtures to room temperature, limited proteolysis was performed by adding 1 μg proteinase K (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# SAE0151) for every 700 μg protein and then incubating at room temperature for 5 min. Digestion was then quenched by immediately adding SDS loading buffer and heating the mixture at 95 °C for 5 min. The effect of chemical compounds to prevent proteolysis was analyzed by immunoblotting. For the in vitro DARTS experiment using recombinant proteins, human recombinant RelA protein (Origene, Cat# TP320780) and IκBα protein (Origene, Cat# TP300711) were incubated with various concentrations of TMAO at room temperature for 1 hour. Proteinase K was then added at a 1:100 protein-to-proteinase ratio, and limited proteolysis was performed for 5 min. The digestion was quenched, and the effect of TMAO on RelA and IκBα degradation was analyzed by immunoblotting.

In vitro IKKβ kinase assay

The direct effect of TMAO on IKKβ kinase activity was evaluated using a commercially available IKKβ Kinase Assay Kit (BPS Bioscience, Cat# 78507) in combination with the Kinase-Glo® MAX Luminescent Kinase Assay (Promega, Cat# V6071), following the manufacturer’s instructions with minor adaptations. Briefly, recombinant active IKKβ kinase (GST-tagged) was diluted to 10 ng/μl in 1× kinase assay buffer immediately before use. The kinase reaction was performed in a white 96-well plate in a total volume of 50 μl per well, containing 1× kinase assay buffer, 500 μM ATP, and 1 mg/ml IKKtide (KKKKERLLDDRHDSGLDSMKDEE) as the substrate. TMAO was tested across a broad range of concentration (1 pM to 1 M). To account for potential solvent effects, equivalent volumes of dilution buffer (1× kinase assay buffer) were used in control wells. The specific IKKβ inhibitor MLN120B (MCE, Cat# HY-107536) was included as a positive control for inhibition.

Reactions were initiated by adding diluted IKKβ enzyme to the reaction mix containing the substrate and TMAO or MLN120B. After incubation at 30 °C for 45 min, 50 μl of Kinase-Glo® MAX reagent was added to each well to terminate the reaction and detect residual ATP levels. The plate was incubated at room temperature for 15 min in the dark, and luminescence was measured. The luminescence signal from the blank control (no enzyme) was subtracted from all values. Kinase activity was expressed as a percentage of the vehicle control (i.e., no TMAO), and dose-response curves were analyzed using nonlinear regression in GraphPad Prism 9.0 to determine IC₅₀ values. The direct effect of TMAO on IKKβ-mediated phosphorylation of full-length protein substrates was assessed using recombinant human IκBα (Origene, Cat# TP300711) and a pre-assembled IκBα: RelA complex. To form the complex, recombinant IκBα and RelA (Origene, Cat# TP320780) were mixed at a 1:1 molar ratio in kinase assay buffer and incubated on ice for 1 h. The kinase assay was performed in a white 96-well plate in a total volume of 50 µL per well, containing 1× kinase assay buffer, 500 µM ATP, 50 ng active IKKβ (BPS Bioscience, Cat# 78507), and either 0.2 µg full-length IκBα or an equimolar amount of the IκBα: RelA complex as substrate. TMAO (0–1000 µM) was pre-incubated with the substrate for 30 min at room temperature before adding IKKβ and ATP to initiate the reaction. Following incubation at 30 °C for 45 min, 50 µL of Kinase-Glo® MAX reagent (Promega, Cat# V6711) was added to each well, and luminescence was measured after 15 min. Kinase activity was calculated as described above and expressed as a percentage of the vehicle (0 µM TMAO) control.

Histological studies

Pancreases isolated from humans or mice were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), then embedded in paraffin and cut into 5-μm-thick sections at 50-μm intervals. For senescence-associated-β-gal staining, mouse pancreas was embedded with OCT compound (Servicebio, Cat# G6059), followed by cryosections, and staining according to a published protocol97. Beta-gal activity of INS-1E cells was detected using senescence β-galactosidase staining kit (CST, Cat# 9860) according to manufacturer’s instructions. For immunohistochemical (IHC) staining, mouse pancreatic sections were processed with antigen retrieval buffer and incubated with the primary antibodies against FMO3 (Abcam, Cat# ab126711) and insulin (Proteintech, Cat# 66198-1-lg) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBST, the slides were incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit (CST, Cat# 7074S) and anti-mouse (CST, Cat# 7076S) secondary antibodies for 90 minutes at room temperature. The chromogenic reaction was carried out with a DAB substrate kit (Abcam, Cat# ab64238) before counterstaining with hematoxylin and mounting. The images were acquired using a Nikon Y-THPL Microscope and analyzed using ImageJ.

For immunofluorescent (IF) staining, the paraffin section was de-waxed and rehydrated to distilled water. Antigen retrieval was performed by boiling in Tris-EDTA antigen retrieval solution (pH 8.0) for 20 min. After cooling, the sections were blocked in PBST with 5% BSA for one hour at room temperature. The sections were incubated with indicated primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. Corresponding secondary antibodies conjugated with fluorescent probes were applied to sections for one hour at room temperature under dark environment. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Invitrogen, Cat# P36984). Images were taken using a fluorescent microscope. Sections were immunostained for insulin (Proteintech, Cat# 66198-1-lg), glucagon (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# SAB4501137) FMO3 (Abcam, Cat# ab126711), p21 (Abcam, Cat# ab188224), RelA (CST, Cat# 8242), and pRelA (CST, Cat# 3033). The fluorescent images were captured using a Nikon Eclipse Ni-U Fluorescent Microscope and analyzed using ImageJ.

Immunoblotting analysis

Tissues and cells were lysed and homogenized in a RIPA lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris HCl [pH 7.4], 2 mM EDTA, 0.1% SDS, 1% NP-40) supplemented with protease inhibitor (MCE, Cat# HY-K0010) and phosphatase inhibitor (Selleck Chemicals, Cat# B15002). Proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membrane (Millipore, Cat# HATF00010). The membrane was then blocked with 5% non-fat milk and probed with the primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies against FMO3 (Abcam, Cat# ab126711), p53 (Proteintech, Cat# 10442-1-AP), p21 (Abcam, Cat# ab188224), RelA (CST, Cat# 8242), pRelA (CST, Cat# 3033), IκBα (CST, Cat# 4814), pIκBα (CST, Cat# 2859), IKKα/β (Beyotime, Cat# AF2221), pIKKα/β (Beyotime Cat# AF5839), IL-1β (CST, Cat# 12242), β-actin (Santa Cruz, Cat# sc-47778), GAPDH (CST, Cat# 2118S), VAMP2 (Abcam, Cat# ab181869), Histone H3 (Proteintech, Cat# 17168-1-AP), HSP90 (Proteintech, Cat# 13171-1-AP), albumin (Proteintech, Cat# 66051-1-Ig), insulin(Proteintech, Cat# 66198-1-lg), FOXO1 (Santa Cruz, Cat# sc-374427), pFOXO1 (CST Cat# 9461), PERK (CST, Cat# 3192) and pPERK (CST, Cat# 3179) were used. The membranes were then washed with TBS-T for four times, followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibodies conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (CST, Cat# 7074 and 7076) for one hour at room temperature. For chemiluminescent detection, the membrane was incubated with Clarity Western ECL substrate (Bio-Rad, Cat# 170-5061) for 1–5 min according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The intensity of protein bands on the film was quantified using ImageJ. Uncropped and unprocessed scans are provided in the Source Data file.

Real-time quantitative PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus Reagent (Takara, Cat# 9109), followed by synthesis of cDNA using PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Cat# RR047A). Real-time quantitative PCR with ViiA 7 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using QuantiNova SYBR Green (Qiagen, Cat# 208056) with the primers targeting the genes of interest. The mRNA expression level was analyzed using the ∆∆Ct threshold cycle method and normalized against β-actin. Details of the primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

RNA sequencing

RNA extraction, library construction, and sequencing of the INS-1E cell sample and pancreatic islet were completed by Lianchuan Biotechnology (Hangzhou, China) and Cloud-Seq Biotechnology (Shanghai, China), respectively. Total RNAs (1 μg) were extracted from the islets of β-FMO3 KO mice and WT mice or from siScramble, siFmo3 and siFmo3 + TMAO transfected INS-1E using TRIZOL reagent, followed by rRNAs removal using Ribo-Zero rRNA Removal Kits (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The rRNA-depleted RNAs were constructed into RNA sequencing libraries by using TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. High-throughput sequencing was done by companies. The quality assessment of the raw sequencing reads was conducted using FastQC (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). The mouse reference genome (GRCm38) and rat reference genome (GRCr8) were acquired from Ensembl (ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/). After read mapping, transcript assembly, and expression estimation, following the previously outlined methodology (25186741), we utilized HISAT2 (25751142) for read alignment, Stringtie (25690850) for transcript reconstruction, and RSEM (21816040) for calculating gene expression levels in each sample. The resultant expression data was then imported into R (version 3.4.0), and differential analysis was executed employing the “DESeq2” package (25516281). Visualization of the data was achieved through implementation of the “ggplot2” package, while enrichment analysis was carried out using the “clusterProfile” package (34557778). To identify potential transcription factors (TFs) regulating the differentially expressed genes, the up-expressed genes identified in both the β-FMO3 KO vs. WT mice comparison and the siFmo3 vs. siScramble INS-1E cells comparison were subjected to a TF-target gene analysis using the TRRUST v2 database (http://www.grnpedia.org/trrust).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 9.0. Data were presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance between two group comparisons was determined using an unpaired Student’s t-test or nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test. Statistical significance between multiple group comparisons was determined using two-way ANOVA with Tukey post-hoc test. Equal variance and normality were determined by Levene’s test and D’Agostino-Pearson omnibus normality test, respectively. A p-value less than 0.05 is considered statistically significant. The types of statistical tests were indicated in each figure legend. In vitro experiments were repeated two or three times independently with similar results. Animal experiments were performed once with each animal as a biologically independent sample.
