Mo-doped NiO@TiO2 electrocatalyst for highly selective and durable hydrogenolysis of furfural to 2-methylfuran
Authors: Guozhou Feng, Jinglin Mu, Jianhua Wang, Ruizi Gong, Yuxin Fan, Zhichao Miao, Jin Zhou, Lechen Diao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis of biomass-derived furfurals to 2-methylfuran (MF) is a vital pathway for sustainable energy development, yet it is hindered by the complex parallel reaction networks and competitive hydrogen evolution reaction. Herein, we rationally designed and synthesized a core–shell-like heterostructure consisting of a structurally stable TiO2 core encapsulated by an open interface-rich Mo-NiO shell electrocatalyst (denoted as Mo-NiO@TiO2). Experimental characterization reveals that the heterojunction reconstructs the local electronic microenvironment and promotes oxygen vacancy generation. Simultaneously, the Mo dopants improved the stability of NiO in acidic electrolytes. Consequently, the Mo-NiO@TiO2 catalyst demonstrates efficient electrocatalytic performance for the hydrogenolysis of furfural to MF, achieving a selectivity of 97% and a faradaic efficiency of 82%, alongside stable cycling performance over 130 hours. Kinetic and mechanistic investigations elucidate that the hydrogenation proceeds via a proton-coupled electron transfer pathway dictated by local proton availability, which profoundly mitigates the kinetic energy barrier and suppresses the parasitic HER. This work delivers deep mechanistic insights into atomic-level electronic engineering and provides a robust paradigm for designing advanced electrocatalysts for sustainable biomass valorization.

Introduction

The valorization of biomass resources is a crucial pathway toward achieving sustainable energy development.1–6 As a platform compound derived from hemicellulose, the catalytic conversion of furfural (FF) has attracted considerable attention.7–11 The hydrogenolysis of FF to produce 2-methylfuran (MF) holds profound research and practical significance, as MF serves not only as a high-quality liquid biofuel with high energy density but also as a vital green solvent and versatile intermediate in the pharmaceutical and chemical industries.12–14 Traditionally, the transformation of FF to MF relies predominantly on thermal catalytic hydrodeoxygenation processes, which typically necessitate harsh operating conditions such as high-temperature and high-pressure H2 gas.15,16 In recent years, electrocatalytic hydrogenation (ECH) has emerged as a green and mild alternative. By utilizing water as the hydrogen source at ambient temperature and pressure, ECH exhibits immense potential for upgrading FF into high-value-added chemicals, thereby becoming a major focus of current academic research (Fig. 1a).17,18

(a) Schematic illustration of electrocatalytic biomass valorization for 2-methylfuran production. (b) Previously reported electrocatalytic system for furfural conversion and our electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis of furfural to 2-methylfuran using the Mo-NiO@TiO2 electrocatalyst.

Although recent research has achieved some advancements in enhancing both MF selectivity and faradaic efficiency (FE), the inherent trade-off between them remains difficult to reconcile.19,20 The challenges mainly originate from the complex parallel and tandem reaction networks intrinsic to catalytic hydrogenation because FF is highly vulnerable to preliminary hydrogenation on the aldehyde group (Fig. 1b).21,22 Besides, the competitive hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) drastically diminishes the FE. Contemporary research predominantly centers on the optimization of macroscopic reaction conditions or the empirical performance evaluation of multi-metal doping but usually overlooks the precise manipulation of the relationship between the catalyst's microscopic structure and performance from the atomic-level interfacial electronic structure. The absence of kinetic understanding of the interactions between molecular orbitals and electron structure impedes the precise control of the FF electrochemical hydrogenolysis reaction pathway, which limits the design of highly efficient and selective catalytic systems.

Nickel-based catalysts have been widely employed in the electrocatalytic conversion of FF derivatives owing to their abundant earth reserves, rich 3d electron count, and distinctive eg orbital configuration.23 Among these, NiO is considered an ideal nickel-based catalyst due to its ease of synthesis and high stability. However, its intrinsic active site architecture severely limits catalytic efficiency. Specifically, the electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis of FF requires the concerted action of FF and protons (H+) at the electrode interface to facilitate a rapid proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) process. In practice, a mismatch between the activation rate of the aldehyde group and the supply rate of H+ often leads to the undesired formation of furfuryl alcohol as a byproduct. Moreover, severe competition from the HER further compromises the FE. Therefore, modulating the activation of the aldehyde group and the proton delivery rate at the atomic scale is critical to enhancing the selectivity and FE of the electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis of FF. In addition, in the electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis reaction of FF, H+ mainly originates from the electrolyte. Tuning the H+ supply capacity of the proton donors in the electrolyte is also critically important.

Recently, the interfacial engineering and heteroatom doping of transition metal electrocatalysts have garnered widespread attention as atomic-level regulation strategies. In these systems, the regulation of the electronic structure plays a decisive role in mediating the metal–intermediate interaction, which in turn controls the adsorption dynamics and electron transfer during electrocatalysis.24,25 For example, Wang's group showed that the Ag@SiO2 core–shell optimized the selectivity for target products by synergistically regulating proton transport and interfacial charge distribution.23 TiO2 is frequently utilized as a material to adsorb and activate FF derivatives, given its unique ability to induce and stabilize a tilted adsorption configuration that effectively activates the C <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" version="1.0" width="13.200000pt" height="16.000000pt" viewBox="0 0 13.200000 16.000000" preserveAspectRatio="xMidYMid meet"><metadata> Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 </metadata><g transform="translate(1.000000,15.000000) scale(0.017500,-0.017500)" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"><path d="M0 440 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z M0 280 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z"/></g></svg> O bond.26–28 Therefore, integrating NiO with TiO2 optimizes the adsorption behavior of FF, which would effectively activate the CO bond.

Herein, we rationally designed and synthesized a core–shell-like heterostructure consisting of a structurally stable TiO2 core encapsulated by an open interface-rich Mo-NiO shell electrocatalyst (Mo-NiO@TiO2). The structurally robust TiO2 core effectively regulates the electronic structure of NiO and prevents agglomeration of the active species. While the judicious incorporation of acid-fast Mo species into the NiO matrix systematically reconstructs the local microenvironment and enhances the corrosion resistance of the catalyst in acidic electrolyte, a strongly acidic electrolyte (0.02 M H2SO4) with a low pKa value provides sufficient H+ at a matching rate, which is indispensable for realizing efficient electrochemical hydrogenolysis of FF to MF. Combining the experimental results with DFT calculations elucidates that Mo doping and integrating NiO with TiO2 collaboratively downshift the d-band center of the Ni sites, which not only optimizes the adsorption configurations of FF and crucial intermediates but also drastically curtails the kinetic energy barrier for FF hydrogenolysis. As a result, the Mo-NiO@TiO2 catalyst delivers outstanding electrocatalytic activity, selectivity, and durability (Fig. 1b). Collectively, this work delivers mechanistic insights into atomic-level electronic engineering and presents a robust paradigm for the design of advanced catalysts for sustainable biomass conversion.

Results and discussion

Catalyst synthesis and characterization

The synthesis of the Mo-NiO@TiO2 catalyst, which involves a sequential hydrothermal and deposition–precipitation process, is schematically illustrated in Fig. 2a. In a typical synthesis process, TiO2 was first prepared via a hydrothermal method at 180 °C for 36 h using tetrabutyl titanate as precursors. The obtained TiO2 powder was then mixed with nickel chloride, ammonium molybdate, and ammonium carbonate, which was then subjected to age under continuous stirring for 8 h. Subsequently, the products were centrifuged, washed, and dried, followed by calcination in air for 2 h at 250 °C. The crystallographic structures of the as-prepared catalysts were examined by X-ray diffraction (XRD). As shown in Fig. 2b, for the Mo-NiO@TiO2 catalyst, characteristic peaks at 37.2°, 43.3°, and 62.9° are assigned to cubic NiO (PDF#47-1049), while the distinct peak at 25.3° corresponds to anatase TiO2 (PDF#21-1272), confirming the successful formation of the heterojunction. Notably, no diffraction peaks attributable to MoOx species were detected in the Mo-doped samples, indicating that Mo atoms are highly dispersed within the NiO lattice.

The microstructural architecture and elemental distribution of the resulting Mo-NiO@TiO2 catalyst were further characterized by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping. As shown in Fig. S1a, the catalyst contains TiO2 nanoparticles inside, with a layer of Mo-NiO loaded on its surface. The HRTEM image (Fig. S1b and c) indicates that the Mo-NiO layer thickness appears non-uniform across different regions of the same particle and does not form a completely dense overlayer. Furthermore, the HRTEM image (Fig. 2c) reveals distinct lattice fringes with interplanar spacings of 0.241 and 0.210 nm, corresponding to the (111) and (200) planes of cubic NiO, respectively. Two additional interplanar spacings of 0.232 and 0.190 nm are assigned to the (112) and (200) planes of TiO2. The obvious interface between these two sets of lattice fringes confirms the successful formation of a Mo-NiO@TiO2 heterojunction. Consistent with the HRTEM images, the selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern (Fig. S1d) exhibits well-defined rings indexed to the crystalline planes of both NiO and TiO2. Spherical aberration-corrected STEM EDS mapping (Fig. 2d) clearly reveals a spatial distribution of Ti, Ni, Mo, and O throughout the entire structure, which corroborated open core–shell-like architecture. Notably, the Mo signal is also homogeneously dispersed, confirming atomic-level doping of Mo into the Mo-NiO@TiO2 framework without detectable phase segregation. Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) analysis (Table S1) reveals that Mo atoms account for 2.4% of all metal atoms in the Mo-NiO@TiO2 matrix.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to probe the surface chemical states and electronic interactions within the catalysts. The high-resolution Mo 3d spectrum (Fig. S2a) exhibits a characteristic doublet at 232.5 and 235.6 eV, corresponding to the Mo6+ oxidation state.29 The incorporation of Mo dopants plays a pivotal role in modulating the defect structure and electronic environment of the NiO@TiO2 heterojunction. Specifically, the substitution of Ni2+ by higher-valence Mo species (Mo6+) triggers a charge compensation mechanism, which promotes the generation of Ov to maintain electrostatic balance.30,31 This is strongly corroborated by the O 1s spectrum (Fig. 2e), where the peak at 531.5 eV, ascribed to oxygen vacancies (Ov), shows a significantly increased relative intensity in Mo-NiO@TiO2 compared with that of NiO and NiO@TiO2. These Ov sites act as locally coordinatively unsaturated centers, effectively enhancing the chemisorption of reactant molecules. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy (Fig. S3) reveals that Mo-NiO@TiO2 exhibits a stronger signal at g = 2.001, indicating a higher density of Ov, which is favorable for FF adsorption.32 Meanwhile, the Ni 2p spectrum (Fig. 2f) reveals the coexistence of Ni2+ and Ni0 species.33 The change of the Ni2+/Ni0 ratio induced by the heterojunction and Mo doping indicates strong electronic coupling and interfacial charge transfer. Moreover, the Ti 2p spectrum (Fig. S2b) presents a typical doublet at 458.5 and 464.2 eV, which is assigned to Ti4+.34 The detection of a Ti signal in the pristine XPS spectrum indicates that the NiO shell is not fully dense but rather partially porous, exposing some of the TiO2 surface. This is consistent with the non-uniform shell thickness observed in HRTEM. Meanwhile, the peak shift towards lower binding energy suggests that TiO2 facilitates electron transfer from NiO across the heterojunction interface. This synergistic effect, characterized by an optimized electronic structure and abundant surface defects, lowers the activation energy of the rate-determining step, ultimately leading to the superior catalytic activity of the Mo-NiO@TiO2 architecture.

Electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis performance

We initially evaluated the as-prepared Mo-NiO@TiO2 catalyst for the electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis of FF using a three-electrode system under ambient temperature and pressure conditions, and qualitative analysis of all substrates and products was performed using gas chromatography (Fig. S4). For comparison, we used TiO2, NiO, and NiO@TiO2 as control catalysts. As shown in Fig. 3a and S5, the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves of NiO exhibit almost negligible performance, while the Mo-NiO@TiO2 catalyst demonstrates impressive efficiency in electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis compared with the TiO2, NiO, and NiO@TiO2 electrodes, confirming the synergistic effect of Mo doping and interfacial engineering. In pure 0.02 M H2SO4, a high current density was observed, which is attributed to the vigorous hydrogen evolution reaction (HER). Upon addition of FF, the total current density decreased noticeably, indicating competitive adsorption of FF on the active sites. This adsorption effectively blocks the access of H+ to the electrode surface, suppressing the competitive HER.35 Although the suppression leads to a lower total current, it implies that electrons are preferentially utilized for FF hydrogenation, which is beneficial for achieving high faradaic efficiency (FE). To distinguish geometric effects from intrinsic electronic effects, we provide Cdl and ECSA-normalized activity data, shown in Fig. S6. Based on the CV curves in the non-faradaic region at various scan rates, the Cdl values were calculated to be 146, 142, 45, and 70 µF for TiO2, NiO, NiO@TiO2, and Mo-NiO@TiO2, respectively. Moreover, as shown in the ECSA-normalized LSV curves (Fig. S6f), after eliminating the geometric factors, Mo-NiO@TiO2 exhibits remarkably higher specific current densities for furfural reduction compared to the other catalysts. This ECSA-normalized data serve as evidence that the superior catalytic performance does not originate from geometric expansion of active sites but is dominantly driven by the strong intrinsic electronic modulation between the components, which significantly boosts the intrinsic turnover efficiency of the existing active sites.

The influence of initial FF concentration on catalytic performance was also examined. As shown in Fig. 3b and c, the current density increased with higher FF concentration, suggesting enhanced reactant availability. At a low concentration of 10 mM, the reaction was limited by mass transfer, and insufficient FF coverage at the electrode interface led to low selectivity and FE for MF. As the FF concentration increased from 10 to 20 mM, the FE toward MF rose sharply from approximately 58% to a peak of 82%; correspondingly, the selectivity achieved 97%. However, further increasing the concentration to 30 and 50 mM led to a gradual decline in selectivity and FE. This is ascribed to the active site saturation by adsorbed FF, which impedes proton supply and thus favors electrocatalytic hydrogenation over further hydrogenolysis. Consequently, 20 mM was selected as the optimal substrate concentration for subsequent experiments. The effect of applied potential on the electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis performance of the catalysts was investigated by constant-potential electrolysis conducted at different potentials from −0.8 to −0.9 V vs. AgCl. The NiO@TiO2 exhibited high MF selectivity but low FE (∼42%) owing to the dominant HER (Fig. S7). Notably, Mo-NiO@TiO2 achieved the best balance of selectivity and catalytic activity, with the highest MF selectivity, FE, and minimal HER at various negative potentials, indicating that Mo-NiO@TiO2 enhanced both activity and selectivity for the FF electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis reaction. These results demonstrate that Mo-NiO@TiO2 exhibits a significant synergistic effect of Mo doping and heterostructure, enhancing the electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis FF performance.

To monitor the catalytic performance changes of the catalyst during the reaction process, we assessed the evolution of selectivity and FE as a function of accumulated charge (Fig. 3d). Throughout the electrolysis process (from 20C to 120C), the selectivity for MF was sustained at a high level (>95%), indicating stable hydrogenolysis performance. The faradaic efficiency evolved dynamically, increasing from 59% at 20C to a plateau of 82% between 60C and 100C. Subsequently, the FE declined to 58% at 120C along with the FF being depleted. As the concentration of residual FF in the electrolyte falls below the optimal threshold, mass transfer limitations become pronounced, thereby exacerbating the competing HER.

Electrocatalyst durability is a crucial indicator for evaluating the electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis performance of FF. The operational durability and structural stability of the catalyst were rigorously evaluated. As shown in Fig. 3f, the Mo-NiO@TiO2 catalyst maintains a faradaic efficiency of 82% and an MF selectivity of 97% during 14 consecutive cycles at −0.85 V, indicating stable cycling performance. The continuous current density–time (i–t) curve for the 14 consecutive cycles was recorded (Fig. S8). The Mo-NiO@TiO2 catalyst exhibited sustained electrocatalytic performance over nearly 130 hours of continuous actual working time, which explicitly confirms its good durability for prolonged electrolysis. In the meantime, the concentration of Ni2+ ion in the electrolyte was monitored by ICP-OES (Fig. 3g). Compared to NiO@TiO2, the Mo-NiO@TiO2 catalyst exhibited a significantly suppressed Ni dissolution rate, indicating that Mo doping effectively stabilizes the host lattice and mitigates the leaching of active components.36 Furthermore, the corrosion resistance was investigated using Tafel polarization curves (Fig. 3h).37 The Mo-NiO@TiO2 electrode showed a distinct positive shift in corrosion potential relative to NiO@TiO2, signifying superior stability and enhanced corrosion resistance under electrochemical conditions. Collectively, these results confirm that Mo doping not only boosts catalytic activity but also fortifies the catalyst against electrochemical corrosion and elemental dissolution. When compared with the previously reported catalysts, the Mo-NiO@TiO2 catalyst demonstrates superior hydrogenolysis performance for FF (Fig. 3i and Table S3).

Thorough post-reaction characterization of Mo-NiO@TiO2 was conducted using Raman spectroscopy, XRD, TEM, and XPS to probe the structural evolution of the catalyst after electrocatalysis. Fig. S9a displays the Raman spectrum of the pristine powder sample, dominated by the characteristic peaks of anatase TiO2 and a broad band near 500 cm−1 assigned to Ni–O bond vibrations. The broadening and slight shift of this band in the composite sample indicate strong electronic interactions and structural disorder at the Mo-NiO@TiO2 interface.38 Notably, the post-reaction Raman spectrum (Fig. S9b) revealed significant surface reconstruction. In addition to the signals from the substrate and pristine oxides, new characteristic peaks emerged at 823 and 963 cm−1, assigned to MoO3 and Ni–O–Mo species, respectively. This phenomenon suggests that Mo species undergo dynamic migration and reconstruction under the applied potential, forming catalytically active and acid-resistant molybdenum oxide species on the surface, which endow Mo-NiO@TiO2 with superior stability in 0.02 M H2SO4 electrolyte. The post-reaction HRTEM image (Fig. S9d) clearly reveals that the distinct lattice fringes (e.g., TiO2 (101) at 0.351 nm and NiO (111) at 0.241 nm) and the intimate interfacial contact are well-maintained, providing atomic-level evidence against severe structural agglomeration or corrosion. Furthermore, XPS quantitative analysis (Fig. S10 and Table S2) reveals a distinct alteration in surface composition: the atomic percentage of Mo increased from 35.8% to 39.59% after the reaction, accompanied by a concurrent decrease of the Ti signal. This phenomenon implies a reaction-induced surface segregation of Mo species, resulting in the formation of a Mo-rich active layer. In the high-resolution Mo 3d spectrum (Fig. S10d), the Mo6+ state remains dominant post-reaction, indicating its chemical stability. Collectively, the XPS and Raman results substantiate that the veritable active phase is a dynamically reconstructed, Mo-rich surface layer derived from Mo-doped NiO.

FF adsorption–activation and H supply

Based on the above results, the as-prepared catalyst exhibits excellent electrocatalytic performance. To elucidate the underlying mechanism, we investigated matched kinetic equilibrium between the adsorption–activation of FF and the hydrogenolysis process from two key aspects of aldehyde activation and proton supply.

The changes in Helmholtz layer adsorption potential at open-circuit potential (OCP) were first tested to assess the adsorption behavior of FF at the electrode–electrolyte interface. As shown in Fig. 4a, Helmholtz layer adsorption potential of Mo-NiO@TiO2 decreased by 0.21 V upon 20 mM FF injection, which is substantially larger than those of TiO2 (0.13 V), NiO@TiO2 (0.14 V), and NiO (0.07 V). This result confirms that Mo doping and heterojunction synergistically enhanced FF adsorption.39In situ ATR-FTIR spectroscopy was conducted in an H-type cell to track FF adsorption and intermediate evolution during the electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis of FF to MF on Mo-NiO@TiO2. The potential was stepped from the OCP to −0.95 V (vs. Ag/AgCl), and spectra was collected in 0.02 M H2SO4 containing 20 mM HMF. As shown in Fig. 4b, two new peaks were observed at 1220 and 1780 cm−1 when the potential reached −0.65 V, which are assigned to the –CH3 stretching vibration of MF and the CO stretching of FF, respectively.40 These features indicate the adsorption of FF and the concurrent formation of MF. Meanwhile, two bands at 1668 and 3520 cm−1, corresponding to the bending vibration and stretching vibration of H2O, decreased progressively, indicating that adsorbed FF occupied the active sites, which impeded the adsorption of H2O. Therefore, these spectroscopic observations provide direct evidence for the electrochemical adsorption and activation of FF.

The influence of FF concentration on activation kinetics was further investigated (Fig. 4c). In the low-concentration regime, a positive reaction order (0.45) is observed, indicative of a diffusion- or adsorption-controlled process, in which its activity increased with the coverage of FF until the catalyst surface became saturated. Conversely, at higher concentrations, the curve plateaus with a slope approaching zero, implying that the catalyst surface becomes saturated with adsorbed FF species and the diffusion of protons to the near-surface electrolyte limits the next reaction step, resulting in nearly zero-order (−0.05) kinetics with respect to the substrate. The dependence of current density on FF concentration exhibits the typical Eley–Rideal (E–R) mechanism, which is in line with the PCET process for FF-to-MF as demonstrated in the next section.14

Then, the impact of H+ concentration was investigated, as shown in Fig. 4d. A plot of log(j) versus log([H+]) reveals a linear relationship with a slope of 0.52, corroborating direct H+ participation in the reaction.41 To further unravel the specific role of interfacial H+ availability in determining the reaction pathway, we systematically evaluated the electrocatalytic performance in a diverse array of electrolytes characterized by varying pKa values.42–46 As illustrated in Fig. 4e and f, both the FE and the selectivity of MF are profoundly dependent upon the proton-donating ability of the local environment. Notably, the catalyst achieves optimal performance in strongly acidic H2SO4, delivering an FE of ∼82% alongside near-unity MF selectivity. Interestingly, as the electrolyte pKa increases, the MF selectivity and FE plummet precipitously, accompanied by a substantial accumulation of furfuryl alcohol (FA). This compelling trend unambiguously confirms that the hydrogenolysis of FF to MF is an intrinsically proton-dependent process. Specifically, strong acid electrolytes with lower pKa values provide a robust proton supply, thereby driving the hydrogenolysis kinetics of FF. Conversely, a weak acid system fails to maintain sufficient proton activity, driving the reaction pathway toward FA formation and severely impairing the overall electrocatalytic efficiency.47–49 Given that H2SO4 is a corrosive chemical reagent, its use is expected to be minimized. To enhance the safety and environmental friendliness of the reaction system, we further optimized the H2SO4 concentration in the electrolyte (Fig. 4g). Optimal selectivity and FE were achieved in 0.02 M H2SO4, indicating that an adequate proton supply can be provided by 0.02 M H2SO4.

To further elucidate the reaction energy barrier and the underlying intrinsic kinetics of the electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis process, temperature-dependent LSV measurements were performed across a temperature range of 20 °C to 60 °C.50 As illustrated in Fig. S11, the gradual enhancement of the cathodic current density with rising temperature at an applied potential of −0.85 V (vs. Ag/AgCl) indicates thermally accelerated reaction kinetics. The apparent activation energy (Ea) was extracted via the Arrhenius equation (ln(|j|) = A − Ea/RT).51 Plotting ln(|j|) versus the reciprocal of the Kelvin temperature yielded an excellent linear correlation (Fig. 4h). Notably, the Mo-NiO@TiO2 catalyst possesses the lowest Ea value of 3.47 kJ mol−1. This indicates a significantly reduced kinetic barrier for FF hydrogenolysis owing to the synergistic enhancement of FF activation by the catalyst and sufficient proton supply from the electrolyte.

To gain deeper insight into the reaction kinetics, Tafel plots derived from the LSV curves were further analyzed (Fig. 4i and S5). In the blank electrolyte (without FF), Mo-NiO@TiO2 exhibited a high Tafel slope of 291.44 mV dec−1, indicating extremely sluggish kinetics for the HER. Upon the introduction of 20 mM FF, the Tafel slope decreased drastically to 121.68 mV dec−1. This marked reduction implies that the electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis of FF is kinetically far more favorable than the HER on the Mo-NiO@TiO2 surface. Furthermore, a Tafel slope of approximately 120 mV dec−1 suggests that the PCET step may be the rate-determining step (RDS) for the conversion of FF to MF.52

Electrocatalytic reaction mechanism

To further determine whether the electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis mechanism of FF proceeds via a proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) pathway, radical scavenging experiments were conducted using tert-butanol (t-BuOH) as an adsorbed H (H*) scavenger. As shown in Fig. 5a, in the presence of 50 mM t-BuOH, the kinetic profiles for FF consumption and MF formation coincided perfectly with those obtained in the blank electrolyte. The negligible variation in the reaction rate upon addition of the scavenger indicates that H* on the catalyst surface does not play a significant role in the conversion process.53 These results provide compelling evidence that FF hydrogenolysis on the Mo-NiO@TiO2 surface may proceed via a PCET mechanism, wherein the reaction occurs between adsorbed FF molecules and H+ from the electrolyte solution rather than H*.

(d) Proposed mechanism of FF electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis on Mo-NiO@TiO2. (e–g) Extension of the substrate scope with Mo-NiO@TiO2 as the electrocatalyst.

To further verify the involvement of H+ in the rate-determining step (RDS), kinetic isotope effect (KIE) experiments were performed using D2O as the electrolyte solvent. As illustrated in Fig. 5b, the reaction rate constant in H2O (kH) was determined to be 2.81 × 10−2 h−1, whereas it decreased significantly to 1.01 × 10−2 h−1 in D2O (kD). The calculated KIE value (kH/kD) is 2.8, indicating a primary KIE during the FF-to-MF conversion. This finding implies that proton transfer is intimately involved in the RDS, which is attributed to the 4H+/4e− PCET process governing the hydrogenolysis of the aldehyde group in FF.14

In conventional thermal catalytic processes for converting FF to MF, FF is first hydrogenated to furfuryl alcohol, which is subsequently hydrogenolysed to MF.12,54 To elucidate the electrocatalytic reaction pathway, we employed furfuryl alcohol as the substrate under identical electrocatalytic conditions using Mo-NiO@TiO2 as the electrocatalyst. As shown in Fig. 5c, both the conversion and selectivity of furfuryl alcohol remained very low, indicating that MF is generated directly from FF during the electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis, rather than passing through the furfuryl alcohol intermediate.

Based on the above experimental results, we propose the following reaction mechanism for the electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis of FF to MF (Fig. 5d). Initially, FF is adsorbed and activated on the catalyst surface via the oxygen atom of its aldehyde group. Subsequently, a H+ and an e− are transferred to its aldehyde oxygen to form a hydroxyl intermediate, which undergoes C–O bond cleavage to eliminate a water molecule via the PCET pathway, yielding a surface-bound alkyl intermediate. Following this, the intermediate is further hydrogenated to form a methyl group through further interaction with H+ species on Mo-NiO@TiO2. Finally, the product MF desorbs from the catalyst surface, completing the catalytic cycle.

Given the above findings and the optimal reaction conditions for electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis of FF, we extended the substrate scope using Mo-NiO@TiO2 as the electrocatalyst. As summarized in Fig. 5e–g and S12, this catalyst exhibited excellent activity across a broad range of aromatic aldehyde derivatives. Specifically, the catalyst delivered superior electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis performance toward benzaldehyde, registering a robust conversion (∼88%) coupled with an outstanding selectivity (∼98%) for toluene. Crucially, when challenged with p-chlorobenzaldehyde, the catalyst exhibited phenomenal chemo-selectivity. It selectively propelled the hydrogenolysis of the aldehyde (–CHO) into a methyl group (–CH3) with ∼95% specificity, while keeping the highly reactive carbon–halogen (C–Cl) bond entirely intact – a formidable feat, given its notorious susceptibility to reductive cleavage. When extending this protocol to 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF), a more intricate bifunctional biomass platform molecule, the catalyst sustained a high conversion rate (∼88%). It predominantly steered the reaction through the deep hydrogenolysis cascade to afford 5-methylfurfuryl alcohol as the dominant product (∼83% selectivity), accompanied by trace amounts of 2,5-bis(hydroxymethyl)furan. These results demonstrate the wide substrate tolerance of Mo-NiO@TiO2 for promoting electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis of aldehyde groups.

Structure–performance relationship analysis

To further elucidate the origin of the strong interaction between the catalyst and reaction intermediates, partial density of states (PDOS) calculation was performed. As shown in Fig. 6a–c, the orbital hybridization between the p-orbitals of the carbon atom in FF (C-p) and the d-orbitals of Ni (Ni-d) was primarily investigated. For both the Mo-NiO@TiO2 (Fig. 6a) and NiO (Fig. 6c) systems, distinct resonance overlap peaks between the Ni-d and C-p orbitals are observed below the Fermi level, confirming the formation of strong chemical bonds between the active Ni sites and FF. Notably, compared with pristine NiO, the core–shell structured Mo-NiO@TiO2 exhibits a significantly altered Ni-d state distribution. The TiO2 core effectively modulates the local electronic structure of the Mo-NiO layer, and this electronic rearrangement optimizes the degree of hybridization between the Ni-d and reactant C-p orbitals. Furthermore, the PDOS near the Fermi level of the Ni-d orbitals displays a highly asymmetric distribution between the spin-up and spin-down channels. This pronounced asymmetry unambiguously confirms the spin-polarized nature of the Ni centers in both the NiO and Mo-NiO@TiO2 systems, which is generally highly conducive to the activation of adsorbed molecules during electrocatalysis. In contrast, pristine TiO2 (Fig. 6b) exhibits highly localized and sharp band features, indicating a distinct and comparatively weaker interaction mode with carbon species.

To comprehensively unravel the chemical bonding nature between the catalyst surface and FF, projected crystal orbital Hamilton population (–pCOHP), crystal orbital bond index (COBI), and charge density difference calculations were further performed.55,56 As illustrated in Fig. 6d, the –pCOHP profile of the Mo-NiO@TiO2 system exhibits prominent bonding states below the Fermi level, indicating that strong orbital interactions drive a highly stable chemisorption process. This is further quantitatively corroborated by the COBI analysis (Fig. 6e). Remarkably, the integrated COBI (ICOBI) value for Mo-NiO@TiO2 reaches 0.8347, which is profoundly higher than those of bare NiO (0.0636) and TiO2 (0.4296). This substantial enhancement in the covalent bond order unambiguously demonstrates that the TiO2 core effectively modulates the electronic structure of the Mo-NiO shell, thereby transforming FF binding from weak physisorption to robust chemisorption.

Visual evidence from charge density difference images (Fig. 6f) is in full agreement with the orbital analyses. FF was adsorbed on Mo-NiO@TiO2 in a tilted configuration, while in a vertical configuration on NiO and TiO2. Significant charge transfer occurs between the three catalysts and FF; however, pronounced and highly localized charge redistribution in the aldehyde group is observed between FF and Mo-NiO@TiO2. This strong interfacial electronic polarization further corroborates the formation of robust covalent bonds. Notably, the rational design of the Mo-NiO@TiO2 architecture optimizes the d-band properties of the active Ni sites, facilitates directional charge transfer, and strengthens interaction with furan-based intermediates, thereby drastically enhancing its intrinsic electrocatalytic activity.

To fundamentally understand the origin of the enhanced catalytic performance, we calculated the Gibbs free energy (ΔG) profiles for the electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis of FF on different catalysts. The detailed optimized geometric configurations of furfural adsorbed on the different catalyst surfaces are provided in Fig. S13. As illustrated in Fig. 6g, the complete reaction pathway for MF formation was investigated. For the NiO surface, the deoxygenation step involving –CH* hydrogenation exhibits a substantial thermodynamic barrier of 0.33 eV, indicating that it is intrinsically difficult for pristine NiO to drive the hydrogenolysis process. In contrast, the rational construction of the Mo-NiO@TiO2 heterojunction profoundly alters the thermodynamic curve of the RDS. Notably, on Mo-NiO@TiO2, the barrier for this critical C–O cleavage step is drastically reduced to a near-thermoneutral value of 0.07 eV. This finding is consistent with the PDOS and COBI results, demonstrating that strong interfacial electronic interactions significantly activate the C–O bond. Furthermore, we evaluated the site selectivity of the initial hydrogenation step on catalysts (Fig. 6h and S14). For Mo-NiO@TiO2, the formation of the FCHOH* intermediate (hydrogenation at the O atom) is an exothermic process (ΔG = −0.09 eV), making it thermodynamically far more favorable than the formation of FCH2O* (hydrogenation at the C atom, ΔG = 0.28 eV). This energetic advantage unequivocally dictates that the reaction predominantly proceeds via the FCHOH* pathway, thereby ultimately facilitating the highly efficient hydrodeoxygenation of furan-based molecules.

Conclusion

In summary, we have developed a robust Mo-NiO@TiO2 electrocatalyst for the highly selective and efficient electrocatalytic hydrogenolysis of FF to MF. The catalyst exploits an open interface-rich Mo-NiO shell and a stable TiO2 core, which synergistically enhanced FF adsorption and aldehyde group activation. Coupling with low pKa electrolyte provides H+ for the proton-coupled electron transfer process and suppress the competing HER. Experimental and theoretical analyses reveal that Mo doping and heterogeneous structure construction optimize the d-band electronic structure of Ni, thereby lowering the activation barrier for C–O bond cleavage and steering the reaction pathway toward deep hydrogenolysis. The catalyst achieves a faradaic efficiency of 82% and 97% selectivity for MF under optimized conditions, with durability over 14 cycles. Furthermore, the substrate scope extends to various aromatic aldehyde derivatives, demonstrating the broad applicability of this catalytic platform. This work provides mechanistic insights into aldehyde group activation and proton supply at the atomic level, establishing a versatile paradigm for designing advanced electrocatalysts for sustainable biomass valorization.
