Stackelberg-Nash controllability for a multi-objective Stefan problem Journal: arXiv (preliminary version)
Thiago C. A de Carvalho, Suerlan Silva, Gilcenio R. de Sousa-Neto, Franciane de B. Vieira

Abstract
We investigate a hierarchical control problem for a one-dimensional Stefan system with localized distributed controls. The setting combines a Stackelberg strategy with a Nash equilibrium among multiple followers, yielding a multi-objective free-boundary problem. The interaction between the hierarchical control and the moving interface results in a nonlinear optimality system, and we show that the original problem reduces to the null controllability of this optimality system. Under suitable geometric conditions on the control regions, we establish a local null controllability result. The proof relies on an observability inequality for a linearized system, obtained through Carleman estimates adapted to the presence of a moving boundary. These results constitute, to the best of our knowledge, the first treatment of a Stefan system within a Stackelberg–Nash framework. Introduction

The phenomena of melting and solidification arise in numerous natural and industrial contexts, such as ice thawing and metal casting. These processes involve a phase transition between solid and liquid states, and they can be described by the
Stefan problem
. Its key feature is a moving interface that separates the phases, so that the spatial domain evolves in time.

Mathematically, the Stefan problem models the temperature field
y(x,t)
in a medium subject to heat diffusion and possible internal sources. The evolution of the temperature is governed by the equation

\left\{\begin{aligned} &y_{t}-y_{xx}+a(x,t)y=f\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{O}}+v_{1}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{1}}+v_{2}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{2}}&&\text{in }Q_{\ell},\\
&y(0,t)=y(\ell(t),t)=0&&\text{in }(0,T),\\
&y(x,0)=y_{0}(x)&&\text{in }(0,\ell_{0}),\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

defined in the time-dependent domain

Q_{\ell}:=\{(x,t)\,:\ 0<x<\ell(t),\ 0<t<T\}.

Here,
y(x,t)
represents the temperature,
a(x,t)
models heat absorption or reaction effects, and the functions
f,v_{1},v_{2}
represent external heat sources acting in the subdomains
\mathcal{O},\mathcal{O}_{1},\mathcal{O}_{2}\subset(0,\ell^{*})
, respectively. The characteristic function of a set
A
is denoted by
\mathds{1}_{A}
. The endpoints
x=0
and
x=\ell(t)
are kept at a reference temperature, set to zero for simplicity.
The spatial domain depends on time and extends from the fixed endpoint
x=0
up to the moving boundary
x=\ell(t)
. The interface between solid and liquid phases is described by the moving boundary function
\ell\in\mathcal{F}(\ell^{*},\ell_{0},B)
, where

\mathcal{F}(\ell^{*},\ell_{0},B)=\left\{\hat{\ell}\in C^{1}([0,T]):\ \hat{\ell}(0)=\ell_{0},\ 0<\ell^{*}<\hat{\ell}(t)<B\text{ for all }t\in[0,T]\right\}.

Here,
T,\ell^{*},\ell_{0},B>0
are given constants.

The evolution of the moving boundary
\ell(t)
is determined by the
Stefan condition

\ell^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{1}{\beta}\,y_{x}(\ell(t),t),\quad t\in(0,T),

(1.2)

where
\beta>0
is the latent heat coefficient. This condition establishes the relation between the motion of the phase interface and the heat flux at the moving boundary, evaluated as a one-sided limit from inside the domain
0<x<\ell(t)
. A negative value
y_{x}(\ell(t),t)<0
indicates that heat flows into the material through
x=\ell(t)
. In this case, the interface velocity satisfies
\ell^{\prime}(t)>0
, meaning that the interface advances outward — the solid melts and the liquid region expands. Conversely, when
y_{x}(\ell(t),t)>0
, the heat flux is directed outward, that is, heat leaves the material through
x=\ell(t)
. Consequently
\ell^{\prime}(t)<0
and the interface recedes, modeling solidification and a reduction of the liquid region.

The Stefan problem has been widely applied beyond classical melting and solidification processes. In engineering, it models free surface flows and fluid–solid interactions in casting, welding, and other thermal processes
[
10
,
29
,
42
]
. In environmental and geotechnical contexts, it describes gas and fluid flow through porous media, where phase changes affect transport properties
[
7
,
43
]
. In biology, Stefan-type models capture the growth and spread of tumors or other cell populations, with the moving interface representing the advancing front of living tissue
[
25
,
23
,
24
]
. More recently, analogous formulations have been used to study the diffusion of information in social networks, where “melting" and “solidification" serve as metaphors for the adoption and stabilization of ideas
[
35
]
.

Our goal in this paper is to analyze the controllability of a multi-objective system based on the Stefan problem. Several works have studied controllability properties for Stefan problems. In
[
22
]
, null controllability was established using Carleman inequalities combined with fixed-point arguments. In
[
20
]
, the authors obtained local null controllability for semilinear problems via a fixed-point method, which was later applied to the viscous Burgers equation in
[
19
]
. A similar approach was used in
[
21
]
to achieve local controllability for nonlinear parabolic equations, based on the Lusternik–Graves theorem. These results were further generalized in
[
14
]
and
[
45
]
, where the authors considered problems with local and nonlocal nonlinearities and quasilinear operators, respectively, using fixed-point techniques and Hölder regularity arguments. Another interesting question was answered in
[
6
]
, where the authors extended the analysis to a two-phase Stefan problem, accounting for the additional complexity of coupled phases. In a different direction, the first local exact controllability result was obtained in
[
26
]
for the viscous Burgers equation. The same approach was later applied in
[
8
]
to parabolic equations using the Lusternik–Graves theorem. In a different context,
[
46
]
establishes the existence of insensitizing controls, while
[
47
]
investigates the Stefan problem with multiplicative controls. Finally, the authors of
[
15
]
studied the null controllability of one-phase Stefan problems in star-shaped domains.

Despite these significant contributions, most existing works focus on single-objective controllability and do not account for multiple criteria simultaneously. In contrast, the present work analyzes a multi-objective controllability problem for the Stefan system.

The multi-objective problem considered here involves three distinct objectives, each associated with one of the control functions
f
,
v_{1}
, and
v_{2}
. We assign the following tasks to each control:

•

Task for
f
: Drive the solution
y
of (
1.1
)–(
1.2
), corresponding to
(v_{1},v_{2})
, to zero at the final time
T
, i.e., achieve null controllability of the system with respect to
f
.

•

Task for
v_{i}
: Ensure that the solution
y
associated with
f
approximates a given target configuration
y_{i,d}
as closely as possible in a prescribed subregion
\mathcal{O}_{i,d}
. This is formulated as the minimization of a suitable functional with respect to
v_{i}
.

These three tasks are in conflict and must therefore be addressed simultaneously. We must then choose an appropriate strategy. In this paper, we adopt the noncooperative Nash equilibrium concept
[
40
]
for the followers
v_{1},v_{2}
, combined with the hierarchical Stackelberg strategy
[
44
]
in which
f
acts as the leader and
v_{1},v_{2}
as followers. This is the so-called Stackelberg–Nash strategy for multi-objective problems.

We now describe our problem precisely. Henceforth, we set the notation

X^{p}(0,T)=L^{p}(0,T;W^{2,p}(0,B)\cap W^{1,p}_{0}(0,B))\cap W^{1,p}(0,T;L^{p}(0,B)),

understood in the natural sense for functions defined on the non-cylindrical domain
Q_{\ell}
.
Let
\mathcal{O}_{1,d},\mathcal{O}_{2,d}\subset(0,\ell^{*})
be open sets where the follower controls aim to track target functions. For each
i=1,2
, let
y_{i,d}=y_{i,d}(x,t)\in L^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{i,d}\times(0,T))
be a given target function describing the desired configuration that the solution of (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) is expected to approximate. We also introduce constants
\mu_{i}>0
.

For each initial condition
y_{0}\in H_{0}^{1}(0,\ell_{0})
, leader control
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{O}\times(0,T))
, and moving boundary
\ell\in\mathcal{F}(\ell^{*},\ell_{0},B)
, we define the nonlinear and convex cost functionals

J_{i}=J_{i}(y_{0},f,\ell):L^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{1}\times(0,T))\times L^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{2}\times(0,T))\to\mathbb{R},\quad i=1,2,

by

J_{i}(v_{1},v_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathcal{O}_{i,d}\times(0,T)}|y-y_{i,d}|^{2}\,dx\,dt+\frac{\mu_{i}}{2}\iint_{\mathcal{O}_{i}\times(0,T)}|v_{i}|^{2}\,dx\,dt,

(1.3)

where

is the solution of (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) corresponding to the data
(y_{0},f,\ell,v_{1},v_{2})

Remark 1.1
.

A solution of the Stefan problem (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) is a pair
(y,\ell)
, consisting of the temperature function and the moving boundary. The Stefan problem does not necessarily admit a unique solution, since different functions
\ell(t)
may satisfy the Stefan condition. However, once a specific boundary
\ell(t)
is fixed (chosen from a pair
(y,\ell)
that solves the Stefan problem), the temperature
y(x,t)
is uniquely determined by system (
1.1
) for given initial data. This ensures that the cost functionals
J_{i}
in (
1.3
) are well-posed for each pair
(y,\ell)
satisfying the Stefan problem.

The concept of Nash equilibrium is given below:

Definition 1.1
.

Given
(y_{0},\ell,f)
such that the functionals
J_{i}=J_{i}(y_{0},f,\ell;\cdot)
,
i=1,2
, are well-posed, a pair
(v_{1},v_{2})\in L^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{1}\times(0,T))\times L^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{2}\times(0,T))
is called a

for the functionals

if

\left|\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle J_{1}(v_{1},v_{2})=\min_{\hat{v}_{1}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{1}\times(0,T))}J_{1}(\hat{v}_{1},v_{2}),\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle J_{2}(v_{1},v_{2})=\min_{\hat{v}_{2}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{2}\times(0,T))}J_{2}(v_{1},\hat{v}_{2}).\end{array}\right.

(1.4)

The hierarchy chosen for our problem designates
f
as the leader. Therefore, our problem consists in finding a null control
f
for the system (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) subject to the condition that
(v_{1},v_{2})
is a Nash equilibrium. Our main result is the following.

Stackelberg strategies in the context of partial differential equations were first introduced in
[
38
]
and
[
39
]
by J.-L. Lions to study bi-objective control problems for the wave and heat equations. Later,
[
16
]
connected Stackelberg strategies with Nash equilibria
[
40
]
, adapting the framework to a setting with one leader and multiple followers. In this formulation, the leader seeks to steer the system toward an approximate controllability objective, while the followers minimize their respective cost functionals, resulting in a Stackelberg–Nash strategy.

In
[
3
]
, the authors studied exact controllability for linear and semilinear parabolic equations with multi-objective controls under Stackelberg–Nash strategies, establishing conditions under which a Nash quasi-equilibrium becomes a true Nash equilibrium. These results were extended in
[
2
]
by relaxing the conditions on the observation domains of the followers. Further developments include the first results for degenerate parabolic equations under the same strategies in
[
1
]
, as well as a comprehensive study of hierarchical control for the semilinear case in
[
18
]
. More recently, the results concerning degenerate parabolic systems were generalized in
[
37
]
. Within hierarchical strategies for coupled systems, the Stackelberg–Nash approach was investigated for parabolic systems in
[
31
,
30
]
. In
[
41
]
, this strategy was applied to a coupled quasilinear parabolic system with controls acting in the interior of the domain. In
[
17
]
, the Stackelberg–Nash strategy was extended to coupled degenerate nonlinear parabolic equations. The existing literature has mainly focused on internal control. In contrast, the authors of
[
4
]
explored scenarios in which the controls act on the boundary, considering a mixed control setting involving both internal and boundary controls. In
[
12
]
and
[
13
]
, the Stackelberg–Nash strategy was applied to the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation, considering internal and distributed controls, respectively.

These results provide a framework for applying Stackelberg-Nash strategies to parabolic systems with multiple controls. Based on this, we now describe the strategy used to prove Theorem
1.1
, presenting the steps justified in the following sections.

Step 1. Incorporation of the leader dependence

As introduced in
[
2
]
, when dealing with constrained problems, it is useful to incorporate the restriction — here, the dependence of the Nash equilibrium controls on the leader
f
— into the system (
1.1
)–(
1.2
).

Let
(y,\ell)
be a solution of the Stefan problem (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) associated with the initial data
y_{0}
and
f
. Let
(v_{1},v_{2})
be a Nash equilibrium for the functionals
J_{i}=J_{i}(y_{0},f,\ell;\cdot)
corresponding to the pair
(y,\ell)
. Since each functional
J_{i}
is convex, a pair
(v_{1},v_{2})
is a Nash equilibrium for
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
if and only if the following Gâteaux derivatives of
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
vanish:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle J_{1}^{\prime}(v_{1},v_{2})\cdot(\hat{v}_{1},0)=0,\quad\quad\displaystyle J_{2}^{\prime}(v_{1},v_{2})\cdot(0,\hat{v}_{2})=0.\end{array}

(1.6)

Since
y
is the unique solution of (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) corresponding to
(y_{0},f,\ell,v_{1},v_{2})
, the following auxiliary system also has a unique solution:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\phi^{i}_{t}-\phi^{i}_{xx}+a(x,t)\phi^{i}=(y-y_{i,d})\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{i,d}}&&\text{in}&&Q_{\ell},\\
&\phi^{i}(0,t)=\phi^{i}(\ell(t),t)=0&&\text{in}&&(0,T),\\
&\phi^{i}(x,T)=0&&\text{in}&&(0,\ell(T)).\end{aligned}\right.

(1.7)

Denoting
(\hat{V}_{1},\hat{V}_{2})=((\hat{v}_{1},0),\ (0,\hat{v}_{2}))
, we can compute that

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle J_{i}^{\prime}(v_{1},v_{2})\cdot\hat{V}_{i}=\iint_{\mathcal{O}_{i}\times(0,T)}(\phi_{i}+\mu_{i}v_{i})\hat{v}_{i}\,dxdt,\end{array}

(1.8)

where
\phi_{i}
solves (
1.7
). Therefore,
(v_{1},v_{2})
is a Nash equilibrium for
J_{1},J_{2}
associated with
(y_{0},f,\ell)
, if and only if

(v_{1},v_{2})=\left(-\dfrac{1}{\mu_{1}}\phi^{1}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{1}},-\dfrac{1}{\mu_{2}}\phi^{2}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{2}}\right).

(1.9)

The above analysis motivates the introduction of the nonlinear optimality system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{llllll}y_{t}-y_{xx}+a(x,t)y=f\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{O}}-\dfrac{1}{\mu_{1}}\phi^{1}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{1}}-\dfrac{1}{\mu_{2}}\phi^{2}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{2}}&&\text{in}&&Q_{\ell},\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr-\phi^{i}_{t}-\phi^{i}_{xx}+a(x,t)\phi^{i}=(y-y_{i,d})\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{i,d}}&&\text{in}&&Q_{\ell},\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr y(0,t)=y(\ell(t),t)=\phi^{i}(0,t)=\phi^{i}(\ell(t),t)=0&&\text{in}&&(0,T),\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr y(\cdot,0)=y_{0}&&\text{in}&&(0,\ell_{0}),\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\phi^{i}(\cdot,T)=0&&\text{in}&&(0,\ell(T)),\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\ell^{\prime}(\cdot)=-\dfrac{1}{\beta}y_{x}(\ell(\cdot),\cdot)&&\text{in}&&(0,T).\end{array}\right.

(1.10)

As observed above, if a Nash equilibrium exists, the problem of finding a null control for the system (
1.1
)–(
1.2
), together with the determination of the Nash equilibrium, reduces to the null controllability of the optimality system (

[

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
(y,f,\ell)\in X^{2}(0,T)\times L^{2}(\mathcal{O}\times(0,T))\times\mathcal{F}(\ell^{*},\ell_{0},B)
be a solution of (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) corresponding to initial data
y_{0}\in H_{0}^{1}(0,\ell_{0})
and targets

,

. There exists a constant
\mu_{01}>0
such that if

for the functionals
J_{1}
and

. Moreover,

(v_{1},v_{2})=\left(-\dfrac{1}{\mu_{1}}\phi^{1}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{1}},-\dfrac{1}{\mu_{2}}\phi^{2}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{2}}\right).

(1.11)

where
(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})
composes the solution of (
1.10
) associated with
(y_{0},f,\ell,y_{1,d},y_{2,d})

The proof of the above approximate controllability result relies on duality arguments that reduce the controllability property to an observability inequality for the adjoint system of
\eqref{optimalsystem}_{1}
–
\eqref{optimalsystem}_{5}
. In our case, the adjoint system is given by

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\psi_{t}-\psi_{xx}+a(x,t)\psi=\gamma^{1}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{1,d}}+\gamma^{2}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{2,d}}&&\text{in}&&Q_{\ell},\\
&\gamma^{i}_{t}-\gamma^{i}_{xx}+a(x,t)\gamma^{i}=-\dfrac{1}{\mu_{i}}\psi\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{i}}&&\text{in}&&Q_{\ell},\\
&\gamma^{i}(0,t)=\gamma^{i}(\ell(t),t)=0,\,\psi(0,t)=\psi(\ell(t),t)=0&&\text{in}&&(0,T),\\
&\gamma^{i}(x,0)=0&&\text{in}&&(0,\ell_{0}),\\
&\psi(x,T)=\psi^{T}(x)&&\text{in}&&(0,\ell(T)),\end{aligned}\right.

(1.15)

This system is connected to
\eqref{optimalsystem}_{1}
–
\eqref{optimalsystem}_{5}
through the identity

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\ell(T)}y(x,T)\psi^{T}(x)dx\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\phantom{0000}=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\ell_{0}}y_{0}(x)\,\psi(x,0)dx+\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\iint_{\mathcal{O}_{i,d}\times(0,T)}y_{i,d}\gamma^{i}dxdt+\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{O}\times(0,T)}f\psi dxdt,\end{array}

(1.16)

which motivates the observability inequality stated in the next theorem.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\ell\in\mathcal{F}(\ell^{*},\ell_{0},B)
,
\psi^{T}\in L^{2}(0,\ell(T))
, and let us assume that one of the geometric configurations
(G1)
,
(G2)
of Theorem
1.2
holds. There exist a constant
\mu_{03}>0
and a positive function
\rho(t)
that blows up at
t=T
and is non-constant only in an arbitrarily small neighborhood of
T
, such that if
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}>\mu_{03}
and the condition
(2)
of Theorem
1.1
is satisfied, then the solution
(\psi,\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
of (
1.15
) associated with
\ell
and
\psi^{T}
satisfies the inequality

\int_{0}^{\ell_{0}}|\psi(x,0)|^{2}dx+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\iint_{Q_{\ell}}\rho^{-2}|\gamma^{i}|^{2}dxdt\leq C_{0}\iint_{\mathcal{O}\times(0,T)}|\psi|^{2}dxdt,

(1.17)

where
C_{0}>0
is a constant independent of
\ell\in\mathcal{F}(\ell^{*},\ell_{0},B)
.

For the proof of the observability inequality, we employ Carleman estimates.
Thus, our main goal is to derive a global estimate for the triple

(\psi,\gamma^{1},\gamma^{2})
in terms of a single observation of
\psi
on

\mathcal{O}\times(0,T)
. Our approach is motivated by
[
2
]
, where the authors
do not use a single Fursikov weight but rather two different weight functions
in order to handle both cases
(G1)
and
(G2)
in (
1.12
). The construction
of suitable weight functions is nontrivial, and becomes even more delicate when the
moving boundary
\ell(t)
is present. In
[
2
]
, the two weights satisfy special compatibility conditions that allow one to combine two weighted Carleman estimates. However, these weights are not directly applicable to free-boundary problems of Stefan type because they do not take into account the motion of the boundary. For free-boundary problems, a viable
alternative is to adapt the weights introduced in
[
21
]
.
Our idea here is to build a new weight function that permits the manipulation and
matching of the two Carleman inequalities in the spirit of the constructions in

[
2
]
and
[
21
]
. The Fursikov-type weight that will be used in this work is introduced in (
2.18
), and its main properties are collected in Lemma
2.1
. For organizational reasons, we postpone to Appendix
A
the proof of the lemma, in which the weight is obtained constructively. An advantage of this constructive approach is that the weight can be easily implemented in numerical schemes, including in the cylindrical case
\ell=const
., which may be useful for computational experiments.

Step 3. Controllability of the nonlinear optimal system

Once the controllability of the linearized system has been established, we
proceed to prove the controllability of the nonlinear problem, that is,
Theorem
1.2
. To this end, we use a fixed-point argument to pass from the
linearized system to the nonlinear one, a procedure that is by now standard in
the literature. However, as pointed out in
[
22
]

(see also
[
6
]
), a major technical difficulty lies in
obtaining compactness for the operator that generates the fixed point. We follow
[
22
]
to obtain compactness from a Hölder estimate for the normal derivative
y_{x}
. However, the essential difference between
[
22
]
and our setting is that we are dealing with a coupled system rather than a single equation. Therefore, we must verify whether such a Hölder estimate holds for the solution
(y,\phi^{1},\phi^{2})
of the linearized optimality system
\eqref{optimalsystem}_{1}
–
\eqref{optimalsystem}_{5}
.

We now outline the structure of the paper. In Section
2
, we derive a Carleman estimate for the adjoint system (
1.15
). This result is then used in Section
3
to derive the observability inequality stated in Theorem
1.4
. Section
4
is devoted to proving the approximate controllability stated in Theorem
1.3
. Finally, in Section
5
, we prove Theorem
1.2
, which solves the multi-objective Stefan problem proposed in this work. We close with concluding remarks in Section
6
, followed by an appendix that contains supporting technical material.

The compactness required by Schauder’s theorem will follow from a suitable parabolic Hölder regularity for the states

in Theorem

. More precisely, let us consider the subdomain

of

, the set

does not intersect any of these. The parabolic Hölder regularity result we use is stated next.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
y_{0}\in W^{1,4}_{0}(0,\ell_{0})
, and let
(y_{\varepsilon,\ell},\phi^{1}_{\varepsilon,\ell},\phi^{2}_{\varepsilon,\ell})
be a solution of
\eqref{optimalsystem}_{1}
–
\eqref{optimalsystem}_{5}
furnished by Theorem

. Then, there exist constants

and

such that

(y_{\varepsilon,\ell},\phi^{1}_{\varepsilon,\ell},\phi^{2}_{\varepsilon,\ell})\in[C^{1+\alpha,(1+\alpha)/2}(\overline{R_{\ell}})]^{3}

and

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\|y_{\varepsilon,\ell}\|_{C^{1+\alpha,\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}(\overline{R_{\ell}})}+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\|\phi^{i}_{\varepsilon,\ell}\|_{C^{1+\alpha,\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}(\overline{R_{\ell}})}\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\phantom{000000000000}\leq\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{1}\left(\|y_{0}\|_{W^{1,4}_{0}(0,\ell_{0})}+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\|\rho y_{i,d}\|_{L^{4}(\mathcal{O}_{i,d}\times(0,T))}\right).\end{array}

(5.63)

In particular,

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\|(y_{\varepsilon,\ell})_{x}(\ell(\cdot),\cdot)\|_{C^{\alpha}([0,T])}\leq\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{1}\left(\|y_{0}\|_{W^{1,4}_{0}(0,\ell_{0})}+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\|\rho y_{i,d}\|_{L^{4}(\mathcal{O}_{i,d}\times(0,T))}\right).\end{array}

(5.64)

The proof of this lemma is similar to the proofs given in

and

. For completeness, we include a brief proof in Appendix

Since the assumptions of Theorem

(5.89)

and

(y_{\varepsilon,\ell_{\varepsilon}})_{x}(\ell_{\varepsilon}(t),t))\rightarrow(y_{\hat{\ell}})_{x}(\hat{\ell}(t),t))\quad\mbox{uniformly,\quad as}\quad\varepsilon\rightarrow 0.

(5.90)

Therefore, the quintuple
(y_{\hat{\ell}},\hat{\ell},f_{\hat{\ell}},\phi^{1}_{\hat{\ell}},\phi^{2}_{\hat{\ell}})
solves the Stefan problem (
1.10
) and satisfies the null controllability condition

y_{\hat{\ell}}(\cdot,T)=0\quad\mbox{in}\quad L^{2}(0,\hat{\ell}(T)).

This concludes the proof of the theorem.

Comments and Conclusions

•

It is possible to invert the leader-follower roles in the Stackelberg (or Pareto) strategy by taking
(v_{1},v_{2})
as the leader and the null control
f
as the follower. In this case, the problem would consist in finding a Nash equilibrium
(v_{1},v_{2})
subject to the condition that
f
is a null control, that is, finding
(v_{1},v_{2})
that solves

\left|\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle J_{1}(f,v_{1},v_{2})=\min_{\mathcal{N}_{1}(v_{2})}J_{1}(f,\hat{v}_{1},v_{2}),\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle J_{2}(f,v_{1},v_{2})=\min_{\mathcal{N}_{2}(v_{1})}J_{2}(f,v_{1},\hat{v}_{2}),\end{array}\right.

where

\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathcal{N}^{1}(v_{2})=\left\{\bar{v}_{1}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{1}\times(0,T));\ y(y_{0},\bar{v}_{1},v_{2},f,\ell;x,T)=0,\ f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{O}\times(0,T))\right\},\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\mathcal{N}^{2}(v_{1})=\left\{\bar{v}_{2}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{2}\times(0,T));\ y(y_{0},v_{1},\bar{v}_{2},f,\ell;x,T)=0,\ f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{O}\times(0,T))\right\},\end{array}

with
y(y_{0},\bar{v}_{1},\bar{v}_{2},f,\ell,x,t)
denoting the solution of (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) associated with
y_{0},\bar{v}_{1},\bar{v}_{2},\ell
. A possible approach would be to follow the ideas of
[
11
]
.

•

Our approach can be applied to other problems. For example, we may introduce a nonlinear term
F
in the Stefan problem, following the ideas of
[
20
]
and
[
3
]
. Another interesting question concerns the viscous Burgers equation
[
19
]
, which can be addressed by combining the techniques in
[
5
]
. Further questions arise from the fact that the moving boundary interacts with the heat flux through the relation
\ell^{\prime}(t)=-\beta^{-1}y_{x}(\ell(t),t)
, which follows from the choice of the Laplacian operator
\mathcal{A}(y)=y_{xx}
. However, the relationship between
\ell
and the operator
\mathcal{A}
may change when other types of operators are considered. This leads to several interesting open questions regarding hierarchical control:

i)

Quasi-linear 1D parabolic equation:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &y_{t}-(a(y)y_{x})_{x}+F(y)=f\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{O}}+v_{1}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{1}}+v_{2}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{2}}&&\text{in}&&Q_{\ell},\\
&y(0,t)=y(\ell(t),t)=0&&\text{in}&&(0,T),\\
&y(\cdot,0)=y_{0}&&\text{in}&&(0,\ell_{0}),\end{aligned}\right.

and consider the Stefan condition:

\ell^{\prime}(t)=-a(y)y_{x}(\ell(t),t),

where
F
is a given
C^{2}
function defined on
\mathbb{R}
with
F(0)=0
and
a(\cdot):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a twice continuously differentiable function.

ii)

Degenerate 1D parabolic equation:
Let us assume that
\gamma\in[0,2]
is an exponent

\left\{\begin{aligned} &y_{t}-(x^{\gamma}y_{x})_{x}=f\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{O}}+v_{1}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{1}}+v_{2}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{2}}&&\text{in}&&Q_{\ell},\\
&y(\ell(t),t)=0\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \left\{\begin{aligned} &y(0,t)=0,&&\text{(Weak)}\\
&\text{or}\\
&(x^{\gamma}y_{x})(0,t)=0,&&\text{(Strong)}\end{aligned}\right.&&\text{in}&&(0,T),\\
&y(\cdot,0)=y_{0}&&\text{in}&&(0,\ell_{0}),\end{aligned}\right.

and consider the Stefan condition:

\ell^{\prime}(t)=-\ell(t)^{\gamma}y_{x}(\ell(t),t).

These problems remain open in our setting. It is important to highlight that, for degenerate systems, the case
(G2)
remains open even on fixed domains. The analysis of these two cases requires a deeper understanding of how to treat the operator, the regularity properties of the Stefan condition, and the corresponding fixed-point arguments (see, for instance,
[
14
,
45
]
).

The proof is standard, relying on a diffeomorphism that maps the noncylindrical domain to a cylindrical one and on the Faedo–Galerkin method to obtain a unique strong solution; we only sketch it below.
