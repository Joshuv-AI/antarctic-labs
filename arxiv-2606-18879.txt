Polyconvexity implies Hill’s inequality in SL ⁡ ( 2 ) {\rm SL}(2)
Ionel-Dumitrel Ghiba, Maximilian P. Wollner, and, Patrizio Neff

Abstract
For compressible nonlinear isotropic elasticity it is well known that rank-one convexity, polyconvexity and the monotonicity of the Cauchy stress tensor with respect to the logarithmic stretch tensor (the true stress-true strain monotonicity, TSTS-M + ) are independent constitutive conditions which should, however, all together be satisfied for a physically meaningful description of idealized elastic materials. In the incompressible case, TSTS-M + turns into Hill’s inequality since the Cauchy stress 𝝈 \boldsymbol{\sigma} reduces to the Kirchhoff stress 𝝉 \boldsymbol{\tau} . Hill’s inequality requires then monotonicity of the Kirchhoff stress in terms of the logarithmic stretch tensor evaluated for incompressible response. In this paper we clarify how the a priori independent notions of Legendre-Hadamard ellipticity (LH), polyconvexity and Hill’s inequality are nevertheless intimately connected. More precisely, by providing several alternative proofs, we show that both LH-ellipticity (rank-one convexity) and polyconvexity imply the weak Hill inequality in the incompressible two-dimensional case. Keywords : polyconvexity, rank-one convexity, Legendre-Hadamard ellipticity, constitutive inequalities, Hill’s inequality, incompressible materials, material stability, true-stress-true-strain monotonicity, monotone Cauchy stress

Introduction

Incompressible nonlinear elasticity is perhaps the most widely used assumption in the analytical and algorithmic treatment of elastomeric materials. While these materials are indeed highly compressible, they still show a comparatively large bulk modulus (high resistance against volumetric deformation) compared to a small shear modulus (low resistance against shape change), so that for certain boundary value problems, the actual response is in fact nearly incompressible (isochoric)
[
41

On the other hand, material stability questions need to be discussed in advance before setting up elastic energies. Among them, the Legendre–Hadamard ellipticity condition
[
18

7

and Ball’s polyconvexity condition
[

Recently, it has been shown that polyconvexity alone is, however, insufficient to guarantee, e.g., monotone increasing Cauchy stress in uniaxial tension with free lateral sides

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(\log V),H\rangle={\rm D}_{\log V}\widehat{W}^{\rm inc}(\log V).H\qquad\forall\,H\in{\rm dev}\Sym(n).

(2.23)

We refer to
\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}:{\rm dev}\Sym(n)\to{\rm dev}\Sym(n)
as the Kirchhoff stress tensor for energies defined on
{\rm SL}(n)
. This is in accord with the Richter-formula (see, e.g.,
[
36
,
35
]
)

\displaystyle\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(\log V)={\rm D}_{\log V}\widehat{W}^{\rm inc}(\log V).

(2.24)

Let us notice that we are working with functions defined on

{\rm dev}\Sym(n)
. If we assume that the energy

\widehat{W}^{\rm inc}:{\rm dev}\Sym(n)\to\mathbb{R}
is the restriction
of a function
\widehat{W}:\Sym(n)\to\mathbb{R}
, then the derivative
can be computed in the ambient space
\Sym(n)
by introducing a Lagrange
multiplier associated with the constraint
{\rm tr}X=0
and we have
6
6

6

More precisely, consider the augmented functional

\mathcal{L}(X,p):=\widehat{W}(X)+p\,{\rm tr}X,

where
p\in\mathbb{R}
is a scalar Lagrange multiplier. Then, for every

H\in\Sym(n)
, one has

{\rm D}_{X}\mathcal{L}(X,p).\,h=\langle{\rm D}_{X}\widehat{W}(X),H\rangle+p\,{\rm tr}H.

Restricting to admissible variations
H\in{\rm dev}\Sym(n)

(i.e.
{\rm tr}H=0
), we recover the derivative of
\widehat{W}^{\rm inc}
:

{\rm D}_{X}\widehat{W}^{\rm inc}(X).\,h=\langle{\rm D}_{X}\widehat{W}(X),H\rangle=\langle({\rm D}_{X}\widehat{W}(X)+p\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}}),H\rangle.

Therefore, the Kirchhoff stress tensor admits the representation

\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)={\rm D}_{X}\widehat{W}(X)+p(X)\,{\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}},\ X\in{\rm dev}\Sym(n),

where
p(X)
is the Lagrange multiplier associated with the constraint

{\rm tr}X=0
. Imposing the condition
\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)\in{\rm dev}\Sym(n)

yields

p(X)=-\frac{1}{n}{\rm tr}\big({\rm D}_{X}\widehat{W}(X)\big),

and thus

\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)={\rm dev}\big({\rm D}_{X}\widehat{W}(X)\big).

The scalar field
p(X)
represents the indeterminate spherical part of
the stress tensor, and is completely analogous to the pressure in
incompressible elasticity.

\displaystyle\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)={\rm dev}\big({\rm D}_{X}\widehat{W}(X)\big)\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(\log V)={\rm dev}\big({\rm D}_{\log V}\widehat{W}(\log V)\big)\quad\forall\,V\in{\rm SL}(n).

(2.25)

2.2.2
The vector of the principal components of the Kirchhoff stress tensor on
{\rm SL}(n)

Similarly, for
\ell_{i}=\log\lambda_{i},

i=1,2,...,n
consider

\displaystyle\Pi:=\left\{\ell\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\middle|\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}=0\right\}=\left\{(\log\lambda_{1},\log\lambda_{2},....,\log\lambda_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\middle|\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\lambda_{i}=0\right\}.

(2.26)

Then
\Pi
is a linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and

T_{\ell}\Pi=\Pi,\ \forall\,\ell\in\Pi.

Let
\widehat{g}^{\rm inc}:\Pi\to\mathbb{R}
be the differentiable function such that the isotropic energy satisfies
\widehat{W}^{\rm inc}(X)=\widehat{g}(\ell)
,
X\in{\rm dev}\Sym(n)
,
\ell\in\Pi
. The intrinsic derivative at
\ell
is the linear map

{\rm D}_{\ell}\widehat{g}^{\rm inc}(\ell):\Pi\to\mathbb{R}

defined by

\displaystyle{\rm D}_{\ell}\widehat{g}^{\rm inc}(\ell).h=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\widehat{g}^{\rm inc}(\ell+th)-\widehat{g}^{\rm inc}(\ell)}{t}\qquad\forall\,h\in\Pi.

(2.27)

Identifying
\Pi^{*}
with
\Pi
via the Euclidean inner product, by the Riesz representation theorem there exists a unique vector
\widehat{\tau}(\ell)\in\Pi
such that

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{\tau}(\ell),h\rangle={\rm D}_{\ell}\widehat{g}^{\rm inc}(\ell).h\qquad\forall\,h\in\Pi.

(2.28)

For any vector
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define its “deviatoric part” by

{\rm dev}(w):=w-\frac{1}{n}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\Big)\mathbf{1},
with
\mathbf{1}=(1,\dots,1).

This is the orthogonal projection of
w
onto
\Pi
. Let
\widehat{g}^{\rm inc}:\Pi\to\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable function, and assume that it is the restriction of a smooth function

g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}.

To compute derivatives intrinsically on
\Pi
, we introduce a Lagrange multiplier associated with the constraint
\sum_{i}\ell_{i}=0
and we have
7
7

7

For this, let us define the augmented functional

\mathcal{L}(v,p)=g(\ell)+p\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i},\ (v,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}.

Then, for every
h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

{\rm D}_{\ell}\mathcal{L}(v,p).\,h=\langle{\rm D}_{\ell}g(\ell),h\rangle+p\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}.

Restricting to admissible variations
h\in\Pi
(i.e.
\sum_{i}h_{i}=0
), we obtain

{\rm D}_{\ell}\widehat{g}^{\rm inc}(\ell).\,h=\langle{\rm D}_{\ell}g(\ell),h\rangle=\langle{\rm D}_{\ell}g(\ell)+p(\ell)\mathbf{1},\,h\rangle.

By the Riesz representation theorem, there exists a unique vector
\widehat{\tau}(\ell)\in\Pi
such that

\langle\widehat{\tau}(\ell),h\rangle={\rm D}_{\ell}\widehat{g}^{\rm inc}(\ell).\,h\qquad\forall\,h\in\Pi.

Hence if we take

\widehat{\tau}(\ell)={\rm D}_{\ell}g(\ell)+p(\ell)\mathbf{1}

then, imposing the constraint
\widehat{\tau}(\ell)\in\Pi
yields

0=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\tau}_{i}(\ell)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{i}g(\ell)+n\,p(\ell),

and therefore

p(\ell)=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{i}g(\ell).

Substituting back, we obtain the intrinsic gradient

\widehat{\tau}(\ell)={\rm D}_{\ell}g(\ell)-\frac{1}{n}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{i}g(\ell)\Big)\mathbf{1}={\rm dev}\big({\rm D}_{\ell}g(\ell)\big).

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}(\ell)={\rm dev}\big({\rm D}_{\ell}g(\ell)\big).

(2.29)

Thus, the intrinsic gradient of
\widehat{g}^{\rm inc}
on
\Pi
is the orthogonal projection of the ambient gradient
{\rm D}_{\ell}g(\ell)
onto
\Pi
. We call
\widehat{\tau}
the vector of the principal components of the Kirchhoff stress tensor. However, in order to argue this definition we have to show that indeed the thus defined vector
\widehat{\tau}
is the vector containing the eigenvalues of the Kirchhoff stress tensor
\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}
.

2.2.3
From the Kirchhoff stress tensor to its principal components and vice versa

We show now the relation between the intrinsic gradient of
\widehat{g}^{\rm inc}
and the intrinsic derivative of
\widehat{W}^{\rm inc}
.
Although the main idea of the proof remains largely unchanged, the
incompressible case requires a separate argument since the admissible
tensors no longer vary in all directions but only along a constrained
subset corresponding to volume preservation. Consequently, monotonicity
must be verified only along directions compatible with this constraint, and
results from the compressible setting do not automatically transfer.
Moreover, one cannot rely on properties of possible extensions outside the
incompressible setting, since different extensions may exhibit different
monotonicity properties, with some being monotone and others not. To the
best of our knowledge, such a proof has not been carried out explicitly in
the literature, and therefore cannot simply be cited.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
\widehat{W}^{\rm inc}:{\rm dev}\Sym(n)\to\mathbb{R}
be an isotropic differentiable function, and let

\displaystyle X=Q\,\diag(\ell)\,Q^{T},\qquad\ell=(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{n})\in\Pi,\qquad Q\in{\rm O}(n),

(2.30)

be the spectral decomposition of
X
. Assume that
\widehat{W}^{\rm inc}
admits the spectral representation

\displaystyle\widehat{W}^{\rm inc}(X)=\widehat{g}^{\rm inc}(\ell)\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\widehat{W}^{\rm inc}(\log V)=\widehat{g}^{\rm inc}(\log\lambda_{1},\log\lambda_{2},...,\log\lambda_{n})

(2.31)

for some symmetric differentiable function
\widehat{g}^{\rm inc}:\Pi\to\mathbb{R}
. Then

\displaystyle\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)=Q\,\diag(\widehat{\tau}(\ell))\,Q^{T}.

(2.32)

In particular, the components of
\widehat{\tau}(\ell)
are exactly the principal values of the Kirchhoff stress tensor
\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)
.

Proof.

Let
h\in\Pi
and define

H:=Q\,\diag(h)\,Q^{T}.

Since
\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}=0
, we have

{\rm tr}H={\rm tr}(\diag(h))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}=0,

hence
H\in{\rm dev}\Sym(n)
.
Now consider the curve

X(t):=X+tH=Q\,\diag(\ell+th)\,Q^{T}.

Because
\widehat{W}^{\rm inc}
is isotropic and admits the spectral representation

\widehat{W}^{\rm inc}(Q\,\diag(w)\,Q^{T})=\widehat{g}^{\rm inc}(w)
, we obtain

\widehat{W}^{\rm inc}(X+tH)=\widehat{g}^{\rm inc}(\ell+th).

Differentiating at
t=0
yields

{\rm D}\widehat{W}^{\rm inc}(X).H={\rm D}\widehat{g}^{\rm inc}(\ell).h

Using the definitions of
\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)
and
\widehat{\tau}(\ell)
through the Riesz representation theorem, we therefore get

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X),H\rangle={\rm D}\widehat{W}^{\rm inc}(X).H={\rm D}\widehat{g}^{\rm inc}(\ell).h=\langle\widehat{\tau}(\ell),h\rangle.

(2.33)

Since
H=Q\,\diag(h)\,Q^{T}
, the left-hand side can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X),H\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X),\,Q\,\diag(h)\,Q^{T}\rangle=\langle Q^{T}\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)Q,\diag(h)\rangle

(2.34)

Since

\langle Q^{T}\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)Q,\diag(h)\rangle=\langle\widehat{\tau}(\ell),h\rangle

and

\langle Q^{T}\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)Q,\diag(h)\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Q^{T}\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)Q)_{ii}h_{i},

it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[(Q^{T}\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)Q)_{ii}-\widehat{\tau}_{i}(\ell)\right]\,h_{i}=0\qquad\forall\,h\in\Pi.

(2.35)

Therefore the vector

\displaystyle\big((Q^{T}\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)Q)_{11}-\widehat{\tau}_{1}(\ell),\dots,(Q^{T}\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)Q)_{nn}-\widehat{\tau}_{n}(\ell)\big)

(2.36)

is orthogonal to
\Pi
. Since

\Pi^{\perp}={\rm span}\{(1,\dots,1)\},

there exists
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle(Q^{T}\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)Q)_{ii}-\widehat{\tau}_{i}(\ell)=c\qquad\forall i=1,\dots,n.

(2.37)

Now both
Q^{T}\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)Q
and
\diag(\widehat{\tau}(\ell))
are trace-free, because

(Q^{T}\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)Q)\in{\rm dev}\Sym(n),

\widehat{\tau}(\ell)\in\Pi.

Thus

\displaystyle 0={\rm tr}(Q^{T}\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)Q)-{\rm tr}(\diag(\widehat{\tau}(\ell)))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big((Q^{T}\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)Q)_{ii}-\widehat{\tau}_{i}(\ell)\big)=n\,c,

(2.38)

and therefore
c=0
. Consequently,

\displaystyle(Q^{T}\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)Q)_{ii}=\widehat{\tau}_{i}(\ell)\qquad\forall i=1,\dots,n.

(2.39)

It remains to show that the off-diagonal entries of
(Q^{T}\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)Q)
vanish. Since
\widehat{W}^{\rm inc}
is isotropic, one has

\widehat{W}^{\rm inc}(QXQ^{T})=\widehat{W}^{\rm inc}(X)\forall\,Q\in{\rm O}(n).

Differentiating this identity with respect to
X
yields

\displaystyle\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(QXQ^{T})=Q\,\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)\,Q^{T}.

(2.40)

Let
Q
be such that

X=Q\,\diag(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{n})\,Q^{T}.

Then

\displaystyle Q^{T}\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)Q=\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(\diag(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{n})).

(2.41)

Since
\diag(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{n})
is diagonal, it follows that

S:=Q^{T}\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)Q

is a function of a diagonal matrix. Since
\widehat{W}^{\rm inc}
is isotropic, one has

\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(PDP^{T})=P\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(D)P^{T},\forall P\in O(n).

In particular, if
PDP^{T}=D
, then

S=PSP^{T}.

Choosing
P=\diag(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n})
with
\varepsilon_{i}=\pm 1
,
we obtain

S_{ij}=\varepsilon_{i}\varepsilon_{j}S_{ij}.

For
i\neq j
, selecting
\varepsilon_{i}=-1
,
\varepsilon_{j}=1
yields

S_{ij}=0.

Thus
S:=Q^{T}\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)Q
is diagonal having on the diagonal the vector
\widehat{\tau}(\ell)
.
∎

Therefore
\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}\colon{\rm dev}\,\,\mathrm{Sym}(n)\to\Sym(n)
is an isotropic tensor function satisfying

\displaystyle\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(Q^{T}\cdot\diag(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},...,\ell_{n})\cdot Q)=Q^{T}\cdot\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(\diag(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},...,\ell_{n}))\cdot Q

(2.42)

and

\displaystyle\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(\underbrace{Q^{T}\cdot\diag(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},...,\ell_{n})\cdot Q}_{X\,\in\,{\rm dev}\Sym(n)})=\underbrace{Q^{T}\cdot\diag(\widehat{\tau}(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},...,\ell_{n}))\cdot Q}_{\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)\,\in\,\mathrm{Sym}(n)}\quad\forall\,Q\in\OO(n)

(2.43)

with a vector-function

\widehat{\tau}=(\widehat{\tau}_{1},\widehat{\tau}_{2},...,\widehat{\tau}_{n})\colon\Pi\to\mathbb{R}^{n},
which fulfills

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}_{i}(\ell_{\pi(1)},\ell_{\pi(2)},...,\ell_{\pi(n)})=\widehat{\tau}_{\pi(i)}(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},...,\ell_{n})

(2.44)

for any permutation
\pi\colon\{1,2,...,n\}\to\{1,2,...,n\}
. The permutation symmetry implies and it is implied by isotropy.

2.3
Hilbert-monotonicity and Hill’s inequality

The results obtained in the previous subsection clarify why the following definitions are well formulated.

Definition 2.8
.

The Kirchhoff stress tensor
\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}\colon{\rm dev}\,\,\mathrm{Sym}(n)\to{\rm dev}\,\,\mathrm{Sym}(n)
is called
strictly Hilbert-monotone
if

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)-\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(\overline{X}),\,X-\overline{X}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}>0\qquad\forall\,X\neq\overline{X}\in{\rm dev}\,{\rm Sym}(n)

\displaystyle\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\langle\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(\log V)-\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(\log\overline{V}),\log V-\log\overline{V}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}>0\qquad\forall\,V\neq\overline{V}\in{\rm Sym}^{+}(n)\,.

(2.45)

We refer to this inequality as
strict Hilbert-space matrix-monotonicity
of the tensor function
\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}
. The Kirchhoff stress tensor
\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}\colon{\rm dev}\,\,\mathrm{Sym}(n)\to{\rm dev}\,\,\mathrm{Sym}(n)
is called
Hilbert-monotone
if

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)-\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(\overline{X}),\,X-\overline{X}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}\geq 0\qquad\forall\,X,\,\overline{X}\in{\rm dev}\,{\rm Sym}(n)\,.

(2.46)

Definition 2.9
.

The vector function
\widehat{\tau}\colon\Pi\to\Pi
is
strictly vector monotone
if

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{\tau}(\ell)-\widehat{\tau}(\overline{\ell}),\,\ell-\overline{\ell}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}>0\qquad\forall\ \ell\neq\overline{\ell}\in\Pi,

(2.47)

and it is
vector monotone
if

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{\tau}(\ell)-\widehat{\tau}(\overline{\ell}),\,\ell-\overline{\ell}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\geq 0\qquad\forall\ \ell,\overline{\ell}\in\Pi,

(2.48)

Definition 2.10
.

If
\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}\colon{\rm dev}\,\,\mathrm{Sym}(n)\to{\rm dev}\Sym(n)
is a continuously differentiable tensor function, then it is called
strongly Hilbert-monotone
if

\displaystyle\langle{\rm D}_{X}\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X).H,H\rangle>0\qquad\text{for all }\;{X\in{\rm dev}\,\rm Sym}(n),\;H\in{\rm dev}\,{\rm Sym}(n)\,.

(2.49)

Definition 2.11
.

If
\widehat{\tau}\colon\Pi\to\Pi
is a continuously differentiable vector function, then it is called
strongly vector monotone
if

\displaystyle\langle{\rm D}_{\ell}\widehat{\tau}(\ell).h,h\rangle>0\qquad\text{for all }\;\ell\in\Pi,\;h\in\Pi\,.

(2.50)

In a forthcoming paper
[
22
]
, we discuss a result concerning matrix monotonicity versus vector monotonicity in the context of compressible isotropic elastic materials, see also Ogden’s work
[
33
, last page in the Appendix]
and Hill’s seminal contributions
[
12
,

]
.

In the following we give some milestones of the proof of the following result to assure the reader that the conclusions regarding the matrix monotonicity vs. vector monotonicity of compressible isotropic elastic materials hold true for incompressible materials, too.

Theorem 2.12
.

The Kirchhoff tensor
\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}\colon{\rm dev}\,\,\mathrm{Sym}(n)\to{\rm dev}\Sym(n)
is (strictly/strongly) matrix monotone if and only if
\widehat{\tau}\colon\Pi\to\Pi
is (strictly/strongly) vector-monotone.

Proof.

Recall that
\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}=\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(\log V)
. We prove only the implication from vector-monotonicity to matrix-monotonicity since the other implication is trivial.
The first step in the proof is to show that for every
x\in\Pi
and all
i\neq j
,

\displaystyle(\ell_{i}-\ell_{j})\,(\widehat{\tau}_{i}(\ell)-\widehat{\tau}_{j}(\ell))\geq 0.

(2.51)

In particular,
v
and
g(\ell)
are sorted in the same algebraic order.

Fix
\ell\in\Pi
and indices
i\neq j
.
Let
\pi
denotes the permutation exchanging only
i
and
j
, and consider

h=\pi(\ell).

Since permutations preserve the condition
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\ell_{k}=0
, we still have
h\in\Pi
.
By vector monotonicity we know that

\displaystyle 0\leq\langle\widehat{\tau}(\ell)-\widehat{\tau}(h),\,\ell-h\rangle.

(2.52)

By permutation-equivariance we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}_{i}(h)=\widehat{\tau}_{i}(\ell_{\pi(1)},\ell_{\pi(2)},...,\ell_{\pi(n)})=\widehat{\tau}_{\pi(i)}(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},...,\ell_{n}).

(2.53)

Hence

\displaystyle 0\leq\langle\widehat{\tau}(\ell)-\widehat{\tau}(h),\,\ell-h\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\widehat{\tau}_{i}(\ell)-\widehat{\tau}_{\pi(i)}(\ell))(\ell_{i}-\ell_{\pi(i)}).

(2.54)

Since the components which are not permuted
lead to zero entries on the sum from the right hand side of the above inequality, it follows that

\displaystyle 0\leq 2\,(\widehat{\tau}_{i}(\ell)-\widehat{\tau}_{j}(\ell))(\ell_{i}-\ell_{j}),

(2.55)

which proves the claim. As a consequence, if
\ell^{\downarrow}
denotes
\ell
sorted in decreasing order, then

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}(\ell)^{\downarrow}=\widehat{\tau}(\ell^{\downarrow}).

(2.56)

The second step is the reduction of the matrix monotonicity to a diagonal–orthogonal form.
Consider two arbitrary matrice in
{\rm dev}\,{\rm Sym}(n)
. Then, there exists
Q,R\in{\rm O}(n)
and
\ell,h\in\Pi
such that

\displaystyle X=Q\,\diag(\ell)\,Q^{T},\qquad Y=R\,\diag(h)\,R^{T}.

(2.57)

Using orthogonal invariance of the trace, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(X)-\widehat{\scalebox{1.25}{$\boldsymbol{\tau}$}}(Y),\,X-Y\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\diag(\widehat{\tau}(\ell))-\widetilde{Q}\diag(\widehat{\tau}(h))\widetilde{Q}^{T},\,\diag(\ell)-\widetilde{Q}\diag(h)\widetilde{Q}^{T}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\diag(\widehat{\tau}(\ell)),\diag(\ell)\rangle+\langle\diag(\widehat{\tau}(h)),\diag(h)\rangle

(2.58)

\displaystyle\quad-\langle\diag(\widehat{\tau}(\ell))\widetilde{Q},\widetilde{Q}\diag(h)\rangle-\langle\diag(\ell)\widetilde{Q},\widetilde{Q}\diag(\widehat{\tau}(h))\rangle,

where

\widetilde{Q}:=Q^{T}R\in O(3).

We remark that the matrix
M=(m_{ij})
given by
m_{ij}=\widetilde{Q}_{ij}^{2}
is doubly stochastic, i.e.,

m_{ij}\geq 0,\ \sum_{j}^{n}m_{ij}=1,\ \sum_{i}^{n}m_{ij}=1,

since the orthogonality of
\widetilde{Q}
is exactly what guarantees that the row vectors
and the column vectors have norm
1
, and squaring the entries converts those norm-
1

conditions into the row and column sum conditions for a doubly stochastic matrix. By the Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem
[
4
,
10
,
15
]
, every doubly stochastic matrix can be written
as a convex combination of permutation matrices.
Hence there exist permutation matrices
\mathcal{P}_{k}
and coefficients

q_{k}\geq 0
,
\sum_{k}q_{k}=1
, such that

M=\sum_{k}q_{k}\mathcal{P}_{k}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\langle a,M.b\rangle=\Bigl\langle a,\Bigl(\sum_{k}q_{k}\mathcal{P}_{k}\Bigr).b\Bigr\rangle=\sum_{k}q_{k}\,\langle a,\mathcal{P}_{k}.b\rangle.

(2.59)

Thus
\langle a,M.b\rangle
is a convex combination of the numbers

\langle a,\mathcal{P}_{k}b\rangle
. Hence

\displaystyle\langle a,M.b\rangle\leq\max_{\mathcal{P}\ \mathrm{permutation\ matrix}}\langle a,\mathcal{P}.b\rangle.

(2.60)

If
P
corresponds to a permutation
\pi
, then

\langle a,\mathcal{P}.b\rangle=\sum_{i}a_{i}\,b_{\pi(i)}.

We recall the rearrangement inequality
[
10
,
20
]
:
if

a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq\dots\leq a_{n},\ b_{1}\leq b_{2}\leq\dots\leq b_{n},

then for every permutation
\pi
of
\{1,2,...,n\}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\,b_{3-i+1}\;\leq\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\,b_{\pi(i)}\;\leq\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\,b_{i}.

(2.61)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\max_{\mathcal{P}\ \mathrm{perm}}\langle a,\mathcal{P}.b\rangle=\langle a^{\downarrow},b^{\downarrow}\rangle\qquad\forall\ a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(2.62)

Combining the above steps, for all
\widetilde{Q}\in O(n)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\langle\diag(\ell)\widetilde{Q},\widetilde{Q}.\diag(h)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\diag(\ell),M.\diag(h)\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\mathcal{P}\ \mathrm{perm}}\langle\diag(\ell),\mathcal{P}.\diag(h)\rangle=\langle\diag(\ell)^{\downarrow},\diag(h)^{\downarrow}\rangle,

\displaystyle\langle\diag(\widehat{\tau}(\ell))\widetilde{Q},\widetilde{Q}.\diag(\widehat{\tau}(h))\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\diag(\widehat{\tau}(\ell))),M.\diag(\widehat{\tau}(h))\rangle

(2.63)

\Rightarrow

4
Conclusion and outlook

In this contribution, we provided two proofs showing that polyconvexity (rank-one convexity) implies Hill’s inequality and, in turn, true-stress-true-strain monotonicity (TSTS-M
+
) in the two-dimensional incompressible case. In the process, we also revisited the transition from tensor arguments to principal-value representations for questions of convexity and monotonicity of isotropic functions.

Concerning the incompressible three-dimensional case, we know that Legendre–Hadamard ellipticity does not imply Hill’s inequality. For instance,
W(F)=\sqrt{{\rm tr}(F^{T}F)-3+\alpha}
for all
\alpha\in(0,3-15\times 2^{-7/3})
is rank-one convex by virtue of
[
8
]
, but does not satisfy Hill’s inequality since in uniaxial tension the Cauchy stress response is not monotone in compression. Recently,
[
17
]
also identified an incompressible polyconvex energy function that does not satisfy Hill’s inequality.

An intermediate open question is to find an energy defined on
{\rm SL}(3)
that is Legendre–Hadamard elliptic but not polyconvex. The difficulty here is that necessary and sufficient conditions for polyconvexity only postulate the existence of a particular higher-dimensional function, but provide little to show lack thereof. A possible avenue would be to prove lack of quasi-convexity which is made difficult by the closed-form construction of an isochoric, heterogeneous perturbation field over which one could volume-average. We nonetheless guess that the construction of such an example is possible in the three-dimensional case, while in two dimensions all conditions coincide, as seen here.

Another open question is the relation between incompressible polyconvexity and Hill’s inequality in 3D for a subclass of energy functions of Valanis-Landel type
[
43
]
, where the reduced functional space might indeed entail an implication similar to the general two-dimensional case discussed here.
