Summary
A four-part assessment using analytical methods and semi-analytical simulation tools finds that
the maximum temperature variation in the highly enriched uranium (HEU) Jemima plate
assembly is less than ten Kelvin (< 10 K) in an abrupt loss of vacuum scenario. The temperature
variation in the metals is likely to be one to four Kelvin (1 K – 4 K) based on the most realistic
conditions in the assessment. The four assessment parts include: (1) the peak gas temperature
bound for the initial influx of the ambient air; (2) the high-heat transfer limit during the high-
speed forced convection period in an insulated HEU plate; (3) the high-heat transfer limit during
the high-speed forced convection period with aluminum spacers; and (4) the longer-time thermal
equilibration period between the air and metal stack. The four-part assessment serves as
conservative upper bound on the temperature variation in the metal stack and remains well below
the 40 Kelvin limit where the temperature-induced stresses could lead to permanent, plastic
deformation.

1. Introduction
The thermal impact of a sudden loss of vacuum on the metal plate assembly in the chamber of
the Thermal/Epithermal eXperiment (TEX) is the focus of this report. Specifically, there is
concern around the elevated temperatures produced by the inrush of ambient air and if it could
create temperature gradients in the HEU “Jemima” plates that cause thermal stress damage. To
estimate the impact of a sudden loss of vacuum, calculations are performed that bound the worst-
case with respect to the possible temperature variations in the metal plate assembly induced by
the sudden influx of ambient air.
Two loss-of-vacuum scenarios were proposed by the TEX experiment team, one being the lifting
of the bell jar off its seal and the other being a failure of the line to the vacuum pump. Under
vacuum, the downforce on the bell jar is estimated to be 2775 lbf. A slide force applied at the top
of the bell jar of 751 lbf would be required to begin to lift the jar from its seat. The other

2. Approach
The assembly stack, including the HEU plates, is initially assumed to be at 233 Kelvin (-40 deg
C). The vacuum line enters the chamber though an LF63 flange. This flange has a minimum
cross sectional opening diameter of 2.37 in. For this analysis it is assumed that the vacuum line is
instantaneously removed, and the full opening is exposed to ambient pressure and temperature
(i.e., a “guillotine cut” to the vacuum line). Ambient air (assumed to be 1 atm, 298 Kelvin) then
rushes into a vacuum and increases in temperature due to the flow work driven by the pressure
differential.
To assess the thermal impacts on the TEX plate assembly, four analyses are used as outlined in
the following subsections. In Section 2.1, a constant volume energy analysis determines an
upper bound on the gas temperature near the plates during the initial inflow period. Section 2.2
has a 1D-axisymmetric model of a single plate between two insulators with the maximum heat
transfer coefficient. This produces the largest temperature variation in the metal plates, and
serves as the worst-case considered in this report. Section 2.3 uses a 2D-axisymmetric model of a
single HEU plate in-between two aluminum plates exposed to the peak temperature from Section
2.1 and the maximum heat transfer coefficient during the high-speed inflow period. This
establishes an upper bound on the magnitude of the temperature variation in the metal plates with
the larger size aluminum spacers. Finally, in Section 2.4, the 2D-axisymetric model contains the
same plate setup as Section 2.3 but now exposed to a region of stationary air, which starts at the

The largest temperature variation in the metal occurs when the heat transfer into the plate is the
highest from the inflow gases. A conservative upper-bound on the variation can thus be obtained
by finding an upper-bound on the heat transfer coefficient h at the outer boundary condition.
The heat transfer coefficient is computed from the Nusselt number for internal flows Nu =
h/(k*Dh), where Dh is the hydraulic diameter, which for the annular flow is the difference
between the outer and inner diameter. There are number of heat transfer correlations for forced
convection that relate the Nusselt number Nu to the Reynolds (Re) and Prandtl (Pr) numbers.
The Prandtl number for air over the range of air temperature from ambient to T2 is nearly
constant at Pr = 0.7.
As the Reynolds number increases, the three Nusselt number correlation considered here have an
increase in the Nusselt number proportional to Re raised to a power between 0.8 and 1.0. Thus,
an upper bound on the Reynolds number flowing in the annulus will produce an upper bound on
the heat flux into the metal plate and an upper bound on the temperature variation. For a fixed
gas and geometry, the maximum Reynolds number occurs at the highest gas velocity or highest
mass flow through the annulus around the metal plates. This peak mass flow occurs when the
flow is choked at the smallest cross-sectional area, which here is taken to be the LF63 flange (df

Section 2.3 Axisymmetric model (2D) of a single HEU plate with the maximum forced conduction
A 2D axisymmetric Comsol3 simulation is used to investigate the unsteady temperature response
of the stack assuming that the air suddenly filling the vacuum flows at a maximum velocity
limited by the choked flow area restriction and maintains a constant peak temperature T2 based
on the analysis in Sections 2.1 and 3.1. The maximum velocity and gas temperature
approximations produce the maximum rate of heat transfer into the stack, which in turn yields a
conservative upper-bound on the temperature variations in the metal disks.

It is important to note that the gas temperature will be less than T2 due to the heat loss to the non-
stack elements of the vacuum assembly. The actual flow velocity around the annulus of the stack
will be significantly slower than the choked flow approximation due to the circuitous path it
must follow from the vacuum line break and the resulting viscous and pressure losses incurred.
The final conservative assumption in this model is that the period of maximum forced convection
is sustained for a sufficiently long time to impact the metal temperature.
Based on an estimate that 40% of the volume within the vacuum chamber is available to be filled
with the ambient air, the total air mass the enters after the line failure is only 30 grams. At the
choked mass flow rate of 0.61 kg/s computed in the previous section, the high-speed, forced
convection period will only last for 50 ms. Even including the slower mass flow rates as the
chamber approaches ambient pressure, the total filling time after the vacuum line breaks is still
less than 100 ms. To ensure a conservative upper-bound, the forced convection period is
assumed to last one second, which is ten to twenty times longer than the mass balance would
allow.

Section 3.4 Results for the axisymmetric model of a single HEU plate with stationary air
The metal temperature is computed as a function of time in the 2D axisymmetric stack and air
gap described in Section 2.4 using Comsol. The initial metal temperature is 233 Kelvin and the
initial air temperature is 417 Kelvin, the maximum gas temperature found in Section 3.1. Figure
8 shows the predicted temperature contours in the thinner HEU plate sandwiched between two
aluminum plates after one, two and three seconds. Between one and three seconds, the air
temperature rapidly drops from more than 20 K above the initial metal temperature to less than
10 K above the initial metal temperature. By comparison, the peak temperature variation in the
metal never increases much beyond 1 Kelvin during the first three seconds. The rapid
equilibration of the air to the metal temperature is due the orders of magnitude difference in
thermal mass or heat capacity between materials. Given the timescales of the sudden inflow of
ambient air and the subsequent cooling of the stationary air, the simulation shown in Figure 8 is
thought to be most representative of the likely temperature response. Thus, the likely
temperature variation of the HEU plate is estimated to be 1 Kelvin to 4 Kelvin by combining the
temperature raise in this Comsol simulation with the 3 Kelvin increase shown in the 1D
simulation in Figure 5 after 100 ms.

4. Conclusion
A four-part assessment with analytic methods and semi-analytical simulation tools was
performed to determine the maximum temperature variation in the highly enriched uranium
(HEU) Jemima plate assembly. Under the most conservative scenario, high-speed forced
convection, lasting 10-20 times longer than the mass balance can sustain, and insulating plates on
either side, the maximum variation in the HEU was found to be 8 Kelvin. This was well below
the 40 Kelvin limit identified in [4] to avoid plastic strain. Results for the four assessments were
presented in Sections (3.1) for the peak gas temperature bound for the initial influx of the
ambient air; (3.2) for the high-heat transfer limit during the high-speed forced convection period
in an insulated HEU plate; (3.3) for the high-heat transfer limit during the high-speed forced
convection period with aluminum spacers; and (3.4) the longer-time limit thermal equilibration
between the air and metal stack. Combining the initial temperature variation in Section 3.2 for
the insulated plate with the gas-metal equilibration simulations in Section 3.4, a peak

temperature variation of 1 Kelvin to 4 Kelvin is obtained, which was believed to be the most
representative of the expected temperature behavior in a vacuum line failure.
