PUBLIC PORTION OF REPORT

Marine cloud systems cover a substantial portion of the world’s oceans. Most of these clouds form
relatively close to the ocean surface, typically within one to two kilometers, a region referred to
by meteorologists as the marine boundary layer. They are composed predominantly of liquid
water, although ice particles can occur in mid- and high-latitude marine clouds during winter. In
satellite imagery, these clouds appear bright against the darker ocean surface below, reflecting a
large fraction of incoming sunlight back into space that would otherwise warm the ocean. Because
marine boundary layer clouds cover such an extensive area of the ocean, they exert a significant
influence on Earth’s overall transfer of solar energy absorbed by the surface and thermal energy
emitted to space, a balance known as the planetary radiation budget.

Marine boundary layer clouds are typically thin, and their formation and dissipation depend on a
delicate balance between processes acting at the ocean surface below and the warm, dry air above.
They are notoriously difficult to simulate accurately in weather forecast models, which often
produce too few marine low clouds in midlatitudes and clouds in tropical regions that are
excessively bright, meaning they reflect too much solar radiation.

The marine boundary layer is frequently characterized by widespread overcast cloud cover that
often transitions from a continuous, single-layer deck to more broken cloud fields toward the
tropics. These transitions typically proceed through an intermediate stage in which shallow, broken
clouds form beneath the overlying stratiform cloud deck. Once broken clouds develop below the
overcast, they frequently self-organize into cloud clusters known as marine boundary layer
convective complexes (MBLCCs), although the mechanisms governing the formation and
organization of MBLCCs remain poorly understood. Accurately representing these transitions in
long-range weather forecast models is essential because they influence the properties of air masses
advected over the continental United States and Europe, and they become increasingly important
for forecasts on seasonal and longer timescales.

We employed two complementary approaches to investigate the processes controlling MBLCCs
and their impact on marine cloud cover. Long-term observations from the U.S. Department of
Energy’s Eastern North Atlantic (ENA) Observatory provided a unique dataset that allowed us to
characterize fundamental properties of MBLCCs, including their typical size and frequency of
occurrence. These observations were combined with high-resolution numerical simulations
performed on supercomputers to examine the evolution of MBLCCs during cold-air outbreaks
over the ENA region.

Observations indicate that MBLCCs span horizontal scales from a few kilometers to several tens
of kilometers, with smaller complexes occurring more frequently. As MBLCCs grow, both wind
speeds and precipitation intensity increase, and downdrafts driven by the evaporation of falling
precipitation are increasingly likely to reach the ocean surface. Consistent with these findings,
simulations show that MBLCC size responds sensitively to ambient wind speed and to the vertical
moisture structure of the marine boundary layer. Based on these results, we developed a simple

relationship linking these environmental factors to changes in MBLCC size, which may enable
improved representation of MBLCCs in future weather and climate models.

DOE ONLY PORTION OF REPORT

Major Goals and Objectives

The science questions that we set for our study were:

 1) What is the multidimensional composite structure of MBLCC’s at the ENA Site?
 2) What processes influence the mesoscale organization modes MBLCC?
 3) How does the mass flux vary across different MBLCC organizational modes?

What was accomplished:

Goal 1: multidimensional composite structure of MBLCC’s

We began our study by seeking information about the different modes of cloud morphology that
were observed during a four-year summertime period at the ENA observatory. Stratocumulus
transitions frequently occur in cool air outbreaks and data revealed that cumulus-coupled
stratocumulus, which contains a subset that consists of MBLCC, was present approximately 33%
of the time at ENA. This morphology is consistent with the cloud transition location of the ENA
observatory, where single layer stratocumulus and cumulus are observed as frequently as cumulus-
coupled stratocumulus, which is a hybrid cloud formation that combines both cumulus and
stratocumulus. The abstract below is from our paper, which was published in the Journal of
Climate. A particularly impactful result is highlighted in bold in the abstract.

Zheng, Q., and M. A. Miller, 2022: Summertime Marine Boundary Layer Cloud, Thermodynamic,
and Drizzle Morphology over the Eastern North Atlantic: A Four-Year Study. J. Climate, 35, 4805–
4825, https://doi.org/10.1175/JCLI-D-21-0568.1.

Abstract
Summertime remote sensor and in situ data from 2016 to 2019 collected at the ARM Eastern North
Atlantic (ENA) Observatory are combined with aircraft measurements from the Aerosol and Cloud
Experiments in the Eastern North Atlantic (ACE-ENA) campaign to quantify marine boundary
layer (MBL) cloud, thermodynamic, and drizzle morphology in the region. A radar reflectivity–
rainfall rate relationship (Z–R) is developed from aircraft data and 6-h cloud morphological
regimes are identified from ENA data using a k-means algorithm driven by three independent
inputs quantifying cloud thickness, drizzle intensity, and cloud field geometric complexity. Four
separate MBL structural regimes representing non- or weakly drizzling single-layer stratocumulus,
drizzling stratocumulus and cumulus-coupled stratocumulus, deep convection, and broken clouds
embedded in northerly flow are identified. Single-layer stratocumulus is indicated when weak
subtropical anticyclones are significantly west of the ENA site, and the MBL is cooler and drier
than when drizzling and cumulus-coupled stratocumulus and broken clouds are observed.
Drizzling and cumulus-coupled stratocumulus clouds are observed on the eastern flank of strong
subtropical anticyclones in deep warm moist air masses with wind speeds exceeding 7 m s−1 and
strong near-surface wind shear. Broken clouds exhibit strong wind shear near the inversion, while
single-layer stratocumulus clouds have lower wind speeds and minimal shear. Net latent heat
fluxes in the subcloud layer resulting from a combination of the ocean surface heat flux and
evaporating drizzle average near zero over long periods in drizzling and cumulus-coupled
stratocumulus. The ECMWF reanalysis version 5 (ERA5) is found to accurately represent
single-layer stratocumulus properties, while producing significant discrepancies when
drizzling stratocumulus and cumulus-coupled stratocumulus are observed.

Goal 2: processes that influence the mesoscale organization modes MBLCC

We expanded our analysis of this four-year period to isolate occurrences of summertime MBLCC
and define, for the first time, the mesoscale size distribution. We showed that the most frequent
size is 5-20 km, and that most structures are smaller than 50-km, although some larger structures
were occasionally observed. Analyzing the microphysical and meteorological conditions in which
each scale was observed, we determined that increases in drizzle and wind speed and shear
accompanied increases in scale. Conservative variable analysis indicated that in at the smallest
MBLCC scales drizzle evaporates in the subcloud layer without producing enough negative
buoyancy to cause a downdraft from cloud base to the surface, but as the scale increases,
downdrafts are increasingly present at the surface. An interesting finding of our study was that the
subcloud relative humidity was scale invariant—constant at all scales! This finding was used in
the third part of our study to understand the relationship between scale size tendency and MBL
structure. We have prepared a manuscript describing this phase of our study, which is part I of II.
The abstract and title of Part 1 are below.

 Marine Boundary Layer Convective Complexes over the Summertime Eastern North
 Atlantic - Part I: Classifications, Observed Characteristics, and Surface Fluxes

 Qiuxuan Zheng, Mark A. Miller, Stephanie Braddock
 1
 Department of Environmental Sciences, Rutgers University

Abstract

Marine boundary layer convective complexes (MBLCCs) represent a transitional cloud regime
between stratocumulus and cumulus, yet their mesoscale structure and organization mechanisms
remain poorly constrained. Using four summers (2016–2019) of remote sensing and in situ
observations from the ARM Eastern North Atlantic (ENA) Observatory, we identify 639 MBLCCs
and classify them by along-wind horizontal scales. Composite analyses reveal systematic scale
dependences upon drizzle, cloud morphology and dynamics. Small MBLCCs (<20 km) exhibit
shallow mixing depths (<200 m), weak surface coupling, and drizzle that evaporates aloft.
Intermediate clusters (20–50 km) are well-mixed to the lifting condensation level, with drizzle-
driven downdrafts connecting the cloud and sub-cloud layers. Larger MBLCCs (50–100 km)
display layered moisture structures, enhanced wind shear, and differential advection indicative of
mesoscale organization, while the largest (>100 km) coincide with frontal environments and deep,
shear-driven coupling from the inversion to the surface. Two independent methods to estimate
mixing depth in the MBL—a profile-matching and a gradient approach—yield consistent mixing
depth estimates that increase with cluster size. These results establish a scale-dependent
observational framework for MBLCCs over the ENA and demonstrate that vertical coupling
increases systematically with horizontal extent, linking mesoscale organizations to drizzle
evaporation and wind shear.

Goals 2 (cont.) and 3: processes that influence the mesoscale organization modes
MBLCC and mass fluxes

To further investigate the processes that influence the mesoscale organization of MBLCC, we
analyzed MBLCCs that were produce in simulations from Kazemirad and Miller (2020). In this
part of the study, we followed the evolution of a single MBLCC for a period of seven hours and
investigated its structure and the processes that shaped it. Several interesting characteristics were
noted. Contrary to the hypothesis in our proposal, cloud top entrainment instability was not present
in the simulations. Mixing at cloud top fueled by overshooting cumulus turrets was extremely
vigorous in the simulated structure and suggested that this process may be integrally important in
the observed mesoscale organization. Multivariable regression suggests that cloud fraction at
cloud base is primarily controlled by the windspeed, while the cloud fraction at cloud top is
primarily controlled by the vertical profile of moisture. Results of this analysis are shown in Table
1.

Based upon the combined results from the analysis of observations and simulations (Goals 1 and
2), we developed an analytical model of the convective updraft mass flux in MBLCC. This model
isolated the thermodynamic processes and used an assumed cylindrical shape to investigate the
mechanisms associated with the tendency of cloud fraction at the base of MBLCC. Results from
the analytical model are shown in Figure 1 and the abstract and title of Part II are below.

 Marine Boundary Layer Convective Complexes over the Summertime Eastern North
 Atlantic - Part II: Simulated Life Cycle

 Mark A. Miller1, Qiuxuan Zheng1, Stephanie Braddock1, Christine Chiu2,
 Peter van Leeuwen2, Tzu-Han Tsu2
 1
 Department of Environmental Sciences, Rutgers University,
 2
 Department of Atmospheric Science, Colorado State University
Abstract

Marine Boundary Layer Cloud Complexes (MBLCCs) represent an important but understudied
phase in the stratocumulus-to-cumulus transition, particularly over the Eastern North Atlantic
(ENA), where cloud coverage trends remain uncertain in a warming climate. In this study, the
second of a two-part series, we analyze the life cycle of a single MBLCC using high-resolution
simulations, employing the WRF model with the MYNN 3.0 planetary boundary layer scheme.
The simulated MBLCC closely resembled structures observed by ground-based radar at the ARM
ENA site, featuring a multi-cellular convective core feeding a stratocumulus anvil and persisting
for over six hours within a decoupled boundary layer. Thermodynamic and dynamic analyses
revealed a pulsing growth–decay cycle, influenced by latent heat flux, wind speed, and mesoscale
entrainment dynamics. A prominent feature of the simulated MBLCC was strong moist thermal
updrafts near cloud top, suggesting enhanced cloud-top entrainment. Multivariable linear
regression suggested that cloud fraction at cloud base is driven by the windspeed, while the cloud
fraction at cloud top is driven by the vertical gradient of mixing ratio in the MBL. A simple updraft
mass-flux model based on boundary-layer thermodynamics captured the MBLCC’s growth phases
but not its contraction, suggesting a secondary role for wind and entrainment dynamics during
decay phases. These findings support the notion that MBLCCs serve as one mode of transitional
structure in stratocumulus breakup and reinforce the need for improved representation of
decoupling and mesoscale organization in global models. In a changing climate, decreases in the
upstream air–sea temperature gradient and wind speed—two key controls on latent heat flux—
could drive reductions in cloud cover over the ENA via suppression of MBLCC formation.

These manuscripts (Part 1 and Part 2) will be submitted before the end of January 2026.
