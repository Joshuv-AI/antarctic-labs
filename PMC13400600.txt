Resistance to the oat leaf blotch pathogen (Pyrenophora avenae) is delimited to a region of chromosome 1D that harbors a candidate resistance kinase fusion protein
Authors: Aaron D. Beattie, Peter E. Eckstein, Alex Woitas, Tajinder S. Grewal, Lindsay J. Griffith, Xiang M. Zhang, Nadia Kamal, Namratha Pavuluri, Curt McCartney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key messageAn oat leaf blotch resistance locus effective against different Pyrenophora avenae isolates was identified on chromosome 1D within a narrowly defined region that harbored a kinase fusion protein-encoding candidate resistance gene. AbstractOat leaf blotch, incited by Pyrenophora avenae, can be found globally in all oat-growing regions and has been reported to cause significant yield loss. However, no information exists pertaining to the inheritance, genomic location or class of resistance genes in oat effective against this pathogen. As such, there is no host–pathogen model to describe the pathosystem. Genetic mapping of leaf blotch resistance in two oat populations using three P. avenae isolates identified a common resistance locus situated within a 995-Kb interval on chromosome 1D in the OT3098 genome. Two candidate resistance genes with well-defined roles in plant defense were identified which encoded a serine/threonine-protein kinase and a cysteine-rich receptor-like kinase fusion protein (Cys RLK). Identical and apparently functional proteins encoded by each candidate gene were annotated in susceptible genotypes, while truncated proteins or the absence of a protein was annotated among resistant genotypes. These observations suggest that the candidate genes act as susceptibility genes, consistent with an inverse gene-for-gene system. If the Cys RLK is ultimately proven to be the susceptibility gene, this would provide another example of a kinase fusion protein functioning in plant immunity. It is proposed that this leaf blotch resistance locus on chromosome 1D can be referred to as Spa1OT3011, to recognize that it is the first described susceptibility gene relevant to P. avenae which was identified in the OT3011 genotype.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00122-026-05313-7.

Introduction

Oat (Avena sativa L.) is the 6th most grown field crop globally, with 23.2 million tonnes of annual production (United States Department of Agriculture 2025) and an annual export trade value of $916 million USD over the past decade (World Bank 2025). In Canada, oat is the 4th largest crop by area, with an average of 1.3 million hectares seeded over the past five years (Statistics Canada 2025). It is estimated that oat contributes over $4.2 billion CAD annually to the Canadian GDP through farm-gate sales, exports, and employment creation (LMC International 2023).

The significant annual production of oat is due in part to it being considered a healthy cereal. For example, oat contains 3–7% beta-glucan, a soluble fiber that has been shown to lower plasma cholesterol and reduce the risk of heart disease (Queenan et al. 2007; Liatis et al. 2009). This functionality has resulted in health claims being established in Canada (Health Canada 2010), the USA (U.S. Federal Food and Drug Administration 1997) and the European Union (European Food Safety Authority 2010). Oat grain also contains 12–20% protein which is predominantly comprised of globulins (70–80%) (Nieto-Nieto et al. 2015) and provides a better balance of essential amino acids, including lysine and threonine, than other cereals (Liu et al. 2009; Klose and Arendt 2012). Oat protein can also be consumed by most people suffering from celiac disease and is thus considered to be gluten-free (Peraaho et al. 2004). As a result of these desirable attributes and the non-GMO nature of the crop, oat is increasingly being explored as an alternative protein for diverse food and beverage applications (Mel and Malalgoda 2022).

Oat production can be limited by diseases, with crown rust (caused by Puccinia coronata f. sp. avenae Pers. Syd. & Syd.) and stem rust (caused by Puccinia graminis Pers. f. sp. avenae Eriks. & E. Henn.) being the two most significant diseases of global importance (Nazareno et al. 2018; Berlin et al. 2013; Gold Steinberg et al. 2005; Li et al. 2015). Yield losses associated with both diseases are typically below 10% (McCallum et al. 2007; Chong et al. 2008), but losses up to 50% due to crown rust have been reported in the USA and South America (Martinelli et al. 1994; Nazareno et al. 2018), while losses from stem rust have reached up to 25% in the USA (Martens 1985), 35% in Canada (Mitchell Fetch and Fetch 2011) and 50–90% in Australia (Dadu and McLean 2022).

Beyond these two important diseases, a prevalent but poorly characterized disease incited by Pyrenophora avenae S. Ito & Kurib (syn: P. chaetomioides Speg.), known as leaf blotch, is found throughout oat-growing regions globally (MyCoPortal 2025). Oat disease surveys conducted from 2011 to 2017 in western Canada indicated that P. avenae was consistently identified in 33–91% of oats fields (Tekauz et al. 2012; Taylor et al. 2014, 2015; Grewal et al. 2016; Woitas et al. 2017, 2018). Šebesta et al. (2001) also identified P. avenae at moderate to high levels in the European Union, Scandinavia and Russia from 1994 to 1998. Primary infection of the coleoptile and seedling leaves by the pathogen arise either from infected seed, in which thick-walled mycelia have survived from the prior growing season, or via conidia produced from crop residue. Cool and wet conditions are favorable to the initial stages of disease development (Gough and McDaniel 1974; Sivanesan 1987). Conidia produced from crop residues or from primary infections may also infect the culm (Luke et al. 1957), upper canopy leaves and panicle later in the growing season (Harder and Haber 1992). Although infection of seedlings may cause plant death, infection of the upper canopy leaves is considered the more important impact of the disease in Canada.

Pyrenophora avenae has the potential to cause significant damage, with up to 100% defoliation of susceptible varieties reported in Texas (Gough and McDaniel 1974). Typically, yield losses are below 10% but have been reported between 10 and 15% in Finland (Rekola et al. 1970) and Germany (Kiewnick 1974, cited by Harder and Haber 1992). Given the susceptibility of some western Canadian oat varieties to P. avenae (A. Beattie, per. obs.), concern exists as to the potential harm the pathogen may pose to yield and grain quality. Although fungicidal seed treatments (Harder and Haber 1992) and fungicide application to the flag leaf (May et al. 2014) can effectively control oat leaf blotch, disease management that involves crop rotation (Harder and Haber 1992), later seeding (May et al. 2014) and growing resistant cultivars is a more economical method of control that is appropriate for both conventional and organic production. This is an important consideration within Canada as oat represents the second largest organically grown crop after wheat (Telford 2022).

One of the first studies to assess resistance to P. avenae evaluated the reaction of 173 oat genotypes, including accessions of A. algeriensis, A. barbata, A. brevis, A. byzantine, A. fatua and A. nuda, against two P. avenae isolates in both greenhouse and field nurseries (Earhart and Shands 1952). Differential reactions were identified across the genotypes, with breeding lines such as BI-47-67 and X279-1 displaying very good resistance and cultivars such as Kareela and Victoria being very susceptible. Subsequent studies continued to report differential reaction to P. avenae among different sets of germplasm, with Clintafe, Bontram (Müller 1963, cited by Šebesta et al. 2000), Iowa 2052, Aigoruda (Kunowski and Breshkov 1981, cited by Harder and Haber 1992), Kasadra and the Pc60 and Pc61 differential lines displaying the best resistance (Šebesta et al. 2001).

Beyond the few reports indicating the occurrence of P. avenae resistance in oat, no study has been conducted to understand the genetic inheritance of resistance or to genetically map resistance within the oat genome. However, the genus Pyrenophora contains other important pathogens of cereal crops, such as P. tritici-repentis and P. teres Drechsler, cause of wheat tan spot and barley net blotch, respectively, which may provide insights into the nature of resistance to P. avenae in oat. In hexaploid wheat (2n = 6x = AABBDD), three dominant susceptibility genes, Tsn1, Tsc2 and Tsc1, confer sensitivity to three corresponding host-specific toxins, Ptr ToxA, Ptr ToxB and Ptr ToxC, produced by the tan spot pathogen (Faris et al. 1996, 1997; Effertz et al. 2001; Friesen and Faris 2004). Four other recessive genes, tsc2 (Singh et al. 2006), tsr3 (Tadesse et al. 2006a), tsr4 (Tadesse et al. 2006b) and tsr5 (Singh et al. 2008), have been identified and are hypothesized to act as susceptibility genes that interact with currently unidentified pathogen effectors. Additionally, non-race-specific QTL, such as the chromosome 3BL QTL, identified in BR34 (Faris and Friesen 2005) and Penawawa (Kariyawasam et al. 2016), and race-specific QTL have been described (reviewed in Faris et al. 2013). Thus, the wheat tan spot pathosystem follows both an inverse gene-for-gene (or toxin) model of resistance, as well as race non-specific resistance.

In barley, eight major resistance genes (Rpt1 to Rpt8) have been described that confer resistance to P. teres but in a non-race-specific manner (reviewed in Clare et al. 2020). The Rpt3 to Rpt7 genes display resistance against both forms of the pathogen, while Rpt1 and Rpt2 are effective only against the net form and Rpt8 is effective only against the spot form. Like wheat, numerous QTL have been described that govern resistance to net blotch (summarized in Tamang et al. 2019; Novakazi et al. 2019). The eight major resistance genes act in a dominant manner, each of which may interact in a classic gene-for-gene model with effector loci that have been mapped within P. teres (Beattie et al. 2007; Lai et al. 2007; Shjerve et al. 2014). However, evidence of inverse gene-for-gene interaction also exists in this pathosystem. Richards et al. (2016) fine mapped a dominant susceptibility locus, Spt1, on chromosome 6H which conferred susceptibility to different isolates of net-form net blotch as either two tightly linked genes or as alleles of a single gene. Further, the PttNE1 proteinaceous effector produced by P. teres was demonstrated to interact with a dominant susceptibility locus located in the same region of chromosome 6H as Spt1 to induce disease symptoms (Liu et al. 2015). More recently a second susceptibility gene, Spt2, was fine mapped to chromosome 5H which confers susceptibility, but only when the gene is present in a heterozygous state (Clare et al. 2024). It therefore appears that like wheat, this pathosystem is complex with aspects of both the inverse gene-for-gene model and dominant race non-specific resistance, depending on the isolate–host combination (Richards et al. 2016).

Understanding the genetic inheritance and location of resistance genes within the oat genome that are effective against P. avenae will help breeders to incorporate resistance into future oat cultivars, thus providing increased value to farmers and oat millers through reduced yield losses and maintenance of good grain quality. In addition, mapping resistance to P. avenae will provide a foundation for functional characterization of resistance genes to better understand the oat-P. avenae pathosystem. To this end, the objectives of this study were to: 1) provide pathogen culture conditions, inoculation methods and a rating scale to evaluate oat infection response to P. avenae, 2) identify oat genotypes resistant to P. avenae and determine the genetic inheritance of resistance, 3) genetically map resistance to P. avenae and identify genetic markers associated with resistance and 4) identify potential candidate genes for future functional validation efforts.

Materials and methods

Pyrenophora avenae isolates and inoculation method to evaluate infection response

Eighteen Pyrenophora avenae isolates were collected from commercial oat fields in Saskatchewan during annual leaf blotch disease surveys conducted from 2014 to 2017. Four isolates (PA101, PA102, PA114 and PA115; Supplementary Table S1) were selected for use in this study based on preliminary experiments that determined they could be cultured on artificial medium, they produced sufficient conidia to allow efficient inoculum production, and they were collected from different locations and years. Single-spore cultures for each of the four isolates were established by placing single conidium on V8A agar (20% v/v V8 juice, 3 g/L CaCO3, 17 g/L agar) Petri plates and incubating at 21 °C with a 12-h photoperiod for 10–14 days. Cultures were then cut into 8 × 8 mm pieces, aseptically transferred to fresh V8A plates and incubated as above to obtain sufficient conidia for inoculations. Conidia were harvested from 10- to 14-day-old cultures by adding 10 mL of sterile distilled water to each Petri plate, scraping the culture with a glass rod and filtering through two layers of cheesecloth. Conidial concentrations were determined by hemocytometer and adjusted accordingly. The PA101 isolate was used only for preliminary experiments to determine the parameters that produced a large range in infection responses across oat genotypes. The parameters assessed were age of inoculated plants, inoculum concentration, days of humidity following inoculation and number of days after inoculation before rating. The eventual set of parameters used to assess P. avenae infection response was determined as inoculation of 14-day-old seedlings (three-leaf stage) with 3 × 104 conidia/mL, placing inoculated seedlings in darkness at 100% humidity for 24 h and rating infection response 7 days later.

Oat seeds were surface sterilized in 0.12% formalin for 1 h, rinsed with distilled water for 10 min and dried overnight. Eight seeds were sown as a cluster, and three clusters were seeded per 1 L pot containing Sunshine Mix#3 (Sun Gro Horticulture Canada Ltd., Seba Beach, AB). Plants were grown under controlled environmental conditions in growth chambers at the University of Saskatchewan. Growth conditions were set to 16 h days at 21 °C and 8 h nights at 18 °C, 75% relative humidity and a light intensity of 575 μmol/m2/s until inoculation. Inoculum was applied to 14-day-old seedlings using a Badger Basic Spray Gun Set Model 250™ (Badger Air-Brush Company, IL, USA) mounted on a D500 Air Compressor (Paasche Airbrush Company, IL, USA). Sufficient inoculum was applied until the plants were uniformly wet (~ 5 mL per pot). Inoculated plants were maintained at a constant 21 °C in darkness at 100% humidity for 24 h following inoculation before being returned to the prior growing conditions which were maintained until infection response was rated.

Infection response was rated using a modification of the 1–9 scale developed by Tekauz (1985) for P. teres f. maculata (spot-form net blotch). Ratings were determined based on the lesion type present on the top two leaves. Infection responses classified as resistant (1–2) or moderately resistant (3–4) displayed lesions that remained restricted in size and were primarily necrotic with a limited amount of chlorosis within the necrotic lesion. The intermediate category (5) showed more chlorosis that extended beyond the necrotic lesions. Infection responses that were moderately susceptible (6–7) or susceptible (8–9) displayed numerous and larger necrotic lesions that were accompanied by more extensive chlorosis that could continue to enlarge over time to encompass the entire leaf (Fig. 1). Oat materials

A diversity panel comprising 25 oat genotypes (Table 1) was screened with three isolates (PA102, PA114 and PA115) to assess infection response variability against the isolates and to identify parents of existing bi-parental populations that differed in reaction to P. avenae which could be used for QTL mapping of leaf blotch resistance.

Based on the differential infection responses of the 25 genotypes, two bi-parental mapping populations were identified in which the parents showed consistent infection responses to the three isolates. The OT3011 × Iowa 2052 population consisted of 150 F4:5 recombinant inbred lines (RILs). The leaf blotch-resistant parent OT3011 (OT293 × CDC Dancer) was bred at the Crop Development Centre (University of Saskatchewan, Saskatoon, SK, Canada), while the susceptible parent Iowa 2052 (unknown pedigree) was bred at Iowa State University (Ames, IA, USA). The AC Assiniboia/S42 × CDC Dancer population was composed of 147 F4:5 RILs with the resistant parent AC Assiniboia/S42 bred at Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada (AAFC; Winnipeg, MB, Canada). AC Assiniboia/S42 is a line derived from the cross AC Assiniboia (Brown et al. 2001) × S42 (Aung et al. 1996). The susceptible parent CDC Dancer (OT344 × W90279) was bred at the Crop Development Centre.

Seven genotypes with assembled genomes (Avni et al. 2025) comprised the pangenome panel. These genotypes were also screened with the same three P. avenae isolates and utilized to help identify leaf blotch resistance gene candidates and assess allelic variation between resistant and susceptible genotypes (Table 1). These data were generated under the oat pangenome project and were generously provided by the international PanOat consortium.

Statistical analyses

The effect of genotype and block on leaf blotch infection response rating was analyzed as a RCBD using a linear mixed effect model in Minitab (v.21.2; Minitab LLC) for data obtained on each RIL population. Genotype was treated as a fixed effect, while block was treated as a random effect. The assumptions of homogeneous variances and normal distribution of residuals were evaluated using plots of predicted values versus residuals and distribution of residuals, respectively.

To evaluate the genetic inheritance of resistance, Chi-square analyses were performed on each bi-parental RIL population with each isolate to determine the goodness of fit against the expected segregation ratio for a single gene (1:1 ratio of susceptible:resistant) using a significance level of 0.05.

DNA extraction, genotyping and QTL analysis

Genomic DNA for oat iSelect 6 K BeadChip array (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA) genotyping was extracted from five-day-old coleoptiles collected from 4–5 seeds germinated on moistened cotton balls using a modified CTAB DNA extraction method (Procunier et al. 1990; modified by excluding the phenol washes). DNA concentrations were adjusted to 50 ng/μL for genotyping. DNA for TaqMan assays was obtained directly from seed using a sodium hydroxide method (Klimyuk et al. 1993) with 1 μL aliquots of the supernatant used for the assay, rather than the treated leaf tissue as described in the method. Six individual seeds per line were tested.

All lines and parents from the OT3011 × Iowa 2052 and AC Assiniboia/S42 × CDC Dancer RIL populations were genotyped with the oat iSelect 6 K BeadChip array at Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada (Morden, MB). Genotyping data were visually screened using GenomeStudio v.2.0.5 (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA) to ensure accuracy of the automated calls. Data were filtered to remove markers that were monomorphic, failed to produce an allele on the parental lines, had > 15% failed or null data points or showed highly skewed segregation (P < 0.01 when tested against a 1:1 ratio using Chi-square analyses).

Linkage groups for each population were generated in JoinMap 4.0 (Kyazma B.V., Wageningen, Netherlands). Initial groups were created using independence LOD scores that sequentially stepped from 2.0 to 10.0. Marker groups within the generated groupings tree were visually assessed, and the initial linkage groups were ordered using the maximum likelihood (ML) mapping algorithm (Jansen et al. 2001) with three map optimization rounds. The physical location of markers within each linkage group was compared against the OT3098 v2 genome assembly, and the linkage groups were combined or split at a higher LOD threshold accordingly. Marker order underlying the subsequently identified chromosome 1D QTL was confirmed using the default settings of the regression mapping algorithm described by Stam (1993) and the Kosambi mapping function (Kosambi 1944). Final linkage groups were visualized using JoinMap 4.0. QTL mapping was conducted in MapQTL 5.0 (Kyazma B.V., Wageningen, Netherlands) using default settings, except the mapping step size was increased to 25 cM. A permutation test based on 10,000 iterations was run to determine the LOD significance threshold for each isolate and population. The genome-wide relative cumulative count threshold at 0.95 or higher was set as the threshold as suggested by van Ooijen (1999). Interval mapping was conducted to identify putative QTL, followed by multiple-QTL model mapping in which peak markers underlying the putative QTL were set as co-factors. Loci which subsequently fell below the significance threshold were considered false positives, removed as co-factors and the analysis repeated. Co-factors were identified using the co-factors tool and confirmed in the same manner.

Physical positions of QTL markers were determined based on the OT3098 v2 assembly found in Graingenes (https://wheat.pw.usda.gov) (Yao et al. 2022).

Genome location and gene content of the leaf blotch resistance locus in oat, barley and wheat

Sequences for oat iSelect 6 K BeadChip array markers underlying the chromosome 1D leaf blotch resistance QTL were queried against the OT3098 v2 assembly found in Graingenes via BLAST (Altschul et al. 1990) to determine their physical positions and order. The boundaries of the QTL, defined by markers GMI_ES14_c2496_651 and GMI_ES22_c18205_366, were used to identify all high confidence genes present within the region for OT3098 (in both the v2 assembly and the re-annotated v1 assembly). Genes in the pangenome genotypes, Leggett, OT380, Victoria, Aslak, Sang and HiFi, were then identified from the orthologous framework generated by Avni et al. (2025). High confidence genes within the region implicated as plant disease resistance genes (i.e., candidate genes) were targeted for further analysis. Alignment of chromosome 1D candidate gene alleles was done in DNAMAN v.7 (Lynnon Biosoft, San Ramon, CA, USA) to characterize amino acid variation across the genotypes.

Sequences for the QTL flanking markers GMI_ES14_c2496_651 and GMI_ES22_c18205_366 were used to query the Morex V3 (Mascher et al. 2021) barley and the Chinese Spring v1.0 (Zhu et al. 2021) wheat genome assemblies available on Graingenes via BLAST to identify syntenic regions in these two species. This was done to determine if previously identified resistance genes or QTL effective against barley net blotch and wheat tan spot were located in these regions and if the candidate resistance genes identified in oat had orthologues present in these regions.

TaqMan assays

DNA sequences for oat iSelect 6 K BeadChip array markers were available from Illumina, while gene sequences were obtained from the OT3098 v2 genome assembly on Graingenes. ABI minor groove binder (ABI-MGB) TaqMan assays (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, USA) were designed for markers and genes underlying the chromosome 1D leaf blotch resistance QTL using the Applied Biosystems online design tool. Specific targeting of assays to chromosome 1D was accomplished by identifying paralogous and homoeologous marker/gene sequences located in other regions of the oat genome via BLAST to the OT3098 v2 and Sang (Kamal et al. 2022) assemblies found on Graingenes, followed by alignment of these sequences with those present in the five pangenome genotypes (Leggett, OT380, Victoria, Aslak, HiFi) provided by the international PanOat consortium.

Bi-allelic assays utilized FAM and VIC reporter dyes in the presence of ROX to standardize for sample-to-sample variation. Assays that passed the design criteria were tested for polymorphism and segregation against the parents and lines (six seeds per line) of the two segregating RIL populations using the same generation of seed that was used for genotyping. TaqMan assays were run under standard conditions recommended by the manufacturer. Reactions were conducted in 10 μL volumes and consisted of TaqPath™ ProAmp™ Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and MGB assay, both at 1 × concentrations. Template consisted of 50 ng of purified DNA or 1 μL aliquots (non-quantified) of template prepared by the sodium hydroxide method. Real-time PCR was conducted on the Step-One-Plus Real-Time PCR (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in ABI MicroAmp Optical 96-well reaction plates with ABI optical adhesive covers. Reaction conditions consisted of a pre-PCR read at 60 °C for 30 s, a hot-start activation at 95 °C for 10 min, 40 two-stage cycles of 92 °C for 15 s, 60 °C (65 °C for AvLB_651) for 30 s and a post-PCR read at 60 °C for 30 s.

Results

Infection response to Pyrenophora avenae

Infection responses (IRs) observed across the 32 oat genotypes of the diversity and pangenome panels against the three P. avenae isolates ranged from resistant to susceptible (Table 1). Among the genotypes evaluated 96-21Cn19, AC Assiniboia/S42, CDC Minstrel, OT3011, SA99572 and OT380 were the most resistant (IRs to all isolates ≤ 2.0), while CDC Dancer, Iowa 2052, Aslak and HiFi were the most susceptible (IRs to all isolates ≥ 6.0) (Table 1). Based on a cutoff value of 4.0 to delineate resistant from susceptible infection responses, the infection response to each of the three isolates was consistent for most genotypes (Table 1), with only Aiguruda, CDC Boyer, CDC Morrison, MN06212 and Provena showing inconsistent infection responses (Table 1). For two of these genotypes the inconsistency was due to the infection responses being close to the cutoff value. In the case of CDC Boyer, the inconsistent infection response associated with PA102 had a value of 3.7. Similarly, MN06212 showed infection responses to PA102 and PA114 (IRs = 3.7) that were all near the cutoff value (Table 1). As such, 17 genotypes were classified as resistant, 10 genotypes were susceptible, and five genotypes were unable to be definitively categorized (Table 1).

The infection response data obtained from the 25 genotypes of the diversity panel were used to identify RIL populations within the oat breeding program at the Crop Development Centre created from parents with strongly contrasting and consistent infection responses to the three isolates. The OT3011 (IRs 1.0–2.0) × Iowa 2052 (IRs 6.0–6.7) and AC Assiniboia/S42 (IRs 1.0–1.3) × CDC Dancer (IRs 6.7–8.3) populations met these criteria and were used for subsequent study.

QTL analysis

After filtering the oat iSelect 6 K BeadChip array markers genotyped on the OT3011 × Iowa 2052 and AC Assiniboia/S42 × CDC Dancer populations, a high-quality set of 1110 and 1059 markers, respectively, were produced and used for linkage mapping. Twenty-eight linkage groups with a total map length of 2425 cM were created for the OT3011 × Iowa 2052 population, while 31 linkage groups with a total map length of 2401 cM were generated for the AC Assiniboia/S42 × CDC Dancer population (Supplementary Table S4). Markers were distributed across 20 of the 21 oat chromosomes (no markers present on chromosomes 7A) in the OT3011 × Iowa 2052 population and all 21 chromosomes in the AC Assiniboia/S42 × CDC Dancer population (Supplementary Table S4).

A single QTL significantly associated with resistance to each of the three P. avenae isolates was identified in both the OT3011 × Iowa 2052 and AC Assiniboia/S42 × CDC Dancer populations (Table 3, Fig. 3). The QTL identified in the OT3011 × Iowa 2052 population was delimited to a narrow interval (78.0–78.6 cM; Fig. 3) and explained 58–71% of the phenotypic variation for each of the three isolates (Table 3). The QTL associated with each isolate was centered on marker ES15_Irc9414_222 which is located at 324.68 Mb on chromosome 1D (Table 3). Similarly, the QTL located in the AC Assiniboia/S42 × CDC Dancer population was defined to an interval spanning 61.9–62.2 cM (Fig. 3) which explained 78–83% of the phenotypic variance associated with each of the three isolates (Table 3). Linkage groups created for the chromosome 1D region surrounding the leaf blotch QTL in each population indicated marker order was well conserved between the populations and to the OT3098 v2 assembly (Fig. 3). Based on this information, the physical location of all iSelect 6 K BeadChip array markers underlying the QTL along with markers proximal and distal of the QTL was identified in the OT3098 v2 assembly. When DNA sequences associated with each marker were used to query the OT3098 v2 assembly, high-quality hits (> 90% coverage of the query sequence and > 75% identity) were identified on chromosome 1D and two regions on chromosome 1A, hereafter referred as 1A-1 and 1A-2, located ~ 142 Mb apart, for all markers (Supplementary Table S5). Occasional hits to other chromosomes were observed for ES22_c1052_894 (chromosomes 5C, 7A and 7D) and GBS_90539 (chromosomes 3C, 4A and 4D) (Supplementary Table S5). In all cases, the query sequence hits displayed the highest coverage and/or highest identity on chromosome 1D (Supplementary Table S5), thus confirming the location of the oat blotch resistance QTL to this chromosome.

For both genes, the susceptible pangenome genotypes displayed a common amino acid sequence, while resistant pangenome genotypes lacked the gene completely, as with the S/T PK, or the amino acid variants displayed significant deletions in comparison to the susceptible sequence, as with the Cys RLK. These observations would suggest that either candidate gene could function as a susceptibility gene in the oat-P. avenae pathosystem. To further support this hypothesis, the Cys RLK and S/T PK homoeologues present on chromosome 1A or 1C in the resistant pangenome genotypes were compared to the corresponding chromosome 1D homoeologue present in susceptible pangenome genotypes to ensure that the resistant pangenome genotypes did not harbor complementary “susceptible” copies of these genes. The comparison indicated that numerous indels and deletions were present in the chromosome 1A and 1C homoeologues of the Cys RLK and S/T PK genes in resistant pangenome genotypes, with similarity ranging from 68.7 to 84.8% and 72.7–84.9%, respectively, to the corresponding chromosome 1D homoeologue present in susceptible pangenome genotypes (Supplementary Tables S7 and S8).

In addition to the Cys RLK and S/T PK candidate resistance genes, 16 other genes were present (Table 4; Supplementary Table S6) within the same 995 Kb region. Among these were three genes with unknown function and several genes implicated in plant defense including two respiratory burst oxidase homolog protein B genes and an E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme gene.

Oat chromosome 1D synteny to barley and wheat

Syntenic regions to the oat chromosome 1D leaf blotch resistance locus, spanning the 3.41-Mb interval delineated by the ES14_c2496_651 and ES22_c18205_366 markers, were identified in barley and wheat (Supplementary Table S6). Synteny to the barley genome was identified from 460.78 to 468.14 Mb on chromosome 1H of the Morex V3 genome, while synteny was observed to chromosomes 1A (474.18–484.33 Mb), 1B (499.19–515.91 Mb) and 1D (374.37–384.51 Mb) in the Chinese Spring v1 genome. There was good agreement in overall gene content and order across the three species; however, the orientation of the region in wheat chromosomes 1A, 1B and 1D was inverted in comparison to the oat and barley genomes (Supplementary Table S6). Of the 71 oat genes annotated within the 3.41 Mb, 37 of these genes were shared across the three species in an identical order, except for a small inversion of three genes within the wheat 1B sub-genome, and an additional 29 genes were present in identical order within the barley and wheat genomes (Supplementary Table S6). Interestingly, none of the six candidate resistance genes were present in the syntenic regions of the barley and wheat genomes (Supplementary Table S6).

To identify recombinants in both RIL populations, only those lines that showed consist resistant or susceptible infection responses to all three isolates were assessed with the TaqMan assays. This resulted in 109 and 134 lines from the OT3011 × Iowa 2052 and AC Assiniboia/S42 × CDC Dancer populations, respectively, being used. Evaluation of both RIL populations with the TaqMan assays identified a single line in the OT3011 × Iowa 2052 population that showed a recombination between the AvLB_366 allele call and the leaf blotch resistance gene. Additionally, a single line from both RIL populations was identified to harbor a recombination between AvLB_651 and the leaf blotch resistance gene. This placed the leaf blotch resistance gene between AvLB_651 and AvLB_366. Subsequently, three additional assays were developed to target this region of interest. The additional assays targeted two wall-associated receptor kinase 1 genes (AVESA.00001b.r3.1Dg0001492 and AVESA.00001b.r3.1Dg0001493) and a gene (AVESA.00001b.r1.1DG00739130) identified during re-annotation of the OT3098 v1 assembly with unknown function located approximately 97 K nucleotides proximal of ES22_c4473_396. These were designated AvLB_K2, AvLB_K3 and AvLB_9130, respectively (Table 6). Evaluation of the consistent infection response subset lines in both RIL populations with the three additional assays revealed the oat blotch resistance locus resided distal of AvLB_K3, based on one recombinant line in the AC Assiniboia/S42 × CDC Dancer population. Thus, the leaf blotch resistance gene was further delineated to a 3.28 Mb region between AvLB_K3 and AvLB_366, with AvLB_9130 and AvLB_396 showing perfect linkage.

The AvLB_K3, AvLB_9130 and AvLB_396 assays were subsequently evaluated across all lines in the OT3011 × Iowa 2052 and AC Assiniboia/S42 × CDC Dancer populations and displayed very good agreement to leaf blotch ratings (Fig. 2, Supplementary Tables S2 and S3). The assay identified 19 and 21 ‘heterozygous’ lines in the OT3011 × Iowa 2052 and AC Assiniboia/S42 × CDC Dancer populations, respectively (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3). After testing six individual seeds from these ‘heterozygous’ lines it was determined that the lines were a heterogeneous mix of homozygous resistant and homozygous susceptible seeds. This is not unexpected given that the populations were derived from F4 plants; thus, theoretically 12.5% of plants would still be heterozygous at the 1D leaf blotch resistance locus before becoming fixed in subsequent generations.

The AvLB_K3, AvLB_9130 and AvLB_396 assays were further evaluated against the 25 genotypes of the diversity panel and the seven genotypes of the pangenome panel (Table 1). Among the 27 genotypes that showed a consistent resistant or susceptible rating to all three isolates, the AvLB_K3 assay correctly categorized all genotypes except Bw103 for which the assay indicated the genotype to be susceptible, but the infection response was resistant (Table 1). This finding would suggest a recombination event was present in this genotype, similar to the one identified in the AC Assiniboia/S42 × CDC Dancer population. The AvLB_9130 assay correctly predicted the infection response of all 27 genotypes (Table 1), similar to the results obtained from both RIL populations. Interestingly, the AvLB_396 assay incorrectly categorized six genotypes, in all cases predicting susceptibility when in fact the genotypes had resistant infection responses (Table 1). These findings indicate that recombination events were present in each of these genotypes which would therefore place the resistance gene between AvLB_K3 and AvLB_396 (a region spanning 995 Kb).

Discussion

This study is the first to report on the genetic inheritance, genomic location and candidate genes in oat associated with resistance to the P. avenae leaf blotch pathogen. The artificial inoculation method and rating scale described to assess oat infection response to P. avenae and the TaqMan assays linked to the leaf blotch resistance gene on chromosome 1D will be useful tools for oat breeders and pathologists to improve resistance to this disease.

Effective growth and sporulation of P. avenae on V8 medium at 21 °C under a 12-h day/night cycle follows from the study of Tekauz and Chico (1980) in which the same media and growth conditions were used to effectively culture P. graminea (causal agent of barley leaf stripe) and P. teres (casual agent of barley net blotch) and which were reported by Harder and Haber (1992) to also work with P. avenae (based on a personal communication by A. Tekauz). Indoor inoculation conditions appropriate for P. avenae have been reported in only one study. Earhart and Shands (1952) germinated oat seeds in a quartz sand medium containing P. avenae conidia, but poor infection rates were observed. They achieved better infection when a mycelia/conidia suspension (concentration not indicated) was applied to 30-day-old seedlings (grown at 18 °C) which were then placed at 24 °C with 100% humidity (lighting conditions not specified) for 48 h before returning plants to 18 °C for 3–4 days before rating. The method described in the current study to assess P. avenae infection response involved inoculation of 14-day-old seedlings (three-leaf stage) with 3 × 104 conidia/mL, placing inoculated seedlings in darkness at 100% humidity for 24 h and rating infection response 7 days later. This method is similar in many respects to Earhart and Shands (1952) but reduces the overall procedure from 36 to 22 days and clarifies parameters related to conidia concentration and lighting conditions following inoculation when plants are held under 100% humidity. It is hoped that the inoculation procedure provided and the reduced time required to establish infection and rate the host response will allow for effective and efficient screening of this disease.

A number of infection rating scales have been described for P. avenae. Šebesta et al. (2001) described three scales used to assess severity of infection in field trials, a scale based on infected leaf area (James 1971) and two 9-point scales based on leaf symptoms (but no details were provided regarding the symptoms). Hilli et al. (2023) used a 1–9 scale based on a method used for ascochyta blight of chickpea (Nene et al. 1981), while Earhart and Shands (1952) used a 0–10 scale that ranged from a “visible lesion” to a “dead leaf,” but again with no detail describing lesions associated with each category. The rating scale and images described in this study borrow from the system created by Tekauz (1985) to rate P. teres infection of barley, a similar pathosystem also involving a cereal host and a pathogen from the Pyrenophora genus, that should help standardize information collected from studies involving P. avenae.

Assessment of leaf blotch infection responses with both the OT3011 × Iowa 2052 and AC Assiniboia/S42 × CDC Dancer populations strongly suggested that a single locus governed resistance to all three P. avenae isolates. Isolate PA102 in the OT3011 × Iowa 2052 was the only case for which the segregation ratio did not fit a single gene model, although the ratio was just outside the significance threshold (P = 0.02). This finding may be due to the larger proportion of infection responses in the OT3011 × Iowa 2052 population against PA102 lying close to the 4.0 rating used to differentiate resistant from susceptible reactions. To support this idea, when the cutoff value was changed to 3.5, the ratio of resistant to susceptible lines was observed to be 71:79 (χ2 = 0.43; P = 0.51) which was clearly consistent with a single gene model of inheritance. Thus, resistance against the PA102 isolate in the OT3011 × Iowa 2052 population may be more sensitive to environmental influence or be governed by additional minor QTL found elsewhere in the genome. Despite this observation, QTL mapping clearly identified a single region on chromosome 1D associated with leaf blotch resistance to all three isolates in both RIL populations that explained a significant portion of the phenotypic variance (58–83%). The QTL was initially delimited by markers GMI_ES14_c2496_651 and GMI_ES15_lrc9414_222, which defined a 1.67 Mb region between 323.01 and 324.68 Mb in OT3098. This region was also determined to have two homoeologous regions in OT3098 that were located on chromosome 1A, separated by ~ 142 Mb, which both displayed strong conservation of gene content and order.

The AvLB_9130 TaqMan assay developed to mark the chromosome 1D leaf blotch resistance gene showed very good specificity across a diversity of genotypes which will make it useful for marker-assisted selection. Across the 27 genotypes of the diversity and pangenome panels that showed consistent infection responses to all three P. avenae isolates, the AvLB_K3 assay correctly categorized all genotypes. It was interesting to note that the 1D leaf blotch resistance was present in such a diversity of germplasm which represented breeding programs in Argentina, Canada, Chile, Sweden, UK and USA. The oat breeding community is fairly small and the exchange of germplasm between breeding programs is common which may explain the prevalence of the gene across a diversity of breeding programs. Alternatively, the resistance gene may already have been widely dispersed in hexaploid Avena species that were eventually domesticated and disseminated as landraces, which in turn formed the base populations on which selection was imposed by early oat breeding programs.

The oat genome is known to harbor numerous translocations with a total of 15 intragenomic and seven intergenomic translocations identified in the A. sativa cultivar Sang via comparison of its genome to three different Avena species (Kamal et al. 2022). Many of these translocated regions are associated with important adaptation and agronomic traits. For example, He et al. (2024) reported a ~ 28 Mb region of chromosome 4A that was translocated and duplicated to the top of chromosome 4D, likely during the domestication of cultivated oat from A. sterilis, and which was present in most cultivated oat accessions evaluated. Genes related to agronomically relevant traits, such as yield, heading date and both biotic and abiotic stress, were located within the duplicated region (He et al. 2024). The well-known 1C-1A (formerly 7C-17A) translocation has been linked to crown freezing tolerance and winter hardiness (Santos et al. 2006; Wooten et al. 2007) and has been attributed as the reason oat was able to adapt from Mediterranean winter to spring cultivation (Jellen and Beard 2000). It is this translocation which accounts for the duplicated region on chromosome 1A, found in five of the seven pangenome genotypes (Legget, OT3098, OT380, Aslak, Sang), that is homoeologous to the leaf blotch resistance region on chromosome 1D. Although the 1C-1A translocation event is very common in cultivated oat, being found in 97% of accessions evaluated by Jellen and Beard (2000), the identification of chromosome 1D homoeologues on chromosomes 1A (single copy only) and 1C in the pangenome genotypes Victoria and HiFi indicates that these genotypes fall within the minority of cultivated oat accessions.

The OT380 pangenome genotype represents an example of the rare 6C/1D translocation which results in the leaf blotch resistance region being located on chromosome 6C in this genotype. This observation brings forth an important consideration with respect to breeding for leaf blotch resistance and highlights the value of understanding the specific genome arrangement of genotypes used by oat breeders. Conceivably, breeding progeny containing both the 1D and 6C resistance regions could be created from the crossing of appropriate parents (e.g., OT3098 × OT380). If the leaf blotch resistance acts as a susceptibility gene, then the appropriate null allele would need to be present at both loci for effective resistance. On the other hand, if the resistance gene acts in a classic dominant gene-for-gene manner, then having two copies may offer advantages both in terms of buffering mutations that may create a null allele and in providing a stronger defense response. For example, the maize ZmLOX5 gene, which provides resistance against fall armyworm (Spodoptera frugiperda (J.E. Smith)), was assessed for resistance in a set of near isogenic lines that contained zero, one or two copies (Yuan et al. 2024). Enhanced resistance, linked to increased expression of ZmLOX5 and greater production of its primary oxylipin product, was observed with increased copy number (Yuan et al. 2024). Similarly, overexpression of the GhMYB36 gene, which confers verticillium wilt (caused by Verticillium dahliae Kleb) resistance in cotton, was demonstrated to provide enhanced resistance (Liu et al. 2022).

The leaf blotch resistance region on chromosome 1D was found to be syntenic to barley chromosome 1H (460.78–468.14 Mb) and wheat chromosomes 1A (474.18–484.33 Mb), 1B (499.19–515.91 Mb) and 1D (374.37–384.51 Mb). Gene content and order was conserved across these regions with 52% of the 71 genes annotated within the oat genome being present in both the barley and wheat genomes, with another 29 genes being shared between barley and wheat. Given the high degree of synteny across the three species, it was notable that none of the six oat genes with immunity-related functions within this region were identified in the barley or wheat genomes. Consistent with this finding was the lack of strong evidence from the literature for the presence of resistance genes or QTL effective against barley net blotch or wheat tan spot within these syntenic regions of the barley and wheat genomes. In barley, Grewal et al. (2012) identified a weak QTL, which explained 8% of the phenotypic variation for one of two net-form net blotch isolates evaluated, that resides ~ 4 Mb (based on position information provided in Clare et al. 2020) distal to the barley syntenic region. Rozanova et al. (2019) identified a wide interval extending from 438.89 to 467.93 Mb (based on position information provided in Clare et al. 2020) within a panel of 96 genotypes that was associated with resistance against one of four net-form net blotch isolates. The distal end of this interval would overlap with the barley region syntenic with the oat leaf blotch resistance region, but its effectiveness against a single isolate contrasts with the major net blotch resistance genes (Rpt1 to Rpt8) that have been identified in multiple studies using different isolates, suggesting it too represents a resistance loci with minor effective resistance on the pathogen population as a whole. In wheat, two regions present on the group 1 homoeologous chromosomes have been described which harbor tan spot resistance genes/QTL. The Tsc1 gene was fine mapped to a region on chromosome 1AS from 4.60 to 4.77 Mb (Running et al. 2022), and a resistance QTL was mapped to the telomeric end on the short arm of chromosome 1B (Li et al. 2011). However, neither of these regions coincides with the 474.18–484.33 Mb and 499.19–515.91 Mb intervals on chromosomes 1A and 1B, respectively, that are syntenic to the oat chromosome 1D leaf blotch resistance region.

Among the major barley net blotch resistance/susceptibility genes, only the Rpt5 resistance gene has been cloned and the Spt2 susceptibility gene has been fine mapped to a degree that candidate genes have been identified. Effertz et al. (2024) cloned a receptor-like protein located at 366.81 Mb on chromosome 6H in the Morex V3 assembly that when transformed into the susceptible Golden Promise conferred increased resistance to the net blotch pathogen. Clare et al. (2024) fine mapped Spt2 to a ~ 198 kb region on chromosome 5H at 438.38 Mb on the Morex V3 assembly and identified a single candidate gene that encoded a pentatricopeptide repeat protein. These proteins have been associated with increased susceptibility to both bacterial and fungal pathogens (Park et al. 2014).

Within the wheat tan spot pathosystem, two of the three dominant susceptibility genes, Tsn1 and Tsc2, which govern interactions with the tan spot pathogen have been cloned. The Tsn1 gene, located on chromosome 5BL, was determined to encode a classic resistance protein that contained a protein kinase, a nucleotide binding domain and a leucine-rich repeat region (Faris et al. 2010). The Tsc2 gene was fine mapped on chromosome 2BS and eventually determined to encode a protein belonging to the Exo70 family, thus representing a new class of susceptibility genes (Singh 2025). The Tsc1 gene has been delineated to an interval on chromosome 1AS spanning 184 Kb between 4.60 and 4.77 Mb on the Chinese Spring assembly which contained seven candidate genes including two that encoded proteins with leucine-rich repeats (LRR), four with protein kinase and LRR domains and one with nucleotide binding and ARC domains (Running et al. 2022).

Two strong candidate genes associated with P. avenae resistance on chromosome 1D were identified with identical, apparently functional, proteins encoded by each gene present in susceptible pangenome genotypes and either absence of a protein due to absence of the gene (in the case of the S/T PK gene) or truncated proteins (in the case of the Cys RLK gene) within resistant pangenome genotypes. Additionally, analysis of the Cys RLK and S/T PK chromosome 1A and 1C homoeologues present in resistant pangenome genotypes indicated significant variation from the corresponding chromosome 1D homoeologues present in susceptible pangenome genotypes, thus ruling out the possibility that complementary “susceptible” copies of these genes existed elsewhere within the genome of the resistant pangenome genotypes. Serine/threonine kinases are a well-known functional domain encoded by genes related to plant defense response. Receptor-like proteins (RLPs) and receptor-like kinases (RLKs) compose one of two broad groups of plant disease resistance genes, with the other group composed of cytoplasmic nucleotide binding domain leucine-rich repeat domain-containing receptors (NLRs) (Liu et al. 2017; Tang et al. 2017). The receptor-like kinases contain an extracellular domain that recognizes pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), a transmembrane domain that orients the protein within the cell membrane and an intracellular S/T PK that initiates the defense response. Examples of such resistance genes include FLS2, an Arabidopsis gene that recognizes bacterial flagellin (Gómez-Gómez and Boller 2000), and the rice Xa21 gene that confers resistance to the bacterial pathogen Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae (Song et al. 1995). However, there are also several examples of S/T PK (without the associated extracellular and transmembrane domains) being the primary determinant of resistance. Cao et al. (2011) demonstrated that the Stpk-V gene is responsible for wheat powdery mildew resistance provided by Pm21, while both the Pto and Pti genes of tomato are involved in resistance to bacterial speck disease (Martin et al. 1994; Zhou et al. 1995). In each of these examples, resistance is provided by a function protein, as opposed to the current study in which the absence of a functional S/T PK is associated with resistance to P. avenae. If future work demonstrates that the S/T PK is responsible for oat leaf blotch resistance, then this would represent a novel way that this group of resistance genes participates in plant defense.

Cysteine-rich RLKs are one of at least 11 groups of RLKs (differentiated by their extracellular binding domains) that have roles in plant growth/development and response to biotic/abiotic stress (Zhu et al. 2023). With respect to plant defense, the CR28 gene from Arabidopsis which encodes a cysteine-rich RLK was shown to be upregulated upon exposure to flg22 from Pseudomonas syringae and interacts with the FLS2 immune complex to provide resistance (Yadeta et al. 2017). The Cys RLK candidate gene identified in this study was annotated as having two tandem kinase domains along with major sperm protein and WD40 domains. Tandem kinase proteins (TKPs) have been identified in species that span the plant kingdom with > 95% belonging to the RLK-Pelle family (Reveguk et al. 2025). Evidence emerging exclusively from Triticeae species indicates that TKPs participate in plant immunity. Examples of resistance genes with TKP domains include the barley Rpg1 stem rust resistance gene (Brueggeman et al. 2002), the barley Un8 loose smut resistance gene (Zang et al. 2015), the wheat Rwt4 blast resistance gene (Arora et al. 2023) and eight other wheat resistance genes that provide resistance against stripe rust (Klymiuk et al. 2018), stem rust (Chen et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2022), leaf rust (Wang et al. 2023) and powdery mildew (Lu et al. 2020; Gaurav et al. 2022; Li et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2024). The four variants of the Cys RLK protein identified within the resistant genotypes demonstrated significant variation from the protein variant common to all susceptible genotypes, including the absence of the kinase 1 domain in both Leggett and OT380, and the absence of the kinase 2, MSP and WD40 domains in Victoria. Given that variation within, or absence of, each domain was observed, it is impossible to determine which domains are critical for interaction with the pathogen. Among examples in which TKPs act as resistance genes, it has been shown that they can directly interact with pathogen effectors. For example, the kinase domain of the wheat RWT4 protein has been shown to interact and transphosphorylate the AvrPWT4 effector from the blast pathogen (Sung et al. 2025). Similarly, the kinase 1 domain of the wheat Sr62TK protein directly interacts with a corresponding AvrSr62 protein present in the stem rust pathogen, thus triggering a defense response (Chen et al. 2025). Each of the TKPs currently associated with resistance requires a functional protein for an effective defense response, so if the Cys RLK identified in this study is proven to be the gene responsible for resistance to P. avenae, then this will provide another example of a TKP gene playing a role in plant immunity, but in this case the absence of function would be a novel feature through which resistance is manifested.

In addition to the Cys RLK and S/T PK candidate resistance genes, several other genes present within the 995-Kb interval could not be conclusively ruled out as possible, although less likely, candidate resistance genes. Among these were three genes with unknown function, two respiratory burst oxidase homolog protein B genes and a gene annotated to encode an E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme. Respiratory burst oxidase homolog protein B genes encode NADPH oxidases that help develop the oxidative burst when rice is challenged by Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae (Khaskhali et al. 2025), while E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzymes have been associated with governing downstream defense signaling, including reactive oxygen species production, stomatal closure, and phytohormone biosynthesis (Gonçalves et al. 2026).

In summary, a leaf blotch resistance locus was mapped to a 995-Kb interval on chromosome 1D positioned at 323.14–324.13 Mb that displayed effective resistance again three different isolates of P. avenae collected from western Canada. Within the interval resided six kinase encoding genes with previously described roles in plant defense. Sequence analysis of these six genes within a panel of seven pangenome genotypes with known reactions to the three P. avenae isolates identified a serine/threonine-protein kinase and a cysteine-rich receptor-like kinase that displayed consistent differences between resistant and susceptible genotypes. Susceptible genotypes displayed a common serine/threonine-protein kinase amino acid sequence, while resistant genotypes showed an absence of the gene. Similarly, susceptible genotypes shared a common amino acid sequence of the cysteine-rich receptor-like kinase, while resistant genotypes showed variously truncated proteins resulting from premature stop codons. In both cases, this would suggest that the candidate genes act as susceptibility genes in their interaction with P. avenae and that resistance manifests by the absence of a functional protein. Such an interaction would be consistent with the wheat susceptibility genes, Tsn1, Tsc1 and Tsc2, that govern interaction with P. tritici-repentis, and the barley susceptibility gene, Spt1, that interacts with the P. teres pathogen. Interestingly, the cysteine-rich receptor-like kinase gene is a TKP, which if ultimately shown to be responsible for resistance/susceptibility would provide another example of a TKP with a role related to plant immunity but with a novel absence of function required for resistance to be manifested. It is proposed that this leaf blotch resistance locus on chromosome 1D is referred to as Spa1OT3011 to recognize that it is the first described susceptibility gene relevant to the P. avenae pathogen which was identified in the OT3011 genotype.
