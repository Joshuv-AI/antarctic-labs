 Abstract
 The rapid growth of hyperscale AI data centers is bringing renewed attention to the reliability risk
 that sustained forced oscillations pose to bulk power systems, with cyclic computational workloads
 emerging as a new forcing source. Unlike the broadband, stochastic disturbances from traditional
 industrial loads such as arc furnaces, AI training and inference facilities can inject large active
 power swings concentrated at specific frequencies over extended durations - characteristics that
 existing grid planning practices do not account for. While the North American Electric Reliability
 Corporation (NERC) has recognized this gap and called for system-level studies of large load
 interconnections, no standardized methodology exists to screen, simulate, and quantify these
 risks at the planning stage.
 This report presents the Risk Assessment Tool for Large Load-induced Events (RATLLE), a
 Python-based, publicly available script suite developed at the Pacific Northwest National Labora-
 tory to evaluate bulk power system reliability risks from data center-induced oscillations. RATLLE
 implements a three-module workflow: a screening module that identifies vulnerable interconnec-
 tion locations and excitable system modes; a simulation module that models cyclic data center
 load behavior using a commercial positive sequence simulation platform; and an analysis module
 that computes risk metrics and generates interactive visualization dashboards. The risk metrics,
 formulated around simulation observables, map oscillation impacts to a three-stage severity scale
 spanning latent equipment fatigue through imminent cascading failure.
 The methodology is demonstrated on two Western Electricity Coordinating Council (WECC)
 system models: a publicly available 240-bus reduced representation and a detailed 2031 Heavy
 Winter planning case. Case studies illustrate that even modest 50 MW forced oscillations at
 resonant frequencies can produce wide-area power swings, N-1 security constraint violations,
 and cascading generator trips through protection actions - outcomes that would not occur under
 normal operating conditions without oscillations present. The results underscore the need for
 standardized oscillation impact assessment in large load interconnection studies and provide a
 reproducible, extensible framework for utilities to adopt or customize within their existing planning
 workflows.

 1.0 Background
 The power grid is experiencing a period of rapid load growth driven by surging energy demand
 from data centers as a global race for artificial intelligence (AI) dominance accelerates. Hyper-
 scale facilities can consume hundreds of MW at a single load site, and their dynamic behavior
 poses unique risks to power grid operations (NERC, 2025b). Recognizing these risks, the North
 American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC) Large Loads Working Group has published
 a series of white papers identifying: key reliability concerns associated with emerging large
 loads (LLs) such as AI-training facilities (NERC, 2025a); gaps in current practices and standards
 (NERC, 2026a); and reliability guidelines for mitigating these risks (NERC, 2026c). NERC has fur-
 ther issued a Level 3 Alert calling upon industry stakeholders to improve load modeling practices,
 conduct system-level studies that incorporate LL operational characteristics, and strengthen per-
 formance monitoring infrastructure (NERC, 2026b). A key reliability risk identified in the NERC
 white papers is forced oscillations induced by cyclic computational loads. While system-wide
 studies have been recommended to understand risks from site-specific behavior, the lack of a
 standardized methodology to quantify oscillation impact remains a gap (NERC, 2026a).

 Figure 1: Example demand periodicity due to an AI-training workload, reproduced from Choukse
      et al. (2025)

 In hyperscale AI training and inference facilities, synchronized computational tasks across
 large GPU/TPU clusters can lead to cyclic power demand characterized by periodic consumption
 bursts followed by cool-down periods (Figure 1) (Choukse et al., 2025). This synchronization
 effect is not exclusive to AI facilities; coordinated task execution across compute clusters in
 general-purpose data centers can similarly produce periodic demand impulses (Mishra et al.,
 2025). Such periodic load patterns can introduce forced oscillations into the grid and/or intermit-
 tently excite natural oscillation modes, posing reliability challenges. The frequency of the demand
 fluctuations determines the nature of reliability risks. High-frequency oscillations (in the ∼5–59 Hz
 range, commonly referred to as subsynchronous oscillations or SSOs) can cause power quality
 problems and interact with generator torsional modes, but their impact is typically confined to the
 vicinity of the oscillation source. On the other hand, oscillations at lower frequencies (roughly
 0.1-1.5 Hz, often called the electromechanical range) can propagate across large portions of
 an electrical interconnection and cause protection/control misoperations. Interaction with control
 loops of inverters or synchronous generators is possible throughout the subsynchronous fre-
 quency range. A detailed discussion of the reliability risks associated with oscillations in different
 frequency ranges is provided in Chapter 2.0.
 The threat of oscillatory instability is well recognized, and grid operators employ various mon-
 itoring and mitigation solutions to manage forced oscillations during operations (Chatterjee et al.,

Background 1

 2023; NERC, 2021). These practices, however, are premised on the assumption that forced
 oscillations arise from equipment malfunction and are not a feature of normal operations. Mal-
 functions are relatively infrequent and inherently difficult to predict; thus, it is more practical to
 address oscillations as they occur rather than to formulate case-specific mitigations during plan-
 ning. The cyclic nature of large computational loads fundamentally challenges this assumption
 and makes assessing forced oscillation risk at the planning stage a critical reliability need.
 This is not to say that load demand periodicity is unfamiliar to the industry. Fluctuating loads
 such as arc furnaces and rolling mills have long been known to inject harmonic distortion, flicker,
 and power quality problems, and mitigation measures, including passive filters and static VAR
 compensators (SVCs), have been deployed to limit their adverse impact on the bulk power sys-
 tem (BPS). However, these disturbances, being broadband, stochastic, and largely confined to
 the local interconnection point, posed limited forced oscillation risks to the BPS in the electrome-
 chanical frequency range. Industry practice, therefore, focused on site-specific power quality
 mitigation. Nonetheless, the potential to affect neighboring utility systems had been acknowl-
 edged, and instances of generator shaft damage attributable to industrial load characteristics
 have been documented (Solanics et al., 2000; Dorfman et al., 1996). In contrast, hyperscale AI
 facilities can drive large active power swings with energy concentrated at specific frequencies,
 persisting for hours over the duration of training or inference workloads. Unlike the broadband,
 stochastic disturbances of arc furnaces, these deterministic and sustained oscillations are not
 adequately mitigated by SVCs - devices designed for reactive power compensation and voltage
 regulation, not for damping active power oscillations.
 The experience with large pulsating loads from particle accelerators offers a closer analogue.
 The first known case of a large accelerator being supplied by a utility system without an interme-
 diate on-site motor-generator set occurred in the midwestern United States in the 1970s (Pinneilo
 and Van Ness, 1971). Utility analysis deemed that the impact of expected load pulsations on
 nearby generators was acceptable, but future load growth might require mitigation measures.
 In the 1990s, the Long Island Lighting Company (LILCO) conducted a system impact analysis
 before interconnecting a synchrotron at the Brookhaven National Laboratory (Rostamkolai et al.,
 1994). Risks from dynamic power swings, voltage fluctuations, torsional interactions with nearby
 turbine-generators, and harmonic voltage distortions were studied. It was found that dynamic
 power swings were the limiting case, and one of the synchrotron’s planned operation modes
 could cause 5% power swings for a nearby generator due to resonance with system modes.
 This was deemed unacceptable, and the load operation mode was redesigned. Additionally, a
 power pulsation monitoring relay (PPMR) was installed to protect the LILCO grid from problems
 on the synchrotron’s power control system. The PPMR analyzed the frequency spectrum of
 the three-phase power on a dedicated 69 kV feeder supplying the load, and was designed to
 issue alarms or trip the supply if power swings crossed design thresholds. Different oscillation
 amplitude limits were prescribed for the 0.75-5 Hz and 10-40 Hz frequency ranges.
 The LILCO study establishes the value of site-specific system impact analyses before inter-
 connecting large cyclic loads, but it also illustrates a fundamental scalability problem. What was
 feasible for a single synchrotron in the 1990s becomes impractical for the hundreds of hyperscale
 data center interconnection requests that utilities now have to process, especially since each fa-
 cility’s workload characteristics may change over its lifetime. Each load site would require a
 custom study, yet most utilities lack the standardized workflows to screen, simulate, and interpret
 these risks systematically. The result is planning studies that are slow, inconsistent, and difficult
 to reproduce. NERC has explicitly acknowledged this gap, noting that “there are significant gaps
 regarding the defining of allowable limits for forced oscillations” (NERC, 2026a).
 To address this, we present the Risk Assessment Tool for Large Load-induced Events (RATLLE)

 - a publicly-available1 Python script suite for evaluating large load-induced oscillation risks in bulk
 power systems developed by the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (Biswas et al., 2026).
 RATLLE is suitable for studying wide-area phenomena with dynamics below ∼10 Hz that are
 typically captured in phasor-domain models used by utilities. The framework guides planners
 progressively through a structured workflow, from system-wide screening that identifies the most
 vulnerable interconnection locations and frequencies, to site-specific risk assessments that quan-
 tify oscillation severity and spatial propagation. The scripts are designed to be adopted directly
 or customized to fit a utility’s existing processes. The current implementation interfaces with
 PSS/E as the underlying simulation engine, with extensions to PSLF and PowerWorld under
 development.
 This report details the methodology underlying the RATLLE framework and illustrates findings
 with case-studies on two system models - a 240-bus publicly available reduced representation of
 the Western Electricity Coordinating Council (WECC) system and a detailed WECC 2031 heavy
 winter planning case (Yuan et al., 2020). The present work extends our earlier work published in
      Biswas et al. (2026).
 The key contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

 • To the authors’ knowledge, RATLLE is the first end-to-end methodology — spanning vul-
 nerability screening, time-domain simulation, and risk quantification — for evaluating large
 load-induced oscillation impacts at the planning stage. The scripts are publicly available, and
 results can be independently reproduced.
 • This work formulates a set of simulation-based risk metrics, organized around a three-stage
 severity scale. This is one of the first attempts to comprehensively quantify BPS reliability
 impact from sustained oscillations, for which no NERC planning standard currently prescribes
 limits.
 • Two practical modeling approaches for cyclic computational loads are presented: a Python-
 scripted representation and a scalable PSS/E user-defined model. Both represent AI workload
 periodicity in positive-sequence planning studies.
 • Case studies on a detailed WECC planning case show that 50 MW oscillations can convert
 an N-1 secure operating condition into one that leads to cascading trips. This motivates a
 proposed criterion for setting oscillation amplitude limits: permissible amplitudes should be
 such that no credible contingency produces failures that would not occur in the oscillation’s
 absence.

 The remainder of the report is organized as follows. Chapter 2 provides a brief overview of
 power system oscillations and the challenges associated with capturing their impact accurately
 in planning studies. Chapter 3 describes the risk assessment methodology and RATLLE script
 architecture, alongside outlining opportunities for further refinement. Besides detailing the tool
 implementation, the chapter also provides insights into design choices made during development
 and their limitations. Chapter 4 illustrates case studies, while Chapter 5 outlines future directions
 and concludes this report.

 2.0 Power System Oscillations
 Understanding power system oscillations requires two complementary perspectives: a causal
 view that identifies the underlying mechanisms driving oscillatory behavior, and a frequency-
 based view that highlights the physical processes and system components most likely to be
 involved and impacted. From a causal standpoint, oscillations can be natural or forced (Rogers
      et al., 2025).
 Power system dynamics can be analyzed by linearizing the system around an operating point.
 Like any linear system, the relationship between inputs and outputs is governed by the system’s
 modes - the eigenvalues of the state matrix. Each mode is characterized by three properties: fre-
 quency, damping ratio, and mode shape. The damping ratio determines how quickly oscillations
 at a given frequency decay once excited. The mode shape describes the relative participation of
 different power system elements in that oscillation. Together, these properties emerge from the
 interactions among generators, loads, and control loops that define the system state matrix.
 Natural oscillations arise from these inherent system modes. During normal operation, stochas-
 tic load and generation variations act as a broadband noise input, continuously exciting low-level
 oscillations that are always present under ambient conditions. Transient disturbances such as
 faults or generation loss act as impulse-like inputs and produce ringdown oscillations that decay
 according to the modal damping. In both cases, the oscillation properties - frequency, damping,
 and shape - are inherited from the system’s modes.
 Forced oscillations (FOs), by contrast, arise when a persistent periodic input is applied to the
 power system. Common sources include malfunctioning control equipment, cyclic industrial or
 computational loads, and resonant interactions with converter-based resources. Unlike natural
 oscillations, the frequency and characteristics of forced oscillations are determined by the forcing
 input rather than the system’s own modes, and the oscillations persist for as long as the forcing
 input is active. If the forcing frequency coincides with a system mode, the resultant oscillations
 may be amplified due to resonance.
 Figure 2 illustrates these phenomena using a simplified example. Consider a power system
 represented as a linear system with three modes: (0.2 Hz, 9% damping), (0.64 Hz, 3% damping),
 and (1.2 Hz, 5% damping). A random noise input excites all three modes simultaneously, pro-
 ducing small broadband oscillations in the output that reflect the superposition of the individual
 modal responses. A step change produces ringdown oscillations in which all three modes are
 excited and then decay, with the speed of decay determined by the damping ratio of each mode.
 In both cases, the output is a superposition of the system’s modal responses, as evident from
 the broad peaks at mode frequencies in the output responses’ power spectral density plots. The
 modal response is clearer in the ringdown oscillations - and hence extracting mode estimates
 from ambient measurements is considered a more challenging problem.
 In the same system, a sinusoidal input at 0.4 Hz produces steady-state output oscillations
 (forced) at the same frequency once the initial transient decays. Since 0.4 Hz does not coin-
 cide with any system mode, the output amplitude remains modest - attenuated from the input
 amplitude. A sinusoidal input at 0.64 Hz, however, directly excites the lightly damped 3% mode,
 and the output amplitude grows substantially - illustrating the amplification that occurs when the
 forcing frequency coincides with a system mode.

 2.1 Oscillations in Different Frequency Bands
 The frequency of oscillations is a valuable diagnostic: it provides insight into the underlying
 physical phenomena, identifies the power system elements most likely to be involved, determines
 how far oscillations can propagate spatially, and guides the choice of modeling approach needed

 reliability risks they introduce, differ considerably. Studying risks associated with high-frequency

 The frequency band in which computation cycle-driven oscillations fall therefore determines which
 power system elements are most likely to be impacted. It is worth noting that the control loops
 of inverter-based resources (IBRs) span a wide frequency range, and hence interaction with IBR
 controls is possible across all four bands.

 2.2 Studying Oscillation Impact in Planning Studies
 As discussed in Chapter 1.0, there is a lack of standardized study procedures to evaluate the
 wide-area reliability impact of load-induced low-frequency oscillations. Prior work has explored
 both small-signal stability studies and explicit time-domain simulations to characterize this impact
      (Biswas et al., 2026; Ko and Zhu, 2026).
 Small-signal stability studies can identify dominant system modes, their excitability, control-
 lability, and damping ratios, helping planners understand which frequencies can be excited by
 a load at a given interconnection point. Commercial tools such as Powertech Lab’s Small Sig-
 nal Stability Analysis Tool (SSAT) support mode estimation through direct eigenanalysis, and
 methods to derive mode properties from time-domain simulations are also used (WECC, 2021).

 A further complication is that load-induced oscillations are rarely ideal sinusoids. The actual

 The risk assessment framework presented in this report utilizes both small-signal analysis
 principles and time-domain simulations, using each where it is most informative. Two limitations
 are worth stating before describing the methodology in further detail. The limitations are inherent
 to planning-stage assessments of this kind, and both shape the design choices described in
 Chapter 3:

 • Planning model limitations: The usefulness of any simulation-based risk assessment ulti-
 mately depends on the accuracy of the underlying model. Interconnection-level planning
 models typically do not include detailed representations of all protection systems and reme-
 dial action schemes, and thus the direct impact of load-induced oscillations on protection
 and control actions cannot be studied within the simulation environment. These models span
 territories that different transmission operators and reliability coordinators are responsible for,
 and the associated modeling limitations cannot be resolved unilaterally by the planning en-
 gineer conducting the risk assessment. In the RATLLE framework, this limitation is partially
 addressed by deriving generic protection element settings and estimating their trip likelihood
 through post-processing of time-domain simulation outputs outside the simulation platform.

 3.0 Methodology
 Addressing the reliability risks posed by large-load-induced oscillations requires a structured,
 repeatable methodology that transmission planners can integrate into existing interconnection
 and planning study workflows. To this end, RATLLE is a Python-based script suite that automates
 the evaluation of bulk power system reliability risk from data center oscillations, using PSS/E as
 the simulation engine. The scripts are written in a modular function-based style, so that they can
 be easily understood, customized, and extended to suit a utility’s needs. A planning case in the
 form of raw and dynamics files serves as the primary input, and users can pass configuration
 parameters to tailor the study process by editing csv files. The codebase is hosted on PNNL’s
 public-facing GitHub repository3 with detailed documentation.

 Figure 4: RATLLE architecture

 Planners may approach the risk assessment problem with two questions:

 • How can vulnerable locations in the system be identified efficiently, without exhaustive sim-
 ulation of every candidate interconnection point?
 • Once a high-risk location is identified, how can a detailed site-specific risk analysis be con-
 ducted?

 RATLLE comprises three modules, designed for screening vulnerable locations, running time-
 series simulations, and analyzing simulation outputs (Figure 4). The screening module answers
 the first risk assessment question, while the simulation module tackles the second. The analysis
 module helps interpret the simulation outputs in terms of risk metrics. Natural decision points
 exist between consecutive modules - for instance, screening results can inform which studies are
 run by the simulation module. The rest of this chapter discusses each module in detail.

 3.1 Module 1: Screening
 The screening module operates in two stages. In the first stage, locations with high voltage and
 bus angle sensitivities to MW and MVar injections are identified. The motivation is simple - at
 high sensitivity locations, a persistent load cycling would produce the highest voltage and angle
 fluctuations. In the second stage, any system modes excitable from the high sensitivity locations
 are determined. Ideally, planners would conduct this analysis with several seasonal cases.

 3.1.1 Voltage sensitivity screening

 The screening methodology is simple – fictitious loads representing ±1 MW and ±1 MVar injec-
 tions are applied at each candidate bus, and a power flow solution is obtained for each pertur-
 bation. The resulting bus voltage deviations are recorded, and the voltage sensitivities ∆V /∆P
 and ∆V /∆Q are computed as the average deviation from the base case across the injection and
 withdrawal scenarios.
 Large loads are typically interconnected to BPS circuits at voltages below 230 kV. By default,
 RATLLE limits the sensitivity screening to buses at voltage levels between 69 and 138 kV, though
 users can modify this range as needed. Figure 5 shows the voltage sensitivities computed for
 69–138 kV buses in the WECC 2031 Heavy Winter planning case. Buses with switched shunts
 are excluded from the screening, as the shunts provide reactive power support. These sensitiivty
 metrics can be supplemented with other system strength indicators such as the short circuit ratio
 (SCR) and its variants.
 Although many sophisticated methods have been proposed in the literature to identify weak
 locations in a power grid, the brute-force approach adopted here was chosen for its implemen-
 tation simplicity. Computation times are modest - sensitivity computations for 9,600 buses in the
 WECC case were completed in under 18 minutes on a 13th Gen Intel Core i7-1365U 1.80 GHz
 processor with 32 GB RAM.
 Buses with high voltage sensitivity are identified as candidates for further screening. A 50
 MW load step is applied at each candidate bus, and a dynamic simulation is run for 3 seconds
 following the disturbance. If the simulation remains stable, the location is deemed capable of
 accommodating a 50 MW step change. If the simulation diverges, the load magnitude is reduced
 in steps of 5 MW until a stable response is obtained, yielding a conservative estimate of the
 maximum allowable load step at that location. It is assumed that if a large load is ultimately sited
 at the bus under study, appropriate network upgrades to maintain local voltages within acceptable

Methodology 10

 limits would be undertaken prior to interconnection.

 3.1.2 Angle sensitivity screening

 Following a procedure analogous to the voltage sensitivity screening, generator buses are iden-
 tified based on their voltage angle sensitivity to active power injection, i.e., the change in voltage
 angle per unit change in active power (∆θ/∆P ). Sensitivity is computed at all buses hosting
 generator units whose combined MVA rating exceeds a specified threshold, filtering out smaller
 units that may exhibit spurious angle sensitivity artifacts due to inaccurate or simplified represen-
 tations in the planning case. Buses with large units and high angle sensitivity indicate locations
 vulnerable to power oscillations, and clusters of such buses delineate high-vulnerability areas in
 the system.

 3.1.3 Oscillation excitability screening

 Consider a planner conducting a detailed risk analysis for a specific interconnection location. A
 key question is whether certain frequencies excitable from that location could lead to resonance
 with system modes. The LILCO study at Brookhaven National Laboratory provides a historical
 precedent: LILCO first performed a frequency-domain analysis using a state-space represen-
 tation of their system to identify frequencies of concern, then validated the findings through
 time-domain simulations (Rostamkolai et al., 1994). Building a state-space representation of a
 utility-scale system is difficult to automate and may need specialized commercial tools. RATLLE
 therefore takes a simulation-based approach: a short 0.5 second load impulse, with magnitude
 below the maximum allowable step determined in the previous screening stage, is applied at the
 candidate bus, and the subsequent ringdown response is analyzed to extract mode estimates.
 This simulation-based approach for analyzing system modes has also been utilized in prior art
 (WECC, 2021). To capture the range of conditions likely to be encountered during operations, it
 is recommended that this modal analysis be repeated across multiple seasonal planning cases
 and with relevant contingencies or local element outages represented.
 The automated ringdown analysis is conducted as described in Figure 6. First, the post-
 impulse voltage angle response is first-differenced, z-score normalized4 , and decimated to 15
 samples per second. This detrends the signal and removes high-frequency components. Next,
 dominant modal frequencies are identified via a zero-padded FFT5 – peaks indicate potential
 modes. If narrow peaks are visible in the frequency spectrum of the ringdown signal, then
 underdamped modes are likely to be present. If prominent peaks are not observed, then the
 location does not strongly excite any system modes.
 Modal parameters are estimated by applying a nonlinear curve fitting technique whose ac-
 curacy is enhanced using information from an initial FFT analysis of the ringdown signal. This
 involves constructing a Hankel matrix from the ringdown signal. The matrix rank (estimated us-
 ing singular value decomposition or SVD) is used to determine the number of damped sinusoids
 needed to represent the signal. The parameter fit itself is performed using Variable Projection
 (VARPRO), a separable nonlinear least-squares method in which the linear amplitude and phase
 coefficients are solved analytically at each iteration, reducing the nonlinear search to the natural
 frequency and damping ratio of each mode (Borden and Lesieutre, 2014). Fitting proceeds in
 two passes: the first fits the FFT-identified frequency seeds and subtracts the result to expose

 3.2 Module 2: Simulation
 The simulation module automates the execution of time-domain simulations based on user-
 specified parameters: the oscillation shape, frequency, and amplitude; and the bus at which
 the load perturbation is introduced. These parameters can be informed either by findings from
 the screening module or by prior knowledge of the planned load’s operational characteristics —
 for instance, a planner may already know the interconnection location and expected workload
 behavior of a prospective data center. Study configurations are passed to the module through
 a CSV file, allowing for straightforward setup and reproducibility. Each simulation run proceeds
 through a sequence of steps, as illustrated in Figure 8.

 3.2.1 Data center load representation

 The major load components in a typical data center include: (a) servers, (b) cooling load for re-
 moving heat dissipated in server racks, and (c) auxiliary load like lighting. They are equipped with
 uninterruptible power supplies (UPS) to protect voltage-sensitive equipment and may also have
 backup generation. Dynamic models that represent data centers as standard PQ or voltage-
 dependent loads fail to capture behavior like load ramps and delayed reconnection after grid
 disturbances. To overcome this, the NERC Load Modeling Working Group (LMWG) recom-
 mends utilizing the Power Electronic Reconnecting and Ceasing (PERC1) load model that is now
 available in the latest versions of leading commercial positive sequence simulators (PowerWorld
 Corporation, 2025). PERC1 is designed to represent aggregations of power electronic loads
 such as data centers, electric vehicle charging stations, and cryptocurrency mining facilities. The
 model captures voltage-dependent real and reactive power response, frequency droop behavior,
 and cease-and-reconnect logic that governs load disconnection and restoration following voltage
 disturbances. However, the current PERC1 load characteristic does not include an option to
 introduce cyclic load profiles representative of AI workloads.
 Studying the impact of forced oscillations in planning cases is relatively new to standard
 industry practice. Although prior industry recommendations, such as those arising from the NERC
 investigation of the 2019 Eastern Interconnection oscillation event, called for developing mature
 methods to model forced oscillations in commercial simulators (NERC, 2019), progress in this
 area remains limited. Earlier work has been largely confined to research settings, either for post-
 event investigation or for evaluating the performance of oscillation source localization algorithms
 through synthetic case studies (ENTSO-E Expert Panel, 2026; Joshi et al., 2026; Biswas et al.,

 2025). Modeling approaches have varied considerably, ranging from generator playback functions
 and user-defined models (UDM) that inject periodic disturbances into governor mechanical power
 or excitation controls (Maslennikov and Wang, 2022; NERC, 2021), to Python-scripted periodic
 adjustments of plant controller setpoints (Biswas et al., 2025). To represent AI workloads, RATLLE
 uses a decoupled load representation as shown in Figure 9. The data center is modeled as two
 load components: Load1 (id: ll), representing the constant component of demand, and Load2
 (id: os), representing the cyclic oscillatory component.
 In the planning model, the simulation module first modifies any existing load at the user-
 specified high-voltage (HV) bus to represent a data center. A medium-voltage (MV) bus connected
 to the HV bus through a step-down transformer is added. Default transformer parameters are
 used, as they are not expected to significantly influence oscillatory behavior, though planners
 with detailed knowledge of the interconnection can override these values. The HV load is moved
 to the MV bus and its dynamic characteristics are represented using the composite load model
 (CMLD) with typical data center parameters recommended by the NERC LMWG, as shown in
 Tables 2 and 3 (NERC Load Modeling Working Group, 2025). The PERC1 model would be a
 more natural fit for representing data center loads; however, the WECC planning cases available
 forstudies were only compatible with older versions of PSS/E where PERC1 was not available.
 CMLD was therefore adopted as a pragmatic alternative. Since the tool is modular, the CMLD
 representation can be readily replaced with PERC1 or any other user-defined model at the user’s
 discretion.
 The cyclic load component is represented by a separate constant-P load (Load2) initialized
 to zero, whose active power is varied periodically during dynamic simulation using PSS/E’s
 load_change_6 command. The grid effectively sees the superposition of the oscillatory load per-
 turbation and the CMLD dynamic response. This representation is an approximation — it does not
 capture all the complexity of real data center behavior — but it is well-suited for planning-stage
 risk assessment, where the goal is to characterize the grid’s sensitivity to oscillatory demand
 rather than to replicate device-level load dynamics with high fidelity. The approach is consistent
 with the reportedly observed behavior of AI training loads, where a cyclic oscillation is overlaid
 on a base consumption level.

 3.2.2 Selecting output channels

 Before running dynamic simulations, RATLLE selects the output channels to be recorded. This
 choice involves a practical tradeoff: too few channels risk missing the impact of oscillations on
 elements outside the monitored set, while too many channels slow down the simulation and
 produce large output files that are difficult to analyze. The default channel selection logic is
 designed to capture both local and wide-area oscillation impact, and can be refined by the user
 based on observations from initial simulation runs to improve computational efficiency. By default,
 RATLLE records the following elements:

 1. In-service loads greater than 50 MW in the source area (bus voltage magnitude, active and
 reactive power injections)
 2. In-service buses above 100 kV in the source area (bus voltage magnitude and angle)
 3. In-service generator buses with aggregated generation above 50 MW in the source area
 (generator terminal voltage, rotor angle, active and reactive power injections)

Methodology 15

 4. In-service transmission lines above 100 kV in the source area (bus voltage magnitudes, active
 and reactive power flows)
 5. All in-service inter-area tie lines (bus voltage magnitudes, active and reactive power flows)

 3.2.3 Cyclic load behavior

 Having fixed what will be observed, the final modeling step is simulating the oscillatory computa-
 tional workloads. Cyclic load behavior is simulated by periodically changing the load value using
 PSS/E’s load_change_6 command (v35 and above), which modifies load as a constant power
 characteristic. Users can specify the oscillation shape, frequency, amplitude, and start time. By
 default, RATLLE sets the dynamic simulation runtime to accommodate eight complete load cy-
 cles at the specified frequency. Two oscillation shapes are supported, as illustrated in Figure 10:
 monoperiodic square waves and bi-periodic pulse trains. In the bi-periodic representation, slower
 variations in the 0.1–1 Hz range reflect rest-to-compute transitions, while faster fluctuations in
 the 5–30 Hz range within compute phases arise from the rapid switching of individual processing
 modules. The default duty cycle parameter is set at 0.5, but can be modified by users.
 Although the approach described above approximates cyclic AI workloads reasonably well,
 some limitations remain. The constant power load characteristic, while representative of com-
 pute load behavior, can introduce numerical instabilities during fault simulations. Additionally,
 repeated interaction with the simulation environment through Python scripting increases run-
 time. To address these limitations, PNNL has developed companion PSS/E user-defined models
 (UDMs) that embed the oscillatory load behavior directly in the dynamic simulation and can be
 used for standalone scenario simulations outside the RATLLE framework. The UDMs provide
 additional capabilities including triangular load variations and the ability to play back arbitrary
 load profiles, and planned work includes migrating the simulation automation in RATLLE from the
 API-based load-changing approach to the UDMs. A full description of the model, its parameters,
 and validation against the script-based approach is provided in Appendix A.

 3.3 Module 3: Analysis
 As illustrated in Figure 11, the analysis module processes simulation outputs to characterize the
 impact of a user-selected forced oscillation scenario. To quantify impact, a set of risk metrics
 is formulated that captures effects on equipment wear and tear as well as the likelihood of
 protection and control misoperations. Oscillation propagation within the load source area is
 assessed through the response of major generators and transmission lines, while spread outside
 the area is captured through tie-line flows. These observables are evaluated against risk metric

Methodology 16

 Figure 11: Process flow in the simulation and analysis modules

 thresholds to identify elements of concern: the greater the number of flagged elements, the higher
 the risk posed by the oscillation scenario under study.
 Elements that significantly exceed the risk threshold also serve as an information proxy for
 vulnerability — these are the locations where protection and control misoperations are likely, and
 where the impact of contingencies is most likely to be exacerbated by the presence of forced
 oscillations. For example, consider a heavily loaded transmission line carrying large MW oscil-
 lations. An N-1 contingency that reroutes additional power through that line may simultaneously
 increase oscillation amplitude, potentially leading to thermal overloads, relay actions, and cascad-
 ing failures — even though the base system is N-1 secure under normal conditions. Prescribing
 oscillation amplitude limits at the load site is therefore essential to prevent such compounding
 failures during operations.
 Assessing the impact of sustained oscillations on power grid reliability is an inherently qual-
 itative problem, as the severity of oscillations depends on their duration, the elements affected,
 prevailing operating conditions, and whether alarms are triggered. A conceptual three-stage
 severity scale, illustrated in Figure 12, captures this range of impacts and serves as the guiding
 principle for the risk metrics formulated in this work. These metrics represent one of the first
 attempts to quantify bulk power system reliability in the context of forced oscillations, and the
 severity scale described herein extends naturally beyond the planning domain that is the primary
 focus of this work.
 Stage 1 in the severity scale maps to small oscillations below control room alarm thresholds
 that may go undetected for extended periods. Despite their low amplitude, prolonged oscillations
 can degrade reliability through cumulative equipment fatigue, accelerated aging of battery energy
 storage systems (BESS) through micro-cycling, and operator desensitization - all of which erode
 the system’s capacity to respond to subsequent disturbances. Stage 2 represents a transition to
 active system stress, where oscillation magnitudes rise to levels that may trigger control room
 alarms. At this stage, the system becomes increasingly vulnerable to hidden failures and device
 misoperations, and the risk of incorrect mitigation actions by operators or generator owners
 compounds the hazard. Contingencies may be exacerbated by the presence of oscillations -
 threatening N-1 security. Stage 3 characterizes imminent collapse, where a triggering event
 precipitates rapid failure propagation with insufficient time for operator intervention. Sustained
 oscillations frequently expose latent vulnerabilities (e.g., relay misoperations, faulty control logic)
 that can precipitate cascading failures (ENTSO-E Expert Panel, 2026). Such hidden failures are
 most consequential when operating reserves are low, with the worst case being a widespread
 blackout.

 The risk metrics in the following section operationalize this severity scale as follows. Some
 thresholds are deliberately set below typical control room alarm levels; a threshold crossing
 therefore indicates that an element has entered Stage 1 — oscillations large enough to drive
 cumulative wear, headroom erosion, or repeated control excursions, yet likely to escape operator
 attention. Crossings at levels comparable to control room alarm settings or the NERC SMWG
 reportability criteria (e.g., line swings above 20 MW, voltages outside operating bands) indicate
 Stage 2, where misoperation risk becomes active and contingencies may be exacerbated. Stage
 3 is not assessed through a static threshold: it is established by overlaying credible contingencies
 on the active oscillation scenario and observing whether element trips or cascading failures result,
 as demonstrated in Chapter 4.0. Each metric in Tables 4 and 5 is accordingly tagged with the
 severity stage(s) it primarily indicates.

 ment damage, or cascading outages; caused a generation loss of at least 50 MW; or exhibited

 The RATLLE framework approximates the likelihood of protection operations as follows:

 1. Voltage and frequency deviations are analyzed to assess the likelihood of under/overvoltage
 and under/overfrequency protection operations.

 2. Zone 3 distance relay misoperations are among the protection events most likely to precipitate
 cascading trips, yet Zone 3 relays are frequently absent from planning cases. RATLLE
 therefore post-processes simulation outputs to compute the impedance trajectory for selected
 lines, estimate likely Zone 3 reach settings, and determine whether the Zone 3 element would
 have picked up. Generic reach settings are derived from NERC loadability criteria (maximum
 permissible reach before load encroachment): Zone 3 relays must not operate when a circuit
 is loaded at 150% of its highest seasonal facility rating (Rate B), with loadability evaluated
 at 0.85 p.u. voltage and a 30◦ power factor angle (NERC System Protection and Control
 Working Group, 2025). If the computed impedance trajectory enters the relay characteristic,
 a misoperation is considered likely. The intended workflow is for the user to identify heavily
 loaded lines with significant power swings via the RATLLE risk visualization dashboard, then
 estimate Zone 3 misoperation likelihood using the distance protection subroutine.
 It must be acknowledged that since RATLLE uses the maximum permissible reach philosophy,
 the estimated relay reach is very likely an overestimate. Actual relay reach settings would
 be lower.

 4.0 Case Studies
 This chapter illustrates the RATLLE risk assessment pipeline using two system models: the
 WECC 240-bus reduced model (Yuan et al., 2020) and the WECC 2031 Heavy Winter planning
 case. Although findings from the 240-bus model do not directly translate to the actual WECC
 system, it serves as a useful medium for demonstrating the methodology. The model is publicly
 available, so all results presented here can be independently reproduced. Moreover, its modest
 size allows new users to familiarize themselves with the RATLLE script suite before applying it
 to larger operational models. For simulations in the WECC planning model, element identifiers
 are anonymized.
 All simulations are carried out using the Siemens PTI PSS/E platform - version 34 and 35
 for the interconnection-scale and reduced models respectively. A few Python API commands

 4.2 WECC Planning Model
 The 2031 Heavy Winter planning model used comprises about 160 GW load, with ∼ 12.5%
 generation contributed by non-synchronous resources. The RATLLE pipeline was applied to the
 WECC model in the following manner. First, the voltage and angle sensitivity screening steps
 identified the 20 highest-vulnerability buses, several of which were spatially clustered. Next,
 load impulses were applied at these locations, and their ringdown response was analyzed to
 identify excitable modes. Buses from which lightly-damped modes were excitable were selected
 for detailed time-domain simulation. The intent was to efficiently identify interesting case studies,
 rather than to comprehensively characterize risk across the interconnection. This process helped
 identify two interesting cases that are discussed next. The case studies presented demonstrate
 the range of outcomes the RATLLE framework can help analyze - one in which a contingency
 converts comparatively local forced oscillations into cascading failure, and one in which wide-area
 propagation occurs but reliability risks remain contained.

 4.2.1 Example 1: N-1 security constraint violation

 The first example is for a location in California. Modal analysis identified two excitable frequencies
 from this location — at approximately 1.15 and 1.7 Hz (Figure 20), both with damping ratios below
 10%. Based on this result, square wave 50-MW load perturbations were introduced at 1.15 Hz.
 This scenario led to risk threshold violations on several system elements, as summarized in
 Table 6. The relatively modest 50-MW swings had a pronounced impact: four monitored trans-
 mission lines experienced active power swings exceeding 10% of their thermal rating (Rate A),
 nine generators had active power swings above 3% of their MVA ratings, and two generators had
 reactive power swings exceeding 3% of their rated MVA capacity. The highest active and reactive
 power swings recorded on any single generator were approximately 13.1 MW and 12.6 MVar (on
 a 134 MVA machine), respectively, while the highest line power swing reached approximately
 185 MW (line normal rating 296 MVA).

 behavior. As shown in Figure 24, the line trip was followed by growing oscillations in both
 Generator A and B, with Generator B again tripped by undervoltage protection at approximately
 nine seconds. After Generator B was lost, growing oscillations ceased in Generator A’s response,
 though forced oscillations persisted in the system.
 In the base case without oscillations, neither contingency caused overloads or element trips
 — the system was N-1 secure. The introduction of 50 MW forced oscillations from the load bus
 changed this: the same contingencies now led to cascading generator loss, constituting a violation
 of the N-1 security criterion. In severity-scale terms, the pre-contingency oscillation condition
 corresponds to Stage 2 — multiple alarm-level threshold crossings — while the contingency
 overlay demonstrates Stage 3 conditionsan: a triggering event producing cascading generator
 loss. The potential of a N-1 security constraint violation yields a physically meaningful and
 operationally grounded way to set acceptable oscillation limits: load oscillations must not, under
 any credible contingency, precipitate element trips or cascading failures that would not have
 occurred in their absence.

 5.0 Implications and Future Directions
 The computational workloads of hyperscale AI data centers present a forced oscillation risk that
 existing planning frameworks were not designed to address. This report has developed and
 demonstrated RATLLE, a structured, automatable methodology for evaluating that risk in bulk
 power systems. Chapter 2 established the physical basis for concern — the conditions under
 which large cyclic loads can excite natural system modes, drive wide-area oscillation propagation,
 and interact with protection and control systems in ways that standard transient stability analysis
 does not capture. Chapter 3 described the three-module RATLLE pipeline, from system-wide
 sensitivity screening through time-domain simulation to risk metric computation, and outlined the
 modeling choices and limitations inherent in applying these methods to interconnection-scale
 planning cases. A framework for quantifying system reliability risk due to the presence of sus-
 tained oscillations is proposed. The case studies in Chapter 4 then grounded these concepts
 in simulation results on both a reduced test system and a detailed WECC planning model, pro-
 ducing findings with direct implications for how interconnection studies should be conducted and
 how oscillation amplitude limits should be set. The remainder of this chapter synthesizes those
 findings and identifies directions for future development.

 5.1 Key Takeaways
 1. Forced oscillations from large loads are a credible planning-stage reliability risk.
 The California case study in Section 4.2.1 illustrates that 50 MW of oscillatory demand, well
 within the range of plausible AI workload fluctuations, can trigger N-1 security constraint violations
 that would not otherwise occur. An operating condition deemed secure under standard contin-
 gency analysis can become failure-prone when sustained oscillations are superimposed. This
 has direct implications for planning practice: system studies involving hyperscale facilities should
 assess N-1 security not only under steady-state or transient conditions but also across represen-
 tative forced-oscillation scenarios. There is also an opportunity to develop automated methods
 for identifying the contingencies most susceptible to N-1 constraint violations in the presence of
 sustained oscillations, so that exhaustive analyses do not need duplication.
 Nonetheless, reaching consensus on the specific modifications needed in current study prac-
 tices remains an open challenge. Planners may have limited visibility into facility workload char-
 acteristics at the interconnection stage; those profiles can evolve over the load’s operational
 lifetime; and as new resources are integrated into the grid, system modes may shift themselves
 — potentially rendering earlier interconnection study conclusions outdated.
 2. Reliability risk is frequency-dependent and location-dependent.
 The screening module results and case studies together reinforce that oscillation risk is not
 simply a function of load magnitude. A 50 MW oscillation at a frequency coinciding with a lightly
 damped local mode, from a location with high mode shape magnitude, can produce larger system-
 wide impact than a 100 MW oscillation at an off-resonance frequency from a weaker coupling
 point. Utilities and hyperscale facility operators can work together to understand if modifying
 the facility workload characteristics can help in accommodating higher oscillation amplitudes - a
 historical precedent is provided by the LILCO experience with interconnecting the Brookhaven
 synchrotron described in Chapter 1.
 3. Inter-area mode excitation warrants attention even when modes are well-damped.
 The Wyoming–Colorado example in Section 4.2.2 shows that forced oscillations can drive
 wide-area propagation, with impacted elements visible across multiple transmission areas, even
 when the excited mode is well-damped. The system remained N-1 secure in this case, and os-

Implications and Future Directions 33

 cillation amplitudes did not grow, suggesting the immediate reliability risk was limited. However,
 the geographic spread of impacted elements underscores that persistent oscillations from the
 interconnected facility would be observed by multiple transmission operators, requiring coordina-
 tion across organizations. These results suggest that wide-area risk exposure from AI workloads
 warrants evaluation.
 4. The N-1 security criterion provides a principled basis for setting oscillation amplitude
 limits.
 A persistent challenge in this domain, acknowledged in NERC LLWG’s white papers, is the
 lack of quantitative standards for permissible forced oscillation amplitudes. The case studies
 in this report suggest a physically grounded approach: oscillation amplitude limits at the load
 interconnection point should be set such that no credible contingency, applied during an active
 oscillation scenario, produces overloads, voltage limit violations, element trips, or cascading
 failures that would not occur in the oscillation’s absence. This criterion avoids the need to define
 oscillation limits in isolation from system conditions. Translating this principle into a systematic
 limit-setting procedure — accounting for seasonal operating conditions, contingency selection,
 and uncertainty in load frequency content — is an important direction for future work.
 5. Planning model limitations constrain but do not invalidate simulation-based risk as-
 sessment.
 As discussed in Section 2.2, interconnection-scale planning models may not fully capture
 protection systems, remedial action schemes, or the dynamic characteristics of all load types.
 The RATLLE framework partially compensates through post-processing — estimating Zone 3
 relay reach, assessing RAS arming likelihood, and flagging undervoltage and underfrequency
 conditions — but these approximations carry uncertainty. The appropriate response is not to
 forgo simulation-based assessment, but to treat its outputs as conservative indicators that flag
 elements and scenarios warranting closer examination, rather than definitive predictions of pro-
 tection system behavior. Where PMU data is available near candidate interconnection locations,
 comparison against simulated ringdown responses can improve confidence in modal estimates
 and partially validate the planning model’s dynamic representation.
 6. Methodology standardization remains the central challenge.
 The LILCO synchrotron study of the 1990s demonstrated that rigorous site-specific impact
 analysis is feasible for oscillations emerging from individual large cyclic loads. What has changed
 is scale: utilities are now processing hundreds of hyperscale interconnection requests from load
 owners who have limited visibility into the grid conditions that govern oscillation risk. The RATLLE
 framework is designed to address this scalability problem by providing a repeatable, automatable
 workflow that can be integrated into existing interconnection study processes. The scripts are
 publicly available and documented, and the modular architecture allows utilities to adopt compo-
 nents selectively or substitute their own modeling approaches where needed. Broader adoption
 will require not only tooling but also the development of industry consensus around study pro-
 cedures, reporting requirements, and acceptable risk thresholds — work that is underway in the
 broad industry and that the methodology presented here is intended to inform.

 5.2 Future Directions
 The findings presented in this report suggest several natural extensions to the RATLLE framework
 that the authors aim to pursue.
 RATLLE currently simulates oscillations injected from a single large load location at a time. As
 hyperscale AI facilities continue to proliferate, analyzing the impact of synchronized workloads
 across multiple facilities owned by the same operator becomes increasingly relevant. Extending

Implications and Future Directions 34

 the platform to simulate such multi-site synchronized demand patterns, and characterizing their
 aggregate impact on the grid, is an area of active development.
 The authors aim to add an automated contingency selection module for identifying which
 contingencies are most likely to produce N-1 violations under active oscillation conditions. This
 would replace the current ad hoc approach and make the risk assessment more systematic and
 reproducible. On the platform front, RATLLE currently uses PSS/E as the underlying simulation
 engine; expanding to other widely used platforms such as PSLF, PowerWorld, and TSAT will
 broaden applicability across the industry. Feedback from early users has also identified several
 visualization improvements to the risk assessment dashboard that the authors plan to incorporate.
 The risk quantification framework and associated thresholds in the current work are empirically
 derived, and further calibration against industry experience and historical oscillation events is
 required to build confidence in their general applicability.
 Finally, while the current focus has been on the planning stage, opportunities exist to extend
 these findings to the operations timeframe. The risk quantification framework and associated case
 studies can inform operator guidance — specifically, helping define when sustained oscillations
 are severe enough to warrant immediate action as opposed to a monitoring posture.

 Appendix A – User-Defined Model for Oscillatory Load
 As described in Chapter 3.0, the RATLLE simulation module introduces cyclic load behavior by
 repeatedly modifying load values through PSS/E’s ‘load_change_6’ command. This script-based
 approach treats the load variation as an externally imposed sequence of discrete events, with
 the simulation stopped, modified, and advanced at each step. It is well-suited to studies involving
 oscillations at a few locations, but scales poorly when many widely distributed loads must be
 represented or when complex oscillatory waveforms are required across a large system.
 To address this, a user-defined model (UDM) has been developed in PSS/E that injects a
 forced oscillation, reproducing the cyclic profile of AI training workloads. Unlike the scripted ap-
 proach, the UDM computes the load variation internally as a function of simulation time. The
 waveform logic therefore remains synchronized with the dynamic solver and requires no inter-
 ruption of the simulation run.

 A.1 Capabilities
 The UDM provides the following options:

 • Load characteristic: constant power, constant current, or constant impedance. Although
 data centers are physically constant-power loads, the constant-current and constant-impedance
 representations are useful under fault or weak-grid conditions, where constant-power loads
 can introduce numerical instabilities.
 • Oscillation waveform: mono-periodic, bi-periodic, triangular, or arbitrary play-in profiles.

 Input parameters are supplied through the model’s DYR data and are described in Table A.1.
 Time-domain examples of the parameterized load profiles are shown in Figure A.1.

 A.2 Implementation
 The model is implemented in Fortran and compiled into a DLL using the Intel Fortran compiler
 within the PSS/E Environment Manager, after which it is available for dynamic simulation. It has
 been developed and tested in PSS/E versions 34, 35, and 36.

 A.3 Validation
 Figure A.2 compares the load profiles produced by the UDM for a square-wave oscillation under
 the three load characteristics. As the zoomed view in Figure A.3 shows, the constant-current and
 constant-impedance cases do not hold a flat peak amplitude, since the power drawn depends
 on the voltage at the load terminals — in contrast to the constant-power case. Figure A.4 com-
 pares the UDM against the Python API-based scripting approach and shows close agreement,
 confirming that the UDM reproduces the intended behavior.
