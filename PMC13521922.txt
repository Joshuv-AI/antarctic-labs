Dynamic remodeling of the pancreas immune landscape in obesity
Authors: Alexey Koshkin, Kranthi Kiran Kishore Tanagala, Anna Eichinger, Michael Chait, Aoife Young, Shanila Shakil, Junichi Yoshikawa, Yosuke Sakamoto, Steven B. Wells, Ladan Fazlollahi, Xiaojuan Chen, Boris Reizis, Donna L. Farber, Stuart P. Weisberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obesity is a known risk factor for diseases of the pancreas, including diabetes, pancreatic cancer and pancreatitis, but mechanisms remain unclear. Here we show by spatial, transcriptomic and functional profiling of human pancreatic immune cells from obese and non-obese organ donors that obesity profoundly impacts pancreatic immune homeostasis. Obesity is associated with higher density of tissue resident memory T-cells (TRM) in the exocrine pancreas which are characterized by high cytotoxic functions, and aggregate around macrophages. Single cell sequencing of pancreatic macrophages distinguishes two main subsets - FOLR2 + CD11c- foetal-derived macrophages with pro-repair and immunoregulatory function and FOLR2- CD11c+ lipid-associated macrophages with greater T-cell interactions and pro-inflammatory function. In obesity, the pancreatic macrophage landscape shifts to lower predominance of FOLR2 + CD11c- macrophages and expansion of FOLR2- CD11c+ macrophages, which interact selectively with the TRM and inflamed exocrine epithelium. Together, these results identify macrophage-T cell circuits and immune epithelial interactions that might lead to chronic pancreatic inflammation in obesity and might contribute to obesity-related pancreatic diseases.

Introduction

Obesity affects >40% of adults in the United States and drives chronic inflammation, a root cause of many diseases. The pancreas, which plays a central role in digestion, metabolic sensing and control, is highly sensitive to obesity. Inflammation in the exocrine pancreas increases the risk for pancreatitis and pancreatic cancer— debilitating diseases with few treatment options1–7. In obese mouse models, cytokines derived from macrophages can cause dysfunction of insulin-secreting endocrine islets, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of type 2 diabetes (T2D)8. Relative to the large burden of obesity-related pancreatic diseases, very little is known about the mechanisms by which obesity influences pancreatic immune regulation.

Macrophages are the predominant immune cell lineage in most tissues and play key roles in controlling tissue homeostasis and inflammation. Tissue macrophages include lineages derived from fetal and adult hematopoiesis, and mouse models have shown that the exocrine pancreas contains an equal mixture of fetal- and adult monocyte-derived macrophages9,10. Mouse studies in diverse organs, including heart, brain and pancreas, indicate that fetal-derived macrophages have homeostatic and repair functions, whereas monocyte-derived macrophages are implicated in pathologic tissue remodeling and inflammation11–16. In obesity, specialized lipid-associated macrophages in adipose tissue, atherosclerotic lesions and the liver help to process excess lipids, yet they also contribute to chronic inflammation17–20. The macrophage populations in the human pancreas and their responses to obesity are not well defined.

Most tissues also contain tissue resident memory T-cells (TRM)-derived from the effector memory T-cells (TEM) of primary immune responses—which have specialized tissue adaptations and site-specific functions21,22. In gastrointestinal organs, TRM are long-lived guardians against secondary infections, provide immunosurveillance against tumors and interface with innate immune cells to boost tissue immune responses23,24. In addition to providing localized immune protection, TRM balance their cytotoxic and effector functions with immunoregulatory functions to avoid excessive inflammation and collateral tissue damage25. Indeed, failure of TRM regulation is implicated in many gastrointestinal inflammatory diseases26,27.

We previously showed that T cells in the human pancreas are predominantly TRM and exhibit distinct phenotypes, robust effector functions, and transcriptional profiles compared to other GI and lymphoid sites using an organ donor tissue resource that has enabled numerous groundbreaking studies of human tissue immunity22,25,27,28. Importantly, we found that pancreatic TRM maintain close in situ interactions with pancreatic macrophages, which express checkpoint and co-stimulatory ligands that maintain TRM in a poised but restrained state27. Our access to organ donor tissues and our optimization of immune cell recovery has enabled studies that integrate demographic and anthropomorphic parameters with tissue immune profiling29,30.

The effect of obesity on the immune landscape of the human pancreas remains poorly defined. Here, we integrate spatial, transcriptomic and functional analysis of pancreatic immune cells across a cohort of 68 adult organ donors spanning a wide range of body mass index. We find that a higher body mass index is associated with accumulation of cytotoxic TRM in the exocrine pancreas and a shift in the macrophage compartment, in which immunoregulatory fetal-derived macrophages give way to pro-inflammatory monocyte-derived macrophages that selectively engage these T-cells and inflamed epithelium. These findings reveal macrophage–T-cell circuits that may sustain chronic pancreatic inflammation, offering a mechanistic framework for understanding how obesity predisposes to pancreatic disease and pointing toward immune-directed strategies to preserve pancreatic health.

Results

Increased density of TRM and macrophages in human pancreas during obesity

We found that BMI and age are independent, and additive predictors of increased intrapancreatic fat in accordance with previous studies (Fig. 1A–C and Table S1)38. To define how obesity impacts the immune landscape of the exocrine and endocrine pancreas, we performed multiplexed imaging on pancreas tissue sections from 32 of these organ donors (“Multiplex imaging cohort 1,” age range 18–71 years, median age, 52 years) (Fig. S1C). The BMI range in the cohort was 21-47 kg/m2, median 33 kg/m2, 62.5% of donors were obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2), and 28% had T2D.

Consistent with our previous results, macrophages were the highest density immune lineage across pancreas, followed by CD8 T-cells, and CD4 T-cells showed the lowest densities27 (Figs. 1D, E, and S1D–F). In the non-obese donors, macrophage and T-cell densities were highest in acinar areas, as compared to ductal, and lowest in islets, defined by the strict boundary between chromogranin+ and chromogranin− cells (Figs. 1D, E, and S1D–F). In obesity, CD4 and CD8 T-cells were increased in acinar areas (Fig. S1E, F) and were positively correlated with donor BMI by simple linear regression (Fig. 1E), whereas acinar macrophage densities were not significantly altered in obesity (Figs. 1E and S1D). Acinar CD8 T-cells, but not macrophages or CD4 T-cells, were also significantly positively correlated with intrapancreatic fat (Fig. S1G–I). Ductal areas showed markedly increased CD8 T-cell densities in obese donors (Figs. 1D, E, and S1E), and BMI was positively correlated with ductal CD8 T-cell density but not with ductal macrophages or CD4 T-cells (Fig. 1E). In islets, macrophages were increased in obese donors and positively correlated with donor BMI (Figs. S1D and 1E). The relationships of pancreatic immune cells with BMI were also found to be statistically significant by multivariable analysis after adjustment for known pancreas disease risk modifiers, including age, T2D, and sex (Tables S2 and S3). In the multivariable models, and by subgroup analysis, T2D had a slight negative influence on acinar CD4 T-cells but otherwise did not have a significant association with pancreatic immune cells (Tables S3 and S1D–F). Thus, obesity is associated with distinct immune cell compositional changes in exocrine and endocrine pancreas, with T-cell accumulation most prominent in exocrine pancreas and macrophage accumulation most prominent in islets.

We assessed BMI-related changes in T-cell subset distribution by flow cytometry. Markers of tissue residency (e.g., CD103) on CD45RA− CCR7− TEM cells (Figs. 1F and S1J) delineate TRM that are retained in, and specially adapted to, the tissue microenvironment27,40. To this end, pancreatic T-cell subset distributions were analyzed in 22 adult donors with BMI of 21–47 kg/m2. CD8+ CD103+ TRM were the predominant pancreatic T-cell subset in obese and non-obese donors (Fig. 1F), and TRM subset proportions did not correlate with BMI (Figs. 1F and S1K). Moreover, no BMI-related changes were observed in the minor populations of naïve (CD45RA+ CCR7+) and central memory (CD45RA− CCR7+) T-cells (Figs. 1F and S1K). Thus, the increased T-cell density in pancreas tissue found in obesity did not alter T-cell subset distributions, with CD8 TRM as the predominant pancreatic T-cell subtype.

Pancreatic macrophages, identified as CD14+ CD163+, expressed high levels of CD64 consistent with our previous work27 (Figs. S1J, L, and 1G), also expressed high levels of TGF-β (Fig. S1M), a master regulator of TRM41–43, and they showed heterogeneity in expression of the pro-inflammatory marker CD11c (Fig. 1G). The CD11chigh macrophage subset had the highest surface levels of CD58, a T-cell adhesion and co-stimulation molecule44 (Fig. 1H), and the antigen presentation molecule MHC-II (Fig. S1N) compared to CD11clow macrophages. Moreover, CD58 expression on CD11chigh macrophages positively correlated with donor BMI (Fig. 1I). Thus, CD11chigh pancreatic macrophages express molecules that promote T-cell residency and activation, including CD58, which is elevated in obesity.

T-cells aggregate around CD11chigh macrophages in the exocrine pancreas of obese donors

We further analyzed the spatial orientation of T-cells and macrophages in the pancreas by defining 7 “neighborhoods” (NH), each with distinct enrichment of spatially associated cell lineages (Fig. S2C). Non-immune cell NH included those with high density of chromogranin+ endocrine cells (NH4) and CK19+ ductal cells (NH1, 5, and 6) (Fig. 2I)45. Pancreatic macrophages were enriched in NH2 and 3 (Fig. 2I). The CD11chigh macrophages, along with CD4 and CD8 T-cells and CD11clow macrophages, were most strongly enriched in NH2 (Fig. 2I), and NH2 frequency uniquely correlated with donor BMI (Fig. 2J, K). Thus, T-cells accumulate as discrete foci clustering around CD11chigh macrophages in the exocrine pancreas of obese donors.

Pancreatic TRM express transcriptome signatures of high cytolytic effector function

We analyzed tissue-specific transcriptomic signatures of pancreatic TRM by performing cellular indexing of transcriptomes and epitopes by sequencing (CITE-seq) on T-cells purified from the pancreas, pancreas draining lymph nodes (PLN), small intestine, and spleen of adult organ donors (Fig. S3A). Cluster analysis showed substantial T-cell transcriptomic variation associated with differentiation state and anatomic site (Figs. 3A and S3B). Clusters with low effector molecule expression (e.g., TBX21, GZMA, and CCL5) (Fig. S3C) showed high expression of CCR7, the lymphoid tissue homing receptor (Fig. 3B), and transcription factor TCF7 (Fig. S3C), consistent with naïve and central memory T-cells (clusters #4, 9, and 10); and these were predominately derived from lymph node and spleen (Fig. S3B). Most cells in the remaining clusters expressed CD8 and high levels of CD103, consistent with TRM (Fig. 3B). These TRM were separated into two distinct tissue specific groups: those derived from pancreas (clusters 0, 2, and 5) and those derived from small intestine (clusters 1, 3, 6, 7, and 11; Figs. 3A and S3B). In pancreas-enriched TRM clusters, there were 523 differentially expressed genes (DEG). Transcripts with higher expression in the pancreas TRM were enriched in pathways related to chemokine signaling, and cytotoxic T cell function. Transcripts with lower expression in pancreatic TRM were enriched in pathways related to immunoregulation (Table S4)46. Unique chemokine receptor profiles also distinguished the pancreatic TRM, including high expression of the CXCL17 receptor, GPR2547, and the CXCL16 receptor, CXCR648, and low expression of the CCR9 intestinal T-cell homing receptor49 (Fig. 3B, C). Pancreatic TRM also showed the highest expression of transcripts implicated in cytotoxic function, including the ZNF683 transcription factor50, multiple effector molecules (i.e., PRF1, GZMA, GZMB, and FASLG) and the pro-inflammatory, pro-tumorigenic IL32 cytokine51 (Fig. 3B, C). In addition, the transcript encoding CD2, the T-cell adhesion and co-stimulatory receptor for CD58, showed the highest expression in pancreatic TRM (Fig. 3C). Conversely, multiple transcripts involved in negative regulation of T-cell function were markedly decreased in pancreatic TRM, including TNFAIP3 and NFKBIA, which encode the NF-κB signaling inhibitors A20 and IκBα, NR3C1, encoding the glucocorticoid receptor, and glucocorticoid-induced genes (e.g., TSC22D3, ZFP36, and BTG1) (Figs. 3C and S3C). By contrast, the intestinal TRM showed markedly lower levels of cytotoxic and effector genes and higher expression of immunoregulation genes (Fig. 3C). Thus, pancreatic TRM express a unique transcriptome signature of pancreas-specific trafficking, macrophage adhesion, and high cytotoxic effector function.

Using the gene signatures that define pancreatic TRM, we also analyzed a publicly available dataset of immune cells from adipose tissue, which also becomes inflamed during obesity, including 11 lymphocyte populations across lean and obese human donors52. Adipose tissue CD8+ T cells (lyC02) are also increased in obesity52 and share core cytotoxic effector genes with pancreatic TRM (GZMB, GZMA, and PRF1) (Fig. S3D). In contrast to the pancreatic TRM, which show distinctly low expression of T cell negative regulation genes (Fig. 3C), the cytotoxic effector program in adipose tissue T-cells is associated with high expression of immunoregulatory genes (TNFAIP3, NFKBIA, NR3C1, and TSC22D3), and gene signature scoring confirmed high co-expression of the “cytotoxic function” and “negative regulation” modules in adipose tissue (Fig. S3D, E). Thus, pancreatic TRM have distinct molecular signatures of immune control as compared to adipose tissue T-cells.

We analyzed the expression of our CITE-seq derived pancreatic TRM gene signatures in a publicly available bulk RNA sequencing dataset of human pancreas tissue from adult organ donors (n = 331, age 21–70 years, BMI 17–35 kg/m2, GTEx v10 pancreas53). After adjustment for relevant covariates, we found BMI to be an independent positive predictor of a “pancreatic T-cell signature,” including pan-T-cell markers (e.g., CD3D, TRAC, and CD2) and pancreas TRM-specific transcripts (e.g., GPR25, CCR5, and CXCR6) and a “T cell cytotoxicity signature,” including the T-cell effector molecules highly expressed in pancreatic TRM (e.g., GZMB, PRF1, and FASLG) (Table S5). Other significant predictors of higher pancreatic T-cell signatures included smoking and T2D (Table S5), consistent with a previous report showing increased pancreatic CD8 T-cells in T2D54.

Pancreatic TRM show tissue-specific pro-inflammatory changes in obesity

Obesity-related gene expression changes were analyzed by bulk RNA sequencing in sorted CD8+ CD69+ TRM from pancreas, intestine, PLN, and spleen (Fig. S3F) from 6 non-obese (BMI 22.7–26.8 kg/m2) and 5 obese (BMI 34.5–46.9 kg/m2) organ donors, including 3 donors from a prior study (GSE135582)27. As we found previously, the TRM transcriptomes varied by tissue site (Fig. S3G)27. Principal component analysis (PCA) within tissue sites showed that pancreatic and intestinal TRM cluster by obesity status while TRM from the lymphoid tissues (PLN and spleen) do not cluster by obesity status (Fig. 3D). Differential gene expression analysis showed higher numbers of obesity-related DEGs in the pancreatic TRM (738 genes) compared to those from the other tissue sites (lymph node and spleen, 5 genes; intestine 402 genes). The pancreatic TRM DEGs that were increased in obesity showed the greatest enrichment in pathways related to interferon response (e.g., STAT3, MX1, MX2, and IFI30), inflammation (e.g., CSF3, ICAM1, and CXCR6), and T-cell effector function (e.g., PRF1, GZMB) (Fig. 3E and Table S6)46. In contrast, intestinal TRM DEGs that were increased in obesity have less enrichment in these pathways and some function as brakes on inflammation (e.g., TNFAIP3, DDX5855) (Fig. 3E). Thus, pancreatic TRM uniquely exhibit altered gene expression in obesity consistent with a pro-inflammatory state.

To determine if the increased density and gene expression changes of pancreas TRM in obesity are related to clonal expansion, TCR sequences were extracted from the RNA sequencing datasets56. Plotting the clonal diversity metric (i.e., the Chao1 index) against tissue site showed lower diversity in TRM from pancreas and small intestine compared to TRM from lymphoid tissues (i.e., PLN, spleen); TRM diversity in each tissue was unchanged in obese compared to non-obese donors. (Fig. 3F). In addition, plotting clonal diversity against age showed significant aging-related decline in clonal diversity for pancreatic (Fig. 3G) and lymphoid (Fig. 3H) TRM without any effect of BMI. Thus, the increased density of pancreatic TRM in obesity is not linked to clonal expansion.

A differentiation trajectory towards CD11chigh macrophages from infiltrating monocytes in human pancreas

To identify transcriptomic signatures underlying phenotypically distinct pancreatic macrophages, we performed CITE-seq from 5 organ donors without pancreatic disease (3 non-obese, 2 obese). Cluster analysis identified three main macrophage populations (Fig. 4A). (1) Cluster 0 showed extensive similarity with fetal-derived pancreatic macrophages in murine fate mapping studies15,16, including FOLR2, CD209, MRC1 (CD206), and LYVE1 (Figs. 4B, C, and S4A, B). These macrophages also express the highest levels of the efferocytosis molecules MERK, AXL, and GAS6, which play key roles in immunoregulation during tissue repair57 (Figs. 4B and S4B). (2) Monocytic macrophages (clusters 3, 5, and 6) selectively express the monocyte chemotaxis receptor, CCR2 (Figs. 4B, C, and S4B), along with genes associated with bone marrow derived monocytes (e.g., S100A12, FCN1, and LYZ58), monocyte migration into tissues (CCR1, SELL, and CD44), and the CD36 phagocytic receptor and fatty acid transporter (Figs. 4B and S4B). (3) CD11chigh macrophages (clusters 1, 2, and 4) have low expression of fetal-derived macrophage markers and monocytic markers (Figs. 4B, C, and S4B) and have high expression of the lipid-associated macrophage markers SPP1, TREM2, and CD9 (Figs. 4B, C, and S4A, B). Cluster 2 of CD11chigh macrophages is particularly enriched in genes associated with lysosome function and lipid metabolism (Fig. 4B and S4B). Clusters 1 and 4 of CD11chigh macrophages expressed the highest levels of CD58, and the pro-inflammatory marker, Podoplanin (PDPN)59–64 (Figs. 4B and S4B). Clusters of cycling macrophages contained both FOLR2+ (cluster 10) and CD11c+ (clusters 7–9) subsets (Fig. 4A–C). Pseudo-temporal trajectory inference was used to assess the lineage relationships of the FOLR2- macrophage subset (clusters #1–9) (Fig. S4C)65. Monocyte markers and transcripts involved in monocyte migration from blood into tissue (e.g., S100A9, CCR1, and CCR2) were highly expressed in cells at the beginning of the trajectory and declined with trajectory progression (Fig. S4C). In contrast, CD11c surface expression was increased over trajectory progression along with lipid-associated macrophage markers (e.g., TREM2, CD9, and SPP1), genes that promote T-cell activation (e.g., HLA-DRA, CD86, CCL4, and IL18), and cell cycle genes (Fig. S4C). These results provide a model for how infiltrating monocytic macrophages may undergo functional specialization in the pancreas, where increased surface expression of CD11c is linked to acquisition of lipid-associated macrophage markers, entry into the cell cycle, and increased capacity for T-cell activation.

We compared pancreatic macrophage gene signatures to those in the adipose tissue immune cells dataset by defining gene signatures for each of our three main pancreatic macrophage populations identified by CITE-seq (Fig. 4A–C) and scoring these signatures across adipose tissue macrophage clusters (Fig. S5A, B)52. Adipose tissue macrophage subsets that are increased in obesity (e.g., clusters myC10 and myC02) express high levels of lysosome and lipid-metabolism genes, similar to CD11c+ pancreatic macrophages (Fig. S5A). The distinct gene signatures of pancreatic macrophage subsets mapped to expected adipose tissue counterparts: the FOLR2+ CD11c− signature scored highest in M2 tissue-resident clusters (myC0, mean score = 1.71), the CD11c+ LAM-like signature to the adipose tissue LAM cluster (myC02, mean = 2.08), and the monocytic signature to metabolically activated adipose tissue macrophages (MMe, myC10, mean = 1.96) and classical monocytes (myC14) (Fig. S5B). These data indicate some parallels between the macrophage dynamics in obesity across pancreas and adipose tissues, including distinct tissue resident macrophages, continuous monocyte recruitment and in situ differentiation of LAMs.

To characterize the ligand–receptor landscape between pancreatic macrophage subsets and T cells, we applied CellChat66, identifying 645 significant interactions across 37 signaling pathways (Supplementary Data 2). Macrophages collectively signaled through a greater number of ligand-receptor interactions compared to T cells (78 outgoing interactions vs. 26), including CD58 and TGF-β (Fig. S6A, Tables S7 and S8). Signaling roles differed among the four macrophage subgroups: CD11chigh and cycling macrophages were the strongest net senders, monocytic macrophages were near-neutral, and fetal-derived macrophages were net receivers (Fig. S6A–C). Pathway-level analysis showed that CD11chigh macrophages delivered 2.6-fold stronger CD58–CD2 co-stimulation than fetal-derived macrophages (probability 0.041 vs. 0.016), reflecting their high CD58 expression (Fig. S6A and Supplementary Data 2).

We validated the subsets identified by CITE-seq using flow cytometry. Pancreatic macrophages (gating in Fig. S6D) from 5 non-obese and 8 obese organ donors were separated based on expression of FOLR2 (Fig. S6E). The FOLR2+ macrophages are predominately CD11c– and CCR2–, whereas the FOLR2– macrophages show a continuum of CD11c and CCR2 expression (Fig. 4D) where declining CCR2 is associated with increasing CD11c and CD58 (Fig. 4D, E). The FOLR2+ CCR2− macrophages expressed high levels of the CD206 and CD209 scavenger receptors and the checkpoint ligand PD-L1 (Fig. 4F–H) and had the lowest levels of CD58 (Fig. 4I). The FOLR2− CCR2high macrophages contained the highest levels of intracellular myeloperoxidase (MPO) (Figs. 4J and S6F)—a bone marrow myelopoiesis marker that is retained by monocytes and declines with macrophage differentiation67–71. Within the FOLR2− macrophage lineage, declining CCR2 (FOLR2− CCR2int, FOLR2− CCR2low) was associated with progressively higher CD11c (Fig. 4E) and CD58 (Fig. 4I) and progressive loss of MPO (Figs. 4J and S6F). Thus, pancreatic macrophages functionally segregate based on their ontogeny and differentiation in the pancreas, where FOLR2+ CD11c– phenotype indicates probable fetal-derived macrophages with immunoregulatory functions, and FOLR2– phenotype indicates monocyte-derived cells in which acquisition of CD11c and loss of CCR2 denotes pro-inflammatory specialization.

Altered pancreatic macrophage subset composition in obesity enhances TRM functions

Based on our analysis of pancreatic macrophage phenotypes and transcriptional states, we subdivided them based on FOLR2 and CD11c expression (Fig. 5A). Quadrant gating of the pancreatic macrophages (Figs. 5A and S6C) defined three dominant macrophage states, comprising >90% of pancreatic macrophages (Fig. 5A)—FOLR2− CD11c− macrophages expressed high CCR2 and low CD58 consistent with newly infiltrated monocyte-derived cells, FOLR2− CD11c+ macrophages expressed low CCR2 and high CD58 consistent with pro-inflammatory specialization; and FOLR2+ CD11c− macrophages expressed low CCR2 and CD58 consistent with fetal-derived macrophages (Fig. S7A). Correlation of the macrophage subset distribution with BMI showed that increasing BMI is correlated with decreased FOLR2+ CD11c− macrophages (Fig. 5B), increased FOLR2− CD11c+ macrophages (Fig. 5C), and no change in the FOLR2- CD11c- macrophages (Fig. S7B). We analyzed the CD11c+ macrophage gene signatures (Figs. 4B and S4C), including LAM markers (e.g., CD11c, TREM2, and APOE) and genes involved in T-cell interaction (e.g., CD58, IL18, and CCL4) in the GTEx dataset of adult organ donors53. After adjustment for relevant covariates including T2D, sex, and age, we found BMI to be a significant and independent positive predictor of the “CD11c+ macrophage” gene signature (Table S9), moreover in univariate analysis, the CD11c+ macrophage gene signature strongly correlated with the “total T-cell” and “cytotoxic” signatures (Fig. S7C, D). These results further support that pro-inflammatory macrophages infiltrate pancreas in human obesity and predict T cell abundance and cytotoxicity. We hypothesized that the altered pancreatic macrophage landscape in obesity can influence the function of TRM, which our previous work showed is shaped by pancreatic macrophages27. We examined the relationship of BMI with the functional profiles of sorted pancreatic TRM (Fig. S3F for gating TRM) after activation in autologous pancreatic macrophage co-culture (Fig. S7E for gating macrophages). In this setting, significant proportions of TRM showed concomitant CD107a degranulation and high expression of the Granzyme B cytotoxic molecule (CD107a+ GZMB+ cells, 21.5 ± 3.5%) (Fig. 5D) and interferon γ (CD107a+ IFN γ+ cells, 24.5 ± 3.2%) (Fig. 5E). The proportions of CD107a+ GZMB+ cells, but not of the CD107a+ IFN γ+ cells, significantly correlated with donor BMI by linear regression (Fig. 5D, E). This enhanced cytotoxic function of pancreatic TRM in obesity may reflect their increased interactions with CD11c+ pro-inflammatory macrophages.

The pro-inflammatory profile of CD11c+ macrophages and high CD58 expression (Fig. 1H) may boost their T-cell interactions, so we compared the capacities of sorted CD11c+ and CD11c− pancreatic macrophage subsets (Fig. S7E for macrophage subset gating) to drive effector and residency molecule induction in naïve T-cells. Both subsets similarly promoted CD103 expression on naïve CD8 T-cells (Fig. S7F) after activation and 72-h co-culture, but the CD11chigh macrophages drove greater CD8 T-cell GZMB production and CD107a degranulation (CD107a+ GZMB+) (Fig. 5F) and significantly higher production of cytotoxic perforin (Fig. S7G). The CD11chigh macrophage subset also promoted higher CD4 T-cell TNF-α production and CD107a degranulation (CD107a+ TNF-α+) (Fig. 5G). These data show that CD11chigh pancreatic macrophages have the greatest capacity to drive T-cell effector functions.

We tested candidate mechanisms of pancreatic macrophage–T cell interaction in a co-culture assay of naïve T cells activated by monomeric anti-CD3 antibody, sorted pancreatic macrophages and specific inhibitors of CD2-CD58 and TGF-β-pathways that were identified by our flow cytometry, CITE-seq and CellChat analysis. After activation and co-culture with pancreatic macrophages, naïve CD8 T-cells showed strong induction of CD103 and GZMB (Fig. 5H, I). Antibodies blocking TGF-β prevented T-cell expression of CD103 in macrophage co-culture and slightly enhanced GZMB (Fig. 5H, I). Antibodies blocking CD58 reduced both GZMB and CD103 induction (Fig. 5H, I). Thus, pancreatic macrophages drive T-cell residency and effector molecule expression by providing TGF-β with CD58 co-stimulation.

In exocrine pancreas of obese organ donors, a subset of E-cadherin+ chromogranin− epithelium showed higher expression of the pro-inflammatory adhesion molecule galectin-3 (Gal-3), which is known to be upregulated in pancreatitis and pancreatic cancer73–79 (Fig. 6B). The proportion of Gal-3+ E-cadherin+ epithelial cells relative to total pancreas cells significantly correlated with donor BMI (Fig. 6C) independent of relevant co-variates (Table S11). To examine T-cell cytotoxic function and its relationship with obesity and epithelial inflammation in situ, we quantified T-cell GZMB staining and classified them as GZMBlow and GZMBhigh (Fig. S8G). The frequencies of GZMBhigh T-cells and total T-cells were significantly positively correlated with BMI (Figs. 6C and S8H). We also observed that Gal-3+ epithelial foci frequently clustered with CD11c+ macrophages and GZMBhigh T cells (Fig. 6B).

To quantify how Gal-3+ epithelial cells are spatially related to the pancreatic immune cells, we calculated the probability of pairwise interaction between cell types (at 20 μm radius)80 (Fig. 6D). Macrophages, T-cells, and endothelial cells had high levels of mutual interaction in pancreas (Fig. 6D). The CD11c+ macrophages and GZMBhigh T cells were found to have selective interactions with Gal-3+ but not Gal-3− epithelium (Fig. 6D), in contrast GZMBlow T cells had lower interactions with Gal-3+ epithelium (Fig. 6D).

To define the multi-lineage spatial patterns across the pancreas, local indicators of spatial association (LISA) was applied to define 5 regions with distinct enrichment of 17 different cell types in close spatial proximity (10–50 μm) (Fig. 6E)81. Pancreas immune lineages, including CD209/FOLR2+ macrophages, T-cells, mast cells, and neutrophils, were highly enriched around endothelial cells in region_3. The Gal-3+ ductal and acinar cells were most highly enriched in region_1 along with CD11c+ macrophages, GZMBhigh T-cells, MPO+ macrophages, and neutrophils. Notably, the frequency of region_1 positively correlated with BMI (Fig. 6F). Thus, pro-inflammatory immune interactions of macrophages and cytotoxic T cells are spatially organized around inflamed Gal-3+ epithelium.

Relationship of pancreatic inflammation with sex and biomarkers of adiposity

Although BMI is the primary metric used in the clinical diagnosis of obesity, and it is a reliable indicator of overall nutritional status and health risk from overnutrition, it does not discriminate fat from lean body mass82. To capture how markers of adiposity correlate with pancreas immune cells, we compiled the data from our two multiplex imaging cohorts and compared the correlations of T-cells and CD11c+ macrophages to BMI, pancreatic fat infiltration and plasma leptin levels—a fat cell secreted hormone and biomarker for overall fat mass83. Both CD11c+ macrophages and T cells correlated most strongly with BMI (Fig. S9A, B). Stratification by sex did not reveal notable differences in the relationships of BMI with pancreas immune cells in males and females (Tables S12and 13). The pancreatic T cells were also significantly and positively correlated with intrapancreatic fat and plasma leptin levels (Fig. S9A), however, the CD11c+ macrophages do not correlate with pancreatic fat or plasma leptin levels (Fig. S9B). Thus, CD11c+ pancreatic macrophages are correlated with overall nutritional status as reflected by the BMI but not to pancreatic fat infiltration or to systemic adiposity.

Discussion

In this study, we identified potential cellular mechanisms by which obesity drives chronic inflammation in the pancreas, a central metabolic sensor and primary tissue site implicated in obesity-associated diseases. Integration of CITE-seq with functional studies and spatial analysis showed how the interactions between pancreatic macrophages and TRM are reshaped with increasing BMI to a more pro-inflammatory configuration. The selective spatial interaction of inflamed Gal-3+ epithelium with pro-inflammatory macrophages and cytotoxic T-cells highlights a previously unappreciated association of obesity with pro-inflammatory epithelial-immune microenvironments. These findings substantially expand our understanding of the pancreatic immune response to obesity84,85 and lay the groundwork for strategies to mitigate chronic pancreatic inflammation and prevent pancreatic disease.

We previously identified CD8 TRM and macrophages as the predominant immune cells in pancreas, which cluster together in the exocrine regions27. Here, we show how obesity significantly alters the pancreatic immune architecture via compartment-specific reorganization. In the exocrine pancreas, TRMs accumulate with increasing BMI (Fig. 1E) and are spatially organized around CD11c+ macrophages (Fig. 2F–J). The selective association of TRM-macrophage foci with inflamed Gal-3+ epithelium shows one way in which immune-epithelial crosstalk may promote exocrine inflammation in obesity (Fig. 6D–F). Pancreatic islets show increased macrophage density with obesity (Fig. 1E), which mirrors findings in mouse models and may reflect macrophage functions in supporting homeostatic islet expansion to increase insulin output in obesity8.

Histological analysis of H&E-stained sections demonstrated that BMI is a significant independent predictor of intrapancreatic fat infiltration (Fig. 1A–C and Table S1), which was associated with acinar-to-ductal metaplasia—a marker of pancreatic stress (Fig. S1B)39. These histological data provide direct tissue-level evidence of obesity-associated pancreatic damage and link the immune remodeling we describe to structural exocrine changes.

Our CITE-seq results define the transcriptomic states underlying the functions and interactions of pancreatic TRM and macrophages. Pancreatic TRM adopt a uniquely cytotoxic posture (high GZMB, PRF1, and IL32)27,86 while simultaneously downregulating multiple NF-κB brakes (TNFAIP3, NFKBIA, and the NR3C1-encoded glucocorticoid receptor)87–91(Fig. 3B, C). The readying of TRM cytotoxic machinery while lowering anti-inflammatory checkpoints may predispose TRM to inappropriate activation. We also identified mechanisms driving TRM localization to pancreas, including upregulation of GPR25, receptor for CXCL1747,92, and CXCR6, receptor for CXCL1693—key chemo-affinity systems for lymphocyte homing and retention in non-intestinal mucosal tissues (Fig. 3B, C). The concurrent low expression of the intestinal-homing receptor, CCR9, by these pancreatic TRM27, indicates a dedicated pancreatic and non-intestinal mucosal-like tissue residency program (Fig. 3B, C).

The specialized pancreas-specific functional state of TRM is associated with distinct responses in obesity, shown in our bulk RNA-seq of TRM from pancreas and neighboring tissues. The unique activation of pro-inflammatory and interferon signatures associated with obesity in the pancreatic TRM (Fig. 3D, E) may reflect their increased coupling with CD11c+ macrophages in situ (Fig. 2F–J) and demonstrate pancreas-specific vulnerability to metabolic stress. Higher CD107a+ GZMB+ pancreatic TRMs correlating with BMI link these gene expression changes with enhanced effector functions (Fig. 5D). Notably, the changes in pancreatic TRM density and function with rising BMI were not associated with altered TCR diversity (Fig. 3G), contrasting with adipose tissue, where obesity further restricts TCR diversity94.

Pancreatic macrophages likely drive the TRM response to obesity, and our results highlight the importance of distinct macrophage subsets in regulating immune homeostasis in the pancreas. The highest surface levels of antigen presentation and co-stimulatory molecules were found on CD11chigh macrophages (Figs. 1G, H, and S1N), enabling them to drive high effector molecule expression in T-cells (Fig. 5F, G). In contrast, the FOLR2+ macrophages have high surface PD-L1 and low CD58 (Fig. 4H, I), which attenuates T-cell activity, while their high efferocytosis transcript expression (MERTK, AXL, and GAS6) may facilitate dead cell clearance without inflammation95–97(Fig. 4B). Ligand–receptor interactome analysis using CellChat further distinguished these subsets, showing that CD11c+ FOLR2− macrophages engaged a broader signaling repertoire with T cells, including through the CD58–CD2 co-stimulation pathway, than fetal-derived macrophages, (Fig. S6A–C). By flow cytometry and imaging, we show how obesity disrupts macrophage homeostasis as regulatory FOLR2+ CD11c− cells are diminished while pro-inflammatory FOLR2− CD11c+ macrophages are expanded (Figs. 5A–C and 6A). Macrophage-derived CD58, which ligates TRM CD298 and drives cytotoxic molecule expression (Fig. 5I), may further intensify pancreatic macrophage-TRM interactions with increasing BMI (Fig. 1H, I).

Intensified macrophage-TRM coupling in obesity may produce metabolically driven inflammatory circuits resistant to resolution. The striking polarization that pancreatic macrophages undergo from monocyte-derived precursors, including induction of LAM markers (TREM2, CD9, and SPP1) and lysosomal/lipid metabolism pathways (Fig. S4C, D), parallel gene signatures of adipose tissue macrophages in obesity (Fig. S5A), suggesting these macrophages may be lipid responsive17,19,20. Notably, the pancreatic CD11c+ macrophages show pronounced expression of T cell activation genes along the monocyte-to-LAM trajectory (Fig. S4D), suggesting that lipid signatures are coupled with T-cell activation. Whereas adipose LAMs surround dying adipocytes in crown-like structures99, pancreatic CD11c+ macrophages preferentially localize around Gal-3+ inflamed epithelium (Fig. 6B, D), and these distinct tissue-specific spatial niches may shape macrophage function.

Given the close spatial proximity and strong communication axis we observed between pancreatic macrophages and T cells, our results suggest that pancreatic macrophages may respond to lipid stress in obesity with pro-inflammatory signals that increase intrapancreatic T cell accumulation. The proliferation of CD11c+ macrophages (Figs. 4B, C, and S8D) in pancreas may enable them to displace the more immunoregulatory fetal-derived macrophages (Fig. 5A, B), contributing further to a pro-inflammatory tissue microenvironment. Accordingly, we found that BMI, intrapancreatic fat and plasma leptin correlate with pancreatic T cells (Fig. S9A) in our imaging datasets, and we found BMI correlates with T cell and macrophage gene signatures in an independent bulk RNA-seq dataset (Tables S5 and S9)53. While our cross-sectional design precludes definitive temporal ordering, our data collectively support a model in which overnutrition-related stress on the acinar compartment directs pro-inflammatory polarization of incoming myeloid cells, which in turn seeds cytotoxic T cell foci. The ligand–receptor analysis identified a richer set of outgoing signaling pathways from macrophages to T cells than in reverse (Fig. S6A, B, and Table S7), supporting a model where macrophages act as upstream organizers of TRM effector programs. The obesity-associated expansion of Gal-3+ epithelium (Fig. 6C) and its selective spatial association with CD11c+ macrophages and GZMB+ cytotoxic T cells (Fig. 6D) suggests that epithelial inflammation and immune cell recruitment may be mutually reinforcing. However, the histological evidence of pancreatic pathology cannot be ascribed definitively to T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity based on cross-sectional data alone.

These findings have significant clinical implications. Obesity-related pancreatic diseases may arise via a self-reinforcing cycle of inflammation, damage, and inadequate resolution. Accumulation of CD11c+ macrophages and TRM near ductal epithelium (Fig. 2B, C) suggests possible direct disruption of barrier function and drainage—key pancreatitis initiators100. Proliferation of FOLR2− CD11c+ macrophages and TRM clustering near inflamed Gal-3+ epithelium (Fig. 6B, D) could create cytokine-rich microenvironments that drive KRAS signaling, progression of pre-malignant lesions and genomic instability via chronic exposure to inflammatory mediators (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, and ROS)101,102. These Gal-3-centered niches may link obesity with increased pancreatic cancer risk by accelerating pre-malignant transformation77,103.

Emerging evidence suggests that interventions to reduce adiposity may modulate these inflammatory circuits. Bariatric surgery produces the largest and most durable weight loss, and imaging studies indicate that it reduces intrapancreatic fat and improves glucose homeostasis104. GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors both improve glycemic control and reduce cardiovascular risk, and accumulating data suggest they exert anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects in adipose tissue, however, effects on pancreas have not yet been examined105,106. Large meta-analyses generally do not show a major change in pancreatic cancer risk with GLP-1 receptor agonists, while SGLT2 inhibitors have been associated with a lower risk of acute pancreatitis compared with DPP-4 inhibitors107,108. It will be interesting in future studies beyond the present one to determine whether these obesity treatments alter the immune environment of the pancreas.

Collectively, our findings show how obesity reshapes pancreas immune homeostasis through the formation of pro-inflammatory macrophage-T-cell circuits that are centered on inflamed exocrine epithelium. These results provide mechanistic insight into how obesity may drive pancreatic diseases and will inform new strategies to better mitigate chronic pancreatic inflammation.

Despite these advances, our study has limitations. The cross-sectional design limits tracking the temporal evolution of inflammatory neighborhoods and determining if immune cell changes are causes or consequences of the inflammation. Although multivariable analysis is useful to adjust for known confounders, it cannot account for unknown confounders to definitively establish causality. Longitudinal studies with serial sampling of healthy pancreas tissue are not feasible in humans. Alternatives to serial tissue sampling that might reveal the state of the immune cells in pancreas are limited because immune cells and cytokines are produced throughout the body, and their tissue-specific activities in obesity cannot be inferred from blood samples alone. Non-invasive imaging to quantify intrapancreatic fat may help define how systemic weight loss translates into effects on pancreatic adiposity as a marker of pancreatic health109. Studies of pancreas specific markers in serum exosomes may be optimized for future studies to monitor pancreatic health110. Also, non-invasive Positron emission tomography imaging using radiolabeled probes for highly specific T cell and cytotoxic effector markers are an emerging modality for studies of tissue immune responses and may, in the future, be applied for analysis of pancreatic inflammation in obesity111.

Also, BMI—although used for diagnosing obesity—does not discriminate between fat and lean body mass or provide information about fat distribution, particularly visceral adiposity, which may be particularly important for pancreatic disease pathogenesis. Our analysis of immune-cell associations with BMI, intrapancreatic fat, and plasma leptin levels (Fig. S9A, B) showed that T-cells correlate with all adiposity markers, whereas CD11c+ macrophages only correlate with BMI. Other factors in overnutrition, aside from fat infiltration, that may drive accumulation of CD11c+ pancreatic macrophages include elevated cholecystokinin stimulation and high demand on digestive enzyme biosynthesis in acinar cells112,113. Lipid-induced activation of these macrophages may promote cytotoxic T-cell influx.

Methods

Human tissue samples

This study complies with all relevant ethical regulations as confirmed by the Columbia University Institutional Review Board. Tissues were obtained from 68 deceased adult organ donors (Supplementary Data 1) as part of organ acquisition for clinical transplantation through an approved protocol and material transfer agreement with organ procurement organizations as described previously114–119. All donors had no reported autoimmune diseases (e.g., type 1 diabetes, lupus), or cancers, tested seronegative for hepatitis B, C, and HIV and did not have a history of chronic pancreatitis or evidence of acute pancreatitis (peak lipase <3 times upper limit of normal, non-indurated)120. Use of organ donor tissues does not qualify as “human subjects” research, as confirmed by the Columbia University IRB, as tissue samples were obtained from brain-dead (deceased) individuals. In all cases, the organ procurement organizations obtained consent from the donor’s next of kin for the research use of donated tissues.

Isolation and preparation of single cell suspensions from tissue samples

After procurement in the operating room, tissue samples were maintained in cold saline or CoStorSol® University of Wisconsin (UW) solution, (Preservation Solutions, Elkhorn, WI, Cat # PS004) and transported to the laboratory within 2–4 h. Pancreas and small intestine were carefully inspected for the presence of lymph nodes, and these were removed and processed separately. Spleen, PLN, and small intestine samples were processed using enzymatic and mechanical digestion. The tissues were placed into digestion media (RPMI L-glutamate, sodium pyruvate, nonessential amino acids, penicillin-streptomycin, collagenase D [1 mg/ml], trypsin inhibitor [1 mg/ml], and DNase I [50–100 μg/ml]), chopped into small pieces, and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Intestinal tissue was washed in PBS prior to digestion to remove intestinal contents, and digestion media was also injected submucosally115–119,121.

Human pancreatic tissue was processed by the Human Islet Core at Columbia University using a Ricordi chamber27. After taking tissue samples for paraffin embedding and image analysis, the body and tail portions of the pancreas were perfused through the pancreatic duct and digested with warmed and circulating enzyme solution in a Ricordi chamber (Biorep Technologies, Inc., Miami Lakes, FL, Cat # RC3-600-MUL) containing Premium Grade Collagenase (SERVA Electrophoresis GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany, Cat # 17455.1) and Neutral Protease (SERVA Electrophoresis GmbH, Cat # 30301.5) until the majority of the islets were separated from exocrine tissue as examined microscopically by Dithizone-staining (Sigma, Cat # D5130). The pancreas cell suspension, was diluted, washed, and collected using at least 8 L of circulating RPMI (Mediatech, Cat # 99-595-CM). Islets were purified using an Optiprep gradient (densities 1.11–1.06) and COBE 2991 blood cell processor, further manually selected to achieve >90% purity by dithizone staining and dispersed using Accutase (Innovative Cell Technologies). Aliquots of the crude pancreas suspension and dispersed islets were immediately cryopreserved in a solution of 90% FBS and 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and stored in a temperature-controlled liquid nitrogen freezer for subsequent immune cell recovery.

Preparation of pancreas tissue for multispectral and H&E staining

All adipose tissue surrounding the pancreas and attached to the capsule was removed. Representative sections 0.5–1.0 cm in thickness from the intracapsular pancreas head, body and tail were removed within 12 h of organ procurement and placed in zinc-buffered formalin (Anatech Ltd.) for 48 h prior to dehydration and embedding in paraffin by the Columbia University Medical Center Molecular Pathology Shared Resource. For H&E staining and imaging with the 7-color Vectra (Akoya) platform full face 5 µm sections of the formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue were used. For imaging with the 32-color PhenoCycler (Akoya) platform, tissue microarrays (TMA) were constructed using 2 mm FFPE representative cores (3 cores per donor, and 10 donors per microarray). For all imaging studies, 5-µm sections were prepared and immediately processed after drying on positively charged Bond Plus slides (Leica).

Sorting pancreas immune cells

Cryopreserved tissue suspension aliquots were rapidly thawed at 37 °C, washed in RPMI with 10% FBS and DNase I (Sigma, Cat # DN25-5G) (50–100 µg/ml). Dead cells were labeled using Zombie-NIR followed by surface staining with antibodies (Supplementary Data 3). For the multi-tissue CITE-seq studies, each different tissue site was labeled with distinct TotalSeq barcoded hashtag antibodies (Biolegend). For sorting, samples were resuspended in PBS with 2% human AB serum and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for added sensitivity in the detection of dead and dying cells. Sorting was performed using either the BD Influx or FACS Aria Cell Sorters. For multi-tissue CITE-seq, hash-tagged cells were sorted from each tissue site at equal proportions. Viable pancreas immune cells were detected as Zombie-NIR− DAPI− CD45+. Due to the low abundance of T cells relative to macrophages in pancreas27, for the multi-tissue T cell CITE-seq studies, the T cells were further selected as CD2+ CD19−. Cells were sorted into Hank's balanced salt solution (HBSS) with 5% human albumin and 10 mM 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (HEPES) buffer. Post-sort viability was confirmed to be >90%. The sorted immune cells were then processed for CITE-seq, bulk RNA-seq and co-culture studies.

CITE-seq of pancreatic immune cells

After sorting, the pancreas immune cells were pelleted, blocked using human TruStain FcX (Biolegend), and then incubated for 30 min at 4 °C with the TotalSeq-B (10× Genomics Chromium Single Cell 3′ sequencing) or TotalSeq-C (for 10× Genomics Chromium Single Cell 5′ sequencing) Universal cocktail per the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the cells loaded into the 10× Genomics Chromium controller, and cDNA synthesis, amplification, and sequencing libraries were prepared using Next GEM Single Cell 5′ or 3′ Kit (10× Genomics) and sequenced on a NovaSeq X (Illumina).

Ligand–receptor interaction analysis

To infer ligand–receptor interactions between macrophage subsets and T cells, the CITE-seq expression matrices for macrophages and T cells from all donors were combined into a single Seurat object (n = 44,897 cells). Macrophages were grouped into four functional subsets based on CITE-seq clustering: CD11c+ FOLR2− macrophages (Mac_CD11c_High; clusters 1, 2, and 4; n = 16,439), monocytic macrophages (Mac_Monocytic; clusters 3, 5, and 6; n = 10,084), fetal-derived macrophages (Mac_Fetal_Derived; cluster 0; n = 7282), and cycling macrophages (Mac_Cell_Cycle; clusters 7–10; n = 5741). T cells were pooled as a single group (n = 5351). CellChat (v2.1.2)66,122 was used with the default CellChatDB (v2).human reference database (1939 curated interactions), augmented with a single ligand–receptor pair (CD58–CD2)123 to capture the co-stimulatory axis identified in our flow cytometry experiments.

Over-expressed ligands and receptors were identified per cell group, and communication probabilities were computed using the triMean method with a minimum threshold of 10 cells per group. Statistical significance was assessed by a permutation test (P < 0.05). Pathway-level communication probabilities were aggregated from individual ligand–receptor pairs, and network centrality measures (sender, receiver, mediator, and influencer roles) were computed for each cell group. Signaling roles were quantified by comparing outgoing and incoming interaction strength for each cell group. Net signaling flow was calculated as the difference between outgoing and incoming strength to classify each group as a net sender or net receiver. All significant interactions, pathway summaries, and directional metrics are reported in Supplementary Data 2. CellChat-inferred interaction networks are shown in Fig. S6A–C.

External validation using GTEx pancreas bulk RNA-seq

To externally validate our spatially-resolved immune signatures, we analyzed bulk RNA-seq data from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project v10 pancreas tissue samples (dbGaP accession phs000424.v10.p2)53. From a total of 363 GTEx datasets, 17 were excluded from donors with type 1 diabetes, and 6 were excluded as outliers with T cell or macrophage signature scores falling 3 standard deviations from the mean (n = 340). For multivariable analysis of the GTEx data, 9 additional donors without available co-variate information were excluded (n = 331 complete cases).

Data processing

Raw gene-level read counts (gene_reads_v10_pancreas.gct.gz) were processed using DESeq2126. Genes with fewer than 10 counts in fewer than 3 samples were removed. Variance-stabilizing transformation (VST) was applied for signature scoring analyses. Sample and subject phenotype annotations were obtained from GTEx v10 metadata files.

VST-based signature scoring

Geometric mean expression scores were computed from VST-transformed data for each signature’s marker gene set. Samples with signature scores exceeding ±3 standard deviations from the mean were removed as statistical outliers.

Statistical analysis

Associations between BMI and immune signature scores were evaluated using ordinary least squares (OLS) linear regression. The outcome variable was the immune signature score (VST-based geometric mean) and was modeled as a continuous variable. BMI (kg/m2) was included as the primary predictor, treated as a continuous variable to maximize statistical power and avoid information loss from categorization.

The full covariate-adjusted model was specified as:

Signature = β0 + β1(BMI) + β2(age) + β3(sex) + β4(T2D) + β5(smoking) + β6(alcohol) + ε

Covariates were defined as follows: age was treated as a continuous variable (years); sex was coded as a binary indicator (male = 1, female = 0); T2D status was derived from medical history annotations and coded as a binary indicator (T2D = 1, non-diabetic = 0); smoking status was derived from the MHSMKSTS field and coded as a binary indicator (smoker = 1, non-smoker = 0); alcohol consumption was derived from the MHDRNKSTS field and coded as a binary indicator (drinker = 1, non-drinker = 0). Complete case analysis was performed, excluding samples with missing covariate data (final N = 329).

To account for potential heteroscedasticity in the residual variance, heteroscedasticity-consistent (HC) robust standard errors were computed using the HC1 estimator127 (White, 1980), implemented via the sandwich package128 in R. The HC1 estimator applies a degrees-of-freedom correction [n/(n − k)] to the heteroscedasticity-consistent covariance matrix, providing unbiased variance estimates under heteroscedasticity. Wald-type 95% confidence intervals were constructed as β̂ ± 1.96 × SE_robust. Statistical significance was assessed using two-sided t-tests with robust standard errors; P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sex-stratified analysis

To evaluate potential sexual dimorphism in BMI-immune associations, sex-stratified multivariable linear regression was performed separately for female (N = 129) and male (N = 200) subgroups, with BMI as the primary predictor adjusting for age, T2D status, smoking, and alcohol consumption. All models utilized heteroscedasticity-consistent robust standard errors (HC1). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Cross-tissue macrophage signature validation

To validate cross-tissue conservation of pancreatic macrophage populations, we scored gene signatures derived from our CITE-seq analysis (Fig. 4B) on an independent adipose tissue single-nucleus RNA-seq atlas. The pre-processed myeloid cell Seurat object was obtained from the adipose tissue dataset (GSE225700), comprising 16 myeloid clusters (myC0–myC15) annotated following the original Massier et al. classifications52.

Signature definition. Ten gene-signature modules were defined from our pancreatic macrophage CITE-seq data corresponding to three main populations: (1) FOLR2+ CD11c− fetal-derived macrophages—fetal-derived macrophage (10 genes: FOLR2, STAB1, LYVE1, SELENOP, PLTP, MAF, MSR1, MRC1, CD163, and CD209), efferocytosis (3 genes: MERTK, AXL, and GAS6), endocytosis (2 genes: MERTK, AXL); (2) monocytic CCR2⁺ cells—monocyte markers (7 genes: S100A12, VCAN, FCN1, EREG, CD36, LYZ, and LGALS3), monocyte migration (4 genes: CCR1, CCR2, SELL, and CD44); (3) CD11c⁺ LAM-like macrophages—lipid metabolism (7 genes: APOC1, LPL, FABP5, CYP27A1, APOE, OLR1, and PDPN), lysosome (9 genes: MARCO, CTSA, CD63, ASAH1, PSAP, ACP5, CD68, CTSD, and NCEH1), pro-inflammatory tissue macrophages (6 genes: CX3CR1, C3, BHLHE41, HTRA1, YWHAH, and CD58); plus proliferation signatures—late mitosis (3 genes: MCM6, PCNA, and CENPF) and early mitosis (2 genes: HIST1H1B, NUSAP1).

Module scoring. Module scores were computed using the Seurat AddModuleScore function on the RNA assay (nbin = 24, ctrl = 100). Mean module scores were calculated per cluster and visualized as a heatmap and violin plots.

Statistical analysis. For macro-level comparisons, myeloid clusters were grouped into functional categories: M2 tissue-resident (myC0, myC01, myC04, myC07–myC09, and myC11–myC13; n = 13,052), LAM (myC02; n = 3029), MMe (myC10; n = 665), and classical monocytes (myC14; n = 2068). Two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were performed to compare module scores between macro-categories. Pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were conducted across all 16 individual clusters for each signature, with P-values adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Statistical analyses were performed in R using Seurat, ggplot2, dplyr, and tidyr.

After staining, the sections were mounted in Vectashield Hard Set mounting media (Vector Labs) and stored at 4 °C for up to 48 h prior to image acquisition. All samples from 32 donors (“multiplex cohort 1,” Supplementary Data 1) were stained together in the same batch.

Multispectral imaging and acquisition at ×20 magnification (numerical aperture 0.75) was performed using the integrated Vectra 3 automated quantitative pathology imaging system (Akoya) per the manufacturer’s instructions, with spectral unmixing and autofluorescence extraction using single-color and unstained pancreas tissue sections27. Representative fields (20–70) from each donor, including both exocrine and endocrine regions, were captured for analysis.

Multiplex staining on the PhenoCycler platform

Multiplexed immunofluorescence was performed using a 32-marker panel (Supplementary Data 3) on the PhenoCycler-Fusion platform from Akoya Biosciences, which is based on the CODEX (CO-Detection by InDEXing) method. This technique employs oligonucleotide-tagged antibodies and fluorophores to detect multiple markers through iterative imaging cycles, each followed by an antibody removal step129–131. FFPE TMA slides from 28 donors (“multiplex cohort 2,” Supplementary Data 1) (3, 2 mm cores per donor) were prepared and stained according to the manufacturer’s protocol for PhenoCycler-Fusion (Akoya Biosciences), with the primary antibody incubation extended to an overnight step at 4 °C to enhance signal quality132,133. To reduce tissue autofluorescence, a bleaching protocol was applied at the end of the staining process134. Fusion-compatible antibodies were either purchased directly from Akoya Biosciences or generated in-house by conjugating purified antibodies with fluorophore-labeled oligonucleotides using the Antibody Conjugation Kit (Akoya Biosciences, 7000009) (Supplementary Data 3).

Imaging was conducted on the PhenoCycler-Fusion imaging system following the manufacturer’s instructions135. The resulting qptiff files were visualized in HALO (Indica Labs, v4.0.4). Raw images of the whole slide TMA scans were processed using the HALO platform for further analysis.

Quantification of intrapancreatic fat and pathological analysis of human pancreas tissue

Image acquisition

All available tissue sections from donors used in this study were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin and immediately scanned at 40× using the Leica SCN400 whole slide digital imager.

Tissue segmentation and classification

All images were analyzed using HALO (Indica Labs, v4.1). A tissue classifier (DenseNet V2) was trained on multiple representative slides across the study to delineate glass background and debris from functional pancreas (acinar, ductal, islet, vascular tissue) and to delineate the areas of intrapancreatic fat. The proportion of intrapancreatic fat was calculated as the ratio of the area classified as “fat” to the functional pancreas areas, excluding the areas classified as glass or debris. Tissue classifier training accuracy was assessed against pancreatic fat proportions quantified separately by a gastrointestinal pathologist blinded to sample identity for a subset of 32 tissue sections using simple linear regression comparing the two methods (manual vs. computationally assisted) and was found to be robust across a wide range of intrapancreatic fat levels (Fig. S1A). All 330 available tissue sections from 63 donors were compiled for complete intrapancreatic fat quantification. A gastrointestinal pathologist blinded to sample identity also quantified the number of acinar-to-ductal metaplasia (ADM)-containing lobules per mm2 for the H&E stained tissue sections in multiplex cohort 1 (32 tissue sections from 32 donors)38 for correlation of intrapancreatic fat to ADM.

Image analysis of multiplex staining using the Vectra platform

Tissue and cell segmentation

Images were analyzed using inForm software (Akoya Biosciences). Raw 7-plex fluorescence imaging data were pre-processed using the unstained slide and single-color controls for spectral unmixing and autofluorescence mitigation per the manufacturer’s recommendations.

Tissue segmentation was performed27 using inForm software (PerkinElmer), to classify the pancreas tissue compartments—islets (chromogranin+ cell clusters), ductal (CK19+) and acinar (highly cellular CK19−/chromogranin− areas between the ducts and islets). Cell segmentation was performed based on the DAPI nuclear counterstain, and immune cell classification was performed on chromogranin− CK19− cells based on lineage markers—macrophage (CD163+), CD4 T cell (CD4+), CD8 T cell (CD8+)27 as shown (Fig. S2C). Matrices of individual cells with classifications, X and Y coordinates were exported for further analysis.

Macrophage subset classification

We implemented a classification strategy to categorize macrophages into two classes based on CD11c expression levels: macrophages with high CD11c (CD11chigh Macs) and low CD11c (CD11clow Macs) expression. Neuroendocrine cells, which lack CD11c marker expression, served as a negative control. We performed Box-Cox (BCx) normalization on the central signal intensity values of CD11c for both neuroendocrine cells and macrophages to approximate a normal distribution136 (Fig. S2A). Histograms of BCx-normalized CD11c intensities revealed that all neuroendocrine cells across all donors consistently exhibited values lower than 0 (Fig. S2A). This allowed us to use BCx-normalized CD11c intensity value of zero as a threshold for classifying macrophages as CD11chigh (above 0) or CD11clow (below 0).

Spatial analysis of macrophage subsets

We quantified the relative abundances of CD11chigh and CD11clow macrophages within distinct pancreatic compartments: ductal, acinar, and islet regions. BCx-transformed intensities of CD11c for CD11c+ macrophages were calculated and normalized against values from all macrophages (Fig. 2C–E). Heatmaps visualized the dynamics of compartmental macrophage composition relative to donor BMI (Fig. S2B).

Spatial relationships between macrophages and T cells were analyzed using a custom Python script Single-cell spatial coordinates of each cell were imported into pandas DataFrames for each donor. Pairwise spatial relationships were computed using radius-based nearest neighbor search implemented with scikit-learn’s NearestNeighbors class. A Euclidean distance threshold of 20 μm identified proximal cell neighbors. Tissue architecture was modeled as an undirected graph using NetworkX, with cells as nodes and edges connecting neighboring cells. This approach quantified spatial proximities between T cell subsets (CD4+ and CD8+) and macrophage subsets (CD11chigh and CD11clow). The percentages of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells localized within 20 μm proximity of each macrophage subset was calculated per donor, and Pearson correlation coefficients and p-values were calculated to evaluate relationships with donor BMI.

Neighborhood analysis

Neighborhood (NH) analysis was applied to Multiplex imaging cohort 145,130,137. For each individual cell across all tissue sections (4,207,602 cells), a “window” was defined comprising the ten nearest cells based on Euclidean distance. Windows were clustered based on their cell type composition using MiniBatchKMeans (k = 10) implemented in scikit-learn. Seven neighborhood types were identified and validated by overlapping data from fluorescent multiplexed imaging: NH0 (acinar NH), NH1 (ductal NH), NH2 (macrophages CD11chigh and T cell NH), NH3 (macrophages CD11clow and T cell NH), NH4 (neuroendocrine NH), NH5 (ductal NH), and NH6 (ductal and macrophages NH) (Fig. 2I). The frequency of each NH was assessed for each donor, and R-square with corresponding p-values were calculated between donor BMI index and NH frequencies (Fig. 2J, K).

Image analysis of multiplex staining using the PhenoCycler platform

Cell segmentation

Cell segmentation was consistently applied across all samples and was performed based on a three-step process to delineate nuclear, cytoplasmic, and membrane compartment using HALO software (v4.0.4). First, DAPI was used as the primary nuclear stain to identify DAPI-positive nuclei. A human expert manually annotated representative nuclei by drawing segmentation outlines around them under the classifier tab. The classifier was configured with a resolution of 0.25 µm and a minimum object size of 10 µm2, categorizing objects into “background” and “nucleus.” After tuning to confirm segmentation accuracy, the trained classifier was saved for subsequent use. Cytoplasmic regions were extended from the nuclear boundary with a maximum cytoplasm diameter of 1 µm to reduce signal contamination, while membrane segmentation was optimized using dyes displaying a clear honeycomb-like pattern for effective cell border separation, with the software extending the cytoplasm to these boundaries. Single-cell matrices—including average marker expression, with X and Y coordinates were extracted, and all detected cells were exported to FCS files via the “data” tab.

Cell quality control classification

Cells were classified using hierarchical gating by a blinded expert using FlowJo. Cells not expressing sufficient marker signal or not corresponding into defined populations were excluded from cell-type-specific analyses. Data preprocessing and analysis was performed using the workflow of Windhager et al.138. The analysis was conducted on an Amazon Web Services (AWS) virtual machine of type r6a.16xlarge (64 vCPUs, 512 GiB of memory, and 25 Gigabit network performance), within a Docker container provided by the Bodenmiller Group (https://github.com/BodenmillerGroup). This containerized setup encapsulated all required dependencies and configurations for the spatial analysis pipeline, enabling seamless and standardized execution.

From a total 3,188,829 segmented cells across 3 tissue microarrays, we delineated 17 cell types based on the combination of the marker expression (Supplementary Data 3 and Supplementary Method 1). For macrophage classification, FOLR2 and the CD209 markers were used interchangeably due to inconsistent marker performance between TMA batches. Data from flow cytometry and CITE-seq validated that FOLR2 and CD209 label the same macrophage subset (Figs. 4C–E and S4A). Classified cell types included CD11c+ FOLR2/CD209− (33,545 cells), CD11c− FOLR2/CD209+ (38,453 cells), CD11c+ FOLR2/CD209− (16,819 cells), CD11c+ FOLR2/CD209+ (8347 cells), GZMBlow T cells (24,099 cells), GZMBhigh T cells (9600 cells), endothelial cells (70,643 cells), galectin− acinar cells (1,495,446 cells), galectin− ductal cells (622,864 cells), galectin+ acinar cells (471,434 cells), galectin+ ductal cells (163,449 cells), islet cells (55,061 cells), mast cells (13,898 cells), MPO+ macrophages (17,992 cells), neutrophils (17,489 cells), pancreatic stellate cells (PSC) (68,086 cells), and smooth muscle cells (61,604 cells).

Spatial cell proximity graph construction and interaction analysis

To analyze spatial relationships between different cell types, single-cell spatial coordinates, corresponding to nuclear centroids, were extracted from the multiplexed immunofluorescence images for each donor. A radius-based nearest neighbor search was implemented using the NearestNeighbors class from scikit-learn to identify proximal cell relationships. For each cell, all neighboring cells within a Euclidean distance threshold of 20 μm were identified as spatial neighbors. The resulting proximity relationships were represented as an undirected graph using NetworkX, where individual cells were represented as nodes, and edges connected pairs of cells within the 20 μm threshold. The graph was constructed separately for each donor to account for donor-specific tissue architecture. Cell neighborhood relationships were formatted into a structured table containing paired cell identifiers and their corresponding donor information and subsequently used for downstream spatial interaction analysis.

Using the spatial graphs constructed above, we assessed spatial interactions between different cell types following the methodology of Schapiro et al. and Windhager et al.80,138. For each pair of cell types, we quantified pairwise interactions to identify significant attractions or avoidances. We calculated an interaction score that represents the degree of spatial association by comparing the observed number of interactions between cells of two types to the expected number under a random distribution hypothesis138:

Interaction score = (observed count-expected count)/√ (expected count)

This standardized residual approach provides a normalized measure of interaction strength that accounts for differences in cell type abundances. The resulting scores ranged from approximately −25 (strong avoidance) to +25 (strong attraction), with a score of 0 indicating no spatial preference (Fig. 6D). Statistical significance was determined using a permutation-based approach with 1000 iterations, where cell type labels were randomly reassigned while maintaining the spatial graph structure. P-values were calculated by comparing the observed interaction score to the distribution of scores obtained from permutations. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control false discovery rate, with interactions considered significant at adjusted p-value < 0.05. The resulting significant interactions were visualized as a color-coded heatmap, with hierarchical clustering applied to identify patterns of coordinated cellular organization across the tissue. The analysis was performed separately for each donor and subsequently aggregated to identify conserved interaction patterns across the cohort.

Local indicators of spatial association (LISA) analysis

Spatial heterogeneity within the pancreatic tissue microenvironment was characterized using local indicators of spatial association (LISA) clustering, a method adapted from geospatial statistics for single-cell multiplex imaging data analysis and was implemented using the lisaClust package81. The analysis was performed on cell type annotations to identify statistically significant local spatial patterns across multiple spatial scales. We applied LISA clustering with k-means parameter k = 7 and at three distinct radii (10, 20, and 50 µm) to capture both fine-grained and broader spatial relationships For each cell, LISA statistics were computed using a centered local multi-type K-function that measures the proportion of neighboring cells of each phenotype within the specified radii (10, 20, and 50 µm), generating a multivariate feature vector representing its local cellular composition. These features were transformed using principal component analysis prior to k-means clustering to reduce dimensionality while preserving variance structure. The LISA algorithm assigned each cell to one of seven spatial context regions (stored as “region” in the data structure), which represented distinct microenvironmental niches within the tissue (Fig. 6E, F).

GZMB+/GZMB− T cell classification and spatial analysis

To assess cytotoxic T cell spatial relationships in the PhenoCycler dataset, CD4 and CD8 T cells were further classified based on GZMB protein expression intensity. Multiple absolute expression threshold equal 2 was selected, yielding four T cell subsets: CD8_Cytotoxic (GZMB+), CD8_Non_cytotoxic (GZMB−), CD4_Cytotoxic (GZMB+), and CD4_Non_cytotoxic (GZMB−). All other cell type annotations were retained from the original classification. Spatial analysis was performed using R-based tools on SpatialExperiment objects. Spatial graphs were constructed using buildSpatialGraph from the imcRtools package (v1.14.0)138 with a k-nearest neighbors graph (k = 20) for neighborhood aggregation and an expansion graph (threshold = 20 pixels) for interaction testing. Neighborhood composition was computed by aggregating cell type counts within each cell’s k = 20 neighborhood, and cells were assigned to spatial neighborhoods using k-means clustering (k = 6, seed = 220705). LISA clustering was performed using lisaClust81 (k = 6, radii = 10, 20, and 50 pixels) with the GZMB-based cell type classification to identify spatial regions enriched for specific cell type combinations. Cell–cell interactions were assessed using testInteractions from spicyR139 on the expansion graph (threshold = 20 pixels); symmetric interaction scores were computed as standardized deviations from expected co-localization, and asymmetric (patch) interactions with patch size = 3. Associations between spatial region frequencies and donor BMI were evaluated using multivariable linear regression (frequency ~ BMI + age + sex + diabetes + TMA).

Bulk RNA-seq analysis

To analyze the effect of obesity on TRM gene expression, RNA-Seq reads from sorted CD8+ TRM were mapped, filtered and read counts computed27. RNA-Seq reads were mapped using TopHat (v2.1.1) with default parameters to the human reference genome build hg19; read counts were computed using HTSeq (v0.11.2)140,141. Data from samples that were included in our previous study27 (GEO: GSE135582) were merged with 24 additional samples, and batch correction was performed using ComBat-seq (v3.56.0)142. For each individual tissue site, principal component analysis and differential gene expression analysis was performed on the adjusted count matrix using DeSeq2 (v1.38.0)126 with a design formula to show the effects of obesity on gene expression (Fig. 3D, E). Gene set enrichment analysis on the differentially expressed genes (DEG) was performed using Enrichr143.

Correlation analysis and data visualization

Simple and multiple linear regression analysis, descriptive statistics of compiled flow cytometry data, graphs and statistical testing were performed using Prism (GraphPad software). Multiple linear regression models were run initially with BMI, age, sex, diabetes, heavy alcohol use (2+ drinks daily), and smoking (>20 pack years ever) as independent variables. Those independent variables showing a potential effect on the outcome (P < 0.3) were included in the final model. The plots showing correlation of cells and regions from the three PhenoCycler stained TMAs (Figs. 6A, C, F, and S6F) were constructed using data that were corrected for TMA batch based on the multiple linear regression models (Table S5, S6). P values (two-tailed) below 0.05 were considered as statistically significant.

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis of T cells

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Single-cell RNA sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger (10× Genomics144), aligning reads to the GRCh38 reference genome. Libraries were prepared using the 10× Genomics Chromium 5’ Gene Expression platforms (v3 chemistry) and TotalSeq-C antibodies (BioLegend) for CITE-seq analysis and hashtag oligo (HTO) multiplexing.

Data preprocessing and quality control

Raw sequencing data were processed with Cell Ranger (10× Genomics) and initially analyzed in Seurat34 in R. Hashtag oligonucleotide (HTO) demultiplexing was performed using HTODemux (positive.quantile = 0.99) after CLR normalization to identify tissue-specific singlets. Quality control retained cells with 200–2500 detected genes and <5% mitochondrial content. Data were imported into Scanpy145. RNA counts were normalized to 10,000 counts per cell and log-transformed. ADT data underwent CLR transformation, with counts log-transformed after pseudo-count addition and scaled relative to the geometric mean per cell.

Normalization and transformation

RNA counts were normalized to 10,000 counts per cell and log-transformed. ADT data were normalized using the centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation. Specifically, ADT counts per cell were log-transformed after pseudo-count addition, and values were scaled relative to the geometric mean expression per cell.

Highly variable gene selection and dimensionality reduction

Highly variable genes (HVGs) were identified using sc.pp.highly_variable_genes (flavor = “seurat_v3”), testing 2000–25,000 HVGs. Based on clustering stability (silhouette scores) and variance explained by the top 10 principal components (PCs), 2500 HVGs were selected. Data were scaled (sc.pp.scale, max_value = 10), and PCA (sc.tl.pca) retained 30 PCs.

Initial integration and phenotypic filtering

An initial integration was performed on the principal component analysis (PCA) embeddings using Harmony. This step corrected for composite batch effects using a single identifier combining donor (batch) and tissue of origin (tissue_group). The integrated data were clustered using the Leiden algorithm (scanpy.tl.leiden, resolution = 1.0). ADT data underwent batch correction using ADTnorm to normalize protein expression across donors, with the normalized values mapped to the concatenated dataset. T cells were separated from non-T cell contaminants in the dataset through iterative rounds of clustering and integration. T cell clusters were identified based on expression of canonical markers (CD8A, CD8B, CD2, CD7, IL7R, CD4, CD3E, and CD3D) and non T cells were removed based on expression of lineage defining genes (B cells: CD19, MS4A1, CD79A; macrophages: CD14, CD163; and mast cells: KIT, TPSAB1) and ADT markers (B cells: Hu.CD19, Hu.CD20.2H7, Hu.CD79b; macrophages: Hu.CD14.M5E2, Hu.CD11b, Hu.CD11c, Hu.CD163; and NK cells: Hu.CD56, Hu.CD16).

Final integration

To preserve biologically relevant signals from the tissue microenvironment, Harmony was run on the purified T cell dataset to correct for donor-level batch effects only (using batch as the key; max_iter_harmony = 20) (Fig. 3A). All subsequent analyses were performed on this final, donor-corrected dataset.

Visualization of cluster and tissue distributions

Cluster and tissue distributions were quantified using crosstabulation to compute the proportional contribution of each tissue site (Islets, PLN, pancreas, small intestine, and spleen) per cluster in the final donor-corrected dataset, normalized by row to percentages (Fig. S3A).

Differential expression and functional enrichment analysis

To identify tissue-specific gene signatures, differential expression analysis was performed on the final, integrated dataset. Pancreatic-enriched T-cell clusters (clusters 0, 2, and 5) were compared against all other clusters and against the CD8 predominant small intestine-enriched clusters (clusters 1, 3, and 7) using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test implemented in Scanpy (scanpy.tl.rank_genes_groups, method = “wilcoxon”). P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Genes with an FDR-adjusted p-value < 0.05 and a log2 fold-change > 0.25 were considered significantly upregulated. Only DEGs identified in both comparisons are visualized on the heatmap depicting single-cell expression (Fig. 3C) and included in gene set enrichment analysis. (Table S3).

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis of macrophages

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Single-cell RNA sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger (10× Genomics144), aligning reads to the GRCh38 reference genome. Libraries were prepared using the 10× Genomics Chromium Single Cell 3′ or 5′ Gene Expression platforms (v3 chemistry). Datasets for 4 donors were sequenced with the 3′ system using TotalSeq-B antibodies, and 1 dataset was sequenced with the 5′ system and TotalSeq-C antibodies (BioLegend) for CITE-seq analysis and hashtag oligo (HTO) multiplexing.

Data preprocessing

Raw sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger, loaded as Seurat objects and underwent initial quality control to remove cells with high mitochondrial genes (>50%)146,147. Each donor sample underwent independent processing with initial dimensionality reduction using buildUMAP and marker identification using findMarkers functions. Multiple rounds of cluster-based sequential selection (2–5 rounds per donor) were performed with comprehensive marker validation, including lineage contamination assessment. Low quality cells were further filtered by removing cells that clustered together with low positive enrichment for immune lineage marker genes, high expression of stress-associated transcripts (e.g., heat shock protein transcripts, GADD34, ATF3) and high mitochondrial gene content. Clusters were excluded based on high expression of lymphocyte markers (CD2, CD7, CD8A, CD3E, and CD19) and mast and epithelial cell markers (SPINK1, KRT8, and TPSAB1). Macrophage confirmation utilized established macrophage markers (CD163, CD14, and CD33). Multi-modal validation compared RNA-protein expression pairs (CD14/Hu.CD14-M5E2, CD163/Hu.CD163, and CD33/Hu.CD33). Per-cell QC metrics are provided (Supplementary Data 4). Final macrophage populations were exported as donor-specific whitelists and saved as cleaned Seurat objects.

Data integration and batch correction

Datasets SW031-034 were generated by 3′ sequencing, whereas SW037 was generated by 5′ sequencing. The SW031-034 macrophage curated datasets were merged, followed by standard processing including NormalizeData, FindVariableFeatures, ScaleData, RunPCA, batch correction using RunHarmony148 with “donor” as the batch variable, then RunUMAP, FindNeighbors, and FindClusters (resolution = 0.7). Initial integration of SW031-034 revealed two minor clusters of non-macrophage contaminants, so these cells were removed. Then the SW037 dataset was added, followed by final integration of all 5 datasets using both “donor” and “chemistry” as correction variables. The final UMAP was generated using RunUMAP (harmony reduction, dims 1:30), FindNeighbors (harmony reduction, dims 1:30), and FindClusters (resolution = 0.8).

Multi-Modal normalization and final selection

RNA data were normalized using log-normalization, while ADT data were normalized using centered log-ratio transformation with normalization.method = “CLR” and margin = 2. Following complete integration, minor clusters 11–14 were excluded due to low expression of macrophage markers, retaining macrophage clusters 0–10 for downstream analysis.

Cluster-specific differential gene expression

Marker genes for each cluster were identified using FindAllMarkers with parameters: only.pos = FALSE, min.pct = 0.25, and logfc.threshold = 0.25 for both RNA and ADT assays using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. The DEG defining each macrophage cluster were analyzed with Enrichr and curated into 10 functional categories (Fig. 4B)

Dotplot visualization

Gene expression dotplots were generated using an analysis pipeline in R with Seurat. Single-cell RNA sequencing data were normalized using LogNormalize with a scale factor of 10,000. The curated gene list of 51 genes organized into 10 functional categories (as described above) was used for visualization. Expression statistics were calculated using a function that computed the percentage of cells expressing each gene (pct.exp) and average expression levels (avg.exp) per cluster. Per-gene scaling was applied across all clusters (0–10), normalizing expression values from 0 to 1 to enable comparison of relative expression patterns. The final visualization combined multiple plot elements using cowplot: (1) a bar chart displaying cell counts per cluster, (2) a dotplot where point size represents the fraction of cells expressing each gene and color intensity represents relative expression levels (navy to darkred gradient), (3) color-coded bars grouping clusters into functional categories. Clusters were ordered as 6, 5, 3, 2, 4, 1, 7, 8, 9, 10, and 0 to facilitate biological interpretation (Fig. 4B).

Donor-cluster matrix generation

For each of the 11 final macrophage clusters (0–10), cells were divided by cluster identity and donor. Average gene expression for each cluster and donor was calculated using AverageExpression with assays = “RNA.” Expression matrices for all clusters were combined to create a final matrix of 71 genes × 55 donor-cluster combinations (5 donors × 11 clusters, Fig. S4B).

RNA–ADT concordance analysis

To validate concordance between transcriptomic and proteomic measurements in the CITE-seq dataset, per-cell Spearman rank correlations were computed between log-normalized RNA and CLR-normalized ADT expression for all matched antibody–gene pairs, separately within macrophages (n = 39,583 cells, 5 donors) and T cells (n = 20,065 cells, 3 donors). All ADT features from the BioLegend TotalSeq-B Universal Human Panel (148 antibodies) were mapped to their corresponding HGNC gene symbols. Isotype controls and features with no matching RNA transcript were excluded, yielding 138 evaluable ADT–RNA pairs for macrophages and 129 for T cells. Three features targeting distinct CD45 epitopes (CD45, CD45RA, and CD45RO) were each evaluated independently against the total PTPRC transcript. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals were derived using the Fisher z-transform. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method; adjusted P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (Supplementary Data 5).

Trajectory inference with Monocle3

Trajectory analysis was conducted using the monocle3 package65,149. The cell data set excluding fetal-derived macrophages (clusters 0 and 10) was preprocessed with principal component analysis (PCA) using 20 dimensions (preprocess_cds, num_dim = 20), followed by UMAP dimensionality reduction (reduce_dimension, reduction_method = “UMAP”). Cells were clustered with cluster_cells, and a trajectory graph was inferred using learn_graph. Pseudotime was calculated with order_cells, with the root cell selected interactively based on biological context. The trajectory was visualized using plot_cells, colored by pseudotime.

Differential expression analysis along pseudotime

Differential expression across pseudotime was assessed using graph_test from Monocle3, leveraging the principal graph with 32 cores for parallel computation. Significant genes (q < 0.05) were identified, and expression trends were evaluated by calculating slopes via linear regression (lm) using the broom package. Upregulated (slope > 0) and downregulated (slope < 0) genes were filtered per partition, ranked by Moran’s I statistic, and the top genes per category were selected. Expression trends for these genes were plotted along pseudotime with a custom color gradient (blue to yellow, Fig. S4C).

Pathway enrichment analysis and heatmap generation

Enrichment analysis143 was conducted on the genes significantly correlated with trajectory progression. Representative genes enriched in biologically relevant pathways and their Z-scored normalized values were visualized with a blue-white-red gradient (Fig. S4D).

Software and environment

Computing infrastructure

Analyses were performed on Amazon Web Services (AWS) virtual machines (r6a.4xlarge with 16 vCPUs, 131 GiB memory, and 12.5 Gigabit network performance; r6a.16xlarge with 64 vCPUs, 512 GiB memory, and 25 Gigabit network performance) and high-performance computing systems with 32 cores. Parallel computation was utilized for computationally intensive analyses, including trajectory inference, differential expression testing, and spatial proximity analysis. Containerization was implemented using Docker to ensure reproducibility of computational environments.

Image analysis

Imaging analyses were performed using the PhenoCycler platform (Akoya Biosciences), inForm image analysis software (v2.3, PerkinElmer), HALO platform (v4.0.4, Indica Labs) for multiplexed immunofluorescence processing and single-cell spatial coordinate extraction, and FlowJo (v9-v10.10.0) for cell type classification, Cell Ranger (v6.0, 10× Genomics) for single-cell RNA-seq read alignment, and Prism (v10.4, GraphPad) for statistical analysis and visualization.

Python analysis environment

Quantification of plasma leptin levels

Leptin levels in human plasma were quantified using the Quantikine™ Human Leptin ELISA kit (DLP00, R&D Systems) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Plasma samples were diluted in Calibrator Diluent RD5P at ratios of 1:10, 1:25, and 1:100 to identify the optimal working range. Among these, the 1:25 dilution produced values within the linear range of the standard curve and was selected for analysis. Assay procedures, including incubation, washing, and detection steps, were performed as specified in the kit protocol. Optical density was measured at 450 nm with wavelength correction at 540/570 nm. Leptin concentrations were calculated from the standard curve generated during the assay.
