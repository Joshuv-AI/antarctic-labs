Greenhouse warming exacerbates El Niño-induced Indian monsoon droughts
Authors: Yutong Zhao, Tao Wang, Chaoyi Xu, Xichen Li, Shilong Piao, Tandong Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The inverse relationship between the El Niño‒Southern Oscillation (ENSO) and the Indian summer monsoon has weakened since the early 1980s. Here, we demonstrate that the historical weakening of the ENSO–monsoon relationship was caused by two unusual events (in 1983 and 1997), and greenhouse warming has actually strengthened rather than weakened this link between 1902 and 2023 and is projected to continue doing so. However, confidence in the projection of this enhancement remains relatively low. We attribute the model spread in ENSO–monsoon links to variations in ENSO-induced Walker circulation anomalies, primarily through zonal wind feedback processes. Through the use of multiple observations to constrain such atmospheric dynamic processes, we find that the observation-constrained sensitivity of monsoons to the ENSO by the end of this century will become almost 40% greater than that from 1963–2023. Such intensification occurs because, even if the degree of El Niño-induced sea surface warming remains unchanged, the response of tropical precipitation to sea surface warming will increase, causing further eastward displacement of Walker circulation anomalies and an increased subsidence over the Indian subcontinent, which will reduce monsoon precipitation further. Our findings suggest intensified hydrological extremes and increased risks for food security in India in a warmer future.

Introduction

Indian summer (June–September) monsoon rainfall (ISMR) accounts for ~80% of the total annual precipitation in the Indian subcontinent, and a slight change in ISMR can profoundly influence the Indian food production system1,2. El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) events during the positive phase (El Niño), which typically encompass a maximum anomalous warming centre in the eastern equatorial Pacific, produce a deficit in ISMR through interactions between the eastward displacement of the ascending branch of the Walker circulation and the regional Hadley circulation3–6. However, a breakdown of this significant relationship has been observed during the late 20th century7–9.

This widely reported significant weakening of the ENSO–ISMR relationship still remains uncertain and has troubled the research community for more than two decades10,11. There are two main schools of thought with respect to the collapsed relationship that are anchored in conflicting evidence from the literature, namely, a response to anthropogenic greenhouse warming7,12 and a result of low-frequency deterministic processes13–18, such as the Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation16 and the Pacific Decadal Oscillation17,18. Studies have also revealed that the disruption of this relationship results from the presence of stochastic fluctuations in a statistically undersampled system19,20. To date, none of these concepts have satisfactorily explained the observations, and there remains debate as to whether a real collapse of the ENSO–ISM relationship is even occurring21–24. The persistence of these contradictory results could overshadow future ISMR projections. Currently, the projections of the ENSO–ISMR relationship obtained with Earth system models are highly uncertain25–29. Specifically, studies have suggested that the ENSO–ISMR relationship will strengthen21,24,25, whereas others have indicated a weakening of this relationship in the 21st century27,28. Moreover, the magnitude of the sensitivity of ISMR to the ENSO is difficult to reliably project, as it varies depending on the choice of mode28–30. Such model inconsistency complicates Indian agricultural policy-making because the response of the real Indian monsoon system to the lowest and highest changes translates into radically different policy options. A robust projection through the use of coupled models is only possible when the uncertainties associated with these models are reduced, and to date, the projected ENSO–ISMR relationship has not been constrained by historical observations.

In this research, we reveal that the ENSO–ISMR relationship remains robust in historical observations and strengthens under greenhouse warming. The previously reported weakening is driven mainly by anomalous ENSO–ISMR relationship deviations in 1983 and 1997. The constrained model results indicate an intensified monsoon response to the ENSO, which is associated with increased convection over the Indo-Pacific and an intensified Walker circulation anomaly that increases subsidence over India.

Results

The inverse ENSO–ISMR relationship did not collapse during the historical period

In this study, we revisited the interannual relationship between the ENSO and ISMR during the historical period and employed all-India summer (June–September) monsoon rainfall and sea surface temperature (SST) anomalies averaged over the Pacific Niño3 region (150°W to 90°W, 5°N to 5°S) to represent ISMR and ENSO intensities, respectively. A widely reported explanation for the collapse of the ENSO–ISMR relationship is that climate variations in other ocean basins, such as the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) and Atlantic Niño, can influence monsoon variability and might subsequently disrupt the ENSO–ISMR relationship. To address this issue, we applied a causal network learning algorithm, namely, the Peter–Clark momentary conditional independence (PCMCI) method31, and determined that on interannual timescales, the causal influences of both the IOD and the Atlantic Niño on ISMR failed to pass the significance test. These results suggest that the interannual ENSO–ISMR relationship was not significantly affected by the IOD or Atlantic Niño events (Supplementary Fig. 1). Furthermore, we employed Community Earth System Model version 2 (CESM2) Pacific Pacemaker ensemble simulations in which the SST over the eastern tropical Pacific region was nudged towards the observations (refer to the Methods). Such nudging in dynamic models could eliminate their well-known cold biases, greatly affecting the accurate simulation of ENSO characteristics. The obtained outcomes can be applied to isolate the effect of the ENSO on ISMR from any feedback from other oceans, including IOD and Atlantic Niño events that are independent of the ENSO. We demonstrated that the interannual ENSO–ISMR relationship in the Pacific Pacemaker simulations is similar to the observed ENSO–ISMR relationship (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3; Supplementary Text 1). This analysis provides additional evidence that IOD and Atlantic Niño events are least likely to disrupt the interannual ENSO–monsoon relationship.

To better understand whether the previously reported collapse of the ENSO–ISMR relationship is sensitive to a few unusual data values, we generated bivariate kernel density estimates constructed from the Niño3 sea surface temperature anomalies and ISMR anomalies of each El Niño (La Niña) event between 1963 and 2023 from the merged data (Fig. 1). There are two events (1983 and 1997), which were instead associated with an increase in ISMR. This association was especially obvious for the 1983 and 1997 events, which occurred outside the 97.5th percentile confidence interval (Fig. 1a). When the Niño3.4 index was used instead of the Niño3 index, the 1983 event was no longer identified as an outlier, consistent with its characterisation as a decaying-phase ENSO event (Supplementary Fig. 4). However, the 1997 event remained an outlier, leading to the apparent weakening of the ENSO–ISMR relationship during the late 20th century (Supplementary Fig. 5). Hence, we suggest that the previously reported weakening of the ENSO–ISMR relationship is a statistical artefact, appearing in both Niño3- and Niño3.4-based correlations because of their high sensitivities to a small number of specific events (e.g., 1983 or 1997). We subsequently analysed outliers using various regression diagnostics, including delta R2, Cook’s distance, and bootstrap analysis metrics, to determine the influence of individual observations on the regression fit (refer to the Methods). The results of this outlier analysis confirmed that both 1983 and 1997 were highly anomalous years that deviated from the typical negative ENSO–ISMR relationship. After these two events were excluded, the correlation between the ENSO and ISMR (RENSO-ISMR) shifted from nonsignificant to significant during the period (1980–1999) when the collapsed relationship was initially reported (Supplementary Fig. 6). Similar results were also obtained when regional rainfall (e.g., South Peninsular, West Central, Central Northeast Indian, and Northwest Indian rainfall) was chosen instead of all-India rainfall (Supplementary Fig. 7) and when the first principal component (PC1) of the Indo-Pacific Ocean interannual SST anomaly (50°E–70°W, 10°N–25°S) was adopted to represent the ENSO (refer to the Methods) (Supplementary Fig. 8). The physical mechanism through which El Niño typically affects ISMR entails the interactions between the equatorial Walker circulation and the meridional Hadley circulation3,24,32. However, in both of the years 1983 and 1997, El Niño events did not result in significant changes in either the Walker circulation or the regional Hadley circulation. Instead, such changes were driven by entirely distinct mechanisms (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10).

In contrast to the decaying phase of a typical El Niño event, SST anomalies exceeding 0.5 °C in the Niño3 region persisted into JJAS period of 1983, indicating an abnormally prolonged decay of the 1982/83 El Niño event (Supplementary Fig. 11)33. However, this persistent warming likely reflected a residual surface warming signal instead of a true, atmosphere–ocean coupled El Niño state. By JJAS in 1983, subsurface temperature anomalies in the equatorial Pacific had already transitioned to a cold state (Supplementary Fig. 12), which suppressed atmospheric convection over the eastern equatorial Pacific and thereafter weakened the eastward displacement of the Walker circulation (Supplementary Figs. 13–14). Instead, intense warming during the peak phase of the 1982 El Niño induced delayed warming over the North Indian Ocean (Supplementary Fig. 15). This delayed warming, which is referred to as the Indian Ocean capacitor effect34,35, induced an anomalous low-level cyclonic circulation, increased moisture transport into the Indian subcontinent, and intensified monsoon precipitation. Consequently, the year of 1983 emerged as a distinct outlier (Fig. 1), as its monsoon response was governed by delayed Indian ocean warming rather than residual Niño3 SST warming associated with prolonged El Niño decay.

In contrast, the monsoon season of 1997 exhibited typical El Niño conditions, characterised by a weak east‒west tilt in the thermocline and a slowdown of the Walker circulation36, which would be expected to induce severe drought in the Indian subcontinent. However, the ISMR level remained near normal ( + 2%). Although a positive Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) has been proposed to offset El Niño-induced drought37–40, the IOD during the 1997 monsoon season was weaker than that in 197241, when El Niño produced severe drought. This finding suggests that the IOD alone cannot explain the anomalous ISMR response in 1997. The near-normal ISMR anomaly in 1997 resulted primarily from increased rainfall from July–August, thereby offsetting the deficits in June and September. The increased rainfall in July and August was due to the interaction among multiple dynamic processes. Specifically, a westward shift in the monsoon ridge was accompanied by an upper-tropospheric anticyclonic anomaly over the Iranian Plateau42. This anticyclonic anomaly triggered a low-level response that increased moisture convergence and strengthened moisture transport from the Indian Ocean, particularly the Arabian Sea, into the Indian subcontinent (Supplementary Figs. 16–21). Furthermore, anomalous warming in the northern Indian Ocean increased moisture availability to the Indian subcontinent41. Together, these processes counteracted the El Niño-induced drought in July and August, causing the 1997 monsoon to remain near normal levels despite notable ENSO forcing.

Finally, we explored the potential mechanisms underlying the increase in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\gamma }_{{{\rm{ENSO}}}}^{{{\rm{ISMR}}}}$$\end{document}γENSOISMR (Supplementary Fig. 38). The background SST in the tropical Pacific Ocean increased with global warming and led to a higher SST threshold for the occurrence of tropical deep convection46 (e.g., tropical precipitation greater than 2 mm day-1) (Supplementary Fig. 39). Such an upward adjustment of the tropical SST threshold could stabilise the mean state of the tropical atmosphere and weaken the climatological Walker circulation47,48, which could decrease \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\gamma }_{{{\rm{ENSO}}}}^{{{\rm{ISMR}}}}$$\end{document}γENSOISMR. However, the peak of tropical rainfall also increases in a warming world, thereby increasing the sensitivity of deep convection to SST anomalies over the tropical Indo-Pacific Ocean. This increased magnitude of sensitivity suggests that smaller SST anomalies in a warming world would be sufficient to sustain the same intensity of deep convection as that observed during the historical period (Supplementary Fig. 39b). Hence, the SST over the central and eastern tropical Pacific would exceed the convective threshold during El Niño events in a warmer future, allowing the same SST anomalies as those in the past to drive more intense deep convection49. This enhanced ENSO-induced deep convection strengthens the feedback of surface zonal winds, thereby increasing the east‒west SLP and SST anomaly gradients. These strengthened east‒west SLP and SST anomaly gradients drove a further eastward shift in the Walker circulation and enhanced subsidence over the Indian subcontinent50 (Supplementary Fig. 40), leading to greater reductions in ISMR.

Discussion

The aim of this analysis was to comprehensively assess the interannual relationship between the ENSO and ISMR under both current and future greenhouse warming conditions, reconciling previous contradictory research findings. In contrast to the widely reported weakening of the ENSO–ISMR relationship over the last two decades of the twentieth century7, we found that the ENSO remains a significant control on the interannual variation in historical ISMR, independent of the period and the proxy employed to indicate the strength of ISMR. The previously documented weakening relationship resulted primarily from two anomalous events (1983 and 1997), when the Niño3 index values indicated the El Niño phase, whereas ISMR exhibited positive values. A similar phenomenon was observed when the Niño3.4 index was applied, as 1997 remained an outlier that could disrupt the ENSO–ISMR statistical relationship. When multisource observations were used to constrain model biases in ENSO-induced variations in the Walker circulation, the sensitivities of ISMR to the ENSO became ~35–55% stronger than those during the historical period (1963–2023). Such intensification is linked to the possibility that greenhouse warming would cause the same SST warming anomaly during an El Niño event to sustain a higher intensity of deep convection over the tropical Indo-Pacific Ocean. This process would drive a further eastward shift in the Walker circulation, resulting in increased subsidence over the Indian subcontinent and a greater reduction in ISMR. The intensification of the ENSO–ISMR relationship has substantial implications for the stability of food production in India51. For example, in a warmer world, the decrease in ISMR would be greater for a given El Niño strength, potentially resulting in crop yield reductions that are 30% greater than those historically observed during El Niño events (Methods).

The emergent constraint approach to the physical process could not address all climate model biases when the ENSO–ISMR relationship was projected. Specifically, the CMIP6 models failed to reproduce the anomalous years of 1983 and 1997 in the exact calendar years of 1983 and 1997, during which the canonical ENSO–ISMR inverse relationship broke down. This limitation introduces uncertainty in determining whether such events may become more frequent under future warming. Although these anomalies were not captured in the corresponding historical years, the CMIP6 models could simulate analogous events, which were characterised by positive or near-neutral ISMR anomalies during El Niño events, across their broader historical and future simulations. By identifying events analogous to those in 1983 and 1997 in the CMIP6 and the Community Earth System Model version 2 (CESM2), we did not obtain clear evidence for an increased frequency of such anomalies under future warming (Supplementary Figs. 41–42). Nevertheless, these results indicate that such events are unlikely to disappear. This finding suggests that although greenhouse warming is projected to strengthen the overall ENSO–ISMR teleconnection, the responses of ISMR to individual El Niño events will continue to exhibit interevent variability. This variability reflects the modulation of the effect of the ENSO on the relationship with ISMR by multiple factors, including El Niño type, phase of the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD), background Indian Ocean warming, and higher-frequency processes such as synoptic variability and intraseasonal oscillations. Improving the representation of these multiscale interactions is therefore critical for generating more reliable projections.

Methods

Data

Observations and reanalysis datasets. We selected three monthly precipitation datasets spanning the period from 1901–2023, including the Indian Meteorological Department (IMD; 0.25° × 0.25°) dataset from the Indian National Data Centre, the Climatic Research Unit (CRU) Time Series dataset version 4 (0.5° × 0.5°) from the University of East Anglia, and the Global Precipitation Climatology Centre (GPCC; 0.5° × 0.5°) dataset from the German Weather Service. With respect to sea-surface temperature (SST), we employed three datasets spanning their respective periods, including the Hadley Centre Sea Ice and Sea Surface Temperature dataset version 4.0.1.0 (HadISST.4.0.1.0, 1° × 1°, 1850–2023) from the UK Met Office Hadley Centre, the Extended Reconstructed Sea Surface Temperature dataset version 5 (ERSST5, 2° × 2°, 1854–2023) from the International Comprehensive Ocean–Atmosphere Dataset (ICOADS), and the Centennial In Situ Observation-Based Estimates of the Variability in SST (COBE, 1° × 1°, 1891–2023) from the Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA). With respect to SLP, we obtained five datasets, including the Hadley Centre Sea Level Pressure dataset (HadSLP, 5° × 5°, 1850–2019) from the UK Met Office Hadley Centre, the International Comprehensive Ocean–Atmosphere Dataset (ICOADS, 2°×2°, 1622–2023), the National Centres for Environmental Prediction (NCEP) Reanalysis dataset (2.5° × 2.5°, 1948–2023), the Japanese 55-year Reanalysis (JRA55, 1.25° × 1.25°, 1958–2023) dataset, and the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts Reanalysis 5 (ERA5) dataset (0.25° × 0.25°, 1950–2023). Additionally, temperature, omega, and wind data at 17 pressure levels (from 1000 to 10 hPa) from 1950 at a spatial resolution of 2.5° were derived from United States National Centres for Environmental Prediction (NCEP) and National Centre for Atmospheric Research (NCAR) reanalysis products. Indian rice yield data were obtained from the Food and Agriculture Organisation (FAO) of the United Nations.

Climate model simulations

We selected 32 Earth System Models (ESMs) from the CMIP6, which provided both historical (1960–2014) and future simulations (2015–2099)52. For the future projections, we considered only a middle-of-the-road shared socioeconomic pathway scenario (SSP2-4.5) in which radiative forcing is stabilised at 4.5 W m−2 by the year 2100. We extracted the monthly mean precipitation (pr), sea surface temperature (tos), eastward wind (uas), sea level pressure (psl), horizontal wind (ua and va), and vertical omega (wap) from the model output datasets. The CMIP6 model experiments include several initial states (r), initialisation methods (i), physics (p), and forcing schemes (f). We only applied the first realisation (r1i1p1f1) for each ESM in our main analysis (Supplementary Table 1). We used all available CMIP6 large-ensemble experiments in 12 models that included at least three simulations (Supplementary Table 2). Moreover, we employed the monthly mean precipitation from 100 simulations of the CESM2 Large Ensemble Community Project53. All the variables from the CMIP6 output were interpolated at a spatial resolution of 1° using the climate data operator (CDO) first-order conservative regridding method. To focus on the interannual signal, we removed the low-frequency signal (period longer than 11 years) from the sea surface temperature, sea level pressure and horizontal wind data using a Hodrick–Prescott filter54.

Climate index

ENSO index

Here, we mainly adopted the canonical Niño3 index, which is defined as the average SST anomaly from June to September over the tropical Pacific Ocean (150°W–90°W, 5°N–5°S), to represent the magnitude of the ENSO during the monsoon season6,7. This choice was motivated by its consistent use in previous studies within the field, where the weakening and subsequent recovery of the ENSO–ISMR relationship have been linked to the Niño3 index7,11,20,23. In addition, we applied other indices, such as the Niño3.4 index, defined as the average SST anomalies from June to September over the tropical Pacific Ocean (170°W–120°W, 5°N–5°S)5,6. In the CMIP6 models, widespread cold tongue biases reduce the west‒east SST gradient and cause a westward shift in maximum warming during El Niño events (Supplementary Fig. 43). Therefore, we adopted a principal component (PC)-based index as an alternative ENSO index54, which captures the temporal and spatial evolution features of SST anomalies instead of relying on fixed regions such as Niño3 and Niño3.4. Specifically, we conducted empirical orthogonal function (EOF) analysis of the interannual SST variability over the Indo-Pacific Ocean during the 1963–2023 period (50°E–70°W, 10°N–25°S). The first EOF (EOF1) explained more than 60% of the total interannual SST variability, and the first principal component (PC1) of the Indo-Pacific Ocean SST anomaly was strongly correlated with the Niño3 index (R = 0.99, P < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 34a) and the Niño3.4 index (R = 0.94, P < 0.001) during the historical period. These results suggest that the first EOF mode (EOF1) can be used to represent ENSO-related SST variability and support the use of the Niño3 index, as it captures the leading mode of interannual SST variability over the Indo-Pacific Ocean. Moreover, PC1 was regressed onto sea-level pressure (850-hPa wind speed) to denote ENSO-related circulation anomalies. The EOF1 pattern could accurately reproduce the typical equatorial tropical Pacific structure of El Niño SST anomalies, with the warming anomaly centre located in the tropical eastern Pacific (Supplementary Fig. 45). In contrast, EOF2 explained only 10% of the total interannual SST variability over the Indo-Pacific Ocean, and the representative spatial pattern depends on the chosen period. Moreover, our results are robust to the use of different detrending methods and are insensitive to the choice of ENSO metrics (Supplementary Figs. 46–48).

Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) index

We primarily used the principal component-based index as the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) index, which was derived from the time series of the leading Empirical Orthogonal Function (EOF) of SST anomalies over the Indian Ocean (40°E–100°E, 5°S–5°N)55. Additionally, we adopted the Dipole mode index (DMI) to represent the IOD39 (Supplementary Fig. 49), which is defined as the difference in SST anomalies between the western Indian Ocean (50°E–70°E, 20°N–20°S) and the eastern Indian Ocean (90°E–110°E, 0°–10°S).

The Atlantic Niño Index

Similar to the calculation of the IOD index, we employed a principal component (PC)-based index as the Atlantic Niño index (Supplementary Fig. 50), which was derived from the time series of the leading Empirical Orthogonal Function (EOF) of SST anomalies over the Atlantic Ocean56. Additionally, the Atlantic Niño index was calculated as the area-averaged SST anomaly over a specific region of the tropical Atlantic (20°W–0°, 3°N–3°S).

Effects of oceanic climate variability on the ENSO–ISMR relationship

Here, we applied causal discovery networks and Pacific Pacemaker ensemble simulations from the CESM2 to identify the potential confounding effects of other oceanic climate variabilities, such as the IOD and Atlantic Niño, on the ENSO–ISMR relationship.

Pacemaker experiments

We applied outputs from the tropical Pacific Pacemaker experiments conducted with the Community Earth System Model (CESM2). In those simulations, ENSO-associated SST anomalies in the central and eastern Pacific were nudged towards observations within the 15°N–15°S latitude range, while the remainder of the climate system evolved freely. Such nudging in dynamic models could eliminate the well-known cold biases that greatly affect the accurate simulation of ENSO characteristics and ensure that the potential influence of internal processes in the tropical Indian Ocean and Atlantic Ocean is eliminated, allowing the interannual variability in ISMR to be primarily and independently driven by realistic ENSO events. We calculated the sensitivity of ISMR to the ENSO for each of the ten ensemble members and subsequently averaged these sensitivities across all members to determine the sensitivity of ISMR to the ENSO (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\gamma }_{{{\rm{ENSO}}}}^{{{\rm{ISMR}}}}$$\end{document}γENSOISMR). By comparing the sensitivity of ISMR to the ENSO derived from the Pacemaker experiments with observations, we could isolate the effect of the ENSO on ISMR from any feedback from other oceans, including IOD and Atlantic Niño events independent of the ENSO. To investigate the individual contributions of SST anomalies in different ocean basins to the variability in ISMR and to separate the complex interactions among the ENSO, IOD, and Atlantic Niño, we also conducted tropical Indian, Atlantic, and Pacific Pacemaker experiments using the CESM1 model57 (Supplementary Fig. 51). These experiments followed the same design as the Pacific Pacemaker experiments with the CESM2, with the key distinction that SST anomalies were independently applied to each of the three ocean basins, Indian, Atlantic, and Pacific Oceans, to isolate their respective influences on the variability in ISMR.

Bootstrap test

To rigorously evaluate the ENSO-related variability and its association with ISMR, we employed a nested bootstrap resampling framework58. This hierarchical approach accounts for two primary sources of uncertainty in climate projections, namely, intermodel diversity and internal climate variability.

First, we conducted R = 5000 iterations of bootstrap resampling across the ensembles of the CMIP6 models. In each iteration, a subset of models was randomly selected (with replacement) to construct an ensemble mean, thereby ensuring that the resulting metrics were not overly sensitive to the inclusion of any single outlier model.

Second, we implemented a block-sampling technique for each selected model. Rather than adopting a fixed historical segment, we randomly sampled a continuous 60-year window from the full length of the pi-Control experiment, which preserved the low-frequency evolution and autocorrelation structures of the tropical Pacific climate system60.

Third, the final ENSO standard deviation and the sensitivity of ISMR to the ENSO index were calculated as the mean of these resampled ensembles. The 90th (95th and 97.5th) confidence intervals were derived via the use of the basic bootstrap percentile method from the resulting distribution. This dual-layer strategy ensures that our reported significance levels reflect a high degree of consensus across the multimodel ensemble while remaining robust against stochastic noise in internal decadal fluctuations.

Calculating ENSO-related rice yield changes

The monthly rice yield data of India from 1963–2023 was obtained from the Food and Agriculture Organisation (FAO). To better understand ENSO-related rice yield changes during the monsoon period, the relationship between the variations in crop yield and climate during the monsoon season from 1963 to 2023 must be determined. To do this, we first conducted a multiple linear regression with the first-differenced yield serving as the dependent variable and the first-differenced ISMR and temperature serving as independent variables. To avoid spurious associations arising from the coincident effects of technological advances on crop yields and long-term climate change (such as global warming), we considered only first-differenced time series. The first difference was calculated by removing the previous year’s value from the current year. Climate change (both changes in ISMR and temperature) explained more than half of the total variability in crop yields during the historical period (R = 0.70, P < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 52). Second, we calculated ISMR changes due to changes in sensitivities and then changes in rice yield based on the above-established multiple regression model.

Filtering 1983- and 1997-like events in the historical and future simulations

To identify unusual events analogous to those observed in 1983 and 1997, which were characterised by the co-occurrence of El Niño events and positive ISMR anomalies in the model simulations, we established thresholds based on the El Niño index and ISMR anomalies observed in 1983 and 1997. Specifically, we analysed the outputs from 32 CMIP6 models and 100 ensemble members from the Community Earth System Model version 2 Large Ensemble (CESM2-LE) project. For the year 1983, we defined an event as unusual if the El Niño index value exceeded 1 °C and if the ISMR anomalies were greater than 15%. For the year 1997, the thresholds were set as an El Niño index value exceeding 2.4 °C and ISMR anomalies greater than 3%. These criteria were applied to the model simulations to identify analogous events across historical and future periods. By subsequently comparing the frequency of these events between the historical and future periods, we determined whether events similar to those in 1983 or 1997 are likely to increase in the future.
