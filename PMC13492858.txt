Accreditation as a catalyst for improving child nutrition services in residential care: A case study
Authors: Dewi Kartikawati, Binahayati Rusyidi, Nurliana C. Apsari, Sri Sulastri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Child nutrition is essential for physical growth, cognitive development, and overall well-being, particularly in residential care institutions that replace family-based care. In Indonesia, accreditation systems were introduced to strengthen the quality of services in Lembaga Kesejahteraan Sosial Anak (LKSA), including nutrition services. However, evidence remains limited on how accreditation influences concrete improvements in child nutrition.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This case study was conducted at LKSA Miftahul Falah, Karawang Regency, West Java Province, Indonesia. Prior to accreditation, nutrition services were irregular and unmonitored, with several children at risk of malnutrition. Accreditation-driven reforms included the development of standard operating procedures (SOPs) for nutrition, structured meal planning based on national guidelines, routine anthropometric examinations, and mandatory caregiver training.RESULTS:Postintervention assessments revealed substantial improvements. Nutrition services became more consistent, meal planning adhered to nutritional standards, and regular anthropometric monitoring was established. Caregiver training enhanced accountability and service delivery. Importantly, child health outcomes improved, with fewer children at risk of malnutrition following the reforms.CONCLUSION:Accreditation served as a catalyst for long-term improvements in child nutrition services at LKSA Miftahul Falah. The experience underscores the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration and evidence-based policy adoption to transform institutional care practices and enhance child welfare outcomes.

Introduction

Proper nutrition played an essential role when it comes to the physical growth of children, their ability to think, as well as their overall well-being and this is especially true in residential care where family-based care is replaced by an institutional care setting. Children with living experience in Lembaga Kesejahteraan Sosial Anak (LKSA) institutions are usually sans an institutional challenge of getting a balanced diet. These obstacles are an inappropriate meal schedule, occasional observance of nutritional status, poor caregivers education, and poor cooperation with the local health system, and these may lead to malnutrition and delay developments.

In order to handle these problems, the Indonesian Ministry of Social Affairs has come up with a national accreditation system that will improve the quality of services offered at the child welfare organizations. Accreditation is used as an institutional tool to implement standards in the fields of nutrition, health, child protection, education, and organizational governance. Although there is a lot of information about the efficacy of accreditation in the healthcare and education systems, a little has been done to investigate the efficacy of accreditation in enhancing nutrition services in residential care facilities. The literature suggests that accreditation may lead to the enhancement of the quality of the delivered services, the strengthening of the organizational learning, and the increased accountability, but the majority of the studies have been done concerning hospitals or the general social services, but not the child residential care.[1]

This case study focuses on LKSA Miftahul Falah in Karawang Regency, West Java Province, as a case of how accreditation can trigger institutional change in child nutrition service. Before accreditation, nutrition practices were informal, nonregular and unmonitored.[2] A number of children showed poor growth and no systematic anthropometric or dietary evaluations were done. standard operating procedures (SOPs) on nutrition were developed in response to the requirements of accreditation to guide interventions, adhere to national and WHO guidelines, come up with structured meal planning and propose the use of routine anthropometric assessments. An extensive caregiver education program was also introduced, as well as official collaboration with the local health posts (Puskesmas) that were used to facilitate periodical health checkups and interventions.[34]

The worth of this study is that it has demonstrated accreditation as a powerful tool in facilitating quantifiable changes in nutrition services in residential care settings a field where there is limited empirical support.[5] The paper goes further to explain how accreditation promotes sustainable change through structured interventions, systematic monitoring and outcome assessment. Moreover, it uses foreign sources related to the impact of accreditation on nonhealth sectors and emphasizes the importance of standardization, evidence-based policies, and interdisciplinary partnerships to improve the quality of services and child health.[6]

Theoretical framework

Description

This positive model shows how the accreditation demands become the agent of the institutional changes in residential child care. Accreditation also enhances development of standard operating procedures (SOPs) in nutrition, structured training of the caregivers, collaboration with the local health centers and adoption of monitoring and evaluation systems.[7] All these reforms help in the quantifiable improvements in the child nutrition services and they include improvement in service quality, improved anthropometric outcomes, improved accountability of caregivers and adherence to national and international health policy standards. This model puts more emphasis on the causation between accreditation and results and how well formulated standards, interdisciplinary co-operation and policies converge to bring a sustainable change in an institution.

Case Presentation and Methodology

Study setting and participants

This case study was conducted at LKSA Miftahul Falah, a residential child care institution in Karawang Regency, West Java Province, Indonesia. The institution provides shelter, education, and basic care for approximately 30 children aged 4-16 years. Prior to accreditation, nutrition services were informal, inconsistent, and lacked standardized monitoring. Several children exhibited suboptimal growth and irregular weight-for-age ratios.

Participant selection

Children were sampled on the basis of purposive sampling, whereby children who had a minimum of 6 months of stay in the institution and who were available to undergo normal anthropometric measurements were sampled. This was a way of having a stationary population to observe both pre- and postintervention nutritional experiences. Institutional Ethics Committee (Approval Code: LKSA/ETH/2023/05) approved the ethical approval, and consent was obtained by the caregivers and the legal guardians.

Project/Study ID: LKSA-NUTRI-2023-001.

Study design

This study employed a single-institution case study design to evaluate the effect of accreditation-driven reforms on child nutrition services.[8] The design focused on documenting and analyzing changes in institutional practices, caregiver behaviors, and child health outcomes before and after implementing accreditation requirements.

Rationale

Case study methodology was chosen because it allows in-depth exploration of processes and outcomes within the real-life context of a residential care institution, providing insights into the mechanisms through which accreditation drives improvement.

Discussion

It has been demonstrated that accreditation may be significant in institutional reform in residential child care, in this case, nutrition services. Structured SOPs introduction, particular training of care providers, routine tracking, and collaboration of health services, led to a child nutritional outcome, and the improvement of the institutional practice.

Effects of nutrition services accreditation

The accreditation standards resulted in an organization wide change in practice with the informality of practice being substituted by the standardized practices that were monitored.[1516] Meal planning previously was not regular, there was no regular follow up to the anthropometric outcome and the caregivers lacked the formal nutrition knowledge that they needed. The nutritional status of the children was also significantly improved after the intervention with one of every five of the underweight kids progressing to normal weight-for-age at the conclusion of six months. The variety of meals also increased, on average of 5 food groups per meal being recommended by the country/WHO. The accountability and the shift in the institutional culture were the results of the SOP compliance of the caregivers.[17]

These findings align with the previous research that suggested accreditation to be organizational learning driver, performance improvement, and accountability.[18] Unlike in the past where reports have been keen on healthcare facilities, this contribution means that accreditation frameworks which are systematized can be probably relevant to residential child care environments, thus implying that they are not only relevant within the normal practice of clinical settings.

Caregiver training and interdisciplinary collaboration role

The key success factors of the observed improvements have been identified as the efficient role of collaboration and incorporation of caregiver training into the local health centers (Puskesmas). The empowerment of the caregivers by training them on how to measure the nutritional status, how to prepare nutritious food and when malnutrition is occurring. The inclusion of collaborations with health workers introduced another external procedure in the validation procedure and contributed to the consistency of the anthropometric measurement that guaranteed the reliability of the data and served the best practices.

This corroborates the evidence of foreign authors, that interdisciplinary collaboration can enhance services offered to children in child welfare organizations, particularly when technical expertise and practice management are involved. In brief, it was the scaffolding and the motor that the accreditation provided to the system and it was the training and collaboration that enabled the quality standards to be put into practice.

Methodological considerations

This study employed a purposeful selection strategy and triangulated the data collection strategies to attain methodological rigor. Anthropometry measurements were recorded in the calibrated equipment, and observation of SOPs was included in the system. Independent checks of a subset of the measurements were conducted to retain interobserver reliability to minimize possibilities of bias. Nevertheless, there were still very few limitations: the study was conducted within a single organization, which limited the ability to generalize the results, and the period of follow-up was relatively short. The results can also have been affected as a result of observer bias and staff flip, and resource constraints.

Outside forces played a role in the improvements, such as donor support and the ongoing involvement of Puskesmas. Such contextual issues are to be taken into account when interpreting the findings and planning to replicate them in other residential institutions of care. Comparison and review of the opposing literature

As the literature presents, accreditation plays a crucial role in the healthcare field to improve the quality of healthcare, the performance of these organizations, and the provision of services.[11] The present paper has the potential to contribute to this growing body of literature, as it demonstrates that the same can be said of the residential care nutrition services. Just like those that are applied in education and social work settings, there is the application of organized standards, education, and evaluation to report sustainable transformation in services.[12]

It is worth noting that accreditation is not a mere compliance process, as the study supports the idea that accreditation serves as a strategic instrument to increase institutional capacity, staff competence, and child outcomes.

Conclusion

Based on this case study, it has been established that accreditation could be regarded as a strategic change agent to help improve the child nutrition services in the residential care facilities. A good mix of the structure of the standard operating procedures (SOPs), the selective training of caregivers, frequent control, and formalization of collaboration with the local health services were predetermined by positive changes in the institutional practice and level of child outcomes.

The evidence has shown that accreditation is not some checklist activity that involves checking the compliance but also becomes a factor that helps the institutions to be responsible and allows employees to be knowledgeable and provide a sustainable quality of their service provision. The upgrading of the meal planning, nutritional control, compliance of caregivers and interdisciplinary collaboration are consistent with the requirements of the global best practices and national child nutrition policies, and it proves that the work meets the standard of the health policy requirements.

The results of the present research will indicate to policy makers and institutional managers the need to put accreditation frameworks into consideration in residential care facilities in order to make evidence-based changes in residential care facilities to secure the provision of harmonized high quality nutritional services to children in residential care. It would be well in future projects to scale interventions on behalf of accreditation across numerous institutions and to consider interventions in a longitudinal manner and determine the sustainability of interventions long term and a broader impact on child welfare outcomes.
