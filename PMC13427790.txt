Regulation of Tph cell differentiation via farnesoid X receptor of dendritic cells in inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Zhen Hu, Minya Liu, Chengkai Zhu, Na Li, Xiaotian Chen, Binlin Da, Yi Li, Chenggong Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeripheral helper T cells represent a recently characterized subset of CD4+ T cells present in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) patients. Bile acids act as signaling molecules in immune regulation. However, the role of bile acids in Tph cell differentiation remains unclear.MethodsImmunofluorescence staining was used to detect the presence of Tph cells in intestinal tissue samples; Wild-type (WT) and Fxr−/− mice were treated with or without the FXR agonist obeticholic acid (OCA) in dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced acute colitis. The proportions of Tph cells and innate immune cells were analyzed by FACS. Bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) and naïve T cells of WT and Fxr−/− mice were sorted, differentiated and cultured in vitro to observe the regulatory effects of FXR on BMDCs function and Tph cell differentiation.ResultsTph proportion was significantly elevated in inflamed intestinal tissues. OCA treatment increased the Tph cell proportion in the intestinal LP of WT mice. The proportion of dendritic cells (DCs) in the LP of Fxr−/− mice was significantly lower than WT mice after DSS-mediated colitis induction. FXR deficiency impaired the ability of BMDCs to induce Tph cell differentiation in vitro. CD11c and MHC-II expression levels were reduced in BMDCs from Fxr−/− mice upon stimulation with LPS. FXR deficiency activated the PPAR-γ signaling pathway and decreased IL-12 expression in BMDCs.ConclusionTph cell levels were elevated in inflamed intestinal tissues. FXR suppression in BMDCs was associated with activated PPAR-γ signaling; and with reduced DC maturation, IL-12 secretion, and Tph cell differentiation.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00011-026-02332-4.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is characterized by extensive dysregulation of intestinal immune responses, including abnormal activation of innate immune cells, abnormal differentiation of helper T cells, and substantial B cell infiltration. B cells play a critical role in IBD pathogenesis. Studies have shown that the expression of cluster of differentiation (CD)86 on activated B cells is a key factor in enhancing pro-inflammatory cytokine production by intestinal CD4+ T cells [1]; activated B cells disrupt stromal-epithelial regenerative cross-talk during mucosal healing [2]. Among helper T cells, certain subsets provide functional support to B cells, including T follicular helper (Tfh; CD4+CXCR5+PD-1high) cells and the recently defined peripheral helper T (Tph; CD4+CXCR5−PD-1high) cells. These two subsets are found in follicular and peripheral inflammatory tissues, respectively; they contribute to the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis [3] and systemic lupus erythematosus [4]. Single-cell RNA sequencing of intestinal tissue from ulcerative colitis patients identified a subset of CXCL13-expressing Tfh-like Tph cells associated with pathogenic B cell responses [5]. Mass cytometry of blood and intestinal tissue from IBD patients also identified CD3+CD4+CD45RO+CXCR5−PD-1+ Tph cells, with significantly higher frequencies in tissue than in blood, suggesting a local role for these cells in intestinal inflammation [6].

Gut microbiota dysbiosis and altered bile acid metabolism contribute to the disruption of intestinal immune homeostasis in IBD. The farnesoid X receptor (FXR), a key bile acid receptor, is widely expressed in the small intestine and colon. Previous studies have demonstrated its importance in immune regulation. It was shown that bile acids can regulate the differentiation of T cell, mainly focused on Th17 and regulatory T (Treg) cells [7, 8]; however, current therapies targeting Th17 cells have yielded limited clinical success. Thus, it is important to explore the roles of other pro-inflammatory T cell subsets in the gut and identify novel therapeutic targets. Tph cells infiltrate inflamed intestinal tissue and contribute to disease progression, targeting Tph cells might be a potential strategy to modulate abnormal B cell responses and lead to novel therapeutic approaches in IBD patients; however, few studies have explored the regulatory effect of bile acids on Tph cell differentiation. This study aimed to clarify the role of FXR in regulating Tph cell differentiation.

Materials and methods

Patients

In total, six IBD patients were included in this study. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing General Hospital of Nanjing Military Command (2022DZKY-048–01). Patients were recruited between March 1, 2024, and May 31, 2024. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The diagnosis of IBD was based on clinical, endoscopic, and histological criteria. Surgical specimens were collected from these patients for immunofluorescence staining.

Mice

Specific pathogen-free C57BL/6 J and Fxr−/− mice (4–6 weeks old) were purchased from the Shanghai Model Organisms Center and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at the Animal Center of Drum Tower Hospital (Nanjing, China). All experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital Clinical College of Nanjing Medical University (2024AE01064).

Acute colitis induction by DSS

WT mice were randomized into three groups (six/group): control, colitis treated with vehicle, and colitis treated with OCA. WT and Fxr−/− mice were randomized into six groups: WT control, WT colitis treated with vehicle, WT colitis treated with OCA, Fxr−/− control, Fxr−/− colitis treated with vehicle, and Fxr−/− colitis treated with OCA. Acute colitis was induced by administering 2.5% (w/v) DSS (MP Biomedicals, molecular weight: 36,000–50,000) in drinking water ad libitum for 5 days, followed by normal drinking water for an additional 2 days. In treatment groups, either vehicle or OCA (5 mg/kg, HY-12222, MedChemExpress, China) was administered by intragastric gavage once daily starting 3 days before DSS induction and continuing until the day before sacrifice. Vehicle control contained equivalent volumes of dimethyl sulfoxide and 20% sulfobutylether-β-cyclodextrin (HY-17031, MedChemExpress). To evaluate DSS-induced intestinal injury and inflammation, DAI scores were recorded daily. Body weight loss, stool consistency, and rectal bleeding were assessed using established criteria [9]. Mice were euthanized by isoflurane inhalation on day 7. The spleen, MLNs, and colons were harvested, cleaned, and processed for RNA extraction, flow cytometry, histological analysis, and western blotting.

Colon histopathological grading and immunofluorescence staining

Mouse colon tissue specimens were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 12–24 h, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Sections were subjected to HE staining; blinded histopathological scoring was performed by two pathologists using Neurath’s scoring criteria as previously described [10]. For human intestinal tissue, multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed according to standardized protocols [11]. Briefly, 4-µm paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized and subjected to antigen retrieval. Non-specific binding was blocked using 10% fetal calf serum and 1% bovine serum albumin. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-PD-1 (1:500, ab237728, Abcam, UK), anti-CD4 (1:2000, ab133616, Abcam), and anti-CXCR5 (1:200, ab254415, Abcam). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (P0131, Beyotime Biotechnology, China).

Single-cell preparation and isolation of intestinal LP

Single-cell suspensions from mouse spleen and MLNs were prepared by mechanical dissociation and subsequent filtration through a 70-μm nylon mesh strainer. Intestinal LP cell isolation was performed as previously described [12], with minor modifications. Briefly, intestinal segments were incubated in Hanks’ balanced salt solution (with Ca2+ and Mg2+) containing 1 mM dithiothreitol and 2 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid at 37 °C in a shaking incubator for 20 min. The tissue was cut into 2-mm pieces and digested in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 0.5 mg/mL type IV collagenase (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and 0.15 mg/mL DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C in a shaking incubator for 20 min. Single cells were isolated using a 40%/80% discontinuous Percoll gradient (Biosharp, China) centrifuged at 1000 × g for 20 min. Cells from the interface layer were collected, washed, and resuspended for downstream analyses.

Flow cytometry (FACS)

Flow cytometry was performed on 1 × 106 cells per sample. For PD-1high T cell quantification, cells were first incubated with FcγR-blocking monoclonal antibody (553,141, BD Biosciences, USA) for 10 min at 4 °C, then washed twice in cold phosphate-buffered saline. Cells were then stained with phosphate-buffered saline containing 1% bovine serum albumin and the following antibodies for 45 min: Fixable Viability Dye eFluor™ 780 (65–0865-14, eBioscience, USA), anti-CD4–fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) (557,307, BD Biosciences), anti-CXCR5–phycoerythrin (PE)–Cy7 (560,617, BD Biosciences), anti-PD-1-BB700 (566,514, BD Biosciences), anti-CD11c-PerCP-Cy5.5 (560,584, BD Biosciences), anti-MHC-II-FITC (562,009, BD Biosciences), anti-ICOSL-PE (12–5985-82, eBioscience), anti-OX40L–allophycocyanin (APC) (17–5905-82, eBioscience), anti-Ly6G-FITC (551,460, BD Biosciences), anti-Ly6C-APC (560,595, BD Biosciences), anti-CD11b–peridinin-chlorophyll-protein (PerCP)–Cy5.5 (550,993, BD Biosciences), and anti-F4/80-PE (565,410, BD Biosciences). For intranuclear transcription factor staining, the Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (00-5523-00, eBioscience) was used in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were stained with anti-Foxp3-PE (560408, BD Biosciences) for 50 min. After staining, cells were washed, resuspended, and analyzed using a BD FACSCanto II Flow Cytometry System.

Isolation and differentiation of BMDCs

WT and Fxr−/− mice were sacrificed, and bone marrow cells were collected and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (20 ng/mL, PeproTech, USA), and IL-4 (10 ng/mL, PeproTech). In the treatment groups, cells were cultured with either dimethyl sulfoxide or OCA (10 μM, HY-12222, MedChemExpress). The culture medium was replaced every 2 days. On day 6, the resulting BMDCs were harvested. The harvested cells were stimulated with 1 μg/mL LPS (L4391, Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 h and collected for further analysis.

Isolation of naïve T cells and co-culture of BMDCs and naïve T cells

Spleens were collected from WT and Fxr−/− mice. After erythrocyte lysis, cell pellets were resuspended and naïve CD4+ T cells were isolated using the Naïve CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (130-104-453, Miltenyi, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the BMDC–T cell co-culture system, 3 × 105 naïve CD4+ T cells from WT or Fxr−/− mice were activated with plate-bound anti-CD3e (16–0031-85, eBioscience, 5ug/ml) for 24 h. They were then co-cultured with 3 × 104 BMDCs from WT or Fxr−/− mice with or without IL-12 (10 ng/mL, PeproTech, USA) in 96-well round-bottom plates for 72 h (37℃, 5% CO2). After incubation, cells were harvested for further analysis.

RNA extraction and reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from colon tissues and cultured cells using FreeZol Reagent (R711-01-AA, Vazyme, China). RNA purity and concentration were determined with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ND-2000c). Complementary DNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using a reverse transcription kit. Quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q312, Vazyme) on a Light Cycler 480 II (Roche). Primers are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Cycle threshold (Ct) values were obtained, and gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blot analysis

Colon tissues or cultured cells were collected and lysed on ice for 30 min using radioimmunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime Biotechnology) supplemented with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (ST505, Beyotime Biotechnology) and a protease inhibitor cocktail (P1005, Beyotime Biotechnology). Lysates were centrifuged, and supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0012, Beyotime Biotechnology). Proteins were denatured at 100 °C for 10 min and separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Electrophoresis was conducted at 80 V for 30 min, followed by 120 V for 60 min. Proteins were then transferred onto a 0.2-μm polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Millipore, USA) at 220 mA for 2 h. Membranes were blocked for 2 h at room temperature in Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 containing 5% skim milk, then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used were anti-FXR (1:1000, 417,200, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and anti-PPAR-γ (1:1000, ab272718, Abcam). Membranes were washed, then incubated with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence western blotting reagents (Millipore) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Luminex and CBA

Concentrations of CCL1, CCL2, CCL3, CCL4, CCL5, CCL7, and CX3CL1 in colon tissue homogenates were measured using the Luminex X-MAP system (Luminex, Austin, TX, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (LXMultiDTH-10, LabEx Co., Shanghai, China). Levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-12p70 were quantified using CBA assays with the following kits: Mouse IL-6 Flex Set (558301, BD Biosciences), Mouse TNF-α Flex Set (558299, BD Biosciences), and Mouse IL-12p70 Flex Set (558303, BD Biosciences). Data were acquired using an LSR II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with CBA analysis software.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA) and are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test, and one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) was used for multiple group comparisons. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001).

Results

Tph and Tfh cell levels were significantly elevated in inflamed tissues of IBD patients and in a mouse model of colitis

To assess the expression of Tph and Tfh cells in IBD, surgical specimens were collected from six IBD patients; samples were obtained from both inflamed and non-inflamed regions. Hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining revealed substantial infiltration of lymphocytes and plasma cells in inflamed areas, along with follicle-like structures in some regions. In contrast, only minimal infiltration was observed in non-inflamed areas (Fig. 1A). Immunofluorescence staining showed that Tfh cells primarily infiltrated follicular structures within inflamed regions, whereas Tph cells were predominantly located outside the follicles. Both Tfh and Tph cells were scarcely detected in non-inflamed tissue from the same patients (Fig. 1B). To confirm these findings, we established a dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)–induced acute colitis model in mice. After 7 days of DSS treatment, the proportions of Tph and Tfh cells in the spleen were significantly increased. Additionally, the proportion of Tph cells in mesenteric lymph nodes (MLNs) was significantly elevated. Although the increase in Tfh cells within MLNs was not statistically significant, an upward trend was observed (Fig. 1C). OCA treatment decreased Tph cell proportions in the spleen and MLNs of wild-type (WT) mice

To investigate whether the bile acid receptor FXR regulates Tph or Tfh cells, we administered the FXR agonist obeticholic acid (OCA) to WT mice via intragastric gavage (Fig. 2A). Previous studies have shown that FXR agonists alleviate DSS-induced colitis, and we sought to verify this effect. Since FXR activation can upregulate the expression of ileal bile acid-binding protein (IBABP), we first examined the mRNA expression level of ibabp in the ileum. The OCA-treated group showed a significant increase in ibabp expression (Fig. 2B), confirming treatment efficacy. Subsequently, we observed significant improvements in colon length (Fig. 2C), body weight (Fig. 2D) and disease activity index (DAI) scores (Fig. 2E) among OCA-treated mice. HE staining revealed a more intact intestinal epithelial structure, reduced crypt damage, and diminished inflammatory cell infiltration in the OCA group, consistent with previous findings (Fig. 2F). To further clarify the role of FXR in regulating Tph and Tfh cells, we assessed their proportions in the spleen and MLNs by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). The results showed a significant reduction in Tph cell proportions in both the spleen and MLNs following OCA treatment, whereas the change of Tfh cell proportions was not significant (Fig. 2G–H). Tph cell proportions in the spleen and MLNs were lower among Fxr−/− colitis mice than among WT mice

To further validate the above hypothesis, we established DSS-induced colitis models in both WT and Fxr−/− mice. The result showed that Fxr−/− mice exhibited more severe colonic injury, as indicated by reduced colon length (Fig. 2I–K), greater body weight loss (Fig. 2L), and higher DAI scores (Fig. 2M). HE staining revealed more pronounced epithelial damage and increased inflammatory cell infiltration in the intestines of Fxr−/− mice (Fig. 2N). FACS analysis of spleen and MLN tissues unexpectedly showed that the proportions of Tph cells were significantly lower in Fxr−/− mice than in WT mice (Fig. 2O–P). Considering that Tph cells predominantly infiltrate inflamed tissues, we next examined the influence of FXR on Tph cell proportions in the intestinal lamina propria (LP).

OCA treatment increased the Tph cell proportion in the LP of WT mice

We induced colitis in WT and Fxr−/− mice, with or without OCA treatment. HE staining demonstrated that OCA alleviated colitis in WT mice, consistent with previous results; however, this effect was absent in Fxr−/− mice, indicating that OCA’s anti-colitis effect is FXR-dependent (Fig. 3A–B). FACS analysis of lamina propria mononuclear cells (LPMCs) showed an increased Tph cell proportion after DSS induction, which further increased upon OCA treatment in WT mice. In contrast, Fxr−/− colitis mice showed only a slight, non-significant increase in Tph cells; this level remained unchanged after OCA administration (Fig. 3C–D). We also measured the Tph cell proportion in the spleens of all six mouse groups. Tph levels were enhanced in DSS treated WT mice, but remained unchanged in Fxr−/− mice; and OCA treatment significantly reduced splenic Tph cell proportions compared with the vehicle group in WT mice, however, in Fxr−/− mice, splenic Tph levels did not significantly change (Fig. 3E–F). Combining the data from the intestinal LP and spleen, we hypothesized that OCA treatment may promote Tph cell differentiation and potentially facilitate chemotactic migration of these cells from the spleen to the intestine. To verify this hypothesis, we used Luminex assays to assess intestinal chemokine expression in WT and Fxr−/− mice after DSS treatment. Considering that Tph cells primarily express C–C chemokine receptor (CCR) type 2, CCR5, and C-X3-C motif chemokine receptor 1 (CX3CR1), we measured the expression levels of their corresponding ligands, including C–C motif ligand (CCL) 1, CCL2, CCL3, CCL4, CCL5, and CCL7. The results showed that expression levels of these chemokines were significantly reduced in the intestines of Fxr−/− mice, whereas C-X3-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (CX3CL1) expression exhibited a non-significant downward trend (Fig. 3G). The dendritic cell proportion in the intestinal LP was significantly reduced among Fxr−/− colitis mice

Considering that T-cell activation and differentiation require antigen-presenting cells, which also serve as key sources of chemokines, we speculated that FXR may regulate Tph cells through interactions between antigen-presenting cells and T cells. To explore this possibility, we analyzed the proportions of innate immune cells in the LP of WT and Fxr−/− mice before and after DSS treatment via FACS. We assessed macrophages (CD11b+F4/80+), monocytes (CD11b+Ly6C+), neutrophils (CD11b+Ly6G+), and dendritic cells (CD11c+MHC-II+). The results revealed a significant reduction in the proportion of dendritic cells in the intestinal LP of Fxr−/− mice compared with WT mice; the proportions of macrophages, monocytes, and neutrophils did not significantly differ (Fig. 4A–B). We also treated the WT mice with OCA, and the results showed that OCA treatment increased the proportion of CD11c+MHC-II+ cells in the LP of WT mice (Fig. 4C–D). FXR deficiency impaired the ability of BMDCs to induce Tph cell differentiation in vitro

Bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) from WT mice were isolated, differentiated, and cultured with or without OCA, then co-cultured with naïve T cells to assess Tph cell differentiation. The results showed that OCA-pretreated BMDCs significantly enhanced the ability to induce Tph cell differentiation from naïve T cells (Fig. 4E, G). In contrast, BMDCs from Fxr−/− mice exhibited an impaired capacity to induce Tph cells (Fig. 4F, H). To exclude potential effects of T cells, BMDCs and naïve T cells from both WT and Fxr−/− mice were cross-cultured. The results indicated that FXR deficiency in naïve T cells led to a slight increase in Tph cell proportions, whereas FXR deficiency in BMDCs resulted in a substantial decrease in Tph differentiation (Fig. 4I–J). These in vitro co-culture experiments indicated that the absence of FXR in BMDCs impairs their ability to induce Tph cell differentiation.

BMDCs with FXR deficiency exhibit reduced maturation capacity

To identify which functional characteristics of dendritic cells are affected by FXR deficiency and potentially responsible for impaired Tph differentiation, we first examined the expression of co-stimulatory molecules in BMDCs. Previous studies have shown that OX40 ligand (OX40L) and inducible co-stimulator ligand (ICOSL) are key co-stimulatory molecules involved in Tfh differentiation. Given the possible overlap in developmental pathways between Tfh and Tph cells, we evaluated OX40L and ICOSL expression in BMDCs from WT and Fxr−/− mice. The results showed a mild increasing trend in their expression after FXR knockout (Fig. 5A–D). We next assessed BMDC maturation by analyzing the expression patterns of CD11c and major histocompatibility complex-II (MHC-II). BMDCs treated with OCA exhibited an upward trend in both markers, indicating enhanced maturation, whereas BMDCs from Fxr−/− mice showed a downward trend (Fig. 5E–H). FXR knockdown activates PPAR-γ signaling in BMDCs and reduces interleukin (IL)-12 expression

To elucidate the mechanism by which FXR influences BMDC maturation, BMDCs derived from WT and Fxr−/− mice were isolated and cultured in vitro with or without lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation. RNA sequencing was performed, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis revealed a significant enrichment of the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) signaling pathway in Fxr−/− BMDCs (Fig. 5I–L). Further in vitro validation verified that PPAR-γ expression was significantly upregulated in BMDCs from Fxr−/− mice at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 5M–N). Previous studies have shown that PPAR-γ activation suppresses synthesis of IL-12, an essential cytokine for Tph cell differentiation. We thus evaluated the IL-12 production capacity of BMDCs. Cytokine levels in BMDC culture supernatants were measured using a cytometric bead array (CBA). The results showed that, upon LPS stimulation, BMDCs from Fxr−/− mice produced increased levels of IL-6 and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, consistent with previous findings. However, their ability to secrete IL-12 was significantly reduced. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis confirmed that IL-12 mRNA expression was substantially downregulated in BMDCs from Fxr−/− mice (Fig. 5O–R). When IL-12 was exogenously supplemented to the BMDC–naive T cell co-culture system, the function of BMDCs from Fxr−/− mice in inducing Tph cell differentiation was enhanced compared to before (Fig. 5S, T), which indicated that IL-12 might serve as an intermediate through which FXR in BMDCs modulates Tph cell differentiation. Collectively, these findings are consistent with the possibility that FXR deficiency in BMDCs is associated with elevated PPAR-γ expression, impaired dendritic cell maturation and decreased IL-12 secretion, which might contribute to the reduced capacity to induce Tph cell differentiation.

Discussion

IBD pathogenesis remains incompletely understood, although intestinal immune dysregulation plays a critical role. Previous studies have primarily focused on Th17 and regulatory T (Treg) cells [13, 14]; however, current therapeutic approaches targeting Th17 cells have not achieved satisfactory results in clinical applications. Thus, it is important to explore the roles of other pro-inflammatory T cell subsets in the gut and identify novel therapeutic targets. Tph cells, first identified in 2017 from the synovial tissue and fluid of rheumatoid arthritis patients [15], represent a recently characterized subset of pro-inflammatory CD4+ T cells (CD4+CXCR5−PD-1high). Tph cells express high levels of programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), secrete IL-21 and CXCL13, and participate in inflammatory responses. Tph cells are abnormally activated in various autoimmune diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, and multiple sclerosis [16]. Functionally, Tph cells resemble Tfh cells in supporting B cell activity, but they differ in localization: Tfh cells are primarily found in follicular structures, whereas Tph cells reside in inflamed tissues outside follicles [17]. In recent years, Tph cells have also been detected in IBD patients [5, 6, 18], although their roles and functional contributions remain unclear. In our study, immunofluorescence staining of surgical specimens from IBD patients confirmed the presence of Tfh cells in follicular-like structures and Tph cells in inflamed regions outside these structures. The proportions of both cell types were substantially reduced in non-inflamed areas within the same patients. Additionally, we observed increased proportions of Tfh and Tph cells in the spleen and MLNs of mice with DSS-induced acute colitis, suggesting that both subsets may be involved in the inflammatory process.

Disorders of intestinal bile acid metabolism have been widely reported in IBD patients [19–21]. In addition to their roles in metabolic processes, bile acids function as signaling molecules that regulate immune responses. FXR, the most extensively studied bile acid receptor, is primarily expressed in intestinal epithelial cells, hepatocytes, and certain immune cells [22]. Beyond its regulation of bile acid absorption and metabolism, FXR exerts immunomodulatory effects. FXR activation reduces pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion and alleviates colitis induced by trinitrobenzenesulfonic acid (TNBS) or DSS, demonstrating a protective role in animal models of IBD [23]. In macrophages, FXR activation suppresses nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB)-dependent pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α [24]. Campbell et al. demonstrated that 3β-hydroxydeoxycholic acid (isoDCA), a derivative of deoxycholic acid, enhances Foxp3 expression in the colon by acting on FXR in dendritic cells and reducing their immunostimulatory properties. This activity promotes the generation of colonic peripheral Treg cells and contributes to adaptive immune regulation [8]. In addition to receptor-mediated effects, bile acids regulate immunity as signaling molecules. For example, 3-oxolithocholic acid (3-oxoLCA) inhibits Th17 cell differentiation by restricting the transcriptional activity of retinoic acid receptor-related orphan receptor γt (RORγt); isoallolithocholic acid promotes mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, induces forkhead box protein P3 (Foxp3) expression, and thus enhances Treg cell differentiation [7]. These findings suggest that intestinal bile acids, chemically modified by commensal bacteria, can regulate host immune responses and represent promising therapeutic targets for IBD.

Preliminary evidence suggests that bile acids influence the differentiation of Tfh and Tph cells through multiple mechanisms. In neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder patients, levels of glycoursodeoxycholic acid (GUDCA) were positively correlated with circulating Tfh cells and CXCL13. Moreover, mice administered exogenous GUDCA exhibited increased proportions of circulating Tfh cells, indicating that GUDCA may promote Tfh cell differentiation [25]. In primary biliary cholangitis patients, the frequency of circulating Tfh cells was elevated, and their function—including IL-21 production, B cell maturation, and autoantibody generation—was enhanced relative to healthy controls. Notably, Tfh cell frequency was significantly lower in responders to ursodeoxycholic acid than in non-responders [26]. Ho et al. reported that the FXR agonist OCA reduced PD-1 expression on CD4+ T cells in the spleens of mice with experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis [27]. In the present study, OCA treatment decreased the proportion of Tph cells in the spleen and MLNs but increased their proportion in the intestinal LP within WT mice. In contrast, Fxr−/− mice exhibited significantly lower proportions of Tph cells in both the spleen and intestinal LP compared with WT controls. Based on the altered expression of chemokines in colon tissues, it is tempting to speculate that FXR activation may be linked to increased Tph cell differentiation and potential intestinal recruitment of Tph cells, while FXR deficiency might affect these processes.

Tph cells have been defined in recent years, and their differentiation pathway remains unclear. However, given the functional and phenotypic similarities between Tph and Tfh cells, current evidence suggests that they may differentiate through similar mechanisms [28]. Unlike Tfh cells, which function within follicular structures, Tph cells assist B cells with antibody production at sites of local inflammation. This may contribute to pathogenic IgG generation in the intestinal tissues of IBD patients. Bile acids, as major intestinal metabolites, play essential roles in metabolic and immunological processes. Accordingly, our study aimed to investigate the role of the bile acid receptor FXR in Tph cell differentiation. Because T cell activation and differentiation require antigen-presenting cells, and previous studies have shown that FXR is predominantly expressed in innate immune cells—such as dendritic cells and macrophages—and minimally in T cells, we focused on the interaction between antigen-presenting cells and Tph cells. FACS analysis of LPMCs from colitis mice revealed that the proportion of CD11c+MHC-II+ dendritic cells was significantly lower in Fxr−/− mice than in WT mice; the proportions of macrophages, monocytes, and neutrophils did not significantly differ. Based on these findings, we explored the regulatory role of FXR in dendritic cells. The results demonstrated that FXR activation enhanced the ability of dendritic cells to induce Tph cell differentiation from naïve T cells, whereas FXR deficiency impaired this function. Co-culture experiments excluded any intrinsic effect of FXR deficiency in T cells. Therefore, our results raise the possibility that altered FXR signaling in dendritic cells, rather than in T cells, may contribute to the changed Tph-inducing ability of DCs. However, since the in vivo data were obtained from systemic Fxr−/− mice instead of dendritic-cell-specific knockout mice, we cannot rule out the potential influences of other FXR-expressing immune cells such as macrophages and monocytes on the observed phenotypes.

Dendritic cells, as highly functional antigen-presenting cells, influence T cell differentiation through several mechanisms [29]: 1) antigen presentation provides the first signal required for T cell activation; 2) co-stimulatory molecules on the dendritic cell surface deliver the second activation signal; and 3) the dendritic cell–shaped cytokine microenvironment affects T cell differentiation. Studies have shown that antigen presentation, co-stimulatory molecules such as ICOSL and OX40L, and cytokines secreted by dendritic cells—including type I interferons, IL-12, and transforming growth factor-β—can drive Tfh and Tph cell differentiation [30–32]. To explore the specific mechanism by which FXR influences this process, we examined the above factors. The expression levels of ICOSL and OX40L were increased in BMDCs derived from Fxr−/− mice, which did not align with the reduced Tph differentiation observed in co-culture experiments. Next, we analyzed BMDC maturation markers, including MHC-II and CD11c. OCA treatment increased the expression levels of these markers, whereas BMDCs from Fxr−/− mice displayed reduced maturation after LPS stimulation. Furthermore, cytokine analysis revealed decreased IL-12 production in BMDCs from Fxr−/− mice. When IL-12 was exogenously added to the BMDC-naïve T cell co-culture system, the differentiation ability of Tph cell induced by BMDCs was enhanced. These findings suggest that FXR in BMDC might modulate Tph differentiation partially through regulating DC maturation and IL-12 secretion. However, the differentiation of T cell is a complex process. Apart from the FXR-dependent regulation of antigen presentation capacity and IL-12 secretion in BMDCs identified in this work, additional regulatory mechanisms may also participate in this process, such as direct signaling via surface co-stimulatory molecules and the functions of other essential cytokines, which warrant further research to explore.

RNA sequencing and KEGG enrichment analysis suggested activation of the PPAR-γ signaling pathway in BMDCs derived from Fxr−/− mice. Previous studies have shown that PPAR-γ plays a regulatory role in inflammation. In mouse macrophages, ligand-dependent activation of PPAR-γ leads to SUMOylation and interaction with histone deacetylase 3 complexes at inflammatory gene promoters, thus repressing NF-κB target gene transcription [33]. In BMDCs, PPAR-γ activation induces an immunosuppressive phenotype characterized by reduced expression of MHC-II, increased IL-10 secretion, and decreased capacity to stimulate CD4+ T cell proliferation [34]. In the present study, BMDCs from Fxr−/− mice exhibited significantly enhanced PPAR-γ expression at mRNA and protein levels after LPS stimulation. The upregulation of PPAR-γ may partially explain the decreased immunostimulatory activity of FXR-deficient BMDCs. Accordingly, it is possible that FXR deficiency in BMDCs results in altered PPAR-γ signaling, which may be related to impaired DC maturation and reduced cytokine secretion—particularly that of IL-12—and consequently may compromise the capacity to induce Tph cell differentiation.

Tph cells represent a newly identified T cell subset, and their roles in IBD pathogenesis have not been fully elucidated. In this study, we found that activation of the FXR receptor by its agonist, OCA, promotes Tph cell differentiation. Both previous research and our current findings have demonstrated that OCA exerts protective effects in DSS-induced colitis models. Tph cells are considered pro-inflammatory cells and should theoretically aggravate inflammation, which appears to be a contradictory result. We address it as follows: FXR is widely expressed in hepatocytes, intestinal epithelial cells, as well as immune cells including macrophages, monocytes and dendritic cells. FXR activation exerts protective effects via multiple regulatory mechanisms. Firstly, FXR activation protects intestinal epithelial cells and helps maintain intestinal mucosal barrier integrity [23, 35]. Secondly, FXR exerts immunomodulatory effects on macrophages and monocytes [24, 36], in these cells, activation of FXR downregulates the expression of multiple inflammatory genes, including i-Nos, tnf-α and il‑1β, through SHP-dependent and -independent pathways. In our study, OCA treatment alleviated DSS-induced colitis in mice through the above multiple mechanisms. But on the other hand, OCA treatment indeed increased the proportion of Tph cells in the LPMC of mice. Thus, OCA exerts multifaceted effects by activating FXR in distinct cell types: some are anti-inflammatory (e.g., in intestinal epithelial cells, macrophages, and monocytes), while others exert proinflammatory-like effects (e.g., enhancing antigen presentation by dendritic cells, and promoting Tph cell differentiation and intestinal chemotaxis). This accounts for our observation that OCA treatment increased the proportion of Tph cells in the LPMC while alleviating colitis. In future work, we will establish DC-specific FXR knockout mice to further clarify the function of dendritic cells in Tph differentiation and minimize confounding influences derived from systemic FXR functions.

Tph cells have been associated with the formation of ectopic lymphoid structures, which are important for the development of peripheral immune responses. However, during chronic inflammation, these ectopic structures may persist and promote ongoing local immune activation through sustained T and B cell interactions [37]. In autoimmune disease settings, antigen-presenting cells recognize self-antigens, activate T cells, and drive a subset of these T cells to differentiate into Tph cells, which support B cell-mediated autoantibody production. As key mediators that bridge adaptive and humoral immunity, Tph cells facilitate T–B cell interactions beyond germinal centers and locally amplify inflammatory responses in the gut. Therefore, Tph cells may serve as therapeutic targets in future clinical interventions. In IBD patients, bile acids activate FXR, which promotes Tph cell differentiation and chemotactic migration to the intestinal mucosa. These intestinal Tph cells may participate in local immune injury by regulating B cell responses in IBD, which needs further validation. Our findings may offer new insight into IBD pathogenesis, particularly the mechanisms initiating B cell activation. They raise the possibility that FXR–Tph axis targeting may help suppress abnormal intestinal B cell responses and reduce autoantibody production, thereby alleviating colitis. However, the current study was performed using the DSS-induced acute colitis mice model, which is characterized by acute epithelial injury and innate inflammatory responses. However, the development of IBD is a chronic inflammatory process involving both innate and adaptive immunity. This process may differ from the DSS model. This represents one of the limitations of our study. In future experiments, we will verify our conclusions in different IBD models (such as the TNBS-induced colitis mouse model) and further explore the role of Tph cells in the adaptive immune response. Meanwhile, we will also investigate to what extent the function and differentiation mechanisms of Tph cells in murine models can recapitulate those of human Tph cells.

Previous investigations have shown that OCA treatment alleviates colitis in mouse models. However, its clinical application remains limited due to potential hepatotoxicity and other adverse effects. Given that FXR is widely expressed in multiple tissues and serves distinct physiological functions in various contexts, its modulation can have broad systemic consequences. As a nuclear receptor, FXR regulates the expression of diverse genes involved in bile acid, glucose, and lipid metabolism. Clinical studies have demonstrated that widespread FXR activation disrupts cholesterol homeostasis [38]. Therefore, the use of full FXR agonists presents certain limitations; further exploration is required to comprehensively delineate the functions of FXR and develop targeted therapeutic strategies at specific cellular and molecular levels.

Dendritic cells are the most potent antigen-presenting cells, and they play critical roles in the activation and differentiation of helper T cells. In autoimmune diseases, immune homeostasis disruption by endogenous or exogenous antigens leads to chronic or relapsing inflammation. In such cases, the induction of a tolerogenic dendritic cell phenotype may help “re-educate” the immune system and restore immune balance by suppressing pathogenic autoimmune responses. Previous research has shown that FXR expression in innate immune cells, including dendritic cells, regulates their antigen-presenting capacity, surface co-stimulatory molecule expression, and cytokine secretion, thus influencing T cell differentiation [39, 40]. Our findings indicate that FXR deficiency in BMDCs reduces the expression levels of CD11c and MHC-II, while promoting a tolerogenic phenotype. When FXR-deficient BMDCs were co-cultured with naïve T cells, their ability to induce Tph cell differentiation was impaired. These results raise the hypothesis that selective targeting of FXR in dendritic cells with specific bile acid derivatives may induce an immune-tolerant dendritic cell phenotype, thereby reduce pro-inflammatory T cell differentiation while avoid systemic effects associated with full FXR agents.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study explores the potential regulatory relationship between bile acid receptor FXR and Tph cell differentiation. Our results suggest that FXR activation in dendritic cells is associated with increased Tph cell differentiation and their accumulation in the intestinal tract. These findings advance the understanding of the interplay between bile acid metabolism and immune regulation in IBD; provide novel insights for developing potential strategies to regulate abnormal B cell responses in IBD. Nevertheless, this study had some limitations. First, the potential involvement of FXR within other antigen-presenting cells (e.g., macrophages and monocytes) in regulating Tph cell differentiation cannot be excluded. Second, specific mechanisms by which dendritic cells influence Tph differentiation—particularly the role of the cytokine microenvironment—require clarification. Third, the mechanisms described were primarily investigated in murine models. Future studies will explore whether these findings are applicable in humans.
