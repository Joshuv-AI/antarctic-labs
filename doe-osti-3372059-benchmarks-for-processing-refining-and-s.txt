Executive Summary
Modern energy technologies rely on critical raw materials with risky supply chains. Due to a number of factors that
contribute to potential supply risks and rapid demand growth, the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) designates certain
materials used in energy technologies as “critical” (DOE, 2023). For example, electric vehicles rely on both lithium-ion
batteries and traction motors that require high performance permanent magnets. Li-ion batteries use varying amounts
of lithium (Li), nickel (Ni), and cobalt (Co) while high performance magnets rely on rare earth elements (REEs) like
neodymium (Nd), praseodymium (Pr), dysprosium (Dy), and terbium (Tb).
Securing reliable supply of these critical materials is a key objective for DOE and major efforts are underway to bolster
U.S. domestic supply. DOE’s Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies Office (AMMTO) invests in
research, development, and deployment (RD&D) activities to accelerate the development of innovative materials and
manufacturing solutions that support a globally dominant U.S. industrial base for a resilient energy system and secure
supply chains.
This study, “Benchmarks for Processing, Refining, and Separating Critical Materials” (BenchPRES-CM), develops a set
of key quantitative performance benchmarks that enable AMMTO, and DOE more broadly, to evaluate the impacts of
RD&D investments relative to current state-of-the-art (SOTA) technologies. These quantitative metrics establish
baselines to track progress, identify performance gaps relative to target goals, enable objective comparisons across
different technology options, and ultimately validate solutions that meet key thresholds for commercial scale-up and
viability. Specifically, this project develops measurable benchmark metrics for three key areas: (1) material efficiency, (2)
economics, and (3) life-cycle environmental impacts. Material efficiency benchmarks include recovery rates and the
quantity of a primary resource mined, processed, and transported to deliver a unit of usable product; economics
benchmarks include production operating costs and a minimum sustainable price that highlights a project’s relative
competitiveness; environmental impact benchmarks include life-cycle energy, water, greenhouse gas footprint, and
pollutants that capture overall resource use and intensity. Figure ES-1 summarizes the benchmarks evaluated by the
BenchPRES-CM project.

The benchmarks developed for the SOTA technologies in Table ES-2 may be compared to nascent producers and
emerging technologies to evaluate tradeoffs in each category. For example, a case study performed by the BenchPRES-
CM team comparing benchmark values between the SOTA and a nascent producer for Li spodumene processes finds
that the SOTA producer outperforms the nascent producer in each benchmark category, indicating that process
improvements for the nascent producer may be necessary for it to become competitive. This case study demonstrates
a proof-of-concept for how the SOTA results and benchmark framework may be applied to future RD&D investment
decisions across heterogeneous pathways.
Certain data gaps and limitations affect benchmark estimation in some cases. Publicly available data on REE metal
production outside of China remains limited, which results in missing life-cycle water and energy usage and the use of
a derived refining cost as a proxy estimate. For some pathways, literature provides total estimates but inconsistent
breakdowns between the extraction and refining steps, which can limit granularity in the material flow analysis and
benchmark estimates. Additionally, recycling data is early stage and sensitive to allocation choices. True benchmark
values are not currently available for REE recycling SOTA technologies, reflecting actively scaling production and a
lack of publicly available data beyond pilot-scale testing, but known data from literature is provided.
The benchmarks may be used collectively as a decision-support tool for AMMTO and other stakeholders for RD&D
investment decisions. They may aid in program design, portfolio selection, milestone tracking, cross-project
comparability, and identifying high-priority research needs. Technology developers may also use these benchmarks to
align their project objectives with measurable performance targets.
Opportunities for future effort include broadening material coverage and pathway diversity and solidifying partnerships
with SOTA producers to more readily validate and update benchmark estimates.

1 Introduction
Raw materials are the building blocks of energy technologies. Modern energy technologies rely on materials
that enable specialized functions in essential components. Due to factors such as high future demand,
substitution limitations, co-production issues, geopolitical sensitivities, high market concentration, and other
supply risks, many of these materials have been designated as “critical” by the U.S. Department of Energy
(DOE) (2023). For example, lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are essential components of electric vehicles that
require the critical materials lithium (Li), nickel (Ni), and cobalt (Co) to function. Hence, ensuring a reliable,
diversified supply of such materials is paramount to achieving the department’s technology objectives.
DOE’s Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies Office (AMMTO) invests in research,
development, and deployment (RD&D) activities to accelerate the development of innovative materials and
manufacturing solutions that support a globally dominant U.S. manufacturing and industrial base for a resilient
energy system and secure supply chains. Within the critical materials domain, AMMTO directs funding to projects
that bolster secure, sustainable, and diversified supply chains through resource diversification, the development
of substitutes, improved production efficiency, and reuse and recycling (DOE, 2021). As projects develop, it is
necessary to understand the impact of these investments on the overall critical material landscape, including
enhancements in extraction and separation technologies, efficiency improvements in materials and
manufacturing processes, and the development of components and technologies that enable the use of
substitutes.
Technology performance benchmarks provide a critical framework for assessing and steering RD&D efforts.
These quantitative metrics establish baselines to track progress, identify performance gaps relative to target
goals, enable objective comparisons across different technology options, and ultimately validate solutions that
meet key thresholds for commercial scale-up and viability. Well-defined benchmarks guide RD&D
prioritization by pinpointing the key parameters that require improvement to advance a technology beyond the
current state-of-the-art (SOTA) capabilities. Benchmarking technical metrics, such as process efficiency,
selectivity, productivity, and manufacturing costs, is vital for determining the economic viability of both mature
and emerging technologies.
The purpose of this project, entitled “Benchmarks for Processing, Refining, and Separating Critical Materials”
(BenchPRES-CM), is to develop a set of key performance benchmark metrics that enable DOE and other key
stakeholders and decision-makers to evaluate the impacts of RD&D investments relative to SOTA processes
and technologies. Benchmarks are discussed for critical materials used in two pivotal energy technology
components—LIBs and rare earth permanent magnets—that span multiple significant sectors, such as energy,
transportation, electronics, and medical devices. Specifically, this project: (a) identifies and develops a set of
measurable benchmark metrics; (b) identifies and defines the industry SOTA production technology for each
material; (c) establishes the benchmark values for the identified SOTA processes; and (d) conducts analysis as
proof-of-concept for a selected nascent process. The results from this analysis provide objective data to inform
technology competitiveness and provide decision support to AMMTO for prioritizing RD&D program
directions for critical materials that underpin energy technologies.
Key performance benchmark metrics measure the cost, environmental impacts, and material efficiency of
processes for the: (1) extraction and refining of Li, Ni, and Co; and (2) separation, refining, and recycling of
rare-earth elements (REEs), specifically neodymium (Nd). Li refers to battery-grade lithium-chemicals (Li-
chemicals)—lithium carbonate (Li2CO3) and lithium hydroxide monohydrate (LiOH•H2O)—that are used in
producing various LIB cathodes, such as lithium cobalt oxide (LCO), lithium iron phosphate (LFP), or nickel-
manganese-cobalt (NMC). Ni and Co refer, respectively, to battery-grade nickel sulfate (NiSO4•6H2O) and
cobalt sulfate (CoSO4•7H2O), both of which are important inputs for producing NMC cathodes for LIBs.
Given the growing use of electrified powertrains in the transportation sector (spanning hybrid, plug-in hybrid,

 1

and battery electric vehicles) and of electronics products, a robust supply chain for these materials is vital to
meeting the rising LIB demand from these sectors. Neodymium-praseodymium oxide, commonly referred to
as “didymium” or NdPr, is used in producing rare-earth-based neodymium-iron-boron (NdFeB) magnets for
various sectors, each of which is showing a major growth in REE use due to varying factors. For instance, the
growing use of motors in vehicles for different applications that expand beyond electric traction machines has
significantly increased the demand for REE-based magnets in these motors.

2 Benchmarking Framework and Scope
2.1 Objectives
Establishing meaningful benchmarks requires metrics that are (a) consistently measurable across diverse
production pathways; (b) comparable between industry incumbents and emerging technologies; and (c) relevant
to the decision-making needs of DOE’s RD&D programs. This section defines the three benchmark
categories—material efficiency, economics, and environment—selected to characterize the performance of
extraction and refining processes for Li, Ni, and Co, and separation, refining, and recycling for REEs.
The selected metrics reflect a deliberate balance between analytical rigor and practical data availability. Critical
material supply chains span multiple continents, involve proprietary processes, and use variable feedstocks to
produce intermediate products that differ in chemical form and purity specifications. A benchmarking
framework must accommodate this heterogeneity while enabling a direct comparison of technologies at
different stages of commercial readiness. The metrics defined herein were chosen because they can be obtained
and/or calculated from publicly available technical reports, peer-reviewed literature, and techno-economic and
life-cycle assessments, and because they address three core questions/concerns facing AMMTO and its
stakeholders:
 1. How efficiently do current processes convert primary resources into usable products?
 2. What are the production costs that determine economic viability?
 3. What environmental risks accompany material production across different pathways?

2.2 Categories and Metrics
Each benchmark category serves a distinct analytical purpose. Material efficiency metrics quantify recovery rates
and identify loss hotspots where RD&D investments could yield the greatest improvements in resource
utilization. Economic metrics establish cost baselines against which emerging technologies must compete to
achieve commercial relevance. Environmental metrics characterize the baseline intensities of input (energy and
water) and the emission impacts of material production to enable the evaluation of alternative technology
pathways. Together, these three categories provide a comprehensive framework for evaluating critical material
technologies from the resource stage to the refined product.

2.2.1 Material Efficiency
Material efficiency is a foundational benchmark category for critical material supply chains because it directly
governs how much primary resource must be mined, processed, and transported to deliver a unit of usable
product (e.g., battery-grade Li-chemicals, NdPr oxide/metal). Higher material efficiency generally reduces
multiple issues of importance, such as exposure to supply risks (by lowering the required feedstock volumes),
waste generation and disposal burdens (such as tailings, slags, process residues, and radioactive wastes in
monazite-derived pathways), and operating costs (OPEX) and environmental impacts (by decreasing the
required energy, reagents, and water needs per unit output).
In this report, material efficiency is assessed by quantifying material recovery and losses across the defined
supply-chain segments (e.g., extraction vs. refining for Li/Ni/Co; and separation vs. metallization for REEs).
Since many critical material pathways involve multiple intermediate products and split streams (e.g., mixed
concentrates, raffinates, precipitates, slags, and/or tailings), a simple “yield” value is often insufficient for
benchmarking. Instead, it is necessary to track material flows through the full system boundary to identify where
losses occur, in what form, and how much of the element of interest ultimately reports to the final product
versus to waste streams.

To implement recovery benchmarking consistently across diverse processes and data sources, this study uses
Material Flow Analysis (MFA) as the core method for tracking material efficiency. MFA is a mass-balance-
based accounting framework commonly used to quantify stocks and flows of materials through industrial
systems and to characterize how much material ends up in products, byproducts, and waste. The approach
follows standard MFA practice as described in the literature (e.g., Brunner & Rechberger, 2016), emphasizing
transparent system boundaries, explicit process nodes, and mass conservation.
Within this report, MFA is used to:
 • Track the target element through each supply-chain segment, translating the reported production and
 assay data (e.g., ore grade, concentrate grade, product purity) into contained-element flows;
 • Quantify material recovery rate and losses to tailings, slags, residues, off-specification material, and
 wastewater streams, where data permit; and
 • Provide a consistent basis for comparing industry SOTA production pathways to emerging or
 alternative processes, even when those processes differ in intermediate products and/or unit
 operations.
MFA is presented in this report using mass-flow diagrams and normalization to a functional unit (typically
1 kilogram [kg] of final product), enabling a direct comparison with future technologies and highlighting
hotspots where RD&D improvements would most influence the overall material recovery.
Several additional metrics were evaluated during metric selection but were not chosen as primary benchmarks
because they are either less consistently reported across sources or are strongly process—and boundary—
dependent. These include the following:
 • Ore-to-product factor/feed intensity (e.g., kg ore per kg product): This metric is informative, but varies
 strongly with ore grade, resource type, and site-specific conditions. In this report, it is captured
 implicitly through MFA-normalized flow diagrams rather than being used as a single benchmark
 metric.
 • Stage yield (unit-operation yield): This metric is useful for diagnosing specific steps (e.g., leaching yield,
 and solvent extraction (SX) distribution efficiency) but is often unavailable in public filings and can be
 difficult to harmonize across different process configurations.

 4

 • Byproduct utilization rate (fraction of non-target material valorized): This metric is important for
 circularity and economics but is not considered because data are typically inconsistent and attribution
 is complex when byproducts serve different markets.
 • Impurity rejection/product purity: This is critical for marketability (e.g., battery-grade specifications) but is
 treated primarily as a performance/quality parameter rather than a material-efficiency metric. Also, it
 is not consistently disclosed in sufficient detail for benchmarking.
Given these considerations, MFA-derived recovery rates were selected as the most robust and comparable
material-efficiency benchmark across the set of materials and processes evaluated in this study.

2.2.2 Economics
A critical aspect of mining and manufacturing feasibility is project economics. Economic feasibility is governed
by a producer’s ability to return value to stakeholders. Key economic metrics shed light on the financial health
of an organization and define a project’s credibility for both community stakeholders and investors. They also
provide detailed insights on major cost hotspots and assess the project’s viability considering expected price
points for the produced material(s) in the future. While several economic metrics can provide such valuable
insights, this study focuses solely on production costs for two dominant reasons: 1) OPEX remain relatively
stable over time, barring in cases of extreme volatility in input prices, and 2) costs are often the primary driver
of investment decisions for private financiers, project managers, and federal partners. Specifically, this study
develops two primary benchmarks:

 1. Operating cost per unit of output: OPEX per unit of output (in this case, $/kg) provides a general
 snapshot of a firm’s day-to-day operations, regardless of the project advancement stage. Yearly OPEX
 for a specific production stage—be it mining, refining, or separation in the case of REEs—are divided
 by the annual production capacity of the material in focus. For example, REE separation costs are
 provided in terms of $/kg of NdPr oxide, and REE refining costs are provided in terms of $/kg of
 NdPr metal. This simple benchmark is useful for a quick snapshot of a firm’s operations normalized
 over the project lifetime for comparison with legacy operators. Importantly, it assumes fixed input
 prices over the project life, so the benchmark will likely fluctuate during periods of market volatility in
 input and power prices, labor shortages, and other events. Additionally, it is not indicative of a project’s
 general financial health, as it does not include capital expenditures, the cost of capital, or debt from a
 firm’s balance sheet.

 2. Minimum sustainable price (MSP) of the material: The MSP depicts the minimum price that a firm
 must charge for their product to maintain a minimum long-term profitability margin. The MSP

In this work, the discount rate is set at 8%, consistent with the market-average discount rate used by mining

inherently biases projects with longer lives. To maintain comparability and the usefulness of benchmarking,

benchmark for comparing processes.

Cost data for both economic metrics are accumulated from project public filings or literature. For industry
SOTA production benchmarks, these include NI 43-101 technical reports, such as Feasibility Studies or
Preliminary Economic Assessments (Ontario Securities Commission, 2011), as well as company filings, such as
Annual Reports or Investor Presentations. For research processes, costs are assembled from techno-economic
assessments (TEAs). These data are supplemented by industry interviews when available. All costs are adjusted
to June 2025 U.S. dollars using the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Producer Price Index for Total
Manufacturing Industries (FRED, 2025). In addition, other currencies are converted to U.S. dollars using the
average exchange rate for the reported year (FRED, 2025) and inflated to 2025 U.S. dollars.

Other commonly used benchmarks in project economics include net present value (NPV) and rate-of-return
calculations from financial cash flow models. Incremental NPV analysis is commonly used to compare mutually
exclusive projects, with the project with the highest NPV considered the best economic choice. However, this
study aims to isolate production costs across stages, which, for integrated firms or advanced manufacturing,
makes it difficult to accurately assess cash flows over an entire service life. Data availability is a limiting factor
for building unique cash flow models for each material and production stage. In addition, these cash flow
models often integrate differing assumptions across projects and production stages, making consistent
benchmarking difficult. Therefore, the selected metrics (OPEX/kg and MSP) most efficiently normalize
benchmarks across materials, production stages, and firms.

2.2.3 Environment
Environmental performance is a vital evaluation category for critical material production technologies as it
captures the consequences of the resources used and the emissions generated to deliver the usable product.
Life-cycle assessment (LCA) provides the most defensible methodological basis for defining the environmental
benchmarks of these production technologies for several reasons. First, LCA offers a standardized accounting
framework for quantifying resource use and emissions within a defined system boundary, expressed relative to
a clearly specified functional unit. Second, LCA helps to support environmental decision-making by enabling
transparency around assumptions that are influential on impact burdens of materials, be it the choice of system
boundary, definition of functional unit, treatment of multi-output processes (and associated impact allocation),
or the inclusion of upstream impact contributions from materials and energy sources. This is important for
critical material production, as many instances of such production involve the co-production of multiple useful
products alongside a given critical material (e.g., combined production of Ni with Co). Finally, benchmarks
established using an LCA-consistent approach avoid the risk of burden-shifting, i.e., shifting impacts from one
life-cycle stage to another.

Within the LCA-based benchmarking framework, this report emphasizes those environmental metrics that (a)
encompass the full range of impact burdens associated with resource use and emissions; and (b) are both
decision-relevant and broadly applicable across diverse critical material pathways. Emissions consider the effect
of effluent treatment processes on process emission reductions. These include:

 1. Life-cycle energy use: For most material production technologies, energy is a key driver of both their
 cost and environmental burdens and provides a common basis to compare technologies that differ in
 their unit operations (e.g., roasting of Ni sulfide ores vs. leaching of Ni laterite ores to produce Ni
 sulfate). Life-cycle energy use or consumption, typically reported as cumulative energy demand per unit
 product (e.g., megajoules [MJ]/kg product), benchmarks the consumption of both direct on-site energy
 (such as fuels and electricity) and embodied energy in process inputs (materials and energy). In this
 report, the life-cycle energy use is provided in MJ/kg of critical material.
 2. Life-cycle water use: Water is often a limiting factor for industrial projects due to their significant water
 requirements, impact on nearby communities, and environmental risks. Further, benchmarking of
 water use must distinguish between water that is withdrawn but returned or recycled back from water
 that is fully consumed (either lost to evaporation, or as slag, or incorporated into products, such as
 battery-grade Li hydroxide, Ni sulfate, and Co sulfate). A life-cycle approach to estimating water use
 accounts for this distinction and also captures the upstream/embodied water use associated with
 different process inputs. In this effort, life-cycle water use is measured in cubic meters (m3)/kg of
 critical material.
 3. Life-cycle Greenhouse Gases (GHGs): Given the global objective of reducing carbon burdens,
 benchmarking of GHG emissions is an important objective for critical material production
 technologies. The use of this category also helps to understand the relative influence of process inputs
 (materials and energy) and outputs (emissions generated via chemical reactions) across different
 production steps on the life-cycle impacts of critical material production. For this report, the unit is kg
 CO2-eq/kg of critical material.
 4. Life-cycle criteria air pollutant (CAP) emissions: In addition to energy, water, and GHG impacts,
 another important set of benchmarking metrics concerns CAPs, as they represent significant, often
 locally consequential burdens associated with mineral processing and refining. This report highlights
 three such CAPs—sulfur oxides (SOX), nitrogen oxides (NOX), and particulate matter (PM10 and

 7

 PM2.5)—in terms of grams per kg of critical material. All these emissions can be generated both on-
 site during critical material production (due to fuel combustion or drying) and upstream (e.g., electricity
 generation). These pollutants are directly linked to human health outcomes and regional air quality
 compliance, which, in turn, can affect a project’s viability and community acceptance. Also, controlling
 these emissions often requires additional energy and capital (e.g., use of scrubbers, baghouses, or acid
 production plants that utilize SOX emissions), creating trade-offs between reducing these pollutants
 and associated cost (and potentially other environmental) impacts.
Data for these environmental benchmarks are obtained using a mix of LCA literature on critical material
production and LCA expertise from Argonne National Laboratory (“Argonne”) experts, housed in Argonne’s
R&D GREET (Research and Development version of GHGs, Regulated Emissions, and Energy use in
Technologies) model.
LCAs can also consider other environmental and social metrics, such as fossil resource depletion, land use, and
N2O emissions. However, these impact categories are not used here as benchmarks for critical material
production, primarily due to the lack of data on these impacts in literature and/or LCA models.
Apart from the literature review, the BenchPRES-CM team also conducted interviews for critical material
production—specifically REEs—with relevant company personnel and in-house experts at Argonne to
understand the current SOTA production technology landscape for these materials, subject to their availability.
These interviews were conducted to understand the exact nature of the processes used to produce these critical
materials and, thereby, to better inform the material efficiency, costs, and environmental implications of these
processes. This included interviews with personnel at MP Materials on REE production and with Re-Element
Technologies on REE recycling, as well as with in-house Argonne experts on REE recycling processes.

3 Industry State of the Art: Projects, Processes, and
  Benchmarks
This section characterizes the industry SOTA production pathways for each critical material and compiles
benchmark values for the three performance categories used in this report—material efficiency, economics,
and environment. For each material, SOTA pathways were identified through a literature review of leading
operating projects and the production technologies they employ, supplemented where possible by technical
reports and publicly available operational disclosures.
To align with the structure of the results that follow, SOTA pathways are organized by the major processing
stages evaluated in this report: extraction and refining for Li, Ni, and Co; and separation and metallization
(refining to metal) for rare earth elements (REEs). Benchmarks are reported on a consistent functional-unit
basis (typically per kg of product) to support comparability across pathways and are derived from the best
publicly available data sources described in Section 2.

3.1 Sources and Projects of Concern
The four critical materials of concern can be produced from a variety of sources. Li is globally produced from
four types of resources: (a) Salar brines in Chile and Argentina with high Li-content (> 2,000 parts per million
[ppm] Li); (b) spodumene ores in Australia that are refined in China; (c) sedimentary clays, primarily located in
the United States; and (d) low Li-content brines (< 600 ppm Li) that are available across various locations in
the United States, such as Salton Sea and Smackover Arkansas (Iyer & Kelly, 2023, 2024). Ni is primarily
produced from sulfide ores (located in Australia, Canada, Russia, and Brazil) and laterite ores (located in
Indonesia, Brazil, and Australia) (Iyer & Kelly, 2022). Co is predominantly mined in the Democratic Republic
of Congo (DRC) and then processed in China via the hydrometallurgical route (Dai et al., 2018). REEs are
produced in a variety of nations, including in the United States, as concentrates; however, the subsequent
separation and refining to rare-earth oxides (REOs) and REEs typically happens in China, which accounts for
~90% of separation and refining outputs (Iyer & Kelly, 2022; Smith et al., 2022).
This study focuses on resources that enable the United States to produce the critical material of concern from
reserves located domestically or in partner countries, thereby enabling major production steps within the United
States and reaping the associated economic and environmental benefits. The sources of critical material
production considered in this study are as follows:
 a. Li – Spodumene ores and sedimentary clays: While the United States does not have commercial
 production of Li from spodumene ores, there exists the potential to unlock those domestic mineral
 deposits or transport these commercially producing ores from friendly locations (e.g., Australia) for
 subsequent processing within the country – an alternative to the current route of Australian spodumene
 ores being processed in China. Sedimentary clays are already available within the United States (Iyer &
 Kelly, 2023) and can be put to use for Li production after modifying the current processing routes
 employed for spodumene-based Li production.
 b. Ni – Sulfide ores: Since the lone Ni mine operating in the United States is a sulfide ore (in Michigan),
 and the nearby region bordering Canada also houses sulfide ores, it offers the scope for increasing
 sulfidic ore-based Ni (or battery-grade Ni sulfate) production domestically. However, it must be noted
 that the Eagle Mine is planned to be closed in 2029, having been exhausted of its Ni capacity, with the
 site planned to be reclaimed (Talon Metals Corp, 2026; JTC Energy Insights, n.d.). Alternative projects
 are under consideration or development in Alaska, Michigan, Minnesota, Nevada, and Oregon (U.S.
 Geological Survey, 2026).
 c. Co – Via Hydrometallurgy: Unlike other resources, primary Co extraction does not currently occur in

 9

 the United States (after suspension of the fully-constructed Idaho Cobalt Operations project,
 previously owned by Jervois, due to unfavorable Co prices), so its processing will have to consider the
 use of hydrometallurgy on DRC Co ores. However, other nations are seeking to establish robust Co
 supply chains via imports of Co concentrate/Co compounds for subsequent conversion to battery-
 grade Co sulfate. Since a similar possibility exists for the United States as well, this source is preferred
 for setting the industry SOTA benchmark for Co.
 d. REEs – Since bastnaesite and monazite ores dominate the landscape of global REE production,
 especially for light-REEs that are much needed for producing REE-based NdFeB magnets, these two
 sources are considered for identifying the industrial SOTA technology for REE production.
SOTA projects have been identified as benchmarks for six mineral types to represent the dominant production
pathways and geographic configurations for current critical material supply chains. Each benchmark project
reflects either market-leading production scale, representative Western operating conditions, or the primary
commercial pathway for a major producing mineral resource. Market-leading production and/or primary
commercial pathway represent the dominant production routes for the critical material of concern. Western
operating conditions are considered as these are somewhat analogous to industrial production in the United
States from an institutional and regulatory perspective and provide a fairly representative picture of similar U.S.-
based critical material production for the expected cost and environmental performance. Details on the chosen
projects and the associated technological process are provided in the following subsections.

3.2 Rare Earth Elements
REEs are produced primarily through the mining, separation, and refining of two types of mineral deposits,
bastnaesite and monazite. While both minerals occur primarily in hard-rock deposits, they have different
chemical compositions: bastnaesite is a rare-earth fluorocarbonate mineral ((REE)CO3F), while monazite is a
rare-earth phosphate ((REE)PO4) mineral. This affects the nature of processing steps used for REE production,
specifically regarding the form of REE leachate generated. Additionally, both bastnaesite and monazite ores
often contain radioactive elements such as thorium and uranium, which in turn poses challenges for radioactive
waste management and disposal (Shaw 1953). China’s Bayan Obo, the largest REE deposit in the world (Fan
et al., 2016), is a mixture of bastnaesite and monazite ores, while Australia’s Mount Weld deposit—the largest
known REE deposit outside of China—is monazite ore.

3.2.1 Process Overview
Regardless of the ore involved, the process route is similar for REE production from both bastnaesite and
monazite ores, as discussed below.

3.2.1.1 REE Extraction
Typically, the ore is mined from open-pit mines and subjected to beneficiation and flotation treatments (Iyer
& Kelly, 2022). The beneficiation step comprises numerous comminution steps, involving crushing and
grinding processes, followed by a multi-step flotation process. The final product of REE extraction is an REE
concentrate.

3.2.1.2 REE Separation
While the separation process is similar for monazite and bastnaesite, the input intensities (i.e., quantities of
material, energy, and water used) for REE separation from concentrates differs for the two ores. This affects
the associated material recovery rates and output streams (i.e., wastewater and CO2e emissions). Nd is primarily
separated from REE concentrate via SX. The SX process is tailored for each concentrate, but a common step

 10

across both ores is high-temperature cracking with either sulfuric acid (H2SO4) or hydrochloric acid (HCl). The
SX organic phase is typically a mixture of kerosene and one of di-2-ethylhexyl phosphoric acid or 2-ethylhexyl
phosphonic acid mono-ethylhexyl ester, with oxalic acid used to separate the rare-earth chloride salts from
solution (Smith et al., 2022). These processes consume large amounts of acid, caustic, and water. For monazite,
during ore processing, small amounts of uranium and thorium must be separated from the rare-earth streams,
which increases the processing cost and complexity. While these processes are based on information provided
in the public domain by Australian and American companies, China still controlled ~90% of global REE
separation capacity in 2021 (Smith et al., 2022). Separation capabilities in the United States are growing, aiming
to diversify supply outside of China.

3.2.1.3 REE Refining
Nd oxide (Nd2O3) is refined into metal via electrowinning and sodium reduction (Smith et al., 2022). In
electrowinning, Nd2O3 is converted to metal (or into alloys) in an electrochemical cell containing a molten
fluoride electrolyte (typically 80–90% rare-earth fluoride, with the remaining volume being Li fluoride).
Depending on the metal or alloy to be produced, the specific electrolyte composition will vary. Electrolysis,
widely practiced in China, uses Nd fluoride (Marx et al., 2018). Alternatively, sodium reduction uses sodium
metal to reduce rare-earth chloride salts in a metallothermic reaction similar to the Hunter process for titanium.
China accounts for ~90% of global REE refining (Smith et al., 2022), and the current U.S.-based NdPr
metallization capacity is limited (Baskaran and Schwartz, 2025). Nd oxide production is independent of the
source deposit, meaning that the metal refining process for output from either deposit type (bastnaesite or
monazite) is identical.

3.2.1.4 REE Recycling
REE recycling processes depend heavily on the input material composition and the waste being recycled.
Conventionally, hydrometallurgy has been the predominant route employed for REE recycling from magnets
(Zhang et al., 2020). However, given the chemical-intensive nature of hydrometallurgical recycling, two
alternative REE recycling routes have gained ground: acid-free dissolution and magnet-to-magnet recycling.
This analysis focuses on the acid-free dissolution process for extracting REOs from electronic waste (e-waste)
as feedstock, as discussed by Sanchez Moran et al. (2024). This process uses an acid-free copper (Cu) sulfate
leach to selectively dissolve REEs from e-waste, while leaving most Cu, aluminum (Al), and iron (Fe) in a
filterable solid residue that retains value. The REE is treated with an oxalate reagent to precipitate rare-earth
oxalates, which are then purified through repeated cycles of re-slurrying with ammonium hydroxide (NH4OH)
at slightly elevated temperatures (< 50–60 °Celsius [C]) to remove remaining Fe impurities. Liquid streams are
routed to wastewater treatment, where sodium hydroxide (NaOH) is used to adjust the resulting solution’s pH
and Cu is recovered for reuse. The REE oxalate precipitate is dried and calcined at 1,000 °C to decompose the
oxalates, leaving behind NdPr oxide and releasing CO2 and water. NdPr oxide can then be sent to facilities that
refine the oxide into metals or alloys.

3.2.2 Rare Earths Extracted from Bastnaesite
For bastnaesite ores, the BenchPRES-CM team considers the Mountain Pass mine owned by MP Materials in
California as the source for industrial SOTA production as it is the world’s second-largest REE mine (MP
Materials, 2025). This ore body has large amounts of cerium (Ce), lanthanum (La), Nd, praseodymium (Pr), and
small amounts of samarium (Sm), but does not contain significant amounts of heavy REEs (Smith et al., 2022).
While there are many light REEs produced from monazite and bastnaesite deposits, NdPr is the key focus of
this study due to its use in NdFeB magnets, which are used for numerous defense and energy applications. The
processes employed by MP Materials for output from Mountain Pass are used to describe separation and
metallization processes from bastnaesite ores.

3.2.4 Rare Earths from Recycled Sources
REO separation and metallization are two of the most critical steps in the REE supply chain, both of which

 16

are largely concentrated in China (Smith et al., 2022). REE procurement from primary production pathways is
thus vulnerable to geopolitical risks and other potential supply disruptions. Recycling has emerged as an
alternative supply method that can alleviate these risks and disruptions. Potential feedstocks for these efforts
include e-waste (such as hard disk drives or HDDs), magnet swarf, and end-of-life (EOL) magnets. Each of
these pathways have undergone significant RD&D and are actively being scaled for commercial production by
different firms.

3.2.4.1 SOTA Technology and Other Details
While hydrometallurgical processing remains the conventional technology used for REE recycling, interviews
with recycling experts at Argonne highlighted two industrial SOTA processes for REE recycling: acid-free
dissolution and magnet-to-magnet recycling. These processes are currently being commercialized and scaled at
Critical Materials Recycling LLC and HyProMag, respectively, both located within the United States.
The e-waste recycling SOTA technology, acid-free dissolution, offers improvements over traditional
hydrometallurgical methods. The process was licensed to Critical Materials Recycling LLC for
commercialization with a goal of producing 100 tonnes of high purity REOs annually. Prior literature on the
process includes techno-economic and LCAs (Sanchez Moran et al., 2024), which detail the costs, life-cycle
impacts, and recovery rates at a significantly smaller scale (2.5 tonnes annually). These benchmarks are highly
likely to change as technology scales up and commercial-level REE recycling becomes more widespread.
Interviews were also conducted with ReElement Technologies, a U.S.-based firm attempting to rapidly scale a
facility that produces high purity REOs from both recycled feedstocks (such as e-waste and swarf) and primary
ore bodies (such as monazites and bastnaesites). ReElement uses ligand-assisted chromatography, which
enables the use of diverse feedstocks in different batch production runs. The firm can currently produce ~250
tonnes of REOs annually, with plans to scale up to 10,000 tonnes per annum. Little is publicly known about
the key operating benchmarks for this process.
Additionally, firms such as Noveon Magnetics and HyProMag are scaling up the magnet-to-magnet recycling
pathway, which involves significantly less chemical processing and separation (Jin et al., 2018). EOL magnets
are refurbished with additional REOs in this pathway to revitalize their efficacy, per interviews with Argonne
experts.

3.3 Lithium
Within the energy sector, two forms of Li-chemicals are considered critical for use in Li-ion batteries: battery-
grade lithium carbonate (Li2CO3) and lithium hydroxide (or lithium hydroxide monohydrate, i.e., LiOH •H2O)
(Iyer & Kelly, 2023; Kelly et al., 2021; Iyer & Kelly, 2024; Schenker & Pfister, 2025; Schenker et al., 2024). This
report confines its focus to Li production from spodumene ores and sedimentary clay resources. In both cases,
mining and concentrate production constitute the Lithium Extraction step, while subsequent battery-grade Li-
chemical production comprises the Lithium Refining stage. The process description for spodumene-based Li-
chemical production is based on Iyer & Kelly (2023), Kelly et al. (2021), Iyer & Kelly (2024), Schenker & Pfister
(2025), Schenker et al. (2024), while that for Li-chemical production from sedimentary clays is based on Iyer &
Kelly (2023 & 2024).

 18

3.3.1 Process Overview

3.3.1.1 Lithium Extraction
Spodumene ore (containing spodumene, or LiAlSi2O6) is abundant in Western Australia. The ore is extracted
and reduced in size via a series of crushing and milling steps. Subsequently, the ore is treated via multiple
flotation steps to separate the valuable spodumene mineral, with the residual content separated as tailings. The
extraction step employs diesel for ore haulage via trucks and water (mainly discharged). The crushing, milling,
and flotation steps employ a combination of electricity and small amounts of flotation reagents and water. The
final product is spodumene concentrate (minimum ~63% pure spodumene, with the remaining content being
a mix of quartz and feldspar). The production schematic for Li extraction from sedimentary clays is similar to
that for spodumene, involving mining, screening (comminution and crushing), and subsequent beneficiation to
produce clay concentrate. For clays, this document considers clay-based production processes as described in
the preliminary economic assessment (PEA) reports of envisioned projects in the United States, which currently
dominates the global landscape of clay-based Li-chemical production.

3.3.1.2 Lithium Refining
Spodumene concentrate contains about 5–8% lithium oxide (Li2O), which is subsequently converted to battery-
grade lithium-chemicals (Li-chemicals). The spodumene in concentrate is α-spodumene—a monoclinic, dense
form of this mineral that is chemically inert. To make it reactive, spodumene concentrate is fed into a roasting
kiln and heated to ~1,000–1,100 °C to convert α-spodumene to β-spodumene—a tetragonal form of
spodumene that is chemically reactive. Next, β-spodumene is cooled, mixed with concentrated H2SO4 at 250 °C
to produce lithium sulfate (Li2SO4). The alumina and silica (SiO2) in spodumene mineral convert to
aluminosilicates. The next step—leaching and purification—involves mixing the acid-roasting output in water
in the presence of lime (CaO) and/or limestone (CaCO3). Li2SO4 dissolves in water, while aluminosilicates are
separated out as tailings. The addition of CaO/ CaCO3 helps in adjusting the pH of the environment and
removing other metal impurities (such as Fe and Al). Subsequently, Li2SO4 is reacted with soda ash (Na2CO3)
or sodium hydroxide (NaOH) to respectively produce battery-grade Li2CO3 or LiOH crystals. A major
byproduct of this step is sodium sulfate, which is typically sold to glass or detergent industries. The final step
involves drying and micronizing of the crystals produced to enable their mixing into cathode slurries for
producing LIB cathodes.
Unlike spodumene, the strength of chemical bonding of Li with other constituent elements is weaker for clay
ores, because of which roasting is not usually needed for Li-chemical production. The rest of the production
schematic is similar, with some important differences. Clay concentrate is leached with dilute H2SO4 in a
counter-current flow at 80-100 °C—lower than that for spodumene ores. The leachate is treated with lime
and/or CaCO3 (to adjust pH) and then reacted with NaOH or alkali hydroxides (Ca(OH)2) to produce Li2CO3
and/or LiOH•H2O. Like for spodumene, the residual solution is crystallized, dried, and micronized to obtain
battery-grade Li-chemicals.

3.4 Nickel
Like Li, Ni is primarily considered a critical material in the energy context for its use in Li-ion batteries. Within
this context, Ni refers to battery-grade nickel sulfate (or Ni sulfate hexahydrate, i.e., NiSO4•6H2O) (Iyer &
Kelly, 2022; Roy, 2025; Mudöd, 2010). Battery-grade Ni sulfate production from sulfide ores comprises five
steps: mining, beneficiation, primary metal extraction via pyrometallurgy, refining (to produce Class I Ni metal),
and final battery-grade Ni sulfate production. Mining and beneficiation together constitute the Nickel Extraction

 24

step, while the remaining three steps constitute the Nickel Refining step. The process description for these steps
is based on Iyer & Kelly (2022), Valencia (n.d.), & Wei et al. (2020).

3.4.1 Process Overview

3.4.1.1 Nickel Extraction
Sulfide ores consist of three minerals: pentlandite ((Ni,Fe)9S8), Ni-containing pyrrhotite (Fe7S8), and
chalcopyrite (CuFeS2)—of which pentlandite is prominently used for Ni production. The ore is mined, typically
from open-pit mines, and then concentrated using a combination of physical treatment (e.g., comminution)
and froth flotation or magnetic separation to produce Ni-rich concentrates (5-15 wt.% Ni). Flotation aids the
removal of chalcopyrite and pyrrhotite from the ore, while magnetic separation is suitable for separating
pyrrhotite when its presence in the ore is significant. Mining involves the use of explosives for ore blasting and
diesel for hauling, as well as electricity for operation of mining equipment. Beneficiation uses a combination of
steel balls (for grinding), and chemicals such as CaO, H2SO4, and Na2CO3 for flotation, along with electricity
for its energy needs.

3.4.1.2 Nickel Refining
Ni-rich concentrate (from the Nickel Extraction step) is processed via pyrometallurgical route to produce Ni-
rich matte (60–80 wt.% Ni). Pyrometallurgy involves three sequential steps conducted in a single unit:
 a) Roasting: Ni-rich concentrate is heated at high temperatures (600-700 °C) in oxygen-enriched air to
 remove its sulfur content as sulfur dioxide (SO2) gas.
 b) Smelting: The roasted product is melted with SiO2 flux to generate two immiscible phases: molten
 sulfide solution (for next step), and ferro-silicate slag that separates the Fe from Cu ore and that is
 subsequently discarded.
 c) Converting: This step involves a combination of roasting and smelting to remove sulfur (as SO2) and
 Fe (as slag) that has not been removed in the previous steps. The final product is purified Ni matte
 that also contains other valuable elements, such as Cu, tellurium (Te), selenium (Se), gold (Au), and
 platinum (Pt).
The produced matte is refined to produce Class I Ni metal (≥ 99.8 wt.% Ni) through hydrometallurgical
processes, such as leaching (using chlorine [Cl], ammonia [NH3], or [H2SO4]), SX, electrowinning, hydrogen
reduction, or carbonyl refining. The key aim of this step is to reduce any impurities (sulfur/oxygen/valuable
elements) present in the input. The final step involves converting Class I Ni metal to battery-grade NiSO4 by
dissolving this Ni in H2SO4 and subsequently crystallizing the resulting NiSO4 in water to produce NiSO4•6H2O
crystals.
Pyrometallurgical and refining processes dominate both material and energy inputs for the Nickel Refining step.
Both processes involve the use of fluxing chemicals (such as CaO, CaCO3, H2SO4, and SiO2) to remove Fe,
oxygen (to remove sulfur), and reducing agents (such as coke, NH3, and hydrogen) to obtain Class I Ni metal.
H2SO4 is used in the acid-dissolution step to produce battery-grade NiSO4•6H2O. In terms of energy inputs,
pyrometallurgical processes use a mix of diesel, natural gas, coal, and electricity, with the first three sources
used mainly for heating (to achieve high temperatures). A mix of natural gas and electricity is used during the
refining step, while the energy consumption for the Ni metal-to-NiSO4•6H2O conversion step is much less
when compared to the process energy consumed to produce Class I Ni metal from ore. Water is consumed in
the production of NiSO4•6H2O (it forms a part of the product itself).

3.5 Cobalt
Co is typically produced from either Cu-dominant or Ni-dominant ores. The critical mineral form of note for
Co is battery-grade CoSO4 (specifically cobalt sulfate heptahydrate or CoSO4•7H2O). Mining, beneficiation,
and crude Co hydroxide production are considered as Cobalt Extraction, while battery-grade CoSO4 production
comprises Cobalt Refining stage. The process description for these steps/stages is based on Dai et al. (2018).

3.5.1 Process Overview

3.5.1.1 Cobalt Extraction
Cobalt is mined with Cu (typically sulfide ores) in a process schematic that is similar to Ni production from
sulfidic ores (see Section 3.3). Briefly, the ore is mined, crushed and milled, and then treated via froth flotation
to produce a mixed Co/Cu oxide concentrate. The concentrate is further ground to produce a fine powder,
increasing the chemical reactivity of the product.
Similar to Ni, the Cu/Co oxide concentrate is roasted to remove sulfur as SO2, which in turn is used to produce
H2SO4. This H2SO4 is used to leach the matte produced during roasting and generate a Cu/Co mixed solution.
SX, involving multiple steps of selective Co extraction from this mixed solution, is used to separate the Co (as
a raffinate solution), with Cu separated and electro-refined to produce metallic Cu cathode. The raffinate solution
is treated with CaO/ CaCO3 and specific oxidants to remove impurities, such as Fe, manganese (Mn), and Al,
by raising the pH of the solution. Purified Co solution is then reacted with magnesia (MgO) in the presence of
water to produce crude cobalt hydroxide (Co(OH)2).

3.5.1.2 Cobalt Refining
Subsequently, Co(OH)2 is leached using H2SO4 and sodium bisulfite (Na2S2O5) to produce a crude CoSO4
solution. The solution is treated via SX (using reagents such as P2O4 and P2O7) to separate Co from impurities
(such as Ni), evaporation and crystallization to produce pure CoSO4 crystals, which are then dried with water
to obtain battery-grade CoSO4. Note that multiple Co-based products (such as pure Co(OH)2, battery-grade
CoSO4, cobalt oxide or Co3O4, and Co metal) are produced sequentially, involving the use of other chemical
inputs, such as ammonium bicarbonate (NH4HCO3), Na2CO3, and NaOH. Given the sequential nature of
processing, in which CoSO4 is used to produce Co3O4 and Co metal, some of these inputs are also allocated to
CoSO4 production in line with typical LCA procedures.

5 Discussion
5.1 Insights from this Report
The determination of benchmark metrics for material efficiency, cost, and environmental parameters for
multiple critical material production stages enables high-level comparisons across different, heterogeneous
pathways. The framework’s use of recovery rates (via MFA) for material efficiency, production cost derived
from NI 43-101 technical reports and TEAs, and life-cycle impacts via LCAs, offer a common basis to compare
options that can differ in several ways, be it in terms of technology, feedstocks used, or unit operations.
A common finding across cost and environmental metrics for all the critical materials evaluated in this study is
the dominance of downstream material processing stages in the environmental benchmark values. Examples
include the following:
 a. REEs: Separation and metallization are major contributors to life-cycle GHG and CAP (SOX) impacts,
 reflecting the chemical- and electricity-intensive nature of SX and electrowinning processes.
 b. Spodumene-based Li-chemical production: Major contributions stem from substantial coal use during
 Li refining for GHG, energy, and CAP emissions.
 c. Ni sulfate: Refining dominates SOX impacts due to the large SO2 emissions generated during
 pyrometallurgical processing of Ni concentrate.
At the same time, exceptions to this trend are also observed, such as the dominance of extraction (mining)
steps to PM2.5 and SOX impacts for REEs and CoSO4 production.
Another key insight from our study is that cost and environmental competitiveness can move in different
directions. For instance, the case study on impact comparison for Li-chemical production from TLC
demonstrates that higher energy intensity and lower recovery rates can coincide with only modest differences
in MSP when driven by assumptions on CAPEX amortization and mine life. This finding reinforces the need
to evaluate technologies with multiple benchmark categories rather than relying on a single headline metric.

5.2 Relevance and Implications for Stakeholders
The benchmark metrics can be used collectively as a decision-supporting tool by AMMTO for RD&D targeting
in various ways. These include:
 a. Program design and portfolio selection: AMMTO can use these benchmark values as reference
 baselines in future Notice of Funding Opportunities (NOFOs) and project selection to assess the basic
 “competitiveness” of prospective projects aimed at production of concerned critical materials (that are
 studied in this analysis). These NOFOs and other efforts can also require applicants to report their
 respective projected performance on these metrics using the same functional units and boundaries.
 b. Stage-gates and milestone tracking: The benchmark values from this report can also be translated into
 milestones for upcoming and nascent technologies and projects to be achieved in a specific time frame.
 MFA-based recovery tracking can also be used to understand the major reasons behind material losses
 for specific projects and changes needed to process design for more efficient flows.
 c. Cross-project comparability: The benchmark values can enable the development of standardized
 reporting templates for existing and future projects on the concerned metrics to evaluate their regular
 performance over time, enabling a comparability across different projects engaged in producing the
 same critical material(s).
 d. Identifying high-leverage RD&D needs: AMMTO can use the identified hotspots from this study to

 34

 design future NOFOs that help in improving the performance of future technologies on important
 metrics. For instance, in the case of REE production, AMMTO can emphasize projects that aim to
 reduce chemical intensities, enable closed-loop reagent recovery, or replace SX with alternative, lower-
 footprint separation technologies. Similarly, AMMTO can also prioritize the development of projects
 that help address higher CAP emissions through use of alternative process routes that enable a better
 capture of these emissions.
 Technology-developers can also use these benchmark values to align their RD&D objectives with
 measurable performance thresholds. For instance, RD&D efforts can focus on reagent substitution, heat
 integration, or alternative leaching chemistry when addressing higher energy/GHG impacts from refining
 processes for critical material production. Similarly, higher REE impacts during REE separation indicate
 the need for targeting future RD&D efforts towards better REE selectivity, circuit optimization, efficient
 recycling loops, and better impurity management.

5.3 Limitations
As with any study, there are specific limitations with obtaining and using the benchmark metrics proposed in
this study. These include:
 • REE metallization and integrated separation–metal cost transparency: Publicly available data on capital
 and operational expenditures, electricity use, consumables (notably graphite), yields, and scrap/off-
 spec rates for NdPr metal production outside China remain limited. This constrains confidence in
 refining cost benchmarks and requires use of proxy/derived estimates. Also, life-cycle impact values
 are not available for all the environmental metrics for REE production across both bastnaesite and
 monazite ores.
 • Stage-resolved environmental inventories are incomplete: For some pathways, literature provides totals
 but not consistent breakdowns by extraction vs. refining vs. conversion steps (particularly for clay-
 based Li and certain REE routes). This limits hotspot attribution and can hide improvement
 opportunities.
 • Inconsistent reporting of yields, purities, and intermediate products: Many sources do not publish unit
 operation yields (leach efficiency, SX distribution coefficients, precipitation yield, recycle rates) or
 impurity specifications. This reduces the granularity of MFA and can lead to uncertainty in cumulative
 recovery.
 • Recycling data are early-stage and sensitive to allocation choices: SOTA benchmark values are not
 available at present for REE recycling, often reflecting material efficiency and environmental impacts
 at pilot or small-scale production. Public benchmarks for chromatography-based REE separation and
 magnet-to-magnet at scale are especially limited.
 • Nature of electric grid: Because SOTA benchmark values focus on technology-associated costs and
 emissions, the economic and environmental benchmarks in this study do not segregate the effects of
 geographic specificity for CMM production. In particular, this impacts assumptions about the sources
 of electricity used for CMM production, as electricity-related life-cycle contributions can vary with the
 mix of generation technologies employed for different processes. Since it is not always possible to
 separate such contributions in literature-based CMM production data, this report makes no attempt to
 do so. This limits the ability to assess the benefits of technology- versus electricity-related benefits on
 life-cycle impacts of production, though it can be addressed to some extent by comparing the life-cycle
 impacts of nascent technologies with SOTA technology for the same electric grid mix.

5.4 Opportunities for Future Effort
Future effort in this specific area of research may include activities such as:
 • Expanded material coverage and product forms: Add other critical materials from DOE’s Critical
 Materials List (e.g., graphite, manganese, REE heavy fractions, gallium, germanium)
 • Increased pathway diversity and “what-if” benchmarking: Include laterite-to-mixed hydroxide
 precipitate-to-sulfate Ni routes, alternative Co routes, direct Li extraction for brines, and additional
 REE separation technologies (chromatography, membranes, ionic liquids), with clear Technology
 Readiness Level (TRL) tagging.
 • Development of a benchmark database and reporting template: Create a versioned dataset (inputs,
 assumptions, functional unit, boundaries, uncertainty ranges) that AMMTO can update annually.
 Provide a standardized applicant worksheet for MFA, cost, and LCA inputs tied to GREET.
 • Solidifying partnerships with SOTA producers to more readily validate benchmark estimates: Enables
 transparent communication and up-to-date information that may not be publicly available. This
 includes providing updates when market and/or operating conditions change.
