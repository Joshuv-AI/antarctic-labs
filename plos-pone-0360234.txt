Diverse genetic pathways drive convergent fluconazole and caspofungin resistance phenotypes in Candida albicans

Mounzer Abbas, Nour Fattouh, Christy Chedraoui, Setrida El Hachem, Tsolaire Sourenian, Ibrahim Bitar, Roy A Khalaf

ABSTRACT
The emergence of antifungal resistance in Candida albicans is a major worldwide public health threat, particularly for immunocompromised patients. Antifungal resistance may arise through uniform resistance mechanisms or through variable evolutionary routes. The aim of this study was to investigate whether antifungal resistance arises in C. albicans through shared or isolate-specific mechanisms, in other words, following convergent or divergent adaptive pathways and genetic mechanisms. Six laboratory-induced fluconazole-resistant and six caspofungin-resistant isolates were analyzed using whole-genome sequencing coupled to phenotypic tests assessing pathogenicity characteristics. All fluconazole-resistant isolates showed increased ergosterol production and elevated ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter efflux pump activity, indicating conserved resistance adaptation mechanisms. Recurrent mutations included ERG24 variants and SOD6 S225P in all isolates while other mutations such as ERG11 T128K varied between isolates. Caspofungin-resistant isolates exhibited diverse CHS3 mutations causing variable chitin increases despite a shared resistance strategy. In addition, many isolates displayed decreased biofilm formation and virulence which suggests that increased resistance subjects virulence mechanisms to fitness trade-offs. Altogether, our laboratory-induced antifungal-resistant C. albicans isolates experienced convergent phenotypic adaptations that were the result of genetically diverse evolutionary routes. YMAP (Yeast Mapping Analysis Pipeline) observations also revealed large scale chromosomal rearrangements in our resistant isolates indicating that in addition to point mutations, rearrangements contribute to the acquisition of resistance. Our study highlights the complexity of resistance development and suggests that similar resistance outcomes may be driven by different genomic mechanisms.

Introduction Fungal infections pose a significant but underestimated global public health concern. Each year, around 6.5 million cases of invasive fungal infections are reported worldwide of which approximately 2.5 million result in mortality, especially in immunocompromised individuals such as HIV and cancer patients [ 1 ]. Among human fungal pathogens, species of the genus Candida represent the most common cause of fungal disease. Over 200 species of Candida exist but only five species: Candida albicans , Candida glabrata , Candida krusei , Candida parapsilosis , and Candida tropicalis cause 90% of Candida -associated infections worldwide [ 2 ]. Although an increasing trend towards isolation of non- albicans Candida species is globally observed [ 3 ], the opportunistic diploid C. albicans accounts for around 40–60% of invasive infections worldwide [ 4 ] and maintains high prevalence in some regions such as the Middle East and North Africa [ 5 ]. In Lebanon for example, C. albicans remains a common etiological agent of candidiasis, contributing substantially to vulvovaginal and bloodstream infections reported at tertiary care centers [ 6 , 7 ]. C. albicans infections are managed according to disease severity and site of infection. Superficial mucosal infections are usually treated by azole antifungals, particularly fluconazole while life-threatening invasive candidiasis and bloodstream infections rely on echinocandins, including anidulafungin, caspofungin, and micafungin [ 8 ]. Azoles inhibit the activity of lanosterol 14α-demethylase encoded by ERG11 , thereby disrupting ergosterol biosynthesis and plasma membrane integrity. In contrast, echinocandins target the enzyme β-(1,3)-glucan synthase involved in fungal cell wall biosynthesis [ 9 ]. C. albicans are highly pathogenic as they are equipped with the ability to transition from yeast to filamentous forms including pseudohyphae and true hyphae [ 10 ]. The transition to true hyphae coupled to a strong adhesive capacity mediated by adhesins such as agglutinin-like sequence family proteins and Hwp1, promotes tissue invasion, biofilm formation, and immune interactions. Biofilms confer intrinsic tolerance to antifungals and promote the emergence of resistance. In parallel, C. albicans activates stress response pathways promoting survival in hostile host niches [ 11 ]. Although virulence remains a major clinical concern in the immunocompromised patients [ 12 ], an additional culprit is the presence of evidence indicating that C. albicans can acquire resistance to antifungals, particularly to fluconazole which is considered the first line of treatment [ 13 ]. Azole resistance is multifactorial, mainly involving target modifications, altered sterol biosynthesis, and increased drug efflux mediated by ABC and major facilitator superfamily transporters, often driven by gain-of-function mutations in key transcriptional regulators [ 14 ]. Echinocandin resistance is relatively low in prevalence but considered a dangerous occurrence since these antifungals are considered the last line of defense in life-threatening infections [ 15 , 16 ]. Echinocandin resistance is the result of point mutations in conserved hot-spot regions of FKS genes coding for β-(1,3)-glucan synthase [ 17 ]. These mutations could reduce fungal fitness but may be accompanied by compensatory cell wall remodeling mechanisms characterized by increased chitin deposition, contributing to increased resistance [ 18 ]. The aim of this study was to better understand the mechanisms underlying azole and echinocandin resistance in C. albicans by determining whether resistance acquired under a defined drug pressure arises through uniform genetic alterations across isolates or through diverse genetic routes that converge on similar resistance phenotypes. To address this question, we studied genomic and phenotypic changes in multiple colonies of a laboratory-induced antifungal-resistant C. albicans generated by sequential exposure to fluconazole or caspofungin at twice the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) from a previous study carried out in our research group by El Hachem et al. [ 19 ]. Through this approach, we were able to observe conserved resistance strategies driven by diverse underlying genetic changes. Results Whole-genome sequencing reveals extensive mutational heterogeneity across fluconazole and caspofungin-resistant isolates Whole-genome sequencing identified substantial genomic diversity among resistant isolates with considerable variation in both the number and identity of mutations across isolates. No two resistant isolates shared identical mutational profiles (Tables 1 and 3). Despite this heterogeneity, several mutations were recurrent within resistance groups and accounted for the highest proportion of shared variants ( Tables 1–4 ). In the fluconazole-resistant group, most isolates carried conserved mutations in the ERG24 and CHS3 genes involved in ergosterol biosynthesis and cell wall remodeling, respectively. In addition, a missense mutation leading to the S225P amino acid substitution in the oxidative stress response gene SOD6 was observed in all fluconazole resistant isolates. Mutations associated with phenotypic switching and biofilm-related regulation were also recurrent, including variants in MFG1 , NAG3 , UME6 , and SFL1 ( Table 2 ). In the caspofungin-resistant group, a limited set of recurrent mutations was detected. All isolates harbored the T486S substitution in the GSC2 gene which codes for a catalytic subunit of β-(1,3)-glucan synthase. Murine survival analysis reveals isolate-dependent virulence differences Virulence was assessed using a murine infection model for fluconazole and caspofungin-resistant isolates. During the first 3 days post-infection, one moribund mouse was observed in the control group infected by the reference strain as well as the groups infected by isolates FL3 and FL6. Over the one month period, survival outcomes differed substantially among isolates. Groups infected by FL1 and FL5 showed the highest survival with 5 of 6 mice alive, followed by FL3 with 4 of 6 mice alive. Groups injected by FL2, FL4, CS1, and CS2 each exhibited 3 mice surviving, while the group injected with the reference strain, FL6, and CS4 had 2 mice that survived 30 days. The groups infected by CS5 and CS6 each had one surviving mouse and CS3 exhibited complete mortality. Overall, fluconazole-resistant isolates generally exhibited reduced virulence compared to the reference strain, with 64% survival, while caspofungin-resistant isolates displayed variable virulence profiles, with an average of 28% survival ( Fig 4 ). 10.1371/journal.pone.0360234.g004 Fig 4 Heatmap of daily morbidity in a murine model of disseminated C. albicans infection. Fluconazole and caspofungin-resistant isolates exhibit increased ergosterol content A significant increase in ergosterol content was observed in the fluconazole and caspofungin-resistant groups of isolates compared to the control. In the fluconazole-resistant group of isolates, FL4, FL5, and FL6 showed the most pronounced increase since they contained 81%, 77%, and 71% higher ergosterol content in comparison to the control. More modest increases in ergosterol content were observed in FL1, FL2, and FL3. Discussion Over the past decade, hospital isolates of C. albicans have experienced increased antifungal resistance [ 4 , 20 – 23 ] but understanding how C. albicans acquires antifungal resistance remains a major challenge in fungal pathogenesis research [ 9 , 24 ]. The study of El Hachem et al. [ 19 ] induced an experimental evolution of resistance under antifungal selective pressures and generated fluconazole and caspofungin-resistant isolates exposed to final concentrations of 16 μg/mL fluconazole and 2 μg/mL caspofungin, respectively. Six randomly selected fluconazole-resistant (FL1 to FL6) and 6 other randomly selected caspofungin-resistant (CS1 to CS6) isolates of the highest resistance category were subjected to further analyses in the current study by integrating whole-genome sequencing with phenotypic assays linked to antifungal resistance and virulence. Our main aim was to determine whether isolates exposed to similar selective pressures converge on common resistance mechanisms or follow diverse evolutionary trajectories. Whole-genome sequencing revealed extensive mutational heterogeneity across isolates, each accumulating a unique set of variants in comparison to the reference genome and none of the isolates shared an identical mutational profile ( Tables 1 and 3 ). We focused on 34 genes ( Fig 10 ) in fluconazole-resistant isolates and 22 genes carried mutations potentially linked to resistance and virulence ( Table 1 ). In caspofungin-resistant isolates, 31 genes were examined and mutations were detected in 20 genes that may contribute to resistance and virulence mechanisms ( Table 3 ). The genes analyzed were selected based on their established role in antifungal resistance and virulence according to the Candida Genome Database (CDG: https://www.candidagenome.org/ ). 10.1371/journal.pone.0360234.g010 Fig 10 Targeted genes analyzed in fluconazole-resistant (FL1-FL6) and caspofungin-resistant (CS1-CS6) isolates. Mash-derived ANI analyses revealed that all antifungal-resistant isolates exhibited a slightly lower genome-wide similarity to the reference strain C. albicans SC5314, indicating genomic divergence following experimental induction of antifungal resistance. Because Mash estimates ANI from shared k -mers rather than whole-genome alignments, it provides a descriptive measure of overall genomic relatedness that is useful for comparing closely related genomes and identifying clustering patterns rather than testing statistical significance. Similar applications of ANI analyses have been recently reported for C. albicans , where highly related isolates display very high ANI values while progressively divergent isolates separate into distinct genomic clusters, reflecting accumulated mutations [ 25 ]. Although all resistant isolates remained highly similar to one another, consistent with their origin from the same parental strain, both fluconazole-resistant and caspofungin-resistant isolates displayed measurable divergence from the reference genome ( Fig 3 ). Within the fluconazole-resistant group of isolates, ANI values remained highly comparable among all 6 isolates ( Fig 1 ), suggesting that independent adaptation occurred without substantial genome-wide divergence despite acquisition of distinct mutations. A similar pattern was observed for the caspofungin-resistant isolates, with the exception of CS6 which exhibited lower ANI values compared to other caspofungin-resistant isolates ( Fig 2 ). Supported by the other genomic analysis in this manuscript, CS6 seemed to accumulate a greater number of sequence variants, suggesting that it followed a more divergent evolutionary trajectory while converging on the same resistant phenotype. The combination of high intra-group similarity and modest divergence from the parental strain supports the concept that resistance emerged through independent evolutionary events and that antifungal pressure limits adaptation to a specific set of solutions at the cellular level; such as increased ergosterol and chitin deposition, even if the adopted genetic routes are variable. YMAP (Yeast Mapping Analysis Pipeline) profile analyses ( S1 File ) across both drug-evolved panels, showed differences from the C. albicans SC5314 parental profile that were driven primarily by copy number alterations and associated allele-ratio shifts. The fluconazole-resistant isolates span a spectrum from the near-euploid FL4 to extensive segmental copy number alterations. In the caspofungin-resistant isolates, genome-scale changes are similarly heterogeneous, ranging from largely diploid isolates with a single focal amplification to isolates with chromosome-scale copy gains and losses. Overall, YMAP supports that drug pressure frequently coincides with genome-structure remodeling dominated by copy number gains and losses, but the affected chromosomes, copy number variations, and amplitudes vary substantially among independently evolved isolates, arguing against a single convergent karyotypic signature for either drug in this set. Ergosterol biosynthesis plays a key role in fluconazole resistance since upregulation of ERG genes or mutations in ERG11 can partially restore ergosterol levels despite inhibition of lanosterol 14α-demethylase [ 13 , 26 , 27 ]. Fluconazole-resistant isolates showed strong increases ( Fig 5 ) reflecting the occurrence of changes in the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway, a known target of azoles. Several isolates carried mutations in ERG11 and ERG4 as in a previous report linking sterol pathway remodeling to azole resistance [ 19 ]. For example, T128K harbored by Erg11 was detected in FL1, FL3, and FL6 ( Table 1 ) as well as in fluconazole-resistant isolates from El Hachem et al. [ 19 ]. T128K was previously detected in fluconazole-resistant C. albicans [ 28 ]. Moreover, M157V harbored by Erg4 in two of our fluconazole-resistant isolates ( Table 1 ) was also reported in El Hachem et al. [ 19 ]. In addition, R18G was detected in one of our fluconazole-resistant isolates. Although the latter mutations are novel, they might have led to ERG4 overexpression which is an important factor linked to azole resistance [ 29 ]. Notably, the novel Q46H and G119S substitutions were recurrently detected in Erg24 for the majority of our fluconazole-resistant isolates and seem to be recurrent mutations involved in fluconazole resistance, probably altering the sterol biosynthesis enzyme composition ( Table 2 ). Taken together these results support the idea that membrane composition changes represent a conserved axis of adaptation when subjected to antifungal pressure. In addition to mutations and changes in gene expression of ERG11 and other genes involved in the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway as well as alterations in sterol composition, azole resistance is also mediated by efflux pump upregulation [ 13 ]. The R6G assay specifically assesses the activity of ABC transporters and no other efflux pumps [ 30 ]. ABC transporters include Cdr1 and Cdr2 which are under the control of the transcriptional regulator Tac1 [ 13 ]. Increased efflux activity was observed in 5 out of 6 fluconazole-resistant isolates ( Fig 7 ) but data was not shown in Table 1 as mutational variations were not recorded in CDR1 , CDR2 , and TAC1 , suggesting that transcriptional changes are inducing increased efflux pump activity [ 31 ]. Increased activation of efflux pumps belonging to the major facilitator superfamily; mainly Mdr1, can also be involved in fluconazole resistance [ 32 ]. Four isolates harbored the I325V amino acid substitution in Mdr1 ( Table 1 ), potentially increasing its specificity to azole transport. No mutations were detected in MRR1 , the regulator of MDR1 but this does not exclude gene expression changes in MDR1 and MRR1 . Similar to ergosterol, chitin content also experienced an increase in all isolates ( Fig 6 ). Chitin is a rigid cell wall polymer that can resist stress, including antifungal pressure. Its increased production has been previously shown to play a key role in caspofungin resistance [ 18 , 33 ]. In caspofungin-resistant isolates, increased chitin deposition can result from inhibition of β-1,3-glucan synthesis which triggers a compensatory reinforcement of the cell wall [ 18 , 33 , 34 ]. Although many mutations were detected in the chitin synthase gene CHS3 across multiple isolates, none were conserved across caspofungin-resistant isolates ( Table 3 ) which indicates that chitin accumulation might be achieved through diverse genetic routes rather than a single recurrent mutation. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that increased chitin content is a compensatory response to echinocandin-induced cell wall stress and can result from activation of several cell wall stress response pathways such as the Ca 2+ -calcineurin, high-osmolarity glycerol, and protein kinase C cell wall integrity pathways [ 18 , 34 – 36 ]. All CHS3 mutations detected in caspofungin-resistant isolates were also found in fluconazole-resistant isolates ( Tables 1 and 3 ). These mutations likely contribute to increased chitin deposition since they occur in both resistant groups, suggesting enhanced Chs3 activity through gain-of-function effects. Although CHS3 mutations seem to be associated with increased chitin production, the absence of recurrent mutations in caspofungin-resistant isolates was noted ( Table 4 ). This mutational pattern suggests different Chs3 substitutions converge on the same phenotype of increased chitin production under caspofungin selective pressure. Caspofungin-resistant isolates also exhibited elevated ergosterol levels ( Fig 5 ) and fluconazole-resistant isolates showed increased chitin deposition compared to the control ( Fig 6 ) suggesting plasma membrane and cell wall remodeling may represent broader stress-adaptive phenotypes. Even though fluconazole mainly targets ergosterol biosynthesis, it can also lead to significant changes in the cell wall proteome and compromise cell wall integrity [ 37 ]. This points to evolutionary adaptation favoring a group of recurrent advantageous adaptive genetic changes. Furthermore, caspofungin-resistant isolates depend on increased chitin production for survival because caspofungin; like all echinocandins, can directly weaken the cell wall of C. albicans by damaging the β-1,3-glucan network. Under echinocandin selective pressure, any CHS3 mutation that raises chitin levels even slightly, can favor survival [ 34 , 36 ]. As a result, diverse CHS3 mutations are maintained, preventing the emergence of dominant recurrent mutations. This may support the detection of CHS3 mutations in both resistance groups but recurrent mutations are mainly found in fluconazole-resistant isolates while caspofungin-resistant isolates exhibit mutational diversity ( Tables 1–3 ). Notably, elevated levels of chitin deposition might be correlated to CHS3 mutations since chitin increases reported by El Hachem et al. [ 19 ] were detected in absence of CHS3 mutations and were moderate compared with the more pronounced increases observed in our current isolates. Before acquiring resistance, C. albicans has to first activate stress response pathways that will eventually lead to cell wall and plasma membrane remodeling. In this study, both fluconazole and caspofungin-resistant isolates displayed almost the same accessory and recurrent mutations across the oxidative stress response genes SOD5 and SOD6 as well as HSP60 and MNL1 which are other stress response actors ( Tables 1–4 ). Mutations in these genes likely reflected general drug-adaptation responses because exposure to both drugs selected similar stress-response mutations that are independent of antifungal-specific resistance mechanisms. These mutations might enhance tolerance to antifungal-induced stress that would favor resistance development [ 38 , 39 ]. In addition to all the above antifungal resistance mechanisms, increased biofilm formation could prevent antifungal compounds from reaching their respective targets in C. albicans , leading to antifungal tolerance and potentially resistance development [ 40 , 41 ]. However, all our isolates displayed reduced biofilm biomass compared to the reference strain ( Fig 8 ) in the same manner as previous reports describing reduced biofilm formation in antifungal resistant C. albicans , particularly azole resistant isolates [ 19 , 42 , 43 ]. The observed decrease in biofilm formation in fluconazole and caspofungin-resistant isolates suggests that resistance evolution imposes fitness trade-offs that reduce virulence phenotypic characteristics [ 44 ]. Under antifungal stress, C. albicans activates survival and adaptation mechanisms such as ergosterol remodeling, efflux pump upregulation, increased chitin synthesis, and activation of stress response pathways. In ALS4 , HWP1 , NAG3 , SFL1 , and UME6 which are the studied group of genes involved in biofilm formation, almost all mutations were common to the fluconazole and caspofungin-resistant isolates ( Tables 1 and 3 ). However, caspofungin-resistant isolates did not carry recurrent mutations in genes implicated in biofilm formation unlike fluconazole-resistant isolates that harbored the recurrent changes A397V in Nag3, N257S in Ume6, as well as T75A and S373L in Sfl1 ( Tables 2 and 4 ). The absence of recurrent mutations in biofilm-associated genes suggests that caspofungin-resistant isolates did not follow a uniform genetic pathway leading to biofilm loss. In contrast, fluconazole-resistant isolates accumulated more recurrent mutations in key biofilm regulators which indicates a more shared genetic basis for the observed biofilm reduction. Antifungal resistance can impose fitness costs that reduce virulence, although this relationship is not universal and depends on the underlying resistance mechanism and associated genomic changes [ 45 ]. Consistent with this concept, the murine disseminated infection model revealed heterogeneous virulence among the resistant isolates ( Fig 4 ), indicating that resistance acquisition did not uniformly alter pathogenicity. Most fluconazole-resistant isolates showed delayed mortality compared to the reference strain, while the caspofungin-resistant isolates generally retained virulence levels similar or higher compared to the reference strain. These observed phenotypic differences were consistent with the genomic analyses. Fluconazole-resistant isolates accumulated numerous mutations in genes involved in adhesion, filamentation, and host interaction including ALS4 , CPH1 , EFG1 , HWP1 , SAP1 , and SFL1 ( Table 1 ), several of which regulate biofilm formation and hyphal development which are major determinants of C. albicans pathogenicity. Therefore, their reduced virulence likely reflects the cumulative fitness cost associated with multiple mutations affecting virulence-associated pathways, in agreement with the reduced biofilm formation observed in these isolates. In contrast, although the caspofungin-resistant isolates also accumulated numerous genomic changes including mutations affecting cell wall remodeling, most retained high virulence in vivo suggesting that the mechanisms conferring echinocandin resistance did not impair host adaptation. The greater genomic divergence observed in the ANI analysis of isolate CS6, did not associate with a markedly altered virulence, suggesting that overall genomic divergence is not necessarily predictive of pathogenicity. Altogether, these findings indicate that resistance evolved through distinct genomic trajectories that differentially affected virulence-associated pathways, supporting the concept that antifungal adaptation can preserve pathogenic potential despite extensive genomic remodeling. From a clinical perspective, this could be concerning because resistant isolates may remain highly virulent while simultaneously acquiring mutations that enhance survival under antifungal pressure. One limitation of this study is that experimental evolution does not fully capture the complexity of clinical infection environments where many factors are at play such as the hosts immunity and niche-specific pressures among others. Another limitation includes the small sample size. Although our genomic analyses detected mutations associated with resistance phenotypes, functional validation was beyond the scope of this project, especially that the main purpose of the study is to compare genomic responses amongst isolates. Despite these limitations, our study supports a model in which antifungal resistance evolution in C. albicans seems to be characterized by sequence-level divergence coupled with pathway level convergence. Also, development of antifungal resistance seems to be supported by a subset of recurrent adaptive mutations while a broader set of accessory mutations promote evolutionary flexibility. As a future direction, transcriptomic analysis targeting candidate genes should be conducted to evaluate gene expression levels. Furthermore, the genomic and transcriptomic profiles of our isolates should be compared with those of publicly available fluconazole-resistant and caspofungin-resistant C. albicans isolates to place our findings in a broader context. To sum up, this study demonstrated that different C. albicans isolates subjected to the same concentrations of fluconazole or caspofungin develop resistance through highly diverse mutational trajectories that converge on shared adaptive phenotypes including plasma membrane and cell wall remodeling, stress adaptation, and upregulation of efflux pumps ( Fig 9 ). Altogether, our findings highlight the predictability of antifungal resistance at the pathway level but show diversity at the genetic level, emphasizing the importance of pathway-focused prophylactic and therapeutic strategies. Materials and methods C. albicans isolates The reference strain C. albicans SC5314 (ATCC MYA-2876) was used as control. This same strain was incubated with fluconazole (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) or caspofungin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) at sequentially increasing concentrations at 2-week intervals over 3 months according to the protocol from El Hachem et al. [ 19 ]. The final drug concentrations to which the C. albicans reference strain was exposed were 16 μg/mL of fluconazole and 2 μg/mL of caspofungin. C. albicans grown in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI-1640) medium (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) supplemented with each drug at the aforementioned highest concentrations were plated on potato dextrose agar (Conda Laboratories, Madrid, Spain) and incubated overnight at 35°C. Six random colonies of different sizes were isolated per condition and grown overnight in RPMI-1640 supplemented with the respective drug concentration before being stored in 15% glycerol at −80°C. FL1 to FL6 designate colonies of C. albicans exposed to a final concentration of 16 μg/mL of fluconazole and CS1 to CS6 those subjected to a final concentration of 2 μg/mL of caspofungin. Whole-genome sequencing and bioinformatic analyses Genomic DNA was extracted using the ZR Fungal/Bacterial DNA MiniPrep kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Whole-genome sequencing at 90x coverage was carried out by MicrobesNG (University of Birmingham, Birmingham, UK). DNA libraries were prepared using Nextera XT Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) on a Hamilton Microlab STAR automated liquid handling system (Hamilton Bonaduz AG, Switzerland) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using a 250 bp paired-end protocol. Raw sequence reads were adapter-trimmed and quality-filtered using Trimmomatic v0.30 [ 46 ], followed by genome assembly with SPAdes v3.7 [ 47 ] and genome annotation using Prokka v1.11 [ 48 ]. The whole-genome sequencing data generated in this study have been deposited in the NCBI GenBank under BioProject number PRJNA1427196 ( https://submit.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/subs/wgs_batch/SUB16037922/overview ). Isolates FL1, FL2, FL3, FL4, FL5, and FL6 have the corresponding accession numbers respectively: SAMN56300551, SAMN56300552, SAMN56300553, SAMN56300554, SAMN56300555, and SAMN56300556. Isolates CS1, CS2, CS3, CS4, CS5, and CS6 have the corresponding accession numbers respectively: SAMN56300557, SAMN56300558, SAMN56300559, SAMN56300560, SAMN56300561, and SAMN56300562. Genome-wide single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) analysis was performed using the Snippy pipeline v4.5.0 [ 48 ] in which whole-genome sequences of C. albicans SC5314 genomes and the studied isolates were aligned against the reference strain C. albicans SC5314. SNPs were identified for each isolate and recurrent genome SNPs shared across all samples were extracted to generate a multiple sequence alignment. One file per isolate listing all the detected nucleotide variations compared to the reference genome was generated. Gene prediction was conducted using the BRAKER2 pipeline v2.1.6 in fungal mode which integrates GeneMark-ES v4.65 and AUGUSTUS v3.4.0 for fungal gene prediction, enabling the identification of gene models along with their corresponding coding sequences and mRNA features [ 49 – 53 ]. InterProScan v5.50-84.0 [ 54 ] was used on the Cluster of Orthologous Groups of proteins (COG) database to create the.xml files to incorporate in the functional annotation pipeline created by Funannotate v.1.8.7 [ 55 ]. The pipeline starts by running HMMscan implemented in the HMMER suite v3.3 against the Pfam database. Orthology-based functional classification was subsequently performed using eggNOG-mapper (emapper) v2.1.2 based on eggNOG orthology assignments [ 56 , 57 ]. Protein sequence similarity searches were conducted using DIAMOND BLASTp against the UniProt database v2021_02 and MEROPS protease database v12.0 [ 58 ]. The resulting annotations were consolidated using Gene2Product v1.69 and SignalP v5.0 was applied to predict secreted proteins [ 59 ]. The completeness and quality of genome assemblies and annotations were evaluated using BUSCO v5.2.2 [ 60 ]. In parallel, variant calling files generated independently by MicrobesNG following whole-genome sequencing were used for complementary analyses and detected mutations were visualized in Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) v2.16.2 [ 61 ]. Subsequent analyses focused on a targeted set of genes of known relevance in order to compare mutation patterns in fluconazole-resistant and caspofungin-resistant isolates. Analysis was focused on targeted genes involved in pathways that can possibly mediate antifungal resistance and adaptive stress responses ( Fig 10 ). Gene names were retrieved from the Candida Genome Database (CGD: http://www.candidagenome.org/ ) [ 62 ]. Pairwise genome similarity analysis Pairwise genomic relatedness among isolates was assessed using Mash to estimate genome-wide distances [ 63 ]. Mash-derived distances were used to construct a pairwise genome similarity matrix, which was visualized as a heatmap using R statistical computing software. To facilitate interpretation of genomic similarity, Mash distances were converted to approximate average nucleotide identity (ANI) values. These ANI values represent approximate genome-wide similarity estimates based on shared k -mers rather than exact nucleotide identity. Consequently, lower ANI values indicate greater genomic divergence across the shared genomic fraction. Comparative chromosomal abnormality analyses The Yeast Mapping Analysis Pipeline (YMAP) ( http://lovelace.cs.umn.edu/Ymap/ ) was used to perform a comparative chromosomal abnormality analyses of all isolates versus the reference strain C. albicans SC5314 and the results are shown in S1 File . Murine model of systemic candidiasis In vivo pathogenicity was assessed using a murine model of systemic C. albicans infection. The protocol was adapted from Khalaf et al., Fattouh et al., and El Hady et al. [ 64 – 66 ]. C. albicans isolates were inoculated into 50 mL of potato dextrose broth (PDB) (Conda Laboratories, Madrid, Spain) and incubated for 18 h at 30°C and 100 rpm. Each isolate was injected into one of the lateral caudal veins of 6 BALB/c female mice aged 6 weeks. For each injection, 10 7 C. albicans were harvested by centrifugation for 5 min at 3000 rpm and resuspended in 0.2 mL of 1x phosphate buffered saline solution (PBS 1x). The number of moribund mice was evaluated on a daily basis. Mice were provided with food and water ad libitum. This experiment was conducted in compliance with the ethical standards of the Lebanese American University’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and approved under protocol number LAU.ACUC.SAS.RK3.28/January/2022. Mice were housed under standard laboratory conditions. Accordingly, 6 mice were injected per strain (a total of 78 mice). Mice were monitored daily for a period of one month. Humane endpoints included refusal to eat, inability to move, or severe lethargy. As such, mice that appeared moribund were humanely euthanized by CO 2 inhalation followed by cervical dislocation. All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering. In summary, out of 78 mice, 44 mice were found dead, and 34 were euthanized at humane endpoints. All staff involved in handling mice have CITI certificates on working with mice in research settings. Quantification of ergosterol content in C. albicans C. albicans isolates were inoculated into 50 mL PDB at 35°C and 100 rpm for 24 h. Cultures were transferred to pre-weighed conical tubes and centrifuged for 5 min at 2700 rpm. The pellet weight was calculated before being suspended in 3 mL of 25% alcoholic potassium hydroxide by manual vortexing for 1 min. The suspensions were transferred to borosilicate glass screw cap tubes, incubated in an 85°C water bath for 1 h then cooled at room temperature for 15 min. Next, 1 mL of sterile water and 3 mL of n -heptane were added to the suspension and manually vortexed for 3 min. After vortexing, phase separation occurred within approximately 1 h of incubation at room temperature. The upper layers containing the extracted sterols were transferred to clean borosilicate glass screw cap tubes and stored for 24 h at −20°C. For quantification, 0.2 mL of the sterol-containing layer was mixed to 0.8 mL of pure ethanol and optical densities (OD) at 230 and 281.5 nm were measured using a Genesys 10S UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Ergosterol content was quantified as a percentage of the wet weight of the cell according to the “sterol quantification method (SQM)” protocol adapted from Arthington-Skaggs et al. [ 67 ] with some modifications. The mathematical formulas from Arthington-Skaggs et al. and Chedraoui et al. [ 67 , 68 ] were adopted. The experiment was performed in biological triplicates. Quantification of chitin content in C. albicans C. albicans isolates were inoculated into 5 mL PDB and incubated overnight at 30°C and 80 rpm. The cell wall was extracted according to the “cell wall isolation” protocol of El Khoury et al. [ 69 ] and chitin was quantified based on the “determination of chitin content in cell walls” protocol adapted from Kapteyn et al. [ 70 ]Cultures were centrifuged for 5 min at 4000 rpm and the pellets were resuspended in 0.5 mL Tris buffer (5 mM, pH 7.8) and transferred to pre-weighed microcentrifuge tubes containing 3 pre-chilled undrilled glass beads of 3–5 mm in diameter (Himedia Laboratories, Mumbai, India). C. albicans cells in the pellet were disrupted by vortexing for 60 min using a multi-tube vortex mixer. The lysate was separated from the glass beads by being transferred to new pre-weighed microcentrifuge tubes. To ensure the complete recovery of lysates, the beads were washed with 0.5 mL of cold 1M NaCl, and the wash solution was combined with its respective sample. The samples were then centrifuged for 5 min at 3000 rpm. The pellet weights were determined before suspension in 0.5 mL protein extraction buffer (150 mM NaCl, 100 mM Na-EDTA, 50 mM Tris, 2% SDS, and 8 μL/mL β-mercaptoethanol, pH 7.8) per 100 mg of pellet wet weight. Suspensions were boiled at 100°C for 10 min, cooled for 5 min, and centrifuged for 5 min at 3000 rpm. The supernatant was discarded and the latter boiling, cooling, and centrifugation steps were repeated one more time. Pellets were then washed twice with 1 mL type II water to remove residual SDS and each step was followed by centrifugation for 5 min at 3000 rpm [ 69 ]. For extraction of cell wall chitin, 1 mL of 6N HCl was added to the pellets and boiled at 100°C for 10 min. After cooling, samples were centrifuged for 5 min at 3000 rpm and the pellets were re-suspended in 1 mL type II water. For quantification, 0.1 mL of sample was mixed with 0.1 mL of a 1.5 N Na 2 CO 3 solution containing 4% acetylacetone. Mixtures were incubated at 100°C for 20 min then, cooled for 5 min at room temperature. Subsequently, 0.7 mL pure ethanol and 0.1 mL of a solution made of 1.6 g of p-dimethylbenzaldehyde dissolved in 30 mL concentrated HCl and 30 mL absolute ethanol were added. Samples were incubated at room temperature for 1h then OD at 520 nm was measured and chitin concentrations were calculated based on a glucosamine standard curve [ 70 , 71 ]. The experiment was performed in biological triplicates. Quantification of R6G efflux in C. albicans The R6G assay was used to evaluate ATP-dependent efflux pump activity based on an adaptation of the protocol from Maesaki et al. [ 72 ]. This assay was only carried out on the fluconazole-resistant isolates because increased activity of ABC transporters is a well-established mechanism of azole resistance in C. albicans . R6G is a fluorescent substrate of ABC transporters, making it a suitable indicator of their efflux activity [ 72 ]. Only fluconazole-resistant C. albicans isolates were inoculated into 5 mL PDB in 50 mL conical tubes and incubated overnight at 32°C and 100 rpm. A total of 10 8 cells were harvested and resuspended in 10 mL PDB then, centrifuged at 4°C for 7 min at 5000 rpm. The pellet was re-suspended in 10 mL PBS 1x and incubated at 32°C for 2h at 90 rpm to deplete intracellular ATP and equilibrate transport activity. To allow the uptake of the R6G dyes, 10 μL of 10 mM R6G was added and samples were incubated for an additional hour at 32°C and 90 rpm. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 4°C for 7 min at 5000 rpm and then, the pellet was washed twice with 10 mL PBS 1x under the same centrifugation conditions to remove residual extracellular dye. The final pellet was resuspended in 10 mL PBS 1x and incubated at 32°C and 90 rpm to initiate R6G efflux. At 15, 30, 45, and 60 min, 1.4 mL aliquots were collected and centrifuged at 9000 xg for 2 min and 1 mL of the supernatant was subjected to OD measurements at 527 nm [ 73 ]. This experiment was performed in biological triplicates and the mean OD per triplicate was calculated. Quantification of C. albicans biofilm biomass C. albicans isolates were inoculated into 5 mL PDB and incubated overnight at 30°C and 80 rpm. In parallel, flat-bottom 96-well microtiter plates were pre-conditioned with 5% fetal bovine serum and incubated overnight at 4°C to enhance cell attachment and then, fetal bovine serum was washed out before adding a final concentration of 2 x 10 6 cells/mL in PDB at a final volume of 0.2 mL in each well of the microtiter plate and incubated for 3 h at 37°C and 100 rpm. Following initial adhesion, the culture medium was removed and replaced by 0.2 mL fresh PDB and plates were incubated for 48 h at 37°C without shaking to allow biofilm formation. To quantify the biofilm biomass, PDB was removed and biofilms were fixed with 0.2 mL of 99% methanol for 15 min at room temperature. Methanol was discarded and plates were left to air-dry for 20 min. Biofilms were stained with 0.2 mL of 0.02% crystal violet for 20 min then, washed five times with type II water to remove excess stain. After 15 min of air-drying, crystal violet was released by adding 33% acetic acid to the wells and OD was measured at 590 nm using a Multiskan FC Microplate Photometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA). This protocol was adapted from Fattouh et al. [ 65 , 74 ]. The experiment was performed in biological triplicates and each biological replicate was analyzed in three technical replicates from which the mean OD was calculated. Statistical analyses and data visualization Statistical analyses and data presentation were carried out in GraphPad Prism v10.6.1. For ergosterol, chitin, and biofilm biomass quantification assays, Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test was used to compare the fluconazole or caspofungin-resistant group of isolates to the control. For the quantification of R6G efflux, each fluconazole-resistant isolate was compared to the control using an ordinary two-way ANOVA with main effects only. For all statistical tests, p-values below 0.05 were considered significant.
