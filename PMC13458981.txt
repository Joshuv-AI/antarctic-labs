Enterovirus D68 Detected in Hospitalized Children With Severe Acute Respiratory Infections in Zambia
Authors: Idah Mweene Ndumba, Michiko Okamoto, Paul Simusika, Takeaki Imamura, Mwaka Monze, Mayuko Saito, Hitoshi Oshitani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEnterovirus D68 (EV‐D68) is a cause of severe acute respiratory infections (SARI) and is associated with acute flaccid paralysis. However, data on EV‐D68 in Africa, where SARI remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among children, remain limited. We conducted a retrospective analysis to detect EV‐D68 in archived samples collected from hospitalized children with SARI in Zambia from 2018 to 2020. We identified eight positive samples in 2020, and genetic sequence analysis showed that all sequences belonged to subclade A2. Continuous monitoring is crucial to enhance understanding of the epidemiology and molecular characteristics of EV‐D68 in Africa.Keywords: enterovirus D68, severe acute respiratory infections, viruses, Zambia

Introduction

Enterovirus D68 (EV‐D68), first identified in the United States in 1962, is now recognized as a re‐emerging pathogen associated with severe acute respiratory illness (SARI) [1]. Studies have documented its detection in cases of SARI, as well as its association with fatal outcomes [2, 3]. During a major outbreak of EV‐D68 in North America in 2014, the increased incidence of acute flaccid myelitis (AFM) was observed [4]. These findings suggest that paralytic syndromes of nonpolio etiology are likely to persist beyond the eradication of polio [5], underscoring the need for continued study of the EV‐D68, including molecular characteristics.

EV‐D68 viruses are classified into three main clades: A–C [6]. Clade B viruses are further divided into Subclades B1–B3, whereas Clade A viruses include Subclades A1 and A2 [7]. Most currently circulating EV‐D68 strains belong to Subclades B3 and A2, with amino acid substitutions in putative neutralizing epitopes within the bc and DE loops, indicative of ongoing antigenic evolution [8].

Although evidence from diverse geographic settings underscores the increasing global clinical relevance of enterovirus D68 (EV‐D68), data from Africa remain scarce, limiting a comprehensive understanding of its regional epidemiology and public health impact. However, there have been reports of EV‐D68 detection among SARI cases in South Africa from 2009 to 2011 [9]. Senegal has reported outbreaks of Subclade B3 in 2016 and 2023 [10, 11]. In Zambia, little is known about EV‐D68. Therefore, this study aimed to identify and characterize EV‐D68 among hospitalized children presenting with SARI in Zambia.

Materials and Methods and Data Analysis

Study Participants and Study Site

A total of 1361 nasopharyngeal swabs were collected between January 2018 and December 2020 from pediatric patients under 5 years of age who were hospitalized with SARI [12] in the children's hospital at the University Teaching Hospital (UTH), Lusaka, Zambia.

Laboratory Procedures

Out of a total of 1361 SARI samples, 1099 samples were available for this study and were tested for EV‐D68, whereas for the rest of the samples, 31 did not meet the age range criteria, and 231 were not found. RNA extraction was performed using a QIAmp Mini extraction kit (Qiagen, Hamburg, Germany) according to the manufacturer's protocols. The eluate served as a template for complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis with Moloney Murine Virus reverse transcriptase and random primers (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, United States). The synthesized cDNA was used for EV‐D68 detection by real‐time polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) targeting the 5′ untranslated region (5′ UTR), using primers EQ 1 and EQ 2 [13] and a probe (5′‐6‐FAM‐GTTGCTTGTCGTAAYGCGCAAGTC‐3′‐BHQ‐1) designed for EV‐D68 detection. Sequencing of the amplified VP1 region of EV‐D68 was carried out using previously described methods [2]. The VP1‐negative samples were re‐extracted and sequenced targeting the 5′ UTR using primers, DK001 and DK004, as previously described [2], and the obtained sequence data were subjected to BLAST search (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) for sequence identification.

A maximum‐likelihood phylogenetic analysis of the EV‐D68 VP1 region was constructed using a web‐based IQ‐TREE v2.0.3 with 1000 replicates. Translation of the nucleotide sequences into amino acids was conducted using MEGA Version 6, and amino acid alignments were also created with MAFFT implemented within MEGA Version 6. Publicly available EV‐D68 VP1 sequences collected up to 2024 were downloaded from GenBank in May 2025 and used for comparison with those obtained in this study. These reference sequences included the top 100 BLAST hits for the Zambian strains, all available African VP1 sequences, and additional sequences randomly selected from other geographic regions.

Results

Out of a total of 1099 samples tested for EV‐D68, 27 samples tested positive by RT‐PCR targeting 5′ UTR, and sequence analysis of VP1 confirmed seven EV‐D68 positives out of the 27 RT‐PCR positives. Sequencing of the VP1 negative samples targeting the 5′ UTR yielded one additional positive sample showing 93.6% identity to EV‐D68 on BLAST search; the rest of the samples did not yield any sequences, and they were considered to be other enteroviruses/rhinovirus or low level or bad quality of the EV‐D68 RNA. Eight samples (0.7%) were successfully sequenced and identified as EV‐D68. Figure S1 shows the temporal distribution of EV‐D68‐positive samples. There were no EV‐D68 positive samples in 2018 or 2019, and all positive samples were detected from January to March 2020. Age of EV‐D68 positive cases ranged from 0 to 36 months: 0–5 months four [50%], 6–11 months one (12.5%), and 12–36 months three (37.5%).

The phylogenetic tree was constructed using VP1 sequences from seven study samples alongside reference VP1 sequences retrieved from GenBank (Figure 1). One positive sample identified by the 5′ UTR sequence was excluded from the analysis due to the inability to analyze the VP1. All sequences from this study are clustered within EV‐D68 Subclade A2. The phylogenetic tree showed close genetic relatedness to the strains from China, the United States, and Europe.

The amino acid patterns of the epitope at the bc loop (Positions 90, 92, 95, 97, 98, and 103) and DE loop (Positions 140, 141, 142, 143, 144, 145, 146, 147, and 148), which are putative antigenic sites, were shown in the recent global sequence analysis [9]. Most Zambian sequences had NTGRIY at the bc loop and GSNNSTYM at the DE loop; both patterns are observed in recently circulating Subclade A2 strains [8]. Only one strain in this study exhibited an amino acid substitution at position 97 (R97Q) within the bc loop. Another strain showed an amino acid substitution at position 307 (L307V) outside of the putative antigenic sites. Both amino acid substitutions, including the one in the bc loop, were found in only one sample each in the present study.

Discussion

This study aimed to examine the proportion of EV‐D68 among hospitalized children with SARI in Zambia. Out of 1099 samples tested, only eight (0.7%) were positive for EV‐D68. The research was conducted from 2018 to 2020; however, no EV‐D68 was detected in the 2018 or 2019 samples. All positive samples were collected from January to March 2020, just before Zambia implemented strict public health measures against COVID‐19.

We obtained sufficient VP1 sequences from seven samples. All sequences analyzed in this study clustered within Subclade A2. A previous study that analyzed samples from African countries between 2008 and 2011 identified A1 as the main subclade in Senegal, Kenya, and Gambia [7]. However, analysis of sequences obtained between 2009 and 2011 from South Africa, which is closer to Zambia, revealed that B2 was the dominant subclade [11]. Recent molecular studies indicate that most circulating EV‐D68 viruses worldwide belong to Subclades B3 and A2, with B3 becoming predominant since 2015 [7, 8, 14]. Also in Africa, a major EV‐D68 outbreak in Senegal in 2023 was caused by subclade B3 [11]. Nevertheless, we did not find any B3 viruses in this study; all detected sequences were classified as Subclade A2. However, the increasing trend of Subclade A2 has been reported [8]. There was a limited number of EV‐D68 detections in 2020 due to the COVID‐19 pandemic in other countries. However, a multicountry European study identified some EV‐D68‐positive cases, including a few A2‐positive cases, in 2019 [15]. Our dataset also included some A2 sequences identified in 2019 and 2020, in Europe, the United States, and China (Figure 1). The A2 sequences identified in this study clustered with sequences from other regions, such as China, the United States, and Spain, but not with sequences from other African countries, such as South Africa and Senegal. These findings suggest a global circulation of EV‐D68, especially Subclade A2. Longer term studies are needed to better understand the circulation patterns in Africa. Further, the implication of EV‐D68 in AFM [4] underscores the need to monitor the disease in children.

The present study has several limitations. We conducted a retrospective analysis of stored samples that may have been affected by long‐term storage. We could not obtain sufficient sequence data for one of the positive samples, and it was excluded from the final phylogenetic analysis. We could have obtained more sequences had the study been conducted prospectively. Additionally, we analyzed samples from only one hospital in Lusaka, the capital of Zambia. Further, we only tested pediatric samples, whereas some studies have reported Subclade A2 infections in adults [7]. The data from this study may not reflect circulating strains nationwide.

Despite these limitations, this study provides novel insights into the epidemiology and molecular characteristics of EV‐D68 in Zambia, contributing to the evidence base for surveillance and future EV‐D68 molecular epidemiological studies.

Conclusions

This study detected EV‐D68 among pediatric SARI cases in Zambia, underscoring its emerging public health relevance and potential contribution to the disease burden. These findings highlight the imperative to further investigate EV‐D68 and to strengthen molecular and epidemiological investigations to better define its transmission dynamics, clinical impact, and role in both respiratory and neurological disease.
