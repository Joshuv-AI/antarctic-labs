ZIP1 Is a Critical DNA Damage‐Responsive Zinc Transporter Induced by Bleomycin via the TNFα–NF‐κB Pathway
Authors: Zichong Li, Jiajian Yuan, Ziqi Zhang, Hong Huang, Mei Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTZinc is an essential trace element that plays a significant role in DNA damage repair (DDR). Cells must maintain zinc homeostasis to effectively respond to DNA damage caused by various stressful stimuli. Members of the Zrt‐, Irt‐related protein (ZIP) family are recognized as key regulators of intracellular zinc levels. Dysfunction of ZIPs has been associated with numerous diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular diseases, and neurodegenerative disorders. In the present study, human bronchial epithelial BEAS‐2B cells were used to investigate the response of ZIPs to bleomycin, a well‐known antibiotic that induces DNA damage. Among the ZIPs that exhibited altered expression following bleomycin treatment, ZIP1 showed the most prompt and significant induction. Both knockdown and overexpression experiments demonstrated that ZIP1 plays a crucial role in the cellular response to DNA damage. Mechanistically, we found that the level of TNFα was upregulated in bleomycin‐treated cells, and inhibiting this cytokine reduced the induction effect of ZIP1 caused by bleomycin. Additionally, we observed an increase in the accumulation of NF‐κB in the cell nuclei, and inhibition of NF‐κB also diminished the effect of bleomycin on ZIP1. Two NF‐κB binding sites were identified in the upstream sequence of SLC39A1, the gene that encodes ZIP1. The application of bleomycin significantly enhanced the binding of NF‐κB to these specific sites. Taken together, our study reveals that bleomycin treatment may activate the TNFα‐NF‐κB pathway, which in turn upregulates ZIP1, helping cells better cope with the DNA damage induced by this antibiotic.Keywords: bleomycin, DNA damage repair, TNFα‐NF‐κB pathway, zinc, ZIP1

Introduction

Zinc (Zn) is one of the most important trace elements, indispensable to the growth and development of microorganisms, plants, and animals [1]. Zinc serves as a cofactor to more than 300 enzymes and plays important roles in stabilizing the structure of a large number of proteins, including signaling enzymes at all levels of cellular signal transduction and transcription factors [2]. Furthermore, Zn is involved in immune responses, oxidative stress, apoptosis, and aging [1]. Zinc content has a marked mechanistic impact on DNA as a component of chromatin structure, DNA replication, transcription, and DNA repair [3]. Deficits in zinc, along with other essential vitamins and minerals, have long been known to induce DNA damage by mechanisms similar to environmental mutagens such as ionizing radiation [4, 5]. Low levels of intracellular zinc were found to cause oxidative DNA damage and suppress the DNA‐binding ability of p53 and other important downstream signals that lead to improper DNA repair, though the expressions of these proteins are induced [6].

The homeostasis of zinc in mammals involves controlled uptake, sequestration, and efflux of this essential metal. For maintaining cellular functions, the delivery of zinc to tissues and cells, intracellular availability, and intracellular distribution are tightly controlled through the coordinated actions of diverse families of transporters that modulate the uptake, efflux, and compartmentalization of zinc [7]. Since the lipid bilayer is impermeable to zinc, its transport across the cellular and organelle membranes is tightly regulated by transporters. The solute carrier family 30A (SLC30A), also known as the Zn transporter (ZnT) family, is efflux transport proteins that mobilize zinc from the cytosol; while the solute carrier family 39A (SLC39A) family, also known as the Zrt‐, Irt‐related protein (ZIP) family, are uptake transporters that are involved in moving zinc into the cytosol from the extracellular space or from intracellular stores, such as the endoplasmic reticulum and the Golgi apparatus [8]. Some members of the ZIP family were also found to transport other metals such as iron [9], manganese [10], and cadmium [11].

DNA damage can be caused by a wide range of environmental factors, such as reactive oxygen species arising from toxic agents and Fenton reactions mediated by heavy metals, ultraviolet light, and ionizing radiation. Many diseases, including multiple kinds of cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, immune deficiency, and aging, are closely linked to DNA damage [12]. Although zinc level is considered to be essential for maintaining the integrity of DNA, whether such a process is related to the level of zinc transporter and/or how cells adjust themselves by altering the expression of zinc transporters in response to DNA damage is not very clear. In the present study, bleomycin, an antibiotic drug that knowingly induces DNA damage [13, 14], was applied to generate DNA damage, and the expression level of zinc transporters was measured after the treatment. It was found that ZIP1 exhibited the most immediate and significant induction, and that the knockdown and overexpression of ZIP1 led to altered DNA repair capacity of the cells. Further investigation revealed that the tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα)‐nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB) pathway is responsible for the upregulation of ZIP1 expression after bleomycin treatment.

Materials and Methods

Materials

The Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM), M‐MLV reverse transcriptase, Platinum Taq DNA Polymerase, TRIzol reagent were from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Quantitative PCR reagents were purchased from Bio‐Rad (Hercules, CA, USA). All other reagents were from Sigma except where otherwise stated.

Cell Culture and Bleomycin Treatment

BEAS‐2B cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA) and grown in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37°C. The cells were allowed to grow for 24 h to ensure proper attachment before undergoing bleomycin treatment. Different concentrations of bleomycin were added to the cell medium for 1 h. After treatment, the medium was removed, and the cells were subsequently grown in either a complete medium, a zinc‐depleted medium, or a zinc‐depleted medium supplemented with different concentrations of zinc chloride. The zinc‐depleted medium was obtained with a method described in our previous study [15]. Briefly, the same batch of FBS was divided into two portions. One portion was left untreated to serve as the control, while the other was treated with Chelex‐100 overnight to remove the zinc content. The FBS with or without zinc was added to the DMEM medium for zinc‐related experiments.

Foci Counting

At the indicated time points, cells grown on glass slips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‐100, then blotted with 3% goat serum and subjected to incubation with Phospho‐Histone H2AX (γ‐H2AX, #9718, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) overnight at 4°C. The bound primary antibodies were detected by reaction with BeyoAb AF488 goat anti‐rabbit IgG antibody (#A1104, Beyotime Technology, Shanghai, China) for 1 h. After thoroughly washing with tris‐buffered saline (with 0.05% Tween 20), the glass slips with samples were mounted in Fluoromount mounting medium and examined using a Leica DMI 6000B inverted microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

The number of γ‐H2AX was measured and calculated according to Lassmann et al. [16] with minor modifications. Briefly, γ‐H2AX number per 10 nuclei was calculated with the formula Σfoci/n total × 10, in which Σfoci represented all the foci counted in the cells at a given observation view and n total being the total number of cells evaluated at that view. At least three observation views were measured for each treatment, and the average value was then calculated to represent the foci number of the corresponding sample in a single experiment. All counting was performed manually by the same operator who was blind to the experimental conditions to minimize variation and bias.

Measurement of Cellular Zinc Content With Zinquin Ethyl Ester

Changes in intracellular zinc content were measured using the cell‐permeable, quinolone‐based fluorescent zinc indicator, zinquin ethyl ester (MCE, Deer Park, NJ, USA). In live cells, the ethyl ester group is cleaved by cytosolic esterases, which prevents the efflux of the remaining negatively charged zinquin across the plasma membrane. Zinquin has high selectivity for zinc, and binding to zinc significantly increases the probe's fluorescence intensity [17]. Therefore, the fluorescence intensity within the cells is directly proportional to the concentration of zinc. For zinc content measurement, cells were washed thoroughly with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) after different treatments. Subsequently, zinquin ethyl ester (40 μM) in FBS‐free DMEM was added to each well, and the cells were incubated at 37°C for 30 min in the dark. After the incubation, the cells were washed twice with PBS before measuring the signals using a GloMax Multi Jr Single Tube Multimode Reader (Promega), with an excitation wavelength of 368 nm and an emission wavelength of 490 nm.

Isolation of RNA and Quantitative PCR

After the treatment with bleomycin, cells were collected at the indicated time points, and total RNA was isolated from cells with TRIzol reagent. Equal amounts of RNA were subjected to reverse transcription reaction, then the resulting cDNA was used for real‐time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). The SYBR Green methodology was utilized as previously described [18] with an ABI Prism 7000 detector (Applied Biosystem, Foster City, CA, USA). Gene‐specific primers for the zinc transporters were designed with the Primer3 software (Whitehead Institute for Biomedical Research, Cambridge, MA, USA) (Table 1). Specificity of the amplification of each gene was verified at the end of each qPCR reaction by analyzing the dissociation curves of the products. Relative quantification of the target mRNA levels was calculated by normalizing the cDNA amount to a GAPDH endogenous reference.

Western Blotting

At the indicated time points after bleomycin treatment, BEAS‐2B cells were washed and dissolved with RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% SDS, 1% NP‐40, protease inhibitors phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 200 μg/mL, leupeptin, 3 μg/mL, pH 7.4). The extracted proteins were released in 4× Laemmli buffer and loaded onto an SDS‐polyacrylamide gel, electrophoretically transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA), and detected with the corresponding antibodies for ZIP1 (#CSB‐PA17954A0Rb, CUSABIO Technology, Wuhan, China), TNFα (#AF8208, Beyotime Technology), or NF‐κB (#AF0246, Beyotime Technology).

The Generation of Knockdown Cell Lines

Knockdown cell lines were created using two sets of short hairpin RNA (shRNA) that target different regions of the coding sequence of the corresponding gene. The target sequences for ZIP1 are as follows: shZIP1#1: GCCGCAGCATTGGCATGATGG and shZIP1#2: GCTGTTGCAGAGCCACCTTAG. The target sequences for TNFα are: TNF#1: CCTGTAGCCCATGTTGTAGCAA and TNF#2: GGCAGGTCTACTT TGGGATCAT. Each pair of shRNA oligonucleotides was cloned into the lentiviral plasmid vector pLKO.1. The constructs were sequence‐verified (ThermoFisher) to ensure accuracy. HEK293T packaging cells were co‐transfected with the shRNA‐pLKO.1 plasmid, packaging plasmid psPAX2, and envelope plasmid pMD2.G using polyethylenimine for lentivirus production. Viral supernatants were harvested 48 and 72 h after transfection, filtered, and concentrated by ultracentrifugation at 25 000× g. BEAS‐2B cells were then transduced with the lentivirus in the presence of polybrene to enhance viral entry. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with fresh culture medium. Puromycin (1 μg/mL) was added to the culture medium 48 h post‐transduction to select for stably transduced cells that incorporated the shRNA vector. The mRNA and protein levels of the target proteins were checked before the knockdown cells were used for further experiments.

Construction of Plasmid DNA Containing Different Fragments of the Upstream Regulatory Sequence of SLC39A1

Plasmids containing various fragments of the upstream regulatory sequence of SLC39A1, the coding gene of ZIP1, were constructed and inserted into the luciferase reporter vector pGL3 (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) using the Omega‐PCR technique [19]. Site‐directed mutagenesis was carried out using the QuikChange Lightning Site‐Directed Mutagenesis Kit from Agilent (Santa Clara, CA, USA). All mutated sequences were verified through full‐length sequencing.

Cell Transfection of Plasmid Constructs

Confluent BEAS‐2B cells were transfected with DNA plasmids with LipofectAMINE 2000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Transfected cells were incubated for 48 h at 37°C before undergoing the following analyses.

Analysis of Luciferase Activity

Luciferase activity was measured using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega), following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, cells in 48‐well plates were lysed using passive lysis buffer. After lysis, Luciferase Assay Reagent II was added, and the samples were measured with the GloMax Multi Jr Single Tube Multimode Reader. Subsequently, Stop & Glo Reagent was added to quench the luminescence of firefly luciferase while simultaneously activating Renilla luciferase.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed using the ChIP assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology) following the methodology outlined by Xiang et al. [20]. In brief, cells were treated with 1% formaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min to induce cross‐linking. The reaction was quenched with glycine, and the cells were then scraped, washed, and lysed with SDS lysis buffer. The lysate was subjected to sonication using a Branson S450‐D digital sonifier (Branson Ultrasonic, Danbury, Connecticut, USA). Antibody (1:100 dilution) was added to the solution and incubated overnight at 4°C to label the target proteins, which were pulled down by the protein G agarose/salmon sperm DNA. The DNA‐protein complexes were released from the beads, then the DNA was purified with High pure PCR product purification kit (Roche Applied Science, Penzberg, Germany) and analyzed using qPCR with the following primers: for site 1—forward: 5′‐TACACTGTAGCACTAGCCAG‐3′, reverse: 5′‐CAGAGCCATTATCTCAGCCT‐3′; for site 3—Forward: 5′‐GCTGAAGCCT CTTATCACAG‐3′, reverse: 5′‐GTTCCGGTAACTAGGCTACA‐3′.

Data Analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical analysis of experimental data was carried out using Student's t‐test when two pairs of samples were compared. For comparison of more than two groups of samples, one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Dunnett's test with Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Differences between means are regarded as significant if p < 0.05.

Results

Bleomycin Caused DNA Damage in BEAS‐2B Cells

Bleomycin is an anti‐tumor drug that is widely used in clinical chemotherapy. The toxic effects of bleomycin are linked to its ability to mediate both single‐stranded and double‐stranded DNA damage [21]. It has been demonstrated that bleomycin can induce γ‐H2AX foci mapped preferentially to replicating domains in interphase nuclei of Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells [13]. To verify the DNA damage effect of bleomycin in the cellular system we utilized, we first investigated the concentration‐dependency of DNA damage in BEAS‐2B cells treated with bleomycin. As shown in Figure 1, bleomycin led to a significantly higher number of γ‐H2AX foci, which form rapidly at the sites of DNA double‐stranded breaks (DSBs) and are considered a sensitive indicator of DNA damage [22]. The higher the concentration of bleomycin, the more severe the DNA damage. However, the foci number gradually decreased during the time period observed with a similar rate in all the treatments, suggesting a progress of DNA damage repair (DDR) and that the DDR capacity in the cells is comparable when treated with different concentrations of bleomycin.

Depletion of Zinc Caused DNA Damage in BEAS‐2B Cells

Next, we wanted to see whether depletion of the extracellular pool of zinc affects the DDR capacity of BEAS‐2B cells after bleomycin treatment. According to a procedure described before [23], FBS was treated with the Chelex‐100 ion exchange resin overnight to chelate the zinc. The Chelex‐100‐treated FBS, in which no zinc content was detected by a Varian SpectrAA 220FS atomic absorption spectrometer (Palo Alto, CA, USA), was then added to the DMEM medium to generate the zinc‐depleted medium [15]. As shown in Figure 2A, the depletion of the extracellular pool of zinc led to a higher number of γ‐H2AX foci even without bleomycin treatment. When cells were treated with bleomycin, the depletion of the extracellular pool of zinc led to a more dramatic elevation of foci number. Moreover, though the cells grown in the zinc‐depleted medium also exhibited reduced foci number during the time period observed, the rate of foci number reduction appeared to be slower compared to cells grown in zinc‐containing medium. Even in the medium that contained a relatively low concentration of zinc that is, 15 μM, cells exhibited higher repair capability, recovered at a rate that is comparable to that in control medium or medium containing higher concentrations of zinc. Furthermore, we measured the zinc content in cells treated with bleomycin, and found that the intracellular zinc level was significantly increased at 3 h after the treatment and gradually reduced to the control level at 12 h post‐treatment (Figure 2B). These results indicated that zinc is involved in the DDR of bleomycin‐treated BEAS‐2B cells. Moreover, cells recovered in a zinc‐depleted environment exhibited lower DNA repair capability.

Expression Level of Zinc Transporters Is Altered After Bleomycin Treatment

The above results suggested that the cellular zinc level is elevated after bleomycin treatment, and the depletion of the extracellular pool of zinc affects the DNA repair capability of the BEAS‐2B cells. Moreover, though bleomycin treatment led to more severe DNA damage in the presence of a low concentration of zinc (15 μM) at an earlier time point (3 h after treatment), the recovery capacity of bleomycin‐treated cells was similar to that of cells grown in higher concentrations of zinc. These findings suggest that cells adjust their zinc levels in response to the DNA damage caused by bleomycin. Therefore, the expression level of zinc uptake transporters that were reported to be localized on the cell surface [24], which are involved in the absorption of zinc from their surrounding environments [25, 26], was analyzed. Among the 11 ZIPs analyzed, ZIP2, ZIP5, and ZIP12 are beyond the detection level or could not be quantified confidently. ZIP1 and ZIP14 both exhibited increased levels of expression after bleomycin treatment, while the expression of ZIP4 and ZIP9 was reduced. Among all the tested ZIPs, ZIP1 showed the most prompt response, displaying significant induction at 3 h after the treatment. Furthermore, the upregulation of ZIP1 sustained till 6 h after the treatment (Figure 3A). Protein expression analysis also revealed that ZIP1 protein significantly increased at 4 h after bleomycin treatment and reduced back to control level at 14 h post‐treatment (Figure 3B). As ZIP1 is the uptake zinc transporter that exhibited the most robust response, we focused our investigation on this transporter in the following studies.

Moreover, to see whether the bleomycin induction of ZIP1 is specific to BEAS‐2B cells, we treated the normal human liver cell line HL7702, the human embryonic kidney cell line HEK293, and the normal human colon mucosal epithelial cell line NCM460 with bleomycin. It was found that ZIP1 was only transiently induced at 6 h post‐treatment in HEK293 cells, while it was promptly and substantially up‐regulated in HL7702 cells. On the other hand, ZIP1 expression in NCM460 cells was suppressed (Figure 3C). These results demonstrated the different responding profiles in different cell lines to bleomycin.

ZIP1 Is Involved in Affecting the DNA Damage Repair Capability of BEAS‐2B Cells

To further verify that ZIP1 is involved in DDR after bleomycin treatment, knockdown cell lines of ZIP1 were generated (Figure 4A). As shown in Figure 4B,C, knockdown cells exhibited a similar foci number to that of the scramble control in unstressed conditions (DMSO‐treatment) at all the tested time points. When DNA damage was induced by bleomycin, the knockdown cells showed a comparable number of γ‐H2AX foci in both scramble and knockdown cells 3 h after the treatment. However, the foci number decreased at a greater rate in scramble cells, reaching a level that is around 180% of the DMSO control at 12 h post‐treatment, while in knockdown cells, the foci number is around 233% of that of the DMSO‐treated control (Figure 4B,C), suggesting that the reduction of ZIP1 interfered with the DDR process of the bleomycin‐treated cells. Furthermore, it was found that in ZIP1 knockdown cells, cellular zinc contents only showed marginal change after bleomycin treatment, while zinc content significantly surged at 3 and 6 h after bleomycin treatment in scramble control cells (Figure 4D).

TNFα‐NF‐κB Pathway Is Involved in Bleomycin Induction of ZIP1

Tumor necrosis factor α (TNFα) is a proinflammatory cytokine and has been demonstrated to be involved in bleomycin‐induced inflammatory responses [27, 28]. To see whether TNFα is involved in the ZIP1 induction by bleomycin, we first measured the TNFα level after bleomycin treatment. As shown in Figure 6A, the level of TNFα is induced at 1 h post‐treatment. Such an induction was sustained till 6 h and reduced to the control level at 12 h after the treatment. TNFα knockdown cell lines were then generated (Figure 6B), and the induction of ZIP1 by bleomycin was investigated. It was found that the upregulation of ZIP1 after bleomycin treatment was attenuated at both the mRNA (Figure 6C) and protein (Figure 6D) levels. These results suggested that TNFα may serve as a mediator for the induction of ZIP1 by bleomycin. The transcription factor NF‐κB is known to be induced by TNFα [29], and is considered a major regulatory factor responsible for transporter expression under inflammatory conditions [30]. It was found that higher level of NF‐κB was accumulated in the nuclei upon bleomycin treatment (Figure 7A). Further, suppression of NF‐κB with BAY11‐7082 (BAY), a selective and potent inhibitor that inhibits the TNFα‐NF‐κB activation pathway, significantly suppressed the response of ZIP1 to bleomycin (Figure 7B). Analysis of the upstream sequence of SLC39A1, the coding gene of ZIP1, revealed three possible NF‐κB binding sites located at −2705 to −2690 bp, −1477 to −1468 bp, and –950 to −941 bp (Figure 7C). By constructing the luciferase reporter vectors that contain different fragments of the SLC39A1 upstream sequence, it was found that fragments with these three binding sites all showed significantly higher luciferase activity after bleomycin treatment (Figure 7D). However, when the highly conserved nucleotides were mutated at different putative binding sites, only the mutations at site 1 (−2705 to −2690 bp) and site 3 (−950 to −941 bp) abrogated the response of the fragments to bleomycin treatment, while mutations at site 2 (−1477 to −1468 bp) showed no effect on the response (Figure 7E). ChIP analysis further confirmed that these two sites interact with the upstream sequence of SLC39A1, and bleomycin treatment significantly enhanced the formation of the NF‐κB‐DNA complexes (Figure 7F).

Discussion

Zinc plays a vital role in the DNA damage repair process by serving as a cofactor for DDR proteins, activating relevant signaling pathways, and acting as an antioxidant [31]. Zinc transporters are essential for maintaining the homeostasis of intracellular zinc levels, and their dysregulation has been linked to various diseases, including cancers [32], cardiovascular diseases [33], and neurodegenerative disorders [34]. In this study, we utilized the DNA damage agent bleomycin to investigate the role of cell membrane zinc transporters in response to DNA damage. Our findings revealed that ZIP1 showed a rapid and significant upregulation following bleomycin treatment. When ZIP1 was knocked down, the cells exhibited a substantially reduced capability to respond to DNA damage. Further analysis indicated that the TNFα‐NF‐κB pathway is responsible for the induction of ZIP1.

Consistent with previous reports [21], treatment with bleomycin resulted in DNA damage, as indicated by the increasing formation of γ‐H2AX foci within the nuclei. However, the number of γ‐H2AX foci gradually decreased over time in normal, zinc‐containing medium. Instead of using the cell‐permeable zinc chelator N, N, N, N‐Tetrakis (2‐pyridylmethyl)‐ethylenedi‐amine (TPEN), which directly reduces intracellular zinc levels, we employed a zinc‐deficient medium during the cell recovery process. This approach allowed us to assess whether, in response to the stress induced by bleomycin, cells need to increase their intracellular zinc levels from an external zinc source. Although zinc itself does not directly participate in DNA repair, it is an essential structural cofactor and signaling molecule required for maintaining genomic stability, participating in virtually every stage of DDR. DNA damage sensor Poly ADP‐ribose polymerase 1 (PPAR1) possesses three zinc finger domains that cooperate to recognize DNA strand breaks [35]. DDR proteins that are involved in base excision repair and nucleotide excision repair need zinc for their proper functions [36]. The DNA‐binding domain of p53 contains one structural zinc atom; hence, zinc is essential for the DNA binding and transcriptional activation of p53 [37]. Additionally, zinc can reduce reactive oxidative stress, which is a significant factor in the DNA damage process [38]. Our findings showed that depleting zinc from the growth medium indeed diminished the DDR capacity of the cells, suggesting that the cells require additional zinc from the exogenous environment during the DDR process.

Further investigation revealed that bleomycin altered the expression of several ZIPs located at the cell membrane, which are recognized as key determinants for cells to absorb zinc from their extracellular environment. Among these transporters, ZIP1 showed the most prompt and significant upregulation, with a 40% increase at the 3‐h time point and around 80% increase at the 6‐h time point. ZIP14 also exhibited an increase post‐treatment, but this occurred at the later 6‐h time point and promptly returned to the control baseline level. ZIP1 is a highly expressed zinc transporter found in various types of cells. It is downregulated in prostate cancer, contributing to the reduced zinc accumulation in malignant prostate cells, and is believed to be a critical early event in the development of prostate cancer [39]. ZIP1 has also been identified to be responsible for zinc uptake in astrocytes and microglial cells [34], human lung alveolar macrophages [40], and heart muscle tissues [41]. The relatively rapid response of this transporter suggests that cells are trying to increase their cellular zinc levels to support the DDR. Indeed, it was shown that knocking down or overexpressing ZIP1 alters the dynamics of the DDR in bleomycin‐treated cells. Interestingly, the knockdown of ZIP1 did not change the DNA damage profile in DMSO‐treated control cells at any of the tested time points, differing from results observed in cells grown in zinc‐depleted medium. This suggests that other ZIPs may play a role in maintaining zinc homeostasis and DNA integrity under normal, unstressed conditions. For example, ZIP8 has been shown to contribute to zinc uptake in lung epithelial cells [42]. However, when cells were challenged with bleomycin, the foci number reduction rate was significantly decreased in ZIP1 knockdown cells compared to the scramble control, particularly during the 6 to 12‐h period. This indicates that ZIP1 is essential for the DDR. Conversely, when ZIP1 was overexpressed, cells showed a better capacity to repair DNA damage caused by bleomycin even at the earlier 3‐h time point. This suggests that the higher zinc content within cells, resulting from the increased level of ZIP1, helps them better cope with bleomycin‐induced DNA damage. The direct measurement of zinc within knockdown and overexpression cells with the fluorescent zinc indicator zinquin ethyl ester further confirmed the change of zinc content upon bleomycin treatment.

Among the 14 ZIPs in the human body, ZIP8 is generally recognized as the transporter responsible for zinc uptake in the lungs and responds to stressful stimuli. Research has shown that ZIP8 contributes to the absorption of zinc by lung epithelial cells and has protective effects on epithelia from various human donors and BEAS‐2B cell cultures during inflammation induced by TNFα [42]. A more recent study indicated that an imbalance in zinc levels due to altered ZIP8 expression can lead to increased and irreversible tissue damage in response to prolonged exposure to tobacco smoke [43]. In contrast, our current study demonstrated that ZIP8 showed no significant changes at any time point following bleomycin treatment. One possible explanation for this inconsistency may be the different stimuli used in our research. While previous studies employed the inflammation‐inducing cytokine TNFα, our study utilized bleomycin, a DNA‐damaging agent that directly integrates into the double helix and breaks the DNA molecule at specific sites [44]. It is noteworthy that although the responding ZIP differs, the signaling pathway regulating their expression remains the same. Previous studies have shown that ZIP8 is a transcription target of NF‐κB in mouse RAW 267.4 cells. ZIP8 is induced by TNFα, and NF‐κB directly binds to the upstream regulatory region of SLC39A8 [45]. The TNFα‐NF‐κB pathway is a canonical pathway in cells responding to various stresses, including DNA damage [46], oxidative stress [47], and inflammation [48, 49]. In our current study, TNFα level showed an increase as early as 1 h after bleomycin treatment, and knockdown experiments confirmed the involvement of this cytokine in the regulation of ZIP1. Zinc has been shown to influence cellular inflammation responses and can negatively regulate the production of TNFα [48, 50]. The TNFα‐NF‐κB pathway was shown to be responsive to fluctuations in cellular zinc levels [51]. Therefore, it is tempting to speculate that when cells incorporate labile zinc into DDR proteins to address the damage caused by bleomycin, a reduction in intracellular zinc levels may activate this pathway, leading to the upregulation of ZIP1 and ultimately increasing the zinc concentration within the cell. The mechanisms responsible for the induction of ZIP1 rather than ZIP8 in the context of direct DNA damage warrant further investigation.

In the current study, we also found that, with the same bleomycin treatment, the response of ZIP1 differed across cell lines. HEK293 only showed a transient upregulation of ZIP1 at the 6‐h post‐treatment time point, while HL7702 sustained a dramatic induction of ZIP1 through all the tested time points. On the other hand, NCM460 mainly exhibited a downward trend in ZIP1 expression. Research has shown that specific ZIPs are integral to regulating zinc homeostasis across different body systems [52]. Dysregulation of these transporters can lead to pathological conditions, including inflammation, various types of cancer, and neurodegenerative diseases [53]. Previous reports have indicated that different ZIPs respond uniquely to various stimuli. For instance, both ZIP8 and ZIP14 exhibited strong reactions to metabolic or oxidative stress conditions, such as radiation and inflammation, and were found to be induced by inflammatory cytokines IL‐6 and TNFα [45, 54]. ZIP9 was shown to be upregulated by X‐ray exposure through TGF‐β/Smad activation, which increases zinc accumulation in skin fibroblasts [55]. A recent study conducted in our laboratory demonstrated that after carbon ions treatment, ZIP14 induced by the PKC‐Nrf2 pathway was involved in the DNA damage response [15]. However, the question of whether different cell types respond differently to the same stimuli, which result in the induction of different ZIPs, remains largely unexplored. Our studies suggest that the responses of ZIPs to DNA damage may be cell‐type specific. This variability could be due to the distinct upstream regulatory sequences of individual ZIPs and the specific signal transduction pathways that lead to the induction. Further systematic studies using high‐throughput proteomic techniques will be needed to comprehensively dissect the underlying mechanisms by which cells perceive various stimuli in a cell‐type‐specific manner and lead to the precise activation of ZIPs, which enable them to promptly and efficiently respond to DNA damage.

In summary, ZIP1 is involved in the DDR induced by bleomycin. Treatment with bleomycin leads to the induction of TNFα, which increases the nuclear accumulation of NF‐κB in stressed cells. NF‐κB binds to the upstream sequence of the ZIP1 coding gene SLC39A1 and enhances the expression of the zinc transporter. This alteration, in turn, modifies the DDR profile of the cells.
