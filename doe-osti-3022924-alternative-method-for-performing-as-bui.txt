 ABSTRACT

Neutronics analyses have been used to assist in the design, development, and qualification of miniature
fuel (MiniFuel) experiments irradiated in the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) at Oak Ridge National
Laboratory (ORNL) since their conception almost a decade ago. In recent years, upon completion of a
MiniFuel target’s irradiation, neutronics analyses that reflect the as-built, as-irradiated conditions of an
experiment have also been performed to validate postirradiation examination (PIE) efforts. Several
radiation transport and depletion/activation software wrapper programs have been developed to increase
the fidelity of these calculations; at ORNL, a program called HFIRCON is the primary software method
employed. However, the utilization of these wrapper programs comes at a cost. To produce accurate
results, the radiation transport portion of these analyses requires significant time and resources to execute.
Therefore, this report discusses an alternative methodology for calculating some of the desired output for
PIE validation—specifically, the output used in evaluating the experiment’s fuel burnup—without
complete reliance on ORNL’s wrapper program. The alternative method is not a new software but rather a
procedure for utilizing the depletion/activation software, SCALE, in conjunction with limited input data
and reasonable assumptions to bypass most of the radiation transport calculations and thereby reduce the
time required for the completion of a standard analysis.

 1. BACKGROUND

1.1 HFIR AND THE MINIFUEL VEHICLE

The High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) is a pressurized, light-water-cooled and -moderated reactor of the
flux trap type design [1]. Its core contains highly enriched 235U fuel clad in aluminum in the form of two
concentric annuli of involute fuel plates encircled by control elements (CEs) and a large beryllium
reflector subdivided into removable, semipermanent, and permanent regions. Along with the removable
beryllium experiment facilities (RB*), the permanent beryllium reflector also contains multiple positions
for irradiation experiments—namely, the vertical experiment facility (VXF) positions. Miniature fuel
(MiniFuel) experiments are currently capable of being inserted within the RB* and the inner small
vertical experiment facility (ISVXF) positions. Figure 1 shows a cross section of HFIR that highlights
these positions.

 Figure 1. Top-down cross section of HFIR’s core layout [2].

 1

The MiniFuel irradiation vehicle, developed at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL), was designed to
perform separate effects irradiation testing of nuclear fuels where fuel performance variables and fuel
phenomena of interest are isolated [3]. Each MiniFuel target holds a stack of six subcapsules separated by
centering thimbles. Stainless-steel springs at each end of the stack secure the subcapsule stack within the
stainless-steel target housing, which is composed of an outer tube and two end caps. The standard
MiniFuel subcapsule features a molybdenum holder of variable outer diameter, a molybdenum end cap,
Grafoil spacers, a SiC thermometry rod, a molybdenum tube, a fuel dish, and a fuel specimen [3, 4]. Each
ISVXF MiniFuel basket can hold a total of 9 experiment targets, enabling a total of 54 MiniFuel
subcapsules to be irradiated simultaneously. Each RB* MiniFuel basket can hold a total of 15 experiment
targets, enabling a total of 90 MiniFuel subcapsules to be irradiated simultaneously. The Radial-Axial-
Subassembly (RAS) nomenclature system, developed by members of ORNL’s Nuclear Energy and Fuel
Cycle Division, is used to identify the position of the subcapsules. The notation increases clockwise for
the radial positions and upward for axial and subassembly positions. The first position for the radial
notation (position 1) is the back position for VXF targets (farthest from the core) and the front left for
RB* targets. The first position for the axial and subassembly notations corresponds to the bottom
positions of the targets and subcapsules, respectively. Figure 2 shows both the ISVXF and RB* basket
assemblies as well as the various RAS positions.

 Figure 2. Experiment positions for the RB* and ISVXF baskets [4] [3].

Each MiniFuel experiment has a set of target parameters that the experiment is designed to achieve. Upon
completion of the experiment irradiation, the targets are transferred to hot cells on site at ORNL for
postirradiation examination (PIE). Prior to any examinations, the MiniFuel target housing and the
individual subcapsules are cut open in-cell using a low-speed saw to recover the fuel disks and the SiC
thermometry pieces. The SiC thermometry is typically shipped to ORNL’s Low Activation Materials
Development and Analysis laboratory to be analyzed via dilatometry and to confirm the postirradiation
experiment irradiation temperatures. The fuel disks typically receive laser flash analysis and profilometry
measurements to collect thermal diffusivity and swelling data, respectively. Additionally, gamma and
mass spectrometry are performed on the fuel disks to determine the burnup achieved during the
irradiation.

1.2 TYPES OF NEUTRONIC ANALYSES

 1) Design-Basis (Scoping) Analyses
 Design-basis (or scoping) neutronics calculations are typically performed during the development
 of each experiment to assist in the experiment design and irradiation plan. In these analyses,

 2

 nominal values for the target geometry and material definitions and HFIR irradiation conditions
 are assumed. Typically, multiple analyses are performed in conjunction to investigate several
 candidate fuel materials. Each model for a specific candidate fuel material also typically assumes
 that all possible target locations of a desired irradiation facility are filled with MiniFuel targets
 containing that material. This allows analysts to map the viable design space of the selected
 facility for that fuel material.
 These analyses require the lowest level of rigor. They do not require the use of software quality
 assurance (SQA) software and hardware or official reviews for technical accuracy. In addition,
 because nominal values can be assumed for most parameters, the development and execution of
 these models is relatively uncomplicated.

2) Safety-Basis Analyses
 Safety-basis neutronics calculations are performed to ensure compliance with HFIR’s experiment
 safety requirements. In these analyses, only the most conservative assumptions are used and
 typically go beyond expected design parameters to create a bounding case for experiments. In
 MiniFuel experiments safety-basis calculations, it is assumed that one radial target position is
 oriented as close to the core as possible, rather than having two radial targets equidistant from the
 core. The axial positions of the targets are also slightly shifted upward to orient the fuel cells of
 one of the subcapsules to align with the midplane of the reactor. In addition, the experiment fuel’s
 mass is maximized, and enrichment is set to a conservatively high, bounding value. The upper
 range of cycle length is also assumed, and the total experiment irradiation time is typically
 simulated until the total target heat generation rate, which is primarily driven by the target fuel’s
 fission rate, reaches steady state.
 These analyses require the highest level of rigor. All software utilized in the analyses must
 undergo ORNL’s SQA validation, and each calculation must undergo both technical and
 independent reviews and receive acceptance from ORNL’s Research Reactors Division prior to
 approval.

3) As-Built, As-Irradiated Analyses
 As-built, as-irradiated neutronics calculations are performed primarily to support PIE efforts.
 These models of these analyses are generally similar to any design-basis neutronics calculations
 performed for the experiment, but they differ in the finer details. Many alterations to an
 experiment design can occur in the course of fabrication, loading, and irradiation. Component
 geometry/masses also commonly do not align with nominal values, and subcapsule and target
 loading positions can change last minute for design optimization purposes or simply to ensure
 insertion of the experiment in a given cycle (due to competition for space in HFIR). HFIR’s cycle
 length can also range from about 23 to 26 days. Cycle length is primarily dependent on
 experiment loading, particularly in RB* positions. When not loaded with experiments, the RB*
 positions are typically loaded with Be plugs because Be is a low absorbing material and a great
 reflector. Thus, longer cycle lengths and greater fluences are achieved with Be plugs, which are
 beneficial to HFIR’s many missions. Unanticipated shutdowns also occasionally occur, which can
 be prompted by a variety of causes.

 These analyses also require a very high level of rigor. Although they do not require the use of
 SQA approved software/hardware, the technical intracacy required for the development and
 execution of these simulations is much more comprehensive than both design- and safety-basis
 analyses. With as-built, as-irradiated simulations, a user’s goal is to design the experiment model
 and simulation (component masses, material definitions, geometry, and irradiation conditions) to
 be as close as possible to the environment that the experiment experienced. Therefore, as few

 3

 assumptions as possible are made for these analyses, typically resulting in very complex models
 and simulation plans.

1.3 CURRENT METHODS

All as-built, as-irradiated neutronics calculations performed at ORNL to date have utilized HFIRCON, an
ORNL-developed code package. HFIRCON was initially developed for 238Pu production targets but was
designed to be generic enough to be used for fuel and target analyses as well. The package uses Python
scripts and C plug-ins to integrate the data transfer between several programs—namely, MCNP transport
simulations and the SCALE ORIGEN depletion and decay calculations. HFIRCON is an attractive choice
for these types of calculations because it automates the workflow for high-fidelity, multicycle fuel and
target depletion and target heat deposition analysis in HFIR [5]. The requirements to run a HFIRCON
calculation are just a slightly modified MCNP input deck and “controller file” in which experimental
input—such as target cell designations, desired depletion time steps, CE positions, and shutdown times—
can be defined. An illustration of the flowchart for HFIRCON is provided in Appendix A-1.

 2. ALTERNATIVE METHOD

2.1 MOTIVATION

Due to the inherent nature of as-built, as-irradiated neutronics calculations, they can only be performed
during or after the actual irradiation of the experiment, but they must also be performed in a timely
manner in order to support PIE activities. However, as-built, as-irradiated calculations have historically
taken significant amounts of effort and time to complete. For a typical HFIRCON MiniFuel simulation, to
accurately capture the flux variation due to fuel burnup and CE movement, each cycle requires eight or
more time steps to be defined. Users define these time steps as times within the cycle that the radiation
transport simulations are executed, and, optionally, ADVANTG variance reduction calculations are
performed. Users can define the time steps at which ADVANTG is executed and the number of particles
simulated at each time step to obtain statistically reasonable results. Because the MiniFuel fueled regions
are very, very small volume targets, ADVANTG is typically performed for the a minumum of three time
steps throughout a cycle, and a minimum of 3.0 × 106 particles are executed per time step to obtain
statistically reasonable results. Accounting for possible time in the queue of the clusters used for these
simulations, a single cycle of as-built, as-irradiated simulation can take up to 2 to 3 days to complete.
Because MiniFuel experiments can also be irradiated for as many as 17 cycles, an entire experiment as-
built, as-irradiated simulation can take several weeks or months. This extended analysis time is
exacerbated by regular unavailability of the clusters on which these calculations are performed due to
either cluster maintenance or user resource competition.

Though HFIRCON is considered to be the gold standard for this type of calculation, an alternative
approach is required to expedite calculations. The focus of this report is the development of a method for
calculating the fuel’s burnup and isotopic inventories of interest (U, Pu, Kr, and other fission products)
with minimal to no reliance on HFIRCON. The validation of this alternative approach to the HFIRCON
results will take place to qualify the method.

2.3 PROCESS DESCRIPTION

An illustration of the alternative method flowchart is provided in Appendix A-2. Because this analysis
methodology was developed as an alternative to a coupled radiation transport and depletion/decay
software, the start of this process is very similar. The first “step” is to acquire experiment details (e.g., the
fuel’s mass and material composition) and define the irradiation conditions. This latter part can be
considered a substep of its own, as an analyst has several options for sourcing flux and possibly cross
section data to be used.

These details are then input into an ORIGEN depletion/decay input file. Specifically, a single ORIGEN
input file is created for each fuel cell of interest, which simulates the entire irradiation of the experiment
when executed.

2.3.1 ORIGEN Depletion/Decay Required Input

At the most fundamental level, an ORIGEN calculation is a 0D explicit analysis of a material’s
transmutation and decay in a given irradiation environment. The basic user input data required for an
ORIGEN calculation are detailed in Table 1.

Within a depletion/decay ORIGEN input deck, the user must first define nuclear data libraries of the
problem, including the neutron and decay interaction data. This can be done by calling standard libraries
built into ORIGEN and/or by manually updating certain parameters. For these calculations, the only
parameters that are not sourced from standard ORIGEN libraries are the neutron flux spectra and
(optionally) the neutron cross section data.

A user can then define each change in the irradiation environment as a unique case. These cases are either
periods of irradiation or periods of decay that reflect the cycle length and shutdown times of an
experiment. The initial material is typically defined in the first case as mass by isotope, and in all
subsequent cases, the material definition is automatically carried over from the previous case’s
transmutated/decayed isotopics. In these analyses, the target material (the fuel specimens) is defined from
experimental fabrication documentation. Each case also allows the input of discretized time steps, total
neutron flux values corresponding to each time step, and output printing controls.

2.3.2 Fidelity Improvement Methods

The first limiting factor of the accuracy of these calculations is the fidelity of the neutron flux conditions
defined. It is possible to utilize preexisting data with this method, but they will have varying levels of
inaccuracies in relation to a current experiment’s as-irradiated conditions. For example, many legacy
HFIR flux mapping calculations have investigated the VXF and RB* target positions; however, most of
these calculations were performed prior to the availability of coupled radiation transport and
depletion/decay software. This means that analysts generally only simulated beginning of cycle (BOC),
end of cycle (EOC), and possibly middle of cycle (MOC). Although these data can technically be utilized
with the simulation method described in this report, they do not accurately capture the nonlinear flux
variation throughout a cycle. These data could possibly be suitable for quick scoping calculations but
would fall well short of the accuracy needed of modern as-built, as-irradiated simulations.

Alternatively, many of the more recent neutronics calculations have utilized coupled radiation transport
and depletion/decay software, such as HFIRCON. These calculations have been primarily performed for
HFIR fuel depletion studies and other MiniFuel analyses, and flux data produced by these calculations
would be preferrable to any legacy calculations due to the improved discretization of time steps analyzed.
Unfortunately, a common pitfall of these calculations is the scope of the analysis performed, as the cells
of relevant RB and VXF positions for a given experiment may not be tallied, and if they were, the flux
data would still contain some level of inaccuracy. Because depletion studies focus primarily on the reactor
fuel, they would only have “representative” material modeled within the possible MiniFuel locations,
such as beryllium plugs for RB* locations and water for VXF (particularly the ISVXF) locations. If
tallied, the flux would only be the average across the volume of the facility’s liner and/or dummy/plug
material, which would typically require scaling by HFIR’s axial neutron profile to produce flux values for
the specific axial positions needed. The flux across these dummy/plug materials would also not consider
any self-shielding effects of a given MiniFuel experiment’s design. The best-case scenario would be a
calculation of a prior MiniFuel experiment that was also simulated in the target position of interest, but
because all MiniFuel experiments are unique, some inaccuracy will be present due to the variation in
geometry and material present in each experiment.

Of course, the optimal means for acquiring accurate flux values is to develop and run a coupled radiation
transport and depletion/decay simulation of each as-built, as-irradiated experiment; however, running an
entire experiment’s irradiation would obviously defeat the purpose of this methodology. Therefore, efforts
have been made to extrapolate flux data from partial analyses. More specifically, an analyst can run a
single cycle HFIRCON simulation of the representative experiment and apply the temporally dependent

2.3.3 Process Improvement Discussion

As the level of rigor for the input increases, as described above in Section 2.3.2, the need for automation
of data extraction from the various sources, manipulation of the data, and formatting of the input files
becomes increasingly important. Automation via processing scripts/programs reduces the likelihood of
human error and expedites the process.

First, a custom Python script was developed for the extraction and data manipulation of flux and cross
section data output by HFIRCON radiation transport calculations. This script extracts flux spectra and all
cross section values for all target cells specified in the HFIRCON controller file for all time steps
available in the calculation directory. The script then removes all data except for user-specified
component cells (i.e., target fuel cells) and generates dataframes of total flux, time-weighted flux spectra,
and time- and flux-weighted fission and capture cross sections for the specified cells. These dataframes
are then output into individual worksheets in an Excel file. This script also currently outputs all raw flux
spectra and cross section data for the specified cell for manual verification of the calculations performed
by the script if desired.

The second primary automation tool utilized was the HFIR Activity Workbook (HAWK) generator, a
wrapper program of a set of Python scripts that automates the generation and execution of ORIGEN input
files. The experiment-specific input for these calculations is controlled by an Excel spreadsheet and
accompanying JSON file containing the necessary experiment details, irradiation conditions, and material
data input by the user. However, HAWK has several shortcomings for the application with the alternative
method described. It was originally developed to perform activity calculations of nonfissioning materials
(e.g., structural material) irradiated within HFIR’s flux trap to support target shipping from HFIR. Thus, it
was developed based on the assumptions that flux and cross sections do not vary much over a cycle and
that infinite dilute cross sections are applicable to structural materials. Therefore, HAWK is formatted to
allow only BOC and EOC total flux values to be input, from which it linearly interpolates for intermediate
timesteps throughout the irradiation period. The card that allows user-specified cross section values
within the neutron specification of the flux library is also absent from HAWK-generated ORIGEN files.
Therefore, HAWK has been used as a “starting point” to rapidly format the cases (cycle lengths/shutdown
times) and initial material definitions of the target fuel of the ORIGEN files, which were then further
modified to include target-specific cross section and irradiation history details (i.e., the time steps and
time-dependent flux values) from the as-built transport calculations.

 8

Lastly, another custom Python script was developed to extract data from the ORIGEN output files. The
basic function of this script is to extract the molar number of all actinide isotopes output for all irradiation
steps simulated, convert these values to number of atoms, and output these data into an Excel file. This
script is currently set executed within a parent directory containing folders that contain ORIGEN output
files. This was done to facilitate the processing of all ORIGEN output files associated with the analysis
performed in Section 2.4 simultaneously. The calculation of time-dependent burnup as well as the
collection of end-of-irradiation (EOI) inventories of the specific isotopes utilized in PIE efforts (detailed
in Section 2) are manually performed within Excel; however, the incorporation of these processes into the
script’s function is planned.

 3. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

This report details the status of the development of an alternative methodology for performing fuel
burnup calculations. The intended end use of this methodology is utilization with as-built, as-irradiated
MiniFuel experiment analyses. The current standard for simulation of as-built, as-irradiated MiniFuel
experiments at ORNL is through the utilization of the coupled radiation transport and depletion/decay
software, HFIRCON. Performing HFIRCON simulations is very resource intensive, however, particularly
regarding the time requirements, and the primary objective of the alternative methodology is to provide
comparable burnup estimations at an expedited rate. As such, methods for improving both the fidelity of
results and time requirements to complete the alternative methodology are discussed. Finally, several
cases of alternative method simulations with varying degrees of fidelity in their input were performed for
fuel targets of an example MiniFuel experiment. These were benchmarked against burnup values
calculated in an existing HFIRCON simulation of the example MiniFuel experiment’s irradiation.

The results and findings of this comparison show that the methodology utilized is conceptually sound and
produces the desired output for PIE efforts. However, several possible pathways for improvement in the
methodology process have been identified. First, because the primary motivation for this method’s
development was to decrease the time needed to perform this type of analysis, the most obvious means for
achieving this would be improving the automation of the process. This would include standardizing,
consolidating, and/or building upon the scripts for generating, executing, and postprocessing of the
ORIGEN simulations. One proposal for achieving this is to create a modified version of the HAWK tool.
The HAWK tool already automatically generates and executes ORIGEN depletion/decay calculations, but
several alterations are needed to perform MiniFuel analyses like those in this report. Firstly, during the
ORIGEN input file generation step of the tool, the capability of inputting user-specified time steps and
flux values must be added, rather than only BOC and EOC flux and times (between which ORIGEN
linearly interpolates values). Secondly, the postprocessing portion of the tool needs to be reworked to
output burnup, rather than isotopic activities. Integration with postprocessing script utilized in this report
would complete half of this task, extracting the actinide inventories (actinide isotopes’ number of atoms)
at the specified time steps, but additional steps need to be added to the script to automate the target’s
burnup calculation from those actinide inventories. Of course, none of this would eliminate possibly the
most significant contribution to both uncertainty and the performance time required, which is the
radiation transport calculation itself. Production of time-dependent and positionally dependent flux and
cross section databases for common fuel forms would altogether remove the need to find an applicable
preexisting neutronics analysis or run a representative radiation transport calculation from which the
needed data could be extracted.

Lastly, alternatives to the general setup and execution process for the ORIGEN files could be
investigated. It may prove to be beneficial to break each specimen’s simulation into individual
irradiation/shutdown periods. A similar methodology was applied in Chandler et al. [9], and it allows
cycle-specific cross section libraries to be generated for each irradiation period rather than applying a
calculated average across the irradiation. This would more accurately account for cross section evolution
for extended irradiation periods.

Ultimately, coupled radiation transport and depletion/decay calculations will likely remain the ideal and
the most accurate means for simulating as-built, as-irradiated experiments if the resources are available
and time is not an issue. However, if the level of uncertainty presented in this report is deemed
acceptable, this alternative methodology provides options for researchers. Although it was initially
conceived for as-built, as-irradiated simulations, it has very promising applications in the experiment
design space, where the need for such high-fidelity results is generally not required. The reduction in time

 12

and resources required inherently makes this method a preferable option, and the accuracy of the results
produced will only improve as this method is utilized, investigated, and updated.
