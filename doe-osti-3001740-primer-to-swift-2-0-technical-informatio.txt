 1. CURRENT STATE OF U.S. WASTEWATER INDUSTRY

More than 16,000 publicly owned municipal wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) in the U.S. treat a
wide range of wastewater (WW) flows ranging from <0.5 to >200 MGD.[1] Municipal WWTPs act as the
last barrier before exposure to water-borne diseases and contaminants, thus serving as a critical
infrastructure protecting human health and the environment. A recent survey [2] by the American Society
of Civil Engineers assigned the U.S.’s WW infrastructure a grade of D+ because of the number of
improvements needed for end-of-life infrastructure with operating conditions being over or under design
capacities. With increasing population growth and urbanization, the municipal WW sector needs to grow
resiliently and sustainably to accommodate increasing sewer flows in the wake of new infrastructure
needs and global priorities. In addition, this growth will be affected by rising concerns over constrained
resources (e.g., electricity, energy, and water) and regulations. This necessitates molding the WW sector
toward sustainability- and resiliency-related planning and innovations to address current and future needs
while simultaneously producing profitable by-products.

Based on a survey of over 300 stakeholders, the wastewater industry is making significant shifts toward
sorting resource vulnerability, planning for adaptation and mitigation, setting goals, and developing
metrics on environmental, social, and governance goals around system resiliency.[3] Along with
traditional water-loss mitigation and conservation strategies, wastewater utilities are looking for energy-
efficient strategies and promoting electrification and renewable energy integration. This is primarily
because wastewater operations are highly energy- and electricity-intensive and may sometimes be the
largest energy consumer (30%–40%) in a nonindustrial community. Municipal wastewater treatment
plants consume 0.8% of U.S. total electricity demand [4] and are one of the most electricity-intensive
sectors at a municipality scale.[5] In 2022, this sector required 32.4 TWh of electricity use, resulting in
$5.2 billion in annual electric costs to treat over 34 billion gal/d [6] of WW. Energy needs are expected to
grow with rising population and industrialization and with stringent water quality requirements, leading to
increased demand for WW treatment. WW production is expected to rise by 1.5 times, and the
water/wastewater (W/WW) treatment market size will be valued at $179.22 billion by 2030.[7][8] These
additions will most certainly increase energy demands for conveyance, treatment, distribution, and
disposal. Therefore, energy recovery and reductions through reclaiming wasted energy or harnessing
energy resources can further reduce energy consumption and demand in the wastewater sector. The
various energy capture strategies can then generate significant economic savings and environmental
benefits wherever applicable.

 2. U.S. DOE’S SWIFTER INITIATIVE

Wastewater treatment facilities provide tremendous opportunities for energy recovery. The Sustainable
Wastewater of the Future1 Initiative was launched by the U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE’s) initiative
to work with water resource recovery facilities to accelerate a pathway toward sustainable infrastructure.
DOE aims to assist the sector with information on energy and related data management, energy efficiency
improvements, advanced technology integration, and project financing for wastewater utilities. The
wastewater industry is evolving on many fronts to achieve greater resiliency in a water-, climate-, and
resource-constrained future. Toward this effort, DOE characterized four technology pillars—energy
capture, energy efficiency, resource recovery, and advanced data management (Figure 1) to provide
resources for the wastewater sector.

Through DOE’s SWIFt program, the agency hosted numerous workshops and webinars and developed
toolkits to help the wastewater industry improve energy efficiency. These free resources, available
through the Wastewater Energy Improvements [9] platform, provide utilities with practical guidance and
tools for implementing sustainable energy solutions. The history of SWIFt is shown in Figure 2,
concluding with the Sustainable Wastewater Infrastructure Accelerator 2.0 (SWIFter) workshop series.
The SWIFt program was designed to advance the WW sector by promoting the adoption of best practices
and emerging technologies while also addressing operational challenges and industry concerns through
collaborative discussions. WW utility participants received technical assistance to help achieve energy
efficiency while fostering community building through shared learning and collaboration.

Additionally, active participants received continuing education units, recognizing their contributions and
engagement in the program. The workshop series focused on four technology tracks (as noted in Figure 1)
and engaged over 25 representatives from small (<5 MGD; 46%), medium (5–100 MGD, 46%), and large
(>100 MGD, 8%) WW utilities. During the workshop discussions (~12 sessions, ~2 hours each), a
sectoral need assessment was conducted to elaborate on the status, challenges, barriers, and opportunities
for improved WW infrastructure. Responses from a diverse range of wastewater utilities, while not
exhaustive, provide valuable insights into industry trends and needs. Drawing on these insights, this
document serves as a “Primer to U.S. Wastewater Industry Needs,” summarizing key themes and
opportunities where DOE can best support the sector. The four follow-up individual technical information
sheets are aimed to address these gaps by providing a resource pool for the WW industry and identifying
areas where further research and development will be needed.

2.1 WW INDUSTRY OUTLOOK TOWARD SUSTAINABILITY

The drivers for change in the WW industry—primarily a nonprofit industry—are very different from
those of typical manufacturing industries; the WW industry prioritizes public health, environmental
protection, and regulatory compliance over financial gains. Therefore, the narrative around
“sustainability” is strongly tied to long-term system resiliency stemming from aging infrastructure. To
gauge where the WW utilities are in the process of setting environmental goals, the sectoral assessment
asked industry representatives about their sustainability goals as well as their goals for reducing
greenhouse gases (GHGs), water use, and energy use (Figure 3). Over the past 5 years (inclusive of the
COVID-19 pandemic), the WW sector respondents noted a shift toward sustainability and resiliency.
About 55% of the respondents noted a major shift, and 33% have seen an increased inclination in
discussing energy, carbon footprints, and water efficiency in addition to regulatory compliance. Moving
in this direction, the WW sector is forecasting a more dramatic transition in incorporating sustainability
within the next 10 years (39% within 3–5 years and another 28% over the next 5–10 years). Most of the
sector (~60%) have sustainability goals under development to support transitional strategies, with up to
25% of respondents having advanced goals for GHG reduction and improving water and energy
efficiency.

 2.2 ENERGY CAPTURE PILLAR FOR SWIFT

 Capturing wasted energy to produce on-site energy reduces reliance on external sources of energy,
 thereby enhancing use of resources. “Energy Capture” avenues most used by WWTPs are (in order): (1)
 biogas capture and production via combined heat and power (CHP) systems for on-site energy or
 producing renewable natural gas (RNG); (2) on-site solar installation by using available land; and (3)
 renewable electricity procurements and power purchase agreements. Following are key industry
 observations in each of the three avenues:

 2.3 BIOGAS CAPTURE AND PRODUCTION

 • 73% of the respondents anticipate capturing methane by biogas utilization to reduce their GHG
 footprint in the future.

 • About 33% of the participants do not have anaerobic digestors (ADs), and the remainder use biogas
 primarily for heating the digesters and flaring the biogas. Only one utility uses ADs for producing
 RNG.

 • For utilities with a CHP system, the key barriers for efficient use of biogas in CHP systems are the
 following:

 Lack of biogas conditioning system

 Additional system operation maintenance demands for microturbines and engines

 Variable steam demand

 Insufficient biogas production for consistent operation and poor generator uptime

 Lack of reliability of the CHP generator

 Additional regulatory requirements with air quality permitting owing to flaring and biogas production

 Primary energy storage systems (ESSs) deployed by the WW sector are pumped hydro-storage (15%)
 and on-site batteries (38%); the remainder of utilities do not use ESSs. The sector is aware of the
 available ESS technologies but has not found any extensive uses to implement these systems.

 4

Renewable electricity procurements are known to 46% of the participants, and the remaining learned
about them for the first time through the wastewater technical assistance (WTA) sessions organized.
Because of the new terminology for the participants, the utilities’ acceptance of using renewable
electricity credits is relatively low (maximum of 3 on a scale of 0 to 5).

2.4 ENERGY EFFICIENCY PILLAR FOR SWIFT

Improving energy efficiency at WWTPs enables process efficiency, resulting in cost savings and
improved system performance through the use of energy management tools, process optimization, and
advanced technologies. The WWTPs can deploy a range of low-cost and no-cost energy efficiency
measures to advanced capital-intensive improvements. However, the research team’s interactions with the
WW cohort have revealed that only 10% have implemented energy-efficient improvements; the
remaining 90% are considering implementing such measure over the span of 3–5 years as part of their
master planning and capital improvement projects. For 50% of the WW utilities, smaller energy
efficiency projects can be implemented without requiring inclusion in a capital improvement plan.
Another 38% of facilities require all energy efficiency projects to be included in their plan, whereas the
remaining 12% can proceed with cost-effective projects independently, outside of the capital planning
process.

The WW utilities have limited familiarity (~10%) with using energy management tools to track energy
consumption and process parameters. Energy management systems and tools such as ISO 50001 Ready
can provide insights into current operations of the WWTPs to analyze opportunities for saving energy and
electricity. The WW utilities were highly interested in receiving assistance from DOE to provide such
resources.

As expected, and as noted in the literature, the following processes are ranked from most to least energy-
consuming: Aeration > Primary Pumping > Disinfection > Solids Processing > Facility Operation

The WW utilities have explored the following energy efficiency measures:

• Load shifting (using electricity during off-peak hours) to lower total load demand, which can help in
 sizing future renewable energy projects

• Replacing pressure-reducing valves in distribution systems with in-line hydropower

• Evaluating the power factor penalty on their utility bills (50% of WW utilities were unaware of such
 penalties)

• Periodically assessing pump efficiency for major pumps (66% of WW utilities did not assess pump
 efficiency)

• Adjusting returned activated sludge rate (66% of WW utilities were unaware of such process
 optimization)

Some large utilities are evaluating advanced technologies to retrofit or replace existing operations for
improved process and system efficiency. The following are some of the cutting-edge initiatives noted by
participants:

• Demonstration of staged anaerobic fluidized-bed membrane bioreactors
• Evaluation of linear motion generators and fuel cells
• Design of AnitaMox processes for anaerobic digestion side streams

• Upgrade to ultraviolet lamps and use of sensors for ultraviolet disinfection systems

2.5 RESOURCE RECOVERY PILLAR FOR SWIFT

Resource recovery at WWTPs includes extracting valuable resources from wastewater and its by-products
by converting “assumed waste” into reusable materials. Typical resource recovery options included (a)
biogas generation (included as part of an energy capture option); (b) biosolids production for land
applications; and (c) water reuse and reclamation (25%).

Codigestion is a well-known strategy to improve the efficiency of ADs leading to enhanced biogas
production for supplementing energy use. However, the uptake of such a method is relatively low, with
only 37% evaluating codigestion at their WWTPs.

Regarding biosolids production, the majority of WW sector respondents (75%) produce Class B biosolids.
The concerns with biosolids production and use are the following:

• Regulations surrounding disposal
• High costs
• Technologies to treat sludge and produce biosolids
• Demand issues (e.g., not enough customers that need biosolids)

Because resource recovery necessitates implementing circularity in WWTP processes by reusing the by-
products, emerging contaminants in WW are a concern, primarily from the regulatory standpoint, for
utility personnel. Some of the key issues highlighted are poly- and per-fluoro alkyl substances (e.g., their
destruction, possible options for disposal and associated costs to manage); quaternary ammonium
compounds (from increased sanitation during and post-COVID); pharmaceuticals and personal care
products; and endocrine disrupting chemicals. About 40% of the utilities that participated in the workshop
have started monitoring and exploring bench-scale and pilot testing for emerging contaminants in their
biosolids and WWTP effluent.

2.6 ADVANCE DATA MANAGEMENT PILLAR FOR SWIFT

The adoption of smart systems and digital technologies in wastewater facilities is growing, though
uneven. Supervisory control and data acquisition systems have been implemented in 83% of facilities,
whereas 17% have not yet adopted them. Smart systems are primarily used in treatment and pumping
stations (50% each), with limited adoption in sewers + collection systems (33%), and distribution systems
(17%). Awareness of digital twins is moderate, with 67% of respondents familiar with the concept and
50% expressing willingness to use them, whereas 33% remain unfamiliar. Benefits of smart systems are
most realized in aeration and pumping stations, followed by anaerobic digestion, energy recovery, and
belt press/clarifiers, with less impact noted in disinfection and facility operations. However, most
facilities reported energy and cost savings <25% or were unaware of specific savings. The key challenges
to implementation of advanced data management systems include cost, operational complexity,
cybersecurity concerns, availability of qualified personnel, and management approval. Most facilities plan
to implement advanced controls within 3–5 years.

 3. BARRIERS AND OPPORTUNITIES FOR SWIFT

The WW sector is evolving to incorporate the technology pillars discussed in this document as pathways
toward improved system infrastructure and resiliency. However, the sector faces the following five
primary challenges towards adopting technology pillars:

 6

1. Prioritization of infrastructure capital improvements, often competing with renewable initiatives for
 prioritization and funding
 • Need for additional assistance in exploring heat recovery strategies to reduce natural gas usage
 and implement carbon capture technologies

2. Lack of sustainability-driven goals
 • Only 44% of participants engage in discussions about net zero and decarbonization.

 • The primary driver for transitioning is resource recovery with monetization (22%), followed by
 GHG footprint reduction (11%); the remainder indicate they are maintaining business-as-usual
 operations.

 • Readiness levels for net zero and decarbonization-based plant operations were rated as low
        (45%), medium (44%), and high (11%).

3. Lack of skilled personnel presents operational challenges, particularly in areas requiring technical
 expertise for implementing new systems

4. Limitations of funding and financing
 • Utilities commonly rely on funding sources such as federal, state, and local grants, Water
 Infrastructure Finance and Innovation Act, Bipartisan Infrastructure Law, and state revolving
 funds

 • Uncertainty in project selection hindering the exploration of organizational funding through
 government incentives

 • About 22% of respondents admitted to being unfamiliar with funding mechanisms, indicating a
 need for more guidance in this area. Additional 22% of respondents have explored augmenting
 organizational funding with government incentives

 • Need for specific information on funding for water generators and heat recovery systems on
 effluent lines

5. Lack of awareness of potential renewable and energy-efficient technologies and their applicability

Additionally, the WW utilities noted their preference in engaging with DOE’s technical assistance via
virtual sessions (100%), fact sheets and technical documents (43%), and on-site workshops (43%). The
four technical information sheets produced as part of the SWIFter Initiative and accompanying this primer
document provide a detailed resource pool for each of the technology pillars, while addressing the topics
of interest of the workshop participants.
