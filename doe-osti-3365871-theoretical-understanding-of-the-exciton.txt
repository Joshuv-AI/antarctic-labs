1.0 Executive Summary
Understanding how to control the nature, energy, and movement of bound electron and holes (excitons) formed
upon optical excitation is a challenge in designing better optoelectronic and photonic materials. The objective of
this project was to utilize first-principles computational approaches to understand optical excitations within low-
dimensional materials, with the ultimate goal of designing new materials by modifying the chemical and physical
structure on the nanoscale. Controlling the nature and migration properties of excitons is a challenge because of
the complex relationship between electrons, vibrations (phonons), spin, defects, disorder, and dynamics. By
analysis of highly accurate quantum mechanical calculations, we determine how to decompose these complexities
into simple, physically intuitive models.

In particular, we focused on understanding the nature and energy of the exciton within different classes of low-
dimensional materials, including the role of spin excitations. By utilizing the state-of-the-art implementations of
electron-phonon interactions, we elucidate the role of atomic vibration on the nature of excitons in magnetic
layered materials and defective single-walled carbon nanotubes. The end goal of these studies is to enable the
formation of physically-motivated design rules for new organic and inorganic nanostructures.

2.0 Introduction
The exciton is a charge redistribution that results from optical excitation, i.e., there is no net addition or removal
of charge upon excitation. In 2D heterostructures, the nature and movement of the exciton is determined to a
large extent by the electron-electron and electron-phonon strengths within individual monolayers, as well as the
coupling between layers, all of which in turn depend on chemistry, solid-state screening properties, and quantum
confinement. Recently, time-resolved spectroscopy has enabled measurement of the time-scales associated with
exciton movement for many materials; now, first-principles theory is needed to understand and explain the nature
of these excitations and energy transfer pathways. One especially important unanswered question at present is
how electron-electron interactions, electron-phonon (atomic vibration) interactions, and structural complexity
can be controlled in order to influence the resultant behavior of excitons.

This new understanding will be particularly impactful in the design of 2D van der Waals-bonded layered materials,
which are promising as inexpensive, light-weight solar energy conversion materials. Importantly, their bandgaps
can be tuned via strain, doping, and chemical modification. Additionally, there is afforded a degree of control in
layering these materials that provides influence over their properties via interfacing. Certain 2D materials naturally
form with the presence of structural defects; such defects can be harmful to the optoelectronic function of the
material but can also be beneficial as they lead to localized excitonic states utilized in single photon emitters and
catalysis. For 2D materials, the reduced screening environment results in unique electronic structure, the physics
of which does not intuitively follow established knowledge. The reduced dimensionality enhances electron-
electron and electron-phonon interactions, which in turn strongly influence exciton dynamics and recombination.
The influence of defects is also increased in low dimensions, with defects dominating the nature and dynamics of
excitons within the heterostructure.

The classes of materials studied in this project, functionalized carbon nanotubes and van der Waals-bonded
layered materials, provide a platform for understanding the fundamental excitonic properties of nanostructured
materials because they are well-characterized with a degree of control over their physical and chemical structure.
These materials are promising as inexpensive, light-weight materials for solar energy conversion and quantum

3.0 Project Objectives
The major research goal of this project was to utilize state-of-the-art first-principles computation to gain new
understanding of electronic excitations within low-dimensional materials. The specific objectives were:

1. To develop a theoretical understanding and simple physical models of electron- and exciton-phonon
 interactions in low-dimensional and layered materials
2. To understand excitons in layered magnetic van der Waals materials
3. To quantify and tune the localization of excitons, exciton-phonon interactions, and dephasing in defective
 low-dimensional materials

4.0 Description of Activities Performed
By pushing the boundaries of state-of-the-art first-principles simulation methods, we developed physical insight
regarding electronic excitations in low-dimensional and layered materials, and developed physically intuitive
models of excitations at interfaces. Below, the significant activities and findings are listed.

 • Role of atomic vibrations in 2D materials
We performed calculations of 114 2D materials to understand the physical properties leading to band gap
renormalization as a measure of electron/exciton-phonon coupling. We applied the special displacement method
to materials included within the AiiDa database and determined underlying relationships between material
properties and phonon-induced band gap renormalization using an ensemble-model-based approach. We
determined that the band gap, electronic dielectric constant, and ionicity are the most important features
determining electron-phonon interactions. We then applied the machine learning model to the C2DB database,
which includes over 1400 2D materials. This model allows us to predict a quantity that at that time remained
expensive and intractable in a high-throughput manner [8]. Additionally, we studied electron-phonon coupling
within two hybrid perovskite materials. We showed that polaronic distortion is associated with low-frequency (30-
60 cm−1) lead iodide octahedral lattice motions. Theory in combination with experiment indicated that ligand
modification can control exciton-phonon coupling, exciton-polaron population, and carrier cooling in this system
[10]. Lastly, we studied polaronic effects in the ionic semiconductor, MgO, demonstrating that an approximate
approach to the description of polaronic effects captures the hole localization that results in the experimentally-
observed Stokes shift [3]. This collective body of work on exciton-phonon interactions led to an invitation to
provide a perspective organized by F. Giustino and Y. Ping, which is in the process of publication [1].

 • Quantum defects in extended materials
We studied the magnetic, electronic, and excitonic properties of Cr-doped GaN as a potential host for qubits,
showing the presence of in-gap states that lead to localized excitons and a red-shift of optical absorption utilizing
a recently developed computational approach. This work allowed us to analyze the make-up and localization of
defect-centered transitions and showed that this approach can be used to describe localized excitons in solids
[13]. In addition, we demonstrated the role of exciton localization on the optical excitation and
photoluminescence in sp3-doped single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNT). By comparison of prediction with
measurement, we determined that the excitation associated with the observed single-photon emission is a
valence to conduction type excitation red-shifted due to defect-induced symmetry breaking and the presence of

the defect-induced in-gap state. Furthermore, we found an asymmetry between the contribution of the two spin
channels, suggesting this system has potential for spin-selective optical transitions [12].

 • Predicting new defective single-walled carbon nanotubes for single photon emission
We studied the electronic and optical properties of (6,5) SWCNT doped with sulfur dioxide (SO2), which was
synthesized by X. Ma and displayed similar low-energy peaks as sp3 defects. By calculating the electronic structure
and adsorption energy of SO2 and its derivatives, and comparing with measurements, we determined that SO2
breaks down to either S or SO on the surface [2,6]. We then continued with the study of Se defect, with the goal
of inducing enhanced spin-orbit coupling for spin transport (in preparation for publication).

 • First-Principles understanding of the synthesis of new 2D Material
We studied the adsorption of NH3 on monolayer MoS2, a precursor to the formation of Mo5N6, a promising new
material for electrocatalysis. We determined that NH3 requires defects to bind to the surface, particularly sulfur
vacancy complexes, with adsorption energy becoming more favorable for 3- and 4-layer MoS2, consistent with
the increased rate of conversion for these layers [5].

 • Excitation of coherent phonons in materials
We performed first-principles simulations of coherent phonons in bismuth and antimony, and formulated a
classical, parametrized model for phonon dynamics, introducing an effective linearization of anharmonic effects
associated with coherent phonons. Using this model, we created a protocol for coherent control of these phonons
that is predictive and is able to describe weakly anharmonic behavior of coherent phonons using linear ordinary
differential equations [11]. Additionally, for bulk GeSe, we demonstrated that machine-learned force fields can
capture the energy profile needed to parameterize this model [4].

 • Correlated exciton-spin state in layered NiPS3
We studied the coupled spin-excitonic state within the layered transition metal phosphorous trichalcogenides
NiPS3, which exhibits a sharp excitonic emission with experimental evidence of coupling of the excitonic state to
spin fluctuations. To describe this spin-correlated state, both magnetic and electronic degrees of freedom must
be appropriately accounted for, which makes it challenging for first-principles theory. In this study, we
systematically investigated the role of the underlying approximation on the excitonic properties of NiPS3, and
characterized both low-energy excitonic and magnonic states (spin waves). Based on this study, we propose that
the sharp excitonic state corresponds to a charge-transfer like transition that weakly couples to the spin degrees
of freedom. This work is in preparation for publication.

 • A framework for description of dendrite growth in electrochemical systems
We developed a multi-scale framework for describing dendrite formation in electrochemical systems, particularly
considering lithium solid batteries. We combined first-principles theory with a mesoscale hydrodynamic model
and evaluated the impact of surface energy as subsurface defects on lithium plating. Using this framework, we
demonstrated that higher energy surfaces suppress dendrite group [9].

 • Bright optical emission in metal-organic chalcogenides
We studied the excitonic properties of a series of metal-organic chalcogenides as bright photoluminescent
materials. We demonstrated a correlation between many-body theory-predicted excitonic properties and
experiment [7] and proposed an experimentally-verified simple design rule for tuning the emission wavelength
based on the ligand band gap (to be published).
