Gromov–Hausdorff convergence of time-slices of singular Ricci flows in dimension three
Yu Li

Abstract
Starting from a closed, orientable three-dimensional Riemannian manifold, we consider the completion of the associated singular Ricci flow with respect to a natural spacetime distance. We show that this completion admits canonical intrinsic metrics on its time-slices, defined by conjugate heat kernel measures, and that these time-slices arise as metric limits of the regular part of the flow. More precisely, for any t 0 > 0 t_{0}>0 and any connected component Z t 0 ′ Z_{t_{0}}^{\prime} of the time-slice Z t 0 Z_{t_{0}} of the completion, we prove that ( ℛ t ′ ¯ , d g t ) → t ↗ t 0 Gromov ​ – ​ Hausdorff ( Z t 0 ′ , d t 0 Z ) , (\overline{\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\prime}},d_{g_{t}})\xrightarrow[t\nearrow t_{0}]{\mathrm{Gromov\text{--}Hausdorff}}(Z_{t_{0}}^{\prime},d_{t_{0}}^{Z}), where ℛ t ′ \mathcal{R}_{t}^{\prime} is the corresponding connected component of the regular part and ℛ t ′ ¯ \overline{\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\prime}} denotes its metric completion. In particular, this yields the Gromov–Hausdorff convergence at the first singular time for closed three-dimensional Ricci flows. We also establish a refined structure theory for the singular set of the completion. In particular, the singular set is horizontally parabolic 1 1 -rectifiable, and its time image has vanishing 1 / 2 1/2 -dimensional Hausdorff measure. Moreover, on each time-slice, the singular set has Minkowski dimension at most 1 1 . The proof relies on heat kernel estimates for singular Ricci flows and the generalization of the structure theory of noncollapsed Ricci flow limit spaces.



1  Introduction

Ricci flow, introduced by Hamilton
[
21
]
, is one of the central tools in geometric analysis and three-dimensional topology. Starting from a Riemannian manifold
(M,g_{0})
, the Ricci flow evolves the metric by

\partial_{t}g_{t}=-2\mathrm{Ric}(g_{t}).

In dimension three, finite-time singularities are unavoidable in general. Hamilton–Perelman’s theory of Ricci flow with surgery
[
23
,
36
,
35
]
and the later theory of singular Ricci flows developed by Kleiner–Lott and Bamler–Kleiner
[
27
,
1
]
provide canonical ways to continue the flow through singularities.

The purpose of this paper is to study the metric behavior of the time-slices of a three-dimensional singular Ricci flow as one approaches a fixed time. More precisely, we are interested in the following question.

Does a singular Ricci flow time-slice admit a canonical Gromov–Hausdorff limit as it approaches a fixed time, and if so, how is this limit related to the associated singular Ricci flow?

For a smooth closed Ricci flow approaching its first singular time
T
, one might hope that the metric spaces
(M,d_{g_{t}})
converge as
t\nearrow T
. A priori, this is not clear. Indeed, in the absence of a uniform lower Ricci curvature bound, one cannot directly apply Gromov’s compactness theorem. Even if sequential Gromov–Hausdorff limits exist, different sequences
t_{j}\nearrow T
could, in principle, lead to different limits. The first goal of this paper is to show that, in dimension three, this ambiguity does not occur.

Let

(M^{3},(g_{t})_{t\in[0,T)})

be a closed Ricci flow with normalized initial condition; see Definition
2.1
. We assume that
T<\infty
is the first singular time. In our previous work
[
18
]
, we proved an
L^{1}
-curvature bound in dimension four and used it to resolve Perelman’s bounded diameter conjecture in dimension three. In particular, for the present three-dimensional flow one has

\sup_{t\in[0,T)}\left(\int_{M}|\mathrm{Rm}|\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}}+\mathrm{diam}_{g_{t}}(M)\right)<\infty.

Using this crucial estimate, we consider the measure

\mu_{t}(A)=\int_{A}(|\mathrm{R}|+1)\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}},\qquad t\in[0,T),

and show in Proposition
3.2
that

\mu_{t}(B_{g_{t}}(x,\epsilon))\geq c_{\epsilon}

for every ball
B_{g_{t}}(x,\epsilon)\neq M
, where
c_{\epsilon}>0
depends only on
\epsilon
and the Ricci flow, but is independent of
t
.

The proof is based on the canonical neighborhood theorem for the high-curvature region of a three-dimensional Ricci flow. A key observation is that, on a
\delta
-tube, namely a region in which every point is the center of a
\delta
-neck, the contribution of the scalar curvature integral is comparable to the diameter of the region. On the other hand,
\mu_{t}(M)
is uniformly bounded by the estimates above. Thus, a standard ball-packing argument implies that any sequence
t_{j}\nearrow T
admits a subsequence such that
(M,d_{g_{t_{j}}})
converges in the Gromov–Hausdorff sense to a compact metric space. The main remaining issue is to identify this limit and prove that it is independent of the sequence.

To do so, we use the spacetime distance introduced in
[
17
]
. More precisely, we equip
M\times[1,T)
with a distance
d^{*}
, defined in terms of conjugate heat kernel measures and the
W_{1}
-Wasserstein distance; see (
3.8
) for the precise definition. Let

(Z,d_{Z},\mathfrak{t})

be the metric completion of
M\times[1,T)
with respect to
d^{*}
, where
\mathfrak{t}
denotes the time function. As in
[
17
, Section 9]
, the completion adds only points at time
T
. We write

Z_{T}:=\mathfrak{t}^{-1}(T).

Following
[
17
]
, the slice
Z_{T}
carries a canonical intrinsic distance
d_{T}^{Z}
, defined by

d_{T}^{Z}(x,y):=\lim_{s\nearrow T}d^{g_{s}}_{W_{1}}(\nu_{x;s},\nu_{y;s}),

where
\nu_{x;s}
and
\nu_{y;s}
are the conjugate heat kernel measures based at
x
and
y
, respectively.

As discussed in
[
17
]
, there is some flexibility in the definition of the completion
(Z,d_{Z},\mathfrak{t})
. On the one hand, one may choose a different lower cutoff time
\sigma\in(0,T)
, rather than
1
, and define the spacetime distance
d^{*}
on
M\times[\sigma,T)
. This may lead to a different completion
(Z,d_{Z})
. On the other hand, one may also adjust the spacetime distance constant
\epsilon_{0}
in the definition of
d^{*}
, which changes both
d^{*}
and
d_{Z}
. Nevertheless, all these choices lead to the same terminal time-slice
Z_{T}
, equipped with the same canonical intrinsic distance
d_{T}^{Z}
.

Our first main theorem identifies this intrinsic terminal slice with the Gromov–Hausdorff limit of the original Ricci flow.

Theorem 1.1

(Gromov–Hausdorff convergence at the first singular time)
.

Let
(M^{3},(g_{t})_{t\in[0,T)})
be a closed Ricci flow with normalized initial condition, and suppose that
T<\infty
is the first singular time. Then

(M,d_{g_{t}})\xrightarrow[t\nearrow T]{\mathrm{Gromov\text{--}Hausdorff}}(Z_{T},d_{T}^{Z}).

In particular, the Gromov–Hausdorff limit at the first singular time is unique.

The proof has two main steps. First, using the
L^{1}
-curvature bound and the bounded diameter theorem, we obtain sequential Gromov–Hausdorff compactness of the time-slices. Given any sequential limit
(X_{\mathrm{GH}},d_{\mathrm{GH}})
obtained from
(M,d_{g_{t_{j}}})
, we then construct an isometric embedding; see Proposition
3.4
,

\iota:(Z_{T},d_{T}^{Z})\longrightarrow(X_{\mathrm{GH}},d_{\mathrm{GH}}),

by approximating points of
Z_{T}
using
H
-centers at times
t_{j}\nearrow T
. The key point is to prove that this embedding is surjective; see Proposition
3.7
. This is achieved through a quantitative covering argument based on the neck decomposition theorem from
[
18
]
. Roughly speaking, every ball of definite radius in
(M,g_{t_{j}})
must contain an
H
-center of some point in
Z_{T}
; see Lemma
3.6
. This rules out the possibility that a point in the sequential Gromov–Hausdorff limit lies outside the image of
Z_{T}
.

We next pass from smooth Ricci flows to general singular Ricci flows. Let

(\mathcal{M},\mathfrak{t},\partial_{\mathfrak{t}},g)

be the singular Ricci flow starting from a normalized, closed, orientable three-dimensional Riemannian manifold. This is a Ricci flow spacetime in the sense of Definition
2.5
. For a fixed constant
T\gg 1
, we consider the spacetime region
\mathcal{M}_{[1,T]}
and define the spacetime distance
d^{*}
using the heat kernel on the singular Ricci flow; see Definition
5.1
.

Recall that the singular Ricci flow
\mathcal{M}
is obtained as the limit of a sequence of Ricci flows with surgery as the surgery parameter tends to zero. Therefore,
\mathcal{M}
itself is not automatically a noncollapsed Ricci flow limit space in the sense of
[
17
, Definition 3.24]
, since the latter is defined as a Gromov–Hausdorff limit of closed Ricci flows. Consequently, one must first establish the relevant analytic estimates, such as heat kernel estimates, directly on the singular Ricci flow. These estimates are needed in order to extend the framework of
[
17
,
19
,
18
]
to the present setting.

A substantial part of the paper, namely Section
4
, is devoted to establishing these analytic estimates. The heat kernel on a singular Ricci flow was constructed by Lai
[
29
]
. We prove several important estimates for this heat kernel that parallel the corresponding estimates for smooth closed Ricci flows, including maximum principles, gradient estimates, Poincaré and logarithmic Sobolev inequalities, Gaussian concentration estimates, and monotonicity formulas for the pointed Nash and
\mathcal{W}
-entropies.

Most proofs are similar to those in the closed case. The main new issue is that, for a singular Ricci flow, each time-slice may be noncompact and incomplete. Thus, in many places, one must justify integrations by parts carefully. Thanks to the canonical neighborhood assumption, one can approximate a time-slice by regions whose boundary components are central two-spheres of
\epsilon
-necks. Using the rapid decay of the conjugate heat kernel near these boundary components, see Lemma
4.14
, the corresponding boundary integrals vanish in the limit.

These estimates allow us to define the spacetime distance
d^{*}
, extend conjugate heat kernel measures to the completion
Z
, and analyze the intrinsic metrics
d_{t}^{Z}
on time-slices. Let

(Z,d_{Z},\mathfrak{t})

be the metric completion of
\mathcal{M}_{[1,T]}
with respect to
d^{*}
. We denote the regular part by

\mathcal{R}:=\mathcal{M}_{[1,T]},

and the singular set by

\mathcal{S}:=Z\setminus\mathcal{R}.

For each time
t\in[1,T]
, we write

Z_{t}:=\mathfrak{t}^{-1}(t),\qquad\mathcal{R}_{t}:=\mathcal{R}\cap Z_{t},\qquad\mathcal{S}_{t}:=\mathcal{S}\cap Z_{t}.

As in the closed case, each slice
Z_{t}
carries a canonical intrinsic extended distance
d_{t}^{Z}
, again defined by conjugate heat kernel measures; see Definition
5.9
.

Let us briefly describe the geometric picture behind the proof. The spacetime completion
(Z,d_{Z},\mathfrak{t})
is a parabolic metric space: the time function satisfies

|\mathfrak{t}(x)-\mathfrak{t}(y)|\leq d_{Z}(x,y)^{2}.

Near regular points,
Z
is modeled on the smooth Ricci flow spacetime
\mathcal{R}
. Near singular points, blow-ups are modeled on quotient cylindrical Ricci shrinkers; see Subsection
5.3
. Once the necessary analytic estimates are established, one can follow the same argument as in
[
18
]
to obtain the neck decomposition theorem; see Theorem
5.19
. This theorem organizes high-curvature regions into cylindrical neck regions and controlled error regions.

Using the same method as in the proof of the Gromov–Hausdorff convergence at the first singular time, we obtain our second main result. It shows that the intrinsic metric slice
(Z_{t_{0}},d_{t_{0}}^{Z})
is the Gromov–Hausdorff limit of the corresponding regular slices as
t\nearrow t_{0}
. Since a singular Ricci flow may have several connected components at a fixed time, the statement is naturally formulated componentwise.

Theorem 1.2

(Gromov–Hausdorff convergence of singular Ricci flow time-slices)
.

Let
(\mathcal{M},\mathfrak{t},\partial_{\mathfrak{t}},g)
be the singular Ricci flow with a normalized, closed, orientable three-dimensional initial condition. For each
t_{0}\in(1,T]
, let
Z_{t_{0}}^{\prime}
be a connected component of
Z_{t_{0}}
, and let
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{R}_{[1,t_{0})}
be the branch determined by
Z_{t_{0}}^{\prime}
; see Definition
5.25
. Then

(\overline{\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\prime}},d_{g_{t}})\xrightarrow[t\nearrow t_{0}]{\mathrm{Gromov\text{--}Hausdorff}}(Z_{t_{0}}^{\prime},d_{t_{0}}^{Z}),

where
\overline{\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\prime}}
denotes the metric completion of
(\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\prime},d_{g_{t}})
. In particular, each connected component of
(Z_{t_{0}},d_{t_{0}}^{Z})
is a compact geodesic metric space.

Note that if
Z_{t_{0}}
contains no singular points, as is the case for
t_{0}
outside a subset of
\mathscr{H}^{1/2}
-measure zero
in
[0,T]
by Theorem
1.3
(2), then

Z_{t_{0}}=\mathcal{R}_{t_{0}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad d^{Z}_{t_{0}}=d_{g_{t_{0}}};

see Proposition
5.27
. Thus, the theorem above can be interpreted as the past-continuity, in the Gromov–Hausdorff sense, of the regular time-slices
(\mathcal{R}_{t},d_{g_{t}})
as
t\nearrow t_{0}
. More generally, if one considers
Z_{t}
with its intrinsic distance
d^{Z}_{t}
, then the same theorem implies the past-continuity of
(Z_{t},d^{Z}_{t})
as
t\nearrow t_{0}
; see Proposition
5.28
.

The next main theme is the structure of the singular set. In the general theory of noncollapsed Ricci flow limit spaces developed in
[
17
]
, the singular set admits a stratification according to the symmetry of tangent flows. In dimension three, the situation is especially rigid: every singular tangent flow is quotient cylindrical. Thus, the singular set behaves like a one-dimensional parabolic object. By generalizing the cylindrical strong uniqueness and parabolic rectifiability results of
[
19
,
18
]
, and using the heat kernel estimates developed here, we prove the following structure theorem.

Theorem 1.3

(Structure of the singular set)
.

The singular set
\mathcal{S}\subset Z
satisfies the following properties.

(1)

\mathcal{S}
is horizontally parabolic
1
-rectifiable.

(2)

The
1/2
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of the set of singular times vanishes:

\mathscr{H}^{1/2}(\mathfrak{t}(\mathcal{S}))=0.

(3)

For any
z_{0}\in Z
with
\mathfrak{t}(z_{0})-2r_{0}^{2}>1
, we have

\mathscr{H}^{1}\bigl(\mathcal{S}\cap B^{*}(z_{0},r_{0})\bigr)\leq C(T)r^{5}_{0}.

(4)

For each
t\in[1,T]
, the Minkowski dimension of
\mathcal{S}_{t}\subset Z_{t}
satisfies

\dim_{\mathscr{M}}(\mathcal{S}_{t})\leq 1

with respect to the intrinsic metric
d_{t}^{Z}
.

Note that Theorem
1.3
(2) improves
[
28
, Theorem 1.4]
. We further conjecture that
\mathfrak{t}(\mathcal{S})
should be a finite set.

We also prove global curvature and diameter estimates for singular Ricci flows. Let
D_{g_{t}}
denote the sum of the diameters of all connected components of
\mathcal{R}_{t}
with respect to
d_{g_{t}}
. Then we have the following theorem.

Theorem 1.4

(
L^{1}
-curvature and diameter bounds)
.

In the above setting, for every
t\in[2,T]
,

\int_{\mathcal{R}_{t}}|\mathrm{Rm}|\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}}\leq\int_{\mathcal{R}_{t}}r_{\mathrm{Rm}}^{-2}\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}}\leq C,

and

D_{g_{t}}\leq C,

where
C
depends only on
T
and the diameter of the initial metric.

These estimates may be viewed as global versions, for singular Ricci flows, of the
L^{1}
-curvature bound and of a generalized form of Perelman’s bounded diameter theorem.

Finally, we explain how the same ideas extend to certain higher-dimensional Ricci flows. Under the positive isotropic curvature condition, in dimensions for which the surgery theory and the classification of
\kappa
-solutions are available, the only noncompact singular model is the standard cylinder
S^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
. In this setting, the same arguments yield analogous convergence and structure results for the spacetime completion of the corresponding singular Ricci flow.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we recall the relevant background on Ricci flows with surgery, singular Ricci flows, and heat kernels. In Section 3, we prove the Gromov–Hausdorff convergence theorem at the first singular time of a closed three-dimensional Ricci flow. In Section 4, we establish the heat kernel estimates on singular Ricci flows that are needed later. In Section 5, we construct the spacetime completion of a singular Ricci flow, define the intrinsic metrics on its time-slices, prove the curvature and diameter estimates, establish the Gromov–Hausdorff convergence of time-slices, and derive the structure theorems for the singular set. In Section 6, we discuss extensions to higher-dimensional Ricci flows with positive isotropic curvature.

2.1  Ricci flows with surgery

Definition 2.1

(Normalized Riemannian manifold)
.

We say that a compact

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Rm}|+\mathrm{inj}^{-1}\leq 10^{-n},

where

In this paper, we mostly consider Ricci flows whose initial data are normalized Riemannian manifolds.

Definition 2.2

(
\kappa
-noncollapsed)
.

A Ricci flow
(M^{3},(g_{t})_{t\in I})
is called

-noncollapsed

, where

(x,t)\in M\times I
and any

, the condition

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Rm}|\leq r^{-2}\quad\text{on }B_{g_{t}}(x,r)

implies that

|B_{g_{t}}(x,r)|_{t}\geq\kappa r^{3}.

Definition 2.3

(
\kappa
-solution)
.

An ancient complete Ricci flow
(M^{3},g_{t})_{t\in(-\infty,0]}
is called a
\kappa
-solution
if
\mathrm{Rm}\geq 0
and is uniformly bounded on
M\times(-\infty,0]
, and if
(M^{3},g_{t})
is
\kappa
-noncollapsed at all scales for every

.

The classification of all three-dimensional

-solutions is known; see
[
22
,
34
,
32
,
33
,
11
,
10
,
7
]
. The complete list consists of the following (up to quotients where allowed):

(i)

Ricci shrinkers. More precisely, these are the standard Ricci flows on

,

, or

;

(ii)

the Bryant steady soliton;

(iii)

.

Definition 2.4

(
\epsilon
-closeness)
.

Let
(M_{i},g_{i},x_{i})
, for

, be two pointed Riemannian manifolds. We say that

Definition 2.5

(Ricci flow spacetime)
.

A Ricci flow spacetime over an interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
is a tuple
(\mathcal{M},\mathfrak{t},\partial_{\mathfrak{t}},g)

(1)

\mathcal{M}
is a four-dimensional smooth manifold with smooth boundary

\partial\mathcal{M}

(2)

\mathfrak{t}:\mathcal{M}\to I

, we denote by

\mathcal{M}_{t}:=\mathfrak{t}^{-1}(t)\subset\mathcal{M}

(3)

\mathfrak{t}(\partial\mathcal{M})\subset\partial I
.

(4)

\partial_{\mathfrak{t}}

(5)

g
is a smooth inner product on the spatial subbundle
\ker(d\mathfrak{t})\subset T\mathcal{M}
. For any
t\in I
, we denote by

the restriction of

\mathcal{M}_{t}
.

(6)

satisfies the Ricci flow equation

\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{\mathfrak{t}}}g=-2\mathrm{Ric}(g).

Here
\mathrm{Ric}(g)
denotes the symmetric
(0,2)
-tensor on

(\mathcal{M}_{t},g_{t})

Next, we recall the definition of Ricci flow with surgery from

Definition 2.6

(Ricci flow with surgery)
.

A Ricci flow with surgery
\mathcal{M}
consists of the following data:

(1)

A collection of Ricci flows

\{(M_{k},g_{k}(t))_{t\in[t_{k}^{-},t_{k}^{+}]}\}_{1\leq k\leq N},

where
N\leq\infty
, each
M_{k}
is a compact (possibly empty) manifold,
t_{k}^{+}=t_{k+1}^{-}

.

(2)

A collection of isometric embeddings

\{\psi_{k}:X_{k}^{+}\to X_{k+1}^{-}\}_{1\leq k<N},

where
X_{k}^{+}\subset M_{k}
and
X_{k+1}^{-}\subset M_{k+1}

We say that

The time functions, time vector fields, and metrics descend to

(\mathcal{M}^{\prime},\mathfrak{t},\partial_{\mathfrak{t}},g)
defined over
[t_{1}^{-},t_{N}^{+}]

For any parameter

\delta,r,\kappa:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)

such that, for any normalized Riemannian manifold, a Ricci flow with surgery starting from this initial condition is defined for all time with respect to

35

Theorem 2.7
.

For any normalized Riemannian manifold

.

(b)

\mathcal{M}
satisfies the

(d)

2.2  Singular Ricci flows and heat kernels

First, we recall the construction of singular Ricci flows from
[
27
]
.

Given a normalized Riemannian manifold
(M^{3},g_{0})
, let
\delta_{i}=\delta_{i}(t)\to 0
be a sequence of surgery parameter functions. By Theorem
2.7
, for each
i
there exists a Ricci flow with surgery
\mathcal{M}_{i}
with initial condition
(M^{3},g_{0})
. Let
\mathcal{M}_{i}^{\prime}
denote the associated Ricci flow spacetime. Using a compactness argument, Kleiner and Lott proved in

[
27
]
that, after passing to a subsequence, one has

\mathcal{M}_{i}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{M}.

The limiting Ricci flow spacetime
(\mathcal{M},\mathfrak{t},\partial_{\mathfrak{t}},g)
is called the
singular Ricci flow
, and it satisfies the following properties:

(a)

for any
T\geq 0
, the scalar curvature function
\mathrm{R}:\mathcal{M}_{\leq T}\to\mathbb{R}
is bounded below and proper;

(b)

\mathcal{M}
satisfies the Hamilton–Ivey pinching condition;

(c)

for some parameter
\epsilon>0
and some functions
\kappa,r:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
,
\mathcal{M}
is
\kappa
-noncollapsed below scale
\epsilon
and satisfies the
(r,\epsilon)
-canonical neighborhood assumption;

(d)

\mathcal{M}
is
0
-complete in the sense of
[
1
, Definition 5.3]
.

In
[
1
, Theorem 1.1]
, it was proved that if
\epsilon<\epsilon_{\mathrm{can}}
, where
\epsilon_{\mathrm{can}}
is a universal constant, then any singular Ricci flow satisfying the above conditions with the same initial data is unique. In the following, we will always assume
\epsilon<\epsilon_{\mathrm{can}}
, and refer to the Ricci flow spacetime
\mathcal{M}
as the singular Ricci flow starting from
(M^{3},g_{0})
.

For a singular Ricci flow
\mathcal{M}
, there exists a heat kernel
K(x;\cdot)
, constructed in
[
29
, Section 5]
. We briefly recall this construction. Fix
x_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
. For the Ricci flows with surgery
\mathcal{M}_{i}
and the associated Ricci flow spacetimes
\mathcal{M}_{i}^{\prime}
above, let
x_{i0}\in\mathcal{M}_{i}^{\prime}
be a sequence converging to
x_{0}
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}_{i}
is given by the data

\{(M_{k},g_{k}(t))_{t\in[t_{k}^{-},t_{k}^{+}]}\}_{1\leq k\leq N}

as in Definition
2.6
, where
t_{N}^{+}=t_{i0}:=\mathfrak{t}(x_{i0})
. On
M_{N}\times[t_{N}^{-},t_{N}^{+}]
, let
u_{i}
be the conjugate heat kernel of
(M_{N},g_{N}(t))
based at
(x_{i0},t_{i0})
. We extend
u_{i}
to
M_{N-1}\times\{t_{N-1}^{+}\}
by setting

u_{i}(x,t_{N-1}^{+})=u_{i}(x,t_{N}^{-})\quad\text{if }x\in\mathrm{Int}(X_{N-1}^{+}),

and

u_{i}(x,t_{N-1}^{+})=0\quad\text{if }x\in M_{N-1}\setminus\mathrm{Int}(X_{N-1}^{+}).

Then, for any
(x,t)\in M_{N-1}\times[t_{N-1}^{-},t_{N-1}^{+}]
, we define
u_{i}
by the reproduction formula

u_{i}(x,t)=\int_{\mathrm{Int}(X_{N-1}^{+})}u_{i}(y,t_{N-1}^{+})\,K_{N-1}(y,t_{N-1}^{+};x,t)\,\mathrm{d}V_{g(t^{+}_{N-1})}(y),

where
K_{N-1}(y,t_{N-1}^{+};x,t)
denotes the heat kernel on
M_{N-1}\times[t_{N-1}^{-},t_{N-1}^{+}]
. Iterating this procedure defines
u_{i}
on all of
\mathcal{M}_{i}
. Thus, we set
K_{i}(x_{0};\cdot)=u_{i}
. As
i\to\infty
, the functions
K_{i}(x_{0};\cdot)
converge to the conjugate heat kernel
K(x_{0};\cdot)
on
\mathcal{M}
.

The following result was proved in
[
29
, Section 5]
.

Theorem 2.8
.

The heat kernel
K
constructed above on the singular Ricci flow
\mathcal{M}
satisfies the following properties:

(a)

For any
x_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
, the function

K(x_{0};\cdot):\mathcal{M}_{t<\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})}\to\mathbb{R}

is a smooth solution to the conjugate heat equation

\square^{*}K(x_{0};\cdot)=(-\partial_{\mathfrak{t}}-\Delta+\mathrm{R})K(x_{0};\cdot)=0.

(b)

For any
h\in C_{c}^{0}(\mathcal{M})
and any
x_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
,

\lim_{t\nearrow\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})}\int_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}K(x_{0};x)\,h(x)\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}}(x)=h(x_{0}).

(c)

Suppose that for some
r_{0},T>0
, we have
|\mathrm{Rm}|\leq r_{0}^{-2}
on

P_{0}:=P(x_{0},r_{0},-r_{0}^{2})

(see
[
29
, Definition 2.4]
) and that
\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})<T
. Then there exists a constant
C=C(r_{0},T)>0
such that

K(x_{0};\cdot)\leq C\quad\text{on }\mathcal{M}_{t<\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})}\setminus P_{0}.

(d)

For any
x_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
and any
t<\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})
, we have

\int_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}K(x_{0};x)\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}}(x)=1.

(e)

For any
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
with
\mathfrak{t}(x)>\mathfrak{t}(y)
, we have

K(x;y)=\int_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}K(x;z)\,K(z;y)\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}}(z)

for every
t\in(\mathfrak{t}(y),\mathfrak{t}(x))
.

(f)

For any
x_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
, the function

K(\cdot;x_{0}):\mathcal{M}_{t>\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})}\to\mathbb{R}

is a smooth solution to the heat equation

\square K(\cdot;x_{0})=(\partial_{\mathfrak{t}}-\Delta)K(\cdot;x_{0})=0.

(g)

For any
h\in C_{c}^{0}(\mathcal{M})
and any
x_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
,

\lim_{t\searrow\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})}\int_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}K(x;x_{0})\,h(x)\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}}(x)=h(x_{0}).

(h)

Suppose that for some
r_{0},T>0
, we have
|\mathrm{Rm}|\leq r_{0}^{-2}
on

P_{0}:=P(x_{0},r_{0},r_{0}^{2})

(see
[
29
, Definition 2.4]
) and that
\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})<T
. Then there exists a constant
C=C(r_{0},T)>0
such that

K(\cdot;x_{0})\leq C\quad\text{on }\mathcal{M}_{t>\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})}\setminus P_{0}.

We will also need the following refined estimate from
[
29
, Theorem 5.6]
, which improves Theorem
2.8
(c) at points of high curvature.

Theorem 2.9
.

Given
r_{0},T>0
with
r_{0}^{2}<T
, and
m\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a constant
C_{m}=C(r_{0},T,m)>0
such that the following holds for any
t_{0}\in(r_{0}^{2},T)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a singular Ricci flow with a normalized initial condition. For any
x_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
with
\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})=t_{0}
, suppose that

|\mathrm{Rm}|\leq r_{0}^{-2}\quad\text{on }P_{0}:=P(x_{0},r_{0},-r_{0}^{2}).

Then, for any
x\in\mathcal{M}_{t<\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})}\setminus P_{0}
, we have

K(x_{0};x)\,\mathrm{R}^{m}(x)\leq C_{m}.

We end this section with the following definition.

Definition 2.10

(Curvature radius)
.

For any
x\in\mathcal{M}
, the curvature radius
r_{\mathrm{Rm}}(x)
is defined to be the supremum of all
r>0
such that
B_{g_{t}}(x,r)
is relatively compact in
\mathcal{M}_{t}
, and the product domain

\displaystyle B_{g_{t}}(x,r)\times\left([\mathfrak{t}(x)-r^{2},\mathfrak{t}(x)+r^{2}]\cap[0,\infty)\right)

is unscathed and satisfies the curvature bound
|\mathrm{Rm}|\leq r^{-2}
.

3  Gromov–Hausdorff convergence at the first singular time

In this section, we assume that
(M^{3},(g_{t})_{t\in[0,T)})
is a closed Ricci flow with an orientable, normalized initial condition
(M,g_{0})
. We assume that
T<\infty
is the first singular time. Note that by the curvature bound in Definition
2.1
, we have
T\geq 2
; see, for instance,
[
16
, Equation (10.14)]
.

Moreover, by the monotonicity of the Nash entropy, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\tau\in(0,t]}\mathcal{N}_{(x,t)}(\tau)\geq\mathcal{N}_{(x,t)}(t)\geq\inf_{\tau\in(0,T]}\boldsymbol{\mu}(g_{0},\tau)\geq-Y_{0},

(3.1)

for any
(x,t)\in M\times[0,T)
. Here,
Y_{0}
is a constant depending only on
T
by Definition
2.1
. Thus, the Ricci flow has entropy bounded below by
-Y_{0}
in the sense of
[
17
, Definition 2.20]
.

3.1  Sequential compactness of time-slices

We first recall the following
L^{1}
-curvature bound from
[
18
, Theorem 1.7]
and the resolution of Perelman’s bounded diameter conjecture from
[
18
, Theorem 1.8]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

There exists a constant
C
, depending only on the flow, such that for any
t\in[0,T)
,

\displaystyle\int_{M}|\mathrm{Rm}|\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}}+\mathrm{diam}_{g_{t}}(M)\leq C.

Next, we prove the following proposition.

Proposition 3.2
.

For any
t\in[0,T)
and any
\epsilon>0
, the maximal cardinality of an
\epsilon
-separated subset of
(M,g_{t})
is bounded by a constant
C_{\epsilon}
independent of
t
.

Proof.

We fix a time
t\in[0,T)
. Throughout the proof, all constants
C_{i}
and
c_{i}
may depend on the given Ricci flow, but are independent of
t
.

Define a measure
\mu_{t}
by

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(A):=\int_{A}(|\mathrm{R}|+1)\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}}

for any Borel subset
A\subset M
. By Theorem
3.1
and
[
17
, Proposition 9.3]
, there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(M)\leq C_{1}.

(3.2)

Next, we claim that for any
x\in M
and any sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, if
B_{g_{t}}(x,\epsilon)\neq M
, then

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(B_{g_{t}}(x,\epsilon))\geq c_{\epsilon}>0,

(3.3)

where
c_{\epsilon}
is a constant depending on the Ricci flow and on
\epsilon
, but independent of
t
. Once (
3.3
) is established, the conclusion follows immediately from (
3.2
).

By the canonical neighborhood theorem (see
[
34
, Theorem 12.1]
and
[
26
, Theorem 52.7]
), for any
\delta>0
, there exists
r_{\delta}>0
such that if
\mathrm{R}(x,t)\geq r_{\delta}^{-2}
, then
(M,g_{t},x)
is
\delta
-close to a
\kappa
-solution at scale
\mathrm{R}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(x,t)
. We will choose
\delta
as a small universal constant later. First, we require that
r_{\delta}
is sufficiently small so that
r_{\delta}\leq\delta\epsilon
.

To prove (
3.3
), we consider two cases.

Case 1.
There exists a point
y\in B_{g_{t}}(x,\epsilon/2)
such that
\mathrm{R}(y,t)<r_{\delta}^{-2}
.

In this case, it follows from
[
27
, Lemma 3.1]
that

\mathrm{R}\leq\eta^{-2}r_{\delta}^{-2}\qquad\text{on }B_{g_{t}}(y,\eta r_{\delta})

for some universal constant
\eta\in(0,1)
. Since
\eta r_{\delta}\leq\epsilon
, Perelman’s no-local-collapsing theorem
[
34
]
implies that

\displaystyle|B_{g_{t}}(x,\epsilon)|_{t}\geq|B_{g_{t}}(y,\eta r_{\delta})|_{t}\geq c_{1}r_{\delta}^{3}>0.

Therefore, in this case we obtain

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(B_{g_{t}}(x,\epsilon))\geq|B_{g_{t}}(x,\epsilon)|_{t}\geq c_{1}r_{\delta}^{3}>0.

(3.4)

Case 2.

\mathrm{R}\geq r_{\delta}^{-2}
on
B_{g_{t}}(x,\epsilon/2)
.

In this case, fix a point
y\in\partial B_{g_{t}}(x,\epsilon/2)
and choose a minimizing geodesic
\gamma(s)
from
x
to
y
with respect to
g_{t}
. By assumption,
(M,g_{t},\gamma(s))
is
\delta
-close to a
\kappa
-solution at scale
\mathrm{R}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\gamma(s),t)
for every
s\in[0,\epsilon/2]
. Next, choose disjoint balls
\{B_{g_{t}}(x_{i},r_{i})\}_{1\leq i\leq N}
, where
x_{i}=\gamma(s_{i})
and
r_{i}=\mathrm{R}^{-1/2}(x_{i},t)
, such that the balls
B_{g_{t}}(x_{i},5r_{i})
cover
\gamma
.

For each
i
, the pointed manifold
(M,g_{t},x_{i})
is
\delta
-close at scale
r_{i}
to one of the following: (a) a
\delta
-neck; (b) a
\delta
-cap; or (c) a closed manifold of constant positive sectional curvature; see
[
27
, A.8]
. In all three cases, comparison with the corresponding model implies that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{g_{t}}(x_{i},r_{i})}|\mathrm{R}|\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}}\geq c_{2}r_{i}>0.

(3.5)

Since
r_{i}\leq r_{\delta}\leq\delta\epsilon
, the pointed manifold
(M,g_{t},x_{i})
cannot be
\delta
-close at scale
r_{i}
to a closed manifold of constant positive sectional curvature. Indeed, otherwise the diameter of
(M,g_{t})
would be bounded by
C_{2}r_{i}\leq\epsilon
, where
C_{2}
is a universal constant, contradicting the assumption that
B_{g_{t}}(x,\epsilon)\neq M
. Moreover, since
\gamma
is a minimizing geodesic and all
x_{i}
lie on
\gamma
, the number of indices
i
for which
(M,g_{t},x_{i})
is
\delta
-close to a
\delta
-cap is at most
2C_{3}
, where
C_{3}
is a universal constant.

Figure 1:
A
\delta
-neck.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that for every
C_{3}+1\leq i\leq N-C_{3}-1
, the pointed manifold
(M,g_{t},x_{i})
is
\delta
-close to a
\delta
-neck at scale
r_{i}
; see Figure
1
. Hence the open set

\displaystyle U:=\bigcup_{C_{3}+1\leq i\leq N-C_{3}-1}B_{g_{t}}(x_{i},5r_{i})

forms a
\delta
-tube; see
[
27
, A.3]
. The total length of
\gamma\cap U
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{length}(\gamma\cap U)\geq\epsilon/2-20C_{3}r_{\delta}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{4},

where the last inequality holds because
r_{\delta}\leq\delta\epsilon
.

On the other hand, each segment
\gamma\cap B_{g_{t}}(x_{i},5r_{i})
, for
C_{3}+1\leq i\leq N-C_{3}-1
, is almost parallel to the axis of the model cylinder. Therefore,

\displaystyle 10\sum_{i=C_{3}+1}^{N-C_{3}-1}r_{i}\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{length}(\gamma\cap U)\geq\frac{\epsilon}{8}.

Combining this with (
3.5
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(B_{g_{t}}(x,\epsilon))\geq\int_{B_{g_{t}}(x,\epsilon)}|\mathrm{R}|\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}}\geq\sum_{i}\int_{B_{g_{t}}(x_{i},r_{i})}|\mathrm{R}|\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}}\geq c_{2}\sum_{i}r_{i}\geq c_{3}\epsilon>0.

(3.6)

Combining (
3.4
) and (
3.6
), we conclude that (
3.3
) holds. This completes the proof.
∎

A direct consequence of Proposition
3.2
, together with the uniform diameter bound in Theorem
3.1
and Gromov’s compactness theorem
[
20
]
, is the following existence result for sequential limits as
t\nearrow T
.

Corollary 3.3
.

In the above setting, for any sequence
\{t_{j}\}
with
t_{j}\nearrow T
, after passing to a subsequence if necessary, we have

\displaystyle(M,d_{g_{t_{j}}})\xrightarrow[t_{j}\nearrow T]{\mathrm{Gromov\text{--}Hausdorff}}(X_{\GH},d_{\GH}),

(3.7)

where
d_{g_{t}}
denotes the distance induced by
g_{t}
. The limit
(X_{\GH},d_{\GH})
is a compact geodesic metric space.

Note that the limit metric space
(X_{\GH},d_{\GH})
may
a priori
depend on the choice of the sequence
\{t_{j}\}
and hence need not be unique. In what follows, we will prove that the limit metric space is in fact unique.

3.2  The spacetime completion and the terminal slice

We consider the
d^{*}
-distance on
M\times[1,T)
, defined as in
[
17
, Definition 3.5]
. More precisely, for any
x^{*}=(x,t),y^{*}=(y,s)\in M\times[1,T)
with
s\leq t
, we define

\displaystyle d^{*}(x^{*},y^{*}):=\inf_{r\in[\sqrt{t-s},\,\sqrt{t-1})}\left\{r\;\middle|\;d_{W_{1}}^{g_{t-r^{2}}}\bigl(\nu_{x^{*};t-r^{2}},\nu_{y^{*};t-r^{2}}\bigr)\leq\epsilon_{0}r\right\}.

(3.8)

If no such
r
exists, we define

d^{*}(x^{*},y^{*}):=\epsilon_{0}^{-1}d_{W_{1}}^{g_{1}}\bigl(\nu_{x^{*};1},\nu_{y^{*};1}\bigr).

Here
\nu
denotes the conjugate heat kernel measure of the Ricci flow; see
[
17
, Definition 2.6]
. Moreover,
d_{W_{1}}^{g_{t}}
denotes the
W_{1}
-Wasserstein distance with respect to
(M,d_{g_{t}})
; see
[
17
, Definition 2.1]
. The constant
\epsilon_{0}\in(0,1]
is the spacetime distance constant, depending only on
Y_{0}
(and hence on
T
); see
[
17
, Definition 3.3]
for the precise definition. By
[
17
]
, the space
M\times[1,T)
has bounded diameter with respect to
d^{*}
, and the time function
\mathfrak{t}
is
2
-Hölder continuous with respect to
d^{*}
, namely,

\bigl|\mathfrak{t}(x^{*})-\mathfrak{t}(y^{*})\bigr|\leq d^{*}(x^{*},y^{*})^{2}.

We then define
(Z,d_{Z},\mathfrak{t})
to be the metric completion of
M\times[1,T)
with respect to
d^{*}
. By construction, the completion adds only points in

Z_{T}:=\mathfrak{t}^{-1}(T)\subset Z.

Moreover, it is proved in
[
17
, Section 9]
that
(Z,d_{Z},\mathfrak{t})
is a noncollapsed Ricci flow limit space over
[1,T]
in the sense of
[
17
, Definition 3.24]
.

As in
[
17
, Section 6]
, for each
t\in[1,T)
, we define a distance function on
Z_{t}:=\mathfrak{t}^{-1}(t)\subset Z
by

\displaystyle d_{t}^{Z}(x,y):=\lim_{s\nearrow t}d_{W_{1}}^{g_{s}}(\nu_{x;s},\nu_{y;s})\in[0,+\infty]

(3.9)

for any
x,y\in Z_{t}
, where the limit exists by
[
17
, Lemma 5.29]
. If
t\in[1,T)
, then
(Z_{t},d_{t}^{Z})
is precisely
(M,d_{g_{t}})
. Moreover,
(Z_{T},d_{T}^{Z})
is isometric to Bamler’s asymptotic boundary introduced in
[
3
, Section 2.6]
; see
[
17
, Lemma 9.1]
.

For the remainder of this section, we fix a sequence
\{t_{j}\}
with
t_{j}\nearrow T
such that (
3.7
) holds.

Proposition 3.4
.

There exists an isometric embedding

\displaystyle\iota:(Z_{T},d_{T}^{Z})\longrightarrow(X_{\GH},d_{\GH}).

Proof.

We first show that
(Z_{T},d_{T}^{Z})
is separable, i.e., it contains a countable dense subset. Fix
\epsilon>0
, and choose a countable
\epsilon
-separated set
\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset Z_{T}
, where
N\in[1,\infty]
. Note that a maximal
\epsilon
-separated set in
Z_{T}
may be uncountable.

For
z_{1}
, let
x_{1,j}^{*}
be an
H_{3}
-center of
z_{1}
on
M\times\{t_{j}\}
, where
H_{3}:=\pi^{2}+4
; see
[
17
, Definition 6.12]
. After passing to a subsequence of
\{t_{j}\}
, we may assume that
x_{1,j}^{*}
converges to some point
x_{1}\in X_{\GH}
in the Gromov–Hausdorff sense.

Repeating this construction for
z_{2},z_{3},\dots
, and then passing to a diagonal subsequence of
\{t_{j}\}
, still denoted by
\{t_{j}\}
, we may assume that for each
z_{i}
, the corresponding
H_{3}
-centers
x_{i,j}^{*}\in M\times\{t_{j}\}
converge to some point
x_{i}\in X_{\GH}
in the Gromov–Hausdorff sense.

We now define a map

\iota:\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\to\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}

by setting

\iota(z_{i})=x_{i},\qquad 1\leq i\leq N.

By the definition (
3.9
), it follows that
\iota
is isometric on
\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, namely,

\displaystyle d_{\GH}\bigl(\iota(z_{i_{1}}),\iota(z_{i_{2}})\bigr)=d_{\GH}(x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}})=d_{T}^{Z}(z_{i_{1}},z_{i_{2}}),\qquad\forall\,i_{1},i_{2}\in[1,N].

In particular,
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is an
\epsilon
-separated subset of
X_{\GH}
. Since
(X_{\GH},d_{\GH})
is compact, we conclude that
N
must be finite. Hence every
\epsilon
-separated subset of
(Z_{T},d_{T}^{Z})
is finite, which implies that
(Z_{T},d_{T}^{Z})
is totally bounded. Since
(Z_{T},d_{T}^{Z})
is complete, it follows that
(Z_{T},d_{T}^{Z})
is compact, and in particular separable.

We may therefore choose a countable dense subset
\{z_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}\subset Z_{T}
. By the same argument as above, there exists a map

\iota:\{z_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}\to X_{\GH}

such that

\displaystyle d_{\GH}\bigl(\iota(z_{i_{1}}),\iota(z_{i_{2}})\bigr)=d_{T}^{Z}(z_{i_{1}},z_{i_{2}}),\qquad\forall\,i_{1},i_{2}\geq 1.

(3.10)

Since
\{z_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
is dense in
Z_{T}
, (
3.10
) allows us to extend
\iota
uniquely to an isometric embedding from
(Z_{T},d_{T}^{Z})
into
(X_{\GH},d_{\GH})
. This completes the proof.
∎

3.3  Identification of the Gromov–Hausdorff limit

Next, we prove that the isometric embedding
\iota
is in fact surjective. Before doing so, we recall the neck decomposition theorem proved in
[
18
, Theorem 1.11]
. Although the theorem there is stated for four-dimensional noncollapsed Ricci flow limit spaces, an analogous neck decomposition also holds in dimension three. This can be seen either by taking the product of a three-dimensional Ricci flow limit space with a flat circle
S^{1}
, or by repeating the same proof verbatim in the three-dimensional setting. In fact, the proof is simpler in dimension three, since the only model space is the standard cylinder
S^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
; note that
\mathbb{RP}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
cannot occur because of the orientability assumption. We state the three-dimensional neck decomposition below. For the relevant definitions and notations, we refer the reader to
[
18
, Definition 5.9, Index]
.

Theorem 3.5

(3D neck decomposition theorem)
.

Let
(Z,d_{Z},\mathfrak{t})
be a noncollapsed Ricci flow limit space arising as the pointed Gromov–Hausdorff limit of a sequence of orientable Ricci flows in
\mathcal{M}(3,Y,T)
. For any constants
\delta>0
and
\eta>0
, if

, then the following holds.

Given

with

, we have the decomposition

\displaystyle B^{*}(z_{0},r_{0})\subset\bigcup_{a}\big(\mathscr{N}_{a}\bigcap B^{*}(x_{a},r_{a})\big)\bigcup\bigcup_{b}B^{*}(x_{b},r_{b})\bigcup S^{1,\delta,\eta},

\displaystyle S^{1,\delta,\eta}\subset\bigcup_{a}\big(\mathscr{C}_{0,a}\bigcap B^{*}(x_{a},r_{a})\big)\bigcup\tilde{S}^{1,\delta,\eta},

(a)

For each

For each

-symmetric.

(c)

Next, we prove the following key lemma.

Lemma 3.6
.

For any
\epsilon>0
and any sequence
\{x_{j}\}\subset M
, after passing to a subsequence of
\{t_{j}\}
if necessary, the set
B_{g_{t_{j}}}(x_{j},\epsilon)\times\{t_{j}\}
contains an
H
-center of some point
z_{j}\in Z_{T}
for all

, where

is a constant.

Proof.

For small parameters
\delta>0
and
\eta>0
, to be chosen later, let
\zeta=\zeta(Y,\delta,\eta)
be such that Theorem
3.5
applies to
(Z,d_{Z},\mathfrak{t})
. By
[

, Equation (9.1)]
, the diameter of

is bounded by a constant depending on

and

. Consequently, by
[

, Propositions 5.34 and 5.35]
, the set

C_{0}

with

and

where

depends on the Ricci flow,
\delta
, and
\eta
. Applying Theorem
3.5
to each ball

and using the fact that

, we obtain

\displaystyle Z_{[T/2,T)}\subset\bigcup_{a}\left(\mathscr{N}_{a}\bigcap B^{*}(x_{a},r_{a})\right)\bigcup\bigcup_{b}B^{*}(x_{b},r_{b}),

(3.11)

together with the content estimate

\displaystyle\sum_{a}r_{a}+\sum_{b}r_{b}\leq C_{1},

(3.12)

where
C_{1}
depends on the Ricci flow,
\delta
, and
\eta
.

By (
3.12
), we may choose a constant
\delta^{\prime}\ll\epsilon
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{r_{a}\leq\delta^{\prime}}r_{a}+\sum_{r_{b}\leq\delta^{\prime}}r_{b}\leq\delta^{\prime}.

(3.13)

From
[
18
, Lemma 6.21(v)]
, it follows that for any
\sigma>0
, if
\eta\leq\eta(T,\sigma)
, then every point

is
(3,\sigma,\sigma r_{b})
-symmetric. If

where

Let

B_{j}:=B_{g_{t_{j}}}(x_{j},\epsilon)\times\{t_{j}\}.

We consider the following cases.

Case 1.
There exist infinitely many

such that

B_{j}\bigcap\bigcup_{r_{b}>\delta^{\prime}}B^{*}(x_{b},r_{b})\neq\emptyset.

Passing to a subsequence of
\{t_{j}\}
if necessary, we may assume that

B_{j}\bigcap\bigcup_{r_{b}>\delta^{\prime}}B^{*}(x_{b},r_{b})\neq\emptyset\qquad\text{for all }j.

By the discussion above, there exists a point

such that

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{Rm}}(y_{j})\geq c(T)r_{b}\geq c(T)\delta^{\prime}>0.

(3.14)

Hence, for all sufficiently large

lying on the flow line of

through

Case 2.
For all

In this case, (

) implies that

\displaystyle B_{j}\subset\bigcup_{a}\left(\mathscr{N}_{a}\bigcap B^{*}(x_{a},r_{a})\right)\bigcup\bigcup_{r_{b}\leq\delta^{\prime}}B^{*}(x_{b},r_{b}).

We decompose

\displaystyle\Omega^{1}_{j}:=B_{j}\bigcap\left(\bigcup_{r_{a}\leq\delta^{\prime}}\left(\mathscr{N}_{a}\bigcap B^{*}(x_{a},r_{a})\right)\bigcup\bigcup_{r_{b}\leq\delta^{\prime}}B^{*}(x_{b},r_{b})\right)

By the same argument as in the proof of
[

, Theorem 6.31]
, we may choose
\delta=\delta(T)
,
\eta=\eta(T)
, and

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{j}^{1}}|\mathrm{R}|\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t_{j}}}\leq C(T)\left(\sum_{r_{a}\leq\delta^{\prime}}r_{a}+\sum_{r_{b}\leq\delta^{\prime}}r_{b}\right)\leq C(T)\delta^{\prime},

(3.15)

where the last inequality uses (
3.13

Next, we analyze
\Omega_{j}^{2}
. Since the number of cylindrical neck regions

\mathscr{N}_{a}

denote its center; for the relevant definitions, see
[
18
, Definition 5.9]
.

Case 2a.
There exist infinitely many
j
such that

B^{a}_{j}:=B_{j}\bigcap\mathscr{N}_{a}\bigcap B^{*}(x_{a},r_{a})=\emptyset.

Passing to a subsequence if necessary, we may assume that
B_{j}^{a}=\emptyset
for all

. Therefore, by (

\displaystyle\int_{B_{j}}|\mathrm{R}|\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t_{j}}}=\int_{\Omega_{j}^{1}}|\mathrm{R}|\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t_{j}}}\leq C(T)\delta^{\prime}.

(3.16)

Case 2b.
For all
j
, we have
B_{j}^{a}\neq\emptyset
.

For any
x\in B_{j}^{a}
, choose
x^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}_{a}

If

, then, provided

H

for some universal constant

. Indeed, by the definition of cylindrical neck regions,

is
(1,\delta,\mathfrak{c}^{-5}d_{x})

; see
[
18
, Definition 5.9]
. If

were not an

for sufficiently large

, then one could find a point

By
[
18
, Definition 5.9(n4)]

such that

d_{Z}(y,y^{\prime})\leq d_{x}/10,

and hence

d_{Z}(x,y^{\prime})\leq d_{x}/5,

contradicting the definition of

.

Thus, to complete the proof in this subcase, we may assume that
x^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}_{a}\setminus Z_{T}
for every

and every

.

For each
j
, by a Vitali covering argument, we can find points
x_{j}^{i}\in B_{j}^{a}
such that the balls
\{B^{*}(x_{j}^{i},2d_{x_{j}^{i}})\}_{i}
are mutually disjoint and

\displaystyle B_{j}^{a}\subset\bigcup_{i}B^{*}(x_{j}^{i},10d_{x_{j}^{i}}).

If for each

m_{j}=x_{j}^{i_{j}}
and

, and choose

By the definition of

, this implies that for some

,

\displaystyle d_{W_{1}}^{g_{T-s_{j}^{2}}}\bigl(\nu_{m_{j}^{\prime};\,T-s_{j}^{2}},\nu_{y^{j};\,T-s_{j}^{2}}\bigr)=\epsilon_{0}s_{j}.

Using the monotonicity of

-distance and Bamler’s non-expanding estimate (see

[
3
, Theorem 9.1]
), it follows that
m_{j}
is an

-center of

, and hence the proof is complete.

Therefore, we may assume that

B^{*}((x_{j}^{i})^{\prime},d_{x_{j}^{i}})\bigcap\mathscr{C}_{a}\bigcap Z_{T}=\emptyset\qquad\text{for all }i,j.

As in the proof of
[
18
, Theorem 6.31]
; see
[
18

, we then obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B_{j}^{a}}|\mathrm{R}|\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t_{j}}}\leq C(T)\sum_{i}d_{x_{j}^{i}}\leq C(T)\sum_{i}\mu_{a}\bigl(B^{*}((x_{j}^{i})^{\prime},d_{x_{j}^{i}})\bigr),

; see

\displaystyle\int_{B_{j}^{a}}|\mathrm{R}|\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t_{j}}}\leq C(T)\,\mu_{a}\bigl(\mathscr{C}_{a}\cap Z_{[2t_{j}-T,T)}\bigr)=\Psi(j^{-1}),

(3.17)

where

as

. Combining (
3.15
) and (

), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B_{j}}|\mathrm{R}|\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t_{j}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{j}^{1}}|\mathrm{R}|\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t_{j}}}+\int_{B_{j}^{a}}|\mathrm{R}|\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t_{j}}}

\displaystyle\leq C(T)\delta^{\prime}+\Psi(j^{-1}).

(3.18)

By (
3.16
) and (
3.3
), we have shown that in Case 2, the integral
\int_{B_{j}}|\mathrm{R}|\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t_{j}}}
can be made arbitrarily small by first choosing

sufficiently small and then taking

sufficiently large.

If
B_{g_{t_{j}}}(x_{j},\epsilon)=M
for infinitely many

, then for any

, an

-center of
z_{0}
at time

lies in

We claim that

|\mathrm{Rm}|\leq C_{3}\qquad\text{on }B_{g_{t_{j}}}(x_{j},\epsilon/2)

for some constant

independent of

. Indeed, if this were false, then by the same argument as in Case 2 of the proof of Proposition

, we would obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B_{j}}|\mathrm{R}|\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t_{j}}}\geq c_{0}\epsilon>0,

where

is independent of

Since

on

∎

With the aid of Lemma
3.6
, we now prove the surjectivity of the map
\iota
constructed in Proposition

.

Proposition 3.7
.

The map
\iota
constructed in Proposition
3.4
is surjective.

Proof.

We argue by contradiction. Since
(X_{\GH},d_{\GH})
is compact,
(Z_{T},d_{T}^{Z})
is complete by
[
17
, Theorem 1.7(a)]
, and

is an isometric embedding, it follows that
\iota(Z_{T})
is compact. Therefore, if
\iota(Z_{T})\neq X_{\GH}
, then there exist
x^{\infty}\in X_{\GH}
and
r_{0}>0
such that

\displaystyle B(x^{\infty},2r_{0})\cap\iota(Z_{T})=\emptyset.

(3.19)

By the convergence (
3.7
), we can find points
x_{j}\in M
such that
(x_{j},t_{j})
converges to
x^{\infty}
in the Gromov–Hausdorff sense. After passing to a subsequence of
\{t_{j}\}
if necessary, Lemma
3.6
implies that for each
j
there exists a point
y_{j}\in B_{g_{t_{j}}}(x_{j},r_{0})
such that
(y_{j},t_{j})
is an
H(T)
-center of some point
w_{j}\in Z_{T}
.

By the construction of
\iota
, there exists a countable dense subset
\{z_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}\subset Z_{T}
such that
\iota(z_{i})
is defined as the Gromov–Hausdorff limit of a sequence
x_{i,j}^{*}\in M\times\{t_{j}\}
, where
x_{i,j}^{*}
is an
H_{3}
-center of
z_{i}
. Since
(Z_{T},d_{T}^{Z})
is compact, there exists a point
z_{i_{0}}
such that

\displaystyle d_{T}^{Z}(w_{j},z_{i_{0}})\leq r_{0}/2

(3.20)

for infinitely many

. Passing to a further subsequence, we may assume that (

) holds for all

.

By the monotonicity estimate in
[
17
, Proposition 2.12]
, we have

\displaystyle d_{g_{t_{j}}}(y_{j},x_{i_{0},j}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq d_{W_{1}}^{g_{t_{j}}}\bigl(\nu_{z_{i_{0}};t_{j}},\nu_{w_{j};t_{j}}\bigr)+C(T)\sqrt{T-t_{j}}

\displaystyle\leq r_{0}/2+C(T)\sqrt{T-t_{j}}.

It follows that any Gromov–Hausdorff limit of

, denoted by

, satisfies

\displaystyle d_{\GH}\bigl(y^{\infty},\iota(z_{i_{0}})\bigr)\leq r_{0}/2.

On the other hand, since

, we obtain

\displaystyle d_{\GH}\bigl(x^{\infty},\iota(z_{i_{0}})\bigr)\leq 3r_{0}/2,

which contradicts (

). This contradiction completes the proof.
∎

Combining Propositions
3.4
and

, we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 3.8
.

We have the Gromov–Hausdorff convergence

\displaystyle(M,d_{g_{t}}){\mathrel{\to}^{\mathrm{Gromov\text{--}Hausdorff}}_{t\nearrow T}}(Z_{T},d^{Z}_{T}).

Note that Theorem
3.8
remains valid even when

is non-orientable. The only difference is that, in the proof of Proposition

, a

\mathbb{RP}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
. Similarly, in the proof of Lemma

, one must also allow quotient cylindrical neck regions modeled on

. The rest of the argument is unchanged.

3.4  Structure of the singular set at the first singular time

The metric space
(Z_{T},d_{T}^{Z})
admits the following regular-singular decomposition:

\displaystyle Z_{T}=\mathcal{R}_{T}\sqcup\mathcal{S}_{T}.

Note that
\mathcal{S}_{T}=\mathcal{S}
, since all singular points are contained in
Z_{T}
.

Theorem 3.9
.

The singular set
\mathcal{S}_{T}
satisfies the following properties:

(1)

There exists a constant
C>0
, depending on the Ricci flow, such that for all
r>0
,

\bigl|\{y\in Z_{T}\mid d_{T}^{Z}(y,\mathcal{S}_{T})<r\}\bigr|_{T}\leq Cr^{2}.

(3.21)

(2)

The Minkowski dimension of
\mathcal{S}_{T}
with respect to
d_{T}^{Z}
satisfies

\dim_{\mathscr{M}}(\mathcal{S}_{T})\leq 1.

(3.22)

Proof.

(1): By
[
17
, Equation (9.1), Propositions 5.34 and 5.35]
, the set
Z_{[T/2,T]}
can be covered by at most
C_{0}
balls
B^{*}(z_{i},r_{0})
with
z_{i}\in Z_{[T/2,T]}
and
r_{0}:=\sqrt{T}/10
, where
C_{0}
is a constant depending on the Ricci flow.

For each ball
B^{*}(z_{i},r_{0})
,
[
18
, Corollary 6.28]
yields

\displaystyle\bigl|\{r_{\mathrm{Rm}}<r\}\cap B^{*}(z_{i},r_{0})\cap Z_{T}\bigr|_{T}\leq C(T)r^{2}.

Taking the union over all such balls, we obtain

\displaystyle\bigl|\{r_{\mathrm{Rm}}<r\}\cap Z_{T}\bigr|_{T}\leq C(T)C_{0}r^{2}.

(3.23)

By
[
17
, Lemma 6.4]
,

\displaystyle\{y\in Z_{T}\mid d_{T}^{Z}(y,\mathcal{S}_{T})<r\}\subset S^{\prime}:=\{y\in Z_{T}\mid d_{Z}(y,\mathcal{S})<\epsilon_{0}^{-1}r\}.

Since
r_{\mathrm{Rm}}=0
on
\mathcal{S}
, it follows from
[
17
, Proposition 7.6]
that every
y\in S^{\prime}
satisfies

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{Rm}}(y)<C(Y)r.

Hence

\displaystyle S^{\prime}\subset\{y\in Z_{T}\mid r_{\mathrm{Rm}}(y)<C(Y)r\}.

Therefore, (
3.21
) follows from (
3.23
).

(2): It suffices to show that for every sufficiently small
r>0
, the set
\mathcal{S}_{T}
can be covered by at most
Cr^{-1}
balls of radius
r
with respect to
d_{T}^{Z}
.

To this end, fix a time
t
and
r>0
, and define

\displaystyle S_{r}(t):=\{(x,t)\mid r_{\mathrm{Rm}}(x,t)<\delta r\},

where
\delta>0
is a small parameter to be chosen. By the same argument as in Case 2 of the proof of Proposition
3.2
, we can choose
\delta
sufficiently small, depending only on the Ricci flow, such that for every
x_{0}\in S_{r}(t)
,

\displaystyle\int_{B_{g_{t}}(x_{0},r)}|\mathrm{R}|\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}}\geq c_{0}r>0,

where
c_{0}>0
depends only on the Ricci flow and is independent of
t
. In view of Theorem
3.1
, a standard covering argument then shows that
S_{r}(t)
can be covered by at most
C_{0}r^{-1}
balls of radius
r
, where
C_{0}
depends only on the Ricci flow.

Finally, by the Gromov–Hausdorff convergence in Theorem
3.8
, it follows that
\mathcal{S}_{T}
can also be covered by at most
C_{0}r^{-1}
balls of radius
r
with respect to
d_{T}^{Z}
. This proves (
3.22
).
∎

4  Heat kernel estimates on singular Ricci flows

To extend the results of Section
3
to singular Ricci flows, one needs to establish the corresponding heat kernel estimates. Such estimates are standard and well known for closed Ricci flows. Therefore, we focus here on the new aspects arising in the singular setting, and refer to
[
2
,
30
]
for arguments that are analogous to the closed case.

Throughout this section, let
(\mathcal{M},\mathfrak{t},\partial_{\mathfrak{t}},g)
be a singular Ricci flow with a normalized initial condition. We denote by
K(\cdot;\cdot)
the heat kernel on
\mathcal{M}
. We also define the conjugate heat kernel measure on the singular Ricci flow by

\displaystyle\nu_{x;t}=K(x;\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}}(\cdot).

Moreover, we define the
potential function

f_{x}
on
\mathcal{M}_{<\mathfrak{t}(x)}
by

\displaystyle K(x;\cdot)=\frac{1}{(4\pi(\mathfrak{t}(x)-\mathfrak{t}(\cdot)))^{3/2}}e^{-f_{x}(\cdot)}.

4.1  Maximum principles and gradient estimates

We begin with the following maximum principle; compare with
[
27
, Lemma 5.1]
.

Lemma 4.1

(Maximum principle I)
.

Suppose that
u
is a smooth function and
X
is a vector field on
\mathcal{M}_{[a,b]}
such that
|u|+|X|\leq C
. Assume that

\displaystyle(\partial_{\mathfrak{t}}-\Delta+X)u\leq 0

on
\mathcal{M}_{[a,b]}
, and that
u\leq 0
on
\mathcal{M}_{a}
. Then
u\leq 0
on
\mathcal{M}_{[a,b]}
.

Proof.

Set

\square_{X}:=\partial_{\mathfrak{t}}-\Delta+X,

and choose a large constant
L>0
to be determined later. Since
X
is bounded, we compute

\displaystyle\square_{X}(\mathrm{R}+Le^{\mathfrak{t}-a})\geq 2|\mathrm{Ric}|^{2}-C|\nabla\mathrm{R}|+L

on
\mathcal{M}_{[a,b]}
. Since
\mathcal{M}
satisfies the canonical neighborhood assumption,
[
27
, Lemma 5.14]
implies that there exists a constant
r_{0}>0
such that every
x\in\mathcal{M}_{[a,b]}
satisfies

\displaystyle|\nabla\mathrm{R}(x)|\leq C\mathrm{R}(x)^{3/2}

(4.1)

if
\mathrm{R}(x)\geq r_{0}^{-2}
, and

\displaystyle|\nabla\mathrm{R}(x)|\leq Cr_{0}^{-3}

(4.2)

if
\mathrm{R}(x)<r_{0}^{-2}
. Here,
C
in both (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) denotes a universal constant. In the first case, by choosing
r_{0}
smaller if necessary, we may arrange that

\displaystyle 2|\mathrm{Ric}|^{2}\geq C\mathrm{R}^{3/2}

at such points. In the second case, we choose
L
sufficiently large so that

L\geq Cr_{0}^{-3}+1.

Hence we may choose
L>0
such that

\displaystyle\square_{X}(\mathrm{R}+Le^{\mathfrak{t}-a})\geq 1\qquad\text{on }\mathcal{M}_{[a,b]},

(4.3)

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}+L\geq 1\qquad\text{on }\mathcal{M}_{a}.

(4.4)

For any
\epsilon>0
, it follows from (
4.3
) that

\displaystyle\square_{X}\bigl(u-\epsilon(\mathrm{R}+Le^{\mathfrak{t}-a})\bigr)\leq-\epsilon.

(4.5)

Set

u_{\epsilon}:=u-\epsilon(\mathrm{R}+Le^{\mathfrak{t}-a}).

By (
4.4
), we have
u_{\epsilon}<0
on
\mathcal{M}_{a}
. We claim that
u_{\epsilon}\leq 0
on
\mathcal{M}_{[a,b]}
. Indeed, otherwise
u_{\epsilon}
would attain a positive maximum at some point
x_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{(a,b]}
, since
u
is bounded and therefore

\displaystyle\limsup_{\mathrm{R}(x)\to+\infty}u_{\epsilon}(x)=-\infty.

However, at such a maximum point
x_{0}
, we have
\square_{X}u_{\epsilon}\geq 0
, contradicting (
4.5
).

Letting
\epsilon\searrow 0
, we conclude that
u\leq 0
on
\mathcal{M}_{[a,b]}
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

As is clear from the proof of the previous lemma, the assumption that
u
is bounded on
\mathcal{M}_{[a,b]}
can be replaced by the weaker condition

\displaystyle\limsup_{\mathrm{R}(x)\to+\infty}\frac{|u(x)|}{\mathrm{R}(x)}=0.

As an application of Lemma
4.1
, we obtain the following estimate.

Lemma 4.3

(Gradient estimate I)
.

Suppose
u
is a bounded smooth solution of the heat equation
\square u=0
on
\mathcal{M}_{[a,b]}
, and assume that

|\nabla u|\leq 1\qquad\text{on }\mathcal{M}_{a}.

Then

\displaystyle|\nabla u|\leq 1\qquad\text{on }\mathcal{M}_{[a,b]}.

Proof.

First, we have

\square|\nabla u|\leq 0

on
\mathcal{M}_{[a,b]}
. Moreover, if
\mathrm{R}(x)
is sufficiently large, then by the canonical neighborhood assumption and parabolic regularity,

|\nabla u(x)|^{2}\leq C\,\mathrm{R}(x).

Therefore, the conclusion follows from Lemma
4.1
and Remark
4.2
.
∎

Next, we generalize the following estimate from
[
37
]
.

Lemma 4.4

(Gradient estimate II)
.

Suppose
u
is a smooth solution of the heat equation
\square u=0
on
\mathcal{M}_{[a,b]}
such that
0<u<1
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{|\nabla u|}{u}\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mathfrak{t}-a}}\,\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{u}}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that

\delta\leq u\leq 1-\delta

for some constant
\delta>0
. Indeed, we may replace
u
by
u(1-2\delta)+\delta
and then let
\delta\searrow 0
.

Since
u
is bounded, standard parabolic regularity together with the same argument as in the proof of Lemma
4.3
implies that

\displaystyle|\nabla u|^{2}\leq C_{\delta}(1+\mathrm{R})\qquad\text{on }\mathcal{M}_{[a+\delta,b]}.

(4.6)

Next, we have (see
[
30
, Proposition 6.1]
)

\displaystyle\square\left((\mathfrak{t}-a-\delta)\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{u}-u\log\frac{1}{u}\right)=-\frac{2(\mathfrak{t}-a-\delta)}{u}\left|\nabla^{2}u-\frac{\mathrm{d}u\otimes\mathrm{d}u}{u}\right|^{2}\leq 0.

Since
\delta\leq u\leq 1-\delta
, it follows from (
4.6
), Lemma
4.1
, and Remark
4.2
that

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{t}-a-\delta)\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}}{u}\leq u\log\frac{1}{u}\qquad\text{on }\mathcal{M}_{[a+\delta,b]}.

Letting
\delta\searrow 0
yields the desired conclusion.
∎

Next, we generalize the gradient estimate in
[
2
, Theorem 4.1]
to our setting. We recall the function

\Phi(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}(4\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-t^{2}/4}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Note that
\Phi_{t}(x):=\Phi(t^{-1/2}x)
is a solution to the one-dimensional heat equation with initial condition
1_{[0,\infty)}
.

Lemma 4.5

(Gradient estimate III)
.

Suppose
u
is a smooth solution of the heat equation
\square u=0
on
\mathcal{M}_{[a,b]}
such that
0<u<1
. Given a constant
L\geq 0
, assume that

|\nabla\Phi_{L}^{-1}(u)|\leq 1\qquad\text{on }\mathcal{M}_{a}

if
L>0
. Then for any
t\in[a,b]
,

\displaystyle|\nabla\Phi_{L+t-a}^{-1}(u)|\leq 1\qquad\text{on }\mathcal{M}_{t}.

Proof.

As before, we may assume that
\delta\leq u\leq 1-\delta
and
L>0
. Write

u(x)=\Phi_{L+\mathfrak{t}(x)-a}\bigl(h(x)\bigr).

Then both
h
and
|\nabla h|
are uniformly bounded on
\mathcal{M}_{[a,b]}
. A direct computation shows that

\displaystyle\square|\nabla h|^{2}

\displaystyle=-2|\nabla^{2}h|^{2}-\frac{1}{L+\mathfrak{t}-a}\langle\nabla(h^{2}),\nabla|\nabla h|^{2}\rangle+\frac{1}{2(L+\mathfrak{t}-a)}(1-|\nabla h|^{2})|\nabla h|^{2}.

(4.7)

Therefore, if we set

v:=(|\nabla h|^{2}-1)_{+},

then (
4.7
) implies that

\displaystyle\square v+\frac{1}{L+\mathfrak{t}-a}\langle\nabla h^{2},\nabla v\rangle\leq 0.

Applying Lemma
4.1
, we conclude that
v\equiv 0
on
\mathcal{M}_{[a,b]}
, and hence the desired estimate follows.
∎

4.2  Branches, Wasserstein distances, and variance monotonicity

Next, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 4.6

(Branch)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a singular Ricci flow, and let
b>0
be a constant. A
branch
is a subset
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{M}_{[0,b]}
such that for every
t\in[0,b]
, the slice
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{t}
is a connected component of
\mathcal{M}_{t}
. Note that a branch is uniquely determined by its final time-slice
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{b}
; see
[
27
, Proposition 5.17]
.

For a branch
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
and
t\in[0,b]
, we denote by
(\overline{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{t}},d_{g_{t}})
the metric completion of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{t}
with respect to
d_{g_{t}}
. By the canonical neighborhood assumption, this completion adds only finitely many points, each corresponding to an
\epsilon
-horn in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{t}
.

In particular, for any
x_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
, there exists a unique branch
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, called the branch
with respect to
x_{0}
, such that
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{t}
contains the support of
\nu_{x_{0};t}
.

It is clear that Lemmas
4.1
,
4.3
,
4.4
, and
4.5
extend verbatim to any branch of
\mathcal{M}
.

Definition 4.7

(Wasserstein distance and variance)
.

For any
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
and
t\leq\min\{\mathfrak{t}(x),\mathfrak{t}(y)\}
, we define the
W_{1}
-Wasserstein distance
and the
variance
associated with the conjugate heat kernels based at
x
and
y
as follows.

Let
\mathcal{M}^{x}
and
\mathcal{M}^{y}
be branches containing
x
and
y
, respectively. If
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{x}=\mathcal{M}_{t}^{y}
, then we define
d_{W_{1}}^{g_{t}}(\nu_{x;t},\nu_{y;t})
to be the usual
W_{1}
-Wasserstein distance on
(\overline{\mathcal{M}_{t}^{x}},d_{g_{t}})
. If
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{x}\neq\mathcal{M}_{t}^{y}
, then we define

d_{W_{1}}^{g_{t}}(\nu_{x;t},\nu_{y;t})=+\infty.

The variance between two probability measures
\nu_{x;t}
and
\nu_{y;t}
at time
t
is defined by

\mathrm{Var}_{t}(\nu_{x;t},\nu_{y;t}):=\int_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}\int_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}d_{g_{t}}^{2}(x_{1},x_{2})\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x;t}(x_{1})\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{y;t}(x_{2}).

Here we set
d_{g_{t}}(x_{1},x_{2})=+\infty
if
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
lie in different connected components of
\mathcal{M}_{t}
. For simplicity, we write

\mathrm{Var}_{t}(\nu_{x;t})=\mathrm{Var}_{t}(\nu_{x;t},\nu_{x;t}).

By Lemma
4.3
, one obtains the following monotonicity formula exactly as in the smooth case; cf.
[
2
, Lemma 2.7]
.

Lemma 4.8

(Monotonicity I)
.

For any
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
and
t\leq\min\{\mathfrak{t}(x),\mathfrak{t}(y)\}
, the quantity

\displaystyle d_{W_{1}}^{g_{t}}(\nu_{x;t},\nu_{y;t})

is nondecreasing in
t
. In particular, if
\mathfrak{t}(x)=\mathfrak{t}(y)=t_{0}
, then

\displaystyle d_{W_{1}}^{g_{t}}(\nu_{x;t},\nu_{y;t})\leq d_{g_{t_{0}}}(x,y).

Next, we prove the geodesic convexity of each connected component of a time-slice of
\mathcal{M}
.

Proposition 4.9

(Geodesic convexity)
.

For each
T>0
, there exists a constant
r_{0}=r_{0}(T)>0
such that the following holds. Define

\displaystyle\Omega_{r}(t):=\{x\in\mathcal{M}_{t}\mid\mathrm{R}(x)\leq r^{-2}\}.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\prime}
is a connected component of
\mathcal{M}_{t}
with
t\leq T
. Then for any
r\leq r_{0}
, there exists
r^{\prime}<r
such that any two points in
\Omega_{r}(t)\cap\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\prime}
can be connected by a minimizing geodesic contained in
\Omega_{r^{\prime}}(t)\cap\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\prime}
.

In particular,
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\prime}
is geodesically convex.

Proof.

Fix
t\leq T
, a connected component
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\prime}
, and
r\leq r_{0}
, and set

\Omega:=\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\prime}\cap\Omega_{r}(t).

It suffices to prove that there exists a compact set
K\subset\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\prime}
containing
\Omega
such that any two points in
\Omega
can be connected by a minimizing geodesic contained in
K
. We may assume that
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\prime}
is noncompact, since otherwise we may simply take
K=\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\prime}
.

By the canonical neighborhood assumption, if
r_{0}
is chosen sufficiently small, then every connected component of
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\prime}\setminus\Omega
is one of the following:

(A)

an
\epsilon
-tube whose two boundary components lie in
\partial\Omega
;

(B)

an
\epsilon
-horn whose unique boundary component lies in
\partial\Omega
;

(C)

an
\epsilon
-cap whose unique boundary component lies in
\partial\Omega
.

See
[
26
, Definition 58.2]
for the relevant definitions. In each case, the boundary of a component of
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\prime}\setminus\Omega
consists of central spheres of
\epsilon
-necks.

We now enlarge
\Omega
by adjoining all components of type (A) and (C) above, and denote the resulting compact set by
\Omega^{\prime}
. Note that every component of
\partial\Omega^{\prime}
is the center of an
\epsilon
-neck, and the intersection of
\Omega^{\prime}
with such an
\epsilon
-neck corresponds to
(-\epsilon^{-1},0]\times S^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}\times S^{2}
. We then enlarge
\Omega^{\prime}
to a compact set
K
so that its intersection with each such
\epsilon
-neck corresponds to

(-\epsilon^{-1},10^{10}]\times S^{2}.

It is clear that every connected component of
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\prime}\setminus K
is an
\epsilon
-horn.

It remains to show that this set
K
has the desired property. Suppose not. Then there exist points
x_{0},y_{0}\in\Omega
such that no minimizing geodesic joining
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
is contained in
K
. By definition, there exists a sequence of curves
\gamma_{i}(s)
in
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\prime}
, parametrized by arclength, joining
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
such that

\displaystyle S_{i}:=\mathrm{length}(\gamma_{i})\to d_{g_{t}}(x_{0},y_{0}).

If, after passing to a subsequence, the curves
\gamma_{i}
are all contained in
K
, then by taking a limit we obtain a minimizing geodesic
\gamma_{\infty}
in
K
connecting
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
, contradicting our assumption. Hence we may assume that
\gamma_{i}
is not contained in
K
for any
i
.

Figure 2:
A shorter curve
\tilde{\gamma}_{i}
.

For each
i
, by the construction of
K
, there exist parameters

0\leq s_{1}<s_{2}<s_{3}<s_{4}\leq S_{i}

such that
\gamma_{i}(s_{1})
and
\gamma_{i}(s_{4})
lie on some component
\Sigma
of
\partial\Omega
, while
\gamma_{i}(s_{2})
and
\gamma_{i}(s_{3})
lie on the corresponding component
\Sigma^{\prime}
of
\partial K
. Moreover,
\gamma_{i}
, when restricted to
[0,s_{2}]\cup[s_{3},S_{i}]
, is contained in
K
. Here,
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
correspond to
\{0\}\times S^{2}
and
\{10^{10}\}\times S^{2}
, respectively, inside an
\epsilon
-neck. Comparing with the standard cylinder, we see that

\displaystyle(s_{2}-s_{1})+(s_{4}-s_{3})\geq 10^{10}\sigma,

where
\sigma
is the scale of the
\epsilon
-neck. On the other hand, the points
\gamma_{i}(s_{1})
and
\gamma_{i}(s_{4})
can be joined by a curve lying entirely in
\Sigma
whose length is at most
10^{5}\sigma
. Replacing the portion of
\gamma_{i}
between
\gamma_{i}(s_{1})
and
\gamma_{i}(s_{4})
by this shorter curve, we obtain a new curve
\tilde{\gamma}_{i}
that is contained in
K
and has strictly shorter length than
\gamma_{i}
; see Figure
2
.

As before, taking a limit of the curves
\tilde{\gamma}_{i}
yields a minimizing geodesic in
K
, contradicting our assumption. This proves the proposition.
∎

Lemma 4.10

(Monotonicity II)
.

For any
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
and any

s<t\leq\min\{\mathfrak{t}(x),\mathfrak{t}(y)\},

we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{s}(\nu_{x;s},\nu_{y;s})+H_{3}s\leq\mathrm{Var}_{t}(\nu_{x;t},\nu_{y;t})+H_{3}t,

where
H_{3}=\pi^{2}+4
.

Proof.

Fix
x_{0},y_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
and times

s_{0}<t_{0}\leq\min\{\mathfrak{t}(x_{0}),\mathfrak{t}(y_{0})\}.

If

\mathrm{Var}_{t_{0}}(\nu_{x_{0};t_{0}},\nu_{y_{0};t_{0}})=+\infty,

then the desired inequality is trivial. Hence we may assume that

\mathrm{Var}_{t_{0}}(\nu_{x_{0};t_{0}},\nu_{y_{0};t_{0}})<+\infty.

By the definition of the variance, this implies that the supports of

\nu_{x_{0};t_{0}}
and
\nu_{y_{0};t_{0}}
lie in the same connected component of

\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}}
. Denote this component by
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{t_{0}}
, and let

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{M}_{[s_{0},t_{0}]}

be the branch determined by
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{t_{0}}
. By the uniqueness of branches
and by the semigroup property of the heat kernel, for every

t\in[s_{0},t_{0}]
the measures
\nu_{x_{0};t}
and
\nu_{y_{0};t}
are supported in

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{t}
.

On the fiber product

\mathcal{P}:=\{(p,q)\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\mid\mathfrak{t}(p)=\mathfrak{t}(q)\in[s_{0},t_{0}]\},

endowed on each time-slice with the product metric
g_{t}\oplus g_{t}
, the function

(p,q)\longmapsto d_{g_{t}}^{2}(p,q)

satisfies

\displaystyle(\partial_{\mathfrak{t}}-\Delta_{p}-\Delta_{q})d_{g_{t}}^{2}(p,q)\geq-H_{3}

(4.8)

in the barrier and viscosity sense. Indeed, the proof of
[
2
, Theorem 3.5]
is local: one applies the second variation formula along a minimizing geodesic between
p
and
q
at the fixed time
t
. Such a minimizing geodesic exists in
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\prime}
by Proposition
4.9
.

Let
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{M}_{s_{0}}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{M}_{s_{0}}^{\prime})
satisfy

0\leq\phi(p,q)\leq d_{g_{s_{0}}}^{2}(p,q).

Define, for
(p,q)\in\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\prime}
and
t\in[s_{0},t_{0}]
,

u(p,q,t):=\iint_{\mathcal{M}_{s_{0}}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{M}_{s_{0}}^{\prime}}\phi(a,b)K(p;a)K(q;b)\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{s_{0}}}(a)\mathrm{d}V_{g_{s_{0}}}(b)-H_{3}(t-s_{0}),

where
K(p;a)
denotes the heat kernel from the spacetime point
p
back to the point
a\in\mathcal{M}_{s_{0}}^{\prime}
. Then

(\partial_{\mathfrak{t}}-\Delta_{p}-\Delta_{q})u=-H_{3},\qquad u(\cdot,\cdot,s_{0})=\phi.

By (
4.8
), the function
u-d_{g_{t}}^{2}
is a viscosity subsolution of the heat equation on
\mathcal{P}
and is nonpositive at time
s_{0}
.

Applying the product maximum principle in the same way as Lemma
4.1
, using the barrier
\mathrm{R}(p)+\mathrm{R}(q)+Le^{\mathfrak{t}-s_{0}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle u(p,q,t)\leq d_{g_{t}}^{2}(p,q)\qquad\text{for all }t\in[s_{0},t_{0}].

(4.9)

Using the semigroup property of the heat kernel and (
4.9
), we obtain

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{M}_{s_{0}}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{M}_{s_{0}}^{\prime}}\phi\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x_{0};s_{0}}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{y_{0};s_{0}}+H_{3}s_{0}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}}^{\prime}}u\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x_{0};t_{0}}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{y_{0};t_{0}}+H_{3}t_{0}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}}^{\prime}}d_{g_{t_{0}}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x_{0};t_{0}}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{y_{0};t_{0}}+H_{3}t_{0}.

(4.10)

Finally, take an increasing sequence of nonnegative compactly supported smooth functions
\phi_{k}
with
\phi_{k}\leq d_{g_{s_{0}}}^{2}
and

\phi_{k}\nearrow d_{g_{s_{0}}}^{2}
almost everywhere with respect to
\nu_{x_{0};s_{0}}\otimes\nu_{y_{0};s_{0}}
. Letting
k\to\infty
in (
4.2
) and applying monotone convergence yields the desired inequality.
∎

The following corollary follows immediately from Lemma
4.10
(cf.
[
2
, Corollary 3.8]
).

Corollary 4.11

(
H_{3}
-concentration)
.

For any
x\in\mathcal{M}
and any
t\leq\mathfrak{t}(x)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{t}(\nu_{x;t})\leq H_{3}\bigl(\mathfrak{t}(x)-t\bigr).

As in
[
2
, Definition 3.10]
, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 4.12
.

A point
z\in\mathcal{M}_{t}
is called an
H
-center
of
x_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
for a constant
H>0
if
t<\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})
and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{t}(\delta_{z},\nu_{x_{0};t})\leq H\bigl(\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})-t\bigr).

By Corollary
4.11
, an
H_{3}
-center exists for every
t<\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})
.

The following result can be proved in the same way as
[
2
, Propositions 3.12 and 3.13]
.

Proposition 4.13
.

Any two
H_{3}
-centers
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{t}
of
x_{0}
satisfy

\displaystyle d_{g_{t}}(z_{1},z_{2})\leq 2\sqrt{H_{3}\bigl(\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})-t\bigr)}.

Moreover, if
z\in\mathcal{M}_{t}
is an
H
-center of
x_{0}
, then for any
L>0
,

\displaystyle\nu_{x_{0};t}\Bigl(B_{g_{t}}\bigl(z,\sqrt{LH(\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})-t)}\bigr)\Bigr)\geq 1-L^{-1}.

Next, we establish estimates for the potential function and higher-order estimates for the conjugate heat kernel, which will play an important role in justifying integration by parts.

Lemma 4.14
.

For any
x_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
and any
t<\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})
, let
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
be the branch associated with
x_{0}
. Suppose that
U\subset\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\prime}
is an
\epsilon
-neck of scale
r
such that

t\leq\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})-2r^{2}.

Then the following estimates hold.

(i)

For any
k\geq 0
and
m\geq 0
, on
U
we have

\displaystyle|\nabla^{k}K(x_{0};\cdot)|\leq C_{m,k}r^{m-k},

where
C_{m,k}
is a constant depending on
x_{0}
,
m
, and
k
.

(ii)

On
U
, we have

\displaystyle|\nabla f_{x_{0}}|^{2}\leq Cr^{-2}(1+f_{x_{0}}),

where
C
is a constant depending on
x_{0}
.

(iii)

For any
k\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle|\nabla^{k}f_{x_{0}}|\leq C_{k}r^{-k}e^{C_{k}\sqrt{f_{x_{0}}}},

where
C_{k}
is a constant depending on
k
and
x_{0}
.

Proof.

For simplicity, write

w=K(x_{0};\cdot),\qquad f=f_{x_{0}}.

(i)
Since
\square^{*}w=0
, the conclusion follows directly from standard parabolic estimates together with Theorem
2.9
.

(ii)
By Theorem
2.8
(c), we have
w\leq C_{0}
on
U
. Since
\square^{*}w=0
on
\mathcal{M}_{[t,t+r^{2}]}
, it follows from
[
16
, Theorem 16.52]
that

\displaystyle\frac{|\nabla w|^{2}}{w^{2}}\leq C_{1}r^{-2}\left(1+\log\frac{C_{0}}{w}\right)

on
U
. In view of the definition of
f
, this proves (ii).

(iii)
In the proof, all constants
Q_{i}
depend on
x_{0}
.

Fix
z\in U
. By (ii), we have

|\nabla\sqrt{f}|\leq Q_{1}r^{-1}.

Hence there exists a constant
Q_{2}>1
such that

\displaystyle f(z)-Q_{2}(\sqrt{f(z)}+1)\leq f(y)\leq f(z)+Q_{2}(\sqrt{f(z)}+1)

for any
y\in B_{g_{t}}(z,2r)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle Q_{3}^{-1}w(z)e^{-Q_{2}\sqrt{f(z)}}\leq w(y)\leq Q_{3}w(z)e^{Q_{2}\sqrt{f(z)}}

(4.11)

for some constant
Q_{3}>1
and all
y\in B_{g_{t}}(z,2r)
.

Since
\square^{*}w=0
, it follows from the parabolic Harnack inequality and (
4.11
) that

\displaystyle w\leq Q_{4}e^{Q_{4}\sqrt{f(z)}}w(z)\quad\text{on }B_{g_{t}}(z,r)\times[t,t+r^{2}].

By the standard parabolic interior estimate, we then obtain

\displaystyle|\nabla^{k}w|(z)\leq Q^{\prime}_{k}r^{-k}e^{Q_{4}\sqrt{f(z)}}w(z),

where
Q_{k}^{\prime}
is a constant depending on
x_{0}
and
k
. Using the identity

f=-\log w-\frac{3}{2}\log\bigl(4\pi(\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})-t)\bigr)

and differentiating repeatedly, we conclude by induction that

\displaystyle|\nabla^{k}f|(z)\leq C_{k}r^{-k}e^{C_{k}\sqrt{f(z)}}.

This proves (iii).
∎

Next, we prove the following identity, which will be used repeatedly.

Lemma 4.15
.

Suppose
u
is a smooth spacetime function defined in a neighborhood of
\mathcal{M}_{t}
such that

|u|+|\nabla u|\leq L(1+\mathrm{R}^{L})

for some constant
L>1
. Then for any
x_{0}
with
\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})\geq t
, if both
|\square u|
and
|\Delta u|
are integrable with respect to
\mathrm{d}\nu_{x_{0};t}
, then

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathfrak{t}}\int_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}u\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x_{0};t}=\int_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}\square u\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x_{0};t}.

Proof.

For simplicity, write
w=K(x_{0};\cdot)
.

We first show that the growth assumption on
u
implies that
u
is integrable with respect to
\mathrm{d}\nu_{x_{0};t}
. Indeed, by Theorem
2.9
, we have

w\leq C\mathrm{R}^{-2L}.

On the other hand, the total volume of
\mathcal{M}_{t}
is finite by
[
27
, Proposition 5.5(1)]
. Hence the assumption on
u
implies that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}|u|\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x_{0};t}<\infty.

Similarly, Lemma
4.14
(i) implies that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}|u\,\Delta w|\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}}<\infty.

A direct computation shows that for any domain
\Omega\subset\subset\mathcal{M}_{t}
,

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathfrak{t}}\int_{\Omega}u\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x_{0};t}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(\square u)w+(\Delta u)w-u(\Delta w)\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\square u\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x_{0};t}+\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\partial u}{\partial\vec{n}}w-u\frac{\partial w}{\partial\vec{n}}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{t},

(4.12)

where
\vec{n}
is the outward unit normal along
\partial\Omega
, and
\mathrm{d}\sigma_{t}
is the induced area form on
\partial\Omega
.

As in the proof of Proposition
4.9
, we can find an exhaustion of the branch
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
associated with
x_{0}
by domains
\Omega_{i}
such that every component of
\partial\Omega_{i}
is a central two-sphere
of an
\epsilon_{i}
-neck of scale
r_{i}
, where
r_{i},\epsilon_{i}\to 0
. For each
i
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{\mathfrak{t}}\int_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}u\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x_{0};t}-\partial_{\mathfrak{t}}\int_{\Omega_{i}}u\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x_{0};t}\right|+\left|\int_{\mathcal{M}_{t}\setminus\Omega_{i}}\square u\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x_{0};t}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathcal{M}_{t}\setminus\Omega_{i}}2|(\square u)|\,w+|(\Delta u)w|+|u(\Delta w)|\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}}\right|\xrightarrow[i\to\infty]{}0,

(4.13)

since

|(\square u)|\,w+|(\Delta u)w|+|u(\Delta w)|

is integrable with respect to
\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}}
.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\partial\Omega_{i}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial\vec{n}}w-u\frac{\partial w}{\partial\vec{n}}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{t}\right|\leq\int_{\partial\Omega_{i}}|\nabla u|\,w+|u|\,|\nabla w|\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{t}.

Note that
\sigma_{t}(\partial\Omega_{i})\leq Cr_{i}^{2}
. Using the assumption on
u
together with Lemma
4.14
(i), we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\left|\int_{\partial\Omega_{i}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial\vec{n}}w-u\frac{\partial w}{\partial\vec{n}}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{t}\right|=0.

Combining this with (
4.2
) and (
4.2
) yields the desired identity.
∎

Next, we prove the following result, which extends Theorem
2.8
(b).

Lemma 4.16
.

For any
x_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
and any
h\in C(\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{[a,\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})]})
satisfying

|h|\leq L(1+\mathrm{R}^{L})

for some constant
L>1
, where
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is the branch associated with
x_{0}
, we have

\lim_{t\nearrow\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})}\int_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}h\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x_{0};t}=h(x_{0}).

Proof.

Set
t_{0}=\mathfrak{t}(x_{0})
and

\Omega_{r}=\{x\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{t_{0}}\mid\mathrm{R}(x)\leq r^{-2}\}.

As in the proof of Proposition
4.9
, we may enlarge
\Omega_{r}
slightly to a domain
\Omega_{r}^{\prime}
such that every component of
\partial\Omega_{r}^{\prime}
is the center of an
\epsilon
-neck of scale
r
. Using the worldlines through
\Omega_{r}^{\prime}
, one constructs a product domain

U_{r}=\Omega_{r}^{\prime}\times[t_{0}-\delta_{r},t_{0}].

If
\delta_{r}
is chosen sufficiently small, then for any
t\in[t_{0}-\delta_{r},t_{0}]
, the slice
\Omega_{r}^{\prime}\times\{t\}
contains all points with
\mathrm{R}\leq 2r^{-2}
, and every boundary component is the center of an
\epsilon
-neck of scale in
[0.9r,1.1r]
. Similarly, one defines
\Omega_{r/2}
,
\Omega_{r/2}^{\prime}
, and

U_{r/2}=\Omega_{r/2}^{\prime}\times[t_{0}-\delta_{r},t_{0}],

which contain
\Omega_{r}
,
\Omega_{r}^{\prime}
, and
U_{r}
, respectively.

Choose a cutoff function
\psi
supported in
U_{r/2}
such that
\psi=1
on
U_{r}
. Then Theorem
2.8
(b) implies that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\nearrow t_{0}}\int_{U_{r/2}\cap\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\prime}}h\psi\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x_{0};t}=h(x_{0}).

(4.14)

On the other hand, by Theorem
2.9
, we have

K(x_{0};\cdot)\leq C\mathrm{R}^{-2L}\qquad\text{on }\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\prime}\setminus U_{r}.

Therefore, using the growth assumption on
h
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0}\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\delta_{r},t_{0}]}\int_{\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\prime}\setminus U_{r}}|h|\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x_{0};t}=0.

Combining this with (
4.14
) completes the proof.
∎

4.3  Pointed entropies on singular Ricci flows

Next, we introduce the following pointed entropy quantities, originally defined in
[
25
]
for closed Ricci flows.

Definition 4.20
.

Perelman’s
\mathcal{W}
-entropy and the Nash entropy based at
x_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}}
are defined, for any
\tau\in(0,t_{0}]
, by

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{x_{0}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}-\tau}}\Bigl(\tau(2\Delta f_{x_{0}}-|\nabla f_{x_{0}}|^{2}+\mathrm{R})+f_{x_{0}}-3\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x_{0};t_{0}-\tau},

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{x_{0}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}-\tau}}f_{x_{0}}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x_{0};t_{0}-\tau}-\frac{3}{2},

where
f_{x_{0}}
is the potential function based at
x_{0}
.

Lemma 4.21
.

For any
x_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}}
and any
\tau\in(0,t_{0}]
, both
\mathcal{N}_{x_{0}}(\tau)
and
\mathcal{W}_{x_{0}}(\tau)
are well-defined and finite.

Proof.

On
\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}-\tau}
, the function
f_{x_{0}}
is bounded from below; see Theorem
2.8
(c). The finiteness of
\mathcal{N}_{x_{0}}(\tau)
then follows from Theorem
4.19
and a standard argument; see
[
30
, Proposition 9.2]
.

By the differential Harnack inequality (see
[
29
, Corollary 5.8]
),
\mathcal{W}_{x_{0}}(\tau)
is well-defined and nonpositive. To show that it is finite, let
\{\Omega_{i}\}
be a sequence of domains exhausting
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{t_{0}-\tau}
, where
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is the branch associated with
x_{0}
, such that every component of
\partial\Omega_{i}
is a central two-sphere
of an
\epsilon_{i}
-neck of scale
r_{i}
, with
r_{i},\epsilon_{i}\to 0
.

Set
v=\tau(2\Delta f_{x_{0}}-|\nabla f_{x_{0}}|^{2}+\mathrm{R})+f_{x_{0}}-3
. Then we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{i}}v\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x_{0};t_{0}-\tau}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{i}}\Bigl(\tau(|\nabla f_{x_{0}}|^{2}+\mathrm{R})+f_{x_{0}}-3\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x_{0};t_{0}-\tau}+2\tau\int_{\partial\Omega_{i}}\frac{\partial f_{x_{0}}}{\partial\vec{n}}K(x_{0};\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{t_{0}-\tau}.

(4.15)

As in the proof of Lemma
4.15
, we estimate

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\partial\Omega_{i}}\frac{\partial f_{x_{0}}}{\partial\vec{n}}K(x_{0};\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{t_{0}-\tau}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\partial\Omega_{i}}|\nabla f_{x_{0}}|\,K(x_{0};\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{t_{0}-\tau}

\displaystyle\leq Cr_{i}\sup_{\partial\Omega_{i}}\sqrt{f_{x_{0}}+1}\,K(x_{0};\cdot),

where in the last inequality we used Lemma
4.14
(ii).

By Theorem
2.9
, it follows that

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\int_{\partial\Omega_{i}}\frac{\partial f_{x_{0}}}{\partial\vec{n}}K(x_{0};\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{t_{0}-\tau}=0.

Combining this with (
4.15
), we conclude that
\mathcal{W}_{x_{0}}(\tau)
is finite.
∎

Remark 4.22
.

After integration by parts, justified as in the proof of Lemma
4.21
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{x_{0}}(\tau)=\int_{\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}-\tau}}\Bigl(\tau(|\nabla f_{x_{0}}|^{2}+\mathrm{R})+f_{x_{0}}-3\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x_{0};t_{0}-\tau}.

4.15

Proposition 4.23

For any
x_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}}

4.4  Heat kernel upper and lower bounds

First, as in the case of closed Ricci flows; cf.
[
34
, Corollaries 9.4, 9.5]
, we have the following lower bound for the heat kernel by using the differential Harnack inequality (see
[
29
, Corollary 5.8]
). Recall that the reduced distance between
x\in\mathcal{M}_{t}
and
y\in\mathcal{M}_{s}
, with

\displaystyle l_{x}(y)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{t-s}}\inf\left\{\mathcal{L}(\gamma)\;:\;\gamma(m)\in\mathcal{M}_{m}\text{ for }m\in[s,t],\ \gamma\text{ connects }y\text{ to }x\right\},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\gamma)=\int_{s}^{t}\sqrt{t-m}\Bigl(|\pi_{S}\gamma^{\prime}(m)|^{2}+\mathrm{R}(\gamma(m))\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}m,

and
\pi_{S}\gamma^{\prime}(m)
denotes the spatial component of
\gamma^{\prime}(m)
in
T\mathcal{M}_{m}
. Note that a reduced geodesic exists for such
x
and
y
, since
\mathcal{M}
satisfies the canonical neighborhood assumption and
\mathrm{R}
is a proper function on
\mathcal{M}
; see also
[
28
, Corollary 5.13]
.

Theorem 4.28

(Heat kernel estimate I)
.

For any
x\in\mathcal{M}_{t}
and

with

, we have

\displaystyle K(x;y)\geq\frac{\exp\bigl(-l_{x}(y)\bigr)}{(4\pi(t-s))^{3/2}}.

As an application of Theorem
4.28
, one can prove the following result exactly as in
[

, Proposition 2.21]

.

For any

along its worldline. If

is an

-center of

\displaystyle d_{g_{t}}(x_{0}(t),z)\leq C\sqrt{t_{0}-t}.

|\mathrm{R}|\leq R_{0}r^{-2}

\displaystyle d_{g_{t}}(x_{0}(t),y(t))\leq d_{g_{t_{0}}}(x_{0},y(t_{0}))+C\sqrt{t_{0}-t}.

4.25

Proposition 4.31

For any

We now state the following sharp upper bound for the heat kernel. This was proved originally in
[
2
, Theorem 7.2]
for closed Ricci flows, with the coefficient
4+\epsilon
replaced by
8+\epsilon
, and was later improved in
[
17
, Theorem 2.5(ii)]
. Note that the proof carries over verbatim from
[
30
, Theorem 11.4]
. The key step is to establish an analogue of
[

, Lemma 11.3]
, whose proof is based on a contradiction argument.

Theorem 4.32

(Heat kernel estimate III)
.

For any
x\in\mathcal{M}_{t}
,
y\in\mathcal{M}_{s}
with
t>s\geq 0
, and any
\epsilon>0
, if
\mathrm{R}\geq\mathrm{R}_{\min}
on
\mathcal{M}_{s}
, then there exists a constant
C=C(\epsilon,\mathrm{R}_{\min}(t-s))>0
such that

\displaystyle K(x;y)\leq\frac{C\exp\bigl(-\mathcal{N}_{x}(t-s)\bigr)}{(t-s)^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{d_{g_{s}}^{2}(z,y)}{(4+\epsilon)(t-s)}\right),

where
z\in\mathcal{M}_{s}
is any
H_{3}
-center of
x
.

Proof.

We sketch the proof following the presentation in
[
30
, Theorem 11.4]
.

Fix
y_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{s_{0}}
. We first claim the following analogue of
[
30
, Lemma 11.3]
: for any
x\in\mathcal{M}_{t}
with
t\geq s_{0}
,

\displaystyle K(x;y_{0})\leq\bar{Q}\frac{\exp\bigl(-\mathcal{N}_{s_{0}}^{*}(x)\bigr)}{(t-s_{0})^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{d_{g_{s_{0}}}^{2}(z,y_{0})}{\bar{Q}(t-s_{0})}\right),

(4.18)

where
z\in\mathcal{M}_{s_{0}}
is any
H_{3}
-center of
x
, and
\bar{Q}
is a constant depending on
\mathrm{R}_{\min}(t-s_{0})
.

Suppose that (
4.18
) fails for some
x_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{t_{0}}
. Then, setting
Q_{k}=2^{k}\bar{Q}
and

t_{k}:=8^{-k}(t_{0}-s_{0})+s_{0},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N},

one may apply the point-picking lemma from
[
30
, Lemma 11.2]
to obtain sequences
x_{k}\in\mathcal{M}_{t_{k}}
and
z_{k}\in\mathcal{M}_{s_{0}}
satisfying

\displaystyle K(x_{k};y_{0})\geq Q_{k}\frac{\exp\bigl(-\mathcal{N}_{s_{0}}^{*}(x_{k})\bigr)}{(t_{k}-s_{0})^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{d_{g_{s_{0}}}^{2}(z_{k},y_{0})}{Q_{k}(t_{k}-s_{0})}\right),

(4.19)

where
z_{k}
is an
H_{3}
-center of
x_{k}
. Set

d_{k}:=d_{g_{s_{0}}}(z_{k},y_{0}),\qquad r_{k}:=\sqrt{t_{k}-s_{0}}.

Then Theorem
4.30
, together with (
4.19
), implies that

\displaystyle\frac{d_{k}}{r_{k}}\geq\sqrt{Q_{k}(\log Q_{k}-C_{0})}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}+\infty

(4.20)

for some constant
C_{0}>0
.

For
k
sufficiently large, we may assume that
r_{\mathrm{Rm}}(y_{0})\gg r_{k}
. For each
k
and each
t\in[s_{0},s_{0}+r_{k}^{2}]
, let
z_{k}^{t}\in\mathcal{M}_{t}
be an
H_{3}
-center of
x_{k}
. Also, let
y_{0}(t)\in\mathcal{M}_{t}
denote the point obtained by flowing
y_{0}
along its worldline.

Claim.
There exists a constant
C_{1}>0
such that

d_{g_{t}}(z_{k}^{t},y_{0}(t))\geq d_{k}-C_{1}r_{k}.

By Proposition
4.29
(i), for any
t\in[s_{0},s_{0}+r_{k}^{2}]
, the point
y_{0}
is an
H
-center of
y_{0}(t)
for some constant
H
independent of
k
and
t
. By Lemma
4.8
, this implies

\displaystyle d_{g_{t}}(z_{k}^{t},y_{0}(t))

\displaystyle\geq d_{W_{1}}^{g_{t}}(\nu_{z_{k}^{t};t},\delta_{y_{0}(t)})-\sqrt{H_{3}(t_{k}-t)}

\displaystyle\geq d_{W_{1}}^{g_{s_{0}}}(\nu_{z_{k}^{t};s_{0}},\nu_{y_{0}(t);s_{0}})-\sqrt{H_{3}(t_{k}-t)}

\displaystyle\geq d_{k}-\sqrt{H_{3}(t_{k}-s_{0})}-\sqrt{H(t-s_{0})}-\sqrt{H_{3}(t_{k}-t)}

\displaystyle\geq d_{k}-C_{1}r_{k},

which proves the claim.

Now define

U_{k}(t):=B_{g_{t}}(y_{0}(t),r_{k}),\qquad t\in[s_{0},s_{0}+r_{k}^{2}].

Then Theorem
4.19
yields

\displaystyle\int_{U_{k}(t)}K(x_{k};\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\exp\left(-\frac{(d_{g_{t}}(z_{k}^{t},y_{0}(t))-r_{k})^{2}}{5r_{k}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\exp\left(-\frac{d_{k}^{2}}{6r_{k}^{2}}\right),

(4.21)

where in the last inequality we used (
4.20
) together with the claim above.

Since
\square^{*}K(x_{k};\cdot)=0
, it follows from (
4.4
), the fact that
r_{\mathrm{Rm}}(y_{0})\gg r_{k}
, and the standard mean value inequality that

\displaystyle K(x_{k};y_{0})\leq C_{4}\exp\left(-\frac{d_{k}^{2}}{6r_{k}^{2}}\right).

This contradicts (
4.19
), in view of (
4.20
).

Once (
4.18
) is established, one can follow exactly the same argument as in
[
30
, Theorem 11.4]
to complete the proof.
∎

5.3  Tangent flows at singular points

First, we prove the following result.

Lemma 5.11
.

For any
x_{0}\in Z_{t_{0}}
and any

with

, suppose that
z\in\mathcal{R}_{t_{0}-r^{2}}
is an

-center of

. Then there exists a point
w\in\mathcal{R}_{t_{0}-r^{2}}
with

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}(w)\leq C(T)r^{-2}.

Proof.

Throughout the proof, all constants
c_{i}
and
C_{i}
depend only on
T
. By Proposition
4.26
(b), we have

\displaystyle|B_{g_{t_{0}-r^{2}}}(z,\sqrt{2H_{3}}\,r)|_{t_{0}-r^{2}}\geq c_{1}r^{3}>0.

(5.4)

Set

B:=B_{g_{t_{0}-r^{2}}}(z,\sqrt{2H_{3}}\,r),

and suppose for contradiction that the conclusion fails.

By the canonical neighborhood assumption, for each point
x\in B
,
(\mathcal{R}_{t_{0}-r^{2}},g_{t_{0}-r^{2}},x)
is
\epsilon
-close to one of the following: (a) an
\epsilon
-neck; (b) an
\epsilon
-cap; or (c) a closed manifold of constant positive sectional curvature. Note that case (c) cannot occur, since otherwise we would have

|B|_{t_{0}-r^{2}}\leq C\mathrm{R}(x)^{-3/2},

contradicting (

). Therefore, under our assumption,
B
is contained in a subset

of one of the following types:

(i)

is an
\epsilon
-tube, diffeomorphic to
S^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
or
S^{2}\times S^{1}
;

(ii)

B^{\prime}
is a capped
\epsilon
-tube, diffeomorphic to

or
\mathbb{RP}^{3}\setminus\overline{D}^{3}

(iii)

is a doubly capped
\epsilon
-tube, diffeomorphic to

,
\mathbb{RP}^{3}
, or
\mathbb{RP}^{3}\#\mathbb{RP}^{3}

Set

By assumption, we have

. For any two points

implies that there exists a minimizing geodesic

\mathrm{length}(\gamma)=d_{g_{t_{0}-r^{2}}}(x,y)\leq 2\sqrt{2H_{3}}\,r.

Using a covering argument as in Case 2 of the proof of Proposition

\displaystyle|B|_{t_{0}-r^{2}}\leq|B^{\prime}|_{t_{0}-r^{2}}\leq C_{2}r_{0}^{2}r,

. This completes the proof.
∎

Next, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 5.12

(Tangent flow)
.

For any
x_{0}\in Z_{t_{0}}
, a

is a Ricci flow obtained as the pointed Cheeger–Gromov limit of

(\mathcal{R},r_{i}^{-2}g_{r_{i}^{2}t+t_{0}},z_{i})

, where

-center of

.

Given a sequence
r_{i}\searrow 0
, Lemma

implies that we can find points

such that

\mathrm{R}(w_{i})\leq Cr_{i}^{-2}.

After passing to a subsequence, there are two possibilities.

Case 1.

\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{R}(w_{i})}{r_{i}^{-2}}=0.

In this case, by the canonical neighborhood assumption (see
[
27
, Lemma 3.1]
), the limiting Ricci flow of

(\mathcal{R},r_{i}^{-2}g_{r_{i}^{2}t+t_{0}},w_{i})

is the static Euclidean flow
(\mathbb{R}^{3},g_{E})
.

Case 2.

\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{R}(w_{i})}{r_{i}^{-2}}=L>0.

In this case, the canonical neighborhood assumption again implies that the limit of

(\mathcal{R},r_{i}^{-2}g_{r_{i}^{2}t+t_{0}},z_{i})

is the Ricci flow associated with a Ricci shrinker, since the Nash entropy of the limiting Ricci flow at the base point is constant for all scales. Thus, the Ricci shrinker must be one of

S^{3}/\Gamma,\qquad S^{2}\times_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}\mathbb{R},\qquad S^{2}\times\mathbb{R},

each equipped with its standard metric.

Thus the tangent flow in Definition

is independent of the choice of the sequence
\{r_{i}\}
and of the

[

\iota:\mathcal{C}\to Z^{\prime}

such that

\iota(\mathcal{C})=Z^{\prime}_{(-\infty,0)}.

Since
Z^{\prime}
is complete, this embedding extends uniquely to an isometric embedding

\iota:\overline{\mathcal{C}}\to Z^{\prime}.

Claim 1.

\iota(\overline{\mathcal{C}})=Z^{\prime}_{(-\infty,0]}
.

Let
y^{\prime}\in Z^{\prime}_{0}
. Choose points
y_{i}\in Z
converging to
y^{\prime}
with respect to the pointed Gromov–Hausdorff convergence in (
5.5
). Fix a small constant
\theta\in(0,1)
, and let
w_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{t_{0}-\theta r_{i}^{2}}
be an
H_{3}
-center of
y_{i}
. After passing to a further subsequence, we may assume that
w_{i}
converges to some point
w^{\prime}\in Z^{\prime}_{-\theta}
in the pointed Gromov–Hausdorff sense. Then Proposition
5.3
(2) implies that

\displaystyle d_{Z^{\prime}}(y^{\prime},w^{\prime})\leq C(T)\sqrt{\theta}.

(5.6)

By the non-expanding estimate (see
[
3
, Theorem 9.1]
), it follows that

d_{g_{t_{0}-\theta r_{i}^{2}}}(w_{i},z_{i})\leq Cr_{i},

where
z_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{t_{0}-\theta r_{i}^{2}}
is an
H_{3}
-center of
x_{0}
. Hence, by the convergence in Definition
5.12
, there exists a point
w_{\infty}\in\mathcal{C}_{-\theta}
such that

\iota(w_{\infty})=w^{\prime}.

Letting
\theta\searrow 0
, we conclude from (
5.6
) that
y^{\prime}\in\iota(\overline{\mathcal{C}})
. This proves Claim 1.

Claim 2.

Z^{\prime}_{(0,\infty)}=\emptyset
.

Suppose not. Then there exist
\theta>0
and a point
y^{\prime}\in Z^{\prime}_{\theta}
. Let
y_{i}\in Z
be a sequence converging to
y^{\prime}
with respect to the pointed Gromov–Hausdorff convergence in (
5.5
). Let
w_{i},z_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{t_{0}-r_{i}^{2}}
be
H_{3}
-centers of
y_{i}
and
x_{0}
, respectively. By the same argument as above, we have

\displaystyle d_{g_{t_{0}-\theta r_{i}^{2}}}(w_{i},z_{i})\leq Cr_{i}.

(5.7)

By Lemma
5.11
and the canonical neighborhood assumption, the rescaled flows

(\mathcal{R},r_{i}^{-2}g_{r_{i}^{2}t+t_{0}},w_{i})

converge smoothly to a
\kappa
-solution

(M_{\infty},g_{\infty,t})_{t\in(-\infty,\theta)}.

On the other hand, (
5.7
) and the assumption on the tangent flow at
x_{0}
imply that

(M_{\infty},g_{\infty,t})_{t\in(-\infty,0)}

is isometric to
\mathcal{C}
, which is impossible. This contradiction proves Claim 2.

Combining Claims 1 and 2, we conclude that
\iota
is a time-preserving isometry from
\overline{\mathcal{C}}
onto
(Z^{\prime},d_{Z^{\prime}},\mathfrak{t}^{\prime})
.
∎

5.4  Rectifiability and structure of the singular set

By the results of the previous subsection, every singular point in
Z

is quotient cylindrical; see
[
18
, Definition 5.24]
.
Following essentially the same—and in several places simpler—arguments
as in
[
3
,
17
,
19
,
18
]
, the main structure results for noncollapsed Ricci flow
limit spaces extend to the present singular Ricci flow setting. We briefly
explain the ingredients needed for this extension.

(1)

The integral estimates for conjugate heat kernel potentials from

[
3
, Proposition 6.2]
carry over to singular Ricci
flows. Indeed, the argument is the same as in the proof of Lemma
4.15
: one exhausts each time-slice by compact domains whose
boundary components are central spheres of sufficiently small necks, and
then lets the exhaustion tend to the full time-slice. The boundary terms
vanish by the decay estimate in Lemma
4.14
. Thus all
integration-by-parts identities used in the closed or limit-space setting
remain valid here.

(2)

The change-of-base estimate for conjugate heat kernel measures from
[
3
, Proposition 8.1]
(see also
[
17
, Appendix A]
) extends verbatim to singular Ricci flows.

(3)

The construction of sharp splitting maps from

[
18
, Section 3.4]
also carries over. As in

[
18
, Lemma 3.1]
, the splitting map is obtained from the
difference of the heat kernel potential functions based at two suitable
points
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
, where
x_{1}
is an independent point relative
to
x_{0}
. The change-of-base estimate and Lemma
4.14
give
the required integral control with respect to the conjugate heat kernel
measure based at
x_{0}
. Consequently, the almost-splitting estimates from

[
17
, Appendix C]
remain valid in the present setting.

(4)

The cylindrical Lojasiewicz inequality

[
19
, Theorem 1.3]
and the corresponding summability
result
[
19
, Corollary 1.4]
near a

(1,\epsilon,r)
-cylindrical point extend to singular Ricci flows.
Although these results were later formulated for noncollapsed Ricci flow
limit spaces in
[
18
, Proposition 5.1]
using the modified

\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
-entropy, in the present setting one may instead
follow the original proof directly and work with the usual

\mathcal{W}
-entropy. The analytic input needed in that proof is supplied
by the heat kernel estimates of Section
4
and by the
integration-by-parts justification above.

(5)

The strong uniqueness of cylindrical tangent flows, the
Ahlfors regularity of the packing measures, and the quantitative
stratification estimates used in
[
19
,
18
]
hold for the
completion
Z
. In particular, the neck decomposition theorem and the
corresponding content estimate follow by the same argument as in
[
18
, Theorem 6.9 and Proposition 6.13]
. The present
three-dimensional situation is simpler than the general setting considered
there, because the canonical neighborhood theorem and the classification of
three-dimensional
\kappa
-solutions imply that the only noncompact
singular models are quotient cylindrical. Thus there is no need to consider flat neck regions.

Consequently, the horizontal parabolic rectifiability theorem

[
18
, Theorem 1.2]
, the vanishing of the

1/2
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of the singular time set analogous to

[
18
, Corollary 5.23]
, the local
\mathscr{H}^{1}
-bound
and time-slice high-curvature volume estimate analogous to

[
18
, Theorem 6.26 and Corollary 6.28]
, and the

L^{1}
-curvature estimate analogous to

[
18
, Theorem 6.31]
all hold in the present singular
Ricci flow setting. We state the consequences needed below.

Theorem 5.17

(
[
18
, Theorem 1.2]
)
.

For each
k=0,1
, the set
\mathcal{S}^{k}
is horizontally parabolic
k
-rectifiable with respect to the distance
d_{Z}
. In particular,
\mathcal{S}
is horizontally parabolic
1
-rectifiable.

The definition of horizontally parabolic
k
-rectifiable can be found in
[
18
, Definition 1.1]
. As an application of Theorem
5.17
, one obtains the following result, analogous to
[
18
, Corollary 5.23]
, which improves
[
28
, Theorem 1.4]
.

Corollary 5.18
.

We have

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}^{1/2}(\mathfrak{t}(\mathcal{S}))=0,

where
\mathscr{H}^{1/2}
denotes the
1/2
-dimensional Hausdorff measure on
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let
d_{P}
denote the parabolic distance on
\mathbb{R}
, namely

d_{P}(s,t)=\sqrt{|s-t|}\qquad\text{for all }s,t\in\mathbb{R}.

It suffices to prove that

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{P}^{1}(\mathfrak{t}(\mathcal{S}))=0,

(5.8)

where
\mathscr{H}_{P}^{1}
denotes the
1
-dimensional Hausdorff measure on
\mathbb{R}
with respect to the metric
d_{P}
.

As in the proof of
[
18
, Corollary 5.23]
, for sufficiently small
\delta>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}^{0}\subset\bigcup_{i}\bigl(\mathscr{C}_{i,0}\cap B^{*}(x_{i},r_{i})\bigr),

where

\mathscr{N}_{i}=B^{*}(x_{i},2r_{i})\setminus B^{*}_{r_{x}}(\mathscr{C}_{i})

is a
(1,\delta,\mathfrak{c},r_{i})
-neck region with
x_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
, where
\mathfrak{c}=10^{-100}
; cf.
[
18
, Definition 5.9]
. Moreover, by the definition of a cylindrical neck region, we have

\mathscr{C}_{i,0}\cap B^{*}(x_{i},2r_{i})\subset\mathcal{S}.

We claim that

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{P}^{1}\bigl(\mathfrak{t}(\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}^{0})\bigr)=0.

(5.9)

To prove (
5.9
), it suffices to show that for each
i
,

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{P}^{1}\bigl(\mathfrak{t}(\mathscr{C}_{i,0}\cap B^{*}(x_{i},r_{i}))\bigr)=0.

(5.10)

Let

A_{i}:=\mathscr{C}_{i,0}\cap\overline{B^{*}(x_{i},r_{i})},

which is a closed subset of
\mathcal{S}
. By Ahlfors regularity (see
[
18
, Theorem 5.21]
), we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}^{1}(A_{i})\leq\mathscr{H}^{1}\bigl(\mathscr{C}_{i,0}\cap B^{*}(x_{i},3r_{i}/2)\bigr)\leq C(n,Y)r_{i}<\infty.

Since
A_{i}
is compact, for any
\epsilon>0
there exists
r_{\epsilon}>0
such that every
x\in A_{i}
is uniformly
(1,\epsilon,r_{\epsilon})
-cylindrical; see
[
18
, Lemma 5.10]
. Hence

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{t}(x)-\mathfrak{t}(y)|\leq\Psi(\epsilon)\,d_{Z}^{2}(x,y)

(5.11)

for any
x,y\in A_{i}
with
d_{Z}(x,y)\leq r_{\epsilon}
.

By the definition of Hausdorff measure, there exists a countable cover
\{B^{*}(y_{j},s_{j})\}
of
A_{i}
with
s_{j}<r_{\epsilon}/2
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{j}s_{j}\leq C(n,Y)r_{i}.

(5.12)

Then, by (
5.11
), for any
x,y\in B^{*}(y_{j},s_{j})
we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{|\mathfrak{t}(x)-\mathfrak{t}(y)|}\leq\Psi(\epsilon)\,d_{Z}(x,y)\leq\Psi(\epsilon)\,s_{j}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{P}^{1}\bigl(\mathfrak{t}(B^{*}(y_{j},s_{j})\cap A_{i})\bigr)\leq\Psi(\epsilon)\,s_{j}.

Summing over
j
and using (
5.12
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{P}^{1}(\mathfrak{t}(A_{i}))\leq\Psi(\epsilon)\,r_{i}.

Since
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary, it follows that

\mathscr{H}_{P}^{1}(\mathfrak{t}(A_{i}))=0,

which proves (
5.10
), and hence (
5.9
).

On the other hand, since
\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathcal{S}^{0})=0
and the time function
\mathfrak{t}
is Lipschitz with respect to
d_{Z}
and
d_{P}
, we also have

\mathscr{H}_{P}^{1}(\mathfrak{t}(\mathcal{S}^{0}))=0.

Combining this with (
5.9
), we obtain (
5.8
). This completes the proof.
∎

Next, we prove the following result, which may be regarded as a generalized form of Perelman’s bounded diameter conjecture.

Theorem 5.23
.

Let
D_{g_{t}}
denote the sum of the diameters, with respect to

, of all connected components of

. Then

\displaystyle D_{g_{t}}\leq C

for any

, where
C>0
is a constant depending on
T
and the diameter of
(M,g_{0})
.

Proof.

Fix
t\in[2,T]
. Define a measure
\mu_{t}
on
\mathcal{R}_{t}
by

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(A):=\int_{A}(|\mathrm{R}|+1)\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}}

for any Borel subset
A\subset\mathcal{R}_{t}
. Suppose that
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\prime}
is a connected component of
\mathcal{R}_{t}
whose diameter with respect to
d_{g_{t}}
is
D_{g_{t}}^{\prime}
. By Proposition
4.9
, there exists a minimizing geodesic
\gamma
in
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\prime}
of length
D_{g_{t}}^{\prime}/2
.

For a small universal constant
\delta>0
, the canonical neighborhood assumption implies that there exists
r_{\delta}>0
depending on
T
such that for any
x\in\mathcal{R}_{t}
with
\mathrm{R}(x)\geq r_{\delta}^{-2}
, the pointed manifold
(\mathcal{R}_{t},g_{t},x)
is
\delta
-close to a
\kappa
-solution. Set
r_{0}:=\theta r_{\delta}
, where
\theta\ll 1
is a universal constant.

Choose a covering
\{B_{g_{t}}(x_{i},2r_{0})\}_{i=1}^{N}
of
\gamma
such that
x_{i}\in\gamma
, the balls
\{B_{g_{t}}(x_{i},r_{0})\}_{i=1}^{N}
are pairwise disjoint, and

\displaystyle C_{0}^{-1}Nr_{0}\leq D_{g_{t}}^{\prime}\leq C_{0}Nr_{0},

(5.13)

where
C_{0}>1
is universal. We may assume that there exists
N^{\prime}\in[0,N]
such that for each
i\in[1,N^{\prime}]
(
[1,N^{\prime}]=\emptyset

), there is a point

. As in the proof of Proposition

, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(B_{g_{t}}(x_{i},r_{0}))\geq c_{1}r_{0}^{3}

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(B_{g_{t}}(x_{i},r_{0}))\geq c_{2}r_{0}

for

, where

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\prime})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{t}(B_{g_{t}}(x_{i},r_{0}))\geq c_{1}N^{\prime}r_{0}^{3}+c_{2}(N-N^{\prime})r_{0}\geq c_{3}D_{g_{t}}^{\prime},

(5.14)

where

is a constant depending on

.

On the other hand, by Theorem
5.21
and
[

, the total mass
\mu_{t}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
is uniformly bounded by a constant depending only on

and the diameter of

5.6  Gromov–Hausdorff convergence of time-slices

For each
t_{0}\in(1,T]
, let

be a connected component of

. We first introduce the following definition.

Definition 5.25
.

A branch
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{R}_{[1,t_{0})}
is
determined
by

if for every
z\in Z_{t_{0}}^{\prime}
, the support of
\nu_{z;s}
is contained in
\mathcal{R}_{s}^{\prime}
for all

.

It is clear from Definition

that

determines a unique branch
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
. Moreover,
Z_{t_{0}}^{\prime}
also determines a branch
Z^{\prime}\subset Z_{[1,t_{0})}
such that

is connected for every

and contains
\mathcal{R}_{s}^{\prime}
.

As before, let
(\overline{\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\prime}},d_{g_{t}})
denote the metric completion of
(\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\prime},d_{g_{t}})
. By the same argument as in the proof of Theorem
3.8
, we obtain the following Gromov–Hausdorff convergence result.

Proposition 5.26
.

In the above setting, we have the Gromov–Hausdorff convergence

(\overline{\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\prime}},d_{g_{t}})\xrightarrow[t\nearrow t_{0}]{\mathrm{Gromov\text{--}Hausdorff}}(Z^{\prime}_{t_{0}},d^{Z}_{t_{0}}).

In particular,
(Z^{\prime}_{t_{0}},d^{Z}_{t_{0}})
is a compact geodesic space.
The last conclusion follows from Proposition
4.9
and the Gromov–Hausdorff convergence.

As a corollary, we prove the following.

Proposition 5.27
.

For any
t_{0}\in[1,T]\setminus\mathfrak{t}(\mathcal{S})
, the distance
d^{Z}_{t_{0}}
agrees with
d_{g_{t_{0}}}
on each connected component of
\mathcal{R}_{t_{0}}
.

Proof.

Since
t_{0}\notin\mathfrak{t}(\mathcal{S})
, the slice
Z_{t_{0}}
contains no singular points. By Corollary
5.24
, every connected component of
\mathcal{R}_{t_{0}}
is compact. Hence each connected component of
\mathcal{R}_{t_{0}}
is also a connected component of

, because by Proposition
5.26
every connected component of

is a compact geodesic space.

Now let
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{t_{0}}
be a connected component of
\mathcal{R}_{t_{0}}
, and let
x,y\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{t_{0}}
. Since
(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{t_{0}},d^{Z}_{t_{0}})
is a geodesic space, there exists a minimizing geodesic

\gamma\subset\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{t_{0}}

with respect to

connecting

and

and hence
d_{g_{t_{0}}}(x,y)\leq d^{Z}_{t_{0}}(x,y)
. On the other hand, we always have

This proves the proposition.

Next, we prove the following past-continuity statement for the intrinsic time-slices.

Proposition 5.28
.

In the above setting, we have the Gromov–Hausdorff convergence

(Z^{\prime}_{t},d^{Z}_{t})\xrightarrow[t\nearrow t_{0}]{\mathrm{Gromov\text{--}Hausdorff}}(Z^{\prime}_{t_{0}},d^{Z}_{t_{0}}).

Proof.

Let
t_{j}\nearrow t_{0}
be an arbitrary sequence. By Proposition
5.26
, for each
j
we may choose
s_{j}<t_{j}
sufficiently close to
t_{j}
such that
s_{j}\nearrow t_{0}
and

d_{\mathrm{GH}}\left((Z^{\prime}_{t_{j}},d^{Z}_{t_{j}}),(\overline{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{s_{j}}},d_{g_{s_{j}}})\right)\leq 2^{-j}.

On the other hand, applying Proposition
5.26
again to the time
t_{0}
, we have

(\overline{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{s_{j}}},d_{g_{s_{j}}})\xrightarrow[j\to\infty]{\mathrm{Gromov\text{--}Hausdorff}}(Z^{\prime}_{t_{0}},d^{Z}_{t_{0}}).

Combining these two facts, we conclude that

(Z^{\prime}_{t_{j}},d^{Z}_{t_{j}})\xrightarrow[j\to\infty]{\mathrm{Gromov\text{--}Hausdorff}}(Z^{\prime}_{t_{0}},d^{Z}_{t_{0}}).

Since the sequence
t_{j}\nearrow t_{0}
was arbitrary, the proof is complete.
∎

Finally, by the same argument, we generalize Theorem
3.9
to the present setting.

Theorem 5.29
.

There exists a constant
C>0
, depending on
T
and the diameter of
(M,g_{0})
, such that the following properties hold for any
t\in[2,T]
:

(1)

For any
r>0
,

\bigl|\{y\in Z_{t}\mid d_{t}^{Z}(y,\mathcal{S}_{t})<r\}\bigr|_{t}\leq Cr^{2}.

(2)

The Minkowski dimension of
\mathcal{S}_{t}
with respect to
d_{t}^{Z}
satisfies

\dim_{\mathscr{M}}(\mathcal{S}_{t})\leq 1.

6  Higher-dimensional extensions under positive isotropic curvature

The main results of this paper can be extended to higher-dimensional Ricci flows under the positive isotropic curvature condition.

Suppose that
(M^{n},g_{0})
is a normalized Riemannian manifold with positive isotropic curvature (PIC) and contains no nontrivial incompressible
(n-1)
-dimensional space form. In addition, we assume that either
n=4
or
n\geq 12
. We also note the preprint

[
14
]
, which states an improvement for
n\geq 9
.

It follows from
[
13
]
and
[
9
]
that one can construct a Ricci flow with surgery starting from
(M^{n},g_{0})
. As in the three-dimensional case, the canonical neighborhood assumption holds: points of high scalar curvature have local geometry modeled on
\kappa
-solutions, where a
\kappa
-solution is a
\kappa
-noncollapsed complete ancient Ricci flow solution with uniformly positive isotropic curvature. The classification of all such
\kappa
-solutions is now complete; see
[
8
,
6
,
15
]
.

By taking a limit of Ricci flows with surgery as the surgery parameter tends to zero, one obtains, as in
[
27
]
, a singular Ricci flow
\mathcal{M}
starting from
(M^{n},g_{0})
. As in dimension three, this singular Ricci flow is unique; see
[
24
]
.

On this singular Ricci flow, one can follow essentially the same arguments as in the previous sections to obtain analogous results. The main difference is that the only noncompact tangent flow is the standard Ricci flow on
S^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
. Accordingly, one needs to work with
(1,\delta,\mathfrak{c},r)
-cylindrical neck regions and the corresponding neck decomposition theorem.

We now list some of the main results.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be the singular Ricci flow starting from
(M^{n},g_{0})
. For any
T>1
, let
(Z,d_{Z},\mathfrak{t})
be the completion of
\mathcal{M}_{[1,T]}
with respect to the spacetime distance
d^{*}
. Then the following statements hold.

(1)

The singular set
\mathcal{S}
is horizontally parabolic
1
-rectifiable with
\mathscr{H}^{1/2}(\mathfrak{t}(\mathcal{S}))=0
.

(2)

For any
z_{0}\in Z
with
\mathfrak{t}(z_{0})-2r_{0}^{2}>1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}^{1}\bigl(\mathcal{S}\cap B^{*}(z_{0},r_{0})\bigr)\leq C(n,T)r_{0}^{n+2}.

(3)

There exists a constant
C>0
, depending on
n
,
T
and the diameter of
(M,g_{0})
, such that for any
t\in[2,T]
, we have

\displaystyle D_{g_{t}}+\int_{\mathcal{R}_{t}}|\mathrm{Rm}|^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}}\leq D_{g_{t}}+\int_{\mathcal{R}_{t}}r_{\mathrm{Rm}}^{-(n-1)}\,\mathrm{d}V_{g_{t}}\leq C,

where
D_{g_{t}}
denotes the sum of the diameters, with respect to
d_{g_{t}}
, of all connected components of
\mathcal{R}_{t}
.

(4)

For each
t_{0}\in(1,T]
, we have the Gromov–Hausdorff convergence

\displaystyle(\overline{\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\prime}},d_{g_{t}})\xrightarrow[t\nearrow t_{0}]{\mathrm{Gromov\text{--}Hausdorff}}(Z_{t_{0}}^{\prime},d_{t_{0}}^{Z}),

where
Z^{\prime}_{t_{0}}
is a connected component of
Z_{t_{0}}
that determines
\mathcal{R}_{t}^{\prime}
for
t<t_{0}
. In particular,
(Z_{t_{0}}^{\prime},d_{t_{0}}^{Z})
is a compact geodesic space.

(5)

For any
t\in[1,T]
, the Minkowski dimension of
\mathcal{S}_{t}
with respect to
d_{t}^{Z}
satisfies

\dim_{\mathscr{M}}(\mathcal{S}_{t})\leq 1.
