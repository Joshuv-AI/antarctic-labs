Introduction

The Cox-Maze IV procedure is an established surgical treatment for atrial fibrillation (AF), particularly in patients undergoing concomitant cardiac surgery. Contemporary biatrial Cox-Maze IV techniques use energy-based ablation modalities, including cryoablation, to create durable linear lesion sets while minimizing procedural complexity. Long-term studies have demonstrated favorable rhythm outcomes and procedural durability.1

Despite these results, late atrial arrhythmia recurrence after surgical maze procedures has been reported. Conduction recovery across the pulmonary vein or linear lesions may contribute to recurrent atrial tachyarrhythmias and has been associated with macroreentrant mechanisms following surgical maze procedures.2 Wazni et al3 demonstrated pulmonary vein–left atrial reconnection in patients undergoing catheter ablation after a prior surgical maze procedure, suggesting that surgical lesions may not remain permanently durable. High-density mapping studies have further identified posterior wall reconnection as an important mechanism of recurrence following surgical ablation.4

Posterior wall isolation is an integral component of many biatrial maze lesion sets because of the shared embryologic origin and arrhythmogenic potential of the pulmonary veins and posterior wall. However, the long-term electrical durability of individual components within surgical posterior wall box lesions remains unclear. Even in catheter-based posterior wall isolation, the reconnection of the posterior wall lines is frequently observed during repeated procedures.5 However, there are no reports specifically addressing late reconnection in cases with documented complete isolation at intermediate follow-up.6 Data specifically evaluating late conduction recovery after previously documented complete bidirectional block are limited.7

Here, we report a case of selective reconnection of the posterior wall inferior line occurring 10 years after biatrial Cox-Maze IV surgery and 5 years after high-density mapping confirmed complete bidirectional block. This case highlights potential chamber- and line-specific heterogeneity in lesion durability and underscores the importance of comprehensive reassessment in patients with late arrhythmia recurrence after surgical AF ablation.

Case report

A 67-year-old woman with a history of AF underwent aortic valve replacement, mitral valve replacement, tricuspid annuloplasty, and a concomitant biatrial Cox-Maze IV procedure 10 years prior. The surgical lesion set included posterior wall box isolation with cryoablation of the posterior wall inferior line and right atrial maze lesions. 5 years postoperatively, she underwent catheter ablation for recurrent atrial tachyarrhythmia. High-density electroanatomic mapping was performed using a multipolar PENTARAY Nav SH mapping catheter (Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA) and the CARTO 3 version 7 Prime 3-dimensional mapping system (Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA). A dense scar was defined as a bipolar voltage (<0.1 mV) and low-voltage areas as 0.1–0.5 mV. A high-density left atrial voltage and activation map was created during pacing from the high right atrium. The posterior wall showed a homogeneous dense scar consistent with complete box isolation.

Bidirectional block was confirmed by both entrance and exit block. Entrance block was demonstrated by the absence of posterior wall activation from the surrounding left atrium during high right atrial pacing. Exit block was assessed by pacing from more than 10 sites distributed across the pulmonary veins and posterior wall using the ablation catheter at a standardized high-output setting of 10 V with a pulse width of 2 ms. Local capture was confirmed at each pacing site, whereas no conduction to the surrounding left atrium was observed. Far-field left atrial capture was excluded based on the local electrogram characteristics and the absence of a propagated activation sequence outside the paced region. There were no regions where pacing assessment could not be performed because of dense scar or catheter instability, and consistent non-capture of the left atrium was confirmed throughout the pulmonary veins and posterior wall (Figure 2). Perimitral atrial flutter was induced during programmed stimulation. 5 years after initial high-density confirmation of complete posterior wall isolation, the patient developed recurrent atrial tachyarrhythmia. At the time of hospital admission, the atrial tachyarrhythmia had already terminated. A second electrophysiological study was performed. Clinical tachycardia was not sustained before detailed electroanatomical mapping, and the patient was in sinus rhythm at the start of the procedure. Repeat high-density electroanatomic mapping was performed using the CARTO 3 version 8.1 Phase 3 system (Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA) with an OCTARAY mapping catheter (Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA). Bipolar voltage thresholds were identical to those used in the prior study (dense scar < 0.1 mV; low-voltage area: 0.1–0.5 mV). Voltage mapping revealed conduction within the posterior wall, accompanied by pulmonary vein reconnection (Figure 3). In contrast, the roof, anterior, and right atrial maze lines maintained a bidirectional block. Activation mapping during proximal coronary sinus pacing revealed a localized breakthrough at the inferior posterior wall corresponding to the prior inferior cryoablation line (Figure 4). We performed bilateral pulmonary vein re-isolation and re-creation of the inferior posterior wall line to restore complete posterior wall isolation. Conduction across the posterior wall was eliminated after completion of these lesions. Programmed stimulation and isoproterenol infusion induced atrial arrhythmia; however, coronary sinus recordings were consistent with AF rather than with stable macroreentrant tachycardia. No sustained atrial tachyarrhythmias were observed at the end of the procedure.

Catheter manipulation and high-density mapping within the left atrium were feasible despite the presence of mechanical aortic and mitral valve prostheses, and no specific limitations were encountered in accessing or ablating the posterior wall region. Anticoagulation therapy was maintained with uninterrupted warfarin, and intravenous heparin was administered during the procedure to maintain an activated clotting time of ≥350 seconds.

During the 1-month blanking period, AF was documented and managed with antiarrhythmic drug therapy. No sustained organized atrial tachyarrhythmias were observed.

During 3 months of follow-up, rhythm monitoring was performed by serial outpatient electrocardiograms, and no recurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmia was documented.

Discussion

The present case demonstrates late manifestation of conduction across a lesion that had previously been considered electrically durable based on intermediate-term electrophysiological assessment, occurring 10 years after biatrial Cox-Maze IV surgery and 5 years after documented complete bidirectional block.

Durability of the Cox-Maze procedure

The possibility of very late conduction recovery after cryothermal injury remains an important consideration. In the context of catheter-based cryoballoon ablation, pulmonary vein reconnection is most commonly observed during repeat procedures within the first year and is generally attributed to incomplete lesion formation or early tissue recovery.8 Reports of conduction recovery occurring several years after an initially durable lesion are limited.9

In this case, conduction across the posterior wall became apparent more than 5 years after prior confirmation of complete bidirectional block. Although this finding highlights the limitations of intermediate-term electrophysiological validation, an initially incomplete lesion or a dormant micro-gap undetected during the previous evaluation cannot be completely excluded. Cellular regeneration of a mature transmural cryothermal lesion several years after lesion formation is biologically unlikely. Therefore, the present findings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of tissue regeneration. In this context, the present case is more appropriately interpreted as a late manifestation of conduction across a lesion previously considered electrically durable. Although the underlying mechanisms remain uncertain, this finding raises the possibility that cryothermal lesions may not always confer permanent electrical isolation, even after intermediate-term validation. However, given that this is a single case observation, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and extrapolation to the long-term durability of cryoballoon pulmonary vein isolation or other cryothermal lesions is not warranted.

The Cox-Maze IV procedure has demonstrated favorable long-term rhythm outcomes, with sustained freedom from atrial arrhythmia reported in a large series.1 These data support the overall durability of the surgical strategy. Nevertheless, late atrial arrhythmia recurrence after surgical maze was observed. Wazni et al3 demonstrated pulmonary vein–left atrial reconnection in patients undergoing catheter ablation after a prior surgical maze. Similarly, Bhatia et al4 identified posterior wall reconnection as a mechanism of recurrence during high-density mapping after surgical ablation.

Although the acute durability of posterior wall isolation has been reported in catheter-based cohorts,6 these studies have primarily addressed early outcomes. To date, late reconnection after complete isolation at intermediate follow-up has not been specifically reported. In most previous studies, lesion integrity was evaluated only at the time of recurrence or during the acute procedural phase. In contrast, our case included independent high-density confirmation of complete posterior wall isolation 5 years before recurrence, suggesting that conduction across a lesion previously considered electrically durable may become clinically evident years later.

Posterior wall vulnerability and line-level durability

Posterior wall isolation is technically challenging. In catheter-based ablation, Markman et al5 reported posterior wall reconnection in approximately 40% of patients undergoing repeat procedures.

In the present case, conduction across the inferior posterior wall became apparent at repeat evaluation and was localized to the inferior posterior wall, corresponding to the prior cryoablation segment, whereas the roof, anterior, and right atrial maze lines remained electrically intact. This pattern supports the concept of heterogeneity within surgical lesion sets. Bulava et al7 demonstrated that cryo-created surgical lesions may exhibit conduction gaps during subsequent electrophysiological evaluations.

Several anatomical and biophysical factors may contribute to selective inferior line vulnerability. Left atrial wall thickness has been associated with sites of acute pulmonary vein reconnection, and the posterior and inferior segments may exhibit greater wall thickness.10 Tailoring ablation parameters according to regional wall thickness has been proposed to improve lesion formation.11 Additionally, surgical lesion formation may be influenced by local heat sink effects and the surrounding tissue characteristics.12 Furthermore, epicardial adipose tissue has been associated with atrial arrhythmia substrates and outcomes after posterior wall box isolation.13

Collectively, these mechanisms may have contributed to the late manifestation of conduction across the inferior cryothermal line in this patient. However, because these factors were not directly assessed in this case, their role remains speculative.

The bipolar voltage thresholds used to define dense scar (<0.1 mV) and low-voltage areas (0.1–0.5 mV) were based on commonly accepted criteria; however, these thresholds were originally validated in non-surgical atria and may not fully distinguish residual electrical activity from far-field signals or noise in a heavily scarred post-surgical left atrium. In this case, the interpretation of lesion integrity was therefore supported not only by voltage mapping but also by pacing maneuvers and activation mapping findings.

Differences in mapping catheter design and mapping system version between the 2 procedures may have influenced point density and voltage interpolation. Furthermore, in heavily scarred atrial tissue, source-sink mismatch during high-output pacing may theoretically produce a false appearance of exit block, and residual conduction through a dormant micro-gap may have remained undetected at the 5-year evaluation. In addition, the higher spatial resolution of the OCTARAY catheter used during the second procedure may have facilitated the detection of micro-potentials not identified during the previous study. Therefore, the possibility of pseudo-block or a false-negative assessment at the initial evaluation cannot be completely excluded. However, the principal finding of this case was not based solely on differences in voltage distribution, but rather on the presence or absence of demonstrable conduction across the posterior wall. In particular, activation mapping during coronary sinus pacing at the second procedure demonstrated a localized breakthrough at the inferior posterior wall, which was not observed at the initial evaluation. Therefore, the observed reconnection is unlikely to be explained solely by differences in mapping technology.

Clinical implications

Although the clinical tachyarrhythmia was not sustained for full activation mapping, a localized inferior posterior wall breakthrough was demonstrated during coronary sinus pacing. After restoration of complete posterior wall isolation, only AF, rather than a stable macroreentrant atrial tachycardia, was inducible. Although partially organized atrial activity suggestive of atrial tachycardia was observed in some regions, the overall rhythm was not consistent with a reproducible macroreentrant tachycardia.

A 12-lead electrocardiogram of the clinical atrial tachyarrhythmia was available; however, direct comparison with the induced rhythm was not feasible because the clinical arrhythmia was not reproducibly induced during the procedure. Therefore, a definitive mechanistic relationship between inferior line reconnection and the clinical tachyarrhythmia could not be established. Nevertheless, the elimination of inducible organized atrial tachyarrhythmia after re-isolation suggests that conduction across the inferior–posterior wall line may have contributed to the arrhythmia substrate.

The original surgical lesion set did not consist of conventional circumferential pulmonary vein isolation, but rather a continuous cryothermal lesion connecting the roof, pulmonary vein antrum, and posterior wall. Accordingly, late manifestation of conduction at a vulnerable segment of this lesion set could produce apparent reconnection of the pulmonary veins and posterior wall as an electrically connected unit.

Although the activation pattern was consistent with conduction to the pulmonary veins via the posterior wall rather than independent pulmonary vein reconnection, the presence of additional conduction gaps cannot be completely excluded. Pulmonary vein re-isolation was performed to reinforce electrical isolation, given the apparent instability of conduction across the inferior posterior wall line.

This case highlights 3 important considerations. Firstly, late manifestation of conduction across lesions previously considered electrically durable may occur after biatrial Cox-Maze IV surgery and documented bidirectional block. Secondly, lesion durability may differ between chambers and individual lines within the same surgical lesion set. Lastly, comprehensive high-density reassessment is warranted in patients with late arrhythmia recurrence after surgical AF ablation.

Limitations

The presenting atrial tachyarrhythmia was not sustained during mapping, precluding a definitive mechanistic confirmation of its relationship with inferior line reconnection.

Activation mapping was performed during coronary sinus pacing, which was relatively close to the suspected gap site. Additional mapping during pacing from a more remote site, such as the high right atrium, was not performed, which may limit the precision in delineating the activation sequence and in confirming a single breakthrough site. Nevertheless, the earliest activation consistently localized to the inferior posterior wall, and conduction across the posterior wall was eliminated following targeted ablation at the inferior line, supporting the presence of a functionally significant conduction pathway at this site. In addition, although the activation pattern was most consistent with conduction to the pulmonary veins via the posterior wall as part of an electrically connected lesion set, independent pulmonary vein reconnection or additional gaps within the lesion set could not be completely excluded. Moreover, follow-up after repeat ablation was limited.

Conclusion

Posterior wall inferior line reconnection can occur years after biatrial Cox-Maze IV surgery and documented complete bidirectional block. This case illustrates the potential chamber- and line-specific heterogeneity in surgical lesion durability and underscores the importance of careful reassessment of patients with late arrhythmia recurrence.
