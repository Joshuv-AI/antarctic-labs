Effects of Recovery Conditions and Aggregation Mechanisms on the Chemical and Physical Properties of Hybrid Poplar and Corn Stover Lignins
Authors: Villő E. Bécsy-Jakab, Dilara N. Dülger, Meenaa Chandrasekar, David B. Hodge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heterogeneity in the physical properties and processing behavior of lignins recovered from biorefining process streams can hinder their effective utilization and industrial valorization. This work investigates lignin recovery by acidification from alkaline pretreatment liquors of varying sources (corn stover and hybrid poplar), compositions, solid contents, and incubation temperatures prior to filtration. Recovered lignins were characterized for filtration performance, mass yield, chemical composition, particle size and morphology, and color. A transition temperature was identified corresponding to marked shifts in lignin physical properties and was a function of the solid content and liquor source. Filtration below this temperature yielded slow-filtering, dark, brittle lignins with smoother particle surfaces, whereas precipitation above this threshold yielded fast-filtering, lighter, powdery lignins with rougher particle surfaces. The impact of temperature and concentration on universal cluster aggregation mechanisms was proposed to explain the differences between lignin particle properties and filtration behavior.

Introduction

Utilizing renewable sources of reduced carbon for fuels, chemicals, and materials offers a sustainable alternative to fossil fuel carbon sources. Lignocellulosic biomass represents a large potential source of renewable carbon, with biomass feedstocks such as hybrid poplar and corn stover recognized as having significant potential in North America for bioenergy and biobased products. Lignocellulosic biomass is predominantly composed of structural biopolymers within plant cell walls and includes aromatic lignin polymers and polysaccharides that are primarily cellulose and hemicelluloses. Lignocellulosic biorefining processes target the deconstruction and conversion of these structural biopolymers to products, with lignin representing an abundant source of renewable aromatics with a diverse range of value-added applications.

Process-derived lignins can exhibit substantial diversity in a range of chemical properties including molar mass, monomer types and abundance, interunit linkages, and cross-linking between polymers. This diversity is derived from both the biological source of the lignin and its processing history, including the pretreatment/delignification process and the lignin recovery or purification process. Delignifying pretreatment, fractionation, or pulping processes remove lignin and hemicellulose from the cell wall matrix through a combination of chemical modification (e.g., depolymerization or functionalization) and solvation (e.g., alkali or organic solvents). To recover these polymers for use in value-added applications, a separation process must be performed and is typically based on altering the solvent environment to precipitate the polymers. Pilot and commercial technologies for lignin recovery from alkaline black liquors have been employed at the pilot and commercial scale. , Key features of these approaches when applied to black liquors from woody feedstocks include liquor concentration and acidification with CO2 or a mineral acid, followed by filtration. Commercial processes for chemical (typically soda) pulping of nonwood (i.e., graminaceous) feedstocks such as rice straw and sugar cane bagasse require alternative processing for lignin combustion and chemical recovery of alkali due to differing organic and inorganic chemical profiles of the biomass. Specifically, these black liquors have higher viscosities than wood-derived black liquors at comparable concentrations due to the higher xylan content, lignins are of lower molar mass, and intrinsic silica from the nonwood feedstocks is solubilized in the black liquor, complicating chemical recovery. To overcome these challenges, a “desilication” step can be applied that may integrate with lignin precipitation and approaches. Desilication processes can integrate with lignin precipitation and recovery, with features that include concentration and CO2 acidification of the black liquor, and in one study, it was shown that more than 90% of the silica can be precipitated by acidification to pH 10 with less than 10% loss of organic material.

Critical to the economic value of lignin as a coproduct are lignin recovery processes yielding granular lignin particles that can consistently meet quality metrics with respect to chemical and physical properties and perform as required in a target application. Processing conditions are crucial in determining lignin aggregation behavior that ultimately impacts processing outcomes such as filterability and chemical and physical properties of the recovered lignins. , During lignin precipitation, lignin behaves as a colloidal system, with destabilization of the system resulting in particle aggregation into large clusters. − The aggregation of colloidal particles can occur according to universal limiting regimes, with previous studies proposing that these aggregation regimes are applicable to lignin. , The solvation and colloidal behavior of lignin is governed by factors that include temperature, pH, type, and concentration of metal anion and ionic strength. As an example, prior work studied the filtration properties of lignin suspensions derived from softwood kraft black liquor subjected to acidification and filtration at different temperatures and liquor strengths and showed that filtration characteristics were strongly dependent on the precipitation conditions. Black liquor with a dry solids content of 30%, acidified with sulfuric acid to a pH below 10 at a precipitation temperature of 80 °C, gave lignin suspensions that were easily filtered. A considerable increase in the average specific filtration resistance was noted at a modest temperature decrease of 10 °C from the optimum of 80 °C. If the temperature was raised above 80 °C, however, larger clumps of lignin were formed that caused plugging problems in the equipment.

Motivated by the challenge of producing lignins from small-scale digester batches that exhibit consistent chemical and physical properties, the present work investigated processing variables during the recovery of lignins from alkaline liquors by acidification and filtration. Using alkaline liquors from the soda pulping of corn stover or hybrid poplar, this work seeks to characterize how acidification conditions govern lignin precipitation, aggregation, and filterability and to ultimately relate these conditions to chemical and physical properties of the recovered lignins. Specific variables tested include lignin concentration and incubation temperature prior to filtration. Processing responses include lignin recovery yield and filtration rate during precipitation as well as particle size distribution, color, chemical composition, and molar mass of the recovered dried granular lignin particles. Finally, recovered lignin properties are linked to fundamental colloidal mechanisms that govern how lignin polymers and particles interact, aggregate, and precipitate.

Materials and Methods

Biomass

Hybrid poplar (NM6, P. nigra × P. maximowiczii) was obtained from the Michigan State University Upper Peninsula Forestry Innovation Center (Escanaba, Michigan). Poplar was harvested in January 2020 from less than 5-year-old trees with stem diameters of 15.5 to 20.3 cm, which were debarked, chipped, and dried to approximately 5% moisture content. A commercial hybrid corn stover was obtained from DuPont via Idaho National Laboratory. The corn stover was harvested in October 2014 in Story County, Iowa, and was subjected to milling first with a Vermeer BG480 bale grinder fitted with a 2-in. sieve, followed by further milling in a Bliss Hammermill fitted with a 1-in. sieve. The biomass moisture content was determined by using an Ohaus MB90 moisture balance (Parsippany, New Jersey).

Alkaline Pretreatment and Liquor Generation

The corn stover was subjected to delignification in a 20-L RegMed model AU/E-20stainless-steel tumbling pulp digester equipped with a TP-2027 lid (RegMed Indústria Técnica de Precisão Ltd.a., Osasco, São Paulo, Brazil). For this, 2.0 kg of biomass (dry basis) was loaded into the digester and mixed with a NaOH solution to achieve a liquor-to-biomass ratio of 9:1, with an alkali loading of 130 mg NaOH/g biomass. The temperature was increased at a rate of 2.5 °C/min until it reached the target temperature of 140 °C, which was maintained for 30 min. Hybrid poplar was subjected to a similar delignification in the same equipment, but with 3.0 kg of biomass, a liquor-to-biomass ratio of 5:1, and an alkali loading of 180 mg NaOH/g biomass. The temperature ramping was identical, but the target temperature was 150 °C, which was maintained for 3 h.

After the reaction, the digester was air-cooled until atmospheric pressure was restored and the temperature fell below 60 °C, at which point it was unloaded. To remove black liquor entrained in the pretreated corn stover, biomass was loaded into a 120-μm nylon CONIE mesh filter bag and compressed using a MacIntosh Apple Cider Press with a stainless-steel basket (Pleasant Hill Grain, Nebraska). The pretreated biomass was thoroughly washed with water, air-dried, and stored for further analysis. The undiluted black liquor recovered directly from pressing was stored at 4 °C without further modification prior to use.

Black liquor concentration was carried out using a wiped film evaporator (Wiped-Film Still, Pope Scientific Inc., Saukville, Wisconsin) equipped with an Edwards RV8 rotary vane vacuum pump, an MKS Series 275 mini-Convectron vacuum gauge, BriskHeat Corporation XtremeFLEX heating tape, a Penta KB Power Penta-Drive DC motor, and a Glas-Col PL312 power control unit. The black liquor obtained from the alkaline pretreatment process was fed into the evaporator in 1.0 L batches. Hybrid poplar liquor was concentrated to 40% total solids and corn stover liquor was concentrated to 30% total solids. Total solids content of the liquor was determined by drying at 105 °C.

Molar Mass Determination

The number-average and weight-average molar masses of the extracted lignins were determined by gel permeation chromatography (GPC) as described in our previous work. Briefly, acetylated lignin samples were analyzed using an Agilent 1260 HPLC system equipped with a Waters Styragel HR 4 column (7.8 × 300 mm, Milford, Massachusetts). HPLC-grade tetrahydrofuran (THF, Millipore Sigma) serves as the mobile phase at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min with UV detection at 280 nm and a column temperature of 35 °C. Monodisperse polystyrene standards ranging from 484 to 65 000 Da (Readycal, Fluka Analytical) were used for reference.

Lignin and Biomass Characterization

Chemical composition of biomass, pretreated biomass, and recovered lignin, including ash, carbohydrate, and acid-insoluble (Klason) lignin content, was performed according to the two-step hydrolysis method outlined in NREL/TP-510-42618 and modified to the 100 mg scale as outlined in our prior work. Monomeric carbohydrate content was quantified from the hydrolysis filtrate using an Agilent Technologies 1260 Infinity II HPLC system equipped with a Bio-Rad Aminex HPX-87H column, with 0.005 M sulfuric acid as mobile phase at a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min.

Lignin Fractional Precipitation

Corn stover or hybrid poplar black liquor was first homogenized for a minimum of 5 min using an anchor impeller on a drill mixer. Next, 310 mL of the liquor was transferred into a 500 mL Erlenmeyer flask. The sample was then acidified to below pH 2 using 72% (w/w) concentrated sulfuric acid, causing the temperature to increase from the storage temperature of 4 °C but remaining below 23 °C. Heating was achieved with an oil bath, and 50 mL samples were taken at 5 °C intervals from room temperature up to 80 °C. Each sample was filtered through Whatman #40 filter paper in a Büchner funnel and washed with 100 mL of deionized water at the same temperature as the sample. Finally, the solids were removed from the filter paper and left to air-dry over 2 days. Temperature, filtering time, final mass, and color were recorded. Due to the availability of the concentrated liquor, the procedure was modified to a smaller sample size. For this, 20 mL of the high-viscosity 30% concentrated corn stover liquor was diluted with 40 mL of deionized water, and 30 mL of the 40% concentrated hybrid poplar liquor was diluted with 30 mL of deionized water, resulting in 12.9 and 21.0% solid contents, respectively, and stored in a refrigerator overnight at 4 °C. Next, the liquors were acidified, heated, and filtered similarly to the dilute black liquor, except using a smaller 5 mL sample size and 25 mL washing water for effective filter cake forming. The recovered solids were air-dried overnight, with most conditions representing 2 to 5 replicates.

Solubility

Solubility differences between recovered lignins were performed following the methodology outlined in our previous work. For this, a 1:10 (w/v) solid-to-solvent ratio was employed, where 300 mg of lignin was suspended in 3.0 mL of solvent, with ethyl acetate and dimethyl sulfoxide selected to represent the two extremes of solubility and solvent polarity reported previously. The mixture was incubated in a sealed test tube for 1 h at 70 °C. Following incubation, the undissolved solids were filtered using Whatman #42 55 mm filter paper, washed with 30 mL of fresh solvent, and air-dried overnight at room temperature.

Colorimetry

Lignin color was determined using an FRU WR18 colorimeter (Shenzhen Wave Optoelectronics Technology, Shenzhen, China). Color data was recorded in the CIELAB color space, with L* ranging from 0 to 100, and a* and b* values ranging from −128 to 128. The measurement was taken through a 0.4 mm diameter window, using a D65 standard light source, a 10 ° CIE standard observer, and an 8/d illumination system with a photodiode array sensor.

Microscopy

Epifluorescence microscopy was performed using an OMAX EPI-Fluorescence Trinocular Microscope (M837ZFLR-C180U3) with an 18 MP camera (A35180U3), a mercury lamp, a blue excitation filter (410–490 nm), and a 515 nm barrier filter. Images were captured at 10× magnification using the ToupLite software. For the microscope slide preparation, a sample was taken from homogenized black liquor. Then, it was acidified with concentrated sulfuric acid to the predetermined pH and diluted 1:100 (v/v) with deionized water. A 1.0 μL aliquot of the diluted sample was pipetted onto a microscope slide, making 10 drops, and allowed to dry. No observed change in particle size during drying according to a video recording during the drying process. Powder lignin samples from different filtering temperatures were suspended in DI water at a 1:100,000 (w/w) ratio, and 10 μL of the suspension was pipetted onto a slide to dry. Confocal microscopic images were obtained using a Leica Stellaris DMI-8 (Leica Microsystems) confocal laser scanning microscopy (CSLM). The CLSM images were analyzed using Fiji software and MATLAB. The lignin particle contour extraction was done using the ImageJ macro Surfcut. The average roughness (R a) and the root-mean-square roughness (R q) were obtained as described in the prior literature.

Particle Size Distribution

Image processing was performed using ImageJ/Fiji software, starting with standard preprocessing steps (excluding images containing artifacts like biomass particles or the edge of the sample drop, calibration, thresholding to binary, etc.). Particle sizes were then determined with the software, excluding small artifacts (<9 pixels) and edge-bound particles. For powder samples, large multiparticle aggregates were excluded. Particle counts and areas were extracted and processed using a MATLAB script to generate particle size distributions and normalized histograms and to calculate mean and variance.

Results and Discussion

Alkaline Pretreatment and Initial Observations

Alkaline pretreatment of corn stover and hybrid poplar was performed according to conditions outlined in the Experimental Section to yield pretreated biomass and lignin-rich liquors that were used for this work. Composition analysis of untreated and pretreated corn stover and hybrid poplar indicates that approximately 63 and 87% of the lignin was solubilized from the hybrid poplar and corn stover, respectively (Figure A). On the basis of the composition analysis and mass yields, the selectivity for lignin versus xylan solubilization was estimated to be 4.7 g lignin/g xylan for the hybrid poplar and 2.3 g lignin/g xylan for the corn stover. This indicates that, besides lignin, a significant fraction of the organics present in the liquors may be oligomeric xylan and xylan degradation products. If the oligomeric xylan remains undegraded in the liquor, it will coprecipitate with the lignin and comprise an important class of nonlignin contaminants. In addition to the composition of the organic and inorganic solubles in the liquor, the total content of soluble solids can impact precipitation kinetics, particle morphology, and precipitation yields. , To yield liquors with differing solids contents, in this work the two recovered weak liquors from corn stover (CS1) and hybrid poplar (HP1) were subjected to concentration to yield concentrated black liquors CS2 and HP2. Measured total soluble solids and estimated Na+ (based on NaOH loading in the original liquors) for the four liquors are presented in Figure B. A key feature of these lignins is that they are relatively dilute (5.5 to 21.0% total solids) relative to industrial black liquors that may range from 13 to 18% total solids after pulping to more than 70% after concentration, with expected differences in processability and performance. Using these recovered black liquors, the impact of select processing conditions was next assessed on the processability as well as the physical and chemical properties of the recovered lignins.

Composition of (A) untreated and pretreated hybrid poplar and corn stover and (B) measured and estimated concentrations of total solids and sodium components in corn stover and hybrid poplar black liquors.

Impact of pH on Precipitation Yield and Particle Size

Fractional precipitation of lignin was performed to assess the impact of precipitation pH on recovered lignin yields and particle size distributions. Results show that decreasing pH to 6.4 resulted in recovery of only 20% of the lignin from the corn stover weak black liquor (CS1), while the remainder precipitated when pH 5.0 was reached (Figure A). This is significantly different from the sequential precipitation behavior of lignins in wood-derived black liquors from CO2 or mineral acid precipitation, , which generally show a strong inflection point at ∼10.5 corresponding to the pK a of the phenolic hydroxyl group in lignin, followed by continuously increasing lignin recovery yields with decreasing pH of precipitation. Potential reasons for this discrepancy may include the low solids content of the liquor (5.6%, Figure B), with prior work showing that higher sodium ion (Na+) concentrations result in a higher onset pH for precipitation and larger particle sizes. Other prior work showed a correlation between increased precipitation yields and higher lignin concentration, higher ionic strength, lower pH, and lower temperatures.

Impact of precipitation pH at room temperature on (A) mass yield for corn stover lignin (CS1), (B) characteristic particle sizes for different recovery pH levels, and particle size distributions for lignins recovered from weak black liquors from (C) corn stover lignin (CS1) and (D) hybrid poplar lignin (HP1).

The precipitated lignin particle projected areas from epifluorescence microscopy were next used to estimate characteristic particle sizes (Figure B) from area-weighted particle size distributions (Figure C,D). Particle size control during recovery is important, as micro- and nanoparticles are challenging to recover due to their colloidal stability and poor filterability. The characteristic particle size was defined as the diameter estimated from the area-weighted mean of the particle projected area distribution assuming a round particle, i.e., D = (4A/π)0.5. These results demonstrate how increasing lignin mass recovery yields with decreasing precipitation pH (Figure A) also correlate with increasing characteristic particle size (Figure B) ranging from approximately 8.0–8.9 μm at near-neutral pH to 30–39 μm for the lowest acidic pH. This suggests a mechanism of increasing yields from particle growth in solution with decreasing recovery pH rather than increasing nucleation and formation of more small particles. Interestingly, corn stover lignins contain more contaminating hemicelluloses such as xylan, and prior work has shown that xylan content impacts particle size, with higher xylan concentrations shifting the size distribution toward smaller particles. In both corn stover and hybrid poplar black liquors, the particle size distribution displays an approximate log-normal distribution (Figure C,D). At lower recovery pH, a characteristic ″tail″ extends toward larger particle sizes, indicating the presence of larger, aggregated particle formation as the pH decreases. This range and distribution of particle sizes is comparable to those found in the literature for a range of kraft lignins recovered by acidification. Together these results emphasize the role of pH in controlling lignin particle size and morphology. Acidification initiates lignin precipitation, with lower pH values promoting the formation of larger lignin particles.

Impact of Incubation Temperature on Lignin Mass Yield and Filterability

Based on findings previously identified in the prior literature, ,, a series of experiments assessing the impact of heating prior to filtration on mass recovery yield and filtration time were performed (Figure ). From these data, it can be observed that approximately 40% higher lignin yields were obtained from the hybrid black liquor relative to the corn stover lignin (Figure A). This can be attributed to the different solids contents of the two liquors (CS1 vs HP1). Additionally, the differences in recovery yields may also be a contributing factor to the differences in characteristic particle sizes between hybrid poplar and corn stover (Figure B). Another trend from these data is that lignin recovery yields from liquors decreased slightly over the temperature range of 23 to 95 °C for both corn stover and hybrid poplar lignins (Figure A). This decrease can be attributed to the increased solubility of lignin, xylan, , and inorganics (e.g., Na2SO4) at elevated temperatures. Prior work in the literature reported a more pronounced decline in yields for lignins recovered from softwood black liquor.

Impact of liquor filtration temperature on recovered mass yield (A) and filtration time for lignins recovered from acidified (B) corn stover black liquor (CS1) and (C) hybrid poplar black liquor (HP1). Error bars represent the data range for replicates.

Next, it can be observed that increasing the incubation temperature prior to filtration significantly decreases the filtration time (Figure B,C), in agreement with the literature. , Several trends are apparent. First, the filtering times for the corn stover lignins for the “slow-filtering” lignins were substantially longer for the corn stover lignins (∼40 to 140 min) relative to the hybrid poplar lignins (4–14 min). Additionally, the filtration time exhibits an approximately linear decrease from room temperature for both the corn stover and poplar lignins. However, the slope of this curve is considerably different with the transition from “slow-filtering” to “fast-filtering” approximately 70 °C for the corn stover versus 40 °C for the hybrid poplar.

As examples from the literature, prior work showed improved filterability of lignins precipitated from alkaline black liquors by acidification at temperatures below 60 °C and filtration above 50 °C. This approach allowed drying at temperatures up to 110 °C while preventing the formation of undesirable ″black″ lignins. In other patent literature, it was found that incubation at elevated temperature prior to spray drying of lignins precipitated by acidification from alkaline black liquors resulted in high surface area particles rather than compact dense particles generated without this incubation. Other prior work found that prior heating enhanced particle uniformity and filterability, which aligns with our observation that higher temperatures lead to decreased filtration times.

Impact of Incubation Temperature on Recovered Lignin Physical and Chemical Properties

The impact of the incubation temperature prior to filtration on lignin physical and chemical properties was next assessed. These physical and chemical properties include the lignin color, chemical composition, molar mass, and solubility. The first physical property, the apparent color of recovered and dried lignins, can serve as a macroscopic indicator of microscopic differences in lignin, with the observed color changes correlating to structural variations in lignin particles that reflect aggregation processes influencing their physical properties. ,

In the present work, apparent colors of recovered, dried lignins showed significant differences in color depending on the incubation temperature prior to filtration and drying (Figure ). Specifically, lignins incubated at “low” temperature prior to filtration and drying were dark and brittle (Figure A,C). In contrast, lignins recovered following a higher temperature incubation were less cohesive and powdery (Figure B,D). The apparent color of the recovered lignins showed a strong dependence on the recovery temperature and, to a lesser extent, solids concentration in the black liquor (Figure E,F,G). In the CIELAB color space, “lightness” is one of the three dimensions used to characterize color (Figure S1) and provides a useful descriptor of these differences. These results show a clear transition from “dark” to “light” lignins with increasing incubation temperature. This transition occurs at approximately 70 °C for CS1 (Figure F) and 35 °C for HP1 (Figure G). This transition is shifted to lower temperatures for the higher-solids-concentration black liquors CS2 and HP2. These results agree with prior literature and patent literature that suggests an incubation at elevated temperatures prior to filtration and drying can yield lighter, more porous lignins. ,,, Interestingly, Andeme Ela et al. reported a different trend, finding that lignin isolated from concentrated mixed hardwood black liquor yielded darker colors at high temperatures, while Helander et al. observed that undesirable, rigid, large lignin particles were formed during high-temperature (70 °C) precipitation.

Variability in color and texture of lignins recovered from corn stover (A, B) and hybrid poplar (C, D) at 23 °C (A, C) and elevated temperatures (75 °C for corn stover (B), 45 °C for hybrid poplar); (E) reconstituted colors for recovered lignins and CIELAB lightness color parameter for recovered corn stover (F) and poplar (G).

Variations in color are due to differences in how lignin absorbs, reflects, transmits, and scatters incident light, with contributions from the physical properties of the surface (particle shape, size, and texture of the particle surface) and chemical composition. Prior work attributed the dark color of lignin to conjugated structures formed during the harsh kraft pulping process. They also emphasized that while drying can affect color, it is not the dominant factor; rather, aggregation plays a crucial role, as less aggregated lignin with lower bulk density tends to be lighter.

Chemical composition and molar mass of the recovered lignins show a strong dependence on the incubation temperature prior to filtration (Figure ). Quantified nonlignin impurities in the recovered lignins include polysaccharides and ash, and it can be observed that there are significantly more nonlignin impurities in corn stover lignin (Figure A) than hybrid poplar lignin (Figure B). This can be attributed to the significantly higher susceptibility to mild alkali extraction of xylans (and lignins) from monocot grass versus woody dicots. Another key result is that the abundance of these nonlignin impurities decreases with increasing incubation temperature for both corn stover (Figure A) and hybrid poplar (Figure B). For corn stover, with increasing temperature, the impurity content decreased 2.6-fold from 22.9 to 13.7% when comparing the room temperature filtration to the highest temperature observed, 80 °C (Figure A). For hybrid poplar, the decrease is smaller, from 3.87 to 2.55%. As discussed previously related to the decreasing mass yields, it is expected that the solubility of impurities in lignin increases with increasing temperature, , therefore decreasing the impurity content of the isolated lignin. Molar mass characterization of recovered corn stover lignins recovered from CS1 demonstrated the trend of increasing number and weight-average MW with increasing recovery temperature (Figure C). One possible interpretation of this is that the low incubation temperatures precipitate more low-molecular-mass lignin oligomers that are more soluble at higher temperatures.

Differences in impurity composition as a function of recovery temperature for (A) corn stover lignin (CS1) and (B) hybrid poplar lignin (HP1) and (C) impact of recovery temperature on recovered lignin molar mass for corn stover.

The solubility of recovered lignins in select organic solvents (DMSO and ethyl acetate) at 50 °C was next assessed (Figure S2). These solvents were selected as they represent the two extremes of solubility and solvent polarity reported previously. For this, a “dark” and a “light” lignin recovered from HP1 liquor at 23 and 45 °C, respectively, were compared. The results showed high solubility in DMSO (85–89% soluble by mass) and relatively low solubility in ethyl acetate (27–28% soluble by mass). The differences in the two lignins for each solvent were not statistically different. This, together with minor differences in the chemical compositions but major differences in physical properties, indicates that the filterability and color differences in the recovered lignins may be primarily related to particle morphology resulting from the conditions during particle precipitation, aggregation, and drying. These morphological differences are investigated in more detail in the subsequent section.

Impact of Incubation Temperature on Recovered Lignin Particle Size and Morphology

Recovered “light” and “dark” lignins from corn stover and hybrid poplar were characterized to assess particle morphology and particle size distribution using fluorescence microscopy and surface roughness using CSLM. The dark, slow-filtering lignins recovered following a lower-temperature incubation (23 °C for both types of biomass) both showed similar morphologies with discrete particles with a smooth, continuous surface (Figure A–D). This can be contrasted with the quick-filtering, light lignins recovered following an elevated-temperature incubation (75 °C for corn stover, 45 °C for hybrid poplar) that exhibited a highly textured surfaces that suggest differences during the particle accretion and/or drying processes (Figure E–H). Interestingly, differences in incubation temperatures yield similar morphological differences for both types of biomass. Projected particle area distributions showed statistically larger particles for the lower-temperature incubation for corn stover (25.7 ± 4.8 μm2 versus 17.16 ± 3.7 μm2; Figure I). It should be noted that the particle images in Figure represent extremes in size (i.e., far to the right side of Figure I,J). Prior work showed differences in particle size distributions of kraft lignins precipitated at pH 9.7 at different temperatures, with 80 °C showing a broad distribution of particle diameters centered at 25 μm, while at 70 °C, a narrow distribution centered on 18 μm was observed.

Hypothesized Impact of Aggregation Mechanisms on Particle Properties

This work has demonstrated that lignins recovered from an aqueous alkali solution by acidification exhibited significant differences in filterability, color, and particle physical and chemical properties that were impacted by incubation temperature and black liquor concentration. As a generalization, lignins recovered with incubation at low temperature from more dilute liquor concentrations exhibited poor filterability, dark color, smooth surfaces, lower-molar-mass lignins, and more contaminating polysaccharides. Conversely, lignins recovered at elevated temperatures and higher concentration liquors showed improved filterability, lighter color, rougher surfaces, higher-molecular-mass lignins, and fewer polysaccharides.

Factors contributing to these differences in lignin properties and filterability could be attributed to (1) differences in solvent environment during coagulation resulting in aggregation mechanisms and differing ordering of polymers into particle aggregates, (2) differences in particle packing during filtration, and (3) further surface modification or reordering of polymers during drying in the presence of a water concentration gradient. For the first point, well-established mechanisms for particle aggregation in colloidal systems include reaction-limited cluster aggregation (RLCA) and diffusion-limited cluster aggregation (DLCA), which have been proposed or demonstrated for a wide range of precipitation in colloidal systems, including lignin. For the RLCA mechanism, aggregation of particles (i.e., nanoparticles or colloidal spheres) is limited by the energy barrier (e.g., electrostatic repulsion) required for particles to “stick”, and many collisions may be necessary before particles aggregate. This mechanism results in slower aggregation kinetics and denser particle aggregates since particles may sample multiple configurations before overcoming the energy barrier. In the DLCA mechanism, the kinetics of aggregation are only limited by the rate of particle diffusion rather than energy barriers and can result in more open, dendritic particles. ,

In the context of the current work, these universal cluster aggregation mechanisms can be proposed to explain the differences in lignin particle properties and filtration behavior (Figure ). For this, the RLCA mechanism can be proposed to dominate at lower temperatures and concentrations, and the DLCA mechanism is proposed to dominate at higher temperatures and liquor concentrations arising from differences in incubation temperature and liquor concentration. Particles with more open, porous structures that may arise from the DLCA mechanism can be expected to exhibit lower filtration resistance (Figure ) and rougher, more textured surfaces (Figure ) that will increase light scattering, yielding a lighter appearance (Figure ). The transition from an RLCA regime to DLCA occurs when the repulsive energy barrier between two particles is less than k B T, such that a critical temperature should define this transition. This study observed distinct transition temperatures, correlating with shifts in lignin color (Figure ), particle size (Figure ), and surface roughness (Figure ) for corn-stover- and hybrid-poplar-derived black liquors, with the corn-stover liquor consistently exhibiting a higher transition threshold than the hybrid poplar. Besides differences in chemical composition, this difference between the corn stover and hybrid poplar transition temperatures could be explained by differences in liquor concentration (Figure B), with the increasing liquor concentration shifting the transition temperature to lower temperatures (Figure F,G). Higher solid content means increased lignin concentration, increasing the likelihood of particle interactions and aggregation and reducing the energy needed to reach a point where lignin particles aggregate. Higher solid content also means increased ionic strength that can reduce the repulsive forces between particles to promote aggregation. Higher solid content favors DLCA at lower temperatures, as the diffusions and collisions are easier with higher concentrations; therefore, the system requires less kinetic energy (lower temperature) to facilitate aggregation. Conversely, at lower solid contents, RLCA is favored as particles are farther apart and the aggregation is more influenced by the rate of reaction, so particles require higher temperatures to overcome the repulsive forces and aggregate.

An alternative interpretation of the results is that the liquors incubated at a low temperature are also within the DLCA regime. In this interpretation, the slower aggregation kinetics result in more open structures that may behave as a water-entrapping hydrogel and exhibit high filtration resistance. Prior work has linked coprecipitation of silica with lignin in nonwood soda black liquors to hydrogel formation and poor filterability. In this scenario, it could be hypothesized that the low-density lignin hydrogel collapses during drying, yielding the dark, smooth particles observed in this work (Figure ).

In summary, motivated by batch-to-batch heterogeneity in lignin chemical and physical properties observed during bench-scale processing, this work investigated the impact of lignin recovery conditions on the lignin properties. Lignin recovered by acidification from alkaline pretreatment liquors was demonstrated to exhibit source- and solids-dependent transition temperatures influencing particle structure and filtration behavior. Dark, brittle, and slow-filtering lignins were recovered below this threshold, while lignins recovered above it were lighter, powdery, and fast-filtering, which were attributed to differences in aggregation mechanisms.
