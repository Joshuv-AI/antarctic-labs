 ABSTRACT

Silicon carbide (SiC) passive thermometry has emerged as a promising post-irradiation examination (PIE)
technique for estimating irradiation temperature near the end of irradiation. While dilatometry techniques
have been traditionally used to analyze prismatic samples after irradiation, Raman spectroscopy has
recently been shown to provide comparable results by analyzing Raman-active phonon modes. In this
work, Raman spectroscopy has been applied to the SiC layer of cross-sectioned irradiation tristructural
isotropic (TRISO) particles from the AGR-5/6/7 experiment to evaluate the feasibility of particle-scale
passive thermometry.

Two-dimensional Raman mapping was used to measure the position of the SiC longitudinal optical (LO)
phonon, which was then converted to an apparent irradiation temperature using a previously established
empirical correlation. Particles from AGR-5/6/7 Compacts 2-2-1 and 5-1-3 were selected as their
calculated time-averaged, volume-averaged (TAVA) temperatures (828°C and 706°C, respectively) fall
within the range of the sensitivity of the experimental approach. The use of SiC thermometry was
anticipated to confirm or highlight potential deviations from calculated end-of-life TAVA temperature
across compacts. Four particles from Compact 2-2-1 and two particles from Compact 5-1-3 were selected
based on 110mAg inventory (either some measurable activity or below the minimum detection limit) which
is commonly used as an indicator of in-pile temperature variation of particles within the same compact.
Across every particle selected it was determined that the average LO peak position was located around
968 cm-1 to 969 cm-1. Using the previously determined empirical correlation, this corresponds to an
irradiation temperature around 950°C to 966°C, which does not align with the reported TAVA
temperature values. This discrepancy in apparent irradiation temperatures likely reflects a combination of
uncertainties in the calculated particle temperatures and differences in irradiation history between the
present specimens and those used to establish the empirical Raman calibration such as neutron flux
(damage rate) and SiC microstructure (as fabricated and irradiated).

 1. INTRODUCTION

Tristructural isotropic (TRISO) coated particle nuclear fuel typically consist of multiphase uranium oxide
and uranium carbide (UCO) kernels surrounded by porous carbon buffer, inner pyrolytic carbon (IPyC),
silicon carbide (SiC), and outer pyrolytic carbon (OPyC) coatings [1]. The buffer accommodates fission-
product recoils and gases from the kernel as well as swelling of the kernel, while the surrounding
structural coatings maintain particle integrity during irradiation. In particular, the chemical-vapor-
deposited cubic (3C) SiC layer serves as the primary barrier to the release of fission products. The
performance of this barrier is influenced by irradiation temperature, fast neutron fluence, burnup, coating
microstructure, and the evolving mechanical state of the particle [2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Temperature has been
inferred to be a primary driver regarding mobility of fission products within intact particles and through
layer release of fission products during irradiation. The influence of temperature on fission-product
release has been inferred from compact-level release behavior compared with calculated compact time-
averaged, volume-averaged temperature (TAVA) [7, 8] and particle-level assessments based on silver
retention correlated with greater distribution of fission product species in coating layers for particles [9].
Accurate determination of irradiation temperature is therefore important for interpreting post-irradiation
examination (PIE) results and understanding particle-to-particle variations in fission-product retention.

The US Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy’s Advanced Gas Reactor (AGR) Fuel
Development and Qualification program has conducted a series of irradiation, safety testing, and PIE
campaigns to establish the performance of UCO TRISO fuel under high-temperature gas-cooled-reactor
conditions [10, 11, 12]. The AGR-5/6/7 irradiation test was the most recent irradiation test and consisted
of five independently controlled capsules containing TRISO fuel compacts irradiated in the Advanced
Test Reactor (ATR) at Idaho National Laboratory. Ongoing PIE is being used to assess in-pile fuel
performance, safety-testing performance, characterize irradiated fuel properties, and provide data for fuel-
performance model validation and qualification.

One notable result from AGR-5/6/7 was the unexpectedly low retention of 110mAg in Capsule 2. Most
Capsule 2 compacts exhibited 110mAg measured versus calculated (M/C) activity ratios of approximately
0.2 or lower, with a capsule average ratio of 0.22 despite a calculated capsule average TAVA fuel
temperature of approximately 817C. Repeated precision gamma scanning (PGS) measurements [13] and
compact level PIE [14, 15] confirmed low 110mAg compact inventories. Substantial 110mAg activity was
detected in the Capsule 2 graphite holder. This behavior was unexpected because Capsule 2 was irradiated
at a comparatively modest calculated temperature and because previous AGR experiments generally
showed comparable 110mAg releases at irradiation temperatures above ~1,100–1,150C [16]. The
AGR-5/6/7 Capsule 2 results motivated the selection of the Capsule 2 compact for particle-scale
examination to determine whether the irradiation temperatures experienced by individual particles could
have differed from the calculated compact TAVA temperature.

Temperature has been an important variable in this investigation because relevant fission products with a
particular emphasis on silver can migrate through otherwise intact TRISO coatings, and prior AGR results
have demonstrated a general dependence of silver release on irradiation temperature [13, 17]. Calculated
TAVA temperatures represent spatially and temporally averaged model predictions and may not capture
localized temperature variation of individual particles caused by compact position, capsule geometry, and
irradiation history or uncertainties in the thermal analysis [18]. A technique capable of determining
irradiation temperature directly from the SiC coating of an individual particle would therefore provide
valuable complementary information for interpreting fission-product retention.

Passive SiC thermometry has traditionally been performed by measuring irradiation-induced dimensional
changes using dilatometry [19, 20]. During post-irradiation annealing, recovery of irradiation-induced

 2

defects produces measurable dimensional changes that can be related to the peak irradiation temperature
near the end of an irradiation. Although dilatometry is an established thermometry technique, it generally
requires dedicated SiC monitor specimens with geometric constraints and provides a bulk measurement
rather than spatially resolved information. It therefore cannot be used to determine whether the SiC
coating of individual TRISO particles selected for detailed PIE experienced temperatures different from
the calculated TAVA temperature value.

Raman spectroscopy offers a potential means of performing passive thermometry directly on irradiated
SiC with micrometer-scale spatial resolution [20, 21]. Neutron irradiation introduces lattice defects and
swelling in 3C-SiC, producing a downshift, broadening, and reduction in intensity of the longitudinal
optical (LO) phonon band. Koyanagi et al. established an empirical relationship between irradiation
temperature and post-irradiation LO band position by characterizing the same irradiated SiC specimens
using Raman spectroscopy and dilatometry [21]. The reported relationship can be expressed as Eq. (1).

However, the applicability of the published empirical correlation to the irradiated TRISO SiC layer has
not been established. The SiC coatings in AGR particles were produced by fluidized-bed chemical vapor
deposition (FBCVD) and may differ from the other passive SiC specimens in grain size, texture, defect
structure, and residual stress. The AGR particles experienced fluences within the range tested in previous
work; however, the neutron fluxes, and therefore damage rates, differed from those used to establish the
empirical correlation. Further, the TRISO fuel particles from the AGR program experienced varying
irradiation temperatures over the course of the irradiation with AGR-5/6/7 Capsule 2 reporting TAVA
min and max values of 736°C and 874°C for all Capsule 2 compacts [22]. This time dependence on
temperature likely impacts the accumulated irradiation-induced defect population and structure. Because
the measured Raman response reflects the retained irradiation-induced defect population, it may depend
on not only irradiation temperature and final accumulated damage but also the rate at which damage was
produced and annealed during irradiation. In addition, stresses generated by irradiation-induced changes
within the SiC layer from the other TRISO layers may influence the measured SiC phonon position.

The objective of this work was to evaluate Raman spectroscopy as a particle-scale passive thermometry
technique for irradiated AGR-5/6/7 TRISO fuel. Compacts for evaluation were selected with compact
TAVA temperatures within the range explored in previous work [20, 21]. Two-dimensional Raman
mapping was performed across the SiC coatings of particles recovered from Capsule 2 Compact 2-2-1
(221, TAVA temperature of 828C and fast fluence of 4.721025 n/m2) [22], which was selected because
of its unexpectedly low retained 110mAg inventory, and Capsule 5 Compact 5-1-3 (513, TAVA
temperature of 706C and fast fluence of 3.391025 n/m2) [22], which was selected as a contrasting
specimen with a lower calculated irradiation temperature and greater 110mAg retention. Apparent
irradiation temperatures were calculated from the measured LO band position using the published
empirical correlation and compared with the calculated compact TAVA temperatures. Spatial variations
in the Raman response were also evaluated to assess the applicability and limitations of existing Raman
thermometry calibrations for irradiated TRISO SiC.

 2. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP

Particles for analysis were selected from AGR-5/6/7 Compact 2-2-1 (TAVA temperature 828C) [22]and
AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-1-3 (TAVA temperature 706°C) [22] based on whether they had a high 110mAg
inventory or no measurable 110mAg inventory. The 110mAg M/C inventory fraction [17, 23] represents the
relative retained inventory fraction of an individual particle relative to calculated inventory for a specific
radionuclide. Particles with “low” 110mAg M/C inventories have measured inventories below the minimum
detection limits (MDL) and are expected to have experienced higher temperatures in each compact
selected relative to the “high” silver particles with 110mAg M/C near or above the mean for measurable
silver respectively [24]. The time-averaged minimum (TA min) and time-averaged maximum (TA max)
temperatures are shown alongside the TAVA temperature in Table 1 [22]. Four particles from AGR-5/6/7
Compact 2-2-1 and two particles from AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-1-3 were selected, as described in Table 1
and depicted in Figure 1. For AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-1-3, the MDL was sufficiently “high” due to 110mAg
experiencing a number of half-life decays prior to inventory analysis such that the particle may have
retained up to ~77% of it calculated inventory fraction. The particles were mounted on a custom
MiniMET mount, cross-sectioned to approximately mid-plane, and polished as detailed in [25]. Each
MiniMET mount held two particles for a total of three MiniMET mounts.

MiniMET mounts were placed in a stainless-steel holder to facilitate measurement of the irradiated
particles in the Raman spectroscopy unit. The holder had a 1-mm-thick ThorLabs Infrasil® optical quartz
window with >90% transmission in the optical and had a pinhole to expose just the particles for
characterization while also providing shielding to limit the radiation dose from the particles to the user.

Raman spectroscopy was performed using the Renishaw InVia Qontor. The system is equipped with a
532 nm 50 mW laser and a Leica Plan N L 50x objective lens to focus the laser on the surface. The spot

 5

size was around 1-to-3 𝜇m with a penetration depth for the SiC expected to be 70 nm [26]. The system
was configured to take automated raster spectra in 1 𝜇m steps from the IPyC layer to the OPyC layer. One
concern was laser heating-induced annihilation of defects within the sample as thermometry approaches
essentially measure defect concentrations. This has been observed with ion-irradiated hexagonal (6H)-
SiC, where annealing irradiated 6H-SiC for 80 minutes was able to restore the LO peak [27]. Spectral
acquisition parameters were evaluated to ensure acquisition parameters would optimize the signal-to-
noise ratio (SNR) while not annealing the SiC. All measurements on irradiated particles were performed
with 10% power (expected 5 mW), 10 s exposure time, and 5 accumulations, capturing data from around
150 cm-1 to around 1920 cm-1. Two additional accumulations to remove signals from cosmic rays from
the spectrum were also performed with each spectrum. On the SiC layer of 513-RS40, 6 points were
measured with varying laser power and exposure times. The laser power was assessed from 1% to 10%,
for 5 s and 10 s exposure times for 5 accumulations.

Due to surface variation that may affect the position of the LO band, a single Raman spectrum may not be
representative of the SiC layer. By using Eq. (1) with a wavenumber 𝜔𝐿𝑂 and wavenumber 𝜔𝐿𝑂 − 1, it
can be determined that each cm-1 corresponds to 15.05°C. In addition, spectral dispersion drastically
affects Raman measurements, resulting in a ~1.76 cm-1 wavenumber resolution at 969 cm-1 [28]. Curve
fitting was employed to fit the LO peak due to the 1.76 cm-1 wavenumber resolution. Between 2400-to-
3500 spectra were collected for each analysis region to determine the temperature across the SiC layer
due to the sensitivity of the thermometry temperature to the LO band position. The gratings were 1800
l/mm. Standard curve fitting with WiRE v5.7 was used to more accurately track the position of the peak.
A diagram depicting the analysis orientation is included in Figure 2. Lopez-Honorato et al. determined
that the laser polarization may interact with optically anisotropic SiC polytypes and stacking faults [28].
To eliminate the effects of anisotropy on the spectra, SiC regions of interest (ROI) were rotated to align
with either an “east” or “west” orientation, as shown in Figure 2. Each particle was measured in two ~45
𝜇m by 30-to-45 𝜇m areas along its cross-section in 1 𝜇m steps. Each area was then processed to remove
the spectra corresponding to areas where no SiC signal was detected (i.e. IPyC and OPyC regions). For
each area, the remaining 2400-to-3500 spectra were analyzed using Eq. (1) to calculate the average
temperature.

3.2 CURVE FIT MODEL

Representative spectra for each of the particles are shown in Figure 4. The spectral features were noted as
follows: features from 150 cm-1 to 300 cm-1 reflect the acoustic modes of SiC [29]. The peak at 150 cm-1
was often exaggerated by the polynomial subtraction process but should not correlate with the resulting
LO peak phonon. From 300 cm-1 to 600 cm-1, there are several peaks representing silicon-silicon (Si)
bond modes [21] and acoustic SiC from possible other polytypes [29]. From 650 cm-1 to 1050 cm-1, the
sharp LO and TO bands are observed corresponding to the SiC structure. There are also bands in this
region corresponding to disordered or amorphous SiC [30] formed during irradiation. A total of five peaks
could be used to represent the spectra between 650 cm-1 to 1050 cm-1. From 1050 cm-1-to-1650 cm-1,
carbon-carbon bonds [31] are found as well as second-order SiC phonon modes [32]. For the purposes of
this study, only the peaks between 650 cm-1 and 1050 cm-1 were fit to accurately establish the position of
the LO peak based on the empirical fit established by Koyanagi et al. [21]. An observation from the
qualitative comparison of the representative spectra in Figure 4 is the magnitude and intensity of the
carbon-carbon bonds and second-order SiC phonon modes from 1050 cm-1-to-1650 cm-1. These features
are more pronounced for AGR-5/6/7 Compact 2-2-1 particles compared to AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-1-3
particles. This observation qualitatively suggests particles may have evolved different SiC defect
structures during irradiation for the two compacts analyzed.

on average in the spectra. To ensure artifacts from the OPyC and IPyC are removed from the data
analysis, a filter was then applied that would exclude any spectra with a 969 cm-1 peak intensity less than
the RMS noise level multiplied by 6. While this would remove most of the IPyC and OPyC, there were
occasionally artifacts from the background subtraction specifically in the IPyC or OPyC regions that were
not removed by the first filter. A separate filter would specifically be applied to remove these artifacts.
This allowed for fitting of areas that included the edge of the SiC.

3.3 DATA PROCESSING AND UNCERTAINTY

Using Renishaw’s WiRE® version 5.7 software, the data for each of the areas was processed using a
fifth-order “intelligent polynomial” baseline subtraction process. The post-baseline subtraction intensity
of 969 cm-1 was used to filter out spectra with intensities below six times the root mean square (RMS)
noise level. Occasionally, there would be artifacts from the processing in the IPyC and OPyC regions
around 969 cm-1 that would cause the primary filter to not be sufficient. In such cases, secondary spatial

 9

filters were applied if necessary to remove spectra with artifacts from the processing in the IPyC and
OPyC regions.

Once the data was processed and filtered, a model was designed to fit peaks from 650 cm-1 to 1050 cm-1.
Once the LO peaks were properly fit, the model data was exported to a Python script that would handle
the data in two ways: (1) average each LO peak as a function of radius to determine any radial trends and
(2) average the LO peaks for entire analysis region. For (1), filtered spectra were removed and the
remaining data was aligned based on their horizontal position across the thickness of the SiC layer
relative to the IPyC/SiC interface, with the far extreme being the SiC/OPyC interface. Each position along
the thickness of the SiC layer had a corresponding set of LO peaks along the vertical, forming a “strip”
corresponding to that horizontal position relative to the IPyC/SiC interface. The LO peaks were averaged
and given an uncertainty equal to its standard error (defined as the standard deviation divided by square
root of the spectra associated with it). The bundled LO peaks were used to determine the average and

 5. DISCUSSION

AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-1-3 particles had calculated TAVA irradiation temperatures bound by prior work
establishing SiC thermometry using Raman spectroscopy. Confidence in temperature was supported by
compact inventory analysis from PGS measurements reporting 110mAg M/C = 0.78 on the compact level
[13, 35]. The LO peak was expected to be partially convolved with the rest of the defect structure for
AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-1-3 particles based on calculated TAVA temperatures and previous results from
Koyanagi et al. [21]. The LO peak position implies these particles were either at higher temperatures
(~950ºC) than the predicted temperature range (TA min 495ºC to TA max 818ºC) or Eq. (1) does not
apply for this specific TRISO-SiC material system. Similarly, analysis of the AGR-5/6/7 Compact 2-2-1
particles reflected calculated temperatures (~950–966ºC) above predicted temperature range (TA min
728ºC to TA max 896ºC). Higher temperatures than calculated were not unanticipated as the silver release
behavior of this compact implied higher in-pile temperature than calculated as this was the initial
motivation for applying SiC thermometry to AGR-5/6/7 particles.

There are three questions to consider with these results:

(1) Why was the sharp LO peak observed in the SiC layer for the AGR-5/6/7 compacts when it was
 expected to be broad and downshifted from defects based on prior literature?

(2) Is there a discernable difference between AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-1-3 and AGR-5/6/7 Compact 2-2-1
 response?

(3) Is there a discernable difference between the particles with measurable silver inventory and the
 particles with MDL silver inventory within the same compact?

Why was the sharp LO peak observed in the SiC layer for the AGR-5/6/7 compacts when it was expected
to be broad and downshifted from defects based on prior literature? All particles had a defined LO
phonon peak around 967 cm-1 to 969 cm-1. By the established linear regression equation [21], this gives
temperatures higher than predicted from the TAVA temperature. One consideration is that the
microstructure of the SiC layers in the analyzed TRISO particles was different from the microstructure of
SiC passive thermometry samples. The TRISO SiC layers produced by fluidized-bed chemical vapor
deposition (FBCVD) have a finer grain structure [36] relative to traditional static chemical vapor
deposition (CVD) fabrication processes [37]. This reflects a greater grain boundary density in the 3C-SiC

 15

microstructure of TRISO particles than traditional passive SiC thermometry samples. Grain boundaries
serve as defect sinks and therefore influence irradiation-induced defect population and structure [4, 21].
The LO band position presented in this study for the AGR-5/6/7 TRISO SiC indicate differences between
AGR-5/6/7 TRISO SiC microstructure and the SiC microstructure that were analyzed in the work of
Koyanagi et al. [21] as well as the ASI SiC microstructure [20]. Both Koyanagi and the ASI SiC
thermometry project used high purity β-SiC from Dow Chemical (formerly Rohn and Haas) [20, 21]. The
microstructure of Dow SiC was analyzed using electron backscattering diffraction (EBSD) before
irradiation has been analyzed by Mulligan et al. and showed large grains dominated the SiC [38]. The
changes in the LO peak are associated with irradiation induced defect generation. The average grain size
of Dow-SiC produced by CVD was on the order of microns [38], the average grain size of the AGR-5/6/7
grains was around 159 nm [36] and approaching a nanocrystalline (nc-) SiC structure. Nanocrystalline-
SiC has a higher resistance to irradiation due to the increase in the density of point defect sinks which, in
turn, leads to a higher chance of annealing and radiation recovery [39]. The difference in the SiC
microstructure between TRISO-SiC and Dow-SiC was hypothesized to be resulting in a different
irradiation induced defect structure and therefore influencing the LO shift observed in TRISO-SiC
compared to Dow-SiC over similar temperature ranges.

Additional variables that are hypothesized to influence the observed response are associated with the
irradiation environment. The empirical fit from Koyanagi et al. was established from irradiations in the
High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) at Oak Ridge National Laboratory [21] while the AGR-5/6/7
irradiation was conducted in the ATR at Idaho National Laboratory. The HFIR irradiation likely
experienced a more constant flux and relatively stable temperature compared to the TRISO fuel samples
in ATR which, by the nature of fueled experiments, experienced a variable flux and temperature history.
This results in two different irradiation histories with different damage rates and thermal histories. The
explicit impact of irradiation conditions on the LO peak response requires further examination.

Is there a discernable difference between AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-1-3 and AGR-5/6/7 Compact 2-2-1
response? As mentioned, damage rate between HFIR and ATR irradiations are different; further,
AGR-5/6/7 Compacts 2-2-1 and 5-1-3 experienced different damage rates, as inferred from differences in
total fast fluence over the same irradiation period [22], within the same experiment which may influence
the final defect concentration. It is also possible that the particles from AGR-5/6/7 Compact 2-2-1 and
Compact 5-1-3 experienced higher than anticipated temperatures. The use of passive thermometry was
expected to be viable in the linear, saturable regime for point defect swelling which begins to breakdown
approaching 1000ºC [20, 21]. While the reported TAVA temperatures are below this regime, the daily as-
run temperatures show peak temperatures mid-irradiation of 1011°C and 942°C for AGR-5/6/7 Compact
2-2-1 and Compact 5-1-3, respectively [22]. These temperatures are near the transition for saturable
regime for point defect swelling, and compact temperatures were generally higher at end-of-life such that
defects associated with the LO peak shift may have annealed and reflected a signature associated end-of-
life and not that of the reported compact TAVA temperature. Also as previously stated the silver release
observed for AGR-5/6/7 Capsule 2 was higher than expected with Compact 2-2-1 showing 110mAg M/C =
0.51 [13]. Assuming similar release versus TAVA temperature trends to the AGR-2 irradiation
experiment [16], this would reflect a TAVA temperature above ~1050°C which would be outside the
range in saturable regime for point defect swelling. The AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-1-3 also reflected an
110m
 Ag M/C value that would suggest silver release, with compact inventory of 110mAg M/C = 0.78 [13].
However, Walker et al. [13] reported “fuel compacts in Capsule 4 and 5 likely retained nearly all their
110m
 Ag despite M/Cs less than 1” due to large uncertainties and limited inventory identified on the
surrounding holders. The sharp LO peak and limited shift in LO peak position would appear to be
indicative of higher temperature irradiation conditions than the reported by TAVA temperature for
particles from both AGR-5/6/7 Compacts explored.

microstructure between TRISO-SiC and Dow SiC and irradiation conditions are contributing to the
deviation from the empirical relationship established by Koyanagi et al. [21]. It is also possible that the

on TRISO specimens is not reasonable due to geometry, in-situ Raman spectroscopy high temperature
annealing studies could be employed to probe ultimate end-of-life temperatures by establishing the
temperature at which defects are annealed similar to the dilatometry approach established [19].

 6. SUMMARY AND FUTURE WORK

Raman SiC thermometry was employed on irradiated TRISO particles from the AGR-5/6/7 irradiation
campaign to support validation of the end-of-life irradiation temperature. By extracting the position of the
SiC LO phonon peak on TRISO particles, which has been demonstrated on irradiated SiC passive
thermometry samples, apparent irradiation temperatures were calculated. However, confirmation of the
irradiation temperature was inconclusive based on the data presented herein. It is possible that the SiC
thermometry was accurate for TRISO particle analysis and that the local SiC temperatures of the particles
are not representative of the compact TAVA temperatures with irradiation temperatures exceeding the
saturable regime for point defect swelling. However, the assumption was that the difference in SiC
microstructure and subsequent evolved defect structure reflects a deviation from the established linear
model for the relationship between LO peak position and temperature. It was also speculated that
differences in irradiation conditions (damage rate and variable temperature histories) between the HFIR
irradiated passive SiC thermometry and ATR irradiated TRISO particles may also contribute to the
observed discrepancy. Currently, there is not enough data to determine what was influencing these results.

Qualitative assessment of the Raman spectra between AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-1-3 and AGR-5/6/7
Compact 2-2-1 did show differences in accumulated SiC defect structure. The spectra associated with
AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-1-3 showed a higher intensity associated with SiC defects from 650 cm-1 to 1050
cm-1, despite no obvious shift in LO peak position. This observation supports the expectation that
AGR-5/6/7 Compact 5-1-3 experienced lower in-pile temperatures than AGR-5/6/7 Compact 2-2-1 and
provides support for the use of Raman spectroscopy to potentially establish a formal correlation between
in pile irradiation temperature and evolved SiC defect structure measured by Raman spectroscopy.
Qualitative assessment between “low” silver and “high” silver particles was inconclusive and likely
requires a larger sampling statistic to establish clear trends.

For future work, a dilatometry calibration will be needed to compare with the LO band of the TRISO SiC
or in-situ Raman spectroscopy high temperature annealing studies could be employed to probe ultimate

 21

end-of-life temperatures. While it determined that Eq. (1) could not be used directly on the TRISO SiC, it
does indicate that there may be some significant differences between the TRISO SiC and SiC used in
other projects that resulted in success with the technique. These differences should be better understood
before Raman spectroscopy can be considered as a valid characterization technique for determining
irradiation temperature using the SiC layers of TRISO fuel particles.
