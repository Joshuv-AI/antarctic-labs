Multi-level static and dynamic graph-theoretical analyses of resting-state functional networks in a Chinese cohort of preterm neonates
Authors: Ting Peng, Suhua Xu, Ying Lin, Jiaqi Li, Chunjie Jiang, Xin Xu, Miaoshuang Liu, Lin Zhang, Mingwen Yang, Zuozhen Lan, Juan Yue, Han Zhang, Jungang Liu, Wenhao Zhou, Guoqiang Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPreterm birth influences early functional brain maturation at term-equivalent age. Using resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging from a large Chinese neonatal cohort (62 term-born and 107 preterm neonates), we examined static and dynamic network organization using multi-level graph-theoretical analyses. Preterm neonates exhibited reduced global integration and segregation, reflected by lower global efficiency, clustering coefficient, and local efficiency, together with increased characteristic path length. Widespread nodal and modular alterations were observed across multiple networks. Dynamic analyses revealed selective edge-level disturbances involving the right parahippocampal gyrus and its connections with default mode, visual, and limbic networks, despite limited group differences in global or nodal dynamic metrics. Developmental analyses further showed associations between specific global metrics and postmenstrual age at scan. Network measures were also associated with prenatal factors, including multiple pregnancy and cesarean delivery. These findings characterize early alterations in static and dynamic functional network organization after preterm birth and highlight prenatal factors potentially related to neonatal brain development.Keywords: graph theory, functional connectivity, preterm neonates, brain development, dynamic network

Introduction

Preterm birth, defined as delivery before 37 weeks of gestation, remains a major global health concern, affecting approximately 9.9% of live births worldwide.1,2 The late gestational period represents a critical stage of human brain development, characterized by rapid volumetric growth, synaptogenesis, and the emergence of large-scale functional network integration, processes that are highly dependent on the intrauterine environment.3,4 Premature exposure to the extrauterine environment may disrupt these maturational processes, resulting in altered cortical folding, reduced white matter integrity, and atypical patterns of functional connectivity (FC).5,6,7 These early disturbances are believed to contribute to the long-term neurodevelopmental impairments frequently observed in preterm populations.8 Therefore, understanding the impact of preterm birth on early brain network organization is essential for elucidating the neural mechanisms underlying these outcomes and identifying potential early biomarkers of neurodevelopmental risk.

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) provides a powerful, noninvasive tool for characterizing spontaneous neural activity and interregional functional coupling in the developing brain by measuring blood oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) signals during rest.9 Through graph-theoretical modeling, the brain can be represented as a complex network composed of nodes (brain regions) and edges (functional connections), enabling quantitative assessment of topological properties such as integration (e.g., global efficiency, Eg), segregation (e.g., clustering coefficient, Cp), and modularity.10,11 These metrics provide critical insights into the emergence and maturation of local specialization and global communication during early life by capturing complementary aspects of network organization, including information integration, segregation, and resilience, all of which are particularly sensitive to neurodevelopmental alterations.10

Accumulating evidence suggests that preterm birth alters functional network topology at term-equivalent age. Multiple studies have reported reductions in network integration and segregation, accompanied by disrupted modular organization, in preterm neonates compared with term controls.12,13,14 For example, Gozdas et al.12 reported lower rich-club coefficients and assortativity in very preterm neonates, even in the absence of overt brain injury. Bouyssi-Kobar et al.13 further observed reduced integration, segregation, and modularity, with bronchopulmonary dysplasia and prolonged oxygen support specifically associated with reduced network integration. Similarly, using data from the developing human connectome project (dHCP), López-Guerrero et al.14 demonstrated gestational age (GA)-dependent reductions in both integration and segregation.

Despite these advances, most neonatal studies have relied on static FC analyses, which assume stable connectivity throughout the scan and therefore overlook the temporal fluctuations inherent to developing brain dynamics.15 Dynamic FC approaches have begun to address this limitation. For instance, sliding-window clustering analyses have shown that late preterm neonates preferentially occupy less integrated connectivity states, suggesting reduced functional flexibility.16 In parallel, methods such as leading eigenvector dynamics analysis (LEiDA) have been applied to characterize recurrent whole-brain phase-locking states and their temporal properties during early life.17 However, these dynamic FC studies have largely focused on state-based descriptions of connectivity patterns, with limited investigation from a graph-theoretical perspective. Consequently, time-resolved alterations in network topology remain poorly understood in preterm populations. Dynamic graph-theoretical analyses may provide a more comprehensive characterization of neural flexibility and functional adaptability.18,19

Although recent studies have begun to investigate dynamic network organization in preterm neonates, a comprehensive multi-level framework integrating both static and dynamic graph-theoretical analyses remains lacking. Furthermore, most previous investigations have been conducted in Western populations, making it unclear whether similar network alterations are present in East Asian cohorts, in which genetic, environmental, and perinatal factors may differ.20

In the present study, we examined the multi-level static and dynamic topological organization of resting-state functional networks in a large cohort of Chinese preterm neonates scanned at near-term-equivalent age. Using the automated anatomical labeling (AAL) parcellation and a hierarchical graph-theoretical framework, we aimed to (1) assess group differences between preterm and term neonates in static global-, modular-, nodal-, and edge-level metrics; (2) evaluate group differences in the temporal variability of dynamic metrics across these network levels; and (3) explore associations among network measures, postmenstrual age (PMA) at scan, and prenatal clinical factors. We hypothesized that preterm neonates would exhibit reduced network integration and segregation, accompanied by lower temporal variability in dynamic connectivity, reflecting delayed or disrupted maturation of large-scale functional brain networks.

Group differences in static graph-theoretical metrics between term and overall preterm groups and across term and preterm subgroups

Comparisons of static global network metrics between term and preterm neonates are illustrated in Figures 2A–2E. After false discovery rate (FDR) correction, term neonates demonstrated significantly higher Eg, Cp, and local efficiency (Eloc) than preterm neonates (all pFDR < 0.01), whereas preterm neonates exhibited significantly greater characteristic path length (Lp) (pFDR < 0.001). No significant group difference was observed for small-worldness index (Sigma) (pFDR = 0.366). Collectively, these results indicate impaired network integration and segregation in preterm neonates, suggesting immature topological organization of brain networks at near-term-equivalent age.

Further analyses across gestational subgroups revealed significant overall group effects for Eg, Cp, and Eloc (all pFDR < 0.05), whereas no significant differences were detected for Sigma or Lp (Figures 2F–2J). Specifically, late preterm neonates exhibited lower Eg (pFDR = 0.025) and Eloc (pFDR = 0.013) than term neonates. At the nodal level, no significant between-group differences in betweenness centrality (Bc) survived FDR correction (Figures 3A–3D). However, preterm neonates displayed significantly reduced degree centrality (Dc), nodal clustering coefficient (NCp), nodal efficiency (Ne), and nodal local efficiency (NLe) across multiple large-scale networks, including the default mode network (DMN), visual network (VIS), limbic network (LIM), and sensorimotor network (SMN). These widespread alterations in nodal strength and efficiency suggest broadly disrupted functional network organization in the preterm neonatal brain. Group differences in dynamic graph-theoretical metrics and edge variability

Group comparisons of the temporal variability of dynamic global metrics are shown in Figures 5A–5E. After FDR correction, no significant group differences were observed in any dynamic global or nodal metrics, indicating broadly comparable time-varying topological properties between term and preterm neonates. Given the absence of significant overall effects, subgroup analyses were not conducted for dynamic metrics, consistent with our predefined statistical strategy.

However, analysis of edge-level variability revealed distinct alterations in temporal FC dynamics (Figures 5F and 5G). Preterm neonates exhibited significantly reduced variability within a network comprising 117 edges and 70 unique nodes (NBS-corrected). This network prominently involved the right parahippocampal gyrus, which participated in 28 connections, accounting for 23.9% of all significant edges. These connections were broadly distributed across the DMN, VIS, and LIM. This pattern indicates reduced temporal variability in both intra- and inter-network communication in preterm neonates.

Associations between global graph-theoretical metrics and PMA at scan were evaluated separately in term and preterm neonates using linear and quadratic regression models. The model with the lower Akaike information criterion (AIC) was selected.

For static global metrics (Figures 6A–6E), Eloc was negatively associated with PMA at scan in term neonates (β = −0.016, p = 0.014, and pFDR = 0.087), whereas Eg showed a marginal negative association (β = −0.006 and p = 0.059). In preterm neonates, Eg was significantly negatively associated with PMA at scan (β = −0.008, p = 0.014, and pFDR = 0.087), whereas Lp exhibited a positive trend (β = 0.081 and p = 0.067).

For dynamic metrics (Figures 6F–6J), a quadratic association was identified between the variability of Sigma and PMA at scan in preterm neonates (β1 = −26.086, β2 = 0.342, and p ≈ 0.044), suggesting a potential nonlinear developmental trajectory. In term neonates, weak quadratic trends were observed for the variability of Lp and Eg (p ≈ 0.120–0.157). None of these associations survived FDR correction, consistent with the exploratory nature of these analyses.

Prenatal influences on static and dynamic global metrics in preterm neonates

Group LASSO regression identified multiple pregnancy as a significant positive predictor of Sigma in preterm neonates (β = 0.713, p < 0.001, and pFDR = 0.003). No other prenatal factors were significantly associated with static global metrics after FDR correction. The association remained significant in a validation model adjusting for GA at birth, postnatal age (PNA), sex, and mean FD (β = 0.056 and p = 0.001).

For dynamic metrics, cesarean delivery emerged as a significant predictor of the variability of Sigma. Specifically, preterm neonates delivered by cesarean section showed lower temporal variability of Sigma than those delivered vaginally (β = −0.875, p = 0.003, and pFDR = 0.022). This association remained significant after adjustment for covariates in the validation model (β = −1.523 and p = 0.006).

Discussion

In this study, we employed a multi-level graph-theoretical framework integrating static and dynamic network analyses to systematically characterize resting-state functional network organization in a large Chinese cohort of preterm neonates scanned at near-term-equivalent age. Our analyses revealed three principal findings: (1) preterm neonates demonstrated widespread reductions in static global, nodal, and modular network metrics, indicating altered network integration and segregation; (2) although dynamic global and nodal metrics were largely preserved, preterm neonates exhibited significantly reduced temporal variability at the edge level, particularly involving the right parahippocampal gyrus and its connections with the DMN, VIS, and LIM; and (3) specific prenatal factors, including multiple pregnancy and cesarean delivery, were predictively associated with alterations in static Sigma and the temporal variability of Sigma, respectively. Collectively, these findings provide systematic evidence from a Chinese preterm cohort that preterm birth affects not only the multilevel static topological organization of functional brain networks but also the maturation of their temporal dynamics, while highlighting the potential influence of prenatal factors on early functional network organization.

Static global network analyses revealed that preterm neonates exhibited lower Eg, Cp, and Eloc, together with higher Lp, compared with term controls. These findings are consistent with previous studies in Western cohorts reporting impaired network integration and segregation in preterm neonates at term-equivalent age.13,14 Notably, Scheinost et al.21 observed decreased Cp but no difference in Lp, which may reflect a limited sample size and reduced statistical power. From a neurodevelopmental perspective, network integration depends on the maturation of long-range white matter pathways that enable efficient communication between distant cortical regions, whereas network segregation relies on the refinement of short-range connections and the specialization of local circuit through synaptogenesis and localized myelination.22,23,24 Premature exposure to the extrauterine environment may disrupt these processes by impairing oligodendrocyte differentiation and synaptic development, thereby weakening both long-range integration and local segregation.25 The absence of a significant group difference in Sigma suggests relative preservation of small-world topology, possibly reflecting compensatory reorganization or delayed maturation rather than irreversible disruption.

At the nodal level, preterm neonates demonstrated widespread reductions in nodal centrality and efficiency, particularly within the VIS and DMN. Within the VIS, abnormalities extended from primary visual cortices to higher-order visual association regions, indicating multi-level disruption of visual processing pathways.26,27 Within the DMN, significant alterations in the hippocampus and parahippocampal gyrus may contribute to later impairments in memory and higher-order cognitive functions.28 More broadly, disrupted network integration and segregation have been associated with more severe neurodevelopmental impairment in preterm neonates,29 and higher Bc in regions such as the parietal and superior temporal cortices predicts better subsequent executive function.30 Moreover, a shift in functional centrality toward visual areas and away from motor regions has been reported in this population.31 These observations are consistent with the static network alterations observed in the present study and may help explain the cognitive and motor difficulties frequently documented in follow-up studies of preterm populations.

Additionally, reductions in Dc were predominantly distributed in the right hemisphere, suggesting a potential lateralized vulnerability of functional networks in preterm neonates that may be related to asymmetric developmental trajectories between the cerebral hemispheres.

Modular analyses further revealed globally reduced intra-module and inter-module connectivity in preterm neonates, whereas the DAN showed relative preservation. Interestingly, although edge-level NBS analyses did not identify significant differences in static connectivity, module-level metrics demonstrated widespread reductions. This discrepancy may reflect differences in methodological sensitivity across analytical scales. NBS is more sensitive to spatially clustered subnetworks with relatively strong effects, whereas module-level metrics aggregate information across multiple edges and may therefore capture more diffuse reductions of smaller magnitude.32 Together, these findings suggest a broadly distributed yet modest weakening of connectivity rather than focal abnormalities within specific subnetworks. The relative preservation of the DAN may reflect early resilience of attentional systems, although its long-term functional implications remain unclear.

In the dynamic network analyses, no significant group differences were observed in global or nodal metrics. This pattern may suggest that large-scale temporal fluctuations in overall network topology have not yet substantially diverged between groups at near-term-equivalent age. However, the relatively modest sample size may have limited statistical power, and the sensitivity of dynamic metrics may have been influenced by methodological choices (e.g., window length) as well as the high inter-individual variability characteristic of this developmental stage. Accordingly, insufficient statistical power or limited metric sensitivity cannot be excluded, and future studies with larger cohorts are needed to clarify these null findings. In contrast, edge-level analyses revealed significantly reduced temporal variability in preterm neonates. The affected subnetwork prominently involved the right parahippocampal gyrus, with numerous dynamic connections linking it to the DMN, VIS, and LIM. Functionally, these couplings support complementary processes, including the integration of contextual and intrinsic representations (DMN),33 the transformation of visual input into memorable scenes (VIS),34 and the association of emotional salience with memory (LIM).35 Temporal variability in connectivity is thought to reflect the capacity of brain networks to flexibly coordinate in response to changing demands; thus, reduced variability may indicate constrained cross-network coordination, particularly within circuits linking perception, memory, and emotion.28,36,37 Given the protracted maturation and perinatal susceptibility of white matter pathways in preterm populations, such reduced flexibility may reflect delayed maturation of associative systems and contribute to later cognitive and socio-emotional difficulties.38,39,40 Together, these findings indicate that preterm birth may influence not only the static organization of functional networks but also the development of their temporal dynamics. Notably, dynamic FC measures are sensitive to analytical parameters such as window length, particularly in neonatal datasets. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted cautiously. Future studies could evaluate the robustness of these findings using multi-window approaches or window-free methods.41,42

Taken together, the coexistence of widespread connectivity reductions and the relative preservation of specific network metrics may reflect the influence of early brain plasticity in preterm neonates. The neonatal period represents a critical window for activity-dependent network reorganization, during which the brain may partially adapt to early disruptions by maintaining core topological properties or redistributing FC.43 Such mechanisms may help mitigate the functional impact of early alterations, even when measurable group-level differences are present.44 However, whether these patterns represent adaptive compensation or delayed maturation remains uncertain. Longitudinal studies suggest that functional networks in preterm populations continue to evolve throughout development, with some alterations diminishing over time while others persist.45,46 Therefore, the present findings likely capture an early developmental stage, and future longitudinal investigations are needed to determine the extent to which these alterations may be attenuated through ongoing maturation.

To examine the developmental trajectories of global network metrics in term and preterm neonates, both linear and quadratic regression models were evaluated. For the static metrics, linear models provided the best fit for term neonates, whereas in preterm neonates, Sigma, Lp, and Eg followed linear trends, whereas Cp and Eloc were better characterized by quadratic models. The developmental patterns observed in the preterm group differed from those reported previously, which may be attributable to differences in postnatal scanning age and adjustment for GA.14,47 Notably, our cohort was assessed at near-term-equivalent age, a developmental stage marked by rapid reorganization and optimization of functional brain networks.24 During this period, selective pruning of excessive or redundant connections may enhance network efficiency. Differences in population characteristics across studies, including ethnic composition, sample size, and perinatal background, may also contribute to variability in the reported findings.

To our knowledge, no previous study has characterized the developmental trajectories of dynamic graph-theoretical metrics. In the present study, the temporal variability of Lp and Eg in term neonates followed quadratic patterns, whereas other dynamic metrics were better described by linear models. In preterm neonates, the temporal variability of Sigma exhibited a significant quadratic trend (p < 0.05, uncorrected), suggesting a potential nonlinear maturation trajectory. This finding highlights the importance of considering nonlinear developmental dynamics in future longitudinal investigations of neonatal brain network maturation.14,48

In the prenatal factor analysis, an exploratory Group LASSO model identified multiple pregnancies as a positive predictor of Sigma in preterm neonates. This association should be interpreted cautiously, as prenatal factors are closely interrelated, and residual confounding by key perinatal variables such as GA and BW cannot be fully excluded. Previous neuroimaging studies have reported only subtle structural and connectivity differences between twins and singletons, with effect sizes often attenuated after adjustment for GA and BW.49,50,51 These findings suggest that observed differences may reflect developmental adaptations or shared environmental influences rather than solely adverse effects. At near-term-equivalent age, such differences may manifest as transient variations in network organization, although their long-term implications remain uncertain. Conversely, cesarean delivery was associated with reduced temporal variability of Sigma, suggesting diminished flexibility of global network organization. However, delivery mode in preterm populations is strongly influenced by GA, perinatal risk, and clinical decision-making, rendering this association susceptible to residual confounding. Although cesarean birth may influence early brain dynamics through altered hormonal fluctuations and microbial colonization, these mechanisms cannot be directly evaluated within the present study and may be secondary to underlying clinical conditions.52,53,54 Overall, these findings suggest statistical associations between prenatal factors and functional network properties in preterm neonates; however, they remain exploratory and are limited by potential residual confounding. Accordingly, causal interpretations are not warranted, and validation in larger, longitudinal cohorts is required.

In summary, this study indicates that preterm birth is associated with disrupted static functional network topology and reduced temporal variability of dynamic edge-level connectivity in neonates scanned at near-term-equivalent age. We further characterized the developmental trajectories of global graph-theoretical metrics in relation to PMA at scan and identified specific prenatal clinical factors associated with global metrics in preterm neonates. By integrating static and dynamic graph-theoretical analyses in a Chinese neonatal cohort, this work extends previous findings and provides additional insight into functional network alterations following preterm birth. Future studies incorporating multimodal neuroimaging, genetic information, and environmental measures will be essential to elucidate the mechanisms underlying these changes and their relevance to long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although head motion and PMA at scan were controlled for, residual confounding arising from physiological noise, subtle age-related variation, or other unmeasured biological and environmental factors cannot be entirely excluded. Second, the AAL90 atlas used in this study was derived from an adult template and excludes cerebellar regions that may contribute to large-scale network organization. Although deep learning-based tissue segmentation and nonlinear registration were applied to improve anatomical alignment, this atlas may not fully capture the rapidly developing architecture of the neonatal brain. Future studies should incorporate higher-resolution, infant-specific functional atlases, and alternative parcellation schemes to better characterize neonatal brain network organization. In addition, future work should specifically investigate cerebellar contributions to large-scale network organization and perform more targeted analyses of functionally relevant regional systems, which may provide further insight into disrupted maturation patterns in preterm neonates. Third, the sliding-window approach employed for dynamic FC estimation may have limited sensitivity to rapid state transitions and remains dependent on parameter selection. Alternative approaches, such as the hidden Markov model (HMM), LEiDA, or other window-free methods, may provide complementary insights.41 Fourth, despite prenatal exposures temporally preceded the postnatal MRI measurements, the single-time-point study design limits causal inference and underscores the need for prospective longitudinal investigations. Finally, although our cohort represents one of the largest East Asian neonatal samples, the present findings were derived from a single Chinese cohort. Future multi-center studies involving geographically and demographically diverse populations are required to confirm the generalizability of these findings and further validate the observed associations across independent cohorts. To address these limitations, future longitudinal studies incorporating repeated neuroimaging assessments throughout early development will be essential to characterize how functional network organization evolves over time. Such designs may clarify whether the observed alterations persist, normalize, or diverge with maturation and help determine whether early disruptions in static and dynamic FC serve as biomarkers of subsequent neurodevelopmental outcomes in preterm neonates.55 Ultimately, integrating longitudinal neuroimaging with clinical follow-up may facilitate early risk stratification and inform targeted interventions. In addition, the contribution of genetic factors to early functional network development warrants further investigation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Participants

A total of 411 neonates were initially recruited between January 2023 and December 2024. To focus on relatively healthy preterm neonates, strict exclusion criteria were applied: (1) PMA at MRI scan outside 36.0–44.5 weeks (n = 17), corresponding to the commonly defined term-equivalent developmental window; (2) clinical abnormalities, including infectious, neurological, developmental, cardiac, congenital, or metabolic disorders (n = 159); (3) missing or poor-quality rs-fMRI data or preprocessing failure (n = 37); (4) mean FD > 0.5 mm (n = 11), consistent with previous rs-fMRI studies16; and (5) among twin pairs meeting all inclusion criteria, the neonate with the lower BW was excluded (n = 18).

After applying these criteria, 169 neonates (62 term and 107 preterm) were included in the final analyses. The participant recruitment flowchart and age distribution of the final cohort are shown in Figures 1A and 1B.

Ethics approval statement

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiamen Children’s Hospital (Approval No. 2022-026) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of all participants before study participation.

Method details

Clinical and perinatal data collection

Clinical and perinatal data were extracted from electronic medical records. Neonatal variables included GA at birth, PMA at scan, PNA, BW, sex, and 1- and 5-min Apgar scores. Maternal and perinatal factors included maternal age, delivery mode, gestational hypertension, gestational diabetes, risk of intrauterine infection, antenatal exposure to corticosteroids or magnesium sulfate, multiple pregnancy, amniotic fluid contamination, fetal distress, and maternal thyroid dysfunction or anemia.

MRI data acquisition and preprocessing

All MRI scans were performed at a PMA of 36.0–44.5 weeks (near-term-equivalent age) using a 3.0 T scanner (uMR890, United Imaging, Shanghai, China) equipped with a 64-channel head coil. Scans were acquired during natural sleep. Neonates were comfortably stabilized with soft padding and fitted with noise-attenuating headphones to minimize head motion.

High-resolution T1-weighted (T1w) structural images were acquired using a 3D fast spoiled gradient-echo sequence (repetition time [TR] = 6.7 ms, echo time [TE] = 2.3 ms, flip angle [FA] = 8°, acquisition matrix = 320 × 300, field of view [FOV] = 256 × 240 mm2, slice thickness = 0.8 mm, 208 slices). rs-fMRI data were acquired using a multiband echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence (TR = 800 ms, TE = 37 ms, FA = 52°, acquisition matrix = 116 × 116, FOV = 209 × 209 mm2, slice thickness = 1.8 mm, 72 slices, 450 volumes). Single-band EPI reference images and spin-echo EPI images with opposing phase-encoding directions were additionally acquired for co-registration and distortion correction.

Preprocessing followed an infant-optimized pipeline designed to accommodate the unique characteristics of the neonatal brain. T1w images were segmented using the United Imaging research platform, which incorporates a deep learning-based model to generate structural references for rs-fMRI co-registration.56 rs-fMRI preprocessing was implemented using FSL (v6.0.3; https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/fslwiki/) and ANTs (v2.4.4; https://github.com/ANTsX/ANTs/wiki/). The main preprocessing steps were as follows: (1) the first 15 volumes were discarded to allow BOLD signals to reach a steady state; (2) head motion correction was performed using the MCFLIRT module in FSL with 6° of freedom, and mean FD was retained as a covariate; (3) EPI distortion along the phase-encoding direction was corrected using the TOPUP algorithm; (4) functional images were co-registered to the corresponding individual T1w images using tissue boundary-based registration; (5) all spatial transformation matrices were concatenated and applied in a single resampling step in the individual T1w space to minimize interpolation-related signal loss; (6) high-pass filtering at 0.001 Hz was applied to remove low-frequency noise from the BOLD time series; (7) individual-level independent component analysis (ICA) was performed with 150 components; (8) noise components were automatically identified using a previously validated deep learning-based model,57 which classifies ICA-derived components (spatial maps and time series) into signal or noise categories using a residual neural network (ResNet)-based attention convolutional neural network (CNN) in a data-driven manner, and subsequently removed, followed by nuisance regression including motion parameters; (9) the denoised data were band-pass filtered (0.008–0.150 Hz) to retain low-frequency oscillations associated with neural activity; (10) all preprocessed images underwent visual quality control to confirm accurate co-registration and minimal motion artifacts; and (11) region of interest (ROI) time series were extracted using the adult AAL atlas, which was nonlinearly projected into each participant’s individual T1w space using deformation fields derived from ANTs registration. The first 90 regions (excluding the cerebellum and brainstem) were retained (AAL90), consistent with prior studies focusing on cortical and subcortical network organization, to ensure methodological consistency and robustness of parcellation.13,58 Volume scrubbing was not applied to preserve the temporal continuity of the time series for subsequent dynamic graph-theoretical analyses.

Static graph-theoretical, edge-level, and modular analyses

Each brain region was defined as a node, and pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients between all nodes were calculated to construct a 90 × 90 FC matrix. Correlation coefficients were Fisher Z-transformed, and self-connections and negative correlations were set to zero.

Static weighted network metrics were computed using the GRETNA toolbox (v2.0; https://www.nitrc.org/projects/gretna) across sparsity thresholds ranging from 0.10 to 0.40 (step = 0.01), consistent with previous studies.47,59 To reduce threshold dependence, the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for each metric.

Global metrics included Sigma, Lp, Eg, Cp, and Eloc. Nodal metrics included Bc, Dc, NCp, Ne, and NLe. These metrics were selected to comprehensively characterize the balance between network integration and segregation, fundamental principles of efficient brain function.10 A detailed description of each metric, including its interpretation and the potential implications of abnormal alterations, is provided in Table 1. Briefly, global metrics (e.g., Eg and Lp) characterize the efficiency of long-range information transfer, whereas Cp and Eloc reflect local clustering and specialized processing. Sigma quantifies the balance between integration and segregation. Nodal metrics (e.g., Bc and Dc) assess the topological importance and communication roles of individual brain regions within the network. Detailed computational procedures followed previous studies.60,61

For modular analyses, AAL90 regions were assigned to the Yeo seven-network parcellation, including the VIS, SMN, DAN, ventral attention network (VAN), LIM, frontoparietal network (FPN), and DMN (regional mappings are listed in Table S3).62,63,64 Mean connectivity strengths within and between modules were calculated for each participant across the same sparsity range, and the corresponding AUC values were obtained for subsequent analyses.

Group-level FC differences were assessed using two-sample t-tests with NBS correction (edge p < 0.001, component p < 0.05, 5,000 permutations).

Dynamic graph-theoretical and edge variability analyses

The sliding-window approach was selected because of its ability to capture continuous temporal variability in FC while maintaining methodological consistency with the graph-theoretical frameworks used in the static analyses.65,66 This consideration is particularly relevant to the present study, which aimed to integrate static and dynamic network topology within a unified analytical framework. In contrast, model-based approaches such as HMM primarily characterize discrete brain states and their transitions and may therefore be less suitable for directly quantifying continuous fluctuations in graph-theoretical metrics over time.67

Dynamic FC was estimated using the DynamicBC toolbox (v2.1; http://restfmri.net/forum/DynamicBC) with a sliding-window length of 50 TRs and 98% overlap, resulting in 386 windows per participant.68 Parameter selection was guided by previous studies applying sliding-window approaches in neonatal populations.58 For each window, a 90 × 90 FC matrix was generated using Pearson correlation followed by Fisher Z-transformation. Dynamic network metrics were computed using the GRETNA toolbox with the same parameters used for the static analyses. Temporal variability for each metric was quantified as the variance of its AUC values across all windows.69,70

For edge variability analysis, a 90 × 90 variance matrix was computed for each participant. Group comparisons were conducted using two-sample t tests with NBS correction (edge p < 0.0001, component p < 0.05, 5,000 permutations).66

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.2.3) and Python (v3.8.2). Between-group comparisons of demographic, perinatal, and motion parameters were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables.

Group differences in static and dynamic global, nodal, and modular metrics were examined using general linear models (GLM) or robust regression models, depending on residual distributions. PMA at scan, sex, and mean FD were included as covariates. Differences in static global metrics among gestational subgroups were further assessed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). When model assumptions were violated, Kruskal–Wallis tests or Welch’s ANCOVA were applied. Subgroups analyses for dynamic metrics were prespecified but conducted only when significant overall group differences were detected to limit multiple comparisons. Gestational subgroups were defined as early preterm (<34 weeks), late preterm (34–37 weeks), and term (≥37 weeks).

Associations between static and dynamic global metrics and PMA at scan were evaluated using both linear and quadratic regression models, adjusting for GA at birth, sex, and mean FD. The optimal model was selected based on the Akaike information criterion.

Within the preterm group, Group LASSO regression was applied to identify prenatal factors associated with static and dynamic global metrics. Candidate factors included maternal age, delivery mode, gestational hypertension, gestational diabetes, risk of intrauterine infection, antenatal corticosteroid or magnesium sulfate exposure, multiple pregnancy, amniotic fluid contamination, fetal distress, and maternal thyroid dysfunction or anemia. Models were adjusted for GA at birth, PNA, sex, and mean FD. The regularization parameter (λ) was determined using 5-fold cross-validation, and the value yielding the highest mean predictive correlation in the validation sets was selected to balance model complexity and predictive performance. Although both prenatal and postnatal data were collected during neonatal hospitalization, prenatal variables reflected exposures occurring before birth and therefore temporally preceded the postnatal MRI measurements. Accordingly, this analysis was treated as a retrospective longitudinal design, in which prenatal factors were considered temporal predictors of postnatal brain network organization. Significant predictors were further validated using ordinary least squares (OLS) and multivariate linear regression analyses. Collinearity among prenatal variables was addressed through Group LASSO’s group-wise penalty, and the condition number was subsequently examined in the OLS models to confirm the absence of harmful multicollinearity.

All statistical tests were two-tailed. Unless otherwise specified, multiple comparisons were corrected using the FDR method, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001). For analyses that did not involve multiple comparisons, an uncorrected p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
