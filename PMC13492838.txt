Effectiveness of unified protocols for transdiagnostic treatment in improving the symptoms of depression, stress, anxiety, emotion regulation, and cognitive flexibility of students with subclinical symptoms of emotional disorders: A randomized clinical trial
Authors: Jafar Sarani Yaztappeh, Seyedeh E. Mousavi, Vahideh Javadi, Saina Fatollahzadeh, Ehsan Taheri, Maryam Bakhtiari, Parinaz S. Amiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:The current study aimed to investigate the effectiveness of unified protocol for the transdiagnostic treatment of emotional disorders (UP) on symptoms of depression, stress, anxiety, emotion regulation (ER), and cognitive flexibility (CF) of students with subclinical symptoms of depression and anxiety disorders.MATERIALS AND METHODS:In this assessor-blinded randomized clinical trial with pretest, post-test, and 6-month follow-up, 30 Farhangian University students in 2024 were selected through convenience sampling and randomly assigned to either an intervention or control group (n = 15 each). The intervention group received 14 90-minute UP sessions. Assessments included the DASS-21, Emotion Regulation Questionnaire, and Cognitive Flexibility Inventory. Data were analyzed using SPSS 26 and ANOVA.RESULTS:ANOVA analysis revealed significant reductions in symptoms of depression (F = 279.54, P = 0.001, ŋ² = 0.90), anxiety (F = 265.55, P = 0.001, ŋ² = 0.90), and stress (F = 194.05, P = 0.001, ŋ² = 0.87) in the intervention group from baseline to post-treatment, and these improvements were sustained at 6-month follow-up. Significant interaction effects (time × group) were also observed for depression (F = 79.58, P = 0.001, ŋ² = 0.85), anxiety (F = 21.06, P = 0.001, ŋ² = 0.60), and stress (F = 97.28, P = 0.001, ŋ² = 0.87), indicating greater improvements in the intervention group compared to controls. Furthermore, post-treatment improvements were significant in cognitive flexibility (between-group: F = 9.80, P = 0.001, ŋ² =0.21) and emotion regulation (interaction: F = 11.6, P = 0.001, ŋ² = 0.46).CONCLUSIONS:The results indicated the effectiveness of UP in improving ER and CF and reducing subclinical symptoms of students in the intervention group, indicating the importance of UP as the preferred treatment in emotional disorders and also the significance of preventing the complete clinical manifestation of subclinical symptoms of emotional disorders.

Introduction

Depression and anxiety disorders are common mental disorders.[1] In general, patients with mixed anxiety and depression exhibit more severe symptoms, a higher level of functional impairment, a greater tendency to commit suicide, and a decrease in the quality of life.[234] According to the genetic, biological, neurological, and psychological pathological information, some components and transdiagnostic features are common in all types of emotional disorders.[56]

Emotion regulation refers to a process that helps people to be aware of their emotions, to know how they experience them, and to know how they should use them.[7] The immediate effect of these processes on emotion, cognition, and behavior can be adaptive or maladaptive.[8] In general, maladaptive emotion regulation strategies, such as rumination, suppression, or self-esteem reduction, are associated with more pathological symptoms,[910] whereas adaptive emotion regulation strategies, such as active problem-solving, acceptance, and retraining, can reduce these symptoms.[91112] Cognitive flexibility is another transdiagnostic factor, which plays an important role in emotional disorders. It is the ability to live in the present moment, fully aware of emotions, feelings, and thoughts, accepting them, and trying to act toward values.[13] It also refers to a person’s ability to adapt her/his thoughts and behavior to environmental changes. The risk of psychological symptoms such as anxiety and depression increases in cases who are not able to adapt their emotional response to the situation.[14] Emotion regulation and cognitive flexibility have significant differences between people with symptoms of emotional disorders and those without.[151617]

Considering the aspects of emotional disorders, the evidence shows that the transdiagnostic approach can be used and is effective for the treatment of these disorders.[1819] Integrative transdiagnostic treatment targets these malfunctioning cores and vulnerability and maintenance factors.[20] The advantages of transdiagnostic preventive therapy make it a preferable treatment to cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT). This protocol can have more extensive and lasting effects on preventing or reducing disorders and symptoms.[21] It can also be used as a preventive program in some academic settings.[22] Since this protocol targets several coexisting disorders, it is time- and cost-effective for therapists and clients.[23] Diagnosis-specific cognitive-behavioral methods are recognized as evidence-based treatments for emotional disorders. Nonetheless, these methods face several limitations, including inadequate consideration of comorbid conditions, reliance on multiple theoretical models for individual diagnoses, the existence of numerous treatment protocols, restricted patient access to the most effective treatments, and challenges in the training of specialized practitioners.[2425] Transdiagnostic approaches focus on the shared characteristics of emotional disorders rather than their distinct features. These methods aim to address comorbid disorders by creating unified and innovative therapeutic protocols.[26]

This emotion-centered therapy consists of five components: training and awareness about emotions, making challenges using anxiety-causing and negative cognitive evaluations, prevention of emotional avoidance, identifying and correcting maladaptive action tendencies or emotion-based behaviors, and increasing awareness and tolerance of bodily sensations during exposure. These components target mood characteristics, especially neuroticism, which was mentioned as an underlying variable, and emotion regulation disorder as the basis of anxiety, depression, and related disorders.[27]

Ito et al.[18] showed that the transdiagnostic approach is effective for patients with anxiety disorders and depression. Yan et al.[28] assessed 102 students with emotional disorders and showed that UP generally reduces negative effects and increases positive effects of depression and anxiety symptoms, emotion regulation problems, and positive effects. Bermudez et al.[29] also showed that UP reduces depression symptoms and improves the quality of life of medical students. In a systematic review and meta-analysis, Ayuso-Bartol et al.[30] reported that UP for emotional disorders are effective for reducing anxiety, depression, and increasing quality of life.

The university years represent a challenging developmental phase marked by continuous efforts to cultivate identity and autonomy. During this time, students encounter a range of challenges, such as distancing from their families, adapting to unfamiliar environments, economic difficulties, and addressing accommodation-related concerns.[31] Cognitive flexibility is associated with fewer depressive symptoms and higher mental wellbeing and plays an important role in coping with challenging situations.[31] Considering the difficulties experienced by students, cognitive flexibility can be a protective factor on students’ mental health.

However, these data should be further confirmed and a few studies have been conducted on UP in low-income countries, such as Iran. A few studies have assessed the effect of UP on emotion regulation and cognitive flexibility and their interaction. The novelty of this study lies in its focus on the effectiveness of the Unified Protocol (UP) for students with subclinical symptoms of emotional disorders—an understudied and high-risk group. While most previous studies have examined UP in clinical populations, this research applies the protocol as a preventive intervention. Additionally, it evaluates both emotion regulation and cognitive flexibility simultaneously, two important transdiagnostic factors rarely studied together in low-income countries like Iran. The randomized controlled design with a 6-month follow-up also adds methodological strength to its innovative approach. Also, students with subclinical symptoms of emotional disorders are vulnerable cases that need prevention and treatment. Accordingly, this research was conducted to investigate the effectiveness of this treatment in this population. The current study aimed to investigate the effectiveness of UP on symptoms of depression, stress, anxiety, emotion regulation, and cognitive flexibility of students with subclinical symptoms of depression and anxiety disorders. The findings of the study will provide an opportunity to evaluate the integration of programs that teach self-stress management such as UP in the students’ curriculum.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This randomized clinical trial, assessor-blinded, parallel-group study was conducted under the supervision and approval of the Iranian Clinical Trials Registry (ID: IRCT20211223053494N4). In addition, it was conducted according to the CONSORT guidelines. The statistical population was all students of Farhangian University in 2024.

Study participants and sampling

The sample size was determined using Sharifi et al.’s[32] study, with 15 people as the number of samples for each group. This study is applied in terms of the purpose, and data collection was done using a random sampling method. Screening of the participants was accomplished in two stages; in the primary screening, they were evaluated using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21), and then, based on the cut-pint of this inventory (DASS-21 ≥ 16), people who were in the subclinical range of depression and anxiety were invited for secondary evaluation. At this stage, the participants were evaluated for clinical interview and evaluation of inclusion and exclusion criteria using a Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5-Research Version (SCID-5-RV). Participants were measured in terms of DASS-21, Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ), and Cognitive Flexibility Inventory (CFI) in three stages: pretreatment, post-treatment, and 6 months follow-up. This study received ethics approval from the Research Ethics Committees of Vice-Chancellor in Research Affairs – Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, and all participants signed informed consent forms.

Data collection tool and technique

At first, recruitment announcements for a study on anxiety and depression were posted on the social media of universities in Tehran, Iran. The place where the treatment sessions were held was in the Mental and Psychiatry Clinic of Tehran city. Students who were interested in participating contacted the research evaluation team to participate in the primary screening by online DASS-21. Among those who participated in the first screening (N = 464), 123 students met the criteria to participate in the second evaluation (DASS-21 ≥ 16). Finally, 30 participants were chosen by the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria included (a) participants who were aged 18–65, (b) those who had symptoms of anxiety and subclinical depression based on the cutoff point of 16 and above on the DASS-21, (c) those being fluent in Persian, and (d) those giving full informed consent to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria also included (a) a history of psychosis, (b) substance abuse disorders, (c) bipolar mood disorder, (d) personality disorders, (e) depression and anxiety related to a medical condition, (f) history of psychological interventions from 1 year ago (more than five sessions), (g) using the psychotic and psychotropic medications, or other mental health therapies, (h) history of hospitalization for depression and anxiety, and (i) having active suicidal thoughts or attempted suicide in the past 6 months (evaluation by a clinical psychologist). Furthermore, if the participants were absent for more than two sessions during the interventions or did not participate in any of the post-treatment and follow-up assessments, they were excluded from the study. 30 people participated in this study and were divided into the control (15 people) and intervention (15 people) groups. In the experimental group, there were 14 women (93.3%) and 1 male (6.7%), and in the control group, there were 14 women (93.3%) and 1 male subject (6.7%). Also, in the experimental group, 6 cases (40%) were married and 9 cases (60%) were single, and in the control group, 9 cases (60%) were married and 6 (40%) cases were single. In addition, in the experimental group, 6 cases (40%) were employed and 9 cases (60%) were unemployed, and in the control group, 6 cases (40%) were employed and (60%) 9 cases were unemployed. Also, in the experimental group, 3 cases (20%) had a diploma, 8 people (53.3%) had a bachelor’s degree, and 4 cases (26.7%) had a master’s degree, whereas in the control group, 1 person (6.7%) had a diploma, 6 cases (40%) were undergraduate students, 6 cases (40%) had a master’s degree, and 2 cases (13.3%) had a PhD.

Participants initially completed an informed consent form, and those eligible (30 people) were randomly allocated to the intervention and control groups. Participants were randomly divided into two completely identical parallel groups. The experimental group received the UP in 14 90-minute group sessions once a week implemented by a psychologist who was a Ph.D. student in clinical psychology under the supervision of a supervisor, and the control group (waiting list) did not receive any intervention. Considering ethical observations, after the intervention, the control group also received the intervention. The ideas, foundations, and practices of UP were covered in a total of 14 90-minute sessions, with content based on Barlow’s book on UP.[33] Allocation is performed by the primary investigator via the Internet using a laptop in front of the eligible participant. During the study, two participants left the group therapy, and three others from the control were excluded from the study. Certain participants did not complete the follow-up or intervention questionnaires, and those who were lost to follow-up were excluded from the analysis. In the end, the data of 30 participants were analyzed (see Figure 1).

Treatment protocol based on UP

Session 1: Motivational Reinforcement: The module is designed to enhance patient motivation for change by fostering self-efficacy and instilling confidence in their ability to succeed. It serves as a tool to sustain strong motivation for behavioral changes throughout the treatment process.

Sessions 2–3: Psychoeducation and Treatment Rationale: The module is focused on educating patients about different emotions, purpose of emotions, the functional aspects of emotions, including their various components such as behavioral, physiological, and cognitive elements, and cycle of avoidance and emotional behaviors.

Sessions 4–5: Emotional Awareness Training: The module seeks to cultivate a comprehensive awareness of one’s current emotional state by observing the interplay between feelings, thoughts, and behaviors. In other words, learning how to test the level of emotions and activities and performing behavioral experiments focused on different emotions are main contents in this module. This aspect is particularly relevant for individuals with alexithymia, who struggle with identifying and articulating emotions.

Sessions 6–7: Cognitive Reappraisal: The module emphasizes the evaluation of emotional experiences and the impact of assessments and attributions on physiological sensations and behaviors, including identification of maladaptive, automatic appraisals. Patients are taught to cultivate cognitive flexibility by generating multiple alternative interpretations when encountering intense emotions.

Session 8: Emotion Driven Behaviors and Emotional Avoidance: The module addresses patterns of emotional avoidance and coping strategies utilized in situations perceived as overwhelming and threatening by patients. It aims to help patients recognize maladaptive emotion-driven behaviors and develop strategies to modify concurrent patterns of emotional response.

Session 9: Awareness and Tolerance of Physical Sensation: In this module, patients participate in interoceptive exercises intended to elicit physical sensations similar to those commonly experienced during anxiety and distress. The goal of these exercises is to enhance awareness of the somatic aspects of emotions, thereby increasing tolerance to uncomfortable sensations.

Sessions 10–13: Interoceptive and Situational Exposure: The module concentrates on addressing emotional experiences that manifest in various situations through in vivo, imaginal, and in-session exposure exercises. Patients are systematically exposed to internal and external emotional triggers in a graded manner, aiming to enhance their tolerance of emotions and facilitate new contextual learning.

Session 14: Relapse Prevention: Throughout the module, patients are reinforced with the understanding that the reemergence of symptoms does not necessarily indicate a relapse. They are reminded that the coping skills acquired during therapy enable them to effectively manage emotional fluctuations and respond in adaptive ways.

Randomization and blinding

A psychologist blinded to the research goals conducted the clinical interview. A simple randomization method was used to assign participants to groups. The randomization method was performed by a research assistant who was not involved in recruitment, enrolment, and treatment. The assessors who collected and analyzed the data were blinded to the group allocation. The therapist was a Ph.D. student of clinical psychology with 3–4 years of experience. The statistical analyst remained blind to ensure unbiased data analysis.

Intervention

The UP group engaged participants in 14 interactive sessions with practical tasks, while participants in the control group received no intervention. All therapy sessions were conducted under the supervision of an assistant professor of clinical psychology.

Ethical consideration

The APA ethical standards were followed in the study, and we received approval from the Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences review board (IR.SBMU.RETECH.REC.1401.407), which is approved in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20211223053494N4) and has been performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki declaration and its later amendments. We obtained informed consent from all participants. They were assured that all gathered information would be kept confidential.

Measures

Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5-Research Version (SCID-5-RV)

The SCID-5-RV is a semistructured interview guide for diagnosing major DSM-5 disorders, typically conducted by trained clinicians or health experts. Sessions typically last 45 to 90 minutes. The research Version covers more disorders than the Clinician Version. Numerous studies have reported acceptable reliability and validity for the SCID-5-RV. The Persian version demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (0.95–0.99), test–retest reliability (0.60–0.79), and kappa reliability (0.57–0.72).[34]

Depression, Anxiety, Stress, Scale-21 (DASS-21)

DASS-21, a briefer version of the DASS-42 developed by Henry and Crawford in 2005, assesses depression, anxiety, and stress through a self-report questionnaire. It uses a 4-point Likert scale for responses. The scale has shown strong internal consistency in both English and Persian versions, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranging from 0.82 to 0.94 in nonclinical samples.[35] Depression and anxiety subscales demonstrated good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.79 and 0.74, respectively, in the Persian version of the DASS-21.[36]

Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ)

The ERQ developed by Gross and John[37] consists of ten items aimed at assessing strategies for managing emotions, categorized into two dimensions: (a) reappraisal and (b) suppression. In their study, Gross and John reported internal correlations of 0.79 for reappraisal and 0.73 for suppression. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for reappraisal was 0.79, while for suppression, it was 0.73. The test–retest reliability of the entire scale over 3 months was found to be 0.69.[37] Hasani,[38] utilizing a back-translation method, introduced the Persian version of the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ–P) in Iran. The alpha coefficients for both subscales and the overall ERQ–P indicated strong consistency, each exceeding 0.80. Structural validity of the ERQ–P was assessed through both explanatory and confirmatory factor analyses, revealing a two-factor structure.[38]

Cognitive Flexibility Inventory (CFI)

The CFI is a 20-item self-report questionnaire designed to measure various aspects of cognitive flexibility, enabling individuals to challenge and replace maladaptive thoughts with more adaptive ones. Respondents rate items on a 7-point Likert-type scale, reflecting their approach to challenging situations accurately. This inventory demonstrated good to excellent internal consistency. Additionally, they reported high test–retest reliability for both the total CFI score and its subscales.[39] The Persian version of The CFI showed good reliability with a test–retest coefficient of 0.71 and a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.90.[40]

Statistical analysis method

The quality of data collection of this study and the way of coding them was done under the direct supervision of the director of the clinical psychology department of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences. The method of data collection in each of the three stages of evaluation was reported to them. After collecting and entering data in SPSS 26 software, the data analyst provided the information in encrypted form. The Shapiro–Wilk test was performed to check the normal distribution of variables in both pretest and post-test. Levene’s test was also used to analyze the homogeneity of variances. Finally, univariate analysis of variance was used. Researchers who are interested in knowing how to collect, code, secure, and store the data of this study can obtain the required information by contacting the corresponding author of this study.

Within-subject differences for each measure (Pairwise Comparisons)

The result of Levene’s test confirmed the assumption of homogeneity of variances (P < 0.05). The results of the multivariate analysis of variance for Wilks’ lambda test to evaluate the total score of cognitive flexibility showed that the within-group (F = 9.80, P = 0.001, ŋ2 = 0.42) and between-group (F = 9.80, P = 0.001, ŋ2 = 0.21) effects were significant, but the interaction effect of time × group (F = 1.01, P = 0.37, ŋ2 = 0.07) was not significant.

Also, the results of the Wilks’ lambda test on the total emotion regulation score showed that the within-group effect (F = 35.96, P = 0.001, ŋ2 = 0.72) and the time × group interaction effect (F = 11.6, P = 0.001, ŋ2 = 0.46) were significant, but the within-group effect (F = 3.08, P = 0.009, ŋ2 = 0.009) was not significant.

Also, the results of the Wilks’ lambda test on depression showed that the between-group effect (F = 279.54, P = 0.001, ŋ2 = 0.90) and the time × group interaction effect (F = 79.58, P = 0.001, ŋ2 = 0.85) were significant, but the within-group effect (F = 0.65, P = 0.52, ŋ2 = 0.04) was not significant. Also, the results of the Wilk’s lambda test on anxiety showed that the within-group effect (F = 9.98, P = 0.001, ŋ2 = 0.42), the intergroup effect (F = 265.55, P = 0.001, ŋ2 = 0.90), and the interaction effect of time × group (F = 21.06, P = 0.001, ŋ2 = 0.60) were significant.

In addition, the results of the Wilks’ lambda test on stress showed that the within-group effect (F = 4.27, P = 0.05, ŋ2 = 0.24), the between-group effect (F = 194.05, P = 0.001, ŋ2 = 0.87), and the time × group interaction effect (F = 97.28, P = 0.001, ŋ2 = 0.87) were significant.

Discussion

This study investigated the effect of UP on symptoms of depression and anxiety, emotion regulation, and cognitive flexibility in students with subclinical symptoms of emotional disorders. This treatment caused a significant difference in the scores of anxiety and depression symptoms and improved emotion regulation and cognitive flexibility of the students.

This treatment, instead of a specific disorder, focuses on the main causes of underlying dysfunction in emotional disorders including the mechanisms that lead to maintaining the symptoms of the disorder, anxiety, and depression, which were simultaneously targeted.[15] This finding is in line with the Unified Protocol that according to the triple vulnerability model, general vulnerabilities are the underlying and common factor in most emotional disorders and should be the focus of therapeutic interventions.[18] This finding is in line with those of other studies.[152841] UP probably improves anxiety sensitivity and emotional avoidance as two major transdiagnostic variables, causing an improvement in the symptoms of emotional disorders. The Unified Protocol reduces the symptoms of emotional disorders in these people by increasing targeted behaviors and improving the general performance of people while experiencing unpleasant emotions.[42] Various studies have pointed to the role of recognizing and accepting emotions in reducing the symptoms of disorders. For example, Sauer-Zavala et al.[42] showed that increasing awareness of emotions and accepting them is associated with decreasing anxiety and depression scores. Boswell et al.[43] also showed that mindfulness can be considered as one of the mechanisms, by which the Unified Protocol reduces depression. In general, the emphasis of the Unified Protocol on the adaptive and functional nature of emotions, tolerating more emotions, and trying to identify and correct maladaptive efforts, while increasing positive emotion regulation strategies and reducing negative strategies, helps to facilitate the improvement of symptoms of emotional disorders, including anxiety and depression.[44]

Transdiagnostic interventions help people learn how to face their inappropriate emotions and respond to their emotions more adaptively. This method tries to reduce the intensity and incidence of using negative emotion regulation strategies, reduce the amount of damage, and increase the function by adjusting positive and adaptive emotion regulation habits. According to Lopez et al.,[45] teaching adaptive emotion regulation strategies helps to improve the control perception of people with emotional disorders. Therefore, it seems that the Unified Protocol, by using second-level mediators, such as emotion regulation, improves the perception of control and, as a result, reduces the anxiety and mood symptoms of people,[44] which was confirmed by the significant improvement of emotion regulation in our intervention group. In addition, emotion regulation strategies based on awareness recognition and acceptance of internal experiences, without changing or avoiding them, are a suitable alternative to maladaptive emotion regulation strategies.[46] In this regard, the Unified Protocol also aims to treat anxiety and depression by increasing the clarity of emotions and accepting them, especially through mindfulness training.

A significant increase in cognitive flexibility in the intervention group was also another important finding. Cognitive inflexibility has been proposed as a transdiagnostic structure that plays a role in the formation of common underlying symptoms in emotional disorders.[4748] Therefore, the effectiveness of UP in cognitive inflexibility can be explained by its transdiagnostic structure, which plays a role in creating emotional disorders. Cognitive flexibility is increased through understanding emotions, emotional awareness based on mindfulness, coping with emotional behaviors, and tolerance and introspection during UP. On the other hand, cognitive reconstruction in UP and identifying and challenging negative automatic thoughts and replacing them with correct thoughts, as one of the important goals of this protocol, can be effective in increasing cognitive flexibility.

Limitations and recommendations

The limitations of the present research include the small sample size, the use of an available sampling method, and the absence of active control groups, such as a placebo intervention or another proven treatment.

In future studies, it is suggested to use larger sample sizes, use a longer follow-up period (more than 6 months), compare the effectiveness of UP with other types of specific and disorder-oriented treatments, and use neuropsychological assessment tools to improve the study results.

Conclusion

The present study was conducted to investigate the effect of UP on depression and anxiety symptoms, emotion regulation, and cognitive flexibility in students with subclinical symptoms of emotional disorders at Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences. UP can be provided to improve subclinical manifestations of emotional disorders. In addition, due to its effectiveness in important cognitive and emotional variables, these findings as preliminary can be important in improving the quality of life of patients, social adaptation, interpersonal relationships, and academic and social performance of students.
