Identification of new fucosyltransferase-8 inhibitors: An integrated biochemical, biophysical, and computational approach

Muhammad Awais, Noor Rahman, Sumaira Javaid, Atia-tul- Wahab, Thirugnanasambandam Rajendran, M Iqbal Choudhary, Humaira Zafar

ABSTRACT
The α-1,6-fucosyltransferase (FUT8) mediated core fucosylation is responsible for several pathophysiological processes, including prostate cancer progression. FUT8 is a promising therapeutic target owing to its effects on cell migration and its capacity to convert androgen-dependent prostate cancer cells into androgen-independent cells. This study, therefore, investigates potential FUT8 inhibitors as therapeutic options for prostate cancer by targeting FUT8-mediated core fucosylation using in vitro, biophysical, and computational approaches. Eight of the 265 compounds showed significant cytotoxicity against the prostate cancer (PC3) cell line in in vitro screening. These compounds demonstrated dose-dependent reductions in cell viability, with IC50 values ranging from 2.94 ± 0.04 to 75.82 ± 2.10 µM. These compounds were non-cytotoxic against the normal human fibroblast (BJ) cell line. Biophysical and in silico analyses were conducted on active compounds to obtain atomic-level structural details of interactions between FUT8 and ligands. Among these, compounds 1, 3, 6, and 7 showed significant Saturation Transfer Difference Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (STD-NMR) effects and induced a decrease in the melting temperature (Tm) of FUT8 in the differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) assay (0.25°C–9°C), indicating ligand-associated destabilization of the protein. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation results showed that compounds 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 7 stably interacted with the FUT8 binding site. These compounds also exhibited anti-migratory activity in a scratch cell assay, as indicated by scratch closure of only 3.14% to 14.18%, compared with 45.45% to 100% in the control group. Our study identified compounds 1, 3, 6, and 7 as promising leads against the FUT8 enzyme. Further mechanistic and in vivo studies are required to evaluate their mechanisms of action for FUT8-driven prostate cancer progression.

Introduction Fucosyltransferases (FUTs) are bi -substrate enzymes responsible for the transfer of the fucose moiety from GDP-fucose (donor substrate) to the N - and O -linked glycans (acceptor substrate). This is termed as fucosylation and is the terminal step in the production of glycoconjugates. To date, 13 FUTs have been identified that differ in their acceptor substrates, including FUTs 1−11 (transfer to N -glycan), and 2 POFUT 1,2 (transferred to O -glycan). Among FUTs, FUT1-FUT2 and FUT3-FUT7 catalyze the formation of α-1,2 and α-1,3 linkages, respectively. FUT8 is the unique member solely responsible for core fucosylation ( α -1,6 linkage) of proteins, a process that involves the addition of a fucose sugar to the innermost component of N -linked glycans [ 1 ]. Previous research highlights the role of core fucosylation in regulating both intracellular and extracellular protein trafficking and function [ 2 , 3 ]. FUT8 has been implicated in various biological and tumor characteristics and is found to be upregulated in several cancers, including lung [ 4 ], liver [ 5 ], colorectal [ 6 ], ovarian [ 7 ], breast [ 8 ], melanoma [ 9 ], thyroid [ 10 ], pancreatic [ 11 ], and prostate [ 12 ]. Core fucosylation, caused by FUT8, is also known for modulating the humoral immune response by altering antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity [ 13 ]. The upregulation of FUT8 across various cancer types suggests that inhibiting its activity could be a promising approach to enhance antitumor immune responses [ 14 ]. Previous studies have shed light on the association between aberrant fucosylation and aggressive prostate cancer, highlighting the therapeutic potential of FUT8 in this context [ 15 – 19 ]. Utilizing prostate cancer models, researchers have demonstrated that the overexpression of FUT8 was sufficient to transform androgen-dependent LAPC4 prostate cancer cells into androgen-resistant cells [ 18 ] while significantly enhancing their migratory abilities. On the other hand, knocking down the FUT8 gene in PC3 cells, an androgen-independent prostate cancer cell line, resulted in a significant reduction in the migratory capacity of PC3 cells [ 19 ]. To date, no clinical fucosyltransferase inhibitor has been reported, despite the use of various strategies. One approach involves mimicking the natural substrate guanosine diphosphate fucose (GDP-Fuc), while alternative methods focus on creating fucose derivatives that are metabolized through the salvage pathway, targeting fucosyltransferases via competitive inhibition [ 20 ]. One such inhibitor, 2-fluoro fucose (SGN-2FF), is metabolized via the salvage pathway and has exhibited promising anti-cancer effects in both in vitro and in vivo studies [ 20 ]. SGN-2FF is a small-molecule inhibitor of fucosylation that directly and indirectly affects immune cells, tumor cells, and the tumor microenvironment [ 21 ]. The inhibitor is passively transported across the cell membrane, deprotected by esterases, and metabolized to its corresponding GDP analog [ 22 ]. SGN-2FF has been employed in various mouse models as an orally bioavailable inhibitor and has undergone testing in a phase 1 multicenter clinical trial for patients with advanced solid tumors (NCT# 02952989). However, the study was terminated after three years due to the overall benefit/risk profile, with no further details disclosed. Therefore, there is an unmet need to identify new fucosyltransferase inhibitors that are more efficacious or offer a more favorable benefit-to-risk ratio. Therefore, this study aimed to identify potential inhibitors of FUT8 through an integrated methodology combining in vitro , biophysical, and computational techniques. These include screening structurally diverse compounds (such as flavonoids, alkaloids, and nucleoside analogs, among others) from our in-house library for anti-proliferative activity against the PC3 cell line. Interactions between the hit compounds and the FUT8 enzyme were explored using Saturation Transfer Difference Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (STD-NMR), differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF), and computational studies. This was followed by assessing the anti-migratory potential of these compounds via the scratch cell assay. This integrative approach addresses the failure of GDP-Fuc mimetics and 2FF by combining biochemical assays, DSF, STD-NMR, and molecular dynamics to identify novel scaffolds. The hit compounds identified through this systematic approach substantially contribute to the development of novel therapeutic options for prostate cancer by targeting FUT8-mediated core fucosylation. Materials and methods Materials The human prostate cancer (PC3; CRL-1435 ™ , ATCC) and fibroblast (BJ; CRL-2522 ™ , ATCC) cell lines were obtained from the BioBank, and the compounds were obtained from the Drug Bank of Dr. Panjwani Center for Molecular Medicine and Drug Research, ICCBS. Human recombinant FUT8 protein (Cat# 11326-H08B) was purchased from SinoBiological, USA. Compound library A total of 265 fully characterized compounds were obtained from the in-house library of the Drug Bank of Dr. Panjwani Center for Molecular Medicine and Drug Research. These compounds were screened at a concentration of 100 μM, prepared in DMSO. Cell culture The PC3 [ 23 ] and BJ [ 24 ] cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 and DMEM, with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in a 5% CO 2 incubator at 37°C. Trypsin-EDTA (0.05%) was used to routinely passage the cells at a 70–80% confluency in T75 flasks. All the assays were performed with exponentially growing cells. Cell proliferation assay Compounds were screened for their anti-cancer and anti-cytotoxic ability using MTT (dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) assay [ 25 ]. Exponentially growing PC3 and BJ cells were plated at a density of 7,000 cells per well in a 96-well plate and incubated to form a monolayer. After a 24-hour incubation, the cells were treated with compounds at a concentration of 100 µM. Following a 48-hour treatment period, the MTT dye (0.5 mg/mL) [ 26 , 27 ] was added to each well, followed by 4 hours of incubation. The resulting formazan crystals were then dissolved in DMSO for spectrophotometric analysis at 550 nm. Controls included a standard vehicle dilution (1% DMSO) and empty wells for baseline readings. In summary, samples comprising protein (1 μM), ligand (1 mM), and Sypro®Orange (1:1000) were added to a PCR tube and subjected to heating at a rate of 0.3°C/min, ranging from 20 to 99.9 °C. All reagents were dissolved in Tris buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0). The fluorescence of the dye was monitored utilizing the Hex channel as a function of temperature. The resulting data were processed and analyzed using CFX Manager™. Three independent experiments were performed for each compound. STD-NMR experiment STD-NMR experiments were conducted on a 600 MHz Bruker Neo Avance spectrometer. The magnet was equipped with an inverse triple-resonance cryoprobe and automated sample changer (SampleCase™, Bruker, Switzerland). Compounds were prepared in DMSO- d 6 , and all experiments were conducted using a 20 mM deuterated Tris- d11 buffer (pH 8.0) at 298K. The procedure involved initial 1 H-NMR and STD-NMR experiments of the ligand (1 mM) in 550 µL of buffer (to prevent false positives), followed by the addition of the FUT8 protein (1 µM) and subsequent STD-NMR assessments. The standard pulse program stddiffesgp.3 (Bruker Biospin, Switzerland) with water signal suppression by excitation sculpting was used for STD-NMR experiments. Key experimental parameters included a 3.1 relaxation delay, a 30 ms spin-lock pulse, a 3.0 s saturation duration, and −0.50 ppm on-resonance and 35 ppm off-resonance offsets. The difference spectrum was obtained by subtracting the on-resonance spectrum from the off-resonance spectrum. The difference spectrum showed signals for the ligand protons that interact with the protein [ 27 , 30 ]. To quantify these interactions, the STD amplification factor for each proton was determined using the formula [ 27 , 31 ]: S T D A m p l i f i c a t i o n F a c t o r = ( I s t d I o ) × L i g a n d E x c e s s , where I o and I s t d represent the signal strengths in the off-resonance and difference spectra, respectively. Computational docking and simulations Receptor and ligand preparation. The structure of human FUT8, co-crystallized with GDP (PDB ID: 6TKV), was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank ( https://www.rcsb.org/ ). The compound structures were prepared, and their 3D coordinates were generated using the LigPrep module of the Schrödinger software suite (version 2022−1). Ligand preparation involved energy minimization, chirality correction, and addressing tautomeric and ionization states, generating 32 conformations per ligand. Protein preparation involved refining the FUT8 structure through preprocessing steps, including removing non-essential molecules, adding missing atoms, and adjusting hydrogens. This was followed by optimization to refine hydrogen bonds and correct protonation states, and minimization using the OPLS4 force field. A receptor grid was then generated around the co-crystallized GDP, ensuring comprehensive coverage and precise site representation, which was validated through careful inspection. This meticulous preparation facilitated subsequent computational docking studies and analyses. Molecular docking. Molecular docking of the prepared compound library with human FUT8, co-crystallized with GDP (PDB ID: 6TKV), was performed utilizing the Glide module of the Schrödinger software suite with extra precision (XP) [ 27 , 32 ] for enhanced accuracy. The docking process began with a comprehensive conformational analysis of each ligand within the receptor’s active site, followed by the application of multiple scoring functions (including Coulombic, van der Waals, and hydrogen-bonding evaluations) to ascertain ligand binding affinities. All XP docking parameters were set to default values, and post-docking minimization was performed to refine ligand geometries and optimize receptor-ligand interactions for stability analysis. Docking was validated by re-docking GDP. RMSD 0.152 Å confirmed the accuracy of the docking protocol (Fig S1 in S1 File ). Post-docking analysis of ligand-protein interactions was conducted to check the stability of the complexes. Energy calculation. The binding energies for the docked complexes involving the human FUT8 and the respective ligands were evaluated using the Molecular Mechanics Generalized Born Surface Area (MM-GBSA) [ 33 ] in the Prime [ 34 ] module of the Schrödinger software suite. The calculation was carried out using the OPLS3e force field [ 35 ] with the variable-dielectric generalized Born (VSGB 2.1) solvation model, incorporating empirical corrections to model the directionality of solvent effects. All parameters were set to default values to ensure consistency with widely accepted practices. Molecular dynamics simulation. The docked ligand-protein complexes were further analyzed through molecular dynamics (MD) simulations [ 36 , 37 ] using the Desmond [ 37 , 38 ] module of the Schrödinger software suite [ 37 ]. Simulation systems were set up in orthorhombic boxes, with a 10.0 Å buffer in all dimensions, using the SPC water model to solvate the complexes. Charge equilibrium within the system was maintained by adding Na + /Cl - ions, supplemented with an additional saline concentration of 0.15 M NaCl. The system was equilibrated using the NPT ensemble, maintaining a temperature of 310K and a pressure of 1 Bar. The OPLS4 force field [ 39 ] was used throughout the simulation, with integration steps proceeding in 2.0-femtosecond increments, facilitating the precise analysis of the complex’s behavior. Subsequent trajectory data from the MD simulations were systematically analyzed to evaluate the complex’s flexibility, structural stability, and key interactions. Scratch cell assay The cellular migration evaluation was conducted using a scratch cell assay [ 40 ]. PC3 cells, revived in RPMI-1640 with 10% FBS, were seeded into six-well plates at approximately 3 × 10 5 cells per well and incubated overnight. Subsequently, a 200 μL pipette tip was used to create a scratch across the central region of each well. Post-scratch, the cells were washed twice with PBS and treated with the respective compounds at their IC 50 concentration. Observation checkpoints were established at the onset, 24, and 48 hours, during which images of the designated areas were captured with a phase contrast microscope at 10X magnification. Scratches of treated cells were compared with those of the non-treated control cells. The experiments were performed in triplicate (biological replicates, n = 3). Image analysis was facilitated through ImageJ software (NIH, USA), focusing on quantifying the percent scratch closure by applying the following formula: Percent closure of scratch = Mean area at 0 h − Mean area at test h Mean area at 0 h × 100 Statistical analysis For the anti-proliferative activity, GraphPad Prism 8.0 (Dotmatics, USA) was used for statistical analysis. After normalizing the data, non-linear regression was used, and the results were presented as the mean IC 50 ± standard error of the mean (SEM). A one-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s test, was used for group comparison, and differences were deemed statistically significant at p < 0.05. To process the NMR data, 4.3.1 (Bruker, Biospin GmbH, Germany) was used. For the thermal shift assay, the fluorescence of the Syprorange dye as a function of temperature was recorded using the HEX (Ex/Em: 538/555 nm) channel, and the data were analyzed using CFX 96 Manager TM software (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA). The magnitude of thermal shift was determined in terms of melting temperature (Tm) of the protein, both in the absence and in the presence of the ligand. Results and discussion Anti-proliferative effects in prostate cancer (PC3) cell line The PC3 cell line is an androgen-independent cancer cell line with high metastatic potential and high expression of FUT8 [ 19 ]. Therefore, this cell line was selected to analyze the anti-cancer effects of a library of commercially available compounds using an MTT assay. A total of 265 compounds were screened, among which eight compounds were found to be active, as presented in Fig 1 . Compounds 1 and 2 showed potent anti-proliferative effects with IC 50 values of 2.94 ± 0.04 and 3.07 ± 0.06 µM, respectively. Compounds 3 and 4 showed significant anti-proliferative effects with IC 50 values of 19.42 ± 0.59 and 19.76 ± 0.61 µM, respectively. Compounds 5 and 6 showed moderate anti-proliferative effects with IC 50 values of 41.33 ± 0.41 and 49.85 ± 1.23 µM, respectively. Furthermore, compounds 7 and 8 exhibited a moderate anti-proliferative effect with IC 50 values of 64.21 ± 1.95 and 75.82 ± 2.10 µM, respectively (Fig S2 in S1 File ). The activities of these compounds were compared with those of 2-fluorofucose (IC 50 : 259.70 ± 4.68 µM), used as a reference compound in the study, and all results were found to be statistically significant ( p < 0.0001). IC 50 ± SEM of all compounds was calculated from three independent experiments (n = 3), and their dose-dependent response curves are presented in supplementary Fig S3 in S1 File . 10.1371/journal.pone.0349302.g001 Fig 1 Names, structures, and the IC 50 values of compounds active against the PC3 cell line. Cytotoxicity effects in the human fibroblast (BJ) cell line The cytotoxicity profile against the human fibroblast BJ cell line was also analyzed, and these compounds were found to be non-cytotoxic to normal cells. Ligand-receptor interactions elucidation using a multidisciplinary approach Since the compounds were identified using the PC3 cell line, further exploration of the direct interactions between the identified anti-proliferative compounds and the recombinant FUT8 enzyme was conducted. Biophysical studies (DSF and STD-NMR) were performed to investigate these interactions. These studies were further supported by computational analyses, including molecular docking, MM-GBSA energy calculations, and molecular dynamics simulations. Analysis by Differential Scanning Fluorimetry (DSF) Differential Scanning Fluorimetry (DSF) is a fluorescence-based thermal shift assay that assists in evaluating the stability of the protein [ 28 , 29 ]. It uses a hydrophobic fluorescent dye that binds to proteins as they unfold, and fluorescence changes are quantified as a function of the melting temperature (Tm). Changes in Tm upon the addition of ligand reflect the stability index of the protein. Ligand binding to a protein may result in stabilizing or destabilizing the tertiary fold of the protein, which is reflected by an increase or decrease in the Tm, respectively. GDP, being an enzyme substrate, exhibited an increase in Tm from 46 to 49°C, indicating stabilization of the tertiary fold of the protein. However, 2FF and all of the identified compounds caused a decrease in the Tm (≥ 0.5°C) of the protein (Table S1 in S1 File ). These observations suggest interaction of the compounds with FUT8 and indicaFte ligand-associated destabilization of its tertiary structure, with the largest thermal shift observed for compounds 7 and 8 ( Fig 2 ). During the initial phase of the simulation (0–40 ns), both RMSD values increased, reflecting relaxation from the docked conformation and equilibration of the solvent and side chains. In the later stage of the simulation (170–300 ns), transient ligand RMSD fluctuations (~2.0–4.0 Å) were observed; however, these variations repeatedly returned to baseline values, indicating localized conformational adjustments of GDP rather than ligand dissociation. Correlation between STD-NMR and MD simulation The complementary use of STD-NMR spectroscopy and molecular docking–molecular dynamics (MD) simulations provided a coherent and mutually supportive interpretation of ligand–FUT8 interactions. Following cell-based and thermal shift assays, STD-NMR analysis of six buffer-soluble compounds ( 1 , 3 , 5 , 6 , 7 , and 8 ) confirmed direct ligand–FUT8 interactions, whereas compounds 2 and 4 could not be evaluated due to solubility limitations in Tris buffer. While STD-NMR spectroscopy effectively showed binding interactions, it could not resolve residue-specific interactions or active-site involvement, nor detect exchangeable protons such as –OH and –NH 2 . To address these limitations, molecular docking and subsequent MD simulations were employed. Docking results indicated favorable binding poses for all compounds; however, MD simulations further refined these findings, revealing differential binding stability. Compounds 5 and 8 exhibited unstable binding and diffused from the FUT8 binding site during the simulation, whereas compounds 1 , 3 , 6 , and 7 remained stably associated with FUT8 throughout the 300 ns trajectory. Furthermore, the interacting protons of these four compounds ( 1 , 3 , 6 , and 7 ) with FUT8 in STD-NMR corresponded to hydrophobic regions that maintained persistent contacts during MD simulations, demonstrating strong agreement between experimental and computational analysis. These integrated analyses strengthen the study that compounds 1 , 3 , 6 , and 7 represent the most promising lead candidates. Strengths and limitations of the study A major strength of this study lies in the integrated, multi-assay approach that combines cell-based assays, thermal shift analysis, STD-NMR spectroscopy, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulations, thereby enabling cross-validation of ligand–protein interactions from both experimental and computational perspectives. STD-NMR spectroscopy provided direct experimental evidence of binding, while MD simulations enabled residue-level and temporal evaluation of ligand stability within the FUT8 binding site, overcoming key experimental limitations. However, certain limitations remained. STD-NMR spectroscopy is inherently limited by the solubility of compounds in aqueous buffers, the inability to detect exchangeable protons, and the lack of site-specific interaction information. Similarly, molecular docking and MD simulations rely on force-field accuracy and simulation timescales, which may not fully capture long-term conformational dynamics. Despite these limitations, the convergence of STD-NMR interaction patterns with MD-derived stability profiles significantly enhances confidence in the identified lead compounds. Overall, these complementary strategies provide a robust framework for lead validation and rational optimization in FUT8-targeted drug discovery. Conclusion A stepwise screening strategy integrating cellular, biophysical, and computational approaches was applied to identify novel FUT8-targeting anti-cancer candidates. Screening of 265 compounds against PC3 cells identified eight compounds with significant anti-proliferative activity and minimal cytotoxicity toward normal BJ cells. Biophysical characterization using DSF and STD-NMR spectroscopy confirmed direct ligand-FUT8 interactions, with compounds 1 , 3 , 6 , and 7 showing strong saturation transfer effects and inducing thermal conformational destabilization of FUT8. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations further demonstrated stable binding of these compounds within the FUT8 binding site, consistent with experimental interaction patterns. In cell-based assays, these lead compounds also inhibited cancer cell migration. Overall, the convergence of cellular efficacy, biophysical validation, and computational stability supports compounds 1 , 3 , 6 , and 7 as promising FUT8-targeting lead candidates for further optimization and in vivo investigation.
