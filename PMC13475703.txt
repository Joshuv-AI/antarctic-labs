Unraveling the interplay of microbial succession, flavor deterioration, and physicochemical dynamics in vacuum-packed marinated silver carp during storage
Authors: Chunming Tan, Yaqin Yu, Minbao Wang, Shi Nie, Zongcai Tu, Wenhua Yang, Lu Zhang, Yunhong Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study systematically investigated the quality and flavor deterioration and microbial succession of marinated silver carp (MSC) during storage. After 60 days of storage, the samples showed significant morphological deterioration, decreased sensory acceptability, while moisture content and pH value initially decreased and then increased. Synergistic protein-lipid oxidation accelerated deterioration, as reflected by rising TVB-N and TBARS levels, along with sulfhydryl group depletion, and carbonyl accumulation. Flavor analysis confirmed sulfides, aldehydes, and ketones as major volatile compounds, with the escape of key aroma components—including eugenol, nonanal, benzeneacetaldehyde, p-anisaldehyde, and linalool, contributed to overall flavor deterioration. Microbial succession analysis revealed Enterococcus supplanting Bacillus as the terminal dominant genus, with 12 potential spoilage bacteria (e.g., Enterococcus, Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter) promoting spoilage via amino acid, carbohydrate, and nucleotide metabolism. Collectively, these findings reveal the potential mechanisms underlying the quality and flavor deterioration of MSC during storage and provide a foundation for developing preservation strategies.Keywords: Silver carp, Marinade, Storage process, Flavor deterioration, Microbial succession

Introduction

Silver carp (Hypophthalmichthys molitrix) is an important economic freshwater fish in China, which is easy to farm and has high yield, making it an ideal raw material for marinated fish (Wei et al., 2023). However, traditional marinated silver carp suffers from serious nutritional losses, flavor deterioration, short shelf life and other problems (Hao et al., 2016). The high moisture content and neutral pH characteristics of marinated silver carp make it susceptible to microbial infestation and quality deterioration during storage (Li et al., 2018), posing challenges to product safety and consumer acceptance. Therefore, process optimization is urgently needed to improve the quality, food safety and shelf life of marinated silver carp products.

Traditional marinated fish preserves through high-salt dehydration, its quality deterioration is primarily driven by lipid oxidation and textural hardening. In contrast, ordinary refrigerated fish suffers from freshness loss due to microbial proliferation and ATP degradation (Tan et al., 2022). Vacuum-packed marinated fish, under low oxygen conditions, undergoes a distinct deterioration pathway mediated by facultative anaerobe metabolism and altered endogenous enzyme activity (Cheng et al., 2025). Given these differences among the three processing/storage regimes, this study selects silver carp prepared by a combined vacuum-packaging and marinating process as the research subject—a choice justified by both practical and scientific considerations. This combination not only circumvents the oxidation and hardening issues inherent to traditional high salt marinating, but also mitigates the microbial and ATP related deterioration observed in conventional refrigeration, while the vacuum environment enables targeted regulation of specific spoilage metabolic pathways.

In recent years, sous-vide cooking technology has obvious advantages in meat processing due to its low-oxygen environment and precise temperature control. Sous-vide cooking is an emerging food processing technology with the potential to improve product quality and extend shelf life (Roascio-Albistur & Gámbaro, 2018). The process involves vacuum-sealing the raw materials in heat-resistant food-grade plastic bags, immersing them in a warm water bath, and heating them at a controlled temperature (typically 50–90 °C) for periods ranging from minutes to hours (Ortuño et al., 2021). This technology has been used to improve the quality and extend the shelf life of pork (Kim et al., 2019), beef (Gil et al., 2022), chicken (Hasani et al., 2023; Song et al., 2023), fish (Pino Hernández et al., 2017). Recently, it has also been used to extend the shelf life of crab meat, which has demonstrated prolonged refrigeration stability under sous-vide cooking conditions (Olatunde & Benjakul, 2021). Therefore, we further employed sous-vide cooking technology to replace traditional marinating process to obtain marinated silver carp with higher quality and shelf life. During storage, quality deterioration may occur due to oxidation or microbial metabolism, and the characteristic flavor formed during the marinating process may also dissipate to varying degrees (Al-Dalali et al., 2021). Changes in fish texture affect consumers' eating experience (Yu et al., 2017), and the degradation of protein and fat produces volatile compounds such as ammonia and sulfides, which can cause off-flavor and rancidity in the product (Dai et al., 2024). Concurrently, microbial proliferation during storage triggers protein and lipid metabolism alongside nutrient breakdown, producing a large number of metabolites. However, the correlation between the quality characteristics of sous-vide cooked silver carp and its microbial diversity and flavor compounds remains unclear. Therefore, it is necessary to reveal the dynamic mechanisms underlying changes in the quality, flavor, and microbial community of sous-vide cooked silver carp during storage.

The objective of this study is to systematically elucidate the dynamic changes in the quality attributes (textural properties, color stability, moisture distribution), flavor characteristics (volatile organic compounds, E-nose profiles), and microbial ecosystems (bacterial diversity, dominant spoilage organisms) of sous-vide MSC during storage. By integrating sensory evaluation, physicochemical analysis, and multi-omics techniques (including GC–MS, high-throughput sequencing), we aimed to: (1) reveal the mechanisms underlying quality deterioration; (2) identify key driving factors (microbial metabolism, biochemical reactions) governing product degradation; and (3) establish a theoretical foundation for shelf-life and MSC processing optimization. This work provides scientific basis for developing targeted quality control strategies to enhance market competitiveness, ensure food safety, and improve consumer experience. It is of great significance to promote the utilization of silver carp resources and foster the sustainable development of the aquatic processing industry.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

20 fresh silver carps (1.52 ± 0.23 kg each) were purchased from Changsheng Market (Nanchang, Jiangxi, China). They were packed in oxygenated plastic bags with water and transported alive to the laboratory within 1 h. Upon arrival at the laboratory, the experimental staff immediately euthanized the live silver carp by rendering it unconscious via electric shock prior to humane killing. All procedures comply with the Guidelines for Experimental Animal Treatment Issued by the Ministry of Science and Technology of China (2006) and Ethical Statement guidelines for animal handling (No. 20231018-A3). Subsequently, removed scales, viscera and head, and cut into fillets (5.0 × 2.0 × 1.5 cm3). Fish meat was randomly assigned to different groups, and three biological replicates were established per group (microbial community analysis with four biological replicates), meaning that the fish meat in each group came from 4 different fish. To minimize biological variation, fish of similar body weight and age were selected at the start of the experiment. The fillets were salt-cured with 2% salt and 0.6% ginger for 1.0 h at room temperature and then dried at 55 ± 0.5 °C for 21 h. The dried fish and marinade were placed in a sous vide cooker at a ratio of 1:2 and marinated at 70 °C for 2 h. The finished product was air-dried with cold air to remove the marinade on the surface of the fish meat, vacuum-packed (0.08 MPa, PA/PE material, oxygen permeability <2 cc/(m2·day·atm)), pasteurized (65 °C, 30 min) and stored at room temperature (15–24 °C). Samples were collected on days 0, 20, 40, 60 and 90 of storage.

Sensory evaluation

Following the method described by Zhao et al. (2024a) with slight modifications. The assessment panel comprised twelve experienced postgraduate students (six males and six females), aged 20–30 years. All 12 panelists passed basic taste/odor sensitivity tests (including anosmia screening); before formal evaluation, they received rigorous two-week calibration training sessions with commercial marinated fish references to calibrate scoring criteria. To mitigate potential order-related biases, all samples were randomly assigned a three-digit code before presentation. Panelists assessed key quality dimensions of silver carp fillets, including color uniformity, odor intensity, texture elasticity, tissue structural integrity, and overall acceptability (without tasting). A standardized 100-point grading scale was implemented across all parameters, with interpretive ranges defined as: 0–30 (severely spoiled), 30–50 (obviously spoiled), 50–70 (marginally acceptable), 70–90 (optimal freshness), and 90–100 (excellent quality). The sensory evaluation complied with laws and regulations, ethical and moral requirements and protected privacy of the participants, who gave their informed and explicit consent. The experiment was approved by the Commitment to Experimental Ethics and Welfare of Jiangxi Normal University (approval No. 20230820-005).

Determination of physicochemical properties

Moisture content

The determination was performed using our previous methods (Zhang et al., 2024a). Briefly, 0.5 g of marinated fish sample was taken and the moisture content was measured using a moisture tester (Guanya Technology & Science Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China). Repeat the measurement 5 times for all samples.

Determination of pH value

1.0 g of sample was homogenized with 10 volumes of distilled water for 30 s, allowed to stand for 30 min, and then measured using an S220 pH meter (Mettler Toledo Instruments Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) (Zhang et al., 2016).

Determination of color

A WSC 2B portable precision colorimeter (Yidian Physical Optics Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was used to measure different parts of the fish block at least five times, and the L*, a* and b* values were recorded. Before measurement, whiteboard calibration and blackboard zeroing were performed (Yi et al., 2018).

Texture profile analysis

Texture profiles were characterized as described by Zhang et al. (2024a). The fish meat was cut into 2.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 cm squares (parallel to the loading axis along the 2.0 cm length of the sample) and analyzed using a TA-XT Plus texture analyzer (Stable Micro Systems Ltd., Godalming, UK). The instrument was equipped with a P/36R probe, with a pre-test speed of 3.0 mm/s, a test speed of 1.0 mm/s, a post-test speed of 3.0 mm/s, and a type variable of 50%. The trigger force was 5 g, and the interval between two compression times was 5 s. The hardness, cohesiveness, chewiness, and resilience were measured, and each sample was tested at least five times.

Total volatile basic‑nitrogen (TVB-N) analysis

The TVB-N content was extracted and measured as described by Zhang et al. (2024a). In brief, 10 g of fish tissue was placed into a digestion tube, followed by the addition of 20 mL of distilled water and 1.0 g of magnesium oxide. The TVB-N content was subsequently quantified using a SKD-800 Kjeldahl Apparatus (Peio Analytical Instruments Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), with a mixed solution of boric acid, bromocresol green, and methyl red serving as the absorption indicator. The titration was performed with 0.01 mol/L HCl standard solution. Distilled water (20 mL) was used as blank control.

Oxidative analysis of MSC samples

Thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) value

A slightly modified version of the protocol from Ghasemi et al. (2024) was used. In brief, 1.0 g of sample was added to 10 mL of 7.5% trichloroacetic acid solution (containing 0.1% EDTA) and shaken for 15 s, and allowed to stand at room temperature for 30 min, then centrifuge at 8000 g, 4 °C for 10 min. 1.0 mL of the supernatant was mixed with 1.0 mL of TBA (0.02 mol/L) and boiled in a water bath for 40 min. After cooling, the reaction mixture was extracted with 2.0 mL of n-hexane, and the absorbance of the lower aqueous layer was measured at 532 nm and 600 nm utilizing a U-2910 UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Hitachi Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The absorbance at 600 nm was used as the non-specific background correction to subtract baseline drift caused by pigments and turbidity. To further minimize pigment interference, protein precipitation was achieved by the initial addition of trichloroacetic acid, and water-soluble pigments were removed during centrifugation, while lipid-soluble pigments were eliminated by the n-hexane extraction step. The TBARS values are expressed as mg malondialdehyde (MDA)/kg sample.

Myofibrillar protein extraction

The extraction method was performed as described by Wang et al. (2022a). In brief, 5.0 g of minced fish meat was mixed with 20 mL of myofibrillar protein extraction solution (0.1 mol/L NaCl, 2.0 mmol/L MgCl2, 1.0 mmol/L EGTA, 10 mmol/L PBS, pH 7.0). The mixture was homogenized and centrifuged at 10,000 g for 5 min, and the supernatant discarded (the process was repeated three times). The precipitate was washed with 20 mL of 0.1 mol/L NaCl and centrifuged under the same conditions, discarding the supernatant (repeated twice). The precipitate was finally dissolved in 15 mL of PBS containing 0.6 mol/L NaCl (pH = 7.0) and the supernatant was filtered through a four-layer membrane. Protein concentration was quantified using a BCA assay kit, with bovine serum albumin serving as the reference standard.

Carbonyl content determination

1.0 mL of myofibrillar protein solution was mixed with 1.0 mL of 10 mmol/L 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH) containing 2.0 mol/L HCl, and allowed to react at room temperature in the dark for 1.0 h. After the reaction, the mixture was treated with 1.0 mL of 20% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and centrifuged at 10,000 g for 5 min to collect the precipitate. The precipitate was washed three times with 1.0 mL ethanol-ethyl acetate solution (1:1, v/v) to remove DNPH, and centrifuged under the same conditions. The precipitate was dissolved in 1.0 mL of 6 mol/L guanidine hydrochloride and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. The mixture was centrifuged and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 370 nm. 1.0 mL of 6 mol/L guanidine hydrochloride was used as a control instead of myofibrillar protein solution.

Sulfhydryl content determination

Sulfhydryl groups were determined with reference to Lin et al. (2019) with slight modification. Briefly, 0.5 mL of myofibrillar protein solution was mixed with 4.5 mL of buffer A (8 mol/L urea, 3 mol/L ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), and 0.2 mol/L Tris-HCl, pH 8.0). 4.0 mL of the solution was then added to 0.5 mL of buffer solution B (10 mmol/L DTNB, 0.2 mol/L Tris-HCl, pH 8.0). The mixture was well mixed and incubated at 40 °C for 25 min. The absorbance was measured at 412 nm after the mixture was cooled down to room temperature. 0.6 mol/L NaCl was used as a control instead of myofibrillar protein solution.

Electronic nose (E-nose) analysis

Following the protocol of Yu et al. (2018) with minor adjustments, the E-nose analysis was conducted. Specifically, a 2.0 g of sample was transferred into a 20 mL headspace vial and sealed with four layers of plastic wrap, equilibrated in a water bath at 50 °C for 30 min, and then the headspace was analyzed using a PEN3 E-nose (AirSense Analytics Co. Ltd., Schwerin, Germany) for measurement. The measurement conditions were as follows: cleaning time 120 s, zeroing time 5 s, preparation time 5 s, measurement time 140 s, carrier gas flow rate 600 mL/min, injection flow rate 600 mL/min, and eigenvalue extraction time set from 137 to 139 s. Table S1 lists the PEN3 sensors and their corresponding representative sensitive material types.

Headspace solid-phase microextraction-gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC-MS) analysis

Volatile flavor compound analysis was performed according to the method of Wu et al. (2023), with a few modifications. 2.0 g sample was accurately transferred into a 20 mL extraction vial, 10 μL of internal standard (o-dichlorobenzene, 1.306 μg/mL) was added and the vial was sealed. The headspace gas was heated at 60 °C for 30 min to reach equilibrium, and then an activated extraction needle was inserted to perform headspace extraction for 30 min. Volatile components were determined using HS-SPME-GC-MS, which was equipped with a GC-MS instrument (Agilent 7890A-5975C), a solid-phase microextraction device (57330-U, Supelco), and a capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm INNOWAX). The conditions of GC were as follows: injector temperature was 250 °C, carrier gas was He with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min, using splitless mode. Heating program: Initially at 45 °C, hold for 5 min, then increase to 240 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min, hold for 10 min, running time 54 min. MS conditions: EI ionization source, ionization voltage 70 eV, ion source temperature 230 °C, quadrupole temperature 150 °C, full scan mode, mass scan range 35–400 amu.

Volatile compounds were qualitatively identified by comparison with the NIST 2.0 mass spectrometry library (>80% similarity) and the MS spectral database, and quantified based on internal standard concentration and peak area. Furthermore, threshold values and odor descriptions of volatile flavor compounds were obtained by referring to existing literature and online databases (https://www.femaflavor.org/flavor-library), and odor activity values (OAV) were calculated based on the threshold values.

Microbial community analysis

Samples were collected using sterilized scissors on a sterile operating table, and each group was randomly divided into four aliquots. 16S rRNA high-throughput sequencing was conducted by Majorbio Bio-Pharm (Shanghai, China). Microbial genomic DNA was extracted from 20 samples, and the V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using universal primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGGAGGCAGC AG-3′) and 806R (5′-GCACTACHVGGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). Amplified products were detected by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, and PCR products were recovered using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Recovery Kit (AXYGEN), followed by quantification using the QuantiFluorTM-ST Blue Fluorescence Quantification System (Promega). The database was established using the TruSeqTM DNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA), and gene sequencing was performed on the NextSeq 2000 platform (Illumina, USA). Raw data quality control for paired-end reads was performed using fastp (v0.23.4) with a quality threshold of Q ≥ 20, and sequence merging was carried out with FLASH (v1.2.11). Denoising and chimera removal were conducted using DADA2 (v1.26) to generate an amplicon sequence variant (ASV) feature table. Taxonomic assignment was performed using the RDP Classifier (with a confidence threshold of 0.7) against the SILVA database (v138.2). Finally, the feature table was rarefied to the minimum library size for normalization. All obtained raw sequence datasets in this work have been uploaded to the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) with BioProject PRJNA1481465. In addition, PICRUSt2 predicted functional profiling of the metagenome based on bacterial amplicon sequencing profiles (Tan et al., 2022). The functional annotation of predicted features was carried out based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were done in triplicate (gene sequencing was performed in quadruplicate), and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Data were plotted using Origin 2021 and GraphPad Prism version 10.1.2. One-way ANOVA was performed using SPSS version 16.0 statistical software. Duncan's test was used for significant difference analysis, p < 0.05 indicates a significant difference.

Results and discussion

Sensory evaluation analysis

The acceptability of MSC samples during storage depends on changes in their sensory attributes. As illustrated by the radar chart in Fig. 1A, the aroma, elasticity, texture, color, overall acceptability, and total sensory evaluation scores all showed a downward trend with increasing storage time. The tissue morphology score decreased most significantly, from 81.13 ± 3.59 (fresh, 0 days) to 21.13 ± 2.57 (severely spoiled, 90 days) (p < 0.05). Notably, considering the cumulative effects of all sensory attributes, the rate of MSC deterioration accelerated dramatically after 60 days of storage (total score <60). Additionally, the deterioration of MSC was evident at 90 days, characterized by darkening color, loose texture, and a distinct rancid odor.

Changes in physicochemical properties

Moisture content, pH, color and texture profiles

Moisture content is a key factor affecting the spoilage of MSC, which provides a favorable growth environment for spoilage bacteria and accelerates the oxidation and degradation of lipids and proteins, thus seriously affecting the flavor of MSC. As shown in Fig. 1B, the moisture content of MSC initially decreases and then increases with storage time, reaching a minimum of 46.50% at 40 days and subsequently increasing to 49.55%. This decrease in MSC moisture content is attributed to the destruction of myofibrillar proteins by spoilage organisms and endogenous enzymes, ultimately leading to a decrease in the water holding capacity of the fish meat (Zhang et al., 2024b). The subsequent increase in moisture content mainly stemmed from water production by microbial metabolism (such as the release of bound water generated by respiration and protein degradation), rather than the restoration of the system's own water retention capacity. It should be noted that the experiment was conducted at room temperature with vacuum-packed products in order to examine the limits of quality changes under extreme temperature and humidity fluctuations and to provide guidance on the actual shelf life.

The pH exhibited dynamic changes during the 90-day storage period. Specifically, pH decreased from 6.95 at day 0 to 6.07 at day 60, and then increased slightly to reach 6.28 at the end of storage (Fig. 1C). The initial decrease in pH might be attributed to early to mid-stage growth of anaerobic acid-producing bacteria (e.g., Enterococcus), and CO₂ dissolution. Additionally, CO₂ delays the growth of aerobic spoilage bacteria, thereby reducing the production of alkaline substances to a certain extent (Hassoun & Karoui, 2016). However, the increase in pH during the later storage might be due to protein degradation by spoilage bacteria, which produces spoilage-associated alkaline substances such as ammonia, dimethylamine, trimethylamine and other amines (Goulas & Kontominas, 2005). These findings are consistent with the report by Hui et al. (2016), which showed that the pH value first decreased and then increased.

As the storage period extends, the color of the marinated fish meat undergoes a significant change (Fig. 1C–E). Color changes were characterized by a significant increase in both a* value (redness/greenness) and b* value (yellowness/blueness) from 27.42 and 18.49 to 31.67 and 22.20, respectively (p < 0.05). The observed increase in b* values was attributed to a non-enzymatic browning reaction involving the interaction of oxidized fatty acid derivatives with the polar headgroup amino groups of proteins or phospholipids (Lv et al., 2021). After 40 days of storage, the L* value (lightness) of fish meat decreased significantly (p < 0.05), from 8.04 ± 0.16 to 5.19 ± 0.34. This decrease is likely due to the loss of moisture during storage (Olivares et al., 2010).

The hardness and chewiness changed most significantly during storage (p < 0.05), initially increasing and then decreasing, demonstrating a biphasic variation pattern (Table 1). Specifically, hardness increased from 2356.11 ± 100.59 g to a peak of 3284.22 ± 73.33 g, then decreased to 1598.53 ± 135.97 g. Chewiness followed a similar trajectory, increasing from 1442.53 ± 33.89 g to 1934.12 ± 140.32 g and later reducing to 1010.93 ± 175.14 g. This mechanical behavior is closely related to the dynamics of moisture content: accelerated water loss during storage directly enhances tissue firmness and resistance to deformation. Furthermore, progressive deterioration of cohesiveness and elasticity provided additional evidence of cumulative quality decline in fish muscle during long-term storage. Protein oxidation and the disruption of muscle microstructure in fish are other key factors contributing to its textural changes.

TVB-N content and TBARS values

The TVB-N is a crucial physicochemical indicator for assessing fish freshness. During MSC storage, amino acids in fish were broken down by endogenous enzymes and spoilage bacteria, resulting in the production of more volatile basic nitrogen. Over a 90-day storage period, the TVB-N content of MSC increased from an initial 19.61 ± 0.36 mg/100 g to a final concentration of 36.55 ± 0.1 mg/100 g (Fig. 1F). This upward trend may be attribute to the autolytic degradation of nucleotides and free amino acids (Hong et al., 2012). In addition, the reduction of trimethylamine oxide to trimethylamine in fish may also contribute to the significant increase in TVB-N content during storage (Sallam et al., 2007).

TBARS mainly reflects the content of malondialdehyde oxidation products and serves as a key indicator for assessing the extent of lipid oxidation and deterioration during MSC storage (Cheng et al., 2019). With long-term storage, especially in the middle and late stages (60 days and 90 days), microbial proliferation begins, and the secondary products of unsaturated fatty acid oxidation such as aldehydes, ketones, and acids continued to increase (Fan et al., 2008). This is accompanied by a significant increase in the TBARS values of MSC (reaching 0.82 ± 0.02 mg/100 g at the end of storage) (Fig. 2A), leading to a decline in quality and the development of a pronounced rancid odor. Fan et al. (2008) suggested that the TBARS value of silver carp increased from 0.37 mg/kg at initial storage to 3.09 mg/kg at the end of storage. In contrast, our study demonstrated that sous vide treatment significantly inhibited the formation of TBARS, suggesting its potential to extend the shelf life of MSC products by effectively controlling lipid oxidation.

Total sulfhydryl and carbonyl content

The degree of protein oxidation is characterized by the total sulfhydryl and carbonyl content. Total sulfhydryl content encompasses both surface and internal reactive groups, which serve as crucial functional groups essential for maintaining the spatial conformation and biological activity of proteins (Wang et al., 2014). Sulfhydryl groups are susceptible to attack by free radicals, oxidizing them into disulfide bonds and thereby reducing sulfhydryl content. This is generally considered the initial reaction in protein oxidation (Zhou et al., 2020). As shown in Fig. 2B, the total sulfhydryl content of MSC showed a significant downward trend during storage, decreasing from 11.72 ± 0.04 nmol/mg to 9.32 ± 0.07 nmol/mg (p < 0.05). This was attributed to the exposure of myofibrillar protein sulfhydryl groups during storage, which are easily oxidized to disulfide bonds and cross-linked, resulting in a decrease in total sulfhydryl group content (Lund et al., 2011).

Carbonyl content is positively correlated with the degree of oxidation (Li et al., 2014). As depicted in Fig. 2C, the protein carbonyl content of fresh MSC exhibited a dynamic trend during storage, with an initial value of 8.21 ± 0.56 nmol/mg, followed by a significant increase (p < 0.05) to a peak of 14.85 ± 0.57 nmol/mg at 60 days, and subsequent decline to 9.58 ± 0.13 nmol/mg at the end of storage. This result is consistent with the observations reported by Park et al. (2007). The underlying mechanism likely involves protein degradation of MSC during storage, the internal amino acids are gradually exposed, and their side chains are easily oxidized to form more carbonyl groups (Qiu et al., 2022). Notably, carbonyl content exhibited a significant reduction (p < 0.05) after 60 days. This phenomenon reflects two temporal dynamics: initially, carbonyl groups are rapidly formed when myofibrillar proteins encounter oxidants, but with prolonged storage, the generation rate slows and falls below the rate of consumption, resulting in a significant reduction in carbonyl content later on. This change may involve multiple complex reaction pathways, but the specific mechanism requires further validation through targeted analyses (e.g., AGEs, isocyanate intermediates, etc.).

Correlation analysis between physicochemical indicators

The Pearson correlation analysis heatmap was shown in Fig. 2D. In the figure, orange indicates negative correlation, blue-green indicates positive correlation, and the stronger the correlation, the deeper the color. The results showed that the moisture content of marinated silver carp was significantly negatively correlated with its hardness and chewiness, with a high correlation coefficient (p < 0.05, r > 0.8), indicating that texture characteristics are significantly correlated with moisture content. In addition, sulfhydryl groups showed significant negative correlations with TVB-N, TBARS and color (a*, b*), while there were significant positive correlations among TVB-N, TBARS and color, suggesting that throughout the storage process, the MSC samples lose their yellowness and redness through lipid oxidation and protein degradation, leading to a yellowing of its color and further deterioration of quality.

E-nose analysis

Electronic nose simulates the human olfactory system and is broadly employed for odor recognition and aroma identification, providing a comprehensive fingerprint of volatile compounds (Sun et al., 2023). The E-nose radar plot of MSC during storage was shown in Fig. 2E, W1W had the highest response value, followed by W2W, W2S, W1S and W5S. This indicated that sulfides, aromatic compounds, organic sulfides, alcohols, aldehydes and ketones, methyl compounds, and nitrogen oxides significantly contributed to the overall flavor of MSC. The results of PCA analysis showed that the contribution rates of PC1 and PC2 were 47.49% and 21.06%, respectively, with a cumulative contribution of 68.55% (Fig. 2F), indicating that PC1 and PC2 can respond to key informative characteristics of MSC flavor at different storage times. It should be noted that the relatively low cumulative contribution rate of PCA is primarily attributable to the cross-sensitivity inherent in sensor responses and the nonlinearity of flavor differences among samples. Nevertheless, the distinct separation of different samples remains clearly achievable, and the classification results are reliable.

GC-MS analysis

Qualitative analysis of volatile organic compounds (VOCs)

To further investigate the effects of different storage times on the volatile flavor compounds in the samples, MSC samples were analyzed using GC–MS. A total of 150 flavor compounds were detected in the five sample groups, of which 50 volatile flavor compounds were common to all groups. And the number of unique volatile flavor components in each sample group was 35, 7, 20, 9 and 17, respectively (Fig. 3A). It is noteworthy that the number of unique flavor compounds in fresh marinated silver carp was higher than that in other groups.

Venn plot (A), stacked bar chart of flavor category content (B), windmill histogram of flavor category relative content (C–G), and ring heat map of VOCs (H) of MSC with different storage time.

The content of volatile flavor compounds in MSC remained stable during the initial 20 days of storage and then decreased significantly starting on day 21 (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3B). At the end of storage, the concentration had decreased to 5627.10 ± 353.58 μg/kg, this result showed that the flavor of the marinated silver carp gradually began to dissipate during the storage process. Specifically, the contents of phenols, aromatic hydrocarbons, alkanes, aldehydes and terpenes were significantly affected by storage time, and their contents except alkanes decreased significantly with the extension of storage time. In summary, the flavor profile of MSC underwent significant changes during storage, with volatile compounds exhibiting a dynamic pattern of initial stabilization followed by progressive depletion. Volatile flavor compounds may be irreversibly depleted through mechanisms mediated by oxidative stress and structural damage (Zhao et al., 2024b). Furthermore, factors such as the pH and moisture content of the food matrix can also affect the stability of volatile flavor substances (Deng et al., 2024).

In order to comprehensively evaluate the impact of storage time on MSC flavor, this work also conducted an in-depth study on the relative content of VOCs in the five sample groups (Fig. 3C–G). The results showed that the proportions of terpenes and alcohols increased in all sample groups, while the proportions of aldehydes, aromatic hydrocarbons, and esters decreased. The volatile flavor compounds in marinated silver carp exhibited dynamic changes during different storage stages, with new volatile flavor compounds being generated and existing volatile flavor compounds gradually disappearing (Fig. 3H). Overall, VOCs such as butylbenzene, citral, 2-pentylpyridine, and nonyl dichloroacetate were identified as characteristic flavor markers in fresh marinated silver carp. At 20 days of storage, rosefuran, hexadecanal, and cis-cinnamaldehyde emerged as the dominant flavor components. When the storage time reaches 40 days, cis-3-octen-1-ol, m-xylene, and m-phthalaldehyde constitute the characteristic flavor of this stage. After 60 days, 3,3-dimethylhexane, cyclohexane ethanol, and 1,2,4a,5,6,8a-hexahydro-1-isopropyl-4,7-dimethylnaphthalene were the unique flavor substances at this time. Notably, after 90 days of storage, eugenol, Z-4-dodecenol, and 3-penten-2-one became the distinctive markers of flavor evolution. However, it should be noted that the identification of VOCs in this study was primarily based on MS library matching, and quantification relied on a single internal standard (o-dichlorobenzene). Consequently, compound assignments should be considered tentative, and the proposed contribution of specific compounds to flavor deterioration requires further validation through additional analytical approaches.

Screening of key volatile flavor compounds

Based on the VOC content data presented in Table S2, an OPLS-DA model was developed with these values serving as the Y variable. In the model, R2X and R2Y reflect how well the X and Y matrices explain the data, while Q2 represents the predictive capability. As noted by Kang et al. (2022), a model demonstrates strong fit when both R2 and Q2 approach 1.0. Fig. 4A-B showed that the model achieved R2Y = 0.984 and Q2 = 0.97, with no overlapping regions among different MSC samples, confirming its strong ability to distinguish and predict sample categories. Freshly harvested MSC (0 day) are located in the lower left quadrant, distinctly separate from the other groups, suggesting a significant difference in their flavor profiles. In contrast, the 20-day and 40-day samples clustered closely together on the principal component analysis plot, indicating similar flavor profiles. Additionally, the 90-day sample group was the most distant from all other groups, highlighting a significant flavor alteration in MSC after 90 days of storage.

OPLS-DA score (A), cross-plot of 200 permutation tests scores (B), bubble diagram of VOCs for VIP >1.5 (C) and VIP >1.3 and OAV >1 of VOCs (D–I) in MSC.

Variable important in the projection (VIP) is commonly used for key variable analysis in OPLS-DA models (Kang et al., 2022). VIP scores greater than 1.0 are considered key markers, and the larger values indicate a greater impact of the key volatile flavor compound on the flavor deterioration of marinated silver carp, and a more critical role in the flavor evolution process. A total of 25 key VOCs (VIP >1.3) were screened, primarily consisting of 4 alcohols, 3 esters, 6 aldehydes, 4 ketones, 3 phenols, and 5 miscellaneous compounds. Nine volatile flavor compounds with VIP >1.5 were identified (Fig. 4C), specifically including furfural, p-anisaldehyde, piperitone, ethyl cinnamate, 5-methylfurfural, isopropyl cyclohexenone, eugenol, α-terpinyl acetate, and 4-terpineol.

The odor activity value (OAV) is a widely used metric in food chemistry and flavor science, calculated by combining the concentration of a compound with the human olfactory threshold. Specifically, the OAV is obtained by dividing the concentration of a compound in a sample by its olfactory threshold. The greater the value, the greater the contribution of the compound to the overall flavor (Ma et al., 2023). Generally, when the OAV of a flavor compound is greater than 1, it is considered to have a significant impact on the overall flavor of meat. As shown in Table 2, a total of 12 volatile flavor components in the MSC have an OAV >1, including four aldehydes, three alcohols, 2-undecanone, eugenol, 2-pentylpyridine, styrene, and a pyrazine with a specific structure. These components collectively contribute to the unique flavor profile of MSC. Notably, the OAV values of linalool, eugenol, nonanal and eucalyptol were greater than 10, indicating that they are the potential key flavor compounds responsible for flavor deterioration during storage. Interestingly, the OAV values of these putative contributors gradually decreased with storage time, suggesting that flavor degradation in the marinated silver carp increased. This change might be attributed to the irreversible covalent binding of some flavor compounds to proteins, resulting in the gradual dissipation of flavor (Ma et al., 2019). It should be noted that these results need to be further verified in conjunction with GC-O, recombination, or omission experiments.

As shown in Fig. 4D, five flavor compounds had VIP >1.3 and OAV >1, namely eugenol, nonanal, benzeneacetaldehyde, p-anisaldehyde and linalool. During the marinating process, the addition of a large amount of spices leads to a significant increase in the eugenol content. Eugenol not only has a low perception threshold but also possesses a strong clove and woody odor (Sun et al., 2023). These characteristics made eugenol a significant contributor to the overall flavor of MSC and a key factor influencing their flavor.

Aldehydes have a low threshold content and changes in their concentration significantly impacted the overall flavor of MSC, which mainly originate from lipid oxidation products and amino acid Strecker degradation. Nonanal, benzeneacetaldehyde, and p-anisaldehyde were considered to be the key volatile flavor components of MSC, which mainly present flavors such as citrus, lemon peel and greasiness. Meanwhile, benzeneacetaldehyde is considered to be an important Strecker aldehyde that contributes to the sweetness and honey flavor (Li et al., 2022), and it plays an important role in the flavor of products (Wang et al., 2023b). Linalool has citrus floral, sweet rose, woody and blueberry aromas, and is one of the key compounds that affects the flavor of marinated silver carp. It mainly comes from spices in sauces and marinades, such as pepper (Zhao et al., 2022).

During storage, the concentrations of the five key VOCs showed a significant downward trend, particularly, nonanal, benzeneacetaldehyde, and p-anisaldehyde decreased the fastest at 60 days (Fig. 4E–I). This may be related to the fact that aldehydes can interact with myofibrillar proteins through irreversible covalent binding and reversible physicochemical interactions, covalent binding likely playing a more dominant role in this interaction (Wang et al., 2022b). Anantharamkrishnan and Reineccius (2020) explored the covalent binding between proteins and specific flavor compounds and found that β-lactoglobulin covalently binds to aldehydes, ketones, phenols, and sulfur-containing compounds, including the formation of Schiff bases, Michael addition reactions, and disulfide bonds, leading to flavor deterioration. However, since this study did not examine the changes in protein–flavor complexes, the exact causal relationship remains to be further validated. In addition, while vacuum packaging can enhance flavor retention through the complex interplay of process parameters, material barrier properties, and compound characteristics, the use of transparent bags inadvertently promotes photooxidation, which causes flavor compounds to escape. All these findings indicated that the flavor of MSC gradually deteriorated and dissipated over time during storage, and the deterioration rate was significantly accelerated at 60 days.

Microbiological analysis

Microbial diversity analysis

The rarefaction curves of the samples showed a plateau toward the end of the curves (Fig. S1), indicating that the sequencing depth achieved is adequate to represent the vast majority of microbial diversity within the samples. Compared with fresh marinated fish samples, the Shannon, Chao, and ACE indices of the microbial community significantly decreased during storage, while the Simpson index increased (Fig. 5A). This indicated that the diversity and richness of MSC significantly decreased during storage, and some bacterial communities gradually adapted to the environment and became dominant in the later stages of storage. These findings are consistent with the results observed by Zhang et al. (2024a) on the changes in microbial community composition during grass carp storage.

Microbial diversity (A), PCA (B), Venn plot (C), stacked bar chart of relative abundance at genus level (D) and heat map of top 20 genus level (E) during storage of MSC.

PCA analysis showed that the contributions of PC1 and PC2 were 40.85% and 25.28%, respectively, and the total contribution rate of the two principal components was 66.13%, indicating that the marinated silver carp samples with different storage times could be significantly distinguished (Fig. 5B). The distance between samples stored at 0 and 90 days was the greatest, indicating that the microbial composition and diversity of MSC changed significantly after 90 days of storage. However, the distance between samples stored at 40 and 60 days was relatively close, indicating a high degree of similarity.

Microbiological composition analysis

This study further explored the microbial composition of MSC during storage. As shown in Fig. 5C, a total of 33 microbial genera were identified at different storage times. Notably, the highest microbial abundance was observed at 0 day, but decreased significantly after 20 days. This finding is consistent with previous studies on microbial diversity. The relative abundance of the top 20 microorganisms at the genus level was analyzed, and it was found that Bacillus was the most abundant genus at 46.2% in the early stages of storage; With the extension of storage time, Enterococcus increased from 1.64% to 51.79%, gradually becoming the dominant species, indicating that these genera are spoilage bacteria in stored MSC (Fig. 5D). Interestingly, the relative abundance of Virginibacillus and Clostridioides increased sharply at 40 days of storage before declining and disappearing, while Oceanobacillus began to appear after 60 days of storage and gradually increased in abundance. Changes in environmental factors (e.g., pH fluctuations and organic acid accumulation) may underlie the ephemeral proliferation of microbial communities; as environmental conditions deteriorate, microbial taxa devoid of competitive advantages are progressively marginalized and eventually excluded from the ecosystem (Jin et al., 2026). These fading microbial communities do not directly contribute to the synthesis of major flavor compounds, but may be indirectly associated with flavor formation by mediating the metabolism of key precursors such as amino acids and fatty acids (Gao et al., 2025). In order to comprehensively analyze the dynamic evolution of microorganisms in MSC during storage, we analyzed not only the relative changes in microbial abundance within samples, but also the overall changes in microbial abundance across samples. The heatmap of the top 20 genera revealed that microbial diversity was high in samples at day 0, but decreased significantly after 20 days of storage, with the emergence of a new genus (Enterococcus) (Fig. 5E). Enterococcus was present throughout the whole storage period. Virginibacillus, Clostridioides and Paeniclostridium gradually became the dominant genera at day 40, and then disappeared. After 60 days of storage, Bacillus, Paenibacillus, Oceanobacillus, and Enterococcus gradually became the dominant genera. Bacillus and Paenibacillus gradually decreased in abundance, while Enterococcus became the dominant genus at day 90, influencing the spoilage process of MSC.

LEfSe and KEGG enrichment pathway analysis

In order to more accurately reveal significant differences in microbial composition or function among different groups, and thus identify key biomarkers with discriminatory ability, this study employed the LefSe analysis method combining the nonparametric factorial Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum test and linear discriminant analysis (LDA) to identify key microorganisms that differ significantly among groups. In this study, microorganisms with LDA score >2 were designated as key bacterial species for MSC spoilage, and 12 key microbial species were identified (Fig. 6A, Table S3), including Enterococcus, Sphingobium, Acinetobacter, Oceanobacillus, Staphylococcus, Massilia, Pseudomonas, Branchiibius, Kocuria, Macrococcus, Lactococcus, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Chloroplast, and two unclassified bacteria. Among them Enterococcus, Pseudomonas and Acinetobacter were common spoilage bacteria in food (Lei et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2022).

LDA >2 key microbial genera (A), differential metabolic pathways (B), correlation analysis of key microbial genera and differential metabolic pathways (C) and correlation network diagram of physicochemical properties, key VOCs and key microorganisms (D) during storage of MSC.

Microbial metabolic pathways can directly reflect the changes of food components at the material metabolism level, and can analyze the metabolic activity of microorganisms in food and the specific changes in the process of food spoilage. Therefore, key microorganisms at the genus level of samples at 0 days and 90 days were selected for KEGG enrichment pathway analysis. As shown in Fig. 6B, Stamp difference analysis was performed on the pathways enriched at the secondary level, and 27 statistically significant metabolic pathways were identified (p < 0.05). These predicted metabolic pathways, as inferred by PICRUSt2, cover a wide range of biological processes, including amino acid metabolism, lipid metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, and carbohydrate metabolism, as well as transcription and translation mechanisms. Based on these functional predictions, microorganisms are suggested to utilize carbohydrates to provide energy for their growth and reproduction under the action of enzymes. Nucleotide hydrolases may generate bases and ribose phosphates, with the latter potentially feeding into the pentose phosphate pathway-glycolysis-tricarboxylic acid cycle metabolic cascade, thereby possibly expanding carbon source utilization and enhancing their proliferation ability (Liew et al., 2020). However, it should be emphasized that these inferred functions represent genomic potentials rather than direct measures of actual microbial metabolic activities. The prediction results need to be further verified through metagenomics or metatranscriptomics.

Correlation and possible mechanism analysis

As shown in Fig. 6C, correlation analysis between key microorganisms and metabolic pathways revealed that 12 key microorganisms were significantly associated with 27 metabolic pathways (p < 0.05 & |r| > 0.8). Two contrasting clusters emerged: one cluster encompassing 10 metabolic pathways related to carbohydrate metabolism, glycan biosynthesis and metabolism, and nucleotide metabolism demonstrated significant positive correlations with Enterococcus and Oceanobacillus species, but significant negative associations with other microbial groups. Conversely, the other cluster exhibited negative correlations, demonstrating a diametrically opposite pattern of associations. These opposing association patterns suggest a functional niche differentiation among spoilage-associated microbiota, wherein Enterococcus and Oceanobacillus may preferentially drive glycolysis and nucleotide catabolic processes, while other groups, such as Pseudomonas, are more engaged in alternative substrate utilization. Indeed, Pseudomonas is known to metabolize carbohydrates (e.g., glucose, sucrose, and galactose) into organic acids such as formic acid, acetic acid, and lactic acid (Casaburi et al., 2015), and also contributes substantially to amino acid metabolism (Xiao et al., 2024). This dual metabolic capacity positions Pseudomonas as a key node connecting early carbohydrate depletion with subsequent proteolysis, thereby linking primary substrate utilization to downstream volatile generation.

The spoilage of MSC is a complex process involving the interaction of multiple factors, among which the changes in physicochemical indexes, the production of volatile flavor components and the growth of key spoilage bacteria were closely related (Wang et al., 2023a). Pearson correlation analysis was performed on the physicochemical properties, key VOCs and key microbial communities during the storage of MSC (Fig. 6D). The results showed that Lactococcus, Acinetobacter and Macrococcus had a strong correlation with the physicochemical indicators (r > 0.8). Lactococcus and Macrococcus were positively correlated with pH, indicating that pH significantly affected the growth of spoilage bacteria and the spoilage rate of MSC. Zang et al. (2018) made similar findings when studying the microbial community succession in traditional fermented sour fish. In addition, microorganisms played an important role in the quality changes of MSC. Among them, Acinetobacter was associated with textural changes of MSC, while Oceanobacillus showed a positive correlation with lipid oxidation, indicating that the presence of this bacterium exacerbates lipid oxidation. Furthermore, Macrococcus not only affected protein oxidation, but also positively correlated with the production of benzeneacetaldehyde. However, it should be noted that Macrococcus does not actively biotransform amino acid substrates into benzeneacetaldehyde through its own enzymatic reaction. This positive correlation is more likely due to concurrent and independent protein degradation pathways, which show statistical synchrony rather than a direct causal metabolic link. This implied that Macrococcus might be a key factor influencing the formation of flavor compounds in MSC. Integrating these findings, we propose a potential mechanistic model wherein the spoilage trajectory of MSC is driven by sequential, overlapping phases: (i) initial carbohydrate metabolism by Pseudomonas and related taxa, leading to organic acid accumulation and pH modification; (ii) subsequent proteolytic and lipolytic activities, mediated by Acinetobacter, Oceanobacillus, and Macrococcus, which release free amino acids and fatty acids; (iii) oxidative degradation of these substrates, yielding aldehydes, ketones, and other VOCs that define both characteristic and off-flavors. Importantly, lipid and protein oxidation are not independent but mutually reinforcing: protein oxidation generates carbonyls and free radicals that propagate lipid peroxidation, while lipid hydroperoxides can oxidize protein thiol groups, accelerating texture failure and flavor deterioration. This cross-amplification is consistent with the synergistic response between spoilage bacterial abundance and key volatile markers reported previously (Jia et al., 2019; Tan et al., 2024). Understanding these microbial–flavor–oxidation interplays provide a scientific basis for quality control in aquatic product processing. Moreover, the successful implementation of such knowledge-driven intervention strategies parallels the broader need for technology-facilitating conditions and green cognition in agricultural systems (Karbo et al., 2025; Yue et al., 2025), underscoring that innovation in food processing must be accompanied by capacity building and supportive policy frameworks.

The integrated potential mechanism is summarized in Fig. 7. After 60 days of storage, the explosive growth and metabolism of potential spoilage microorganisms are strongly associated with quality deterioration, which correlates with the dissipation of characteristic flavors and the formation of off-odors. This time point coincides with the initial decline in sensory acceptability, during which morphological deterioration is most pronounced. The changes in TVB-N, TBARS, sulfhydryl/carbonyl content, and shifts in water status and pH all converged around day 60, indicating that protein–lipid co-oxidation reaches a qualitative threshold at this juncture. At the flavor level, the dissipation of key flavor compounds (eugenol, nonanal, benzeneacetaldehyde, anisaldehyde, and linalool) and the succession of dominant spoilage microorganisms also completed their primary transitions during this phase. Collectively, day 60 represents the convergence point of synchronized deterioration across multiple quality dimensions, thereby serving as a robust indicator of quality deterioration. In industrial production, it is recommended to set the 60th day of storage as a critical quality monitoring point, increase the frequency of sampling inspections before and after this date, implement a graded shipment strategy, and use high-barrier materials to cope with the risk of accelerated deterioration in the later stage. At the same time, in order to address the loss of flavor in the later stage, microencapsulated flavorings or post-addition compensation schemes can be designed.

Conclusion

This study reveals that the quality deterioration of MSC during storage is driven by the synergistic effects of protein/lipid oxidation, flavor deterioration, and microbial community succession. The sensory decline and textural changes after 60 days are primarily attributed to oxidative processes and microbial changes, while the escape of five key volatile compounds—eugenol, nonanal, benzeneacetaldehyde, p-anisaldehyde, and linalool—serves as a chemical marker for flavor deterioration. Sulfides, aromatic components, organic sulfides, alcohols, aldehydes and ketones, methyl compounds, and nitrogen oxides significantly contributed to the overall flavor of the MSC. Microbiologically, a decisive shift from Bacillus-dominance to Enterococcus-dominance occurs during storage, and this change was significantly associated with spoilage-associated genera (e.g., Enterococcus, Pseudomonas, and Acinetobacter) through amino acid, carbohydrate, and nucleotide metabolic pathways. Collectively, these findings provide novel theoretical insights into the quality and flavor deterioration, as well as the microbial evolution of MSC during storage. Furthermore, they offer a direct theoretical basis for estimating the shelf life of aquatic products and for developing targeted antibacterial preservation strategies for aquaculture industry. In the future, further in-depth analysis of the molecular regulatory networks between microorganisms and fish quality deterioration should be conducted.
