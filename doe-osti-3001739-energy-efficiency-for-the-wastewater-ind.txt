 1. BACKGROUND OF THIS RESOURCE

This resource was developed through the U.S. Department of Energy’s Sustainable Wastewater
Infrastructure of the Future (SWIFt) Initiative, which ran from 2016 to 2024. SWIFt was designed to help
municipal governments tackle the significant energy demands of drinking water and wastewater plants,
which often account for 30-40% of local energy consumption. SWIFt provided technical assistance,
training, and resources to help over 328 facilities across 43 states achieve energy savings between 5-25%,
with some partners identifying annual savings opportunities exceeding $130,000.

DOE’s work with facility partners produced a number of tools and resources that have since been
compiled and published into the Wastewater Energy Management Toolkit [1]. The toolkit contains dozens
of resources to help facilities track their energy performance, identify no-and low-cost savings strategies,
learn more about funding and financing mechanisms, and establish energy improvement master plans.
SWIFt concluded in 2024, but its impact endures through the Better Plants [2] program, which continues
to support water utilities in pursuing long-term energy, water, and waste reduction goals.

 2. PURPOSE OF THIS DOCUMENT

This information sheet discusses the technology pillar, Energy Efficiency, as a pathway toward improving
wastewater infrastructure sustainability and resiliency. To supplement existing literature on current
technologies and policies for improving resiliency at wastewater treatment plants, this document aims to
accomplish the following:

• Summarize energy management systems (EMSs).

• Present existing energy-efficiency processes, practices, and emerging technologies.

• Identify challenges, concerns, and limitations associated with the implementation of existing
 technologies.

• Serve as a comprehensive (though not exhaustive) repository for energy efficiency for wastewater
 utilities.

The Energy Efficiency Technical Information Sheet should be viewed as a general guide to established
best practices for the water and wastewater (W/WW) sector when considering implementing energy
capture technologies. Further details on associated energy capture and resource recovery avenues in
W/WW facilities are presented in the Energy Capture and Resource Recovery Technology Information
Sheet.1

 3. ENERGY EFFICIENCY FOR THE WASTEWATER INDUSTRY

Water and energy efficiency are critical because of growing resource uncertainty, including fluctuating
energy costs and drought conditions across the U.S. The water and wastewater (W/WW) industry faces
challenges such as aging infrastructure, increasing demand from population and economic growth, and
stricter water quality regulations. Because the W/WW industry is heavily reliant on electricity for its
operations, energy is one of the largest controllable costs. Therefore, prioritizing efficiency ensures that
improved process efficiency and infrastructural changes help control operating, energy, and labor costs
and prepares organizations for future regulatory requirements. Additionally, improving efficiency helps

The barriers to energy efficiency in the W/WW sector stem from a culture focused primarily on water
quality and service, skepticism about energy savings potential, concerns over permit compliance, and a
lack of knowledge about energy efficiency. Furthermore, funding for energy-efficiency assessments and
trainings is often limited. For instance, many energy utility incentives exclude W/WW agencies. W/WW
utilities are often unaware of alternative funding and financing mechanisms. On the technological front,
challenges include uneven adoption of existing technologies, hesitation toward implementing innovative
technologies, and difficulties in assessing energy consumption and performance.

To improve energy efficiency at wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs), the following strategies are
recommended:

 • Strategy 1: Understanding plant-wide energy use and implementing energy management systems
 • Strategy 2: Implementing low- and no-cost efficiency measures
• Strategy 3: Evaluating and optimizing processes and treatment trains for improved efficiency

                4.   STRATEGY 1 | USING ENERGY MANAGEMENT SYSTEMS

An energy management system (EMS) enables organizations to optimize energy use, enhance
productivity, and reduce costs by establishing an energy policy, setting measurable targets, and
implementing action plans to achieve them. It may involve adopting energy-efficient technologies,
minimizing energy waste, and improving existing processes. An EMS delivers long-term solutions
through structured plans, timelines, and accountability, fostering improved management practices and
behaviors by securing buy-in from involved stakeholders. By equipping end-users with tools and
processes to meet goals, EMS promotes continuous improvement and capability development. An
effective EMS implementation includes identifying key personnel (e.g., the plant manager, engineering
manager, operations manager, energy manager, and director/CEO) and establishing key performance
indicators to measure performance.

 2

As part of the Better Buildings Initiative, the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) has created tools such as
ISO 50001 Ready [3] that can be used extensively by W/WW utilities for understanding their energy
needs. 50001 Ready is focused on the ISO 50001 standard, which is a globally recognized, voluntary
standard for establishing an EMS. ISO 50001 fosters a culture of continuous improvement across entire
organizations rather than focusing on individual projects. It emphasizes leadership commitment, data-
driven energy performance analysis, and scalable implementation, making it transferable across multiple
facilities and adaptable to different sectors. By following the proven plan-do-check-act cycle, ISO 50001
enhances efficiency, identifies energy-saving opportunities, and ensures sustained results. It also supports
organizational resiliency by documenting practices, easing staff and management transitions, and
maintaining operational continuity during energy disruptions. The standard enables facilities to achieve
long-term energy savings and efficiency improvements. The interface for the WW-specific 50001 Ready
playbook is shown in Figure 2.

                      5.   STRATEGY 2 | LOW- AND NO-COST MEASURES

A range of low- and no-cost measures can help wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) improve energy
efficiency and reduce operational costs without major capital investments. These practical actions—
identified through DOE’s Industrial Assessment Centers and implemented at various water resource
recovery facilities nationwide—demonstrate that meaningful savings can often be achieved with minimal
upfront expense. On average, such measures have yielded payback periods of two years or less, making
them attractive first steps toward broader energy management and sustainability goals.

 3

5.1 MANAGING DEMAND RESPONSE

Although not a direct method for improving energy efficiency, managing a wastewater (WW) utility’s
electricity and demand can help mitigate energy costs. This involves and encourages a WW utility to be in
contact with its electricity and energy provider by evaluating utility bills and understanding any offerings
for financial incentives to shed (temporarily reduce electricity demand) or shift (transfer to off-peak
hours) energy and electricity load during times of high electric grid stress. Following are some strategies
for managing energy demand response:

• Evaluating whether centrifuges and backwash pumps can be operated at different times or in
 staggered fashion to shift the electricity load.

• Developing an understanding of energy and electricity bills with the retailers. See Understanding
 Your Utility Bills: Electricity [9] for further information.

• Identifying a retailer’s peak, shoulder, and off-peak times to use pay-on-time discounts, metering
 charges, and time-of-use variations discounts

See the Demand Response in Industrial Facilities Error! Reference source not found. manual for
approaches to managing facility demand. DOE’s Energy Performance Indicator [11] tool can be used to
establish baseline energy consumption and to understand energy consumption variations.

5.2 INSTALLATION MEASURES

Installation measures refer to equipment upgrades or system modifications that enhance the operational
efficiency of wastewater treatment plants. By improving the performance of pumps, motors, aeration
systems, and other energy-intensive components, these measures directly reduce electricity consumption,
optimize process control, and extend equipment life—all contributing to improved overall energy
efficiency and lower operating costs. A list of installation measures is:

• Install timers on light switches and occupancy sensors in little-used areas and adjust for scheduled
 operations.

• Install programmable thermostats and use at night set-back/setup settings.

• Turn off unnecessary lighting and install occupancy sensors.

• Identify and use energy-efficient belts compatible with the facility’s equipment.

• Change aeration blower intake filters regularly to minimize air intake resistance. Use static pressure
 to check if filters need replacement.

• Use automatic controls when available to optimize equipment, process monitoring, and operations.

5.3 ASSESSMENT MEASURES

Assessment measures involve evaluating current plant operations to identify inefficiencies and
opportunities for energy savings. These measures typically help wastewater treatment plants pinpoint
where energy is being wasted and prioritize cost-effective improvements. By establishing a data-driven
understanding of system performance, assessment measures lay the groundwork for targeted actions that
enhance overall energy efficiency and reduce operational costs. A list of assessment measures is:

 4

• Review and assess ventilation requirements to optimize efficiency, reduce space conditioning during
 nonworking hours, and manage space conditioning energy use during unoccupied times.

• Assess the potential to remove organics before entering the secondary treatment system. Assess the
 capability for high-strength organic dischargers to feed directly to an anaerobic digester.

• Review operations to identify any pumps or blowers being throttled and assess them to determine if
 they can be adjusted to operate more efficiently.

• Assess air and water piping systems in need of insulation (exposed piping).

• Identify equipment speeds and resheave blowers where needed.

• Consult the energy utility account manager to evaluate rate schedules and determine the most
 efficient rate.

5.4 OPERATION MEASURES

Operation measures focus on optimizing how existing systems and equipment are managed to improve
performance and reduce energy use. By adjusting operating schedules, refining process controls,
maintaining equipment properly, and training staff on energy-efficient practices, wastewater treatment
plants can achieve significant energy savings without major capital investments. These measures enhance
system reliability while lowering overall operational costs. A list of operation measures is:

• Test, calibrate, and maintain dissolved oxygen level/sensors in aeration tank(s), which requires
 emptying aeration tanks.

• Shift to smaller horsepower (HP) pumps/blowers during nightly or seasonal low-flow periods, if
 applicable. Note that the systems must have more than sporadic (i.e., significantly frequent) nightly
 low-flow periods.

• Reduce blower pressure to the minimum required through proper maintenance of aeration diffusers
 and distribution systems to minimize head loss. Control the set point in the aeration blower control
 strategy. Also identify, assess, and repair aeration system air main leaks (e.g., replace gasket, repair
 corrosion, perform underground maintenance) and lower aeration tank levels to reduce air header
 static pressure, if applicable (sensing O2 level may be required).

• Turn off equipment when not in use (e.g., turn off aerobic digester blower periodically or operate
 intermittently).

• Adjust system operations when the wastewater load changes.

• Raise wet well levels to reduce static head in the pump system. Coordinate all control points (low- or
 high-level alarm, pump start/stop) to adjust the wet well level upward, giving consideration to the
 facility’s hydraulic profile.

• Eliminate leaks in inert gas and compressed air lines/valves.

• Operate select aeration tanks as needed while also establishing operating protocols to enable the plant
 to bring tanks back online efficiently.

 5

• Routinely clean UV lamp sleeves to enhance transfer efficiency and decrease the number of UV
 lamps where/when possible while still meeting disinfection needs.

• Idle aeration basins/zones, if not needed; however, periodic maintenance may still be needed.

• Reschedule plant operations or reduce load to avoid on-peak hours (e.g., operate dewatering
 equipment during off-peak, load digesters during off-peak, repair equipment, and shift recycling of
 supernatant to off-peak).

              6.   STRATEGY 3 | MEDIAN ENERGY SAVINGS OPPORTUNITIES

The following actionable energy efficiency improvements across systems have been identified as possible
ways to achieve long-term cost savings and operational resilience:

1. Aeration and Mixing Efficiency

 • Dissolved Oxygen (DO) Control:

Set optimal DO levels to reduce blower energy consumption by maintaining appropriate DO levels.

Use automated DO control to improve oxygen transfer efficiency and reduce energy use.

Use ammonia-derived DO control to adjust DO levels based on real-time ammonia concentrations.

 • Mechanical Mixing and Bubble Diffusion:

Optimize mechanical mixing and bubble diffusion for enhanced aeration efficiency.

Upgrade from coarse to fine bubble diffusion to improve energy efficiency and reduce blower load.

 • Use variable frequency drives (VFDs) for blowers to adjust speed based on real-time process demands.

 • Employ motors and turbo blowers designed for high energy efficiency.

2. Disinfection System Optimization

 • UV Lamp Control:

Use turbidity sensors to control UV lamp operations.

Optimize the number and intensity of operating UV lamps to reduce energy while maintaining effective
disinfection.

Upgrade to low-pressure, high-output UV lamps for increased efficiency.

 • Chlorination/Dechlorination:

Consider energy implications and byproduct concerns when using chlorine-based disinfection compared
with UV systems.

3. Pumping System Efficiency

 6

 • Variable Speed Control:

Proper application of VFDs ensures pumps operate only at required speeds, reducing energy waste.

 • Optimal Pump Selection and Operation:

Match pump type and size (horsepower) to the task to maximize efficiency.

Use intelligent control systems like supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) for real-time
operation adjustments.

Incorporate clog-free technologies and blockage removal practices.

 • System Redesign:

Modify pumps or re-design systems to reduce friction losses, static pressure, and unnecessary flow rates.

4. Biosolids Management

 • Anaerobic Digestion and Biogas Utilization:

Use biogas/methane for process heating, space heating, and water heating.

Treat gas for use in internal combustion engines and maximize methane production by incorporating fats,
oils, and grease.

Implement solar sludge drying to reduce energy consumption.

 • Mixing System Optimization:

Adjust digester mixing systems to use the minimum mixers needed for effective operation.

Upgrade to linear motion mixing systems for improved efficiency.

5. Secondary Treatment and Return Activated Sludge (RAS) Optimization

 • RAS Management:

Minimize RAS pumping rates while ensuring sufficient microbial activity for carbon and nitrogen
treatment.

Balance microbial activity and air consumption to optimize energy use.

 • Aeration and Flow Improvements:

Improve diffusers and blowers for energy-efficient aeration.

Train operators on effectively managing flows, RAS rates, and DO levels.

 7. CONSIDERING EMERGING TECHNOLOGIES

Certain wastewater specific processes have emerged that enable improved treatment efficiency, reduced
energy consumption, and enhanced resource recovery. Emerging advanced biological processes,
enhanced solids removal methods, and cutting-edge energy recovery systems may also assist in
operations, lower costs, and compliance with evolving environmental regulations. The following section
presents a list, though not exhaustive, of new process improvements. Each process will require careful
technoeconomic evaluation before implementation because not every process will be suitable for every
utility.

1. Process Improvements

7.1 DEAMMONIFICATION [13]

Deammonification is an innovative biological treatment process targeting high-strength ammonia
wastewater, commonly found in recycle streams from dewatering anaerobically digested sludge. These
streams can carry up to 20–25% of a treatment plant’s total ammonia load. Key characteristics and
benefits of deammonification include the following:

• Ammonia to nitrogen conversion: Converts NH₃ to N₂ biologically, without requiring an organic
 carbon source, thereby reducing reliance on conventional chemical-based methods.

• Energy efficiency: Requires 55–60% less aeration energy compared with traditional
 nitrification/denitrification processes.

• Carbon usage: Consumes 0–10% of the carbon typically required in conventional processes,
 reducing CO₂ emissions while acting as a net consumer of CO₂.

• Operational benefits:

Reduces alkalinity demand for ammonia oxidation by 45%.

Decreases sludge production, minimizing handling and disposal costs.

 • Adoption: Successfully implemented in 14 U.S. WWTPs.

7.2 SIDE-STREAM ENHANCED BIOLOGICAL PHOSPHATE REMOVAL [14][15]

This process enhances biological phosphate removal by diverting RAS into a side-stream anaerobic
reactor. Key advantages include the following:

 8

• Enhanced hydrolysis and fermentation: Improved sludge hydrolysis with supplemental carbon
 from primary sludge fermentate increases phosphate removal efficiency.

• Elimination of chemical additives: Enhanced performance and stability reduce or eliminate the need
 for chemical phosphate removal agents.

7.3 ANAEROBIC MEMBRANE BIOREACTOR [16]

Combining anaerobic digestion with physical separation membranes, anaerobic membrane bioreactors
optimize organic load removal and biogas production. Key features include the following:

• Efficiency:

Biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) removal >99.5%.

Total suspended solids (TSS) removal ~100%.

• High-strength wastewater treatment: Ideal for streams rich in organics, TSS, fats, oils, grease,
 solid and semisolid wastes, and slurries.

• Retrofitting capability: Existing anaerobic digesters can be upgraded to incorporate this technology,
 maximizing resource recovery.

7.4 SOLIDS REMOVAL TECHNOLOGIES [17]

The following advanced solids removal methods improve wastewater treatment efficiency:

• Filtration and sedimentation:

Compressible media filtration

Magnetite ballasted sedimentation

Ballasted high-rate clarification

• Advanced filtration:

Nanofiltration and reverse osmosis

Multistage filtration

• Optimization:

Solids minimization in cyclic metabolic environments

Enhanced settleability using magnetite ballasted activated sludge

7.5 INTEGRATED PRIMARY TREATMENT

The following are innovative systems for primary treatment to integrate automation, compaction, and
dewatering:

 9

• Salsnes filter2: Reduces influent BOD by 40% and TSS by 65%, with integrated sludge thickening
 and dewatering

• Advanced grit removal systems: Enhance efficiency and protect downstream processes

1. Energy Recovery

7.6 COUPLED AEROBIC-ANOXIC NITROUS DECOMPOSITION OPERATION [18]

This process enhances energy recovery by leveraging partial nitrification and denitrification to produce
nitrous oxide (N₂O), which is co-combusted with methane (CH₄). Key features include the following:

• Nitrous oxide production:

Converts 60%–80% of nitrite (NO₂⁻) to N₂O

Developed for ammonia treatment in wastewater

 • Energy recovery: Combustion of N₂O with CH₄ produces significant energy gains

7.7 MICROBIAL FUEL CELL [19]

Microbial fuel cells generate electricity by harnessing bacterial metabolism of organic matter, typically
from chemical oxygen demand in wastewater. Key aspects include the following:

• Electricity generation: Converts organic waste into direct electrical energy.

• Hydrogen production: Capable of producing pure H₂ in oxygen-absent environments, presenting
 potential for green energy applications.

 8. IMPLICATIONS AND POTENTIAL EFFICIENCY OPPORTUNITIES

Energy efficiency encompasses both operational improvements and energy recovery initiatives, with
energy neutrality being a natural progression of efficient practices. By addressing inefficiencies, adopting
advanced technologies, and fostering innovation (Figure 3), the W/WW sector could achieve significant
cost savings, regulatory compliance, and enhanced sustainability.

1. Improved operational efficiency:

Enhancing primary treatment and solids capture in thickening and dewatering reduces energy demands by
lowering BOD, TSS, and total Kjeldahl nitrogen levels, which in turn minimizes the energy required for
downstream biological treatment.

Addressing fouling in fine bubble diffusers through improved operations and maintenance and adopting
energy-efficient equipment like high-efficiency blowers and VFDs can significantly reduce aeration
blower electricity use, a major component of energy costs in W/WW facilities.

Use frameworks like ISO 50001 to develop structured energy management plans, incorporating
continuous improvement cycles (plan-do-check-act) for sustained efficiency gains.

2. Leveraging advanced technologies:

Adoption of high-efficiency blowers, VFD replacements, and innovative aeration systems (e.g., parabolic
invent mixers) improves energy performance and reduces operational costs.

Whereas energy recovery from anaerobic digestion is a key component of energy neutrality, integrating it
into energy-efficient practices with combined heat and power (CHP) systems enhances overall system
efficiency. Additionally, exploring opportunities to expand the use of food waste and biosolids for
codigestion enhances biogas production, contributing to energy recovery and supporting energy-efficient
operations.

Investigate alternatives to traditional CHP systems, such as linear motion engines for air pumping, while
addressing challenges in gas conditioning for biogas applications.

Explore advanced data management applications (such as digital twin environment) for process
optimization and invest in low-energy technologies for nitrification/denitrification to enhance treatment
efficiency.

3. Regulatory and process drivers:

Increasingly stringent regulations and the need for energy-efficient nutrient removal, such as through
side-stream treatment for ammonia and phosphorous are driving innovation in energy-efficient practices
to meet regulatory requirements while focusing on reducing energy usage in the process.

Limited regulatory acceptance of emerging technologies like fuel cells highlight the need for targeted
research and optimization.

Achieving energy efficiency in wastewater treatment plants requires a balanced approach that integrates
operational improvements, advanced technologies, and energy recovery strategies. By focusing on
process optimization, proactive maintenance, and the adoption of innovative solutions, utilities can
significantly reduce energy consumption while enhancing treatment performance and reliability. These
efforts not only move facilities closer to energy neutrality but also strengthen their long-term
sustainability and resilience.

The Glenbard Wastewater Authority (GWA), Illinois

In using DOE’s 50001 Ready
Navigator tool, GWA staff found the
energy mapping exercises to be most

stock of the finer details of the
facility’s operations—such as
tracking runtimes and evaluating
energy bills—ensured that staff were
capturing the system accurately.

Having numbers at hand also
allowed them to determine the
facility’s biggest energy users, some
of which were affecting energy use
in ways that previously had gone
unnoticed. A detailed case study is available here [20].

The Noman M. Cole, Jr. Pollution Control Plant (NMCPCP), Fairfax County, Virginia

 50001 Ready helped NMCPCP’s Energy Team account
 for inefficiencies across its WW treatment process. For

 remained relatively unchanged for nearly 10 years, only
 occasionally adjusting the chemicals used in that process.

 Engaging with 50001 Ready provided the plant’s staff
 with a framework within which they could perform a
 thorough assessment of their treatment process; this effort
 enabled the energy team to identify savings opportunities
 in the tank mixing process. A detailed case study is
available here [21].

 CASE STUDY: STRATEGY 2

UV Disinfection Retrofit Study by Narragansett Bay Commission [22]

The Narragansett Bay Commission had a 10-year-old disinfection system at its 20 MGD Bucklin Point
WWTP facility.

Problem: Elevated fecal coliforms at low summer
flow formed algae and floc solids that shield fecal
coliforms from UV radiation. Elevated UV
disinfection dose was not fixing the problem.

Solution:

 •  Clean UV channel periodically
 •  Ensure all lamps are replaced at the same
 time
 • Automate disinfection system
 • Upgrade final clarifiers
Impact:

 • Maintained compliance to effluent limits
 • Normalized electric use reduced 48% in 3 years

 CASE STUDY: STRATEGY 3

Victor Valley Wastewater Reclamation Authority [23]

Plant Capacity: 12.2 MGD Average Flow

Process Under Consideration:

 •  Activated sludge modified Ludzack-Ettinger
 process
 • Chlorination/dechlorination
Problem

 •  Increasing electrical cost and demand owing to
 regulatory nitrogen limitations and
 trihalomethanes
 • Need for nutrient removal and UV disinfection
 system
 • Not enough biogas to justify a CHP system for
 additional energy generation
Solution

 •  Optimized conventional activated sludge process for biological nutrient removal
 •  Replaced gas chlorination disinfection system with UV disinfection
 •  Updated and redesigned the hauled waste and digestion facility to accept high-strength waste to
 come from local industries
 • Installed CHP system (two 800 kW internal combustion engines)
Impacts

 • Significantly reduced nutrient discharge
 • Optimized UV system and reduced UV system electrical demand by 50%
 • Increased biogas production by 300%
 • Increased revenue
 • Reduced operational costs and improved process stability
