Variants of the Quantum Phase Operator for the Harmonic Oscillator
Bogdan D. Djordjević, Nikolay A. Ivanov

Abstract
We introduce and study quantum phase operators associated with the Quantum Harmonic Oscillator (QHO). We show that these operators are trace-class perturbations of the Susskind–Glogower operators and examine their mathematical and physical properties. The construction is motivated by the physically relevant two-phase case.



MSC 2020 Classification: 47A05; 47B93; 78A97; 78M99; 81Q10.

Keyphrases: Quantum Harmonic Oscillator, Spectral Theory, Quantum Phase Operator.

1
Introduction

Physical relevance of the quantum phase operators has been long recognized and extensively discussed in the literature; see, for example,
[
7
,
9
]
. The notion of phase plays a fundamental role in quantum theory, particularly in systems where only relative phases (phase differences) possess physical significance, reflecting the invariance of quantum states under global phase transformations.

On this occasion, our main concern is mathematical definition and analytic treatment of phase operators occurring in the Quantum Harmonic Oscillator (QHO). In this scenario, a distinctive feature of the phase observable is its intimate connection with the excitation level of the system. In particular, the concept of phase becomes problematic in the vacuum state, for which no physically meaningful phase can be assigned. Conversely, both theoretical considerations and experimental observations indicate that phase can be determined with increasing precision as the number of photons increases. This behavior is especially evident in high-intensity laser fields, where large occupation numbers allow for a more accurate characterization of phase-related properties.

Following the framework developed in
[
12
]
, in this paper we define and study a variant for quantum phase operators that arise from the correspondence between Bose–Einstein statistics and the quantum harmonic oscillator (QHO). In Section
2
we develop mathematical foundation for the phase operators, examine their commutator and spectral properties. The theoretical motivation and heuristics of our considerations are then discussed in detail in Section
3
.

1.1
Theoretical setup

Recall that (see e.g.,
[
13
]
) for the QHO the hamiltonian of the system is

H=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}+\frac{m}{2}\omega^{2}x^{2},

(1)

where the position operator
x
and momentum operator
p
satisfy the well-known canonical commutation relation:

[x,p]=i\hbar I.

(2)

The following observation follows immediately:

Proposition 1.1
.

The following relations are true for
H
,
x
, and
p
:

[H,x]=-i\frac{\hbar}{m}p,

(3)

[H,p]=i\hbar\omega^{2}mx.

(4)

Proof.

from (
2
) it follows that

xp^{2}-p^{2}x=(xp)p-p(px)=(px+i\hbar)p-p(xp-i\hbar)=2i\hbar p,

therefore

[H,x]=\frac{-1}{2m}(xp^{2}-p^{2}x)=\frac{-i\hbar}{m}p.

Similarly,

x^{2}p-px^{2}=x(xp)-(px)x=x(px+i\hbar)-(xp-i\hbar)x=2i\hbar x,

therefore

[H,p]=\frac{m\omega^{2}}{2}(x^{2}p-px^{2})=i\hbar\omega^{2}mx.

∎

It is a known fact that (see
[
14
]
)
H
from (
1
) is self-adjoint, bounded from below, and has a purely point spectrum
\sigma(H)
, comprising out of eigenvalues of the half-integer form:

\sigma(H)=\left\{\hbar\omega\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right):n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\right\},

(5)

where each eigenvalue is simple, and the corresponding eigen-function is the
n-
th Hermite function
\Psi_{n}(x)
:

H\psi(x)=\hbar\omega\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\psi(x)\Leftrightarrow\psi(x)=\Psi_{n}(x),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

(6)

with

\Psi_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}n!}}\left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4}e^{-\frac{m\omega x^{2}}{2\hbar}}H_{n}\left(\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}}x\right),

(7)

where
H_{n}
is the physicists’ Hermite polynomial,

H_{n}(z)=(-1)^{n}e^{z^{2}}\frac{d^{n}}{dz^{n}}\left(e^{-z^{2}}\right),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(8)

Combined, by writing
\Psi_{n}
as
n
, the eigen-problem (
5
)-(
6
) reads

H|n\rangle=\hbar\omega\left(n+1/2\right)|n\rangle,\quad,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(9)

Accordingly, the ladder operators are defined as:

a=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2m\omega\hbar}}\left(-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-im\omega x\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2m\omega\hbar}}\left(p-im\omega x\right),

(10)

a^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2m\omega\hbar}}\left(-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+im\omega x\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2m\omega\hbar}}\left(p+im\omega x\right),

(11)

which satisfy the canonical commutation relations:

[a,a^{\dagger}]=I

(12)

[H,a]=-\hbar\omega a,\quad[H,a^{\dagger}]=\hbar\omega a^{\dagger}.

(13)

Ergo,
a
is a lowering operator while
a^{\dagger}
is a raising operator, in the sense that:

\begin{cases}a|n\rangle=\sqrt{n}|n-1\rangle,\ n\geq 1;\quad a|0\rangle=0;\\
a^{\dagger}|n=\sqrt{n+1}|n+1\rangle.\end{cases}

(14)

The operators
a
and
a^{\dagger}
produce the number operator
N
,

N=a^{\dagger}a

(15)

which is self-adjoint, bounded from below, and

H=\hbar\omega\left(N+\frac{1}{2}I\right)=\hbar\omega\left(a^{\dagger}a+\frac{1}{2}I\right)=\hbar\omega\left(aa^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}I\right).

(16)

Finally, by adding and subtracting (
10
) from (
11
) respectively, we get

p=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega\hbar}{2}}(a+a^{\dagger}),\quad x=i\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\left(a-a^{\dagger}\right).

(17)

The latter shows that
a+a^{\dagger}
and
i(a-a^{\dagger})
are self-adjoint operators.

The Quantum phase operator was considered and theoreticized in
[
12
]
. Later on it was studied by many
scholars, e.g.
[
2
]
and
[
1
]
.

1.1.1
Positive square root and its inverse

Since
H
is a positive operator, there exists a unique positive square root
H^{1/2}
, defined on the same domain as the operator
H
is. This is guaranteed by the Kato theorem below:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
6
]
)
.

Let
E:D_{E}\mapsto{\mathcal{H}}
be a strictly accretive linear operator,
D_{E}\subset{\mathcal{H}}
. Then there exists a unique strictly accretive square root
E^{1/2}
defined in
D_{E}
, such that
\forall u\in D_{E}
:
\left(E^{1/2}\right)^{2}u=Eu
, and

E^{1/2}u=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\sqrt{t}\right)^{-1}E\left(t+E\right)^{-1}udt.

(18)

Consequently, the operator
H^{1/2}
as such exists, defined in the same set
D_{H}
as
H
, and obeys to the formula (
18
) above:

H^{1/2}u=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\sqrt{t}\right)^{-1}H\left(t+H\right)^{-1}udt.

(19)

Recall that Hermite polynomials comprise the eigen-functions for
H
that form its corresponding egienstates, denoted as (
9
). Then by the spectral mapping theorem we have

{H}^{1/2}|n\rangle=\sqrt{\omega\hbar\left(n+1/2\right)}|n\rangle,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(20)

Therefore, its spectrum is also bounded from below, and by the closed graph theorem, the operator
H^{1/2}
has a bounded inverse, and due to associativity one has that

\left(H^{1/2}\right)^{-1}=\left(H^{-1}\right)^{1/2},

where
H^{-1}
is the bounded inverse of
H
and
\left(H^{-1}\right)^{1/2}
is the unique positive square root of
H^{-1}
. In that sense, the operator

H^{-1/2}:=\left(H^{1/2}\right)^{-1}=\left(H^{-1}\right)^{1/2}

(21)

is well-defined bounded linear operator, and by the spectral mapping theorem yet again it follows that

H^{-1/2}|n\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\omega\hbar\left(n+1/2\right)}}|n\rangle,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(22)

Therefore, throughout the remaining text we will understand that
H^{-1/2}
denotes the bounded inverse of the positive square root of the given Hamiltonian, satisfying (
21
)–(
22
).

2.1
Exact Sine and Cosine Operators

Ideally, one would want to define time-like operators
T_{0}
and
T_{0}^{\dagger}
in the following manner:

T_{0}:=\frac{\sqrt{\hbar\omega}}{2}\left(aH^{-1/2}+H^{-1/2}a\right),\quad T_{0}^{\dagger}:=\frac{\sqrt{\hbar\omega}}{2}\left(a^{\dagger}H^{-1/2}+H^{-1/2}a^{\dagger}\right).

(23)

This is well-motivated by the two phase case in Section
3
.

By the virtue of (
21
) and (
22
), the operators
T_{0}
and
T_{0}^{\dagger}
are well-defined and bounded. Additionally, by (
23
), they preserve the lowering-raising properties inherited from the operators
a
and
a^{\dagger}
, respectively:

Lemma 2.1
.

The operators
T_{0}
and
T_{0}^{\dagger}
satisfy

[H,T_{0}]=-\hbar\omega T_{0},\quad[H,T_{0}^{\dagger}]=\hbar\omega T_{0}^{\dagger}.

(24)

Proof.

Proof follows directly from (
13
) and a simple algebraic property:

[H,aH^{-1/2}+H^{-1/2}a]=[H,a]H^{-1/2}+H^{-1/2}[H,a].

The same procedure goes when we write
a^{\dagger}
instead of
a
.
∎

For convenience, we are going to write

b:=\sqrt{\hbar\omega}a,\quad b^{\dagger}:=\sqrt{\hbar\omega}a^{\dagger}.

(25)

That way the operators
T_{0}
and
T_{0}^{\dagger}
read

T_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\left(bH^{-1/2}+H^{-1/2}b\right),\quad T_{0}^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{2}\left(b^{\dagger}H^{-1/2}+H^{-1/2}b^{\dagger}\right).

(26)

The following lemma will be useful for the later text:

Lemma 2.2
.

For the operator
b
introduced in (
25
), the following are true:

(a)

[H,b]=-\omega\hbar b,\quad[H,b^{\dagger}]=\omega\hbar b^{\dagger},\quad[b,b^{\dagger}]=\hbar\omega I.

(27)

(b)

[H^{-1},b]=\hbar\omega H^{-1}bH^{-1},\quad[H^{-1},b^{\dagger}]=-\hbar\omega H^{-1}b^{\dagger}H^{-1}.

(28)

Consequently, both
[H^{-1},b]
and
[H^{-1},b^{\dagger}]
are bounded operators.

(c)

[H^{-1/2},b]=\hbar\omega\bar{b},\quad[H^{-1/2},b^{\dagger}]=-\hbar\omega\bar{b}^{\dagger},

(29)

where
\bar{b}
and
\bar{b}^{\dagger}
are respectively the unique bounded solutions to

H^{-1/2}\bar{b}+\bar{b}H^{-1/2}=-H^{-1}bH^{-1},\quad H^{-1/2}\bar{b}^{\dagger}+\bar{b}^{\dagger}H^{-1/2}=-H^{-1}b^{\dagger}H^{-1}.

(30)

Consequently, the commutators
[H^{-1/2},b]
and
[H^{-1/2},b^{\dagger}]
are bounded.

Proof.

The relations (
27
) follow immediately from (
12
) and (
13

28
) follow from the commutator property:

[H^{-1},b]=H^{-1}(bH-Hb)H^{-1}=-H^{-1}[H,b]H^{-1}=\omega\hbar H^{-1}bH^{-1},

and similarly for
b^{\dagger}
. In order to prove (
29
), we first conduct a similar transform as the previous one,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H^{-1/2}(H^{1/2}b-bH^{1/2})H^{-1/2}=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H^{1/2}\left(H^{-1/2}bH^{-1/2}\right)-\left(H^{-1/2}bH^{-1/2}\right)H^{1/2}.

Note that
H^{-1/2}bH^{-1/2}
is a bounded operator. That being said, the formula (

and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &[b,H^{-1/2}]=\\
&H^{1/2}\left(H^{-1/2}bH^{-1/2}\right)-\left(H^{-1/2}bH^{-1/2}\right)H^{1/2}=\\
&\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-1/2}H\left(t+H\right)^{-1}\left(H^{-1/2}bH^{-1/2}\right)udt-\\
-&\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-1/2}\left(H^{-1/2}bH^{-1/2}\right)H\left(t+H\right)^{-1}udt,\end{aligned}

(31)

for each
u
from the domain of
H
. Since the operators at hand leave the domain
D_{H}
invariant, we have that for every
t\geq 0
and for every

:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\left(H\left(t+H\right)^{-1}\left(H^{-1/2}bH^{-1/2}\right)-\left(H^{-1/2}bH^{-1/2}\right)H\left(t+H\right)^{-1}\right)u=\\
&\left(t+H\right)^{-1}\left(H\left(H^{-1/2}bH^{-1/2}\right)\left(t+H\right)-\left(t+H\right)\left(H^{-1/2}bH^{-1/2}\right)H\right)\left(t+H\right)^{-1}u=\\

(32)

[H^{-1/2},b]u=\frac{\hbar\omega}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{1/2}(t+H)^{-1}\left(H^{-1/2}bH^{-1/2}\right)(t+H)^{-1}udt,

for every

On the other hand, notice that the operator Sylvester equation

\left(-H^{1/2}\right)y+y\left(-H^{1/2}\right)=H^{-1/2}bH^{-1/2}

(34)

is regular on

(35)

see

By writing

[H^{-1/2},b]=\hbar\omega\bar{b}.

An analogous procedure goes for showing that

[H^{-1/2},b^{\dagger}]=-\hbar\omega\bar{b}^{\dagger},

where
\bar{b}^{\dagger}=\left(\bar{b}\right)^{\dagger}

Since

do not commute, it is natural to suspect that

Theorem 2.1
.

The operators
T_{0}
and
T_{0}^{\dagger}

[T_{0},T_{0}^{\dagger}]=\hbar\omega H^{-1}-\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\operatorname{Re}\left(H^{-1}bH^{-1}b^{\dagger}+(b^{\dagger}\bar{b}+b\bar{b}^{\dagger})H^{-1/2}\right).

Proof.

First, direct computations give

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &4[T_{,}T_{0}^{\dagger}]=H^{-1/2}(bb^{\dagger}-b^{\dagger}b)H^{-1/2}+\left(bH^{-1}b^{\dagger}-b^{\dagger}H^{-1}b\right)+\\
&[bH^{-1/2},b^{\dagger}H^{-1/2}]+[H^{-1/2}b,H^{-1/2}b^{\dagger}].\end{aligned}

(37)

The first summand in (
37
) is evaluated by (
27
) as

H^{-1/2}(bb^{\dagger}-b^{\dagger}b)H^{-1/2}=\hbar\omega H^{-1}.

(38)

Next, by writing
bH^{-1}
as

bH^{-1}=H^{-1}b-[H^{-1},b]=H^{-1}b-\hbar\omega H^{-1}bH^{-1},

where the latter is obtained by utilizing (
28
), we rewrite the second summand in (

\displaystyle bH^{-1}b^{\dagger}-b^{\dagger}H^{-1}b=

\displaystyle H^{-1}bb^{\dagger}-\hbar\omega H^{-1}bH^{-1}b^{\dagger}-b^{\dagger}H^{-1}b=

\displaystyle[H^{-1}b,b^{\dagger}]-\hbar\omega H^{-1}bH^{-1}b^{\dagger}.

By invoking that

[AB,C]=A[B,C]+[A,C]B,

(39)

the latter transforms into

[H^{-1}b,b^{\dagger}]-\hbar\omega H^{-1}bH^{-1}b^{\dagger}=H^{-1}[b,b^{\dagger}]+[H^{-1},b^{\dagger}]b-\hbar\omega H^{-1}bH^{-1}b^{\dagger}.

The first two summands of the right-hand-side in the last equation are evaluated by (
27
) and (
28
), respectively, and combined give

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &bH^{-1}b^{\dagger}-b^{\dagger}H^{-1}b=\\
&\hbar\omega H^{-1}-\hbar\omega H^{-1}b^{\dagger}H^{-1}b-\hbar\omega H^{-1}bH^{-1}b^{\dagger}=\\
&\hbar\omega H^{-1}-2\hbar\omega H^{-1}\operatorname{Re}\left(bH^{-1}b^{\dagger}\right).\end{aligned}

(40)

Note that
H^{-1}
and
bH^{-1}b^{\dagger}

*-
commute: this follows from

\displaystyle[H^{-1},bH^{-1}b^{\dagger}]=H^{-1}bH^{-1}b^{\dagger}-bH^{-1}b^{\dagger}H^{-1}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\left([H^{-1},b]b^{\dagger}+b[H^{-1},b^{\dagger}]\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\left(H^{-1}bb^{\dagger}-bH^{-1}b^{\dagger}+bH^{-1}b^{\dagger}-bb^{\dagger}H^{-1}\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\left(H^{-1}bb^{\dagger}-bb^{\dagger}H^{-1}\right)=0,

where the last equality follows from the fact that

and

commute. Dually,

and

Returning to (

bH^{-1}b^{\dagger}-b^{\dagger}H^{-1}b=\hbar\omega H^{-1}-2\hbar\omega\operatorname{Re}\left(H^{-1}bH^{-1}b^{\dagger}\right).

(41)

Finally, the summands
[bH^{-1/2},b^{\dagger}H^{-1/2}]
and

are evaluated by first utilizing the algebraic identity (

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &[bH^{-1/2},b^{\dagger}H^{-1/2}]=b[H^{-1/2},b^{\dagger}H^{-1/2}]+[b,b^{\dagger}H^{-1/2}]H^{-1/2}=\\
&b[H^{-1/2},b^{\dagger}]H^{-1/2}+[b,b^{\dagger}]H^{-1}+b^{\dagger}[b,H^{-1/2}]H^{-1/2}=\\

(42)

and, analogously,

[H^{-1/2}b,H^{-1/2}b^{\dagger}]=\hbar\omega H^{-1}-\hbar\omega H^{-1/2}\left(\bar{b}b^{\dagger}+\bar{b}^{\dagger}b\right).

(43)

Substituting (

.

The operators

Proof.

Directly.
∎

\frac{1}{4i}\left(\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{2n}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{2n}}}\right)|n-1\rangle-\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{2(n+1)}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{2(n+1)}}}\right)\right)|n+1\rangle,\quad n\in\mathbb{N},\end{cases}

(52)

2.2
Asymptotic analysis and eigenfunctions

For a sufficiently large
n
, the expressions
(n\pm 1/2)^{-1/2}
transform into

(n\pm 1/2)^{-1/2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(1\pm\frac{1}{2n}\right)^{-1/2}\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(1\mp\frac{1}{4n}\right),\quad n>>1,

and
T_{0}
is asymptotically estimated as

T_{0}|n\rangle\approx|n-1\rangle,\quad n>>1,

(53)

i.e.,
T_{0}
approximately acts as the lowering operator.
By writing
n+1/2=n+1-1/2
and
n+3/2=n+1+1/2
we get the same estimate for
T_{0}^{\dagger}
, i.e.,

(n+1\pm 1/2)^{-1/2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}\left(1\pm\frac{1}{2(n+1)}\right)^{-1/2}\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}\left(1\mp\frac{1}{4(n+1)}\right),

when
n>>1
, therefore
T_{0}^{\dagger}
is asymptotically estimated as

T_{0}^{\dagger}|n\rangle\approx|n+1\rangle,\quad n>>1,

(54)

i.e.,
T_{0}^{\dagger}
is approximately the raising operator.
Finally, this implies that

C_{0}|n\rangle\approx\frac{1}{2}\left(|n-1\rangle+|n+1\rangle\right),\quad S_{0}|n\rangle\approx\frac{1}{2i}\left(|n-1\rangle-|n+1\rangle\right),\quad n>>1.

(55)

This shows that the operators
T_{0}
,
T_{0}^{\dagger}
,
C_{0}
, and
S_{0}
asymptotically behave (mathematically speaking) as the operators introduced by Susskind and Glogower in
[
12
]
: indeed, the operators

T_{SG}:=(N+1)^{-1/2}a,\quad T^{\dagger}_{SG}:=a^{\dagger}(N+1)^{-1/2},

(56)

introduced by Susskind and Glogower in the said paper
[
12
]
, behave as the lowering and raising operators:

T_{SG}|n+1\rangle=n,\quad T_{SG}|0\rangle=0,\quad T^{\dagger}_{SG}|n\rangle=n+1,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(57)

Therefore, the cosine and sine operators, defined in
[
12
]
in the natural way:

C_{SG}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(T_{SG}+T^{\dagger}_{SG}\right),\quad S_{SG}:=\frac{1}{2i}\left(T_{SG}-T^{\dagger}_{SG}\right),

(58)

satisfy the commutator property:

[C_{SG},S_{SG}]|0\rangle=1;\quad[C_{SG},S_{SG}]|n\rangle=0,\ n\in\mathbb{N},

(59)

where this rank-one commutator indicates that
C_{SG}
and
S_{SG}
cannot be observed simultaneously (see
[
12
]
for details), justifying the time-energy uncertianty principle (see below).

Given that our operators
T_{0}
,
T^{\dagger}_{0}
,
C_{0}
, and
S_{0}
asymptotically do behave as the respective operators
T_{SG}
,
T^{\dagger}_{SG}
,
C_{SG}
, and
S_{SG}
from
[
12
]
, in what follows, we proceed to estimate by how much the operators
T_{0}
,
T_{0}^{\dagger}
,
C_{0}
, and
S_{0}
deviate from their asymptotic approximations (
53
)–(
55
). We conduct this analysis by introducing a perturbation operator
M
, which is
H
-bounded and defined in a supset of
D_{H}
, i.e.,
D_{M}\supset D_{H}
. The idea is to introduce a perturbed time operator
T_{M}
and its adjoint
T_{M}^{\dagger}
, provided in the manner that

T_{M}=\frac{1}{2}\left(H^{-1/2}b+bH^{-1/2}\right)+M,\quad T_{M}^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{2}\left(H^{-1/2}b^{\dagger}+b^{\dagger}H^{-1/2}\right)+M^{\dagger},

(60)

for the said
H-
bounded operator
M
. As before, one has

C_{M}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(T_{M}+T_{M}^{\dagger}\right),\quad

(61)

S_{M}:=\frac{1}{2i}\left(T_{M}-T_{M}^{\dagger}\right).

(62)

Note that, due to the time-energy uncertainty principle, the time operators
T_{M}
and
T_{M}^{\dagger}
, and consequently, the operators
C_{M}
and
S_{M}
, are not uniquely determined at the ground state
|0\rangle
. This is because at the lowest state, the phase
\phi_{0}
is not unique but rather experiences arbitrary dispersion over
[0,2\pi)
, indicating that the energy fluctuates even when there are no particles occupying the ground state. As such, the phase
\phi_{0}
is often regarded as a quasi-probability distribution. We treat this ambiguity by introducing some arbitrary vectors
v(\phi_{0})
and
u(\phi_{0})\in{\mathcal{H}}
,
\|u\|
,
\|v\|\leq 1
, and we point out that
\phi_{0}
cannot be recovered from them (see
[
12
]
). Consequently, for fixed (but arbitrary) vectors
v(\phi_{0})
and
u(\phi_{0})
, we define

c_{0}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(v(\phi_{0})+u(\phi_{0})\right),\quad s_{0}:=\frac{1}{2i}\left(v(\phi_{0})-u(\phi_{0})\right).

(63)

In
[
12
]
it was assumed that
c_{0}=\frac{1}{2}|1\rangle
, and
s_{0}=\frac{i}{2}|1\rangle
, which need not be the only possibility.
Below we proceed to evaluate admissible operator(s)
M
for which the cosine operator
C_{M}
and the sine operator
S_{M}
satisfy the sought properties:

C_{M}|n\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left(|n-1\rangle+|n+1\rangle\right),\quad n\geq 1;\quad C_{M}|0\rangle=c_{0}.

(64)

S_{M}|n\rangle=\frac{1}{2i}\left(|n-1\rangle-|n+1\rangle\right),\quad n\geq 1;\quad S_{M}|0\rangle=s_{0}.

(65)

Define the operator
L
as:

L|n\rangle=\begin{cases}0,\quad n=0,\\
\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1/2}}\right)\right)|n\rangle,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.\end{cases}

(66)

Lemma 2.3
.

The operator
L
is self-adjoint and in the trace-class.

Proof.

That
L
is self-adjoint follows from the SVEP and its definition in (
66
). Moreover, by analyzing the real positive sequence

\lambda_{n}=1-\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1/2}}\right)

(67)

we see that
\lambda_{n}
is decreasing and tends to zero when
n\to\infty
. By the same estimate for Taylor expansion,

(n\pm 1/2)^{-1/2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(1\pm\frac{1}{2n}\right)^{-1/2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(1\mp\frac{1}{4n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\right),\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

we have that

\lambda_{n}=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right),\quad n\to\infty.

(68)

This proves that the sequence
\left(\lambda_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is in
\ell_{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Equivalently,
L
is a self-adjoint trace-class operator.
∎

Let
V
be defined as

V|n\rangle=|n-1\rangle,\ \quad n\in\mathbb{N},\quad V|0\rangle=0.

(69)

Accordingly, its conjugate
V^{\dagger}
is the opposite shift

V^{\dagger}|n\rangle=|n+1\rangle,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(70)

Note that
V^{\dagger}
is the unilateral shift.

Theorem 2.3
.

For a given
H-
bounded linear operator
M
, let
T_{M}
,
T^{\dagger}_{M}
,
C_{M}
, and
S_{M}
be defined as (
60
), (
61
), and (
62
), respectively. The following claims are equivalent:

(a)

The operator
C_{M}
satisfies (
64
) and the operator
S_{M}
satisfies (
65
).

(b)

The operator
M
is a (bounded) trace-class operator, and is exactly of the form

M|n\rangle=\begin{cases}v(\phi_{0}),\quad n=0;\\
VL|n\rangle,\quad n\in\mathbb{N},\end{cases}

(71)

where
L
is defined as (
66
), and
V
is the lowering operator (
69
).

Proof.

We start with the lowest eigenstate
|0\rangle
. By (
47
) and (
48
) we have

S_{M}|0\rangle=s_{0}\Leftrightarrow\frac{1}{2i}\left(M-M^{\dagger}\right)|0\rangle-s_{0}=\frac{1}{4i}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3/2}}\right)|1\rangle.

(72)

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} c_{0}=&C_{M}|0\rangle\Leftrightarrow\\
c_{0}=&\frac{1}{2}\left(M+M^{\dagger}\right)|0\rangle+\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{3/2}}\right)|1\rangle=M|0\rangle-is_{0},\end{aligned}

(73)

Giving

M|0\rangle=c_{0}+is_{0},\quad M^{\dagger}|0\rangle=c_{0}-is_{0}.

(74)

For
n>0
, it follows from (
47
), (
48
), (
60
), and (
61
) that:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} C_{M}|n\rangle=&\frac{1}{2}\left(M+M^{\dagger}\right)|n\rangle+\\
+&\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1/2}}\right)\right)|n-1\rangle+\\
+&\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{n+1}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+3/2}}\right)\right)|n+1\rangle.\end{aligned}

(75)

Therefore (
64
) holds if and only if

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\left(M+M^{\dagger}\right)|n\rangle=\\
&\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1/2}}\right)\right)|n-1\rangle+\\
+&\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{n+1}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+3/2}}\right)\right)|n+1\rangle=\\
=&\left(\lambda_{n}|n-1\rangle+\lambda_{n+1}|n+1\rangle\right)=\\
=&\left(VL|n\rangle+LV^{\dagger}|n\rangle\right)=\\
=&\left(VL+LV^{\dagger}\right)|n\rangle.\end{aligned}

(76)

Since
L
is trace-class and self-adjoint, while
V
is a partial isometry, it follows that
VL
is bounded and
(VL)^{\dagger}=LV^{\dagger}
. Then (
76
) transforms into

\left(M+M^{\dagger}\right)|n\rangle=\left(VL+\left(VL\right)^{\dagger}\right)|n\rangle,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(77)

If
M=VL
then
M^{\dagger}=(VL)^{\dagger}
and vice-versa. In this case
M
and
M^{\dagger}
are trace-class. Otherwise, if
M=\left(VL+F\right)
, where
F\neq 0
, then
F
is also
H-
bounded with the same domain as
M
, and in that case

M^{\dagger}=\left(VL\right)^{\dagger}+F^{\dagger}
. But then

\left(M+M^{\dagger}\right)|n\rangle=\left(VL+\left(VL\right)^{\dagger}+F+F^{\dagger}\right)|n\rangle=\left(VL+\left(VL\right)^{\dagger}\right)|n\rangle

implying that

\left(F+F^{\dagger}\right)|n\rangle=0,\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

so
F
is skew-symmetric.

On the other hand, from (
47
), (
48
), (
60
), and (
62
) it follows that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} S_{M}|n\rangle=&\frac{1}{2}\left(M-M^{\dagger}\right)|n\rangle+\\
+&\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1/2}}\right)\right)|n-1\rangle-\\
+&\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{n+1}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+3/2}}\right)\right)|n+1\rangle.\end{aligned}

(78)

Therefore (
65
) holds if and only if

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\left(M-M^{\dagger}\right)|n\rangle=\\
&\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1/2}}\right)\right)|n-1\rangle-\\
-&\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{n+1}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+3/2}}\right)\right)|n+1\rangle=\\
=&\left(\lambda_{n}|n-1\rangle-\lambda_{n+1}|n+1\rangle\right)=\\
=&\left(VL|n\rangle-LV^{\dagger}|n\rangle\right)=\\
=&\left(VL-\left(VL\right)^{\dagger}\right)|n\rangle.\end{aligned}

(79)

As before, if
M=VL
then
M^{\dagger}=(VL)^{\dagger}
. In this case
M
and
M^{\dagger}
are trace-class. Otherwise, if
M=VL+P
, where
P\neq 0
, then
P
is also
H-
bounded with the same domain as
M
, and in that case

M^{\dagger}=\left(\left(VL\right)^{\dagger}-P^{\dagger}\right)
. But then

\left(M-M^{\dagger}\right)|n\rangle=\left(VL-\left(VL\right)^{\dagger}+P-P^{\dagger}\right)|n\rangle=\left(VL-\left(VL\right)^{\dagger}\right)|n\rangle

implying that

\left(P-P^{\dagger}\right)|n\rangle=0,\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

so

is a symmetric densely defined operator. Combining the calculations from (
77
) and (
79
), we conclude that either

on

, or

VL+P-F=LV^{\dagger}

for some symmetric

, both defined in

. From the former one has that

, i.e.,

, therefore (

\dagger
and obtains

implying that

and consequently

for

.
∎

3
Difference of two phases

For the standard setup (see
[
7
]
and
[
9
]
), let us have two monochromatic lasers of the same frequency pointed at a 50:50 beam-splitter. Let the amplitudes of the lasers at the half-silvered mirror (the signals) be
E_{1}(t)=A_{1}e^{i\varphi_{1}+i\lambda t}
and
E_{2}(t)=A_{2}e^{i\varphi_{2}+i\lambda t}
.
Then the outgoing lasers would have amplitudes at the detectors

E_{3}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(E_{1}(t)+iE_{2}(t))
and

E_{4}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(iE_{1}(t)+E_{2}(t))
. The intensities (or the energies) would be:

I_{1}\ =\ |E_{1}(t)|^{2}\ =\ A_{1}^{2}

I_{2}\ =\ |E_{2}(t)|^{2}\ =\ A_{2}^{2}

I_{3}\ =\ |E_{3}(t)|^{2}\ =\ \frac{A_{1}^{2}+A_{2}^{2}}{2}+A_{1}A_{2}\sin(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})

I_{4}\ =\ |E_{4}(t)|^{2}\ =\ \frac{A_{1}^{2}+A_{2}^{2}}{2}-A_{1}A_{2}\sin(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})

Therefore

I_{3}-I_{4}\ =\ 2A_{1}A_{2}\sin(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})

or equivalently

\sin(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})\ =\ \frac{I_{3}-I_{4}}{2A_{1}A_{2}}

(81)

Next, by adding a
\lambda/4
shifter to the second laser, we end up with a modified

E_{2}^{\prime}(t)\ =\ A_{2}e^{i(\phi_{2}+\frac{\pi}{2}+\lambda t)}\ =\ iA_{2}e^{i\phi_{2}+i\lambda t}\ =\ iE_{2}(t)

This yields

E_{3}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(E_{1}(t)-E_{2}(t))\ \ \ E_{4}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(iE_{1}(t)+iE_{2}(t))

And thus

I_{3}^{\prime}\ =\ |E_{3}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}\ =\ \frac{A_{1}^{2}+A_{2}^{2}}{2}-A_{1}A_{2}\cos(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})

I_{4}^{\prime}\ =\ |E_{4}^{\prime}(t)|^{2}\ =\ \frac{A_{1}^{2}+A_{2}^{2}}{2}+A_{1}A_{2}\cos(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})

Thus

I_{3}^{\prime}-I_{4}^{\prime}\ =\ -2A_{1}A_{2}\cos(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})

or equivalently

\cos(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})\ =\ -\frac{I_{3}^{\prime}-I_{4}^{\prime}}{2A_{1}A_{2}}

(82)

When attempting to quantize this, we argue that since all
I_{i}
and
I_{i}^{\prime}
represent energies, we can take

I_{1}\sim H_{1}
and
I_{2}\sim H_{2}
, where
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are the Hamiltonians of two identical Harmonic oscillators, acting on two different isomorphic copies
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
of the standard Harmonic oscillator Hilbert space.

Let us promote
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
to operators representing the quantized version of the above classical experiment and denote them
\hat{E_{1}}
and
\hat{E_{2}}
, respectively. From the above equations, we see that
|\hat{E_{1}}|^{2}
,
|\hat{E_{2}}|^{2}
behave like
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
, and
|\hat{E_{3}}|^{2}
and
|\hat{E_{4}}|^{2}

i=3,4
behave like the resulting Hamiltonians
H_{3}
and
H_{4}
. There is an ambiguity whether to set
\hat{E_{i}}\hat{E_{i}}^{*}=H_{i}
or
\hat{E_{i}}^{*}\hat{E_{i}}=H_{i}
, for
i=1,2,3,4
. This is because, by using the creation and annihilation operators, we can write

2|\hat{E}_{i}|^{2}\ =\ a_{i}^{*}a_{i}+a_{i}a_{i}^{*}\ =\ 2H_{i},\ \ i=1,2.

(83)

However, for the definition of the quantized version
S_{12}
of
\sin(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})

there is no such ambiguity:

2\hat{E_{3}}\hat{E_{3}}^{*}\ =\ \hat{E_{1}}\hat{E_{1}}^{*}-i\hat{E_{1}}\hat{E_{2}}^{*}+i\hat{E_{2}}\hat{E_{1}}^{*}+\hat{E_{2}}\hat{E_{2}}^{*}

2\hat{E_{4}}\hat{E_{4}}^{*}\ =\ \hat{E_{1}}\hat{E_{1}}^{*}+i\hat{E_{1}}\hat{E_{2}}^{*}-i\hat{E_{2}}\hat{E_{1}}^{*}+\hat{E_{2}}\hat{E_{2}}^{*}

Therefore

2[\hat{E_{3}}\hat{E_{3}}^{*}-\hat{E_{4}}\hat{E_{4}}^{*}]\ =\ 2i[\hat{E_{2}}\hat{E_{1}}^{*}-\hat{E_{1}}\hat{E_{2}}^{*}]

On the other hand

2\hat{E_{3}}^{*}\hat{E_{3}}\ =\ \hat{E_{1}}^{*}\hat{E_{1}}+i\hat{E_{1}}^{*}\hat{E_{2}}-i\hat{E_{2}}^{*}\hat{E_{1}}+\hat{E_{2}}^{*}\hat{E_{2}}

2\hat{E_{4}}^{*}\hat{E_{4}}\ =\ \hat{E_{1}}^{*}\hat{E_{1}}-i\hat{E_{1}}^{*}\hat{E_{2}}+i\hat{E_{2}}^{*}\hat{E_{1}}+\hat{E_{2}}^{*}\hat{E_{2}}

Therefore, yet again

2[\hat{E_{3}}^{*}\hat{E_{3}}-\hat{E_{4}}^{*}\hat{E_{4}}]\ =\ 2i[\hat{E_{1}}^{*}\hat{E_{2}}-\hat{E_{2}}^{*}\hat{E_{1}}]

S_{12}\ \ "="\ \ \frac{\hat{E_{3}}^{*}\hat{E_{3}}-\hat{E_{4}}^{*}\hat{E_{4}}}{2\sqrt{H_{1}H_{2}}}\ =\ \frac{\hat{E_{3}}\hat{E_{3}}^{*}-\hat{E_{4}}\hat{E_{4}}^{*}}{2\sqrt{H_{1}H_{2}}}

"="\ \frac{i}{2}\left[\left(\frac{\hat{E_{1}}}{\sqrt{\hat{H_{1}}}}\right)\left(\frac{\hat{E_{2}}}{\sqrt{\hat{H_{2}}}}\right)^{*}\ -\ \left(\frac{\hat{E_{2}}}{\sqrt{\hat{H_{2}}}}\right)\left(\frac{\hat{E_{1}}}{\sqrt{\hat{H_{1}}}}\right)^{*}\right]\\

Proof.

Using the formula

(1+x)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\ =\ 1-\frac{x}{2}+\frac{3x^{2}}{8}+O(x^{3})

we obtain:

\frac{m+1}{4m}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{m+1/2}+\sqrt{m+3/2}}\right]^{2}\ =\ 1+\frac{3}{16m^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{m^{3}}\right)

\frac{n}{4}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-1/2}}\right]^{2}\ =\ 1+\frac{3}{16n^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{4}}\right)

Therefore

\left[\frac{(m+1)n}{16}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m+1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{m+3/2}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-1/2}}\right)^{2}-\right.\\
\left.\frac{m(n+1)}{16}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m+1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{m-1/2}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+3/2}}\right)^{2}\right]\ =\\
\left[1+\frac{3}{16m^{2}}-\frac{3}{8m^{3}}+O\left(\frac{1}{m^{4}}\right)\right]\left[1+\frac{3}{16n^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{4}}\right)\right]\ -\\
\left[1+\frac{3}{16m^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{m^{4}}\right)\right]\left[1+\frac{3}{16n^{2}}-\frac{3}{8n^{3}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{4}}\right)\right]\ =\\
-\frac{3}{8m^{3}}+\frac{3}{8n^{3}}+O\!\left(\frac{1}{m^{4}}\right)+O\!\left(\frac{1}{n^{4}}\right)+O\!\left(\frac{1}{m^{3}n^{2}}\right)+O\!\left(\frac{1}{m^{2}n^{3}}\right)

hence, (i).

Similarly,

\left[\frac{(m+1)n}{16}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m+1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{m+3/2}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-1/2}}\right)^{2}+\right.\\
\left.\frac{m(n+1)}{16}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m+1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{m-1/2}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1/2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+3/2}}\right)^{2}\right]\ =\\
\left[1+\frac{3}{16m^{2}}-\frac{3}{8m^{3}}+O\left(\frac{1}{m^{4}}\right)\right]\left[1+\frac{3}{16n^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{4}}\right)\right]\ +\\
\left[1+\frac{3}{16m^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{m^{4}}\right)\right]\left[1+\frac{3}{16n^{2}}-\frac{3}{8n^{3}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{4}}\right)\right]\ =\\
2+\frac{3}{8}\left(\frac{1}{m^{2}}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)-\frac{3}{8}\left(\frac{1}{m^{3}}+\frac{1}{n^{3}}\right)+\frac{9}{128}\frac{1}{m^{2}n^{2}}-\frac{9}{128}\left(\frac{1}{m^{3}n^{2}}+\frac{1}{m^{2}n^{3}}\right)+\\
O\!\left(\frac{1}{m^{4}}\right)+O\!\left(\frac{1}{n^{4}}\right)+O\!\left(\frac{1}{m^{3}n^{3}}\right)

Hence, (ii).
