Abstract
This report presents machine learning (ML) analysis of temperature-strain relationships for
structural health monitoring of nuclear reactor stainless steel (SS) pipes with the strain gauge
sensor directly printed on the pipe with a 3D conformal aerosol jet printer. We investigate
correlations for two sensor pairs installed on the same SS304 pipe: commercial K-type
thermocouple with a printed gold strain gauge (TC3-SG3), and commercial K-type thermocouple
with commercial Kyowa strain gauge (TC0-SG0). The temperature ranges for the sensor pairs
TC0-SG0 and TC3-SG3 are 20.00°C to 266.37°C and 39.95°C to 219.28°C respectively. ML
algorithms in this study include Linear Regression (baseline method), Ridge Regression, Lasso
Regression, and Gradient Boosting. Performance evaluation metrics include Root Mean Square
Error (RMSE), Mean Square Error (MSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), R² Score, and Explained
Variance. Using advanced feature engineering techniques, we extracted 27 temperature-based
features and 30 strategic inclusion features. The best performance was obtained with the Gradient
Boosting method, which achieves prediction accuracy of R² = 0.9999 and RMSE = 7.69 μStrain
for TC0-SG0, and R² = 0.9998 and RMSE = 18.03 μStrain for TC3-SG3. While the temperature-
strain correlations are weaker for the gauge directly printed on the pipe than for the commercial
strain gauge, deployment-ready performance exceeding industry standards is achieved for both
sensor pairs.

1. Introduction
1.1. Research Background and Motivation
The structural health monitoring (SHM) of nuclear reactor thermal hydraulic system pipes presents
a critical challenge to sustainability of existing light water reactors, and commercialization of
advanced reactors [1-6]. The focus of this work is on comparison of performance of a strain gauge
sensor directly printed on the pipe with a 3D conformal aerosol jet printer with that of a
conventional strain gauge [7,8]. Qualification of the printed strain gauge for nuclear reactor SHM
applications involves development of sensor monitoring methods. Traditional monitoring
approaches often fail to capture the intricate relationships between thermal loading and structural
strain response, leading to inadequate assessment of structural health and potential failure risks.
The development of advanced sensor technologies, coupled with machine learning capabilities,
offers new opportunities for SHM of nuclear systems [9-18].

1.2. Research Objectives
The primary objectives of this work include:
 • Comparison of performance of strain gauge sensor directly printed on the pipe with a 3D
 conformal aerosol jet printer with that of a conventional strain gauge
 • Development of a comprehensive machine learning framework for temperature-strain
 relationship analysis
 • Implementation of advanced feature engineering techniques to extract physics-informed
 features from sensor data
 • Evaluation and benchmarking of multiple machine learning algorithms for strain prediction
 accuracy
 • Establishment of a robust methodology for SHM and predictive maintenance of nuclear
 systems

1.3. Scope and Significance
This research addresses the critical gap in understanding complex temperature-strain relationships
through innovative machine learning approaches. The developed framework enables accurate
prediction of strain responses under varying temperature conditions for strain gauge sensor directly
printed on the pipe with a 3D conformal aerosol jet printer.

4. Advanced Feature Engineering Framework
4.1. Temperature Feature Engineering
Single temperature captures limited strain behavior, advanced feature engineering needs be
performed to enhance prediction accuracy and reduce overfitting. The research implements
sophisticated feature engineering extracting 27 temperature-based features per sensor dataset,
categorized into eight distinct groups:

4.1.1 Basic Polynomial Transformations
Three fundamental transformations capture nonlinear temperature effects:

 • Temperature squared (T²): Captures quadratic thermal expansion effects
 • Temperature cubed (T³): Models higher-order nonlinearities
 • Square root (√T): Represents diminishing thermal effects at high temperatures

4.1.2 Temperature Rolling Statistics
Multiple window sizes (5, 10, 20, 50 points) for temporal averaging:

Rolling Mean Features:

 • Smooths measurement noise while preserving thermal trends
 • Captures thermal mass effects and heat capacity dynamics
 • Distinguishes between short-term fluctuations and long-term trends
Rolling Standard Deviation Features:

 • Quantifies temperature variability within windows
 • Low values indicate stable heating conditions
 • High values suggest fluctuating thermal conditions
Physical Significance:
Materials exhibit thermal inertia resisting rapid temperature changes. Different window sizes
capture multi-scale heat transfer mechanisms:

 • 5-point windows: Convective heat transfer effects
 • 10-point windows: Conductive heat transfer through material
 • 20-point windows: Radiative heat transfer and ambient effects
 • 50-point windows: Thermal equilibrium and environmental cycles

 10

Rolling statistics help ML models distinguish between short-term fluctuations and long-term
thermal trends.

4.1.3 Temperature Rate of Change Features
Dynamic thermal features capturing heating/cooling rates:

 • First derivative (diff): Rate of thermal expansion (dT/dt)
 • Second derivative (diff_2): Thermal acceleration indicating rate changes
 • Gradient: Smooth estimate of instantaneous thermal rate
Physical Interpretation:
Rapid temperature changes create thermal stress gradients in materials. Different heating/cooling
rates produce distinct strain patterns, enabling ML models to distinguish between gradual
thermal expansion and thermal shock conditions.

4.1.4 Temperature Lag Features
Temporal dependencies for lag values:

 • Captures thermal memory effects
 • Models thermal diffusion delays through structure
 • Represents material inertia in thermal response
Physical Basis:
Current strain depends on thermal history: ε(t) = f(T_now, T_1min_ago, T_3min_ago,
T_10min_ago). This captures realistic thermal diffusion through pipe structures where thermal
expansion isn't instantaneous. ML models learn optimal time windows for thermal-strain coupling.

4.1.5 Cumulative Temperature Features
Long-term thermal accumulation effects:

 • Cumulative sum: Total thermal loading (ΣT(i))
 • Cumulative maximum: Peak temperature reached
 • Cumulative minimum: Minimum temperature experienced
These features represent integrated thermal loading over time, capturing long-term thermal effects
on structural behavior and thermal history dependence of material properties. ML models can learn
cumulative damage patterns.

4.1.6 Temperature-Time Interaction
Non-linear interaction between thermal level and temporal position:

 11

 • Models Arrhenius-type thermal aging: k = A × exp(-Ea/RT) [19]
 • Captures time-temperature coupled phenomena
 • Represents evolving thermal sensitivity
Reaction rates depend on T×t. Thermal oxidation, creep, and fatigue are time-temperature coupled,
Early measurements and late measurements may show different T-strain relationships, ML models
can capture time-evolving thermal sensitivity.

4.1.7 Physics-Informed Thermal Expansion Features
Advanced nonlinear thermal modeling:

 • Thermal expansion potential = T × ΔT = T × (T - Tmin) [20]
 • Captures nonlinear thermal expansion: ε_thermal = α₁×ΔT + α₂×ΔT² + α₃×ΔT³
 • Models temperature-dependent expansion coefficients
Linear term: α ₁ ×ΔT (standard thermal expansion), Nonlinear term: α ₂ ×ΔT² ≈ captured by TΔT
feature, ML can capture nonlinear thermal-strain relationships beyond simple α×ΔT.

4.1.8 Temperature Normalization Features
Statistical scaling for ML optimization:

 • Min-max normalization: Scales to range
 • Z-score standardization: Centers at mean=0, scales to std=1
 • Ensures numerical stability and equal feature weighting
Scale invariance: ML algorithms perform better with normalized inputs.

5. Machine Learning Model Development
5.1. Training and Testing Dataset Preparation
Systematic dataset preparation ensures robust model training:

Dataset Configuration:

 • First 10,000 chronological samples selected
 • Includes complete dynamic thermal cycling
 • Reduces redundant idle samples
 • 80/20 random split for training/testing
 • 27 temperature features for initial models
TC0-SG0 Dataset:

 • Training set: 7,960 samples × 27 features
 • Testing set: 1,991 samples × 27 features
 • Temperature range maintained across splits
 •   Training temp range: 20.00°C to 266.37°C; Testing temp range: 20.00°C to 265.91°C
TC3-SG3 Dataset:

 • Training set: 7,960 samples × 27 features
 • Testing set: 1,991 samples × 27 features
 • Full temperature range preserved
 •   Training temp range: 40.18°C to 219.28°C; Testing temp range: 40.18°C to 218.12°C

5.2. Machine Learning Algorithms
Four different algorithms were implemented and evaluated:

5.2.1 Linear Regression
Linear regression is a statistical method modeling linear relationships between temperature
features and strain. The method works by finding the straight "best-fit" line through data points,
which allows for the prediction of the dependent variable's value based on a given independent
variable's value [21]. In this work, linear regression is used to establish the baseline performance
for comparison with other algorithms described in the subsequent sections.

 15

5.2.2 Ridge Regression
Ridge regression is a statistical technique used to create more stable and reliable linear models by
addressing the problems of multicollinearity and overfitting [22]. It works by adding an L2
regularization penalty to the standard Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression formula, which
shrinks the model's coefficients toward zero.

5.2.3 Lasso Regression
Lasso (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) regression is a regularization technique
in machine learning that shrinks model coefficients to zero, effectively performing feature
selection by creating simpler, more interpretable models and preventing overfitting in linear
regression models [23].

5.2.4 Gradient Boosting
Gradient boosting is a machine learning technique for both regression and classification tasks that
sequentially builds an ensemble of weak prediction models (like decision trees) to create a strong,
accurate predictive model. The method works by fitting new models to the residuals (errors) of the
previous models, iteratively improving the overall prediction by using a gradient descent procedure
to minimize a differentiable loss function [24].

5.3. Evaluation Metrics
Comprehensive evaluation using multiple metrics:

 • RMSE (Root Mean Square Error): Square root of MSE in original units (μStrain)
 • MSE (Mean Square Error): Average squared prediction errors (μStrain²)
 • MAE (Mean Absolute Error): Average absolute differences (μStrain)
 • R² Score: Proportion of variance explained (0 to 1)
 • Explained Variance: Variance proportion explained by model

6.2. Strategic Inclusion Features Enhancement
Advanced feature engineering incorporating strategic strain features further improves
performance, while preventing data leakage through careful feature engineering, to further enhance
prediction accuracy and reduce overfitting. Based on max correlation with strain, 30 features
automatically designated from ML algorithms

6.2.1 Additional Strain Features
Besides temperature features previously used, 10 strain features were added:

Lagged Strain Features:

 •   Past strain values [lag 1, 2, 3, 5, 10]
 • Captures structural memory effects
 • Prevents data leakage using only historical information
Strain Dynamics Features:

 • First and second derivatives (diff, diff_2)
 • Gradient for smooth rate estimation
 • Captures strain rate-dependent behavior with rate features from past data
Strain Rolling Statistics:

 • 5-point rolling mean and standard deviation with memory effects from past

 19

 • Represents local structural response
 • Filters measurement noise
Physics-Informed Interaction Features:

 • Temperature-strain rate coupling features (use past information)
 • Thermal loading rate (heating rate × past strain)
 • Models thermomechanical interactions

6.2.2 Feature Selection Methodology
Machine learning algorithms select top 30 features based on maximum correlation with strain
using f_regression scoring [25]. The ML works by applying a feature selection method to select
the top 30 features from each sensor dataset based on statistical tests. It evaluates each feature
independently based on its relationship with the target variable using f_regression scoring function,
computes the linear correlation between each individual feature and the target variable of Strain.

ML algorithms Inputs: All 27 temperature features along with 10 added strain features and physics-
informed interaction features are used. ML algorithms Outputs: 30 strategic inclusion features
selected based max correlation with Strain.

7. Conclusions
 Results presented in this report have established a machine learning (ML) framework for
analyzing temperature-strain relationships in structural health monitoring (SHM) of nuclear
reactor stainless steel (SS) pipes with the strain gauge sensor directly printed on the pipe with a
3D conformal aerosol jet printer. We investigate correlations for two sensor pairs installed on the
same SS304 pipe: commercial K-type thermocouple with a printed gold strain gauge (TC3-SG3),
and commercial K-type thermocouple with commercial Kyowa strain gauge (TC0-SG0). The
temperature ranges for the sensor pairs TC0-SG0 and TC3-SG3 are 20.00°C to 266.37°C and
39.95°C to 219.28°C respectively. ML algorithms in this study include Linear Regression
(baseline method), Ridge Regression, Lasso Regression, and Gradient Boosting. Performance
evaluation metrics include Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Mean Square Error (MSE), Mean
Absolute Error (MAE), R² Score, and Explained Variance.
 Through advanced feature engineering, the study successfully extracted 27 temperature-based
features and 30 strategic inclusion features, capturing comprehensive thermomechanical
phenomena across multiple scales. The best performance was obtained with the Gradient Boosting
method, which achieves prediction accuracy of R² = 0.9999 and RMSE = 7.69 μStrain for TC0-
SG0, and R² = 0.9998 and RMSE = 18.03 μStrain for TC3-SG3. The Gradient Boosting algorithm
consistently outperformed traditional methods, effectively modeling complex nonlinear
relationships, feature interactions, and hierarchical decision structures inherent in the temperature-
strain coupling mechanisms. The multi-scale analysis framework captured phenomena across
temporal windows ranging from local (5-point) to global (50-point) scales, enabling
comprehensive characterization of thermal expansion, dynamic loading effects, and thermal
memory responses. The physics-informed approach successfully integrated domain knowledge
with machine learning capabilities, achieving the critical benchmark of RMSE < 20 μStrain with
R² > 0.999 for industrial applications. While the temperature-strain correlations are weaker for the
gauge directly printed on the pipe than for the commercial strain gauge, deployment-ready
performance exceeding industry standards is achieved for both sensor pairs (RMSE < 20 μStrain,
R² > 0.999).
