Abstract
Electrical potting is an important process to protect semiconductor devices from damaging

external elements (mechanical stress, moisture, high voltage, etc.) and provides structural

support for delicate devices for electrical connectivity to a larger platform. This internship will

focus on the development of a universal potting jig for photoconductive semiconductor switches

(PCSS). The goal is to design a reliable compression jig that is cross compatible with various PCSS

designs that allow for easy potting of devices with a high dielectric breakdown strength material.

The challenge with the current potting jig is that the design is only compatible with one style of

PCSS and applies uneven pressure that can lead to leakage of potting material. Our approach is

to move from a design that applies pressure by binder clip to utilizing a micrometer based

compressing jig for a reliable packaging process that can pot a variety of devices without having

to have a unique compressing jig for each of style of PCSS. Possible roadblocks with the new

design include epoxy leakage into the optical path and misalignment of the electrode – metal

contact bond.

Description of Current Potting Process
Figure 1 shows the current packaging design of Photoconductive Semiconductor Switches (PCSS).

The bonding area on the top and back Ni/Au plated copper electrodes are bonded to the Cr/Au

contacts on the semiconductor substrate, as seen in Figure 1. Previous efforts replaced mold

injection potting with a dielectric PEEK ring to allow for open injection of the EP30 epoxy material

to encapsulate the bonding interfaces of the PCSS device to provide structural support to the

delicate structure.

Challenges with Current Potting Process
The compression jig in Figure 2 was useful in proving the viability of the PEEK fill method over the

mold style potting, however, it is not compatible with all electrode designs and only works for a

singular style of electrodes with a specific height. Figure 3 shows the compression jig does not

The compression jig uses binder clips to provide pressure from the rod to the backside electrode,

which can lead to varying pressures on the bond due to the non-uniform clamping forces of the

binder clips. This can lead to epoxy leakage into the optical path that can electrically disconnect

the top/back electrodes from the semiconductor substrate and kill the device if enough epoxy

leaks through, as seen in Figure 4.

The alignment of the Cr/Au back/front electrodes to the rings on the semiconductor substrate is

also a critical feature of packaging. Figure 5 shows current bonding areas have ring thickness that

vary from 250-700um, so misalignment must be avoided to ensure good electrical connectivity of

the substrate to the Ni/Au plated copper electrodes.

 Figure 5: Outer and Inner dimension of Cr/Al contacts on switch.

There are new electrode designs that have incorporated the PEEK style bonding that require a

compression jig for potting without epoxy leakage into the optical path or shifting of the electrode

– to – ring bonding alignment.

Efforts to Address Potting Issues

To address these issues, a new height adjustable compression jig design was created to

accommodate current and future electrode designs, as seen in Figure 6. The new compression jig

design utilizes a mechanical micrometer head mounted to an aluminum stand. The spindle has a

diameter of 5.08mm that closely matches the 5mm outer diameter of the backside electrode.

The electrode sits in a hollow on the aluminum stand. The rotating spherical tip spindle was

aligned to the backside contact to provide compression. The rotating micrometer spindle was

selected for initial testing due to a lower cost compared to a non-rotating mechanical micrometer.

Test 1
An initial test with the first iteration of the compression jig was tested with the shorter PEEK ring

style electrode. No real devices were utilized for testing; instead, dummy switches with Cr/Au

metal rings were fabricated from low-cost borosilicate glass coverslips to mimic the real

semiconductor. Figure 7 shows the Cr/Al metal contact is misaligned by 185um from the backside

contact and epoxy leakage is seen in the optical path of the frontside electrode.

The misalignment of the Cr/Al ring to electrode was suspected to occur due to the spherical

nature of the spindle head rotating down and twisting the backside contact, causing it to shift

slightly. The electrode, unlike the previous compressing jig design, sat on a hollow that did not

provide a hard bracing surface, presumably leading to the EP30 leakage.

Test 2

A second test was run to verify that the rotating micrometer spindle head was causing

misalignment issues seen in Figure 7. The smaller electrode was placed on a flat aluminum plate

to add a bracing surface to improve compression by the spindle head on the bonding area. Figure

8 shows the flat bracing plate improved the compression of the jig on the backside contact, but

the (b) misalignment of the backside contact to the Cr/Al grid on the substrate was still present.

Test 3

To address the misalignment issues with the ball tip rotating micrometer spindle and reduce cost

of components, a new compression jig was re-designed with a flat non-rotating spindle and off

the shelf components. The jig utilizes a 2x2 inch breadboard base with pre-machined holes, 2

optical threaders, and a micrometer head mount that holds the non-rotating spindle. The

modular design in Figure 9 allows for easy adaptation to new electrode styles without having to

machine a new base. An adapter head was designed to reduce the surface area of the

compression rod from 5mm to 3.5mm to prevent epoxy from bonding the adapter head to the

electrode, as seen in Figure 9 (b). The adaptor micrometer body has a 7.5mm outer diameter that

allows the PEEK ring to be slipped over for aligning. The part was placed on the bread board stand

and hand aligned to the adapter head for compression.

Testing of the updated non-rotating compression jig was successful. There was no misalignment

of the Ni/Au plated electrodes to the Cr/Au rings or epoxy leakage into the optical path. The final

design for the compression jig will include interchangeable metal alignment plates that can be

screwed to the bread board base and will concentrically align the backside contact to the adapter

head to make alignment easy.

Conclusion

A new compression jig for potting PCSS devices for high voltage operation was designed to replace

the previous compression jig design. The new design replaced the standard jig with a compression

jig that utilizes a micrometer head to apply pressure on the electrode assembly and allow for

stable injection of the EP30 epoxy to electrically pot the PCSS devices. The micrometer jig was

successfully tested, and the process was repeated with a real substrate, proving the universal

compression jig can be used to pot real devices with no misalignment and minimal EP30 leakage

into the optical path. The new compression jig will replace the original binder clip aluminum

potting jig and provide a reliable and high-output method for potting the current devices and

allow a pathway for potting non-standard PCSS in the future.
