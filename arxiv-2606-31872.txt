Hyperformalism for Relevant Modal Logics
Thomas Macaulay Ferguson, Shay Allen Logan

Abstract
The property of hyperformalism has proven to be a powerful tool in the analysis of relevant logics, revealing that increasingly weak relevant logics are closed under increasingly strong classes of non-uniform substitutions. In such substitutions, two instances of the same atom may be treated independently in virtue of syntactic features of their appearances in a complex. In this work, we extend the scope of hyperformalism to relevant modal logics by considering MPos -hyperformalism, that is, a property in which relevant modal logics are closed under substitutions in which nesting within the scope of modal operators is taken into account. We prove that the weak relevant modal logic 𝖡 □ \mathsf{B}^{\Box} is MPos -hyperformal and investigate the classes of non-uniform substitutions under which several extensions are closed. We then consider corresponding refinements of the variable sharing property that hold of such logics. We conclude by introducing a modal logic 𝖪 𝖬𝖯𝗈𝗌 \mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}} that constitutes the largest MPos -hyperformal sublogic of the classical modal logic 𝖪 \mathsf{K} and provide soundness and completeness results.

Introduction

In typical formal logics, atomic formulas are treated as being entirely independent. This is (at least partly, and maybe wholly) captured by their being closed under uniform substitutions, since closure under uniform substitutions means that one can safely replace occurrences of distinct atoms with distinct formulas without needing to worry about in any way coordinating these substitutions.

Hyperformal
logics take the notion of independence captured by uniform substitutions and run with it. More concretely (but still abstractly) a logic is hyperformal when occurrences of the same atom in sufficiently different syntactic contexts can be treated as independent. As with uniform substitutions and independence for atoms, the independence enforced by hyperformalism is made concrete in a logic
\mathsf{L}
by the requirement that
\mathsf{L}
-theoremhood be preserved under non-uniform substitutions treating such instances independently.
E.g.
,
[
11
]
shows that the relevant logic
\mathsf{DR}
is closed under substitutions that treat atoms occurring in the scope of different numbers of conditionals—which is to say, that occur at different
depths
—as independent. For example: in the formula
p\rightarrow(q\rightarrow p)
, the two instances of the atom
p
appear at different depths and are thus are treated as independent,
i.e.
, as though the they were distinct atoms. Consequently, the formula
p\rightarrow(q\rightarrow r)
follows from a depth substitution. That the latter formula lacks any variable common to its antecedent and consequent ensures that it is not a theorem of
\mathsf{DR}
, whence
p\rightarrow(q\rightarrow p)
is not a theorem. Increasingly fine-grained species of hyperformalism have since been introduced (see
e.g.

[
6
,
22
]
)---along with corresponding fine-grained variable sharing properties---proving hyperformalism to be a powerful tool in the analysis of relevant logics.

2.2
The Base System

As promised, we begin by examining
\mathsf{B}^{\Box}
. Given the preceding definitions, we can quickly demonstrate the main result concerning
\mathsf{B}^{\Box}:

Theorem 2.1
.

\mathsf{B}^{\Box}
is
\mathsf{MPos}
-hyperformal.

Proof.

By induction on derivations. The details for the axioms and rules of
\mathsf{B}
follow from arguments virtually identical to those provided for Theorems 2 and 3 of
[
22
]
, so we consider the axiom C and rule RM.

For C, we must show that every substitution applied to an instance of this axiom is itself provable. For an arbitrary substitution
\sigma
, some manipulation reveals that
\sigma_{\varepsilon}((\Box A\wedge\Box B)\rightarrow\Box(A\wedge B))
is
(\Box\sigma_{\mathtt{bc}}(A)\wedge\Box\sigma_{\mathtt{bc}}(B))\rightarrow\Box(\sigma_{\mathtt{bc}}(A)\wedge\sigma_{\mathtt{bc}}(B))
. But this is another instance of C, so provable.

For RM, we must show that for an arbitrary substitution
\sigma
that if
\Box A\rightarrow\Box B
is a theorem due to an application of RM, then
\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\Box A\rightarrow\Box B)
is a theorem as well. So suppose that
\Box A\rightarrow\Box B
is a theorem of
\mathsf{B}^{\Box}
yielded by the application of RM to an earlier theorem
A\rightarrow B
. Fix an arbitrary
\sigma
and define an further substitution
\sigma^{\star}
by the following scheme:

\sigma^{\star}_{\mathtt{\overline{x}}}(p)=\begin{cases}\sigma_{\mathtt{\overline{y}bc}}(p)&\mbox{ if }\mathtt{\overline{x}}=\mathtt{\overline{y}c}\\
\sigma_{\mathtt{\overline{x}}}(p)&\mbox{ otherwise}\end{cases}

Clearly,
\sigma^{\star}
is faithful and is thus itself a substitution. Moreover, a trivial induction shows that
\sigma^{*}_{\mathtt{\overline{x}}}(A)=\sigma_{\mathtt{\overline{y}bc}}(A)
when
\mathtt{\overline{x}=\overline{y}c}
for arbitrary (not necessarily atomic) formulas
A
. Consequently, by induction hypothesis,
\sigma^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(A\rightarrow B)
is a theorem, whence
\sigma^{\star}_{\mathtt{c}}(A)\rightarrow\sigma^{\star}_{\mathtt{c}}(B)
is a theorem, whence
\sigma_{\mathtt{bc}}(A)\rightarrow\sigma_{\mathtt{bc}}(B)
is a theorem. Take a derivation of this formula and apply RM to yield
\Box\sigma_{\mathtt{bc}}(A)\rightarrow\Box\sigma_{\mathtt{bc}}(B)
. With some manipulation, this establishes theoremhood of
\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\Box A\rightarrow\Box B)
; as
\sigma
was arbitrary, we infer that RM preserves hyperformalism.
∎

Now, we can turn to the matter of the types of variable sharing properties that are enjoyed by
\mathsf{B}^{\Box}
. As in
[

6

22
]

\mathsf{B}^{\Box}
enjoys very strong versions of the traditional hallmark of relevant logics. First, consider some definitions and lemmas:

Definition 2.2
.

For two positions
\mathtt{\overline{x}}
and
\mathtt{\overline{y}}
, say that
\mathtt{\overline{x}}\sim_{\mathtt{nn}}\mathtt{\overline{y}}
if one can produce one from the other by the addition or deletion of instances of the string
\mathtt{nn}
. Say that a substitution
\sigma
is
atomic
in case its range is
\mathsf{At}
and
injective up to faithfulness
in case
\sigma_{\mathtt{\overline{x}}}(A)=\sigma_{\mathtt{\overline{y}}}(B)
holds if and only if
\mathtt{\overline{x}}\sim_{\mathtt{nn}}\mathtt{\overline{y}}
and
A=B
.

The “faithfulness” of the equivalence relation
\sim_{\mathtt{nn}}
rests in its assurance that axioms reflecting double negation introduction and elimination are preserved. In considering the results for modal logics based on weak relevant logics in which axioms like
A\rightarrow\neg\neg A

2.1

\sigma_{\overline{\mathtt{x}}\mathtt{nn}\overline{\mathtt{y}}}(p)=\sigma_{\overline{\mathtt{xy}}}(p)

For a formula
A
and instance of a subformula
B

A

in

\mathsf{pos}(A[B])

\mathsf{pos}(\Box(p\rightarrow\Box q)[q])=\mathtt{brb}

We will first state the strongest
\mathsf{MPos}
-based variable sharing property available in the case of
\mathsf{B}^{\Box}

Definition 2.3
.

A logic in the language
\mathbf{L}
enjoys the
\mathsf{MPos}
-variable sharing property in case whenever

is provable, there are
A[p]
in

and

\mathsf{pos}(A[p]\rightarrow B)\sim_{\mathtt{nn}}\mathsf{pos}(A\rightarrow B[p])

To prove this, we first observe the following:

Lemma 2.4
.

\mathsf{B}^{\Box}
has the variable sharing property.

Proof.

This follows from Theorem 4.4 of
[

∎

.

\mathsf{B}^{\Box}

Proof.

Suppose that

is a theorem of
\mathsf{B}^{\Box}
. Choose a function

. Define the

Theorem 2.7

By induction on derivations. Appealing to Theorem

For T, suppose
\mathrm{T}\in S

\sigma_{[\varepsilon]_{S}}(\Box A\to A)=\Box\sigma_{[\mathtt{bc}]_{S}}(A)\to\sigma_{[\mathtt{c}]_{S}}(A)

\Box\sigma_{[\mathtt{bc}]_{S}}(A)\to\sigma_{[\mathtt{c}]_{S}}(A)

For 4, suppose that
\mathrm{4}\in S
. We show that
\sigma_{[\varepsilon]_{S}}(\Box A\to\Box\Box A)

\Box\sigma_{[\mathtt{bc}]_{S}}(A)\to\Box\Box\sigma_{[\mathtt{bbc}]_{S}}(A)

\mathtt{bc}\sim_{4}\mathtt{bbc}

\sigma_{[\mathtt{bc}]_{S}}(A)=\sigma_{[\mathtt{bbc}]_{S}}(A)

For K, suppose
\mathrm{K}\in S
. We compute that
\sigma_{\mathtt{[\varepsilon]}_{S}}(\Box(A\to B)\to(\Box A\to\Box B))

\Box(\sigma_{\mathtt{[lbc]}_{S}}(A)\to\sigma_{\mathtt{[rbc]}_{S}}(B))\to(\Box\sigma_{\mathtt{[blc]}_{S}}(A)\to\Box\sigma_{\mathtt{[brc]}_{S}}(B)).

Now observe that since

Lemma 3.4

Let

Suppose

4
Future Work and Concluding Remarks

We’ve characterized
\mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}}
semantically and via a tableaux system. To better understand it, one would like an axiom system.
9
9

9

Thanks to a referee for pushing us on this point.
But to date logics formed by restricting known logics to their largest hyperformal (in some sense) fragments have resisted axiomatization. The only thing that
is
known on this front is the rather unsatisfying result proved in
[
10
]
: for logics like these
admit
recursive axiomatizations. Producing intelligible axiomatizations remains, in spite of this, as an outstanding project.

\mathsf{B}^{\Box}
is
\mathsf{MPos}
-hyperformal and we can use this to extract a rather shockingly strong variable sharing result for its theorems. But it’s not the strongest logic with these features, nor is it the largest subset of classical logic with these features.
\mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}}
holds that honor.

Perhaps there are very good reasons for preferring
\mathsf{B}^{\Box}
to
\mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}}
. If so, they ought to be enunciated, and we now know some things they
can’t
be: they can’t be related to variable sharing or subclassicality. And one must admit that semantically,
\mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}}
definitely has advantages over
\mathsf{B}^{\Box}
. Also note that since
\mathsf{B}^{\Box}
is subclassical and closed under
\mathsf{MPos}
-substitutions, it is contained (properly as it turns out, though we leave it to the reader to verify this) in
\mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}}
. So anything you might want to do with a logic like
\mathsf{B}^{\Box}
, you can also do with
\mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}}
.

Another thing worth noting: “relevant” logics defined by pasting a simple filter on top of classical logic (a ‘syntactic sieve’, as pejoratively described by Richard Sylvan in
[
18
]
), can be easily seen to incur nontransitivity in the sense that they will, for some
A
,
B
, and
C
, include
A\to B
and
B\to C
without including
A\to C
. Were this true for
\mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}}
, it would be a fairly serious reason to set it aside. But while
\mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}}

is
the largest sublogic of
\mathsf{K}
with a given feature, and it is
a
sublogic of
\mathsf{K}
with the
\mathsf{MPos}
-variable sharing property,
\mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}}
is
not
the largest sublogic of
\mathsf{K}
with the
\mathsf{MPos}
-variable sharing property.

As an example, consider the contraction axiom:
(p\to(p\to q))\to(p\to q)
. The leftmost
p
and the
p
antecedent of the terminal conditional both occur at the position
\mathsf{lc}
. So this formula
does
have the
\mathsf{MPos}
-variable sharing property. But it isn’t in
\mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}}
because
\mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}}
isn’t defined by its possessing a given variable sharing property but by its possessing a given closure property—namely, closure under
\mathsf{MPos}
-substitutions. Since not all
\mathsf{MPos}
-substitutions map contraction to a theorem of classical logic, contraction isn’t a member of
\mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}}
.

So
\mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}}
isn’t plausibly characterized as
\mathsf{K}
with a syntactic sieve attached, and thus isn’t subject to any of the usual criticisms that attach to such logics. And there’s more: not only can’t it be criticized on those fronts, it also doesn’t
suffer
on those fronts. In particular, we can show the following:

Theorem 4.1
.

\mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}}
is transitive.

Proof.

Suppose
\sigma_{\varepsilon}(A\to C)=0
. Then
\sigma_{\mathsf{c}}(A)=1
and
\sigma_{\mathsf{c}}(C)=0
. Now observe that if
\sigma_{\mathsf{c}}(B)=1
, then
\sigma_{\varepsilon}(B\to C)=0
and if
\sigma_{\mathsf{c}}(B)=0
, then
\sigma_{\varepsilon}(A\to B)=0
. So if
A\to C\not\in\mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}}
, then either
A\to B\not\in\mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}}
or
B\to C\not\in\mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}}
. Contraposing, if
A\to B\in\mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}}
and
B\to C\in\mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}}
, then
A\to C\in\mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}}
.
∎

This observation, then, cuts against a charge of artificiality in the formulation of
\mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}}
insofar as the pathologies that follow from truly artificial approaches to relevance—like syntactic sieves—do not haunt the present system.

All told, we would like it if this paper was understood as a challenge. Those who would prefer ‘traditional’ relevant modal logics are being asked to give reasons for that preference. Absent such, we’d recommend that folks in need of relevant modal logics look to logics like
\mathsf{K}^{\mathsf{MPos}}
rather than to logics like
\mathsf{B}^{\Box}
.
