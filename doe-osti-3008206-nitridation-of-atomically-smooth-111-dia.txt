Public Abstract
Diamond is a material with a wide band gap that can host a variety of isolated paramagnetic defects, called
“color centers”1. These color centers have attractive optical and magnetic properties suitable for quantum
applications including quantum computing, nanophotonics and quantum sensing. The most common one is
the negatively charged nitrogen vacancy (NV) color center. For quantum sensing, it is desirable to reduce
the distance between the NV center and the analyte by using shallow color centers in order to increase
sensitivity. In this context, the diamond surface termination is especially critical. The appropriate surface
termination is required to stabilize the color centers and mitigate surface magnetic noise. The nitrogen
termination of diamond has been postulated as highly desirable for the NV color center2,3.
The goal of this project is to take advantage of an electron beam-generated ExB low temperature plasma
developed by the Princeton Collaborative Low Temperature Plasma Research Facility (PCRF) at the
Princeton Plasma Physics Laboratory (PPPL) to nitridate the surface of (100) diamond single crystals with
minimal surface damage. In this reactor, the use of a magnetized plasma enables gentle processing of
materials sensitive to ion damage. This is in strong contrast to radiofrequency plasma processing reactors
which are known to etch and sputter the surface. XPS measurements indicate the incorporation of nitrogen
and oxygen atoms at the surface in similar amounts. XAS measurements confirm the nitridation and indicate
that the nitrogen termination is different from the nitrogen termination obtained using radiofrequency
plasma treatment4. The combination of these results indicates the formation of amid species at the (100)
diamond surface that are promising for the stabilization of NV centers for quantum sensing.

We have successfully determined conditions with significant nitridation as indicated by a N 1s peak, but
also observed a significant O 1s peak (Figure 2, left), using either H- or O-terminated diamond substrates.

The surface atom quantification derived from the XPS measurements indicates a high atomic coverage of
40-50% for both O and N atoms, which means that the combined coverage of the (100) diamond surface is
close to complete.

We hypothesized that the presence of oxygen termination could arise from reactive surface carbon radicals
that are quenched by atmospheric oxygen upon air exposure during sample removal from the reactor. To
mitigate this issue, we treated nitridated diamonds either with a flow of hydrogen gas or with a low-
temperature hydrogen plasma. In both cases, the XPS spectrum shows a similar amount of O atoms on the
diamond surface, which refutes this hypothesis. The oxygen incorporation could be coming from remaining
oxygen/moisture traces on the reactor surface and gas tubing. We are currently upgrading our setup with
materials that do not trap oxygen and adding baking capacity to the reactor to evacuate any traces of oxygen
more efficiently in an effort to produce a nitrogen-only surface termination.
Upon increasing the counter-cathode voltage, we observed that a shoulder grows in the XPS C 1s peak at
low binding energies (green in Figure 2, right compared to middle), which is characteristic of the formation
of sp2 carbon atoms at the surface of the diamond. This peak is diagnostic of the surface damage that occurs
when the energy of the plasma particles increases.

Figure 3: Left – N K-edge XAS spectra of a (100) diamond nitridated under mild conditions with the low-temperature plasma.
Middle – N K-edge XAS spectra of a (100) diamond nitridated with the radiofrequency plasma (from Stacey et al. Adv. Mater.
Interfaces 2015, 2, 1500079). Right – Atomic structure of the N-N dimer and N-C(H) terminations.

To complement the XPS measurements, we measured X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) at the N K-
edge which provides detailed insight into the local geometric and electronic structure of nitrogen species.
Figure 3 shows the N K-edge XAS spectrum of (100) diamonds nitridated with the low-temperature plasma
(left) and with a conventional radiofrequency plasma (middle). A peak is observed at 399 eV which was
previously assigned to the transition into the σ*N-C orbital of N-C(H) terminated (100) diamond surface

(right), while radiofrequency plasma treatment produces terminal N-N dimers as indicated by the low
energy transition into the low-lying σ*N-N orbital4.
The combination of these XPS and XAS measurements are consistent with the presence of amide surface
species. We are currently in the process of further confirming this assignment with vibrational spectroscopic
methods.
Summary
In conclusion, this study shows that a low-temperature plasma is suitable for nitridation of diamond single
crystal. The resulting surface has a mixed nitrogen and oxygen termination that we preliminarily assign as
a new amide termination. Subsequent steps will consist in the implantation of shallow NV centers in a (100)
diamond single crystal and terminate the surface with this new nitridation process, in order to investigate
the impact of this new termination on the NV center photophysical properties, while atomic force
microscopy measurements will probe the extent of surface damage. We will also pursue the nitridation of
(111)-terminated single crystals and investigate how the surface termination changes.
