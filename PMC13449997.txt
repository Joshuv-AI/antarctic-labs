Pragmatic Assessment of VA-ECMO Decannulation Readiness After Extracorporeal Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation for Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest
Authors: Garima Dahiya, Laith Alhuneafat, Jason Bartos, Steffen Ventz, Daniel Gonzalez, Meagan Nowariak, Guido Tavazzi, Ziou Jiang, Gregory Mello, Jalal Agakishi, Natalie Hunninghake, Alejandra Gutierrez Bernal, Demetris Yannopoulos, Andrea Elliott
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundReadiness for venoarterial-extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) decannulation after extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) for out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) remains poorly defined.ObjectivesThe objective of the study was to identify bedside clinical variables associated with successful decannulation in OHCA patients treated with ECPR.MethodsConsecutive OHCA patients treated under the Minnesota Resuscitation Consortium ECPR protocol from December 2015 to March 2023 were analyzed. Hemodynamic, vasoactive medication, echocardiographic, and mechanical circulatory support variables were compared between successful and failed decannulation. Feature importance analysis was conducted using Bayesian additive regression trees and multivariable logistic regression.ResultsAmong 390 OHCA ECPR patients, 125 underwent VA-ECMO decannulation; 110 (88%) were successful. Failed decannulations were less likely to have been witnessed arrests (73% vs 100%; P = 0.04) and had lower rates of bystander CPR (36.4% vs 68.6%; P = 0.05). On weaning readiness assessment, left ventricular ejection fraction at lowest VA-ECMO flow (OR: 0.96; 95% CI: 0.92-0.99; P = 0.024), arterial pulsatility (OR: 0.95; 95% CI: 0.90-0.99; P = 0.027), Vasoactive inotrope score (OR: 1.16; 95%-CI: 1.04-1.32; P = 0.013) and days on VA-ECMO support (OR: 1.40; 95%-CI: 1.13-1.79; P = 0.003) were significantly associated with decannulation success. A Bayesian additive regression trees model incorporating left ventricular ejection fraction, pulsatility, vasoactive-inotrope score, predecannulation VA-ECMO flow, and concomitant intra-aortic balloon pump support predicted success with an area under the curve of 0.91.ConclusionsOur study identifies several pragmatic variables, noninvasive and accessible at the bedside that are predictive of successful decannulation among OHCA patients undergoing ECPR. Prospective validation of a composite decannulation prediction score incorporating these variables is warranted to aid decision-making.

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) remains a leading cause of deth worldwide, with survival rates remaining poor despite advances in resuscitation and postarrest care.1,2 Extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) (ECPR) has emerged as an important therapeutic strategy for patients with refractory OHCA who fail to achieve return of spontaneous circulation through conventional advanced life support measures.3, 4, 5 This approach provides temporary circulatory and respiratory support while the underlying etiology of cardiac arrest is addressed.3,5,6 Despite increasing use and potential to improve survival, outcomes remain heterogeneous, and optimal management strategies continue to evolve.

The ECPR population is distinct from conventional venoarterial-extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) cohorts with cardiogenic shock or postcardiotomy failure. These patients experience prolonged low-flow states during cardiopulmonary resuscitation, multiple defibrillation attempts, and profound ischemia-reperfusion injury, resulting in myocardial stunning with biventricular failure, systemic inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction.7, 8, 9 Consequently, ECPR patients frequently develop vasoplegia and capillary leak syndromes, leading to refractory hypotension and volume shifts that complicate hemodynamic assessment.7,10 These physiologic disturbances, combined with uncertain neurologic recovery and multiorgan dysfunction, render determination of decannulation readiness uniquely challenging.

Timely decannulation is critical, as prolonged VA-ECMO support is associated with systemic complications, including bleeding, thrombosis, limb ischemia, infection, and impacts biventricular physiology.11 Conversely, premature removal may precipitate hemodynamic collapse, irreversible end-organ injury, and death. The balance between ensuring sufficient recovery and avoiding unnecessary support remains one of the most intricate aspects of ECPR management. Assessing readiness for VA-ECMO decannulation depends on evidence of recovery of myocardial contractility, improvement of end-organ perfusion, and restoration of vascular tone, yet decannulation timing is often based on clinical judgment and institutional experience rather than a standardized criterion.11,12

Reported decannulation success rates vary widely across different clinical indications for ECMO initiation, from 30% to 70%, highlighting the absence of consistent physiologic benchmarks and the need for pragmatic tools to guide decision-making.12 Indicators of recovery before decannulation have been studied in small cohorts of mixed etiologies including postcardiotomy, end-stage cardiomyopathy, post-transplantation patients requiring VA-ECMO support and include improving pulsatility, decreasing vasoactive-inotropic requirements, evidence of end-organ recovery, and rising left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) or aortic velocity–time integral.12, 13, 14, 15 However, assessment of these tools among OHCA patients treated with ECPR is yet to be defined. Accordingly, in this study, we aimed to develop a reproducible, physiologically informed framework to aid clinicians in assessing decannulation readiness in this uniquely complex population with bedside-accessible variables.

Methods

Study design and population

We evaluated consecutive patients presenting with refractory OHCA treated under the Minnesota Resuscitation Consortium ECPR protocol between December 2015 and March 2023.16 Patients aged 18 to 75 years, who presented with refractory OHCA, largely with a shockable presenting rhythm, and failure to achieve return of spontaneous circulation after at least 3 defibrillation attempts. Patients were excluded if they exhibited two or more of the following: end-tidal CO2 <10 mmHg, PaO2 <50 mm Hg, or lactate >18 mmol/L. After VA-ECMO cannulation, coronary angiography was performed, with a decision to pursue percutaneous coronary intervention at the discretion of the interventional cardiologist. Intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) counterpulsation therapy was used in patients with severe nonrevascularized coronary artery disease or arterial pulsatility <10 mm Hg after VA-ECMO initiation per institutional protocol. Cannulated patients underwent multidisciplinary care in the cardiac intensive care unit under the supervision of critical care cardiologists.

Assessment of decannulation readiness and variable definitions

Patients were evaluated for decannulation readiness by bedside VA-ECMO turndown studies. Eligibility for an ECMO weaning evaluation, henceforth referred to as a “turn-down study,” was assessed by the cardiac intensive care unit team based on evidence of myocardial recovery including hemodynamic stability, improved pulsatility, and evidence of end-organ perfusion. Repeat ECMO turndown studies were performed at the discretion of the attending physician until the hemodynamics met the heuristic eligibility for decannulation. During the turndown assessment, flow was decreased by 0.5 to 1 liters per minute (LPM) every 2 minutes, to a minimum flow between 1 to 2 liters based on the patient’s hemodynamic response and anticoagulation status. Bedside transthoracic echocardiography and hemodynamic data were assessed after 2 minutes at each successive flow de-escalation and at the lowest level of flow. Each transthoracic echocardiography study was retrospectively read by a second board-certified echocardiographer for agreement. Both left ventricular systolic function, evaluated as LVEF, and right ventricle (RV) systolic function were categorized by visual estimation. Pulsatility was measured as the difference between systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure at the lowest flow during the turndown study. If an IABP was present, the augmentation was paused to measure pulsatility at the lowest flow during the turndown study. Assessment for decannulation readiness was aborted at the discretion of the critical care cardiologist if there was evidence of hemodynamic instability including sudden rise in vasoactive medications or new onset tachyarrhythmias. When a patient was deemed eligible and ready to ECMO decannulation, VA-ECMO support was removed in the operating room or bedside by the cardiovascular surgery team via cut down and vascular repair. VA-ECMO flow and sum of vasoactive and inotropic agents (vasoactive-inotropic score [VIS]) immediately before decannulation were used for analyses.

Patients with devastating neurological injury, as defined by brain death or herniation, refractory hypotension despite maximal medical and mechanical circulatory support (MCS), and those whose families opted to transition to comfort-focused care, based on patient preferences, were excluded from decannulation readiness assessment. Decannulation outcomes were categorized into 2 groups, successful and failed decannulation.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized via the mean and SD and compared across groups using Wilcoxon rank sum test. Categorical variables were summarized using proportions and compared across groups via chi-square or Fisher exact test. Univariable and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the relationship between clinical variables and decannulation success. The predictive performance of univariable and multivariable logistic models was assessed using receiver operating characteristic curves.17 Bayesian additive regression trees (BART) was also used to predict failed decannulations, and the corresponding variable importance (the proportion of times a variable was used to split a tree) was used to rank the importance of predictors.18

The R library “bartMachine” was used to fit the BART model. Markov chain Monte Carlo was used for posterior inference, with a burn-in period of 1,000 iterations, followed by an additional 2000 iterations to approximate the posterior distribution. Institutional review boardapproval

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Minnesota Medical Center (protocol number 1703M11301). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the analysis and the use of deidentified data.

Results

Study population

Among 1,026 patients who underwent VA-ECMO cannulation at the University of Minnesota between December 2015 and March 2023, 390 patients were treated per the Minnesota Resuscitation Consortium ECPR protocol for ECPR. Of the 390 ECPR patients, VA-ECMO decannulation readiness assessment by turndown studies was performed in 150 patients (Figure 1). Reasons for exclusion from turndown studies included neurologically devastating injury postarrest (N = 148), refractory ventricular tachyarrhythmias with inability to achieve stable organized rhythm (N = 16), death due to refractory vasoplegia despite maximal VA-ECMO and vasopressor support (N = 22), and multiorgan failure with transition to comfort-focused care due to poor prognosis (N = 54). Among the 150 patients who were assessed for VA-ECMO decannulation readiness, 18 patients failed turndown trials and were not deemed ready for decannulation due to hemodynamic instability, and 7 patients showed evidence of cardiac recovery with readiness for decannulation; however, were not taken off VA-ECMO support due to progression of neurological injury to brain death. Therefore, 125 of the 390 ECPR OHCA patients (32%) underwent decannulation of VA-ECMO support. A total of 110 ECPR patients (28%) achieved successful decannulation and 15 patients (4%) failed VA-ECMO decannulation, defined as either: 1) need for recannulation with VA-ECMO support within the same hospitalization; or 2) death with cardiovascular etiology, defined as cardiogenic shock, incessant malignant arrhythmia leading to death or cardiac arrest following VA-ECMO decannulation (Figure 1).Figure 1Study PopulationECPR = extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation; MCS = mechanical circulatory support; OHCA = out-of-hospital cardiac arrest; VA-ECMO = venoarterial-extracorporeal membrane oxygenation.

Baseline characteristics and arrest details

The study cohort had a mean age of 56.5 ± 12.7 years, 79% were males and 73% identified as Caucasian (when race data were available). There were no significant differences in race, sex, or body mass index were observed between the successful and failed decannulation groups. A history of hyperlipidemia (67% vs 37%; P = 0.02), obstructive sleep apnea (40% vs 15%; P = 0.02) and chronic kidney disease (27% vs 8%; P = 0.05) were more prevalent in the failed decannulation group (Table 1). Importantly, a history of prior resuscitated ventricular tachycardia/ventricular fibrillation arrests (6.6% vs 1.8%; P = 0.03) and history of cardiomyopathy or reduced ejection fraction (53% vs 21%; P = 0.01) were more common in the failed decannulation group (6.6% vs 1.8%; P = 0.03). There were no significant differences in the prevalence of prior coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation, or tobacco use between the 2 groups. Detailed arrest characteristics for the cohort are provided in Table 2. Arrests occurring at home (56.4%) and in public settings (43.6%) were evenly distributed between the groups. Successful decannulations were more likely to be witnessed arrests (100% vs 73%; P = 0.04) and had higher rates of bystander CPR (68.6% vs 36.4%; P = 0.05). Factors predicting failed decannulation

As shown in Table 3, patients who failed decannulation had significantly lower LVEF during the turndown study at the time of lowest VA-ECMO flow of 1 LPM (27.5% vs 42.5%; P = 0.023), higher VIS (6.3 vs 1.1; P = 0.004), and longer duration of VA-ECMO support (6.0 vs 4.3 days; P = 0.003). Pulsatility (42 mm Hg vs 36 mm Hg; P = 0.477), VA-ECMO flow before decannulation (3.3 LPM vs 3.4 LPM; P = 0.927) and use of concomitant IABP counterpulsation therapy (47% vs 52%; P = 0.82) were comparable between the groups. In regression analysis (Table 4), higher LVEF at the time of lowest VA-ECMO flow (OR: 0.96; 95% CI: 0.92-0.99; P = 0.024), lower VIS (OR: 1.16; 95% CI: 1.04-1.32; P = 0.013), and shorter duration of VA-ECMO support (OR: 1.40; 95% CI: 1.16-1.98; P = 0.02) remained independently associated with higher odds of successful decannulation. In addition, greater arterial pulsatility emerged as an independent predictor in the adjusted model (OR: 0.95; 95% CI: 0.90-0.99; P = 0.027). Figure 2A shows individual receiver operating characteristic curves (sensitivity and specificity) and the corresponding AUCs for the aforementioned four significant covariates in univariate analyses, for example, the individual effect of each variable on failed decannulation. On multivariable logistic regression, VIS (OR: 1.19; 95% CI: 1.01-1.41; P = 0.03) and days on VA-ECMO support (OR: 1.49; 95% CI: 1.16-1.98; P = 0.02) remained independent predictors of decannulation failure, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.84. In comparison, the BART model demonstrated excellent discrimination, achieving an AUC of 0.91 (Figure 2B). The optimal subsets of predictors that most closely approximated the performance of the full BART model were identified as follows: with 1 predictor (k = 1), the model included VIS before decannulation; with 2 predictors (k = 2), VIS and the duration of VA-ECMO support; and with 3 predictors (k = 3), LVEF at decannulation, VIS, and duration of VA-ECMO support. The correlations (R2) between the reduced models and the full BART fit were 0.52, 0.77, and 0.91, respectively (Figure 2C). Discussion

In this single-institution cohort of patients undergoing ECPR for refractory OHCA, a population that is inherently difficult to study at scale, we identified pragmatic, bedside-assessable variables associated with successful VA-ECMO decannulation. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the performance of simple, clinically available parameters specifically within an ECPR population for OHCA. Several clinical and hemodynamic parameters were associated with decannulation success in our regression and BART model. During the weaning readiness assessment, we identified LVEF at the lowest VA-ECMO flow, arterial pulsatility, concomitant IABP use, predecannulation VIS, and VA-ECMO flow contributed meaningfully within a multifactorial framework (Central Illustration). This reinforces that successful decannulation depends on the interplay between myocardial recovery, vascular tone, and magnitude of MCS rather than any single determinant.

Echocardiographic assessment for decannulation readiness

Prior studies have similarly identified echocardiographic parameters useful for VA-ECMO decannulation success prediction, although most were conducted in mixed patient cohorts with variable ECPR patient populations.12, 13, 14 Aissaoui et al12 studied as smaller population of 51 patients on VA-ECMO for a mix of postcardiotomy shock, cardiomyopathy, and cardiac arrest, demonstrating that aortic velocity-time integral ≥ 10 cm, LVEF >20%–25%, and lateral mitral annulus velocity >6 cm/s were predictive of successful decannulation. Pappalardo et al14 similarly analyzed a small cohort of 42 heterogeneous patients on VA-ECMO for indications including myocardial infarction, arrhythmia, and cardiac arrest, confirming the value of LVEF and pulsatility in assessing recovery. Our study is unique in evaluating a substantially larger cohort of homogenous, OHCA ECPR patients, expanding on prior findings by focusing specifically on this distinct subgroup. Huang et al studied 46 patients, 26 of whom had cardiac arrest, and reported that smaller RV dimensions, higher RV ejection fraction, and greater RV strain were associated with successful decannulation, with 3-dimensional RV ejection fraction demonstrating strong discriminative accuracy (AUC: 0.90, cutoff 24.6%).13 Although these findings highlight the potential of advanced RV metrics, their clinical applicability remains limited by imaging feasibility and operator dependency. In our study, RV function was associated with decannulation outcomes on univariate analysis but lost significance on multivariable adjustment, and its contribution within the BART model was small. This attenuation may reflect the unique characteristics of the ECPR population with initially shockable rhythms, in whom myocardial infarction is a leading cause of arrest, revascularization is frequent and ischemic RV dysfunction is often relatively short lived. In addition, the technical challenges of echocardiographic assessment in this population, who have high incidence of rib fractures, poor acoustic windows, mechanical ventilation, and chest wall edema, likely reduce the precision and reproducibility of RV measurements. These physiological and technical factors together may explain the weaker predictive performance of RV parameters in our cohort compared with prior mixed-population studies.

Vasopressor support, pulsatility, and IABP use before decannulation

Arterial pulsatility is a clinically meaningful determinant of readiness for VA-ECMO decannulation because it primarily reflects native left ventricular contractility, whereas circuit flow is continuous and all contributions to the mean blood pressure are nonpulsatile.20,21 In our cohort, pulsatility was assessed during standardized turndown testing at the lowest tolerable ECMO flow, consistent with published “flow-challenge” approaches that transiently reduce pump flow to assess native cardiac output. At this minimal flow, patients who were successfully decannulated had higher mean pulsatility, comparable to prior studies supporting this observation.12,14,17 Aissaoui et al12 reported that among 38 cardiogenic shock patients undergoing ECMO flow reduction, those successfully decannulated had a higher mean pulsatility (52 ± 12 mm Hg vs 39 ± 15 mm Hg). Pappalardo et al14 reported a mean pulsatility of 59 (53–69) mm Hg among 49 successfully weaned patients, six of whom had experienced cardiac arrest but unclear if they were ECPR patients. Similarly, Park et al17 found that a higher mean pulsatility over the first 6 hours of extracorporeal life support was an independent predictor of successful decannulation and survival (HR: 0.96 [0.94-0.98] per mmHg increase; P < 0.001) in 69 adult patients supported for various causes. Although no clear predictive threshold has been established, our findings, consistent with prior studies, demonstrate that higher pulsatility at the time of decannulation were associated with successful decannulation, reflecting improved myocardial contractility and reduced ECMO dependence.

In our study, lower vasopressor support as measured by VIS emerged as a key predictor of successful decannulation. Several contemporary VA-ECMO cohorts have similarly identified lower VIS as a marker of recovery potential and a strong predictor of decannulation success. Dunton et al22 analyzed an adult single-center a mixed indication cohort of 265 VA-ECMO patients and found that those with a 24-hour VIS <6 were significantly more likely to survive to decannulation than those with VIS ≥6. Likewise, Lee et al23 examined 213 patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) complicated by cardiogenic shock and demonstrated that serial reductions in VIS and corresponding increases in pulsatility at 12 and 24 hours predicted successful decannulation (151 of 213, 70.9%). Similarly, Keller et al24 reported that among VA-ECMO patients, higher VIS was independently associated with lower odds of successful decannulation and survival, even when echocardiographic indices of biventricular function were not predictive. Together, these findings reinforce the role of declining vasoactive-inotropic requirements as a pragmatic and clinically meaningful indicator of readiness for VA-ECMO decannulation across various indications of VA-ECMO.

In addition, the presence of concomitant IABP counterpulsation therapy was not independently associated with increased decannulation success in our cohort. However, when contextualized in the multivariable BART model, it ranked importantly enough to improve odds of decannulation. This aligns pathophysiologically among patients with refractory ventricular tachycardia/ventricular fibrillation in our cohort who had an underlying etiology of AMI, in whom IABP counterpulsation therapy was chosen for severe nonrevascularized coronary artery disease or initial arterial pulsatility <10 mmHg after VA-ECMO initiation. Prior evidence evaluating the role of IABP has shown similar results, where Vallabhajosyula et al25 showed increased decannulation success among patients with AMI-cardiogenic shock requiring VA-ECMO support. However, these benefits are inconsistently observed in mixed indication for VA-ECMO and pure postcardiotomy cardiogenic shock populations.26,27

A majority of patients in the failed decannulation cohort (53%) had prior history of cardiomyopathy and deemed not to be candidates for advanced heart failure therapies; therefore, transition to other temporary MCS devices—such as surgical left ventricular support (e.g., Impella 5.5 Abiomed) or combined left- and right-sided support configurations (e.g., Impella with Protek Duo)—were not employed as de-escalation strategies. Although IABP counterpulsation therapy was routinely employed among patients with severe nonrevascularized coronary artery disease or low arterial pulsatility <10 mmHg, device transition to other forms of temporary MCS (micro-axial flow pumps)—represent an important and increasingly utilized option to facilitate successful decannulation that were not systematically captured in our study. Although this study did not systematically evaluate the efficacy of these approaches, our findings demonstrate that successful decannulation can be predicted and achieved in the vast majority of OHCA ECPR patients included in this cohort.

Implications for clinical practice

Our study provides clinical decision-making guidance for the management of OHCA patients rescued with ECPR. The identification of pragmatic clinical parameters may help inform the timing of weaning attempts and the likelihood of success when assessing a patient for decannulation, and the BART model specifically emphasizes and ranks the importance of multifaceted noninvasive assessment tools during the weaning process. Our findings support a structured evaluation of myocardial recovery and pave the way for prospective development and validation of a composite decannulation prediction scoring system that incorporates readiness variables for a favorable hemodynamic response following decannulation to provide a standardized approach to decannulation.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective, single-center design, including potential confounding and selection bias. However, the single-center registry also provided the advantage of detailed, granular data collection and consistent application of institutional ECPR and decannulation protocols. Such standardization may reduce unrecognized variability and confounding that often occur across centers with heterogeneous practices. Our findings may have limited generalizability to other institutions with different patient selection criteria, ECMO management strategies, or ECMO weaning protocols. We also observed that the failed decannulation group had lower rates of bystander CPR, which may have contributed to worse outcomes due to delayed resuscitation and greater myocardial ischemic injury. In our selective cohort, IABP support was systematically utilized in patients with very low pulsatility (<10 mm Hg) or severe nonrevascularized coronary artery disease, patients who likely represent a higher-risk physiological subset with differing myocardial recovery potential. This inherent selection bias may have influenced IABP use as a predictor for successful decannulation. Lastly, we did not assess outcomes beyond hospital discharge.

Future directions

Prospective, multicenter studies are warranted to validate our findings. Future work should additionally explore whether the identified risk factors—such as elevated vasoactive-inotropic requirements, low pulsatility, or impaired ventricular function—represent modifiable targets to reduce decannulation failure. Interventions aimed at optimizing myocardial recovery, vascular tone, and timing of support de-escalation could inform strategies to improve outcomes. Future prospective studies should also clarify how adjunctive mechanical supports, such as IABP and microaxial flow pumps, influence decannulation success in ECPR populations. In addition, defining characteristics of patients with highly successful decannulation may guide tailored management pathways and inform thresholds for earlier decannulation, future studies should systematically evaluate the utilization of these variables leads to earlier decannulation. Development of a validated decannulation readiness score may help standardize decannulation decisions across centers and identify patients suitable for earlier decannulation. Finally, integrating advanced analytics, including machine learning approaches, could enhance risk prediction and enable individualized decannulation strategies in this high-risk population.

Conclusion

In this study of OHCA patients undergoing ECPR, we identified pragmatic, bedside-assessable and noninvasive variables including LVEF at lowest VA-ECMO flow, arterial pulsatility, VIS, VA-ECMO flow before decannulation and concomitant IABP use as key factors associated with successful VA-ECMO decannulation. Our findings highlight the value of integrating hemodynamic and echocardiographic assessments to guide decannulation readiness in this complex population. Although individual parameters provided partial insight, composite modeling using BART improved predictive accuracy, emphasizing that decannulation success depends on multiple interrelated factors, which were noninvasive and easily assessable at the bedside. These results highlight the importance of physiology-based decision-making and suggest that optimizing modifiable factors such as myocardial recovery, vasopressor requirements, and ECMO flow requirements to improve decannulation outcomes.
