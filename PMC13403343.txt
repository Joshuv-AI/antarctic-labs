Experimental study and thermodynamic modelling of the Nb–Sb system
Authors: Pavel Brož, Jan Vřešťál, Vitaliy Romaka, Xinlin Yan, Jiří Buršík, Olga Fabrichnaya, Mario Kriegel, Peter Rogl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thermodynamic modelling of the Nb–Sb system was performed by the CALPHAD method, based on SGTE data of the Gibbs energy for pure elements, as well as on data from experimental studies in the literature combined with our new information, obtained via DTA, EDX-EPMA, XPD and ab initio calculations. Our re-investigation confirms the three intermetallic compounds reported earlier: Nb3Sb, Ni5Sb4 and NbSb2. Particularly, our detailed study of the Nb-rich part of the system revealed an intermediate phase with a narrow composition range for Nb3Sb, extending at 950 °C from 74.0 at% Nb to 75.3 at% Nb (EMPA-WDX data). Battling a high Sb evaporation above about 1300 °C (monitored by thermogravimetry) high-temperature DTA data (Y2O3 crucibles) unambiguously defined the peritectic decomposition of Nb3Sb at 1840 ± 25 °C. In contrast to earlier information in the literature, Nb5Sb4 was found to form peritectically at 1550 ± 10 °C, whereas the temperature of the peritectic reaction of NbSb2 appeared at 1320 ± 10 °C. The eutectic temperature of 624 ± 8 °C was confirmed by low-temperature DTA measurement (sealed quartz ampoules). Furthermore, data on heat of formation for all the compounds were provided by ab initio calculations, which served to optimize model parameters for the calculation of the Nb–Sb phase diagram. The calculated binary phase diagram obtained agrees well with the experimentally determined one. The thermodynamic database is attached.

Introduction

The discovery of highly efficient thermoelectric materials in the systems Nb1−xTxFeSb (T = Ti,Zr,Hf,V…)1–7 and particularly their syntheses fostered the demand for detailed knowledge of phase relations in these systems, for which the Nb–Sb binary system plays a key role. The thorough reinvestigation of the Nb–Sb system (for concentrations richer than 30 at% Sb) by Melnyk et al.8 (X-ray powder diffraction (XPD), electron probe microanalysis (EPMA) and differential thermal analysis (DTA) in W-lined closed Ta-crucibles) removed many inconsistencies of earlier phase diagram studies (for details see the early assessment of Massalski,9 which essentially relied on the experimental data of Myzenkova et al.10). Although Lomnytska et al.11 confirmed the findings of Melnyk et al.8 on the existence of only three binary compounds: Nb3Sb, Nb5Sb4 and NbSb2, it seems that Lomnytska reintroduced the older DTA data by Myzenkova et al.10 A further reinvestigation by Failamani et al.12 on the basis of XPD and isothermal annealing essentially confirmed the phase relations as derived by Melnyk et al.8 Due to the high-temperature limit of the DTA used by Melnyk et al.,8 the Nb-rich part (<30 at% Sb) of the diagram has hitherto not been re-investigated: according to the early experiments of Myzenkova et al.10 it reveals a large solubility of Sb in Nb, a peritectic formation of Nb3Sb at 1750 ± 25 °C and a significantly large homogeneity region for Nb3Sb. Despite these experimental facts, thermodynamic assessment of the Nb–Sb phase diagram has not been performed yet.

As a basis for a thermodynamic assessment (CALPHAD calculation) of the Nb–Sb system, the present paper intends to collect (i) experimental details on the formation temperature of Nb3Sb in the Nb-rich part of the phase diagram via high-temperature DTA followed by EDX-EPMA and XPD in order to check on the high and low temperature Sb-solubility in Nb. Furthermore, (ii) a re-investigation of all the reaction isotherms in the Nb–Sb binary became an issue of the present work. The thermodynamic calculation (iii) relies on the heat of formation data calculated in this work by ab initio methods.

Materials and methods

A set of 10 alloys with a total weight of 3 grams each has been prepared from elemental powders (Alfa Ventron, 99.9 mass% Nb and 99.999% Sb) with compositions (in at%): Nb95Sb5, Nb90Sb10, Nb85Sb15, Nb80Sb20, Nb75Sb25, Nb70Sb30, Nb55Sb45, Nb50Sb50, Nb33Sb67 and Nb10Sb90. The powder blends were compacted without lubricants in a steel die and vacuum-sealed in a quartz tube for annealing at 950 °C. After each third day (for 3 times) the sinter cake was powdered to a grain size of less than 60 µm, re-compacted and sealed for heat treatment again. As in all cases the sinter cake appeared porous, densification was achieved for cylindrical plates (∅ = 10 mm, height ∼1 mm, relative density >95%) via high-pressure torsion (HPT) at room temperature in steel dies at 4 GPa and up to 5 revolutions. The HPT-treated alloys were again annealed in a quartz tube for 2 days at 950 °C prior to use in the DTA. Some of the sintered alloys were also arc melted in order to reveal phase relations at high temperature. As cast as well as polycrystalline powders were analyzed by X-ray powder diffraction (XPD) either with Ge-monochromated Cu-Kα1 radiation (λ = 0.154056 nm) employing a Guinier-Huber image plate recording system or a Bruker Diffractometer and Si-wafers as support and Ni-filtered Cu-Kα radiation. Rietveld refinements were performed using the program FULLPROF,13 whilst precise lattice parameters were obtained from least squares fits with the program STRUKTUR14 whereby Ge served as an internal standard (aGe = 0.5657906 nm).

All samples (before and after DTA) were ground on SiC papers and polished with Al2O3 powders (down to 0.3 µm) via standard procedures and have been examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The microstructure and chemical composition of the alloys were analysed by a Jeol JSM-6460 scanning electron microscope equipped with an energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) detector operated at 20 kV and by a Tescan LYRA 3XMH FEG/SEM scanning electron microscope equipped with an X-Max80 Oxford Instruments EDX detector operated at 20 kV. In some cases, wavelength dispersive X-ray (WDX) analyses were employed in a Jeol JSM-6460 operated at 20 kV using Nb-Lα, Sb-Lα and PET spectrometer crystals for Nb and Sb (PET = Pentaerythritol). In all cases quantitative evaluation of compositions was performed with the INCA-software.15

High-temperature differential thermal analysis (DTA) combined with thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed using SETSYS-EVOLUTION2400 (SETARAM, Caluire, France) under a 20 ml min−1 He-flow. Two independent measurements on two pieces from each alloy with a mass of 50–100 mg were carried out in the temperature region 25–2000 °C. Zirconia (Yttria stabilized) crucibles (YSZ) proved to be proper to prevent reaction between the molten sample and crucible. To decrease Sb evaporation, Y2O3 powder was applied on the surface of the samples, lids were used and the samples were heated with a fast rate of 30 K min−1 up to 1700 °C (temperature below peritectic temperature 1750 ± 25 °C)10 followed by a slow rate of 10 K min−1 up to 2000 °C. After heating the samples were cooled down with a rate of 30 K min−1 to reduce contamination of the experimental device and to keep the final composition of the samples. The simultaneous thermogravimetric analysis was performed to control evaporation processes during heating the samples at high temperatures. For accurate temperature measurements a DTA calibration was performed on melting temperatures of Al, Au, Ni and Pt. For accurate mass loss determination first measurements with both empty crucibles were performed which were subtracted then from sample measurements.

Low-temperature DTA measurements were performed in a Netzsch STA 409 CD/3/403/5/G apparatus, a specially-adapted type of the commercial STA 409 CD-QMS 403/5 Skimmer Coupling Instrument16,17 on samples sealed in quartz ampoules to prevent Sb evaporation. The ampoules had the shape of the original alumina crucibles for DTA. Three heating and cooling cycles at a heating and cooling rate of 5 K min−1 from room temperature up to 1100 °C under an argon flow rate of 70 ml min−1 were performed on the Sb-rich alloy Nb10Sb90 to exactly specify the low-temperature phase transformations.

Computational details

The DFT calculations were carried out using an all-electron full-potential linearized augmented plane wave (FP-LAPW) code Elk v4.3.06 18 and the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) v.5.4.419 PAW-type potentials.20 In both cases, the Perdew-Burke-Enzerhoff exchange correlation functional in the generalized gradient approximation (GGA)21 with the Monkhorst–Pack k-mesh22 and 0.02 2π/Å k-spacing were used. The planewave cut-off was set to 300 eV for VASP. For the final total energy calculations the optimized crystal structures were used. Thermodynamic modelling of phase equilibria in the Nb–Sb system was performed based on the CALPHAD approach23 using program Pandat (CompuTherm, LLC, USA).24 Thermodynamic modelling of phases relied on the well-known Compound Energy Formalism (CEF), which allows to respect the real crystallographic structure of a phase by means of a sublattice description.25 The thermodynamic description of a pure component i in a given phase in the given temperature interval is taken from ref. 26. The thermodynamic description of the Gibbs energy of solid solutions and the liquid, as well as contributions of the ideal mixing to the Gibbs energy and of the excess Gibbs energy, expressed by the Redlich-Kister polynomial,25 have been outlined in ref. 25 and 27–30 (for details the reader is referred to our earlier publication31).

Results and discussion

New experimental phase diagram data

Our novel investigations of the Nb-rich part of the Nb–Sb system – covering the melting point and homogeneity region of Nb3Sb – revealed an intermediate phase with a narrow composition range for Nb3Sb extending at 950 °C from 74.0 at% Nb to 75.3 at% Nb (EMPA-WDX data). Rietveld refinement of the alloy Nb70Sb30, annealed at 950 °C, confirmed the equilibrium: Nb3Sb (Cr3Si-type, Pm3̄n ; a = 0.52673 nm) + Nb5Sb4 (Ti5Te4-type, I4/m; a = 1.03357, c = 0.35668 nm). At the same temperature, the alloy Nb80Sb20 at 950 °C revealed the equilibrium of Nb-rich Nb3Sb (a = 0.52654 nm) + (Nb), which dissolved a maximum of 2.3 at% Sb (W-type, Im3̄m; a = 0.33136 nm). In as-cast alloys, however, Sb-solubility in Nb approached a much higher value of Nb80.7Sb19.3 (a = 0.33127 nm). The Sb-rich solvus line of niobium is thus close to that published by Myzenkova et al.,10 who reported a solubility of ∼4 at.%Sb in Nb at 800 °C rising to about 20 at% Sb at the peritectic temperature 1750 ± 25 °C. However, the compounds reported by Myzenkova et al.,10 namely “Nb3Sb2”, “NbSb” and “Nb4Sb5”, have neither been confirmed in our investigations and the investigation by Melnyk et al.8 nor by Failamani et al.12

High-temperature DTA with antimonides is difficult to perform due to the high volatility of Sb whenever experiments exceed its boiling point at 1587 °C. Nevertheless, a well pronounced peak at about 1840 ± 25 °C in samples with 20, 25, 30, 45 and 50 at% Sb and at about 1550 ± 10 °C in samples with 30, 45 and 50 at% Sb was detected. In the samples with 50 and 67 at% Sb (NbSb2), a well pronounced peak at about 1320 ± 10 °C appeared, and the low-temperature DTA on the sample Nb10Sb90 sealed in a quartz ampoule revealed a well pronounced peak at 624 ± 8 °C. No peak, corresponding to that at 1080 °C, published by Melnyk et al.,8 was detected in the investigated samples. This discrepancy likely arose from the reaction of antimony with the tantalum crucible revealing the reaction: at 1080 ± 10 °C.12 In all other Nb-rich samples, no DTA peaks indicating high-temperature phase transformations were detected, confirming their absence beyond the temperature limit of the high-temperature DTA device.

As found by SEM, the final composition of the samples after high-temperature DTA (samples measured in YSZ crucibles covered with YSZ lids) was shifted to a lower content of Sb due to its evaporation. Examples of DTA on alloys Nb70Sb30 and Nb50Sb50, showing the detected phase transformations at 1840, 1550 and 1320 °C, are presented in Fig. 1 and 2. An example of a TGA measurement in the temperature range of 30–1600 °C is shown in the inset of Fig. 1. The information from TGA was used to evaluate the composition of the samples at the temperatures of phase transformations 1320 and 1550 °C. For the highest temperature of the phase transformation, 1840 °C, where already intense Sb evaporation was observed, the average value of the initial nominal composition and that after DTA (the mean time of the DTA heating and cooling cycle corresponding to this phase transformation) was considered as the final one for every sample. Although this approach may introduce some artificial error, taking the average value from the heating and cooling process, which both are accompanied by Sb evaporation, was the most appropriate method for assessing the situation under these extreme conditions. However, the temperature of the phase transformation is the same in the broad composition range because an invariant reaction was studied. Since no or negligible Sb evaporation was observed at the temperature of 1320 °C (see the insert in Fig. 1), the compositions of the samples were taken as the initial nominal ones for this phase transformation. Fig. 3 shows the result of the low-temperature DTA performed on sample Nb10Sb90 in a sealed quartz ampoule with a eutectic transformation at 624 °C. The heating curves slightly differ due to approaching equilibrium. However, the difference between the curves is 1.3 °C, which is well within the 8 °C uncertainty, presented in the phase diagram in Fig. 6. This effect is not related to such as sample reactions or changes in Sb distribution since no other peaks were observed. Thermodynamic data from ab initio calculations

Enthalpies of formation of the compounds Nb3Sb, Nb5Sb4 and NbSb2 were obtained as the result of ab initio calculations, using Elk and VASP programs. The structure data and the enthalpies of formation are listed in Table 1. The values obtained by the two different DFT methods are close to each other for the Nb3Sb and NbSb2 antimonides and match well with those reported in the AFLOWLib database.32 The enthalpies of the Nb5Sb4 compound calculated by the FLAPW and PAW methods are in agreement as well but differ from those reported in the AFLOWLib database (Table 1 and Fig. 5). Due to the lack of experimental data on the enthalpies of formation of the binary compounds, the obtained ab initio results can only be compared with the enthalpy of formation of the Nb50Sb50 composition (−477 meV per atom), calculated by semi-empirical Miedema's model,33 which appears to be of the same order of magnitude. The data calculated in this work form a traditional convex hull (Fig. 5), which indicates the thermodynamic stability of all three binary compounds mentioned above in the Nb–Sb system.

Conclusions

The Nb–Sb system was investigated experimentally and theoretically. Results of investigation of a series of alloys by high- and low-temperature differential thermal analysis, electron probe microanalysis and powder X-ray analysis and of ab initio calculations of formation enthalpies led to the creation of experimental and theoretical assessment of the Nb–Sb system. Experimentally, we found an intermediate phase with a narrow composition range Nb3Sb, extending from 74.0 at% Nb to 75.3 at% Nb, and a maximum solubility of 2.3 at% Sb in Nb at 950 °C. Furthermore, this phase is stable up to 1840 °C, in contradiction with the temperature of 1750 °C published earlier. The experimental data and the results of DFT calculations for the intermetallic compounds Nb3Sb, Nb5Sb4 (peritectic formation at 1550 °C) and NbSb2 (peritectic formation at 1320 °C), were used for the thermodynamic assessment of Nb–Sb. The calculated phase diagram of the Nb–Sb system is in good agreement with the new experimental data on the constitution of the Nb–Sb binary.
