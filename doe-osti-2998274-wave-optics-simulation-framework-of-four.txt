  Wave-Optics Simulation Framework of Fourier Transform Holography with XMCD

Abstract
We present a wave-optics simulation framework, implemented within the SRW, for
modeling FTH with XMCD contrast. The framework propagates coherent, circularly
polarized X-ray wavefronts from an undulator source through the sample and optics to the
detector, reconstructing real-space images from the resulting holograms. Three principal
extensions are introduced: (i) incorporation of polarization-dependent refractive indices in
the sample plane, enabling direct simulation of XMCD contrast, (ii) a memory-efficient
wavefront-splitting propagation scheme that treats individual apertures independently and
coherently combines their fields at detector, and (iii) an integrated reconstruction module
that delivers holographic images within the same framework. Together, these advances
establish a versatile tool for quantitative exploration of coherence, aperture geometry, and
detector sampling, and for the design and interpretation of XMCD-FTH experiments at
synchrotron beamlines.

1. Introduction
Holography was first introduced by Gabor in 1948 as a method for electron-microscope
wavefront reconstruction, enabling recovery of both amplitude and phase for later optical
reformation [1,2]. With the advent of coherent lasers, the concept was extended to visible
light, giving rise to optical holography and ultimately laying the foundation for x-ray
implementations [3]. The development of high-brightness synchrotron and free-electron-
laser sources in the late twentieth century made x-ray holography experimentally viable,
offering nanometer-scale resolution and element-specific contrast without the aberrations
of imaging optics [4].
Among lensless strategies, Fourier Transform Holography (FTH) is particularly attractive for
its experimental simplicity: the interference pattern recorded between a sample and a
reference aperture can be inverted by a single Fourier transform, eliminating the need for
iterative phase-retrieval algorithms [3,5]. The first X-ray FTH experiments demonstrated
nanoscale magnetic imaging in thin-film samples [4], establishing this method as a powerful
tool for charactering magnetic domain structure with high spatial resolution. In such cases,
contrast arises from X-ray Magnetic Circular Dichroism (XMCD) [6]. XMCD is the difference
in absorption (and phase) for right- versus left-circularly polarized X-rays at element-specific
absorption edges, providing sensitivity to local magnetization [8]. By combining XMCD
contrast with FTH’s single-step reconstruction, one obtains a technique that is element-

specific, robust against noise, and capable of tens-of-nanometer resolution in a non-
invasive photon-in/photon-out geometry [6]–[8]).

To support the design and interpretation of XMCD-FTH experiments, accurate simulations
must capture the full chain—from source coherence through sample interaction to image
reconstruction—while treating polarization effects explicitly. Existing Synchrotron Radiation
Workshop (SRW) capabilities already allow detailed source and optics modeling [9–12], but
lacked three elements critical for XMCD holography: polarization-dependent sample
interaction, efficient handling of multi-aperture masks, and integrated reconstruction. In
this work we extend SRW to include these features, enabling high-fidelity modeling of an
entire XMCD-FTH imaging pipeline. The resulting framework provides not only a means to
reproduce experimental data but also a platform for predictive exploration of parameter
space, directly informing experimental design.

2. Theoretical Background and Methodology
Before presenting the formalism, we summarize the simulation geometry and numerical
parameters that serve as a running example in subsequent derivations. Using a realistic
beamline configuration avoids repeated forward references and provides a concrete
foundation for the methodology developed in Sections 2.2–2.4.

2.1 Experimental Configuration and Simulation Parameters
The simulations replicate an FTH experiment with XMCD contrast based on the Coherent
Soft X-ray Scattering (CSX) beamline at NSLS-II. Coherent, circularly polarized X-rays at
778 eV illuminate the sample in transmission geometry. The optical configuration follows
the CSX Sirepo model (Fig. 1a), providing realistic beamline parameters.
A holography mask, co-located with the magnetic specimen, defines the field of view
through a 720 nm object aperture and introduces one or more nanoscale reference
apertures (40–60 nm), separated by 2 µm center-to-center (Fig. 1b). The detector is placed
33 cm downstream, comprising 980 pixels with 30 µm pitch, corresponding to ~29.4 mm
coverage in the far field.
For demonstration, we consider a thin-film magnetic multilayer, [Pt(2.7 nm)/Co60Fe20B20(0.8
nm)/MgO(1.5 nm)]×15, with effective optical constants at 778 eV listed in Table 1. These
values, derived from CXRO tabulations with dichroic corrections from Co L3-edge spectra,
parameterize the polarization-dependent refractive index decrement and absorption index
used in Section 2.3.

2.2 FTH Principles
Holography is an interferometric technique in which an object is reconstructed not through
direct imaging, but from the interference pattern formed between its scattered wave and a
reference wave. In FTH, the reference is introduced in the same plane as the object, so that
together they constitute the effective “sample” (Fig. 2a). Two features define this approach:
the hologram is recorded in the far field (Fig. 2b), and the reference encodes the phase
information of the object wave, thereby solving the phase problem and enabling a
straightforward reconstruction. In the simplest case, where the reference acts as a point
scatterer, the object distribution can be retrieved in a single step by Fourier transforming the
hologram (Fig. 2c) [5,6].

2.2.2 Optimal Parameters
For reliable FTH reconstruction, several well-established conditions must be satisfied.
First, the object–reference separation must be large enough to avoid overlap between the
reconstructed object and its autocorrelation. Since the autocorrelation typically extends to
twice the object size D, a separation of at least 1.5D is required [3]. In our configuration, the
reference apertures are displaced by 2 µm from the 720 nm object aperture, which
comfortable exceeds this criterion.
Second, the reconstructed field of view must be large enough to contain the object, its
autocorrelation, and two sidebands. This condition is expressed as 𝑠𝑠0 = λZ / ΔX > 4D, where
λ is the wavelength, Z is the sample-to-detector distance, and ΔX is the detector pixel size
[4,5]. Using the parameters defined in Section 2.1 (λ = 1.59 nm at 778 eV, Z = 33 cm, ΔX = 30
µm), the reconstructed field of view is 𝑠𝑠0 ≈ 17.5µm which comfortably larger than the ~3 µm
object extent used in our simulations.
Finally, the same set of parameters also defines the effective numerical aperture of the
system and thus the spatial resolution, given by R ≈ λZ / (NΔX), where N is the number of
detector pixels [15]. For N = 980, the achievable resolution is ~17.9 nm in the sample plane,
sufficient to resolve nanoscale XMCD contrast.
Together, these considerations confirm that the CSX–based configuration satisfies the
physical requirements for FTH and supports robust nanometer-scale reconstruction.

By default, SRW treats samples as scalar transmission objects defined by refractive index

2.4 Numerical Implementation of Hologram Reconstruction
The mask plane contains both the object aperture (transmitting the sample-modulated
wavefront) and one or more reference apertures. These contributions are propagated to the
detector using SRW’s Fraunhofer propagator (“from waist,” single FFT). For the present
geometry—778 eV photon energy, apertures of 40–720 nm, detector distance of 33 cm—the
far-field condition Z ≫ D²/λ is well satisfied [15].
At the detector, the object and reference waves interfere to produce polarization-dependent
holograms Hσ(u, v). Subtracting the two helicities isolates XMCD contrast, and a single
inverse propagation reconstructs the magnetic hologram.
A naive uniform grid sized to resolve the smallest reference aperture would require
prohibitively fine sampling across the full mask, resulting in multi-gigabyte FFTs. To
overcome this, we developed a wavefront-splitting strategy: each aperture is sampled and
propagated independently at a resolution matched to its size, and the detector-plane fields
are coherently summed. This preserves the accuracy of the interference fringes while
reducing computational demands by orders of magnitude, making high-resolution XMCD
holography simulations tractable on a workstation.
With this implementation in place, Section 3 presents simulation results that demonstrate
how polarization-dependent optical constants manifest in the reconstructed holograms.

3. Simulation Results
Representative results of the SRW-based framework are shown in Fig. 3. Panels 3(a) and 3(b)
display the far-field intensity distributions, i.e. the holograms Hσ(u, v), obtained for right- and
left-circularly polarized illumination. The two holograms appear nearly identical by visual
inspection, reflecting the fact that the non-magnetic background dominates the diffraction
pattern. The weak magnetic contribution is isolated by subtraction, HΔ = Hσ⁺ − Hσ⁻, which
effectively suppresses the non-magnetic terms and reveals the XMCD contrast [Fig. 3(c)].
The reference domain configuration used in the simulation is shown in Fig. 3(d). While in
experiments this information is unknown, in simulations it provides a ground truth for
assessing reconstruction fidelity. The Fourier inversion of the differential hologram [Fig. 3(e)]
recovers the main domain features and domain-wall morphology, with some blurring
attributable to the finite reference-aperture size and numerical sampling. An additional
cross-check is provided by numerically back-propagating the detector wavefront over
distance -Z (i.e., numerically propagating the complex wavefront from the detector back
toward the sample plane) [Fig. 3(f)]. In the Fraunhofer regime, free-space propagation

corresponds to a Fourier transform operation; thus, inverse propagation approximates the
inverse transform.
Both the Fourier-inversion reconstruction [Fig. 3(e)] and the back-propagated field [Fig. 3(f)]
represent only approximate reconstructions of the original object. This is a general
consequence of the finite experimental geometry: the limited detector extent truncates
high-angle scattering, finite pixel size introduces sampling and quantization, and the finite
reference aperture sets an effective resolution envelope. These constraints apply equally to
direct Fourier inversion and to numerical back-propagation, and they define the ultimate
fidelity achievable in either approach. The distinction is that Fourier inversion in FTH relies
only on measured intensities and a reference aperture to encode phase, whereas numerical
back-propagation in simulation has access to the full complex detector field. In principle,
this would allow exact inversion of the forward propagation; in practice, the inversion
remains approximate for the same reasons noted above. The comparison therefore serves
mainly as an internal consistency check: the back-propagated intensity is consistent with
the direct Fourier inversion, confirming that both methods converge within the numerical
limits of the detector geometry and sampling scheme.
Together, these results demonstrate that the extended framework accurately reproduces
the sequence from polarization-dependent hologram formation through XMCD-enhanced
reconstruction. The ability to subtract polarizations, validate reconstructions against known
ground-truth domains, and perform internal propagation consistency checks underscores
the framework’s utility for both experimental design and methodological benchmarking.

4. Discussion and Conclusions
We have developed and demonstrated a simulation framework for FTH with XMCD contrast.
The implementation integrates three new capabilities: polarization-dependent refractive
indices in the sample plane, a wavefront-splitting scheme for memory-efficient propagation
of multi-aperture masks, and an integrated reconstruction module. Using the CSX beamline
configuration as a test case, the framework reproduces the full sequence from polarization-
resolved hologram formation to XMCD-enhanced image reconstruction, with back-
propagation serving as an internal consistency check.

Beyond reproducing known results, the framework provides a platform for systematic
exploration of experimental parameter space. Future simulations will address three
practical aspects of XMCD holography: (i) the influence of limited transverse coherence on
fringe visibility and reconstruction fidelity [6, 16], (ii) the effect of object aperture size on the
reconstructed field of view [17], and (iii) the trade-off between resolution and signal strength
as a function of reference aperture size [18, 19]. These studies will help quantify tolerances
and guide experimental choices at synchrotron beamlines.

In summary, the present work establishes a robust and versatile simulation environment for
XMCD-enabled FTH, linking source physics, sample interactions, and image reconstruction
within a single wave-optics framework. This tool enables not only the validation of
experimental data but also predictive optimization of holography experiments, supporting
the broader use of XMCD-FTH as a quantitative probe of nanoscale magnetism.
