 Catalytic Reaction Intermediates Revealed with Femtosecond M-edge XANES

Major Goals:

The major goals of this work were to measure the electronic structure of earth-abundant catalysts
at key steps in their catalytic cycles using ultrafast UV/Visible, Extreme Ultraviolet, and X-ray
spectroscopy. Our central idea was that by using these sophisticated probes, we could uncover
hidden reaction mechanisms and provide guidance for catalyst design. A second major goal was
the development of Extreme Ultraviolet (XUV) spectroscopy as an accessible, tabletop probe of
transition metal electronic structure. Spectra in this 30-100 eV energy range probe 3d3p
transitions in 3d metals and 5p/4f5d transitions in 5d metals, and share the oxidation state, spin
state, and ligand field specificity of hard X-ray spectroscopy. The Vura-Weis group previously
established the basic utility of this technique for measuring the excited-state cascades of
transition metal chromophores, and in this grant we intended to extend its scope to catalytic
intermediates.

Accomplishments

Many of our findings over the six year period of this grant centered on Ni photocatalysis. The
prototypical molecule in this family is (dtbbpy)Ni(o-tol)Cl (dtb = 4,4′-di-tert-butyl, bpy =
bipyridine, o-tol = ortho-tolyl). Photoexcitation with visible light leads to a transient state that
persists for ~5 ns, but whose excited-state character remained ambiguous. This state was first
assigned as a metal-to-ligand charge transfer (MLCT) state on the basis of DFT calculations and
transient UV/Visible spectroscopy. Later reports re-assigned this state as a 3(d,d) state based on
transient infrared spectroscopy. We used picosecond X-ray absorption spectroscopy to
conclusively show that the excited state is a tetrahedral Ni(II) triplet, as compared to the square
planar, singlet ground state. Transient Ni L-edge XANES (probing the 2p3d spectrum)

showed the spin state change, and transient Ni K-edge XANES (probing 1s3d,4p) showed the
change in geometry. This publication (J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 2024, 15, 18, 4976–4982)
established picosecond X-ray absorption spectroscopy as a crucial tool for this family of
photocatalysts.

       In a related study (J. Am. Chem. Soc. 2025, 147, 20, 17315–17329), we investigated the
photoactivity of four NiII tolyl chloride complexes supported by either a bulky bidentate
[2.2]pyridinophane (HN2) or the traditional 2,2′-bipyridine (tBubpy). We observed comparable
quantum yields for photodegradation of the four Ni complexes despite a change in the bidentate
ligand framework, while observing a previously uninvestigated O2-reactivity that leads to C-O
bond formation. By leveraging the newly developed bidentate HN2 ligand, we hindered such side
reactivity upon irradiation. By varying the location of the methyl group on the phenyl ring, we
observed that tBubpyNi(p-tolyl)Cl reacts readily with O2 without being irradiated whereas the more
sterically encumbering ortho-tolyl version is stable under O2 until irradiated. Time-dependent
 tBu
density functional theory (TDDFT) revealed that bpyNi(p-tolyl)Cl has the smallest
HOMO/LUMO gap and most accessible tetrahedral-geometry triplet state, highlighting its unique
propensity for radical chemistry and consistent with its O2 reactivity and photochemistry. This
comparison of photoactive nickel compounds supported by bidentate pyridinophanes vs. bipyridyl
ligands showed how side reactivity can be selectively controlled by the steric environment around
the Ni center and that such photochemistry is not unique to bipyridyl-supported Ni compounds.

 In a third publication on Ni photochemistry (ACS Catal. 2026, 16, 2, 1522–1532), we
uncovered surprising anti-Kasha photochemistry. Kasha’s Rule is one of the foundations of
photochemistry: because relaxation between electronic states is much faster than most other
processes such as molecular diffusion, all reactivity occurs from the lowest-energy excited state
no matter what state was initially excited. As we explored tridentate pyridinophane (RN3) ligand
frameworks, we observed wavelength-dependent product yields and branching ratios.
Illumination at 380 nm resulted in 95% C-O bond formation and 5% C-H bond formation,
whereas illumination at 365 nm gave 46% C-O and 47% C-H. This is a drastic change in
product ratio for only a wavelength shift of 15 nm! Using UV/Visible transient absorption
spectroscopy we showed that there are actually two populations of photocatalysts in solution –
the intended iPrN3NiCl2 5-coordinate complex and 6-coordinate complex with axially-bound

MeOH. These two complexes have slightly shifted UV/Vis absorption bands, such that a change
in wavelength changes the relative population of their excited states and the resulting reactivity.
Most importantly, this study led us to reconceptualize the catalytic cycle: prebinding of an
alcohol to the catalytic site suppresses protodehalogenation of the substrate and promotes the
desired C-O formation pathway.

 The second major aim of this project is to study the reaction mechanisms of
metalloporphyrin photocatalysts. μ-oxo ironIII bisporphyrins have been widely studied as earth-
abundant and atom-economical photocatalysts for hydrocarbon oxidation using only light and
molecular oxygen. However, the photonic quantum yield (moles of products vs moles of photons
absorbed) in these experiments is too low (10-4) to be industrially relevant. The generally-accepted
explanation for this low quantum yield is rapid recombination of the PFeII/PFeIV=O monomers
produced after excited-state disproportionation. This near-consensus was reached on the basis of
a transient UV/Visible signature that lasts for ~4 ns after 400 nm ligand-to-metal charge transfer
(LMCT) excitation. However, there was a small literature disputing that assignment and claiming
that the transient spectrum is better assigned as a PFeIII+/PFeIII-O- ion pair. We used femtosecond
XUV spectroscopy to show that the μ-oxo bis[tetraphenylporphyrinato iron(III)] dimer does not
dissociate unto catalytically active PFeII/PFeIV=O monomers as its primary photopathway. The
differential signal from the metals disappears with a time constant of less than a picosecond, and
there is no evidence for any long-lived FeIV species the signal-to-noise ratio of our experiment
(Catal. Sci. Technol., 2022,12, 6092-6097).

 We followed up on this result using femtosecond hard X-ray spectroscopy at the Linac
Coherent Light Source. The XUV experiments were performed in polymer-embedded thin films
due the ultrahigh vacuum sample environment required for the experiment. Given that our result
upended decades of assumptions by the synthetic community (mostly based on uv/visible transient
absorption spectroscopy which attempts to infer the metal oxidation state from changes in the
porphyrin π-π* transitions), it was important for us to confirm that the lack of FeIV was not simply
the result of the lack of solvent. We therefore performed solution-phase femtosecond Fe K-edges
X-ray absorption and emission spectroscopy at the linac coherent light source. Transient spectra
and kinetics are shown both both experiments in Figure 4. Immediately after photoexcitation at
400 nm, a transient signal appears that is consistent with formation of the initial LMCT state (PFeII-

O•+- PFeIII). In the XANES, this results in a redshift of the XANES signal, causing a positive
transient feature at ~7124 eV. In the XES, the decreased spin state of the FeII quintet/triplet vs the
original FeIII sextet causes a redshift in the Kβ emission. In both experiments, the majority of the
transient signal disappears with a ~1 ps time constant, indicating that most photoexcited molecules
have returned to their high-spin FeIII ground electronic state. There is a weak long-lived signal in
both experiments that is consistent with a square planar PFeIII cation. Transient scattering data
also indicates that the bisporphyrin is in fact dissociating into two species, as opposed to simply
undergoing electronic recombination to the initial PFeIII-O- PFeIII. To summarize: solution-phase
hard X-ray measurements confirm our tabletop XUV measurements, and show that the majority
photoproduct is not PFeII/PFeIV=O. A manuscript describing these results is in the final states and
should be submitted in Feb 2026.

 Using the experience we gained at the hard X-ray source, we also teamed up with the
groups of Roseanne Sension and Jim Penner-Hahn at the University of Michigan to revisit
competing explanations about the photochemistry of a simpler porphyrin: iron(III)
tetraphenylporphyrin chloride (FeTPPCl). This prototypical photocatalyst was first described as
relaxing to an intermediate-spin metal-centered excited state that lasted for approx. 20 ps. In early
work from the Vura-Weis group (before this DOE Catalysis grant), we had use XUV transient
absorption spectroscopy to instead claim that after initial excitation of a π-π* state, a LMCT state
formed in 20 fs, which then relaxed to a vibrationally-excited ground state in ~1.3 ps. We claimed
that this hot ground state masqueraded in the transient UV/Vis spectrum as the metal-centered
state. This claim had serious implications for the molecule’s photochemistry: a 20 ps excited state
could plausibly react with neat substrates, but a 1 ps excited state followed by a hot ground state
would be photocatalytically inert. In the new study as part of this grant, we used femtosecond x-
ray absorption, emission, and scattering to show that the majority of photoexcited molecules do
relax on the 1-ps timescale, but approximately 30% are trapped in an LMCT state for tens of
picoseconds. This observation reconciled all previous studies of the photochemistry of this
molecule (including Raman experiments not described here) into a unified picture of the excited-
state dynamics, and highlighted the need to consider excited-state branching pathways in even
relatively simple molecules (J. Phys. Chem. A 2025, 129, 49, 11269–11280)

 In addition to these studies of 3d transition metals, we initiated collaborations with Tom
Teets at the University of Houston and TJ Rohrabaugh at Army Research Labs to use XUV
spectroscopy to measure the electronic structure of Ir photocatalysts. We showed that we the
5p,4f5d transitions in this energy region can measure the absolute position of the eg and t2g
orbitals, which are important for understanding and predicting redox potentials. By measuring a
series of Ir complexes with weak and strong-field ligands we directly related the energy of these
transitions to the ligand field strength, which is difficult to measure using traditional techniques
(J. Phys. Chem. A 2022, 126, 50, 9510–9518)
