Reason, Retrieve, Re-rank: A Zero-Shot Reasoning-Aware Framework for Composed Video Retrieval
Ali Alavi

Abstract
Composed Video Retrieval (CoVR) seeks the target video that results from applying a free-form textual modification to a reference video. We address the Reason-Aware CoVR (CoVR-R) challenge at the CVPR 2026 VidLLMs workshop, where retrieval is strictly zero-shot. We present R3-CoVR ( Reason, Retrieve, Re-rank ), a training-free pipeline built entirely from frozen foundation models. A multimodal large language model (Qwen3-VL-8B) reasons about the after-effects an edit implies—state transitions, action phases, scene, camera and tempo—and verbalises a concise post-edit description; a contrastive video–text encoder (SigLIP-2) embeds this description and the gallery for first-stage retrieval; finally a constraint-aware re-ranking stage uses the same multimodal model as a judge that scores each shortlisted candidate against the intended edited result. On the challenge test set, R3-CoVR attains 91.9% R@1 and 98.2% R@10 . Two findings drive these results: (i) matching the description length to the contrastive encoder’s text window lifts R@1 from 67.5 67.5 to 72.7 72.7 ; and (ii) the constraint-aware re-ranker, which reorders only the shortlist, lifts R@1 from 72.7 72.7 to 91.9 91.9 —the single largest gain. We analyse the re-ranker’s behaviour, the retrieve/re-rank blend, and the shortlist depth, and we release a clean three-layer implementation.



1
Introduction

Composed Video Retrieval (CoVR) generalises text-to-video retrieval with a
visual anchor: given a reference video
V_{r}
and a modification instruction

E
, a system must retrieve, from a large gallery, the target video that
reflects the requested compositional change while preserving the relevant
context of
V_{r}

covr2024
. The task underpins content-based video
recommendation, intelligent editing, and creative search.

The CoVR-R challenge at the CVPR 2026 VidLLMs workshop sharpens the problem
along two axes. First, retrieval is
zero-shot
: models may not be trained
or tuned on the challenge data, so the gallery must be matched by
generalisation rather than memorisation. Second, the benchmark is

reasoning-aware
: edits are written so that their visual consequences are

implied
rather than stated. “Change typing to frustration” implies
clenched fists and a closing laptop; “make it a close-up” implies tighter
framing and shorter duration. Hard distractors that share surface vocabulary
with the edit but violate its implied consequences defeat naïve keyword
matching
covrr2026
.

These constraints point away from trained fusion networks and toward a

verbalize-then-match
strategy, as established for composed
image

retrieval by training-free, language-driven methods such as
CIReVL
cirevl2024
and CoTMR
cotmr2025
. We adopt this view for
video and push it further with an explicit re-ranking stage. Our system,

R3-CoVR
, has three stages (Fig.
1
):

1.

Reason.
A frozen multimodal LLM watches
V_{r}
and reasons about
the after-effects of
E
as a structured trace, then writes a concise
post-edit target description.

2.

Retrieve.
A frozen contrastive video–text encoder embeds the
description and every gallery video; cosine similarity yields a shortlist.

3.

Re-rank.
The multimodal LLM acts as a constraint judge, scoring
each shortlisted candidate on how well it realises the intended result; scores
are fused with the retrieval rank to reorder the head of the list.

Our contributions are: (1) a fully zero-shot, training-free CoVR pipeline that
attains
91.9\%
R@1 on the challenge test set; (2) the observation that

description length must match the contrastive text encoder’s token
window
, worth
+5.2
R@1; (3) a constraint-aware re-ranking stage that
contributes
+19.2
R@1, with analysis of the retrieve/re-rank blend, the
shortlist depth, and the judge’s score resolution; and (4) a reusable
three-layer (data / model / runner) implementation.

2
Related Work

Composed retrieval.

Composed image retrieval (CIR) fuses a reference image with a modification
text and was popularised by CIRR and FashionIQ; CoVR extends the setting to
video, with WebVid-CoVR providing large-scale weakly-supervised
triplets
covr2024
. Trained approaches learn a cross-attention fusion
module, e.g. the enriched-context, discriminative-embedding line on
Dense-WebVid-CoVR
bsecovr2024
.

Training-free, language-driven retrieval.

A complementary direction replaces the learned fusion module with language.
CIReVL
cirevl2024
captions the reference, lets an LLM rewrite the
caption under the modification, and retrieves with a frozen vision–language
encoder; CoTMR
cotmr2025
adds chain-of-thought, multi-scale reasoning.
These methods show that
language is a strong compositional interface
and
that training-free pipelines are competitive. The CoVR-R
baseline
covrr2026
brings this to video with a frozen Qwen3-VL that
reasons about edit consequences before matching.

Re-ranking.

Re-ranking a first-stage shortlist is standard in retrieval. In the
training-free composed setting, constraint-based re-rankers such as Soft
Filtering
soft2025
verify prescriptive and proscriptive textual
constraints to sharpen precision. R3-CoVR’s Stage 3 is a multimodal
realisation of this idea: the judge inspects the candidate
video
and
scores realisation of the edit, which is what most improves R@1.

3
Method

3.1
Problem setup

Given a reference video
V_{r}
and a modification
E
, rank a gallery

\mathcal{G}=\{V_{1},\dots,V_{N}\}
so the ground-truth target
V^{\star}
appears as
high as possible. We never train on challenge data; every model is frozen.

3.2
Stage 1 – Reason

A frozen Qwen3-VL-8B observes
V_{r}
(sampled at
1
fps) and is prompted to
emit a structured
after-effect
trace

R=\{\textsf{states},\textsf{actions},\textsf{scene},\textsf{camera},\textsf{tempo}\}

followed by a target description
D
, in a single generation. Reasoning about
consequences—rather than copying edit keywords—is what separates the target
from hard distractors. To stabilise the description we draw
K{=}3
samples
(self-consistency) and average their embeddings downstream. The trace also
serves as the human-legible
reasoning trace
required by the challenge.

3.3
Stage 2 – Retrieve

A frozen SigLIP-2 contrastive encoder maps text and video into a shared metric
space. Each gallery video is encoded from
8
uniformly sampled frames,
mean-pooled and
L_{2}
-normalised; the query embedding
q
is the normalised
mean of the description embeddings. Retrieval ranks
\mathcal{G}
by cosine
similarity
s(V)=q^{\!\top}v(V)
, excluding the query’s own reference, and
returns a top-
K
shortlist.

Description length matters.

The contrastive text encoder has a fixed token window (64 tokens for SigLIP-2).
A vivid, paragraph-length description is truncated, discarding salient
post-edit content. Constraining
D
to a
concise, front-loaded
caption
(
\leq 40
words) that names the changed subjects, objects and setting fits the
window and is the single most effective Stage-2 choice
(§
4.2
). The rich reasoning trace is retained separately for the
challenge’s reasoning metric.

3.4
Stage 3 – Re-rank

Stage 2 reliably places the target in the shortlist (R@10
{\approx}97\%
)
but not always at rank 1. Stage 3 reorders the shortlist with the same frozen
Qwen3-VL acting as a
constraint judge
: shown a candidate video, the edit

E
, and the intended description
D
, it returns an integer match score in

[0,100]
, normalised to
s_{j}\in[0,1]
. The final score blends the judge with
the retrieval rank,

s_{\text{final}}(V)=\lambda\,s_{j}(V)+(1-\lambda)\,\mathrm{rank}_{K}(V),

(1)

where
\mathrm{rank}_{K}(V)=(K-i)/K
for shortlist position
i
. The judge is
discriminative—most distractors receive
0
while true matches cluster near

1
—so the blend with the retrieval rank breaks ties and guards against the
occasional false positive. Re-ranking touches only the
K
shortlisted
candidates, bounding cost.

De-noising the judge.

A greedy judge emits only a handful of distinct scores, so a target and a
distractor frequently tie at the maximum and the tiebreak decides rank 1. We
average the score over
5
sampled generations, increasing the number of
distinct score values by an order of magnitude (
8\!\rightarrow\!86
in our
test pool) and resolving these ties.

4
Experiments

4.1
Dataset, metrics, implementation

The CoVR-R challenge spans two galleries: WebVid (2,679 clips) and
Something-Something-v2 (1,654 clips). The test split has
301
queries
(
4
WebVid,
297
SS2); each query is ranked against all videos in its
gallery. We report Recall@
K
for
K\in\{1,5,10,50\}
, the official metric;
the headline is R@1. Stage 1/3 use Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct; Stage 2 uses

siglip2-so400m-patch16-naflex
. All models are frozen and run on a
single A100 (40 GB). Gallery embeddings are computed once and cached; the
shortlist depth is
K{=}10
unless noted, and the blend is
\lambda{=}0.8
.

4.4
Implementation notes

The system is organised in three layers—
data
,
model
, and

runner
—behind stable interfaces, with factory-instantiated encoders
and reasoners, so a backbone can be swapped without touching the orchestration.
Gallery embeddings are cached once per encoder; per-query reasoning and judge
scores are cached and resumable. First-stage retrieval over both galleries and
reasoning over all
301
queries complete in well under an hour on one A100;
re-ranking the top-10 adds
{\sim}2
s per query.

4.5
Discussion and limitations

Three properties make R3-CoVR practical. It is
training-free
: every
component is a frozen, publicly available model, so there is no risk of
over-fitting to the challenge data and the pipeline transfers directly to new
galleries. It is
interpretable
: the Stage-1 trace and the Stage-3 judge
rationale expose
why
a video is preferred, which the challenge also
rewards through its reasoning-trace metric. And it is
modular
: stronger
encoders or judges drop in behind the factory interface.

Several limitations point to future work. (i) The re-ranker’s cost grows
linearly with the shortlist depth
K
and with the judge’s per-candidate
latency; very large galleries would benefit from a cheaper first pass or a
cascaded judge. (ii) The judge inherits the backbone’s perceptual biases; the
residual gap between R@1 (
91.9
) and the R@10 ceiling (
96.8
–
98.2
)
is dominated by a handful of fine-grained pairs that an
8
B judge scores
identically, and a larger judge (e.g. a
72
B variant) is the most direct way
to close it. (iii) The contrastive encoder’s
64
-token text window bounds how
much of the reasoned description can inform first-stage retrieval; a
long-context or video-native retriever would relax this and likely lift the
shortlist recall that upper-bounds the re-ranker. (iv) Self-consistency and the
de-noised judge trade compute (3
\times
generation, 5
\times
scoring) for
robustness; the trade-off is favourable here but should be revisited at scale.

5
Conclusion

R3-CoVR shows that reason-aware composed video retrieval is well served by a
fully zero-shot, three-stage pipeline of frozen models: reason about the
edit’s after-effects in language, retrieve with a contrastive encoder, and
re-rank the shortlist with the same multimodal model used as a constraint
judge. Two simple but consequential choices—fitting the description to the
encoder’s token window, and re-ranking only the shortlist—take R@1 from

67.5
to
91.9
on the challenge test set, with the re-ranker contributing the
majority of the gain. The approach trains nothing, exposes interpretable
reasoning traces, and is straightforward to reproduce.
