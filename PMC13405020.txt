Strengthening patient safety in a Cambodian hospital through structured weekly reporting: a low-cost quality improvement initiative
Authors: Suren Kanayan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPersistent patient safety issues in low-resource hospitals are often underreported, delaying corrective actions and increasing preventable harm.ObjectiveThis project aimed to implement a low-cost, structured weekly reporting system to improve patient safety processes and outcomes across departments at a tertiary hospital in Phnom Penh, Cambodia.MethodsA one-page Departmental Wise Checklist was introduced in the emergency room/intensive care unit (ER), obstetrics and gynaecology, outpatient department and paediatrics. Weekly reviews, feedback loops and departmental champions supported consistent reporting and follow-through. Implementation emphasised planning, stakeholder engagement, iterative refinement through Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles and integration into routine workflows. Challenges including resistance to extra work, inconsistent submissions and escalation fatigue were addressed through simplified checklists, coaching and departmental champions, while oversight from the hospital quality improvement committee ensured accountability.ResultsAfter 3 months, process measures showed substantial change: formally adopted Standard Operating Procedures (SOP) increased from 0 to 10, Basic Life Support-trained ER staff rose from 38% to 98%, incidents of expired blood products fell from 2 per month to none and reporting compliance averaged 92%. Concurrently, patient-level outcomes demonstrated change: monthly cases of neonatal hypothermia decreased from 6 to 2 and ER adverse events dropped from 4 to 1.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that structured weekly reporting, combined with iterative feedback and active departmental engagement, may be associated with strengthening patient safety and accountability in low-resource settings. Long-term sustainability of these improvements is likely to depend on embedding the process into routine quality assurance activities, ongoing staff training and continued leadership oversight. However, sustainability beyond the 12-week intervention period has not yet been empirically assessed. The approach may provide a practical model for consideration and adaptation in similar low- and middle-income country hospitals, subject to local context and resources.Keywords: Patient safety, Quality improvement, Communication

Problem

Persistent patient safety challenges were observed in a tertiary hospital in Phnom Penh, Cambodia. These included expired blood units in the emergency department, inconsistent clinical documentation and communication gaps during shift handovers. At baseline, safety reporting was informal and unstructured, resulting in limited visibility for leadership into systemic vulnerabilities.

The hospital serves a diverse urban patient population, with multiple high-acuity departments including emergency/intensive care unit (ICU), obstetrics and gynaecology (OB/GYN), paediatrics and outpatient services. Staff numbers are constrained and workflows are often stretched due to patient volume and limited resources.

The focus of this project was to implement a low-cost, sustainable mechanism for frontline staff to report patient safety concerns, ensure timely escalation and track follow-through.

The SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound) aim was to achieve >90% structured safety reporting compliance and a ≥50% reduction in key adverse events (expired blood products, neonatal hypothermia) within 12 weeks. Success with this aim might enable leadership to gain actionable insights, support accountability mechanisms and potentially contribute to patient safety culture.

Background

Patient safety is a fundamental component of healthcare quality, yet in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) formal reporting mechanisms for safety incidents are often absent or inconsistently applied.1 2 This results in underreporting of adverse events, delayed corrective actions and ongoing preventable harm to patients. Evidence from LMIC hospitals indicates that recurring safety risks—such as expired blood products, inconsistent documentation and communication breakdowns during handovers—frequently remain undetected without a structured reporting system.2 3

Previous studies have demonstrated that structured reporting interventions can improve the recognition and management of safety hazards. For example, Pronovost et al4 showed that implementing standardised reporting and feedback in intensive care settings reduced catheter-related bloodstream infections and improved adherence to care protocols. Herzer et al5 reported improvements in patient safety metrics through multidisciplinary reporting teams combined with ‘Good Catch’ awards, highlighting the importance of feedback and staff engagement. Evidence also suggests that combining structured reporting with departmental ownership, iterative review and feedback loops may be important for sustainable improvement.6 7

Attempts to improve patient safety in resource-limited settings face specific challenges. Kruk et al3 and Pichumani et al2 emphasise that incomplete reporting, lack of ownership and absent feedback loops may diminish the impact of interventions. Staff may lack time, experience or incentives to submit reports, and leadership may have limited capacity to monitor systemic vulnerabilities. Without mechanisms to capture and act on safety data, hospitals risk persistent process failures, including delays in addressing expired medications or blood products, inconsistent implementation of SOPs and preventable adverse events.

Few published reports from Southeast Asia describe sustainable, low-cost mechanisms for real-time safety reporting integrated into hospital governance structures. In the context of a tertiary hospital in Phnom Penh, Cambodia, these challenges were evident: safety incidents were recorded ad hoc, and there was no structured mechanism to escalate or resolve issues. This gap limited leadership oversight and hindered systematic process improvement.

Structured weekly reporting using a simple, one-page departmental checklist has been proposed as a low-cost, feasible intervention to capture actionable safety information, support accountability and provide a platform for iterative improvement.4 5 By combining standardised data collection, departmental champions and governance oversight, hospitals may potentially improve team communication, support adherence to SOPs and contribute to reduction of preventable adverse events. Evidence indicates that these strategies, when implemented with ongoing feedback, may yield measurable improvements in both process and patient-level outcomes even in low-resource hospital contexts.6 7

Design

The project team included department heads, nursing leads, quality assurance staff and laboratory personnel, representing the multidisciplinary perspectives essential for designing a feasible and effective intervention. The goal was to create a standardised, low-cost mechanism for frontline staff to report patient safety issues, trigger timely corrective actions, and monitor follow-through. Simplicity and clarity were prioritised, as complex tools could hinder adoption in a busy, resource-constrained environment.

A one-page Departmental Wise List (a structured checklist for each department) was developed, combining tick-box items with optional short notes to capture urgent, ongoing, or deferrable concerns across patient care, documentation, staffing and equipment. Weekly submission to the hospital quality improvement (QI) Committee was standardised to ensure governance oversight and accountability.

To assess feasibility and usability, a 2-week pilot was conducted in the ER/ICU (ER/ICU). Feedback from frontline staff was used to refine wording, reduce reporting burden and optimise submission timing.

Sustainability was ensured through multiple mechanisms: embedding the Wise List into routine departmental workflows, appointing departmental champions to maintain momentum and provide ongoing coaching, and establishing regular oversight by the hospital QI Committee. Departmental champions were given clear responsibility for escalating unresolved issues and ensuring follow-through on assigned actions. This governance structure provided ethical guidance and operational accountability, reinforcing a culture of safety and continuous improvement. Process mapping illustrated the flow of information from Wise List completion to committee review, showing how the intervention streamlined reporting, improved interdepartmental communication and accelerated corrective actions.

The intervention required approximately one staff hour per department per week for data entry and review, with all documentation completed electronically in line with the hospital’s green initiative. Weekly QI committee meetings lasted about 30 min, representing minimal additional cost.

Strategy

The overarching aim of the project was to implement a structured, low-cost weekly reporting system to improve patient safety and accountability across multiple departments, including the ER/ICU, OB/GYN, paediatrics and outpatient department (OPD). To achieve this, a series of Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles were employed, iteratively refining the intervention based on real-world feedback.

PDSA cycle 1

The Departmental Wise List was drafted, and department leads were oriented to its purpose and use. The Wise List was piloted in the ER/ICU for 2 weeks. Observations and staff feedback revealed ambiguous items, barriers to timely submission and concerns about workload, which limited engagement.

PDSA cycle 2

The Wise List was simplified. Tick boxes were emphasised, free-text responses minimised and brief coaching sessions clarified its intent and use. Weekly submission compliance improved, though early submissions remained inconsistent, averaging 75% during the pilot.

PDSA cycle 3

Implementation was expanded to OB/GYN, paediatrics and OPD. Weekly QI committee meetings provided oversight, classified reported issues, assigned ownership and tracked resolution. A significant challenge emerged: escalation fatigue, as recurrent issues persisted without resolution. To address this, the departmental champions introduced in the Design phase took on enhanced responsibility for follow-up and accountability to ensure issues were resolved or escalated appropriately. Reported items were categorised by urgency to reduce data overload and focus attention on high-priority patient safety concerns.

Continuous review of performance data, frontline feedback and iterative refinements ensured that actionable safety concerns were systematically addressed. Overall, this strategy strengthened engagement, enhanced reporting compliance and fostered a culture of accountability. These experiences provide practical guidance for other low-resource hospitals seeking to implement similar QI interventions.

Results

Process and patient-level outcomes after three months

Over the 12-week intervention period, concurrent with the implementation of the weekly reporting system, substantial changes were observed in both process measures and patient-level outcomes. At baseline, no formal SOPs were in place, only 38% of ER staff were trained in BLS, and reporting compliance was negligible.

By week 12, formal SOP adoption increased from 0 to 10 across the ER, OB/GYN and other departments, reflecting structured process improvements and better standardisation of care. BLS training coverage in the ER showed a substantial increase from 38% to 98% after retraining 24 staff members, demonstrating enhanced readiness for emergency situations. Departmental compliance with weekly checklist submissions averaged 92% over the 12-week period, indicating successful integration of structured reporting into routine workflows. Interdisciplinary huddles were established in all departments, providing forums for reviewing safety issues, assigning responsibility and accelerating problem resolution. These results suggest that a low-cost, structured weekly reporting system—combined with iterative feedback, departmental champions and oversight by the hospital QI committee—was associated with measurable changes in both process and patient safety outcomes in this low-resource hospital setting. Integration of quantitative metrics, visual run charts and narrative documentation of challenges reinforced accountability, encouraged continuous improvement and facilitated timely resolution of safety issues. Interdisciplinary huddles played a key role in this process, fostering better communication and rapid problem-solving across departments. The structured SOP implementation, along with optimised lab and clinical protocols, showed maintenance throughout the intervention period. Frontline staff reported improved communication and faster resolution of issues during weekly huddles.

Lessons

This project suggests that structured weekly reporting was associated with fostering transparency, accountability and measurable changes in outcomes even in a low-resource hospital setting. Narrative documentation of challenges, iterative adaptations and clear assignment of ownership were important components of the intervention’s effectiveness, alongside quantitative metrics. Feedback loops, supported by departmental champions, were critical for timely follow-through, while governance oversight by the hospital QI Committee maintained ethical rigour and operational accountability.

Several key lessons emerged: (1) Engaging front-line staff in checklist design increased buy-in and facilitated integration into routine workflows. (2) Iterative refinement through pilot testing and PDSA cycles allowed identification and resolution of practical barriers, such as ambiguous items, submission delays and workload concerns. (3) The role of departmental champions in ensuring follow-up and accountability was critical to sustainability, particularly in addressing escalation fatigue and maintaining engagement throughout the intervention period.

Limitations

This project has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings:

Short-term evaluation and sustainability assessment

The evaluation period of 12 weeks is insufficient to assess long-term sustainability of the intervention. While we have implemented systems intended to support durability (embedding in routine workflows, ongoing staff training, leadership oversight), empirical assessment of sustainability beyond the initial 12-week period has not yet been conducted. The improvements observed may not persist once external evaluation attention diminishes or if leadership commitment wavers. Long-term sustainability depends on continued embedding of structured reporting into routine quality assurance processes, consistent leadership oversight and ongoing staff training. Going forward, sustainability will be monitored through quarterly audits of reporting compliance, semiannual assessment of SOP adherence and annual review of patient-level outcomes for at least 12–24 months. This extended evaluation will provide empirical data on whether improvements are sustained beyond the initial intervention period.

Reporting bias and data source limitations

The intervention relied substantially on departmental self-reporting through the weekly Wise List. Several sources of bias should be acknowledged: staff may have been motivated to report favourably due to awareness of the evaluation, or conversely, may have underreported concerns due to concerns about consequences. The accuracy of self-reported data was not independently verified for all metrics. While we cross-checked checklist submissions with available departmental records and incident logs, this validation was not comprehensive for all reported items. Self-reporting of incident frequencies may reflect changes in reporting behaviour rather than true changes in event occurrence. Data from incomplete sources (eg, blood bank logs that may not capture all expired units) may not reflect the true baseline frequency of safety issues.

Absence of formal assessment of balancing measures

This evaluation did not formally assess balancing measures, including staff workload, reporting burden, time spent on the weekly reporting process or impact on clinical staff satisfaction and burnout. This is a critical limitation. While we collected anecdotal observations that staff reported improved communication and faster issue resolution during informal feedback, formal measurement of staff satisfaction, perceived burden of the reporting process or impact on clinical workload was not conducted. Reporting systems that reduce patient safety events but increase staff burden are not sustainable. Plans to address this gap in future cycles include formal survey-based assessment of staff satisfaction with the reporting process, measurement of time spent on weekly reporting and evaluation of perceived impact on workload using validated instruments to assess clinician burnout and job satisfaction.

Limited scope of patient-level outcomes

Patient-level outcomes were restricted to selected, measurable events (neonatal hypothermia, expired blood units, ER adverse events). Broader assessments of morbidity, mortality, patient satisfaction or impact on quality of life were not conducted. These specific metrics were chosen for feasibility and measurability in a resource-limited setting but do not capture the full spectrum of patient safety impacts. The selected outcomes may reflect only a portion of the safety improvements achieved by the intervention.

Single-site design and limited generalisability

The project was conducted in a single tertiary hospital in urban Phnom Penh with specific staffing levels, patient populations, infrastructure and resources. Findings may not be directly transferable to other hospitals with different contexts, levels of baseline resources, staff capacity, patient populations, geographic location or healthcare systems. Replication in other settings will require careful adaptation to local context. Regional evidence supports similar approaches in Southeast Asia, showing that structured reporting combined with feedback and departmental ownership may improve patient safety and care quality.2 4 5 8 9

External confounding factors

External factors such as staff turnover, variation in departmental workloads, seasonal variation in patient volume or concurrent changes in hospital policy, staffing or leadership priorities may have influenced results independently of the intervention. These factors were not systematically controlled for or measured and may have contributed to the observed improvements.

Conclusions

This project demonstrated that implementing structured weekly reporting, supported by narrative documentation of planning, challenges and iterative adaptations, was associated with observable improvements in patient safety and accountability in a low-resource hospital setting. Our SMART aim—to introduce a low-cost, sustainable reporting mechanism that improves process compliance and patient-level outcomes—showed progress: formal SOP adoption increased from 0 to 10, BLS training coverage rose from 38% to 98%, expired blood units were eliminated, and neonatal hypothermia and ER adverse events declined.

The measures selected were appropriate to capture both process and patient-level outcomes, and the use of departmental champions, iterative PDSA cycles and weekly QI oversight ensured follow-through and sustained engagement during the intervention period. The intervention was low-cost, requiring minimal additional resources and resulted in potential indirect financial savings by reducing waste from expired blood products and improving operational efficiency.

However, important limitations must be acknowledged: long-term sustainability has not been empirically assessed, formal evaluation of balancing measures (staff workload, satisfaction, burnout) has not been conducted, and generalisability to other settings is uncertain.

Future steps include: (1) ongoing monitoring of reporting compliance and patient-level outcomes for at least 12–24 months to assess sustainability; (2) quarterly audits of SOP adherence and semiannual review of patient outcomes metrics; (3) formal assessment of balancing measures including staff satisfaction using validated surveys, perceived workload impact and reporting burden; (4) evaluation of staff burnout and retention patterns; (5) embedding reporting processes in departmental Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) to ensure accountability and (6) expanding the intervention to other hospital units with context-specific adaptation. Further study should explore broader patient-level outcomes, cost-effectiveness analysis, specific sustainability mechanisms and applicability in hospitals with different resource levels and patient populations.

Embedding structured safety reporting within national hospital accreditation frameworks could strengthen patient safety culture across Cambodian hospitals and other LMICs, providing a practical model for consideration and adaptation in similar settings, subject to careful evaluation and local context assessment.
