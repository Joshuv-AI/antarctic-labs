ABSTRACT
Nuclear weapon component assessment tests at the Z Machine rely on accurate X-ray yield
measurements for model validation, design and analysis of component survivability, and source
optimization. Yield measurement devices currently used do not provide the requisite certainty to
enable accurate and efficient data analysis of test results, which leads to an increase in Z-shots
required for evaluation, longer device development times, and higher operation costs. Additionally,
current X-ray flux detectors lack high temporal resolution at the tails. Similarly, shock measurement
techniques used for component assessment often lack the ability to spatially resolve wave behavior
thereby limiting the ability to measure shock propagation dynamics needed to design the next
generation of ND components. Here we present the development of a photonic micro-calorimeter
and shock sensor consisting of meter-long waveguide spirals that are optomechanically coupled to
X-ray absorbing layers to characterize yield and shock propagation at nanosecond timescales.

1. MOTIVATION
Nuclear weapon component assessment tests at the Z Machine rely on accurate X-ray yield and
power measurements for model validation, design and analysis of component survivability, and
source optimization. X-ray yield is most reliably calculated from measured temperature changes or
mechanical expansion of X-ray absorbing metals, where the temperature change or material
deformation is proportional to the amount of X-ray absorption. Thermal calorimeters currently in
operation use a thermocouple placed in contact with a target tantalum plate to measure the
temperature increase in the plate. While inexpensive and easy to use, thermocouples suffer from
cold junction compensation errors due to temperature variations at the reference junction and are
susceptible to metallurgical and aging imperfections which introduce uncertainty in calibration and
measurement. Moreover, thermocouple response is limited to millisecond timescales (at best)
making them unable to characterize shock propagation or thermal dynamics. An alternative, contact-
less, measurement system uses optical interferometry to measure thermo-elastic expansion of the
absorbing plate. These thermo-elastic calorimeters focus a laser beam on to a small area of an
aluminum absorber and measure phase changes in the reflected beam due to mechanical expansion
allowing for measurements on the order of nanosecond timescales. While the optical readout
provides higher measurement sensitivity and enables limited temporal resolution of X-ray absorption
dynamics, the use of a single focused spot strictly prohibits measurement of any spatial shock
dynamics and cannot take advantage of optical resonance phenomena to further enhance sensitivity.
Given their size and cost, scaling the optical systems to include more focused beams is often not
feasible. An ideal X-ray yield sensor would be a thermo-elastic calorimeter with high measurement
sensitivity to mechanical changes and high temporal resolution provided by optical probing, all the
while being small and inexpensive enough to allow for the integration of multiple devices across a
test sample to spatially measure shock propagation dynamics. The proposed devices will consist of
integrated photonic devices in silicon (Si) or Silicon nitride (SiN) having a compact footprint (100
um2 to 5000 um2) that enable measurements with both high temporal and spatial resolution. It will
be a completely passive electrically, making immune to electro-magnetic interference. The device
platform will consist of CMOS-compatible materials and can be processed in high volume at
Sandia’s own fabrication facilities for a relatively low-cost per device. Furthermore, the device
architecture easily allows for a high degree of tailorability for optimizing temporal resolution, spatial
resolution, and the sensitivity of measurements for targeted applications.

2. DESIGN
As part of the LDRD, a non-resonant but highly sensitive temperature and strain measurement
technique would use an off-chip optical interferometer to measure transmitted power variations due
to phase changes on the interferometer test arm. The test arm would be routed to an on-chip
photonic spiral [1] that would sense induced strain via the thermo-optic and photo-elastic effects.
The spiral is the least sensitive structure to fabrication nonidealities since it consists of a
continuously running waveguide with no gaps, holes, or critically small features. This is at the
expense of an even larger footprint (up to 5000 um^2), but with the added benefit that a tunable
laser and resonance feedback loop is no longer required thereby simplifying the test and
measurement equipment. Additionally, the photonic spiral + interferometer technique can be easily
modified to accommodate temperatures and strains of large magnitude or be designed to operate
using an alternative fringe counting technique which enables an ultra-high dynamic range.
An improvement to the fiber interferometer application described above, forgoes all external
electronic controls by integrating the interferometer on chip. In this configuration, the MZI must be
designed so that a phase difference is generated when the sensor is exposed to temperature
variations or strain. This can be achieved in one of two ways: 1) adding an absorbing element on top
of one of the MZI arms that blocks incident x-rays from the other arm to ensure X-ray-induced
perturbations only affect one arm, and 2) purposefully designing the MZI so that the arms are highly
unbalanced. In this second arrangement, the longer arm will experience a greater phase shift, and the
sensitivity of the device is based on the how highly unbalanced the MZI is.

2.2. Low-confinement waveguides
In a MZI configuration, temperature and strain sensitivity are linearly dependent on the length of
the sense arm. Typical propagation losses for SiN waveguides can vary from of 0.1db/cm to 2
dB/cm depending on type of SiN deposited, laser wavelength used, and SiN thickness, which limit
the sensitivity. To-date, one of the more promising architectures that enable low propagations
losses, while maintaining ease of fab, is the high aspect ratio, low-confinement waveguide design [2].
For SiN waveguides in the visible and telecom wavelengths, the dominating source of propagation
losses is the sidewall roughness which scatters the coupled light out of the waevguide. This can be
verified by inspection (but also by rigorous analytical calculation) by considering the large overlap
between the optical mode and the sidewall in the “Standard” cross-sectional Finite Element Method
(FEM) mode simulation shown in Figure 3.

 Figure 3 Cross-sectional FEM simulations of waveguides for different configurations.
One approach to reduce the modal interaction with the rough sidewalls (lossy region) is to make the
waveguide wider. However, while waveguide losses are reduced, wide waveguides enable the
existence of higher order optical modes which results in another mechanism for loss. In order to
maintain the fundamental mode in a multi-mode waveguide geometry, turns must consist of large
bend-radii, and any tapering (for example for edge coupling) has to be very slow, resulting in a much
larger footprint than is usually desirable. Additionally, PIC elements (like directional couplers) are
difficult to design in multimode geometries if only the fundamental mode is desired. In a similar
vein, a popular alternative is to make the waveguide very thick using a SiN Damascene process [3],
however this requires non-standard processing and was not considered for the project. A counter

 15

intuitive approach to reduce modal-sidewall interaction is to make the waveguide very narrow which
had the effect of “squeezing” the optical mode out of the waveguide core such that more of the
mode propagates in the cladding and away from the sidewalls. Although promising, this application
also requires large bends since the mode is now “weakly” guided by the SiN core. Additionally, the
target waveguide width for optimally narrow waveguides is close to the fabrication limit, which can
be problematic for the very long waveguides we are targeting. The low-confinement architecture
implemented takes the best of both words: it widens the waveguide width to reduce modal-sidewall
overlap but maintains the single mode propagation scheme by using very thin nitride waveguide
layer which also squeezes the mode out. Propagation losses as low as 0.1dB/m [2], a 100x
improvement over other the “standard” geometry, have been reported for telecom frequencies. For
our application, and with the aid of FEM simulations (not shown), we determined the nominal
waveguide thickness to be 80nm, with 8µm of Silicon Dioxide (SiO2) to serve as top and bottom
cladding (16µm total). Because the mode is minimally confined, large waveguide tuns are
unfortunately still a requirement. To overcome this, we explored a new design that introduces an
etch near the waveguide to help “cradle” the mode and keep it confined during tight turns, shown in
axis-symmetric FEM simulation in Figure 4a. The high-index contrast cladding/air interface would
prevent the mode from radiating and enable turn with 10x smaller radii than otherwise expected.

  Figure 4 a) in axis-symmetric FEM simulation of a low confinement waveguide making a 200um
 turn. The simulation shows how the mode begins to radiate outwards (in the +x direction), away
from the waveguide, but also how a cladding/air interface 6um away can prevent the full radiation.
 b) Plot of multiple bend radius and interface distance configurations showing an extremely high
 quality factor, indicating minimal radiation losses, can be achieved.

The 2D axis-symmetric model is effectively modeling the cross section of an optical ring resonator,
as such, we can quantify any enhancements provided by the cladding air interface by calculating the
optical quality factor (Q-factor) associated with the geometry. The Q-factor values are plotted in
figure 4b and show a clear preferential geometry, bend losses can be minimized for a 300um bend
radius if a 750nm cladding spacer is added between the waveguide edge and the cladding/air
interface. It is important to note that this calculated Q-factor only considers losses from radiation
and not from imperfections (rough sidewalls, bulk and interface absorption, geometry variability)
otherwise present in physical systems which results in astronomically high Q-factor values.

3. FABRICATION
The low confinement devices were fabricated in Sandia’s MESA Facility. A variety of material
variations were explored in both the SiFab and the µFab. All of the calorimetry devices were
fabricated in the SiFab.

3.1. General Fab flow
The simplified fabrication steps and details for each step are outlined below.

 Figure 5 Schematic illustration of the fabrication steps required to make the low confinement
 spirals
 1. Start with a Single side polish silicon wafer.
 2. Deposit 8µm of SiO2. This step is more difficult than it seems. Nominally, 8µm thermal
 oxide would be deposited, however, the time it would take to achieve this thickness is
 prohibitive. As such, a combination of thermal, TEOS, and Silicon Oxynitride (SiON) were
 used.
 3. Deposit, pattern, and etch 80 nm of SiN for the waveguides.
 4. Deposit an additional 8µm of SiO2 using a combination of TEOS and SiON
 5. Pattern and etch isolating trenches.
 6. The isolation trenches were targeted to land on the Si handle to also allow for a release step.
 By exposing the wafer Xenon Difluoride (XeF2) gas, we can selectively remove the Si
 substrate to suspend the waveguide photonics. For calorimetry, this will reduce the thermal
 mass, so we get less thermal diffusion and a higher equilibrium temperature.

3.3. SiFab Revision 2
The second revision of devices abandoned the integrated trench around the spirals in favor of etch
pits which would still enable release from the substrate for thermal isolation, shown in the inset of
Figure 7a. The same layer stack and GDS color-layer identification scheme as in Revision 1 was
used. The holes were placed far enough, ~15µm, from the waveguide to minimize perturbative
effects from the evanescent optical field. Spirals with and without release holes were included as a
precaution to avoid yet-unknown processing non idealities associated with the release holes. Several

test devices to explore tight bends with trenches were still included in hopes that careful tuning of

Fabrication went smoothly and devices with the etch pits were successfully characterized. As will be
discussed in the Section 5, the optical propagation losses were about 100x larger than expected, thus

and so the sensors were integrated with fibers and packaged to be fielded at Z-machine and IBL.

3.4. SiFab Revision 3
The third design revision made two major improvements from the previous one: 1) The waveguide
input/output facets were routed to the same side of the chip, shown in Figure 8a. This would enable
easier packaging for fielding when using commercial off-the-shelf fiber array assemblies. 2) Periodic
regions in the release pit array were removed to create “anchoring” posts, shown in Figure 8b. By
removing several etch pits we ensured that the isotropic XeF2 cavity forming from one pit would
not reach the cavity from another pit..

Additionally, in an attempt to identify the source of the large propagation losses measured, the third

While optical loss due to absorption from charge centers at the SiN-oxide interface is known [4-5],
little was found to have been reported about using SiON or about t light interactions at the SiN-
SiON interface. Since the SiN-based low-confinement material stacks presented in literature do not
use SiON, we hypothesized that the higher propagation losses being measured could be attributed to
unknown interactions at the core/cladding interface. As such, in the wafers processed as part of
Revision3, the SiON layer was omitted in the bottom cladding first on a set of wafers, and
completely omitted when processing the remaining. If our hypothesis were correct, that the
interfaces between SiON, the SiN, and the surrounding oxide are the sources of excess loss, then we
should be able to measure lower propagation losses when less SiON is present.

3.4.1. Revision 3 in the µFab (bonus stack)
While growing 8µm of thermal SiO2 in the Si fab was time-prohibitive, we were given the
opportunity to realize the material stack shown in Figure 5 (with homogeneous oxide layers) in the
uFab by having a dedicated wafer ride along all thermal SiO2 growths over the course of a few
months to build the requisite 8µm thick under cladding. 80nm of LPCVD SiN was added on top the
the thermal oxide, patterned and etched using ebeam lithography, and finally capped with another
8µm of PECVD SiO2. The same mask designed for Revision 3 was used when making the PICs in
the uFab.

3.5. Absorber integration
To optimize X-ray absorption, we explored several attempts to incorporate a high-Z material (either
tungsten or tantalum were chosen) in contact with the sensor above the photonics.

3.5.1. Conductive Epoxy
The simplest way to integrate the metal absorber is by directly gluing a piece on to the sensor chip.
We chose to bond 0.5mm thick tantalum (Ta) pieces to the sensor using silver filled epoxy that is
thermally conductive and cures at around 100-200C. Frequency-Domain Thermoreflectance
(FDTR) measurements were made on the Ta-bonded samples revealing bond quality issues. The
subsurface map from FDTR at a 5 um depth is shown in Figure 10.

3.5.3. Aerosol Spray
An alternative approach to deposit the high Z-material was using the Additive Manufacturing
Aerosol Spray capabilities from org 1834. Unlike the gluing approach, where you can glue arbitrarily
thick high-Z materials, the aerosol spray is limited to depositions that are several hundred microns
thick. For the proof-of-concept test, we targeted 50µm of tantalum (Ta). The microscopically flat
SiO2 sensor surface (200-300nm of roughness as determined by a Keyence microscope surface
measurement) was roughened with 300, 600, and 1200 grit sandpaper to provide a proper bonding
surface. Purified Ta, P#1, AQ40319786 powder was used, and varying nozzle sizes (to control
particle speed) were explored, along with varying number of deposition passes, to obtain the target
film thickness. General deposition rates are around ~ 10 microns per pass, however, no Ta adhesion
was visible at 5 passes. A brute force test at 50 passes was performed with negligible amounts of Ta
being bonded to the top oxide, microscope images of the sample post deposition are shown in
Figure 12.

3.5.4. Electron Beam Deposition
The final approach explored to deposit high-Z materials is electron beam deposition in the uFab.
Unlike the previous two deposition approached, electron beam is the most limited in terms of
maximum material thickness due to high intrinsic stresses of the deposited films that lead to film
delamination. For tungsten, delamination will start to occur before 100nm thicknesses area achieved.
For the proof of concept, we targeted 50nm of tungsten. Deposition was a success with no issues,
unfortunately, the utility of such a thin layer of absorbing material is to be contemplated.

4. IN-LAB CHARACTERIZATION
For the Revision 1 design, the testing setup included two bare polarization maintaining (PM) fibers
coupled into the chip on either side, see figure 13a. A fiber-coupled, tunable 1550nm laser is routed
to polarization paddles to ensure the light is polarized along the slow axis of the polarization
maintaining (PM) panda fibers that couple light on to the chip. Optical modes aligned along the slow
axis of a PM fiber are more confined than the fast axis and also more robust against external
perturbations. To ensure the light coupled into the PIC waveguide is a Transverse Electric (TE)
mode, polarized horizontally along the plane of the chip’s surface chi, rotating fiber chucks were
employed to rotate the PM fiber to match the slow axis with the TE mode’s axis. Unfortunately, this
step is time-consuming and has a high probability for drift-related misalignments when curing the
epoxy that adheres the fibers to the chip. The misalignment tolerance can be less than one micron,
as such, any minor drift during curing can result in substantial or total coupling loss.

  Figure 13 a) Schematic of in-lab testing setup. b) Revision 1 chip design with red light coupling
into the chip using the fibers on opposite ends. c) Revision 3 chip design with same-side coupling
 method using a fiber array.

Design from the third revision, which incorporated a fiber array that enabled waveguide routing to
one side instead of two, replaced the rotating fiber chicks with one 6-degree-of-freedom fiber array
holder. The fiber array is loaded with PM fibers that are pre-aligned with the slow axis in the
horizontal position which makes this coupling method is much more efficient for in-lab testing.
Additionally, the larger size of the fiber array provides a greater surface area for gluing, which makes
the fiber+chip assembly more robust for fielding. When bonding the fiber array to the chip, the
fibers are not affected by the curing epoxy and stay in the position set beforehand. Testing of both
revisions uses NanoMax stages to precisely align the individual fibers or fiber array, with sub-micron
precision, to the device under test. The light goes into fiber 1 in the array (the input fiber), couples
into the device and couples out into fiber 2 (the output fiber), which is routed to a photodetector
whose signal (representing transmitted power) is then displayed on a recording oscilloscope. Because

 23

the operational 1550nm wavelength is invisible to the naked eye, coupling to the devices is initially
very difficult. To facilitate the first-light finding process, a laser fault finder at 633nm wavelength
(visible to the naked eye: red color) is implemented, as seen in Fig. 11b and c, which causes the
device to light up when roughly coupled. Transmission at 1550nm is optimized after first light, and
the fibers or fiber array is bonded to the chip and ready for the next step in packaging.

4.1. Propagation loss results
A target metric for success of the project is achieving low propagation losses (~1dB/m), as such
significant effort was placed on characterizing all waveguide spirals fabricated. Propagation losses
can be readily calculating by measuring the optical transmission of waveguide circuits with varying
length. The measured power transmission values, 𝑃𝑜𝑢𝑡, can be converted to decibel units by using

power exponentially as a function of length, are linear in decibel scale plots. As such, a linear fit, in
the form 𝑦 = 𝑚𝑥 + 𝑏, of the converted data will readily reveal the propagation losses by the slope (
𝑚) and insertion losses by the intercept (𝑏). The data obtained by averaging multiple die per wafer

more of the optical field into a region closer to the substrate, thereby increasing propagation losses.

4.2. Temperature Calibration
While the insertion losses and propagation losses were higher than expected, this does not preclude
the device from function as a calorimeter. Moreover, temperature and strain sensitivity can be
recovered by increasing the input power into the system by using higher power lasers or laser
amplifiers since sensitivity scales linearly with input power. As such, the fabricated devices’
temperature response was characterized at the University of Arizona using similar experimental
setup as that shown in Figure 13 to couple light on and off, while also incorporating more optical
and electronic components to characterize the devices; setup shown in Figure 15.

 .
Figure 15 Schematic outline of the experimental setup used to characterize temperature response
 of devices
In brief, the testing protocol consist of placing the fabricated sensor in one arm of a Mach-Zehnder
Interferometer (MZI), heating up the sample, and measuring the amplitude response at the output
of the interferometer due to thermo-optic phase changes imparted on the sensor. The
characterization setup comprised of a fiber MZI where on one MZI arm, light is coupled on/off the
chip using a testing configuration like that shown in Figure 15. The arms consist of control opto-

 25

electronic components to balance and lock the MZI which include: a fixed fiber-optic delay line for
coarse balancing, a variable delay line for fine balancing, and a fiber stretching for active balancing
and modulation. The element that combined the signal form the two MZI arms is a variable coupler,
which was selected to better balance powers and optimize the MZI visibility. The recombined signal
is directed to a photoreceiver (a photodetector with integrated gain), whose signal is split and routed
to an oscilloscope/DAQ for viewing/recording and also to a PI (Proportional, Integral) Servo
Controller. The PI-controller can lock the system to a specific transmission value by analyzing the
measured output MZI signal and generating compensating signals to actively drive the fiber stretcher
to the desired setpoint and correct for unwanted phase changes from external sources (such as
vibration on a fiber, room temperature changes, etc). A resistive heater is glued on to the spiral and
is controlled by an external DC power supply, and a thermocouple is placed on the sample to
measure temperature changes.
With this setup, there are two ways to characterize the temperature response:
 • Increase the temperature by a small, known, amount and record the PI-controller’s
 correcting signal. From the manufacturer (and independent verification), the optical phase
 change per volt applied on by fiber stretcher can be known, and a simple calculation can tell
 you what the optical phase per degree (temperature), 𝑑𝜙/𝑑𝑇, value is. The caveat, however,
 is that this signal will be convoluted with all other correction signals from the environmental
 noise sources the PI-controller is trying to compensate for. As such, the only way this
 method work is if the temperature can be modulated at a rate that is faster than the fiber
 stretcher’s bandwidth (i.e. temperature changes faster than can be actively corrected for).
 • Alternatively, large temperature changes can be applied to the sample which will drive the
 signal across multiple interferometer fringes (as predicted by the transfer function defined in
 Section 2). In this scheme, the PI-controller is bypassed which requires the temperature rise
 to be faster than the characteristic timescale of the environmental noise sources that drive
 the interferometer across a fringe. Environmental related signal drifts are relatively low
 amplitude and slow. With repeated measurements, a reliable optical phase change per degree
 of temperature henge can be derived.
The fist method was inaccessible to us due to the limited bandwidth of the temperature sensors
available. What follows are the characterization results using the second method.

Figure 16a shows a close-up picture of the sensor with the external heater, thermocouple, and
input/output fibers. The heater has an integrated thermistor which serves to read the heater’s
temperature, while the thermocouple will measure the sample’s temperature. When the heater is
tuned on (green step function in Figure 16b), the sensor’s temperature begins to rise driving the
MZI output through multiple interferometer fingers, as seen by the purple trace. Figure 16c shows a
composite plot of an example test where the temperature data of a heating and cooling cycle is
shown in the blue line with corresponding values on the left y-axis, and the MZI transmission signal
in orange with its corresponding y-axis on the right side. From these measurements we, determined
the sensor’s temperature response to be 5.5 fringes per degree C or 34.56 𝑟𝑎𝑑 / 𝐶° for a 0.5m long
spiral. In a fielding setting, if we used a New Focus Fiber-Optic Receiver (Model: 2011-FC) with a
NEP of 0.19 𝑝𝑊/ 𝐻𝑧, we’d expect a minimum detectable signal of 19pW with the 10,000 kHz
electronic low pass filter enabled. If the max transmitted power were 5µW (a reasonable assumption
if the input laser power were 50mW, insertion losses were 10dB/ facet, and propagation losses were
20dB/meter, in agreement with the optical characterization data in section 4.1), then the
temperature change corresponding to a power shift on the order of the minimum detectable signal
can be calculated as follows:

5.2. Device Packaging
It is necessary to maintain robust control of the photonic integrated circuit (PIC) and optical fiber
mounting assembly during the X-Ray radiation event while fielding the devices at Z-Pulsed Power
Facility. As such, the packaging assembly must 1) allow for precise alignment between optical fibers
and PIC, 2) safeguard optical fibers from physical damage, 3) be small enough to fit in line-of-sight
enclosure and, 4) have a modular design to enable efficient swapping of the device under test. Like
the fabrication and PIC designs, the packaging assembly and components underwent multiple
revisions. The first revision of the device sub mount, which directly holds the photonic chip and is
shown in Figure 18a, facilitated optical fiber integration to the PIC, while minimizing the strain on
the fiber and chip assembly. Single fibers were routed to opposite ends of the chip to facilitate in-lab
characterization. However, this made the sensor harder to field. The redesign of both the on-chip
sensor and the device holder enabled the use of fiber arrays which streamlined the assembly process
and eliminated the necessity for polarization optimization, shown in Figure 18b. Revision 1 of the
sub mount utilized fragile bare fibers, a time-consuming fiber coupling process, and bare fibers were
aligned on opposite ends of the chip, which has a higher probability of breaking when fielding.
Revision 2 instead, utilizes robust fiber arrays aligned on one side of the chip and are pre-aligned for
optimal polarization, significantly cutting down the fiber coupling process. A base to secure the sub
mount to the line-of-sight port opening was designed to adhere to the tight spatial requirements of
the port. As seen in Figure 18c, the base design features a rail for flexible positioning of the sub

 28

mount and a removable filter frame. The aluminum base was revised to no longer have the filter
frame.

Figure 18 a) SolidWorks model of the first revision sub mount. b) SolidWorks model of the second
 revision sub mount c) SolidWorks model of the aluminum base and d) realized test setup.

Instead, a 3D-printed resin lid will be attached to the top of the chip. The filter will be attached to
the top of the lid instead of the filter frame it previously attached to. The fiber array connection to
the chip is very fragile and highly likely to break apart, the lid protects that bond. The lid (without
the filter attached) can be seen in Figure 18d. To facilitate cable management, a spool shield was
added which functions as a physical boundary to protect the fibers and the assembly against nicks,
bumps, or collisions when bolted into place, shown in Figure 18d. Once assembled, flange piece is
bolted into place at the Z machine. The package design allows for PICs to be easily interchanged,
and offer the structural integrity and support needed by PIC components to enable robust and
repeatable testing.
