PROJECT SCOPE AND OBJECTIVE
 The goal of this project was to develop a high caliber monitoring, reporting, and
veriﬁcation (MRV) technology for commercial use, that integrates, for the ﬁrst time, ground-
truthing data, remote sensing, and machine learning, with a mechanistic dynamic
vegetation ecosystem model (FATES) that simulates detailed carbon accounting to
substantially improve accuracy in forest-based carbon dioxide removal projects. There are
several shortcomings in existing MRV protocols for Improved Forest Management (IFM) and
re-/aƯorestation eƯorts that the technology prototyped here helped to ameliorate, and
reduce uncertainty, risk, and biases in the carbon market. In year one we developed and
tested the FATES-MRV prototype technology at real world carbon market projects. In year
two we advanced the technology-to-market milestones of improving CDR baselines,
predicting risk reversal, and conducting climate change projections at all testbed sites to
ensure commercial readiness.
 In this project, we focused on IFM and re-/aƯorestation eƯorts, which are the two
promising types of forest-based CDR (FbCDR) approaches that may lead to actual
sequestration as opposed to avoided loss. However, for accurate quantiﬁcation of forest
ecosystem carbon uptake, better approaches and tools are required to quantify forest
growth, soil carbon accumulation, and disturbance dynamics both in the presence of
carbon removal and in the baseline, as well as advanced MRV to ensure that carbon
removal is occurring. Novel tools that have been developed for global coupled carbon-
climate modeling, which include detailed process-based models of how ecosystems
respond to climate extremes, and which are informed by detailed remote sensing and other
datasets, may allow better quantiﬁcation of the risks and potential of FbCDR.

Project Objectives we Addressed:
 1. Improve carbon storage baselines
 2. Predict climate-change-dependent estimates of reversal risk
 3. Model carbon storage projections of IFM and re/aƯorestation FbCDR treatments
 4. Design a framework for a surrogate FATES-MRV tool to be piloted at testbed sites for
 technology readiness

BENEFIT TO DOE MISSION
This project developed a monitoring, reporting, and veriﬁcation (MRV) prototype
technology that combined ground-truthing data, remote sensing, and ecosystem carbon
modeling via the FATES model, now called FATES-MRV. This tool was piloted at three forest-
based carbon dioxide removal testbed sites where the technology was tested for accuracy
in MRV metrics, larger-scale deployment and market application. This tool will allow for
better quantiﬁcation of CDR potential over existing approaches via improved baselines of
carbon stocks and using dynamic baselines, including uncertainties, and predicting carbon
removal for multiple management strategies.

Beyond carbon accounting, the integration of remote sensing and predictive ecosystem
modeling provides actionable insight into forest structure, fuel accumulation, and

 5

disturbance risk. This capability supports improved understanding of wildﬁre dynamics,
enables scenario testing of fuel-reduction and management interventions, and contributes
to proactive wildﬁre risk mitigation strategies—helping reduce long-term suppression costs
and economic losses borne by taxpayers.
Furthermore, strengthening forest and soil carbon stocks enhances landscape resilience to
climate extremes, including drought and heat, which directly supports DOE priorities
related to energy resilience. By improving ecosystem stability and reducing the likelihood of
catastrophic wildﬁre events that threaten transmission infrastructure and energy systems,
FATES-MRV contributes to safeguarding grid reliability and reducing climate-related energy
disruptions. Collectively, this project supports DOE’s goals in carbon management, Earth
system predictability, wildﬁre resilience, and energy system security, while accelerating
scientiﬁcally credible pathways for scalable carbon dioxide removal.

COMMERCIALIZATION ADVANCEMENTS

MARKET ADVANCEMENTS
State of Market at Project Start vs. Completion
At Project Start (FY2023–2024): The voluntary and compliance carbon markets were
dominated by static, lookup-table–based MRV approaches for forest and soil carbon. Most
Improved Forest Management (IFM) and re/aƯorestation projects relied on regional default
baselines, infrequent ﬁeld inventories, and periodic remote sensing snapshots. Market
conﬁdence was weakened by public and regulatory scrutiny around over-crediting,
baseline inﬂation, and insuƯicient treatment of reversal risk (ﬁre, drought, storms).
At Project Completion (FY2026): Our tool and technology developed here, FATES-MRV
now occupies a distinct, high-value market position: a dynamic, mechanistic MRV platform
that quantiﬁes carbon stocks and ﬂuxes under both baseline and treatment scenarios,
explicitly models climate-driven reversal risk. This directly addresses the market’s top
unmet needs: credibility, defensibility to auditors and regulators, and scalability at
reasonable cost.
Market Advancements: The FATES-MRV tool plus remote sensing capabilities oƯers a
more scientiﬁcally defensible basis for forest-based CRD projects, because more
mechanistic carbon cycle modeling can reduce over-crediting risk. Supports evolving
standards around dynamic baselines and permanence, and more regulatory alignment,
and enables diƯerentiated credit pricing based on modeled reversal risk.

CONCLUSION

PATH FORWARD
 Building oƯ the application of FATES to the improved forest management projects
investigated in this project, the next steps are focused around two key areas: (1) increasing
conﬁdence in model projections of both baselines and IFM treatments, and (2)
simpliﬁcation of the procedures to allow non-expert users to apply the model to quantify
IFM treatments. To increase conﬁdence in model projections, we anticipate needing to

 9

apply the model to more IFM projects across a range of ecosystems, such as in the
Western US, as well as longer-term monitoring of existing IFM projects such as those
investigated as part of this project, to understand if projected short-term responses are
consistent with observations. To simplify procedures, we need to reduce parameter
uncertainty and streamline workﬂows to enable easier application of the model to a given
new site. The increased use of FATES in global and regional applications should lead to
reduced parameter uncertainty, as eƯorts to calibrate the model across scales in diƯerent
ecosystems further mature. When appropriate, we also aim to stay in touch with contacts
from Rubicon Carbon, Isometric, and CARB based on the initial connections discussed
above. Speciﬁcally, with the company Isometric, regarding their newly released certiﬁed
protocol for IFM projects, and staying in touch as they further reﬁne the scope of the
standard.

LESSONS LEARNED
 Through the course of this project we learned there are challenges and some
technical hurdles with deploying the FATES-MRV at CDR project-level scale. There is initial
onboarding complexity of the model and tool, which requires site data, model calibration,
and technical setup. The representation of improved forest management in the model led
to new parameters in the model, which necessitated further calibration and testing against
observations, alongside existing parameter uncertainty in the model such as those
governed by plant traits. For early-stage commercialization other competing platforms may
oƯer more polished user interfaces and enterprise integrations, but less model
sophistication, accuracy, and the multiple use modules that FATES-MRV oƯers. Finally,
expanding the social science co-production approach—engaging CDR project developers,
carbon registries, and on-the-ground forest practitioners in the joint design, testing, and
reﬁnement of the FATES-MRV machine-learning emulator—would ensure that scientiﬁc
rigor is maintained while the tool evolves into a more practical, transparent, and user-
friendly platform for real-world deployment.

Lidar data acquisition and ﬂight path planning

 On March 8, 2024, Lidar point cloud data was collected in the San Rafael region and
organized into four distinct zones (Fig. 1). The aerial (UAV) surveys were conducted using
the DJI L1 sensor, resulting in an average point density of 197.7 points/m² (Table 1). As
illustrated in Fig. 1, the spatial arrangement of these zones reﬂects a systematic approach
to aerial surveying, with yellow pinpoints indicating the take-oƯ and landing sites. Each
zone was strategically delineated to ensure comprehensive coverage of the region,
enabling detailed analysis of its unique environmental characteristics. It is important to
note that the Lidar point cloud data was initially processed by GLOBHE, which merged four
LAS ﬁles into one to encompass the entire area eƯectively.

Lidar Data processing
 All point cloud data acquired from the four UAV ﬂights were stored in LAS v1.2
format 3 and subsequently merged into a single LAS ﬁle to provide complete coverage of
the region. The ﬂowchart in Fig. 2 outlines the systematic process for processing Lidar point
cloud data. The initial step involves deleting duplicate points to maintain dataset integrity,
followed by the application of Statistical Outlier Removal (SOR) for denoising, which

 11

eliminates noise and erroneous points. Next, the points are classiﬁed using the Cloth
Simulation Function (CSF) to distinguish between ground and non-ground points,
facilitating a better understanding of the terrain and vegetation structure. Point cloud
normalization is then performed to standardize the non-ground point data, ensuring
consistent elevation and spatial references. The workﬂow progresses to generating a
Canopy Height Model (CHM) from the classiﬁed normalized points, which is subsequently
reﬁned through median ﬁltering (5x5 window) to smooth out anomalies. The ﬁnal output,
the Filtered CHM, provides an accurate representation of the vegetation structure.

Fig. 2 Flowchart of lidar point cloud process to generate Canopy Height Model.

Canopy size, tree height and above ground biomass mapping
 Following the generation of the Filtered Canopy Height Model (CHM) from the Lidar
point cloud data, the next steps involve creating maps for canopy size, tree height, and
tree-level aboveground biomass (Fig. 3). The process begins with individual tree detection
using the dynamic search algorithm applied to the ﬁltered CHM, which allows for the
identiﬁcation and delineation of individual trees, in the same time, to extract the tree
height. Subsequently, the crown size was derived from the segmented CHM using
Watershed algorithm. To estimate tree-level biomass, a power relationship model based on
height-to-biomass relationships is employed, utilizing ﬁeld measurements and allometric
equations derived from diameter at breast height (DBH). The model then was applied to the
detected trees to produce an aboveground biomass map at the tree level, oƯering insights
into the biomass distribution within the forest. This comprehensive workﬂow enables the
generation of detailed maps that are essential for improved forest management, ecological
assessments, and conservation planning in the San Rafael region.

Results
 The spatial distribution of tree crown size, height, and aboveground biomass in the
San Rafael region reveals signiﬁcant patterns and relationships among these three
ecological metrics. The histograms illustrate a common trend of right-skewed distributions
for both tree height and crown size, indicating that while many trees are relatively small,
there are a few signiﬁcantly larger specimens, which could play a crucial role in biodiversity
and habitat provision. As shown in Fig. 5, tree crown sizes vary markedly across the
landscape, the small crown size ( <37.4 m², Fig. 4) predominant in the region, which
corresponds to tree heights of below 22.9 meters (Fig. 6).
 The spatial distribution maps further enhance the understanding by visually
representing the heterogeneity of tree sizes and biomass across the landscape.
Aboveground biomass distribution illustrated in Fig. 9 supports the interrelationship among
crown, height and biomass. Regions with high crown sizes and tree heights show elevated
biomass levels, concentrated in the same areas where larger crowns and taller trees are
found. Conversely, areas with smaller crown sizes and lower tree heights correlate with
reduced biomass, indicating that these metrics are interconnected. The observed
variations in biomass across the landscape also highlight areas of ecological signiﬁcance,
e.g., potential hotspots for conservation eƯorts or regions that may require management
interventions to enhance forest resilience. Collectively, these ﬁndings emphasize the
importance of comprehensive forest assessments in informing sustainable management
practices and conservation strategies in the San Rafael Region.

Appendix C

The FATES (Functionally Assembled Terrestrial Ecosystem Simulator) model to
estimate the carbon and resilience beneﬁts of diƯerent forest management practices
in California

Brief: LBNL is adapting FATES to quantify the carbon and resilience outcomes of diƯerent
forest management practices.
FATES is uniquely positioned to address many of the carbon accounting issues found in
current oƯset protocols. For example, FATES[1] was designed to mechanistically track the
full ecosystem carbon cycle storage and ﬂuxes (through the soil, roots, all plant organs,
and back through litter and deadwood) in the presence of environmental competitive
conditions and climate change. In addition, FATES simulates the carbon response to a wide
variety of disturbances, and includes a detailed ﬁre behavior and eƯects model, based on
weather-driven ﬁre risk factors, fuel accumulation and ﬂammability, and ﬁre eƯects
mediated by plant traits such as bark thickness and ﬂame height. FATES has been
calibrated across a wide variety of environmental conditions; calibrated and tested in
managed forests under logging treatments; and extensive testing of transient climate
change such as evaluating the impact of the CO2 fertilization eƯect on forest growth
trajectories (Holm et al. 2020[2]; Needham et al. 2020[3]).

We have recently updated the wood logging module to include various Improve Forest
Management (IFM) treatments (e.g., selective logging, reduced impact logging, size-
speciﬁc logging, extended rotations) and track carbon sequestration potential following a
variety of management plans. By using a subset of forest inventory ﬁeld data, FATES can be
calibrated to predict the historical and current biomass stocks of forests in California, then
we apply future impacts of climate change to predict carbon permanence and any changes
in carbon estimates.

The adapted model can be used as a tool for planning forest management for multiple
goals including resilience and carbon storage in the context of climate change. It also can
serve as an alternative to carbon oƯset protocols to more accurately quantify the carbon
and resilience beneﬁts of forest management interventions. FATES has two important
advantages over current oƯset protocols:
Baselines: Inaccurate baselines have been the largest source of over-crediting from
improved forest management oƯset protocols to date. The model can project forward
dynamic baselines, alongside the IFM treatment, taking climate change into account as
well as diƯerent land management practices.

 23

Wildﬁre: It can also project forward disturbance from ﬁre and water stress in diƯerent land
management regimes which can be used to take future risk into account in estimating the
lasting beneﬁts of forest management changes.

 FATES has an active development team that is continually improving the model over time,
and can be used dynamically to capture changes in timber markets. At any point in the
lifetime of a project we can also adjust the model to account for changes to management
practices, and re-run new scenario predictions.
