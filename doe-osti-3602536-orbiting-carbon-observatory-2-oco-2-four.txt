1.0 Summary
 The Orbiting Carbon Observatory-2 (OCO-2) Science/Validation Team have deployed, operated, and
 maintained ground-based solar-viewing Fourier transform spectrometer (FTS) mobile laboratories at the
 Lamont, Oklahoma Southern Great Plains (SGP) observatory of the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE)’s
 Atmospheric Radiation (ARM) User Facility. The data is being used to validate space-based CO2 and CH4
 retrievals from a variety of different satellite instruments. In particular, the data collected by the
 instruments at ARM’s SGP site play a key role in validating data from the National Aeronautics and
 Space Administration (NASA)'s OCO-2, OCO-3, and Greenhouse Gases Observing Satellite
 (GOSAT)/Airborne Carbonic Oxides and Sulfide Spectrometer (ACOS) projects. As a result of repeated
 aircraft in situ profiling above SGP by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA),
 NASA, the National Science Foundation (NSF), and DOE, the activities at SGP enable data from the
 space-based sensor to be placed on the same absolute scale as the global in situ CO2 and CH4 network.
 The FTS measurements at the ARM site started in 2009 and are ongoing (as of August 2026).

 We request support from the ARM staff to continue operation of the FTS at SGP through 2029. The
 OCO-2 mission is expected to continue operations until at least September 2030.

2.0 Results
 The Lamont FTS instrument plays a central role in a large international network – The Total Carbon
 Column Observing Network (TCCON). TCCON is a network of ground-based FTS instruments that
 record spectra of the sun in the near-infrared. From these spectra, accurate and precise column-averaged
 abundances of atmospheric constituents including CO2, CH4, N2O, HF, CO, H2O, and HDO are retrieved.
 Details on TCCON can be found here: https://tccon-wiki.caltech.edu with site-specific details about the
 Lamont facility here: https://tccon-wiki.caltech.edu/Main/Lamont.

 The remote location of SGP has enabled relatively easy aircraft and ballooning operations, allowing
 NASA, NOAA, and NSF to repeatedly obtain profiles using in situ instruments over the FTS. These data
 (shown in Figure 1) have allowed us to place the remotely sensed columns from TCCON (and by
 extended comparison, satellite observations) onto the absolute World Meteorological Organization
 (WMO) calibration scale. Profile measurements have continued during the entire Lamont TCCON
 observation record, and have included profiles from NOAA aircraft and balloon-borne instruments
 (NOAA AirCore).

Figure 1. Integrated aircraft and balloon profiles of CO2 are compared with uncalibrated FTS
 remote-sensing retrievals. The determined scale factor, 1.00885, is used to place the FTS
 measurements on the same absolute WMO CO2 calibration as the global in situ network. In
 this figure, profiles obtained with NOAA (AirCore), NASA (Deep Convective Clouds and
 Chemistry [DC3], Studies of Emissions and Atmospheric Composition, Clouds and Climate
 Coupling by Regional Surveys [SEAC4RS]), and NSF (O2/N2 Ratio and CO2 Airborne
 Southern Ocean Study [ORCAS]) support are illustrated. The x-axis is a diagnostic quantity
 used to assess the operational state of the FTS; plotting the FTS/in situ XCO2 ratio versus this
 diagnostic allows us to assess the magnitude of systematic biases introduced by the state of
 the FTS.

The excellent viewing conditions and generous technical support by ARM personnel have allowed us to
obtain a mostly uninterrupted record of CO2 (and other greenhouse gases) at the SGP site (Figure 2).
These data have been used in numerous analyses both to understand the Earth’s carbon and hydrological
cycles and for evaluation of multiple space-based remote sensing products (see section 3). Such long-term
records are essential for this science as the carbon dynamics evolve slowly.

For OCO-2 in particular, Lamont is one of four ‘primary’ validation sites. This results in special focus on
obtaining data over SGP. For example, the OCO-2 spacecraft has a special mode of operation called
‘Target Mode’ where the observatory is slewed continuously as it flies over enabling thousands of
observations to be made over a dense 10 x 10 km2 region during a single orbit. These data together with
the simultaneous measurements from the FTS are used to calibrate the on-orbit data (Figure 3).

Data from the ARM site also plays a key role in the development of new OCO-2 data releases. Evaluating
space-based estimates of XCO2 against data collected at the ARM site provides information about the
quality, potential biases, and errors in the OCO-2 data record. It contributes to the derivation of
threshold-based quality filters and provides valuable information for correcting feature-based biases in the
space-based data (e.g., airmass- and albedo dependencies).

 Lamont. Only good-quality measurements are shown. Similar records have been obtained for
 methane (XCH4) and carbon monoxide (XCO).
