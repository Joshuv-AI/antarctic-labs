NCOA4 Accelerates Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm Formation by Regulating Macrophage Activation and Ferroptosis via STAT1–CH25H Axis

Zhinan Wu, Yuxuan Qian, Wenyi Xu, Fei Liao, Xueliang Liu, Xiao Kuang, Guqing Luo, Xinjie He, Lixian Jiang, Song Ding

Abstract

Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is a life‐threatening condition lacking effective pharmacological treatments, with emerging evidence highlighting the fundamental role of ferroptosis in its progression. Nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4), known to mediate ferritinophagy and contribute to ferroptosis, is upregulated in macrophages from both angiotensin II (Ang II)‐induced murine AAA models and human AAA tissues. Global deletion of Ncoa4 in mice suppressed Ang II‐induced AAA formation. Combined approaches using bone marrow transplantation and macrophage‐specific adeno‐associated virus (AAV) confirmed that Ncoa4 knockdown in macrophages alleviates extracellular matrix (ECM) degeneration, inflammation, and vascular damage. Mechanistically, NCOA4 interacts with signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) to co‐activate the transcription of cholesterol 25‐hydroxylase (CH25H), an enzyme that catalyzes the production of 25‐hydroxycholesterol (25‐HC). Elevated 25‐HC levels were detected in the serum of both Ang II‐induced AAA mice and human AAA patients, and they promoted macrophage activation and ferroptosis, thereby driving AAA progression. Importantly, knockdown of Ch25h using lipid nanoparticle‐loaded microbubbles (LNP/MBs) attenuated macrophage ferroptosis and AAA progression. These findings reveal a critical role of the NCOA4/STAT1/CH25H axis in AAA pathogenesis and offer promising biomarkers and therapeutic strategies for the disease.
Under pathological stimulation, NCOA4 in macrophages interacts with STAT1 to promote CH25H transcription. The subsequent increase in 25‐HC production activates macrophages, triggers inflammatory responses and ferroptosis, and accelerates the progression of AAA. Intravenous injection of SI‐Ch25h‐LNP/MB combined with localized ultrasound bursts can suppress CH25H expression in macrophages within the abdominal aortic wall, thereby offering a therapeutic strategy for AAA.

Introduction

Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is a life‐threatening vascular disease characterized by progressive focal weakening and pathological dilation of the abdominal aortic wall []. Despite its clinical significance, the precise etiology and pathophysiology of AAA remain poorly understood []. Most AAAs progress asymptomatically until rupture, making early diagnosis and prevention difficult []. The rising morbidity further exacerbates its substantial healthcare burden worldwide. Current treatment options are limited to surgical interventions, including open repair or endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR), which carry significant risks and are often unsuitable for small or moderate aneurysms []. Given the challenges in timely diagnosis and the lack of effective pharmacological therapies, it is urgent to develop reliable biomarkers and novel therapeutic targets for AAA management.

The pathogenesis of AAA involves a complex interplay of multiple factors, including advanced age, male gender, ethnicity, hypertension, metabolic disorders, platelet dysfunction, and systemic inflammation []. Macrophages play a pivotal role in AAA pathogenesis, driving disease progression at every stage []. Recruited by chemokines and damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), circulating monocytes infiltrate the vascular wall, differentiating into macrophages alongside resident populations []. Once activated, these macrophages augment vascular inflammation through pro‐inflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α, IL‐6, IL‐1β) and disrupt extracellular matrix (ECM) homeostasis by producing matrix metalloproteinases (MMP‐2, MMP‐9) and suppressing collagen synthesis []. Concurrently, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) and inflammatory mediators induce endothelial cell and vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) damage, promoting ferroptosis and other forms of programmed cell death that accelerate pathological vascular remodeling []. Growing preclinical and clinical evidence supports targeting macrophages as a promising therapeutic strategy for AAA, highlighting the importance of further mechanistic studies [, ].

NCOA4 (Nuclear Receptor Coactivator 4), also known as ARA70, is a multifunctional protein that has emerged as a key regulator of cellular homeostasis []. Numerous studies revealed that NCOA4's role in ferritinophagy involves selectively binding to ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1), facilitating ferritin degradation in lysosomes and releasing free iron, thereby maintaining intracellular iron homeostasis []. Pathologically, NCOA4‐mediated excessive iron release can lead to elevated ROS production via the Fenton reaction, ultimately inducing cell ferroptosis []. Beyond its role in ferritinophagy, NCOA4 exhibits multifaceted biological functions. NCOA4 was initially identified as a transcriptional co‐activator of the androgen receptor (AR), which participates in regulating transcription and DNA replication []. Subsequent studies revealed that NCOA4 regulates hematopoiesis through both transcription‐dependent and transcription‐independent mechanisms [, ]. Growing evidence highlights the critical involvement of NCOA4 in various cardiovascular diseases and demonstrates the therapeutic potential of targeting the NCOA4‐FTH1 interaction [, ]. However, the precise contribution of NCOA4 in AAA remains to be fully elucidated, necessitating further research to evaluate its potential as a therapeutic target for AAA.

In this study, we aimed to elucidate the role of NCOA4 in the pathogenesis of AAA. Our findings demonstrated, for the first time, that global Ncoa4 knockout or macrophage‐specific Ncoa4 deficiency attenuated AAA progression. Mechanistically, NCOA4 interacts with signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) to transcriptionally upregulate cholesterol 25‐hydroxylase (CH25H), thereby increasing 25‐Hydroxycholesterol (25‐HC) levels, augmenting inflammatory responses and ferroptosis in macrophages. Besides, targeting CH25H via lipid nanoparticle‐loaded microbubbles (LNP/MBs) in vivo significantly attenuated AAA progression. In summary, our study delineates a novel NCOA4/STAT1/CH25H axis in macrophages that drives AAA pathogenesis. Furthermore, our results identify 25‐HC as an emerging biomarker and suggest potential therapeutic approaches, which collectively could benefit future AAA diagnosis and management. (Scheme ).

Results

Macrophage‐Derived NCOA4 is Upregulated in AAA

To investigate the potential role of NCOA4 in AAA progression, we first analyzed its expression pattern in human AAA tissues and non‐aneurysmal control aortas. The individuals' baseline characteristics are summarized in Table . Western blot revealed significant upregulation of NCOA4 protein levels in AAA tissues compared to normal aortic samples (Figure ). However, RT‐qPCR analysis showed no significant differences in NCOA4 mRNA levels between groups (Figure ), suggesting post‐transcriptional regulation of NCOA4. This observation was further corroborated in a murine AAA model induced by angiotensin II (Ang II) infusion, where elevated NCOA4 protein expression was detected without corresponding changes in mRNA levels (Figure ). Immunofluorescence (IF) staining localized NCOA4 expression predominantly in CD68+ macrophages within the aortic wall (Figure ), implicating macrophages as the primary cellular source of NCOA4 during AAA pathogenesis. Consistent with this finding, Ang II‐stimulated primary peritoneal macrophages (PMs) exhibited increased NCOA4 protein level without alterations in Ncoa4 at the transcript level (Figure ), further supporting post‐transcriptional regulation of NCOA4 in AAA development.

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying this regulation, we performed immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry (IP/MS) in PMs (Figure ). Among the identified interacting partners, we focused on tripartite motif‐containing 3 (TRIM3), a member of the E3 ubiquitin ligase family known to participate in post‐transcriptional regulation []. Co‐immunoprecipitation experiments in both PMs and Human embryonic kidney 293T (HEK‐293T) cells confirmed the physical interaction between NCOA4 and TRIM3 (Figure ). Notably, TRIM3 co‐expression increased NCOA4 ubiquitination and down‐regulated NCOA4 protein expression in HEK‐293T cells (Figure ). Further investigation revealed significant downregulation of TRIM3 in both human and murine AAA tissues (Figure ). In vitro studies demonstrated that Ang II markedly decreased TRIM3 expression in PMs (Figure ), while treatment with N‐acetylcysteine (NAC), a ROS scavenger, effectively prevented Ang II‐induced TRIM3 downregulation (Figure ). These findings collectively suggest that Ang II promotes ROS‐mediated TRIM3 suppression, leading to decreased NCOA4 ubiquitination and subsequent protein accumulation in macrophages during AAA progression.

Global Ncoa4 Ablation Attenuated AAA Progression

Global Ncoa4 knockout mice were generated to investigate the functional role of NCOA4 in AAA pathogenesis. (Figure ). The successful knockout of NCOA4 was verified using PMs from Ncoa4
+/+ mice and Ncoa4
−/− mice (Figure ). Given NCOA4's established role in ferritinophagy and ferroptosis [], we first examined key markers and found that NCOA4 deficiency significantly upregulated ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1) protein levels, while glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) expression only showed an increasing trend (Figure ). Using an established Ang II‐induced AAA model (Figure ), we first confirmed that NCOA4 deficiency did not affect blood pressure and serum lipid profiles (cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL‐C, and LDL‐C) (Figure ). Of interest, Ncoa4 knockout significantly improved the survival rates of mice infused with Ang II (Figure ). The AAA incidence and the mean maximal abdominal aortic diameter were both down‐regulated in Ncoa4
−/− mice compared to littermate controls (Figure ). Histopathological analysis revealed that Ncoa4 knockout significantly ameliorated AAA progression; Hematoxylin‐eosin (HE) staining, Masson's trichrome staining, and elastic Van Gieson (EVG) staining all demonstrated significantly reduced aortic remodeling in Ncoa4
−/− mice, with reduced elastin degradation grade and maintained collagen content (Figure ). At the molecular level, NCOA4 deficiency attenuated the inflammatory response induced by Ang II, as evidenced by downregulation of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (Il1b, Il6, Tnf, and Ptgs2) in aortic tissues (Figure ). The infiltrated macrophages within the aortic wall in the Ncoa4
−/− group also decreased (Figure ). Furthermore, Western blot analysis showed reduced expression of matrix metalloproteinases (MMP2 and MMP9) in Ncoa4
−/− mice (Figure ), consistent with the observed reduced ECM degradation after Ncoa4 knockout. These findings collectively demonstrate that genetic ablation of Ncoa4 protects against Ang II‐induced aortic remodeling and blunts AAA progression.

NCOA4/STAT1 Complex Synergistically Modulates Macrophage Activation

To uncover the specific role of NCOA4 in macrophages, we performed RNA‐sequencing on PMs isolated from Ncoa4
+/+ mice and Ncoa4
−/− mice (Figure ). Consistent with its transcriptional regulatory function, Ncoa4 deficiency led to widespread suppression of gene expression, with 631 genes down‐regulated and only 107 genes up‐regulated in Ncoa4
−/− PMs compared to Ncoa4
+/+ PMs (Figure ). Bioinformatics analyses revealed that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were predominantly enriched in immune‐related pathways, including cytokine‐cytokine receptor interaction, antigen processing and presentation, and cell adhesion molecules (Figure ), suggesting that NCOA4 plays a critical role in Ang II‐induced macrophage activation and inflammatory responses. In vivo experiments further confirmed that NCOA4 deficiency diminished Ang II‐induced macrophage activation. Key DEGs such as Arg1, Ccr5, Irf7, Thbs2, Ccl5, and Gdf3 were all down‐regulated in Ncoa4
−/− PMs (Figure ), which were all related to macrophage activation and inflammatory response, supporting the conclusion that NCOA4 transcriptionally regulates macrophage activation.

Given NCOA4's role as a transcription co‐activator, we conducted transcription factor predictive analysis on the RNA‐seq data and identified the signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) family as potential partners of NCOA4 (Figure ). Revisiting our IP/MS data, we found that STAT1 physically interacts with NCOA4. This interaction was validated by co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) assays in both PMs and HEK‐293T cells overexpressing Flag‐NCOA4 and HA‐STAT1 (Figure and Figure ). To further elucidate the mechanism, we employed Cleavage Under Targets and Tagmentation (CUT & Tag) for NCOA4 in PMs from Ncoa4
+/+ mice and Ncoa4
−/− mice (Figure ). Notably, downregulated STAT1 binding peaks in Ncoa4
−/− PMs were enriched in pathways critical for macrophage activation, including PI3K‐Akt, Ras, and Rap1 signaling (Figure ). The above results demonstrated that NCOA4 forms a functional complex with STAT1 to cooperatively regulate transcriptional activities essential for macrophage activation.

NCOA4 Transcriptionally Regulates Ch25h in a STAT1‐Dependent Manner

We performed integrated analysis of RNA‐seq, CUT & Tag, and online human and mouse AAA datasets to identify direct targets of the NCOA4/STAT1 complex during AAA pathogenesis (Figure ). Among the three overlapping candidate genes (Ch25h, Nfatc4, and Ciita), Ch25h exhibited the most pronounced downregulation in Ncoa4
−/− PMs (Figure ). Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) analysis revealed significantly reduced STAT1 binding at the Ch25h promoter in Ncoa4
−/− PMs, suggesting suppressed Ch25h transcription (Figure ). The putative binding site of the NCOA4/STAT1 complex at the promoter of the mouse Ch25h locus was predicted by JASPAR [], which was confirmed by chromatin immunoprecipitation‐quantitative polymerase chain reaction (ChIP‐qPCR) in PMs (Figure ). Dual‐luciferase reporter assays further demonstrated that NCOA4 and STAT1 synergistically enhanced transcription of Ch25h, an effect abolished upon mutation of the binding site (Figure ). Given that this transcriptional activity is mediated by STAT1, we investigated the effect of NCOA4 deficiency on STAT1 expression and activation. Of interest, NCOA4 deficiency in macrophages did not alter total STAT1 expression but reduced STAT1 phosphorylation at Serine 727 (p‐STAT1‐S727), indicating suppressed STAT1‐mediated transcriptional activity (Figure ). Consistent with these findings, Ncoa4 deficiency led to a marked reduction in CH25H protein levels, as confirmed by Western blot and IF staining (Figure ). We also verified our conclusions in Bone Marrow‐Derived Macrophages (BMDMs) and observed the identical results (Figure ). Collectively, these results confirm Ch25h is a key transcriptional target of the NCOA4/STAT1 complex in macrophages during AAA development.

CH25H/25‐HC Contributes to AAA Progression via Augmenting Macrophage Inflammation and Ferroptosis

Our examination of CH25H variation during AAA modeling revealed that Ang II infusion significantly increased CH25H expression at both protein and mRNA levels, an effect abolished by Ncoa4 knockout (Figure ). IF staining further confirmed elevated CH25H expression in macrophages within AAA lesions (Figure ). Notably, serum levels of 25‐hydroxycholesterol (25‐HC), the enzymatic product of CH25H [], were markedly increased in Ang II‐infused mice (Figure ). Consistent with this, CH25H expression is also up‐regulated in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from those mice (Figure ). These findings were corroborated in human samples. Compared with controls, AAA patients exhibited significantly elevated CH25H expression in both aneurysmal aortic tissues and PBMCs, accompanied by increased serum 25‐HC levels (Figure ). The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants enrolled in the PBMC study are summarized in Table . The above results suggest that macrophage‐derived CH25H contributes to AAA progression via 25‐HC production.

In vitro experiments revealed that Ang II stimulation increased 25‐HC levels in culture medium, an effect abolished by Ch25h knockdown (Figure ). Ch25h knockdown also attenuated Ang II‐induced macrophage activation, as evidenced by reduced expression of pro‐inflammatory factors (Il1b, Il6, Tnf, and Ptgs2; Figure ). Given recent reports implicating 25‐HC in ferroptosis induction [], we confirmed that Ch25h knockdown preserved expression of ferroptosis inhibitors, including NRF2, SLC7A11, and GPX4, and reduced iron accumulation, ROS production, and lipid peroxidation (Figure ). Besides, mitochondrial dysfunction and lipid peroxidation were also mitigated, as shown by decreased signals of Mitotracker Red, FerroOrange, and MitoPeDPP staining (Figure ).

To further verify the NCOA4/STAT1/CH25H axis in macrophages, we overexpressed NCOA4 in macrophages while simultaneously knocking down Stat1 expression via siRNA transfection. As anticipated, Stat1 knockdown significantly abrogated NCOA4‐driven CH25H expression. Concurrently, the associated macrophage phenotypes—specifically macrophage ferroptosis—were effectively reversed (Figure ). Furthermore, we overexpressed CH25H in Ncoa4‐knockout macrophages. Importantly, CH25H overexpression successfully compensated for NCOA4 deficiency, recapitulating the ferroptotic phenotypes that were initially suppressed by Ncoa4 loss (Figure ). To further validate this relationship in vivo, we employed AAV‐mediated Ncoa4 and Stat1 knockdown. Aligning with our in vitro findings, depleting STAT1 robustly blunted AAA progression in the control group. Conversely, Stat1 knockdown yielded no such protective effect in mice with NCOA4, confirming that the pro‐aneurysmal role of STAT1 depends on the presence of NCOA4 (Figure ). These findings establish a mechanistic link between NCOA4/STAT1‐mediated CH25H up‐regulation, 25‐HC‐induced macrophage activation and ferroptosis, and ultimately AAA progression.

Spatiotemporal Targeting CH25H Attenuates AAA progression In Vivo

To overcome the challenge of targeting macrophages within abdominal aortic tissues under hemodynamic conditions, we developed an acoustically responsive delivery system based on microbubbles (MBs) for SI‐Ch25h delivery. Initially, lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) carrying SI‐Ch25h were synthesized using the microfluidic mixing method and connected to SonoVue MBs, an FDA‐approved clinical ultrasound contrast agent, via a Michael addition reaction (Figure ). Bright‐field imaging using an all‐in‐one fluorescence microscope showed that SI‐Ch25h‐LNP/MB retained a uniform spherical morphology similar to unconjugated MB (Figure ). Hydrodynamic size measured by Particle Sizing Systems (PSS) revealed values of approximately 2.05 μm for bare MB and approximately 2.22 μm for SI‐Ch25h‐LNP/MB, respectively (Figure ). Zeta potential measurements, coupled with dynamic light scattering (DLS), further indicated a zeta potential of 47.79 ± 0.98 mV and a particle size of 303±112 nm for SI‐Ch25h‐LNP; −57.77 ± 10.39 mV and 1431 ± 315 nm for bare MB; and −29.92 ± 4.36 mV and approximately 1416 ± 416 nm for SI‐Ch25h‐LNP/MB (Figure ). Collectively, these results demonstrate that surface binding with SI‐Ch25h‐LNP preserved the morphological characteristics of MB but modified their surface charge and slightly changed the particle size.

Utilizing an in vitro hemodynamic model, we successfully confirmed the ability of the LNP/MB system to penetrate into the microfluidic hydrogel tube wall with ultrasound destruction (Figure ). To evaluate the effect of SI‐Ch25h‐LNP/MB in vivo, we utilized a clinically used ultrasound system, the Mindray Resona R9 Pro (Microbubble Destruction Mode), leveraging its capacity to induce MB cavitation and facilitate local delivery to the aortic wall. Mice were randomly assigned to four groups after mini‐osmotic pump implantation. SI‐NC, SI‐Ch25h, SI‐Ch25h‐LNP, or SI‐Ch25h‐LNP/MB were intravenously administered on Day 7 after mini‐osmotic pump implantation. Following injection, real‐time imaging of the abdominal aorta was performed with Dual Live mode, and several seconds of Microbubble Destruction mode were performed to destroy MBs and deliver the SI‐Ch25h‐LNP into the abdominal aortic tissue via microstreaming and microjets caused by MB destruction (Figure ). At 21 days post‐treatment, the abdominal aortic tissues from each group were harvested, and IF staining was performed, revealing that abdominal aortic tissues treated with SI‐Ch25h‐LNP/MB had significantly reduced CH25H expression with aneurysm (Figure ). Besides, we have also thoroughly evaluated the systemic biodistribution of this approach (Figure ). Taken together, these findings confirm the successful development of an effective system for lesion‐targeted knockdown of CH25H in macrophages.

After confirming the biosafety of SI‐Ch25h‐LNP/MB and SI‐Ch25h‐LNP/MB with ultrasound destruction at the level of HE staining, liver and kidney function indices, and complete blood count (Figure and Table ), we conducted in vivo experiments to further assess the therapeutic efficacy of this strategy (Figure ). As expected, lesion‐targeted knockdown of CH25H in vivo significantly attenuated AAA progression and improved survival rates (Figure ). The incidence of AAA and maximal aortic diameters were markedly reduced following administration of SI‐Ch25h‐LNP/MB (Figure ). The administration did not alter blood pressure or lipid profiles (Figure ). Histological analysis further demonstrated improved aortic remodeling (Figure ) and reduced macrophage infiltration (Figure ) in the SI‐Ch25h‐LNP/MB + Ang II group compared to the SI‐NC‐LNP/MB + Ang II group, corroborating the therapeutic benefits of our approach. The above results indicated the effects of our strategy; we further employed our strategy in mice with advanced aneurysm (Figure ) and further verified its therapeutic effects. SI‐Ch25h‐LNP/MB administration effectively deterred the progression of AAA compared to the control group (Figure ).

Discussion and Conclusion

In the present work, we elucidate the precise role of NCOA4 in AAA pathogenesis, revealing several critical discoveries: First, macrophage‐derived NCOA4 promotes inflammation, ECM degradation, aortic remodeling, and AAA progression. Second, NCOA4 interacts with STAT1 to transcriptionally upregulate Ch25h, thereby elevating 25‐HC levels, inducing macrophage activation, pro‐inflammatory responses, and ferroptosis. Third, we observed elevated 25‐HC levels in serum from both mice and humans with AAA, which could potentially serve as a non‐invasive diagnostic biomarker. Furthermore, targeting CH25H via LNP/MBs in vivo demonstrated therapeutic effects against AAA, suggesting it may hold promise as a future strategy. In general, our study provides novel insights into the pathogenesis of AAA by uncovering a previously unrecognized role of the NCOA4/STAT1/CH25H axis in macrophages. Furthermore, these findings propose a potential biomarker and suggest a novel therapeutic approach that could ultimately contribute to AAA management.

The central role of macrophages in AAA formation and progression has been emphasized by recent studies. At the onset of AAA, classically activated macrophages release pro‐inflammatory cytokines and interact with T cells and mast cells, forming a self‐amplifying feedback loop that sustains chronic inflammation []. Targeting the IRF5/PI3Kγ pathway ameliorated AAA by inhibiting macrophage infiltration and migration []. Supporting this, Wu et al. demonstrated that Ninj1 knockout suppressed macrophage activation and inflammatory responses, thereby attenuating AAA progression []. During AAA development, ADAR1 or ILF3 exacerbate inflammation via the NF‐κB pathway, disrupting the balance between pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory responses [, ]. The suppression of alternatively activated macrophages further compromises vascular repair, leading to an imbalance between tissue damage and regeneration []. Concurrently, excessive MMP release accelerates ECM degradation, while complement activation and NETosis intensify oxidative stress []. Recent evidence further indicates that elevated ROS and lipid peroxidation drive ferroptosis and lead to AAA rupture, while inhibiting ferroptosis via hydrogen sulfide (H2S) supplementation mitigated AAA []. Here, our study identified a previously unrecognized NCOA4‐dependent signaling pathway that promotes macrophage activation in AAA, offering novel directions for AAA management.

As a multifunctional protein, NCOA4 exhibits a broad spectrum of biological functions beyond its well‐characterized role in ferritinophagy. For instance, it has been shown to exacerbate sepsis‐induced injury by activating the AGER‐NF‐κB pathway []. Notably, one of the most significant aspects of NCOA4 is its involvement in transcriptional regulation. Dysregulation of NCOA4 expression, particularly through chromosomal translocations that fuse NCOA4 with the RET tyrosine kinase gene (resulting in the NCOA4‐RET fusion), is closely associated with the pathogenesis of papillary thyroid carcinoma, highlighting its critical role in transcriptional control []. Furthermore, NCOA4 can function as a transcriptional co‐regulator by binding to AR to promote prostate cancer progression [], or by cooperating with peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor γ (PPARγ) to regulate transcription in adipocytes []. Our study confirmed for the first time that in macrophages, NCOA4 physically interacts with STAT1 to form a complex that synergistically enhances Ch25h transcription. Through thorough high‐throughput sequencing and molecular experiments, we conclusively demonstrated that genetic ablation of Ncoa4 significantly attenuates STAT1‐mediated transcriptional activation of Ch25h, suppressing macrophage activation and ferroptosis. Beyond the classical hemorrhage‐driven Fenton reaction and NCOA4‐mediated ferritinophagy, we demonstrate for the first time that NCOA4 also drives ferroptosis through transcriptional regulation. Specifically, our newly identified NCOA4‐STAT1‐CH25H axis transcriptionally amplifies lipid peroxidation. Ultimately, the synergistic interplay between canonical iron metabolism and this novel NCOA4‐dependent transcriptional pathway comprehensively orchestrates macrophage ferroptosis during AAA progression.

A major challenge in dissecting AAA pathogenesis is the intertwined nature of inflammation and ferroptosis. Rather than operating in a linear sequence, our findings, along with emerging evidence, suggest a feed‐forward loop: early inflammatory signals and ROS drive macrophage activation, with ferroptosis arising as a downstream cellular outcome [, ]. In turn, ferroptotic cells release Damage‐Associated Molecular Patterns (DAMPs) and cytokines that intensify local inflammation and accelerate tissue degradation [, ]. We acknowledge that our current approaches cannot fully separate the effects of ferroptosis from broader anti‐inflammatory actions. Nonetheless, our data strongly support ferroptosis as a critical amplifying mechanism within the macrophage‐driven inflammatory cascade in AAA. These results define a vital, ferritinophagy‐independent role for NCOA4 in inflammation and the initial stage of ferroptosis, emphasizing its fundamental importance in regulating this cell death process.

While NCOA4 has been implicated in various disease processes, it is crucial to recognize its indispensable role in hematopoiesis and erythropoiesis. Research has demonstrated that NCOA4 serves as a critical cofactor for thyroid hormone (TH)‐mediated transcriptional regulation during terminal erythrocyte differentiation, with its deficiency resulting in TH signaling impairment and consequent anemia []. Moreover, as a central regulator of iron metabolism and homeostasis, NCOA4 orchestrates murine erythropoiesis through both cell‐autonomous and non‐autonomous mechanisms [, ]. Considering the inherent risks of hemorrhage associated with AAA rupture and surgical intervention, we conclude that systemic targeting of NCOA4 is not the best choice for AAA management.

CH25H plays a pivotal role in modulating immune responses and lipid metabolism by catalyzing cholesterol oxidation into 25‐HC []. Extensive research has demonstrated that CH25H regulates glial cell activation, enhances the secretion of inflammatory factors and chemokines, and exacerbates neuroinflammation, thereby accelerating neurodegeneration [, , , ]. Under LPS stimulation, CH25H expression is up‐regulated, leading to increased 25‐HC synthesis and secretion, which aggravates pulmonary inflammation and tissue damage []. Furthermore, macrophage‐derived 25‐HC has been shown to promote vascular inflammation, atherogenesis, and atherosclerotic lesion remodeling, highlighting its role in activating immune cells (e.g., macrophages) and amplifying inflammatory responses []. Recent studies also reveal that 25‐HC induces ROS generation, disrupts oxidative stress balance, and sensitizes cells to ferroptosis [, ]. Our investigation initially revealed upregulated CH25H expression in macrophages derived from both patients and mice with AAA. Consistently, high serum 25‐HC levels were detected in both AAA patients and experimental mice, suggesting its potential as a novel serological biomarker for AAA diagnosis. Importantly, in vivo and in vitro experiments confirmed that Ch25h knockdown significantly reduced Ang II ‐induced 25‐HC production, macrophage activation, and ferroptosis, ultimately mitigating AAA progression. These findings underscore CH25H/25‐HC as a critical therapeutic target for AAA.

Given the inherent risks and limited applicability of surgical interventions, recent research efforts have focused on developing effective pharmacological treatments for AAA. While certain therapeutic agents, such as antihypertensive drugs, lipid‐lowering drugs, antiplatelet agents, and anti‐inflammatory compounds, have demonstrated partial efficacy, their clinical outcomes remain suboptimal. Notably, emerging macrophage‐targeted therapies have shown promising advancements in AAA treatment, exhibiting superior therapeutic potential. Building upon these developments, our study has specifically focused on the CH25H/25‐HC pathway, establishing a novel macrophage CH25H‐targeted therapeutic strategy that offers a novel approach for AAA management. Our siRNA delivery system is based on the FDA‐approved ultrasound contrast agent SonoVue. Both LNPs and SonoVue MBs are widely used in clinical situations [], conferring favorable biocompatibility and prospects of clinical translation to this system. SI‐Ch25h‐LNP/MB generate microstreaming and microjets in response to appropriate acoustic irradiation, which can facilitate penetration across the abdominal aortic wall and enable efficient siRNA delivery into macrophages residing within the abdominal aortic tissues. Furthermore, owing to the deep tissue penetration and focal tunability of ultrasound, gene delivery can be confined to the intended site, minimizing systemic exposure. Our strategy paves the way for highly efficient and targeted therapy for AAA. This approach is readily applicable in a clinical setting, as it simply combines intravenous injection with ultrasound detection, enabling simultaneous diagnosis and treatment.

We acknowledge several limitations in the present study. First, regarding cellular specificity, while bone marrow transplantation and AAV‐CD68‐shNcoa4 approaches strongly support a macrophage‐dependent mechanism, they lack absolute cell specificity. Future studies using lineage‐specific conditional knockout mice (e.g., Lyz2‐Cre; Ncoa4
fl/fl or Cx3cr1‐Cre; Ncoa4
fl/fl) are warranted to definitively establish the cell‐autonomous role of NCOA4 in macrophage‐driven AAA progression. Second, although 25‐HC exhibits strong translational potential, large‐scale, prospective clinical cohorts are essential to validate its diagnostic efficacy as an AAA biomarker. Third, the multiple murine AAA models employed necessitate varying diets, potentially introducing metabolic variability. However, since no single model perfectly recapitulates human AAA, the consistent phenotypes observed across these distinct models reinforce the robustness and clinical translatability of our findings. Fourth, we utilized exclusively male mice due to the significantly higher incidence of AAA in males. This single‐sex approach remains a limitation of our study. Therefore, validating our findings in female murine models is essential to elucidate potential sex‐specific mechanisms prior to future clinical translation. Finally, the exact intermediate mediating NCOA4‐dependent STAT1 Ser727 phosphorylation remains undetermined. Given that STAT1 lacks intrinsic kinase activity, NCOA4 may function as a scaffold to recruit kinases, sterically hinder phosphatases, or indirectly modulate upstream inflammatory/ROS homeostasis. Identifying these specific kinases or phosphatases represents a crucial direction for future research.

In conclusion, the identification of NCOA4 functioning as a transcriptional co‐regulator in macrophage activation and ferroptosis might broaden our understanding of the multiple biological functions of NCOA4. Our study unveils a previously undescribed NCOA4/STAT1/CH25H axis that contributes to AAA, providing new insights into the transcriptional networks of AAA pathogenesis and, most importantly, offering novel biomarkers and therapeutic strategies for AAA management.

Experimental Section

Human Study

All human studies were approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Renji Hospital, School of Medicine, Shanghai Jiao Tong University (KY2025‐0006‐C). The study was performed according to the criteria set by the Declaration of Helsinki (2013). Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. All study participants were recruited after providing informed consent and with approval by the Ethics Committee of Renji Hospital, School of Medicine, Shanghai Jiao Tong University.

Human AAA tissue samples were obtained from patients who underwent open surgical repair. Healthy abdominal aorta samples from brain‐dead organ donor patients were used as controls. The management of AAA patients was performed according to the 2018 Society of Vascular Surgery Practice Guidelines on the care of patients with an AAA []. In another series of studies, venous blood samples were collected from 10 AAA patients and 10 healthy donors. There were non‐significant differences in age, sex, general diseases, and medication between groups. Animal Experiment Procedures

The protocols of animal experiments in this study were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Renji Hospital, School of Medicine, Shanghai Jiao Tong University (RJ2024‐176B), and all the procedures were conducted following the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All mice used in this study were housed under specific pathogen‐free conditions at a temperature of 23°C with 60% relative humidity and a 12 h dark/light cycle. Ncoa4
−/− mice on a C57BL/6J background were produced by Shanghai Biomodel Organism Co., Ltd. Briefly, heterozygous Ncoa4
+/− mice were hybridized to produce homozygous Ncoa4
−/− mice and littermate Ncoa4
+/+ mice as wild‐type controls. Male mice aged 8 weeks were used in this study. To determine the effect of Ch25h knockdown on AAA formation, male ApoE
−/− mice (GemPharmatech Co., Ltd.) aged 8 weeks were used. For the induction of AAA modeling, anesthesia was initially induced in mice with 2.5% isoflurane. Throughout the mini‐pump implantation procedure, anesthesia was maintained using 1.5% isoflurane. At the end of the experiments, euthanasia was performed via intravenous injection of a lethal dose of pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg). All animal experiments were carried out independently by two investigators who remained blinded to the group assignments. Randomization was conducted using the RAND function in Excel to assign groups based on body weight, and assignment information was concealed from the experimenters for the duration of the study. The sample size for the mouse aortic aneurysm experiments was determined a priori with PASS software, based on preliminary data from similar AAA models previously generated in our laboratory. A minimum of 8 animals per group was deemed necessary to achieve 90% power (β = 0.1) for detecting significant differences in maximal aortic diameter at a significance level of α = 0.05. Each mouse was considered an independent experimental unit.

The Angiotensin II (Ang II)‐induced AAA models were established as reported [, ]. A single injection of recombinant type 8 adeno‐associated virus of murine proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 mutants (rAAV8/D377Y‐mPCSK9, 2 × 1011 vg) was performed in each mouse 2 weeks prior to osmotic pump implantation, combined with a Paigen diet (TP28640, Trophic Animal Feed High‐Tech Co., Ltd, China) to induce hypercholesterolemia. The ALZET osmotic pumps (model 2004, Durect, Cupertino, CA, USA) were infused with Ang II (MCE, HY‐13948, China) or saline. For AAA modeling, mice were first subjected to 2.5% isoflurane to induce anesthesia. During the process of mini pump implantation, mice were continuously anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane. The delivery of Ang II (1000 ng/kg/min) or saline lasted 28 days. Aneurysm progression was evaluated by ultrasound imaging, and the severity of AAA was determined by measuring the maximal aortic diameters using a caliper at sacrifice. Macroscopic photos of aortas were taken with a Nikon camera, and periadventitial tissues were removed before taking pictures. We used the well‐accepted criterion to define AAA: the maximal aortic diameter of an aneurysm is at least 1.5 times the aortic diameter of mice infused with normal saline []. Mice that died prior to the experiment termination were excluded from further analysis except for calculating mortality and rupture rates. To determine the causes of death, necropsies were performed. The fatal events included isolated thoracic aortic dissection/rupture, combined thoracic and abdominal aortic rupture, and isolated abdominal aortic rupture. Mice with isolated thoracic aortic dissection/rupture were excluded from the survival analysis.

Bone marrow transplantation experiments were performed as previously reported []. Briefly, mice aged 6 weeks were maintained on antibiotic water containing neomycin (100 mg/L) and polymyxin B sulphate (10 mg/L) for 7 days before and 14 days after transplantation. Recipient mice were subjected to total body irradiation (9 Gy) and injected intravenously with 5 × 106 bone marrow cells from pools of bone marrow from Ncoa4
−/− and Ncoa4
+/+ donor mice. Tail clip samples and peritoneal macrophages were used for genotyping and confirmation of reconstitution. After 2 weeks of recovery, mice aged 8 weeks were then subjected to AAA modeling as previously described.

To study the effect of in vivo Ch25h knockdown in macrophages on AAA, male ApoE
−/− mice (GemPharmatech Co., Ltd) aged 8 weeks were used. An II‐induced AAA model was performed as previously described. For histological analysis, aortas were flushed with saline and subsequently submerged in paraformaldehyde for fixation. For molecular experiments, aortas were quickly removed, flushed, and then stored in liquid nitrogen.

To determine the effect of macrophage‐specific NCOA4 deficiency on AAA formation, male ApoE
−/− mice aged 6 weeks were randomly divided into 2 groups and injected with pAAV‐hCD68‐MCS‐EGFP‐3xFLAG‐miR30shRNA(NC)‐WPRE (AAV‐ShCtrl) or pAAV‐hCD68‐MCS‐EGFP‐3xFLAG‐miR30shRNA(Ncoa4)‐WPRE (AAV‐ShNcoa4) (5 × 1011 vg). Ang II‐induced AAA model was established 2 weeks after AAV injection. Mice were fed the Western diet during the modeling. In another set of experiment, male ApoE
−/− mice aged 6 weeks were randomly divided into 4 groups and injected with pAAV‐hCD68‐MCS‐EGFP‐3xFLAG‐miR30shRNA(NC)‐WPRE (AAV‐ShCtrl) or pAAV‐hCD68‐MCS‐EGFP‐3xFLAG‐miR30shRNA(Ncoa4)‐WPRE (AAV‐ShNcoa4) and pAAV‐hCD68‐MCS‐EGFP‐3xFLAG‐miR30shRNA(NC)‐WPRE (AAV‐ShNc) or pAAV‐hCD68‐MCS‐EGFP‐3xFLAG‐miR30shRNA(Stat1)‐WPRE (AAV‐Sh Stat1) (5 × 1011 vg). Ang II‐induced AAA model was established 2 weeks after AAV injection. Mice were fed the Western diet during the modeling.

Systolic blood pressure (SBP) was evaluated using a non‐invasive tail‐cuff method (BP‐2010E; Softron) as previously reported. Briefly, mice were acclimated to the system and restraint procedure for seven consecutive days prior to the actual measurements. During the procedure, mice were placed in restraint tubes maintained at 37°C, and their tails were passed through the cuff sensor. Measurements were conducted at the same time daily for 20 min, with at least six valid readings obtained per animal.

Cell Isolation and Culture

Primary peritoneal macrophages (PMs) were obtained using Ncoa4
−/− mice and Ncoa4
+/+ mice aged 8–10 weeks. Mice were intraperitoneally injected with 1 mL sterile 6% starch solution. 3 days later, mice were euthanized via single intravenous injection of a lethal dose of pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg) and sterilized by immersion in 75% ethanol for 5 min. Under aseptic conditions, the abdominal cavity was opened, and the peritoneum was gently lavaged three times with 10 mL of ice‐cold sterile PBS (supplemented with 1% penicillin‐streptomycin) per wash. The lavage fluid was collected and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was treated with red blood cell lysis buffer for 1 min, followed by two washes with PBS. Finally, the cells were resuspended in DMEM complete medium (supplemented with 10% FBS) and seeded in culture dishes. After 2 h of incubation at 37°C with 5% CO2, non‐adherent cells were removed. After 24 h, the PM was subjected to transfection or Ang II administration.

Primary bone marrow‐derived macrophages (BMDMs) were obtained using Ncoa4
+/+ mice and Ncoa4
−/− mice aged 8–10 weeks. Mice were euthanized via a single intravenous injection of a lethal dose of pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg) and sterilized by immersion in 75% ethanol for 5 min. The femurs and tibias were excised, and the surrounding muscular tissues were carefully removed. The ends of the bones were cut, and the bone marrow cavity was gently flushed three times with ice‐cold sterile PBS (supplemented with 1% penicillin‐streptomycin) using a 1 mL syringe fitted with a 25‐gauge needle. The bone marrow suspension was collected and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was treated with red blood cell lysis buffer for 1 min, followed by two washes with PBS. Finally, the cells were resuspended in DMEM complete medium (supplemented with 10% FBS and 20 ng/mL recombinant murine M‐CSF) and seeded in culture dishes. The cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2, and the culture medium was refreshed every 3 days. After 7 days of incubation, non‐adherent cells were removed. The mature BMDMs were then subjected to transfection or Ang II administration.

Human embryonic kidney 293T (HEK293T) cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; USA). HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS in a humidified culture incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. For statistical comparisons, the Shapiro–Wilk test was performed initially to evaluate data for normality. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± Standard Error of the mean (SEM), while non‐normally distributed data are presented as median and interquartile range. The statistical differences between groups were assessed as follows: (1) For independent samples between two groups, statistical differences were assessed using two‐tailed unpaired Student's t‐tests with homogeneous variances. Two‐tailed unpaired Student's t‐test with Welch correction was applied in the presence of heterogeneity in variances. (2) One‐way ANOVA was applied in scenarios of comparisons involving more than 2 independent groups with a single factor. For non‐normally distributed data, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used, followed by post hoc analysis via the Dunn multiple comparisons test. (3) Two‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparison tests was used for comparing differences among more than two groups with two factors. (4) For non‐normally distributed data, the nonparametric Mann‐Whitney U test was used for comparisons of two samples, whereas the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied for comparisons of more than two data samples. (5) Kaplan–Meier method plus a stratified log‐rank test was applied to evaluate the survival rates between groups. (6) For data with unequal variances, Brown‐Forsythe and Welch ANOVA tests were used. (7) Fisher's Least Significant Difference (LSD) post‐hoc analysis was applied following one‐way ANOVA where specified. p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
