Congruences for Overcubic Partition k k -Tuples
Daniel Chacón, James A. Sellers

Abstract
. In the last few years, a number of authors have proved divisibility properties satisfied by various functions which count the number of overcubic partition k k –tuples of weight n n for small values of k k . In this work, we use generating functions to prove some of their results as well as multiple infinite families of new congruences for overcubic partition k k -tuples which do not yet appear in the literature. In particular, we focus on a new perspective which provides insights as to why these functions are often divisible by powers of 2, and we also prove families of congruences whose moduli are odd. For example, we prove that, for all m ≥ 0 m\geq 0 , b ¯ 4 ​ ( 22 ​ m + 11 ) ≡ 0 ( mod 11 ) \overline{b}_{4}(22m+11)\equiv 0\pmod{11} and we also prove infinite families such as b ¯ 9 ​ l + 2 ​ ( 9 ​ m + 3 ) ≡ 0 ( mod 3 ) \overline{b}_{9l+2}(9m+3)\equiv 0\pmod{3} for all m , l ≥ 0 m,l\geq 0 .

1.
Introduction

A partition
\lambda
of a positive integer
n
is a finite non-increasing sequence of positive integers
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{r}
such that
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}=n
. Each
\lambda_{i}
is called a part of the partition
\lambda
.
For example, there are five partitions when
n=4
which are

4\text{, }3+1\text{, }2+2\text{, }2+1+1,\text{ and }1+1+1+1.

For all
n\geq 0
, let
p(n)
be the number of partitions of
n
with
p(0):=1
. From the example above, we have
p(4)=5
.

In the work below, we will be interested in the generating functions for various integer partition functions. As such, we will utilize
q
-Pochhhammer notation which is defined as follows: For
j\geq 1
,

(a;q)_{j}=\prod_{i=1}^{j}(1-aq^{i-1})

where
(a;q)_{0}=1
. For
|q|<1
, we have

(a;q)_{\infty}=\lim_{j\to\infty}(a;q)_{j}.
To simplify our notation even further, we define
f_{k}=(q^{k};q^{k})_{\infty}
for fixed
k\geq 1
.

As Euler proved, the generating function for
p(n)
is given by

\sum_{n\geq 0}p(n)q^{n}=\frac{1}{f_{1}}.

Approximately a century ago, Ramanujan
[
26
]
noticed congruences satisfied by
p(n)
for the moduli
5,7,
and
11
. Using generating function manipulations, he proved the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

For all
n\geq 0
,

(1)

\displaystyle p(5n+4)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{5},

(2)

\displaystyle p(7n+5)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{7},\text{ and}

(3)

\displaystyle p(11n+6)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{11}.

These congruences, and others that have been proven for various partition functions since the time of Ramanujan, serve as the motivation for the work below.

In order to describe the family of functions that we wish to study, we must provide a few more definitions.

An overpartition of
n
is an integer partition of
n
where the first occurrence of any part may be overlined. To illustrate, there are eight overpartitions of 3:

3\text{, }\overline{3}\text{, }2+1\text{, }\overline{2}+1\text{, }2+\overline{1}\text{, }\overline{2}+\overline{1}\text{, }1+1+1,\text{ and }\overline{1}+1+1.

We define
\overline{p}(n)
to be the number of overpartitions of
n
, so we have
\overline{p}(3)=8
thanks to the example above.

Overpartitions and their congruences have been well-studied; see
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,

]
for examples of such work. The generating function for
\overline{p}(n)
is given by

\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{p}(n)q^{n}=\frac{(-q;q)_{\infty}}{(q;q)_{\infty}}=\frac{f_{2}}{f_{1}^{2}}.

In 2010, Hei-Chi Chan
[
4
,
5
]
introduced the cubic partition function
b(n)
in connection with Ramanujan’s cubic continued fraction. The generating function for
b(n)
is given by

(4)

B(q):=\sum_{n\geq 0}b(n)q^{n}=\frac{1}{(q;q)_{\infty}(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}=\frac{1}{(q;q^{2})_{\infty}(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}^{2}}.

It is clear that
b(n)
also counts the number of integer partitions of
n
where each even part may appear in one of two colors. For example,
b(4)=10
thanks to the following:

4_{1}\text{, }4_{2}\text{, }3+1\text{, }2_{1}+2_{1}\text{, }2_{1}+2_{2}\text{, }2_{2}+2_{1}\text{, }2_{2}+2_{2}\text{, }2_{1}+1+1\text{, }2_{2}+1+1,\text{ and }1+1+1+1

Note that the subscripts represent the two possible colors for the even parts.
Many congruences and related arithmetic properties satisfied by
b(n)
have been proven
[

,

,

,
37
]

Soon after the work of Chan appeared, Kim
[
20
]
combined the ideas of overpartitions and cubic partitions to define overcubic partitions. As one might imagine, these are partitions where the first occurrence of any part may be overlined and each even part comes in one of two colors.
For example, there are twelve overcubic partitions of 3 which are

3\text{, }\overline{3}\text{, }2_{1}+1\text{, }\overline{2}_{1}+1,2_{1}+\overline{1}\text{, }\overline{2}_{1}+\overline{1}\text{, }2_{2}+1\text{, }\overline{2}_{2}+1,2_{2}+\overline{1}\text{, }\overline{2}_{2}+\overline{1}\text{, }1+1+1,\text{ and }\overline{1}+1+1.

Thus, if we define
\overline{b}(n)
to be the number of overcubic partitions, then the above example yields
\overline{b}(3)=12
. The generating function for
\overline{b}(n)
is given by

(5)

\overline{B}(q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}(n)q^{n}=\frac{f_{4}}{f^{2}_{1}f_{2}}.

As is the case for its counterparts
\overline{p}(n)

These results will be fully generalized when we transition to overcubic partition

–tuples, the primary set of combinatorial objects under consideration in this work.

A partition
k
-tuple of weight
n
is a list of
k
different partitions whose total sum is
n
. Note that some of the
k
partitions in such a
k
-tuple may be empty; in that case, we denote such an empty partition with the symbol

\displaystyle(2_{1},\emptyset)\text{, }(\overline{2}_{1},\emptyset)\text{, }(2_{2},\emptyset)\text{, }(\overline{2}_{2},\emptyset)\text{, }(\emptyset,2_{1})\text{, }(\emptyset,\overline{2}_{1})\text{, }(\emptyset,2_{2})\text{, }(\emptyset,\overline{2}_{2}),

\displaystyle(1+1,\emptyset)\text{, }(\overline{1}+1,\emptyset)\text{, }(1,1)\text{, }(\overline{1},1)\text{, }(1,\overline{1})\text{, }(\overline{1},\overline{1})\text{, }(\emptyset,1+1),\text{ and }(\emptyset,\overline{1}+1).

Based on the generating function for

with
\overline{b}_{0}(n)=0
for all

Theorem 2.1

We have

Next, we will need to understand what happens in Theorem

Lemma 2.2

We have

(-q;-q)_{\infty}=\frac{f_{2}^{3}}{f_{1}f_{4}}.

Proof.

Using elementary generating function manipulations, we have

\displaystyle(-q;-q)_{\infty}

\displaystyle=(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}(-q;q^{2})_{\infty}

\displaystyle=f_{2}\cdot\frac{(q^{2};q^{4})_{\infty}}{(q;q^{2})_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=f_{2}\cdot\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}}\cdot\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q;q)_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{2}^{3}}{f_{1}f_{4}}.

∎

In the same vein, it will be beneficial in the work below to consider the generating function

Corollary 2.3

We have

\overline{B}_{k}(-q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{k}(n)(-q)^{n}=\frac{f_{1}^{2k}f_{4}^{3k}}{f_{2}^{7k}}.

Proof.

Beginning with the definition of

\displaystyle\overline{B}_{k}(-q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{k}(n)(-q)^{n}

\displaystyle={\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}}\frac{(1-(-q)^{4i})^{k}}{(1-(-q)^{i})^{2k}(1-(-q)^{2i})^{k}}

\displaystyle={\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}}\frac{(1-q^{4i})^{k}}{(1-q^{2i})^{k}}{\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}}\frac{1}{(1-(-q)^{i})^{2k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{k}_{4}}{f^{k}_{2}}\left({\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}}(1-(-q)^{i})\right)^{-2k}.

Using Lemma

\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{k}(n)(-q)^{n}=\frac{f^{k}_{4}}{f^{k}_{2}}\left(\frac{f_{2}^{3}}{f_{1}f_{4}}\right)^{-2k}=\frac{f^{k}_{4}}{f^{k}_{2}}\frac{f_{1}^{2k}f_{4}^{2k}}{f_{2}^{6k}}=\frac{f_{1}^{2k}f_{4}^{3k}}{f_{2}^{7k}}.

3.
A Different Perspective on Congruences Modulo Powers of 2

We begin this section by noting the following functional equation for
\overline{B}_{k}(q)
which serves as a generalization of Theorem
1.4
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For all
k\geq 1
,
\overline{B}_{k}(q)=\varphi^{k}(q)\varphi^{k}(q^{2})\left(\overline{B}_{k}(q^{2})\right)^{2}
.

Corollary 3.2
.

For all
k\geq 1
,
\overline{B}_{k}(q)=\varphi^{k}(q){\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}}\left(\varphi(q^{2^{i}})\right)^{3k\cdot 2^{i-1}}
.

Note that Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
3.2

immediately follow from Theorem
1.4
and Corollary
1.5
by raising both sides of the corresponding equations to the power
k
.

A priori, it should be clear that Corollary
3.2
provides an extremely beneficial perspective on the generating function of
\overline{b}_{k}(n)
. Simply put, given that

\varphi(q)=1+2\sum_{n\geq 1}q^{n^{2}},

we must expect that
\overline{b}_{k}(n)
will likely satisfy a wide variety of congruences modulo powers of 2. This is true because, thanks to the Binomial Theorem, for a fixed positive integer
t
, we have

(\varphi(q))^{t}=\left(1+2\sum_{n\geq 1}q^{n^{2}}\right)^{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{t}\binom{t}{j}2^{j}\left(\sum_{n\geq 1}q^{n^{2}}\right)^{j}

and the factors of the form
2^{j}
will vanish modulo small powers of 2, providing a great deal of simplification when attempting to prove congruences satisfied by
\overline{b}_{k}(n)
modulo a small power of 2.

For example, with Corollary
3.2
in hand, we can now greatly extend Theorem
1.7
which characterized the number of overcubic partitions modulo 4.

Theorem 3.3
.

We have the following:

•

For all
n\geq 1
and
k
even,
\overline{b}_{k}(n)\equiv 0\pmod{4}
.

•

For all
n\geq 1
and
k
odd,

\overline{b}_{k}(n)\equiv\begin{cases}2\pmod{4}\text{ if }n=l^{2}\text{ or }n=2l^{2}\text{, and}\\
0\pmod{4}\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Thanks to Equation (
6
) and Corollary
3.2
, we have

\displaystyle\overline{B}_{k}(q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{k}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\varphi^{k}(q)\varphi^{3k}(q^{2})\prod_{i\geq 2}^{\infty}\left(\varphi(q^{2i})\right)^{3k\cdot 2^{i-1}}

\displaystyle\equiv\varphi^{k}(q)\varphi^{3k}(q^{2})\pmod{4}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(1+2\sum_{l\geq 1}q^{l^{2}}\right)^{k}\left(1+2\sum_{l\geq 1}q^{2l^{2}}\right)^{k\cdot 3}\pmod{4}.

Consider the case of
k
even.
Then,
k=2j
for some
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Now, our congruence becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{2j}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(1+2\sum_{l\geq 1}q^{l^{2}}\right)^{2\cdot j}\left(1+2\sum_{l\geq 1}q^{2l^{2}}\right)^{2\cdot 3j}\pmod{4}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(1+4\sum_{l\geq 1}q^{l^{2}}+4\left(\sum_{l\geq 1}q^{l^{2}}\right)^{2}\right)^{j}\left(1+4\sum_{l\geq 1}q^{2l^{2}}+4\left(\sum_{l\geq 1}q^{2l^{2}}\right)^{2}\right)^{3j}\pmod{4}

\displaystyle\equiv 1\pmod{4}.

This yields our first result.

Next, consider the case of
k
odd.
Then,
k=2j+1
for some
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Using this and our work above, we know

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{2j+1}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(1+2\sum_{l\geq 1}q^{l^{2}}\right)^{2\cdot j+1}\left(1+2\sum_{l\geq 1}q^{2l^{2}}\right)^{(2\cdot j+1)\cdot 3}\pmod{4}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(1+2\sum_{l\geq 1}q^{l^{2}}\right)^{2\cdot j}\left(1+2\sum_{l\geq 1}q^{l^{2}}\right)\left(1+2\sum_{l\geq 1}q^{2l^{2}}\right)^{2j\cdot 3+2}\left(1+2\sum_{l\geq 1}q^{2l^{2}}\right)\pmod{4}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(1+2\sum_{l\geq 1}q^{l^{2}}\right)\left(1+2\sum_{l\geq 1}q^{2l^{2}}\right)\pmod{4}.

This yields our second result.
∎

As an aside, one could easily imagine using the above proof technique to generalize Theorem
1.8
as well. Admittedly, the
q
-series manipulations would become slightly more complicated than what appears above for the mod 4 characterization; nevertheless, this could be done. We leave the details to the interested reader.

Given this new perspective on finding characterizations modulo small powers of 2 for the functions
\overline{b}_{k}(n)
, we now wish to revisit some of the results that already exist in the literature with the goal of providing very elementary (and short) proofs.

As an example, Shivaprasada Nayaka et al.
[
30
]
note many congruences modulo 4 satisfied by
\overline{b}_{3}(n)
.
Using Theorem
3.3
, we can verify some of their results by way of example.

Theorem 3.4

(
[
30
]
)
.

For all
m,\alpha\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\overline{b}_{3}(64m+48)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{4},

\displaystyle\overline{b}_{3}(128m+80)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{4},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\overline{b}_{3}(256\cdot 2^{\alpha}m+192)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{4}.

Proof.

We provide a proof which relies on Theorem
3.3
and is very different in nature to the proofs used in
[
30
]
. Using Theorem
3.3
, we only need to prove that the arithmetic progressions mentioned above can never contain a square or twice a square.

•

We have
64m+48=16(4m+3)
.
Now,
4m+3\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, hence it cannot be square.
Therefore, no number of the form
16(4m+3)=64m+48
can be square.
Also,
64m+48=2\cdot 4(8m+6)
.
Note that
8m+6\equiv 2\pmod{4}
.
Therefore, no number of the form
64m+48
can be twice a square.

•

We have
128m+80=16(8m+5)
.
We know
8m+5\equiv 5\pmod{8}
, and the whole expression cannot be a square.
Also,
128m+80=2\cdot 4(16m+10)
.
Note that
16m+10\equiv 2\pmod{8}
, and the whole expression cannot be twice a square.

•

We have
256\cdot 2^{\alpha}m+192=64(4\cdot 2^{\alpha}m+3)
.
We know
4\cdot 2^{\alpha}m+3\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, so the whole expression cannot be a square.
Also
256\cdot 2^{\alpha}m+192=2\cdot 16(8\cdot 2^{\alpha}m+6)
.
Note that
8\cdot 2^{\alpha}m+6\equiv 2\pmod{4}
, so the whole expression cannot be a square.

Therefore, by Theorem
3.3
, all of the congruences mentioned above must hold.
∎

Note that the specific value
k=3
does not play a role in the above proof (other than the fact that 3 is odd). Therefore, we see that the above theorem can be extended immediately to an infinite family of congruences modulo 4.

Theorem 3.5
.

For all
k,m,\alpha\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\overline{b}_{2k+1}(64m+48)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{4},

\displaystyle\overline{b}_{2k+1}(128m+80)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{4},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\overline{b}_{2k+1}(256\cdot 2^{\alpha}m+192)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{4}.

Of course, the above theorem can be generalized even further to include other arithmetic progressions, as long as such arithmetic progressions never contain a square or twice a square. We stop here for the sake of brevity.

Congruences Involving Odd Moduli

We now transition to congruences satisfied by
\overline{b}_{k}(n)
for various values of
k
where the moduli in question are odd.
We begin by focusing on a few results for specific values of
k
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For all
m\geq 0
,
\overline{b}_{4}(14m+7)\equiv 0\pmod{7}
.

Proof.

We have

\overline{B}_{4}(q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{4}(n)q^{n}=\frac{f_{4}^{4}}{f_{1}^{8}f_{2}^{4}}=\frac{f^{4}_{4}}{f^{4}_{2}}\frac{1}{f^{8}_{1}}=\frac{f^{4}_{4}}{f^{4}_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{f^{4}_{1}}\right)^{2}.

Using Theorem
2.11
, our equation becomes

\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{4}(n)q^{n}=\frac{f^{4}_{4}}{f^{4}_{2}}\left(\frac{f^{14}_{4}}{f^{14}_{2}f^{4}_{8}}+4q\frac{f_{4}^{2}f_{8}^{4}}{f_{2}^{10}}\right)^{2}=\frac{f^{4}_{4}}{f^{4}_{2}}\left(\frac{f^{28}_{4}}{f^{28}_{2}f^{8}_{8}}+8q\frac{f^{14}_{4}}{f^{14}_{2}f^{4}_{8}}\frac{f_{4}^{2}f_{8}^{4}}{f_{2}^{10}}+16q^{2}\frac{f_{4}^{4}f_{8}^{8}}{f_{2}^{20}}\right).

Considering the odd powers of
q
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{4}(2n+1)q^{2n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{4}_{4}}{f^{4}_{2}}\left(8q\frac{f^{14}_{4}}{f^{14}_{2}f^{4}_{8}}\frac{f_{4}^{2}f_{8}^{4}}{f_{2}^{10}}\right)=8q\frac{f_{4}^{20}}{f_{2}^{28}}.

Dividing both sides by
q
and substituting
q
for
q^{2}
, our generating function becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{4}(2n+1)q^{n}

\displaystyle=8\frac{f_{2}^{20}}{f_{1}^{28}}=8\frac{f_{2}^{21}}{f_{1}^{28}f_{2}}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{14}^{3}}{f_{7}^{4}}\frac{1}{f_{2}}\pmod{7}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{14}^{3}}{f_{7}^{4}}\sum_{N\geq 0}p(N)q^{2N}.

The powers of
q
in the last expression above are of the form
7N^{\prime}+2N
for some integers
N^{\prime}
and
N
.
Our goal remains to find
\overline{b}_{4}(14m+7)
, and substituting
7m+3
for
n
above creates such an argument within our function.
Completing this substitution, and then comparing the powers of
q
on both sides, we have

\displaystyle 7m+3

\displaystyle=7N^{\prime}+2N

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle 3

\displaystyle\equiv 2N

\displaystyle\pmod{7}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle 4(3)

\displaystyle\equiv 4(2N)

\displaystyle\pmod{7}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle 5

\displaystyle\equiv N

\displaystyle\pmod{7}.

Thus,
N=7j+5
for some integer
j
. This means

\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{4}(14m+7)q^{7m+3}\equiv\frac{f_{14}^{3}}{f_{7}^{4}}\sum p(7j+5)q^{14j+10}\pmod{7}.

By Theorem
1.1
, we know
p(7j+5)\equiv 0\pmod{7}
for all
j\geq 0
. Thus, since
\frac{f_{14}^{3}}{f_{7}^{4}}
is a function of
q^{7}
, our result holds.
∎

We can prove a similar result for
\overline{b}_{4}(n)

Theorem 4.2
.

For all
m\geq 0
,
\overline{b}_{4}(22m+11)\equiv 0\pmod{11}
.

Proof.

Using the work in the proof of the previous theorem, we know

\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{4}(2n+1)q^{2n+1}=8q\frac{f_{4}^{20}}{f_{2}^{28}}=8q\frac{f_{4}^{22}}{f_{2}^{33}}\frac{f_{2}^{5}}{f_{4}^{2}}.

Using Theorem
2.12
, our equation becomes

\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{4}(2n+1)q^{2n+1}=8q\frac{f_{4}^{22}}{f_{2}^{33}}\left(\sum_{N=-\infty}^{\infty}(6N+1)q^{3N^{2}+N}\right).

Dividing both sides by
q
and substituting
q
for
q^{2}
, we have

\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{4}(2n+1)q^{n}=8\frac{f_{2}^{22}}{f_{1}^{33}}\left(\sum_{N=-\infty}^{\infty}(6N+1)q^{\frac{3N^{2}+N}{2}}\right)\equiv 8\frac{f_{22}^{2}}{f_{11}^{3}}\left(\sum_{N=-\infty}^{\infty}(6N+1)q^{\frac{3N^{2}+N}{2}}\right)\pmod{11}.

Our goal remains to find
\overline{b}_{4}(22m+11)
, and substituting
11m+5
for
n
above creates such a pattern within our function.
Completing this substitution, and then comparing the powers of
q
on both sides of the congruence, we have

\displaystyle 11m+5

\displaystyle=11N^{\prime}+\frac{3N^{2}+N}{2}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle 5

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{3N^{2}+N}{2}

\displaystyle\pmod{11}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle 24\cdot 5

\displaystyle\equiv 24\left(\frac{3N^{2}+N}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\pmod{11}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle 120+1

\displaystyle\equiv 36N^{2}+12N+1

\displaystyle\pmod{11}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle 121

\displaystyle\equiv(6N+1)^{2}

\displaystyle\pmod{11}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\equiv(6N+1)^{2}

\displaystyle\pmod{11}.

This means that, within the arithmetic progression of interest to us,
6N+1\equiv 0\pmod{11}
. Since
\frac{f_{22}^{2}}{f_{11}^{3}}
is a function of
q^{11}
, our result follows.
∎

Next, we have a congruence modulo 5 satisfied by

Theorem 4.3
.

For all
m\geq 1
,
\overline{b}_{8}(5m)\equiv 0\pmod{5}
.

Proof.

We know

\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{8}(n)q^{n}=\frac{f_{4}^{8}}{f_{1}^{16}f_{2}^{8}}=\frac{f_{4}^{5}}{f_{1}^{15}f_{2}^{10}}\frac{f_{2}^{2}f_{4}^{3}}{f_{1}}=\frac{f_{4}^{5}}{f_{1}^{15}f_{2}^{10}}\frac{f_{2}^{2}}{f_{1}}f_{4}^{3}=\frac{f_{4}^{5}}{f_{1}^{15}f_{2}^{10}}\psi(q)f_{4}^{3}.

Using Equation (
20
), and considering only the terms of our
q
-series of the form

, our equation becomes

\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{8}(5n)q^{5n}=\frac{f_{4}^{5}}{f_{1}^{15}f_{2}^{10}}\left(f_{5}^{3}\varphi(-q^{10})-5q^{15}f_{100}^{3}\psi(q^{25})\right)\equiv\frac{f_{20}}{f_{5}^{3}f_{10}^{2}}f_{5}^{3}\varphi(-q^{10})\pmod{5}.

Now, we substitute
q
for
q^{5}
to get

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{8}(5n)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{4}}{f_{1}^{3}f_{2}^{2}}f_{1}^{3}\varphi(-q^{2})\pmod{5}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{4}}{f_{2}^{2}}\frac{f_{2}^{2}}{f_{4}}\pmod{5}\text{ from Equation \eqref{Negative Phi Gen Fn}}

\displaystyle\equiv 1\pmod{5}

\displaystyle\equiv 1+0q+0q^{2}+0q^{3}+0q^{4}+\dots\pmod{5}.

Therefore, for all

,
\overline{b}_{8}(5m)\equiv 0\pmod{5}
.
∎

Interestingly enough, Theorem
4.3
can be used to prove an infinite family of congruences modulo 5.

Theorem 4.4
.

For all
j\geq 1
,
m\geq 0
, and all
1\leq r\leq 4
,
\overline{b}_{25j+8}(5(5m+r))\equiv 0\pmod{5}
.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{25j+8}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{4}^{25j+8}}{f_{1}^{2(25j+8)}f_{2}^{25j+8}}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{4}^{25j}}{f_{1}^{50j}f_{2}^{25j}}\cdot\frac{f_{4}^{8}}{f_{1}^{16}f_{2}^{8}}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{4}^{25j}}{f_{1}^{50j}f_{2}^{25j}}\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{8}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{100}^{j}}{f_{25}^{2j}f_{50}^{j}}\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{8}(n)q^{n}\pmod{5}.

Thus, we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{25j+8}(5n)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{20}^{j}}{f_{5}^{2j}f_{10}^{j}}\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{8}(5n)q^{n}\pmod{5}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{20}^{j}}{f_{5}^{2j}f_{10}^{j}}\cdot 1\pmod{5}

thanks to the proof of Theorem
4.3
. Since
\frac{f_{20}^{j}}{f_{5}^{2j}f_{10}^{j}}
is a function of
q^{5}
, we know that, for any
n
which is not divisible by 5, we must have

\overline{b}_{25j+8}(5n)\equiv 0\pmod{5}
. The theorem follows.
∎

We next move to a set of divisibility properties modulo 3. We begin with a pair of congruences modulo 3 satisfied by
\overline{b}_{5}(n)

Theorem 4.5
.

For all
m\geq 0
,
\overline{b}_{5}(27m+9)\equiv\overline{b}_{5}(27m+18)\equiv 0\pmod{3}
.

Proof.

Note that replacing
q
by
-q
in a partition function will not change its divisibility properties.
Using Corollary
2.3
and Equation (
16
), we have

\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{5}(n)(-q)^{n}=\frac{f^{10}_{1}f^{15}_{4}}{f^{35}_{2}}=\frac{f^{9}_{1}f^{15}_{4}}{f^{33}_{2}}\frac{f_{1}}{f^{2}_{2}}\equiv\frac{f^{3}_{3}f^{5}_{12}}{f^{11}_{6}}\frac{1}{\psi(q)}\pmod{3}.

Using Equation (
19
), we know

\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{5}(n)(-q)^{n}\equiv\frac{f^{3}_{3}f^{5}_{12}}{f^{11}_{6}}\left(\frac{\psi(q^{9})}{\psi(q^{3})^{4}}\left(P(q^{3})^{2}-qP(q^{3})\psi(q^{9})+q^{2}\psi(q^{9})^{2}\right)\right)\pmod{3}.

Define
B^{\prime}_{5}(n)=(-1)^{n}\overline{b}_{5}(n)
so that

\sum_{n\geq 0}B^{\prime}_{5}(n)q^{n}\equiv\frac{f^{3}_{3}f^{5}_{12}}{f^{11}_{6}}\left(\frac{\psi(q^{9})}{\psi(q^{3})^{4}}\left(P(q^{3})^{2}-qP(q^{3})\psi(q^{9})+q^{2}\psi(q^{9})^{2}\right)\right)\pmod{3}.

Considering the terms of our
q
-series of the form
q^{3n}
, our congruence becomes

\sum_{n\geq 0}B^{\prime}_{5}(3n)q^{3n}\equiv\frac{f^{3}_{3}f^{5}_{12}}{f^{11}_{6}}\frac{\psi(q^{9})}{\psi(q^{3})^{4}}P(q^{3})^{2}\pmod{3}.

Substituting
q
for
q^{3}
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}B^{\prime}_{5}(3n)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f^{3}_{1}f^{5}_{4}}{f^{11}_{2}}\frac{\psi(q^{3})}{\psi(q)^{4}}P(q)^{2}\pmod{3}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f^{3}_{1}f^{6}_{4}}{f^{12}_{2}}\frac{f_{2}}{f_{4}}\frac{\psi(q^{3})}{\psi(q^{3})\psi(q)}P(q)^{2}\pmod{3}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{3}f^{2}_{12}}{f^{4}_{6}}\frac{f_{2}}{f_{4}}\frac{f_{1}}{f^{2}_{2}}P(q)^{2}\pmod{3}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{3}f^{2}_{12}}{f^{4}_{6}}\frac{f_{1}}{f_{2}f_{4}}P(q)^{2}\pmod{3}.

Using Theorem
2.4
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}B^{\prime}_{5}(3n)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{3}f^{2}_{12}}{f^{4}_{6}}\frac{f_{1}}{f_{2}f_{4}}\left(\frac{f_{2}f^{2}_{3}}{f_{1}f_{6}}\right)^{2}\pmod{3}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{3}f^{2}_{12}}{f^{4}_{6}}\frac{f_{1}}{f_{2}f_{4}}\frac{f^{2}_{2}f^{4}_{3}}{f^{2}_{1}f^{2}_{6}}\pmod{3}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f^{5}_{3}f^{2}_{12}}{f^{6}_{6}}\frac{f_{2}}{f_{1}f_{4}}\pmod{3}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f^{5}_{3}f^{2}_{12}}{f^{6}_{6}}\frac{1}{\psi(-q)}\pmod{3}

using Equation (
17
).
Using the 3-dissection from Equation (
19
) and substituting
-q
for
q
, we know

\sum_{n\geq 0}B^{\prime}_{5}(3n)q^{n}\equiv\frac{f^{5}_{3}f^{2}_{12}}{f^{6}_{6}}\left(\frac{\psi(-q^{9})}{\psi(-q^{3})^{4}}\left(P(-q^{3})^{2}+qP(-q^{3})\psi(-q^{9})-q^{2}\psi(-q^{9})^{2}\right)\right)\pmod{3}.

Considering the terms of the above
q
-series of the form
q^{3n}
, our congruence becomes

\sum_{n\geq 0}B^{\prime}_{5}(9n)q^{3n}\equiv\frac{f^{5}_{3}f^{2}_{12}}{f^{6}_{6}}\frac{\psi(-q^{9})}{\psi(-q^{3})^{4}}P(-q^{3})^{2}\pmod{3}.

Substituting
q
for
q^{3}
again, we get

\sum_{n\geq 0}B^{\prime}_{5}(9n)q^{n}\equiv\frac{f^{5}_{1}f^{2}_{4}}{f^{6}_{2}}\frac{\psi(-q^{3})}{\psi(-q)^{4}}P(-q)^{2}\pmod{3}.

Applying Corollary
2.5
, our congruence becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}B^{\prime}_{5}(9n)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f^{5}_{1}f^{2}_{4}}{f^{6}_{2}}\frac{\psi(-q)^{3}}{\psi(-q)^{4}}\left(\frac{f_{1}f_{4}f^{5}_{6}}{f^{2}_{2}f^{2}_{3}f^{2}_{12}}\right)^{2}\pmod{3}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f^{5}_{1}f^{2}_{4}}{f^{6}_{2}}\frac{1}{\psi(-q)}\frac{f_{1}^{2}f_{4}^{2}f_{6}^{10}}{f_{2}^{4}f_{3}^{4}f_{12}^{4}}\pmod{3}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f^{5}_{1}f^{2}_{4}}{f^{6}_{2}}\frac{f_{2}}{f_{1}f_{4}}\frac{f_{1}^{2}f_{4}^{2}}{f_{2}^{4}}\frac{f_{6}^{10}}{f_{3}^{4}f_{12}^{4}}\pmod{3}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{1}^{6}f_{4}^{3}}{f_{2}^{9}}\frac{f_{6}^{10}}{f_{3}^{4}f_{12}^{4}}\pmod{3}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{3}^{2}f_{12}}{f_{6}^{3}}\frac{f_{6}^{10}}{f_{3}^{4}f_{12}^{4}}\pmod{3}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f^{7}_{6}}{f^{2}_{3}f^{3}_{12}}\pmod{3}.

We close this section by proving congruence families modulo 3 for infinitely many different functions
\overline{b}_{k}(n)
along specific arithmetic progressions. The proofs still follow from the elementary techniques demonstrated above.

Theorem 4.6
.

For all
l,m\geq 0
,
\overline{b}_{27l+8}(27m+18)\equiv 0\pmod{3}

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{27l+8}(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{12}^{9l+3}}{f_{3}^{18l+5}f_{6}^{9l+3}}\left(\frac{\psi(-q^{9})}{\psi(-q^{3})^{4}}\left(P(-q^{3})^{2}+qP(-q^{3})\psi(-q^{9})-q^{2}\psi(-q^{9})^{2}\right)\right)\pmod{3}.

Considering the terms of our
q
-series of the form
q^{3n}
, our congruence becomes

\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{27l+8}(3n)q^{3n}\equiv\frac{f_{12}^{9l+3}}{f_{3}^{18l+5}f_{6}^{9l+3}}\frac{\psi(-q^{9})}{\psi(-q^{3})^{4}}P(-q^{3})^{2}\pmod{3}.

Substituting
q
for
q^{3}
, we now have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{27l+8}(3n)q^{n}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{4}^{9l+3}}{f_{1}^{18l+5}f_{2}^{9l+3}}\frac{1}{\psi(-q)}P(-q)^{2}\pmod{3}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{12}^{3l-3}}{f_{3}^{6l+5}f_{6}^{3l-9}}\frac{1}{f_{6}}\frac{f_{4}}{f_{1}}\pmod{3}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{12}^{3l-3}}{f_{3}^{6l+5}f_{6}^{3l-8}}\frac{f_{4}}{f_{1}}\pmod{3}

using Theorem
2.9
.

2.10
and considering the terms of our

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{27l+8}(9n)q^{3n}

q
for

Theorem 4.7
.

For all
l
and
m\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\overline{b}_{27l+10}(3m+2)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{3},

\displaystyle\overline{b}_{27l+10}(27m+18)

\displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{3}.

Proof.

We know

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{27l+10}(n)q^{n}

Finally, we consider the terms of our

-series of the form

∎

5.
Concluding Thoughts

We close by highlighting two sets of thoughts. First, it is very easy to prove the following infinite family of congruences modulo
p
for any prime
p
.

Theorem 5.1
.

For
p
prime,
l\geq 1
,
m\geq 0
, and
a
such that
1\leq a<p
,
\overline{b}_{pl}(pm+a)\equiv 0\pmod{p}
.

Proof.

Let
p
be prime and
l\geq 1
.
Using Lemma
2.13
, note that

\sum_{n\geq 0}\overline{b}_{pl}(n)q^{n}=\frac{f_{4}^{pl}}{f_{1}^{2pl}f_{2}^{pl}}\equiv\frac{f_{4p}^{l}}{f_{p}^{2l}f_{2p}^{l}}\pmod{p}.

Our result immediately follows since

\frac{f_{4p}^{l}}{f_{p}^{2l}f_{2p}^{l}}

is a function of
q^{p}
.
∎

Secondly, in
[
3
, Theorem 3.1]
, Buragohain and Saikia note that

\overline{b}_{2^{\alpha}l+j}(n)\equiv\overline{b}_{j}(n)\pmod{2^{\alpha+1}}

for
l,n\geq 0
and
\alpha,j\geq 1
.
This result also holds for
\alpha=0
and
j=0
, and this leads to a significant family of divisibility properties moduli arbitrarily large powers of 2.

Theorem 5.2
.

For
\alpha,l\geq 0
and
n\geq 1
,

\overline{b}_{2^{\alpha}l}(n)\equiv 0\pmod{2^{\alpha+1}}.

Some of the congruences modulo small powers of 2 which we mentioned above (for example, some of the work of Shivaprasada Nayaka et. al.
[
30
]
) appear to follow from the above theorem.
