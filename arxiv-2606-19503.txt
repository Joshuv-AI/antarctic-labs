Kernel transformations and bounds for smeared spectral functions
William I. Jay, Matteo Saccardi

Abstract
This work develops a framework for transforming between smeared spectral functions computed using different smearing kernels. The kernel-transformation problem naturally arises when information is available for one family of energy-smeared observables, while phenomenology or comparison with other calculations require a different smearing. For exact transformations, analytic conditions are established for the maps to exist and converge without arbitrary regularization. Explicit expressions are provided for several kernel classes of interest, including Cauchy-to-Gaussian transformations and Gaussian-to-Cauchy width mixtures. When exact transformations are unavailable, the inverse problem is tackled through regulated maps paired with bounds on the associated systematic error, directly computable from the given input data. Errors on the input smeared spectral functions, either statistical or in the form of pointwise rigorous bounds, are then propagated to the target observables. Enforcing spectral positivity can be used to tighten the bounds.

Smeared spectral densities constitute a phenomenologically important class of observables in quantum field theories, especially quantum chromodynamics (QCD).

The bulk of the present work begins one step later.
The idea of the kernel-transformation problem is to use a family of energy-smeared observables as input to compute the same spectral function smeared with another kernel.
Different smearing kernels possess distinct theoretical and practical virtues.
For instance, Gaussian kernels have been argued to be useful for approaching the unsmeared limit

21

Meanwhile, the analytic structure of Cauchy and regulated principle-value kernels is closely related to the
i\varepsilon

Other kernels of interest include, but are not limited to, kinematically motivated kernels.
Kernel transformations may then be leveraged to define a convenient intermediate class of observables from which bounds on different regulated extractions of

In general, the input data for kernel transformations take the form of spectral integrals

\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\rho(\omega)\phi_{\alpha}(\omega)

The reconstruction of the target observable

The central novel contributions of the present work are as follows.
First, we develop the formalism of linear kernel-to-kernel transformations, establishing conditions under which these maps exist and converge without requiring arbitrary regularization, avoiding distortion of the target smeared observable.
Second, we provide explicit analytic expressions for several transformations between common smearing kernels, with the Cauchy-to-Gaussian map in

Third, we show how rigorous pointwise bounds on an input smeared spectral function can be propagated directly through a transformation using a sign-splitting argument inspired by Riesz–Kantorovich operator theory, leading to

Fourth, we formulate the propagation of bounds in a general kernel transformation as a convex-optimization problem, imposing spectral positivity and leading to certified upper and lower bounds in

The two methods naturally extend to the case of statistical error propagation.
Finally, whenever an approximation is introduced to render a kernel transformation practically tractable, we show how to certify the associated systematic error from the known input data in

II
Kernel transformations

The preceding discussion of kernel transformations as maps between families of kernels suggests a natural strategy to compute
\rho[\kappa]
from
\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]
.
Rather than targeting generically ill-defined direct reconstruction of
\rho(\omega)
, one can leverage linearity to express the target kernel
\kappa(\omega)
as a linear combination of the input kernel family
\{\phi_{\alpha}\}
.
The aim is to define a set of “transition kernels”
K^{\kappa\leftarrow\phi}(\alpha)
satisfying

\kappa(\omega)=\int_{A}d\alpha\,K^{\kappa\leftarrow\phi}(\alpha)\,\phi_{\alpha}(\omega)\,.

(4)

Whenever this representation is convergent, and the interchange of integrations over
\omega
and
\alpha
is justified, linearity of the functional
\rho[\kappa]
yields a direct map from the input data to the target observable:

\rho[\kappa]=\int_{A}d\alpha\,K^{\kappa\leftarrow\phi}(\alpha)\,\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]\,.

(5)

When such a map can be constructed, it provides the desired kernel transformation from the input data to the target observable.

The validity of the formal construction in
Eq.

5
depends strongly on the analytic properties of the kernels involved.
An elegant illustration of the success of this approach is provided by the time-momentum representation of the hadronic contributions to the muon anomalous magnetic moment,
a_{\mu}^{\rm{HVP}}

13
.
More generally, for

\phi_{t}(\omega)=e^{-\omega t}
in
Eq.

3
, the feasibility of kernel transformations is known to be closely related to the analytic properties of the target smearing kernel.
In particular, for LQCD applications, the existence of a mass gap
\omega_{0}>0
restricts the support of
\rho(\omega)
to
\omega>\omega_{0}
, and kernel transformations from
\phi_{t}=e^{-\omega t}
are well-defined only as long as any non-analytic structure of the target kernel
\kappa(\omega)
is confined to points
\omega_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
below the mass gap, i.e.,
\real\omega_{i}<\omega_{0}
.
This situation is indeed the case for
a_{\mu}^{\rm{HVP}}
.
A similar picture will be shown to hold for generic kernel transformations.

The construction of generic kernel transformations hinges on three logically distinct requirements:

1.

the input family of kernels must be complete on the space of target kernel(s),

2.

the transition kernel must exist as in
Eq.

5
without the need for regularization to tame uncontrolled divergences, and

3.

the interchange of the
\omega
and
\alpha
integrations must be justified.

Failure of any of these requirements prevents the unambiguous construction of an exact kernel transformation.

The remainder of this section develops a framework, based largely on standard techniques from Fourier analysis, to construct kernel transformations when they exist and to understand the analytic obstructions when they do not.

Section

II.1
formalizes the approach and establishes notation.

Section

II.2
treats the core examples of Cauchy and Gaussian smearing kernels used throughout the paper.

Section

II.3
records how the same criteria apply to finite-window and principal-value targets.

Section

II.4
discusses the construction of regulated transition kernels and the possibility to estimate the associated bias.

II.1
General realization of kernel transformations

Many smearing kernels display a dependence on continuous parameters beyond the integration variable (cf.
Eqs.

1
and
3
).
Common examples include Cauchy and Gaussian smearing kernels

\displaystyle K^{\mathtt{c}}_{\varepsilon}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon/\pi}{(\omega-\omega^{\prime})^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}\equiv\delta^{\mathtt{c}}_{\varepsilon}(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\,,

(6)

\displaystyle K^{\mathtt{g}}_{\sigma}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-\frac{(\omega-\omega^{\prime})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\equiv\delta^{\mathtt{g}}_{\sigma}(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\,,

(7)

which also depend on the width (
\varepsilon
and
\sigma
, respectively) and center (
\omega^{\prime}
) of the smearing.
For such kernels,
Eq.

1
can be rewritten as

\rho^{\mathtt{k}}_{\varepsilon}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}\,K^{\mathtt{k}}_{\varepsilon}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\rho(\omega^{\prime})\equiv[K^{\mathtt{k}}_{\varepsilon}\rho](\omega)\,,

(8)

where
\mathtt{k}\in\{\mathtt{c},\mathtt{g}\}
and the shorthand notation

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathtt{k}}_{\varepsilon}(\omega)\equiv[K^{\mathtt{k}}_{\varepsilon}\rho](\omega)\equiv\rho[\kappa](\omega)

(9)

is introduced for convenience.
The additional parameters provide a handle to construct the transition kernels in
Eq.

4
, by identifying them with the parameters
\alpha
.
For example, focusing on the dependence on
\alpha\equiv\omega
at fixed
\varepsilon
, the goal is to construct the linear operator

K^{\mathtt{k}_{2}\leftarrow\mathtt{k}_{1}}_{\varepsilon_{2}\leftarrow\varepsilon_{1}}:\rho^{\mathtt{k}_{1}}_{\varepsilon_{1}}(\omega)\mapsto\rho^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}_{\varepsilon_{2}}(\omega)

(10)

satisfying
Eq.

4
, i.e.

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}_{\varepsilon_{2}}(\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=[K^{\mathtt{k}_{2}\leftarrow\mathtt{k}_{1}}_{\varepsilon_{2}\leftarrow\varepsilon_{1}}\rho^{\mathtt{k}_{1}}_{\varepsilon_{1}}](\omega^{\prime})

(11)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\omega\,K^{\mathtt{k}_{2}\leftarrow\mathtt{k}_{1}}_{\varepsilon_{2}\leftarrow\varepsilon_{1}}(\omega^{\prime},\omega)\rho^{\mathtt{k}_{1}}_{\varepsilon_{1}}(\omega)\,.

Appendix
A
contains additional details relating the normalization of input and target smearing functions with that of the transition kernels.

Postponing momentarily questions of existence and convergence, one formally finds

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}_{\varepsilon_{2}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=[K_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}\rho](\omega)

(12)

\displaystyle=[K_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}(K_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\mathtt{k}_{1}})^{-1}][K_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\mathtt{k}_{1}}\rho](\omega)

(13)

\displaystyle=[K_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}(K_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\mathtt{k}_{1}})^{-1}]\rho^{\mathtt{k}_{1}}_{\varepsilon_{1}}(\omega)

(14)

where
(K_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\mathtt{k}_{1}})^{-1}
indicates the formal operator inverse of
K_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\mathtt{k}_{1}}
.
The linear map that transforms between kernels is therefore given formally by

\displaystyle K^{\mathtt{k}_{2}\leftarrow\mathtt{k}_{1}}_{\varepsilon_{2}\leftarrow\varepsilon_{1}}(\omega^{\prime},\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\omega^{\prime\prime}\,K_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}(\omega^{\prime},\omega^{\prime\prime})(K_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\mathtt{k}_{1}})^{-1}(\omega^{\prime\prime},\omega)\,.

(15)

In other words,
Eq.

4
is explicitly realized as

\displaystyle K_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\omega^{\prime\prime}\,K^{\mathtt{k}_{2}\leftarrow\mathtt{k}_{1}}_{\varepsilon_{2}\leftarrow\varepsilon_{1}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime\prime})K_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\mathtt{k}_{1}}(\omega^{\prime\prime},\omega)\,.

(16)

Of course, the existence of the inverse and the convergence of the integrals above are essential for any practical use of these expressions.
The key point of this construction is that, although single inverse integral operators might remain analytically intractable and possibly diverge, only the product
K_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\kappa_{2}}(K_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\kappa_{1}})^{-1}
is actually required.
When the latter converges, exact kernel transformations can be realized without any approximation or regularization.

For a general pair of kernels, computing
K^{\mathtt{k}_{2}\leftarrow\mathtt{k}_{1}}_{\varepsilon_{2}\leftarrow\varepsilon_{1}}
may be analytically cumbersome, both for convergent integrals as well as for cases requiring regularization.
In any case, it is envisioned that the transition kernel can be approximated numerically, effectively regulating the problem.
Irrespective of the details of this realization,
Eq.

12
can be defined via intermediate regularized products in
Eq.

14
even when
K^{\mathtt{k}_{2}\leftarrow\mathtt{k}_{1}}_{\varepsilon_{2}\leftarrow\varepsilon_{1}}
remains formally inaccessible by itself.

Perhaps simplest strategy is to discretize the energies along a suitable grid, so that the right-hand side of
Eq.

8
should be understood as a matrix-vector product.
Other approaches are possible to approximate the expansion of the target on the input kernels in
Eq.

16
.
For instance, an approximation might emerge by minimizing a certain notion of distance (e.g.,
L^{2}
in Ref.
35
) between the approximate and target kernels.
Given any explicit regularization of the problem,
Section

II.4
discusses a method to quantify the associated systematic effects.
This quantification does not depend on the details of the realization of the approximate target smearing kernel and is phrased in terms of quantities which can be directly accessed from the input data only.

II.2
Gaussian and Cauchy kernel transformations

For translationally invariant input kernels

K^{\mathtt{k}_{1}}_{\varepsilon_{1}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\delta_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\mathtt{k}_{1}}(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\,,

(17)

the kernel transformation map of

With the Fourier conjugate of the kernel defined via

\hat{\delta}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{k}}(\tau)\equiv\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\delta_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{k}}(\omega)e^{-i\omega\tau}\,,

the convolution theorem gives the transition kernel

2

The factor of

\displaystyle f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}dy\,K(x-y)g(y)\,,

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\tau)\equiv\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{dx}{2\pi}f(x)e^{-i\tau x}=2\pi\hat{K}(\tau)\hat{g}(\tau)\,,

\displaystyle K(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\tau\,\hat{K}(\tau)e^{+i\tau x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\tau}{2\pi}\hat{f}(\tau)\hat{g}(\tau)^{-1}e^{+i\tau x}\,.

K^{\mathtt{k}_{2}\leftarrow\mathtt{k}_{1}}_{\varepsilon_{2}\leftarrow\varepsilon_{1}}(\omega^{\prime},\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\tau}{2\pi}\hat{K}_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}(\omega^{\prime},\tau)\hat{\delta}_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\mathtt{k}_{1}}(\tau)^{-1}e^{+i\tau\omega}

where
\hat{K}_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}(\omega^{\prime},\tau)
indicates the Fourier transform of

with respect to
\omega
only.
If
K_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}
also depends on the difference
\omega-\omega^{\prime}
only through
K^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}_{\varepsilon_{2}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\delta_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}(\omega-\omega^{\prime})

K^{\mathtt{k}_{2}\leftarrow\mathtt{k}_{1}}_{\varepsilon_{2}\leftarrow\varepsilon_{1}}(\omega^{\prime},\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\tau}{2\pi}\hat{\delta}_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}(\tau)\hat{\delta}_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\mathtt{k}_{1}}(\tau)^{-1}e^{+i\tau(\omega-\omega^{\prime})}

(20)

which is a function of
\omega^{\prime}-\omega
, as expected.
For the sake of concreteness and practical interest, consider the Cauchy and Gaussian kernels defined in
Eqs.

6
and
7

Their Fourier conjugates are

\displaystyle\hat{\delta}_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{g}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\delta_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{g}}(\omega)e^{-i\omega\tau}=\frac{e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2}\sigma^{2}}}{2\pi}\,,

(21)

\displaystyle\hat{\delta}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{c}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\delta_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{c}}(\omega)e^{-i\omega\tau}=\frac{e^{-\varepsilon|\tau|}}{2\pi}\,.

(22)

The next three examples form the core exact transformations used to organize the later discussion.
The Gaussian-to-Gaussian and Cauchy-to-Cauchy cases show the familiar widening semigroups, while the Cauchy-to-Gaussian case provides the main nontrivial exact bridge used below for bound propagation and numerical tests.
The discussion then turns to transformations that fail to converge in the fixed-width Fourier representation of
Eq.

20
, as well as to a different transformation realized by a linear combination of kernels centered around the same energy but with different smearing widths.

Gaussian to Gaussian.

The transition kernel between two Gaussians is given via
Eq.

20
by

\displaystyle K_{\sigma_{2}\leftarrow\sigma_{1}}^{\mathtt{g}\leftarrow\mathtt{g}}(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\tau}{2\pi}\hat{\delta}_{\sigma_{2}}^{\mathtt{g}}(\tau)\hat{\delta}_{\sigma_{1}}^{\mathtt{g}}(\tau)^{-1}e^{+i\tau(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)}

(23)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\tau}{2\pi}e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2}(\sigma_{2}^{2}-\sigma_{1}^{2})}e^{+i\tau(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)}

(24)

\displaystyle=\delta^{\mathtt{g}}_{\sqrt{\sigma_{2}^{2}-\sigma_{1}^{2}}}(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)\,.

(25)

The integral in the second line is absolutely convergent for
\sigma_{2}\geq\sigma_{1}
only.

Eq.

25
defines the well-known semigroup property of Gaussian kernels under convolution.
Unregulated deconvolution (
\sigma_{2}<\sigma_{1}
) is impermissible, while additional smearing (
\sigma_{2}>\sigma_{1}
) presents no obstruction.
Unsurprisingly, Gaussian data can be transformed exactly to a broader Gaussian resolution, but not sharpened by this unregulated map.

Cauchy to Cauchy.

For the case of two Cauchy kernels,
Eq.

20
becomes

\displaystyle K_{\varepsilon_{2}\leftarrow\varepsilon_{1}}^{\mathtt{c}\leftarrow\mathtt{c}}(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\tau}{2\pi}\hat{\delta}_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\mathtt{c}}(\tau)\hat{\delta}_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\mathtt{c}}(\tau)^{-1}e^{+i\tau(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)}

(26)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\tau}{2\pi}e^{-(\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1})|\tau|}e^{+i\tau(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)}

(27)

\displaystyle=\delta^{\mathtt{c}}_{\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}}(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)\,,

(28)

where the integral in the second line is absolutely convergent for
\varepsilon_{2}\geq\varepsilon_{1}
only.

Eq.

28
defines the semigroup property of Cauchy kernels under convolution.
As with the preceding Gaussian-to-Gaussian example, unregulated deconvolution is not defined, while additional smearing proceeds without problem.
As discussed below, this fact can be interpreted in terms of the analytic structure of the Cauchy kernel in the complex plane.
Thus, Cauchy data can also be transformed exactly to a broader Cauchy resolution, while sharpening requires a regulated treatment.

Cauchy to Gaussian.

For the reconstruction of a Gaussian target smearing from a Cauchy input, the transition kernel from
Eq.

20
takes the form

\displaystyle K_{\sigma\leftarrow\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{g}\leftarrow\mathtt{c}}(\omega^{\prime},\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\tau}{2\pi}\,\hat{\delta}_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{g}}(\tau)\hat{\delta}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{c}}(\tau)^{-1}e^{+i\tau(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)}

(29)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\tau}{2\pi}\,e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2}\sigma^{2}}e^{+\varepsilon|\tau|}e^{+i\tau(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\mathrm{Re}\left\{e^{z^{2}}[1+\erf(z)]\right\}\,,

Gaussian to Cauchy.

The reverse transformation, from Gaussian-smeared data at one fixed width to a specified Cauchy target, fails the Fourier-ratio convergence criterion in
Eq.

20

In this setting only the center of the Gaussian is varied, and the desired fixed-width transition would require

\displaystyle K_{\varepsilon\leftarrow\sigma}^{\mathtt{c}\leftarrow\mathtt{g}}(\omega^{\prime},\omega)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\tau}{2\pi}\,\hat{\delta}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{c}}(\tau)\hat{\delta}_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{g}}(\tau)^{-1}e^{+i\tau(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\tau}{2\pi}\,e^{+\tfrac{1}{2}\tau^{2}\sigma^{2}}e^{-\varepsilon|\tau|}e^{+i\tau(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)}

(34)

\displaystyle=\infty\,.

(35)

In other words, the transformation from a fixed Gaussian width to a specified Cauchy kernel via
Eq.

11

However, this obstruction does not rule out every Gaussian-to-Cauchy construction.
If, instead, one has access to Gaussian-smeared observables over a continuum of widths at a fixed center, a Cauchy-smeared observable at that same center can be formed by a linear mixture of different widths.
The starting point is the elementary integral

\frac{1}{x}=\int_{0}^{\infty}dy\,e^{-yx}\,.

(36)

Taking the denominator on the left-hand side to be that of the Cauchy kernel,
x=(\omega-\omega^{\prime})^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}

\delta_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{c}}(\omega-\omega^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\sigma\,2\sigma f(\sigma^{2};0,\varepsilon^{2})\delta_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{g}}(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\,,

(37)

where

f(x;\mu,c)\equiv\sqrt{\frac{c}{2\pi}}\frac{e^{-\frac{c}{2(x-\mu)}}}{(x-\mu)^{3/2}}

(38)

\sigma
to the dimensionless quantity
x=\sigma/\varepsilon
yields

\delta_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{c}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,2xf(x^{2};0,1)\delta_{x\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{g}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\,.

The variable
x
can be interpreted as the fraction of Gaussian smearing width with respect to the target Cauchy smearing width.
The corresponding smeared spectral function is then recovered as in
Eq.

39
through

\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{c}}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,2xf(x^{2};0,1)\rho_{x\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{g}}(\omega)\,.

This does not contradict the fixed-width failure above: the input data here are the family
\{\rho_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{g}}(\omega)\}_{\sigma>0}
at the chosen center, rather than a single Gaussian width as the center varies.
Note that contributions from small values of
\sigma
are exponentially suppressed by the Lévy function’s zero of infinite order at
\sigma=0
.
This behavior may be beneficial for practical reconstructions, since
\rho_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{g}}
is typically the most difficult to obtain in that region, with the uncertainties increasing sharply for
\sigma\to 0
.

The analogous reverse width mixture cannot be defined unambiguously, as it would require a function
g(\varepsilon)
satisfying

\delta_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{g}}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\varepsilon\,g(\varepsilon)\delta_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{c}}(\omega)\,.

(40)

Taking the Fourier transform of both sides, using
Eqs.

and

reveals
g(\varepsilon)
to be the inverse Laplace transform of
e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}
,

e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\varepsilon\,g(\varepsilon)e^{-\varepsilon|\tau|}\,,

(41)

which in turn does not exist, even as a highly singular or tempered distribution.

Cauchy to a generic kernel.

The preceding examples involving Cauchy kernels can be understood as special cases of a general analytic criterion.
Consider the transformation from a Cauchy kernel to an arbitrary, translationally invariant kernel
\kappa(\omega^{\prime},\omega)=\delta^{\mathtt{k}}_{\sigma}(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)
with Fourier conjugate
\hat{\delta}^{\mathtt{k}}_{\sigma}(\tau)
.
The question of existence again hinges on the convergence of
Eq.

20
in the form

\displaystyle K_{\sigma\leftarrow\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{k}\leftarrow\mathtt{c}}(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\tau}{2\pi}\hat{\delta}^{\mathtt{k}}_{\sigma}(\tau)e^{+\varepsilon|\tau|}e^{+i\tau(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)}\,.

(42)

A practical sufficient condition can be stated in terms of the analytic structure of
\delta^{\mathtt{k}}_{\sigma}(z)
, considered as a function of
z\in\mathbb{C}
.
To ensure convergence of
Eq.

42
, it suffices to require that
\delta^{\mathtt{k}}_{\sigma}(z)
be a meromorphic function whose poles
z_{i}
lie farther from the real line than the input Cauchy width, i.e., satisfying
\min_{i}|\imag z_{i}|>\varepsilon
.
This result is a consequence of the classical Paley–Wiener theorem
57
(see also Theorem IX.13 of Ref.
58
).

The preceding results may also be interpreted in light of this theorem.
For example, the success of the Cauchy-to-Cauchy smearing for
\varepsilon_{2}>\varepsilon_{1}
in
Eq.

28
is an immediate corollary.
The failure of fixed-width Gaussian-to-Cauchy smearing in
Eq.

34
is the
\varepsilon\to\infty
limiting case of this general result, ultimately traceable to the Gaussian kernel’s essential singularity at complex infinity.
Thus, Cauchy-smeared data of width
\varepsilon
can be mapped exactly to target kernels whose nearest complex singularities are separated from the real axis by more than
\varepsilon
.

II.3
Phenomenologically motivated target kernels

The same convergence logic applies to target kernels that are not in the classes of Gaussian or Cauchy test smearings considered above.
The following examples indicate how convergence requirements emerge from the Fourier-space damping of the target kernel.

Finite-interval observables.

Many physical observables can be expressed as weighted integrals of the spectral function over a finite interval of the real line

\displaystyle\Gamma=\int_{\omega_{0}}^{\omega_{1}}d\omega\,R(\omega)\rho(\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\omega\,R(\omega)W(\omega;\omega_{0},\omega_{1})\rho(\omega)\,,

(43)

where
R(\omega)
is a known rational function and
W(\omega;\omega_{0},\omega_{1})
is a rectangular window with support on
[\omega_{0},\omega_{1}]
, i.e.,

\displaystyle W(\omega;\omega_{0},\omega_{1})=\vartheta(\omega-\omega_{0})\vartheta(\omega_{1}-\omega)\,,

(44)

where
\vartheta(\cdot)
is the Heaviside step function.
In physical terms, the lower integration bound
\omega_{0}
is often related to the presence of a mass gap in the theory, below which the spectral function does not have support.
The upper bound
\omega_{1}
instead typically comes from a phase-space threshold or cutoff, e.g., the mass of a heavy decaying particle.
Kinematic considerations usually dictate the functional form of the rational function
R(\omega)
.

The abrupt bounds of the window function in
Eq.

44
are often smoothed in applications
34
;
33
;
31
.
Let us introduce the following smeared version of the function
R(\omega)W(\omega;\omega_{0},\omega_{1})

\displaystyle RW_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}(\omega;\omega_{0},\omega_{1})\equiv\int d\omega^{\prime}\,\delta^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}_{\sigma}(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)R(\omega^{\prime})W(\omega^{\prime};\omega_{0},\omega_{1})\,,

(45)

with
\delta^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}_{\sigma}(\omega)\to\delta(\omega)
as
\sigma\to 0
.
A possible choice for this purpose is either kernel in
Eqs.

7
and
6
, but other alternatives can be considered.

Equation

45
induces a smoothed version of the integral

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\int d\omega\,RW^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}_{\sigma}(\omega)\rho(\omega)

(46)

\displaystyle=\int d\omega^{\prime}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\tau\,\widehat{RW^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}_{\sigma}}(\tau;\omega_{0},\omega_{1})e^{i\tau\omega^{\prime}}\rho(\omega^{\prime})\,.

(47)

To rewrite the integral above in terms of the (input) spectral function smeared with
\delta_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{k}_{1}}(\omega)
, it is useful to leverage the identity

e^{i\tau\omega^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{2\pi\hat{\delta}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{k}_{1}}(\tau)}\int d\omega\,\delta_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{k}_{1}}(\omega-\omega^{\prime})e^{i\tau\omega}\,,

(48)

which holds for all
\varepsilon>0
.
Exchanging the order of integrations gives:

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\int d\omega\,\left[\int\frac{d\tau}{2\pi}\frac{\widehat{RW^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}_{\sigma}}(\tau;\omega_{0},\omega_{1})}{\hat{\delta}^{\mathtt{k}_{1}}_{\varepsilon}(\tau)}e^{i\tau\omega}\right]\rho^{\mathtt{k}_{1}}_{\varepsilon}(\omega)

(49)

\displaystyle=\int d\omega\,\left[\int\frac{d\tau}{2\pi}\widehat{RW}(\tau;\omega_{0},\omega_{1})\frac{\hat{\delta}^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}_{\sigma}(\tau)}{\hat{\delta}^{\mathtt{k}_{1}}_{\varepsilon}(\tau)}e^{i\tau\omega}\right]\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{k}_{1}}(\omega)\,,

(50)

where the convolution theorem has been used in the second identity, and
\widehat{RW}
denotes the Fourier transform of the rational function on the window,
R(\omega)W(\omega;\omega_{0},\omega_{1})
.
Whenever it converges, the final line defines the transition kernel mapping the smeared spectral function
\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{k}_{1}}(\omega)
to a smeared version of
Eq.

43
satisfying
\Gamma^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}_{\sigma}\to\Gamma
as
\sigma\to 0
.

In order for
Eq.

50
to provide a well-defined transition kernel, the decay of
\widehat{RW}(\tau;\omega_{0},\omega_{1})\hat{\delta}_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}(\tau)
must compensate the growth of
\hat{\delta}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{k}_{1}}(\tau)^{-1}
.
The sharp window by itself gives only power-law Fourier damping.
This can be seen directly in the special case
R(\omega)=1
, for which

\displaystyle\widehat{RW}(\tau;\omega_{0},\omega_{1})

\displaystyle=\widehat{W}(\tau;\omega_{0},\omega_{1})=\int_{\omega_{0}}^{\omega_{1}}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}e^{-i\omega\tau}

(51)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2\pi\tau}\left(e^{-i\omega_{1}\tau}-e^{-i\omega_{0}\tau}\right)\,,

(52)

which is finite at
\tau=0
and decays as
1/\tau
as
|\tau|\to\infty
.
The same asymptotic behavior holds for a nontrivial rational function
R(\omega)
, provided it is analytic in the interval
[\omega_{0},\omega_{1}]
.
The power-law scaling follows from integration by parts:

\displaystyle\widehat{RW}(\tau;\omega_{0},\omega_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2\pi\tau}\bigg\{[R(\omega_{1})e^{-i\omega_{1}\tau}-R(\omega_{0})e^{-i\omega_{0}\tau}]

(53)

\displaystyle-\int_{\omega_{0}}^{\omega_{1}}d\omega\,R^{\prime}(\omega)e^{-i\omega\tau}\bigg\}\,.

By the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma and analyticity of
R
in the interval
[\omega_{0},\omega_{1}]
, the remaining integral is bounded and tends to zero as
|\tau|\to\infty
.
Thus the sharp-window contribution is
O(1/|\tau|)
.

The convergence of
Eq.

50
is therefore guaranteed, provided that the asymptotic decay of
\hat{\delta}^{\mathtt{k}_{2}}_{\sigma}(\tau)
dominates the rise in
\hat{\delta}^{\mathtt{k}_{1}}_{\varepsilon}(\tau)^{-1}
.
For this reason, a similar picture as that of
Section

II.2
emerges.
For instance, an input Cauchy-smeared spectral function
\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{c}}(\omega)
can always be mapped to a Gaussian-smeared
\Gamma^{\mathtt{g}}_{\sigma}
.
Likewise, an input Gaussian-smeared spectral function
\rho_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{g}}(\omega)
can be mapped to a Gaussian-smeared
\Gamma^{\mathtt{g}}_{\sigma^{\prime}}
provided
\sigma^{\prime}>\sigma
, and similarly for the Cauchy-to-Cauchy case.

Another possible approach to smoothing
Eq.

43
comes from directly smearing the step functions.
Introducing a regularized Heaviside function
\vartheta_{\sigma}
, with common examples including the logistic or error functions

\displaystyle\vartheta^{\rm logistic}_{\sigma}(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x/\sigma}}\,,\quad\vartheta^{\rm erf}_{\sigma}(x)=\frac{1+\mathrm{erf}(x/\sigma)}{2}\,,

(54)

leads to the smeared window

\displaystyle W^{\mathtt{x}}_{\sigma}(\omega;\omega_{0},\omega_{1})=\vartheta^{\mathtt{x}}_{\sigma}(\omega-\omega_{0})\vartheta^{\mathtt{x}}_{\sigma}(\omega_{1}-\omega),

(55)

with
\mathtt{x}\in\{\mathrm{logistic},\mathrm{erf}\}
.
It is also common only to smear the step function localized at the kinematic threshold
\omega_{1}
, relying on integration against
\rho(\omega)
to remove contributions from
\omega<\omega_{0}
.
Either case leads to the smoothed version of the integral in
Eq.

43
as

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\mathtt{x}}_{\sigma}=\int d\omega\,R(\omega)W_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{x}}(\omega;\omega_{0},\omega_{1})\rho(\omega)\,.

(56)

The derivation of
Eq.

50
relies crucially on the convolution theorem.
The choice of the smeared window in
Eq.

56
, a similar explicit discussion is complicated by the fact that the product
R(\omega)W_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{x}}(\omega;\omega_{0},\omega_{1})
amounts to the convolution
(\hat{R}*\hat{W}_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{x}})(\tau;\omega_{0},\omega_{1})
in Fourier space, rather than a simpler product.
Although the calculation now depends on the functional form of the rational function, the general picture remains unchanged, as can be explicitly verified e.g., for
R(\omega)=1
.
The Fourier-space damping implied by the smoothed window function must dominate the growth of
\hat{\delta}^{\mathtt{k}_{1}}_{\varepsilon}(\tau)^{-1}
in order to define the corresponding transition kernel.

Regulated principal-value kernels.

Regulated versions of the principal value (P.V.) prescription constitute another class of phenomenologically interesting smearing kernels, which arise naturally in non-perturbative applications of the LSZ formula
20
;
29
.
The associated kernel is translationally invariant and has the form

\Pi_{\varepsilon}(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\equiv\real\left[\frac{1}{\omega-\omega^{\prime}+i\varepsilon}\right]=\frac{\omega-\omega^{\prime}}{(\omega-\omega^{\prime})^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}\,.

(57)

The standard P.V. prescription is recovered from the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit of the integral of this kernel with a test function.

For concreteness, consider the transition map from a Cauchy kernel of width
\varepsilon_{1}
to a P.V. kernel of width
\varepsilon_{2}
.
Translational invariance allows
Eq.

42
to be applied directly.
The Fourier transform of
Eq.

57
is:

\hat{\Pi}_{\varepsilon_{2}}(\tau)=-\frac{i}{2}e^{-\varepsilon_{2}|\tau|}\left[\vartheta(\tau)-\vartheta(-\tau)\right]\,.

(58)

Therefore the transition map is given by

\Pi_{\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}}(\omega-\omega^{\prime})=\frac{\omega^{\prime}-\omega}{(\omega^{\prime}-\omega)^{2}+(\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1})^{2}}\,.

(59)

Thus, a transition to a P.V. kernel of width
\varepsilon_{2}
from a Cauchy kernel of width
\varepsilon_{1}
is accessible whenever
\varepsilon_{2}>\varepsilon_{1}
.
This result is also easily interpretable in terms of the analytic properties of the input and smearing kernels (see the discussion below
Eq.

42
), which are respectively related to the imaginary and real parts of the same function, cf.
Eq.

57
.
For the same reason, this discussion generalizes straightforwardly to the other kernel transformations considered above.
Given the identical asymptotic decay of the P.V. and Cauchy kernels in Fourier space, the convergence conditions are analogous to those derived for transitions involving the Cauchy kernel.

II.4
Regulated kernel transformations

The discussion so far has mostly focused on exact kernel transformations that exist unambiguously.
However, the results of
Section

II.2
already presented instances of maps where the desired operator does not always exist as a continuum object without intermediate regularization.
This extends to instances of inverse problems from Euclidean correlation functions as input data.
To render these transitions tractable, one possibility is to replace the target kernel
\kappa
by an approximation
\kappa_{T}
that can actually be realized from the input family.
The observable
\rho[\kappa_{T}]
can then be exactly extracted as a smeared observable for the modified kernel.
The difference
\rho[\kappa]-\rho[\kappa_{T}]
represents a bias that must be quantified, for instance with bounds.
Moreover, even when an exact operator exists in the continuum, it can be practically convenient to make this replacement for numerical applications.

For example, an approximation can be explicitly realized by regularizing any of the above integrals in Fourier space as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\tau\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\tau F_{T}(\tau)\,,

(60)

where the cutoff function
F_{T}(\tau)\in[0,1]
satisfies
F_{T}(\tau)\to 1
for
|\tau|\ll T
and
F_{T}(\tau)\to 0
for
|\tau|\gg T
.
This treatment defines regulated transition kernels, generically denoted
K^{\kappa\leftarrow\phi}_{T}
to mirror
Eq.

5
, and thus

\displaystyle\rho[\kappa]=\lim_{T\to\infty}\rho[\kappa_{T}]\,,\quad\rho[\kappa_{T}]=\int_{A}d\alpha\,K^{\kappa\leftarrow\phi}_{T}(\alpha)\,\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]\,.

(61)

The cutoff function
F_{T}(\tau)
can be realized in different ways, e.g., through a hard or smooth Heaviside
\vartheta(T-|\tau|)
function.

Alternative approximations are possible, for instance by discretizing the energy-space,

\displaystyle\int d\omega\,f(\omega)\to\sum_{i}\Delta\omega_{i}f(\omega_{i})\,,

(62)

which leads immediately to the approximations
4
4

4

The vector
[K_{\kappa}]_{i}
also depends on any additional parameters defining the target kernel
\kappa
.
The present focus is solely on the internal index
i
related to discretized energies.

\displaystyle\rho[\phi_{\alpha_{j}}]=\int d\omega\,\phi_{\alpha_{j}}(\omega)\rho(\omega)\approx\sum_{i}[K_{\phi}]_{ji}\rho(\omega_{i})\,,

(63)

\displaystyle\rho[\kappa]=\int d\omega\,\kappa(\omega)\rho(\omega)\approx\sum_{i}[K_{\kappa}]_{i}\rho(\omega_{i})\,,

(64)

in terms of the finite matrix
[K_{\phi}]_{ji}=\phi_{\alpha_{j}}(\omega_{i})
and the finite vector
[K_{\kappa}]_{i}=\kappa(\omega_{i})
.
The regulated transition operator
K^{\kappa\leftarrow\phi}
is then given by

\displaystyle\rho[\kappa_{T}]=K_{T}^{\kappa\leftarrow\phi}\rho[\phi]\,,\quad K_{T}^{\kappa\leftarrow\phi}\equiv K_{\kappa}K_{\phi}^{-1}\,,

(65)

where the summation over matrix indices is left implicit.
Ref.
62
proposes a similar strategy for the case of
\phi_{\alpha_{j}}(\omega_{i})=e^{-\alpha_{j}\omega_{i}}
, with useful considerations on the singular value decomposition of the associated
K_{\phi}
.

Regardless of the details of its realization, any regularized transition kernel effectively defines a
different
smearing kernel
\kappa_{T}
, with which the spectral function is smeared exactly.
For instance,
\kappa_{T}(\omega)
is implicitly defined by
Eq.

61
.
By first inserting the cutoff function
F_{T}(\tau)
into
Eqs.

19
and
20
, integrating gives

\displaystyle\kappa_{T}(\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\tau F_{T}(\tau)\hat{\kappa}(\tau)e^{+i\tau\omega}\,.

(66)

Likewise, the approximate kernel in
Eq.

65
takes the form

\displaystyle\kappa_{T}(\omega)=\sum_{i}[K_{\kappa}]_{i}\delta_{T}(\omega_{i},\omega)\,,

(67)

where
\delta_{T}
defines a regularized Dirac
\delta
function:
5
5

5

Notice that
\delta_{T}(\omega_{i},\omega_{j})=\delta_{ij}
when evaluated at the grid point
\omega=\omega_{j}
, but this is not true for a generic
\omega
at fixed regulator (energy spacing).
As the regulator is removed, i.e., as the grid is taken to be dense,
\delta_{T}
approaches the Dirac
\delta
function in the usual distributional sense.

\displaystyle\delta_{T}(\omega_{i},\omega)=\sum_{j}[K_{\phi}^{-1}]_{ij}\phi_{\alpha_{j}}(\omega)\,.

(68)

Note that this is a common aspect of many approaches to spectral reconstructions, including Backus–Gilbert
8
;
36
, HLT
35
;
48
, Chebyshev polynomials
9
, and Mellin-based methods
17
.
These strategies realize different approximations to
\kappa_{T}(\omega)
and
\delta_{T}(\omega_{i},\omega)
; see, e.g., Ref.
17
for explicit examples.
The key question is the extent to which the bias induced by the regularization,

\displaystyle\mathrm{bias}=\rho[\kappa]-\rho[\kappa_{T}]\,,

(69)

can be successfully estimated or reliably bounded.

II.5
Bias in regulated kernel transformations

Regulated transformations reduce the remaining problem to that of quantifying the associated kernel-mismatch bias.
A precise quantification of the bias is often inaccessible in practice, but the input data alone can still suffice to estimate an upper bound.
For any non-negative spectral density
\rho
and positive smearing kernel
\kappa
, Hölder’s inequality implies

\displaystyle\rho[\kappa]-\rho[\kappa_{T}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\rho(\omega)\left[\kappa(\omega)-\kappa_{T}(\omega)\right]

(70)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{\kappa(\omega)-\kappa_{T}(\omega)}{\phi_{\alpha}(\omega)}\right\|_{\infty}\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]

(71)

for any arbitrary positive kernel function
\phi_{\alpha}
, with the inequality holding for any fixed value of
\alpha
.
The norm is to be understood as the pointwise maximum of the absolute value of its argument.
6
6

6

More formally,
\|f(\omega)\|_{\infty}=\sup_{\omega}|f(\omega)|
.
Since the difference between
\inf/\min
and
\sup/\max
plays no important role in the applications at hand, this distinction is omitted here and elsewhere in the main text.

The right-hand side of
Eq.

71
can now be quantified directly from the input data for
\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]
.
Alternative bounds, specific to particular reconstruction methods, exist in certain cases including Ref.
43
;
16
;
17
;
62
.
In such instances,
Eq.

71
constitutes a complementary estimate.

The bound in
Eq.

71
is certainly not necessarily tight.
Given the freedom in choosing
\alpha
, it might be useful to minimize this bound with respect to
\alpha
.
However, this line of thinking can only be of practical use when the norm is not divergent or too large compared to
\rho[\kappa_{T}]
.
In particular, the ratio
\kappa(\omega)/\phi_{\alpha}(\omega)
should not diverge in the first place.
For example, this tail-compatibility requirement excludes Gaussian-to-Cauchy targets but allows Gaussian-to-Gaussian cases that are not feasible through the exact analytic transformations defined in
Section

II.2
.

For the case of input Euclidean time correlation functions, where
\phi_{t}(\omega)=e^{-\omega t}
,
Eq.

71
furnishes upper bounds on the bias for a target Gaussian smearing kernel, but not for a Cauchy one.
Alternative smearing kernels might be targeted instead, allowing a proper quantification of the bias.
For example, one could use

\displaystyle\delta_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{c}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime};t_{0})=t_{0}e^{-\omega t_{0}}\delta_{\varepsilon t_{0}}^{\mathtt{c}}(e^{-\omega t_{0}},e^{-\omega^{\prime}t_{0}})\,,

(72)

which recovers a standard Cauchy kernel in
Eq.

6
in the
t_{0}\to 0
limit, while still satisfying
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\delta_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{c}}(\omega,\omega^{\prime};t_{0})=\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})
, since the prefactors of the modified Cauchy kernel precisely cancel those coming from the composition rule of the Dirac delta function for the argument
\phi_{t_{0}}(\omega)=e^{-\omega t_{0}}
.

Although
Eq.

71
is the main kernel-level bound used later, it is not the only possible estimate.
As a secondary illustration, the Fourier-space cutoff in
Eq.

60
provides an alternative strategy to quantify the bias.
For the sake of concreteness, consider a Gaussian-to-Gaussian transformation.
In Fourier space, the smeared spectral function takes the form

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{g}}(\tau)=e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\hat{\rho}(\tau)\,,

(73)

with its regularized version being
F_{T}(\tau)\hat{\rho}_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{g}}(\tau)
.
The energy-space bias in
Eq.

69
is precisely that induced by the cutoff function,

\displaystyle\rho_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{g}}(\omega)-[{\rho}^{\mathtt{g}}_{\sigma}]_{T}(\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\tau\,(1-F_{T}(\tau))\,\hat{\rho}^{\mathtt{g}}_{\sigma}(\tau)\,e^{i\tau\omega}\,.

(74)

This expression immediately implies a pointwise bound on the bias:

\displaystyle\lVert\rho_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{g}}-[{\rho}_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{g}}]_{T}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\tau\,|1-F_{T}(\tau)|\,|\hat{\rho}^{\mathtt{g}}_{\sigma}(\tau)|

(75)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\tau\,|1-F_{T}(\tau)|\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\,|\hat{\rho}(\tau)|\,.

(76)

In the case of a hard cutoff
F_{T}(\tau)=\vartheta(T-|\tau|)
, Hölder’s inequality further implies

\displaystyle\lVert\rho_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{g}}-[{\rho}_{\sigma}^{\mathtt{g}}]_{T}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\lVert\rho\rVert_{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\mathrm{erfc}\left(\frac{\sigma T}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\,.

(77)

Here the bound uses
\rho(\omega)\geq 0
together with the estimate:

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{\rho}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\max_{\tau}\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\rho(\omega)e^{-i\omega\tau}\right|

(78)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\rho(\omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\lVert\rho\rVert_{1}\,,

(79)

which in turn might be quantifiable, for example from sum rules.
In other words, the cutoff smeared spectral function converges exponentially as the cutoff is removed.
Similar results can be derived for other kernel transformations.
Note that this bound is uniform across different centers of the target Gaussian smearing kernel, whereas the bound in
Eq.

71
is specific to a given center.

III
Bounds and Asymptotics

This section is dedicated to the problem of propagating bounds in kernel transformations, leveraging the positivity of the underlying spectral function.
Assume that pointwise upper and lower bounds are available for the input smeared quantities,

\displaystyle\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]_{-}\leq\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]\leq\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]_{+}\,.

(80)

The goal is to construct rigorous bounds on a target observable
\rho[\kappa]
, either after an exact transformation such as
Eq.

5
or after a regulated replacement of the target kernel.
If the transition kernels were non-negative, this propagation would be immediate by integrating the lower and upper input bounds against the same positive weights.
However, as
Fig.

1
shows, transition kernels are generically oscillatory.

This section develops two complementary responses to handle these signed integrands.
The first is a Riesz–Kantorovich (RK) construction, described in
Section

III.1
, that propagates the input intervals directly through the signed transition kernel.
It is inexpensive, general, and rigorous, but can be overly conservative because each input point used to compute such bounds may saturate its interval independently, even for large signed fluctuations.
The second, detailed in
Section

III.2
, is an optimized construction that explicitly enforces the non-negativity of the underlying physical spectral function.
It can produce improved and potentially tight rigorous bounds, at the cost of a numerical optimization and a separate bias-correction step, described in
Section

III.3
when continuum inequalities are enforced on a grid.

Section

III.5
then discusses how the size of propagated bounds changes and can be optimized with respect to the input smearing. Finally, rigorous certification and optimization of bounds on regulated kernel transformations are included in
Section

III.5
.

III.1
Riesz–Kantorovich bounds for kernel transformations

For concreteness, this subsection restricts the discussion to kernel transformations defined with respect to energies, as for Cauchy or Gaussian input smearing kernels, with
the same sign-splitting logic applying to other kernel transformations.
Suppose that pointwise non-negative upper and lower bounds are known for a smeared spectral function,

\displaystyle 0\leq\rho_{\varepsilon-}^{\mathtt{k}}(\omega)\leq\rho^{\mathtt{k}}_{\varepsilon}(\omega)\leq\rho_{\varepsilon+}^{\mathtt{k}}(\omega)\,,

(81)

for some positive kernel
\mathtt{k}
with parameter
\varepsilon
, e.g.,
\mathtt{k}\in\{\mathtt{c},\mathtt{g}\}
as in
Eqs.

6
and
7
.
The goal is to establish bounds

\displaystyle[K\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{k}}]_{-}\leq K\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{k}}\leq[K\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{k}}]_{+}\,

(82)

on the quantity (cf.
Eq.

11
)

K\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{k}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\omega\,K(\omega)\rho^{\mathtt{k}}_{\varepsilon}(\omega)\,,

(83)

where
K(\omega)
denotes a transformation kernel.
Note that
K(\omega)
might additionally depend on other variables, such as
\omega^{\prime}
or
\varepsilon_{2}
in
Eq.

15
; such dependence plays no important role in the present discussion and will be left implicit.

The general sign-splitting argument is local in
\omega
and intuitive to understand.
The goal is to minimize cancellations conservatively in the integrand.
For the upper bound on the target, the upper bound
\rho^{\mathtt{k}}_{\varepsilon+}(\omega)
on the input is used whenever
K(\omega)
is positive.
Likewise, the lower bound
\rho^{\mathtt{k}}_{\varepsilon-}(\omega)
on the input is used whenever
K(\omega)
is negative.
Reversing these choices gives the lower bound on the target.
Equivalently, the Riesz–Kantorovich (RK) formulas are (cf. Ref.
7
and references therein):

\displaystyle[K\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{k}}]_{-}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\omega\,\min(K\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{k}})(\omega)\,,

(84)

\displaystyle[K\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{k}}]_{+}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\omega\,\max(K\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{k}})(\omega)\,,

(85)

where the integrands are defined by the following expressions:

\displaystyle\min(K\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{k}})(\omega)

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{cases}K(\omega)\rho_{\varepsilon-}^{\mathtt{k}}(\omega)\,,&K(\omega)\geq 0\\
K(\omega)\rho_{\varepsilon+}^{\mathtt{k}}(\omega)\,,&K(\omega)<0\end{cases}

(86)

\displaystyle\max(K\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{k}})(\omega)

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{cases}K(\omega)\rho_{\varepsilon+}^{\mathtt{k}}(\omega)\,,&K(\omega)\geq 0\\
K(\omega)\rho_{\varepsilon-}^{\mathtt{k}}(\omega)\,,&K(\omega)<0\,.\end{cases}

(87)

A more thorough, self-contained derivation of these bounds is detailed in Appendix
B
.
Notice that, while the bounds in
Eqs.

84
and
85
hold under general smoothness requirements on the transition kernels and the underlying smeared spectral integrals, their pointwise realizations in
Eqs.

86
and
87
depend on the positivity assumption on the bounds in
Eq.

82
, with straightforward modifications to more general cases.

An important property of kernel transformations can already be appreciated from this simple construction.
In particular, one could wonder how rigorous pointwise bound propagation compares between a single
\mathtt{k}_{i}\to\mathtt{k}_{f}
and a chain
\mathtt{k}_{i}\to\mathtt{k}_{1}\to\dots\to\mathtt{k}_{N-1}\to\mathtt{k}_{f}
of
N>1
(possibly regulated) kernel transformations.
One option consists in iteratively propagating pointwise RK bounds on all intermediate
\mathtt{k}_{n}\to\mathtt{k}_{n+1}
kernel transformations.
While rigorous, this strategy would yield unnecessarily inflated bounds on the final target observable.
This is due to neglected correlations in intermediate smeared quantities, which necessarily emerge even from pointwise (and hence diagonal) input bounds.
Given the linearity of the transformations, correlations can be maintained to the greatest extent possible
by computing RK bounds directly on the kernel transformation resulting from the composition of all intermediate ones.

III.2
Positivity-optimized bounds

The RK construction is rigorous and local in the input parameter: each value of
\alpha
may independently saturate whichever endpoint is most favorable for the signed transition kernel.
This locality is also the source of its conservatism.
If the target and input kernels are positive, positivity of the underlying spectral function guarantees a nonnegative target observable and a nontrivial RK upper bound,

\displaystyle 0\leq\int d\alpha\,K(\alpha)\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]\leq[K\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]]_{+}\,,

(91)

with
[K\rho[\phi]]_{\pm}
defined analogously to
Eqs.

86
and
87
but in terms of the pointwise bounds in
\alpha
, cf.
Eq.

80
.

However, the same assumptions do not guarantee that the RK lower bound is positive.
Since
\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]_{-}
is only assumed to be nonnegative, while
\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]_{+}
can be arbitrarily large, the weighted difference in
\min(K\rho[\phi])(\alpha)
can in principle achieve negative values.
Depending on the cancellations across different values of
\alpha
, the RK lower bound can either be positive, negative, or zero.
In other words, whether or not the RK lower bound improves on the trivial lower bound (
K\rho[\phi]\geq 0
) depends on the details of the original bounds for
\rho[\phi]
and on the size of the fluctuations of the transition kernel
K
.

A stronger construction is possible that naturally incorporates the positivity assumptions (when they hold) to tighten the above bounds.
Instead of allowing each input pointwise bound to be saturated independently, the largest and smallest possible values of
\rho[\kappa]
are identified by scanning among all nonnegative spectral densities compatible with all input bounds in
Eq.

80
.
This construction naturally preserves the physical constraint that the input observables must arise from a single
\rho(\omega)\geq 0
.
The general idea is closely related to that of Refs.
1
;
46
;
54
.

It is convenient to re-express the input bounds in terms of a central value
\overline{\rho}[\phi_{\alpha}]
and half-width
\Delta\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]
,

\displaystyle\overline{\rho}[\phi_{\alpha}]\equiv\frac{\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]_{+}+\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]_{-}}{2}\,,

(92)

\displaystyle\Delta\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]\equiv\frac{\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]_{+}-\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]_{-}}{2}\,.

(93)

The exact upper bound can then be written as the following optimization over admissible spectral densities:

\displaystyle\rho[\kappa]_{+}^{\star}=\max_{\rho}\quad\rho[\kappa]

(94)

\displaystyle\text{such that}\,\,\left|\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]-\overline{\rho}[\phi_{\alpha}]\right|\leq\Delta\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]\quad\forall\alpha\,,

\displaystyle\text{and}\,\,\rho(\omega)\geq 0\quad\forall\omega\in[0,\infty)\,.

The lower bound is obtained analogously by substituting
\rho[\kappa]_{-}^{\star}=\min_{\rho}\rho[\kappa]
in the first line, or equivalently by negating the objective.
The (primal) semi-infinite program (SIP) in
Eq.

94
defines the positivity-optimized bounds.

The dual point of view has a simple kernel interpretation.
Suppose a finite linear combination of input kernels,

\kappa_{\lambda}(\omega)=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\phi_{\alpha_{i}}(\omega)\,,

(95)

gives an upper bound for the target kernel,
\kappa_{\lambda}(\omega)\geq\kappa(\omega)
for all
\omega\geq 0
.
For any nonnegative spectral density, this kernel inequality implies
\rho[\kappa_{\lambda}]\leq\rho[\kappa]
.
The input intervals then bound
\rho[\kappa_{\lambda}]
by the central values and half-widths in
Eq.

92
.
Minimizing this upper estimate over all such
\kappa_{\lambda}
gives the dual upper bound:

\displaystyle\rho[\kappa]_{+}^{\star}=\min_{\{\lambda_{i}\}}\,\,\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\overline{\rho}[\phi_{\alpha_{i}}]+\sum_{i}|\lambda_{i}|\cdot\Delta\rho[\phi_{\alpha_{i}}]

(96)

\displaystyle\text{such that}\,\,\kappa_{\lambda}(\omega)\geq\kappa(\omega)\quad\forall\omega\in[0,\infty)\,.

The lower bound
\rho[\kappa]_{-}^{\star}
is formulated identically by using kernel lower bounds, taking the maximum over
\{\lambda_{i}\}
, and reversing the signs of the penalty and continuum inequality.
The derivation of the stated primal-dual equivalence is given in Appendix
C
.
Note that choice of a particular finite set of input kernels
\{\phi_{\alpha_{i}}\}

does not introduce any approximation provided
some
upper bound
\kappa_{\lambda}(\omega)
can be identified, whether optimal or not.
That is, the associated bound will generically be conservative.

Similar care as for RK bound propagation must be applied when composing kernel transformations through iterative SIP steps. In particular, while iterative propagation of pointwise intermediate certified intervals preserves rigor, it is generally more conservative than a direct SIP solve for the composed transformation.

The primal and dual problems in
Eqs.

94
and
96
have several important differences with respect to similar problems considered in Refs.
46
;
1
;
54
.
First,
Eqs.

94
and
96
are phrased in terms of essentially arbitrary positive input kernels
\phi_{\alpha}
, extending previous treatments focused on Euclidean correlator data.
Second, the pointwise bounding constraint on the input data in the primal problem
Eq.

94
leads to an absolute value in the second term of the dual objective function in
Eq.

96
.
As discussed above, due care must taken when composing several kernel transformations.
This is complementary to previous focuses on statistical uncertainty propagation
46
;
1
;
54
.
Such statistical variants can be included in the definition of the the primal problem, cf.
Eq.

127
, but they are not the main focus here.
Finally, as described in the following subsection, certified bounds on approximate solutions to
Eq.

96
can be obtained from a variant of
Eq.

71
, cf.
Eq.

100
, complementing rigorous tight results obtained for certain classes of target smearing kernels
1
.

III.3
Numerical certification of positivity-optimized bounds

In practice, the kernel inequalities in
Eq.

96
are enforced on a finite energy grid.
The resulting solution gives a
candidate
upper bound
\rho[\kappa]_{+}^{\mathrm{cand}}
and a candidate bounding kernel
\kappa_{\lambda^{\ast}_{+}}(\omega)=\sum_{i}(\lambda_{+}^{\ast})_{i}\phi_{\alpha_{i}}(\omega)
.
Because the grid in
\omega
is finite,
\kappa_{\lambda_{+}^{\ast}}(\omega)
can violate the continuum inequality
\kappa_{\lambda_{+}^{\ast}}(\omega)\geq\kappa(\omega)
between grid points.
Similarly, the candidate lower bound
\rho[\kappa]_{-}^{\mathrm{cand}}
comes from a candidate minorant
\kappa_{\lambda^{\ast}_{-}}(\omega)
that may violate
\kappa_{\lambda_{-}^{\ast}}(\omega)\leq\kappa(\omega)
between grid points.

The positivity of the spectral density allows these residual continuum violations to be absorbed a posteriori by following a similar strategy as that used for deriving
Eq.

71
.
Define the normalized maximal violations

\displaystyle\delta_{\pm}(\alpha)\equiv\max\left[0,\,\max_{\omega}\left(\pm\frac{\kappa(\omega)-\kappa_{\lambda_{\pm}^{\ast}}(\omega)}{\phi_{\alpha}(\omega)}\right)\right]

(97)

at a chosen fixed value of
\alpha
.
The upper
(+)
and lower
(-)
values may differ due to the signed argument of the maximum; taking both equal to the larger of
\delta_{+}(\alpha)
and
\delta_{-}(\alpha)
recovers the more conservative, symmetric absolute-value structure of
Eq.

71
.
In other words, the normalized violations in
Eq.

97
allow corrected kernel bounds to be computed, for all
\omega
, by

\displaystyle\kappa_{\lambda_{-}^{\ast}}(\omega)-\delta_{-}(\alpha)\phi_{\alpha}(\omega)\leq\kappa(\omega)

(98)

\displaystyle\kappa(\omega)\leq\kappa_{\lambda_{+}^{\ast}}(\omega)+\delta_{+}(\alpha)\phi_{\alpha}(\omega)\,.

(99)

Bounds on
\rho[\kappa]
immediately follow by correcting the candidate bounds according to

\displaystyle\rho[\kappa]_{\pm}^{\mathrm{rig}}=\rho[\kappa]_{\pm}^{\mathrm{cand}}\pm\delta_{\pm}(\alpha)\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]_{+}\,.

(100)

Note that, since only bounds on
\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]
are presumed to be known, the second term must include
\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]_{+}
to construct rigorous bounds on both
\rho[\kappa]^{\rm rig}_{\pm}
.
An analogous situation occurs for the right-hand side of
Eq.

71
.

As in
Eq.

71
, the choice of
\alpha
can be optimized to reduce the size of the overall bounds.
If the kernel inequality in
Eq.

96
is verified for all
\omega
, then
\delta_{\pm}(\alpha)=0
and the candidate bounds are already rigorous.
This separates the numerical search for good candidate kernels from the mathematical certification of the final interval.
When the certification in
Eq.

97
is handled numerically, it is useful to plot the bounding kernels to ensure reliability (cf.
Fig.

6b
below.)
The same certification idea may also be useful for related dual approaches such as those of Refs.
1
;
46
;
54
.

III.4
Optimal input smearing and RK error scaling

The preceding constructions leave open a design question: which input smearing should be used to obtain the tightest target interval?
For exact transformations, the RK bounds can be computed at each fixed input smearing width
\varepsilon
, but the resulting interval reflects a competition between the quality of the input bounds and the stability of the transition kernel.
The bounds from
Eqs.

86
and
87
immediately imply that the total width of the RK interval is

\displaystyle[K\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{k}}]_{+}-[K\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{k}}]_{-}

\displaystyle=\int d\omega\,|K(\omega)|\left(\rho_{\varepsilon+}^{\mathtt{k}}(\omega)-\rho_{\varepsilon-}^{\mathtt{k}}(\omega)\right)

\displaystyle=\lVert K\cdot(\rho_{\varepsilon+}^{\mathtt{k}}-\rho_{\varepsilon-}^{\mathtt{k}})\rVert_{1}

(101)

\displaystyle\leq\lVert K\rVert_{1}\cdot\lVert\rho_{\varepsilon+}^{\mathtt{k}}-\rho_{\varepsilon-}^{\mathtt{k}}\rVert_{\infty}\,,

(102)

where the third line follows from Hölder’s inequality, and may be replaced by other versions of the same inequality.
For instance, if both the kernel and the difference of the bounds are square integrable,
L^{2}
-norms may be used in
Eq.

102
.

Eq.

101
gives a simple way to understand the limiting behavior of the RK width as
\varepsilon
is varied.
For illustration, consider a Cauchy-to-Gaussian kernel transformation at fixed Gaussian width
\sigma
.
For large
\varepsilon
, the input bounds
\rho_{\varepsilon\pm}^{\mathtt{c}}(\omega)
may become increasingly tight, since in this limit
\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{c}}(\omega)
is primarily sensitive to the overall normalization of the underlying distribution
\rho(\omega)
.
At the same time, the transition kernel becomes increasingly oscillatory, and its
L^{1}
norm grows exponentially with
\varepsilon/\sigma
.
The RK width therefore grows at large
\varepsilon
.
For small
\varepsilon
, the transition kernel
K
reduces to a Gaussian (cf.
Fig.

1
), so that
\lVert K\rVert_{1}\to 1
.
The original bounds
\rho_{\varepsilon\pm}^{\mathtt{c}}(\omega)
are expected to grow in this regime, since the limit
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{c}}(\omega)=\rho(\omega)
approaches the unsmeared distribution.
Together, these two regimes indicate the presence of a minimizing input width for a symmetric interval,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\star}=\argmin_{\varepsilon}\left([K\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{c}}]_{+}-[K\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\mathtt{c}}]_{-}\right)\,.

(103)

Intuitively,
\varepsilon^{\star}
is the input smearing that best matches the resolution of a given target kernel under the available input bounds.
The same input-smearing question can also be posed for the positivity-optimized bounds, although the resulting interval widths need not follow the symmetric RK estimate in
Eq.

101
.

Section

IV
discusses this optimization for a Cauchy-to-Gaussian kernel transformation (see
Fig.

4
).
The numerical value of
\varepsilon^{\star}
will generally depend on both the target smearing width
\sigma
and the energy
\omega^{\prime}
at which it is evaluated.

III.5
Optimal bounds for regulated kernel transformations

Regulated transformations use the same bound-propagation tools as exact ones, but require one additional step.
The target kernel
\kappa
is replaced by an approximant
\kappa_{T}
, where
T
denotes the regulator.
One then propagates the input bounds to the regulated observable
\rho[\kappa_{T}]
, certifies the mismatch between
\kappa
and
\kappa_{T}
, and enlarges the propagated interval by that certificate.

Let

\rho[\kappa_{T}]_{-}\leq\rho[\kappa_{T}]\leq\rho[\kappa_{T}]_{+}

(104)

denote the propagated RK or positivity-optimized bounds for the regulated kernel, at fixed choice of regulator.
Define the propagated center and half-width by

\displaystyle\overline{\rho[\kappa_{T}]}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\rho[\kappa_{T}]_{+}+\rho[\kappa_{T}]_{-}}{2}\,,

(105)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\rm prop}(T)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\rho[\kappa_{T}]_{+}-\rho[\kappa_{T}]_{-}}{2}\,.

(106)

For RK bounds obtained from a transition kernel
K_{T}
, this half-width is equivalent to propagation of the input half-width defined in
Eq.

93
through the absolute-value kernel, i.e.,

\Delta_{\rm prop}(T)=\int d\alpha\,|K_{T}(\alpha)|\,\Delta\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]\,.

(107)

As the regulator is removed, the large oscillations in
K_{T}
tend to cancel in the central value but not in
\Delta_{\rm prop}(T)
.

The mismatch between
\kappa
and
\kappa_{T}
is quantified using the rigorous bound on the kernel-level estimate in
Eq.

71
.
For any positive input kernel
\phi_{\alpha}
, define

\Delta_{\rm sys}(T;\alpha)\equiv\left\|\frac{|\kappa(\omega)-\kappa_{T}(\omega)|}{|\phi_{\alpha}(\omega)|}\right\|_{\infty}\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]_{+}\,.

(108)

The systematic error used in the final bound can be optimized according to

\Delta_{\rm sys}(T)\equiv\min_{\alpha}\Delta_{\rm sys}(T;\alpha)\,,

(109)

or any computable upper bound from varying
\alpha
.
The total certified half-width is then

\Delta_{\rm tot}(T)\equiv\Delta_{\rm prop}(T)+\Delta_{\rm sys}(T)\,,

(110)

which gives the rigorous interval

\overline{\rho[\kappa_{T}]}-\Delta_{\rm tot}(T)\leq\rho[\kappa]\leq\overline{\rho[\kappa_{T}]}+\Delta_{\rm tot}(T)\,.

(111)

The useful regulator is therefore chosen by minimizing this certified total width,

T^{\star}\in\argmin_{T}\Delta_{\rm tot}(T)\,.

(112)

An alternative strategy, closer to the positivity-optimized construction of
Eq.

100
, consists in tuning the regulator to separately identify the sharpest upper and lower envelopes,

\displaystyle\max_{T}

\displaystyle\left\{\rho[\kappa_{T}]_{-}-\Delta_{\rm sys}(T)\right\}

(113)

\displaystyle\leq\rho[\kappa]\leq\min_{T}\left\{\rho[\kappa_{T}]_{+}+\Delta_{\rm sys}(T)\right\}\,.

Since the optimal upper and lower certificates may occur at different regulator values, this one-sided optimization is not equivalent to minimizing the total width in
Eq.

112
.
We show numerical results using these two optimization methods in
Section

IV
for a sharpening Cauchy-to-Cauchy regulated kernel transformation (see
Fig.

6
).

Although it is not the focus of this work, the reformulation of RK bound propagation extends naturally to linear propagation of statistical errors.
In particular, assuming the input data have an associated covariance matrix
\Sigma_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}
, the propagated variance on
\overline{\rho[\kappa_{T}]}
is

\Delta_{\text{stat}}^{2}(T)=\int d\alpha\,d\alpha^{\prime}\,K_{T}(\alpha)\Sigma_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}K_{T}(\alpha^{\prime})\,.

(114)

In such cases, one can envision an optimization of the regulator based on a minimization of the total error, including the statistical uncertainty.

The Cauchy-to-Cauchy sharpening example in
Section

IV
uses a Tikhonov realization of this general construction.
It is useful to record here the practical details of the Tikhonov regularization as an explicit instance of regulated kernel transformations with a tunable parameter.
Let
\{\omega_{j}\}
be a dense energy grid and let
\{\alpha_{i}\}
be the grid of input kernel centers.
For an input Cauchy width
\varepsilon_{1}
, define the input-kernel matrix and target kernel vector respectively as

\displaystyle[K_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\mathtt{c}}]_{ji}

\displaystyle\equiv\delta_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\mathtt{c}}(\alpha_{i}-\omega_{j})\,,

(115)

\displaystyle[K_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\mathtt{c}}(\omega^{\prime})]_{j}

\displaystyle\equiv\delta_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\mathtt{c}}(\omega^{\prime}-\omega_{j})\,.

(116)

The coefficient vector
g_{\xi}(\omega^{\prime})
is defined as the solution to the Tikhonov problem

g_{\xi}(\omega^{\prime})=\argmin_{g}\left\{\lVert K_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\mathtt{c}}g-K_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\mathtt{c}}(\omega^{\prime})\rVert_{2}^{2}+\xi\lVert g\rVert_{2}^{2}\right\},

(117)

with closed-form solution

g_{\xi}(\omega^{\prime})=\left[(K_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\mathtt{c}})^{T}K_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\mathtt{c}}+\xi\mathbb{I}\right]^{-1}(K_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\mathtt{c}})^{T}K_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\mathtt{c}}(\omega^{\prime})\,.

(118)

This defines the regulated target vector

\overline{K}_{\varepsilon_{2},\xi}^{\mathtt{c}}(\omega^{\prime})=K_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\mathtt{c}}g_{\xi}(\omega^{\prime})\,,

(119)

or equivalently the regulated kernel
\kappa_{\xi}
obtained as a linear combination of input kernels centered at
\alpha_{i}
.
The corresponding regulated observable is the same linear combination of the input smeared data, and the final bound is obtained from
Eq.

110
.
Although regularization schemes besides Tikhonov regularization are often used in practice, the details of the regularization are secondary for the present discussion, as long as the associated bias is properly quantified. In this case, a practical realization of a regularized scheme is presented in detail to serve as a concrete example for the construction of a regulated kernel approximation through a deterministic linear operator controlled by the stabilizing parameter
\xi
. The latter can then be directly optimized as in
Eq.

112
, with a detailed discussion postponed to
Section

IV.2
.

IV
Numerical Realization

This section provides numerical demonstrations of the framework developed above.
The first three examples use the toy positive spectral function and artificial input bounds of

3

88
and
89

V
Conclusions

This work has developed a framework for constructing maps between smeared spectral functions defined with different smearing kernels.
Although the methods can be applied directly to Euclidean-time inputs, the main focus has been on the kernel-transformation problem: given continuous energy-smeared input observables
\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]
, and possibly rigorous pointwise bounds on them, determine a target smeared observable
\rho[\kappa]
without returning to the original inverse problem.
Exact transformations are controlled by the analytic and Fourier structure of the input and target kernels.
When an exact transformation is not available, the target kernel can be replaced by a regulated approximant, and the resulting bias from kernel mismatch can be sharply quantified from the available input data.
Pointwise rigorous bounds then propagate through the transformation either via a general sign-splitting argument or, where applicable, through optimized bounds that leverage positivity of the underlying spectral function.
Extensions to statistical error propagation can be naturally incorporated as well.

This framework supports the analysis strategy emphasized in the introduction.
Rather than laboriously returning to the original inverse problem in terms of a finite Euclidean-time basis for every desired inclusive observables, one instead has the freedom to target the most convenient smeared spectral information:

\rho[\phi_{t}=e^{-\omega t}]\to\rho[\phi_{\alpha}]
.
Exact or certified regulated kernel transformations can then transport that information to other smeared or weighted observables.
The resulting transformation path can be chosen to minimize the total certified uncertainty, including propagated input uncertainty, loss of correlations arising from intermediate transformations, and (where relevant) any systematic contribution from kernel mismatch.

The first lesson is that exact transformations are available when the input family and target kernel have compatible analytic structure.
In particular, the table is organized into three blocks, corresponding to different classes of kernel transformations.
The first block describes known examples of inverse problems from Euclidean-time correlators.
The second block includes the widening semigroups for Gaussian and Cauchy kernels and, more importantly for the present work, the Cauchy-to-Gaussian map used as the main exact example for rigorous bound propagation.
The third block contains the case of the Lévy mixture of different smearings at fixed energy, which allows a Gaussian-to-Cauchy transformation.

The bound-propagation methods are complementary.
RK bounds are direct, inexpensive, and apply to any signed transition kernel once pointwise input bounds have been specified.
Positivity-optimized bounds enforce the physical requirement of the positivity of the underlying spectral density,
and can therefore yield tighter intervals at the price of a numerical optimization and a certification step.
The numerical examples illustrate both methods in controlled settings with a toy model mimicking realistic lattice-data analysis.

Regulated transformations extend the toolkit to cases where exact maps fail or where a finite-dimensional approximation is used in practice.
A key novel observation in the present work is that bias can be bounded from the knowledge of input data only, and the regulator can be tuned to produce optimized, certified bounds.
The Cauchy-to-Cauchy sharpening example illustrates this procedure and shows explicitly how the cost of sharpening enters through enlarged, rigorously certified intervals.

Moreover, the certification of semi-infinite solutions offers a complementary strategy to those presented in Refs.

Another interesting application may lie in propagating the finite-volume systematic uncertainties, recently computed for Cauchy-smeared spectral functions in Ref.

Also related to systematic uncertainties, bounds arising from kernel transformations offer a broad range of self-consistency conditions for different smeared spectral quantities, which can be used to verify the internal consistency of a calculation.
To retain the benefit of kernel transformations without unnecessarily inflating the associated errors, correlation on bounds on intermediate smeared spectral observables must be properly propagated.
The natural next step is to apply these methods in practical LQCD calculations.

In particular, an operator-theoretic argument is presented to translate bounds on smeared spectral quantities between different kernels.

Let

Consider the linear functional

\displaystyle Tf=\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,K(x)f(x)\equiv F

Suppose that pointwise non-negative bounds for

\displaystyle 0\leq f_{-}(x)\leq f(x)\leq f_{+}(x)\,.

(139)

The theory of order-bounded linear operators provides an abstract way to translate bounds on

7

The discussion begins with the notion of a positive operator.
An operator

To see this, decompose the kernel

\displaystyle K=K^{+}-K^{-}

\displaystyle K^{\pm}

\displaystyle:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle K^{\pm}(x)

Pointwise bounds on the integrand

\displaystyle\inf_{f_{-}\leq f\leq f_{+}}K(x)f(x)

\displaystyle=(K^{+}f_{-}-K^{-}f_{+})(x)

\displaystyle\equiv\inf(Kf)(x)\,,

Appendix C
Derivation of the Semi-Infinite Programming Dual

This appendix gives the primal-dual derivation supporting the positivity-optimized bounds in
Section

III.2
.
The unconstrained dual problem,
Eq.

96
, is derived from the semi-infinite primal problem,
Eq.

94
.
The focus will be on the upper bound; the lower bound follows from an identical procedure by negating the objective function.

The primal problem seeks to maximize the functional
\rho[\kappa]=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\kappa(\omega)\rho(\omega)
subject to the spectral positivity constraint
\rho(\omega)\geq 0
and a discrete set of
L_{\infty}
box constraints on the input data

\overline{\rho}_{i}-\Delta\rho_{i}\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\phi_{i}(\omega)\rho(\omega)\leq\overline{\rho}_{i}+\Delta\rho_{i}\,,

(153)

where
i
indexes the input kernels and we adopt the shorthand notation
\overline{\rho}_{i}\equiv\overline{\rho}[\phi_{\alpha_{i}}]
and
\Delta\rho_{i}\equiv\Delta\rho[\phi_{\alpha_{i}}]
is adopted.
The solution of the constrained problem is obtained by the method of Lagrange multipliers.

To construct the Lagrangian, two sets of non-negative dual variables are introduced:
\nu(\omega)\geq 0
for all
\omega
, associated with the spectral positivity constraint, and
\lambda_{i}^{+}\geq 0
and
\lambda_{i}^{-}\geq 0
, associated with the upper and lower inequality constraints respectively.
The Lagrangian is then given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle(\rho,\nu,\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\kappa(\omega)\rho(\omega)

(154)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\nu(\omega)\rho(\omega)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{+}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\phi_{i}(\omega)\rho(\omega)-\overline{\rho}_{i}-\Delta\rho_{i}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{-}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\phi_{i}(\omega)\rho(\omega)-\overline{\rho}_{i}+\Delta\rho_{i}\right)\,.

Grouping the terms involving the spectral density
\rho(\omega)
, the Lagrangian can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\rho,\nu,\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})=\sum_{i}(\lambda_{i}^{+}-\lambda_{i}^{-})\overline{\rho}_{i}+\sum_{i}(\lambda_{i}^{+}+\lambda_{i}^{-})\Delta\rho_{i}

(155)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\rho(\omega)\left[\kappa(\omega)+\nu(\omega)-\sum_{i}(\lambda_{i}^{+}-\lambda_{i}^{-})\phi_{i}(\omega)\right]\,.

Invoking the minimax theorem in the usual way, the optimal solution is obtained by noting that

\sup_{\rho}\,\,\inf_{\nu(\omega),\lambda^{\pm}_{i}\geq 0}\mathcal{L}\leq\inf_{\nu(\omega),\lambda^{\pm}_{i}\geq 0}\,\,\sup_{\rho}\mathcal{L}\,,

(156)

which allows the optimization to be performed first over the space of all permissible spectral densities (without any additional constraint, to be enforced by the Lagrange multipliers).

The supremum over the integral in
Eq.

155
diverges unless the bracketed term evaluates to zero everywhere.
Therefore, a finite dual minimum only exists if

\nu(\omega)=\kappa_{\lambda}(\omega)-\kappa(\omega)\,,\quad\kappa_{\lambda}(\omega)=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\phi_{i}(\omega)\,,

(157)

where the unrestricted dual variable
\lambda_{i}\equiv\lambda_{i}^{+}-\lambda_{i}^{-}\in\mathbb{R}
was introduced.
For a fixed value of
\lambda_{i}
, the term proportional to
\Delta\rho_{i}
on the right-hand side
Eq.

155
acts as a penalty.
A standard calculation shows that this penalty is minimized by choosing the dual variables such that they do not simultaneously take nonzero values.
Specifically, setting
\lambda_{i}^{+}=\max(\lambda_{i},0)
and
\lambda_{i}^{-}=\max(-\lambda_{i},0)
immediately implies that
\lambda_{i}^{+}+\lambda_{i}^{-}=|\lambda_{i}|
.
The minimization of the dual function then reduces exactly to the optimization problem presented in
Eq.

96
:

\min_{\{\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\}}\,\,\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\overline{\rho}_{i}+\sum_{i}|\lambda_{i}|\Delta\rho_{i}

(158)

subject to the semi-infinite constraint

\kappa_{\lambda}(\omega)=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\phi_{i}(\omega)\geq\kappa(\omega)\quad\forall\,\omega\in[0,\infty)\,.

(159)

The
L_{1}
absolute-value penalty emerges naturally as a direct analytic consequence of the
L_{\infty}
box constraints on the input data.
