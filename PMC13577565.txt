Targeting lipid metabolic vulnerabilities with small-molecule inhibitors in acute myeloid leukemia: Emerging insights and therapeutic opportunities
Authors: Jui-Hung Yen, Trung Q. Phan, Je-Wen Liou, Pei-Yi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMetabolic reprogramming has recently been recognized as a hallmark of cancer. In acute myeloid leukemia (AML), clinically relevant metabolism-targeted therapies have primarily focused on inhibiting mitochondrial energy production; however, their clinical progress has been limited by substantial and nonspecific toxicity. Emerging evidence indicates that reprogramming of lipid metabolism represents a defining feature of leukemic transformation. Lipids not only serve as fundamental structural components of cellular membranes but also function as key signaling molecules and energy sources. In AML cells, lipid uptake, storage, and de novo synthesis are markedly increased, thereby supporting rapid proliferation, survival, and leukemic progression. Consequently, dysregulated lipid metabolism has attracted growing attention as a promising therapeutic vulnerability in AML. This review summarizes the conceptual framework underlying AML cell dependence on cholesterol, fatty acids (FAs), sphingolipids, and broader lipid metabolic pathways. We highlight recent advances in understanding aberrant lipid metabolic programs in AML, including alterations in cholesterol biosynthesis, FA uptake and lipogenesis, FA oxidation, and sphingolipid metabolism. Particular emphasis is placed on the regulatory mechanisms that maintain lipid metabolic homeostasis and how their disruption contributes to leukemogenesis and therapy resistance. Furthermore, we discuss emerging therapeutic strategies aimed at targeting lipid metabolic pathways, with a focus on small-molecule inhibitors that selectively interfere with lipid metabolism-associated enzymes and signaling networks. By delineating key molecular targets and their pharmacological inhibitors, this review highlights the potential of lipid metabolism-based interventions as innovative and effective treatment strategies for AML.

INTRODUCTION

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is the most common adult leukemia and a clonal hematopoietic malignancy characterized by the accumulation of transformed stem and progenitor cells with uncontrolled proliferation and impaired differentiation, leading to infiltration of hematopoietic and extramedullary tissues [1]. AML is a heterogeneous disease with an incidence that increases with age and an overall poor prognosis [2]. Younger patients generally achieve better clinical outcomes, whereas older adults experience inferior survival due to limited tolerance to intensive chemotherapy and a higher prevalence of therapy-resistant genetic alterations [3]. Despite significant advances in the treatment of leukemia over the past decade, AML remains a highly challenging malignancy with an overall poor prognosis. Therefore, the development of new targeted therapies remains an urgent need. The rapid expansion of immature myeloid blasts not only disrupts normal hematopoiesis but also requires substantial energy and biosynthetic resources, rendering AML cells highly dependent on rewired metabolic pathways for survival and proliferation [4]. Exploiting metabolic vulnerabilities through targeted therapeutic interventions may provide novel and effective strategies against AML.

Nutrient addiction, driven by metabolic reprogramming, is a hallmark of cancer that enables malignant cells to survive and proliferate in nutrient-poor microenvironments. This adaptation involves coordinated alterations in glucose, glutamine, and lipid metabolism, together with rewiring of nutrient-sensing signaling pathways, to meet increased bioenergetic, biosynthetic, and redox demands [5]. A central feature of this reprogramming is the Warburg effect, characterized by elevated aerobic glycolysis that supports rapid ATP generation and biomass production at the expense of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). In addition to serving metabolic functions, key metabolites act as signaling molecules that promote tumor growth and progression [6]. Accordingly, targeting cancer-associated metabolic dependencies has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy across multiple malignancies, including AML, with validation in both preclinical and clinical studies [7].

This review summarizes recent advances in the regulation of lipid metabolism in AML, including cholesterol homeostasis, fatty acid (FA) uptake and de novo synthesis, FA oxidation (FAO), and sphingolipid metabolism. We also discuss emerging small-molecule therapeutic strategies targeting lipid metabolic vulnerabilities in AML.

CLASSIFICATION AND SUBTYPES OF ACUTE MYELOID LEUKEMIA

Recent advances in molecular profiling of AML have shifted its classification from a morphology-based system to incorporate genomic, cytogenetic, and clinical data, thereby strengthening the therapeutic vulnerabilities in AML. Based on the 2022 AML classification from the World Health Organization (WHO), AML is primarily defined by specific genetic abnormalities. Major categories include AML with recurrent genetic alterations, such as t(8;21); RUNX1–RUNX1T1, inv (16); CBFB–MYH11, and PML-RARA; AML with myelodysplasia-related changes, and therapy-related myeloid neoplasms. Cases that do not meet these criteria are classified as AML, not otherwise specified, and may be further subdivided by morphology [8].

With advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies, AML classification has also been incorporated with somatic mutations that are prognostic and therapeutically significant. These include AML with FLT3 mutation (particularly FLT3-ITD), NPM1 mutation, and biallelic CEBPA mutation. In addition, mutations in epigenetic regulators such as DNMT3A, isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1/2), and TET2 are common in AML. These subtypes of AML show distinct biological features for targeted therapies [9]. Meanwhile, the European LeukemiaNet (ELN) classification stratifies patients into favorable, intermediate, and adverse risk groups by integrating cytogenetic and molecular data to guide diagnosis, response assessment, and treatment decisions in AML. For example, NPM1-mutated AML without FLT3-ITD is associated with favorable outcomes; however, AML with complex karyotypes, TP53 mutations, and certain myelodysplasia-related abnormalities is associated with a poor prognosis [10], indicating that current classification systems improve diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic decisions in AML.

LIPID METABOLIC REPROGRAMMING AND CANCER

Lipid metabolic reprogramming is a key feature of cancer, including AML. Enhanced lipid uptake, synthesis, and utilization supply cancer cells with energy, membrane components, and signaling lipids that support malignant progression [11]. Lipids have emerged as more than just energy substrates. They are now recognized as critical contributors to tumor pathogenesis. Lipids function as structural components of cellular membranes and as bioactive signaling molecules [12]. Cancer cells coordinate anabolic lipid metabolism by upregulating lipogenic enzymes and disrupting cholesterol homeostasis, processes regulated mainly by sterol regulatory element-binding proteins (SREBPs). These alterations support the increased metabolic demands of rapid cell proliferation [13]. This metabolic rewiring leads to lipid droplet accumulation. Lipid droplets act as adaptive organelles that buffer lipotoxic stress and help tumor cells survive under unfavorable conditions [14]. Specialized membrane microdomains called lipid rafts play a key role in organizing and amplifying oncogenic signaling complexes. These complexes drive tumor growth, progression, and invasion [15]. During metastasis, cancer cells rely even more on lipid metabolism. Enhanced FA uptake and oxidation enable them to resist anoikis, modulate the immune microenvironment, and colonize distant tissues [16]. As a result, therapeutic targeting of lipid metabolic networks has become a promising strategy for cancer treatment.

Profound metabolic reprogramming is a key characteristic of AML progression, enabling malignant cells to meet increased biosynthetic demands and to support survival under metabolic stress caused by nutrient deprivation [17]. Unlike many highly proliferative solid tumors that predominantly rely on aerobic glycolysis, AML cells, particularly leukemia stem cells (LSCs), display a distinct metabolic phenotype characterized by a strong dependence on mitochondrial oxidative OXPHOS for energy production [18]. Consequently, most clinically relevant metabolism-targeted therapeutic strategies in AML have focused on inhibiting mitochondrial energy production [19]. However, the clinical translation of these approaches has been hampered by substantial and nonspecific toxicity. Some OXPHOS inhibitors, such as the complex I inhibitor IACS-010759, have been associated with peripheral neuropathy in Phase I clinical trials involving patients with relapsed or refractory AML. These findings suggest that therapies targeting cellular energy production may cause a significant risk of toxicity and remain challenging, highlighting the need for further optimization [20].

DYSREGULATION OF LIPID METABOLIC PATHWAYS IN ACUTE MYELOID LEUKEMIA

Increasing evidence indicates that AML cells rely heavily on lipid catabolism and biosynthesis to maintain energy homeostasis, support membrane biogenesis, and sustain oncogenic signaling. Aberrant lipid metabolism in AML involves coordinated alterations in lipid synthesis, degradation, and storage, which are essential for leukemogenesis and the maintenance of leukemic stem cells [21]. In addition, altered expression of lipid metabolism-related genes correlates with clinical outcomes and provides prognostic biomarkers in AML [22].

Lipid metabolic reprogramming in acute myeloid leukemia

Lipid metabolic reprogramming is tightly interconnected with key oncogenic drivers in AML. For example, AML cells harboring IDH1 mutations have been shown to undergo extensive lipid metabolic reprogramming, underscoring the role of oncogenic mutations in shaping lipid dependency [23]. In FLT3-mutated AML, FLT3 kinase signaling promotes FA biosynthesis and regulates polyunsaturated FA (PUFA) metabolism via the transcription factor CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein α (C/EBPα). Pharmacologic or genetic inhibition of FLT3 or C/EBPα downregulates stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD), leading to PUFA accumulation and increased susceptibility to lipid peroxidation [24]. In addition, mannose-6-phosphate isomerase (MPI) has been identified as a metabolic regulator associated with poor prognosis in FLT3-mutated AML. Loss or inhibition of MPI enhances lipid uptake, partly by upregulating the FA transporter CD36, while concurrently suppressing FAO, leading to intracellular FA accumulation. This metabolic imbalance promotes lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis, thereby sensitizing AML cells to chemotherapy and FLT3 inhibitors [25]. These findings highlight how oncogenic signaling pathways converge on lipid metabolic reprogramming to drive AML progression and therapeutic resistance.

Dysregulation of lipid metabolism in LSCs

Growing evidence indicates that LSCs are the primary drivers of leukemia progression, therapeutic resistance, and relapse, making them a major challenge in the treatment of AML. LSCs are responsible for cancer initiation and relapse due to their quiescent nature and intrinsic resistance to chemotherapy, characteristics that are sustained by mitochondrial OXPHOS. Lipid metabolism is frequently dysregulated in LSCs compared with normal hematopoietic cells. Furthermore, lipid metabolism plays a fundamental role in regulating LSC maintenance and survival, highlighting lipid metabolic pathways as promising therapeutic targets in AML [1726].

The FA dependency of LSCs is strongly influenced by the bone marrow (BM) microenvironment, in which AML blasts induce lipolysis in BM adipocytes by phosphorylating hormone-sensitive lipase, thereby releasing free FAs. LSCs actively uptake these free FAs via the CD36, and elevated CD36 expression is associated with poor prognosis [27]. FAO is essential for maintaining LSCs and plays a key role in mediating chemoresistance. Accordingly, targeting FAO by inhibiting carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (CPT1) effectively eliminates ex vivo quiescent, LSC-like populations. Importantly, LSC metabolism is highly dynamic. Relapsed/refractory LSCs exhibit marked metabolic plasticity, shifting toward FAO to sustain OXPHOS under nutrient-restricted conditions [28].

Dysregulation in cholesterol biosynthesis and homeostasis pathways

Intracellular cholesterol homeostasis is maintained through a dynamic balance among cholesterol biosynthesis, uptake, efflux, and esterification [13]. Cholesterol is a fundamental component of membrane biogenesis and is essential for the proliferation and differentiation of hematopoietic cells. Dysregulation of cholesterol homeostasis is a key driver of leukemogenesis in AML. Leukemic cells exhibit increased dependence on cholesterol to sustain proliferation and develop chemoresistance [29].

Cholesterol biosynthesis proceeds through approximately 30 sequential enzymatic reactions originating from acetyl–coenzyme A (acetyl-CoA). Among these, 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (HMGCR) and squalene synthase function as major rate-limiting enzymes. Cholesterol biosynthesis is a highly energy-intensive process that requires substantial inputs of acetyl-CoA, ATP, oxygen, and reducing cofactors such as NADPH and NADH. Consequently, this pathway is subject to stringent, multilayered regulatory control [30].

Cholesterol metabolism and homeostatic regulation. Cholesterol is synthesized via the mevalonate pathway in the endoplasmic reticulum, with 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (HMGCR) as the rate-limiting enzyme. This multistep process begins with acetyl-CoA, proceeds through the formation of mevalonate and squalene, and ultimately generates cholesterol. In addition to de novo synthesis, cells acquire cholesterol via LDLR-mediated endocytosis of LDL particles, followed by trafficking through endosomal and lysosomal compartments. Cholesterol efflux is primarily mediated by ATP-binding cassette transporters – ABCA1 and ABCG1. The transcription factor SREBP2 upregulates HMGCR and LDLR expression, whereas LXR-α promotes ABCA1 and ABCG1 expression to maintain cholesterol homeostasis. Modulation of cholesterol homeostasis, either by inhibiting cholesterol biosynthesis or by activating liver X receptors (LXRs) to enhance reverse cholesterol transport, impairs leukemic cell viability and disrupts membrane integrity in both in vitro and in vivo models, highlighting cholesterol metabolism as a therapeutically exploitable vulnerability in AML [29]. At the molecular level, cholesterol homeostasis is regulated by two antagonistic transcriptional programs mediated by SREBP2 and LXRα. SREBP2 functions as the master regulator of the mevalonate pathway by transcriptionally activating genes required for cholesterol and isoprenoid biosynthesis, including HMGCR. SREBP2 drives intracellular cholesterol homeostasis by upregulating low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) to support the metabolic demands of leukemic cells [3132]. LXRα attenuates SREBP2-mediated anabolic programs by activating genes involved in reverse cholesterol transport and efflux, thereby preserving intracellular cholesterol homeostasis [33]. In AML, disruption of this balance leads to cholesterol accumulation, promoting leukemic stem cell maintenance, therapeutic resistance, and immune evasion. In addition, cholesterol biosynthesis generates isoprenoid intermediates, including farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP) and geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate (GGPP), which are required for the activation of oncoproteins such as Ras and Rho GTPases [34]. Together, these mechanisms identify cholesterol homeostasis as a central metabolic vulnerability and therapeutic target in AML.

Dysregulation of fatty acid uptake, lipogenesis, and desaturation

Figure 2 illustrates FA uptake, lipogenesis, desaturation, and FAO, highlighting pathways that are potential therapeutic targets in AML. Lipogenesis primarily converts excess dietary carbohydrates into lipid droplets for energy storage, offering an energetic advantage since lipid oxidation generates roughly twice the energy of carbohydrates. This process is catalyzed by FA synthase (FASN), a homodimeric enzyme composed of seven functional domains [35]. Cancer cells upregulate FA metabolism to support energy production and lipid accumulation. Lipid biosynthesis substrates are derived from either extracellular FAs or de novo synthesis. FAs transport across the plasma membrane is tightly controlled by specialized proteins, including FA-binding proteins (FABPs), FA transport proteins (FATPs), and FA translocase (FAT/CD36) [36]. In malignant cells, this balance shifts toward elevated lipogenesis to sustain rapid proliferation and survival [37].

Fatty acid metabolism: uptake, de novo synthesis, desaturation, and β-oxidation. Cells acquire fatty acids (FAs) through extracellular uptake and de novo synthesis. In the cytosol, de novo FA synthesis from citrate is catalyzed by ACLY, ACC1, and fatty acid synthase, producing 16-carbon palmitate. Monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) are generated by desaturation of saturated FAs via stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1. FA uptake occurs through FA transport proteins or CD36/FA-binding protein-mediated pathways. Once inside the cell, FAs are activated to fatty acyl-CoA and transported into the mitochondrial matrix via carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (CPT1) and CPT2. In the matrix, fatty acyl-CoA undergoes β-oxidation to generate acetyl-CoA, which enters the TCA cycle and drives OXPHOS for ATP production. Pyruvate from glycolysis is converted to acetyl-CoA, which condenses with oxaloacetate to form citrate in the TCA cycle. Cytosolic citrate is cleaved by ATP citrate lyase (ACLY) to regenerate acetyl-CoA, which ACC carboxylates to malonyl-CoA. FASN then produces palmitate (C16:0) [38]. Palmitate can be converted to palmitoyl-CoA for β-oxidation, desaturation by SCD to form monounsaturated FAs (MUFAs), or elongation by elongation of very long-chain FA enzymes to generate long-chain FAs such as stearate (C18:0). Repeated desaturation and elongation reactions produce a diverse FA pool with varying chain lengths and saturation [39].

Dysregulated lipogenesis in cancer is characterized by coordinated upregulation of key proteins, including ACLY, ACC, FASN, and SCD1, which are established therapeutic targets in cancer treatment [36]. ACLY is a cytosolic homotetramer that converts citrate and CoA into acetyl-CoA and oxaloacetate, serving as a key link between glycolysis and lipid metabolism [40]. Frequently overexpressed in many cancers, ACLY has been identified as a significant prognostic factor in AML, with lower expression levels associated with improved overall survival [41]. Silencing or knockdown of ACLY induces growth arrest in AML cells, highlighting its essential role in leukemic proliferation [42].

The first rate-limiting step of lipogenesis is catalyzed by ACC1, which undergoes degradation, triggering metabolic reprogramming that supports the energy demands of leukemic progression. In contrast, stabilizing ACC1 suppresses leukemia-initiating cell self-renewal and promotes myeloid differentiation, protecting AML mice from leukemic death [43]. ACC1 has been identified as a synthetic lethal target in IDH1-mutant cancers. Disruption of ACC1 activity selectively impairs mutant IDH1 cancer cell growth, and this growth inhibition is not reversed by ivosidenib treatment. Significantly, ACC1 inhibition potentiates venetoclax efficacy in mutant IDH1 AML [44].

FASN is frequently overexpressed in multiple cancers and is strongly associated with poor clinical outcomes and enhanced tumor malignancy [45]. In AML, FASN overexpression supports the high demand for membrane lipid production and is essential for leukemogenesis. In AML with FLT3-ITD, FASN inhibition reduces p-Akt, phospho-S6 kinase, and Hedgehog-Gli1 expression, consistent with noncanonical Gli regulation by FLT3-ITD signaling and highlighting disruption of critical AML survival pathways [46]. In AML with PML-RARA fusion, inhibition of FASN combined with all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) enhances granulocytic differentiation and may expand the applicability of differentiation therapy in AML [47].

To prevent lipotoxicity and maintain membrane fluidity, saturated FAs are desaturated by desaturases, such as SCD1, into MUFAs. Recent studies suggest that elevated FAs in cancer cells increase their reliance on desaturases. Targeting desaturase activity may therefore provide a selective strategy to disrupt cancer cell metabolism [48]. SCD1 has been reported to influence ferroptosis, affect tumor prognosis and drug resistance, and promote tumor progression, invasion, and metastasis in vivo [49]. In FLT3-ITD AML, adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) drives malignant progression by suppressing ferroptosis via the CEBPα/SCD1 axis [50]. Furthermore, downregulation of SCD1 and the consequent reduction in MUFA synthesis exert antileukemic effects in AML cells [51].

Dysregulation in fatty acid oxidation pathways

FAO primarily occurs in mitochondria through a series of cyclic reactions, with each cycle generating NADH, FADH2, and acetyl-CoA [Figure 2]. NADH and FADH₂ generated during FAO feed into the electron transport chain (ETC) to drive ATP synthesis. FAs are first activated to fatty acyl-CoA by fatty acyl-CoA synthetase. Long-chain fatty acyl-CoA is then converted to fatty acyl-carnitine by CPT1 on the outer mitochondrial membrane for transport into mitochondria. Carnitine/acylcarnitine translocase (CACT) in the inner mitochondrial membrane then transports acyl-carnitine into the mitochondrial matrix, where CPT2 reconverts it to fatty acyl-CoA. Within mitochondria, fatty acyl-CoA is degraded into acetyl-CoA through a repeated four-step cycle catalyzed sequentially by acyl-CoA dehydrogenase, hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase, enoyl-CoA hydratase, and 3-ketoacyl-CoA thiolase (3-KAT). The resulting acetyl-CoA enters the TCA cycle and is coupled to OXPHOS to generate ATP [52].

FAO is a key component of metabolic reprogramming and supports cancer cell survival, proliferation, metastasis, and therapy resistance [53]. CPT1 is a key rate-limiting enzyme in FAO and promotes metabolic adaptation in cancer cells. Moreover, CPT1 is closely interconnected with multiple cellular signaling pathways, making it a multifunctional mediator in cancer development and progression [54]. To sustain elevated OXPHOS activity, AML cells rely on alternative energy substrates, particularly FAO, which is markedly more energy efficient than glucose oxidation. Overexpression of CPT1 is frequently observed in AML and is associated with poor clinical outcomes, supporting its potential as a therapeutic target [55].

Dysregulation in sphingolipid and ceramide metabolism pathways

Sphingolipids are bioactive lipids, including ceramides, sphingosine, dihydroceramide, and sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P), that play essential roles in cellular proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and signal transduction [56]. Figure 3 illustrates the sphingolipid and ceramide metabolic pathways, highlighting potential therapeutic targets for AML treatment [57].

Sphingolipid and ceramide metabolic pathways. Ceramide can be generated through de novo biosynthesis in the endoplasmic reticulum, where serine palmitoyltransferase catalyzes the conversion of palmitoyl-CoA and serine into 3-ketosphinganine, which subsequently undergoes a series of reactions to form ceramide. Alternatively, ceramide can be produced via hydrolysis of plasma membrane sphingomyelin by sphingomyelinase or through lysosomal degradation of glycosphingolipids by various enzymes. Ceramide is further converted into sphingosine by acid ceramidase; sphingosine can re-enter the cytosol and be recycled back into ceramide via ceramide synthase, constituting the salvage pathway. In addition, sphingosine can be phosphorylated by sphingosine kinase to generate sphingosine-1-phosphate. Small-molecule inhibitors targeting acid ceramidase, including LCL204 and LCL805, are indicated in red. (Created with BioRender.com)

Ceramides, composed of a sphingoid base and FA, are generated via de novo synthesis from serine and palmitoyl-CoA or through breakdown of glycosphingolipids, sphingomyelin, and ceramide-1-phosphate. Ceramide synthases form ceramides, while acid ceramidase (ASAH1) converts them to sphingosine, a precursor for S1P, balancing cell survival and death [58]. The dynamic interconversion between ceramide and S1P, referred to as the ceramide–S1P flux, is a critical sphingolipid regulatory axis implicated in cancer, including AML. Disruption of this balance, characterized by reduced intracellular ceramide levels and elevated S1P content, promotes cancer cell growth and survival [59]. Therefore, inhibition of ASAH1 enzyme activity leads to the accumulation of ceramides, thereby triggering cell death.

Analyses of primary AML patient samples revealed that ASAH1 is markedly overexpressed in AML compared with standard controls, highlighting its AML-specific overexpression. The primary AML cells exhibit elevated ASAH1 expression and activity, leading to increased antiapoptotic MCL-1 signaling, higher S1P levels, and reduced ceramide accumulation [60]. In AML, sphingosine kinase 1, which phosphorylates sphingosine to generate S1P, is also overexpressed and constitutively active, resulting in excessive S1P production [61]. Moreover, accumulating evidence links ceramide metabolism to the activity of key AML oncogenes. For example, FLT3-ITD mutations are associated with suppression of pro-cell death ceramide generation. Pharmacological inhibition of FLT3 restores ceramide production in AML cells and triggers ceramide-dependent mitophagy and cell death [62].

CURRENT LIPID METABOLISM-TARGETING STRATEGIES FOR ACUTE MYELOID LEUKEMIA TREATMENT

The therapeutic landscape of AML has been transformed, expanding beyond conventional chemotherapy to include targeted agents and lower-intensity regimens. Recent therapies include tyrosine kinase inhibitors, immune checkpoint inhibitors, monoclonal antibodies, bispecific T-cell engagers, and pro-apoptotic agents. The inhibitors targeting recurrent mutations such as IDH1/2 and FLT3; menin inhibitors for KMT2A-rearranged and NPM1-mutated AML; the antibody-drug conjugate gemtuzumab ozogamicin; oral azacitidine; and BCL-2 inhibitor venetoclax-based combination chemotherapy. Despite these advances, drug resistance and disease relapse remain major clinical challenges, highlighting the need for novel therapeutic strategies targeting alternative mechanisms [63].

Although aberrant lipid metabolism in cancer has been recognized for over two decades, no Food and Drug Administration-approved therapies currently target these pathways. Recent insights into lipid metabolic reprogramming in cancer cells, the role of lipid metabolism in tumor-infiltrating immune cells, and contributions from diet- and tumor microenvironment-derived lipids highlight the clinical potential of targeting lipid metabolism for cancer therapy [64]. Targeting lipid metabolism may therefore serve as a complementary strategy to enhance antileukemic efficacy, overcome resistance, and exploit metabolic vulnerabilities in specific AML subtypes. Integration of these lipid-metabolism targeting approaches into current treatment paradigms may offer new opportunities to improve AML patient outcomes. Targeting cholesterol biosynthesis and homeostasis

Statins

Statins inhibit the mevalonate pathway by targeting HMGCR, thereby reducing cholesterol biosynthesis. Based on their solubility, statins can be classified as either hydrophilic or lipophilic. Predominantly lipophilic statins, including atorvastatin, fluvastatin, lovastatin, pitavastatin, and simvastatin, readily enter cells and are widely distributed across different tissues. In contrast, hydrophilic statins, including pravastatin and rosuvastatin, exhibit greater liver specificity and are primarily excreted through the kidney [79]. These statins possess distinct pharmacokinetic and biochemical properties that may influence their therapeutic efficacy in cancers.

In vitro studies have shown that prolonged statins exposure inhibits cell growth and proliferation while inducing apoptosis in various human cancer cell types, including breast cancer (MCF-7, SKBr-3, and MDA-MB-231 cells), colorectal cancer (Caco-2 and HCT116 cells), liver cancer (Hep3B2 and HepG2 cells), lung cancer (Calu-3, Calu-6, A549, and H1299 cells), nasopharyngeal carcinoma (C666-1 cells), prostate cancer (PC-3 and LNCaP cells), and skin cancer (SCC-M7 and SCC-P9 cells). In vivo animal studies using xenograft mouse models further support these findings. For example, nude mice implanted with human lung, prostate, colorectal, or lymphoma cancer cells exhibited reduced tumor growth, as well as suppressed cancer cell migration and proliferation following statin treatment. In addition, statins have been used in combination with chemotherapeutic agents to enhance antiproliferative and proapoptotic effects. Ongoing clinical trials are evaluating the therapeutic potential of statins across various cancer types, based on promising preclinical evidence [8081].

Among these statins, lovastatin, rosuvastatin, pravastatin, and pitavastatin have been reported to exhibit potential anticancer effects in AML. Lovastatin and pitavastatin can cross cell membranes via passive diffusion, potentially enhancing their direct effects on leukemia cells. In particular, pitavastatin is a relatively newer statin with potent lipid-lowering activity and minimal cytochrome P450 metabolism, making it a promising candidate for combination therapies. In contrast, rosuvastatin and pravastatin exhibit more hepatoselectivity and favorable safety profiles, supporting their evaluation in combination with standard chemotherapy [798283].

In preclinical AML studies, lovastatin induces apoptosis, indicating that statin-mediated inhibition of the mevalonate pathway suppresses leukemic cell survival [65]. In addition, statins exert antileukemic effects by blocking the synthesis of FPP and GGPP. This depletion prevents the posttranslational prenylation of critical oncogenic GTPases, thereby impairing their membrane localization and downstream signaling [84]. Rosuvastatin pretreatment impedes GGPP production and restores chemosensitivity, suggesting that the mevalonate pathway is a potential therapeutic target in resistant TP53-mutated AML [66].

In clinical studies, the addition of pravastatin to idarubicin and cytarabine regimens has been evaluated in AML. Although this combination was safe in early-phase trials, larger studies failed to demonstrate consistent improvements in response rates [36]. More recently, statins have shown enhanced efficacy when combined with venetoclax-based therapies in primary leukemia and lymphoma. This has led to the successful completion of a Phase I clinical trial evaluating pitavastatin in combination with venetoclax in AML and chronic lymphocytic leukemia [6768]. Together with growing preclinical evidence supporting the therapeutic role of statins in hematologic malignancies, these findings have renewed interest in translational research targeting lipid metabolic pathways in AML.

Jaceosidin

Jaceosidin (Jace), a polymethoxyflavone derived from Artemisia species, exerts antileukemic activity by disrupting lipid homeostasis. It shows potent cytotoxicity in AML cell lines, including cytarabine-resistant MV4-11 CR cells, induces G1 cell cycle arrest, and promotes monocytic differentiation. In zebrafish xenograft models, jaceosidin reduces leukemic burden without evident developmental or cardiotoxic effects. Mechanistically, it transcriptionally suppresses lipid metabolic genes, including HMGCR, FASN, SCD1, and ACAT2, resulting in decreased intracellular cholesterol and triglyceride levels [69]. These findings suggest that jaceosidin inhibits leukemia progression by targeting both cholesterol and FA homeostasis, thereby disrupting lipid biosynthesis and cell cycle regulation.

α-Tomatine

α-Tomatine, a steroidal glycoalkaloid enriched in immature tomatoes, exerts cytotoxic effects by binding membrane cholesterol, disrupting lipid bilayers, and altering cellular homeostasis. In HL-60 cells, α-tomatine induces apoptosis and suppresses leukemic xenograft growth without toxicity, identifying membrane cholesterol as a lipid metabolic vulnerability in AML [70].

Zaragozic acid A

Zaragozic acid A (ZAA) is a fungal-derived metabolite that acts as an inhibitor of squalene synthase, a key enzyme in the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway, thereby suppressing cholesterol synthesis [71]. ZAA inhibits squalene synthase activity, leading to decreased cholesterol biosynthesis and reduced lipid raft-associated cholesterol in cancer cells. Consequently, ZAA attenuates cell proliferation and promotes cell death in prostate cancer cells [85]. In AML, treatment of NB4 cells with ZAA reduces cellular cholesterol levels and induces apoptosis, and it can be used in combination with radiotherapy and chemotherapy [72].

Dendrogenin A

Dendrogenin A (DDA), a cholesterol-derived metabolite and partial LXR agonist, exhibits antitumor activity by activating autophagy-related genes. It inhibits the cholesterol biosynthetic enzyme 3β-hydroxysteroid Δ8,7-isomerase (D8D7I), leading to sterol accumulation and enhanced autophagy [73]. DDA has demonstrated anticancer efficacy across various mouse and human cancer models, including breast cancer, melanoma, and AML [86]. A combination of DDA with cytarabine demonstrates that DDA can potentiate and sensitize AML cells to cytarabine in vitro and in vivo, including in primary patient samples [87]. These findings support the clinical evaluation of DDA alone or in combination with anticancer drugs for AML treatment.

Targeting de novo lipogenesis, fatty acid metabolism, and desaturation

Epigallocatechin-3-gallate

Epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG) is a bioactive polyphenolic phytochemical and the major catechin found in Camellia sinensis (green tea). EGCG has been reported to exhibit anticancer activity by targeting the lipogenesis pathway and inhibiting FASN in human solid tumors, including breast cancer and lung cancer [8889]. In AML cell lines (MOLM-13, OCI-AML2), EGCG suppresses mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling and promotes autophagy by targeting FASN, facilitating the nuclear translocation and activation of transcription factor EB (TFEB), and activating CLEAR network genes. It also upregulates death-associated protein kinase 2 (DAPK2), a positive regulator of granulocytic differentiation. Together, these findings indicate that EGCG overcomes differentiation blocks in AML by disrupting lipid metabolic signaling through FASN downregulation and the mTOR–TFEB axis [47].

Polyphyllin I

Polyphyllin I (PPI) is a bioactive steroidal saponin phytochemical isolated from the rhizomes of Paris polyphylla [90]. Recent studies have shown that PPI may exert broad-spectrum antitumor effects across various cancer types [90]. The MOLM-13 AML cell line showed that PPI exhibits potent cytotoxicity. Its activity is linked to PI3K signaling and lipid regulation, as PI3K inhibition enhanced erastin-induced ferroptosis. In vivo, PPI treatment significantly suppressed tumor growth and prolonged overall survival in leukemia-bearing mice with minimal toxicity. Mechanistic studies, including molecular docking and kinase inhibition assays, have identified PPI as a specific inhibitor of PI3K. By targeting the PI3K/SREBP-1/SCD1 signaling axis, which regulates lipid biosynthesis and desaturation. PPI disrupts cellular lipid homeostasis, leading to intracellular iron accumulation and extensive lipid peroxidation [74]. These findings suggest that PPI targets the PI3K/SREBP-1/SCD1 axis to disrupt lipid metabolism, induce lipid peroxidation, and trigger ferroptosis in AML cells.

Lycorine

Lycorine is a bioactive alkaloid phytochemical derived from Amaryllidaceae plants, including Lycoris radiata [91]. Lycorine inhibits pancreatic cancer cell proliferation and induces apoptosis by suppressing FA metabolism via downregulation of ALDH3A1 [92]. In AML, molecular docking suggests that lycorine binds to the FABP5 ligand pocket, thereby inhibiting FABP5 and its downstream target, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ). Reduction of triglyceride synthesis and lipid droplet formation disrupts FA metabolism required for leukemic cell survival. Given that FABP5 is overexpressed in AML and associated with poor prognosis and drug resistance, lycorine decreases cell viability and induces apoptosis. Overall, lycorine exerts antileukemic effects by targeting the FABP5–PPARγ axis and lipid metabolic dependency in AML [75].

SB-204990

SB-204990, a cell-penetrant gamma-lactone compound, is a potent small-molecule inhibitor of ACLY. Recent studies indicate that SB-204990 exerts antitumor effects by inhibiting ACLY activity in thyroid cancer and oral squamous cell carcinoma cells [93]. In AML cell lines, including MOLM-13 and THP-1, SB-204990 significantly suppresses cell proliferation [42]. Therefore, SB-204990 represents a promising therapeutic agent that targets lipid biosynthetic pathways to inhibit leukemic progression.

Targeting fatty acid oxidation

Avocatin B

Avocatin B (Avo B) is a bioactive lipid derived from Persea americana (avocado) seeds and consists of a 1:1 mixture of avocadyne and avocadene [94]. In vitro, Avo B selectively induces cytotoxicity in leukemia cell lines and primary AML blasts. In vivo, treatment with Avo B markedly reduces leukemic engraftment in non-obese diabetic/severe combined immunodeficiency (NOD/SCID) mouse models [76]. Mechanistically, Avo B selectively accumulates in mitochondria and inhibits FAO, leading to leukemia cell death [76]. In particular, its constituent avocadyne directly targets and inhibits VLCAD, thereby impairing mitochondrial FA catabolism [95]. This inhibition leads to a marked depletion of intracellular NADPH levels, accompanied by increased ROS production, ultimately triggering mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis characterized by the release of cytochrome c and apoptosis-inducing factor [76]. Notably, Avo B-induced cytotoxicity depends on CPT1, as CPT1-deficient cells exhibit resistance to Avo B [76]. Collectively, these findings indicate that Avo B exerts therapeutic effects by disrupting lipid homeostasis, specifically inhibiting mitochondrial FAO, thereby targeting a metabolic vulnerability unique to AML cells.

Etomoxir and ST1326

Targeting FAO effectively deprives chemoresistant AML cells of essential metabolic substrates. Etomoxir, a CPT1A inhibitor, sensitizes leukemic cells to Ara-C and BCL-2 inhibitors [96]. However, the clinical development of Etomoxir was discontinued due to severe hepatotoxicity and off-target effects observed at high concentrations. This limitation has redirected attention toward alternative FAO-targeting agents, including ST1326, which selectively inhibits CPT1A with reduced toxicity compared to Etomoxir [28]. ST1326 has been reported to exert antitumor effects by inhibiting FAO in paclitaxel-tolerant ovarian cancer persister cells and Burkitt lymphoma cells [9798]. In AML, downregulation of CPT1A suppresses leukemic cell proliferation. Notably, ST1326 in combination with the venetoclax demonstrated a marked synergistic effect, inducing apoptosis in both AML cell lines and primary patient blasts [99].

Targeting sphingolipid and ceramide metabolism

LCL204

Ceramidase inhibitors such as N-dimethylglycine (DMG)-B13 and next-generation lysosomal-targeting analog prodrugs have been developed. LCL204 is a lysosomotropic ceramide analog that functions as a synthetic inhibitor of ASAH1. Increased expression of ASAH1 has been observed in prostate cancer. Treatment with LCL204 induces lysosomal destabilization, mitochondrial depolarization, caspase activation, and ultimately apoptosis in prostate cancer cells [77]. ASAH1 is also markedly upregulated in AML blasts, and its overexpression confers resistance to apoptosis. Pharmacological inhibition of ASAH1 by LCL204 increases intracellular ceramide levels and reduces MCL-1 protein expression through posttranslational mechanisms, thereby inducing apoptotic cell death. Consistently, LCL204 treatment decreases cell viability and promotes apoptosis in AML patient samples and cell lines, and significantly reduces leukemic burden while prolonging survival in mice engrafted with primary human AML cells in patient-derived xenograft models [60].

LCL805

The DMG-B-13 prodrug LCL-805 exhibits robust in vitro antileukemic activity in AML cells and primary patient samples by suppressing ASAH1 enzyme activity, thereby increasing intracellular ceramide levels and reducing sphingosine content. Co-treatment with C6-ceramide nanoliposomes, currently advancing into early-phase clinical trials for relapsed or refractory AML, markedly potentiates LCL805-mediated cytotoxicity. Mechanistically, LCL805 disrupts Akt signaling and induces an iron-dependent, noncanonical form of cell death distinct from classical ferroptosis [78]. Collectively, these findings indicate that modulation of sphingolipid ceramide homeostasis, particularly through ASAH1, represents a novel and therapeutically promising target in AML.

CHALLENGES AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES

Conventional therapeutics in AML are limited by systemic toxicity and the emergence of drug-resistant leukemic clones. Developing selective antileukemic strategies that target lipid metabolic networks, therefore, represents a novel and important approach. In recent years, targeting lipid metabolism has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy in AML. Most evidence for targeting lipid metabolism in AML treatment comes from preclinical studies data, and clinical data and translation remain limited. Lipid metabolic pathways are essential for normal cell homeostasis, and achieving efficacy without toxicity poses a significant challenge. Furthermore, the mechanisms by which lipid metabolic heterogeneity and microenvironmental influences support cancer cell survival and therapeutic resistance are still poorly understood in AML. The pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of most lipid-targeting agents, along with their effects on the metabolic plasticity of AML cells and LSCs, remain to be elucidated in future studies.

To address these challenges, it is critical to define lipid metabolic dependencies across distinct genetic and mutational contexts in AML subtypes and to delineate metabolic conditions that confer actionable therapeutic vulnerabilities while establishing a therapeutic window between malignant and normal cells. Future efforts may focus on clinical trials and combination strategies to integrate lipid metabolism inhibitors to advance the clinical translation of lipid-targeting approaches in AML.
