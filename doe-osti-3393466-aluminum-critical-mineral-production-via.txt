Abstract
Aluminum is designated as a critical mineral by the USGS and is used in many applications,
including air and ground vehicles, packaging (beverage containers and foils), construction,
electrical (including power transmission and solar racking), consumer durables, and
machinery/equipment. However, no commercially viable mining of bauxite for aluminum
production occurs in the United States. According to the EPA, from 1960 to 2018, 103 million
tons of aluminum was disposed of in landfills, which is about 20 times the five million tons of
aluminum consumed by the US in 2018. Since the greatest US concentration of high-grade
aluminum “ore” is in landfills, recovering and recycling this aluminum has several benefits:
 1. Reduce US dependence on imported bauxite for aluminum production.
 2. Reduce energy and greenhouse gas emissions as recycling aluminum requires only 5% of
 the energy needed for primary aluminum production from bauxite.
 3. Reclaim landfill materials such as iron, other nonferrous metals, plastics, and glass.
 4. Reduce landfill volume by processing organics and removing metallics.
The project had four primary tasks as summarized below.
 1. Policy and Community Impacts: Demand for refuse-derived fuel contributed to early
 landfill mining (LFM) experiments and policymaking in the 1990s, and in the future
 critical minerals policies could provide a new “policy window” for LFM policies
 designed to incentivize LFM for aluminum recovery. Future work could leverage
 nationwide data sets for landfills and flood risk to identify candidate states for LFM.
 Regulatory policy is not a significant barrier to LFM, but policies could be better
 designed and more widely adopted to for LFM, particularly LFM with resource recovery.
 2. Techno-Economic Analysis (TEA) and Life Cycle Analysis (LCA): Metal concentration,
 post-mining land use, and equipment selection (diesel versus electric) are the most
 significant factors affecting project viability. Landfill mining can be economically and
 environmentally beneficial when combined with favorable landfill compositions and
 high-value post-mining land uses. All scenarios produce aluminum with less CO2
 emissions that primary Al, but they are higher current scrap aluminum sources.
 3. Separation: Of the recovered aluminum, predominantly beverage cans and foil, all
 aluminum foil was corroded, while most beverage cans remained metallic due to
 protective coatings. Recovery is highly dependent on aluminum form and corrosion
 coating. An aerodynamic separator should be positioned downstream of excavation,
 drying, and coarse screening and upstream of the magnetic separation, eddy-current
 separation, and optical sorting equipment used for final aluminum and metal recovery.
 Removing lightweight plastic contamination in a single, chemical-free pass reduces the
 volume and contamination load presented to downstream recovery equipment, addressing
 directly the coarse-fraction plastic problem identified during hand-sorting.
 4. Avoiding downcycling of recovered aluminum: Computational tools were developed to
 design an alloy with a challenging composition of high Mg and high Fe, and it performed
 similar to a baseline 319, but more work is needed.
There is real value in the metallic contents of landfills, and there are economic factors that can
support landfill mining. However, having quantified this process, it is clear that separating
valuable metals from the waste stream before burying them is a much more efficient strategy,
and this should be pursued to the greatest extent possible.

Introduction (edited from proposal)
Project Objectives
Aluminum is designated as a critical mineral by the USGS (USGS 2022) and is used in many
applications, including air and ground vehicles, packaging (beverage containers and foils),
construction, electrical (including power transmission and solar racking), consumer durables, and
machinery/equipment (Plunkert 2000). However, no commercially viable mining of bauxite for
aluminum production occurs in the United States (OECD 2012). According to the EPA, from
1960 to 2018, 103 million tons of aluminum was disposed of in landfills (US EPA 2020a), which
is about 20 times the five million tons of aluminum consumed by the US in 2018 (USGS 2020).
Since the greatest US concentration of high-grade aluminum “ore” is in landfills, recovering and
recycling this aluminum would have several benefits:
 1. Reduce US dependence on imported bauxite for aluminum production.
 2. Reduce energy and greenhouse gas emissions as recycling aluminum requires only 5% of
 the energy needed for primary aluminum production from bauxite (Das et al. 2010).
 3. Reclaim landfill materials such as iron, other nonferrous metals (e.g., copper), plastics,
 and glass.
 4. Reduce landfill volume by processing organics and removing metallics.
Given the value of the landfilled aluminum and other materials, why isn't significant landfill
mining occurring in the US? A potential reason is that uncertainty and risk make the techno-
economic analysis appear unfavorable. Some key questions are: What exactly is in a landfill,
both valuable commodities and hazardous materials? How difficult and expensive will it be to
extract and separate the contents? What are the environmental and community impacts of landfill
mining? This project will utilize an interdisciplinary team of engineers (environmental,
metallurgy, and mineral processing) and social scientists to address these questions. The
hypothesis is that the site selection is critical to assessing the technical and economic feasibility
of landfill mining for materials. Social science analysis of the landfill history/contents and
community concerns will be key to selecting a landfill pilot with a high probability of being
viable economically, environmentally, and favorably viewed within the community. To teach
this approach to problem solving to Michigan Tech undergraduates, aspects of this project will
be analyzed by interdisciplinary senior design teams.

Background (edited from proposal)

Merit Review Criterion 1: Scientific and Technological Merit
Municipal solid waste (MSW) composition and production in the US is tracked by the
Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). The most recent analysis was in 2018, in which the
generation and disposal of 13 categories of materials was tabulated (Figure 1). The box above the
first five materials shows the focus of this project and represents 25% (by mass) of MSW
currently with a 20% recycling rate. The four center materials are 63% of MSW and can either
be recycled or composted (50% were in 2018). Finally, the last four material types are 12% of
MSW and only 15% are recycled. Combusted refers to the process of incineration of MSW; the
resultant fly ash and bottom ash is usually landfilled, as beneficial use of ash is “nearly non-
existent” in the US (Oehmig et al. 2015). Given this fact, for recycling estimates, it will be
assumed that combusted metal waste is primarily landfilled and available for recovery, while
plastics are fully combusted and glass is significantly downgraded. Also it should be noted that
this analysis does not include construction and demolition debris, which for 2018 totaled 600
million tons or double the MSW production amount (US EPA 2020b). About 75% of this was
recycled or reused, but this still leaves 25% (150 million tons) deposited in landfills.

Johnson states, “the concentration of aluminum in many US landfills is higher than the
concentration of aluminum in bauxite” (Hogland, Hogland, and Marques 2011). Landfill mining
had been studied extensively in Europe from 2000 through the present, with a peak of activity
around 2014-1016. Since 1995, Europe has implemented effective resource recovery in MSW
streams, and landfill mining focuses on landfills active from 1960 to 1995 (Hogland et al. 2011).
This paper and others (Danthurebandara et al. 2015; Van Passel et al. 2013) focus on the concept
of enhanced landfill mining (ELFM), which Danthurebandara defines as an “integrated
optimisation of materials and energy recovery, while bringing the final residue volume to almost
zero while mitigating CO2 emissions.” Van Passel´s team has developed a 5-step procedure to
optimize private and societal costs and benefits. This concept of a landfill mining system is
what this project seeks to develop, while explicitly considering the impacts on the local
community and environment, as well as society at large.

Technical Approach and Understanding

2.2.1 Task 1-Social science assessment of landfills and landfill-adjacent communities
Survey of landfill history: While national-level estimates on landfill contents are available, there
is significant variation at state and local levels. Through historical analysis of archival records,
trade publications, and official data sets, landfill development will be characterized and
periodized, with a focus on aluminum consumption and waste patterns in the US. This research
will identify geographic variations in aluminum waste, taking into account landfill policies,
siting, size, populations served, and state and local waste diversion policies (particularly can
deposits and municipal recycling). The data will guide the selection of research sites for later
phases of the project.
Community impacts: Survey qualitative, quantitative, and geospatial literature on landfills and
landfill-adjacent communities in the US to identify key trends and impacts of past landfill
activities on communities. Potential hazards, nuisances, and justice concerns posed by landfill
mining will be evaluated. Best practices for identifying and engaging with stakeholders and

5

communities will be developed. Once approved by the Michigan Tech Institutional Review
Board, research will begin and outreach initiated at sites identified in the landfill survey.
2.2.2 Task 2-Modeling sustainability assessment of landfill mining
Life cycle assessment (LCA): Develop a gate-to-gate life cycle assessment on the process of
landfill mining, with the system boundary starting at the point of waste recovery from the landfill
and continuing through to the production of separated materials available for use as secondary
feedstocks to other manufacturing or agricultural processes. The life cycle inventory will be
developed in collaboration with the flowsheet development in Task 3, describing the material
and energy inputs for the unit operations involved in materials recovery and separation for the
integrated landfill mining process, and additionally, the inventory will be extended to include
transportation, further processing of materials (e.g., composting of organics), and changes in
landfill gas dynamics that are expected. Life cycle modeling will be performed in the SimaPro
LCA modeling platform using the most geographically and technologically relevant life cycle
input data available, such as the US Life cycle inventory data or the ecoinvent database. A
number of scenarios will be developed to illustrate the potential differences in environmental
outcomes between processing configurations, technical assumptions, and uncertainties in key
parameters. Allocation of environmental burdens between products will be performed to
highlight the impacts of different regulatory regimes. Special attention will be given to the
tradeoffs between environmental metrics of concern to the local community, such as particulate
matter air pollution or water quality deterioration, and globally-relevant metrics like greenhouse
gas emissions to recognize potential tradeoffs or reduce impacts on local communities.
Techno-economic assessment (TEA): The process flowsheet will be utilized as the basis for a
detailed life cycle model of the environmental performance of the integrated landfill mining
system, in order to ensure favorable economic outcomes for a business or community willing to
invest in such a system. Process inputs of materials and utilities will be combined with input data
on labor requirements, likely capital costs for all equipment required, and anticipated taxes,
insurance, and other costs. These expenses will be balanced against the expected production and
market values of the range of products expected over the operational horizon of the process,
typically assumed to be 20 years, to develop a detailed cash flow model that will calculate key
financial metrics such as the minimum selling prices of the products at different levels of internal
rate of return. Like the LCA modeling, these economic models will be developed under several
scenarios to illustrate the importance of key process assumptions, input costs, and different
process configurations, in order to outline the zone of operating conditions that will strongly
predict a favorable economic return on a large capital investment for landfill mining.
2.2.3 Task 3-Separation of landfilled resources
Flowsheet development: Based on the landfill composition, an appropriate flowsheet will be
designed to produce marketable products, with a particular focus on critical materials that may
be present. Aluminum will likely be a key material, as will copper and any significant quantity of
electronic devices which may be processable for their component materials. The flowsheet will
6

also be designed to ensure that landfill components are used in constructive applications to avoid
re-landfilling.
Test apparatus construction: A pilot plant capable of processing up to a few hundred kilograms
per hour will be constructed, with the ultimate objective of testing it either on-site at the landfill,
or with landfill contents brought to Michigan Technological University. The shredded and
cleaned products generated with the test apparatus, particularly recovered aluminum and other
metals, will be provided raw materials in the Task 3 recycling and recovery studies.
2.2.4 Task 4-Aluminum recovery from separated landfill waste
The process of producing commercial quality metal from aluminum recovered from the MSW
waste stream will focus on two processes:
 ● Remelting and casting high quality ingots for characterization of the cast microstructure
 and mechanical properties (tensile, fatigue). Extrusion billets will also be cast so that cast
 and wrought materials may be compared.
 ● In addition to traditional melt processing, solid state thermo-mechanical processing via
 extrusion will be performed on billets made from compacted aluminum MSW. These
 materials will be characterized in the same way as the melt-processed materials.
2.2.5 Task 5-HDSTEM interdisciplinary capstone senior design experience on landfill mining
Dr. Sanders currently coordinates the MSE Capstone Senior Design program, and with Dr.
Robins, he will lead the formation of an interdisciplinary senior design team to study aspects of
landfill mining. The team will be composed of social science and engineering students and will
participate as one team within the MSE senior design program. Primarily this means weekly
meetings with faculty advisors and stakeholders as well as weekly updates presented to all the
other MSE student teams and faculty advisors.

Task 1: Landfill Site History & Community Impacts

Background: Landfills and landfill mining in the United States
The U.S. currently has more than 1,200 operational municipal solid waste (MSW) landfills, and
well over 10,000 closed landfills [1]. Since 2000, most new permitted MSW landfills have been
sited in rural areas or outlying industrial districts of cities, where low population density reduces
conflicts over noise, odors, and other nuisances between landfill operators and local residents
[2,3,4]. However, more than half of currently-operating landfills are more than 35 years old and
many have been surrounded by newer urban and suburban development. These land-locked
landfills create challenges and opportunities for communities that landfill mining can address.
Additionally, US communities host thousands of closed landfills ranging from 20 to 100 years
old. These sites hold a wide variety of MSW and hazardous waste in close proximity to
residential areas.

To date, the recovery of metals and other materials has not been a primary motive for landfill
mining in the US. Landfill mining has been practiced since the 1970s to achieve four different
objectives [5]:
 1) Energy. In the 1970s-1980s, federally- and state-funded projects in Florida, Pennsylvania,
 New York, and several other states mined landfills to produce refuse-derived fuel (RDF)
 for use in waste-to-energy plants. The rate of new waste-to-energy plant construction fell
 dramatically in the 1990s, and there has not been any LFM for RDF production since
 2000.
 2) Airspace. Municipalities with aging landfills are running out of storage capacity, and
 cannot expand the physical footprint of existing landfill sites in urban and suburban areas.
 Approximately half of the volume of an old landfill is composed of cover material. This
 cover material can be reclaimed and reused, significantly extending the life of a landfill
 (Texas, New Jersey, Florida).
 3) Remediation. Many closed landfills and older cells in active landfills predate more
 stringent 1988 rules for landfill design. Leachate from unlined or failed landfill systems
 poses risks to groundwater. Landfill mining has been used to remove waste for reburial in
 modern landfill cells (Virginia).
 4) Redevelopment. Typical beneficial use cases for closed landfills involve the construction
 of parks and other light surface infrastructure. Landfill mining allows more substantial
 redevelopment, including residential and commercial construction and novel
 opportunities like stormwater detention.

To understand opportunities and barriers for landfill mining in the US and the conditions that
could incentivize LFM for aluminum recovery, an extensive review was conducted of the
scientific literature as well as “gray literature” where many reports on landfill mining appear. A
review of the national news media identified past and current LFM projects, including potential
case studies in Texas, North Carolina, and Virginia. These cases were investigated by contacting
landfill operators, state regulators, and community organizations using an interview protocol
approved by the Michigan Tech Institutional Review Board (IRB).

12

Historical characterization
The profile of US MSW deposits changed with consumer habits and the expansion of recycling
systems. To understand the kinds of aluminum materials present in landfills, historical data were
collected on landfill and salvage practices and on consumer aluminum products from the
Aluminum Association and the archives of Alcoa and the Reynolds Metals Company [6,7,8].
It was found that foil was a minor component of the total mass of landfilled aluminum. Given the
thin nature of foil products and the contamination resulting from typical foil uses, foil was not
expected to be recoverable as metallic aluminum. Other categories including cookware,
furniture, and consumer durables are present in landfills, but in highly variable quantities
depending on local landfill management and diversion policies.

Aluminum beverage cans (UBCs) constitute the largest category of aluminum in the MSW
stream by mass [6,9]. Estimates show that landfills closed before 1980 are unlikely to contain
enough UBCs to justify landfill mining without additional co-benefits. Early-adopting regions
like Texas, Florida, and California had robust aluminum-recycling programs in the 1970s,
diverting UBCs from landfills. Quantities of cans sold nationwide increased significantly from
1980-1990, but the continued presence of “bimetallic” steel cans with aluminum lids in this
period may complicate recovery. By 1990, fully-aluminum cans displaced bimetallic cans and
composed about 2% of the MSW stream, despite increasing success with recycling programs.
[10] Post-1990 landfills offer the greatest potential for recovering metallic aluminum from cans.
However, these landfills postdate the 1988 EPA “Subtitle D” rules and are less likely to require
remediation. Many are still in operation, making them less promising candidates for LFM.
Student intern Allison Dodd collected data on landfill size and contents and developed a
methodology for calculating current and projected accumulations of aluminum in landfills across
each site’s life expectancy. Dodd and MS student Anabel Needham applied the methodology on
data for Texas, identifying 8 sites with expected aluminum accumulations over 1 million tons,
and 50 other sites ranging from 250,000-940,000 tons [11]. With additional state-level data on
recycling rates, the method can be applied nationally to identify candidate landfills with large
deposits of aluminum (Table 6).

Policy analysis and policy history
The second focus of Task 1 sought to understand the history and current status of landfill mining
policies in the US. Work on this subtask spanned all three years of the project, including a
collaboration between Robins and policy scientist Adam Wellstead (MTU), with support from
student research interns Gabriella Blumenschein, Emma Strutz, and Erin Loeschmann. Student
interns helped compile and code a database of current state policies for landfills and landfill
mining.

The EPA issued a short guidance document in 1997, but there are no LFM-specific federal
regulations [14]. Data shows that 7 US states offer clear regulatory processes for landfill mining:
Maine, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New York, Texas, and Virginia. Landfill mining is still
possible in the remaining 43 states, but requires experimental or research permits or waivers. All
LFM activities are governed by Federal regulations on the disturbance of landfilled MSW.
The lack of clear policies in most states compounds the uncertainties inherent in landfill mining,
discouraging landfill operators from pursuing LFM. This research on LFM policy contributes to
a growing worldwide literature on policy barriers in LFM, and in an article submitted to Waste
Management Wellstead and Robins propose additional conceptual tools from the policy sciences
for understanding how and why effective policies for LFM could be enacted [15]. Demand for
refuse-derived fuel contributed to early LFM experiments and policymaking in the 1990s [5],
and in the future critical minerals policies could open a new “policy window” for LFM policies
designed to incentivize LFM for aluminum recovery.

The historical and policy data pointed to Texas as a promising state for additional research
(Table 7). Texas has a large population with high rates of aluminum can consumption, relatively
low historic rates of can recycling, and clear state rules for LFM permitting and procedures. A
proposed LFM project in Denton, TX was identified, but interviews were not possible due to
cancellation of the project. The Denton project was designed to recover landfill airspace, with
material recovery as a secondary goal. Revised cost estimates showed that material recovery
would not cover the cost of excavation and recovery, and Denton opted to raise the height of the
existing landfill rather than mining.

Conclusions
This research evaluated the potential aluminum content of landfills and identified additional co-
benefits, including stormwater detention, that might make LFM projects financially feasible. It
was found that regulatory policy is not a significant barrier to LFM, but that policies could be
better designed and more widely adopted to encourage LFM, particularly LFM with resource
recovery. Potentially contradictory aims for LFM were identified that policymakers must
address. Landfill operators, including municipal governments, primarily see LFM as a tool to
extend the working life of landfills, with remediation of current or potential hazards as an
important secondary goal. Community leaders see LFM as a tool to remove unwanted sites and
potential environmental hazards from their communities. While currently-open landfills offer the
highest potential for recovering aluminum, LFM on these sites would keep open unwanted
landfills and may generate community opposition to LFM. A win-win scenario would identify
closed landfills where multiple co-benefits could cover the costs of LFM while also addressing
community concerns by removing and remediating landfill sites.

Task 2: LCA and TEA: Economic Evaluation of landfill mining
Introduction
This report outlines the development of the techno-economic assessment (TEA) and life cycle
assessment (LCA) scenarios for the recovery of aluminum and copper through landfill mining.
Key processing scenario options are outlined, including alternative use cases for the landfill
space after materials excavation, as well as a modified process flow diagram that incorporates
aerodynamic separation based on the work being done by Daniel Amponsah - Berko and Dr. Tim
Eisele in Task 3 of this project. Student Frank Neshumba (MS in Chemical Engineering) has
been instrumental in the development of this work, and his MS report “ TECHNO-ECONOMIC
AND LIFE CYCLE ASSESSMENT OF ALUMINUM AND COPPER RECOVERY FROM
LANDFILL MINING” has been published. A publication to a peer-reviewed journal is being
submitted from this work as well.

Updated Model
One of the potential benefits of landfill mining is that it can be performed in conjunction with
other activities, and potentially deliver multiple co-benefits to a community. The initial modeling
work focused on the opportunity for redeveloping landfill space into stormwater detention
basics, as this was a primary need shown in Houston Texas at the Ruffino Hills site that has
informed much of this work. Alternatively, many communities are running low on landfill space,
and the opportunity to excavate older, poorly designed landfills enable landfill redesign into a
modern facility that is capable of holding much larger volumes of MSW through proper bank
stabilization and other techniques. A model of landfill space reuse is proposed that would allow
the base case landfill site, with an MSW depth of 12-15 feet like Ruffino Hills, to be redeveloped
with a 40 foot MSW depth, which is a fairly conservative redevelopment plan. Accounting for
the costs of this new facility (site redevelopment, compacted soil liner, geomembrane, leachate
system, etc.), it seems that they would be more than made up for by the additional tipping fees
collected by the enlarged facility over its lifetime. This landfill redevelopment option has the
potential to be an order of magnitude more profitable for a 100-acre model facility than the base
case assumption of stormwater redevelopment (~$60 million net revenue vs $5 million). This is
very dependent on the assumed tipping fees and operational expenses of the landfills, so the
literature was surveyed to get reasonable estimates for both. The Environmental Research and
Education Foundation (EREF) has published a 2024 report on MSW tipping fees across the
country that appears to be a good starting point, and operating costs are generally assumed to be
60-70% of tipping fees on a per-ton basis, but that can vary by facility size and type.

Additional work has focused on developing an alternative process flow diagram to model an
alternative scenario that relies on aerodynamic separation (Figure 6) to split the MSW sample
into material streams that can be isolated for future processing. If aerodynamic separation
identified in Task 3 can be a low cost, effective materials separation technique, it could reduce
the volume of material sent downstream to the magnetic separation and eddy current separation
stages, lowering the capital and operational costs. Current work with the Task 3 team entails
quantifying material separation efficiencies and understanding how they might scale to a system
20

that requires several tons per hour. The prototype air classifier built at Michigan Tech has a small
inlet for introducing MSW to the system, but a larger commercial-scale version of this system
would be capable of handling much more MSW per hour through several inlets of larger size, so
a defensible scaled-up model of this commercial-scale air classifier is needed to understand the
sizing of this unit operation for future economic and environmental modeling.

The techno-economic and environmental feasibility of recovering aluminum and copper from a
legacy municipal solid waste landfill using the 100-acre Ruffino Hills Landfill in Houston, Texas
was assessed with two processing pathways:
 1. A primary pathway consisting of excavation, screening, and metal separation.
 2. A revised pathway incorporating magnetic separation, shredding, and aerodynamic
 separation.
Techno-economic and life cycle assessments were conducted for both pathways. Three post-
mining land-use scenarios were evaluated: redevelopment of the reclaimed site as a stormwater
detention basin, landfill re-use, or a public recreational park.

The results show that metal concentration, post-mining land use, and equipment selection (diesel
versus electric) are the most significant factors affecting project viability. The stormwater
detention basin scenario (right columns) was not economically feasible at either national-average
or sampled Marquette landfill metal concentrations, generating net losses of approximately $14.5
million and $10.2 million for the primary and revised pathways, respectively (Table 8). In
contrast, landfill re-use cases (left columns) produced the strongest economic performance,

Task 3: Landfill Separation Flowsheet
Overview
Task 3 addresses the central empirical question underlying aluminum recovery through landfill
mining: how much aluminum is actually present in a real landfill, in what form, and in what
physical condition. Working in partnership with the Marquette County Solid Waste Management
Authority (MCSWMA, Director Mike Stannard), a field-scale excavation and material
characterization study at the MCSWMA landfill was completed, supported by a controlled
laboratory-scale barrel simulation of landfill aging. This work quantified aluminum abundance,
distribution, and corrosion behavior in real excavated waste. Building on what this
characterization revealed — in particular, that lightweight plastic contamination significantly
complicates sorting —a low-cost aerodynamic separator was developed to remove the
lightweight fraction ahead of downstream metal recovery equipment. The two efforts are
reported below.

Marquette County Landfill Waste Excavation and Characterization

Site and Field Sampling
The MCSWMA landfill is a state-permitted regional solid waste facility in Sands Township,
Michigan, established in 1988 under an intergovernmental agreement representing all 23
municipalities of Marquette County. As of December 31, 2024, the site had received a
cumulative 2,081,474 tons of non-hazardous municipal solid waste, with 105,368 tons received
during 2024 alone, and had approximately 47 years of permitted capacity remaining. The landfill
was selected as the field site for this study after attempts to obtain waste from other facilities
were restricted by environmental and safety regulations governing the handling of municipal
solid waste for research purposes; MCSWMA offered a feasible, controlled, and accessible
source of real landfill material.

  Figure 8. Field excavation at the MCSWMA landfill during December 2024 gas-well drilling
 program showing CZM EK125 rotary drilling rig used to access buried waste (left), and waste
 samples collected by hand from depths of 70–100 feet (center, right).

Field access was obtained in December 2024 when MCSWMA initiated a pilot drilling operation
to install methane extraction gas wells in their landfill using a CZM EK125 heavy-duty rotary
24

drilling rig. During drilling, waste material was collected with shovels from depths of 70–100
feet and placed into twenty one 5-gallon buckets (Figure 8) , providing a rare opportunity to
sample real, deeply buried landfill waste directly rather than relying on surface material or
synthetic waste analogs.

Waste Processing and Characterization Methods
A total of 266 kg of excavated wet waste was processed in batches using a flow diagram to guide
processing (Figure 9). Each batch was weighed and dried at 70°C for 48 hours.
 1. The dried material was then manually screened to separate a fine fraction (<7 mm) from a
 coarse fraction (>7 mm), with the mesh size chosen to remove residual soil and daily
 cover material from the waste itself.
 2. The coarse fraction — 140 kg in total — was then hand-sorted into ten material
 categories (paper/cardboard, plastics, rubber, textiles, wood, ferrous metal, aluminum,
 copper, glass, ceramic, and stones), with each category weighed and recorded.
 3. Recovered aluminum was further inspected visually and classified as either metallic
 (non-oxidized) or corroded (oxidized), and each group was weighed to assess
 recyclability.

 Figure 9. Process flow diagram for waste samples obtained from MCSWMA.

Simulation of landfill environment using barrels to study aluminum corrosion rate
To assess aluminum stability under longer-term, controlled landfill conditions, two 208-liter (55-
gallon) HDPE barrels were loaded with municipal solid waste from the nearby Houghton County
Materials Management Facility, supplemented with known quantities of aluminum foil, beverage
cans, and filings (600 g in the first barrel, 300 g in the second) to ensure sufficient aluminum was
present for evaluation (Figure 10). Water was added to bring each barrel to approximately 25%
water by weight, the waste was compacted, and the barrels were loosely sealed to permit limited
25

air exchange to approximate semi-aerobic to anaerobic landfill conditions. The barrels were left
undisturbed for six months, after which the contents were air-dried (Figure 11) and the aluminum
components recovered, classified as metallic or corroded, and weighed. Aluminum foil samples
from before and after the six-month exposure were additionally examined by scanning electron
microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) to characterize surface
compositional changes directly.

Metals and Aluminum Recovery
Metals accounted for 2.3 wt.% of the total dried waste mass. Following manual recovery of all
metallic items, magnetic separation identified ferrous metals (nails, food cans, and household
items) as the dominant metal category, with most items showing extensive surface rust and
pitting (Figure 12). Aluminum was the second most abundant metal, recovered at 0.43 wt.% of
total dried mass, while copper was recovered only in trace amounts (< 0.1 wt.%), primarily as
electrical wire fragments and a copper rod, none of which showed visible corrosion. Of the
recovered aluminum — predominantly beverage cans and foil (Figure 13) — approximately 40%
showed surface corrosion, while the remaining 60% retained a metallic surface. Critically, all
recovered aluminum foil was corroded, while most beverage cans remained metallic. This is
attributed to the protective coatings present on cans, which resist oxidation, versus the lack of
any protective coating on foil, leaving it vulnerable to the pH shifts, moisture, and chloride
exposure that disrupt aluminum's passive oxide layer under landfill conditions.

Aluminum Corrosion Behavior: Barrel Simulation and SEM-EDS Evidence
The six-month barrel simulation reproduced the same pattern observed in the field-excavated
waste: aluminum cans largely remained metallic with only minor surface contamination, while
approximately 50% of the aluminum foil placed in the barrels developed visible corrosion and
pitting (Figure 14). SEM-EDS analysis of foil samples before and after exposure confirmed this
transformation of the surface chemistry. Metallic (pre-exposure) foil showed a smooth,
homogeneous surface dominated by aluminum (95.4 wt.%), with only minor carbon (3.7 wt.%)
and oxygen (0.8 wt.%). Corroded (post-exposure) foil showed a rough, pitted surface with
aluminum content reduced to 39.7 wt.%, accompanied by substantially elevated oxygen (29.7
wt.%) and carbon (28.5 wt.%) and a trace of calcium (2.2 wt.%) (Table 10) — direct evidence of
oxide formation and adsorption of organic residue from the surrounding waste.

Implications for Landfill Mining and Resource Recovery
Taken together, the field excavation and barrel simulation results carry three direct implications
for aluminum recovery through landfill mining.
 1. First, recovery efforts should prioritize coated aluminum products, particularly beverage
 cans, which retain metallic integrity over decades of burial, rather than treating landfilled
 aluminum as a uniform target; uncoated foil is reliably corroded and contributes little
 recoverable value.
 2. Second, while hand-sorting the coarse fraction, plastic emerged as a disproportionate
 handling problem: it accounted for only 7.28 wt.% of dried mass, but its low density
 meant it occupied a much larger share of the material by volume, and bulky, lightweight
 plastic significantly complicated sorting. The same contamination would be expected to
 reduce the efficiency of the automated separation equipment — magnetic, eddy-current,
 and optical sorters — needed for metal recovery at full scale, directly motivating the
 pretreatment separator described below.
 3. Third, given the low overall aluminum concentration observed at MCSWMA (0.43
 wt.%), aluminum recovery alone is unlikely to justify excavation on its own, but can
 meaningfully improve project economics when combined with ferrous metal recovery,
 refuse-derived fuel production from the combustible fraction (plastics, wood, and textiles
 together account for over 17 wt.% of the coarse fraction, with an estimated calorific value
 of 12–14 MJ/kg), and reuse of inert stone, glass, and ceramic fractions as secondary
 aggregate or daily cover.
29

Regional policy also plays a role: Michigan's bottle-deposit system likely reduced the volume of
aluminum entering this landfill in the first place, a factor that should be accounted for when
estimating recoverable aluminum inventories at other sites. This same excavation program also
supplied the material used for the archaeological can-dating exercise described under Task 1, in
which printed can graphics were used to help confirm the landfill's disposal history.

Aerodynamic Separator: A Pretreatment Response to Lightweight Plastic
Contamination
To address the interference of lightweight materials such as plastics during the material-recovery
separation stage, a custom horizontal aerodynamic separator was designed, built, and tested as a
low-cost pretreatment stage that removes the lightweight fraction from excavated waste before it
reaches this downstream equipment, so that subsequent separation of metal-bearing material is
more effectively. The separator works by exploiting the fact that a particle's terminal settling
velocity governs how far it is displaced by a horizontal air stream: dense metal-bearing particles
fall almost vertically near the feed point, while lightweight plastic and fibrous particles are
carried further downstream and captured in separate collection chambers (Figure 15). The final
unit is an 8-foot open-return wind tunnel with a plywood-on-lumber frame, a dual box-fan air
supply, a cardboard-tube laminarizer to condition the airflow, and a removable six-chamber
collection trough beneath the tunnel.

 Figure 15. Front view of the horizontal aerodynamic separator constructed as a pretreatment
 step for excavated landfill waste.

30

Because a shredder capable of downsizing the excavated landfill waste to feed the separator was
not available at the time of the test run, the separator's underlying capability and optimum
operating conditions were first established using shredded lithium-ion battery waste (copper,
aluminum, and plastic fragments) as a controlled calibration feed, in which recovery and purity
could be quantified. This calibration program, run across three particle sizes and three air
velocities, confirmed that the separator reliably recovers dense metal-bearing material (up to
99.3% recovery, 100.0% purity) while displacing the lightweight fraction, and identified an air
velocity of 1.75 m/s and a feed size below roughly 0.64 cm as the optimum operating condition
(Figure 16). A supplementary wet-feed test also showed that moisture is the most detrimental
variable to performance, cutting lightweight-fraction recovery from 94.0% to 44.1% at 25 wt.%
moisture — establishing pre-drying as a requirement before most excavated waste is processed.

With operating conditions established, the separator was applied to excavated MCSWMA waste.
The fine fraction (<7 mm) was used as the test feed for this demonstration, since the separator's
feed inlet could not accommodate the larger, more irregular material making up the coarse
fraction; incorporating a size-reduction step to allow the coarse fraction to be tested directly is a
priority for future work. Processed in a single pass at 1.95 m/s, the six collection chambers
showed a clear, consistent density-based gradient (Figure 17), in which the chamber nearest the
feed point was dominated by dense, compact material — gravel, stone, glass, and metal-bearing
fragments — while the distant chambers were progressively dominated by lightweight fibrous
debris and degraded plastic film. This confirms that the separator's underlying separation
mechanism functions on real, heterogeneous MCSWMA waste and not only on the controlled
battery-waste calibration feed, supporting its intended role: stripping lightweight plastic
31

contamination from excavated material — whether fine or coarse — before it reaches the
downstream equipment used to recover aluminum and other metals.

Integration into the Landfill Mining Separation Flowsheet
Positioned within the broader landfill mining flowsheet, the aerodynamic separator is intended to
sit downstream of excavation, drying, and coarse screening — the same steps used to generate
the characterization data reported above — and upstream of the magnetic separation, eddy-
current separation, and optical sorting equipment used for final aluminum and metal recovery.
By removing the bulk of the lightweight plastic contamination in a single, chemical-free pass, it
reduces the volume and contamination load presented to downstream recovery equipment,
addressing directly the coarse-fraction plastic problem identified during hand-sorting. This role is
reflected in the revised process pathway evaluated under Task 2, which incorporates
aerodynamic separation ahead of the techno-economic and life cycle assessment comparisons
reported there; the 62% reduction in greenhouse gas emissions found for the revised pathway is
attributable in part to the more efficient use of downstream separation capacity that this
pretreatment step provides.

  Microstructure morphology in a recycled cast aluminum alloy with addition of Ce & Ni

Abstract
Recycled aluminum alloys typically have low strength and poor elongation which excludes them
from use in structural applications. This study aims to assess the effectiveness of Ni and Ce to
increase yield strength, elongation, and fracture toughness. Previous work has looked at squeeze-
casting of higher-purity Al with Ni additions or cast aluminum parts with Ce but with limited
heat treatment trials. A focus of this study is to analyze both mechanical and microstructural
properties of several combinations of Ni and Ce additions in 319 aluminum to improve
permanent mold casting properties from secondary Al scrap with high Fe content. Several Al
alloys, each with varying levels of Ni and Ce, were cast into A4 ASTM B-108 permanent mold
tensile bars and were aged with a T6 heat treatment. SEM microscopy analysis with EDS was
used to determine phase content and fraction, and this data was correlated to mechanical
properties. Analysis of data from these compositions suggests Ni and Ce influence phase
morphology and phase quantity, reducing embrittling Al-Fe phases, but casting quality issues,
such as shrinkage porosity, must be addressed to fully correlate results to mechanical properties.

 for Automotive Use

Abstract
The objective of this work is to develop predictive simulations of the aluminum extrusion
process to improve understanding and performance. Initially, the extrusion behavior of 6005-29,
an 6005A aluminum alloy, was modeled using physics-based material models developed at
extrusion temperatures and strain rates. Previous work has not utilized material data cards for
this specific extrusion alloy chemical composition. These cards were developed utilizing
calculated thermophysical parameters with experimentally calibrated constitutive modeling to
create comprehensive material files for extrusion simulation. Material property data was obtained
through Thermo-Calc calculation of density and thermal properties, and high temperature, high
strain rate uniaxial Gleeble compression testing to parameterize a Sine Hyperbolic Inverse
constitutive model.

This model was then implemented in Altair Inspire Extrude to simulate ram force, exit
temperature, and exit velocity of 6005-29 over a range of billet temperatures and ram velocities.

resulting model developed for 6005-29 reflected higher accuracy than the generic 6005 model
present in the Inspire Extrude database. This extrusion model will support accelerated production
timelines for new extrusion parts and die designs while also reducing the associated risk of
process failure. After establishing an extrusion model for 6005-29 the effect of homogenization
on extrudability was assessed.

A dual step homogenization process was proposed as a way to reduce the amount of solute in
solution by forming additional dispersoid and precipitate phases. The overall flow stress of the
material would decrease and allow for lower extrusion pressure as solid solution strengthening is
the dominant strengthening mechanism in 6xxx series aluminum at extrusion temperatures. The
hypothesis of this report is that if 6005-29 undergoes a second homogenization step at 470°C,
then the material flow stress will decrease because the Mg and Si in solution decrease due to
precipitate formation. Billets of the same composition underwent an additional homogenization
treatment through induction followed by quenching. The homogenization treated billet material
was tested using high temperature, high strain rate uniaxial Gleeble compression testing (Figure

Recommendations
 5. Task 1: Policy and Community Impacts
 a. Demand for refuse-derived fuel contributed to early LFM experiments and
 policymaking in the 1990s, and in the future critical minerals policies could open
 a new “policy window” for LFM policies designed to incentivize LFM for
 aluminum recovery.
 b. Out of 123 open and closed landfills in the region, 45 were located in flood zones,
 with 91 located within 0.5 km of flood zones. LFM for stormwater detention can
 mitigate flood impacts, while also removing waste that may be exposed in flood
 events. Future work could leverage nationwide data sets for landfills and flood
 risk to identify candidate states for LFM.
 c. Regulatory policy is not a significant barrier to LFM, but policies could be better
 designed and more widely adopted to encourage LFM, particularly LFM with
 resource recovery.
 6. Task 2: TEA and LCA
 a. Metal concentration, post-mining land use, and equipment selection (diesel versus
 electric) are the most significant factors affecting project viability.
 b. Landfill mining can be economically and environmentally beneficial when
 combined with favorable landfill compositions and high-value post-mining land
 uses.
 c. All scenarios produce aluminum lower than the 14.5 kg CO2/ kg Al for primary
 aluminum, but they are higher than the 0.28 kg CO2/ kg Al from current scrap
 aluminum sources.
 7. Task 3: Separation
 a. Of the recovered aluminum, predominantly beverage cans and foil, all recovered
 aluminum foil was corroded, while most beverage cans remained metallic due to
 protective coatings present on cans. Recovery is highly dependent on aluminum
 form and corrosion coating.
 b. An aerodynamic separator should be positioned downstream of excavation,
 drying, and coarse screening and upstream of the magnetic separation, eddy-
 current separation, and optical sorting equipment used for final aluminum and
 metal recovery. Removing lightweight plastic contamination in a single,
 chemical-free pass reduces the volume and contamination load presented to
 downstream recovery equipment, addressing directly the coarse-fraction plastic
 problem identified during hand-sorting.
 8. Task 4: Avoiding downcycling of recovered aluminum
 a. Computational tools were developed to design an alloy with a challenging
 composition of high Mg and high Fe, and it performed similar to a baseline 319,
 but more work is needed.
 9. There is real value in the metallic contents of landfills, and there are economic factors
 that can support landfill mining. However, having quantified this process, it is clear that
 separating valuable metals from the waste stream before burying them is a much more
 efficient strategy, and this should be pursued to the greatest extent possible.
