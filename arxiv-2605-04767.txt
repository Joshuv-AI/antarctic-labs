Scheduling Entanglement Flows in Multi-channel Quantum Networks
GONGYU NI 1, LESTER HO 1

Abstract
This paper addresses resource allocation for entanglement distribution in multi-channel quantum networks. A system model is proposed that integrates a multi-channel quantum network architecture with heterogeneous link characteristics and user-centric entanglement request handling, including queuing and retry mechanisms. Classical allocation methods for assigning channels and quantum processors to generate entanglement between end nodes are implemented, including the Dynamic Efficient algorithm, Static Efficient algorithm, and the Success Enhancement algorithm. In addition, a Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)–based allocation approach driven by a reward function is proposed. These methods are evaluated through multi-slot simulations using metrics such as request delay, total number of successful entanglement requests, network capacity utilization, and the entanglement request handling rate. The results show that Dynamic Efficient achieves the lowest delay, while Success Enhancement improves the number of successful requests through multipath allocation. The PPO-based method provides the best overall balance by improving capacity utilization and achieving both low delay and a high number of successful entanglement requests.

I
Introduction

Quantum networks are an emerging communication technology designed to transmit quantum information between interconnected nodes. These nodes support a variety of applications, including quantum key distribution, distributed quantum computing, and quantum sensing. The fundamental building block of a quantum network is the generation of entanglement between nodes, enabling quantum correlations that facilitate communication.

Although entanglement has been demonstrated over a spatial separation of
1.3
kilometres
[
7
]
, long-distance quantum networking is fundamentally limited by exponential photon loss in optical fibres
[
15
]
. Consequently, quantum repeater architectures employing multiplexed architecture have been proposed to enhance entanglement distribution rates
[
9
,
11
,
6
]
.

The capacity of multi-channel quantum networks is highly dependent on the available quantum memory resources that are used as buffers
[
21
]
, making the optimisation of the use of these limited quantum resources important. The use of quantum switches to optimize the entanglement swapping process has been shown in
[
6
]
to reduce latency and improve entanglement success rates. However, from a user-centric perspective, where entanglement requests originate from end users, the design of efficient routing protocols and entanglement flow scheduling strategies that support concurrent entanglement generation across multiple source–destination pairs remains an open challenge.

In this paper, a centralized scheduling framework is proposed to jointly support multi-channel communication (i.e., neighbouring nodes sharing entangled pairs) and multi-request concurrency (i.e., the simultaneous servicing of multiple user demands). The central scheduler allocates quantum memories and communication channels and determines optimal paths for a given batch of entanglement requests.

This work investigates the resource allocation for entanglement distribution in quantum networks under varying conditions, including network size, topology, photon loss rates, and link distances. An entanglement request retry mechanism is also incorporated and analysed. Four allocation methods are proposed: Dynamic Efficient and Static Efficient, designed to reduce request delay; Success Enhancement, which aims to improve the success rate; and a Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO)-based reinforcement learning method, which leverages a reward-driven learning framework to derive effective strategies for handling queued requests across varied network topologies.

The proposed methods are evaluated through multi-slot simulations against benchmark approaches, namely Dynamic FIFO and Static FIFO. Performance is evaluated using metrics including request delay, total number of successful requests, capacity utilization, and per-slot handling rate.

II
Related Work

Due to the probabilistic nature of quantum operations and practical resource constraints such as finite quantum processors, quantum memory, and communication channels, routing and resource allocation play a critical role in quantum networks.

Regarding the routing problem, under restrictions imposed by specific network topologies, Schoute et al.
[
16
]
proposed a framework for studying routing in quantum networks. However, their analysis is limited to ring and spherical topologies. Pant et al.
[
13
]
proposed routing solutions for entanglement distribution in grid networks. In addition, Chakraborty et al.
[
3
]
studied the performance of routing algorithms over ring, grid, and recursively generated network topologies.

Beyond routing, the scheduling of newly arrived and pending requests in each time slot are also important. Cicconetti et al.
[
4
]
proposed a heuristic-based scheduling framework designed to minimize delay while optimizing entanglement generation rate and fidelity. Le and Nguyen
[
8
]
focused on routing requests in resource-constrained quantum networks by introducing the Deep Quantum Routing Agent (DQRA), a deep reinforcement learning model that enhances entanglement routing paths to maximize request handling capability. Ni et al.
[
12
]
applied a Deep Q-Network (DQN)-based scheduling scheme to balance the conflicting objectives of minimizing delay and maximizing fairness among entanglement requests.

In multi-channel settings, Vardoyan et al.
[
22
]
formulated a mixed-integer quadratically constrained program to optimize bipartite entanglement distribution when multiple entangled pairs can be established between a single source–destination pair over multiple available paths. To address the scheduling of concurrent entanglement requests across different source–destination pairs, Chakraborty et al.
[
2
]
proposed a linear programming (LP) formulation to maximize the achievable aggregate entanglement distribution rate under end-to-end fidelity constraints. Furthermore, Zeng et al.
[
24
]
modelled multi-user entanglement throughput optimization as a two-stage integer programming problem, aiming to maximize the total expected throughput across all user pairs.

Given the high computational complexity of integer programming, mixed-integer programming, and linear programming approaches, heuristic algorithms have relative computation cost. Shi et al.
[
18
]
proposed a routing algorithm that improves long-distance entanglement success by dynamically selecting candidate paths based on local link-state recovery, under the assumption that each node has knowledge of the link states of its neighbours. Li et al.
[
10
]
, proposed a propagatory update method scheduling algorithm that applies proportional sharing and progressive filling for capacity allocation in a lattice and homogeneous quantum networks.

III
Contributions

In this paper, entanglement request scheduling in multi-channel quantum networks is studied from a user-centric perspective. Unlike previous studies in which fixed topologies or homogeneous link conditions are often assumed, randomly generated network topologies with heterogeneous photon loss rates across quantum channels are considered.

Entanglement request scheduling in multi-channel quantum networks is studied from a user-centric perspective, considering randomly generated topologies with heterogeneous photon loss rates. A multi-slot simulation framework is developed to model request generation, queuing, retries, and execution under limited quantum memory and channel resources. The concurrent request scheduling problem is formulated, and several allocation strategies are proposed and compared, including Dynamic Efficient, Static Efficient, Success Enhancement, and a PPO-based reinforcement learning method. Results show that Dynamic Efficient reduces request delay, Success Enhancement increases successful requests, and the PPO-based method achieves a balanced performance with both low delay and a high number of successful requests through improved capacity utilization.

IV
System Model

The integrated system model used in this paper incorporates a quantum model that captures depolarizing and dephasing error, as well as photon loss during propagation. It also includes a network model that characterizes request arrivals, pending, and retry mechanisms within a multi-slot simulation framework.

IV-A

Quantum model

In the quantum model, Bell pairs
|\Phi\rangle
(Eq. (
1
)) are used to build entanglement between two nodes.

|\Phi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|0\rangle|0\rangle+|1\rangle|1\rangle\right)

(1)

In a directly connected two-node system, Bell pairs are distributed between the two nodes.
When intermediate nodes (repeaters) exist between the end nodes, quantum operations such as entanglement swapping and correction are employed to establish end-to-end entanglement
[
19
]
. This process involves three main steps: (1) the intermediate node establishes entanglement with adjacent neighbours, (2) it performs a Bell-state measurement to swap entanglement, and (3) the measurement outcomes are transmitted via classical channels to the end nodes, which apply appropriate
X
and
Z
corrections to their local qubits. To simplify the multi-node chain entanglement, repeaters that are within the path of two end nodes, are treated equivalently to end nodes, forming a network composed of nodes and quantum channels.

To evaluate the success of distributing entanglement, fidelity
F
is used to measure the difference between the received density matrix
\rho
and the expected state
|\psi\rangle

[
20
]
.

F(|\psi\rangle,\rho)=\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle

(2)

To model photon loss during propagation, the photon loss probability
p_{\text{loss}}
over a channel is computed using Eq. (
3
), where
d
denotes the physical length of the link between two connected nodes. The fibre attenuation
r_{\text{loss}}
, representing classical optical loss, is randomly sampled from a predefined range (
0.15
to
0.35

[
14
]
. This sampling is used during quantum network topology generation to model heterogeneous link characteristics.

p_{\text{loss}}=1-10^{-\frac{d\cdot r_{\text{loss}}}{10}}

(3)

The probability of dephasing errors

occurring in quantum memories is modelled as

p_{\text{dephase}}=1-e^{-t_{\text{dephase}}\cdot r_{\text{dephase}}},

(4)

where
t_{\text{dephase}}
denotes the storage duration of a qubit in quantum memory, and
r_{\text{dephase}}
represents the corresponding dephasing rate.

Similarly, depolarizing errors
p_{\text{depol}}
during quantum operations are modelled as

p_{\text{depol}}=1-e^{-t_{\text{depol}}\cdot r_{\text{depol}}},

(5)

where
t_{\text{depol}}
denotes the duration of the quantum operation, and
r_{\text{depol}}

IV-B

Network Model

In the network model, as illustrated in Figure
1
, connectivity between two nodes is determined by the presence of a physical link (i.e. an existing optical fibre) and the requirement that each node is equipped with at least two quantum memories for quantum state storage. This type of node has also been discussed in
[
9
]
for connecting neighbouring nodes. Moreover, the model considers multiple entanglement requests within each time slot, encompassing both newly arrived requests and those pending from the previous time slot.

When generating arriving requests, the source and destination nodes are chosen randomly. The number of arrivals
N_{a}
follows a truncated Poisson distribution over the interval
[a,b]
, as given in Eq. (
6
), where
a
and
b
denote the minimum and maximum number of requests per time slot, respectively.

N_{a}\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)\big|_{[a,b]}

(6)

It is assumed that only one group of requests is executed in parallel per time slot, referred to as the executed group. Requests not selected for execution are placed in a pending group due to the unavailability of paths for establishing entanglement.

Upon executing the requests in the executed group, each request is classified as either successful or failed. Successful requests are defined as those that meet or exceed the fidelity threshold and are completed within a timeslot. Their delay, success status, and resulting fidelity are recorded. Failed requests are moved to the pending group and retried in subsequent time slots if they have not reached the maximum number of retries; otherwise, they are permanently aborted.

In summary, all requests are placed in a queue to be processed by the network scheduler. At each time slot, the scheduler selects and executes requests, manages the pending queue, and discards requests that exceed the maximum allowed number of retries.

V
Problem formulation

Since physical connections, node positions, and quantum memories are fixed and replenished at the start of each time slot, once a resource is occupied by an entanglement request, the corresponding link becomes unavailable for further use within the same slot. As a result, only entanglement requests with non-conflicting resource constraints can be executed in parallel during a given time slot.

V-A

Path cost

Consider a quantum network
G=(N,E)
, where
N
denotes the set of nodes and
E
denotes the set of edges. Each edge
(u,v)\in E
, connecting nodes
u
and
v
, is associated with a link cost
c_{uv}
, which depends on the physical distance
d_{uv}
between the nodes and the photon loss rate
r_{\text{loss}}
.

For a path
p=(v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
connecting a source node
v_{0}
to a destination node
v_{k}
, the intermediate nodes are regarded as repeaters. The costs of the edges between consecutive nodes
v_{j}
and
v_{j+1}
are defined as

c_{v_{j}v_{j+1}}=r_{\text{loss}}\,d_{v_{j}v_{j+1}}+\sigma\,d_{v_{j}v_{j+1}},

(7)

where
\sigma
is a weighting coefficient that captures the impact of physical distance on the link cost through increased entanglement distribution time, and the term
r_{\text{loss}}\,d_{v_{j}v_{j+1}}
represents the accumulated photon loss over the corresponding distance.

The total cost of the path
p
is then given by

C_{p}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}c_{v_{j}v_{j+1}}.

(8)

Due to the probabilistic nature of the entanglement swapping in quantum memories and the presence of depolarizing and dephasing noise in both memories and quantum operations (Section
IV-A
), the quality of the entanglement degrades along a path. These imperfections increase the entanglement distribution time with physical distance and reduce the success probability. Consequently, paths with higher total cost generally exhibit worse performance compared to paths with lower costs..

V-B

Scheduling Parallelized Disjoint Paths

In each time slot, a set of requests
n_{r}
is queued for processing, including newly arrived requests and those pending from previous time slots.

Given resource constraints, a trade-off arises between allocating more resources to enable multiple paths for a single request and maximizing the number of served requests by assigning only one path per request. All selected paths must be mutually disjoint as we assume that once a path (including its required channels and quantum memories) is allocated, its resources cannot be shared with other paths within the same time slot.

To evaluate the performance of different allocation schedulers, we adopt the following metrics: delay time in successful requests, total number of successfully served entanglement requests over the entire simulation, capacity utilization (measuring link resource usage), and handling rate (reflecting request processing efficiency).

t_{d}=t_{f}-t_{g}

The capacity utilization rate

VII
Resource allocation methods

In this section, four different allocation methods that aims to achieve this are described: Dynamic Efficient, Static Efficient, Success Enhancement and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

VII-A

Dynamic Efficient Allocation

The goal of the Dynamic Efficient allocation method (Algorithm
2
) is to accommodate as many entanglement requests as possible within each time slot, aiming to handle most of the requests.

The steps involved are as follows.
First, the lowest-cost path that requires the lowest quantum memories is assigned for a given request, in comparison with all other requests. This strategy minimizes the impact on subsequent requests, as a low-cost path is typically shorter and has a higher probability of success. Next, the links of the selected path are removed from the graph, resulting in a sub-graph. Based on this sub-graph, the steps above are repeated for the next requests. This process continues until all requests are accommodated or no additional requests can be added due to resource limitations.

VII-B

Static Efficient Allocation

For the Static Efficient Allocation method, the network graph and the corresponding paths for the requests are not updated. This method achieves minimal computational complexity by first computing the lowest-cost path for each request and sorting all requests in ascending order of path cost. Requests are then combined sequentially, starting with the request with the minimum-cost path. When the next candidate path cannot be accommodated together with the already selected paths due to resource constraints, the process terminates. The resulting set of compatible requests is scheduled to be executed in parallel within the current time slot.

Compared with the Dynamic Efficient allocation shown in Figure
2
, the Static Efficient allocation does not update paths based on the latest sub-graph. Although the Dynamic Efficient allocation can process more entanglement requests in parallel, it may assign higher-cost paths to requests near the end of the queue. In contrast, the Static Efficient allocation guarantees that each request is served on its optimal path, at the cost of potential delays when requests are deferred to subsequent time slots.

VII-C

Success Enhancement Allocation

In the Success Enhancement allocation method (Algorithm
3
), requests are classified into three categories: good, medium-worst and worst, based on their lowest path cost, as computed in Eq. (
8
). Requests with a minimum path cost below the threshold
\tau_{g}
are classified as good, those with a cost exceeding the threshold
\tau_{w}
are classified as worst, and the remaining requests are categorized as medium-worst.

During each time slot, resource allocation prioritizes the medium-worst requests by executing them first. For these requests, multiple paths are allocated when their costs are similar, that is, when their cost is within a similarity threshold
\tau_{s}
relative to the lowest-cost path. This multipath strategy increases the probability of successful entanglement establishment: if one path fails, alternative paths may succeed, and once any path successfully establishes entanglement, the corresponding end nodes are entangled.

In contrast, good requests are generally easier to fulfil, while worst requests typically require more time and exhibit a higher probability of failure. Therefore, only a single path is allocated to these two categories. This approach conserves resources for good requests while avoiding excessive resource consumption and time wastage for worst requests.

VII-D

Proximal Policy Optimization allocation

The Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) model is a reinforcement learning approach
[
17
]
. In this paper, we adapt PPO model to our resource allocation framework, as illustrated in Fig.
3
.

In the adapted PPO model, the concatenated input state is fed into the actor network. At each step
t
, the actor selects an action
a_{t}
representing a path, iterating until all paths are chosen or no further paths are compatible with previously selected ones, forming a set of parallelized paths. Executing these paths yields rewards, and the transition
(s_{t},a_{t},r_{t},s_{t+1})
is stored in the rollout buffer, where
s_{t}
and
s_{t+1}
are the states before and after the action, and
r_{t}
is the path’s reward, calculated backward from the final reward of the parallelized path group. The critic estimates the advantage to guide the actor in optimizing its policy. Both the Actor and Critic compute losses that are fed into a common loss function, which is used to update and improve the Actor’s policy. To stabilize training, the Actor network is updated every
1024
steps with a batch size of
64
over
3
iterations, using a cosine scheduler to decay the learning rate from
1\times 10^{-4}
to
1\times 10^{-7}
.

2
Action

The model is trained to sequentially select paths. At each step

, the PPO agent selects an action
a_{t}
, corresponding to a path, according to the policy
\pi(a\mid s_{t})
, where
s_{t}

Once a path is selected, it is marked as unavailable. The agent continues this process until either all paths have been selected or no additional feasible paths can be accommodated. The final output is a set of parallelized paths.

VII-D
3
Reward

The reward
r
for each parallel path group is designed to jointly encourage
(i) efficient utilization of network resources and
(ii) a high success rate of selected entanglement requests, while penalizing request failures.
It is defined as

r=\alpha R_{l}+\beta R_{s}-\gamma N_{f}.

(18)

The link-level efficiency term is defined as

R_{l}=\frac{l_{s}}{l_{u}},

(19)

where
l_{u}
denotes the number of physical links utilized in the current time slot, and
l_{s}
represents the subset of those links that successfully contribute to entanglement establishment. If multiple paths corresponding to a single request succeed, only the links associated with the path having the shortest execution time are counted in
l_{s}
. This ratio quantifies the efficiency with which allocated links lead to successful entanglement establishment between end nodes.

The request-level success ratio is defined as

R_{s}=\frac{N_{s}}{\min(N_{r},N_{m})},

(20)

where
N_{s}
is the number of successfully completed requests, each having at least one successful entanglement path.
N_{r}
represents the total number of requests in the current time slot, including newly arrived and pending requests from previous time slots, while
N_{m}
is the maximum allowable number of requests, introduced to stabilize the learning process and mitigate reward inflation under heavy traffic conditions.

Furthermore,
N_{f}
denotes the number of failed requests, introducing a penalty term that discourages inefficient or unstable routing decisions.

The coefficients
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\gamma
control the relative importance of each component and should be selected based on the training process as well as network conditions and topology. Typically,
\beta>\alpha

VII-E

Benchmark

To serve as benchmarks for the four resource allocation methods described above, two allocation methods are used, which are based on the First-In-First-Out (FIFO) strategy.

The FIFO allocation strategy assigns entanglement resources strictly based on the order of request arrival, without prioritization to optimize objectives such as maximizing the number of accommodated requests or improving success rates. Requests that cannot be served within the current time slot, due to insufficient physical channels or quantum memories, are deferred and granted priority over newly arrived requests in the subsequent time slot.

VII-E
1
Dynamic FIFO Allocation

In the Dynamic FIFO allocation, similar to the Dynamic Efficient approach, the lowest cost path for subsequent requests is updated dynamically after each request is served. This enables later requests to better accommodate previously selected requests.

VII-E
2
Static FIFO Allocation

In contrast, the Static FIFO allocation method assigns each request a fixed path corresponding to the lowest cost based on the initial network topology. Consequently, subsequent requests cannot exploit dynamic adjustments to alternative paths, but each request is guaranteed its lowest cost path, similar to the Static Efficient method.

VIII-A

Simulation settings

To investigate their performance in different types of network topologies, two classes of random graphs were generated. The first is the Watts-Strogatz graph
G(N,K,p)

[
23
]
. Each graph is initialized as a regular ring lattice with
N
nodes and
K
edges per node, after which each edge is independently rewired with probability
p
. This construction yields networks with high clustering. A higher rewiring probability results in shorter path lengths and shortcuts connecting distant nodes.

The second is a random geometric graph
G(N,r_{e})
, in which connectivity is constrained by physical distance. In this topology,
N
nodes are distributed uniformly at random within a bounded region, and an undirected edge is established between any pair of nodes separated by a Euclidean distance that does not exceed
r_{e}
. The radius
r_{e}
in unit square controls the network density: larger values of
r_{e}
increases the average number of links per node and reduce path lengths, while smaller values can produce sparse or disconnected networks.

The primary difference between these two topologies lies in node distribution. In the Watts-Strogatz graph, nodes are initially arranged in a ring, forming a single clustered structure. In contrast, in the random geometric graph, nodes are randomly distributed within a unit square, forming multiple local clusters determined by the radius
r_{e}
. As
r_{e}
increases, the graph becomes more connected; however, it does not form a single cluster as in the Watts-Strogatz graph.

To ensure comparable hop distances, we preserve the node connection within the generated topology while assigning hop distances randomly from the set
\{5,6,7,8,9,10\}
km. Moreover, the fibre attenuation rate in Eq. (
3

I
. Once assigned, all hops characteristics remain fixed.

To execute a path for an entanglement request, path information, including distance, number of nodes, and photon loss rate, was provided to the NetSquid quantum simulator
[
5

. The simulator provided execution time, fidelity, and success status of each path. A fidelity threshold of
0.5
was used to classify the entanglement as successful, as states below this threshold are not suitable for purification
[
1

. Each allocation method was evaluated over
1000

80{,}000
ns per slot. This duration was chosen to ensure that most paths have sufficient time to complete entanglement generation between their end nodes, based on the simulation results of the node chain entanglement.

In the Success Enhancement allocation method, we set the path cost thresholds
\tau_{g}=12
and
\tau_{w}=15
to classify requests into good, medium-worst, and worst categories. These threshold values are determined through simulation to identify the path cost region in which multiple paths with similar costs are most likely to succeed.

The Dynamic Efficient and Dynamic FIFO algorithms achieve the lowest mean delay under varying network loads. However, they consistently result in the lowest number of successful requests. 1

For both the Static Efficient and Static FIFO methods, the lowest-cost path is fixed and repeatedly used for all requests. This design increases the likelihood of success, as requests consistently traverse the most reliable path. However, it results in a lower handling rate compared with their dynamic versions, as shown in Fig.

Regarding the number of successful requests, both the Success Enhancement strategy and the PPO-based allocation approach achieve the highest success counts. The reason these two methods complete more entanglement requests is that they provide multiple candidate paths for each request.

The Success Enhancement strategy prioritizes the medium-worst requests over the other requests, which may lead to increased delays for queued requests. In contrast, guided by the reward function, the PPO model jointly optimizes the request handling and success rate, at the expense of higher capacity rate (as shown in Fig.

In addition, as the network size increases, the number of links grows, creating more opportunities for optimization. Consequently, the advantage of the PPO-based allocation strategy becomes more pronounced, achieving both lower mean delay and a higher number of successful requests compared to the Static Efficient strategy.

IX
Conclusion

This paper studied resource allocation for entanglement distribution in multi-channel quantum networks with heterogeneous link qualities, varying topologies, and retry-based request handling. A multi-slot simulation framework was developed to evaluate several scheduling strategies, including Dynamic Efficient, Static Efficient, Success Enhancement, FIFO benchmarks, and a PPO-based reinforcement learning method.

The results show clear trade-offs between delay, success count, capacity utilization, and request handling rate. Dynamic Efficient and Dynamic FIFO achieve low delay by adapting paths to available resources, but may reduce the number of successful requests. Static methods improve path reliability but can increase delay. Success Enhancement increases successful requests through multipath allocation, though at higher resource cost and delay.

The PPO-based method provides the best overall balance, achieving high success counts while maintaining a relatively low delay by learning allocation policies that jointly accounts for link utilization, request success, and failures. Its advantage is more apparent in larger networks with richer path diversity. Although retry mechanisms reduce the benefit of multipath allocation, PPO remains competitive by adapting to available resources and traffic conditions. These results indicate that reinforcement learning is a promising approach for scheduling entanglement requests in constrained multi-channel quantum networks.
