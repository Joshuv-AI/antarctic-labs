Executive Summary
This report describes the deployment of 11 laser sensors to measure the clearance between blades and tower on a 1.5-
MW wind turbine whose rotor was mounted first in upwind and then in downwind configurations. The experimental
recordings are compared to the numerical predictions generated by an aeroservoelastic model of the turbine. Good
agreement is found between the two datasets, although discrepancies up to 30 cm are observed. The sources of this
error are discussed. This methodology is found to be a valuable resource for the validation of the numerical predic-
tions of the flapwise deflections of wind turbine blades. The accurate prediction of these deflections is increasingly
important as wind turbines grow in size and become increasingly flexible.

1 Introduction
The National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) embarked on an experiment during the fall of 2023 and spring of
2024 to measure the loads and acoustic emissions of a modern utility-scale wind turbine (Bortolotti et al. 2025).
The experiment aimed to support the investigations around the feasibility, reliability, performance, and economic
viability of downwind-oriented wind turbines. The experiment consisted of operating a GE 1.5-MW wind turbine
located at the NLR Flatirons Campus in a downwind configuration while monitoring loads, power, and aeroacoustic
emissions, which can then be compared to the upwind operation of the wind turbine. To switch from upwind to
downwind, the nacelle of the turbine has to yaw by 180◦ , and the three blades have to pitch by 180◦ . For the same
wind and the same observer, the rotor keeps spinning clockwise. The rotor, however, spins in the opposite direction
with respect to the nacelle. The pitch rate—-both speed and direction—-remains the same, but the pitch actuators
need to operate between 180◦ and 270◦ . The unique dataset that was generated during the experiment is used to
validate the numerical models describing the dynamic behavior, performance, and aeroacoustic emissions of the
downwind-oriented wind turbine in both the upwind and downwind rotor configurations.
The blades are built with prebend that works to keep the tips away from the tower during normal upwind operation.
However, this prebend brings the blades closer to the tower during downwind operation. As such, it was desired
to quantify blade tip clearance during the experiment. While blade tip clearance could not be measured directly,
lasers were used to measure blade passing distance inboard from the tip and estimate blade tip clearance. A graded
approach was taken whereby blade clearances were validated against numerical modeling using OpenFAST, an
aero-elastic wind turbine simulator from NLR, in the upwind configuration. Then, clearances were monitored as the
envelope was expanded to ensure that blades would not contact the tower. It should be noted that normal operation
in the downwind configuration pulls the blades away from the tower as wind speed increases. However, the blades
can spring back during an emergency stop event. Close comparison to numerical models was necessary to ensure the
blades would not hit the tower in this limiting scenario.
This report explores the methodology employed to monitor blade clearances. It is organized as follows. First, the
system components are described in detail. Then, the installation process is recounted. The real-time processing al-
gorithms and the offline analysis methods are detailed. Lastly, results of validation, upwind clearance, and downwind
clearance are all explored.

2 Methodology
The measurement system consists of eleven sensors spaced evenly around the circumference of the tower just
above tip height, an embedded industrial controller reconfigurable input-output (cRIO) real-time controller made
by National Instruments (NI), and a custom EtherCAT-based NI data acquisition system (DAS) for recording the
long-term data with a fixed data rate of 50 Hz for this test. The laser signals are processed on the cRIO and then
transferred to the DAS through an analog output on the cRIO and an analog input on the DAS. The cRIO samples the
laser data at 1,000 Hz. It takes the minimum distance from the 11 sensors every sample period and stores that value
for a period of time that is long enough to ensure the 50 Hz data acquisition rate will actually pick up the minimum
blade passing distance. This algorithm is described in greater detail in Section 2.5.

2.1 Tip Clearance Considerations
As shown in Figure 1, the blades have two levels of prebend. One starts from the root of the blade and the other is
more distinct and starts about 4 m from the tip of the blade. Both of these prebends work to keep the blades away
from the tower as they deflect during upwind operation. However, during downwind operation, the blades are ro-
tated 180◦ relative to the hub, and the prebend leads to reduced tower clearance. Estimates of prebend location and
magnitude were made using blade drawings and photogrammetry.
Due to their location along the height of the tower, the laser measurements did not measure actual tip clearance,
rather, they measured clearance near the knee right above the tip prebend. The lasers were mounted at an altitude of
45.0 m, which was 3.5 m from the tip of the blade and near the root of the tip prebend. Ideally, the lasers would have
been mounted at the exact root of the tip prebend or even inboard by 0.5 m, but a set of pressure belts were mounted
on the blade and tower during a later phase of the experimental campaign. This final location kept the lasers 1.7 m
below the pressure belts.

 Figure 1. Prebend profile of blades prior to installation on the U.S.
 Department of Energy 1.5-MW turbine. Photo by Lee Jay Fingersh, NLR.

Although the sensors measured blade clearance inboard of the tip, tip clearance is the desired physical quantity of
interest. Tip clearance can be estimated in two ways. One method is to apply a correction factor to the measured

 2

laser clearance to estimate tip clearance. This method was used in the moment during the experimental campaign
to make quick and rough estimates of blade tip clearance. However, accuracy is an issue with this method, as parts
of the correction factor depend on turbine operating conditions. For example, blade deformed slope and shape
depend heavily on blade loading. Instead, to make an accurate estimate of blade tip clearance, one can validate and
tune an aeroelastic model using laser clearance measurements and then use this model to estimate blade clearance
in a variety of operating conditions. This second method was utilized in post-processing to ensure the blade tip
would have adequate clearance throughout the operating conditions expected to be encountered in the downwind
configuration. Validation of the aeroelastic model is detailed in Section 5.
To make a rough, real-time estimate of how much closer the blade tip was to the tower than where the laser measured
during the experimental campaign, the worst-case blade deflection curve from simulation was calculated. As blade
deflection curves could not be derived in real time, the worst-case blade deflection curve from simulation was used
to provide a conservative estimate of the position of the tip of the blade relative to the laser measurement position.
As such, one could use the laser measurements in real time corrected by this factor and have confidence that the
blade clearance was adequate. In this deflected configuration, the difference between the outboard tip clearance and
the measured laser distance was calculated to be 829 mm. This correction factor was used throughout the campaign
as part of the predicted total difference between observed laser distance and actual tip clearance. Other factors are
described in Section 3. Worst-case blade deflection was derived numerically. The turbine model was subjected to
a variety of design load cases (DLC) with the goal of compiling worst case deflections at each case. Note, DLCs
are simulated wind conditions that are designed to produce limiting deflections in a wind turbine during simulation.
They are designed to load the turbine in a unique and specific way for various operating conditions. The most ex-
treme tip deflection was observed and derived from a simulation of DLC 1.4 with an extreme coherent gust with
direction change.

2.5 Real-Time Processing
The laser sensors are sampled at a rate of 1,000 Hz on the cRIO. At this rate, the real-time cRIO system takes ap-
proximately 14 measurements as each blade passes by the sensor when the turbine is running at the rated speed of
18.3 RPM. During every sample period, the system records the minimum distance from the 11 sensors and stores
that value as the blade passage distance. This step allows the system to identify which sensor is actually measuring
the passing blade. Note that directly down-sampling this signal to the 50-Hz rate of the DAS would likely result in
missing the blade passage as the blade spends less than 0.02 seconds in front of the laser sensor. Thus, the minimum
blade distance is artificially held in the output of the cRIO for longer than the blade is actually in front of the laser
sensor. At rated RPM, the blade passage frequency is on the order of 1 Hz. Ten percent of this blade passage fre-
quency was chosen as a good balance of time to hold the output value but also clear the value before the next blade
passage. Holding this value is accomplished using a running minimum on the blade passage distance with a win-
dow of 100 ms. Thus, as each blade passes by the laser sensors, it appears to remain in front of the sensor for 100
ms, longer than it actually took to pass the sensor. This allows the minimum blade distance to be easily identified in
post-processing.

3 Uncertainty
Several sources of uncertainty are present in both the experimental measurements generated by the lasers and in the
predictions generated numerically:

 1. The accuracy of the laser itself. This value was taken from the specification sheet as ±7 mm.
 2. The laser not measuring perpendicular to the rotor plane, causing the path of the laser to be longer than the
 blade clearance distance. For 11 sensors, this error could be up to 134 mm.
 3. The outer diameter of the tower where belts were installed. A discrepancy of 140 mm exists in the outer tower
 circumference between the value specified in the aeroelastic model and the value measured by the rappelling
 team on-site.
 4. The impact of drivetrain flexibility. The drivetrain is currently assumed stiff in OpenFAST in the bending
 direction. This assumption could generate an error in the shaft tilt. The NLR team used an inclinometer to
 measure the actual nacelle tilt, which showed discrepancies compared to the nominal value as high as 0.3◦
 depending on the location of the measurements.
 5. Complex beam behavior. The structural dynamics of the blades were simulated adopting a Euler-Bernoulli
 formulation that neglects the torsional deformations of the blades as well as more complex dynamics caused
 by cross-coupling terms such as bend-twist coupling. The rotor of the U.S. Department of Energy 1.5-MW
 turbine is fairly stiff, but the modeling simplifications are likely to cause modeling errors. The magnitude of
 these errors is unknown.
 6. Airfoil polars. The loads and performance of the rotor were simulated assuming a lifting-line formulation
 to model the rotor aerodynamics. The lifting-line relies on 2D curves of lift, drag, and moment coefficients
 that represent the performance of the airfoils along the span of the blade. The lifting line theory itself has
 some limitations, but more importantly, the airfoils of the U.S. Department of Energy 1.5-MW turbine blade
 were modeled stepwise, which is certainly not realistic. Again, the magnitude of the errors introduced by this
 assumption is unknown.
 7. Controller. The dynamic controller of the wind turbine was provided by GE in a compiled form, and the NLR
 team has no access to it. It is possible that the controller used in the numerical simulations is not identical to
 the controller operating the turbine in the field. Once again, the magnitude of the errors caused by a mismatch
 in the dynamics of the controller is unknown.

4 Analysis Methods
4.1 Experimental Data
The post-processing of the experimental data was performed in Python. First, the .tdms files generated by the in-
dependent DAS of the turbine storing data at 50 Hz were imported. Next, the code simply checked when the clear-
ance signal at 50 Hz coming from the lasers was smaller than 7 m. When that happened, it meant that one of the
lasers was “seeing” the blade. The code then recorded the minimum value of the signal. When the signal again ex-
ceeded 7 m, the code assumed that the blade had moved out of the azimuthal sector where the laser could see it, and
recorded the minimum as the actual data point for that blade passage. Once the signal came back to being smaller
than 7 m, the process started again and a new data point was generated.

4.2 Aeroelastic Model
The GE 1.5-MW turbine is modeled in OpenFAST, the flagship numerical tool released by the National Renewable
Energy Laboratory to simulate the aeroservoelastic behavior of wind turbines. OpenFAST is publicly available at
https://github.com/OpenFAST/openfast. The OpenFAST model of the turbine was created when the turbine was
installed starting from a Flex model shared by the manufacturer. The model of the turbine used in this study was up-
graded to OpenFAST v3.3, which was in turn coupled to the industrial controller provided by GE. The simulation of
the structural dynamics of the blades rely on the Euler-Bernoulli beam model implemented in the module ElastoDyn,
which also simulates the inertia of the nacelle components and the structural dynamics of the tower. The aerody-
namics of the rotor are simulated in the module AeroDyn15. An unsteady airfoil aerodynamic model based on the
Beddoes-Leishman formulation was used together with the continuous implementation of the Oye’s dynamic inflow
model. These models are described in the documentation of OpenFAST together with the appropriate references.

The geometrical inputs available in the OpenFAST model were used to predict the recording of the laser. The follow-
ing geometric inputs were used:
 • Hub radius
 • Blade length

 • Distribution of blade prebend
 • Distribution of tower outer diameters
 • Vertical distance between tower top flange and rotor apex
 • Shaft tilt angle

 • Horizontal distance between tower axis and rotor apex
 • Height of the lasers
 • Absolute thickness of the blade where laser hits

The values of these inputs are proprietary to GE and cannot be shared publicly.

5 Validation Results
The following sections describe three sets of experimental results, namely, the measurement of parked and oper-
ational clearance in upwind, the measurement of parked and operational clearance in downwind, and the case of
an emergency stop. In the first two cases, the experimental recordings are compared to the numerical predictions
generated by OpenFAST.

5.1 Parked and Operational Clearance in Upwind
The first test consisted of placing one of the three blades in run position, that is, pitch angle at 0◦ , right in front of
one of the 11 lasers. The laser recorded 4.53 m. This value could then be compared to the numerical prediction
generated using the virtual laser as described in Section 4.2. The numerical prediction was equal to 4.58 m, showing
strong agreement between the model and the experimental data.

Next, the recordings of the lasers were processed for one day of upwind operations, namely, February 7, 2024. The
recordings are plotted as blue markers in Figure 10, which also reports the numerical predictions from OpenFAST
in terms of mean, standard deviation, and minimum. OpenFAST was run for six turbulent seeds of DLC 1.1, which
corresponds to the normal operation of the turbine under the normal turbulence model. Notably, the numerical
predictions are somewhat shifted upward compared to the experimental recordings, with an offset of approximately
20 cm. A source of mismatch could be in the deformation of the drivetrain, which is assumed vertically rigid in
bending but in reality deforms under the gravity loading of the rotor. Another source of the offset could originate in
some unknown mismatch between the actual geometries of the turbine and the geometry modeled in OpenFAST.

5.2 Parked and Operational Clearance in Downwind
The same process discussed in Section 5.1 was repeated during downwind operations. First, clearance was checked
by placing a blade in run position right in front of one of the lasers. The numerical model predicted a reading of

the park clearance, although the source of the discrepancy is not clear.

6 Conclusion
A robust method to measure blade tip deflections has been demonstrated through a long-duration (>1 year) deploy-
ment of the laser sensors described in this paper. It is important to note that these sensors store blade clearance
measurements in a dataset that is sampled much slower than the interval at which the blade passes in front of a sen-
sor. Agreement is seen between the aeroservoelastic model and the experimental results within ±30 cm. Controlling
the uncertainties of Section 3 with more sensors and advanced modeling could help reduce this gap. Tuning the
aeroservoelastic model as demonstrated in this paper allows for future estimation of blade tip clearances.

Appendix A. Install Procedure
The following document was provided to the rope access team to detail the installation procedure of the sensors.

 Tools and supplies required

 • 50m tape measure (NLR to provide)
 • Adhesive tape measure for circumference of tower (https://www.mcmaster.com/1909A28/) (NLR to provide)
 • Tin snips to cut the adhesive tape measure (Rope Partner to provide)
 • 11 sensor and magnet mount assemblies (NLR provided)
 • 11 cable assemblies (NLR provided)
 • Foil tape to mount cables (NLR provided)
 • Personal tape measure (25’) (Rope Partner to provide)

 Install procedure

 • On the ground, attach every sensor to its corresponding wire and flake the wire into the equipment bags. TAKE
 EXTREME CARE NOT TO CROSS-THREAD THE CONNECTOR – it is very easy to misthread the connector if you’re
 not careful.
 • Locate and mark an elevation on the west side of the tower approximately facing the met tower that is 45.0m
 above that of the foundation
 • In partnership with the NLR team, determine the azimuthal location on the tower that is directly in line with the
 met tower. This will be accomplished using photogrammetry and the network camera installed on the met
 tower. NLR staff will point the camera at the center of the tower at the elevation marked in the previous step.
 They will use pixel counting to determine the exact center of the tower in the image. Rope Partner staff will then
 point at the tower and NLR staff will direct them over cell phone or radio to move their finger left or right until
 they are pointing directly at the center of the tower. This location will be at an azimuthal angle of 280° true.
 • Using the azimuth and elevation determined in the previous two steps as a starting point, install the adhesive
 tape measure around the circumference of the tower with the measurements increasing from left to right.
 NOTE: it will likely be difficult to keep the tape level and from not forming a helix around the tower. Once
 elevation of the start of the tape is established, measure off the nearest weld line to keep the tape level as it
 progresses around the circumference of the tower.
 • Trim the tape to length once it’s wrapped the whole way around the tower.
 • Provide the measurement of the tower circumference as measured using the adhesive tape to NLR staff. NLR will
 use this measurement to calculate the azimuthal locations of the 11 sensors to be installed. Sensors will be
 evenly spaced around the circumference of the tower with one sensor pointing at 292° true. NLR will provide
 the azimuthal measurements along the adhesive tape for the location of these sensors via text or radio
 depending on technician preference the day of.
 • Mount the 11 sensors directly above the adhesive tape at the provided azimuthal locations, connector down.
 The sensors use a magnetic mounting system with an on/off switch for the magnet. Each sensor has a number
 on it which will correspond to a specific location. Wires also are labeled to correspond to specific sensors.
 • Route the cable around the tower to the door location.
 • CAREFULLY LOWER THE CABLES TO THE GROUND TAKING CARE TO PROTECT THE SPLICE IN THE CABLE. THIS
 SPLICE SHOULD NOT BE SHOCK LOADED. Run the cables in four bundles of 3, 3, 3 and 2 cables each down the
 tower. Each bundle of 2 or 3 should be placed under its own run of foil tape.
 • Take pictures of the installation as it progresses. A combination of close-ups of sensor locations and wide angles
 that show cable routing is desired.
 • For each cable that is run downtower, NLR staff will work in conjunction with the Rope Partner to test operation
 of the sensor.
 • NLR staff will route the cables through the door of the tower.
