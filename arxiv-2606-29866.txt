Characterization of the alternating and symmetric groups by the order and conjugacy class sizes
Ilya Gorshkov, Andrey V. Vasil’ev

Abstract
. We prove that an arbitrary finite group G G having the same order and same set of conjugacy class sizes as an alternating or symmetric group S S must be isomorphic to S S . From this and previously known results it follows that the same holds true for every simple group S S . Keywords: finite group, symmetric group, conjugacy class size. MSC: 20B10, 20E45, 20D60.



1.
Introduction

Given a finite group
G
, we set
\operatorname{cs}(G)=\{|x^{G}|:x\in G\}
for the set of conjugacy class sizes of
G
, and
\operatorname{cs}^{*}(G)
for the same collection of parameters but counting their multiplicities. For example,
\operatorname{cs}(A_{5})=\{1,12,15,20\}
but
\operatorname{cs}^{*}(A_{5})=\{\{1,12,12,15,20\}\}
, as there are two conjugacy classes of elements of order
5
in the alternating group
A_{5}
.

It is quite natural to wonder how the structures of these sets reflect on the structure of a group. The recent example can be found in
[
15
]
. There for every special irreducible representation
E
of a Weyl group, Lusztig defined a finite group
\Delta_{E}
satisfying some natural conditions, see also
[
14
]
for details. To establish the correctness of the definition he had to check that the symmetric groups
S_{n}
for
n\leq 5
are uniquely determined by
\operatorname{cs}^{*}(S_{n})
. In fact, as we show below, the same assertion holds true for all positive integers
n
. Moreover, it remains true under much weaker hypothesis.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
S
be a finite alternating or symmetric group. If
G
is a group with
|G|=|S|
and
\operatorname{cs}(G)=\operatorname{cs}(S)
, then
G\simeq S
.

Since the order of a finite group is the sum of the sizes of its conjugacy classes, the following holds.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
S
be a finite alternating or symmetric group. If
G
is a group with
\operatorname{cs}^{*}(G)=\operatorname{cs}^{*}(S)
, then
G\simeq S
.

As the reader can see below, our proof of Theorem
1.1
is relatively short. However it is based on a quite involved technique developed to verify a conjecture attributed to J. G. Thompson, see
[
10
, Problem 12.38]
. The conjecture claims that every finite nonabelian simple group
S
is uniquely determined by the set
\operatorname{cs}(S)
in the class of all finite groups with trivial center.

The Thompson conjecture was proved for all simple groups except the alternating groups
[
8
]
, while in the case of the alternating groups it was done only modulo the binary Goldbach conjecture
[
7
]
. Nevertheless, the main result of
[
7
]
(see also Proposition
3.1
below) guarantees that a centerless finite group
G
with
\operatorname{cs}(G)=\operatorname{cs}(S)
, where
S
is an alternating or symmetric group of degree
n\geq 23
, has a composition factor isomorphic to
A_{m}
, where
m
belongs to the interval
[p,n]
and
p
is the greatest prime not exceeding
n
.

In order to apply the latter assertion in our case, we prove (see Lemma
2.4
) that a group
G
satisfying the equalities
\operatorname{cs}(G)=\operatorname{cs}(S)
and
|G|=|S|
for an alternating or symmetric group
S
of degree
n\geq 4
must have the trivial center. This does not prove Theorem
1.1
yet, but makes the proof sufficiently straight.

The proof of Lemma
2.4
is based on the fact that for every prime divisor
r
of the order of an alternating or symmetric group
S
there is an element
x\in S
such that the
r
-parts of
|x^{S}|
and
|S|
are the same (Lemma
2.3
). The last fact holds true for every other nonabelian simple group
S
as it follows from the description of adjacency in the prime graph
GK(S)
(also called the Gruenberg-Kegel graph) in
[
19
]
. Therefore, the equalities
\operatorname{cs}(G)=\operatorname{cs}(S)
and
|G|=|S|
yield
Z(G)=1
for every nonabelian simple group
S
. Adding the validity of the Thompson conjecture for the nonabelian simple groups distinct from the alternating groups and Theorem
1.1
to these arguments, we obtain the following.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
S
be a finite simple group. If
G
is a group with
\operatorname{cs}(G)=\operatorname{cs}(S)
and
|G|=|S|
, then
G\simeq S
.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
S
be a finite simple group. If
G
is a group with
\operatorname{cs}^{*}(G)=\operatorname{cs}^{*}(S)
, then
G\simeq S
.

2.
Preliminaries

The
cycle structure
of a permutation
x\in S_{n}
is the list of the lengths of its cycles arranged in non-increasing order, i. e., the record

(1)

x=(1^{i_{1}},2^{i_{2}},\ldots,n^{i_{n}})

means that
x
has
i_{k}
cycles of length
k
for
k=1,\ldots,n
, and
\sum_{k=1}^{n}k\,i_{k}=n
. Sometimes for brevity we do not mention missing cycles in the list, e. g., instead of
x=(1^{0},\ldots,(n-1)^{0},n^{1})
we write simply
x=(n^{1})
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
x=(1^{i_{1}},2^{i_{2}},\ldots,n^{i_{n}})\in S_{n}
. Then

C=C_{S_{n}}(x)\simeq\bigotimes_{k=1}^{n}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}\wr S_{i_{k}}),\,\,|C|=\prod_{k=1}^{n}k^{i_{k}}\cdot k!\mbox{ and }|x^{S_{n}}|=n!/|C|,

where
\mathbb{Z}_{k}\wr S_{i_{k}}
is supposed to be the trivial group for each
i_{k}
equal to
0
.

Proof.

See, e.g.,
[
2
, Theorem 1.15]
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
x=(1^{i_{1}},2^{i_{2}},\ldots,n^{i_{n}})\in A_{n}
. Then
C_{A_{n}}(x)=C_{S_{n}}(x)
if and only if
i_{k}=0
for all even
k
and
i_{k}\leq 1
for all odd
k
, where
k=1,\ldots,n
.

Proof.

The proof is clear.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose that
V_{n}\in\{A_{n},S_{n}\}
and
n\geq 4
. Then for every prime divisor
r
of
|V_{n}|
, there is
x\in V_{n}
such that the
r
-parts of
|x^{V_{n}}|
and
|V_{n}|
are the same.

Proof.

Suppose that
n=\sum_{j=0}^{t}2^{j}\,b_{j}
is the binary representation of
n
. Put
x=(1^{a_{1}},2^{a_{2}},\ldots,n^{a_{n}})
, where
a_{k}=b_{j}
, if
k=2^{j},j=0,\ldots,t
, and
a_{k}=0
otherwise. Lemma
2.1
implies that
|C_{S_{n}}(x)|
is a power of
2
, so the
r
-parts of
|x^{S_{n}}|
and
|S_{n}|
are the same for every odd prime
r
.

If
x\in A_{n}
, then
|C_{A_{n}}(x)|
divides
|C_{S_{n}}(x)|
, so it is also a power of
2
. If
x\in S_{n}\setminus A_{n}
but
n\neq\widehat{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{t}2^{j}
, then there is
j\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
such that
b_{j}=1
and
b_{j-1}=0
in the binary representation of
n
. Replacing the cycle
c
of length
2^{j}
in
x
with the product
\widehat{c}
of two cycles of lengths
2^{j-1}
such that
c
and
\widehat{c}
have the same support, we come to the new element
\widehat{x}
. It is clear that
\widehat{x}\in A_{n}
and
|C_{S_{n}}(\widehat{x})|
is a
2
-power, whence the
r
-parts of
|\widehat{x}^{A_{n}}|
and
|A_{n}|
are the same for every odd prime
r
. If
n=\widehat{n}
, then replacing cycles of lengths
2
and
1
in
x
with the cycle of length
3
, we obtain an even permutation
\widehat{x}
such that the
r
-parts of
|\widehat{x}^{A}_{n}|
and
|A_{n}|
coincide for each odd prime
r\neq 3
. In the same situation, to get an element which centralizer is not a multiple of
3
, it suffices to replace the cycles of lengths
4
and
1
in
x
with the cycle of length
5
.

In order to find an element
x
of
V_{n}
such that the
2
-parts of
|x^{V_{n}}|
and
|V_{n}|
are the same, let
t\in\{n-1,n\}
be even. Then
t\geq 4
. Set

x=\begin{cases}(3^{1},(t-3)^{1}),\mbox{ if }\gcd(3,t)=1;\\
(5^{1},(t-5)^{1}),\mbox{ if }3\,|\,t\mbox{ and }\gcd(5,t)=1;\\
(3^{1},5^{1},(t-9)),\mbox{ if }15\,|\,t=n;\\
(3^{1},5^{1},(t-7)),\mbox{ if }15\,|\,t=n-1.\end{cases}

Observe that
x
is an even permutation. Applying Lemma
2.1
, it is easy to check that the centralizer of
x
in
S_{n}
has an odd order, so the
2
-part of
|x^{V_{n}}|
and
|V_{n}|
are the same, and we are done.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
V_{n}\in\{A_{n},S_{n}\}
and
n\geq 4
. If
G
is a group with
|G|=|V_{n}|
and
\operatorname{cs}(G)=\operatorname{cs}(V_{n})
, then
Z(G)=1
.

Proof.

Suppose that
Z(G)\neq 1
. Then there is a prime divisor
r
of
|Z(G)|
. It follows that the
r
-part of every element in
\operatorname{cs}(G)
is strictly less than the
r
-part of
|G|=|V_{n}|
. This contradicts Lemma
2.3
.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
V_{n}\in\{A_{n},S_{n}\}
and
n\geq 4
,
x\in V_{n}
, and
C=C_{V_{n}}(x)
. Suppose that
p
is a prime such that
n/2<p\leq n
and
p
divides
|C|
. The either

(i)

|C|=p|C_{V_{n-p}}(y)|
for some
y\in V_{n-p}=S_{n-p}\cap V_{n}
, or

(ii)

|C|
is a multiple of
|V_{k}|
, where
k\geq p
and
V_{k}=S_{k}\cap V_{n}
.

Proof.

This follows from
[
5
, Lemma 2.7]
.
∎

For the centralizer
C
from Lemma
2.5
, we say that
C
is
of the first type
, if
|C|
as in item (i) of the lemma, and
C
is
of the second type
otherwise. As readily seen, the order of every centralizer of the first type is strictly less than the order of the centralizer of the second type.

Lemma 2.6
.

If
n\geq 11
, then the interval
(4n/5,n]

Proof.

For

this can be easily verified. For

this follows from
[
16
]
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
K
be a normal subgroup of a group
G
and
\overline{G}=G/K
. Let
\overline{x}
be the image of
x\in G
in
\overline{G}
. Then the following hold:

(i)

|x^{K}|
and
|\overline{x}^{\overline{G}}|
divide

(ii)

if

and

are coprime, then
C_{\overline{G}}(\overline{x})=C_{G}(x)K/K
.

Item (i) is well known. Item (ii) is

.

Recall that a group
H
is a section of a group
G
if
H
is a homomorphic image of some subgroup of
G
.

Lemma 2.8
.

[
17
, Proposition 2.7]

Suppose that
A_{m}
is a section of
\operatorname{GL}_{d}(q)
for some
d\geq 1
. Then
d\geq m-2
for
m\geq 9
.

Recall that a finite group (an element of a group) is a
\pi
-group (a
\pi
-element) if its order is a
\pi
-number. A subgroup
H
is called a Hall
\pi
-subgroup of
G
if
|H|
is a
\pi
-number and
|G:H|
is a
\pi^{\prime}
-number. Following
[
12
]
, we say that a finite group
G
is a
D_{\pi}
-group
(or
G
has the property
D_{\pi}
) if
G
contains a Hall
\pi
-subgroup, all Hall
\pi
-subgroups of
G
are conjugate, and every
\pi
-subgroup of
G
is contained in some Hall
\pi
-subgroup of
G
. In this language, the celebrated Hall theorem claims that the solvable groups are
D_{\pi}
-groups
[
11
]
. The following assertion provides a useful criterion for a group to has the
D_{\pi}
-property.

Lemma 2.9
.

[
20
, Theorem 6.6]

Let
\pi
be a set of primes, let
G
be a group and
N
a normal subgroup of
G
. The group
G
is a
D_{\pi}
-group if and only if
N
and
G/N
are
D_{\pi}
-groups.

The following obvious observation on
D_{\pi}
-groups is quite helpful.

Lemma 2.10
.

Suppose that
G
is a
D_{\pi}
-group and
H
is a Hall
\pi
-subgroup of
G
. Then each
\pi
-element of
G
is conjugate to some element of
H
.

Proof of the main result

Let
V_{n}\in\{A_{n},S_{n}\}
, let
G
be a finite group with
\operatorname{cs}(G)=\operatorname{cs}(V_{n})
and
|G|=|V_{n}|
.

Observe that the theorem can be easily verified for
n\leq 4
. So we assume that
n\geq 5
. Since
Z(G)=1
in view of Lemma
2.4
, we may suppose that
n,n+1,n+2
are not primes for
V_{n}=A_{n}
; and

are not primes for
V_{n}=S_{n}
, because Thompson’s conjecture holds true otherwise, see
[
1
]
. Moreover, Thompson conjecture has been verified when

and

in
[
18
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
21
]
,
[
13
]
, respectively. Thus, we may suppose that
n\geq 27
for
V_{n}=A_{n}
and

for
V_{n}=S_{n}
.

Further,
p
is the greatest prime not exceeding

.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
n\geq 23
, then there exists a normal subgroup
K
of
G
such that
A_{m}\leq\overline{G}=G/K\leq S_{m}
, where
m\in[p,n]
.

Proof.

Lemma
2.4
yields
Z(G)=1
. Therefore, the claim follows from the main result of
[
7
]
.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

If
n\geq 11
, then there exists a normal subgroup
K
of
G
such that
A_{m}\leq\overline{G}=G/K\leq S_{m}
, where
m\in[p,n]
.

Proof.

By the above arguments, we need to consider the groups

for

only.

Let

. Suppose that
x
is an element of
G
of order

and

is of the second type, see Lemma
2.5
(ii). Then according to this lemma

divides
|C|
. Since

, there are only two possible numbers

in
\operatorname{cs}(G)\setminus\{1\}
such that

can divide the ratio

,

. These numbers are

, so

is equal to

or

for

and

, respectively; and

possible only for

. It follows that for

there is a Sylow

-subgroup

of

with

of index

. Since

is maximal in

, it intersects

. Then the index

must be coprime to

. Since
\operatorname{cs}(S_{15})
and
\operatorname{cs}(S_{16})
do not have such numbers, we derive a contradiction.

Thus,

is of the first type, see Lemma

(i). In particular, each prime

distinct from

does not divide the order of

. Furthermore, since

, the numbers
|G|/sp
and

are coprime. Suppose that

and one of such

divide the orders of distinct composition factors of

. Then Lemma

implies that

is a

-group for

. Therefore,

has a Hall

-subgroup

which contains the element

, see Lemma

. The subgroup

must be cyclic, which contradicts the fact that

does not divide the order of

. Thus, there is a normal subgroup

of

such that
\overline{G}=G/K
has a nonabelian simple subgroup

whose order is a multiple of all primes from the interval

. In particular,

is nonsolvable. Since the order of

must divide the order of

, using
[
22
]

, where

.

The case

can be considered in the same manner. Here

, and one can take

. Then again there is an element

from the center of a Sylow

of

such that

of

such that

which order is a multiple of all primes from the interval

Proposition 3.3
.

The theorem holds for
V_{n}=S_{n}
and

.

Proof.

Let

be an element of order

. Suppose first that

Let

. Since

divides

. Since

divides
|C|
, we have

]

. If

Lemma 3.5
.

C_{G}(K)\leq K
.

Proof.

Assume the opposite. Then
A_{m}\leq C_{G}(K)K/K\leq S_{m}

. Observe that

are coprime, so Lemma

c=|C_{G}(x)|=|C_{\overline{G}}(\overline{x})||K|=\frac{p\,(m-p)!\,n!}{d\,m!},\mbox{ where }d=|V_{n}|/|A_{n}|.

Denote by

the maximum of the orders of the centralizers of first type from item (i) of Lemma

. Since

, we have
c_{1}<c
. On the other hand,
p\geq 4n/5
in view of Lemma
2.6
. Hence
c<p!
, so
c
is less than the order of any centralizer of the second type from item (ii) of Lemma
2.5
. This is a contradiction, because
|G|/c\in\operatorname{cs}(G)=\operatorname{cs}(V_{n})
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Suppose that
R
is a Sylow
r
-subgroup of
K
for some prime
r
. Then

|R|<n\cdot r^{n/5}.

Proof.

Let
t=n/5
and
s=[\log_{r}n]
. In view of Lemma
2.6
, we have
n-m\leq t
. It follows from Legendre’s formula that

|R|=\left(\frac{n!}{m!}\right)_{r}=r^{\sum_{i=1}^{s}([n/r^{i}]-[m/r^{i}])}\leq r^{s+\sum_{i=1}^{s}[t/r^{i}]}\leq n\cdot r^{\sum_{i=1}^{s}[t/r^{i}]}<n\cdot r^{t/(r-1)}\leq n\cdot r^{t}.

∎

We are ready to finish the proof of the theorem. Lemma
3.5
implies that there is a prime divisor
r
of the order of
|K|
such that
N_{G}(R)/C_{G}(R)
includes a section isomorphic to
A_{m}
, where
R
is a Sylow
r
-subgroup of
K
. Now Lemma
2.8
yields
|R|\geq r^{m-2}
. Since
n\geq 15
, it follows from Lemma
2.6
that
m\geq 4n/5
. Applying Lemma
3.6
, we obtain

n\cdot r^{n/5}>r^{4n/5-2}
, so
n>r^{(3n-10)/5}
. This gives us a contradiction for
n\geq 15
. The theorem is proved.
