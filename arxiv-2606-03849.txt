Second-Best Bilateral Trade is 1 / 2 1/2 Efficient
Zhengyang Liu, Ying Qin, Zeyu Ren, Zihe Wang

Abstract
The landmark Myerson-Satterthwaite Theorem establishes a fundamental impossibility in bilateral trade: no Bayesian incentive-compatible mechanism can simultaneously achieve ex-post efficiency, individual rationality, and strong budget balance. We resolve a long-standing open question regarding the efficiency loss imposed by these constraints. Specifically, we prove that the Bayesian-optimal (second-best) mechanism always captures at least half of the first-best gains from trade ( SB ≥ 1 2 ​ FB \mathrm{SB}\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{FB} ). This result is tight, definitively closing the gap between the previously best-known bounds of 0.317 0.317 and 0.736 0.736 .

Introduction

The design of efficient mechanisms is a foundational challenge at the intersection of economics and computer science. In its most elemental form,
bilateral trade
involves a single seller and a single buyer with private valuations,
b
and
s
, drawn from public prior distributions
F_{B}
and
F_{S}
. The goal is to maximize the
Gains from Trade
(GFT), defined as the expected surplus
b-s
generated whenever a trade occurs.

In an ideal
first-best
setting, trade occurs if and only if
b\geq s
, thereby capturing the full social surplus. However, the landmark Myerson-Satterthwaite Theorem
[
17
]
establishes a celebrated impossibility: no Bayesian incentive-compatible (BIC) mechanism can simultaneously satisfy ex-post efficiency, individual rationality (IR), and strong budget balance (SBB). This “trilemma” implies that any mechanism operating without an external subsidy must inevitably sacrifice some efficiency to maintain strategic integrity and financial self-sufficiency.

Faced with this impossibility, the classical economic approach seeks the
second-best
mechanism, that is, the Bayesian-optimal design that maximizes GFT for a given pair of prior distributions. While mathematically elegant, second-best mechanisms are often notoriously complex and brittle, relying heavily on the exact specification of priors. This fragility raises a fundamental question that defines this line of research:

To what extent can a second-best mechanism approximate the first-best efficiency?

To circumvent the analytical barriers posed by the exact second-best mechanism, the field of algorithmic game theory has extensively studied simple and robust alternatives. A recent breakthrough by Deng, Mao, Sivan and Wang
[
8
]
demonstrated that the random-offerer (RO) mechanism, which randomizes between buyer-optimal and seller-optimal take-it-or-leave-it offers, achieves at least a
\frac{1}{8.23}\approx 0.121
fraction of the first-best efficiency. This approximation guarantee was subsequently improved to
\frac{1}{3.15}
by Fei
[
9
]
. Complementing these advances, Hartline and Wang
[
12
]
provided a geometric analysis of these bounds, offering deeper intuition into their performance. Concurrently, a series of negative results have established theoretical limits on the RO mechanism’s maximum potential
[
1
,
5
,
6
]
.

Despite the rapid progress in understanding simple mechanisms, our structural understanding of the second-best benchmark remains surprisingly limited. To date, the only established theoretical upper bound indicates that the second-best is at most
\frac{2}{e}
-efficient relative to the first-best
[
3
]
. Consequently, the worst-case approximation ratio for the second-best mechanism is currently known only to lie within the wide interval:

\left[\frac{1}{3.15}\approx 0.317,\frac{2}{e}\approx 0.736\right].

This substantial gap between the best known lower bound for a simple mechanism and the best known upper bound for the optimal mechanism highlights a significant void in our understanding of the fundamental limits of bilateral trade.

In this paper, we resolve the long-standing uncertainty regarding the efficiency of the second-best benchmark by closing the approximation gap.

Theorem 1.1

(Main Result, informal)
.

For every pair of independent buyer and seller distributions,

\mathrm{SB}\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{FB}.

Moreover, the ratio
1/2
is tight: there exist distributions where no Bayesian incentive compatible, individually rational, and strongly (or even weakly) budget-balanced mechanism can recover more than half of the first-best surplus.

1.2
Technical Overview

Beyond resolving this long-standing open question, our result introduces a novel paradigm for reasoning about optimal mechanisms in bilateral trade. While recent literature has primarily demonstrated that remarkably simple mechanisms yield constant approximations to the first-best, our work shifts the focus back to the optimal mechanism itself. We show that, despite its complex implicit definition, the universal performance guarantee of the exact second-best mechanism admits a strikingly clean and sharp characterization.

Analytically, our proof departs fundamentally from prior work on simple mechanisms. Rather than bounding the performance of a specific, explicitly specified mechanism, we analyze the second-best benchmark directly. The primary technical challenge is that this benchmark is defined only implicitly, as the solution to a constrained optimization problem over all BIC, IR, and WBB mechanisms. This implicit nature renders direct structural analysis highly non-trivial.

To overcome these barriers, our approach proceeds in three main stages.

1.

We reduce the problem from general continuous distributions to discrete distributions supported on a finite grid. This reduction allows us to utilize a combinatorial representation of feasible mechanisms while preserving the worst-case approximation ratio.

2.

We reformulate the approximation guarantee as a statement regarding the feasibility of a greedy allocation procedure. Specifically, by employing a Lagrangian formulation, the task of proving

\mathrm{SB}\geq\beta\cdot\mathrm{FB}

is reduced to showing that a certain algorithmic construction never fails when
\beta=1/2
. This conceptual shift bridges the classical mechanism-design optimization with a structural condition on the evolution of the allocation rule.

3.

Finally, we identify the extremal configuration of these allocations and prove that the hardest instances can be captured by a state evolution. We then show that this evolution cannot violate the target inequality at
\beta=1/2
, thereby establishing the desired universal lower bound. Complementing this, we demonstrate the tightness of our result by showing that for any
\epsilon>0
, there exist distributions such that
\frac{\mathrm{SB}}{\mathrm{FB}}<\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
.

Preliminaries

Notation.

We denote the sets of non-negative real numbers and non-negative integers by
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, respectively.

The standard bilateral trade problem involves a seller and a buyer.
The seller holds a single indivisible good with a private valuation
s
. The buyer has a private valuation
b
for acquiring the good.
These valuations are independent random variables drawn from prior distributions
F_{S}
and
F_{B}
, respectively.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
F_{S}
and
F_{B}
are supported on
[0,1]
, as our analysis focuses solely on the scale-invariant efficiency ratio.
The mechanism designer knows these prior distributions but does not observe the realized valuations
s
and
b
. Our primary objective is to maximize the expected surplus, or the Gains from Trade (GFT),
b-s
, generated upon a successful transaction.

A mechanism is fully characterized by an
allocation rule

x:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]
, which specifies the probability of trade given the reported valuations, and a
payment rule
, which dictates the financial transfers. We denote the buyer’s expected payment by
p_{B}:[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
and the seller’s expected revenue by
p_{S}:[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
. The expected gain from trade achieved by an allocation rule
x
is formally defined as:

\mathrm{GFT}_{F_{S},F_{B}}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(b-s)x(s,b)\mathrm{d}F_{B}(b)\mathrm{d}F_{S}(s).

The ideal benchmark is the
first-best
mechanism, which achieves full ex-post efficiency by ensuring trade occurs if and only if the buyer’s value meets or exceeds the seller’s. Formally, this is defined by the allocation rule
x(s,b)=1
when
b\geq s
, and
0
otherwise.

The gain from trade in the first-best outcome, denoted by
\mathrm{FB}_{F_{S},F_{B}}
is defined as:

\mathrm{FB}_{F_{S},F_{B}}:=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{b}(b-s)\mathrm{d}F_{S}(s)\mathrm{d}F_{B}(b).

In standard mechanism design, we require mechanisms to satisfy key strategic and participation constraints.
First, we define the interim expected
utility
for a buyer with true valuation
b
reporting
b^{\prime}
, and a seller with true valuation
s
reporting
s^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle u_{B}(b,b^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim F_{S}}[x(s,b^{\prime})\cdot b-p_{B}(s,b^{\prime})];

\displaystyle u_{S}(s,s^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{b\sim F_{B}}[p_{S}(s^{\prime},b)-x(s^{\prime},b)\cdot s].

A mechanism is
Bayesian incentive-compatible
(BIC) if truth-telling maximizes each agent’s expected utility. That is, for any
b,b^{\prime},s,s^{\prime}\in[0,1]
:

u_{B}(b,b)\geq u_{B}(b,b^{\prime})\quad\text{and}\quad u_{S}(s,s)\geq u_{S}(s,s^{\prime}).

Furthermore, a mechanism is
interim individually rational
(IR) if both agents are guaranteed a non-negative expected utility from participating, meaning
u_{B}(b,b)\geq 0
and
u_{S}(s,s)\geq 0
for all
b,s\in[0,1]
.

Finally, to ensure the mechanism is financially self-sufficient and does not rely on external subsidies, we impose a budget balance constraint. In this paper, we formalize this requirement as follows:

Definition 2.1

(Budget Balance Constraints)
.

A mechanism is
strongly budget-balanced
(SBB) if the payment collected from the buyer exactly equals the revenue paid to the seller for every valuation profile
(s,b)
:

p_{B}(s,b)=p_{S}(s,b).

A mechanism is
weakly budget-balanced
(WBB) if the payment collected from the buyer is at least the revenue paid to the seller, meaning the mechanism never runs a deficit:

p_{B}(s,b)\geq p_{S}(s,b).

In their seminal work, Myerson and Satterthwaite
[
17
]
characterized the second-best mechanism, which maximizes the expected gains from trade subject to Bayesian incentive compatibility (BIC), interim individual rationality (interim IR), and strong budget balance (SBB) constraints. Assuming the prior distributions
F_{S}
and
F_{B}
admit continuous and strictly positive density functions
f_{S}
and
f_{B}
, a central result of their analysis is that an allocation rule
x(s,b)
is implementable under these constraints if and only if its interim allocation probabilities are monotonic and it satisfies the following budget balance discriminant:

\Lambda(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\left(b-\frac{1-F_{B}(b)}{f_{B}(b)}-s-\frac{F_{S}(s)}{f_{S}(s)}\right)x(s,b)f_{B}(b)f_{S}(s)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}b\geq 0.

We denote by
\mathrm{SB}_{F_{S},F_{B}}
the expected gains from trade achieved by the second-best mechanism, formally defined as the supremum of the following optimization program:

\displaystyle\sup

\displaystyle~\mathrm{GFT}_{F_{S},F_{B}}(x)

s.t.

\displaystyle~\Lambda(x)\geq 0,

\displaystyle~x_{B}(b_{1})\leq x_{B}(b_{2}),\forall b_{1},b_{2}\in[0,1],b_{1}<b_{2},

(1)

\displaystyle~x_{S}(s_{1})\geq x_{S}(s_{2}),\forall s_{1},s_{2}\in[0,1],s_{1}<s_{2},

where
x_{B}(b)=\int_{0}^{1}x(t,b)f_{S}(t)\mathrm{d}t
is weakly increasing and
x_{S}(s)=\int_{0}^{1}x(s,t)f_{B}(t)\mathrm{d}t
is weakly decreasing.

In this paper, we study the worst-case approximation ratio
\beta
between the second-best and first-best outcomes, that is,

\beta:=\inf_{F_{B},F_{S}\in\Delta([0,1])}\frac{\mathrm{SB}_{F_{S},F_{B}}}{\mathrm{FB}_{F_{S},F_{B}}},

where
\Delta([0,1])
denotes the set of all probability measures over the interval
[0,1]
.

Remark
.

Hereafter, we drop the subscripts on FB, SB, GFT, and related terms whenever the underlying measures
F_{B}
and
F_{S}
are clear from the context.

From Continuous to Discrete Distributions

Since the original optimization problem (
2
) is formulated over a continuum of type profiles, our first step is to extend it to the setting of Borel probability measures. Subsequently, we specialize this formulation to discrete distributions by restricting the measures to finite supports. All omitted proofs from this section are deferred to Appendix
A
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
F_{S}
and
F_{B}
be Borel probability measures on
[0,1]
, the gain from trade of the second-best mechanism with BIC, interim IR, SBB (or WBB) constraint is the optimal value of the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\sup_{x}\quad

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{2}}(b-s)\,x(s,b)\,\mathrm{d}F_{S}(s)\,\mathrm{d}F_{B}(b)

s.t.

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathrm{gen}}(x)=\int_{[0,1]}\!\!\left(b\,x_{B}(b)-\int_{0}^{b}x_{B}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\mathrm{d}F_{B}(b)-\int_{[0,1]}\!\!\left(s\,x_{S}(s)+\int_{s}^{1}x_{S}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\mathrm{d}F_{S}(s)\geq 0,

\displaystyle x_{B}(b_{1})\leq x_{B}(b_{2}),\qquad\forall\,b_{1}<b_{2},

\displaystyle x_{S}(s_{1})\geq x_{S}(s_{2}),\qquad\forall\,s_{1}<s_{2},

where

x_{B}(b)=\int_{[0,1]}x(s,b)\,\mathrm{d}F_{S}(s),\qquad x_{S}(s)=\int_{[0,1]}x(s,b)\,\mathrm{d}F_{B}(b).

Proposition
3.1
establishes a crucial equivalence: under BIC and interim IR, the distinction between strong and weak budget balance does not affect the set of implementable allocation rules. Both budget constraints are characterized by the identical condition
\Lambda_{\mathrm{gen}}(x)\geq 0
.

Consequently, the classical conclusions regarding the second-best mechanism in Myerson and Satterthwaite
[

]
and Blumrosen and Mizrahi

apply equally under both SBB and WBB constraints.
Hereafter, we refer to this simply as the budget balance (BB) constraint.

To facilitate our proof, we restrict our attention to discrete distributions defined on equally spaced grid points. As we will demonstrate, this restriction is entirely without loss of generality. We now specialize Proposition
3.1
to this discrete setting. Given a positive integer
N
, let the seller’s values be supported on
\{s_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N}
with
0\leq s_{0}<s_{1}<\dots<s_{N}\leq 1
, and the buyer’s values on
\{b_{j}\}_{j=0}^{N}
with
0\leq b_{0}<b_{1}<\dots<b_{N}\leq 1
. Let
f_{S}(\cdot)
and
f_{B}(\cdot)
denote the respective probability mass functions, with their associated cumulative distribution functions defined as:

F_{S}(s_{i})=\sum_{t\leq i}f_{S}(s_{t}),\qquad F_{B}(b_{j})=\sum_{t\leq j}f_{B}(b_{t}).

Corollary 3.2
.

For a given
N>0
, suppose the seller’s and buyer’s values are drawn from
F_{S},F_{B}
, supported on
\{s_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N}
and
\{b_{j}\}_{j=0}^{N}
, respectively. Then, under this discretization, second-best mechanism with BIC, interim IR, SBB (or WBB) can be reformulated as:

\displaystyle\sup_{x}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=0}^{N}x(s_{i},b_{j})\,f_{S}(s_{i})\,f_{B}(b_{j})\,(b_{j}-s_{i})

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=0}^{N}x(s_{i},b_{j})\,f_{S}(s_{i})\,f_{B}(b_{j})\,\Bigg(b_{j}+(b_{j}-b_{j+1})\frac{1-F_{B}(b_{j})}{f_{B}(b_{j})}-s_{i}-(s_{i}-s_{i-1})\frac{F_{S}(s_{i-1})}{f_{S}(s_{i})}\Bigg)\geq 0,

\displaystyle x_{S}(s_{i})\geq x_{S}(s_{i+1}),\qquad\forall\,i=0,\ldots,N-1,

\displaystyle x_{B}(b_{j})\leq x_{B}(b_{j+1}),\qquad\forall\,j=0,\ldots,N-1,

where
s_{-1}:=s_{0}
,
b_{N+1}:=b_{N}
and

x_{B}(b_{j})=\sum_{i=0}^{N}x(s_{i},b_{j})f_{S}(s_{i}),\qquad x_{S}(s_{i})=\sum_{j=0}^{N}x(s_{i},b_{j})f_{B}(b_{j}).

To discretize the Borel probability measures, we restrict our analysis to an equidistant grid. Normalizing the support to the unit interval
[0,1]
, we define the grid points as
s_{i}=i\epsilon
and
b_{j}=j\epsilon
for
i,j\in\{0,\dots,N\}
, where
\epsilon=1/N
.

Given Borel probability measures
F_{S}
and
F_{B}
, we construct their discrete counterparts
\hat{F}_{S}
and
\hat{F}_{B}
by concentrating the probability mass onto this grid. Specifically, we assign the seller’s mass on the interval
(s_{i-1},s_{i}]
to the upper endpoint
s_{i}
, and the buyer’s mass on
[b_{j-1},b_{j})
to the lower endpoint
b_{j-1}
. The discrete probability mass functions are thus defined as:

\hat{f}_{S}(s_{i})=F_{S}(s_{i})-F_{S}(s_{i-1}),\qquad\hat{f}_{B}(b_{j-1})=F_{B}(b_{j}^{-})-F_{B}(b_{j-1}^{-}),

where
F_{B}(x^{-})
denotes the left-hand limit of the cumulative distribution function at
x
.

Let
\hat{x}
denote the optimal allocation rule for the discrete optimization problem (Corollary
3.2
) under the distributions
\hat{F}_{S}
and
\hat{F}_{B}
. We extend
\hat{x}
back to the continuous domain by defining a piecewise-constant step function,
\tilde{x}
. Formally, for any continuous type profile
(s,b)\in(s_{i-1},s_{i}]\times[b_{j-1},b_{j})
, we set the extended allocation to:

\tilde{x}(s,b)=\hat{x}(s_{i},b_{j-1}).

Lemma 3.3
.

The continuous extension
\tilde{x}
satisfies BIC, interim IR, and BB constraints under the original distribution
F_{S}
and
F_{B}
.

Lemma
3.3
shows that feasibility is preserved
when a discrete allocation rule is extended to the continuous domain. It remains
to verify that the corresponding objective values are also consistent in the continuum limit. We establish this convergence by employing a sequence of dyadic grids.

Throughout the remainder of the analysis, we operate on a dyadic sequence parameterized by a positive integer
M
. For each
M
, we define the grid points
s_{i}^{(M)}=i\cdot 2^{-M}
and
b_{j}^{(M)}=j\cdot 2^{-M}
. We partition the unit interval into cells

I_{0}^{(M)}:=\{0\},\qquad I_{i}^{(M)}:=(s_{i-1}^{(M)},s_{i}^{(M)}]\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,2^{M},

and

J_{j}^{(M)}:=[b_{j}^{(M)},b_{j+1}^{(M)})\text{ for }j=0,\ldots,2^{M}-1,\qquad J_{2^{M}}^{(M)}:=\{1\}.

Let
\widehat{f}_{S}^{(M)}(s_{i}^{(M)})
and
\widehat{f}_{B}^{(M)}(b_{j-1}^{(M)})
denote the integrated probability masses over
I_{i}^{(M)}
and
J_{j}^{(M)}
.

Let
\mathcal{X}_{M}
denote the feasible set of discrete allocation rules on
the
2^{-M}
-grid. The discretized second-best expected surplus is given by:

\widehat{\mathrm{SB}}_{M}:=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{M}}\sum_{j=1}^{2^{M}}\sum_{i=1}^{2^{M}}\left(b_{j-1}^{(M)}-s_{i}^{(M)}\right)x\left(s_{i}^{(M)},b_{j-1}^{(M)}\right)\widehat{f}_{S}^{(M)}\left(s_{i}^{(M)}\right)\widehat{f}_{B}^{(M)}\left(b_{j-1}^{(M)}\right).

Let
\widehat{x}_{M}\in\mathcal{X}_{M}
be an optimal allocation. We denote its step-function extension by
\widetilde{x}_{M}
, where
\widetilde{x}_{M}(s,b)=\widehat{x}_{M}(s_{i}^{(M)},b_{j-1}^{(M)})
for all
(s,b)\in I_{i}^{(M)}\times J_{j}^{(M)}
. Symmetrically, we define the discretized first-best benchmark as:

\widehat{\mathrm{FB}}_{M}:=\sum_{j=1}^{2^{M}}\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\left(b_{j-1}^{(M)}-s_{i}^{(M)}\right)\widehat{f}_{S}^{(M)}\left(s_{i}^{(M)}\right)\widehat{f}_{B}^{(M)}\left(b_{j-1}^{(M)}\right).

We rely on a fundamental consistency property between successive discretizations: because the
2^{-M}
-grid strictly refines the
2^{-(M-1)}
-grid, allocations map seamlessly between them. Any feasible allocation on the coarser
2^{-(M-1)}
-grid can be lifted to the finer
2^{-M}
-grid by duplicating its value across all corresponding sub-rectangles. Conversely, any feasible allocation on the finer grid can be pooled to the coarser grid by taking the mass-weighted average over the sub-rectangles that comprise each cell of the coarser partition.

Lemma 3.4
.

There exists an increasing sequence
\{M_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
such that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{GFT}_{F_{S},F_{B}}(\widetilde{x}_{M_{m}})}{\mathrm{FB}_{F_{S},F_{B}}}\geq\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{\widehat{\mathrm{SB}}_{M}}{\widehat{\mathrm{FB}}_{M}}.

We can now lift the discrete performance guarantee to the continuous setting. By Lemma
3.3
, each step-function extension
\widetilde{x}_{M_{m}}
remains fully feasible for the original continuous instance. Therefore, the continuous second-best benchmark naturally satisfies:

\frac{\mathrm{SB}_{F_{S},F_{B}}}{\mathrm{FB}_{F_{S},F_{B}}}\geq\frac{\mathrm{GFT}_{F_{S},F_{B}}(\widetilde{x}_{M_{m}})}{\mathrm{FB}_{F_{S},F_{B}}}.

Taking
m\to\infty
and applying Lemma
3.4
yields:

\frac{\mathrm{SB}_{F_{S},F_{B}}}{\mathrm{FB}_{F_{S},F_{B}}}\geq\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{\widehat{\mathrm{SB}}_{M}}{\widehat{\mathrm{FB}}_{M}}.

Thus, any uniform lower bound established for the dyadic discrete instances immediately applies to the original Borel measures.

Conversely, any discrete measure defined on a finite grid is indeed a valid Borel probability measure on
[0,1]
. Consequently, the worst-case efficiency ratio over all general Borel instances cannot exceed the worst-case ratio over finite-grid instances. Combining these two inequalities, we conclude that the discrete and continuous worst-case approximation ratios perfectly coincide.

Next, we simplify the discrete domain. For a fixed
\epsilon=1/N
, the
\epsilon
-equidistant grid on
[0,1]
is isomorphic to the integer grid
\{0,1,\ldots,N\}
up to a scaling factor of
\epsilon
. Because the surplus objective
b-s
is homogeneous of degree one in the agents’ valuations, both
\widehat{\mathrm{SB}}
and
\widehat{\mathrm{FB}}
scale by this exact same factor under the linear transformation. As a result, the ratio
\widehat{\mathrm{SB}}/\widehat{\mathrm{FB}}
is strictly scale-invariant. This allows us to equivalently evaluate any finite equidistant grid on
[0,1]
as a finite subset of the integers. Let
\Delta(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})
denote the space of all finitely supported probability distributions on the non-negative integers.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\Delta([0,1])
be the set of all probability measures supported on
[0,1]
. The worst-case efficiency ratio over the continuous domain is strictly equivalent to that over the discrete integer domain:

\inf_{F_{B},F_{S}\in\Delta([0,1])}\frac{\mathrm{SB}}{\mathrm{FB}}=\inf_{\widehat{F}_{B},\widehat{F}_{S}\in\Delta(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})}\frac{\widehat{\mathrm{SB}}}{\widehat{\mathrm{FB}}}.

Accordingly, for the remainder of this paper, we restrict our analysis without loss of generality to discrete distributions supported on
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

4
From Optimization to Greedy Algorithm

In this section, we develop the theoretical framework necessary to analyze the second-best benchmark. We demonstrate that characterizing the optimal efficiency bound over integer domains can be reduced to an algorithmic feasibility problem.

Our analysis proceeds in three main stages.
We first introduce a Lagrangian formulation, which reframes the efficiency gap in terms of virtual values. By establishing the property of discrete weak regularity (and applying an ironing procedure where necessary), we ensure that the virtual value function is structurally well-behaved, simplifying the subsequent optimization.
Leveraging this regularity, we apply Sion’s Minimax Theorem to exchange the order of optimization. This crucial transformation pivots the problem from a worst-case robustness evaluation over all buyer distributions to a sequence of local feasibility constraints on the allocation rule itself.
Finally, we prove that these local constraints are satisfied if and only if a specific greedy allocation procedure terminates successfully. This effectively bridges the gap between static theoretical optimality and a dynamic, constructive check for the target efficiency bound
\beta
.

For brevity, all omitted proofs for this section are deferred to Appendix
B
.

4.1
The Lagrangian Dual Representation

Following Corollary
3.2
, we characterize the feasibility of a mechanism through constraints.
For a given pair of distributions
(F_{S},F_{B})
, we use
\Lambda_{D}(x)\geq 0
to represent the first constraint, where

\Lambda_{D}(x)=\sum_{s,b}x(s,b)\,f_{S}(s)\,f_{B}(b)\,\left(b-\frac{1-F_{B}(b)}{f_{B}(b)}-s-\frac{F_{S}(s{-1})}{f_{S}(s)}\right)\geq 0.

The remaining two constraints, namely the monotonicity conditions

x_{B}(b)\leq x_{B}(b{+1})

and

x_{S}(s)\geq x_{S}(s{+1})
for any
s,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
are incorporated into the feasible domain. Accordingly, let
\mathcal{R}_{1}
denote the set of all allocations satisfying these two constraints.
The problem of finding the second-best benchmark can thus be expressed as the following linear program:

\mathrm{SB}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{R}_{1}}\ \mathrm{GFT}(x)\qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad\Lambda_{D}(x)\geq 0.

To analyze this constrained program, we introduce the Lagrangian
L(x,\alpha)
for a multiplier
\alpha\geq 0
:

L(x,\alpha):=\mathrm{GFT}(x)+\alpha\,\Lambda_{D}(x).

Given
F_{S}
and
F_{B}
, the optimum GFT problem is a linear programming problem in terms of

. By strong duality we have the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

The second-best efficiency admits the following minimax representation:

\mathrm{SB}=\inf_{\alpha\geq 0}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{R}_{1}}L(x,\alpha).

4.2
Discrete
\alpha
-Weak Regularity and Ironing

Since shifting both buyer and seller valuations by a common constant does not affect the gains from trade or the validity of
\alpha
-weak regularity, we may normalize the distributions without loss of generality. Specifically, we shift the distributions such that the seller’s support begins exactly at zero.

Lemma 4.2
.

To prove the approximation ratio
\mathrm{SB}\geq\beta\cdot\mathrm{FB}
, it is without loss of generality to assume that the seller’s distribution has strictly positive mass at the origin, i.e.
f_{S}(0)>0
.

We now define the
\alpha
-virtual values and the ironing procedure used to handle non-regular distributions.

Definition 4.1

(
\alpha
-Virtual Value and
\alpha
-Weak Regularity)
.

Given a parameter
\alpha\geq 0
and a discrete seller distribution
F_{S}
, we define the seller-side
\alpha
-virtual value as:

\phi_{S}(s):=(1+\alpha)s+\alpha\frac{F_{S}(s-1)}{f_{S}(s)}\quad\forall s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},

where we adopt the convention
F_{S}(-1)=0
. We say that
F_{S}
is
\alpha
-weakly regular
if
\phi_{S}(s)
is weakly increasing in
s
.

In many standard settings, the distribution naturally satisfies
\alpha
-weak regularity. However, when
\phi_{S}(s)
fails to be weakly increasing, standard optimization techniques break down. To restore monotonicity, we define an ironed version of the virtual value using a convex envelope approach.

Definition 4.2

(Ironed
\alpha
-Virtual Value)
.

Let
F_{S}
be a discrete seller distribution. Let
q_{s}:=F_{S}(s)
for
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
(with
q_{-1}:=0
), and let
\Phi_{S}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be the piecewise-linear function satisfying:

\Phi_{S}(q_{s}):=\sum_{t=0}^{s}\phi_{S}(t)f_{S}(t).

Let
\overline{\Phi}_{S}
denote the convex envelope (i.e., the least convex majorant) of
\Phi_{S}
on
[0,1]
. The
ironed
\alpha
-virtual value
, denoted
\phi_{S}^{\mathrm{ir}}
, is defined as the slope of
\overline{\Phi}_{S}
on each interval
(q_{s-1},q_{s}]
:

\phi_{S}^{\mathrm{ir}}(s):=\frac{\overline{\Phi}_{S}(F_{S}(s))-\overline{\Phi}_{S}(F_{S}(s-1))}{f_{S}(s)}.

By construction,
\phi_{S}^{\mathrm{ir}}
is weakly increasing in
s
. If
F_{S}
is already
\alpha
-weakly regular, then
\overline{\Phi}_{S}=\Phi_{S}
, yielding
\phi_{S}^{\mathrm{ir}}=\phi_{S}
. We write
F_{S}^{\mathrm{ir}}
for the
\alpha
-weakly regular distribution associated with
\phi_{S}^{\mathrm{ir}}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
\alpha\geq 0
, the Lagrangian problem can be restricted, without loss of generality, to the set of point-wise monotone allocations:

\mathcal{R}_{2}:=\left\{x:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2}\to[0,1]\;\middle|\;\begin{array}[]{l}x(s,b)\leq x(s,b^{\prime})\text{ for all }b<b^{\prime}\\
x(s,b)\geq x(s^{\prime},b)\text{ for all }s<s^{\prime}\end{array}\right\}.

Furthermore, we can restrict our focus entirely to
\alpha
-weakly regular distributions. The following lemma establishes that applying our ironing procedure to an irregular distribution constitutes a worst-case reduction, as it can only decrease the overall approximation ratio.

Lemma 4.4
.

Fix
\alpha\geq 0
. Replacing
F_{S}
with its ironed version
F_{S}^{ir}
leaves
\mathrm{SB}
unchanged, but weakly increases
\mathrm{FB}
. Consequently,

\frac{\mathrm{SB}_{F_{S}^{ir},F_{B}}}{\mathrm{FB}_{F_{S}^{ir},F_{B}}}\leq\frac{\mathrm{SB}_{F_{S},F_{B}}}{\mathrm{FB}_{F_{S},F_{B}}}.

Equipped with Lemmas
4.3
and
4.4
, we may assume without loss of generality that the seller’s distribution is
\alpha
-weakly regular and the allocation rule lies in
\mathcal{R}_{2}
. The problem of establishing the universal efficiency bound
\beta
thus reduces to verifying the following minimax inequality:

\inf_{\alpha\geq 0}\inf_{\;\alpha\text{-weakly regular}\;F_{S}}\ \inf_{F_{B}\in\Delta(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})}\ \sup_{x\in\mathcal{R}_{2}}\left(\mathrm{GFT}(x)+\alpha\Lambda_{D}(x)-\beta\cdot\mathrm{FB}\right)\geq 0.

4.3
The Minimax Swap

To establish the universal lower bound
\beta
, we apply Sion’s Minimax Theorem
[
18
]
to the inner optimization. Note that the space of allocations
{\cal R}_{2}
is compact and convex, the domain of buyer distributions
\Delta(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})
is convex, and our objective function as below is a linear function of the probabilities of the discrete values, satisfying the necessary semicontinuity requirements.

\displaystyle\mathrm{GFT}(x)+\alpha\cdot\Lambda_{D}(x)-\beta\cdot\mathrm{FB}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s,b}x(s,b)\,f_{S}(s)\,f_{B}(b)\,(b-s)+\alpha\cdot\sum_{s,b}x(s,b)\left[f_{S}(s)f_{B}(b)(b-s)-f_{S}(s)\sum_{t>b}f_{B}(t)-f_{B}(b)F_{S}(s{-}1)\right]

\displaystyle-\beta\cdot\sum_{b}\sum_{s<b}(b-s)f_{B}(b)f_{S}(s).

Theorem 4.5

(Sion’s Minimax Theorem
[
18
]
)
.

Let
X
be a compact convex subset of a linear topological space, and let
Y
be a convex subset of a linear topological space. Let

f:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}

be a function such that:

1.

for every
y\in Y
, the function
x\mapsto f(x,y)
is upper semicontinuous and quasiconcave on
X
;

2.

for every
x\in X
, the function
y\mapsto f(x,y)
is lower semicontinuous and quasiconvex on
Y
.

Then

\sup_{x\in X}\inf_{y\in Y}f(x,y)=\inf_{y\in Y}\sup_{x\in X}f(x,y).

Applying Theorem
4.5
to our target inequality, we swap the inner minimization and maximization operators:

\displaystyle\inf_{\alpha\geq 0}\inf_{F_{S}\,\alpha\textrm{-weakly regular}}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{R}_{2}}\inf_{F_{B}\in\Delta(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})}[\mathrm{GFT}(x)+\alpha\cdot\Lambda_{D}(x)-\beta\cdot\mathrm{FB}]\geq 0.

(2)

This reformulation shifts our perspective to a mechanism design game: for any fixed
\alpha
and
\alpha
-weakly regular seller distribution
F_{S}
, we must construct a single allocation rule
x\in{\cal R}_{2}
that satisfies the
\beta
-efficiency bound for all possible buyer distributions
F_{B}
. If such a robust allocation can be constructed, then
\beta
is established as a universal lower bound on the second-best benchmark.

4.4
The Greedy Algorithm

Having fixed the multiplier
\alpha\geq 0
and an
\alpha
-weakly regular seller distribution
F_{S}
, we now direct our attention to the inner minimax problem:

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{R}_{2}}\;\inf_{F_{B}\in\Delta(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})}\Bigl[\mathrm{GFT}(x)+\alpha\cdot\Lambda_{D}(x)-\beta\cdot\mathrm{FB}\Bigr].

At first sight, this is a global optimization problem, since the allocation rule
x
must simultaneously guarantee the desired lower bound for every buyer distribution
F_{B}
. The key observation, however, is that once
F_{S}
is fixed, the bracketed objective is strictly linear with respect to the buyer’s discrete probability masses
f_{B}
. Therefore, the adversarial choice of
F_{B}
can be reduced to a family of local constraints indexed by the buyer value
b
. In this way, the universal requirement over all buyer distributions is transformed into a buyer-wise feasibility condition.

The formal argument proceeds in three steps. First, we decompose the global
objective into buyer-wise coefficients. Second, we use these coefficients to
derive a sequential construction of the allocation rule. Finally, we prove
that this greedy construction is equivalent to the original maximin problem:
success of the algorithm is exactly the existence of an allocation rule
certifying the desired lower bound.

Buyer-Wise Decomposition.

We begin with the first step. Observe that, for a fixed allocation
x
, the quantity

\mathrm{GFT}(x)+\alpha\Lambda_{D}(x)-\beta\,\mathrm{FB}

is linear in the probabilities
f_{B}
. We may therefore represent it in the following form

\mathrm{GFT}(x)+\alpha\Lambda_{D}(x)-\beta\,\mathrm{FB}=\sum_{b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}f_{B}(b)\,H(b;x),

(3)

where

H(b;x):=\sum_{0\leq s<b}f_{S}(s)\Biggl[(b-s)\left((1+\alpha)x(s,b)-\beta\right)-\alpha\sum_{s<t<b}x(t,b)-\alpha\sum_{s<b^{\prime}<b}x(s,b^{\prime})\Biggr].

(4)

Because the probability masses
f_{B}(b)
are non-negative and sum to one, the global objective is non-negative for every valid buyer distribution if and only if every term in the summation is individually non-negative. This yields the fundamental equivalence:

\mathrm{GFT}(x)+\alpha\Lambda_{D}(x)-\beta\,\mathrm{FB}\geq 0\ \text{for }F_{B}\in\Delta(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad H(b;x)\geq 0\ \text{for }b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

(5)

Equation (

constraint into a sequence of local feasibility requirements. It remains to
construct a monotone allocation rule

satisfying all of

to preserve monotonicity. If this already gives

the current buyer value by the smallest amount that makes the local
constraint tight.

Upper Bound: Tightness via an Example

Section 5 showed that the bottleneck in the lower-bound analysis occurs only in the limiting regime
\alpha\to\infty
when
\phi_{S}
is constant. For ease of verification, this section presents an explicit continuous analogue
of the same regime and includes several additional discussions showing that the
approximation ratio indeed converges to
\tfrac{1}{2}
. This establishes the tightness of the half-approximation bound.

Let
\alpha>0
and
c>0
be parameters, and define

\displaystyle F_{S}(s)=\left(\frac{c}{c+(1+\alpha)(1-s)}\right)^{a},\quad F_{B}(b)=1-\left(\frac{c}{c+(1+\alpha)b}\right)^{a},

for
s\in(0,1],b\in[0,1)
, where
a=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}
.
This is a symmetric family of distributions on
[0,1]
, with matching boundary masses at
s=0
and
b=1
, both equal to

P=\left(\frac{c}{c+1+\alpha}\right)^{a}.

The family above is a particular instance of a general class of
mixed distributions we use throughout this section: those whose
seller distribution has a single atom at
s=0
and whose buyer
distribution has a single atom at
b=1
, with absolutely
continuous mass elsewhere. Formally, we assume

\mathrm{d}F_{S}(s)=p_{S}\,\delta_{0}(\mathrm{d}s)+f_{S}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,\qquad\mathrm{d}F_{B}(b)=f_{B}(b)\,\mathrm{d}b+p_{B}\,\delta_{1}(\mathrm{d}b),

where
\delta_{x_{0}}
denotes the Dirac measure concentrated at
x_{0}
,
characterized by
\int g(x)\,\delta_{x_{0}}(\mathrm{d}x)=g(x_{0})
for every
bounded measurable
g
. Thus
F_{S}
consists of a point mass of size

p_{S}
at
s=0
plus an absolutely continuous part with density
f_{S}

on
(0,1)
, and symmetrically for
F_{B}
at
b=1
. For interior points define

\phi_{S}(s):=s+\frac{F_{S}(s)}{f_{S}(s)},\qquad\phi_{B}(b):=b-\frac{1-F_{B}(b)}{f_{B}(b)},\qquad s,b\in(0,1),

and use the endpoint conventions

\widehat{\phi}_{S}(0):=0,\qquad\widehat{\phi}_{S}(s):=\phi_{S}(s)\ (s\in(0,1)),

\widehat{\phi}_{B}(b):=\phi_{B}(b)\ (b\in(0,1)),\qquad\widehat{\phi}_{B}(1):=1.

Proposition 6.1

(Endpoint-mass absolutely continuous case)
.

Under the endpoint-mass notation above, the mixed-distribution problem in
Proposition
3.1
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sup_{x}\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{GFT}(x)=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}(b-s)x(s,b)\,\mathrm{d}F_{S}(s)\,\mathrm{d}F_{B}(b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\textrm{con-mass}}(x)=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\bigl(\widehat{\phi}_{B}(b)-\widehat{\phi}_{S}(s)\bigr)x(s,b)\,\mathrm{d}F_{S}(s)\,\mathrm{d}F_{B}(b)\geq 0,

\displaystyle x_{B}(b_{1})\leq x_{B}(b_{2}),\qquad\forall\,b_{1}<b_{2},

\displaystyle x_{S}(s_{1})\geq x_{S}(s_{2}),\qquad\forall\,s_{1}<s_{2},

Equivalently, decomposing the product measure into the endpoint atom, the two
one-dimensional boundaries, and the interior gives

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\textrm{con-mass}}(x)

\displaystyle=p_{S}p_{B}\,x(0,1)+p_{S}\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{B}(b)x(0,b)f_{B}(b)\,\mathrm{d}b+p_{B}\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(1-\phi_{S}(s)\bigr)x(s,1)f_{S}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(\phi_{B}(b)-\phi_{S}(s)\bigr)x(s,b)f_{S}(s)f_{B}(b)\,\mathrm{d}b\,\mathrm{d}s.

We compute
\mathrm{FB}
and
\mathrm{SB}
in closed form. The most critical technical step is showing that the SB mechanism is boundary-restricted for this family. By comparing the marginal gain in
\mathrm{GFT}
to the marginal loss in the feasibility constraint
\Lambda
, we demonstrate that boundary trades (where
s=0
or
b=1
) strictly dominate any interior trades
(s,b)
in terms of their contribution to the objective per unit of feasibility slack consumed. Finally, we compute the ratio
\text{SB}/\text{FB}
and show that as
c\to 0^{+}
and
\alpha\to\infty
, the ratio
\mathrm{SB}/\mathrm{FB}
tends to
\tfrac{1}{2}
. Therefore, no mechanism can guarantee a ratio strictly larger than
\tfrac{1}{2}
.

Theorem 6.2
.

No mechanism that is BIC, IR, and WBB can guarantee a worst-case approximation ratio to the first-best GFT exceeding
1/2
.

Why we do not impose constant virtual values on the original scale.

A natural alternative would be to require

b-\frac{1-F_{B}(b)}{f_{B}(b)}\equiv-c,\qquad s+\frac{F_{S}(s)}{f_{S}(s)}\equiv 1+c

directly on
[0,1]
. This yields

F_{B}(b)=\frac{b}{c+b},\qquad F_{S}(s)=\frac{c}{c+1-s},

with atoms
c/(1+c)
at
b=1
and
s=0
. For this family,

\mathrm{FB}(c)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{c^{2}}{(c+b)(c+1-b)}\,\mathrm{d}b=\frac{2c^{2}}{1+2c}\log\!\left(1+\frac{1}{c}\right).

Moreover, one can verify that the optimal second-best allocation is again supported only on the two boundary lines; writing
u=b
on
\{s=0\}
and
u=1-s
on
\{b=1\}
, the WBB constraint truncates both lines at the common threshold
u\geq t
, where
t=\frac{c}{1+2c}
, and therefore

\mathrm{SB}(c)=\left(\frac{c}{1+c}\right)^{2}+2\frac{c}{1+c}\int_{t}^{1}u\,\frac{c}{(c+u)^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}u=\frac{2c^{2}}{1+c}\log\!\left(1+\frac{1}{2c}\right).

Hence both
\mathrm{FB}
and
\mathrm{SB}
are governed by the same logarithmic term:

\mathrm{FB}(c)=2c^{2}\log(1/c)+O(c^{2}),\qquad\mathrm{SB}(c)=2c^{2}\log(1/c)+O(c^{2}).

Equivalently, the WBB constraint only changes the lower cutoff of the same logarithmic integral, from
u=0
to
u=t=\Theta(c)
, and therefore does not alter its leading coefficient. In particular,

\frac{\mathrm{SB}(c)}{\mathrm{FB}(c)}\to 1\qquad\text{as }c\to 0^{+}.

Thus the direct equal-virtual-value construction does not realize the bottleneck identified in Section 5. The family above is introduced precisely because it approaches the critical regime only through the limit
a=\alpha/(1+\alpha)\uparrow 1
.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Considering the primal problem

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{R}_{1}}\ \mathrm{GFT}(x)\qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad\Lambda_{D}(x)\geq 0,

its optimal value is

P^{*}:=\sup\left\{\mathrm{GFT}(x):x\in\mathcal{R}_{1},\ \Lambda_{D}(x)\geq 0\right\}.

To put the problem into the standard Lagrangian form, we multiply both the objective and the constraint by
-1
. Then the problem is equivalently written as

-P^{*}=\inf_{x\in\mathcal{R}_{1}}\ \left(-\mathrm{GFT}(x)\right)\qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad-\Lambda_{D}(x)\leq 0.

Now we define the standard Lagrangian for this minimization problem:

\widetilde{L}(x,\alpha):=-\mathrm{GFT}(x)+\alpha\left(-\Lambda_{D}(x)\right),\qquad\alpha\geq 0.

That is,

\widetilde{L}(x,\alpha)=-\mathrm{GFT}(x)-\alpha\,\Lambda_{D}(x).

Since both the objective function and the constraints are linear with respect to the allocation rule
x
(for any fixed
F_{S}
and
F_{B}
), the problem constitutes a linear program in
x
. It follows from strong duality for linear programming that

-P^{*}=\sup_{\alpha\geq 0}\ \min_{x\in\mathcal{R}_{1}}\ \widetilde{L}(x,\alpha).

Substituting the definition of
\widetilde{L}
gives

-P^{*}=\sup_{\alpha\geq 0}\ \min_{x\in\mathcal{R}_{1}}\left(-\mathrm{GFT}(x)-\alpha\,\Lambda_{D}(x)\right).

Finally, multiply both sides by
-1
. Using

-\sup_{\alpha\geq 0}\min_{x\in\mathcal{R}_{1}}a(x,\alpha)=\inf_{\alpha\geq 0}\max_{x\in\mathcal{R}_{1}}\left(-a(x,\alpha)\right),

we obtain

P^{*}=\inf_{\alpha\geq 0}\ \max_{x\in\mathcal{R}_{1}}\left(\mathrm{GFT}(x)+\alpha\,\Lambda_{D}(x)\right).

This is exactly the claimed representation.
∎

Proof of Lemma
5.6
.

Throughout the proof, empty products are understood as
1
. Let

a:=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}
. Since
\alpha>0
, we have
a\in(0,1)
and

1-a\in(0,1)
. Recall that, in the case
r=0
,

\widetilde{F}_{S}^{(N)}(t-1)=\prod_{j=N-t}^{N-2}\left(1+\frac{a}{j}\right),\qquad 1\leq t\leq N-1,

and

c_{j}=1-\frac{1}{j(1+\alpha)}=1-\frac{1-a}{j}=\frac{j-1+a}{j}.

Moreover,

\widetilde{G}_{N-1}^{(N)}=\sum_{t=1}^{N-1}\frac{\widetilde{F}_{S}^{(N)}(t-1)}{t(1+\alpha)}\prod_{j=t+1}^{N-1}c_{j}.

We first prove strict monotonicity in
N
. Since
\alpha>0
, define

\kappa:=\frac{1}{a}=\frac{1+\alpha}{\alpha}>0
. For fixed
N\geq 2
and

1\leq t\leq N-1
, set

\Delta_{t}:=\widetilde{F}_{S}^{(N+1)}(t)-\widetilde{F}_{S}^{(N+1)}(t-1).

Since

\widetilde{F}_{S}^{(N+1)}(t)=\widetilde{F}_{S}^{(N+1)}(t-1)\frac{\kappa(N-t)+1}{\kappa(N-t)},

we have

\Delta_{t}=\frac{\widetilde{F}_{S}^{(N+1)}(t-1)}{\kappa(N-t)}>0.

Moreover,

\widetilde{F}_{S}^{(N)}(t-1)=\frac{\kappa(N-t)+1}{\kappa(N-t)}\cdot\frac{\kappa(N-1)}{\kappa(N-1)+1}\widetilde{F}_{S}^{(N+1)}(t-1),

and therefore

\widetilde{F}_{S}^{(N)}(t-1)-\widetilde{F}_{S}^{(N+1)}(t-1)=\frac{\kappa(t-1)}{\kappa(N-1)+1}\Delta_{t}.

It follows that

\widetilde{G}_{N-1}^{(N)}-\widetilde{G}_{N-1}^{(N+1)}=\sum_{t=1}^{N-1}\frac{\kappa(t-1)}{t(1+\alpha)(\kappa(N-1)+1)}\left(\prod_{j=t+1}^{N-1}c_{j}\right)\Delta_{t}.

(25)

On the other hand, by the recursive form of
\widetilde{G}^{(N+1)}
,

\widetilde{G}_{N}^{(N+1)}-\widetilde{G}_{N-1}^{(N+1)}=\frac{\widetilde{F}_{S}^{(N+1)}(N-1)-\widetilde{G}_{N-1}^{(N+1)}}{N(1+\alpha)}.

(26)

Since
1-c_{t}=\frac{1}{t(1+\alpha)}
, we have

\frac{1}{t(1+\alpha)}\prod_{j=t+1}^{N-1}c_{j}=\prod_{j=t+1}^{N-1}c_{j}-\prod_{j=t}^{N-1}c_{j}.

Hence, for every
1\leq s\leq N-1
,

\sum_{t=s}^{N-1}\frac{1}{t(1+\alpha)}\prod_{j=t+1}^{N-1}c_{j}=1-\prod_{j=s}^{N-1}c_{j}.

(27)

Using

\widetilde{F}_{S}^{(N+1)}(t-1)=1+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\Delta_{s}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{N-1}^{(N+1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{t(1+\alpha)}\left(\prod_{j=t+1}^{N-1}c_{j}\right)\left(1+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\Delta_{s}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}c_{j}\right)+\sum_{s=1}^{N-1}\left(1-\prod_{j=s+1}^{N-1}c_{j}\right)\Delta_{s},

where (
27
) was used in the second equality.
Since

\widetilde{F}_{S}^{(N+1)}(N-1)=1+\sum_{s=1}^{N-1}\Delta_{s},

we get

\widetilde{F}_{S}^{(N+1)}(N-1)-\widetilde{G}_{N-1}^{(N+1)}=\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}c_{j}+\sum_{s=1}^{N-1}\left(\prod_{j=s+1}^{N-1}c_{j}\right)\Delta_{s}.

(28)

Substituting (
28
) into
(
26
) gives

\widetilde{G}_{N}^{(N+1)}-\widetilde{G}_{N-1}^{(N+1)}=\frac{1}{N(1+\alpha)}\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}c_{j}+\sum_{t=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{N(1+\alpha)}\left(\prod_{j=t+1}^{N-1}c_{j}\right)\Delta_{t}.

(29)

Subtracting (
25
) from
(
29
), we obtain

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{N}^{(N+1)}-\widetilde{G}_{N-1}^{(N)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N(1+\alpha)}\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}c_{j}+\sum_{t=1}^{N-1}\left[\frac{1}{N(1+\alpha)}-\frac{\kappa(t-1)}{t(1+\alpha)(\kappa(N-1)+1)}\right]\left(\prod_{j=t+1}^{N-1}c_{j}\right)\Delta_{t}.

The coefficient in brackets equals

\frac{t+\kappa(N-t)}{Nt(1+\alpha)(\kappa(N-1)+1)},

which is strictly positive for
1\leq t\leq N-1
. Since
c_{j}>0
for every

j\geq 1
and
\Delta_{t}>0
for every
1\leq t\leq N-1
, we conclude that

\widetilde{G}_{N}^{(N+1)}-\widetilde{G}_{N-1}^{(N)}>0.

Thus
\{\widetilde{G}_{N-1}^{(N)}\}_{N\geq 2}
is strictly increasing.

It remains to compute the limit as
N\to\infty
. We first rewrite each
summand in Gamma-function form. Since

\widetilde{F}_{S}^{(N)}(t-1)=\prod_{j=N-t}^{N-2}\left(1+\frac{a}{j}\right),

we have

\widetilde{F}_{S}^{(N)}(t-1)=\frac{\Gamma(N-1+a)}{\Gamma(N-t+a)}\frac{\Gamma(N-t)}{\Gamma(N-1)}.

(30)

Similarly,

\prod_{j=t+1}^{N-1}c_{j}=\prod_{j=t+1}^{N-1}\frac{j-1+a}{j}=\frac{\Gamma(N-1+a)}{\Gamma(t+a)}\frac{\Gamma(t+1)}{\Gamma(N)}.

(31)

Using
1-a=1/(1+\alpha)
and
\Gamma(t+1)=t\Gamma(t)
, the
t
-th summand

A_{t,N}:=\frac{\widetilde{F}_{S}^{(N)}(t-1)}{t(1+\alpha)}\prod_{j=t+1}^{N-1}c_{j}

can be written as

A_{t,N}=\frac{1-a}{N-1}\left(\frac{\Gamma(N-1+a)}{\Gamma(N-1)}\right)^{2}\frac{\Gamma(t)}{\Gamma(t+a)}\frac{\Gamma(N-t)}{\Gamma(N-t+a)}.

(32)

Therefore,

\widetilde{G}_{N-1}^{(N)}=\sum_{t=1}^{N-1}A_{t,N}.

By the standard Gamma-ratio asymptotic formula,

\frac{\Gamma(x+b)}{\Gamma(x+c)}=x^{b-c}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)\right),\qquad x\to\infty,

we obtain, whenever
t/(N-1)\to x\in(0,1)
,

A_{t,N}=\frac{1-a}{N-1}x^{-a}(1-x)^{-a}\left(1+o(1)\right).

Since
a<1
, the function
x^{-a}(1-x)^{-a}
is integrable on
(0,1)
.
Moreover, the two boundary sums with
t\leq\delta(N-1)
and

t\geq(1-\delta)(N-1)
are bounded by a constant times

\delta^{1-a}
, uniformly in
N
. Hence the preceding pointwise
asymptotics give the Riemann-sum limit

\lim_{N\to\infty}\widetilde{G}_{N-1}^{(N)}=(1-a)\int_{0}^{1}x^{-a}(1-x)^{-a}\,dx.

Using the Beta-function identity,

\int_{0}^{1}x^{-a}(1-x)^{-a}\,dx=B(1-a,1-a)=\frac{\Gamma(1-a)^{2}}{\Gamma(2(1-a))},

we obtain

\lim_{N\to\infty}\widetilde{G}_{N-1}^{(N)}=(1-a)\frac{\Gamma(1-a)^{2}}{\Gamma(2(1-a))}.

Equivalently, since
1-a=1/(1+\alpha)
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\widetilde{G}_{N-1}^{(N)}=\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\,\frac{\Gamma\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{1+\alpha}\bigr)^{2}}{\Gamma\!\bigl(\tfrac{2}{1+\alpha}\bigr)}.

Finally, we analyze the supremum over
\alpha>0
. For

z:=1-a\in(0,1)
, define

g(z):=z\frac{\Gamma(z)^{2}}{\Gamma(2z)}.

By the Weierstrass product formula for the Gamma function,

\Gamma(z)=\frac{e^{-\gamma z}}{z}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{z/n}}{1+z/n},\qquad z>0,

where
\gamma
is the Euler–Mascheroni constant. Hence

g(z)=2\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1+2z/n}{(1+z/n)^{2}}.

Taking logarithms and differentiating term by term on compact subintervals of

(0,1)
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dz}\log g(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2/n}{1+2z/n}-2\frac{1/n}{1+z/n}\right)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{n+2z}-\frac{1}{n+z}\right)

\displaystyle=-2z\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n+z)(n+2z)}<0.

Thus
g(z)
is strictly decreasing in
z\in(0,1)
. Since

z=1/(1+\alpha)
, the limit

\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\,\frac{\Gamma\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{1+\alpha}\bigr)^{2}}{\Gamma\!\bigl(\tfrac{2}{1+\alpha}\bigr)}=g\!\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\right)

is strictly increasing in
\alpha>0
.

As
\alpha\to\infty
, we have
z=1/(1+\alpha)\to 0^{+}
. Using

\Gamma(z)\sim\frac{1}{z},\qquad\Gamma(2z)\sim\frac{1}{2z},\qquad z\to 0^{+},

we obtain

g(z)=z\frac{\Gamma(z)^{2}}{\Gamma(2z)}\longrightarrow 2.

Therefore,

\sup_{\alpha>0}\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\,\frac{\Gamma\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{1+\alpha}\bigr)^{2}}{\Gamma\!\bigl(\tfrac{2}{1+\alpha}\bigr)}=2.

The supremum is not attained at any finite
\alpha>0
, and is approached
only in the limit
\alpha\to\infty
. This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
6.1
.

Let

x_{B}(b)=\int_{[0,1]}x(s,b)\,dF_{S}(s)=p_{S}x(0,b)+\int_{0}^{1}x(s,b)f_{S}(s)\,ds,

and

x_{S}(s)=\int_{[0,1]}x(s,b)\,dF_{B}(b)=p_{B}x(s,1)+\int_{0}^{1}x(s,b)f_{B}(b)\,db.

Starting from Proposition
3.1
,
the buyer-side term is

\displaystyle\Lambda_{B}(x_{B})

\displaystyle:=\int_{[0,1]}\left(b\,x_{B}(b)-\int_{0}^{b}x_{B}(t)\,dt\right)dF_{B}(b)

\displaystyle=p_{B}\left(x_{B}(1)-\int_{0}^{1}x_{B}(t)\,dt\right)+\int_{0}^{1}\left(b\,x_{B}(b)-\int_{0}^{b}x_{B}(t)\,dt\right)f_{B}(b)\,db.

By Fubini,

\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{b}x_{B}(t)\,dt\,f_{B}(b)\,db=\int_{0}^{1}x_{B}(t)\int_{t}^{1}f_{B}(b)\,db\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}x_{B}(t)\bigl(1-F_{B}(t)-p_{B}\bigr)\,dt.

Hence

\displaystyle\Lambda_{B}(x_{B})

\displaystyle=p_{B}x_{B}(1)+\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(bf_{B}(b)-(1-F_{B}(b))\bigr)x_{B}(b)\,db

\displaystyle=p_{B}x_{B}(1)+\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{B}(b)x_{B}(b)f_{B}(b)\,db.

Similarly, the seller-side term is

\displaystyle\Lambda_{S}(x_{S})

\displaystyle:=\int_{[0,1]}\left(s\,x_{S}(s)+\int_{s}^{1}x_{S}(t)\,dt\right)dF_{S}(s)

\displaystyle=p_{S}\int_{0}^{1}x_{S}(t)\,dt+\int_{0}^{1}\left(s\,x_{S}(s)+\int_{s}^{1}x_{S}(t)\,dt\right)f_{S}(s)\,ds.

Again by Fubini,

\int_{0}^{1}\int_{s}^{1}x_{S}(t)\,dt\,f_{S}(s)\,ds=\int_{0}^{1}x_{S}(t)\int_{0}^{t}f_{S}(s)\,ds\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}x_{S}(t)\bigl(F_{S}(t)-p_{S}\bigr)\,dt.

Therefore

\displaystyle\Lambda_{S}(x_{S})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(sf_{S}(s)+F_{S}(s)\bigr)x_{S}(s)\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{S}(s)x_{S}(s)f_{S}(s)\,ds.

Combining the two parts yields

\Lambda_{\text{con-mass}}(x)=p_{B}x_{B}(1)+\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{B}(b)x_{B}(b)f_{B}(b)\,db-\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{S}(s)x_{S}(s)f_{S}(s)\,ds.

Now substitute the formulas for
x_{B}
and
x_{S}
. After expanding and rearranging,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\text{con-mass}}(x)

\displaystyle=p_{S}p_{B}\,x(0,1)+p_{S}\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{B}(b)x(0,b)f_{B}(b)\,db

\displaystyle\qquad+p_{B}\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(1-\phi_{S}(s)\bigr)x(s,1)f_{S}(s)\,ds+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(\phi_{B}(b)-\phi_{S}(s)\bigr)x(s,b)f_{S}(s)f_{B}(b)\,db\,ds.

This is exactly

\Lambda_{\text{con-mass}}(x)=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\bigl(\widehat{\phi}_{B}(b)-\widehat{\phi}_{S}(s)\bigr)x(s,b)\,dF_{S}(s)\,dF_{B}(b),

with the endpoint conventions
\widehat{\phi}_{S}(0)=0
and
\widehat{\phi}_{B}(1)=1
.

The expression for
\mathrm{GFT}(x)
follows from the same decomposition of the product measure:

dF_{S}(s)dF_{B}(b)=p_{S}p_{B}\,\delta_{(0,1)}+p_{S}\,\delta_{0}(ds)f_{B}(b)\,db+p_{B}\,f_{S}(s)\,ds\,\delta_{1}(db)+f_{S}(s)f_{B}(b)\,ds\,db.

∎

Proof of Theorem
6.2
.

We show that for every
\epsilon>0
there exists a pair of distributions such that
\frac{\mathrm{SB}}{\mathrm{FB}}<\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
. The proof proceeds by deriving the closed-form analytical expressions for
FB
and
SB
, extracting their asymptotic limits as
c\to 0^{+}
, and finally evaluating the ratio as
\alpha\to\infty
.

First-best benchmark and asymptotics.

By definition, the ideal expected surplus FB is:

\displaystyle\text{FB}(\alpha,c)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{b}(b-s)f_{B}(b)f_{S}(s)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}b

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}F_{S}(b)(1-F_{B}(b))\mathrm{d}b

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{c^{2}}{(c+(1+\alpha)(1-b))(c+(1+\alpha)b)}\right)^{a}\mathrm{d}b.

We analyze the asymptotic behavior of
\mathrm{FB}(\alpha,c)
as
c\to 0^{+}
. Factoring out
c^{2a}
, the dominant term of the integral is governed by the
c\to 0^{+}
limit of the denominator:

\lim_{c\to 0^{+}}\frac{\text{FB}(\alpha,c)}{c^{2a}}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{((1+\alpha)^{2}\cdot b(1-b))^{a}}\mathrm{d}b=(1+\alpha)^{-2a}\int_{0}^{1}b^{-a}(1-b)^{-a}\mathrm{d}b.

Structure of the second-best allocation.

We first demonstrate that the optimal allocation rule restricts trade exclusively to the boundaries

{\cal T}:=\{(s,b):s=0,\ b\geq 1-y\}\cup\{(s,b):s\leq y,\ b=1\},

where
y
is the truncation point.

By Proposition
6.1
, for the present family the second-best problem is

\max_{x}\ \mathrm{GFT}(x)\qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad\Lambda(x)\geq 0,

with

\displaystyle\mathrm{GFT}(x)

\displaystyle=P^{2}x(0,1)+P\int_{0}^{1}b\,x(0,b)f_{B}(b)\,db+P\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)x(s,1)f_{S}(s)\,ds

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(b-s)x(s,b)f_{S}(s)f_{B}(b)\,db\,ds,

and

\displaystyle\Lambda(x)

\displaystyle=P^{2}x(0,1)+P\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{B}(b)x(0,b)f_{B}(b)\,db+P\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(1-\phi_{S}(s)\bigr)x(s,1)f_{S}(s)\,ds

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(\phi_{B}(b)-\phi_{S}(s)\bigr)x(s,b)f_{S}(s)f_{B}(b)\,db\,ds.

Substituting the present family gives

\phi_{B}(b)=-\frac{c+b}{\alpha},\qquad 1-\phi_{S}(s)=-\frac{c+1-s}{\alpha},\qquad\phi_{B}(b)-\phi_{S}(s)=-\frac{(b-s)+2c+1+\alpha}{\alpha},

for
0<b<1
,
0<s<1
, and
0<s<b<1
, respectively, while the atom
(0,1)
contributes
1
to both
\mathrm{GFT}
and
\Lambda
.

Thus, after factoring out the positive measure elements
P^{2}
,
Pf_{B}(b)\,db
,
Pf_{S}(s)\,ds
, and
f_{S}(s)f_{B}(b)\,db\,ds
, the pointwise coefficients in the objective and in the feasibility constraint are

\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}\text{location}&\text{coefficient in }\mathrm{GFT}&\text{coefficient in }\Lambda\\
\hline\cr(0,1)&1&1\\[2.84526pt]
(0,b),\ 0<b<1&b&-\dfrac{c+b}{\alpha}\\[5.69054pt]
(s,1),\ 0<s<1&1-s&-\dfrac{c+1-s}{\alpha}\\[5.69054pt]
(s,b),\ 0<s<b<1&b-s&-\dfrac{(b-s)+2c+1+\alpha}{\alpha}\end{array}

Hence
(0,1)
is the unique location at which increasing trade relaxes the feasibility constraint. Therefore any optimum sets
x(0,1)=1
. After this point mass is fully used, every additional trade raises
\mathrm{GFT}
but lowers
\Lambda
, so the question is where the available feasibility slack should be spent.

We first compare two points on the seller boundary
b=1
. Let
0<s_{1}<s_{2}<1
. Suppose we increase trade probability at
(s_{1},1)
by
\Delta_{1}
and decrease trade probability at
(s_{2},1)
by
\Delta_{2}
, choosing them so that the feasibility constraint is unchanged:

\frac{c+1-s_{1}}{\alpha}\Delta_{1}=\frac{c+1-s_{2}}{\alpha}\Delta_{2}.

The corresponding change in the objective is

\displaystyle\Delta\mathrm{GFT}

\displaystyle=(1-s_{1})\Delta_{1}-(1-s_{2})\Delta_{2}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{1}\left[(1-s_{1})-(1-s_{2})\frac{c+1-s_{1}}{c+1-s_{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{c(s_{2}-s_{1})}{c+1-s_{2}}\Delta_{1}>0.

Thus, for a fixed loss of feasibility slack, trading with a lower seller type strictly dominates trading with a higher seller type. By symmetry, on the buyer boundary
s=0
, a higher buyer type strictly dominates a lower buyer type. Therefore, whenever trade occurs on the boundaries, it must take the form

\{(s,1):s\leq y\}\cup\{(0,b):b\geq 1-y\}

for some cutoff
y\in[0,1]
.

We next compare boundary trade with interior trade. Fix a boundary point
(s,1)
with
0<s<1
, and an interior point
(s^{\prime},b^{\prime})
with
0<s^{\prime}<b^{\prime}<1
. Write

u:=b^{\prime}-s^{\prime}\in(0,1).

Suppose we increase trade probability at
(s,1)
by
\Delta_{B}
and decrease trade probability at
(s^{\prime},b^{\prime})
by
\Delta_{I}
, again choosing them so that the feasibility constraint is unchanged:

\frac{c+1-s}{\alpha}\Delta_{B}=\frac{u+2c+1+\alpha}{\alpha}\Delta_{I}.

Then the induced change in the objective is

\displaystyle\Delta\mathrm{GFT}

\displaystyle=(1-s)\Delta_{B}-u\Delta_{I}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{B}\left[(1-s)-u\frac{c+1-s}{u+2c+1+\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta_{B}}{u+2c+1+\alpha}\Bigl[(1-s)(u+2c+1+\alpha)-u(c+1-s)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta_{B}}{u+2c+1+\alpha}\Bigl[(1-s)(2c+1+\alpha)-cu\Bigr].

For fixed
s
, the last expression is strictly decreasing in
u
. Hence the strongest interior competitor is obtained at the maximal surplus level
u\uparrow 1
, i.e. in the limit
b^{\prime}\to 1
,
s^{\prime}\to 0
. Therefore the boundary point
(s,1)
dominates every interior point whenever

(1-s)(2c+1+\alpha)-c>0.

Let
y_{1}
be the boundary cutoff at which equality holds. Then

(1-y_{1})(2c+1+\alpha)=c,

so

y_{1}=\frac{c+1+\alpha}{2c+1+\alpha}.

Hence every boundary point with
s<y_{1}
yields a strictly larger increase in the objective than any interior point for the same decrease in feasibility slack. It follows that an optimal allocation cannot place positive mass in the interior before exhausting the boundary segment up to
y_{1}
.

Budget exhaustion and second-best surplus.

We now verify the feasibility of reaching
y_{1}
. Let the actual trade interval length on the boundary be
t
, and let
y=1-t
be the length of the non-trading gap. For the seller boundary, the expected virtual surplus is:

P\int_{0}^{t}(1-\phi_{S}(s))f_{S}(s)\mathrm{d}s=P\int_{0}^{t}(1-s)f_{S}(s)\mathrm{d}s-P\int_{0}^{t}F_{S}(s)\mathrm{d}s.

Applying integration by parts yields:

\int_{0}^{t}(1-s)f_{S}(s)\mathrm{d}s=(1-t)F_{S}(t)-P+\int_{0}^{t}F_{S}(s)\mathrm{d}s.

Thus, the boundary virtual surplus evaluates to
P[(1-t)F_{S}(t)-P]
. Equating the total virtual surplus to zero for budget balance gives
P^{2}+2P[(1-t)F_{S}(t)-P]=0
, which reduces to
2y\cdot F_{S}(1-y)=P
. Substituting the respective distribution functions, we have
2y\left(\frac{c}{c+(1+\alpha)y}\right)^{a}=\left(\frac{c}{c+1+\alpha}\right)^{a}
.

We now verify this budget exhaustion. As derived from the budget balance condition, the actual gap
y
is the root of the continuous function
g(y):=2y-\left(\frac{c+(1+\alpha)y}{c+1+\alpha}\right)^{a}=0
. We evaluate
g(\cdot)
at the critical threshold
y_{1}
:

\displaystyle g(1-y_{1})

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{c}{2c+1+\alpha}\right)-\left(\frac{c+(1+\alpha)\frac{c}{2c+1+\alpha}}{c+1+\alpha}\right)^{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{2c}{2c+1+\alpha}-\left(\frac{2c}{2c+1+\alpha}\right)^{a}.

Let
z=\frac{2c}{2c+1+\alpha}
. Since
c>0
and
\alpha>0
, we have
0<z<1
. Moreover, since

a=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}<1
, it follows that
z<z^{a}
. Therefore

g(1-y_{1})=z-z^{a}<0.

On the other hand,

g(1/2)=1-\left(\frac{c+\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}{c+1+\alpha}\right)^{a}>0.

Hence, by the Intermediate Value Theorem, the budget-balancing gap
y
satisfies

y\in(1-y_{1},1/2).

Equivalently, the corresponding boundary trading length
t=1-y
satisfies

1/2<t<y_{1}.

Thus the feasibility constraint binds at a cutoff
t
that is strictly below the critical cutoff
y_{1}
at which interior points could become relevant in the marginal comparison. For every boundary point in the traded segments

\{(s,1):0\leq s\leq t\}\cup\{(0,b):1-t\leq b\leq 1\},

the gain in
\mathrm{GFT}
per unit loss of feasibility slack is strictly larger than that of any interior point
(s,b)
with
0<s<b<1
. Consequently, the optimal second-best allocation is

x^{\star}(s,b)=\begin{cases}1,&(s,b)\in\{(0,1)\}\cup\{(s,1):0\leq s\leq 1-y\}\cup\{(0,b):y\leq b\leq 1\},\\[2.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Therefore, the actual Second-Best expected surplus is the sum of the point mass surplus at the boundary
(0,1)
and the actual surplus from the other boundary trades:

\text{SB}(\alpha,c)=P^{2}+2P\int_{0}^{1-y}(1-s)f_{S}(s)\mathrm{d}s.

Applying the same integration by parts, this expands to
P^{2}+2P\left[yF_{S}(1-y)-P+\int_{0}^{1-y}F_{S}(s)\mathrm{d}s\right]
. By enforcing the budget balance condition
2yF_{S}(1-y)=P
, the discrete algebraic terms cancel (
P^{2}+P^{2}-2P^{2}=0
), collapsing the surplus to a pure integral:

\text{SB}(\alpha,c)=2P\int_{0}^{1-y}F_{S}(s)\mathrm{d}s=2\left(\frac{c}{c+1+\alpha}\right)^{a}\int_{0}^{1-y}\frac{c^{a}}{(c+(1+\alpha)(1-s))^{a}}\mathrm{d}s.

Evaluating this fractional integral yields
c^{a}\left[(c+1+\alpha)^{1-a}-(c+(1+\alpha)y)^{1-a}\right]
. Factoring out
(c+1+\alpha)^{1-a}
and utilizing the relation
\frac{c+(1+\alpha)y}{c+1+\alpha}=(2y)^{1/a}
derived from the budget constraint, the expression becomes:

\mathrm{SB}(\alpha,c)=2(c+1+\alpha)\left(\frac{c}{c+1+\alpha}\right)^{2a}\left[1-(2y)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right].

As
c\to 0^{+}
, the budget constraint
2y=\left(\frac{c+(1+\alpha)y}{c+1+\alpha}\right)^{a}
simplifies to
2y\sim y^{a}
, yielding
y\sim\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{1+\alpha}
.
Substituting the invariant
1-(2y)^{1/\alpha}=1/2
, we obtain:

\text{SB}(\alpha,c)\sim 2(1+\alpha)\cdot c^{2a}(1+\alpha)^{-2a}\cdot\frac{1}{2}=c^{2a}(1+\alpha)^{1-2a}.

Limiting approximation ratio.

Then, we define the limit ratio
R_{0}(\alpha)=\lim_{c\to 0^{+}}\frac{\text{SB}(\alpha,c)}{\text{FB}(\alpha,c)}
.
Dividing the asymptotic expression of SB by FB, the
c^{2a}
terms cancel. Recalling that
-2a=-\frac{2\alpha}{1+\alpha}
, we simplify the remaining
(1+\alpha)
exponents:

R_{0}(\alpha)=\frac{(1+\alpha)^{1-2a}}{(1+\alpha)^{-2a}B\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha},\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\right)}=\frac{1+\alpha}{B\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha},\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\right)}.

Expressing the Beta function in terms of Gamma functions
\Gamma(\cdot)
using the identity
B(z,z)=\frac{\Gamma(z)^{2}}{\Gamma(2z)}
, we acquire the ratio by the parameter
\alpha
:

R_{0}(\alpha)=(1+\alpha)\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{2}{1+\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha}\right)^{2}}.

Finally, we evaluate
R_{0}(\alpha)
as
\alpha\to\infty
. Let
z=\frac{1}{1+\alpha}
. As
\alpha\to\infty
,
z\to 0^{+}
. The ratio function rewrites to:

R_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{z}\frac{\Gamma(2z)}{\Gamma(z)^{2}}.

To resolve the
\frac{\infty}{\infty}
singularity at
z\to 0^{+}
, we apply the elementary recurrence relation of the Gamma function:
\Gamma(z)=\frac{\Gamma(z+1)}{z}
,
\Gamma(2z)=\frac{\Gamma(2z+1)}{2z}
.
Substituting these into the ratio:

R_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{z}\cdot\frac{\frac{\Gamma(2z+1)}{2z}}{\left(\frac{\Gamma(z+1)}{z}\right)^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(2z+1)}{\Gamma(z+1)^{2}}.

Because the Gamma function is strictly continuous for positive real numbers and
\Gamma(1)=1
, we can now directly evaluate the limit as
z\to 0^{+}
:

\lim_{z\to 0^{+}}R_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(1)}{(\Gamma(1))^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}.

Therefore, taking the sequential limits
\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\lim_{c\to 0^{+}}\frac{\text{SB}(\alpha,c)}{\text{FB}(\alpha,c)}
yields precisely
1/2
, completing the proof.
∎
