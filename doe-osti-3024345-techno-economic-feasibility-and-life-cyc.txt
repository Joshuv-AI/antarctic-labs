Executive Summary
The Propane Education and Research Council (PERC) has engaged with the National Laboratory
of the Rockies (NLR) to generate information that is essential to understanding the current and
future landscape for renewable propane (RP) and the value proposition for recovery of RP from
existing and planned biorefineries. This work provides a 2025 update to NLR’s 2022 report,
Techno-Economic, Feasibility, and Life-Cycle Analysis of Renewable Propane (the “2022 RP
Report”), and reassesses the technical and economic value proposition for RP in light of evolving
markets, policies, and project configurations.

As renewable diesel (RD) and sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) production accelerates,
hydroprocessed esters and fatty acids (HEFA) biorefineries represent the most mature pathway
and primary near-term source of RP. This updated analysis focuses on RP recovery from HEFA
facilities while also surveying other biorefinery platforms that can contribute to RP supply over
the longer term.

This 2025 update builds on the 2022 RP report and adds several enhancements:

 • Updated techno-economic analysis (TEA) assumptions by escalating capital and
 operating expenditures for RP recovery, storage, and handling to 2024 dollars and
 updating chemical and fuel prices and tax-credit valuations using 2020–2025 data.
 • Expanded policy coverage by including into the analysis the Inflation Reduction Act
 (IRA) Section 45Z Clean Fuel Production Credit in addition to the Renewable Fuel
 Standard (RFS) and California Low Carbon Fuel Standard (LCFS).
 • Broader HEFA biorefinery configuration by evaluating RP recovery from RD- and SAF-
 focused HEFA facilities at two processing capacities: 3,000 barrels per day (BPD) (41.4
 million gallons per year, MGPY) and 75,000 BPD (1,034.8 MGPY) of tallow.
 • Integrated impact assessment of RP recovery on the HEFA liquid fuels’ carbon intensity
 (CI) and tax credits.

Overall, the updated results confirm that RP recovery from HEFA biorefineries remains
attractive under a wide range of conditions:

 • Base-case economics: For a 3,000 BPD (41.4 MGPY) of feedstock (tallow) RD-focused
 HEFA facility generating 1 million standard cubic feet (MM scf) per day of fuel gas that
 subsequently recovers 3.5 MGPY of RP, payback periods are ~18 months based on a
 total installed cost (TIC, for RP recovery equipment) and ~36 months based on total
 capital investment (TCI, for RP recovery project); the analogous case recovering RP (4.3
 MGPY) from a SAF-focused HEFA facility is slightly shorter.
 • Role of policy incentives: In the base case, RINs are the largest single revenue source,
 followed by RP sales, LCFS and 45Z credits.
 • Range of outcomes: Across the sensitivity analysis, the best-case payback is ~2 months
 (large RD-focused HEFA plant with favorable CAPEX and policy), while the worst-case
 payback is ~91 months (~8 years) for a small SAF-focused plant accounting for RP
 revenue offsetting reduced HEFA liquid-fuel tax credits.

 v

 • Sensitivity analysis shows that CAPEX magnitude is the single largest driver of payback
 period. As expected, economies of scale substantially reduce payback periods by 14-33
 months when scaling RP recovery processing capacity from 1 to 25 MMscf/day of fuel
        gas (generated from a 3,000 to 75,000 BPD [41.4-1,034.8 MGPY] HEFA facility).
 • RIN prices strongly affect payback. At a worst-case $0.38/RIN, payback extends by 8–21
 months; current valuation (2025) is closer to the base-case assumption (~$1.05/RIN).
 • RP CI governs credit eligibility, at 60 gCO2e/MJ (~28% emissions reduction,
 corresponding to the highest CI among California Air Resources Board [CARB]-certified
 RP production pathways) (1), RP does not qualify for D5 RINs or 45Z credits, and RP
 recovery becomes uneconomic. Therefore, ensuring RP’s CI is below ~46 gCO2e/MJ is
 critical to achieve ≥50% reduction and qualify for RIN credits and maintain the RP
 recovery value proposition.
 • Diverting HEFA fuel gas to RP recovery and replacing it with NG increases the CI of
 HEFA liquid fuels by ~2.6–3.1 gCO2e/MJ, reducing liquid-fuel tax credits by about
 $0.05–$0.15/gal, depending on feedstock type, product slate, and RP recovery.
 Incorporating the resulting reductions in HEFA liquid fuel tax credits increases payback
 40% for the RD-focused facility and nearly doubles it for the SAF-focused facility.
 However, in both cases, incorporating RP recovery generates incremental positive net
 income, reinforcing its role as a value-enhancing co-product strategy.
 • Section 45Z rounding rules create stepwise effects in tax credit valuation: when RP
 recovery increases the CI of HEFA liquid fuels, driven by the substitution of fuel gas
 with natural gas, enough to cross one of these steps, 45Z credit value for liquid fuels can
 fall sharply. Accordingly, RP recovery should be evaluated within an integrated
 biorefinery framework that captures interactions, rather than as a stand-alone, isolated
 project.

HEFA facilities are expected to remain the dominant near-term potential source of RP, with
current and planned plants offering a sizable co-product opportunity that can materially
strengthen project economics. Other pathways (e.g., gasification–FT, alcohol-to-jet, and
advanced biorefineries) may add RP supply over time but are unlikely to contribute substantial
volumes in the near term. At the same time, the expansion of clean-fuel programs beyond
California (e.g., Oregon, Washington, New Mexico) creates additional credit opportunities for
RP when used as a transportation fuel. Overall, the 2025 update confirms RP recovery from
HEFA biorefineries as an economically attractive value-enhancing co-product strategy, while
emphasizing its dependence on policy design, CI performance, and project scale.

1 Introduction and Background
Biofuel production is concentrated in production of renewable diesel (RD) and jet fuels, also
known as sustainable aviation fuel (SAF), where demand growth and industry emissions-
reduction goals are strongest. As shown in Figure 1, RD output has expanded accordingly, with
the Hydroprocessed Esters and Fatty Acids (HEFA) technology supplying the majority of
volumes (2). HEFA upgrades ester- and fatty-acid-based feedstocks (vegetable oils, animal fats,
algal oil, and waste greases) via hydroprocessing to diesel, jet, and naphtha. Additional RD/SAF
routes—gasification with Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (GFT) and alcohol-to-jet (ATJ)— are in
demonstration or early commercial deployment and, like HEFA, can potentially co-produce
propane and butanes. These pathways, including HEFA, co-produce a light-ends gas stream (C1–
C5), with propane and butanes comprising a major weight fraction (about 5 to 8 wt.% of the
process output); these light ends are the focus of this project.

2 Highlights From the 2022 RP Report
The NLR’s 2022 RP Report describes the HEFA process and mechanisms for generation of
propane in the hydroprocessing step. HEFA typically uses two hydrotreaters (HT): HT1
performs the triglyceride “propane cleave”, and HT2 hydroisomerizes/hydrocracks to tune fuel
properties and the SAF/RD split. The report tested whether raising HT2 severity
(temperature/space time/H₂ treat rate) could increase RP production. Modeled incremental
propane from HT2 is small: ~0.76 wt% at low severity to ~2.08 wt% at high severity (basis: HT2
feed). Aspen modeling corroborates that RP gains are minor. Butane increases more sharply with
HT2 severity (up to ~4 wt%), suggesting LPG (propane+butane) recovery may have more value
than chasing small propane-only increments when incrementing HT2 severity. Thus, increasing
HT2 severity is unlikely to deliver economically meaningful additional RP.

To define the value proposition of RP recovery, the 2022 RP Report assesses revenue sources
available when RP is produced as a co-product, including market sales and federal/state tax
incentives. The report presents the policy framework that supports biofuel production, including:

 • Renewable Fuel Standard (RFS) and Renewable Identification Number (RIN):
 Under the RFS, obligated parties—gasoline and diesel refiners and importers—comply
 by blending renewable fuels or acquiring RINs to demonstrate compliance at year-end.
 RINs are created when eligible renewable fuel is produced and can be traded. Obligated
 parties may buy fuel with attached RINs or purchase separate RINs. Accordingly, RP
 producers eligible for RIN generation can sell those credits to obligated parties under the
 RFS. EPA has approved several HEFA-based pathways to produce renewable liquified
 petroleum gas (LPG, a fuel mixture of propane and other components) from HEFA
 processes to generate D5 RINs (4).
 • Low Carbon Fuel Standard (LCFS): California’s LCFS requires stepwise carbon
 intensity (CI) reductions and runs a Credit Clearance Market (CCM) with a price cap
 ($221.67 per ton CO2e in 2021) (5). At the time of the 2022 RP Report publication,
 CARB had approved nine RP/LPG pathways (all hydrotreating fats/oils/greases at REG
 Geismar) with CIs ranging from 20.5 to 43.5 gCO2e/MJ.
 • States clean fuel program (CFP): Besides California, other states have adopted or
 proposed similar clean fuel programs that support renewable fuel production. Oregon’s
 CFP program requires annual CI reduction by 10% in the statewide fuel pool’s CI rating
 from 2015 levels by 2025. Washington’s CFP requires fuel suppliers to reduce the CI of
 fuels to 20% below 2017 levels by 2038.
To assess the value proposition of RP co-production in HEFA facilities, the 2022 RP Report
estimated the capital expenditure (CAPEX) and operating expenditure (OPEX) of RP recovery,
storage, and handling under a greenfield assumption (all new equipment). The base case
considered a 3,000 barrels per day (BPD) (41.4 million gallons per year, MGPY) tallow to RD
process (about 20 MGPY) that generates 1 million standard cubic feet per day (MMscf/day) of
fuel gas, rich in propane. A process simulation was used to quantify recoverable RP meeting
LPG or H-5 specifications, establish mass and energy balances, and size major equipment.
Results indicate the base case can produce 3.5 MGPY of RP (H-5 quality: ~97 vol% propane and
2 vol% butanes).

 2

Techno-economic analysis (TEA) considers two revenue streams: (i) sales from RP as
transportation fuel and (ii) policy incentives, D5 RINs under the RFS and credits under
California’s LCFS. Revenues use 2019-2021 average valuations for wholesale conventional
propane, D5 RINs, and LCFS credits. Using the payback period (based on total installed
equipment cost) as the evaluation metric, the base case (recover 3.5 MGPY of RP H-5 quality)
yields a payback period of 14 months. Sensitivity analysis on D5 RIN valuations, plant scale,
CAPEX, OPEX, RP carbon intensity, and LCFS price shows the payback period is most
sensitive to D5 RIN valuation and plant size.

The analysis also assessed recovering LPG (~84 vol% propane and ~15 vol% butanes). Avoiding
the propane purification column, and its associated CAPEX and OPEX, shortens the payback to
5 months, about 9 months faster than producing HD-5 quality RP. TEA considers revenue from
RINs and LCFS. However, to generate RINs the LPG must be used as a transportation fuel and
meet motor-fuel specifications—namely ASTM D1835 (6), under special-duty propane (<2.5
vol% butane) type equivalent to HD-5 propane, tailored to meet needs of internal combustion
engines. California Air Resources Board specifications for LPG specify a maximum of 2.5 vol%
of butanes and heavier (7). Thus, based on these requirements, LPG, at the composition
estimated from the process simulations would not qualify for RINs or LCFS.

The 2022 RP Report presents an overview of alternative renewable fuel processes, besides
HEFA, from which RP can be co-produced. This overview includes feedstock availability,
operating and announced facilities, pilot scale efforts, and potential RP production. These
findings established the strong baseline value proposition for RP recovery from HEFA, which
this 2025 update revisits under revised market conditions and policy frameworks.

3 Update to the Value Proposition for Renewable
  Propane
Since the 2022 RP Report, fuel prices, credit valuations, and renewable fuel policies have
significantly changed. The current analysis revises the value proposition for RP recovery relative
to the 2022 RP Report by:
 • Updating RIN and LCFS valuations and propane price assumptions to reflect 2020–2025
 observations
 • Updating operating and equipment cost inputs to 2024 dollars
 • Including the IRA Section 45Z Clean Fuel Production Credit as an additional revenue
 source
 • Expanding analysis to evaluate a HEFA case configured to maximize SAF production
 with RP recovery
 • Extended system boundaries to estimate the impacts of RP recovery on HEFA liquid-fuel
 CI and the associated tax-credit qualifications.

IRA Section 45Z Clean Fuel Production Credit: The Inflation Reduction Act’s Section 45Z
Clean Fuel Production Credit applies to fuel produced beginning Jan 1, 2025, with the credit
window extended by the 2025 “One Big Beautiful Bill Act” (OBBBA) through Dec 31, 2029.
45Z covers both aviation and non-aviation transportation fuels. Eligible non-SAF categories
include low-GHG biodiesel, butanol, diesel, dimethyl ether, ethanol, gasoline, hydrogen, LPG
(meeting ASTM D1835), methanol, and NG (13, 14). To qualify for the 45Z credit, eligible
transportation fuels must have an emissions rate of no more than 50 kg CO2e per MMBTU and
must be produced in the U.S. (15). Section 45Z offers a $1.00/gallon tax credit for aviation and
non-aviation fuels that qualify for credits. Greater credit values are provided for fuels with lower
emissions rates. Emission rates need to be calculated using either the most recent 45ZCF-
GREET model or the CORSIA methodology (14, 16).

Initial guidance on Section 45Z includes rules for rounding emissions factors to the nearest
multiple of 0.1, which can affect credit calculations. Under these rules, certain fuels with
emissions rates at or below the qualifying threshold of 50 kg CO2e/MMBTU may not generate a
credit if rounding reduces their emissions factor to 0.0, effectively disqualifying them from credit
eligibility (14, 16).

3.2 Economics and Sustainability
The present TEA extends the 2022 RP analysis by applying NLR’s updated HEFA models and

Payback Period
To quantify the value proposition of recovering RP, this analysis uses the payback period as the
primary metric. The payback period indicates the time required for the biorefinery to recover its
capital investment in the RP recovery process. The average payback period is calculated, as in
the 2022 RP Report, by dividing the total installed cost (TIC) or total capital investment (TCI) by
the annual cash flow generated by the recovery of RP (net income as shown in Figure 7). As
noted earlier, recovering RP from fuel gas requires the biorefinery to purchase supplemental NG
to meet the internal conversion process energy demands. Accordingly, the annual cash flow
includes the additional NG expenditures required by the HEFA process.

The TIC-based payback period for the base case is estimated at 18 months (Table 3),
approximately four months longer than reported in the 2022 RP Report. This difference is
driven by updated input prices, RP selling prices, and revised tax credit valuations. When
calculated on a TCI basis, the payback period increases to 36 months, reflecting the
additional project-development costs covered under the TCI.

3.2.2 RP Recovery From a HEFA Process Focused on SAF
Using HEFA process models, the flow rate and composition of fuel gas produced under a SAF-
maximizing configuration were estimated. The SAF-focused HEFA case generates 1.21
MMscf/day of fuel gas, compared to 1.00 MMscf/day in the RD-focused base case. SAF-range
(C8–C16) production requires greater carbon chain cracking than renewable diesel (C8–C23),
necessitating higher hydrocracking severity and consequently leading to increased formation of
light hydrocarbons (C1–C4) (2). Thus, the SAF-oriented fuel gas contains approximately 50%
more butanes and about 13% less propane relative to the RD-focused case. When this
information is incorporated into the RP recovery simulation, the estimated RP production is 4.3
MGPY—higher than the 3.5 MGPY estimated for the RD-focused case.

The economic results for RP recovery from the SAF-focused HEFA case are presented in Figure
8. Natural gas expenses are higher in the SAF-focused configuration than in the RD case because
the difference between the fuel-gas generated in the HEFA process and the residue-gas stream
recycled back to the unit is larger, increasing the required NG makeup. The higher RP
production in the SAF-focused case results in greater revenue, offsetting the additional NG costs
and yielding an incremental $2.1 MM in annual earnings when compared to the RD-focused
HEFA case. Consequently, the SAF-focused configuration slightly strengthens the overall
business case for RP recovery relative to the HEFA biorefinery RD-focused configuration.

In addition to assessing revenues and tax credits attributable solely to RP recovery and sales, as
well as the associated payback period for RP recovery investments, it is essential to evaluate the
broader process impacts of RP recovery within the HEFA facility. Specifically, diverting fuel gas
that would otherwise meet HEFA process energy demand to enable RP recovery, and using
natural gas to supply that energy, can increase the CI of the HEFA liquid fuels, thereby
diminishing the value of associated tax credits. These system level impacts must be accounted
for when evaluating the overall RP recovery value proposition, and it is detailed in the following
section 3.2.4. The impact of offsetting reductions in HEFA liquid fuel’s tax credits resulting
from RP recovery is illustrated in the bottom rows of the tornado plots in Figure 9 and Figure 10

3.2.4 Impact of RP Recovery on HEFA Liquid Fuels CI and Associated Tax
 Credits
By recovering RP from the fuel gas, the biorefinery will most likely need to import NG to meet
heating demands that would otherwise be satisfied by the fuel gas. Using conventional NG as
process fuel increases the CI of the HEFA liquid fuels, which in turn reduces the tax credits for

 17

which these fuels are eligible and thereby affects overall biorefinery revenue. Therefore,
quantifying the impact of RP recovery on the CI of HEFA-derived liquid fuels and associated tax
credits is essential to accurately assess the value proposition of RP recovery from HEFA
biorefineries.

To estimate the CI of the liquid fuels, we used the 45ZCF-GREET model (Rev. May 2025) (35),
which is specifically designed to quantify emissions for claiming IRA 45Z tax credits, together
with mass and energy data from the process simulations.

For cases in which RP is recovered, the makeup NG required to supply process fuel—otherwise
provided by the fuel gas—is entered in 45ZCF-GREET as fossil NG. The model also allows
producers to substitute NG with renewable NG (RNG), sourced from landfill gas, wastewater
sludge, or animal manure. In these pathways, landfill gas is captured via a gas collection system
and upgraded to RNG, whereas wastewater sludge and animal manure are converted to RNG via
anaerobic digestion (36). In the present analysis, we consider only fossil NG.

For cases where RP is NOT recovered, the fuel gas produced is used to meet the HEFA process
energy requirements. In some scenarios, the energy content of the fuel gas is sufficient to cover
all HEFA process heating needs, as estimated by process simulations, with a surplus remaining
(for the base case, about 40% of the energy demand). Publicly available information on HEFA
facility operations (37–39) indicates that excess fuel gas can be used in several ways: (i) as
combustion fuel to supply energy for hydrogen production, (ii) as feedstock to SMR unit for
hydrogen production, or (iii) as fuel for an off-site process, which does not generate a co-product
credit. The specific fuel gas utilization strategy depends on process design, operating campaign,
and the fuel gas flow rate and composition. Because our HEFA process simulations do not
include a hydrogen production unit, and 45ZCF-GREET does not allow modification of
hydrogen production operating conditions,1 the CI impact from using excess fuel gas (beyond
internal heating demands) as a fuel or feedstock for hydrogen production was not quantified.
While this impact is expected to fall within the sensitivity bounds of this explorative analysis,
assessing it will require additional modeling and is identified as a topic for future work.

The Section 45Z rounding rule for the emissions factor requires the emissions factor to be
rounded to the nearest 0.1 (14, 16).2 Thus, in practice, changes in CI, and thus credits taxes per
gallon of fuel, only occur in steps. In our calculations, the CI increase for the SAF-focused
biorefinery with and without RP recovery crosses one of these steps, reducing the 45Z tax credit
per gallon, whereas the CI change for the RD-focused biorefinery does not, leaving its 45Z credit
unchanged. Consequently, the RD-focused case experiences no reduction in earned 45Z tax
credits, whereas the SAF-focused case incurs a measurable decline in tax-credit-derived revenue.
Regardless of whether a facility is configured to maximize RD or SAF production, these results
underscore the importance of evaluating the impact of RP recovery on the HEFA liquid fuels’ CI
and associated tax credits. It is also important to note that 45Z tax credits are currently available
only for 2025-2029.

Even after accounting for the reduction in HEFA liquid fuels’ tax credits, implementing RP
recovery still generates positive net income. When this reduction is incorporated into the
payback period calculations, on top of the associated RP recovery CAPEX and OPEX
requirements, the payback increases by approximately 40% for the RD-focused biorefinery and
nearly doubles for the SAF-focused biorefinery (Figure 9 and Figure 10).

3.3 Hydrogen Production From RP and LPG
Dedicated hydrogen production is primarily performed via the steam methane reforming (SMR)
of NG, which represents 76% of global hydrogen production. The balance of the dedicated
global hydrogen production relies on coal (23%), with a small fraction using renewable sources
such as water electrolysis, which accounts for ~1-2% (2). Hydrogen can be produced via steam
reforming of light hydrocarbons like methane, ethane, propane, and butane mixtures. One option
explored in the 2022 RP Report is the use of RP or LPG to produce hydrogen for sale. The report
concluded that due to the high cost of propane when compared to NG, use of RP as substitute
feedstock for NG appears difficult to achieve economic feasibility for industrial hydrogen
productions. Additionally, using propane in an existing methane reformer is technically
challenging without major modifications. Propane’s much higher hydrogen yield would either
overload downstream equipment (requiring new CAPEX and higher OPEX) at the same feed
rate, or, if the feed rate is reduced to keep hydrogen output constant, the reformer would operate
outside its design space velocity, necessitating reactor redesign or replacement. Thus, selling RP
as a transportation fuel offers a stronger economic value proposition, particularly when tax
credits such as RINs, LCFS, and 45Z are taken into account (29).

For the 2023–2025 RFS standards, EPA clarified that its life cycle assessment estimates are
based on average domestic fuel production and therefore do not reflect the effects of additional
energy technologies (e.g., RNG, CCS, or renewable electricity) on fuel CI or emission rates. This
may be extended to RP and renewable LPG. Nevertheless, EPA acknowledges that such
technologies do affect the life cycle emissions of biofuels (40). As discussed in Section 3.2.4,
HEFA fuel gas can be used not only to meet HEFA process heating requirements but also as a
fuel or as a feedstock for hydrogen production. Under Section 45Z, however, off-site alternative
hydrogen production can only be modeled using the 45VH2-GREET model (Rev. May 2025)

to grow substantially in the upcoming years (Figure 14). The other renewable fuel technologies
with high TRL will most likely utilize, in the near-term, cellulosic biomass and waste resources.

HEFA have been announced and are under planning or construction. Depending on the specific
technology configuration and prevailing market conditions, RP could also emerge as a valuable
co-product from the alcohol to jet, gasification with Fischer-Tropsch, and pyrolysis pathways.

Gasification with Fischer–Tropsch (GFT) is a thermochemical pathway in which biomass is
converted to synthesis gas (syngas)—a mixture composed primarily of carbon monoxide and
hydrogen, with smaller amounts of other gases—followed by catalytic upgrading to liquid
hydrocarbons, also known as syncrude, via FT synthesis. The alpha value, α, is defined as the
ratio of the chain-propagation rate to the sum of the propagation and chain-termination rates and
depends on reaction temperature, catalyst formulation, and the hydrogen-to-carbon ratio. In FT
operation, typical α values range from about 0.70 to 0.95 for low- and high-temperature FT,
respectively (71), lower α values favor the formation of light hydrocarbons (C1–C4), whereas
higher α values increase selectivity toward longer-chain products (72). For α values between
0.75 and 0.90, the propane content in the FT reactor output is estimated to range from

 32

approximately 2 to 11 wt%, based on the carbon-number distribution predicted by the Anderson–
Schulz–Flory (ASF) model. The syncrude typically requires further refining to meet commercial
fuel specifications, including cracking and isomerization, followed by product separation. This
downstream upgrading can occur in a stand-alone biorefinery or via co-processing of the
syncrude with fossil intermediates in existing petroleum refineries. The preferred feedstock for
the GFT pathway is woody biomass, primarily because of its relatively low and less problematic
ash content. In contrast, herbaceous feedstocks such as agricultural residues are generally
considered less attractive options: their higher ash content can negatively affect overall
conversion efficiency.

Although gasification and FT synthesis are themselves mature technologies (with extensive

via gasification and FT has not yet been commercially demonstrated, however, several

Pyrolysis was classified under partially commercialized process pathways in the 2022 RP
Report, defined as pathways in which at least one process step has been commercialized while
other steps remain in the exploratory stage. However, in light of recent developments in
pyrolysis demonstration projects, we have reclassified pyrolysis—specifically fast pyrolysis—
into the announced commercial and demonstration project category.

In the pyrolysis process, organic material is heated to elevated temperatures in the absence of
oxygen, causing them to decompose into three main product streams: a solid carbon-rich residue
(char), a condensable liquid fraction (often called bio-oil or pyrolysis oil), and a non-condensable
gas (NCG). Fast pyrolysis is a type of pyrolysis that maximizes liquid bio-oil production by
using high heating rates, moderate temperatures (typically about 300°C–650°C), finely ground
feedstock, and very short vapor residence times (fractions of a second to a few seconds),
followed by rapid quenching of the vapors to form a liquid product (101). Under optimized
conditions, fast pyrolysis of lignocellulosic biomass can yield roughly 50–75 wt% bio-oil, 12–25
wt% char, and 10–15 wt% gas on a dry feed basis (102, 103). Although fast pyrolysis primarily
targets bio-oil production, these pathways can generate light gases and LPG-range co-products
that may offer future RP opportunities, depending on configuration and market dynamics.

Fast pyrolysis bio-oil’s (FPBO) oxygen content is typically around 40 wt.%, which is
substantially higher than that of crude petroleum, where oxygen levels rarely exceed 2 wt.%
(102). This high oxygen content impacts stability of FPBO and its suitability for upgrading to
fuels. Thus, most of the current FPBO production is centered in the production of FPBO for use
in heating, chemicals, and food applications.

3.4.3 Partially Commercialized Processes
As explored in the 2022 RP Report, processes for which at least one step in the process have
been commercialized but other steps are still in the exploratory stage include:

Sugar fermentation: fermentation of sugars for ethanol production is at commercial scale, from
starch, such as the corn ethanol industry in the U.S., and from lignocellulosic biomass more
widely commercialized outside the U.S., for example by Razen in Brazil (117). Opportunities for
producing RP/LPG arise in the downstream processing/upgrading of the hydrocarbons generated
from fermentations, such as methanol, ethanol, of butanol. Gevo process for fermenting sugars to
isobutanol, which can be converted to RD or SAF, but which can be redirected to LPG
production, similar to the ethanol to jet pathway.

The production of isobutanol for transportation fuel generation has been explored by Gevo.
Gevo’s planned Net-Zero 1 (now referred to as the ATJ-60) project near Lake Preston, South
Dakota, was originally conceived as a greenfield plant that would ferment corn to isobutanol and
upgrade to fuels, producing 62 MGPY of hydrocarbons, including about 55 MGPY of SAF,
using renewable electricity and other decarbonized energy sources (118, 119). Later, Gevo
shifted focus for the Lake Preston facility from isobutanol-based configuration to ethanol-to-jet
fuel, emphasizing corn-ethanol as primary intermediate for the ATJ conversion. Early 2025, the
Lake Preston facility—marketed as the ATJ-60 Project- focus on converting corn into 60 MGPY
of liquid transportation fuels. However, late-2025 updates indicate that Gevo is seeking to
transfer its U.S. DOE loan guarantee support to a smaller ethanol to jet project (30 MGPY of
SAF) at the company’s existing ethanol and CCS facility in North Dakota, potentially slowing or
pausing the Lake Preston NZ1/ATJ-60 project (120).

Glycerin Dehydration/hydrogenation: The biodiesel industry produces approximately 1 kg of
glycerol for every 10 kg of biodiesel. As biodiesel production grew, the market was flooded with
glycerol, reducing its value. This "glycerol glut" provides a low-cost feedstock opportunity for
propane production (121). The conversion of glycerin to RP via hydrodeoxygenation was
explored in the 2022 RP Report. In 2019, it was reported that Renewable Energy Group was
considering a direct conversion of glycerin to RP but no recent news about this approach has
been made public (121). No full-scale commercial glycerol-to-propane plant is online in the U.S.
as of 2025, and these projects remain at pilot phases.

industry and HEFA has captured much of this supply rendering, some biodiesel plants have been
uncompetitive (2). The U.S. biodiesel production capacity has declined in recent years as
renewable diesel production has expanded, capturing both feedstock and market share served by

3.4.4 Pilot Plants
In this section, processes that have been demonstrated at a pilot scale with a focus on
transportation fuel production are shortly described here.

In catalytic fast pyrolysis, biomass is heated to approximately 450–600 °C in an oxygen-free
environment for residence times typically under 2 seconds, generating vapors that are
immediately contacted with a solid catalyst. The catalyst promotes cracking, deoxygenation, and
aromatization reactions, converting raw pyrolysis vapors into stable, energy-dense hydrocarbons,
including benzene, toluene, xylenes (BTX), and light olefins. Compared with conventional fast
pyrolysis, catalytic fast pyrolysis lowers the oxygen content of the raw bio-oils, producing a
partially upgraded bio-oil or, in some configurations, fully deoxygenated hydrocarbon fuels. The
process can be implemented in situ (with catalyst mixed directly with the biomass) or ex situ
(with vapor upgrading in a separate reactor) and typically incorporates catalyst regeneration to
maintain activity. Pilot plants include:

 • Anellotech operates a pilot plant (TCat-8, 0.5 ton/day) located at Trecora’s facility in
 Silsbee, TX, which counts of a fluidized-bed catalytic pyrolysis reactor with continuous
 catalyst regeneration (FCC-style). The target product are aromatic hydrocarbons,
 primarily BTX and light olefins (123). The Bio-TCat process produces a liquid product
 that is >98% C₆⁺ aromatics from biomass, while the Plas-TCat process converts waste
 plastics into BTX aromatics for chemical feedstocks and high-octane fuels. Anellotech
 announced that the product produced at the TCat-8 facility via the Plas-TCat technology
 has been certified under the Fédération Internationale de l’Automobile framework for use
 in advanced low-carbon fuels for Formula 1 (124). In addition, the TCat-8 plant is

 38

 undergoing upgrades, including in-house nitrogen generation and enhancements to heat
 exchange and product collection to increase its nameplate capacity (125).

 • Gas Technology Institute (GTI): IH2 pilot unit in Des Plaines, IL, evaluates the
 integrated hydropyrolysis and hydroconversion (IH2), in which biomass is fed into a
 pressurized fluidized-bed catalytic pyrolysis reactor under hydrogen gas. A catalyst
 promotes deoxygenation/hydrogenation of vapors, and a second-stage hydroconversion
 catalyst further upgrades the vapor to fully deoxygenated hydrocarbons. The plant can
 process about 50 kg/day. The technology has been proved at pilot scale and is licensed to
 Shell for commercialization (126). The project “Integration of IH2 with the Cool
 Reformer for the Conversion of Cellulosic Biomass to Drop-In Fuels” shows that the IH2
 process can be hydrogen self-sufficient with the integrated Cool Reformer (127). As part
 of the Cool Reformer technology applications GTI introduced a Cool LPG technology
 that converts biogas, biosyngas, or biogenic CO2+H2 into renewable LPG (128), and
 potentially RP.

 • NLR Thermal & Catalytic Process Development Unit: Pilot-scale fast pyrolysis unit in
 Golden, Colorado, configurable for in-line catalytic upgrading. It uses an entrained-flow
 biomass pyrolyzer (for fast pyrolysis) coupled with a recirculating regenerating catalyst
 reactor to upgrade pyrolysis vapor, for catalytic fast pyrolysis. The unit has been used to
 test both fast and catalytic pyrolysis (129).

 • RTI International: Pilot Xcelerator Facility (Durham, North Carolina) has a catalytic fast
 pyrolysis system (fluidized bed reactor) capable of processing 1 ton/day. The unit
 includes a hopper feed and a catalyst regenerator, enabling continuous operation. The
 system generates crude bio-oil that can be distillated and upgraded into fuels (130). RTI
 International has partnered with Topsoe for catalyst development for catalytic fast
 pyrolysis and bio-oil upgrading (131).

 • Iowa State University’s catalytic autothermal pyrolysis (CAP) pilot plant is based at the
 BioCentury Research Farm near Boone, Iowa, where the fast pyrolysis process
 development unit has been modified to operate in autothermal mode and serve as a test
 bed for CAP process (132). The fluidized-bed pyrolysis process development unit
 processes a variety of biomass feedstocks and uses a patented stage-fractionation system
 to collect multiple bio-oil cuts. It can operate continuously at about 22 kg/h of biomass,
 and when run autothermally, it achieves roughly a three-fold increase in throughput
 compared with conventional nitrogen-blown fast pyrolysis while still producing sugar-
 rich and phenolic oil fractions plus biochar for soil amendment and carbon sequestration
 (133). The Iowa State University also has pilot units for fast pyrolysis, for example the
 unit at the BioCentury Research Farm can processes up to 8 kg/h (131).

Aqueous phase reforming is a water-based catalytic process that converts plant-derived sugars
and sugar alcohols into hydrogen and alkanes, which are then upgraded to produce drop-in
hydrocarbon fuels covering LPG and light fuel gases through naphtha, jet, and diesel ranges.

 39

Virent’s aqueous phase reforming technology, BioForm Synthesized Aromatic Kerosene (SAK),
was validated at its Eagle pilot plant in Madison, Wisconsin. On November 28, 2023, Virgin
Atlantic operated the first commercial transatlantic flight powered by 100% SAF, which
included the BioForm SAK made by Virent (134). Virent’s SAK provided the necessary
aromatics for swelling O-ring seals in jet engine to prevent leaks, enabling a 100% "drop-in"
solution without requiring fossil fuel blending. In 2025, the Virent pilot plant was closed with the
commercial activities moving into its parent company, Marathon Petroleum (135). In 2025,
Comstock Fuels, a developer of woody biomass conversion technology, entered into a
commercial lease for the former Virent facility in Madison (136).

The HEFA pathway represents the most commercially mature route for the production of
renewable liquid fuels and offers the most near-term potential for RP production as co-product.
As such, the HEFA industry represents the primary source of RP supply in the short term.
However, broader deployment of the HEFA industry is constrained by the limited availability of
suitable lipid feedstocks to support expanding production capacity. Beyond HEFA, several other
high TRL technologies, including ETJ, GFT, and fast pyrolysis, can also contribute to RP
production in the near- or mid-term. In addition, a range of emerging technologies, partially
demonstrated at industrial scale, are under active development and have the potential of
producing RP as coproduct. Collectively, these pathways have the potential of importantly
increase RP production and enhance diversification and resilience of the propane supply chain
over the medium to long term.
