 ABSTRACT

 The fuel elements containing thousands of TRI-structural ISOtropic (TRISO) particles embedded
in a graphite matrix are heterogeneous in nature, due to the random positioning of particles, which
may create local stress fields and thermal gradients that cannot be accurately modeled using simplified
1D or 2D approaches. Consequently, this work show the development and demonstration of realistic
TRISO compact meshing capabilities for 3D modeling, used to predict more detailed dimensional changes,
internal particle pressure buildup, and fission product transport pathways under diverse irradiation and
thermal conditions. Differently from the usual modeling approach, these do not rely on the assumption
of homogenized properties or XCT images. Compared with real fuel elements, the packing algorithm
has shown realistic packing estimation. The subsequent mesh generated demonstrated a good behavior
through heat conduction simulations under representative in-reactor operating conditions. This capability
lays the foundation for detailed investigation of key design variables for compact-scale fuel performance,
which can directly impact the predicted fuel performance and safety margins.

 1. INTRODUCTION

 In order to fulfill the United States’ ambitious goals for achieving energy security and advancing its energy
production capacity, and given that radioactive material release is a major concern in nuclear energy, power
plant licensing regulations set strict limits on the release of Fission Product. TRISO fuel offers enhanced
reactor safety and performance due to its layered design. The Pyrolytic Carbon and Silicon Carbide layers in
TRISO particles act as the primary pressure vessel: they withstand increasing internal pressure from FP gases
and prevent their release. In addition, this fuel type can operate at much higher temperatures and burnups
than conventional Zircaloy-clad light-water reactor fuel rods, while still offering high resistance to failure
[1]. TRISO fuel was primarily developed for High Temperature Reactors, which can use either spherical
pebbles or prismatic graphite fuel blocks containing cylindrical TRISO compacts. More recently, this type
of fuel element has been proposed for use in other advanced reactors—for example, Fluoride salt-cooled
High-temperature Reactors, microreactors, and nuclear thermal propulsion systems [2].
 Each TRISO particle consists of a microscopic fuel kernel covered by at least three layers, each contributing
significantly to the retention of Fission Product: a low-density porous carbon buffer, followed by an Inner
Pyrolytic Carbon layer, a Silicon Carbide layer, and an Outer Pyrolytic Carbon layer. The manufacturing
process for these tiny particles begins with the production of fuel kernels through the sol-gel process, as
described in [3]. These kernels can be separated by diameter through sieving or roller sorting, and aspherical
kernels can be filtered out by tabling. Once the kernels are ready, each layer of the TRISO coating is deposited
using a chemical vapor deposition method, in which an inert gas flows upward through a bed of kernels,
causing them to agitate and behave like a fluid while reactive gases deposit each successive layer.
 Thousands of these particles are then hot-pressed into a graphite matrix to form fuel elements, which can
take different shapes [4, 5], the most common being cylindrical compacts and spherical pebbles. Both types of
fuel elements can be either solid or annular, as shown in Figure 1. Pebbles are produced in a similar manner:
first, the TRISO particles are densely compacted into a spherical shape through isostatic or quasi-isostatic
pressing [6], forming the fuel zone. This initial pebble, composed of the fuel zone, can then undergo a second
pressing step, in which it is surrounded by an additional layer of matrix material to form the fuel-free shell.
The annular pebble design contains a lower-density spherical core, around which the TRISO particles are
pressed.

 Given the variety of reactor types for which TRISO fuel is expected to be used, advanced modeling tools
are required to analyze reactor performance and behavior under different operating conditions [7]. One such
tool used to model TRISO fuel is the BISON fuel performance code, developed at Idaho National Laboratory
for the U.S. Department of Energy [8, 9]. Much of its underlying physics is inherited from the Multiphysics
Object-Oriented Simulation Environment framework [10], an open-source simulation environment also
developed and maintained by INL.
 BISON offers substantial capabilities for TRISO fuel modeling; however, current particle-level modeling
typically relies on lower-dimensional approaches—1D or 2D—and treats particles in isolation, i.e., without
matrix material [11, 12]. When modeling a whole fuel element using 3D models, the properties of the particles
and matrix are usually smeared together, and—when particles are discretized—heat and mass transport are
modeled using point sources at particle centroids, which can be derived from X-ray Computed Tomography
scans [13]. Hegazy shows in [14] how this approach can lead to significant discrepancies in temperature
distributions and FP diffusion. These simulations provide valuable insight into compact behavior under
irradiation, but they fail to properly capture (1) the effect of the matrix material on particle performance,
(2) the effect of the particles on the matrix, and (3) particle-particle interactions. As a result, meaningful
stress effects at the compact scale—and potentially some at the particle scale, which could significantly affect
particle failure—are typically not captured.
 This work describes a new method for estimating the random packing of spherical fuel particles into
different types of compacts and pebbles. The method can be used to generate fully resolved 3D meshes for
BISON [9] simulations. Analyses of 3D compact temperature and stress distributions during irradiation,
enabled by these meshes, are expected to support further study of matrix and particle performance, failure
probability, and FP release. The particle-packing algorithm is described in Section 2, the development of the
3D mesh is described in Section 3, a demonstration of the mesh’s use is presented in Section 4, the results
obtained are discussed in Section 5, and the main conclusions of this work are presented in Section 6.

 2. DEVELOPMENT OF THE PACKING ALGORITHMS

 The numbered algorithms described below will be generally referred to by packing algorithm. These
packing algorithms were developed in a way to mimic the fuel manufacturing processes. Starting with
the compacts, as particles are usually loaded from the top of the compact die, it is easier for the bottom
layer to have a close packing arrangement. From there, the particles disposition at each subsequent layer is
influenced by the layer below it. Algorithm 1 generates random particles to a certain height and then applies
the Deepest-Bottom-Left-Fill method [15], moving the generated particle the in x,y, and z directions until it
cannot be positioned any lower.
 Algorithm 1 takes as inputs: the compact outer radius Cor , the compact inner radius Cir , the compact
height Ch , the compact fuel-free shell thicknessCshell , the TRISO particle radius r p , and the target amount of
particles to be packed Nspheres ; h represents the current packing height. Note that the particles are packed as
hard-spheres, i.e., the particles are not deformed during the packing process.
 A similar packing routine is used to estimate the placement of particle centroids in a pebble, Algorithm
2. To simulate the isostatic compression experienced by particles during pebble compaction, the algorithm
places each particle at a random position on a spherical shell of fixed radius and incrementally moves the
particle toward the pebble center until it is blocked by an already-placed particle or the fuel zone boundary.
For both algorithms, the particle position validity is given by: the particles must be inside the fuel zone and
must not overlap each other. Note that either packing algorithm work for the geometry of a solid or annular
geometry; for a solid compact or pebble, the inner radius, Cir , is simply set to 0.

 Hawkes [33] and Collin [32] modeled the AGR-2 capsules with three fuel stacks each. These fuel stacks
were assumed to be homogenized cylinders that were sized to match the stacks. For each capsule, the minimum,
maximum, and average temperature of the three stacks were estimated. For simplification, we apply the heat
loss Boundary Condition as the minimum fuel stack temperature estimated by Hawkes [33] for capsule 2 as
the compact surface temperature, following the profile in Figure 4b.
 Because cylindrical fuel compacts are typically positioned in a vertical stack, the majority of heat transfer
is expected to be in the radial direction. As such, adiabatic BC were applied to the top and bottom surfaces.
So, as we simplified the compact temperature BC, making it overall lower than what Hawkes found, our
temperatures are expected to be correspondingly lower.
 Thermal conductivity for the UCO fuel was calculated based on the kernel temperature according to the
correlations from [34, 35], and its specific heat was estimated from temperature and molar mass, following the
work of [36]. The thermal properties of the buffer are from the PARFUME [34] default model. Monolithic
material was used for the SiC layer, and its thermal conductivity was estimated from [34]. The matrix material

 8

is defined as being PCEA-grade graphite, and its thermal conductivity was estimated using the correlations
from [37, 38], where the initial unirradiated matrix conductivity is given by [39, 40]. In BISON, the properties
for the graphite matrix can be homogenized depending on the packing fraction; however, to consider the effect
of the TRISO particles’ presence in the compacts, the particles in this work were discretized, and a packing
fraction of zero was used for the matrix materials.

 5. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

 The heat conduction modeling of the fuel compact is presented in Figures 5 and 6, which depict the
compact temperature distribution along the centerline and mid-diameter; blue peaks represent TRISO particle
temperatures, while the red line represents the matrix temperature. Figure 6a exhibits a symmetric temperature
profile across the diameter, whereas Figure 6b reveals elevated temperatures at the bottom of the compact
relative to the top.

Figure 5. Compact temperature distribution at 300 Effective Full Power Days (EFPD).

 Figure 6c presents the diametral temperature distribution at several axial elevations. Temperatures are
markedly elevated in the compact’s central region (spanning heights of 5 mm to 19 mm) relative to the two
edges, a pattern governed by the imposed BCs. Although identical BCs were applied at both ends, the bottom
edge (heights 0.0 mm and 0.75 mm) displays higher temperatures than the top edge (heights 24 mm and 25
mm). This asymmetry stems from the increased particle density within the first layer: because this layer forms
a more ordered packing configuration, it contains more particles than the layers above it, resulting in elevated
local heat generation.
 Since the purpose of this modeling exercise was to illustrate the performance of the generated meshes in
realistic simulation scenarios, close agreement with reference results was not anticipated, largely because
of simplifications made to the compact surface temperature. Nevertheless, the temperature distribution
predicted in this work supports meaningful qualitative comparison. Figure 7a contrasts the maximum fuel
temperature history obtained from our simulation with the corresponding analyses by Hawkes [33] and Collin
[32]. As shown, the temperatures calculated here are lower than those of the reference studies, consistent
with the reduced overall surface temperature and the resulting larger temperature gradient across the compact.
Likewise, Figure 7b shows that the average temperature computed for the outer surface of the OPyC layer in
our model exceeds the corresponding average reported for the OPyC layer in the existing AGR-2 assessment
case for compact 2-1-3 within BISON.

 The discretized mesh also enables examination of the thermal and mechanical interactions between
individual particles. Figure 8 illustrates the temperature profile across two neighboring particles — one
situated at the compact’s center and another near its edge. The influence of particle proximity on thermal
behavior is evident: the outer particle exhibits a higher temperature on the face oriented toward its neighbor,
and this gradient becomes more pronounced near the compact edge, reaching an approximate 5 K difference
across the particle. In contrast to simplified modeling strategies, this particle-resolved representation allows

 10

field variables such as temperature, stress, and strain to be evaluated individually across each particle layer.
Such resolution will be especially valuable for future stress analyses, where accurately capturing local gradients
at layer interfaces is critical to reliable failure probability predictions.

 6. CONCLUSIONS

 This work presented a methodology for predicting particle placement within fuel elements, yielding
realistic packing fractions of spherical particles in both cylindrical compacts and pebbles (solid and annular
configurations). Given manufacturing specifications as inputs, the algorithms generated packing fractions and
particle centroid distributions that align qualitatively with those observed in physically fabricated specimens.
Coupling the packing algorithm with BISON and MOOSE mesh-generation tools, a fully resolved compact
mesh was constructed and validated through heat conduction simulations under conditions representative of
reactor operation. The resulting mesh demonstration showed reasonable consistency with previously reported
simulation results.
 The discretized mesh presented here facilitates analysis of mechanical interactions both among particles
and between particles and the surrounding matrix, offering a means to investigate fracture behavior in TRISO
particles and the matrix under irradiation. This capability holds particular significance for advanced reactor
concepts, including High-Temperature Gas-cooled Reactor designs and other Generation IV systems, in which
TRISO fuel is subjected to high temperatures and burnup levels. Under such conditions, detailed mechanical
modeling can guide fuel design and operating parameters, potentially enabling margin reduction and the
associated economic advantages.
