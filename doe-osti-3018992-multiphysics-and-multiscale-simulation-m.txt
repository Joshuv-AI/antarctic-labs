1. Executive Summary

This report summarizes the technical accomplishments of the four-year research project “Multi-
physics and Multiscale Simulation Methods for Electromagnetic Energy Assisted Fossil Fuel to
Hydrogen Conversion” (Award No. DE-FE0032092), conducted at Howard University and the Uni-
versity of Houston (subawardee) from September 2021 to August 2025.
 The project successfully achieved all four major objectives:

 1. 3D Structural Characterization – Developed 3D optical imaging and mechanical sec-
 tioning methods to characterize catalyst distribution and support morphology in nickel foam
 substrates. Successfully reconstructed 3D geometries and imported them into COMSOL Mul-
 tiphysics for electromagnetic simulations.

 2. EM Hotspot Simulation – Created all-frequency stable electromagnetic formulations and
 3D nodal discontinuous Galerkin (NDG) methods for coupled electromagnetic-thermal-fluid
 problems in multiscale catalytic media. Demonstrated stable solutions from DC to microwave
 frequencies.

 3. Multiphysics Coupling – Developed multiscale simulation methods coupling FEM electro-
 magnetic solvers with thermal transport equations. Reactive molecular dynamics (ReaxFF
 MD) simulations were performed to investigate catalytic reaction mechanisms at the atomistic
 level. Demonstrated electromagnetic-thermal co-simulation capabilities for porous catalyst
 structures.

 4. System Optimization – Designed and optimized EM-assisted catalytic systems using nickel
 foam and carbon foam structures, demonstrating significant temperature increases due to
 microwave heating. Observed and characterized plasma generation in carbon fiber catalysts.
 Investigated multiple reaction chamber geometries for improved microwave energy deposition.

 The project produced significant scientific contributions including 15+ peer-reviewed publica-
tions, trained multiple Ph.D. students and undergraduate researchers, and advanced the under-
standing of microwave-assisted hydrogen production from fossil fuels.

2.2 Technical Accomplishments
2.2.1 Objective 1: 3D Structure Characterization
The 3D structural characterization work was performed at the University of Houston, which con-
tributed the optical imaging system and expertise in microscopy-based catalyst characterization.

3D Optical Microscopy System An in-situ real-time 3D optical microscope was established
for imaging electrode surface morphology. The system is based on linearly scanning the objective-
sample distance, imaging samples at different planes, and reconstructing the height maps for each
pixel (Fig. 1) in the reaction process. At a given imaging plane, only a thin layer of the 3D
electrode is in focus (Fig. 1a). By sequentially changing the objective-electrode distance, different
planes of the 3D structure are imaged and saved (Fig. 1a). A home-built motorized scanning stage
controls the movement of the sample stage, and the camera frame rate and stage scanning speed are
carefully selected to obtain enough cross-section images. The vertical interval between two images

is optimized to be around 200 nm for best height detection. In addition, the mechanical drift of the
stage is minimized by decreasing the stage weight, and a drift correction algorithm was developed
and used to correct the additional drift. After scanning the sample in the entire vertical range,
the 3D morphology is extracted from the image stack using variance and wavelet transfer methods
(Fig. 1b).

 The morphology of a Zn-Mn electrode was obtained using in-situ optical 3D microscopy and
compared with SEM. The Zn-Mn electrode has three-dimensional structures on the surface that
minimize Zn dendrite formation by controlling the reaction kinetics using its 3D morphologies. The
localized electrochemical environments (LEEs) created by the 3D structure were studied for their
capabilities to control the reaction kinetics and minimize dendrite formation. The 3D electrode
was fabricated using electrodeposition of Zn-Mn alloy performed in two-electrode electrochemical

cells by a galvanostatic method. A 200 × 200 × 80 µm volume was scanned using the in-situ
3D microscope. The 3D morphology of the Zn-Mn alloy electrode is shown in Figure 1b. Due to
hydrogen generation during the Zn-Mn alloy electrode fabrication process, protruding (white arrows
in Fig. 1b) and trench (black dashed circles in Fig. 1b) regions were created, and these 3D geometries
define the localized reaction environments and control reaction kinetics. To compare and verify the
3D microscope, the SEM image of the same location was obtained (Fig. 1c). The protruding and
trench regions can be identified in the SEM image and the shape of these morphologies matches
those in the optical 3D image. Although the spatial resolution of the optical microscope is not as
good as SEM, the 3D optical microscope provides a solution to perform in-situ 3D mapping during
the Zn deposition processes, allowing morphology changes to be imaged with sub-micrometer spatial
resolution in the entire reaction process. Figures 1d–g show the corresponding 3D morphology and
the SEM image at zoomed-in areas of the 3D substrate. There are no obvious contrasts in the
SEM images (Fig. 1d–e), while the morphologies in the 3D optical image clearly show the height
differences (Fig. 1f–g).
 To image the 3D morphology dynamics of a Zn-Mn electrode during the Zn deposition process,
the electrochemical cell was carefully designed to allow optical access to the region of interest
without disturbing the reactions. In this design, objective, top, and bottom electrodes were all
immersed in the electrolyte (Fig. 1a left). Since the visible light cannot penetrate through the
thick Zn alloy electrodes, the bottom electrodes were extended and the 3D morphology changes
were imaged on this extended area. To minimize the effect of the non-uniform current density
in the extended area, the observation window was chosen as close as possible to the projection
of the top electrode. The observation region is about 200–400 µm away from the edge of the top
electrode, significantly smaller than the top and bottom electrode separation (∼2 mm). A COMSOL
model was built to simulate the current density and electric field distribution in the extended area,
confirming that the current density in the observation area only decreased by 5% compared with the
area inside the projection of the top electrode. During the Zn plating and stripping processes, the
3D morphologies were continuously scanned and recorded. The stage scanning rate was increased
to achieve an 80 µm vertical scan within 10 s. The geometry changes within 10 s are insignificant
compared with the total deposition time (1000–2000 s): generally less than 180 nm in thickness
even with 80 mA/cm2 current density.

Nickel Foam 3D Reconstruction Since the Ni foam is non-transparent, a sectioning, imaging,
and reconstruction process was used to build the 3D geometry. The porous geometry in Ni foam is
on the order of 300–500 µm in diameter, which is too large for FIB-SEM imaging. Therefore, an
optical microscope was used to image and reconstruct the 3D geometry. A 5X zoom objective and
a 0.5X zoom-out lens were used to achieve a total 2.5X zoom imaging. Since the large geometry
does not require high-resolution imaging, mechanical polishing was used to remove thin layers of Ni
foam, with 2D images taken before each polishing step and subsequently aligned and reconstructed
to build the 3D geometry.
 Sectioning Challenges and Solutions: During the development, two challenges were iden-
tified:

  1. Metal structure movement: The Ni structure is relatively soft and the mechanical pol-
 ishing introduces shear force that moves the metal structure. For example, the structure in
 the red dashed circle in Fig. 3a moved to a new location in Fig. 3b. Solution: Epoxy was

 Page 6

 introduced to fix the metal structures in the Ni foam. The epoxy soaks into the Ni foam and
 after crystallization, the hard epoxy supports the metal structure during mechanical polishing
 (Fig. 4). However, due to the high viscosity of epoxy, it was difficult to inject it into thicker
 layers of Ni foam. This challenge was solved by heating up the epoxy to decrease the viscosity,
 allowing it to flow into the Ni foam.

  2. Low cross-section contrast: The polished Ni structure did not show sufficient contrast
 for segmentation. Solution: The Ni foam was painted entirely dark using ink. The polishing
 process introduces a bright, shiny metal surface indicating the newly formed cross-section,
 creating enhanced contrast to distinguish the cross-section region from other metal structures
 (Fig. 5).

Figure 5: Polished cross-section of Ni foam. (a) Polished in air showing scratches (green arrows)
and epoxy residue (red arrows). (b) Polished in water, showing much cleaner surface with enhanced
contrast between the cross-section and background.

 Optimized Polishing Procedure: The initial polishing in air generated metal particles that
scratched through the entire surface (green arrows in Fig. 5a), significantly affecting image quality.
In addition, the epoxy scraped to adjacent regions, leaving grey color residue marks (red arrows in
Fig. 5a). To solve these issues, the following optimized polishing procedure was developed:
  1. Rough polishing in water with 400 grit sandpaper

  2. Fine polishing in water with 1200 grit sandpaper

  3. Clean surface with water and ethanol

  4. Align and photograph cross-section
Since water was introduced as lubricant, removed metal particles are flushed out quickly without
introducing scratches (Fig. 5b). The cleaning process using water and ethanol also removes extra
epoxy residue, creating a much darker background.

Improved Image Segmentation and 3D Reconstruction The initial 15-frame image stack
(corresponding to about 112.5 µm) was not sufficient to represent the full Ni foam geometry. Using
the modified polishing procedure with heated epoxy for better penetration, a new image stack with
71 frames was successfully obtained, corresponding to approximately 525 µm thickness. However,
the reconstruction revealed several new issues: (1) separated particles might still show up on the
3D structure; (2) image alignment between different layers was not perfect; (3) a stepwise layered
structure could be seen on the reconstructed 3D geometry.
 To address these issues, the reconstruction procedure was modified (Fig. 8). All images were
processed using the open-source software ImageJ with the following steps: (1) import the raw image
of the cross-section of Ni foam; (2) apply a median filter to remove bright particles on the surface;
(3) segment by setting a threshold; (4) apply dilation and erosion to smooth the boundaries; (5)
fill holes to remove white areas in the black part; (6) remove small particles; (7) align adjacent
images; (8) invert colors; (9) build the 3D stack in z-direction. After obtaining the 3D geometry, the
smoothing function in ImageJ was applied (iteration number 6) to remove the stepwise geometry
structure. Figures 9a–c show the 3D geometry before smoothing, and Figures 9d–f show the
geometry after smoothing. The stepwise shape was significantly removed, and the entire structure
is approximately 1.8 mm wide.

Import into COMSOL Multiphysics After 3D smoothing in ImageJ, the geometry was im-
ported into MESHLAB to remove extra small domains caused by image reconstruction errors.
Laplacian smoothing was also applied to the geometry. The resulting geometry was imported into
COMSOL Multiphysics. Figure 10 shows the 3D geometry in COMSOL, including views with and
without the mesh (a–b), a zoomed-in region (c), and a different viewing angle (d).
 However, the imported 3D geometry in COMSOL still had many errors, including self-intersecting
faces and edges. For example, Figure 11a shows a self-intersecting face in the blue dashed rectangle.
To solve this problem, a multi-step procedure was designed: (1) use the balling function to split
the self-intersecting surfaces (Fig. 11a); (2) delete the surfaces surrounding the error coordinate
(Fig. 11b); (3) create new edges by corresponding vertices (red arrows in Fig. 11c); and (4) create
the new surfaces to close the loop (red arrow in Fig. 11d). Using these steps, all the self-intersecting
surfaces and edges were successfully removed, and the 3D geometry could be properly meshed in
COMSOL.

Interaction of 3D Nickel Foam with the Electromagnetic Field Using the established
model, the electric field distribution in the microwave reactor was simulated. Figure 14b shows the
electric field distribution on the 3D Ni foam surface (in the air side of the interface). The electric
field distribution was found to be highly non-uniform across the structure. The electric field at
edges and tips is much larger than in flat regions, caused by the corona discharge effect. To confirm
this effect, the electric field distributions at different observation angles were plotted (Fig. 14c–d),
showing that all relatively sharp tip areas have a larger electric field. However, since Ni (metal) has
relatively large conductivity compared with other materials, most of the electromagnetic energy is
reflected from the Ni surface, with only a small portion penetrating into the metal and converting
to heat.

Interaction of 3D Carbon Structure with the Electromagnetic Field Carbon is one of
the most important catalysts for methane reforming reactions. Carbon is a particularly interesting
material with properties between insulators and conductors. For example, carbon black, one type
of carbon widely used for methane reforming reactions, has a conductivity of approximately 0.1–
100 S/cm. This value is much smaller than that of metals (∼107 S/cm) but much larger than
insulators. Therefore, carbon can quickly absorb a large amount of electromagnetic field energy
and convert it to heat very efficiently. In addition, the thermal conductivity of carbon is much
smaller than metals (Table 1). The heat generated from the carbon material is more localized
compared with the temperature distribution in metal structures.
 To simulate the response on carbon material, the material properties of the 3D Ni foam geometry
were changed to carbon black. Figure 16a shows the electromagnetic field distribution on the 3D
carbon foam structure. The distribution is similar to that of the Ni foam, which also shows a strong
corona discharge effect (Fig. 16b–d).

Comparison of Carbon and Nickel Foam Heating To further understand the high heating
efficiency of carbon material, the electric field distribution across the cross-section of both materials
was compared (Fig. 18). The carbon foam (Fig. 18a) shows a much larger electric field inside the
structure (102 –104 V/m), and the penetration depth is relatively large. However, the electric field
inside the Ni foam structure is extremely small (from 5 to 0.0001 V/m) and most of the electric field
is confined to the surface (Fig. 18b). This is because the metal has a high conductivity and reflects
most of the electromagnetic waves away from the surface, resulting in a much smaller heating effect.
Therefore, carbon and metal provide two extreme materials that can be combined to design and
optimize the best structure for manipulating the electromagnetic wave and improving the reaction
efficiency.

Plasma Generation Imaging System A microwave reactor imaging system was developed
and built (Fig. 19). A U-shaped quartz tube was installed through holes opened on top of the
microwave. Different gases can be flowed through the quartz tube to maintain the reaction en-
vironment. Targeted materials, such as carbon fibers, were loaded inside the tube. During the
reaction, the microwave is illuminated onto the carbon fiber, which absorbs the EM energy and
generates heat and plasma. To image and record this process, a hole was drilled from the back side
of the microwave reactor, and a zoom-in camera was installed. One challenge was that interference
from the microwave disables the camera. This was solved by covering the camera with a metal
mesh (Fig. 19b) to protect it. The fastest frame rate this camera can achieve is 340 fps, which
allows imaging of the initial spark of the plasma.

2.2.2 Objective 2: Electromagnetic Simulation Methods
All-Frequency Stable Formulation A major contribution of this project was the development
of an all-frequency stable electromagnetic formulation for the accurate modeling and simulation of
electromagnetic problems with multiscale features, which are usually caused by the size differences
of the geometrical structures in different parts of the objects under consideration. Typical exam-
ples include the modeling and simulation of porous materials, antenna arrays, integrated circuits,
and electrical machines. When applied to solve such problems, the traditionally used vector wave
equation, which solves for either the electric or the magnetic field intensity, breaks down due to
the decoupling of these two fields at low frequencies. This is known to the computational electro-
magnetics community as the “low-frequency breakdown catastrophe,” which results in erroneous
solutions and must be addressed properly.

Mixed Continuous-Discontinuous Galerkin Method A mixed continuous–discontinuous
Galerkin (CDG) method was developed to solve the all-frequency stable formulation numerically.
The discontinuous Galerkin method (DGM) is known to be a very flexible numerical method in
solving mathematical/physical equations of different types, but it doubles the number of degrees
of freedom (DoFs) on the elemental interfaces, leading to a larger system matrix compared to the
traditional continuous Galerkin method (CGM) such as the finite element method. In this work,
the DGM was hybridized with the CGM to enjoy the benefits from both. In particular, in homoge-
neous media, CGM is used to reduce the number of DoFs and ensure field continuity, while on the
interface of two different media, DGM is used to permit field discontinuity and provide numerical
flexibility. Based on the idea of the interior penalty, the mixed CDG method for the all-frequency
stable formulation was designed, which minimizes the total number of unknowns in a given problem
while allowing the basis functions to be discontinuous wherever needed.

Iterative Solution Performance The iterative performance of both formulations was inves-

data at six frequencies. The convergence performance, peak memory requirement, and CPU time

mance of the vector-potential formulation deteriorates as frequency increases, with slower iterative
convergence and increased peak memory and total CPU time. The reduction of total number of

vector-potential-only formulation, the original all-frequency stable formulation exhibits a consistent
performance over a wide frequency range and requires less memory and time to solve, although it
involves a larger number of unknowns.

Simplified Two-Potential Formulation The three-potential formulation requires that three
sets of unknowns A, ϕ, and χ be solved simultaneously in a simulation. Since the auxiliary potential
χ is not a variable of interest, it can be removed from these equations. Instead, a penalty term
can be introduced to enforce the gauge condition implicitly. The introduction of the penalty
term enforces the inhomogeneous Coulomb gauge implicitly and weakly, leading to accurate field
solutions. Moreover, the newly introduced term contributes to the mass matrix in the finite element
discretization, helping re-balance the stiffness and mass matrices and leading to a better numerical
condition of the discrete system. This leads to the following simplified formulation:
 1 ϵr
                           ∇×     ∇ × A − ω 2 ϵ̃A + A + j ωϵ̃∇ϕ = Jimp                             (18)
 µ µ0
                                           −j ω∇ · ϵ̃A − ∇ · ϵ̃∇ϕ = ρimp                           (19)

This two-potential formulation involves only two potential variables A and ϕ, with a smaller number
of unknowns and consequently lower memory requirement and higher simulation efficiency. Taking
the divergence of the first equation and adding it to the second, the inhomogeneous Coulomb gauge
∇ · ϵr A = 0 can be revealed, confirming the implicit enforcement of the gauge condition.

Thermal Finite Element Method To couple the EM field with thermal effects, the all-

capability. Due to the large difference in time scales between the electromagnetic problem and

Hysteresis Modeling Hysteresis refers to a phenomenon in which the state of a system depends
on its operation history. It is an intrinsic characteristic of many materials, such as ferromagnetic
compounds, piezoceramics, electrostrictive polymers, and shape-memory alloys. Many models have
been developed to describe hysteresis phenomena, including the Preisach model, the Jiles-Atherton
(JA) model, and the Prandtl-Ishlinskii (PI) model. Compared to the Preisach and JA models, the
PI model is particularly advantageous because of its simplicity and accuracy. In this work, the
PI model was simplified to symmetrical cases, resulting in fewer parameters to be identified. A
Gauss-Newton method was then applied to identify the model parameters. Compared to existing
parameter identification methods, the proposed method is simple to implement, converges fast, and
is robust against measurement noises. In the symmetrical PI model, the magnetic flux density B

case, since more lossy particles absorb more EM energy. Interestingly, for 100 nickel particles, the
maximum temperature is 340 K, actually 20 K lower than the 50-particle case, because more nickel
particles reflect the incident wave more strongly, reducing the total EM energy dissipation.

In this research, the model is obtained through 3D optical imaging or FIB-SEM sectioning and

describe the randomness of the distributions of the micro-constituents. In this model, the size, vol-

to the real measured data, while their distributions are randomly generated. Using this model, the
electromagnetic field distribution was calculated, and the field hotspots were obtained, which could
then be used to calculate the electromagnetic power dissipation in the material. Once the power
dissipation is calculated, the heat transfer equation can be solved to obtain the temperature profile.
 As an example, a 30 × 30 random checkerboard was considered, in which two different micro-
constituents are modeled: one has a relative permittivity ϵr = 5 and a volume fraction of 60%, and
the other has a relative permittivity ϵr = 1 and a volume fraction of 40%. A gradient voltage was
applied on the boundary of the checkerboard where the lower-left corner corresponds to the highest
voltage and the upper-right corner corresponds to the lowest voltage. Using the all-frequency stable
formulation, this porous model was solved, and the field distribution is displayed in Figure 46. It
can be seen very clearly that due to the random distribution of the two-phase material and the way
the electrical field is excited, field hotspots are generated, which results in higher power dissipation
that generates heat to sustain the thermochemical reaction. In the next step, porous structures
obtained from Objective 1 were utilized to calculate the field and temperature hotspots.

Time-Domain Validation Two numerical examples were presented to demonstrate accuracy
and unconditional stability.
 Example 1: Capacitor. As the first example, a capacitive structure is analyzed. The geometry,
shown in Figure 47, consists of two parallel circular conductive plates, each with a radius of 5 mm,
a thickness of 0.5 mm, and a conductivity of σ = 106 S/m. The plates are separated by a gap of
0.5 mm, filled with a dielectric material having a relative permittivity of ϵr = 100. Additionally, the
structure includes conductive wires connected to the plates, each with a radius of 1 mm and a con-
ductivity of σ = 106 S/m. The capacitor is driven by a sinusoidal voltage source of 1000 sin(ωt) V,
applied at various frequencies.
 Figure 48 presents a comparison of the simulated conduction current distributions along the
capacitor with the results obtained from Ansys Maxwell. The comparisons, conducted at four
different frequencies, 1 Hz, 100 Hz, 1 kHz, and 10 kHz, demonstrate that the proposed formulation
accurately captures the current variation across all frequencies considered. In all simulations,
the time step size was set to ∆t = T /40, where T denotes the period of the sinusoidal signal.
Furthermore, in all four cases, stable numerical results were achieved using the same time step size,
regardless of the operating frequency. This outcome highlights the time-domain stability of the
Newmark-beta scheme.
 Example 2: Inductor. In the second example, an inductive structure is analyzed. As depicted in
Figure 49, the inductor comprises a spiral coil with a radius of 10 mm and a spacing of 1 mm between
its four wire loops. The wire has a diameter of 3 mm and a conductivity of σ = 5.8 × 107 S/m.
The inductor is driven by a sinusoidal voltage source of 1.0 sin(ωt) V at two operating frequencies,
50 Hz and 3 kHz. Figure 50 compares the calculated conduction currents along the inductor at two
operating frequencies, with results from Ansys Maxwell serving as a reference. As in the capacitor
example, the time step size was set to ∆t = T /40. Excellent agreement is observed at both 50 Hz
and 3 kHz, further validating the accuracy and numerical stability of the proposed method.
 Under the 3 kHz sinusoidal voltage excitation, the voltage distributions in the inductor at four

density distributions displayed in Figure 51(e)–(h). These time steps represent t = T /4, T /2, 3T /4,
and T , aligning with the input voltage oscillating at 1 V, 0 V, −1 V, and 0 V, respectively.
The figures clearly demonstrate a 90-degree phase delay in the current distribution relative to the
voltage, and a gradual decrease in peak current values due to conduction losses. This trend is also
evident in Figure 50(b).

2.2.4 Objective 3: Multiphysics Investigation
The multiphysics investigation combined the experimental characterization from Objective 1, the
electromagnetic and thermal simulation capabilities from Objective 2, and the atomistic modeling

far more efficient at absorbing microwave energy and converting it to heat compared to metallic

Reaction Chamber Optimization Shown in Figure 58 is the geometry of a reacting quartz
tube hosting a porous catalyst bed that permits methane stream to flow through. The tube has
a radius of 2.5 cm and a length of 7.5 cm. The porous catalyst bed has 2029 air pores of 2 mm
radii and is made of carbon black. This quartz tube is then placed into a reaction chamber made
of a microwave cavity that is placed either horizontally or vertically. As shown in Figure 59,
different geometrical shapes were considered to test the microwave energy deposition rate under
the consideration of reaction chamber geometry and reacting tube and porous catalyst geometry.
A standard rectangular waveguide is connected to the reaction chamber for microwave input. An
incident microwave at 2.45 GHz heats up the reaction catalyst bed and the reactant to initiate and
sustain the thermochemical reaction of methane to hydrogen conversion.
 To maximize the energy conversion rate, it is important that the input microwave energy is
absorbed by the catalyst bed and the reactant as much as possible. This can be achieved by
designing and optimizing the reaction chamber geometry. Considered in this study are a few
simple cases where only the chamber geometrical shape and orientation are investigated. When
the cuboidal chamber is horizontally placed as shown in Figure 59(a), the input microwave cannot
penetrate into the quartz tube and the porous catalyst bed efficiently (Figures 60a and 60b) and
a large amount of energy gets reflected back, resulting in a low energy absorption rate of 33.8%.
When the chamber is placed vertically as in Figure 59(b), the incident microwave penetrates into
the quartz tube and the porous medium much more efficiently, as can be seen in Figures 60c and
60d. This results in a much-improved microwave energy absorption rate of 71.0%.
 Shown in Figures 61 and 62 are the electric field distributions in cylindrical and diamond reaction
chamber cases. Similar to the cuboid case, when the cylindrical chamber is horizontally placed, the
microwave energy absorption rate is 4.36%, and when the chamber is vertically placed, the rate
becomes 78%. For the diamond chamber, these values are 48.5% and 73.5% for the horizontally
and vertically placed configurations, respectively.

6. Changes and Problems

6.1 Changes in Approach
The original plan included FIB-SEM sectioning for 3D structure characterization. Due to the
large geometry of nickel foam (300–500 µm pore size), optical microscopy sectioning proved more
practical and was adopted as the primary method. This change did not affect the project outcomes.

6.2 Problems Encountered
 • Metal structure movement during polishing – Resolved by introducing epoxy injection

 • Low contrast in cross-section images – Resolved by painting structures with ink

 • Self-intersecting geometries in mesh – Developed custom repair procedures

 • COVID-19 pandemic impacts – Addressed through enhanced remote collaboration
