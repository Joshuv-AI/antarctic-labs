Acenaphthene Derivatives as Signatures of C 11 ​ H 9 + \text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}} Reactivity with Methylated Naphthalenes
Ana I. Lozano, Anthony Bonnamy, Aude Simon, Christine Joblin

Abstract
C 11 ​ H 9 + \text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}} ion is the dominant fragment cation formed from methyl-naphthalene (MeNp) and dimethyl-naphthalene (diMeNp). Using the multiplex capabilities of PIRENEA, a setup dedicated to laboratory astrophysics, we studied the reactivity of the benzylium-like isomers of C 11 ​ H 9 + \text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}} with diMeNp under isolated conditions relevant to radiative association. Two reaction products are observed, C 12 ​ H 11 + \text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{12}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}} – also formed in the reaction with MeNp – and C 13 ​ H 13 + \text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{13}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{13}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}} , with branching ratios that depend on the specific diMeNp isomer. The reaction products were subsequently exposed to UV–visible irradiation to gain insight into their structures. The acenaphthylene radical cation, C 12 ​ H 8 ∙ + \text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{12}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{8}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{$\bullet$+}} , was identified as the most stable photofragment. We show that this experimental approach, supported by density functional theory calculations and molecular dynamics simulations, provides new constraints on the chemistry of benzylium-type species. We highlight the role that long-lived ion–molecule complexes can have in promoting C–C coupling and the formation of a pentagonal cycle. Moreover, the chemistry uncovered here highlights new pathways for the formation of pentagonal rings during PAH growth under low-pressure and cold conditions. In particular, it can lead to efficient formation of acenaphthylene-like species, recently detected in the TMC-1 cold cloud.

1
Introduction

A recent milestone in astrochemistry is the identification of individual gas-phase polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) in the cold, dense cloud TMC-1. Using the Green Bank Telescope (GBT), McGuire et al. reported the detection of 1- and 2-cyanonaphthalene
45
. Shortly afterward, the pure bicyclic aromatic indene was detected by Cernicharo et al.
19
with the highly sensitive receivers of the Yebes 40 m radio telescope, and this detection was soon confirmed at the GBT
17
. Since then, additional PAHs have been identified, including cyano-acenaphthylene
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{12}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{7}}}\text{CN}
(1- and 5- isomers)
21
, phenalene
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{13}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{10}}}
18
, cyano-pyrene
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{16}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}\text{CN}
(1-, 2- and 4- isomers)
68
;
70
and cyano-coronene
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{24}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{CN}
, the largest PAH identified so far
69
. Most PAHs detected to date are cyano-substituted species, whose large dipole moments significantly enhance their radio detectability.

The formation of cyano-PAHs under the cold conditions of dark molecular clouds is thought to result from reactions between PAHs and CN radicals; for benzene and toluene, this reaction has been shown to be barrierless
10
;
22
;
46
. This connection makes it possible to infer the abundances of the unsubstituted parent PAHs from the observed abundances of their cyano derivatives
70
;
69
. The inferred values indicate that PAHs must be produced efficiently in cold clouds
2
. Although the underlying processes remain uncertain, current evidence favors a bottom-up gas-phase chemistry
20
;
21
;
18
. Moreover, consistent with the pioneering work of Herbst and Klemperer on dense-cloud chemistry
33
, these findings further highlight the key role of ion–molecule reactions in building molecular complexity
42
.

PAHs – including methylated and, more broadly, alkylated PAHs – have been found to be abundant in laboratory analyses of primitive Solar System matter, particularly in carbonaceous chondrite meteorites
48
;
53
;
26
;
24
;
38
and, more recently, in samples from the asteroid Ryugu returned by the Hayabusa-2 mission
5
;
57
. Zeichner et al.
72
investigated the
13
C isotopic composition of several small PAHs in Ryugu material and concluded that these molecules were formed through distinct chemical processes: some at the very low temperatures characteristic of molecular clouds (
\sim
10 K), and others at much higher temperatures (>1000 K) that may prevail in circumstellar environments or within the parent body.

Methylated PAHs may contribute to the 3.4
\mu
m emission band
36
, which appears as a satellite feature of the 3.3
\mu
m aromatic infrared band (AIB) – both of which are key targets of James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) observations in the Milky Way and in external galaxies
51
;
58
;
65
. The 3.4
\mu
m band is known to be particularly sensitive to UV irradiation
36
;
52
, consistent with its carriers containing aliphatic C–H bonds, either in alkyl side groups or in the form of superhydrogenated PAHs with additional H atoms
37
;
43
.

Previously, we investigated the properties of the
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
ions – the H-loss fragments of methyl-naphthalene cations – and highlighted the relevance of these ions in environments exposed to mild UV irradiation due to their photophysical properties
41
. Three long-lived isomers were identified: 1- and 2-naphthylmethylium (1- and 2-
\text{NpCH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
) and benzyltropylium (
\text{BzTr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
). Their reactions with the parent neutral lead to two products of unknown structure,
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{12}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
and
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{22}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{19}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
, the latter corresponding to an adduct. It was further shown that
\text{BzTr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
is unreactive, whereas the other two isomeric populations – very likely 1- and 2-
\text{NpCH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
– display reaction rate constants differing by an order of magnitude.

This article investigates the reactivity of
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
ions with several isomers of dimethyl-naphthalene (diMeNp), namely 2,3-, 1,4-, and 1,5-diMeNp. Using the multiplex capabilities of PIRENEA, a setup dedicated to laboratory astrophysics, we studied these reactions under isolated conditions relevant to radiative association. Moreover, the reaction products were exposed to UV–visible light to gain further insight into their structures and to identify fragments with enhanced photostability. We show that this experimental approach, supported by DFT calculations, not only improves our understanding of the chemistry of these species – including isomer-specific effects – but also reveals new chemical pathways for the formation of cosmic PAHs under cold and mild-UV irradiation conditions.

Methodologies

2.1
Experimental Methods

The experiments were performed using the PIRENEA setup
66
, operated under the configuration described in detail elsewhere
41
.
The apparatus consists of a Fourier Transform Ion Cyclotron Resonance Mass Spectrometer (FTICR-MS) maintained under ultra-high vacuum conditions (
\sim 10^{-9}
mbar at room temperature), enabling ion trapping under isolated conditions and for long time scales. PIRENEA can also be run at cryogenic temperatures but this capability was not used in this study due to the sticky nature of the injected molecules.
When required, specific ions in the ICR cell (e.g.,
12
C isotopologs) were isolated by selectively exciting the cyclotron motion of unwanted ions, thereby ejecting them.

\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
cations were produced from 2,3-, 1,4-, and 1,5-diMeNp (Figure
1
), all of which possess sufficient vapor pressure at room temperature to be used in our experiments.
The neutral precursors were ionized using the fourth harmonic (266 nm, 4.66 eV) of a Nd:YAG Surelite I laser (Continuum), producing ions that were trapped in the ICR cell. The pure
12
C parent ions were then isolated, and the production of
\hphantom{{}^{\text{12}}_{\text{}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
was optimized through photoprocessing of the trapped parent ions using a high-pressure Xe arc lamp equipped with a 610 nm color filter (CF). Under these conditions, the parent ions dissociate at low energies, and the resulting fragment ions do not absorb further. Consequently, the
\hphantom{{}^{\text{12}}_{\text{}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
fragments are expected to have low internal energy and thermalize with the background during ion production, which occurs over 20 s. Typical mass spectra illustrating the different steps in the case of 1,4-diMeNp are shown in Figure S1.

Reactions between
\hphantom{{}^{\text{12}}_{\text{}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
ions and their neutral precursors were conducted at room temperature and a typical pressure of
\sim 10^{-8}
mbar. The reaction time was adjusted between 30 and 120 s to ensure complete conversion of the reactive ion population. Sufficient time (up to 200 s) was allowed to recover a low background pressure (below a few 10
-9
mbar) before recording the mass spectrum.

Photodissociation kinetics were measured for
\hphantom{{}^{\text{12}}_{\text{}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{12}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
and its reaction cationic products using broadband visible irradiation from a Xe arc lamp. A 400 nm CF filter was used, with or without a neutral-density filter (NDF) providing 37% transmission.

Finally, our most challenging experiment imvolved recording a multiple-photon dissociation (MPD) spectrum of one the reactivity product,
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{13}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{13}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
. After the reaction and sufficient pumping time, the product of interest was isolated and irradiated using a mid-band tunable optical parametric oscillator (OPO) laser (Panther EX, Continuum), operating at 10 Hz with 5 ns pulse duration. The dissociation yield was recorded as a function of OPO wavelength using a fixed irradiation time of 10 s (100 laser pulses) and a pulse energy of 5.5 mJ. This multi-stage mass spectrometry (MS
n
) procedure required 3 min per recorded wavelength.

For all experiments, the recorded intensities were normalized to the summed ion intensity to account for variations in absolute ion intensities across different measurements.

2.2
Computational Methods

Following the methodology proposed by
61
, we performed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to investigate the dissociation of the three diMeNp cations studied here.
In this approach, molecular dissociation is modeled in the electronic ground state, with the internal energy represented as vibrational excitation.
This regime is consistent with the building of internal energy in the cations by absorption of multiple photons from the Xe lamp
15
.
However, the energies need to be sufficiently high for dissociation to occur within the nanosecond timescale accessible to the simulation.
This approach has been recently applied to rationalize the dissociation dynamics of both MeNp radical cations (
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{10}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{$\bullet$+}}
) and their
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
fragments
41
.

Briefly, the MD simulations were carried out in the Born–Oppenheimer approximation; the electronic structure is computed on-the-fly using the self-consistent charge density functional based tight-binding (SCC-DFTB) method
27
, employing the recently revisedC–H parameter set
35
. A Fermi temperature of 1500 K was applied to prevent oscillations in the self-consistent DFTB procedure, which frequently occur in dissociated or near-dissociated systems. This also ensured continuity of energies and gradients during level crossing.
61

This explicit treatment of the electronic structure enables the description of chemical evolution processes such as isomerization and dissociation.
For simplicity, this approach will hereafter be referred to as MD/DFTB.

Each simulation was initiated from the ground-state optimized geometry with randomized initial velocities, propagated in the microcanonical (NVE) ensemble.
Energies and gradients were computed every 0.2 fs.
All MD/DFTB simulations were performed using the deMonNano code
56
.
In the present work, a total of 180 simulations were run for the three diMeNp cations at internal energies of 15.0 and 16.5 eV, for simulation durations of 2 ns and 1 ns, respectively.
This strategy enables insight into reaction kinetics, branching ratios, and underlying mechanisms and thus contributes to the rationalization of experimental results.

In addition to MD/DFTB simulations, DFT local optimisations and all atoms harmonic frequency calculations were performed at the B3LYP
13
/6-31G(d,p) level of theory to support and interpret experimental results. Unless it is explicitly mentioned, all reported molecular structures were obtained at this level of theory and all reported energy values include zero-point energy corrections. In specific cases, wavefunction (CCSD(T)/cc-pvtz) single point energy calculations were performed. Vertical excitation energies were computed using the Time-Dependent (TD) DFT method with the PBE0 functional
1
in conjunction with the cc-pvtz basis set. This level of theory has been shown to yield satisfactory excitation energies for
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
isomers (see Table S3 in the Supplementary material of
41
). All these calculations were achieved with the Gaussian16 suite of programs
28
.

4
Discussion

The results presented in Section
3
establish
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
as a key intermediate in the photodissociation and subsequent reactivity of methylated naphthalenes.
Its prominence as a common photofragment of both diMeNp and MeNp, together with its ability to react with these species, leads to the formation of two primary reaction products,
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{12}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
and
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{13}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{13}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
. Photophysical characterization of these two products further reveals distinct fragmentation pathways.

In the following sections, we examine how our experimental findings data support the formation of acenaphthene derivatives and can be used to propose chemical scenarios that could account for the production of these species.

4.1
Evidence for acenaphthene derivatives

Among the candidate structures for the
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{12}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
products, our results point to protonated acenaphthene, [ACN+
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
] (Figure
10
).
This assignment is supported by dissociation via 2H/
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
loss, consistent with the results reported by
64
, who showed that [ACN+
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
] dissociates primarily through the ejection of two mass units, attributed to
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
loss from the pentagonal ring.

The trends observed in the MPD spectrum are consistent with

5
Implications

The chemistry discussed in this work is centered on the reactivity of the
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
ion, a common fragment produced during the dissociative ionization (or sequential ionization and dissociation) of methyl- and dimethyl-naphthalene. Species in which one hydrogen atom of a PAH is replaced by a methylene cation (
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
) form a family of carbocations that are expected to be highly reactive
50
. In addition, they are resonance-stabilized through charge delocalization over the aromatic
\pi
-system. Their study is of interest not only for fundamental gas-phase chemistry, but also for understanding processes in astrochemical environments such as cold interstellar clouds and Titan’s ionosphere.

Early studies on the benzylium/tropylium (
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{7}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{7}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
) system showed that benzylium reacts with toluene to form
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{8}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
59
;
8
, via a proposed mechanism involving
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
transfer from the ion to the neutral molecule without H scrambling (see Scheme II in Shen & Dunbar
59
).
This mechanism alone is insufficient to explain all our experimental results. Instead, we propose an alternative mechanism based on the formation of a long-lived complex (adduct), which can facilitate conformer exploration, thereby promoting H migration, C–C coupling, and, in most cases, the formation of a pentagonal cycle.
Our calculations also suggest that the geometry of the (2-
\text{NpCH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
)(1-MeNp) complex is less favorable for C-C coupling. A more detailed exploration of the ion-molecule potential energy surface (PES) would allow to conclude on the attribution of the
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{22}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{19}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
species to this complex.

Two isomers of cyanoacenaphthylene (1- and 5-
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{12}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{7}}}\text{CN}
) were recently detected in TMC-1
21
. Interestingly, the derived column density of this species is 1.7 times higher than that of the smaller cyanonaphthalene isomers (1- and 2-
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{10}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{7}}}\text{CN}
)
45
. Phenalene (
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{13}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{10}}}
) has also been detected in TMC-1
18
. The authors of that study proposed that phenalene is most likely formed through the reaction of acenaphthylene (ACYN) with
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
, producing
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{13}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
via radiative association, followed by dissociative recombination to yield
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{13}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{10}}}
. However, no pathway was proposed for the formation of ACYN itself, highlighting missing reactions in current chemical networks that could enable efficient PAH growth in cold dark clouds
21
.

The chemistry presented here reveals an efficient route to
acenaphthene derivatives through ion–molecule reactions, which are expected to proceed efficiently at low temperatures. Although the experiments were conducted at 300 K rather than under cryogenic conditions, the isolation conditions ensure that only radiatively stabilized processes occur, mirroring those relevant in astrophysical environments. The key step involves the reactivity of
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
, a typical fragment arising from dissociative ionization of methyl- and dimethyl-naphthalene and its complexation with these neutral species favoring CC coupling. Photoprocessing of the two reactivity products,
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{12}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
and
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{13}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{13}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
leads to the formation of the acenaphthylene radical cation,
\text{ACYN}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{$\bullet$+}}
.
In astrophysical environments, additional energy input could be supplied by mild UV photons or electron recombination.

Jochims et al. reported a critical energy of 7.8 eV for the survival of
\text{ACYN}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{$\bullet$+}}
in astrophysical environments, compared to only 3.9 eV for methyl-naphthalene cations
37
. More recent experiments using electrostatic storage rings have enabled measurements of improved astrophysical relevance by probing the competition between radiative cooling and dissociation of isolated ions over long timescales
44
;
62

\text{ACYN}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{$\bullet$+}}
remains stable up to vibrational energies of approximately 7.6 eV
63

\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{14}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{10}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
)
44
and significantly higher than the 5.8 eV derived for naphthalene cations
39

\text{ACYN}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{$\bullet$+}}
was identified as the main fragment ion produced in the dissociative ionization of
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{14}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{10}}}
(anthracene and phenanthrene)
11

\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{14}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{10}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}

\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{14}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{10}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}

\text{ACYN}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{$\bullet$+}}

The chemistry explored in our study may also influence hydrocarbon chemistry in dense and FUV-irradiated regions, such as the Orion Bar. These photodissociation regions (PDRs) contain dense and warm gas (T
gas
= 300–600 K) and exhibit active chemistry of small hydrocarbon radicals, such as
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
29

-7

-4

-6

\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
is among the abundant large cations observed by

\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{6}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{7}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}

6
Conclusion

Using the multiplex capabilities of PIRENEA, we studied the reactivity of
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
with methylated naphthalenes under isolated conditions relevant to gas-phase chemistry in space
34
. The
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
ions at
m/z
141 were consistently identified as key fragments produced in the photodissociation of MeNp and diMeNp cations. These ions comprise three long-lived
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
isomers – 1-
\text{NpCH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
, 2-
\text{NpCH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
, and
\text{BzTr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
– previously identified from 1-MeNp and 2-MeNp
41
.

The benzylium-like isomers of
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
react with neutral MeNp and diMeNp to form two primary products at
m/z
155 and
m/z
169, with relative abundances that depend on the precursor. The corresponding reaction products,
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{12}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
and
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{13}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{13}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
, exhibit distinct photodissociation pathways:
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{12}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
predominantly undergoes sequential hydrogen loss, whereas
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{13}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{13}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
follows two competing channels, yielding either
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{12}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{10}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{$\bullet$+}}
or regenerating
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{11}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
. In both cases,
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{12}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{8}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{$\bullet$+}}
emerges as a stable terminal photofragment upon irradiation with visible light from the Xe lamp.

We propose a chemical scenario involving long-lived ion-molecule complexes, where conformer exploration can facilitate C–C coupling and the formation of a pentagonal cycle. While the proposed reaction pathways require further investigation to fully validate each step, our experimental results, supported by DFT calculations, demonstrate that this chemistry offers a viable mechanism for the formation of acenaphthene derivatives in TMC-1, for which no formation pathway has yet been identified
21
