Methodological and Outcome Interpretation Concerns for the Food-Effect Study of Bersiporocin [Letter]
Authors: Danfeng Shen, Hongxing Xu
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We read with great interest the article by Lee et al regarding the food effect study of bersiporocin.1 Although the authors’ work characterizing the effect of meal timing on pharmacokinetics (PK) and gastrointestinal (GI) tolerability is commendable, we argue that multiple critical methodological and interpretative flaws compromise the validity of their conclusions. These concerns have substantial regulatory and clinical implications that warrant careful reconsideration.

First, the authors’ conclusion that food “did not have a clinically significant effect on systemic exposure” is based exclusively on the observation that AUClast geometric mean ratios (GMRs) remained within the 80–125% bioequivalence range. However, this conclusion completely ignores the magnitude of Cmax reduction, a parameter that regulatory agencies universally recognize as co-primary with AUC in food effect assessments.2,3 Under the 30-minute postprandial condition (Treatment B), the Cmax GMR for bersiporocin was 0.5262 (90% CI: 0.4334–0.6388); under the 2-hour postprandial condition (Treatment C), it was 0.6744 (90% CI: 0.5540–0.8211). Both confidence intervals fall far outside the 80–125% acceptance range that defines “no food effect”.2,3 As recently reviewed, even when AUC remains unchanged, a food-induced Cmax change of 30–50% is consistently considered clinically meaningful and often triggers explicit dosing recommendations.4 For a chronic disease like Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF), where maintaining consistent therapeutic levels is essential, a 33–48% reduction in peak concentration cannot be dismissed as insignificant.

Second, the authors’ mechanistic explanation for the increased GI adverse events (AEs) in the 30-minute postprandial group is physiologically inconsistent with their own PK data. Specifically, they proposed that gastric contents and elevated intragastric pH can compromise the integrity of the enteric coating and induce premature drug release. To our knowledge, the claim that high-fat meals impair enteric coating integrity lacks direct evidence. The enteric coating is designed to resist dissolution at gastric pH and only dissolve after reaching the higher pH environment of the small intestine.5 If premature drug release occurs in the stomach, accelerated rather than delayed drug absorption would be expected. However, the research results show that the median Tmax increased from 3 h in the fasted state to 6 h in both fed conditions. This paradoxical pattern—earlier release but a later systemic peak—cannot be explained by the previously proposed mechanism. Instead, it can be better accounted for by the fact that prolonged gastric residence under fed conditions is more likely to delay drug release into the intestine.6 Therefore, the increased incidence of GI AEs in the 30-minute postprandial group may be due to other underlying mechanisms.

Third, the authors’ attempt to distinguish peripheral (local GI irritation) from central (systemic exposure) mechanisms of nausea and vomiting is undermined by a circular reasoning flaw. The authors note that “systemic exposure did not differ between participants with and without vomiting” and use this observation to argue against a central mechanism. However, if vomiting occurred shortly after dosing (before complete drug absorption), lower systemic exposure would actually be expected in vomiters compared with non-vomiters. The fact that AUC values were comparable between groups does not exclude a systemic mechanism; it merely indicates that whatever vomiting occurred did not sufficiently reduce overall absorption to produce a detectable difference in AUC. Moreover, nausea and vomiting are more closely associated with the rate of drug absorption and the resulting peak plasma concentration (Cmax) rather than total systemic exposure (AUC).7 The authors did not analyze Cmax or the rate of concentration rise (which were substantially altered by food) in relation to GI AEs occurrence, leaving their mechanistic conclusion unsupported.

Fourth, the open-label design represents a serious methodological limitation for a study with subjective safety endpoints such as nausea and vomiting. Unlike objective PK parameters, GI symptoms are highly susceptible to participant expectation and investigator bias. Participants who knew they had consumed a high-fat meal may have been more likely to report GI discomfort. The absence of blinding or placebo control introduces substantial risk of bias that the authors neither acknowledge nor adequately address in their interpretation.

Fifth, the study evaluated only single-dose administration, yet IPF is a chronic condition requiring long-term therapy. GI tolerability often worsens with repeated dosing due to cumulative mucosal effects. The authors’ assertion that “the relatively short half-life suggests minimal accumulation, which may reduce the likelihood of meaningful changes in tolerability with multiple dosing” is speculative and not supported by any data from repeated-dose studies. As highlighted in recent industry analyses, single-dose food-effect findings cannot substitute for repeated-dose safety characterization, and decisions on dosing instructions should be informed by both single- and multiple-dose data where possible.4

In conclusion, the recommendation that bersiporocin “should be administered under fasted conditions or at least 2 hours after a meal” may be prudent, but this recommendation requires more rigorous scientific evidence than provided by the current study. We recommend that future studies adopt Cmax as a co-primary endpoint and use blinded repeated-dose designs to more reliably characterize the food effect profile of bersiporocin.
