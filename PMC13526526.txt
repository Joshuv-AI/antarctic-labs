Generation, differentiation, and potential of three radial glial subtypes in human cortical organoids
Authors: Mu Seog Choe, Jonghun Kim, In-Hyun Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadial glia are the principal neural stem cells in the developing human cerebral cortex and have been classified as three molecularly distinct subtypes: ventricular radial glia (vRG), outer radial glia (oRG), and truncated radial glia (tRG). Human cortical organoids (hCOs) from human pluripotent stem cells that recapitulate the developmental program of the human cortex have shown this progenitor diversity. vRG and oRG are readily generated and identified in hCOs from most differentiation protocols with oRG emergence dependent on specific signaling pathways, such as LIF/STAT3 supplementation. However, tRGs that have only recently been defined at the molecular level have not been systematically examined in any organoid system. Here we review the biology, signaling, and lineage potential of each radial glial subtype and assess how faithfully they are represented in cortical organoids. We highlight and discuss the studies on the tRG from hCOs. We discuss data consistent with latent tRG competence in hCOs, including ependymal cell generation from radial glia within organoids, CRYAB-positive progenitors in three-dimensional culture systems, and tRG-associated transcriptomic shifts upon genetic perturbation, while noting that definitive tRG identification has not yet been achieved. However, definitive identification through combinatorial marker co-expression and morphological validation has not yet been achieved. Faithfully establishing molecular signatures and signaling frameworks for tRG and their existence in hCOs will provide the foundation for systemically investigating and function of this cell type in vitro, and we discuss future directions for resolving whether tRG are generated during the neurogenesis-to-gliogenesis transition.Keywords: Brain development, Cortical organoid, Outer radial glia, Radial glia, Truncated radial glia

INTRODUCTION

Radial glia (RG) in the developing cerebral cortex were originally considered passive structural scaffolds guiding neuronal migration. Live imaging and clonal analysis overturned this view by demonstrating that RG undergo asymmetric division to produce neurons directly (1), establishing RGs as the principal neural stem cells of the mammalian cortex (2). By self-renewal and sequential generation of neurons, intermediate progenitor cells (IPCs), and glial precursors, RG drive cortical neurogenesis and gliogenesis.

Morphological, live-imaging, and transcriptomic studies of the developing human cortex have resolved RG heterogeneity and identified three subtypes, each defined by position, process morphology, and molecular signature (Table 1, Fig. 1). Ventricular radial glia (vRG, also known as apical RG, aRG) represent the classical population: cell bodies in the ventricular zone (VZ), apical and basal processes spanning the cortical wall, and expression of SOX2, PAX6, and NESTIN (3, 4). Their apical-basal polarity organizes tissue architecture but it also instructs the balance between symmetric self-renewal and asymmetric neurogenic division and segregates fate determinants between daughter cells, making vRG the source of both the progenitor pool and the earliest cortical neurons. Outer radial glia (oRG, also known as basal RG, bRG) are first identified in the human outer subventricular zone (oSVZ). They retain a basal process but lose apical contact, express HOPX, FAM107A, and PTPRZ1, and exhibit mitotic somal translocation (4, 5). oRG arise from vRG through a stepwise delamination cascade, and they sustain their own proliferation within the oSVZ, a basally expanded germinal zone. By amplifying the progenitor pool, oRG are thought to contribute to the expansion and gyrification of the primate cortex (6). The third subtype, truncated radial glia (tRG), was first identified in 2016 (7). tRG retain apical VZ contact yet possess a truncated or absent basal process. They express CRYAB, FOXJ1, S100B, and CLDN11 and give rise to ependymal cells and astrocytes while maintaining a protracted neurogenic capacity (8, 9). Functional studies in ferret demonstrated that tRG emerge during the neurogenesis-to-gliogenesis transition (8), and a lineage-resolved atlas of the human cortex revealed tRG identity, fate, and unexpected glutamatergic output (9). These three-subtypes, vRG, oRG, and tRG, now constitute the standard classification of cortical radial glia (4, 7, 10).

Comparison of radial glial subtypes: in vivo features and organoid recapitulation

Radial glial subtypes in the developing cortical organoids. The schematic illustrates the distinct characteristics of the three primary radial glial cells. vRGs, the classical progenitor population, reside in the ventricular zone (VZ) and exhibit a bipolar morphology with an apical process contacting the ventricle and a long basal process extending to the pia mater. oRGs are situated in the subventricular zone (SVZ) and are characterized by the absence of an apical attachment while retaining a basal process. tRGs are located in the VZ alongside vRGs, maintaining apical contact, but are defined by a truncated or absent basal process that terminates within the cortical wall.

Human cortical organoids (hCOs) derived from human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs; we use “cortical organoids” for directed differentiation protocols and “cerebral organoids” for unguided protocols) recapitulate key features of early-to-mid cortical development, including progenitor zone formation and sequential neurogenesis (11-14). vRG emerge reliably across most protocols, forming ventricular zone-like rosettes with canonical marker expression (3, 15), and oRG generation has improved through leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF)/STAT3 supplementation, which expanded an oSVZ-like compartment containing HOPX-positive progenitors (16). Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) studies, however, raised questions regarding the identities of different progenitor populations in hCOs. Progenitor subtypes showed only partial classification with in vivo counterparts, with elevated glycolytic and endoplasmic reticulum stress signatures (17, 18). Despite these limitations, cortical organoids remain the primary platform for studying human RG biology.

However, tRG remain a radial glial subtype that has not been systematically examined in organoids. First identified in human cortex in 2016 (7), the molecular and functional characterization of tRG have been made only recently (8). Thus, the very markers needed to identify tRG were unavailable when hCOs were assessed for the cellular repertoire (8, 9, 18). Now recognized as a tRG marker, CRYAB exemplifies the confusion in defining cell types in hCOs. CRYAB was originally proposed to be enriched in vRG at mid-gestation (3), and is now also recognized as a tRG marker at later developmental stages, reflecting stage-dependent expression rather than a marker for new class of cell types. Additionally, its role as a stress-responsive heat shock protein creates confusion in classifying cell types specific to organoid culture, issues discussed in detail in the tRG section below (18). Despite this ambiguity, evidence supporting the existence of tRG in hCOs has begun to accumulate. Ependymal cells, a primary tRG lineage output, have been generated in brain organoids through forced expression of the multiciliogenesis regulators GEMC1 and MCIDAS (19), and tRG-associated transcriptomic programs have been observed in organoid progenitors in KIAA0319 knockout hCO model (20). These observations raise the possibility that tRG are present in cortical organoids but have been unrecognized owing to absence of standard marker panels for different RG, and stress-related gene signatures.

In this mini-review, we examine how each radial glial subtype is generated and differentiated in cortical organoids. We integrate in vivo discovery of RG with organoid studies, and propose that tRG represent an underappreciated progenitor population whose hypothesized presence in organoids warrants systematic investigation using recently established molecular markers and signaling frameworks. We conclude by discussing future directions for identifying and characterizing tRG in organoid systems.

VENTRICULAR RADIAL GLIA: IDENTITY AND RECAPITULATION IN ORGANOIDS

Molecular identity and apical-basal polarity

Ventricular radial glia (vRG) are the founding progenitor population of the developing cerebral cortex. They reside in the ventricular zone (VZ) with a cell body lining the ventricle, an apical process contacting the cerebrospinal fluid, and a long basal process extending toward the pial surface (Fig. 2, upper panel) (21, 22). In the human fetal cortex, vRG express the canonical markers SOX2, PAX6, and NESTIN, but these markers alone cannot distinguish vRG from other radial glial subtypes. Thomsen and colleagues addressed this limitation by performing single-cell RNA sequencing on gestational week (GW) 16-18 human cortical tissue and identified several vRG-enriched markers, among which ANXA1 most reliably separates vRG from oRG (3). This marker panel, SOX2, PAX6, and ANXA1, serves as a standard reference for validating vRG identity in cortical organoid systems.

Disease associations for tRG remain to be defined.

Beyond marker expression, vRG maintain a strict apical-basal polarity that directly instructs cell fate. At the ventricular surface, N-cadherin-based adherens junctions anchor the apical endfoot, while the Par complex, comprising PAR3, PAR6, and atypical protein kinase C (aPKC), establishes and maintains apical domain identity (23, 24). Scuderi and colleagues demonstrated that three-dimensional culture actively sustains the N-cadherin-Notch feedback loop: adherens junctions at the apical surface activate Notch signaling, which in turn reinforces N-cadherin expression and apical attachment, a circuit that collapses in two-dimensional monolayer culture (25). Critically, PAR3 segregates asymmetrically during cell division, delivering the Notch antagonist Numb preferentially to one daughter cell: that cell suppresses Notch signaling and commits to differentiation, while the sibling retains high Notch activity and maintains progenitor identity (26). Polarity, therefore, does not simply organize tissue architecture. it partitions molecular fate determinants between daughter cells.

Division modes and fate-determining signals

During early cortical development, vRG divide predominantly symmetrically, expanding the progenitor pool by generating two daughter radial glia per division. As neurogenesis begins, the balance shifts toward asymmetric neurogenic divisions in which one daughter retains the radial glial identity, inheriting the basal process, while the other differentiate as an IPC or nascent neuron (23, 27, 28). Unlike in humans, rodent vRG divide almost exclusively along the vertical axis throughout neurogenesis. Early on vRG divide mostly symmetrically, but later vRG switch to asymmetric divisions that produce one RG and either an IPC or a neuron. Division orientation alone therefore cannot reveal whether a division is symmetric or asymmetric (29). Although orientation does not decide the fate of single divisions in rodent, the outcome changes when many divisions are reoriented. Boulan and colleagues showed that genetic inactivation of the spindle regulators GPSM2 (LGN) and SAPCD2 reoriented divisions in mouse neocortex, increased basal progenitor production over sixfold, and generated oRG-like cells with altered Hippo signaling (30). The precise balance between symmetric expansion and asymmetric neurogenic divisions thus determines both the final size of the cortex and the diversity of progenitor subtypes it can generate (24).

Several molecular pathways determine whether a newly born daughter cell self-renews or differentiates. Notch1 and Notch2 maintain radial glial identity, with Jagged1 from neighboring RG promoting self-renewal and Delta-like ligands from IPCs driving differentiation (31, 32). The asymmetric inheritance of Numb reinforces this system in a cell-autonomous manner. The Numb-inheriting daughter suppresses Notch even in a Jagged1-rich environment and commits to an IPC fate (23, 27). In parallel, fibroblast growth factor (FGF) signaling through ERK restrains the RG-to-IPC transition. FGF-ERK signaling inhibits premature radial glial differentiation and thus sustained ERK activity expands the RG pool, extending the neurogenic period (33, 34). Beyond maintaining vRG, FGF-ERK signaling promotes oRG generation by facilitating delamination of vRG from the ventricular surface (35, 36). Zhang and colleagues revealed how these pathways converge into a tripartite ERK/PKA-YAP/TAZ-SHH network. During active neurogenesis, ERK and PKA jointly suppress both YAP/TAZ and Sonic Hedgehog (SHH) signaling, and their progressive decrease in activity at later stages releases gliogenic programs (37, 38).

Recapitulation of vRG at hCOs and disease modeling

Given the complexity of regulating the vRG fate, cortical organoids must recapitulate not only vRG marker expression but also the signaling architecture that sustains progenitor identity. Among all radial glial subtypes, vRG are the most reliably generated across hCO protocols (39-41). Rosebrock and colleagues demonstrated that a short, early course of dual SMAD and WNT inhibition is sufficient to establish robust cortical neural stem cell identity with well-organized rosette structures containing apically localized adherens junctions (42). How well vRG are generated determines the quality of differentiation at later stages of hCOs. Enhanced vRG specification and rosette organization showed a direct correlation with improved emergence of oRG at later timepoints (42). The three-dimensional requirement identified by Scuderi and colleagues (25) provides a mechanistic explanation. Only in 3D vRG maintain the N-cadherin-Notch polarity loop that sustains apical-basal organization and the asymmetric fate-determinant segregation that regulate the neurogenesis afterwards.

Disease gene studies in organoids have dissected vRG regulatory mechanism in a more clear manner, often uncovering progenitor-stage functions of genes that have a known role in mature neurons (Fig. 2, lower panel). Birtele and colleagues showed that SYNGAP1 localizes to the apical domain of vRG and regulates cytoskeletal dynamics and division plane orientation. SYNGAP1 haploinsufficiency disrupted radial glial scaffolding, leading to an imbalance in the progenitor-to-neuron ratio, and accelerating neuronal maturation in both human organoids and mouse cortex (43).

Despite these advances, vRG in current hCO systems remain arrested at an early-to-mid gestational transcriptomic state and do not yet reach the maturational stages observed in second-trimester human fetal cortex. It remains unclear whether the limited developmental states in vRG are sufficient to support the timely emergence of oRG.

OUTER RADIAL GLIA: EXPANSION, SIGNALING, AND ORGANOID MODELING

Discovery and the delamination cascade

Two independent studies published in 2010 identified self-renewing progenitors in the outer subventricular zone (oSVZ) of the human (5) and ferret cortex (44), revealing the importance of basally expanded germinal zone that the classical vRG-centric model had not predicted. These oRG retain a basal process extending toward the pial surface but lack apical contact with the ventricular surface and occupy a proliferative compartment distinct from the ventricular zone (Fig. 2, lower panel) (44-46). oRG are defined behaviorally by mitotic somal translocation (MST), a rapid basal movement of the cell body along the basal fiber that occurs immediately before cytokinesis (47, 48). Single-cell transcriptomic profiling subsequently defined human oRG at the molecular level, identifying HOPX, PTPRZ1, FAM107A, and LIFR as markers that distinguish oRG from vRG (49). In macaque, Betizeau and colleagues tracked individual dividing progenitors by long-term live imaging to reconstruct their division lineages. They found that oSVZ progenitors comprise several morphologically distinct types. These types differ in proliferative behavior and interconvert through bidirectional lineage transitions (50). By expanding the progenitor pool through sustained self-renewal, oRG are thought to contribute to the evolutionary increase in cortical surface area and gyrification in primates and other gyrencephalic mammals (51-54).

vRG do not differentiate into oRG in a single step. The transition requires a coordinated molecular cascade of transcriptional priming, spindle reorientation, and apical junction disassembly. TRNP1, a DNA-associated protein that at high levels promotes radial glial self-renewal and tangential cortical expansion, must be downregulated before oRG can emerge. Its knockdown in the mouse cortex expands oRG-like cells and induces cortical folding in an otherwise lissencephalic brain (55, 56). This downregulation is timely regulated during the cortical development. In ferret, Martinez-Martinez et al. showed that downregulation of TRNP1 together with CDH1 (E-cadherin) triggers a burst of oRG production that expand the oSVZ. Once established, the oRG population sustains itself through self-renewing divisions (57). At the cellular level, the microtubule-associated molecule Lzts1 dose-dependently promotes oblique divisions in mouse cortex by disrupting apical junctional organization. This destabilizes adherens junctions and allows the basally displaced daughter cell to delaminate as oRG (58). Together, these findings establish a delamination cascade, TRNP1 downregulation, LZTS1 upregulation, CDH1 loss, spindle reorientation, in which each step is necessary to convert an apical vRG into a basal oRG (36, 59). Extracellular matrix components enriched in the human oSVZ further promote oRG self-renewal (60).

Expansion signals and self-renewal

Once generated, oRG require Sonic Hedgehog (SHH) signaling to expand. Wang and colleagues first showed that SHH expands basal progenitors and induces cortical folding (61), and Hou and colleagues subsequently demonstrated that SHH operates through a biphasic mechanism in ferret cortex (62). Early SHH activity promotes oRG-producing division modes of vRG, while later SHH signaling shifts to sustaining oRG self-renewal. Matsumoto and colleagues further resolved oRG heterogeneity in ferret by identifying HOPX-positive and HOPX-negative subtypes that differ in self-renewal capacity. The HOPX-positive population accumulates in prospective gyral regions and is selectively regulated by SHH (63). Several additional mechanisms promote oRG expansion. Human- and primate-specific genes are known to further amplify this population: ARHGAP11B expands basal progenitors and drives neocortical expansion in mouse and ferret (64, 65), and TMEM14B expands oRG and induces cortical folding in mouse (54). Hyperactivation of the mTORC1 pathway in SZT2-mutant organoids causes oRG overproduction, which may explain the macrocephaly in patients with SZT2 loss-of-function (66). The LIF/STAT3 axis, active in human fetal oRG, directly connects oRG biology to organoid engineering, as discussed below.

Modeling oRG in hCOs

Modeling and investigating oRG have been substantially advanced, but differences are found acrros protocols (Fig. 2, lower panel). Bershteyn and colleagues first demonstrated that oRG exhibit MST in iPSC-derived hCOs and used this system to show that Miller-Dieker syndrome oRG display prolonged mitosis and altered division planes (67). Walsh and colleagues achieved the most faithful oRG representation to date by activating LIF/STAT3 signaling, which is in general missing in standard neural differentiation media. Their organoids harbored a structured oSVZ-like compartment with HOPX-positive, GFAP-positive, and LIFR-positive oRG that closely matched the marker profile of human fetal oRG (16). Notably, co-culture with pericytes recapitulated the LIF-like effect through endogenous cytokine production, suggesting that more physiological niche-reconstitution approaches are feasible (16). vRG quality is also important in forming oRG. Rosebrock and colleagues showed that optimized cortical patterning enhances both vRG identity and subsequent oRG emergence, linking the two stages in a functional progenitor hierarchy (42). Most standard hCO protocols produce only sparse HOPX-positive cells without true oSVZ laminar organization. MST has been observed but not systematically quantified across platforms, and without a pial surface and basal lamina, organoid oRG basal processes terminate in a structurally incomplete environment. Stress signatures that are well known in hCOs may further confound oRG identity by affecting progenitor transcriptomes (18).

oRG contribute to gliogenesis in addition to neuronal production. Huang and colleagues showed that oRG generate EGFR-expressing pre-oligodendrocyte precursor cells (pre-OPCs) that themselves undergo MST, establishing an oSVZ-derived oligodendroglial lineage (68). In glioblastoma, oRG-like cancer stem cells reactivate the MST program for tumor invasion. PTPRZ1 mediates both translocation and invasiveness, effectively co-opting a developmental mechanism for pathological motility (69). mTORC1-driven oRG overproduction observed in SZT2-mutant organoids further demonstrates that quantitative dysregulation of a single progenitor population can produce clinically significant cortical malformations (66). These findings position oRG at the intersection of cortical expansion, gliogenesis, and disease. Faithful recapitulation of oRG in organoids is therefore a prerequisite for translational applications. Beyond mid-gestation, oRG dominate the progenitor pool during the second trimester but progressively decline as the size of oSVZ decreases toward the third trimester, precisely when truncated radial glia emerge in the ventricular zone. Whether these two populations are developmentally linked, and what signals govern their separation, remains unresolved.

TRUNCATED RADIAL GLIA: EVIDENCE IN THE DEVELOPING BRAIN AND THE QUESTION OF ORGANOID RECAPITULATION

Molecular identity and developmental origin

The late ventricular zone of the developing cortex harbors a progenitor population, namely tRG, that retains apical contact with the ventricular surface but possesses a truncated or absent basal process, terminating within the cortical wall rather than reaching the pia (Fig. 2, upper panel). tRG were first noted in a developmental atlas of the human fetal cortex in 2016 (7). Single-cell transcriptomics analysis at GW 16-24 identified ventricular zone cells with truncated basal processes among catalogued progenitors, revealing morphological heterogeneity within the vRG compartment. However, tRG remained a cell type of unique morphology without a consensus molecular identity or functional characterization until 2023 (8). Transcriptomic profiling of the ferret cortex defined tRG by the expression of CRYAB, FOXJ1, S100B, and CLDN11, and demonstrated that tRG differentiate into ependymal cells and astrocytes (8). The human cortex atlas confirmed tRG as a transcriptomically and functionally unique population with defined differentiation potential (9). However, the expression of CRYAB has been a persistent source of confusion in defining tRG. Thomsen and colleagues classified CRYAB as a vRG-enriched marker at GW16-18 (3), which is not a classification error but reflects the expression of CRYAB at different cell types at different developmental stages - in vRG during mid-gestation and in tRG at later gestation. Furthermore, CRYAB expression increases as a heat-shock protein is upregulated under cellular stress, and thus CRYAB alone cannot reliably define tRG in organoid systems, where the stress-related genes are prevalently expressed. Thus, the combinatorial expression of FOXJ1, S100B, or CLDN11 in addition to CRYAB is essential to annotate tRG (8, 9). More broadly, transcriptomic similarity alone suggests but cannot confirm subtype identity. Where possible, molecular markers should be supported by additional evidence such as process morphology, apical-basal position, and lineage relationship. Cross-species comparisons confirm that tRG are conserved across gyrencephalic mammals. Barcoded lineage tracing in ferret and human revealed multiple parallel radial glial lineages, including a tRG-containing trajectory, that are absent in the lissencephalic mouse cortex (70).

The developmental origin of tRG involves a direct transformation of vRG through basal process retraction. In the human cortex, vRG lose their pia-contacting basal processes and acquire a truncated morphology accompanied by a distinct molecular program (CRYAB and NR4A1 upregulation, HOPX and FAM107A downregulation). This transforms the “continuous” radial glia scaffold into a “discontinuous” scaffold in which oRG basal fibers become the principal conduit to the pial surface during this transition. Pseudo-time trajectory analysis in ferret confirmed a continuous progression from early RG through late RG to tRG (8). In primates, this transformation is more complete. the conventional scaffold disappears entirely by mid-gestation (7). tRG are therefore regarded not as a separately specified lineage but as the terminal gliogenic phase of the vRG lifecycle, governed by progressive decrease in ERK/PKA activity (38).

The ERK/PKA-YAP/TAZ-SHH network and lineage diversification

tRG fate specification converges on a tripartite signaling network of ERK/PKA, YAP/TAZ, and SHH. During active neurogenesis, ERK/PKA signaling maintains radial glia in a neurogenic state. Its decline at late gestational stages then permits gliogenic fate transitions (34). Using mouse genetic models, Zhang and colleagues demonstrated that ERK/PKA simultaneously suppresses both YAP/TAZ and SHH activity. As ERK/PKA progressively weakens, these two parallel fate-determining pathways are de-repressed. Reanalysis of human cortical single-cell data confirmed that this network architecture is conserved (38). YAP and TAZ are transcriptional co-activators of the Hippo pathway that function as mechanotransducers (71) and have established roles in radial glia self-amplification (72, 73). In postnatal cortical progenitors, the two effectors have different functions. YAP promotes cell cycle progression, whereas TAZ drives astrocyte differentiation (74).

Within the tRG lineage, YAP/TAZ activation drives ependymal commitment, while SHH directs an alternative trajectory through tripotential intermediate progenitor cells (Tri-IPCs) (37, 38). Validation in ferret cortex revealed regional specialization. Medial tRG, where YAP/TAZ activity predominates, preferentially generate ependymal cells, whereas lateral tRG, where SHH signaling is stronger, produce Tri-IPCs and their gliogenic and interneurogenic descendants (75).

tRG lineage output is broader than initially suggested. In the postnatal mouse brain, ependymal maturation proceeds through a transcriptional cascade in which Nfix activates Foxj1 (76, 77). FOXJ1 expression is conserved in human tRG-derived ependymal precursors (8, 9), suggesting that this regulatory hierarchy is maintained, though direct demonstration in the human tRG lineage is lacking. In ferret, Bilgic and colleagues demonstrated that tRG simultaneously produce ependymal cells and astrocytes. YAP/TAZ drives ependymal commitment (38, 75), while TAZ may specifically promote the astrocytic arm, as postnatal NPC data suggest (74). In mouse and ferret cortex, the SHH-responsive lateral tRG generates Tri-IPCs that give rise to astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and olfactory bulb interneurons. Distinct downstream pathways regulate each fate, such as Notch for astrocytes, sustained ERK for oligodendrocytes, and continued SHH for interneurons (38, 75). Lineage tracing in human fetal tissue further revealed that tRG maintain a protracted glutamatergic neurogenic capacity and produce late-born neurons expressing deep-layer rather than superficial-layer molecular markers. This departs from the canonical inside-out laminar pattern and may reflect continued generation of subplate-like neurons.

Indirect evidence and the case for organoid representation

The prevailing view has been that hCOs do not recapitulate the full spectrum of fetal radial glia diversity. This view was mainly based on the extensive analysis by Bhaduri and colleagues, who showed that hCO progenitors exhibit elevated stress signatures and impaired molecular subtype specification compared with their in vivo counterparts (18). However, the conclusion that tRG are absent should be considered with caution. This study was published six years ago, prior to the detailed molecular definition of tRG (8, 9), and its analytical framework lacked the reference signatures necessary for tRG annotation. The association of CRYAB with stress response detailed in the preceding section further limited tRG detection in the study. tRG marker definitions, together with more recent fetal brain atlases (78), now provide the molecular signatures against which organoid data can be retrospectively interrogated.

Accumulating evidence nonetheless suggest that tRG, or at minimum tRG-competent progenitors, exist in organoid systems. Vassalou and colleagues generated ependymal cells in brain organoids by forcing expression of the multiciliogenesis regulators GEMC1 and MCIDAS in apical radial glia (19). In vivo, the ependymal lineage passes through a tRG intermediate (38), and the GEMC1/MCIDAS program acts downstream of YAP/TAZ-mediated ependymal commitment. The “apical radial glia” in their system may therefore include cells that meet current tRG criteria, though this was not directly assessed. Separately, Watanabe and colleagues detected CRYAB-positive radial glia in neurosphere-derived aggregates (NEDAS), a three-dimensional culture system that shares features with hCOs (79). Third, Scandura and colleagues showed that KIAA0319-knockout shifted hCO progenitor differentiation from vRG toward a tRG-associated transcriptomic profile (20). Because this single genetic perturbation reduced an endogenous constraint rather than introducing an exogenous program, the result indicates that the transcriptional machinery for tRG specification is latent within organoid progenitors. Such a developmental shift by gene knockout may reflect a compensatory state rather than a bona fide developmental trajectory and therefore points to latent competence rather than authentic tRG identity. Collectively, these findings establish latent tRG competence in organoid progenitors but do not demonstrate authentic tRG identity.

Despite these evidence supporting the presence of tRG in organoids, we acknowledge its limitations. No study has identified the cells co-expressing CRYAB and FOXJ1 that have truncated morphology in any organoid system, and no lineage tracing from a defined tRG population in organoids has been reported. We therefore do not claim that tRG definitively exist in cortical organoids. Rather, we suggest that (i) current evidence for their absence is weak, depending on analytical frameworks that were performed before tRG were well defined; (ii) multiple independent observations are consistent with the presence of tRG; and (iii) the field is better equipped to resolve this question directly. Recently established tRG molecular signatures, together with an increasingly detailed understanding of the signaling networks governing tRG specification, provide the foundation for systematic investigation of this cell type in organoid systems. As these molecular tools and analytical frameworks are applied systematically, the field will be positioned to deliver a definitive answer.

CROSS-SUBTYPE COMPARISON OF ORGANOID FIDELITY

Comparative fidelity across the three radial glial subtypes

Organoid fidelity at the radial glial level can be evaluated through four features of each subtype: marker expression, process morphology, spatial position, and lineage output. Meeting all four reflects faithful recapitulation, whereas matching only a subset reflects partial representation. The three radial glial subtypes occupy markedly different positions along a gradient of hCO recapitulation fidelity of in vivo cortex. vRG are effectively generated. Most protocols reliably produce SOX2＋/PAX6＋ progenitors organized into rosettes that exhibit apical-basal polarity, undergo interkinetic nuclear migration, and generate cortical neurons through asymmetric division (12, 25, 42). Velasco and colleagues showed that optimized protocols yield virtually indistinguishable cell types across individual organoids, with 20 of 21 samples matching in cell-class composition, confirming that vRG-dependent neurogenesis is robust and reproducible (80, 81). oRG generation, by contrast, remains inconsistent. The LIF/STAT3 breakthrough provided a defined pathway toward oSVZ-like expansion, but most cortical organoid protocols still produce oRG only sporadically. No protocol to date generates a spatially segregated oSVZ zone with the cellular density of the human fetal cortex (16, 67). This architectural deficit impedes not only oRG laminar organization but also the morphological and positional criteria required to identify tRG, whose definition depends on apical contact combined with a truncated basal process; incomplete spatial organization is therefore a direct barrier to subtype identification rather than a cosmetic limitation. tRG occupy the far end of this gradient, not merely difficult to generate, but untested. Single-cell genomic analyses confirmed that organoids recapitulate human-specific developmental trajectories distinct from those of non-human primates (82, 83). Established reproducibility benchmarks assessed broad cell classes: excitatory neurons, interneuron progenitors, and astroglia. Finer progenitor subtypes, including tRG, fell outside the analytical framework entirely (80).

Microenvironmental gaps and the barrier to maturation

Organoid cultures lack several microenvironmental cues that are present in the developing brain, each with specific relevance to radial glial biology (84). These include cerebrospinal fluid contact, vascular perfusion, pial basement membrane, meningeal signaling, and the mechanical forces from ventricular pressure. For oRG, the lack of a pial surface means that basal processes, which in vivo extend to the pia mater and transduce extracellular signals, terminate without their normal target. For tRG, this deficit may be more consequential. The YAP/TAZ pathway central to tRG specification is mechanosensitive, and ventricular zone remodeling during late gestation involves hydrostatic pressure changes that organoids do not experience (85). Transplantation experiments reinforce this point. hCOs grafted into rodent cortex undergo pronounced neuronal maturation and accelerated astrogliogenesis, demonstrating that organoid cells possess greater maturational capacity than standard culture conditions elicit (86, 87). Moreover, long-term culture studies show that hCOs can reach late developmental stages relevant to tRG biology. Sloan and colleagues maintained human cortical spheroids for 590 days, during which astrocytes progressively transitioned from a fetal toward a more mature transcriptomic state (88). Gordon and colleagues confirmed that long-term cultures recapitulate key early postnatal developmental transitions (89). These extended cultures also produce complex oscillatory network activity resembling preterm neonatal electroencephalography (90). Because tRG arise during the late neurogenesis-to-gliogenesis transition, their detection is expected to depend critically on culture duration: only long-term systems reaching later developmental stages are likely to generate or reveal this subtype, whereas shorter protocols may lack tRG simply because they are harvested before the relevant developmental window opens.

DISCUSSION

Human cortical organoids have changed how we study human brain development by providing direct access to progenitor populations that cannot be studied in living human tissue. hCOs readily recapitulate two of the three radial glial subtypes. vRG emerge reliably across most protocols, forming polarized rosettes that execute asymmetric division and sequential neurogenesis. oRG can now be enriched through adjusted medium supplementation to approximate the marker profile of their in vivo counterparts. These advances establish hCOs as the only system in which human-specific radial glial biology can be studied directly. The natural next step is to determine whether the third subtype, truncated radial glia, may also be present. tRG were molecularly defined in 2023 and further resolved in the human cortex in 2025 (8, 9). The evidence reviewed here suggests that tRG-like progenitors exist in hCOs but remain unrecognized due to stress-induced CRYAB expression and reliance on single-marker annotation. Independent observations, from ependymal cell generation to tRG-directed transcriptomic shifts upon genetic perturbation, support this possibility and await definitive confirmation.

In vivo, vRG, oRG, and tRG function as a temporally coordinated relay in which each subtype enables the next developmental phase. tRG possess broad lineage potential, generating ependymal cells, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and late-born glutamatergic neurons, and serve as the principal gateway to the gliogenic phase. Capturing this third node would extend organoid utility from neurogenic modeling, where hCOs already perform well, into gliogenic territory. The tools needed to address this question are now in place. The ERK/PKA-YAP/TAZ-SHH signaling framework provides a rational basis for targeted tRG induction, and long-term culture platforms already reach the developmental stages at which tRG emerge in vivo. We suggest that the most productive first step is systematic reanalysis of existing long-term datasets with updated tRG signatures. hCOs have repeatedly exceeded initial expectations, generating oRG, producing complex oscillatory activity, and recapitulating human-specific developmental trajectories. There is good reason to expect that tRG will also prove accessible once the field searches with the right markers. Current tRG signaling data derive largely from ferret and mouse and require human validation. The mechanosensitive YAP/TAZ pathway may need biomechanical support that standard suspension cultures cannot provide. These challenges point to productive experiments rather than fundamental barriers.

Future assessment frameworks should adopt the complete three-subtype hierarchy, vRG, oRG, and tRG, as a standard benchmark for organoid fidelity. hCOs have already shown they can recapitulate the first two nodes of this developmental relay. Extending that success to the third will clarify how faithfully these systems represent the full arc of human cortical development and whether tRG can be used for translational applications that remain out of reach today.
