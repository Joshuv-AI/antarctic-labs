 Task 1.6 – Three-Dimensional Printing of All-Solid-State Lithium Batteries
 (Jianchao Ye, Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory)

Objectives. The project has two primary objectives: (1) down select three-dimensional (3D) printing and
post-processing approaches for solid-state electrolyte (SSE) / cathode integration, and (2) understand battery
failure mechanisms via ex situ and in situ characterization.
Impact. The adoption of a thin separator layer, thick cathode structure, and metallic lithium anode will lead to
electric vehicle batteries with > 350 Wh/kg energy density for increased mileage per charge. The higher ionic
conductivity with suppression of lithium dendrite growth will allow high critical current densities for
fast-charging applications. The improved electrode/electrolyte contact will increase battery cycle life for
long-term service.
Approach. The technical approaches include advanced manufacturing based on 3D printing and related
techniques, ex situ / in situ characterizations, and battery testing. Direct-ink writing and projection micro
stereolithography 3D-printing techniques will be employed to fabricate thin-film SSEs (< 100 µm), gradient
SSEs, and 3D interfaces for battery performance evaluation. Three approaches, including sintering-free, hybrid,
and co-sintering, will be investigated. The knowledge obtained from studying these approaches is transferable
and complementary to each technique.
Out-Year Goals. The long-term vision of the team is to 3D-print all components of the all-solid-state lithium
battery (ASSLB) to facilitate the scale-up of ASSLB manufacturing. In this project, the team will tackle the
issues emerging from integrating solid electrolyte with electrodes. The project goal is to demonstrate a
successful 3D-printing approach to integrate SSE into electrodes and show reasonable capacity retention
(> 80%) after 300 cycles at current density ≥ 1 mA/cm2.
Collaborations. The team will work closely with a computational partner (Task 3.6 led by B. Wood) to better
understand battery failure mechanisms and design new battery architectures and chemistries for
performance improvement.

 Milestones

1. Successfully printing a 3D porous separator for 250 Wh/kg gravimetric energy density design, with
 satisfying porosity and mechanical/thermal stabilities. (Q1, FY 2025; Completed)
2. Achieving conductivity > 10-4 S/cm at room temperature along with good stability with lithium anode and
 LiFePO4 or Ni-Mn-Co cathode for the polymer electrolyte (PE). (Q2, FY 2025; Completed)
3. Successfully printing a 3D porous separator to further increase the gravimetric energy density to
 300 Wh/kg, with improved chemical/mechanical/thermal stabilities. (Q3, FY 2025; Completed)
4. Integrating the PE into the printed porous separator while maintaining > 10-4 S/cm at room-temperature
 conductivity. (Q4, FY 2025; Completed)

 Task 1.6 – Ye, LLNL

 Progress Report

The ionic conductivity of the solid polymer electrolyte (SPE) infilled 1,6-Hexanediol diacrylate (HDDA)
membranes was measured to be 6 x 10-4 S/cm, as reported in FY25 Q2, fulfilling FY25 Q4 milestone. The team
continued to design and print a series of three-dimensional membranes (3DMs) for systematic studies of
geometrical effects on 3D battery performance.

Interdigitated and honeycomb 3DMs were printed for Li/LiFePO4, Li/NMC, and Graphite/NMC cell use, using
a BMF S240 printer. Figure 1 shows the optical images from two sides to reveal the geometries. They were all
designed with 50 µm thick membrane, 260 µm overall 3DM thickness, 8mm-diameter circle, and 10 x 10 mm
overall sample dimensions. The solid edges were added to allow facile sample transfer and prevent shorting
from edges during infilling and cell assembly. Anode and cathode channel volumes were varied according to
the corresponding chemistries. Honeycomb design is close to concentric 3D battery concept, but with struts for
mechanical stability, and it is more isotropic than the interdigitated structure.

 Task 1.6 - Figure 1. Optical images of 3DMs with various architectural designs.

The team also collaborated with LLNL simulation team (Dr. Hanyu Li, Dr. Thomas Roy) to develop topology
optimized 3DMs to maximize the active material utilization in ultra-thick electrodes. As shown in Figure 1 right
two panels, one topology optimization is from 2D simulation of a unit cell followed with mirror imaging,
repeating, and extrusion. The other one is from 3D simulation of a unit cell that is then mirrored and repeated
for expanding to larger areas. A unique feature of the topology optimized 3DMs is the enlarged head of the
interdigitated fingers, in direct comparison with straight channels in other designs. The rationale is that the head
region is surrounded by the opposite electrode in three directions, while the neck region is surrounded only in
two directions.

In addition, 3DMs with different resin recipes, light intensities, and layer thicknesses were printed to tune the
intrinsic porosity and mechanical properties. They were summarized in Table 1. While increasing intensities
can improve mechanical strength, it also reduces porosity, which is reflected from the mass variation. The
porosity can be increased by using a lower HDDA content recipe and using a larger layer thickness setting. So
far, the 25wt% HDDA recipe with 20 µm layer thickness led to the lowest 3DM mass. However, the recipe still
needs fine tuning to prevent over polymerization, which was observed when using higher intensities or when
the photo absorber content was not sufficient. As shown in Figure 2 and Table 1, the top separator thickness is
larger compared with computer design values. Lateral over polymerization was also observed in 25% HDDA
recipe. This issue can be addressed by increasing photoabsorber content (1.2 wt% of HDDA) to decrease the
light penetration depth and lateral diffusion.
