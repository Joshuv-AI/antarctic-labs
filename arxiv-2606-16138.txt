Closing the Approximation Gap in Simulation-free Latent SDEs
Henry D. Smith Brian L. Trippe Scott W. Linderman Stanford University {smithhd,btrippe,swl1}@stanford.edu

Abstract
Recovering dynamical systems from noisy observations is a recurring challenge across scientific domains, including neuroscience and physics. Latent stochastic differential equations (SDEs) address this by modeling the system as an unobserved state that evolves according to a learnable SDE and generates the observations. Variational inference (VI) provides a tractable objective for fitting latent SDEs. Traditional VI algorithms evaluate this objective by numerical simulation over a time discretization, trading fidelity for computational cost. A recent class of algorithms, simulation-free VI, sidesteps this tradeoff by parameterizing the posterior through its instantaneous marginals rather than its drift. In this work, we show that the efficiency of existing simulation-free VI algorithms comes at a price: their parameterizations restrict the approximate posterior to a subset of the SDEs available to simulation-based methods, degrading posterior inference and parameter learning. We propose Helmholtz-SDE, a simulation-free VI algorithm that closes this gap by optimizing over path laws compatible with a prescribed collection of marginals. Helmholtz-SDE recovers dynamics more faithfully than prior simulation-free methods, with the largest gains under high posterior uncertainty. It further matches the performance of simulation-based VI at a fraction of the runtime.



1
Introduction

Latent stochastic differential equations (SDEs) enable the unsupervised discovery of dynamical systems from noisy, irregularly sampled time series, a setting common across scientific domains.
They posit an unobserved latent state that evolves continuously in time and generates observations at a finite set of time points
[
2
,
41
]
.
Latent SDEs have been applied to neuroscience
[
20
,
29
]
, disease modeling
[
19
]
, and reduced-order fluid dynamics
[
15
]
.
Fitting these models requires both learning the latent dynamics and approximating the posterior over continuous trajectories.

Variational inference (VI) translates the problem of fitting latent SDEs into an optimization problem.
However, evaluating the variational objective (ELBO) requires computing an integral over the full time horizon (a path-space KL divergence).
Simulation-based VI algorithms estimate this term by sweeping across a time discretization at each gradient step, either by integrating an ODE to compute the posterior marginals or by simulating sample trajectories from the variational SDE.
A recent line of work, simulation-free VI
[
15
,
16
,
6
]
, sidesteps sequential simulation using stochastic VI.
Rather than integrating, they compute an unbiased estimate of the objective by sampling a random time together with a state at that time.
They make this estimate efficient by parameterizing the posterior through its one-time marginal distributions, i.e., the distribution of the latent state at each time, allowing states to be sampled directly without simulating the preceding trajectory.

In this work, we show that the family of approximate posterior distributions over which simulation-free VI optimizes is strictly smaller than that available to simulation-based VI.
One-time marginals do not determine a path law.
Existing simulation-free methods choose a single compatible drift from many possibilities, implicitly restricting the variational posterior.
When observations are sparse or noisy, this arbitrary choice can substantially degrade both posterior inference and learned dynamics.

We propose
Helmholtz-SDE
, a simulation-free VI algorithm that removes this restriction.
Starting from any drift compatible with the prescribed marginals, we compute a Helmholtz correction that preserves those marginals while optimizing the ELBO.
The resulting posterior family remains simulation-free but spans a larger class of path laws, closing the gap to simulation-based VI.

Model.

We study

K

Let

The prior over the latent state is the solution to an SDE

\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}}\!\!:\;d\bm{x}(t)=\bm{f}_{p}(\bm{x}(t)|\bm{\theta})dt+\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta})^{1/2}d\bm{w}(t),\quad\bm{x}(0)\sim p(\bm{x},0|\bm{\theta}),\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

For simplicity of presentation, we restrict notation to a time-independent drift and a state- and time-independent diffusion coefficient.

These observations are conditionally independent given the latent state:
p(\bm{y}|(\bm{x}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T},\bm{\theta})=\prod_{n=1}^{N}p(\bm{y}(t_{n})|\bm{x}(t_{n}),\bm{\theta})
, where
\bm{y}=\{\bm{y}(t_{n})\}_{n=1}^{N}
.
We describe our technical assumptions on the generative model in
Section

A.1
.

There are two goals in latent SDE models: (i) to learn the parameters
\bm{\theta}
that maximize
\log p(\bm{y}|\bm{\theta})

(\bm{x}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}

\mathcal{D}

\bm{\theta}

In practice, one may have multiple trials
\{\bm{y}^{(m)}\}_{m=1}^{M}

In this case, the model parameters
\bm{\theta}
are learned using all trials, and the posterior factorizes over trials. For simplicity, we suppress the trial index
m
.

Intractability.

Both goals of learning and inference are generally intractable.
For learning, evaluating the marginal likelihood
p(\bm{y}|\bm{\theta})

involves marginalizing over the prior on latent trajectories (
1
); this integral cannot be evaluated in closed form.
For inference, the posterior law is the solution to an SDE with the same diffusion coefficient
\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}
as the prior and drift obtained by solving a linear partial differential equation
[
39
,
62
,
51
,
4
]
.
In nonlinear or non-Gaussian settings, the posterior drift is unavailable in closed form, and solving the associated PDE can be computationally prohibitive even for small
K
.

3
Background

Variational inference (VI) provides a tractable framework for joint learning and inference in latent SDEs. We first describe the variational objective and survey existing VI algorithms. We then identify a class of stochastic VI algorithms that avoid sequential simulation, the setting of our method.

3.1
Variational inference for latent SDE models

Variational inference (VI) approximates the intractable posterior with a distribution belonging to a tractable family, known as a
variational family

[
31
,
12
]
.
For latent SDEs, we consider the family
\mathcal{Q}
comprised of diffusion processes

\mathbb{Q}_{\bm{\phi}}\!\!:\;d\bm{x}(t)=\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x}(t),t|\bm{\phi})dt+\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta})^{1/2}d\bm{w}(t),\quad\bm{x}(0)\sim q(\bm{x},0|\bm{\phi}),\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

(2)

where the variational parameters
\bm{\phi}
specify the posterior drift
\bm{f}_{q}(\cdot|\bm{\phi})
and the initial state distribution
q(\bm{x},0|\bm{\phi})
.
This family is chosen because, when
\bm{f}_{q}(\cdot|\bm{\phi})
and
q(\bm{x},0|\bm{\phi})
are sufficiently expressive,
the exact posterior will be in
\mathcal{Q}

[
28
, see, e.g.,]
.

We minimize the negative evidence lower bound (ELBO) over distributions in
\mathcal{Q}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{n=1}^{N}{-}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{\bm{\phi}}}\log p(\bm{y}(t_{n})|\bm{x}(t_{n}),\bm{\theta})}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}}}+\underbrace{\textrm{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\left(q(\bm{x},0|\bm{\phi})\;\|\;p(\bm{x},0|\bm{\theta})\right)}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{init}}}+

(3)

\displaystyle\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{\bm{\phi}}}\|\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta})^{-1/2}\{\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x}(t),t|\bm{\phi})-\bm{f}_{p}(\bm{x}(t)|\bm{\theta})\}\|^{2}dt}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{path}}},

where
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}}
is the reconstruction loss,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{init}}
is the KL divergence to the prior initial distribution, and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{path}}
is the path-space KL divergence to the prior.

The negative ELBO is an upper bound on the negative marginal log likelihood
-\log p(\bm{y}|\bm{\theta})
, and the gap is the KL divergence between
\mathbb{Q}_{\bm{\phi}}
and the exact posterior.
Therefore, when the exact posterior is contained in
\mathcal{Q}
it is the unique minimizer of
eq.

3
.

Section

A.2
gives a derivation.
However, unlike the negative log likelihood, the ELBO provides a tractable objective for learning model parameters, which we seek to estimate by jointly optimizing with respect to
\bm{\phi}
and
\bm{\theta}
.

Existing VI algorithms for latent SDEs fall into two broad classes based on how they evaluate the negative ELBO
eq.

3
: simulation-based and simulation-free.
We describe each in turn.

3.2
Simulation-based VI

Simulation-based
algorithms evaluate the path KL
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{path}}
over a discretization of the interval
[0,T]
at each gradient step.
When
\mathbb{Q}_{\bm{\phi}}
is restricted to a tractable family, such as a Gaussian process with affine
\bm{f}_{q}
and state-independent
\bm{\Sigma}
, the marginals of
\mathbb{Q}_{\bm{\phi}}
can be computed by integrating an ODE
[
2
,
28
,
3
,
18
,
69
,
38
,
71
,
52
,
53
]
.
When
\bm{f}_{q}
is parameterized more flexibly, e.g., by a neural network
[
41
,
58
,
67
,
34
,
49
]
, samples
\bm{x}(t)\sim\mathbb{Q}_{\bm{\phi}}
are obtained by simulating the variational SDE (
2
).
In both cases, the time complexity per gradient step scales with the discretization of
[0,T]
, introducing a tradeoff between approximation fidelity and computational cost.

3.3
Simulation-free VI

Simulation-free
VI algorithms introduce two ideas to avoid discretizing the variational SDE and accelerate optimization of
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi}).

The first is to optimize
a Monte Carlo estimate of
eq.

3

\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})=-N\log p(\bm{y}(t_{n})|\bm{x}(t_{n}))+\log\frac{q(\bm{x}(0),0|\bm{\phi})}{p(\bm{x}(0),0|\bm{\theta})}+\frac{T}{2}\|\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta})^{-1/2}(\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x}(t),t|\bm{\phi})-\bm{f}_{p}(\bm{x}(t)|\bm{\theta}))\|^{2}.

Here,
n\sim\mathcal{U}(\{1,\dots,N\})
is a random observation index,
t\sim\mathcal{U}([0,T])
is a random time, and
\bm{x}(0),\bm{x}(t_{n}),\bm{x}(t)\sim\mathbb{Q}_{\bm{\phi}}
are posterior samples.
The estimate
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})
avoids the sum over all observations in
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}}
, the integral over the full trajectory in
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{path}}
, and the expectations over
\mathbb{Q}_{\bm{\phi}}
in all three terms of
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})
.
Compared to optimizing the negative ELBO, this approach introduces variance that is managed by averaging across Monte Carlo samples.
Because the estimate samples random times rather than full paths, optimization amounts to a stochastic VI algorithm
[
26
]
.

The second idea is to parameterize the drift
\bm{f}_{q}
that defines
\mathbb{Q}_{\bm{\phi}}
implicitly through its one-time marginal distributions.
In order for optimizing
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})
to provide runtime benefits over optimizing the negative ELBO, we must be able to draw samples
\bm{x}(t)\sim\mathbb{Q}_{\bm{\phi}}
without simulating the variational SDE
eq.

2
.
This is achieved by reversing the usual construction of
\mathbb{Q}_{\bm{\phi}}
: we first prescribe the marginal distribution of the posterior at any single time
\{q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
and then choose a drift
\bm{f}_{q}
compatible with those marginals.
One can sample
\bm{x}(t)
from the posterior with time complexity that does not depend on
t
.

For example, SDE Matching (
6
) parameterizes the posterior as having Gaussian marginals,
q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}(t|\bm{\phi}),\bm{S}(t|\bm{\phi}))
and show that a time-dependent, affine drift
\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})=\bm{A}_{q}(t|\bm{\phi})\bm{x}+\bm{b}_{q}(t|\bm{\phi})
defines a variational SDE with precisely these marginals.

and
\bm{S}(t|\bm{\phi}))

\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})=\left[\left(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{S}(t|\bm{\phi})^{1/2}\right)\bm{S}(t|\bm{\phi})^{-1/2}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta})\bm{S}(t|\bm{\phi})^{-1}\right][\bm{x}(t)-\bm{m}(t|\bm{\phi})]+\frac{d}{dt}\bm{m}(t|\bm{\phi}),

(4)

where

SVISE (

4
Helmholtz-SDE: Augmented simulation-free variational inference

The simulation-free VI framework we described in
Section

3.3
leaves an important ambiguity unresolved: the one-time marginal distributions

\{q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
do not uniquely determine the variational path law
\mathbb{Q}_{\bm{\phi}}
.
In general, many drifts
\bm{f}_{q}
realize the same collection of marginals, and existing simulation-free VI algorithms resolve this ambiguity by selecting a particular compatible drift for each set of marginals, independently of the variational objective in
eq.

3
.
This choice is not a benign implementation detail: drifts with identical one-time marginals can induce different path laws, including different multi-time joint distributions
(\bm{x}(s),\bm{x}(t))
for
s\neq t
(
Figure

1
).
When posterior uncertainty is high, fixing a single compatible drift can therefore substantially degrade both posterior inference and learned dynamics.

We introduce Helmholtz-SDE, a simulation-free VI algorithm that removes this restriction by optimizing over path laws sharing the same one-time marginals.
We first characterize the path-law ambiguity left unresolved by marginal-based parameterizations: drifts realizing the same marginals differ by divergence-free vector fields, which can be added without altering those marginals (
Section

4.1
).
We then show that the variational objective selects an optimal drift among this class (
Section

4.2
).
Finally, we turn this characterization into a practical algorithm for Gaussian one-time marginals and state-independent diffusion (
Section

4.3
).

4.1
The Fokker-Planck equation and divergence-free vector fields

The Fokker-Planck equation characterizes the ambiguity in the posterior path-law that arises from parameterizing the one-time marginals.
Let
\mathbb{Q}_{\bm{\phi}}\in\mathcal{Q}
be a variational distribution with drift
\bm{f}_{q}
and one-time marginals
\{q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
. The marginals evolve according to the PDE

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})=-\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\bigl[q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})\bigr]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\bm{\Sigma}_{ij}(\bm{\theta})\partial_{\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{j}}q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi}),\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

(5)

known as the
Fokker-Planck equation
, where
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\bm{f}=\sum_{i}\partial_{\bm{x}_{i}}\bm{f}_{i}(\bm{x})
is the divergence operator.

Equation

5
depends on the variational drift
\bm{f}_{q}
only through the divergence of
q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})
.
Consequently, any vector field
\bm{v}
satisfying
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot[q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})\bm{v}(\bm{x},t)]=0
for all
(\bm{x},t)
leaves the marginals unchanged when added to
\bm{f}_{q}
.
We call such vector fields
q
-divergence-free
. The following
proposition shows that divergence-free vector fields are the only source of non-uniqueness.

Proposition 1
.

Consider two diffusions having drifts
\bm{f}
and
\tilde{\bm{f}}
and common diffusion coefficient. They induce the same one-time marginals
\{q(\bm{x},t)\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
iff
\bm{f}-\tilde{\bm{f}}
is
q
-divergence-free.

Proof.

One analyzes the Fokker-Planck equations for the two diffusions. See
Section

A.3
.
\square

4.2
Optimizing over path laws at fixed marginals

In
Section

4.1
, we identified the family of drifts compatible with a given set of one-time marginal distributions.
Existing simulation-free VI algorithms commit to a single member, independent of the ELBO.
We show next that optimizing the ELBO over this family produces an optimal drift.

Problem formulation.

Fix a collection of one-time marginal distributions
\{q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
.
We call a drift
\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})
a
reference drift
for these marginals if the SDE with drift
\bm{f}_{q}
and diffusion coefficient
\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}
induces one-time marginals
\{q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
.
For Gaussian one-time marginals,
eq.

4
provides an example of a reference drift.
By
Proposition

1
, every other drift with the same one-time marginals can be written as
\bm{f}_{q}+\bm{v}
, where
\bm{v}(\bm{x},t)
is
q
-divergence-free. Thus, optimizing over all path laws compatible with these marginals is equivalent to optimizing over all divergence-free perturbations to the reference drift.

In the ELBO, the reconstruction loss and the initial-time KL are unchanged by
\bm{v}
, so the only dependence on the path law is through the path KL. The optimal compatible drift solves

\inf_{\bm{v}}\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\|\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta})^{-1/2}\{\bm{f}_{p}(\bm{x}(t)|\bm{\theta})-\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x}(t),t|\bm{\phi})-\bm{v}(\bm{x}(t),t)\}\|^{2},

(6)

subject to
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot[\bm{v}(\bm{x},t)q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})]=0,\ (\bm{x},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{K}\times[0,T]
.
Since modifying
\bm{v}
at time
s
does not affect the marginal distribution at time
t
, the problem decouples over times
0\leq t\leq T

Solution via the Helmholtz decomposition.

Define the
residual vector field

\bm{r}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})=\bm{f}_{p}(\bm{x}|\bm{\theta})-\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})
which measures the gap between the prior and the reference posterior drifts.
Intuitively, we would like to choose
\bm{v}=\bm{r}
so that
eq.

6
becomes zero.
However, the divergence-free constraint prevents us from adding the part of
\bm{r}
that would alter the marginals.

To isolate the part of
\bm{r}
that alters the marginals, we appeal to the
q
-weighted Helmholtz decomposition

\bm{r}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})=\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta})\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi(\bm{x},t)+\bm{h}(\bm{x},t),

(7)

where
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi
is a gradient field and
\bm{h}
is a
q
-divergence-free vector field
[
10
]
.

\bm{\Sigma}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi
points in directions that alter the one-time marginals, while
\bm{h}
points in directions that leave them invariant.
The
q
-weighted Helmholtz decomposition thus separates
\bm{r}
into exactly the two components we care about: the part we must leave alone to preserve marginals, and the part we are free to add.

Substituting
\bm{r}=\bm{f}_{p}-\bm{f}_{q}
into
eq.

6
and using the
q
-weighted Helmholtz decomposition yields

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\|\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta})^{-1/2}\{\bm{h}(\bm{x}(t),t)-\bm{v}(\bm{x}(t),t)\}\|^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\|\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta})^{1/2}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi(\bm{x}(t),t)\|^{2}.

(8)

The cross term vanishes by integration by parts, using that
\bm{h}
and
\bm{v}
are
q
-divergence-free. The second term in
eq.

8
does not depend on
\bm{v}
, so the optimum of
eq.

6
is achieved by
\bm{v}^{\star}=\bm{h}
.

In other words, among all diffusions sharing the prescribed marginals, the negative ELBO is minimized by adding the
q
-divergence-free component of
\bm{r}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})
to the reference drift.
While previous simulation-free VI algorithms commit to a single drift consistent with a set of marginals, we have shown that it is possible to optimize over
all

4.3
A variational inference algorithm for Gaussian one-time marginals

Next, we translate our insights into a simulation-free VI algorithm that optimizes over multiple path laws for each collection of one-time marginals.
We follow prior works
[
15
,
16
,
6
]
and take the variational family to have Gaussian one-time marginals
q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}(t|\bm{\phi}),\bm{S}(t|\bm{\phi}))
with
\bm{m}(t|\bm{\phi})
and
\bm{S}(t|\bm{\phi})
continuously differentiable in
t
and
\bm{S}(t|\bm{\phi})
positive definite.
We further restrict to state- and time-independent diffusion
\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta})^{1/2}
.

To leverage the simulation-free stochastic VI framework from
Section

3.3
, we must compute the variational drift for each collection of marginals
\{q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})\}
. By

4.2
, the optimal such drift is given by the
q
-weighted Helmholtz decomposition of the residual
\bm{r}=\bm{f}_{p}-\bm{f}_{q}
, where we choose the reference drift to be that in
eq.

4
. We now describe a tractable approximation to this decomposition and then state the resulting theoretical guarantee.
Algorithm

1

Polynomial approximation.

As we discussed in
Section

4.2

q
-weighted Helmholtz decomposition admits a probabilistic solution that can be approximated via Monte Carlo. However, in our preliminary experiments, we observed that this approximation produced high variance gradient estimates. We therefore approximate
\bm{r}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})
by a low-order Taylor expansion about the posterior mean
\bm{m}(t|\bm{\phi})
and compute the
q
-weighted Helmholtz decomposition of the polynomial exactly.

As a simple example, let
\tilde{\bm{r}}^{(1)}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})
denote the linearization of
\bm{r}
about
\bm{m}(t|\bm{\phi})
:

\tilde{\bm{r}}^{(1)}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})=\bm{r}(\bm{m}(t|\bm{\phi}),t|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})+D_{\bm{x}}\bm{r}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})|_{\bm{x}=\bm{m}(t|\bm{\phi})}(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t|\bm{\phi})),

(10)

where
D_{\bm{x}}\bm{r}
is the Jacobian of
\bm{r}
.
In the setting of Gaussian marginals, the
q
-weighted Helmholtz decomposition of a polynomial produces gradient and divergence-free components of the same degree.
The decomposition of
\tilde{\bm{r}}^{(1)}
is therefore linear, and its coefficients can be obtained by solving a linear system.
The computation scales cubically in the latent dimension.
Since the reference drift
\bm{f}_{q}
is also linear, the corrected SDE has linear drift, and its path law is a Gaussian process.

More generally, for an order-
\ell
polynomial approximation
\tilde{\bm{r}}^{(\ell)}(\bm{x},t)
, the Helmholtz decomposition incurs time complexity
O(K^{2+\ell})
, which is tractable for linear and quadratic approximations in moderate latent dimensions; see
Section

B.3
for details. Corrections with
\ell\geq 2
leave the Gaussian process family, allowing non-Gaussian multi-time joint distributions while preserving Gaussian marginals. All experiments in
Section

6
use
\ell=1
; we observed no notable improvements with
\ell>1
.

Optimality of Helmholtz-SDE.

When the prior is linear and the observation model is linear-Gaussian, the true posterior is a Gaussian process given by the Kalman–Bucy smoother
[
32
]
.
Existing simulation-free VI algorithms commit to a single posterior drift for each
(\bm{m}(t|\bm{\phi}),\bm{S}(t|\bm{\phi}))
and, as we show in
Section

C.1
, can incur an
unbounded
KL gap to the true posterior even in this conjugate setting.
Helmholtz-SDE provably closes the gap:

Theorem 1

(Optimality of Helmholtz-SDE in the conjugate setting)
.

Suppose the prior
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}}
is linear and the observation model is linear and Gaussian. Let
\mathcal{Q}
be the family of path laws induced by Helmholtz-SDE applied to all continuously differentiable
\bm{m}(t)
and positive-definite
\bm{S}(t)
. Then
\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}}\textrm{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\left(\mathbb{Q}\;\|\;\mathbb{Q}^{\star}\right)=0
, where
\mathbb{Q}^{\star}
is the true posterior.

Proof.

Linearity of the residual makes the Helmholtz decomposition exact; see
Section

B.2
.
\square

7
Discussion

Simulation-free variational inference promises the accuracy of simulation-based algorithms at a fraction of the cost, but only when the variational family is rich enough to represent the posterior. We have shown that under high posterior uncertainty, existing simulation-free algorithms fall short: by committing to an arbitrary drift consistent with their one-time marginals, they restrict the posterior to a subset of the path laws available to simulation-based methods. Helmholtz-SDE removes this restriction. Starting from any reference drift, it adds a closed-form correction that preserves the prescribed marginals while optimizing over a larger family of path laws. The result is a simulation-free algorithm that closes the expressivity gap to simulation-based VI and yields more faithful recovery of the underlying dynamics.

Two limitations point to natural extensions.
First, the analytical form for the Helmholtz correction depends on the state independence of the diffusion coefficient and Gaussianity of the marginals. Relaxing either assumption, for example by learning the divergence-free correction, would broaden the algorithm’s applicability.
Second, higher-order polynomial corrections to the residual scale unfavorably as the latent dimension grows; in our experiments we did not observe gains beyond the linear approximation, but more efficient parameterizations may be beneficial in settings we have not explored.
More broadly, we view simulation-free VI as a promising path toward scalable unsupervised discovery of dynamical systems, and Helmholtz-SDE as a step toward making that path competitive with, and ultimately preferable to, its simulation-based counterpart.

Assumption (A1)

\displaystyle\|\bm{f}(\bm{x},t)-\bm{f}(\bm{y},t)\|+\|\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x},t)^{1/2}-\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{y},t)^{1/2}\|_{F}\leq C_{1}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|,\quad

\displaystyle\text{(Lipschitz)}

\displaystyle\|\bm{f}(\bm{x},t)\|+\|\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x},t)\|_{F}\leq C_{2}(1+\|\bm{x}\|).\quad

\displaystyle\text{(linear growth)}

We additionally require that, for every

\{p(\bm{x},t)\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
and

Assumption (A2)

The initial laws

and

Absolute continuity.

Finally, the prior and posterior path laws are taken to be mutually absolutely continuous, i.e., they admit densities with respect to each other.

Assumption (A3)

(Absolute continuity)

The prior SDE

1

2

Novikov’s criterion (

We restate the SDE form of Girsanov’s theorem below for later reference.

Theorem 2

(Girsanov’s theorem for SDEs)
.

Let the SDEs

\displaystyle\nu_{1}:d\bm{x}(t)=\bm{b}_{1}(\bm{x}(t),t)dt+\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x},t)^{1/2}d\bm{w}(t),\quad 0\leq t\leq T

\displaystyle\nu_{2}:d\bm{x}(t)=(\bm{b}_{1}(\bm{x}(t),t)+\bm{b}_{2}(\bm{x}(t),t))dt+\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x},t)^{1/2}d\bm{w}(t),\quad 0\leq t\leq T

\nu_{1}(\bm{x}(0))=\nu_{2}(\bm{x}(0))

(A1)
. Then
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
are mutually absolutely continuous, with Radon–Nikodym derivative

\frac{d\nu_{2}}{d\nu_{1}}(\bm{x})=\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\left[\bm{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\bm{b}_{2}(\bm{x}(t),t)\right]_{i}d\bm{w}^{\nu_{1}}_{i}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\left[\bm{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\bm{b}_{2}(\bm{x}(t),t)\right]_{i}^{2}dt\right\},

where
(\bm{w}^{\nu_{1}}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
denotes a Brownian motion under
\nu_{1}

\Phi
defined on continuous functions,

\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{2}}[\Phi(\bm{x})]=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{1}}\!\left[\Phi(\bm{x})\,\cfrac{d\nu_{2}}{d\nu_{1}}(\bm{x})\right].

A direct corollary of
Theorem

2
expresses the KL divergence between
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\textrm{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\left(\mathbb{Q}\;\|\;\mathbb{P}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[\log\!\left(\frac{q(\bm{x}(0),0)}{p(\bm{x}(0),0)}\cdot\frac{d\mathbb{Q}(\cdot|\bm{x}(0))}{d\mathbb{P}(\cdot|\bm{x}(0))}(\bm{x})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\textrm{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\left(q(\bm{x},0)\;\|\;p(\bm{x},0)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\|\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x}(t),t)^{-1/2}(\bm{f}_{p}(\bm{x}(t),t)-\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x}(t),t))\|^{2}dt.

(12)

The expectation of the stochastic integral term vanishes since it is a martingale.
The first term is the KL divergence between the initial distributions and the second is the “path-space” KL divergence.

are solutions to SDEs

where

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{\geq}\int\log\left(p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})\frac{p(\bm{x}(0),0)}{q(\bm{x}(0),0)}\cdot\frac{d\mathbb{P}(\cdot|\bm{x}(0))}{d\mathbb{Q}(\cdot|\bm{x}(0))}\right)\mathbb{Q}(d\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\log p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})]-\textrm{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\left(\mathbb{Q}\;\|\;\mathbb{P}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\log p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})]-\textrm{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\left(q(\bm{x},0)\;\|\;p(\bm{x},0)\right)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\|\bm{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\{\bm{f}_{p}(\bm{x}(t),t)-\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x}(t),t)\}\|^{2}dt.

Equality (i) is a consequence of Girsanov’s theorem, and inequality (ii) is an application of Jensen’s inequality. Taking the negative of this expression yields the negative ELBO
eq.

3
.

Notice that each term in the negative ELBO depends both on the parameters of the generative model
\bm{\theta}
and on the parameters of the variational posterior
\bm{\phi}
.

A.3
Divergence-free vector fields

In this subsection, we discuss the role of
q
-divergence-free vector fields in determining the expressivity of the variational family
\mathcal{Q}
. We prove
Proposition

1
, which characterizes the non-uniqueness in path law that arises from marginal-based parameterizations of the variational posterior.

Divergence-free vector fields.

In
eq.

5
, the Fokker-Planck equation is stated as a pointwise equality for
(\bm{x},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{K}\times[0,T]
. As mentioned we in
Section

A.1
, this is only valid under additional regularity assumptions on the posterior SDE. Nonetheless, we can still state the divergence-free condition using the weak form of the Fokker-Planck equation

\int\bm{v}(\bm{x},t)\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}\varphi(\bm{x})q(\bm{x},t)d\bm{x}=0\quad\text{a.e.~$t$}

(14)

for all test functions
\varphi
(infinitely differentiable, having compact support). Indeed, if
\bm{v}(\bm{x},t)
and
q(\bm{x},t)
are sufficiently smooth then the integration by parts identity implies

0=\int\bm{v}(\bm{x},t)\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}\varphi(\bm{x})q(\bm{x},t)d\bm{x}=-\int\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot[q(\bm{x},t)\bm{v}(\bm{x},t)]\varphi(\bm{x})d\bm{x}\quad\text{a.e.~$t$}

for all test functions
\varphi
. By Fubini’s theorem, this implies
\nabla\cdot[q(\bm{x},t)\bm{v}(\bm{x},t)]=0
a.e.
(\bm{x},t)
.

Having stated the divergence-free condition in its weak form, we are prepared to prove
Proposition

1
, which states that among diffusion processes having a shared diffusion coefficient
\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}
, the only degree freedom is the choice of divergence-free vector field.

Proof of
Proposition

1
.

Let
\mathbb{Q}
and
\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}
be two diffusion processes having drifts
\bm{f}
and
\tilde{\bm{f}}
, respectively, common diffusion coefficient
\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}
, and common initial marginal density
q(\bm{x},0)
.

\mathbb{Q}
and
\widetilde{\mathbb{Q}}
induce the same one-time marginals
\{q(\bm{x},t)\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
if and only if the two diffusions satisfy the same weak Fokker–Planck equation (
13
) for the common marginals. Since the diffusion coefficients are equal, this statement becomes

\displaystyle\int\bm{f}(\bm{x},t)\cdot\nabla\varphi(\bm{x})q(\bm{x},t)d\bm{x}=\int\tilde{\bm{f}}(\bm{x},t)\cdot\nabla\varphi(\bm{x})q(\bm{x},t)d\bm{x}\quad\text{a.e.~$t$}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\int(\bm{f}(\bm{x},t)-\tilde{\bm{f}}(\bm{x},t))\cdot\nabla\varphi(\bm{x})q(\bm{x},t)d\bm{x}=0\quad\text{a.e.~$t$}

(15)

for all test functions
\varphi
.

Equation

15
is precisely the statement that
\bm{f}-\tilde{\bm{f}}
is
q
-divergence free, in the weak formulation
eq.

14
.
∎

There are also diffusion processes with different diffusion coefficients that share the same one-time marginals. This fact is leveraged by
6
to learn the shared prior and posterior diffusion coefficient.
However, for a fixed prior SDE
eq.

1
, the diffusion coefficient of the posterior
eq.

2
must be equal to that of the prior.
This is because, by Girsanov’s Theorem (
Theorem

2
), diffusion processes whose diffusion coefficients differ are generally not mutually absolutely continuous.
In other words, the diffusion coefficient must be viewed as a parameter of the generative model, rather than a degree of freedom in the posterior process. On the other hand, any two diffusion processes with shared diffusion coefficient (and drifts that differ only by a divergence-free vector field) will be mutually absolutely continuous.

A.4
Gaussian one-time marginals

In the case of Gaussian one-time marginals, the non-uniqueness of the path law described in
Section

A.3
can be made explicit.
In this subsection, we construct an infinite collection of (distinct) path laws whose one-time marginals are identical. Throughout, we assume
q(\bm{x},t)=\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}(t),\bm{S}(t))
, where
\bm{m}(t),\bm{S}(t)
are continuously differentiable on
[0,T]
and
\bm{S}
is positive definite.

We first characterize these path laws in terms of the solution to a matrix Lyapunov equation.

Proposition 2
.

Let
q(\bm{x},t)=\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}(t),\bm{S}(t)),0\leq t\leq T
be a family of Gaussian one-time marginal distributions, with
(\bm{m}(t),\bm{S}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
continuously differentiable in
t
and
\bm{S}(t)
positive definite. Let
(\bm{K}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
be any solution to the matrix Lyapunov equation

\frac{d}{dt}\bm{S}(t)=\bm{K}(t)\bm{S}(t)+\bm{S}(t)\bm{K}(t)^{\top},\quad 0\leq t\leq T.

(16)

Then the SDE with drift
\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{A}_{q}(\bm{x},t)\bm{x}+\bm{b}_{q}(\bm{x},t)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\partial_{\bm{x}_{j}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\cdot,j}(\bm{x},t)
,

\bm{A}_{q}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{K}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta})\bm{S}(t)^{-1},\quad\bm{b}_{q}(\bm{x},t)=\frac{d}{dt}\bm{m}(t)-\bm{A}_{q}(\bm{x},t)\bm{m}(t),

(17)

and diffusion coefficient
\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x},t)^{1/2}
has marginals
q(\bm{x},t)=\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}(t),\bm{S}(t))
,
0\leq t\leq T
.

The Lyapunov equation (
16
) has infinitely many solutions
\bm{K}(t)
, so a single collection of Gaussian one-time marginals
\{q(\bm{x},t)\}
corresponds to infinitely many compatible diffusions.
Note that
Proposition

2
does
not
characterize the complete set of diffusions consistent with Gaussian one-time marginals.
For example, when
\bm{\Sigma}
is state-independent,
Proposition

2
only describes Gaussian processes i.e., stochastic process whose multi-time joint distributions are multivariate Gaussian. There exist stochastic processes with Gaussian one-time marginals that are not Gaussian processes.

The explicit construction is obtained by plugging the drift from
Proposition

2
into the Fokker-Planck equation, which yields the
continuity equation

eq.

18
for Gaussian marginals distributions and a linear vector field.
Unlike the Fokker-Planck equation, the continuity equation does not depend on the diffusion coefficient
\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}
, which is a consequence of the two terms in the posterior drift cancelling with the diffusion term in the Fokker-Planck equation.
The same strategy is used to convert between ODE and SDE samplers for diffusion and flow matching models
[
6
,
61
,
1
]
.See also
Section

A.5
.

Proof of
Proposition

2
.

Note that since
q(\bm{x},t)
and
\bm{f}_{q}
are sufficiently differentiable, we can work directly with the strong form of the Fokker–Planck equation
eq.

5
.

Starting first with the diffusion term, we have by the chain rule and the formula for the Gaussian density

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\partial_{\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j}}[q(\bm{x},t)\bm{\Sigma}_{ij}(\bm{x},t)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\partial_{\bm{x}_{i}}\left[q(\bm{x},t)\sum_{j}\left\{\partial_{\bm{x}_{j}}\bm{\Sigma}_{ij}(\bm{x},t)-[\bm{S}(t)^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))]_{j}\bm{\Sigma}_{ij}(\bm{x},t)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\left[-q(\bm{x},t)\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x},t)\bm{S}(t)^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))+q(\bm{x},t)\sum_{j}\partial_{\bm{x}_{j}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\cdot,j}(\bm{x},t)\right].

Notice that the same terms with the opposite sign appear in the drift term of the Fokker–Planck equation, by our definition of the drift
\bm{f}_{q}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle-\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot[\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x},t)q(\bm{x},t)]=

\displaystyle-\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\bigg[\bigg(\bm{K}(t)(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))+\frac{d}{dt}\bm{m}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x},t)\bm{S}(t)^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\partial_{\bm{x}_{j}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\cdot,j}(\bm{x},t)\bigg)q(\bm{x},t)\bigg],

where we used

\bm{A}_{q}(\bm{x},t)\bm{x}+\bm{b}_{q}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{A}_{q}(\bm{x},t)(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))+\frac{d}{dt}\bm{m}(t).

Canceling these terms, this shows that the Fokker–Planck equation reduces to the continuity equation

\frac{\partial q(\bm{x},t)}{\partial t}=-\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\left[\left(\bm{K}(t)(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))+\frac{d}{dt}\bm{m}(t)\right)q(\bm{x},t)\right],\quad 0\leq t\leq T.

(18)

Next, computing both sides of
Equation

18
explicitly and dividing by
q(\bm{x},t)
, we obtain

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\bigg[\mathrm{tr}\left(\bm{S}(t)^{-1}\frac{d}{dt}\bm{S}(t)\right)-(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))^{\top}\bm{S}(t)^{-1}\frac{d}{dt}\bm{S}(t)\bm{S}(t)^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))-2\frac{d}{dt}\bm{m}(t)^{\top}\bm{S}(t)^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))\bigg]

\displaystyle=-\left[\mathrm{tr}(\bm{K}(t))-\left(\bm{K}(t)(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))+\frac{d}{dt}\bm{m}(t)\right)^{\top}\bm{S}(t)^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))\right].

The linear terms involving
\frac{d}{dt}\bm{m}(t)
cancel, so this further simplifies to

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left[\mathrm{tr}\left(\bm{S}(t)^{-1}\frac{d}{dt}\bm{S}(t)\right)-(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))^{\top}\bm{S}(t)^{-1}\frac{d}{dt}\bm{S}(t)\bm{S}(t)^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))\right]

(19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left[\mathrm{tr}(\bm{K}(t))-(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))^{\top}\bm{K}(t)^{\top}\bm{S}(t)^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))\right].

Now, we must determine conditions on
(\bm{K}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
under which equality (
19
) holds. If
\bm{K}(t)
solves the Lyapunov equation (
16
), then

\displaystyle\bm{S}(t)^{-1}\frac{d}{dt}\bm{S}(t)\bm{S}(t)^{-1}=\bm{S}(t)^{-1}\bm{K}(t)+\bm{K}(t)^{\top}\bm{S}(t)^{-1}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))^{\top}\bm{S}(t)^{-1}\frac{d}{dt}\bm{S}(t)\bm{S}(t)^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))=2(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))^{\top}\bm{K}(t)^{\top}\bm{S}(t)^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t)).

Moreover,
\mathrm{tr}(\bm{S}(t)^{-1}\frac{d}{dt}\bm{S}(t))=2\mathrm{tr}(\bm{K}(t))
. Hence, whenever
\bm{K}(t)
solves the Lyapunov equation, the diffusion process with drift
\bm{f}_{q}
and diffusion coefficient
\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}
satisfies the Fokker–Planck equation.
∎

To conclude this subsection, we provide an alternate (but equivalent) characterization of the Lyapunov equation
eq.

16
for SDEs that induce the same one-time Gaussian marginals.
We do so via square root factorizations of
\bm{S}(t)
.

Proposition 3
.

Fix any continuously differentiable square root factorization
\bm{L}(t)
satisfying
\bm{L}(t)\bm{L}(t)^{\top}=\bm{S}(t)
. Then
\bm{K}(t)
solves the Lyapunov equation (
16
) if and only if

\bm{K}(t)=\left(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{L}(t)\right)\bm{L}(t)^{-1}+\bm{L}(t)\bm{\Omega}(t)\bm{L}(t)^{-1},\quad 0\leq t\leq T

(20)

for some skew-symmetric matrix-valued function
\bm{\Omega}(t)=-\bm{\Omega}(t)^{\top}
.

Proof.

Sufficiency.

Suppose is
\bm{K}
defined as in
eq.

20
. Substituting this expression into the right-hand side of the Lyapunov equation
eq.

16
yields

\displaystyle\bm{K}\bm{S}+\bm{S}\bm{K}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{L}\right)\bm{L}^{-1}+\bm{L}\bm{\Omega}\bm{L}^{-1}\right)\bm{L}\bm{L}^{\top}+\bm{L}\bm{L}^{\top}\left(\bm{L}^{-\top}\left(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{L}\right)^{\top}+\bm{L}^{-\top}\bm{\Omega}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{L}\right)\bm{L}^{\top}+\bm{L}\bm{\Omega}\bm{L}^{\top}+\bm{L}\left(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{L}\right)^{\top}+\bm{L}\bm{\Omega}^{\top}\bm{L}^{\top}

\displaystyle\overset{(\star)}{=}\left(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{L}\right)\bm{L}^{\top}+\bm{L}\left(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{L}\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}(\bm{L}\bm{L}^{\top})=\frac{d}{dt}\bm{S}.

Equality
(\star)
holds by skew-symmetry
\bm{\Omega}+\bm{\Omega}^{\top}=\bm{0}
.

Necessity.
Now suppose
\bm{K}
solves the Lyapunov equation. Solving
eq.

20
for
\bm{\Omega}
yields
\bm{\Omega}=\bm{L}^{-1}\left(\bm{K}-\left(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{L}\right)\bm{L}^{-1}\right)\bm{L}
.
Hence, we need to show
\bm{\Omega}
is skew-symmetric.
A direct calculation reveals

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}+\bm{\Omega}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\bm{L}^{-1}\left(\bm{K}\bm{S}-\left(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{L}\right)\bm{L}^{\top}\right)\bm{L}^{-\top}+\bm{L}^{-1}\left(\bm{S}\bm{K}^{\top}-\bm{L}\left(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{L}\right)^{\top}\right)\bm{L}^{-\top}

\displaystyle=\bm{L}^{-1}\left(\bm{K}\bm{S}+\bm{S}\bm{K}^{\top}-\frac{d}{dt}\bm{S}\right)\bm{L}^{-\top}=\bm{0}.

∎

The two terms in (
20
) have distinct roles.

(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{L})\bm{L}^{-1}
depends on the chosen square root and is itself a solution to the Lyapunov equation.
Arbitrarily changing
\bm{L}
will change
\bm{L}\bm{L}^{\top}
and hence the one-time marginals.
On the other hand,
\bm{L}\bm{\Omega}\bm{L}^{-1}
is allowed to vary as
\bm{\Omega}
takes different values in the
\binom{K}{2}
-dimensional vector space of skew-symmetric matrices. For fixed
\bm{L}
, each choice of
\bm{\Omega}
yields the same one-time marginals.

In this subsection, we explain how existing simulation-free VI methods SVISE

\bm{S}(t)

Before describing each choice of posterior drift, we recall

\displaystyle\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{A}_{q}(\bm{x},t)\bm{x}+\left(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{m}(t)-\bm{A}_{q}(\bm{x},t)\bm{m}(t)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\partial_{\bm{x}_{j}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\cdot,j}(\bm{x},t)

\bm{K}(t)
is a solution to the Lyapunov equation

.
As we will see, SVISE and SDE Matching select the drift

for different

Symmetric gauge.

Suppose
\bm{\Sigma}(t)=\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x},t)
is state-independent and
\bm{f}_{q}
is defined as in

2
.
Then
\bm{A}_{q}(t)=\bm{A}_{q}(\bm{x},t)
also satisfies a Lyapunov equation

\frac{d}{dt}\bm{S}(t)-\bm{\Sigma}(t)=\bm{A}_{q}(t)\bm{S}(t)+\bm{S}(t)\bm{A}_{q}(t)^{\top},\quad 0\leq t\leq T.

(21)

In SVISE
[
15
,
16
]
, the authors choose the posterior drift
\bm{A}_{q}(t)
to be unique symmetric solution to
eq.

21

Unlike the square root gauge, the symmetric gauge does not naturally extend to the case of a state-dependent diffusion coefficient. This is because, in general, the matrix
\bm{K}

.

Square root gauge.

6
instead identify the one-time Gaussian marginals with the drift function

defined as in

for

The SDE Matching framework, in theory, allows for any square root
\bm{\sigma}(t)
such that

However, in their implementation,

The authors arrive at the choice of variational drift by converting from a probability flow ODE
[

to an SDE. In particular, suppose
\bm{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbb{I}_{K})

.
If
\tilde{\bm{x}}(t)=F(\bm{\varepsilon},t)

\tilde{\bm{x}}(t)
evolves according to the ODE

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{\bm{x}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\bm{m}(t)+\left(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{S}(t)^{1/2}\right)\bm{\varepsilon}

From the ODE (

\displaystyle d\bm{x}(t)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{m}(t)+\left(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{S}(t)^{1/2}\right)\{\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}(\bm{x}(t)-\bm{m}(t))\}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x},t)\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log q(\bm{x}(t),t)

\displaystyle+(1/2)\sum_{j}\partial_{\bm{x}_{j}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\cdot,j}(\bm{x}(t),t)\bigg)dt+\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x}(t),t)^{1/2}d\bm{w}(t),\quad\bm{x}(0)\overset{d}{=}q(\bm{x},0)

has the same one-time marginal distributions as

. Substituting in the Gaussian score and grouping terms yields the drift

\displaystyle\left\{\left(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{S}(t)^{1/2}\right)\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x}(t),t)\bm{S}(t)^{-1}\right\}\bm{x}(t)+

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bm{m}(t)-\left\{\left(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{S}(t)^{1/2}\right)\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{x}(t),t)\bm{S}(t)^{-1}\right\}\bm{m}(t).

Although the construction in

allows the marginals

to be non-Gaussian (e.g., by parameterizing

as a normalizing flow), the paper itself evaluates the method only in the Gaussian marginal setting. We therefore restrict our discussion to that case.

An extension of SDE Matching.

The framework of SDE Matching
[
6
]
can in principle be extended to span every solution to the Lyapunov equation (
16
).
This is not discussed or implemented by the authors.
As described in the previous paragraph, their parameterization
\bm{S}(t)=\bm{\sigma}(t)\bm{\sigma}(t)^{\top}
induces the linear coefficient
\bm{K}(t)=(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{\sigma}(t))\bm{\sigma}(t)^{-1}
.
Fixing
\bm{\sigma}
to a specific square root selects a single path law for each set of one-time marginals.
By
Proposition

3
, the full set of solutions to the Lyapunov equation is parameterized by an additional skew-symmetric matrix
\bm{\Omega}(t)
via

\bm{K}(t)=\left(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{\sigma}(t)\right)\bm{\sigma}(t)^{-1}+\bm{\sigma}(t)\bm{\Omega}(t)\bm{\sigma}(t)^{-1},\quad 0\leq t\leq T.

Augmenting SDE Matching with a learned
\bm{\Omega}(t)
would in principle allow the posterior to span the full
\binom{K}{2}
-dimensional space of compatible linear drifts.

This extension has two limitations.
First, it requires introducing and optimizing over
\bm{\Omega}(t)
, sacrificing the closed-form structure of SDE Matching’s original parameterization.
Helmholtz-SDE, by contrast,
computes the optimal correction in closed form
via the Helmholtz decomposition of the residual vector field (
Section

4
).
Second, for state-independent diffusion coefficient, the resulting path law remains a Gaussian process.

Proposition

2
characterizes only diffusions with linear drift consistent with the prescribed Gaussian marginals; non-linear drifts can also yield Gaussian one-time marginals while inducing non-Gaussian multi-time joint distributions.
Helmholtz-SDE’s polynomial extension (
Section

B.3
) leverages this freedom, expressing corrections outside the Gaussian-process family.

Appendix B
Helmholtz-SDE

In this section, we provide further explanation of our algorithm, Helmholtz-SDE.

First, in
Section

B.1
we describe how the
q
-weighted Helmholtz decomposition can be obtained for arbitrary smooth, strictly positive marginal densities

by inverting a Stein operator. This yields a probabilistic solution that can be estimated via Monte Carlo.
In

In

\bm{r}=\bm{f}_{p}-\bm{f}_{q}
.
Lastly, in

B.1
Computing the Helmholtz decomposition

We first discuss how the Helmholtz decomposition can be computed for an arbitrary smooth, strictly positive density
q
as the inverse of a Stein operator. The resulting probabilistic expression can be approximated via Monte Carlo. For ease of notation, we consider state-independent diffusion coefficient, although the same construction holds in the state-dependent setting.

The Helmholtz decomposition and diffusion Stein operator.

As described in
Section

4.2
, the optimal correction to the reference drift
\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})
is obtained by computing the
q
-weighted Helmholtz decomposition of the residual vector field
\bm{r}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})=\bm{f}_{p}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta})-\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})
and taking the
q
-divergence-free vector field.
In particular, we want to find a decomposition of
\bm{r}
as

\bm{r}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})=\bm{\Sigma}(t|\bm{\theta})\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi(\bm{x},t)+\bm{h}(\bm{x},t),\quad(\bm{x},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{K}\times[0,T],

(23)

where
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi(\bm{x},t)
is a gradient field and
\bm{h}(\bm{x},t)
is a
q
-divergence-free vector field.

Suppose
q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})
is a smooth, strictly positive density.
To obtain such a decomposition, observe that if
eq.

23
holds, then multiplying both sides by
q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})
and computing the divergence yields

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot[q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})\bm{\Sigma}(t|\bm{\theta})\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi(\bm{x},t)]=\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot[q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})\bm{r}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})],\quad(\bm{x},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{K}\times[0,T],

(24)

since
\bm{h}
is
q
-divergence-free.
Dividing both sides by
q
and expanding the left-hand side yields

\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}(t|\bm{\theta})\nabla_{\bm{x}}^{2}\psi(\bm{x},t))+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})\cdot\bm{\Sigma}(t|\bm{\theta})\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi(\bm{x},t)=\frac{1}{q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot[q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})\bm{r}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})].

(25)

From
eq.

25
, we make two observations. First, the left-hand side can be written as
[\mathcal{L}_{t}^{\bm{\Sigma}}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi(\cdot,t)](\bm{x})
, where
\mathcal{L}_{t}^{\bm{\Sigma}}
is the differential operator

[\mathcal{L}_{t}^{\bm{\Sigma}}\bm{f}](\bm{x})=\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}(t|\bm{\theta})D_{\bm{x}}\bm{f}(\bm{x},t))+\bm{\Sigma}(t|\bm{\theta})\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})\cdot\bm{f}(\bm{x},t)

(26)

defined for differentiable vector fields
\bm{f}:\mathbb{R}^{K}\to\mathbb{R}^{K}
, where
D_{\bm{x}}\bm{f}
represents the Jacobian of
\bm{f}
.
This is the
diffusion Stein operator
corresponding to the density
q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})
and constant covariance coefficient
\bm{\Sigma}(t|\bm{\theta})

[
5
,
23
]
.
When
\bm{\Sigma}(t|\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{I}_{K}
, it reduces to the
Langevin Stein
operator

[\mathcal{L}_{t}\bm{f}](\bm{x})=\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\bm{f}(\bm{x})+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})\cdot\bm{f}(\bm{x}).

We refer the reader to
23
for background on diffusion Stein operators.

Second, whenever
\bm{r}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})
and

\mathcal{L}_{t}^{\bm{\Sigma}}[\bm{r}(\cdot,t|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})](\bm{x})
are integrable, the right-hand side of
eq.

25
has expectation

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{\bm{\phi}}}\left[\frac{1}{q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot[q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})\bm{r}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})]\right]=\int\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot[q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})\bm{r}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})]d\bm{x}=0

The final equality holds by the Divergence theorem
[
43
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Overall, we have reduced computing the Helmholtz decomposition to solving

\displaystyle[\mathcal{L}_{t}^{\bm{\Sigma}}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi(\cdot,t)](\bm{x})=\eta(\bm{x},t),\quad(\bm{x},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{K}\times[0,T],

(27)

\displaystyle\eta(\bm{x},t)=\frac{1}{q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot[q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})\bm{r}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})]

where
\eta(\bm{x},t)
has mean zero under
q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})
.
Intuitively, in order to solve
eq.

27
, we need to find an inverse of the diffusion Stein operator
\mathcal{L}_{t}^{\bm{\Sigma}}
.
Doing so has been the subject of prior literature, namely
5
,
50
,
23
.

Inverting the diffusion Stein operator.

Fix
t\in[0,T]
. Consider the time-homogeneous diffusion in an auxiliary time variable
s
with diffusion coefficient
\bm{\Sigma}(t|\bm{\theta})^{1/2}
:

d\bm{z}^{(t)}(s)=\frac{1}{2}\bm{\Sigma}(t|\bm{\theta})\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log q(\bm{z}^{(t)}(s),t|\bm{\phi})ds+\bm{\Sigma}(t|\bm{\theta})^{1/2}d\bm{w}(s).

(28)

When
\bm{\Sigma}(t|\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{I}_{K}
,
eq.

28
is recognizable as the overdamped Langevin diffusion.
In particular,
eq.

28
has invariant density
q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})
.
Its
infinitesimal generator
is

\displaystyle(\bm{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2q(\bm{z},t|\bm{\phi})}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\left[q(\bm{z},t|\bm{\phi})\bm{\Sigma}(t|\bm{\theta})\nabla_{\bm{z}}u(\bm{z})\right]

(29)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}(t|\bm{\theta})\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}u(\bm{z}))+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\Sigma}(t|\bm{\theta})\nabla_{\bm{z}}\log q(\bm{z},t|\bm{\phi})\cdot\nabla_{\bm{z}}u(\bm{z})

for
u:\mathbb{R}^{K}\to\mathbb{R}
twice continuously differentiable and satisfying integrability conditions
[
33
]
.
This implies, by
eq.

26
,
\mathcal{A}_{t}u=\mathcal{L}_{t}^{\bm{\Sigma}}(\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\bm{x}}u)
.
Hence, solving
eq.

27
is equivalent to solving

[\mathcal{A}_{t}u(\cdot,t)](\bm{x})=\eta(\bm{x},t),\quad(\bm{x},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{K}\times[0,T]

(30)

and then setting
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi(\bm{x},t)=\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\bm{x}}u(\bm{x},t)
.

Equation

30
is known as the
Poisson equation

[
50
,
22
]
.

Assume that, for each fixed
t
,
\eta(\bm{x},t)
and the coefficients of the above diffusion satisfy the regularity conditions of
23
. Then
23
implies that

u(\bm{x},t)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[\eta(\bm{z}^{(t)}(s),t)|\bm{z}^{(t)}(0)=\bm{x}]ds

(31)

solves the Poisson equation. The integrand is computed by starting the diffusion
eq.

28
from
\bm{x}
and then running it forward until time
s
.

Therefore,

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi(\bm{x},t)=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[\eta(\bm{z}^{(t)}(s),t)|\bm{z}^{(t)}(0)=\bm{x}]ds

(32)

solves
eq.

27
. Consequently, defining
\bm{h}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{r}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})-\bm{\Sigma}(t|\bm{\theta})\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi(\bm{x},t)

gives
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\left[q(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})\bm{h}(\bm{x},t)\right]=0
.
Thus
\bm{h}(\cdot,t)
is the
q
-divergence-free component of the residual vector field.

B.2
The Helmholtz decomposition for Gaussian marginals

In
Section

B.1
, we discussed the general recipe for computing the
q
-weighted Helmholtz decomposition of a vector field. In this subsection, we make this recipe fully rigorous for the setting of Gaussian one-time marginals and state-independent diffusion coefficient. As a consequence of the existence of the Helmholtz decomposition, we obtain a proof of
Theorem

1
.

First, we describe conditions on the residual vector field under which we will show that the
q
-weighted Helmholtz decomposition exists uniquely.

Assumption (A4)

(regularity of the residual vector field)
.

Assume that the following hold:

(i)

(\bm{m}(t),\bm{S}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
is continuously differentiable in
t
, and
\bm{S}(t)
is positive definite.

(ii)

\bm{r}
is twice differentiable in
\bm{x}
, and its derivatives are continuous in
(\bm{x},t)
.

(iii)

\bm{r}
,
D_{\bm{x}}\bm{r}
, and
D_{\bm{x}}^{(2)}\bm{r}
have polynomial growth in
\bm{x}
, uniformly on compact intervals in
t
.

(iv)

\bm{\Sigma}(t|\bm{\theta})
is continuous and positive definite.

D_{\bm{x}}\bm{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
and
D_{\bm{x}}^{(2)}\bm{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K\times K}
represent the derivative tensors of the residual vector field
\bm{r}:\mathbb{R}^{K}\to\mathbb{R}^{K}
.

We are now prepared to state and prove our main existence and uniqueness result.

Theorem 3

(Existence and Uniqueness of Helmholtz decomposition)
.

Assume that the residual vector field
\bm{r}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})=\bm{f}_{p}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta})-\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})

satisfies the smoothness and growth conditions in
(A4)
.
Then it admits a
q
-weighted Helmholtz decomposition
\bm{r}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})=\bm{\Sigma}(t|\bm{\theta})\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi(\bm{x},t)+\bm{h}(\bm{x},t)
.

Moreover,
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi
admits a closed-form probabilistic representation
eq.

41
.
Among functions satisfying the same regularity conditions, the vector fields
\bm{\Sigma}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi
and
\bm{h}
are unique.

Proof.

Fix
t\in[0,T]
.
Since
\bm{S}(t)
is positive definite,
q(\bm{x},t)
is a smooth, strictly positive density on
\mathbb{R}^{K}
.
Recall from
Section

B.1
that solving the Stein operator equation
eq.

27
for
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi
reduces, via the identity
\mathcal{A}_{t}u=\mathcal{L}_{t}^{\bm{\Sigma}}(\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla_{\bm{x}}u)
, to solving the Poisson equation
eq.

30
for
u
:

[\mathcal{A}_{t}u(\cdot,t)](\bm{x})\;=\;\frac{1}{2q(\bm{x},t)}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\left[q(\bm{x},t)\bm{\Sigma}(t)\nabla_{\bm{x}}u(\bm{x},t)\right]\;=\;\eta(\bm{x},t),

(33)

where
\mathcal{A}_{t}
is the infinitesimal generator of the Langevin diffusion
eq.

28
and
\eta(\bm{x},t)
is the source term in
eq.

27
.
The gradient component of the Helmholtz decomposition is then recovered as
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi(\bm{x},t)=\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla_{\bm{x}}u(\bm{x},t)
.

Our goal is to show that the candidate
u(\bm{x},t)
in
eq.

31
satisfies
eq.

33
pointwise, and that
u
is twice continuously differentiable in
\bm{x}
with derivatives continuous in
(\bm{x},t)
.
Once
eq.

33
is established, defining

\bm{h}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{r}(\bm{x},t)-\bm{\Sigma}(t)\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi(\bm{x},t)

(34)

gives
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\left[q(\bm{x},t)\bm{h}(\bm{x},t)\right]=0
, yielding the desired Helmholtz decomposition.

Our proof proceeds in four main steps:

(i)

Reexpress the candidate
u
in whitened coordinates, where the associated Langevin diffusion becomes an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process.

(ii)

Prove that the candidate
u
is well-defined and satisfies the desired regularity conditions.

(iii)

Verify that
u
satisfies the Poisson equation
eq.

33
pointwise.

(iv)

Show that
\bm{\Sigma}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi
and
\bm{h}
are unique.

Step
(i)
:
The candidate
u
proposed in
Section

B.1
(
eq.

31
) is expressed as an integral of expectations along the Langevin diffusion
eq.

28
with invariant law
q(\bm{x},t)
. We rewrite this candidate in whitened coordinates, in which the Langevin diffusion becomes an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process with invariant law
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbb{I}_{K})
. This whitened representation simplifies the subsequent regularity estimates and the verification of the Poisson equation.

Whiten the Gaussian marginals via the change of variables

\tilde{}\bm{x}=\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t)),\qquad\chi(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)=u(\bm{m}(t)+\bm{S}(t)^{1/2}\tilde{}\bm{x},t),

and define

\bm{\rho}(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)=\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}\bm{r}(\bm{m}(t)+\bm{S}(t)^{1/2}\tilde{}\bm{x},t),\qquad\bm{C}(t)=\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}\bm{\Sigma}(t)\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}.

Since
\bm{S}(t)
and
\bm{\Sigma}(t)
are positive definite,
\bm{C}(t)
is positive definite for each
t
.

Applying the change of variables to the Langevin diffusion
eq.

28
, the whitened diffusion is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process

d\tilde{}\bm{z}^{(t)}(s)=-\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{C}(t)\,\tilde{}\bm{z}^{(t)}(s)\,ds+\bm{C}(t)^{1/2}\,d\bm{w}(s),\qquad\tilde{}\bm{z}^{(t)}(0)=\tilde{}\bm{x},

(35)

whose invariant law is
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbb{I}_{K})
and whose infinitesimal generator is

(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}f)(\tilde{}\bm{x})=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\bm{C}(t)\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}^{2}f(\tilde{}\bm{x})\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\tilde{}\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{C}(t)\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}f(\tilde{}\bm{x})

for
f:\mathbb{R}^{K}\to\mathbb{R}
twice continuously differentiable with derivatives having polynomial growth. Define also the whitened source

g(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)=\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}\cdot\bm{\rho}(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)-\tilde{}\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\rho}(\tilde{}\bm{x},t).

(36)

A direct computation shows that
g(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)=\eta(\bm{m}(t)+\bm{S}(t)^{1/2}\tilde{}\bm{x},t)
, where
\eta
is the source term in
eq.

27
. Overall, we have shown that in
\tilde{\bm{x}}
coordinates, the Poisson equation becomes

(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}\chi(\cdot,t))(\tilde{}\bm{x})=g(\tilde{}\bm{x},t).

(37)

By
(A4)
,
\bm{r}
is differentiable in
\bm{x}
, and both
\bm{r}
and
D_{\bm{x}}\bm{r}
have polynomial growth; the same therefore holds of
\bm{\rho}(\cdot,t)
and
D_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}\bm{\rho}(\cdot,t)
. Stein’s identity gives

\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{}\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbb{I}_{K})}\!\left[\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}\cdot\bm{\rho}(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{}\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbb{I}_{K})}\!\left[\tilde{}\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\rho}(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)\right],

so
g(\cdot,t)
has mean zero under the OU invariant law.

In these whitened coordinates, the candidate from
eq.

31
takes the form

\chi(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}(P_{s}^{t}g)(\tilde{}\bm{x})\,ds,\qquad(P_{s}^{t}g)(\tilde{}\bm{x})=\mathbb{E}\!\left[g\!\left(\tilde{}\bm{z}^{(t)}(s),t\right)\,\big|\,\tilde{}\bm{z}^{(t)}(0)=\tilde{}\bm{x}\right],

(38)

where
(P_{s}^{t})_{s\geq 0}
is the OU semigroup associated with
eq.

35
.
Equivalently,
u(\bm{x},t)=\chi(\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t)),t)
.

Step
(ii)
:
We first justify that
eq.

38
is well-defined.
For all times
0\leq s<\infty
, the marginal distribution of the OU process
(
35
) satisfies

\tilde{}\bm{z}^{(t)}(s)\overset{d}{=}\bm{A}_{t}(s)\tilde{}\bm{x}+\bm{B}_{t}(s)\bm{\xi},\qquad\bm{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbb{I}_{K}),

where

\bm{A}_{t}(s)=e^{-s\bm{C}(t)/2},\qquad\bm{B}_{t}(s)=\left(\mathbb{I}_{K}-e^{-s\bm{C}(t)}\right)^{1/2}.

Thus

(P_{s}^{t}g)(\tilde{}\bm{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbb{I}_{K})}\left[g\!\left(\bm{A}_{t}(s)\tilde{}\bm{x}+\bm{B}_{t}(s)\bm{\xi},t\right)\right].

Using
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbb{I}_{K})}[g(\bm{\xi},t)]=0
, we may write

(P_{s}^{t}g)(\tilde{}\bm{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbb{I}_{K})}\left[g\!\left(\bm{A}_{t}(s)\tilde{}\bm{x}+\bm{B}_{t}(s)\bm{\xi},t\right)-g(\bm{\xi},t)\right].

Hence,

\left|(P_{s}^{t}g)(\tilde{}\bm{x})\right|\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbb{I}_{K})}\left|g\!\left(\bm{A}_{t}(s)\tilde{}\bm{x}+\bm{B}_{t}(s)\bm{\xi},t\right)-g(\bm{\xi},t)\right|.

Now decompose

\displaystyle g\!\left(\bm{A}_{t}(s)\tilde{}\bm{x}+\bm{B}_{t}(s)\bm{\xi},t\right)-g(\bm{\xi},t)

\displaystyle=\Big(g\!\left(\bm{A}_{t}(s)\tilde{}\bm{x}+\bm{B}_{t}(s)\bm{\xi},t\right)-g\!\left(\bm{B}_{t}(s)\bm{\xi},t\right)\Big)

\displaystyle+\Big(g\!\left(\bm{B}_{t}(s)\bm{\xi},t\right)-g(\bm{\xi},t)\Big).

Applying the mean value theorem to each term and using that
\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}g
has at most polynomial growth,
there exist constants
C,m>0
such that

\|\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}g(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)\|\leq C(1+\|\tilde{}\bm{x}\|^{m}).

Since
\bm{\xi}
is Gaussian, all of its moments are finite. Moreover,

\|\bm{A}_{t}(s)\|\leq C_{t}e^{-c_{t}s},\qquad\|\bm{B}_{t}(s)-\mathbb{I}_{K}\|\leq C_{t}e^{-c_{t}s},

for some constants
C_{t},c_{t}>0
. It follows that

\left|\mathbb{E}\!\left[g\!\left(\tilde{}\bm{z}^{(t)}(s),t\right)\,\big|\,\tilde{}\bm{z}^{(t)}(0)=\tilde{}\bm{x}\right]\right|\leq C_{t}^{\prime}(1+\|\tilde{}\bm{x}\|^{m+1})e^{-c_{t}s},

for some constant
C_{t}^{\prime}>0
. In particular,
eq.

38
is finite for every
(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)
.

We next show that
\chi
is twice continuously differentiable in
\tilde{}\bm{x}
with derivatives continuous in
(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)
.
The estimates below also justify passage of the spatial derivatives, and hence of
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}
, under the integral sign in step
(iii)
.

Using the notation introduced above, we can rewrite

eq.

38
as

\chi(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbb{I}_{K})}\left[g\!\left(\bm{A}_{t}(s)\tilde{}\bm{x}+\bm{B}_{t}(s)\bm{\xi},t\right)\right]ds.

(39)

We first study the gradient of
\chi
.
To do so, we must justify the exchange of the gradient and integral in
eq.

39
. Applying Stein’s identity to the integrand of
eq.

39
yields

F(\tilde{}\bm{x},t,s)=\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}(P_{s}^{t}g)(\tilde{}\bm{x})=\bm{A}_{t}(s)\bm{B}_{t}(s)^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{I}_{K})}\left[\bm{\xi}\,g\!\left(\bm{A}_{t}(s)\tilde{}\bm{x}+\bm{B}_{t}(s)\bm{\xi},t\right)\right].

The polynomial growth of
g
implies

\displaystyle\|F(\tilde{}\bm{x},t,s)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{A}_{t}(s)\bm{B}_{t}(s)^{-1}\|\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{I}_{K})}\left[\|\bm{\xi}\|\,|g\!\left(\bm{A}_{t}(s)\tilde{}\bm{x}+\bm{B}_{t}(s)\bm{\xi},t\right)|\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\|\bm{A}_{t}(s)\bm{B}_{t}(s)^{-1}\|\left(1+\|\bm{A}_{t}(s)\tilde{}\bm{x}\|^{m}\right),

where we used that
\bm{\xi}
is Gaussian and
\bm{B}_{t}(s)
is bounded.
Now, as
s\downarrow 0
,
\|\bm{A}_{t}(s)\bm{B}_{t}(s)^{-1}\|=O(s^{-1/2})
,
while as
s\to\infty
,

\|\bm{A}_{t}(s)\|\leq C_{t}e^{-c_{t}s},\quad\|\bm{B}_{t}(s)^{-1}\|\leq C_{t}.

Therefore

\|F(\tilde{}\bm{x},t,s)\|\leq\begin{cases}C(1+\|\tilde{}\bm{x}\|^{m})s^{-1/2},&0<s\leq 1,\\
C_{t}(1+\|\tilde{}\bm{x}\|^{m})e^{-c_{t}s},&s\geq 1,\end{cases}

and the right-hand side is integrable on
(0,\infty)
. Moreover, for every compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{K}\times[0,T]
, this integrable bound can be chosen uniformly for
(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)\in K
. By dominated convergence we deduce

\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}\chi(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}F(\tilde{}\bm{x},t,s)ds.

We next study the Hessian of
\chi
.
Since
g
is continuously differentiable in
\tilde{}\bm{x}
, for each fixed
s>0
the map

(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)\mapsto F(\tilde{}\bm{x},t,s)
is continuously differentiable in
\tilde{}\bm{x}
, and

\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}F(\tilde{}\bm{x},t,s)=\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}^{2}(P_{s}^{t}g)(\tilde{}\bm{x})=\bm{A}_{t}(s)\bm{B}_{t}(s)^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{I}_{K})}\left[\bm{\xi}\,\left(\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}g\!\left(\bm{A}_{t}(s)\tilde{}\bm{x}+\bm{B}_{t}(s)\bm{\xi},t\right)\right)^{\top}\right]\bm{A}_{t}(s).

Using that
\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}g
has at most polynomial growth, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}F(\tilde{}\bm{x},t,s)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{A}_{t}(s)\bm{B}_{t}(s)^{-1}\|\,\|\bm{A}_{t}(s)\|\,\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{I}_{K})}\left[\|\bm{\xi}\|\,\|\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}g\!\left(\bm{A}_{t}(s)\tilde{}\bm{x}+\bm{B}_{t}(s)\bm{\xi},t\right)\|\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\|\bm{A}_{t}(s)\bm{B}_{t}(s)^{-1}\|\,\|\bm{A}_{t}(s)\|\left(1+\|\bm{A}_{t}(s)\tilde{}\bm{x}\|^{m}\right).

Hence,

\|\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}F(\tilde{}\bm{x},t,s)\|\leq\begin{cases}C(1+\|\tilde{}\bm{x}\|^{m})s^{-1/2},&0<s\leq 1,\\
C_{t}(1+\|\tilde{}\bm{x}\|^{m})e^{-c_{t}s},&s\geq 1,\end{cases}

which is again integrable on
(0,\infty)
. Again, for every compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{K}\times[0,T]
, this integrable bound may be chosen uniformly for
(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)\in K
, and dominated convergence yields

\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}^{2}\chi(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}F(\tilde{}\bm{x},t,s)\,ds.

Hence,
\chi
is twice continuously differentiable in
\tilde{}\bm{x}
, for all
t
.

For each fixed
s>0
, the functions
F(\tilde{}\bm{x},t,s)
and
\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}F(\tilde{}\bm{x},t,s)
are continuous in
(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)
by dominated convergence in the Gaussian expectation.
Since
t\in[0,T]
, continuity and positive definiteness of
\bm{S}(t)
and
\bm{\Sigma}(t)

imply uniform spectral bounds for
\bm{C}(t)
on
[0,T]
.
Therefore the estimates above may be chosen locally uniformly
jointly
in
(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)
, and dominated convergence in

shows that both
\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}\chi(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)
and
\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}^{2}\chi(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)
are continuous in
(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)
.

Finally, by the chain rule and the identity
u(\bm{x},t)=\chi(\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t)),t)
from step

,

\nabla_{\bm{x}}u(\bm{x},t)=\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}\chi\!\left(\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t)),t\right),

and

\nabla_{\bm{x}}^{2}u(\bm{x},t)=\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}^{2}\chi\!\left(\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t)),t\right)\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}.

By
(A4)
(i)
,
t\mapsto\bm{m}(t)
and
t\mapsto\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}
are continuous, so
\nabla_{\bm{x}}u(\bm{x},t)
and
\nabla_{\bm{x}}^{2}u(\bm{x},t)
are continuous in
(\bm{x},t)
. The same therefore holds of
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi(\bm{x},t)=\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla_{\bm{x}}u(\bm{x},t)
and
\nabla_{\bm{x}}^{2}\psi(\bm{x},t)
.

Step
(iii)
:
We now verify that
u
satisfies the Poisson equation
eq.

33
pointwise.
By step
(i)
, it suffices to show that
\chi
satisfies the Poisson equation in the whitened coordinates (
37
).

The estimates established in step
(ii)
show that
(P_{s}^{t}g)(\tilde{}\bm{x})
is twice continuously differentiable in
\tilde{\bm{x}}
.
And for each
s>0
,
(P_{s}^{t}g)
is also continuously differentiable in
s
and satisfies the backward Kolmogorov equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}(P_{s}^{t}g)(\tilde{}\bm{x})=(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}P_{s}^{t}g)(\tilde{}\bm{x}),\qquad s>0.

Moreover, the spatial derivatives of
(P_{s}^{t}g)
admit integrable bounds in
s
, locally uniformly in
(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)
.
Therefore the operator
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}
may be passed under the integral sign in

38
, yielding

\displaystyle(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}\chi(\cdot,t))(\tilde{}\bm{x})

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}P_{s}^{t}g)(\tilde{}\bm{x})\,ds

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}(P_{s}^{t}g)(\tilde{}\bm{x})\,ds

\displaystyle=g(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)-\lim_{R\to\infty}(P_{R}^{t}g)(\tilde{}\bm{x}).

By the tail estimate established in step
(ii)
,

|(P_{R}^{t}g)(\tilde{}\bm{x})|\leq C_{t}^{\prime}(1+\|\tilde{}\bm{x}\|^{m+1})e^{-c_{t}R}\to 0\qquad\text{as }R\to\infty,

for each

.
Hence
(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{t}\chi(\cdot,t))(\tilde{}\bm{x})=g(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)
for all

, and consequently

satisfies

33

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi(\bm{x},t)=\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla_{\bm{x}}u(\bm{x},t)
.

Step
(iv)
:
It remains to prove uniqueness of the vector fields

\bm{\Sigma}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi
and
\bm{h}
within the same regularity class.
Suppose that

\bm{r}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{\Sigma}(t)\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi_{1}(\bm{x},t)+\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{\Sigma}(t)\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi_{2}(\bm{x},t)+\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{x},t)

are two

-weighted Helmholtz decompositions satisfying the same smoothness
and growth conditions, with

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\left[q(\bm{x},t)\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{x},t)\right]=\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\left[q(\bm{x},t)\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{x},t)\right]=0.

Let
\varphi(\bm{x},t)=\psi_{1}(\bm{x},t)-\psi_{2}(\bm{x},t)
.
Subtracting the two decompositions gives

\bm{\Sigma}(t)\nabla_{\bm{x}}\varphi(\bm{x},t)=\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{x},t)-\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{x},t).

(40)

Since
\bm{h}_{2}-\bm{h}_{1}
is also
q
-divergence-free, we have

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\left[q(\bm{x},t)\left(\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{x},t)-\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{x},t)\right)\right]=0.

Multiplying by
\varphi(\bm{x},t)
and integrating over
\mathbb{R}^{K}
, the integration
by parts identity yields

\int\nabla_{\bm{x}}\varphi(\bm{x},t)^{\top}\left(\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{x},t)-\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{x},t)\right)q(\bm{x},t)\,d\bm{x}=0.

The boundary term vanishes because the
vector fields satisfy the assumed polynomial growth conditions. Using
(
40
), we obtain

\int\nabla_{\bm{x}}\varphi(\bm{x},t)^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}(t)\nabla_{\bm{x}}\varphi(\bm{x},t)q(\bm{x},t)\,d\bm{x}=0.

Because
\bm{\Sigma}(t)
is positive definite and
q(\bm{x},t)>0
, this implies
\nabla_{\bm{x}}\varphi(\bm{x},t)=0
,
q(\cdot,t)
a.e.
Therefore,

\bm{\Sigma}(t)\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi_{1}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{\Sigma}(t)\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi_{2}(\bm{x},t),\qquad\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{x},t),

for
q(\cdot,t)
-a.e.
\bm{x}
.
Since this holds for a.e.
t
, Tonelli’s theorem gives uniqueness up to
(\bm{x},t)
-a.e. equality.
By continuity of

\bm{\Sigma}(t)\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi_{1}(\bm{x},t)
and
\bm{\Sigma}(t)\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi_{2}(\bm{x},t)
, as well as
\bm{h}_{1}(\bm{x},t)
and
\bm{h}_{2}(\bm{x},t)
, jointly in
(\bm{x},t)
, we conclude (pointwise) uniqueness.
Finally,

\nabla_{\bm{x}}(\psi_{1}-\psi_{2})=0
shows that
\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}
is constant in

\bm{x}
, possibly depending on
t
.
∎

Theorem

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi(\bm{x},t)=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}\chi\!\left(\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t)),t\right)

(41)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\tilde{}\bm{x}}\chi(\tilde{}\bm{x},t)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\bm{A}_{t}(s)\bm{B}_{t}(s)^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbb{I}_{K})}\!\left[\bm{\xi}\,g\!\left(\bm{A}_{t}(s)\tilde{}\bm{x}+\bm{B}_{t}(s)\bm{\xi},t\right)\right]ds

As a corollary of
Theorem

3
, we obtain a proof of
Theorem

1
from the main text, which states that the variational family implied by Helmholtz-SDE is arbitrarily close in KL distance to the true posterior whenever the prior is linear and the observation model is linear and Gaussian.

Proof of
Theorem

1
.

When the prior SDE is linear and the observation model is linear-Gaussian, the true posterior
\mathbb{Q}^{\star}
is a Markovian Gaussian process (‘Gauss–Markov’ process) whose drift
\bm{f}^{\star}(\bm{x},t)
is affine in
\bm{x}
and is given by the Kalman-Bucy smoother
[
32
,
59
]
. The one-time marginals are Gaussian,
q^{\star}(\bm{x},t)=\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}^{\star}(t),\bm{S}^{\star}(t))
, with
\bm{S}^{\star}(t)
positive definite. The functions
\bm{m}^{\star}(t)
and
\bm{S}^{\star}(t)
are continuous on
[0,T]

but only piecewise continuously differentiable
: their derivatives jump at the observation times
\{t_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, since the smoother updates discontinuously when conditioning on each observation (see e.g.,
28
Appendix J).

By assumption,
\mathcal{Q}
is the set of path laws produced by Helmholtz-SDE applied to any continuously differentiable
\bm{m}(t)
and positive-definite
\bm{S}(t)
. Concretely, for each such input pair, Helmholtz-SDE selects a linear reference drift
\bm{f}_{q}
that realizes the marginals
\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}(t),\bm{S}(t))
(
Proposition

2
) and adds the
q
-divergence-free correction
\bm{h}
obtained from the order-
1
Helmholtz construction. The resulting diffusion has drift
\bm{f}_{q}+\bm{h}
and one-time marginals
\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}(t),\bm{S}(t))
, and constitutes the corresponding element of
\mathcal{Q}
. Since
(\bm{m}^{\star},\bm{S}^{\star})
is not continuously differentiable, the true posterior
\mathbb{Q}^{\star}
does not, in general, lie in
\mathcal{Q}
. We argue instead that
\mathbb{Q}^{\star}
can be approximated arbitrarily closely in KL by
\mathcal{Q}
.

Fix
\epsilon>0
. Since
\bm{m}^{\star}
and
\bm{S}^{\star}
are continuous and
piecewise
C^{1}
, we may smooth them inside neighborhoods of the observation
times whose total Lebesgue measure is at most
\epsilon
. This gives
continuously differentiable functions
(\bm{m}^{\epsilon},\bm{S}^{\epsilon})
such that

\bm{S}^{\epsilon}(t)\succ 0
for all
t
, the pair

(\bm{m}^{\epsilon},\bm{S}^{\epsilon})
agrees with

(\bm{m}^{\star},\bm{S}^{\star})
outside these neighborhoods, and

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\{\|\bm{m}^{\epsilon}(t)-\bm{m}^{\star}(t)\|+\|\bm{S}^{\epsilon}(t)-\bm{S}^{\star}(t)\|_{F}\right\}\leq\epsilon.

Positive definiteness is preserved for sufficiently small
\epsilon
because

\lambda_{\min}(\bm{S}^{\star}(t))
is uniformly bounded away from zero on the
compact interval.
Let
\mathbb{Q}^{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{Q}
denote the path law produced by Helmholtz-SDE on input
(\bm{m}^{\epsilon},\bm{S}^{\epsilon})
, with reference drift
\bm{f}_{q}^{\epsilon}
and divergence-free correction
\bm{h}^{\epsilon}
.

Since
\bm{f}_{p}
and
\bm{f}_{q}^{\epsilon}
are both affine in
\bm{x}
, the residual
\bm{r}^{\epsilon}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{f}_{p}(\bm{x},t)-\bm{f}_{q}^{\epsilon}(\bm{x},t)
is linear in
\bm{x}
. Hence, its order-
1
Taylor expansion is exact.
Theorem

3
therefore yields a
q^{\epsilon}
-weighted Helmholtz decomposition
\bm{r}^{\epsilon}=\bm{\Sigma}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi^{\epsilon}+\bm{h}^{\epsilon}
, in which the divergence-free component
\bm{h}^{\epsilon}
is exactly the correction added by Helmholtz-SDE. Hence the corrected drift
\bm{f}_{q}^{\epsilon}+\bm{h}^{\epsilon}
differs from the prior drift
\bm{f}_{p}
exactly by the gradient component
\bm{\Sigma}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi^{\epsilon}
.

It remains to show that
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\left(\mathbb{Q}^{\epsilon}\;\|\;\mathbb{Q}^{\star}\right)\to 0
as
\epsilon\to 0
. Both
\mathbb{Q}^{\epsilon}
and
\mathbb{Q}^{\star}
are diffusions with diffusion coefficient
\bm{\Sigma}^{1/2}
and Gaussian initial distributions
\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}^{\epsilon}(0),\bm{S}^{\epsilon}(0))
and
\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}^{\star}(0),\bm{S}^{\star}(0))
, respectively, with drifts
\bm{f}_{q}^{\epsilon}+\bm{h}^{\epsilon}
and
\bm{f}^{\star}
. Girsanov’s theorem (
Theorem

2
) therefore gives

\textrm{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\left(\mathbb{Q}^{\epsilon}\;\|\;\mathbb{Q}^{\star}\right)=\textrm{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\left(\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}^{\epsilon}(0),\bm{S}^{\epsilon}(0))\;\|\;\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}^{\star}(0),\bm{S}^{\star}(0))\right)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{\epsilon}}\|\bm{\Sigma}^{-1/2}(\bm{f}_{q}^{\epsilon}+\bm{h}^{\epsilon}-\bm{f}^{\star})\|^{2}\,dt.

The initial-time KL vanishes as
\epsilon\to 0
by continuity of the Gaussian KL in its parameters, since
\bm{m}^{\epsilon}(0)\to\bm{m}^{\star}(0)
and
\bm{S}^{\epsilon}(0)\to\bm{S}^{\star}(0)
.
For the path-KL term, recall that on the complement of the
\epsilon
-neighborhoods of
\{t_{n}\}
, the smoothed and true marginals coincide:
(\bm{m}^{\epsilon},\bm{S}^{\epsilon})=(\bm{m}^{\star},\bm{S}^{\star})
.
Thus
\bm{f}_{q}^{\epsilon}+\bm{h}^{\epsilon}
is the Helmholtz solution of the
fixed-marginal path-KL minimization problem for
q^{\star}
. The true posterior
drift
\bm{f}^{\star}
solves the same problem: once the one-time marginals are
fixed to
q^{\star}
, the reconstruction and initial-KL terms are fixed, so
optimality of
\mathbb{Q}^{\star}
reduces to minimizing the path KL.
By strict convexity of the weighted
L^{2}(q^{\star},\bm{\Sigma}^{-1})
objective, the minimizer is unique up to
q^{\star}
-a.e. equality. Hence
\bm{f}_{q}^{\epsilon}+\bm{h}^{\epsilon}=\bm{f}^{\star}
a.e. outside the smoothing neighborhoods.
Indeed, if another drift with the same marginals had smaller path KL to the prior, replacing the posterior drift by that drift would leave the reconstruction and initial KL terms unchanged and would produce an ELBO strictly larger than that of the exact posterior, a contradiction.

Inside the smoothing neighborhoods, the affine coefficients of

\bm{f}_{q}^{\epsilon}+\bm{h}^{\epsilon}
and
\bm{f}^{\star}
are uniformly bounded for
small
\epsilon
. Moreover, the uniform convergence of
(\bm{m}^{\epsilon},\bm{S}^{\epsilon})
to
(\bm{m}^{\star},\bm{S}^{\star})
implies
\sup_{\epsilon,t}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{\epsilon}}\|X_{t}\|^{2}<\infty
.
Combining these two facts, there exists a constant
C

independent of
\epsilon
such that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{\epsilon}}\left\|\bm{\Sigma}(t)^{-1/2}\{\bm{f}_{q}^{\epsilon}(X_{t},t)+\bm{h}^{\epsilon}(X_{t},t)-\bm{f}^{\star}(X_{t},t)\}\right\|^{2}\leq C

on these neighborhoods.

The path-KL term is therefore
O(\epsilon)
. We conclude
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\left(\mathbb{Q}^{\epsilon}\;\|\;\mathbb{Q}^{\star}\right)\to 0
as
\epsilon\to 0
, and hence
\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}}\textrm{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\left(\mathbb{Q}\;\|\;\mathbb{Q}^{\star}\right)=0
.
∎

B.3
Polynomial approximation to residual vector field

We now describe how to compute the order-
\ell
polynomial approximation to the weighted Helmholtz decomposition used in our implementation. In addition to Gaussianity of the posterior marginals, we assume the regularity conditions in
(A4)
. For an order
\ell>2
approximation, we need to further assume that
\bm{r}
has derivatives up to order
\ell
with at most polynomial growth.

Let
\bm{r}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})=\bm{f}_{p}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta})-\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\phi})
, and let us drop the dependence on
\bm{\theta}
and
\bm{\phi}
. We will consider the order-
\ell
Taylor approximation of
\bm{r}(\bm{x},t)
about
\bm{m}(t)

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{r}}^{(\ell)}(\bm{x},t)=

(42)

\displaystyle\bm{r}(\bm{m}(t),t)+D_{\bm{x}}\bm{r}(\bm{x},t)|_{\bm{x}=\bm{m}(t)}(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))+\cdots+\frac{1}{\ell!}D_{\bm{x}}^{(\ell)}\bm{r}(\bm{x},t)|_{\bm{x}=\bm{m}(t)}[(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))^{\otimes\ell}]

where
D_{\bm{x}}^{(l)}r(\bm{x},t)_{ij_{1},\ldots,j_{l}}=\frac{\partial^{l}}{\partial\bm{x}_{j_{1}},\ldots,\partial\bm{x}_{j_{l}}}\bm{r}_{i}(\bm{x},t)
,
1\leq i,j_{1},\ldots,j_{l}\leq k
denotes the
l
-th derivative tensor for
1\leq l\leq\ell

and
D_{\bm{x}}^{(l)}\bm{r}(\bm{m}(t),t)[(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))^{\otimes l}]
denotes contraction of
D_{\bm{x}}^{(l)}\bm{r}(\bm{m}(t),t)
with the order-
l
tensor product
(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))^{\otimes l}
.

Equation

42
can be obtained by performing a multivariate Taylor expansion for each output dimension of
\bm{r}
separately.
Note that
D_{\bm{x}}^{(l)}r(\bm{x},t)
is
(l+1)
-dimensional.
Moreover, each output dimension of
\tilde{\bm{r}}^{(\ell)}
is a polynomial in
K
variables of degree at most
\ell
.

The full
l
-th derivative tensor has

K^{l+1}
entries. Thus, explicitly forming this tensor by automatic differentiation costs at
least
O(K^{l+1})
operations, and roughly
O(K^{l+1}C_{\bm{r}})
when
C_{\bm{r}}

denotes the cost of a single evaluation of
\bm{r}
.

Our goal will be show that we can analytically compute the Helmholtz decomposition of
\tilde{\bm{r}}^{(\ell)}(\bm{x},t)
with computational complexity
O(K^{\ell+2})
. For latent spaces of moderate dimension, this is tractable for
\ell\in\{1,2\}
, which we find work well in our experiments.

The Helmholtz decomposition as solving a linear system.

From the proof of
Theorem

3
in
Section

B.2
, we know that it suffices to find a function
\psi(\cdot,t):\mathbb{R}^{K}\to\mathbb{R}
that solves the Poisson equation

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\left[q(\bm{x},t)\bm{\Sigma}(t|\bm{\theta})\nabla\psi(\bm{x},t)\right]=\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\left[q(\bm{x},t)\tilde{\bm{r}}^{(\ell)}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta})\right].

Then, the Helmholtz decomposition of
\tilde{\bm{r}}^{(\ell)}
with respect to
q
is

\tilde{\bm{r}}^{(\ell)}(\bm{x},t|\bm{\theta})=\bm{\Sigma}(t|\bm{\theta})\nabla\psi(\bm{x},t)+\bm{h}(\bm{x},t).

To obtain such a solution, we first performed the whitening transformation
\bm{z}=\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))
, in which case the Poisson equation became

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left(\bm{C}(t)\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}\chi(\bm{z},t)\right)-\bm{z}^{\top}\bm{C}(t)\nabla_{\bm{z}}\chi(\bm{z},t)=g(\bm{z},t)\

(43)

where
\chi(\bm{z},t)=\psi(\bm{m}(t)+\bm{S}(t)^{1/2}\bm{z},t)
is the solution in whitened coordinates;
g(\bm{z},t)=\nabla_{\bm{z}}\cdot\bm{u}(\bm{z},t)-\bm{z}^{\top}\bm{u}(\bm{z},t)
is the right-hand side in whitened coordinates;
\bm{u}(\bm{z},t)=\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}\tilde{\bm{r}}^{(\ell)}(\bm{m}(t)+\bm{S}(t)^{1/2}\bm{z},t|\bm{\theta})
is the residual vector field in whitened coordinates; and
\bm{C}(t)=\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}\bm{\Sigma}(t)\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}
.

From
eq.

43
we observe that since each output dimension of
\tilde{\bm{r}}^{(\ell)}
is a degree
\ell
polynomial in
\bm{z}
, then the inner product of
\bm{u}(\bm{z},t)
with
\bm{z}
implies that right-hand side of the Poisson equation
eq.

43
will be a degree
(\ell+1)
polynomial in
\bm{z}
. Moreover, since the gradient operator
\nabla_{\bm{z}}
in the left hand side of
eq.

43
reduces the degree of a polynomial by one, and the inner product with
\bm{z}
increases it by one, then the solution
\chi(\bm{z},t)
to
eq.

43
will also be a polynomial of degree
(\ell+1)
.

Consider the monomials in
K
variables of total degree at most
\ell+1
, namely

p_{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{z})=\prod_{j=1}^{K}\bm{z}_{j}^{\bm{\alpha}_{j}},\quad\bm{\alpha}\in\mathcal{N}_{K,\ell+1},\quad\mathcal{N}_{K,\ell+1}=\left\{\bm{\alpha}\in\{0,1,\ldots,\ell+1\}^{K}\bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{K}\bm{\alpha}_{j}\leq\ell+1\right\}.

These monomials are a basis for the subspace of multivariate polynomials of order at most
\ell+1
, and there are
\binom{K+\ell+1}{\ell+1}=\Omega(K^{\ell+1})
such monomials for fixed
\ell
.
One strategy for solving
eq.

43
would be to expand
g(\bm{z},t)
and
\chi(\bm{z},t)
in this monomial basis and then to solve a linear system.
Indeed, we could construct a
\Omega(K^{\ell+1})\times\Omega(K^{\ell+1})
matrix whose columns describe the action of the operator
p(\bm{z})\mapsto\mathrm{tr}\left(\bm{C}(t)\nabla_{\bm{z}}^{2}p(\bm{z})\right)-\bm{z}^{\top}\bm{C}(t)\nabla_{\bm{z}}p(\bm{z})
on each
p_{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{z})
,
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathcal{N}_{k,\ell+1}
. In general, this matrix will be dense. Then, solving
eq.

43
amounts to solving a dense
\Omega(K^{\ell+1})
-dimensional linear system; doing so via Gaussian elimination has computational complexity
\Omega(K^{\ell+1})^{3}
.

The obvious downside to this approach is that the computational complexity scales poorly with the latent dimension. For example, with a
K=10
-dimensional latent space and an order
\ell=2
polynomial approximation,
K^{\ell+2}=10^{4}
, whereas
K^{3\ell+3}=10^{9}
. This motivates us to develop a more efficient algorithm to solve the linear system.

Triangularizing the polynomial solver.

The reason we could not efficiently solve the linear system was that the
\Omega(K^{\ell+1})\times\Omega(K^{\ell+1})
matrix was dense. We will show that by performing an appropriate change-of-variable, the solution to
eq.

43
can be obtained by a sparse linear solve.

Indeed, let

\bm{C}(t)=\bm{Q}(t)\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1}(t),\ldots,\lambda_{k}(t))\bm{Q}(t)^{\top},\quad\bm{Q}(t)\bm{Q}(t)^{\top}=\mathbb{I}_{K},

be an eigendecomposition of
\bm{C}(t)
, and define the rotated coordinate system
\bm{v}=\bm{Q}(t)^{\top}\bm{z}
.
Writing
\chi(\bm{z},t)=\tilde{\chi}(\bm{v},t)
and
g(\bm{z},t)=\tilde{g}(\bm{v},t)
, the Poisson
equation
eq.

43
becomes

\sum_{i=1}^{K}\lambda_{i}(t)\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bm{v}_{i}^{2}}-\bm{v}_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{v}_{i}}\right)\tilde{\chi}(\bm{v},t)=\tilde{g}(\bm{v},t).

(44)

We now expand
\tilde{\chi}
and
\tilde{g}
in the monomial basis.
For a monomial
p_{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{v})
,
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathcal{N}_{K,\ell+1}
the operator in
eq.

44
acts as

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{K}\lambda_{i}(t)\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\bm{v}_{i}^{2}}-\bm{v}_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{v}_{i}}\right)p_{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{v})

(45)

\displaystyle=-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K}\lambda_{i}(t)\bm{\alpha}_{i}\right)p_{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{v})+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\lambda_{i}(t)\bm{\alpha}_{i}(\bm{\alpha}_{i}-1)p_{\bm{\alpha}-2\bm{e}_{i}}(\bm{v}),

where
\bm{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
is the
i
th standard basis vector and we interpret

\bm{\alpha}_{i}-2(\bm{e}_{i})_{i}=0
whenever
\bm{\alpha}_{i}<2
. Thus, the operator maps a monomial of
degree
l
to itself plus terms of degree
l-2
.

This implies that if the monomials are ordered by total degree, then the matrix representation of
the operator in
eq.

44
is block lower triangular. In particular, the
coefficients of the degree-
(\ell+1)
terms in
\tilde{\chi}
are uncoupled from all lower-degree
terms. If we write

\tilde{\chi}(\bm{v},t)=\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathcal{N}_{K,\ell+1}}a_{\bm{\alpha}}(t)p_{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{v}),\qquad\tilde{g}(\bm{v},t)=\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathcal{N}_{K,\ell+1}}b_{\bm{\alpha}}(t)p_{\bm{\alpha}}(\bm{v}),

then for each vector
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathcal{N}_{K,\ell+1}
with
\sum\bm{\alpha}_{j}=\ell+1
,

-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K}\lambda_{i}(t)\bm{\alpha}_{i}\right)a_{\bm{\alpha}}(t)=b_{\bm{\alpha}}(t)\Rightarrow a_{\bm{\alpha}}(t)=-b_{\bm{\alpha}}(t)/\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K}\lambda_{i}(t)\bm{\alpha}_{i}\right).

(46)

Hence the top-degree coefficients are obtained by coefficient-wise division.
Once these coefficients are known, the coefficients of degree
\ell-1
can be obtained by subtracting off the contribution of the second derivative term in
eq.

45
coming from the degree
\ell+1
terms.
Proceeding recursively over degrees
\ell+1,\ell-1,\ell-3,\ldots
yields the coefficients for half of the terms in the monomial basis expansion of
\tilde{\chi}
.
An identical recursion over degrees
\ell,\ell-2,\ldots
yields the coefficients for the second half of the terms.

Recovering the decomposition in the original coordinates.

After solving for
\tilde{\chi}(\bm{v},t)
, we compute

\nabla_{\bm{z}}\chi(\bm{z},t)=\bm{Q}(t)\nabla_{\bm{v}}\tilde{\chi}(\bm{Q}(t)^{\top}\bm{z},t).

Since
\bm{z}=\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t))
, the chain rule gives

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\psi(\bm{x},t)=\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}\nabla_{\bm{z}}\chi(\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}(\bm{x}-\bm{m}(t)),t).

This yields the weighted Helmholtz decomposition of the polynomial surrogate

\tilde{\bm{r}}^{(\ell)}(\bm{x},t)
.

Computational complexity of Helmholtz decomposition.

The number of monomials of total degree at most
\ell+1
is

\Omega(K^{\ell+1})
for fixed
\ell
. Thus, after diagonalizing
\bm{C}(t)
, solving

eq.

44
requires only
\Omega(K^{\ell+1})
coefficient updates rather than
a dense solve of a
\Omega(K^{\ell+1})\times\Omega(K^{\ell+1})
linear system. The diagonalization
of
\bm{C}(t)
costs
O(K^{3})
, which is dominated by
O(K^{\ell+2})
for fixed
\ell\geq 1
.
Computing
\nabla_{\bm{v}}\tilde{\chi}(\bm{v},t)
contributes an additional factor of
K
.
Therefore, the overall
cost of computing the polynomial Helmholtz decomposition is
O(K^{\ell+2})
for fixed
\ell
.

C.1
Theoretical results for OU spiral

In this subsection, we theoretically characterize the suboptimality of the symmetric and square root gauges when the prior and likelihood models are known and fixed.
In other words, we quantify

d_{\mathcal{Q}}^{\star}=\inf_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}}\textrm{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\left(\mathbb{Q}\;\|\;\mathbb{Q}^{\star}\right),

(48)

where
\mathbb{Q}^{\star}
is the true posterior for the latent SDE model (
Section

3.1
).
In the OU spiral model, since the prior SDE is linear and the likelihood model is linear and Gaussian,
\mathbb{Q}^{\star}
is a Gaussian process.

Even though exact inference is possible, we show that
existing simulation-free VI algorithms cannot be expected to recover the posterior
.
Specifically, consider prior initial distribution
p(\bm{x},0)
equal to the stationary distribution
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},1/(2\alpha)\mathbb{I}_{2})
and all continuously differentiable marginal parameters
(\bm{m}(t),\bm{S}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
with
\bm{S}(t)
positive definite.
We show that
d_{\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{F}}}^{\star}
grows linearly with
\omega
for the corresponding variational families defined by the symmetric and square root gauges.
We denote them by
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{sym}}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{sqrt}}
, respectively.

Theorem 4

(Suboptimality of simulation-free VI algorithms in the OU spiral)
.

Consider the OU spiral model
eq.

47
, and let
\mathbb{Q}^{\star}
denote the (true) posterior SDE.

Fix any
c_{0}>1
and
\omega\geq c_{0}\alpha
. Then every distribution
\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{sym}}\cup\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{sqrt}}
satisfies

\textrm{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\left(\mathbb{Q}\;\|\;\mathbb{Q}^{\star}\right)\geq\min\left\{\frac{1}{8},\ \frac{1-\frac{1}{2c_{0}}}{2\sqrt{2}}\right\}T\omega-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}+C_{\mathrm{OU}}

where

C_{\mathrm{OU}}(\omega,\alpha,\bm{C},\bm{d},\sigma^{2},T,\bm{y})=\log p(\bm{y})+N\log(2\pi\sigma^{2}).

(49)

Moreover, if
p(\bm{x},0)=\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\frac{1}{2\alpha}\mathbb{I}_{2})
, then
C_{\mathrm{OU}}
is lower bounded by a constant
C_{\mathrm{OU-stationary}}
that does not depend on
\omega
.

Proof.

Our proof consists of five key steps:

(i)

Express the KL divergence to
\mathbb{Q}^{\star}
at any pair
(\bm{m}(t),\bm{S}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
as sum of two functions
f_{\bm{m}}
and
f_{\bm{S}}
, where
f_{\bm{m}}
is a function of the marginal means
(\bm{m}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
and
f_{\bm{S}}
is a function of the marginal covariances
(\bm{S}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
.

(ii)

Justify that
f_{\bm{m}}
is minimized at the posterior mean
(\bm{m}^{\star}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
.

(iii)

Rewrite
f_{\bm{S}}
in terms of the eigendecomposition of
\bm{S}(t)
at each time
0\leq t\leq T
.

(iv)

Use an occupation-time argument to show that either
\lambda_{1}(t)
is often bounded away from zero, in which case the rotational mismatch term is large, or else
\lambda_{1}(t)
is often close to zero, in which case keeping it small requires a large negative control and hence large control cost.

(v)

Show that when
p(\bm{x},0)
is the stationary distribution of the prior (
47
),
\log p(\bm{y})
is lower bounded by a constant not depending on
\omega
.
This is done using uniform (in
\omega
) spectral bounds on the observation covariance matrix.

Step
(i)
:
Let
\mathbb{Q}
be an SDE with diffusion coefficient
\mathbb{I}_{2}
and Gaussian marginals
q(\bm{x},t)=\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}(t),\bm{S}(t))
, where
(\bm{m}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
is a differentiable mean function and
(\bm{S}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
is a differentiable and symmetric, positive definite covariance function.
Moreover, suppose that
\mathbb{Q}
has drift function of the form

\displaystyle\bm{f}_{q}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{A}_{q}(\bm{x},t)\bm{x}+\frac{d}{dt}\bm{m}(t)-\bm{A}_{q}(t)\bm{m}(t)\quad\bm{A}_{q}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{K}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\bm{S}(t)^{-1}

where
\bm{K}(t)
is a solution to the Lyapunov equation
eq.

16
. In
Proposition

2

q(\bm{x},t)

Using Girsanov’s Theorem (
Theorem

2
), the KL divergence to the posterior decomposes as

\textrm{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\left(\mathbb{Q}\;\|\;\mathbb{Q}^{\star}\right)=f_{\bm{m}}(\bm{m})+f_{\bm{S}}(\bm{S})+C_{\mathrm{OU}}(\omega,\alpha,\bm{C},\bm{d},\sigma^{2},T,\bm{y}),

where
f_{\bm{m}}
depends only on the mean path,
f_{\bm{S}}
depends only on the covariance path, and

is a constant defined in

49

\mathbb{Q}

These functions are given by

\displaystyle f_{\bm{m}}(\bm{m})=\frac{1}{2}(\bm{m}(0)-\bm{\mu}(0))^{\top}\bm{V}(0)^{-1}(\bm{m}(0)-\bm{\mu}(0))+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\frac{d}{dt}\bm{m}(t)-(-\alpha\mathbb{I}_{2}+\omega\bm{J})\bm{m}(t)\right\|_{2}^{2}dt

\displaystyle\ +\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(\bm{C}\bm{m}(t_{i})+\bm{d}-\bm{y}(t_{i})\big)^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{R}^{-1}\big(\bm{C}\bm{m}(t_{i})+\bm{d}-\bm{y}(t_{i})\big),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{tr}\left(\big((\bm{K}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\bm{S}(t)^{-1})-(-\alpha\mathbb{I}_{2}+\omega\bm{J})\big)\bm{S}(t)\big((\bm{K}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\bm{S}(t)^{-1})-(-\alpha\mathbb{I}_{2}+\omega\bm{J})\big)^{\mathsf{T}}\right)dt

\bm{f}_{\bm{S}}

\{\bm{y}(t_{n})\}_{n=1}^{N}

Since
\bm{R}=\sigma^{2}\bm{C}\bm{C}^{\mathsf{T}}

\bm{C}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{R}^{-1}\bm{C}=\sigma^{-2}\mathbb{I}_{2}

and therefore

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\bm{R}^{-1}\bm{C}\bm{S}(t_{i})\bm{C}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{tr}\!\big(\bm{S}(t_{i})\big)\geq 0.

We will drop these two terms from
\bm{f}_{\bm{S}}
when constructing our lower bound. The single remaining term is the path KL term.

(ii)
:
Because the posterior path law is Gaussian, the map
\bm{m}\mapsto f_{\bm{m}}(\bm{m})

(\bm{m}^{\star}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}

\bm{K}(t)=(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{S}(t)^{1/2})\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}

\bm{S}(t)^{1/2}=\bm{U}(t)\bm{\Lambda}(t)^{1/2}\bm{U}(t)^{\top}
is the symmetric square root. Rewriting
\bm{K}(t)
in terms of
\bm{U}(t)
and
\bm{\Lambda}(t)
yields

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{S}(t)^{1/2}\right)\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}=\frac{d}{dt}\bm{U}(t)\bm{U}(t)^{\top}+(1/2)\bm{U}(t)\frac{d}{dt}\bm{\Lambda}(t)\bm{\Lambda}(t)^{-1}\bm{U}(t)^{\top}+

(54)

\displaystyle\bm{U}(t)\bm{\Lambda}(t)^{1/2}\bm{\Omega}(t)^{\top}\bm{\Lambda}(t)^{-1/2}\bm{U}(t)^{\top}.

Multiplying

54

\bm{U}(t)^{\top}
on the left and
\bm{U}(t)
on the right yields

\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{q}(t)=\bm{\Omega}(t)+(1/2)\frac{d}{dt}\bm{\Lambda}(t)\bm{\Lambda}(t)^{-1}-\bm{\Lambda}(t)^{-1/2}\bm{\Omega}(t)\bm{\Lambda}(t)^{1/2}-(1/2)\bm{\Lambda}(t)^{-1}.

Hence, the entries of
\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{q}(t)
are

(\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{q}(t))_{ij}=\begin{cases}(\frac{d}{dt}\lambda_{i}(t)-1)/2\lambda_{i}(t)&\text{if $i=j$}\\
\bm{\Omega}_{ij}(t)(1-\sqrt{\lambda_{i}(t)/\lambda_{j}(t)})&\text{if $i\neq j$}\end{cases}.

(55)

Overall, the integrand is equal to

\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}(t)\bm{\Lambda}(t)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}(t)^{\mathsf{T}}\right)=\lambda_{1}(t)(\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}(t)_{11}^{2}+\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}(t)_{21}^{2})+\lambda_{2}(t)(\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}(t)_{12}^{2}+\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}(t)_{22}^{2}),

(56)

where we have now computed
\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}(t)
for the symmetric and square root gauges.

Next, we explicitly minimize

56
with respect to
w(t)
, for each of the symmetric and square root gauges.
Note that only the off-diagonal entries of
\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}(t)
depend on
w(t)
.
In particular let
\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}(t)_{12}=aw(t)+\omega
and
\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}(t)_{21}=bw(t)-\omega
for

. Then the contribution of the off-diagonal terms to the integrand can be written as

\lambda_{1}(t)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}(t)_{21}^{2}+\lambda_{2}(t)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}(t)_{12}^{2}=(\lambda_{1}(t)b^{2}+\lambda_{2}(t)a^{2})w(t)^{2}+2\omega(\lambda_{2}(t)a-\lambda_{1}(t)b)w(t)+(\lambda_{1}(t)+\lambda_{2}(t))\omega^{2}.

Completing the square yields the optimal value of
w(t)

w(t)^{\star}=-\omega(\lambda_{2}(t)a-\lambda_{1}(t)b)/(\lambda_{1}(t)b^{2}+\lambda_{2}(t)a^{2}).

The contribution of the off-diagonal terms at this optimum is

\displaystyle(\lambda_{1}(t)+\lambda_{2}(t))\omega^{2}-\omega(\lambda_{2}(t)a-\lambda_{1}(t)b)^{2}/(\lambda_{1}(t)b^{2}+\lambda_{2}(t)a^{2})

(57)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega^{2}\lambda_{1}(t)\lambda_{2}(t)(a+b)^{2}/(\lambda_{1}(t)b^{2}+\lambda_{2}(t)a^{2}).

For the symmetric gauge,
a=b=-(\lambda_{2}(t)-\lambda_{1}(t))/(\lambda_{1}(t)+\lambda_{2}(t))
, so

\lambda_{1}(t)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}(t)_{21}^{2}+\lambda_{2}(t)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}(t)_{12}^{2}\geq 4\omega^{2}\lambda_{1}(t)\lambda_{2}(t)/(\lambda_{1}(t)+\lambda_{2}(t))\geq 2\omega^{2}\lambda_{1}(t).

In the last inequality, we used the ordering of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{2}(t)\geq\lambda_{1}(t)
.

And for the square root gauge, we have
a=r(t)-1
,
b=1-r(t)^{-1}
where we have defined
r(t)=\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(t)/\lambda_{2}(t)}
. This implies

\displaystyle(a+b)^{2}=(r(t)^{2}-1)^{2}/r(t)^{2},

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(t)b^{2}+\lambda_{2}(t)a^{2}=(r(t)-1)^{2}(\lambda_{1}(t)/r(t)^{2}+\lambda_{2}(t))=2\lambda_{2}(t)(r(t)-1)^{2}

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(t)\lambda_{2}(t)(a+b)^{2}/(\lambda_{1}(t)b^{2}+\lambda_{2}(t)a^{2})=\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{2}(t)(r(t)+1)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(t)}+\sqrt{\lambda_{2}(t)})^{2}.

Hence,

\lambda_{1}(t)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}(t)_{21}^{2}+\lambda_{2}(t)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}(t)_{12}^{2}\geq\frac{\omega^{2}}{2}(\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(t)}+\sqrt{\lambda_{2}(t)})^{2}\geq 2\omega^{2}\sqrt{\lambda_{1}(t)\lambda_{2}(t)}\geq 2\omega^{2}\lambda_{1}(t).

In both cases, we have proven that for each open interval that makes up

\min_{w(t)}\left\{\lambda_{1}(t)\widetilde{\Delta}_{21}(t)^{2}+\lambda_{2}(t)\widetilde{\Delta}_{12}(t)^{2}\right\}\geq 2\omega^{2}\lambda_{1}(t),\quad\text{for a.e.~$t$}.

Therefore, for almost every

\mathrm{tr}\left(\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}(t)\bm{\Lambda}(t)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}(t)^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\geq\frac{\big(\frac{d}{dt}\lambda_{1}(t)-1+2\alpha\lambda_{1}(t)\big)^{2}}{4\lambda_{1}(t)}+2\omega^{2}\lambda_{1}(t).

We now proceed to the second set
E
. On this set, the covariance is isotropic:
\bm{S}(t)=\lambda(t)\mathbb{I}_{2},\lambda(t)=\lambda_{1}(t)=\lambda_{2}(t)
.
No eigenbasis is needed here. Since
\bm{S}-\lambda\mathbb{I}_{2}=0
on
E

\frac{d}{dt}\bm{S}(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\lambda(t)\mathbb{I}_{2}

for almost every

\bm{A}_{q}(t)
solves

\frac{d}{dt}\lambda(t)\mathbb{I}_{2}=2\lambda(t)\bm{K}(t)+\mathbb{I}_{2},

and in the symmetric square root gauge,

\bm{A}_{q}(t)=\left(\frac{d}{dt}\bm{S}(t)^{1/2}\right)\bm{S}(t)^{-1/2}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{S}(t)^{-1}=\frac{\frac{d}{dt}\lambda(t)-1}{2\lambda(t)}\mathbb{I}_{2}.

Thus, in either gauge,

\bm{A}_{q}(t)=\frac{\frac{d}{dt}\lambda(t)-1}{2\lambda(t)}\mathbb{I}_{2}\qquad\text{for a.e. }t\in E.

A direct calculation then yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left(\big(\bm{A}_{q}(t)-(-\alpha\mathbb{I}_{2}+\omega\bm{J})\big)\bm{S}(t)\big(\bm{A}_{q}(t)-(-\alpha\mathbb{I}_{2}+\omega\bm{J})\big)^{\mathsf{T}}\right)=\frac{\big(\frac{d}{dt}\lambda(t)-1+2\alpha\lambda(t)\big)^{2}}{2\lambda(t)}+2\omega^{2}\lambda(t),

which is stronger than the preceding bound.

Combining both cases, we conclude that for almost every
t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left(\big(\bm{A}_{q}(t)-(-\alpha\mathbb{I}_{2}+\omega\bm{J})\big)\bm{S}(t)\big(\bm{A}_{q}(t)-(-\alpha\mathbb{I}_{2}+\omega\bm{J})\big)^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\geq\frac{u(t)^{2}}{4\lambda_{1}(t)}+2\omega^{2}\lambda_{1}(t)

\displaystyle u(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\lambda_{1}(t)-1+2\alpha\lambda_{1}(t).

Therefore,

f_{\bm{S}}(\bm{S})\geq I=\int_{0}^{T}\left[\frac{u(t)^{2}}{8\lambda_{1}(t)}+\omega^{2}\lambda_{1}(t)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

Step

:
Fix

.
Since
\lambda_{1}(t)<\delta

2\alpha\lambda_{1}(t)\leq 2\alpha\delta=\frac{\alpha}{2\omega}\leq\frac{1}{2c_{0}}.

Define

Then, for all

\frac{d\lambda_{1}(t)}{dt}=1-2\alpha\lambda_{1}(t)+u(t)\geq a_{0}+u(t).

If

\lambda_{1}(a_{j})=\lambda_{1}(b_{j})=\delta,

so the boundary term vanishes. If

By the AM-GM inequality,

:
Lastly, we show that when

Consider the vector of stacked observations

Under the stationary OU spiral prior, the latent process is mean-zero Gaussian and

\mathrm{Cov}(\bm{x}(t_{i}),\bm{x}(t_{j}))=\frac{1}{2\alpha}e^{-\alpha|t_{i}-t_{j}|}\bm{R}_{\omega(t_{i}-t_{j})},

where
\bm{R}_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
represents the

\theta
.

Therefore the stacked observation vector
\bm{y}_{1:N}
is Gaussian,

\bm{y}_{1:N}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\big(\bar{\bm{d}},\bm{\Sigma}_{\omega}\big),

with block covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times 2N}

(\bm{\Sigma}_{\omega})_{ij}=\frac{1}{2\alpha}e^{-\alpha|t_{i}-t_{j}|}\bm{C}\bm{R}_{\omega(t_{i}-t_{j})}\bm{C}^{\mathsf{T}}+\mathbf{1}\{i=j\}\,\sigma^{2}\bm{C}\bm{C}^{\mathsf{T}}.

and mean

We first derive an
\omega
-uniform lower spectral bound. Since
\bm{\Sigma}_{\omega}
is a covariance matrix, its latent contribution is positive semidefinite. Hence,

\bm{\Sigma}_{\omega}\succeq\sigma^{2}\,\mathrm{diag}\!\big(\bm{C}\bm{C}^{\mathsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{C}\bm{C}^{\mathsf{T}}\big).

It follows that

\lambda_{\min}(\bm{\Sigma}_{\omega})\geq\sigma^{2}\lambda_{\min}(\bm{C}\bm{C}^{\mathsf{T}})=m_{*}>0,

uniformly in
\omega
.

Next we derive an
\omega
-uniform upper spectral bound. Let

\bm{z}=\big(\bm{z}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{z}_{N}^{\mathsf{T}}\big)^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N},\qquad\bm{z}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Then

\bm{z}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\omega}\bm{z}=\sigma^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{z}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{C}\bm{C}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{z}_{i}+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}e^{-\alpha|t_{i}-t_{j}|}\bm{z}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{C}\bm{R}_{\omega(t_{i}-t_{j})}\bm{C}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{z}_{j}.

Since
\bm{R}_{\omega(t_{i}-t_{j})}
is orthogonal
\|\bm{C}\bm{R}_{\omega(t_{i}-t_{j})}\bm{C}^{\mathsf{T}}\big\|\leq\|\bm{C}\|^{2}.

Therefore

\bm{z}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\omega}\bm{z}\leq\sigma^{2}\|\bm{C}\|^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\bm{z}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\|\bm{C}\|^{2}}{2\alpha}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\|\bm{z}_{i}\|_{2}\|\bm{z}_{j}\|_{2}.

Using

\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\|\bm{z}_{i}\|_{2}\,\|\bm{z}_{j}\|_{2}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\bm{z}_{i}\|_{2}\right)^{2}\leq N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\bm{z}_{i}\|_{2}^{2},

we obtain

\bm{z}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\omega}\bm{z}\leq\|\bm{C}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}\left(\sigma^{2}+\frac{N}{2\alpha}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\bm{z}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}.

Hence

\lambda_{\max}(\bm{\Sigma}_{\omega})\leq\|\bm{C}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}\left(\sigma^{2}+\frac{N}{2\alpha}\right)=M_{*},

again uniformly in
\omega
.

Thus, for every
\omega
,

m_{*}\mathbb{I}_{2N}\preceq\bm{\Sigma}_{\omega}\preceq M_{*}\mathbb{I}_{2N}.

Since
\bm{y}_{1:N}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar{\bm{d}},\bm{\Sigma}_{\omega})
, its density is

p_{\omega}(\bm{y}_{1:N})=(2\pi)^{-N}\det(\bm{\Sigma}_{\omega})^{-1/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\bm{y}_{1:N}-\bar{\bm{d}})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\omega}^{-1}(\bm{y}_{1:N}-\bar{\bm{d}})\right).

Using the spectral bounds above,

(\bm{y}_{1:N}-\bar{\bm{d}})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\omega}^{-1}(\bm{y}_{1:N}-\bar{\bm{d}})\leq\frac{\|\bm{y}_{1:N}-\bar{\bm{d}}\|_{2}^{2}}{m_{*}},\quad\det(\bm{\Sigma}_{\omega})\leq M_{*}^{2N}.

Therefore,

p_{\omega}(\bm{y}_{1:N})\geq(2\pi)^{-N}M_{*}^{-N}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|\bm{y}_{1:N}-\bar{\bm{d}}\|_{2}^{2}}{2m_{*}}\right).

Taking logarithms yields

\log p_{\omega}(\bm{y}_{1:N})\geq-N\log(2\pi)-N\log M_{*}-\frac{\|\bm{y}_{1:N}-\bar{\bm{d}}\|_{2}^{2}}{2m_{*}}.

Thus the claimed bound holds with

C_{\mathrm{OU-stationary}}=N\log\sigma^{2}-N\log M_{*}-\frac{\|\bm{y}_{1:N}-\bar{\bm{d}}\|_{2}^{2}}{2m_{*}}.

which is independent of
\omega
.

Simulation experiment details

In this subsection, we provide details regarding our OU spiral experiments.
These experiments support the theoretical results in
Section

B.2
and
Section

C.1
.
In summary, these results state that Helmholtz-SDE achieves zero KL gap to the true posterior for all rotation speeds
\omega
, while the symmetric
[
15
,
16
]
and square root
[
6
]
gauges incur a KL gap that grows linearly in
\omega

These results characterize the variational families themselves, not the optimization problem solved in practice: with finite-capacity parameterizations of
\bm{m}(t|\bm{\phi})
and
\bm{S}(t|\bm{\phi})
, and with stochastic gradient-based optimization, neither family attains its theoretical optimum.
Our experiments test whether the theoretical separation between Helmholtz-SDE and the gauges translates into practical differences.

For both our inference and learning experiments, we use the Adam optimizer
[
35
]
with initial learning rate
10^{-3}
, final learning rate
10^{-4}
, and exponential learning rate decay. We run experiments on an NVIDIA A100 or H100 GPU.

We generate the OU dataset by sampling latents according the initial distribution
p(\bm{x},0)=\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},1/(2\alpha)\mathbb{I}_{2})
and prior dynamics
eq.

47
on
[0,5]
.
For all experiments we fix
\alpha=0.2
.
We sample using Euler–Maruyama with stepsize
5\cdot 10^{-4}
. To generate observations, we select a random subset of times
\{t_{n}^{(m)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
on the interval
[0,5]
for each trial
m
, and then we draw
\bm{y}^{(m)}(t_{n}^{(m)})\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{C}_{\mathrm{true}}\bm{x}^{(m)}(t_{n})+\bm{d}_{\mathrm{true}},\sigma^{2}\bm{C}_{\mathrm{true}}\bm{C}_{\mathrm{true}}^{\top})

\bm{C}_{\mathrm{true}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
and
\bm{d}_{\mathrm{true}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are chosen to have standard normal entries, with
\bm{C}_{\mathrm{true}}
normalized to have determinant
1
.
In our experiments, we vary the rotation speed
\omega
, the number of observation

, and the observation noise
\sigma>0

Inference.

We first investigate the gap in expressivity of the variational family when the parameters of the generative model are held fixed at their true values.

We do so by performing inference over
16
independent trials.
For each trial, we can compute the true posterior by running the Kalman–Bucy smoother.
We do so with the SING codebase, which
28
prove converges to the true posterior in a single step in the conjugate setting.
We fix the time discretization of the posterior to stepsize
5\cdot 10^{-4}
.

To represent the variational posterior, we discretize
[0,5]
on a grid with constant separation
5\cdot 10^{-3}
(
1001
total grid points).
At each point we parameterize the posterior mean and covariance, and we interpolate linearly between the grid times.
This yields approximately
6\cdot 10^{3}
variational parameters to learn, per trial.
Moreover, note that for a randomly sampled time, the unbiased estimate to the path KL depends only on the two neighboring grid points.
For this reason, the variational posterior is slow to converge using a single Monte Carlo sample.
Instead, we use
N_{\mathrm{time}}=1001
samples to compute each gradient update (i.e., on average, one sample per grid point).
At each time point we draw
N_{\mathrm{space}}=1
spatial sample.
Increasing the sample count this way adds minimal computational overhead, since both sampling and gradient computation parallelize across samples.
We perform training for
6\cdot 10^{5}
iterations.

To compute each direction (forward and backward KL divergence) of the symmetric KL divergence between the true and approximate posterior, we use Girsanov’s formula
eq.

12
.
In particular, we discretize the interval
[0,5]
with stepsize
5\cdot 10^{-4}
and at each time evaluate the integrand of the path KL
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{path}}
.
Since both the true and approximate posterior drifts are affine, this can be done in closed form using Gaussian expectations.

Broadly, we see that the suboptimality of choosing a single posterior drift per one-time Gaussian marginals (either the symmetric or square root gauge) increases as the number of observations decreases, the observation noise increases, and the rotational speed increases.
Even when the rotation speed is strong, choosing a single posterior drift suffices as long as posterior uncertainty is small, i.e., the observations are strongly informative of the latent trajectory.
In settings where the posterior is weakly constrained by the data, we see order-of-magnitude differences between Helmholtz-SDE and the symmetric and square root gauges.

Joint inference and learning.

Next, we study the impact of the constrained variational family imposed by prior simulation-free VI algorithms on dynamics learning.

To do so, we parameterize the prior drift with a shallow neural network

(2)
\to
Linear(2, 100)
\to
Softplus
\to
Linear(100, 2)
\to
(2)

We learn prior dynamics together with the affine mapping
\{\bm{C},\bm{d}\}
and output noise
\bm{R}

We perform learning using
1024

For our experiments, we considered numerous parameterizations of the variational posterior, including a per-trial grid posterior as in our inference experiments and an encoder-based amortized posterior as in our Lorenz experiments.
For the grid posterior, we observed very slow convergence, even with a large number of Monte Carlo samples

We hypothesize that this is because the parameter space is high dimensional (1001 grid points

6\cdot 10^{6}

On the other hand, training the neural network posterior was unstable; it required choosing a large hidden dimension and performing aggressive gradient clipping.
We did not observe that any parameterization preferred a specific gauge, i.e., the choice of posterior parameterization was independent of the gauge.

We settled on the kernel-GLM posterior parameterization introduced by
15

At a high level, this parameterization chooses a grid of temporal inducing locations
\bm{\tau}=\{\tau_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{induce}}}

[0,T]

It then defines the posterior mean
\bm{m}(t|\bm{\phi})

\bm{M}\bm{C}k(\bm{\tau},t|\bm{\phi})
, where
k(\cdot,\cdot|\bm{\phi})
is a kernel function,
k(\bm{\tau},t|\bm{\phi})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{induce}}\times 1}
is a gram vector,
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times N_{\mathrm{induce}}}
contains the inducing values, and
\bm{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{induce}}\times N_{\mathrm{induce}}}
is a pre-conditioning matrix.
If
\bm{C}

.

A similar parameterization is used for the covariance
\bm{S}(t|\bm{\phi})
, which is chosen to be dense.
See
[
15

We use
N_{\mathrm{induce}}=200

The kernel parameters (but not the inducing values) are shared across all trials.

3\cdot 10^{4}

Following

\bm{\epsilon}(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\eta^{2}\mathbb{I}_{3})

In the standard stochastic Lorenz attractor, the authors choose

and parameters
(\Delta t,\sigma,\eta)=(2.5\cdot 10^{-2},1.5\cdot 10^{-1},10^{-2})

Figure

11

We instead simulate the latents over a long interval
T=5
with larger process noise
\sigma=5

\Delta t=2.5\cdot 10^{-1}
, and larger measurement noise
\eta=0.3

\sigma\bm{s}^{-1}\approx(6.0\cdot 10^{-1},5.2\cdot 10^{-1},5.2\cdot 10^{-1})
. We find that this setting leads both to more interesting latent trajectories (frequent switching between lobes of the attractor, which is not demonstrated in the original dataset;
Figure

12

We found that the Gaussian process parameterization of the posterior described by
16
(see Appendix D therein) led to poor empirical performance. This is because, at a high level, the posterior defines the mean and covariance at each time as a linear combination of means and covariances at observation times.
Since in our setting the observations occur sparsely in time, interpolating in the space of means and covariances directly is inappropriate.
Instead, we adopt the posterior parameterization from SDE Matching, wherein interpolation occurs in the embedding space
[
6

.

Moreover,
16
proposes chunking the full time series of observations into non-overlapping windows. For each observation within a given window, they produce an embedding using a specified number of future observations, and then they aggregate embeddings within the window. We find that choosing window size and number of observation shorter than the full time series degrades performance. For this reason, we consider a window equal to the full time series and allow each embedding to depened on all future observations.

Metrics.

We evaluate the fidelity of the posterior and prior dynamics according to five metrics, which we explain below.

nELBO.
Average per-trial negative ELBO (the variational objective). Smaller values are better but do not imply better posterior recovery or learning.

Posterior inference.

To evaluate posterior quality, we use the log likelihood of the true latents under the posterior distribution averaged over time
T
and trials
M
, calculated as

\frac{1}{TM}\sum_{m,t}\log\bar{q}(\bm{y}^{(m)}(t),t).

(59)

\bar{q}(\bm{y},t)
represents the posterior predictive density of
\bar{\bm{y}}(t)=\bm{C}\bm{x}(t)+\bm{d}+\bm{\epsilon}(t),\ \bm{\epsilon}(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{R})
and
\bm{y}(t)=\bm{C}\bm{x}(t)+\bm{d}
is the true latent at time
t
, passed through the output mapping.
Note that for all cases except latent-SDE
[
41
]
,
q(\bm{x},t)=\mathcal{N}(\bm{m}(t),\bm{S}(t))
is Gaussian, in which case the posterior predictive distribution admits a closed form
\bar{q}(\bm{y},t)=\mathcal{N}(\bm{C}\bm{m}(t)+\bm{d},\bm{C}\bm{S}(t)\bm{C}^{\top}+\bm{R})
.
For latent-SDE, we estimate
\bar{q}(\bm{y}^{(m)}(t),t)
by drawing
N_{\mathrm{density}}=64
samples
\bm{x}^{(k)}(t)\sim q(\bm{x},t)
, computing
\bar{\bm{y}}^{(k)}(t)=\bm{C}\bm{x}^{(k)}(t)+\bm{d}
, and then evaluating

\frac{1}{TM}\sum_{m,t}\log\left(\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{density}}}\sum_{k}\mathcal{N}(\bm{y}|\bar{\bm{y}}^{(k)}(t),\bm{R})\right).

(60)

Note that
eq.

60
is a sensible approximation to
eq.

59
since

\displaystyle\bar{q}(\bm{y},t)

\displaystyle=\int q(\bm{x},t)\mathcal{N}(\bm{y}|\bm{C}\bm{x}+\bm{d},\bm{R})d\bm{x}=\mathbb{E}_{q(\bm{x},t)}[\mathcal{N}(\bm{y}|\bm{C}\bm{x}+\bm{d},\bm{R})]\approx\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{density}}}\sum\mathcal{N}(\bm{y}|\bar{\bm{y}}^{(k)}(t),\bm{R}).

Prior learning.

For evaluating the learned prior dynamics, we draw
2048
samples from the learned prior, starting from
q(\bm{x},0)
and simulated using Euler–Maruyama with discretization
10^{-3}
. We evaluate the learned output mapping at these samples, from which we compute the following metrics:

(i)

One-time symmetric KL divergence
: symmetric KL divergence at a each time
[0,T]
, averaged over times. For samples from two distributions
p
and
q
, we estimate the KL distance
\textrm{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\left(p\;\|\;q\right)
by fitting two Gaussian kernel density estimates and computing a Monte Carlo average.

We do so using the following procedure, which mirrors that of
37
:

(a)

Using 5-fold CV, select the KDE bandwidth for
q
; fit the density estimate (on all samples) using the chosen bandwidth.

(b)

Split the samples from
p
into
10
folds. Holding out a single fold at a time, repeat the following subprocedure:

i.

Choose the kernel bandwidth for
p
using 5-fold CV; fit the density estimate (on all training folds) using the chosen bandwidth.

ii.

Compute a Monte Carlo estimate to the KL divergence using the held-out fold.

Importantly, the outer CV step for samples from
p
ensures that the negative entropy is not systematically downward biased.

(ii)

Global autocorrelation error
: Using the samples from learned prior and true prior, we estimate the correlation matrix at lag
0\leq\tau\leq T
,
\mathrm{Corr}(\bm{x}(t),\bm{x}(t+\tau))
, for all
t
such that
t,t+\tau
lie on the sampling grid.
At each such time, we compute the squared Frobenius norm difference between the two correlation matrices and average over all valid times. We repeat this procedure for all lags
\tau
on a linear grid of
[0,2]
with spacing
5\cdot 10^{-2}
, and we average over these lags.
Finally, we normalize by the total number of entries in the correlation matrix (
3\times 3=9
) and take the square root of the result.

(iii)

Lobe-wise autocorrelation error
:
The Lorenz attractor produces trajectories that alternate between two “lobes” and exhibit local rotational motion within each lobe (
Figure

12
A). The global autocorrelation error averages over the full attractor and mixes errors in inter-lobe switching with errors in the within-lobe rotational dynamics. Therefore, we compute a separate “lobe-wise” autocorrelation error, which assesses whether the dynamics are learned accurately within each each lobe of the Lorenz attractor.

We achieve this via the following procedure:

(a)

Assign each point to one of the two lobe centers, i.e., fixed points, according to Euclidean distance.

(b)

From these labels, fit 2D planes (using PCA) to the points belonging to each lobe. Choose the offset such that the lobe center lies on the plane.

(c)

Project the points within each lobe onto their respective planes.

(d)

Using these projections, identify contiguous time segments of minimum time
>5\cdot 10^{-2}
(i.e., rapid transitions between lobes are dropped).

(e)

Using these segments within each lobe, compute the lagged autocorrelation error.
If the segment is shorter than the lag, it is dropped.
Note that we divide by
2\times 2=4
correlation matrix entries instead of
9
.

D.2
Experimental predator-prey dynamics

In this subsection, we provide details regarding our experiments with the algae-rotifer predator-prey system from
11
.

Preprocessing.

We obtain the dataset made public by the authors at
https://figshare.com/articles/dataset/Time_series_of_long-term_experimental_predator-prey_cycles/10045976/1
.

In our analysis, we consider only the first trial, which is the longest and spans
374
days.
We choose not to consider multiple trials (
4
total in the same experimental condition) simultaneously since there are apparent differences between them, for example, the mean and standard deviation of population sizes. Modeling the dynamics across multiple trials using, for example, a hierarchical Bayesian model is interesting future work.

Since we learn autonomous (non time-dependent) dynamics, we restrict each trial to periods of coherent oscillation as determined
11
. Otherwise, the latent dynamics must explain both coherent and non-coherent (no systematic phase lag) oscillations.
For the trial of interest, this yields four subtrials
\{[19.5,130.5],[159.5,240.5],[274.5,295.5],[314.5,370.5]\}
.

We next log transform the data. The reason for this decision is that, in stochastic models of population dynamics, modeling the noise as multiplicative in the state is a popular choice, particularly due to the stability properties it affords
[
45
]
.
Log transforming the data then allows us to model the noise as state-independent, which is the setting in which Helmholtz-SDE operates.
For example, consider the Lotka-Volterra equations with state-dependent noise for predator
y
and prey
x

\displaystyle dx(t)=(\alpha x(t)-\beta x(t)y(t))dt+\sigma_{x}xd\bm{w}_{1}(t)

Wavelet analysis.

We assess the quality of the learned prior dynamics using the wavelet analysis from
11
.
In particular, we draw
1024
samples from the learned prior with stepsize
2\cdot 10^{-3}
days, each of length
100
days.
From each sample, we downsample to a grid of
1
day and extract the peak cyclic period (
Figure

4
C), the lag of predator behind prey (
Figure

4
D), and the rotational coherence (
Figure

4
D).
We then report the median and IQR of these statistics across
10
replicates of fitting the latent SDE model.

We use the authors’ public codebase
https://github.com/berndblasius/WaveletAnalysis
to perform our analyses.
Next, we briefly discuss how we compute each of these statistics.
We refer the interested reader to
11
as well as
65
for additional background on wavelet analysis.

1. The continuous wavelet transform.

The
continuous wavelet transform
of a one-dimensional signal
(x(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
is defined as the convolution of the signal with the complex-valued wavelet function, centered at time
t
and dilated by scale
s>0

W_{x}(s,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}\int_{0}^{T}x(t^{\prime})\psi^{*}\left(\frac{t^{\prime}-t}{s}\right)dt^{\prime}.

(63)

Here,
\psi^{*}
represents the complex conjugate of
\psi
.
While there are many possible choices of wavelet function, we consider the Morlet wavelet with
\omega_{0}=6
:

\psi(\tau)=\pi^{-1/4}\exp\{i\omega_{0}\tau\}\exp\{-\tau^{2}/2\}.

To build intuition, consider what
eq.

63
would compute if
\psi
were a pure complex exponential
\psi(\tau)=\exp\{2\pi i\tau\}
. Then
|W_{x}(s,t)|
reduces to the magnitude of the Fourier transform of
x
at frequency
1/s
: it measures how strongly the signal oscillates at period
s
, but the result does not depend on
t
and so carries no information about when in the time series this oscillation occurs.
The Morlet wavelet recovers time localization by multiplying the complex exponential by a Gaussian envelope
\exp\{-\tau^{2}/2\}
, which restricts the integral in
eq.

63
to a neighborhood of
t
.
The price of this localization is that
\psi
no longer corresponds to a single frequency; instead, each scale
s
is associated with a
Fourier period
obtained by matching the dominant frequency of
\psi
to that of a complex exponential.
For the Morlet wavelet, this correspondence is

T(s)=\frac{4\pi s}{\omega_{0}+\sqrt{2+\omega_{0}^{2}}},

(64)

so that
T(s)\approx 1.03s
when
\omega_{0}=6

[
65
]
. With this identification,
|W_{x}(s,t)|
measures the local oscillatory content of the signal at time
t
and period
T(s)
.

Following
11
, we evaluate the wavelet transform on a logarithmic grid of scales. The smallest scale is
s_{0}=2
days, and the largest scale is
s_{0}2^{J}
, where
J=5
is the number of octaves, and we use
100
grid points per octave.

2. Wavelet power spectrum and cross spectrum.

The local oscillatory content of a single signal is summarized by the
wavelet power spectrum
(WPS),
|W_{x}(s,t)|^{2}
.
To compare two signals,
x_{1}
(algae) and
x_{2}
(rotifers), we form the
wavelet cross-spectrum
(WCS)
W_{x_{1}x_{2}}(s,t)=W_{x_{1}}(s,t)W_{x_{2}}^{*}(s,t)
, whose magnitude
|W_{x_{1}x_{2}}(s,t)|
quantifies the joint oscillatory power at scale
s
and time
t
, and whose argument

\phi(s,t)=\arg W_{x_{1}x_{2}}(s,t)

(65)

gives the local phase difference between the two signals at scale
s
and time
t
.
A nonzero phase difference
\phi(s,t)
indicates that one signal leads the other; converting to a time lag at the corresponding Fourier period yields
\Delta t(s,t)=\phi(s,t)T(s)/(2\pi)
.
Following
11
, we smooth both the WPS and WCS using a Gaussian temporal filter and a moving-average filter over scales; we denote this operator by
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)
.

A high value of
|W_{x_{1}x_{2}}(s,t)|
does not by itself indicate that the two signals are oscillating coherently: it may simply reflect that both signals have large individual power at
(s,t)
, even if their phases drift independently. To distinguish coherent oscillation from coincident power, we use the
wavelet coherence
(WCO), defined as the normalized, locally smoothed cross-spectrum

R(s,t)=\frac{\left|\mathcal{S}\left(W_{x_{1}x_{2}}(s,t)\right)\right|}{\sqrt{\mathcal{S}\left(|W_{x_{1}}(s,t)|^{2}\right)\cdot\mathcal{S}\left(|W_{x_{2}}(s,t)|^{2}\right)}},

(66)

Coherence takes values in
[0,1]
and is invariant to the marginal amplitudes of
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
:
R^{2}(s,t)\approx 1
at points
(s,t)
where the two signals oscillate with a stable phase relationship over a local neighborhood.
As pointed out by
65
, smoothing is necessary since otherwise the wavelet coherence is identically
1
for all times and scales.

3. Cone of influence.

Because the wavelet transform
eq.

63
is computed on a finite signal, the integral is implicitly zero-padded outside
[0,T]
. This padding contaminates
W_{x}(s,t)
near the boundaries
t=0
and
t=T
, with the contamination extending farther into the interior at larger scales
s
. The
cone of influence
(COI) is the region of the time-scale plane where this edge contamination is small; following
65
, points
(s,t)
outside the COI are discarded.

4. Extracted statistics.

For each prior sample, we use the WCS, WCO, and per-signal WPS to extract three summary statistics matching those reported by
11
.

1.

Peak cyclic period
: The peak period of each individual population (algae, rotifers) is obtained by averaging the WPS over the COI and selecting the period at which the time-averaged amplitude is maximized:

T^{\star}_{x_{i}}=\arg\max_{s}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{COI}}(s)|}\int_{\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{COI}}(s)}|W_{x_{i}}(s,t)|\,dt,

(67)

where
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{COI}}(s)
is the COI corresponding to scale
s
. This is mapped to a period via
eq.

64
.
The peak period for a given sample is the average of the rotifer and algae peak periods. We report the median peak period across all samples,
T^{\star}_{\mathrm{ensemble}}
.

2.

Predator-prey phase lag
: To estimate the phase lag, we restrict attention to scales in a neighborhood of the dominant joint period, computed as in
eq.

67
but using the WCS instead of the WPS. We also restrict to time-scale points
(s,t)
that lie within the COI and have wavelet coherence
R^{2}(s,t)
exceeding the significance threshold
0.83
used by
11
.
At each retained time
t
, we record the phase
\phi(s^{\star}(t),t)
at the scale
s^{\star}(t)
of locally maximal coherence within the band; times at which
s^{\star}(t)
falls at the band boundary are excluded. For each prior sample, we summarize these phases by their circular mean

\bar{\phi}^{(n)}=\arg\left(\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{\mathrm{coh}}}e^{i\phi(s^{\star}(t),t)}\right),

where
\mathcal{T}^{(n)}_{\mathrm{coh}}
is the set of retained times for sample
n
.
The ensemble phase lag is the arithmetic mean of
\bar{\phi}^{(n)}
across prior samples with at least one retained time.
We convert this angle to a lag in time units by multiplying by
T^{\star}_{\mathrm{ensemble}}/(2\pi)
, where
T^{\star}_{\mathrm{ensemble}}
is the corresponding ensemble peak period from the previous statistic. We report the lag computed from the median peak period
T^{\star}_{\mathrm{ensemble}}
.

3. Rotational coherence
: The rotational coherence is the fraction of all time points
t
whose within-band coherence argmax
s^{\star}(t)
lies inside the COI and whose coherence value
R^{2}(s^{\star}(t),t)
exceeds the significance threshold
0.83
,

\rho_{\mathrm{coh}}=\frac{|\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{coh}}|}{|\mathcal{T}|},

where
\mathcal{T}
is the length of the full signal.
This statistic measures the proportion of the trajectory over which algae and rotifers oscillate with a stable phase relationship near the dominant period, and is a sample-level analogue of the “rotational fraction” reported by
11
.
We report the median across prior samples.

D.3
Reduced-order models for fluid dynamics

In this subsection, we discuss our experiments modeling a two-dimensional incompressible flow past a circular cylinder.
We obtain the public dataset from
15
at
https://github.com/coursekevin/svise
.

The data is simulated on the domain
[-0.98,28.98]\times[-1.98,1.98]
with a circular cylinder of radius
0.5
placed at
(0,0)
(
Figure

5
). The velocity field is obtained by numerically solving the incompressible Navier–Stokes equation

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{u}_{i}(\bm{z},t)}{\partial t}+\sum_{j}\bm{u}_{j}(\bm{z},t)\frac{\partial\bm{u}_{i}(\bm{z},t)}{\partial\bm{z}_{j}}=-\frac{\partial\bar{p}(\bm{z},t)}{\partial\bm{z}_{i}}+\frac{1}{\mathrm{Re}}\sum_{j}\frac{\partial^{2}\bm{u}_{i}(\bm{z},t)}{\partial\bm{z}_{j}^{2}}

(68)

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{\partial\bm{u}_{i}(\bm{z},t)}{\partial\bm{z}_{i}}=0,\quad i\in\{1,2\},\quad(\bm{z},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}

where
\bm{u}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\bar{p}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are the non-dimensionalized velocity and pressure, respectively, and
\mathrm{Re}
is the Reynolds number.

\mathrm{Re}
represents the ratio of inertial to viscous forces.
For our experiments we use
\mathrm{Re}=2\cdot 10^{3}
, which yields strong vortices in the wake of the cylinder.

Equation

68
is solved by discretizing the spatial domain on a grid of size
596602
, and solving the Navier–Stokes equation on this grid (in time), subject to the boundary conditions defined by the outer domain and 2D cylinder (
Figure

5
A).
The dataset simulates the dynamics for
305.1
time units with snapshots every
0.1
time units.

Preprocessing.

We follow the same preprocessing steps as in
15
.
Namely, we drop the first
20\%
of the snapshots representing transients in the system.
We then use the interval
[61,256.2]
for training and the subsequent
10
time units
[256.2,266.2]
for forecasting.
From the training data, we perform the proper orthogonal decomposition (POD)
[
7
]
and retain the leading spatial modes that together capture
90\%
of the time-averaged kinetic energy; this amounts to
38
modes.
We then project the snapshots onto these
38
POD modes to obtain a low-dimensional representation of the system.
Lastly, we normalize the POD modes by the maximum standard deviation, computed along each dimension (i.e., normalization is by a scalar).

Modeling the dynamics in the space of the top POD modes will yield a reduced-order model of the fluid dynamics.
Given an initial condition, one can project the velocity field on the grid into the space of POD modes, roll forward the (stochastic) dynamics, and reconstruct the trajectory on the original grid.
Mathematically, this is equivalent to taking the dynamics
f
defined in the space of POD modes and defining the dynamics
\bar{f}=\bm{\Phi}f(\bm{\Phi}^{\top}(\bm{z}_{\mathrm{grid}}-\bar{\bm{z}}_{\mathrm{grid}}))
in the original data space, where
\bar{\bm{z}}_{\mathrm{grid}}
is the mean of the velocity measurements across time and
\bm{\Phi}
is the matrix whose columns contain the top POD modes.
Note that the dynamics
\bar{f}
live on a low-dimensional affine subspace.

To generate observations, we subsample the POD modes to a grid of size
\Delta t=0.3
.
We observe that many of the higher-order modes oscillate at a smaller scale than the lower-order modes.
In order to add Gaussian noise in such a way that the higher order modes do not have low signal-to-noise ratio, we add noise proportional to the standard deviation along each dimension.
And to demonstrate a setting with high posterior uncertainty, we choose noise standard deviation equal to the
0.3
times the standard deviation along each dimension (signal-to-noise ratio
3.33
).
We observe that with more frequent observations (e.g.,
\Delta t=0.1
) and smaller noise scale (e.g.,
0.1
times the standard deviation along each dimension), the discrepancy between the learned dynamics is less apparent.

We learn a
K=38

The prior initial state is modeled as Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{0},\bm{V}_{0})
,
\bm{\mu}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\bm{V}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{38\times 38}

eq.

1

\mathcal{N}(\bm{y}(t)|\bm{x}(t),\bm{R})

\bm{R}
is the diagonal observation noise, which we fix to its true value.

Metrics.

In
Figure

5

We draw samples from the prior starting at the true initial condition
\bm{x}(0)
at the beginning of the training window, and simulating forward for
10
time units using Euler–Maruyama with stepsize
5\cdot 10^{-3}
.
We observe that some trajectories begin to numerically diverge beginning at approximately
15-20
time units, so we only analyze the first
10
units.

At each sampled trajectory, we reconstruct the velocity field on the original grid by projecting back through the POD modes, and we extract the streamwise velocity component
\bm{u}_{1}(\bm{z},t)
along the strip
[1,10]\times[-0.1,0.1]
in the near wake.
We then compute the joint space–time autocorrelation

C(\Delta\bm{z}_{1},\Delta t)=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{\bm{u}}_{1}(\bm{z}_{1},t)\tilde{\bm{u}}_{1}(\bm{z}_{1}+\Delta\bm{z}_{1},t+\Delta t)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{\bm{u}}_{1}(\bm{z}_{1},t)^{2}\right]},

where
\tilde{\bm{u}}_{1}=\bm{u}_{1}-\mathbb{E}[\bm{u}_{1}]
denotes the fluctuation about the sample mean and the expectation is approximated by averaging over reference points
(\bm{z}_{1},t)
along the strip and across prior samples.

There are two apparent patterns in the space–time autocorrelation: (1) oscillation in streamwise lag (from the periodic vortex pattern in the wake) and (2) diagonal banding in time (vortices being carried downstream).
The failure mode of simulation-free VI is specifically a loss of temporal coherence.
This is because temporal correlations are those that depend on the joint posterior path law which, in turn, degrades the prior path law. The spatial correlations, on the other hand, exist within a single snapshot of time.

10
-unit forecasting window.

38
POD vectors. Finally, we compute the MSE between the reconstructed velocity and the projection of the (true) velocity onto the top-
38
POD modes. We normalize by the total grid size.
