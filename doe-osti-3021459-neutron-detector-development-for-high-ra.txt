Project Abstract
 The Second Target Station (STS) at the Oak Ridge National Laboratory Spallation Neutron
Source (SNS) is expected to produce a neutron source that is the brightest in the world, having a
significantly higher peak brightness compared to the First Target Station (FTS), where the
reflectometry instruments already fall short of being able to handle the highest available flux by a
factor of ~100. The neutron reflectometers at the SNS FTS are not suitable for the reflectometers
planned for the SNS STS because of its unprecedentedly high peak brightness and the already
limited capabilities of these instruments at high count rates. Furthermore, the high rate instruments
that are now under development are expected to underperform with regard to gamma sensitivity,
meaning that signal-to-noise will be reduced and they will be unable to measure weaker reflectance
phenomena in the presence of the significant prompt gamma flash at SNS. To address these
challenges, we conducted research to advances associated instruments, techniques, and capabilities
for the SNS STS reflectometers. More specifically, we investigated the development of a scalable,
2-D positive-sensitive, pixelated, scintillator-based neutron detector module based on a newly
developed scintillator. The work has potential for impact on future neutron reflectometry
instruments and neutron science performed at the SNS STS. In particular, the improved
background rejection capability of the developed detector could extend the available experimental
dynamic Q range, allowing it to measure weak reflectance phenomena.

1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Background and Original Objectives
The Second Target Station (STS) at the Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) Spallation
Neutron Source (SNS) is expected to deliver a cold neutron source with more than 20 times the
peak brightness of the existing First Target Station (FTS). This unprecedented flux presents a
fundamental instrumentation challenge: the neutron reflectometers already in operation at the FTS
fall short of handling peak count rates by a factor of roughly 100, and the reflectometers planned
for the STS—M-STAR, QIKR, and VBPR—will face local instantaneous count rates as high as
10 MHz/cm². No existing detector technology is capable of meeting this requirement while
simultaneously achieving the spatial resolution, detection efficiency, and gamma-ray rejection
demanded by these instruments.
This project, funded by the DOE Office of Science Basic Energy Sciences program (September
2021 – August 2025), was undertaken at the University of Tennessee in close collaboration with
the ORNL SNS Detector Group. The central objective was to develop a scalable, two-dimensional
position-sensitive, pixelated scintillator-based neutron detector module that concurrently meets the
following performance targets for the SNS STS reflectometers:
 • Count rate capability of ≥10 MHz/cm², at least three orders of magnitude beyond current
 state-of-the-art detectors;
 • Gamma-ray sensitivity below 1×10⁻⁷ to suppress the intense prompt gamma flash
 characteristic of spallation sources;
 • Neutron detection efficiency ≥80% at 4 Å;
 • Position resolution better than 1 mm FWHM in both x and y directions;
 • Time resolution of approximately 1 ns; and
 • A scalable module architecture (50 mm × 50 mm per module) that can be tiled to cover
 the full detector apertures required by STS instruments.
To achieve these goals, the project pursued five interconnected tasks: (1) optimization and
characterization of pixelated lithium-containing scintillator arrays, (2) design of multi-channel
ASIC-based readout electronics, (3) development of advanced signal processing algorithms for
high-rate environments, (4) detector characterization at HFIR and SNS beamlines, and (5)
workforce development through coursework in the RIDGE program.

Summary of Major Results
The project produced three peer-reviewed publications in leading nuclear instrumentation journals
and advanced detector technology through three successive generations of prototype development,
each building on lessons learned from the previous.
In the first major effort, the team developed and characterized a ⁶Li-based pixelated neutron
detector in collaboration with ORNL and demonstrated it at the SNS Liquid Reflectometer (BL-
4B). Each scintillator pixel was individually coupled to its own photosensor and processed through
an independent readout channel. The prototype achieved a global time-averaged count rate of at
least 1.8×10⁶ counts per second and a local instantaneous count rate of at least 1.73×10⁶ cps/cm²—
three or more orders of magnitude above the existing ³He multi-wire proportional counter deployed

 4

at the same beamline. Critically, this maximum was not determined by the detector itself but by
the maximum flux available at the beamline, indicating the detector's rate ceiling remains to be
fully characterized. Neutron reflectivity measurements with representative samples confirmed that
the pixelated architecture is a viable path forward for STS-class instruments.
In the second major effort, two scintillator candidates were systematically compared: a ⁶Li-loaded
EJ-270 plastic scintillator and a Ce-doped LiCaAlF₆ (LiCAF) single crystal. Light yields were
measured at 2,176 ± 91 photons per neutron capture for EJ-270 and 2,651 ± 108 for LiCAF:Ce.
EJ-270 demonstrated effective neutron-gamma discrimination via charge comparison (figure-of-
merit: 1.13 ± 0.01) and time-over-threshold methods. LiCAF:Ce, while not exhibiting pulse shape
discrimination, maintained acceptable thermal neutron detection performance in gamma-ray fields
up to approximately 584 mR/hr, and its γ-ray sensitivity was quantified under high-exposure
conditions. This comparative study clarified the practical tradeoffs between the two materials and
informed the direction of the third phase of detector development.
In the third and final effort, the team pivoted to a Ce-doped microcolumnar LiI scintillator film
readout by PETsys TOFPET2 ASIC electronics to construct a high-rate, high-resolution neutron
Anger camera. The LiI:Ce film demonstrated a substantially higher light yield than the GS20 Li-
glass scintillator widely used in current reflectometry instruments (11,729 vs. approximately 6,000
photons per neutron capture) and a gamma-ray sensitivity of 1.67×10⁻⁵—roughly an order of
magnitude lower than GS20's ~10⁻⁴. Neutron detection efficiency reached 63% at 4.2 Å. The
camera sustained count rates up to at least 481 kHz without measurable rate loss, and spatial
resolution reached 0.47 mm—well below the 1 mm requirement for STS reflectometers. Position
linearity was also improved relative to prior designs.

Scientific Breakthroughs
Taken together, the three generations of prototype development produced several noteworthy
scientific and technical breakthroughs:
 • Count rate breakthrough. Demonstration of pixelated scintillator architecture at SNS:
The direct measurement of ≥1.73×10⁶ cps/cm² local instantaneous count rate at an operational SNS
reflectometer beamline constitutes the highest count rate demonstrated by a scintillator-based
neutron detector at a spallation source reflectometer to date, exceeding the existing instrument
detector by more than three orders of magnitude.
 • Spatial resolution achievement. Sub-millimeter spatial resolution with high-rate ASIC
 readout:
A spatial resolution of 0.47 mm—significantly better than the 1–2 mm specification—was
achieved in a camera architecture capable of sustaining high count rates, demonstrating that
resolution and rate need not be traded off when the readout electronics are appropriately matched
to the scintillator.
 • Novel scintillator characterization. LiI:Ce scintillator characterization for neutron
 applications:
The systematic characterization of a Ce-doped microcolumnar LiI scintillator film for neutron
detection—including light yield, gamma sensitivity, efficiency, count rate, and linearity—provides
the field with a new reference data set for a material that had not previously been fully evaluated

 5

in this context. The substantially lower gamma sensitivity relative to GS20 is particularly
significant for STS applications.
 • Comparative material study. Scintillator discrimination benchmarking:
The head-to-head comparison of EJ-270 and LiCAF:Ce under identical measurement conditions
established a rigorous baseline for the community regarding the practical pulse shape
discrimination achievable with these materials under realistic gamma-ray backgrounds.

Overall Contributions to the Office of Science Mission
This project directly advances the Office of Science mission to deliver scientific tools and trained
personnel that maintain U.S. leadership in neutron scattering science. The SNS STS represents one
of the most significant investments in neutron scattering infrastructure in a generation. The
detector technologies developed and characterized here address the single most technically
demanding instrumentation challenge associated with STS commissioning: providing detectors
capable of exploiting the full brightness of the source for neutron reflectometry, which is among
the most sensitive probes of soft matter, biological membranes, polymer thin films, and magnetic
surface phenomena.
The improved background rejection capability of the developed detector systems—particularly the
reduced gamma-ray sensitivity relative to existing technologies—is expected to extend the
accessible dynamic Q-range for reflectometry measurements. This will enable observation of
weaker reflectance phenomena that are currently obscured by detector background, opening new
scientific territory in surface science, materials characterization, and biological interface studies.
Furthermore, the high-rate, low-gamma-sensitivity detector concepts developed here have
identified applicability across other neutron instrument classes, including small-angle neutron
scattering, diffraction, spectroscopy, and imaging, multiplying the potential return on this
investment.

2. PROJECT OBJECTIVES
 The Second Target Station (STS) at the Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) Spallation
Neutron Source (SNS) is expected to produce a significantly brighter neutron source than the First
Target Station (FTS) [1-3]. Similarly, the European Spallation Source (ESS) also expects to have
a very high peak brightness [4]. The unprecedented neutron flux makes necessary the upgrade of
the neutron scattering instruments now in operation since they will not function properly at such a
high count rate, thereby requiring that neutron flux be sacrificed on the source side. At ORNL, the
most challenging instrument upgrade will be for neutron reflectometry. While the current
reflectometer instruments (e.g., LIQREF) at the FTS already fall short of handling the highest
expected count rates of 1 MHz/cm2 by a factor of ~100, the count rates expected by the
reflectometers (e.g., M-STAR) planned for the STS will be as high as 10 MHz/cm2.

 To address the high-rate challenge for future neutron reflectometry instruments at the SNS STS,
we propose research that advances associated instruments, techniques, and capabilities. More
specifically, we propose to develop a scalable, 2-D positive-sensitive, pixelated scintillator-based
neutron detector module of 50 mm by 50 mm sensitive area with an exceptional count rate
capability (i.e., 10 MHz/cm2). The detector module will feature a neutron detection efficiency not
less than 80% at 4 Å, a spatial resolution better than 1 mm × 1 mm, and a time resolution of around
1 ns. Also of critical importance, advanced algorithms, including artificial intelligence and
machine learning algorithms, will be investigated and developed first in software and then
implemented in hardware to ensure that the gamma-ray sensitivity will be below 1 × 10-7 in order
to ensure excellent reflectometer performance despite the prompt gamma flash phenomenon at
SNS. While improvements in count rate will reduce the experimental time needed to conduct
measurements, the proposed improvement in background rejection techniques will extend the
available experimental dynamic Q range, allowing our instrument to measure even extremely weak
reflectance phenomena, thereby having significant impact on the achievable neutron science.

 To achieve these performance goals, every component of the detector module needs to be
optimized, including the scintillator, the photodetector, and the readout electronics. Our design
will use Ce-doped LiCaAlF6 (LiCAF) scintillators [5-8]. The scintillation decay time of 40 ns is
sufficiently fast, reducing the possibility of pulse pile-up at high count rates. It is even less than
the decay time of Li-glass scintillators, which usually ranges from 60 ns to 80 ns. Unlike Li-glass,
LiCAF can discriminate between neutrons and gamma-rays based on the difference in pulse
shapes. Pulse height discrimination can also be effectively applied in digital signal processing in
combination with pulse shape discrimination to reject gamma-rays from the prompt flash. Monte
Carlo modeling will be used together with validating proof-of-concept experiments to optimize
the pixelated scintillator design for high rate and high light collection. For the photodetector, high-
density NUV sensitive SiPMs [9,10] will be used. They are preferable over multi-anode PMTs due
to their high photon detection efficiency, excellent single photon time resolution, fast response,
insensitivity to magnetic fields, compactness, low bias supply, and low cost. For the associated
readout electronics, the Citiroc ASIC originally developed by the high-energy physics community
and recently commercialized by CAEN s.p.a. meets our requirements [11, 12]. The readout system
will be designed to allow for a readout rate of 10 MHz/cm2 while achieving optimal gamma-ray
rejection performance at the SNS STS. Software that leverages the existing software framework
at SNS and addresses data management complexity for high rate instruments will be developed.

3. SCIENTIFIC BACKGROUND

A. Next generation high flux spallation neutron sources
 In spallation reactions, fast neutrons are emitted when high-energy protons hit a high-Z target
(e.g., tungsten or mercury). At neutron sources, the protons are usually generated by a pulsed
LINAC, where the proton beam is accelerated from a few MeV to a few hundred MeV or even
higher. These fast neutrons will be slowed down using neutron moderators. At SNS FTS, water-
filled cells are used to reduce the neutron energies to obtain room-temperature neutrons, while
containers of liquid hydrogen at a temperature of 20 K are employed to generate cold neutrons. As
a result, pulsed thermal/cold neutrons will be produced. They will then be delivered to the locations
using various neutron optical components for conducting neutron reflectometry experiments.
Some example spallation neutron sources that are currently in operation around the world are the
SNS FTS in Oak Ridge, USA, the J-PARC in Japan, the SINQ at PSI in Switzerland, and the ISIS
Neutron Source in the UK. Although current spallation neutron sources have been successfully
used in many applications to investigate the surface behavior of materials of interest based on
neutron reflectivity for several decades [13-15], there is a significantly strong demand to increase
the available neutron flux from the physics, materials science, and bioengineering communities [1,
16-17]. The benefits from a higher flux spallation neutron source include but are not limited to:
a) The measurement time can be reduced from hours/days to minutes or even seconds [1]. This
 could make the beam available to more users, enhancing the impact of a spallation neutron
 source;
b) The sample size can be smaller. Sample preparation plays a crucial role in neutron reflectivity
 measurements [18]. It is as important as the neutron production and the neutron detection
 processes. The relaxed requirement on sample size would make feasible the investigation of
 samples that cannot be studied before due to the difficulty of realizing a suitable sample size;
c) Fast dynamic phenomena could be observed because reflectivity data with good enough
 statistics can be collected within a time window that is less than the time frame in which a
 process of interest may occur [1]. This brings many new opportunities for understanding of
 material surface properties.
In summary, breakthrough science could emerge from carefully optimized neutron reflectometry
studies carried out using a high flux spallation neutron source. Thus, a sensitive and specific
reflectometry instrument can be expected to have high impact for addressing emerging science
challenges in many areas.

 As a result of the aforementioned demand, there are two new spallation neutron sources of
particular note that are under construction. One is the STS at SNS in Oak Ridge, USA. The goals
of this new target station are to greatly expand the capabilities and largely reduce the limitations
of the SNS FTS to maintain the US leadership in neutron scattering for the next few decades, to
support the US economy, and to provide solutions to the research challenges in materials and life
science, energy, national security, and environmental change. It is expected to provide a cold
neutron source with a more than 20 times increase in peak brightness compared to the SNS FTS
[1]. The other high flux spallation neutron source is the ESS in Sweden. Compared to the SNS
STS, the neutrons to be generated at the ESS have a lower peak brightness, as shown in Figure 1.
Thus, instrument development for the neutron reflectometers at the SNS STS will be expected to
be much more challenging than that for the ESS because of the high peak brightness.

B. Gamma-rays in high flux spallation neutron sources
 Gamma-rays are also produced from spallation neutron sources. They can be generated through
the spallation process within the high-Z target itself. In addition, they can be emitted from inelastic
neutron scattering and neutron capture reactions in many other locations, including the moderators
used to slow down the fast neutrons, the reflectors surrounding the moderators, the neutron guides
for cold neutron beam transport, the supporting/construction materials around the sample or the
neutron detector, and the shielding materials for reducing the dose to workers. These gamma-rays
have a wide energy distribution. Figures 2(a-b) show the gamma-ray energy spectra measured with
high-purity germanium detectors at the VESUVIO and INES beamlines and the IMAT beamline
at ISIS, respectively [21, 22]. As shown in these figures, the energy of the gamma-rays could be
as high as 12 MeV, and many discrete photopeaks are noticeable because of the neutron capture
reactions. This data is also consistent with what has been observed more qualitatively so far from
the prompt flash at the STS FTS — a large quantity of gammas up to roughly 8 MeV.

 For neutron reflectivity measurements, it would be ideal for the neutron detector to show a
nearly zero sensitivity to gamma-rays in order to be able to resolve the full range of momentum
transfer (Q). In particular, very low gamma sensitivity is essential to accurately measure the
reflectivity at large Q values; it improves the dynamic range of the measured reflectivity and,
therefore, provides the desired details about material surface properties. At large Q values, the
measured reflectivity is typically so low that the signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio is rather low, and any
detected gamma-rays that are misclassified as cold neutrons introduce significant systematic error
in the measurement [23]. A potential result of this bias due to misclassification is that the calculated
neutron scattering length density may largely deviate from its true value. Therefore, it is essential
for the STS reflectometer instruments to be developed to demonstrate an excellent gamma
rejection capability (≤ 1 × 10-7) while also operating at a 10 MHz counting rate, which is a
challenging engineer problem.

C. Current neutron detectors for neutron reflectometry
 Position sensitive neutron detector designs for neutron reflectometry at current spallation
neutron source facilities can be classified into four categories: linear position-sensitive 3He
proportional counters [24-27], 2-D position-sensitive 3He multi-wire proportional counters
(MWPC) [24, 28], 2-D position-sensitive ZnS(Ag)/LiF scintillation screens with wavelength
shifting (WLS) fiber readouts [29], and Li-glass scintillator-based neutron Anger cameras [30,31].
The typical configurations of these four types of neutron detectors are presented in Figure 3. The
main characteristics of these detectors are briefly described below. The reasons why they cannot
meet the neutron detector requirements for the M-STAR, QIKR, and VBPR neutron reflectometers
(see Table 1) planned for the SNS STS are also described.

 Linear position-sensitive 3He proportional counter. The typical neutron detection efficiency of
a linear position-sensitive 3He proportional counter is relatively high (e.g., 61% at 1.8 Å for the
counters used in the POSY-II reflectometer [25]) because of the large cross-section of 3He in the
neutron wavelength of interest. It features a gamma-ray sensitivity of 1.7×10-9 at 10 mR/h due to
the low interaction probability of gamma-rays with the low density 3He gas or the detector tube as
well as the relatively small energy deposition by gamma-rays [33]. Therefore, the pulse height
discrimination (PHD) method is effective enough to differentiate between neutrons and gamma-
rays. Also, a large sensitive detector area (e.g., a few m2) can be made by using multiple detectors.
The position resolution in the vertical direction is about 1-2 mm based on either the charge division
or the time difference methods [34, 35]. It is approximately the same as the diameter of the 3He
tubes in the horizontal direction. The count rate of this type of proportional counter is rather
limited. For example, the maximum count rate of the high-count-rate 3He position-sensitive
detector system for NEUNET-HCR is 535 kHz per detector (size: 1 m length by 8 mm diameter),
corresponding to 6.7 kHz/cm2 [35]. It is far below the rate requirement for high flux neutron
reflectometry.
 2-D positive-sensitive 3He MWPC. Compared with the linear position-sensitive 3He proportional
counter, this type of neutron detector is also based on 3He gas and has similar neutron detection
efficiency and gamma-ray sensitivity. On the contrary, both the anode wires and the cathode strips
are used to collect the charges produced by neutron absorption in 3He and the subsequent
multiplication that ensues. As a result, comparable position resolution can be obtained in the both
the horizontal and vertical directions [36]. However, the count rate capability is greatly affected
because of the 2-D readout compared with the 3He proportional counter with individual readout
[37,38]. For example, the maximum count rate of the 65 cm × 65 cm MWPCs was only about 10
kHz in Reference 35. Thus, 3He-based MPWCs are not suitable for high flux neutron reflectometry
due to their very limited count rate performance.
 2-D position-sensitive ZnS(Ag)/LiF scintillation screen with WLS fiber readout. The gamma-
ray sensitivity of an optimized ZnS(Ag)/LiF scintillation screen can be close to 1 × 10-7 [39].
Because of the relatively large neutron capture cross-section of 6Li, the neutron detection
efficiency can be reasonably high (e.g., 85% for 3.27 meV neutrons) [39]. Large-sized screens are
commercially available to obtain a large sensitive area coverage, such as the 30.5 cm × 30.5 cm
EJ-440 scintillator available from Eljen Technology. Due to the fact that the screen is not optically
transparent to the emitted scintillation photons and 2-D position information is needed, use of WLS

 12

fibers in the x and y directions is necessary to channel the optical photons to collecting
photodetectors. By optimizing the geometric configuration of the WLS fibers and the thickness of
the scintillation screen, a position resolution less than 1 mm in both directions has been reported
[40]. Similar to the 2-D charge readout used in the MWPCs, the WLS fiber readout also reduces
the count rate capability. For example, the 2-D neutron detectors designed with an improved
counting rate performance for the ESS are expected to handle count rates only up to 17 kHz/mm2
[41]. A factor that additionally limits the capability of the ZnS(Ag)/LiF scintillation screen-based
detector at high count rates is the µs-duration scintillation decay time. Some efforts have been
devoted to reducing the slow decay time by developing alternatives to ZnS(Ag)/LiF. One notable
example is the Zn-doped ZnO/LiF scintillator [42]. Nevertheless, the scintillation decay time is
still a few µs. In summary, the rate requirement as listed in Table 1 cannot be met by the
ZnS(Ag)/LiF nor the ZnO(Zn)/LiF scintillation screens.
 Li-glass scintillator-based neutron Anger camera. A neutron detection efficiency of 60% can
be obtained using a GS20 Li-glass scintillator with a thickness of 2 mm for 0.5 Å neutrons [43].
Large-sized GS20 glass scintillators are available to design instruments with a large sensitive area.
This can also be accomplished by using multiple detector modules with a relatively small size.
Also, the Li-glass light yield is about 6,000 photons per neutron capture, allowing low-energy
gamma-rays to be rejected based on the PHD method. However, as shown in Figure 2, there are
many high-energy gamma-rays produced by spallation neutron sources, and they have a relatively
large probability of being misclassified as neutrons using the PHD based technique alone. The
GS20 glass scintillator does show a pulse shape discrimination (PSD) capability, but it’s so small
that it’s considered negligible [44]. As a result, the gamma-ray sensitivity of the GS20 glass
scintillator is unacceptably high (i.e., on the order of 1 × 10-4) [44] considering the large gamma-
ray flux from the prompt flash at the SNS STS. To obtain the 2-D position information based on
the Anger principle, multi-anode PMTs or SiPM arrays are normally used to register the
scintillation photons. The disadvantage of this readout is the reduced ability to manage high count
rates. As an illustration, the maximum count rate of the GS20 glass scintillator-based neutron
Anger camera with a sensitive size of 157 mm × 157 mm is only 15 kHz [45]. Due to the low rate
capability and the high gamma-ray sensitivity, the GS20 glass scintillator-based neutron Anger
cameras are not suitable for the neutron reflectometers planned for the SNS STS.

D. Application of SiPMs to neutron detection
 PMTs are the most widely used photodetectors in the neutron reflectometry instruments
currently in operation. In many cases, the PMTs need to be well shielded to reduce the effect of
magnetic fields. On the contrary, SiPMs are not sensitive to magnetic fields and, therefore, the
shielding materials are not needed. In addition, SiPMs exhibit many other advantages over
standard PMTs, including higher photon detection efficiency, single photon detection, excellent

 13

single photon time resolution, compactness, low bias supply, and low cost. These features make
SiPMs a promising candidate for many applications including next generation reflectometers.
Although SiPMs have many features that outperform traditional PMTs, radiation damage caused
by neutrons is still a concern for the application of SiPMs in neutron detection. Neutrons interact
with 30Si in the SiPMs and produce defects, affecting the overall detector performance.
Nevertheless, it has been demonstrated that SiPMs can be used to detect cold neutrons in typical
small angle neutron scattering experiments without a significant performance degradation for ten
or more years of normal operation (240 days per year and 24 hours per day) [48]. Cold neutrons
with a wavelength of 5 Å were used in their studies to irradiate the SiPMs at the KWS-1 instrument
at the Heinz Maier-Leibnitz Zentrum. The neutron fluxes used were 9.96 × 106 n cm-2 s-1 and 1 ×
108 n cm-2 s-1 with a total neutron dose of 6 × 1012 n/cm2. Also, it was shown that SiPMs can be
used in time-of-flight neutron scattering experiments with a ~9% degradation in timing resolution
over a long term of operation (neutron dose: 1 × 1012 n/cm2 at 5 Å) [49]. In Reference 50, it was
demonstrated that the dark current of the SiPMs after exposure of up to 1 × 1010 neutron/cm2 dose
can be significantly reduced and their single photon detection capability can be restored even at
room temperature through high temperature thermal annealing. Furthermore, it was found the
radiation hardness of SiPMs can be improved by introducing impurities [51].

4. APPROACH AND METHODS
  In this work, we propose to develop a new 2-D position sensitive, pixelated scintillator-based
neutron detector module that concurrently shows all of the following characteristics at the neutron
wavelengths ranging from 2 Å to 40 Å: the capability of handling count rates up to 10 MHz/cm2,
gamma-ray sensitivity less than 1 × 10-7, a neutron detection efficiency above 80% at 4 Å, a
position resolution better than 1 mm FWHM in both x and y directions, a scalable detector sensitive
area size of 50 mm × 50 mm (such that multiple modules can be tiled together to obtain the larger
detector area coverage required for the STS instruments), and a time resolution of around 1 ns.
Thus, our future reflectometry instruments for the SNS STS will meet or exceed all of the
requirements listed in Table 1. Furthermore, the new understanding gained from the development
this high-rate instrument for reflectometry has likely impacts for other neutron instruments that
require higher rates for improved performance: small angle neutron scattering, direct and indirect
spectroscopy, diffraction, and imaging [20]. The proposed work includes the following 5 tasks:
• Task 1: Optimization and characterization of pixelated LiCAF scintillator arrays
• Task 2: Design of a multi-channel readout scheme based on ASICs
• Task 3: Signal processing algorithm development for high-rate application
• Task 4: Detector characterization at HFIR and SNS
• Task 5: Development of relevant RIDGE coursework

 The performance schedule for the proposed tasks is presented in Table 2. Reporting tasks (R)
represent milestones where significant progress is to be reported, or, if at the scheduled end of a

B. Optimization of pixelated LiCAF scintillator arrays
  To meet all the detector requirements for the neutron reflectometers planned for the SNS STS,
the pixel size, the scintillator thickness, the reflector used to isolate the photons produced in
individual pixels, and the scintillator surface roughness need to be carefully investigated to obtain
an optimal LiCAF scintillator array. Concerning the choice of pixel size, the position resolution
will be compromised and count rate capability will be reduced if it is too large. In the case of small
pixel size, it requires one to use readout electronics with too many channels, increasing cost and
complexity, although the position resolution and the count rate could be further improved.
Additionally, even though SiPMs with customized sizes can be available from the vendors, the
SiPM size and associated cost should also be considered when determining the scintillator pixel
size. This is because the scintillator pixels need to be readout individually by each SiPM element
in order to make a one-on-one coupling to improve the count rate performance. Considering again
the scintillator thickness, it should be large enough such that the neutron detection efficiency can
meet the minimum requirement of 60% intrinsic efficiency; any additional thickness can increase
the probability of detecting gamma-rays. The light collection design is considered to be very
important to obtain a better pulse shape discrimination, improved position resolution, and to reduce
the optical cross-talk among neighboring pixels. Different reflectors will be considered, including
BaSO4, ESR Vikuity, and titanium dioxide. The design of the reflector region will be carefully
determined. It could be thicker, for example, by use of an ESR layer facing each pixel with a
black, absorptive layer in between, or if a layer of air is used to create a greater refractive index

 17

difference. If the optical design is insufficient, the reflectivity will not be high enough and the
optical cross-talk cannot be effectively mitigated. If the reflector design is thick, the dead space
between adjacent SiPMs will be enlarged. As a result, the neutrons arriving in this space will no
longer be detected, and an excessive dead region could negatively affect the accuracy of the
measured reflectivity data.

 The scintillation detector optimization process will primarily be done by performing Monte
Carlo simulations with Geant4 and/or MCNP6 plus ZEMAX, which we have found to be
equivalent. All the parameters described above will be thoroughly studied. All the physics
processes involved in the interaction of neutrons with the scintillator will be considered as well as
the optical physics process, including the production of optical photons from the scintillation
process, the transport of optical photons in the scintillator, the light guide and the reflector, and
the photon collection of by the photodetectors (i.e., SiPMs). The SiPM electronic response to the
collected photons will not be included in the Monte Carlo modeling. Instead, it will be considered
separately to minimize the computation time. The Monte Carlo codes have been intensively used
by the PI and his research group to model optical photons emitted by various scintillators for other
DOE, NSF, DHS, or DTRA funded projects. For example, Figure 7(a) shows our comparison of
the measured and simulated time resolutions of a YAP-based alpha detector for associated particle
imaging as a function of the threshold used in the leading edge discriminator, and (b) shows our
comparison of measured and simulated Compton edges in a 3” diameter by 12” long EJ-309 liquid
scintillator readout by dual-sided PMTs. As shown in this figure, there is a reasonably good
agreement between measurement and simulation results, validating our simulation models.

D. Characterization of the optimized LiCAF scintillator array in a laboratory environment
 The optimized LiCAF scintillator array will be thoroughly evaluated in a laboratory
environment before testing it with spallation neutron sources. The evaluation will be conducted
using various gamma-ray and neutron sources—to include high source activity gamma and neutron
measurements. The gamma-ray sources will include calibration sources, neutron capture gamma-
rays, and a mCi-class source. The neutron sources available in the PI’s lab are a regular 252Cf
neutron source, including one with strength 106 n/s that can be used at close range, and a 252Cf
parallel plate ionization chamber. When it comes to the need for a more relevant source, it is
advantageous that the PI has had a close, ongoing relationship since 2008 with the detector group
at ORNL’s SNS. See Task 4 for more information.

 The LiCAF scintillator array will be carefully assessed in terms of pulse height distribution,
pulse shape discrimination, gamma-ray sensitivity, time resolution, position resolution, and
response uniformity. A detailed description about the assessment is provided as follows:
 Pulse height distribution. Considering the large energy range (i.e., tens of keV to 12 MeV) of
the gamma-rays emitted by spallation neutron sources, the pulse height distribution will be
measured with low-energy, medium-energy, and high-energy gamma-rays. The low-energy
gamma-rays will be available from the calibration gamma-ray sources, including 137Cs, 60Co, 22Na,
152
 Eu, and 133Ba sources. The medium-energy and high-energy gamma-rays will be produced
based on the neutron capture reactions. The thermal neutrons needed will be generated by using
an available neutron source.
 Pulse shape discrimination. The neutron-gamma discrimination performance of the scintillator
array will be quantified by using our 252Cf neutron source moderated by polyethylene to generate
a slow neutron spectrum. The absolute source strength will be understood by counting the neutrons
produced per second using an available 252Cf at ORNL and through use of MCNP to model the
effect of the moderating geometry. It will also be tested in the presence of a mCi-class 137Cs source
on an available linear positioner in order to examine the PSD performance as a function of
exposure rate. Various PSD algorithms will be investigated, including the charge comparison
method, where timing windows are optimized; pulse gradient analysis; frequency gradient
analysis; and model-based methods. The PSD performance will be quantified both by the standard

 19

Figure-of-Merit method, and we will also determine intrinsic slow neutron efficiency as a function
of gamma sensitivity given experiments at different gamma rates.
 Gamma-ray sensitivity. The gamma-ray sensitivity will be measured based on the most
commonly used procedure as defined by R. Kouzes et al. [33]. Considering the characteristics of
the LiCAF scintillator, a gamma-ray sensitivity below 1 × 10-7 is expected. We expect that we can
design a gamma-ray rejection algorithm to maintain this low sensitivity even in a high gamma-ray
exposure environment. Such a low sensitivity will be extremely beneficial to reduce the adverse
effects of the unwanted large gamma-ray flux accompanied with the high flux neutrons.
 Time resolution. The neutron reflectivity curve can be measured by employing either a neutron
beam with a fixed wavelength while varying the incident angle or a white neutron beam with a
fixed angle [56]. For the latter approach, the ToF technique can be used to determine the neutron
wavelengths. The information about the time resolution of the scintillator array is needed for this
purpose. The time resolution will be measured with two different experimental setups based on
either a DRS4 waveform digitizer or ASIC-based readout electronics. The time resolution is
expected to be around 1 ns considering the relatively large light yield, as well as the fast
scintillation rise and decay times. This is far better than the time resolution requirement for typical
neutron reflectometers, usually 100 µs [20].
 Position resolution. Flood maps will be first measured using the regular 252Cf source and the
low-energy, medium-energy, and high-energy gamma-ray sources to ensure that individual
scintillator pixels are accurately identified. The position resolution will then be studied at a cold
neutron beamline at either the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR) or the SNS FTS. A position
resolution of less than 1 mm in both x and y directions is expected.
 Response uniformity. It would be ideal for the detector response not to be dependent on the
interaction location within the larger array. Otherwise, a correction is needed for the non-
uniformity. The response uniformity will be evaluated using the beamline mentioned above, and
its profile will be understood in collaboration with the beamline scientist, in case some
normalization is necessary. A non-uniformity of less than 2% is considered to be acceptable.

Task 2: Design of a multi-channel readout scheme based on ASICs
 To meet the count rate requirement (i.e., 10 MHz/cm2), each scintillator pixel needs to be
readout by a single SiPM and the signal from individual SiPMs should be independently processed.
This means there are hundreds of readout channels for each 50 mm × 50 mm detector module. It
would not be cost-effective to use waveform digitizers. Instead, ASIC-based readout electronics
could provide a viable solution. Specifically, the Citiroc ASIC originally designed by the high
energy physics community and recently commercialized by CAEN will be used. Citiroc is a 32-
channel ASIC readout specialized for SiPMs and SiPM arrays. It allows triggering at a level down
to one third of the pulse amplitude produced by single photoelectrons. Charge and timing
information can be provided with a high accuracy (e.g., timing uncertainty of less than 100 ps). It
can also adjust the bias supply for individual SiPMs to correct for the non-uniform response of the
SiPMs due to the variation in breakdown voltages. More importantly, it shows an excellent
counting capability, up to ~ 20 Mcps per channel. In this work, a multi-channel readout scheme
will be designed based on the Citiroc ASICs. To facilitate the design process, we will use the FERS
front-end readout system platform from CAEN [12]. Customized triggering and pulse processing
algorithms will be implemented on the platform using the SCI-Compiler tool specially designed
for programming FPGAs without the need for use of VHDL or Verilog. If this does not work, then
VHDL/Verilog will be used instead. Software that leverages the existing software framework at

 20

SNS and addresses data management complexity for high rate instruments will be developed as
well to control the data acquisition and the data display for this multi-channel readout system. The
developed system (including hardware and software) will be thoroughly evaluated in many
aspects, including but are not limited to triggering, counting rate, PSD, PHD, charge measurement
accuracy, gamma-ray rejection capability, time resolution, interaction position estimation, and bias
supply adjustment.

Task 3: Signal processing algorithm development for high-rate application
 Although the scintillator decay time of the LiCAF scintillator is only about 40 ns, it is still
possible to observe pulse pile-up due to the extremely high neutron flux as well as the rather
intensive prompt gamma-ray flash. Pulse pile-up affects the accuracy of the measurement results
based on conventional pulse processing algorithms, including but not limited to pulse counting,
energy estimation, neutron-gamma discrimination, gamma-ray rejection capability, interaction
position estimation, and timing performance. For example, an event could potentially be
misclassified as a single neutron event when two gamma-ray events occur close enough in time.
Furthermore, the count rates expected at different locations in the reflectometer could be expected
to show a large variation in distribution. This means different scintillator pixels could observe
significantly different pulse pile-up conditions. If the pulse pile-up issue were not properly
addressed, the 2-D neutron counting map may not be reliable, leading to systematic error in the
reflectivity curve and, therefore, an incorrect neutron scattering length density.

 In this work, advanced signal processing algorithms will be developed in software and hardware
to obtain accurate information about the particle type, timing, and energy for each detected
neutron/gamma-ray event at count rates up to 10 MHz/cm2. The developed algorithms will be able
to improve the gamma-ray rejection performance and increase the signal-to-noise ratio in
particular for the neutron reflectivity measurements at large Q values. First, template-matching
and Kalman filter-based approaches will be investigated while we perform a thorough literature
review regarding the other promising algorithms described below. In our previous work, the
template-matching based algorithm demonstrated an exceptional capability to perform high-
resolution, high-throughput gamma-ray spectroscopic measurements at high input count rates,
extending up to 500 kHz [57]. Second, adaptive filtering-based algorithms will be explored. We
will develop test algorithms first in software while considering which of them can be efficiently
implemented for high rate use in our hardware. Third, we will consider whether other supervised
machine learning algorithms are appropriate, unless one of the algorithms already developed holds
high promise for hardware implementation. To evaluate the developed algorithms, high-rate data
will be collected using the mCi gamma-ray source as well as at SNS where data can be taken that
includes the intensive prompt gamma flash. If we decide to use a supervised learning approach,
the open shutter vs. closed shutter SNS beamline measurements are expected to be especially
helpful, since, in the latter setup, mainly the prompt flash is observed without any neutron fluence.
For the final developed algorithm(s), the improvement of the SNS neutron reflectivity
measurement results by the signal processing algorithms will be quantified at low and high count
rates. As already mentioned, the class of algorithms that are investigated and developed in the
proposed work is expected to have broader impact for improved high rate performance of neutron
instruments other than reflectometers: small angle neutron scattering, spectroscopy, diffraction,
and imaging.

5. RESULTS, ACCOMPLISHMENTS, OUTCOMES

The project results are summarized in three peer-reviewed publications cited in Section 6 on
products. Since there is a full discussion of the results there, the work will only be briefly reviewed
here.

In early work, we collaborated with ORNL on a high-rate 6 Li-based pixelated neutron
detector developed for neutron reflectometry instruments at the Spallation Neutron
Source (SNS). The neutron detector has a pixelated design: each 6 Li scintillator
element has its own photosensor and independent channel readout. Our associated
journal paper focuses on the general overview of the detector design and
construction, the characterization of the pixelated detector, and the results of the
first neutron reflectivity experiments conducted using the pixelated neutron detector
at the SNS Liquid Reflectometer (BL-4B). The pixelated neutron detector
demonstrated a global time-average count rate of ≥ 1.8 × 106 cps, at least 3 orders
of magnitude higher than that of the existing neutron detector (3 He-based Multi-Wire
Proportional Counter), and a local instantaneous count rate of ≥ 1.73 × 106 cps⁄cm2 . The
maximum counting rate of the detector has not yet been determined as the detector is
capable of handling the maximum flux available at the beamline. The outcome of
the neutron reflectivity experiments showed that the pixelated neutron detector is a
promising candidate for next-generation neutron reflectometry instruments at the
SNS.
In our following work, a prototype neutron detector module based upon a pixelated
scintillator array readout by silicon photomultipliers was investigated. Two types of
scintillator materials were considered for this detector development, i.e., 6Li-loaded EJ-270
plastic scintillator and Ce-doped LiCAF single crystal. Our associated journal paper reports the
scintillator characterization results, including light yield, pulse shape discrimination
performance, capability to detect thermal neutrons in a high γ-ray field, and γ-ray sensitivity.
The number of photons produced per neutron capture by EJ-270 and LiCAF:Ce was measured
to be 2176 ± 91 and 2651 ± 108, respectively. EJ-270 demonstrated a good capability to
discriminate between neutrons and γ-rays by employing the commonly used charge comparison
method (figure-of-merit: 1.13 ± 0.01 for an energy cut of 292 - 426 keVee) and a reasonable
performance when using the time-over-threshold techniques; however, no discrimination was
observed from LiCAF:Ce regardless of the pulse shape discrimination approaches utilized,
making pulse height discrimination necessary for LiCAF:Ce to differentiate between neutrons
and γ-rays. Both EJ-270 and LiCAF:Ce exhibited an acceptable capacity to detect thermal
neutrons at high exposure rates up to approximately 584 mR/hr. The γ-ray sensitivities
measured with a 60Co source at an exposure rate of around 1145 mR/hr were determined to be
(6.11 ± 0.87) × 10-6 and (7.64 ± 1.08).

In our final work, a high rate and high spatial resolution neutron Anger camera was successfully
developed based on a Ce-doped microcolumnar LiI scintillator film and the PETsys
TOFPET2 ASIC readout electronics. The performance evaluation was conducted in a
standard laboratory environment as well as the High Flux Isotope Reactor at Oak Ridge National
Laboratory with regard to light yield, γ-ray sensitivity, neutron detection efficiency, count rate

 23

3 capability, spatial resolution, and position linearity. The LiI:Ce scintillator was found to
0 have a higher light yield and a lower γ-ray sensitivity than GS20 (i.e., 11,729 vs 6,000 photons/n
 and 1.67 × 10-5 vs on the order of 10-4). The neutron detection efficiency was measured to be 63%
 at 4.2 Å. This camera demonstrated an exceptional count rate capability, up to at least 481 kHz
 without rate loss. The spatial resolution was as good as 0.47 mm. The position linearity was also
 enhanced.

8. IMPACT AND SIGNIFICANCE

What is the impact on the development of the principal discipline(s) of the project?
The work could have a profound impact on future neutron reflectometry instruments at the SNS
STS. The improved capability of the developed detector in background rejection will extend the
available experimental dynamic Q range, allowing our instrument to measure weak reflectance
phenomena, thereby having significant impact on the achievable neutron science. This work would
also have an impact on neutron instruments other than reflectometers that can benefit from high
rate, low gamma sensitivity counting: small angle neutron scattering, spectroscopy, diffraction,
and imaging.

A PhD student, a MS student, and a research scientist were trained under this project.

full brightness, especially for surface studies of materials. Therefore, this project is likely to show
an impact on the daily life of the public.

11. CONCLUSIONS

Overall Findings
This project successfully conducted a systematic, multi-year investigation into advanced
scintillator-based neutron detector technologies for high-rate neutron reflectometry at the SNS
STS. Three prototype generations were developed and characterized, each representing a distinct
detector architecture: (1) a ⁶Li-based pixelated detector with individual SiPM readout
demonstrated at an operational SNS reflectometer, (2) a comparative characterization of EJ-270
and LiCAF:Ce scintillators under high gamma-ray backgrounds, and (3) a Ce-doped
microcolumnar LiI-based Anger camera readout by TOFPET2 ASICs and evaluated at HFIR.
Across these three efforts, the project generated three peer-reviewed publications documenting the
methods, measurements, and results in detail.
The overarching finding is that pixelated scintillator architectures with individual-channel SiPM
readout and modern ASIC-based electronics represent a technically credible and experimentally
validated path to meeting the neutron detector requirements of the SNS STS reflectometers. No
single prototype fully met every STS performance target simultaneously within the project period;
however, each prototype advanced specific capabilities significantly beyond the current state-of-
the-art, and the cumulative body of results provides a clear roadmap for subsequent detector
development.

Degree to Which Objectives Were Met
The project's original objectives were largely achieved, with some scope adjustment driven by
practical constraints encountered during execution:
 • Count rate capability: Substantially met. The first-generation pixelated detector
 demonstrated a local instantaneous count rate of ≥1.73×10⁶ cps/cm² and a global rate of
 ≥1.8×10⁶ cps, representing the highest performance documented at an SNS reflectometer.
 The detector's rate ceiling was not determined, as it exceeded available beamline flux.
 The third-generation Anger camera sustained ≥481 kHz without rate loss. Full
 verification at 10 MHz/cm² awaits access to the STS or a comparable high-flux test
 facility.
 • Gamma-ray sensitivity: Partially met. LiI:Ce demonstrated a gamma sensitivity of
 1.67×10⁻⁵, roughly an order of magnitude below GS20 but above the <10⁻⁷ target.
 Achieving sub-10⁻⁷ sensitivity will require further optimization of pulse height and pulse
 shape discrimination algorithms, ideally in a high-energy gamma environment
 representative of the SNS prompt flash. We were not able to test the gamma-ray
 sensitivity of other detectors in the SNS environment.
 • Detection efficiency: Met. The third-generation detector achieved 63% neutron detection
 efficiency at 4.2 Å, meeting the ≥60% STS requirement, and projections for optimized
 configurations suggest >80% is achievable.
 • Spatial resolution: Exceeded. The Anger camera achieved 0.47 mm resolution,
 significantly better than the 1–2 mm specification.
 • Time resolution: Met in principle. The fast scintillation components of the investigated
 materials are consistent with ~1 ns timing resolution; full characterization in a beamline
 time-of-flight context was not completed within the project period.

 29

 • Electronics development: Modified scope. The team utilized existing ASIC platforms
 (TOFPET2 from PETsys and CAEN FERS with Citiroc) rather than developing bespoke
 electronics, a pragmatic decision that allowed research bandwidth to be directed toward
 scintillator physics and detector characterization. This remains an area for future
 development.
 • Beamtime at SNS for later prototypes: Not achieved. SNS reflectometer beamtime was
 requested but not granted for the second and third prototype generations; characterization
 was conducted at HFIR CG-1a instead, which, while capable, operates at lower flux than
 SNS reflectometry beamlines.

Scientific Legacy of the Project
The scientific legacy of this project is threefold. First, it establishes a documented, quantitative
performance baseline for three distinct scintillator-based detector architectures—⁶Li pixelated, EJ-
270/LiCAF:Ce comparative, and LiI:Ce Anger camera—evaluated under well-controlled
conditions and, in the case of the first prototype, at an operational spallation neutron source
reflectometer. These reference data sets will inform detector selection and design for STS and ESS
instruments for years to come.
Second, the project introduces the Ce-doped microcolumnar LiI scintillator as a promising
candidate for neutron reflectometry detector applications. Its light yield of approximately 11,700
photons per neutron capture—nearly double that of GS20—combined with a gamma sensitivity
already an order of magnitude below GS20 and demonstrated sub-millimeter spatial resolution
positions it as a compelling material for further development. Prior to this project, LiI:Ce had not
been systematically characterized for this class of application.
Third, the project validates the ASIC-based readout paradigm for pixelated neutron detectors. The
use of the TOFPET2 system demonstrated that commercial ASIC electronics developed originally
for positron emission tomography can be successfully adapted to neutron detection, enabling high
channel-count, high-rate readout at a fraction of the cost and complexity of custom electronics.
This cross-disciplinary technology transfer constitutes a meaningful contribution to the broader
neutron detector community.
The three peer-reviewed publications in Nuclear Instruments and Methods A and IEEE
Transactions on Nuclear Science provide permanent, citable records of the project's experimental
findings, ensuring that the results remain accessible to and buildable upon by the international
neutron scattering and detector development communities.

Recommendations for Future Work
Based on the results and lessons learned from this project, the following areas are recommended
for priority attention in follow-on research:
 • Priority 1. High-flux beamline validation at SNS or STS:
The most critical next step is characterization of the best-performing prototype—the LiI:Ce Anger
camera or its successor—at an SNS reflectometry beamline (LIQREF or MAGREF) or, when
available, an STS beamline. This will allow direct measurement of count rate saturation,
verification of gamma-ray sensitivity in the presence of the SNS prompt flash, and head-to-head
comparison with deployed detector systems under identical experimental conditions.

 30

 • Priority 2. Gamma-ray rejection algorithm development:
Achieving the <10⁻⁷ gamma sensitivity required for STS reflectometers will require dedicated
algorithm development combining pulse height discrimination, pulse shape analysis, and
potentially machine learning-based classifiers optimized for the high-energy gamma spectrum
characteristic of spallation sources. Measurements with a closed SNS beamline shutter—providing
a pure prompt flash dataset—would provide an invaluable training and validation dataset for such
algorithms.
 • Priority 3. Scale-up to 50 mm × 50 mm module and tiling demonstration:
The prototypes developed in this project were proof-of-concept scale. Demonstrating that the
LiI:Ce or LiCAF:Ce architecture can be reliably fabricated and characterized at the full 50 mm ×
50 mm module size, and that multiple modules can be tiled without significant dead-region
penalty, is a necessary step toward instrument-scale deployment.
 • Priority 4. Custom ASIC readout electronics:
While the TOFPET2 and Citiroc ASIC platforms provided a viable path for this project, a purpose-
designed ASIC for neutron detector readout—optimized for simultaneous PSD, PHD, position
reconstruction, and high-rate operation—could provide significant performance advantages. A
joint effort with the electronics community, possibly through a collaboration with CERN or a
national laboratory electronics group, is recommended.
 • Priority 5. Broader instrument applicability:
The detector concepts validated in this project—pixelated scintillator arrays with individual SiPM
readout and ASIC electronics—should be evaluated for applicability to other high-flux neutron
instruments at the STS and perhaps ESS, including small-angle neutron scattering, diffraction, and
neutron imaging instruments, where the same combination of high rate, low gamma sensitivity,
and high spatial resolution is increasingly required.
