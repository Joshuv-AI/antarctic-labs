Encapsulated cell therapy for local anti-CTLA-4 delivery in cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Adrien Engel, Remi Vernet, Emily Charrier, Muriel Urwyler, Olivier Von Rohr, Valentin Saingier, Fabien Courtout, Sarah Abukscem, Thomas Bouvarel, Aurelien Lathuiliere, Davy Guillarme, Nicolas Mach, Julien Grogg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryImmune checkpoint inhibitors such as anti-PD-1 and anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) antibodies have transformed cancer therapy, but their clinical use is often limited by severe systemic toxicities, particularly with anti-CTLA-4. To address this, we developed Myo-P3, a retrievable hollow-fiber encapsulated cell therapy device containing genetically engineered human myoblasts that secrete low doses of anti-CTLA-4 directly into the tumor microenvironment. In a murine colorectal cancer model, peritumoral implantation of Myo-P3 significantly delayed tumor progression and improved survival versus untreated controls. Treatment induced local immune modulation, with early reductions in intratumoral FoxP3+ regulatory T cells and hCTLA-4+ CD4+ T cells, consistent with CTLA-4 blockade, while maintaining substantially lower systemic antibody levels than systemic ipilimumab. Although this model does not permit comprehensive assessment of long-term secretion or toxicity, these data support the feasibility and biological activity of localized anti-CTLA-4 delivery and highlight a controllable, retrievable platform to reduce systemic exposure.Keywords: delivery device, cell encapsulation, retrievable, CTLA-4 blockade, intratumoral, peritumoral

Introduction

Targeted and sustained delivery of therapeutic proteins directly to the tumor site is a key priority in the development of innovative oncology treatments.1 Indeed, the systemic delivery of immunogenic drugs, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors, results in their widespread distribution to healthy tissues, often causing significant toxicity.2 Various approaches, including antibody-drug conjugates targeting cancer cells, bispecific antibodies, gene therapy, local injections of the therapeutic protein, and encapsulated cell technology (ECT), have been developed to minimize and reduce the frequency and severity of side effects.3,4 Among these, ECT offers distinct advantages, including continuous in situ synthesis of therapeutic proteins and elimination of the need for repeated drug administrations.5,6 By locally delivering the active protein to the tumor, it may achieve efficacy while potentially reducing systemic exposure and associated toxicities.

The blockade of cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) by antibodies has become a widely utilized therapeutic strategy in combination with other immune checkpoint inhibitors in cancer treatment. Systemic administration of CTLA-4 blocking antibodies (ipilimumab) effectively restores endogenous antitumor immunity and has demonstrated durable clinical benefits, particularly in patients with melanoma.7,8,9,10 The combination of anti-PD1 and anti-CTLA-4 is known to synergize to induce anti-tumor activity.11 The monoclonal antibody works by blocking the CTLA-4 receptor on T cells, which normally downregulates immune responses. This inhibition enhances T cell activation and proliferation, allowing the immune system to increase the response against cancer cells.12 Additionally, in some preclinical models, it depletes regulatory T cells (Tregs) in the tumor microenvironment.13 However, Treg (defined by their expression of the transcription factor FOXP3) depletion has not been consistently observed in humans, even though the disruption of self-tolerance is pronounced.14 Systemic delivery of anti-CTLA-4 alone or in combination with anti-PD-1 is associated with a high risk of severe immune-related adverse events (IRAEs), such as dermatitis, colitis, pneumonitis, hepatitis, and endocrine disorders (pituitary, thyroid, adrenals). These toxicities limit the use of systemic CTLA-4 blockade for neoadjuvant, adjuvant therapy in early-stage cancers and pose a challenge to expanding its application across a broader range of cancers.15,16,17,18

Recent strategies to mitigate systemic toxicities have focused on localizing checkpoint inhibitors such as anti-PD-119 or anti-CTLA-4 directly to the tumor microenvironment in murine models20,21 and also in the clinic.22,23,24 Local biomaterial-based systems have also been developed to achieve sustained intratumoral immunotherapy and control post-surgical tumor recurrence.25,26 The direct intratumoral injection of anti-CTLA-4 achieves high local antibody concentrations while minimizing systemic exposure, thereby reducing the risk of treatment-related side effects.27 However, these frequently repeated intratumoral injections are required to maintain therapeutic exposure in order to maintain an acceptable local level of anti-CTLA-4 antibody at the tumor site. The local and sustained delivery of a protein of interest to the tumor can be achieved with ECT. Several encapsulation strategies have been developed, including micro- and macroencapsulation systems, each with distinct properties.5 Macroencapsulation devices have been shown to support long-term survival of implanted allogeneic or xenogeneic cells in vivo while allowing local diffusion of therapeutic proteins.28,29 It offers other significant advantages, such as preventing repetitive administrations of the protein. Moreover, it enables in situ synthesis of therapeutic proteins by encapsulated cells. Implantation of the macrocapsules is performed in a clinical setting under local anesthesia through a minimally invasive and painless procedure that can be completed within a few mins, while in mice implantation is straightforward, typically requiring less than 1 min, with postoperative analgesia provided using paracetamol.

To overcome the challenges of systemic toxicities and limited sustained delivery, our group has developed a retrievable ECT capable of delivering therapeutic proteins continuously. Our hollow fiber device is cylindrical in shape. In the two versions of the design that were previously described, the size-selective permeability of the membrane allows a bidirectional molecular exchange between the inner and outer environments.30,31 The encapsulation device (Myo-P) not only enables prolonged delivery but also offers the advantage of being fully retrievable, allowing for reversal of the therapy in cases of loss of function or adverse events, thereby significantly improving safety. We engineered an immortalized human myoblast cell line (IHM) (MVX-3) to secrete low doses of a human IgG1 monoclonal antibody targeting the human version of CTLA-4.32 These cells are encapsulated within the Myo-P device, which protects them from direct contact with the host immune system while enabling continuous antibody secretion in the vicinity of the tumor.

In this study, we present feasibility and efficacy data for the Myo-P3 device (encapsulated MVX-3 cells) administered peritumorally in a colon adenocarcinoma mouse model. We also evaluate its impact on immune cell populations within the tumor. We report the effects of the local secretion peritumorally of anti-CTLA-4 on tumor growth, survival, and immune cell modulation, including the proportions of Tregs within the tumor. We also describe systemic antibody levels and the occurrence of treatment-related toxicities in comparison with systemic therapy.

These results illustrate the potential of ECT to enable localized delivery of immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as anti-CTLA-4, while limiting systemic exposure, to address key limitations of recombinant protein delivery for cancer treatments.

Results

Development of the MVX-3: anti hCTLA-4 secreting IHM cell line that can be encapsulated

The local and constant secretion of a protein of interest to the site of interest can be achieved with ECT. The localized delivery of an immunogenic protein into the tumor can induce an effective antitumor immune response while minimizing the risk of the drug reaching healthy tissues.33,34 As previously described,30 the Myo-P device (Figure 1A) is made of a hollow fiber membrane with large membrane pores that allow delivery of large therapeutic molecules such as antibodies. In the same time, it protects the encapsulated cells from the immune system of the host. The Myo-P device (singlepod) is scalable, allowing multiple units to be connected to increase secretion capacity. A larger version, the quadripod (Myo-P(x4)), consists of four Myo-P devices joined at their extremities (Figure S4). We previously characterized an IHM that was genetically modified to secrete diverse functional molecules, including anti-CTLA-4: ipilimumab. The IHM was transduced with 2 different lentiviral vectors encoding the heavy or light chains of anti-human CTLA-4 under the control of the human PGK promoter (scheme in Figure 1B). Single cell cloning was performed, and anti-human anti-CTLA-4 secretion in medium was assessed by human IgG ELISA after 24 h release (Figure 1C). Due to its higher secretion level of human IgG (higher than 7 pg/cell/day) and its expression of myoblast markers (CD146, CD82, CD56) and phenotype, clone 1 was selected. The copy number of the light and heavy chains of anti-human anti-CTLA-4 was determined using ddPCR assays. The results showed that clone 1 of MVX-3 had 7.1 ± 0.7 copies of the heavy chain and 5.6 ± 0.06 copies of the light chain (Figure 1D). The anti-human anti-CTLA-4 antibody biological activity was assessed by blockade activity based on the interaction between samples or control samples with CTLA-4, which would block the interaction between CTLA-4 and CD80/86. This activity was compared with a Yervoy biosimilar antibody. Myoblast-secreted anti-CTLA-4 in culture supernatant displayed significant blockade activity against the interaction between CTLA-4 and CD80/86 (Figure 1E). This activity of the secreted anti-human anti-CTLA-4 from clone 1 was assessed and compared to the Yervoy antibody (Figure 1E). The EC50 of the release of MVX-3 cells (0.223 μg/mL) appeared 10 times lower compared to Yervoy (2.99 μg/mL), confirming normal activity for the IgG secreted by the modified cells and a higher potency. Finally, chromatography analysis revealed that the purity of the secretion of encapsulated anti-human anti-CTLA-4 from Myo-P3 reached more than 99% of intact anti-CTLA-4 (Figure 1F).

Efficacy of Myo-P3

We evaluate the local administration of a low dose of anti-human CTLA-4 antibody with Myo-P3 and its impact on cancer progression and the antitumor T cell response in a colon cancer mouse model. Human CTLA-4 knock-in (hCTLA-4 KI) mice, which express the human form of the CTLA-4 receptor were implanted with the MC38 colorectal tumor model (1 × 106 cells, subcutaneously in the dorsal flank) to evaluate the efficacy of sustained, localized delivery of anti-human CTLA-4 near the tumor. Tumor growth, skin fur, and weight were monitored for up to 72 days.

After nine days post-tumor implantation (at day 0), when tumors were measurable, mice were randomized into four groups: (1) peritumoral implantation of encapsulated Myo-P3 cells, (2) peritumoral implantation of encapsulated untransduced human myoblasts (Myo-PWT, serving as a negative control), (3) intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of ipilimumab (10 mg/kg, bi-weekly doses; ipi IP group), and (4) an untreated control group (Figure 2A). The injection of ipilimumab at 10 mg/kg, bi-weekly, is widely used in both clinical practice and preclinical murine models.16,35 Mice were sacrificed upon tumor size reaching the size limit (80–150 mm3) or for ethical reasons. Tumor growth analysis revealed that both the Myo-P3 and ipi IP groups exhibited a statistically significant delay in tumor progression compared to the untreated control group (Figure 2B). No significant differences were observed between the Myo-PWT and the three other groups (Myo-P3, Ipi IP, and untreated). Long-term survival analysis (Figure 2C) demonstrated a significant survival benefit for mice treated with Myo-P3 or ipi IP compared to untreated controls, with tumor rejection rates of 8/10 for ipi IP and 2/8 for Myo-P3. Notably, a trend toward improved survival was observed for Myo-P3 compared to Myo-PWT (p = 0.064). No significant differences were observed between the Myo-P3 and the ipi IP group. The mice that survived from the ipi IP and Myo-P3 groups were rechallenged with 1 × 106 MC38 tumor cells in the dorsal flank, and tumor growth was monitored for up to 30 days. In all the mice, no tumor growth was observed during the entire period (2/2 for the Myo-P3 group and 8/8 for the ipi IP group).

Toxicity

IRAEs were carefully monitored throughout the study, with a particular focus on the skin and fur of the mice (Figure 2D). Signs of immune-mediated cutaneous toxicity (alopecia) were observed in 42% of the mice in the ipi IP group, primarily affecting the neck, dorsal region, nose, and head. Notably, none of the mice in the Myo-P3, Myo-PWT, or untreated control groups exhibited any signs. Moreover, 2 mice from the ipi Ip group died a few days after the injection, probably due to the toxicity of the injection.13 Translating mouse toxicity data from checkpoint blockade studies is difficult; these results rather suggest that reducing systemic antibody exposure could help lower toxicity. Earlier studies have reported that anti-CTLA-4-mediated anticancer activity was associated with elevated levels of intratumoral effector T cells and a reduction in Tregs.13 Therefore, in tumors collected seven days after the intervention (implantation or injections), lymphocyte populations could be observed (Figure 2E). The proportion of Tregs (CD4+FoxP3+) was significantly lower in the Myo-P3 and ipi IP groups compared to both the untreated and Myo-PWT groups (Figure 2F). Similarly, the proportion of hCTLA-4-positive cells among CD4+ T cells was significantly reduced in the Myo-P3 and ipi IP groups, suggesting effective depletion of Tregs by both local and systemic delivery of anti-CTLA-4. Furthermore, the ipi IP group exhibited the highest proportion of CD8+ T cells among CD3+ T cells, surpassing the levels observed in the untreated and Myo-PWT groups. The Myo-P3 group also showed an increased proportion of CD8+ T cells compared to Myo-PWT, although the difference was less pronounced.

Building on earlier studies that reported elevated Treg levels in patients treated systemically with CTLA-4 blocking antibodies36 as well as in experimental models.37 We compared the Treg levels in the lymph nodes between all the groups. Seven days after treatment, Tregs were significantly elevated in the tumor-draining lymph node (TDLN) and spleen of systemically treated mice (Figure S5). In contrast, the injection of Myo-P3 did not significantly alter Treg levels compared to the untreated group.

Dose escalation

A dose-escalation study was conducted to evaluate the long-term secretion of anti-CTLA-4 and its effects on tumor progression. Mice were subcutaneously engrafted with 1 × 106 MC38 tumor cells in the dorsal flank, and tumor growth was monitored for up to 104 days. On day 13, mice were randomized into five groups: (1) peritumoral implantation of Myo-P3(x4) quadripods, (2) peritumoral implantation of Myo-PWT(x4) quadripods as a negative control, (3) bi-weekly intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of ipilimumab (10 mg/kg; ipi IP group), (4) an untreated control group, and (5) a local injection of 8 μg of ipilimumab near the tumor.

Tumor growth analysis (Figure 3A) showed a significant delay in tumor progression in the Myo-P3(x4) group compared to the untreated control group. Long-term survival analysis (Figure 3B) demonstrated a significant survival benefit for mice treated with Myo-P3(x4) or ipi IP compared to untreated controls. Notably, 80% of mice in the ipi IP group survived until the end of the study, outperforming all other groups (Myo-P3(x4), ipi SC, Myo-PWT(x4)). No significant difference in survival was observed between the Myo-PWT(x4) and Myo-P3(x4) groups. Toxicity assessments revealed signs of immune-mediated cutaneous toxicity in 50% of the mice in the ipi IP group and in 30% of the mice in the ipi SC group, while none of the mice in the other groups exhibited signs of alopecia. Lymphocyte populations in the tumors were assessed 8- and 14-day post-intervention (Figures 3C and 3D). Eight days after intervention, the proportion of Tregs (FoxP3+ cells among CD4+ cells) was significantly lower in the Myo-P3(x4) and ipi IP groups compared to the untreated control and Myo-PWT(x4) groups. Similarly, the proportion of hCTLA-4+ cells among CD4+ cells was significantly reduced in the Myo-P3(x4) and ipi IP groups compared to the Myo-PWT(x4) group. However, no differences in the CD8+ cell populations were observed among the groups (Figure 3D). Interestingly, the proportion of CD25+FoxP3+ was approximately 50% lower in the Myo-P3(x4) group compared to the ipi IP group (Figures 3E and 3F). Unexpectedly, by day 14 post-intervention, no significant differences were observed across all groups in the proportions of Tregs, hCTLA-4+ cells, or CD8+ cells (Figure S4). Similarly to the previous experiment, immune-mediated cutaneous toxicity was seen in 50% of the ipi IP group and 30% of the ipi SC group. No cases were observed in the Myo-P3(x4), Myo-PWT(x4), or untreated control groups. Additionally, histopathological evaluation of the liver was conducted 14 days post-intervention to assess the potential toxicity of pro-immunogenic drugs in hCTLA-4 mice. The inflammation scores revealed a higher tendency for immune cell infiltration in the ipi IP group compared to the Myo-P3(x4) group (3/5 vs. 1/5) (Figures 3H and 3I).

Pharmacokinetics

The long-term secretion of anti-human anti-CTLA-4 by encapsulated clone 1 was assessed to verify the stability of the secretion of the antibody. After encapsulation of clone 1 in the MyoPod device, the system could secrete anti-human anti-CTLA-4 for at least 9 months in cell culture conditions ranging from 4000 to 1200 ng/capsule/day. (Figure 4A). Even the Myo-P3(x4) were easy to implant in vivo (Figure 4B). Capsules implanted next to the tumor were well tolerated (Figure 4C). We monitored the concentration of hIgG in the plasma from the experiments shown in Figures 2 and 3 and the secreting capabilities of Myo-P3 over time in our preclinical mouse model. Before implantation, Myo-P3 was able to secrete around 2130.0 ng of human IgG ±202/capsule/day (Figure 4D). After one week of implantation s.c in vivo, capsule secretion was similar to before implantation, with 1894 ± 433 ng/capsule/day. Expectingly, the secretion of anti-hCTLA-4 of quadripods was higher than single pods (x3.34), 7160.3 ± 759 ng/capsule/day before implantation; after explantation the quadripods showed a lower secretion capability, 4900.0 ± 847 ng/capsule/day. In order to know whether local treatment with a low dose of anti-human anti-CTLA-4 antibody is detected in a systemic manner, the serum level of anti-human anti-CTLA-4 was assessed at day 7 post-capsule implantation. As expected, the level of anti-human anti-CTLA-4 in the MVX-3 capsule group is 29 times lower than in the group receiving 2 doses of ipilimumab i.p (Figure 4E). The viability of the encapsulated cells was assessed by H&E and EVG staining (Figure 4F). Histological examination of the capsules before implantation shows densely packed, well-organized, and morphologically intact cells, indicating high viability. After 7 days implanted in vivo, the capsules display reduced cell density, acellular regions, and morphological alterations, reflecting a loss of viability due to suboptimal environmental conditions. Nevertheless, even after more than 30 days implanted, a fraction of cells remains present and functional, consistent with the continued antibody secretion observed post-explantation.

Tolerability

To assess whether Myo-P3 implantation elicits an immune response in immunocompetent mice, Myo-P3 (x1 or x4) was implanted subcutaneously into C57BL/6 mice. Fifteen days later, serum concentrations of murine IgG (mIgG) and IgM (mIgM) were quantified (Figure 4G). No significant difference in mIgG levels was observed between either Myo-P3 group and non-implanted controls. In contrast, mIgM measurements in mice bearing Myo-P3 devices (161 μg/mL for Myo-P3(x1) and 131 μg/mL for Myo-P3(x4)) were higher than those in non-implanted controls (43.6 μg/mL), although this difference did not reach statistical significance.

Discussion

The use of genetically engineered encapsulated cells for the delivery of immune checkpoint inhibitors represents a promising alternative to both repeated local injections and systemic administrations, potentially enhancing the therapeutic index in cancer treatment. In our preclinical colorectal cancer model, we have demonstrated that the localized, sustained release of low-dose anti-CTLA-4 antibodies via ECT is both feasible and associated with anti-tumor activity. We have previously shown the feasibility of ECT in humans. The first generation of the device has been previously tested in the clinic for the delivery of human GM-CSF in the context of a cancer vaccine, where no safety signals and feasibility issues were encountered.38 Moreover, the Myo-P device has been evaluated in a clinical trial (NCT05071846) utilizing an immortalized myoblast cell line engineered to secrete GM-CSF as part of a cancer vaccine for solid tumors.30,31,37 MyoP3 may reduce some of the strongest systemic toxicity associated with conventional anti-CTLA-4 therapies while showing preliminary indications of antitumor activity. In our experimental setting, the device also showed evidence of biocompatibility and good tolerability. Furthermore, the platform is designed to be scalable and can be administered, retrieved, or replaced through minimally invasive procedures.

EC50 of secretion of MVX-3

In this study, we found that Myo-P3 can secrete a high quantity of anti-CTLA-4 in vitro for at least 9 months (Figure 4). Furthermore, we demonstrated that the anti-CTLA-4 antibody secreted by these cells exhibits an EC50 that is 13 times lower than that of commercial ipilimumab (Figure 1). This difference may be partially attributed to the origin of the cells used for antibody production: while commercial ipilimumab is produced in Chinese hamster ovary cells (CHO), the anti-CTLA-4 secreted by Myo-P3 is produced in IHM cells of human origin. Both CHO and human cells are capable of correctly folding and assembling antibodies; however, subtle differences in folding and post-translational modifications, such as glycosylation, could lead to changes in binding performance. Unlike CHO cells, which produce non-human glycan patterns, our human myoblast cell line produces human-like glycosylation, which may impact the biological activity of the antibody.39 Other modifications such as oxidation, deamidation, or isomerization could also alter the antibody’s tertiary structure and binding interface. These modifications can also affect the affinity or kinetics of CTLA-4 binding, thereby shifting the EC50. Further studies are required to determine if the difference in EC50 exclusively stems from differences in post-translational modifications between the Myo-P3 and CHO-produced antibodies.

Viability of encapsulated cells

When implanted in vivo, a decrease in secretion capacity was observed, despite the implant still releasing significant amounts of anti-CTLA-4 in mice after explantation (Figure 4). Specifically, a reduction in secretion capability was noted when comparing pre-implantation levels to post-explantation levels after seven days of implantation, with a 1.5-fold decrease for Myo-P3(x4) (and no decrease for the single pod). This decline in the quadripod can be attributed to reduced cell viability observed in encapsulated cells following in vivo residence compared to pre-implantation conditions (Figure 4F). Encapsulated cells face challenges in vivo due to suboptimal conditions, including limited oxygen and nutrient availability, as well as lower temperatures compared to ideal cell culture conditions. Additionally, the host mice’s fully functional immune system, coupled with the human origin of the encapsulated cells, may trigger a xenogeneic immune response. This is supported by the observed increase in mIgM levels in implanted mice (Figure 4F). Elevated IgM suggests a mild initial foreign body reaction, potentially representing an early-phase humoral response.40 However, the absence of a sustained IgG response indicates that the encapsulation device may effectively mitigate deeper or chronic immune rejection. These data are in line with previous work showing that well-designed encapsulation membranes can dampen the graft versus host response.29,41 In an allogenic setting, where the implanted cells are of the same species as the host, the viability of encapsulated myoblasts was shown to remain largely stable over a large period.31,32,42 This could mitigate some of the viability and secretion challenges seen in this study, paving the way for improved outcomes in future applications.

Efficacy of Myo-P3

The Myo-P3-treated group showed a modest delay in tumor progression and a trend toward improved survival compared with untreated controls. A similar tendency was also observed in the Myo-PWT group, suggesting that the presence of encapsulated cells may contribute to changes in the tumor environment even in the absence of anti-CTLA-4 secretion. Such effects could potentially arise from factors including local competition for nutrients or a localized immune response related to the xenogeneic origin of the encapsulated cells, which may induce a non-specific inflammatory reaction.43 However, the magnitude, reproducibility, and precise mechanisms underlying this observation remain unclear and warrant further investigation. Ultimately, further evaluation in a human setting will be important to better understand the potential immunological impact of this approach, as the complexity of the human immune system and tumor microenvironment cannot be fully recapitulated in preclinical models.43

The Myo-P3 device successfully delivered antibodies directly to the tumor microenvironment, achieving a reduction in tumor-infiltrating Tregs comparable to systemic administration. This mechanism is well known in the mode of action of anti-CTLA-4 in human CTLA-4 KI mice.44,45,46 It has been shown that the slow release of anti-CTLA-4 does not demonstrate efficacy when it is not administered close to the tumor.20 Showing that the local depletion of Tregs might be the main mechanism of action.

We observed a significant increase in Tregs within the TDLNs and spleens of systemically treated mice (ipi IP) compared to the Myo-P3 group. The elevation of the frequencies of extra-tumoral Tregs in mice after prolonged systemic anti-CTLA-4 antibody therapy has previously been observed in mice and humans in a dose-dependent fashion.36 The depletion of intra-tumoral Tregs is observed only one week after the intervention and not at two weeks in both systemic therapy and Myo-P3(x4). This transient, time-limited pattern of intratumoral Treg depletion following anti-CTLA-4 administration is not well characterized in the existing literature and warrants further investigation.47

Regulatory T cell modulation in the tumor microenvironment

Tregs comprise heterogeneous populations with distinct developmental origins and functional properties. In general, thymus-derived Tregs (often referred to as natural Tregs, nTregs) arise during thymic development and play a key role in maintaining systemic immune tolerance, whereas peripherally induced Tregs (iTregs) can develop from conventional CD4+ T cells in response to the local environment.48

In our study, we observed differences in the distribution of FoxP3+ CD4+ T cell populations characterized by differential CD25 expression between treatment groups. In particular, tumors from Myo-P3-treated mice displayed a lower proportion of CD25low FoxP3+ cells compared with mice receiving systemic anti-CTLA-4 administration. These observations suggest that localized delivery of anti-CTLA-4 may influence specific Treg populations within the tumor microenvironment compared to systemic administration.

However, CD25 expression alone is not sufficient to definitively distinguish thymus-derived from peripherally induced Treg populations. Additional markers such as Helios or Neuropilin-1 would be required to accurately discriminate between these subsets. Nevertheless, these findings are consistent with the concept that localized immune checkpoint blockade may differentially modulate Treg populations within the tumor microenvironment, which could contribute to antitumor immune activation while limiting systemic immune perturbation. Further studies using additional markers would be required to determine whether distinct Treg subsets are differentially affected by localized versus systemic CTLA-4 blockade.49

Safety profile

As discussed, the safety profile of anti-CTLA-4 antibodies remains a significant concern, with many patients experiencing grade 3 to 4 adverse events, including dermatitis, colitis, hypophysitis, and hepatitis. However, mice tend to be more resistant than humans to autoimmune adverse events and can better tolerate these therapies. This difference may be attributed to the shorter duration of treatment in animal models, which is typically limited to a few weeks, compared to the prolonged treatment periods in patients. Autoimmune side effects have been previously described in anti-CTLA-4-treated mice, with depigmentation and hepatocyte infiltration among the reported manifestations.50 These observations underscore the need for strategies to mitigate immune-related adverse effects while maintaining therapeutic efficacy.51 An aspect of Myo-P3 is the absence of overt toxicity signs observed in our experimental setting. Unlike systemic ipilimumab, which caused notable depigmentation and mortality in a subset of mice, Myo-P3(x4)-treated animals exhibited no visible signs of treatment-related toxicities. Immune-mediated cutaneous toxicities were previously reported for systemic checkpoint blockade.18 These local tissue reactions likely reflect an exaggerated inflammatory response, highlighting the toxic effects of high systemic antibody levels. By contrast, no comparable skin or fur changes were seen in mice implanted with Myo-P3 devices, supporting the premise that localized, low-dose delivery of anti-CTLA-4 may alleviate the autoimmune or inflammatory sequelae often associated with systemic immunotherapy. Nonetheless, additional investigation is needed to clarify whether these dermatologic changes represent a direct immune-mediated phenomenon or a secondary effect of systemic inflammation. Further work will also be required to more thoroughly characterize the safety profile of this approach, including comprehensive preclinical toxicology studies, prior to potential clinical evaluation. The mean immune cell infiltration score in the liver was lower in the Myo-P3(x4) group compared to the systemic treatment group, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. Further investigations, ideally with larger sample sizes and more detailed histopathological analyses, are required to conclusively determine whether local anti-CTLA-4 secretion offers a significant advantage over systemic administration toward liver toxicities. The mean immune cell infiltration score in the liver was lower in the Myo-P3(x4) group compared to the systemic treatment group, although this difference was not statistically significant. Given that the current murine model may not be sensitive enough to detect subtle hepatic toxicities, an issue noted in previous studies of checkpoint inhibitor-induced liver injury.17 Further investigation using more sensitive models or detailed histopathological assessments is warranted to fully elucidate the safety advantages of local anti-CTLA-4 secretion over systemic administration.46

In this study, we demonstrated that peritumoral administration of Myo-P3, a cell-based therapy designed for sustained local delivery of anti-CTLA-4 antibody, was associated with tumor growth delay and survival benefit relative to untreated controls at a fraction of the systemic dose. Mechanistically, Myo-P3 recapitulated the early on-target effects of systemic ipilimumab, with a comparable reduction of intratumoral CTLA-4+ and Treg cells after one week, yet with ∼29-fold lower systemic exposure and an absence of visible IRAEs. Taken together, these observations are consistent with the possibility that continuous low-dose local anti-CTLA-4 delivery may maintain aspects of therapeutic activity while potentially improving tolerability. These findings warrant further investigation of Myo-P3 as a modular and retrievable platform for localized immunotherapy delivery.

Combining Myo-P3 with an anti-PD-1 antibody could provide deeper insights into the advantages of this approach in terms of both safety and efficacy.46 Furthermore, encapsulated cells can co-secrete cytokines alongside antibodies.30 Co-secretion of IL-2 alongside anti-CTLA-4 could potentiate antitumor immune responses while mitigating the adverse effects commonly associated with systemic IL-2 administration. IL-2 is crucial for stimulating effector T cell proliferation, yet its systemic use is limited by the simultaneous expansion of Tregs. By restricting IL-2 secretion to the tumor microenvironment through encapsulated cells, this approach may preferentially support effector T cell activation while limiting Treg expansion, thereby amplifying antitumor immunity.52

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this work warrant consideration. First, the murine tumor model employed does not fully capture the complex biology of human tumors, particularly with respect to immune cell infiltration, vascularization, and the heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment. This limitation may affect the translatability of the efficacy and toxicity findings to clinical settings. Second, although localized delivery via the Myo-P3 device was associated in this study with lower systemic antibody exposure and fewer observable gross toxicity signs than systemic administration, the present work did not comprehensively assess the full spectrum of treatment-related toxicities. While cutaneous changes and hepatic immune cell infiltration were examined, broader systemic toxicities, including gastrointestinal, endocrine, pulmonary, and cardiac effects, were not systematically evaluated. Dedicated studies with larger cohorts, longer follow-up, and broader histopathological assessment will therefore be required to more rigorously define the safety profile of localized anti-CTLA-4 delivery. Third, the use of human myoblasts in a murine model constitutes a xenogeneic system that may trigger an immune response, as indicated by the increased IgM levels observed after implantation. Although definite immune rejection was not observed, potential long-term host responses to encapsulated cells warrant further investigation, particularly in allogeneic settings.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal studies

Female human CTLA-4 knock-in (C57BL/6 human CTLA-4 Knockin (hCTLA-4 KI) mice) mice (Mus musculus) were obtained from GenOway and used for all in vivo studies. C57BL/6 were obtained from Charles River. Mice were 12–15 weeks of age at study initiation and weighed at least 16 g. Animals were acclimatized for a minimum of 14 days prior to study initiation. Mice were housed in individually ventilated cages (IVCs) under controlled environmental conditions at Crown Bioscience UK with ad libitum access to Teklad 2919 chow and water. Animal identification was performed using implanted transponders. Animal welfare monitoring, anesthesia, analgesia, and husbandry procedures were conducted according to Crown Bioscience UK standard operating procedures.

All animal procedures were performed at Crown Bioscience UK and complied with the UK Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986 (ASPA) and Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes. Studies were conducted under Home Office Project Licenses P85D8ACF6 and PP1144128 and approved according to Crown Bioscience UK institutional animal welfare policies.

To establish tumors, mice were inoculated subcutaneously with 1 × 10ˆ6 MC38 murine colon adenocarcinoma cells suspended in 100 μL PBS. Tumors were implanted in the flank and monitored three times weekly using digital calipers. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula 0.5 × (length × width2).

Only female hCTLA-4 mice were used in this study. Female mice were selected because they are less prone to aggression and fighting when group-housed, thereby reducing stress-related variability and improving animal welfare during prolonged tumor growth and capsule implantation studies. Nevertheless, potential sex-specific differences in treatment response were not evaluated and therefore remain a limitation of the study.

Cell lines

The murine colon adenocarcinoma cell line MC38 was obtained from FDCC and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mM L-glutamine, 0.1 mM non-essential amino acids (NEAA), 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 10 mM HEPES. Cell suspensions were prepared according to Crown Bioscience UK standard operating procedures, and cell viability exceeded 90% prior to implantation. Cell line authentication was relied on supplier-provided identification.

The immortalized human myoblast cell line (IHM) was previously described and served as the parental line for generation of MVX-3. Cell identity was confirmed by supplier and internal cell bank records. Cells were routinely tested and confirmed negative for mycoplasma contamination throughout culture and expansion.31

The medium used for the culture of all myoblast cell lines (MVX3 or WT) is customized growth medium (GM) that was prepared using Ham’s F-10 Nutrient Mix supplemented with GlutaMAX (Gibco, 41550021) and enriched with 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 0.5 mg/mL bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma-Aldrich, A4503), 0.5 mg/mL fetuin (Desert Biological, 302070), 10 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (R&D Systems, 236-GMP-200), 0.39 μg/mL dexamethasone (PharmaServ, 8016), 0.04 mg/mL insulin (Sigma-Aldrich, I9278), 149 μg/mL creatine monohydrate (PharmaServ, 8114), 100 μg/mL sodium pyruvate (Gibco, 11360039), 50 μg/mL uridine (Sigma-Aldrich, U3003), and 5 μg/mL gentamicin (Gibco, 15710049).

Mycoplasma testing was conducted regularly when cells were in culture.

Method details

MVX-3 cell generation

The transduction method and the generation ipilimumab secreting human myoblast cells (MVX-3) was previously described.30 Myoblasts were transduced with lentiviral particles containing the plasmids: coding for the light chain and the other for the heavy chain both under the expression of the human PGK promoter. The sequences of the two plasmids are in the supplementary (Figure S1). Shortly, immortalized Myoblasts (WT) were cultured at a density ranging from 6 × 104 cells/mL in a 24 well plate. Then the infection was performed one day after the plating of WT myoblast. in conditioned cultured medium solution with 5 μg/mL polybrene and viral particles (one for the light chain and one for the heavy chain) were added to the myoblasts with a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 30 for 24 h. Individual clones were then allowed to grow and expand in culture to assess the secretion level by ELISA. The Copy number determination and the sequencing was done at Microsynth using ddPCR assays specific for the light and heavy chain relative to single copy reference gene B2M.

hCTLA-4 Blockade Bioassay

hCTLA-4 Blockade Bioassay was purchased from Promega (JA3001; Madison, WI, USA) and performed according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. A serial dilution of each antibody was generated in assay buffer (90% RPMI 1640/10% FBS, supplied in the kit) in a sterile 96-well plate. CTLA-4 effector cells were thawed and diluted into assay buffer and the cell suspension was added to each of the inner 60 wells of a white flat-bottom 96-well plates. Medium with the appropriate antibody dilutions or the medium containing the release of MVX-3 cells were added to the wells containing CTLA-4 effector cells. Then, artificial antigen presenting (aAPC)/Raji cells were thawed and diluted into assay buffer and added to the same wells. Plates were incubated for 12 h at 37°C 5% CO2 before adding Bio-Glo Reagent. After 15 min of incubation at room temperature, the luminescence was measured.

Cell encapsulation

Encapsulation devices (Myo-P) were manufactured as previously described (without the retrieval tube).30,31 MVX-3 cells were cultured in T-Flasks at a defined seeding density (12,000 to 18,000 cell/cm2), in a defined volume of culture medium and incubated for 3 to 4 days. Given the adherent nature of the MVX-3 cells, a trypsinization process was required to pass and count cells. Cells were counted in duplicate with an automated cell counter (Countess II device, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and resuspended in culture medium. Each Myo-P was loaded with 30 mL of the cell suspension containing 1M of cells through a loading tube (vasculon). After loading, the vasculon was cut and Myo-P capsules were sealed with UV-curable adhesive. For the Myo-P3 (x4) configuration (photo in Figure S4), four Myo-P capsules were aligned end-to-end and joined together using the same UV-curable adhesive. This assembly method created a continuous multi-compartment system while maintaining the integrity of each individual loaded Myo-P capsule. Myo-P were then placed in 12-well plates with 2 mL of culture medium and maintained in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37°C. The medium was then collected for protein quantification. Results were reported in ng/caps/day.

Efficacy Myo-P3

Murine experiments were done in compliance with the UK Animals Scientific Procedures Act 1986 (ASPA) in line with Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament and the Council of 22 September 2010 on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes. All experimental data management and reporting procedures were in strict accordance with applicable guidelines and standard operating procedures. Experiments using human CTLA-4 knock-in (HuGEMM) mice were performed at Crown Bioscience (UK). Full-length hCTLA-4 knock-in mice were previously described35 and were purchased at Genoway (France). MC38 cells were acquired from FDCC. Six to 9-week-old, female, human CTLA-4 KI mice were injected subcutaneously at the lower flank with 106 MC38 cells in 100 μL of PBS. Tumors were allowed to establish until tumor volume reached the indicated size. Tumor and body weight were measured in a blinded fashion three times a week. Tumor volumes were estimated using the formula 0.5 (LxW2) where, L as tumor length (the longest tumor dimension), and W is tumor width (the longest tumor dimension perpendicular to L). Once tumors have reached 80–150 mm3 (at day 10–13 after engraftment), Mice were then randomized, capsules (Myo-P3(x4) or WT) were implanted subcutaneously. Implantation occurred adjacent to the site of the tumor. Pre-operative and post-operative analgesia were given. During anesthesia, capsules were placed far enough away from the incision to ensure that the wound healing was not impaired. Mice were monitored for breakthrough or pressure necrosis, and migration of the capsule. Tumor cells were also implanted in naive, WT C57BL/6 mice as a positive control. Mice were sacrificed with a lethal injection of sodium pentobarbital. Capsules were dissected and retrieved from the mice and were either fixed for further histological analysis or placed in culture medium for further quantification of recombinant protein expression. Mice from the Figure 2 that experienced a complete response post randomization were re-challenged at a later time point. The re-challenge experiment consisted in the injection of MC38 tumor cells implanted subcutaneously in the opposite flank region.

Flow cytometry

The tumors were dissociated with the murine tumor dissociation kit (Miltenyi, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) and GentleMACS Octo Dissociator with Heaters set. Single cell suspensions from lymph nodes and spleen were also prepared.

For FACS analysis cells were blocked with 1 μg/mL Fc-Block (mouse Fc Block, BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) for 15 min at 4°C. Cells were then stained with surface antibodies for 30 min on ice in the dark. Cells were then washed twice and fixed with Fixation/Permeabilization working solution (eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA) and then washed twice with 1× Permeabilization buffer (eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA). Then, the intracellular staining was performed in 1× Permeabilization buffer. Following intracellular staining, cells were washed twice with 1× Permeabilization buffer and data was collected using a flow cytometer. AB used CD45, CD3, CD4, CD8, Foxp3, CD11b, F4/80, Ly6c, Ly6g, CD335, CD206, IA/IE, CD19, PD-1, PD-L1, Live/Dead. Data were analyzed using FlowJo 10. Gating strategy is available in the supplemental information.

Protein quantification

For recombinant protein release analysis, cells were cultured in a T75 cm2 culture flask. Once confluency was reached, cells were washed with HBSS and incubated in 5 mL of fresh GM for 2 h at 37°C in a standard incubator. The medium was then collected for quantification and cells were counted using an automated cell counter. Two ELISA kits were used for supernatant analysis: IgG (Total) Human ELISA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, BMS2091) and ipilimumab specific kit (Abcam). All kits were used according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Results were reported either in pg/cell/day or in ng/capsule/day.

Chromatography samples

Ultrapure water was obtained from a MilliQ purification system from Millipore (Bedford, MA, USA). Acetonitrile was UPLC-MS grade and purchased from Fisher Scientific (Reinach, Switzerland). DL-dithiothreitol (DTT, ≥98%), and trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, ≥99%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Buchs, Switzerland). Ipilimumab was obtained as a research grade ipilimumab antibody (R&D systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). A stock solution was prepared by diluting intact ipilimumab sample at 100 μg/mL directly with culture medium (without FBS). Intact ipilimumab samples (100 μL) were obtained by diluting stock solution at 1, 2, 5, 10, and 20 μg/mL with culture medium. The 100 μL samples were then then transferred to HPLC vial for the analysis without further preparation.

RPLC-FL

Measurements were performed on Acquity UPLC I-Class system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) equipped with a binary solvent delivery pump, an autosampler (15 μL flow-through-needle injector), and a fluorescence detector (FL, excitation at 280 nm, emission at 340 nm). For all measurements, a sample volume of 10 μL was injected. Data acquisition and instrument control were performed by Empower Pro 3 software (Waters). The Waters Bioresolve RP mAb polyphenyl (2.1 × 150 mm, 2.7 μm) column was employed under reversed phase liquid chromatography (RPLC) conditions. Mobile phase consisted of H2O + 0.1% TFA (A) and ACN +0.1% TFA (B). Flow rate and column temperature were set at 500 μL.min-1 and 80°C, respectively. The applied gradient started with an initial isocratic step of 1 min at 30 %B. Next, the gradient increased from 30 to 40%B in 10 min, then from 40 to 80 %B in 3 min, and remained at 80 %B for 1 min. Finally, the mobile phase composition was set back to 30 %B in 0.1 min to let the system equilibrating during 10 min. Pure ACN and a mixture of H2O/ACN (90/10, ν/ν) were used as strong and weak needle washes, respectively.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was assessed by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed Student’s t test for comparison between two groups. Survival analyses were performed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves and compared using the log rank (Mantel–Cox) test. For categorical variables, including the incidence of visible toxicity signs, overall group differences were analyzed using a chi-square test and pairwise comparisons were performed using Fisher’s exact test.

The experimental unit (n) represents individual biological replicates unless otherwise stated. Tumor measurements and body weight assessments were performed in a blinded manner.

Statistical significance between groups is presented as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001 and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001. Data analyses were performed using the software package GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software).
