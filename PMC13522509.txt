Single-crystal organic electrode for aqueous proton batteries
Authors: Na Ju, Yao Wang, Zilong Liu, Yiming Wang, Zhenyu Wang, Suyan Niu, Xin Chen, Mingjie Jia, Die Shao, Yongjian Ai, Guangwen Xu, Hong-bin Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aqueous organic batteries attract attention owing to their intrinsic safety, low cost, and low polarization. However, while most studies focus on molecular design, the influence of supramolecular assembly and crystal structure beyond the molecular scale remains somehow largely overlooked. In this work, a single crystal electrode of aqueous proton batteries, 2,3-Dichloro-1,4-naphthoquinone is investigated by the means of combined characterization techniques including nuclear magnetic resonance, single crystal X ray diffraction, operando X ray diffraction, and cryogenic transmission electron microscopy combined with theoretical calculations. The roles of molecular structure, supramolecular assembly and crystal structure in proton storage performance are elucidated. Notably, the π-donor-acceptor stacked bimolecular pairs form a reinforced 100 facet with the lowest proton adsorption energy, enabling 96.82% capacity utilization. The full cell using this material as negative electrode and lead dioxide as positive electrode delivers stable cycling performance at 5 A g−1 over a wide-temperature from −20 oC to 40 oC with a capacity retention of 95% after 300 cycles. This work provides insights into the structure property relationships in aqueous organic battery materials across molecular, supramolecular and crystalline levels.

Introduction

Large-scale energy storage systems (ESS) are regarded as a promising solution for promoting the utilization of renewable energy and advancing carbon neutrality. Chemical batteries with inherent safety, sustainability, and economic viability hold critical priorities1–3, while aqueous organic batteries (AOBs) have attracted increasing attention due to their elemental resource abundance4–6. Aqueous proton batteries (APBs), a subclass of AOBs, show considerable promise7–9. This advantage arises from the favorable charge-carrier properties of protons (H⁺) in aqueous systems, including the smallest hydration radius, rapid diffusion kinetics, negligible polarization losses, and high energy efficiency10,11. Consequently, significant research efforts have focused on developing advanced proton electrode materials, particularly the organic compounds owing to their cost effectiveness, eco friendliness, and molecularly tunable electrochemical characteristics12,13.

Despite their promise, aqueous organic proton batteries remain at an early stage of development, with most studies targeting improved organic molecules and electrolyte additives10,14–16. For insoluble electrodes, however, electrochemical performance is governed not only by molecular design but also by stacking and hydrophobic interactions, which play crucial roles in suppressing electrode dissolution17,18. While the theoretical capacity of organic electrode materials is determined by their molecular architecture, their electrochemical performance is often constrained by factors beyond the molecule itself, such as supramolecular assembly and crystallographic configurations. These factors directly regulate the accessibility of redox-active sites and subsequently govern the cyclic stability of electrodes under electrochemical cycling19,20.

The molecular packing arrangement of organic materials is dictated by a combination of intermolecular interactions, including π-π stacking, van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonding, and charge-transfer interactions21. Notably, π-π interaction and hydrophobic effects play the central roles in directing the self-assembly of organic molecules into supramolecular assemblies22–25. These interactions critically influence the formation of ion-transport channels and the overall structural stability of the materials. Moreover, the crystalline structure of organic materials not only determines electronic conductivity and ion diffusion pathways but also regulates the structural integrity of electrode materials during repeated charge and discharge processes26. Consequently, a comprehensive understanding of supramolecular assembly and crystalline structure is essential for the rational design of efficient aqueous organic proton batteries.

In this work, 2,3-dichloro-1,4-naphthoquinone (DCNQ) and its single crystal (SC-DCNQ) are employed to investigate the role of molecular packing conformation and crystal structure in aqueous organic proton batteries. SC-DCNQ generates a paired-molecular assembly by π-donor-acceptor (π-DA) interactions. This distinctive molecular arrangement confers supramolecular characteristics, featuring a low energy barrier for intermolecular proton transport. As an electrode material, SC-DCNQ demonstrates rapid and stable proton storage behavior, delivering its theoretical capacity at a specific current of 0.5 A g−1 in 2 M H2SO4 electrolyte, whereas commercial sample gives only 44% molecular capacity. The supramolecular assembly and crystalline structure contribute synergistically to maximize functional groups utilization, endowing the material with favorable rate capability and long-term cycling stability. Meanwhile, theoretical calculations provide substantial support for its protonation reaction pathway and accompanying structural evolution, offering mechanistic insight into the underlying proton storage process.

Results

Quinone functional groups are well-established as efficient proton storage units in electrochemical systems. We first screened representative small-molecule quinones based on their calculated highest occupied molecular orbital-lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (HOMO-LUMO) energy levels, including 1,4-naphthoquinone (NQ), 2,3-diamino-1,4-naphthoquinone (DANQ), 2-chloro-1,4-naphthoquinone (CNQ), and 2-methyl-1,4-naphthoquinone (MNQ) (Fig. 1a). DCNQ emerges as the optimal candidate, exhibiting a relatively narrow HOMO-LUMO energy gap (only 3.88 eV), whereas the DNQ and NQ have wider band gaps are 4.07 eV and 4.33 eV, respectively. Although DANQ has an even smaller gap (2.77 eV), its high solubility in 2 M H2SO4 renders it unsuitable as an electrode material (Supplementary Fig. S2). The LUMO energy of DCNQ is lower than that of MNQ, meaning it more readily accepts electrons and exhibits enhanced redox capability27,28. The cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves demonstrate that DCNQ delivers higher capacity, improved reversibility, and lower polarization (Supplementary Fig. S3). The Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy shows the characteristic C = O stretching vibration of quinone at 1678.73 cm−129,30. The simulated FT-IR spectra of DCNQ were compared with experimental data to validate the calculation method (Supplementary Fig. S4a and Supplementary Table S1), while the good agreement confirms the suitability of the adopted density functional theory (DFT) approach. To further elucidate the electronic structure and intermolecular interactions of DCNQ, we performed detailed theoretical analyses using localized orbital locator-π (LOL-π) and electron localization function (ELF) calculations31. The resulting color-filled maps reveal strong conjugation and well-delocalized π-electrons throughout the DCNQ molecule (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. S5).

We employed various crystallization techniques to obtain well defined DCNQ crystals, including sublimation and solvent crystallization. X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. S6) reveals that the crystal structure depends on the preparation method. The sublimation-grown crystal exhibits the highest signal-to-noise ratio and thus enhanced crystallinity, namely SC-DCNQ. These processing methods did not affect the molecular integrity, evidenced by 1H NMR (Supplementary Fig. S7) and 13C NMR (Supplementary Fig. S8) spectra of SC-DCNQ. The SC-DCNQ demonstrates enhanced exposure of the (002) and (100) planes at 11.1° and 13.5°, respectively. The peak at 27.2° corresponding to the (200) crystal plane represents a common feature across materials prepared by different growth methods. The (100) plane is distinctive to SC-DCNQ, which may be the key factor to its enhanced performance. The intense signal at 13.5° in SC-DCNQ can also be attributed to its characteristic crystal motif arrangement with a spacing of 3.45 Å, which is conducive to carrier transport and material stability32. Consequently, we conducted single crystal XRD (SCXRD) analysis on the SC-DCNQ sample. The resolved structure shows that two DCNQ molecules form a head-to-tail molecular pair (Fig. 1d). This characteristic arrangement stems from the cooperative π-DA interactions, in which the electron-withdrawing chloroquinone moiety acts as an acceptor and the adjacent benzene ring serves as a donor. This interaction stabilizes a supramolecular assembly with C2 symmetry dimer that propagates along the crystal axis in a slipped, quasi-parallel stacking motif (herringbone angle ≈ 52°). Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) depth profiling confirms that this dimeric association is an intrinsic assembly feature. Throughout the 0–250 nm thickness of SC-DCNQ, the secondary ion intensity ratio of (2M-H)-/(M-H)- remained highly constant at 0.46 ± 0.02. This ratio is an order of magnitude higher and markedly more stable than that of the reference DCNQ sample (0.05 ± 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. S9). This result demonstrates that the π-π interaction governs the vapor assembling during the sublimation-grown process.

Consistent with this structure, the solid-state ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectra exhibit pronounced band broadening (Supplementary Fig. S10a) and a distinct blue shift in the first-derivative spectrum (Supplementary Fig. S10b) relative to the monomer, characteristic of dominant H-aggregation of the dimers33. Although head-to-tail packing is typically associated with J-aggregation (red shift), in this specific π-DA assembly strong donor-acceptor coupling inverts the excitonic manifold, resulting in H-type behavior within the dimeric motif aggregation34. FT-IR spectra further support a centrosymmetric dimeric structure, as evidenced by the disappearance of the 1049 cm−1 band due to dipole cancellation in the head-to-tail configuration (Supplementary Fig. S4b).

Supplementary Fig. S11 presents the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image of SC-DCNQ, revealing uniform and smooth rod-like crystals composed of comb-like details. In contrast, the commercial and solvent-recrystallized sample exhibit irregular morphologies with varying sizes. This well-ordered architecture likely facilitates the formation of continuous ion-transport channels. Optical microscopy further confirms the bright and highly crystalline nature of SC-DCNQ (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. S12).

As illustrated in Fig. 1f, the SC-DCNQ exhibits an elevated density of electronic states near the Fermi level, resulting in modified partial density of states (PDOS) for C and O atoms. This effect is predominantly driven by the presence of C = O groups embedded within the π-π conjugated framework, which enhance electron delocalization and intrinsic charge mobility of the SC-DCNQ35. Figure 1g further highlights pronounced π-π intermolecular interactions between adjacent planar layers, indicated by the prominent green spike of the sign (λ2) ρ and corresponding gradient iso-surface (red dashed ellipses) in the range of −0.02 to 0.02 a.u36. Weak interactions between bimolecular DCNQ pairs are shown in the Fig. 1h. Therefore, the DFT results demonstrate that the bimolecular pair SC-DCNQ exhibits enhanced π-DA interaction, elevated intrinsic charge mobility, and improved molecular stability, collectively facilitating efficient proton and electron transport.

To correlate lattice-scale structural evolution with electrochemical behavior, we first performed operando XRD to track real-time lattice changes during cycling (Fig. 2a). As shown in Fig. 2a, the XRD peaks originally at 11.1°, 13.5°, and 27.2° shift to 11.3°, 13.3°, and 26.9° during the charge and discharge process, respectively, indicating no substantial phase transformation occurred. The three intense peaks correspond to the (002), (100), and (200) crystal planes of SC-DCNQ, with corresponding lattice spacings of 0.798 nm, 0.654 nm, and 0.328 nm, respectively. The (002) crystal plane peak at 11.3° shifted rightward by 0.6° after fully discharged, indicating lattice contraction. Meanwhile, the (100) crystal plane peaks at 13.5° exhibited lattice expansion, shifting leftward by 0.2° toward smaller angles. The (200) crystal plane peaks at 26.9° exhibited lattice expansion, shifting leftward by 1.2° toward smaller angles when fully discharged. To gain a deeper understanding of the structural evolution during the electrochemical process, we systematically investigated the discharge product structure of SC-DCNQ by assembled batteries using large single crystals (without grinding) and discharged them thoroughly at a low specific current of 1 A g-1. Subsequent SCXRD analysis of the discharged product revealed good crystallinity, indicating that 2,3-dichloro-1,4-dinaphthol (DCNP) maintained a single crystal state (SC-DCNP) during the discharge process while preserving the supramolecular assembly of bimolecular pairs (Supplementary Fig. S13). SCXRD reveals that both SC-DCNQ and SC-DCNP molecules adopt a π-π interaction, which ensures efficient interfacial charge transfer and dynamic redox chemistry. The π-π stacking structure is consistent with the calculated reduced density gradient (RDG) (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. S14), LOL-π (Supplementary Fig. S15), and XRD results.

SCXRD analysis also revealed structural changes in the SC-DCNQ bimolecular pairs during the charge-discharge processes. Each bimolecular pair contains four oxygen atoms labeled as 1,4, 1’ and 4’ as shown in Fig. 1d, exhibiting three O-O distances: the intramolecular O-O distance between positions 1 and 4 (5.357 Å), the C2 symmetric intermolecular O-O distance between 1 and 1’ (3.931 Å), and the cross-pair O-O distance between 1 and 4’ (6.668 Å). Upon discharge, these distances decrease slightly to 5.350 Å, 3.926 Å, and 6.660 Å, showing reductions of 0.007, 0.005, and 0.008 Å, respectively. Additionally, the C = O bond contracts from the original 1.212 Å to 1.209 Å, while the intercalation of protons will reduce the O-O bond distance37. Although the O-H bond formed during discharge reaches a length of 0.840 Å, the accompanying contraction of the aromatic backbone and the lattice shrinkage of the (002) crystal plane counterbalance any volumetric expansion caused by proton intercalation, consistent with the operando XRD results. As a result, the energy storage functional groups remain fully accessible. After full charging, the structure returns to its original angle, demonstrating good reversibility.

To gain deeper insight into the structural characteristics and evolution of SC-DCNQ and SC-DCNP during the electrochemical process, we conducted cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM) analysis. The cryo-TEM images (Supplementary Fig. S16 and Supplementary Fig. S17) and high-magnification images (Figs. 2c-2d) provide direct evidence of the lattice structure at the atomic scale. For SC-DCNQ, lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.798 nm were observed (Fig. 2d), corresponding to the (002) plane as confirmed by SCXRD refinement and consistent with the diffraction peak at 11.3°. Upon discharge to SC-DCNP, this spacing decreased by 0.006 nm, indicating lattice contraction (Fig. 2f). Additionally, the (100) plane exhibited a spacing of 0.654 nm before discharge and expanded to 0.656 nm after discharge, aligning well with the XRD peak at 13.3° and the lattice fringes observed in Fig. 2e, f. The good agreement between cryo-TEM and XRD data provides clear evidence of anisotropic structural changes during proton insertion, namely contraction along the (002) and expansion along the (100) planes, which are distinctive to SC-DCNQ. These findings highlight the critical role of directional lattice dynamics in the proton storage mechanism. As shown in the radial distribution function (RDF) profile (Fig. 2b), SC-DCNQ forms a highly ordered self-assembled structure capable of sustaining a continuous inter-layer hydrogen-bond network over the length scale required for Grotthuss hopping, thereby enabling efficient proton transport. Together with the operando XRD results, these findings confirm that SC-DCNQ establishes effective proton-transport pathways.

The SC-DCNQ electrode delivers specific capacities of 228.6, 199.7, 192.0, 184.8, 180.1, and 172.7 mAh g-1 at specific currents of 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 A g-1, respectively (Fig. 3d). Even at higher specific currents of 10–40 A g-1, substantial capacities remain at 156.3, 150.8, 143.6, and 137.9 mAh g-1, demonstrating favorable rate capability. When the specific current is restored to 0.5 A g-1, the specific capacity recovered to 228.2 mAh g-1, demonstrating good reversibility.

This capacity approaches the theoretical capacity (96.82% of 236.1 mAh g-1 Supplementary Note 1), highlighting the favorable electrochemical performance. In contrast, pristine commercial DCNQ, DMF-DCNQ, and NMP-DCNQ exhibit lower specific capacities and reduced reversibility across the full specific current range (Fig. 3d). The enhanced performance of SC-DCNQ is further reflected in its long-term cycling stability. At a specific current of 1 A g-1, its specific capacity stabilizes at 178.6 mAh g-1 after the initial 20 cycles and retains 95.41% after 1000 cycles (Supplementary Fig. S20). By comparison, DCM-DCNQ shows an initial capacity of 151.0 mAh g-1 and substantial fading beyond 500 cycles (78.72% retention at 1000 cycles), while MeOH-DCNQ suffers rapid degradation with a cycle life of only 202 cycles. Other samples (EtOH-DCNQ, IPA-DCNQ, ACN- DCNQ, AC-DCNQ and EA-DCNQ) display both lower capacities (no more than 130.4 mAh g-1) and lower retention (63.66–82.85% after 1000 cycles). After extended cycling for 4000 cycles at 1 A g-1, SC-DCNQ retained a capacity of 154.8 mAh g-1, retaining capacity higher than that of the comparison samples (Fig. 3g). All cycling comparison Coulombic efficiency data are provided in Supplementary Fig. S21. This stability is attributed to the ordered supramolecular assembly, smooth morphology, and preferential exposure of the (100) crystal plane, which collectively stabilize the proton-transport framework.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) reveals low intrinsic resistance both before and after cycling (Fig. 3e). Notably, the SC-DCNQ electrode exhibits minimal series resistance (Rs = 0.12–0.28 Ω) and charge-transfer resistance (Rct = 0.5–0.64 Ω), indicating facilitated charge transfer due to π-electron delocalization and supramolecular assembly.

To clarify the applicability of SC-DCNQ in various media, SC-DCNQ was tested for 500-cycle at 1 A g-1 (Supplementary Fig. S22) in several acidic electrolytes covering pH range from -0.3 to 1.0. Capacity increases linearly with proton concentration and is insensitive to alkali-metal cations, confirming H⁺ as the sole charge carrier. Even in the mildest medium, 1 M NH4HSO4 (pH ≈ 1.0), the electrode retains 74 % capacity after 500 cycles, demonstrating that the π-DA stacked lattice tolerates a wide pH window.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the bimolecular pair configuration and distinctive crystalline structure of SC-DCNQ facilitate efficient proton and electron transport, underpinning its enhanced capacity, rapid kinetics, and long-term cycling stability in acidic energy storage systems.

To investigate the electrochemical reaction mechanism of SC-DCNQ electrodes during charge and discharge process, we performed characterizations including operando FT-IR (Fig. 4a), operando Raman (Fig. 4b), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Fig. 4c). Operando FT-IR spectra reveal a reversible reduction of carbonyl groups during discharge. The C = O stretching band at ~1650 cm-1 is progressively attenuated, while a new feature assigned to C-O-H bending ( ~ 1318 cm-1) emerges, indicating conversion of carbonyls into hydroxyl groups. In the 1550–1600 cm-1 region, the benzene/quinone skeletal vibrations weaken and broaden as the C = O signal decreases, reflecting a redistribution of π-electrons within the ring system. These spectral changes fully reverse upon charging, indicating restoration of the quinoid structure. Operando Raman spectra exhibit distinct and fully reversible intensity evolution of characteristic vibrational bands near 1659 cm-1 and 1218 cm-1 during cycling40, consistent with the reversible carbonyl redox chemistry revealed by FT-IR. XPS further confirms this reversible redox chemistry, with O 1 s spectra evolving from dominant C = O character in the pristine state to mixed C = O/C-O contributions in the discharged state and fully recovering after recharge (Supplementary Fig. S23 and Fig. 4c), establishing carbonyl groups as the active redox centers for proton storage41,42. Density functional theory calculations provide further insight into the proton-coupled electron-transfer (PCET) pathway in π-π stacked SC-DCNQ at the molecular, supramolecular, and crystal levels (Fig. 4d, e, and 4g, h). First, the bimolecular form of SC-DCNQ was analyzed to identify reactive sites. Molecular electrostatic potential (MESP43) mapping (Fig. 4d) shows electronegative regions around the four O atoms in the π-DA stacked SC-DCNQ units, while Fukui function analysis (Supplementary Fig. S24) further indicates that proton-accepting reactions preferentially occur at the C = O sites.

To exclude the H3O+ of the SC-DCNQ electrode44, this study employed micro fluidized bed reaction analyzer (MFBRA) to conduct temperature programmed tests on the material (at 300 °C) for monitoring water release during the thermal treatment. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S25, to conduct isothermal tests on the discharged product, SC-DCNP, exhibited no substantial weight loss or water signal at 300 °C for 300 s, ruling out the possibility of water participation and thereby confirming that the redox reaction follows a proton intercalation mechanism.

The proton insertion/extraction mechanism of π-DA stacked SC-DCNQ electrodes was further elucidated through DFT theoretical calculations. The detailed reaction pathway is illustrated in Fig. 4e. The first proton inserts into a C = O group, forming SC-DCNQ-H+ (ΔE = -15.55 eV), then the second proton inserts at the carbonyl group at the 4’-position to yield SC-DCNQ-2H2+ (ΔE = -14.89 eV). Upon accepting one electron, the bimolecular system forms a distinctive protonated the intermediate radical cation SC-DCNQ-2H•+ (ΔE = +18.72 eV for the stepwise pathway), which is circumvented in practice by concerted PCET that stabilizes the semiquinone radical at <S2 > = 0.758 with an effective ΔE of -23.21 eV; the sequential electron-injection pathway is thermodynamically forbidden due to the +6.83 eV barrier and incorrect triplet spin state (S2 = 2.024), consistent with the experimentally observed reversibility, low polarization, and transient electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) signal. This intermediate undergoes further PCET to generate the fully protonated SC-DCNP (ΔE = -34.19 eV), with the radical alternating between 1,4’ positions across the dimer to avoid charge accumulation. The existence of this intermediate radical was corroborated by EPR spectroscopy, which detected unpaired electron signals during discharge, and restored during charging (Supplementary Fig. S26)45. The pristine SC-DCNQ is EPR-silent, indicating a closed-shell electronic configuration. Upon discharging, a symmetric EPR signal emerges with a g-factor ranging from 2.0022 to 2.0029. This g-value is highly consistent with our theoretical calculations, confirming the formation of radical cations (SC-DCNQ-2H•+) where the unpaired electron is delocalized over the π-conjugated framework. The evolution of the signal intensity during the discharge/charge cycle demonstrates the reversible generation and quenching of radical species, facilitated by the transient association/dissociation of protons. Such reversible radical-mediated PCET behavior is consistent with the single pair of redox peaks observed in CV and supports a Grotthuss-type proton hopping mechanism facilitated by π-π stacked SC-DCNQ assemblies. The proton transfer in SC-DCNQ requires 0.51 eV lower energy than in isolated DCNQ molecules (Supplementary Fig. S27), indicating facilitated kinetics associated with bimolecular assembly.

We further calculated the band-structure of π-DA stacked SC-DCNQ (Supplementary Fig. S28a) and fully protonated product SC-DCNP (Supplementary Fig. S28b) to correlate the electronic evolution with the EPR-observed PCET process. The results show that the fully discharged SC-DCNP (hydroquinone skeleton after H-insertion) exhibits a valence-band maximum that is 0.06 eV lower and a conduction-band minimum 0.24 eV higher than those of the pristine SC-DCNQ, resulting in a 0.30 eV wider theoretical band gap. The widened band gap reflects the stabilization and quenching of transient semiquinone states, consistent with the EPR-observed PCET mechanism in which charge compensation proceeds via localized PCET, indicating the effective electron-accepting characteristics of the supramolecular assembly of bimolecular pairs.

Temperature-dependent XRD (Fig. 4f) was collected at 25, 50, 100 and 150 °C. Within this range the (002) and (100) reflections of SC-DCNQ show no detectable shift (Δd ≤ 0.005 Å, Fig. 4f), demonstrating that the slipped π-π stacked framework effectively restrains inter-layer expansion. In the 13C solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (13C ssNMR) spectrum (Supplementary Fig. S29), the aromatic carbons of SC-DCNQ exhibit a 1.32 ppm up-field shift relative to those of pristine DCNQ, indicating an enhanced π-electron delocalization in the stacked solid. We conducted atomic force microscopy (AFM) characterization (Supplementary Fig. S30) on electrode sheets of DCNQ and SC-DCNQ in their pristine, fully discharged, and fully charged states. The results reveal that the roughness difference between the fully discharged and fully charged states of SC-DCNQ is only 0.087 nm, indicating that the π-π stacked crystalline framework is highly stable. Collectively, these results demonstrate that strong π-DA interactions and π-π stacking lock SC-DCNQ into a stable architecture that simultaneously stabilizes the lattice and enables fast, reversible PCET, underpinning its favorable long-term electrochemical performance. Harmonic oscillator model of aromaticity (HOMA) values near unity and negative nucleus-independent chemical shift (NICS)(1)zz values confirm that SC-DCNQ maintains persistent π-conjugation and aromaticity upon protonation, enabling its favorable electrochemical stability and performance (Supplementary Fig. S31).

To further verify the proton storage mechanism, DFT calculation were performed to simulate the proton adsorption energies on different crystal planes. The adsorption energies of protons on the (002), (100), and (200) planes were -1.0035, -1.1916, and -0.9566 eV, respectively, indicating a preferential proton binding affinity for the (100) plane (Fig. 4g, h), which is minimally exposed in the commercial and solvent-crystallized DCNQ samples. Combined SCXRD and cryo-EM analyses show that proton intercalation occupies interstitial sites and forms hydrogen bonds, inducing reversible lattice expansion along the (100) plane. These results demonstrate that the supramolecular bimolecular assembly and crystal structure of SC-DCNQ create well-defined proton transport channels and provides favorable proton storage performance.

So far, we have elucidated the influence of molecular structure, supramolecular assembly, and crystal structure in proton batteries. To further demonstrate the application voltage of the SC-DCNQ electrode, we assembled and tested a full cell consisting of a lead oxide (PbO2) positive electrode, SC-DCNQ negative electrode, and 5 M H2SO4 electrolyte. The reaction mechanism in this aqueous proton batteries closely resembles that of lead-acid system, as both employ sulfuric acid as the electrolyte. Discussion

This study demonstrates that sublimated 2,3-dichloro-1,4-naphthoquinone single crystals (SC-DCNQ) exhibit favorable proton storage performance, exceeding commercially and solvent-crystallized DCNQ. The enhanced performance of SC-DCNQ is attributed to a synergistic triple-control mechanism involving molecular redox chemistry, supramolecular assembly, and facet-engineered crystal architecture. Beyond molecule, DCNQ pairs into dimers tethered by π-DA forces, creating a supramolecular assembly that establishes intermolecular proton and electron transport channels. Furthermore, the single crystal exposes distinctive (100) crystal facets with the lowest proton adsorption energy barrier, facilitating rapid and reversible proton intercalation. Owing to these supramolecular characteristics, the SC-DCNQ electrode achieves high utilization of energy storage unit, whereas commercial DCNQ exhibits limited functional group utilization below 44%. The robust crystalline structure ensures phase stability during charge/discharge cycles, after 4,000 cycles at 1 A g-1, SC-DCNQ retained a capacity of 154.8 mAh g-1. When assembled in a full cell with a PbO2 positive electrode, the system displays favorable cycling performance across a wide temperature range. Collectively, this work elucidates the critical factors governing proton storage in organic materials beyond molecular design. By bridging molecular, supramolecular, and crystallographic insights, this study provides a comprehensive framework for advancing next generation energy storage technologies.

Methods

Materials

2,3-Dichloro-1,4-naphthoquinone (98%, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) and N-Methylpyrrolidone (NMP, AR, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE, 60% aqueous dispersion, Hefei Kejing Materials Technology Co., Ltd., China) was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical (China). KB conductive additive (Ketjenblack, EC-600JD, Hefei Kejing Materials Technology Co., Ltd., China) and Titanium mesh (Guangdong Canrd New Energy Technology Co., Ltd., China) were purchased from Guangdong Canrd New Energy Technology (China). Acetone (AC, AR, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), Dichloromethane (DCM, AR, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), Acetonitrile (ACN, AR, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), 98% concentrated sulfuric acid (AR, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), N,N-Dimethylformamide (DMF, AR, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), Ethyl Acetate (EA, AR, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), Methanol (MeOH, AR, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), Ethyl Alcohol (EtOH, AR, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China), Isopropanol (IPA, AR, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China) and other reagents were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent (China).

Preparation of electrode materials

1 gram of 2,3-dichloro-1,4-naphthoquinone (DCNQ) was put into a porcelain boat, calcined to 170 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C per minute in an argon atmosphere, and kept warm for 10 hours to obtain 2,3-dichloro-1,4-naphthoquinone single crystal (SC-DCNQ). (According to Supplementary Fig. S1). Precisely weigh 1 g of DCNQ and dissolve it in 10 mL of each solvent (DMF, NMP, EA, MeOH, AC, DCM, EtOH, ACN, and IPA). Heat the solution for 2 h, followed by hot filtration and cooling to obtain crystallized products. Naming: The resulting crystals are designated as DMF-DCNQ, NMP-DCNQ, EA-DCNQ, MeOH-DCNQ, AC-DCNQ, DCM-DCNQ, EtOH-DCNQ, ACN-DCNQ, and IPA-DCNQ, respectively. HRMS: C10H4Cl2O2H [M + H]+ test result is 226.9661, and the actual value is 225.96. GC-MS: m/z (%) 227 (50).

Electrochemical measurements

Electrochemical measurements were carried out using both three-electrode and full-cell configurations.

Three-electrode configuration

The three-electrode measurements were conducted in a glass electrochemical cell (50 mL volume). A DCNQ or SC-DCNQ composite electrode was used as the working electrode, an Ag/AgCl electrode (saturated KCl, model R0306, purchased from Shanghai Yueci Electronics Co., Ltd.) as the reference electrode, and a graphite rod electrode (diameter 6 mm, length 90 mm, purchased from Shanghai Yueci Electronics Co., Ltd.) as the counter electrode. The electrolyte was 2 M H2SO4 aqueous solution. The graphite rod electrode was used as received without further treatment.

Electrode preparation

The composite electrodes were prepared by mixing the active material (DCNQ or SC-DCNQ), conductive carbon black (Super P, Shenzhen Kejing), and polytetrafluoroethylene binder (PTFE, Shenzhen Kejing, 60 wt% dispersion) at a mass ratio of 60:30:10. Isopropyl alcohol ( ≥ 99.5%) was added to form a homogeneous slurry.

The slurry was coated onto titanium mesh current collectors (100 mesh, wire diameter of 0.1 mm, aperture size of 0.154 mm) using a roller coating machine (MSK-2150, Shenzhen Kejing). The thickness of the coated electrode was approximately 0.25 mm. The electrodes were dried under vacuum at 80 ± 1 °C for 12 h and cut into 1 × 1 cm2 pieces using a precision cutter. The electrodes were single-side coated with an active material loading of ~1.5 mg cm-2. No additional surface treatment was applied to the current collectors prior to electrode fabrication.

Full-cell configuration

SC-DCNQ | |PbO2 full cell

The full cell was assembled using SC-DCNQ as the negative electrode and PbO2 as the positive electrode with 5 M H2SO4 aqueous solution as the electrolyte. The electrode area was 1 cm², and both electrodes were single-side coated. The mass loading of the SC-DCNQ negative electrode was ~1.5 mg cm-2. The PbO2 positive electrode material was recovered from a commercially available lead-acid battery. The electrode plate was disassembled, and the active material was separated from the current collector, followed by thorough washing with deionized water until neutral pH was reached to remove residual electrolyte. The material was then dried under vacuum at 60 °C for 12 h and used directly as the positive electrode. No further chemical or structural modification was applied.

MnO2-based full-cell configuration

A cathode-free aqueous cell configuration was constructed using SC-DCNQ as the negative electrode and graphite felt as the current collector. The electrolyte consisted of 1 M MnSO4 + 1 M H2SO4 aqueous solution. In this system, no preloaded solid cathode material was used. Instead, Mn2+ ions in the electrolyte undergo reversible electrochemical deposition and dissolution on the graphite felt current collector, forming MnO2 during charging and reverting to Mn2+ during discharge. Therefore, the cathodic reaction is governed by a solution-mediated Mn2+/MnO2 redox process.

The graphite felt (thickness 3 mm, purchased from Haitian Muzi Graphite Products Factory, China) was used as received without further treatment. The SC-DCNQ negative electrode was prepared as described above with an aerial mass loading of ~1.5 mg cm-2.

Large-format full-cell configuration

A large-format aqueous full cell was assembled using a custom-designed casing fabricated by 3D printing (ABS material). The cell configuration consisted of an SC-DCNQ negative electrode and a PbO2 positive electrode (3N-2P stacked configuration) with 5 M H2SO4 as the electrolyte.

In this work, a commercially available lead-acid battery (4 V, 250 mAh) was employed as the lead source. The PbO2 positive electrode was recovered from a commercially available lead-acid battery. The electrode plate was disassembled, and the active material was separated, washed thoroughly with deionized water until neutral pH, and dried under vacuum at 60 °C for 12 h before use. The effective geometric area of the PbO2 electrode was 5.2 × 4.1 cm2. The SC-DCNQ anode was prepared as described above and coated on a current collector with a geometric area of 4 × 4 cm2 in a single-side configuration. The mass loading of the active material was 4 mg cm-2. A filter paper separator was used between the electrodes. The electrolyte was added in excess to ensure full wetting of both electrodes. This configuration was designed with an excess negative electrode capacity to ensure that the electrochemical performance is primarily governed by the SC-DCNQ negative electrode. The electrolyte volume in each cell was ~15 mL. The cell was assembled in a custom aqueous electrochemical cell with a chemically resistant casing.

Electrochemical testing conditions

Galvanostatic charge/discharge (GCD) measurements were conducted using a LAND-CT3001A battery testing system (Wuhan LAND Electronic Co., Ltd.) at various current densities. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were performed on a CHI660E electrochemical workstation (CHI Instruments). All measurements were carried out at 25 ± 2 °C under ambient laboratory conditions without the use of a climatic chamber. EIS measurements were performed in potentiostatic mode with an AC amplitude of 5 mV over a frequency range from 100 kHz to 0.01 Hz, with 10 data points per decade. The measurements were conducted after stabilizing the cell at open-circuit voltage for 30 min.

Sample handling for ex situ measurements

For ex situ characterization, electrode samples were collected at different electrochemical states, the test condition is at specific current of 1 A g-1, after 3 cycles, 25 ± 2 °C, including the pristine state, selected charged states (0.2 V and 0.3 V), fully charged state, selected discharged states (0.3 V, 0.2 V, and 0.1 V), and fully discharged state.

After disassembly, the electrodes were immediately removed from the cells, rinsed thoroughly with deionized water to remove residual electrolyte, followed by washing with ethanol. The samples were then dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 12 h before further characterization. All sample handling procedures were carried out under ambient laboratory conditions (25 ± 2 °C).

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements were performed on these electrodes collected at different electrochemical states. All samples were prepared following the same ex situ procedure to ensure consistency. The EPR spectra were recorded under identical experimental conditions to allow direct comparison of signal intensity and g-values across different states. The evolution of the EPR signals with electrochemical states reflects changes in the electronic structure during proton storage and release processes.

Sample handling for operando measurements

For operando characterization, electrochemical measurements were performed in a three-electrode cell configuration with SC-DCNQ as the working electrode, Ag/AgCl as the reference electrode, and a graphite rod as the counter electrode. The cell was first subjected to one complete charge-discharge cycle at a specific current of 1 A g-1 from the open circuit voltage to fully discharged state and back to fully charged state. Operando measurements were conducted at 25 ± 2 °C.

Characterizations

The mechanism of the quinone anode material was characterized by SEM (SU8010), FT-IR spectroscopy (Bruker TENSOR Π) and XRD (Rigaku MiniFlex600, Cu), 1H NMR, 13C NMR (JEOL ECS-400). Single crystal XRD (Double-microfocal single crystal diffractometer), cryo TEM (Themis300), EPR (ESR5000), 13C ssNMR (JNM-ECZ600R), Raman (inVia), XPS (Thermo Scientific K-Alpha), solid state UV-vis (Lambda 650S), AFM (Bruker Nano DIMENSION ICON), and TOF-SIMS (TOF.SIMS 5-100, ION-TOF GmbH). The decomposition gas product of SC-DCNP was obtained through Micro Fluidized Bed Reaction Analyzer (MFBRA) testing.

Solution NMR spectroscopy (1H NMR)

Solution-state 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were recorded on a JEOL ECS-400 spectrometer (JEOL, Japan). Chemical shifts are reported in parts per million (ppm) relative to the residual solvent signals.

Solution NMR spectroscopy (13C NMR)

Solution-state 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were recorded on a JEOL ECS-400 spectrometer (JEOL, Japan). Chemical shifts are reported in parts per million (ppm) and referenced to the residual solvent signals. Samples were dissolved in deuterated solvents, and standard acquisition parameters were applied. The spectra were processed using the instrument’s standard software.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

SEM images were obtained using a SU8010 microscope (Hitachi, Japan). The electrode samples were directly mounted onto conductive substrates prior to measurement. All images were acquired under high-vacuum conditions.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR)

FT-IR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Tensor II spectrometer (Bruker, Germany). The spectra were collected over a typical wavenumber range of 500–4000 cm-1 using standard acquisition parameters.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

XRD patterns were collected using a MiniFlex 600 diffractometer (Rigaku, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). The measurements were performed over an appropriate 2 Theta range with standard scanning conditions.

Samples were dissolved in deuterated solvents, and standard acquisition parameters were used. The spectra were processed using the instrument’s standard software.

Single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD)

SCXRD data were collected on a SuperNova diffractometer (Rigaku Oxford Diffraction) equipped with an Atlas S2 detector using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54184 Å). Data collection, cell refinement, and data reduction were performed using the CrysAlisPro software package (Rigaku Oxford Diffraction). A multi-scan absorption correction was applied using the SCALE3 ABSPACK algorithm implemented in CrysAlisPro. The crystal structure was solved and refined using standard crystallographic methods. All non-hydrogen atoms were refined anisotropically, and hydrogen atoms were placed in calculated positions and refined using a riding model.

Crystallographic data for the structure have been deposited at the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre (CCDC) under deposition number 2549668-2549669.

Cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM)

Cryo-TEM measurements were performed on a Themis 300 transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at an accelerating voltage of 300 kV.

Samples were prepared by dispersing the material in ethanol, followed by drop-casting onto holey carbon-coated copper grids. The grids were then rapidly vitrified and transferred into the microscope under cryogenic conditions to preserve the native structure. All observations were conducted under low-temperature conditions to minimize beam-induced damage. The acquired images were processed using standard digital image analysis software.

Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (EPR) Spectroscopy

EPR measurements were performed on an ESR5000 spectrometer (Bruker) operating at X-band frequency ( ~ 9.5 GHz) with a 100 kHz field modulation. The instrument parameters were as follows: center field 3510 G, sweep width 200 G, microwave power 2 mW, modulation amplitude 1 G, and receiver gain adjusted according to signal intensity. Samples were collected at different electrochemical states, prepared following the ex situ procedure described above, and sealed in quartz EPR tubes (4 mm outer diameter) under argon atmosphere to prevent oxidation. All spectra were recorded at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) and calibrated against a standard Mn2+/MgO marker. The g-values were obtained directly from the instrument software. Signal intensities were normalized to sample mass for quantitative comparison across different states.

Solid-state NMR spectroscopy (13C ssNMR)

Solid-state 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were recorded on a JNM-ECZ600R spectrometer (JEOL, Japan) at a resonance frequency of 150 MHz. The measurements were performed using a cross-polarization magic-angle spinning (CP/MAS) technique with a rotor diameter of 3.2 mm. The spinning rate was set to 12 kHz. A relaxation delay of 5 s was used, and a total of 1760 scans were accumulated. The line broadening factor was 80 Hz. Chemical shifts were referenced to adamantane, with characteristic peaks at 38.5 and 29.5 ppm. The spinning sidebands were identified at ±80 ppm.

Operando Raman spectroscopy

Operando Raman spectroscopy was performed using a inVia Raman microscope (Renishaw, UK). The electrochemical Raman measurements were conducted using a custom-designed cell that allowed simultaneous spectroscopic and electrochemical characterization.

Raman spectra were collected during electrochemical cycling starting from the open-circuit potential (OCP). Spectra were recorded at selected potentials during the cycling process to monitor the structural evolution of the electrode material. All spectra were processed using the instrument’s standard software with consistent parameters applied across all measurements.

Solid-state UV-Vis Spectroscopy

Diffuse reflectance UV-vis spectra were recorded on a Lambda 650S spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer) equipped with an integrating sphere accessory. The measurements were performed in the wavelength range of 200–800 nm at a scanning rate of 240 nm min−1 with a data interval of 1 nm. BaSO4 powder was used as a reference standard for baseline correction. Samples were prepared by dispersing the powdered material in BaSO4 (1:10 mass ratio) and packing the mixture into a sample holder to ensure uniform surface coverage. All measurements were conducted at ambient laboratory conditions (25 ± 2 °C) without the use of a climatic chamber.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM)

AFM measurements were performed using a Dimension Icon system (Bruker Nano Inc., USA). The electrode samples were fixed onto glass substrates using adhesive tape prior to measurement.

Imaging was conducted in Peak Force Tapping mode (Bruker) under ambient conditions. The scan area was set to 500 × 500 nm2. All images were processed using the instrument’s standard analysis software with consistent parameters applied across samples.

Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS)

TOF-SIMS measurements were performed using a TOF.SIMS 5-100 instrument (ION-TOF GmbH, Germany). A Bi3++ primary ion beam with an energy of 30 keV was used for surface analysis, with an incident angle of 45 degree. The scanned area was 200 μm × 200 μm. Negative secondary ions were collected over a mass range of 0-900 amu.

For depth profiling, a gas cluster ion beam (GCIB) with an energy of 10 keV was employed as the sputtering source at an incident angle of 45 degree. The sputtering rate was approximately 0.14 nm s-1 (calibrated using SiO2 as a reference).

All measurements were conducted under high vacuum conditions following standard instrument protocols.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)

XPS measurements were performed using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha spectrometer equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source (hν = 1486.6 eV). The electrode samples were cut into pieces of approximately 5 × 5 mm2 and mounted onto the sample holder prior to analysis. For electrodes obtained after electrochemical testing, the samples were rinsed thoroughly with deionized water to remove residual electrolyte and then dried under vacuum prior to analysis.

The base pressure in the analysis chamber was maintained at ~3 × 10-7 mbar. The X-ray spot size was set to 400 μm, with an operating voltage of 12 kV and a filament current of 6 mA. Survey spectra were collected with a pass energy of 150 eV and a step size of 1 eV, while high-resolution spectra were acquired with a pass energy of 50 eV and a step size of 0.1 eV.

Binding energy calibration was performed using the C 1 s peak of adventitious carbon at 284.8 eV as a reference. Background subtraction was carried out using a Shirley-type background, and peak fitting was performed using a mixed Gaussian-Lorentzian (GL) function. All spectra were processed using the instrument’s standard analysis software with consistent fitting parameters across all samples.

Fast thermochemical reaction analysis

The decomposition gas product of SC-DCNP was obtained through pulse micro fluidized bed (PMFB) testing. Fast thermochemical reaction experiments were conducted using a custom-built PMFB reactor coupled with an online electron ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometer (EI-TOF-MS). The PMFB system consists of a sample injector, a fluidized bed reactor, and a gas purification unit. The reactor was constructed from high-temperature- and high-pressure-resistant steel, allowing operation under elevated temperature conditions. The outlet gas from the reactor was continuously monitored by an EI-TOF-MS system (electron ionization source), enabling real-time analysis of gaseous products during the reaction.

Prior to each experiment, a clean reaction tube was loaded with 6 g of quartz sand as the bed material, corresponding to a bed height of approximately 2 mm. Approximately 20 mg of sample was placed in the injector for pulse introduction. The system was assembled and purged with carrier gas to ensure gas tightness. The reactor temperature was increased from room temperature to 300 °C at a heating rate of 20 °C min-1. Helium was used as the fluidizing gas. After reaching the target temperature, the system was stabilized until a steady baseline signal was obtained from the mass spectrometer. The sample was then injected in a pulse mode to initiate the reaction. The outlet gas stream was split into two pathways: one directed to the EI-TOF-MS through a capillary inlet for real-time monitoring of gaseous species evolution, and the other vented. All experiments were repeated at least three times to ensure reproducibility.

In the three-electrode system, we chose a DCNQ/SC-DCNQ electrode, an Ag/AgCl electrode and an graphite rod electrode as working, reference and counter electrode, respectively, and 2 M H2SO4 as electrolyte. The PbO2 electrode was used as the cathode, the SC-DCNQ electrode as the anode and 5 M H2SO4 as the electrolyte in the full cell test.

Theoretical Calculations

The structures of the studied organic material as well as its complexes with protons were fully optimized at the B3LYP/6-31 G(d), and the total energy calculated at B3LYP /def2TZVP level of theory by the commercial Gaussian 16 program package46. The solvent (H2O) effect was included in the calculations based on polarizable continuum model (PCM) through integral equation formalism variant (IEFPCM). The vibrational frequencies of the optimized structures were carried out at the same level of theory to obtain their Gibbs free energy. Molecular surface electrostatic potential (ESP) was achieved according to the commercial Gaussian 16 program package. A negative (red color) ESP suggests electrophilic properties, while a positive (blue color) ESP denotes nucleophilic properties. Models such as positioning orbit positioner-π (LOL-π) and reducing Density Gradient (RDG), harmonic oscillator models of aromaticity (HOMA) and nucleus-independent chemical shift (NICS) were implemented according to Multiwfn 3.8 (dev) code, and visualized using Visual Molecular dynamics (VMD) software47,48. In this study, we used first-principles density functional theory (DFT) calculations in a generalized gradient approximation (GGA) using the Bodu-Burke theory (DFT) formulation. All theoretical calculations were carried out in the Vienna Ab Novo Computational Simulation Package (VASP). Exchange-related effects are described by Burst (PBE). The projector augmented wave (PAW) pseudopotential was used to handle the covalent interaction, and a cut-off energy of 400 eV was used. The Castep module in MS was used to optimize the energy of the model, and in the geometry optimization, when the energy and force were reduced to 10-5 eV and below 0.01 eV/A, all calculations reached the convergence standard. The Brillouin zone integral is a structure performed using 1 × 1 × 1 Munchhorst-Pack K point sampling. DFT computation was performed by using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)49,50 with the projected augmented wave (PAW)51,52 pseudopotential. The exchange-correlation interaction was described by the generalized gradient approximation (GGA)53 parametrized by Perdew Burke Ernzerhof (PBE)54,55. The cut-off energy for the plane-wave basis was set to be 520 eV. Grimme’s semiempirical DFT-D3 scheme of dispersion correction was employed to account for the van der Waals (vdW) interactions56. The Brillouin zone was sampled by using a 1 × 1 × 1 Mon khorst Pack k-point grid57 in structure optimizations and The convergence criterion for total energy change was set to 10-5 eV, and 0.02 eV Å−1 for residual force, respectively. A vacuum space of 10 Å was applied in the perpendicular z direction to avoid the interaction between two periodic units, based on this, targeted cleaving and structural optimization were conducted for (002), (100) and (200) crystal facets of the structure. Raw energy data were retrieved from the output files and further processed to derive the adsorption energy data. CIF files for adsorption energy calculations, and molecular dynamics simulation trajectories are provided in Supplementary Data 1-4.
