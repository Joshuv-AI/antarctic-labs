HYPOChLorous Acid TEsting Studies in percutaneous DRAINAGE (HYPOCLATES:DRAINAGE): protocol for a single-centre, controlled, ambispective cohort study comparing hypochlorous acid versus saline irrigation in percutaneous drainage of intra-abdominal abscesses
Authors: Mika P Nadvornik, Tobias Hoch Al Hessen, Niels Siegel, Axel Kramer, Jonathan M Harnoss, Pascal Probst, Mohammed Al-Saeedi, Miriam Klauss, Martin Loos, Christoph W Michalski, Julian C Harnoss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPercutaneous drainage is the standard of care for intra-abdominal abscesses. Additional saline irrigation provides mechanical cleansing but lacks antimicrobial activity. Hypochlorous acid (HOCl), an endogenous antiseptic with broad-spectrum microbicidal activity and high tissue compatibility, is a promising alternative for intracavitary infection control. This study evaluates whether HOCl irrigation shortens the indwelling time of percutaneous irrigation drains and systemic antibiotic duration compared with saline irrigation in patients with intra-abdominal abscesses.Methods and analysisFollowing an institutional change in routine practice on 1 December 2025, this single-centre, controlled, ambispective cohort study at the Department of General, Visceral and Transplantation Surgery, Heidelberg University Hospital, compares a prospectively followed cohort treated with continuous HOCl-containing irrigation (Granudacyn) with a non-concurrent, retrospectively assembled saline cohort from the preceding period. Both cohorts include drains managed with continuous high-volume irrigation (at least 1 L per 24 h for a minimum of 3 consecutive days). The primary endpoints are the indwelling time of the index irrigation drain (placement to removal) and the duration of systemic antibiotic therapy. Secondary endpoints include hospital and intensive care unit stay, reinterventions, complication severity (Clavien–Dindo), time to fistula closure and infection-related parameters. Confounding is addressed using propensity-score methods, and time-to-event endpoints are analysed accounting for competing events.Ethics and disseminationThe study has received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Heidelberg University’s Medical Faculty (S-520/2025). As pseudonymised routine clinical data are used without additional patient contact or deviation from standard care, individual informed consent is waived in accordance with the national regulations and General Data Protection Regulation. Study findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications.Trial registration numberDRKS00038653.

Introduction

Intra-abdominal fluid collections and abscesses are associated with prolonged hospitalisation, repeated interventions and sustained morbidity.1 Minimally invasive percutaneous drainage represents the established standard in their therapeutic management.2 These systems function either as passive outflow drainages or, particularly in suspected infections, as irrigation drainages enabling mechanical debridement through lavage.3

Isotonic saline solution is the current standard for intracavitary irrigation in interventional drainage therapy.3 It is widely used due to its mechanical cleansing effect, availability and favourable safety profile.4 5 However, saline has no antimicrobial activity, which may limit its effectiveness in contaminated or infected collections.6

Antiseptic agents enhance infection control, but some—such as chlorhexidine or octenidine—exhibit relevant cytotoxicity. While the optimal balance between antimicrobial efficacy and tissue compatibility remains unclear, hypochlorous acid (HOCl) represents a promising candidate.7 8

This is because the HOCl/OCl⁻ (hypochlorite anion) redox system is an endogenous antimicrobial mechanism of the innate immune response, decomposing into non-toxic water, oxygen and chloride ions.9 10 Coherently, in vitro and in vivo studies already demonstrate exceptional biocompatibility.10–13 In addition, literature suggests that repeated HOCl exposure can enhance antimicrobial efficacy against biofilms,14 which supports the concept of regimens with frequent or sustained application. Despite this favourable safety profile and mechanistic rationale, clinical studies evaluating HOCl specifically in percutaneous drainage therapy remain scarce.15

Granudacyn, a CE-certified medical product containing HOCl, is already used in acute and chronic wound management and approved for intraperitoneal application.16 Previous work further indicates that intensified catheter irrigation strategies—such as high-volume isotonic lavage in severe acute pancreatitis—may improve infection-related outcomes.17 These observations support evaluating HOCl as the irrigation solution within an established continuous high-volume irrigation platform.

Against this background, the Department of General, Visceral and Transplantation Surgery at Heidelberg University Hospital adopted continuous HOCl-containing irrigation as its routine institutional standard for percutaneous intra-abdominal irrigation drains on 1 December 2025, motivated by accumulating evidence supporting its tissue compatibility and broad-spectrum antimicrobial efficacy. The present study, HYPOCLATES:DRAINAGE, is a part of the HYPOChLorous Acid TEsting Studies evaluating HOCl-based irrigation strategies and leverages this change in routine practice to provide the first comparative clinical evidence on continuous HOCl irrigation in percutaneous drainage.

Methods and analysis

Study design

HYPOCLATES:DRAINAGE is a single-centre, controlled, ambispective cohort study conducted at the Department of General, Visceral and Transplantation Surgery, University Hospital Heidelberg. It compares two consecutive cohorts defined by the irrigation solution used in routine care: a retrospectively assembled saline cohort serving as a historical control and comprising eligible patients from a defined period before the practice change and a prospectively followed HOCl cohort comprising eligible patients from 1 December 2025 onwards, when continuous irrigation with pH-neutral hypotonic electrolyte solution Granudacyn containing stabilised HOCl (50 ppm) and sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl, 50 ppm) in equilibrium became the institutional standard.18 The change in routine practice was an independent clinical decision and was not introduced for the purpose of this study. Accordingly, under national regulations the study is considered non-interventional, as treatment allocation follows routine practice and the CE-marked device is used within its approved intended purpose.

To ensure that the cohorts differ in the irrigation solution and not in irrigation modality, both cohorts comprise exclusively drains managed with continuous high-volume irrigation, administered at a minimum rate of 1000 mL per 24 hours for at least 3 consecutive days through the percutaneously placed irrigation drainage. Drains in the saline cohort must have received continuous irrigation with isotonic saline under identical volume and duration thresholds.

Data are obtained exclusively from in-hospital patients with percutaneously placed intra-abdominal irrigation drainages as part of routine clinical care. No study-specific procedures or alterations to therapeutic management are introduced. Modifications of irrigation therapy, including cessation of lavage or drain removal, are determined by the surgical team according to clinical condition and institutional protocols. Irrigation may be discontinued in cases of infection resolution, adverse events (eg, bleeding, perforation and leakage), uncorrectable drain obstruction or lack of therapeutic benefit with all deviations being documented.

Continuous HOCl irrigation is integrated into standard ward routines, with daily irrigation volumes recorded by medical staff in the electronic hospital information system. Documentation of the retrospective saline cohort is verified via structured chart review. Systemic antibiotic therapy follows clinical judgement and institutional guidelines. All other supportive and therapeutic measures remain unrestricted.

Study population

Eligible participants are adults (≥18 years) of any sex who receive a therapeutic, percutaneously placed irrigation drainage while hospitalised at the Department of General, Visceral and Transplantation Surgery, University Hospital Heidelberg. All percutaneous irrigation drainages placed for intra-abdominal use are included, irrespective of the underlying indication, provided the drain is managed with continuous high-volume irrigation (at least 1 L per 24 hours for a minimum of 3 consecutive days) as part of the intended therapeutic management. The full spectrum of indications encountered at this tertiary referral centre is anticipated, with a predominance of post-operative collections and leaks (including post-pancreatectomy collections and anastomotic leaks) alongside primary or spontaneous infections (eg, appendicitis and diverticulitis) and pancreatitis-associated collections. Collections may be benign-associated or malignancy-associated. The indication is recorded for every patient and grouped into pre-specified categories (post-operative collection/leak, primary/spontaneous infection, pancreatitis-associated collection, other), which are used both as an adjustment covariate and to define pre-specified subgroups (see Statistical analysis).

Exclusion criteria include the inability to administer an antibiotic regimen, organ transplantation and pregnancy or breastfeeding. Prophylactic or purely diagnostic drainages and extra-abdominal drainages (eg, pleural, biliary or nephrostomy) are not included, nor are drains that did not receive continuous high-volume irrigation (at least 1 L per 24 hours for a minimum of 3 consecutive days). Insufficient documentation is defined objectively as the absence of missing data on the assigned irrigation regimen.

The unit of analysis is the index irrigation drain, defined as the first drain in a patient that meets the continuous high-volume irrigation criteria. Where a patient has more than one qualifying drain, the first qualifying drain is used. Drain indwelling time is defined as the interval from placement to removal of this index drain. No additional centre-specific or surgeon-specific eligibility criteria apply, as all procedures represent routine clinical practice at a single tertiary institution. Study flow

The saline (control) cohort comprises all consecutive eligible patients who received qualifying percutaneous irrigation drainages during the 16-week period preceding the practice change (11 August 2025 to 30 November 2025).

The prospective HOCl cohort comprises all consecutive eligible patients treated with continuous Granudacyn irrigation from 1 December 2025 onwards, with recruitment continued until 22 March 2026.

If the number of eligible patients was lower than anticipated, the observation periods of the two cohorts will be extended by 14 weeks each, equalling 30 weeks per cohort, to ensure an adequate number of included cases and to improve the precision and interpretability of the planned analysis.

Each patient’s medical record is reviewed for at least 30 days after hospital discharge to capture readmissions. The overall study design and cohort flow are illustrated in figure 1.

Data collection

The collected data are structured into baseline characteristics, procedural parameters, post-interventional course, and clinical outcomes.

Baseline characteristics include age, sex, body mass index, Charlson Comorbidity Index, the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status, immunosuppression, diabetes mellitus, malignancy, smoking status, previous abdominal surgery and the indication for drainage placement.

Drainage-related and procedure-related variables comprise the type of drainage (suction-irrigation vs cook), catheter calibre, anatomical location, indication for placement, date of insertion, imaging modality used for guidance, abscess or collection characteristics (diameter, capsulation, multiloculation, and presence, type and origin of any fistulous communication), microbiological results from aspirates and the initial irrigation regimen.

Peri-interventional and post-interventional clinical course includes drainage patency or obstruction, need for drainage exchange or repositioning, duration of irrigation, duration of drainage, systemic antibiotic therapy (antibiotic choice, start, end, changes and escalations), vital signs as clinically indicated and dates of discharge and readmission.

Infection-related parameters include microbiological culture results (pathogen spectrum and resistance profiles) and inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein (CRP), leucocyte count and procalcitonin (PCT)).

Complications and reinterventions are recorded according to the Clavien–Dindo classification.19 Reinterventions are categorised as (1) radiological (eg, drainage exchange, repositioning and new percutaneous access), (2) surgical (laparoscopy or laparotomy) or (3) other invasive procedures (eg, aspiration). Intensive care unit (ICU) admission and length of stay are also recorded.

Outcome variables correspond to the predefined primary and secondary endpoints, including drainage duration, antibiotic duration, duration of proven fistula (eg, postoperative pancreatic fistula or lymphatic fistula), length of hospital stay, ICU stay, need for reintervention, complication grade, mortality and readmission within 30 days after discharge.

Outcomes and hypotheses

The primary outcomes are the indwelling time of the index irrigation drain, defined as the number of days between placement and removal of the index drain (the first drain meeting the continuous high-volume irrigation criteria), and the duration of systemic antibiotic therapy, defined as the number of days of systemic antibiotic therapy directed at the underlying cause of the index drainage, from the first to the last day of administration. If more than one qualifying drain is placed simultaneously, the one drain being removed last is chosen as index drain in both cohorts.

Accordingly, the primary hypothesis states that continuous HOCl irrigation reduces index drain indwelling time and antibiotic duration compared with continuous saline irrigation in percutaneous therapy for intra-abdominal abscesses.

Secondary outcomes cover hospital course, morbidity and reinterventions, and time to fistula closure, and are detailed in table 2. Inflammatory markers (CRP, leucocyte count and PCT) are pre-specified secondary endpoints reflecting resolution of the intracavitary infection, captured as their trajectory over time. Because their normalisation also forms a part of the objective criteria for drain removal and antibiotic cessation, they are correlated with the primary endpoints by design and are interpreted as mechanistically supportive rather than as independent confirmation.

Data management

Clinical data, including demographic variables, comorbidities, peri-interventional parameters and infection markers, are extracted from the electronic hospital information system (SAP IS-H, SAP SE, Walldorf, Germany) and entered into a dedicated study database. All datasets are pseudonymised at the point of extraction.

All data will be processed in compliance with the European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and national data protection laws. No personally identifiable information will be shared with third parties.

Data are stored on secure servers of Heidelberg University Hospital with restricted, role-based access for authorised study personnel only. Data integrity is ensured through routine plausibility checks and version-controlled dataset management.

The anonymised study dataset will be retained for 10 years in accordance with institutional policy and regulatory requirements. Responsibility for data management, accuracy and secure handling lies with the principal investigator.

Sample size

As a non-interventional cohort study, the sample size is not based on a formal hypothesis-driven power calculation but is determined by the number of eligible drains arising in routine care.

Across the two observation periods, approximately 300 percutaneous intra-abdominal irrigation drains will be placed at the Department of General, Visceral and Transplantation Surgery. However, only a subset of these will be managed with continuous high-volume irrigation for at least 3 consecutive days and therefore meet the eligibility criteria. The number of eligible drains is consequently an uncertain fraction of the overall caseload and cannot be reliably pre-estimated.

The study is therefore designed for estimation rather than formal hypothesis testing: its aims are to (1) describe drain indwelling time and clinical outcomes, (2) obtain adjusted effect-size estimates with CIs using parsimonious, propensity-score-based methods appropriate for a modest, non-randomised sample, and (3) inform the design and sample size of future multicentre trials. Given the anticipated sample, the analysis prioritises estimation over significance testing, and the number of covariates is deliberately limited to avoid overfitting (see Statistical analysis).

Potential sources of bias

Potential sources of bias include temporal confounding, secular changes in clinical practice and selection bias from differences in case mix between periods. Restricting both cohorts to drains managed with continuous high-volume irrigation may itself select a particular, more severely affected subgroup. The most important concern specific to this before–after design is that treating clinicians cannot be blinded to the irrigation era, which may influence decisions on drain removal and antibiotic cessation that determine the primary endpoints (performance and ascertainment bias). To mitigate these factors, all consecutive eligible patients within the defined periods are included. Drain removal and antibiotic cessation are anchored to pre-specified objective criteria applied identically in both cohorts (declining or normalised inflammatory markers, low drain output after irrigation has stopped and clinical stability). The statistical analysis is performed with cohort labels masked until the analysis is locked and measured confounding is addressed using propensity-score methods. As an observational study, residual and unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded, and the objective criteria above reflect institutional practice that will be verified against the medical records.

Statistical analysis

All analyses will be performed on a patient-level analysis set including all eligible patients with at least one percutaneously placed intra-abdominal irrigation drainage within the predefined observation windows. Descriptive statistics will be used to characterise both cohorts. Categorical variables will be presented as counts and percentages, and continuous variables as mean±SD or median with IQR, as appropriate. Baseline characteristics will be compared between cohorts using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables.

The analysis set comprises all eligible patients contributing an index continuous-irrigation drain in each cohort. Because the two cohorts are not randomised, the primary approach to confounding control is propensity score analysis. A propensity score for treatment with HOCl versus saline is estimated by logistic regression using a small, pre-specified set of clinically important confounders—indication category, comorbidity burden (Charlson Comorbidity Index or ASA physical status), baseline infection severity (CRP) and age—the number of which is deliberately limited in view of the modest expected sample to avoid overfitting. Cohorts are balanced primarily by optimal full matching on the propensity score, which retains all patients and is well suited to small, imbalanced samples. As a sensitivity analysis, k:1 nearest-neighbour matching with a calliper width of 0.2 SD of the logit propensity score was performed. Covariate balance before and after matching is assessed using standardised mean differences, with a value <0.1 considered adequate.

Given the non-randomised design and the anticipated small sample, the analysis is oriented towards estimation with CIs rather than formal hypothesis testing, and the complexity of any model is governed by the number of observed events. Effect estimates from matched analyses are reported with appropriate (cluster-robust or matched-set-stratified) SEs. Covariates are pre-specified on clinical grounds rather than selected from the data, and no data-driven model building is performed.

The primary endpoints, index drain indwelling time and antibiotic duration, are time-to-event outcomes subject to censoring and competing events. Removal of the index drain or cessation of antibiotics is the event of interest; death and conversion to open surgery before the event are treated as competing events rather than as censoring. Cumulative incidence functions are estimated for each cohort, and the treatment effect is quantified on the matched sample using a Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model, complemented by cause-specific Cox proportional hazards models, reporting subdistribution or cause-specific HRs with 95% CIs. If the number of events is too small to support these models reliably, results are presented descriptively as cumulative incidence functions and median times with 95% CIs, without formal significance testing. The proportional hazards assumption is assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and log-minus-log plots.

Secondary outcomes are analysed on the matched sample with the same estimation-focused approach. Continuous outcomes (length of hospital and ICU stay and irrigation duration) are summarised and compared as adjusted differences. Binary outcomes (radiological or surgical reintervention, in-hospital mortality and 30-day readmission) as risk differences or ORs with 95% CIs and complication severity (Clavien-Dindo) descriptively or, where feasible, by dichotomising major complications (grade ≥III vs <III). Heavily parameterised multivariable models are avoided given the sample size. Pre-specified subgroup analyses by indication category are reported as exploratory and interpreted with caution in view of limited power.

Crude (unadjusted) outcomes are reported alongside the propensity score-adjusted estimates for transparency. Pre-specified sensitivity analyses include the alternative k:1 matching scheme described above and, where the data permit, inverse-probability-of-treatment weighting with stabilised weights as an alternative to matching.

Missing data patterns will be described. If the proportion of missing values for key variables is low (eg, <5%–10%), analyses will primarily be conducted using complete cases. If missingness is substantial and plausibly at random, multiple imputation by chained equations may be used in sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of estimates. All tests will be two-sided with a significance level of α=0.05. Given the exploratory nature of the study, no formal adjustment for multiple testing will be performed. Secondary and exploratory analyses will be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

All statistical analyses are performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (V.29.0 or higher).

Ethics and dissemination

The study has received positive ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Heidelberg University’s Medical Faculty (S-520/2025, 18 November 2025). As it uses pseudonymised routine clinical data without additional patient contact or deviation from standard care, individual informed consent is waived per national regulations and GDPR. All data management adheres to institutional policies and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and presentations at national/international scientific meetings. No individual-level patient data will be publicly shared.

Discussion

Risk–benefit-assessment

As a non-interventional study leveraging a predefined, independent change in institutional practice, the project does not introduce any study-specific procedures, randomisation or deviations from routine clinical care. All patients receive the irrigation strategy (continuous saline or continuous HOCl-based irrigation) that is in place during their respective treatment period. Because both strategies use the same continuous high-volume irrigation modality, the cohorts differ only in the irrigation solution. No additional risks arise from study participation.

The irrigation solution used in the prospective cohort (Granudacyn) is a CE-marked wound irrigation solution approved for intraperitoneal use. Continuous irrigation via percutaneous drainages is an established clinical technique and no increased risk of adverse events is expected compared with continuous saline irrigation. Potential procedure-related risks (eg, drainage obstruction, discomfort, skin irritation or need for drainage exchange) are inherent to routine care and not influenced by study participation.

In summary, the study addresses a relevant evidence gap in irrigation strategies for intra-abdominal collections, while isolating the irrigation solution by holding the irrigation modality constant across cohorts. Its findings are intended as preliminary, hypothesis-generating effect-size and safety estimates to inform the design and sample size of future multicentre randomised trials, rather than as definitive evidence of efficacy. Important limitations follow from the design: the single-centre, non-randomised comparison with a non-concurrent (historical) saline control; the heterogeneity of underlying indications, addressed by restrictive inclusion criteria, pre-specified indication strata and propensity-score adjustment; and the modest sample, which yields wide CIs and leaves residual or unmeasured confounding possible. Given the minimal incremental risk and the potential to inform routine care, the risk–benefit balance is favourable.
