Evidence‐Based Nursing Management During Mavacamten Therapy in an 89‐Year‐Old Patient With Symptomatic Hypertrophic Obstructive Cardiomyopathy: A Case Report
Authors: Feifei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn older patients receiving mavacamten, a reduction in NT‐proBNP levels may coexist with persistent severe LVOTO. Nurses should interpret this biomarker–echocardiography discordance as a safety signal and maintain structured cardiovascular surveillance rather than assuming treatment success based solely on biomarker improvement.Keywords: 80 and over aged, case reports, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Mavacamten, nursing care

Introduction

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is a common inherited myocardial disease characterized by unexplained left ventricular hypertrophy [1, 2]. In obstructive HCM, dynamic left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO) may cause exertional chest tightness, dyspnoea, palpitations, syncope, mitral regurgitation, and heart failure symptoms [1, 2]. Contemporary guidelines recommend comprehensive history taking, physical examination, family history assessment, electrocardiography, and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) for diagnostic evaluation and longitudinal follow‐up [1, 2].

Mavacamten is a selective cardiac myosin inhibitor that reduces excessive actin‐myosin cross‐bridge formation, thereby alleviating myocardial hypercontractility and left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO) [3, 4]. Clinical trials have shown that mavacamten improves symptoms, functional capacity, left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) gradients, and cardiac biomarkers in selected adults with symptomatic obstructive HCM [3, 5]. Current guidelines and prescribing information recommend initiating mavacamten only in patients with preserved LVEF. Regular clinical and echocardiographic monitoring is required because excessive reduction in contractility may lead to systolic dysfunction or heart failure [1, 6].

Older patients with obstructive HCM frequently present with multimorbidity, polypharmacy, frailty, impaired mobility, malnutrition risk, anxiety, and reduced physiological reserve [7, 8, 9, 10]. These factors underscore the importance of comprehensive nursing assessment, early recognition of clinical deterioration, patient education, and coordinated rehabilitation planning. This case report was prepared in accordance with the CARE reporting guidelines. It describes the clinical course and evidence‐based nursing management of an 89‐year‐old woman treated with mavacamten for obstructive HCM [11].

Case Presentation

Patient Information

An 89‐year‐old woman was admitted to the geriatric cardiovascular ward of a tertiary hospital in China on November 27, 2024, with recurrent chest tightness for 13 years, with worsening symptoms over the past 5 months. Thirteen years prior to admission, she developed chest tightness after long‐distance walking or stair climbing. The discomfort was mainly located in the mid‐to‐lower retrosternal region and was described as a pressure‐like sensation. An outpatient electrocardiogram obtained at that time showed ST‐T changes, and coronary artery disease was suspected. The available record did not document the exact frequency, duration, or treatment response of that first episode.

Symptoms recurred in 2013. Coronary angiography showed no coronary artery abnormality, and left ventriculography obstructive HCM. She subsequently received long‐term metoprolol, with fewer recorded chest‐tightness episodes, although the exact frequency was not available. Five months before the current admission, exertional chest tightness recurred despite ongoing medical therapy at another hospital, prompting admission for further evaluation.

Her medical history included primary hypertension, carotid atherosclerosis, abdominal aortic atherosclerosis, lower‐limb arterial atherosclerosis, intestinal flora imbalance, osteoporosis, anemia, previous breast tumor resection, rectal polyp with bleeding, vertebral‐artery‐type cervical spondylosis, hyperuricaemia, and chronic constipation. She had no known drug allergy. She reported no known family history of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or sudden cardiac death.

Clinical Findings and Systematic Nursing Assessment

On admission, the patient's temperature was 36.4°C, pulse rate was 60 beats/min, respiratory rate was 18 breaths/min, and blood pressure was 114/63 mmHg. The patient weighed 52 kg and was 154 cm tall, corresponding to a body mass index of 21.9 kg/m2. During hospitalization, the patient's heart rate ranged from 50 to 70 beats/min, with occasional episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, while blood pressure remained between 130 and 150/70 mmHg.

General and Functional Assessment

The patient was conscious and cooperative. Activities of daily living were evaluated using the Barthel Index. The score was 40, indicating severe dependence in daily activities [12]. The nursing fall‐risk assessment indicated that the patient was at high risk of falling. Identified fall‐related risk factors included advanced age, known osteoporosis, chronic low back pain, impaired activity tolerance, cardiovascular symptoms, and intermittent arrhythmia.

Cardiovascular Assessment

The patient reported recurrent exertional chest tightness before admission. No peripheral oedema was observed or documented on admission. Jugular venous pressure was not elevated. Cardiac examination revealed no obvious precordial abnormalities. Heart rate was approximately 60 beats/min with a regular rhythm during bedside assessments performed on admission and again on December 1. A grade 3/6 harsh systolic murmur was audible at the third and fourth left intercostal spaces along the sternal border. No abnormal peripheral vascular findings were noted. A 15‐lead electrocardiogram obtained during the current admission demonstrated a heart rate of 63 beats/min, sinus rhythm, ST‐T changes, and left ventricular hypertrophy. The 15‐lead recording was obtained according to the local electrocardiography protocol and included the standard 12 leads plus additional chest leads. The original tracing was available for review within the hospital information system but could not be exported for submission. Holter monitoring was not performed during this admission. Baseline NT‐proBNP was 8164 pg/mL. Baseline troponin T was not available in the submitted case material. Respiratory rate remained between 18 and 20 breaths/min, and bilateral breath sounds were clear without dry or moist rales.

Nutrition and Gastrointestinal Assessment

The patient scored ten on the Mini Nutritional Assessment–Short Form (MNA‐SF), indicating a risk of malnutrition [13]. Chronic constipation was present. The institutional nursing aspiration‐risk screen recorded grade II risk, corresponding to moderate risk in local nursing documentation. No coughing during meals, choking episode, aspiration pneumonia, or formal swallowing‐test result was documented in the available record. Therefore, aspiration precautions for the patient were implemented as geriatric safety measures according to the institutional nursing risk assessment rather than because of confirmed dysphagia or HOCM itself. The patient also reported chronic low back pain, with a visual analogue scale score of three. This was considered a mobility and fall‐risk factor rather than a manifestation of HOCM.

Psychological and Person‐Centered Assessment

Anxiety and depressive symptoms were evaluated using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder‐7 (GAD‐7) scale and the Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9). The scores were 12 and 13, respectively, indicating moderate anxiety symptoms and moderate depressive symptoms [14, 15]. The patient reported avoiding physical activity because of fear of chest discomfort and concern about falling. Individualized nursing goals were established through a person‐centered approach, focusing on symptom safety, confidence during assisted mobilization, and discharge preparedness.

Diagnostic Assessment and Differential Diagnosis

TTE performed on May 10, 2024 was consistent with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and showed severe LVOTO, left ventricular diastolic dysfunction, severe mitral regurgitation, aortic valve degeneration with mild regurgitation, and trace pericardial effusion.

TTE on November 28, 2024 showed asymmetric septal hypertrophy and persistent severe LVOTO with preserved LVEF. Measurements retrieved from the institutional electronic health record (EHR) system included: left atrial diameter of 42 mm, interventricular septal thickness of 19 mm, left ventricular posterior wall thickness 16 mm, left ventricular end‐diastolic diameter 46 mm, LVEF 65%, LVOT peak velocity 4.1 m/s, and estimated peak LVOT gradient 67 mmHg. Mitral regurgitation area was approximately 4.9 cm2, aortic regurgitation area 1.4 cm2, tricuspid regurgitation area 2.5 cm2, and estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure 27 mmHg.

The principal differential diagnoses included progression of ischaemic heart disease, valvular heart disease with heart failure, hypertensive left ventricular hypertrophy, and symptomatic obstructive HCM. Ischaemic heart disease was considered less likely as the primary explanation because previous coronary angiography showed no coronary artery abnormality. Valvular regurgitation and hypertension may also have contributed to the patient's symptoms. However, the combination of asymmetric septal hypertrophy, severe dynamic LVOTO, preserved LVEF, a systolic murmur, electrocardiographic evidence of left ventricular hypertrophy, and the previous ventriculographic diagnosis strongly supported symptomatic obstructive HCM. The ST–T changes described in the patient's medical history referred to an outpatient ECG performed 13 years before admission, whereas the 15‐lead ECG described above was obtained during the current hospitalization. Holter monitoring was not performed during this admission; therefore, no dynamic electrocardiographic data were available.

Therapeutic Intervention

After repeat clinical assessment and repeat echocardiography demonstrated no major change from baseline and confirmed preserved LVEF, mavacamten was initiated at 2.5 mg orally once daily on December 1, 2024 [1, 6]. The dose remained unchanged at 2.5 mg once daily throughout hospitalization and the early follow‐up period, with no dose adjustment recorded. Long‐term metoprolol therapy had been used before admission but was discontinued during hospitalization. Sacubitril/valsartan (Entresto) was administered during hospitalization as part of the patient's standard cardiovascular management. Additional inpatient medications documented in the medication administration records included therapies for gastrointestinal disease, osteoporosis, and other cardiovascular comorbidities. Evidence‐Based Nursing Management

Cardiovascular surveillance throughout mavacamten therapy was the highest nursing priority. Nurses performed regular assessments of chest tightness, dyspnoea, dizziness, palpitations, heart rate, cardiac rhythm, blood pressure, respiratory status, peripheral oedema, fatigue, and activity tolerance. Serial NT‐proBNP and troponin T measurements were obtained as prescribed. Because mavacamten may reduce LVEF and lead to systolic dysfunction, nurses were instructed to promptly report hypotension, bradycardia, dyspnoea, peripheral oedema, syncope, or reduced exercise tolerance. Serial echocardiography was performed to evaluate changes in LVEF and LVOTO [1, 6].

Activity management and fall‐prevention strategies were individualized according to the patient's symptoms, blood pressure, heart rate, and fatigue level. The patient received assistance with position changes, toileting, and early ambulation. Frequently used items were placed within easy reach, and non‐slip footwear, adequate lighting, call‐bell education, and environmental safety measures were provided. Mobilization was discontinued immediately if chest tightness, dizziness, palpitations, marked fatigue, or shortness of breath developed.

Nutrition, bowel, aspiration‐risk, pain, and psychological care were delivered as geriatric safety measures. Nurses assessed dietary intake, stool frequency, hydration status, pain, anxiety, and depressive symptoms. Nurses emphasized dietary recommendations that promoted adequate energy and protein intake, fiber‐rich foods when tolerated, and fluid intake appropriate for the patient's cardiovascular condition. Aspiration precautions for the patient were implemented according to the institutional nursing risk assessment and the patient's functional status rather than because of confirmed dysphagia or HOCM itself. Discharge education included medication adherence, recognition of warning symptoms, fall prevention, nutritional support, bowel management, and scheduled follow‐up echocardiography.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The patient reported a marked improvement in exertional chest tightness by December 6, 2024. Serial NT‐proBNP levels decreased from 8164 pg/mL at baseline to 4174 pg/mL on December 10, 2302 pg/mL on December 17, and 1472 pg/mL on January 17, 2025. Serial troponin T levels were 32.3 pg/mL, 34.0 pg/mL, and 27.1 pg/mL on the corresponding follow‐up dates. No falls, aspiration events, overt heart failure exacerbation, or drug‐related intolerance were documented during hospitalization. Despite improvement in cardiac biomarkers, severe LVOTO persisted despite improvement in NT‐proBNP levels. Continued outpatient follow‐up with serial symptom assessment, NT‐proBNP measurement, LVEF evaluation, and echocardiographic assessment of the LVOT gradient was therefore recommended.

Patient Perspective

The patient reported that chest tightness became less frequent after initiation of mavacamten and that she felt more confident performing daily activities. She considered the individualized nursing care, including assistance with safe mobilization, medication education, dietary guidance, and emotional support, to be beneficial in improving her sense of safety during hospitalization. Overall, she expressed satisfaction with both the treatment process and the nursing care received.

Discussion

This case illustrates the complexity of managing an older patient with symptomatic obstructive HCM during mavacamten therapy. Initiation of mavacamten was considered clinically appropriate because the patient had symptomatic obstructive HCM with preserved LVEF and met the current guideline recommendations for treatment [1, 2, 6]. The observed reduction in NT‐proBNP together with improvement in chest tightness was consistent with the expected therapeutic response reported in previous clinical trials [3, 5]. However, serial TTE still showed severe LVOTO, with peak gradients of approximately 67 mmHg at baseline, 68 mmHg on December 10, and 85 mmHg on December 23. This discordance highlighted the importance of interpreting biomarker changes together with serial echocardiographic findings rather than relying on biomarkers alone when evaluating treatment response, particularly in older patients receiving mavacamten.

From a nursing perspective, cardiovascular surveillance remained the highest nursing priority throughout hospitalization. Fall prevention, nutritional support, bowel management, aspiration precautions, pain management, and psychological support remained important components of comprehensive geriatric nursing care. However, these interventions were integrated into an overall nursing strategy centered on continuous cardiovascular surveillance because mavacamten directly alters myocardial contractility.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this report describes a single patient with a relatively short follow‐up period. Second, Holter monitoring and formal swallowing assessment were not performed. Third, although the original 15‐lead ECG was available within the hospital information system, it could not be exported for publication. Longer‐term follow‐up is needed to evaluate dose adjustment, sustained symptom improvement, long‐term LVEF safety, changes in LVOTO, functional recovery, and adverse events.

Conclusion

In this 89‐year‐old woman with obstructive HCM, mavacamten therapy was associated with early improvement in symptoms and a reduction in NT‐proBNP levels; however, severe LVOTO persisted on serial echocardiography. Improvement in NT‐proBNP alone should not be interpreted as evidence of treatment success. Instead, biomarker findings should always be interpreted alongside serial echocardiographic findings, clinical symptoms, functional status, and comprehensive geriatric assessment during follow‐up.
