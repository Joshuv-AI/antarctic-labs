Postoperative Complications Following Spine Surgery in Myasthenia Gravis Patients: A Propensity-Matched National Database Analysis
Authors: Daniel Rusu, Aidan R. Lindgren, Mirbahador Athari, Maya S. Abu-Zahra, Niels Mohty, Marc A. Abdou, Joshua Davood, Jeffrey C. Wang, Raymond J. Hah, Ram K. Alluri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Study DesignRetrospective National Cohort Study.ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate complication profiles in myasthenia gravis (MG) versus non-MG patients across four routine spine surgeries: lumbar interbody fusion (LIF), lumbar decompression (LD), anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF), and posterior cervical decompression and fusion (PCDF).MethodsFrom 2010 to 2023, all LIF, LD, ACDF, or PCDF patients were queried from the PearlDiver Mariner Database using ICD-9/10 and CPT procedure codes. MG patients with prior thymectomy were excluded. Nearest-neighbor 3:1 propensity score matching based on age, sex, and comorbidity burden was performed. Postoperative complication rates were assessed during index hospitalization and 90 days postoperatively.ResultsAmong those included, 829, 1198, 527, and 129 MG patients underwent LIF, LD, ACDF, and PCDF, respectively. Understandably, MG patients had elevated rates of myasthenic crisis (5.4-8.5%) across all four cohorts at both time points. During initial hospital stay, MG patients had increased rates of pneumonia (4.0% vs 2.3%, p=0.017) and respiratory failure (4.9% vs 3.3%, p=0.016) following LIF and LD, respectively. Conversely, ACDF MG patients had decreased rates of CVA (0.9% vs 2.7%, p=0.028) and AKI (1.3% vs 3.5%, p=0.017). Similar trends were observed 90 days postoperatively. Length of stay was comparable across cohorts.ConclusionsMyasthenia gravis patients undergoing routine spine surgery face elevated risk of respiratory complications postoperatively. Other systemic complications rates were comparable following matching suggesting complication profiles may be surgery-specific rather than uniformly increased with MG. Appropriate preoperative optimization and multidisciplinary perioperative management allows for safe spine surgery in MG patients.

Introduction

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is a chronic autoimmune neuromuscular disorder with an estimated prevalence between 150 to 200 cases per million persons. 1 MG is characterized by fluctuating muscle weakness and increased perioperative risk, particularly of respiratory, infectious, and cardiovascular complications.1-3 Prior research shows MG patients undergoing major surgery experience higher rates of postoperative complications attributable to immunosuppressive therapy such as infection, anemia, pneumonia, respiratory failure, rehospitalization, and generalized muscle weakness.4-6 However, published analyses evaluating the impact of MG on outcomes after myasthenia-related, general orthopaedic, or spine procedures are limited, with existing data largely confined to case reports and non-comparative cohorts.5,7 Rare case reports address postoperative complications in MG patients following anterior cervical spine surgery, anecdotally noting increased dysphagia and infection risk due to prolonged steroid or immunosuppressant use. 7 Furthermore, while studies have explored the impact of MG on anesthetic management, they have largely focused on anesthesia outcomes rather than perioperative surgical outcomes.8,9

The present study aims to systematically analyze complication profiles in MG versus non-MG patients across four routine spine operations—lumbar interbody fusion (LIF), lumbar decompression (LD), anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF), and posterior cervical decompression and fusion (PCDF)—using the highly powered national claims dataset PearlDiver database. By matching cohorts on key demographic and comorbidity characteristics, the study aims to investigate whether MG independently predicts increased risk of complications in the perioperative period.

Methods

Data Source and Study Population

Patients were identified using the PearlDiver Mariner Database (PearlDiver Technologies, Colorado Springs, CO, USA). All patients who underwent the most common spine surgeries between January 1, 2010 and April 30, 2023 were identified. PearlDiver is a national insurance claims database with approximately 170 million deidentified patient records. PearlDiver provides patient demographics and descriptive characteristics. Due to this study’s retrospective nature and the use of anonymized data, institutional review board approval was waived.

Identification of Study Cohorts

Patients who underwent four of the most common spine surgeries between 2010 and 2023 were identified using ICD-9/10 and CPT codes (Supplementary Table 1). Patients were categorized as follows: 1-level LIF, 1-level LD, 1- and 2-level ACDF, or 1- and 2-level PCDF. All 4 cohorts were further subdivided for patients with a diagnosis of myasthenia gravis prior to surgery without a prior thymectomy, using ICD-9/10 and CPT codes (Supplementary Table 1). MG patients with prior thymectomy were excluded, as thymectomy is a definitive treatment for MG and leads to significant symptomatic improvement and remission, which would lead to heterogeneity in disease severity. Patients were included if they had active records for at least 90 days. Patients with less than 90 days of follow-up were excluded. Surgical indications for trauma, infection, or malignancy were excluded. Demographics, comorbidities, length of stay (LOS), and discharge to a skilled nursing facility (SNF) were analyzed between myasthenia gravis and non-myasthenia gravis patients across all four cohorts.

Outcomes

This study aimed to assess the difference in complication rates between myasthenia gravis and non-myasthenia gravis patients across all four surgical cohorts. Complications included cerebrovascular accident (CVA), pneumonia (PNA), respiratory failure, myocardial infarction (MI), acute kidney injury (AKI), deep vein thrombosis (DVT), anemia, pulmonary embolism (PE), malignant hyperthermia, myasthenic crisis, and sepsis. These complications were assessed during hospital stay and at 90 days postoperatively. Rates of surgical site infections (SSI), hardware infection, mechanical complications, and readmissions were also collected within 90 days following surgery.

Statistical Analysis

Welch’s t-tests and Pearson chi-squared tests of independence were utilized to compare continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Nearest-neighbor propensity score matching was performed at a 3:1 ratio with a caliper of 0.2 across all 4 cohorts to match non-myasthenia gravis patients with myasthenia gravis patients. Patients were matched based on age, sex, and Elixhauser Comorbidity Index (ECI). Cohorts were considered appropriately matched if standardized mean differences (SMD) <0.1 (Supplementary Table 2). A 3:1 propensity match ratio was selected to increase precision due to the relatively small sample size of the MG cohort compared with a large control group. Following propensity score matching, univariate logistic regression was performed to assess if myasthenia gravis was a risk factor for rates of complications and readmission rates during the hospital stay and at 90 days postoperatively. Odds ratios (OR) of univariate analysis was reported. Statistical analyses were conducted using Rstudio (Version 4.4.2) within the PearlDiver Mariner Database program. A p-value <0.05 indicates statistical significance.

Patient Demographics

Prior to matching, MG patients were older and had higher comorbidity burden when compared to non-myasthenia gravis patients in each cohort (all p<0.001). Myasthenia gravis patients had a longer hospital stay, specifically after LD (3.548 vs 2.874 days, p<0.001). Further, they had higher rates of discharge to a SNF following LIF (9.2% vs 4.6%), LD (4.8% vs 1.6%), and ACDF (3.0% vs 1.2%) (all p<0.001). In LIF, myasthenia gravis patients had lower rates of tobacco use, but higher rates of other comorbidities such as rheumatoid arthritis, diabetes, congestive heart failure, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease, coronary artery disease, preoperative steroid use, and osteoporosis (all p<0.05). These trends were mirrored in MG patients who underwent LD, ACDF, and PCDF as well (Table 1). However, following appropriate 1:3 propensity score matching, MG patients had comparable LOS to controls across all spine surgeries. MG patients only had increased rates of discharge to SNFs following LD (4.76% vs 3.28%, p=0.023), with comparable rates for the other procedures.

Complication Rates

Following appropriate 1:3 propensity score matching, complication rates following each surgery were assessed during the initial hospital stay and within 90 days postoperatively. During the initial hospital stay following LIF, MG patients had higher rates of pneumonia (4.0% vs 2.3%, p=0.017; OR: 1.736, p=0.013) and myasthenic crisis (5.5% vs 0%, p<0.001). These trends of increased rates of pneumonia and myasthenic crisis were consistent at 90 days postoperative; however MG patients also had decreased rates of MI (0.5% vs 1.7%, p=0.014; OR: 0.276, p=0.014). Additionally MG patients had greater likelihood of sepsis (OR: 1.502, p=0.047). Discussion

This study aimed to assess complication rates in myasthenia gravis patients following common spine surgeries including 1-level lumbar interbody fusion, 1-level lumbar decompression, 1- and 2-level anterior cervical discectomy and fusion, and 1- and 2-level posterior cervical decompression and fusion using a large national database and propensity-matched analysis. Patients with MG had greater risk of pneumonia, sepsis, and respiratory failure following LIF and LD, respectively, but decreased rates of MI, CVA, and AKI following LIF and ACDF. These decreased complication rates are likely multifactorial and may be explained due to increased monitoring during the perioperative period; however, possible selection bias and residual confounding may lead to such an artifact despite rigorous matching. Understandably, myasthenia gravis patients had increased rates of postoperative myasthenic crisis due to the physiological burden of surgery, with the majority presenting during the initial hospital admission. Of note, length of stay was similar across all spine surgeries for MG patients, and discharge to SNFs was likewise comparable, with the exception of LD. To our knowledge, this is the first study using a national dataset that assesses the complication profile of routine spine surgeries in myasthenia gravis patients.

Postoperative complications in MG patients have been documented within other surgical specialties.4,5,10-13 Using Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database, Chang et al. analyzed 2290 MG patients following major inpatient surgeries, defined as operations that required hospitalization for at least 1 day. The authors demonstrated that myasthenia gravis patients had higher risk of postoperative pneumonia (OR: 2.09), septicemia (OR: 1.31), postoperative bleeding (OR: 1.71), and overall complications (OR: 1.70). 4 These findings broadly align with the present work, demonstrating increased risk of respiratory and infectious complications, while further refining our understanding of the distinct complication profiles of different spine surgeries–which routinely require overnight hospital admissions–in MG patients. Further, complication rates in myasthenia gravis patients following orthopaedic surgeries have previously been studied, specifically in total knee arthroplasty (TKA) and total hip arthroplasty (THA).6,14 In 2021, Sherman et al. assessed complications rates following TKA or THA in MG patients utilizing the PearlDiver national database. Sherman et al. reported that patients with myasthenia gravis had increased rates of CVA, pneumonia, respiratory failure, sepsis, MI, acute renal failure, anemia, and DVT at 90 days post-discharge. 6 These findings demonstrate that MG patients experience increased complication rates; however, our study may provide a more nuanced view due to propensity score matching of the control group for age, sex, and comorbidity burden (ECI). This is corroborated by both studies reporting increased rates of numerous comorbidities in MG patients. Although arthroplasty and spine surgery differ in technique and complication profiles, these findings may suggest that patients with myasthenia gravis may not experience as broadly elevated perioperative risk as previously assumed, and may rather be surgery-specific. As a result, it is important that myasthenia gravis patients undergoing spine surgery have appropriate preoperative counseling, increased clinical vigilance for respiratory complications in the perioperative period, and multidisciplinary collaboration with neurology subspecialists. Risk mitigation strategies include pulmonary function tests (PFTs) to assess for respiratory insufficiency and preoperative corticosteroids for stable myasthenia gravis, or plasmapheresis or IVIG in more severe cases.

Myasthenia gravis leads to additional unique challenges during spine surgery, as documented by several case reports.7,8 Li et al. documented a case of a 62 year old male with long-standing myasthenia gravis who underwent anterior cervical corpectomy of C4, discectomy of C5-6, and fusion of C3-6. Twenty days postoperatively, the patient developed worsening dysphagia with radiographs demonstrating vertebral fracture, implant extrusion, and cage sliding, necessitating revision surgery. The authors stated that no definitive etiology was determined, however, long-term steroid-induced osteoporosis may have led to graft failure. 7 This case demonstrates the potential effect of long-term MG treatment on bone density, which has broad implications for this patient population undergoing any orthopaedic procedure. Across all spine surgeries, MG patients had higher reported rates of preoperative osteoporosis, an important consideration during preoperative planning. Although the present study did not find evidence of hardware complications following spine surgery, steroid-induced osteoporosis in long-standing MG may impact rates of subsequent revision rates at longer-term follow-up. Further, Swain et al. elaborates on the delicate balance of lumbar spine surgery in myasthenia gravis patients, from an anesthetic perspective with 2 successful cases. 8 The authors explain the critical balance during surgery of avoiding use of muscle relaxants in MG patients to avoid precipitating respiratory failure, while maintaining minimal patient movement to avoid major complications intraoperatively. To overcome this challenge, intraoperative strategies such as significantly reduced muscle relaxant dosage and using sevoflurane and propofol, due to their rapid clearance, have been proposed.15,16

The most severe life-threatening manifestation of myasthenia gravis is myasthenic crisis, which occurs in 15-20% of patients with myasthenia gravis. 17 Observed rates of myasthenic crisis (5.4-8.5%) in the present study align with a recent review demonstrating a postoperative myasthenic crisis of 10% in the general surgery literature. 18 Myasthenic crisis is characterized by an acute worsening of MG symptoms with respiratory muscle failure requiring noninvasive or invasive ventilatory support, usually due to physiological stressors such as medications, surgery, or infections. Treatment of myasthenic crisis includes admission to the intensive care unit, frequent vital capacity monitoring, treatment of any precipitating factors, plasmapheresis or IVIG, and glucocorticoids. 17 Specifically, postoperative myasthenic crisis is defined as delay in extubation beyond 48 hours following surgery or the need for hospitalization to monitor respiratory status or severe dysphagia.17,19-22 Importantly, this definition may lead to discrepancies between reported rates of respiratory failure and myasthenic crisis, as cases of respiratory failure may be attributed to alternative etiologies. This may be particularly relevant following ACDF, where the highest rates of myasthenic crisis were observed and where postoperative airway compression represents a serious complication capable of causing respiratory compromise independent of myasthenia gravis exacerbation. Further, surgery acting as a trigger for myasthenic crisis may unmask underlying myasthenia gravis in previously undiagnosed patients, as observed in the present study’s ACDF cohort where 1 patient in the non-MG cohort had a diagnosis of myasthenic crisis during the initial hospital stay. Accordingly, it is important that myasthenia gravis remain on a spine surgeon’s differential whenever a patient experiences respiratory insufficiency in the postoperative setting.

Limitations

The study has several limitations worth noting that are inherent to a retrospective insurance claims database. First, this database relies on administrative coding which is susceptible to human error in reporting. As a result, this may lead to over or underreporting of complications. Furthermore, use of CPT codes in insurance claims databases to identify spine surgery does not include claims during the initial hospital stay to assess for complications. However, this was mitigated in two ways by including ICD-9 and ICD-10 procedure codes, which include such in-hospital claims, and by assessing complication rates at 90 days. This study does not include all spine surgeries, but assessed some routinely performed operations to allow for an adequate sample size. Additionally, this database does not contain or account for MG-specific severity markers or baseline pulmonary function which may increase heterogeneity. Lastly, national databases such as PearlDiver do not contain granular detail on operations by specific vertebral level, which may introduce other confounding factors.

Conclusion

In this national propensity-matched analysis, myasthenia gravis patients had increased risk of respiratory complications and myasthenic crisis following routine spine surgeries including 1-level lumbar interbody fusion, 1-level lumbar decompression, 1- and 2-level ACDF, and 1- and 2-level PCDF. Patients with myasthenia gravis had comparable rates for other systemic complications postoperatively following propensity matching for comorbidities. This suggests that myasthenia gravis might not uniformly confer increased perioperative risk but may be surgery-specific. With appropriate preoperative optimization, perioperative vigilance, and multidisciplinary management, spine surgery is feasible in patients with myasthenia gravis. However, preoperative counseling regarding the postoperative respiratory risk profile and a standardized perioperative protocol including preoperative pulmonary function assessment, minimizing neuromuscular blocking agent use intraoperatively, and postoperative monitoring are recommended to optimize outcomes in this high-risk population.
