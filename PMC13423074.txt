Environmental exposome study for prostate cancer in elderly men from Western China: preliminary findings from a case–control study in the WeEndPd cohort

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo investigate the association between exposure to specific endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs), air pollutants and the risk of localised prostate cancer (PCa) and to evaluate the combined effect of mixed EDC exposures.DesignA case–control study.SettingSecondary care; a single tertiary hospital in Western China.ParticipantsA total of 580 patients with histologically confirmed localised PCa who underwent radical prostatectomy between May 2021 and June 2023 were included as cases. They were matched 1:2 with 1160 cancer-free controls from the same cohort on age (±5 years) and date of biological sample collection. Key exclusion criteria were a history of other malignancies, androgen deprivation therapy prior to surgery or acute urinary infection.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was the odds of PCa associated with individual and mixed exposures. Primary exposures were urinary concentrations of nine bisphenols (BPs) and nine phthalate metabolites (quantified via high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry) and estimated ambient air pollution exposure (sulphur dioxide (SO2) and PM₂.₅) based on geocoded residential addresses.ResultsA total of 580 cases and 1160 controls were included in the final analysis. Exposure to the highest quartile of sulphur dioxide (SO2) over 5 years (9.37–28.79 µg/m3) was significantly associated with PCa, yielding an OR of 1.65 (95% CI 1.08 to 2.72; p<0.001) compared with the lowest quartile (3.61–6.89 µg/m3). Urinary concentrations of multiple phthalate metabolites (MECPP, MEHHP and MEHP) and BPs (bisphenol A (BPA) and BPZ) were significantly higher in cases (all p<0.05). Weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression analysis demonstrated a significant positive association between the mixture of EDCs and PCa risk (WQS OR=1.26, 95% CI 1.20 to 1.33; p<0.001).ConclusionsExposure to SO2 and specific EDCs (both individually and as a mixture) is significantly associated with localised PCa.Trial registration numberChiCTR1900024623.Keywords: PUBLIC HEALTH, Prospective studies, Prostatic Neoplasms, Particulate Matter, Prostate

Introduction

Prostate diseases pose significant health risks and adversely affect the quality of life for elderly men. The most common prostate diseases include prostate cancer (PCa) and benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH). PCa is particularly concerning, as it stands as the second most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide and ranks fifth in cancer-related mortality among men globally.1 In the USA, the American Cancer Society estimates that 299 010 new cases of PCa will be diagnosed in 2024, making it the most prevalent cancer among men; with a projected 35 250 deaths.2

In recent years, researchers have increasingly realised that diseases are not only related to a single environmental factor, but also closely related to the entire environmental exposure spectrum, the Exposome.3 The Exposome concept covers all environmental exposures throughout an individual’s life, including ambient air pollutants, endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs), and other potentially harmful chemicals. Ambient air pollutants are environmental pollutants that have been the focus of research in recent years. They may have a significant impact on the pathogenesis and progression of various diseases. Ambient air pollutants such as particulate matter, bioaerosols and chemical compounds including sulphur dioxide (SO2), nitrogen oxides (NOx) and carbon monoxide (CO) have been extensively studied for their impact on human health.4

EDCs are a group of environmental substances known to interfere with hormone synthesis, metabolism and action pathways, potentially leading to disease development.5 Common EDCs include substances found in plastics, fuels and industrial chemicals. Studies from Europe have detected EDCs such as bisphenols (BPs) and phthalates (PAEs) in human urine and plasma.5 Emerging research suggests that exposure to these EDCs may promote tumour progression or abnormal cell proliferation through various mechanisms. For instance, bisphenol A (BPA), a widely used bisphenol, has been shown to up-regulate pro-cell cycle genes and down-regulate anti-proliferative genes, facilitating tumour growth.6 Some epidemiological studies have attempted to explore the association between EDC exposure and PCa, which found a positive correlation between higher BPA levels and increased PCa incidence.7 However, these studies are limited by small sample sizes, homogeneity and a reliance on retrospective, cross-sectional designs based primarily on self-reported data, which diminishes their evidence-based robustness.

To systematically assess the impact of EDCs and ambient air pollutants on prostate health, we established the West China Exposome Cohort for Environmental Disruptors and Prostatic Diseases (WeEndPd). This ongoing prospective cohort was initiated in May 2021 at West China Hospital, Sichuan University, to investigate the long-term effects of environmental exposome—encompassing EDCs and air pollutants—on the development and progression of prostate diseases, particularly PCa and BPH, among elderly men in Western China. In the WeEndPd, ambient air pollutants including PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NOx, CO and O3 were monitored. Endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) were assessed by measuring nine bisphenol analogues (BPF, BPE, BPA, BPAP, BPZ, BPAF, BPS, BPP and BPB) and nine phthalate metabolites (MEP, MECPP, MEHHP, MiBP, MEOHP, MNBP, MCMHP, MBZP and MEHP), for realistic exposure representativeness and reliability of measurement methods.8 Here, we present preliminary findings from the WeEndPd cohort, highlighting initial insights into the association between environmental pollutants and the occurrence of PCa.

The WeEndPd cohort will conduct cross-sectional analyses of endogenous exposures and ambient pollutants across the spectrum of prostate diseases, based on systematic data and biospecimen collection. Over the long term, through continued disease follow-up and in-depth multiomics profiling, the cohort is designed to establish predictive models for prostate disease risk associated with both endogenous and exogenous pollutants.

Methods

Study design and population

The WeEndPd is an ongoing prospective cohort established in May 2021 at West China Hospital, Sichuan University, and the present study reports enrolment outcomes from May 2021 to December 2024. This cohort is designed to investigate the impact of long-term exposure to EDCs and ambient air pollutants on the development and progression of prostate diseases, particularly PCa and BPH, among elderly men in Western China. The figure 1 shows the design of the study. In the current article, we present the initial results of this cohort based on a case–control analysis within the WeEndPd cohort. The WeEndPd study was registered under ChiCTR1900024623. Data confidentiality and participant privacy are strictly maintained, and all research procedures are designed to minimise any potential risks to participants.

For the PCa group, the research team keep prospectively enrolling patients who underwent surgery in the Department of Urology, West China Hospital, Sichuan University since May 2021. Research team collect the patients’ baseline information and clinical pathological information such as pathological diagnosis, baseline prostate-specific antigen (PSA), clinical and pathological TNM stage, pathological Gleason score and group, preoperative neoadjuvant status, postoperative pathological characteristics.

The participants of the control group came from the ongoing West China Natural Population Cohort study (WCNPCS). More details about the study can be found in previously published papers.9 10 In short, they came from communities of different participating areas in Western China. Participants filled out a questionnaire covering their occupation, lifestyle, symptoms and signs, medical history, etc. They were also invited to participate in a free comprehensive health check-up, which included biometric, blood pressure and pulse. Between these procedures, biosamples were collected from participants for more detailed studies.

Sample collection and handling

For PCa group, after the patient is admitted to the ward and before receiving any form of treatment, the urine is collected from the patient. A polypropylene urine collection tube is used to collect 15 mL of urine from the research subject. The collected urine is placed in an ice box, transported back to the laboratory in the dark and immediately packaged in polypropylene centrifuge tubes, 5 mL per tube and stored in a −80°C refrigerator. A 15 mL pure water blank tube is set up for each batch of urine specimens as a control.

For the control group, urine samples were collected on-site at the community research site. A 15 mL of urine was collected from the subjects using a polypropylene urine collection tube and temporarily stored in the refrigerator at the research site. The urine was transported back to the laboratory in the dark on the same day and immediately packaged in polypropylene centrifuge tubes, 5 mL per tube, and stored in a −80°C refrigerator. In order to avoid the influence of the urine collection device itself on the results, a 15 mL pure water blank tube was set up for each batch of urine specimens as a control.

Environmental exposure assessment

Residential addresses of participants are geocoded using a standardised application programming interface to obtain precise latitude and longitude coordinates. These coordinates were then used to assign daily ambient concentrations of the six monitored air pollutants—PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, SO2, NOx, CO and O₃—to each participant based on their residential location. The pollutant data are sourced from the China High Air Pollutants (CHAP) dataset, which provides high-resolution estimates of pollutant concentrations across China. Bilinear interpolation is employed to estimate individual exposure levels over the 5 years preceding participants’ enrolment, based on the four nearest grid cells surrounding each participant’s residence. At a spatial resolution of 1 km × 1 km, the average daily exposure levels of ambient air pollutants for patients 5 years and 6 months before surgery were determined with the gridded daily concentrations of pollutants and meteorological conditions. This approach has been described in previous research literature.11 The unit for air pollutants is µg/m3. The analysis mainly calculates the average concentration of patients before surgery or the average concentration of the control group in the 5 years and 6 months before enrolment. Generally, the analysis is performed in four groups from low to high concentration, without any other conversion.

The detection and quantification of EDCs in biological samples are conducted using high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS). The primary EDCs of interest include nine kinds of BPs and nine kinds of phthalates. Samples from participants are processed using a standardised protocol that involves enzymatic digestion, solid-phase extraction and precise measurement using HPLC-MS/MS. To account for variability in urine concentration due to factors such as hydration status, EDC concentrations in urine samples are normalised to creatinine levels. The detailed test methods have been described previously in the literature.8 A total of 18 kinds of EDCs were analysed in the current study (PAEs: MEP, MECPP, MEHHP, MiBP, MEOHP, MNBP, MCMHP, MBZP, MEHP; BPs: BPF, BPE, BPA, BPAP, BPZ, BPAF, BPS, BPP and BPB). The pollutant units at the time of measurement were μg/L, adjusted for creatinine and then to μg/g. Logarithmic transformation was used for continuous pollutant exposure data during analysis to reduce the influence of data skewness.

Data management in the WeEndPd study is rigorous and adheres to strict protocols to ensure the highest standards of data integrity. The researchers mainly include urologists from the hospital, environmental toxicologists from the School of Public Health and rigorously trained full-time field staff. Data are subjected to multiple levels of validation, including self-checks by data collectors, double-checks by independent reviewers and random audits to ensure consistency and accuracy. Follow-up procedures of WeEndPd

Participants are followed up regularly to monitor health outcomes and environmental exposures. Contact information, including telephone numbers, is collected at baseline to facilitate follow-up. For patients with PCa, follow-up focuses on monitoring for biochemical or clinical recurrence, using PSA levels and imaging studies as primary indicators. In the control group, the follow-up aims to track changes in health status, environmental exposures and the onset of prostate-related symptoms or conditions.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (V.4.1.2). Baseline characteristics were summarised descriptively: continuous variables were expressed as means (SD), while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages and continuous variables were reported as means with SD. Group comparisons used the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables.

To minimise the impact of multiple confounding factors between the PCa group and the natural population control group, we employed propensity score matching (PSM). The propensity score—representing the probability of being diagnosed with PCa—was calculated for each participant using a multivariable logistic regression model. The model included the following baseline covariates: age, body mass index (BMI), marital status, smoking history, alcohol consumption history and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)). Patients were matched using a 1:2 ratio via the nearest neighbour matching algorithm without replacement. To ensure high-quality matching, a calliper of 0.02 (SD of the logit of the propensity score) was applied.

The association between pollutant exposure and PCa occurrence was evaluated by comparing exposure levels between the PCa group and the control group. Multivariable logistic regression models were employed to assess the relationship between air pollution exposure and the occurrence of PCa. Primary exposures of interest were the median concentrations of ambient air pollutants, assessed over two distinct periods—the 6 months preceding study enrolment and the cumulative average over the preceding 5 years. Models were adjusted for potential confounders, including age, BMI, urban versus rural residency, baseline comorbidities (hypertension and diabetes). Additionally, we conducted a detailed analysis of EDC exposure profiles between the two groups. To explore the relationship between EDC exposure and PCa occurrence, we applied multivariable logistic regression analysis and further employed weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression analyses. This method allowed for the evaluation of cumulative exposure effects by quantifying the joint contribution of multiple pollutants. Each regression model included only one pollutant at a time for analysing air pollutants and EDCs.

WQS regression was employed to evaluate the overall association of the EDC mixture. This method integrates multiple exposures into a single WQS index, where each chemical is assigned a weight reflecting its contribution to the combined effect. Results are presented as the OR for an increase in the WQS index, along with its 95% CI. When performing WQS analysis, we adjusted for the following covariates: age, BMI, place of residence (urban or rural), marital status, smoking history, alcohol consumption history, hypertension and diabetes.

All statistical tests were two sided, and a p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results were considered statistically significant at p<0.05 and were presented as ORs with 95% CIs.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

In the current study, we aimed to reveal the relationship between environmental pollutants, mainly air pollutants and EDCs, and the occurrence of PCa, as a preliminary achievement of the WeEndPd study.

We analysed the eligible PCa patient data collected until June 2023 to report preliminary results from the WeEndPd study. A total of 580 patients with PCa and a 1:2 propensity score matched control group (n=1160) were included in this analysis, and inclusion and exclusion criteria for the control group are detailed in online supplemental figure 2, while the baseline characteristics of the cohort prior to PSM are presented in online supplemental table 1. The baseline characteristics of the two groups are shown in table 1. The baseline characteristics of the PCa group and the control group were largely comparable, with no significant differences in age (mean 67.24 vs 67.11 years; p=0.291), BMI (mean 24.35 vs 24.51; p=0.144), marital status (98.6% vs 97.1% married; p=0.068), smoking history (30.9% vs 27.2%; p=0.128), alcohol history (24.7% vs 28.2%; p=0.132), hypertension (27.7% vs 28.8%; p=0.693), diabetes (13.8% vs 11.3%; p=0.153) and COPD prevalence (12.1% vs 11.0%; p=0.575). However, a significant difference was observed in residence distribution, with a higher proportion of the PCa group residing in urban areas (65.8% vs 57.0%; p<0.01). Association between ambient air pollutants exposure and PCa occurrence

We regarded the data of the first 6 months of enrollment as short-term exposure and the data of 5 years as long-term exposure. We found notable differences in air pollution exposure between patients with PCa and a natural population cohort. SO2 levels were significantly higher in the PCa group compared with the control group, both in short-term (10.17 μg/m2 and 8.88 μg/m2) and long-term (11.67 μg/m2 and 10.53 μg/m2) exposure (p<0.01). CO exposure was also slightly elevated in the PCa group over the 6-month period (p<0.01), although this difference was not observed over 5 years. No significant differences were found for PM2.5, PM10, O3 or NO2 between the two groups, regardless of exposure duration. These results suggest that specific pollutants, particularly SO2, may have a more prominent role in PCa occurrence and progression, warranting further investigation into their potential impact. Online supplemental table 3 showed the detailed distribution of ambient air pollutants data. Online supplemental table 4 presents the baseline characteristic differences between the PCa group and the control group before PSM.

Further, using multivariate logistic regression analysis and adjusting for relevant covariates, we examined the relationship between air pollution and PCa occurrence. For short-term exposure, compared with the lowest quartile of SO2 exposure, the highest quartile showed a statistically significant association with PCa occurrence, distinct from other pollutants (OR: 2.34; 95% CI 1.35 to 3.89; p<0.001). In contrast, exposures to PM2.5, PM10, O3 or NO2 did not display any notable associations with PCa. In the long-term exposure analysis, SO2 remained significantly associated with PCa occurrence, while the other pollutants continued to show no significant links (OR: 1.65, 95% CI 1.08 to 2.72, p<0.001) (table 2).

Association between EDC exposure and PCa occurrence

After removing samples with low quality control, the EDCs exposure spectrum data (mainly BPs and phthalate acid esters (PAEs)) of 249 PCa patient samples and 277 natural elderly male patients were finally included. A total of 18 kinds of EDCs were analysed in the current study (PAEs: MEP, MECPP, MEHHP, MiBP, MEOHP, MNBP, MCMHP, MBZP, MEHP; BPs: BPF, BPE, BPA, BPAP, BPZ, BPAF, BPS, BPP, and BPB).

The comparison of EDCs concentrations between the PCa group and the control group revealed significant differences for several compounds (table 3). For phthalates, MECPP, MEHHP, MEOHP, MBZP and MEHP all showed significantly higher concentrations in the PCa group compared with the control group (p<0.05), and MiBP and MNBP showed significantly lower concentrations in the PCa group (p<0.05). In contrast, concentrations of MCMHP and MEP did not differ significantly between the two groups (p>0.05). Among BPs, BPE and BPA showed higher concentrations in the PCa group, with demonstrating statistical significance (p=0.011 and p=0.026). BPAP and BPF showed no significant differences between the two groups (p>0.05), while BPZ had a significant difference (p=0.001) (table 3). Spearman correlation was used to analyse the correlation of each substance. The results are shown in online supplemental figure 3. There is a significant positive correlation between the PAEs metabolites MEHP, MEHHP, MEOHP and MECPP (correlation coefficient>0, p<0.0001). The coefficient is between 0.71 and 0.97, which is consistent with the homology of these substances. And we found that BPA also has a slightly weaker and significant positive correlation with these substances (p<0.05), indicating a possible correlation between BPA and these PAEs, including similar environmental exposure pathways and metabolic pathways.12 13 In addition, there is also a significant positive correlation between MnBP and MiBP (correlation coefficient>0, p<0.001), which is consistent with the homology of these two substances.

Discussion

Here, we introduced the WeEndPd cohort, a cohort from western China that aims to explore the relationship between environmental exposures and prostate diseases, including PCa and BPH. As preliminary results from this cohort, we specifically analysed data from patients with PCa in the cohort. This study employs a unique and comprehensive approach to elucidate the complex relationship between the environmental exposome—particularly ambient air pollutants and EDCs—and the risk of PCa within the context of the WeEndPd cohort. Previous research on PCa and environmental factors has often examined single exposures in isolation, focusing either on air pollution or EDCs, which may not reflect the multifaceted nature of real-world environmental exposures. By integrating detailed assessments of both air pollutants and EDCs, we significantly advance the understanding of how these environmental factors contribute to PCa. The findings from this study have critical implications for public health, environmental science and clinical practice.

The WeEndPd cohort extends beyond measuring environmental pollution levels by integrating detailed biological and clinical assessments. Biomarkers such as BPs and phthalate concentrations are measured directly from participants’ samples. This approach enables tracing how these pollutants interact with biological processes, which are established pathways leading to carcinogenesis.14 Clinical epidemiological studies on environmental pollutant exposure and PCa are few in number and small in scale, and most studies are retrospectively designed. The research methods are mostly questionnaire surveys, and such studies rarely determine the level of human exposure to pollutants such as EDCs through true mass spectrometry. At present, there is a particular lack of exposure data on the two major types of environmental pollutants, EDCs and air pollutants, for normal community populations and PCa populations from mainland China. As an industrial power, China’s bisphenol consumption increased 2000 times between 2000 and 2014, reaching about 30 000 tons/year.15

Air pollution, while not a new concern in terms of health risks, has traditionally been studied primarily in relation to respiratory and cardiovascular diseases. In fact, researchers have begun to pay attention to the health effects of air pollution on systemic non-respiratory diseases and believe that air pollution can affect a variety of diseases such as breast cancer and reproductive diseases through oxidative stress, immune interference and other factors.16 17 Evidence is mounting that pollutants such as SO2 and NO2, commonly emitted by vehicles and industrial processes, not only exacerbate respiratory issues but also significantly contribute to cancer development.18 The WeEndPd study adds evidence showing that long-term or short exposure to these pollutants correlates with a marked increase in PCa risk, even when exposure remains within legally permissible limits.18

One of the key mechanisms through which air pollution increases cancer risk is the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) by pollutants such as NO2. These ROS can cause extensive cellular damage, including DNA mutations, which are essential triggers in the development of cancer. In prostate tissue, prolonged exposure to ROS not only induces genetic mutations but also creates a chronic inflammatory environment. This sustained inflammation can promote cellular changes that facilitate tumour initiation and progression.19 Furthermore, air pollutants can exacerbate the body’s immune response, leading to a cycle of inflammation that weakens tissue integrity and allows cancerous cells to proliferate more easily.20 The WeEndPd study’s findings align with numerous other studies on air pollution, reinforcing the growing body of evidence that more stringent environmental controls are needed.

Our study found that higher concentrations of several phthalates and bisphenol A were significantly associated with an increased prevalence of PCa. This aligns with numerous other studies that have demonstrated the role of these EDCs in promoting cancer risk through various biological mechanisms. For example, BPA has been shown to bind to oestrogen receptors, including ERα and ERβ, which triggers abnormal cell proliferation and can lead to oncogenic processes in prostate cells.21 22 Androgens play a key role in regulating prostate cell growth and differentiation, and disturbances in this balance have been implicated in cancer development. Phthalates, through their interaction with the androgen receptor (AR), can impair AR function, leading to aberrant gene expression and tumourigenic transformation of prostate cells.23

Moreover, our study used WQS regression to assess cumulative exposure to multiple EDCs and found a significant association between combined EDC exposure and increased risk of PCa. This result suggests that EDC mixtures pose a greater risk than individual exposures alone. This synergistic effect of chemical mixtures is supported by previous research showing that EDCs can collectively disrupt multiple signalling pathways that contribute to carcinogenesis. For example, a research team found that BPA and phthalates promote epithelial–mesenchymal transition, a process that enables cancer cells to become more invasive and metastatic.23 Our findings, which highlight the cumulative risk of multiple EDC exposures, underscore the importance of considering real-world exposure scenarios where individuals are continuously exposed to a complex mix of pollutants. In addition to promoting cancer initiation, EDCs such as BPA can also influence cancer therapy resistance. Studies have shown that BPA exposure can induce resistance to chemotherapeutics, including doxorubicin and cisplatin, by modulating drug efflux pumps and cell survival pathways.6 Current guidelines often focus on individual chemical exposures, but our study demonstrates that the combined effects of EDC mixtures were associated with more substantial risk.

Understanding the role of environmental exposures in PCa development can inform more personalised and preventive approaches to patient care. Clinicians could incorporate environmental exposure histories into routine risk assessments for PCa, particularly for individuals residing in high-pollution areas or with known occupational exposures to harmful chemicals.

This study has several limitations due to methodological and temporal constraints. First, the WeEndPd cohort assesses associations between pollutant exposures and PCa by comparing individuals with PCa and healthy controls; therefore, causal inferences regarding the impact of exposures on PCa development cannot be made. The cohort does, however, conduct regular follow-up of postoperative PSA levels, testosterone levels and androgen deprivation therapy status in patients with PCa. Future analyses will examine the temporal relationship between environmental exposures and PCa progression. Second, the WeEndPd cohort is based in Western China, which may introduce regional bias and limit the generalisability of findings. The cohort includes long-term local residents and patients with PCa predominantly from Western China, and both air pollutant and EDC exposures are specific to this population. Third, ambient air pollution estimates were derived from the CHAP dataset, which may not fully reflect individual-level exposure. The CHAP data reflect outdoor concentrations and do not account for indoor influences such as household fuel use, air conditioning, air purifiers or ventilation. Moreover, exposure heterogeneity within cities and time spent away from the residential address further contribute to exposure misclassification. In subsequent follow-ups, we plan to implement time-weighted exposure assignment to better capture changes in residential location. Fourth, although the healthy controls were defined by a PSA level<4 ng/mL, this criterion may not entirely exclude individuals with latent PCa. To mitigate this possibility, we further excluded those with a family history of PCa. Leveraging the WCNPCS, in which the control participants undergo annual health follow-up, we will exclude any individuals from the control group who are newly diagnosed with PCa in subsequent progression analyses.

Conclusion

The WeEndPd cohort study provides evidence that environmental exposures, particularly to air pollutants (SO2) and EDCs, were associated with PCa.
