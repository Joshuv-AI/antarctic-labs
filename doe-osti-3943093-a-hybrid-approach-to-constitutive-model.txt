ABSTRACT
This report summarizes the work performed during a three-year LDRD project that focused on
developing hybrid constitutive models that combine conventional phenomenological models with
physics-augmented machine learning components for improved predictive accuracy in thermo-
mechanical simulations. We review major technical innovations including novel neural network
architectures that embed physical constraints by design, thermodynamically consistent frameworks
for thermoviscoplasticity, data-driven anisotropic yield functions calibrated on sparse experimental
data, and composition-dependent constitutive models for material families. The report also enu-
merates project metrics and concludes with a discussion of future needs in this rapidly evolving
field.

1. OVERARCHING GOALS

In contrast to the well-known conservation laws of continuum mechanics and the tried and true
numerical techniques for solving them, phenomenological constitutive models are often a notable
source of prediction error in computational simulations due to their empirical nature. The trustwor-
thiness of a simulation hinges on the ability of these models to emulate real materials in multiphysics
environments. Many continuum-scale material models have been successful in certain scenarios,
but others are unable to match experimental predictions due to their preconceived functional forms.
We hypothesized that this gap could be closed through the development of hybrid constitutive mod-
els that mix conventional models with physics-augmented machine learning (ML)-based corrections
that specifically target model discrepancies. We sought to train these hybrid models with existing
high-resolution mechanical and thermal experimental data. Our primary goal was to deliver a more
agile, efficient modeling framework to meet today’s tight deadlines that adheres to experimental
data in regimes where it is available and has the trust of traditional models in extrapolation.
The scientific ML (SciML) community has come to the realization that the replacement of con-
stitutive models with off-the-shelf data-driven surrogates is untenable. The extent of training data
required to cover all possible states a material is likely to experience, incompatibilities with the
data measurable in an experiment (displacement, temperature) compared to that currently required
for training (stress-strain pairs), and satisfaction of physical constraints (e.g. symmetry considera-
tions, material objectivity, thermodynamic properties) that traditional constitutive models satisfy
by design all contribute to the challenge. Furthermore, such approaches do not lend themselves to
implementation in production-level finite element (FE) codes.
We developed methods for constitutive model discovery that supplant and hybridize traditional
models with data-driven, physics-augmented neural networks (PANNs). We operated within a
modular framework where both the conventional and ML components were based on founda-
tional theories that satisfy physical principles. This approach enables trustworthy extrapolation by
known functional forms and ML-driven adjustments where these forms fail to capture data trends.
We focused on two important phenomenologies for metal plasticity, thermomechanics and yield
anisotropy, to close predictivity gaps in mission problems where simulations currently are missing
physics or have poorly understood phenomena.

2. OVERVIEW OF APPROACH

Constitutive models play a vital role in computational simulations, where they serve as closure
relations for the general conservation laws of physics. In thermoplasticity, a complex multiphysics
phenomenology, there are open questions about their structure and functional form that have sig-
nificant impacts on simulation accuracy. Recent projects at Sandia have produced a wealth of
high-fidelity experimental data that is perfectly suited to answering these questions, but computa-
tional techniques that make full use of this data do not yet exist.
We hypothesized that we can meet this challenge through a hybrid approach to constitutive mod-
eling that consists of conventional constitutive models with judiciously-chosen physics-augmented
ML components. We pursued a “best of both worlds” strategy that combines the robustness of
conventional constitutive models with the expressiveness of ML methods to greatly increase their
predictive power and use of data. To develop our approach, we made advances in three thrusts:
calibration of hybrid models with experimental data, development of physics-augmented ML tech-
niques for thermomechanics, and their implementation in differentiable codes.
A major focus of our work was material model calibration — the process of estimating the pa-
rameters in a constitutive model from experimental characterization data. Generally, experiments
provide indirect measurements of a material’s response (e.g. applied force vs extension), which can
only be directly related to constitutive model outputs in special circumstances (e.g. uniaxial stress
in a tensile test prior to necking). Consequently, calibration is often framed as a physics-constrained
optimization problem, where the constraint is a FE model of the characterization experiment(s) that
provides a means to obtain the indirect response. The constitutive model parameters are found by
minimizing an objective function that quantifies the mismatch between experimental measurements
and corresponding FE model predictions. While these constrained optimization approaches are
often computationally expensive, their robustness and flexibility have led to increased usage in both
research and production settings [3, 4].
As noted in a recent review of the use of ML in experimental solid mechanics, “Similar to the
case of surrogate models, one of the most significant challenges to date for ML models on inverse
problems is the lack of experimental data inclusion” [5]. Indeed, many use synthetically generated
data (i.e. they train ML models to existing models) and/or consider direct measurements (i.e.
stress-strain data) to avoid the cost and complexity of embedding ML methods within a FE model
[6, 7, 8]. This focus on direct measurements is stifling progress by divorcing ML models from
their ultimate application within large-scale physics simulations and preventing the use of sources
of experimental data that provide indirect measurements. These include infrared thermography [9]
and digital image correlation [10], information-rich metrology techniques for obtaining full-field
temperature and deformation (i.e. displacement and strain) measurements, respectively, on the
surface of a test specimen. In our work, we endeavored to use real experimental data to develop
and evaluate our methods whenever possible.

augmented neural networks (iPANN/fPANN) for distribution-free uncertainty quantification in

calibration of the hybrid models in a FE setting.
The University of Texas at Austin (Prof. Jan Fuhg; PhD students Asghar Jadoon, L. River Spencer,
and Aryan Tyagi). Professor Fuhg’s group was a driving force behind the development of novel
PANN architectures. They led the development of Input Specific Neural Networks (ISNNs) [17]
and permutation-invariant input-convex neural networks (PI-ICNNs) [18]. These networks were
exercised in a multiscale topology optimization framework [19] and a full-field thermomechanical

3.2. Research Highlights

This section presents a few of the most impactful research results from the project. We refer the
reader to the publications listed in Section 3.1 for further technical details.

3.2.1. Novel Neural Network Architectures for Constitutive Modeling

A central challenge in using neural networks for constitutive modeling is imposing structural con-
straints (e.g. convexity, monotonicity, symmetry, and thermodynamic consistency) that traditional
models satisfy by design. Standard “black-box” networks cannot enforce different constraints on
different subsets of their inputs, which is precisely what many constitutive models require: for
example, a stored energy function must be convex in strain invariants but may depend non-convexly
on temperature or composition.
We developed two new neural network architectures that address this challenge. First, the input
specific neural network (ISNN) [17] enables multiple, distinct structural constraints to be imposed
on different input subsets within a single scalar-valued network. ISNNs unify convex, monotone-
convex, monotone, and unconstrained mappings in a single architecture for the first time. Analytical
first- and second-order derivatives for ISNNs were derived and shown to yield significant computa-
tional savings over automatic differentiation in FE implementations. We demonstrated that ISNNs

can automatically learn which structural relationships (e.g. polyconvexity) are appropriate for a
given dataset through a binary gating mechanism, and that they require fewer invariant inputs than
standard input convex networks.

Second, we proposed a significant enhancement to the internal state variable-neural ordinary
differential equation (ISV-NODE) framework for modeling path-dependent inelastic behavior [22].
The key innovations were: (a) a partially input convex neural network (pICNN) stress potential
that enforces polyconvexity in the observable deformation while leaving inferred internal states

 18

unconstrained, and (b) an attention-based gating mechanism in the internal state flow model that
drives evolution of internal state only in dissipative regimes. This architecture accurately models the
full spectrum of conservative and dissipative behavior from hyperelasticity through viscoelasticity
to elastoplasticity while maintaining thermodynamic principles by design.

3.2.2. Hybrid and Data-Driven Anisotropic Yield Functions

Plastic anisotropy in metals remains challenging to model because conventional phenomenological
yield criteria struggle to combine flexible representation with thermodynamic constraints such as
convexity. We developed two complementary data-driven frameworks for anisotropic yield function
discovery and applied them to experimental data [18].
The first is a hybrid model that augments the classical Hill yield criterion with an ICNN to obtain a
flexible, convex anisotropic yield function in six-dimensional stress space. This approach embodies
one of the project’s core ideas: known physics provides a robust baseline, and ML components
correct its deficiencies.
The second approach used permutation-invariant input convex neural networks (PI-ICNNs) that
learn an isotropic yield function in principal stress space and embed anisotropy through linear stress
transformations. On a sparse Al-7079 extrusion experimental dataset comprising only 12 uniaxial
samples with measured yield stresses and Lankford ratios, the PI-ICNN framework outperformed
the 18-parameter Yld2004-18p model on validation and test data, as shown in Figure 3-2. These
results demonstrate that physics-augmented neural networks can achieve better generalization than
established phenomenological models from minimal experimental data.
We also developed a general, automated method for constructing structural tensors of arbitrary
order [23], which provides the invariant-based representations needed to characterize anisotropy in
neural network constitutive models. This method replaces classical methods (e.g. the Pipkin-Rivlin
polynomial integrity basis) with a systematic nullspace computation via numerical linear algebra,
and we demonstrated its use in learning the symmetry class and orientation of materials from
stress-strain data via sparse regression. We expect it to be a foundational component of future
data-driven formulations for anisotropic metal plasticity.

3.2.3. A Thermodynamically Consistent Framework for Thermoviscoplasticity

Many high-consequence engineering simulations involve viscoplastic processes that generate sub-
stantial heat, and materials may transition from a nearly isothermal, rate-independent regime to a
viscous, temperature-dependent regime during loading. Modeling this full spectrum of behavior
within a single, thermodynamically consistent framework was a central goal of our project.
We developed a physics-augmented neural network framework for general temperature- and rate-
dependent inelastic processes [2] that is firmly grounded in the second law of thermodynamics and
coordinate equivariance. Key architectural innovations include an adaptively rectified dissipation
potential that can represent both rate-independent plasticity (a non-smooth limit) and fully viscous
behavior within a single input convex, potential-based neural ordinary differential equation (NODE)

3.2.4. Composition-dependent Constitutive Modeling and Foundation Material
 Models

Many engineering applications require constitutive models for families of related materials whose
mechanical behavior varies with composition, processing conditions, or microstructural descriptors.
Fitting separate models for each material composition provides no means to predict the response of
new, untested compositions and does not leverage shared physics across the family. We pursued a
novel approach in which composition or material descriptors serve as additional inputs to physics-
augmented neural networks, enabling a single model to span a material family.
We demonstrated this concept on multi-material 3D printed digital materials [11], where poly-
mer jetting mixes distinct photopolymers at the micron scale to create composites with tunable
mechanical properties. We applied this approach to a dataset that contained five such material
blends under uniaxial tension and torsion across multiple strain rates, revealing nonlinear and
rate-dependent responses that strongly depend on composition. A PANN that combined a pICNN
for the composition-dependent hyperelastic strain energy with a quasi-linear viscoelastic formula-
tion for time-dependent response accurately captured this behavior and generalized to interpolated
compositions not seen during training.

Building on this success, we developed a mixture of polyconvex neural potentials architecture [12]
as a modular, data-efficient approach to modeling material families. Each mixture component is
a convex, monotonic potential with respect to polyconvex strain invariants, while a conditioning
network maps material descriptors to mixture weights. Compared to monolithic partially input-
convex networks, the mixture architecture generalized better to unseen material descriptors (see
Figure 3-5) and was less sensitive to model hyperparameters, suggesting that representing a material
family through shared convex building blocks provides a useful structural prior. This direction
points toward “foundation material models” that can be pre-trained on broad material families and
fine-tuned for specific applications.

4. FUTURE NEEDS

While the project achieved significant advances in physics-augmented ML for constitutive modeling,
several important challenges remain that warrant continued investment.
Moving beyond hyperelasticity. The broader SciML community for constitutive modeling remains
largely focused on isothermal hyperelastic (path-independent) material models, which are the
simplest class of nonlinear constitutive models in solid mechanics. Our project made substantial
progress toward path-dependent thermoviscoplasticity [2, 22], but the complexity of coupling
thermal, viscous, and plastic phenomena — each with its own physical constraints — means
that significant challenges remain. The thermoelastic calibration framework described in [20]
is a notable development but additional work is required to handle the full range of coupled
thermomechanical behavior encountered in mission-relevant scenarios. The community’s recent
interest in full-field datasets is encouraging, but these efforts again primarily consider isothermal
hyperelastic materials.
Implementation gaps between Python and production codes. Researchers by and large use
Python to implement PANNs for constitutive models. However, production finite element codes
at Sandia and elsewhere are written in C++. Bridging this gap — deploying trained models
efficiently in production-level codes while retaining the ability to compute stresses, tangent moduli,
and potentially sensitivities for calibration and design — remains a practical barrier to technology
insertion. The analytical derivative capabilities of ISNNs [17] represent a step in this direction, but
seamless approaches to model export (from Python) and insertion (in production C++ codes) that
maintain computational efficiency will be extremely important moving forward.
Training challenges and data efficiency. Training physics-augmented neural networks on exper-
imental data, as opposed to synthetic data, introduces challenges related to noise, sparsity, and
over/underfitting. The sparsification algorithms that we frequently employed in this project [24]
and the uncertainty quantification methods we developed [13] address aspects of this problem, but
robust, automated training pipelines that can handle the messy realities of experimental data are
missing today.
Multi-specimen calibration and model selection. Calibration with data from multiple exper-
iments that probe different aspects of material behavior is essential for developing constitutive
models that are trustworthy across a range of loading conditions. Identifiability (i.e. determining
which model parameters can actually be inferred from available data) was shown to be a significant
challenge for many of the experimental datasets we examined. Systematic approaches to multi-
specimen calibration, model selection, and identifiability analysis for hybrid ML models are sorely
needed.

5. CONCLUSIONS

We significantly advanced constitutive model discovery on several fronts. First, we devised novel
thermodynamically-consistent, modular modeling frameworks for multiphysics, path-dependent
engineering materials, including architectures capable of representing the full spectrum from rate-
independent elastoplasticity to temperature-dependent viscoplasticity [2, 22].
Second, while much of the current ML constitutive modeling literature consists of proof-of-concept
demonstrations with artificially generated data for path-independent materials, we leveraged a
diverse array of experimental datasets to develop and evaluate our methods against real material
behavior [18, 11, 12].
Third, we developed novel neural network architectures, e.g. ISNNs [17], PI-ICNNs [18], and
attention-gated ISV-NODEs [22], that embed physical constraints (convexity, monotonicity, ther-
modynamic consistency) directly into the network structure rather than enforcing them through
penalty terms or post-processing. These architectures provide the building blocks for trustworthy
hybrid constitutive models that combine conventional model forms with ML-driven corrections or
wholesale replacements.
The project contributed to the ESRF Investment Area goals in three principal ways:
 • We developed physics-augmented ML methods for constitutive model discovery that signifi-
 cantly advanced the state of the art. The project established unique capabilities for improving
 the predictive accuracy of computational simulations of thermomechanical processes.
 • We pursued an aggressive outreach and dissemination strategy that resulted in 12 journal
 publications, 20 conference presentations, and the organization of 6 minisymposia at national
 and international conferences.
 • We had three SUPN collaborations with Cornell University, UC Berkeley, and UT Austin
 that engaged multiple PhD students and a postdoctoral researcher. Our time spent working
 together has built lasting academic partnerships and contributed to workforce development.
