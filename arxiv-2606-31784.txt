Kinetic derivation of thermal viscous models for nematic liquid crystal dynamics
P. E. Farrell 1,2, J. Málek 2, O. Souček 2 and U. Zerbinati 1

Abstract
We develop a macroscopic thermodynamic theory of nematic liquid crystals starting from a kinetic theory of ordered fluids with a collision operator of Bhatnagar–Gross–Krook (BGK) type. The kinetic description incorporates mean-field alignment interactions through a Vlasov potential and relies on a separation of time scales, with orientational relaxation occurring on a faster time scale than translational momentum relaxation. At the continuum level, we establish the balance equations for mass, linear and angular momentum, energy, and entropy. Using the zeroth- and first-order Chapman–Enskog expansions, we derive a constitutive equation for the Helmholtz free energy and identify the associated structural form of the entropy production rate. We then exploit additional information from the kinetic description to determine a constitutive relation for the entropy production rate itself. Finally, by applying the constrained maximisation procedure of Rajagopal and Srinivasa, we obtain constitutive equations for the Cauchy stress and couple-stress tensors, as well as for the energy and entropy fluxes. In this way we generalise the recent inviscid kinetic theory of Farrell, Russo, and Zerbinati to account for viscous, thermal, and spin-diffusive effects, using the simplest BGK-type approximation of the collision operator. Both compressible and incompressible variants of the theory are presented.

In recent work

A key limitation of that approach is that the closure is performed around an already aligned state, meaning that collective orientational behaviour is effectively imposed rather than emerging from the dynamics. While this assumption is appropriate in the context of polyatomic gases under strong external fields, it is insufficient for liquid crystals, where the isotropic-to-nematic transition is a fundamental emergent phenomenon. In this work, we overcome both limitations to derive a compressible

[
2

To address the macroscopic viscous behaviour, we apply the thermodynamically consistent moment closure recently explored for the classical Boltzmann equation in
[
12

Our approach does not recover the full Leslie viscous stress tensor with its five independent viscosities, because we use an isotropic collision operator of Bhatnagar–Gross–Krook (BGK) type to make the calculations tractable. Instead, it yields a viscous stress tensor governed by a single viscosity parameter that does not coincide with any single classical Leslie coefficient. We conjecture that recovering the complete Leslie viscous stress tensor is possible with an anisotropic BGK collision operator involving several relaxation timescales. Investigating this is the subject of ongoing work.

A kinetic theory of ordered fluids

Calamitic fluids consist of rod-like, i.e. calamitic, molecules that typically interact via a hard potential
2
2

2

We refer to an interaction as hard when the corresponding collision kernel increases with the relative velocity of the particles. In
[
12
]
, the authors discuss how to generalise the BGK approximation to more general Boltzmann-type kinetic models with hard interaction kernels.
. In his seminal work
[
20
]
, Onsager showed that a system of molecules interacting via a steric potential can undergo a phase transition from an isotropic to a liquid crystalline state as the density of the system rises above a critical value
3
3

3

Onsager’s original work was based on a virial expansion of the free energy. Such expansions are a classical tool in the statistical mechanical treatment of gases and are rigorously justified primarily in dilute regimes, similarly to the Boltzmann–Grad limit adopted in
[
2
]
. A modern presentation of Onsager’s theory can be found in
[
21
]
, where the author also extends the range of validity of the theory.
.

The liquid crystalline state is characterised by the emergence of collective behaviour in the form of molecular alignment, as the system tends to maximise its entropy, much like matches in a box tend to align when shaken. The emergence of alignment suggests that this phase possesses orientational order. Accordingly, we refer to a calamitic fluid in this phase as an ordered fluid. In this section, we give a brief introduction to the kinetic theory of ordered fluids proposed in
[
2
]
, which describes fluids whose molecular constituents are endowed with a microstructure—here, orientation—that exhibits collective behaviour, such as alignment.

The kinetic theory of ordered fluids proposed in
[
2
]
is based on the hypothesis that the molecular interactions are of weak-order nature, see
[
2
, Section 3.3]
, i.e. that only the interactions between the molecular order parameters are long-ranged, and that there is a separation of scales between the relaxation time of the microstructural degrees of freedom and the relaxation time of the translational degrees of freedom, i.e. the microstructural degrees of freedom relax much faster than the translational ones. A detailed discussion of the validity of these assumptions can be found in
[
2
, Remark 3.7]
. Under these assumptions, the resulting kinetic equation takes the form

\partial_{t}f+m^{-1}\bm{p}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}f+\underline{\underline{B}}(\nu)^{-1}\bm{\varsigma}\cdot\nabla_{\nu}f+\bm{\mathcal{V}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\varsigma}}f=\mathcal{Q}(f,f),

(1)

where

\bm{x}

\bm{p}

\nu

\bm{\varsigma}

is a generalised inertia tensor that may depend on

is a mean-field torque (referred to as the Vlasov torque) generated by an interaction potential

Vanishing-girth limit

Following

\displaystyle-\nabla_{\bm{x}}\!\cdot\!\bm{Q}.

7
First-order hydrodynamics

The zeroth-order hydrodynamics derived in the previous section, and in particular the balance laws (
137
), capture only the inviscid, non-dissipative response of the system. To recover viscous, heat-conducting and orientational dissipation we perform a first-order Chapman–Enskog expansion of the kinetic equation (
1
) with the BGK collision operator (
17
) of
Section

2.3
. The anisotropic extension is the subject of ongoing work. The crucial point that distinguishes the present calculation from a standard Chapman–Enskog procedure is that the time derivatives appearing in the linearised problem must be substituted using the zeroth-order balance laws (
137
), not the compressible Euler equations: the Ericksen-stress and couple-stress contributions to
\mathbb{T}^{(0)}
and
\mathbb{M}^{(0)}
, carrying the Frank modulus
K=K_{\mathrm{kin}}
of
Remark

5
, propagate through the substitution rule and produce structurally new terms in
f^{(1)}
. The structure of the calculation parallels the Chapman–Enskog procedure for the BGK operator
[
11
, Appendix A]
, but is generalised to the kinetic theory of ordered fluids, in which the orientational distribution
\lambda(\bm{\nu},t)
and the Vlasov mean-field force introduce new challenges and torque contributions.
We non-dimensionalise the kinetic equation (
1
) by introducing the Knudsen number
\epsilon=\mathrm{Kn}=\ell_{\mathrm{mfp}}/\ell_{\mathrm{macro}}
as the formal small parameter and rescale the BGK relaxation time as

\tau\;=\;\epsilon\,\bar{\tau},

(138)

with
\bar{\tau}=\mathcal{O}(1)
. The kinetic equation (
1
) together with (
17
) then reads

\mathcal{D}f\;:=\;\partial_{t}f+m^{-1}\bm{p}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}f+\underline{\underline{B}}^{-1}\bm{\varsigma}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\nu}}f+\bm{\mathcal{V}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\varsigma}}f\;=\;-\frac{1}{\epsilon\,\bar{\tau}}\bigl(f-f^{(0)}\bigr),

(139)

where
\mathcal{D}
is the streaming operator. We introduce a multi-scale time decomposition,

\partial_{t}=\partial_{t_{0}}+\epsilon\,\partial_{t_{1}}+\epsilon^{2}\,\partial_{t_{2}}+\cdots,

(140)

and an asymptotic expansion of the distribution,

f=f^{(0)}+\epsilon\,f^{(1)}+\epsilon^{2}\,f^{(2)}+\cdots.

(141)

The constraint that the macroscopic moments
(\rho,\bm{u},e,\bm{\mu})
, together with the orientational distribution
\lambda(\bm{\nu},t)
, are entirely captured by the local Maxwellian
f^{(0)}
amounts to the solvability condition that, for every
k\geq 1
,

\int f^{(k)}\,\psi\,\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\Xi}=0\quad\text{for every collision invariant }\psi\text{ in~\eqref{eq:collinv}},

(142)

together with the orthogonality of
f^{(k)}
with respect to the orientational distribution
\lambda
, namely
\int f^{(k)}\,\,\mathrm{d}\bm{V}\,\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\Sigma}=0
pointwise in
\bm{\nu}
. These conditions guarantee that the corrections
f^{(k)}
for
k\geq 1
encode purely off-equilibrium fluxes and do not redefine the macroscopic state. Substituting (
140
) and (
141
) into (
139
) and matching powers of
\epsilon
produces an infinite hierarchy of equations, the first two of which are analysed below.

At leading order in
\epsilon
, the right-hand side of (
139
) forces
f^{(0)}-f^{(0)}=0
, which is satisfied trivially. The slow evolution of the macroscopic fields
(\rho,\bm{u},e,\bm{\mu})
on the time scale
t_{0}
is then governed by the zeroth-order balance laws (
137
):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot(\rho\bm{u})=0,

(143a)

\displaystyle\rho\left[\partial_{t}\bm{u}+(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\bm{u})\bm{u}\right]-\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\mathbb{T}^{(0)}=0,

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}^{(0)}=-p\underline{\underline{I}}-K\!\left(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\widehat{\bm{\nu}}\odot\nabla_{\bm{x}}\widehat{\bm{\nu}}\right),

(143b)

\displaystyle\rho\bigl[\partial_{t}\bm{\mu}+(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\bm{\mu})\bm{u}\bigr]-\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\mathbb{M}^{(0)}=0,

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}=C_{m,\ell}\,\widehat{\bm{\nu}}\times\dot{\widehat{\bm{\nu}}},\quad\mathbb{M}^{(0)}=mK\!\left(\widehat{\bm{\nu}}\times\nabla_{\bm{x}}\widehat{\bm{\nu}}\right),

(143c)

\displaystyle\rho\bigl[\partial_{t}\widetilde{e}+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}\widetilde{e}\bigr]-\mathbb{T}^{(0)}:\nabla_{\bm{x}}\bm{u}-\tfrac{1}{mC_{m,\ell}}\mathbb{M}^{(0)}:\nabla_{\bm{x}}\bm{\mu}=0,

\displaystyle\bm{Q}^{(0)}=\bm{0},\quad p=R_{s}\,\rho\,\theta,\quad\theta=\frac{2}{5\,R_{s}}\,\widetilde{e},

(143d)

\displaystyle\rho\bigl[\partial_{t}\eta+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}\eta\bigr]=0,

\displaystyle\xi_{\tau}^{(0)}=0.

(143e)

We emphasise that (
143
) differs from the standard compressible Euler equations precisely by the Ericksen-stress and couple-stress contributions inherited from the closures (
133
)–(
134
) with the rotational specific energy of (
126

f^{(1)}
.

Collecting the order-
\epsilon^{1}
terms in (

f^{(1)}\;=\;-\bar{\tau}\,\mathcal{D}f^{(0)}.

(144)

The remainder of this section is devoted to the explicit computation of the streaming derivative

7.1
Expansion independence of the equation of state

The solvability conditions (
142

f^{(1)}
. Following
[
12

when

\varphi[f]=\varphi\Bigl[\textstyle\sum_{i=0}^{k}\epsilon^{i}f^{(i)}\Bigr]\qquad\text{for every }k\geq 0,

(145)

that is, when the observable is unchanged on every truncation of the Chapman–Enskog expansion (
141

\varphi^{(k)}
for the observable evaluated on the truncation
\sum_{i=0}^{k}\epsilon^{i}f^{(i)}
. The three results below are, for the present model, the analogues of
[

, the only new ingredients being the rotational component and the orientational distribution
\lambda

Proposition 4

(Expansion independence of the conserved fields)
.

Under the solvability conditions (
142
) the mass density
\rho
, the bulk velocity
\bm{u}
, the total internal energy
\widetilde{e}
, and the bulk intrinsic angular momentum

are expansion independent.

Proof.

Each of these fields is a moment of
f
against a collision invariant of (
5
). For the density,

\rho^{(k)}=\int m\sum_{i=0}^{k}\epsilon^{i}f^{(i)}\,\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\Xi}=\int mf^{(0)}\,\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\Xi}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\epsilon^{i}\int mf^{(i)}\,\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\Xi}=\rho,

since every term with
i\geq 1
vanishes because
m
is a collision invariant. The same computation with the momentum
\bm{p}
, with the total energy
\tfrac{1}{2}(\left|\bm{p}\right|^{2}/m+\bm{\varsigma}^{T}\underline{\underline{B}}^{-1}\bm{\varsigma})
, and with the angular momentum of (
5
) shows that
\rho\bm{u}
, the total energy density
\rho(\tfrac{1}{2}\left|\bm{u}\right|^{2}+\widetilde{e})
and
\bm{\mu}
coincide with their zeroth-order values. As
\rho
and
\rho\bm{u}
are expansion independent, so is
\bm{u}
, and as the total energy density is then expansion independent, so is the internal energy
\widetilde{e}
.
∎

Proposition 5

(Expansion independence of the entropy)
.

Under the solvability conditions (
142
) the specific entropy satisfies
\eta^{(1)}=\eta^{(0)}+O(\epsilon^{2})
.

Proof.

Truncating (

\log(f^{(0)}+\epsilon f^{(1)})=\log f^{(0)}+\epsilon\,f^{(1)}/f^{(0)}+O(\epsilon^{2})
, so the entropy density

The first integral is
\rho\eta^{(0)}
of (
74

\log f^{(0)}
of (
147

\tfrac{1}{2}(m\left|\bm{V}\right|^{2}+\left|\bm{\Sigma}\right|^{2}/C_{m,\ell})
and the orientational term

\int f^{(1)}\,\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\Xi}=0

These two propositions transfer the equation of state of

7.2
Streaming derivative

We compute the streaming derivative
\mathcal{D}f^{(0)}
via the chain rule,
\mathcal{D}f^{(0)}=f^{(0)}\,\mathcal{D}\log f^{(0)}
, where, from (

\log f^{(0)}=\log\rho-\frac{5}{2}\log\theta-\frac{m\,\left|\bm{V}\right|^{2}}{2k_{B}\theta}-\frac{\left|\bm{\Sigma}\right|^{2}}{2C_{m,\ell}k_{B}\theta}+\log\lambda(\bm{\nu})+\mathrm{C},

where
\bm{V}=\bm{v}-\bm{u}
,
\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{\varsigma}-\bm{\sigma}
,
\bm{\sigma}=\left\langle\!\left\langle\bm{\varsigma}\right\rangle\!\right\rangle
, and
\mathrm{C}
is a constant independent of any physical quantity. We collect the contribution of each term to
\mathcal{D}\log f^{(0)}
. Throughout, we substitute the time derivatives of the macroscopic fields using the zeroth-order balance laws (
143
).

Acting with
\mathcal{D}
on
\log\rho

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\log\rho

\displaystyle=\rho^{-1}\bigl(\partial_{t_{0}}\rho+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}\rho\bigr)=\rho^{-1}\bigl(-\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot(\rho\bm{u})+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}\rho\bigr)=-\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\bm{u}+\rho^{-1}\bm{V}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}\rho.

(148)

Acting with
\mathcal{D}
on
\left|\bm{V}\right|^{2}=(\bm{v}-\bm{u})\cdot(\bm{v}-\bm{u})
and using the momentum balance in (
143
), that is, the Euler–Ericksen system derived in
Section

6
with the Ericksen stress and the pressure

\partial_{t_{0}}\bm{u}=-(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\bm{u})\bm{u}-\rho^{-1}\nabla_{\bm{x}}p-\rho^{-1}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\!\cdot\!\bigl[K\,(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\widehat{\bm{\nu}}\odot\nabla_{\bm{x}}\widehat{\bm{\nu}})\bigr],

(149)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left|\bm{V}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=-2\bm{V}\cdot\bigl(\partial_{t_{0}}\bm{u}+(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\bm{u})\bm{v}\bigr)=-2\,\bm{V}\cdot(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\bm{u})\bm{V}+\frac{2}{\rho}\bm{V}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}p+\frac{2}{\rho}\bm{V}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}\!\cdot\!\bigl[K\,(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\widehat{\bm{\nu}}\odot\nabla_{\bm{x}}\widehat{\bm{\nu}})\bigr].

(150)

The thermal part of the pressure gradient will cancel classically against the density-gradient and temperature-gradient sources, as we will see in
Theorem

7.1
below. The interaction part combines with the Ericksen divergence into the vector

\bm{W}\;:=\;\nabla_{\bm{x}}\!\cdot\!\bigl[K\,(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\widehat{\bm{\nu}}\odot\nabla_{\bm{x}}\widehat{\bm{\nu}})\bigr],

\bm{V}

\zeta_{\mathrm{aniso}}\,\mathcal{B}(A)\,(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\widehat{\bm{\nu}}\odot\nabla_{\bm{x}}\widehat{\bm{\nu}})
, whose trace part is the anisotropic bulk correction and whose deviatoric part is a texture-aligned contribution to the viscous stress. Relation (

) provides the couple-stress closure, including coupling both to the velocity gradient through the Ericksen contraction in

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\widehat{\bm{\nu}}\to 0

8.2
The closed system and the director form of the angular-momentum balance

We now recast the angular-momentum balance as an evolution equation for the nematic director, following the discussion around
[

, and summarise the closed system obtained in this work. Recall the zeroth-order identification
\bm{\mu}=C_{m,\ell}\,\widehat{\bm{\nu}}\times\dot{\widehat{\bm{\nu}}}
of (

\dot{\widehat{\bm{\nu}}}\times\dot{\widehat{\bm{\nu}}}=\bm{0}

\rho\,\frac{d\bm{\mu}}{dt}\;=\;\rho\,C_{m,\ell}\,\widehat{\bm{\nu}}\times\ddot{\widehat{\bm{\nu}}}.

(196)

Moreover, every contribution to the closed couple stress is, up to the higher-order terms already discarded in
Section

g\,(\widehat{\bm{\nu}}\times\nabla_{\bm{x}}\widehat{\bm{\nu}})
or
mC_{m,\ell}^{2}\,\zeta_{\mathrm{ang}}(\widehat{\bm{\nu}}\times\nabla_{\bm{x}}\dot{\widehat{\bm{\nu}}})

\nabla_{\bm{x}}\!\cdot\!\bigl[g\,(\widehat{\bm{\nu}}\times\nabla_{\bm{x}}\widehat{\bm{\nu}})\bigr]\;=\;\widehat{\bm{\nu}}\times\nabla_{\bm{x}}\!\cdot\!\bigl(g\,\nabla_{\bm{x}}\widehat{\bm{\nu}}\bigr)

\widehat{\bm{\nu}}\times\Bigl[\,\rho\,C_{m,\ell}\,\ddot{\widehat{\bm{\nu}}}\;-\;m\,\nabla_{\bm{x}}\!\cdot\!\Bigl(\bigl(K-\zeta_{\mathrm{aniso}}\,\mathcal{B}(A)\bigr)\,\nabla_{\bm{x}}\widehat{\bm{\nu}}\Bigr)\;-\;mC_{m,\ell}^{2}\,\nabla_{\bm{x}}\!\cdot\!\bigl(\zeta_{\mathrm{ang}}\,\nabla_{\bm{x}}\dot{\widehat{\bm{\nu}}}\bigr)\,\Bigr]\;=\;\bm{0}.

8.3
The incompressible model

The Rajagopal–Srinivasa procedure delivers the incompressible counterpart of (

Derivation of the Maxwellian

The Maxwellian distribution (
18

2.3

By the Boltzmann inequality
[
2

\bm{\nu}
alone, which are annihilated by the collision integral because
