 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 Phase 1 (Original CRADA, plus no-cost extension modifications #1-3, 6/1/2017 to 3/13/2021):

 The Australian Department of Defense (AUDoD) is performing accelerated aging tests of Li-ion
 batteries to benchmark their reliability and degradation characteristics. Using its previously
 developed battery lifetime predictive model framework, the National Laboratory of the Rockies
 (NLR) will develop analytical models based the AUDoD data to predict lifetime of the multiple
 Li-ion battery chemistries under real-world use scenarios of interest to AUDoD. The NLR model
 is based on physical degradation mechanisms encountered by Li-ion batteries and has been
 previously validated.

 1

 Phase 2 (CRADA modification #4, plus no-cost extension modification #5, 2/22/2021 to
   3/30/2025):

 Train and support Australian Department of Defence personnel to use NLR software for model-
 based estimation of Li-ion battery lifetime using accelerated battery aging data collected by the
 Australian Department of Defence.

 Under separate DOE funding from 2019 to 2021, NLR enhanced its battery life-prediction
 software using machine learning algorithms to automate portions of the model-fitting process,
 requiring significantly less labor and expert judgment and also adding uncertainty quantification,
 increasing statistical rigor. Under Phase 2, NLR will customize NLR Software and provide it to
 AuDoD. NLR will enhance its NLR Model to capture aging modes of AuDoD’s multi-cell
 modules, including cell-balancing effects. NLR will develop example single-cell and multi-cell
 models based on one AuDoD battery aging dataset. NLR will train AuDoD personnel on NLR
 Software. By the conclusion of the project, NLR will have provided AuDoD the training
 materials, a user manual and software needed to perform their own analysis of additional and/or
 future battery aging datasets.

 Phase 1 Tasks:

 Task 1: Transfer aging data for three Li-ion chemistries from Excel spreadsheet to
 structured Matlab format

 AUDoD delivered three Excel spreadsheets containing accelerated-aging laboratory test data for
 three Li-ion cathode chemistries: lithium iron phosphate (LFP), nickel manganese cobalt (NMC),
 and nickel cobalt aluminum (NCA). Each data set contained cycle aging results at several
 temperatures and depths of discharge (DOD) and calendar aging results at several temperatures
 and states of charge (SOC). Twelve cell replicates were used for each test condition. Figure 1
 shows the top few rows of an example Excel spreadsheet used to store and transfer all data. An
 automated script was created to load all Excel data into a structure format used by the Phase 1
 Matlab code.

 2

 Figure 1. Excel spreadsheet format used to log laboratory accelerated aging (a) calendar-fade and
 (b) cycling-fade data. Each new reference performance test (RPT) result is stored in a new column
 as each cell ages. Each RPT produces four health metrics that are stored in rows: Amp-hour
 capacity, Watt-hour energy, the low-frequency (LF) intercept of an electrochemical impedance
 spectroscopy measurement, and direct-current pulse resistance of the cell. Only two cells are
 shown for each example. Each of the hundreds of cells tested adds another group of rows to the
 spreadsheet.

 Task 2: Regress model of each chemistry, run example life prediction simulations, and
 estimate model prediction accuracy

 The NREL Battery Life Predictive Model simulates performance fade that occurs during the
 lifetime of a Li-ion battery. The framework includes submodels of multiple degradation
 mechanisms (electrode film growth and disturbance, Li loss, site loss, electrolyte breakdown)
 that, when combined, describe the dependence of battery performance metrics (capacity and
 resistance) on the battery’s use history (charge/discharge duty-cycle, state-of-charge window and
 temperature). The model regression process is semi-automated and requires some expert
 judgement. Different physical degradation mechanisms are selected in the model based on
 dominant trends evident in the test data. For reference, NREL has previously published studies
 [1] using the identification tool used in Phase 1 of this CRADA.

 NREL developed models for both absolute energy fade and relative energy fade (normalizing all
 aging data by dividing by the first measurement of energy at the beginning of life). Figure 2
 shows an example relative-energy fade model vs data for one of the three Li-ion chemistries.

 3

 Relative energy models had slightly better quality of fit, with R2=0.996, and root-mean-square
 error (RMSE) of 0.5% of capacity. This RMSE (0.5%) is smaller than cell-to-cell standard
 deviation or discharge energy (0.85%) at beginning of life. Instead of fitting each of the 12
 replicate cells’ data for each test condition, we instead fit the average result across all 12 cells.
 We found this decreased the noise in the data and produced a higher-quality fit with cleaner
 trends.

 Figure 2: (a) Relative energy fade, model compared to data. (b) Model error of relative energy fade
 approaches 1.5% after 400 days of aging for the model’s worst-case aging conditions. Most
 conditions stay within 1% error.

 Task 3: Quantify cell-to-cell aging variability and impact on lifetime

 The life model from the previous task predicted the aging behavior of the average cell. To
 quantify cell-to-cell variability, NREL compared the average-cell model to individual cell’s
 aging response across the 12 cell replicates for each test condition. Figure 3 shows cell
 variability expressed as (a) relative cell energy and (b) absolute cell energy in Watt hours.
 Figure 3b shows that the standard deviation between model and individual cell response,
 expressed as absolute cell energy standard, stays relatively constant throughout lifetime. This
 indicates that, if duty cycle and temperature stay constant, cell-to-cell variability at end of life
 will be similar in magnitude to beginning of life. This neglects system-level thermal and
 electrical imbalances that can accumulate, a follow-on topic later explored in Phase 2.

 4

 Figure 3. Standard deviation across 12 cell replicates for each aging test condition compared to
 the model’s average-cell aging prediction. Standard deviation is expressed as both (a) relative cell
 energy (no units) and (b) absolute cell energy (Watt hours).

 Task 2.1: Perform single-cell life model fitting

 In performance of this task, NREL will Deliver NREL Software, specifically machine learning
 automated life-model identification code to AuDoD. This flexible code allows the user to input
 arbitrary degradation mechanism equations, segment and fit battery aging data. NREL will
 perform single-cell life modeling as follows:

 Data provided by AuDOD was used to identify a battery degradation model using NREL’s AI-
 Batt software [2,3]. The battery degradation model incorporates fitting of both calendar aging
 and cycle aging accelerated testing data, according to subtasks 2.1.1 and 2.1.2. Cycle aging data
 is modeled after subtracting the contribution of calendar aging to the cycling cells, so calendar
 aging data is modeled first.

 Task 2.1.2: Cycle life fitting

 Deliver to AuDoD and provide Support for cycle life fitting NREL Software.

 For the data set in this work, cycling fade was dominated by two mechanisms: one, loss of
 lithium inventory (LLI), which also causes calendar degradation, and the other, loss of active
 material (LAM), which causes accelerating degradation near the end of life for stressful cycling
 conditions. To separate these mechanisms, AI-Batt was used to fit polynomial models to every
 curve, which then enabled calculations of the analytical first and second order derivatives to split
 the data into LLI and LAM dominated regions.

 Figure 5: Separation of Loss of Lithium Inventory dominated fade from Loss of Active Material
 dominated fade.

 The LLI dominated region was then fit after fitting the calendar fade model. The calendar fade
 during cycling was predicted and then subtracted from the LLI-dominated cycling test results,
 leaving just the ‘pure cycling fade’. This cycling fade can then be modeled using essentially the
 same procedure as used above for the calendar fade:

 8

 Figure 6: (A) Bilevel optimization of a power law versus cycle count, where the power is fit to all
 curves while the degradation rate is optimized independently for each curve. (B) Visualization of
 the degradation versus the test conditions, temperature and depth-of-discharge (DOD). (C)
 Simulations of degradation rate curves versus DOD and temperature from a machine-learned
 degradation rate submodel. (D) Optimization of the global cycling fade model on the LLI-
 dominated cycling data, incorporating the degradation rate submodel that predicts the cycling
 degradation rate as a function of temperature and depth-of-discharge. (E) Optimization of the
 combined calendar aging and cycling aging degradation rate models for the LLI-dominated data.

 LAM dominated cycle aging data is then modeled completely separately; overall battery
 degradation will simply be the minimum of the two models (LLI-dominated fade and LAM-
 dominated fade). As with the other modeled degradation modes, LAM-dominated cycling fade is
 modeled using a procedure of bilevel regression, machine-learned degradation rate submodel
 identification, and global optimization:

 Figure 7: (A) Fitting of the LAM-dominated cycling fade data with a sigmoidal function, where two
 parameters are globally fixed and the degradation rate is independently optimized to each curve.
 (B) Visualization of the LAM-dominated cycling fade degradation rate as a function of the testing
 conditions, temperature and depth-of-discharge, showing mainly depth-of-discharge dependence.
 (C) Simulation of the machine-learned degradation rate submodel, showing high degradation rates
 at high DOD and low temperatures, which is expected for the underlying degradation
 mechanisms. (D) Optimization of the global LAM-dominated cycling fade model, which is a
 sigmoidal function of cycle count with the machine-learned degradation rate submodel predicting
 degradation rate as a function of temperature and DOD. (E) The combined LLI-dominated and
 LAM-dominated models predicting degradation across the entire data set with low error. (F) An
 example of the competition between LLI and LAM on one of the cycling cells.

 Task 2.1.3: Automatic generation of a life-predictive model for dynamic aging scenarios

 Use the NREL Software previously provided and data provided by AuDoD to deliver to AuDoD
 and provide Support for the automatic generation of a life-predictive model for dynamic aging
 scenarios.

 Simulation of lifetime under dynamic operation is done by mathematically converting the
 learned degradation models into state functions; rather than predicting degradation as a function
 of time, state functions update the value of each independent degradation mode through aging,
 predicting the incremental change of the state as a function of the operating condition at each
 time step. This enables the models that were trained on constant test conditions to simulate
 degradation during dynamic real-world operation.

 10

 For AuDOD, this process was streamlined to make the model development process easier, and
 key changes were made to the internal structure of the code so that uncertainty from the model
 training could be propagated through to dynamic simulations, treating model parameters as
 distributions rather than single point values. See below of an example, where two different
 scenarios are simulated with uncertainty, resulting in range of degradation trajectories.

 Figure 8: Example simulation of battery degradation with uncertainty for two different operating
 protocols.

 Degradation models with uncertainty were used to create remaining useful life distributions from

 Sensitivity of useful life on operating conditions was determined by using the capacity fade

 Optimal system design was analyzed by using degradation models to predict the total system cost

 of an example battery that can last 20 years at full DOD.

 Task 2.2: Perform multi-cell modeling

 NREL will enhance, deliver, and Support a multi-cell aging model. NREL does not presently
 have U.S. DOE program funds supporting this model. While much of the software code for the
 multi-cell aging model already exists as an NREL Model, under this project, NREL will enhance
 the NREL Model code per AuDoD’s needs as described below. As the NREL Model is enhanced
 and delivered to AuDoD, NREL will provide Support to AuDoD. NREL will perform multi-cell
 modeling as follows:

 Multi-cell modeling was performed using NREL’s BLAST software [4]. The BLAST software is
 an electrical-thermal-aging model of battery systems, where the various key physical phenomena
 are modeled using applicable models:

 • An equivalent circuit model is used to predict voltage losses of the batteries
 • An open-circuit voltage model is used to predict thermodynamic voltage of the batteries
 • An electrical model is used to model the voltage losses due to wiring and distribute the
 current between parallel cells in a multi-cell battery system
 • Empirical aging models for key battery parameters, such as the capacity fade model
 above, are used to model battery degradation during operation
 • A lumped thermal model is used to predict thermal gradients in the battery system during
 operation

 The overall structure of the multi-cell battery simulation is summarized below:

 Figure 12: Schematic of multi-cell model simulation approach.

 This software was substantially improved during this project to meet AuDOD needs. Subtasks

 Task 2.2.1: Develop single cell equivalent electrical circuit model linked to life model

 Deliver to AuDoD and provide Support for NREL Model, specifically NREL’s equivalent circuit
 electrical model linked to life model in single-cell form.

 The single cell equivalent electrical circuit model (ECM) is a model that predicts the
 overpotential of the battery during operation, e.g., the resistive losses due to electronic/ionic
 resistance, charge transfer resistance, and mass transfer resistance. For AuDOD, the battery
 resistance was parameterized by fitting an ECM model to electrochemical impedance
 spectroscopy data recorded by AuDOD throughout the aging of the batteries. The ECM model is
 a model with 2 charge-transfer processes (parallel resistor-capacitor pairs) and a mass transfer
 process (i.e., diffusion, modeled by the WO element (short-circuit Warburg), and capacitance).
 Also needed to fit the test data is an inductor (L), which is simply due to the wires connecting the
 battery to the test equipment. This model was fit to all measurements of all cells; an example fit
 of one cell’s data is shown below.

 Figure 13: Parameterization of battery resistance using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy
 recorded through lifetime.

 All of the fit parameters from all EIS measurements were then correlated to the capacity of the
 batteries to determine which parameters were most sensitive to the state-of-health. It was
 determined that the two charge-transfer resistances were the most sensitive, while the Ohmic
 resistance and diffusion resistance were also somewhat sensitive to the degradation state.

 15

 Figure 14: Correlation of EIS parameters with battery state-of-health. Only highly correlated
 parameters were used to model cell behavior through lifetime.

 Task 2.2.2: Couple equivalent electrical circuit model to cell state-of-health

 Customize, demonstrate to AuDoD, and provide Support for NREL Model, specifically
 degradation functions that couple electrical-circuit model parameters to cell state-of-health
 (SOH). Typically, capacity and series-resistance electrical-circuit parameters are functions of
 SOH. Dynamic resistances may also be modified if needed to match AuDoD data.

 The four ECM parameters sensitive to degradation identified above were all coupled to battery
 capacity using AI-Batt model fitting. Ohmic resistance and diffusion resistance were observed to
 be linearly related to capacity with no temperature dependence, while the charge transfer
 resistances had a non-linear relationship with capacity and strong temperature dependence. Each
 of the identified models shows strong agreement with data.

 Figure 15: Coupling of EIS parameters modeled through lifetime to discharge capacity.

 Task 2.2.4: Incorporate passive and active cell balancing into the multi-cell model

 Enhance NREL Model to incorporate passive and active cell balancing, predict the lifetime
 impacts of each, deliver, and provide Support for code.

 Passive balancing and active balancing were incorporated into BLAST. Passive balancing occurs
 naturally when the battery model describes all key physics; the current oscillations during charge
 and discharge in the simulation results above show passive balancing. However, passive
 balancing only occurs between cells that are short-circuited to one another, that is, cells that are
 in a parallel connection. Parallel groups in series, however, will slowly become more imbalanced
 as the pack degrades, as the varying temperature and voltage window of the difference cell
 groups causes differential aging. To balance between cell groups in series, charge needs to be
 moved between cell groups; this process is called active balancing.

 In the real world, there are a wide variety of active balancing approaches, with many pros and
 cons. To simplify the simulation of active balancing, we implemented a simple active balancing
 scheme where groups of cells can be shorted together, but the maximum current is limited and
 there is an associated efficiency with the movement of charge, reflecting real world current and
 efficiency limitations of active balancing circuits. Below is a plot of the state-of-charge of
 several cells in series for a system that was instantiated with poorly balanced cells and active
 balancing is turned on. The slight downward slope reflects the lost charge due to active balancing
 inefficiency. Active balancing simulation can occur during any battery operating mode
 (charging, discharging, or rest), reflecting real world systems.

 Figure 18: Demonstration of active balancing simulation with efficiency loss.
