1.0 Summary
During the current project period the following tasks were completed:
 (1) We demonstrated the feasibility of a new way of measuring CH3 radicals in a plasma environment
 using a single, femtosecond (fs) pulsed laser for the first time, in a CH4 + Ar flow. A single fs
 pulsed Ti:Sa laser was split into two beams and with beam conversion using crystals we generated
 two beams of 275 nm and 205 nm. The 275 nm was used to predissociate the CH3 radicals to
 CH2+H, followed by 205 nm Two Photon Laser Induced Fluorescence (TALIF) detection of H
 atoms, with a 11 ns delay from 275 nm predissociation pulse.
 (2) We demonstrated preliminary CH3 concentration measurements with spatial (< 100 µm) and time
 resolution (< 10 ns) by image processing of H and Kr TALIF signal images.
 (3) The methodology to quantify CH3 concentrations was developed.
 (4) Preliminary measurements of laser dissociation cross sections for CH4 and CH3 to (CH3+H), and
 (CH2+H), respectively, were conducted.
 (5) 275 nm laser dissociation of CH4 is a multiphoton process, and the CH4 dissociation cross section
 is a more sensitive function of the laser pulse energy than CH4 concentration. In the coming
 months, we will make more precise measurements of the dissociation cross sections, 𝑘275, 𝐶𝐻4 and
 𝑘275, 𝐶𝐻3 , by varying the laser energy while keeping the flow composition and conditions constant.
 This will enable more precise quantification of CH3 radical concentrations.

More detailed measurements of CH3 radicals will be conducted in the following months of 2025 (Sept-Dec)
in a Titanium Isopropoxide (TIP) + H2 + Ar flow meant for pure phase Ti thin film deposition by Chemical
Vapor Deposition (CVD).
More precise measurements of 275 nm laser dissociation cross sections for CH4 (to CH3 + H) and CH3 (to
CH2 + H) will be conducted in the future. Detailed 275 nm laser predissociation cross sections for CH4 and

CH3 dissociation to (CH3+H), and (CH2+H), respectively, as a function of gas heating, plasma power, and
laser pulse energy will be evaluated. This will enable quantification of CH3 concentrations in the precursor
flow over a substrate with time and spatial resolution.
Comparison of the concentrations with 2D numerical simulations can lead to understanding of plasma
surface interactions during pure metallic Ti thin film deposition using environmentally safer non-halogen
TIP precursor. Flow conditions for pure Ti thin film deposition using non-halogen precursor (TIP) will be
identified in the following months.

2.0 Background
The thin film deposition of pure metallic Ti and Ti-containing compounds like TiN or TiC, is an important
process in many fields, including aerospace, biomedical, and semiconductors1–4. Pure metallic Ti thin
films are used in several high-performance applications due to its attractive features such as excellent
corrosion resistance, high strength to weight ratio, biocompatibility, and good electrical conductivity.
Applications include (1) microelectronics and semiconductors (barrier layers, adhesion layers, contact
metal and getter layers); (2) biomedical devices (implant coatings, sensors, MEMS); (3) energy
applications (hydrogen storage, electrodes in electrochemical cells and supercapacitors, protective layers,
photovoltaics; (4) Aerospace and defense (corrosion-resistant caotings, optical coatings); (5) surface
engineering (wear resistant coatings); (6) sensors and thin film devices (strain gauges, gas sensors, resistive
heating elements); (7) various multilayer optical coatings for use in neutron supermirrors, Xray mirrors,
astronomy, laboratory laser experiments, semiconductor processing) etc.

The most common methods for pure metallic Ti thin film deposition are (1) Physical Vapor Deposition
(PVD) methods such as Sputtering (DC or Magnetron sputtering), Electron beam evaporation, thermal
evaporation; (2) Chemical Vapor deposition (CVD); (3) Atomic Layer Deposition (ALD); (4) Pulsed Laser
Deposition (PLD); (5) Cathodic Arc Deposition (CRD).
Challenges: Apart from CVD, most methods are slow, intensive in investment using expensive equipment
and resources. CVD offers a fast, scalable method for pure Ti thin film coating. However, deposition of Ti
thin films using CVD is challenging due to Ti’s high reactivity with oxygen and nitrogen5. Ti precursors
have been mostly used to deposit films of TiO2 or TiN. The common precursors for traditional thermal
CVD of Ti based depositions have been TiCl4 (or TTC), TiBr4, Titanium Alkylamides (e.g., TDMAT –

Tetrakis(dimethylamido)titanium), Titanium Organometallics such as (a) Titanocene dichloride: Cp₂TiCl₂;
(b) Tetrakis(cyclopentadienyl)titanium; (c) Ti(OiPr)₄ (Titanium isopropoxide – usually yields TiO₂ or
TIP), with halogen based TTC being the preferred precursor for most fabrications6 (Fig. 1).
Plasma approach to solution using non-halogen precursor: In recent years Plasma Enhanced Chemical
Vapor deposition (PECVD) has been studied intensively for their advantages in deposition of diverse
materials, not possible with traditional thermal CVD methods, with additional advantage of safer low
substrate temperatures7,8.
Traditionally, plasma deposition (e.g., PECVD) of Ti is produced by bringing a metal containing vapor
precursor and a carrier gas into a reactor vessel where it is dissociated into several radical fragments in the
plasma field. Ti radicals adsorb onto the desired substrate and remaining radicals are ideally flushed out of
system. The most common Ti precursor is titanium tetrachloride (TiCl 4, TTC).2 TTC is favored for it’s
simple molecular structure, yielding only Ti and Cl radicals in the plasma zone, and therefore, only Cl
radicals as potential sources of contamination or impurities in the Ti film. However, TTC explosively forms
TiO2 and HCl acid when exposed to ambient air. The resulting acid cloud is extremely toxic and harmful to
the environment. As such, there is a growing need to replace TTC with safer Ti precursors, such as titanium
isopropoxide (TiC12H28O4, TIP). While TIP is a much more benign and environmentally friendly precursor,
the O and CH3 group radicals formed during the LTP deposition process are potential sources of film
impurities (O, C, H), even with the expensive H2 co-flow. Thus, an in-situ LTP diagnostic tool is critical to
understanding the concentrations and behaviors of these O and CH3 radicals and their effects on the plasma-
substrate chemistry during deposition.
Need for optical and laser diagnostics of plasma-surface interactions: Due to the complexity of plasma
chemical reactions and plasma-surface interactions, it would be difficult to accurately predict the plasma
surface interaction processes and give meaningful practical recommendations for real applications. Most of
the physical and chemical interaction of technological plasmas which are of interest are confined at the
boundaries between plasma and materials. In particular, when the boundaries are actively treated and
processed by the plasma (and any associated neutral or radical species), such as in etching or deposition
applications, it is important to understand the spatial and temporal distribution of the species involved in
the plasma-surface interaction at the boundary. This highlights the importance of non-destructive evaluation
(NDE) experiments to quantify the ion concentrations and energies, and other properties close to the surface
such as electronic energy, rotational-vibrational temperatures of various species, for clear understanding of
the relation between various operation parameters and the PECVD film quality.
It would be useful to measure the various plasma chemical species close to the substrate with high time (ns)
and spatial (<10 µm) resolution to characterize the LTP deposition process accurately. This will help in
advancing the PECVD technology for various future applications. It will lead to repeatability, predictability,
and controllability of the deposited layers with minimized contamination and impurities.
Current methods of laser diagnostics: Currently various methods have been used to study plasma-surface
interactions. Thermoreflectance methods include Time Domain Thermoreflectance (TDTR) and Frequency
Domain Thermoreflectance (FDTR) to measure surface temperature changes. They work on the principle
of change in surface emissivity with temperature. These will require complex scanning methods to generate
spatial mapping and required resolution which can compromise on the required time resolution. For
thermoreflectance measurements, tedious surface emissivity calibration procedures are required before the
measurements which is not always possible.
Raman scattering to measure gas phase and adsorbed chemical species give weak or negligible signals at
low pressures of operation. To increase the signal level, the laser power can be increased and focused to the

 4

point of interest for measurements. However, such high laser powers can damage the surfaces being probed.
1D spatial resolution using Raman scattering has been obtained in only high-pressure combustion
environments.
Optical Emission Spectroscopy (OES) has been used to give abundant information about plasma. However,
OES is a line-of-sight signal collection method and does not achieve the desired spatial or temporal
resolution.
Laser-Induced Fluorescence (LIF) imaging: In Laser Induced Fluorescence (LIF), molecules are
electronically excited using a well-defined resonant laser wavelength characteristic of the molecule of
interest. The molecule de-excites, usually, by emitting light of a different wavelength. Using appropriate
optical band pass filters, the fluorescence can be collected or imaged to obtain the spatial resolution we
need.
LIF is a very sensitive technique to measure concentrations of species at very low concentrations (ppm
level).3,4 The collision quenching of electronically excited molecules by neighboring molecules is lower at
lower pressures. Therefore, LIF is a more sensitive measurement technique at lower pressures.
Two Photon Laser-Induced Fluorescence: LIF measurements of some species requires deep UV (< 200
nm). This can be circumvented by using two photon excitation LIF, using wavelengths twice that required
for one photon LIF excitation. The collision quenching cross sections are known for most molecular
collision partners. The PI has used laser pulse power as low as 10 µJ to obtain TALIF signals from O atoms
(<5 ppm) in air plasma at 60 torr5. The expected concentrations of several plasma species can be higher in
a low-pressure plasma due to low level of collisional quenching reactions. For e.g., O atom concentrations
were found to be ~368,000 ppm (mole fraction ~0.368) in a 6 mTorr Ar/O2 plasma6.

H atom concentration measurements by TALIF
The excitation wavelengths of light atoms are located, however, in the vacuum ultraviolet (VUV) spectral
range and are, therefore, only accessible with considerable experimental effort. Other problems are the
strong absorption of VUV radiation by many gases and even by the medium under investigation at elevated
densities (optical thickness).
These problems can be circumvented by two-photon laserinduced fluorescence (TALIF) spectroscopy (Fig.
3). The two-photon excitation is, however, much less efficient than the singlephoton excitation, so that
fluorescence signals can be obtained in most cases only with focused laser radiation. This entails good

 6

spatial resolution which is generally determined by the dimensions of the focus. The nonlinear character of
the two-photon excitation process excludes the applicability of Rayleigh calibration.
The proposed method is based on a comparative measurement at a spectrally close two-photon resonance
of a noble gas. This method is independent of other diagnostics and needs no major experimental
modifications. The well-known two-photon resonances of atomic hydrogen, nitrogen,and oxygen have
corresponding transitions in krypton and xenon.
Principle of the TALIF calibration method
The calibration is based on a reference measurement with a noble gas of known concentration. A
prerequisite for its applicability is that both TALIF excitations are performed with identical spatial, spectral,
and temporal intensity distribution of the laser radiation. This removes the necessity to know the intensity
distribution explicitly. The condition is best fulfilled if the two-photon resonances are spectrally as close as
possible. It is also necessary that the signal intensity as a function of laser energy obeys the same scaling
law for both measurements. It is therefore only possible to compare measurements where the excitation is
not influenced by saturation.

material and nanomaterial synthesis processes and how to control said chemistries to push the frontiers of
plasma science. In the proposed program, ACT will approach this overall goal by starting with an
exploration of the plasma-surface interactions in PECVD depositions of Ti thin films using the safe and
environmentally friendly TIP precursor (Fig. 6). To accomplish this effort, ACT will break down the work

Task 3- Conduct TALIF experiments at PCRF: ACT travelled to PCRF with the plasma reactor designed
and modified in Task 2 to conduct the preliminary experiments and measurements presented here.

 Fig. 15: Schematic of the laser setup to obtain 275 nm and tunable 205 nm (delayed by 11 ns) laser beams for the
 laser diagnostics.

A single laser was used to generate the required 275 nm and 205 nm beams as shown in Fig. 9.
The schematic of the laser setup up used for the measurements is shown in Fig. 16.
The laser is Spectra-Physics Solstice ACE, 800nm, 100fs, 1kHz, 6mJ/pulse. The laser pumps a Light
Conversion Topas Plus optical parametric amplifier (OPA), which has a wavelength converter allowing
tunable pulses from 190-2600nm (Fig. 16). The OPA generates 275nm, and using sum-frequency-mixing
with the fundamental (275nm+800nm) also generates 205nm at the same time. Using a stage delay we
controlled the relative time between the 275 and 205 beams, with 205 nm delayed by 11 ns. They are both
100fs@1kHz, and the pulse energies are ~2uJ/pulse for 205nm, and ~10uJ for 275nm. We use the 275nm
to dissociate the molecular species (CH4, CH3, CH2, but mainly CH4), and 205nm for the two-photon
excitation of the resulting H. Our experiments measured 3 µJ/pulse at 205 nm and 9 µJ/pulse at 275 nm.

6.0 Future tasks
Task 4- Quantify LTP-surface interactions during PECVD Ti deposition at PCRF: With calibration
complete, the next aim within the proposed three-week timeframe at PCRF is a design of experiments that
will be set up to systematically use fs TALIF to understand the effects of PECVD process parameters on Ti
film deposition. The aim is to specifically quantify and map methyl impurities across a substrate surface to
elucidate the LTP surface interactions driving the deposition chemistry. A list, though not exhaustive, of
process parameters includes flow rates, total pressure, substrate temperature and bias, power source,
magnitude of input power, and operational modes (continuous vs. burst). The use of fs TALIF will
demonstrate how these parameters influence radical generation, diffusion, and surface reactions.
Task 5- Measure LTP-surface interactions during optimal pure Ti and TiC deposition: Before the end
of the three-week stay at PCRF, all of the data garnered in tasks 3 and 4 will be analyzed to determine the
PECVD process parameters that best mitigate the impact of LTP methyl and O chemistry. With that
understanding, the last week at PCRF will be used to run PECVD parameters producing the least amount

 23

of methyl and O radicals to study the surface chemistry of Ti and TiC deposition using TIP as the precursor.
The fs TALIF measurements taken for O and H radicals will confirm previous findings in tasks 3 and 4.
Task 6- Ti and TiC film characterization: After returning from the trip to PCR
F, each of the Ti and TiC samples will be characterized to understand the nature of each deposited film.
This characterization will be conducted at the Materials Characterization Lab (MCL) at Penn State
University. Of specific interest will be the presence of methyl and O impurities within the films. The
characterizations will consist of at least the following techniques: XRD, XPS, SEM, EDS. The presence or
absence of impurities will correlate fs TALIF measurements to film characteristics, and work to establish
relationships between plasma parameters, radical behavior, and film characteristics.
Task 7- Reporting:
The reporting will consist of three major briefing meetings with contributions from the entire team: a kick-
off meeting, mid-project update, and final debrief. Furthermore, quarterly informal reports will be provided
to the DOE project manager. Additional meetings and teleconferences will take place as deemed necessary
to meet deadlines. A final report will be submitted at the end of the program.
A manuscript for a journal article publication is planned to be submitted in few weeks.

8.0 Changes in Approach or Aims
We developed a more novel method to predissociate CH3 to CH2+H as compared to the original proposal.
In the original proposal, we proposed to use two nanosecond lasers at 266 nm (for predissociation of CH3)
and 205 nm (for H atom TALIF measurements). However, after the award, the team developed a novel
method to use a single femtosecond laser for these measurements. A single beam from the laser is split into
two beams and converted to 275 nm and 205 nm (with delay) for the new scheme. This new method is
much simpler using a single laser and while providing spatially and time resolved measurements of CH3.
Use of femtosecond laser ensures higher levels of TALIF signals even at lower gas pressures.
