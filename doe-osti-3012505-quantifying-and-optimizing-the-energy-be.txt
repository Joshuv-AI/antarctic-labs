 ABSTRACT

The International Mass Timber Alliance (IMTA) is a global organization of industry leaders, engineers,
scientists, and associations dedicated to advancing mass timber construction. Its mission is to generate
and disseminate scientific data supporting the development of standardized construction and energy
efficient practices that promote the adoption of mass timber worldwide. IMTA collaborated with Oak
Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) to leverage ORNL’s expertise in building envelope modeling and
testing to evaluate how mass timber construction can reduce peak heating and cooling demand, lower
overall energy use, and improve resilience during power outages.

A previous study of 80 mass timber buildings in Finland found measured energy use up to 50% lower
than predicted by simulation. This project aimed to validate and extend those findings for U.S. buildings
through analytical modeling, laboratory testing, and full-scale building evaluations. The research focused
on the thermal performance of low-embodied-energy wall assemblies, such as cross-laminated timber
(CLT) panels and log walls, with particular attention to the effects of thermal inertia on indoor comfort
and energy performance.

While mass timber’s structural and fire-resistance properties are well documented, its whole-building
thermal behavior has received limited attention. Field data, simulation results, and resilience testing from
this study will inform future modeling practices, design guidelines, and construction practices by
quantifying the unique thermal and demand-flexibility benefits of mass timber construction.

 1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 PROJECT BACKGROUND

The International Mass Timber Alliance (IMTA) is an international organization comprising industry
leaders, engineers, scientists, and member associations working to promote mass timber building
globally. The organization operates exclusively for scientific purposes – testing, accumulating, and
disseminating data to support the development of standardized construction and energy efficient practices
that advance mass timber’s role in building construction.

In this project, IMTA and ORNL are building on the Laboratory’s extensive expertise in modeling and
measuring building envelope performance to investigate how mass timber construction influences energy
use, peak demand, and resilience. A primary objective is to quantify how thermal inertia can buffer
heating and cooling loads, reduce reliance on mechanical systems during peak demand, and extend
habitable conditions during power outages.

1.2 ADMINISTRATIVE NOTE

This Cooperative Research and Development Agreement (CRADA) formally concluded in March 2025
due to expiration of the agreement term without renewal. The research is continuing under Oak Ridge
National Laboratory’s Annual Operating Plan (AOP) to complete the experimental, modeling, and
analysis activities originally defined under the CRADA.

1.3 PRIOR WORK

A study conducted on 80 mass timber buildings in Finland revealed measured annual energy demands up
to 50% lower than those predicted by simulation. This project seeks to replicate and validate those

 1

findings in U.S. climate conditions using analytical modeling, laboratory testing, and full-scale field
monitoring of mass timber and lightweight-frame buildings.

 2. STATEMENT OF OBJECTIVES

The objective of this collaboration is to evaluate the thermal performance of mass timber structures
through a combination of controlled laboratory experiments and full-scale field studies conducted under
diverse climatic conditions. The research will quantify potential reductions in annual energy consumption
and peak demand achievable with mass timber construction, relative to conventional North American
lightweight-frame assemblies. Comparative performance data will be used to develop a predictive metric
for assessing the thermal behavior of mass timber assemblies, enabling improved accuracy in building
energy simulations and informing future design guidelines and construction practices.

 3. TECHNICAL APPROACH

3.1 LABORATORY TESTING

This section documents laboratory testing conducted on small test cubes constructed from two wall
materials: Cross-Laminated Timber (CLT) and Lightweight (LW) construction. Tests were conducted
under controlled "Summer" and "Winter" conditions to evaluate heating energy requirements, internal
temperature stability, and occupant comfort parameters, specifically Predicted Mean Vote (PMV) and
Predicted Percentage of Dissatisfied (PPD).

3.1.5 Conclusions from Laboratory Testing

  CLT cubes demonstrated lower and more stable heating energy demands, superior internal
 temperature stability, and generally better occupant comfort compared to LW cubes.
  Radiative and convective heating methods affected heating energy differently, with radiative
 heating generally causing greater variability in energy consumption differences between CLT and
 LW cubes.

The results demonstrate the thermal benefits of using CLT walls under controlled heating conditions,
mainly due to the advantageous impact of thermal mass.

3.2 FIELD TESTING

3.2.1 Locations and Setup

The project has constructed two test cubes (10’x10’x10’) in three different climate locations. One cube
was built with lightweight framing (2x6) and one with a single layer of mass timber (6 7/8” CLT). All six
sides of the cubes have the same structure and materials except the floor that has a floor OSB instead of
drywall. The cubes were assembled side by side in Oak Ridge, TN, Golden, CO, and Nacogdoches, TX
(Figure 10).

 Figure 10. Field test locations with corresponding climate zones.

Full-scale (10′ × 10′ × 10′) CLT and LW cubes were installed at:
  Oak Ridge, TN (Climate Zone 4A) – operational August 2024
  Golden, CO (Climate Zone 5B) – operational February 2025
  Nacogdoches, TX (Climate Zone 3A (~2A)) – operational June 2025

Figure 11 shows how the cubes were placed in the north-south axis to avoid shadowing at any time of the
year, and the picture shows the lightweight and mass timber cubes with mini-splits and doors on the north
side of the cubes. The cubes are intentionally simple with only opaque envelope components focused on
walls. Each test cube has a one-ton mini-split for heating and cooling. The cubes have been instrumented
to monitor indoor air temperature and operative temperature, air humidity, moisture contents of CLT,
temperature and relative humidity in the wall cavities (lightweight cube only), interior and exterior
surface temperatures, heat fluxes through the interior surfaces of walls, and electricity consumed by the
mini-splits.

3.2.2 Construction Details

All lightweight test cubes were clad with LP® SmartSide® siding, providing consistent exterior finishes
across locations. In Oak Ridge, TN, the CLT cube received a clear coat finish, while in Golden, CO, and
Nacogdoches, TX, the CLT cubes were stained to achieve solar absorption coefficients comparable to
those of the lightweight cubes. The siding’s solar reflectance is 0.29. The CLT panel reflectance values
were measured at 0.45 in Oak Ridge (clear-coated), 0.19 in Golden (stained), and 0.27 in Nacogdoches
(stained).

Lightweight cubes were constructed with conventional 2 × 6 wood framing at 16 inches on center,
sheathed with oriented strand board (OSB) incorporating an integrated water-resistive barrier (WRB) and
taped seams. Wall cavities were insulated with R-19 fiberglass batts in Oak Ridge and Nacogdoches, and
R-21 batts in Golden. Interior finishes consisted of 0.5-inch gypsum board in the lightweight cubes only.
CLT cubes, in contrast, consisted of a single 6 ⅞-inch-thick CLT layer with no additional cavity
insulation or interior finish. All cubes were fitted with 35-inch × 49-inch doors rated at R-5 h·ft²·°F/Btu.
The framing fraction of the lightweight cubes was approximately 23%, representative of typical North
American construction.

3.2.3 Air tightness

Prior to monitoring, air leakage tests were performed, and adjustments were made to ensure matched
airtightness between paired cubes at each site. In Oak Ridge, both cubes achieved 3 ACH50 (air changes
per hour at 50 Pa pressure difference), while in Golden and Nacogdoches, values were 6 ACH50.
Although these ACH values appear high, when expressed as air leakage per envelope area, the rates
ranged from 0.10 to 0.20 cfm/ft² at 75 Pa – well below typical limits such as the 0.25 cfm/ft² at 75 Pa
required by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE, 2012). It is important to note that ACH values
are influenced by the volume-to-surface-area ratio, making them unsuitable for direct comparison across
buildings of different sizes. Air leakage normalized by envelope surface area provides a more reliable
measure of airtightness for cross-scale comparisons.

3.2.4 Simulation tools

Measured field data will be used to validate the predictive capabilities of EnergyPlus™ (U.S. Department
of Energy, 2024) and WUFI®-Plus (Fraunhofer Institute for Building Physics, 2024) for assessing
thermal, moisture, and energy performance. Once validated, these models will be applied to extrapolate
the small-scale cube findings to whole-building scenarios across multiple climate zones. This modeling

 12

will support the evaluation of building envelope performance standards for mass timber walls relative to
conventional lightweight framing systems.

 4. TEST RESULTS

Field monitoring focused on quantifying differences in energy use, peak demand, and resilience between
mass timber (CLT) and lightweight-frame (LW) test cubes. Data were analyzed for daily and seasonal
performance, as well as under simulated power outage conditions.

4.1 ENERGY USE AND PEAK DEMAND

In Golden, CO, both cubes were conditioned to 23 °C (73.4 °F) during March 2025. Figure 12 presents
hourly mini-split energy use over three days. Peak demand occurred between 5:00 a.m. and 7:00 a.m. in
both cubes, with nearly identical magnitudes. While the CLT cube maintained continuous heating
throughout the day, the LW cube typically required no afternoon heating. Over the three-day period, the
CLT cube consumed ~50% more total energy than the LW cube – less than predicted based on effective
U-values.

Effective R-value measurements derived from in-situ heat flux and surface temperature data indicated R-
8.2 h·ft²·°F/Btu for the 6 ⅞-inch CLT layer in Golden (R-1.2 per inch), consistent with typical values for
solid wood. The area-weighted effective U-value of the LW cube was approximately 56% of that of the
CLT cube in Golden, and 60% in Oak Ridge, TN, and Nacogdoches, TX.

Summer cooling data (Figure 13) show that from 8:00 a.m. to 6:00 p.m. on July 9, the LW cube
consumed more cooling energy than the CLT cube, illustrating the latter’s ability to moderate and shift
internal heat gains due to thermal inertia. Monthly consumption profiles indicate that the CLT cube used,
on average, 57%, 35%, and 40% more electricity than the LW cube in January, April, and July 2025,
respectively (Figure 14, Figure 15, and Figure 16).

4.2 PREDICTED PERFORMANCE WITH SIMULATIONS

Preliminary WUFI® Plus and EnergyPlus™ simulations (Figure 17) for Golden, CO, using TMY weather
data predicted a significant delay in cooling demand onset for the CLT cube compared to the LW cube – a
shift of approximately four hours in peak cooling load (from ~5:00 p.m. to 9 pm). At the component level,
the CLT wall provided a 6 – 7 hour delay in peak heat flux. This predicted delay was not observed in
measured field data, indicating the need for further model validation. Simulation accuracy is sensitive to
thermal property inputs, particularly density, specific heat, and thermal conductivity. Because wood is
hygroscopic, these properties vary with moisture content (Flechas et al., 2025). Increases in moisture
content substantially raise thermal mass and modestly increase thermal conductivity, both of which can

 16

affect hygrothermal and energy modeling outcomes. Adjusting these properties to account for expected
in-service moisture contents is recommended, especially for unconditioned or high-humidity applications.
The thermal properties of the mass timber in the simulations were: density 31.2 lb/ft3 (500 kg/m3),
specific heat capacity 0.287 Btu/(lb·°F) (1200 J/kg/K), and thermal conductivity 0.0693 Btu·in/(h·ft²·°F)
(0.12 W/mK).

4.3 RESILIENCE DURING POWER OUTAGE

A simulated summer power outage was conducted in Golden, CO, by disabling active cooling while
keeping the circulation fan running. Over the eight-day event (Figure 18), peak operative temperatures
reached 94.5 °F in the LW cube and 87.9 °F in the CLT cube on the hottest day. This 6.6 °F difference
demonstrates the improved passive survivability of mass timber structures under extreme heat conditions.
Indoor air temperatures closely tracked operative temperatures in both cases.

 5. DISCUSSION AND PRELIMINARY CONCLUSIONS

Field testing in three climates confirmed that mass timber’s thermal behavior differs substantially from
lightweight-frame construction. While CLT assemblies consistently used more total heating and cooling
energy than LW assemblies under matched setpoints, the difference was smaller than predicted from
nominal R-values alone. Peak heating and cooling demands were often comparable, suggesting that
thermal inertia helps mitigate peak loads despite lower nominal insulation levels.

Key findings were:

  Energy Use: CLT cubes consistently exhibited greater total heating and cooling energy
 consumption compared to LW cubes when subjected to identical setpoints. However, the
 observed differences were smaller than those anticipated based on the nominal R-values of the
 materials. According to the effective U-values of the complete envelopes, the CLT cubes were
 expected to consume 100% more energy (twice as much) as the LW cubes. In reality, though, the
 measured energy usage was only 35–60% higher, depending on the location and season.
  Peak Demand: Peak heating and cooling loads were often comparable between CLT and LW
 cubes, indicating that thermal inertia helps limit peak demand despite lower insulation levels.
  Thermal Inertia Benefits: Summer results demonstrated that CLT assemblies can effectively
 moderate indoor temperature fluctuations and delay cooling loads by several hours –
 approximately four hours overall, and six to seven hours in terms of wall heat flux. This effect
 was particularly notable in shifting cooling loads from daytime to nighttime periods.
  Passive Survivability: Simulated outage testing demonstrated that CLT maintained significantly
 lower indoor peak temperatures (7 °F during hot days in Golden, CO) during extended cooling
 loss, enhancing occupant survivability in extreme conditions.
  Modeling Gap: Preliminary simulations predicted greater peak-load shifting than observed in
 field data (6-7 vs. 4 hours), highlighting the need to refine thermal property inputs particularly for
 hygroscopic materials whose properties vary with moisture content.

Summer results and simulated outage testing demonstrated that mass timber can moderate indoor
temperature swings, extending habitable conditions during cooling loss events. Preliminary simulations
suggest even greater peak load shifting potential than observed in the field, highlighting the need for
further validation of thermal property assumptions in energy models.

These results challenge the notion that lower nominal insulation levels in mass timber automatically result
in proportionally higher operational energy consumption. Instead, the combination of substantial thermal
mass and stable indoor conditions offers operational and resilience advantages not fully captured by
current modeling practices. The findings support:
  Refinement of building practices and design standards to recognize the unique thermal
 performance of mass timber.
  Adjustment of energy modeling tools to account for moisture-dependent thermal properties.

  Greater integration of resilience metrics into building envelope performance evaluations.

 6. BENEFITS TO THE BUILDING TECHNOLOGIES OFFICE’S MISSION

This project directly supports the Building Technologies Office’s goal to enable high-performing,
affordable buildings that meet Americans’ need for resiliency and health by evaluating how building
envelope characteristics influence demand flexibility and passive survivability. While mass timber’s
structural, fire resistance, and moisture performance benefits are well documented, its impacts on
operational energy, peak demand, and resilience remain underexplored.

By isolating and quantifying these thermal effects, this work advances the understanding of mass timber
as a building envelope strategy that can
  Moderate and shift heating/cooling loads to reduce peak demand
  Improve comfort and survivability during extended power outages
  Enhance the accuracy of whole-building simulation tools and
  Inform cost-effective, climate-appropriate design practices.

These outcomes contribute to BTO’s mission to deliver affordable, comfortable, and high-performance
buildings by integrating building envelope performance with energy efficiency and demand flexibility
objectives.
