Investigation of the Effects of Perfluorooctane Sulfonic Acid (PFOS) and Perfluorooctanoic Acid (PFOA) Exposure on Interleukin‐17 Signalling and Poly(I:C)‐Induced Lung Inflammation in BALB/c Mice
Authors: Eda Nur İnkaya, Seçil Karahisar Turan, Nurhayat Barlas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe aim of this study was to investigate the effects of co‐exposure to perfluorooctane sulfonic acid (PFOS) and perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) on the IL‐17‐related immune responses and the Poly(I:C)‐induced lung inflammation. A total of 66 adult male BALB/c mice were divided into 11 groups, including controls, dose groups receiving PFOS+PFOA (1, 3, 9 mg/kg/day), and combination groups challenged with intratracheal Poly(I:C). Immunological responses were evaluated using flow cytometry, RT‐qPCR and ELISA assays. Poly(I:C) stimulation increased Th1, Th17 and Th22 cell percentages, consistent with immune activation. PFOS+PFOA exposure attenuated these responses in a dose‐dependent manner under Poly(I:C) (+) conditions. Conversely, increased Th2 cell percentages were observed in PFOS+PFOA exposure groups. Gene expression analyses of Stat3, Il17ra and Ror‐γt further suggested tissue‐level modulation of IL‐17‐related signalling pathways. Overall, PFOS+PFOA exposure may modulate the pulmonary immune microenvironment and alter Th1/Th17/Th22‐associated responses to Poly(I:C)‐induced inflammatory stimulation. These findings suggest that PFAS mixtures may influence pulmonary immune regulation under inflammatory conditions and support the inclusion of immune endpoints in PFAS toxicological evaluation.

Introduction

Per‐ and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are a large group of highly fluorinated chemicals. The superior properties conferred by the carbon‐fluorine (C–F) bond include high chemical and thermal stability as well as water and oil repellency. Therefore, these compounds have become a preferred choice in industrial applications, which has facilitated their rapid and widespread adoption in consumer products [1]. Originally utilized in military and heavy industrial applications, their use extended to consumer sectors such as textiles, cookware, food packaging and fire‐fighting foams [2]. Currently, more than 20 000 PFAS‐related compounds have been identified in international inventories and databases [3, 4]. However, this silent threat first became apparent with the recognition of the bioaccumulation potential and environmental persistence of PFAS. The C8 Science Panel project, which was conducted to characterize exposure risks and stands as one of the most comprehensive epidemiological studies in the field, revealed that the problem was a global public health issue far exceeding initial estimates (C8 [5, 6]). In light of findings from subsequent studies, these chemicals, particularly PFOA (perfluorooctanoic acid) and PFOS (perfluorooctane sulfonic acid), have been associated with serious pathologies such as kidney and testicular cancer, thyroid disorders and high cholesterol [7]. In its 2024 evaluation report published under Monograph 135, the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classified PFOA as Group 1 (carcinogenic to humans) and PFOS as Group 2B (possibly carcinogenic to humans) [8]. Over the approximately 80‐year period from their initial industrial application to their current classification as carcinogens, the production volume of these chemicals has steadily increased. Consequently, PFAS compounds have become global pollutants, pervading soil, water resources and the food chain [1, 9]. Human biomonitoring studies have revealed that these substances not only accumulate in tissues but are also detected in vital body fluids such as blood serum, urine and even cerebrospinal fluid [10]. However, the most concerning aspect of this situation is the transfer of these compounds into breast milk, which is the primary source of nutrition for infants [11]. Neonates are now exposed to PFAS, referred to in the literature as ‘forever chemicals’, during the earliest stages of their lives. Furthermore, studies indicate that this exposure stems primarily from the maternal ‘body burden’ accumulated over time, rather than the mother's short‐term dietary intake alone. The elevated levels of PFOS and PFOA observed in the serum of exclusively breastfed infants demonstrate the severity of this situation [12].

PFAS exposure has been associated with adverse health outcomes, including immunosuppression and other disease‐related endpoints [9]. This relationship has gained particular attention during the COVID‐19 pandemic, where environmental and chemical exposures have been discussed as potential modifiers of immune response and disease susceptibility. The pandemic not only exposed global technological and sociological challenges but also revealed the ‘invisible’ impact of environmental pollutants on the immune system [13]. Despite living in an era of advanced technology, the variability in post‐vaccination antibody responses and the clinical course of the disease have not been fully explained. Previous studies on PFAS‐associated immunity suggest that immunotoxic chemicals, such as PFAS, may reduce vaccine antibody titres and compromise resistance to infections by attenuating the immune response [14]. A comprehensive systems biology analysis conducted by Wu et al. [15] investigated the molecular links between Endocrine Disrupting Chemicals (EDCs), such as PFAS, and COVID‐19 pathogenesis using in silico methods. The study revealed that the Th17 (IL‐17) signalling pathway is the most dysregulated mechanism shared by both conditions [15].

From a regulatory perspective, numerous PFAS compounds, including PFOA and PFOS, have been subjected to risk assessment by the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) under the Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA). Furthermore, analyses conducted within the framework of the Endocrine Disruptor Screening Program (EDSP) are investigating the potential effects of these compounds on oestrogen receptor bioactivity [3]. Despite these evaluations and identified risks, the use of short‐chain alternatives in place of long‐chain PFAS such as PFOA and PFOS continues in industrial applications under the assumption that they are safer. However, this approach has been described as a ‘regrettable substitution’, as the environmental persistence and biological effects of short‐chain compounds remain deeply concerning [16].

Although both PFOS and PFOA have been globally restricted under the Stockholm Convention [17, 18, 19], regulatory approaches and implementation strategies remain inconsistent across countries and regions [20]. Moreover, due to their high chemical stability, environmental persistence, long half‐lives and continued presence in legacy products manufactured before regulatory restrictions, PFOS and PFOA are still detected in environmental and indoor samples [2, 21].

Considering that current regulations evaluate PFAS individually rather than as a group, leaving the use of other compounds in a regulatory grey area, and given the existing knowledge gaps in immunotoxicity mechanisms, this study aims to investigate the effects of PFOS and PFOA mixture exposure on the basal IL‐17 signalling pathway. Furthermore, it seeks to determine how this interaction modulates Poly(I:C)‐induced lung inflammation. The computational prediction by Wu et al. [15] serves as the primary rationale for our study. Building on this theoretical framework, the experimental design is structured to translate these predictions into a laboratory setting and to empirically examine the interaction between PFAS exposure and a Poly(I:C)‐induced inflammatory stimulation on the IL‐17 axis. This study is based on the hypothesis that silent modulation of the basal immune response may lead to attenuated or exaggerated inflammation, thereby dysregulating the T‐helper response. In this context, the study aims to highlight the importance of considering the modulatory effects of environmental chemical exposures on the host immune landscape as a potential risk factor.

Material and Methods

Chemicals

Perfluorooctane sulfonic acid (PFOS; heptadecafluorooctanesulfonic acid potassium salt, CAS No. 1763‐23‐1, purity 95%) was obtained from Agilent Technologies (Santa Clara, USA). Perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA; pentadecafluorooctanoic acid, CAS No. 335‐67‐1, purity 95%) was purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, USA). Polyinosinic‐polycytidylic acid (Poly(I:C), CAS No. 31852‐29‐6) was procured from InvivoGen (Toulouse, France).

Experimental Animals and Ethical Approval

BALB/c mice ( Mus musculus ) are frequently used as model organisms in immune system studies and Poly(I:C)‐induced lung inflammation models. A review of the literature on lung inflammation indicates that adult mice aged 6–12 weeks are generally preferred [22, 23, 24]. Furthermore, pathologies such as acute lung inflammation have been found to follow a more severe course in males [25]. Based on these scientific considerations, 9‐week‐old adult male BALB/c mice weighing approximately 25–35 g were used in this study. The sample size was calculated using power analysis with G*Power 3.1 software (Heinrich‐Heine‐Universität, Düsseldorf, Germany). Based on an effect size of 0.7, a power of 0.95, and an alpha value of 0.05, the total sample size was determined to be 66. Accordingly, a total of 66 BALB/c male mice were obtained from the Hacettepe University Laboratory Animals Application and Research Center (Ankara, Türkiye). All experimental procedures and animal husbandry were approved by the Hacettepe University Local Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments (HÜ‐HADYEK, Approval No. 2022/05‐02). The study was conducted in strict accordance with the Turkish National Regulation on the Welfare and Protection of Animals Used for Experimental and Other Scientific Purposes (Official Gazette No. 28141, dated 13 December 2011), which is harmonized with the EU Directive 2010/63/EU. Animal Housing and Husbandry Conditions

Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups and housed in autoclave‐compatible polycarbonate cages (20 × 40 × 22 cm) equipped with wire lids. To ensure uniform exposure to laboratory conditions, cage positions were rotated regularly. During the experimental period, the photoperiod was maintained at a 12‐h light/12‐h dark cycle, with relative humidity at 50% ± 5% and temperature at 21°C ± 2°C. High‐absorbency, non‐dusting, and autoclave‐compatible standard bedding material was used. The animals were fed a standard pellet diet containing essential nutrients, vitamins, and minerals (Korkutelim Feed Factory, Afyon, Türkiye). Food and water were provided ad libitum. Body weights, as well as daily food and water consumption, were measured and recorded throughout the study; food and water supplies were replenished daily.

Preparation of Chemicals and Dose Selection

The exposure duration was established as 28 days, in accordance with the OECD Test Guideline 407 (Repeated Dose 28‐day Oral Toxicity Study in Rodents) protocol [26]. The study was conducted in accordance with the Basic & Clinical Pharmacology & Toxicology policy for experimental and clinical studies [27]. Due to the frequent co‐occurrence of PFOS and PFOA in environmental exposure conditions, the compounds were mixed in a 1:1 ratio to evaluate their potential cumulative toxicity. Since no universally accepted Lowest Observed Adverse Effect Level (LOAEL) has been established for PFOS+PFOA mixture exposure, dose selection was based on previously reported LOAEL levels and low‐effect dose ranges for each compound individually [28, 29]. In this context, based on the LOAEL values, the mixture doses were determined as 1 mg/kg/day, 3 mg/kg/day and 9 mg/kg/day, respectively. The selected dose range was designed to include both low‐effect and effect‐inducing exposure levels to evaluate potential dose–response and non‐linear biological effects associated with PFAS toxicology. Previous studies have reported that PFAS compounds may exhibit non‐linear toxicokinetic behaviour due to protein binding, renal transporter systems and long biological half‐lives [30, 31].

Stock solutions of PFOS and PFOA were prepared separately at a concentration of 2 mg/mL in distilled water (dH2O) containing 0.5% Tween‐20 [32]. Stocks were stored at +4°C and refreshed weekly to ensure stability. The administration volumes for the mice were calculated based on daily body weight measurements. The required amount was drawn from the stock solution and adjusted to the final volume with dH2O. Oral gavage was selected as the route of administration to mimic the primary human exposure pathway via contaminated drinking water [33]. Administrations were performed at the same time daily for 28 days. Following the 28‐day exposure period, Poly(I:C) was used to induce lung inflammation. Poly(I:C) induces pulmonary inflammation by triggering innate immune activation and increasing pro‐inflammatory cytokine levels [23, 24].

The steps for inducing lung inflammation were performed as follows: Mice were anaesthetised via intraperitoneal (IP) injection of a mixture of ketamine (65 mg/kg) and xylazine (4 mg/kg). Petrolatum was applied to the eyes to prevent corneal desiccation, and body temperature was maintained using a heating pad. The anaesthetised mice were positioned vertically at a 90° angle for intubation, and the tongue was gently retracted to visualise the trachea. A 22‐gauge intravenous catheter, which had been shortened to 20 mm, was inserted through the vocal cords into the trachea.

Poly(I:C) at a dose of 10 μg/g body weight was slowly instilled via the catheter. The vehicle control group received an equivalent volume (10 μL) of sterile PBS. To ensure homogeneous distribution of the fluid into the distal airways, a 50 μL air bolus was delivered immediately after instillation. Successful administration was confirmed by transient respiratory depression observed during the procedure. To prevent backflow of the fluid, mice were held in a vertical position for 30 s after instillation and monitored on a heating pad until recovery. Under deep anaesthesia, animals were euthanized by exsanguination 48 h after Poly(I:C) instillation.

Following exsanguination, the cervical region was disinfected with 70% ethanol to prevent contamination, and the trachea was exposed via dissection. For the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) procedure, the trachea was stabilized with a suture, and a small incision was made in the proximal region. A 22‐gauge intravenous catheter was inserted into the trachea and secured with a suture to prevent leakage. For the lavage, 0.5 mL of cold sterile PBS followed by 0.1 mL of air was instilled into the trachea via a syringe. After a 60‐s dwell time to allow for diffusion into the alveolar spaces, the fluid was gently aspirated. This procedure was repeated three times using fresh solution for each cycle, and the recovered aspirate (approximately 1.5 mL) was pooled into a single tube. All steps were performed on ice. The collected BAL fluid was centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min at +4°C. The supernatant was aspirated, aliquoted and stored at −80°C for cytokine analysis. The remaining cell pellet was transferred to sterile cryotubes and stored at −80°C for subsequent flow cytometry analysis of T lymphocyte and neutrophil populations.

To preserve tissue integrity and prevent blood contamination, lungs were perfused in situ with cold sterile isotonic saline (0.9% NaCl) prior to excision. Perfusion continued until the lung parenchyma was cleared of blood and appeared pale/whitish. Following the washing procedure, lung lobes were dissected and allocated for specific analyses. Samples were stored at −80°C for the determination of Stat3, Ror‐γt (or Ror‐c) and Il‐17ra gene expression levels.

Flow Cytometric Analysis of Bronchoalveolar Lavage Fluid (BALF) Samples

For BALF flow cytometry analyses, six mice were included in each experimental group. Because the number of cells recovered from individual BALF samples ranged from approximately 20 000 to 50 000 cells, and the multicolour flow cytometry panel required a minimum of 50 000 cells per analysis, BALF samples were pooled prior to staining and acquisition. Although pooling limits the direct assessment of animal‐level variability, independent pooled biological samples were generated by combining BALF cells from two mice within the same experimental group, resulting in three independent pooled samples per group. Each mouse contributed to only one pooled sample, and no individual BALF sample was used more than once in the final flow cytometry analysis. Therefore, the statistical unit for BALF flow cytometry data was the independent pooled biological sample, with n = 3 independent pools per group. Leukocyte and neutrophil populations were analysed using an Agilent NovoCyte 2060R flow cytometer (San Diego, CA, USA). To prevent non‐specific binding via Fc receptors, cell suspensions were incubated with anti‐CD16/32 antibody for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Following incubation, cells were washed with cell staining buffer (Elabscience, E‐CK‐A107). For surface staining, cells were stained with CD45‐PerCP (Cytek, Fremont, CA, USA) and Ly6G‐FITC (Invitrogen/11‐9668‐82, USA) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. After washing, samples were analysed on the flow cytometer. During analysis, debris was excluded based on FSC/SSC characteristics, and a CD45+ leukocyte gate was established. Within this population, neutrophils were defined as Ly6G+ SSC‐high cells. The remaining Ly6G− cell population was classified as non‐neutrophil cells and was not further characterized using specific lineage markers. No specific markers for macrophage subsets (e.g., CD11c, Siglec‐F) were included in the panel; therefore, macrophage populations could not be reliably identified or distinguished from other myeloid cell types.

For intracellular transcription factor staining, following Fc blocking, cells were subjected to fixation and permeabilization using the True‐Nuclear Transcription Factor Buffer Set (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Subsequently, cells were stained with a cocktail of CD45‐PerCP (Cytek/67‐0451‐U100), CD3‐CFBlue (Immunostep/MO3CFB), CD4‐Elab Bright Violet 510 (Elabscience/E‐AB‐F1353R1), RORγt‐FITC (Biorbyt/brb399909), GATA3‐PE (Bioss/bs‐1452R‐PE), T‐bet‐PE‐Cy7 (Invitrogen/25‐5825‐82), PU.1‐AF647 (Cell Signalling/2240S) and AHR‐AF750 (Novus Biologicals/NB100‐2289AF750) for 30 min in the dark. Afterwards, stained samples were analysed with Agilent NovoCyte 3005 flow cytometer. Briefly, cells were identified using FSC‐SSC plot and then CD45+ lymphocytes were gated using their CD45 positivity and low SSC properties. Doublet discrimination was performed using height vs. area plot of FSC detector and T helper cells were identified and gated using CD3 vs. CD4 plot. Finally, Th cell subsets were characterized based on their transcription factor expression profiles. FMO controls were used for transcription factor antibodies to define positive gates and minimize false‐positive gating. The same gating strategy was applied consistently to all experimental groups.

RNA Isolation and RT‐qPCR Analysis of Lung Tissue

To examine the effects of PFOS+PFOA on the IL‐17/Th17‐associated inflammatory axis at the gene expression level, the mRNA expression levels of Ror‐γt, a transcription factor associated with Th17 differentiation; Stat3, a signalling molecule involved in Th17‐related responses; and Il17ra, the receptor subunit for IL‐17 signalling, were analysed in lung tissue [34, 35]. Lung tissue samples were stored at −80°C until RNA isolation. RNA extraction was performed using the RNAqueous Total RNA Isolation Kit according to the manufacturer's protocol (Thermo Scientific, AM1912, USA). During the isolation process, 30 mg of tissue was weighed into RNase‐free EppendorF (Germany) tubes. Samples were homogenized in 300 μL of lysis solution using a plastic pestle. Subsequently, 600 μL of Proteinase K (Thermo Scientific, 00107841, USA) was added to the mixture and incubated at 24.5°C for 10 min. Following incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 10 min at 4°C to collect the supernatant. The supernatant was transferred to new RNase‐free tubes, and 450 μL of ethanol (96%–100%) was added and mixed thoroughly. A total of 700 μL of this mixture was transferred to filter columns and centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 1 min at 4°C. The flow‐through was discarded, and the remaining mixture was loaded onto the same column and centrifuged under the same conditions. A total of 700 μL of Wash Solution‐I was added to the column and centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 1 min at 4°C. After discarding the flow‐through, 600 μL of Wash Solution‐II was added to the column and centrifuged under the same conditions, and the flow‐through was discarded. Then, 250 μL of Wash Solution‐II was added to the column and centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 2 min at 4°C. In the final step, the column was transferred to a new RNase‐free Eppendorf tube, and 100 μL of nuclease‐free water was added to the center of the membrane. Samples were incubated at room temperature for 5 min and then centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 2 min at 4°C. Isolated RNA samples were stored at −80°C until further use. cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using the cDNA Synthesis Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions (Origene Technology, HP100042, USA). Synthesized cDNA samples were stored at −80°C until use. RT‐qPCR was performed using a SYBR Green‐based qPCR Master Mix (Thermo Scientific, K0221, USA) on a LightCycler 480 II Real‐Time PCR System (Roche Applied Science, Germany). Each reaction was carried out under standard amplification conditions, including initial denaturation followed by 40 cycles of denaturation, annealing and extension. Cycle threshold (Ct) values were obtained from the amplification curves. Gene‐specific primers were used to determine relative differences in mRNA expression levels between experimental groups. Primer sets targeting Stat3, Ror‐γt, and Il17ra and Gapdh as the reference gene (housekeeping gene) were commercially obtained from Origene Technology (USA). The corresponding reference sequences were NM_213659, NM_011281, NM_008359 and NM_008084, respectively. The specificity of the commercially obtained primer sequences was verified using the NCBI BLAST (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool) platform. Since there are different isoforms of the Ror‐γ gene (Ror‐γ and Ror‐γt), it was confirmed that the primers used in the study matched the mouse‐specific Ror‐γt mRNA. The sequences of the primers were as follows:

Ror‐γt: Forward: 5′‐GTGGAGTTTGCCAAGCGGCTTT‐3′

Reverse: 5′‐CCTGCACATTCTGACTAGGACG‐3′

Stat3: Forward: 5′‐AGGAGTCTAACAACGGCAGCCT‐3′

Reverse: 5′‐GTGGTACACCTCAGTCTCGAAG‐3′

Il17ra: Forward: 5′‐CTGTATGACCTGGAGGCTTTCTG‐3′

Reverse: 5′‐CGAGTAGACGATCCAGACCTTC‐3′

Calculations were performed using the following formula: ΔΔCt=ΔCtexperimental group−ΔCtcontrol group. ΔCt=Cttarget gene−CtGapdh. Relative expression=2−ΔΔCt

Cytokine Analyses

To evaluate the IL‐17‐associated cytokine response following PFOS+PFOA exposure, BALF levels of interleukin‐6, interleukin‐17A, interleukin‐17F, interleukin‐22 and interleukin‐23 were analysed. Cytokine levels in BALF samples were measured using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits according to the manufacturers' instructions. Commercial ELISA kits for IL‐6 (Cat. No. E‐EL‐M0044), IL‐17A (Cat. No. E‐EL‐M007), IL‐22 (Cat. No. E‐EL‐M2446), IL‐23 (Cat. No. E‐EL‐M0731) and the bicinchoninic acid (bcA) protein assay kit (Cat. No. E‐BC‐K318‐M) were obtained from Elabscience (China). The IL‐17F kit (Cat. No. E1183Mo) was obtained from BT‐Lab (China). For each BALF sample, total protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay, and cytokine concentrations were normalized to total protein content. Data were expressed as pg/mg protein. All analyses were performed using six biological replicates and two technical replicates. Absorbance values were measured at 450 nm using a BioTek μQuant microplate reader, and cytokine concentrations were calculated using a four‐parameter logistic (4PL) standard curve according to the manufacturers' instructions.

Statistical Analysis

ELISA and RT‐qPCR analyses were performed using individual biological samples, with n = 6 per experimental group. For BALF flow cytometry data, independent pooled biological samples were used as the statistical unit, with n = 3 pools per experimental group. Since, flow cytometry data expressed as percentages (%) may exhibit mean‐dependent variance, an arcsine‐square root transformation was applied to improve conformity with parametric test assumptions. The transformation formula used was: Y′=arcsin(Y/100). While statistical significance tests were conducted on transformed data to better meet ANOVA assumptions, graphs present untransformed raw data (percentage values) to maintain interpretability. The statistical analysis was conducted in two distinct stages. In the first stage, focused on model validation, one‐way ANOVA was employed to assess whether the vehicle substances and administration procedures induced any artefactual effects by comparing the Control, Tween‐20 (vehicle), PBS, Poly(I:C) and Tween‐20 + Poly(I:C) groups. Homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene's test; when this assumption was met, Tukey's post hoc test was applied, whereas Welch's ANOVA followed by the Games–Howell multiple comparison test was used when the assumption of homogeneity was not met. Since these preliminary analyses revealed no statistically significant differences between the Control and Tween‐20 vehicle groups (p > 0.05), the second stage of the analysis proceeded using the Tween‐20 group as the reference. Consequently, a two‐way ANOVA was conducted to evaluate the effects of PFOS+PFOA exposure dose, Poly(I:C) condition, and their interaction. Following two‐way ANOVA, Šídák's post hoc test was used to compare Poly(I:C) (−) and the corresponding Poly(I:C) (+) subgroups within each exposure level. Tukey's post hoc test was used to compare PFOS+PFOA dose groups separately within the Poly(I:C) (−) and Poly(I:C) (+) conditions.

All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.6.1, Boston, MA, USA). Asterisks (*) in figures denote significant differences between Poly(I:C) (−) and Poly(I:C) (+) subgroups (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001). Differences between dose groups (within Poly(I:C) (−) subgroups or Poly(I:C) (+) subgroups) are indicated by explicit p‐values on the graphs. A schematic overview of the statistical analysis workflow is provided in Figure S1.

Results

Immunophenotypic Alterations in BALF

One‐way ANOVA showed no significant differences between the Control and Tween‐20 vehicle groups and between the Poly(I:C) and Tween‐20 + Poly(I:C) groups (Figure S2). Two‐way ANOVA analysis revealed a statistically significant interaction between PFOS+PFOA dose and Poly(I:C) stimulation on CD45+ leukocytes (%) (F(3, 16) = 5.091, p = 0.012). This finding indicates that the effect of Poly(I:C) stimulation on CD45+ leukocytes (%) varied depending on the level of PFOS+PFOA exposure. In contrast, Poly(I:C) stimulation alone did not exert a significant main effect on CD45+ leukocytes (%) (F(1, 16) < 0.001, p = 0.993). Notably, a significant main effect of PFOS+PFOA exposure was observed (F(3, 16) = 3.427, p = 0.043), suggesting that PFOS+PFOA exposure modulated the rate of CD45+ leukocytes within the BALF (Figure 1A). Specifically, Šídák's post hoc analysis showed that Poly(I:C) stimulation significantly decreased CD45+ leukocytes (%) in the 9 mg/kg/day PFOS+PFOA subgroup. However, no significant differences were detected in the other treatment subgroups. According to the Tukey's multiple comparison test, no significant differences were detected among groups in the Poly(I:C) (−) condition. However, in the Poly(I:C) (+) condition, the 9 mg/kg/day PFOS+PFOA group showed a significant decrease in comparison with the tween‐20 vehicle and 1 mg/kg/day groups (Figure 2A).

Discussion

This study presents findings on how subacute PFOS+PFOA exposure may affect pulmonary immune responses in a Poly(I:C)‐induced lung inflammation model. PFAS exposure has previously been reported in both toxicological and epidemiological studies to have immunomodulatory effects. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying PFAS‐associated immunomodulation have not yet been fully elucidated [30, 36].

Only a limited number of studies have examined PFAS‐related effects on Th‐associated immune responses. However, available studies provide useful insights. In the present study, under Poly(I:C) (−) conditions, the percentage of Th2 cells increased in PFOS+PFOA exposure subgroups. Under Poly(I:C) (+) conditions, although the increase in Th2 cells was less noticeable, elevated levels were still maintained in exposure groups. This pattern suggests that PFOS+PFOA exposure may modulate the basal pulmonary immune microenvironment towards a Th2‐associated immune profile. Under Poly(I:C) (−) conditions, the percentage of Th1 cells was lower in the 9 mg/kg PFOS+PFOA group compared with the Tween‐20 vehicle subgroup. Under Poly(I:C) (+) conditions, Th1 cell percentages showed a dose‐dependent decrease across the PFOS+PFOA exposure subgroups. This pattern may indicate that PFOS+PFOA exposure modulates Th1‐associated cellular immune responses. This finding may be relevant because Th1‐associated immune responses play an important role in cellular immunity, particularly in host defence against intracellular pathogens [37, 38]. The Th1‐cell findings may be of particular relevance because, unlike the Th2‐cell response, a significant interaction between PFOS+PFOA exposure and Poly(I:C) stimulation was detected for Th1 cells. Therefore, attenuation of the Th1‐cell response under Poly(I:C) (+) condition following PFOS+PFOA exposure may suggest a reduced capacity to mount an effective cellular immune response following inflammatory stimulation. A previous PFOS animal study similarly reported modulation towards a Th2‐like cytokine profile, based on decreased Th1‐type cytokines and increased Th2‐related cytokine production in ex vivo splenocyte cultures [39]. In another animal study, mice were exposed to PFOA before infection with Theiler's murine encephalomyelitis virus. In contrast to studies reporting modulation toward a Th2‐like immune state after PFOS exposure, PFOA exposure was associated with an altered Th1 cytokine response and suppression of Th2 cytokines in serum samples [40]. Together, these studies suggest that the Th1/Th2 axis may represent a potential immunological target of PFAS exposure.

Under Poly(I:C) (+) conditions, Th17 and Th22 cell percentages decreased with increasing PFOS+PFOA dose across the exposure subgroups. A similar pattern was also observed for IL‐17A and IL‐17F, the main cytokines associated with Th17 responses, and for IL‐22, the main cytokine associated with Th22 responses. These findings suggest that PFOS+PFOA exposure may attenuate Th17‐ and Th22‐associated airway immune responses under Poly(I:C)‐induced inflammatory conditions. To date, only a limited number of studies have examined this issue, and their findings remain heterogeneous. In a mouse model of influenza A virus infection, Post et al. [41] examined the effects of a PFOS+PFOA mixture on immune responses. They reported no significant differences in Th1, Th2, Th17 or Tfh cell subsets in mediastinal lymph nodes on day 9 post‐infection [41]. Although Th17 cells are considered the major source of IL‐17A and IL‐17F, these cytokines are not exclusively produced by Th17 cells. They may also originate from other type 17 immune cells, including γδ T cells, Tc17 cells, and ILC3s. Therefore, the reduced BALF IL‐17A and IL‐17F levels observed under PFOS+PFOA exposure may reflect broader disruption of type 17 cytokine output rather than changes in Th17 cells alone [42]. Suo et al. [43] investigated intestinal immunity in a mouse model of Citrobacter rodentium infection following PFOS exposure. In the early phase of infection, PFOS reduced pathogen burden and increased IL‐22 production from intestinal ILC3s. At this stage, IL‐17 production and Th17 cell percentages were not markedly affected. However, during the late phase of infection, PFOS exposure was associated with increased IL‐17 and IL‐22 production from ILC3s and Th17 cells. Despite this enhanced cytokine response, PFOS‐treated mice showed impaired pathogen clearance, accompanied by persistent intestinal inflammation [43]. These findings suggest that mucosal immune endpoints may be important for understanding PFAS‐related immunomodulatory effects. IL‐17A and IL‐17F are known to regulate protective immune responses at pulmonary, mucosal and epithelial barrier surfaces [44]. In this context, the significant interaction between Poly(I:C) stimulation and PFOS+PFOA exposure affecting IL‐17A and IL‐17F levels may indicate a disruption of lung mucosal immune regulation. Although long‐term outcomes were not assessed in the present study, these findings should be further explored to better understand the potential consequences of PFOS+PFOA exposure for pulmonary barrier‐associated immune defence. In addition, IL‐23 levels were increased, suggesting that upstream type 17‐promoting cytokine signals were present. Therefore, the decrease in IL‐17A/IL‐17F despite increased IL‐23 levels may indicate a possible uncoupling within the IL‐23/IL‐17 axis. This suggests that IL‐23 upregulation alone may not have been sufficient to sustain IL‐17A/IL‐17F production or release following PFOS+PFOA exposure under Poly(I:C) (+) condition [45, 46].

The most notable pattern in our data was the upregulation of Stat3, Rorγt and Il17ra mRNA expression levels in lung tissue, particularly under Poly(I:C) (+) conditions. This pattern suggests a type 17‐related transcriptional response in the lung tissue. However, because Stat3 activation was not assessed at the level of protein phosphorylation, increased Stat3 mRNA expression should not be interpreted as confirmed activation of Stat3 signalling. In addition, the increase in Stat3 mRNA expression observed in lung tissue homogenates may reflect not only T‐cell‐associated signalling but also stress and repair responses of structural cells, particularly epithelial cells. In this context, the reduced IL‐22 levels observed in BALF may also be relevant to epithelial barrier integrity and tissue‐repair responses. Previous studies have shown that the IL‐22/STAT3 axis contributes to epithelial protection and repair during inflammatory‐related lung injury [47, 48, 49]. Therefore, reduced IL‐22 responses under PFOS+PFOA exposure in both Poly(I:C) (−) and (+) conditions may suggest a limitation in cytokine‐mediated epithelial protective signalling, whereas increased Stat3 mRNA expression in lung tissue may reflect a broader tissue‐level stress or repair‐associated transcriptional response. Together, these findings suggest a possible imbalance between epithelial repair‐associated transcriptional responses in lung tissue and protective cytokine signals detected in the airway compartment. Il17ra upregulation in lung tissue may reflect enhanced responsiveness of pulmonary target cells to IL‐17 family cytokines. Therefore, together with increased Stat3 and Rorγt expression, this finding supports a broader type 17/IL‐17‐related tissue response in the lung, despite the reduced IL‐17A and IL‐17F levels detected in BALF.

However, the direction of this immune modulation may vary depending on the specific PFAS compound, exposure conditions, dose, inflammatory context and the tissue or biological sample examined. This variability may partly reflect differences in the focus of previous studies, including the use of single PFAS compounds or different PFAS mixtures, different infectious or inflammatory models, and distinct immune compartments such as serum, cell cultures, lymph nodes, intestinal tissue or lung tissue [39, 40, 41, 43]. In this context, the present study adds to the existing literature by examining the effects of subacute PFOS+PFOA mixture exposure on pulmonary Th‐associated cellular responses, BALF cytokine levels and lung tissue gene expression under both basal and Poly(I:C)‐induced inflammatory conditions. Similar to animal studies, human studies also suggest that PFAS exposure may affect immune function. However, the available evidence remains heterogeneous across populations and immune‐related endpoints [50, 51, 52].

The variability observed in animal and human studies highlights the difficulty of predicting PFAS‐related immune outcomes, particularly under environmentally relevant conditions in which exposure occurs as a mixture rather than as a single compound. Therefore, the combined immunological effects of PFAS mixtures may not be easily inferred from single‐compound studies alone, supporting the need for mixture‐based exposure models.

The present study was designed to provide exploratory mechanistic insights into how PFOS+PFOA exposure may modulate pulmonary immune responses under Poly(I:C)‐induced inflammatory conditions, not to directly assess viral infection severity or clinical outcomes. Our findings are consistent with recent reviews on PFAS‐related immunotoxicity, which have highlighted that these chemicals may affect immune responses at multiple levels and should be considered within a broader public health perspective [7].

A major strength of the present study is the evaluation of PFOS+PFOA exposure under both basal and Poly(I:C)‐induced inflammatory conditions. This design allowed us to assess not only the effects of PFOS+PFOA exposure on the basal pulmonary immune environment, but also its impact on the immune response to a Poly(I:C)‐induced inflammatory stimulus. Several limitations should also be acknowledged. In the present study, the 28‐day PFOS+PFOA exposure period was selected as a repeated‐dose exposure window to evaluate subacute immunomodulatory effects [26]. However, because PFOS and PFOA concentrations were not quantified in serum, lung tissue or excreta, the internal exposure burden, tissue accumulation levels, and toxicokinetic steady state associated with the applied doses could not be confirmed. Future studies including quantitative measurements of PFOS and PFOA levels in serum, lung tissue and excreta would contribute to a more detailed evaluation of the internal dose–response relationships. In addition, the 48‐h time point following Poly(I:C) stimulation was selected to evaluate acute inflammatory responses based on previous Poly(I:C)‐induced lung inflammation studies [23, 53]. Although this time point may not necessarily represent the absolute peak of inflammation, it reflects an appropriate window for evaluating acute inflammatory responses. Although the Poly(I:C) model reproduces several features of virus‐mimetic lung inflammation through TLR3 activation, it does not fully recapitulate the complex host‐pathogen interactions observed during live viral infections, including viral entry, replication and systemic immune responses. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as mechanistic observations obtained in a controlled Poly(I:C)‐induced lung inflammatory condition rather than as direct evidence from an infectious disease model.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that subacute exposure to PFOS+PFOA may reshape the pulmonary immune environment and modify the response to a Poly(I:C)‐induced inflammatory stimulus. Specifically, PFOS+PFOA exposure may alter the basal balance of Th cell subsets, thereby influencing the nature of inflammatory responses. Importantly, these effects did not follow a linear dose pattern across all endpoints. This suggests that PFOS+PFOA exposure may induce context‐ and dose‐dependent immune modulation rather than a simple pattern of immune suppression. Future studies should investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying tissue‐BALF uncoupling in both sexes, including post‐transcriptional regulation, cytokine release and immune‐cell pathways. Our findings support the inclusion of immune endpoints sensitive to PFAS mixtures in PFAS toxicological evaluation, particularly in immune activation models.
