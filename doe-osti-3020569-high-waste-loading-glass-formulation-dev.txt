 SECTION 1.0
 INTRODUCTION

 Projections of the number of high level waste (HLW) canisters to be produced in the
Hanford Tank Waste Treatment and Immobilization Plant (WTP) (e.g., [1]) are based upon the
inventory of the tank wastes, the anticipated performance of the sludge treatment processes, and
current understanding of the capability of the borosilicate glass waste form. The WTP HLW
melter design, unlike earlier Department of Energy (DOE) melter designs, incorporates a glass
bubbler system. The bubblers create active glass pool mixing and thereby improve heat and mass
transfer and glass melting rate. The WTP HLW melters each have a glass surface area of 3.75 m 2
and depth of ~1.1 m. The two melters in the HLW facility together are designed to produce up to
7.5 MT of glass per day at 100% availability. Further increases in HLW waste processing rates
can potentially be achieved by optimization of the feed and glass formulations, increasing the
waste loading in the glass product, and by increasing the melter operating temperature above
1150C. Increasing the waste loading also has the added benefit of decreasing the number of
canisters for storage.

 The baseline WTP glass formulation efforts have been conservative in terms of
achievable waste loadings. These baseline formulations have been specified to ensure that the
glasses are homogenous, contain essentially no crystalline phases, are processable in joule-
heated, ceramic-lined melters, and meet WTP contract requirements. The overall WTP mission
will require the immobilization of tank waste compositions that are dominated by mixtures that
include aluminum, chromium, bismuth, iron, phosphorous, zirconium, and sulfur compounds as
waste loading limiting components. In order to improve waste loadings, DOE’s Office of River
Protection (ORP) initiated a testing program to develop and characterize HLW glasses for wastes
that are limited by Al, Al plus Na, Bi, and Cr [1, 2]. Results of that work have demonstrated the
feasibility of increases in waste loading from about 25 wt% to 33-50 wt% (based on oxide
loading) in the glass, depending on the waste stream. It is expected that these higher waste
loading glasses will reduce the HLW canister production requirement by about 25% or more [3].
Furthermore, it has been shown that a key technological risk area relates to the strong
dependence of glass production rate on waste composition [3]. The extent of this variation across
the full spectrum of HLW waste types needs to be quantified in order to accurately project waste
treatment rates.

 Under a separate contract with Bechtel National Incorporated (BNI) to support the WTP,
the Vitreous State Laboratory (VSL) of the Catholic University of America (CUA) has
developed and tested glass formulations for WTP HLW compositions to provide data to meet the
WTP contract requirements and to support system design activities [4-9]. That work was based
upon small­scale batch melts (“crucible melts”) using waste simulants. Selected formulations
were also tested in continuously fed, joule-heated melters, DuraMelter (DM)100 and DM1200
[10-15]. That testing was directed towards waste streams from the then-planned early feed tanks
for the WTP (i.e., AZ-101, AZ-102, C-106/AY-102, and C-104/AY-101). These wastes are high

 10

in iron (AZ-101, AZ-102 and C-106/AY-102) or thorium (C-104/AY-101) and are significantly
different from those used in subsequent enhancement tests performed for ORP (i.e., wastes
limited by Al, Al/Na, Bi, and Cr). Baseline glass formulations to treat these high-Fe wastes were
developed under the BNI contract. During that time, the throughput requirement for the HLW
melter was initially 400 kg/(m2·day), which was subsequently increased to 800 kg/(m2·day). As a
result, the baseline high-Fe HLW glass formulations for WTP perform only slightly better than
the 800 kg/(m2·day) processing rate requirement. Furthermore, the baseline waste loadings for
the Fe-limited HLW compositions are only slightly higher than the BNI contract minimum. In
subsequent work performed for ORP on other HLW compositions, VSL has developed small-
scale test methods to assess the processing rates of melter feeds and included them as an integral
part of glass formulation development. This methodology was used successfully to develop glass
formulations for high-Al Hanford HLW that showed processing rates in excess of
2000 kg/(m2·day) while achieving high waste loadings. The same methodology can be applied to
the development of improved glass formulations for other Hanford HLW streams in order to
provide ORP with a significantly more robust operating envelope with reduced risk of
throughput shortfalls. Consequently, a wide range of HLW Hanford waste streams rich in
constituents such as iron [16], bismuth [17], sulfur [18, 19], chromium [20], strontium [21], and
zirconium [22], which have the potential to limit waste loadings and production rates, are being
addressed in a series of glass formulation development and melter testing tasks.

 This report describes the results from work performed to develop and test new glass and
feed formulations in order to increase waste loadings for Hanford HLW with high concentrations
of manganese. A further objective of the work was to determine the effect of the form of
manganese in Hanford HLW on feed processing rates. A number of HLW batches to be
processed at the WTP have manganese concentrations that extend above about thirteen weight
percent on an oxide basis. These high manganese HLW streams frequently contain significant
amounts of chromium, iron, and nickel, which together have the tendency to form secondary
phases such as spinels that can limit waste loading or lead to poor cold cap behavior and slow
processing rates [23]. Previous studies for ORP have explored some of the HLW streams that
contain relatively high concentrations of iron and chromium with manganese [23]; however,
those studies did not focus on HLW streams for which glass waste loading is manganese limited.

 Manganese is present in Hanford tank waste in a variety of forms including MnO, MnO2,
MnCO3, spinels and hydroxides with iron and nickel, and oxalates [24-26]. These differences in
manganese form have the potential to affect waste and melter feed rheology, feed processing
rates, and the extent of crystalline phases in the glass product, as has been observed with
different forms of aluminum and iron in HLW waste simulants [27-29]. In particular, glass
formulation development for high manganese HLW can be challenging due to spinel formation,
especially in the presence of other spinel forming elements such as iron, chromium, and nickel
[6, 12, 23, 30]. In addition, foaming of the glass pool during vitrification has been attributed to
manganese oxidation state changes [13-15] and this may also be affected by the form of
manganese in the waste. These issues were addressed by developing glass formulations for a
Hanford HLW stream with high concentrations of manganese followed by melter testing with the
selected glass composition with feed containing manganese in three different forms likely to be
present in Hanford tank wastes. A similar strategy was employed successfully for determining

 11

the differences in the feed and glass properties and feed processing characteristics of HLW feeds
with different forms of aluminum and iron [27-29].

 Testing was conducted to identify glass and melter feed formulations that optimize waste
loading and waste processing rate while meeting all processing and product quality requirements.
A high-manganese HLW composition derived from the inventory data on insoluble components
from the Best Basis Inventory (BBI) Hanford Tank Waste Operations Simulator (HTWOS)
model run (April 17, 2011) [31] was used as the basis for the present glass formulation
development and melter testing. The work included preparation and characterization of crucible
melts to evaluate the effects of waste loading on glass properties and the maximum waste
loadings that can be achieved. Testing included assessment of melt rate using a Vertical Gradient
Furnace (VGF) system to identify formulations with high melt rates as well as high waste
loadings. The results from crucible-scale testing supported subsequent melter tests on a DM100
vitrification system designed to examine the effects of enhanced glass and feed formulations on
waste processing rate and product quality. DM100 melter tests were conducted with feeds
containing the different forms of manganese under nominal conditions (1150°C glass
temperature, 9 lpm bubbling) so that the effect of the form of manganese on feed processing rate
and processing characteristics could be determined. Samples of the glass product were collected
and analyzed for composition and crystalline phases. Data on processing rate and any processing
difficulties such as melt pool foaming, off-gas composition, and system operating parameters
were collected. This work builds on previous work performed at the VSL for ORP to increase
waste loadings and processing rates of HLW glass formulations [3, 16, 20-22, 32, 33]. The
testing was performed according to the Test Plan that was developed for this work [34].

1.1 Test Objectives

 One of the primary objectives of the work described herein was to develop and identify
HLW glass compositions and glass forming additive blends that achieve high waste loadings and
processing rates for high manganese HLW streams while maintaining acceptable glass
properties. Another objective was to determine the effect of the form of manganese (MnO,
MnO2, MnCO3) on feed processing properties, glass production rates, and product quality while
vitrifying a high manganese HLW stream. This was accomplished through a combination of
crucible-scale tests, vertical gradient furnace tests, and confirmation tests on a DM100 melter
system.

1.3 DM100 Melter System

        1.3.1    DM100 Feed System

 A schematic diagram of the DM100 vitrification system is shown in Figure 1.1. The
melter feed is introduced in batches into a feed container that is mounted on a load cell for
weight monitoring. The feed is stirred with a variable speed mixer and constantly recirculated
except for periodic, momentary interruptions during which the weight is recorded. A peristaltic
pump is used to provide a uniform delivery of feed to the melt surface. Feed is directed from the
recirculation loop that extends to the top of the melter and then diverted to the peristaltic pump,
which regulates the flow of feed through a Teflon-lined feed line and water-cooled feed tube into
the melter.

 1.3.2 Melter System

 Cross-sectional diagrams of the DM100-BL melter are shown in Figures 1.2.a-c. The
DM100-BL unit is a ceramic refractory-lined melter fitted with five electrodes: two pairs of
opposing Inconel 690 plate electrodes and a bottom electrode. Power can be supplied in either
three-phase or single-phase configurations. All of the tests in the present work were performed
with the upper and lower electrodes on each side connected together and powered by a
single-phase supply; the bottom electrode was not powered. Melt pool agitation is achieved by
either a removable lance bubbler entering from the top of the melter or a permanent bubbler
installed through the bottom electrode. In these tests, the lance bubbler was used. The glass
product is removed from the melter by means of an airlift discharge system. The melter has a
melt surface area of 0.108 m 2 and a variable glass inventory of between 110 kg, when only the
 P
 P

bottom pair of electrodes is used, and about 170 kg when both pairs of electrodes are used, which
was the case in the present tests.

 1.3.3 Off-Gas System

 For operational simplicity, the DM100-BL is equipped with a dry off-gas treatment
system involving gas filtration operations only. Exhaust gases leave the melter plenum through a
film cooler device that minimizes the formation of solid deposits. The film-cooler air has
constant flow rate and its temperature is thermostatically controlled. Consequently, under
steady-state operating conditions, the exhaust gases passing through the transition line (between
the melter and the first filtration device) can be sampled at constant temperature and airflow rate.
The geometry of the transition line conforms to the requirements of the 40-CFR-60 air sampling
techniques. Immediately downstream of the transition line are cyclonic filters followed by
conventional pre-filters and high efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters. The temperature of the
cyclonic filters is maintained above 150°C while the temperatures in the HEPAs are kept

 13

sufficiently high to prevent moisture condensation. The entire train of gas filtration operations is
duplicated and each train is used alternately. An induced draft fan completes the system.

1.4 Experimental Methods

 1.4.1 Feed Conversion by Vertical Gradient Furnace (VGF) Testing

 Figure 1.3 shows a schematic diagram of the VGF setup. The temperature gradient inside
the VGF is maintained by two separate sets of heating elements, both of which are arranged in
cylindrical form and aligned along their axes. The inner heater is set at 1150oC, which is the
nominal temperature of the glass pool, and the ambient heater is set at 600oC, which is similar to
the melter plenum temperature. A ceramic crucible (4 inches tall) is used to contain the reacting
melter feed. The temperature gradient in the test crucible is shown in Figure 1.4. For a typical
feed conversion test, 10 grams of glass of identical chemical composition to the test feed
(expressed on an oxide basis) is preheated in the ceramic crucible positioned in the inner heater
before the dried melter feed (to yield 17 grams of glass) is introduced. Feed reactions under the
controlled temperature gradient are allowed to continue for the designated test duration
(typically, from 30 to 90 minutes) and then stopped by rapid cooling in room temperature air.
The top surface of the reacted feed material is then inspected and photographed. The crucibles
with their feed contents are then cross-sectioned to reveal the conversion progress of feed blends.
The saw cuts of the crucibles are performed dry (without lubricant) to avoid loss of any soluble
material.

 To characterize the reacted feed material, visual inspection and digital imaging of the top
(by photography) and cross section (using an optical scanner) of the reacted sample are
performed. The VGF results are used to evaluate the melt rate on a relative scale using the degree
of melting that has occurred, the structure of the feed materials that are undergoing reaction and
transformation, and the feed conversion progression with time. A VGF sample showing
relatively more feed conversion and melting is classified as fast whereas a sample showing
relatively less feed conversion and melting is classified as slow. In cases where multiple VGF
samples are tested, a numerical ranking of relative melt rate is assigned based on a correlation
using feeds that were tested on the VGF and whose melt rates had been determined previously in
DM100 melter tests [27]. The numerical rankings of feed samples based on the extent of feed
conversion from observation of the top and cross section of VGF samples is given in Table 1.1.
The rankings span the range of 1 to 6, with a feed sample ranked 1 expected to exhibit a fast
processing rate and a feed sample ranked 6 expected to show a slow processing rate.

 1.4.2 Feed Composition and General Properties

 Feed samples were taken directly from as-received drums and the melter feed
recirculation line during each test. Feed samples were poured into a platinum/gold crucible and
placed into a programmed furnace for drying and fusion to form a glass. The glass produced
from this fusion was ground to less than 200 mesh and sealed in 20-ml vials for subsequent
analysis by X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy (XRF), or by acid digestion followed by

direct-current plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (DCP-AES) on the resulting solution. The
feed samples were also characterized for their density, pH, water content, and glass yield.

 1.4.3 Crucible and Melter Product Glasses

 The glass product is discharged from the melter into 5-gallon steel pails periodically
using an air-lift system. The discharged product glass is sampled at the end of each test by
removing sufficient glass from the top of the pails for compositional analysis and secondary
phase determination. In addition, sufficient glass from the end of tests with each form of
manganese was collected to perform the Product Consistency Test (PCT, 7 days at 90 oC) and
Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure (TCLP). Prior to those tests, characterization with
respect to PCT, TCLP, melt electrical conductivity, melt viscosity, and secondary phases was
performed on the crucible melt compositions to select a formulation for melter testing that is
compliant with the present WTP contract requirements. All of these procedures are routinely
conducted at VSL and, therefore, standard operating procedures are in place [36]. Sample
preparation for chemical analysis begins with size reduction and sieving. All samples are then
subjected to XRF analysis to determine the concentrations of all elements except boron and
lithium. A series of National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) reference materials
are used for confirmation of the XRF data. Boron and lithium are determined by total acid
dissolution of ground glass samples in HF/HNO3 and subjecting the resulting solutions to
DCP-AES analysis. Glass samples from melter tests with different manganese sources were also
analyzed for iron redox state by Mössbauer Spectroscopy using glass prepared as for the XRF
analysis.

                 1.4.3.1 Viscosity

 The melt viscosity, η, is measured using a Brookfield viscometer. Measurements are
performed in the temperature range of 950-1250ºC and the data are interpolated to standard
temperatures using the Vogel-Fulcher equation: ln η = [A/(T-To)]+B, where A, B, and To are
fitting parameters. The equipment is calibrated at room temperature using standard oils of known
viscosity and then checked at 950-1250ºC using a NIST standard reference glass (SRM 711).
Both precision and accuracy of the viscosity measurements are estimated to be within ±15
relative%.

 1.4.3.2 Electrical Conductivity

 The electrical conductivity, σ, of each glass is determined by measuring the resistance of
the glass melt as a function of frequency using a calibrated platinum/rhodium electrode probe
attached to a Agilent model 4194A impedance analyzer. Measurements are performed over
similar temperature ranges to those employed for the melt viscosity measurements. The results
are analyzed to obtain the DC electrical conductivity. The electrical conductivity data are then
interpolated to standard temperatures using the Vogel-Fulcher equation: ln  = [A/(TTo)] + B,

where A, B and To are fitting parameters. Estimated uncertainties in the electrical conductivity
measurements are  20 relative%.

 1.4.3.3 Product Consistency Test (PCT)

 The product consistency test (PCT; ASTM C 1285) [37] is used to evaluate the relative
chemical durability of glasses by measuring the concentrations of the chemical species released
from 100-200 mesh crushed glass (75-149 μm) to the test solution (de-ionized water in this case).
PCTs on the HLW glasses are performed at 90ºC, in accordance with the current WTP contract
requirement. The ratio of the glass surface area to the solution volume for this test is about
2000 m-1 (typically, 4 g of 100-200 mesh glass is immersed in 40 ml of deionized water). All
tests are conducted in triplicate, in 304L stainless steel vessels, and in parallel with a standard
glass included in each test set. The internal standard is the Argonne National Laboratory – Low
Activity Reference Material (ANL-LRM) glass [38] and/or the Defense Waste Processing
Facility – Environmental Assessment (DWPF-EA) glass [39], both of which have undergone
round-robin testing. The leachates are sampled at predetermined times, the first of which is seven
days. One milliliter of sampled leachate is mixed with 20 ml of 1M HNO3 and the resulting
solution is analyzed by DCP-AES; another 3 ml of sampled leachate is used for pH
measurement.

 1.4.3.4 Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure (TCLP)

 The TCLP was performed at VSL using SW-846 Method 1311, which employs leaching
of crushed glass (< 3/8”) in a sodium acetate buffer solution for 18 hours at 22C with constant
end-over-end agitation. A mass of about 100 grams of glass is leached in 2 liters of TCLP
extract, according to the extraction method for non-volatiles. The surface area to volume ratio for
this test is about 20 m-1, which is about two orders of magnitude lower than that in the PCT. The
leachates are analyzed by DCP-AES according to VSL standard operating procedures.

 1.4.3.5 Secondary Phases

 Secondary phases in the glass samples were determined by optical microscopy and
scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) in
combination with X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. Secondary phases due to crystallization and
phase separation can be identified using these methods. The samples for SEM analysis are
processed by grinding and sieving (-18 mesh, < 1 mm) in order to provide a representative
measure of the overall crystal fraction in the sample. Ground glass samples are mounted in
epoxy, polished to expose cross sections of the glass grains, coated with carbon, and examined
using SEM/EDS. The amount of crystals in glass samples was determined by SEM in
conjunction with image analysis.

 1.4.3.6 Crystalline Phases by X-Ray Diffraction

 Quantitative XRD was used to determine the crystal content in glasses and in solids from
the cyclone filter in the off-gas system. Powder diffraction data were gathered using a Thermo-
Fisher X-tra theta-theta diffractometer fitted with a Cu X-ray tube. Glass samples were ground
and sieved to 325 mesh. Five weight percent of a corundum internal standard was mixed with the
powdered sample for quantitative analysis of the crystalline phases. Each powder pattern was
scanned from 5 to 80 degrees 2 theta using a collection time of 2 seconds per data point. JADE9
software was used to perform the search-match identification of the crystalline phases as well as
the whole pattern fitting to determine the weight percent of each crystalline phase in the sample.

 1.4.3.7 Mössbauer Spectroscopy

 Mössbauer spectroscopy is an analytical technique that utilizes the recoil-free emission
and resonant absorption of gamma rays by nuclei bound in solids. The energies of the gamma ray
emitter (source) and absorber (sample) have to be closely matched and, therefore, the number of
elements that can be studied using this technique is limited. One of the elements that has been
studied extensively using Mössbauer spectroscopy is iron. In this work, Mössbauer spectroscopy
was used to measure the fraction of iron in the Fe2+ and Fe3+ states, which can be used as a
measure of the redox state of the glass sample. Mössbauer spectroscopy was also used to analyze
the various iron minerals in the feed materials and deposits in the off-gas system.

 Mössbauer spectra were collected using an American Magnetic/Ranger Scientific
MS-1200 system equipped with a 57Co source in a rhodium matrix with glass powder as the
sample. Both divalent and trivalent iron show doublet peaks in the Mössbauer spectrum and the
peak areas are proportional to the concentrations of the respective species in the glass. Even
though the peaks overlap partially, software can be used to deconvolute the peaks and calculate
the peak areas. The ratio of the areas of the Fe2+ and Fe3+ peaks is equal to the ratio of their
concentrations in the glass. The redox measurements are calibrated using a set of six standard
glass samples with Fe2+ contents ranging from 7 to 90% of the total iron (Fetotal). The standards
include a NIST traceable Obsidian Rock (SRM 278), five standard glasses analyzed by Corning,
Inc. for their redox state, and the DWPF-EA glass. The site populations provided by the fitting is
mol% Fe, assuming that the recoil-free fraction is the same for each site.

 The NIST standard iron foil (SRM 1541) is used to calibrate the instrument and
determine the zero velocity channel. Since Doppler shifts in energy are measured in Mössbauer
spectroscopy, the velocity is a measure of the shift in energy; knowing the zero velocity channel
therefore helps in identifying the Fe2+ and Fe3+ peaks.

2.2 Manganese Forms in Hanford HLW and Melter Feed

 Manganese in Hanford tank wastes can be present in multiple oxidation states, as various
oxides and hydroxides, and in compounds with carbonates, oxalates, and other anions.
Compilations of observed manganese forms in single and double shell Hanford tanks have
identified the following:

 • Seven different phases: MnC2O4·2H2O (lindbergite), MnCO3 (rhodochrosite),
 Mn/FeO(OH), Fe,Mn oxide, Mn2MnO4, Fe2MnO4 (jacobsite), Mn/FeO(OH) [25].
 • Nine different phases: MnFe2(PO4)(OH)2 (pseudolaueite), Fe1.75Mn0.25O3 (hematite-
 type oxide), MnC2O4·2H2O (lindbergite), MnCO3 (rhodochrosite), FeMnO4 (iron
 manganate), Mn2CrO4 (manganese chromium oxide), Mn1.5Cr1.5O4 (manganese
 chromium oxide), (Mn,Fe)3O4 (manganese iron oxide), and Fe2MnO4 (jacobsite) [26].
 • Denser and dehydrated Mn phases including the Mn(IV) compound MnO2, the Mn(III)
 compound MnOOH, manganese spinels such as Mn2MnO4, other spinels that include
 Fe or Cr, and a mixed phase incorporating other metals and phosphorus [24].

 Previous modeling work to predict the form of manganese in Hanford Tank waste by
specifying the manganese as divalent gave an outputs of Mn3(PO4)2, Mn(OH)2, and MnCO3 [24].
Another model represented all Mn as the equivalent of MnO2 in light of the stability of MnO2
over a wide pH range in aqueous environments containing no anions except hydroxide [41]. For
the majority of the previous HLW melter feed testing, MnO2 was used as the manganese source
in the feed [10-16, 21-23, 29, 42, 43]; MnO was assumed to be the dominant form of manganese
in the product glass based on measurements made on melter glasses [44]. In more recent tests
conducted in support of HLW vitrification enhancements for ORP, MnO was used as the source
of manganese [18-22, 45, 46]. The manganese content targeted in the glasses during these melter
tests was less than four percent MnO, except in one test conducted to determine the effect of
manganese on processing rate in which the concentration was eight percent MnO [43]. It is worth
noting that the production rate achieved with the eight percent MnO targeted glass was only 750
kg/m2/day, which is well below the rate achieved for the same formulation at nominal
manganese content and the WTP requirement of 800 kg glass /m2/day.

 For the present tests, MnO and MnO2 were selected to represent the reduced and oxidized
forms of manganese species present in the waste and to provide a direct comparison of their
effects. Testing was also performed with rhodochrosite (MnCO3), which was identified in the
C-106 tank [24, 25]. Unlike the various mixed phase spinels that have been observed in the tank
waste, these manganese compounds are commercially available in well characterized forms and
in quantities suitable for producing feed for melter testing; they are therefore natural choices for
the initial testing.

2.3 Development of Glass Formulation for High Mn HLW

 2.3.1 High Manganese HLW and Glass Formulation Strategy

 The simulant composition in Table 2.1 is the basis for the present HLW glass formulation
development. As evident from the waste composition expressed in terms of oxides, the major
waste constituents, Al, Cr, Fe, Mn, and Ni are all highly compatible with spinel formation, a
common liquidus phase for HLW glasses. Thus, one of the issues that is likely to limit waste
loading in glasses to treat this Mn-rich HLW is spinel crystallization.

 As discussed above, melter testing of this Mn-rich HLW was conducted to evaluate the
impact of different manganese sources on melt rate and processing. However, only MnO was
used for glass formulation development because divalent Mn2+ has been determined to be the
dominant valence state for manganese in borosilicate glasses [44].

 The glass formulation work was aimed at development of HLW glass compositions for a
high-Manganese HLW stream at a high waste loading that has acceptable product quality and
processing characteristics. The approach employed small-scale tests and crucible melts to
identify such high waste loading glass formulations.

 Properties that affect processability and product quality include glass crystallization, melt
viscosity, melt electrical conductivity, PCT releases, and TCLP releases of the product glass.
Since crystallization is likely to be the most constraining property in view of the high
concentrations of spinel-compatible elements in the waste, all prepared glasses were screened
with respect to crystallinity after isothermal heat treatment. Glasses that did not meet the
crystallinity constraint were not characterized further with respect to the other properties. The
final glass composition selected for melter tests were characterized with respect to all properties
relevant to processing and product quality for HLW glasses [47, 48].

 A total of 20 crucible melts were prepared and characterized to develop a high-Mn HLW
glass formulation suitable for melter testing (Table 2.3). Results from crucible scale testing to
support the selection of the candidate glass are summarized below in groups by the waste loading
in the glasses that were tested. Target and XRF analyzed glass compositions are given in Table
2.3; a summary of measured properties is given in Table 2.4; measured melt viscosities and
electrical conductivities are compiled in Table 2.5; PCT releases are given in Table 2.6; and
TCLP results are given in Table 2.7.

 2.3.2 Formulation of High-Mn HLW Glasses at 45 wt% Waste Loading

 Four glasses were formulated at 45 wt% waste loading with different combinations of
major alkaline additives. HLW-HMn-6 with 18.3 wt% Na2O showed 2.4 vol% spinel crystals
after heat treatment at 950oC for 70 hours. Addition of 4 wt% of CaO and 3 wt% of B2O3 at the
expense of Na2O did not reduce the spinel crystal content in HLW-HMn-8 (Tables 2.3, 2.4).
HLW-HMn-2, formulated by substitution of 2 wt% Li2O and 3 wt% SiO2 for CaO and Na2O in

HLW-HMn-8, showed similar amounts of spinel crystallization. HLW-HMn-4, with addition of
2 wt% K2O for SiO2 in HLW-HMn-8, did not show reduced spinel crystallization after heat

6.5 wt% MnO, and considerable Fe and Ni from the waste, more than 2 vol% of spinel tended to

work. Consequently, formulations with lower waste loadings were investigated.

 2.3.3 Formulation of High-Mn HLW Glasses at 40 wt% Waste Loading

 Four glasses were formulated at 40 wt% waste loading with different combinations of
major alkaline additives. HLW-HMn-5 with 6 wt% Li2O showed 1.2 vol% spinel crystals after
70 hours at 950oC. When Li2O was decreased to 4 wt % accompanied by increases in Na2O and
B2O3 and a decease in SiO2, the same amount of spinel was found in HLW-HMn-3. A further
reduction of Li2O to 2 wt% with the addition of 3 wt% K2O did not change the spinel content
after heat treatment at 950oC in HLW-HMn-1. Addition of 2 wt% K2O for Li2O in
HLW-HMn-7 increased spinel crystallization slightly at all four heat treatment temperatures
(Tables 2.3, 2.4). In general, the four glasses formulated at 40 wt% waste loading showed
considerably less spinel than those with 45 wt% waste loading. However, the spinel contents in
the heat treated glass samples were still above 1 vol% after heat treatment for 70 hours at 950oC.

 2.3.4 Formulation of High-Mn HLW Glasses at 35 wt% Waste Loading

 High-Mn HLW glass formulations at 45 wt% and 40 wt% waste loadings showed higher
than acceptable crystallization of Fe-Mn-Cr-Ni spinels. Consequently, six glasses were
formulated at 35 wt% loading to reduce spinel crystallization (HLW-HMn-9, -10, -11, 12, -13,
-14). At about 10 wt% K2O, 1.4 vol% spinel crystals were present in HLW-HMn-11 after heat
treatment at 950oC for 70 hours (Tables 2.3, 2.4). Substituting 6 wt% Li2O and 5 wt% SiO2 for
10 wt% K2O and 1 wt% B2O3 reduced spinel crystallization to 1 vol% at 950oC in
HLW-HMn-9. The spinel crystal content decreased further to 0.7 vol% at 950oC when both K2O
and Li2O were used in HLW-HMn-10. However, the variations of HLW-HMn-10 at the same
K2O content but different B2O3 concentration (HLW-HMn-12), or at different Li2O and V2O5
concentrations (HLW-HMn-13), or Li2O and P2O5 concentrations (HLW-HMn-14), resulted in
higher spinel crystallization (1.6 vol%, 1.1 vol%, and 1 vol% spinel for HLW-HMn-12, -13, 14,
respectively, after heat treatment for 70 hours at 950oC).

 Although the high combined concentrations of K2O and Li2O in HLW-HMn-10 were
effective in decreasing spinel crystallization while still meeting the melt viscosity and electrical
conductivity requirements (Tables 2.3, 2.4), the PCT releases were too high as compared to the
DWPF EA glass (Table 2.4). The PCT releases did not improve significantly with additions of
V2O5 (HLW-HMn-13) or P2O5 (HLW-HMn-14). Interestingly, HLW-HMn-9, with 6 wt% Li2O
and no additional K2O but higher SiO2 showed PCT releases well below the requirements (Table
2.4). The higher Li2O concentration in HLW-HMn-9 (compared to HLW-HMn-10) not only
increased its electrical conductivity at the melter processing temperature, but increases the
potential risk for structural damage to the K-3 refractory [49]. Therefore, additional formulations

at 35 wt% or lower waste loadings were investigated in order to improve the glass product

 2.3.5 Formulation of High-Mn HLW Glasses at 32 wt% Waste Loading

 Two glasses were formulated at 32 wt% waste loading (Tables 2.3, 2.4). At this lower

 As described in Section 2.3.4, HLW-HMn-10 failed the PCT release requirements but
other glass properties were within acceptable limits. At the same 35 wt% waste loading and with

 2.4.4 Vertical Gradient Furnace

 The vertical gradient furnace has been used as a useful screening tool for ranking HLW
slurry feeds for their processing rate [16, 21, 22, 27-29]. Melter feed samples from each of the
three tests with different forms of manganese, all producing the same HLW-HMn-20 glass
composition, were tested under the standard VGF conditions in an attempt to evaluate the
potential impact of the form of manganese on melt rate. Aliquots from one liter sized slurry feed
samples were dried and subjected to the test protocol described in Section 1.4.1. The chemical
compositions of the sampled feeds are given in Table 2.12. Additional tests were performed on
samples produced at the crucible scale on the HLW-HMn-10 composition (see Tables 2.3 and
2.4) to evaluate the effect of added reductant to feed containing MnO 2. In a standard VGF
experiment, feed samples are examined for the extent of feed reactions, melting, appearance of
the feed sample such as the presence of foam, formation of a dome-like structure, etc., that are
indicative of the rate of feed conversion to glass. The conversion of each type of feed material
was evaluated primarily based on images of the top and cross sections of the reacted feed
samples after 15, 30, and 60 minute VGF tests, depending on the observed completion of
reactions. The top and cross sectional images of VGF samples are shown in Figures 2.5 – 2.8. A
summary of the visual observations and relative rankings of the feeds are compared to the
previously tested HLW-NG-Fe2 composition with various forms of iron [16, 29] in Table 2.13.

 24

 The two feeds with divalent manganese (MnO and MnCO3) from the present tests were
quickly converted to glass, earning the highest ranking for feed conversion using the VGF
method. The feed conversion with more oxidized manganese (MnO2) was considerably slower
with a thick foamy layer on the wall of the reaction vessel after 30 and 60-minute VGF tests,
resulting in a ranking of 3. Typically, formation of such a thick layer that is full of gas vesicles
indicates episodes of degassing, foaming, and subsequent collapsing of an over-arching dome
structure, resulting in the overall conversion rate from melter feed to glass being slower.
Previous tests with the high iron HLW-NG-Fe2 composition did not show differences in melt
rate with different iron oxidation states; both ferrous and ferric oxides resulted in rapidly melting
feeds. The carbonate associated with MnCO3 did not have an effect on the observed melt rate. In
previous tests, the hydroxide form of iron resulted in slower melt rates than the oxide form, in
contrast to aluminum hydroxide, which resulted in faster melt rates than aluminum oxides [27];
therefore, it is difficult to predict whether feeds with manganese hydroxide would melt faster
than the oxides tested in the present work.

 In addition to the tests with melter feed samples, HLW-HMn-10 at 35 wt% waste loading
was subjected to feed conversion tests to assess its processing rate and potential impact due to
different Mn chemical forms. Very similar VGF results were obtained for the HLW-HMn-10
formulation as for the feed samples from the melter tests: feed samples reacted fast with MnO
and MnCO3, but slower with considerable foam with MnO2 (Figure 2.8). The observed porous
structures in VGF samples of the MnO2-sourced HLW-HMn-20 and HLW-HMn-10 formulations
were probably caused by redox reactions of manganese during feed conversion. As identified
from structural analysis of Mn-bearing glasses, MnO is the dominant form of manganese in
HLW glasses quenched from melter processing temperature (1150oC) [44]. Therefore, it is
expected that the MnO2 present in melter slurry feed would be reduced to MnO with a
concomitant release of oxygen gas. The observed foaming was likely the result of the oxygen gas
trapped within a viscous fluid formed from the partially molten feed blend. One effective method
to suppress undesirable foaming during melter processing is to add a reductant to the slurry feed.
In the WTP low activity waste (LAW) flow-sheet, sugar (sucrose) is used for this purpose.
Therefore, the addition of sugar was investigated with respect to its effects on feed reactions in
the HLW-HMn-10 formulation with MnO2. Two feed samples were tested with two levels of
sugar in the feed formulation. One feed sample was prepared with an amount of sugar
corresponding to the 0.5 carbon-to-nitrate stoichiometry, which is the baseline for mitigation of
foaming in high-nitrate LAW feeds [50]. Using this basis, for each mole of MnO2 in the feed,
approximately 0.02 moles of sugar was used. This corresponds to about half of the stoichiometric
amount of carbon required to convert into CO2 all of the oxygen generated from the
decomposition of the MnO2 to MnO. In the second feed, twice this amount of sugar was added.
Foaming, similar to that observed in the MnO2-sourced feed samples, developed for the feed
with 0.5 stoichiometric carbon. However, at twice that sugar content, the feed reacted very fast
and completed conversion with no sign of foaming, very similar to the MnO-sourced feed of the
same HLW-HMn-10 formulation. These results indicate that the addition of a reductant at a
sufficient level is effective in suppressing foaming due to the reduction of MnO2 during feed
conversion.

reductants (organic compounds) and oxidants (nitrates and nitrites) in the melter feed. The high
manganese simulant processed in the present tests contained very little organic carbon, which
would limit the amount of reduced iron and its tendency to increase spinel formation.

 MONITORED OFF-GAS EMISSIONS

5.1 Particulate Sampling

 The melter exhaust was sampled for metals/particles according to 40-CFR-60 Methods 3,
5, and 29 at steady-state operating conditions during each test segment. The concentrations of
off-gas species present as particulates and gaseous species were derived from laboratory data on
solutions extracted from air samples (impinger solutions, filters, and rinse solutions) together
with measurements of the volume of air sampled. Particulate collection required isokinetic
sampling, which entails removing gas from the exhaust at the same velocity that the air is
flowing in the duct (40-CFR-60, Methods 1-5). Typically, a sample size of 30 dscf was taken at a
rate of between 0.5 and 0.75 dscfm. Total particulate loading was determined by combining
gravimetric analysis of the standard particle filter and chemical analysis of probe rinse solutions.
An additional impinger containing 2 N NaOH was added to the sampling train to ensure
complete scrubbing of acid gases. The collected materials were analyzed using direct current
plasma atomic emission spectroscopy for the majority of the constituents and ion
chromatography (IC) for anions. Melter emission fluxes are compared to feed fluxes and
emission samples taken while processing the same glass compositions in Table 5.1. Notice the
distinction that is made between constituents sampled as particles and as "gas". The "gaseous"
constituents are operationally defined as those species that are scrubbed in the impinger solutions
after the air stream has passed through a 0.3 µm heated filter. All samples were well within the
90 - 110% limits for isokinetic sampling.

 Particulate emissions while processing the HLW-HMn-20 glass composition with MnO
constituted from 0.23 to 0.34 percent of feed solids for the optimized bubbling and fixed
bubbling tests, respectively. These levels of carryover are considerably lower than the range
measured while processing various feeds containing high iron HLW simulants processed on the
same melter at a temperature of 1150oC: AZ-102 (0.57 - 1.47 percent) [15]; C-106/AY-102 SIPP
(0.61 to 0.81 percent) [12]; the former C-106/AY-102 baseline (0.3 - 0.74 percent) [42]; the
C-106/AY-102 high waste loading formulation (0.66 and 0.71 percent) [16]; high chromium
HLW-HCr-16 formulation (0.70 and 0.62 percent) [20]; high strontium HLW-HSr-7 formulation
(1.14 and 0.58 percent) [21]; high zirconium HLW-ZrA-07 formulation (1.57 and 2.51 percent)
[22]; high zirconium HLW-Zr-11 formulation (3.05 and 1.77 percent) [22]. However, the
emissions from the present tests were within the range of measured carryover for previous tests
with chromium, bismuth, aluminum, and aluminum plus sodium limited HLW wastes (0.04 –
0.57) [3, 27, 28], high bismuth HLW-Bi-F3 formulation (0.30 and 0.39 percent) [17], high
phosphate HLW-HP-16 composition (0.34 and 0.23) [32], and high bismuth HLW-Bi-F9
formulation (0.18 percent) [17] when processed under the same melter conditions. Carryover is a
function of the composition of the feed, particularly the amount of volatile constituents in the
feed, and the conditions in the melter, particularly the completeness and uniformity of the cold
cap. The relatively low particulate carryover in the present tests, the tests with high bismuth

 33

formulations [3, 17], and the tests with high aluminum formulations [3, 26, 27], and high
phosphate [32] were a result in part of the low volatile content of the feed. Much higher levels of
carryover were observed in tests with higher amounts of volatile constituents such as rhenium,
cesium, and chlorine, which are emitted primarily as fine particulate [55]. Two of the volatile
constituents in the HLW-HMn-20 composition, fluorine and sulfur, are emitted in larger
proportion as gaseous species and therefore do not contribute as significantly to the calculated
carryover of feed solids. Also the two most volatile constituents in the HLW-HMn-20 feed,
fluorine and thallium, are targeted in the glass at 0.09 wt% or less and therefore have a limited
effect on the overall total particulate carryover. Increases in particulate carryover, 0.23 vs. 0.34
percent, were observed with increases in bubbling rate, in keeping with several previous tests
processing HLW compositions [3, 10, 11, 16, 20-22]. Carryover values for tests conducted with
the nominal bubbling rate with each manganese form ranged only from 0.23 to 0.3 percent of
feed solids indicating that the form of manganese in the melter feed has little effect on the
amount of particulate carryover.

 The composition of the particulate in the melter exhaust is reflective of the feed
composition, with some enrichments or depletions based on elemental volatility. Elements
present in the particulate at a proportion lower than in the feed are aluminum, silicon, nickel,
phosphorus, and zirconium, while boron, chromium, alkalis, lead, fluorine, thallium, and sulfur
were emitted at rates considerably higher than that for the total particulate. Other elements,
including manganese, were emitted at similar rates to the overall solids carryover rate. The
highest carryover was clearly observed for the most volatile elements, thallium, sulfur, and
fluorine, as expected. No difference in these trends was observed with the changing form of
manganese over the course of the tests indicating that the relative carryover of manganese is not
affected by the mineralogy or grain size of the different forms of manganese. Boron, fluorine,
and sulfur were the only elements detected in the impinger solutions collected downstream of the
heated particle filter in the sampling train, which constitutes the “gas” fraction of the melter
emissions.

5.2 Particulate Sampled from Off-Gas System

 The amount of material accumulated in the cyclones downstream of the film cooler is
listed in Table 5.2. The amount of material removed from the cyclones accounts for less than a
tenth of a percent of the feed solids from the tests. This is lower than the amount of particulate
sampled in the melter exhaust, suggesting that some of the particulate is too fine to be removed
in the cyclone or solids are deposited in the line in between the exhaust sampling point and the
cyclone. The analyzed mineral content and chemical composition of material accumulated in the
cyclones is provided in Tables 5.2 and 5.3, respectively. Cyclone solids contain all of the feed
components in similar proportions to the feed for most elements with significant enrichments
over feed for volatile elements boron, thallium (12-16 times), and sulfur (4-6 times) as well as a
twofold depletion of silicon. These proportions of volatile and non-volatile elements in the
cyclone particulate indicate bulk carryover of feed from the melter coupled with the loss of the
most volatile components from glass pool and cold cap. The enrichment of thallium and sulfur in
the cyclone deposits confirms the high measured elemental carryover in the melter exhaust (see
Table 5.1). The results from XRD analysis show that most of the mineral phases detected in the

 34

cyclone deposits were present in the feed as either additives (quartz and zircon) or waste
components (eskolite, manganosite, and pyrolucite), suggesting direct entrainment of feed
components into the melter exhaust stream. It is worth noting that the mineral form of
manganese in the cyclone deposits matches the form fed for the respective test. Also no
rhodocrosite (MnCO3) was detected in the cyclone deposits, suggesting that the carbonate
decomposes at lower temperatures than the two oxides. The hematite detected in the cyclone
deposits originates from the Fe(OH)3 in the waste simulant.

5.3 Gases Monitored by FTIR

 Melter emissions were monitored in each test for a variety of gaseous components, most
notably CO and nitrogen species, by Fourier Transform Infra-Red Spectroscopy (FTIR). The
off-gas system temperature is maintained well above 100°C beyond the sampling port
downstream of the DM100 HEPA filter to prevent analyte loss due to condensation prior to
monitoring. The data, therefore, represent the relative concentrations of volatile gaseous species
in the melter exhaust. A summary of the range and average concentrations of gaseous species
monitored during the tests is provided in Table 5.4. The analytes listed in Table 5.4 are those that
were thought likely to be observed during the tests based on previous work; no other species
were detected in the off-gas stream by FTIR. The concentrations of the most abundant monitored
species, nitrogen monoxide, carbon dioxide, and water, are plotted in Figures 5.1.a - 5.1.c.
Generally, emissions from the DM100 were relatively low as a result of the low concentrations
of nitrogen, organic carbon, and ammonia in the feed. The most abundant nitrogen species
monitored was NO, which is in keeping with previous melter tests with both HLW and LAW
feeds. The concentrations of the most abundant measured species, water and carbon dioxide,
increased with the feed processing rate since the feed water and carbon content were nominally
constant for all feeds except for the last test with MnCO3, which showed higher concentrations of
carbon dioxide. Carbon monoxide and ammonia were detected only in the test with MnCO3,
which also had NO concentrations about three times less than in the other tests; the reason for
this is not clear but it may indicate that the MnCO3 has trace level contamination of organic
carbon. The scatter in the emissions data over the course of the tests is due in part to changes in
the cold cap.

 SECTION 6.0
 SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

 This report describes the results from work performed to develop and test new glass and
feed formulations in order to increase waste loading for HLW with high concentrations of
manganese. A further objective of the work was to determine the effect of the form of
manganese in Hanford HLW on feed processing rates. A number of HLW batches to be
processed at the WTP have manganese concentrations that extend above about thirteen weight
percent on an oxide basis. These high manganese HLW streams frequently contain significant
amounts of chromium, iron, and nickel, which when combined with manganese have the
tendency to form secondary phases such as spinels that can limit waste loading or lead to poor
cold cap behavior and slow processing rates [23]. Previous studies for ORP have explored some
of the HLW streams that contain relatively high concentrations of iron and chromium with
manganese [23]; however, these studies did not focus on HLW streams for which glass waste
loading is manganese limited.

 Manganese is present in Hanford tank waste in a variety of forms including MnO, MnO2,
MnCO3, spinels and hydroxides with iron and nickel, and oxalates [24-26]. These differences in
manganese form have the potential to affect waste and melter feed rheology, feed processing
rates, and the extent of crystalline phases in the glass product, as has been observed with
different forms of aluminum and iron in HLW waste simulants [27-29]. In particular, glass
formulation development for high manganese HLW can be challenging due to spinel formation,
especially in the presence of other spinel forming elements such as iron, chromium, and nickel
[6, 12, 23, 30]. In addition, foaming of the glass pool during vitrification has been attributed to
manganese oxidation state changes [13-15] and this may also be affected by the form of
manganese in the waste. These issues were addressed by developing glass formulations for a
Hanford HLW stream with high concentrations of manganese followed by melter testing with the
selected glass composition with feed containing manganese in three different forms likely to be
present in Hanford tank waste. The work included preparation and characterization of crucible
melts to evaluate the effects of waste loading on glass properties and the maximum waste
loadings that can be achieved. The results from crucible-scale testing supported subsequent
DM100 melter tests designed to further assess the enhanced glass and feed formulations with
respect to processing rate and product quality. The DM100-BL, which is one of two DM100
melter systems installed at VSL, was selected as the platform for these tests due to its extensive
previous use in processing rate determination for various HLW streams and glass compositions
[3, 11-17, 20-23, 27-29, 33, 42, 43, 45, 46]. A strategy similar to that employed successfully for
determining the differences in the feed and glass properties and feed processing characteristics of
HLW feeds with different forms of aluminum and iron [27-29] was used for the present tests
with different forms of manganese.

 Twenty glass formulations were prepared and characterized to identify a high waste
loading glass composition to treat the present high-Mn HLW. Crystallization of spinel was

 36

expected to limit waste loading in glasses formulated for this HLW, because, in addition to Mn,
this HLW contains high concentrations of components such as Al, Cr, Fe, and Ni that can
promote spinel formation. Accordingly, initial glass formulation efforts were aimed at limiting
spinel crystallization by increasing the amount of components such as alkalis, alkaline earth
oxides, and boron oxide in the glass composition. All of the glasses formulated at waste loadings
of 45 and 40 wt% crystallized more than one vol% spinel after heat treatment for 70 hours at
950ºC. Varying the concentrations of glass former additives Li2O, K2O, Na2O, CaO, B2O3 and
SiO2 did not reduce spinel crystallization to less than one volume percent in these glasses.
Subsequently, glasses were formulated and tested at waste loadings of 35 and 32 wt%. At these
lower waste loadings, spinel crystallization could be limited to less than one vol% after heat
treatment for 70 hours at 950ºC. Glass HLW-HMn-10 formulated at 35 wt% waste loading with
2.0 wt% Li2O and 8.22 wt% K2O as additives showed acceptable crystallization, melt viscosity
and melt electrical conductivity, but failed the PCT release limits. This issue was addressed in
glass HLW-HMN-20 by increasing Li2O (to 3.5 wt%) reducing K2O (to 4.22 wt%), adding ZrO2
(3.09 wt%), and increasing SiO2 and Al2O3 concentrations. As shown in Table 2.8, glass
HLW-HMN-20 meets all of the WTP processing and product quality requirements and was,
therefore, selected for further testing on the DM100 melter to determine its processing
characteristics.

 Melter tests were conducted on the DM100 with the new HLW-HMn-20 glass
formulation using the Batch 3939 high-manganese HLW simulant. Tests on the DM100 were
conducted at 1150oC at the nominal bubbling rate of 9 lpm and also with optimized bubbling to
achieve maximum production rates; these conditions were selected to allow comparison to
results obtained previously with other HLW simulants [16, 17, 20-22, 27, 32]. The feeds with
MnO were processed as the feed rate was adjusted to provide the desired complete cold cap.
Glass production rates of 920 and 1560 kg/m 2 /day were achieved with nominal and optimized
 P P

bubbling, respectively. The rate obtained at nominal conditions was less than that achieved for
any of the other high waste loading formulations and marginally above the WTP HLW baseline
requirement; however, processing rates can exceed the WTP HLW baseline requirement by a
factor of two with optimization of melt pool bubbling. Additional DM100 melter tests were
conducted with feeds containing two other forms of manganese (MnO2 and MnCO3) under
nominal conditions (1150°C glass temperature, 9 lpm bubbling) so that the effect of the form of
manganese on feed processing rate and processing characteristics could be determined.
Production rates for these feeds were at or below the WTP HLW baseline requirement of
800 kg/m2/day and showed the following melt rate trend:

 MnO2 < MnCO3 < MnO

This trend is contrary to that observed for feeds with different forms of iron, which showed no
effect on production rate with transition metal oxidation state [29]. Also, the relative percentage
change in production rate with different forms of manganese is twice that for different forms of
iron, despite the manganese content in the HLW-HMn-20 glass composition being less than a
third of the iron concentration in the HLW-NG-Fe2 glass composition. Feeds with MnO2
subjected to VGF testing were also shown to be slower melting than feeds with other forms of
manganese.

 37

 Samples of the glass product were collected and analyzed for composition, crystalline
phases, iron oxidation state, and product quality from each test. Crystalline phases in the form of
spinels were measured in melter glasses at 0.6 to 1.1 volume percent with no clear differences
based on the form of manganese in the feed. The measured amount of spinels in melter glasses
was higher than the 0.5 volume percent measured on crucible melt glasses. The measured iron
oxidation state of melter glasses ranged from totally oxidized in tests with MnO and MnO2 to
about four and half percent divalent iron in tests with MnCO3. The small amounts of divalent
iron in tests with MnCO3 are probably attributable to trace contamination of the MnCO3 with
organic carbon. Glass from each of the tests far exceeded chemical durability criteria for the PCT
and TCLP methods.

 Particulate emissions while processing the HLW-HMn-20 glass composition constituted
only 0.23 to 0.34 percent of feed solids and were lower than for most HLW compositions tested
previously. Carryover values for comparable tests conducted with MnO, MnO2, and MnCO3
ranged from only 0.23 to 0.3 percent of feed solids with no obvious trends between tests with
different forms of manganese. The composition of the particulate in the melter exhaust is
reflective of the feed composition with some enrichment (boron, chromium, alkalis, lead,
fluorine, thallium, and sulfur) or depletion (silicon, nickel, phosphorus, and zirconium) based on
elemental volatility. No difference in these trends was observed with the changing form of
manganese over the course of the tests, indicating that the relative carryover of manganese is not
affected by the mineralogy or grain size of the different forms of manganese. The composition of
the particulate was confirmed by retrieval and analysis of solids from the cyclone filter in the
off-gas system. XRD analysis of these particulates indicates entrainment of feed minerals in the
exhaust as well as enrichments of the most volatile elements, particularly thallium and sulfur.
Manganese was observed as either MnO or MnO2 in cyclone deposits from the respective tests,
while MnCO3 was not detected in any of the cyclone deposits indicating that MnCO3 more
readily decomposes in the cold cap.

 The results of the testing presented herein further demonstrate the viability of the
enhancement and optimization strategies that have been developed in previous work for ORP [3,
16, 17, 21-22, 27-29, 32, 33, 54]. The results extend the potential for enormous savings in cost
and schedule that result from that work. It is recommended that testing and evaluation of these
very successful strategies be continued in order to provide a solid basis for their broad
implementation in order to maximize the cost and schedule benefits while minimizing technical
risk. Specifically, a glass formulation for a high manganese HLW stream was developed which
conforms to all product quality and processing requirements. The waste loading of 35 wt% was
limited by spinel formation since the waste stream also contained relatively high concentrations
of nickel and chromium, which also contribute to spinel crystallization. Production rates
exceeding the WTP HLW baseline were obtained for feed containing MnO but not with MnO2 or
MnCO3 at nominal conditions. These observations further confirm that the form of waste
components can significantly affect glass production rates [3, 16, 27-29] and, in the case of
manganese, at concentrations of less than five weight percent oxide. The extent of secondary
phase formation, amount of reduced iron, and product quality were unaffected by the form of
manganese in the melter feed. The forms of manganese in the deposits within the off-gas system
were the same as that in the feed, indicating entrainment carry over from the cold cap.

 38

 These results show the importance of evaluating the effects of all probable forms of waste
components on the vitrification process. While identifying the forms of waste components is
complicated by limited knowledge and sampling of tank wastes, as well as chemical reactions
occurring in tanks over time, during pretreatment, and in the melter feed, the available
information can be used to perform testing to assess the potential magnitude of these effects and
to develop useful bounds. The results from the present work indicate that the Batch 3939
high-manganese HLW stream would benefit from additional formulation development to further
increase the potential processing rate for all forms of manganese likely to be present. Testing is
also recommended to address other forms of manganese likely to be present in Hanford HLW
streams.
