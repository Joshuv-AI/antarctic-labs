Navigating Solid Food Introduction: A Qualitative Exploration of Caregivers’ Beliefs, Readiness, and Influences on Infant Feeding Practices
Authors: Hodan Jibrell, Sara Malik, Claire King, Waheeda Samady
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate beliefs and practices during solid food introduction (SFI) among a diverse sample of parents/caregivers using a qualitative approach. SFI aspects evaluated included timing, approach, beliefs, family/cultural influences, environmental factors, and interactions with their pediatrician.MethodsSemistructured interviews were conducted with parents of children between 7 and 13 months of age. Interview questions used the theory of planned behavior. Parents were recruited from academic, community, and private clinics in Chicago. Data were analyzed using a modified grounded theory approach, incorporating both deductive and inductive coding. Two researchers independently coded all interviews, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus.ResultsA total of 49 interviews were conducted. Participants were primarily mothers (92%). By race, 21 (43%) identified as White, 14 (29%) as Black, 4 (8%) as Asian, 1 (2%) as Native American, 7 (14%) as mixed race or other, and 2 (4%) as unknown. By ethnicity, 14 (29%) identified as Latino or Hispanic. Twenty-eight participants (57%) had Medicaid. Most parents introduced solids between 5 and 6 months of age, primarily following pediatrician advice. Several major themes emerged around SFI: (1) conflicting beliefs about timing and caregiver readiness, (2) fear of choking, (3) parental control over feeding, (4) desire for a good start, (5) siblings shaping feeding practices, (6) cultural and family influences, and (7) adjusted feeding practices based on circumstances.ConclusionsAlthough numerous factors influence SFI, many caregivers relied on pediatric guidance as an anchor for decision-making. However, caregivers reported gaps in support and information, suggesting that more flexible, practical, and culturally responsive recommendations are needed to better align with families' diverse experiences and circumstances.Keywords: caregiver beliefs, caregiver readiness, complementary feeding, early childhood nutrition, feeding influences, infant feeding practices, solid food introduction

Solid food introduction (SFI) marks a critical transition in infant feeding and development.1,2 The timing of this transition matters: both early initiation (<4 months) and late initiation (>6 months) are associated with negative health outcomes.3,4 Introducing solids before 4 months may increase the risk of conditions such as diabetes, obesity, and eczema, in part by disrupting metabolic programming and the gut microbiota.4,5,6 Early feeding can also displace breastmilk or formula, leading to nutrient deficiencies such as low iron, poor absorption, and a higher risk of anemia or diarrhea.1 Conversely, delaying the introduction of solids well beyond 6 months may limit opportunities for infants to develop oral motor skills necessary for successfully transitioning to complementary foods, which are needed post infancy to support growth, brain development, and immune function.2,3 Timing also shapes allergic outcomes: evidence now indicates that the delayed introduction of allergens like peanuts and eggs is associated with higher rates of food allergy, whereas very early introduction may overstimulate an immature immune system.7,8

Leading health organizations, including the American Academy of Pediatrics, the American College of Obstetrics and Gynecology, and the American Academy of Family Physicians, generally recommend introducing solids around 6 months of age, with readiness determined by developmental cues such as head control and interest in food.9,10,11 Despite established guidelines, studies show that many parents introduce solids earlier than recommended, with nearly one-half starting before 4 months of age,12,13 while others delay introduction beyond 7 months.14 This disconnect highlights the importance of understanding caregiver knowledge, beliefs, and decision-making, as well as the need for better communication between families and healthcare professionals.

Parental decisions around SFI are shaped by a complex interplay of personal beliefs, social influences, cultural norms, and structural circumstances. Caregivers frequently encounter conflicting information from multiple sources, including healthcare providers, family members, and online platforms, which contributes to uncertainty and inconsistent practices.15, 16, 17, 18 Given these challenges, this study aimed to explore current parental perceptions and concerns regarding SFI using a qualitative approach.

Methods

We conducted a qualitative participatory research study using virtual semistructured interviews (lasting 30–45 minutes) to explore factors influencing SFI, including caregiver decision-making processes, internal and external influences, contextual circumstances, facilitators, and barriers. This analysis is part of a larger qualitative study examining a broad range of factors shaping infant feeding practices among caregivers in the Chicago metropolitan area, including breastfeeding, formula feeding, and the transition to solid foods. The findings reported here focus specifically on caregiver experiences and practices related to SFI.

Before the interviews, a short 20-question sociodemographic survey was distributed, inquiring about race/ethnicity, family structure, parental education, medical insurance, and breastfeeding and formula feeding history. The interview guide was informed by prestudy focus groups with 24 caregivers of 8- to 16-month-old infants. During the focus groups, various versions of the interview questions were tested to gauge caregiver interpretation and response. We also found that parents of children older than 13 months sometimes had difficulty recalling feeding practices used during SFI, resulting in the age inclusion criteria being modified to 7- to 13-month-old infants. The institutional review board at the children's hospital and outpatient network approved this study.

Given the limited theoretical research examining caregiver decision-making surrounding SFI, the interview guide was informed by the theory of planned behavior (TPB).19 The TPB was selected because it provides a well-established framework for understanding how attitudes, perceived social influences (subjective norms), and perceived behavioral control shape behavioral intentions and practices. These constructs aligned closely with the study objectives of exploring caregivers' knowledge, beliefs, decision-making processes, feeding practices, and sources of information related to SFI. Although alternative frameworks such as social cognitive theory emphasize observational learning and self-efficacy, and the health belief model focuses primarily on perceived threat and susceptibility, the TPB was most appropriate here because it explicitly captures the role of social norms and perceived control, both of which are central to caregiver feeding decisions.20,21 The TPB has been successfully applied in infant feeding and complementary feeding research to identify factors influencing caregiver feeding decisions and practices, including decisions regarding the introduction of complementary foods and breastfeeding behaviors.19,22,23 Furthermore, the TPB has demonstrated utility in identifying modifiable determinants of caregiver behavior that may serve as targets for future interventions.22 Interview questions were developed to explore TPB domains, including knowledge and beliefs about SFI, perceived behavioral control over feeding practices, and the influence of information sources and social networks.

Setting and Participants

All English-speaking parents of infants between 8 and 13 months of age who were patients at several Chicago-area clinics were invited to participate. Couples from the same household were invited to participate jointly in a single interview; however, the majority of interviews were conducted with 1 caregiver. All interviews, whether individual or joint, represented a single household unit and were analyzed accordingly. The exclusion criteria included parents of children with medical conditions that would alter SFI practices and parents involved in other feeding-related studies. These criteria were applied using medical chart review and the study's 20-question screening survey. To obtain data from a diverse group of parents, recruitment occurred at 2 academically affiliated pediatric clinics located in 2 different communities, a private practice network, and a large federally qualified healthcare center.

Interview Procedures and Analysis

Interviews were conducted via a cloud-based video conferencing program by a trained qualitative investigator, recorded, and professionally transcribed. Transcripts were coded using a deductive approach,24 guided by the TPB framework and the study's interview questions. Coding was conducted independently by 2 investigators using MaxQDA 24.7 (VERBI Software, Berlin, Germany). The initial codebook was created using the interview questions as a guide. Both investigators independently coded the first 5 transcripts, discussed coding differences, and refined the codebook until consensus was reached. The remaining 44 transcripts were then independently coded by both investigators in their entirety. Any coding differences across all transcripts were discussed and resolved by consensus across the full analysis team.

The analysis team consisted of 3 members: 1 research pediatrician and 2 public health researchers. Thematic analysis was conducted using a modified grounded theory approach, in which a priori theoretical categories drawn from the TPB framework were used as a starting point while also allowing new themes to emerge inductively from the data.25 This approach differs from classical grounded theory in that it does not aim to generate a new theory from scratch, but rather uses existing theoretical constructs to organize and interpret the data while remaining open to emergent findings. The team independently reviewed and annotated transcripts, followed by group discussion to identify and finalize major and minor themes by consensus. Although thematic saturation was reached for many themes after 35 interviews, a review of sociodemographic data revealed that single-parent households and Medicaid-enrolled families were underrepresented. Targeted recruitment was initiated using the screening survey and medical chart review and continued until thematic saturation was observed across all demographic groups, resulting in a total of 49 interviews.

Results

From September 2023 to December 2024, a total of 431 participants were screened and invited via email and postcard. Of these, 105 (24%) responded to the initial invitation, with 31 ultimately agreeing to participate. All nonrespondents were subsequently contacted via phone call and text, resulting in an additional 29 participants. Of the 60 participants scheduled for an interview, 49 (83%) completed the study.

Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of caregivers participating in this study. Participants were primarily mothers (92%). By race, 21 (43%) identified as White, 14 (29%) as Black, 4 (8%) as Asian, 1 (2%) as Native American, 7 (14%) as mixed race or other, and 2 (4%) as unknown. By ethnicity, 14 (29%) identified as Latino or Hispanic. Analysis of the qualitative interviews yielded 7 overarching themes: (1) conflicting beliefs about timing and family readiness, (2) fear of choking, (3) parental control over feeding, (4) desire for a good start, (5) siblings shaping feeding practices, (6) cultural and family influences, and (7) adjusted feeding practices based on circumstances. Table 2 presents representative caregiver quotes in relation to these themes, which highlight the lived experiences, challenges, and perspectives shaping SFI. These excerpts provide context and depth to the thematic findings described in this article.

Theme 1: Conflicting Beliefs about Timing and Caregiver Readiness

Caregivers described a range of motivations and hesitations regarding the timing of introducing solid foods. Motivations for earlier introduction included beliefs about supporting infant development, improving feeding skills, increasing food acceptance, and addressing nutritional needs, particularly for infants perceived as large or small. Some viewed early introduction as a way to promote dietary diversity or prevent allergies, while others introduced solids in response to observable infant cues such as reaching for food or watching others eat. Reasons for delaying introduction included the desire to continue breastfeeding, maintaining formula feeding routines, lack of caregiver readiness, and the demands of food preparation. Some caregivers postponed introduction due to uncertainty or a lack of information, and others described receiving conflicting advice from online sources, family members, or prior experience, which contributed to hesitation.

In our sample, most caregivers introduced solids between 4 and 6 months, although a few reported beginning as early as 3 months based on their own research, and others waited until after 9 months. One of the most prominent factors influencing timing was pediatric guidance. Many caregivers described relying on recommendations from their pediatrician when navigating conflicting information about readiness cues and appropriate timing. One mother described using her pediatrician's 6-month recommendation as her primary anchor when online information felt overwhelming.

I just had the 6-month mark in my mind. He would definitely watch us eating and stuff like that, but I kind of heard mixed things on the internet about signs to know that he's ready. So I just really was okay with it. I heard 6 months, I'm going to go with that. I don't need to investigate that any deeper. (White mother, first-time parent)

Caregivers also described their own readiness as influencing feeding practices. Some felt less prepared than expected when moving beyond purees, which shaped how and when they introduced new textures. One mother described feeling more cautious than anticipated when introducing nonpureed foods.

I think I was more nervous than I expected to be with giving her nonpurees. I don't think I ever planned to do baby-led weaning, but I thought I might be a little more open to it, and it turns out I wasn't. I ended up being very careful about cutting food into tiny pieces and doing a lot of the feeding myself. I leaned into those things more than I thought I would. (First-time parent, private insurance)

Perceptions of infant readiness also influenced timing decisions, with caregivers describing observable cues such as reaching for food, interest in family meals, and teething as signals that their child was ready to begin or advance solid foods.

Theme 2: Fear of Choking

Fear of choking was a commonly reported concern that influenced both the timing and method of SFI. Caregivers frequently described avoiding small pieces or textured foods due to safety concerns, which led to slower progression with solids and closer monitoring of developmental readiness. To address these concerns, caregivers sought guidance from pediatricians, feeding programs, or online resources, with visual guides on appropriate food shapes, sizes, and textures being particularly valued. Readiness for nonpureed foods was sometimes associated with physical indicators, such as the number of teeth the infant had, reflecting broader concerns about caregiver confidence in managing choking risk. One caregiver described uncertainty about whether they would be able to respond appropriately if their child experienced a choking event.

I think choking is actually a big thing that you think about when you start to feed the young child, because it's the component of, do I know how to take care of this if she starts choking? And that was a big consideration. (Hispanic mother of 3)

Similarly, another caregiver described feeling torn between encouraging independent eating and concerns about choking, which contributed to hesitation about baby-led weaning.

I did definitely have that initial concern about her choking and stuff, because I was just going back and forth. Like, oh, I want her to be an independent eater, but I also don't want her to choke. So, I was kind of hesitant about baby-led weaning. (White mother of 2)

These concerns were not limited to typically developing infants. Caregivers of preterm infants described a heightened level of anxiety around choking, often choosing to progress more slowly and follow infant cues closely before advancing textures.

Theme 3: Parental Control over Feeding

Parents described varying levels of control over their child's feeding. Some created structured food introduction plans, but these were sometimes altered when family members or daycare staff introduced foods earlier than intended. Some parents reported that daycare provided foods they had not yet introduced at home, while others described adopting daycare feeding practices at home. When food choices outside the home were beyond their control, parents described compromising in those settings while emphasizing regaining control once feeding occurred within the household. One parent described how grandparents and daycare staff introduced foods earlier than planned.

Everyone has the plan, right? And then family and daycare and everyone else just does it. And at that point, you're just like, okay, they tried it. I did kind of have a plan that I just wanted to do fruit. And then grandparents and school were just like, and here are the vegetables. At first I was like, but they're thicker, they're bigger, they feel more dangerous. Then it was like, well, too late, they already ate them and they were perfectly fine. So I'm like, okay, for now. (Black mother, first-time parent)

Parents also described modifying feeding practices based on the realities of daily caregiving. Time constraints and concerns about mess sometimes limited opportunities for self-feeding, even when parents valued infant autonomy at mealtimes. One caregiver explained how these practical demands affected her approach.

She does do a lot of self-feeding, but there are also times when it's like, okay, feed your baby yogurt, or something more liquidy or just really messy, and they're like, give them a spoon and let them self-feed, and I'm just like, I can't right now have my baby throwing a bowl of yogurt all over the house. She's not going to eat anything, and I just don't have the bandwidth to deal with this mess right now. (White first-time parent, private insurance)

These accounts highlight that parental feeding intentions were frequently shaped by external circumstances, including childcare arrangements and the practical demands of daily life, rather than by knowledge or motivation alone.

Theme 4: Desire for a Good Start

Across interviews, parents consistently expressed intentions to foster positive eating habits from the outset, often described as giving their child a good start. This goal translated into deliberate efforts to limit or avoid salt, sugar, and processed foods during early feeding. Caregivers reported preparing homemade meals specifically tailored for their infants, emphasizing whole ingredients and natural flavors. Some cooked separate meals for their child, adjusting seasonings and textures to suit developmental needs while avoiding additives commonly found in store-bought options. One caregiver described preparing homemade foods to avoid added sugars and other ingredients commonly found in processed products.

I tried not to give him any processed food. Everything I gave him was all homemade because I didn't want to give him that additive sugar or any other things that were in the boxed treats or whatever… I want him to eat the foods, but I can make it. (Asian mother, Medicaid recipient)

Vegetables were frequently prioritized as a key part of the infant's early diet, with caregivers describing early and repeated exposure to vegetables as a deliberate strategy for shaping long-term food preferences and reducing pickiness. Regardless of whether foods were prepared at home or purchased from a store, parents described being highly attentive to ingredient quality. Even caregivers who did not prepare homemade meals described carefully reviewing food labels for sodium, sugar, and preservative content when selecting products for their children. One caregiver explained avoiding premade foods specifically because of concerns about sodium and sugar content.

I didn't want her to give her like a lot of like canned foods and premade foods, because I can't control the salt level and if it's sugar and stuff in there (Black mother, Medicaid recipient)

Theme 5: Siblings Shaped Feeding Practices

Parents reported that older siblings influenced feeding practices for younger children in meaningful ways. When older siblings had healthy eating habits, parents described replicating those strategies with younger children. When older siblings were picky eaters or presented feeding challenges, parents reported making deliberate changes to their approach, such as adjusting the variety or texture of foods offered or removing distractions during meals. Some parents described intentionally broadening the range of foods offered to younger children based on lessons learned from feeding older siblings. One caregiver explained wanting to ensure her younger child had the opportunity to try a variety of foods before forming preferences.

I wanted to make sure that she tries everything like the rest of her siblings got a chance to do so and not just force the fact of her not liking things without her trying them first. (Black mother of 4)

For other caregivers, challenges experienced with older siblings motivated changes in their feeding approach with subsequent children. One parent described how her older child's selective eating directly shaped her strategy for introducing foods to a younger sibling.

My 3-year-old's really selective with food. So I kind of wanted to see if I did something different, maybe hopefully that wouldn't happen with [child's name]. (Hispanic mother of 2)

Across these accounts, sibling experiences served as a practical reference point that shaped caregivers' confidence, flexibility, and food variety decisions during SFI.

Theme 6: Cultural and Family Influences

Cultural background and family traditions influenced caregivers' decisions about the foods introduced to their children. Parents reported including culturally significant foods such as rice and beans, plantains, traditional soups, and specific fruits or vegetables, with preparation methods adjusted for the child's developmental stage through pureeing, mashing, or removing strong spices. Some caregivers described tension between offering culturally familiar foods and concerns about specific ingredients such as salt, oil, or spices, and how these changes might affect their child's health. Foods considered staples in the family's culture were often incorporated into the child's diet from the start, with adjustments made for texture or portion size.

Some caregivers described adapting cultural food practices to ensure greater dietary variety for their child compared with what they themselves had experienced growing up. One parent explained intentionally broadening the range of foods offered due to limitations in traditional family diets.

My main concern was to offer her a variety of different foods and different tastes and textures. The diet for babies in my parents' culture is more limited because they do a lot of soups that are just meat and flour… no vegetables. I kind of wanted to make sure her diet included more variety, and with that, balancing all the other factors and making sure that she's getting enough. (White mother of 2)

Caregivers also described uncertainty and variation within cultural feeding norms, particularly regarding timing and the introduction of specific foods. One participant described receiving mixed messages within her cultural community about when infants should begin eating family foods, ultimately relying on her pediatrician to navigate that uncertainty.

My culture, we're Mexican. And I feel like a lot of Mexican people are like, oh, they can eat anything since like 6 or 7 months. And other people are like, no, you should wait because of the allergies. I wasn't sure until the doctor told me that it's perfectly fine, and even then I kind of felt like, hmm. But then we did it. (Hispanic/Latinx mother of 2)

These findings underscore the complex interplay between cultural identity, family tradition, and professional guidance in shaping caregiver feeding decisions, with many parents navigating competing expectations as they introduced solid foods to their children.

Theme 7: Adjusted Feeding Practices Based on Circumstances

Time constraints were a common challenge for caregivers navigating SFI. Caregivers with busy work schedules, multiple children, and household responsibilities reported limited capacity to prepare fresh or elaborate meals. Strategies to address these issues included using prepackaged baby foods, offering leftovers, relying on foods prepared by family members, and serving adjusted portions of family meals. Financial constraints also shaped food choices, with some caregivers preferring organic or higher-quality products, but reporting that these were often cost prohibitive. Adaptations included purchasing items on sale, obtaining food from food pantries, and preparing homemade purees from lower-cost bulk ingredients. One caregiver described how rising food costs created ongoing budgeting challenges for the household.

Even with the price, food prices are just going up, and sometimes you're just limited in what you can get. So that has also been something that you have to budget for, the baby's food, the toddler, and then us. So yeah, some restrictions there too. (Mother of 2, Medicaid recipient)

For some caregivers, financial and time constraints intersected in ways that affected both food quality and preparation methods. One stay-at-home father described the guilt associated with being unable to meet his own standards for fresh food preparation.

Pricing especially, yes. I know that it's better to stick with organic food, like veggies, fruits, or even yogurt, but I decided not to go with that because of finances. And not having a full-time job as a stay-at-home dad, I would spend more time preparing food, but I really hate giving him food once I make a batch, freeze it in a cube, and defrost it. It doesn't really taste the same as fresh made. So I feel really guilty for that. It's all from financial reasons or time constraints. (Asian father, private insurance)

Beyond time and finances, food availability and access also influenced feeding decisions. Some caregivers reported difficulty obtaining desired products in stores, and seasonal availability affected the selection of fruits and vegetables offered to their children. Caregivers also described relying on family members and childcare settings to assist with meal preparation, and returning to work after parental leave was identified as a significant barrier to maintaining preferred feeding practices. As one caregiver explained, the demands of employment directly limited her ability to implement the feeding approach she had planned.

Returning to work right away was a factor. Sometimes not having the time to prepare ahead of time, even if I have the necessary tools like a baby blender… I was thinking I'd boil some apples and make my own applesauce. But always working and being on the go, that's what prevented me from doing what I wanted to do for her when she started eating solids. (Hispanic/Latinx mother, Medicaid recipient)

Discussion

This qualitative study explored how caregivers navigate the introduction of solid foods across diverse household contexts. Seven themes emerged from the data: conflicting beliefs about timing and caregiver readiness, fear of choking, parental control over feeding, desire for a good start, siblings shaping feeding practices, cultural and family influences, and adjusted feeding practices based on circumstances. Although most participants introduced solids between 4 and 6 months of age, consistent with pediatric guidelines, the process was far from standardized. Instead, feeding decisions reflected a negotiation among external advice, infant cues, household readiness, cultural traditions, and structural constraints. These findings build on prior qualitative work demonstrating that early feeding is shaped as much by context and lived experience as by formal guidance.22,26,27 Unlike prior studies that have relied primarily on survey or cohort data,4,12,14 this study offers an in-depth, contextualized account of how caregivers interpret and adapt feeding guidance within their everyday environments.

Caregivers in this study consistently described tension between following standardized recommendations and adapting feeding practices to fit their household realities. Although evidence-based guidelines provide a critical framework for ensuring safety and nutritional adequacy across populations,2,4 caregivers' ability to implement these recommendations is often shaped by sociocultural norms, resource constraints, and lived experience.22 These findings support the value of a flexible, family-centered approach to SFI guidance, one that acknowledges contextual realities while maintaining a foundation in evidence-based practice.2 Practical strategies for delivering this kind of tailored guidance include anticipatory counseling during pediatric visits, culturally adapted educational materials, and outreach through government-sponsored nutrition programs, such as the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children.28

Caregivers in this study described a range of desires and concerns that shaped their feeding decisions, including intentions to provide nutritious foods and establish healthy eating habits, alongside concerns about choking risk, developmental readiness, and the practical demands of food preparation. Feeding decisions were rarely made in isolation. Considerations from family members, siblings, and childcare providers exerted significant influence, sometimes supporting and other times complicating parental intentions. This dynamic is consistent with the TPB framework that guided this study, in which subjective norms and perceived behavioral control play central roles in shaping behavioral intentions and practices.19,22 The influence of social networks and external caregivers on feeding decisions reflects the subjective norm construct in particular, whereby the perceived expectations of family members and other caregivers shaped what parents felt they should or could do.19 These findings underscore the need for healthcare providers to deliver guidance that directly addresses the motivators and barriers caregivers encounter, including safety concerns, conflicting advice, and practical constraints, to support informed and confident feeding decisions.2,22

Caregivers frequently described receiving conflicting guidance from healthcare providers, online sources, and cultural traditions, which contributed to uncertainty in decision-making. For many, this tension extended beyond timing to questions about what foods to introduce. Several caregivers reported avoiding or delaying culturally familiar foods, such as spiced dishes, grains, or textured family meals, out of concern for their child's health or fear of deviating from pediatric advice, favoring foods perceived as safer even when cultural practices suggested otherwise. Consistent with prior research demonstrating that cultural feeding norms strongly influence caregiver perceptions of readiness and appropriate foods,2,27 mixed messaging was perceived as undermining parental confidence, particularly regarding timing and food textures. These findings reinforce the need for healthcare professionals to deliver culturally responsive guidance that respects traditional feeding practices while clarifying safety and nutritional recommendations.

The proliferation of online nutrition information and feeding apps represents an additional source of conflicting guidance for caregivers. Parents in this study described turning to internet searches and social media platforms when professional or family guidance felt insufficient or contradictory, consistent with prior research documenting the widespread use of online sources for infant feeding information.17,18 Although digital resources offer accessible and convenient guidance, the accuracy and quality of nutrition information available online varies considerably, and caregivers may lack the health literacy tools needed to evaluate source credibility.17 Apps and online platforms focused on infant feeding have grown in popularity and, in some cases, appear to function as substitutes for professional guidance, particularly among caregivers with limited access to healthcare providers.29 Healthcare providers are well positioned to help families navigate this landscape by directing them toward evidence-based digital resources and addressing misinformation proactively during clinical encounters.2

Fear of choking emerged as a significant barrier to advancing beyond purees, consistent with prior research documenting that parental safety concerns contribute to the delayed introduction of lumpy and textured foods in diverse populations.4 For many caregivers in this study, this anxiety overshadowed awareness of the developmental and nutritional importance of texture progression, including the role of varied textures in supporting oral motor development and dietary diversification.2 Caregivers frequently described seeking practical, visual, and developmentally appropriate resources to build confidence in their feeding practices, noting that written guidelines alone were often insufficient to address their concerns. These findings suggest that anticipatory guidance delivered during pediatric visits, including hands-on demonstrations, visual feeding guides, and instruction in infant choking response, may help caregivers to progress more confidently toward age-appropriate feeding.28 Addressing choking-related anxiety through targeted skill-building and supportive counseling has the potential to reduce delays in texture advancement, increase dietary variety, and foster a more positive feeding experience for both caregivers and infants.

Caregivers' shared intention to provide a good start for their children highlighted a recurring tension between feeding ideals and practical feasibility. Although many parents described aspirations to avoid processed foods, prepare homemade meals, and prioritize dietary variety, time constraints, financial limitations, and food access barriers often shaped what was realistically achievable. For caregivers managing multiple children, demanding work schedules, or limited household resources, these structural constraints directly influenced the quality and variety of foods offered during SFI. A lack of time for meal preparation and limited access to feeding education or counseling may also contribute to lower caregiver self-efficacy around feeding decisions, particularly during the postpartum period, when guidance needs are high.30 These findings reflect the broader structural inequities inherent in early feeding, whereby financial and social resources shape caregivers' capacity to meet their own feeding goals. Similar disparities have been documented in prior studies linking socioeconomic status and resource access with variation in complementary feeding practices and dietary quality.4,31,32

Not all studies have found health professionals to play a central role in guiding SFI. Weber et al. conducted a qualitative study using focus group discussions with a diverse sample of mothers of children under 2 years of age, stratified by income and race/ethnicity, at an academic medical center in Atlanta, Georgia.33 Their findings indicate that mothers made feeding decisions based largely on their own instincts, consulted clinicians least often among available guidance sources, and frequently felt frustrated by strict guidelines and negative messaging from healthcare providers. These findings contrast with the present study, in which pediatric recommendations were consistently described as a primary anchor for caregiver decision-making, particularly when online or family-based information felt contradictory or overwhelming. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. The present study recruited caregivers directly from pediatric clinic settings, which likely skewed the sample toward families with more frequent and positive healthcare engagement. Additionally, the greater proportion of college-educated caregivers in the present sample may have contributed to a greater reliance on professional guidance, because health literacy has been associated with the increased use of provider recommendations in infant feeding contexts.4 Importantly, both studies converge on a key implication: caregivers are most receptive to guidance when they feel supported, valued, and respected in the decision-making process, rather than subject to prescriptive or judgmental recommendations. This shared finding underscores the importance of a collaborative, strengths-based approach to anticipatory feeding guidance across diverse clinical settings.

Several limitations of this study should be considered when interpreting these findings. The sample was recruited from pediatric clinic settings in the Chicago metropolitan area, which may limit generalizability to caregivers in suburban or rural settings, as well as those with less frequent healthcare engagement. The exclusion of non–English-speaking caregivers represents a meaningful gap given the linguistic diversity of the study's urban context. This exclusion reflected the resource constraints of the current grant mechanism, as rigorous multilingual qualitative research requires interviewers, coders, and analysts who can conduct the entire research process in the participants' native language. A parallel grant mechanism is being pursued to extend this work to non–English-speaking families. Nuances in feeding experiences may have been lost when perspectives were conveyed in English rather than participants' native language. Although the sample included racial and ethnic diversity, it contained a greater proportion of college-educated caregivers compared with the general population; however, with one-third of participants lacking a bachelor's degree, the study captured a broader educational range than many prior qualitative studies in this area. The low participation rate of 11% of initially contacted individuals, despite multiple outreach methods, raises the possibility of self-selection bias, with the sample potentially skewed toward caregivers who were more motivated or engaged with feeding-related topics. This low rate likely reflects both the limited bandwidth of families with young infants and the constraints of remote recruitment during a period when post-COVID protocols prevented in-person clinic recruitment, which would have been the most effective method of directly engaging families.

Recruitment was conducted remotely due to post-COVID restrictions, with interviews conducted via a cloud-based video conferencing program or phone. Although this approach may have introduced technology-related access barriers that further shaped who was able to participate, conducting interviews remotely also increased accessibility for families of young infants by removing travel and childcare burdens. Phone interviews were available as an alternative for participants unable to use video, and all participants who completed the study elected the cloud-based video option voluntarily, suggesting that technology access was not a prohibitive barrier among those who participated. Although couples were invited to participate jointly, most interviews were conducted with a single caregiver, and in the small number of joint interviews, the presence of a partner may have influenced individual responses. Finally, conclusions about feeding practices relied on caregiver self-report, and, although the research team worked collaboratively to interpret responses and reach consensus, the possibility of misclassification or recall bias remains.

The introduction of solid foods is a complex and dynamic process shaped by an ongoing negotiation among professional recommendations, cultural traditions, family dynamics, safety concerns, and structural circumstances. This study highlights that, although many caregivers follow professional guidance on timing, their feeding decisions are deeply influenced by personal values, household constraints, and the social contexts in which they live. These findings underscore the need for a flexible, family-centered approach to early nutrition guidance, one that recognizes and accommodates the diverse lived experiences of caregivers rather than applying uniform prescriptive recommendations. Pediatricians and healthcare providers are well positioned to strengthen their guidance by addressing the specific motivators and barriers that caregivers encounter, delivering culturally responsive recommendations, and connecting families with practical, accessible resources. By offering adaptable and context-sensitive support, providers can better equip caregivers to navigate SFI with confidence and meet the nutritional needs of their infants during this critical developmental period.
