Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

Create a detailed manufacturing, assembly, load-out, transportation, upending, turbine
integration, and offshore transportation plan for a hypothetical project in one of the California
lease areas off the central coast. Develop a detailed schedule and cost estimation for each step of
the process and compare with conventional floating platform designs.

Summary of Research Results:

Task 1: Assist Participant modeling assembly and installation process

Contractor will assist Participant in modeling the cost and schedule requirements for
manufacturing, assembly, load-out, transportation, upending, turbine integration, and offshore
transportation of a hypothetical offshore wind energy project on the California central coast.

Task 1 Results:

For this task, NREL used the Offshore Renewables Balance-of-system and Installation Tool
(ORBIT) to model offshore wind installation costs and schedules. The ORBIT model is an open-
source tool developed by NREL to simulate individual processes during the installation phases,
incorporating the effects of weather delays and vessel interactions [1]. This task included some
model development focused on adapting NREL’s existing tools to represent Aikido’s technology
and process.

We customized the ORBIT implementation for Aikido’s process by adding a transportation stage
for the completed substructures to go from the manufacturing site to the S&I site and adding wet
storage capacity at the S&I site. There are also some differences in the timing and sequencing of
steps within the turbine integration process, which we addressed by adjusting the default values
in the standard ORBIT sequence.

ORBIT requires hourly wind speed and wave height data to model the effects of operational
constraints under location-specific weather conditions. For this study, there are two primary
locations for installation activities: the Morro Bay wind energy area (WEA) and San Pedro Bay,
near the Port of Los Angeles. Wind turbines are integrated with the floating platforms in San
Pedro Bay, then they are towed to Morro Bay WEA where they are moored for operations. To
represent weather at the wind plant site, we selected a point within the Morro Bay WEA (35.6°N,
121.8°W) at which data was available from the ERA5 reanalysis [2] and extracted significant
wave height and wind speed at a height of 100 m.

To create a representative weather time series for San Pedro Bay, we relied on a combination of
data sources. Although we consider the ERA5 reanalysis data to be a good representation of
weather conditions in the Morro Bay offshore wind energy area, ERA5 wave data are less
reliable for nearshore locations including San Pedro Bay. Observational data are available from
the National Data Buoy Center [3] at various coastal buoy locations. Buoy #46253 is located
within San Pedro Bay and reports hourly significant wave height. However, the buoy does not
measure wind speed and there are some data gaps. We constructed a weather input file for the
San Pedro Bay using the ERA5 wind speed at 100 m above sea level and the buoy-measured
significant wave height from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2015. For any hours in which
buoy data was not available, we used the ERA5 wave height.

 2

We modeled the assembly and installation schedule for a 1-GW wind power plant on the central
coast of California with no weather delays and with weather corresponding to Morro Bay and
San Pedro Bay (Figure 1). Although ORBIT does not have the capability to model weather at
multiple locations, we can adjust the wind and wave constraints on individual processes to
approximate the delays that accrue at each location. For the Aikido installation process, we
needed to consider activities within San Pedro Bay and in the open ocean along California’s
central coast. To simulate operation at these locations, we used the Morro Bay WEA weather
input file, but adjusted the wave height and wind speed limits for processes taking place in San
Pedro Bay. The adjusted limits were chosen such that the number of hours above the respective
thresholds in the Morro Bay WEA were the same as the number of hours above the original
limits in San Pedro Bay. The resulting installation time and costs are shown in Figure 1.

Task 2: Compare Participant process costs and schedule with conventional floating
platform designs

Contractor will model the cost and schedule requirements for conventional steel and/or concrete
semisubmersible floating platform assembly and installation, using inputs that are consistent with
the assumptions used to model the Participant process to enable cost comparisons between the
technology alternatives.

Task 2 Results:

The “conventional” floating platform assembly process relies on the standard implementation of
ORBIT for the substructure design, assembly time, turbine integration process, and tow to site.
To set up a scenario equivalent to the Aikido case, we added a transportation step between
substructure assembly at a manufacturing site and turbine integration at the S&I port in San
Pedro Bay. The other project inputs (number of turbines, port distance, cable types, etc.)
remained the same as the Aikido case. The Morro Bay offshore wind site weather profile was
used for both cases.

 3

Project costs for the Aikido and Conventional cases are shown in Figure 2. The modeled CapEx
for most project elements is the same in both cases; however, there is a large difference in the
platform and turbine installation cost and a smaller difference in the substructure procurement
cost. The higher installation cost corresponds with a longer installation time for the Conventional
case. The additional time required for the Conventional case is mainly attributable to the longer
substructure assembly time; however, Aikido’s turbine integration process also contributes to
lowering the installation time.
