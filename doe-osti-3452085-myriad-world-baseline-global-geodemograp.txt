 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The LandScan Myriad World Baseline (MWB) method produces global, residential (nighttime/home-
location) gridded geodemographic estimates based on 5-year age/gender cohorts—at 30-arcsecond
(≈1 km) resolution. MWB is designed to fill gaps where detailed, georeferenced survey data (e.g.,
Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS)) are missing or outdated, and to provide a baseline that
can support human security analysis, including consequence assessment, “patterns of life” model-
ing, and scenario-based population futures. MWB’s workflow spatializes household-level age/gender
characteristics from the GLOPOP-S dataset by conflating household and gridded expected rela-
tive wealth adapted from Global Gridded Relative Deprivation Index (GRDI), then adjusts them
to a target year of interest. Age/gender estimates are then applied to harmonize lowest-administrative-
level statistics with LandScan residential counts, yielding final geodemographic estimates. Two
validation case studies are presented: Ghana (2021) and Tokyo/Kanagawa, Japan (2020), illus-
trating spatial variability in demographic cohorts and comparing MWB outputs to official gridded
statistics. Results show close overall alignment relative to validation criteria including population
pyramids and age-dependency ratios.

 2. INTRODUCTION
Improving situational awareness toward emerging global human security challenges – including
population risk/vulnerability and population change – requires geodemographic estimates rep-
resenting variability in demographic composition (e.g., school-age children, working-age adults,
senior adults; labor force eligibility by gender) at high spatial resolution (buildings to neighbor-
hoods). A fundamental component of geodemographics is representing the distribution of residen-
tial population, which serves as a starting point a variety of population dynamics models, includ-
ing those centered on patterns of life, resource access, and spatial demography.
The LandScan Myriad project’s core model expands the practice of demographic characteriza-
tion using global population and health surveys [17] by incorporating building-based and environ-
mental attributes, enabling a granular representation of geodemographics with national/regional
coverage [12]. However, this approach requires georeferenced surveys such as those from the De-
mographic and Health Surveys (DHS) program, which are not consistently available globally. The
Myriad World Baseline (MWB) model addresses this challenge by producing baseline geodemo-
graphic estimates that fill coverage gaps in DHS and other population and health surveys. MWB
estimates are based on 5-year age/gender cohorts and can be produced at 30-arcsecond (≈1km)
resolution for most of the world.
The MWB workflow builds upon GLOPOP-S, a 2015 subnational synthetic population dataset
available for 182 countries [19]. For a given country and year of interest, MWB develops a prior
expectation of age/gender distribution by (1) downscaling GLOPOP-S to LandScan residential es-
timates for 2015 at 30-arcsecond resolution by conflating wealth/income with the Center for Inte-
grated Earth System Information (CIESIN) GRDI [2] then (2) forward-projecting 5-year age/gender
cohorts to the target year using time series data from the Census International Database (IDB).
For the target year, the prior estimates are then combined with lowest-administrative-level age/gender
statistics and reweighted to harmonize them with the LandScan residential population. The end
product provides a refined geodemographic estimate broadly applicable to human security-related
research problems.

 3. DATA SOURCES
3.1 GLOPOP-S
GLOPOP-S provides the foundational population data for producing MWB estimates. The 2015
baseline synthetic population for GLOPOP-S is generated by fitting data from the DHS pro-
gram and Luxembourg Income Study (LIS) to subnational population statistics, first by simu-
lating households and individuals from national-level joint distributions, then downscaling these
to Global Data Lab (GDL) regions (see 3.2) [19]. When survey data is not available for a country
or region, it is approximated from those with similar subnational human development index lev-
els (roughly 55% of countries) [19]. Due to the dataset’s large size, MWB obtains country-specific
data from its Harvard Dataverse Repository [18] on an as-needed basis. To facilitate this, MWB
uses a fork of the GLOPOP-S GitHub repository1 that modifies its Python input/output (I/O)
functionality2 to (1) parameterize country GDL code and data path and (2) write results by GDL
region to a specified local output path in a Zstandard-compressed [4] Apache Parquet file.

3.2 GLOBAL DATA LAB BOUNDARIES
GLOPOP-S is georeferenced to the GDL v4 boundaries, produced by the Institute for Manage-
ment Research at Radboud University3 . GDL regions typically match a single administrative level
1 (Adm1) within a country but sometimes may be a larger collection of Adm1s. To facilitate more
efficient storage and I/O operations for MWB, we converted the GDL v4 boundaries from their
native ESRI Shapefile format to GeoParquet.

3.3 LANDSCAN RESIDENTIAL ESTIMATES
LandScan Mosaic Time Series (LSM-TS) [24], an extension of LandScan Mosaic [1] that backcasts
and forecasts population from 1975-2025 based on the Global Human Settlement Layer Built-Up
Surface (GSHL BUILT-S) [13], serves as the spatial and population reference both for downscal-
ing GLOPOP-S (2015) and producing final age/gender estimates for the target year. MWB uses
a specialized version of LSM-TS for nighttime constrained to residential buildings from the Land-
Scan Building Layer (LBL) and aggregated to 30-arcsecond resolution, LandScan Mosaic Time
Series Residential (LSM-TSR).

3.4 GLOBAL GRIDDED RELATIVE DEPRIVATION INDEX
The Global Gridded Relative Deprivation Index (GRDI), produced by the Columbia University
CIESIN, is a 30-arcsecond resolution global grid representing social and material deprivation based
on a composite of subnational human development, infant mortality, child dependency ratio, night-
time lights intensity, and built up cover [2]. GRDI is meant to loosely represent the time period
between 2010 and 2020 [2], making it a suitable match for the GLOPOP-S reference year (2015).
In our case, the GRDI serves as the spatial basis for downscaling GLOPOP-S to the LandScan
residential grid relative to household wealth/income. For the sake of stability, MWB still uses
GRDI v1 [3] – the current release when MWB was initially developed – but we will consider v1.1,
which offers updated preprocessing and weighting methods [2], for future versions.

3.5 CENSUS INTERNATIONAL DATABASE
MWB uses the Census IDB application programming interface (API) [21] to fetch annual time
series of age/gender totals for a country of interest. These are used to forward-project the down-
scaled GLOPOP-S estimates to a more current year based on rates of change in age/gender co-
hort estimates. The Census IDB API provides both national tables that stratify a country’s
population by 1-year age intervals and gender, and subnational tables for a smaller selection
of countries that stratify a country’s population by 5-year age intervals, gender, and Adm1 name
[22].

3.6 COUNTRY CODES
To facilitate country-specific I/O operations and data queries, MWB uses a Geopolitical Entities,
Names, and Codes (GENC) table from the US Department of State [23]. The table includes 2-
digit GENC codes (for Census IDB API queries), 3-digit GENC codes (for fetching and caching
GLOPOP-S data, as well as storing results), and short-form names (for labeling country-specific
results).

3.7 SUBNATIONAL DEMOGRAPHIC ESTIMATES
Subnational demographic estimates are used to harmonize the initial MWB expectation of grid-
ded distribution with fine-grain, subnational characteristics and LSM-TS for the current year.
These are represented by age/gender cohorts in 5-year age intervals : {[0, 5), [5, 10), [10, 15)...[80, inf)}.
Due to MWB’s somewhat coarse 30-arcsecond resolution, we aim to produce the harmonized es-
timates at administrative level 3 (Adm3), which generally represents municipalities, wards, or
sub-districts [16]. When Adm3-level data is not available, administrative level 2 (Adm2) data
(provinces, districts) is used instead. While LandScan’s primary source for subnational demo-
graphic data is a country’s national statistical office (NSO), other sources including the Census
IDB, IPUMS International (IPUMS-I), and UN Humanitarian Data Exchange (HDX) are useful
for filling data gaps.

 4. METHODOLOGY
4.1 DOWNSCALING GLOPOP-S TO THE LANDSCAN GRID
The downscaling procedure begins by fetching the GLOPOP-S data matching the country of in-
terest. Each source file, one per GDL region in compressed comma-separated values (CSV) format
(csv.gz) is downloaded locally, uncompressed, and cached to avoid repeat downloads.
The data stack used to facilitate GLOPOP-S downscaling is then produced, using the following
four steps:
  1. The LandScan residential estimates are clipped to the country’s GDL boundaries, with
 the subset referenced to the LandScan global grid system by row and column ID. Due to
 differences between LandScan and GDL boundaries, some edge cells may not belong to the
 country according to LandScan definitions. As such, only cells matching the country ac-
 cording to the LandScan boundary layer are retained. Finally, cells with zero population are
 masked with NA values to preserve only those with available population for downscaling.
  2. The GDL region boundaries are rasterized by region code based on the LandScan res-
 idential estimates such that every populated cell is attributed to a region for subnational
 stratification.
  3. A gridded representation of urban/rural place type is created as an additional stratifi-
 cation layer to match GLOPOP-S. This is achieved by first estimating the national share of
 urban/rural population from GLOPOP-S. Then, ordering the LandScan estimates ascend-
 ing, the grid cell index closest to the percent rural cutoff is identified. The LandScan grid
 is stratified accordingly, with the leading slice assigned R (rural) and the trailing slice U (ur-
 ban).
  4. The Expected Relative Wealth Index (ERWIN), used to conflate relative wealth/income
 between GLOPOP-S and the population grid, is estimated as a population-weighted inverse
 of GRDI, under the assumption that social/material deprivation is negatively associated
 with relative wealth. First, the GRDI grid is clipped to GDL region boundaries and resam-
 pled to the LandScan grid using the nearest-neighbor method. An imputation procedure is
 then performed to ensure that all populated LandScan cells have a GRDI value. A subset
 of cells are inevitably missing GRDI values at populated cells due to disagreement between
 the GRDI source population estimates (GHS-POP) and LandScan. To overcome this, the
 imputation procedure first attempts a focal mean operation (default k = 10 nearest neigh-
 bors) weighted by population. For cells with no valid neighbors in the focal window, the
 population-weighted mean of GRDI by GDL region is then assigned as a failsafe. The im-
 puted GRDI is then masked to populated LandScan cells to ensure that value indices are
 consistent. Computing ERWIN consists of (a) ordering LandScan estimates ascending by
 population, (b) computing the cumulative proportion of population by grid cell, (c) ordering
 GRDI cells descending, and (d) matching the ordered GRDI cells to population percentiles.
The downscaling procedure is carried out on GLOPOP-S stratified into subregions defined by
GDL region and place type. For each subregion, information from the file representing the con-
taining region is queried via duckdb by place type to extract:
 • The unique household ID, written as <GDL_CODE>_<HID>, where HID is converted to a
 string with 9 leading digits to accommodate regions with large household totals.

 • Household-level ERWIN, which is either WEALTH for DHS-based countries or INCOME for
 LIS-based countries.
 • Household size, or population per household.
Extracted households are then ordered by household-level ERWIN quintile label (ERWIN-HHQ)
(ascending) and household size (descending) to prepare for grid allocation.
Grid allocation is performed on the subset of the country data stack matching the subregion.
Grid cells are ordered by ERWIN (ascending) to match the household level. ERWIN-ordered pop-

a local directory ./results/<GENC3>.

4.2 FORWARD-PROJECTING DOWNSCALED AGE/GENDER ESTIMATES
The downscaled GLOPOP-S households are next used to forward-project 5-year age/gender co-
horts to the target year. This procedure (1) uses time series data from the Census IDB API to
estimate change between 2015 and the target year, then (2) applies them to age/gender estimates
by LandScan cell by Adm1. Because the downscaled GLOPOP-S households are georeferenced
to a finer spatial scale than GDL regions, they can be matched to Adm1s for compatibility with
census estimates. This is accomplished using the same LandScan grid reference lookup table de-
scribed in Section 4.1, which identifies all known administrative region labels for the country of
interest.
The forward projection routine begins by extracting annual time series estimates of age/gender
cohort totals from the Census IDB API via representational state transfer (REST) query. A re-
quest for subnational (Adm1) estimates of 5-year age/gender cohorts for the country of interest –
see Table 1 – is first requested. If the REST query successfully returns subnational estimates, any
differences between the LandScan and Census Adm1 names are reconciled, and the estimates are
then organized by Adm1 name and census year. If the initial REST query fails, the single-year
national cohort estimates are fetched instead, with the single-year age intervals binned into 5-year
ones and organized by census year. Change coefficients for each age/gender cohort are then com-
puted from the time series estimates. For each cohort described by age group a and gender g, the
change coefficient δag is the cumulative product of the annual rate of change in population totals
between 2015 and the target year.
A key difference between GLOPOP-S and the Census IDB estimates is that the former uses coarser
age cohorts than the desired 5-year intervals. Because the 5-year age intervals fully nest within
the GLOPOP-S ones, however, it is possible to downsample 5-year interval labels based on their
prevalence within the Census IDB estimates. To facilitate this, a series of sampling probabilities

4.3 FINALIZING THE ESTIMATES
The final procedure involves harmonizing the lowest-administrative-level age/gender totals with
LandScan cell totals for the target year using the initial MWB estimates developed through the
downscaling and forward projection procedures. This is accomplished using two-dimensional Iter-
ative Proportional Fitting (IPF) [5] to reweight the cell values within by administrative unit, with
row margins set to the LandScan grid cell totals for the target year, and column margins set to
the age/gender cohort totals for the target year, both scaled proportionally.
To initialize IPF, the MWB prior matrix developed in Sections 4.1 and 4.2 is blended with a
naïve prior, which represents a proportionally uniform distribution of age/gender cohorts among
grid cells within the lowest administrative-level units4 . The advantage of this approach is twofold:
(1) the MWB prior updates the naïve prior, accounting for spatial heterogeneity in demographic
cohorts unknown within lowest administrative units and (2) the naïve prior applies smoothing to
any modeling artifacts in the MWB prior – namely localized demographic change not accounted
for at the coarse spatial resolution of the downscaling/forward projection procedures (Adm0-1)
including new development, densification, and depopulation. The naïve prior is defined by (1)
spreading the age/gender proportions from the lowest administrative units among all constituent
cells and then (2) multiplying these by LandScan cell counts. The blended prior is a weighted av-
erage of the MWB and naïve priors, with weights set by variance among all cell values in each:

 5. CASE STUDIES
To demonstrate MWB capabilities and provide validation and verification, we developed two case
studies of countries with official gridded population estimates available through a national sta-
tistical office (NSO), Ghana and Japan. These selections enable us to evaluate both Low and
Middle-Income Country (LMIC) (Ghana) and High-Income Country (HIC) (Japan) countries
based on different GLOPOP-S data sources (DHS and LIS, respectively).
Demographic estimates
For each country, we produced two different demographic estimates to illustrate variation in age/gender
composition:
 • Age dependency ratio, the ratio of individuals ages 0-14 and ages 65+ to those of labor
 force age (15-64) by cell. Demonstrates variation in age composition by grid cell.
 • Females of reproductive age, the share of females ages 15-49 out of the total number of
 females per cell. Demonstrates variation in age/gender-specific criteria.
Validation and verification
For each study area, we produced a population pyramid to visually validate distributions unit-
wise proportions of age/gender cohorts between the modeled MWB and NSO results, providing a
high-level overview of alignment in demographic characterization.

5.1 GHANA
5.1.1 Analysis
Our Ghana demonstration centered on 2021, the most recent census year available for that coun-
try. The MWB reweighting procedure used a blended prior matrix supported by Adm2-level age/gender
statistics available from the DHS program via the US Census Bureau [8].
For validation and verification, we compared the MWB estimates to gridded age (10-year group
totals, top censored at age 80) and gender (total female, male) estimates for Ghana in 2021 at
roughly 30-arcsecond resolution, available from the Ghana Statistical Service (GSS) [6]. The Ghana
gridded estimates were obtained from GSS via REST API query in vector (GeoParquet) format.
A key difference in this vectorized version of the estimates is that many grid cells are grouped
into small polygons, perhaps as an artifact of raster-to-vector conversion on the provider side. De-
spite this limitation, this vector-based “quasi-grid” representation still offers much higher spatial

resolution than age/gender estimates released through many NSOs and is straightforward to com-
pare with the modeled MWB estimates. We applied zonal statistics based on point (grid cell cen-
troid) membership within the quasi-grid cells, summing the MWB-reconstructed age and gender
estimates to their containing cells. Because the GSS estimates do not directly describe the inter-
action between age and gender, we approximated distributions of the combined age/gender co-
horts to represent GSS in the population pyramid based on their joint probabilities in each quasi-
grid cell.

 6. LIMITATIONS
• Data-specific: The MWB workflow’s primary data-specific limitation is its reliance on
  lowest-administrative-level census statistics, which may remain difficult to obtain for very
  data poor world regions. However, efforts to collect and catalog these are accelerating, both
  both internally at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) as well as in within the larger
  population modeling community [11]. Additionally, a newer version of GRDI exists (v1.1)
  containing major refinements for delineating populated areas, accounting for input features
  including child-dependency ratios and nighttime lights, and input weighting [3]. This ver-
  sion was released while MWB development was in progress, and it will be evaluated and
  benchmarked in future iterations of the model.
• Model-specific The process for downscaling the GLOPOP-S estimates (Section 4.1) fea-
  tures several known limitations. The first of these relates to the delineation of urban and
  rural zones within GDL regions for allocating GLOPOP-S households for the LandScan
  grid. We define these labels relative to LSM-TS, and they likely vary from the source DHS
  or LIS survey. However, because neither survey provides a full geographic delineation of
  these labels, an approximation remains necessary. Second, our downscaling procedure fea-
  tures some rough edges that we plan to address in future work, including a limited num-
  ber of wealth/income labels in GLOPOP-S and uneven allocation of households by size in
  the downscaling algorithm. The former of these issues can be addressed by enhancing the
  wealth index measure, for example, by combining it with highest level of education in the
  household (GLOPOP-S EDUC) or through data fusion with some ancillary criteria. With
  regards to the latter issue in the downscaling procedure (Section 4.1), households are allo-
  cated by wealth/income label in order of household size to ensure cell capacity is sufficient.
  This means that household sizes on either tail of the distribution (very large, one-person)
  are likely to exclusively comprise some cells. This in turn affects cellular age/gender tabu-
  lation, since age/gender labels are likely conditional on household size. A routine following
  the initial assignment (e.g., combinatorial optimization) is likely needed to more realistically
  simulate the variability in households occurring within each grid cell.
• Case study-specific: The most notable limitation of our case studies is that each could
  only be carried out at the coarser Adm2 level due to data sparseness (Japan) or unavail-
  ability (Ghana) at the Adm3 level. As such, it remains unclear how the performance of the
  MWB model improves against official gridded age/gender estimates when reweighting at the
  Adm3 level.
  Another current issue involves large gaps in LSM-TSR coverage for each country due to in-
  complete identification of residential use type labels in the LandScan Mosaic building stack
  [1]. The most notable of these gaps occur in the Japan case study for the Yamanote Loop
  Line within the Tokyo Prefecture (largely empty Adm2 units visible in the northeastern cor-
  ner of Figures 6, 7, and 9, as well as in the Ghana case study for the Anloga and Keta Mu-
  nicipal Adm2s immediately east of Greater Accra (Figures 1, 2, and 4). In future releases,
  the LSM-TS capability will be adapted for greater inclusion of buildings with potential resi-
  dential use (e.g., above a threshold probability of residential vs. other uses).

 7. CONCLUSION
MWB enables near-global coverage of baseline geodemographics, supporting enhanced under-
standing of population risk and future population scenarios. Toward population risk, MWB pro-
vides a basis for representing population heterogeneity and patterns of life. The MWB estimates
are easily reaggregated to spatial catchments for human mobility (e.g., school districts; social me-
dia/digital trace derived products), providing a more realistic demographic characterization of ac-
tivity spaces than at the administrative level. In the context of population futures, MWB provides
a starting point for simulation of population change under national/regional economic develop-
ment or decline, urbanization and infrastructure development, shifting availability of water and
nutritional resources, and the emergence of violent conflict, which in turn influence population
processes including fertility and mortality, migration, and displacement. In this way, the MWB
approach can integrated into scenarios of global political and economic change such as the Shared
Socioeconomic Pathways [14], delivering a spatially-explicit representation of anticipated shifts in
population distribution toward the year 2100.
Beyond addressing the improvements listed in Section 6, further development of MWB will ex-
plore incorporating other, more current, subnational population data such as Multiple Indica-
tor Cluster Surveys (MICS) [20], as well as indicators of relative wealth such as subnational per-
capita Gross Domestic Product (GDP) [10].
