 Will Fenwick and Bob Deri

Project Overview
Diode-pumped solid state lasers (DPSSLs) are important for a variety of scientific and national
security applications. Laser diodes (LDs) used to pump these lasers are susceptible to failure
caused by pump light that impinges back on these devices, which is known as back-irradiance
(BIR). While BIR failures have been widely reported, their underlying physics of failure is not well
understood. The objective of this project was to develop an understanding of BIR failure physics
that provides predictive capability for use in the design of both DPSSLs and LDs.
  Our team measured BIR-induced catastrophic failures on over 100 devices obtained from two
different LD manufacturers to study the dependence of failure threshold on operating
parameters (pulsewidth, temperature, and LD output power) and certain device parameters
(packaging, optical coating materials). The experimental results were complemented by
thermo-optical modeling & simulation and post-failure characterization. A theoretical model was
developed that explains all the experimental, simulation, and characterization information
obtained.
 Our results show that BIR-induced failures are driven by a thermal runaway process at the
output surface of the LDs, of similar nature to certain failures that occur in the absence of BIR.
The data are encapsulated in a theoretical model that will provide predictive power for the design
of future DPSSLs. Our data show that BIR robustness can be improved by (i) designing DPSSLs to
operate at shorter pump pulsewidths and (ii) by using particular optical designs for delivering
pump light to the main laser. They also show that device manufacturers can improve BIR
robustness by controlling certain LD packaging details that affect the heat sinking near its output
surface, and potentially by modifications of the LD optical coatings.

Mission Impact
The results of this work will enable the design and delivery of more capable laser systems by
LLNL and its partners for a variety of missions relevant to NNSA, DOE, and other government
agencies. Such lasers are building blocks in system solutions that address national security
challenges in several domains, and are anticipated to provide metrology capability for NNSA
missions. They are also key for the DPSSLs needed for future laser-driven, inertial fusion
energy activities in DOE's energy and environmental security missions.
