What’s in a Name? Morphological Shortcuts by LLMs in Pharmacology
Kaijie Mo, Thomas Yang, Chantal Shaib, Qing Yao, William Rudman, Ramez Kouzy, Kanishka Misra, Byron C. Wallace, Junyi Jessy Li

Abstract
The morphological form of a word can often give cues to its meaning, but purely relying on these mappings can lead to overgeneralization in high-stakes domains. In the medical domain, for instance, LLMs can confidently reason about fictitious drugs from their affixes alone (e.g., wugcillin ) and generate plausible-looking clinical content. We present a behavioral and mechanistic study of LLM ‘‘affix heuristics’’ in pharmacology. Using fictitious drug names built from real affixes, we show that affix signals alone elicit class-level pharmacological responses. We introduce a framework for identifying whether a model’s drug semantics are driven mainly by the affix, the stem, or the drug name as a whole. Applied across 653 drugs, our framework reveals that models often induce drug meaning primarily through affix cues, yet rarely explicitly indicate this reliance, and sometimes incorrectly conflate properties among affix-sharing drugs. Activation patching across models further localizes this behavior to early-mid layers. These findings show that morphological shortcuts pose a subtle but measurable risk to safety 1 1 1 GitHub project: https://github.com/KaijieMo-kj/Morphological-Shortcuts .

Introduction

Humans naturally use morphological cues (prefixes, suffixes, and stems) to infer the meaning of unfamiliar words
Tamminen et al. 2015
;
Solaja and Crepaldi 2024
;
Dawson et al. 2021
.
For example, even without prior knowledge of “
ampicillin
”, humans may infer from the suffix “
-cillin
” that it is likely a medication, and medically trained individuals may further recognize it as likely an antibiotic. Recent work shows that LLMs, too, generalize from morphological structure
Hofmann et al. 2025
;
Weller-Di Marco and Fraser 2024
;
Anh et al. 2024
.
Surface form is suggestive but does not always align with factual meaning. As shown in Figure
1
, while LLMs have mastered surface form, they may lack this calibration: under next-token prediction, an affix such as "
-cillin
" can on its own predict subsequent pharmacological content, and such morphology-driven extrapolation may be expressed with the same confidence as memorized fact. This raises the possibility of a morphological shortcut, in which predictions are driven by morphology rather than specific factual knowledge.

Figure 1:

Example of morphology-driven inference. Humans may cautiously infer that
dimicillin
resembles an antibiotic due to the suffix “
-cillin
”, while LLMs may produce similarly confident continuations for both real and fictitious drugs. We systematically quantify this behavior at both the behavioral and mechanistic levels.

This issue is particularly salient in medicine, where morphological patterns are highly systematic and there are inherent safety risks.
Unlike ordinary vocabulary, many drug-name affixes are standardized by the WHO under the INN system to signal pharmacological or chemical class
World Health Organization 2017
, plausibly exposing LLMs to unusually consistent form–class correspondences during training. Yet we understand little about morphological generalization under such conditions.
We therefore investigate the extent to which LLMs rely on these affix signals, whether such reliance can be systematically quantified and mechanistically localized, and what potential risks it may pose in medical settings.
Our analysis has three parts:

(1) Behavioral analysis:
We first evaluate the degree to which LLMs infer properties from affixes in medical settings. Using fictitious drug names to isolate pure morphological signals from drug-specific knowledge, we show that affix cues alone are sufficient to elicit category-level pharmacological responses. For example, a fictitious drug
dimicillin
(nonce stem “
dimi-
” + real affix “
-cillin
”) is treated as a
-cillin
antibiotic and described as treating bacterial infections.

(2) Diagnostic framework:

Next, we introduce metrics for
where the drug semantics come from
(affix, stem, or the whole word).
We reveal that for a large set of drugs, models induce their meaning mainly through affix cues, yet they rarely explicitly indicate so.
Further, we show that they sometimes conflate pharmacological facts between drugs that share the same affix (e.g., attributing the properties of one drug with a
-cillin
affix to another.)

(3) Mechanistic analysis:
To understand how such behavior arise internally, we conduct mechanistic analyses on
OLMo-3-7B-Instruct
and
Qwen2.5-7b-Instruct
, which show clear affix-driven behavior and offers transparent access to model internals. We localize affix-driven heuristics to the early-middle layers and identify distributed causal directions which mediate pharmacological category recognition from morphological cues.

Together, these findings show that affix-driven shortcuts are systematic to how LLMs process drug names. They often emerge from morphology alone, generalize across diverse clinical inputs, and shape representations of real drugs.
Moreover, LLMs do not typically reveal when outputs have relied on morphological inferences. They are also prone to within-class confusion between same-affix drugs. Affix reliance can be localized and causally controlled using model internals.
This work offers tools for auditing medical LLM behavior and a mechanistic target for future safety interventions.

2
Related Work

A growing body of work asks whether LLM outputs reflect knowledge of the world or statistically licensed continuations of surface form
Bender and Koller 2020
;
Mitchell and Krakauer 2023
;
Quattrociocchi et al. 2025
. The two are difficult to separate at the output level: a plausible, confident model output may rest on parametric knowledge
or on extrapolation from surface cues
Geirhos et al. 2020
;
Du et al. 2023
;
Yuan et al. 2024
. This raises a specific question: which features of the input language can, on their own, license confident factual-sounding output?

Morphological structure offers a particularly informative window onto this problem. Recent work shows that LLMs can generalize derivational patterns beyond memorized words and infer semantic regularities from recurring affixes
Anh et al. 2024
;
Pantelidou et al. 2026
. Human studies similarly suggest that affixes provide strong form–meaning cues during novel word learning
Tamminen et al. 2015
;
Dawson et al. 2021
;
Solaja and Crepaldi 2024
. However, prior work has largely focused on linguistic productivity or semantic inference for nonce forms, rather than the real-world consequences of such inferences in safety-sensitive domains. We address this gap through a controlled evaluation of medical affixes.

The medical domain provides an especially clean instance: drug nomenclature is engineered, with standardized affixes designed to signal pharmacological class
World Health Organization 2017
;
Serafini et al. 2021
. It is also a domain where mistaking fluent continuation for factual knowledge carries concrete consequences. Prior work has documented hallucination risks in clinical decision support
Kim et al. 2025
, including adversarial prompts that elicit confident elaboration of false clinical details
Omar et al. 2025
, and benchmarks show that both general and medical LLMs produce misleading outputs while failing to recognize them as hallucinations
Agarwal et al. 2024
. Notably, LLMs have even mistaken Pokémon names in medication lists for real drugs
Henry et al. 2026
. Collectively, these studies establish hallucination as a significant concern but do not examine whether
existing, standard morphological structures in pharmacy can systematically lead to undesirable overgeneralization.

4
Behavioral Analysis: Affix Signals Generalize to Fictitious Drug Names

When presented with a fictitious drug name ending with a real pharmacological affix, do models infer drug-class properties as if the “drug” were real?
We evaluate model behavior in two QA settings.

4.1
Experimental Design

We evaluate affix-driven behavior using two tasks:

(1) Multiple-choice (MC)

Given the prompt “What is {drug_name}?”, models choose among:
(i) the correct affix-implied drug-class definition,
(ii) two randomly sampled definitions from unrelated real drugs, and
(iii) a “non-existent drug” option. The option order is randomly shuffled for each data point (see details in Appendix
B
).

(2) Open-ended user queries (OE).

For each affix, we generate natural user-style questions from real drugs (examples in Appendix
B
) and replace the original drug name with a fictitious drug, yielding queries such as “Is {drug_name} effective for treating {condition}?”. In Section
4.2
, we further experiment on queries without any clinical context.

Evaluation.

For MC, we measure whether the model selects the affix-consistent definition. For OE, we use
Claude-Sonnet-4.5
as an LLM judge to determine whether the response treats the drug as real (Appendix
B
). “Yes” indicates treating the drug as real, while “No” indicates not treating it as a real medication. On 300 manually annotated cross-model cases, the judge achieved 92.3 accuracy and 89.3 F1 (Appendix
C
).

Models

We evaluate nine LLMs.

4.2
Results

Figure
2
shows that models perform well on real drug names, while fake drug names consistently shift responses toward affix-consistent definitions or higher Yes rates relative to the Nonce condition, demonstrating that affix cues alone can trigger class-level pharmacological predictions. This effect scales with model size in the multiple-choice task, with larger models (
Llama-3.1-70B
,
Gemini
variants) showing stronger generalization than 7B models. In OE, the pattern is more polarized: specialized medical models, particularly
Meditron3-7B
and
HuatuoGPT
, exhibit the strongest overgeneralization, often treating Real, Fake, and even Nonce inputs similarly as valid medications. Across both tasks, Nonce words elicit more conservative responses than affix-plausible Fake drugs, confirming that valid affix structure is a key driver of fictitious drug acceptance. CoT prompting generally increases model conservativeness (Appendix
F
), reducing affix-consistent predictions and affirmative efficacy judgments for fictitious drugs, while also slightly suppressing recognition of real drugs.

Per-Affix Analysis.

To analyze the stability of affix-driven generalization, we measure each affix across 10 nonce-stem variants on
OLMo-3-7B-Instruct
and
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
(see Appendix
G
). Some affixes overgeneralize consistently across both tasks, causing models to treat fictitious drug names as real drugs from the corresponding class.
Many of the strongest cases involve inhibitor classes (e.g., PARP, EGFR, KRAS, and MEK), whose affixes encode highly predictive semantics.
As a result, models infer drug categories, fabricate therapeutic effects, and generate plausible explanations for entirely fictitious drug names.

Additional Settings

We further evaluate two additional settings: (1) bare questions without clinical context (e.g., “What is {drug name}?”) and (2) fictitious drugs formed by attaching real pharmacological affixes to everyday words (e.g.,
tablecillin
). Across open- and closed-source, medical-tuned, and reasoning-oriented models, affix-driven generalization still appears (Appendix
E
). As shown in App. Figures
6
and
7
, models tend to treat these fictitious terms as real medications and fabricate pharmacological properties, even without explicit medical context. Pharmacological affixes can also override strong semantic priors from familiar objects (e.g., reinterpreting
tablecillin
as a plausible drug). Across both settings,
Meditron3-7B
shows the strongest affix-driven overgeneralization, while
OLMo-3-7B-Think
remains the most conservative.

5
A Diagnostic Framework for Affix Dependence in Real Drugs

To analyze affix-driven shortcuts in real drugs, we
design metrics that tell apart to what extent drug knowledge comes from affix, stem, or both.
This reveals systematic shifts in knowledge sources across tasks, links different knowledge regimes to downstream behavior, and connects holistic representations with training exposure.

5.1
Framework Design

Figure 3:

The framework used to compute the Affix, Stem, and Holistic scores.
(a) For each real drug condition
\mathbf{RR}
, we construct matched perturbation conditions
\mathbf{NR}^{(i)}
,
\mathbf{RN}^{(i)}
, and
\mathbf{NN}^{(i)}
variants and obtain model probabilities across multiple variants.
(b) These probabilities are used to compute the total signal, affix signal, and stem signal.
(c) Final normalized scores (AffixScore, StemScore, HolisticScore) are obtained by normalizing the corresponding component signals by the total signal.

Our framework asks whether a model’s behavior on drug names is mainly driven by morphological cues (e.g., suffixes such as “-cillin”) or by drug-specific representations tied to the full name. For each real drug, we construct a 2
\times
2 perturbation over stem and affix:
RR
is the original drug (Real stem + Real affix);
NR
replaces the stem with a nonce form while preserving the affix;
RN
preserves the stem but replaces the affix; and
NN
replaces both.

For each condition
X\in\{
RR
,
NR
,
RN
,
NN
\}
, let
p_{X}
denote the model’s predicted probability over the candidate answers. For MC,
p_{X}=P(\text{definition})
is computed via a softmax over the logits of the four answer options. For OE,
p_{X}=P(\text{Yes})
is computed via a softmax over the logits of the two labels {“Yes”, “No”} (Appendix
H
details token processing),
which we use as a proxy for whether the model treats the drug as real.

As depicted in Figure
3
, for a given real drug
RR
, we generate 10 nonce variants with length-controlled substitutions, creating a set of 4-tuples
\{\textbf{RR},\textbf{NR}^{(i)},\textbf{RN}^{(i)},\textbf{NN}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{10}
. Each variant
i
is evaluated under the same shuffled option order in the MC task, while the option order may differ across variants. Although
RR
itself is fixed across
i
, its probability
p_{\textbf{RR}}^{(i)}
in the MC task depends on the accompanying answer options.
In contrast, the
p_{\textbf{RR}}^{(i)}
in OE is constant across
i
.

Total signal.

We define the total signal for a given
RR
drug as the average difference between the matched
RR
and
NN
variants:

\displaystyle\mathrm{TotalSignal}(\textbf{RR})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(p_{\textbf{RR}}^{(i)}-p_{\textbf{NN}}^{(i)}\right).

Intuitively, this quantity measures how much the model distinguishes real drug-name semantics from nonce semantics. We treat it as the total amount of drug-related semantics captured by the model.

Affix Score.

This measures how affix information
alone
influences drug-name semantics.

\displaystyle\mathrm{AffixScore}(\textbf{RR})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{p_{\textbf{NR}}^{(i)}-p_{\textbf{NN}}^{(i)}}{p_{\textbf{RR}}^{(i)}-p_{\textbf{NN}}^{(i)}}

(1)

The numerator measures the influence of introducing only the real affix on drug-name semantic (which is the AffixSignal in Figure
3
). The normalized score therefore measures the affix-only signal relative to the total signal.

Stem Score.

This measures how stem information alone influences drug-name semantics.

\displaystyle\mathrm{StemScore}(\textbf{RR})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{p_{\textbf{RN}}^{(i)}-p_{\textbf{NN}}^{(i)}}{p_{\textbf{RR}}^{(i)}-p_{\textbf{NN}}^{(i)}}

(2)

The numerator measures the influence of introducing only the real stem on drug-name semantics (which is the StemSignal in Figure
3
). The normalized score therefore measures the stem-only signal relative to the total signal.

Holistic Score.

This measures the influence of the interaction between affix and stem on drug-name semantics. Intuitively, it captures knowledge that emerges only when the correct stem and affix co-occur in the original drug name. Let
r_{i}=p_{\textbf{RR}^{(i)}}-p_{\textbf{NR}}^{(i)}-p_{\textbf{RN}}^{(i)}+p_{\textbf{NN}}^{(i)}
, we define:

\displaystyle\mathrm{HolisticScore}(\textbf{RR})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{r_{i}}{p_{\textbf{RR}}^{(i)}-p_{\textbf{NN}}^{(i)}}

(3)

The numerator
r_{i}
measures the relative influence of the stem–affix synergy in the
2\times 2
factorial design: semantic signal that emerges only when both the stem and affix are correct (HolisticSignal in Figure
3
).
The normalized score measures the relative contribution of the stem–affix interaction to the total signal.

Diagnostic Categories.

We categorize drugs into five diagnostic types.
Drugs are classified as
No signal
when the total signal (probability difference between
RR
and
NN
) is not reliably above 0.1 across variants, i.e.,
\mathrm{mean}-\mathrm{std}\leq 0.1
, indicating behavior that is not reliably distinguishable from fully nonce strings. For the remaining drugs, we compare the three diagnostic scores. Let
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
denote the largest and second-largest scores. If
s_{1}-s_{2}\geq 0.1
, the drug is assigned to the dominant category as
Holistic
,
Affix-dependent
, or
Stem-dependent
; otherwise, it is labeled
Mixed signal
.
These thresholds are chosen to provide conservative but non-degenerate operating points (Appendix
I
).

Analysis setup.

We focus on
OLMo-3-7B-Instruct
,
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
, and
Meditron3-Qwen2.5-7B
, which exhibit clear affix effects across both tasks among similarly sized open-source models.
OLMo
enables analysis of training-corpus exposure, and the
Meditron
–
Qwen
comparison isolates the effect of medical fine-tuning. Grouping drugs by diagnostic category, we analyze definition selection under different signal types (MC) and whether models treat the drug as a real medication during drug–condition reasoning (OE). We report
Prob Acc
, the proportion of variants where the definition option receives the highest probability, and
Gen Acc
, the final generated behavior from Section
4

Category distributions.

For both models, a large proportion of real drugs fall into the
No signal
category; this indicates that many representative real drugs from the AMA list elicit little stable total signal beyond fully randomized forms. At the same time,
Affix-dependent
drugs are also prevalent, suggesting that model behavior for many real drugs is driven more by pharmacological morphology than by holistic drug-specific knowledge. In contrast,
Stem-dependent
and
Mixed signal
cases remain relatively rare across both tasks and models.

Holistic knowledge and behavioral reliability.

Across models and tasks, drugs with dominant holistic signals show the most reliable behavior, especially in the open-ended setting, suggesting that drug-specific knowledge provides a more robust basis for downstream reasoning than morphology alone. In contrast,
No signal
drugs consistently perform worse, indicating that models often treat them similarly to fully randomized forms (
Meditron3-7B
is an exception in OE task, where it tends to treat nearly all strings as real drugs; Figure
2
). Consistent with this pattern, our NR analysis (Appendix
K
) shows that holistic signals largely collapse into the
No signal
category after stem perturbation, whereas affix-driven signals are mostly preserved, suggesting that holistic recognition depends on the complete drug form.

Task-dependent morphology utilization.

The two tasks yield different knowledge distributions. In the MC task, many drugs are
Affix-dependent
, indicating frequent reliance on affix semantics for pharmacological categorization. In the OE task, affix-dependent drugs decrease substantially, while
Holistic
drugs become more prevalent. This shift suggests that models flexibly rely on different drug signals across tasks: affix-level morphology is often sufficient for category selection, whereas drug–condition reasoning depends more heavily on holistic drug-specific knowledge.

Training exposure and scores correlation.

Holistic signals positively correlate with real-drug corpus frequency in the
OLMo-3-7B-Instruct
training corpus across both tasks (Table
3
), and remain significant after controlling for overall
p_{\textbf{RR}}
. In contrast, morphological signals show negative correlations with frequency: affix-based signals in MC and stem-based signals in OE. This pattern mirrors the task-dependent use of morphology observed earlier: MC relies more on affix cues for drug-class identification, whereas OE depends more on drug-specific information, where stems may function as identity markers within an affix-defined class. Overall, the results suggest that the model may rely more on holistic representations for frequently observed drugs, whereas lower-frequency drugs are more likely to elicit reliance on morphological cues.

7
Conclusion

Whether LLM outputs reflect knowledge or extrapolation from surface form is, in safety-sensitive domains, not just a conceptual question. First, we show that morphological shortcuts are real and consequential in the medical domain: affix cues alone are sufficient to elicit category-level pharmacological responses, even for fictitious drugs. Second, our diagnostic framework reveals that much of what looks like pharmacological knowledge is morphological extrapolation: across 653 drugs and multiple models, drug-related outputs are often driven by affix structure rather than drug-specific facts, this reliance is rarely surfaced in the output itself, with fact confusion sometimes appearing between drugs sharing the same affix. Third, mechanistic analysis localizes this behavior to causally manipulable directions in the early-middle layers. These findings reveal morphological shortcuts as a subtle but quantifiable risk to LLM reliability, one that is invisible at the output level but measurable, mechanically locatable.

Limitations

A limitation of this study is that our diagnostic framework cannot be applied to models that do not expose token probabilities or logits, excluding many advanced closed-source models from our analysis of model reliance on different drug information sources. As a result, our analysis is primarily restricted to models with sufficient transparency. Additionally, due to computational constraints, our mechanistic interpretability experiments were conducted only on two 7B-scale models, which may limit the generalizability of the observed internal behaviors and representational patterns. Future work should investigate whether similar findings extend to larger-scale models and a broader set of architectures.

G.1
Per-Affix Generalization Setup

To quantify affix-driven generalization, we define an affix-preference margin for each task. For the multiple-choice task, the margin measures how strongly the model prefers the affix-consistent definition over all competing options:

\displaystyle m_{\mathrm{MC}}^{(i)}=

\displaystyle p_{i}(\mathrm{definition})

\displaystyle-\max\bigl\{p_{i}(\mathrm{non\text{-}existent}),\,p_{i}(\mathrm{distractor})\bigr\}

where probabilities are obtained by softmax-normalizing the logits over the four answer options. For the open-ended task, the margin is the probability assigned to the
Yes
label, obtained by softmax-normalizing the Yes/No logits:

m_{\mathrm{OE}}^{(i)}=p_{i}(\mathrm{Yes})

where a higher
p_{i}(\mathrm{Yes})
indicates that the model treats the fictitious drug name as a real medication, serving as a proxy for successful affix-driven generalization.

For each affix, we generate
N
nonce stem variants and compute the mean and standard deviation of the margin across variants:

\bar{m}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}m^{(i)},\qquad\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(m^{(i)}-\bar{m})^{2}}

where
N=10
denotes the number of nonce stem variants for each affix,
\bar{m}
reflects the overall strength of affix-driven generalization, and
\sigma
reflects its consistency across stems.

Let
\tau
denote the task-specific decision threshold (
\tau=0
for MC and
\tau=0.5
for OE). We categorize affixes as exhibiting
stable generalization
when
\bar{m}-\sigma>\tau
,
partial generalization
when
\bar{m}-\sigma\leq\tau
and
\bar{m}+\sigma\geq\tau
, and
weak generalization
when
\bar{m}+\sigma<\tau
.

Finally, We further define a joint category by combining the MC and OE labels for each affix: (i)
Stable generalization
, when the affix consistently exhibits stable generalization; (ii)
MC stable only
, when only MC is stable; (iii)
OE stable only
, when only OE is stable; (iv)
Partial generalization
, indicating cases that do not generalize consistently; and (v)
Weak generalization
, indicating cases that consistently fail to generalize.

Appendix J
Partial Spearman Correlation Analysis

To test whether occurrence–score correlations simply reflect overall recognition confidence, we compute partial Spearman correlations controlling for
p_{RR}
. Since frequently observed drugs tend to receive higher
p_{RR}
values, and decomposition scores are also partly derived from RR/NR/RN/NN probabilities, raw correlations between occurrence frequency and decomposition scores may arise from shared dependence on overall recognizability rather than differences in knowledge structure. We therefore estimate
\mathrm{partial\_spearman}(\mathrm{occurrence},\mathrm{score}\mid p_{RR})
at the drug level. Here, occurrence corresponds to RR corpus frequency, score refers to the AffixScore, StemScore, or HolisticScore, and
p_{RR}
is the semantic probability assigned to the correct RR response, averaged across the 10 morphological variants associated with each drug.
No signal
drugs are excluded. Following standard Spearman-style partial correlation procedures, all variables are first rank-transformed, after which ranked occurrence counts and ranked scores are separately residualized against ranked
p_{RR}
using ordinary least squares. The final partial correlation is computed as the Spearman correlation between the two residuals. This analysis is intended as a control analysis rather than a causal identification strategy, testing whether the observed occurrence–score correlations can be explained primarily by overall recognition strength or familiarity effects.

To study how class-level drug representations emerge during training, we construct two prototype groups: 50

s=\overline{\log p}_{\text{matched}}-\overline{\log p}_{\text{control}}.

Positive
s
indicates that the drug name functions as a cue for the correct pharmacological class.

We track
s
across intermediate checkpoints of
OLMo-3-7B-Instruct
to examine when holistic and affix-level signals emerge during training.

Appendix P
Distributed Alignment Search and Steering Experiments

P.1
Distributed Alignment Search

DAS training pairs.

Following prior work on learned subspace interventions
(
Geiger et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2024
)
, we train a low-rank direction that controls affix-driven behavior, where the probability distribution over answer options provides a precise measure of whether the model selects the affix-consistent semantics. We restrict to NR examples and compute normalized probabilities over the four MC options. We retain only examples whose top-option margin exceeds
\tau=0.2
, yielding two groups:
definition-preferring
(
x_{b}
), where the affix-consistent definition is the top choice, and
non-existent-preferring
(
x_{s}
), where the non-existent option is. Training pairs
(x_{b},x_{s})
are constructed within the same affix group and prompt context (Section
4.1
), and the swapped pair
(x_{s},x_{b})
is included in every batch.

DAS intervention objective.

Let
h_{b}
and
h_{s}
denote the residual-stream representations at layer
\ell
for the final token of the drug-name span. Given learned low-rank matrix
U
, the intervened representation is:

h_{\mathrm{int}}=h_{b}+UU^{\top}(h_{s}-h_{b}).

We optimize

DAS Training Setup

We apply DAS to OLMo-3-7B-Instruct using low-rank dimension 1, learning a single intervention direction per layer. DAS is trained independently for all 32 transformer layers for 10 epochs using Adam with learning rate
1\times 10^{-3}
and linear warmup (10% of total steps). Each epoch dynamically resamples 1024 intervention pairs, corresponding to 256 optimization steps. We use batch size 4 with gradient accumulation 4, yielding an effective batch size of 16. The resulting matrix captures an affix-related decision direction that controls whether the model generalizes from the affix or rejects the drug as non-existent.

Layer selection and stability.

We train DAS at layers 7–10 using four random seeds with different stem-based train/validation/test splits. Performance is evaluated using Top-1 accuracy and KL reduction. Layer 8 achieves the best average performance with relatively low variance, indicating stable learning of an effective intervention direction across splits.

Steering setup.

It is a non-benzodiazepine sedative-hypnotic and is available in immediate-release and extended-release formulations (e.g., Zolpimistine for extended).

Effectiveness

- For Severe, Short-term Insomnia:

Zolpidem is effective in helping people fall asleep quickly when taken as directed. It works by enhancing the effects of GABA in the brain, which promotes sleep.

- Evidence Base:

Numerous clinical studies support its use for short-term insomnia. It is often recommended when insomnia is due to a one-time event or temporary sleep disruption.

- Not for Chronic Use:

Zolpidem is not recommended for chronic insomnia (lasting more than a few weeks). Long-term use can lead to tolerance, dependence, and withdrawal symptoms, and it does not address the underlying causes of insomnia.

Appropriateness

Zolpidem’s appropriateness depends on several factors:

1. Severity and Duration of Insomnia

- Appropriate if: Sleep is needed for a few days or a short period (e.g., due to travel, illness, stress).

- Not appropriate if: Insomnia is chronic or you have difficulty maintaining sleep over weeks or months.

2. Underlying Causes of Insomnia

- Zolpidem is not a treatment for the causes of insomnia, such as sleep apnea, anxiety, depression, or poor sleep hygiene. It addresses the symptom (sleep onset) but not the problem.

- Appropriate if: Insomnia is clearly due to inability to fall asleep and is not related to an underlying medical or psychiatric condition.

- Not appropriate if: Your insomnia is associated with daytime impairment, cognitive issues, or if you have a history of substance dependence (especially with benzodiazepines or sedative use).

3. Side Effects and Risks

- Risks include: Drowsiness, impaired memory and cognitive function (especially with higher doses or extended-release forms), increased risk of accidents, and dependence with long-term use.

- Not suitable for: People with certain medical conditions (e.g., respiratory depression, alcohol abuse)
