ABSTRACT:
This project investigated the mechanisms of new particle formation (NPF) and growth in the Houston
urban atmosphere as part of the DOE Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) TRacking Aerosol
Convection interactions ExpeRiment (TRACER). The project had three main objectives: (I) perform
measurements of ambient gas-phase precursors and size-resolved ultrafine particle composition, as well as
climate-relevant aerosol properties during the TRACER field campaign; (II) conduct measurements of
size-resolved particle growth using the novel Comprehensive Aerosol Growth and Evolution (CAGE)
chamber; and (III) develop process-level models describing NPF in Houston to improve mechanistic
understanding of particle formation in urban environments. Following a productive pre-campaign in
Riverside, CA (April-May 2022), the main TRACER-UFI (Ultrafine aerosol Formation and Impacts) field
campaign was conducted in Houston, Texas from July 1 to August 31, 2022. The measurement suite
included Chemical Ionization Mass Spectrometry for gas-phase precursors, Thermal Desorption Chemical
Ionization Mass Spectrometry for size-resolved nanoparticle composition, CAGE chamber measurements
of particle growth, and auxiliary measurements of particle size distributions. Post-campaign analysis
combined detailed chemical characterization with advanced process-level modeling. Key scientific
advances included: (1) identification of dominant species and mechanisms driving NPF in the Houston
atmosphere; (2) development of an enhanced 1D column model (SOM-TOMAS) incorporating
urban-relevant processes including volatile chemical products (VCPs), particle-phase viscosity effects,
and recent nucleation schemes; (3) demonstration that stationary (Eulerian) observations can be deceptive
when interpreting air parcel evolution (Lagrangian perspective), with broad implications for how the
community interprets NPF observations worldwide; and (4) improved mechanistic understanding of urban
NPF processes that informs future development of aerosol representations in regional and global climate
models.

Introduction
Atmospheric aerosol particles play a critical role in Earth's climate system through their direct interactions
with solar radiation and their indirect effects on cloud properties. A significant fraction of these
climate-relevant particles originate from new particle formation (NPF), the gas-to-particle conversion
process by which molecular clusters nucleate and grow to form nanometer-sized particles. Understanding
the chemical species and mechanisms responsible for NPF is essential for predicting aerosol indirect
effects on climate, which remain one of the largest uncertainties in climate projections. Additionally,
ultrafine particles (those smaller than 100 nm in diameter) have been linked to adverse health outcomes,
making their characterization in urban environments a priority for both climate science and public health.
Urban atmospheres present a particularly complex and poorly understood environment for NPF research.
Cities emit diverse mixtures of potential NPF precursors including sulfur dioxide, nitrogen oxides,
ammonia, amines, and a wide range of volatile organic compounds. In recent years, volatile chemical
products (VCPs), such as personal care products, paints, coatings, and cleaning agents, have emerged as
increasingly important contributors to urban organic aerosol budgets as transportation-related emissions
have declined. Houston, Texas, represents a unique urban setting due to its large petrochemical industrial
complex, high biogenic emissions from surrounding vegetation, warm and humid climate, and frequent
convective activity. These characteristics make Houston an ideal location for studying the interplay
between anthropogenic and natural emissions in driving NPF.
This project was conducted as part of the DOE Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) TRacking
Aerosol Convection interactions ExpeRiment (TRACER), which took place in Houston from October
2021 through September 2022. Our sub-campaign, designated TRACER-UFI (Ultrafine aerosol
Formation and Impacts), focused specifically on understanding NPF mechanisms through comprehensive
chemical characterization and innovative measurement approaches. The project brought together three
research groups: the Smith group at UC Irvine for detailed gas-phase and nanoparticle composition
measurements, the Collins group at UC Riverside for measurements using the novel Comprehensive
Aerosol Growth and Evolution (CAGE) chamber, and the Pierce group at Colorado State University for
process-level and global-scale modeling. This final report summarizes the major findings and impacts of
this collaborative effort conducted from 2020 through 2025.

Project Objectives
This project had three main objectives:

Objective I: Perform measurements of ambient gas-phase precursors and size-resolved ultrafine particle
composition during the TRACER-UFI field campaign. Leveraging other relevant observations, we sought
to understand the dominant species that participate in NPF and their potential impacts on clouds and
climate.

Objective II: Perform measurements of size-resolved growth of particles nucleated from exposure of
ambient air to sunlight during the TRACER-UFI field campaign using the CAGE chamber. CAGE also
allows measurements of gas-particle partitioning of ambient vapors onto size-selected nanoparticles of
known composition.

Objective III: Build a process-level model that describes NPF in the Houston atmosphere, comparing
model predictions of size-resolved ultrafine particle growth with measurements in order to test our
mechanistic understanding of particle formation in this region. Use these insights to improve
representation of urban NPF processes in larger-scale models.

Pre-Campaign Preparations and Instrument Development (2020-2022)
Prior to and following the decision to postpone the TRACER intensive from summer 2021 to summer
2022 (due to the COVID-19 pandemic), the project team used the additional time productively to enhance
instrument capabilities and develop measurement protocols.
CAGE Chamber Development (UC Riverside). The UCR group worked to prepare the CAGE chamber
and aerosol generation and sampling equipment for deployment in Houston. The delay provided an
opportunity to make design and operational changes expected to improve performance and reliability.
Several challenges with components required modest changes in the chamber design. The chamber was
operated outdoors at UCR with connections to instruments inside the laboratory through a wall port,
allowing the team to work through procedures and identify areas for improvement. This setup also
enabled testing and characterization of auxiliary aerosol instrumentation that would be used with the
chamber during the TRACER-UFI campaign. Software was developed and tested to control and
coordinate the chamber and auxiliary equipment, with the goal of fully automating operation in advance
of the campaigns. Graduate student Xuanlin Du received extensive training on chamber operation and use
of supporting instruments.
Instrument Preparation and Training (UC Irvine). The UCI group worked to train graduate student
Jeremy Wakeen in the operation of relevant instruments: Thermal Desorption Chemical Ionization Mass
Spectrometer (TDCIMS) and Chemical Ionization Mass Spectrometer (CIMS). The group worked with
ARM staff to identify infrastructure needed at the sampling site, most importantly the rental of a Mobile
Mini portable office and installation of electrical service for the campaign. The UCI group also
participated in planning activities, including working with other TRACER co-PIs to develop an email
distribution list to facilitate communication during the planning phase.
During this period, the UCI group took advantage of a parallel activity not funded by DOE but closely
related to TRACER goals: measurements of nanoparticle composition in Beijing, China, using TDCIMS
(Li et al. 2021). This experience provided valuable comparative data and operational experience with an
almost identical instrument suite (minus the CAGE chamber) that would be deployed in Houston.
Model Development (Colorado State University). The CSU modeling team made significant advances to
the SOM-TOMAS model, the primary process-level model used in this work. Key model enhancements
relevant to NPF in the Houston urban environment included:
 1.​ Representation of oxidation and secondary organic aerosol (SOA) generation from volatile
 chemical products (VCPs) commonly found in urban atmospheres
 2.​ Explicit representation of particle-phase viscosity/diffusivity affecting condensation/evaporation
        (He et al. 2021)
 3.​ Implementation of recent organic and inorganic nucleation schemes (He et al. 2022)
 4.​ Treatment of organic oligomerization (He et al. 2021)
Graduate student Sam O'Donnell focused initial efforts on HI-SCALE data analysis as part of another
ASR-funded project, developing skills and methodologies that would be applied to TRACER data.
Pre-TRACER Campaign (April-May 2022). In April-May 2022, a critical TRACER-UFI pre-campaign
was conducted at the Center for Environmental Research and Technology (CE-CERT), UC Riverside.
This campaign successfully tested measurement protocols and data analysis procedures, addressing key
questions about the integration of CAGE chamber operations with TDCIMS measurements.

Specific questions resolved during the pre-campaign included:
 ●​ Whether seed aerosol concentrations generated in CAGE would be sufficient for successful
 TDCIMS measurements
 ●​ The exact protocol for combining CAGE measurements with ambient air measurements

 ●​ Whether sample line losses of trace gases and aerosols would compromise measurement quality

This pre-campaign proved invaluable in ensuring the success of the subsequent Houston field campaign
and provided preliminary data for the modeling group to develop and test their methods. All three groups
(UCI, UCR, and CSU) participated in planning and analysis activities, establishing the collaborative
framework that would be used during TRACER.

TRACER-UFI Field Campaign (July-August 2022)
The main TRACER-UFI field campaign was conducted from July 1 through August 31, 2022, at the main
site in La Porte, Texas. The campaign was extended to eight weeks (from the originally planned six
weeks) to better align with other TRACER participant groups and maximize data collection during
periods of potential NPF activity.
Measurement Suite. The comprehensive measurement suite deployed during TRACER-UFI included:
Gas-phase measurements (UCI): Chemical Ionization Mass Spectrometry (CIMS) for sulfuric acid,
amines, and other trace gas precursors that may participate in NPF and early particle growth.
Particle composition measurements (UCI): Thermal Desorption Chemical Ionization Mass Spectrometry
(TDCIMS) for size-resolved ultrafine particle composition, providing detailed chemical characterization
of particles in the 10-100 nm size range.
CAGE chamber measurements (UCR): Enables measurements of particles with known initial size and
composition that are exposed to filtered, ambient air under exposure to sunlight.
Auxiliary measurements: Particle size distributions measured using Scanning Mobility Particle Sizers
(SMPS) and other aerosol instrumentation to characterize the full particle size distribution and track NPF
events.
Measurement Protocol. The UCI team operated the TDCIMS and CIMS instruments to measure the
composition of gas-phase precursors and size-resolved nanoparticles during both ambient sampling
periods and CAGE-processed sampling periods. The UCR team operated the CAGE chamber,
coordinating with the UCI team to alternate between ambient air sampling and CAGE-processed air
sampling. This approach allowed direct comparison of particle growth and composition in ambient air
versus particles that had been captured in CAGE and allowed to evolve under more controlled conditions.
Infrastructure and Safety. Infrastructure at the TRACER site included a Mobile Mini portable office
housing the UCI instruments, dedicated electrical service for power-intensive mass spectrometry
instruments, and placement of the CAGE chamber with appropriate sampling lines and controls. All team
members completed required safety training for TRACER operations and went through lab-specific
orientation and training. Safety protocols included operation of an O₂ alarm in case of nitrogen leaks
inside the laboratory spaces.

Data Analysis and Modeling (2022-2024)
Following the field campaign, extensive analysis of the TRACER-UFI dataset was conducted in
combination with process-level modeling efforts. This phase of the project produced the most significant
scientific findings and publications.

Houston NPF Mechanisms. The primary manuscript from this work (O'Donnell et al., submitted to
Aerosol Science & Technology, 2025) presents a comprehensive analysis titled "Houston, We have
Particles: Probing the Potential Drivers of New Particle Formation and Growth During TRACER." This
study combines the detailed chemical measurements from UCI and UCR with the process-level modeling
from CSU to identify the key species and mechanisms responsible for NPF in Houston. The analysis
integrates:
 ●​ Size-resolved nanoparticle composition from TDCIMS

 ●​ Gas-phase precursor measurements from TI-CIMS

 ●​ Particle growth observations from CAGE chamber experiments

 ●​ Process-level box modeling using SOM-TOMAS

 ●​ Meteorological and air quality data from the TRACER site and regional monitoring networks

This comprehensive dataset allowed the team to evaluate the relative importance of different NPF
pathways and growth mechanisms in the Houston atmosphere, providing insights into how urban
emissions of sulfur dioxide, ammonia, amines, and organic compounds combine to drive particle
formation.
TDCIMS Data Collection and Analysis. Ultrafine particles were charged and collected in 5-minute
intervals and subsequently thermally desorbed to volatilize their constituent species. The vaporized
particles were then analyzed using a chemical ionization high-resolution time-of-flight mass spectrometer
with O2- serving as the reagent ion. TDCIMS-measured ions were normalized by collected particle mass
and classified into subgroups of oxygen-, nitrogen-, and sulfur-containing organic compounds, as well as
organic and inorganic sulfur fragments produced during vaporization. Across both ambient air and
CAGE-processed air, sulfur-containing species of either organic or inorganic origin were consistently the
dominant contributors to NPF, indicating the central role of particulate sulfate formation in this
environment.
These sulfur-dominant trends are further supported by collaborators in the manuscript by Jensen et al.
(2025, Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society) entitled “Studying Aerosol, Clouds, and Air
Quality in the Coastal Urban Environment of Southeastern Texas.”
Lagrangian vs. Eulerian Perspectives on NPF. A particularly innovative aspect of the data analysis
addressed a fundamental question in atmospheric NPF research: how do stationary (Eulerian)
observations relate to the actual evolution of air parcels (Lagrangian perspective)? The manuscript by
O'Donnell et al. (2025, Journal of Geophysical Research) entitled "Going Off Grid: A Comparative Study
of the Lagrangian and Eulerian Perspectives of New Particle Formation Events" tackled this important
methodological question.

 5

This work demonstrated that interpreting chemical and physical processes from stationary observations
can be deceptive when air masses are advecting past the measurement site. Apparent particle growth rates
and formation rates measured at a fixed location may not accurately represent the actual evolution of
particles within an air parcel as it moves through space. The study developed methods to account for these
effects and provided guidance for the community on when Eulerian interpretations may be misleading.
This work has important implications for how NPF observations at fixed ground sites worldwide should
be interpreted. This research was highlighted in multiple invited presentations, including keynote
addresses at international conferences and invited seminars at leading research institutions.

Implications for Larger-Scale Modeling
The process-level understanding developed through TRACER-UFI provides important insights that can
inform future development of aerosol representations in regional and global climate models. The detailed
chemical characterization of NPF precursors and products in Houston, combined with the process-level
modeling framework developed for this project, establishes a foundation for improved representation of
urban NPF in larger-scale models.
Related work by project collaborators demonstrated broader applications of these insights: Luu et al.
(2025, ACS Earth and Space Chemistry) used GEOS-Chem to perform global simulations of phase state
and equilibration timescales of secondary organic aerosols, incorporating understanding of particle-phase
processes relevant to NPF and growth. Additionally, Shrivastava et al. (2024, Journal of Geophysical
Research) demonstrated that anthropogenic extremely low volatility organic compounds (ELVOCs)
govern the growth of molecular clusters over the Southern Great Plains, connecting urban emissions to
broader regional air quality and climate impacts. Shrivastava et al. (2024, One Earth) examined how
different particle size modes formed within vegetation fire smoke affect convective clouds in the Amazon,
demonstrating how ultrafine particles from combustion sources can affect cloud properties.
Currently, the Pierce group is performing 3D aerosol modelling focused on the TRACER campaign,
combining with TRACER, TRACER-UFI, and TRACER-MAP measurements. Preliminary findings from
this work are: (1) NPF in Houston is more often urban/local-scale rather than regional scale. NPF initiates
in and around Houston and growth occurs as the air advects away in the urban plume. This “local NPF
with growth during advection” explains the large number of “apple”-type events observed in Houston. (2)
Airmasses that pass over by the W.A. Parish coal-fired power plant (located just SW of Houston) prior to
reaching the TRACER observations explain the majority of sustained (>1-2 hour) NPF events observed
during TRACER.
While direct integration of Houston-specific NPF mechanisms into operational global climate models was
not achieved during this project period, the mechanistic insights and modeling frameworks developed
provide a pathway for such integration in future work.

Scientific and Societal Impact
Scientific Impact. This project advanced fundamental understanding of new particle formation and
growth processes in urban atmospheres through several key contributions:
Mechanistic Insights: The comprehensive chemical characterization of gas-phase precursors and
size-resolved nanoparticle composition, combined with CAGE chamber measurements and process-level
modeling, provided unprecedented insight into the species and mechanisms driving ultrafine particle
formation in Houston. These findings advance scientific understanding of how urban emissions lead to
particle formation.
Methodological Innovation: The Lagrangian vs. Eulerian analysis (O'Donnell et al. 2025, JGR) has
important implications for how the atmospheric science community interprets NPF observations at fixed
measurement sites worldwide. This work demonstrates that commonly used analysis approaches may lead
to incorrect conclusions about formation and growth mechanisms when air masses are advecting past
observation sites. The community will need to reconsider interpretation of many previous NPF studies in
light of these findings.
CAGE Chamber Validation: This project demonstrated the successful deployment and operation of the
CAGE chamber in a field setting combined with comprehensive chemical characterization. This
methodology can be applied in future field campaigns to understand NPF in other important atmospheric
environments.
Model Development: The enhanced SOM-TOMAS model as applied to the CAGE chamber, with
improved representations of urban-relevant processes, provides a tool for investigating NPF in urban
environments and for extrapolating observations to other locations and conditions.
Climate Impact. By improving mechanistic understanding of NPF and incorporating these processes into
models, this project contributes to reducing uncertainties in aerosol indirect effects on climate. The work
demonstrates how urban emissions of precursor species lead to formation of particles that can grow to
climate-relevant sizes (cloud condensation nuclei), thereby affecting cloud properties and Earth's radiation
balance. The process-level understanding developed through this project provides a foundation for
improved representation of urban NPF in regional and global climate models.
Health Impact. Better predictions of the physical and chemical properties of sub-100 nm particles,
particularly in urban environments like Houston, may be critical to understanding their health impacts.
This project provides detailed characterization of nanoparticle composition in an urban atmosphere with
significant petrochemical industrial activity, contributing to understanding of human exposure to ultrafine
particles in cities.
Methodological and Technological Impact. The successful integration of CAGE chamber measurements
with detailed chemical characterization using TDCIMS and CIMS represents a methodological advance
for studying NPF. The pre-campaign testing approach, which resolved key operational questions before
the main field deployment, provides a model for future complex multi-institutional field campaigns. The
automated chamber control systems developed during this project may benefit future studies using similar
approaches.
