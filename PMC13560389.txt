Integrated landscape of salivary metagenome and multi-biofluid metabolome characterizes a microbial-metabolic axis in upper gastrointestinal cancer progression
Authors: Shuai Zhang, Nan Zhang, Xiaofeng Zhang, Yanxiu Liu, Yukun Feng, Junling Xiang, Jun Zhang, Hengmin Ma, Youhua Lu, Tao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUpper gastrointestinal cancer (UGIC) imposes a major global health burden, yet the stage-specific molecular changes along the microbial-metabolic axis remain limited understood. We aimed to delineate this molecular landscape across UGIC progression and evaluate its potential as non-invasive methods for precision screening.ResultsDerived from a multi-center population-based UGIC screening program, we enrolled 420 individuals, stratified into normal, low-grade intraepithelial neoplasia (LGIN), high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia (HGIN), and UGIC (n = 105 per group). Integrated salivary metagenomics and paired salivary/plasma metabolomics were performed to capture local and systemic dysregulation. We uncovered distinct stage-specific divergence during UGIC progression: profound remodeling of the salivary microbiota (104 differential species) and salivary metabolomics (80 differential metabolites) initiated early at the LGIN stage, whereas plasma metabolic dysregulation (40 differential metabolites) peaked significantly later at the HGIN stage. Integrative analysis revealed salivary microbiota related more closely with salivary metabolome than plasma metabolome. Moreover, statistical evidence suggested that dysbiotic salivary microbiota was associated with altered lysine- and tryptophan-related catabolic pathways converging on Acetyl-CoA-related metabolic nodes, supporting a potential metabolic mechanism in precancerous lesions. Finally, the discriminative model integrating metagenomic and metabolomic markers demonstrated promising diagnostic performance in distinguishing these precancerous lesions (LGIN: area under the curve [AUC] = 0.83; HGIN: AUC = 0.77) and UGIC (AUC = 0.76) from normal.ConclusionThis study characterizes a stage-specific microbial-metabolic axis that facilitates the comprehensive understanding of UGIC pathogenesis. These multi-biofluid signatures offer a promising non-invasive triage strategy for detecting precancerous lesions and optimizing endoscopic resource allocation. Background

Upper gastrointestinal cancer (UGIC), primarily comprising esophageal and gastric cancer, remains a formidable global health burden that accounted for 11.4% of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. The pathogenesis of UGIC typically follows a defined histological progression, evolving from normal mucosa through low-grade intraepithelial neoplasia (LGIN) and high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia (HGIN) before culminating in invasive carcinoma [2, 3]. Crucially, these precancerous stages represent a vital therapeutic window where curative intervention can effectively reverse disease progression. The current gold-standard diagnostic method is upper gastrointestinal endoscopy with biopsy, which enables a thorough examination of both the esophagus and stomach, facilitating the detection and removal of precancerous lesions and effectively reducing the risk of UGIC and its associated mortality [4, 5]. However, the invasive nature of endoscopy, coupled with substantial resource requirements, hinders its large-scale implementation for population-based screening. Consequently, there is an urgent need to decipher the early molecular landscapes during this progression and identify promising non-invasive detection methods for early detection.

Emerging evidence underscores the pivotal role of the microbial-metabolic axis in gastrointestinal carcinogenesis [6–8]. Given the anatomical continuity of the digestive tract, the upper gastrointestinal mucosa is particularly susceptible to oral microbial colonization. Previous studies have demonstrated that the oral microbial profile is significantly altered in patients with established esophageal or gastric cancer, often characterized by an enrichment of periodontal pathogens such as Porphyromonas, Fusobacterium, and Prevotella, alongside a depletion of commensals like Streptococcus and Neisseria [9–12]. While oral dysbiosis has been hypothesized as a potential early-warning system for upper gastrointestinal lesions, taxonomic profiling alone mainly identifies which microbial taxa are altered and does not fully capture the functional consequences of these compositional shifts, particularly their effects on host-microbe metabolic interactions [13, 14]. The microbiome can act as a metabolically active community, producing bioactive metabolites, such as amino acids, short-chain fatty acids and polyamines that modulate the local microenvironment and exert systemic effects through circulation [15, 16]. Such dysbiosis-induced metabolic reprogramming can fuel inflammation, suppress anti-tumor immunity, and provide energy substrates for neoplastic cells [6, 17]. Therefore, integrating metagenomic taxonomy with metabolic functional readouts is essential to capture the complex microbiome-metabolome interactions underlying UGIC progression.

Despite recent progress, critical knowledge gaps persist regarding the early alteration of UGIC. Existing studies have predominantly compared healthy controls with advanced cancer cases, thereby obscuring the molecular shifts occurring during the critical transition phases of upper gastrointestinal precancerous lesions. Moreover, these studies have been restricted to single-omics approaches or single biological fluids, failing to capture the continuous alignment or divergence between local microbial dysbiosis (e.g., in saliva) and systemic metabolic dysregulation (e.g., in plasma) [9, 18–20]. To address these limitations, we conducted a comprehensive multi-center study encompassing the entire spectrum of UGIC progression. By enrolling matched upper gastrointestinal screening participants stratified into normal, LGIN, HGIN, and UGIC groups, we performed an integrated analysis of salivary metagenomics and paired salivary/plasma metabolomics. This study aims to delineate stage-specific molecular landscapes within the “saliva-blood” axis, identifying the critical inflection points for local versus systemic dysregulation. By uncovering a potential microbial-metabolic axis in which dysbiotic bacteria may contribute to Acetyl-CoA-related metabolic reprogramming in precancerous lesions, we provide insights into carcinogenesis and deliver promising multi-omics signatures for precise, non-invasive early detection.

Methods

Study subjects and sample collection

This multi-center study was derived from a population-based UGIC screening program initiated across 13 hospitals in Shandong Province, China. Between March and December 2024, residents aged 40 to 69 years from cluster-sampled villages were invited for screening. Eligible participants, defined as permanent residents with no prior history of malignancy, mental disorders, or contraindications for endoscopy, completed a baseline questionnaire regarding potential UGIC risk factors.

After an overnight fast of at least 8 h, all eligible participants underwent high-definition upper gastrointestinal endoscopy performed by experienced endoscopists, with comprehensive examination of the esophagus, cardia, stomach, and descending duodenum. To enhance the visualization of subtle mucosal changes, a standardized chromoendoscopy protocol was implemented: 1.2% Lugol’s iodine solution and 0.2% indigo carmine, or narrow band imaging, were applied to delineate suspicious lesions. Biopsy collection followed a combined strategy of targeted and pre-scheduled systematic sampling. In addition to biopsies taken from any endoscopically suspicious lesions, systematic biopsies were pre-scheduled from predefined standard sites to ensure accurate staging. All biopsy specimens were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, paraffin-embedded, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Pathological diagnoses were independently adjudicated by two senior gastrointestinal pathologists who were blinded to the clinical and omics information of the participants. When biopsies from sampling sites differed in degree of pathologic severity, the higher-grade diagnosis was used for the final pathological diagnosis [4].

To characterize the molecular landscape across the progressive stages of carcinogenesis, we focused on the “normal mucosa-precancerous lesion-cancer” sequence. Participants were stratified into four groups: normal (no evidence of atrophy, intestinal metaplasia, or dysplasia); LGIN (low-grade squamous or glandular intraepithelial neoplasia, characterized by mild to moderate cellular atypia); HGIN (high-grade squamous or glandular intraepithelial neoplasia, exhibiting marked cytologic atypia and complex architectural abnormalities); and UGIC (squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma).

Saliva and blood samples were collected at least 2 h before endoscopic procedures to prevent procedural interference. All participating hospitals followed a unified standard operating procedure for sample collection, processing, storage, and transportation to minimize pre-analytical variation and external contamination. Fasting venous blood was collected in EDTA anticoagulant tubes and centrifuged at 1400 × g for 15 min at 4 °C within 40 min to isolate plasma. Saliva samples were obtained using a sterile swab collection tube (SARSTEDT, Germany) after mouth rinsing with purified water 30 min prior to collection, followed by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 3 min. All samples were aliquoted and stored at −80 °C within 4 h of collection. No other processes were performed on biospecimens before metagenomics and metabolomics sequencing. Information related to sample collection, including collection time, physical condition, and recent medication use such as antibiotic exposure, was also recorded through the questionnaire.

A total of 20,544 participants were enrolled in our screening program. 4119 individuals spanning the specific UGIC progression stages (108 UGIC, 177 HGIN, 691 LGIN and 3143 normal controls) were identified who possessed a complete baseline questionnaire and biospecimens. To minimize confounding bias, the UGIC group served as the reference for 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) with the HGIN, LGIN, and normal groups based on age, sex, and body mass index (BMI), resulting in final analytical subjects of 420 participants (105 per group). The detailed enrollment and selection process is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Salivary DNA extraction and metagenomic sequencing

Total DNA was extracted from saliva samples using the QIAamp Fast DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration and integrity were assessed by a NanoDrop2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively. DNA was fragmented by S220 Focused-ultrasonicators (Covaris, USA) and cleaned up by Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter Co., USA). Then the libraries were constructed using TruSeq Nano DNA LT Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The Metagenome sequencing and analysis were conducted by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

The libraries were sequenced on DNBSEQ-T7 platform (MGI Tech Co., Ltd.). Reads were trimmed and filtered using fastp (v.0.20.1). Host pollution control was needed if the DNA was extracted from host-related environment [21]. The post-filtered pair-end reads were aligned against the host genome using bbmap (v.38.93–0) and the aligned reads were discarded [22]. Metagenome assembly was performed using MEGAHIT (v.1.2.9) after getting valid reads [23]. ORF prediction of assembled contigs (longer than 500 nt) using prodigal (v.2.6.3) was performed and translated into amino acid sequences [24]. The non-redundant gene sets were built for all predicted genes using MMSeqs2 (v.13.45111) [25]. The clustering parameters were 95% identity and 90% coverage. The longest gene was selected as representative sequence of each gene set. Clean reads of each sample were aligned against the non-redundant gene set (95% identity) use salmon (v.1.8.0), and the abundant information of the gene in the corresponding sample was counted [26]. Using DIAMOND (v.2.1.9) software, the representative sequences of the non-redundant gene set (amino acid sequences) were annotated by aligning against the various databases [27]. To comprehensively capture both canonical oral taxa and non-classical or transient species, taxonomic profiling was resolved using the comprehensive NR database. The abundance of the species-level taxa was calculated using the corresponding abundance of the genes.

Metabolite extraction and untargeted LC–MS analysis

Samples stored at −80 °C were thawed on ice. For saliva and plasma, 100 μL aliquots were mixed with 300 μL and 400 μL, respectively, of the methanol–acetonitrile protein precipitant (V/V = 2:1) containing the mixed internal standard (4 μg/mL). The extract was ultrasonicated in an ice-water bath for 10 min, allowed to stand at −40 °C for 2 h, and followed by 20 min centrifugation at 13,000 rpm and 4 °C. One hundred fifty microliters of the supernatant was loaded into liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS) injection vials were stored at − 80 °C until LC–MS analysis. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by mixing aliquots from all samples.

The metabolomic data analysis was performed by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). An ACQUITY UPLC I-Class plus (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) fitted with Q-Exactive mass spectrometer equipped with heated electrospray ionization (ESI) source (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to analyze the metabolic profiling in both ESI positive and ESI negative ion modes. An ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) was employed in both positive and negative modes. The binary gradient elution system consisted of (A) water (containing 0.1% formic acid, V/V) and (B) acetonitrile and separation was achieved using the following gradient: 0 min, 5% B; 2 min, 5% B; 4 min, 30% B; 8 min, 50% B; 10 min, 80% B; 14 min, 100% B; 15 min, 100% B; 15.1 min, 5% and 16 min, 5% B. The flow rate was 0.35 mL/min and column temperature was 45 °C. All the samples were kept at 10 °C during the analysis. The injection volume was 5 μL.

The mass range was from mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) 70 to 1050. The resolution was set at 60,000 for the full MS scans and 15,000 for HCD MS/MS scans. The Collision energy was set at 10, 20, and 40 eV. The mass spectrometer operated as follows: spray voltage, 3800 V (+) and 3200 V (−); sheath gas flow rate, 35 arbitrary units; auxiliary gas flow rate, 8 arbitrary units; capillary temperature, 320 °C; Aux gas heater temperature, 350 °C; S-lens RF level, 50.

The original LC–MS data were processed by the software Progenesis QI V3.0 (Nonlinear, Dynamics, Newcastle, UK) for baseline filtering, peak identification, integral, retention time correction, peak alignment, and normalization. Main parameters of 5 ppm precursor tolerance, 10 ppm product tolerance, and 5% product ion threshold were applied. Compound identification was based on precise m/z, secondary fragments, isotopic distribution, and retention time using The Human Metabolome Database (HMDB 4.0), Lipidmaps (v.2.3), Metlin, and LuMet-Animal 3.0 self-built databases [28–30]. The extracted data were then further processed by removing any peaks with a missing value (ion intensity = 0) in more than 50% in groups, replacing zero values with half of the minimum value, performing log2 processing on the data after zero value padding, and by screening according to the qualitative results of the compound. Compounds with resulting scores below 36 (out of 80) points were also deemed to be inaccurate and removed. A data matrix was combined from the positive and negative ion data.

Metagenomic and metabolomic profiles preprocessing and quality control

For the metagenomic data, the read counts of bacterial species and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) orthology genes (KO genes) were converted into relative abundances. To minimize the influence of low-abundance background noise and sporadic potential contaminants, a subsequent prevalence- and abundance-based filtering step was implemented to retain exclusively species and KO genes with a relative abundance exceeding 0.01% in at least 10% of the samples. In order to account for the compositional nature of metagenomic data and to normalize the skewed distribution of microbial features, centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation was performed using the geometric mean of relative abundances of these features as the CLR denominator [31]. Before the transformation, pseudo-abundances of 1e -11 and 1e -9 were added respectively to the species and KO profiles based on the minimum relative abundance. The amalgamation of these processes resulted in the generation of abundance matrices encompassing 758 bacterial species-level taxa and 8213 KO genes.

For the metabolomics data, a multi-step quality control and preprocessing pipeline was applied. Initially, metabolic ion peaks exhibiting a missing rate > 50% in any sample group were excluded. Subsequently, the relative standard deviation (RSD) of each metabolic ion was computed across QC samples, and ions with an RSD < 0.3 were retained to ensure data reproducibility. Furthermore, a total of 919 Level 1 metabolites in saliva and 934 Level 1 metabolites in plasma were retained for subsequent analysis. According to the definition established by the Metabolomics Standards Initiative, Level 1 refers to metabolites that have been unambiguously identified by matching precise precursor mass-to-charge ratios, retention times, and tandem mass-to-mass spectra with a standard metabolite library [32]. For each metabolite, missing values were imputed using the K-nearest neighbors algorithm, and principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to evaluate the reproducibility and stability of the metabolomics data. Finally, the data were log10-transformed prior to downstream statistical analyses.

Identification of microbial and metabolic markers across UGIC progression

Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were utilized to examine the disparities in microbial abundances and metabolic profiles among upper gastrointestinal cancer, precancerous lesion, and normal control groups, respectively. The proportion of variance in metagenomic and metabolomic composition that can be explained by phenotypes, demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, oral health status, and disease history was calculated by permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) using Bray–Curtis distance matrices with 999 permutations following a previous study [33]. All variables significantly associated with variation were identified as confounding factors using a Benjamini–Hochberg corrected false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of < 0.05.

The microbial community composition was characterized at the phylum and genus levels, and α-diversity was measured using Shannon and Simpson indices. Associations between microbial features and phenotypes were evaluated using linear discriminant analysis (LDA) and microbiome multivariable associations with linear model (MaAsLin2) [34], which provides a highly flexible integrated framework for microbiome analysis. Significant differential species and KO genes were identified with an LDA effect size > 2 and a MaAsLin2 P-value < 0.05. Group differences in dominant phyla, dominant genera, and α-diversity were also evaluated using MaAsLin2. Similarly, metabolites were considered as significant differential features if they fulfilled the following criteria: (1) a variable importance in projection (VIP) score > 1 using the PLS-DA and (2) a P-value < 0.05 using the generalized linear model (GLM) analysis. To minimize potential disturbance, all differential analyses were adjusted for significant variables identified in the PERMANOVA analysis above, including screening hospital and other confounders.

Integrating metagenomics and metabolomics data

We employed a complementary analytical framework to integrate metagenomics and metabolomics datasets: (1) Bray–Curtis distance matrices for saliva microbiota and Aitchison distance matrices for saliva/plasma metabolites were calculated based on the identified differential features above. The PCoA dimensionality reduction analysis was then performed on the distance matrices. Subsequently, the overall correspondence between metagenomics and metabolomics distance matrices was evaluated using Procrustes analysis, which involves the examination of the shape distribution of data mappings and the iterative pursuit of a canonical shape, to compare the consistency of multivariate datasets [35]. The significance of rotation consistency was obtained through the execution of 999 permutations. Mantel test was also applied in order to verify the overall correspondence [36]. (2) Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (Lasso) penalized regression was employed to identify specific paired associations between differential salivary microbial species and differential saliva/plasma metabolites. Assuming that microbial perturbations influence metabolite expression, Lasso regression models were constructed with the species abundance matrix as predictors and each metabolite as the response variable. Leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) was used to determine the tuning parameter lambda. The regularization projection method of de-sparsified Lasso was then used to obtain the coefficients of each species predictor related to the given metabolite, as well as the 95% confidence interval and P-value [37]. Due to Lasso models being sensitive to minor variations in predictors, the stability selection analysis was repeated 100 times using a random subset comprising half of the sample size [38]. Finally, we identified robust species-metabolite paired associations with an FDR < 0.1 by the Lasso model, as well as those present in at least 60% of the fitted models by stability selection.

MetOrigin and KEGG pathway functional analysis

Retrospective traceability analysis of differential saliva and plasma metabolites was performed using MetOrigin 2.0, a comprehensive bioinformatics tool that enables accurate discrimination of metabolite origins through integrated database searching, rapid identification of microbial metabolites, and characterization of their metabolic functions [39]. In addition, the “ko00001.keg” hierarchy was retrieved from the KEGG BRITE database in order to investigate metabolic pathway modules associated with salivary microorganisms. The KO genes related to key metabolic pathways were selected based on the predominant classes of differential metabolites, including “amino acid metabolism”, “lipid metabolism”, and “metabolism of other amino acids”.

Random forest discriminative model construction

To identify metagenomic and metabolomic biomarkers capable of distinguishing LGIN, HGIN, and UGIC participants from normal controls, we established random forest models based on salivary species, salivary metabolites, and plasma metabolites. The model construction was conducted in two stages: initially, Lasso regression was applied to select features from the identified differential markers specific to LGIN, HGIN, and UGIC. By determining the lambda value that minimized the mean squared error, variables with non-zero coefficients at that lambda were retained as potential biomarkers. Subsequently, random forest classifiers for LGIN, HGIN, and UGIC were built using conventional epidemiological risk factors, the selected salivary species, salivary metabolites, plasma metabolites, and integrated multi-omics biomarkers. The models were validated using the LOOCV approach. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted based on the predicted probabilities of each sample, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate the discriminatory performance of each model.

Statistical analyses

Depending on the data distribution, Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was used to evaluate differences in quantitative data. Pearson’s chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables. To evaluate whether lesion origin contributed to the observed omics variation and affected the pooling strategy, PERMANOVA analyses were performed among participants with upper gastrointestinal lesions. Anatomical origin was defined as esophageal/gastric lesions, and histological origin was defined as squamous/glandular lesions. In addition, differential analyses were conducted by regrouping participants as normal controls, esophageal lesions, and gastric lesions. The resulting differential features were compared with those identified in the main stage-based analyses to assess the robustness of the microbial and metabolic signatures. Statistical significance was defined as FDR < 0.05 or P-value < 0.05, where appropriate. All statistical analyses were two-sided and conducted using the R platform (version 4.2.0).

Results

Study cohort and participants characteristics

The study population was derived from a large-scale, multi-center upper gastrointestinal cancer screening program conducted across 13 hospitals in Shandong Province, China. Between March and December 2024, a total of 20,544 participants were enrolled and underwent a standardized protocol, including baseline questionnaires, biological sample collection, and rigorous endoscopic examinations. From this screening population, we identified 4119 eligible individuals with complete questionnaire data and biospecimens who represented the histological progression sequence: 3143 normal controls, 691 with LGIN, 177 with HGIN, and 108 with UGIC. All eligible individuals were additionally assessed for antibiotic use within 1 month before biospecimen collection, and none reported recent antibiotic exposure.

To minimize confounding effects and ensure comparability for multi-omics analysis, we implemented 1:1 PSM based on age, sex, and BMI. This selection process resulted in a final analytical cohort of 420 participants, stratified into four groups (normal, LGIN, HGIN, and UGIC; n = 105 per group) (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S1). The distribution of participants across the hospitals is detailed in Supplementary Table S1. All biological specimens were processed under a unified standard operating procedure to ensure data consistency. Among the 420 participants, the median age was 62 years (interquartile range [IQR] 57.8–67.0 years), 311 (74.0%) were male, the median BMI was 24.4 kg/m2 (IQR 22.2–26.4 kg/m2), 41.9% of participants were current smokers, and 50.7% were current alcohol drinkers. None had a prior history of malignancy. Compared with the normal group, the overall case group (n = 315) exhibited higher frequencies of high-temperature food consumption, history of endoscopic examination and Helicobacter pylori infection. Conversely, the prevalences of bean products and fried food consumption were lower. Among the 315 upper gastrointestinal cases, lesions were located in the esophagus (63.8%) and stomach (36.2%, including cardia). Of these 105 UGIC patients, 30 were diagnosed with esophageal cancer, while 75 were diagnosed with gastric cancer (including gastric cardia cancers). Global multi-omics perturbations characterize the transition from normal to upper gastrointestinal lesions

Among the 420 participants enrolled in our study, plasma metabolomic data were available for all individuals. Due to the limited volume of saliva samples provided by certain participants, not all saliva samples could be used for both metagenomic and metabolomic analyses. Consequently, salivary metagenomic profiling was successfully performed for 416 participants (104 normal controls, 104 with LGIN, 103 with HGIN, and 105 with UGIC), while salivary metabolomic data were obtained for 371 participants (88 normal controls, 92 with LGIN, 96 with HGIN, and 95 with UGIC). All analyzed samples were derived from the same matched participants, and no samples from additional participants were included. QC assessments revealed a substantial number of shared metagenomic microbial species across the comparison groups (Supplementary Fig. S2A, B). Furthermore, a total of 919 Level 1 metabolites in saliva and 934 Level 1 metabolites in plasma were retained for analysis. Comprehensive data about the identified metabolites can be found in the Supplementary Tables S3 and S4. Metabolomic QC samples exhibited tight clustering near the origin in the PCA plot (Supplementary Fig. S2C, D). These findings collectively attest to the high stability, reproducibility, and reliability of both the metagenomic and metabolomic datasets, confirming their suitability for subsequent downstream analyses.

Dimensionality reduction analyses, employing PCoA and PLS-DA, revealed that participants with composite upper gastrointestinal lesions exhibited distinguishable microbial and metabolic profiles when compared to the normal group (Fig. 1B–D). Given that microbial and metabolic composition is substantially influenced by host factors, we employed PERMANOVA to quantify the contribution of these factors to the microbiota and metabolite composition (Fig. 1E). Collectively, these factors accounted for 23.1%, 26.6%, and 22.6% of the inter-individual variations in the salivary microbiota, salivary metabolites, and plasma metabolites, respectively. Among all examined host and dietary factors, a total of 6, 6, and 8 variables, including several additional demographic, lifestyle, oral health, and disease-history variables, were significantly associated with variation in each respective omics dataset (FDR < 0.05), and these factors were consequently adjusted in subsequent differential analyses. The participating hospital, which served as a composite variable reflecting both regional population heterogeneity and possible pre-analytical variation, exhibited the highest explanatory power for the observed variability. Similar population-based studies in China have also emphasized that geographic or regional factors can substantially affect microbiome profiles, even within a single country or province [40, 41]. The upper gastrointestinal lesion classification, whether defined by a binary-categorical grouping (normal and case) or four-categorical stratification (normal, LGIN, HGIN, and UGIC), was a prominent contributor to the inter-individual differences in both the saliva microbiota (1.4% for binary-categorical and 2.0% for four-categorical) and saliva metabolites (2.6% for binary-categorical and 3.7% for four-categorical). This effect, however, was absent in the plasma metabolome (Supplementary Table S5). This critical divergence underscores the potential utility of saliva samples for stratifying upper gastrointestinal disease progression.

Given the imbalance in anatomical and histological origins across pathological stages, we further quantified the contribution of lesion origin among participants with upper gastrointestinal lesions. Case-only PERMANOVA analyses showed that anatomical and histological origin explained only a limited proportion of omics variation, with R2 values ranging from 0.5 to 0.8% (Supplementary Table S6). Statistically significant associations were observed only in the plasma metabolome, whereas no significant effects were detected in the salivary microbiome or salivary metabolome. These results suggest that lesion origin contributed modestly to the overall omics variation.

We subsequently characterized microbial and metabolic alterations between the case and normal groups. Following adjustment for potential confounders identified by PERMANOVA, a total of 85 salivary species, 126 salivary metabolites, and 22 plasma metabolites exhibited significantly differential abundance. While metagenomic profiling revealed an enrichment of 54 salivary microbial species in the case group relative to the normal group (Fig. 1F), the metabolomic landscape displayed a predominant trend of depletion. Specifically, 124 salivary and 19 plasma metabolites were significantly depleted in the case group, whereas only 2 salivary and 3 plasma metabolites were significantly enriched (Fig. 1G, H). These findings suggest a potential mechanistic link wherein microbial expansion may connect to the consumption of specific host metabolites.

Alteration of the saliva microbial ecosystem initiates at early precancerous stages

To characterize the salivary microbiota associated with upper gastrointestinal cancer and precancerous lesions, we performed a metagenomic multi-group comparative analysis (Fig. 2A). The average composition of salivary bacterial communities at the phylum and genus levels was illustrated in Fig. 2B, C. At the phylum level, Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, and Bacillota constituted the three dominant taxa across the four groups. Relative to the normal group, covariate-adjusted MaAsLin2 analysis showed that Pseudomonadota was significantly enriched in both the LGIN and HGIN groups, whereas Bacteroidota exhibited decreased relative abundance in these precancerous lesions. The most pronounced compositional shifts were observed between the LGIN and normal groups. Conversely, no statistically significant differences were detected between the UGIC and normal groups (Supplementary Fig. S3A). At the genus level, Pseudomonas, Prevotella, and Neisseria predominated across all groups. Consistent with the phylum-level findings, the most substantial disparities in the relative abundance of Pseudomonas and Prevotella were evident between the LGIN and normal groups (Supplementary Fig. S3B). α-diversity analysis of the salivary microbiota at the species level revealed a stage-associated reduction in microbial diversity. After covariate adjustment using MaAsLin2, the Shannon index was not significantly different between lesion groups and the normal group; however, the Simpson index was significantly lower in the LGIN and HGIN groups compared with normal controls (Fig. 2D, E). This stage-specific decline in Simpson index was consistently observed in both esophageal and gastric lesion subgroups, with both anatomical sites exhibiting a significant drop starting at the LGIN stage (Supplementary Fig. S3C, D). PCoA based on salivary microbiota composition revealed significant structural heterogeneity across the four groups (R2 = 0.020, P-value = 0.001, PERMANOVA, Fig. 2F). LDA and MaAsLin2 were further employed to identify significantly different species-level taxa to investigate the characteristic changes in salivary microbiota. Compared to the normal mucosa, a total of 104, 77 and 8 species were significantly altered in LGIN, HGIN, and UGIC, respectively (LDA > 2 and a MaAsLin2 P-value < 0.05, Supplementary Table S7). Consistent with the community composition analysis, a substantial number of differentially abundant bacteria were affiliated with the three dominant phyla (Fig. 2G). Bacterial species within Pseudomonadota were uniformly enriched, and those in Bacillota were consistently depleted. In contrast, Bacteroidota exhibited members that were both enriched (primarily the genus Pedobacter) and depleted (primarily the genus Prevotella). The enriched species belonging to Pseudomonadota and Bacteroidota appeared shared in the LGIN and HGIN, whereas many of the species from Bacillota were stage-specific (Fig. 2H). Specifically, several aerotolerant or facultative anaerobic taxa, including Pseudomonas syringae, Pseudomonas simiae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Pseudomonas fluorescens, Acinetobacter guillouiae, and Acinetobacter baumannii, exhibited higher relative abundance in precancerous lesions, especially at the LGIN stage. Although some of these species are known to be associated with environmental sources and potential contamination, previous oral microbiome studies have detected members of Pseudomonas and Acinetobacter in oral samples, suggesting that they may also represent opportunistic or transient colonizers under altered oral ecological conditions. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously as candidate microbial signatures rather than definitive disease-driving taxa [42–44]. Furthermore, contrary to some studies focusing on late-stage tumors, our salivary metagenomic profiling revealed several typical oral anaerobes, including Prevotella (e.g., Prevotella salivae, Prevotella nigrescens and Prevotella intermedia), Porphyromonas (e.g., Porphyromonas gingivalis) and Peptostreptococcus (e.g., Peptostreptococcus stomatis), were significantly downregulated in the lesion groups compared to normal (Fig. 2G). Collectively, these analyses identified the LGIN stage as a critical inflection point in the ecological succession of the salivary microbiota. The profound loss of diversity and distinct structural reconfiguration observed specifically at this precancerous phase suggest that salivary dysbiosis is an early-onset event possibly associated with the initial transition from normal mucosa to low-grade lesions.

To further assess whether these microbial alterations were linked to anatomical origin, we performed sensitivity differential analyses by regrouping participants as normal controls, esophageal lesions, and gastric lesions. Using the same criteria for differential feature identification, 91 differential species were identified in the site-based analyses, of which 87 had already been detected in the main LGIN/HGIN/UGIC stage-based analyses. Importantly, the directions of change for all overlapping species were fully concordant between the site-based and stage-based analyses (Supplementary Table S8, Supplementary Fig. S3E). These findings suggest that the observed salivary microbial signatures were not solely attributable to esophageal or gastric origin, but reflected shared microbial alterations across upper gastrointestinal lesions.

Differential abundance analysis identified 156 significantly altered salivary metabolites and 82 plasma metabolites (VIP > 1 and GLM P-value < 0.05, Supplementary Tables S9 and S10). A divergence was observed in these two metabolic profiles. Salivary metabolic perturbations were dominated by carboxylic acids and derivatives (62 metabolites), fatty acyls (34 metabolites), and glycerophospholipids (16 metabolites). Reflecting the local microenvironment, the number of differential salivary metabolites peaked early at the LGIN stage (80 metabolites), aligning with the initial microbial shifts (Fig. 3D). Further inspection showed that the carboxylic acids and derivatives largely represented amino acid-, peptide-, and analogue-related metabolites and displayed a predominantly depleted pattern, especially at the LGIN stage. These metabolites included a broad spectrum of dipeptides, tripeptides, and amino acid derivatives containing branched-chain, aromatic, lysine, glutamate/glutamine, and tryptophan residues, suggesting early remodeling of peptide- and amino acid-associated metabolism in the salivary microenvironment. In contrast, the systemic metabolic response in plasma was primarily characterized by fatty acyls (18 metabolites), steroids and steroid derivatives (14 metabolites) and carboxylic acids (11 metabolites). This systemic dysregulation exhibited a delayed trajectory, peaking significantly later at the HGIN stage (40 metabolites) (Fig. 3E). This disparity reinforces the notion that metabolic dysregulation initiates locally in the oral cavity during early carcinogenesis before triggering a detectable systemic response at the high-grade lesion stage.

Sensitivity analyses stratified by anatomical site further supported the robustness of the metabolomic findings. When participants were regrouped as normal controls, esophageal lesions, and gastric lesions, 138 differential salivary metabolites were identified, of which 81 overlapped with those detected in the main stage-based analyses (Supplementary Table S11, Supplementary Fig. S4A, C). Similarly, the site-based sensitivity analyses identified 55 differential plasma metabolites, of which 26 overlapped with the stage-based differential plasma metabolites (Supplementary Table S12, Supplementary Fig. S4B, D). The directions of change for all overlapping metabolites were fully concordant between the site-based and stage-based analyses. Although these analyses do not completely exclude a potential contribution of anatomical origin, they indicate that the principal metabolomic signatures identified during UGIC progression were largely preserved after regrouping by anatomical site.

Subsequently, we employed the fuzzy C-means clustering to identify the dynamic shifts of these metabolites over upper gastrointestinal lesions progressed. In the salivary metabolome, the dominant pattern was a U-shaped trajectory (Clusters 1–3), characterized by a profound depletion of metabolites in the LGIN stage, followed by a recovery or distinct shift in HGIN and UGIC. The predominant components of this cluster including amino acids, peptides, and long-chain fatty acids (e.g., oleic acid, stearic acid, docosahexaenoic acid), suggest a potential consumption phenotype in the precancerous microenvironment (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. S5A). Conversely, the plasma metabolome displayed more complex fluctuations centered around the HGIN stage. An inverse U-shaped pattern (Clusters 1–2) was identified, where metabolite levels transiently increased specifically in HGIN. This cluster was characterized by elevated levels of specific fatty acids including hexadecanedioic acid, 2-hydroxydecanoic acid, 3-hydroxy stearic acid and 9-hydroxy stearic acid. Meanwhile, a U-shaped cluster (Cluster 3) in plasma highlighted the depletion of steroids and steroid derivatives during the HGIN phase (Fig. 3G and Supplementary Fig. S5B). Collectively, these dynamic patterns highlight HGIN as a critical metabolic inflection point, marking the transition from local nutrient depletion to systemic lipid and steroid metabolic reprogramming.

Integrative analysis uncovers closer associations between salivary microbiota and metabolites

Previous studies have identified microbiome-metabolites associations in human upper gastrointestinal disorders, including esophageal squamous cell carcinoma and gastric cancer [18, 20, 45]. To dissect the association between microbial composition and metabolic profile, we implemented a complementary analytical approach using differential multi-omics features for 370 participants with complete salivary metagenomic, salivary metabolomic, and plasma metabolomic data (normal: 88, LGIN: 91, HGIN: 96, UGIC: 95, Supplementary Fig. S6). Procrustes analysis revealed a consistent overall correlation between salivary microbiome and salivary metabolome across all subgroups (P-value = 0.001, Fig 4A). In contrast, the concordance between the salivary microbiome and the plasma metabolome was generally weak, with a significant correlation observed only during the LGIN phase. These findings were validated via Mantel tests.

To decode specific interactions, we constructed Lasso penalized regression models followed by stability selection. The analysis uncovered 155 significant associations among 33 salivary species and 90 salivary metabolites (FDR < 0.1, Supplementary Table S13), with the phyla Bacteroidetes and Bacillota exhibiting the strongest correlations with carboxylic acids and derivatives (Fig. 4B). This aligns with our metabolomic findings, where carboxylic acids were the primary differential salivary metabolites. Both stage-specific and shared networks across different upper gastrointestinal lesions revealed complex and close relationships between salivary microbes and metabolites. In the LGIN stage, Paracidovorax wautersii and Tannerella forsythia showed broad associations with multiple metabolite categories. Notably, as the disease progressed to the HGIN stage, key periodontal pathogens including Porphyromonas gingivalis and Hoylesella marshii, showed positive associations with carboxylic acids, potentially reflecting microbial-metabolite co-variation during the HGIN stage. Peptostreptococcus stomatis demonstrated consistent positive correlations with metabolites across the entire LGIN-HGIN-UGIC progression, highlighting its role as a persistent microbial marker associated with metabolic dysbiosis (Supplementary Fig. S7). In contrast, 23 significant correlations were identified between 16 salivary species and 19 plasma metabolites (Supplementary Table S14). Bacillota has been observed to exhibit associations with various plasma metabolites (Fig. 4C). Intriguingly, Parvimonas micra showed significant positive correlations with multiple diacylglycerols, specifically in the HGIN stage. This suggests that the depletion of P. micra may contribute to the systemic lipid metabolic reprogramming identified in our plasma metabolomic analysis. Fusobacterium nucleatum maintained significant associations with plasma metabolites across all disease stages (Supplementary Fig. S8). We further examined microbial KO gene abundances to corroborate this metabolic activity at the functional potential level. PCoA analysis revealed significant functional differentiation across the groups (Supplementary Fig. S10A, B). Subsequently, pathway modules were generated by modifying KEGG pathway maps relating to amino acid and lipid metabolism. A total of 71 differential KO genes of upper gastrointestinal lesions compared to normal were identified based on MaAsLin2 after adjustment for potential confounders (Supplementary Fig. S10C and Supplementary Table S17). Consistent with the taxonomic shifts, the functional gene landscape exhibited the most obvious reconfiguration occurring at the LGIN stage, confirming that salivary functional dysbiosis is an early-onset event. In amino acid metabolism and lipid metabolism function, most KO genes of several functional modules, specifically fatty acid degradation, valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation, lysine degradation, arginine biosynthesis, tyrosine metabolism, phenylalanine metabolism and tryptophan metabolism were significantly enriched in LGIN and HGIN groups compared to normal controls (Fig. 5C).

To explore metabolic signatures and microbial enzyme-metabolite interactions, we integrated metabolite markers with KO markers identified in LGIN and HGIN groups, leveraging established metabolic reactions documented in the KEGG database. The integrative analysis revealed an inverse abundance pattern suggesting an accelerated catabolic flux into Acetyl-CoA. Specifically, in the lysine degradation pathway (Fig. 5D), the key metabolic substrates D-Lysine and L-Pipecolate were significantly depleted in the lesion groups. Concurrently, the microbial KO genes encoding the necessary catabolic enzymes, including gabT, gabD, GCDH, paaF, fadB, and ACAT, were significantly enriched in the LGIN or HGIN stages. Similarly, KO genes encoding enzymes such as GCDH, paaF, fadB, and ACAT were also enriched in the tryptophan metabolism pathway (Fig. 5D). Together, the depletion of lysine- and tryptophan-related substrates and the enrichment of microbial KO genes involved in these catabolic pathways suggest enhanced microbial-associated amino-acid catabolic potential in LGIN and HGIN, with convergence on Acetyl-CoA-related metabolic nodes. This convergence does not indicate direct microbial release of Acetyl-CoA, but rather points to a potential route by which dysbiotic salivary microbiota may reshape exchangeable metabolic intermediates, such as acetate, other short-chain fatty acids, or related organic acids, that can subsequently enter host Acetyl-CoA metabolism [16, 46–49].

Integrative multi-omics signatures empower accurate early detection of precancerous lesions

Many upper gastrointestinal precancerous lesions can be effectively treated or removed by endoscopic intervention, but endoscopy remains invasive and resource-intensive. We therefore sought to translate our metagenomic and metabolomic findings into non-invasive diagnostic classifiers for endoscopy prioritization. We constructed random forest classification models to discriminate LGIN, HGIN, and UGIC from normal using five feature sets: epidemiological risk factors, salivary microbiota, salivary metabolites, plasma metabolites, and an integrated multi-omics panel.

Notably, the integrated multi-omics model demonstrated promising diagnostic performance for precancerous lesions, achieving the highest AUC for LGIN with 0.83, followed by HGIN (AUC = 0.77) and UGIC (AUC = 0.76), all of which outperformed the individual models in the discrimination of LGIN (AUC 0.59–0.76), HGIN (AUC 0.54–0.74) and UGIC (AUC 0.58–0.69), respectively (Fig. 5E). The performance of the integrated model stems from the complementary nature of local and systemic biomarkers across disease stages. Feature importance analysis further corroborated our biological findings (Supplementary Fig. S11). In the LGIN classification, high-ranking features were mainly saliva metabolites, including Leu-Pro-Ala, Phenylalanyl-Lysine and Serylalanine. These metabolites have previously been identified as potential markers for certain types of cancer, which further demonstrates the crucial role of salivary amino acid metabolites in the LGIN phase. As the disease progresses to the HGIN stage, the contribution of systemic markers becomes more pronounced. The integrated model for HGIN effectively captured this transition, with top-ranking features including plasma metabolites such as 4-oxo-2E-hexadecenoic acid and (22alpha)-hydroxy-cholestanol. This shift aligns with our observation that HGIN represents a critical inflection point for systemic metabolic reprogramming. These results demonstrate that integrating salivary signatures (sensitive to early local dysbiosis) with plasma signatures (sensitive to systemic metabolic reprogramming) is essential for achieving precise, stage-specific diagnosis across the entire spectrum of UGIC progression.

Discussion

In this multi-center population-based study, we delineated the stage-specific molecular landscapes of the microbial-metabolic axis across the complete “Normal-LGIN-HGIN-UGIC” histological spectrum. By providing a high-resolution view of the local and systemic perturbations that characterize UGIC progression, we identified LGIN and HGIN as critical inflection points and observed coordinated changes in amino acid-related metabolites and microbial KO genes converging on Acetyl-CoA-related metabolic pathways in early neoplastic lesions. By establishing multi-biofluid and multi-omics non-invasive diagnostic models, this study offers a potential strategy to bridge the current gap in population-based UGIC screening.

A primary discovery of this study is that the transition from normal mucosa to LGIN represents the defining ecological tipping point for salivary dysbiosis. This early-onset reconfiguration was characterized by a reduction in Simpson diversity and a shift in dominant taxa, particularly the expansion of Pseudomonas and the concomitant depletion of Prevotella. Several Pseudomonas species, including P. fluorescens, P. simiae, and P. aeruginosa, showed increased relative abundance during the LGIN phase. Given that Pseudomonas includes both environmental-associated organisms and opportunistic taxa detected in human-associated microbial communities, this pattern should be interpreted cautiously as a candidate signal of altered salivary ecology. Rather than providing definitive evidence of a causal role in carcinogenesis, the enrichment of these taxa may reflect a disturbed oral microenvironment accompanying early upper gastrointestinal lesion development [50, 51]. Intriguingly, our findings differ from the conventional paradigm in which periodontal red and orange complex pathogens, such as Porphyromonas gingivalis, Tannerella forsythia, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Prevotella intermedia, and Prevotella nigrescens are expected to accumulate progressively during carcinogenesis [52–54]. Instead, we observed a substantial decrease in these obligate anaerobes during the LGIN stage. This inverse phenomenon may be influenced by the metabolic adaptability and biofilm-forming capabilities of Pseudomonas [55]. As facultative anaerobes, the expansion of Pseudomonas species may competitively exclude oxygen-sensitive red and orange complex bacteria by altering the local redox potential or secreting antimicrobial metabolites [56]. Stage-specific tissue remodeling in LGIN may create an environment hostile to obligate anaerobes while providing a metabolic niche for Pseudomonas to thrive [57]. Furthermore, the salivary microbiota in UGIC patients showed less pronounced differences from normal controls compared to those with precancerous lesions, suggesting that the initial transition from normal mucosa to LGIN acts as the primary ecological tipping point for oral dysbiosis. These findings highlight the potential of using specific oral microbial shifts as an early warning system to detect latent precancerous lesions.

By performing metabolomic profiling of saliva and plasma, we revealed a striking spatiotemporal divergence in metabolic reprogramming during UGIC carcinogenesis. Salivary metabolic perturbations, mirroring the observed metagenomic shifts, peaked at the LGIN stage and were characterized by a profound depletion of carboxylic acids and fatty acyls. This local exhaustion of metabolites suggests that the salivary microenvironment is heavily influenced by both the expanding dysbiotic microbiota and the emerging neoplastic cells during the earliest phase of lesion formation. Notably, the plasma metabolome remained relatively stable during the LGIN phase, with systemic dysregulation peaking at the HGIN stage. This indicates that the metabolic signals of progression are initially sequestered within the local oral-gastrointestinal axis before spilling over into the systemic circulation as the disease reaches a critical precancerous threshold [58, 59]. The HGIN stage thus represents a pivotal metabolic inflection point, marking the transition from localized nutrient depletion to systemic lipid and steroid metabolic reprogramming. This staged divergence is consistent with longitudinal metabolic profiles previously observed in gastric cancer progression, where a substantial number of plasma metabolites exhibited marked alterations specifically at the LGIN and HGIN stages [60]. Furthermore, our identification of an inverse U-shaped pattern in plasma aligns with our previous observations in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [61]. Such patterns likely reflect a systemic response to the escalating metabolic burden of high-grade lesions. Collectively, these findings underscore why single-fluid omics often fail to capture the full evolutionary spectrum of carcinogenesis and highlight the necessity of multi-omics sampling to achieve a holistic understanding of UGIC development. Importantly, although esophageal and gastric cancers have distinct etiologies, our additional analyses support the presence of shared microbial-metabolic alterations across different upper gastrointestinal lesion sites. Quantitative PERMANOVA analyses showed that anatomical and histological origins explained only a small fraction of omics variation, despite the imbalanced distribution of esophageal and gastric lesions across pathological stages (Supplementary Table S6). Moreover, sensitivity differential analyses demonstrated that the principal microbial and metabolic signatures identified in the stage-based analyses were largely preserved when lesions were regrouped by anatomical site, with completely concordant directions of change for overlapping markers (Supplementary Fig. S3E, Supplementary Fig. S4C, D). Therefore, pooling upper gastrointestinal lesions enabled us to capture conserved microbial-metabolic signatures related to UGIC progression, rather than site-specific profiles alone.

Our multi-omics integrative analysis reveals that members of the red and orange complexes are not merely taxonomic markers of UGIC progression, but may be functionally linked to the local metabolic microenvironment. During the LGIN stage, the broad associations between Tannerella forsythia and salivary metabolites may reflect the early establishment of microenvironmental dysbiosis. T. forsythia is a red-complex anaerobe with multiple host-interaction and nutrient-acquisition strategies, including sialidase- and protease-mediated degradation of host glycoproteins and proteins, which may provide carbon or amino acid substrates and thereby contribute to local metabolic perturbation [62]. As the disease transitions into the pivotal HGIN stage, core red complex pathogens, particularly Porphyromonas gingivalis, exhibit a significant positive correlation with carboxylic acids. This is biologically plausible because P. gingivalis is an asaccharolytic periodontal pathogen that relies largely on peptides and amino acids as carbon and energy sources, with gingipains and exopeptidases supporting protein degradation and amino acid acquisition [63, 64]. Therefore, its association with carboxylic acids may reflect amino acid fermentation and organic-acid-related metabolic exchange within the dysbiotic mucosal microenvironment, rather than a purely compositional signal. This metabolic coupling may contribute to a nutrient-depleted and inflammatory niche that is unfavorable for normal epithelial homeostasis but permissive for lesion progression [65]. Furthermore, the orange complex member Parvimonas micra specifically correlated with plasma diacylglycerols during the HGIN stage, suggesting that selected oral anaerobes may be linked to systemic lipid remodeling at the high-grade precancerous stage. In contrast to these stage-specific shifts, Peptostreptococcus stomatis and Fusobacterium nucleatum demonstrated a persistent metabolic footprint across the entire LGIN-HGIN-UGIC spectrum. The consistent correlation between P. stomatis and salivary metabolites highlights its role as a foundational pathogen that maintains chronic metabolic instability in the UGI mucosa [66]. Meanwhile, the association of F. nucleatum with plasma metabolites reinforces its status as a core member of the tumor-associated oral microbiota; previous studies have shown that F. nucleatum can promote tumor progression and remodel the tumor-immune microenvironment, supporting the possibility that it sustains a pro-oncogenic systemic milieu through metabolic byproducts, inflammatory signaling, or immune-modulatory effects [67, 68]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the dominant microbial signatures identified here may correlate with metabolomic alterations through distinct but interconnected metabolic capabilities, including protein degradation, amino acid utilization, organic acid exchange, and lipid-associated systemic remodeling.

Combined with microbial KO gene abundances with metabolomic profiles, we discovered significant perturbations in amino acid metabolism during the LGIN and HGIN phases. MetOrigin analysis further demonstrated that tryptophan metabolism and lysine degradation are primarily enriched via host-microbe co-metabolism, emphasizing that these metabolic shifts are not isolated microbial events but reflect a profoundly altered host-microbe functional interplay. We observed an inverse abundance pattern, where the depletion of key metabolic substrates—specifically D-lysine and L-pipecolate—was robustly associated with the enrichment of microbial genes encoding critical catabolic enzymes, such as gabT, GCDH, and ACAT. This convergent evidence points toward a microbial-metabolic axis centered on Acetyl-CoA-related metabolic reprogramming. Mechanistically, we propose a metabolic fueling hypothesis: the dysbiotic salivary microbiota in precancerous stages may be associated with the degradation of lysine- and tryptophan-related substrates, with these catabolic pathways converging on Acetyl-CoA-related metabolic nodes. Rather than implying direct bacterial release of Acetyl-CoA to host cells, this process may involve exchangeable microbial metabolites, such as acetate or other short-chain fatty acids, which can subsequently enter host Acetyl-CoA metabolism. As a pivotal metabolic intermediate, Acetyl-CoA participates in the TCA cycle, lipid synthesis, and protein acetylation—processes closely related to the energetic and biosynthetic demands of proliferating neoplastic cells [69, 70]. This suggests that oral bacteria do not merely coexist with the host but actively remodel the local and systemic metabolic landscapes to provide energy substrates that contribute to UGIC progression. This concept aligns with the emerging paradigm of the microbiome as a metabolically active organ capable of modulating the tumor microenvironment through bioactive metabolites [71]. Collectively, our findings underscore that UGIC progression is a collaborative process, where a dysbiotic microbiome and an altered host environment synergistically orchestrate metabolic reprogramming.

The clinical imperative for characterizing these molecular shifts lies in the optimization of early detection strategies for UGIC. Currently, the invasive nature and high cost of endoscopy preclude its implementation as a primary tool for large-scale population screening. As observed in previous studies, exploring the diagnostic value of a single-omics modality alone has inherent limitations, as taxonomic, metabolic, or systemic profiles each capture only one dimension of the disease-associated molecular perturbations [6, 20, 72]. Taxonomic signatures indicate microbial compositional shifts, whereas metabolomic profiles provide functional readouts of host-microbe metabolic activity. Therefore, their integration can more comprehensively reflect the biological processes underlying UGIC progression. Our findings demonstrate that integrating salivary and plasma biomarkers significantly surpasses the performance of any single-omics modality, offering a prospective non-invasive molecular foundation, particularly for the detection of early precancerous lesions. Notably, our integrated classifier achieved an AUC of 0.83 for identifying LGIN, a critical stage where endoscopic intervention can successfully halt or even reverse disease progression. Indeed, the multi-omics identified in this study exhibit distinct signatures across LGIN, HGIN, and UGIC groups. Feature importance analysis confirmed that the detection of LGIN is primarily linked to local salivary amino acid signatures, whereas the identification of HGIN increasingly necessitates the inclusion of systemic plasma markers. These findings suggest that metabolomic analyses of saliva and blood may be sufficient to construct a simplified screening panel in future translational applications, particularly because metabolites provide direct functional readouts of local and systemic dysregulation. However, metagenomic profiling remains important in the biomarker discovery phase, as it identifies microbial compositional and functional alterations, clarifies the potential microbial sources of metabolic perturbations, and improves the biological interpretability of disease-stage-specific biomarkers. Therefore, rather than being mutually exclusive, metabolomics and metagenomics provide complementary information: metabolomics may support a more practical downstream screening assay, whereas integrated metagenomic-metabolomic profiling offers a more comprehensive framework for deriving and interpreting biomarkers of UGIC progression [6, 73]. These results describe a stage-aware screening paradigm: leveraging local salivary signals to capture the earliest mucosal transitions and systemic plasma signals to track more advanced high-grade lesions. With further validation and feasibility assessment, such a multi-matrix, multi-omics approach may serve as a triage tool within population-based screening programs [74]. By helping identify individuals who may benefit from confirmatory endoscopy, this strategy has the potential to improve resource allocation and the efficiency of UGIC screening. For future population-based screening, the fasting sampling conditions should also be considered. Because all biospecimens were collected after an overnight fast of at least 8 h, as routinely required for upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, the identified microbial and metabolic markers are unlikely to be strongly affected by short-term dietary intake. This is supported by PERMANOVA analysis, in which most dietary habits explained only a small proportion of inter-individual omics variation, generally no more than 1% (Supplementary Table S5). Nevertheless, future applications should initially adopt a similar fasting protocol, and further validation is needed to assess whether these signatures remain robust in non-fasting settings.

Our study possesses several notable strengths, including its multi-center, population-based design. By encompassing the entire spectrum from normal mucosa to invasive cancer, we were able to identify the critical inflection points of local versus systemic dysregulation that are often missed in traditional case–control studies. Furthermore, the representativeness of our findings is strengthened by the consistent stage-specific perturbations observed in both esophageal and gastric cancer subgroups, indicating that the identified microbial-metabolic markers are stable across the entire upper gastrointestinal spectrum. The integration of metagenomic functional potential with untargeted metabolomics provided mechanistic insights into the saliva-blood axis. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the acetyl-CoA-related metabolic mechanism remains hypothesis-generating. Although microbial KO genes and metabolites showed coordinated changes, our data cannot establish causality or define the exact metabolic route, further targeted metabolomics and experimental validation are needed. Second, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to determine the temporal sequence of microbial and metabolic alterations. Longitudinal studies are required to assess whether these signatures precede lesion progression and predict future UGIC risk. The sample size may also be insufficient to detect rare microbial-metabolic interactions. Third, the study was conducted in Shandong Province, China, and the participating hospital explained a notable proportion of omics variation. Although standardized procedures were used and the hospital was adjusted for in differential analyses, residual regional, hospital-level, or pre-analytical effects cannot be fully excluded. External validation in independent populations is therefore needed. Finally, although standardized sampling and prevalence/abundance filtering were applied, negative extraction controls and water blank controls were not included. Potential reagent- or water-derived contamination, particularly for environmental-associated taxa, should therefore be considered and addressed in future studies.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive spatiotemporal map of the salivary-blood microbial-metabolic axis throughout the UGIC spectrum. We demonstrate that local salivary dysbiosis and metabolic depletion are early-onset events initiated at the LGIN stage, while systemic metabolic reprogramming serves as a critical biomarker for the transition to high-grade lesions. Mechanistically, we identify a metabolic fueling hypothesis involving an acetyl-CoA-related metabolic axis, in which dysbiotic oral bacteria may enhance amino-acid catabolic pathways during precancerous stages, potentially influencing the metabolic environment of early neoplastic tissues. Beyond pathobiological insights, these findings may have potential relevance for precision screening. The performance of our integrated multi-omics classifier suggests a possible non-invasive triage approach that could optimize screening efficiency and enhance early diagnosis outcomes in high-risk populations.
