Kernel-based identification of nonlinear port-Hamiltonian systems
Brayan M. Shali, and, Henk J. van Waarde

Abstract
Port-Hamiltonian systems provide a structured framework for modeling physical systems by explicitly capturing their energy storage, dissipation, and exchange. However, deriving such models often requires detailed physical insight and precise knowledge of system parameters, which may not be available in practice. In this paper, we propose a kernel-based framework for the identification of port-Hamiltonian systems from input-state-output data. In contrast to conventional parametric approaches, the maps defining the port-Hamiltonian system are represented in suitably chosen reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces. This leads to an infinite-dimensional optimization problem over the corresponding function spaces. Our main result establishes a representer theorem that reduces this problem to a tractable finite-dimensional one. Since the reduced problem is non-convex, we further provide an algorithm for its solution and prove its convergence.



I
Introduction

Port-Hamiltonian systems provide a unifying framework to model real-world physical systems by capturing their energy storage, dissipation, and exchange in a structured way
[
1
]
. Their modular, energy-based formulation makes them especially powerful for representing interconnected multi-domain systems while guaranteeing consistency with physical laws
[
2
]
. At the core of port-Hamiltonian models lies the property of passivity, which guarantees their stability and enables robust control design through energy shaping and damping injection
[
3
,
4
]
. However, deriving such models often requires deep insight into the system’s physics and precise knowledge of its parameters, which can be challenging in practice. In this context, a data-driven approach can play a complementary role by enabling the estimation of relevant structures and parameters directly from data. We take such an approach in this paper by proposing a framework for identifying nonlinear port-Hamiltonian systems from input-state-output data.

Identification of port-Hamiltonian systems has received considerable attention in recent years. Most contributions in the control community focus on the linear case, e.g.,
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
. The approach in
[
5
]
consists of two steps: an unstructured system is identified from input-output data and subsequently approximated by a port-Hamiltonian system. In
[
6
]
and
[
7
]
, the system is identified directly from frequency-response data, with
[
7
]
additionally accounting for measurement noise. In
[
8
]
, direct identification from input-state-output data is achieved via dynamic mode decomposition
[
10
]
under the assumption that the Hamiltonian is known
a priori
. Finally,
[
9
]
provides a comprehensive treatment of the single-input-single-output case.

In contrast to these works, we consider the identification of
nonlinear
port-Hamiltonian systems and adopt a
kernel-based
approach. Kernel-based methods have emerged as a popular framework for system identification
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
. In this framework, identification is formulated as a function or operator estimation problem in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
[
15
]
. Kernel-based methods are particularly well suited for modeling nonlinear functions due to their expressive power and flexibility
[
16
,
17
]
. Furthermore, although the resulting estimation problems are typically infinite-dimensional, computational tractability is retained due to the celebrated representer theorem, which reduces them to finite-dimensional problems depending only on the data.

While kernel-based methods are well suited for identifying nonlinear models, accurate interpolation of the observed data alone does not guarantee that the resulting models are physically interpretable or accurate outside the observed operating regime. This motivates the incorporation of physical properties and structure into the model and identification procedure. In the kernel-based literature, several works incorporate properties such as stability
[
18
]
, positivity
[
19
,
20
]
, (incremental) dissipativity
[
21
]
, and fading memory
[
22
]
. Related ideas also appear in our previous work
[
23
]
, where nonnegativity of the input-output operator is imposed as a necessary condition for passivity, and in
[
24
]
, which considers kernel-based estimation of the frequency response of a strictly passive linear system.

In this work, we incorporate a port-Hamiltonian structure directly into a kernel-based identification framework. This presents several challenges. First, the Hamiltonian must be identified from data on its gradient rather than from direct function evaluations. Second, the dissipative structure must satisfy a nonnegativity constraint, which is inherently nonlinear and therefore cannot be handled within standard RKHS formulations. To address these challenges, we model the Hamiltonian and input maps in suitable RKHSs, while the interconnection and dissipation structure are modeled in a sum-of-squares fashion
[
25
,
26
]
. Our main contributions are twofold. First, we establish a representer theorem for the resulting infinite-dimensional estimation problem, allowing it to be reduced to a finite-dimensional one. Second, since the reduced problem is nonconvex, we propose a solution algorithm and prove its convergence.

The relevance of the proposed framework extends beyond system identification to control design. Models that accurately reproduce observed data may still fail to capture properties that are important for controller synthesis, which has motivated a growing interest in so-called
identification for control

[
27
]
. A related line of work considers the direct synthesis of controllers from data, thereby bypassing the identification step altogether, see
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
for examples in the kernel-based setting. Although control is not considered explicitly in the present paper, the proposed identification framework produces port-Hamiltonian models that are passive by construction. This enables the use of a broad range of passivity-based control techniques and provides a natural starting point for robust control design. As an example,
[
31
]
employs a kernel-based framework for passive controllers, where passivity guarantees closed-loop stability while the controller parameters are optimized for performance.

Our work is also related to recent developments in physics-informed machine learning
[
32
]
, where learning methods are designed to respect underlying physical structure. In particular, there has been significant interest in incorporating Hamiltonian and port-Hamiltonian structures
[
33
]
. Examples include Gaussian-process models of Hamiltonian systems
[
34
]
and kernel-based approaches for learning Hamiltonian vector fields
[
35
]
. A particularly influential development is the introduction of Hamiltonian neural networks (HNNs)
[
36
]
, which have since been extended to account for external inputs
[
37
]
and dissipation
[
38
]
. Building on these developments, various learning methods for full port-Hamiltonian systems have been proposed in
[
39
,
40
,
41
,
42
]
. In contrast to the present work, these methods rely on finite-dimensional parameterizations, whereas we adopt a kernel-based formulation.

II-A

Hilbert spaces

Given a Hilbert space
\mathcal{W}
, we denote the inner product by
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}
and the induced norm by
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}
. We often omit the subscript when the space is clear from the context. Given another Hilbert space
\mathcal{V}
, we denote the Banach space of bounded linear operators from
\mathcal{W}
to
\mathcal{V}
by
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{V})
and the corresponding operator norm by
\lVert\cdot\rVert
. We use the shorthand notation
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W})=\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W})
. We denote the adjoint of
Q\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{V})
by
Q^{*}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})
. The operator
Q\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W})
is
nonnegative
if
\left\langle Gw,w\right\rangle\geq 0
for all
w\in\mathcal{W}
. We denote the subset of nonnegative operators by
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W})_{+}\subset\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W})
. Given an operator
Q\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W})
, we write
Q\geq 0
to indicate that
Q
is nonnegative and self-adjoint, i.e,
Q=Q^{\ast}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W})_{+}
. In the case where
\mathcal{W}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and, thus,
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W})=\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, this means that
Q\geq 0
if
Q
is symmetric positive semidefinite. Note that an operator
Q\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W})
is nonnegative if and only if
Q+Q^{\ast}\geq 0
. Given self-adjoint operators
A,B\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W})
, we write
A\geq B
or
B\leq A
to indicate that
A-B\geq 0
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{W}
is separable and the vectors
w_{i}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
, form an orthonormal basis. We say that
Q\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W})
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator if its Hilbert-Schmidt norm

\lVert Q\rVert_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lVert Qw_{i}\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{W}}

(1)

is finite. We denote the subset of Hilbert-Schmidt operators by
\mathscr{H}(\mathcal{W})\subset\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W})
, and the subset of of nonnegative Hilbert-Schmidt operators by
\mathscr{H}(\mathcal{W})_{+}\subset\mathscr{H}(\mathcal{W})
.

II-B

Matrix-valued maps and convexity

Consider a differentiable map
A:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
. We denote the partial derivative of
A
with respect to the
i
-th component by
\partial_{i}A:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
. The gradient of
A
is the operator-valued map
\partial A:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{p\times p})
given by

\partial A(x)y=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{i}A(x)y_{i}

(2)

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
y_{i}
is the
i
-th component of
y
. The
linearization
of
A
around
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by the map

x\mapsto A(\bar{x})+\partial A(\bar{x})(x-\bar{x})

(3)

We say that
A
has a
pointwise
property if
A(x)
has this property for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, e.g.,
A
is pointwise symmetric if
A(x)
is symmetric for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A pointwise symmetric
A
is
positive semidefinite convex (psd-convex)
if

A((1-\lambda)x_{1}+\lambda x_{2})\leq(1-\lambda)A(x_{1})+\lambda A(x_{2})

(4)

for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Examples of psd-convex maps include
A(x)=x^{\top}x
and
A(x)=xx^{\top}
. We say that
A
is
psd-concave
if
-A
is psd-convex. Note that affine maps are both psd-convex and psd-concave, the finite sum of psd-convex maps is psd-convex, and the composition of a psd-convex map and an affine map is psd-convex. If
A
is psd-convex, then its linearization around
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an underestimation of
A
, i.e.,

A(\bar{x})+\partial A(\bar{x})(x-\bar{x})\leq A(x)

(5)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, see
[
43
, Lemma 2.2]
. If
p=1
, i.e.,
A
is scalar-valued, then the definition of psd-convexity coincides with the usual definition of convexity. In this case, we say that
A
is
strongly convex
with
convexity parameter

\rho>0
if the map
x\mapsto A(x)-\frac{1}{2}\rho\lVert x\rVert^{2}
is convex. Finally, we note that the definitions above extend to any matrix-valued map
A:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
where
\mathcal{V}
is a finite-dimensional Hilbert space and, thus, isometrically isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In particular, they extend to any
\mathcal{V}
that can be written as the Cartesian product of spaces of the form
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and spaces of the form
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

III
Problem formulation

We consider the problem of identifying a (nonlinear) port-Hamiltonian system from input-state data. In particular, we consider an input-state-output port-Hamiltonian system

\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle=(J(x)-R(x))\partial H(x)+G(x)u,

(6a)

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=G(x)^{\top}\partial H(x),

(6b)

with input
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, state
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and output
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Here,
J:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a pointwise skew-symmetric map that describes the lossless interconnection structure, while
R:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a pointwise symmetric and nonnegative map that describes the dissipative resistive structure. The map
H:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is referred to as the
Hamiltonian
and represents the stored energy in the system, while
G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
is the input (port) map that describes how the external input enters the state dynamics and how the output is paired with the effort variables of the energy-storing elements. It is well-known that port-Hamiltonian systems are cyclo-passive in general and passive if the Hamiltonian
H
is bounded from below, see
[
4
, Section 6.1]
for details.

In the context of this paper, it is useful to reformulate the dynamics (
6a
) as

\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle=-F(x)\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}(x)+G(x)u,

(7)

where
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is pointwise nonnegative. To see that the two formulations are equivalent, note that

F(x)=-J(x)+R(x)

(8)

is nonnegative if
J(x)=-J(x)^{\top}
and
R(x)\geq 0
. Conversely, if
F(x)
is nonnegative, then

\displaystyle J(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(F(x)^{\top}-F(x)\right),

(9)

\displaystyle R(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(F(x)^{\top}+F(x)\right),

(10)

are such that
J(x)^{\top}=-J(x)
and
R(x)\geq 0
.

Now, suppose that we are given a data set

(x_{i},\dot{x}_{i},u_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m},\quad i\in[N],

(11)

consisting of
N\in\mathbb{N}
samples of the state, state derivative and input of an unknown port-Hamiltonian system. At a high-level, we consider the problem of identifying a system of the form (
7
) that fits the data well. In other words, we want to find
H:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and pointwise nonnegative
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that the
data misfit

L(F,H,G)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lVert\dot{x}_{i}+F(x_{i})\partial H(x_{i})-G(x_{i})u_{i}\rVert^{2}

(12)

is small. We formalize what small means by introducing an appropriate cost functional in the next section.

For simplicity of exposition, we assume that
m=1
so that
G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The case where
m>1
follows by considering the components of
u
as separate inputs such that

G(x)u=\sum_{i=1}^{m}G_{i}(x)u^{i},

(13)

where
u^{i}
is the
i
-th component of
u
and
G_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We also note that we can also incorporate data on the output by augmenting the data misfit. In particular, if we also have
N
samples of the output
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
i\in[N]
, then the augmented data misfit contains the additional term

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lVert y_{i}-G(x_{i})^{\top}\partial H(x_{i})\rVert^{2}.

(14)

Further details can be found in Remark
2
and Remark
5
.

IV
Representer theorem

In this section, we provide a partial solution to the problem stated in the last section. We do this by leveraging the theory of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces. First, we formalize the problem statement by formulating an appropriate infinite-dimensional constrained optimization problem. Then, as the main result in this section, we obtain a representer theorem for the latter that reduces it to a finite-dimensional constrained optimization problem. Although finite-dimensional, this problem is nonconvex and, thus, nontrivial to solve. Therefore, the next section is devoted to providing an algorithm to compute a solution.

Our approach to solving the problem leverages the theory of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces. The relevant background on this theory can be found in the appendix. In particular, we model
H:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
as a map from a reproducing kernel Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with kernel
\mathfrak{h}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, and
G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
as a map from a reproducing kernel Hilbert space
\mathcal{G}
with kernel
\mathfrak{g}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. We make no special assumptions on the spaces
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{G}
, or, equivalently, the kernels
\mathfrak{h}
and
\mathfrak{g}
. However, we note that the particular choice of kernels matters as it (partially) determines the
subclass
of port-Hamiltonian systems that are used to fit the data. For example, we can ensure that the identified system is passive by choosing a kernel
\mathfrak{h}
which guarantees that the identified map
H
is bounded from below, see Remark
3
.

In the meantime, note that we cannot naively model a pointwise nonnegative map
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
as an arbitrary member of a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. This is because pointwise nonnegative maps are not preserved under negative scalar multiplication, hence no vector space can consist of only pointwise nonnegative maps. Instead, we take a different approach inspired by the sum-of-squares idea introduced in
[
25
]
how the output is paired with the energy variables, and further developed in
[
23
]
for operator-valued kernels, see Appendix
-C
for details.

To this end, consider a feature map
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathcal{W})
, where
\mathcal{W}
is a Hilbert space, and let
\mathfrak{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the corresponding kernel, i.e.,
\mathfrak{f}(x,y)=\phi(x)^{\ast}\phi(y)
. We model a pointwise nonnegative map
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
as

F=\phi(\cdot)^{\ast}Q\phi(\cdot),

(15)

where
Q\in\mathscr{H}(\mathcal{W})
is nonnegative, that is,
Q\in\mathscr{H}(\mathcal{W})_{+}
. The map
F
is pointwise nonnegative because

\left\langle F(x)\xi,\xi\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}=\left\langle Q\phi(x)\xi,\phi(x)\xi\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}\geq 0

(16)

for all
x,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where we used the fact that
Q
is nonnegative.

Remark 1

The properties of the model classes considered above depend strongly on the choice of kernel. The problem of selecting an appropriate kernel is beyond the scope of this paper. Nevertheless, we briefly recall several notions of universality that characterize the approximation capabilities of the resulting model classes. A kernel (or feature map) is called
universal
if any continuous function can be approximated to arbitrary precision on compact sets by functions from its associated RKHS
[
16
]
. A kernel is called
differentially universal
if any continuously differentiable function and its derivatives can be approximated in the same sense by functions from its associated RKHS and their derivatives
[
44
]
. The Gaussian kernel is a well-known example of a differentially universal kernel. Moreover, the model class (
15
) is universal for pointwise nonnegative continuous maps whenever the feature map is universal, that is, any continuous pointwise nonnegative map can be approximated in the sense above by a map of the form (
15
). This is shown in
[
25
]
for scalar-valued maps, and in
[
26
]
for symmetric-valued maps.

With the models of
F
,
H
and
G
established, our goal is to find
(Q,H,G)\in\mathscr{H}(\mathcal{W})_{+}\times\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{G}
such that the data misfit
L(F,H,G)
is small, where
F
is given by (
15
). More precisely, we consider the regularized least squares problem

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle L(F,H,G)+\alpha\lVert Q\rVert_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}+\beta\lVert H\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+\gamma\lVert G\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}

(17)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle(Q,H,G)\in\mathscr{H}(\mathcal{W})_{+}\times\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{G}\text{ and \eqref{eq:model_F} holds},

where
\alpha>0
,
\beta>0
and
\gamma>0
. Here, the first term promotes small misfit
L(F,H,G)
, i.e., a good fit of the data. The other terms promote small norms
\lVert Q\rVert_{\mathscr{H}}
,
\lVert H\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}
and
\lVert G\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}
, which is interpreted as low complexity of the maps
F
,
H
and
G
, respectively. The parameters
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\gamma
regulate the balance between good fit of the data and model complexity.

Note that (
17
) is an infinite-dimensional constrained optimization problem, which is difficult (if at all possible) to solve directly. Fortunately, the following representer theorem reduces it to a
finite-dimensional
problem and, thus, provides a path to a tractable solution.

Theorem 1

Problem (
17
) admits a minimizer of the form

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\phi(x_{i})M_{ij}\phi(x_{j})^{\ast},\qquad

\displaystyle M_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},

(18a)

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial^{\textrm{s}}\mathfrak{h}(\cdot,x_{i})^{\top}c_{i},\qquad

\displaystyle c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(18b)

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathfrak{g}(\cdot,x_{i})v_{i},\qquad

\displaystyle v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

(18c)

where the block matrix

M=\begin{bmatrix}M_{11}&\cdots&M_{1N}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
M_{N1}&\cdots&M_{NN}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{nN\times nN}

(19)

is nonnegative. Furthermore, the map

F=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\mathfrak{f}(\cdot,x_{i})M_{ij}\mathfrak{f}(\cdot,x_{j})^{\top},

(20)

is such that
F=\phi(\cdot)^{\ast}Q\phi(\cdot)
.

Proof:

Let
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}\subset\mathscr{H}(\mathcal{W})_{+}
be the subset of operators of the form (
18a
), where the block matrix in (
19
) is nonnegative. Similarly, let
\bar{\mathcal{H}}\subset\mathcal{H}
and
\bar{\mathcal{G}}\subset\mathcal{H}
be the subspaces of maps of the form (
18b
) and (
18c
), respectively. Moreover, let the map
c:\mathscr{H}(\mathcal{W})_{+}\times\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be the cost of (
17
), that is,

c(Q,H,G)=L(F,H,G)+\alpha\lVert Q\rVert_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}+\beta\lVert H\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+\gamma\lVert G\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}

where
F=\phi(\cdot)^{\ast}Q\phi(\cdot)
. Let
(Q_{0},H_{0},g_{0})\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}\times\bar{\mathcal{H}}\times\bar{\mathcal{G}}
be arbitrary and let
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
,
\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
and
\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{0}
be the closed and bounded subsets of
Q\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}
,
H\in\bar{\mathcal{H}}
and
G\in\bar{\mathcal{G}}
, respectively, such that
\alpha\lVert Q\rVert_{\mathscr{H}}\leq c_{0}
,
\beta\lVert H\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\leq c_{0}
and
\gamma\lVert G\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}\leq c_{0}
, where

c_{0}=c(Q_{0},H_{0},g_{0}).

(21)

We will show that the problem

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle c(Q,H,G)

Remark 3

Port-Hamiltonian systems are cyclo-passive, i.e., the internal energy of the system (the Hamiltonian) does not increase on closed trajectories of the state. More precisely, if the state trajectory is such that
x(t_{0})=x(t_{1})
for some
t_{0},t_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
, then
H(x(t_{0}))\leq H(x(t_{1}))
. If the Hamiltonian is bounded from below, then the system is passive, i.e., the internal energy of the system does not increase on
any
trajectory of the state. In view of Theorem

, the Hamiltonian obtained by solving problem (

are bounded from below for all

and
j\in[n]
. In other words, we can ensure that the identified system is passive by choosing a kernel
\mathfrak{h}
whose partial derivative kernel sections are bounded from below. This is the case for, e.g., the Gaussian kernel.

V
Optimization algorithm

In this section, we present an algorithm that provides a tractable solution to problem (
31
) and, thus, to the problem considered in this paper. The algorithm follows the general approach of
[
43
]
, where the authors consider a class of (nonconvex) optimization problems with convex cost and psd-convex-concave matrix inequality constraints. The key idea is to solve the problem by successively solving subproblems obtained by linearizing the concave part of the constraints. This renders each subproblem convex and therefore tractable using standard convex optimization techniques. Although
[
43
, Theorem 4.3]
establishes convergence of this algorithm under a set of technical assumptions, these are difficult to verify for the problem considered here. Instead, we provide a direct convergence proof based on
[
45
, Theorem 3.1]
.

V-A

Problem reformulation

We first show that (
31
) can be reformulated as a problem with a convex cost and psd-convex-concave matrix inequality constraints. The only nonconvex term in the cost of (
31
) is
\bar{L}(M,c,v)
. We can move this term to the constraints by introducing appropriate auxiliary variables
p_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
i\in[N]
. In particular, problem (
31
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}^{2}+\alpha\tr(\mathbf{F}M\mathbf{F}M^{\top})+\beta c^{\top}\mathbf{H}c+\gamma v^{\top}\mathbf{G}v

(37)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\lVert\dot{x}_{i}+\mathbf{F}_{i}M\mathbf{F}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{i}c-u_{i}\mathbf{G}_{i}v\rVert^{2}\leq p_{i}^{2},\ i\in[N],

\displaystyle M+M^{\top}\geq 0

where, for ease of notation, we have omitted the constraints
M\in\mathbb{R}^{nN\times nN}
,
c\in\mathbb{R}^{nN}
,
v\in\mathbb{R}^{nN}
,
p_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
i\in[N]
. The
i
-th constraint is equivalent to

\begin{bmatrix}p_{i}I&\dot{x}_{i}+\mathbf{F}_{i}M\mathbf{F}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{i}c-u_{i}\mathbf{G}_{i}v\\
\ast&p_{i}\end{bmatrix}\geq 0

(38)

by the Schur complement characterization of positive semidefiniteness, hence (
37
) is an optimization problem with convex cost and matrix inequality constraints.

The constraint (
38
) is biaffine and can therefore be written as a psd-convex-concave matrix inequality. To see this, define the linear map
X_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{nN\times nN}\to\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}
by

X_{i}(M)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{F}_{i}M^{\top}\mathbf{F}_{i}^{\top}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},

(39)

the linear map
Y_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{nN}\to\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}
by

Y_{i}(c)=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbf{H}_{i}c\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},

(40)

and the affine map
Z_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{nN}\to\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
by

Z_{i}(v)=\begin{bmatrix}0&\dot{x}_{i}-u_{i}\mathbf{G}_{i}v\\
\dot{x}_{i}^{\top}-u_{i}v^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{i}^{\top}&0\end{bmatrix}.

(41)

With these definitions, the constraint (
38
) can be written as

X_{i}(M)^{\top}Y_{i}(c)+Y_{i}(c)^{\top}X_{i}(M)+Z_{i}(v)+p_{i}I\geq 0.

(42)

Note that

X^{\top}Y+Y^{\top}X=X^{\top}X+Y^{\top}Y-(X-Y)^{\top}(X-Y)

(43)

for all
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}
, and the maps

\displaystyle(X,Y)

\displaystyle\mapsto X^{\top}X+Y^{\top}Y,

(44)

\displaystyle(X,Y)

\displaystyle\mapsto(X-Y)^{\top}(X-Y)

(45)

are psd-convex, see, e.g.,
[
43
, Lemma 3.1]
.

Now, define the map
A_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{nN\times nN}\times\mathbb{R}^{nN}\to\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
by

A_{i}(M,c)=X_{i}(M)^{\top}X_{i}(M)+Y_{i}(c)^{\top}Y_{i}(c)

(46)

and the map
B_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{nN\times nN}\times\mathbb{R}^{nN}\to\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
by

B_{i}(M,c)=(X_{i}(M)-Y_{i}(c))^{\top}(X_{i}(M)-Y_{i}(c))

(47)

Note that
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
are psd-convex since
X_{i}
and
Y_{i}
are linear, and the maps in (
44
) and (
45
) are psd-convex. Moreover, (
42
) can be written as

B_{i}(M,c)-A_{i}(M,c)-Z_{i}(v)-p_{i}I\leq 0

(48)

and, thus, problem (
37
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle f(p,M,c,v)

(49)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle M+M^{\top}\geq 0\text{ and }\eqref{eq:AB_constraint}\text{ holds for all }i\in[N]

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{nN\times nN}\times\mathbb{R}^{nN}\times\mathbb{R}^{nN}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

f(p,M,c,v)=p^{\top}p+\alpha\tr(\mathbf{F}M\mathbf{F}M^{\top})+\beta c^{\top}\mathbf{H}c+\gamma v^{\top}\mathbf{G}v,

and
p\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the vector whose
i
-th entry is
p_{i}
. Note that
f
is convex because
\mathbf{F}
,
\mathbf{H}
and
\mathbf{G}
are positive semidefinite, see Remark
.1
. Since
Z_{i}
is affine, it is also psd-convex. Therefore,
A_{i}(M,c)+Z_{i}(v)+p_{i}I
is psd-convex and (
48
) defines a psd-convex-concave matrix inequality constraint.

V-B

Iterative algorithm

We solve the nonconvex problem (
49
) by an iterative algorithm in which each step consists of solving a convex subproblem obtained by linearizing the concave part of the constraints. Specifically, let
(p^{k},M^{k},c^{k},v^{k})
denote the
k
-th iterate of the algorithm. The linearization of
A_{i}
around this point is given by

A^{k}_{i}(M,c)=A_{i}(M^{k},c^{k})+\partial A_{i}(M^{k},c^{k})(M-M^{k},c-c^{k})

It is straightforward to verify that

\hskip-8.53581pt\partial A_{i}(M^{k},c^{k})(M,c)=X_{i}(M)^{\top}X_{i}(M^{k})+X_{i}(M^{k})^{\top}X_{i}(M)\\
+Y_{i}(c)^{\top}Y_{i}(c^{k})+Y_{i}(c^{k})^{\top}Y_{i}(c),

hence
\partial A_{i}(M^{k},c^{k})(M^{k},c^{k})=2A_{i}(M^{k},c^{k})
and, thus,

A^{k}_{i}(M,c)=-A_{i}(M^{k},c^{k})+\partial A_{i}(M^{k},c^{k})(M,c).

(50)

The
k
-th subproblem is obtained by replacing
A_{i}
in (
48
) with its linearization
A^{k}_{i}
, resulting in the convex constraint

B_{i}(M,c)-A_{i}^{k}(M,c)-Z_{i}(v)-p_{i}I\leq 0.

(51)

The latter is equivalent to the affine matrix inequality

\begin{bmatrix}A_{i}^{k}(M,c)+Z_{i}(v)+p_{i}I&X_{i}(M)^{\top}-Y_{i}(c)^{\top}\\
X_{i}(M)-Y_{i}(c)&I\end{bmatrix}\geq 0

(52)

by the Schur complement characterization of negative semidefiniteness. The next iterate
(p^{k+1},M^{k+1},c^{k+1},v^{k+1})
is obtained by solving the (convex)
k
-th subproblem

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle f(p,M,c,v)

(53)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle M+M^{\top}\geq 0\text{ and }\eqref{eq:AB_constraint_k_linear}\text{ holds for all }i\in[N].

The constraints in this subproblem are affine matrix inequalities, whereas the cost function is convex quadratic. Indeed, in view of Remark
.1
, the cost
f(p,M,c,v)
can be written as

\begin{bmatrix}p\\
\mathrm{vec}(M)\\
c\\
v\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}I&0&0&0\\
0&\alpha\mathbf{F}\otimes\mathbf{F}&0&0\\
0&0&\beta\mathbf{H}&0\\
0&0&0&\gamma\mathbf{G}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}p\\
\mathrm{vec}(M)\\
c\\
v\end{bmatrix}.

where we recall that
\mathbf{H}
,
\mathbf{G}
,
\mathbf{F}
and, thus,
\mathbf{F}\otimes\mathbf{F}
are positive semidefinite. Consequently, the
k
-th subproblem (
53
) can be reformulated as a linear semidefinite program.

Remark 4

The
k
-th subproblem is easily seen to be strictly feasible. Indeed,
M+M^{\top}>0
for
M=I
, while the inequality (
52
), equivalently (
51
), can be made strict by choosing
p_{i}>0
sufficiently large,
i\in[N]
. Consequently, since
f
is convex, continuous and bounded from below, the
k
-th subproblem has at least one minimizer for any
(p^{k},M^{k},c^{k},v^{k})
. In particular, this means that the iteration is well-defined for any initial guess
(p^{0},M^{0},c^{0},v^{0})
. If, in addition,
f
is
strongly
convex, i.e.,
\mathbf{F}
,
\mathbf{H}
and
\mathbf{G}
are invertible, then the
k
-th subproblem has a
unique
minimizer for any
(p^{k},M^{k},c^{k},v^{k})
.

Although not strictly necessary, we may choose the initial guess
(p^{0},M^{0},c^{0},v^{0})
to be a strictly feasible point of the original problem (
49
), i.e.,
M^{0}+(M^{0})^{\top}>0
and

B_{i}(M^{0},c^{0})-A_{i}(M^{0},c^{0})-Z_{i}(v^{0})-p_{i}^{0}I<0

(54)

for all
i\in[N]
. The latter is equivalent to

\lVert\dot{x}_{i}+\mathbf{F}_{i}M^{0}\mathbf{F}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{i}c^{0}-u_{i}\mathbf{G}_{i}v^{0}\rVert^{2}<p_{i}^{0},

(55)

for all
i\in[N]
, hence we can take, e.g.,

M^{0}=I,\ c^{0}=0,\ v^{0}=0,\ p_{i}^{0}=\lVert\dot{x}_{i}\rVert^{2}+1,\ i\in[N].

(56)

This leads to the following summary of the iterative algorithm for solving problem (
49
).

49
)

Define
X_{i}
,
Y_{i}
,
Z_{i}
,
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
according to (

(p^{0},M^{0},c^{0},v^{0})

.

Remark 5

We can modify Algorithm

to incorporate output data. Building on the modifications in Remark

\hat{X}_{i}(v),\hat{Y}_{i}(c)\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)\times 2}

\hat{X}_{i}(v)^{\top}\hat{Y}_{i}(c)+\hat{Y}_{i}(c)^{\top}\hat{X}_{i}(v)+\hat{Z}_{i}+\hat{p}_{i}I\geq 0.

to the

V-C

Convergence analysis

We now prove that the sequence
(p^{k},M^{k},c^{k},v^{k})
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, generated by Algorithm
1
converges to a stationary point of (
49
). We do this under the assumption that the Gram matrices
\mathbf{F}
,
\mathbf{H}
and
\mathbf{G}
are invertible and, thus, positive definite. This ensures that the cost function
f
is
strongly
convex with convexity parameter

\rho_{f}=\min\left(1,\alpha\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{F})^{2},\beta\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{H}),\gamma\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{G})\right)>0.

(62)

where we used the fact that
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{F}\otimes\mathbf{F})=\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{F})^{2}
. In particular, this means that the
k
-th subproblem (
53
) has a
unique
minimizer for any
(p^{k},M^{k},c^{k},v^{k})
, see Remark
4
.

We prove convergence of Algorithm
1
by rewriting it as a fixed-point iteration and applying
[
45
, Theorem 3.1]
. Define

\Theta=\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{nN\times nN}\times\mathbb{R}^{nN}\times\mathbb{R}^{nN}

(63)

and let
\theta^{k}=(p^{k},M^{k},c^{k},v^{k})\in\Theta
for all
k\in\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}
. Note that problem (
49
) can be rewritten as

\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\ f(\theta)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad B(\theta)-A(\theta)\leq 0,

(64)

where the maps
A,B:\Theta\to\mathbb{S}^{(n+1)N+nN}
are psd-convex. In particular, for
\theta=(p,M,c,v)
,
A(\theta)
is the block diagonal matrix with
A_{i}(M,c)+Z_{i}(v)+p_{i}I
,
i\in[N]
, and
M+M^{\top}
on the block diagonal, and
B(\theta)
is the block diagonal matrix with
B_{i}(M,c)
,
i\in[N]
, and
0\in\mathbb{R}^{nN\times nN}
on the block diagonal.

Next, consider the set-valued map
C:\Theta\rightrightarrows\Theta
given by

C(\theta)=\left\{\vartheta\in\Theta\,\left|\,\vphantom{\vartheta\in\Theta}B(\vartheta)-A(\theta)-\partial A(\theta)(\vartheta-\theta)\leq 0\right.\right\}.

Note that

The assumption that

VII
Conclusion

We proposed a kernel-based framework for the identification of nonlinear port-Hamiltonian systems from input-state-output data. We modeled the Hamiltonian and input maps in RKHSs and represented the interconnection and dissipation structure in a kernel-based sum-of-squares form. We formulated the identification problem as an infinite-dimensional optimization problem over these model classes. As our first main result, we established a representer theorem that reduces this problem to a finite-dimensional one. As our second main result, we developed an iterative solution algorithm for the resulting nonconvex optimization problem and proved its convergence.

We also illustrated our results with an example, where we saw that the flexibility of the model classes and the nonconvexity of the estimation problem can lead to suboptimal solutions. At the same time, we saw that incorporating additional prior knowledge is straightforward and can significantly improve the quality of the identified models. Future work will investigate the influence of the kernel choice on the resulting approximation and explore how the port-Hamiltonian structure of the identified models can be exploited in control-oriented applications.

In this appendix, we introduce some of the theory of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS’s) that is relevant to the discussion in this paper. One of the most attractive features of the this theory is that certain function (operator) estimation problems have computationally tractable solutions due to the
representer theorem
. More specifically, the representer theorem states that the minimizers of a particular class of optimization problems over an RKHS belong to a finite-dimensional subspace of the RKHS. Therefore, these typically infinite-dimensional problems can be solved by solving an associated finite-dimensional problem.

This appendix is split into three subsections, each with a representer theorem as the main result. First we present the standard theory of RKHS’s, including kernels, the reproducing property, the Gram matrix, feature maps, and the classic representer theorem. Second, we present results concerning
partial derivatives
in RKHS’s, namely, we prove a partial derivative reproducing property and a representer theorem in the case where the cost of the optimization problem depends on partial derivative data. Third, we present an approach to modelling pointwise nonnegative maps and prove a representer theorem in the case where the decision variable of the optimization problem is restricted to be a pointwise nonnegative map.

Consider a Hilbert space
\mathcal{G}

G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}

if it admits a reproducing kernel, defined below.

Definition .1

A map
\mathfrak{g}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is a
reproducing kernel
(or, simply,

) for
\mathcal{G}

\mathfrak{g}(\cdot,x)v\in\mathcal{G}
for all

2.

\left\langle G(x),v\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}=\left\langle G,\mathfrak{g}(\cdot,x)v\right\rangle_{\mathcal{G}}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
v\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and
G\in\mathcal{G}
.

The second property in Definition
.1
is referred to as the
reproducing property
. The interpretation of the reproducing property is clearer in the case where the kernel is scalar-valued, i.e.,

. In this case, the reproducing property states that
G(x)=\left\langle G,\mathfrak{g}(\cdot,x)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{G}}
for all
G\in\mathcal{G}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The operator
\mathfrak{g}(\cdot,x)\in\mathcal{G}
is called a
kernel section
, hence the reproducing property states that the inner product of an operator and a kernel section at a given point
reproduces
the value of the operator at that point. This implies that the point evaluation functionals in an RKHS are
continuous
. In fact, that latter is often used as the defining property of an RKHS.

The class of reproducing kernels is completely characterized by two properties, namely, symmetry and positive semidefiniteness, defined below.

Definition .2

The map
\mathfrak{g}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is:

1.

symmetric
if
\mathfrak{g}(x,y)^{\top}=\mathfrak{g}(y,x)
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
;

2.

positive semidefinite
if

\vskip-2.84526pt\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle v_{i},\mathfrak{g}(x_{i},x_{j})v_{j}\right\rangle\geq 0

(88)

for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
i\in[N]
.

Any reproducing kernel needs to be symmetric and positive semidefinite due to the reproducing property. More notably, the converse also holds and is known as the Moore-Aronszajn theorem
[
49
]
, see
[
47
]
for the extension to the general case.

The properties of symmetry and positive semidefiniteness of the map
\mathfrak{g}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
can be expressed using the so-called
Gram matrix
. Given
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i\in[N]
, we define the Gram matrix
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{pN\times pN}
as

\mathbf{G}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathfrak{g}(x_{1},x_{1})&\cdots&\mathfrak{g}(x_{1},x_{n})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathfrak{g}(x_{n},x_{1})&\cdots&\mathfrak{g}(x_{n},x_{n})\end{bmatrix},

(89)

Then, (
88
) reads

\left\langle\mathbf{G}(v_{1},\dots,v_{n}),(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{pN}}\geq 0,

(90)

hence the map
\mathfrak{g}
is symmetric and positive semidefinite if and only if, for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i\in[N]
, the corresponding Gram matrix
\mathbf{G}
in (
89
) is symmetric positive semi-definite.

Symmetric positive semidefinite maps
\mathfrak{g}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
can be defined via an auxiliary Hilbert space
\mathcal{W}
and a map
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{p},\mathcal{W})
by setting
\mathfrak{g}(x,y)=\phi(x)^{*}\phi(y)
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The space
\mathcal{W}
is referred to as a
feature space
and the map
\phi
as a
feature map
. The following proposition, see
[
50
, Theorem 7]
, states that each symmetric positive semidefinite map has an associated feature map and vice versa.

Proposition .1

The map
\mathfrak{g}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is symmetric and positive semidefinite if and only if there exists a Hilbert space
\mathcal{W}
and a feature map
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{p},\mathcal{W})
such that
\mathfrak{g}(x,y)=\phi(x)^{*}\phi(y)
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The feature map is central in developing kernel-based algorithms and can allow for simpler analysis. Namely, it can be shown that the reproducing kernel Hilbert space
\mathcal{G}
with reproducing kernel
\mathfrak{g}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and associated feature map
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{p},\mathcal{W})
consists of operators of the form
G=\phi(\cdot)^{*}w
, where
w
belongs to the closure of
\spn\left\{\phi(x)v\,\left|\,\vphantom{\phi(x)v}x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},v\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\right.\right\}\subset\mathcal{W}
, and
\lVert G\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}=\lVert w\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}
. We refer to
[
48
, Theorem 5 and Theorem 6]
for a proof of this statement in the case where the kernel is scalar-valued. The proof in the general case follows similar reasoning.

As mentioned at the beginning of this appendix, an attractive feature of the theory of RKHS’s is that certain function estimation problems have computationally tractable solutions. In particular, let
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i\in[N]
, and consider the problem

\displaystyle\min_{G\in\mathcal{G}}

\displaystyle L(G(x_{1}),\dots,G(x_{N}))+\alpha\lVert G\rVert_{\mathcal{G}},

(91)

where
\alpha>0
and
L:(\mathbb{R}^{p})^{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a continuous loss function. In the context of estimating a function from data, the term
L(G(x_{1}),\dots,G(x_{N}))
measures the data misfit, while
\lVert G\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}
measures the complexity of
G
and acts as a regularizer. Although
\mathcal{G}
is typically infinite-dimensional, the minimizer of (
91
) belongs to a finite-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{G}
. This is the content of the representer theorem
[
47
, Theorem 4.2]
, which follows from the following technical lemma.

Lemma .2

Let
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i\in[N]
. For all
G\in\mathcal{G}
, there exist
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i\in[N]
, such that the map

\displaystyle\bar{G}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathfrak{g}(\cdot,x_{i})v_{i}

(92)

satisfies
\bar{G}(x_{i})=G(x_{i})
,
i\in[N]
, and
\lVert\bar{G}\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}\leq\lVert G\rVert_{\mathcal{G}}
.

Then, the representer theorem states the following.

Proposition .3

Let
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i\in[N]
. Suppose that
\alpha>0
and
L:(\mathbb{R}^{p})^{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is continuous. Then, problem (
91
) has a minimizer of the form (
92
).

As a a consequence of the representer theorem, we can solve (
91
) by substituting
G
of the form (
92
), and solving for the coefficient vectors
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
i\in[N]
. This yields a finite-dimensional optimization problem that can be compactly expressed using the Gram matrix. Namely, we can show that any operator
G
of the form (
92
) satisfies

G(x_{i})=\mathbf{G}_{i}v,\quad\lVert G\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{G}}=v^{\top}\mathbf{G}v,

(93)

where
v=\col(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})
,
\mathbf{G}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times nN}
is given by

\mathbf{G}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathfrak{g}(x_{i},x_{1})&\cdots&\mathfrak{g}(x_{i},x_{N})\end{bmatrix}

(94)

for all
i\in[N]
, and
\mathbf{G}=\col(\mathbf{G}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{G}_{N})
is the Gram matrix associated with
\mathfrak{g}
and
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i\in[N]
. Consequently, a minimizer of (
91
) can be obtained by solving

\min_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{pN}}\quad L(\mathbf{G}_{1}v,\dots,\mathbf{G}_{N}v)+\alpha v^{\top}\mathbf{G}v

(95)

and substituting the resulting minimizer in (
92
).

-B

Partial derivatives in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces

Next, we discuss an extension of the standard theory of RKHS’s that deals with partial derivatives. In particular, we show that RKHS’s whose kernels are (sufficiently) differentiable have a partial derivative reproducing property. This allows us to obtain a representer theorem for a class of optimization problems where the cost depends on partial derivative data. The latter is essential for the solution of the problem considered in this paper since the data misfit (
12
) depends on the partial derivatives of the Hamiltonian
H
. The results in this subsection can be found in
[
51
]
. The authors of
[
51
]
consider operators whose domain is a
compact
subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Here, we consider the extension to operators whose domain is the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Although this extension is fairly straightforward, we still provide the relevant proofs for the sake of completeness.

Throughout this subsection, we consider an RKHS
\mathcal{H}
with a scalar-valued kernel
\mathfrak{h}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
(the notation is chosen to be consistent with the notation in the main body of this paper). We are interested in solving an optimization problem of the form (
91
), where the cost depends on values of the
partial derivatives
of an operator
H\in\mathcal{H}
. For this problem to be well-posed, the operators in
\mathcal{H}
must be differentiable, which leads to the following proposition.

Proposition .4

Suppose that
\mathfrak{h}
is twice continuously differentiable. Then, the following statements hold for all
i\in[n]
:

1.

\partial^{\textrm{s}}_{i}\mathfrak{h}(\cdot,y)\in\mathcal{H}
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
;

2.

\partial_{i}H(y)=\left\langle H,\partial^{\textrm{s}}_{i}\mathfrak{h}(\cdot,y)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
for all
H\in\mathcal{H}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof:

Let
i\in[N]
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For each
\lambda>0
, the map

h_{\lambda}=\frac{\mathfrak{h}(\cdot,y+\lambda e_{i})-\mathfrak{h}(\cdot,y)}{\lambda}

(96)

is such that
h_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{H}
and

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}h_{\lambda}(x)=\partial^{\textrm{s}}_{i}\mathfrak{h}(x,y)

(97)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The reproducing property implies that

\displaystyle\lVert h_{\lambda}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{h_{\lambda}(y+\lambda e_{i})-h_{\lambda}(y)}{\lambda}.

(98)

Since
\mathfrak{h}
is twice continuously differentiable, it follows that

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\lVert h_{\lambda}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\partial_{i}h_{\lambda}(y)=\partial^{\textrm{f}}_{i}\partial^{\textrm{s}}_{i}\mathfrak{h}(y,y),

(99)

hence the set
\left\{h_{\lambda}\,\left|\,\vphantom{h_{\lambda}}0<\lambda\leq\delta\right.\right\}\subset\mathcal{H}
is bounded for sufficiently small
\delta>0
. Every bounded sequence in a Hilbert space has a weakly convergent subsequence. This follows from the Eberlein-Smulian theorem
[
52
, Theorem 13.1]
, where we note that the unit ball in
\mathcal{H}
is weakly compact because Hilbert spaces are reflexive
[
52
, Theorem 4.2]
. Therefore, there exists a sequence
\lambda_{k}>0
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, that converges to
0
, while the sequence
h_{\lambda_{k}}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, converges weakly to some
h\in\mathcal{H}
, i.e.,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\langle h_{\lambda_{k}},H\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\left\langle h,H\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

(100)

for all
H\in\mathcal{H}
. Taking
H=\mathfrak{h}(\cdot,x)
yields

\lim_{k\to\infty}h_{\lambda_{k}}(x)=h(x)

(101)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
\lambda_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
, (
97
) and (
101
) imply that
\partial^{\textrm{s}}_{i}\mathfrak{h}(\cdot,y)=h\in\mathcal{H}
. Finally, we note that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\langle h_{\lambda_{k}},H\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{H(y+\lambda_{k}e_{i})-H(y)}{\lambda_{k}}=\partial_{i}H(y)

for all
H\in\mathcal{H}
, hence (
100
) yields

\partial_{i}H(y)=\left\langle\partial^{\textrm{s}}_{i}\mathfrak{h}(\cdot,y),H\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\left\langle H,\partial^{\textrm{s}}_{i}\mathfrak{h}(\cdot,y)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

(102)

for all
H\in\mathcal{H}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Recall the defining properties of the reproducing kernel
\mathfrak{h}
of the RKHS
\mathcal{H}
, namely, all kernel sections of
\mathfrak{h}
belong to
\mathcal{H}
and the reproducing property holds. Proposition
.4
tells us that analogous properties hold for partial derivatives, namely, all
partial derivative
kernel sections of
\mathfrak{h}
belong to
\mathcal{H}
and a
partial derivative
reproducing property holds. In fact, this partial derivative reproducing property implicitly shows that the operators in
\mathcal{H}
are differentiable, i.e.,
\partial_{i}H(y)
exists for all
i\in[N]
,
H\in\mathcal{H}
, and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Furthermore, it allows us to obtain a representer theorem for optimization problems where the cost depends on partial derivative data. To this end, consider the following technical lemma.

Lemma .5

Let
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i\in[N]
. For all
H\in\mathcal{H}
, there exist
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i\in[N]
, such that the map

\displaystyle\bar{H}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial^{\textrm{s}}\mathfrak{h}(\cdot,x_{i})^{\top}c_{i}\in\mathcal{H}

(103)

satisfies
\partial\bar{H}(x_{i})=\partial H(x_{i})
,
i\in[N]
, and
\lVert\bar{H}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\lVert H\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}
.

Proof:

Consider the finite-dimensional subspace

\bar{\mathcal{H}}=\spn\left\{\partial^{\textrm{s}}_{j}\mathfrak{h}(\cdot,x_{i})\,\left|\,\vphantom{\partial^{\textrm{s}}_{j}\mathfrak{h}(\cdot,x_{i})}i\in[N],\ j\in[n]\right.\right\}\subset\mathcal{H},

(104)

and its orthogonal complement
\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{\perp}\subset\mathcal{H}
.
Write
H=\bar{H}+\bar{H}^{\perp}
, where
\bar{H}\in\bar{\mathcal{H}}
and
\bar{H}^{\perp}\in\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{\perp}
, and note that

\partial_{j}H(x_{i})=\left\langle H,\partial^{\textrm{s}}_{j}\mathfrak{h}(\cdot,x_{i})\right\rangle=\left\langle\bar{H},\partial^{\textrm{s}}_{j}\mathfrak{h}(\cdot,x_{i})\right\rangle=\partial_{j}\bar{H}(x_{i})

for all
j\in[n]
and
i\in[N]
. Furthermore, we have that

\lVert H\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}=\lVert\bar{H}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}+\lVert\bar{H}^{\perp}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}},

(105)

hence
\lVert\bar{H}\rVert\leq\lVert H\rVert
. Since
\bar{H}\in\bar{\mathcal{H}}
, we can write

\bar{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial^{\textrm{s}}_{j}\mathfrak{h}(\cdot,x_{i})c_{ij}

(106)

where
c_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
i\in[N]
and
j\in[n]
. The latter can be compactly written as in (
103
), which concludes the proof.
∎

The following representer theorem is a special case of
[
51
, Theorem 2]
, which we prove for the sake of completeness.

Proposition .6

Let
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i\in[N]
. Suppose that
\beta>0
and
L:(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is continuous. Then, the problem

\displaystyle\min_{H\in\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle L(\partial H(x_{1}),\dots,\partial H(x_{N}))+\beta\lVert H\rVert_{\mathcal{H}},

(107)

has a minimizer of the form (
103
).

Proof:

Let the subspace
\bar{\mathcal{H}}\subset\mathcal{H}
be given by (
104
), and the map
c:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be given by

c(H)=L(\partial H(x_{1}),\dots,\partial H(x_{N}))+\beta\lVert H\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}.

(108)

Let
H_{0}\in\bar{\mathcal{H}}
be arbitrary and
\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
be the closed and bounded subset of operators
H\in\bar{\mathcal{H}}
such that
\beta\lVert H\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}\leq c(H_{0})
. Note that
\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
is compact because
\bar{\mathcal{H}}
is finite-dimensional. Let

\bar{H}_{0}=\argmin_{H\in\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{0}}c(H),

(109)

which exists because
c
is continuous and bounded from below, and
\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
is compact. We claim that
\bar{H}_{0}
is a minimizer of (
107
). Indeed, due to Lemma
.5
, for all
H\in\mathcal{H}
, there exists
\bar{H}\in\bar{\mathcal{H}}
such that
c(\bar{H})\leq c(H)
. If
c(\bar{H})\leq c(H_{0})
, then, in particular,
\beta\lVert\bar{H}\rVert\leq c(H_{0})
, hence
\bar{H}\in\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
and, thus,
c(\bar{H}_{0})\leq c(\bar{H})
by definition of
\bar{H}_{0}
. On the other hand, if
c(H_{0})<c(\bar{H})
, then
c(\bar{H}_{0})<c(\bar{H})
because
H_{0}\in\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
and, thus,
c(\bar{H}_{0})\leq c(H_{0})
. It follows that
c(\bar{H}_{0})\leq c(H)
for all
H\in\mathcal{H}
, hence
\bar{H}_{0}
is a minimizer of (
107
). Since
\bar{H}_{0}\in\bar{\mathcal{H}}
, it is indeed of the form (
103
), which concludes the proof.
∎

Just as the classical representer theorem, the representer theorem stated in Proposition
.6
provides a tractable solution to (
107
), where a minimizer can be obtained by substituting
H
of the form (
103
) and solving for the coefficient vectors
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i\in[N]
. To obtain the resulting finite-dimensional problem, note that, if
H
is of the form (
103
), then

\partial H(x_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{H}_{ij}c_{j}=\mathbf{H}_{i}c,

(110)

for all
i\in[N]
, where
\mathbf{H}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is given by

\mathbf{H}_{ij}=\begin{bmatrix}\partial^{\textrm{f}}_{1}\partial^{\textrm{s}}_{1}\mathfrak{h}(x_{i},x_{j})&\cdots&\partial^{\textrm{f}}_{1}\partial^{\textrm{f}}_{n}\mathfrak{h}(x_{i},x_{j})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\partial^{\textrm{f}}_{n}\partial^{\textrm{s}}_{1}\mathfrak{h}(x_{i},x_{j})&\cdots&\partial^{\textrm{f}}_{n}\partial^{\textrm{f}}_{n}\mathfrak{h}(x_{i},x_{j})\end{bmatrix}

(111)

for all
i,j\in[N]
,
c=\col(c_{1},\cdots,c_{n})
, and

\mathbf{H}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{H}_{i1}&\cdots&\mathbf{H}_{iN}\end{bmatrix}

(112)

for all
i\in[N]
. Let
\mathbf{H}=\col(\mathbf{H}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{H}_{N})
. We refer to
\mathbf{H}
as the
partial derivative Gram matrix
associated with
\mathfrak{h}
and
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i\in[N]
. This is because
\mathbf{H}
can be seen as the Gram matrix associated with a reproducing kernel
\mathfrak{H}
obtained from the partial derivatives of
\mathfrak{h}
, as shown below.

Proposition .7

The map
\mathfrak{H}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
given by

\mathfrak{H}(x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}\partial^{\textrm{f}}_{1}\partial^{\textrm{s}}_{1}\mathfrak{h}(x,y)&\cdots&\partial^{\textrm{f}}_{1}\partial^{\textrm{f}}_{n}\mathfrak{h}(x,y)\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\partial^{\textrm{f}}_{n}\partial^{\textrm{s}}_{1}\mathfrak{h}(x,y)&\cdots&\partial^{\textrm{f}}_{n}\partial^{\textrm{f}}_{n}\mathfrak{h}(x,y)\end{bmatrix},

(113)

is symmetric and positive semidefinite.

Proof:

In view of Proposition
.1
, it is enough to show that there exists a feature map
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathcal{H})
such that
\mathfrak{H}(x,y)=\phi(x)^{\ast}\phi(y)
. To this end, let
\phi
be such that

\phi(x)v=\partial^{\textrm{s}}\mathfrak{h}(\cdot,x)^{\top}v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial^{\textrm{s}}_{i}\mathfrak{h}(\cdot,x)v_{i},

(114)

for all
x,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is the
i
-th entry of
v
. Note that
\phi(x)v\in\mathcal{H}
due to the first item in Proposition
.4
. Due to the second item, we have that

\left\langle H,\phi(x)v\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{i}H(x)v_{i}=\left\langle\partial H(x),v\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

(115)

for all
H\in\mathcal{H}
and
x,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, hence
\phi(x)^{\ast}H=\partial H(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
H\in\mathcal{H}
. This implies that

\phi(x)^{\ast}\phi(y)v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial^{\textrm{f}}\partial^{\textrm{s}}_{i}\mathfrak{h}(x,y)v_{i}=\mathfrak{H}(x,y)v

(116)

for all
x,y,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and, thus,
\mathfrak{H}(x,y)=\phi(x)^{\ast}\phi(y)
.
∎

Proposition
.7
shows that
\mathfrak{H}
is a reproducing kernel. Note that
\mathbf{H}_{ij}=\mathfrak{H}(x_{i},x_{j})
for all
i,j\in[N]
, hence
\mathbf{H}
is the Gram matrix associated with
\mathfrak{H}
and
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i\in[n]
, This implies that
\mathbf{H}
is positive semidefinite. Furthermore, we have the following result regarding the norm of
H
.

Lemma .8

If
H\in\mathcal{H}
is of the form (
103
), then

\lVert H\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=c^{\top}\mathbf{H}c.

(117)

Proof:

Let the feature map
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathcal{H})
be defined as in the proof of Proposition
.7
. Note that we can write
H=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi(x_{i})c_{i}
, which implies that

\displaystyle\left\langle H,H\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left\langle\phi(x_{i})c_{i},\phi(x_{j})c_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}c_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{ij}c_{j},

because
\phi(x_{i})^{\ast}\phi(x_{j})=\mathfrak{H}(x_{i},x_{j})=\mathbf{H}_{ij}
for all
i,j\in[N]
. It is easily seen that the latter is equivalent to (
117
).
∎

Finally, using (
110
) and (
117
), we conclude that a minimizer of (
107
) can be obtained by solving

\min_{c\in\mathbb{R}^{nN}}L(\mathbf{H}_{1}c,\dots,\mathbf{H}_{N}c)+\beta c^{\top}\mathbf{H}c,

(118)

and substituting the resulting minimizer in (
103
).

Pointwise nonnegative maps

In this subsection, we leverage the theory of RKHS’s to model pointwise nonnegative maps. We also prove a representer theorem for a class of optimization problems of the form (
91
) where the decision variable is constrained to be a pointwise nonnegative map from this model class. The results in this section are an extension of the results in
[
25
]
, where only scalar-valued maps are considered, and
[
26
]
, where only pointwise nonnegative
and symmetric
maps are considered. We also refer to our previous work
[
23
]
, where we consider nonnegative input-output operators.

Consider the map
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
. It is easily seen that there exist maps
l_{i},r_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
i\in[p]
, such that

F=\sum_{i=1}^{p}l_{i}(\cdot)r_{i}(\cdot)^{\top}.

(119)

Indeed, one can take
l_{i}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
to be the
i
-th column of
F(x)
, and
r_{i}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
to be the
i
-th standard basis vector. We can model
F
via
l_{i},r_{i}
,
i\in[p]
, which can be modeled as maps in an RKHS. Let
\mathcal{F}
be an RKHS with kernel
\mathfrak{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and feature map
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{p},\mathcal{W})
, where
\mathcal{W}
is a Hilbert space. Recall that
\mathcal{F}
consists of maps of the form
\phi(\cdot)^{\ast}w
, where
w\in\mathcal{W}
. Consequently, we can write
l_{i}=\phi(\cdot)^{\ast}u_{i}
and
r_{i}=\phi(\cdot)^{\ast}v_{i}
for some
v_{i},u_{i}\in\mathcal{W}
,
i\in[p]
, which results in

F=\phi(\cdot)^{\ast}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}u_{i}v_{i}^{\ast}\right)\phi(\cdot).

(120)

The latter can be compactly written as

F=\phi(\cdot)^{\ast}Q\phi(\cdot),

(121)

where
Q=\sum_{i=1}^{p}u_{i}v_{i}^{\ast}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W})
has finite rank and is, thus, a Hilbert-Schmidt operator, i.e.,
Q\in\mathscr{H}(\mathcal{W})
. Note that
F
is pointwise nonnegative if
Q
is nonnegative since

\left\langle F(x)v,v\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}=\left\langle Q\phi(x)v,\phi(x)v\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}\geq 0

(122)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Therefore, we can model a pointwise nonnegative map
F
as in (
121
) with
Q\in\mathscr{H}(\mathcal{W})_{+}
.

This model class is highly expressive. In particular, if the feature map
\phi
is universal, then any continuous pointwise nonnegative map
F
can be approximated arbitrarily well on compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This has already been established for pointwise nonnegative scalar-valued maps in
[
25
]
and pointwise symmetric and nonnegative matrix-valued maps in
[
26
]
. Extending these results to the case at hand, i.e., without pointwise symmetry, is fairly straightforward but outside of the scope of this paper. Instead, we prove yet another representer theorem using the following technical lemma.

Lemma .9

Let
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i\in[N]
. For all
Q\in\mathscr{H}(\mathcal{W})
, there exist
M_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
,
i,j\in[N]
, such that the operator

\bar{Q}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\phi(x_{i})M_{ij}\phi(x_{j})^{\ast}

(123)

satisfies
\phi(x_{i})^{\ast}\bar{Q}\phi(x_{i})=\phi(x_{i})^{\ast}Q\phi(x_{i})
for all
i\in[N]
, and
\lVert\bar{Q}\rVert_{\mathscr{H}}=\lVert Q\rVert_{\mathscr{H}}
. Furthermore, if
Q
is nonnegative, then
\bar{Q}
is nonnegative and the block matrix

M=\begin{bmatrix}M_{11}&\cdots&M_{1N}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
M_{N1}&\cdots&M_{NN}\end{bmatrix}

(124)

is nonnegative.

Proof:

Let
\Pi\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{W})
be the orthogonal projection onto the finite-dimensional subspace
\bar{\mathcal{W}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\im\phi(x_{i})\subset\mathcal{W}
, and define
\bar{Q}=\Pi Q\Pi
. Note that
\Pi=\Pi^{\ast}
and
\Pi\phi(x_{i})=\phi(x_{i})
for all
i\in[N]
, hence
\phi(x_{i})^{\ast}\bar{Q}\phi(x_{i})=\phi(x_{i})^{\ast}\bar{Q}\phi(x_{i})
for all
i\in[N]
. Moreover, the composition of a Hilbert-Schmidt operator and a bounded operator is also a Hilbert-Schmidt operator
[
52
, p.267]
, hence
\bar{Q}\in\mathscr{H}(\mathcal{W})
and

\lVert\bar{Q}\rVert_{\mathscr{H}}\leq\lVert\Pi\rVert^{2}\lVert Q\rVert_{\mathscr{H}}=\lVert Q\rVert_{\mathscr{H}},

(125)

where
\lVert\Pi\rVert=1
because
\Pi
is an orthogonal projection. As shown in
[
23
, Lemma 3]
, there exist
M_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
,
i,j\in[N]
such that (
123
) holds. Furthermore,
\bar{Q}
and the block matrix in (
124
) are nonnegative if
Q
is nonnegative.
∎

We obtain the following representer theorem for optimization problems over pointwise nonnegative maps.

Proposition .10

Let
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i\in[N]
. Suppose that
\gamma>0
and
L:(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is continuous. Then, the problem

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle L(F(x_{1}),\dots,F(x_{N}))+\gamma\lVert Q\rVert_{\mathscr{H}}^{2},

(126)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle Q\in\mathscr{H}(\mathcal{W})_{+}\text{ and }F=\phi(\cdot)^{\ast}Q\phi(\cdot)

has a minimizer of the form (
123
), where the block matrix in (
124
) is nonnegative. Furthermore, the map

\displaystyle\bar{F}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\mathfrak{f}(\cdot,x_{i})M_{ij}\mathfrak{f}(\cdot,x_{j})^{\top},

(127)

is such that
\bar{F}=\phi(\cdot)^{\ast}\bar{Q}\phi(\cdot)
.

Proof:

Let
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}
be the subset of operators of the form (
123
), where the block matrix in (
124
) is nonnegative. The latter implies that the operators in
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}
are nonnegative. With this in mind, let the map
c:\mathscr{H}(\mathcal{W})_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be given by

c(Q)=L(F(x_{1}),\dots,F(x_{N}))+\gamma\lVert Q\rVert_{\mathscr{H}},

(128)

where
F=\phi(\cdot)^{\ast}Q\phi(\cdot)
. Let
Q_{0}\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}
be arbitrary and let
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
be the closed and bounded subset of operators
Q\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}
such that
\gamma\lVert Q\rVert_{\mathscr{H}}\leq c(Q_{0})
. Note that
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
is compact because
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}
is a subset of a finite-dimensional subspace. Let

\bar{Q}_{0}=\argmin_{Q\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}}c(Q),

(129)

which exists because
c
is continuous and bounded from below, and
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
is compact. We claim that
\bar{Q}_{0}
is a minimizer of (
126
). Due to Lemma
.9
, for all
Q\in\mathscr{H}(\mathcal{W})_{+}
, there exists
\bar{Q}\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}
such that
c(\bar{Q})\leq c(Q)
. If
c(\bar{Q})\leq c(Q_{0})
, then, in particular,
\gamma\lVert\bar{Q}\rVert\leq c(Q_{0})
, hence
\bar{Q}\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
, and, thus,
c(\bar{Q}_{0})\leq c(\bar{Q})
by definition of
\bar{Q}_{0}
. On the other hand, if
c(Q_{0})<c(\bar{Q})
, then
c(\bar{Q}_{0})<c(\bar{Q})
because
Q_{0}\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
and, thus,
c(\bar{Q}_{0})\leq c(Q_{0})
. It follows that
c(\bar{Q}_{0})\leq c(Q)
for all
Q\in\mathscr{H}(\mathcal{W})_{+}
, hence
\bar{Q}_{0}
is a minimizer of (
126
). Since
\bar{Q}_{0}\in\bar{\mathcal{Q}}
, it is indeed of the form (
103
), which concludes the proof. Finally, (
127
) follows from the fact that
\phi(\cdot)^{\ast}\phi(x_{i})=\mathfrak{f}(\cdot,x_{i})
for all
i\in[N]
.
∎

Like the previous representer theorems, the representer theorem stated in Proposition
.10
provides a tractable solution to (
126
), where a minimizer can be obtained by substituting
Q
of the form (
123
) and solving for coefficient matrices
M_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
,
i,j\in[N]
. The resulting finite-dimensional problem can be compactly expressed using the Gram matrix. In particular, if
F
is of the form (
127
), then

F(x_{i})=\mathbf{F}_{i}M\mathbf{F}_{i}^{\top}

(130)

for all
i\in[N]
, where
M
is given by (
124
) and

\mathbf{F}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathfrak{f}(x_{i},x_{1})&\cdots&\mathfrak{f}(x_{i},x_{N})\end{bmatrix}

(131)

is the
i
-th block row of the Gram matrix
\mathbf{F}=\col(\mathbf{F}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{F}_{N})
associated with
\mathfrak{f}
and
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i\in[N]
. We also have the following result regarding the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of (
123
).

Lemma .11

If
Q\in\mathscr{H}(\mathcal{W})
is of the form (
127
), then

\lVert Q\rVert^{2}_{\mathscr{H}}=\tr(\mathbf{F}M\mathbf{F}M^{\top}),

(132)

where
M
is given by (
124
).

Proof:

Let the operator
\Phi\in\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{pN},\mathcal{W})
be given by

\Phi(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi(x_{i})z_{i}

(133)

where
z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
i\in[N]
, and
z=\col(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})
. We can write
Q=\Phi M\Phi^{\ast}
, which is easily verified using the block operator notation

\Phi=\begin{bmatrix}\phi(x_{1})&\cdots&\phi(x_{N})\end{bmatrix}.

(134)

Let
w_{i}
,
i\in[k]
, be an orthonormal basis for the finite-dimensional subspace
\im\Phi\subset\mathcal{W}
, and extend it to an orthonormal basis
w_{i}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
, for the whole space
\mathcal{W}
. Note that
\left\langle\Phi c,w_{i}\right\rangle=0
for all
c\in\mathbb{R}^{pN}
and
i>k
, hence
\Phi^{\ast}w_{i}=0
for all
i>k
. Consequently, we obtain

\lVert Q\rVert_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lVert Qw_{i}\rVert_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lVert\Phi M\Phi^{\ast}w_{i}\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{W}}

(135)

Now, for all
i\in[k]
, there exists
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{pN}
such that
w_{i}=\Phi c_{i}
. Since
\left\langle w_{i},w_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}=0
for all
i\neq j
, it follows that

\left\langle\Phi c_{i},\Phi c_{j}\right\rangle=\left\langle c_{i},\mathbf{F}c_{j}\right\rangle=\langle\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}c_{i},\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}c_{j}\rangle=0

(136)

where we used the fact that
\Phi^{\ast}\Phi=\mathbf{F}
is symmetric positive semidefinite, hence there exists a symmetric positive semidefinite
\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{pN\times pN}
such that
\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}=\mathbf{F}
. Similarly, since
\lVert w_{i}\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{W}}=1
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, it follows that
\lVert\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}c_{i}\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{pN}}=1
for all
i\in[k]
. In other words, the vectors
v_{i}=\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{pN}
,
i\in[k]
, are orthonormal. Note that
k
is the rank of
\Phi
, which equals the rank of
\mathbf{F}
and, thus
\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}
. This implies that
v_{i}
,
i\in[k]
form an orthonormal basis for the subspace
\im\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{pN}
, which can be extended to an orthonormal basis
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{pN}
,
i\in[nP]
, for the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{pN}
. Since
\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}
is symmetric, this basis is such that
\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}v_{i}=0
for all
i>k
.

With this in mind, since
\Phi^{\ast}\Phi=\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}
, and
w_{i}=\Phi c_{i}
and
v_{i}=\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}c_{i}
for all
i\in[k]
, it follows that

\lVert\Phi M\Phi^{\ast}w_{i}\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{W}}=\left\langle\Phi M\mathbf{F}c_{i},\Phi M\mathbf{F}c_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}=\lVert\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}M\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}v_{i}\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{pN}}^{2}

for all
i\in[k]
. This implies that

\lVert Q\rVert_{\mathscr{H}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lVert\Phi M\Phi^{\ast}w_{i}\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{W}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lVert\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}M\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}v_{i}\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{pN}}^{2},

(137)

Since
\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}v_{i}=0
for all
i>k
, we can write

\lVert Q\rVert_{\mathscr{H}}=\sum_{i=1}^{nP}\lVert\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}M\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}v_{i}\rVert_{\mathbb{R}^{pN}}^{2}=\lVert\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}M\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}\rVert^{2}_{\mathscr{H}},

(138)

Recall that the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of a matrix is simply the Frobenius norm, hence

\lVert\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}M\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}\rVert^{2}_{\mathscr{H}}=\tr(\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}}M\mathbf{F}M^{\top}\mathbf{F}^{\frac{1}{2}})=\tr(\mathbf{F}M\mathbf{F}M^{\top}),

where we used the fact that
\tr(AB)=\tr(BA)
for all matrices
A
and
B
of appropriate sizes.
∎

Remark .1

It can be shown that

\tr(\mathbf{F}M\mathbf{F}M^{\top})=\mathrm{vec}(M)^{\top}(\mathbf{F}\otimes\mathbf{F})\mathrm{vec}(M),

(139)

where
\otimes
is the Kronecker product. Since
\mathbf{F}
is positive semidefinite, it follows that
\mathbf{F}\otimes\mathbf{F}
is positive semidefinite, which immediately shows that
\tr(\mathbf{F}M\mathbf{F}M^{\top})
is convex in
M
. Moreover, if
\mathbf{F}
is invertible, then
\tr(\mathbf{F}M\mathbf{F}M^{\top})
is strongly convex with convexity parameter
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{F}\otimes\mathbf{F})=\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{F})^{2}
.

Finally, using (
130
) and (
132
), we conclude that a minimizer of (
126
) can be obtained by solving

\displaystyle\min_{M\in\mathbb{R}^{pN\times pN}_{+}}

\displaystyle L(\mathbf{F}_{1}M\mathbf{F}_{1}^{\top},\dots,\mathbf{F}_{N}M\mathbf{F}_{N}^{\top})+\gamma\tr(\mathbf{F}M\mathbf{F}M^{\top}),

and substituting the resulting minimizer in (
127
).
