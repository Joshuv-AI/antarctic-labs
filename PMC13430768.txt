Enantioselective synthesis of silicon-stereogenic sulfur-containing silanes via organocatalytic remote C–H sulfenylation of arenes
Authors: Shu-Kun Li, Xin-Yu Zhang, Qing-Yun Cai, De-Ren Chu, Yu-Xuan Huo, Fan Wang, Xue-Qing Gong, Zhi-Min Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, we report a remote C–H sulfenylation of 5,10-dihydrophenazasilines enabled by a cooperative catalytic system comprising a chiral Lewis base and an achiral Brønsted acid for the first time. The synergistic use of a chiral phosphoric triamide sulfide catalyst featuring a bis((S)-1-phenylpropyl)amine structural motif and an achiral sulfonic acid co-catalyst with precisely tuned acidity is essential for achieving efficient remote stereocontrol. This methodology provides efficient access to chiral silicon-stereogenic sulfur-containing organosilanes. A broad substrate scope is demonstrated, affording the desired products in moderate to excellent yields (up to 94%) and with moderate to excellent enantioselectivities (up to >99% ee). Mechanistic investigations through control experiments suggest that the reaction proceeds via sequential desymmetrization and kinetic resolution processes. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations further demonstrate that multiple weak C–H⋯π interactions, together with the torsional distortion of the substrate, are crucial for the observed enantioselectivity.

Introduction

Due to silicon's unique electronic structure and relatively large atomic radius, chiral silicon-stereogenic organosilicon compounds often exhibit significantly different physical and chemical properties compared to chiral carbon-stereogenic counterparts. As a result, these compounds hold considerable potential for applications in asymmetric synthesis, functional materials, and biomedicine (Scheme 1a).1 Consequently, the development of efficient catalytic asymmetric methods for constructing chiral silicon stereocenters has become an important research direction in the field of asymmetric catalysis. However, compared to the diverse and well-established catalytic asymmetric synthesis strategies for chiral carbon stereocenters, the asymmetric synthesis of chiral silicon-stereogenic organosilicon compounds remains relatively limited (Scheme 1b).2 For instance, chiral carbon stereocenters can often be efficiently constructed through catalytic asymmetric addition reactions involving carbon–carbon double bonds or carbon-heteroatom double bonds. However, the larger atomic radius of silicon makes silicon–silicon or silicon-heteroatom double bonds inherently unstable, rendering such strategies difficult to directly apply to the synthesis of chiral silicon-stereogenic organosilicon compounds. Currently, reported asymmetric synthesis methods for chiral silicon-stereogenic organosilicon compounds primarily rely on transition-metal catalysis,3 while successful examples of asymmetric organocatalysis remain rare4 (Scheme 1b). Compared to transition-metal catalysis, organocatalysis offers several distinct advantages, including the absence of metals, enhanced environmental compatibility, operational simplicity, and superior functional group tolerance. Additionally, research on catalytic asymmetric construction of chiral silicon stereocenters containing heteroatoms such as sulfur or selenium is still in its early stages.5 Notably, chiral compounds containing sulfur and selenium have already demonstrated significant promise in drug discovery and the design of bioactive molecules.6 Therefore, there is a pressing need for the development of efficient and novel organocatalytic strategies to enable the asymmetric synthesis of structurally diverse organosilicon compounds featuring chiral silicon stereocenters.

The design of the chiral Lewis base-catalyzed remote C–H sulfenylation of 5,10-dihydrophenazasilines.

Our group has long been focusing on the development of catalytic asymmetric electrophilic sulfenylation and selenylation methodologies. By employing chiral Lewis bases as catalysts,7 we have established a suite of enantioselective electrophilic sulfenylation and selenylation reactions for unsaturated hydrocarbons, as well as asymmetric C–H sulfenylation protocols for aromatic systems.8 These advances have enabled the efficient synthesis of structurally diverse chiral organosulfur and organoselenium compounds. Building upon this foundation, we aim to develop an asymmetric aromatic C–H sulfenylation reaction catalyzed by chiral Lewis bases, for the efficient construction of chiral silicon-stereogenic sulfur-containing organosilanes. As illustrated in Scheme 1c, this transformation presents three key challenges: (i) achieving remote stereocontrol—specifically, transferring chiral information from the catalyst to the prochiral silicon center located distal to the site of C–S bond formation;9 (ii) reconciling competing regioselectivity (ortho vs. para sulfenylation) and chemoselectivity (C–S vs. N–S bond formation);8i and (iii) distinguishing between two distinct pathways—namely, a simple desymmetrization process versus a sequential desymmetrization/kinetic resolution sequence.10 To address these challenges, we propose a rationally designed Lewis base catalyst incorporating multiple chiral motifs, enabling simultaneous chiral induction across distinct spatial regions of the substrate and thereby achieving effective remote stereocontrol. Furthermore, the strategic selection of electrophilic sulfenylating reagents with different substituents is anticipated to enhance both regioselectivity and chemoselectivity. Finally, fine-tuning of reaction parameters, including temperature and stoichiometry of the sulfenylating reagent, will be employed to modulate the reaction pathway and optimize enantioselectivity and overall catalytic efficiency.

Herein, we report a chiral phosphoric triamide sulfide and an achiral sulfonic acid co-catalyzed asymmetric remote C–H sulfenylation of 5,10-dihydrophenazasilines.4f This reaction exhibits high chemo-, regio-, and enantioselectivity, enabling the efficient synthesis of chiral silicon-stereogenic sulfur-containing organosilanes. The process proceeds through sequential desymmetrization and kinetic resolution pathways (Scheme 1d).

Results and discussion

Reaction condition optimization

To validate our hypothesis, symmetric 5,10-dihydrophenazasiline 1a was employed as the model substrate and saccharin-derived 2-(trifluoromethyl)phenylthio compound 2a as the model sulfenylating reagent. Following an extensive screening of reaction parameters (see the SI for more details on the screening of catalysts and acids), an optimized catalytic system was established: a chiral phosphoric triamide sulfide bearing a bis((S)-1-phenylpropyl)amine motif (Cat.1) served as the Lewis base catalyst; 4-tert-butylbenzenesulfonic acid (acid.1) functioned as the Brønsted acid co-catalyst; a mixed solvent of chloroform and chlorobenzene (v/v = 9 : 1) was used; and 5 Å molecular sieves (5 Å MS) were included as an additive. The reaction was carried out under an argon atmosphere at −50 °C for 48 h, followed by gradual warming to −40 °C for an additional 20 h. Under these conditions, the silicon-stereogenic sulfur-containing silane 3a was obtained in 56% isolated yield and 93% enantiomeric excess (ee), albeit with concomitant formation of byproduct 4a (37% yield) (Table 1, entry 1). Substitution of Cat.1 with structurally related catalysts Cat.2–Cat.4 resulted in decreases in both yield and enantioselectivity (Table 1, entries 2–4), indicating that the precise chiral architecture—including multiple stereogenic elements and carefully tuned spatial structure on the amine substituents—is critical for high asymmetric induction. Solvent effects were also pronounced: using chloroform alone led to a slight erosion in ee (entry 5); ethyl acetate (EA) completely suppressed reactivity (entry 6); dichloromethane enabled higher conversion (81% yield) but severely compromised enantioselectivity (73% ee, entry 7); and toluene afforded a lower yield (50%, entry 8). Acid co-catalyst screening revealed that diphenyl phosphate (DPP) failed to promote productive turnover (negligible conversion, entry 9), underscoring the requirement for sufficient Brønsted acidity. Similarly, tris(pentafluorophenyl)borane—a strong Lewis acid—led to slower kinetics and diminished enantiocontrol (70% ee, entry 10), further supporting the necessity of a Brønsted acidic environment. Omission of 5 Å molecular sieves caused a sharp decline in ee to 70% (entry 11). Temperature profiling experiments demonstrated that maintaining either −50 °C or −40 °C throughout the entire reaction duration compromised efficiency: constant −50 °C led to incomplete kinetic resolution, whereas constant −40 °C induced over-resolution and diminished the efficiency of kinetic resolution (entries 12–13). Varying the stoichiometry of sulfenylating reagent 2a also impacted kinetic resolution efficiency, thereby modulating both yield and ee (entry 14). The optimization studies suggest that the transformation proceeds through a sequential mechanism involving enantioselective desymmetrization followed by kinetic resolution.

Substrate scope

With the optimal reaction conditions established, the substrate scope of symmetric 5,10-dihydrophenazasilines was systematically investigated to evaluate the generality of this transformation (Scheme 2). Gratifyingly, a broad range of 5,10-dihydrophenazasilines proved to be compatible with this catalytic system. Initially, keeping the phenyl group attached to the silicon atom constant, the methyl group was successively replaced with ethyl, n-butyl, and isobutyl groups. It was observed that extending the carbon chain led to substantial improvements in both yield and enantioselectivity (3a–3d). The absolute configuration of 3b was determined to be R by X-ray crystallography.11 To our surprise, the substrate 1e with a methoxy group on the long chain only yielded product 3e with 71% yield and 80% ee. The 3,3,3-trifluoropropyl-substituted substrate afforded 3f in 94% yield with 93% ee, with negligible byproduct from kinetic resolution. Furthermore, using a substrate containing a (trimethylsilyl)methyl group, the enantioselectivity of product 3g exceeded 99% ee. Subsequently, substrates bearing different alkyl substituents on the para position of the phenyl ring were investigated. It was found that a p-tolyl group still maintained high enantioselectivity. However, the introduction of a 4-(tert-butyl)phenyl group resulted in a significant erosion of enantioselectivity to 70% ee (3h and 3i). Notably, regardless of whether the para position of the phenyl ring bore electron-donating substituent such as methoxy or electron-withdrawing groups like fluorine or chlorine, the enantioselectivity of the corresponding products remained largely unaffected (3d, 3j–3l). However, a decrease in yield was observed with the methoxy-substituted substrate (3j). When the methoxy group was moved from the para to the meta position, both the yield and enantioselectivity remained essentially unchanged (3m). In contrast, placing electron-withdrawing groups such as fluorine or chlorine at the meta position led to a noticeable decrease in enantioselectivity (3n and 3o). Additionally, substrates featuring multi-substituted phenyl rings were also found to be compatible with this system (3p). Finally, the applicability of various sulfenylating reagents was evaluated. It was observed that when an unsubstituted phenylthio reagent was used, the enantioselectivity of the product decreased markedly (3q). In contrast, introduction of an isopropyl group at the ortho position or a trifluoromethyl group at the para position of the phenyl ring led to a comparatively modest reduction in enantioselectivity (3r and 3s). To our satisfaction, a wide range of sulfenylating reagents proved to be compatible with this asymmetric catalytic system (3t–3w). Furthermore, substrates bearing dialkyl or diaryl groups on the silicon atom failed to afford satisfactory enantioselectivity (3x–3y), indicating their incompatibility with this catalytic system (see the SI for details).

Synthetic applications

To demonstrate the synthetic utility of this transformation, a gram-scale reaction of substrate 1k was initially conducted under standard conditions (Scheme 3a). To our delight, product 3k was obtained in 80% yield with 99% ee. To further showcase the practicality of this method, derivatization studies of the chiral diarylsilane products were carried out (Scheme 3b). Oxidation of 3k with 1.1 equiv. of m-chloroperbenzoic acid (m-CPBA) at −20 °C afforded sulfoxides 5k-1 and 5k-2 in 90% total yield with a diastereomeric ratio of approximately 1 : 1.2. Both isomers retained their original enantioselectivities, and the absolute configuration of 5k-1 was determined by X-ray crystallography.11 When the oxidation was performed with 3.0 equiv. of m-CPBA at 40 °C, sulfone 6k was obtained in 88% yield with 99% ee. Subsequently, to explore the potential of the reaction products as electron donor segments in organic light-emitting diode (OLED) devices, C–N coupling reactions were carried out (see the SI for details).12 Products 7g and 7v were obtained in 56% and 48% yield, respectively, with excellent retention of enantioselectivity. The photophysical properties of 7g and 7v were further investigated. Their UV-Vis spectra exhibited absorbance peaks centered at approximately 285 nm. In toluene solution, both compounds emitted structureless blue light with emission maxima centered at 481 nm. The CD spectra in the region of 290–360 nm for (R/S)-7g and 280–345 nm for (R/S)-7v displayed a bisignate Cotton effect. Compound 7g showed clear mirror-image circularly polarized luminescence (CPL) spectra with a luminescence dissymmetry factor (glum) of up to 7.4 × 10−4. The glum of compound 7v was up to 8.0 × 10−4. The fluorescence quantum yields in toluene solution were determined to be 24% for 7g and 27% for 7v, while in the solid state, the quantum yields were 54% for 7g and 44% for 7v. Our method may provide a new approach for the development of sulfur-containing OLED devices.

Mechanistic study

To further verify the reaction pathway, substrate 1d was subjected to systematic variation in the stoichiometric ratio of sulfenylating reagent 2d at −50 °C (Scheme 4a). Increasing the equivalents of 2d led to a progressive rise in the yield of the disulfenylated byproduct 4s, a concomitant decrease in the yield of the target product 3s, and a steady enhancement in its ee. These observations provide compelling evidence for a cascade process comprising initial asymmetric desymmetrization followed by kinetic resolution—wherein the minor (S)-enantiomer of the product 3s undergoes faster subsequent sulfenylation than the major (R)-enantiomer. This differential reactivity amplifies the overall enantioselectivity of the final product. To further validate the kinetic resolution behavior, rac-3s was subjected to the standard reaction conditions using 0.5 equivalent of 2d. Consistent with our hypothesis, (R)-3s was isolated in 47% yield and 41% ee, while the disulfenylated product 4s was obtained in 50% yield (Scheme 4b). Accordingly, the mechanistic pathway depicted in Scheme 4c is proposed: (i) enantioselective desymmetrization of prochiral 1d affords predominantly (R)-3s alongside a minor amount of (S)-3s; (ii) the latter undergoes preferential sulfenylation to generate disulfenylated product 4s, thereby effecting kinetic resolution and enriching the enantiopurity of the remaining (R)-3s.

DFT calculations

To elucidate the origin of the high enantioselectivity, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed on the reaction pathways at the ωB97X-D/def2-TZVPP-SMD//ωB97X-D/6-31G(d)-IEFPCM level of theory.13 A detailed comparison of the weak interactions in the two transition states was also carried out. Given that the reaction between substrate 1f and reagent 2a yields product 3f with negligible kinetic resolution and involves only a single desymmetrization process, this transformation was chosen as the computational model system. As illustrated in Scheme 5a, the reaction begins with reagent 2a, which reacts with Cat.1 and acid.1 to form intermediate Int-1, concomitantly releasing one equivalent of saccharin. This step involves the formation of a sulfur–sulfur bond between Cat.1 and 2a. Subsequently, Int-1 acts as a sulfur-transfer reagent, reacting with substrate 1f through the transition state TS-1-major (activation barrier = 11.5 kcal mol−1) to yield intermediate Int-2-major and regenerate Cat.1. Int-2-major then undergoes rapid deprotonation to afford the final product (R)-3f. In contrast, the alternative pathway viaTS-1-minor requires overcoming a higher activation barrier (14.2 kcal mol−1). Considering the presence of the background reaction that competes with this catalytic pathway and thereby reduces the overall enantioselectivity to some extent (see the SI for details), the energy difference between TS-1-major and TS-1-minor (ΔΔG‡ = 2.7 kcal mol−1) is reasonable in view of the experimentally observed 93% ee for (R)-3f.

Computational studies on the reaction pathways and the origin of enantioselectivity.

Independent gradient model based on Hirshfeld partitioning (IGMH, Scheme 5b) and atoms-in-molecules (AIM, see the SI) analyses were conducted to characterize the weak interactions present in the two transition states.14 It was found that the transition state is cooperatively stabilized by multiple weak interactions, including hydrogen bonds and C–H⋯O interactions between 1f and acid.1, π–π and C–H⋯F interactions between 1f and the sulfenylating reagent, as well as C–H⋯π interactions between acid.1 and the sulfenylating reagent. In TS-1-major, in addition to the C–H⋯π interaction between the 1-hydrogen of 1f and the catalyst, the system energy is also lowered, to some extent, by three C–H⋯π interactions involving the phenyl group attached to silicon with the catalyst and the sulfenylating reagent. In contrast, in TS-1-minor, the lowering of the system energy is predominantly attributed to the C–H⋯π interaction between the 1-hydrogen of 1f and the catalyst. Although C–H⋯F and van der Waals interactions are present between the trifluoropropyl group and the catalyst, these effects are considerably less pronounced when compared with the C–H⋯π interactions observed in TS-1-major. Moreover, the dihedral angle (C–Si–C–C) was determined to be −175.2° in TS-1-major and 166.3° in TS-1-minor. The greater deviation from 180° in TS-1-minor results in increased torsional distortion, which in turn raises the system energy. Consequently, the combination of weaker noncovalent interactions and enhanced torsional distortion collectively renders TS-1-minor the less favorable transition state. Furthermore, the two transition-state structures for the first step leading to product 3q, which exhibits lower enantioselectivity, were also calculated. The activation barrier difference was determined to be 0.6 kcal mol−1, substantially smaller than the value of 2.7 kcal mol−1 obtained for (R)-3f. This energy gap corresponds to an ee value of approximately 60% (calculated at 223.15 K). Considering the presence of a kinetic resolution pathway, the calculated value is in acceptable agreement with the experimental value of 68%, and the reliability of our computational model is thus supported (see the SI for calculation details).

Conclusions

In summary, we report the first enantioselective C–H sulfenylation strategy for the synthesis of chiral silicon-stereogenic sulfur-containing organosilanes, enabled by a synergistic catalytic system comprising a chiral phosphoric triamide sulfide and an achiral sulfonic acid. The transformation proceeds via sequential desymmetrization and kinetic resolution, affording a wide scope of target compounds in moderate to excellent yields and with moderate to excellent enantioselectivities. DFT calculations provide mechanistic insights into the origin of enantiocontrol and reveal that multiple weak C–H⋯π interactions, together with the torsional distortion experienced by the substrate, serve as the key stereochemical determinants. This work presents an organocatalytic asymmetric strategy for the efficient construction of chiral silicon-stereogenic sulfur-containing organosilanes, while also offering a reference framework for the development of novel strategies in remote stereocontrol.
