Abstract
Advanced non-light-water reactor components may operate at elevated temperature while
experiencing cyclic loading, significant neutron irradiation, and exposure to reactor coolant.
ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code, Section III, Division 5, provides design rules for
elevated-temperature service but does not include specific procedures to account for envi-
ronmental effects on material properties. This report develops an initial framework and
methodology for an Environmental Effects Design Guide (EEDG) focused on neutron irra-
diation; coolant-environment effects are reserved for future work. The proposed approach
treats irradiation as a property-based overlay on the existing Division 5 design process, with
two routes: a sparse-data route applying two reduction factors — FCR on creep-rupture
strength and FF on fatigue life — for the creep-fatigue evaluations that typically control
the design of advanced high-temperature reactor components, and a fuller framework de-
veloping the property-to-rule chain across the four Division 5 checks (primary load, strain
limits and ratcheting, creep-fatigue, and buckling), together with swelling and weldments
as scope items. Both routes are scoped by an in-pile qualification that restricts the use of
post-irradiation-examination-derived properties in regimes where an in-pile mechanism could
control the design outcome. Illustrative outputs derived on a compiled annealed Type 316
database — FCR ≈ 0.78–0.86 and FF ≈ 0.4 — demonstrate the calculation method within
that specific dataset. The framework is an initial, testable design-rule concept; it identifies
a practical path for preliminary design evaluations under sparse data and the material data
and testing needed to develop the framework further.

1 Introduction
Advanced non-light-water reactors expose structural components to combinations of elevated
temperature, cyclic loading, neutron irradiation, and aggressive reactor coolants. These
service conditions can act concurrently and can change the material response that underlies
structural design. A structural-integrity assessment therefore needs a traceable means of
carrying environmentally induced material changes into the design quantities used to evaluate
each relevant failure mode. The present report focuses on neutron irradiation. Coolant-
environment effects are part of the broader Environmental Effects Design Guide (EEDG)
effort but are reserved for future work.
 The U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission has endorsed ASME Boiler and Pressure Ves-
sel Code, Section III, Division 5 as an acceptable design and construction code for high-
temperature reactor components [1]. Division 5 provides the design rules for metallic Class A
components and addresses the principal high-temperature failure modes, including primary-
load failure, excessive deformation and strain accumulation, creep-fatigue damage, and buck-
ling or structural instability. These evaluations depend on allowable stresses, design curves,
strain limits, and material-response models. Division 5 recognizes that service environments
can degrade material performance and assigns responsibility for addressing those effects, but
it does not provide a general irradiation-specific procedure for converting a component ex-
posure history into adjusted material properties and then into the allowables and response
inputs used by the design checks [2]. That missing connection is the central problem ad-
dressed in this report.
 The required treatment cannot be reduced defensibly to one generic irradiation penalty.
Irradiation hardening, for example, may increase yield strength while reducing work-hardening
capacity and ductility. Crediting the strength increase could enlarge a stress limit even
though the material has less capacity to redistribute strain. Irradiation may also reduce
creep-rupture strength, creep-rupture ductility, or fatigue life; irradiation creep may relax
stress while adding inelastic strain; and swelling may be benign when it is spatially uniform
and unconstrained but may produce distortion or load transfer when it is nonuniform or re-
strained. The magnitude and direction of these changes depend on the alloy, product form,
metallurgical condition, irradiation temperature, dose, neutron spectrum, helium produc-
tion, dose rate, test temperature, service time, and loading history. Irradiation adjustments
must therefore be tied to the particular material property that controls an allowable, de-
sign curve, or deformation calculation, and each adjustment must be limited to a defined
applicability domain.
 The EEDG is a coordinated activity of several national laboratories under the U.S. De-
partment of Energy’s Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies program. This
report develops an initial framework and methodology for treating neutron-irradiation effects
as an overlay on the existing Division 5 design process. The intent is to retain the Code’s
failure-mode checks and acceptance criteria wherever practicable and to modify only the ma-
terial properties underlying the design allowables and curves that irradiation can adversely
affect.
 The proposed framework is a traceable sequence: define the material and exposure do-
main; identify the irradiation-sensitive property for the active failure mode; obtain an ir-
radiated property or a conservative, bounded adjustment factor; use it to adjust the corre-
sponding allowable, curve, or model input; and apply the existing Division 5 check. This

ANL-AMMT-030                                 1

property-to-allowable-to-rule connection makes the basis of each change visible and avoids
creating a separate structural-design system for irradiated components.
 The framework also recognizes that irradiation testing is difficult, expensive, and of-
ten constrained by specimen size, dose, temperature, and available hot-cell capabilities. Its
common base-metal evidence is therefore organized around three related property groups
rather than a complete reproduction of the unirradiated Code database: matched tensile
data, complete creep strain–time records through rupture, and fatigue-life data. Tensile
data support the yield and ultimate-strength inputs and separate checks on ductility and
strain hardening. Complete creep records can support creep-rupture strength, creep defor-
mation, tertiary-creep onset, and rupture ductility without requiring unrelated tests for each
derived quantity. Fatigue data support an irradiated design curve or a bounded fatigue-life
adjustment. Where direct irradiated properties are unavailable, conservative factors may be
applied to the corresponding unirradiated property or allowable only when the supporting
data establish a lower-bound relationship over the intended domain. A factor derived for
one property is not assumed to represent a different failure mechanism. Irradiation-creep,
swelling, elastic-property, cyclic-constitutive, buckling-specific, weldment, and in-pile data
are invoked when the component or failure-mode screen shows that they are relevant.
 The proposed framework is an initial, testable design-rule concept; it is not an ASME
Code requirement, a qualified set of irradiation allowables, or a material approval. Nu-
merical examples developed from the available Type 316 database illustrate how irradiation
adjustments can be constructed and evaluated, but they do not establish universal factors or
qualify another material or exposure condition. If available data do not bound the material,
exposure, time, or failure mode being evaluated, the qualified domain must be narrowed
or the affected design branch must be supported by targeted testing or a separately vali-
dated analysis. This treatment is deliberately conservative about missing information: an
unmeasured irradiation effect is not assigned a neutral factor by default.
 The remainder of the report reviews the Division 5 baseline and transferable irradiation
precedents, summarizes the governing degradation mechanisms and available material data,
develops the proposed design-rule concepts, identifies the associated data requirements and
test capabilities, and concludes with the principal limitations and priorities for calibration
and validation. Chapter 2 reviews Division 5 and relevant irradiation precedents; Chapter 3
summarizes the material behavior and data that inform the framework; Chapter 4 develops
the initial design-rule concepts, with a sparse-data creep-fatigue route as the practical initial
design evaluation and a fuller property-to-rule framework for evaluations in which more
irradiated data are available; Chapter 5 identifies the associated data needs and available
test capabilities; and Chapter 6 summarizes the findings and future work.

2.1 Overview and comparison

ASME Section III, Division 5 is the baseline design code for the metallic Class A components
within the scope of this report. The review also considers RCC-MRx and the ITER Struc-

which are fitness-for-service assessment procedures rather than construction codes. The pri-

Hecht [6]. Table 2.1 summarizes the origin and irradiation-related rules of each source.

2.2 ASME Section III, Division 5 (baseline)

Class A elevated-temperature construction — the principal focus of this report — is governed
by Subsection HB, Subpart B of ASME Section III, Division 5, referred to as HBB [2].
The temperature at which the HBB rules must be applied in place of the corresponding
low-temperature rules is 700 ◦ F (370 ◦ C) for ferritic and martensitic steels and 800 ◦ F
(425 ◦ C) for austenitic stainless steels and the listed nickel-base alloys. Because advanced
high-temperature reactor components operate above these boundaries, HBB is the baseline
for the present framework; lower-temperature irradiation concerns are treated only where
they affect the transition into, or the assumptions used by, the HBB evaluations.
 The HBB design-by-analysis approach protects against a defined set of structural failure
modes. Time-independent failure modes include ductile rupture from short-term loading,
gross distortion due to incremental collapse and ratcheting, fatigue failure from cyclic loading,
and buckling under short-term compressive load. Time-dependent failure modes add creep
rupture from sustained loading, creep ratcheting, creep-fatigue failure, and creep buckling.
These failure modes are addressed by four design evaluation procedures: a load-controlled

ANL-AMMT-030                                   3

primary-load check and three deformation-controlled checks — the strain-limits check, the
creep-fatigue check, and the buckling check.
 The primary-load check compares primary stress intensities against allowable stresses
derived from tensile and creep-rupture data. The governing allowables are Sm , a time-
independent allowable stress built from yield and ultimate strengths; St , a time-dependent
allowable taken as the least of 100% of the average stress to reach 1% total strain, 80% of the
minimum stress to initiate tertiary creep, and 67% of the minimum stress to cause rupture,
Sr ; Smt = min(Sm , St ) for Service Level primary-load checks; and So for the Design Condition
primary-load check, which corresponds to the Section II, Part D, Table 1A allowable stress
S — determined from both time-independent (tensile and yield) and time-dependent (creep-
rupture-based) criteria — except in the limited cases at lower temperatures where Smt at
300,000 h exceeds S, in which case So is taken as Smt at 300,000 h (HBB-II-3100).
 The strain-limits check protects against ratcheting due to elevated-temperature cyclic
service. The Code restricts total accumulated inelastic strain over any cross-section of the
wall thickness to 1% averaged through the thickness, 2% at the surface for the equivalent
linear distribution, and 5% locally at any point.
 The creep-fatigue check evaluates the combined effect of cyclic fatiguePand time-dependent
creep damage. Fatigue damage is accumulated as a Miner’s-rule sum (n/Nd )j over cycle
types, where Nd is the allowable number of cycles read from the design fatigue curve at the
appropriate strain range P  and maximum metal temperature. Creep damage is accumulated as
a time-fraction integral (∆t/Td )k over time intervals of constant stress and temperature,
where Td is the allowable duration from the expected minimum stress-to-rupture curve. The
two damage fractions must fall within a material specific bilinear interaction envelope.
 The buckling check guards against structural instability. At elevated temperature the
Code distinguishes load-controlled buckling, characterized by continued application of the
load in the post-buckling regime, from strain-controlled buckling, characterized by immediate
relief of the driving load upon initiation and by self-limiting deformation. In the time-
independent regime the limits are given as load factors for load-controlled buckling and strain
factors for strain-controlled buckling. In the time-dependent regime only load-controlled
buckling requires explicit factors, because strain-controlled loads relax concurrently with the
loss of buckling resistance when creep is significant.
 Collectively, the four checks draw on a defined set of material-specific design parameters:
the allowables Sm , St , Sr , Smt , and So ; thermal-aging factors applied to Sy and Su that feed
Sm and Smt ; isochronous stress–strain curves that link stress, strain, temperature, and time
for the deformation-controlled checks; design fatigue curves generated at strain rates high
enough to suppress creep effects, so that creep damage enters only through the separate
time-fraction integral; the bilinear creep-fatigue interaction envelope; and, for welds, the
stress-rupture factor R. This property-to-allowable structure defines the interfaces at which
irradiation modifications enter the framework developed in Chapter 4: each irradiation-
sensitive underlying property (tensile, creep-rupture, fatigue, ductility) is adjusted, the ex-
isting HBB construction of Sm , St , Sr , and the fatigue and interaction curves is retained,
and the design checks are re-executed against the modified allowables.
 Division 5 acknowledges that service environments can change material behavior but does
not prescribe a general irradiation-specific design method [2]. Prior evaluations by Messner
et al. [7] identify the resulting limitations of Division 5 for the design of advanced-reactor

 ANL-AMMT-030                                   4

components exposed to significant neutron irradiation. HBB-2160 assigns consideration of
service-induced material deterioration to the Owner. HBB-3124 identifies fast-neutron irra-
diation as a possible cause of increased brittle-fracture transition temperature and reduced
upper-shelf fracture resistance in ferritic vessels. HBB-3121 requires an allowance for envi-
ronmental thinning. HBB-Y-4200 states that adverse coolant or irradiation effects on the
air-based material data and derived allowables must be characterized and that the Owner or
Operator must demonstrate that those effects are adequately addressed. These provisions
establish the need for an irradiation technical basis but do not provide guidance.
 Division 5 also provides two useful precedents for managing long-term property uncer-
tainty. HAA-1130(d) states that changes in properties caused by neutron radiation may
be checked periodically through material surveillance. In addition, notes associated with
the long-time yield- and tensile-strength reduction factors in HBB-II-2100 and HBB-II-2200,
and notes associated with weld strength-reduction factors, direct the designer to consider
further reductions for longer lives and identify enhanced surveillance or inspection as possible
measures [2].

2.3 UK R5

R5 [8] is a UK fitness-for-service procedure for assessing the high-temperature response
and continued operation of existing structures. It does not provide rules to account for
environmental degradation.
 The R5 concept most relevant to the EEDG is ductility exhaustion for the creep part
of creep-fatigue damage. In this formulation, the creep-damage increment is related to
accumulated creep strain divided by the multiaxial creep ductility available at the applicable
stress and temperature, rather than solely to elapsed time divided by rupture time [9]. This
makes an irradiation-induced loss of creep ductility enter the damage calculation directly.
The concept is physically attractive for materials in which irradiation reduces creep ductility
strongly, but it requires qualified creep-strain histories and irradiated multiaxial ductility
information that are rarely available. For that reason, the initial EEDG treats ductility
exhaustion as a refined qualification route, not as the minimum-data default.

2.4 French RCC-MRx

RCC-MRx contains an explicit screening structure for irradiation [10]. RB 3216.2 permits
irradiation effects to be neglected when either the whole-life fluence is below the temperature-
dependent negligible-irradiation curve in Appendix A3.32 or a cumulative fluence fraction
is no greater than unity. If that test is not satisfied, RB 3216.3 or RB 3216.4 selects the
significant-irradiation rules according to whether thermal creep is negligible or significant.
Those rules may be used only below the maximum irradiation limits in Appendix A3.33.
The code distinguishes type P damage, associated with load-controlled failure, from type S
damage, which includes progressive deformation and fatigue or creep-fatigue.
 For type P damage with significant irradiation and negligible creep, RB 3251.2 retains
the applicable unirradiated checks and adds limits based on irradiation-dependent mem-
brane and total elastic allowable stresses, Sem and Set , together with the irradiated tensile

ANL-AMMT-030                                  5

stress–strain curve and fracture-resistance input. For significant creep combined with signifi-
cant irradiation, RB 3252.2 applies only to the specified solution-annealed or work-hardened
austenitic stainless steels, below the Appendix A3.33 maximum dose and below 625 ◦ C, and
requires the type P usage-fraction limits U and W to be 0.1 rather than 1.0. These provi-
sions show two possible design strategies: reconstruct irradiation-dependent properties and
allowables when sufficient data exist, or apply a conservative fixed margin within a tightly
bounded material and exposure domain.
 For type S damage, RB 3261.2 requires significant-irradiation progressive-deformation
evaluations to use irradiated material properties; use of unirradiated properties is allowed
only when conservatism is justified, with particular attention to loss of ductility. The fatigue
evaluation uses an irradiated fatigue curve, although the 2015 code permits the unirradiated
curve as a fallback. For the limited austenitic-stainless-steel domain with both significant
creep and significant irradiation, RB 3262.2 requires the creep-rupture usage fraction W to
be multiplied by 10. The code text reviewed for this report does not provide a mechanistic
derivation for the factor of 10. The EEDG adopts the RCC-MRx architecture — expo-
sure screening, irradiated properties where needed, and bounded factors where full data are
unavailable — but does not directly take over the RCC-MRx numerical factors.

2.5 ITER Structural Design Criteria

The ITER Structural Design Criteria (ISDC) [11] include material allowables and damage
provisions that depend explicitly on neutron fluence. The criteria classify irradiation-induced
creep, irradiation-induced swelling, and irradiation-induced changes in material properties as
separate damage types in IRB 2151, IRB 2152, and IRB 2153, respectively; elastic follow-up
is addressed in IRB 2161. This separation is useful to the EEDG because it distinguishes
changes to conventional strength and ductility inputs from irradiation strains that must be
included in the structural response.
 IRB 2723 constructs the time-independent allowable stress intensity Sm from both unir-
radiated and irradiated minimum tensile and yield strengths as functions of temperature and
fluence. Retaining the unirradiated candidates prevents irradiation hardening from increas-
ing Sm . IRB 2724 defines Se using irradiated ultimate strength, uniform elongation, Young’s
modulus, and elastic follow-up to protect against immediate plastic-flow localization. IRB
2725 defines Sd using irradiated ultimate strength, true rupture strain, Young’s modulus,
triaxiality, and elastic follow-up to protect against local ductile fracture. These rules connect
irradiation-sensitive tensile properties to the ITER allowable-stress hierarchy; adopting the
full construction would require more irradiated tensile characterization than the minimum
EEDG screening route provides.
 IRB 2726 defines the time-dependent allowable St as the least of three independently
based quantities: two-thirds of the minimum rupture strength, 80% of the minimum stress
for initiation of tertiary creep, and the minimum stress that produces a specified creep strain
in time t. The strain target for the third quantity is the smaller of 1% and one-fifth of the
minimum creep-rupture ductility. Thus, when irradiation reduces creep ductility below 5%,
the allowable strain target is reduced below the conventional 1% value. This rule provides
a defensible physical connection between irradiated rupture ductility and a deformation-
controlled contribution to St . The EEDG’s FCR -based sparse-data route in Section 4.2 uses

 ANL-AMMT-030                                   6

a single creep-rupture-strength factor as a screening approximation; the fuller framework in
Section 4.3 preserves the three separate contributions to St and modifies each on its own
irradiated response.
 For ratcheting, IRB 3311 provides elastic routes based on a 3Sm limit or a Bree-type
diagram. The diagram uses yield strength averaged over the minimum and maximum tem-
perature and fluence states in the cycle. IRB 3312 provides an inelastic route based on
accumulated plastic strain and fluence- and temperature-dependent tensile ductility. The
ITER approach therefore supports using irradiated tensile properties when they reduce the
shakedown margin or the strain capacity. In the simplified EEDG route, irradiation hard-
ening is not credited: the yield strength used to normalize the existing HBB shakedown
diagram is capped at the corresponding unirradiated value, and retained tensile ductility or
strain-hardening capacity is checked separately. Under those two restrictions, an additional
modified shakedown diagram is not automatically required. If the ductility screen fails, a
low-ductility rule or qualified inelastic analysis is required. The reviewed ITER document
does not provide a time-dependent fatigue procedure, so it cannot by itself supply the EEDG
creep-fatigue rule.

2.6 Japanese ETSDG literature (Monju and 316FR)

The original Japanese ETSDG documents were not directly reviewed for this report. This
section is based on Iida et al. [3], which describes the construction rules developed for the
Monju prototype fast breeder reactor, and Asayama et al. [4], which describes evaluation
procedures for irradiation and sodium-environment effects and their extension from SUS304

exposure limit with bounded irradiation adjustments to creep life and creep rate. They do
not establish a generally applicable rule for other materials, neutron spectra, or exposure

 Asayama et al. [4] report post-irradiation tensile tests on 316FR at 673–923 K to fast-
neutron fluence of 1.1 × 1026 n/m2 and creep tests at 773–923 K, with rupture times to
4.5 × 103 h, fast fluence of 5 × 1022 to 5 × 1025 n/m2 , and thermal fluence of 9 × 1021 to
2 × 1024 n/m2 . Within that database, irradiation increased yield strength, had little effect
on ultimate tensile strength, and reduced fracture elongation. The ratio of irradiated to
unirradiated creep life ranged from 0.07 to 0.84, and the ratio of irradiated to unirradiated
steady-state creep rate ranged from 0.66 to 4.18. The spread demonstrates why a property
factor must be a lower-bound envelope over a qualified domain rather than a best-fit average
or a universal constant.
 The procedure described by Asayama et al. [4], summarized in Figure 2.1, first limits
accumulated fast-neutron fluence Φf (E > 0.1 MeV) to a temperature-dependent allowable
Φallowed (T ) chosen so that a minimum tensile fracture elongation of 10% is maintained.
Within that ductility-bounded envelope, no additional irradiation treatment is prescribed
when thermal creep is negligible. When creep is significant, the procedure applies a thermal-
fluence-dependent creep-life reduction factor ft bounded by 3 and a steady-state creep-rate
increase factor kt bounded by 2, and modifies the creep life and creep rate accordingly.
No tensile-strength reduction factor was required for the particular 316FR data because
tensile strength was not degraded. Asayama et al. also note that the equivalence of post-

2.7 U.S. RDT core-component criteria

The original RDT F9-7, F9-8, and F9-9 documents [12] were not available for direct review.
This section is limited to the draft criteria described by Nelson [5] and the FFTF application

F9-8 as Guidelines for Analysis, and F9-9 as the supporting Rationale, aimed at short-
life replaceable fast-reactor core structural components made principally from annealed and
20% cold-worked Type 316 stainless steel. The draft criteria used temperature- and fluence-
dependent yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, elongation, creep, and fatigue properties,

 ANL-AMMT-030                                  8

with nominal values reduced where the irradiation database was limited. For primary mem-
brane loading, the draft criteria used irradiated yield and ultimate strengths and used the

 The draft RDT criteria also included a modified shakedown diagram for highly irradiated

problems during the FFTF application. The 1% transition is therefore a historical rule from
a particular core-component program, not a validated universal threshold.

2.8 Synthesis: what is transferable to the EEDG

The reviewed sources support a common architecture rather than a common set of numerical
irradiation factors. The existing Division 5 stress classifications, acceptance equations, and
design margins can remain the structural framework. Irradiation is introduced through the
material properties and allowables used by those equations, together with separate treatment
of irradiation strains when swelling or irradiation creep is relevant. Every adjusted property
or factor should be qualified over a stated material, product form and condition, temperature,
dose, spectrum or helium production, test mode, and service-time range.
 For the primary-load check, tensile data determine the time-independent strength inputs
and creep records determine the time-dependent inputs. The minimum-data route uses a
lower-bound tensile factor to reduce Sm when irradiation decreases the controlling tensile
strength, but caps the factor at unity so that irradiation hardening cannot increase the allow-
able. It uses a separate lower-bound time-dependent factor to reduce Sr and, provisionally,
St . Applying one factor to St is defensible only when the available creep records show that it
bounds the relevant deformation and rupture response; otherwise the creep-strain, tertiary-
creep, and rupture legs must be qualified separately. Smt and So then follow the existing
Division 5 selection logic rather than receiving independent irradiation factors. Irradiated
tensile and rupture elongations are retained as applicability and deformation screens because
strength factors alone do not protect against loss of ductility.
 For the strain-limits and ratcheting check, irradiation can increase yield strength while de-
creasing strain-hardening capacity and ductility. Crediting only the increased yield strength
would enlarge the normalized shakedown region without demonstrating that the material
can sustain the required redistribution. The screening rule therefore uses the lower of ir-
radiated and unirradiated yield strength and separately verifies retained tensile ductility or
strain hardening. With both controls, the existing HBB shakedown diagram can be retained.
Failure of the ductility screen invokes a refined low-ductility rule or inelastic analysis; the
modified diagram in the draft RDT criteria is a useful historical precedent for that branch,
but its numerical boundaries are not adopted without validation.
 For creep-fatigue, both damage coordinates use check-specific irradiation adjustments

ANL-AMMT-030                                   9

when irradiation is significant. Allowable fatigue cycles are obtained from an irradiation-
qualified design curve or a conservative lower-bound fatigue-life factor over a stated domain.
Creep damage is evaluated using the irradiation-adjusted rupture relation and a consistent
stress-relaxation history. The existing Division 5 bilinear interaction envelope is retained
provisionally on the basis of the analysis presented in Section 3.2 (Figure 3.7), in which
unirradiated and post-irradiation (Dc , Df ) point clouds for 316 SA at 700 ◦ C occupy com-
parable positions relative to the ASME envelope once the FCR and FF coordinate reductions
are applied. R5 ductility exhaustion remains a refined option when adequate irradiated
creep-ductility and strain-history data exist.
 Swelling, irradiation creep, elastic-modulus change, weld behavior, and buckling-property
changes require additional evaluation only when a component-specific screening check shows
that the mechanism can affect the governing limit state. The overall approach follows the
practical lesson common to the reviewed precedents: begin with exposure screening and a
small matched property dataset, use conservative bounded factors where they are demon-
strably adequate, and escalate to direct irradiated curves or constitutive models only when
the simplified rule cannot bound the response. Chapter 4 develops these concepts as initial
design rules; they are not qualified Code allowables.

3.1 Governing degradation mechanisms and operative regimes

Neutron irradiation can alter the mechanical and dimensional behavior of structural alloys
through two primary physical processes: displacement damage and nuclear transmutation.
This section identifies the principal degradation mechanisms produced by these processes
and the variables governing their relevance. It is intended to support mechanism screening
for the proposed EEDG framework, rather than to provide a material-by-material review or
establish universal irradiation limits.
 Energy transferred from an incident neutron to a lattice atom may exceed the displace-
ment threshold, creating a primary knock-on atom and, at sufficiently high recoil energy,
a displacement cascade containing vacancies and self-interstitial atoms. The accumulated
displacement exposure is conventionally expressed in displacements per atom (dpa). Dpa
is a calculated exposure metric; it does not equal the concentration of defects that survive
cascade recombination and does not uniquely determine the resulting microstructure or ma-
terial response. The effects associated with a given dpa depend on irradiation temperature,
dose rate, neutron spectrum, alloy composition, initial microstructure, applied stress, and
irradiation history.
 Nuclear transmutation changes the elemental and isotopic composition of the material.
The resulting transmutation products depend strongly on the neutron-energy spectrum and
the reaction cross sections of the alloying elements. Helium is of particular interest because
it has low solubility in metals, can accumulate at defects and grain boundaries, and may
contribute to swelling, reduced ductility, or high-temperature intergranular embrittlement
under applicable conditions.
 In a nonthermalized fast spectrum, helium is produced primarily through threshold (n,α)
reactions. Nickel is an important contributor to this production in commercial structural
alloys. Figure 3.1 shows predicted helium concentrations for a range of commercial steels
irradiated to approximately 5 dpa in EBR-II, which had essentially no thermal-neutron
component. Under these specific conditions, the predicted helium concentration increased
approximately linearly with nickel content, indicating that nickel dominated threshold helium
production across the alloys considered [13].
 The trend in Figure 3.1 is specific to the EBR-II spectrum and the stated exposure. It
should not be interpreted as a universal relationship between nickel content, dpa, and helium
concentration. In thermal or mixed neutron spectra, the two-step 58 Ni(n,γ)59 Ni(n,α)56 Fe se-
quence can become an important helium source. Helium accumulation through this pathway
is nonlinear with exposure because 59 Ni must first be produced, and the subsequent 59 Ni(n,α)

ANL-AMMT-030                                11

reaction has a large cross section at low neutron energies. Consequently, transfer of irra-
diation data between reactors requires a spectrum-dependent reaction-rate calculation or
another qualified basis for relating displacement damage and helium production.

 The material changes produced by displacement damage and transmutation can be
grouped into several degradation mechanisms relevant to structural design:

  Radiation-induced hardening and associated reductions in strain-hardening capacity,
 ductility, or toughness;
  Irradiation creep and, where applicable, irradiation growth;
  Void and gas-bubble swelling;
  High-temperature helium-related embrittlement; and
  Radiation-induced segregation and phase instability, including irradiation-induced or
 irradiation-accelerated precipitation.

 These mechanisms are not independent and may operate concurrently. For example,
radiation-induced segregation and precipitation can change strength and ductility, while he-
lium can interact with vacancies and microstructural sinks and thereby influence swelling

 ANL-AMMT-030                                12

or grain-boundary degradation. Irradiation creep can alter stress redistribution while con-
tributing to accumulated deformation. The relative importance of the mechanisms therefore
cannot be inferred from dose alone.
 The principal variables governing mechanism relevance include alloy composition and
microstructure, product form and fabrication history, irradiation temperature, dose and
dose rate, neutron spectrum, helium concentration or helium-to-dpa ratio, applied stress, and
irradiation and thermal histories. Test temperature and the time elapsed between irradiation
and testing can also affect the measured retained properties. These variables define the
material and exposure domain over which an irradiated property, correlation, or adjustment
factor may be applied.
 Homologous temperature, expressed as T /Tm using absolute temperatures, provides a
useful first-order basis for organizing the approximate temperature regimes in which different
irradiation mechanisms may become important. Figure 3.2 presents a qualitative screening
map developed from the mechanism summaries of Zinkle, Tanigawa, and Wirth [14] and
Sham [15]. The map is intended to identify mechanisms that require further evaluation; it
does not establish material acceptance limits or universal temperature or dose thresholds.

helium production, applied stress, and exposure history can shift the apparent mechanism
boundaries. The figure should therefore be used only as an initial screening tool. The
applicability and significance of each mechanism must ultimately be established using data
or qualified models for the specific material and exposure domain being evaluated.
 The purpose of mechanism screening in the EEDG is to identify the material properties
and structural-response quantities that may require irradiation adjustment. The mechanisms
themselves do not enter the Division 5 calculations as independent damage terms unless the
selected analysis method explicitly represents them. Instead, their effects are carried into the
design evaluation through the properties, design curves, deformation relations, or imposed
strain fields associated with the applicable limit state.
 Irradiated yield and ultimate tensile strengths affect the time-independent material resis-
tance used in primary-load evaluations. Yield strength also enters the stress normalization or
pseudo-yield quantities used by some Division 5 strain-limits methods. Under the proposed
irradiation overlay, a reduction in strength is included where supported by the applicable
data, but irradiation-induced hardening is not credited to increase an allowable stress or
enlarge an acceptable shakedown region.
 Tensile stress-strain behavior, uniform elongation, total elongation, and retained strain-
hardening capacity provide information about deformation localization and whether the
selected strain-limits or inelastic-analysis method remains applicable to the irradiated ma-
terial. These ductility quantities are evaluated separately from the primary-load strength
calculation. An increase in yield strength does not demonstrate that the material retains
adequate ductility or capacity for strain redistribution.
 Time-dependent material resistance requires several distinct creep properties. Creep
strain-time behavior and the initiation of tertiary creep contribute to the construction of St ,
while creep-rupture strength contributes to the rupture branch of St , the stress-to-rupture
relation Sr , and creep-damage calculations. Creep-rupture ductility is a separate property
relevant to the applicability of creep-fatigue methods and, where sufficient data are available,
to ductility-exhaustion evaluations. A measured change in rupture strength does not by itself
establish the corresponding changes in creep deformation, tertiary-creep initiation, or creep
ductility.
 Fatigue data affect the irradiated fatigue curve or a qualified fatigue-life adjustment.
Irradiated creep-rupture and fatigue-life data modify the creep- and fatigue-damage coordi-
nates, respectively, but they do not by themselves establish whether irradiation changes the
interaction between creep and fatigue damage. Retention or modification of the Division 5
creep-fatigue interaction envelope must therefore be considered separately.
 Irradiation creep is treated as a stress-, temperature-, and flux-dependent inelastic-strain
contribution rather than as a strength allowable. When non-negligible, it can affect accumu-
lated strain, stress relaxation, stress redistribution, creep-fatigue damage, and potentially
time-dependent structural stability. It should not be added separately when it is already
represented in the material relation or constitutive model used in the analysis.
 Free, spatially uniform swelling produces dimensional change but does not, by itself, con-
stitute accumulated plastic or creep strain for comparison with the Division 5 strain limits.
Swelling may nevertheless affect component dimensions, clearances, contact conditions, ge-
ometry, load paths, and functional performance. When swelling is spatially nonuniform or
mechanically constrained, it can generate stresses and associated plastic, thermal-creep, or

 ANL-AMMT-030                                  14

irradiation-creep strains. Those mechanically induced stresses and strains must be included
in the applicable structural evaluation.
 Changes in elastic modulus, Poisson’s ratio, thermal expansion, thermal conductivity, or
other physical properties are conditional inputs. They require explicit irradiation adjustment
when a mechanism screen or sensitivity analysis shows that the changes could materially af-
fect the calculated stresses, strains, temperatures, load redistribution, or buckling resistance.
Similarly, radiation-induced segregation, precipitation, and phase instability are represented
in the design evaluation only to the extent that the qualified irradiated properties and models
bound the relevant microstructural, irradiation, and thermal histories.
 These property-to-check connections form the basis of the proposed EEDG framework.
They do not imply that every irradiation mechanism can be represented by a single generic
reduction factor. Each adjustment must be tied to the property or response quantity used
by the applicable Division 5 check and limited to a defined material and exposure domain.
Section 3.2 examines the available post-irradiation mechanical-property evidence and uses
the comparatively extensive Type 316 literature to demonstrate how matched irradiated and
unirradiated data can be expressed as property-specific ratios. Section 3.3 then evaluates the
additional limitations that arise when post-irradiation data are used to represent behavior
under continuing neutron flux.

The literature compilation used in this report draws on the mechanical-property data and

3.3 In-pile versus post-irradiation creep

The most important open question of the EEDG is whether post-irradiation creep data
conservatively bound in-pile creep behavior. The compiled literature does not give a con-
sistent answer. Three datasets frame the question. Lovell, Chin, and Gilbert [19], using
pressurized-tube specimens of 20% cold-worked AISI 316 in the Experimental Breeder Re-
actor II (EBR-II), concluded that the in-pile rupture lives of 20% CW AISI 316 exceeded
the rupture times predicted from post-irradiation tests, and were equal to or greater than
the unirradiated rupture times at the same conditions for times up to approximately 7,000
h. Lovell et al. attributed the longer in-pile rupture life to dynamic point-defect generation
during irradiation, which is absent during post-irradiation testing. The ordering they report
is in-pile > unirradiated > post-irradiated.
 Ukai, Mizuta, Kaito, and Okada [23], using pressurized-tube specimens of 20% CW mod-
ified 316 stainless steel in the Materials Open Test Assembly (MOTA) in the Fast Flux Test
Facility (FFTF), reached the opposite conclusion: in-pile rupture lives were shorter than
the out-of-reactor rupture lives at the same conditions, and in-pile creep strain rate was
significantly accelerated. The ordering they report is in-pile < unirradiated.
 The direct contradiction between Lovell et al. [19] and Ukai et al. [23] — both on 20%
cold-worked variants of 316, both using pressurized-tube in-pile tests, but reaching opposite
conclusions about the ordering of in-pile and out-of-reactor rupture life — is the specific
empirical evidence that the question is not resolved in the literature. The two studies
differ in alloy composition (with PNC316 having controlled minor-element additions), in
reactor facility (EBR-II vs. FFTF), and in test-temperature window; each of these differences
plausibly contributes to the opposite conclusion. Neither study, however, gives a general rule
for when in-pile behavior is bounded by post-irradiation behavior.
 The most systematic analysis of in-pile creep-rupture data available in the open literature
is that of Wassilew et al. [21]. They complied tests performed on pressurized-tube specimens
of the stabilized austenitic stainless steels 1.4970, 1.4981, and 1.4988 and on the nickel-
base alloy Hastelloy-X, using the Mol-2 irradiation rig in the BR-2 reactor at Mol, Belgium
(maximum fast fluence 0.70 × 1026 n/m2 [E > 0.1 MeV], maximum helium content 140
appm, irradiation temperatures 615–720 ◦ C) and the MONIX experiment in the Materials
Open Test Assembly at the Fast Flux Test Facility (maximum fast fluence 1.01 × 1027 n/m2 ,
maximum helium content 20 appm, irradiation temperatures 605–670 ◦ C). Specimens were
tested in the cold-worked, cold-worked-and-aged, solution-annealed, and solution-annealed-
and-aged metallurgical conditions.
 When the in-pile creep rate is plotted against equivalent stress on log-log axes at each
test temperature, an abrupt change in slope occurs at a stress that Wassilew et al. call

 The physical picture that Wassilew et al. develop to account for these observations is the
following. During irradiation, displacement events continuously produce Frenkel pairs and,
via (n,α) transmutation of nickel and iron, helium atoms. The same displacement events
also cause dynamic resolution of helium from pre-existing bubbles and clusters, releasing
helium back into the matrix as free atoms. The free helium migrates in the interstitial
mode at low temperature (low activation energy) and as vacancy-helium complexes at high
temperature (higher activation energy), and a net helium flux ends up at grain boundaries.
On boundaries oriented perpendicular to the applied stress the helium supply drives the
nucleation and growth of stable helium bubbles; once the critical bubble radius r c for the
corresponding applied stress is attained, unstable growth and coalescence produce a rapidly
propagating intergranular crack and rupture occurs. The rate-controlling step is the stable-
bubble-growth stage supplied by the dynamic-resolution helium flux, not the initial helium
generation itself. This mechanism is not operative in post-irradiation tests: after the reactor
is shut off, dynamic resolution ceases; the helium remaining in the material is bound in
existing bubbles and clusters and can only be supplied to grain boundaries by classical
thermal diffusion. The rate-controlling process for post-irradiation creep rupture is therefore
fundamentally different from that for in-pile creep rupture, and — as Wassilew et al. observe
— the stress and temperature dependences of the two are different. Wassilew et al. [21]
conclude explicitly that the use of unirradiated and post-irradiation data to predict in-pile
creep and rupture is invalid for the four materials they studied.
 For the EEDG, the practical implication of the Wassilew et al. [21] dataset is threefold.
First, the unified low-stress form t R σˆ(-5/2) / θ Rm(T) is a plausible template for the in-
pile creep-rupture correlation that would underlie an in-pile-data-based strength-reduction
factor in the primary-load and creep-fatigue checks; if in-pile data on the alloys of interest
confirm the Wassilew structure, the correlation collapses to a small set of material-specific
constants. Second, the systematic in-pile-versus-post-irradiation life gap that Wassilew et
al. document — with the in-pile rupture life shorter than the post-irradiation rupture life
at the design-relevant low-stress conditions — argues against a framework in which post-
irradiation data alone are treated as bounding for in-pile behavior below the transition
stress. Third, because direct in-pile mechanical testing of the alloys of interest is not always
practical, the framework could include an explicit design margin for the sub-transition-stress
regime, supplemented by in-service material surveillance to close the gap over the design life.
This is the middle-ground strategy developed in Section 5.4.

4 Initial Design-Rule Concepts
This chapter proposes an initial method for incorporating neutron-irradiation effects into
the ASME Section III, Division 5, Class A design checks. The intent is not to replace
the Division 5 procedures or to reproduce the full Code material-property database under
irradiation. It is to keep the existing checks in place and change only the material inputs
— allowables, curves, and deformation properties — that irradiation can adversely affect.
Each change is expressed as a property-to-rule chain: an irradiated property, or a conservative
irradiation factor, is used to obtain an adjusted allowable or analysis input, and that adjusted
quantity is then used in the existing Division 5 check.
 The rules are simplified in their data requirements, but the simplification is not achieved
by treating different material responses as interchangeable. Tensile strength, creep-rupture
strength, creep deformation, fatigue life, ductility, irradiation creep, and swelling enter differ-
ent parts of the design evaluation. A property may be transferred by a common conservative
factor only when the supporting data show that the factor bounds the specific response being
adjusted over a stated applicability domain.
 Section 4.1 states the proposed irradiation overlay and the exposure and applicability
screens that gate its use. Section 4.2 develops the practical design case: a sparse-data
reduction-factor route for the creep-fatigue-controlled evaluations that typically controls the
design of advanced high-temperature reactor components. Section 4.3 develops the full
framework across the four Division 5 checks, plus swelling and weldments as scope items, for
evaluations in which more than the minimum irradiated database is available. Section 4.4
addresses in-pile behavior as a scope-and-applicability issue that can violate the assump-
tions used in Sections 4.2 and 4.3. Section 4.5 summarizes the chapter and presents the
consolidated design flow.

4.1 Proposed irradiation overlay

Proposed rule. When irradiation at a component location is significant and the exposure re-
mains within a qualified material domain, the following changes are applied to the Division 5
evaluation:
 1. Time-independent primary-load check is based on irradiated tensile strength or on a
 conservative tensile-strength factor. Irradiation hardening is not credited to increase
 an allowable.
 2. Time-dependent primary-load check is based on irradiated creep-rupture and creep-
 deformation data, or on conservative factors derived from complete creep records.
 3. The existing HBB strain-limits methods are retained, but use a capped yield input,
 irradiation-adjusted time-dependent properties, a separate irradiated-ductility screen,
 and any non-negligible irradiation-creep or constrained-swelling strain.
 4. The creep-fatigue calculation retains the HBB damage form; both damage fractions
 use irradiation-adjusted properties.
 5. Buckling is evaluated with irradiation-adjusted material or geometric inputs only when
 the governing instability mode is sensitive to those changes.

  6. Swelling is treated as a material eigenstrain. It first enters dimensional and functional
 checks; when constrained or spatially nonuniform, it enters the structural analysis

  7. Weldments and in-pile behavior are treated as scope-and-applicability issues rather
 than as universal numerical penalties.

 Exposure screening. Three outcomes are possible at each evaluated location. If the total

design, life, or material must change. Between those limits, the overlay above is applied.

4.2 Practical design case: sparse-data reduction factors for the creep-fatigue check

Proposed rule. Advanced high-temperature reactor components are, in most locations of
interest, controlled by the HBB creep-fatigue check. When the irradiated database for a
candidate material is limited to a handful of matched conditions, the initial design evaluation
should not attempt to reconstruct the complete irradiated allowable set. It should instead:

 1. Identify the two properties that drive the creep-fatigue outcome: the creep-rupture
 strength (which controls the allowable time entering the creep damage fraction) and
 the fatigue life at the applicable strain range and temperature (which controls the
 allowable cycles entering the fatigue damage fraction).

 2. Derive a conservative lower-bound reduction factor for each of these two properties
 over a narrowly defined applicability domain drawn from the available data.

 3. Apply those two factors to the corresponding unirradiated HBB inputs, evaluate the
 check in the usual way, and accept the result only if the qualified applicability domain
 encloses the design condition.

 If the design condition falls outside the applicability domain for either factor, the affected
component region is not qualified by the sparse-data route. The design must change to fall
within the domain, or targeted irradiated testing must be performed. Missing data are not
assigned a value of one.
 The two factors. The creep-rupture strength reduction factor FCR (T, t, χ) is the ratio of
the irradiated rupture stress to the unirradiated rupture stress at the same temperature and
rupture time, over the applicability domain χ. It adjusts the allowable rupture time used in
the creep damage fraction of the creep-fatigue check. Because the stress–time relationship is
nonlinear, FCR is not multiplied directly into an allowable time: the adjusted rupture relation
is used, and the allowable time is read from it at the calculated stress and temperature.

 ANL-AMMT-030                                   30

(The same FCR also adjusts Sr on the primary-load side of the check; that use is described
in Section 4.3.1.)
 The fatigue-life reduction factor FF (∆ε, T, χ) is the ratio of irradiated cycles-to-failure
to unirradiated cycles-to-failure at the same strain range, temperature, and applicability
domain. It adjusts the allowable cycles: Ndirr = FF · Nd . Because the underlying fatigue-life-
versus-strain-range curves for irradiated and unirradiated material can have different slopes,
a single scalar FF is defensible only over a bounded strain-range window.
 Unirradiated interaction envelope is retained. Retention of the unirradiated HBB material-
specific envelope is supported by the interaction-envelope comparison performed on the an-
nealed Type 316 database (Section 3.2, Figure 3.7). Within that dataset the post-irradiation
and unirradiated (Dc , Df ) points occupy comparable positions relative to the ASME bilin-
ear envelope once the FCR and FF reductions are applied, so no significant additional effect
of irradiation on the creep-fatigue interaction is observed. Retention here is therefore an
evidence-supported initial screening choice, consistent with the damage fraction adjustment-
only treatment in RCC-MRx framework.
 The scope of that support is limited. The comparison covers one alloy, one metallurgical
condition, and one temperature. Retention is therefore an initial screening choice, not a
demonstration that irradiation leaves the interaction physics unchanged in general.
 Illustrative example — annealed Type 316. The construction of FCR and FF is demon-
strated in Section 3.2 on a compiled annealed Type 316 database. Two representative out-
comes are:

 Extension to a ductility-exhaustion creep damage measure. For evaluations that use a
ductility-exhaustion form of the creep damage measure, the same principle applies. The two
irradiation-sensitive inputs become the irradiated creep-rupture ductility (which replaces
the rupture-strength role of FCR in defining the creep damage increment) and the irradiated
fatigue life (unchanged). A ductility reduction factor Fε (T, χ) is defined analogously as the

 Technical basis and limits. This route is defensible as an initial design evaluation be-
cause it acts on the two properties that actually govern the creep-fatigue outcome, over a
documented applicability domain, and it inherits the existing Code hierarchy and interaction
envelope for everything else. It is not defensible when the qualified domain does not enclose
the design condition, when the ductility screen fails, and when creep-fatigue does not control
the design. Section 4.3 provides the fuller property-to-rule framework for those cases and for
evaluations in which more than the minimum irradiated database is available.

irradiation-induced contributions to the calculated accumulated inelastic strain when they

  1. Use the capped irradiated yield strength (defined in Section 4.3.1) wherever the selected

  2. Include irradiation-creep strain in the accumulated inelastic strain when it is non-

 Yield-strength cap. The capped yield input is one-sided by design. Irradiation hardening

 Creep-deformation input. The specific deformation property used depends on the HBB

 Irradiation creep and constrained swelling. Irradiation creep is a contribution to the

the mechanically induced plastic, thermal-creep, and irradiation-creep strains it produces
enter the strain-limits calculation.

This subsection extends the sparse-data creep-fatigue treatment of Section 4.2 for evaluations
in which more than the two-factor minimum irradiated database is available.
 Proposed rule. Both damage fractions of the HBB creep-fatigue evaluation use irradiation-

rather than combined into a single environmental factor, and because it avoids an irradiated
interaction matrix unless interaction controls. It is not defensible when the unirradiated
fatigue or rupture basis is used unchanged in a significant-irradiation domain without evi-
dence that it bounds the irradiated response, or when beneficial irradiation-creep relaxation
is credited without the associated strain and load redistribution.

4.3.4 Buckling and structural-instability check
Proposed rule. The HBB buckling categories and existing load and strain factors are re-
tained. The material and geometric inputs to the selected instability mode are replaced with
irradiated values or conservative bounds only when irradiation can materially change the
result. A complete set of irradiation-specific buckling charts is not an initial requirement.
 Different inputs matter for different modes: yield strength and stress–strain shape for
inelastic instability; elastic modulus and tangent stiffness for elastic and nonlinear instabil-
ity; thermal and irradiation creep for time-dependent deformation; and swelling, growth,
bowing, ovalization, or contact evolution for the service geometry. A reduction in Sm or St
alone does not necessarily bound these effects, so the input actually used by the selected
calculation is what gets adjusted. Irradiation hardening is not credited to increase buck-
ling resistance. If the calculation depends on post-yield curve shape, an irradiation-qualified
expected-minimum curve or a conservative envelope is used. The service geometry used for
buckling represents the design-life condition when irradiation-induced deformation can affect
stability; free uniform swelling is not automatically an imperfection, but spatial variation,
constraint, and the resulting geometry changes are.
 Technical basis and limits. This structure ties additional data to the sensitivity of the

 ANL-AMMT-030                                     36

governing instability mode instead of requiring every irradiated elastic, constitutive, and ge-
ometric property for every component. Existing HBB exemption charts or simplified meth-
ods remain usable when the screening evaluations show that irradiation does not adversely
change the controlling inputs beyond what the method already bounds; otherwise, a direct
time-dependent stability analysis or an irradiation-specific chart is used.

4.3.5 Swelling, dimensional stability, and load transfer
Proposed rule. No universal allowable swelling percentage is established. Three component-
specific evaluations are made:

  1. Calculate the design-life swelling or irradiation-growth field from a qualified material
 and exposure correlation.

  2. Compare the resulting dimensional change with component clearances, alignment, tol-
 erances, and functional requirements.

  3. Evaluate the stresses, inelastic strains, and load transfer produced by swelling gradients
 or constraint.

 The material input is swelling strain as a function of irradiation temperature, exposure,
spectrum or helium descriptor when relevant, and metallurgical condition. The allowable
dimensional change is set by the component design: the same material swelling can be ac-
ceptable in a freely expanding part and unacceptable in a close-clearance or restrained region.
Free isotropic swelling is an eigenstrain: it enters total-deformation and dimensional-stability
calculations but is not added to the HBB accumulated plastic-and-creep strain limit. When
swelling is spatially nonuniform or constrained, the structural analysis calculates the elastic,
plastic, thermal-creep, and irradiation-creep response generated by that eigenstrain; those
mechanically induced strains enter the strain-limits evaluation (Section 4.3.2) and the result-
ing stresses enter the applicable primary-load, creep-fatigue, buckling, or contact assessment.
Nelson (1978) and Hecht (1979) show that swelling can transfer load in specific FFTF core-
component configurations but do not establish a general numerical threshold for automatic
stress reclassification. Where a swelling-induced stress cannot be shown to be self-limiting
or to relax without adverse load transfer, the corresponding stress is classified as primary
rather than secondary and is evaluated against the primary-load allowables of Section 4.3.1,
while the associated mechanically induced inelastic strains continue to enter the strain-limits
calculation of Section 4.3.2. This double-accounting — the same swelling-driven quantity
treated as a primary stress on the load-controlled side and as an inelastic-strain contribu-
tion on the deformation-controlled side — is the mechanism by which a non-self-limiting
swelling-induced stress is prevented from escaping either check. If either the primary-stress
classification or the inelastic-strain estimate cannot be bounded by a simplified bounding
calculation, a coupled swelling–irradiation-creep structural analysis is used to establish the
actual behavior.
 Technical basis and limits. The rule separates the material-level swelling strain from its
component-level consequence, so a swelling value that is acceptable in one geometry can
be flagged as unacceptable in another; this avoids reliance on a single universal allowable

ANL-AMMT-030                                  37

swelling percentage. A qualified swelling correlation is required only where the mechanism
is relevant to the component evaluated, and a coupled swelling–irradiation-creep structural
analysis is required only when the simple dimensional check and the bounding load-transfer
check cannot together establish acceptance.

4.3.6 Weldments
Proposed rule. For a weld in a significant-irradiation region, the applicable HBB weld re-
duction factors are applied on top of the irradiation-adjusted base metal allowables and the
irradiation-adjusted base metal design fatigue curves.
 Technical basis and limits. The Code assigns weld strength-reduction factors to unirradi-
ated base metal allowables to account for the observed strength reduction of weld metal,
heat-affected zone, and cross-weld regions. Applying those same factors on top of the
irradiation-adjusted base metal allowables is a defensible screening rule: it uses two es-
tablished, separately qualified adjustments — one for irradiation, one for the weld — rather
than a single combined factor that would require an irradiated-weldment database that does
not exist. This is proposed as a screening rule; it does not demonstrate that irradiation
affects weld metal, heat-affected zone, and cross-weld response identically to base metal.
Where a controlling weld has representative irradiated evidence, that evidence is preferred
and replaces the multiplicative screening rule.

4.4 In-pile behavior

In this section, in-pile refers to material behavior when mechanical stress and neutron flux
act concurrently on the material, as distinct from post-irradiation examination (PIE), in
which specimens are exposed to neutron irradiation with no mechanical load applied during
the exposure and are then removed from the flux for mechanical testing. The distinction
matters because in-pile behavior can violate assumptions used in Sections 4.2 and 4.3, both
of which use PIE-derived properties as their default input.
 The distinction is mechanistically important because certain irradiation-driven damage
processes — most notably helium-bubble nucleation and growth on grain boundaries under
stress — depend on the simultaneous presence of stress, defect production, and helium
generation, and cannot be reproduced by PIE specimens no matter how the irradiation
exposure and the subsequent mechanical test are conducted.
 Proposed rule. PIE-based properties are used for time-dependent design only within an
applicability domain in which the in-pile response has been shown to be bounded by the PIE
response, or in which a validated in-pile correction is available. Where an in-pile mechanism
could control the design outcome, the available options are:

  1. material-specific evidence that PIE data bound the in-pile response over the applicable
 stress–temperature–time–exposure domain;

  2. a validated in-pile correction, applied to the PIE-derived property or factor before it
 enters the design check; or

 ANL-AMMT-030                                 38

  3. restriction of the qualified domain to the region in which the PIE-based route is de-
 fensible, with any part of the design condition falling outside that region evaluated by
 another qualified route.

 Missing in-pile evidence does not, by itself, disqualify a PIE-based evaluation, but neither
does its absence justify assuming that PIE data are conservative.
 Technical basis and limits. This rule reflects the contrast reviewed in Section 3.3. The
Wassilew et al. (1987) results for stabilized austenitic and nickel-base alloys show that at
long times and low stresses, in-pile rupture life can be shorter than PIE rupture life at the
same conditions — the sub-transition-stress regime — because helium-driven grain-boundary
damage develops under the combined action of stress and continuing flux in a way that is
not reproduced in PIE specimens, which see the flux exposure and the mechanical loading
in separate stages. Other Type-316 datasets show different orderings, in which PIE rupture
life is shorter than in-pile life. Neither a universal assumption of PIE conservatism nor a
universal in-pile penalty is defensible on the current evidence base; the ordering depends on
alloy, metallurgical condition, temperature, stress, dose rate, and helium production.
 The design consequence is that whenever an evaluation of Section 4.2 or Section 4.3
uses PIE-derived properties in a stress–temperature–time regime where an in-pile mecha-
nism could control — most notably in the long-time, low-stress creep damage part of the
creep-fatigue check for helium-generating exposures — the PIE input carries an in-pile qual-
ification requirement, not a presumption of conservatism. Where that qualification cannot
be established, the qualified applicability domain is restricted accordingly, and the affected
part of the design condition is evaluated by an in-pile-appropriate route (targeted in-pile
testing, a validated correction, or a design change).
 A designer with limited in-pile evidence might consider substituting an in-service surveil-
lance program for the in-pile qualification described above. Surveillance is an in-service
confirmation activity, however, and cannot replace the initial in-pile qualification: the ap-
plicability domain must be defensible before service begins. Surveillance considerations are
developed further as a longer-term recommendation in Section 6.3.

5 Data Requirements and Test Capabilities
5.1 Data required by the proposed framework

The proposed framework does not require an irradiation-specific reproduction of the complete
unirradiated ASME material database before an initial design evaluation can begin. It
requires enough evidence to adjust the property basis of each design check that is active at
the evaluated component location. The common base-metal evidence is organized around
matched tensile records, complete creep strain-time records through rupture, and fatigue-life
data. Additional irradiation data are conditional and are requested only when the associated
mechanism, component feature, or refined analysis can control.
 The matched tensile group supplies yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, uniform
elongation, total elongation, and, when needed, the stress–strain curve. The strength val-
ues support the capped yield basis and the time-independent factor FT I used to adjust
Sm ; the derived allowables S, So , and Smt follow through the existing Code hierarchy of
Section 4.3.1. The elongation and curve-shape information support the separate strain-
hardening and ductility screens used in the primary-load, strain-limits, and inelastic-buckling
evaluations. These roles are kept separate because an irradiated-to-unirradiated strength ra-
tio does not establish the deformation capacity needed for redistribution or resistance to
localization.
 The complete creep-record group supplies rupture time, the full strain–time history, min-
imum creep rate, tertiary-creep initiation when resolvable, and rupture strain. Rupture
strength supports FCR and Srirr . The strain and tertiary-onset portions of the record support
F1% and Fter and the lower-envelope factor FT D used to adjust St . The same records also
support the creep-ductility screen, the strain-limits inelastic-strain accounting, the stress-
relaxation treatment, and the allowable-time basis used in the creep-damage fraction. This
reuse is the main reason for carrying a limited number of creep tests through rupture with
continuous strain measurement rather than collecting rupture endpoints alone.
 The fatigue group supplies an irradiation-specific design fatigue curve or a bounded
fatigue-life factor FF over a stated strain-range, temperature, material, and exposure do-
main. It adjusts allowable cycles in the fatigue-damage coordinate and does not modify a
primary-load allowable. A scalar factor is acceptable only when it is the conservative bound
over the qualified domain and when its derivation preserves the uncertainty and margin in
the underlying design curve. The approximately 0.4 nominal ratio discussed in Section 3.2
(Figure 3.6) is used as the illustrative FF in the sparse-data creep-fatigue route of Section 4.2;
it is a demonstration from a limited annealed Type 316 dataset and is not a value for another
material or exposure condition.
 The three evidence groups are common inputs, but each remains check-specific in use. A
tensile factor cannot replace a fatigue-life factor. A rupture-strength factor can adjust Sr and
the rupture-time basis of creep damage, but it does not adjust St unless the creep-strain and
tertiary-onset responses are also bounded. A fatigue-life factor changes allowable cycles but
does not establish that the unirradiated creep-fatigue interaction envelope remains conser-
vative. These separations prevent the minimum-data approach from turning one convenient
dataset into a surrogate for several different failure modes.
 Additional data are conditional. An irradiation-creep correlation is needed when irra-
diation creep is not negligible in the strain, relaxation, or time-dependent buckling eval-

ANL-AMMT-030                                  43

uation. A swelling or irradiation-growth correlation is needed when dimensional change,
constraint, contact, or load transfer can affect acceptance. Irradiated elastic or thermo-
physical properties are needed when their change materially affects stress, strain range, or
instability. Cyclic constitutive data are needed when a simplified relaxation or ratcheting
bound fails or when favorable redistribution credit is sought. Buckling-specific curves are
needed when the existing HBB screening methods cannot be shown to bound the irradiated
material response and service geometry. Weldment data are needed for a controlling weld
in a significant-irradiation region. In-pile evidence or a qualified in-pile correction is needed
when post-irradiation behavior may not bound the governing long-time response, per the

 Every property or factor is accompanied by an applicability record covering the mate-
rial and heat treatment, product form, irradiation temperature and exposure, spectrum or
helium-production descriptor, test temperature, loading range, time range, specimen geom-
etry, and uncertainty basis. Data outside that domain may support mechanism screening or
test planning, but they do not directly qualify the design input. When evidence is sparse,
the first response is to narrow the domain or use a more conservative lower bound. If the
controlling property cannot be bounded, the affected branch requires targeted testing, re-
fined analysis, a design change, or a different material; the missing property is not assigned
a value by assumption.
 Post-irradiation examination (PIE) is the primary evidence path for quantifying irradia-
tion effects on the tensile, fatigue, and creep response of an irradiated microstructure, pro-
vided the retained damage and transmutation inventory represent the design condition. It
cannot, by itself, reproduce mechanisms that require the simultaneous presence of stress and
neutron flux, such as helium-bubble nucleation and growth on grain boundaries under stress;
in-pile evidence is the mechanism-specific complement in that regime (Section 4.4). The con-
trasting PIE-vs-in-pile results reviewed in Section 3.3 show that neither PIE conservatism nor
a universal in-pile penalty can be assumed. Where an in-pile mechanism could control, the
design basis requires one of the three qualification options developed in Section 4.4: material-
specific evidence that PIE data bound the in-pile response; a validated in-pile correction;
or restriction of the qualified applicability domain to the stress–temperature–time–exposure
region in which the PIE-based route is defensible, with any part of the design condition
falling outside that region evaluated by another qualified route or eliminated by a design
change (for example, a lower design temperature, a shorter design life, or another change in
the design condition that brings it within the qualified applicability domain).

5.4 Bridging sparse data without assuming missing properties

Sparse data can support a bounded screening rule, but they cannot create an unmeasured
material property. The reduction-factor approach of Sections 4.2 and 4.3 is defensible be-
cause each factor is tied to an observable property ratio and is capped at unity; its conser-
vatism still depends on the data enclosing the design domain and on the factor bounding
the specific allowable to which it is applied. A rupture-strength factor may reduce Sr , but
it cannot be presumed to reduce the 1%-total-strain and tertiary-onset responses of St (Sec-

ANL-AMMT-030                                 47

tion 4.3.1). A tensile-strength factor may screen Sm , but it cannot demonstrate adequate
uniform elongation or strain-hardening capacity. A fatigue-life factor changes Nd , but it
does not by itself establish that the retained unirradiated creep-fatigue interaction envelope
remains conservative — that is a separate question, addressed by the interaction-envelope
comparison in Section 3.2 (Figure 3.7). These separations are deliberate safeguards against
using one convenient dataset to stand in for several different failure modes.
 Where the available data are sparse, the first response — consistent with the sparse-data
route of Section 4.2 — is to narrow the qualified domain to the tested or defensibly inter-
polated material, temperature, irradiation, stress, and time range. Within that domain,
matched irradiated-to-unirradiated ratios are fitted as lower-bound envelopes with docu-
mented treatment of heat-to-heat scatter, specimen geometry, measurement uncertainty,
and curve slope. Extrapolation beyond the observed time range requires a stated model and
validation basis; it is not achieved by multiplying several unspecified margins. If uncertainty
cannot be bounded, the appropriate outcome is a lower allowable, a shorter life or lower
dose, a redesigned component, a different material, or a refined qualification program.
 A generic additional penalty for the difference between PIE and in-pile behavior is not
proposed. Consistent with the conclusion of Wassilew et al. [21] reported in Section 3.3, PIE
can be non-conservative for particular stabilized austenitic steels and Hastelloy-X in a low-
stress regime, but the direction and magnitude are not universal across the compiled datasets.
For a design condition in which an in-pile mechanism could control, the available options
are those developed in Section 4.4: material-specific evidence that PIE data bound the in-
pile response, a qualified in-pile correction with its own applicability domain, or exclusion
of that condition from the qualified domain. This makes the limitation visible rather than
embedding an untraceable margin in every allowable.
 Surveillance specimens can confirm that the service material remains within the assumed
degradation envelope and can trigger a predefined re-evaluation if measured properties ap-
proach or cross that envelope. Surveillance does not supply a missing initial design property,
justify setting an unknown factor to unity, or automatically permit relaxation of an initial
margin. Any planned update of a design factor must be defined before service, includ-
ing specimen relevance, test method, statistical treatment, acceptance threshold, re-analysis
procedure, and action if the result is adverse. The role of surveillance in this framework is
therefore verification and managed re-evaluation, not deferred qualification; the longer-term
data-collection and program-planning implications are developed in Section 6.3.
 The Asayama et al. [4] data provide a potentially useful research path for making short
creep tests more informative. A replot of their reported minimum creep rate and time to
rupture shows an approximately linear log-log relation with similar slopes for the unirradiated
and irradiated data considered. Asayama et al. did not present this as a Monkman–Grant
design rule, and the replot does not establish a universal relation, a lower-bound rupture
prediction, or the irradiated 1%-total-strain and tertiary-onset responses of St . Before design
use, the observation must be reconstructed from the source data, statistically tested by
material and irradiation condition, checked for intercept and slope changes, and validated
against withheld rupture results. If successful, it could help select tests and support a
bounded extrapolation model; it cannot presently replace the complete creep record or justify
updating an allowable from minimum-creep-rate data alone.
 The resulting sparse-data strategy is therefore progressive but not permissive. It begins

 ANL-AMMT-030                                  48

with the three minimum property groups, assigns each measured effect only to the allowable
or response quantity that it can support, and invokes conditional data when a component-
specific screen fails. Mechanistic models, ion-irradiation studies, specimen-size correlations,
and surveillance can strengthen interpolation, prioritize tests, and verify continued appli-
cability. They do not erase the need for a neutron-irradiation anchor or for a lower-bound
property basis at the design condition. This distinction should remain explicit when the
framework is calibrated for an individual alloy.

6 Conclusions
6.1 Summary of findings

ASME Section III, Division 5 [2] recognizes material degradation due to irradiation but does
not provide rules on how to account for irradiation effects in the Class A elevated-temperature
design evaluation (Chapter 2). RCC-MRx, the ITER SDC-IC, the Japanese ETSDG, and
the historical U.S. RDT/FFTF criteria contain irradiation provisions of differing scope; R5
contributes deformation and ductility concepts rather than a neutron-irradiation rule set.
These sources support the architecture adopted here, but none supplies a universal set of
factors that can be inserted unchanged into Division 5. The compiled literature (Chapter 3)
establishes that irradiation can change tensile strength, reduce tensile and creep ductility,
reduce creep-rupture strength, alter fatigue life, produce irradiation creep and swelling, and
— under some material and exposure conditions — make in-pile behavior more severe than
post-irradiation behavior; the magnitude and even the ordering of effects depend on alloy,
condition, temperature, dose, spectrum, helium production, stress, and test history.
 The proposed EEDG (Chapter 4) is a property-based overlay on the existing Division 5
Class A design flow: the Code stress classifications and acceptance structure are retained, and
only the material properties underlying each design allowable are adjusted for irradiation.
Two routes are developed. The practical design case (Section 4.2) is a sparse-data route for
the creep-fatigue evaluations that typically control the design of advanced high-temperature
reactor components: two conservative reduction factors — FCR on creep-rupture strength
and FF on fatigue life — derived over a documented applicability domain are sufficient
for an initial design evaluation, with the unirradiated interaction envelope retained on the
evidentiary basis of the Section 3.2 comparison. The fuller framework (Section 4.3) develops
the property-to-rule chain across the four Division 5 checks — primary load, strain limits
and ratcheting, creep-fatigue, and buckling — together with swelling and weldments as
scope items, for evaluations in which more than the two-factor minimum irradiated database
is available. Both routes are scoped by an in-pile qualification (Section 4.4) that restricts the
use of PIE-derived properties in stress–temperature–time–exposure regimes where an in-pile
mechanism could control the design outcome. Illustrative outputs derived on a compiled
annealed Type 316 database — FCR ≈ 0.78–0.86 and FF ≈ 0.4 — are demonstrations of
the calculation method within that specific dataset, not qualified design factors for another
material or exposure condition.
 The report proposes an initial, testable design-rule concept; it is not a qualified set of irra-
diation allowables or a material approval. The data requirements (Chapter 5) are organized
around three common evidence groups — matched tensile records, complete creep records
through rupture, and fatigue-life data — with conditional branches for irradiation creep,
swelling, elastic and thermophysical properties, cyclic constitutive response, buckling-specific
curves, weldments, and in-pile behavior activated only where the associated mechanism or
check controls. Current SPL and IMET capabilities can support much of the tensile and
creep work and selected fatigue development, but miniature-creep transfer, approximately
1,000–2,000 h post-irradiation test durations, and the absence of a complete hot-cell creep-
fatigue pathway constrain the qualified domain. The next stage is calibration and validation
of the property factors, applicability domains, and conditional branches using the prioritized
data program identified in Chapter 5.

6.2 Future work

Several activities have been identified as candidates for future work to build on the initial
framework of the present report.

  1. Fluid-exposure (coolant-environment) effects. Compile the literature on coolant-environment
 degradation and develop the corresponding EEDG framework, to complement the
 present report’s neutron-irradiation-only scope.

  2. R5 ductility-exhaustion form of the creep-damage fraction. Evaluate the R5 ductility-
 exhaustion form of the creep-damage fraction in the creep-fatigue check (Section 2.3),
 in which the creep-damage increment is accumulated creep strain divided by multi-
 axial creep ductility at the applicable stress and temperature, rather than elapsed
 time divided by rupture time. Determine whether the required irradiated creep-strain
 histories and multiaxial creep-ductility data can be obtained on the materials of inter-
 est and, if so, whether this form provides a validated refined-route alternative to the
 sparse-data creep-fatigue check of Section 4.2 in cases where irradiation reduces creep
 ductility strongly.

  3. Statistical procedures for lower-bound factors. Develop statistical procedures for the
 lower-bound irradiation factors and their applicability domains.

  4. Component-specific swelling and load transfer. Develop and validate a component-
 specific method for swelling-induced dimensional change and load transfer. The method
 should distinguish free swelling from constrained or spatially nonuniform swelling, de-
 termine whether induced stresses are self-limiting, and identify when coupled defor-
 mation analysis or dual primary/deformation evaluation is required. Historical FFTF
 experience could be the starting point.

  5. Asayama minimum-creep-rate replot. Digitize and independently reproduce the Asayama
 [4] minimum-creep-rate versus rupture-time replot, test the apparent common log–log
 slope by material and irradiation condition, quantify intercept and scatter, and vali-
 date against withheld data. Only after these steps should the relation be considered as
 a bounded extrapolation aid; it should not be used to infer rupture ductility, tertiary
 onset, or an unmeasured St response.

6.3 Longer-term recommendations

  1. Qualified irradiated fatigue pathway. Establish subsized high-temperature fatigue spec-
 imens and procedures capable of producing an irradiation-qualified design fatigue curve
 or a bounded fatigue-life factor FF over documented strain-range, temperature, ma-
 terial, and exposure domains. A full in-cell creep-fatigue capability, when it becomes
 available, would support validation of the retained HBB interaction envelope (Sec-
 tion 4.2) and of the stress-relaxation treatment used in the fuller creep-fatigue route
     (Section 4.3.3).

 ANL-AMMT-030                                 52

  2. Qualified miniature-creep pathway. Qualify miniature creep specimens and instrumen-
 tation to obtain complete strain–time records through rupture and to bridge SSJ,
 miniature-buttonhead, and larger-specimen behavior before and after irradiation. This
 capability supports FCR , the response-specific reconstruction of St (Section 4.3.1),
 the creep-rupture ductility screen, deformation inputs for the strain-limits check (Sec-
 tion 4.3.2), and the allowable-time basis for the creep-damage coordinate (Sections 4.2
     and 4.3.3).

  3. Preplanned in-service material surveillance. Define, during initial design qualification,
 a material surveillance program [25] that generates in-service in-pile evidence for the
 components under surveillance. The initial EEDG relies on PIE-derived reduction
 factors — FCR , FF , and the related factors of Chapter 4 — because PIE evidence is
 what is available before service. Once the plant is operating, surveillance data collected
 under the actual combined stress, flux, and thermal history of the component supply
 the in-pile evidence anticipated by Section 4.4, and can be used to update, calibrate,
 or replace the PIE-derived factors.
