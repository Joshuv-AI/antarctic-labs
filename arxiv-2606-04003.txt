A sharp analysis of Root-MUSIC: locations of correct and extraneous roots
Hana Huber, Weilin Li

Abstract
Root-MUSIC is a spectral estimation algorithm that approximates the unknown signal frequencies by constructing a high-degree polynomial and finding a subset of roots which are closest to the complex unit circle. Previous works found asymptotic expectation formulas for the performance of Root-MUSIC under the implicit assumption that the aforementioned root selection criterion does not select extraneous roots – those which are unrelated to the correct parameters. This paper removes the need for this assumption by showing all extraneous roots lie outside an annulus of a certain thickness and therefore are not selected by the algorithm. This paper also provides sharp, non-asymptotic, and explicit error bounds for the correct roots in terms of fundamental model parameters. All results hold under a natural separation condition on the correct signal frequencies and are applicable in both the single- and multi-snapshot models. More specifically, in the multi-snapshot model, we prove that Root-MUSIC estimates the frequencies with error at most O ⁡ ( σ / ( m ​ n ) ) O(\sigma/(m\sqrt{n})) , where σ 2 \sigma^{2} is the noise variance, m m is the number of sensors, and n n is the number of snapshots. A novelty of this non-asymptotic bound is the explicit 1 / m 1/m decay, which indicates that there is a significant advantage in utilizing additional sensors. Numerical simulations confirm our theory. The main mathematical insight of this paper is a geometric property of the Root-MUSIC polynomial: its correct roots are highly stable to noise while its extraneous roots must lie outside of an annulus.

Introduction

1.1
Motivation

A typical spectral estimation problem is to recover the frequencies
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}\subseteq[0,2\pi)
of a Fourier sum from noisy temporal samples. A collection of highly celebrated “subspace methods”
[
23
,
3
,
21
,
12
]
were created for this task. They start by approximating the correct “signal subspace”
{\boldsymbol{U}}
that is associated with the correct frequencies
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}
. The correct signal subspace
{\boldsymbol{U}}
has dimension
s
in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
, where
m
is usually proportional to the number of collected samples. The analytical and statistical theories of subspace estimation are fairly well-understood, see
[
26
,
7
]
for overviews. Once an approximate or empirical signal subspace
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
is computed using the observations, each subspace method carries out a different routine to process
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
to produce estimated signal frequencies, which hopefully approximate the correct signal frequencies.

The Root-MUSIC algorithm
[
3
]
is a variation of the classical MUSIC algorithm
[
22
,
25
]
. Both are foundational subspace methods which are readily available to a general audience – for example, both are included in MATLAB’s Signal Processing Toolbox. While the classical MUSIC algorithm finds the
s
smallest local minima of a particular “MUSIC function” through a brute force search, Root-MUSIC was developed as a computationally faster alternative, which we will explain momentarily.

The Root-MUSIC algorithm uses an approximate subspace
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
to define a “Root-MUSIC polynomial”
\widetilde{P}
of degree at most
2m-2
. Its roots away from zero come in pairs of the form
(w,1/\overline{w})
, with
w\not=0
inside the closed unit disk
\overline{\mathbb{D}}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
and its reflection
1/\overline{w}\not\in\mathbb{D}
. Root-MUSIC selects the
s
roots of
\widetilde{P}
(mod reflection) closest to the unit circle, denoted
\{\widetilde{w}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}
, and
\{\widetilde{\theta}_{k}:=\arg(\widetilde{w}_{k})\}_{k=1}^{s}
serves as the estimated signal frequencies. Since
w
and its reflection
1/\overline{w}
have the same argument, we ignore all reflected roots to simplify subsequent discussion. Root-MUSIC is significantly faster than classical MUSIC due to the former’s ability to use fast root-finding procedures such as Newton’s method instead of doing an expensive brute force search.

In the noiseless setting, the correct signal subspace
{\boldsymbol{U}}
can be computed and the noiseless Root-MUSIC polynomial
P
has exactly
s
“correct” roots on the unit circle, which are precisely
\{e^{i\theta_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{s}
. We call the remaining roots of
P
“extraneous”, for which there could be up to
m-1-s
many (mod reflection). Previous papers
[
20
]
and
[
14
]
provided asymptotic expectation formulas for the perturbation of correct roots under statistical assumptions. However, they are insufficient to guarantee the success of Root-MUSIC in the presence of noise. To see why,
Fig.

1
depicts a potential issue where a perturbed extraneous root of
P
is closer to the unit circle than a perturbed correct root. Consequently, the Root-MUSIC algorithm would select a perturbed extraneous root whose argument (or phase, angle) does not approximate any of the correct signal frequencies, resulting in a failure of the estimation. This pathological example could result from
P
having an extraneous root that is too close to the unit circle.

One motivation of this paper is to study whether this behavior can actually occur under natural assumptions. Another motivation is to derive explicit and non-asymptotic expressions for the frequency error in terms of fundamental model parameters, such as the number of samples and noise level, with minimal assumptions. Letting
d(t,u)
be the arc-length between
e^{it}
and
e^{iu}
, we naturally define the

\text{frequency error}:=\max_{k=1,\dots,s}\ \ \ \min_{\text{permutations }\sigma}\ \ \ d(\theta_{k},\widetilde{\theta}_{\sigma(k)}),

The main obstacles lie in getting delicate control over perturbations of both the correct and extraneous roots of
P
. The most important technical assumption of this paper is a separation condition on the correct frequencies
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}
. For a large enough absolute constant
\beta\geq 4
, we consider the situation where

\Delta:=\min_{j\not=k}d(\theta_{j},\theta_{k})\geq\frac{2\pi\beta}{m}.

(1.1)

This is a common and natural separation assumption found in other papers on spectral estimation such as
[
6
,
9
,
19
,
10
]
. While certain algorithms can still succeed in the “super-resolution regime” where
\Delta
is significantly smaller than
\pi/m
, this setting is considerably different from the one considered here and is beyond the scope of this paper – we refer the reader to
[
17
,
4
]
and references therein for an overview of the mathematical theory of super-resolution. A second main assumption of this paper is that the noise is sufficiently small. This is captured in an abstract requirement that for an absolute
\varepsilon>0
,

\text{subspace error}:=\Big\|{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}\Big\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon.

Note that the subspace error is the largest canonical angle between the subspaces
{\boldsymbol{U}}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
.

1.3
Related work

The first contribution of this paper, showing that Root-MUSIC does not select any perturbed extraneous root, is completely new, to our best knowledge.

The second contribution of this paper, inequality (
1.2
) for the perturbation of correct roots, is also a new result. Past papers
[
20
]
and
[
14
]
considered the multi-snapshot setting described in
Section

4.2
and produced asymptotic error formulas that depend on complicated and/or implicit quantities such as the noise subspace and the eigenvectors of the spatial covariance matrix. Consequently, it is not immediately clear how these bounds scale with the number of samples, sources, and different types of noise. In contrast, we obtain sharp and transparent inequalities which explicitly highlight how the error behaves in important model parameters as seen in
Table

1
. A more technical and detailed comparison can be found in
Remark

4.5
.

There exist modified Root-MUSIC algorithms, such as the one in
[
27
]
. This paper develops a modified Root-MUSIC algorithm to extend its applicability to non-uniform linear arrays. An open direction is to carry out perturbation analysis on this “irregular Root-MUSIC” algorithm. We also refer the reader to
[
27
]
for numerical comparisons between Root-MUSIC, irregular Root-MUSIC, and other popular spectral estimation methods. The present paper is of a theoretical nature and does not advocate for one particular method over another.

The strategy of bounding the frequency error in terms of the subspace error is a well-known template that appears in numerous other papers which analyze the performance of subspace methods
[
20
,
25
,
14
,
16
,
18
,
28
,
11
,
10
]
. The steps required to prove such a result and the form in which it appears varies depending on which subspace method is being considered. As explained earlier, an important feature in (
1.2
) for Root-MUSIC is that the frequency error is at most
R/m
times the subspace error, which has practical implications and implies optimality. This
1/m
factor is not present in all spectral estimation theory. For instance, it is unknown if ESPRIT also achieves the general bound (
1.2
). At the present moment, the only known algorithms that enjoy (
1.2
) are three MUSIC algorithms: classical MUSIC, Gradient-MUSIC (both proved in
[
10
]
) and Root-MUSIC.

We refer the reader to
[
17
,
4
]
for a detailed overview of further theoretical results, especially for the complementary setting of clustered frequencies.

Review of Root-MUSIC

This section reviews the Root-MUSIC algorithm, which was first developed in
[
3
]
and later analyzed in
[
20
,
14
]
. Several known facts related to the classical and Root-MUSIC algorithms are stated as
Observation
, which are not reproved unless mentioned specifically. Compared to
[
20
]
, our exposition is more streamlined because
[
20
]
derives a special function (which will be denoted
Q
in our notation) from first principles, whereas we take a shortcut of starting with the correct definition of
Q
.

First step. Subspace estimation.
The first step of MUSIC algorithms and other subspace methods is to estimate the subspace on which the correct signal parameters
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}
lie on. To this end, define the Fourier (or Vandermonde) matrix with
m
rows associated to
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}
as

{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}:={\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(m,\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}):=\left[e^{ij\theta_{k}}\right]_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0,1,\dots,m-1\\
k=1,2,\dots,s\end{subarray}}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times s}

Here, the columns of
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
have
\ell^{2}
norm
\sqrt{m}
. The correct signal subspace in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
associated with
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}
is precisely

{\boldsymbol{U}}:={\boldsymbol{U}}(m,\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s})=\text{range}({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}(m,\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}))\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{m}.

Although we will not need the following fact, we mention that there is a bijection between all subspaces in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
defined this way and all possible subsets of cardinality
s
contained in
\mathbb{T}
, see
[
10
, Lemma 5.3]
. We will frequently drop dependence on
m
and
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}
which will be understood from context, and the symbol
{\boldsymbol{U}}
is reserved for denoting the subspace associated to
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}
. Let
{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}:={\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}(m,\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s})
be the orthogonal complement of
{\boldsymbol{U}}
.

Throughout, we slightly abuse notation and let
{\boldsymbol{U}}
(resp.,
{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}
) also denote a matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis for the subspace
{\boldsymbol{U}}
(resp.,
{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}
). Then
{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}
is the projection onto its range. As we will see, choice of bases for the ranges of
{\boldsymbol{U}}
and
{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}
are completely inconsequential for the MUSIC-type algorithms.

Subspace estimation is about computing an approximate subspace
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
of
{\boldsymbol{U}}
from noisy observations. The theory developed in this paper will be completely agnostic to the method in which an
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
is computed because the main theorem (
Theorem

3.1
) will be applicable to any
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
for which the subspace error is small enough. Since the subspace error will appear frequently in this paper, we denote it as

\rho:=\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}\right\|_{2}.

(2.1)

This quantity is the sine-theta distance between
{\boldsymbol{U}}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
, which makes it a natural metric. The theory of subspace estimation is fairy well-understood at this point and is not the main focus of this paper. For completeness, we provide two concrete spectral estimation models in
Section

4
, the single- and multi- snapshot cases. They rely on computing
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
through a truncated singular value decomposition of a Hankel or covariance matrix.

Second step. Root-MUSIC polynomial and root-finding.
Now we are in position to explain the general mechanisms of the Root-MUSIC algorithm. Define the “steering vector”
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{m}
by

{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\begin{bmatrix}1,&z,&z^{2},&\cdots,&z^{m-1}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

Here we have chosen to normalize
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
by
1/\sqrt{m}
so that
\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(e^{it})\|_{2}=1
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
is a vector valued differentiable function of
z
, we have

{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\begin{bmatrix}0,&1,&2z,&\cdots,&(m-1)z^{m-2}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

Consider the complex function
Q\colon\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{C}
defined as

Q(z):={\boldsymbol{\phi}}(1/\overline{z})^{*}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z).

(2.2)

Using that
{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}^{*}+{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}={\boldsymbol{I}}
and that
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(1/\overline{z})^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)=1
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
, we obtain the alternative formula

Q(z)=1-{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(1/\overline{z})^{*}\,{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z).

(2.3)

There is a close relationship between the function constructed in the classical MUSIC algorithm
[
23
]
and
Q
. If
Q
is restricted to the unit complex circle and viewed as a
2\pi
periodic function, then we get the function
q\colon\mathbb{T}\to[0,1]
defined as

q(t):=Q(e^{it}).

The classical MUSIC algorithm seeks to find the
s
smallest local minima of
q
, typically done with a brute force grid search. The MUSIC algorithm relies on the following equivalence
[
23
]
whose proof we omit.

Observation 2.1
.

Suppose
m\geq s+1
. Fix any subset
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}
and let
{\boldsymbol{U}}
be its associated subspace. For any
t\in\mathbb{T}
, it holds that
q(t)=0
if and only if
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(e^{it})\in{\boldsymbol{U}}
if and only if
t\in\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}
. Moreover, each
\theta_{k}
is a double zero of
q
.

While the last part of this observation asserts that
q^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{k})>0
for each correct zero
\theta_{k}
, there is a quantitative lower bound in
[
10
]
which will be stated in
Lemma

6.1
.

A computation shows that
Q
is a Laurent polynomial of degree at most
m-1
and can be written as

Q(z):=\sum_{j=-m+1}^{m-1}q_{j}z^{j},

(2.4)

for some coefficients
\{q_{j}\}_{j=-m+1}^{m-1}
, which are also the Fourier coefficients of
q
.

Importantly,
Q
satisfies a certain symmetry property that requires introducing some terminology. For each
z\not=0
, define
reflection
through the circle by

z\to\frac{1}{\overline{z}}.

This fixes the unit circle and maps
\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
to the complement of
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. It also does not change the argument of a complex number and only inverts its modulus. For this reason, we have called it reflection, which is consistent with other convention such as
[
15
, Chapter I.3, Exercise 2]
.

The zeros of
Q
come in pairs of the form
(w,1/\overline{w})
. This was first proved in
[
20
]
, but to the importance of this property for Root-MUSIC, we reprove it for the reader’s convenience in
Section

6.7
.

Observation 2.2
.

Suppose
m\geq s+1
. Then
Q
has exactly
2s
zeros on
\partial\mathbb{D}
, which are precisely
\{e^{i\theta_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{s}
, each with multiplicity two. Additionally
Q
has at most
2m-2
complex zeros, and they come in pairs with the following reflection symmetry:
w
is a zero of
Q
if and only if
1/\overline{w}
is a zero. Finally, there is an algebraic polynomial
B
of degree at most
m-1
whose roots are all in
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
such that

Q(z)=B(z)\overline{B(1/\overline{z})}\quad\text{for all}\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

(2.5)

Consequently, we have the identity

Q(z)=\overline{Q(1/\overline{z})}\quad\text{for all}\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}.

As a consequence of Observation
2.2
, we enumerate the
n\leq m-1
roots of the polynomial
B
by

w_{1}=e^{i\theta_{1}},\,w_{2}=e^{i\theta_{2}},\,\dots,w_{s}=e^{i\theta_{s}},\,w_{s+1},\,,\dots,\,w_{n}.

(2.6)

Here, the first
s
roots lie on
\partial\mathbb{D}
, while the remaining
n-s
roots have to be in
\mathbb{D}
due to
2.1
. We use this convention throughout the paper.

In the presence of noise, suppose we obtain a
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
that approximates
{\boldsymbol{U}}
, and let
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}
be a matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis for the orthogonal complement of the range of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
. Again, see
Section

4
for examples. Using
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
instead of
{\boldsymbol{U}}
in formulas (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) yields a perturbed function,

\widetilde{Q}(z)={\boldsymbol{\phi}}(1/\overline{z})^{*}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)=1-{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(1/\overline{z})^{*}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z).

(2.7)

This function is also a Laurent polynomial of degree at most
m-1
and can be written as

\widetilde{Q}(z):=\sum_{j=-m+1}^{m-1}\widetilde{q}_{j}z^{j},

(2.8)

for some coefficients
\{\widetilde{q}_{k}\}_{k=-m+1}^{m-1}
. The Laurent polynomial
\widetilde{Q}
also enjoys symmetry properties, which we reprove it for the reader’s convenience in
Section

6.7
.

Observation 2.3
.

Suppose
m\geq s+1
. Then
\widetilde{Q}
has an even number of complex zeros and they come in pairs with the following reflection symmetry:
w
is a zero if and only if
1/\overline{w}
is a zero. We have the identity

\widetilde{Q}(z)=\overline{\widetilde{Q}(1/\overline{z})}\quad\text{for all}\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}.

Unlike
Q
, the Laurent polynomial
\widetilde{Q}
does not necessarily have any zeros on the unit circle and it is not immediate that
\widetilde{Q}
has any zeros whatsoever (e.g., it is not immediately clear that
\widetilde{Q}
is non-constant).

Instead of working with the Laurent polynomials
Q
and
\widetilde{Q}
, it is more convenient to work with the
Root-MUSIC polynomial

P
and its noisy version
\widetilde{P}
, which are defined as

P(z)=z^{m-1}Q(z)\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{P}(z)=z^{m-1}\widetilde{Q}(z).

(2.9)

Both are polynomials of degree at most
2m-2
in view of equations (
2.4
) and (
2.8
). It is also clear that
Q
and
P
(resp.
\widetilde{Q}
and
\widetilde{P}
) have the same zeros in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, though
P
and
\widetilde{P}
may vanish at zero. We will eventually show in
Theorem

3.3
that
\widetilde{Q}
and
\widetilde{P}
have at least
2s
many zeros and quantify their locations.

Root-MUSIC computes the roots of
\widetilde{P}
through a root finding algorithm (such as Newton’s method), selects the
s
roots mod reflection that are the closest to the circle (if they exist), and their arguments are the estimated signal frequencies.

We need to be careful about the definition of “closest to the circle” since it should not be defined in the usual sense. The roots of
\widetilde{P}
necessarily come in pairs satisfying the reflection symmetry provided in
2.3
. Since this reflection does not change the argument of a complex number, it is not important if a root
w
or its reflection
1/\overline{w}
is found by the root finding procedure, despite them having different distances to the circle. For this reason, it is natural to measure the distance between
w\in\mathbb{C}
to the circle by

\min\left\{\left||w|-1\right|,\,\left||w|^{-1}-1\right|\right\},

(2.10)

which is clearly invariant under reflection of
w
. Hence, the Root-MUSIC algorithm selects the
s
distinct roots modulo reflection symmetry that are closest to the circle in the sense of (
2.10
). Throughout, the
s
roots of
\widetilde{P}
in

\overline{\mathbb{D}}
that are closest to the circle are denoted

\widetilde{w}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{w}_{s}\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}.

This is purely a matter of conventional convenience, since in practice,
1/\overline{\widetilde{w}_{k}}
may be found by the root finding procedure instead of
\widetilde{w}_{k}
, which has no impact on the final output of the algorithm since
\widetilde{w}_{k}
and
1/\overline{\widetilde{w}_{k}}
have the same argument.

As mentioned in the introduction and depicted in
Fig.

1
, there is a possibility that the Root-MUSIC algorithm selects a perturbation of an extraneous root of
P
, which would lead to failure. Although the notation is suggestive, we defined
\widetilde{w}_{k}
as a root selected by the algorithm and
it is not defined as a perturbation of the correct root
w_{k}=e^{i\theta_{k}}
. Under the assumptions of
Theorem

3.1
, we will show that
\widetilde{w}_{k}
is in fact a perturbation of the correct root
w_{k}
.

3
Main result and outline of proof

The following theorem is the main result of this paper and is proved in
Section

6.5
.

Theorem 3.1

(Main theorem for Root-MUSIC)
.

There are absolute constants
R,\varepsilon,\tau,\beta>0
and
m_{0}\geq 100
such that the following hold. For any
m\geq m_{0}
,
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
such that
\Delta(\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s})\geq 2\pi\beta/m
, and subspace
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
such that
\|{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon
, the Root-MUSIC polynomial
\widetilde{P}
enjoys the following properties.

1.

It has exactly
2s
roots, denoted
\{\widetilde{w}_{k},1/\overline{\widetilde{w}_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{s}
, in the annulus

\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}\colon 1-\frac{\tau}{m}\leq|z|\leq\left(1-\frac{\tau}{m}\right)^{-1}\right\}.

2.

For each
k\in\{1,\dots,s\}
,

\max\left\{\left|e^{i\theta_{k}}-\widetilde{w}_{k}\right|,\left|e^{i\theta_{k}}-1/\overline{\widetilde{w}_{k}}\right|\right\}\leq\frac{R}{m}\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}\right\|_{2}.

(3.1)

Part (1) of this theorem gives us that
\widetilde{P}
has at least
s
roots (mod reflection), and that the
s
roots produced by the Root-MUSIC algorithm (which selects the
s
roots closest to the unit circle) are the only roots in an explicit annulus around the unit circle. Part (2) confirms that those roots are close to
\{e^{i\theta_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{s}
as desired. It also says that as the subspace error decreases or the number of samples increases, the distances of the selected roots of
\widetilde{P}
to
\{e^{i\theta_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{s}
decrease as well.

The bound in (
3.1
) cannot be predicted from heuristic arguments and standard wisdom. Since
\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(w)\|_{2}=1
whenever
w\in\partial\mathbb{D}
,

\sup_{w\in\partial\mathbb{D}}|P(w)-\widetilde{P}(w)|\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}\right\|_{2}.

That is, the perturbation of
P
on the circle scales linearly with the subspace error with a constant that does not depend on
m
(this is generally sharp and cannot be improved). To make matters worse, perturbation theory says that double roots are generally more sensitive to perturbations than simple roots
[
24
]
. To give an example, the quadratic polynomial
z^{2}
has a double root at
z=0
, yet a
\varepsilon>0
perturbation of
z^{2}
may result in the perturbed polynomial
z^{2}-\varepsilon
, which has a roots
\pm\sqrt{\varepsilon}
. Each
e^{i\theta_{k}}
is a double root of
P
, so general theory cannot produce anything better than

\max\left\{\left|e^{i\theta_{k}}-\widetilde{w}_{k}\right|,\left|e^{i\theta_{k}}-1/\overline{\widetilde{w}_{k}}\right|\right\}\lesssim\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}\right\|_{2}^{1/2}.

Here, the implicit constant at best does not grow in
m
and the square root comes from
e^{i\theta_{k}}
being a double root. This bound is obviously much worse than (
3.1
) which scales linearly in the subspace error and has an additional
1/m
factor.

An outline of the proof of
Theorem

3.1
is given in
Section

3
. There, we explain how
P
has special properties and
P-\widetilde{P}
has hidden cancellations in an appropriate neighborhood of each
e^{i\theta_{k}}
, which enables us to prove
Theorem

3.1
through Rouche’s theorem and parameter tuning arguments. It is precisely these special properties which enables
P
and
\widetilde{P}
to avoid bad behavior that are present for general polynomials with double roots.

3.1
Outline of Proof of
Theorem

3.1

To relate the zeros of
P
and
\widetilde{P}
, we will use Rouche’s theorem,
[
1
, page 153]
.

Theorem 3.2

(Rouche)
.

Let
\gamma
be homologous to zero in a set
U\subseteq\mathbb{C}
such that for each
z\not\in\gamma
, the winding number
n(\gamma,z)
of
\gamma
with respect to
z
is either zero or one. If
f
and
g
are analytic in
U
and
|f(z)-g(z)|<|f(z)|
for all
z\in\gamma
, then
f
and
g
have the same number of zeros in the set enclosed by
\gamma
.

As mentioned in the introduction, it is convenient to think of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
as fixed and phrase everything in terms of the subspace error,

\rho:=\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}\right\|_{2}.

Step 1. Control over the correct roots.
Recall our notation that
w_{1}=e^{i\theta_{1}},\dots,w_{s}=e^{i\theta_{s}}
are the roots of
P
on
\partial\mathbb{D}
, where each one has multiplicity two. For appropriately chosen
R>0
and an
\alpha>R\rho
, we will apply Rouche’s theorem to
P
and
\widetilde{P}
, and use circles

C_{k}:=\partial D\left(w_{k},\frac{R\rho}{m}\right),\quad\widetilde{C}_{k}:=\partial D\left(w_{k},\frac{\alpha}{m}\right).

Assuming this can be done, Rouche’s theorem implies that
\widetilde{P}
has

•

exactly two roots of the form
z_{k}
and
1/\overline{z_{k}}
in the disk enclosed by
C_{k}
.

•

exactly two roots in the disk enclosed by
\widetilde{C}_{k}
.

Together, they imply that
z_{k}
and
1/\overline{z_{k}}
are both inside the disk enclosed by
C_{k}
. In order to use Rouche’s theorem, we need to to lower bound
|P(z)|
(step 1a) and upper bound
|P(z)-\widetilde{P}(z)|
(step 1b), when
z\in C_{k},\widetilde{C}_{k}
.

Step 1a. Lower bounds for
|P|
near
w_{k}
.
Since
P
has a double root at
w_{k}
, it is locally a quadratic function. Whenever
z
is near
w_{k}
, we expect that

P(z)=\frac{1}{2}P^{\prime\prime}(w_{k})(z-w_{k})^{2}\left(1+O(z-w_{k})\right)\quad\text{as}\quad z\to w_{k}.

To derive a quantitative bound for how small
|z-w_{k}|
needs to be, the key step is to show that

|P^{\prime\prime}(w_{k})|\geq c_{0}m^{2}

for an absolute constant
c_{0}>0
. This step leverages a result from prior analysis of MUSIC in
[
10
]
. This will allow us to establish that

|P(z)|\gtrsim m^{2}|z-w_{k}|^{2}=\begin{cases}R^{2}\rho^{2}&\quad\text{for}\quad z\in C_{k},\\
\alpha^{2}&\quad\text{for}\quad z\in\widetilde{C}_{k}.\end{cases}

(3.2)

Step 1b. Upper bounds for
|P-\widetilde{P}|
near
w_{k}
.
Let us first examine the simpler matter of controlling
|P-\widetilde{P}|
on the unit circle. A naive estimate for this is

\|P-\widetilde{P}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial\mathbb{D})}\leq\rho.

This is far too weak to invoke Rouche’s theorem for
C_{k}
because in view of (
3.2
), we will not be able to establish that
|P(z)-\widetilde{P}(z)|<|P(z)|
for
z\in C_{k}
and all small
\rho
. So at this point, it seems impossible to use Rouche’s theorem without imposing a stronger noise condition or making the radius of
C_{k}
larger (hence would yield a worse perturbation bound).

However, there is some additional structure in
P
that leads to some hidden cancellations. We establish a substantially stronger local estimate,

|P(z)-\widetilde{P}(z)|\lesssim\begin{cases}R\rho^{2}&\quad\text{for}\quad z\in C_{k},\\
\alpha&\quad\text{for}\quad z\in\widetilde{C}_{k}.\end{cases}

(3.3)

Importantly, the upper bound (
3.3
) is linear in
R,\alpha
while the lower bound (
3.2
) is quadratic in
R,\alpha
, and both have the same scaling in
\rho
. By making
R,\alpha
large enough absolute constants, we can apply Rouche’s theorem. The case for
\widetilde{C}_{k}
is proven as
Lemma

6.5
to set up the following theorem, which is proved in
Section

6.3
.

Theorem 3.3
.

There are absolute constants
R,\varepsilon_{1}>0
such that the following hold. Let
m\geq 100
,
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
such that
\Delta(\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s})\geq 8\pi/m
, and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
such that

\rho:=\|{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{1}.

The Root-MUSIC polynomial
\widetilde{P}
has exactly two roots with reflection symmetry that are contained in the disk
D(w_{k},R\rho/m)
for each
k\in\{1,\dots,s\}
.

Step 2. Control over the extraneous roots.
The first step is insufficient to complete the main objective of this paper because the remaining roots of
\widetilde{P}
could be closer to
\partial\mathbb{D}
than the
s
roots (mod reflection) described in the theorem, which could result in the Root-MUSIC algorithm selecting a perturbed extraneous root. To show that this does not happen, we will prove a stronger assertion that
\widetilde{P}
has no other roots in an appropriate annulus containing
\partial\mathbb{D}
.

It is helpful to work with wedges that respect the natural reflection symmetry of the Root-MUSIC polynomial. For a
w\in\partial\mathbb{D}
,
r\in(0,1)
, and
\phi\in[0,\pi]
, define the closed wedge

W(w,r,\phi):=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}\colon r\leq|z|\leq r^{-1},\,|\arg(z)-\arg(w)|\leq\phi\right\}.

(3.4)

Note that
W(w,r,\phi)
is invariant under reflection across the circle. Also,
W(w,r,\pi)
is an annulus with radial thickness asymptotically equal to
2(1-r)
as
r\to 1
.

The second assertion of
Theorem

3.3
implies that
\widetilde{P}
has no other roots besides
z_{k}
and
1/\overline{z_{k}}
in a
\alpha/m
neighborhood of each
w_{k}
. We will show that for some absolute constants
\tau_{0},\alpha_{0}>0
, the polynomial
\widetilde{P}
has no other roots aside from
z_{k}
and
1/\overline{z_{k}}
in the wedge

W_{k}:=W\left(w_{k},1-\frac{\tau_{0}}{m},\frac{\alpha_{0}}{m}\right).

Next, we will show that
\widetilde{P}
has no other zeros aside from
\{z_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}
and their reflections in the annulus

\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}\colon 1-\frac{\tau_{0}}{m}\leq|z|\leq\left(1-\frac{\tau_{0}}{m}\right)^{-1}\right\}.

(3.5)

This will be accomplished by first deriving a lower bound for
|P(z)|
when
|\arg(z)-\arg(w_{k})|\geq\alpha_{0}/m
for each
w_{k}
. Through a radial perturbation argument, we will show that if
z
lies in the annulus (
3.5
) and
|\arg(z)-\arg(w_{k})|\geq\alpha_{0}/m
for all
k
, then
|\widetilde{P}(z)|>0
. This will show that
\widetilde{P}
has precisely
s
roots (mod reflection) in an annulus of thickness
\tau_{0}/m
.

Consequently, the Root-MUSIC algorithm will always select either
z_{k}
or
1/\overline{z_{k}}
for
k=1,\dots,s
. As a result, we can say that
z_{k}=\widetilde{w}_{k}
.

4
Subspace estimation for two models

4.1
Single-snapshot model

Consider a finite Fourier series
h\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
defined as

h(t):=\sum_{k=1}^{s}a_{k}e^{i\theta_{k}t}.

(4.1)

Without the knowledge of its “amplitudes”
{\boldsymbol{a}}=\{a_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}\subseteq\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and its “frequencies”
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
, the single-snapshot spectral estimation problem is to accurately approximate
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}
from perturbed samples of
h
. Suppose we collect
2m-1
consecutive samples,

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}=\begin{bmatrix}h(j)\end{bmatrix}_{j=0,1,\dots,2m-2}+{\boldsymbol{\eta}},

where
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathbb{C}^{2m-1}
represents some unknown perturbation which could be deterministic/adversarial or stochastic. By rescaling, we can assume without loss of generality that
|a_{k}|\geq 1
for each
k
.

To compute a
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
, we form a Hankel matrix

H(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}})=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{y}_{0}&\widetilde{y}_{1}&\cdots&\widetilde{y}_{m-1}\\
\widetilde{y}_{1}&\iddots&\iddots&\widetilde{y}_{m}\\
\vdots&\iddots&\iddots&\vdots\\
\widetilde{y}_{m-1}&\widetilde{y}_{m}&\cdots&\widetilde{y}_{2m-2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}.

Under appropriate conditions,

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}:=\text{top $s$-left singular vectors of }H({\boldsymbol{y}}).

(4.2)

The following result was implicitly proved in the proof of
[
10
, Theorems 4.1 and 4.2]
. It is essentially a combination of Wedin’s theorem with quantitative control over the smallest singular value of Fourier matrix
[
2
]
when
m\Delta\gtrsim 1
.

Proposition 4.1
.

There are absolute constants
C,c>0
such that the following hold. Let
m\geq 100
and
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
such that
\Delta(\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s})\geq 8\pi/m
.

(a)

Let
p\in[1,\infty]
and
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathbb{C}^{2m-1}
with
\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{p}\leq cm^{1/p}
. Then
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
in (
4.2
) is well-defined and

\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{C\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{p}}{m^{1/p}}.

(b)

Let
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
where
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
is diagonal. For any
t>0
, with probability at least
1-2m^{1-t^{2}}-2m\exp(-cm^{2}/{\rm tr}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}))
, the subspace
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
in (
4.2
) is well-defined and

\left\|{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{Ct\sqrt{{\rm tr}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})\log(m)}}{m}.

In the first case of deterministic noise, by assuming
\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{p}\leq cm^{1/p}
for an absolute
c>0
, the subspace error can be made small by making
c
small enough. Combining this with
Theorem

3.1
yields the conclusions in the first row of
Table

1
.

In the second case of stochastic noise, a standard example is
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=\sigma^{2}{\boldsymbol{I}}
, in which case
{\rm tr}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})=\sigma^{2}(2m-1)
and the subspace error is
\lesssim\sigma\sqrt{\log(m)/m}
. By assuming
m
is large enough, the subspace error can be made arbitrarily small. Combining this with
Theorem

3.1
yields the conclusions in the second row of
Table

1
.

In both cases, if the subspace error is small enough, then the correct value of
s
can be determined by examining the singular values of
H(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}})
as shown in
[
10
, Lemma 5.6]
.

Remark 4.2

(Statement in terms of noise-to-signal ratio)
.

The standard definition of noise-to-signal ratio

\gamma^{2}
is the ratio of the noise energy
\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{2}^{2}
versus signal energy
\|{\boldsymbol{y}}\|_{2}^{2}
, where
{\boldsymbol{y}}
denotes the noiseless signal and is
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}
when
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}={\boldsymbol{0}}
. Recall that
\sigma_{1}({\boldsymbol{\Phi}})\asymp\sigma_{s}({\boldsymbol{\Phi}})\asymp\sqrt{m}
when
m\Delta\geq 8\pi
due to
[
2
]
. Also recall that
\min|a_{k}|\geq 1
and that
{\boldsymbol{y}}={\boldsymbol{\Phi}}{\boldsymbol{a}}
. Putting these together, we see that

\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{2}^{2}}{m\|{\boldsymbol{a}}\|_{2}^{2}}\lesssim\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|{\boldsymbol{y}}\|_{2}^{2}}\lesssim\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{2}^{2}}{m}.

Proposition

4.1
says that if
\gamma\lesssim 1
, then the subspace error is
\lesssim\gamma
and
Theorem

3.1
says that Root-MUSIC estimates the signal frequencies with error
\lesssim\gamma/m
.

4.2
Multi-snapshot model

For
\ell=1,\dots,n
, consider a finite Fourier series
h_{\ell}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
defined as

h_{\ell}(t):=\sum_{k=1}^{s}a_{k,\ell}\,e^{i\theta_{k}t}.

(4.3)

For each
\ell
, the amplitude vector
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\ell}:=\{a_{\ell,k}\}_{k=1}^{s}
varies whereas the frequencies
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}
do not. Without the knowledge of
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{n}
and
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
, the multi-snapshot spectral estimation problem is to accurately approximate
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}
from perturbed samples of
h_{1},\dots,h_{n}
. This is sometimes also referred to as collecting multiple measurement vectors.

More specifically, suppose for each
\ell
, we obtain noisy samples of
h_{\ell}
at
t=0,1,\dots,m-1
. The
\ell
-th snapshot is the vector

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\ell}=\begin{bmatrix}h_{\ell}(j)\end{bmatrix}_{j=0,1,\dots,m-1}+{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\ell},

where
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
represents noise for the
\ell
-th snapshot. We can collect all of these measurements into matrices,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Y}}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{1}&\cdots&\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{n}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{A}}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}&\cdots&{\boldsymbol{a}}_{n}\end{bmatrix},

A natural choice of

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}:=\text{top $s$-left singular vectors of }\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Y}}.

(4.4)

This choice of

\frac{1}{n}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Y}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Y}}^{*}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\ell}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\ell}^{*}

To make this well-defined and a reasonable estimator for the correct subspace

Assumption 4.3
.

For absolute constants
K>0
and
\sigma>0
, assume the following hold.

(a)

Assumption on noise. Let
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{n}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
, where
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
is a
K\sigma
-subgaussian distribution with uncorrelated entries that are mean zero and have variance

.

(b)

Assumption on amplitudes. Let
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{a}}_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{s}
be deterministic and assume the amplitude covariance matrix

{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\ell}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\ell}^{*}

(4.5)

has rank
s
.

The rationale for these assumptions is well known. The assumption that the amplitude covariance matrix has full rank is required to ensure that
\mathbb{E}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Y}}
has rank
s
. The assumption on the noise implies that the population noise covariance matrix is
\sigma^{2}{\boldsymbol{I}}
, which implies the leading
s
-eigenvectors of
\mathbb{E}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Y}}
is exactly
{\boldsymbol{U}}
, making
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}
a consistent estimator as
n\to\infty
.
4.3
assumes the entries of
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
are uncorrelated which is weaker than the standard assumptions used for direction-of-arrival estimation, such as those found in
[
25
]

To control the subspace error, we appeal to a stochastic analogue of Wedin’s sine-theta theorem

Remark 4.5

To this end, we define the vector

, which does not require the full SVD of a Hankel/Toeplitz/covariance matrix and has fewer columns than

We again emphasize that the main contribution of this paper is a theoretical analysis of the roots of
\widetilde{P}
generated by the Root-MUSIC algorithm, which has important implications to its performance. A full numerical study and professional implementation are beyond the scope of this paper. One direction of improvement concerns the numerical stability of Root-MUSIC. Computing
z^{m}
(hence
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(z)
all other downstream terms) will result in numerical overflow for
|z|>1
and large

. This means that a naive implementation of Root-MUSIC with standard Newton’s method is likely to be numerically unstable when
m
is sufficiently large. Since

, a calculation together with formula (

Consequently, Root-MUSIC is more stable than naively expected, in an annulus of thickness
\tau/m
around the circle, which is the region of interest according to our theory. This suggests that numerical instability can be tempered by employing a Newton’s method with appropriate exit termination when an iterate leaves such an annulus.

6.1
Lemmas for Step 1a

As explained in
Section

3
, we will lower bound
|P|
by approximating it by a quadratic function, which involves dealing with
|P^{\prime\prime}(w_{k})|
. We recall the following result in
[
10
, Lemma 9.4]
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Suppose
m\geq 100
and
\Delta(\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s})\geq 8\pi/m
. There is an absolute constant
c_{0}\in(0,1/6)
such that for all
k
,

q^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{k})\geq c_{0}m^{2}.

Although the
c_{0}
that appears in this lemma is explicit and can be computed, we will not need its exact value. The important part of this inequality is that
c_{0}
does not depend on either
m
or the cardinality of
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s}
.

The next lemma truncates the power series expansion of
P
at
w_{k}
and shows that third and higher order terms are negligible whenever
|z-w_{k}|\leq c_{0}/(5m)
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Suppose
m\geq 100
and
\Delta(\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{s})\geq 8\pi/m
. If
z\in D(w_{k},c_{0}/(5m))
, then

|P(z)|\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{8}{15}e^{2c_{0}/5}\right)c_{0}m^{2}|z-w_{k}|^{2}.

Proof.

We plan to calculate the power series expansion of
P
at its double root
w_{k}=e^{i\theta_{k}}
.
We have

P(z)=\frac{1}{2}P^{\prime\prime}(w_{k})(z-w_{k})^{2}+\sum_{j=3}^{2m-2}\frac{P^{(j)}(w_{k})}{j!}(z-w_{k})^{j}.

(6.1)

By Bernstein’s inequality for complex polynomials, we see that

\max_{z\in\partial\mathbb{D}}|P^{(j)}(z)|\leq(2m-2)^{j}\max_{z\in\partial\mathbb{D}}|P(z)|=(2m-2)^{j}\|q\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leq(2m-2)^{j}.

Using this, we see that

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j=3}^{2m-2}\frac{P^{(j)}(w_{k})}{j!}(z-w_{k})^{j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 8(m-1)^{3}|z-w_{k}|^{3}\,\sum_{j=3}^{2m-2}\frac{2^{j-3}(m-1)^{j-3}|z-w_{k}|^{j-3}}{j!}

\displaystyle\leq 8(m-1)^{3}|z-w_{k}|^{3}\,\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{2^{j}(m-1)^{j}|z-w_{k}|^{j}}{(j+3)!}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{3}(m-1)^{3}|z-w_{k}|^{3}\,\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{2^{j}(m-1)^{j}|z-w_{k}|^{j}}{j!}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{3}(m-1)^{3}\,|z-w_{k}|^{3}\,e^{2(m-1)|z-w_{k}|}.

Additionally,
2.2
implies that
Q(w_{k})=Q^{\prime}(w_{k})=0
since each
w_{k}
is a root with multiplicity two of
Q
. Then a calculation shows that

P^{\prime\prime}(w_{k})=w_{k}^{m-1}Q^{\prime\prime}(w_{k})\quad\text{and}\quad q^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{k})=-w_{k}^{2}\,Q^{\prime\prime}(w_{k}).

By
Lemma

6.1
, there is a
c_{0}\in(0,1/6)
such that
q^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{k})\geq c_{0}m^{2}
. Thus,

|P^{\prime\prime}(w_{k})|=|Q^{\prime\prime}(w_{k})|=|q^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{k})|\geq c_{0}m^{2}.

Thus, inserting the above inequality into (
6.1
), we have

\displaystyle|P(z)|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}|P^{\prime\prime}(w_{k})||z-w_{k}|^{2}-\left|\sum_{j=3}^{2m-2}\frac{P^{(j)}(w_{k})}{j!}(z-w_{k})^{j}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}c_{0}m^{2}|z-w_{k}|^{2}-\frac{4}{3}(m-1)^{3}|z-w_{k}|^{3}e^{2(m-1)|z-w_{k}|}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}m^{2}|z-w_{k}|^{2}\left(c_{0}-\frac{8}{3}m|z-w_{k}|e^{2m|z-w_{k}|}\right).

By assumption,
|z-w_{k}|\leq c_{0}/(5m)
, and so

c_{0}-\frac{8}{3}m|z-w_{k}|e^{2m|z-w_{k}|}\geq c_{0}-\frac{8}{15}e^{2c_{0}/5}c_{0}.

Combining the above completes the proof.
∎

6.2
Lemmas for Step 1b

We start with some bounds for
q
, before extending this result to the complex plane. We have the global and unconditional perturbation bound,

\max_{t\in\mathbb{T}}|q(t)-\widetilde{q}(t)|\leq\|{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}^{*}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}^{*}\|_{2}=\|{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}\|_{2}=\rho.

(6.2)

As mentioned in
Section

3
, we need a stronger inequality to invoke Rouche’s theorem. We recall the following (also unconditional) inequality
[
10

\max_{k}|q(\theta_{k})-\widetilde{q}(\theta_{k})|\leq\rho^{2}.

(6.3)

To control
|P(z)-\widetilde{P}(z)|
for
z
close enough to a
w_{k}

Lemma 6.3
.

For any
k\in\{1,\dots,s\}
,
m>s
, and

, we have

\displaystyle|P(z)-\widetilde{P}(z)|

\displaystyle\leq 2m\rho\,|z-w_{k}|+\rho^{2},

\displaystyle|P(1/\overline{z})-\widetilde{P}(1/\overline{z})|

\displaystyle=|z|^{-2m+2}|P(z)-\widetilde{P}(z)|.

Proof.

It will be helpful to define
H=P-\widetilde{P}
, which is also a polynomial of degree at most
2m-2
. Fix any
z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. Let
\gamma
be the straight line segment contained in
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
that connects
w_{k}
and
z

H(z)=H(w_{k})+\int_{\gamma}H^{\prime}(w)\,dw.

By the max modulus principle and Bernstein’s inequality, we see that

\displaystyle|H(z)|

\displaystyle\leq|H(w_{k})|+|z-w_{k}|\,\max_{w\in\mathbb{D}}|H^{\prime}(w)|

\displaystyle\leq|H(w_{k})|+|z-w_{k}|\,(2m-2)\max_{w\in\partial\mathbb{D}}|H(w)|.

Observe that the definition of

\displaystyle|H(w_{k})|

\displaystyle=|q(\theta_{k})-\widetilde{q}(\theta_{k})|\leq\rho^{2},

\displaystyle\max_{w\in\partial\mathbb{D}}|H(w)|

\displaystyle=\max_{t\in\mathbb{T}}|q(t)-\widetilde{q}(t)|\leq\rho.

Combining the previous two displayed inequalities proves the first inequality of this lemma.

For the second inequality, recall Observations

\displaystyle P(1/\overline{z})-\widetilde{P}(1/\overline{z})

\displaystyle=\left(1/\overline{z}\right)^{m-1}\left(Q(1/\overline{z})-\widetilde{Q}(1/\overline{z})\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1/\overline{z}\right)^{2m-2}\left(\overline{P(z)}-\overline{\widetilde{P}(z)}\right).

Taking the absolute value completes the proof.

For any

For the remaining case where

\displaystyle|P(z)-\widetilde{P}(z)|

\displaystyle\leq|z|^{2m-2}|P(1/\overline{z})-\widetilde{P}(1/\overline{z})|
