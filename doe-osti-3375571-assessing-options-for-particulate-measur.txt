Executive Summary of CRADA Work:
For approximately 40 years, particulate matter (PM) from residential wood heaters has been
certified in North America using an EPA dilution tunnel with a filter-based gravimetric
measurement. While reasonably repeatable, this approach reports only a single value for the
entire burn sequence and does not provide information on PM at different points within the
sequence, which would support improved wood heater designs. PM monitors that report with
high time resolution offer a potential solution, but their performance for various types of heaters
and across heater operating conditions has not been extensively evaluated. In this 18-month
collaborative project, the Hearth, Patio & Barbecue Association (HPBA) and Lawrence Berkeley
National Laboratory (LBNL) tested five continuous PM instruments spanning three measurement
principles—light-scattering, inertial mass (TEOM), and triboelectric—against gravimetric and
size-resolved references on three residential heater types (cordwood, catalytic cordwood, and
pellet) across startup, high-burn, and low-burn operating phases.

The project demonstrated that instrument suitability depends strongly on heater type and
operating condition. Gravimetrically corrected light-scattering instruments tracked the size-
resolved reference well for cordwood heaters but showed weaker agreement for the pellet heater
when PM mass was dominated by particles smaller than 300 nanometers (nm) in diameter. The
error was greatest for the optical particle counter (PP8306). Because the optical instruments do
not reliably detect particles smaller than 300 nm, gravimetric correction is essential for accurate
quantitative PM mass measurements. Two of the three optical instruments (DustTrak II 8530 and
pDR-1500) include an onboard gravimetric filter cartridge, which simplifies implementation,
while the PP8306 requires a separate gravimetric measurement device. The TEOM provided
time-resolved mass measurement but under-predicted gravimetric totals and showed negative
concentrations during rapidly changing emission events. The triboelectric instrument, while
limited quantitatively, may be useful for direct stack monitoring from the chimney instead of
using a dilution tunnel.

In terms of practical and economic effectiveness, portable light-scattering instruments in this
study ($5,000–$10,000) offered the best balance of cost and portability for both laboratory and
field use, with the essential caveat that gravimetric correction is required for accurate
quantitative PM mass measurements. Two of the three optical instruments (DustTrak II 8530 and
pDR-1500) include integrated onboard gravimetric filter cartridges, while the PP8306 requires
separate gravimetric instrument. For extended field monitoring requiring accurate results,
cartridge replacement in the DustTrak II 8530 and pDR-1500 adds a step that should be
considered in deployment planning. The TEOM ($25,000–$40,000) is bulky and slated for
discontinuation in June 2026, and the low-cost triboelectric monitor would require application-
specific calibration for quantitative use, though it may still track relative trends without it. The
findings from this project inform manufacturers, certification labs, and the wood heater
community on selecting affordable, robust measurement tools to accelerate innovative wood
heater designs and support in-home performance monitoring.

Summary of Research Results:
Overview
Wood heater performance has traditionally been evaluated using time-integrated gravimetric
measurements from an EPA dilution tunnel. While repeatable, this approach provides only a
single, integrated PM value per test, obscuring the transient combustion events that dominate
real-world PM output. The tunnel is also costly and confined to the laboratory, leaving field
performance to be inferred from laboratory tests alone. Continuous PM instruments have been
proposed to address both limitations, offering high sampling resolution and compact form factors
appropriate for in-home use. However, their performance under the wide range of conditions
encountered in residential wood combustion has not been formally evaluated.

This project aimed to evaluate whether commercially available continuous PM instruments can
provide reliable, time-resolved mass concentration measurements for residential wood heaters
across diverse operating conditions. The primary goal was to identify which instruments, if any,
are suitable for laboratory and field use by testing them against established reference methods
under realistic operating conditions. Five instruments spanning three measurement principles
were compared against gravimetric and size-resolved references using three heater types
(cordwood, catalytic cordwood, and pellet) operated in startup, high-burn, and low-burn phases.

Approach and Activities Over the Period of Performance
The 18-month project was conducted following the Statement of Work (SOW) between HPBA
and LBNL and comprised six tasks:
 1. Project management and monthly meetings
 2. Identify and prepare real-time particle measurement instruments
 a. HPBA established baseline selection criteria for PM instruments that included
 requirements for accuracy, durability, cost, ease of use, and maintenance
 requirements.
 b. LBNL evaluated candidate technologies spanning beta-attenuation, mass
 accumulation, composite light-scattering, and size-resolved particle counting
 principles.
 c. Together, the team selected five instruments that best matched the selection
 criteria. Selected instruments included light-scattering (TSI DustTrak II 8530,
 Thermo MIE pDR-1500, Particles Plus 8306), inertial mass (Thermo TEOM
 1405-D), and triboelectric (Sintrol S203) measurement principles.
 3. Develop wood heater operating procedure
 a. The team developed operating protocols capturing startup, high-burn, and low-
 burn phases for a cordwood heater and a pellet heater. The protocols were
 informed by field data on homeowner use collected during prior LBNL work.
 b. Preliminary experiments confirmed that the protocols were repeatable and
 appropriately evaluated the limits of the real-time PM instruments.
 4. Install PM equipment and validate the experimental setup
 a. LBNL modified its dilution tunnel facility to accommodate sampling from all five
 PM instruments simultaneously. A secondary dilution system was added for
 instruments with lower concentration ranges, and the data acquisition software
 was updated to log measurements from all instruments.
 b. The experimental setup was validated through preliminary testing.

 3

 5. Evaluate real-time particle instruments and provide recommendations
 a. Two replicate tests were conducted for each heater and operating phase, with
 instruments benchmarked against gravimetric filter measurements (modified
 ASTM E2515-11) and against a size-resolved mass reference reconstructed from
 three particle size spectrometers spanning 5.6 nm to 20 µm.
 b. Agreement was quantified using cumulative mass ratios, Lin's concordance
 correlation coefficient, and coefficient of determination on 10-second averaged
 data.
 c. Results were reviewed with HPBA, and the team identified instruments
 appropriate for laboratory and field use.
 6. Publish results and present findings to the wood heater community (Task 6)
 a. This final report was prepared summarizing instruments evaluated, methods,
 results, and recommendations. Results were further disseminated through
 HPBExpo educational sessions, and a peer-reviewed manuscript is in preparation.

All milestones and deliverables were completed, with the following modifications. The team
evaluated three heaters rather than the two called for in the SOW, adding a second cordwood
heater so that both catalytic and non-catalytic models were represented. This expansion better
captured the diversity of residential wood heating technologies and allowed the team to
characterize instrument response to catalyst engagement events. Additionally, more than three
replicate tests were conducted for most instruments, satisfying the SOW requirement. Because
the TEOM was available for only a limited period, we only report data from replicates when all
five instruments operated simultaneously. This enabled direct comparison across all instruments.
Replicate tests with the other instruments outside the reported data showed consistent results,
giving confidence that the reported results are representative of overall instrument performance.

Technical Results and Outcomes
The project successfully evaluated all five selected instruments across the planned heater types
and operating conditions, meeting the primary technical objectives outlined in the SOW. The
instruments evaluated are shown in Figure 1. Key findings are summarized below.

When gravimetrically corrected, the light-scattering instruments closely tracked the size-resolved
reference during cordwood and catalytic-cordwood operation, especially during startup and high-
burn conditions. During pellet heater operation, all three light-scattering instruments showed
weaker agreement with the reference. This is consistent with the known limitation that optical
instruments do not reliably detect particles smaller than 300 nm, which dominated PM mass in
pellet exhaust. The decrease in agreement was most severe for the PP8306, moderate for the
pDR-1500, and least pronounced for the DustTrak II 8530, suggesting that scattering geometry
and wavelength selection influence performance. Gravimetric correction is essential for accurate
quantitative PM mass measurements with optical instruments. The DustTrak II 8530 and pDR-
1500 include an onboard gravimetric filter cartridge, while the PP8306 does not and requires a
separate gravimetric instrument. For extended field monitoring where accurate quantitative PM
measurements are needed, filter replacement introduces an additional step that may interrupt
continuous data collection and should be factored into deployment planning. For qualitative
monitoring of relative trends, field use without frequent filter replacement may be acceptable,
though results should be interpreted accordingly.

 4

The TEOM provided direct mass measurement but did not reliably report total emitted PM mass
per phase. The two nominally redundant TEOM measurement channels (A and B) diverged in
several tests, and these conditions were only identifiable through retrospective review of
auxiliary flow logs rather than real-time instrument alerts (see Figure 2). Channel-averaged ratios
to the gravimetric reference were 0.73 for high-burn and 0.57 for low-burn, with individual
channels exhibiting sustained negative cumulative mass values during certain tests. These trends
suggest that material may have been shed from the instrument's oscillating filter, that semi-
volatile compounds may have evaporated from it, or both, under the rapidly changing, high-
concentration conditions typical of residential wood heaters. The data collected here cannot
determine which mechanism dominated. Notably, the manufacturer has announced
discontinuation of this TEOM model in June 2026.

The triboelectric instrument showed limited suitability for dilution-tunnel measurements as
configured in this study. It did not detect the increase in PM during startup and reported a large
false signal following catalyst engagement. The false signal did not correspond to any change in
PM, sample temperature, or sample humidity (see Figure 3). It also exhibited substantial zero-
point drift when concentrations were too low for it to measure reliably during pellet operation.
This instrument may be better suited for monitoring in high concentration environments, such as
sampling directly from the chimney.

The results show that no single instrument is universally suitable across all heater types and
operating conditions. Instrument selection requires balancing data needs against practical factors
including portability, cost, consumable requirements, and operational reliability. Detailed
methods, data, and analyses supporting these conclusions will be available in a peer-reviewed
publication.

Products, Applications, and Technology Transfer
The project produced several outputs supporting technology transfer to the residential wood
heater community. A peer-reviewed manuscript summarizing the experimental evaluation and
statistical analysis has been prepared for submission to a relevant journal, providing the
community with detailed performance data for the evaluated instruments. Results were also
presented at HPBExpo educational sessions to help the wood heating sector make more informed
PM instrument selections for their own development work.

A central outcome of this project was providing practical guidance on PM instrument selection
for wood heater research. Table 1 summarizes the key strengths and limitations of the five
instruments tested under dilution-tunnel conditions.

Overall, the DustTrak II 8530 and pDR-1500 performed well for dilution-tunnel research, both
demonstrating strong correlation with size-resolved reference measurements, fast setup, and
portability. Both instruments showed elevated error during pellet heater operation, where PM
mass was dominated by particles smaller than 300 nm, with the pDR-1500 being somewhat more
affected than the DustTrak II 8530. Gravimetric correction is essential for accurate quantitative
assessment, and both instruments include an integrated gravimetric filter cartridge to facilitate
this measurement. The Particles Plus 8306 offers unique size-resolved data at low cost but is
constrained by a narrow concentration range (max 20 mg/m³) that necessitates higher dilution
than other instruments. It also showed the largest errors of the three optical instruments during
pellet heater operation. Like the other optical instruments, gravimetric correction is essential for
the PP8306. However, unlike the DustTrak II 8530 and pDR-1500, the PP8306 has no onboard
gravimetric cartridge and requires a separate gravimetric instrument. The TEOM 1405-D
provides direct mass measurement but did not reliably report total emitted mass under the test
conditions, sometimes showing mass loss rather than accumulation on its filter. It is further
limited by high cost, long setup time, and the manufacturer's announced discontinuation in June
2026. The Sintrol S203 performed poorly under dilution-tunnel conditions but may be a
candidate for in-stack monitoring of relative trends, where concentrations are higher.

The project also fostered collaboration between LBNL, HPBA, and wood-heating manufacturers
who provided test equipment and technical guidance. The experimental dataset, test protocols,
and analysis methods developed during the project provide a foundation for future evaluations of
additional instruments, such as opacity sensors and black carbon monitors recommended for
follow-on work. By characterizing how well each continuous instrument resolves transient PM
events under controlled laboratory conditions, the project helps inform manufacturer design
decisions and provides a basis for evaluating which instruments are most promising for future
field deployment. Together, these contributions advance the measurement infrastructure needed
to support continued innovation and improved performance in residential wood heating
technology that benefits both consumers and manufacturers.
