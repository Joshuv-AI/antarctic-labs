Introduction
This report introduces two research papers and their associated supporting materials, focusing
on the integration of Cyber-Informed Engineering (CIE) and Model-Based Systems Engineering
(MBSE). These papers explore how combining the principles of CIE with the methodology of
MBSE can significantly enhance the security and resilience of critical engineered systems,
representing a key area of research and development of CIE implementation.

Cyber-Informed Engineering (CIE) is a forward-thinking approach that embeds cybersecurity
principles directly into the engineering process. By doing so, CIE aims to prevent the
consequences of cyber-attacks from impacting the safety, reliability, and performance of critical
infrastructure systems. This approach ensures that the impacts of potential cyber threats are
addressed from the outset, providing a robust defense mechanism against cyber vulnerabilities
that is separate and distinct from, yet complementary to, existing cybersecurity information
security control practices.

Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE), on the other hand, offers a structured and visual
methodology for designing complex systems using digital models. MBSE integrates design
decisions across the various disciplines used to realize a complex engineered system and
ensures traceability and consistency throughout the system's lifecycle. This formalized approach
supports system requirements, design, analysis, verification, and validation activities, making it
an indispensable tool for managing the complexity of modern engineering projects and a key
method to successfully implement CIE in engineering practices.

The integration of CIE and MBSE begins by leveraging CIE as a new set of system requirements
during the requirements definition phase. This initial step is crucial as it embeds digital risk
considerations into the system design process from the very beginning. The use of Systems
Modeling Language (SysML) plays a necessary role in this integration, providing traceability for
CIE by linking its principles to the structure and behavior of subsystems and components as
demonstrated in the first report. This ensures compliance with CIE guidelines and maintains a
clear connection between cybersecurity and CIE requirements and system design.

Evaluating this digital risk is a central aspect of modern engineering design and one that is
directly mitigated by the adoption of CIE principles. Specifically, engineers use system
requirements to organize, quantify, and enforce the expected behavior of the engineered
system. CIE offers the principled mindset that helps engineers identify opportunities for
engineered controls, which control the impact and recovery resulting from cyber threats,
contributing to the overall protection of the system and the organizations managing it. This
collaboration between engineers and traditional cybersecurity professionals ensures a
comprehensive approach to system security.

As systems become increasingly complex, practices like MBSE are essential to ensure that all
disciplines and organizations involved in the engineering system lifecycle are coordinated,
integrated, and evaluated to deliver a successful system and its operation. MBSE's structured
approach is a key example that helps manage this complexity by providing a clear and
consistent framework for system design and analysis.

The results of these reports highlight numerous opportunities for future work within the CIE and

Abstract

Engineering design in the field of industrial engineering, such as designing automated factories
or warehouses, is critical for the effective operation of facilities. Any design flaws introduced early
can result in significant capital expenses to correct. However, early-stage engineering design is
inherently complex. The systems are not yet built, requiring designers to integrate various aspects,
including digital engineering and cybersecurity, to support virtual representations throughout the
design process.
In this study, we propose an approach to integrate Cyber-Informed Engineering (CIE) principles
into model-based systems engineering (MBSE). This approach facilitates the development of a
digital thread for engineering systems, ensuring secure digital artifacts in the design of industrial
engineering systems.

Keywords: Model-Based Systems Engineering, Cyber-informed Engineering, digital engineering

1. Introduction
As digital engineering supports engineers in accelerating decision-making, modeling behaviors,
and validating requirements, cybersecurity has emerged as a critical concern across industries.
Over time, the sophistication of attack methodologies and the frequency of cyber-attacks have
escalated to unprecedented levels. Despite growing awareness of cyber risks, traditional
engineering practices often treat cybersecurity as an afterthought rather than a fundamental
element of system design, resulting in costly and challenging-to-mitigate vulnerabilities post-
deployment. Given this, it is imperative that conventional engineering methodologies evolve to
incorporate cybersecurity considerations from the outset [1].

Incorporating cybersecurity considerations from the outset of engineering design is a complex
question. In 2022, the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) released the Congressionally-directed
National Cyber-Informed Engineering Strategy [2], which addresses this challenge. Cyber-
Informed Engineering (CIE) offers a systematic approach to embedding cybersecurity principles

 Page 4 of 21

throughout the system lifecycle, from initial design to retirement. It achieves this by incorporating
twelve core principles, each targeting a different aspect of system security enhancement and
resilience. These principles guide engineering teams to proactively address cybersecurity
concerns, distinguishing CIE from traditional security approaches that primarily rely on reactive
measures. By applying these principles across the design process, engineers can enhance
system defenses, ensuring that security is integrated at every stage of development and
minimizing potential attack vectors. The twelve core CIE principles are: Consequence-Focused
Design, Engineered Controls, Secure Information Architecture, Design Simplification, Layered
Defenses, Active Defense, Interdependency Evaluation, Digital Asset Awareness, Cyber-Secure
Supply Chain Controls, Planned Resilience, Engineering Information Control, and Organizational
Culture [3].

While CIE provides key principles to help engineering designers cultivate a cybersecurity mindset
throughout the system lifecycle, effectively implementing these principles remains a challenge.
Engineers need a robust method to seamlessly integrate these principles into their existing design
processes or models. For example, Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE) and the System
Modeling Language (SysML) have become widely adopted methodologies in engineering, offering
a structured, visual approach to designing complex systems through digital models. The versatility
of MBSE offers an opportunity to embed security measures at each stage of development,
enhancing traceability and consistency [4]. In light of this, it is crucial to incorporate CIE principles
into the MBSE framework, empowering engineering designers to integrate cybersecurity
considerations throughout the entire system lifecycle.

In this paper, we propose a systematic approach to integrate CIE principles into the MBSE
framework. This framework leverages CIE principles as system requirements during the
requirements definition phase and uses traceability in SysML to link these principles to the
structure and behavior of subsystems or components, ensuring that the design complies with CIE
guidelines. The proposed framework not only enhances system security but also improves the
efficiency of the project design process by identifying and mitigating cyber risks early in the design
phase, thereby reducing costly security retrofits and operational disruptions.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section 2 presents a literature review on CIE,
MBSE, and their integration. Section 3 details the proposed methodology for incorporating CIE
principles into MBSE. Section 4 illustrates the application of this methodology through a practical
case study. Finally, Section 5 provides concluding remarks and future research directions.

2. Literature Review
This section reviews key topics related to the study, including cybersecurity and its integration
within the MBSE framework.

Cybersecurity is an increasingly concern, with cyberattacks resulting in financial losses,
reputational damage, and system failures, all of which compromise reliability, availability,
maintainability, and safety (RAMS) [5], [6]. In 2023, cyber incidents in the United States led to
losses totaling $12.3 billion [7]. As cyberattacks grow more sophisticated, there is a stronger
emphasis on ‘secure-by-design’ methodologies, which prioritize embedding cybersecurity from

 Page 5 of 21

the outset [8]. CIE aligns with this shift by integrating security controls throughout the engineering
lifecycle, mitigating vulnerabilities and strengthening resilience [2].

MBSE is a formalized approach that uses modeling to support system requirements, design,
analysis, verification, and validation activities, starting in the conceptual design phase and
continuing throughout development and later lifecycle stages [9]. It is widely used across
industries, including automotive, aerospace, and critical infrastructure, to enhance traceability and
communication among stakeholders [10]. However, key advantages of MBSE—such as managed
complexity, reduced risk and cost, and improved communication—remain underutilized, leaving
critical gaps in risk mitigation [11].

Effectively implementing CIE requires a structured framework to ensure that cybersecurity
principles are integrated into system design from the outset [8]. While existing literature primarily
discusses CIE conceptually, it has yet to be incorporated into the MBSE framework. This research
aims to bridge this gap by defining CIE principles as explicit requirements within MBSE models,
ensuring their traceability across all stages of development. A traceability matrix will be used to
map CIE principles to system components, including blocks, use cases, and activity diagrams. By
embedding cybersecurity considerations at every stage, this research aims to enhance system
security and resilience through a structured MBSE approach.

3. Methodology
3.1 Overview
To integrate CIE principles into the MBSE framework, the proposed methodology incorporates
these principles as explicit system requirements during the requirements definition phase. It
employs traceability within SysML to map CIE principles to system-level structures and behaviors
to any sub-level components, ensuring their integration throughout the system lifecycle. System
requirements, typically defined in the early design stage, guide subsequent phases, including
logical architecture, physical architecture, model validation, verification, and integration. By
embedding CIE principles as fundamental system needs, this approach ensures that
cybersecurity considerations are systematically incorporated throughout the design process,
achieving CIE compliance. Establishing a structured linkage between security principles and
system components enhances system security while improving design efficiency. Furthermore,
this approach enables early identification and mitigation of cyber risks, reducing the need for
costly security retrofits and minimizing potential operational disruptions.

3.2 Library for CIE Principles
We implement all CIE principles as requirements and integrated them into a reusable CIE model
library within the MBSE framework. See Figure 1. This model library ensures that cybersecurity
considerations are embedded throughout the design process, allowing for a structured and
standardized approach to addressing potential vulnerabilities. By capturing these principles as
formal requirements within the MBSE environment, we enable the system to systematically
incorporate cybersecurity measures, ensuring compliance with Cyber-Informed Engineering from
the outset.

Figure 1. CIE principles implemented as system requirements.

Using a CIE principles requirement library provides several significant benefits, particularly in
terms of reusability. By capturing cybersecurity requirements as formalized principles within a
library, the system's security framework can be reused across multiple projects, streamlining the
design process and ensuring consistency. This approach incentivizes engineers to consider
cybersecurity from the outset, encouraging proactive thinking about security risks during the
early stages of design. Integrating CIE principles directly into MBSE tools ensures that
cybersecurity is not an afterthought but a foundational element of the system architecture. This
early incorporation of security considerations leads to more resilient systems, with vulnerabilities
addressed in the design phase rather than after deployment, enhancing both efficiency and
system protection.

3.3 Maintain Traceability Through the Design Process
During the design process, the designer may perform system decomposition, define logical and
physical architectures, and carry out model verification and validation. To maintain traceability, we
can use satisfy or trace relationships, ensuring that we can identify which elements of the design
address specific CIE principles. Typically, the larger the system being modeled, the more intricate
the design becomes. This increased complexity can make it challenging to maintain a clear and
coherent understanding of the overall model. By using these relationships to preserve traceability,
we assist both the model designer and external users in an easy and efficient understanding of
the connections between the system design and the relevant CIE principles.

The use of traceability for the implementation of the CIE requirement library offers several key
benefits. First, it allows us to maintain clear and consistent traceability throughout the design
process, ensuring that we can easily review the design for each CIE principle at any stage. This
capability enables quick identification of potential gaps or vulnerabilities, ensuring that
cybersecurity considerations are continuously addressed. Additionally, maintaining traceability
ensures flexibility in adapting to changes. If customer requirements evolve, we can still incorporate
and align CIE principles without disrupting the overall design integrity, allowing for a more
adaptable and resilient system. Ultimately, traceability enhances both the consistency of the
design and the system's ability to meet evolving security and functional needs.

3.4 Perform Overview of CIE Compliance
The requirement traceability matrix is a powerful tool for assessing whether a system design is
CIE compliant. This matrix enables us to utilize not only satisfy relationships but also recursive
relationships to trace modeling components back to their alignment with CIE principles.

By creating and referencing the traceability matrix (see Figure 2), which maps the twelve CIE
principles to the relevant system components, we can easily identify which components support
these principles. The requirement traceability matrix provides a comprehensive overview of how
the system will satisfy the twelve CIE principles, allowing the designer to quickly identify any
gaps in CIE compatibility throughout the design process.

The requirement traceability matrix offers several key benefits. First, it provides a systematic
overview of how the CIE principles are implemented across the design, offering a
comprehensive view of the system's alignment with cybersecurity requirements. This clarity
allows stakeholders to easily track the progress of CIE integration throughout the design
process. Additionally, the matrix facilitates the easy identification of the structural components or
behavioral elements responsible for realizing each specific CIE principle, making it simpler to
pinpoint areas of the design that need attention or improvement. This level of organization
enhances both the efficiency and effectiveness of the design process, ensuring that
cybersecurity considerations are thoroughly addressed.

4. Conclusion
This paper proposes an approach to incorporating CIE principles into the MBSE framework. By
leveraging MBSE’s structured, visual approach to system design, engineers can ensure that
cybersecurity considerations are integrated at every stage of development. The proposed
framework provides a method to incorporate CIE principles as system requirements and
establishes traceability through SysML to ensure compliance and enhance system security. This
not only improves system resilience but also mitigates cyber risks early in the design phase,
reducing the need for costly retrofits and minimizing operational disruptions.

The integration of CIE principles into MBSE represents a significant step forward in strengthening
the security of engineered systems. Future research can explore further refinements to this
methodology and its application across various industries to improve the effectiveness of
cybersecurity measures in system design.

Abstract

Water booster pumps play a critical role in infrastructure by enabling the distribution of water from
treatment plants to residential and commercial areas. With many of these systems now operated
remotely and managed via cloud-based controls, securing them against cyber threats is crucial to
prevent disruptions, malicious manipulation, and targeted attacks. This abstract illustrates how the
Object-Oriented Systems Engineering Method (OOSEM), combined with Model-Based Systems
Engineering (MBSE) using System Modeling Language (SysML), integrates Cyber-Informed
Engineering (CIE) principles into water booster pump station design. By adopting proactive
cybersecurity mindsets from the earliest stages, CIE helps mitigate vulnerabilities through
embedded controls and defense mechanisms. MBSE’s comprehensive modeling framework
supports robust simulation and analysis, enhancing system resilience against cyber threats while
enabling secure remote monitoring. This approach not only improves the reliability, safety, and
sustainability of water supply systems but also sets a new systems engineering approach for
critical infrastructure engineering. Addressing unique challenges in smaller municipal utilities, this
methodology fosters secure, efficient, and adaptable water systems that meet evolving community
needs and defend against growing cyber risks, building a more resilient water infrastructure.

Keywords: Model-Based Systems Engineering, Critical Infrastructure Protection, Cyber-informed
Engineering

1. Introduction
Critical infrastructure is the foundation of modern society, encompassing essential sectors such
as energy, water, transportation, communications, healthcare, and financial services. Disruptions
to these systems can have severe consequences, affecting economic stability, public safety, and
national security. As the world becomes increasingly interconnected, ensuring the resilience of
critical infrastructure is more important than ever.

                                                                                      Page 11 of 21

Recent years have seen a rise in cyber threats targeting these vital systems. Hackers now have
the capability to inflict widespread damage through remote digital attacks, fundamentally altering
the threat landscape. This evolving risk necessitates a shift in how critical infrastructure is secured,
requiring proactive and innovative approaches to cybersecurity.

The increasing reliance on digital controls has exposed critical infrastructure systems to significant
cybersecurity risks. Traditionally, these systems were designed without cybersecurity
considerations, with security measures applied only after completion. As a result, they now face
inherent vulnerabilities that demand immediate attention to prevent disruptions and ensure
operational resilience.

The advancement of digital engineering [3] and Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE) [4]
has transformed the traditional design of such systems. Digital engineering leverages modeling
languages such as System Modeling Language (SysML) [5] and digital twins to enhance system
development, enabling more precise modeling and analysis throughout the lifecycle. MBSE, as a
structured methodology, replaces traditional document-based approaches with comprehensive
digital models, improving system integration, traceability, and collaboration across disciplines.
Together, these innovations streamline the design process, enhance risk assessment capabilities,
and facilitate the early incorporation of design considerations.

While digital engineering enhances the early design process by enabling more precise modeling
and analysis throughout a system’s lifecycle, a critical gap remains—the integration of
cybersecurity considerations from the beginning. Many companies still view cybersecurity as a
separate discipline rather than an integral part of early engineering design. As a result, security
measures are often implemented only after the system has been developed, making cybersecurity
protections more costly and challenging to integrate effectively.

To address this critical issue, this research focuses on embedding a cybersecurity mindset into
the systems engineering design process. Specifically, we use Cyber-Informed Engineering (CIE)
[6] as the foundational concept to integrate cybersecurity considerations into the system lifecycle
within a Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE) environment. By incorporating cybersecurity
principles early in the design phase, this approach enhances system resilience and security. This
integration represents a key contribution, bridging the gap between traditional engineering
practices and proactive cybersecurity protection. We will use the design of a water booster pump
station as a case study to demonstrate the integration of CIE principles throughout the entire
product lifecycle. This example will illustrate how cybersecurity considerations can be embedded
at every stage of the design process, ensuring a more secure and resilient system.

and defense principles. Simultaneously, OOSEM provides a structured modeling framework that

enhances system analysis and strengthens traceability throughout the system lifecycle. Together,

This approach not only strengthens system reliability, safety, and sustainability but also
establishes a novel methodology for critical infrastructure engineering. A key aspect of
incorporating CIE into system design is the to-be analysis, which serves as the foundation for

3. Water Booster Pump Specific System Design
3.1 As-Is Analysis
The as-is analysis evaluates the current state of the water booster pump station. Its primary
objective is to document the existing system and its operation without modifications. This analysis
provides a clear understanding of the system’s present (as-is) design, enabling the organization
to implement effective changes and work toward an improved (to-be) design. By identifying
deficiencies and limitations in the current system, engineers can make informed improvements to
enhance efficiency and performance.

In the case of a water booster pump system within critical infrastructure, a small municipal water
utility faces significant efficiency challenges. Currently, the utility’s booster pump stations are
operated manually, requiring operators to frequently travel between remote locations. This
reliance on manual operation increases operational costs and reduces productivity, underscoring
the need for a more automated and efficient solution.

Figure 2 illustrates the current as-is water booster pump system, which has several critical
limitations. All pump adjustments require direct manual intervention, and the utility lacks
continuous real-time monitoring of pump status. The existing communication subsystem—limited
to an alarm and a telephone—is ineffective in the absence of on-site personnel. As a result, the
utility operates reactively, addressing issues only after they arise rather than proactively
preventing them.

Beyond system design, an as-is analysis must also include an assessment of users and
organizational structure within the municipal water utility. A Block Definition Diagram (BDD) is
developed to illustrate the hierarchy of the utility, showing how different departments and specific
user roles are structured. This will provide a comprehensive understanding of operational
workflows and support the development of a more efficient future-state system.

3.2. Cybersecurity Challenging
To improve efficiency and streamline operations, the municipal water utility plans to implement a
cloud-based control system, centralizing station monitoring and management within a single
command center. While this transition offers significant benefits—enhancing operational
efficiency and enabling remote access—it also introduces new cybersecurity risks. Integrating
digital control components increases the system’s vulnerability to cyber threats, which, if
exploited, could have severe consequences for the surrounding community. Unauthorized access
could allow hackers to disrupt water distribution, manipulate water quality parameters, or even
cause physical damage to pumping equipment and the pipeline network. Such attacks may result
in water shortages from burst pipes, contamination risks due to chemical tampering, and other
serious public health hazards.

3.2.1 PROTECTING AGAINST AN ON/OFF ATTACK
One of the most severe threats associated with integrating cloud-based control is the risk of a
hacker gaining unauthorized access to a remote pump’s pressure controls. In this scenario, the
hacker could simultaneously activate any pump at a remote location, exploiting the redundancy
designed for routine maintenance. This action could trigger a catastrophic event known as water
hammer—a sudden surge in pressure caused by abrupt changes in water flow. Such a pressure
spike could lead to widespread pipe ruptures across the distribution network, resulting in

                                                                                  Page 15 of 21

extensive infrastructure damage, water loss, service disruptions, and potential contamination
risks.

To mitigate this threat using CIE, a mechanical time-delay relay can be added into the system.
This relay automatically prevents any pump status changes for several minutes, creating a crucial
buffer for human intervention. Upon activation, an immediate alert is sent to utility staff, giving
them time to reverse harmful changes before they take effect. This mechanical time-delay relay
exemplifies a proactive cybersecurity measure. By introducing a physical safeguard between
digital commands and actual pump operations, the utility can strengthen its defense against cyber-
attacks, ensuring that potential threats are intercepted before they can inflict widespread damage.

3.2.2 PROTECTING AGAINST A VALVE ATTACK
Another critical vulnerability identified during the consequence-focused design phase is the
potential for hackers to gain control over valve operations, both at individual pumps and across
the distribution network. If an attacker were to abruptly close valves within the system, it could
trigger a severe water hammer effect, similar to the pump on/off attack scenario. The sudden
valve closure would rapidly decelerate fluid flow, converting kinetic energy into a dangerous
pressure surge. By restricting water flow to certain areas, the hacker could create localized
pressure buildups in the remaining open sections of the piping system. This abrupt redirection
and concentration of water flow could overload the infrastructure, resulting in pipe ruptures and
widespread service disruptions.

To mitigate this valve-targeted attack, the utility can implement a mechanical time-delay relay
system specifically designed for valve operations. This safeguard mirrors the protective measure
used in the pump on/off scenario but is tailored to prevent unauthorized valve manipulations.
When a valve status change is triggered via cloud control, the system activates a brief buffer
period, temporarily preventing any immediate adjustments. Simultaneously, an alert is dispatched
to utility staff, notifying them of the attempted change. This delay provides personnel with critical
time to assess the situation, verify the legitimacy of the command, and, if necessary, override any
suspicious or potentially harmful instructions.

3.2.3 PROTECTING AGAINST A CHEMICAL ATTACK
The final critical vulnerability identified through consequence-focused design is the potential
manipulation of the chemical subsystem at booster pump stations. This subsystem is responsible
for adding chlorine to the water throughout the distribution network, ensuring its safety for
consumption. A chemical-targeted cyberattack could involve a hacker gaining access to the
chlorine controls and setting the chlorine input to zero. This scenario is particularly dangerous
because, while an increase in chlorine levels would be quickly detected due to its noticeable odor,
a complete absence of chlorine could go undetected for an extended period. The lack of
disinfection could lead to widespread water contamination, posing severe health risks to the
surrounding community.

To safeguard against this vulnerability, the utility can implement a layered defense strategy based
on the CIE principles of defense in depth and engineering controls. The new design introduces
redundancy by incorporating a second chemical intake tank into the system. If an adversary
attempts to manipulate the chlorine levels in the active tank, the system is programmed to

                                                                                      Page 16 of 21

automatically lock down and halt chlorine addition from the compromised tank. Simultaneously,
control seamlessly switches to the redundant tank, ensuring uninterrupted water treatment.
Following this automated response, the entire chemical subsystem enters a secure lockdown
state, preventing any further unauthorized manipulation. Utility staff must manually override the
system to restore normal operations, requiring either a specific security code or dual-factor
authentication, adding an extra layer of protection.

3.3 To-Be Analysis
The “To-Be” analysis serves as the foundation for implementing the necessary changes identified
during the “As-Is” analysis. This phase enables organizations to clearly define, design, and test
proposed modifications to the current system. The process begins with the development of a
vision statement and operational concept, establishing a high-level framework for the system's
transformation and redesign. These initial steps set the direction and tone for subsequent detailed
analysis and planning, ensuring a structured and strategic approach to implementation.

3.3.1 VISION STATEMENT
Our vision is to safeguard the future of water infrastructure by protecting remotely operated water
booster pump systems—managed through cloud-based control—from cyber threats. With a
proactive approach, we aim to prevent unplanned shutdowns, malicious pump manipulation, and
targeted attacks. Through enhanced engineering design, we will ensure a safe, reliable, and
sustainable water supply for our communities, preserving both efficiency and security in an era of
evolving cyber risks.

3.3.2 OPERATIONAL CONCEPT
The operational concept for securing water booster pump systems against cyber threats requires
embedding a cybersecurity-first mindset from the earliest stages of design through
implementation. While MBSE provides a structured methodology for managing the entire system
lifecycle—from conception and design to verification and validation—it currently lacks integrated
cybersecurity considerations. By incorporating CIE principles, this approach strengthens MBSE
by addressing cybersecurity risks during the design phase. The core objective of this project is to
seamlessly integrate MBSE and CIE, ensuring that cybersecurity is embedded from concept
development through verification and validation. This integrated approach will provide
comprehensive protection for water booster pump systems, safeguarding them against a wide
range of cyber threats.

3.3.3 USE CASES
Building upon the vision statement and operational concept, the next step is developing use cases,
which serve as a tool for defining system functionality in terms of how it will be used to meet
various user objectives. In the context of a water booster pump system, key use cases include
“Protecting Against an On/Off Attack,” “Protecting Against a Valve Attack,” and “Protecting
Against a Chemical Attack.” These scenarios are typically illustrated using two key SysML
diagrams. The Use Case Diagram provides a high-level stakeholder analysis, clarifying their
interactions with the system and helping stakeholders understand the system’s scope and the
various user roles engaged in security operations. The Activity Diagram can be directly tied to

                                                                                    Page 17 of 21

each use case and outlines the step-by-step actions actors must take to eliminate threats or
achieve a desired outcome. By offering a clear, structured system processes, these diagrams
enhance understanding for both technical and non-technical stakeholders, ensuring effective
communication of security measures and operational workflows.

3.3.4 REQUIREMENTS
After developing the use cases, the next step in the “To-Be” analysis is deriving the desired system
requirements. These requirements define the essential functionalities and capabilities that the
system must have to operate effectively and align with the vision of the system. To organize and
illustrate these requirements, a SysML Requirements Diagram is employed, offering a visual tool
that clearly represents individual requirements and their interconnections. For example, in the
water booster pump system, a key requirement is the “Mechanical Time Delay Relay
Requirement”, which specifies that the pump must be equipped with a functional mechanical time
delay relay capable of immediately halting operations when activated. From this primary
requirement, a more specific, derived requirement is the “At least15-Minute Stop Period”, which
dictates that, once triggered, the mechanical time delay relay must suspend pump operations for
at least 15 minutes. These requirements are derived from the use cases of “Protecting Against an
On/Off Attack” and “Protecting Against a Valve Attack”.

3.3.5 FUNCTIONAL ARCHITECTURE
After deriving the requirements, we design the functional architecture of the system. The
functional architecture illustrates the key objectives of what the system must achieve. This
architecture is represented using a SysML BDD. The logical architecture is crucial in breaking
down the system into its logical components and demonstrating how these components interact
and collaborate to accomplish the system's goals. For the water booster pump system, examples
of logical blocks include the “mechanical time delay relay” and the “pump.” While these represent
specific aspects of the system, they are conceptual components that define what the system must
do, rather than the actual physical elements, which will be addressed later in the physical
architecture.

3.3.7 PHYSICAL ARCHITECTURE
After developing the logical architecture, the next step is to design the physical architecture of the
new system, which is illustrated using a SysML BDD. The physical architecture uses blocks to
represent the actual physical components of the system. It outlines how the system will perform
the specific tasks identified in the logical architecture. Once both the logical and physical
architectures are developed, a diagram can be created to connect these two BDDs, further
illustrating the relationships between the system's logical components and their physical
counterparts.

3.3.8 PARAMETRIC DIAGRAM
Another important model is the SysML Parametric Diagram, which is used to define mathematical
and logical constraints on system properties. It serves as an analysis tool to ensure the system is
performing as expected. SysML Parametric Diagrams illustrate key performance metrics, such as
the flow rate of the pump and the energy conversion between hydraulic and electrical power.

4. Conclusion
As critical infrastructure systems become increasingly digitized, integrating cybersecurity into
their design is essential for resilience and security. This research demonstrates how CIE,
combined with MBSE and OOSEM, proactively mitigates cyber risks by embedding security from
the outset. Using a water booster pump system as a case study, we illustrate how CIE enhances
system security while MBSE and OOSEM provide a structured framework for analysis and
lifecycle management. This approach strengthens infrastructure resilience against emerging
threats.

Future work will refine CIE integration across sectors, develop standardized cybersecurity
frameworks, and explore scalability, ensuring more secure and sustainable critical infrastructure
systems.
