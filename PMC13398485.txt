Holocene ocean-atmosphere coupling and Mediterranean sensitivity to Atlantic circulation: Lessons from the Late Bronze Age collapse
Authors: Katherine Power, Qiong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Climate variability has profoundly influenced societies in the semiarid Eastern Mediterranean. Proxy records indicate progressive mid-Holocene drying culminating in severe droughts ∼1250 BCE, coincident with the Late Bronze Age collapse (1300 to 900 BCE). Using the EC-Earth3-Tr8K transient Holocene simulation, we reconstruct regional hydroclimate evolution and its underlying mechanisms. The model reproduces a gradual orbitally driven aridification, spatially heterogeneous across the region, with persistent drying in the Levant but enhanced effective moisture in parts of the Balkans and Anatolia due to cooling-induced evapotranspiration decline. Superimposed on this long-term trend are extreme drought events, which arise from the alignment of Atlantic internal variability across centennial to millennial timescales, amplifying drying on an already arid background state. These results provide model-based evidence of how evolving amplitude and phase structure of Atlantic internal variability reshaped Eastern Mediterranean hydroclimate through the mid-to-late Holocene. We demonstrate that oceanic variability can manifest as abrupt regional crises when ecological and societal thresholds are crossed.

INTRODUCTION

A mid-Holocene long-term drying trend has been widely documented across the Eastern Mediterranean, extending from the Balkans in the north (1–9), across Anatolia (10–18), and southward into the Levant (19–22). Superimposed upon this gradual aridification, repeated episodes of intensified drought are recorded during the late second millennium BCE (1250 BCE) in the Levant (22, 23), the Dead Sea (24), the Peleponnese (9, 25), and Anatolia (15, 18, 26). These drought intervals broadly coincide with the Late Bronze Age (LBA) collapse (1350 to 1050 BCE), a period marked by widespread political fragmentation, demographic change, and economic contraction (27–30) across several Eastern Mediterranean societies, including the Mycenaeans in Greece (31), the Hittites in Anatolia (32, 33) and the Minoans in Crete (34).

The alignment between hydroclimatic deterioration and societal disruption has motivated extensive debate regarding the role of climate in the LBA transition (22, 35, 36). Hydroclimate variability exerts a disproportionate influence on societies situated in semiarid transition zones. With ecosystems and agricultural systems operating close to moisture thresholds, relatively modest shifts in the balance between precipitation and evaporative demand can trigger nonlinear responses in vegetation, food production, and resource stability. In precipitation-dependent agrarian societies, such as those populating the Eastern Mediterranean during the LBA, vulnerabilities were amplified, sustained reductions in effective moisture imposing additional stress on food production and trade networks, potentially triggering large-scale migrations. However, the extent to which hydroclimatic variability contributed to these transformations remains contested.

Although the temporal correspondence between hydroclimatic stress and societal disruption has been extensively discussed, the climatic mechanisms responsible for this evolution remain unresolved. The late Holocene drying trend unfolds over millennia, whereas extreme drought intervals appear clustered over much shorter timescales. Whether these centennial-scale droughts represent the amplified expression of the long-term background trend, or reflect distinct modes of climate variability superimposed upon it, remains unclear. Resolving this distinction is essential for understanding how gradual climate evolution interacts with shorter-timescale variability to produce hydroclimatic extremes. Although spatially extensive and methodologically diverse, proxy archives integrate multiple climatic signals and cannot, on their own, isolate the dynamical mechanisms responsible for observed hydroclimatic variability.

Climate model simulations offer a powerful yet underused tool to investigate long-term climate dynamics and their potential association with societal change. Long transient simulations capture both gradual climate variability, evolving over centuries to millennia, and abrupt climatic events, providing a coherent picture of teleconnections affecting local climates from afar. Here, we provide a process-based demonstration of the evolving hydroclimate of the Eastern Mediterranean during the mid-Holocene, using the EC-Earth3 Transient 8K simulation (hereby after Tr8K). We assess whether the progressive mid-to-late Holocene drying trend documented by proxies, including an extreme drought interval comparable to that associated with the LBA, emerges in a fully transient simulation forced only by known external drivers. Specifically, we (i) evaluate the long-term evolution of regional hydroclimate, (ii) test whether LBA-scale drought conditions arise in the model, and (iii) diagnose the drivers behind both gradual change and that capable of producing extreme hydroclimatic events.

HOLOCENE TRANSIENT CLIMATE MODEL SIMULATIONS

Holocene transient simulations track the gradual evolution of climate in response to changes in external forcings including orbital variations, greenhouse gas concentrations, volcanic activity, and solar radiation (37), which are based on historical data or reconstructions. They yield a more realistic representation, compared to equilibrium climate model experiments, of climate variability over millennia, capturing both long-term trends and abrupt climate changes (38, 39). Here, we investigate mid-Holocene long-term Eastern Mediterranean hydroclimate variability by using the EC-Earth3-Tr8K simulation, a Holocene transient experiment covering the period 8 thousand years (ka) before the present (B.P.) to the present (1950 CE), from the EC-Earth3 Earth System Model developed by the European consortium (40).

Model configuration and experimental design

The EC-Earth3-Tr8K simulation is run using the EC-Earth 3.3.1 model (40), with the following configuration: the atmospheric module IFS 36r4 (1.125° horizontal resolution), Ocean module Nemo3.6 (1° resolution) (41), the Sea Ice module Lim3 (42), Vegetation model LPJ-GUESS (43–46), and Land-surface module HTESSEL (47). These are all coupled via the OASIS3-MCT coupler (40). The forcings are time-varying orbital parameters, calculated internally in IFS following the framework by Berger (48), and annually varying greenhouse gas concentrations (CO2, CH4, and N2O) from ice-core reconstructions (49), with the solar constant fixed at 1360.747 W m−2. Aerosol concentrations, dust and volcanic particles, are prescribed at preindustrial (PI) levels, following the PMIP4 protocols (50) and ice sheets are prescribed with PI state. As the simulation does not include time-varying volcanic forcing, externally forced multidecadal variability associated with volcanic eruptions is not explicitly represented. The experiment is therefore not intended to reproduce the exact phasing of individual hydroclimatic events. Instead, it is designed to investigate the dynamical and thermodynamic mechanisms that generate variability under orbital and greenhouse gas forcing. Internal variability arises freely within the coupled system, and its temporal phasing should not be interpreted as a deterministic reconstruction of specific historical drought years.

The initial setup involves an equilibrium of a PI simulation with orbital parameters and greenhouse gas concentrations set to 8 ka B.P. values, which is integrated for ∼300 years to reach equilibrium, followed by an additional 700 years with constant forcings to produce a stable 8 ka B.P. equilibrium climate state. Tr8K is then initiated from this state and integrated forward for the subsequent 8000 years, with both orbital parameters and greenhouse gas concentrations varying through time.

Motivation for using EC-Earth3-Tr8K

The primary rationale for selecting EC-Earth3-Tr8K lies not only in the demonstrated ability of EC-Earth3 to realistically simulate climate responses during past warm periods, including the mid-Holocene, the Last Interglacial, and the mid-Pliocene (51–53), but also in the critical role its configuration and parameterizations play in studies focusing on the Eastern Mediterranean region. A key strength lies in its relatively high atmospheric resolution (125 km). This enables finer representation of topographic features, land sea contrasts, and regional-scale atmospheric dynamics. Finer spatial resolution is particularly valuable in the Eastern Mediterranean, where precipitation is strongly modulated by complex orography, coastal circulation patterns, and localized convective systems (54, 55). Higher resolution also enhances the simulation of synoptic systems such as Mediterranean cyclones, primary drivers of winter rainfall in the region (56, 57). Resolution limitations have been noted in previous studies attempting to reconstruct Holocene hydroclimate in the Mediterranean (58, 59), reinforcing the importance of selecting appropriately configured models when investigating regional-scale interactions. Coarser resolution likely contributes to misrepresentation or smoothing of critical spatial gradients, limiting its capacity to capture localized droughts and precipitation variability, especially in heterogeneous regions like the Peloponnese (60), contributing to biases in seasonal and interannual rainfall patterns (61). Although the atmospheric component benefits from relatively high spatial resolution, the ocean component uses the ORCA1 configuration (∼1° horizontal resolution), which does not fully resolve Mediterranean mesoscale dynamics, narrow boundary currents, or detailed exchange processes at the Strait of Gibraltar. This limitation may smooth fine-scale density gradients and underrepresent small-scale circulation variability within the basin. However, fully coupled transient Holocene simulations at higher ocean resolution remain computationally prohibitive, and ORCA1 currently represents one of the highest-resolution configurations available for multimillennial experiments. The mechanisms identified here operate primarily at basin to hemispheric scale monsoon shifts, Atlantic overturning variability, density-driven buoyancy adjustments, and large-scale hydroclimate balance, which are robustly represented at this resolution. Therefore, although localized circulation features may be underresolved, the large-scale dynamical and thermodynamic relationships discussed in this study remain physically consistent and appropriate for the spatial scale of the analysis.

When assessing drought conditions in climate models, the critical variable is not merely precipitation or storm track deflection, but the integrated response of the land surface, particularly changes in soil moisture, which directly affects vegetation stress and water availability (62). Accurately capturing drought therefore depends not only on atmospheric dynamics but also on how the model represents land atmosphere feedbacks. Therefore, a model with a more sophisticated land surface scheme and dynamic vegetation coupling will perform better in simulating the onset, severity, and persistence of droughts. EC-Earth3-Tr8K includes a dynamic vegetation module (LPJ-GUESS) and thus simulates two-way interactions between vegetation, soil moisture, and atmospheric processes. This facilitates realistic feedbacks between evapotranspiration, land surface albedo, and water stress that modulate both short-term anomalies and long-term drying trends (63).

EC-Earth3-Tr8K also uses modern convection schemes, including mass-flux parameterizations that better represent vertical moisture transport and convective rainfall dynamics (64). This allows for simulation of a broader range of precipitation extremes and intraseasonal variability, essential for accurately capturing hydroclimatic fluctuations in the Eastern Mediterranean. Furthermore, it is strongly sensitive to orbital forcing and therefore captures well the long-term drying trend of the late Holocene—a response to declining summer insolation due to orbital precession (48).

We first validate the use of EC-Earth3-Tr8K by comparing its precipitation trends with those simulated by MPI-ESM1.2-Tr8K, a parallel mid-Holocene experiment based on the MPI-ESM1.2 model. The strong agreement between the two simulations confirms that the climate patterns identified are robust and not artifacts specific to the EC-Earth model (fig. S1 and table S1). We use the MPI model for validation as it has been extensively evaluated against a wide range of paleoclimate proxies and shown to accurately reproduce key Holocene climate trends (65). Moreover, MPI-ESM1.2-Tr8K shares several key features with EC-Earth3, including the use of a dynamic vegetation model (JSBACH), multilayer soil hydrology, and modern convection schemes. Both simulations are forced with identical orbital parameters (48) and greenhouse gas concentrations and operate at comparable spatial resolutions (200 km). We further validate EC-Earth3-Tr8K with a thorough proxy comparison.

SIMULATED MID-HOLOCENE EASTERN MEDITERRANEAN CLIMATE AND VEGETATION CHANGES

Evolving hydroclimate

Tr8K captures a small but statistically significant long-term drying over the Eastern Mediterranean during the mid-Holocene (Fig. 1A), indicating a persistent, although weak, long-term aridification tendency. The strongest drying trend occurs in the Balkans, followed by the Levant, whereas Turkey shows no significant trend. A concurrent, modest Eastern Mediterranean cooling trend (Fig. 1B) is most pronounced in the Balkans and weaker elsewhere.

These coupled trends in drying and cooling produce a spatially heterogeneous but hydrologically coherent response, where lower temperatures moderate the effects of declining rainfall. In the Balkans, declining precipitation combined with a pronounced decrease in potential evapotranspiration (PET) (Fig. 1C) reduces atmospheric water demand and enhances effective moisture (P-PET; Fig. 1D). Turkey exhibits a similar increase in moisture availability as stable rainfall coincides with cooling and a marked drop in evaporative potential. The aridity index (AI; ratio between the average annual precipitation and the PET) (66) (Fig. 1E) rises across both regions, indicating a transition toward more humid conditions driven by reduced evaporative stress. In contrast, the Levant shows a weak but significant decline in precipitation and negligible changes in temperature or PET, maintaining persistently low effective moisture (AI ∼ 0.36). This highlights a persistent north-south hydroclimatic gradient: cooling-induced moisture gains in the north versus enduring aridity in the southern sector.

Superimposed on long-term hydroclimatic tendencies, centennial-scale fluctuations are punctuated by multicentury drought episodes, identified through a sliding 600-year window analysis of the 5-year standardized precipitation-evapotranspiration index (SPEI) (Fig. 2, A to C). Three major intervals of enhanced drought frequency and severity are evident across the Eastern Mediterranean: ∼4000 to 3500 BCE, ∼2600 to 2100 BCE, and ∼1400 to 900 BCE. The earliest interval coincides with a sustained warm phase in the Balkans and Turkey, peaking near 3600 BCE, and is associated with marked reductions in effective moisture (P-PET) and AI, particularly in the northern regions. The second interval represents a region-wide desiccation pulse. All three subregions experience abrupt hydroclimatic shifts, with Turkey showing the sharpest drop in rainfall and temperature, and the Balkans registering the strongest hydrological stress due to a spike in PET—the largest positive anomaly of the mid-Holocene. Across these northern regions, effective moisture (P-PET) declines by up to 7.5 mm month−1 relative to the mean, and the AI drops sharply, marking one of the most arid intervals in the northern sector. The Levant exhibits comparable declines in effective moisture, although the overall aridity is muted relative to the northern regions due to its already dry baseline.

Following this episode, the declining precipitation and temperature trend continues, with an accelerated cooling across all regions ∼2000 to 1800 BCE. Minor changes in the slope of temperature trends, identified through statistically significant breakpoints around 1788 and 1781 BCE for the Balkans and Turkey, indicate subtle variations in the rate of cooling rather than abrupt events. The final drought interval, ∼1400 to 800 BCE, begins with declining precipitation in the Balkans ∼1400 BCE. A statistically significant breakpoint around 1445 BCE indicates a modest change in the rate of decline, reflecting a subtle variation in trend rather than an abrupt hydroclimatic shift. This is followed by widespread decreases in rainfall (∼1100 BCE) and temperature (∼1050 BCE) across all regions (Fig. 1, A and B). During this episode, anomalies in precipitation and temperature exceed those of the previous drought event, signifying that this hydroclimatic disruption occurs over a more deviated shift in background state. PET surges to its highest values of the preceding millennium, whereas P-PET and AI record their steepest declines. Effective moisture drops by 10 to 12 mm month−1 and AI decreases by ∼0.15 in the Balkans and Turkey. By ∼800 BCE, hydroclimatic indicators recover, resuming the background trend of declining PET and rising P-PET and AI. Precipitation remains subdued while temperatures continue a gradual descent until 0 CE (Fig. 1, A and B). This stabilization marks the establishment of a new, cooler, and more hydrologically efficient state following transient but severe drought disruptions. Analysis of SPEI-based drought frequency across multiple thresholds (−1, −1.5, and −2) confirm that, although multicentury droughts punctuate the record, the overall long-term tendency is a gradual decline in drought frequency with significant long-term decreasing trends in all three regions.

Overall, Tr8K reproduces the broad mid-Holocene Eastern Mediterranean drying seen in proxy reconstructions. The model provides a more mechanistic view of its evolution; rather than a uniform aridification, there was a nuanced interplay between declining rainfall and cooling-induced reductions in PET, demonstrating the model’s ability to delve deeper than the fragmentary proxy record. In the northern and central regions (Balkans and Turkey), reduced energy availability led to improved effective moisture and less arid conditions, whereas in the southern Levant, limited cooling and persistent rainfall decline sustained drier hydroclimatic conditions. Notably, the model captures major drought events, which punctuated the long-term trends, with remarkable fidelity. The event 2600 to 2100 BCE corresponds broadly to the 4.2-ka event, an abrupt-change Holocene event (67), characterized by dry climatic conditions in many parts of the world, including the Mediterranean region and the Levant 185 (68). The major drought event ∼1400 to 900 BCE, aligns precisely with the 3.2-ka event and the timing of the LBA collapse, occurring following a progressive aridification culminating in a transition to a drier and cooler state. These findings establish that the overall pattern of eastern Mediterranean aridification reflected the interplay of centennial-scale drought episodes superimposed on a gradual cooling-driven reorganization of the regional hydroclimate.

Proxy validation of Tr8K hydroclimate evolution

To evaluate whether the multicentennial drought variability identified in the SPEI analysis (Fig. 2) is reflected in independent paleoclimate evidence, the simulated hydroclimatic evolution is compared with a suite of proxy records from across the Eastern Mediterranean (Fig. 3). Considering the chronological uncertainty, differing temporal resolution, and variable sensitivity to moisture availability associated with individual proxies, the comparison is intended to assess broad regional and multicentennial patterns of variability rather than the exact event-scale synchrony. Across the Balkans, Anatolia, and the Levant, intervals of reduced simulated P-E (precipitation minus evaporation) and SPEI minima generally correspond with positive excursions in speleothem and lake δ18O, reduced marine productivity, and declines in pollen-based precipitation reconstructions (Fig. 3, A to C). The LBA (1400 to 900 BCE) drought interval identified in Fig. 2 exhibits the clearest qualitative correspondence between the model and proxy synthesis, with numerous records indicating sustained aridity and reduced moisture availability during the interval of simulated drying. In contrast, the expression of the 4.2-ka event is more spatially and temporally heterogeneous across the proxy network. This difference may reflect the development of the LBA drought upon an already arid mid-to-late Holocene background state, amplifying terrestrial and marine proxy responses and producing more persistent signals.

Spatial comparisons between the proxy synthesis and Tr8K indicate regionally distinct expressions of mid-to-late Holocene hydroclimatic evolution. In the Balkans and Anatolia, several proxy records show a gradual long-term shift toward drier conditions, superimposed by more pronounced arid excursions during major drought intervals such as the 4.2-ka event and the LBA. This behavior is particularly evident in speleothem δ18O records from Mavri Trypa and Marion Caves in the Balkans (25, 69), and Kocain and Sofular Caves in Turkey (18, 21), where sustained increases in δ18O through the late Holocene are punctuated by distinct positive excursions during intervals of simulated drying. Lake sediment records from Lakes Shkodra, Nar, and Gölhisar (3, 13, 16) similarly exhibit multicentennial variability consistent with episodic hydroclimatic deterioration superimposed on a progressively more arid background state. In contrast, Levantine proxy records primarily indicate recurring drought episodes rather than a persistent long-term trend toward increasing aridity. Pollen-based precipitation reconstructions from Ein Gedi and Tell Akko (70), together with Eastern Mediterranean marine records such as GeoB7702-3 (12), show clear dry excursions associated with the principal drought intervals identified in Tr8K, particularly during the LBA. However, unlike many Balkan and Anatolian records, these proxies do not exhibit a sustained long-term progression toward increasingly dry conditions following these events. This regional contrast broadly parallels the simulated evolution described in Fig. 2. In Tr8K, long-term drying tendencies are most evident in the Balkans, whereas the Levant remains persistently arid throughout the simulation with comparatively limited long-term change in effective moisture. Anatolia occupies an intermediate position, characterized by substantial multicentennial variability and pronounced drought episodes superimposed upon weaker long-term trends.

Although the simulated long-term drying trend (0.3 to 0.5 mm century−1) is modest in absolute magnitude, it is consistent with a gradual mean-state hydroclimatic adjustment through the mid-to-late Holocene. In contrast, substantially larger anomalies in effective moisture and precipitation occur during the multicentennial drought intervals, indicating that relatively weak long-term background trends were periodically amplified by persistent drought episodes. The magnitude of these simulated drought anomalies (Fig. 2) is broadly comparable to proxy-inferred estimates and remains physically plausible within the context of both Holocene and modern hydroclimatic variability. During the 4.2-ka event, proxy evidence from Hermes Cave δ18O (71) suggests rainfall reductions of ∼200 to 300 mm year−1, equivalent to an estimated 15 to 30% decrease in precipitation. Over the same interval, Tr8K simulates effective moisture (P-PET) reductions of ∼7.5 mm month−1 (90 mm year−1) across the Balkans and Anatolia. Although these quantities are not directly equivalent, the simulated anomalies are of a similar order of magnitude when accounting for the inclusion of evaporative demand in P-PET and the persistence of the simulated deficit over multidecadal to centennial timescales. During the LBA drought interval, Tr8K produces some of the strongest negative effective moisture anomalies of the mid-Holocene (120 to 144 mm year−1) together with pronounced declines in AI, whereas proxy reconstructions such as Soreq Cave (72) indicate rainfall reductions approaching 100 mm during 1050 to 650 BCE. Comparison with modern hydroclimatic variability further supports the physical realism of the simulated anomalies. Severe twentieth-century Mediterranean droughts are associated with annual precipitation deficits of ∼60 to 100 mm, whereas long-term observed hydroclimatic trends remain comparatively modest over the instrumental period (73). The simulated paleodrought anomalies in Tr8K therefore fall within, or slightly exceed, the range of the most severe modern drought conditions, whereas the underlying millennial-scale drying trend remains substantially weaker. Together, these comparisons suggest that Tr8K produces hydroclimatic variability and drought magnitudes that are broadly consistent with proxy reconstructions and physically plausible relative to observed Mediterranean hydroclimatic variability.

Transitioning vegetation

Tr8K captures a broad transition from open, grass-dominated landscapes to denser, tree-dominated vegetation states with greater canopy cover and biomass across the Eastern Mediterranean. This excludes the Levant, which shows negligible or nonsignificant vegetation trends (Fig. 4). Tree cover demonstrates a steady, monotonic expansion in the Balkans and a more dynamic rise in Turkey (Fig. 4 and table S4), mirrored by positive trends in total vegetation fraction and leaf area index (LAI), indicating expanding canopy biomass. In contrast, grass cover declines region-wide, most sharply in Turkey, reflecting the progressive replacement of herbaceous vegetation by woody forms.

This vegetation expansion is driven by cooling enhanced hydrological efficiency. Rising effective moisture (P-PET) and AI values (Fig. 1, D and E) enhance water availability even as absolute rainfall declines. Statistical analyses confirm temperature as the dominant control: Vegetation-temperature relationships (R2 = 0.38 to 0.47 in the Balkans; 0.20 to 0.23 in Turkey) exceed those with precipitation (R2 < 0.38 and R2 < 0.02, respectively). Negative coefficients for tree fraction (−5.3 Balkan, −6.684 Turkey) and positive ones for grass fraction (2.3 Balkan, 3.426 Turkey) highlight this cooling-driven woody vegetation expansion, and an overall nonexistent, to weak, negative correlation between precipitation and woody vegetation variables (tree fraction, total vegetation, and LAI) (r = −0.16 to −0.33, P < 0.001 Eastern Mediterranean and Balkans, r = ±0.1 Turkey) further underscores that vegetation greening was not rainfall driven but energy limited.

The strongest vegetation increase occurs in Turkey (Fig. 4), a transitional zone near the climatic threshold between dry steppe and subhumid woodland. Here, small improvements in effective moisture (increasing P-PET) are amplified into large ecological shifts, pushing the system across the threshold for tree establishment (Fig. 4J). Once above this threshold, forest expansion slows toward a saturation point. The Balkans, already forested under modest moisture deficits, displays greater stability. Vegetation is largely buffered, and thus forest expansion is gradual. Tree cover remains resilient and responds more gradually to additional moisture (Fig. 4, I and L). This contrast illustrates differing hydrological sensitivities—Turkey as a threshold-limited system and the Balkans as a buffered one.

In the Levant, vegetation is tightly coupled to precipitation anomalies (r = 0.4 to 0.73, R2 = 0.61 to 0.71), yet no significant long-term change occurs. The AI (0.36) lies well below the 0.65 “dryland” threshold [United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) classification] and dictates a semiarid climate. The Levant MAP (352 mm year−1) sits between the ecological transition between shrub-steppe (∼250 to 350 mm year−1) and open woodland (∼400 to 500 mm year−1) (74, 75). Even with minor improvements in effective moisture (Fig. 1A), persistent water deficits keep the system below the woody establishment threshold (Fig. 4, K and N). Thus, vegetation remains grass dominated but does not collapse, reflecting stable adaptation to chronic aridity. This emerging gradient—temperature-limited systems in the north/west and precipitation-limited systems in the south/east—mirrors modern Eastern Mediterranean climate controls (76, 77).

The major hydroclimatic disruptions (∼ 2600 to 2200 BCE and ∼1300 to 900 BCE), which are prominent in temperature, precipitation, and SPEI records (Figs. 1 and 2), leave only subtle imprints on vegetation. Minor dips in tree cover and brief grass recoveries ∼2600 and 2300 BCE suggest transient responses to the 4.2-ka event, but vegetation rapidly returns to its long-term trajectory, underscoring its resilience to short-lived aridity. The most pronounced ecological change occurs between 2000 and 1500 BCE, concurrent with an abrupt cooling ∼2000 to 1800 BCE (Fig. 1, B and D). During this interval, tree cover and LAI increase sharply while grass cover collapses, marking a tipping point in vegetation structure.

This episode represents a biophysical crossing, and the onset of a vegetation transition to a new regime (evident from the Hovmöller plots; Fig. 4, E to H). Rapid atmospheric cooling triggers a step-change in moisture balance and ecosystem state. PET drops abruptly by 3.5 mm month−1 below the mean, driving a sustained rise in P-PET of −8 to −10 mm month−1 (Turkey and Balkans, respectively), which persists for nearly two centuries. This prolonged increase in effective moisture allows woody vegetation to cross its ecological threshold, initiating rapid forest expansion (Fig. 4, E to H). Woody vegetation expansion then stabilizes at a new state.

THE ROLE OF OCEAN ATMOSPHERE DYNAMICS IN EVOLVING EASTERN MEDITERRANEAN HYDROCLIMATE

Holocene centennial-scale hydroclimate evolution

The steady mid-to-late Holocene drying of the Eastern Mediterranean, as captured by both the proxy record and Tr8K, is most consistent with the orbitally driven reorganization of Northern Hemisphere atmospheric circulation (Fig. 5). Progressive decline in boreal summer insolation reduced land-sea thermal contrast, leading to a systematic weakening of the tropical monsoon systems [West African Monsoon (WAM)], and a southward displacement of the intertropical convergence zone (ITCZ). This large-scale, hemispherically organized monsoon response to orbital forcing is reflected clearly in the Tr8K simulation; the three indices display strong mutual coherence over the Holocene (Fig. 6A), supported by robust statistical relationships (table S5).

Schematic illustrating how the long-term drying of the Eastern Mediterranean was part of a hemispheric-scale hydroclimate adjustment to declining Holocene summer insolation.

Progressive weakening of major Northern Hemisphere monsoon systems, including the WAM and a southward contraction of the ITCZ, led to a diminished northward moisture transport from tropical Africa and a decline in continental runoff, reducing freshwater delivery to the Mediterranean. Drivers of long and short-term hydroclimate variability.

The weakened monsoon/ITCZ systems thereby reduces the Eastern Mediterranean freshwater input. Precipitable water (PRW) closely tracks the WAM index, resulting in a progressive reduction in column integrated atmospheric moisture availability, whereas reduced Northern African river discharge leads to a decline in Mediterranean runoff. Ultimately, P-E drops over the region (Fig. 6B), all indices strongly correlated to WAM strength and ITCZ position (table S5). The winter jet stream shifts northward as monsoon intensity declines, enhancing subtropical subsidence and reducing the mid-latitude storm track penetration into the Mediterranean, further suppressing precipitation via reduced cyclone frequency and moisture advection. The strong coupling between hydroclimate variables demonstrates that the long-term Mediterranean drying is not an isolated signal but rather the mid-latitude expression of a broader tropical-subtropical circulation adjustment forced by declining insolation. As freshwater input progressively declines, Mediterranean salinity systematically increases (Fig. 6C), emerging as a hydrographic consequence of sustained atmospheric drying. The strong negative correlations between monsoon strength and hydroclimate indicate that salinity primarily reflects the integrated freshwater deficit of the basin.

Atlantic meridional overturning circulation (AMOC) weakening is also part of the broader, orbitally forced reorganization of Northern Hemisphere climate, exhibiting strong covariance with nearly all major Holocene trends (table S6). Declining AMOC strength likely reflects the same large-scale insolation-driven shifts in atmospheric circulation that weaken monsoons, shift the ITCZ southward, and promote Mediterranean drying. Its evolution therefore appears to represent a secondary oceanic adjustment embedded within the shared background Holocene trend.

The tight coupling evident here arises from mean-state adjustment to orbital forcing. Although tropical-subtropical coupling persists to a degree without the orbital trend (table S5 shows that the monsoon intensity and ITCZ variability have a moderate influence on long-term Mediterranean moisture balance and jet position), after removing the long-term trend, Mediterranean hydroclimate is primarily dominated by internal variability driven by regional thermodynamic control. Without the slow insolation-driven reorganization of circulation, the Mediterranean hydroclimate signal simplifies markedly, dependent on internal variability. The only robust relationships to P-E and PRW are via upper-ocean potential temperature (TAO) or surface air temperature [mean annual temperature (MAT)] (fig. S2), representing that moisture fluctuations reflect temperature-dependent changes in atmospheric water-holding capacity, consistent with Clausius-Clapeyron scaling. Therefore, at the long-term scale, variability represents both internal thermally mediated fluctuations superimposed on the longer-term orbitally forced drying trend.

AMOC’s centennial-scale coupling to Mediterranean hydroclimate weakens substantially once the trend is removed, reinforcing the interpretation that coevolution of AMOC primarily reflects shared mean-state adjustment rather than continuous dynamical forcing. This is reiterated by the lack of dynamic centennial-scale relationship between AMOC and Gibraltar inflow, implying that the long-term parallel evolution of AMOC and Gibraltar exchange most likely reflects a common response to long-term climatic forcing rather than direct dynamical control.

Multidecadal hydroclimate fluctuations

At the multidecadal timescale, the system represents a classic Atlantic-Mediterranean thermodynamic coupling mode. Both AMOC and Atlantic multidecadal variability (AMV) are strongly coupled to Mediterranean upper-ocean temperature (TAO), and all three variables exhibit a strong relationship with Mediterranean PRW (Fig. 6, D and E). AMOC variability is tightly coupled to thermal (ρT) Mediterranean surface density anomalies, whereas the relationship with the salinity contribution (ρS) is weak and episodic and nonexistent with total surface density (Fig. 6F). This demonstrates that density-driven circulation adjustments linked to heating and cooling dominate fast Mediterranean AMOC coupling, whereas salinity plays a secondary role. Furthermore, freshwater-driven density variability does not regulate dynamical Gibraltar inflow. Instead, Gibraltar exchange (Fig. 6G) responds primarily to Mediterranean temperature–driven density variability. This means that Atlantic variability modifies the Mediterranean temperature and that Mediterranean density structure controls the Gibraltar exchange.

Therefore, at the multidecadal scale, Tr8K establishes a thermodynamically mediated coupling where AMOC variability modulates the Mediterranean temperature, temperature-driven buoyancy changes regulate the density structure and exchange dynamics, and regional hydrology responds to the enhanced atmospheric moisture capacity. Thus, fast Mediterranean-Atlantic thermodynamically mediated internal variability mode is distinct from the slower orbitally forced Holocene evolution.

Potential drivers of major drought events

Wavelet analysis on the Eastern Mediterranean SPEI is used to distinguish the drivers of the drought events occurring alongside the long-term Holocene drying trend. Eastern Mediterranean hydroclimate variability is hierarchically organized, with strong power associated with millennial (>1000 years) and centennial to multidecadal (110 to 200 years) modes (Fig. 7A). Decomposition of the Eastern Mediterranean SPEI into reconstructed period bands (according to the identified top periods) reveals that drought events occur due to negative phase alignment of multiple modes (Fig. 7B). This constructive interference amplifies the magnitude of the drought beyond what any single mode would produce. For the 2600 to 2100 BCE drought (corresponding to the 4.2-ka event), the extreme dry conditions arise from full-spectrum alignment across all scales: a dry phase in the millennial-scale background trend, negative dips in the 300- to 500-year envelope, and superimposed by stark centennial-scale amplitude growth. The 1400 to 900 BCE drought event (aligning with the 3.2-ka event/LBA collapse) follows a similar mechanistic pattern; a dry millennial baseline is augmented by negative phases of the multicentennial envelope, whereas centennial oscillations reach their most extreme negative amplitudes, intensifying hydroclimatic stress.

The >1000-year band aligns strongly with both oceanic and atmospheric long-term North Atlantic variability (Fig. 7C). Changes in sea surface temperature (SST) marginally precede those of the SPEI, whereas there is a near-zero phase difference with the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO) Index. This indicates that a sustained positive NAO phase, leading to a stronger subtropical high, northward displaced storm tracks and reduced Mediterranean precipitation, alongside millennial-scale positive North Atlantic SST anomalies, contribute to prolonged dry phases of the SPEI. Internal thermal anomalies in the Mediterranean basin are shown to be the primary driver of multicentennial-scale drought variability (Fig. 7D). A robust relationship between Mediterranean TAO and SPEI shows that multicentennial thermal anomalies lead hydroclimate changes by roughly one-sixth of a cycle. This behavior points to a self-sustained internal Mediterranean mode, in which upper-ocean temperature acts as a “memory” that modulates local evaporation, surface density, and moisture availability. The 110- to 200-year band corresponds closely with Atlantic Ocean–driven variability (Fig. 7E), illustrating a strong relationship with the AMOC and North Atlantic SST at the centennial scale, whereby ocean variables lead the SPEI by 300 to 350 years. This phase relationship indicates that temporary reductions in AMOC strength and/or regional North Atlantic warming precede and contribute to centennial-scale negative SPEI anomalies, with ocean temperature changes modulating Mediterranean moisture through thermodynamically driven buoyancy effects.

Our result—Eastern Mediterranean drought events emerged from the confluence of Atlantic internal variability across multiple timescales—agrees strongly with other studies discussing these events. Although no single universal driver has been agreed on for the 4.2-ka event, many studies invoke a “proximity principle,” whereby regional anomalies primarily reflect variability in the nearest ocean basin, with additional modulation via interbasin teleconnections (78). Considering this, North Atlantic processes would therefore take responsibility for the Mediterranean expression of the 4.2-ka event, and a similar pattern of coupled Atlantic forcing likely also characterized the 3.2-ka event. Hypothesis for both events discuss a positive NAO phase, which likely shifted the westerly jet and the North Atlantic storm track northward (68, 79, 80) and may have combined with North Atlantic cooling driven by a temporary AMOC slowdown (81), collectively reducing moisture flux into the Mediterranean. This aligns exceptionally well with our findings—a positive NAO-driven background dry state, superimposed by AMOC fluctuations, and further supports that it was constructive interference across differing scales that produced the observed extreme drops in SPEI.

Intrinsic vulnerability of the Eastern Mediterranean hydroclimate system

Using the EC-Earth3-Tr8K transient Holocene simulation, we demonstrate that the mid-to-late Holocene hydroclimate evolution of the Eastern Mediterranean reflects a hemispheric-scale adjustment to declining Northern Hemisphere summer insolation. The regional mean state evolved in close coupling with orbital forcing, producing a gradual but persistent shift toward greater aridity. This long-term trend represents a background boundary condition, a slow drying trajectory upon which thermodynamically mediated internal variability is superimposed. The major drought intervals around 4.2 and 3.2 ka illustrate how hydroclimatic extremes emerge from cross-scale alignment of multiple modes of variability, rather than from any single forcing mechanism.

These findings underscore the intrinsic vulnerability of the Eastern Mediterranean hydroclimate system. The region responds nonlinearly to relatively modest shifts in Atlantic circulation through thermodynamic amplification: Atlantic variability modulates the Mediterranean temperature and density structure, altering regional moisture availability and precipitation efficiency. When millennial, multicentennial, and centennial modes synchronize in their dry phases, extremes arise from internal variability alone. With prescribed volcanic forcing, externally forced multidecadal perturbations are not resolved in this simulation. Volcanic eruptions would likely modulate the amplitude and phasing of centennial-scale variability, potentially altering the precise timing of drought maxima. However, they would not fundamentally change the large-scale dynamical structure identified here, namely, Atlantic variability, thermodynamically mediated Mediterranean feedbacks, and cross-scale mode interaction. The conclusions are therefore process-based rather than event-date dependent. Although a reinitialized simulation would produce different realizations of internal variability, the capacity of the system to generate droughts of comparable magnitude through constructive interference of multiple modes remains robust.

Within this framework, we suggest that the LBA collapse represents the societal expression of internally generated Atlantic variability acting upon a progressively arid orbital background state. The convergence of these processes likely pushed the Eastern Mediterranean across a hydroclimatic threshold, constraining water availability and amplifying food insecurity within an already stressed sociopolitical system.

Threat of future drought under rapid anthropogenic warming

These results carry important implications for the future. The modern Mediterranean basin is experiencing rapid anthropogenic warming, increasing salinity contrasts, and projected shifts in storm tracks, whereas model projections indicate potential weakening of the Atlantic overturning circulation. The mechanisms identified here, cross-scale amplification and thermodynamic sensitivity to Atlantic variability, suggest that future drought risk may depend not only on mean-state drying but also on changes in the amplitude and phase structure of internal Atlantic variability. Future work needs to explore the extent to which present day and future hydroclimatic risks mirror the dynamics reconstructed here, especially under projected AMOC weakening scenarios. EC-Earth3-Tr8K’s ability to resolve these interconnected mechanisms supports its use for further investigation into climatic drivers of socioenvironmental change in both paleo and future contexts.

More broadly, these findings highlight the value of integrating high-resolution paleoclimate simulations with proxy reconstructions to understand how societies respond to environmental stress. Such coupled paleo-future frameworks, which unite physical climate models, robust proxy data, and socioenvironmental feedbacks, offer powerful means to assess not only how past civilizations were tested by climatic extremes but also how modern societies may confront similar challenges under emerging climate regimes. Collectively, these findings provide a physically consistent model-based mechanism linking the evolving amplitude and phase structure of Atlantic internal variability to the hydroclimatic shifts that reshaped the mid-Holocene Eastern Mediterranean. The emerging picture is one of a region whose climate sensitivity to remote oceanic and atmospheric variability profoundly influenced the environmental backdrop against which ancient societies evolved, flourished, and ultimately declined.
