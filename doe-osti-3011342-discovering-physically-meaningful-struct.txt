1. Executive Summary
This final technical report summarizes outcomes associated with the DOE Award
DE-SC0022323 at Columbia University during the period September 1, 2021 through
August 31, 2024. The project, as articulated in the proposal, aimed to develop a
probabilistic and physically informed machine-learning framework—centered on deep
latent variable models—to discover “hidden structures” in high-dimensional,
spatiotemporal climate extremes data; to enforce physical constraints such as symmetry
and conservation in learned representations; and to quantify uncertainty in ways
compatible with rare, long-tailed events and partial observability.

Within the Columbia scope, scientific activities emphasized (i) learning physically
meaningful structure from complex geophysical systems and related multiscale
processes, (ii) connecting representation learning to uncertainty-aware inference and
model evaluation, and (iii) translating these advances into published outputs relevant to
extreme-event organization, parameterization, and robust emulation/forecasting. Across
the award period, the work contributed to peer-reviewed publications and preprints
spanning convective organization and precipitation stochasticity, differentiable
memory-based parameterization, and broader efforts in uncertainty characterization and
data-driven emulation. Several outputs explicitly acknowledge DOE support associated
with DE-SC0022323 (see publication list). Additional related outputs acknowledge DOE
support in more general terms, and a small subset of adjacent publications did not
consistently carry the award number in the acknowledgments despite scientific overlap
with the project’s themes; these are described as “related products”.

Finally, the proposal anticipated contributions in causal transportability and related
causal-inference directions under Aim 1; however no peer-reviewed publication
attributable to this DOE award emerged from the causal-inference component during
the period of performance by co-PI Bareinboim.

2. Background and Project Objectives

The proposal framed climate extremes as simultaneously (i) rare and thus statistically
long-tailed, (ii) highly multivariate and spatiotemporal, and (iii) increasingly
nonstationary under climate change, motivating a probabilistic scientific-ML framework
capable of learning structure from both simulation and sparse observations. The central
objective was to develop deep latent variable model (LVM) methods that discover
low-dimensional structure while remaining physically meaningful and uncertainty aware.

The project objectives were organized into three intertwined aims:
Aim 1 (Discover hidden structures): Design unsupervised/self-supervised
methods—e.g., sequential VAEs and tractable/tensor LVMs—to discover
low-dimensional latent structure in abundant simulation output and sparse observations;
incorporate ideas of causal transportability to support interpretation and transfer from
models to observations.
Aim 2 (Inject physical principles): Encode physical constraints (symmetry,
conservation, disentanglement, weak supervision priors, equivariant architectures)
directly into the latent representations to improve interpretability and generalization.
Aim 3 (Quantify uncertainty): Develop scalable Bayesian inference approaches
(variational inference and MCMC families, including preconditioning and hybridization)
suited to long-tailed extremes and high-dimensional latent spaces, with the objective of
calibrated uncertainty estimates in scientific prediction tasks.

From the Columbia perspective, these aims were implemented as a tightly coupled
scientific program: climate-physics questions (e.g., convective organization and
stochastic precipitation;  microphysical process structure; uncertainty  in
parameterizations and emulators) were used to stress-test representation learning and
inference ideas in regimes characteristic of extreme events and multiscale dynamics.

3. Work Performed and Technical Progress
3.1. Implicitly learned convective organization as a low-dimensional control on
precipitation variability and extremes

A central scientific result aligned with the project’s “hidden structure” hypothesis is the
demonstration that subgrid convective organization can be represented by a learned,
low-dimensional latent space that dramatically improves coarse-grained precipitation
prediction. Using global storm-resolving simulations at 2.5km resolution and machine
learning, we showed that a neural network driven only by large-scale predictors can
reproduce mean precipitation behavior but substantially underpredicts variability and
extremes. By contrast, informing the model with an organization metric that is implicitly
learned from high-resolution precipitable-water structure yields a strong increase in skill
and enables realistic extremes and spatial variability at climate-model-relevant scales.
Importantly, the learned organization metric was shown to exhibit hysteresis and
temporal persistence, supporting the interpretation that convective organization induces
a form of memory in the atmospheric column, and that this memory can be forecasted
as a relatively simple (linear) dynamical process using past values of the metric itself.

From a project-level perspective, this outcome provided a concrete demonstration of
Aim 1’s discovery objective—identifying a compact representation of a multiscale

extremes-relevant process directly from data—while simultaneously reinforcing Aim 2’s
emphasis on physically meaningful representations, since the latent organization metric
maps onto an interpretable physical concept (organization and persistence of
moisture/convective structure) rather than an arbitrary embedding. The PNAS study
explicitly acknowledges DOE Grant DE-SC0022323 support in the funding statement.

2.2. Memory-based parameterization using differentiable solvers for stable
coarse-grained dynamics

A complementary methodological contribution addressed a known failure of purely
instantaneous, (Markovian) closures for chaotic systems: even when short-term
tendencies are well fit, long integrations often drift, destabilize, or lose physically
meaningful regime statistics. The arXiv preprint “Memory-based parameterization with
differentiable solver” develops a framework in which unresolved-scale effects are
represented with an explicit memory dependence, and the parameterization is trained
end-to-end through a differentiable time-integration procedure. In this construction, the
learned closure was optimized not only for one-step accuracy but for multi-step
trajectory fidelity. As such the model directly targeted stability and long-term horizon
behavior rather than treating them as emergent properties of a Markovian rollout. DOE
Office of Science support under Award No. DE-SC0022323 was acknowledged in the
manuscript

2.3. Reduced-order latent coordinates for cloud microphysics process rates and
intrinsic dimensionality

Another outcome consistent with the project’s aim of discovering compact, physically
meaningful coordinates is the reduced-order modeling analysis of warm-rain
microphysical process rates, framed around the droplet size distribution (DSD) as a
high-dimensional state. Lamb et al. 2024 describes an approach that learns the
predictors of microphysical rates in an unsupervised manner by simultaneously learning
low-dimensional representations of the DSD and predicting microphysical evolution,
rather than assuming that a small set of prognostic moments is sufficient. A key finding
is that collision–coalescence exhibits an intrinsic dimension of approximately three,
which can be interpreted as a lower bound on the degrees of freedom required for
faithful representation; the learned latent space retains more information than standard
moment-based bases and enable a “global linearization” of process rates in the intrinsic
coordinate system. This offers promises for new types of parameterizations.

This result is scientifically relevant to extremes because warm-rain microphysics
governs precipitation intensity distributions and regime transitions in convective
systems, and it is methodologically relevant because it explicitly links representation
learning to an interpretable structural property (intrinsic dimension) and to a concrete
modeling parameterization objective (predicting process rates). This work
acknowledges DOE funding.

2.4. Uncertainty quantification and experimental design logic via
perturbed-parameter ensembles

The Egusphere preprint by Carslaw et al. provides a synthesis view focused on
uncertainty quantification and sensitivity analysis in climate modeling via
perturbed-parameter ensembles (PPEs). The work argues that PPEs are an essential
bridge between model structural/parametric uncertainty and useful UQ, and it
emphasizes modern opportunities, especially data-driven and surrogate approaches, to
make PPE exploration more computationally efficient and diagnostically meaningful.

This aligns with Aim 3 of the proposal, which foregrounded the need for scalable
inference and robust calibration in long-tailed regimes. While the preprint is a
perspective/analysis contribution rather than an algorithmic deliverable, its value in a
final report is that it codifies an “end-to-end” UQ approach for Earth system modeling.
Indeed, it defines: (i) how to perturb the parameter space to reflect correct process
uncertainty, (ii) then how to optimally run or emulate PPEs, and (iii) finally how to
connect ensemble structure back to uncertainty in predictions and extreme diagnostics.
The manuscript acknowledges the DOE award context.

2.5. Meta-learning for sparse-observation upscaling and a global carbon flux
dataset

The MetaFlux Scientific Data paper (Nathaniel and Gentine 2023) developed a new
high-resolution (0.25°), long-term (2001–2021) global dataset of gross primary
production and ecosystem respiration generated via a meta-learning paradigm designed
to be robust in data-sparse regimes. The paper demonstrated that meta-learning trained
models reduced the validation error by approximately 5–7% relative to non-meta-trained
counterparts and yielded improved robustness to extreme events, with error reductions
in the range of 4–24% in those regimes. In particular, compared to standard alternative
machine-learning carbon products (e.g., FLUXCOM from Max Planck), MetaFlux
indicated especially large gains in the tropics and semi-arid regions (10–40%). The
paper also emphasized reproducibility with a Zenodo data release and a public code
repository. The student was supported by this award and acknowledged DOE funding.

2.6. Related generative modeling output (not confirmed as acknowledging the
award in accessible text)

“Generative emulation of chaotic dynamics with coherent prior” (Computer Methods in
Applied Mechanics and Engineering) developed a new diffusion-based generative
framework (“Cohesion”) in which large-scale coherent structure is based on a reduced
order model (Koopman operator) aligned with Aim 1., which was used as conditioning
guidance during diffusion denoising. The coherent priors were efficiently approximated
using reduced-order models (including deep Koopman operators), emphasizing that
complex multiscale saptio-temporal turbulent data could be reduced in dimension
through modern machine learning. We then evaluated the results against canonical
chaotic systems and climate-relevant subseasonal-to-seasonal predictions,
demonstrating improved long-range stability and physical consistency under partial
observability. The student was supported by this award and acknowledged DOE
funding.

5. Scientific Findings and Impact
Across the award period, the Columbia contributions advanced a consistent scientific
message: in high-dimensional geophysical systems, “structure discovery” is inseparable
from physical constraints and uncertainty. The PNAS work demonstrates that
organization in convective systems—an emergent, multiscale phenomenon—can be
learned implicitly in a way that explains precipitation stochasticity and provides a route
to more faithful representations of variability. This directly supports the proposal’s Aim 1
thesis that latent structure can be learned from simulations (and potentially transferred
to observations) and that doing so clarifies mechanisms rather than merely improving
predictive scores.
The parameterization thread complements this by addressing how unresolved physics
should enter coarse models. Memory-based parameterizations coupled to differentiable
solvers provide a practical and conceptual bridge: the “memory” is not a black-box
approach but a structured encoding of nonlocal dependence in time, while
differentiability makes the approach compatible with modern machine learning pipelines.

In the broader ecosystem of turbulence and climate modeling, this direction is aligned
with DOE priorities around robust, uncertainty-aware prediction and scalable
computational workflows.
Finally, the project’s emphasis on uncertainty and robustness resonates in later outputs
on coherent-prior generative emulation and on the broader argument that perturbed
parameter ensembles remain an underused but essential tool for reducing model
spread.

6. Deviations, Uncompleted Elements, and Lessons Learned
The proposal anticipated a strong causal-transportability component under Aim 1 led by
co-PI Bareinboim, including the use of causal methods to help “transport causality from
simulation to observations.” Within Bareinboim’s scope of work, this research did not
culminate in a peer-reviewed publication explicitly attributable to Award DE-SC0022323
during the period of performance. This outcome reflects the realities of interdisciplinary
research timelines and the fact that several threads (convective organization,
parameterization, and uncertainty-aware modeling) matured more rapidly into
publishable contributions over the award window. The causal-inference objectives
remain scientifically well-motivated and, in our view, continue to represent an important
frontier for follow-on work.

7. Conclusions
DOE Award DE-SC0022323 enabled Columbia University activities that contributed to
the broader project goal of discovering physically meaningful structure in climate
extremes and related multiscale geophysical processes. The award’s impact is most
clearly evidenced by (i) peer-reviewed work explicitly acknowledging the award number,
most notably the PNAS paper led by Shamekh and Lamb with Gentine, and (ii) a
coherent set of methodological advances emphasizing physically structured
representations, differentiable and memory-aware parameterizations, and uncertainty
as a first-class modeling target. The combined outputs support DOE-relevant priorities
in robust predictive science, interpretable machine learning for Earth-system dynamics,
and scalable methods that can be integrated into next-generation modeling workflows.
