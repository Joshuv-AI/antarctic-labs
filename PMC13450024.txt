Eradicating Helicobacter pylori and reversing precancerous intestinal metaplasia by gastric epithelial cells-localizable oral nanomedicines
Authors: Shijie Ma, Zhirui Zhang, Jiabao Hao, Juan Li, Bingyun Lu, Suqi Hao, Yuhan Fan, Shihui Yu, Jinyao Liu, Haiyan Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ability to treat Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection and eliminate its associated gastric cancer risk is highly desirable but has proven to be extremely difficult. In this study, pilot proteomic screening of clinical gastric mucosal samples suggested a progressive decline in Sirtuin 1 abundance along the H. pylori-associated pathological cascade. Based on these findings, gastric epithelial cells-localizable oral nanomedicines (GLONs) are developed, whereby H. pylori eradication and reversal of precancerous intestinal metaplasia (IM) are simultaneously achieved via Sirtuin 1 restoration. GLONs are constructed by coating resveratrol, lactoferrin, and disulfide modified-fucoidan (DFu) co-assembled nanoparticles (RLF) with engineered mucin-overexpressing gastric epithelial cell membranes. The shell of GLONs resists gastric acid, enhances mucus penetration and epithelial cells uptake. After internalization, DFu undergoes oxidative destabilization in the H2O2-enriched infectious microenvironment induced by H. pylori, thereby triggering RLF core dissociation and subsequent component release. The resveratrol restored H. pylori infection-induced impairment of Sirtuin 1, thereby activating autophagy. Meanwhile, lactoferrin promoted antimicrobial peptide production and synergized with fucoidan-mediated enhancement of antigen presentation, ultimately enabling the clearance of both intracellular and extracellular H. pylori. In metaplastic gastric cells, Sirtuin 1 repairs damaged DNA, and inhibits malignant proliferation. In mouse models, under the tested 7-day regimen, GLONs produced greater reductions in gastric H. pylori burden and more pronounced improvements in IM-related phenotypes than the abbreviated triple-therapy. GLONs represent an innovative and highly efficient therapeutic platform for H. pylori infection and its complications.

Introduction

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infects approximately 50% of the global population and is closely associated with dyspepsia, chronic gastritis, peptic ulcers, and gastric cancer [1,2]. Current first-line therapies against H. pylori infection still rely on antibiotic-based triple or quadruple regimens. However, due to the increasing emergence of drug-resistant strains and the acid instability of antibiotics, the clinical efficacy of antimicrobial agents, including clarithromycin and levofloxacin, has dramatically declined [3,4]. Recent statistics indicate that the eradication rate of clarithromycin-based triple therapy has dropped from 90% in the 1990s to approximately 70% at present [5]. More critically, through repeated exposure to antibiotics, H. pylori has evolved evasion strategies such as intracellular survival, thereby resisting clearance by antibiotics [[6], [7], [8]]. Consequently, despite the rapid iteration of antibiotics, complete bacterial eradication remains challenging to achieve [9].

Recent advances in H. pylori-targeted nanomedicine, including MOF-based platforms for spatiotemporally coordinated multi-target intervention and hybrid biomembrane vesicle systems, have broadened the therapeutic strategies for bacterial eradication [10,11]. Nevertheless, recurrent bacterial colonization and the ensuing persistent inflammatory microenvironment continue to drive the initiation and progression of gastric precancerous lesions. Clinical evidence has demonstrated that H. pylori infection-induced lesions follow a well-defined cascade: chronic gastritis - atrophic gastritis - intestinal metaplasia - dysplasia - gastric cancer [[12], [13], [14]]. Among these stages, IM represents a critical precancerous node, pathologically characterized by the replacement of gastric glandular epithelial cells with intestinal-type epithelial cells (e.g., goblet cells). This stage is recognized as the final controllable intervention point to suppress the “inflammation-to-carcinoma” transformation [[15], [16], [17]]. Importantly, IM cells exhibit a vicious cycle of “DNA damage - failed repair - clonal expansion”, which significantly increases the risk of malignancy [[18], [19], [20]]. Therefore, based on an in-depth analysis of clinical infection targets, developing a drug delivery system capable of completely eradicating H. pylori and efficiently reversing IM is of great clinical significance.

By integrating sequencing data from clinical samples, we report for the first time a gradual decline in the expression of the key regulatory protein Sirtuin 1 (Sirt 1) during the progression from H. pylori infection to IM, resulting in dysregulated host inflammatory control. Previous studies have established that Sirt 1, an NAD+-dependent deacetylase, regulates cellular autophagy, inflammatory responses, and oxidative stress [21,22]. Specifically, Sirt 1 promotes autophagy by deacetylating key autophagy-related proteins such as LC3 and Atg5, and its activation restores H. pylori-impaired autophagic flux to eliminate intracellular bacteria [23,24]. Therefore, restoring Sirt 1 abundance to enhance autophagy represents an optimal strategy for eliminating intracellular H. pylori. More promisingly, Sirt 1 also potentiates the activity of multiple DNA repair enzymes, thereby modulating DNA damage repair, maintaining genomic stability, and suppressing aberrant host cell proliferation [25,26]. Resveratrol, a natural polyphenolic compound, acts as a Sirt 1 repairer, enhancing its deacetylase abundance and activity [[27], [28], [29]]. Resveratrol may also modulate the AMPK-Sirt 1-autophagy signaling network, thereby promoting autophagy activation and preserving cellular homeostasis [30]. However, the poor gastric acid stability and inadequate targeting of resveratrol severely limit its oral therapeutic efficacy, necessitating the development of an effective delivery system.

Herein, we present gastric epithelial cells-localizable oral nanomedicines (GLONs) that enable H. pylori eradication and precancerous IM reversal via Sirt 1 restoration (Scheme 1). Inspired by the natural protective effect of gastric mucin on epithelial cells, engineered mucin-overexpressing gastric epithelial cell membranes are constructed to serve as the shell of GLONs, conferring resistance to gastric acid degradation. Importantly, the hydrophilic mucin coating facilitates mucus penetration and homologous targeting of GLONs. Co-assembled nanoparticles (RLF), formed from resveratrol, lactoferrin, and disulfide modified-fucoidan (DFu) are encapsulated within the engineered mucin membrane, allowing targeted delivery to gastric epithelial cells. In response to the high H2O2 environment caused by H. pylori infection, the RLF components are released. The resveratrol restored H. pylori infection-induced impairment of Sirt 1, thereby activating autophagy. Meanwhile, lactoferrin promoted antimicrobial peptide production and synergized with fucoidan-mediated enhancement of antigen presentation, ultimately enabling the eradication of both intracellular and extracellular H. pylori. Moreover, In metaplastic gastric cells, Sirt 1 repairs damaged DNA, and reverses the metaplastic state. In mouse models of H. pylori infection and IM, GLONs show superior therapeutic efficacy over conventional antibiotics. Given the remarkable performance of GLONs in both in vitro and in vivo studies, we anticipate that this strategy will help minimize the risks of bacterial infection and cancer development.

Results and discussion

Progressive dysregulation of Sirt 1 during infection

The activity of mammalian Sirt 1 is strictly dependent on the intracellular concentration of NAD+, positioning it as a key molecular sensor that links cellular energy metabolism with gene expression regulation. Within the gastric mucosa, Sirt 1 governs multiple biological processes, including cell proliferation, apoptosis, inflammatory responses, and lineage differentiation [25,31,32]. To elucidate the regulatory role and dynamic progression of Sirt 1 during H. pylori infection and the development of gastric IM, we integrated multi-omics analysis with immunofluorescence imaging to systematically characterize the molecular and cellular features of 12 clinical gastric mucosal biopsy samples (Fig. 1A). Using a significance threshold of p < 0.05, differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified among the comparison groups. The highest number of differential proteins (625) was detected between uninfected and IM tissues, indicating substantial remodeling of the protein expression profile in metaplastic tissues (Fig. 1B). Volcano plots further illustrated the distribution of differential proteins across each comparison group. Compared with uninfected tissues, both H. pylori-infected and metaplastic tissues exhibited expression changes in proteins such as ATP2C2 and EPHA1, suggesting that the transition from infection to metaplasia is accompanied by widespread dysregulation of proteins involved in keratinization and proliferation control (Fig. 1C; Fig. S1 and Fig. S2). Venn diagram analysis revealed 11 common differential proteins, all of which represent core regulators persistently altered during the evolution from normal gastric mucosa to H. pylori infection and subsequent IM (Fig. S3). The heatmap showed that proteins maintaining normal gastric mucosal homeostasis and function, including RPS19BP1, OLR1, and FAR2, were progressively lost during infection and metaplasia (Fig. 1D). Gene Ontology (GO) biological process enrichment analysis of the differential proteins revealed that along with the loss of core regulatory factors, multiple fundamental biological processes such as mitochondrial energy metabolism, steady-state protein translation, and cellular stress response underwent comprehensive reconstruction (Fig. 1E). Co-immunoprecipitation analysis revealed that RPS19BP1, also known as AROS and a well-characterized functional regulator of Sirt 1 deacetylase activity, is a bona fide Sirt 1-interacting protein (Fig. S4) [33,34]. Ranking of protein expression changes placed RPS19BP1 among the most significantly altered differential proteins, indicating that it serves as an important molecular discriminator between normal gastric mucosa and metaplastic tissue (Fig. 1G). Consistently, Sirt 1 protein expression showed a progressive decrease along the gastric mucosal pathological cascade from uninfected tissues to H. pylori-infected tissues and IM tissues (Fig. 1H). Immunofluorescence imaging more clearly visualized the expression changes of Sirt 1 and γ-H2AX across different stages (Fig. 1I). Compared with uninfected and H. pylori-infected tissues, IM tissues displayed the lowest Sirt 1 abundance, accompanied by severe DNA damage (Fig. 1J and K). At the cellular level, the degree of Sirt 1 protein impairment increased with the bacterial infection load and infection duration (Fig. 1L and M). To further validate the pathological relevance of Sirt 1, we performed a loss-of-function assay using a Sirt 1 inhibitor and found that Sirt 1 inhibition markedly increased γ-H2AX expression, indicating aggravated DNA damage in gastric epithelial cells. Moreover, Sirt 1 inhibition also elevated the expression of caudal type homeobox 2 (CDX2), a key marker of IM, supporting the functional involvement of Sirt 1 loss in gastric epithelial injury and metaplastic progression (Fig. S5). Collectively, these findings reveal that Sirt 1, as a core regulatory factor, participates in the gradual evolution of gastric mucosa from normal state to H. pylori infection and IM, and that its expression impairment is an important contributor to cellular DNA damage and the development of IM.

Preparation and characterization of RLF

Inspired by the above findings, we sought to deliver resveratrol to gastric epithelial cells to upregulate Sirt 1 protein levels, thereby modulating the cellular state of gastric host cells during H. pylori infection. To this end, we encapsulated resveratrol within a co-assembled system based on lactoferrin and disulfide modified-fucoidan, which effectively addressed the key bottlenecks of poor stability and low cellular internalization efficiency. We modified disulfide bonds on fucoidan through esterification reaction, forming fucoidan with H2O2 responsive cleavage effect (Fig. 2A). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy of the synthesized DFu revealed a characteristic peak at 1731 cm−1, confirming ester bond formation (Fig. 2B). Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy further corroborated the successful DFu synthesis. As shown in Fig. 2C, native fucoidan showed typical broad proton signals of the polysaccharide backbone. In the spectrum of DFu, the broad resonance at δ 3.5–4.2 ppm was assigned to the sugar ring protons of fucoidan, while the signal at δ 1.0–1.3 ppm was attributed to the methyl protons at C-6 of fucose residues. The signal at δ 4.70 ppm was assigned to residual HOD. Compared with Fu, two new proton signals appeared in DFu at δ 2.75–2.85 ppm and δ 2.95–3.15 ppm, corresponding to the methylene protons adjacent to the carbonyl groups and disulfide bonds of DTDP, respectively. These results indicated the successful introduction of DTDP into fucoidan. Based on the 1H NMR spectrum, the estimated DS of DFu was approximately 0.53 DTDP units per fucosyl residue. ESI-MS analysis showed a broad ion envelope of native fucoidan, while a new series of mass distributions shifted by +191.99 Da was observed after esterification with 3,3′-dithiodipropionic acid, indicating successful grafting (Fig. S6). We next evaluated the H2O2-responsive properties of DFu by monitoring the release of thiol groups (Fig. 2D). The concentration of free thiols increased with escalating H2O2 concentrations, resulting in enhanced absorbance in the Ellman's assay. The minimum H2O2 concentration required to trigger DFu-mediated nanoparticle dissociation was approximately 10 μM, which was substantially lower than the intracellular H2O2 levels detected in H. pylori-infected and metaplastic gastric epithelial cells, supporting the physiological relevance of the H2O2-responsive dissociation of GLONs after cellular internalization (Fig. S7). Given the introduction of disulfide bonds in the synthesized DFu, we assessed the cytotoxicity of both native fucoidan and DFu against gastric epithelial cells. To improve the encapsulation efficiency of resveratrol, we optimized the mass ratio of lactoferrin to resveratrol under fixed DFu conditions. The results showed that an Lf:Res ratio of 15:1 yielded the highest encapsulation efficiency (93.98%) (Fig. 2F). In parallel, the in vitro anti-H. pylori activity of formulations with varying lactoferrin/resveratrol mass ratios was assessed as an additional bioactivity-based optimization criterion. Notably, the 15:1 formulation resulted in the lowest bacterial load among all tested ratios, demonstrating superior antibacterial efficacy and further validating its selection as the optimized formulation (Fig. S8). The co-assembled RLF nanoparticles exhibited a hydrodynamic diameter of 119.6 nm with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.200 and displayed a pronounced Tyndall effect (Fig. 2G). We further used NaCl to disrupt electrostatic interactions and Triton X-100 to disrupt hydrophobic interactions to explore co-assembly mechanisms. The results indicate that electrostatic interactions are the main driving force for co-assembly, while hydrophobic interactions play a complementary role (Fig. S9 and Fig. S10). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging coupled with elemental mapping revealed that RLF possessed a typical spherical morphology and contained carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and sulfur (S). Notably, the enrichment of sulfur within the nanoparticles further confirmed the successful integration of DFu into the nanostructure (Fig. 2H). As shown in Fig. 2I, RLF underwent structural disruption upon exposure to H2O2, leading to nanoparticle disintegration and subsequent drug release. The cumulative release of resveratrol was examined under physiological conditions and simulated gastric juice (H2O2 is added to simulate the infectious microenvironment). RLF exhibited limited resveratrol release at pH 7.4 and in simulated gastric juice, whereas H2O2 markedly accelerated resveratrol release under both conditions, confirming its H2O2-responsive release capability (Fig. S11). Analogous to coordination chemistry-based strategies for regulating biomaterial assembly and stimulus responsiveness, RLF nanoparticles achieved microenvironment-responsive resveratrol release under gastric acidic and oxidative conditions, supporting their potential for targeted intervention in the H. pylori-associated gastric microenvironment [[35], [36], [37]]. Collectively, these results demonstrated the successful construction of co-assembled RLF with H2O2-responsive drug release behavior.

Construction and characterization of GLONs

Inspired by the protective role of the mucin layer on gastric epithelial cells, we constructed a mucin-overexpressing gastric epithelial cell membranes for resisting degradation by gastric acid. The transmembrane anchoring capability of Mucin1 (MUC1‌) enabled its stable presentation on the nanoparticle surface, providing steric protection against gastric acid- and enzyme-mediated degradation while promoting specific uptake by gastric epithelial cells via homotypic targeting [38,39]. To upregulate MUC1 expression on gastric epithelial cells, we pretreated GES-1 cells with quercetin. The results showed that MUC1 protein expression increased in a concentration-dependent manner with escalating quercetin concentrations (Fig. 3A and B). Considering cell viability, we selected a quercetin concentration of 100 μM for subsequent experiments (Fig. S12). At this concentration, quercetin treatment significantly upregulated MUC1 mucin expression in GES-1 cells (Fig. 3C). Subsequently, cell membranes were isolated from normal cells and MUC1-upregulated cells via ultrasonication followed by density gradient centrifugation. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis showed that RLF coated with blank cell membrane (GRLF) had a hydrodynamic diameter of 133.7 nm, whereas the diameter of RLF coated with MUC1-overexpressing cell membrane (GLONs) increased to 155.0 nm (Fig. 3D). TEM imaging revealed that RLF was encapsulated within a phospholipid bilayer, displaying a distinct core–shell structure with a shell thickness of approximately 15-35 nm, consistent with the combined dimensions of the cell membrane and the MUC1 glycocalyx layer (Fig. 3E). To further verify the membrane coating of GLONs, the RLF core was labeled with FITC, while the MUC1 membrane protein was labeled with Cy5 for dual-fluorescence analysis. The images showed partial overlap between Res and MUC1 signals, with Cy5-MUC1 fluorescence frequently distributed around the RLF cores, supporting the successful coating of RLF nanoparticles with MUC1 cell membrane components (Fig. S13). Incorporation of MUC1 mucin further enhanced the negative surface charge of the nanoparticles to −36.3 mV and maintained system stability for 14 days (Fig. 3F; Fig. S14). MUC1 expression on GLONs remained largely unchanged from day 1 to day 14, with a retention rate of approximately 94.8%, indicating good preservation of the biomimetic membrane shell during storage (Fig. S15). SDS-PAGE analysis demonstrated that GLONs retained the original protein profile of the cell membrane while incorporating lactoferrin structural proteins (Fig. 3G). Immunoblotting further confirmed significantly higher MUC1 mucin expression in GLONs. The mucus layer serves as the primary physiological barrier encountered by orally administered drugs, limiting drug penetration via physical entrapment [42,43]. To evaluate the mucus-penetration ability of the formulations, we coated porcine gastric mucus onto Transwell inserts, added fluorescently labeled formulations to the apical chamber, and quantified fluorescence in the basolateral compartment (Fig. 3I). Both GCLF (Coumarin 6 replaces resveratrol) and C6@GLONs exhibited enhanced mucus penetration, likely due to the hydrophilic mucin coating on the membrane surface, which facilitates passage through the dense gastric mucus layer. Notably, the apparent permeability coefficient (Papp) of C6@GLONs was slightly lower than that of GCLF, potentially because MUC1 mucin loading increased steric hindrance, thereby reducing nanoparticle mobility. Nanoparticle tracking analysis further corroborated these findings (Fig. 3J). The cumulative penetration proportion-time profiles demonstrated that C6@GLONs achieved mucus penetration efficiency comparable to that of C6@PEG (Fig. S16). The high concentration of pepsin in gastric fluid poses a critical challenge to the stability of orally administered nanomedicines. In simulated gastric fluid (pH 1.5, pH 2.0, and pH 5.0), GLONs exhibited excellent stability, with no significant degradation observed during the critical 4-h gastric emptying window (Fig. 3K; Fig. S17 and Fig. S18). This protective effect is attributed to the dense glycan architecture of MUC1 mucin, which sterically hinders access of macromolecular enzymes such as pepsin to the lipid bilayer, thereby safeguarding the membrane structure from enzymatic degradation. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that quercetin treatment significantly increased the thickness of the glycocalyx layer on gastric epithelial cells (Fig. 3L and M). Molecular docking simulations further revealed that conjugation of MUC1 mucin to phosphatidylcholine significantly reduced the number of potential binding sites for pepsin (Fig. 3N). Similar trends were observed in docking analyses of sphingomyelin with pepsin (Fig. S19), further underscoring the critical role of the MUC1 glycocalyx in GLONs.

Cellular uptake and intracellular Anti-H. pylori activity

For orally administered nanomedicines designed to treat H. pylori-associated gastric lesions, efficient gastric retention is critical for sustaining local drug exposure and facilitating subsequent interaction with the gastric mucosal surface. After oral administration, DiR@GLONs exhibited stronger and more persistent fluorescence signals in the stomach than free DiR, with clear gastric fluorescence still observed at 4 h, indicating prolonged gastric retention of the DiR@GLONs (Fig. S20). Flow cytometric (FCM) analysis of isolated gastric epithelial cells showed that, compared with free DiR, DiR-GLONs significantly increased the proportion of DiR+ epithelial cells, supporting enhanced delivery and association of GLONs with gastric epithelial cells in vivo (Fig. S21). Subsequently, we evaluated the uptake efficiency of various formulations by gastric epithelial cells using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) and FCM. CLSM images revealed that GES-1 cells internalized minimal amounts of free C6, exhibiting the weakest green fluorescence signal. In contrast, cells treated with C6@GLONs displayed the strongest green fluorescence intensity (Fig. 4A), a finding corroborated by FCM analysis (Fig. 4B). We further examined the uptake efficiency of C6@GLONs in different cell lines. Owing to homotypic targeting effects, GES-1 cells exhibited the highest internalization efficiency (Fig. 4C). Notably, RAW264.7 macrophages also showed considerable fluorescence intensity, second only to GES-1 cells, which may be attributed to their inherently high phagocytic activity. To elucidate the internalization mechanism of C6@GLONs, we employed various endocytosis inhibitors [44]. Pretreatment with nystatin significantly reduced GLONs uptake, indicating that cholesterol-dependent membrane processes are involved in GLONs internalization. These processes may include lipid raft/caveolae-associated uptake and possible membrane fusion. Therefore, membrane fusion may contribute to GLONs uptake, but other endocytic pathways cannot be excluded (Fig. 4D). Upon treatment, GLONs responded to the elevated H2O2 levels in the infected microenvironment, releasing resveratrol intracellularly. Notably, GLONs treatment significantly restored Sirt 1 expression and upregulated autophagy compared with the H. pylori infection (Fig. 4F). Cryo-electron microscopy images further revealed that H. pylori infection compromised autophagic activity (red arrows), whereas GLONs treatment markedly increased the number of intracellular autophagosomes (Fig. 4G), suggesting its potential for eliminating intracellular H. pylori. We next employed the gentamicin protection assay to evaluate intracellular clearance efficacy. After eliminating extracellular bacteria, GLONs treatment significantly reduced the intracellular green fluorescence intensity, indicating effective eradication of intracellular H. pylori (Fig. 4H). Consistent with the fluorescence imaging results, the spread plate method further confirmed that GLONs treatment significantly reduced the number of H. pylori colonies from cell lysates (Fig. 4I and J). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that GLONs, following membrane fusion-mediated cellular uptake, restores Sirt 1 expression, upregulates autophagy, and effectively eliminates the intracellular niche of H. pylori within host cells.

Extracellular bacterial clearance: effects and mechanisms

Comprehensive clearance of extracellular H. pylori is essential for controlling bacterial infection in the stomach [45,46]. We employed a Transwell model to mimic antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) production and H. pylori clearance, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 5A. Following treatment with different formulations, the viable H. pylori burden in the lower chamber was markedly reduced, with GLONs displaying the strongest antibacterial efficacy (Fig. 5B). Consistently, spread plate analysis of H. pylori recovered from the lower chamber revealed no detectable viable colonies in the GLONs-treated group, further substantiating the potent bactericidal activity of GLONs (Fig. S22). We performed quantitative real-time PCR to assess the expression levels of nine AMPs genes in GES-1 cells seeded in the upper Transwell chamber. Notably, compared with the blank group, GLONs treatment upregulated the expression of LTF and CAMP by 7.55-fold and 5.08-fold, respectively (Fig. 5C and D). Based on the good linearity of the corresponding ELISA calibration curves, the concentrations of the antimicrobial peptides were quantitatively determined (Fig. S23). The concentrations of Lactoferricin-related peptide and LL-37 peptides in the lower chamber were markedly elevated following GLONs treatment, confirming its capacity to induce AMPs secretion from gastric epithelial cells (Fig. 5E). Although the structural integrity and functional persistence of free AMPs in the acidic, protease-rich gastric microenvironment warrant further investigation, the observed AMP-associated responses may cooperate with other host-defense mechanisms and collectively contribute to bacterial clearance. The integrity of the gastric mucosal epithelial barrier relies on tight junctions and adherens junctions between adjacent cells [49,50]. Virulence factors such as H. pylori CagA disrupt barrier function by targeting tight junction proteins, including ZO-1 and E-cadherin, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of chronic atrophic gastritis and IM [12,51]. Consequently, repairing tight junction protein expression represents a promising therapeutic strategy for H. pylori-associated gastric disorders. We employed a Transwell model to evaluate transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) and FITC-dextran paracellular transport, as depicted in Fig. 5G. H. pylori infection significantly compromised barrier function, as evidenced by a sharp decline in TEER values by day 14 (Fig. 5H). Notably, GLONs treatment restored TEER more effectively than other treatments, achieving levels comparable to those of the blank group. Consistently, the transepithelial transport of FITC-dextran in the GLONs group was similar to that in the blank group, demonstrating the ability of the nanoparticles to repair cell barriers and tight junction proteins (Fig. 5I). Western blot analysis corroborated these findings, confirming that GLONs effectively restored gastric mucosal barrier protein expression (Fig. 5J). Owing to its combined effects on eradicating extracellular bacteria and restoring the epithelial barrier, GLONs treatment led to a marked reduction in the fluorescence intensity of H. pylori on the cell surface, thereby substantially mitigating bacterial invasion of host cells (Fig. 5K).

Reversal of IM in vitro: effects and mechanisms

H. pylori infection-induced gastric IM represents a critical precancerous step in the progression from chronic gastritis to gastric adenocarcinoma. During the IM stage, host cells proliferate actively and are more likely to progress to dysplasia and intestinal-type gastric cancer, driven by continued damage and accumulation of mutations [16,52,53]. Building upon our previous findings, we established an in vitro IM model to evaluate the therapeutic potential of various formulations in reversing this pathological state [54]. The results showed that compared with the IM model group, GLONs significantly upregulated the expression of gastric-type genes MUC5AC and TFF-1, while downregulating the expression of intestinal-type genes CDX2 and MUC2 (Fig. 6A–D). CLSM further corroborated these findings at the protein level, demonstrating enhanced fluorescence intensity of the gastric mucin MUC5AC following GLONs treatment (Fig. 6E). Reversal of the IM phenotype may constrain the clonal expansion of genomically compromised cells, thereby suppressing malignant progression [55]. Colony formation assays, which reflect the proliferative capacity and tumorigenic potential of cell populations, revealed that compared with normal cells, IM cells exhibited obvious aggregative growth and rapid proliferation characteristics. After treatment with different nanoparticles, the colony-forming ability of cells generally decreased. IM cells are characterized by a hyperproliferative state, as evidenced by an increased proportion of cells in the S and G2/M phases and a correspondingly shortened G1 phase, leading to rapid yet aberrant differentiation [56]. Therefore, restoring normal gastric epithelial homeostasis requires cells to exit the hyperproliferative state and re-enter the G1 phase for repair and redifferentiation. Cell cycle analysis demonstrated that GLONs most effectively restored the G1 phase (Fig. 6G), underscoring its capacity to modulate cell cycle progression. DNA damage induced by N-methyl-N′-nitro-N-nitrosoguanidine (MNNG) and H. pylori infection exhibits a high degree of endpoint consistency and effect convergence in their mechanisms. The accumulation of mutations caused by both is an important factor in the occurrence and development of malignant tumors. In the IM cell model, the DNA damage marker γ-H2AX was significantly elevated, directly reflecting genomic instability. Remarkably, GLONs treatment substantially enhanced the DNA damage repair capacity of host cells (Fig. 6H). Pharmacological inhibition of Sirt 1 with EX-527 attenuated the GLONs-mediated improvements in DNA damage and IM-associated phenotypes, supporting a functional contribution of Sirt 1 restoration to these effects under in vitro conditions (Fig. S26). The present in vitro findings provide mechanistic support for a partial contribution of Sirt 1 to GLONs-mediated epithelial repair. Given the limitations of in vitro studies, the mechanism by which GLONs reverse IM within the complex gastric tissue microenvironment will be further investigated and validated. The comet assay further verified the repairing effect of the nanoparticles on DNA strand breaks. Specifically, GLONs significantly reduced the percentage of DNA in the tail of cells, which was not significantly different from that of normal cells, demonstrating excellent genomic repair capability (Fig. 6I). DNA fiber analysis was further employed to investigate the regulatory effect of the nanoparticles on replication dynamics in IM cells. Model cells exhibited canonical replication stress responses, characterized by activation of DNA damage checkpoints, global replication suppression, and significantly shortened IdU tract lengths. In contrast, GLONs treatment reduced the CIdU/IdU ratio, indicating restoration of replication fork homeostasis and regulated cell cycle progression (Fig. 6J). Collectively, GLONs achieved reversal of IM by correcting aberrant cell cycle progression and facilitating DNA damage repair, thereby highlighting its efficacy in impeding the advance of precancerous states.

In vivo Anti-H. pylori efficacy and biosafety evaluation

To evaluate the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of the nanoparticles against H. pylori infection, we established a C57BL/6 mouse model of H. pylori colonization (Fig. 7A). Specifically, mice were orally gavaged with H. pylori suspension every other day for a total of seven doses. Ten days after the final inoculation, successful model establishment was validated by assessing gastric bacterial load, urease activity, and immunohistochemical staining. Compared with blank mice, the model group exhibited significantly elevated gastric H. pylori colonization, positive urease reaction in gastric homogenates, and increased H. pylori positive areas in gastric tissue sections, collectively confirming successful infection (Fig. 7B–D). Infected mice were subsequently randomized into six groups: model, resveratrol, RLF, GRLF, GLONs, and abbreviated triple-therapy. Mice received daily oral gavage of the respective formulations for seven consecutive days and were euthanized seven days after the final administration. Given that GES-1 cells are not syngeneic with C57BL/6 mice, primary murine gastric epithelial cells were isolated via enzymatic digestion and differential centrifugation. The results of FCM showed that the expression levels of epithelial structural protein (EpCAM) and epithelial marker (E-cadherin) were abundant, and the cell purity was greater than 75% (Fig. 7E; Fig. S27). Using the same preparation strategy, gastric epithelial cell-localizable oral nanomedicines were successfully fabricated, with a hydrodynamic diameter of 167.2 nm, a zeta potential of −34.8 mV, and a MUC1 expression level of 28.8% (Table S1). As shown in Fig. 7F, all nanoparticles reduced gastric bacterial load to varying degrees compared with the model group. Notably, the average H. pylori load in mice of the GLONs group and triple therapy group was 4.45 and 3.41 orders of magnitude lower than that in the model group, respectively. To further investigate the therapeutic potential of GLONs in reversing IM in vivo, we established a mouse model of gastric IM by combining H. pylori infection with MNNG chemical induction. Specifically, mice were orally gavaged with H. pylori suspension every other day for a total of nine doses. Starting from the ninth day after the first bacterial administration, MNNG was added to the drinking water, and mice drank the water for 9 weeks (Fig. 9A). Accumulating evidence suggests that H. pylori eradication promotes regression of gastric IM, which has been incorporated into multiple clinical guidelines [58,59]. We next investigated the antibacterial effect of GLONs and triple therapy in the IM model. The results showed that both treatments significantly reduced H. pylori colonization and blocked the bacterial driving effect on the IM process at the source (Fig. 9F). Benefiting from its combined effects on bacterial clearance and Sirt 1 restoration, GLONs reactivated gastric mucous cells and normalized the morphology and staining characteristics of the gastric mucosa (Fig. 9G). In contrast to the marked upregulation of Ki-67 and aberrant proliferation patterns observed in the IM model, GLONs restored normal cellular proliferation dynamics and tissue homeostasis (Fig. 9H). We further analyzed the expression levels of the gastric lineage marker MUC5AC and the IM marker CDX2 (Fig. 9I). Compared with abbreviated triple-therapy, GLONs significantly reshaped the expression of the gastric lineage-specific marker MUC5AC (Fig. 9J). Concurrently, GLONs reduced CDX2 protein abundance by 7.33-fold relative to the model, thereby alleviating CDX2-mediated reprogramming of gastric epithelial gene expression (Fig. 9K). To elucidate the regulatory mechanism underlying CDX2 reprogramming, we evaluated host autophagic flux. GLONs activated autophagic flux, promoting the clearance of aberrant protein aggregates and damaged organelles within IM cells, thereby suppressing abnormal proliferation (Fig. 9L). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that GLONs promote gastric mucosal phenotypic reversion and restore proliferative homeostasis, providing proof-of-concept evidence for effective reversal of IM through a species-matched gastric epithelial cell membrane-coating strategy. Nevertheless, because the murine membrane-coated formulation was not directly compared with its human GES-1 membrane-coated counterpart in vivo, continued investigation of the biodistribution, immunogenicity, safety, and targeting performance of human-derived GLONs is warranted to facilitate their clinical translation.

Conclusion

Exploratory analysis of clinical samples suggested a progressive reduction in Sirt 1 abundance along the H. pylori-associated Correa cascade. Building upon these finding, we developed the GLONs that simultaneously eradicate H. pylori and reverse precancerous IM by restoring Sirt 1. Unlike conventional antibiotics that only target extracellular bacteria and fail to address host cellular damage, GLONs achieve a dual therapeutic effect through a rationally designed biomimetic shell-core architecture. The mucin-overexpressing cell membrane shell confers resistance to gastric acid, facilitates mucus penetration, and enables specific uptake by gastric epithelial cells, overcoming major barriers in oral drug delivery to the stomach. After internalization, H2O2-responsive disulfide bond cleavage in RLF triggers core dissociation, releasing resveratrol, lactoferrin, and fucoidan. These synergistically clears intracellular and extracellular H. pylori by activating autophagy, inducing antimicrobial peptide production, and enhancing antigen presentation. More importantly, in metaplastic gastric cells, resveratrol rescues H. pylori-caused impairment of Sirt 1, repairs damaged DNA, and inhibits malignant proliferation. This combination of antibacterial and host-restorative functions distinguishes GLONs from existing therapies. Importantly, under the tested 7-day regimen, GLONs achieved greater bacterial clearance and more pronounced improvement of IM than the abbreviated triple-therapy in mouse models. These findings highlight the clinical potential of Sirt 1 restoration as a therapeutic axis for H. pylori-associated gastric cancer prevention. In a word, GLONs represent a promising oral nanoplatform that integrates pathogen clearance with host cell repair, opening a new avenue for managing H. pylori infection and its complications.

Nevertheless, given the limited size of the proteomic cohort (n = 4 per group), the observed reduction in Sirt 1 should be considered an exploratory observation and warrants further validation in larger, independent clinical cohorts. The current findings were obtained under an abbreviated 7-day preclinical regimen. Therefore, the comparative efficacy of GLONs and a guideline-concordant 14-day antibiotic eradication regimen warrants further investigation, with systematic evaluation of bacterial clearance, IM-related pathological outcomes, treatment safety, and post-treatment recurrence. Given the ideal therapeutic efficacy of GLONs in mouse models, we believe that considering a more comprehensive clinical evaluation before transformation will be beneficial for achieving drug efficacy. Large-scale manufacturing will need standardized MUC1-overexpressing gastric epithelial cell banks, controlled passage numbers and culture conditions, and predefined quality-control criteria for membrane yield, MUC1 abundance, membrane protein and lipid composition, endotoxin levels, and biological targeting activity to ensure batch-to-batch consistency. The effects of cryopreservation and long-term storage on membrane integrity, MUC1 activity, and epithelial-targeting performance should also be systematically assessed. Moreover, because gastric emptying generally occurs within several hours, it will be important to determine whether the effective gastric exposure and mucosal retention window of GLONs is sufficient to sustain antibacterial and epithelial-repair effects under clinically relevant conditions. In addition to monotherapy, sequential or combination regimens integrating GLONs with existing antibiotics should be explored to enhance bacterial eradication, reduce the required antibiotic dose and treatment duration, and potentially limit recurrence and resistance. Comprehensive evaluation of these parameters will be essential for advancing GLONs toward clinical translation.
