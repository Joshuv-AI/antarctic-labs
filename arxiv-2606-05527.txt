On the Cryptographic Structure Required for Verifying Qubits
James Bartusek, Itay Shalit

Abstract
Classically testing for the presence of anti-commuting operators on a quantum device is a critical tool underpinning recent progress in classical verification of quantum computation. While we can base such tests on certain cryptographic assumptions, known results require highly structured assumptions, e.g. trapdoor claw-free functions. In this work, we seek to explain this state of affairs by constructing strong cryptography from (certain forms of) classical tests of anti-commutation. In particular, we formulate the notion of a “test of non-commutation” (ToNC), which is an interactive protocol between a quantum prover and classical verifier where, in the final round, the prover applies one of two binary observables P 0 , P 1 P_{0},P_{1} depending on the verifier’s challenge bit c c . An ( ϵ , δ ) (\epsilon,\delta) -ToNC is any such protocol where there exists a quantum strategy that attains ϵ \epsilon advantage in making the verifier accept, but any quantum strategy with commuting P 0 , P 1 P_{0},P_{1} can attain at most δ \delta advantage. We then show the following results. • ( ϵ , δ ) (\epsilon,\delta) -ToNC implies (classical-communication) key agreement (KA) for any δ < 5 ​ ϵ − 1 4 \delta<\frac{5\epsilon-1}{4} . • ( ϵ , δ ) (\epsilon,\delta) -ToNC plus one-way functions implies (classical-communication) oblivious transfer (OT) for any δ < ϵ 2 + ϵ 2 \delta<\frac{\epsilon^{2}+\epsilon}{2} . Along the way, we develop tools for and provide the first known results on hardness amplification for post-quantum KA and OT, where the communication is classical but the adversary may be quantum. In particular, we prove the following results of independent interest. • Post-quantum hard-core measure theorem: For any efficiently sampleable, high min-entropy distribution D D over classical ( x , b ) (x,b) such that quantum circuits have advantage at most δ \delta in predicting b b given x x , there exists a sub-distribution M ⪯ D M\preceq D of density 1 − δ 1-\delta such that b b is (nearly) optimally quantum-hard to predict on ( x , b ) ← M (x,b)\leftarrow M . • Post-quantum interactive XOR lemma: Given any classically-interactive protocol such that quantum adversaries have advantage at most δ \delta in guessing a private challenger bit b b , repeating sequentially yields a protocol where quantum adversaries have advantage at most δ 2 + negl ⁡ ( λ ) \delta^{2}+{\rm negl}(\lambda) in guessing the XOR of the two challenger bits b 1 ⊕ b 2 b_{1}\oplus b_{2} .

Introduction

The qubit, being the basic unit of quantum information, lies at the foundation of quantum computation and the theory of quantum mechanics itself. While one can define a qubit as a unit vector in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, it can alternatively be understood as any pair of anti-commuting binary observables
[
Vid20
]
. This idea traces its origins back to the Heisenberg view of quantum mechanics, and is justified by the fact that any pair of anti-commuting binary observables is, up to an isometry, equivalent to the Pauli observables
\sigma_{X}
and
\sigma_{Z}
operating on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. It turns out that this definition has particularly nice operational properties, as discussed at length in
[
Vid20
]
.

Verifying qubits.

A natural question of importance to the theory of quantum mechanics, and especially so in the early days of quantum information-processing, is whether it is possible to
classically
test for the presence of a qubit on a purported quantum device. That is, can we validate the existence of anti-commuting operators just by sending classical signals and receiving classical responses? This motivates the following definition of a “qubit test”.

Qubit test (informal).

A qubit test is a classical interactive protocol between a classical (polynomial-time) verifier and a quantum (polynomial-time) prover, whose description includes two binary observables
P_{0},P_{1}
. In the final round, the verifier sends a challenge
c\in\{0,1\}
, and the prover returns the bit obtained by applying
P_{c}
to its current state. For any prover that makes the verifier accept with probability greater than some threshold
\alpha
, it must be the case that
P_{0}P_{1}\approx-P_{1}P_{0}
.

While qubit tests are of inherent interest as a bridge between the classical and quantum worlds, in recent years they have also played a key role in the construction of several more advanced classical verification protocols. For example, methods developed to test for the presence of qubits underpin certifiable randomness protocols
[
BCM+21
]
, classical verification of general BQP computation
[
Mah22
,
NZ23
]
, verifiable remote state preparation (and applications)
[
GV19
,
Zha25
,
Zha23
]
, and computational self-testing (and applications)
[
MV21
,
MDCAF21
]
, to name a few.

However, a common pattern can be identified behind these results. In particular, all instantiations rely on mathematically “structured” assumptions (LWE, group actions, LIP) that imply strong forms of asymmetric cryptography such as (classical-communication) oblivious transfer. In fact, it remains open to construct qubit tests even given some basic forms of asymmetric structure, such as a (plain) trapdoor function.

Comparison with proofs of quantumness.

This situation can be contrasted with what is known about the relaxed goal of (classically) verifiable
proofs of quantumness
. Here, the goal is to devise a protocol where quantum provers have a strictly greater advantage than classical provers, but do not guarantee anything beyond the fact that the prover is non-classical. It is known that efficiently-verifiable proofs of quantumness exist in the random oracle model (that is, from a heuristic use of unstructured cryptography)
[
YZ24
]
and
inefficiently
-verifiable proofs of quantumness exist if (and only if) one-way puzzles exist
[

[

,
Kre21

MY22

While proofs of quantumness have relevance to the goal of establishing quantum advantage, they do not appear to serve as a bedrock for obtaining more advanced forms of classical verification. This state of affairs brings into focus a potential dividing line between proofs of quantumness and qubit tests, both in terms of functionality (the methods behind qubit tests appear to have much more powerful applications) and feasibility (proofs of quantumness can be instantiated with hash functions, while qubit tests are only known from structured assumptions). The goal of this work is to gain a more principled understanding of why this line exists.

Tests of non-commutation.

In fact, we consider a weaker primitive which merely certifies that the prover’s observables do not
fully
commute, rather than that they approximately anti-commute. We refer to this primitive as a test of non-commutation (ToNC). It differs from a qubit test only in the soundness guarantee: For any quantum polynomial-time prover that succeeds with probability greater than
\alpha
, we require that
P_{0}P_{1}\neq P_{1}P_{0}
in place of the stronger conclusion that
P_{0}P_{1}\approx-\,P_{1}P_{0}.
We proceed to show that ToNCs imply certain strong forms of cryptography, which establishes the same for full-fledged qubit tests.

1.1
Results

We give constructions of fully-secure oblivious transfer and key agreement protocols with purely classical communication, starting from (even weakly-secure) tests of non-commutation (ToNC). Along the way, we obtain the first
post-quantum
hardness amplification results for bit agreement (BA) and oblivious transfer (OT), which are of independent interest. Our main novel techniques go into building weakly-secure OT, and amplifying both weakly-secure BA and OT to their fully-secure variants. In order to present these results precisely, we first fix definitions of ToNC, weak bit agreement, and OT.

Definitions.

We begin by formalizing our notion of ToNC. As discussed above, we consider interactive protocols where the prover’s strategy consists of three parts: a quantum polynomial-time (QPT) interactive machine
P_{\mathsf{Prep}}
that interacts with a PPT verifier
V
, and two (potentially non-commuting) binary observables
P_{0},P_{1}
. The interactive phase is denoted as

\ket{\psi},c,a^{*}\leftarrow\langle P_{\mathsf{Prep}}(1^{\lambda}),V(1^{\lambda})\rangle,

where
\ket{\psi}
is the prover’s final state,
c\in\{0,1\}
is a challenge bit sent to the prover, and
a^{*}
is the “correct” answer bit kept private by the verifier. We then define a
(\epsilon,\delta)
-ToNC as satisfying the following properties.

\Pr\left[a=a^{*}:\begin{array}[]{r}\ket{\psi},c,a^{*}\leftarrow\langle P_{\mathsf{Prep}}(1^{\lambda}),V(1^{\lambda})\rangle\\

Post-quantum hardness amplification.

Our main results are obtained in two steps: (1) construct weak forms of BA and OT from ToNC, and (2) amplify the hardness of the BA or OT.

Even though our protocols utilize only classical communication, the honest parties may be quantum, and the adversaries of course may be quantum as well. Thus, we need
post-quantum
hardness amplification for BA and OT. While BA and OT amplification enjoys a long history of study in the purely classical setting, to the best of our knowledge there is no prior work that considers post-quantum hardness amplification for BA or OT.

We first consider post-quantum BA amplification. Here, the adversarial Eve receives a classical input (the transcript of the protocol) and applies a quantum circuit in an attempt to guess the shared bit. In the purely classical setting, Holenstein
[
Hol05
]
gave a tight amplification result, establishing that
(\epsilon,\delta)
-BA implies fully-secure key agreement whenever
\delta<\frac{2\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}
. This is tight since
(\epsilon,\delta)
-BA exists unconditionally whenever
\delta\geq\frac{2\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}

[
Hol05
]
.

This result was derived from a tight hard-core set theorem, improving on that of
[
Imp95
]
, and stated roughly as follows. Consider any function
f
and predicate
P
such that for any polynomial-size classical circuit,
\Pr_{x\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{n}}[C(f(x))=P(x)]\leq 1-\frac{\delta}{2}
. Then there exists a set
S\subset\{0,1\}^{n}
of density roughly
\delta
such that
C
has very close to
1/2
advantage on
x\leftarrow S
.

We give a post-quantum analogue of this tight hard-core set theorem. The fact that the predictor is now a quantum predictor requires modifying the proof of the original theorem, as well as slightly modifying the statement. We discuss these changes further in
Section

2.3
.

Theorem 1.3
.

(Informal: Post-quantum hard-core measure)
Let
\{D_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}}
be any family of distributions over
(x,b)
that can be sampled in quantum polynomial time, such that
\max_{(x,b)}\{D_{\lambda}(x,b)\}={\rm negl}(\lambda).
If for any polynomial-size quantum circuit
Q
,

\Pr_{(x,b)\leftarrow D_{\lambda}}[Q(x)=b]\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2},

then there exists a measure
M_{\lambda}\preceq D_{\lambda}
of density roughly
1-\delta
such that

\Pr_{(x,b)\leftarrow M_{\lambda}}[Q(x)=b]\approx\frac{1}{2}.

By combining our post-quantum hard-core measure theorem with the techniques of
[
Hol05
]

Theorem 1.4

For constants

, post-quantum
(\epsilon,\delta)

(1,0)
-BA if and only if

For any

Discussion.

Our work is motivated by the apparent gap between the type of cryptography known to be sufficient for qubit tests (and related forms of classical verification) and the type of cryptography sufficient for weaker primitives such as tests of quantumness.

Starting with the work of
[
IR89
]
, cryptographers have studied an often elucidating characterization of cryptographic primitives into two groups: (1) those that exist unconditionally in the random oracle model (sometimes called “minicrypt” primitives), and (2) those that
provably do not
exist unconditionally in the random oracle model (sometimes called “cryptomania” primitives). While the group (1) primitives can be heuristically instantiated with unstructured cryptography such as hash functions, group (2) primitives seem to require highly structured, e.g. algebraic, forms of cryptographic hardness.

While tests of quantumness are known to exist unconditionally in the (quantum) random oracle model
[
YZ24
]
, and thus exist in group (1), we so far do not know such a result for qubit tests. Our work shows that (at least, in the parameter ranges covered by our theorem statements above) establishing qubit tests as a group (1) or “minicrypt” primitive would
also
establish classical-communication key agreement and oblivious transfer as minicrypt primitives. This would be a highly surprising result, for the following reasons. First of all, even though the study of (classical-communication) key agreement goes back to the earliest days of modern cryptography
[
DH76
]
, there have been no candidates proposed from unstructured cryptography. This is partially explained by the result of
[
IR89
]
, who showed that there is no black-box construction from one-way functions assuming that the honest parties and adversaries make only
classical
queries to the one-way function. Extending this result to the setting of quantum queries remains an open question, though there has been recent progress towards this goal
[
ACC+22
,
LLLL25
]
.

In fact, current results suggest that qubit tests may require
more
structure than even classical-communication key agreement. For example, consider that injective trapdoor functions, which are known to imply key agreement, are not known to imply qubit tests. Instead, current constructions of qubit tests utilize
two-to-one
trapdoor functions, at the very least.

This situation can be partially explained by our result establishing that tests of non-commutation plus one-way functions imply oblivious transfer. Indeed, while injective trapdoor functions imply key agreement, they are black-box separated from oblivious transfer
[
GKM+00
]
.
5
5

5

Again, note that this result only considers honest parties and adversaries that make classical queries to the primitives, and the quantum-query case remains open
Thus, if one could build qubit tests from injective trapdoor functions (which trivially also imply one-way functions), this would yield classical-communication OT from injective trapdoor functions, which would be considered a very surprising result.

Related work

Proofs of quantum memory
[

.

Recently,
[
HMOY25
]
introduced the notion of a proof of quantum memory (PoQM), which is a classical-communication protocol that occurs in two stages. After the first stage, there is a one-message challenge from the verifier that is answered by the prover. It has completeness
\alpha
if there exists a prover that can cause the verifier to accept with probability
\alpha
, and it has soundness
\beta
if any prover that maintains only
classical
memory between stages can cause the verifier to accept with probability at most
\beta
.
6
6

6

They also consider a more general variant, where there is a number of qubits of memory that suffice for completeness, and a fewer number of qubits for which the soundness bound holds.

PoQM is, in general, a less structured primitive than ToNC. For example, it is not hard to see that (normal form)
(\epsilon,\delta)
-ToNC implies PoQM with completeness
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}
and soundness
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}
, as well as “extraction probability”
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}
, which refers to the probability that the verifier can predict the prover’s final message. Moreover,
[
HMOY25
]
establish that PoQM with (1) extraction probability
1-1/{\rm poly}
and, (2) a
1/{\rm poly}
gap between completeness and soundness, implies (fully-secure) classical-communication key agreement. Thus, our result that
(1,\delta)
-ToNC implies KA for any
\delta<1
can also be achieved by an alternate route that goes through PoQM.

However, the majority of our results do not follow from
[
HMOY25
]
. This includes our constructions of KA from
(\epsilon,\delta)
-ToNC for any setting of
\epsilon<1
(covering important examples such as the compiled CHSH game), as well as all of our constructions of OT from ToNC (plus one-way functions), explaining the lack of approaches to building ToNC from weaker forms of asymmetric cryptography such as plain trapdoor functions.

In fact, they require one-way functions as well to obtain the notion of OT that we consider in this paper.

OSP is a stronger primitive than ToNC: While OSP is known to imply
(2\cos^{2}(\pi/8)-1,0.5)
-ToNC
[
BK25
]
by compiling the CHSH game, the reverse direction is not known. Whether we can obtain (some form of) OSP from ToNC is an interesting question, though appears somewhat difficult.

Foundations of interactive quantum advantage
[
TZ25
]
.

In another recent work,
[
TZ25
]
study the cryptographic implications of verifiable quantum advantage. Their result most relevant to this work establishes that there is no construction of constant-round interactive quantum advantage from the black-box use of indistinguishability obfuscation and one-way permutations. Since constant-round
(\epsilon,\delta)
-ToNC immediately implies constant-round quantum advantage for any
\delta<\epsilon
, this establishes the same for any non-trivial constant-round ToNC. Our results differ from theirs in the following ways:

•

Our cryptographic implications of ToNC apply to ToNC with any number of rounds, and are not limited to black-box constructions of ToNC.

•

Our results give evidence that ToNC cannot be constructed from random oracles, while
[
TZ25
]
’s results do not rule out the possibility that ToNC can be constructed from collision-resistant hashing (which exists in the random oracle model).

(Post-)quantum parallel repetition
[
BQSY24
,
HK25
]
.

A couple of recent works have ushered in the study of hardness amplification in the post-quantum and quantum settings, in particular when protocols are
parallel
repeated. However, as we discuss further in
Section

2
, these results do not have implications to post-quantum key agreement and oblivious transfer amplification, for the following reasons.
[
HK25
]
focus primarily on
public-coin arguments
, where the job of the adversary is to
convince the verifier to accept
, as opposed to guess a hidden bit known to a private-coin challenger. While they do have a private-coin result, it is limited to three-message protocols.
[
BQSY24
]
focus on
three-message, quantum-communication
protocols (they also have a round-compression theorem for quantum-communication arguments, but this does not apply to classical-communication protocols). While they do derive a quantum analogue of Yao’s XOR lemma, it does not apply to the classically-interactive private-coin setting that we require for OT amplification.

1.3
Open problems

Our work motivates several directions for future research, which we outline here.

1.

Can we tighten our implications from ToNC to key agreement and OT? For example, there exists a region of
\delta<\epsilon
for which we don’t know how to derive OT from
(\epsilon,\delta)
-ToNC. Moreover, can we hope to remove the reliance on one-way functions and establish OT from ToNC unconditionally?

2.

As mentioned earlier, our BA amplification techniques only apply to adversaries with non-uniform classical advice, while our OT amplification techniques only apply to adversaries with non-uniform quantum advice. Can we give post-quantum BA and OT amplification for
both
types of advice? Can we give post-quantum BA and OT amplification in the
uniform
setting?

3.

Can we continue to improve our understanding of the relationships between PoQM, ToNC, OSP, and OT? For example, does some variant of ToNC imply OSP? Does OT imply ToNC, or is there a separation? One current gap between what is known about OT vs. ToNC is the fact that trapdoor permutations are known to imply OT, but not known to imply ToNC.

4.

In this work, we consider ToNC where the prover’s final-round strategy consists of
binary-outcome observables
. While this is a natural setting, one could consider a more general type of protocol where the prover’s final-round strategy outputs a string of bits as opposed to a single bit. In fact, this is the setting considered by the original qubit test protocol of
[
BCM+21
]
. Can we show cryptographic implications from such multi-bit answer ToNCs?

5.

As we are the first to study post-quantum hardness amplification for key agreement and OT, several questions remain. For example, can we derive more general amplification results for OT? Such results would presumably extend the parameter regime in which our construction of OT from ToNC remains valid. Additionally, are there other applications of our post-quantum hard-core measure theorem or post-quantum interactive XOR lemma?

Technical overview

In this overview, we will first cover our constructions of weak bit agreement (BA) and weak oblivious transfer (OT) from tests of non-commutation (ToNC), and proceed to discuss our post-quantum hardness amplification techniques.

Normal-form ToNC.

Recall from the previous section that we work with a notion that we call “normal-form” ToNC. In such a ToNC, the interaction between quantum prover and classical verifier always reaches a final stage where the prover receives a challenge bit
c
and the verifier obtains a (private) answer bit
a^{*}
. The prover then applies one of two binary observables
P_{0}
or
P_{1}
to its state
\ket{\psi}
, depending on the choice of
c
, to obtain an answer
a
. We say that the prover has
\epsilon
advantage if
\Pr[a=a^{*}]=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}
.

In
Section

4.2
, we show that a more-general variant of ToNC (where the verifier may sometimes accept both or neither possible answers) can be compiled into normal-form ToNC with the same completeness-soundness gap. Hence, in the remainder of this section, we will focus on building cryptography from normal-form ToNC.

2.1
Weak BA from ToNC

It turns out that (normal-form) ToNC can essentially already be seen as a weak bit agreement between prover and verifier. Indeed, suppose that the prover computes
a
but keeps it private. Then we have that the prover and verifier agree on a bit
a=a^{*}
with advantage
\epsilon
.

Now consider an eavesdropper
E
who may observe the (classical) transcript
\tau
of interaction between the prover and verifier, and suppose that they are able to guess the value of
a^{*}
with advantage
\gamma
(meaning probability
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2}
). Since the transcript
\tau
is disjoint from the prover’s state
\ket{\psi}
, such an
E
can be used to derive a
commuting
strategy with advantage
\frac{\epsilon+\gamma}{2}
. In particular, consider a prover that, prior to seeing the final challenge bit
c
, flips a random coin
b\leftarrow\{0,1\}
. If
b=c
, it runs the honest prover strategy on its final state
\ket{\psi}
while if
b=1-c
, it runs
E
on the classical transcript of the protocol. Such a prover is commuting by inspection and has advantage exactly
\frac{\epsilon+\gamma}{2}
.

Now, suppose we start with a
(\epsilon,\delta)
-ToNC according to the definition provided in the previous section (and formally in
Section

4.1
and
Section

4.1
). Then we obtain a bit agreement protocol with correctness advantage
\epsilon
, and where any
E
has advantage at most
2\delta-\epsilon
in guessing the verifier’s bit (call her Alice). Due to technical reasons in the ensuing bit agreement amplification, we care in particular about
E
’s advantage
conditioned
on Alice and Bob outputting the same bit. Thus, the best
conditional
advantage bound we can derive comes out to

\delta^{\prime}=\frac{1+4\delta-3\epsilon}{1+\epsilon},

yielding a
(\epsilon,\delta^{\prime})
-BA protocol for such
\delta^{\prime}
according to the definition given in the previous section (and formally in
Section

3.1
). The formal protocol and analysis are given in
Section

5.1
.

2.2
Weak OT from ToNC

Next, we discuss our construction of weak OT from ToNC. Building OT presents several complications that did not arise in the construction of BA. For instance, the adversary
actively
participates (potentially maliciously) in the protocol. Moreover, to prepare for our OT amplification step, we will actually build a stronger notion of OT than what was presented in the previous section, which we call committed-bit OT.

Skeleton protocol.

In this overview, we give a sense of the core of our idea by presenting a bare-bones OT protocol that works only if several conditions are satisfied. In the body, we utilize techniques such as cryptographic commitments (which introduces an additional assumption of one-way functions), cut-and-choose, and random-termination to “compile” the skeleton protocol into a full-fledged committed-bit OT. However, the main intuition can be appreciated with the following skeleton, which uses a (normal-form)
(\epsilon,\delta)
-ToNC.

•

Let the OT sender be the verifier and the OT receiver be the prover in two runs of the ToNC. That is, run

\ket{\psi_0},c_{0},a^{*}_{0}\leftarrow\langle P_{\mathsf{Prep}}(1^{\lambda}),V_{\mathsf{Prep}}(1^{\lambda})\rangle,\quad\ket{\psi_1},c_{1},a^{*}_{1}\leftarrow\langle P_{\mathsf{Prep}}(1^{\lambda}),V_{\mathsf{Prep}}(1^{\lambda})\rangle.

However, the sender (verifier) does not send
(c_{0},c_{1})
to the receiver (prover) yet.

•

The receiver samples
c_{R,0},c_{R,1}\leftarrow\{0,1\}
and computes answers

a_{R,0}\leftarrow P_{c_{R,0}}(\ket{\psi_0}),\quad a_{R,1}\leftarrow P_{c_{R,1}}(\ket{\psi_1}).

•

The receiver sends
c_{R,0}\oplus c_{R,1}
and the protocol only continues if
c_{R,0}\oplus c_{R,1}\neq c_{0}\oplus c_{1}
. In this case, the sender sends
c_{0},c_{1}
to the receiver and outputs
r_{0}=a_{0}^{*}
and
r_{1}=a_{1}^{*}
as its OT bits.

•

The receiver sets
b
to be the unique bit such that
c_{R,b}=c_{b}
, and sets
r=a_{R,b}
.

First, note that if the receiver uses the honest prover strategy, then
r=r_{b}
with advantage
\epsilon
, as in this case the challenge
c_{R,b}
it used to produce
a_{R,b}
was the same as the verifier’s challenge
c_{b}
.

Next, note that receiver security is optimal: The sender learns nothing about the receiver’s bit
b
since it learns
c_{R,0}\oplus c_{R,1}
but neither bit individually.

Finally, it remains to prove a bound on the advantage that the (potentially adversarial) receiver has in guessing
r_{1-b}
.

Enforcing good behavior.

We say that an adversarial receiver is “well-behaved” if (1) its advantage in guessing
a_{R,b}=a^{*}_{b}
is indeed (roughly)
\epsilon
, (2)
c_{R,0},c_{R,1}
are chosen uniformly at random, and (3) it honestly reports the value of
c_{R,0}\oplus c_{R,1}
. In the full protocol, we enforce that the receiver is well-behaved by using a coin-flipping protocol to sample
c_{R,0},c_{R,1}
, having the receiver commit to its challenges
c_{R,0},c_{R,1},c_{R,0}\oplus c_{R,1}
and answers
a_{R,0},a_{R,1}
, and running a cut-and-choose plus random-termination strategy that repeats the protocol and requires them to open all but one of these sets of commitments.

Reduction to ToNC.

Now, we argue that any well-behaved receiver with advantage
\gamma
in guessing
r_{1-b}
implies a
commuting
prover strategy in the ToNC with advantage roughly
\frac{\epsilon+\gamma}{2}
. To see this, consider a prover
\widetilde{P}
that utilizes the well-behaved receiver as follows.
\widetilde{P}
simulates the receiver’s interaction with the sender in two ToNC executions except that it performs the verifier itself in one of them, and forwards messages to its own verifier in the other. Which one is forwarded to the “real” verifier will be chosen uniformly at random, but for the sake of discussion let’s say protocol 0 is forwarded and protocol 1 is simulated in
\widetilde{P}
’s head.

Condition on
c_{R,0}\oplus c_{R,1}\neq c_{0}\oplus c_{1}
and let
b
be such that
c_{R,b}=c_{b}
. If
b=0
, then we know the receiver’s answer
a_{R,b}
will yield advantage
\epsilon
in predicting the verifier’s bit
a^{*}
, so
\widetilde{P}
will return
a_{R,b}
to its verifier. Otherwise, if
b=1
, then we know that the receiver’s guess for
r_{b-1}=r_{0}
has advantage
\gamma
in predicting the verifier’s bit
a^{*}
, so
\widetilde{P}
will return its guess for
r_{b-1}
instead. Since
c_{R,0},c_{R,1}
are random, and we randomize the choice of which of the two protocols are forwarded to the verifier, both cases happen with probability exactly 1/2, yielding an overall advantage of
\frac{\epsilon+\gamma}{2}
. By soundness of the ToNC, we have that
\frac{\epsilon+\gamma}{2}<\delta
, and thus
\gamma<2\delta-\epsilon
.

Using non-uniformity.

This is the main idea that underlies our result that
(\epsilon,\delta)
-ToNC implies
(\epsilon,2\delta-\epsilon,0)
(committed-bit) OT. However, before moving on, we point out one complication that arises in the above strategy. On the one hand,
\widetilde{P}
actually has to
learn
the values
c_{R,0},c_{R,1},a_{R,0},a_{R,1}
used by the adversarial receiver in the OT protocol, but on the other hand, these cannot be revealed in the transcript as they may leak the receiver’s choice bit. To remedy this, we have the receiver
commit
to these values and then have
\widetilde{P}

extract
them in the reduction. As we use only one-way functions for the commitment, this extraction procedure is not efficient. However, it turns out that we can push all of the inefficiency required to
before
the beginning of the ToNC protocol, and have
\widetilde{P}
make use of non-uniformity to perform an inefficient operation before the beginning of the protocol. Thus, our final result is actually that
(\epsilon,\delta)
-ToNC secure against adversaries with non-uniform (potentially quantum) advice implies
(\epsilon,2\delta-\epsilon,0)
(committed-bit) OT. The formal protocol and analysis are given in
Section

5.2
.

2.3
BA amplification

In the remainder of the paper, we prove amplification theorems for BA and OT in the post-quantum setting. That is, we address the setting where adversaries and the communicating parties are quantum (rather than classical) polynomial-time machines. Due to structural differences between BA and OT, we were compelled to take different amplification routes for each. We begin by describing at a high level how we amplify weak BA into full-fledged key agreement, presented in

The classical setting.

Our starting point is Holenstein’s tight classical amplification theorem
[

]
, which characterizes exactly when weakly-secure BA can be amplified to fully-secure key agreement. We briefly recall the classical approach and then explain the modifications needed in our (post-quantum) setting.

Secure random variables.

Following

secure weak BA, where a single execution produces correlated bits

(X,Y)

One says that

(\epsilon,\delta)

if (i)

and

are unbiased, (ii)
\Pr[X=Y]\geq\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}

such that
\Pr[E\mid X=Y]\geq\delta

That is, conditioned on

An information-theoretic amplification protocol.

Given

\delta>\frac{1-\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}.

It is now left to show that applying such a compilation protocol to multiple independent

Hard-core set lemma.

Fix some arbitrarily small constant
\gamma>0
, and suppose that any adversarial circuit of size
s
given the communication transcript can succeed in guessing the output bit (conditioned on the parties agreeing on the same bit) with probability at most
1-\delta/2
. Then, there exists a set
S
of transcripts whose total mass depends on
\delta,s

\frac{1+\gamma}{2}

Amplification in the (classical) computational security setting.

The amplified protocol works exactly as in the information-theoretic case.

1.

The parties perform
\ell(\lambda)={\rm poly}(\lambda)
independent instances of the weak computationally-secure BA protocol, producing pairs of bits
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i=1,\ldots,\ell}
.

2.

They run the compilation protocol on
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i=1,\ldots,\ell}

k_{A},k_{B}
.

Using the hard-core set lemma, one proves that any potential adversary attempting to break the amplified protocol over weak computationally-secure BA instances, cannot do significantly better than it would on a series of information theoretic
(\epsilon,\delta)
-secure random variables.

Indeed, given a hard-core set
S
, define the event
E
to be "the transcript corresponds to
x\in S
and the parties output the same bit". Conditioned on
E
, the weak BA transcript
z
carries essentially no efficiently-computable information about the shared bit
x=y

(x,y,z)
is indistinguishable from an information-theoretically secure
(\epsilon,\delta_{\sec})
-secure random variable, with
\delta_{\sec}\approx\Pr[E\mid X=Y]
. The proof is completed using an appropriate hybrid argument.

Moving to the quantum setting.

It turns out that given an appropriate post-quantum analogue of the hard-core set lemma, the BA amplification works using an argument similar to that described above. So we will focus on the hard-core set lemma itself. In particular, our goal is to re-prove it in the setting where the adversary can apply a quantum circuit rather than a classical circuit.

The approach laid out by

A hard set for each predictor.

Per-set predictors

Interactive XOR lemma.

The interactive XOR lemma can be motivated by considering the following desired form of OT amplification. Suppose we have a

directly after the
i
’th repetition of the protocol (in which case it would be trivial to show that all guesses are correct with probability at most
\delta^{\lambda}
), but are rather asking that they guess a single bit at the end of all
\lambda
repetitions.

A useful abstraction of this setting can be phrased as follows. Suppose we have a protocol
\Gamma
that takes place between a (potentially quantum) adversary
A
and a classical verifier
V
, who obtains an output
b\in\{0,1\}
at the end of the protocol. Let
\delta
be the maximum advantage that any
A
can obtain in guessing the verifier’s output
b
. Then, if we sequentially repeat the protocol twice, letting
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
denote the two verifier outputs, the maximum advantage that any
A
can obtain in guessing
b_{1}\oplus b_{2}
is
\delta^{2}+{\rm negl}(\lambda)
. Note that in the full statement (
Theorem

6.16
), we allow for different protocols
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
with different advantage bounds
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}
.

In fact, this is precisely the post-quantum analogue of a classical claim due to Halevi and Rabin
[
HR08
, Lemma 3.1]
, which itself is an interactive analogue of Levin’s Isolation Lemma
[
Lev85
]
, as stated in
[
GNW11
]
. Unfortunately, the classical proof makes heavy use of rewinding, which is non-trivial and sometimes impossible in the quantum setting. However, the core rewinding idea
has
been adapted to the quantum setting in a recent work of
[
BQSY24
]
, though we note that their theorems do not directly apply to our setting as they are stated for three-message parallel-repeated quantum-communication protocols. We proceed to give an overview of our proof technique, which is somewhat related to
[
BQSY24
]
, but ultimately different and self-contained.

Proof technique.

For convenience, let’s move to the
\{+1,-1\}
basis for classical bits, and suppose that the adversary’s output is
b_{A}\in\{+1,-1\}
, while the two verifier outputs are
b_{1},b_{2}\in\{+1,-1\}
. Then, for contradiction, we assume the existence of an adversary
A
such that

\E[b_{A}\cdot b_{1}\cdot b_{2}]>\delta^{2}.

Rearranging, we have that

\E[b_{1}\cdot\E[b_{A}\cdot b_{2}\ \ |\ \ket{\psi}]]>\delta^{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\E\left[b_{1}\cdot\E\left[\frac{b_{A}\cdot b_{2}}{\delta}\ \ \bigg|\ \ket{\psi}\right]\right]>\delta,

where
\ket{\psi}
is the intermediate state that
A
has between the executions of
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
. By security of
\Gamma_{2}
we know that for any such
\ket{\psi}
, it holds that

-\delta\leq\E[b_{A}\cdot b_{2}\ \ |\ \ket{\psi}]\leq\delta.

This suggests the following strategy for breaking security of the
first
protocol:

1.

Run the first stage of
A
until it finishes interacting with
\Gamma_{1}
and produces state
\ket{\psi}
.

2.

Use
\ket{\psi}
to obtain a real number
\alpha\in[-\delta,\delta]
whose expectation is distributed identically to
\E[b_{A}\cdot b_{2}]
.

3.

Output
b_{A}\in\{1,-1\}
sampled as a Bernoulli random variable which takes the value
1
with probability
\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\alpha}{\delta}+1)
.

The only step that is potentially problematic in the quantum setting is (2). Indeed, in the classical setting, this is accomplished by repeatedly simulating executions with
\Gamma_{2}
and estimating the probability of success. Note that it does
not
suffice to run a single execution, as this would output a value in
\{+1,-1\}
which has the correct expectation but is not within the range
[-\delta,\delta]
required to get step (3) to work.

Fortunately, Marriott-Watrous
[
MW04
]
style rewinding applied to
\ket{\psi}
allows us to implement step (2). Indeed, alternating the projector
\Pi_{0}
corresponding to initializing the verifier for
\Gamma_{2}
and the projector
\Pi_{1}
corresponding to running the interaction and checking whether
b_{A}=b_{2}
allows us to (approximately) sample from a distribution with the correct expectation
and
the correct support. However, to prove that the distribution is supported on
[-\delta,\delta]
, we must actually assume that
\Gamma_{2}
is secure against adversaries with non-uniform
quantum
advice. In particular, this guarantees that the eigenvalues of
\Pi_{0}\Pi_{1}\Pi_{0}
all fall in the range
[-\delta,\delta]
. Indeed, Marriott-Watrous (approximately) samples from a distribution supported only on eigenvalues, and thus the output
\alpha
will be in
[-\delta,\delta]
(with high probability).

Completing OT amplification.

Recursively applying the interactive XOR lemma allows us to prove our motivating example, that
(1,\delta,0)
-OT implies
(1,0,0)
-OT for any
\delta<1
.
12
12

12

In fact this also establishes that
(1,0,\delta)
-OT implies
(1,0,0)
-OT by first applying a standard OT reversal.
While this result is already of inherent interest, it does not suffice to complete our desired implications from ToNC. In particular, if the ToNC does not have completeness 1 (e.g. the compiled CHSH game), then we obtain some
(\epsilon,\delta,0)
for both
\epsilon<1
and
\delta<1
.

To boost correctness of
(\epsilon,\delta,0)
-OT, we adapt an approach of
[
Wul07
]
establishing that for any
\delta<\epsilon^{2}
,
classical semi-honest

(\epsilon,\delta,0)
-OT implies
classical semi-honest

(1,\delta^{\prime},0)
-OT for some
\delta^{\prime}<1
. The approach involves two steps:

1.

Boost security by applying a sequential XOR lemma (which also brings correctness down with it). We already have the tool for analyzing this.

2.

Boost correctness by repeating the protocol several times with the
same
input bits from each party.

In step (2), it is imperative for security that the receiver indeed uses the same choice bit
b
in each execution. While this holds by definition in the classical semi-honest model, we have to somehow enforce that our quantum malicious receiver exhibits this behavior. To do so, we rely on another layer of cut-and-choose, utilizing the fact that our weak OT protocol is in fact a “committed-bit” OT. This means that after the protocol has completed, the receiver can
open
the interaction to its choice bit
b
, convincing the sender that it indeed used this
b
during the interaction. This allows us to implement step (2) securely, and we thus establish that
(\epsilon,\delta,0)
committed-bit OT implies
(1,1/2,0)
-OT for any
\delta<\epsilon^{2}
.

Preliminaries

Let
\lambda
be the security parameter. By default, we consider non-uniform families of quantum adversaries, but the
type
of non-uniformity will vary. In particular, we will sometimes consider adversaries with non-uniform
classical
advice, given by
\{Q_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, and sometimes consider adversaries with non-uniform
quantum
advice, given by
\{\rho_{\lambda},Q_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. In both cases, we will often suppress the parameterization over

.

Definition 3.1

(Sub-distribution measure)
.

Let
\Omega
be a finite set. A
sub-distribution
(equivalently, a
sub-probability measure
) on
\Omega

is a function
M:\Omega\to[0,1]
such that its
total mass

\mu(M)\;:=\;\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}M(\omega)

satisfies
\mu(M)\leq 1
. If
\mu(M)>0
, we write
\omega\leftarrow M
to denote sampling from the normalized
distribution
M/\mu(M)
, i.e.,

\Pr[\omega=\omega_{0}]\;=\;\frac{M(\omega_{0})}{\mu(M)}\qquad(\omega_{0}\in\Omega).

Given a distribution
D
on
\Omega
, we write
M\preceq D
if
M(\omega)\leq D(\omega)
for all
\omega\in\Omega
.

Theorem 3.2

(von Neumann’s minimax theorem
[
vN28
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a finite set, let
Y\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a nonempty compact convex set,
and let
u:\mathcal{A}\times Y\to{\mathbb{R}}
be such that
u(a,\cdot)
is affine
on
Y
for every
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
Write
\Delta(\mathcal{A})
for the simplex of distributions over
\mathcal{A}
, and define

\bar{u}(p,y):=\E_{a\sim p}[u(a,y)]
.
Then

\min_{y\in Y}\ \max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}u(a,y)\;=\;\max_{p\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})}\ \min_{y\in Y}\bar{u}(p,y).

In particular, there exists
p^{\star}\in\Delta(\mathcal{A})
such that for all
y\in Y
,

\bar{u}(p^{\star},y)\ \geq\ \min_{y^{\prime}\in Y}\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}u(a,y^{\prime})
.

Definition 3.3

(Inefficiently-extractable commitment)
.

An inefficiently-extractable commitment between a classical committer and classical receiver consists of an interaction

\mathsf{st}_{\mathsf{Com}},\tau\leftarrow\langle\mathsf{Com}(1^{\lambda},b),\mathsf{Rec}(1^{\lambda})\rangle,

It should satisfy the following properties.

Correctness

b\in\{0,1\}
,

\Pr\left[\mathsf{Ver}(\mathsf{st}_{\mathsf{Com}},\tau,b)=\top:\begin{array}[]{r}\mathsf{st}_{\mathsf{Com}},\tau\leftarrow\langle\mathsf{Com}(1^{\lambda},b),\mathsf{Rec}(1^{\lambda})\rangle\end{array}\right]=1-{\rm negl}(\lambda).

•

Hiding
: For any QPT adversarial receiver
\rho,\widetilde{\mathsf{Rec}}
outputting a bit
\widetilde{b}
,

\displaystyle\bigg|\Pr\left[\widetilde{b}=0:\widetilde{b}\leftarrow\langle\mathsf{Com}(1^{\lambda},0),\widetilde{\mathsf{Rec}}(\rho)\rangle\right]-\Pr\left[\widetilde{b}=0:\widetilde{b}\leftarrow\langle\mathsf{Com}(1^{\lambda},1),\widetilde{\mathsf{Rec}}(\rho)\rangle\right]\bigg|={\rm negl}(\lambda).

•

Binding:
There exists a (potentially inefficient) function
\mathsf{Ext}
such that for any unbounded adversarial committer
\widetilde{\mathsf{Com}}
,

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\mathsf{Ver}(\widetilde{\mathsf{st}}_{\mathsf{Com}},\tau,1-b)=\top:\begin{array}[]{r}\widetilde{\mathsf{st}}_{\mathsf{Com}},\tau\leftarrow\langle\widetilde{\mathsf{Com}},\mathsf{Rec}(1^{\lambda})\rangle\\
b\coloneqq\mathsf{Ext}(\tau)\end{array}\right]={\rm negl}(\lambda).

Definition 3.5

(Bit Agreement)

An
(\epsilon,\delta)
-weak bit agreement (BA) protocol with classical communication consists of an interaction between polynomial-time (potentially quantum) parties
A
and
B
, denoted by

(k_{A},k_{B},\tau)\leftarrow\langle A(1^{\lambda}),B(1^{\lambda})\rangle,

is

Lack of bias.

\Bigg|\Pr_{(k_{A},k_{B},\tau)\leftarrow\langle A(1^{\lambda}),B(1^{\lambda})\rangle}[k_{A}=0]-\frac{1}{2}\Bigg|={\rm negl}(\lambda),\ \ \ \Bigg|\Pr_{(k_{A},k_{B},\tau)\leftarrow\langle A(1^{\lambda}),B(1^{\lambda})\rangle}[k_{B}=0]-\frac{1}{2}\Bigg|={\rm negl}(\lambda),

Correctness.

For any QPT adversary

\Pr_{(k_{A},k_{B},\tau)\leftarrow\langle A(1^{\lambda}),B(1^{\lambda})\rangle}\left[\widetilde{E}(\rho,\tau)=k_{A}\ |\ k_{A}=k_{B}\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}+{\rm negl}(\lambda).

We introduce two definitions of oblivious transfer (OT). First, we give a standard game-based definition, specified as follows.

Definition 3.6

(Oblivious transfer)
.

An
(\epsilon,\delta,\gamma)
-weak oblivious transfer (OT) with classical communication is a protocol that takes place between a polynomial-time (potentially quantum) sender
S
and a polynomial-time (potentially quantum) receiver
R
, denoted by

(b,r),(r_{0},r_{1}),\tau\leftarrow\langle R(1^{\lambda}),S(1^{\lambda})\rangle,

where
(b,r)
is the output of
R
,
(r_{0},r_{1})
is the output of
S
, and
\tau
is the (classical) transcript of interaction produced by the protocol. It should satisfy the following properties.

•

Correctness
: It holds that

\Pr[r=r_{b}:\begin{array}[]{r}(b,r),(r_{0},r_{1}),\tau\leftarrow\langle R(1^{\lambda}),S(1^{\lambda})\rangle\\
\end{array}]\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}-{\rm negl}(\lambda).

•

Receiver security
: For any QPT adversarial sender
\rho,\widetilde{S}
,

\Pr[\widetilde{b}=b:(b,r),\widetilde{b}\leftarrow\langle R(1^{\lambda}),\widetilde{S}(\rho)\rangle]\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2}+{\rm negl}(\lambda).

•

Sender security
: For any QPT adversarial receiver
\rho,\widetilde{R}
,

\Pr[\widetilde{r}=r_{0}\oplus r_{1}:\begin{array}[]{r}\widetilde{r},(r_{0},r_{1})\leftarrow\langle\widetilde{R}(\rho),S(1^{\lambda})\rangle\end{array}]\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}+{\rm negl}(\lambda).

Remark 3.7
.

We refer to
(1,0,0)
-weak OT as “standard OT”, or just “OT”, as it satisfies the standard notions of correctness and security.

Next, we give a specialized variant of OT that we call

Definition 3.8

(Committed-bit OT)
.

An
(\epsilon,\delta,\gamma)
-weak committed-bit OT with classical communication is a protocol that takes place between a polynomial-time (potentially quantum) sender
S
and a polynomial-time (potentially quantum) receiver
R
. The protocol begins with a setup phase, denoted by

\mathsf{init}_{R},\tau_{\mathsf{Setup}}\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}\langle R(1^{\lambda}),S(1^{\lambda})\rangle,

where
\mathsf{init}_{R}
is
R
’s private state and
\tau_{\mathsf{Setup}}
is the public (classical) transcript of interaction. Next, for any
b\in\{0,1\}
, the parties run

(r,\mathsf{st}_{R}),(r_{0},r_{1},\mathsf{st}_{S}),\tau\leftarrow\mathsf{OT}\langle R(b,\mathsf{init}_{R}),S(\tau_{\mathsf{Setup}})\rangle.

In addition, there exists a (potentially inefficient) function
\mathsf{TrapGen}
, a polynomial-time function
\mathsf{Ext}
, and a verification protocol

\{\top,\bot\}\leftarrow\mathsf{Ver}\langle R(\mathsf{st}_{R}),S(b,\mathsf{st}_{S})\rangle,

such that the following properties hold.

•

Correctness
: For any
b\in\{0,1\}
,

\Pr[r=r_{b}:\begin{array}[]{r}\mathsf{init}_{R},\tau_{\mathsf{Setup}}\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}\langle R(1^{\lambda}),S(1^{\lambda})\rangle\\
(r,\mathsf{st}_{R}),(r_{0},r_{1},\mathsf{st}_{S}),\tau\leftarrow\mathsf{OT}\langle R(b,\mathsf{init}_{R}),S(\tau_{\mathsf{Setup}})\rangle\end{array}]\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}-{\rm negl}(\lambda).

•

Completeness
: For any
b\in\{0,1\}

\Pr\left[\mathsf{Ver}\langle R(\mathsf{st}_{R}),S(b,\mathsf{st}_{S})\rangle=\top:\begin{array}[]{r}\mathsf{init}_{R},\tau_{\mathsf{Setup}}\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}\langle R(1^{\lambda}),S(1^{\lambda})\rangle\\

•

Receiver security
: For any QPT adversarial sender
\rho,\widetilde{S}
,

\displaystyle\Bigg|\Pr[\widetilde{b}=0:\begin{array}[]{r}\mathsf{init}_{R},\mathsf{init}_{S},\tau_{\mathsf{Setup}}\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}\langle R(1^{\lambda}),\widetilde{S}(\rho)\rangle\\
(r,\mathsf{st}_{R}),(\widetilde{b},\mathsf{st}_{S}),\tau\leftarrow\mathsf{OT}\langle R(0,\mathsf{init}_{R}),\widetilde{S}(\mathsf{init}_{S})\rangle\end{array}]

\displaystyle-\Pr[\widetilde{b}=0:\begin{array}[]{r}\mathsf{init}_{R},\mathsf{init}_{S},\tau_{\mathsf{Setup}}\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}\langle R(1^{\lambda}),\widetilde{S}(\rho)\rangle\\
(r,\mathsf{st}_{R}),(\widetilde{b},\mathsf{st}_{S}),\tau\leftarrow\mathsf{OT}\langle R(1,\mathsf{init}_{R}),\widetilde{S}(\mathsf{init}_{S})\rangle\end{array}]\Bigg|\leq\gamma+{{\rm negl}(\lambda)}.

•

Sender security
: For any unbounded algorithm
\widetilde{R}_{\mathsf{init}}
, with probability
1-{\rm negl}(\lambda)
over

\mathsf{init}_{R},\tau_{\mathsf{Setup}}\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}\langle\widetilde{R}_{\mathsf{init}},S(1^{\lambda})\rangle,\quad\mathsf{td}\coloneqq\mathsf{TrapGen}(\tau_{\mathsf{Setup}}),

it holds that for any QPT adversarial receiver
\widetilde{R}
,

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\widetilde{R}(\mathsf{st}_{R},r_{b})=r_{1-b}:\begin{array}[]{r}\mathsf{st}_{R},(r_{0},r_{1},\mathsf{st}_{S}),\tau\leftarrow\mathsf{OT}\langle\widetilde{R}(\mathsf{init}_{R}),S(\tau_{\mathsf{Setup}})\rangle\\
b\coloneqq\mathsf{Ext}(\mathsf{td},\tau)\end{array}\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}+{\rm negl}(\lambda),

and,

\Pr\left[\mathsf{Ver}\langle\widetilde{R}(\mathsf{st}_{R}),S(1-b,\mathsf{st}_{S})\rangle=\top:\begin{array}[]{r}\mathsf{st}_{R},(r_{0},r_{1},\mathsf{st}_{S}),\tau\leftarrow\mathsf{OT}\langle\widetilde{R}(\mathsf{init}_{R}),S(\tau_{\mathsf{Setup}})\rangle\\
b\coloneqq\mathsf{Ext}(\mathsf{td},\tau)\end{array}\right]={\rm negl}(\lambda).

.

We note that any

-weak committed-bit OT implies

-weak OT secure against adversaries with non-uniform quantum advice. Correctness and receiver security are immediate, while sender security follows due to the fact that if

Jordan decomposition.

By Jordan’s lemma applied to
(\Pi_{A},\Pi_{B})
,
\mathcal{H}
decomposes orthogonally as

\mathcal{H}\;=\;\bigoplus_{j}S_{j},

where each
S_{j}
has dimension at most
2
, and is preserved by both projections. For a
2
-dimensional block
S_{j}
choose
orthonormal pairs
\{\ket{v^{A}_{j,1}},\ket{v^{A}_{j,0}}\}
and

\{\ket{v^{B}_{j,1}},\ket{v^{B}_{j,0}}\}
such that

\ket{v^{A}_{j,1}}\in\mathrm{im}(\Pi_{A})
,
\ket{v^{A}_{j,0}}\in\ker(\Pi_{A})
and similarly for
B
.
Define the overlap parameter

p_{j}:=\bigl|\mbox{$\Braket{v^{A}_{j,1}|v^{B}_{j,1}}$}\bigr|^{2}\in[0,1].

By convention, phases may be fixed so that within
S_{j}
, the bases are related by a planar rotation:

\ket{v^{A}_{j,1}}=\sqrt{p_{j}}\,\ket{v^{B}_{j,1}}+\sqrt{1-p_{j}}\,\ket{v^{B}_{j,0}},\qquad\ket{v^{B}_{j,1}}=\sqrt{p_{j}}\,\ket{v^{A}_{j,1}}+\sqrt{1-p_{j}}\,\ket{v^{A}_{j,0}}.

Corollary 3.10
.

In the Jordan decomposition notation above, fix a
2
-dimensional block
S_{j}
and let

p_{j}:=|\langle v^{A}_{j,1}\mid v^{B}_{j,1}\rangle|^{2}
, where the bases are chosen so that

|v^{A}_{j,1}\rangle\;=\;\sqrt{p_{j}}\,|v^{B}_{j,1}\rangle+\sqrt{1-p_{j}}\,|v^{B}_{j,0}\rangle.

(1)

Then

(\Pi_{A}\Pi_{B}\Pi_{A})\,|v^{A}_{j,1}\rangle\;=\;p_{j}\,|v^{A}_{j,1}\rangle.

In particular, each Jordan parameter
p_{j}
is an eigenvalue of the restriction

\left.\Pi_{A}\Pi_{B}\Pi_{A}\right|_{\mathrm{im}(\Pi_{A})}
. Consequently, if

\mathrm{spec}\!\left(\left.\Pi_{A}\Pi_{B}\Pi_{A}\right|_{\mathrm{im}(\Pi_{A})}\right)\subseteq[a,b],

then every Jordan parameter
p_{j}
lies in
[a,b]
.

Proof.

Fix
j
and abbreviate
|v^{A}\rangle:=|v^{A}_{j,1}\rangle
and
|v^{B}\rangle:=|v^{B}_{j,1}\rangle
.
Since
|v^{A}\rangle\in\mathrm{im}(\Pi_{A})
we have
\Pi_{A}|v^{A}\rangle=|v^{A}\rangle
.
Using the rotation relation (
1
) and that
\Pi_{B}|v^{B}_{j,1}\rangle=|v^{B}_{j,1}\rangle
and

\Pi_{B}|v^{B}_{j,0}\rangle=0
, we get

\Pi_{B}|v^{A}\rangle\;=\;\sqrt{p_{j}}\,|v^{B}\rangle.

Applying
\Pi_{A}
again and using the second rotation relation

|v^{B}\rangle=\sqrt{p_{j}}\,|v^{A}\rangle+\sqrt{1-p_{j}}\,|v^{A}_{j,0}\rangle
together with

\Pi_{A}|v^{A}_{j,0}\rangle=0
, we obtain

\Pi_{A}|v^{B}\rangle\;=\;\sqrt{p_{j}}\,|v^{A}\rangle.

Combining,

\Pi_{A}\Pi_{B}\Pi_{A}|v^{A}\rangle\;=\;\Pi_{A}\Pi_{B}|v^{A}\rangle\;=\;\sqrt{p_{j}}\,\Pi_{A}|v^{B}\rangle\;=\;p_{j}\,|v^{A}\rangle,

as claimed. The final implication follows since
p_{j}
is an eigenvalue of

\left.\Pi_{A}\Pi_{B}\Pi_{A}\right|_{\mathrm{im}(\Pi_{A})}
.
∎

Definition 4.1

(Test of Non-Commutation)

An

is applied in the case that the first stage outputs

•

Completeness
: It holds that
13
13

13

In an abuse of notation, we write

with projective measurement

\Pr\left[V_{\mathsf{Ver}}(\mathsf{st},a)=1:\begin{array}[]{r}\ket{\psi},(c,\mathsf{st})\leftarrow\langle P_{\mathsf{Prep}}(1^{\lambda}),V_{\mathsf{Prep}}(1^{\lambda})\rangle\\

•

Soundness

Definition 4.2

(Normal form test of non-commutation)
.

We say that the test of non-commutation (

•

\mathsf{st}=a^{*}\in\{0,1\}

That is, there is always exactly one answer bit that the verifier accepts.

Remark 4.3
.

In any normal-form ToNC, both
\beta,\alpha\geq 1/2
since there always exists a trivial strategy which outputs a uniformly random bit. Thus, it is often convenient to write the parameters of a normal-form ToNC using the
advantage
of the prover as opposed to the raw probability of success. That is, we will often refer to a normal form

-ToNC where

-ToNC, where

, as justified in our discussion in

1.1

Next, we specify a stronger completeness guarantee stating that for
all
preambles, the (honest) prover’s advantage is at least
\epsilon

\epsilon=2\cos^{2}(\pi/8)-1

Definition 4.4

(Test of non-commutation with robust completeness)
.

We say that a (normal form)

-ToNC has

if with probability 1 over
\ket{\psi},(c,\mathsf{st})\leftarrow\langle P_{\mathsf{Prep}}(1^{\lambda}),V_{\mathsf{Prep}}(1^{\lambda})\rangle,

\Pr\left[V_{\mathsf{Ver}}(\mathsf{st},a)=1:a\leftarrow P_{c}(\ket{\psi})\right]=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

4.2
Normal form compiler

In this subsection, we present a procedure that transforms a less structured form of ToNC into a normal-form ToNC as in Definition
4.1
. This transformation incurs an inverse-polynomial parameter loss. Whereas in the final round of a normal-form ToNC the verifier accepts exactly one of the two possible prover answers, in the less structured form, the verifier may alternatively accept both or neither. Since our constructions of oblivious transfer and key
agreement use normal-form ToNCs, the compilation procedure below shows that the
same implications hold for a broader class of ToNC protocols. Moreover, this compilation procedure suggests that normal-form ToNC is not merely a convenient technical restriction, but a natural abstraction: it captures, up to bounded parameter loss, a broad class of ToNC protocols.

Theorem 4.5

(Normal form compiler)
.

Let
I
be two-stage protocol satisfying Definition
4.1
, except that the second-stage predicate

may (for some values of
\mathsf{st}

or accept neither. Then for any inverse-polynomial function
\varepsilon(\lambda)

\beta^{\prime}-\alpha^{\prime}\;\geq\;\beta-\alpha-O(\varepsilon).

Remark 4.6

As we explain in the proof below, the verifier in the constructed normal-form test

and proceeds as follows:

•

, it answers using

.

•

, it answers using

applied to the (classical) transcript

.

can execute a

:
sample

; measure using

to obtain

; compute

(where

.

Conditioned on

(probability

),

behaves like

on the final round, and thus has advantage at least

. Conditioned on

(probability

),

has advantage

\frac{\epsilon+\gamma}{2}\leq\delta\quad\Rightarrow\quad\gamma\leq 2\delta-\epsilon.

Since
\Pr[k_{A}=k_{B}]\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}
, we have

\displaystyle 2\Pr[\widetilde{E}(\tau)=k_{A}\mid k_{A}=k_{B}]-1

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\frac{\Pr[\widetilde{E}(\tau)=k_{A}]}{\Pr[k_{A}=k_{B}]}\right)-1

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\frac{\frac{1}{2}+\delta-\frac{\epsilon}{2}}{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}}\right)-1

\displaystyle=\frac{2+4\delta-2\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}-1

\displaystyle=\frac{1+4\delta-3\epsilon}{1+\epsilon},

which completes the proof.

Now, in the case that the ToNC satisfies robust completeness, we have that the event

is

, and thus

\displaystyle 2\Pr[\widetilde{E}(\tau)=k_{A}\mid k_{A}=k_{B}]-1=2\Pr[\widetilde{E}(\tau)=k_{A}]-1=\gamma\leq 2\delta-\epsilon.

The measure vs. set discrepancy comes from the fact that the proof in

Definition.

F(\tau)=\E[\Delta_{X}w(X)]
.

Using the concrete choice
\alpha=\xi\varepsilon/800
and
\varepsilon\leq 1
without loss of generality, we have
\alpha\leq\xi/800
, and hence

\frac{\xi}{8\tau}+\frac{\alpha}{2\tau}\ \leq\ \frac{\xi}{8\tau}+\frac{\xi}{1600\tau}\ <\ \frac{\xi}{2\tau}.

(20)

Therefore,

\Pr_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\bigl[Q(x)=P(x)\bigr]\geq 1-\frac{\delta_{\tau}}{2}+\frac{F(\tau)-\xi}{2\tau}-\nu.

Finally, by Step 4 we have
0\leq\delta_{\tau}-\delta\leq\varepsilon/200
except with probability
{\rm negl}(\lambda)
,
and by Step 3 we have
F(\tau)\geq\varepsilon/150
and
\tau\leq 1+\xi
.
Since
\xi=\varepsilon^{2}/1000
, for all sufficiently large
\lambda
,

\frac{F(\tau)-\xi}{2\tau}\ \geq\ \frac{\varepsilon/150-\varepsilon^{2}/1000}{2(1+\xi)}\ \geq\ \frac{\varepsilon}{400}\ \geq\ \frac{\delta_{\tau}-\delta}{2}.

Plugging this in gives

\Pr_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\bigl[Q(x)=P(x)\bigr]\geq 1-\frac{\delta_{\tau}}{2}+\frac{\delta_{\tau}-\delta}{2}-\nu=1-\frac{\delta}{2}-\nu,

which completes the predictability claim.

Obliviousness.

The obliviousness property stems directly from the definition of
Q
.

Efficiency.

All estimation steps use
{\rm poly}(\lambda,1/\varepsilon)
oracle calls to

\mathcal{C}
, and the grid has
{\rm poly}(\lambda,1/\varepsilon)
points.
Thus
\mathsf{Gen}
runs in
{\rm poly}(\lambda,1/\varepsilon)
time. By inspection,
S
and
Q
are obtained by composing a
{\rm poly}(\lambda,1/\varepsilon)
-number
of invocations of
\mathcal{C}
arising only from the estimation subroutines, with

{\rm poly}(\lambda,1/\varepsilon)
additional non-oracle computation. Hence there exist fixed
polynomials
q(\cdot,\cdot)
and
r(\cdot,\cdot)
such that
S
and
Q
make at most

q(\lambda,1/\varepsilon)
oracle calls to
\mathcal{C}
and have non-oracle size at most

r(\lambda,1/\varepsilon)
. Therefore, the compiled circuits satisfy

|\widehat{Q}|\leq q(\lambda,1/\varepsilon)\cdot|\mathcal{C}|+r(\lambda,1/\varepsilon)
, and
similarly for
\widehat{S}
.

∎

Fix
\lambda

\lambda

Define a deterministic predicate.

A sampler for

\bigl((x,b_{0}),b_{0}\bigr)
.

Build an oracle circuit for Theorem
6.1
.

Given the predictor
C:{\cal X}\to\{0,1\}

\mathcal{C}:{\cal X}^{\prime}\times\{0,1\}\to\{0,1\}

.

Invoke

with the accuracy parameter set to

, obtaining

\xi,\tau
, circuits

As in Theorem

,

p_{x^{\prime}}(b):=\Pr[\mathcal{C}(x^{\prime},b)=1],\qquad\Delta_{x^{\prime}}:=p_{x^{\prime}}(P(x^{\prime}))-p_{x^{\prime}}(1-P(x^{\prime})).

For

Then, the total mass of

(23)

Lower bound the total mass
\mu(M)
.

We claim

property from Theorem
6.1
, except with negligible probability,

\delta_{\sf out}\ \leq\ \delta_{\tau}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|\delta_{\sf out}-\delta_{\tau}|\leq\varepsilon/200.

By the

\Pr_{X^{\prime}\sim{\cal D}}\bigl[Q(X^{\prime})=P(X^{\prime})\bigr]\ \geq\ 1-\frac{\delta_{\sf out}}{2}-{\rm negl}(\lambda)\ >\ 1-\frac{\delta}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}-{\rm negl}(\lambda).

Define

We claim that for every

Indeed, by

.

Since

and
(x,1)
.
Therefore the output distribution of

, establishing

\Pr_{(X,B)\sim D}\bigl[Q((X,B))=B\bigr]\;=\;\Pr_{(X,B)\sim D}\bigl[Q((X,0))=B\bigr]\;=\;\Pr_{(X,B)\sim D}\bigl[\widetilde{Q}(X)=B\bigr].

Consequently,

\Pr_{(X,B)\sim D}\bigl[\widetilde{Q}(X)=B\bigr]\;=\;\Pr_{X^{\prime}\sim{\cal D}}\bigl[Q(X^{\prime})=P(X^{\prime})\bigr]\ >\ 1-\frac{\delta}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}-{\rm negl}(\lambda).

Size of the compiled predictor.

We now argue that

Recall that

is produced by invoking Theorem

By Theorem

|\widehat{Q}|\ \leq\ q(\lambda,1/\varepsilon)\cdot|\mathcal{C}|\;+\;r(\lambda,1/\varepsilon).

In our construction,

and compares it to

, and hence

|\mathcal{C}|\ \leq\ |C|+n.

Therefore, the size condition (
21
) implies
|\widehat{Q}|\leq s(\lambda)
. This contradicts the assumed hardness of

against size-

circuits. Therefore
\delta_{\sf out}\geq\delta-\epsilon
, and since

\mu(M)=\delta_{\tau}\ \geq\ \delta-\epsilon.

(25)

An upper bound on the success of
C
on

.

We analyze
C
under the normalized distribution

\displaystyle\Pr_{(X,B)\leftarrow M}\bigl[C(X)=B\bigr]=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\E_{(X,B)\leftarrow M}\bigl[\Delta_{(X,B)}\bigr]=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2\delta_{\tau}}\cdot\E_{(X,B)\sim D}\bigl[\Delta_{(X,B)}\,w_{\tau}(X,B)\bigr].

(26)

) and (

F(\tau):=\E_{X^{\prime}\sim{\cal D}}[\Delta_{X^{\prime}}\,w_{\tau}(X^{\prime})]

P^{\prime}(x^{\prime}):=P(x^{\prime})\oplus S(x^{\prime},P(x^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{C}(x^{\prime},P(x^{\prime}))=1\mid x^{\prime}]-\Pr[\mathcal{C}(x^{\prime},P^{\prime}(x^{\prime}))=1\mid x^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pr[P^{\prime}(x^{\prime})=1-P(x^{\prime})]\cdot\left(\Pr[\mathcal{C}(x^{\prime},P(x^{\prime}))=1\mid x^{\prime}]-\Pr[\mathcal{C}(x^{\prime},1-P(x^{\prime}))=1\mid x^{\prime}]\right)

\displaystyle\Pr[S(x^{\prime},P(x^{\prime}))=1\mid x^{\prime}]\cdot\Delta_{x^{\prime}}.

Averaging over

A:=\left|\E_{X^{\prime}\sim{\cal D}}\Bigl[\Delta_{X^{\prime}}\cdot\Pr[S(X^{\prime},P(X^{\prime}))=1\mid X^{\prime}]\Bigr]\right|\ \leq\ \varepsilon.

Combining with (

|F(\tau)|\ =\ |\E_{X^{\prime}\sim{\cal D}}[\Delta_{X^{\prime}}\,w_{\tau}(X^{\prime})]|\ \leq\ A+\varepsilon/200+{\rm negl}(\lambda)\ \leq\ \varepsilon+\varepsilon/200+{\rm negl}(\lambda)\ \leq\ 2\varepsilon

for all sufficiently large
\lambda
. From (

) we have the identity

\Pr_{(X,B)\leftarrow M}\bigl[C(X)=B\bigr]=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2\delta_{\tau}}\cdot F(\tau),

Because

\Pr_{(X,B)\leftarrow M}\bigl[C(X)=B\bigr]\ \leq\ \frac{1}{2}+\frac{|F(\tau)|}{2\delta_{\tau}}\ \leq\ \frac{1}{2}+\frac{2\varepsilon}{2(\delta-\epsilon)}\ =\ \frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta\epsilon}{\delta-\epsilon}\ \leq\ \frac{1}{2}+2\epsilon.

Here, the second inequality relies on (

In the next proof, we make use of the following lemma.

Lemma 6.4

(
[
BSW03
]
)
.

Let
D
be a distribution on
\{0,1\}^{n}
,
F
be a set of functions
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to[-1,1]
, and
\overline{f}
be a convex combination of functions from
F
. Then for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and for some
k\leq n/2\epsilon^{2}
, there exist functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}
such that

\max_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\Bigg|\overline{f}(x)-\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}(x)\right)\Bigg|\leq\epsilon.

Remark 6.5
.

We note that Lemma
6.1
does not guarantee that the functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}
can be computed efficiently given
\overline{f}
. Therefore, the following theorems we prove in this section, which all rely on Lemma
6.1
, are stated in the non-uniform security model.

Lemma 6.6

(Minimax fixing lemma)
.

Let
\{D_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a family of distributions over

\{0,1\}^{n(\lambda)}\times\{0,1\}
.
Fix functions
\alpha(\lambda)\in(0,1]
and
\gamma(\lambda)\in(0,1)
, and a size bound

t(\lambda)
.

Assume that for all sufficiently large
\lambda
, for every measure

M_{\lambda}\preceq D_{\lambda}
with
\mu(M_{\lambda})\geq\alpha(\lambda)
, there exists a
quantum circuit
Q^{\prime}_{\lambda}
of size at most
t(\lambda)
such that

\Pr_{(X,B)\leftarrow M_{\lambda}}\bigl[\,Q^{\prime}(X)=B\,\bigr]\ >\ \frac{1}{2}+\gamma(\lambda).

Define

t^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\ :=\ \Bigl(\frac{n(\lambda)}{\gamma(\lambda)^{2}}+1\Bigr)\cdot t(\lambda)\;+\;\log\!\Bigl(\frac{n(\lambda)+1}{\gamma(\lambda)^{2}}\Bigr).

Then for all sufficiently large
\lambda
, there exists a quantum circuit

Q^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}
of size at most
t^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)
such that for every measure

M_{\lambda}\preceq D_{\lambda}
with
\mu(M_{\lambda})\geq\alpha(\lambda)
,

\Pr_{(X,B)\leftarrow M_{\lambda}}\bigl[\,Q^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}(X)=B\,\bigr]\ >\ \frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma(\lambda)}{2}.

Proof.

Fix
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
and suppress it from the notation.

Mass-normalization.

Throughout this proof, the notation
(X,B)\leftarrow M
denotes sampling from the

normalized
distribution
M/\mu(M)
, so
\E_{(x,b)\leftarrow M}[\cdot]
is not linear in
M
.
To apply minimax, we may w.l.o.g. restrict Bob to measures of
exact
mass
\alpha
.
Indeed, for any measure
M\preceq D
with
\mu(M)\geq\alpha
, define the scaled measure

\widetilde{M}\ :=\ \frac{\alpha}{\mu(M)}\cdot M.

Then
\widetilde{M}\preceq D
,
\mu(\widetilde{M})=\alpha
, and the normalized distributions coincide:

\frac{\widetilde{M}}{\mu(\widetilde{M})}\ =\ \frac{M}{\mu(M)}.

Hence, for every circuit
Q
,

\Pr_{(X,B)\leftarrow M}[Q(X)=B]\ =\ \Pr_{(X,B)\leftarrow\widetilde{M}}[Q(X)=B].

(28)

Therefore, the lemma’s assumption and desired conclusion are unchanged if we quantify Bob only over

R_{\alpha}\ :=\ \{\,M\preceq D\mid\mu(M)=\alpha\,\}.

Note that
R_{\alpha}
is nonempty (since
\alpha D\in R_{\alpha}
), convex, and compact when identified
with a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{\{0,1\}^{n}\times\{0,1\}}
.

The game.

For any circuit
Q^{\prime}
, define the function
g_{Q^{\prime}}:\{0,1\}^{n}\times\{0,1\}\to[-1,1]
by

g_{Q^{\prime}}(x,b)\ :=\ 2\Pr[Q^{\prime}(x)=b]-1\ =\ \Pr[Q^{\prime}(x)=b]-\Pr[Q^{\prime}(x)=1-b].

Then for any measure
M
,

\E_{(x,b)\leftarrow M}[g_{Q^{\prime}}(x,b)]\ =\ 2\Pr_{(x,b)\leftarrow M}[Q^{\prime}(x)=b]-1.

Consider the following zero-sum game between Alice and Bob.
Alice chooses a circuit
Q^{\prime}
of size at most
t
;
Bob chooses a measure
M\in R_{\alpha}
;
and Alice’s payoff is

\Pi(Q^{\prime},M)\ :=\ \frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{x,b}M(x,b)\cdot g_{Q^{\prime}}(x,b)\qquad\text{for }M\in R_{\alpha}.

For
M\in R_{\alpha}
we have
\Pi(Q^{\prime},M)=\E_{(x,b)\leftarrow M}[g_{Q^{\prime}}(x,b)]
, and
\Pi(Q^{\prime},\cdot)
is
linear in
M
on
R_{\alpha}
. By the lemma assumption, for every
M\in R_{\alpha}
there exists a circuit
Q^{\prime}
of size at most
t

such that

\Pr_{(X,B)\leftarrow M}[Q^{\prime}(X)=B]\ >\ \frac{1}{2}+\gamma,

and hence

\Pi(Q^{\prime},M)\ =\ 2\Pr_{(X,B)\leftarrow M}[Q^{\prime}(X)=B]-1\ >\ 2\gamma.

Equivalently,

\min_{M\in R_{\alpha}}\ \max_{Q^{\prime}:\,|Q^{\prime}|\leq t}\ \Pi(Q^{\prime},M)\ >\ 2\gamma.

Minimax.

Apply von Neumann’s minimax theorem with Alice’s finite action set being the set of size-
t
circuits (with a finite universal gate set),
Bob’s action set
Y=R_{\alpha}
(compact convex), and payoff
u(Q^{\prime},M)=\Pi(Q^{\prime},M)

(affine in
M
).
We obtain a mixed strategy for Alice, i.e. a distribution over size-
t
circuits which we denote

\overline{Q}
, such that for all
M\in R_{\alpha}
,

\E_{Q^{\prime}\sim\overline{Q}}\bigl[\Pi(Q^{\prime},M)\bigr]\ >\ 2\gamma.

Define the (pointwise) averaged payoff function

g_{\overline{Q}}(x,b)\ :=\ \E_{Q^{\prime}\sim\overline{Q}}\bigl[g_{Q^{\prime}}(x,b)\bigr].

By linearity,

\E_{Q^{\prime}\sim\overline{Q}}\bigl[\Pi(Q^{\prime},M)\bigr]\;=\;\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{x,b}M(x,b)\,g_{\overline{Q}}(x,b)\;=\;\E_{(x,b)\leftarrow M}\bigl[g_{\overline{Q}}(x,b)\bigr],

so we have for every
M\in R_{\alpha}
,

\E_{(x,b)\leftarrow M}\bigl[g_{\overline{Q}}(x,b)\bigr]\ >\ 2\gamma.

(29)

Sparsifying the mixed strategy.

Note that
g_{Q^{\prime}}(x,1)=-g_{Q^{\prime}}(x,0)
for every
x
, since
\Pr[Q^{\prime}(x)=0]+\Pr[Q^{\prime}(x)=1]=1
.
Define the one-argument function
h_{Q^{\prime}}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to[-1,1]
by

h_{Q^{\prime}}(x)\ :=\ g_{Q^{\prime}}(x,0),

so that for all
(x,b)
,

g_{Q^{\prime}}(x,b)\ =\ (1-2b)\,h_{Q^{\prime}}(x).

Define
h_{\overline{Q}}(x):=\E_{Q^{\prime}\sim\overline{Q}}[h_{Q^{\prime}}(x)]
; then

g_{\overline{Q}}(x,b)=(1-2b)\,h_{\overline{Q}}(x)
.

Now apply Lemma
6.1
to the convex combination
h_{\overline{Q}}

over the domain
\{0,1\}^{n}
:
there exist circuits
Q^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,Q^{\prime}_{k}
of size at most
t
with

k\ \leq\ \left\lceil\frac{n}{\gamma^{2}}\right\rceil

such that

\max_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\left|h_{\overline{Q}}(x)\ -\ \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}h_{Q^{\prime}_{i}}(x)\right|\ \leq\ \gamma.

Multiplying by
(1-2b)\in\{\pm 1\}
shows that, for all
(x,b)
,

\left|g_{\overline{Q}}(x,b)\ -\ \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}g_{Q^{\prime}_{i}}(x,b)\right|\ \leq\ \gamma.

Define
Q^{\prime\prime}
as follows: on input
x
, sample
i\leftarrow[k]
uniformly and output
Q^{\prime}_{i}(x)
.
Then for every
(x,b)
we have

g_{Q^{\prime\prime}}(x,b)\ =\ \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}g_{Q^{\prime}_{i}}(x,b),

and hence for every
M\in R_{\alpha}
,

\E_{(x,b)\leftarrow M}[g_{Q^{\prime\prime}}(x,b)]\ \geq\ \E_{(x,b)\leftarrow M}[g_{\overline{Q}}(x,b)]\ -\ \gamma\ >\ 2\gamma-\gamma\ =\ \gamma,

where the strict inequality uses (
29
).

Extending back to all
\mu(M)\geq\alpha
.

Let

R\ :=\ \{\,M\preceq D\mid\mu(M)\geq\alpha\,\}.

For any
M\in R
, let
\widetilde{M}=\frac{\alpha}{\mu(M)}M\in R_{\alpha}
.
By (
28
), sampling from
M
or
\widetilde{M}
yields the same normalized
distribution, so the same success probability for
Q^{\prime\prime}
.
Therefore, for every
M\in R
,

\Pr_{(x,b)\leftarrow M}[Q^{\prime\prime}(x)=b]\ =\ \frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\,\E_{(x,b)\leftarrow M}[g_{Q^{\prime\prime}}(x,b)]\ >\ \frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2}.

Size bound.

By construction,
Q^{\prime\prime}
can be implemented by wiring in the
k
circuits
Q^{\prime}_{1},\dots,Q^{\prime}_{k}

and using
\log k
bits to sample
i
; thus

|Q^{\prime\prime}|\ \leq\ k\cdot t\ +\ \log k.

With
k\leq\lceil n/\gamma^{2}\rceil
, we have
k\leq n/\gamma^{2}+1
, and since
\gamma\in(0,1)
,

\log k\ \leq\ \log\!\Bigl(\frac{n}{\gamma^{2}}+1\Bigr)\ \leq\ \log\!\Bigl(\frac{n+1}{\gamma^{2}}\Bigr).

Hence

|Q^{\prime\prime}|\ \leq\ \Bigl(\frac{n}{\gamma^{2}}+1\Bigr)\cdot t\ +\ \log\!\Bigl(\frac{n+1}{\gamma^{2}}\Bigr)\ =\ t^{\prime\prime},

as required.
∎

Theorem 6.7

(Post-quantum hard-core measure)
.

Let
\{D_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a family of distributions over

\{0,1\}^{n(\lambda)}\times\{0,1\}
that are sampleable in time
{\rm poly}(\lambda)
, and assume the min-entropy condition

\max_{(x,b)\in\{0,1\}^{n(\lambda)}\times\{0,1\}}D_{\lambda}(x,b)\ \leq\ {\rm negl}(\lambda).

Fix a function
\delta(\lambda)\in(0,1)
and a size bound
s(\lambda)
.
Let
q(\cdot,\cdot)
and
r(\cdot,\cdot)
be the polynomials from Theorem
6.1
. Assume that for all sufficiently large
\lambda
, for every quantum circuit

Q:\{0,1\}^{n(\lambda)}\to\{0,1\}
of size at most
s(\lambda)
,

\Pr_{(X,B)\leftarrow D_{\lambda}}\bigl[\,Q(X)=B\,\bigr]\ \leq\ 1-\frac{\delta(\lambda)}{2}.

Let
\gamma(\lambda)
be any function such that
\gamma(\lambda)\in[1/{\rm poly}(\lambda),\delta(\lambda)]
, and define

\epsilon(\lambda)\ :=\ \frac{\gamma(\lambda)}{4},\qquad\varepsilon(\lambda)\ :=\ \delta(\lambda)\cdot\epsilon(\lambda)\ =\ \frac{\delta(\lambda)\gamma(\lambda)}{4},

and

s^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\ :=\ \left\lfloor\frac{s(\lambda)-r\!\bigl(\lambda,1/\varepsilon(\lambda)\bigr)}{q\!\bigl(\lambda,1/\varepsilon(\lambda)\bigr)}\right\rfloor-n(\lambda),\qquad s^{\prime}(\lambda)\ :=\ \left\lfloor\frac{\gamma(\lambda)^{2}}{4\,n(\lambda)}\cdot s^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\right\rfloor.

Then for all sufficiently large
\lambda
, there exists a measure

M_{\lambda}\preceq D_{\lambda}
such that

\mu(M_{\lambda})\ \geq\ \delta(\lambda)-\epsilon(\lambda)\ =\ \delta(\lambda)-\frac{\gamma(\lambda)}{4},

and for every quantum circuit

Q^{\prime}:\{0,1\}^{n(\lambda)}\to\{0,1\}
of size at most
s^{\prime}(\lambda)
,

\Pr_{(X,B)\leftarrow M_{\lambda}}\bigl[\,Q^{\prime}(X)=B\,\bigr]\ \leq\ \frac{1}{2}+\gamma(\lambda).

Proof.

Fix a sufficiently large security parameter
\lambda
and suppress it from the notation.
Write

n:=n(\lambda)
,
\delta:=\delta(\lambda)
,
\gamma:=\gamma(\lambda)
,

\epsilon:=\gamma/4
, and
\varepsilon:=\delta\epsilon=\delta\gamma/4
.
Note that
0<\gamma<\delta<1
and also
n\geq 1
: indeed, if
n=0
then the domain has
size
2
and hence
\max_{x,b}D(x,b)\geq 1/2
, contradicting

\max_{x,b}D(x,b)\leq{\rm negl}(\lambda)
. We first dispose of the degenerate case
s^{\prime}\leq 0
.

Case 1:
s^{\prime}<0
.

Then there is no circuit of size at most
s^{\prime}
, so the prediction condition is vacuous.
Take
M:=(\delta-\epsilon)\cdot D
. Then
M\preceq D
and
\mu(M)=\delta-\epsilon
, as required.

Case 2:
s^{\prime}=0
.

Let
p_{b}:=\Pr_{(X,B)\leftarrow D}[B=b]
for
b\in\{0,1\}
.
By the hypothesis of the theorem (applied to the constant circuits of size
0
),

p_{b}=\Pr_{(X,B)\leftarrow D}[Q_{b}(X)=B]\ \leq\ 1-\frac{\delta}{2}\qquad\text{for}\qquad Q_{b}(x)\equiv b.

Hence
p_{1-b}=1-p_{b}\geq\delta/2
, so in fact
p_{0},p_{1}\geq\delta/2
.
Define a measure
M\preceq D
by reweighting labels:

M(x,b)\ :=\ \frac{\delta-\epsilon}{2p_{b}}\,D(x,b)\qquad b\in\{0,1\}.

Since
p_{b}\geq\delta/2\geq(\delta-\epsilon)/2
, we have
(\delta-\epsilon)/(2p_{b})\leq 1
,
and therefore
M\preceq D
. Moreover,

\mu(M)=\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}\sum_{x}M(x,b)=\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}\frac{\delta-\epsilon}{2p_{b}}\sum_{x}D(x,b)=\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}\frac{\delta-\epsilon}{2p_{b}}\,p_{b}=\delta-\epsilon.

Finally, under
M
the label is perfectly balanced:

\Pr_{(X,B)\leftarrow M}[B=b]=\frac{\sum_{x}M(x,b)}{\mu(M)}=\frac{(\delta-\epsilon)/2}{\delta-\epsilon}=\frac{1}{2}.

Thus any size-
0
circuit is constant and succeeds with probability exactly
1/2
,
which is at most
1/2+\gamma
. This establishes the theorem when
s^{\prime}=0
.

Case 3:
s^{\prime}\geq 1
.

Assume towards contradiction that for every measure
M\preceq D
with

\mu(M)\geq\delta-\epsilon
there exists a quantum circuit
Q^{\prime}
of size at most
s^{\prime}

such that

\Pr_{(X,B)\leftarrow M}\bigl[Q^{\prime}(X)=B\bigr]\ >\ \frac{1}{2}+\gamma.

(30)

Apply Lemma

with parameters

\alpha:=\delta-\epsilon
,

, and
\gamma
(as above).
We obtain a single quantum circuit

such that for all measures
M\preceq D

with

,

(31)

and whose size satisfies

|Q^{\prime\prime}|\ \leq\ \left(\frac{n}{\gamma^{2}}+1\right)s^{\prime}\;+\;\log\!\left(\frac{n+1}{\gamma^{2}}\right)\leq 2\left(\frac{n}{\gamma^{2}}+1\right)s^{\prime}.

(32)

Recall the definition

and note that

s^{\prime\prime}>0

s^{\prime\prime}
below preserves inequalities.
Thus, we get

|Q^{\prime\prime}|\ \leq\ 2\left(\frac{n}{\gamma^{2}}+1\right)\cdot\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4n}\,s^{\prime\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{n}\right)s^{\prime\prime}.

Finally, since

and

\frac{1}{2}(1+\gamma^{2}/n)\leq 1

|Q^{\prime\prime}|\ \leq\ s^{\prime\prime}.

(33)

Applying Corollary
6.1
.

We verify that the min-entropy premise of Corollary

,

\Pr[X=x]=\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}D(x,b)\ \leq\ 2\cdot\max_{(x,b)}D(x,b)={\rm negl}(\lambda).

Apply Corollary
6.1
to the circuit

C:=Q^{\prime\prime}
with parameter
\epsilon:=\gamma/4
.
We verify the corollary’s size condition (
21
).
By (

) and the definition

,
we have

|C|+n\ \leq\ s^{\prime\prime}+n\ \leq\ \left\lfloor\frac{s-r(\lambda,1/\varepsilon)}{q(\lambda,1/\varepsilon)}\right\rfloor.

Let
q_{\lambda}:=q(\lambda,1/\varepsilon)

r_{\lambda}:=r(\lambda,1/\varepsilon)
.
Since

(interpreting
q_{\lambda}
as the integer oracle-call bound from Theorem

)
,

q_{\lambda}

which is exactly the needed size condition. Therefore the corollary yields a measure

with

such that

\Pr_{(X,B)\leftarrow M^{\star}}\bigl[Q^{\prime\prime}(X)=B\bigr]\ \leq\ \frac{1}{2}+2\epsilon\ =\ \frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2}.

This contradicts (

) applied to

Thus there exists a measure

6.2
Key agreement amplification

In this section, we prove a theorem which enables generically amplifying a weak bit agreement protocol to a full-fledged key agreement protocol, in a manner that preserves post-quantum security.

Theorem 6.9
.

Assuming there exists a post-quantum
(\epsilon,\delta)
-BA protocol secure against adversaries with non-uniform classical advice for constants
\epsilon,\delta\in[0,1]
such that

\delta<\frac{2\epsilon}{1+\epsilon},

there exists a
(1,0)
-BA protocol secure against adversaries with non-uniform classical advice.

A proof of this theorem is presented at the end of this section, after developing the necessary tools. We begin by defining the notion of a
(\epsilon,\delta)
-secure random variable, and of an information theoretic key-agreement amplification for a set of such independent variables.

Definition 6.10

(
(\epsilon,\delta)
-secure random variable
[
Hol05
]
)
.

A triple
X\times Y\times Z
of random variables over
\{0,1\}\times\{0,1\}\times\{0,1\}^{*}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-secure for
\epsilon=\epsilon(\lambda)
and
\delta=\delta(\lambda)
if

•

\Pr[X=0]=\Pr[X=1]=\Pr[Y=0]=\Pr[Y=1]=1/2
.

•

\Pr[X=Y]\geq 1/2+\epsilon/2
.

•

There exists an event
E
which implies
X=Y
such that
\Pr[E\ |\ X=Y]\geq\delta
and
I(X;Z\ |\ E)=0
. That is, conditioned on
E
,
Z
gives no information about
X
.

Definition 6.11

(Information-theoretic key agreement protocol for
(\epsilon,\delta)
-secure random variables
[
Hol05
]
)
.

Let
\epsilon=\epsilon(\lambda),\delta=\delta(\lambda)
,
n=n(\lambda)
, and
\{(X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i})\}_{i\in[\ell]}
be
\ell
independent
(\epsilon,\delta)
-secure random variables. Let
X=\{X_{i}\}_{i\in[\ell]}
,
Y=\{Y_{i}\}_{i\in[\ell]}
,
Z=\{Z_{i}\}_{i\in[\ell]}
. An information-theoretic key agreement protocol for
(\epsilon,\delta)
-secure random variables is a protocol where
A
has input
(1^{\lambda},X)
,
B
has input
(1^{\lambda},Y)
, and they communicate (classically) to produce a transcript
T
, an output
k_{A}
for
A
, and an output
k_{B}
for
B
. The guarantee is that
(k_{A},k_{B},(T,Z))
is an
(1-2^{-\lambda},1-2^{-\lambda})
-secure random variable, and the protocol is called
efficient
if the running time for both
A
and
B
is
\mathsf{poly}(\lambda)
.

We will rely on the following claim:

Claim 6.12

(
[
Hol05
]
)
.

For any constants
\epsilon,\delta
such that
\delta>\frac{1-\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}
, there exists an efficient information-theoretic key agreement protocol for
(\epsilon,\delta)
-secure random variables.

Next, we state and prove an amplification theorem of weak bit-agreement to key agreement.

Theorem 6.13

(Adaptation of Theorem 2.17 from
[
Hol05
]
)
.

Let
\epsilon(\lambda),\delta(\lambda):{\mathbb{N}}\to(0,1)
. If there exists a post-quantum
(\epsilon(\lambda),\delta(\lambda))
-BA protocol secure against adversaries with non-uniform classical advice, and an efficient information-theoretic key agreement protocol for

(\epsilon(\lambda),1-\delta(\lambda)-\tfrac{1}{{\rm poly}(\lambda)})
-secure random variables, then there exists a
(1,0)
-WBA, and thus a standard (computationally-secure) key agreement protocol.

Proof.

Let
\ell=\ell(\lambda)
be the number of invocations of the WBA protocol required by the information-theoretic key agreement protocol, and let
n=n(\lambda)
be the number of bits in the transcript of each WBA protocol. Let
D=\{D_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be the distribution over transcripts and output bits
(Z,X)\in\{0,1\}^{n}\times\{0,1\}
in the WBA protocol, conditioned on
A
and
B
obtaining the same output bit
X
. Let

\delta_{\sf hc}(\lambda)\ :=\ 1-\delta(\lambda).

By the theorem’s assumption, there exists an inverse-polynomial function
\kappa(\lambda)=1/{\rm poly}(\lambda)
such that the information-theoretic key agreement protocol works for

(\epsilon(\lambda),\delta_{\sf hc}(\lambda)-\kappa(\lambda))

-secure random variables. By decreasing
\kappa
if necessary, we assume that
\kappa(\lambda)\leq\delta_{\sf hc}(\lambda)/2
for all sufficiently large
\lambda
.

Now consider the following key agreement protocol.
A
and
B
first use the WBA protocol
\ell
times to obtain outputs

X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell}\qquad\text{and}\qquad Y_{1},\dots,Y_{\ell}

along with transcripts
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{\ell}
. Then, for every
i\in[\ell]
, party
A
publicly samples and sends a uniformly random bit
G_{i}\leftarrow\{0,1\}
, and the parties define

\overline{X}_{i}:=X_{i}\oplus G_{i},\qquad\overline{Y}_{i}:=Y_{i}\oplus G_{i},\qquad\overline{Z}_{i}:=(Z_{i},G_{i}).

They use
\overline{X}_{1},\dots,\overline{X}_{\ell}
and
\overline{Y}_{1},\dots,\overline{Y}_{\ell}
as inputs to the information-theoretic key agreement protocol and run it, producing a transcript
T
. Their outputs
k_{A}
and
k_{B}
are the outputs of the information-theoretic key agreement protocol.

The public masks preserve correctness, since
\overline{X}_{i}=\overline{Y}_{i}
if and only if
X_{i}=Y_{i}
. They also preserve the WBA security of each coordinate: any predictor for
\overline{X}_{i}=X_{i}\oplus G_{i}
given
(Z_{i},G_{i})
can be converted into a predictor for
X_{i}
given
Z_{i}
by XORing the predictor’s output with
G_{i}
. Suppose for contradiction that there exists a QPT adversary
\{\mathsf{Adv}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that

\Pr\left[\mathsf{Adv}_{\lambda}(\overline{Z}_{1},\dots,\overline{Z}_{\ell},T)=k_{A}\mid k_{A}=k_{B}\right]\geq\frac{1}{2}+\mathsf{non}\text{-}\mathsf{negl}(\lambda).

Define its conditional key-guessing advantage by

\beta(\lambda):=\Pr\left[\mathsf{Adv}_{\lambda}(\overline{Z}_{1},\dots,\overline{Z}_{\ell},T)=k_{A}\mid k_{A}=k_{B}\right]-\frac{1}{2}.

By assumption,
\beta(\lambda)=\mathsf{non}\text{-}\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)
. Our goal is to obtain a QPT adversary
\{\mathsf{Adv}^{\prime}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that, for infinitely many
\lambda
,

\Pr_{(Z,X)\leftarrow D_{\lambda}}\left[\mathsf{Adv}^{\prime}_{\lambda}(Z)=X\right]\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta(\lambda)}{2}+\mathsf{non}\text{-}\mathsf{negl}(\lambda).

(34)

Min-entropy alignment.

We denote

\eta(\lambda):=\max\{\Pr_{(Z,X)\leftarrow D_{\lambda}}[Z=z]\mid z\in\{0,1\}^{n(\lambda)}\}.

If it is not the case that
\eta(\lambda)\leq{\rm negl}(\lambda)
, we modify
\Pi_{\mathsf{WBA}}
so that, at the end of the protocol, one of the parties sends a uniformly random bitstring
U\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{\lambda}
, which is then ignored. We include
U
as part of the WBA transcript. Since
U
is independent of the parties’ outputs, this leaves correctness and security unchanged and ensures that

\max_{(z,x)}D_{\lambda}(z,x)\leq{\rm negl}(\lambda).

Henceforth, we assume without loss of generality that this min-entropy condition holds.

Parameter translation.

The WBA security guarantee can be rewritten as

\Pr\bigl[\widetilde{E}(\tau)=k_{A}\mid k_{A}=k_{B}\bigr]\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta(\lambda)}{2}+{\rm negl}(\lambda)=1-\frac{\delta_{\sf hc}(\lambda)}{2}+{\rm negl}(\lambda).

Define

\delta_{*}(\lambda):=\delta_{\sf hc}(\lambda)-\frac{\kappa(\lambda)}{2}.

Earlier, we defined
\kappa(\lambda)\leq\delta_{\sf hc}(\lambda)/2
for all sufficiently large
\lambda
. Hence
\delta_{*}(\lambda)\in(0,1)
for all sufficiently large
\lambda
.

Fixing the hard-core parameter.

Since
\beta(\lambda)=\mathsf{non}\text{-}\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)
, there exists a polynomial
p(\cdot)
and infinitely many security parameters
\lambda
such that

\beta(\lambda)\geq\frac{1}{p(\lambda)}.

In the remainder of the proof, we restrict attention to this infinite set of security parameters. Define

\gamma(\lambda):=\min\left\{\frac{\delta_{*}(\lambda)}{8},\,\frac{\kappa(\lambda)}{4},\,\frac{1}{8\ell(\lambda)p(\lambda)}\right\}.

(35)

Then
\gamma(\lambda)=\Omega(1/{\rm poly}(\lambda))
on this infinite set, and for all sufficiently large
\lambda
we have

0<\gamma(\lambda)\leq\delta_{*}(\lambda),

as required in Corollary
6.1
.

Invoking the hard-core contrapositive.

We will apply Corollary
6.1
to the distribution
D_{\lambda}
over
(Z,X)
with density parameter
\delta_{*}(\lambda)
and hard-core parameter
\gamma(\lambda)
. It suffices to show that for every measure
M_{\lambda}\preceq D_{\lambda}
satisfying

\mu(M_{\lambda})\geq\delta_{*}(\lambda)-\frac{\gamma(\lambda)}{4},

(36)

there exists a non-uniform QPT predictor
\mathsf{Adv}^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}
such that

\Pr_{(Z,X)\leftarrow M_{\lambda}}\left[\mathsf{Adv}^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}(Z)=X\right]>\frac{1}{2}+\gamma(\lambda),

(37)

where
(Z,X)\leftarrow M_{\lambda}
denotes sampling from the normalized distribution
M_{\lambda}/\mu(M_{\lambda})
. Indeed, once this is shown, Corollary
6.1
yields a non-uniform QPT predictor
\mathsf{Adv}^{\prime}_{\lambda}
such that, for infinitely many
\lambda
,

\Pr_{(Z,X)\leftarrow D_{\lambda}}\left[\mathsf{Adv}^{\prime}_{\lambda}(Z)=X\right]>1-\frac{\delta_{*}(\lambda)}{2}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta(\lambda)}{2}+\frac{\kappa(\lambda)}{4}.

contradicting WBA security.

A hybrid argument.

Fix any such measure
M:=M_{\lambda}\preceq D:=D_{\lambda}
, and define

\rho(z,x):=\begin{cases}\frac{M(z,x)}{D(z,x)},&D(z,x)>0,\\
0,&D(z,x)=0.\end{cases}

Since
M\preceq D
, we have
\rho(z,x)\in[0,1]
for every
(z,x)
. For
i\in\{0,\dots,\ell\}
, define the hybrid
{\cal H}_{i}
as follows. First, run the WBA protocol
\ell
times to produce

X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell},\qquad Y_{1},\dots,Y_{\ell},\qquad Z_{1},\dots,Z_{\ell}.

Set initially

(\widehat{X}_{j},\widehat{Y}_{j}):=(X_{j},Y_{j})\qquad\text{for every }j\in[\ell].

For every
j\in[\ell]
, if
X_{j}=Y_{j}
, sample a flag bit
B_{j}
according to

\Pr[B_{j}=1\mid X_{j},Y_{j},Z_{j}]=\rho(Z_{j},X_{j}).

If
X_{j}\neq Y_{j}
, set
B_{j}:=0
. For every
j\leq i
such that
B_{j}=1
, replace
(\widehat{X}_{j},\widehat{Y}_{j})
by
(R_{j},R_{j})
,
where
R_{j}\leftarrow\{0,1\}
is freshly sampled and uniform. After this replacement step, sample independent public bits

G_{1},\dots,G_{\ell}\leftarrow\{0,1\},

and define

\overline{X}_{j}:=\widehat{X}_{j}\oplus G_{j},\qquad\overline{Y}_{j}:=\widehat{Y}_{j}\oplus G_{j},\qquad\overline{Z}_{j}:=(Z_{j},G_{j}).

Run the information-theoretic key agreement protocol on

\overline{X}_{1},\dots,\overline{X}_{\ell}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{Y}_{1},\dots,\overline{Y}_{\ell}

to obtain transcript
T
and output bits
(k_{A},k_{B})
. Run

\mathsf{Adv}_{\lambda}(\overline{Z}_{1},\dots,\overline{Z}_{\ell},T)

to obtain
c
. The hybrid outputs
1
if and only if

c=k_{A}\qquad\text{and}\qquad k_{A}=k_{B}.

We first note that the joint distribution of the input bits

(\overline{X}_{1},\overline{Y}_{1}),\dots,(\overline{X}_{\ell},\overline{Y}_{\ell})

to the information-theoretic key agreement protocol is identical in all hybrids. Indeed, the only replacement occurs when
X_{j}=Y_{j}
, and after the public mask
G_{j}
is applied, both

(X_{j}\oplus G_{j},Y_{j}\oplus G_{j})\qquad\text{and}\qquad(R_{j}\oplus G_{j},R_{j}\oplus G_{j})

are distributed as a uniformly random equal pair. Therefore, if

p_{\sf agr}:=\Pr[k_{A}=k_{B}],

then
p_{\sf agr}
is the same in all hybrids. The hybrid
{\cal H}_{0}
is exactly the real attack experiment for the amplified protocol. Hence, by the contradiction assumption,

\Pr[{\cal H}_{0}=1]=p_{\sf agr}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}+\beta(\lambda)\right).

(38)

We next argue that in
{\cal H}_{\ell}
, the variables

(\overline{X}_{j},\overline{Y}_{j},\overline{Z}_{j})_{j\in[\ell]}

are independent
(\epsilon(\lambda),\delta_{\sf hc}(\lambda)-\kappa(\lambda))
-secure random variables.

Fix any

. Since
G_{j}
is uniform and independent, we have

\Pr[\overline{X}_{j}=0]=\Pr[\overline{X}_{j}=1]=\Pr[\overline{Y}_{j}=0]=\Pr[\overline{Y}_{j}=1]=\frac{1}{2}.

Moreover, equality is preserved by the mask, and the replacement step only occurs when
X_{j}=Y_{j}
. Therefore

\Pr[\overline{X}_{j}=\overline{Y}_{j}]=\Pr[X_{j}=Y_{j}]\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\epsilon(\lambda)}{2}-{\rm negl}(\lambda).

Define the event

F_{j}:=\{X_{j}=Y_{j}\ \wedge\ B_{j}=1\}.

Then
F_{j}
implies
\overline{X}_{j}=\overline{Y}_{j}
. Furthermore, because
\overline{X}_{j}=\overline{Y}_{j}
if and only if
X_{j}=Y_{j}
, and conditioned on
X_{j}=Y_{j}
the pair
(Z_{j},X_{j})
is distributed according to
D
, we have

\Pr[F_{j}\mid\overline{X}_{j}=\overline{Y}_{j}]=\E_{(Z,X)\leftarrow D}[\rho(Z,X)]=\mu(M).

By (
36
) and the definitions of
\delta_{*}
and
\gamma
, we have

\mu(M)\geq\delta_{*}(\lambda)-\frac{\gamma(\lambda)}{4}=\delta_{\sf hc}(\lambda)-\frac{\kappa(\lambda)}{2}-\frac{\gamma(\lambda)}{4}.

Since
\gamma(\lambda)\leq\kappa(\lambda)/4
, this implies

\mu(M)\geq\delta_{\sf hc}(\lambda)-\frac{\kappa(\lambda)}{2}-\frac{\kappa(\lambda)}{16}\geq\delta_{\sf hc}(\lambda)-\kappa(\lambda).

Thus the event
F_{j}
has sufficiently large conditional probability to witness that the variables in
{\cal H}_{\ell}
are
(\epsilon(\lambda),\delta_{\sf hc}(\lambda)-\kappa(\lambda))
-secure random variables. Finally,

I(\overline{X}_{j};\overline{Z}_{j}\mid F_{j})=0.

Indeed, conditioned on
F_{j}
, in
{\cal H}_{\ell}
we have

\overline{X}_{j}=R_{j}\oplus G_{j},

where
R_{j}
is uniform and independent of
(Z_{j},G_{j})
.

Thus the variables in
{\cal H}_{\ell}
satisfy the input condition for the information-theoretic key agreement protocol. Hence the output triple

(k_{A},k_{B},(T,\overline{Z}_{1},\dots,\overline{Z}_{\ell}))

is an
(1-2^{-\lambda},1-2^{-\lambda})
-secure random variable. In particular,

p_{\sf agr}=\Pr[k_{A}=k_{B}]\geq 1-{\rm negl}(\lambda),

and any predictor of
k_{A}
from
(T,\overline{Z}_{1},\dots,\overline{Z}_{\ell})
has success probability at most
1/2+{\rm negl}(\lambda)
conditioned on
k_{A}=k_{B}
. Therefore

\Pr[{\cal H}_{\ell}=1]=p_{\sf agr}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}+{\rm negl}(\lambda)\right).

(39)

Combining (
38
) and (
39
), we obtain

\tau(\lambda):=\Pr[{\cal H}_{0}=1]-\Pr[{\cal H}_{\ell}=1]=p_{\sf agr}\cdot\left(\beta(\lambda)-{\rm negl}(\lambda)\right).

Since
p_{\sf agr}\geq 1-{\rm negl}(\lambda)
, for all sufficiently large
\lambda
in the infinite set under consideration,

\tau(\lambda)\geq\frac{\beta(\lambda)}{2}.

(40)

Therefore, there exists an index
i=i(\lambda)\in[\ell]
such that

\Pr[{\cal H}_{i-1}=1]-\Pr[{\cal H}_{i}=1]\geq\alpha(\lambda),\qquad\text{where }\alpha(\lambda):=\frac{\beta(\lambda)}{2\ell(\lambda)}.

(41)

Let
W
denote the tuple of post-randomization values in all coordinates except
i
:

W:=\bigl((\overline{X}_{j},\overline{Y}_{j},\overline{Z}_{j})\bigr)_{j\neq i}.

The distribution of
W
is the same in
{\cal H}_{i-1}
and
{\cal H}_{i}
. By averaging, there exists a value
w=w(\lambda)
in the support of

such that

\Pr[{\cal H}_{i-1}=1\mid W=w]-\Pr[{\cal H}_{i}=1\mid W=w]\geq\alpha(\lambda).

(42)

Defining
\mathsf{Adv}^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}
.

Hard-code
i=i(\lambda)
and
w=w(\lambda)
into
\mathsf{Adv}^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}
. Given input a WBA transcript

, the predictor
\mathsf{Adv}^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}
operates as follows.

1.

Sample independent uniform bits
R_{i},G_{i}\leftarrow\{0,1\}
, and set

\overline{x}_{i}=\overline{y}_{i}:=R_{i}\oplus G_{i},\qquad\overline{z}_{i}:=(z,G_{i}).

Combine this coordinate with the hard-coded values
w
in all coordinates
j\neq i
, and run the information-theoretic key agreement protocol to obtain transcript
T
and outputs
(k_{A},k_{B})
.

2.

Query

\mathsf{Adv}_{\lambda}(\overline{z}_{1},\dots,\overline{z}_{\ell},T)

to obtain

.

3.

If

and
k_{A}=k_{B}
, output
R_{i}
. Otherwise, output
1\oplus R_{i}
.

Let
s_{\mathsf{Adv}}(\lambda)
be an upper bound on the circuit size of
\mathsf{Adv}_{\lambda}
, and let

upper bound the circuit size needed to execute the efficient information-theoretic key-agreement protocol once on
\ell(\lambda)
input bits, producing
(T,k_{A},k_{B})
. Both

and
s_{\sf KA}
are polynomial in
\lambda
. Hard-coding

contributes

O(\ell(\lambda)\cdot(n(\lambda)+3))

bits of advice and hence
O(\ell(\lambda)\cdot n(\lambda))
circuit-size overhead. Therefore the constructed non-uniform predictor
\mathsf{Adv}^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}
can be implemented by a quantum circuit of size at most

t(\lambda):=s_{\mathsf{Adv}}(\lambda)+s_{\sf KA}(\lambda)+O(\ell(\lambda)\cdot n(\lambda))+{\rm poly}(\lambda)={\rm poly}(\lambda).

(43)

Recall

F_{i}=\{X_{i}=Y_{i}\ \wedge\ B_{i}=1\}.

By construction, conditioned on
W=w
, the hybrids
{\cal H}_{i-1}
and
{\cal H}_{i}
are identical conditioned on
\neg F_{i}
. Conditioned on
F_{i}
, they differ only in the value fed to the information-theoretic protocol in coordinate

: in
{\cal H}_{i-1}
it is
X_{i}\oplus G_{i}
, while in
{\cal H}_{i}
it is
R_{i}\oplus G_{i}
for an independent uniform

. Therefore

\displaystyle\Pr[{\cal H}_{i-1}=1\mid W=w]-\Pr[{\cal H}_{i}=1\mid W=w]

\displaystyle=\Pr[F_{i}\mid W=w]\cdot\Bigl(\Pr[{\cal H}_{i-1}=1\mid W=w,F_{i}]-\Pr[{\cal H}_{i}=1\mid W=w,F_{i}]\Bigr).

Combining this identity with (
42
), and using

, yields

\Pr[{\cal H}_{i-1}=1\mid W=w,F_{i}]-\Pr[{\cal H}_{i}=1\mid W=w,F_{i}]\geq\alpha(\lambda).

(44)

Let
G_{\sf out}
denote the event that the hybrid outputs
1
, namely

G_{\sf out}:=\{c=k_{A}\ \wedge\ k_{A}=k_{B}\}.

Then (
44
) says

\Pr[G_{\sf out}\mid W=w,F_{i},\overline{X}_{i}=X_{i}\oplus G_{i}]-\Pr[G_{\sf out}\mid W=w,F_{i},\overline{X}_{i}\leftarrow R_{i}\oplus G_{i}]\geq\alpha(\lambda).

Since
R_{i}
is uniform and independent of
X_{i}
, the second term is the average of the two cases

\overline{X}_{i}=X_{i}\oplus G_{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{X}_{i}=(1-X_{i})\oplus G_{i}.

Hence

\displaystyle\Delta_{G}

\displaystyle:=\Pr[G_{\sf out}\mid W=w,F_{i},\overline{X}_{i}=X_{i}\oplus G_{i}]

(45)

\displaystyle-\Pr[G_{\sf out}\mid W=w,F_{i},\overline{X}_{i}=(1-X_{i})\oplus G_{i}]\geq 2\alpha(\lambda).

Conditioned on
F_{i}
, the pair
(Z_{i},X_{i})
is distributed according to the normalized measure
M
. Indeed, for every
(z,x)
in the support of
D
,

\displaystyle\Pr[(Z_{i},X_{i})=(z,x)\mid F_{i}]

\displaystyle=\frac{D(z,x)\rho(z,x)}{\E_{(Z,X)\leftarrow D}[\rho(Z,X)]}

\displaystyle=\frac{M(z,x)}{\mu(M)}.

Therefore, by construction of
\mathsf{Adv}^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}
,

\displaystyle\Pr_{(Z_{i},X_{i})\leftarrow M}\left[\mathsf{Adv}^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}(Z_{i})=X_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr[G_{\sf out}\wedge R_{i}=X_{i}\mid W=w,F_{i}]

\displaystyle+\Pr[\neg G_{\sf out}\wedge R_{i}=1-X_{i}\mid W=w,F_{i}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\Pr[G_{\sf out}\mid W=w,F_{i},R_{i}=X_{i}]

\displaystyle+\Pr[\neg G_{\sf out}\mid W=w,F_{i},R_{i}=1-X_{i}]\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(1+\Delta_{G})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}+\alpha(\lambda).

On the infinite set under consideration,
\beta(\lambda)\geq 1/p(\lambda)
, and hence

\alpha(\lambda)=\frac{\beta(\lambda)}{2\ell(\lambda)}\geq\frac{1}{2\ell(\lambda)p(\lambda)}>\gamma(\lambda),

by (

\Pr_{(Z_{i},X_{i})\leftarrow M_{\lambda}}\left[\mathsf{Adv}^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}(Z_{i})=X_{i}\right]>\frac{1}{2}+\gamma(\lambda),

which establishes (
37
).

Finally, choose the size bound
s(\lambda)
in Corollary
6.1
so that for all sufficiently large
\lambda
,

s(\lambda)\geq q(\lambda,1/\varepsilon(\lambda))\cdot\Bigl(n(\lambda)+\Big\lceil\frac{4n(\lambda)}{\gamma(\lambda)^{2}}\,(t(\lambda)+1)\Big\rceil\Bigr)+r(\lambda,1/\varepsilon(\lambda)),

where

\varepsilon(\lambda)=\frac{\delta_{*}(\lambda)\gamma(\lambda)}{4}.

Then the resulting parameter

from Corollary
6.1
satisfies

, so
\mathsf{Adv}^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}
is a valid witness circuit in the premise of the corollary. Therefore Corollary
6.1
, invoked with density parameter
\delta_{*}(\lambda)
and hard-core parameter
\gamma(\lambda)
, gives a predictor
\mathsf{Adv}^{\prime}_{\lambda}
such that, for infinitely many
\lambda
,

\Pr_{(Z,X)\leftarrow D_{\lambda}}\left[\mathsf{Adv}^{\prime}_{\lambda}(Z)=X\right]>1-\frac{\delta_{*}(\lambda)}{2}.

By definition of
\delta_{*}(\lambda)
,

1-\frac{\delta_{*}(\lambda)}{2}=1-\frac{\delta_{\sf hc}(\lambda)}{2}+\frac{\kappa(\lambda)}{4}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta(\lambda)}{2}+\frac{\kappa(\lambda)}{4}.

Since
\kappa(\lambda)=1/{\rm poly}(\lambda)
, this exceeds the WBA security threshold

\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta(\lambda)}{2}+{\rm negl}(\lambda)

by an inverse-polynomial amount for all sufficiently large
\lambda
in the infinite set under consideration. This contradicts WBA security.

This contradiction proves that the amplified protocol is computationally secure. Its correctness and lack of bias follow from the guarantee of the information-theoretic key agreement protocol, and hence the protocol is a fully secure bit-agreement protocol. This completes the proof.

∎

-ToNC such that

-WBA with

, it suffices that for some slack

Substituting

6.3
Post-quantum interactive XOR lemma

The multiplicative Isolation Lemma—introduced and proved by Levin—is used in his work to prove Yao’s XOR lemma
[
Lev85
]
. Halevi and Rabin
[
HR08
, Lemma 3.1]
extend Levin’s proof of Yao’s XOR lemma, which relies on the Isolation Lemma, to the setting of classical interactive protocols. In particular, they show that if every computationally bounded adversary can predict the protocol’s single secret bit with correlation advantage at most
\varepsilon
, then the
t
-fold sequential XOR-composition amplifies secrecy so that every efficient adversary’s advantage drops to roughly
\varepsilon^{t}
(up to negligible terms).

We extend their result to the post-quantum setting, by proving an analogous theorem which enables amplifying security against computationally-bounded
quantum
adversaries. The main challenge in proving such a theorem, is the reliance of the classical proof on rewinding the adversary, which is non-trivial and at times potentially impossible when the adversary is quantum, due to the no-cloning principle. In order to overcome it, we utilize an approach for quantum rewinding developed by Marriott and Watrous
[
MW04
]
and further analyzed by
[
CMSZ22
]
(see our Preliminaries section for an introduction to this approach).

Theorem 6.16
.

Let
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
be two protocols such that for any
i\in\{1,2\}
, for any QPT adversary
A
and state
\ket{\psi}
,

\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{*},\\
b_{A},\ \xi\leftarrow\Gamma_{i}\langle A(\ket{\psi}),V_{i}(r)\rangle\end{subarray}}\left[b_{A}\cdot P_{i}(r,\xi)\right]\leq\delta_{i},

where
V_{i}
is the verifier in
\Gamma_{i}
,
r_{i}
are its random bits,
\xi
is the interaction transcript,

{\rm poly}(\lambda)

is the adversary’s output.

Then for any QPT adversary
A
and state
\ket{\psi}
,

\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},r_{2}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{*},\\
\ket{\psi'},\ \xi_{1}\leftarrow\Gamma_{1}\langle A_{1}(\ket{\psi}),V_{1}(r_{1})\rangle,\\
b_{A},\ \xi_{2}\leftarrow\Gamma_{2}\langle A_{2}(\ket{\psi'}),V_{2}(r_{2})\rangle\end{subarray}}\left[b_{A}\cdot P_{1}(r_{1},\xi_{1})\cdot P_{2}(r_{2},\xi_{2})\right]\leq\delta_{1}\cdot\delta_{2}+{\rm negl}(\lambda),

where we view

as a sequential adversary

: first

interacts with

on input
\ket{\psi}

\xi_{1}

; then

, initialized with
\ket{\psi'}
, interacts with
V_{2}(r_{2})

\xi_{2}
and output bit

.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that there exist a QPT adversary
A
and a quantum state
\ket{\psi}
such that

\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},r_{2}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{*},\\
\ket{\psi'},\ \xi_{1}\leftarrow\Gamma_{1}\langle A_{1}(\ket{\psi}),V_{1}(r_{1})\rangle,\\
b_{A},\ \xi_{2}\leftarrow\Gamma_{2}\langle A_{2}(\ket{\psi'}),V_{2}(r_{2})\rangle\end{subarray}}\left[b_{A}\cdot P_{1}(r_{1},\xi_{1})\cdot P_{2}(r_{2},\xi_{2})\right]>\delta_{1}\cdot\delta_{2}+\kappa(\lambda).

for a function
\kappa=\Omega(1/{\rm poly}(\lambda))
. We can write this as

\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{*}\\
\ket{\psi'},\ \xi_{1}\leftarrow\Gamma_{1}\langle A_{1}(\ket{\psi}),V_{1}(r_{1})\rangle\end{subarray}}\left[P_{1}(r_{1},\xi_{1})\cdot\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{2}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{*}\\
b_{A},\ \xi_{2}\leftarrow\Gamma_{2}\langle A_{2}(\ket{\psi'}),V_{2}(r_{2})\rangle\end{subarray}}\left[b_{A}\cdot P_{2}(r_{2},\xi_{2})\right]\right]>\delta_{1}\cdot\delta_{2}+\kappa(\lambda),

(46)

Now, for any
\ket{\psi'}
, let

T(\ket{\psi'})\coloneqq\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{2}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{*}\\
b_{A},\ \xi_{2}\leftarrow\Gamma_{2}\langle A_{2}(\ket{\psi'}),V_{2}(r_{2})\rangle\end{subarray}}\left[b_{A}\cdot P_{2}(r_{2},\xi_{2})\right].

Due to the security guarantee of
\Gamma_{2}
, for any state
\ket{\psi'}

|T(\ket{\psi'})|\leq\delta_{2}.

(47)

Note that if

using an adversary
A_{2}
, we obtain an adversary which breaks the security by flipping the sign of the predictions of
A_{2}
. This justifies the bound on the

of
T(\ket{\psi'})
.

Due to (
46
), we have that

\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{*}\\
\ket{\psi'},\ \xi\leftarrow\Gamma_{1}\langle A_{1}(\ket{\psi}),V_{1}(r_{1})\rangle\end{subarray}}\left[P_{1}(r_{1},\xi_{1})\cdot\frac{T(\ket{\psi'})}{\delta_{2}}\right]>\delta_{1}+\frac{\kappa(\lambda)}{\delta_{2}}.

(48)

To complete the proof, it suffices to give a procedure
B
that, given
\ket{\psi'}
, outputs a real number
\alpha\in[-\delta_{2},\delta_{2}]
so that for any
\ket{\psi'}
,

\tau(\lambda):=\left|\E[B(\ket{\psi'})]-T(\ket{\psi'})\right|

such that

\frac{\kappa-\tau}{\delta_{2}}=\Omega(\frac{1}{{\rm poly}(\lambda)}).

(49)

Indeed, given such a
B
, an adversary
A
could perform the following steps:

1.

Run
A_{1}
in the interaction with
v_{1}
and keep the intermediate state
\ket{\psi'}
.

2.

Run
B(\ket{\psi'})
and obtain
\alpha
.

3.

Output
b_{A}\in\{1,-1\}
sampled as a Bernoulli random variable which takes the value
1
with probability
\frac{\alpha/\delta_{2}+1}{2}
.

In that case, we would have

\displaystyle\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{*},\\
b_{A},\ \xi\leftarrow\Gamma_{1}\langle A,V_{1}(r)\rangle\end{subarray}}\left[b_{A}\cdot P(r,\xi)\right]

(50)

\displaystyle=\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{*},\\
\ket{\psi'},\ \xi\leftarrow\Gamma_{1}\langle A_{1},V_{1}(r)\rangle\end{subarray}}\left[\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\leftarrow B(\ket{\psi'}),\\
b_{A}\leftarrow\mathsf{Ber}(\frac{\alpha/\delta_{2}+1}{2})\end{subarray}}[b_{A}]\cdot P_{1}(r,\xi)\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{*}\\
\ket{\psi'},\ \xi_{1}\leftarrow\Gamma_{1}\langle A_{1},V_{1}(r_{1})\rangle\end{subarray}}\left[\frac{\E[B(\ket{\psi'})]}{\delta_{2}}\cdot P_{1}(r_{1},\xi_{1})\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{*}\\
\ket{\psi'},\ \xi_{1}\leftarrow\Gamma_{1}\langle A_{1},V_{1}(r_{1})\rangle\end{subarray}}\left[\left(\frac{T(\ket{\psi'})}{\delta_{2}}+\frac{\E[B(\ket{\psi'})]-T(\ket{\psi'})}{\delta_{2}}\right)\cdot P_{1}(r_{1},\xi_{1})\right]

\displaystyle\geq\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{*}\\
\ket{\psi'},\ \xi_{1}\leftarrow\Gamma_{1}\langle A_{1},V_{1}(r_{1})\rangle\end{subarray}}\left[\frac{T(\ket{\psi'})}{\delta_{2}}\cdot P_{1}(r_{1},\xi_{1})\right]\;-\;\frac{1}{\delta_{2}}\,\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{*}\\
\ket{\psi'},\ \xi_{1}\leftarrow\Gamma_{1}\langle A_{1},V_{1}(r_{1})\rangle\end{subarray}}\left[\left|\E[B(\ket{\psi'})]-T(\ket{\psi'})\right|\right]

\displaystyle\geq\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{*}\\
\ket{\psi'},\ \xi_{1}\leftarrow\Gamma_{1}\langle A_{1},V_{1}(r_{1})\rangle\end{subarray}}\left[\frac{T(\ket{\psi'})}{\delta_{2}}\cdot P_{1}(r_{1},\xi_{1})\right]\;-\;\frac{\tau(\lambda)}{\delta_{2}}.

a contradiction with the hardness of
\Gamma_{1}
. In the second equality, we rely on the following identity

\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\leftarrow B(\ket{\psi'}),\\
b_{A}\leftarrow\mathsf{Ber}(\frac{\alpha/\delta_{2}+1}{2})\end{subarray}}[b_{A}]=\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\leftarrow B(\ket{\psi'})\end{subarray}}\left[2\E[\mathsf{Ber}(\frac{\alpha/\delta_{2}+1}{2})]-1]\right]=\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\leftarrow B(\ket{\psi'})\end{subarray}}\left[\alpha/\delta_{2}\right]=\frac{\E[B(\ket{\psi'})]}{\delta_{2}}

Combined with (
48
),
we get:

\E\left[b_{A}\cdot P_{1}(r_{1},\xi_{1})\right]\;=\;\E\left[\frac{\E[B(\ket{\psi'})]}{\delta_{2}}\cdot P_{1}(r_{1},\xi_{1})\right]\;>\;\delta_{1}+\frac{\kappa(\lambda)}{\delta_{2}}-\frac{\tau(\lambda)}{\delta_{2}}=\delta_{1}+\Omega(\frac{1}{{\rm poly}(\lambda)}),

(51)

contradicting the security of
\Gamma_{1}
. The last equality is due to (
49
).

We next define
B
. Let
X
be the adversary register holding the intermediate state
|\psi^{\prime}\rangle
at the start of the

second
execution. Let
Y
be an auxiliary register that contains the ancilla qubits to be used by both parties for computations and communication. It includes one designated qubit

that indicates whether the final outputs match. By purification and deferred measurement, there exists a unitary

that coherently implements the full second execution of
\Gamma_{2}
between
A_{2}
and
V_{2}
.

Define the match projector by

\Pi_{\mathsf{match}}\;:=\;I_{X,Y\setminus Z}\otimes|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{Z},

and define

\Pi_{1}\;:=\;U^{\dagger}\Pi_{\mathsf{match}}U.

Also define the projector onto the clean start state of
Y
:

\Pi_{0}\;:=\;I_{X}\otimes|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{Y}.

Fix
\varepsilon,\eta>0
and let

n\;:=\;\left\lceil\frac{\ln(1/2\eta)}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\right\rceil

.

On input
|\psi^{\prime}\rangle_{X}
, algorithm
B
:

1.

Prepares

in
|0\rangle_{Y}
, so the joint state is

|\Psi_{0}\rangle=|\psi^{\prime}\rangle_{X}|0\rangle_{Y}\in\mathrm{im}(\Pi_{0}).

2.

Performs
n
alternating binary projective measurements, starting with
\Pi_{1}
:

(\Pi_{1},I-\Pi_{1}),\;(\Pi_{0},I-\Pi_{0}),\;(\Pi_{1},I-\Pi_{1}),\;(\Pi_{0},I-\Pi_{0}),\ldots

Let
b_{i}\in\{0,1\}
be the outcome bit of the
i
-th measurement.

3.

Sets

\widetilde{p}:=\mathsf{NReps}(1,b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}),\qquad\widetilde{\alpha}:=2\widetilde{p}-1.

4.

Outputs the clipped value

\alpha\;:=\;\max\{-\delta_{2},\;\min\{\delta_{2},\;\widetilde{\alpha}\}\}\in[-\delta_{2},\delta_{2}].

Let
q(\psi^{\prime}):=\Pr[b_{A}=P(r_{2},\xi_{2})\,:\,\Gamma_{2}\langle A_{2}(|\psi^{\prime}\rangle),V(r_{2})\rangle]
be the
probability in the second execution, so that

By Proposition
3.2
applied with
B=(\Pi_{0},\mathbb{I}-\Pi_{0})
and

,

\widetilde{p}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\alpha}]\;=\;2\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{p}]-1\;=\;2q(\psi^{\prime})-1\;=\;T(|\psi^{\prime}\rangle).

Furthermore, the hardness of
\Gamma_{2}
implies that all eigenvalues of
\Pi_{0}\Pi_{1}\Pi_{0}

\mathrm{im}(\Pi_{0})

. Let us show this. Fix any unit vector

. Since

\Pi_{0}=I_{X}\otimes|0\rangle\!\langle 0|_{Y}
, every such vector is of the form

By construction of
\Pi_{1}:=U^{\dagger}\Pi_{\mathsf{match}}U
, the quantity

\langle\psi^{\prime}0|\Pi_{1}|\psi^{\prime}0\rangle
. Moreover, due to (

.
Therefore, for every unit

\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{\delta_{2}}{2}\;\leq\;\langle\phi|\Pi_{0}\Pi_{1}\Pi_{0}|\phi\rangle\;\leq\;\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{\delta_{2}}{2},

3.2
implies that every Jordan parameter

.

always, yielding the expectation bound

\big|\mathbb{E}[\alpha]-\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\alpha}]\big|\;\leq\;2\varepsilon+2\eta.

Combining the last two displays,

\tau(\lambda):=\big|\mathbb{E}[B(|\psi^{\prime}\rangle)]-T(|\psi^{\prime}\rangle)\big|\;=\;\big|\mathbb{E}[\alpha]-T(|\psi^{\prime}\rangle)\big|\;\leq\;2\varepsilon+2\eta.

Choosing, for example,

, implies

as required.

Remark 6.17
.

We stress that our Theorem holds in the non-uniform computational model, where the security of the protocol holds against any QPT adversary with non-uniform
quantum
advice. Levin’s Isolation Lemma is also proven in the non-uniform model, and Goldreich et al. extend it to the uniform model under an assumption on sampling
[
GNW11
]
. We leave the question of extending our result to the uniform model for future work.

By applying
Theorem

6.16
repeatedly, we immediately obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 6.18
.

Let
\{\Gamma_{i}\}_{i\in[\ell]}
be
\ell={\rm poly}(\lambda)
many protocols such that for each
i\in[\ell]
, any QPT adversary
A
, and any state
\ket{\psi}
,

\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{*},\\
b_{A},\ \xi\leftarrow\Gamma_{i}\langle A(\ket{\psi}),V_{i}(r)\rangle\end{subarray}}\left[b_{A}\cdot P_{i}(r,\xi)\right]\leq\delta_{i},

where
V_{i}
is the verifier in
\Gamma_{i}
,
r
are its random bits,
\xi
is the interaction transcript,
P_{i}(r,\xi)\in\{-1,+1\}
is a
{\rm poly}(\lambda)
-time computable predicate, and
b_{A}\in\{-1,+1\}
is the adversary’s output.

Then for any QPT adversary
A
and state
\ket{\psi}
,

\E_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{r_{i}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{*}\}_{i\in[\ell]},\\
\ket{\psi'},\ \xi_{1}\leftarrow\Gamma_{1}\langle A_{1}(\ket{\psi}),V_{1}(r_{1})\rangle,\\
\vdots\\
b_{A},\ \xi_{\ell}\leftarrow\Gamma_{\ell}\langle A_{\ell}(\ket{\psi'}),V_{\ell}(r_{\ell})\rangle\end{subarray}}\left[b_{A}\cdot P_{1}(r_{1},\xi_{1})\cdot\dots\cdot P_{\ell}(r_{\ell},\xi_{\ell})\right]\leq\prod_{i\in[\ell]}\delta_{i}+{\rm negl}(\lambda),

where we view
A
as a sequential adversary
A=(A_{1},\dots,A_{\ell})
: first
A_{1}
interacts with
V_{1}(r_{1})
on input
\ket{\psi}
, producing transcript
\xi_{1}
and leaving a residual state
\ket{\psi'}
; this repeats
\ell
times sequentially until
A_{\ell}
, initialized with
\ket{\psi'}
, interacts with
V_{\ell}(r_{\ell})
producing transcript
\xi_{\ell}
and final output bit
b_{A}
.

Oblivious transfer amplification

First, we give an OT amplification theorem that follows fairly immediately from

.

Theorem 6.19
.

Given any
(1,\delta,0)
-OT or
(1,0,\delta)
-OT secure against adversaries with non-uniform quantum advice (

3.1
) for
\delta\leq 1-1/{\rm poly}(\lambda)

Proof.

Note that
(1,0,\delta)
-weak OT implies
(1,\delta,0)
-weak OT by standard OT reversal. So let
S^{\prime},R^{\prime}
be an
(1,\delta,0)
-weak OT. We perform standard sequential repetition to achieve the standard OT:

\underline{(1,0,0)\ \mathsf{OT}}

•

Let
\ell=\lambda/(1-\delta)
. For
i\in[\ell]
, the parties run

(b_{i},r_{i}),(r_{0,i},r_{1,i})\leftarrow\langle R^{\prime}(1^{\lambda}),S^{\prime}(1^{\lambda})\rangle.

•

R
samples
b\leftarrow\{0,1\}
, sends
\{b\oplus b_{i}\}_{i\in[\ell]}
to
S
, and outputs
r_{b}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{i\in[\ell]}r_{i}
.

•

S
receives
\{b^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i\in[\ell]}
and outputs

Correctness follows by inspection, and receiver security follows from a standard hybrid argument. Sender security follows from
Section

6.3
. Indeed, we fix each of
\Pi_{1},\dots,\Pi_{\ell}
to be the interactive protocol between honest sender
S^{\prime}
and adversarial receiver, where the predicate
P
outputs the XOR of the sender outputs
r_{0,i}\oplus r_{1,i}
. Then note that
\delta^{\ell}+{\rm negl}(\lambda)\leq e^{-\lambda}={\rm negl}(\lambda)
.

Next, we amplify our committed-bit OT to the point where we can apply the above theorem. As discussed in

, our strategy is inspired by a strategy from
[

in the classical semi-honest case.

Lemma 6.20
.

Given any
(\epsilon,\delta,0)

3.1
) for constants

\delta<\epsilon^{2}

(1,1/2,0)

Proof.

Let
\mathsf{Setup}^{\prime},\mathsf{OT}^{\prime},\mathsf{Ver}^{\prime},\mathsf{TrapGen}^{\prime},\mathsf{Ext}^{\prime}
be the committed-bit OT. Set
\ell=\log_{\epsilon^{2}/\delta}(2\lambda)
, and

. Note that
\ell=O(\lambda)
and
t={\rm poly}(\lambda)
. Our protocol between receiver
R
and sender
S
is defined as follows.

(1,1/2,0)
committed-bit OT

•

\mathsf{Setup}\langle R(1^{\lambda}),S(1^{\lambda})\rangle
:

–

For
i\in[\lambda],j\in[t],k\in[\ell]
, the parties run
\mathsf{init}_{R,i,j,k},\tau_{\mathsf{Setup},i,j,k}\leftarrow\mathsf{Setup}^{\prime}\langle R^{\prime}(1^{\lambda}),S^{\prime}(1^{\lambda})\rangle
.

–

Set
\mathsf{init}_{R}\coloneqq\{\mathsf{init}_{R,i,j,k}\}_{i,j,k}
.

•

\mathsf{OT}\langle R(b,\mathsf{init}_{R}),S(\mathsf{init}_{S})\rangle
:

–

For
i\in[\lambda]
:

*

R
samples
b_{i}\leftarrow\{0,1\}
.

*

S
samples
i^{*}\leftarrow[\lambda]
. For
i\in[\lambda]\setminus\{i^{*}\}

*

sends
b_{i}
.

*

For
j\in[t]
,
k\in[\ell]
, the parties run

\mathsf{Ver}^{\prime}\langle R^{\prime}(\mathsf{st}_{R,i,j,k}),S^{\prime}(b_{i},\mathsf{st}_{S,i,j,k})\rangle,

and
S

\bot
.

–

R
sends
b^{\prime}\coloneqq b\oplus b_{i}
.

–

S
samples
r_{0},r_{1}\leftarrow\{0,1\}
, sends

\left\{r^{\prime}_{j,b^{\prime}}\coloneqq r_{0}\oplus\bigoplus_{k\in[\ell]}r_{i^{*},j,k,b^{\prime}},\quad r^{\prime}_{j,1\oplus b^{\prime}}\coloneqq r_{1}\oplus\bigoplus_{k\in[\ell]}r_{i^{*},j,k,1\oplus b^{\prime}}\right\}_{j\in[t]}

\mathsf{st}_{S}\coloneqq b^{\prime},i^{*},\{\mathsf{st}_{S_{i^{*},j,k}}\}_{j,k}

\mathsf{st}_{R}\coloneqq\{\mathsf{st}_{R_{i^{*},j,k}}\}_{j,k}

\mathsf{Ver}\langle R(\mathsf{st}_{R}),S(b,\mathsf{st}_{S})\rangle

–

For

\mathsf{TrapGen}(\tau_{\mathsf{Setup}})

\mathsf{Ext}(\mathsf{td},\tau)

j\in[t],k\in[\ell]

First, we argue correctness. By correctness of
\mathsf{OT}^{\prime}
, we have that for any fixed

\Pr[\forall k\in[\ell]\ r_{i^{*},j,k}=r_{i^{*},j,k,b_{i}}]\geq\epsilon^{\ell}-{\rm negl}(\lambda).

Thus, by a Hoeffding inequality, we have that

\Pr\left[\mathsf{maj}\left\{\bigoplus_{k\in[\ell]}r_{i^{*},j,k}\oplus\bigoplus_{k\in[\ell]}r_{i^{*},j,k,b_{i}}\right\}=0\right]\geq 1-e^{-2(\epsilon^{\ell}-{\rm negl}(\lambda))^{2}t}\geq 1-e^{-(\epsilon^{2}/\delta)^{\ell}}-{\rm negl}(\lambda)=1-{\rm negl}(\lambda).

By construction, this event implies that

Next, completeness follows from the completeness of

, and receiver security follows from the receiver security of

We define

•

For
