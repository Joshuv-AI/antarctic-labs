Abstract

Introduction

A finite point set in the plane is in
convex position
if it is the vertex set of a convex polygon, and is in
general position
if no three points are collinear. Let
ES(k)
denote the minimum integer such that every planar point set in general position with at least
ES(k)
points contains
k
points in convex position. Classical theorems of Erdős and Szekeres
[
6
,
7
]
show that

2^{k-2}+1\leq ES(k)\leq\binom{2k-4}{k-2}+1,

and they famously conjectured that the lower bound is sharp. After a long sequence of improvements
[
5
,
11
,
14
,
15
]
, the first author
[
13
]
proved that this conjecture is asymptotically correct by establishing the bound
ES(k)=2^{k+o(k)}
. The current best bound on the lower-order term is due to Holmsen, Mojarrad, Pach, and Tardos
[
10
]
, who showed that
ES(k)\leq 2^{k+O(\sqrt{k\log k})}.

A variant of the Erdős–Szekeres problem concerns empty convex polygons. Given a finite point set
P
, a
k
-element subset
X\subset P
is called a
k
-hole
if the points of
X
are in convex position and the interior of
\mbox{\rm conv}(X)
contains no point of
P
. A celebrated construction of Horton
[
9
]
shows that arbitrarily large planar point sets in general position may contain no
7
-hole, while the empty hexagon theorem of Gerken
[
8
]
and independently Nicolás
[
12
]
shows that every sufficiently large planar point set in general position contains a
6
-hole.

For each fixed integer
k\geq 3
, let
f_{k}(n)
denote the minimum number of

k
-holes determined by an
n
-element point set in the plane in general
position. Hence, Horton’s construction implies that
f_{k}(n)=0
for
k\geq 7
.
For
k\leq 6
, estimating the asymptotic behavior of
f_{k}(n)
has attracted
considerable attention. It is known that
f_{3}(n)=\Theta(n^{2})
and

f_{4}(n)=\Theta(n^{2})
, see, e.g.,
[
4
,
1
]
for the best known constants.
The recursive construction of Horton determines only
\Theta(n^{2})

5
-holes and
\Theta(n^{2})

6
-holes (see
[
4
]
). It is conjectured that these
quadratic upper bounds are tight, that is,

f_{5}(n)=\Theta(n^{2})\qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{6}(n)=\Theta(n^{2}).

For many years, the best known lower bound for
f_{5}(n)
was linear.
Aichholzer et al.
[
2
]
established the first superlinear bound, proving
that
f_{5}(n)=\Omega(n(\log n)^{4/5})
. Very recently,
Astudillo-Marbán and Solé-Pi
[
3
]
announced a proof of the improved
bound
f_{5}(n)=\Omega(n^{20/11})
. For
k=6
, no superlinear lower bound is known. The empty hexagon theorem
of Gerken
[
8
]
and Nicolás
[
12
]
implies only a linear
lower bound.

Without any additional restriction, the maximum number of
k
-holes determined
by a point set of size at most
n
is clearly
\binom{n}{k}
, since every

k
-subset of the vertices of a convex
n
-gon forms a
k
-hole. We
consider the following Turán-type problem: how many
k
-holes can a point
set of size at most
n
determine if it contains no
(k+1)
-hole?

For each fixed integer
k\geq 6
, let
h_{k}(n)
be the maximum number of

k
-holes determined by a planar point set in general position, of size at
most
n
, and containing no
(k+1)
-hole. In other words, any point set of size at most
n
with more than
h_{k}(n)

k
-holes guarantees the existence of a
(k+1)
-hole. Note that
h_{k}(n)
is clearly monotone by definition. We prove the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

There are absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that the following holds. For every integer
n>k\geq 6
,

\left(\frac{c_{1}}{k}\right)^{\lfloor k/3\rfloor}n^{\lfloor k/3\rfloor}\leq h_{k}(n)\leq\left(\frac{c_{2}}{k}\right)^{\lceil k/2\rceil}n^{\lceil k/2\rceil}.

We believe that the correct exponent of
n
is closer to the lower bound in
Theorem
1.1
than to the upper bound. This is already consistent with
the behaviors of the Horton sets and the grid Horton sets (see
[
4
]
), which both contain no
7
-hole but determine

\Theta(n^{2})

k
-holes for each
k=3,4,5,6
. We conjecture the
following.

Conjecture 1.2
.

For every fixed integer
k\geq 6
, we have

h_{k}(n)=O_{k}\!\left(n^{\lceil k/3\rceil}\right).

2
Horton sets and preliminary lemmas

We begin by recalling Horton’s classical construction, which requires the following definitions.
Given a finite point set
P
in the plane, we say that
P
is in
strongly general position
if
P
is in general position and no two points in
P
share the same
x
-coordinate. Let
X
be an
r
-element point set in the plane in general position. We say that
X

forms an
r
-cup
(
r
-cap
) if
X
is in convex position and its convex hull is bounded above (below) by a single edge.

Given an
r
-cup
X\subset P
, we say that
X
is
open
if no point of
P
lies above the
r
-cup, that is, no point of
P
lies strictly above some point of the lower chain of
\mbox{\rm conv}(X)
. Likewise, given an
r
-cap
X\subset P
, we say that
X
is
open
if no point of
P
lies below the
r
-cap, that is, no point of
P
lies below the upper chain of
\mbox{\rm conv}(X)
. We say that a finite point set
P
is
upper
r
-closed
(
lower
r
-closed
) if it contains no open
r
-cups (respectively, no open
r
-caps).

Given two finite point sets
P
and
Q
in the plane, we say that
P
lies
high above

Q
(or
Q
lies
deep below

P
) if
P
lies above every line generated by two points in
Q
and
Q
lies below every line generated by two points in
P
.

Let
H=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}\}
be an
n
-element point set in the plane in strongly general position, ordered with respect to increasing
x
-coordinates. We say that
H
is a
Horton set
if it can be obtained by finitely many applications of the following rules:

1.

Every empty set and every one-point set is a Horton set.

2.

If the sets
H^{(1)}=\{h_{i}\in H:\textnormal{\emph{i} is odd}\}
and
H^{(2)}=\{h_{i}\in H:\textnormal{\emph{i} is even}\}
are Horton sets, and
H^{(1)}
lies deep below or high above
H^{(2)}
, then
H
is a Horton set.

In
[
9
]
, Horton constructed the following Horton set
P_{t}
of size
2^{t}
that contains no 7-hole:

P_{t}=\{(i,d_{i}):0\leq i<2^{t}\},

where

d_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{t}a_{j}(2^{t}+1)^{t-j},

and
i=\sum_{j=1}^{t}a_{j}2^{j-1}
is the binary expansion of
i
. Since the recursive definition of a Horton set is preserved under taking subsequences in increasing
x
-coordinate order, every such subsequence is again a Horton set.

Observation 2.1
.

For each integer
m\geq 1
, there is a Horton set of size
m
in the plane.

We will also need the following basic lemmas concerning Horton sets.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
9
]
)
.

Every Horton set is both upper 4-closed and lower 4-closed, and contains no
7
-hole.

Lemma 2.3
.

Every Horton set of size
n
has at least
\left\lfloor\frac{n-2}{2}\right\rfloor
open 3-caps, and at least
\left\lfloor\frac{n-2}{2}\right\rfloor
open 3-cups.

Proof.

If
n\leq 2
, then the statement is trivial. Assume that
n\geq 3
, and let
H=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}\}
. Set
H^{(1)}=\{h_{i}\in H:\textnormal{\emph{i} is odd}\}
and
H^{(2)}=\{h_{i}\in H:\textnormal{\emph{i} is even}\}
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
H^{(1)}
lies deep below
H^{(2)}
, since a symmetric argument would follow otherwise. For each odd integer
i\leq n-2
, the triple

(h_{i},h_{i+1},h_{i+2})

forms an open 3-cap, since
H^{(1)}
lies deep below
H^{(2)}
.
Similarly, for each even integer
i\leq n-2
, the triple
(h_{i},h_{i+1},h_{i+2})
forms an open 3-cup.
This gives at least

\lfloor(n-2)/2\rfloor

open 3-caps and the same number of open 3-cups.
∎

We say that
H
is a
Horton set with respect to a segment
s
if, after rotating the plane so that
s
becomes horizontal, the image of
H
is a Horton set. Likewise, we say that
X\subset H
is an
open cap (cup) with respect to segment
s
if, after properly rotating the plane so that
s
becomes horizontal, the image of
X
is an open cap (cup) in
H
. We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.1
.
