An Asymptotic Theory of Chain-of-Thought in In-Context Learning
Kaito Takanami, Cengiz Pehlevan

Abstract
Chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning has become a widely used mechanism for eliciting multi-step reasoning in large language models by generating intermediate reasoning steps at inference time. Yet the scaling behavior of generalization with CoT depth remains poorly understood. To address this question, we study a theoretically solvable model of CoT for in-context weight prediction in linear regression, where test-time reasoning is represented as an iterative refinement of the weight-parameter estimate. Using tools from random matrix theory under high-dimensional asymptotics, we derive an exact formula for the generalization error as a function of reasoning depth, pretraining data amount, and context length. Our analysis reveals a sharp phase transition separating exponential and polynomial improvement, saturation, and overthinking, and characterizes how the optimal reasoning depth scales. We further show that deeper reasoning is most effective with sufficiently rich pretraining and in-context information, whereas limited pretraining or context makes longer reasoning prone to error amplification or saturation. We also validate these predictions through experiments on fully learned linear attention and softmax attention models. Our results provide a unified theoretical account of how test-time CoT depth affects generalization.

Introduction

Chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning refers to a test-time procedure in which a model generates intermediate reasoning steps before producing its final answer
[
36
]
.
In large language models (LLMs), CoT has become a widely used prompting strategy because it often improves performance on tasks that require multi-step reasoning beyond direct one-shot prediction
[
36
,
18
,
30
]
.
From a practical perspective, an important advantage of CoT is that it enables performance gains through test-time computation, which is increasingly valuable as continued scaling through pretraining alone faces growing constraints in both data and training resources
[
33
,
5
]
.

Despite its empirical effectiveness, how test-time CoT depth affects generalization remains poorly understood.
In particular, recent empirical studies have shown that increasing the number of reasoning steps does not lead to uniformly monotone gains: depending on the setting, deeper reasoning may improve performance, saturate, or even amplify errors through overthinking
[
37
,
22
]
.
A central open question is what governs these qualitatively different regimes, and in particular how they depend on the properties of the data seen during pretraining and on the structure of the information available at test time.
While empirical studies can reveal these phenomena, they cannot by themselves identify the principles that govern them, which motivates the study of a theoretically tractable model in which test-time reasoning dynamics can be analyzed explicitly and related to generalization.

To address these questions, we study a theoretically tractable model of CoT in an in-context learning (ICL) problem for linear regression, where the task is to predict the underlying regression weight from a sequence of contextual examples.
This setting provides a minimal framework that still captures the essential structure needed to analyze the effect of test-time reasoning depth
[
14
,
16
,
15
]
.
During pretraining, the model directly predicts the regression weight from the context. At inference time, CoT is modeled as a sequence of intermediate updates that progressively refine the weight estimate before the final prediction.
This formulation enables us to derive a closed-form expression for the generalization error in the high-dimensional limit and to analyze test-time reasoning dynamics explicitly.

Our main results are summarized as follows:

•

We introduce an in-context weight prediction model for pretraining and test-time CoT, and derive an exact characterization of its test-time error dynamics in the high-dimensional limit, where the input dimension
D
, number of pretraining samples
M
, and number of in-context examples
L
go to infinity simultaneously with fixed ratios (Section
4
).

•

We identify a phase-transition structure in test-time CoT across data regimes, separating four regimes: an exponential-improvement regime, a polynomial-improvement regime, a saturation regime, and an overthinking regime in which deeper reasoning amplifies error (Section
5
).

•

The phase transition further reveals how pretraining task diversity and in-context examples jointly govern test-time scaling. Pretraining task diversity determines the stability of iterative refinement and the optimal reasoning depth, while in-context examples control the error decay rate and the asymptotic information limit at test time (Sections
5.1
–
5.3
).

•

We validate these theoretical predictions in nonlinear softmax-attention models and find that the same qualitative behavior persists beyond the solvable linear setting (Section
6
).

These results provide unified theoretical insights into how test-time CoT depth affects generalization, and yield principled understandings for when deeper reasoning helps, saturates,
or becomes harmful

Related Works

CoT and test-time reasoning in LLMs.

Recent work has brought increasing attention to test-time scaling in LLMs.
A central reason is that additional inference-time computation can improve reasoning capabilities that are not easily unlocked by scaling pretraining alone
[
36
,
30
,
24
]
.
This practical importance is reflected in recent frontier reasoning-oriented systems, which have adopted test-time reasoning as a core design principle rather than a purely prompt-level heuristic
[
27
,
9
]
.
At the same time, empirical studies have shown that increasing reasoning depth is not uniformly beneficial: longer reasoning can yield diminishing returns and saturation
[
35
]
, or even harm performance through overthinking
[
31
,
37
,
22
,
11
]
.
However, these works are largely empirical and do not provide a general account of what governs the transition between improvement, saturation, and harmful overthinking.
Moreover, much of the existing literature emphasizes problem difficulty, while paying comparatively less attention to the role of data properties.
More broadly, theoretical understanding of test-time scaling remains relatively limited
[
10
,
16
,
29
,
13
,
21
,
20
]
.
Motivated by this gap, we theoretically investigate how pretraining quality and in-context information govern these regimes and their scaling laws.

Theoretical studies of CoT and ICL.

Theoretical studies of in-context learning have long used linear regression as a tractable benchmark for understanding how transformers can implement learning algorithms
[
6
,
1
,
34
,
38
]
.
One line of development has pushed this framework further through asymptotic analyses that make exact characterization possible, revealing richer phenomena such as double descent and phase transitions associated with the emergence of genuine in-context generalization beyond memorization
[
23
,
19
,
25
,
3
,
32
]
.
A separate line of work has connected this broader ICL setting to CoT through in-context weight prediction models for linear regression, where transformers iteratively refine task-parameter estimates without learning
[
14
,
16
,
15
]
.
In particular,
[
16
]
theoretically showed that increasing test-time compute can reduce the amount of contextual information required during training, but can also hurt performance when the training data fails to represent the input directions that are important for the downstream task.
Our work builds on both lines of research by combining tractable CoT modeling with asymptotic analysis.
This allows us to view the qualitatively different effects of test-time reasoning depth as phase transitions, and to identify when deeper reasoning improves generalization, when it saturates, and when it becomes harmful through overthinking.
We further show how these transitions are shaped by pretraining quality and in-context information.

3
Model

In this section, we introduce the model used in our analysis.
We study a variant of in-context linear regression model
[
6
]
.
While standard in-context linear regression aims to predict the output for a new query input given a prompt of labeled examples,
our objective is to infer the regression weight itself, as proposed in
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
.
During pretraining, the model learns an update rule from prompts of contextual examples.
At inference time, CoT is generated by iteratively applying this learned update rule to a new task.

Training data.

We consider
M
linear regression tasks indexed by
\mu=1,\dots,M
.
For each task
\mu
, the training examples
\{(\mathbf{x}_{\mu,l},y_{\mu,l})\}_{l=1}^{L}
are generated as

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{\mu,l}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},I_{D}/D),\quad y_{\mu,l}=\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{\mu,l}+\epsilon_{\mu,l},\quad\epsilon_{\mu,l}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\mu}^{2}),

(1)

where
L
is the length of the context, and
\mathbf{w}_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
denotes the task-specific ground-truth parameter vector, sampled from a Gaussian normal distribution.

Prompt construction.

For each task
\mu
, we form a prompt matrix from the labeled examples together with a dedicated slot that stores the current estimate of the task parameter.
Let

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{\mu}=[\mathbf{x}_{\mu,1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{\mu,L}]\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times L},\qquad\mathbf{y}_{\mu}=[y_{\mu,1},\dots,y_{\mu,L}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}.

(2)

We define the embedding matrix as

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mu}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{X}_{\mu}&\mathbf{0}_{D}\\
\mathbf{y}_{\mu}^{\top}&0\\
0_{D\times L}&\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mu}^{\text{init}}\\
0_{1\times L}&1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2D+2)\times(L+1)}.

(3)

The first
L
columns encode the labeled examples, while the last column functions as a slot for the predicted task parameter.
It is initialized with
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mu}^{\text{init}}
and updated during reasoning, unless otherwise specified, we take
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mu}^{\text{init}}=\mathbf{0}
.
The final row serves as a marker that distinguishes the example columns, which take value
0
, from the parameter-estimate column, which takes value
1
.

Architecture.

We consider a single-layer linear self-attention model with residual connection. For an input matrix

\mathbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2D+2)\times T}
, the layer is defined as

\displaystyle\text{{Atten}}(\mathbf{E};\mathbf{W}_{K},\mathbf{W}_{Q},\mathbf{W}_{V})=\mathbf{E}+\ab(1/L)(\mathbf{W}_{V}\mathbf{E})(\mathbf{W}_{K}\mathbf{E})^{\top}(\mathbf{W}_{Q}\mathbf{E}),

(4)

where
\mathbf{W}_{K},\mathbf{W}_{Q},\mathbf{W}_{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2D+2)\times(2D+2)}
.
Defining
\mathbf{W}:=\mathbf{W}_{K}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{Q}
,
\mathbf{V}:=\mathbf{W}_{V}
, and
\theta:=(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{V})
, we equivalently write

\displaystyle\textbf{Atten}(\mathbf{E};\theta)=\mathbf{E}+\ab(1/L)\mathbf{V}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{E}.

(5)

The readout is taken from the weight slot of the final column, which is given by

\displaystyle\textbf{Read}(\mathbf{E})=\mathbf{E}_{(D+2):(2D+1),(L+1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{2D+2}.

(6)

The resulting estimate of the task parameter is thus given by
\textbf{Read}(\textbf{Atten}(\mathbf{E};\theta))
.

Simplified parameterization.

To obtain a tractable model, we retain only the blocks that directly control the update of the parameter-estimate token.
Accordingly, we use the block parameterization

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}=\begin{pmatrix}0_{D\times D}&0_{D\times 1}&I_{D\times D}&0_{D\times 1}\\
0_{1\times D}&0_{1\times 1}&0_{1\times D}&-1\\
0_{D\times 1}&0_{D\times D}&0_{D\times 1}&0_{D\times D}\\
0_{1\times D}&0_{1\times 1}&0_{1\times D}&0_{1\times 1}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{V}=\begin{pmatrix}0_{D\times D}&0_{D\times 1}&0_{D\times D}&0_{D\times 1}\\
0_{1\times D}&0_{D\times D}&0_{1\times D}&0_{D\times D}\\
-D\mathbf{A}&0_{D\times 1}&0_{D\times D}&0_{D\times 1}\\
0_{1\times D}&0_{D\times D}&0_{1\times D}&0_{1\times 1}\end{pmatrix}.

(7)

Here
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
is the only learnable parameter; all blocks of
\mathbf{W}
and all other blocks of
\mathbf{V}
are fixed.
In both
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{V}
, the row and column indices are partitioned into four consecutive blocks of sizes
D,1,D,1
.
This restriction is motivated by prior gradient-flow analyses of related in-context weight-prediction settings
[
14
,
16
]
.
These works show that, under suitable initializations, gradient flow converges to solutions with the same sparse block structure.
Consistent with this motivation, our full-parameter training experiments suggest that these fixed blocks are learned close to the prescribed values or remain negligible, while the retained block
\mathbf{A}
governs the nontrivial update of the parameter estimate at inference time.
See Appendix
A
for empirical support for this simplification.

Under this parameterization, the attention layer maps the embedding matrix to

\displaystyle\textbf{Read}(\textbf{Atten}(\mathbf{E}_{\mu};\theta))=\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mu}^{\text{init}}-\ab(D/L)\mathbf{A}\left(\mathbf{X}_{\mu}\mathbf{X}_{\mu}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\mu}^{\text{init}}-\mathbf{X}_{\mu}\mathbf{y}_{\mu}\right).

(8)

Thus, the readout is an updated estimate of the task parameter obtained by a linear transformation of the empirical gradient.
Detailed calculations and discussions are given in Appendix
B
.

Training objective.

We learn
\mathbf{A}
by ridge-regularized empirical risk minimization so that the readout matches the ground-truth task parameter.
Under the above simplification, the empirical objective is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{A})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\ab\|{\mathbf{w}}_{\mu}-\textbf{Read}(\textbf{Atten}(\mathbf{E}_{\mu};\theta))\|^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\mathbf{A}\|_{F}^{2},

(9)

where
\lambda\geq 0
is a ridge regularization parameter. We denote the minimizer of Eq. (
9
) by
\mathbf{A}^{*}
.

CoT in inference phase.

At inference time, we consider a new linear regression task generated from the same distribution as in pre-training.
Specifically, the task parameter
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
denotes the ground-truth parameter vector, and the examples
\{(\mathbf{x}_{l},y_{l})\}_{l=1}^{L}
are generated without noise as

\mathbf{x}_{l}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},I_{D}/D)
and
y_{l}=\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{l}
, for
l=1,\dots,L
.
Let
\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{L}]\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times L}
and
\mathbf{y}=[y_{1},\dots,y_{L}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
.
We then generate a CoT trajectory by repeatedly constructing a prompt from the observed examples and the current sequence of parameter estimates, and applying the learned attention layer.
At step
t
, we define

\hat{\mathbf{W}}_{t}=[\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{0},\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{1},\dots,\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}]\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times(t+1)}
,
and construct the prompt

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{X}&0_{D\times(t+1)}\\
\mathbf{y}^{\top}&0_{1\times(t+1)}\\
0_{D\times L}&\hat{\mathbf{W}}_{t}\\
0_{1\times L}&\mathbf{1}_{1\times(t+1)}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2D+2)\times(L+t+1)}.

(10)

Starting from
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
, we define the inference-time CoT trajectory by
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}:=\textbf{Read}(\textbf{Atten}(\mathbf{P}_{t};\theta^{*}))
,
where
\theta^{*}=\mathbf{A}^{*}
is the learned parameter.
Here,
t
can be interpreted as the amount of test-time computation, since the computation required at inference is proportional to
t
.

Our CoT can be viewed as modeling iterative test-time computation, in which the same learned Transformer update is repeatedly applied to an evolving intermediate state.
From this perspective, our formulation can be viewed as a form of scratchpad reasoning
[
26
]
and is closely related to recurrent-depth or looped Transformers, which improve inference-time performance by repeatedly applying the same learned recurrent update
[
8
,
28
,
17
]
.
In all of these cases, performance is improved through repeated reuse of the same model over an updated intermediate state or context.

Evaluation.

To study how the CoT depth at inference time
t
affects the final prediction performance, we evaluate the parameter estimation error on a new task.
We then define the mean squared error (MSE) by
\mathcal{E}_{t}:=\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{w}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\|^{2}]/D
,
where the expectation is taken over the pre-training and inference data distributions.

High-dimensional limit.

We consider the high-dimensional limit in which
D,L,M\to\infty
with the ratios

L/D\to\alpha,\quad M/D\to\tau,

(11)

where
\alpha,\tau\in(0,\infty)
are fixed constants.
Here,
\alpha
represents the number of in-context examples per task (context length), and
\tau
represents the amount of task diversity.
The advantage of this regime is that the generalization error can be characterized quantitatively in terms of only a few macroscopic system parameters, with sample-to-sample fluctuations becoming asymptotically negligible.

4
Precise characterization of test-time CoT dynamics

Our goal is to characterize the generalization error at a finite CoT depth
t
in the high-dimensional limit.
The key observation is that the test-time CoT iteration induces a linear recursion for the estimation error (see Appendix
C.2
), which can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}=\left(\mathbf{I}-\ab(1/\alpha)\mathbf{A}^{*}\mathbf{S}\right)^{t}\mathbf{w},

(12)

where
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
is the inference covariance matrix and
\mathbf{A}^{*}
is the learned update matrix.

Operationally, this means that CoT behaves like a learned iterative algorithm: pretraining determines the update matrix
\mathbf{A}
, and inference applies this update repeatedly to the prompt examples so as to progressively refine the estimate of the underlying task parameter
[
16
]
.
This representation implies that the MSE depends only on the matrix product generated by the CoT dynamics.
In particular, the MSE is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t}=\mathbb{E}\Tr\left[\left(\mathbf{I}-\ab(1/\alpha)\mathbf{A}^{*}\mathbf{S}\right)^{t}\left(\mathbf{I}-\ab(1/\alpha)\mathbf{S}{\mathbf{A}^{*}}^{\top}\right)^{t}\right]/D.

(13)

Thus, evaluating the error reduces to understanding a family of mixed moments of
\mathbf{A}^{*}\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{S}{\mathbf{A}^{*}}^{\top}
.
To organize these moments, we introduce the two-point generating function

\displaystyle F(u,v):=\mathbb{E}\Tr\ab[(\mathbf{I}-u\mathbf{A}^{*}\mathbf{S})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-v\mathbf{S}{\mathbf{A}^{*}}^{\top})^{-1}]/D.

(14)

A coefficient comparison shows that
\mathcal{E}_{t}
is obtained from the derivatives of
F(u,v)
at
u=v=0
.
Therefore, the problem of evaluating the generalization error is reduced to computing the large-
D
limit of the scalar function
F(u,v)
.

In the proportional high-dimensional limit, the generating function
F(u,v)
with randomness converges to a deterministic limit, which we denote again by
F(u,v)
.
Combined with the reduction above, this implies that the finite-depth generalization error is asymptotically determined by the derivatives of this limiting function at
u=v=0
.

\lambda=0

5

Phase transition of test-time scaling law

In this section, we extract the asymptotic test-time scaling law from the finite-depth characterization of the generalization error using Result
1
.
As
t\to\infty
, the behavior of
\mathcal{E}_{t}
is controlled by two quantities: the limiting error
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}
and the exponential rate
\Lambda(\alpha,\tau,\sigma^{2})
.
Their interaction yields a sharp phase transition, separating regimes of exponential improvement, polynomial improvement, information-limited saturation, and overthinking.
More precisely, we obtain the following asymptotic test-time scaling law.

Result 2
.

Assume
\tau>1
.
Asymptotically as
t\to\infty
, the test-time scaling law of the generalization error obeys

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t}-\mathcal{E}_{\infty}\sim K\,t^{-1/2}\,\Lambda(\alpha,\tau,\sigma^{2})^{t},

(15)

where
K
is a positive constant and the limiting value
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\infty}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{(1-\alpha)(\tau-1)}{\tau-2-\sigma^{2}},&0<\alpha<1\text{ and }\tau\geq\tau_{c}(\alpha,\sigma^{2})\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(16)

and the exponential rate satisfies

\displaystyle\Lambda(\alpha,\tau,\sigma^{2})\begin{cases}>1,&\tau<\tau_{c}(\alpha,\sigma^{2}),\\
=1,&\tau=\tau_{c}(\alpha,\sigma^{2}),\\
<1,&\tau>\tau_{c}(\alpha,\sigma^{2}).\end{cases}

(17)

Here, the critical value
\tau_{c}(\alpha,\sigma^{2})
is given by

\displaystyle\tau_{c}(\alpha,\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle=1+\frac{(1+\sigma^{2})(\alpha+1+2\sigma^{2}-\sqrt{\Delta})(2\alpha+2+2\sigma^{2}-\sqrt{\Delta})}{2\alpha\sqrt{\Delta}},

(18)

with
\Delta:=(\alpha+1+2\sigma^{2})^{2}-4\alpha
.

The detailed derivation is given in Appendix
F
.

Result
2
shows that the large-
t
behavior of test-time CoT is controlled by two quantities:
the exponential factor
\Lambda(\alpha,\tau,\sigma^{2})
, which determines whether the finite-depth error grows or decays, and the limiting error floor
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}
, which determines whether perfect asymptotic recovery is possible.
Their combination yields four qualitatively distinct asymptotic regimes. When
\Lambda>1
, deeper reasoning amplifies the error, giving the overthinking regime. At the critical boundary
\Lambda=1
, the exponential improvement disappears and the error decays only polynomially in depth.
When
\Lambda<1
, the error decays exponentially fast; this corresponds to the exponential-improvement regime if
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}=0
, and to the saturation regime if
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}>0
, where the dynamics remains stable but converges to a nonzero error floor.

Figure

5.1

Exponential/polynomial scaling regime

In the exponential/polynomial scaling regime, repeated test-time refinement remains beneficial and the generalization error vanishes asymptotically (
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}=0
).
When
\tau>\tau_{c}(\alpha,\sigma^{2})
, the error decays exponentially fast (
\Lambda(\alpha,\tau,\sigma^{2})<1
), whereas at the critical boundary
\tau=\tau_{c}(\alpha,\sigma^{2})
, the exponential factor disappears and only polynomial decay remains (
\Lambda(\alpha,\tau,\sigma^{2})=1
).

In this regime, repeated refinement remains stable, so increasing the CoT depth consistently reduces the generalization error rather than amplifying it.
Moreover, the error converges to zero asymptotically, implying that perfect recovery is achievable in the large-depth limit.
The following theorem gives a representative asymptotic scaling law in this favorable setting.

Theorem 3
.

Assume the population-risk regime
\tau\to\infty
, ridgeless learning
\lambda=0
, and
\alpha>1
.
Then, as
t\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t}

\displaystyle\asymp t^{-3/2}\ab(\frac{2\sqrt{\alpha}+\sigma^{2}}{\alpha+1+\sigma^{2}})^{2t}.

(19)

Especially, in the context-rich limit
\alpha\gg\sigma^{2}
,
\mathcal{E}_{t}\asymp t^{-3/2}(4/\alpha)^{t}
.

The proof is given in Appendix
G
.

The simplified form Theorem
3
clarifies that the decay rate is controlled by
\alpha
, namely the effective amount of in-context examples.
Larger
\alpha
leads to faster exponential improvement, meaning that each refinement step becomes more informative when the context is richer.

The main implication is that, sufficiently accurate pretraining makes additional test-time compute genuinely useful for improving performance.
Moreover, increasing the number of in-context examples in this regime further improves the efficiency of test-time scaling by accelerating the decay rate.
In this sense, pretraining enables effective test-time scaling, while in-context information determines how rapidly its benefit is realized.

5.2

Overthinking regime

In the overthinking regime, the effect of increasing test-time CoT depth is non-monotone.
At small depths, additional reasoning can still improve performance, but beyond a certain point it amplifies error instead of reducing it.
This behavior is governed by task diversity
\tau
, which determines the quality of pretraining.
More specifically, when
\tau
is small, the learned operator
\mathbf{A}^{*}
retains systematic error, which is then amplified by repeated test-time refinement.
As a result, the overthinking regime gives rise to a nontrivial optimal stopping depth.
The following result characterizes its scaling near the phase boundary, together with the asymptotic error achieved at the optimum.

Result 4
.

Assume that
(\alpha,\tau,\sigma^{2})
is in the overthinking regime.
Let
t^{*}:=\argmin_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}\mathcal{E}_{t}
.
Then, as
\tau\to\tau_{c}(\alpha,\sigma^{2})
from below,

\displaystyle t^{*}=C_{t}(\alpha,\sigma^{2})\ab(\tau_{c}(\alpha,\sigma^{2})-\tau)^{-1}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr),

(20)

for some positive function
C_{t}(\alpha,\sigma^{2})
.
Moreover, the generalization error at
t^{*}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t^{*}}=C_{E}(\alpha,\sigma^{2})\,(t^{*})^{-1/2}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr),

(21)

for some positive function
C_{E}(\alpha,\sigma^{2})
.

The detailed derivation is given in Appendix
H
.

Result
4
shows that, in the overthinking regime, the benefit of test-time reasoning is intrinsically finite-depth.
As
\tau
approaches
\tau_{c}(\alpha,\sigma^{2})
from below, the optimal stopping depth
t^{*}
diverges, meaning that deeper reasoning remains useful for increasingly longer horizons.
At the same time, the best achievable error decreases only polynomially, as
(t^{*})^{-1/2}
, rather than exponentially.
Thus, improving pretraining extends the useful range of test-time CoT, but does not immediately restore the efficient exponential scaling.

This behavior reflects the origin of overthinking.
When
\tau
is small, pretraining task diversity is insufficient, and the learned update rule
\mathbf{A}^{*}
is systematically imperfect.
Repeated test-time refinement then amplifies this residual imperfection, so deeper reasoning eventually becomes harmful.
The bottleneck is therefore not test-time compute itself, but the quality of the learned rule produced by pretraining.

The broader implication is that test-time scaling is conditional on pretraining quality.
In the overthinking regime, increasing reasoning depth does not provide an unconditional path to better performance, because its benefit is confined to a finite useful horizon before error amplification takes over.
Deeper reasoning therefore becomes reliably effective only when pretraining is strong enough to delay the onset of overthinking and sustain useful refinement over longer horizons.

5.3

Saturation regime

In the saturation regime, repeated test-time refinement remains stable and converges (
\Lambda(\alpha,\tau,\sigma^{2})<1
), but the generalization error does not vanish asymptotically (
\mathcal{E}_{\infty}>0
).
This nonzero limit reflects an information bottleneck at test time.
Even when pretraining is sufficiently accurate, too few in-context examples leave the prompt itself incomplete, so some information required for prediction is simply unavailable at inference time.
It cannot reconstruct information that is absent from the context.

This mechanism also explains a seemingly counterintuitive phenomenon: increasing the number of in-context examples does not always lead to better long-horizon performance.
As shown in Fig.
1
C, a setting with larger
\alpha
may perform better at small depths, yet be overtaken at sufficiently large depths by the saturation regime because long-horizon behavior is governed by stability of the test-time refinement dynamics.
This qualitative prediction is consistent with
[
7
]
, which reports that adding more in-context CoT examples does not monotonically improve performance.

These results imply that, in this regime, performance is limited mainly by the amount of in-context information rather than by test-time compute.
Therefore, increasing the CoT depth alone yields diminishing returns, while enriching prompt examples is the more effective way to improve performance.
This identifies the saturation regime as an information-limited phase of test-time scaling.

6

Experiments

In the preceding sections, we theoretically analyzed how the effect of test-time CoT depends on the number of pretraining tasks and in-context examples using a simplified linear attention model.
Here, we ask if the same qualitative predictions continue to hold in more expressive settings. To this end, we consider two extensions: a full linear attention model and a softmax attention model.
As in the theoretical setting,
L
denotes the number of in-context examples per task and
M
denotes the number of training tasks.
In both models, we use the same basic prompt format as in Section
3
, initialize
\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\text{init}}
as a random Gaussian vector, and read out the prediction from the parameter block of the final token.
In both extensions, the attention mechanism is parameterized by query, key, and value matrices.

Because these expanded models permit more general token interactions than the simplified theory, we explicitly control which source tokens are visible to the final parameter-estimate token through an attention mask.
Concretely, at each step, we treat the final parameter-estimate token as the query token and allow it to attend to the
L
in-context example tokens and all previously generated estimate tokens, while masking out the query token itself.
This mask makes CoT an iterative update process anchored to the observed examples, while allowing previously generated estimates to be explicitly reused as intermediate states.
These details are given in Appendix
I
.

7

Conclusion

We studied the effect of test-time chain-of-thought depth on generalization in a solvable model of in-context learning for linear regression.
Using a two-point generating function approach combined with random matrix theory, we derived an exact asymptotic formula for the test-time generalization error dynamics.
This formula revealed a sharp phase transition separating four qualitatively distinct regimes of test-time scaling.
When pretraining is sufficient, deeper reasoning reduces the error exponentially at a rate controlled by the in-context examples.
When pretraining is insufficient, reasoning beyond a finite optimal depth amplifies error, and the useful range of test-time compute grows only as pretraining improves.
When in-context information is limited, reasoning remains stable but saturates at a nonzero error floor, since it cannot recover information absent from the context.
Our results provide a unified theoretical account of empirical test-time scaling phenomena, including improvement, saturation and overthinking, and clarify that the primary bottleneck shifts from pretraining quality to context information to test-time compute across these regimes.
These findings not only lay a theoretical foundation for future research on test-time scaling, but also offer practical insights for the design of pretraining strategies and test-time reasoning in LLMs.

Limitations.

While our analysis provides an insightful unified theoretical framework for understanding the test-time CoT dynamics,
it is restricted to a linear in-context learner in which CoT is formalized as iterative weight refinements.
Extending the theory to cover richer CoT structures and realistic architectures to characterize the reasoning process is an important direction for future work.

denotes the expectation over both the pre-training randomness defining
\mathbf{A}
and the inference-time randomness defining
\mathbf{S}
.

To extract this quantity, we introduce the two-variable generating function

\displaystyle F(u,v):=\frac{1}{D}\mathbb{E}\Tr\Bigl[(\mathbf{I}-u\mathbf{A}\mathbf{S})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-v\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}^{\top})^{-1}\Bigr].

(54)

Expanding both resolvents around
u=v=0
, we have the absolutely formal power series

\displaystyle F(u,v)=\sum_{p,q\geq 0}u^{p}v^{q}\,\frac{1}{D}\mathbb{E}\Tr\bigl[(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{S})^{p}(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}^{\top})^{q}\bigr].

(55)

On the other hand, expanding the powers in (
53
) by the binomial formula gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D}\mathbb{E}\Tr\left[\sum_{p=0}^{t}\binom{t}{p}\left(-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{p}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{S})^{p}\sum_{q=0}^{t}\binom{t}{q}\left(-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{q}(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}^{\top})^{q}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{t}\sum_{q=0}^{t}\binom{t}{p}\binom{t}{q}\left(-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{p+q}\frac{1}{D}\mathbb{E}\Tr\bigl[(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{S})^{p}(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}^{\top})^{q}\bigr].

(56)

Comparing this with (
55
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t}=\sum_{p=0}^{t}\sum_{q=0}^{t}\binom{t}{p}\binom{t}{q}\left(-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{p+q}\frac{1}{p!\,q!}\partial_{u}^{p}\partial_{v}^{q}F(u,v)\Big|_{u=v=0}.

(57)

Equation (
57
) shows that the asymptotic evaluation of
\mathcal{E}_{t}
reduces to the determination of the scalar function
F(u,v)
.
The remaining task is therefore to compute the large-
D
limit of
F(u,v)
.

C.3

Extended matrix representation of the resolvent

We next rewrite
F(u,v)
as a block trace of the resolvent of a larger matrix.
This step is crucial because the matrix
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}^{-1}
contains the inverse
\mathbf{C}^{-1}
, which is inconvenient to manipulate directly.
The extended matrix formulation removes this inverse by embedding
\mathbf{A}
into a linear block system.

Recall that

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}^{-1},\qquad\mathbf{A}^{\top}=\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{B}^{\top},

(58)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top},\qquad\mathbf{C}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\mathbf{I}.

(59)

We introduce the extended block matrix

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(u,v):=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}&-u\mathbf{B}&-\mathbf{I}&0\\
-\mathbf{S}&\mathbf{C}&0&0\\
0&0&\mathbf{I}&-v\mathbf{S}\\
0&0&-\mathbf{B}^{\top}&\mathbf{C}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{4D\times 4D},

(60)

and denote its inverse by

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}(u,v):=\mathcal{M}(u,v)^{-1}.

(61)

We write
\mathbf{G}
in
D\times D
block form as

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{G}_{11}&\mathbf{G}_{12}&\mathbf{G}_{13}&\mathbf{G}_{14}\\
\mathbf{G}_{21}&\mathbf{G}_{22}&\mathbf{G}_{23}&\mathbf{G}_{24}\\
0&0&\mathbf{G}_{33}&\mathbf{G}_{34}\\
0&0&\mathbf{G}_{43}&\mathbf{G}_{44}\end{pmatrix}.

(62)

The lower-left zero block follows from the upper block-triangular structure of
\mathcal{M}(u,v)
.

To identify
\mathbf{G}_{13}
, it is convenient to decompose
\mathcal{M}(u,v)
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(u,v)=\begin{pmatrix}M_{11}&M_{12}\\
0&M_{22}\end{pmatrix},

(63)

where

\displaystyle M_{11}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}&-u\mathbf{B}\\
-\mathbf{S}&\mathbf{C}\end{pmatrix},\qquad M_{12}:=\begin{pmatrix}-\mathbf{I}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad M_{22}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}&-v\mathbf{S}\\
-\mathbf{B}^{\top}&\mathbf{C}\end{pmatrix}.

(64)

Since
\mathcal{M}(u,v)
is upper block triangular, its inverse is

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}=\mathcal{M}(u,v)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}M_{11}^{-1}&-M_{11}^{-1}M_{12}M_{22}^{-1}\\
0&M_{22}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.

(65)

Hence the
(1,3)
-block of
\mathbf{G}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{13}=\bigl(-M_{11}^{-1}M_{12}M_{22}^{-1}\bigr)_{11}=\bigl(M_{11}^{-1}\bigr)_{11}\bigl(M_{22}^{-1}\bigr)_{11}.

(66)

We now evaluate these two factors by the Schur complement formula.
For
M_{11}
, the
(1,1)
-block of the inverse is

\displaystyle\bigl(M_{11}^{-1}\bigr)_{11}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\mathbf{I}-(-u\mathbf{B})\mathbf{C}^{-1}(-\mathbf{S})\Bigr)^{-1}=(\mathbf{I}-u\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{S})^{-1}=(\mathbf{I}-u\mathbf{A}\mathbf{S})^{-1}.

(67)

Similarly, for
M_{22}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\bigl(M_{22}^{-1}\bigr)_{11}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\mathbf{I}-(-v\mathbf{S})\mathbf{C}^{-1}(-\mathbf{B}^{\top})\Bigr)^{-1}=(\mathbf{I}-v\mathbf{S}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{B}^{\top})^{-1}=(\mathbf{I}-v\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}^{\top})^{-1}.

(68)

Substituting these identities into (
66
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{13}=(\mathbf{I}-u\mathbf{A}\mathbf{S})^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-v\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}^{\top})^{-1}.

(69)

Taking the normalized trace and expectation, we obtain the desired representation of
F(u,v)
:

\displaystyle F(u,v)=\frac{1}{D}\mathbb{E}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{13}).

(70)

Thus, the original problem has been reduced to the analysis of one block of the resolvent of the extended matrix
\mathcal{M}(u,v)
.

In the remainder of the proof, we study the large-
D
behavior of
\mathbf{G}=\mathcal{M}(u,v)^{-1}
through a finite set of normalized traces, which we call order parameters.
The key point is that the block structure of
\mathcal{M}(u,v)
allows these order parameters to satisfy a closed deterministic system in the high-dimensional limit.
Once that system is solved, (
70
) yields
F(u,v)
, and then (
57
) gives the asymptotic generalization error.

Similarly, considering the average error when
\mu
is chosen uniformly at random from the set of trained tasks is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t}(\mathbf{A})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D}\mathbb{E}_{X}\,\Tr\ab(\ab(\mathbf{I}-\frac{D}{L}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\ab)^{t}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\top}\ab(\mathbf{I}-\frac{D}{L}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\ab)^{t})

(71)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D}\mathbb{E}_{X}\,\Tr\ab(\ab(\mathbf{I}-\frac{D}{L}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\ab)^{t}\ab(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\top})\ab(\mathbf{I}-\frac{D}{L}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\ab)^{t})

(72)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{t}\sum_{q=0}^{t}\binom{t}{p}\binom{t}{q}\ab(-\frac{1}{\alpha})^{p+q}\frac{1}{p!\,q!}\,\partial_{u}^{p}\partial_{v}^{q}\tilde{F}(u,v)\Big|_{u=v=0}

(73)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{F}(u,v)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D}\mathbb{E}_{X}\,\Tr\ab[(\mathbf{I}-u\mathbf{A}\mathbf{S})^{-1}\ab(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\top})(\mathbf{I}-v\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}^{\top})^{-1}\ab]

(74)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D}\mathbb{E}_{X}\,\operatorname{tr}\ab(\mathbf{G}_{13}\ab(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\top})).

(75)

C.4

Order parameters and basic identities from
\mathbf{G}\mathcal{M}(u,v)=\mathbf{I}

We now introduce the normalized traces that will be used to characterize the resolvent
\mathbf{G}=\mathcal{M}(u,v)^{-1}
.
Our aim is to reduce the full matrix-valued problem to a finite set of scalar quantities that remain deterministic in the high-dimensional limit.
These quantities are chosen so that the block matrix identity

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}\mathcal{M}(u,v)=\mathbf{I}_{4D}

(76)

closes into a finite system once combined with the cavity relations derived in the following subsections.

Order parameters.

Recall that
\mathbf{G}
is written in
D\times D
blocks as

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{G}_{11}&\mathbf{G}_{12}&\mathbf{G}_{13}&\mathbf{G}_{14}\\
\mathbf{G}_{21}&\mathbf{G}_{22}&\mathbf{G}_{23}&\mathbf{G}_{24}\\
0&0&\mathbf{G}_{33}&\mathbf{G}_{34}\\
0&0&\mathbf{G}_{43}&\mathbf{G}_{44}\end{pmatrix}.

(77)

We first define the normalized traces of these blocks:

\displaystyle m_{11}:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{11}),\qquad m_{12}:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{12}),\qquad m_{13}:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{13}),\qquad m_{14}:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{14}),

\displaystyle m_{21}:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{21}),\qquad m_{22}:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{22}),\qquad m_{23}:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{23}),\qquad m_{24}:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{24}),

\displaystyle m_{33}:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{33}),\qquad m_{34}:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{34}),\qquad m_{43}:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{43}),\qquad m_{44}:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{44}).

(78)

Next, since the block equations generated by
\mathbf{G}\mathcal{M}=\mathbf{I}
involve the matrices
\mathbf{S}
,
\mathbf{B}
,
\mathbf{B}^{\top}
, and
\mathbf{C}
, we also introduce mixed normalized traces in which these matrices are inserted.
For the terms involving the inference covariance
\mathbf{S}
, we define

\displaystyle s_{12}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{12}\mathbf{S}),

\displaystyle s_{13}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{13}\mathbf{S}),

\displaystyle s_{22}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{22}\mathbf{S}),

\displaystyle s_{23}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{23}\mathbf{S}),

\displaystyle s_{33}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{33}\mathbf{S}),

\displaystyle s_{43}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{43}\mathbf{S}).

(79)

For the terms involving the task-dependent matrix
\mathbf{B}
, we define

\displaystyle b_{11}:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{11}\mathbf{B}),\qquad b_{21}:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{21}\mathbf{B}),

(80)

and for the terms involving
\mathbf{B}^{\top}
, we define

\displaystyle\beta_{14}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{14}\mathbf{B}^{\top}),\qquad\beta_{24}:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{24}\mathbf{B}^{\top}),

\displaystyle\beta_{34}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{34}\mathbf{B}^{\top}),\qquad\beta_{44}:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{44}\mathbf{B}^{\top}).

(81)

Finally, for the terms involving
\mathbf{C}
, we define

\displaystyle c_{12}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{12}\mathbf{C}),\qquad c_{14}:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{14}\mathbf{C}),

\displaystyle c_{22}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{22}\mathbf{C}),\qquad c_{24}:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{24}\mathbf{C}),

\displaystyle c_{34}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{34}\mathbf{C}),\qquad c_{44}:=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{44}\mathbf{C}).

(82)

The quantity of ultimate interest is
m_{13}
, because by (
70
) we have

\displaystyle F(u,v)=\frac{1}{D}\mathbb{E}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{13})=\mathbb{E}[m_{13}].

(83)

The remaining order parameters are auxiliary variables introduced only to obtain a closed system.

Block equations from
\mathbf{G}\mathcal{M}=\mathbf{I}
.

We now derive the basic algebraic relations satisfied by these order parameters.
Multiplying
\mathbf{G}
and
\mathcal{M}(u,v)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}\mathcal{M}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{G}_{11}&\mathbf{G}_{12}&\mathbf{G}_{13}&\mathbf{G}_{14}\\
\mathbf{G}_{21}&\mathbf{G}_{22}&\mathbf{G}_{23}&\mathbf{G}_{24}\\
0&0&\mathbf{G}_{33}&\mathbf{G}_{34}\\
0&0&\mathbf{G}_{43}&\mathbf{G}_{44}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}&-u\mathbf{B}&-\mathbf{I}&0\\
-\mathbf{S}&\mathbf{C}&0&0\\
0&0&\mathbf{I}&-v\mathbf{S}\\
0&0&-\mathbf{B}^{\top}&\mathbf{C}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{G}_{11}-\mathbf{G}_{12}\mathbf{S}&-u\mathbf{G}_{11}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{G}_{12}\mathbf{C}&-\mathbf{G}_{11}+\mathbf{G}_{13}-\mathbf{G}_{14}\mathbf{B}^{\top}&-v\mathbf{G}_{13}\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{G}_{14}\mathbf{C}\\
\mathbf{G}_{21}-\mathbf{G}_{22}\mathbf{S}&-u\mathbf{G}_{21}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{G}_{22}\mathbf{C}&-\mathbf{G}_{21}+\mathbf{G}_{23}-\mathbf{G}_{24}\mathbf{B}^{\top}&-v\mathbf{G}_{23}\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{G}_{24}\mathbf{C}\\
0&0&\mathbf{G}_{33}-\mathbf{G}_{34}\mathbf{B}^{\top}&-v\mathbf{G}_{33}\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{G}_{34}\mathbf{C}\\
0&0&\mathbf{G}_{43}-\mathbf{G}_{44}\mathbf{B}^{\top}&-v\mathbf{G}_{43}\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{G}_{44}\mathbf{C}\end{pmatrix}.

(84)

Since
\mathbf{G}\mathcal{M}(u,v)=\mathbf{I}_{4D}
, each block must match the corresponding block of the identity matrix.
Taking the normalized trace of each nontrivial block yields the following scalar relations.

From the
(1,1)
,
(1,2)
,
(1,3)
, and
(1,4)
blocks, we obtain

\displaystyle m_{11}-s_{12}

\displaystyle=1,

(85)

\displaystyle-u\,b_{11}+c_{12}

\displaystyle=0,

(86)

\displaystyle-m_{11}+m_{13}-\beta_{14}

\displaystyle=0,

(87)

\displaystyle-v\,s_{13}+c_{14}

\displaystyle=0.

(88)

From the
(2,1)
,
(2,2)
,
(2,3)
, and
(2,4)
blocks, we obtain

\displaystyle m_{21}-s_{22}

\displaystyle=0,

(89)

\displaystyle-u\,b_{21}+c_{22}

\displaystyle=1,

(90)

\displaystyle-m_{21}+m_{23}-\beta_{24}

\displaystyle=0,

(91)

\displaystyle-v\,s_{23}+c_{24}

\displaystyle=0.

(92)

From the
(3,3)
,
(3,4)
,
(4,3)
, and
(4,4)
blocks, we obtain

\displaystyle m_{33}-\beta_{34}

\displaystyle=1,

(93)

\displaystyle-v\,s_{33}+c_{34}

\displaystyle=0,

(94)

\displaystyle m_{43}-\beta_{44}

\displaystyle=0,

(95)

\displaystyle-v\,s_{43}+c_{44}

\displaystyle=1.

(96)

Equations
(
85
)–(
96
)
are exact identities that hold for every realization of the randomness.
They express the primary traces
m_{ab}
in terms of the mixed traces involving
\mathbf{S}
,
\mathbf{B}
,
\mathbf{B}^{\top}
, and
\mathbf{C}
.
At this stage the system is not yet closed, because the quantities
s_{ab}
,
b_{ab}
,
\beta_{ab}
, and
c_{ab}
still depend on the full matrix structure of
\mathbf{G}
.

The role of the cavity method is precisely to close this system.
In the next subsection, we analyze the dependence on the inference samples
\{\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
and express the
s
-variables in terms of the
m
-variables alone.
Subsequently, by removing one pre-training task at a time, we derive analogous relations for
b
,
\beta
, and
c
.
Once these relations are combined with
(
85
)–(
96
),
the order parameters satisfy a closed deterministic system in the high-dimensional limit.

C.5

Cavity equations for the
S
-dependent order parameters

We next derive closed equations for the quantities

s_{12},s_{13},s_{22},s_{23},s_{33},s_{43}
,
which contain the inference-time sample covariance

\mathbf{S}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{\top}
.
The key idea is to remove one inference sample
\mathbf{x}_{\ell}
from
\mathbf{S}
, compare the full resolvent with the leave-one-out resolvent, and then use concentration of quadratic forms.
Since the randomness of
\mathbf{S}
comes from the independent Gaussian vectors
\{\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
, this leave-one-out argument closes the
s
-variables in terms of the basic traces
m_{ab}
.

Leave-one-out decomposition.

Fix
\ell\in\{1,\dots,L\}
, and define the leave-one-out sample covariance

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}^{[\ell]}:=\sum_{j\neq\ell}\mathbf{x}_{j}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\top}=\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{\top}.

(97)

Correspondingly, we introduce the leave-one-out extended matrix

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{[\ell]}(u,v):=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}&-u\mathbf{B}&-\mathbf{I}&0\\
-\mathbf{S}^{[\ell]}&\mathbf{C}&0&0\\
0&0&\mathbf{I}&-v\mathbf{S}^{[\ell]}\\
0&0&-\mathbf{B}^{\top}&\mathbf{C}\end{pmatrix},

(98)

and its inverse

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}^{[\ell]}:=\bigl(\mathcal{M}^{[\ell]}(u,v)\bigr)^{-1}.

(99)

Then the full matrix can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(u,v)=\mathcal{M}^{[\ell]}(u,v)+\Delta_{\ell},

(100)

where

\displaystyle\Delta_{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0\\
-\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{\top}&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&-v\,\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{\top}\\
0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(101)

This perturbation has rank
2
, and it is convenient to factorize it as

\displaystyle\Delta_{\ell}=U_{\ell}V_{\ell}^{\top},

(102)

with

\displaystyle U_{\ell}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
-\mathbf{x}_{\ell}&0\\
0&-\sqrt{v}\,\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{4D\times 2},\qquad V_{\ell}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}&0\\
0&0\\
0&0\\
0&\sqrt{v}\,\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{4D\times 2}.

(103)

Resolvent identity.

Applying the Woodbury formula to

\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}^{[\ell]}+U_{\ell}V_{\ell}^{\top}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{G}^{[\ell]}-\mathbf{G}^{[\ell]}U_{\ell}\Bigl(\mathbf{I}_{2}+V_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\ell]}U_{\ell}\Bigr)^{-1}V_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\ell]}.

(104)

We denote the
2\times 2
matrix in the middle by

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{\ell}:=V_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\ell]}U_{\ell}.

(105)

Using the explicit forms (
103
), a direct block computation gives

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}-\,\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{12}^{[\ell]}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}&-\,\sqrt{v}\,\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{13}^{[\ell]}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\\[5.69054pt]
0&-\,v\,\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{43}^{[\ell]}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\end{pmatrix}.

(106)

The advantage of the leave-one-out construction is that
\mathbf{x}_{\ell}
is independent of
\mathbf{G}^{[\ell]}
, because
\mathbf{G}^{[\ell]}
depends only on
\{\mathbf{x}_{j}\}_{j\neq\ell}
and on the pre-training randomness.
Since
\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},I_{D}/D)
, standard concentration of quadratic forms implies that, for any matrix
Q^{[\ell]}
independent of
\mathbf{x}_{\ell}
with bounded normalized trace norm,

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{\top}Q^{[\ell]}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}-\frac{1}{D}\Tr(Q^{[\ell]})\;\longrightarrow\;0

(107)

in probability as
D\to\infty
.
Applying this to each entry of (
106
), and using the fact that removing a single sample does not affect the normalized traces at leading order, we obtain the deterministic equivalent

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{\ell}\;\asymp\;\bar{K}(u,v):=\begin{pmatrix}-m_{12}&-\sqrt{v}\,m_{13}\\
0&-v\,m_{43}\end{pmatrix},

(108)

where
\asymp
means equality up to terms vanishing in the high-dimensional limit.

Extracting the
S
-inserted traces.

To derive the equations for the
s
-variables, we must compute the quadratic forms that appear when
\mathbf{G}
is sandwiched by
\mathbf{x}_{\ell}
.
For this purpose, define

\displaystyle L_{\ell}^{\top}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{\top}&0&0&0\\
0&\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{\top}&0&0\\
0&0&\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{\top}&0\\
0&0&0&\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 4D},

(109)

\displaystyle R_{\ell}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
\mathbf{x}_{\ell}&0\\
0&\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{4D\times 2}.

(110)

Then

\displaystyle L_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{G}R_{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{12}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}&\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{13}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\\
\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{22}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}&\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{23}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\\
0&\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{33}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\\
0&\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{43}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\end{pmatrix}.

(111)

Averaging over
\ell
, the definitions of the
s
-variables in (
79
) yield

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}L_{\ell}^{\top}GR_{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}s_{12}&s_{13}\\
s_{22}&s_{23}\\
0&s_{33}\\
0&s_{43}\end{pmatrix}.

(112)

Indeed, for example,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{12}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}=\frac{1}{L}\Tr\!\left(\mathbf{G}_{12}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}^{\top}\right)=\frac{1}{L}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{12}\mathbf{S})=\frac{1}{\alpha}\,s_{12}.

(113)

Thus one may equivalently work either with the average over
\ell
or directly with the normalized traces.
Below we present the final equations in terms of the normalized quantities
s_{ab}
defined in (
79
).

We now apply (
104
) between
L_{\ell}^{\top}
and
R_{\ell}
:

\displaystyle L_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{G}R_{\ell}=L_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\ell]}R_{\ell}-L_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\ell]}U_{\ell}(\mathbf{I}_{2}+\mathbf{K}_{\ell})^{-1}V_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\ell]}R_{\ell}.

(114)

Each term can now be evaluated by the same concentration argument as above.

First term.

Since
\mathbf{x}_{\ell}
is independent of
\mathbf{G}^{[\ell]}
, we have

\displaystyle L_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\ell]}R_{\ell}\;\asymp\;\frac{1}{D}\begin{pmatrix}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{12}^{[\ell]})&\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{13}^{[\ell]})\\
\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{22}^{[\ell]})&\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{23}^{[\ell]})\\
0&\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{33}^{[\ell]})\\
0&\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{43}^{[\ell]})\end{pmatrix}\;\asymp\;\begin{pmatrix}m_{12}&m_{13}\\
m_{22}&m_{23}\\
0&m_{33}\\
0&m_{43}\end{pmatrix}.

(115)

Second term.

Using (
103
), we compute

\displaystyle L_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\ell]}U_{\ell}\;\asymp\;\begin{pmatrix}-m_{12}&-\sqrt{v}\,m_{13}\\
-m_{22}&-\sqrt{v}\,m_{23}\\
0&-\sqrt{v}\,m_{33}\\
0&-\sqrt{v}\,m_{43}\end{pmatrix},

(116)

and

\displaystyle V_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\ell]}R_{\ell}\;\asymp\;\begin{pmatrix}m_{12}&m_{13}\\
0&v\,m_{43}\end{pmatrix}.

(117)

Moreover, from (
108
),

\displaystyle I_{2}+\bar{K}=\begin{pmatrix}1-m_{12}&-\sqrt{v}\,m_{13}\\
0&1-vm_{43}\end{pmatrix},

(118)

so its inverse is

\displaystyle(I_{2}+\bar{K})^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{1}{1-m_{12}}&\dfrac{\sqrt{v}\,m_{13}}{(1-m_{12})(1-vm_{43})}\\[8.53581pt]
0&\dfrac{1}{1-vm_{43}}\end{pmatrix}.

(119)

Substituting these expressions into the second term of (
114
), we find after a direct matrix multiplication that

\displaystyle L_{\ell}^{\top}G^{[\ell]}U_{\ell}(I_{2}+\bar{K})^{-1}V_{\ell}^{\top}G^{[\ell]}R_{\ell}

\displaystyle\asymp\begin{pmatrix}-\dfrac{(m_{12})^{2}}{1-m_{12}}&-\dfrac{m_{12}m_{13}}{(1-m_{12})(1-vm_{43})}-\dfrac{v\,m_{13}m_{43}}{1-vm_{43}}\\[11.38109pt]
-\dfrac{m_{12}m_{22}}{1-m_{12}}&-\dfrac{m_{22}m_{13}}{(1-m_{12})(1-vm_{43})}-\dfrac{v\,m_{23}m_{43}}{1-vm_{43}}\\[11.38109pt]
0&-\dfrac{v\,m_{33}m_{43}}{1-vm_{43}}\\[11.38109pt]
0&-\dfrac{v\,(m_{43})^{2}}{1-vm_{43}}\end{pmatrix}.

(120)

Final equations.

Combining (
114
), (
115
), and (
120
), and then summing over
\ell
, we obtain the deterministic relations

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}s_{12}&s_{13}\\
s_{22}&s_{23}\\
0&s_{33}\\
0&s_{43}\end{pmatrix}=\alpha\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{m_{12}}{1-m_{12}}&\dfrac{m_{13}}{(1-m_{12})(1-vm_{43})}\\[11.38109pt]
\dfrac{m_{22}}{1-m_{12}}&\dfrac{m_{23}}{1-vm_{43}}+\dfrac{m_{22}m_{13}}{(1-m_{12})(1-vm_{43})}\\[11.38109pt]
0&\dfrac{m_{33}}{1-vm_{43}}\\[11.38109pt]
0&\dfrac{m_{43}}{1-vm_{43}}\end{pmatrix}.

(121)

Equivalently, componentwise,

\displaystyle s_{12}

\displaystyle=\alpha\,\frac{m_{12}}{1-m_{12}},

(122)

\displaystyle s_{13}

\displaystyle=\alpha\,\frac{m_{13}}{(1-m_{12})(1-vm_{43})},

(123)

\displaystyle s_{22}

\displaystyle=\alpha\,\frac{m_{22}}{1-m_{12}},

(124)

\displaystyle s_{23}

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(\frac{m_{23}}{1-vm_{43}}+\frac{m_{22}m_{13}}{(1-m_{12})(1-vm_{43})}\right),

(125)

\displaystyle s_{33}

\displaystyle=\alpha\,\frac{m_{33}}{1-vm_{43}},

(126)

\displaystyle s_{43}

\displaystyle=\alpha\,\frac{m_{43}}{1-vm_{43}}.

(127)

These equations complete the treatment of the
S
-dependent order parameters.
They show that the six quantities
s_{ab}
can be expressed entirely in terms of the primary traces
m_{ab}
.

C.6

Cavity equations for the
B
-dependent order parameters

We now turn to the order parameters
b_{11}
and
b_{21}
, which contain the pre-training matrix

\mathbf{B}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}.

Unlike the
S
-dependent quantities treated in the previous subsection, these variables depend on the randomness of the pre-training tasks.
Accordingly, we now perform a leave-one-task-out analysis with respect to the index
\mu\in\{1,\dots,M\}
.

The main point is that both
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{C}
depend on the same task variables
(\mathbf{w}_{\mu},\mathbf{v}_{\mu})
, so removing one task produces a perturbation that simultaneously affects two blocks of the extended matrix.
This is why the corresponding cavity matrix has rank
4
, rather than rank
2
as in the
S
-cavity analysis.

Leave-one-out decomposition.

Fix
\mu\in\{1,\dots,M\}
, and define

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{[\mu]}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\nu\neq\mu}\mathbf{w}_{\nu}\mathbf{v}_{\nu}^{\top},

(128)

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{[\mu]}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\nu\neq\mu}\mathbf{v}_{\nu}\mathbf{v}_{\nu}^{\top}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\mathbf{I}.

(129)

We then introduce the leave-one-out extended matrix

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{[\mu]}(u,v):=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}&-u\mathbf{B}^{[\mu]}&-\mathbf{I}&0\\
-\mathbf{S}&\mathbf{C}^{[\mu]}&0&0\\
0&0&\mathbf{I}&-v\mathbf{S}\\
0&0&-\bigl(\mathbf{B}^{[\mu]}\bigr)^{\top}&\mathbf{C}^{[\mu]}\end{pmatrix},

(130)

and denote its inverse by

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}:=\bigl(\mathcal{M}^{[\mu]}(u,v)\bigr)^{-1}.

(131)

Since

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{B}^{[\mu]}+\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top},\qquad\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{C}^{[\mu]}+\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top},

(132)

the full matrix can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(u,v)=\mathcal{M}^{[\mu]}(u,v)+\Delta_{\mu},

(133)

where

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\dfrac{u}{M}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}&0&0\\
0&\dfrac{1}{M}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}&0&0\\
0&0&0&0\\
0&0&-\dfrac{1}{M}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\top}&\dfrac{1}{M}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}.

(134)

It is convenient to write this perturbation in factorized form

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mu}=U_{\mu}V_{\mu}^{\top},

(135)

with

\displaystyle U_{\mu}:=\begin{pmatrix}-\sqrt{\dfrac{u}{M}}\,\mathbf{w}_{\mu}&0&0&0\\
0&\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}&0&0\\
0&0&0&0\\
0&0&-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}&\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{4D\times 4},

(136)

and

\displaystyle V_{\mu}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0\\
\sqrt{\dfrac{u}{M}}\,\mathbf{v}_{\mu}&\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}&0&0\\
0&0&\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}&0\\
0&0&0&\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{4D\times 4}.

(137)

Woodbury formula and the cavity matrix.

Applying the Woodbury formula to

\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}^{[\mu]}+U_{\mu}V_{\mu}^{\top}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}-\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}U_{\mu}\Bigl(\mathbf{I}_{4}+V_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}U_{\mu}\Bigr)^{-1}V_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}.

(138)

We denote the corresponding
4\times 4
cavity matrix by

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\mu}:=V_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}U_{\mu}.

(139)

Using (
137
), we compute

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}-\dfrac{u}{M}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{21}^{[\mu]}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}&\dfrac{\sqrt{u}}{M}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{22}^{[\mu]}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}&-\dfrac{\sqrt{u}}{M}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{24}^{[\mu]}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}&\dfrac{\sqrt{u}}{M}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{24}^{[\mu]}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}\\[8.53581pt]
-\dfrac{\sqrt{u}}{M}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{21}^{[\mu]}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}&\dfrac{1}{M}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{22}^{[\mu]}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}&-\dfrac{1}{M}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{24}^{[\mu]}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}&\dfrac{1}{M}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{24}^{[\mu]}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}\\[8.53581pt]
0&0&-\dfrac{1}{M}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{34}^{[\mu]}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}&\dfrac{1}{M}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{34}^{[\mu]}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}\\[8.53581pt]
0&0&-\dfrac{1}{M}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{44}^{[\mu]}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}&\dfrac{1}{M}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{44}^{[\mu]}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}\end{pmatrix}.

(140)

To simplify this expression, we use the moment relations
(
44
)–(
47
)
from the preliminaries.
As in the previous subsection, removing a single task does not affect normalized traces at leading order.
Therefore
\mathbf{J}_{\mu}
is asymptotically equivalent to the deterministic matrix

\displaystyle\bar{J}:=\begin{pmatrix}-\dfrac{u}{\tau}m_{21}&\dfrac{\sqrt{u}}{\tau}c\,m_{22}&-\dfrac{\sqrt{u}}{\tau}c\,m_{24}&\dfrac{\sqrt{u}}{\tau}c\,m_{24}\\[8.53581pt]
-\dfrac{\sqrt{u}}{\tau}m_{21}&\dfrac{1}{\tau}c\,m_{22}&-\dfrac{1}{\tau}c\,m_{24}&\dfrac{1}{\tau}c\,m_{24}\\[8.53581pt]
0&0&-\dfrac{1}{\tau}m_{34}&\dfrac{1}{\tau}m_{34}\\[8.53581pt]
0&0&-\dfrac{c}{\tau}m_{44}&\dfrac{c}{\tau}m_{44}\end{pmatrix},

(141)

where
\tau=M/D
.

Choice of test vectors.

We now derive equations for
b_{11}
and
b_{21}
.
By definition,

\displaystyle b_{11}=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{11}\mathbf{B}),\qquad b_{21}=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{21}\mathbf{B}).

(142)

Since
\mathbf{B}
is a sum of rank-one terms
\mathbf{w}_{\nu}\mathbf{v}_{\nu}^{\top}/M
, these quantities can be extracted by testing
\mathbf{G}
against the vectors
\mathbf{w}_{\mu}
and
\mathbf{v}_{\mu}
.
More precisely, define

\displaystyle{L_{\mu}^{b}}^{\top}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}&0&0&0\\
0&\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}&0&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 4D},

(143)

\displaystyle R_{\mu}^{b}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}\\
0\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{4D\times 1}.

(144)

Then

\displaystyle{L_{\mu}^{b}}^{\top}\mathbf{G}R_{\mu}^{b}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{11}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}\\
\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{21}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}\end{pmatrix},

(145)

so after normalization and averaging over
\mu
, the limit is exactly
(b_{11},b_{21})^{\top}
.

Applying (
138
) between
{L_{\mu}^{b}}^{\top}
and
R_{\mu}^{b}
, we obtain

\displaystyle{L_{\mu}^{b}}^{\top}\mathbf{G}R_{\mu}^{b}={L_{\mu}^{b}}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}R_{\mu}^{b}-\bigl({L_{\mu}^{b}}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}U_{\mu}\bigr)(\mathbf{I}_{4}+\mathbf{J}_{\mu})^{-1}\bigl(V_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}R_{\mu}^{b}\bigr).

(146)

Evaluation of each factor.

We now evaluate the three factors on the right-hand side of (
146
).

First, by the quadratic-form concentration stated in
(
47
) and the independence between

(\mathbf{w}_{\mu},\mathbf{v}_{\mu})
and the leave-one-out resolvent
\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D}{L_{\mu}^{b}}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}R_{\mu}^{b}\;\asymp\;\begin{pmatrix}m_{11}\\
m_{21}\end{pmatrix}.

(147)

Indeed, for the first component,

D^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{11}^{[\mu]}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}\to D^{-1}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{11}^{[\mu]})
,
where we used
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}_{\mu}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}]=\mathbf{I}_{D}
from
(
46
).
The second component is treated in the same way.

Next, a direct multiplication using (
137
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D}{L_{\mu}^{b}}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}U_{\mu}\;\asymp\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\begin{pmatrix}-\sqrt{u}\,m_{11}&c\,m_{12}&-c\,m_{14}&c\,m_{14}\\
-\sqrt{u}\,m_{21}&c\,m_{22}&-c\,m_{24}&c\,m_{24}\end{pmatrix},

(148)

and similarly

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D}V_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}R_{\mu}^{b}\;\asymp\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{u}\,m_{21}\\
m_{21}\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(149)

At this point, only the first two columns of
(\mathbf{I}_{4}+\bar{J})^{-1}
contribute, because the vector in (
149
) has vanishing third and fourth entries.
Therefore only the upper-left
2\times 2
block matters.
From (
141
), this block is

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1-\dfrac{u}{\tau}m_{21}&\dfrac{\sqrt{u}}{\tau}c\,m_{22}\\
-\dfrac{\sqrt{u}}{\tau}m_{21}&1+\dfrac{c}{\tau}m_{22}\end{pmatrix},

(150)

whose determinant is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\Bigl(\tau-u\,m_{21}+c\,m_{22}\Bigr).

(151)

We therefore introduce the shorthand

\displaystyle D_{1}:=\tau-u\,m_{21}+c\,m_{22}.

(152)

A short calculation then shows that

\displaystyle\bigl({L_{\mu}^{b}}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}U_{\mu}\bigr)(\mathbf{I}_{4}+\bar{J})^{-1}\bigl(V_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}R_{\mu}^{b}\bigr)\;\asymp\;\frac{1}{\tau}\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{m_{21}(c\,m_{12}-u\,m_{11})}{D_{1}}\\
\dfrac{m_{21}(c\,m_{22}-u\,m_{21})}{D_{1}}\end{pmatrix}.

(153)

Substituting this together with (
147
) into (
146
) yields the closed equations for
b_{11}
and
b_{21}
.

Final equations.

We conclude that, in the high-dimensional limit,

\displaystyle b_{11}

\displaystyle=m_{11}-\frac{m_{21}(c\,m_{12}-u\,m_{11})}{\tau-u\,m_{21}+c\,m_{22}},

(154)

\displaystyle b_{21}

\displaystyle=m_{21}-\frac{m_{21}(c\,m_{22}-u\,m_{21})}{\tau-u\,m_{21}+c\,m_{22}}.

(155)

These relations express the
B
-dependent order parameters entirely in terms of the primary traces
m_{ab}
.
Together with the identities derived from
G\mathcal{M}=I
, they already determine the
u
-sector once the
C
-dependent quantities are also closed.

C.7

Cavity equations for the
B^{\top}
-dependent order parameters

We next derive closed equations for the quantities

\beta_{14},\beta_{24},\beta_{34},\beta_{44}
,
which contain the transpose matrix

\mathbf{B}^{\top}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\top}.

As in the previous subsection, the randomness enters through the pre-training tasks
\{(\mathbf{w}_{\mu},\mathbf{v}_{\mu})\}_{\mu=1}^{M}
, and we therefore use the same leave-one-task-out decomposition.
The underlying mechanism is the same as for the
b
-variables, but now we must probe the third and fourth block columns of the resolvent, because
\mathbf{B}^{\top}
appears in the
(4,3)
-block of the extended matrix
\mathcal{M}(u,v)
.

Setup.

We keep the leave-one-task-out notation introduced in the previous subsection.
For each fixed
\mu
, we write

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(u,v)=\mathcal{M}^{[\mu]}(u,v)+U_{\mu}V_{\mu}^{\top},

(156)

with
U_{\mu}
,
V_{\mu}
, and
\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}=\bigl(\mathcal{M}^{[\mu]}(u,v)\bigr)^{-1}
defined in
(
137
).
The corresponding Woodbury formula is

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}-\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}U_{\mu}\bigl(\mathbf{I}_{4}+\mathbf{J}_{\mu}\bigr)^{-1}V_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]},\qquad\mathbf{J}_{\mu}:=V_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}U_{\mu},

(157)

and, in the high-dimensional limit,
\mathbf{J}_{\mu}
can be replaced by its deterministic equivalent
\bar{J}
given in (
141
).

Our goal is to evaluate

\displaystyle\beta_{14}=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{14}\mathbf{B}^{\top}),\qquad\beta_{24}=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{24}\mathbf{B}^{\top}),\qquad\beta_{34}=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{34}\mathbf{B}^{\top}),\qquad\beta_{44}=\frac{1}{D}\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{44}\mathbf{B}^{\top}).

(158)

Since
\mathbf{B}^{\top}
is a sum of rank-one terms
\mathbf{v}_{\mu}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\top}/M
, these quantities can again be extracted by testing the resolvent against the vectors
\mathbf{w}_{\mu}
and
\mathbf{v}_{\mu}
.

Choice of test vectors.

Define

\displaystyle{L_{\mu}^{\beta}}^{\top}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\top}&0&0&0\\
0&\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\top}&0&0\\
0&0&\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\top}&0\\
0&0&0&\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 4D},

(159)

\displaystyle R_{\mu}^{\beta}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
0\\
\mathbf{v}_{\mu}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{4D\times 1}.

(160)

Then

\displaystyle{L_{\mu}^{\beta}}^{\top}\mathbf{G}R_{\mu}^{\beta}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{14}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}\\
\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{24}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}\\
\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{34}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}\\
\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{44}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}\end{pmatrix},

(161)

so that, after normalization and averaging over
\mu
, the limiting vector is precisely

(\beta_{14},\beta_{24},\beta_{34},\beta_{44})^{\top}
.

Applying the Woodbury formula between
{L_{\mu}^{\beta}}^{\top}
and
R_{\mu}^{\beta}
, we obtain

\displaystyle{L_{\mu}^{\beta}}^{\top}\mathbf{G}R_{\mu}^{\beta}={L_{\mu}^{\beta}}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}R_{\mu}^{\beta}-\bigl({L_{\mu}^{\beta}}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}U_{\mu}\bigr)(\mathbf{I}_{4}+\mathbf{J}_{\mu})^{-1}\bigl(V_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}R_{\mu}^{\beta}\bigr).

(162)

Evaluation of the three factors.

We now analyze each term on the right-hand side of (
162
).

First, by the same quadratic-form concentration
(
47
) and the leave-one-out argument as before,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D}{L_{\mu}^{\beta}}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}R_{\mu}^{\beta}\;\asymp\;\begin{pmatrix}m_{14}\\
m_{24}\\
m_{34}\\
m_{44}\end{pmatrix}.

(163)

Indeed, the
\mu
-th task is independent of
\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}
, and replacing quadratic forms by their normalized traces yields the corresponding block traces of the leave-one-out resolvent.

Next, using the explicit form of
U_{\mu}
in (
137
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D}{L_{\mu}^{\beta}}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}U_{\mu}\;\asymp\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\begin{pmatrix}-\sqrt{u}\,m_{11}&m_{12}&-m_{14}&m_{14}\\
-\sqrt{u}\,m_{21}&m_{22}&-m_{24}&m_{24}\\
0&0&-m_{34}&m_{34}\\
0&0&-m_{44}&m_{44}\end{pmatrix}.

(164)

Here the first two columns come from the first two block columns of
U_{\mu}
, which contain
\mathbf{w}_{\mu}
and
\mathbf{v}_{\mu}
, while the last two columns reflect the two opposite contributions in the fourth block row of
U_{\mu}
.

Similarly, using the explicit form of
V_{\mu}
, we find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D}V_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}R_{\mu}^{\beta}\;\asymp\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{u}\,c\,m_{24}\\
c\,m_{24}\\
m_{34}\\
c\,m_{44}\end{pmatrix}.

(165)

The factor
c
appears whenever
\mathbf{v}_{\mu}
is contracted with itself, reflecting the asymptotic norm

D^{-1}\|\mathbf{v}_{\mu}\|^{2}\to c
.

Reduction of
(\mathbf{I}_{4}+\bar{J})^{-1}
.

To simplify the cavity correction in (
162
), we use the block upper-triangular structure of
\bar{J}
.
Introduce the shorthand

\displaystyle D_{1}

\displaystyle:=\tau-u\,m_{21}+c\,m_{22},

(166)

\displaystyle D_{2}

\displaystyle:=\tau-m_{34}+c\,m_{44}.

(167)

These quantities are the determinants of the relevant
2\times 2
blocks of
\mathbf{I}_{4}+\bar{J}
, up to the common factor
\tau^{-1}
.
More precisely, the upper-left block of
\mathbf{I}_{4}+\bar{J}
has determinant
D_{1}/\tau
, while the lower-right block has determinant
D_{2}/\tau
.

Because the vector (
165
) has nonzero entries in both the first two and the last two components, both sectors contribute here.
A direct but straightforward matrix multiplication using (
164
), (
165
), and the explicit inverse of
\mathbf{I}_{4}+\bar{J}
yields the cavity correction.
After simplifying the resulting expressions, we obtain the following closed equations.

Final equations.

In the high-dimensional limit,

\displaystyle\beta_{14}

\displaystyle=m_{14}-\frac{c\,m_{24}(m_{12}-u\,m_{11})}{D_{1}D_{2}/\tau}-\frac{m_{14}(c\,m_{44}-m_{34})}{D_{2}}

\displaystyle=m_{14}-\frac{\tau\,c\,m_{24}(m_{12}-u\,m_{11})}{D_{1}D_{2}}-\frac{m_{14}(c\,m_{44}-m_{34})}{D_{2}},

(168)

\displaystyle\beta_{24}

\displaystyle=m_{24}-\frac{\tau\,c\,m_{24}(m_{22}-u\,m_{21})}{D_{1}D_{2}}-\frac{m_{24}(c\,m_{44}-m_{34})}{D_{2}},

(169)

\displaystyle\beta_{34}

\displaystyle=m_{34}-\frac{m_{34}(c\,m_{44}-m_{34})}{D_{2}},

(170)

\displaystyle\beta_{44}

\displaystyle=m_{44}-\frac{m_{44}(c\,m_{44}-m_{34})}{D_{2}}.

(171)

Equivalently, writing out the denominators explicitly,

\displaystyle\beta_{14}

\displaystyle=m_{14}-\frac{c\,m_{24}(m_{12}-u\,m_{11})}{\tau\left(1-\frac{u}{\tau}m_{21}+\frac{c}{\tau}m_{22}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{\tau}m_{34}+\frac{c}{\tau}m_{44}\right)}-\frac{m_{14}(c\,m_{44}-m_{34})}{\tau\left(1-\frac{1}{\tau}m_{34}+\frac{c}{\tau}m_{44}\right)},

(172)

\displaystyle\beta_{24}

\displaystyle=m_{24}-\frac{c\,m_{24}(m_{22}-u\,m_{21})}{\tau\left(1-\frac{u}{\tau}m_{21}+\frac{c}{\tau}m_{22}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{\tau}m_{34}+\frac{c}{\tau}m_{44}\right)}-\frac{m_{24}(c\,m_{44}-m_{34})}{\tau\left(1-\frac{1}{\tau}m_{34}+\frac{c}{\tau}m_{44}\right)},

(173)

\displaystyle\beta_{34}

\displaystyle=m_{34}-\frac{m_{34}(c\,m_{44}-m_{34})}{\tau\left(1-\frac{1}{\tau}m_{34}+\frac{c}{\tau}m_{44}\right)},

(174)

\displaystyle\beta_{44}

\displaystyle=m_{44}-\frac{m_{44}(c\,m_{44}-m_{34})}{\tau\left(1-\frac{1}{\tau}m_{34}+\frac{c}{\tau}m_{44}\right)}.

(175)

These relations close the
\mathbf{B}^{\top}
-dependent order parameters in terms of the primary traces
m_{ab}
.
Together with the equations for
s
and
b
, they determine the contributions coming from the blocks involving
\mathbf{S}
,
\mathbf{B}
, and
\mathbf{B}^{\top}
.

C.8

Cavity equations for the
C
-dependent order parameters

We finally derive closed equations for the quantities

c_{12},c_{14},c_{22},c_{24},c_{34},c_{44}
,
which contain the matrix

\mathbf{C}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\mathbf{I}.

This subsection completes the closure of the order-parameter system.
As in the previous two subsections, we use a leave-one-task-out decomposition with respect to the pre-training index
\mu
.
The derivation closely parallels the one for the
\mathbf{B}^{\top}
-dependent quantities, but here both left and right test vectors involve
\mathbf{v}_{\mu}
, so the asymptotic norm factor
c
enters more systematically.

Setup.

We keep the leave-one-task-out notation introduced above.
For each fixed
\mu
, recall that

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(u,v)=\mathcal{M}^{[\mu]}(u,v)+U_{\mu}V_{\mu}^{\top},

(176)

with
U_{\mu}
,
V_{\mu}
, and
\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}=\bigl(\mathcal{M}^{[\mu]}(u,v)\bigr)^{-1}
defined in (
137
).
The associated Woodbury formula is

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}-\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}U_{\mu}\bigl(\mathbf{I}_{4}+\mathbf{J}_{\mu}\bigr)^{-1}V_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]},

(177)

where
\mathbf{J}_{\mu}=V_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{[\mu]}U_{\mu}
, and in the high-dimensional limit we may replace
\mathbf{J}_{\mu}
by the deterministic matrix
\bar{J}
given in (
141
).

Our aim is to evaluate the mixed traces involving
C
.
Since

\mathbf{C}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}\mathbf{v}_{\mu}^{\top}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\mathbf{I},

each term
\Tr(\mathbf{G}_{ab}\mathbf{C})
splits into a contribution from the empirical covariance of the

We numerically evaluate the theoretical formula in Result

A direct implementation of (

(259)

To do so, we first expand

\displaystyle h(x):=g\!\left(-\frac{x}{\alpha(1-x)}\right)=\sum_{n\geq 0}h_{n}x^{n},

where
g(u)
is the resolvent function appearing in Result
1
(see (
239

Substituting

into the quadratic relation (

\displaystyle ug(u)^{2}-\bigl(c+(1-\alpha)u\bigr)g(u)+c=0

\displaystyle xh(x)^{2}+\ab(\alpha c-\ab(2+\sigma^{2})x)h(x)-\alpha c(1-x)=0,

(262)

with

as in (

Writing

and matching coefficients yields the recurrence

\displaystyle h_{0}

\displaystyle=1,

(263)

\displaystyle h_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ab(2+\sigma^{2})h_{n-1}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}h_{k}h_{n-1-k}-\alpha c\,\mathbf{1}_{\{n=1\}}}{\alpha c},\qquad n\geq 1.

For

After the change of variables above, let

Appendix F

Derivation of Result
2

In this appendix, we derive the switching criterion stated in Result
2
.
The starting point is the diagonal coefficient representation of the quantity

\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\mathrm{new}}
, which allows us to study its large-
t
behavior through the singularities of a two-variable generating function.
Our goal is to identify the singular point that controls the diagonal asymptotics and to determine when its modulus crosses the unit circle.
This yields an explicit critical value
\tau_{c}(\alpha,\sigma^{2})
at which the exponential rate changes from growth to decay.

We begin by restating the result.
See
2

We now derive this result in detail.

First, from the coefficient formula,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\mathrm{new}}=[x^{t}y^{t}]\,B(x,y),

(273)

where

\displaystyle B(x,y):=\frac{1}{(1-x)(1-y)}\,m_{13}\!\left(-\frac{x}{\alpha(1-x)},-\frac{y}{\alpha(1-y)}\right).

(274)

The function
m_{13}(u,v)
is meromorphic away from the branch locus of the scalar Wishart resolvent
g
, and its singularities are determined by the vanishing of its denominator

\displaystyle D(u,v):=\alpha-(g(u)-1)(g(v)-1)\Bigl[1+\beta\bigl(\alpha+g(u)+g(v)-1\bigr)\Bigr],

(275)

with

\displaystyle\beta=\frac{1+\sigma^{2}}{\alpha(\tau-1)}.

(276)

Accordingly, the singular variety relevant for the diagonal coefficient is

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}:=\{(x,y):D(u(x),v(y))=0\},

(277)

where

\displaystyle u(x):=-\frac{x}{\alpha(1-x)},\qquad v(y):=-\frac{y}{\alpha(1-y)}.

(278)

Because both the singular variety and the diagonal direction
(t,t)
are invariant under the exchange
x\leftrightarrow y
, the natural candidate for the dominant critical point is a symmetric point of the form

\displaystyle(x,y)=(\xi,\xi)\in\mathcal{V}.

(279)

Throughout this appendix, we assume that this symmetric point is indeed the unique dominant critical point and that no other singularity of smaller modulus contributes to the diagonal asymptotics.
Under this assumption, the problem reduces to a one-parameter analysis along the symmetric branch, which we now describe.

Introduce the variables

\displaystyle a:=1-g(u),\qquad b:=1-g(v).

(280)

On the symmetric branch we set
a=b
.
The scalar resolvent
g(u)
satisfies the quadratic equation

\displaystyle u\,g(u)^{2}-\bigl(c+(1-\alpha)u\bigr)g(u)+c=0,

(281)

where

\displaystyle c=1+\frac{1+\sigma^{2}}{\alpha}.

(282)

Substituting
g(u)=1-a
into this equation and solving for
u
, we obtain

\displaystyle u=-\frac{c\,a}{(1-a)(\alpha-a)}.

(283)

Combining this with

\displaystyle u(x)=-\frac{x}{\alpha(1-x)},

(284)

we obtain a parametrization of the symmetric branch in the
x
-plane:

\displaystyle x=x(a)=\frac{\alpha c\,a}{(1-a)(\alpha-a)+\alpha c\,a}=\frac{(\alpha+1+\sigma^{2})a}{\alpha+\sigma^{2}a+a^{2}}.

(285)

Next, we rewrite the singularity condition on the diagonal.
Since

\displaystyle g(u)-1=-a,

(286)

the condition
D(u,u)=0
becomes

\displaystyle\alpha-a^{2}\Bigl[1+\beta(\alpha+1-2a)\Bigr]=0,

(287)

or equivalently,

\displaystyle a^{2}\Bigl[1+\beta(\alpha+1-2a)\Bigr]=\alpha.

(288)

Therefore, the symmetric singular points are parametrized by the solutions of (
288
), and their location in the
x
-plane is given by (
285
).

Under the dominant symmetric critical-point assumption, the diagonal coefficient is governed by a smooth critical point
(\xi,\xi)
on the singular variety.
Standard smooth-point asymptotics in two variables then gives

\displaystyle[x^{t}y^{t}]\,B(x,y)\sim K\,t^{-1/2}\,(\xi^{2})^{-t}

(289)

for some nonzero constant
K
.
Equivalently,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\mathrm{new}}\sim K\,t^{-1/2}\,\Lambda(\alpha,\tau,\sigma^{2})^{t},\qquad\Lambda(\alpha,\tau,\sigma^{2}):=|\xi|^{-2}.

(290)

Thus the long-time behavior is determined by the position of
\xi
relative to the unit circle:

\displaystyle|\xi|<1

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\Lambda>1\qquad\text{(exponential growth)},

(291)

\displaystyle|\xi|=1

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\Lambda=1\qquad\text{(critical polynomial behavior)},

(292)

\displaystyle|\xi|>1

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\Lambda<1\qquad\text{(exponential damping)}.

(293)

We now determine the parameter value at which this crossing occurs.
Along the relevant symmetric branch, the transition takes place when the dominant point reaches the unit circle.
The branch connected to the large-
\tau
regime crosses at
\xi=-1
.
Substituting
x=-1
into (
285
), we obtain

\displaystyle a^{2}+(\alpha+1+2\sigma^{2})a+\alpha=0.

(294)

Define

\displaystyle\Delta:=(\alpha+1+2\sigma^{2})^{2}-4\alpha.

(295)

Then the two roots of (
294
) are

\displaystyle a=-\frac{\alpha+1+2\sigma^{2}\pm\sqrt{\Delta}}{2}.

(296)

On the branch relevant to the dominant symmetric point, we must take

\displaystyle a_{c}=-\frac{\alpha+1+2\sigma^{2}-\sqrt{\Delta}}{2}.

(297)

It is convenient to write

\displaystyle a_{c}=-s,\qquad s:=\frac{\alpha+1+2\sigma^{2}-\sqrt{\Delta}}{2}>0.

(298)

We now substitute
a=-s
into the diagonal singularity equation (
288
).
This gives

\displaystyle s^{2}\Bigl[1+\beta_{c}(\alpha+1+2s)\Bigr]=\alpha,

(299)

where
\beta_{c}
denotes the value of
\beta
at the transition point.
Solving for
\beta_{c}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\beta_{c}=\frac{\alpha-s^{2}}{s^{2}(\alpha+1+2s)}.

(300)

To simplify this expression, note that
s
satisfies the quadratic equation obtained from (
294
),

\displaystyle s^{2}-(\alpha+1+2\sigma^{2})s+\alpha=0.

(301)

Hence

\displaystyle\alpha-s^{2}=s(\alpha+1+2\sigma^{2}-2s)=s\sqrt{\Delta},

(302)

and also

\displaystyle\alpha+1+2s=2\alpha+2+2\sigma^{2}-\sqrt{\Delta}.

(303)

Substituting these identities into (
300
), we find

\displaystyle\beta_{c}=\frac{2\sqrt{\Delta}}{(\alpha+1+2\sigma^{2}-\sqrt{\Delta})(2\alpha+2+2\sigma^{2}-\sqrt{\Delta})}.

(304)

Finally, since

\displaystyle\beta=\frac{1+\sigma^{2}}{\alpha(\tau-1)},

(305)

the critical value of
\tau
is determined by

\displaystyle\tau_{c}=1+\frac{1+\sigma^{2}}{\alpha\beta_{c}}.

(306)

Substituting the above expression for
\beta_{c}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\tau_{c}(\alpha,\sigma^{2})=1+\frac{(1+\sigma^{2})(\alpha+1+2\sigma^{2}-\sqrt{\Delta})(2\alpha+2+2\sigma^{2}-\sqrt{\Delta})}{2\alpha\sqrt{\Delta}}.

(307)

This is exactly the expression stated in Result
2
.

Appendix G

Derivation of Theorem
3

In this appendix, we derive the asymptotic scaling law stated in Theorem
3
.
We begin by restating the result.
See
3

Proof.

We work in the population-risk regime
\tau\to\infty
under ridgeless learning
\lambda=0
, and assume
\alpha>1
.
In this regime, the test-time generalization error can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t}=\int\left(1-\frac{s}{\alpha c}\right)^{2t}\mu_{\alpha}(ds),

(308)

where
c=1+(1+\sigma^{2})/\alpha
and
\mu_{\alpha}
is the Marchenko–Pastur law with aspect ratio
\alpha
. Since
\alpha>1
, there is no atom at the origin, and thus

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}(ds)=\frac{\sqrt{(s_{+}-s)(s-s_{-})}}{2\pi\alpha s}\,\mathbf{1}_{[s_{-},s_{+}]}(s)\,ds,\qquad s_{\pm}=(1\pm\sqrt{\alpha})^{2}.

(309)

We first identify the exponential rate from (
308
) and (
309
).
Since

\displaystyle\alpha c>s_{+}=(1+\sqrt{\alpha})^{2},

(310)

we have

\displaystyle 0<1-\frac{s}{\alpha c}<1\qquad(s\in[s_{-},s_{+}]).

(311)

Moreover, the map
s\mapsto 1-s/(\alpha c)
is strictly decreasing, so its maximum on the support
[s_{-},s_{+}]
is attained at the lower edge
s=s_{-}
. Define

\displaystyle r_{\alpha}:=1-\frac{s_{-}}{\alpha c}.

(312)

Using
s_{-}=(\sqrt{\alpha}-1)^{2}
and
\alpha c=\alpha+1+\sigma^{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle r_{\alpha}=\frac{2\sqrt{\alpha}+\sigma^{2}}{\alpha+1+\sigma^{2}}.

(313)

Thus
0<r_{\alpha}<1
, and this already gives the candidate exponential decay rate
r_{\alpha}^{2t}
.

Next, we determine the polynomial prefactor by analyzing the contribution near the lower edge.
Fix a small
\varepsilon>0
, and split (
308
) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t}=\int_{s_{-}}^{s_{-}+\varepsilon}\left(1-\frac{s}{\alpha c}\right)^{2t}\mu_{\alpha}(ds)+\int_{s_{-}+\varepsilon}^{s_{+}}\left(1-\frac{s}{\alpha c}\right)^{2t}\mu_{\alpha}(ds).

(314)

We begin with the edge contribution.
Write

\displaystyle s=s_{-}+x,\qquad x\in[0,\varepsilon].

(315)

Since

\displaystyle s-s_{-}=x,\qquad s_{+}-s=(s_{+}-s_{-})-x,

(316)

and
s_{-}=(\sqrt{\alpha}-1)^{2}>0

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{(s_{+}-s)(s-s_{-})}}{2\pi\alpha s}\asymp x^{1/2}\qquad(x\downarrow 0).

(317)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle 1-\frac{s}{\alpha c}=1-\frac{s_{-}+x}{\alpha c}=r_{\alpha}-\frac{x}{\alpha c}=r_{\alpha}\left(1-\frac{x}{\alpha c\,r_{\alpha}}\right).

(318)

Hence, by (
318

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{s}{\alpha c}\right)^{2t}=r_{\alpha}^{2t}\left(1-\frac{x}{\alpha c\,r_{\alpha}}\right)^{2t}.

(319)

For

\varepsilon

\displaystyle e^{-C_{2}tx}\leq\left(1-\frac{x}{\alpha c\,r_{\alpha}}\right)^{2t}\leq e^{-C_{1}tx}

(320)

for some constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0

t
.

\displaystyle\int_{s_{-}}^{s_{-}+\varepsilon}\left(1-\frac{s}{\alpha c}\right)^{2t}\mu_{\alpha}(ds)\asymp r_{\alpha}^{2t}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}e^{-Ctx}x^{1/2}\,dx,

(321)

317

C>0
is irrelevant for
\asymp
-estimates.
By the change of variables

, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}e^{-Ctx}x^{1/2}\,dx=t^{-3/2}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon t}e^{-Cu}u^{1/2}\,du\asymp t^{-3/2}.

(322)

Substituting (
322
) into (
321
), it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{s_{-}}^{s_{-}+\varepsilon}\left(1-\frac{s}{\alpha c}\right)^{2t}\mu_{\alpha}(ds)\asymp t^{-3/2}r_{\alpha}^{2t}.

(323)

We now show that the contribution away from the edge is exponentially smaller.
Because
s\mapsto 1-s/(\alpha c)
is continuous and strictly decreasing, there exists
\eta>0
such that

\displaystyle 1-\frac{s}{\alpha c}\leq r_{\alpha}-\eta\qquad(s\in[s_{-}+\varepsilon,s_{+}]).

(324)

Since
\mu_{\alpha}
is a finite measure, this yields

\displaystyle\int_{s_{-}+\varepsilon}^{s_{+}}\left(1-\frac{s}{\alpha c}\right)^{2t}\mu_{\alpha}(ds)\leq C(r_{\alpha}-\eta)^{2t}

(325)

for some constant
C>0
.
This term is negligible compared with (
323
).

Combining (
314

323

325

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t}\asymp t^{-3/2}r_{\alpha}^{2t}=t^{-3/2}\left(\frac{2\sqrt{\alpha}+\sigma^{2}}{\alpha+1+\sigma^{2}}\right)^{2t}.

(326)

This proves the first claim.

Finally, in the context-rich regime
\alpha\gg\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\frac{2\sqrt{\alpha}+\sigma^{2}}{\alpha+1+\sigma^{2}}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr),

(327)

and therefore, using (
313

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t}\asymp t^{-3/2}\left(\frac{4}{\alpha}\right)^{t}.

(328)

Appendix I

Experimental details

In this appendix, we summarize the experimental details for the fully learned
linear-attention and softmax-attention experiments in
Section
6
.
All experiments used synthetic linear-regression tasks with ambient dimension

D=50
.
Each task was defined by a ground-truth parameter

\mathbf{w}_{\mu}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},I_{D})
, and each in-context example was
generated as

\mathbf{x}_{\mu,l}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},I_{D}),\qquad y_{\mu,l}=\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{\mu,l}.

Thus, the experiments were noiseless.
We used the same prompt structure as in Section
3
, but
initialized the first parameter-estimate token with a random Gaussian vector.

I.1

Model architecture

Both models used a single residual self-attention layer acting on tokens in

\mathbb{R}^{2D+2}
.
For
D=50
, the token dimension was therefore
2D+2=102
.
To compute the next estimate from reasoning depth
t
, the prompt contained

L
example tokens followed by
t+1
parameter-estimate tokens,
corresponding to

[\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{0},\ldots,\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}].

The total number of tokens was therefore

T=L+t+1.

The prediction was always read from the parameter block of the final token,
which we denote by
\mathbf{z}_{T}
.

In both models, only the final token was updated according to

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{T}\mapsto\mathbf{z}_{T}+W_{V}\sum_{s=1}^{T}a_{s}\mathbf{z}_{s},

(342)

where
W_{K},W_{Q},W_{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2D+2)\times(2D+2)}
denote the key,
query, and value matrices.

In the fully learned linear-attention model, we used

\displaystyle a_{s}=m_{s}^{(t)}\frac{1}{L}\,\bigl(W_{K}\mathbf{z}_{s}\bigr)^{\top}\bigl(W_{Q}\mathbf{z}_{T}\bigr),

(343)

where
m_{s}^{(t)}
is a mask over source positions that determines which
source tokens are visible to the final token.

In the softmax-attention model, we used the same bilinear score, but normalized
it with a softmax over the visible source positions:

\displaystyle a_{s}=\frac{\exp\ab(\frac{\ab(W_{K}\mathbf{z}_{s})^{\top}\ab(W_{Q}\mathbf{z}_{T})}{\sqrt{2D+2}}+b_{s}^{(t)})}{\sum_{r=1}^{T}\exp\ab(\frac{\ab(W_{K}\mathbf{z}_{r})^{\top}\ab(W_{Q}\mathbf{z}_{T})}{\sqrt{2D+2}}+b_{r}^{(t)})},

(344)

where
b_{s}^{(t)}
is an additive attention mask.

For both the fully learned linear-attention and softmax-attention experiments,
we used a full-history visibility pattern for the final parameter-estimate
token.
That is, the final token was allowed to attend to all
L
example tokens and
all previously generated estimate tokens, but not to itself.
For the fully learned linear-attention model, this mask was

\displaystyle m_{s}^{(t)}=\begin{cases}1,&1\leq s\leq L+t,\\
0,&s=T.\end{cases}

(345)

For the softmax-attention model, the corresponding additive mask was

\displaystyle b_{s}^{(t)}=\begin{cases}0,&1\leq s\leq L+t,\\
-\infty,&s=T.\end{cases}

(346)

Thus, in both models, the visible source tokens consisted of the
L
example
tokens and the previous estimate tokens

[\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{0},\ldots,\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{t-1}]
, while the final query token

\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}
was masked from attending to itself.

I.2

Learning protocol

For each pair
(M,L)
, we first sampled a fixed training set of
M
tasks,
each with
L
in-context examples.
We then trained the model parameters on this fixed set by minimizing the MSE between the predicted parameter vector and the ground-truth task
parameter, with
\ell_{2}
regularization on all trainable matrices.

Training used a one-step objective.
For each sampled training task, we initialized the first parameter estimate

\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{0}
as a random Gaussian vector, constructed a prompt containing
the
L
example tokens and this single estimate token, and trained the model
to predict the ground-truth parameter
\mathbf{w}
from the updated final token.

Optimization used Adam with learning rate
10^{-3}
, minibatch size
300
,

3000
gradient steps, and regularization coefficient

\lambda=10^{-5}
.

I.3

Inference protocol

After training, we evaluated each model on newly sampled test tasks from the
same noiseless distribution.
For each test task, we initialized the first parameter estimate

\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{0}
as a random Gaussian vector and then iteratively reapplied
the same learned attention layer to generate a trajectory

(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.

At reasoning depth
t
, the prompt contained the
L
example tokens and the
history of generated estimates

[\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{0},\ldots,\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}].

The model updated the final estimate token to produce the next estimate

\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{t+1}
.
Under the full-history mask, the final token could attend to the example
tokens and the previous estimate tokens

[\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{0},\ldots,\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{t-1}]
, but not to itself.
Thus, both the fully learned linear-attention and softmax-attention models had
access to the full generated history during inference, except for the current
final token itself as an attention source.

We evaluated the MSE of the parameter estimate

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t}=\frac{1}{D}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\mathbf{w}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\|^{2}\bigr]

(347)

for reasoning depth
t
.
Evaluation used
4096
independently sampled test tasks for each trained
model.
