Guidewire–bile duct angle as a determinant of stent delivery success in EUS-guided hepaticogastrostomy: a prospective double-lumen dilator cohort with propensity score-matched historical controls
Authors: Naoto Aoyama, Takeshi Ogura, Yoshitaro Yamamoto, Saori Ueno, Atsushi Okuda, Nobu Nishioka, Junichi Nakamura, Nobuhiro Hattori, Kimi Bessho, Takafumi Kanadani, Nga Nguyen Trong, Ly Kim Chhun, Ahmad F Aboelezz, Yi-Jun Liao, Szu-Chia Liao, Hiroki Nishikawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Failure of stent delivery remains a major technical limitation of endoscopic ultrasound-guided hepaticogastrostomy (EUS-HGS), often necessitating additional tract dilation and increasing procedural complexity and cost.Objectives:The aim of this study was to evaluate whether the guidewire–bile duct angle is associated with stent delivery success in EUS-HGS, and to explore whether a double-lumen dilator (DLD) is associated with procedural improvement consistent with an angle-mediated mechanism rather than direct mechanical advantage.Design:Prospective observational study with a propensity score-matched historical control.Methods:This was a prospective single-arm observational study of patients undergoing EUS-HGS using a DLD, compared with a propensity score-matched historical (retrospective) cohort that underwent conventional dilation. The main outcome was successful stent delivery without additional tract dilation.Results:A total of 25 patients in the DLD group were compared with 25 matched controls. Successful stent delivery without additional dilation was significantly higher in the DLD group than in the conventional group (92% vs 48%, p = 0.001). The guidewire–bile duct angle measured immediately before stent delivery-system insertion was significantly larger in the DLD group than in the conventional group (153.4° vs 123.3°, p < 0.001). On multivariable analysis, a larger guidewire–bile duct angle remained independently associated with successful stent delivery without additional tract dilation (odds ratio per 10-degree increase, 5.49; 95% confidence interval, 2.79–10.83; p < 0.001), whereas DLD use itself was not independently associated with success after adjustment for angle. Device-related costs were lower in the DLD group due to reduced need for additional dilation. The guidewire–bile duct angle showed excellent apparent discrimination in this cohort, with an exploratory threshold of 126° (AUC 0.969), requiring external validation before clinical implementation.Conclusion:In conclusion, the improvement associated with DLD use was consistent with an angle-mediated mechanism rather than a direct effect of dilation force; these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating mechanistic evidence rather than mechanistic proof.Keywords: bile peritonitis, dilator, endoscopic ultrasound-guided hepaticogastrostomy, ERCP, EUS-HGS

Introduction

An endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided approach such as EUS-guided hepaticogastrostomy (HGS) is now widely attempted as an alternative technique for endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP).1 –7 Technical tips for EUS-HGS can be divided into four steps: bile duct puncture, guidewire deployment, tract dilation, and stent deployment. Sufficient tract dilation may be associated with stent delivery system insertion. To achieve successful tract dilation, penetration to insert the dilation device and the dilation force to insert the stent delivery system should be considered during dilation device selection. In addition, we must also pay attention to preventing bile leakage after tract dilation. As another factor, the angle between the guidewire and the bile duct may also be associated with successful stent delivery system insertion. 8 The double-guidewire (DGW) may be useful to increase this angle.9,10 However, to perform the DGW technique, a dedicated ERCP catheter should be inserted prior to tract dilation; therefore, procedure time may be prolonged, and its cost is also high. Previous approaches have focused primarily on device selection to improve tract dilation; however, the geometric relationship between the guidewire and bile duct, particularly the guidewire–duct angle, may be a more fundamental determinant of successful stent delivery. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate whether the guidewire–bile duct angle is associated with stent delivery success in EUS-HGS, and to explore whether the benefit observed with a double-lumen dilator (DLD) is consistent with an angle-mediated mechanism rather than a direct effect of dilation force.

Patients and methods

This single-arm prospective observational study with a propensity score-matched historical comparison evaluated the clinical outcomes of EUS-HGS using a novel DLD (Meissa, type OT, Japan Life Line, Tokyo, Japan). Consecutive patients treated between January and October 2025 were prospectively enrolled and compared with a historical cohort treated using conventional dilation devices from January 2022 to December 2024. To minimize selection bias, propensity score matching was performed. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age >18 years and (2) failed or anatomically inaccessible ERCP, including patients with surgically altered anatomy or duodenal stenosis in whom transpapillary access was not feasible. In all included patients, transpapillary biliary drainage by ERCP had been attempted or was deemed technically infeasible at the index procedure. The exclusion criteria were: (1) total gastrectomy, (2) presence of ascites, (3) uncontrolled bleeding tendency, defined as platelet count <50,000/μL, PT-INR >1.5, clinically significant uncorrected coagulopathy, or inability to safely discontinue or manage antithrombotic agents before EUS-HGS according to institutional periprocedural practice; and (4) refusal to participate in this study. The IRB approval covered both the prospective DLD cohort and the retrospective comparative analysis with the historical control cohort. The reporting of this study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement. 11

Devices and procedure protocol

Figure 1(a) shows the DLD, and Figure 1(b–d) show technical tips for EUS-HGS using the DLD (DLD group). The intrahepatic bile duct is punctured using a 19-G needle across sufficient hepatic parenchyma. 12 Then, a 0.025-inch guidewire is inserted through the needle into the biliary system using the physician-controlled guidewire technique. Subsequently, DLD insertion into the biliary tract is attempted. If DLD insertion fails, another dilation device is used. After successful DLD insertion, bile juice aspiration or additional contrast medium injection is performed, if necessary. An additional 0.025-inch guidewire is deployed into the biliary tract via the side hole of the DLD. Next, 8.5-Fr stent delivery system insertion (8 mm × 12 cm Spring Stopper; Taewoong Medical, Seoul, Korea) is attempted, and stent deployment from the intrahepatic bile duct to the stomach is also performed using intrascope channel release technique. If the stent delivery system insertion fails, another dilation device is used. In patients who undergo EUS-HGS using a conventional dilation device (conventional group), a 4-mm balloon catheter (REN biliary balloon catheter; KANEKA, Osaka, Japan), a bougie dilator (7Fr, ES dilator; Zeon Medical Inc., Tokyo, Japan), or a drill dilator (Tornus ES; Asahi Intecc, Aichi, Japan) is used. During stent delivery-system insertion, adjustment of the endoscope position was attempted when resistance was encountered. Additional tract dilation was performed only when stent delivery remained unsuccessful after routine endoscope position adjustment. Procedures were performed by or under the direct supervision of experienced therapeutic endoscopists. The DGW technique was not used in any case in the conventional dilation group.

Outcome measurements and definitions

The main outcome was the success rate of stent delivery system insertion without additional tract dilation. The additional analyses were performed to compare clinical outcomes between the DLD and conventional groups. Clinical success was defined as a 50% decrease in bilirubin within 2 weeks after stent insertion, or achieving a value of less than 25% of pre-procedure bilirubin levels within 4 weeks after stent insertion, in accordance with the Tokyo Criteria 2024 for biliary drainage. Adverse events were graded according to the severity grading system of the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy lexicon. 13 Bile peritonitis was defined as new-onset abdominal pain after EUS-HGS accompanied by elevation of inflammatory markers and/or imaging findings consistent with intra-abdominal bile collection. Cholangitis was defined according to the Tokyo Guidelines criteria, requiring evidence of systemic inflammation, cholestasis, and biliary imaging findings. The grade of each adverse event (mild, moderate, severe) was assigned according to the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) lexicon. In this study, an expert-operator status was defined as an endoscopist with substantial experience in both therapeutic ERCP and interventional EUS, including experience with at least 33 EUS-HGS/EUS-BD procedures, based on previous learning-curve data. 14

The diameter of the bile duct and the length of hepatic parenchyma at the puncture site were measured on EUS. The guidewire–bile duct angle was assessed on fluoroscopic still images. All EUS-HGS procedures were performed with the patient in the prone position, and fluoroscopic images were obtained in a standard frontal projection with the X-ray beam directed perpendicular to the patient. Because this angle represents a three-dimensional relationship in clinical practice, fluoroscopic measurement was regarded as a two-dimensional surrogate. Three-dimensional reconstruction was not available in this study; therefore, no geometric correction for the three-dimensional direction or puncture site was performed. To standardize the measurement, the angle was defined as the angle between the tangent line of the intrahepatic bile duct axis at the puncture site and the axis of the guidewire segment entering the bile duct. In the conventional dilation group, the angle was measured after guidewire placement and after initial tract dilation. In the DLD group, the angle was measured after single-guidewire placement and after deployment of the second guidewire. For the primary comparative and predictive analyses, the angle measured immediately before stent delivery-system insertion was used. Two experienced endoscopists independently re-measured all angles according to this predefined protocol while blinded to clinical outcomes and the original measurements. The final value used for analysis was the mean of the two measurements. Interobserver reproducibility was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient. Compared with single guidewire (Figure 2(a) and (b)), the angle was larger after DGW deployment (Figure 2(c) and (d)). Bile leakage during EUS-HGS was evaluated on EUS images. The assessment was conducted at every device exchange until the next device insertion. If obvious bile leakage was observed on EUS, it was defined as positive. In cases of an uncertain diagnosis, color Doppler was used, and the presence of flow was considered indicative of bile leakage. Post-procedural CT was routinely performed on the day after EUS-HGS in all patients to evaluate intra-abdominal fluid collection. Bile leakage detected on EUS during the procedure and post-procedural fluid collection detected on CT were evaluated separately. Procedure time was measured from bile duct puncture to successful stent deployment.

Statistical analysis

Since the aim of the present study was to evaluate technical feasibility, a sample size of 24 patients was deemed necessary on the basis of an exact binomial single-arm design. Assuming an expected technical success rate of 90% with a clinically unacceptable lower bound of 70%, a one-sided alpha of 0.05 and power of 0.80, the required sample size was 24, consistent with prior feasibility studies of EUS-HGS device evaluation. 15 To allow for possible patient dropout, it was estimated that a final sample size of 25 patients in the DLD group would be required. Propensity score matching was performed using a logistic regression model including seven baseline variables available before tract dilation: sex, age ⩾75 years, indication for EUS-HGS (duodenal stenosis vs surgically altered anatomy), malignant versus benign disease, puncture site (B3 vs B2), bile duct diameter ⩾3 mm, and hepatic parenchymal length ⩽25 mm. One-to-one nearest-neighbor matching without replacement was performed using a caliper width of 0.2, and the resulting 25 matched pairs were included in the comparative analysis. Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (range) for continuous variables after assessing normality with the Shapiro–Wilk test (mean ± SD when normally distributed; otherwise median with range or interquartile range). Performance status and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score were treated as ordinal categorical variables and are presented as median (range) without assuming equal intervals between categories. Categorical variables are presented as n (%). Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test (for normally distributed data) or the Mann–Whitney U test (for non-normally distributed data), as appropriate, and categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. Differences with a p-value <0.05 were considered significant.

Interobserver reproducibility of guidewire–bile duct angle measurement was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient based on a two-way random-effects absolute-agreement single-measure model. To evaluate the clinical relevance of guidewire–bile duct angulation, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed in the overall cohort to assess the ability of the guidewire–bile duct angle to predict successful stent delivery without additional tract dilation. The optimal cut-off value was determined using the Youden index. The area under the curve (AUC) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using bootstrap resampling (10,000 iterations). In addition, multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted in the overall cohort to identify independent factors associated with successful stent delivery without additional tract dilation. Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and availability before stent delivery and included guidewire–bile duct angle, use of the DLD, bile duct diameter at the puncture site, hepatic parenchymal length at the puncture site, and puncture site (B3 vs B2). The guidewire–bile duct angle was modeled as a continuous variable (per 10-degree increase). Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were calculated. Given the limited sample size of the propensity score-matched cohort, multivariable analysis was not performed in the matched cohort to avoid model instability, and these analyses were restricted to the overall cohort. In addition, to evaluate whether the association between the guidewire–bile duct angle and stent delivery success was not solely driven by between-group differences, a conventional-group-only analysis was performed using ROC analysis and logistic regression. To address potential confounding by operator-related factors, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed by adding expert-operator status to the multivariable logistic regression model for successful stent delivery without additional tract dilation. A conventional-group-only sensitivity analysis including guidewire–bile duct angle and expert-operator status was also performed to evaluate whether the association between angle and stent delivery success was not solely driven by between-group differences.

Economic evaluation

A procedure-level cost-effectiveness analysis was performed from the hospital procedural perspective. Effectiveness was defined as successful stent delivery without additional tract dilation. This outcome was considered clinically meaningful because it captures procedural simplification, reduced need for rescue device use, and improved technical efficiency during EUS-HGS. The cost outcome for the cost-effectiveness plane was the incremental additional device-related cost required during the procedure. Additional device-related costs included the cost of the second guidewire used in the DLD group and any rescue dilation devices used after failed initial stent delivery in either group. The initial dilation device cost was not included in the cost-effectiveness plan because it was protocol-determined once the initial dilation strategy had been selected. Total device-related cost, calculated as the sum of the initial dilation device cost and additional device-related cost, is reported separately in Table 4. Costs related to hospital stay, management of adverse events, reinterventions, and indirect costs were not included. This restricted cost scope was chosen because the primary purpose of the economic analysis was to evaluate the direct procedural impact of device selection on resource use during EUS-HGS.

Results

Characteristics of patients in the DLD group (prospective cohort)

A total of 25 patients (11 men; median age 75 years) were enrolled in this study (Table 1). The primary disease was mainly pancreatobiliary carcinoma (n = 18), followed by bile duct stones (n = 5). The reason for EUS-HGS was mainly surgically altered anatomy (n = 16), followed by duodenal stenosis (n = 9). The mean diameter of the bile duct puncture site was 3.4 mm.

Clinical outcomes in the DLD group (prospective cohort)

The main puncture site was B3 (88%, n = 22/25; Table 2). The mean length of hepatic parenchyma at the puncture site was 21.6 mm. The technical success rate of initial DLD insertion was 100% (25/25). The mean angle between the guidewire and the bile duct at the puncture site was 107.4° with a single guidewire; however, after additional guidewire deployment, this angle was increased to 153.4°. After DLD, stent delivery system insertion was successful in 92% of patients (23/25). In cases of failed stent delivery system insertion, after additional tract dilation using a 4-mm balloon or drill dilator, all patients underwent successful EUS-HGS. Regarding adverse events, although mild bile peritonitis (n = 1) and cholangitis (n = 1) were observed, conservative treatment was successful in these patients. Mean initial, additional, and total device-related costs were 413.3, 34.08, and 447.4 USD, respectively.

Patients’ characteristics and clinical outcomes in the conventional dilation group (retrospective cohort)

A total of 90 patients (53 men; median age 81 years) were enrolled (Table 3). As dilation devices, a 4-mm balloon catheter (n = 51) was used most often, followed by a drill dilator (n = 25) and a bougie dilator (n = 14). The technical success rate of dilation device insertion into the biliary tract was 100%. The mean guidewire–bile duct angle was 124.3° at guidewire placement and 126.6° immediately before stent delivery-system insertion after initial tract dilation. The technical success rate of stent delivery system insertion after initial tract dilation was 58.9% (53/90). Therefore, additional tract dilation was needed in 37 patients. Additional tract dilation was performed using a 4-mm balloon catheter in 28 patients and a 6-mm balloon catheter in 9 patients. After additional tract dilation, stent delivery system insertion was successful in all patients. Successful stent delivery without additional tract dilation according to the initial dilation device was achieved in 82.4% of patients treated with a balloon catheter, 24.0% treated with a drill dilator, and 35.7% treated with a bougie dilator. During EUS-HGS, bile leakage on EUS images was observed in 18.9% of patients (17/90). The mean procedure time was 16.7 min, and the clinical success rate was 97.8% (88/90). Finally, adverse events such as bile peritonitis (n = 15) and cholangitis (n = 1) were observed, but all patients recovered with conservative treatment.

Reproducibility of guidewire–bile duct angle measurement

All guidewire–bile duct angles were independently re-measured by two experienced endoscopists using the standardized fluoroscopic measurement protocol. Interobserver reproducibility was excellent. In the conventional dilation group, the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was 0.994 (95% CI, 0.992–0.996) at guidewire placement and 0.990 (95% CI, 0.982–0.996) after tract dilation. In the DLD group, the ICC was 0.994 (95% CI, 0.985–0.998) after single-guidewire placement and 0.996 (95% CI, 0.989–0.998) after DGW deployment. The re-measured values were used for the revised analyses, and the main results were not materially changed. Interobserver reproducibility data are summarized in Supplemental Table 2.

Propensity score-matched analysis of patients’ characteristics between the DLD and conventional dilation groups

After propensity score matching, 25 patients were included in each group (Table 4). The guidewire–bile duct angle measured immediately before stent delivery-system insertion was significantly larger in the DLD group than in the conventional group (153.4° vs 123.3°, p < 0.001). The technical success rate of stent delivery system insertion after initial tract dilation was significantly higher in the DLD group than in the conventional group (92% vs 48%, p = 0.001). Bile leakage during EUS-HGS on EUS images and bile peritonitis were both less frequent in the DLD group (4% vs 24% for each outcome), although these differences did not reach statistical significance after matching (both p = 0.098). Procedure time was not significantly different between the two groups. Post-procedural CT performed on the day after EUS-HGS showed no new intra-abdominal fluid collection in any patient. No cases of bile leakage were detected only after the procedure by post-procedural imaging.

Cost-effectiveness analysis

In the propensity score-matched cohort, bootstrap analysis (10,000 iterations) demonstrated a mean incremental effectiveness of +0.44 and a mean incremental additional device-related cost of −170.7 USD. The 95% CIs were 0.20–0.64 for effectiveness and −270.0 to −72.6 USD for cost. Nearly all bootstrap replications were located in the southeast quadrant of the cost-effectiveness plane, indicating that the DLD strategy was associated with both lower additional device-related cost and greater effectiveness (Figure 3).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis for successful stent delivery without additional tract dilation

ROC analysis using the guidewire–bile duct angle measured immediately before stent delivery-system insertion demonstrated excellent apparent discrimination for predicting successful stent delivery without additional tract dilation, with an AUC of 0.969 (bootstrap 95% CI, 0.931–0.996; Table 5 and Figure 4). The optimal cut-off value determined by the Youden index was 126°, yielding a sensitivity of 90.8% and an apparent specificity of 100.0%. At this exploratory threshold, successful stent delivery without additional tract dilation was achieved in 69 of 69 patients with an angle ⩾126° and in 7 of 46 patients with an angle <126°; the corresponding confusion matrix is shown in Supplemental Table 1. The distribution of guidewire–bile duct angles according to stent delivery outcome is shown in Supplemental Figure 1.

In multivariable logistic regression analysis, the guidewire–bile duct angle remained independently associated with successful stent delivery without additional tract dilation (OR per 10-degree increase, 5.49; 95% CI, 2.79–10.83; p < 0.001), whereas DLD use itself was not independently associated with success after adjustment for angle and other covariates. In an operator-adjusted sensitivity analysis, the guidewire–bile duct angle remained independently associated with successful stent delivery without additional tract dilation after adding expert-operator status to the multivariable model (OR per 10-degree increase, 5.50; 95% CI, 2.79–10.84; p < 0.001). Expert-operator status was not independently associated with successful stent delivery (OR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.24–4.43; p = 0.966). In the conventional-group-only analysis, the guidewire–bile duct angle also remained significantly associated with success (OR per 10-degree increase, 4.66; 95% CI, 2.50–8.67; p < 0.001), whereas expert-operator status was not significantly associated with success (OR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.21–3.50; p = 0.836; Supplemental Table 3).

Because the exploratory 126° threshold showed an apparent specificity of 100.0%, Firth penalized logistic regression was performed as a sensitivity analysis to address potential quasi-separation and model instability. This analysis showed consistent results: the guidewire–bile duct angle remained independently associated with successful stent delivery without additional tract dilation (OR per 10-degree increase, 4.27; 95% CI, 2.45–7.45; p < 0.001), whereas DLD use itself was not independently associated with success after adjustment for angle and other covariates.

In a conventional-group-only analysis, the guidewire–bile duct angle still showed excellent apparent discrimination for successful stent delivery without additional tract dilation, with an AUC of 0.955. The optimal threshold was 126°, with a sensitivity of 86.8% and an apparent specificity of 100.0%. In logistic regression restricted to the conventional dilation group, the angle remained significantly associated with success (OR per 10-degree increase, 4.66; 95% CI, 2.50–8.68; p < 0.001).

Discussion

The present data support a three-step framework linking device selection to procedural success through guidewire–bile duct angle. First, DLD use was associated with a substantially larger guidewire–bile duct angle immediately before stent delivery-system insertion than conventional dilation (153.4° vs 123.3°; p < 0.001). Second, the guidewire–bile duct angle was the strongest independent procedural determinant of stent delivery success in multivariable logistic regression (OR per 10° increase, 5.49; 95% CI, 2.79–10.83; p < 0.001). Third, after adjustment for angle, the association between DLD use and procedural success was substantially attenuated and no longer statistically significant (p = 0.944), a pattern consistent with an angle-mediated mechanism. We acknowledge, however, that DLD use and DGW deployment are inseparable in this design and that formal causal mediation analysis was not performed; the present findings should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating mechanistic evidence rather than mechanistic proof. A more obtuse guidewire–bile duct angle reduces angular discordance between the delivery trajectory and the bile duct axis, thereby improving coaxial alignment, pushability, and reducing axial resistance during stent advancement. The clinical consequences may be relevant, although not statistically significant in the matched cohort: bile peritonitis occurred in 24% of patients in the conventional dilation group versus 4% in the DLD group (p = 0.098), a sixfold numerical absolute difference that did not reach statistical significance after matching. In the unmatched cohort, bile peritonitis was observed in 4% (1/25) of the DLD group and 16.7% (15/90) of the conventional group, supporting a directionally consistent reduction in adverse events with the DLD strategy. This reduction is consistent with the hypothesis that angle optimization reduces failed delivery attempts requiring traumatic re-instrumentation, which predisposes to peritonitis. Achieving a guidewire–bile duct angle of approximately 126° may serve as a candidate intra-procedural target requiring prospective validation during EUS-HGS, irrespective of device selection. However, the apparent specificity of 100.0% at the 126° threshold should be interpreted cautiously because this threshold was derived and evaluated in the same cohort. Therefore, this threshold should be considered an exploratory intra-procedural target rather than a validated predictive cut-off.

For tract dilation, two factors need to be considered. First, the dilation device should be inserted into the bile duct across the stomach and bile duct wall, which is called the penetration function. 16 Penetration function may be associated with the hardness and sharpness of the tip of the dilator. From the perspective of this theory, a DLD may be ideal. In the present study, initial DLD insertion was successful in all patients; however, stent delivery without additional tract dilation failed in two patients. These failures were successfully managed with additional dilation using a balloon catheter or drill dilator. A drill dilator or an electrocautery dilator can resolve this issue,17,18 but there are several concerns regarding these devices, such as requiring additional dilation to insert a self-expanding metal stent or the risk of bleeding. Therefore, further randomized trials comparing various dilation devices are needed to clarify which dilation device is the most appropriate.

Second, dilation force may play an important role in achieving the technical success of the stent delivery system insertion. According to an experimental study of dilation force, 16 a balloon catheter has a higher dilation force than a bougie, drill, or ERCP catheter. However, because a balloon catheter can dilate not only the stomach or bile duct wall but also the hepatic parenchyma, this technique has the risk of bile leakage. To prevent bile leakage, biliary access should be obtained across sufficient hepatic parenchyma, at least over 25 mm, to obtain a tamponade effect. 12 However, this might sometimes be challenging. Indeed, in the present study, the mean length of hepatic parenchyma at the puncture site was about 22 mm in both groups. In contrast, other dilation devices such as a drill or bougie dilator can prevent excessive tract dilation due to their low dilation force. However, if stent delivery system insertion fails, additional tract dilation is needed. As a result, procedure time may be prolonged, and the risk of adverse events may increase.

The DLD is best understood as an implementation tool for angle optimization. Its principal contribution is the capacity to deliver a second guidewire through a dedicated side lumen during simultaneous tract creation, increasing the guidewire–bile duct angle from a mean of 107.4° to 153.4°—thereby establishing the geometric conditions required for successful stent delivery. It should be acknowledged that the guidewire–bile duct angle can also be modified by other maneuvers, including endoscope position adjustment after EUS-guided puncture and conventional DGW technique using a separate ERCP catheter. The DLD is therefore not the only means of achieving angle optimization; rather, it is one practical implementation that allows simultaneous tract creation and second-guidewire deployment without exchanging devices. Because the DLD inherently enables DGW deployment, the device effect and the second-guidewire effect are inseparable in this study, and the present results should be interpreted as supporting an angle-mediated mechanism rather than as evidence of a unique advantage of the DLD itself. The resulting safety benefit was numerically larger in the DLD group, although this difference did not reach statistical significance after matching: bile peritonitis occurred in 4% of the DLD group versus 24% of the conventional dilation group (p = 0.098). The lower frequency of bile leakage in the DLD group may have resulted from several factors, including bile/contrast aspiration after DLD insertion, controlled mechanical tract dilation without excessive balloon expansion, reduced need for additional dilation or repeated device exchange, and DGW-mediated angle optimization. In particular, a more favorable guidewire–bile duct angle may have reduced failed stent delivery attempts and subsequent re-instrumentation, thereby decreasing opportunities for bile leakage. However, these mechanisms could not be separated in the present clinical dataset, and further experimental studies and prospective randomized trials are required to determine the relative contribution of each factor. Device-related costs were lower in the DLD group as a secondary consequence of fewer additional dilation procedures required; however, this analysis captures only direct procedural device costs and should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than a full health economic evaluation. Future research should evaluate angle-optimization strategies more broadly—including alternative DGW techniques and scope-based angle adjustment—to determine whether the safety and efficacy benefits are attributable to angle achievement rather than device-specific properties.

The present study has several important limitations. First, this was a non-randomized study comparing a prospective DLD cohort with a retrospective historical control, and residual confounding cannot be excluded despite propensity score matching. In addition, because the conventional group was used as a historical control, differences in treatment period, operator experience, device availability, and procedural strategy may have influenced the comparative results. Second, the sample size was small, limiting statistical power for secondary endpoints. Third, the cost analysis was restricted to direct device-related procedural costs and did not incorporate costs of adverse event management or reinterventions; this finding should be interpreted as exploratory. In addition, although the cost of the second guidewire required for DLD use was already included in the incremental cost calculation, the cost benefit of the DLD strategy is largely driven by avoidance of rescue dilation devices in cases of failed initial dilation; in patients whose single-guidewire angle already exceeds the 126° threshold, the probability of needing rescue devices is low and the additional cost of deploying a second guidewire may not be justified. A strategy of measuring the single-guidewire angle first and selectively applying DGW techniques only when the angle is below the threshold therefore warrants prospective evaluation. Fourth, the conventional dilation cohort was retrospective and heterogeneous, including balloon, drill, and bougie dilators with different mechanical properties and dilation forces. In our previous randomized study comparing balloon and drill dilators for EUS-HGS, successful stent delivery without additional dilation was higher with balloon dilation. 17 Therefore, the relatively low single-device success rate in the conventional cohort may partly reflect the inclusion of drill and bougie dilators, and the comparison may have been biased in favor of the DLD group. Accordingly, the present findings should not be interpreted as demonstrating the superiority of the DLD over all conventional dilation strategies, particularly balloon dilation. In addition, because the DLD inherently enables DGW deployment, the device effect, second-guidewire effect, and guidewire–bile duct angle effect could not be evaluated independently in this dataset. Similarly, the device-cost findings should be regarded as exploratory because they may have been influenced by the greater need for rescue dilation devices in the historical conventional cohort. Although all procedures were performed in the prone position and fluoroscopic images were obtained in a standardized frontal projection with the X-ray beam directed perpendicular to the patient, the guidewire–bile duct angle was measured using two-dimensional fluoroscopic images and may not fully reflect the true three-dimensional relationship among the guidewire, bile duct, and endoscope axis. Although two independent expert reviewers re-measured all angles using a standardized protocol and interobserver reproducibility was excellent, projection-related measurement bias cannot be completely eliminated. In addition, the 126° threshold was derived and evaluated in the same cohort and may therefore overestimate discriminatory performance, particularly the apparent specificity of 100.0%. External validation is required before clinical implementation. Although the puncture site was included in the propensity score matching and multivariable models, residual bias related to differences in the three-dimensional bile duct course between B2 and B3 punctures cannot be completely excluded, particularly because the number of B2 puncture cases was limited. Operator-related factors may also have influenced stent delivery success. Although an operator-adjusted sensitivity analysis including expert-operator status showed that the guidewire–bile duct angle remained independently associated with successful stent delivery, this variable may not fully capture all aspects of operator skill, endoscope manipulation, or the timing and extent of expert intervention. Therefore, residual confounding by operator-related factors cannot be completely excluded.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the improvement in procedural success associated with DLD use was consistent with an angle-mediated mechanism via simultaneous DGW deployment, rather than a direct effect of dilation force. The guidewire–bile duct angle measured immediately before stent delivery may serve as a candidate intra-procedural target, but the exploratory threshold identified in this study requires external validation.
