 1. ABSTRACT

Raising turbine inlet temperature is a key lever for improving industrial gas-turbine efficiency and
power output, but it increases thermo-mechanical demands on hot-gas-path components. Additive
manufacturing (AM), particularly laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF), enables complex internal
cooling features in critical components such as turbine tip shoes that are difficult to produce by
conventional casting. However, qualification of new high-temperature AM alloys and aggressive
geometries is often limited by trial-and-error iteration of build parameters and post-build heat
treatments, with cracking during stress relieving or hot isostatic pressing (HIP) being a recurring
failure mode.

This project established a physics-informed computational–experimental workflow to evaluate the
processability of Carpenter Technology Corporation (CTC)’s GammaPrint-1100™ alloy for L-
PBF components, with emphasis on predicting residual stress, distortion, and HIP-induced micro-
cracking. In the first part of project, multiple sets of simplified GammaPrint-1100™ artifacts were
fabricated to calibrate and validate ORNL’s AM process model. Measured melt-pool dimensions
were used to calibrate key phenomenological inputs, while residual-stress-driven deflections
measured after wire-EDM cutting of samples provided quantitative validation of residual stress
and distortion predictions. In the second part of project, the framework was extended to simulate
stress redistribution during HIP and to incorporate precipitation strengthening during the HIP
thermal cycle. γ′ precipitation evolution predicted via TC-PRISMA was reduced to an efficient
coarsening model and coupled to a yield-strength formulation to enable precipitation-aware
thermo-mechanical simulation. A dedicated set of residual-stress cracking artifacts with various
levels of residual stress concentration was designed and tested to support damage-criterion
selection and parameter calibration. The resulting toolset enables rapid screening of print–HIP
strategies and provides actionable guidance for geometry and process tuning to reduce cracking
propensity in high-temperature AM turbine components.

4.2 L-PBF AM Process Thermal-Mechanical Model

The L-PBF AM process model was developed at ORNL and is described in detail in Refs. [2,3].
The simulation setup, along with the initial and boundary conditions applied for the L-PBF
process, is illustrated in Fig. 4. For modeling the GammaPrint-1100™ test artifacts, two categories
of input parameters are required: physics-based (material) parameters and phenomenological
parameters.

The physics-based parameters for GammaPrint-1100™ were provided by CTC as temperature-
dependent datasets, including density, specific heat, thermal conductivity, coefficient of thermal
expansion (CTE), elastic modulus, yield stress, and strain-hardening exponent. In addition, the
solidus and liquidus temperatures, latent heat, emissivity, convective heat-transfer coefficient, and
Poisson’s ratio were collected and treated as temperature-insensitive constants in the model.
Powder material properties were represented using effective values. Specifically, the powder-bed
density was assumed to be 66% of the bulk solid density to reflect packing effects, and the powder
thermal conductivity was assumed to be 1% of the bulk value, consistent with common practice in
the literature (e.g., Ref. [4]). The key phenomenological parameter in the thermal model is the
laser power absorption ratio, which was calibrated by matching the simulated melt-pool
dimensions to measurements from the fabricated short-block artifacts, as shown in Fig. 5.

model prediction

4.3 Hot Isostatic Pressing (HIP) Tests And FE Simulation

In the second phase of the project, we investigated crack formation in additively manufactured
GammaPrint-1100™ during post-build thermal processing, with a particular focus on hot isostatic
pressing (HIP). HIP was carried out by placing as-printed specimens in a sealed, high-pressure
vessel and using argon as the pressure-transmitting medium to impose a uniform hydrostatic
compressive pressure of 100 MPa. The HIP thermal cycle consisted of a linear ramp from room
temperature to 1176.7°C at 32°C/min, an isothermal hold at 1176.7°C for 4 hours, and subsequent
air cooling.

To interrogate the thermo-mechanical drivers of cracking under HIP, finite element (FE)
simulations were performed to replicate the HIP conditions by applying the same hydrostatic
pressure boundary condition and temperature-time history described above. The simulation setup
is shown in Fig. 8a. Critically, the HIP analysis did not start from an idealized stress-free state: the
 7

spatially heterogeneous residual-stress and plastic-strain fields predicted by the L-PBF process
simulation were transferred to the HIP model and applied as initial conditions (Fig. 8b). As
temperature increases during the simulated HIP ramp, the material’s yield strength decreases
substantially, enabling relaxation of the as-built residual stresses and driving redistribution of the
stress field within the component. This coupling between the inherited as-built state and the HIP
thermal-mechanical response provides the basis for assessing crack initiation tendencies during
post-build HIP processing.

Fig. 8. (a) FE mode setup and boundary conditions for HIP simulation, and (b) the initial residual
stress distribution.

Unlike the L-PBF process, where steep thermal gradients and rapid cooling suppress precipitation,
the HIP thermal cycle evolves sufficiently slowly to enable 𝛾′ precipitation. During the heating
stage, 𝛾′ precipitates can nucleate and rapidly coarsen over a characteristic intermediate-
temperature window, increasing the flow strength through precipitate strengthening.

For a precipitation-free material, residual stresses generally relax during thermal exposure because
elevated temperature promotes dislocation recovery and annihilation, enabling plastic
accommodation and reducing internal stresses. In contrast, when precipitation occurs during the
thermal cycle, the resulting 𝛾′ population impedes dislocation motion and can significantly retard
stress relaxation. Consequently, high local residual stresses may be retained and can promote
damage mechanisms such as micro-voiding or brittle cracking in susceptible microstructural

 8

regions. For this reason, the influence of 𝛾′ precipitates on yield strength is explicitly included in
the constitutive description.

4.4 Gleeble Tensile Tests

To quantify the effect of γ′ precipitation on mechanical response, Carpenter conducted a series of
thermo-mechanical tensile tests using a Gleeble rapid-heating system. The test concept and thermal
histories are illustrated in Fig. 8. The measured results (Fig. 9) show a clear dependence of strength
on heating rate: higher temperature ramp rates produce lower ultimate tensile strength (UTS),
which is consistent with reduced γ′ precipitation during the heating and loading sequence. In
particular, tests conducted at a 1000°C/s ramp rate are considered sufficiently rapid that the
material remains effectively precipitate-free during tensile deformation.

 9

To estimate γ′ precipitation during the remaining Gleeble conditions and during HIP, Carpenter
performed a suite of TC-PRISMA simulations to compute the evolution of γ′ precipitate radius
and volume fraction as functions of temperature and holding time (Fig. 10). These TC-PRISMA
predictions were selectively benchmarked against available SEM-based measurements of γ′
precipitate size to provide validation. The validated TC-PRISMA outputs were then reduced to a
simplified Lifshitz–Slyozov–Wagner (LSW) coarsening model through curve fitting (Fig. 11).
This reduced-order LSW representation enables efficient integration into the FE framework,
allowing γ′ evolution to be estimated under arbitrary thermal histories without repeated TC-
PRISMA calculations.

Finally, Using the LSW model to predict γ′ precipitate for each Gleeble thermal history, and
substituting these values into the UTS relation in Eq. (3), the precipitate-strengthening contribution
in the Gladman yield-stress model (Eq. (1)) was calibrated. Using the same precipitation model
and applied to the HIP thermal cycle, γ′ evolution during HIP process can be predicted, as shown
in Fig. 12a, and the corresponding evolution of yield stress and UTS during the HIP process
temperature ramp is shown in Fig. 12b.

4.5 Residual Stress-Cracking Test Artifacts

With the capability to simulate L-PBF AM process-induced residual stress concentration and HIP
process induced residual stress redistribution, a dedicated set of residual-stress cracking test
artifacts was designed by the team and fabricated at CTC, as shown in Fig. 13. These artifacts
incorporate engineered notches and sharp-corner features intended to amplify residual-stress
concentrations after L-PBF printing and thereby increase the propensity for cracking during
subsequent HIP exposure, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 14.

Residual-stress localization arising from these geometric features was quantified using the L-PBF
process model. The simulations indicate that the maximum principal stress concentrates at the
notch tip and at sharp block corners, with the principal tensile direction oriented approximately
normal to the notch face—consistent with a mode I crack-driving stress state. The magnitude of
this concentration can be systematically tuned through geometric parameters such as notch depth,
notch radius, and notch width. Leveraging this controllability, we designed a suite of artifacts
spanning a broad range of predicted residual-stress concentration levels (as characterized by the
peak maximum principal stress), as summarized in Fig. 15. This design-of-experiments approach
enables estimation of the material’s tolerance to residual-stress concentration and identification of
threshold conditions associated with cracking. Finally, to assess whether support structures

 12

mitigate stress localization associated with top-surface features, six selected geometries were also
printed with bottom support structures, as shown in Fig. 15.

For each artifact design across the four categories (notch-based, block-based, notch-based with
bottom support, and block-based with bottom support), three replicate samples were fabricated at
CTC. No cracking was observed in any as-printed specimens following the L-PBF build.
Subsequently, two replicates from each design were HIP processed while still attached to the base
plate, whereas the third replicate was first removed from the base plate by wire-EDM and then
HIP processed. After HIP, all specimens were inspected for cracking; the outcomes are
summarized in Figs. 16–19.

The results exhibit strong repeatability. For most designs that exhibited cracking, all three
replicates cracked; only two designs showed cracking in one out of three specimens. When
cracking occurred, crack initiation sites were highly consistent: cracks formed either at the notch
root (notch-based designs) or at the sharp block corner (block-based designs). These locations
coincide with the predicted residual-stress concentration sites from the process simulations (Fig.
16). In addition, removing the part from the base plate prior to HIP (via wire-EDM) did not produce
a discernible change in the cracking outcome for the tested designs, suggesting that the controlling
driver is the retained local stress state rather than the base-plate constraint during HIP.

 14

Fig. 16. Illustration of cracked samples, the crack occurs either at notch-tip or block corners which
are consistent with the predicted residual stress concentration locations.

For the notch-based designs (Fig. 17), a clear qualitative trend is observed: specimens with sharp
notches and high predicted local stress concentration cracked during HIP, whereas specimens with
smoothed notches remained uncracked. When comparing candidate stress metrics from the
simulations, both the maximum principal stress and the hydrostatic stress fields are broadly
consistent with the observed crack/no-crack ranking, while the von Mises stress does not provide
a clear discriminator. Similar trends are observed for the other three artifact categories (Figs. 18
and 19).

However, when all four categories are considered together, maximum principal stress alone is not
sufficient to uniquely distinguish cracked from uncracked cases across the full design space. In
contrast, hydrostatic stress shows a much more consistent correlation with cracking propensity
across categories, with the only exception being artifact design E, for which all three samples
cracked despite an intermediate predicted hydrostatic-stress level. This discrepancy may indicate
that the residual-stress reduction associated with the bottom support structure was overestimated
in the simulations. Overall, the comparison between the residual-stress cracking results and the
computed stress metrics suggests that tensile hydrostatic stress—through its association with void
growth and ductility loss—is a dominant driver of HIP cracking in these artifacts.

4.6 Discussion

This study combined process simulation, precipitation-aware constitutive modeling, and targeted
experiments to interrogate HIP cracking in L-PBF GammaPrint-1100™. Across all artifact
families, cracking was absent in the as-printed condition but emerged during HIP, and crack
initiation consistently localized at engineered stress concentrators (notch roots and sharp corners).
This repeatability indicates that cracking is primarily governed by the inherited as-built residual
stress state and its interaction with the HIP-induced precipitation response, rather than random
defect variability. A key finding is that hydrostatic stress correlates with crack/no-crack outcomes
more robustly than von Mises stress and, when all geometries are considered together, more
consistently than other stress components such as maximum principal stress.

These observations also clarify what classes of damage models are—and are not—appropriate for
predicting HIP cracking in GammaPrint-1100™. Damage formulations driven primarily by
accumulated plastic strain (or equivalent plastic strain) are unlikely to be predictive in this setting
because plastic flow during HIP is governed largely by the deviatoric stress state, whereas the
experimental crack/no-crack ranking tracks most consistently with tensile mean (hydrostatic)

 17

stress. More importantly, a strain-driven criterion can become physically misleading when
precipitation is active: under rapid heating, suppressed γ′ formation allows easier dislocation
motion, producing more plasticity and greater residual-stress relaxation—conditions that should
reduce cracking—yet a plastic-strain-triggered model would generally predict the opposite (i.e.,
higher damage from higher plastic strain). This conflicts with the precipitation-assisted cracking
mechanism suggested from this study.

A more suitable approach is therefore a stress-state-sensitive damage model in which the damage-
driving force increases with tensile hydrostatic stress and is not directly proportional to integrated
plastic strain or total strain. One practical option is creep cavitation / Kachanov–Rabotnov-type
continuum damage formulations [7], where damage evolves as a function of an effective stress
that can be defined to weight the hydrostatic component strongly and the sensitivity on
temperature/precipitates can enter the effect stress naturally. The key requirement is decoupling
damage accumulation from “more plastic strain leading to more damage,” and instead tying crack
initiation to retained tensile mean stress during the precipitation window, consistent with the
empirical dominance of hydrostatic stress across artifact families.

 6. COMMERCIALIZATION POSSIBILITIES

The outcomes of this project are not intended for direct commercialization as a standalone product.
Instead, the primary commercial value lies in the foundational process-qualification and design-
enablement capability established for GammaPrint-1100™ and similar high-performance AM
alloys. The validated modeling–testing workflow can be used to (i) guide L-PBF process
optimization to reduce residual-stress generation, (ii) inform component geometric design and
modification to avoid severe stress concentrators, and (iii) screen print–HIP strategies to mitigate
post-build cracking. By reducing trial-and-error iteration and accelerating qualification of defect-
free builds, this work supports eventual commercial deployment of crack-free AM turbine
components fabricated from GammaPrint-1100™ and related high-temperature alloys.

 7. PLANS FOR FUTURE COLLABORATION

Carpenter Technology Corporation is grateful to the Department of Energy, advanced materials
and manufacturing technology office for supporting this project which fostered new connections
between the Carpenter Technology Corporation and Oak Ridge National Laboratory
Manufacturing Science Division team. The Carpenter Technology Corporation and Oak Ridge
National Laboratory teams will pursue future work to further develop the L-PBF process
optimization method to reduce the residual stress generation during AM, or residual stress
relieving method that does not trigger cracking. In addition, it is also of interest to advance the
current hydrostatic-stress-based screening approach into a quantitative, predictive crack-initiation
capability for HIP of GammaPrint-1100™. While the present results indicate that hydrostatic
tension provides the most consistent qualitative discriminator across artifact families, a single
“tolerable maximum hydrostatic stress” criterion is not sufficient to capture effects of stress
redistribution, dwell time, temperature history, and precipitation-controlled stress relaxation. We
will attempt to implement and calibrate a rate-dependent continuum damage formulation—such
as a Kachanov–Rabotnov-type model—whose damage evolution explicitly depends on tensile
mean stress, temperature, and time, and whose resistance terms can be linked to the evolving γ′
state (via the reduced-order precipitation model). The residual-stress cracking artifact matrix
provides an ideal calibration and validation dataset to identify damage parameters and assess
transferability across notch/corner geometries and support/no-support conditions. These results
will be further analyzed for publication.

 8. CONCLUSIONS

This project establishes a physics-informed computational toolset to evaluate the processability of
Carpenter Technology Corporation (CTC)’s GammaPrint-1100™ alloy for laser powder bed
fusion (L-PBF) components, with emphasis on predicting residual-stress evolution and heat-
treatment–induced micro-cracking. In the first part of the project, the team designed and fabricated
multiple sets of simplified additively manufactured artifacts in GammaPrint-1100™ to enable
efficient, high-fidelity model calibration and validation. Experimental characterization including

 19

sample cross-sections melt-pool geometry and residual-stress–driven deflection measurements
obtained through wire EDM cutting provide direct, quantitative benchmarks for calibrating
phenomenological process-model parameters, complementing the alloy’s physics-based
thermal/mechanical property data supplied by Carpenter. Using these data, a coupled
thermomechanical AM process model capable of predicting residual stress and distortion was
calibrated with satisfactory agreement to experiments.

In the second part of the project, we extended the framework to simulate stress redistribution
during post-build hot isostatic pressing (HIP) process and to predict crack initiation driven by
retained residual stresses and precipitation hardening. By coupling γ′ precipitation evolution
prediction from Thermo-Calc-Precipitation Module (TC-PRISMA) to a strength model and
implementing several candidate damage criteria with element deletion, we established a
preliminary crack-prediction capability. A unique set of “residual stress-cracking test artifacts” are
being fabricated and tested to select the most appropriate failure criterion and calibrate parameters.
Initial results from the residual stress-cracking test artifacts indicate that peak tensile hydrostatic
stress provides the most consistent correlation with post-HIP crack/no-crack outcomes across
geometries, supporting hydrostatic-stress-driven damage as a primary cracking driver. The
resulting toolset will support rapid evaluation of print–HIP strategies and guide geometry and
process tuning to reduce cracking propensity in advanced, high-temperature AM turbine
components.
