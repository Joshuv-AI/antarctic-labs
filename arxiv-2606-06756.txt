A Primal–Dual Penalty Algorithm and Optimal Control of the Double Integrator
Regina S. Burachik, C. Yalçın Kaya, Xuemei Liu

Abstract
We investigate the behaviour of a primal–dual penalty scheme applied to all possible instances, i.e., boundary conditions, of an optimal control problem involving the double integrator. The proposed duality-based framework enables us to derive closed-form expressions for the associated dual functions as well as for the optimal control variables. Furthermore, we provide a complete characterization of the least exact penalty parameter for this problem. The iteration dynamics of the algorithm are examined by constructing and analyzing the trajectories of the constraint functions for every admissible instance of the problem. Finally, we compare the two step-size rules employed in our primal–dual algorithm with two alternative Polyak-type step-size strategies.



Key words
:
Primal–dual algorithm, Optimal control, Double integrator, Augmented Lagrangian, Duality scheme, Nonsmooth optimization, Subgradient methods, Penalty function methods, Least exact penalty parameter.

Introduction

The
double integrator
is one of the simplest ODEs, given by
\ddot{y}(t)=u(t)
, where
\ddot{y}:=d^{2}y/dt^{2}
and
u(t)
is a specified “forcing term”. The solution to this ODE can be obtained by integrating
u(t)
twice, hence the name “double integrator”. For a mechanical system,
y(t)
,
\dot{y}(t):=dy(t)/dt
and
\ddot{y}(t)
represent the position, velocity, and acceleration at time
t
, respectively, of a unit point mass in rectilinear motion on a frictionless horizontal plane. The double integrator also models the analogous rotational-mechanical and electrical systems
[
32
]
, and acts as a building block for cubic splines, which are variational curves
y(t)
that minimize the squared
{\cal L}^{2}
-norm of their acceleration
\ddot{y}

[
21
,
29
]
.

Because of its simplicity, the double integrator has long been used to illustrate both basic and new concepts in optimal control theory and to test the performance of numerical algorithms in solving optimal control problems; see, for example,
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
12
,
25
]
, and the research-level text book
[
27
]
that is solely devoted to this model. The role of the double integrator in optimal control is arguably similar to the role of the
Dahlquist test problem
in numerical analysis: the simple initial value problem
\dot{y}(t):=\lambda\,y(t)
,
y(0)=y_{0}
, whose solution can be written immediately as
y(t)=y_{0}\,e^{\lambda\,t}
with
\lambda
a constant parameter, is typically used to evaluate the stability of ODE solvers (e.g., Runge–Kutta methods) and perform error analysis; see, for example,
[
15
, Section 5.11]
and
[
18
,
19
]
.

Our aim in the present paper is to study the behaviour of the
primal–dual penalty
(PDP)
algorithm
applied to the optimal control of the double integrator. The PDP algorithm has recently been proposed in
[
10
]
(also see
[
26
]
) to solve (general) constrained infinite-dimensional optimization problems and, in particular, optimal control problems. Although the working and efficiency of the PDP algorithm were illustrated via some challenging optimal control problems in
[
10
]
, motivated by the aforementioned practices, our objective here is to study in utmost detail the behaviour of the PDP algorithm applied to solve a test optimal control problem involving the double integrator.

Let
x_{1}:=y
and
x_{2}:=\dot{y}
, and consider the
boundary conditions

y(0)=s_{0}
,
y(1)=s_{f}
,
\dot{y}(0)=v_{0}
,
\dot{y}(1)=v_{f}
, for the double integrator over the time horizon
[0,1]
. We aim to use the following
optimal control problem
, which is given in standard form, as a test problem for the PDP algorithm.

\mbox{(P)}\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{rlll}\displaystyle\min_{u(\cdot)}&\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}u^{2}(t)\,dt&\!\!\!=:\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\,\|u\|_{{\cal L}^{2}}^{2}&\\[11.38109pt]
{\rm subject\,to}&\dot{x}_{1}(t)=x_{2}(t)\,,&x_{1}(0)=s_{0}\,,&x_{1}(1)=s_{f}\,,\\[5.69054pt]
&\dot{x}_{2}(t)=u(t)\,,&x_{2}(0)=v_{0}\,,&x_{2}(1)=v_{f}\,,\end{array}\right.

where the functions
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
are called the
state variables
and the function
u
the
control variable
. Problem (P) is concerned with finding the
optimal control

u
that minimizes the
functional
expressed as the squared
{\cal L}^{2}
-norm of
u
, denoted by
\|u\|_{{\cal L}^{2}}^{2}
, subject to the double integrator (dynamics) and boundary conditions. Problem (P) is traditionally referred to as a
minimum-energy control problem
in the optimal control literature
and is a favourite example in lecture courses and text books on optimal control. In fact, the solution to Problem (P) can be easily written down via the calculus of variations: the resulting optimal control
u(t)
is linear in
t
and so the corresponding
x_{1}(t)
(or
y(t)
) after integrating
u(t)
twice is cubic in
t
. In fact,
x_{1}(t)
obtained by solving (P) is an example of a
variational curve
(albeit simple) in the approximation theory and differential geometry (see Section
2.3
for the detailed solution).

The PDP algorithm exploits a generalized Lagrangian duality scheme for infinite-dimensional constrained optimization problems, a scheme where the classical Lagrangian is augmented by a general penalty term, as described and studied in
[
14
,
7
]
. More details on augmented Lagrangian duality and its properties can be found in
[
14
,
10
,
7
]
, as well as in
[
26
]
. When the PDP algorithm is applied to solve Problem (P), for which we have the analytical solution, the steps of the algorithm are likely to reveal new properties of the problem, especially those properties arising from duality (see Contributions C1–C3 below). Moreover, we can also better understand the behaviour of the algorithm itself (see Contribution C4 below).

Next, we describe the theoretical framework for studying Problem (P). Let
H
be a Hilbert space. Consider the following infinite-dimensional equality-constrained optimization problem.

\min_{u\in H}\ \varphi(u)\ \ {\rm s.t.}\ \ h(u)=0\,,

(1)

where
u
is the
primal variable
, the functional
\varphi:H\to\mathbb{R}
is lower semi-continuous, and
h:H\to H
is some given continuous mapping. We will show in Section
2
that Problem (P) can be reformulated as the general problem expressed in (
1
).

As in
[
10
]
, the type of augmented Lagrangian we consider for (
1
) is an extension of the augmented Lagrangian in
[
4
,
8
]
to infinite dimensions. The infinite-dimensional problem in (
1
) has recently been studied in
[
14
]
, where optimal control problems are addressed via a primal–dual scheme and the augmented Lagrangian
\widetilde{L}:H\times H\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined for (
1
) as follows:

\widetilde{L}(u,z,c):=\varphi(u)-\langle z,A(h(u))\rangle+c\,\sigma(h(u))\,,

(2)

where
z
and
c
are the
dual variables
,
A:H\to H
is a suitable map, and
\sigma:H\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
verifies
\sigma(v)=0
if and only if
v=0
. The resulting primal–dual scheme is paired in
[
14
]
with an epsilon subgradient technique that solves the dual problem. An important advantage of the Lagrangian
\widetilde{L}
defined in (
2
) is that, unlike other available versions of the augmented Lagrangian (such as those proposed in
[
24
,
5
,
11
,
6
,
7
,
9
]
), it induces a penalty function method for the choice
A=0
; also see
[
13
]
for a penalty method emanating from the duality of (
2
), although it is in finite dimensions.

Indeed, we will take
A=0
and
\sigma
as a suitable norm in
H
. In this situation,
\sigma
becomes the
penalty term function
and
c
the
penalty parameter
. Therefore, we redefine the augmented Lagrangian as
L:H\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

L(u,c):=\varphi(u)+c\,\sigma(h(u))\,,

(3)

where
c
is the only dual variable. The fact that the method constitutes a penalty approach justifies the term primal–dual penalty (PDP) algorithm for the resulting epsilon-subgradient method. We thus can interpret the Lagrangian
L
in (
3
) is a
penalty function
.
The
dual function
corresponding to the Lagrangian
L
is defined as

q(c):=\inf_{u\in H}\,L(u,c)\,,

(4)

and the
dual problem
induced by these choices is

\max_{c\geq 0}\,q(c)\,.

(5)

As mentioned above, the PDP algorithm uses an
epsilon subgradient direction in its update rule (for the penalty parameter
c
) and improves (i.e., increases) the dual values in each iteration. The main aim of the present paper is to further exploit this
duality induced
penalty update and to use the resulting method to better understand and discover new aspects of the PDP algorithm, as well as to establish the resulting duality features of the double integrator problem. The contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows.

C1

For the primal–dual framework that we use, we first rewrite the differential equation constraints in Problem (P) as integral constraints. In particular, we derive and define the vector of constraint functions
h(u)
(see (
8
) below), and thus express the generalized augmented Lagrangian (or the penalty function) specific for Problem (P) in (
9
). Subsequently, by minimizing this Lagrangian, we obtain and present closed-form expressions for the dual function in Theorem
3.1
, on a case-by-case basis. The different cases arise by considering all instances of Problem (P), classified by the possible choices of the initial data
s_{0},s_{f},v_{0},v_{f}
.

C2

A
least exact penalty parameter
is the smallest positive number
\overline{c}
such that for every
c\geq\overline{c}
a minimizer of the Lagrangian in (
3
) is a solution to Problem (P). In Corollary
3.2
, we provide a closed form expression (given only in terms of the boundary conditions of the double integrator) for the least exact penalty parameter
\overline{c}
for Problem (P).

C3

In Section
3.2
, we look at the behaviour of the constraint function and compute the dual function, for all possible choices of the initial conditions. The resulting trajectories of the constraint function are visualized in Figure
3
and interpreted within various regions that are uniquely defined by the boundary conditions.

C4

In Sections
3.3
and
3.4
, we compute PDP iterates and make comparisons for different choices of the step-size used to update
c
.

Preliminaries

2.1
A reformulation of Problem (P)

We now reformulate (P) as an equality constrained problem, which allows us to write down the Lagrangian in (
3
). Indeed, using the ODE constraints and the properties of definite integrals, we can rewrite Problem (P) as

\mbox{(P1)}\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\min&{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}u^{2}(t)\,dt}\\[11.38109pt]
{\rm subject\,to}&x_{1}(1)-s_{f}=0\,,\\[5.69054pt]
&x_{2}(1)-v_{f}=0\,,\\
\end{array}\right.

where
x_{1}(1)
and
x_{2}(1)
are affine functions of
u
expressed from the ODEs in Problem (P) as

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\displaystyle x_{2}(1)=v_{0}+\int_{0}^{1}u(\tau)\,d\tau\,,\\[14.22636pt]
&\displaystyle x_{1}(1)=s_{0}+\int_{0}^{1}x_{2}(\tau)\,d\tau=s_{0}+\int_{0}^{1}\left[v_{0}+\int_{0}^{\tau}u(s)ds\right]d\tau\,.\end{array}

(6)

Next, define the constants

r_{1}:=v_{0}+s_{0}-s_{f}\quad\hbox{and}\quad r_{2}:=v_{0}-v_{f}\,,

(7)

and the vector
r:=(r_{1},r_{2})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Equations (
6
) and (
7
) suggest that the equality constraints can be defined through the vector mapping
h:{\cal L}^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
as

h(u):=\left[\begin{array}[]{l}h_{1}(u)\\[5.69054pt]
h_{2}(u)\\
\end{array}\right]:=\left[\begin{array}[]{l}r_{1}+\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}u(s)ds\right]d\tau\\[14.22636pt]
r_{2}+\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}u(\tau)d\tau\\
\end{array}\right].

(8)

With this definition, it is direct to check that the constraints in Problem (P1) can be rewritten as
h(u)=0
.
Taking now
\varphi(u):=(1/2)\|u\|_{{\cal L}^{2}}^{2}
, we have reformulated (P1) as a special case of (
1
).

The penalty function
\sigma
is chosen as the finite-dimensional
\ell_{1}
-norm, denoted as
\|\cdot\|_{1}:=|\cdot|+|\cdot|
, where
|\cdot|
is the
absolute value function
. Therefore, the augmented Lagrangian given in (
3
) can then be expressed for Problem (P1) as

L(u,c)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}u^{2}(t)\,dt+c\left(\,\left|r_{1}+\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}u(s)\,ds\right]d\tau\right|+\left|r_{2}+\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}u(\tau)\,d\tau\right|\,\right).

(9)

2.2
The PDP algorithm

In what follows, we provide a description of the PDP algorithm from
[
10
]
.

Algorithm 1

(Primal–Dual Penalty (PDP) Algorithm)

Let
\alpha,\varepsilon>0
. Choose a sequence
\{\alpha_{k}\}\subset(0,\alpha)
.

Step
0
. (Initialization) Choose
c_{0}>0
and let
k:=0
.

Step
1
. (Solution of Subproblem and Stopping Criterion)

(a)

Find
\displaystyle u_{k}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{u\in H}{L(u,c_{k})}
.

(b)

If
\|h(u_{k})\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon
, stop.

Step
2
. (Selection of step-size and Update of Dual Variables)

Choose
s_{k}>0
and set
\tilde{s}_{k}=(\alpha_{k}+1)s_{k}
. Update the penalty parameter by

c_{k+1}:=c_{k}+\tilde{s}_{k}\,\|h(u_{k})\|_{1}\,.

Set
k:=k+1
, go to Step
1
.

The step-size
s_{k}
to be used in the PDP algorithm is chosen as in
[
7
, Algorithm 2]
, which gives rise to the versions PDP-1 and PDP-2 of the PDP algorithm.

•

Algorithm PDP-1: Take two parameters
\beta>\eta>0
. Let
u_{k}
be as in Step 1(a). Consider the step-size

s_{k}\in[\eta_{k},\beta_{k}]\,,

(10)

where
\eta_{k}:=\min\{\eta,\|h(u_{k})\|_{2}\}
and
\beta_{k}:=\max\{\beta,\|h(u_{k})\|_{1}+\|h(u_{k})\|_{2}\}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the finite-dimensional
\ell_{2}
norm.

•

Algorithm PDP-2: Take
\beta>0
and a sequence
\{\theta_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

\sum_{j}\theta_{j}=\infty
, and
\theta_{k}\leq\beta
for all
k
.
Let
u_{k}
be as in Step 1(a). Consider the step-size

s_{k}\in[\eta_{k},\beta_{k}]\,,

(11)

where
\eta_{k}:=\theta_{k}/\|h(u_{k})\|_{1}
and
\beta_{k}:=\beta/\|h(u_{k})\|_{1}
.

Lemma 3.1
(Unique minimizer of the Lagrangian)

Given

, re-write the Lagrangian in (

Remark 3.5
(the Newton method and the PDP Algorithm)

Note that the iterations we observe in Figure

. Recall that the Newton iterations are given by

c^{k+1}=c^{k}-\frac{q(c^{k})-2}{q^{\prime}(c^{k})}\,,\ \ \ k=0,1,\ldots\,.

The graph implies that the first derivative

4
Conclusion and Discussion

We solved the double integrator optimal control problem
via an augmented Lagrangian duality scheme that can be interpreted as a penalty method. To consider all possible instances of the double integrator problem, we divided the problem space into 13 disjoint classes according to the boundary values. For each of these 13 classes, we obtained analytical expressions of (i) the dual function, (ii) the control variable, and the (iii) least exact penalty parameter.

We graphically compared the iterations of the dual sequence using four different choices of step-sizes: (
10
), (
11
), and two choices of Polyak type step-size.

To understand the behaviour of the method, we plotted the trajectory of the constraint functions as a parametric equation involving the penalty parameter
c
. We showed the correspondence between the switchings of the trajectory and the changes of the dual function.

Appendix

Preliminaries to the Proof of Lemma
3.1

To establish Lemma
3.1
, we need
Remark A.1
and
Lemma A.1
, given next.

Remark A.1
(Derivative of a linear functional)

Recall that the space
{\cal L}^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R})
is a Hilbert space with scalar product given by

\langle u,u^{\prime}\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}u(s)\,u^{\prime}(s)ds,

for every
u,u^{\prime}\in{\cal L}^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R})
. By
[
16
, Theorem 5.5]
(Riesz–Frechet representation theorem in Hilbert spaces), for every linear functional
\Psi:{\cal L}^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}
, there exists a unique element
w\in{\cal L}^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R})
such that

\Psi(v)=\langle w,v\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}v(s)w(s)ds,\,\,\forall\,\,v\in{\cal L}^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}).

(A.1)

Moreover, it is easy to deduce that
w=\dfrac{d\Psi}{dv}\in{\cal L}^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R})
is the derivative of
\Psi
. This follows directly from observing that

\lim_{v\to 0}\frac{\Psi(u+v)-\Psi(u)-\langle w,v\rangle}{\|v\|}=\lim_{v\to 0}\frac{\langle w,u+v\rangle-\langle w,u\rangle-\langle w,v\rangle}{\|v\|}=0,

where we used (
A.1
). The above expression implies that
w
is the Frechet derivative of
\Psi
.

Lemma A.1
(Derivative of the double integrator)

Consider the function

\Lambda_{1}:{\cal L}^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\Lambda_{1}(u)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left[\int_{0}^{t}u(\tau)d\tau\right]dt,

where
u\in{\cal L}^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R})
. Define
\gamma:[0,1]\to[0,1]
as
\gamma(s):=1-s
for every
s\in[0,1]
.
Then
\Lambda_{1}
is linear and we have
\dfrac{d\Lambda_{1}}{du}=\gamma
.

Proof.
The linearity of
\Lambda_{1}
is directly related to the definition and properties of the integral. By Remark
A.1
, to prove that
\dfrac{d\Lambda_{1}}{du}=\gamma
, it is enough to show that

\Lambda_{1}(u)=\langle u,\gamma\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}u(s)\gamma(s)ds,\,\,\forall\,\,u\in{\cal L}^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}).

(A.2)

Indeed, for a fixed
t\in[0,1]
define
{\mathcal{\chi}}_{(0,t]}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
, as

\chi_{(0,t]}(\tau):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\tau\leq t,\\
0,&\tau>t,\\
\end{array}\right.

for every
\tau\in[0,1]
. Similarly, for a fixed
