Velo (BAT) buffet of oral nicotine pouches in Pakistan: varying brand variant levels of nicotine, menthol and other flavour chemicals
Authors: Joanna E Cohen, Maryam Ibrahim, Sejal Saraf, Kevin Welding, Wentai Luo, Kevin J McWhirter, Katherine Clegg Smith, James F Pankow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGlobal sales of oral nicotine pouches are increasing. 10 brand variants of Velo (BAT) pouches were purchased in Karachi, Pakistan in 2022 and assessed for levels of nicotine, numerous flavour chemicals, packaging and price.MethodsPurchase prices were recorded, and colours, images and words on the containers were assessed. Samples were analysed for nicotine and 180 individual flavour chemicals. Data were examined using the values of several flavour chemical groups, including total fruit flavour chemicals (TFFCs), total non-menthol mint chemicals (TNMMCs) and total menthol/mint chemicals (TM/MCs).ResultsAll brand variants indicated the nicotine content on the container (6, 10 or 14 mg/pouch). The 14 mg variants were packaged with black container tops and cost 25% more than the others, which had off-white container tops. Measured nicotine levels were similar to the labelled values. 46 of the 180 flavour compounds were detected at least once in the 10 products; four compounds were detected in all 10 products. Variation in the levels of TFFCs, TNMMCs and TM/MCs was much larger than the variation for the measured values of nicotine.ConclusionThis is the first independent assessment of oral nicotine pouch brand variants. Nicotine is available over a range of levels; it could be considered as a regulatory target. Consumers are also offered a buffet of options with a range of flavour levels and flavour mixes. Regulation of flavour chemicals in oral nicotine pouches should be considered in Pakistan given that flavours are known to increase product appeal, initiation by adolescents and quitting difficulty.

Introduction

Global sales of oral nicotine pouches (‘nicotine pouches’ hereafter) are increasing. Among the most popular brands are Velo (BAT), on! (Altria) and ZYN (Swedish Match).1 2 Nicotine pouches are typically placed between the lip and gum,2 where the nicotine is then absorbed into the bloodstream.3 4 Nicotine pouches usually include nicotine along with flavour chemicals, filler material and other possible additives. They come in a range of nicotine strengths and flavours.5 6

In 2022, BAT reported selling 4 billion nicotine pouches with an expected 500% increase in the nicotine pouch market by 2026.7 Globally, excluding the USA, Velo led nicotine pouch sales in 2023,7 with particularly high prevalence of use in low- and middle-income countries such as those in South Asia, where these products are sold with little to no regulation.8 In Pakistan in 2019, an estimated 9.6 million adults used smokeless tobacco, and 15.6 million used smoked products.9 10 In 2019, BAT’s Velo was the first nicotine pouch brand available in Pakistan, and the market has since grown with additional international (eg, ZYN) and local brands.11 According to Euromonitor, “volume sales of nicotine pouches are substantial in Pakistan,” and BAT’s Velo has the largest brand market share of smokeless tobacco (including nicotine pouches), e-cigarettes and heated tobacco products at 52.4% in 2023 among products covered by Euromonitor, an increase of more than double from 2021 (25.6%).12

The inclusion of flavours in tobacco products is highly problematic. Many studies have found that flavours appeal to both youth and young adults and are a major contributor to initiation and continued use of tobacco products.13 14 Moreover, the inclusion of flavours can make tobacco products more difficult to quit.14 Additionally, some flavour chemicals are toxic and some are carcinogenic.15 16

Several countries have worked to restrict or ban flavours in cigarettes, e-cigarettes and smokeless tobacco products.14 17 18 As of 2021, at least 34 countries regulate nicotine pouches.19 In 20 of these, nicotine pouches are sold with limits on advertising, requirements for health warning labels and/or minimum purchasing ages; in 14, the sale of nicotine pouches is restricted by methods including requiring premarket authorisation prior to sale, purchase only through a prescription and outright bans.

Although smokeless tobacco remains largely unregulated, Pakistan has implemented some restrictions on its sale near educational institutions.20 However, there are no specific policies governing the sale, marketing or promotion of nicotine pouches. Additionally, under the current tobacco control law, there are no restrictions on flavoured tobacco products, including nicotine pouches.21 This regulatory gap raises concern, given their growing market presence and potential health impact.

While there have been studies of the flavour chemicals in cigarettes, e-cigarettes and little cigars,22–28 there have been no independent assessments to date assessing the range of flavour chemicals in nicotine pouches. And, while there have been many studies describing and assessing tobacco product packaging,29–35 no studies to date, of any tobacco product, have looked at both ingredients and packaging, and how they relate to price, for any tobacco product. The primary purpose of this study was to examine, for the first time, whether different brand variants of a nicotine pouch brand varied in terms of their flavour chemical composition—both in terms of the specific flavour chemicals present and in terms of the levels of these chemicals. A secondary purpose was to examine the variation in nicotine and price across brand variants. We examined these elements in 10 brand variants of Velo nicotine pouches purchased in Pakistan.

Methods

Terminology

Various words appear in all caps on the containers, including the brand name and strength descriptors (figure 1). We use lowercase here for easier readability.

Sample

We set out to collect a census of every unique brand variant of Velo. Velo was chosen because it had the largest brand market share of nicotine pouches (~95% of the nicotine pouch market in Pakistan in 2023),12 it was being heavily promoted in Pakistan at the time and it had a number of brand variants on the market which allowed us to explore the differences in flavours and nicotine across brand variants within a brand.

10 containers (plastic) of Velo nicotine pouches were purchased in Karachi, Pakistan on 31 October and 21 November 2022. The purchase price was recorded. These were bought at kiosks and shops that sell tobacco products, paan (a chewed preparation containing betel leaf, betel nut, flavourings and slaked lime) and other small convenience items. To our knowledge, these 10 represented all of the Velo variants available in Karachi at the time of purchase; however, due to the wide variety of points of sale for nicotine pouches in Pakistan, it is possible brand variants may have been missed.36 Photographs of the containers are provided in figure 1. The tops were labelled ‘max’, ‘strong’ or ‘medium’, and the bottoms were correspondingly labelled ‘14 mg’, ‘10 mg’ or ‘6 mg’, followed immediately by ‘nicotine pouches’. On purchase, each container was placed in a polyethylene Ziplock bag and sent by courier to Portland State University for analysis. On receipt, each product was transferred to a barrier foil Ziplock bag (Ted Pella, Redding, California), then stored at 4°C. All of the products were extracted and analysed within 2 weeks of receipt.

Chemicals

Authentic standard materials for the flavour analytes and xylitol were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St Louis, Missouri) and TCI America (Portland, Oregon). Solvents used for preparation of standard solutions and extractions were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich.

Flavour chemical parameter groups

The list of 180 target flavour analytes is provided in online supplemental table S1. The total flavour chemical (TFC) parameter is the sum of the levels for all 180 analytes, measured in milligram per pouch. The 180 analytes were selected based on previous research examining flavours in combustible tobacco products, snuff, wraps and e-cigarettes based on the analytical chromatograms from the gas chromatography/mass spectrometry (GC/MS) analyses.

The total fruit flavour chemical (TFFC) group is a subset of 107 chemicals (see online supplemental table S1), selected based on flavour information in the Good Scents Company Information System.37

The total non-menthol mint chemical (not including menthol) (TNMMC) group is the sum of the 18 flavour chemicals (see online supplemental table S1) with mint-related flavour characteristics, also selected based on flavour information in the Good Scents Company Information System.

The total menthol/mint compound (TM/MC) group is defined as menthol plus the non-menthol mint group (TNMMCs), given that we found (see below) that the values for the non-menthol mint group were well correlated with the values for menthol (R2=0.93). (Of the 18 compounds in the non-menthol mint group, nine were detected at least once in the samples analysed. For values >0.1 mg/pouch, (R/S)-carvone provided ≥97.9% of the group value. For values <0.1 mg/pouch, ≥97.6% of the group value was provided by (R/S)-carvone with one other compound, namely eucalyptol, menthyl acetate, ethyl salicylate or thymol.)

We calculated the coefficient of variation (CV) (standard×100%/mean) for nicotine and for each flavour grouping as a measure of the variation in the values.

Sweeteners

The bottom side of each product container listed two artificial sweeteners as ingredients in the pouches, xylitol and sucralose.38 Xylitol was amenable to determination by GC, as used here for the flavour analytes.

Analytical methods

For all the pouches analysed here, the mean weight±1 SD was 0.66 g±0.03 g/pouch. Each of the chemical analyses used the addition of a known mass of a surrogate standard (SS) compound to the sample. For the nicotine determinations, the SS compound was quinoline. For the flavour chemical determinations, the SS compound was 1,3,5-trichlorobenzene. For each method, the chosen SS compound was added to the pouch material prior to initiation of the extraction step. The SS compound was then extracted from the sample material for determination of the recovery efficiency of an analyte not expected in the sample. The desired SS recovery value is always 100.0%. (Calculated SS recovery values >100% are possible due to normal statistical fluctuations in the outcomes of the multiple analytical steps, as with a fluctuation in the instrument response above the employed calibration.) Similarly, for each method, a known mass of the chosen internal standard (IS) compound was added to the final extract prior to injection on the analytical instrument. The IS signal was then subject, along with each of the analytes, to the variations affecting the instrumental response. The sample-to-sample variation in the IS signal was used to correct for those variations.

To determine levels of nicotine, solvent extraction was carried out using a method adapted from CORESTA.39 Duplicate samples were processed, each using one pouch. The pouch was placed inside a 40 mL ‘VOA’ (volatile organics analysis) vial, then cut open with sterilised stainless steel surgical scissors. One millilitre of a quinoline SS stock solution at 4000 µg/mL in MTBE was added to the sample material. To place the nicotine fully in the non-ionic, free-base form, 7 mL of 5 normal NaOH was added, then allowed to wet the sample material for 15 min. 20 mL of MTBE was then added. The vial was capped, placed on a shaker for 2 hours, then placed in a refrigerator overnight. The next day, after warming to room temperature, shaking for 1 hour, then quiescent settling for 1 hour, 1 mL of the extract was transferred into a 1 mL GC autosampler vial. 20 µL of the 1,2,3-trichlorobenzene IS stock solution at 2000 ng/μL was added. Analysis by GC/MS followed using a GC injection split ratio of 25 to 1 and other instrument conditions as described previously.40

For determination of the flavour chemicals and xylitol in the nicotine pouches, solvent extraction using isopropanol was carried out using two replicates, each using two pouches. Isopropanol was chosen for its good solvent and GC properties for flavour chemicals, and for its low toxicity. The pouches were placed inside a 40 mL ‘VOA’ vial, then cut open with clean stainless steel surgical scissors. 0.20 mL of the 1,3,5-trichlorobenzene SS solution at 4000 µg/mL in isopropanol was added to the sample material. 20 mL of isopropanol was then added to the vial. Each vial was capped, placed on a shaker for 2 hours, then placed in a refrigerator overnight. The next day, after warming the vial to room temperature, shaking for 1 hour, then quiescent settling for 1 hour, 1 mL of the extract was transferred into a 1 mL GC autosampler vial. 20 µL of the 1,2,3-trichlorobenzene IS solution at 2000 ng/μL was added. Analysis by GC/MS using the same instrument conditions was described previously,36 with a GC injection split ratio of 10 to 1.

All of the 180 target analyte flavour chemicals (and indeed all aromatic flavour chemicals in use with tobacco products) are by definition amenable to determination by GC/MS. No aromatic non-target flavour chemical at significant concentration was at risk of escaping detection in the study. Indeed, two non-target flavour compounds (γ-terpineol and anethole) were detected in two brand variants, and their levels were estimated (see below).

SS recoveries

For the nicotine analyses, the SS extraction recovery values were close to 100%, ranging from 95.3% to 98.6% with a mean of 97.2%. For the flavour chemical analyses, the values ranged from 95.8% to 101.1% with a mean of 98.5%.

Results

Nicotine levels and packaging elements

Each of the container tops included images of five or four circles, representing the level of nicotine. The two brand variants with five of five filled circles also included ‘max’ on the container to denote the nicotine strength and the labelled level of ‘14 mg’ per pouch; the background for these container tops was black. For the lower nicotine levels, the containers were off-white. Six had three of four filled circles with ‘strong’ and ‘10 mg’ per pouch on the container; the remaining two had two of four filled circles with ‘medium’ and ‘6 mg’ per pouch on the containers. For every brand variant, the replicate analyses were within 8%. The measured nicotine levels (table 1) ranged from a minimum of 5.1 mg/pouch to a maximum of 13.0 mg/pouch. For each product, the measured level was similar to, though always somewhat lower than, the labelled level. For the variability among the 10 variants, the final analytical mean values for nicotine in the 10 brand variants were found to be characterised by an arithmetic CV of 28%.

Flavour chemicals

Measurable levels of flavour chemicals were found in all 10 brand variants (table 1). All 180 flavour chemicals were detectable at 0.004 mg/pouch or below. For compounds found at 0.020 mg/pouch or above, the replicate precision was 8% or less. 46 of the 180 flavour chemicals were detected in at least one of the 10 products; four compounds were detected in all 10 products, namely benzyl alcohol (cherry flavour), menthol, α-terpineol (cardamom flavour) and carvone (a mint flavour). Other than the 46 detected target flavour compounds, two non-target flavour compounds were detected in two brand variants at significant concentration: g-terpineol and anethole. Their concentrations were estimated based on follow-up calibration standards. The detected 46 flavour chemicals are marked in ‘online supplemental table S1’ by virtue of their non-zero values for their detection frequency (10%, 20%, …90% or 100%) among the 10 brand variants.

Figure 2 plots the values of the parameters in table 1 for each of the 10 variants and includes the CV values. The variation in the measured levels of TFCs (77%), fruit flavours (TFFCs) (180%), menthol (71%), non-menthol mint flavours (TNMMCs) (81%) and menthol/mint flavours (TM/MCs) (75%) was much larger than the variation for the measured values of nicotine (28%), indicating a broad range of flavour chemicals and flavour mixes across the 10 brand variants.

The three ‘Polar Mint’ variants had high levels of menthol and menthol/mint (TM/MCs). Their fruit flavour (TFFCs) values were very low. We hypothesise that these low levels were possibly unintentional, for example, due to incidental uptake of the measured fruit flavour compounds because of proximal/same equipment manufacturing of other products. (cf The common ‘may contain’ warning carried by many food products regarding food allergens, as with the possible presence of low levels of nuts even when nuts were not among the intended ingredients.) The CV values for the fruit flavours (TFFCs) in figure 2 were computed excluding the three ‘Polar Mint’ values.

Because of the large variations in the TFC values and in the values of its different component parameters, we probed how the component parameters varied across the 10 brand variants. The sum of the fruit flavour (TFFCs) and menthol/mint (TM/MCs) parameters accounts for ≥97% of the TFC value for eight of the 10 variants (table 1). The exceptions were: (1) ‘Elaichi Blast’ (53%), for which most of the balance was α-terpineol (a compound found in abundance in the essential oil of the spice cardamom (or, in Hindi, ‘elaichi’)) at 0.32 mg/pouch (all other target flavour analytes at <0.002 mg/pouch); and (2) ‘Exotic Black’ (72%) for which most of the balance for the target analytes was p-anisaldehyde (sweet, floral and strongly aniseed). Notably, ‘Elaichi Blast’ and ‘Exotic Black’ were each found to contain one significant non-target flavour analyte, γ-terpineol (pine, floral and lilac) at 0.11 mg/pouch and anethole (sweet, anise and liquorice) at 2.9 mg/pouch, respectively. When the values for these non-target analytes are added to the target flavour chemical numbers, the results are 0.78 mg/pouch for ‘Elaichi Blast’ and 3.0 mg/pouch for ‘Exotic Black’ (table 1).

Xylitol (sweetener)

Every Velo brand variant was found to contain a significant level of Xylitol, namely ~16 mg/pouch. This is many times higher than the levels of TFCs found (including non-target flavour chemicals), making the presence of this sweetener a major product characteristic.

Price

At the time of purchase, all containers cost 120 Pakistani rupees (~US$0.43), except the two containers with the highest level of nicotine which cost 150 Pakistani rupees (~US$0.54). Packs of 20 cigarettes purchased at the same time as the nicotine pouches ranged in price from 100 to 500 Pakistani rupees (~US$0.34–1.72).

Discussion

Prior studies have found that highlighting flavours can increase positive perceptions of tobacco products41 42; we found a wide variation in flavour levels and flavour mixes in BAT Velo nicotine pouches on sale in Karachi. Overall, the per pouch flavour chemical levels for the 10 brand variants of nicotine pouches were markedly lower than what we have found previously for the per stick values for cigarettes. For example, the all-product average for other flavour chemicals in the nicotine pouches was 0.42 mg/pouch, as compared with a median value of 0.48,27 0.83,40 0.57 and 1.39 mg/stick28 for cigarettes in Indonesia, Mexico, Philippines and Vietnam, respectively; for comparison, the average was 1.15 mg/stick for cigarettes purchased in Pakistan at the same time as the pouches (unpublished data). Similarly, the all-product average for menthol/mint (TM/MCs) in the nicotine pouches was 0.18 mg/pouch, as compared with 3.60,27 6.93,40 6.63 and 5.40 mg/stick28 for cigarettes purchased in Indonesia, Mexico, Philippines and Vietnam, respectively. Two likely reasons for this are that: (1) the material in the pouches (mostly microcrystalline cellulose) can be expected to be less generally retentive of flavour chemicals than tobacco, cigarette flavour capsules and cigarette filter material; and (2) flavour chemicals in a cigarette suffer significant losses due to combustion and pyrolysis as well as some diversion to sidestream smoke.43

While we have not been able to find independent studies of flavour chemicals in nicotine pouches, there have been reports of nicotine content. Mallock et al purchased a convenience sample of 44 nicotine pouches in Germany in 2021 from 20 different manufacturers and found that nicotine content ranged from 1.79 mg/pouch to 47.5 mg/pouch; brand names were not reported.44 In an analysis of a range of nicotine pouch brands purchased in 2019 in the Atlanta area (USA), Stanfill and Ashley reported total nicotine content ranging from 1.29 mg/pouch to 6.11 mg/pouch.25 Moodie et al reported finding nicotine pouches for sale in Scotland with 97.5 mg of nicotine per pouch (variants were Rabbit Blue Ice, Cuba Ice Cool and Trillionz Blueberry); these pouches weighed 0.65 g.45 The levels of nicotine in the Velo (BAT) brand variants bought in Pakistan and reported here ranged from 5.1 mg/pouch to 13.0 mg/pouch in packaging that presented nicotine levels of 14, 10 or 6 mg. Prior research has found nicotine pouches displaying a 6 mg nicotine level to be perceived as a potential substitute for cigarettes but less harmful.46 Discrepancies in nicotine concentration are expected and align with previous studies that have found this to be common among tobacco products.44 47–49

It is interesting that the price for the 6 mg (medium) and 10 mg (strong) containers of nicotine pouches was the same. Differing levels of TFCs do not appear to be dictating the sales price. The level of nicotine for the 14 mg (max) containers does come at a premium. Overall, the price of nicotine pouches makes them competitive with the lower end of the cigarette market. The price equivalency of the 6 and 10 mg containers may lead consumers to view the higher concentrate product as a better cigarette substitute. (Note: a few studies have found max plasma nicotine concentrations for 8–10 mg nicotine pouches to be similar to that for a cigarette, as reported by Mallock-Ohnesorg et al.50)

The findings cannot be generalised to all flavoured nicotine pouches available on the global market: we only examined a convenience sample of brand variants of one major nicotine pouch brand, purchased in one city of one country. Given that we found that the flavour chemicals varied significantly across Velo’s brand variants, even if there were additional brand variants on the market that were not included in our sample, the conclusions would remain the same. The nicotine pouches were purchased in 2022, and the brand variants may have changed since then. We returned to a few stores in Rawalpindi and Islamabad in June 2024 and found seven of the 10 brand variants for sale; we did not find ‘Paan Rush’ 10 mg, ‘Berry Frost’ 6 mg or ‘Polar Mint’ 6 mg; two shops indicated that they could get stronger variants and different flavours if requested. Price information was based on one purchase per brand variant container. Despite these limitations, this first independent study to examine nicotine and flavour chemical levels, packaging and price in nicotine pouches offers unique, hypothesis-generating findings along with a model for replicating this work with other nicotine pouch brands and in other countries.

Manufacturers should be transparent about the ingredients and flavour chemicals used in their products, providing clear and comprehensive information to consumers. In addition to replicating this work in other settings, future research could examine how the various packaging elements (eg, nicotine level, nicotine-level descriptor, nicotine-level image, flavour descriptors and colours) influence consumer perceptions and behaviours, including appeal and abuse liability potential.
