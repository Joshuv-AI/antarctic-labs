 1 SUMMARY OF CARBON DIOXIDE PIPELINE SYSTEMS

Pipelines are historically seen as the primary transportation mode for carbon dioxide (CO 2)
streams in the context of carbon capture and storage (CCS) and oil and gas industries. Pipeline
transmission of CO2 over longer distances is regarded as most efficient and economical when the
CO2 is in the dense phase, i.e., in liquid or supercritical regime, due to transporting CO 2 in dense
phase that allows for a smaller-diameter pipeline to move a given flow, which optimizes
project cost.

1.1 CHARACTERIZATION OF THE INDUSTRY

Pipeline transport of CO2 dates back over 80 years and was initially associated with the dry ice
and beverage business. More than 40 years ago, large-scale transport by pipeline began when
CO2 was used for enhanced oil recovery (EOR) operations, and this CO2 was sourced primarily
from natural deposits and gas processing plants. Approximately 90% of the CO 2 pipeline
infrastructure in the United States today is used for CO2 EOR operations. These pipelines were
constructed to provide a direct link between a CO2 source and an associated CO2 sink (a
reservoir), creating a pipeline industry that is independent of the ownership of the various assets
involved in CO2 EOR (UH Energy n.d.).

Carbon dioxide pipelines boast a wide capacity range, from 890 tons to 103,000 tons per day,
surpassing shipping (46,000 tons per vessel) and rail (80 to 83 tons per railcar) (National
Petroleum Council 2021). Furthermore, these pipelines ensure uninterrupted operations that
nearly run without stopping, ensuring a consistent flow of CO₂. They transport the carbon
dioxide, often pumped as a fluid in its dense phase, from capture sources to storage locations or
oil fields, with pumping stations along the way to maintain pressure and flow.

Totaling over 5,300 miles, CO2 pipelines have operated in various regions of the United States
for decades, largely between natural sources of CO2 and enhanced oil recovery fields. As
Figure 1 presents, the miles of CO2 pipeline have increased over the past 20 years (PHMSA
2025a).

The CO2 pipeline network of over 5,300 miles in the United States is far surpassed by the
country’s nearly 260,000-mile hazardous liquid pipeline network and nearly 3-million-mile
natural gas pipeline network ( (PHMSA 2025b, PHMSA 2025c). It is also discontinuous across
five geographic zones. As presented in Figure 2, the United States has a significant CO2 pipeline
network.

Several regions in the United States have large CO2 pipeline networks, including the Permian
Basin, the Gulf Coast, and the Rocky Mountain area. The Permian Basin has the most extensive
network, with a pipeline system owned by Kinder Morgan and Occidental Petroleum. These
companies transport nearly 2 billion standard cubic feet per day (BSCF/D), or 42 million short
tons per annum (Mstpa), of CO2 to eastern New Mexico, West Texas, and southeastern Utah.
The pipelines carry CO2 to internal and external customers who use it for EOR in mature oil
fields. The Cortez Pipeline—Kinder Morgan’s longest CO2 pipeline—stretches 500 miles from
southwestern Colorado to Denver City, Texas, and can transport 1.5 BSCF/D, which is
equivalent to 31 Mstpa. Occidental Petroleum operates the Sheep Mountain and Bravo Dome
Pipeline networks, which extend over 1,100 miles with a capacity of over 0.8 BSCF/D
(16 Mstpa).

Although CO2 and conventional hydrocarbon pipelines are similar, they differ in their design,
construction, and operation, primarily due to carbon dioxide’s specific characteristics. CO 2’s
molecular weight is approximately 50% higher than air; that is, at ambient condition the density
of gaseous CO2 will be greater than air, which has implications for how CO2 disperses when
released to the ambient. CO2 pipes are also more vulnerable than conventional hydrocarbon
pipelines to a fracture propagating after it initiates, primarily due to the unique properties of CO2
and the potential for rapid, sustained pressure-driven crack propagation (Accufacts Inc. 2022). In
addition, due to a sudden drop in pressure during a pipeline failure triggering a complex
sequence of events involving rapid depressurization, phase transitions, and extreme temperature
drops, CO2 pipelines have higher material requirements due to the risk of brittle fracture
propagation.

Such factors as temperature, pressure, and stream composition (which varies with the CO 2
source) influence CO2 phase behavior. CO2 exists as a solid, liquid, or gas depending on pressure
and temperature (Figure 3). When pressures exceed 1,070 psi (or 7.38 MPa / 72.8 atm) 1, pure
CO2 enters what is referred to as its dense phase, which includes both supercritical and dense
phase liquid CO2 (Figure 3). Dense phase CO2 exhibits liquid-like density and gas-like viscosity.
Because of its higher density and lower pressure losses compared to gas phase CO 2, dense phase
CO2 is generally the preferred phase for CO2 pipeline transport.

Dense phase CO2 pipelines typically operate at pressures between 73 atm and about 207 atm
(1,075 psig to 3,045 psig). A compressor is used to increase the pressure of the CO2 to its dense
liquid phase (72.8 atm, referred to as the critical pressure) and pumps are used to further boost
pressures to those required for transport in a pipeline. Figure 4 presents the elements of a typical
CO2 pipeline (Witkowski, et al. 2014), including a pipeline supervisory control and data
acquisition (SCADA) system, valves (to moderate and/or shut off CO 2 flow), and various
instrumentation to measure flow, pressure, and temperature for leak detection and monitoring of
CO2 pipeline conditions. Most CO2 pipeline operators monitor and manage their pipelines
remotely through a control room using the SCADA system. CO2 pipelines must have pressure
monitors to know when a leak or rupture occurs and can initiate shut-off valves to mitigate the
release of CO2 from the pipeline.

up to 2,500-2,800 psig, to ensure single-phase flow and minimize costs (National Petroleum
Council 2021).3 Figure 5 indicates that CO2 pipeline diameters can range anywhere from 4 to

The actual pipeline itself, commonly called line pipe, consists of a strong carbon steel material
engineered to meet standards set by the American Petroleum Institute (API), American Society
of Testing and Materials (ASTM), and American National Standards Institute (ANSI).

Pipeline routes are determined based on factors like topography, geology, and proximity to CO 2
sources and storage sites. The general recommendation with respect to CO 2 pipeline routing
is that a standard approach as for route selection for hydrocarbon pipelines should be applied
(API n.d.)

Pipelines are usually buried underground; the burial depth varies depending on the local
geography along the pipeline route as well as such factors as water crossings, transportation
crossings (e.g., rail), residential proximity, frost line considerations, and others. Normal depth
requirements are 2 to 4 feet to the top of the pipe. Many transmission pipeline companies
transport CO2 to EOR and storage sites. Figure 6 presents the installation of a typical
transmission pipe in a remote location (PHMSA 2025b).

Carbon dioxide is pressurized as it travels through an interstate/intrastate pipeline to expedite its
flow. Pumping stations are essential to maintain the necessary pressure for efficient and safe
transportation and to ensure that the CO2 flowing through any one pipeline remains pressurized.
Pumping stations also play a role in controlling the flow rate of CO 2 through the pipeline.
Multiple pressure boosting, or “booster stations,” can be required to efficiently transport CO2
while maintaining the product in dense phase.

1.2 EXISTING U.S. STANDARDS AND REGULATIONS

In the United States, several federal statutes govern the regulation of pipelines, especially
pipeline safety. Regulation of hazardous liquids began with the Hazardous Liquids Pipeline
Safety Act of 1979 (PHMSA n.d.-b), which defined a hazardous liquid as “petroleum or any
petroleum product.” On July 12, 1991, CO2 was added to the regulations governing the
transportation of hazardous liquids by interstate pipelines (see Section 2.2; 49 CFR Part 195
(United States Code of Federal Regulations 2025)).4 Federal regulations for CO2 pipelines are
primarily overseen by the Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration (PHMSA), a
division of the U.S. Department of Transportation (DOT). PHMSA is responsible for developing
and enforcing regulations related to the design, construction, operation, and maintenance of
pipeline infrastructure in the United States, including supercritical CO 2 pipelines (United States
Code of Federal Regulations 2025). These regulations aim to protect public safety and the
environment by minimizing risks associated with CO2 pipeline transport; however, the
regulations do not apply to gaseous phase CO2.

The federal government holds statutory jurisdiction for siting CO2 pipelines when they cross
federal lands (Nordhaus and Pitlick n.d.). If any pipeline needs to traverse federal lands, federal
statutes apply for the part of it on federal land. The U.S. Department of the Interior Bureau of
Land Management (BLM), for example, has authority to grant rights-of-way for federal lands it
administers under the Mineral Leasing Act of 1920 (MLA) or the Federal Land Policy and
Management Act of 1976 (BLM 2016). Other federal lands (forests, for example) are regulated
by other agencies, such as the U.S. Department of Agriculture Forest Service. Pipelines granted a
right-of-way by BLM under the MLA will also carry a common-carrier transportation status.5

Common carrier status is afforded to those pipelines within BLM-granted right-of-way that offer
services to third parties (the public), subject to contractual agreements.

Regulations set forth in the U.S. Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) prescribe requirements for
the design, materials, construction, assembly, inspection, testing, operation, and maintenance of
pipeline systems. The pipeline operator must justify any deviations from such standards and will
be subject to inspection by the regulator. The most commonly used standards in North America
are detailed in regulation 49 CFR Part 195, “Transportation of Hazardous Liquids by Pipeline,”
in the United States and CSA Z662 (CER 2022) in Canada. Current PHMSA regulations at
49 CFR Part 195 prescribe hundreds of requirements on the construction, inspection,
maintenance, monitoring, and incident response for CO2 pipelines. PHMSA inspects and
enforces compliance with potential fines on pipeline operators violating federal CO 2 pipeline
safety requirements. Operators are required to conduct more rigorous inspections and testing for
pipelines located in areas near schools, residential areas, hospitals, and urban population centers.

Other regulations relate to specific operational situations, including how pipelines cross rivers,
streams, lakes, wetlands, and other bodies of water, intended to safeguard water quality, aquatic
ecosystems, and the surrounding environment. A full list of authorities related to CO2
transportation and sequestration is available from the EPA (n.d.).

Industry standards for CO2 pipelines focus on ensuring safety, reliability, and efficiency in the
transportation of carbon dioxide. These standards address pipeline design, construction,
operation, and maintenance, with an emphasis on preventing leaks, managing potential hazards,
and ensuring the integrity of the pipeline system. Industry standards such as the American
Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME) standard ASME B31.4 (ASME 2022) and API
Standard 1104 “Welding of Pipelines and Related Facilities” (API 2021) are incorporated by
reference within federal code.

Multiple government agencies work together to ensure pipeline safety and environmental
protection. State and local governments may have their own regulations and ordinances
regarding pipeline siting, construction, and operation, but these can vary significantly. Counties
across the Midwest states impacted by proposed CO2 pipelines—Iowa, South Dakota, North
Dakota, Nebraska, Illinois, and Minnesota—took action to file objections with state agencies
overseeing permitting processes and also put in place ordinances and moratoria on CO2 pipelines
(Heffinger 2023). In May 2022, the Minnesota Public Utilities Commission (MN PUC) issued a
ruling determining that it held authority to regulate carbon dioxide pipelines and initiated a
rulemaking to make its rules consistent with state and federal regulations. The MN PUC issued a
determination in January 2023 that carbon pipelines must undergo a full Environmental Impact
Statement (EIS) review process overseen by the agency. South Dakota’s governor signed a bill
into law on March 6, 2025, that bans the taking of private property for building CO2 pipelines
(AP News 2025). On the other hand, Nebraska’s current laws provide for no regulatory oversight
of CO2 pipelines, and no state agency is tasked with any permitting or review process (Heffinger
2023). The landscape for state and local governments is constantly changing based on constituent
feedback; it is important to confirm and understand the latest rulemaking with regard to pipeline
regulations.

1.3 CANADIAN STANDARDS AND REGULATIONS

The United States and Canada face similar safety challenges related to CO 2 pipeline transport,
including potential leaks and ruptures, and both have standards and regulations that oversee CO 2
pipeline operations and safety. The following information on Canadian standards and regulations
related to CO2 is intended to provide an example of international developments in transport and
storage.

Pipelines in Canada are regulated based on jurisdiction according to Canada’s constitution. In
Canada, the Canada Energy Regulator, or CER (formerly the National Energy Board) regulates
CO2 pipelines that cross provincial or international boundaries, while provinces regulate intra-
provincial pipelines, including those for CO2 transport. The CER uses oil and gas pipeline safety
standards, such as CSA Z662, to regulate CO2 pipelines.

The Canadian Energy Regulator Act, or CER Act, enacted on August 28, 2019, imposes absolute
liability and requires financial resources requirements for pipeline companies that are authorized
under the CER Act to construct or operate a pipeline (CER 2024). “Absolute liability” means
that a company authorized to construct or operate a pipeline is liable for unintended or
uncontrolled releases, without proof of fault or negligence, up to the limit of liability prescribed
by the CER Act or regulations. The absolute liability limit for a company that operates one or
more authorized pipelines that transport CO2 or water is $5 million (Canadian).

In Canada, provinces have jurisdiction over their subsurface resources and regulate geological
CO2 storage (CER 2025). Province regulations include the following:

 • Alberta has enacted a comprehensive regulatory framework for carbon capture and
 storage (CCS). The Alberta Energy Regulator regulates facilities that capture CO 2, CO2
 pipelines, and subsurface operations to store CO2.

 • Saskatchewan’s Ministry of Energy and Resources regulates the development of CO 2-
 dedicated underground storage in the province.

 • The British Columbia government passed the Energy Statutes Amendment Act in 2022
 updating its regulatory framework on CCS. The B.C. Energy Regulator is responsible for
 the regulation of CCS activity, including CO2 storage.

 • On June 4, 2024, the Manitoba government passed the Captured Carbon Storage Act
 establishing a regulatory framework for licensing and operating CCS in the province.

 • Ontario is taking a phased approach to enabling and regulating carbon storage in the
 province. In March 2023 it removed the prohibition to develop underground carbon
 storage. In June 2023 the Ontario government amended the Oil, Gas and Salt Resources

 11

 Act to enable “special projects” for carbon storage demonstration projects, followed by
 corresponding regulations in January 2024. As of August 2024, the province is working
 on the design of a regulatory framework for commercial geologic carbon storage projects.

The Government of Canada has a legal duty to consult Aboriginal groups whose Aboriginal and
treaty rights may be adversely affected by any proposed project that requires a federal decision.
For major pipeline projects over 40 kilometers (25 miles), the Government of Canada engages
Aboriginal groups early in the review process and also relies on the CER hearing processes, to
the extent possible, to fulfill its duty to consult. It may also engage Aboriginal groups on broader
issues, including identifying and advancing opportunities to enhance participation in pipeline
development, safety, and operations (e.g., monitoring, incident response, advancing the use of
federal programs to support training and skills development, etc.) (National Resources Canada
2025).

 2 SUMMARY OF DEPARTMENT OF TRANSPORTATION’S PIPELINE AND
 HAZARDOUS MATERIAL SAFETY ADMINISTRATION (PHMSA) SAFETY
 DATA AND RESEARCH

PHMSA within the U.S. Department of Transportation regulates the construction, operation, and
maintenance of CO2 pipelines, including compliance, general inspection, repair, material
inspection, transportation of pipe, welding, pipeline location, installation, valving, associated
equipment (pumping), protection, and record keeping.

CO2 pipelines have a strong safety record since their introduction in 1972, with some of the
lowest rates of incidents affecting people or the environment per mile of pipeline across the
United States (Great Plains Institute 2024).

Additionally, the U.S. Department of Energy has launched new programs that will support
regional pipeline development, including front-end engineering and design studies to bolster the
safe operation of the CO2 pipeline network and loans and grants for infrastructure construction
(Global CCS Institute 2024).

A CO2 pipeline accident must be reported to PHMSA if any one of the following events occur
(PHMSA 2024):

 • Explosion or fire not intentionally set by the operator.

 • Release of 5 gallons or more, or 5 barrels or more if release occurs during maintenance.

 • Injury requiring hospitalization or a death.

 • Estimated total property damage exceeding $50,000.

In the event of a reportable accident, an operator must submit an accident report to PHMSA
within 30 days and may be required to notify the National Response Center within one hour if
the accident meets certain criteria, such as a release from a facility or vessel in a quantity that
equals or exceeds the reportable quantity.

2.1 PIPELINE INCIDENT DATA

PHMSA publishes data from pipeline accident reports on its website (PHMSA 2023), which are
used in this section. These datasets include the mileage, incidents, causes, and consequences of
pipeline failures as well as the trends and inventories of pipeline infrastructure. The first
recorded CO2 pipeline accident was in 1994, after PHMSA was authorized to enforce safety
regulations related to CO2 pipelines beginning in 1988.

With regard to the recorded incidents involving CO2 pipelines, between 2002 and 2024, a total of
69 incidents related to onshore CO2 transmission pipelines were recorded in the PHMSA
database. Figure 9 shows the number of incidents recorded for the CO 2 pipeline from 2002 to
2024. During that time period, CO2 pipelines have had an average of 4.3 reportable incidents per

 13

year and have never had more than nine incidents in a single year. None of the recorded incidents
resulted in fatalities.

remained at near zero between 2004 and 2009. Incidents peaked in 2016 and 2017 (9 incidents
per year) and dropped sharply after 2017. The majority of reportable CO2 pipeline incidents
involve leaks 6 with only four ruptures 7 of CO2 pipelines during that time (the 2020 failure near
Satartia, MS, was reported as an “Other” type failure but could be better characterized as a
rupture).

Pipeline failure rates are usually defined based on miles of installed pipeline; this provides an
indication of the operating experience and the size of the data sample. The total length of
installed pipeline is fundamental to calculating the exposure, which is calculated as the length of
installed pipeline multiplied by its exposed duration and is expressed in kilometers-years.

pipelines have a lower annual pipeline failure rate compared to refined petroleum products.

refined petroleum products was even higher.
