 ABSTRACT

The Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies work package for fiscal year 2026, titled
“Component Manufacturing and Demonstrations from AM 316 SS,” includes three activities that
showcase qualities of additively manufactured (AM) 316 SS that are relevant to the domestic nuclear
power sector. The design and fabrication of capsules for the Idaho National Laboratory provide a basis for
studying the complex interactions of AM 316H SS with the salt used in molten salt nuclear reactors.
Printing compact, high-efficiency, defect-free triply periodic minimal surface heat exchangers may
enhance the performance of the Microreactor Applications Research Validation and Evaluation
(MARVEL) demonstration microreactor. Moreover, fabricating AM pre-forms of selected replacement
components may shorten the time required for repair and maintenance of nuclear reactor subsystems. This
report includes details of each activity and discusses opportunities to improve component quality and
qualification.

 1

 1. INTRODUCTION

The manufacturing demonstration efforts for fiscal year 2026 of the Advanced Materials and
Manufacturing Technologies (AMMT) work package, titled “Component Manufacturing and
Demonstrations from AM 316 SS,” have focused primarily on the design and fabrication of 316H
stainless steel (SS) capsules for studies at Idaho National Laboratory (INL) of the corrosion and radiolysis
of fuel salts used in molten salt reactors. Other demonstration activities include printing 316L SS pre-
forms for Electric Motor & Contracting Company, Inc. (EMC), for use in fabricating a shaft-locking
collar for a pump at the Surry Nuclear Power Plant and collaborating with INL to print 316H triply
periodic minimal surface (TPMS) structures for possible use as nuclear reactor heat exchangers.
Collectively, these efforts are intended to identify and showcase the potential of additively manufactured
(AM) 316 SS components relevant to the domestic nuclear power sector. This report contains a detailed
description of each activity.

 2. INL CAPSULES FOR RADIOLYSIS AND CORROSION

Understanding the properties of fuel salts and their interactions with 316H SS at high temperatures is
essential for the successful commercial deployment of molten salt reactors. The US Department of
Energy’s Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) has collaborated with INL to design and fabricate three
components for testing the properties of molten salts and fuel salts with AM 316H SS.

2.1 RADIOLYSIS CAPSULES

The first component, a 316H SS radiolysis capsule, was designed to capture for analysis the volatile
chemical byproducts arising from the radiolysis, or decomposition, of fuel salt exposed to pulses of high-
energy (8 MeV) electrons (Figure 1). The flux of high-energy electrons simulates the energetic ionizing
conditions found within an operating nuclear power reactor. The individual components of the capsule are
illustrated in Figure 1a.

In Figure 1b, a carrier gas flows into the Gas In tube of the assembly and over the fuel salt while pulses of
high-energy electrons irradiate and dissociate the salt or fuel salt. Volatile dissociative byproducts from
within the fuel salt are carried up and out the Gas Out tube for analysis. The simplicity of the capsule
design tends to reduce the reaction and recombination of byproducts and enhance the quality of the gas
analysis. Thirty radiolysis capsule tubes and 15 radiolysis down tubes were printed from 316H SS powder
using a Renishaw AM250 laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) printer and shipped to INL for testing
(Figure 2).

2.2 EX SITU CORROSION CAPSULES

A second 316H SS component printed for INL was an ex situ corrosion capsule (Figure 3). The
monolithic design includes a lower portion to contain a molten salt (or fuel salt) and an upper portion to
hold a solid, pelletized zeolite absorbent. A gas-permeable support, printed between the lower and upper
portions, maintains physical separation of the salt from the absorbent while allowing volatile off-gas from
the salt to be absorbed by the zeolite. This design facilitates loading of the solids (salt and absorbent)
without cross contamination.

 4

 Figure 3. INL corrosion capsule: (a) individual components of the capsule and (b) an assembly of the
 components for fuel salt corrosion testing.

Corrosion at the interface between the molten salt (or fuel salt) and the 316H capsule wall is examined
destructively by sectioning the capsule after testing. Twenty corrosion capsules were printed from 316H
SS powder with the Renishaw AM250 printer and shipped to INL for testing (Figure 2). Additionally, 17
such capsules were printed using a GE Aerospace (GE) Concept M2 L-PBF printer and shipped to INL
for testing (Figure 4).

Figure 4. Seventeen 316H SS corrosion capsules printed using a GE Concept M2 L-PBF printer and shipped
 to INL for testing.

2.3 IN SITU CORROSION LINER CAPSULES

A third 316H SS component printed for INL was a liner capsule for in situ corrosion studies in the INL
Advanced Test Reactor. The liner capsule was designed to enable a direct comparison of the corrosion
behavior of molten salt on two printed 316H SS surfaces: an as-printed surface and a machined surface.
Figure 5 shows the preparation sequence for fabricating a printed corrosion liner with both as-printed and

machined inner diameter and the other with a half-machined inner diameter—are shown in Figure 6b.

2.4 CAPSULE DISTORTION: A CLOSER LOOK

All three components previously described were printed with downward-facing hemispherical bases.
Printing such components typically requires a support structure that extends from the substrate to the
hemispherical base. The support structure should provide adequate mechanical and thermal connectivity
between the component and the substrate during printing while allowing the component to be removed
from the substrate after printing.

Two styles of extruded support structure were initially used to print the corrosion liners. One is a version
of the standard support provided with the slicing software that consists of an orthogonal array of fine
crosses; the other is an enhanced support that consists of a radial array of robust rectangles (Figure 7).
The walls of the crosses in the standard support are printed with single laser passes and are approximately
160 m thick. In contrast, the walls of the rectangles in the enhanced support are 500 m thick.

 7

 Figure 7. Cross-sectional views of the support structures used for printing the corrosion liners: (a) standard
 support and (b) enhanced support.

Although the fine crosses of the standard support may tend to break away from the base of the liner more
easily after printing than the thick rectangular members of the enhanced supports, they may also be more
susceptible to lateral flexure during powder recoating, which in turn may contribute to component
distortion. To test this hypothesis, the lower portion of five liner capsules were printed—one on 10 mm
long thick enhanced supports and the others on 2.5, 5.0, 7.5, and 10 mm long thin standard supports.
Figure 8 shows the liner capsules as viewed from the direction first encountered by the recoater (Figure
8a) and from the direction last encountered by the recoater (Figure 8b).

 8

Figure 8. Optical images of the lower portions of five liners printed with different support types and lengths,
 as annotated: (a) viewed from the direction first encountered by the recoater and (b) viewed from the
direction last encountered by the recoater. (Abbreviations: EXL = enhanced extra long; SL = standard long;
 SM = standard medium; SS = standard short; SXL = standard extra long.)

For the standard supports viewed from the direction first encountered by the recoater (Figure 8a),
distortion of the liner base above the points of attachment of the supports is evident and appears to
increase with increasing support length. No such distortion is evident for the enhanced supports viewed
from either direction or for the standard supports viewed from the direction last encountered by the
recoater (Figure 8b).

To quantitatively assess the distortion after printing, the liners were removed from the substrate and
supports and scanned with a GOM ATOS Q 3D scanner using structured blue light. Figure 9 shows both
optical images of the freestanding liners and GOM deviation color maps of the ends of each liner.

 9

Figure 9. Side-view images of the lower portions of liners printed with various support types and lengths and
 their corresponding top-view GOM deviation color maps.

For the standard supports, the spatially averaged magnitude of deviation increases with increasing support
length. For each liner, the deviation is generally negative on the side of the liner facing the approaching
recoater (i.e., the left side) and positive on the side of the liner facing the retreating recoater (i.e., the right
side). As shown in Figure 9, very little deviation is evident for the liner printed with 10 mm enhanced
supports.

A simple mechanism that accounts for the deviations observed in Figure 9 is illustrated in Figure 10. The
force exerted by the moving recoater on the printed material permanently shifts the material laterally in
the direction of recoater motion for the mechanically compliant standard supports. No such shift occurs
for the rigid enhanced supports. Because the lateral compliance of the standard supports increases with
increasing support length, greater lateral redistribution of material is expected (and observed in Figure 9)
for longer standard supports.

 10

 Figure 10. Illustration of a possible mechanism for material redistribution on compliant supports.

Figure 11a indicates a somewhat more complex distortion mechanism for the liners printed with standard
supports. It shows diametric planar sections of the CAD model and the GOM data map for the outer wall
of the liner with 10 mm standard supports. A comparison of these sections suggests that the recoater
displaces the printed material along its path both to the left (as illustrated in Figure 10) and downward
beneath the level of the powder bed.

 Figure 11. (a) Diametric planar sections of the CAD model and the GOM data map for the outer wall of the
 liner with 10 mm standard supports and (b) pairs of optical images captured before and after powder
 recoating using side-light illumination for various layers of the liner.

Replotting the data in Figure 11a, Figure 12a shows the material displacement along the diametric section
more explicitly as a deviation from CAD for liners with 2.5, 5.0, and 10 mm long standard supports and
10 mm long enhanced supports. Where the deviation is negative, the outer edge of the liner lies below the

 11

level at which the powder was melted. This corresponds to threshold radial distances of about +5, +3, +2,
and 0 mm for the standard support lengths of 2.5, 5.0, and 10 mm and the enhanced support length of 10
mm, respectively. For radial distances less than these thresholds, abnormally printed features lie below
the powder bed level and may not be directly visible from overhead. Conversely, for radial distances
greater than these thresholds, anomalous features may be visually detectable from overhead.

  Figure 12. (a) GOM deviation from the CAD model for diametric planar sections of the outer walls of the
 liners and (b) swelling anomaly intensity as a function of print radius.

To better understand the printing process, tools have been developed at ORNL [1–3] to collect images
during printing and to identify features, referred to as anomaly classes, of these in situ, layer-wise images.
Figure 11b shows pairs of optical images taken before (side-light image) and after (post-recoater image)
powder recoating of the liner with 10 mm standard supports using side-light illumination for selected
layers. Features in these images can be used to help detect characteristics of the liner, such as the onset
and development of distortion. As an example of qualitative image analysis, the post-recoater image at
layer 255 shows no indication of the underlying liner, which is a result expected for a normal layer. With
additional layers, however, the right side of the liner becomes increasingly evident up to layer 325. This
behavior roughly mimics the development of depletion on the right side of the liner, as depicted in
Figure 11a.

Results of a quantitative analysis of images collected during the printing of the liners are shown in Figure
12b. The results show the intensities of the swelling anomaly class as a function of the print radius of the
liner base for several support lengths. The swelling anomaly intensity exceeds the noise floor at and
above a print radius of about 5.5 mm for all standard support lengths. For radii greater than about 5.5 mm,
the swelling anomaly intensity (Figure 12b) mimics the degree of GOM deviation from CAD
(Figure 12a).

In summary, the ability to measure the geometric deviations of a component from its solid model after
printing is useful to ensure quality and dimensional compliance. Even better is the ability to assess these
deviations during printing. The analysis of layer-wise, in situ, high-resolution images conducted at ORNL

 12

[1–3] potentially provides a means to assess these deviations while printing. Because some material
displacement occurs below the powder bed during the printing of these liners and some occurs after
printing because of thermal contraction, analysis of in situ overhead images has some limitations in its
sensitivity to the resulting deviations. Such an analysis, when conducted during the printing process, can
nevertheless provide a valuable prediction of the printed result.

 3. AM PRE-FORMS FOR EMC

EMC is a small business located in Chesapeake, Virginia, that specializes in electric motor and pump
repair, remanufacturing, and reengineering for the US nuclear energy sector. To reduce the time required
for component fabrication, ORNL partnered with EMC to explore the possibility of printing near-net-
shape pre-forms of a component for subsequent inventorying and machining to final dimensions upon
receipt of an order from a customer. A simple component—a lock ring on a motor powering a pump for a
non-safety-critical pump in a nuclear power plant—was chosen for demonstration.

Figure 13a shows two 316L SS printed pre-forms and four mechanical test blanks. The printing was
accomplished using a GE Concept M2 L-PBF printer and required 134 h of machine time to complete.
The chemical analysis and tensile properties of the as-printed material in both the vertical and horizontal
orientations met the ASTM A276/A276N-25 requirements for hot-finish 316 SS. One pre-form ring was
machined at EMC and provided to a customer for installation (Figure 13b). The successful outcome of
this effort has stimulated discussions with EMC regarding follow-on demonstrations, including a pre-
form for a 450 lb thrust runner for a major nuclear reactor supplier.

 machined lock ring awaiting shipment to a customer for installation in a pump motor.

 4. TPMS HEAT EXCHANGERS

TPMS structures have recently attracted interest as building blocks for compact, high-efficiency heat
exchangers for nuclear energy applications at INL [4]. Because the surfaces of these structures include
periodic, unsupported saddle points, printing TPMS heat exchangers using L-PBF can be challenging.
Anomalies during printing at and near saddle points can lead to flaws in the printed walls and failure of
the heat exchanger. Furthermore, detecting flaws can be difficult, particularly within large heat
exchangers.

ORNL is developing sensor hardware and analytics based on machine learning to collect and analyze
images during the printing of AM components. To test the effectiveness of these tools for detecting flaws,

 14

INL has provided solid models of representative TPMS structures of several scales and wall thicknesses
for L-PBF printing.

Figure 14 shows optical images of nine TPMS cubes printed on a substrate with 316H SS using L-PBF.
Three large cubes, 40 mm on a side, were printed with various wall thicknesses (thick—2.50 mm,
medium—1.25 mm, and thin—0.75 mm). Scaled versions of these cubes were also printed (medium
cubes, 20 mm on a side, and small cubes, 10 mm on a side). When viewed from above, little evidence of
surface flaws is apparent for any cube.

 Figure 14. Optical images of nine TPMS cubes printed on a substrate with 316H SS using L-PBF: (a)
 isometric view and (b) top view.

After printing, the cubes were removed from the substrate using wire electrical discharge machining
(EDM) and visually examined. Figure 15a shows images of the cubes from above (top view) and below
(bottom view). Although little surface distortion is visible near the saddle points of any of the cubes when
viewed from above, it appears to increase when either the wall thickness or cube size increases when
viewed from below. These qualitative trends are illustrated in Figure 15b.

Figure 15. (a) Top and bottom views of the nine TPMS cubes after removal from the substrate and (b) images
 showing trends in the magnitude of surface distortion on the undersides of the saddle points with cube size
 and wall thickness.

 15

Throughout printing, images of the powder bed were recorded for each layer (50 µm thickness) both
immediately after laser melting was completed and after the recoater distributed the next layer of powder.
Analysis of these images resulted in the identification of various anomalies within each cube. Figure 16
shows an example of the outcome of this analysis. The layer-average over melt anomaly intensity is
shown as a function of height for each cube.

  Figure 16. Layer-average over melt anomaly intensity as a function of height for the TPMS cubes. The blue
 arrows show the approximate heights at which saddle points are printed. Note that the over melt intensity peaks for
the thick-walled cubes (green) occur at lower heights than those for the medium-walled (red) and thin-walled (black)
 cubes. This occurs because saddle-point printing begins at lower heights for thick walls than for thinner walls.

For the same cube size, the over melt intensity peak for the thick-walled cube (green) in Figure 16 occurs
at a lower height than those for the medium- and thin-walled cubes (red and black, respectively).
Similarly, the saddle point for the thick-walled cube occurs at a lower height than those for the medium-
and thin-walled cubes. This correlation may suggest sensitivity of the over melt anomaly to features of the
cube in the vicinity of the saddle point.

To examine this notion, the over melt intensity integrated over the build height for each cube is calculated
and shown in Figure 17a. The integrated over melt intensity generally increases with both wall thickness
and cube size, except for the small 10 mm cube, which shows little over melt for all wall thicknesses.
Figure 17b shows an optical image of the underside of each cube. A qualitative assessment of the saddle-
point regions of the cubes shows trends similar to those in Figure 15b. This suggests that over melt
intensity derived from the analysis of in situ images may be a suitable metric for tracking saddle-point
flaws in TPMS structures during L-PBF printing. A quantitative measure of surface topology, such as
x-ray computed tomography (XCT), would be helpful for clarifying this approach to flaw detection, but
such measurements are outside the present scope of this effort.

Figure 17. (a) Over melt intensity integrated over the build height for each cube and (b) optical images of the
 underside of each TPMS cube.

 5. CONCLUSIONS

The activities for the AMMT Component Manufacturing and Demonstrations from AM 316 SS work
package for fiscal year 2026 have been described—AM capsules for molten salt studies, AM pre-forms,
and AM TPMS heat exchangers. These activities highlight opportunities provided by AM to meet the
needs of the nuclear energy sector. Both the fabrication and qualification of components have been
discussed and suggest pathways for refinement, as well as limitations, of digital qualification based on in
situ imaging.
