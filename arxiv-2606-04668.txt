Cluster dynamics in a two-group Stuart-Landau model analyzed by the second-order phase reduction Preprint: APS/123-QED
Yernur Baibolatov, Oleh E. Omel’chenko, Michael Rosenblum

Abstract
We analyze cluster states in an ensemble of Stuart-Landau oscillators with two subpopulations of different frequencies. Our main goal is to compare the descriptions of the system’s dynamics obtained via the standard first-order phase approximation and the second-order phase reduction. We demonstrate that the second-order model not only provides quantitative improvements in the description but also reveals new dynamical states not present in the standard Kuramoto theory. In particular, it describes bistability of synchronous states in the minimal setup of two coupled oscillators and the existence of three-cluster states, forbidden in the first-order phase description by the Watanabe-Strogatz theory. The very good agreement between the second-order approximation results and the results of numerical simulations of the original Stuart-Landau network highlights the usefulness of high-order phase-reduction models.



Phase approximation is a widely used standard tool for analyzing
weakly coupled oscillatory networks. Typically, phase oscillator
models are derived via first-order phase reduction; for a network of
interacting Stuart-Landau oscillators, this yields the celebrated
Kuramoto model. Many recent studies have sought to extend this
approach by deriving high-order phase oscillator models as power
series in the coupling strength. Here, we use the second-order model
to analyze cluster states in a system of coupled Stuart-Landau units;
the population consists of two groups of equal size, each with its own
frequency. We demonstrate that, under certain conditions, when the
interaction in the first-order approximation vanishes,
accounting for quadratic terms becomes crucial and reveals new effects.
For example, in the simplest case of
two interacting oscillators, a bistability between in-
and anti-phase synchronous states occurs, and its analytical description fully agrees with the
numerical simulation for the original Stuart-Landau model. Moreover, only exploiting the second-order approximation, we can explain the three-cluster states in which the fastest
group splits into two clusters, while the slow one forms one. Such
states do not exist in the first-order Kuramoto model - they are
forbidden by the Watanabe-Strogatz theory - but are present in the
Stuart-Landau network. With these examples, our study demonstrates the
usefulness of high-order phase reduction
.

Kuramoto developed his now-classical model by applying the phase-reduction technique to obtain a simplified description of a population of weakly interacting Stuart-Landau (SL) oscillators as the first-order approximation in the coupling strength. For many years, the higher-order approximations remained a theoretical challenge; only recently have many groups tackled this problem, going beyond the leading order. The most interesting features of this model are the appearance of multi-body interaction terms and pairwise interaction terms for non-connected units, as well as frequency dependence of the terms’ coefficients. Of particular interest are qualitative results, when the second-order phase equations describe effects not captured by the traditional Kuramoto model.

In this paper, we present another example of a second-order analysis of an oscillatory network. We study the dynamics of a two-group population of SL units and demonstrate that accounting for second-order terms not only quantitatively improves the system’s description but also reveals new effects not captured in the first-order phase approximation. Remarkably, the second-order model allows for an analytical treatment. As a result, we obtain a detailed description of different cluster states, their stability, and synchronization transitions involving them.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section II, we present the two-group Stuart-Landau model and its second-order phase approximation.
In Section III, we analyze the simplest setup of two coupled oscillators. Next, Section IV presents the analysis of the two- and the three-cluster states. In Section V, we discuss our results. Some technical details are given in Appendices.

III
The simplest case: two oscillators

In this section, we consider the simplest case of model (
2
),
where each population consists of a single oscillator, i.e,
N_{\mathrm{g}}=1
.
We simplify the notations:
using
\phi_{1,2}
and
\omega_{1,2}
instead of
\phi_{1}^{(n)}
and
\omega^{(n)}
and expanding the sums, we explicitly write:

\dot{\phi}_{1}=\omega_{1}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sin\alpha-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sin\left(\phi_{1,2}-\phi_{2,1}-\alpha\right)+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8\kappa}\biggl(-\sin(2\alpha)+\sin(2\phi_{1,2}-2\phi_{2,1})-\sin(2\phi_{1,2}-2\phi_{2,1}-2\alpha)\biggr)\,.

(7)

Introducing
\psi=\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}
and

\delta=\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}
, and subtracting equations for
\dot{\phi}_{1,2}
, we obtain

\dot{\psi}=\delta-\varepsilon\cos\alpha\sin\psi+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4\kappa}\biggl(1-\cos(2\alpha)\biggr)\sin(2\psi)=\delta+f(\psi)\;,

(8)

where the quadratic in
\varepsilon
term represents the second-order correction to the well-known
Adler equation.

To introduce some notations, we recall the solution of the Adler equation

\dot{\psi}=\delta-\varepsilon\cos\alpha\sin\psi
. The synchronous solution
\psi=\text{const}
exists
within the triangular Arnold tongue; its semi-width for some coupling strength
\varepsilon
is

D=|\varepsilon\cos\alpha|
. The value of the constant phase shift
\psi
depends on the
detuning
\delta
. For attractive coupling,
\varepsilon\cos\alpha>0
, one has

-\Psi\leq\psi\leq\Psi
, with the maximal phase shift
\Psi=\pi/2
(the in-phase solution).
For repulsive coupling,
\varepsilon\cos\alpha<0
, one has

\pi-\Psi\leq\psi\leq\pi+\Psi
(the anti-phase solution).
As shown below, the tongue’s semi-width and maximal phase shift generally differ for the in-phase and anti-phase states; therefore, we use the notations
D_{\mathrm{i,a}}
and
\Psi_{\mathrm{i,a}}
in the following. We will also see that in the second-order approximation,
\Psi_{\mathrm{i,a}}
is generally not constant but depends on
\varepsilon,\alpha
.

Let us start the analysis of the second-order Adler equation by considering
two special cases.

•

Case 1,
\alpha=0
. The second-order correction vanishes, Eq. (
8
) reduces to

\dot{\psi}=\delta-\varepsilon\sin\psi\;.

Depending on the sign of
\varepsilon
, we have either an in-phase or an anti-phase solution.
The tongue’s semi-width is
D_{\mathrm{i}}=D_{\mathrm{a}}=|\varepsilon|
and

\Psi_{\mathrm{i}}=\Psi_{\mathrm{a}}=\pi/2
.

•

Case 2,
\alpha=\pi/2
. Now, the first-order term vanishes, meaning that in the first approximation, the coupling is neutral and there is no synchrony for any
\varepsilon
.
The analysis of the second-order equation

\dot{\psi}=\delta+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2\kappa}\sin(2\psi)\;

is simple and yields the synchrony condition
|\delta|\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2|\kappa|}
(we recall
the Floquet exponent
\kappa<0
). An
interesting feature is the appearance of bistability: in the synchronous state,

\sin(2\psi)=2|\kappa|\delta/\varepsilon^{2}
; if some
\psi_{0}
satisfies this equation,

\psi_{0}+\pi
is the root, too.
Thus, the in-phase states
-\pi/4\leq\psi\leq\pi/4
and anti-phase states

\pi-\pi/4\leq\psi\leq\pi+\pi/4
coexist.
Hence,
D_{\mathrm{i}}=D_{\mathrm{a}}=\varepsilon^{2}/2|\kappa|
and the phase shift variation parameter

\Psi_{\mathrm{i}}=\Psi_{\mathrm{a}}=\pi/4
.

(9)

With account of
\cos(2\psi)=2\cos^{2}\psi-1
, this yields a quadratic equation for
\cos\psi
; existence of two roots means bistability.
We rewrite Eq.

IV
Cluster states

So far, we have analyzed the simplest case of two coupled oscillators. We now turn to the general case
N_{g}\gg 1
,
where each population contains many oscillators. In this regime, the system exhibits various cluster states, which
we classify using the
(p+q)
notation: the first group splits into
p
clusters and the second group into
q

clusters, with all oscillators within each cluster sharing the same phase.

The most common regime is the
(1+1)
state, where each group forms a single cluster. The entire ensemble of
2N_{g}

oscillators then behaves as a system of two macroscopic oscillators, and the phase difference between the clusters
obeys the same second-order Adler equation (
8
) as in the two-oscillator case. Depending on the parameters,
the two clusters can be mutually synchronized or asynchronous. The crucial difference from the genuine
two-oscillator system is that clusters can lose their stability and evaporate, i.e., individual oscillators can
detach from the cluster, destroying the cluster state.

Beyond the
(1+1)
state, we also observe the
(2+1)
regime, where one (faster) group splits into two clusters
while the other group remains synchronized. We suspect that other
(p+q)
combinations are possible in
principle, but have not observed them in our simulations. In all cases, we observed that the clusters within each
group are of equal size, although our analysis does not rely on this property.

To analyze the cluster states, it is convenient to collect the terms with
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\phi_{i}^{(n)}}

and rewrite Eq. (
6
) in the following form:

\dot{\phi}_{i}^{(n)}=\Omega^{(n)}+\mathrm{Im}\left[f_{1}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\phi_{i}^{(n)}}+f_{2}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\phi_{i}^{(n)}}\right],

(13)

where
f_{1}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)
and
f_{2}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)

are the functions of the mean fields only, and
\Omega^{(n)}
are constants. In the
(p+q)
state the phases of
the oscillators of the first group are
\phi_{i}^{(1)}\in\{\Theta_{1},\dots,\Theta_{p}\}

and the phases of the oscillators of the second group are
\phi_{i}^{(2)}\in\{\Phi_{1},\dots,\Phi_{q}\}
, where
\Theta_{s}

and
\Phi_{r}
are phases of the clusters of the first and second groups, respectively. Next, we can write
equations of the form (
13
) for each
\Theta_{s}
and
\Phi_{r}
:

\displaystyle\dot{\Theta}_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}+\mathrm{Im}\left[f_{1}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Theta_{s}}+f_{2}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\Theta_{s}}\right],\qquad s=1,\dots,p,

(14)

\displaystyle\dot{\Phi}_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Omega_{2}+\mathrm{Im}\left[f_{1}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Phi_{r}}+f_{2}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\Phi_{r}}\right],\qquad r=1,\dots,q.

(15)

To assess the transversal stability of each cluster, i.e., whether individual oscillators remain locked to their
cluster or evaporate from it, we follow
13
;
25
, and add a virtual
test oscillator to each cluster. This oscillator is subject to the same forcing as all cluster elements but does not contribute to the mean field.
By linearizing Eqs. (
14
)
and (
15
)
for a small perturbation of the test oscillator,
we obtain the linear equations that determine the cluster’s stability.
The only assumption underlying the stability analysis is that each cluster is sufficiently large so that the effect of a single oscillator’s evaporation, which is of order
\sim N^{-1}
, has a negligible effect on the mean field.

More specifically, the phases of the test oscillators
can be written as
\theta_{s}=\Theta_{s}+\delta\theta_{s}
and
\phi_{r}=\Phi_{r}+\delta\phi_{r}
,
where
\delta\theta_{s}
and
\delta\phi_{r}
denote the deviations from the dynamics of the corresponding clusters. For small

\delta\theta_{s}
and
\delta\phi_{r}
, the exponent terms linearize as

\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\delta\theta_{s}}\approx 1-\mathrm{i}\delta\theta_{s}
and
\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\delta\theta_{s}}\approx 1-2\mathrm{i}\delta\theta_{s}
, and we obtain the instantaneous growth-rate factors

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\delta\theta_{s}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}=\Lambda_{s}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\delta\theta_{s},\qquad\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\delta\phi_{r}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}=\Lambda_{r}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\delta\phi_{r},

where

\displaystyle\Lambda_{s}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Re}\left[f_{1}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Theta_{s}}+2f_{2}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\Theta_{s}}\right],

(16)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{r}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Re}\left[f_{1}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Phi_{r}}+2f_{2}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\Phi_{r}}\right].

(17)

The transversal Lyapunov exponents (TLEs)
of the
(p+q)
-cluster state are defined
as the average decay/growth rates
of the test oscillator perturbations; they are given by

\lambda_{s,r}=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Lambda_{s,r}\left(Z^{(1,2)}(t),Z_{2}^{(1,2)}(t);\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\;.

(18)

Note that from the original phase-reduced equations (
6
), we have

\displaystyle\Omega_{1,2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{1,2}-\varepsilon\sin\alpha,

(19)

\displaystyle f_{1}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2\kappa}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}\right)\xi_{1}-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8\kappa}\xi_{2},

(20)

\displaystyle f_{2}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8\kappa}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\alpha}\xi_{1}^{2},

(21)

where

\xi_{1}=Z^{(1)}+Z^{(2)},\qquad\xi_{2}=\left(\overline{Z}^{(1)}+\overline{Z}^{(2)}\right)\left(Z_{2}^{(1)}+Z_{2}^{(2)}\right).

(22)

Therefore, to analyze the state with a specific cluster configuration, we only need to express the mean
fields
Z
,
Z_{2}
. In particular, for clusters of equal sizes within each group, the mean fields of a general
(p+q)
state are given by

\displaystyle Z^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\sum_{s=1}^{p}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{s}},\qquad Z^{(2)}=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{r=1}^{q}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Phi_{r}},

(23)

\displaystyle Z_{2}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\sum_{s=1}^{p}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\Theta_{s}},\qquad Z_{2}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{r=1}^{q}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\Phi_{r}}.

(24)

IV.1
Two clusters

The simplest cluster state configuration is the two-cluster state, or
(1+1)
state with
p=1
and
q=1
. In this state,
the oscillators in each group synchronize; the phases fulfill
\phi_{i}^{(1)}=\Theta
and
\phi_{i}^{(2)}=\Phi
,
for all
i=1,\ldots,N_{g}
.
The cluster phases
\Theta
and
\Phi
obey Eqs. (
14
),(
15
).
Treating each cluster like a macroscopic oscillator,
we write, in analogy to Eq. (
8
), the second-order Adler equation for the phase shift

\Psi=\Theta-\Phi
:

\dot{\Psi}=\delta-\varepsilon\sin\Psi\cos\alpha+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4\kappa}\biggl(1-\cos(2\alpha)\biggr)\sin(2\Psi)\,.

(25)

We emphasize the crucial difference from the two-oscillator case: Eq. (
25
) is valid only as long as the clusters remain stable.

Before proceeding with the analysis of the clusters’ stability, we mention that since two clusters behave like two macroscopic oscillators,
the same locking conditions apply.
Thus, for given
\varepsilon
, we find the range of frequency detuning
\delta
such that the phase difference
\Psi

remains constant.
(Similarly to the two-oscillator case, we write for this constant

-\Psi_{\mathrm{i}}^{(c)}\leq\Psi\leq\Psi_{\mathrm{i}}^{(c)}
and
\pi-\Psi_{\mathrm{a}}^{(c)}\leq\Psi\leq\pi+\Psi_{\mathrm{a}}^{(c)}
, for the in-phase and anti-phase solutions, respectively.)
However, we expect (and confirm below) that the synchrony domain is smaller than for the two-oscillator case due to cluster instability.

IV.1.1
Stability of the two-cluster solution in the second-order approximation

We quantify the transversal stability of the
(1+1)
state using Eqs. (
16
,
17
). Substituting the mean fields for the
(1+1)

state
Z^{(1)}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta}
,
Z^{(2)}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Phi}
, and
Z_{2}^{(1)}=\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\Theta}
,
Z_{2}^{(2)}=\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\Phi}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}(\Psi;\alpha,\varepsilon,\kappa)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\biggl(\cos\alpha+\cos(\Psi-\alpha)-\frac{\varepsilon}{4\kappa}\bigl[1-2\cos(\Psi-2\alpha)+\cos(2\Psi)+2\cos\Psi-2\cos(2\Psi-2\alpha)\bigr]\biggr)\,,

(26)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}(\Psi;\alpha,\varepsilon,\kappa)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\biggl(\cos\alpha+\cos(\Psi+\alpha)-\frac{\varepsilon}{4\kappa}\bigl[1-2\cos(\Psi+2\alpha)+\cos(2\Psi)+2\cos\Psi-2\cos(2\Psi+2\alpha)\bigr]\biggl)\,.

(27)

Thus, clusters are stable if the expressions in the brackets are (on average) positive.

Below, we discuss separately synchronous and asynchronous cluster states. Synchrony in this context means that two clusters adjust their frequencies and have some constant phase shift. More precisely, the synchronous cluster state
corresponds to a stable fixed point
\Psi_{*}

of the reduced Eq. (
25
),
while the asynchronous cluster state
corresponds to its drifting solution.
Note that according to our previous consideration
of Adler equation (
8
), there are two types
of synchronous cluster states:
synchronous in-phase states (with
\cos\Psi_{*}>0
)
and synchronous anti-phase state (with
\cos\Psi_{*}<0
).
Next, we want to analyze all these cluster states
for their transversal stability,
using the general framework developed at the beginning of this section.
Since the synchronous cluster state corresponds to a fixed point
\Psi_{*}
of Eq. (
25
),
the transversal Lyapunov exponents of this state are given by

\lambda_{1,2}=\Lambda_{1,2}(\Psi_{*};\alpha,\varepsilon,\kappa)\,.

(28)

On the other hand, in the case of an asynchronous

(1+1)
cluster state, Eq. (
25
)
has a drifting solution
\Psi(t)
.
Therefore, the expressions of
\Lambda_{1,2}(\cdot)

depend on time, and the corresponding TLEs
are given by formulas

\lambda_{1,2}=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Lambda_{1,2}(\Psi(t);\alpha,\varepsilon,\kappa)\,\mathrm{d}t\,,

which we write in an equivalent form using the ergodicity property:

\lambda_{1,2}=\frac{1}{C}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\Lambda_{1,2}(\psi;\alpha,\varepsilon,\kappa)}{\left|\delta-\varepsilon\sin\psi\left(\cos\alpha-\frac{\varepsilon}{\kappa}\sin^{2}\alpha\cos\psi\right)\right|}\,\mathrm{d}\psi\,,

(29)

where

C=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\psi}{\left|\delta-\varepsilon\sin\psi\left(\cos\alpha-\frac{\varepsilon}{\kappa}\sin^{2}\alpha\cos\psi\right)\right|}\,.

(30)

Before presenting the results of TLE computation, we, for comparison, obtain TLE in the framework of the standard first-order phase reduction.

IV.1.2
Comparison of the results of the first- and second-order approximation

We obtain the corresponding expressions neglecting the terms
\sim\varepsilon^{2}
terms in Eqs. (

,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1,2}(\Psi,\alpha,\varepsilon,\kappa)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\biggl(\cos\alpha+\cos(\Psi\mp\alpha)\biggr)\,,

(31)

and

\lambda_{1,2}=\frac{1}{C}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\Lambda_{1,2}(\psi,\alpha,\varepsilon,\kappa)}{\left|\delta-\varepsilon\sin\psi\cos\alpha\right|}\,\mathrm{d}\psi\,,\qquad C=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\psi}{\left|\delta-\varepsilon\sin\psi\cos\alpha\right|}\,.

(32)

Synchronous state.

In the first-order approximation, we deal with the standard Adler equation

\dot{\Psi}=\delta-\varepsilon\cos\alpha\sin\Psi\;.

(33)

If
\cos\alpha\neq 0
its
properties are determined by the ratio

\chi=\delta/\varepsilon\cos\alpha\;.

(34)

If
|\chi|<1
, the Adler equation has two fixed points:

\Psi_{\mathrm{i}}=\arcsin\chi\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Psi_{\mathrm{a}}=\pi-\arcsin\chi\,.

(35)

Moreover, if
\cos\alpha>0
,
then
\Psi_{\mathrm{i}}
is stable and
\Psi_{\mathrm{a}}
is unstable.
On the contrary, if
\cos\alpha<0
,
then
\Psi_{\mathrm{i}}
is unstable and
\Psi_{\mathrm{a}}
is stable (we remind that we assumed
\varepsilon>0
).
Recalling Eq. (
28
) and substituting
\Psi_{\mathrm{i,a}}
into Eq. (
31
), we
explicitly compute TLEs
\lambda_{1,2}^{(\mathrm{i})}
and

\lambda_{1,2}^{(\mathrm{a})}
for the in-phase and anti-phase locked states, respectively (see Appendix
A
). We demonstrate that the anti-phase solution is always unstable, while the in-phase locked state can be stable with respect to “evaporation” of oscillators from a cluster for certain values of
\alpha
and
\delta
. Namely,
for
0<\alpha<\pi/4
, the clusters are stable within the whole locking domain determined by the Adler equation,
|\delta|<\varepsilon\cos\alpha
, while
for
\pi/4<\alpha<\pi/2
, the clusters lose their stability at the critical value

\delta_{\mathrm{cr}}^{(\mathrm{i})}=\pm\varepsilon\cos\alpha\sin 2\alpha\;.

We illustrate these results in Fig.
4
.

Asynchronous state.

In the case
|\chi|>1
, Eq. (
33
) has
a drifting solution and we have to compute the integrals in Eq. (

). Performing these computations in Appendix
A
, we find that the clusters are stable for
0<\alpha<\pi/4
, while for

\pi/4<\alpha<\pi/2
they are stable for

|\delta|>\delta_{\mathrm{cr}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sin\alpha}\,.

In the second-order approximation, we find the boundaries of the

More precisely, we obtain the stability

IV.2
Three clusters

Another cluster state that we observe is the
(2+1)
state, when the group with higher frequency

\omega_{1}>\omega_{2}
splits into two equally sized clusters with phases
\Theta_{1}
and
\Theta_{2}
,
while the oscillators in the second group merge into a single cluster with phase
\Phi_{1}
.
The dynamics of this state is determined by Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
),
where we set
p=2
and
q=1
:

\displaystyle\dot{\Theta}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}+\mathrm{Im}\left[f_{1}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Theta_{1}}+f_{2}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\Theta_{1}}\right]\,,

(37)

\displaystyle\dot{\Theta}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}+\mathrm{Im}\left[f_{1}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Theta_{2}}+f_{2}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\Theta_{2}}\right]\,,

(38)

\displaystyle\dot{\Phi}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Omega_{2}+\mathrm{Im}\left[f_{1}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Phi_{1}}+f_{2}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\Phi_{1}}\right]\,,

(39)

and where
Z^{(1)}=(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{1}}+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta_{2}})/2
and
Z^{(2)}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Phi_{1}}
.
The stability of this state depends on two factors.
First, the configuration
\Theta_{1}(t)
,
\Theta_{2}(t)
, and
\Phi_{1}(t)
should be a stable solution of the system (
37
)–(
39
).
We call this macroscopic stability.
Second, each of the three clusters should be transversally stable
such that neither cluster evaporates.
This type of stability can be analyzed
by the instantaneous growth-rate factors
given by Eqs. (
16
)
and (
17
).
For the specific
(2+1)
state, they read

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\Theta_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Re}\left[f_{1}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Theta_{1}}+2f_{2}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\Theta_{1}}\right],

(40)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\Theta_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Re}\left[f_{1}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Theta_{2}}+2f_{2}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\Theta_{2}}\right],

(41)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\Phi_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Re}\left[f_{1}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Phi_{1}}+2f_{2}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\Phi_{1}}\right],

(42)

where the factors
\Lambda_{\Theta_{1}}
,
\Lambda_{\Theta_{2}}
, and
\Lambda_{\Phi_{1}}
,
correspond to clusters with phases
\Theta_{1}
,
\Theta_{2}
, and
\Phi_{1}
, respectively.

System (
37
)–(
39
) allows for a detailed bifurcation analysis,
since using the nondegenerate linear transformation

(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2},\Phi_{1})\rightarrow((\Theta_{1}+\Theta_{2})/2,(\Theta_{1}-\Theta_{2})/2,\Theta_{1}-\Phi_{1})
, it can be rewritten in an equivalent form with a r.h.s.,
which depends only on two phase differences
\Theta_{1}-\Theta_{2}
and
\Theta_{1}-\Phi_{1}

(see Appendix
B
). This means that the dynamics of system (
37
)–(
39
) is mainly determined by the dynamics
of the corresponding two-dimensional system
on a torus parameterized by
\Theta_{1}-\Theta_{2}
and
\Theta_{1}-\Phi_{1}
.
By fixing the frequencies
\omega_{1}>\omega_{2}
in this system
and varying the phase lag
\alpha
from small to large values,
we typically observe the following bifurcation scenario,
see Fig.
6
. For small values of
\alpha
,
system (
37
)–(
39
) has two solutions:
a stable symmetric solution with
\Theta_{1}=\Theta_{2}

and an unstable asymmetric solution with
\Theta_{1}\neq\Theta_{2}
.
Note that only the latter solution corresponds to a
(2+1)
cluster state,
while the former solution represents a
(1+1)
cluster state.
Therefore,
(2+1)
cluster states are macroscopically unstable for small
\alpha
.
As
\alpha
increases, the stability of the symmetric solution remains unchanged,
while the unstable solution with
\Theta_{1}\neq\Theta_{2}

undergoes a subcritical pitchfork bifurcation of limit cycles,
leading to the appearance of one stable and two unstable asymmetric solutions.
The occurrence of such a bifurcation is not surprising,
since system (
37
)–(
39
) is reflection symmetric
with respect to the permutation of
\Theta_{1}
and
\Theta_{2}
.
We emphasize that only after this bifurcation,
can stable
(2+1)
cluster state be expected
(although its transversal stability still needs to be verified).
Importantly, unstable asymmetric solutions arising
from the subcritical pitchfork bifurcation
act as boundaries in the phase space, separating the
basins of attraction for stable symmetric and asymmetric solutions.
As a result, bistability between the
(2+1)
cluster state
and the
(1+1)
cluster state becomes possible.

V
Discussion

In this paper, we explored the benefit of the second-order phase approximation for describing the dynamics of networks of Stuart-Landau oscillators. The object of our analysis was a two-group population with global coupling, quantified by the complex coupling coefficient
\varepsilon\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}
.

As is well known, the first-order phase approximation for coupled Stuart-Landau units yields the Kuramoto model with terms
\sim\varepsilon
. The second-order model additionally contains the terms
\sim\varepsilon^{2}/\kappa
, with the Floquet exponent
\kappa
, which are essential for strong coupling and/or weak limit cycles’ stability. Therefore, in many cases, the second-order model provides only a quantitative improvement to the first-order description. However, there are also examples where the inclusion of second-order terms yields qualitative effects
(
14
;
20
;
6
)
.
Our study provides further examples of this kind. More specifically, the second-order terms turn out to be crucial for describing the dynamics of our system for
\alpha\approx\pi/2
(nearly neutral coupling).
For example, for two coupled oscillators, only the second-order terms describe the bistability between in-phase and anti-phase locking. At the same time, in the first-order approximation, it does not appear. Furthermore, using the second-order model, we can describe three-cluster states in which one group of identical Stuart-Landau units splits into two halves, while the other forms a single cluster. Although, according to the Watanabe-Strogatz theory
(
30
;
31
;
26
)
, such configurations are impossible in the first-order model.

We also want to emphasize another aspect of our results.
The Kuramoto model has a relatively simple mathematical form, which makes it easier to consider analytically. On the other hand, the second-order phase reduction leads to much more complicated equations, and a natural question arises
as to whether they have any practical utility.
Several issues follow immediately from the structure of these equations. So, triplet terms in the second-order model reveal the emergence of a phase-oscillator hypernetwork in pairwise-coupled Stuart-Landau oscillators; the appearance of pairwise coupling terms for non-connected units explains the remote synchrony
(
14
)
. Moreover, the dependence of the coupling terms on the frequencies of oscillatory units explains the
observation of the frequency dependence in the process of coupling function reconstruction from observations
(
9
)
.
More remarkable is that our second-order model admits analytical investigation. In particular, our results regarding the behavior of two interacting units are analytical. In addition, we recall that the domains of existence of the
(1+1)
and
(2+1)
cluster states were found semi-analytically (by numerically evaluating some explicitly defined integrals).
Overall, this demonstrates that second-order phase reduction is not an abstract mathematical construct, but a useful tool for analyzing oscillatory networks.

As a final remark, we note that third- and higher-order models can, in principle, be constructed
(
21
)
. However, analytical treatment of such models is hardly possible, and using them for simulation offers no advantage, since simulating the original system is typically faster.

Appendix A
Computing the transverse Lyapunov exponents in the first-order approximation

In the first-order approximation,
the expressions of the TLEs of the
(1+1)
cluster state are relatively simple.
They can be written explicitly
for both synchronous and asynchronous states.
The resulting formulas allow for further investigation,
so that in this case the transversal instability boundaries (red curves in Fig.
4
)
can also be described analytically.

A.1
Synchronous state

Substituting
\Psi_{\mathrm{i,a}}
given by formulas (
35
)
into Eq. (
31
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{(\mathrm{i})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(\cos\alpha+\cos(\Psi_{\mathrm{i}}-\alpha)\right)=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(\cos\alpha+\cos\Psi_{\mathrm{i}}\cos\alpha+\sin\Psi_{\mathrm{i}}\sin\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left((1+\sqrt{1-\chi^{2}})\cos\alpha+\chi\sin\alpha\right),

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}^{(\mathrm{i})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(\cos\alpha+\cos(\Psi_{\mathrm{i}}+\alpha)\right)=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(\cos\alpha+\cos\Psi_{\mathrm{i}}\cos\alpha-\sin\Psi_{\mathrm{i}}\sin\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left((1+\sqrt{1-\chi^{2}})\cos\alpha-\chi\sin\alpha\right),

and

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{(\mathrm{a})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(\cos\alpha+\cos(\Psi_{\mathrm{a}}-\alpha)\right)=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left((1-\sqrt{1-\chi^{2}})\cos\alpha+\chi\sin\alpha\right),

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}^{(\mathrm{a})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(\cos\alpha+\cos(\Psi_{\mathrm{a}}+\alpha)\right)=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left((1-\sqrt{1-\chi^{2}})\cos\alpha-\chi\sin\alpha\right),

where
\chi=(\delta/\varepsilon)\cos\alpha
.

Using these expressions of
\lambda_{1}^{(\mathrm{a})}
and
\lambda_{2}^{(\mathrm{a})}
,
it can be shown that synchronous anti-phase cluster states
are always unstable for
\chi\neq 0
.
For this, we recall that such states correspond
to stable fixed points of Eq. (
33
)
only if
\cos\alpha<0
,
i.e. for
\pi/2<|\alpha|<\pi
.
In addition, we note that

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{(\mathrm{a})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(1-\sqrt{1-\chi^{2}})\cos\alpha\>\left(1+\frac{\chi}{1-\sqrt{1-\chi^{2}}}\tan\alpha\right),

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}^{(\mathrm{a})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(1-\sqrt{1-\chi^{2}})\cos\alpha\>\left(1-\frac{\chi}{1-\sqrt{1-\chi^{2}}}\tan\alpha\right).

Now it is clear that if
\chi\tan\alpha>0
and
\cos\alpha<0
, then
\lambda_{1}^{(\mathrm{a})}>0
.
On the other hand, if
\chi\tan\alpha<0
and
\cos\alpha<0
, then
\lambda_{2}^{(\mathrm{a})}>0
.
This means unconditional transversal instability
of synchronous anti-phase cluster states.

Unlike synchronous anti-phase cluster states,
their in-phase counterparts can be transversely stable
for certain values of
\alpha
and
\delta
.
To find these values, we consider the equations

\lambda_{1}^{(\mathrm{i})}=0
and
\lambda_{2}^{(\mathrm{i})}=0
,
with the additional constraint
\cos\alpha>0
.
The former equation is equivalent to

1+\sqrt{1-\chi^{2}}+\chi\tan\alpha=0.

(43)

Rearranging the terms and squaring both sides of the new equation, we obtain

1-\chi^{2}=(1+\chi\tan\alpha)^{2},

or

\frac{\chi(\chi+\sin 2\alpha)}{\cos^{2}\alpha}=0.

It is easy to verify that
\chi=0

is not a solution of Eq. (
43
)
and
\chi=-\sin 2\alpha
satisfies Eq. (
43
) but only for
\cos 2\alpha<0
.
Similarly, we can analyze the equation
\lambda_{2}^{(\mathrm{i})}=0
and obtain the second
stability boundary
\chi=\sin 2\alpha
.
Together with formula (
34
),
this gives the following stability boundaries
of synchronous in-phase cluster states

\delta_{\mathrm{cr}}^{(\mathrm{i})}=\pm\varepsilon\cos\alpha\sin 2\alpha\quad\mbox{for}\quad\pi/4<\alpha<\pi/2.

A.2
Asynchronous state

The integrals in Eq. (
32
) can be found analytically. For this, we rewrite
formulas (
32
) as

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{\varepsilon}{2C}\right)\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\cos\alpha+\cos(\psi-\alpha)}{|\chi-\sin\psi|}\,\mathrm{d}\psi=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(\cos\alpha+\frac{\sin\alpha}{C}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\sin\psi\>\,\mathrm{d}\psi}{|\chi-\sin\psi|}\right),

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{\varepsilon}{2C}\right)\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\cos\alpha+\cos(\psi+\alpha)}{|\chi-\sin\psi|}\,\mathrm{d}\psi=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(\cos\alpha-\frac{\sin\alpha}{C}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\sin\psi\>\,\mathrm{d}\psi}{|\chi-\sin\psi|}\right).

where

C=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\psi}{|\chi-\sin\psi|}=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{\chi^{2}-1}}.

Then, it is easy to verify that

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\sin\psi\>\,\mathrm{d}\psi}{|\chi-\sin\psi|}=-2\pi\>\mathrm{sign}\>\chi+\chi C.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(\cos\alpha+\left(\chi-\sqrt{\chi^{2}-1}\>\mathrm{sign}\>\chi\right)\sin\alpha\right),

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(\cos\alpha-\left(\chi-\sqrt{\chi^{2}-1}\>\mathrm{sign}\>\chi\right)\sin\alpha\right).

In this case, the equations
\lambda_{1}=0
and
\lambda_{2}=0
can also be solved analytically.
In particular, the former equation has a solution

\chi=-\frac{1}{\sin 2\alpha}\quad\mbox{for}\quad\pi/4<\alpha<\pi/2,

while the latter equation has a solution with the opposite sign.
Using the definition of
\chi
,
we conclude that an asynchronous cluster state
loses its transversal stability
for the critical frequency detuning

\delta_{\mathrm{cr}}=\pm\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sin\alpha}.

Appendix B
Performing the variable transformation in the three-cluster state

For the
(2+1)
state, let us rewrite system (
37
)-(
39
),
using a variable transformation

(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2},\Phi_{1})\rightarrow(\Xi,\Psi,\Phi)
, where

\Xi=\frac{\Theta_{1}+\Theta_{2}}{2},\quad\Psi=\frac{\Theta_{1}-\Theta_{2}}{2},\quad\Phi=\Theta_{1}-\Phi_{1},

or inversely

\Theta_{1}=\Xi+\Psi,\quad\Theta_{2}=\Xi-\Psi,\quad\Phi_{1}=\Xi+\Psi-\Phi.

By adding or subtracting two equations
from the system (
37
)-(
39
), we
obtain:

\displaystyle\dot{\Xi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}+\mathrm{Im}\left[f_{1}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cos{\Psi}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Xi}+f_{2}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\cos(2\Psi)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\Xi}\right]\,,

(44)

\displaystyle\dot{\Psi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Re}\left[f_{1}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\sin\Psi\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Xi}+f_{2}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)\sin(2\Psi)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\Xi}\right]\,,

(45)

\displaystyle\dot{\Phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}+\mathrm{Im}\left[f_{1}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)(1-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Phi})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Psi}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Xi}\right.

(46)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.f_{2}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)};\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa\right)(1-\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\Phi})\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\Psi}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\Xi}\right]\,.

The explicit form of functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}

is given by the formulas (
20
)–(
22
), where variables
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}
are polynomial functions
of the mean fields
Z^{(1,2)}
and
Z_{2}^{(1,2)}
.
Using Eq. (
22
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\xi_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Z^{(1)}+Z^{(2)}=\left(\cos\Psi+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\Psi-\Phi)}\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Xi}\,,

\displaystyle\xi_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\overline{Z}^{(1)}+\overline{Z}^{(2)}\right)\left(Z_{2}^{(1)}+Z_{2}^{(2)}\right)=\left(\cos\Psi+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\Psi-\Phi)}\right)\left(\cos 2\Psi+\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}(\Psi-\Phi)}\right)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Xi}\,,

\displaystyle\xi_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\cos\Psi+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\Psi-\Phi)}\right)^{2}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\Xi}\,.

This allows us to rewrite the functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
as

f_{1}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)}\right)=g_{1}(\Psi,\Phi;\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Xi}\,,\quad f_{2}\left(Z^{(1,2)},Z_{2}^{(1,2)}\right)=g_{2}(\Psi,\Phi;\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\Xi}\,,

where

\displaystyle g_{1}(\Psi,\Phi;\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2\kappa}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}\right)\left(\cos\Psi+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\Psi-\Phi)}\right)-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8\kappa}\left(\cos\Psi+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\Psi-\Phi)}\right)\left(\cos 2\Psi+\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}(\Psi-\Phi)}\right)\,,

(47)

\displaystyle g_{2}(\Psi,\Phi;\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8\kappa}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\alpha}\left(\cos\Psi+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\Psi-\Phi)}\right)^{2}\,.

(48)

Inserting these expressions into Eqs. (
44
)–(
46
), we find that all the terms with
\Xi
cancel out.
Thus, we obtain an equivalent system

\displaystyle\dot{\Xi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}+\mathrm{Im}\left[g_{1}(\Psi,\Phi;\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa)\cos{\Psi}+g_{2}(\Psi,\Phi;\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa)\cos(2\Psi)\right]\,,

(49)

\displaystyle\dot{\Psi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Re}\left[g_{1}(\Psi,\Phi;\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa)\sin\Psi+g_{2}(\Psi,\Phi;\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa)\sin(2\Psi)\right]\,,

(50)

\displaystyle\dot{\Phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}+\mathrm{Im}\left[g_{1}(\Psi,\Phi;\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa)(1-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Phi})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Psi}+g_{2}(\Psi,\Phi;\varepsilon,\alpha,\kappa)\cdot(1-\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\Phi})\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\Psi}\right]\,.

(51)

Note that the r.h.s. of all three equations depend only on two variables
\Psi
and
\Phi
,
which means that the dynamics of the three-cluster state is essentially two-dimensional.
More precisely, it is a dynamics on a two-dimensional torus
parameterized by variables
\Psi
and
\Phi
.
The third variable
\Xi
is simply a slave variable,
since the r.h.s. of Eq. (
49
) does not depend on
\Xi
.
