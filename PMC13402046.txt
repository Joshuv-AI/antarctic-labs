Pharmacokinetics determine persister formation in Escherichia coli
Authors: Khairy M Ali, Ronan A Murphy, Caroline Zanchi, Sara Zunk-Parras, Alexandro Rodríguez-Rojas, Jens Rolff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPersisters are bacterial subpopulations of slow- or non-growing cells that are refractory to antimicrobials and can provide a starting point for antimicrobial resistance evolution. Several mechanisms of persister formation have been studied, and it has been shown that a short exposure to sublethal concentrations of antimicrobials can induce persistence.ObjectivesWe hypothesized that the pharmacokinetics—the temporal changes of drug concentrations under treatment—impact persister formation. We predicted that the slower the rate of drug concentration increase, the longer bacteria spend under sublethal drug concentrations, and the more persisters would form.Methods Escherichia coli cells that were either primed or not with the antimicrobial peptide pexiganan were subjected to different pharmacokinetics of pexiganan using a set-up with small chemostats.ResultsWe found that faster pharmacokinetics resulted in lower persister numbers compared with slower pharmacokinetics. This finding provides proof of principle that pharmacokinetics, which can be influenced by treatment procedures, has consequences for persister formation.ConclusionsOur results suggest that faster pharmacokinetics could minimize persister numbers and hence lower the risks of bacterial infection relapse and resistance evolution.

Introduction

Bacterial subpopulations that are refractory to antimicrobials without change to their antimicrobial susceptibility—persisters—are thought to contribute to the relapse of chronic infections.1 Typically antimicrobials eliminate most bacteria but persisters survive, as demonstrated by Hobby et al. in 1942 for penicillin, which was ineffective against non-growing staphylococci.2,3 Accumulating experimental evidence supports that dormancy in persister cells is characterized by the absence of growth, transcription and translation, rendering most antimicrobials ineffective.2,4–6 Persisters can cause a relapse of infection and result in resistance evolution.2,7–9 Bacterial populations that contain persisters are described by a biphasic killing curve that shows a small subpopulation dying at a much slower rate, although this relationship is still being studied.8,10

While persisters emerge spontaneously, they can also be induced: for example, heat, pH, oxidative stress and antimicrobials have been linked to persister formation.11 We found previously that priming (short, sub-MIC exposures) of Escherichia coli by two antimicrobial peptides (AMPs)—melittin and pexiganan—increases persister formation leading to greater subsequent survival at lethal doses of the same AMPs.12

AMPs are found across all domains of life and are a fundamental component of innate defences. They are also promising new antimicrobials and currently constitute 15% of all antimicrobials on the WHO list of antimicrobials under preclinical trials.13 In innate immunity, AMP production starts at sub-lethal concentrations, taking several hours to reach lethal levels, upon infection.14 Pexiganan, the AMP we use here, was the first eukaryotic AMP developed into a drug.15 The same applies to clinical antimicrobial treatments where the increase of drug concentrations takes time.16,17

While our previous findings showed that a brief sub-MIC exposure to AMPs induced persister formation, that study investigated statically applied concentrations. Here we set out to explore the effects of concentration increases over time. This approach highlights the role of pharmacokinetics, where drug concentrations increase gradually in a manner resembling clinical applications (during antimicrobial infusion). We posit that the longer a population of bacteria is exposed to increasing sub-MIC concentrations of antimicrobials, the greater the likelihood that persisters will form.12 This could improve our understanding of recurrent bacterial infections after treatments.

We used chemostats to study how different pharmacokinetics of pexiganan affect the induction of persistence. The chemostats allowed dynamic control of the concentration of the stressor, a critical advancement over previous studies that relied on static concentrations.12 By increasing the pexiganan concentration at different rates, we investigated how varying exposure rates influence persister formation. This new approach provides insights into how bacteria adapt phenotypically over time, making it particularly relevant for understanding antimicrobial persistence.

Methods

Bacterial strain

E. coli MG1655 (stored at −70°C in glycerol) was used for all experiments, and revived onto lysogeny broth agar (LB agar) (Carl Roth, Germany). Overnight liquid cultures were performed in Mueller–Hinton broth (MHB) (Carl Roth, Germany) in 50 mL Falcon tubes.

AMP priming

Priming is defined12 as a 30 min exposure to MIC/10. Overnight bacterial cultures were diluted 1:100 into 10 mL of fresh MHB in 50 mL Falcon tubes and incubated until mid–log phase (OD600 0.5, ∼2 × 108 cfu/mL) for ∼2 h. Bacterial cultures were exposed to 0.4 mg/L pexiganan for 30 min at 37°C with shaking. Non-primed cultures were incubated in parallel. Following incubation, pexiganan was removed by two steps of centrifugation at 4000 g for 10 min, supernatant removed and resuspended in an equal volume of fresh MHB. Cultures were allowed to recover for 60 min at 37°C with shaking.

Persister enumeration

We determined the persister fraction as previously12: a 4 h exposure to ciprofloxacin at 2 mg/L (16× the strain MIC) performed at 37°C. After exposure, bacteria were washed twice with 0.9% NaCl, serially diluted and plated on MHB agar. The bacterial density immediately before the addition of ciprofloxacin was also determined (timepoint = 0). The fraction of persisters was calculated as [bacterial number after ciprofloxacin treatment/bacterial number before ciprofloxacin treatment].12

Dynamic AMP application using a chemostat

After recovery, 3 mL cultures were loaded into sterile 5 mL chemostats, placed on a magnetic stirrer (Variomag Multipoint HP 6) and connected to a syringe pump (Braintree Scientific Programmable Syringe Pump). Syringes were connected to the chemostats by peristaltic tubes. The chemostat system was placed inside an incubator at 37°C (Figure S1, available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online).

Cultures were challenged with pexiganan supplied in three application regimes (Figure 1b and Figure S1): for Slow application, 40 mg/L was applied at a rate of 25 µL/min (MIC reached after 13 min), for Intermediate 80 mg/L at 45 µL/min (MIC reached after 4 min), and for Fast 80 mg/L at 100 µL/min (MIC reached after 1.5 min).

(a) Experimental timeline for cfu/mL and persister quantification in the chemostat system. Cultures were first subjected to a priming phase and recovery period before transfer to the chemostat. Cultures were sampled at defined timepoints for cfu/mL quantification, shown in the upper panel, while persister quantification is indicated in the lower panel. Coloured markers denote sampling points along the timeline. (b) Pharmacokinetics of AMP concentration increases in the chemostat systems. Pexiganan (PEX) was applied in three distinct regimes: Slow (concentration 40 mg/L, rate 25 µL/min), Intermediate (concentration 80 mg/L, rate 45 µL/min) and Fast (concentration 80 mg/L, rate 100 µL/min). As a result, the pexiganan MIC for E. coli MG1655 was reached at 13 min for Slow, 4 min for Intermediate, and 1.5 min for Fast. Inset: shading indicates the time spent sub-MIC under different application regimes.

During chemostat runs of each dynamic application, bacteria were harvested (10 µL), serially diluted in PBS and spot-plated onto LB agar, to determine cfu/mL at timepoints 2.5, 5, 7, 15, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min. Pumping was stopped at 120 min and a final sample was collected 30 min later. In parallel to each run, a control run was sampled consisting of a mid-log bacterial culture connected to a syringe containing MHB only (no priming, no challenge) (Figure S2).

Persister fractions were detected using ciprofloxacin, as above. Bacteria were harvested (1 mL), washed twice and resuspended in an equal volume of MHB—to remove pexiganan—before ciprofloxacin addition. Persisters were tested during Slow (15, 60 and 120 min), Intermediate (15 and 60 min) and Fast (15 min) dynamics (being limited in some later timepoints due to extensive cell death).

Minimum bactericidal concentrations

The MBC of pexiganan during dynamic applications was determined as the first concentration at which ≥99.9% inhibition of bacterial viability occurred. The median bacterial population was 2.5 × 109 cfu/mL across primed and non-primed cultures after the priming protocol had been carried out but immediately before addition to chemostats; therefore, 99.9% inhibition was <2.5 × 106 cfu/mL.

Statistics

All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1.18 We first analysed whether priming increased the fraction of persisters in bacterial cultures. The ratio of persisters/total population size was analysed as a response variable with the ‘betareg’ package.19 Priming was the explanatory variable. Figures were generated using the ‘ggplot2’ package.20 Since there were samples with zero cfus, a value of 1 was added to all final cfu/mL counts prior to visualization on a log10 scale.

The change in bacterial population size over time between the primed and non-primed treatments under the three different application regimes was analysed in two steps. Because there were samples from which we could not retrieve viable cfu/mL, we first analysed the presence/absence of cfu/mL in samples with a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) fitted for a binomial distribution using ‘glmmTMB’ (‘glmmTMB’ package21), according to time/concentration, priming treatment, and the interaction between them as explanatory variables.21 The interaction tests the interdependent effect of priming treatment with time/concentration. The starting bacterial culture was included as a random factor.

We then focused on samples from which we could retrieve viable cfu/mL. Here, the number of cfu/mL was analysed with a GLMM (gaussian distribution), with time/concentration and priming and their interaction as factors and the starting culture as a random factor.21

We analysed whether persister formation differed between the regimes. The ratio of persisters:total population size was analysed as a response variable at each timepoint of each pexiganan application regime (where possible). We then analysed the proportion of persisters separately for each timepoint with a beta regression (‘betareg’ package) according to the application regime and the priming treatment as explanatory variables and the interaction between them: at 15 min the data relative to the Slow, Intermediate and Fast were analysed; at 60 min the data of Slow and Intermediate were analysed; at 120 min only the data relative to the Slow regime were analysed, according to the priming treatment.

The effect of the explanatory variables that were significantly affecting the response variables were obtained using the ‘effects’ package. Model-predicted estimates and 95% CIs are reported in the main text alongside the results of the corresponding statistical tests as estimate. Additionally, they are represented in Figure S6, which allows pairwise comparisons by eye. The difference between two levels was deemed significant when the 95% CIs did not overlap for more than half of their length.22,23

Results

AMP priming

We induced persister formation by priming E. coli MG1655 with a sublethal pexiganan concentration and found a higher persister proportion than in non-primed cultures (χ2 = 4.3132; primed: 0.005; 95% CI, 0.00301–0.00835; non-primed: 0.0239; 95% CI, 0.0011–0.0048; P = 0.03782; n = 26) (Figure S3).12

Slow AMP application

AMP-primed cultures did not survive a dynamic antimicrobial application better than non-primed cultures during the Slow increase of pexiganan concentration. We analysed the proportion of samples in which viable cells were obtained and found no interaction between time/concentration and priming on numbers (χ2 = 0.0300, P = 0.8, n = 25) (Figure 2a). When we compared primed and non-primed cultures over the entire experiment there was no difference in proportions of survivors (χ2 = 0.0052, P = 0.9, n = 25). This indicates that the proportion of samples with detectable E. coli colonies present was not affected by the AMP-priming performed before inoculating the chemostats. Across primed and non-primed samples, time/concentration did have a significant effect on the proportion of surviving populations: the proportion of surviving populations decreased over time/concentration (χ2 = 6.1498, P < 0.005, n = 25).

We then analysed the cfu/mL in surviving populations under pexiganan application (excluding zeros from the model). In surviving populations there was no significant effect of priming, neither in interaction with time/concentration (χ2 = 3.8632, P = 0.5693, n = 25) nor as single effect (χ2 = 0.0722, P = 0.7881, n = 25) (Figure 2a and Figure S5). The number of cfu/mL in the samples significantly decreased over time/concentration, from a median 8.0 × 108 cfu/mL at the first timepoint to 5.5 × 106 cfu/mL at the final timepoint (χ2 = 83.2032, P < 0.005, n = 25). The MBC of pexiganan was never reached as median E. coli concentrations remained greater than 2.5 × 106 cfu/mL at all timepoints (no effect of priming), meaning inhibition never exceeded 99.9% (Figure 2a). Only progression of time (and a concomitant AMP increase) resulted in significant changes to population sizes.

Intermediate AMP application

In our Intermediate application regime AMP priming did not affect the outcome of the experiments. The proportion of surviving populations was not affected by the interaction between time/concentration and priming (χ2 = 1.7512, P = 0.1857, n = 18) while priming alone also showed no effect (χ2 = 2.6551, P = 0.1032, n = 18) (Figure 2b and Figure S5). The proportion of surviving populations did significantly decrease as time/concentration increased (χ2 = 5.0620, P < 0.005, n = 18). The majority of populations in both primed and non-primed cultures were undetectable at 90 min (37.3 mg/L pexiganan).

Analysing only populations with viable bacteria, no significant effect of priming was seen in the interaction with time/concentration (χ2 = 1.7637, P = 0.1842, n = 18) nor as a single effect (χ2 = 0.9679, P = 0.3252, n = 9). The number of cfu/mL in the samples significantly decreased as time/concentration increased, from a median of 8.0 × 108 cfu/mL at 2.5 min to 0 cfu/mL at 150 min (χ2 = 40.3886, P < 0.005, n = 18).

The first timepoint at which 99.9% inhibition (MBC) was achieved was 90 min (median 0 cfu/mL, pexiganan concentration 37.3 mg/L). Bacterial concentrations remained below the MBC at all subsequent times.

Time and increased concentrations were significant and, in contrast to Slow application, eliminated viable bacteria from all population samples after 90 min.

Fast AMP application

For our Fast regime we did not analyse the proportion of samples containing viable cfus as 7/9 primed populations and 9/10 non-primed populations had no detectable viable colonies at 30 min (34.5 mg/L pexiganan).

We analysed the number of cfu/mL in populations that survived up to 15 min (Figure 2c and Figure S5). No significant interaction was seen between time/concentration and priming (χ2 = 2.2536, P = 0.521477, n = 19), nor an effect of priming (χ2 = 0.2163, P = 0.641894, n = 19). There was a significant effect of time/concentration on the number of cfu/mL retrieved from the samples, which decreased as the experiment progressed, from a median 5.0 × 108 cfu/mL at the earliest timepoint tested to 0 cfu/mL at the final sampling (χ2 = 13.1786, P < 0.005, n = 19).

The MBC of pexiganan in our chemostat was 34.5 mg/L at 30 min when median cfu/mL was 0. Culture populations remained below the MBC threshold for the remaining collection timepoints.

We noticed that in the Slow regime, while the populations present in some samples did collapse starting from 60 min, the subsequent timepoints comprised a mixture of viable populations and collapsed populations (2 out of 14 in primed collapsed, and 2 out of 11 in non-primed). In the Intermediate regime, the drop in the proportion of samples containing viable cfus was more dramatic, since the populations present in eight of nine primed and seven of nine non-primed samples had collapsed to zero cfu/mL at 90 min (37 mg/L). For Fast nearly all populations (primed and non-primed) collapsed by 30 min (34.5 mg/L pexiganan).

Presence of persisters in different application regimes

The survival rates of E. coli MG1655 displayed a lack of impact of our prior AMP priming on bacterial survival during dynamic pexiganan increases. We also noted discrepancies in time/concentrations between Intermediate and Fast becoming non-viable; for Intermediate this occurred at 90 min (37.3 mg/L pexiganan) but for Fast it was at 30 min (34.5 mg/L), while at 60 min in Intermediate a concentration similar to the collapse point of Fast (33.6 mg/L) still had a high viable cell count (median 3.5 × 106 cfu/mL, n = 18) (Figure 2). We next investigated the impact of persister formation within our AMP application regimes (Figure 3). We analysed the effect of priming and pharmacokinetics on the proportion of persisters separately for the three timepoints. At 15 min, the Slow, Intermediate and Fast application regimes all contained viable cells. At 60 min, only Slow and Intermediate application regimes had viable cfu/mL. At 120 min analysis was only possible on the Slow regime.

At 15 min we found no effect of priming on the proportion of persisters, neither in interaction with application regime (χ2 = 0.5904, P = 0.7444, n = 48), nor as a single effect (χ2 = 1.4812; Slow: 0.0148; 95% CI, 0.00925–0.0237; Fast: 0.0066; 95% CI, 0.0036–0.0118; P = 0.22359; n = 48). There was a significant effect of the application regime on the proportion of persisters (χ2 = 7.7443, P < 0.005, n = 48) (Figure 3a and Figure S6). Figure S6 shows that the proportion of persisters in the Slow application regime was significantly higher than in the Fast regime. The proportion of persisters in the Intermediate regime, however, did not significantly differ from either (Figure S6).

At 60 min (Slow and Intermediate) the proportion of persisters was not explained by an interaction between priming and application regimes (χ2 = 0.1406, P = 0.7077, n = 34) (Figure 3b). When analysed separately, the single effects of priming (primed versus non-primed) (χ2 = 0.3258, P = 0.5682, n = 34) and application (Slow versus Intermediate) (χ2 = 2.945, P = 0.08614, n = 34) were also found to be non-significant. These results suggest that, despite primed cultures beginning with a greater number of persisters, this was no longer the case during exposure to these application regimes.

Finally, at 120 min (Slow only) we saw no significant effect of priming on the persister proportion (χ2 = 0.0294, P = 0.8639167, n = 16) (Figure 3c).

Discussion

We report a relationship between bacterial survival, persister formation, and AMP pharmacokinetics. To our knowledge, this study is the first to use chemostats to test the effect of AMPs on persistence and to model more closely how bacteria are exposed to antimicrobials in natural settings. Overall, we observed that priming prior to dynamic pexiganan application did not affect survival or persister formation but that different dynamics resulted in different survival and persistence outcomes.

A faster increase in the concentration of pexiganan increased the rate of bacterial death: from 30 min onwards Fast pexiganan application rendered the majority of the populations non-viable, whereas all replicates in the Intermediate and Slow application regimes contained viable cells. From 90 min onwards in the Intermediate application regime the majority of replicates were not viable whereas in the Slow application regime the majority of cells were viable throughout the duration of the experiment (150 min). Examining when population levels crossed the MBC yielded results identical to our data modelling of the cfu/mL during dynamic AMP increases. Fast increase caused cultures to reach their MBC 1 h earlier (30 min versus 90 min) and at a lower concentration (34.5 versus 37.3 µg/mL) than the Intermediate rate. Slow application meant pexiganan never crossed this threshold despite reaching >4 × MIC of the AMP. At 15 min, when all regimes still contained viable bacteria, we could retrieve more persisters in the Slow than in the Fast application regimes of pexiganan (all application regimes were past the MIC).

For the persister sub-population, in contrast to previous work with static antimicrobial challenges, induction of persistence by AMP priming did not confer a survival advantage to E. coli when the challenge was dynamically administered.12 While we recognize that any enumeration of a sub-population is imperfect, the consistency of bacterial strain and methodology employed here and in our previous study make the results comparable. Despite finding differences between persister formation under different pharmacokinetics and timepoints, no differences in the proportion of persisters were seen between primed and non-primed bacterial populations.

In static experiments, the AMP challenge by pexiganan imposed on the population was sudden and fixed, giving the primed bacteria an advantage over non-primed cells.12 By contrast, given the gradual increase of AMP concentrations within the chemostat, non-primed populations were not suddenly challenged with a lethal stress but rather were also pre-exposed to sub-MIC concentrations. This likely negates the priming advantage by providing an opportunity for non-primed populations to catch up in their ability to form persisters: priming is unavoidable during dynamic application of pexiganan.

Pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic models explain resistance evolution and treatment failure by showing how antimicrobial dynamics shape bacterial survival.24 Consistent with the modelling approach reviewed by Witzany et al.,25 which demonstrated that the rate of drug application influences bacterial population dynamics, we observed a decline in the overall population sizes from the start of treatment under all three dynamics. Under Fast pharmacokinetics, the drastic increase in concentration translated into a short survival period for the overall populations and resulted in fewer persisters. In contrast, Slow pharmacokinetics allowed for prolonged bacterial survival and higher persister numbers due to extended sub-MIC exposure.

The relationship between rate of AMP application and bacterial survival rates could also be partially explained by the inoculum effect (the phenomenon whereby the higher the bacterial population density the higher the drug concentration required to kill, particularly pronounced for AMPs26–28). Our Slow pexiganan increase extended the time spent sub-MIC, allowing high numbers of cells to survive, thereby maintaining a high population density. This elevated density could amplify the inoculum effect, which raises the effective MIC required for eradication. Even when pexiganan levels surpass the MIC, the surviving population remains dense enough for long enough to resist AMP killing, while the prolonged sub-MIC stress simultaneously promotes persister formation—a vicious cycle of more cells negating concentration increases and fuelling persister formation. In contrast, Fast/Intermediate regimes rapidly exceed MIC, limiting the duration of sub-MIC exposure, resulting in a reduced reservoir for persister formation.

These results also have implications for the evolution of antimicrobial resistance: persister populations can drive resistance evolution by allowing persisters to survive antimicrobials and re-grow and increase mutagenesis.9 Our results demonstrate the salience of pharmacokinetics of antimicrobial application in eliminating that persister fraction. Moreover, work has demonstrated that during exposure to high concentrations of antimicrobials, bacteria favour increased persister production.29 Here we show that not only are the concentrations important but also the speed at which they are achieved is a major factor for persister formation.

Our results are a proof of principle that exposure time to sub-lethal concentrations of antimicrobials caused by different pharmacokinetics has consequences for persister formation. The slower the increase in drug concentration, the more persisters are formed. If this finding was to be repeated in other drug/bacteria combinations, this effect has potentially important implications for treatments. Given that persisters can cause relapse of infection, reducing the proportion of persisters should be a goal of treatment.8,30
