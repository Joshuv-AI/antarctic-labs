Latent profiles and characteristic networks of second victim experiences among junior nurses: a latent profile analysis and association rule mining study
Authors: Yaokang Xue, Xiaoshuang Zhao, Hairui Sun, Baorong Zhang, Shanshan Yu, Hua Yuan, Yuxuan Yang, Ruixin Guan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundJunior nurses (clinical experience ≤ 5 years) are prone to increased second victim distress after adverse events, yet the heterogeneity within this population remains poorly understood. This study used a novel combination of latent profile analysis (LPA) and association rule mining (ARM) to identify distinct second victim profiles, investigate factors associated with profile membership, and design corresponding care-bundle prototypes.MethodsA cross-sectional, STROBE-compliant survey of 387 junior nurses with ≥ 1 self-reported adverse event in the previous 12 months was conducted in three Jilin Province hospitals (provincial, municipal, county) between March and September 2025. Validated scales assessed second victim experiences, resilience, social support, coping, and personality, alongside items on adverse-event characteristics, understanding from patients’ families, and institutional response. LPA identified latent subgroups; multinomial logistic regression identified factors independently associated with profile membership; ARM (Apriori algorithm) extracted multi-factor co-occurrence rules and characteristic networks.ResultsThree profiles emerged: Low Support–Low Distress (42.12%, n = 163), Moderate Support–Moderate Distress (39.28%, n = 152), Low Support–High Distress (18.60%, n = 72). Moderate Support–Moderate Distress membership was most strongly associated with monthly income < 4,500 CNY (OR = 13.254), reflecting demand–reward mismatch. Low Support–High Distress membership was independently associated with low social support (OR = 7.707), poor resilience (OR = 6.777), and lack of understanding from patients’ families (OR = 5.080). For the group with the highest observed distress, ARM isolated a three-element combination, {≥10 night shifts/month + lack of understanding from patients’ families + high negative coping} (confidence 0.920, lift 4.945), which may represent a candidate signal requiring validation.ConclusionsJunior nurses’ second victim experiences form three heterogeneous profiles. The LPA + ARM combination identified profile-specific multi-factor co-occurrence patterns and supports person-centred early identification. Three care-bundle prototypes (high-distress stabilisation, demand–reward rebalancing, peer-mentor capacity building) provide a preliminary, data-informed framework for tiered support, pending independent validation.

Background

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), a patient safety incident is defined as “an event or circumstance that could have resulted, or did result, in unnecessary harm to a patient” [1]. Among these, an adverse event specifically refers to an incident resulting in harm to a patient caused by healthcare management or intervention, rather than by the patient’s underlying condition [2]. Nursing adverse events encompass unexpected injuries or complications arising during clinical nursing practice, including medication errors, procedure-related complications, and healthcare-associated infections. Because nurses are directly involved in medication administration, patient monitoring, and fundamental care, nursing adverse events not only result in patient harm but can also cause significant psychological trauma to the nurses involved [2].

To describe this phenomenon, Wu first introduced the concept of the “second victim” (SV) in 2000 [3]. In 2009, Scott et al. further defined second victims as healthcare providers who suffer continuing negative effects following unexpected medical errors or adverse events [4]. In 2022, the European Researchers Network Working on Second Victims (ERNST) proposed a broader, evidence-based consensus definition that encompasses all healthcare workers who experience negative consequences as a result of their direct or indirect involvement in an adverse event [5]. Conceptually, the SV experience is grounded in three interrelated mechanisms. (1) An acute emotional reaction, including guilt, shame, sadness, and anxiety, typically arises within hours of the event and reflects an internalised sense of failure to uphold professional duty [6]. (2) Moral distress develops when nurses perceive a misalignment between the standard of care they intended to deliver and the actual outcome, particularly when systemic factors prevented full enactment of best practice; this moral injury component overlaps with, but is conceptually distinct from, generic occupational stress [6]. (3) A substantial subset of SVs progresses to clinically meaningful trauma-related symptoms, including intrusive memories, hypervigilance, sleep disturbance, and avoidance, that resemble post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) or sub-syndromal PTSD; previous reviews have documented PTSD-like presentations in 10% to 72.6% of affected clinicians [6]. SV experiences therefore share phenomenological features with PTSD but differ in their occupational specificity, their tight temporal coupling to an identifiable workplace event, and the central role of secondary stressors such as institutional response and peer attitudes. Clarifying this conceptual continuum is important for understanding how transient distress in some nurses may evolve into more severe and persistent psychological morbidity in others.

Internationally, the SV phenomenon has been documented across diverse healthcare systems, although geographical coverage remains uneven. A recent multinational survey of intensive care unit (ICU) nurses across four Latin American countries reported elevated SV-related psychological and professional distress, with deficits in institutional support emerging as a consistent finding regardless of country [7]. In Brazil, qualitative work by Quadros et al. integrated process modelling of adult patient falls with semi-structured interviews of nursing staff, demonstrating that nurses involved in falls with moderate-to-severe harm experienced guilt, fear, anguish, and even trivialisation, particularly in the absence of structured institutional support [8]. Together, these studies show that the SV phenomenon transcends specific national systems and is repeatedly observable in regions where the empirical literature has historically been sparse. Our study extends this global evidence base by focusing on a within-system stratification of junior nurses in the Chinese context, while drawing on internationally validated instruments and conceptual frameworks.

In the Chinese healthcare context, the SV phenomenon is shaped by unique cultural and systemic characteristics. China’s nursing workforce has long faced structural shortages, and disparities exist between contract and permanent nursing staff in terms of remuneration and job security [9]. Additionally, tense nurse–patient relationships and high expectations from patients’ families regarding clinical outcomes create heightened external pressure on nurses following adverse events [10]. At the organisational level, Chinese healthcare institutions tend to adopt an accountability-oriented approach to adverse event management, including financial penalties, denial of awards, and similar disciplinary measures, which may further exacerbate nurses’ psychological burden [11].

Within the nursing workforce, junior nurses are generally defined as registered nurses with five or fewer years of clinical experience [12]. This group is in a critical transitional period of professional role development, characterised by limited work experience, developing clinical skills, and evolving professional identity [13]. Research indicates that junior nurses are more susceptible to anxiety, depression, burnout, and even PTSD following adverse events, and some may develop intentions to leave the profession [14]. Recognising nurses with higher observed distress and elucidating the underlying mechanisms of their SV experiences is therefore of considerable importance for support planning and the promotion of occupational health.

Existing SV research has predominantly focused on general nursing or physician populations, with limited in-depth analysis of junior nurses as a distinct group. A latent profile study by Choi et al. [15] conducted among Korean nurses did not stratify participants by years of clinical experience. Domestic Chinese studies have examined occupational adaptation in junior nurses [16], but most have employed variable-centred approaches that treat all junior nurses as a homogeneous group, overlooking individual differences in psychological response patterns. In reality, SV experiences are complex outcomes determined by the dynamic interaction of demographic characteristics, psychological resources, occupational environments, and social support [6].

Two complementary theoretical frameworks inform the interpretation of SV experiences in this study. Conservation of Resources (COR) theory proposes that psychological distress arises from the actual or threatened loss of valued personal resources, including resilience, social connectedness, and financial security [17]. The Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model complements this by positing that sustained imbalance between occupational demands and available personal or organisational resources drives psychological strain [18]. Applied to the SV context, these frameworks suggest that junior nurses are at greatest risk for sustained psychological distress when personal resources are depleted and occupational demands remain high after an adverse event. Depleted personal resources in this study refer to low psychological resilience, low positive coping, and limited social support; high occupational demands refer to heavy night-shift load, intensive-care placement, lack of understanding from patients’ families, and institutional punishment after the index event. Specific configurations of these deficits are expected to differentiate distinct SV experience profiles.

To address these limitations, this study employed LPA as a classification tool to identify internally homogeneous latent subgroups of junior nurses based on their scores across the six subscales of the Second Victim Experience and Support Tool (SVEST). Multinomial logistic regression was then used to identify independent predictors of profile membership. Finally, the Apriori algorithm within ARM [19] was applied to identify multidimensional feature combination patterns within each profile and construct a visualised network characteristic map. This combined methodological approach has been validated in nursing research [20] and can compensate for the limitations of traditional regression analysis in capturing nonlinear, multi-factor synergistic mechanisms.

In summary, the specific objectives of this study were to: (1) identify latent profiles of SV experiences among junior nurses; (2) compare between-profile differences in demographic, occupational, and psychosocial characteristics; (3) examine factors independently associated with each profile and feature combination patterns; (4) develop preliminary, data-informed support proposals for future evaluation.

Methods

Participants

A cross-sectional survey design was used, with data collected between March and September 2025. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [21]. To ensure coverage across provincial, municipal, and county administrative levels, convenience sampling was used to recruit participants from three hospitals in Jilin Province, China: one provincial regional medical centre, one municipal tertiary-grade-A hospital, and one county-level tertiary general hospital.

Participants were recruited using an event-exposed sampling strategy: nurses were eligible only if they self-reported involvement in at least one adverse event in the preceding 12 months. This criterion was chosen because the study aimed to examine heterogeneity in SV experiences following adverse events rather than to estimate SV prevalence among all junior nurses. Self-report was used rather than official institutional incident records because formal reporting systems may systematically under-capture events in accountability-oriented organisational contexts [22], although this approach is subject to recall bias and variability in how individual nurses interpret what constitutes a nursing adverse event; these constraints are discussed further in the Limitations section.

Recruitment procedure and voluntariness safeguards. The research team distributed the invitation through the participating hospitals’ standard nursing department communication channels accessible to all junior nursing staff. Nurse managers played no direct role in the recruitment process and had no access to data that could identify individual respondents. Each nurse self-reported eligibility based on his or her own recall of involvement in an adverse event during the preceding 12 months, applying the inclusion criteria described below. The invitation stated that the study was conducted independently of participants’ performance evaluation and employment processes, and that individual responses would be received only by the research team. The online platform (Wenjuanxing) did not record participant identities. Hospital administrators were informed about the study and may learn of aggregate findings through subsequent dissemination, but no individual responses or identifying information were made available to them, and no action would be taken against any individual on the basis of study participation. Participation was entirely voluntary, and participants could decline or withdraw at any time without consequences for employment, evaluation, or institutional standing.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) holding a valid Nurse Practice Certificate of the People’s Republic of China; (2) engaged in clinical nursing practice with ≤ 5 years of experience; (3) self-reported involvement in at least one adverse event in the preceding 12 months; and (4) provision of informed consent and voluntary participation. Exclusion criteria were: (1) nursing students or visiting nurses; (2) cumulative absence from duty exceeding three months during the survey period; and (3) a prior documented diagnosis of a psychiatric disorder or current use of psychotropic medication.

Sample size was estimated as follows: LPA requires a minimum of 300 participants to ensure model stability [23]; for adequate scale structural validity, a sample-to-item ratio of 10–20 is recommended, and with the Chinese SVEST comprising 24 items, a ratio of 15 yields a minimum of 360 participants; for the subsequent regression analysis, with 15 predictor variables and a 15:1 ratio, a minimum of 225 participants was required; G*Power 3.1 power analysis (α = 0.05, 1 − β = 0.95, 15 predictors, OR = 2.5) indicated a minimum of 201 participants. The highest requirement was 360 participants. Accounting for a 20% invalid response rate, 450 questionnaires were planned for distribution. A total of 387 valid questionnaires were recovered via the Wenjuanxing platform, yielding a valid response rate of 86.0%.

Instruments

General information questionnaire

A self-designed questionnaire collected information on sex, age, initial education level, monthly income, work unit, weekly working hours, number of night shifts per month, and adverse event experience (including number of adverse events, whether understanding was received from patients’ families, and whether any institutional punishment had been experienced).

Second Victim Experience and Support Tool (SVEST)

The Chinese version of the SVEST, originally developed by Burlison et al. [24] and translated and psychometrically validated by Chen et al. [25], was used. The original English-language SVEST comprises nine dimensions, including the outcome dimensions turnover intentions and absenteeism; in the validated Chinese version, these were incorporated into a broader “professional distress” factor together with professional self-efficacy items, rather than retained as separate subscales. The validated Chinese version therefore comprises 24 items across six subscales: psychological distress, physical distress, professional distress, colleague support, managerial support, and family/friend support.

Connor-davidson resilience scale (CD-RISC)

The Chinese version, developed by Connor et al. [26] and validated by Yu and Xiao [27], comprises 25 items across three dimensions. Items are rated on a five-point scale (0 = never; 4 = almost always), with total scores ranging from 0 to 100; higher scores indicate greater resilience. In this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.815.

Chinese big five personality inventory (Brief Version; CBF-PI-B)

Developed by Wang et al. [28], this 40-item scale covers five dimensions: neuroticism, conscientiousness, agreeableness, openness, and extraversion, each rated on a six-point scale (1 = not at all; 6 = very much so). In this study, Cronbach’s α for the total scale was 0.845.

Social support rating scale (SSRS)

Developed by Xiao [29], this 10-item scale comprises three subscales with total scores ranging from 12 to 66; higher scores indicate greater social support. In this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.762.

Trait coping style questionnaire (TCSQ)

Developed by Jiang [30], this scale contains two 10-item subscales measuring positive coping (PC) and negative coping (NC), each rated on a five-point scale (1 = definitely not; 5 = definitely yes). Higher subscale scores indicate a greater tendency toward that coping style. In this study, Cronbach’s α for the total scale was 0.707.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0, Mplus 8.3, and Python 3.13. All tests were two-tailed, with a significance level of α = 0.05; p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) models were fitted in Mplus 8.3 using the six SVEST subscale scores as indicators, incrementally increasing the number of profiles from one. Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (aBIC), Entropy, Lo–Mendell–Rubin Likelihood Ratio Test (LMR-LRT), and Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT). The optimal number of profiles was determined by integrating fit indices, profile size proportions, and clinical interpretability [23].

Basic statistical analyses and multinomial logistic regression were conducted using SPSS 26.0. Continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation (®x ± s); categorical variables were described as frequency and proportion (n, %). For univariate analysis, continuous variables were first tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test; variables meeting the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), while others were analysed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test; categorical variables were compared using the chi-square (χ²) test. To control for type I error due to multiple comparisons, the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction procedure was applied. With the LPA-identified profile as the outcome variable and the Low Support–Low Distress group as the reference category, a multinomial logistic regression model was constructed using variables that were statistically significant in the univariate analysis (p < 0.05). Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF); variables with VIF > 5 were excluded. Odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated for each variable.

Association Rule Mining (ARM) was conducted using the Apriori algorithm in the Python mlxtend 0.23.4 library. Categorical variables showing statistically significant between-group differences in the univariate analysis were set as antecedents, and membership in the three latent profiles was set as the consequent. The minimum support was set at 0.05, corresponding to approximately 19 cases, well below the size of the smallest latent profile (n = 72), so that rules specific to that profile would not be excluded by the threshold; minimum confidence was 0.80 and the lift threshold was > 1.0. Rules were ranked in descending order of lift, and the top five strongest association rules for each profile were selected. Python NetworkX 3.6.1 was used to construct an interactive network graph to visually present the characteristic network for each profile.

ARM and multinomial logistic regression address different aspects of the same dataset. Regression estimates the independent contribution of each predictor with the others held constant, and therefore answers the question of which variables are independently associated with profile membership. ARM identifies recurring multi-variable co-occurrence patterns of the form “if antecedents A, B, and C are present, then the consequent (profile X) follows with a defined confidence and lift”, and therefore answers the question of which observable feature combinations cluster with each profile. Used together, the two methods can reveal nonlinear, multi-factor patterns that either alone would obscure; the LPA + ARM combination has been applied in recent nursing research [20]. The lift statistic is the multiplicative increase in the probability of the consequent given the antecedents, relative to the consequent’s marginal probability; lift > 1 indicates a positive association.

Ethical considerations and confidentiality

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Hospital of Jilin University (approval number 24K246-001). Electronic informed consent was obtained from each participant before access to the questionnaire was granted; those who declined were directed to an exit page thanking them for their consideration and were not presented with any questionnaire items. The online platform (Wenjuanxing) did not collect names, employee identifiers, or contact details, and survey data were stored on a password-protected server accessible only to the research team. The recruitment notice, the introductory page at the start of the questionnaire, and the closing page at the end of the questionnaire each provided the contact number of each participating hospital’s existing psychological counselling service for participants experiencing emotional discomfort, together with a research team contact for any study-related questions.

Results

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 387 junior nurses who had experienced nursing adverse events were included. The sample was predominantly female (86.82%, n = 336), aged 25 years or below (76.74%, n = 297), educated at the bachelor’s degree level (68.48%, n = 265), unmarried (92.25%, n = 357), and without religious affiliation (95.87%, n = 371). Monthly income was most commonly in the range of 4,500–9,000 CNY (43.67%, n = 169), and most participants worked 31–50 h per week (43.41%, n = 168). Regarding adverse event experience, 44.96% (n = 174) had experienced one or two events and 16.28% (n = 63) had experienced five or more; 30.49% (n = 118) had encountered a lack of understanding from patients’ families, and 27.13% (n = 105) had experienced institutional punishment. With respect to psychosocial characteristics, 24.55% (n = 95) had poor psychological resilience and 19.12% (n = 74) had excellent resilience; 33.07% (n = 128) scored high on neuroticism; moderate social support was most common (56.33%, n = 218); 37.73% (n = 146) used high positive coping and 24.81% (n = 96) used high negative coping. SVEST subscale scores

The mean total SVEST score among junior nurses was 69.97 ± 13.34, with a mean item score of 2.92 ± 0.56, indicating a moderate-to-high overall level. Because subscales differ in the number of items, mean item scores were used for cross-subscale comparison. As noted in the Methods, distress subscale scores are interpreted in the conventional direction, while support subscale scores are reverse-coded (higher = greater support deficit). Among the support subscales, family/friend support (mean item score: 3.32) and colleague support (mean item score: 3.31) showed the greatest perceived support deficits, followed by managerial support (mean item score: 3.21). Among the distress subscales, physical distress had the highest score (mean item score: 2.63), followed by psychological distress (mean item score: 2.55); professional distress was relatively lower (mean item score: 2.15). Latent profile analysis results

LPA models with one to six profiles were estimated based on the six SVEST subscales (Table 3). Considering all model fit indices, the three-profile solution showed the best fit: AIC, BIC, and aBIC showed diminishing improvement beyond three profiles; Entropy was 0.969; both LMR-LRT (p < 0.05) and BLRT (p < 0.001) were significant, indicating that the three-profile model was significantly better than the two-profile model. Although LMR-LRT and BLRT remained significant for models with four or more profiles, these solutions included profiles with proportions below 5%, compromising model stability and clinical interpretability. The minimum profile proportion in the three-profile model was 18.60% (n ≈ 72), which balanced statistical power and clinical interpretability; the three-profile model was therefore selected as the final solution.

Based on the reverse-scoring property of the SVEST support subscales and the mean item scores for each subscale (Fig. 1), the three profiles were named as follows: the Low Support–High Distress group (Class 1: highest support deficits, mean item scores 3.91–4.04; highest distress, 3.65–3.75); the Low Support–Low Distress group (Class 2: high support deficits, 3.70–3.75; lowest distress, 1.71–2.21); and the Moderate Support–Moderate Distress group (Class 3: best perceived support among the three profiles, 2.32–2.56; intermediate distress, 2.52–2.89).

Univariate analysis of between-profile characteristics

The three latent profiles were used as the grouping variable to compare between-group differences in sociodemographic and psychosocial characteristics (Table 4). Statistically significant differences were observed across the three groups in initial education level (χ²=13.772, p = 0.008), monthly income (χ²=11.208, p = 0.024), work unit (χ²=43.755, p < 0.001), and number of night shifts per month (χ²=48.521, p < 0.001). The Low Support–High Distress group showed a pronounced high-workload occupational profile: 65.28% of nurses in this group worked ≥ 10 night shifts per month, and 55.56% worked in intensive care settings.

Discussion

Throughout this Discussion, all observed relationships should be read as cross-sectional associations rather than causal effects.

Using LPA, this study identified three latent profile subgroups among 387 junior nurses who had experienced adverse events: the Low Support–Low Distress group (42.12%, n = 163), the Moderate Support–Moderate Distress group (39.28%, n = 152), and the Low Support–High Distress group (18.60%, n = 72). This classification is broadly comparable to that reported by Choi et al. [15] in Korean nurses, who also identified three latent profiles, namely a mild symptom group (16.4%), a moderate symptom group (69.0%), and a severe symptom group (14.6%), though differences in the distribution exist, suggesting that SV experiences differ to some extent between Chinese and Korean nursing contexts.

The SVEST support subscales indicated multidimensional support deficits following adverse events across all three profiles. This finding is particularly salient for junior nurses because adverse event exposure occurs during a transitional period in which professional identity, confidence in clinical judgment, and collegial trust are still being established [13]. This vulnerability is particularly acute in the immediate post-graduation period, when the gap between the protected learning environment of undergraduate training and the accountability demands of autonomous clinical practice is widest.

A scoping review by Catalán et al. found that healthcare students and early-career trainees experiencing the SV phenomenon were especially affected by communication gaps with supervisors and by the absence of structured managerial support, with many suffering in silence and self-blaming, a pattern attributed in part to punitive organisational cultures and to the absence of established peer relationships that facilitate emotional processing [31]. Unlike experienced nurses who can draw on consolidated collegial networks and professional confidence, junior nurses confronting early adverse events often lack the relational capital to seek peer support and may not yet have developed the coping repertoires needed to navigate institutional accountability responses without internalising responsibility. The development of effective coping strategies and peer support networks is itself a process that unfolds over years of clinical experience; for nurses still in the early stages of this process, institutional structures must compensate for what individual and relational resources cannot yet provide. The same review found that peer and supervisor support were the most consistently identified protective factors in this population, underscoring that support deficits among junior nurses reflect structural gaps in institutional post-event support rather than individual deficiency alone [31].

This transitional vulnerability was compounded in the present sample by the predominantly unmarried status of participants (92.25%) and frequent night-shift work, both of which restrict informal social support outside the workplace. At the institutional level, even nurses in the Low Support–Low Distress profile perceived managerial responses as primarily evaluative rather than supportive, pointing to a persistent gap between the accountability function that managers currently perform and the psychological recovery function that junior nurses need after adverse events [32–33].

The substantial heterogeneity in psychological adaptation observed among junior nurses following adverse events is best interpreted through two complementary, evidence-based frameworks. The Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model [18] characterises occupational well-being as the dynamic outcome of two parallel processes: a health-impairment process driven by job demands, including night-shift load, intensive-care placement, lack of understanding from patients’ families, and institutional punishment, and a motivational process driven by job and personal resources, including institutional, colleague, and family support, psychological resilience, and positive coping. Conservation of Resources (COR) theory [17] then explains the dynamics within each profile: when core resources such as income, resilience, and social support are depleted, secondary resources are mobilised but only partially compensate, which may correspond to observable profile differences in distress severity across the three subgroups. The three latent profiles identified in this study can therefore be read as three different empirical configurations along a demand–resource–depletion continuum.

The Low Support–High Distress group represented the clinical end of the SV spectrum, defined by the simultaneous accumulation of organisational, relational, and personal vulnerabilities rather than by any single dominant exposure. Within this profile, structural exposures, namely heavy night-shift load, intensive-care placement, and institutional punishment after the index event, co-occurred with relational adversity, specifically lack of understanding from patients’ families, and depleted personal resources including poor psychological resilience and high negative coping. Multivariable estimates were consistent with this configuration: low social support (OR = 7.707), poor psychological resilience (OR = 6.777), and lack of understanding from patients’ families (OR = 5.080) were independently associated with higher odds of membership. ARM further isolated a three-element candidate combination {≥10 night shifts/month + no understanding from patients’ families + high negative coping} → Low Support–High Distress (confidence 0.920, lift 4.945). Within the JD-R model [18], this profile represents the maximal-imbalance configuration: high job demands (night-shift load, intensive-care placement, institutional punishment, lack of understanding from patients’ families) co-occur with severely depleted job and personal resources (low social support, poor resilience, high negative coping). COR theory [17] further accounts for the clinical severity observed in this group: when multiple core resources are depleted simultaneously, the resource-replacement capacity of secondary resources is overwhelmed, which is consistent with the highest distress level observed across the three profiles.

The Low Support–Low Distress group represented the adaptive end of the SV spectrum and was defined less by the absence of any specific stressor than by a coherent pattern of protective resource accumulation. Lower occupational demand exposure (most worked fewer than 10 night shifts per month), greater economic adequacy (most earned ≥ 4,500 CNY per month), and a strong psychological resource base (most rated good or excellent on resilience) co-occurred within this profile. The corresponding ARM signature was particularly distinct: {≤5 night shifts/month + high positive coping + 1–2 adverse events + understanding from patients’ families: yes} → Low Support–Low Distress with confidence 1.000 and lift 2.374, indicating perfect within-sample confidence for this antecedent combination. Within the COR framework [17], this pattern can be understood as follows: when occupational demands remain within manageable bounds and internal psychological resources are well-stocked, secondary resource depletion appears less prominent in this subgroup, and the broadly insufficient external support architecture documented across our sample becomes a less critical variable for this subgroup. Notably, the support subscale scores observed even in this ‘low distress’ profile remained elevated, indicating that perceived support deficits persisted across all three profiles; the distinguishing feature of this group is therefore not abundant external support but an internal buffering capacity that compensates for the broadly limited support environment.

The Moderate Support–Moderate Distress group occupied a clinically important intermediate position whose defining feature was not the magnitude of any single exposure but a structural mismatch between high demands and limited material resources. Heavy occupational demands co-occurred with limited economic compensation: 40.13% of this group worked in intensive care settings, 43.42% worked ten or more night shifts per month, and nearly half earned below 4,500 CNY per month, a configuration captured most strongly by the association between income below this threshold and profile membership (OR = 13.254). The fact that most members held a bachelor’s degree or higher suggests that this mismatch reflected structural reward inadequacy relative to occupational demand rather than any deficit in qualification or training. Consistent with the JD-R model [18], this pattern indicates that sustained workload demands may not be sufficiently offset when material rewards and available resources remain limited. COR theory [17] provides a complementary explanation for this profile’s intermediate distress level: when a core resource such as income is persistently depleted, secondary resources such as collegial support can only partially buffer ongoing occupational demands, which is consistent with the moderate and sustained distress pattern observed in this profile.

These findings can be organised across four interrelated levels: structural, interpersonal, institutional, and individual. The first three describe the work-environment context in which adverse events occur, while the fourth captures person-level resources and traits that modulate response to that context. At the structural level, monthly income below 4,500 CNY (approximately USD 650), reported by 40.57% of the sample, was associated with higher odds of membership in both the Low Support–High Distress group (OR = 9.163) and the Moderate Support–Moderate Distress group (OR = 13.254). At the interpersonal level, lack of understanding from patients’ families, reported by 30.49% of participants, was associated with higher odds of Low Support–High Distress membership (OR = 5.080), consistent with evidence on family responses following adverse events in Chinese healthcare settings [34–36]. At the institutional level, institutional punishment, reported by 27.13% of participants, was associated with higher odds of Low Support–High Distress membership, whereas its absence was independently associated with lower odds of membership in this profile (OR = 0.168), reflecting the accountability-oriented institutional approach documented in prior work [11].

Within the institutional level, one specific organisational mechanism warrants closer attention: the institutional response to the index adverse event. Both the absence of institutional punishment (OR = 0.168) and the absence of a department-level case review (OR = 0.164) were associated with lower odds of Low Support–High Distress membership. For nurses already exhibiting self-blame and anxiety, repetitive event reviews appear to function as an additional psychological burden rather than as a learning opportunity, a pattern previously linked to secondary psychological trauma [37, 38]. These observations align with the principles of just culture [39–41], which distinguish systemic from individual contributors to harm; the management practice gaps observed here suggest that just culture principles have not yet been fully operationalised in the management of junior nurses.

At the individual level, personality characteristics were associated with profile membership. In the Low Support–High Distress group, low agreeableness (OR = 0.090) and low neuroticism (OR = 0.078) functioned as protective factors in the statistical sense, that is, were associated with lower odds of membership; high agreeableness and high neuroticism were correspondingly more prevalent in this group, consistent with the Austrian SeViD-A2 findings [42]. Individuals high in agreeableness tend to internalise responsibility, while those high in neuroticism show greater emotional sensitivity and more limited regulatory capacity [43, 44]. Within the COR framework [17], these traits can be read as features of an individual’s personal-resource configuration: high agreeableness and high neuroticism may reduce the efficiency with which emotional and cognitive resources are mobilised and replenished after an adverse event, leaving the resource buffer more easily depleted under the demand conditions captured by the JD-R model [18]. The interaction is therefore best understood as a person-by-situation pattern: the same occupational demand may have different psychological consequences depending on the individual’s resource-regulation profile. A practical implication is that the distress of nurses who are habitually mild-mannered and emotionally sensitive may be more concealed and therefore harder for clinical managers to detect through routine observation.

The highest-yield rule for each profile can be reframed as a short, observable feature pattern that may serve as a candidate prompt for bedside identification, pending independent validation in larger samples. For the Low Support–High Distress profile, the rule {≥10 night shifts/month + lack of understanding from patients’ families + high negative coping} (confidence 0.920, lift 4.945) describes a three-element signature that ward managers could ascertain from routine rostering data, post-event family debriefs, and a brief coping screen; in samples where independent replication has been established, nurses meeting all three criteria could be considered for early outreach under Bundle A as a candidate alternative to the full regression model. For the Moderate Support–Moderate Distress profile, the rule {no institutional punishment + monthly income < 4,500 CNY + ≥ 10 night shifts/month + bachelor’s degree + low negative coping} (confidence 0.952, lift 2.425) describes a structurally over-extended subgroup whose vulnerability is not primarily psychological but reflects the demand–reward mismatch addressed by Bundle B. For the Low Support–Low Distress profile, the rule {≤5 night shifts/month + high positive coping + 1–2 adverse events + understanding from patients’ families present} (confidence 1.000, lift 2.374) describes a resilient subgroup who, under Bundle C, can be trained as peer supporters. Each rule is short enough to memorise and observable from data already collected at ward level; the candidate links to specific bundles described below may be operationalised in future validation studies. With only 72 nurses in the Low Support–High Distress profile, these rules should be read as hypothesis-generating prototypes that require independent replication before clinical adoption.

The above analysis suggests that the psychological distress experienced by junior nurses following adverse events reflects an integrated interplay between individual traits, organisational management practices, and healthcare system characteristics. Translating these findings into practice requires intervention designs that are theory-informed, parsimonious, feasible, and subject to empirical evaluation. We therefore propose three profile-specific prototype care bundles, each consisting of 3–5 elements designed in accordance with the bundle development principles articulated by the Institute for Healthcare Improvement and recently systematised by Sebastiani et al. [45]. These prototypes are preliminary, data-informed proposals derived from the patterns observed in our cross-sectional sample, and each is intended to provide a structured framework for pilot evaluation rather than a validated clinical pathway; the specific elements and parameters that follow should therefore be read as starting points for subsequent feasibility testing rather than as prescriptive recommendations.

Bundle A: High-distress stabilisation bundle (for the Low Support–High Distress profile)

Element 1: Psychological First Aid within 24 h of the index adverse event, delivered by a trained peer or charge nurse following the Medically Induced Trauma Support Services (MITSS) model [46], with structured follow-up contact at days 3, 7, and 14 to maintain continuity of support through the acute phase. Element 2: A just-culture case review that separates systemic from individual contributory factors, with explicit suspension of financial penalties for events with predominantly systemic root causes [41]. Element 3: A time-limited structured psychological intervention for nurses who meet threshold trauma symptom criteria at 4-week reassessment. Where in-person specialist Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) is accessible, brief-format protocols (single-session or 4-session brief CBT) are preferred over standard 12-session courses; where in-person delivery is impractical given shift patterns or resource constraints, asynchronous or app-based CBT modules and brief mindfulness practices embedded within shift breaks serve as feasible alternatives. The 4-week threshold distinguishes acute stress responses, which are normative and expected to remit with Elements 1 and 2 in place, from persistent trauma symptoms that warrant structured clinical intervention, thereby avoiding over-pathologising a transient though distressing experience. Element 4: Temporary night-shift reduction to no more than five shifts per month for eight weeks, targeting nurses who meet the ARM-identified candidate profile signal in this study. Element 5: A standing referral pathway to occupational mental health services, with explicit confidentiality safeguards. Direct costs associated with Elements 1–5, including training, supervision time, and any digital licences, should be borne by the institution rather than by individual nurses, in keeping with the principle that SV-related support forms part of the institution’s duty of care.

Bundle B: Demand–reward rebalancing bundle (for the Moderate Support–Moderate Distress profile)

Element 1: A unit-level salary review for junior nurses earning less than 4,500 CNY per month. Income showed the strongest statistical association with membership in this profile (OR = 13.254), and persistent financial strain is theoretically consistent with COR theory’s account of chronic resource depletion: when core economic resources remain persistently insufficient, secondary resources such as collegial support can only partially offset sustained occupational demands. Pay-related adjustment may therefore represent a structural lever to be evaluated for this profile. The 4,500 CNY threshold reflects the income distribution in our sample; institutions implementing this bundle should adjust the threshold according to their local salary benchmarks and cost-of-living context. Element 2: A dedicated adverse-event-response allowance consisting of a small fixed payment per incident that triggers structured aftercare attendance, designed to remove the financial disincentive for nurses to engage with support services. Elements 1 and 2 address different levels of the reward deficit: Element 1 targets the structural base-pay gap, while Element 2 addresses the immediate behavioural barrier to support-seeking following an adverse event. Element 3: Scheduling optimisation to cap consecutive night shifts and ensure adequate inter-shift recovery time, directly targeting the heavy night-shift burden that co-occurs with this profile. Element 4: Quarterly Balint Group participation delivered within paid shift time, to avoid imposing additional uncompensated demands on nurses [47]. Element 5: A workload-intensity warning mechanism that triggers a managerial check-in when an individual nurse exceeds defined thresholds, such as ≥ 10 night shifts in a given month combined with at least one reported adverse event.

Bundle C: Resilience-building and peer-mentor bundle (for the Low Support–Low Distress profile)

Element 1: Formal training to qualify nurses in this profile as peer supporters within Bundles A and B, consistent with peer-support evidence for early-career healthcare workers reviewed by Catalán et al. [31]. Element 2: Quarterly resilience-maintenance training to preserve the internal-resource advantage observed in this group [48]. Element 3: Introduction of Trauma-Informed Care (TIC) principles [49] to help these nurses recognise and manage vicarious trauma accumulated while supporting Bundle A peers. Element 4: A formal recognition pathway, for example a peer-supporter credential, a modest stipend, or protected non-clinical time, to confer professional acknowledgement and reduce the risk of burnout arising from the supporter role itself. The inclusion of recognition and protection mechanisms is critical: without structural safeguards, deploying the most resilient nurses as peer supporters risks eroding the very resource buffer that characterises this profile. The exact form of recognition should be determined by local institutional policy and available resources, rather than prescribed uniformly across settings.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. (1) Study design and causal inference. The cross-sectional design precludes determination of causal direction, and dynamic changes in nurses’ psychological status over time could not be tracked. Longitudinal designs are needed to examine temporal pathways and determine whether the identified risk configurations are associated with subsequent SV trajectories.

(2) Sample definition and generalisability. The study deliberately included only nurses who self-reported at least one adverse event in the preceding 12 months, because the concept of a second victim requires involvement in an adverse event. Consequently, nurses who had never experienced an adverse event were not represented, and the distress estimates reported here should be interpreted as conditional distress among adverse-event-exposed nurses rather than as estimates generalisable to the entire population of junior nurses. In addition, the sample was drawn from three hospitals in Jilin Province, China. Although institutional variation was considered by sampling across provincial, municipal, and county levels, generalisability to other regions, healthcare systems, and nursing populations should be interpreted with caution. Nurses who had already left the profession before the survey were also not included, which may introduce survivor bias, although preliminary institutional data indicated that overall nurse turnover was low (< 2%) and that departures specifically attributed to SV experiences were rare.

(3) Self-report measurement and information bias. Adverse event exposure and related psychosocial experiences were assessed through self-report rather than verified institutional records. Official incident reporting systems in China may be subject to systematic under-reporting in accountability-oriented organisational contexts [22]; however, self-report data are also vulnerable to recall bias, particularly for events occurring earlier within the 12-month reference period, social desirability bias in environments where adverse events carry professional consequences, and variability in how individual nurses interpret what constitutes a nursing adverse event. Under such conditions, participants may under-report psychological symptoms or present a more favourable appraisal of managerial support than they actually received. Subsequent work should consider integrating verified, de-identified incident data with self-report measures.

(4) Instrument structure and outcome measurement. Although the validated Chinese version of the SVEST was appropriate for assessing second victim experiences in this population, its dimensional structure integrates absenteeism and turnover intention items, together with professional self-efficacy items, into a broader “professional distress” factor rather than retaining them as separable subscales. This study therefore could not examine work-attendance behaviours or intention to leave as independent outcomes. In addition, a study-specific psychological support referral pathway was not embedded directly into the questionnaire workflow, although contact numbers for the participating hospitals’ existing psychological counselling services and the research team were provided alongside the questionnaire. Researchers investigating similarly sensitive topics should consider integrating a direct, embedded support pathway.

(5) Subgroup size and exploratory ARM analysis. The Low Support–High Distress profile had a comparatively small subgroup size (n = 72). The ARM thresholds used in this study (support ≥ 0.05, confidence ≥ 0.80, lift > 1.0) were selected to prioritise rule reliability within this constraint, but support, confidence, and lift estimates for this profile remain subject to greater sampling variability than for the two larger groups. The rules identified for this profile should therefore be treated as hypothesis-generating rather than prescriptive; independent replication in larger samples is needed before these combinations are applied as clinical triage criteria.

Future research should prioritise multicentre longitudinal designs, integrate verified adverse event records with self-report data to reduce ascertainment bias, develop and evaluate care-bundle-based stratified interventions grounded in the ARM profiles identified here, and employ qualitative methods to examine how junior nurses interpret managerial support, blame attribution, and recovery following adverse events.

Conclusions

Combining latent profile analysis with association rule mining, this study identified three heterogeneous SV experience profiles among 387 junior nurses and characterised the multi-factor co-occurrence patterns that distinguished them. The findings indicate that psychological adaptation following adverse events reflects the nonlinear interplay of individual psychological resources, occupational exposure, socioeconomic status, and organisational response, rather than the influence of any single variable. The visualised characteristic networks describe profile-specific feature combinations that may help nursing managers focus screening attention on subgroups at higher statistical likelihood of distress, and the three care-bundle prototypes derived from these patterns offer a preliminary, data-informed framework for tiered support. Given the cross-sectional design and the relatively small size of the profile with the highest observed distress (n = 72), the identified rules and bundles should be read as hypothesis-generating proposals rather than as validated clinical tools, and independent replication in larger samples together with pilot evaluation of the proposed bundles is required before adoption as triage or intervention standards. Future research should prioritise multicentre longitudinal designs, integrate verified adverse-event records with self-report measures, evaluate the real-world feasibility and effectiveness of the proposed care bundles, and use qualitative methods to deepen understanding of how junior nurses interpret managerial support and recovery following adverse events.
