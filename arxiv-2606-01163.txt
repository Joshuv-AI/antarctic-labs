Minimization Principle for Polynomial-Time Predicates and Forcing in Bounded Arithmetic
Mykyta Narusevych

Abstract
We formulate a factorization hypothesis for linear orders definable by oracle polynomial-time machines. Assuming this hypothesis, we provide an approach to constructing a nonstandard model satisfying the minimization scheme for polynomial-time predicates while violating the tournament principle for graphs with polynomial-time edge relation.



1
Introduction

Unprovability of combinatorial statements in weak fragments of bounded arithmetic is a topic with a long history
[
6
, Chapter 11]
. While the weak theory
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\alpha)
and some of its extensions come with known unprovability results
[
10
,
3
,
9
]
, there are no general methods able to tackle theories above
\textsf{T}^{2}_{2}(\alpha)
, except the
switching lemma

[
1
]
and
lifting theorems

[
5
]
(which also use the switching lemma). However, mentioned methods seem difficult to adapt to fine-grained separations between successive theories
\textsf{T}^{i}_{2}(\alpha)
and
\textsf{T}^{i+1}_{2}(\alpha)
: while those theories are known to be distinct
[
6
, Section 10.4]
, no formulas of
fixed complexity
are known to separate all of them.

In this paper, we follow on an earlier work of analyzing extensions of
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\alpha)
and identify a computational condition on polynomial-time definable linear orderings, the
Factorization Hypothesis
. Our main result is that, over the base theory
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\alpha)
, the
tournament principle
is not implied by the
minimization principle

[
5
]
restricted to those polynomial-time linear orders which satisfy this hypothesis. In fact, we conjecture that the Factorization Hypothesis is satisfied by all polynomial-time linear orders.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Bounded Arithmetic

We start with a brief overview of bounded arithmetic. For further details, refer to
[
4
]
and
[
6
]
.

Our primary focus is the relativized bounded arithmetic theory
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\alpha)
, with
\alpha
being a unary predicate symbol. The language of this theory, in addition to
\alpha
, includes the usual arithmetical symbols
0,1,+,\cdot,\leq
, together with unary operations
\lfloor\frac{\cdot}{2}\rfloor
and
|\cdot|
, and the binary function symbol
\#
. The symbol
|\cdot|
denotes bit length, i.e.,
|0|=0
and, for
x>0
,
|x|=\lfloor\log_{2}x\rfloor+1
. The symbol
\#
denotes the binary
smash function
, standardly defined as
2^{|x|\cdot|y|}
. We denote this language by
L_{\textsf{S}_{2}}(\alpha)
, with
L_{\textsf{S}_{2}}
denoting
L_{\textsf{S}_{2}}(\alpha)\setminus\{\alpha\}
.

A formula is called
sharply bounded
if it is bounded and the bounding terms are of the form
|t|
. The class of sharply bounded formulas is denoted by
\Delta^{b}_{0}(\alpha)
.

The class
\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
is defined as the smallest set of bounded formulas extending
\Delta^{b}_{0}(\alpha)
and closed under
\land
,
\lor
, sharply bounded quantification, and bounded existential quantification. The class
\Pi^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
is defined as the set of formulas
\varphi
such that
\neg\varphi\in\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
, equivalently, as the closure of
\Delta^{b}_{0}(\alpha)
under
\land
,
\lor
, sharply bounded quantification, and bounded universal quantification.

The theory
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\alpha)
is axiomatized by a finite number of true statements expressing the most basic properties of the symbols, such as commutativity of
+
and
\cdot
, or that
|2x+1|=|x|+1
, forming the so-called
BASIC
theory; see
[
6
, Definition 5.2.1]
. This is augmented by the induction axiom scheme for all
\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
-formulas. Formulas are allowed to contain free variables.

For a formula
\varphi(x,\overline{y})
, denote by
\textsf{PIND}(\varphi)
the following sentence:

\displaystyle\forall\overline{y}\>[(\varphi(0,\overline{y})\land\forall x(\varphi(\lfloor\frac{x}{2}\rfloor,\overline{y})\to\varphi(x,\overline{y})))\to\forall x\varphi(x,\overline{y})].

The corresponding axiom scheme is called
polynomial induction
. The theory
\textsf{S}^{1}_{2}(\alpha)
is defined as the already mentioned
BASIC
theory, not treated in the current paper, augmented by the polynomial induction scheme for
\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
-formulas.

A
\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
-formula
\varphi(\overline{x})
belongs to the class
\Delta^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
if there is a
\Pi^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
-formula
\psi(\overline{x})
so that
\textsf{S}^{1}_{2}(\alpha)\vdash\forall\overline{x}\>\varphi(\overline{x})\equiv\psi(\overline{x})
. We note that it is more common to denote the previously defined formula class as
\Delta^{b}_{1}(\alpha)

in

\textsf{S}^{1}_{2}(\alpha)
to emphasize the theory over which the equivalence is established.

Theorem 2.1

(Buss
[
4
]
, see also
[
6
, Section 7]
)
.

A relation
R(\overline{x})
on numbers is computable in polynomial time with
\alpha
-oracle access if and only if it is definable by a
\Delta^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
-formula
\varphi(\overline{x})
.

2.2
Forcing

Our treatment of forcing follows
[
2
]
. The reader may consult the mentioned paper for a more general treatment of the subject; here, we focus only on features relevant to the current work.

We fix a countable nonstandard model of true arithmetic in
L_{\textsf{S}_{2}}
, denoted by
\mathbb{M}
. Throughout this subsection, formulas, terms, and so on are allowed to contain parameters from
\mathbb{M}
, unless stated explicitly. In the next section, we restrict the parameter space.

A
forcing frame
is a partially ordered set
(P,\preceq)
(in general, this poset need not be related to
\mathbb{M}
in any way). Its elements are called
conditions
. When
p\preceq q
, we say that
p

extends

q
. Two conditions
p,q
are said to be
compatible
, denoted
p\|q
, if they have a common extension; that is, if there exists
r\preceq p,q
. Otherwise, such conditions are said to be
incompatible
, denoted
p\perp q
.

A binary relation
\Vdash
between conditions and
L_{\textsf{S}_{2}}(\alpha)(\mathbb{M})
-sentences is called a
forcing
if it satisfies the following properties for all sentences
\varphi,\psi
, all formulas
\chi(x)
, and all conditions
p,q
.

•

p\Vdash\neg\varphi\>
if and only if
\>\forall q\preceq p:\>q\nVdash\varphi
;

•

p\Vdash\varphi\land\psi\>
if and only if
p\Vdash\varphi
and
p\Vdash\psi
;

•

p\Vdash\varphi\lor\psi\>
if and only if
\>p\Vdash\neg(\neg\varphi\land\neg\psi)
;

•

p\Vdash\forall x\chi(x)\>
if and only if, for all
a\in\mathbb{M}
,
\>p\Vdash\chi(a)
;

•

p\Vdash\exists x\chi(x)\>
if and only if
\>p\Vdash\neg\forall x\neg\chi(x)
;

•

if
p\Vdash\varphi
and
q\preceq p
, then
q\Vdash\varphi
;

•

if
\forall q\preceq p\>\exists r\preceq q\>r\Vdash\varphi
, then
p\Vdash\varphi
.

A forcing
\Vdash
is called
conservative
if, for any sentence
\varphi
not involving the symbol
\alpha
, and any condition
p
,
p\Vdash\varphi
if and only if
\varphi
is true in
\mathbb{M}
.

To consider only
reasonable

\Vdash
, we impose the following additional requirement on every conservative forcing relation considered below. This is essentially a stronger and simpler version of
[
2
, Lemma 2.18]
: given a condition
p
, and closed terms
s,t
, if
\mathbb{M}\vDash t=s
and
p\Vdash\alpha(t)
, then
p\Vdash\alpha(s)
.

A subset
G\subseteq P
is called a
filter
if it satisfies

•

for any
p,q\in G
, there exists
r\in G
so that
r\preceq p,q
;

•

for any
p\preceq q
, if
p\in G
, then
q\in G
.

A subset
D\subseteq P
is called
dense
if any
p\in P
can be extended by some
q\in D
, and a filter
G
is called
generic
if it intersects all sets from a selected countable collection of dense subsets of
P
. The full definition of genericity
[
2
, Definition 2.9]
is not relevant for the current discussion. What matters is that such filters exist
[
2
, Lemma 2.12]
, and that they satisfy Theorem
2.2
below.

Theorem 2.2

(Truth Lemma
[
2
, Theorem 2.19]
, and Forcing Completeness
[
2
, Corollary 2.20 (a)]
)
.

Let
\Vdash
be a conservative forcing relation, and let
G
be a generic filter. Then there exists an
L_{\textsf{S}_{2}}(\alpha)
-expansion of
\mathbb{M}
, denoted by
\mathbb{M}[G]
, satisfying, for any
L_{\textsf{S}_{2}}(\alpha)(\mathbb{M})
-sentence
\varphi
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}[G]\vDash\varphi\>\text{ if and only if }\>p\Vdash\varphi\>\text{ for some }p\in G.

Moreover, for any
p\in P
and any
L_{\textsf{S}_{2}}(\alpha)(\mathbb{M})
-sentence
\varphi
,

\displaystyle p\Vdash\varphi\>\text{ if and only if }\>\mathbb{M}[H]\vDash\varphi\>\text{ for all generic $H$ containing }p.

3
TOUR
,
MIN
, and the Transfer Lemma

Let
n
be a nonstandard number in
\mathbb{M}
, and let
\mathbb{I}
be the
small canonical cut
over
n
, i.e., the set of numbers in
\mathbb{M}
bounded above by parameter-free terms in
n
. As usual, the initial interval
[0,\dots,n-1]
is denoted by
[n]
.

Recall the
pairing function

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, defined as

\langle x,y\rangle:=\frac{(x+y)(x+y+1)}{2}+y.

This is a bijective mapping between
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{N}
, and the same term defines, internally in
\mathbb{M}
, a bijection between
\mathbb{M}\times\mathbb{M}
and
\mathbb{M}
. With such a function, we can turn the unary
\alpha(\cdot)
into a binary relation
\alpha(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, which we denote by
\alpha
for simplicity as well.

Definition 3.1

(
[
6
, 12.1]
)
.

A
tournament
is a directed graph
(V,E)
with exactly one directed edge between any two distinct nodes.

A set
X\subseteq V
is said to be
dominating
if, for any vertex
w\in V\setminus X
, there is a
v\in X
so that
(v,w)\in E
.

It is a well-known fact that any tournament with
m
vertices contains a dominating set of size
\leq|m|+1
; see, e.g.,
[
8
, 2.5]
.

The following formula
\textsf{TOUR}(\alpha,u)
expresses the above fact formally for graphs with edge-relation defined by
\alpha
, with
u
being a free variable:

\displaystyle\exists x,y<u\>(x\neq y)\land(\neg\alpha(x,y)\land\neg\alpha(y,x))

\displaystyle\lor

\displaystyle\exists x,y<u\>(x\neq y)\land(\alpha(x,y)\land\alpha(y,x))

\displaystyle\lor

\displaystyle\exists X\subseteq[u](|X|\leq|u|+1)\>\forall x<u\>\bigl(x\in X\lor\exists y<u\>(y\in X\land\alpha(y,x))\bigr).

The sets
X
quantified in the above expression are represented as sequences of length at most
|u|+1
, or equivalently by padded sequences of length
|u|+1
together with a length parameter. Thus they can be encoded as numbers from
\mathbb{M}
of size
O(u^{|u|+1})
.

Let
(P,\preceq)
denote the forcing frame of definable in
\mathbb{M}
partial orientations of
K_{n}
, the complete graph on
n
vertices, of size polynomial in
|n|
. Conditions of such a frame can be identified with sets of ordered pairs of distinct elements of
[n]
, containing at most one of
(a,b)
and
(b,a)
for each unordered pair
\{a,b\}
. We then define a conservative forcing relation
\Vdash
in the natural way:
p\Vdash\alpha(a,b)
if and only if
(a,b)\in p
, for numbers
a,b\in\mathbb{M}
.

The following can be proved in the exact same way as similar statements in
[
2
, Section 4]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

A generic expansion
\mathbb{M}[G]
violates
\textsf{TOUR}(\alpha,n)
and satisfies the least number principle for all
\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
-formulas with parameters from
\mathbb{I}
.

As a corollary, the structure
\mathbb{I}[G]
, defined as the substructure of
\mathbb{M}[G]
with domain
\mathbb{I}
, is a model of
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\alpha)
violating
\textsf{TOUR}(\alpha,n)
.

Remark
.

It can be shown that
\mathbb{I}[G]
preserves the
pigeonhole principle
for
\Delta^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
-formulas as well; see
[
9
, Section 5.1]
.

The main goal of the current work is to present a condition on
\Delta^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
-definable linear orders, stated formally in Hypothesis
4.6
, that implies that
\mathbb{I}[G]
satisfies the full
minimization scheme
for such orders. Moreover, we conjecture that all
\Delta^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
-definable linear orders obey this condition. As a corollary, our conjecture implies that the tournament principle is not provable from the minimization principle, a statement which currently seems to lie outside the scope of known separation techniques.

Definition 3.3

(
[
5
]
)
.

Given a formula
\varphi(x,y)
, possibly with additional free variables, and a variable
u
distinct from those of
\varphi
, define
\textsf{MIN}(\varphi,u)
as the disjunction of the following five formulas:

\displaystyle\textsf{MIN}_{0}(\varphi,u)

\displaystyle:=\exists x<u\>\varphi(x,x),

\displaystyle\textsf{MIN}_{1}(\varphi,u)

\displaystyle:=\exists x,y<u\>(x\neq y)\land\varphi(x,y)\land\varphi(y,x),

\displaystyle\textsf{MIN}_{2}(\varphi,u)

\displaystyle:=\exists x,y<u\>(x\neq y)\land\neg\varphi(x,y)\land\neg\varphi(y,x),

\displaystyle\textsf{MIN}_{3}(\varphi,u)

\displaystyle:=\exists x,y,z<u\>(x\neq y)\land(y\neq z)\land(x\neq z)\land\varphi(x,y)\land\varphi(y,z)\land\neg\varphi(x,z),

\displaystyle\textsf{MIN}_{4}(\varphi,u)

\displaystyle:=\exists x<u\>\forall y<u\>(x=y)\lor\varphi(x,y).

Hence,

\textsf{MIN}(\varphi,u)=\bigvee_{i=0}^{4}\textsf{MIN}_{i}(\varphi,u).

The corresponding axiom scheme is called the
minimization scheme
.

We denote by
\textsf{TMIN}(\alpha)
the theory
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\alpha)
augmented by the minimization scheme for all
\Delta^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
-formulas.

Remark
.

It follows from the Riis’ theorem
[
10
]
that
\textsf{TMIN}(\alpha)
is strictly stronger than
\textsf{T}^{1}_{2}(\alpha)
. In fact, by considering lexicographical ordering on witnesses, it is easy to show that, over a sufficiently strong base theory, for example
\textsf{S}^{1}_{2}(\alpha)
, the minimization principle for
\Delta^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
-formulas implies the least number principle for
\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
-formulas.

To formulate the main conjecture, Hypothesis
4.6
, we begin by directly analyzing the following question. Recall that
(P,\preceq)
refers to the forcing frame of definable partial orientations on
K_{n}
with sizes polynomial in
|n|
.

Question 3.4
.

Does
\mathbb{I}[G]\vDash\textsf{TMIN}(\alpha)
?

Following the standard forcing argumentation, we pick an arbitrary condition
p
, a number
m\in\mathbb{I}
, and a
\Delta^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
-formula
\varphi(x,y)
with parameters from
\mathbb{I}
, with the goal of showing that
p
can be extended to a condition
q
forcing
\textsf{MIN}(\varphi,m)
.

First, recall a fundamental property of
\Vdash

[
2
, Lemma 2.6 (4)]
, stating that
q\nVdash\psi
if and only if there exists
r\preceq q
such that
r\Vdash\neg\psi
, for all conditions
q
and any sentence
\psi
. Thus, we may assume

p\Vdash\bigwedge_{i=0}^{3}\neg\textsf{MIN}_{i}(\varphi,m),

i.e.,
p
forces that
\varphi(x,y)
defines a strict linear ordering on
[m]
. Otherwise, there is an extension
q\preceq p
forcing the negation of this conjunction, hence forcing one of the first four disjuncts of
\textsf{MIN}(\varphi,m)
, and we are done.

It is then enough to show that
p\nVdash\neg\textsf{MIN}_{4}(\varphi,m)
. Equivalently, by the forcing clauses from Section
2.2
, it is enough to find a number
m_{0}\in[m]
and a condition
q\preceq p
such that

q\Vdash\forall x<m\>\bigl((x=m_{0})\lor\varphi(m_{0},x)\bigr).

A priori, this is not a simple task. Forcing the universal sentence

\forall x<m\>\bigl((x=m_{0})\lor\varphi(m_{0},x)\bigr)

is equivalent to forcing all
\Delta^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
-sentences
\varphi(m_{0},m_{1})
for all
m_{1}\neq m_{0}<m
simultaneously. By itself, this might not be possible to accomplish via conditions of small size.

The only way to overcome the above problem is to use, in a non-trivial way, the fact that
\varphi

is forced
to define a strict linear ordering.

Below, we treat the cut
\mathbb{I}
as an
L_{\textsf{S}_{2}}
-substructure of
\mathbb{M}
.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{O}
be a class of total orientations of
K_{n}
. Neither the class nor its members are required to be definable in
\mathbb{M}
. For
O\in\mathcal{O}
, we denote by
(\mathbb{I},\alpha^{O})
the expansion of
\mathbb{I}
obtained by interpreting
\alpha
as
O
.

Given a condition
q
and an
L_{\textsf{S}_{2}}(\alpha)
-sentence
\psi
with parameters from
\mathbb{I}
, we say that
q

forces

\psi

with respect to

\mathcal{O}
, denoted by
q\Vdash^{*}_{\mathcal{O}}\psi
, if and only if

(\mathbb{I},\alpha^{O})\vDash\psi

for every
O\in\mathcal{O}
such that
O\supset q
.

Remark
.

We deliberately use the notation
\Vdash^{*}_{\cdot}
, instead of
\Vdash_{\cdot}
, to emphasize that, depending on the class
\mathcal{O}
, the relation
\Vdash^{*}_{\mathcal{O}}
may not satisfy the conditions of forcing from Section
2.2
. Nevertheless, the forcing notation is convenient and will be used throughout.

From this perspective, the forcing completeness theorem, Theorem
2.2
, equates the standard forcing relation
\Vdash
with the relative forcing relation
\Vdash^{*}_{\mathcal{O}}
for
\mathcal{O}
being the class of all orientations induced by generic filters. The other
\mathcal{O}
of obvious interest is the class of all internally coded total orientations of
K_{n}
. Equivalently, these are the orientations definable in
\mathbb{M}
with parameters from
\mathbb{M}
. We denote this class by
\mathcal{D}
.

Now, given some
q
and a bounded sentence
\psi
, we want to express
q\Vdash^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}\psi
as an
L_{\textsf{S}_{2}}
-sentence “
q\Vdash^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}\psi
” with parameters from
\mathbb{M}
, so that
q\Vdash^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}\psi
if and only if
\mathbb{M}\vDash
“
q\Vdash^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}\psi
”. In more detail, we first apply a Paris–Wilkie style propositional translation to
\psi
; see
[
7
, Section 10]
. This gives a propositional formula
\langle\psi\rangle
on
\binom{n}{2}
variables (while
\alpha
is binary on
[n]
, since we restrict ourselves only to orientations of
K_{n}
, it is enough to consider only unordered pairs of distinct elements of
[n]
, hence the size
\binom{n}{2}
). Note that such a formula can be encoded as a number in
\mathbb{M}
. The condition
q
is then identified with the corresponding partial assignment to those variables denoted as
\tilde{q}
. Since every internally coded total assignment is definable in
\mathbb{M}
from its code, the fact that
q\Vdash^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}\psi
is equivalent to the statement that
\langle\psi\rangle|_{\tilde{q}}
is a tautology. The latter can be expressed in
\mathbb{M}
, which we denote as “
q\Vdash^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}\psi
”. Notice that the sentence “
q\Vdash^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}\psi
” contains parameters outside of
\mathbb{I}
, as we are quantifying over the set of all total assignments to
\binom{n}{2}
-many variables.

Lemma 3.6

(Transfer Lemma)
.

Let
\psi
be a
\Pi^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
-sentence with parameters from
\mathbb{I}
, and let
q
be a condition. Then the following are equivalent:

1.

q\Vdash\psi
;

2.

q\Vdash^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}\psi
;

3.

\mathbb{M}\vDash``q\Vdash^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}\psi"
.

Proof.

The argument rests on the observation that the sentence
\psi
can be put into a CNF whose clauses have size polynomial in
|n|
. More formally, after expanding sharply bounded quantifiers and applying the Paris–Wilkie translation,
\psi
can be represented, for the purposes of forcing, in the form

\displaystyle\forall x<a\exists y<|n|^{C}\>\theta(x,y),

for some fixed standard constant
C
and some
a\in\mathbb{M}
, where each
\theta(x,y)
is an
\alpha
-literal with parameters from
\mathbb{I}
. Concretely, we may assume that there is an
L_{\textsf{S}_{2}}
-definable function
f(\cdot,\cdot)
with values in
\mathbb{I}
, and an
L_{\textsf{S}_{2}}
-formula
\theta^{*}(x,y)
not containing
\alpha
, such that

\theta(x,y)\equiv\bigl(\theta^{*}(x,y)\land\alpha(f(x,y))\bigr)\lor\bigl(\neg\theta^{*}(x,y)\land\neg\alpha(f(x,y))\bigr).

The translation is such that
p\Vdash\psi
if and only if
p\Vdash\psi^{*}
, and similarly for
\Vdash^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}
, for any condition
p
.

Assume
q\nVdash\psi^{*}
. We find
r\preceq q
such that

r\Vdash\exists x<a\>\forall y<|n|^{C}\>\neg\theta(x,y).

Extending further if necessary, we may fix a particular
b<a
such that

r\Vdash\forall y<|n|^{C}\>\neg\theta(b,y).

By the forcing clauses from Section
2.2
, and the definition of
\theta
, it follows that, for each
c<|n|^{C}
, the condition
r
decides the corresponding
\alpha
-literal so as to force
\neg\theta(b,c)
. Altogether, this implies

r\Vdash^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}\forall y<|n|^{C}\>\neg\theta(b,y),

and hence
q\nVdash^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}\psi^{*}
.

Conversely, assume
q\nVdash^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}\psi^{*}
. We pick an internally coded total orientation
D
extending
q
such that

(\mathbb{I},\alpha^{D})\vDash\neg\psi^{*}.

Then there is a particular
b<a
such that

(\mathbb{I},\alpha^{D})\vDash\forall y<|n|^{C}\>\neg\theta(b,y).

Since
D
is internally coded in
\mathbb{M}
, we can pick the
|n|^{C}
-sized subset of
D
already witnessing all oracle answers needed to verify

\forall y<|n|^{C}\>\neg\theta(b,y).

Such a subset is a condition from
(P,\preceq)
compatible with
q
, and its union with
q
clearly forces
\neg\psi^{*}
.

The equivalence between points 2 and 3 follows from the discussion preceding Lemma
3.6
.
∎

The main utility of the Transfer Lemma, Lemma
3.6
, is to equate a statement
q\Vdash\psi
to “
q\Vdash^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}\psi
”, for
\Pi^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
-sentences
\psi
, which can then be analyzed inside the ground model
\mathbb{M}
. This is in line with how the least number principle for
\Sigma^{b}_{1}(\alpha)
is forced in
[
2
]
; see the notion of
partial definability
of
\Vdash
in the mentioned paper. The main difference here is that we identify a class of
globally definable structures
, i.e., totally defined interpretations of
\alpha
, for which the transfer holds, instead of relying on
small partial substructures
, as is usually the case.

Corollary 3.7
.

Assume
p
is a condition such that

p\Vdash\bigwedge_{i=0}^{3}\neg\textsf{MIN}_{i}(\varphi,u).

Then,

``p\Vdash^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}\bigwedge_{i=0}^{3}\neg\textsf{MIN}_{i}(\varphi,u)^{\prime\prime}

holds in
\mathbb{M}
.

Furthermore, assume

can be extended to

such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}\vDash``q\Vdash^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}\forall y<m\>(m_{0}=y)\lor\varphi(m_{0},y)"

4
Factorization Hypothesis

By Theorem
2.1
, the relation
\varphi(x,y)
can be decided by a Turing machine
T[\alpha](x,y)
which queries an oracle
\alpha
, and which runs in time polynomial in the bit lengths of the input numbers. We choose this notation to emphasize that
\alpha
is not a regular input, but an oracle queried by
T
during the computation.

Since parameters of
\varphi
are from
\mathbb{I}
, and
x,y
are restricted to the interval
[m]
with
m\in\mathbb{I}
, the runtime of
T[\alpha](x,y)
is bounded above by a polynomial in
|n|
. Oracles are identified with total assignments to a fixed set of
\binom{n}{2}
variables. Given such a total assignment
s
, we denote by
T[\alpha_{s}](x,y)
the result of substituting
s
for
\alpha
. Then the condition
p
is identified with a partial assignment to the same variables, and the class
\mathcal{D}
is identified with all internally coded total assignments.

The statement

``p\Vdash^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}\bigwedge_{i=0}^{3}\neg\textsf{MIN}_{i}(\varphi,m)^{\prime\prime}

now means that the Turing machine
T[\alpha_{s}](x,y)
, for all total assignments
s
agreeing with
p
, defines a strict linear order on
[m]
. Finding
q\preceq p
satisfying

``q\Vdash^{*}_{\mathcal{D}}\forall y<m\>(m_{0}=y)\lor\varphi(m_{0},y)^{\prime\prime}

amounts to assigning polynomially many additional variables so as to fix the smallest element of the ordering defined by
T[\alpha](x,y)
on
[m]
.

This leads to the following problem, with
p
fixed to
\emptyset
without loss of generality.

Question 4.1
.

Let
n
be a number, and let
T[\alpha](\overline{x},x,y)
be a polynomial-time oracle Turing machine, with oracle being a
0/1
assignment to the variables encoded as integers from
[\binom{n}{2}]
. A query of
T
is of the form
a\mapsto?
for a number
a\in[\binom{n}{2}]
, with the oracle answering
0
or
1
consistently with previous answers.

Let
\overline{a}
be a tuple of numbers all polynomial in
|n|
, let
m
be a number quasi-polynomial in
n
.

Assume that, for all total assignments
s
,
T[\alpha_{s}](\overline{a},x,y)
defines a strict linear order on
[m]
. Denote by
m_{s}
the minimal element of such an ordering.

Is there a partial assignment
q
of size polynomial in
|n|
, so that
m_{s}=m_{l}
for any pair of total assignments

agreeing with

?

Theorem 4.2
.

Question
4.1
has a positive answer for
T
which can query the oracle at most once during each computation.

Proof.

We prove a slightly stronger statement. Concretely, we show that there is a standard constant
C
such that, for any subset
X\subseteq[m]
and any partial assignment
p
, there exists a partial assignment
q
extending
p
of size

|q|\leq|p|+C\log_{2}|X|

so that, for all total assignments agreeing with
q
, the orderings defined by
T[\alpha](\overline{a},x,y)
and restricted to
X
share the same minimal element. Here
|X|
denotes the cardinality of
X
, and
|p|
denotes the number of variables assigned by
p
. Note that
\log_{2}|X|
is polynomial in
|n|
.

We choose
C
large enough so that

3\leq C\cdot\log_{2}3,

and

1+C\log_{2}\left(\frac{5}{6}N+\frac{1}{2}\right)\leq C\log_{2}N

for all
N\geq 4
.

We proceed by complete induction on
|X|
, with the base cases
|X|\in\{0,1,2\}
trivial, and the case
|X|=3
following from the assumption
3\leq C\cdot\log_{2}3
.

Let
x\triangleleft y
be shorthand for
T[\alpha](\overline{a},x,y)=1
. We borrow the forcing notation and, given a partial assignment
p
and a pair of numbers
a,b
, denote
p\Vdash a\triangleleft b
if and only if, for all total assignments
s
agreeing with
p
, it holds that

T[\alpha_{s}](\overline{a},a,b)=1.

Step 1.

Assume that, for some distinct
a,b\in X
,
p\Vdash a\triangleleft b
. By induction, there exists a number
c\in X\setminus\{b\}
and an extension
q
of
p
of size

|q|\leq|p|+C\log_{2}(|X|-1)

so that
q\Vdash c\triangleleft d
for all
d\in X\setminus\{b\}
distinct from
c
. It follows that
q\Vdash c\triangleleft b
as well, since
q\Vdash c\triangleleft a
,
p\Vdash a\triangleleft b
, and
p
forces transitivity of
\triangleleft
on the whole set
[m]
. Thus
c
is forced to be the minimum of
X
.

For the remainder of the proof, we assume that
p
does not force
a\triangleleft b
for any two distinct members of
X
.

Step 2.

For distinct
a,b\in X
, by the previous discussion, there exists a single variable that
T
queries so as to compute the output on
a,b
. Denote such a variable by
v_{a,b}
. Since
p
does not decide either direction between
a
and
b
, both computations on
(a,b)
and
(b,a)
must depend on their unique oracle query. Moreover, because
p
forces exactly one of
a\triangleleft b
and
b\triangleleft a
, these two queried variables must coincide. Otherwise, one could choose the two oracle answers independently and make the two computations agree, contradicting the fact that
p
forces a strict linear order. Hence
v_{a,b}=v_{b,a}
, and

\displaystyle p\cup\{v_{a,b}=1\}\Vdash(a\triangleleft b)\quad\text{and}\quad p\cup\{v_{a,b}=0\}\Vdash(b\triangleleft a),

or

\displaystyle p\cup\{v_{a,b}=1\}\Vdash(b\triangleleft a)\quad\text{and}\quad p\cup\{v_{a,b}=0\}\Vdash(a\triangleleft b).

Step 3.

Lemma

(Triangle Lemma)
.

For any triplet of distinct
a,b,c\in X
, it holds that

|\{v_{a,b},v_{a,c},v_{b,c}\}|\leq 2.

Suppose otherwise that the three variables
v_{a,b}
,
v_{a,c}
, and
v_{b,c}
were distinct. Since none of the pairwise comparisons is decided by
p
, and since each comparison depends on its corresponding variable, we could assign these three variables independently so as to force

a\triangleleft b,\quad b\triangleleft c,\quad c\triangleleft a.

This contradicts the fact that every total assignment extending
p
defines a strict linear order. This proves the Triangle Lemma.

Step 4.

Fix an arbitrary
a\in X
, and denote
v_{a,b}
simply by
v_{b}
for
b\in X\setminus\{a\}
. We define the following binary relation
\sim
on
X\setminus\{a\}
:

b\sim c\quad\text{if and only if}\quad v_{b}=v_{c}.

Since
\sim
is an equivalence relation, it induces a partition

X\setminus\{a\}=\bigsqcup_{i}X_{i}.

Step 5.

Assume that some component
X_{i}
has cardinality at least
\frac{1}{3}|X|
. Let
v
denote
v_{b}
for an arbitrary
b\in X_{i}
. By averaging, there is
s\in\{0,1\}
so that, for at least half of the elements
c\in X_{i}
,

p\cup\{v=s\}\Vdash a\triangleleft c.

Denote the set of such elements
c
by
X^{\prime}
. It follows that

|X^{\prime}|\geq\frac{1}{6}|X|.

By induction, we can extend
p\cup\{v=s\}
to a condition
q
of size

|q|\leq|p|+1+C\log_{2}\left(\frac{5}{6}|X|\right)\leq|p|+C\log_{2}|X|

which forces the
\triangleleft
-minimality of some
d\in X\setminus X^{\prime}
. It follows that
q\Vdash d\triangleleft a
, or
d=a
, and hence such
d
is
\triangleleft
-minimal on the whole set
X
as well.

For the remainder of the proof, we assume that all of the components
X_{i}
have cardinality less than
\frac{1}{3}|X|
.

Step 6.

Given distinct
X_{i},X_{j}
, and arbitrary
b\in X_{i}
,
c\in X_{j}
, the Triangle Lemma implies

v_{b,c}\in\{v_{b},v_{c}\}.

We then define the following grid
G
, indexed by ordered pairs of elements of
X\setminus\{a\}
:

G_{b,c}=\begin{cases}*,&b\sim c;\\
0,&v_{b,c}=v_{b}(=v_{a,b});\\
1,&v_{b,c}=v_{c}(=v_{a,c}).\end{cases}

Denote by
G_{b}
and
G^{c}
the row and column of
G
corresponding to
b
and
c
, respectively.

Step 7.

Assume that some row
G_{b}
contains at least
\frac{1}{3}|X|
many
0
s. Let
X^{\prime}
denote the set of all
c
such that
G_{b,c}=0
. For any
c\in X^{\prime}
, it holds that
v_{b,c}=v_{b}
. We can then proceed similarly to Step 5: find an
s\in\{0,1\}
such that
p\cup\{v_{b}=s\}
forces
b\triangleleft c
for at least half of the elements of
X^{\prime}
, and apply induction to
X\setminus X^{\prime}
, whose size is at most
\frac{5}{6}|X|
.

For the remainder of the proof, we assume that every row of
G
contains fewer than
\frac{1}{3}|X|
many
0
s.

Step 8.

Note that each row and each column of
G
contains fewer than
\frac{1}{3}|X|
many
*
s. Let
o
denote the maximum number of
1
s contained in a single column of
G
. By double-counting the number of
1
s in
G
, and using the assumptions that every row contains fewer than
\frac{1}{3}|X|
many
0
s and fewer than
\frac{1}{3}|X|
many
*
s, it follows that

|X\setminus\{a\}|\cdot o>|X\setminus\{a\}|\cdot(|X\setminus\{a\}|-\frac{2}{3}|X|),

which implies
o>\frac{1}{3}|X|-1
.

The column version of Step 7 now applies: if a column
G^{c}
contains at least
\frac{1}{3}|X|-1
-many
1
s, then we can fix
v_{c}
appropriately to
s
to force
c\triangleleft b
for at least
\frac{1}{6}|X|-\frac{1}{2}
-many
b
s. By induction, we can extend
p\cup\{v_{c}=s\}
to a condition
q
of size

|q|\leq|p|+1+C\log_{2}\left(\frac{5}{6}|X|+\frac{1}{2}\right)\leq|p|+C\log_{2}|X|,

finishing the proof.
∎

Unfortunately, we are unable to extend the above proof method to the general case needed for resolving Question
4.1
. We believe that the crucial missing detail is a more general analogue of the Triangle Lemma.

Below, we formulate a stronger, but arguably more natural, condition on orderings defined by polynomial-time oracle Turing machines that is enough to resolve Question
4.1
. This condition may be of independent interest.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
T[\alpha](\overline{x},x,y)
,
n
,

, and

be as in Question

. We say that

factorizes on

if and only if there is a polynomial-time oracle Turing machine

satisfying the following properties:

•

there is a single number

quasi-polynomial in

such that

for all total assignments

and all

;

•

for any total assignment

, the function

is injective on

;

•

for any distinct numbers

, and any total assignment

,

if and only if

We choose the name because the computation of

into two independent computations

and

Proposition 4.4

Proof.

Since the image of

when varying

and

Hypothesis 4.6

(Factorization Hypothesis)
.

Any
T[\alpha](\overline{x},x,y)
as in Question
4.1
factorizes.

It is important to keep in mind that
f
does not need to calculate the exact index of the corresponding number in the given ordering. In other words,
f
need not be surjective on
[m]
.

Below, we give an example of
T
that factorizes, but for which no polynomial-time indexing function can be constructed.

Example
.

We define
T[\alpha](x,y)
for
m=\binom{n}{2}
as follows. Given distinct
a,b\in[m]
, the machine
T[\alpha](a,b)
queries the variables encoded by
a
and
b
. If both queries result in the same answer, the numbers are ordered by the usual numerical ordering. Otherwise, the number with query answer
0
is treated as the smaller.

To see that such
T
factorizes, we construct
f
as follows:

f[\alpha](a)=\begin{cases}a,&\text{if the query }a\mapsto?\text{ is answered }0;\\
a+m,&\text{if the query }a\mapsto?\text{ is answered }1.\end{cases}

To see that no polynomial-time oracle machine can compute the exact index of
a
in the induced ordering, note that the exact rank of
a
depends on the number of indices whose oracle answers are
0
or
1
. For
a
near
\lfloor m/2\rfloor
, changing any one of linearly many still-unqueried oracle bits can change this rank. Since
m=\binom{n}{2}
is much larger than any polynomial in
|n|
, no algorithm making only polynomially many queries in
|n|
can determine the exact rank uniformly.
