Action on the Sphere: An Interfering Mean-Field Propagator for the Bose-Hubbard Dimer
Elana F. Todd-Miller, Eva-Maria Graefe

Abstract
The Bose-Hubbard system has been studied extensively both theoretically and experimentally, in particular in the context of ultracold atomic gases in optical lattices. Even in the two-mode case the many-particle dynamics display complex interference effects resulting in revival and breakdown phenomena as well as tunnelling. The most basic theoretical description is the mean-field approximation, which can be derived from a time-dependent variational principle assuming the many-particle wave function is an SU ⁡ ( 2 ) \mathrm{SU}(2) coherent state. Here we build on this to construct a simple initial-value coherent state propagator, summing over mean-field trajectories and keeping track of their phases, given by the corresponding mean-field actions. This yields an approximation to the full time-dependent many-particle state, and is able to reproduce breakdown and revival dynamics. Applying a time-slicing procedure on top of this, we are able to accurately capture many-particle tunnelling effects. While in this paper we focus our analysis on the Bose-Hubbard dimer, the methods developed can be applied to more general SU ⁡ ( 2 ) \mathrm{SU}(2) Hamiltonians, and can be extended to SU ⁡ ( M ) \mathrm{SU}(M) systems.



Keywords
: Bose-Hubbard dimer; semiclassical methods; SU(2) coherent states

1
Introduction

The correspondence between many-particle and mean-field descriptions of multi-boson quantum systems is formally identical to that of the quantum and classical descriptions of a single particle. This fact has led to a resurgence of semiclassical methods adapted and applied to many-particle quantum systems. One of the most prominent examples is the Bose-Hubbard model, which can be experimentally realised using ultracold bosons in optical lattices, and displays a plethora of non-trivial many-body effects
[
5
,
15
,
14
,
20
,
30
,
22
,
38
]
. These systems are promising candidates for practical applications, as quantum simulators
[
16
,
7
]
.
Already the two-mode Bose-Hubbard Hamiltonian, modelling for example an ensemble of cold atoms in a double-well potential
[
27
,
2
,
13
,
53
]
, displays characteristic breakdown and revival phenomena
[
27
,
39
]
and many-particle tunnelling between mean-field self-trapping states
[
32
]
.

The mean-field approximation of the full many-particle dynamics, is often derived using multi-mode Glauber coherent states, yet, an
N
-boson condensed state is mathematically correctly described by an
\mathrm{SU}(M)
coherent state
[
36
,
29
,
52
]
. In the limit of large particle numbers, the two coherent-state approximations yield equivalent mean-field dynamics, but only the treatment via
\mathrm{SU}(M)
coherent states correctly preserves the
U(1)
symmetry of the many-particle system, leading to mean-field dynamics on a compact phase space, reflecting the well-defined particle number of the many-particle system
[
6
,
46
,
47
,
35
]
. While the mean-field dynamics is often a sufficient approximation for the dynamical behaviour of cold atoms in optical lattices for large numbers of particles and high filling factors, strong interactions and smaller particle numbers lead to a breakdown of the mean-field approximation
[
48
,
12
,
28
]
. Taking the initial quantum fluctuations into account leads to the commonly applied truncated Wigner approximation
[
44
,
43
,
31
,
30
]
, which essentially propagates an ensemble of initial mean-field trajectories and sums them up incoherently, i.e., excluding phase factors. This can reproduce the typical breakdown observed in the expectation values in the presence of interaction. Neither the subsequent revival behaviour nor many-particle tunnelling effects can be captured by this approximation.

To go beyond the truncated Wigner approximation one needs to take the phases of the individual mean-field trajectories into account. In this spirit, the Herman-Kluk semiclassical propagator, based on the flat space multi-mode Glauber coherent states, has been shown to accurately capture breakdown and revival dynamics in the single
[
37
]
, and two- and three-mode
[
42
]
Bose-Hubbard model. Further
[
10
,
11
,
45
,
26
]
use the Glauber coherent state basis to derive full Van Vleck type saddle-point approximations to a path integral propagator. Although this is a powerful approach, its implementation is highly non-trivial, and the recovery of the full quantum state beyond single mode systems would require major effort.

A tangential approach to the semiclassical treatment, uses a time-dependent variational principle with a linear combination of coherent states. A single-coherent state approximation yields the mean-field dynamics
[
51
]
; a coupled coherent state approximation
[
41
,
40
]
can be employed to simulate many-body dynamics to arbitrary accuracy
[
33
,
34
]
. For this purpose a time-dependent variational principle is applied to a wavefunction expressed as a superposition of
\mathrm{SU}(2)
coherent states, leading to coupled equations of motion for the coefficients of each coherent state in addition to the mean-field dynamics of the individual states. In principle this can exactly reproduce the full many-body state, however, the computational cost grows significantly as more coherent states are added to the ansatz.

Here we follow a different route, closer in spirit to semiclassical ideas, propagating a set of uncoupled
\mathrm{SU}(2)
coherent states, similar to the Gaussian propagator for flat phase spaces
[
17
,
25
]
. For this purpose, we apply the time evolution operator to an arbitrary state expressed in the overcomplete basis of coherent states. The evolution of each component state is then approximated using the mean-field equations of motion, crucially keeping track of each one’s phase which is given by the action of the corresponding mean-field trajectory. This yields an approximation of the full many-particle state using only mean-field dynamics, in the spirit of an
\mathrm{SU}(M)
initial value coherent state propagator
[
49
]
. Numerically we implement the integral on the sphere using Gauss-Legendre quadrature. Alternatively the integral could be handled with a Monte Carlo approach. The resulting propagator is exact in the non-interacting case, and is highly effective in capturing the breakdown and revival phenomena displayed in the full many-body dynamics. While the grid implementation may not be feasible for systems with larger phase-space dimension, the method does not involve coupled equations of motion between the different component states, and is thus highly parallelisable.

This propagator is extended using a time-slicing procedure, similar to the well-known procedure in the flat case
[
19
,
4
]
. This method is computationally faster, and most importantly is able to replicate even many-particle tunnelling dynamics. We note that in the time-slicing, we pick up an accumulated effect of an infinitesimal error in the propagator, which we are able to estimate from analysis of the pure interaction term in the semiclassical limit. Interestingly, this manifests as a scaling factor in the interaction term, the asymptotic behaviour of which we derive analytically, and which we numerically find for smaller values of the particle number. Incorporating this scaling factor, the states propagated with the time-sliced interfering mean-field propagator are almost indistinguishable from the true dynamics even for long propagation times.

The paper is organised as follows. We begin in section
2
with a short introduction to the Bose-Hubbard dimer and its relation to the
\mathrm{su}(2)
algebra, and provide the necessary background on
\mathrm{SU}(2)
coherent states. We briefly review how the mean-field dynamics is obtained from a coherent-state approximation via a time-dependent variational principle, including a phase factor. In section
3
we introduce the Interfering Mean-Field (IMF) propagator. For the pure interaction Hamiltonian, a closed form expression for the matrix elements of the propagator in the
\hat{J}_{z}
basis can be found. Using a saddle-point approximation we find a reduced expression in the limit of large particle number
N
, which differs from the exact result. Nevertheless, as we show through examples, this yields a very good approximation for expectation values and even the full time-evolved Wigner functions. We discuss the numerical implementation of this propagator for a general Hamiltonian and show that it reproduces break-down and revival behaviour, but stops short of tunnelling.
We then introduce a time-sliced version of the IMF propagator in section
4
. We show that this can reproduce tunnelling behaviour to high accuracy, even far from the semiclassical limit, with a scaling factor in the interaction parameter. Based on the results of the saddle-point approximation from the previous section we predict this factor in the limit of large
N
. We further use numerical optimisation to find the factors for a range of small
N
.
We finish with a summary and outlook in section
5
. Some technical details are left for the appendices.

2
Bose-Hubbard dimer and
\mathrm{SU}(2)
coherent states

A two-mode bosonic system with on-site two-body interaction may be described by a Bose-Hubbard Hamiltonian of the form

\hat{H}_{BH}=\varepsilon(\hat{n}_{1}-\hat{n}_{2})+\nu(\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{2}+\hat{a}_{1}\hat{a}_{2}^{\dagger})+c\left(\hat{n}_{1}(\hat{n}_{1}-1)+\hat{n}_{2}(\hat{n}_{2}-1)\right),

(1)

where
\hat{a}_{k},\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}
are the bosonic annihilation and creation operators for the
k
-th mode, and
\hat{n}_{k}=\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{k}
are the number operators associated to each site. The coefficient
\epsilon
denotes the energy difference between the two modes,
\nu
, the single particle hopping amplitude, and
c
the on-site interaction strength. The Hamiltonian commutes with the overall particle number
\hat{N}=\hat{n}_{1}+\hat{n}_{2}
, which is thus dynamically conserved.
For a fixed particle number
N=n_{1}+n_{2}
, the Hilbert space is
(N+1)
-dimensional with basis Fock states
|n_{1},n_{2}\rangle
, with
\hat{n}_{k}|n_{1},n_{2}\rangle=n_{k}|n_{1},n_{2}\rangle
.

Near zero temperature, all bosons populate the same single-particle state, forming a condensate. This condensed state can be expressed as

|\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\left(\psi_{1}\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}+\psi_{2}\hat{a}_{2}^{\dagger}\right)^{N}|0,0\rangle,

(2)

where
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in\mathds{C}
are the amplitudes of the two-mode single particle state.

The system can equivalently be described as a spin
j=N/2
system via the Jordan-Schwinger transformation

[
52
]

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{2}+\hat{a}_{1}\hat{a}_{2}^{\dagger}),

(3)

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\rmi}(\hat{a}_{1}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{2}-\hat{a}_{1}\hat{a}_{2}^{\dagger}),

(4)

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{n}_{1}-\hat{n}_{2}).

(5)

With these, the raising and lowering operators may be defined as
\hat{J}_{\pm}=\hat{J}_{x}\pm\rmi\hat{J}_{y}.
These operators satisfy the standard
\mathrm{su}(2)
commutation relations

[\hat{J}_{k},\hat{J}_{l}]=\rmi\epsilon_{klm}\hat{J}_{m},\quad[\hat{J}_{+},\hat{J}_{-}]=2\hat{J}_{z},\quad[\hat{J}_{z},\hat{J}_{\pm}]=\pm\hat{J}_{\pm}.

(6)

Note that we will use rescaled units with
\hbar=1
throughout this paper. The inverse particle number, i.e. the inverse spin quantum number will act as effective
\hbar
.

In terms of the spin operators, up to overall terms proportional to the fixed particle number
\hat{N}
, the Bose-Hubbard Hamiltonian (
1
) becomes

\hat{H}_{BH}=2\varepsilon\hat{J}_{z}+2\nu\hat{J}_{x}+2\frac{\kappa}{j}\hat{J}_{z}^{2},

(7)

where we have introduced
\kappa=Nc
, such that
\kappa
remains fixed in the macroscopic limit of
N\to\infty
. This ensures that the interaction energy per particle stays constant while the system size grows, permitting the comparison of different spin systems as well as resulting in well-defined classical dynamics. In this formulation the Fock states
|n_{1},n_{2}\rangle
can be identified with the
\hat{J}_{z}
eigenbasis,
|j,m\rangle
where
j=N/2
and
m=(n_{1}-n_{2})/2
, and the condensed states are none other than the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
or spin coherent states.

2.1
\mathrm{SU}(2)
coherent states

\mathrm{SU}(2)
coherent states are commonly defined as
[
36
,
52
,
29
]

\displaystyle|\zeta\rangle:=|\zeta;j\rangle=\frac{1}{(1+|\zeta|^{2})^{j}}e^{\zeta\hat{J}_{-}}|j,j\rangle,

(8)

where
\zeta=e^{\rmi\phi}\tan\frac{\theta}{2}
is a stereographic projection of the unit sphere with spherical co-ordinates
(\theta,\phi)
, onto the flat complex plane. Alternatively, the coherent states may be expressed in terms of the canonical conjugate variables
z=\cos\theta
, and
\phi
. In the
\hat{J}_{z}
basis, coherent states have the form

\displaystyle{}|\zeta\rangle

\displaystyle=(1+|\zeta|^{2})^{-j}\sum_{m=-j}^{j}\sqrt{\binom{2j}{j-m}}\zeta^{j-m}|j,m\rangle,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{j}}\sum_{m=-j}^{j}\sqrt{\binom{2j}{j-m}}\,(1+z)^{(j+m)/2}(1-z)^{(j-m)/2}e^{\rmi\phi(j-m)}|j,m\rangle.

(9)

A short calculation shows that this equivalent to the condensed states (
2
), where

\psi_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{1+z}{2}},\quad\psi_{2}=\sqrt{\frac{1-z}{2}}\rme^{\rmi\phi}.

(10)

The
\mathrm{SU}(2)
coherent states form an overcomplete basis on the
(2j+1)
‑dimensional Hilbert space, forming the resolution of the identity
[
52
]

\displaystyle{}\hat{I}

\displaystyle=\frac{2j+1}{\pi}\int_{\mathds{c}}\frac{|\zeta\rangle\langle\zeta|}{(1+|\zeta|^{2})^{2}}\rmd^{2}\zeta

\displaystyle=\frac{2j+1}{4\pi}\int^{2\pi}_{0}\int^{1}_{-1}|z,\phi\rangle\langle z,\phi|\rmd z\rmd\phi.

(11)

The expectation values of the spin operators in
\mathrm{SU}(2)
coherent states are given by

\displaystyle\langle\hat{J}_{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{j(\zeta+\zeta^{*})}{1+|\zeta|^{2}}=j\sqrt{1-z^{2}}\ \cos\phi,

(12)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{J}_{y}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{j(\zeta-\zeta^{*})}{\rmi(1+|\zeta|^{2})}=j\sqrt{1-z^{2}}\,\sin\phi,

(13)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{J}_{z}\rangle

\displaystyle=j\frac{1-|\zeta|^{2}}{1+|\zeta|^{2}}=jz,

(14)

We will frequently use scaled expectation values

x=\frac{\langle\hat{J}_{x}\rangle}{j},\quad y=\frac{\langle\hat{J}_{y}\rangle}{j},\quad z=\frac{\langle\hat{J}_{z}\rangle}{j}.

(15)

Expectation values of products of spin operators can be deduced from the group theoretical properties of the coherent states (see
A
).

2.2
Time-dependent variational principle for a single SU(2) coherent state

The assumption that the state remains condensed for all times, and can be described by a single particle mean-field state, is equivalent to a coherent-state approximation. The corresponding equations of motion can be derived from a time-dependent variational principle.

Starting from the time-dependent Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle\rmi\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\psi(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\hat{H}|\psi(t)\rangle,

(16)

for an initially coherent state
|\psi_{0}\rangle=c_{0}|\zeta_{0}\rangle
,
constraining the state to remain coherent over time up to a phase factor,

\displaystyle|\psi(t)\rangle=c(t)|\zeta(t)\rangle\quad\text{ such that }\quad|\psi(0)\rangle=c_{0}|\zeta_{0}\rangle,

(17)

yields equations of motion for the coherent state coordinate
\zeta(t)
and the prefactor
c(t)
, given by

\displaystyle\rmi\dot{\zeta}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+|\zeta|^{2})^{2}}{2j}\frac{\partial\langle\hat{H}\rangle}{\partial\zeta^{*}},

(18)

\displaystyle\rmi\dot{c}

\displaystyle=c\Big[\langle\hat{H}\rangle+\rmi\frac{j}{1+|\zeta|^{2}}(\zeta^{*}\dot{\zeta}-\zeta\dot{\zeta}^{*})\Big]

(19)

where we have used the abbreviation
\langle\hat{H}\rangle=\langle\zeta|\hat{H}|\zeta\rangle
to denote the expectation value of the Hamiltonian in coherent states.
We will dub these the single coherent state time dependent variational principle (SCS-TDVP) equations of motion.
For details of the derivation see
B
.

Alternatively, we may write the equation for
\dot{c}
as

\displaystyle\rmi\dot{c}

\displaystyle=c\Big[\langle\hat{H}\rangle-\frac{1+|\zeta|^{2}}{2}(\zeta\frac{\partial\langle\hat{H}\rangle}{\partial\zeta}+\zeta^{*}\frac{\partial\langle\hat{H}\rangle}{\partial\zeta^{*}})\Big]

(20)

In terms of the real coordinates
z=\cos\theta
and
\phi
with
\zeta=\tan\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\rme^{\rmi\phi}
, the SCS-TDVP equations of motion take the form

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{j}\frac{\partial\langle\hat{H}\rangle}{\partial z},

(21)

\displaystyle\dot{z}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{j}\frac{\partial\langle\hat{H}\rangle}{\partial\phi},

(22)

\displaystyle\rmi\dot{c}

\displaystyle=c\Big[\langle\hat{H}\rangle+(1-z)\frac{\partial\langle\hat{H}\rangle}{\partial z}\Big].

(23)

That is, the variables
(\phi,z)
follow Hamiltonian dynamics where
\frac{1}{j}\langle\hat{H}\rangle
acts as Hamiltonian function. Expressing
c
as

c(t)=c_{0}e^{\rmi S(t;\zeta_{0})}

(24)

we recognise
S
as the classical action, which can be expressed as

\displaystyle S(t;\zeta_{0})

\displaystyle=\int^{t}_{0}\rmi\frac{j}{1+|\zeta(t^{\prime})|^{2}}(\zeta^{*}(t^{\prime})\dot{\zeta}(t^{\prime})-\zeta(t^{\prime})\dot{\zeta}^{*}(r^{\prime}))-\langle\hat{H}\rangle(t^{\prime})\rmd t^{\prime},

(25)

\displaystyle=\int^{t}_{0}j\dot{\phi}(t^{\prime})[z(t^{\prime})-1]-\langle\hat{H}\rangle(t^{\prime})\rmd t^{\prime}.

(26)

For a Hamiltonian that is linear in the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
generators
\hat{J}_{k}
, an initial coherent state remains coherent for all times, and thus the SCS-TDVP equations of motion (
21
)-(
23
) recover the exact dynamics. For more general Hamiltonians, such as the Bose-Hubbard dimer (
7
) the time-dependent state (
17
) is an approximation of the true quantum state, closely related to the mean-field approximation.

Note that the equations of motion for
z
and
\phi
have been derived for the specific case of a Bose-Hubbard dimer using an equivalent approach in
[
51
]
. The single coherent state ansatz here is a special case of the coupled coherent state approach of
[
33
,
34
]
.

Let us now derive the SCS-TDVP equations of motion for the Bose-Hubbard Hamiltonian (
7
). Using that in coherent states it holds that

\displaystyle\langle\hat{J}_{k}^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{J}_{k}\rangle^{2}(1-\frac{1}{2j})+\frac{j}{2}.

(27)

one finds

\frac{\langle\hat{H}_{BH}\rangle}{j}=2\varepsilon z+2\nu\cos\phi(1-z^{2})^{1/2}+2\kappa(1-\frac{1}{2j})z^{2},

(28)

where we have omitted an additive constant
\kappa
, which does not affect the dynamics and only adds an overall constant phase, independent of the initial conditions.
The constraint-coherent state dynamics are governed by the equations of motion

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}

\displaystyle=2\varepsilon-\frac{2\nu z\cos\phi}{(1-z^{2})^{1/2}}+4\kappa(1-\frac{1}{2j})z,

(29)

\displaystyle\dot{z}

\displaystyle=2\nu(1-z^{2})^{1/2}\sin\phi,

(30)

\displaystyle\dot{c}

\displaystyle=-\rmi jc\Big[2\varepsilon+\frac{2\nu(1-z^{2})^{1/2}\cos\phi}{1+z}-2\kappa(1-\frac{1}{2j})z(z-2)\Big].

(31)

As expected, in the limit of
j\to\infty
the equations for
\phi
and
\zeta
reduce to the well-known mean-field dynamics
[
27
]
, which, in the linear case
\kappa=0
describe the exact dynamics for an initial coherent state.

3
The Interfering Mean-Field Propagator using
\mathrm{SU}(2)
coherent states

Using the overcompleteness of
\mathrm{SU}(2)
coherent states (
11
), we can build on the SCS-TDVP to construct a simple “semiclassical” propagator in the spirit of Heller’s frozen Gaussian propagator
[
17
]
. For linear Hamiltonians, this yields the exact many-particle time evolution for arbitrary initial states, and for nonlinear (i.e. interacting) Hamiltonians yields a good approximation of the exact dynamics; in particular it is able to replicate coherent breakdown and revival phenomena.

An arbitrary time evolved state
|\psi(t)\rangle=\hat{U}(t)|\psi(0)\rangle
may be expanded as

\hat{U}(t)|\psi(0)\rangle=\frac{2j+1}{4\pi}\int^{2\pi}_{0}\int^{1}_{-1}\hat{U}(t)|z_{0},\phi_{0}\rangle\langle z_{0},\phi_{0}|\psi(0)\rangle\rmd z_{0}\rmd\phi_{0}.

(32)

This is an exact representation of the quantum dynamics. This can now be approximated by using the SCS-TDVP dynamics for the time-evolution of each initial coherent state
|z_{0},\phi_{0}\rangle
in the integral. That is, we replace

\hat{U}(t)|z_{0},\phi_{0}\rangle
with the SCS-TDVP approximation. However, instead of using the coherent state expectation value
\langle\hat{H}\rangle
of the quantum Hamiltonian as generator in the equations of motion, we use the
classical/mean-field limit
of the Hamiltonian, given by

H=\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{\langle\hat{H}\rangle}{j}.

(33)

This omission of the higher order
j
corrections in the SCS-TDVP is to avoid overcounting of uncertainty effects that are described by these terms, but also encoded into the dynamics of neighbouring initial states in the integral (
32
). We have verified numerically that using the SCS-TDVP trajectories with the correction term leads to consistently worse correspondence with the numerically exact many-particle dynamics.

The Interfering Mean-Field (IMF) propagator is thus defined as

\hat{U}^{IMF}(t):=\frac{2j+1}{4\pi}\int^{2\pi}_{0}\int^{1}_{-1}c(t;z_{0},\phi_{0})|z(t;z_{0},\phi_{0}),\phi(t;z_{0},\phi_{0})\rangle\langle z_{0},\phi_{0}|\rmd z_{0}\rmd\phi_{0},

(34)

where
z(t)
,
\phi(t)
, and
c(t)
are the solutions to the equations of motion

\dot{\phi}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial z},\qquad\dot{z}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\phi},\qquad\rmi\dot{c}=jc\Big[H+(1-z)\frac{\partial H}{\partial z}\Big].

(35)

at time
t
, with initial conditions
z(t=0)=z_{0}
,
\phi(t=0)=\phi_{0}
, and
c(t=0)=1
.

For the Bose-Hubbard Hamiltonian considered here we have (up to an additive constant)

H(z,\phi)=2\varepsilon z+2\nu\cos\phi(1-z^{2})^{1/2}+2\kappa z^{2},

(36)

the equations of motion are given by the mean-field equations

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}

\displaystyle=2\varepsilon-\frac{2\nu z\cos\phi}{(1-z^{2})^{1/2}}+4\kappa z,

(37)

\displaystyle\dot{z}

\displaystyle=2\nu(1-z^{2})^{1/2}\sin\phi,

(38)

\displaystyle\dot{c}

\displaystyle=-\rmi j\Big[2\varepsilon+\frac{2\nu(1-z^{2})^{1/2}\cos\phi}{1+z}-2\kappa z(z-2)\Big]c.

(39)

In what follows, we consider initially coherent states for our examples as they yield interesting and clear dynamical behaviour. The method is in no way restricted to such states and performs equally well on arbitrary initial states.

3.1
Pure Interaction Case

In the linear case, propagation via the IMF propagator yields exact results for arbitrary initial states. To gain insights into the performance of the approximation in the general case, it is instructive to consider just the Kerr-like interaction term in the Bose-Hubbard Hamiltonian, given by

\hat{H}_{\rm Kerr}=\frac{2\kappa}{j}\hat{J}_{z}^{2}.

(40)

The matrix elements of the time-evolution operator in the
\hat{J}_{z}
basis are given as

\displaystyle U^{True}_{n,m}(t)

\displaystyle:=\langle j,n|\hat{U}^{True}(t)|j,m\rangle,{}

\displaystyle=\delta_{nm}e^{-2\rmi\kappa tm^{2}/j}.

(41)

The quantum dynamics display the famous breakdown and revival phenomena
[
27
]
, illustrated for an example in figures
1
and
2
.

The mean-field equations of motion are directly integrated to yield

\displaystyle z(t)

\displaystyle=z_{0},

(42)

\displaystyle\phi(t)

\displaystyle=4\kappa z_{0}t+\phi_{0},

(43)

\displaystyle c(t)

\displaystyle=c_{0}e^{\rmi 2j\kappa z(z-2)t}.

(44)

The mean-field trajectories move along circles of latitude, with frequency of rotation proportional to the value of
z_{0}
. Figure
1
displays the mean-field trajectory in comparison to the expectation values of the full quantum dynamics for an initial coherent state with
z_{0}=0.8,\phi_{0}=0
. The quantum expectation value shows the typical breakdown behaviour due to the stretching of the quantum wave-packet, not accounted for in the individual mean-field trajectory, followed by a full revival at longer times (not shown in the figure) due to quantum interference.

Figure 2:
Comparison of the quantum (blue) and IMF (red)
x
-expectation values for spin 10 (top) and spin 50 (bottom) with
\kappa=0.5
, for an initial coherent state at
z_{0}=0.8,\phi_{0}=0
.

We will now demonstrate that while the IMF propagator is not exact for Kerr-interaction (even in the limit of
j\to\infty
), it does in fact recover the breakdown and revival behaviour well for even relatively small values of
j
(such as
j=10
).

The matrix elements of the IMF propagator in the
\hat{J}_{z}
basis are given by

\displaystyle U^{IMF}_{m,n}(t)=

\displaystyle\frac{2j+1}{4\pi}\int^{2\pi}_{0}\int^{1}_{-1}e^{\rmi 2j\kappa z(z-2)t}\langle j,m|z_{0},4\kappa z_{0}t+\phi_{0}\rangle\langle z_{0},\phi_{0}|j,n\rangle\rmd z_{0}\rmd\phi_{0}.

(45)

Using that

\langle j,m|z_{0},\phi_{0}\rangle=\frac{1}{2^{j}}\sqrt{\binom{2j}{j-m}}\,(1+z_{0})^{\frac{j+m}{2}}(1-z_{0})^{\frac{j-m}{2}}\,e^{\rmi\phi_{0}(j-m)},

(46)

and completing the square in the exponent yields

\displaystyle{}U^{IMF}_{m,n}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2j+1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\binom{2j}{j-m}\binom{2j}{j-n}}e^{-\rmi 2\kappa tm^{2}/j}\times\int^{2\pi}_{0}e^{\rmi\phi_{0}(n-m)}\rmd\phi_{0}

\displaystyle\times\int^{1}_{-1}2^{-(2j+1)}(1+z_{0})^{j+\frac{m+n}{2}}(1-z_{0})^{j-\frac{m+n}{2}}e^{\rmi 2jt\kappa(z_{0}-\frac{m}{j})^{2}}\rmd z_{0}.

(47)

Performing the
\phi
integration leads to the final expression

\displaystyle U_{n,m}^{IMF}=\delta_{nm}e^{-\rmi 2\kappa tm^{2}/j}g_{m}(2\kappa t),

(48)

where we have defined the function
g_{m}(A)
is as

\displaystyle g_{m}(A)=\frac{(2j+1)!}{2^{2j+1}(j+m)!(j-m)!}\int^{1}_{-1}(1+z_{0})^{j+m}(1-z_{0})^{j-m}e^{\rmi Aj(z_{0}-\frac{m}{j})^{2}}\rmd z_{0}.

(49)

Importantly, the IMF propagator preserves the diagonal structure of the true propagator, however, there is an additional
m
-dependent factor.

Figure 3:
The norms of the quantum state propagated with the IMF for
\kappa=0.5
, for an initial coherent state at

are shown with spin 10 in magenta and spin 50 in black.

As a sanity check, we observe that in the case of vanishing interaction (
\kappa=0
), the IMF propagator reduces to the identity matrix:

\displaystyle U^{IMF}_{m,n}|_{\kappa=0}

\displaystyle=\delta_{nm}\frac{(2j+1)!}{2^{2j+1}(j+m)!(j-m)!}\int^{1}_{-1}(1-z_{0})^{j-m}(1+z_{0})^{j+m}\rmd z_{0},{}

\displaystyle=\delta_{nm}\frac{2(2j+1)!}{(j+m)!(j-m)!}\frac{B(j+m+1,j-m+1)}{2},{}

\displaystyle=\delta_{nm},

(50)

where
B(x,y)
denotes the beta-function and we have used that for integer
k,l
it holds
B(k+1,l+1)=\frac{k!l!}{(k+l+1)!}
.

For
\kappa\neq 0
, the integral
g_{m}(2\kappa t)
is not exactly solvable, but can be evaluated in a saddle-point approximation (see
C
for details) in the semiclassical limit
j\to\infty
to yield

\displaystyle U^{IMF}_{n,m}(t;\text{large }j)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{nm}e^{-\rmi 2\kappa tm^{2}/j}}{\sqrt{1-\rmi 2\kappa t(1-\frac{m^{2}}{j^{2}})}}.

(51)

Thus, we conclude that even in the
j\to\infty
limit the IMF propagator is only an approximation to the true propagator with an
m
dependent scaling factor. For small times or small interaction strength this factor is close to unity.

Note that the approximation is not unitary, resulting in non-physical norm decay. This is a feature that is commonly found in semi-classical propagators, and in flat space can often can be mitigated by Herman-Kluk type generalisations
[
37
,
18
]
.

4
A Time-Slicing Initial Value Coherent State Propagator

Though the IMF propagator is not exact in the non-linear case, it may be argued that for short times, on which the individual coherent states do not spread significantly, it is a good approximation. This motivates the use of a time-slicing method, similar to that of Huber’s and Heller’s Hybrid Mechanics in flat space
[
19
,
8
]
, where the propagator is applied for a short time before re-expanding the resulting state in the original coherent-state basis and repeating the procedure. As we shall show, this will allow us to recover even the many-body tunnelling dynamics between mean-field self-trapping states.

Beginning at time zero, we evolve the initial state up to time
\delta t
using the IMF propagator, and take the result as our new initial state, resetting the time to zero. We repeat this process a further
M-1
times, until the desired final time
t=M\delta t
, to find our final approximate time evolved state. Thus, the time-sliced IMF time-evolution operator can be expressed as

\hat{U}^{TS}(t)=\prod^{M}_{m=1}\hat{U}^{IMF}(\delta t).

(53)

If the grid is large enough, in principle the smaller one chooses
\delta t
the better the result, but, there is a trade off with an accumulated error from the repeated re-expansion on a finite grid. In practice, however, we have found that choosing a smaller
\delta t
allows for a comparatively smaller grid size, as less coherent states are needed to approximate the dynamics for short times. Further, we only need to solve the individual ODEs once for the first short time interval, which is much shorter than the time necessary for the single-slice IMF implementation, which leads to a significant speed up when moving to a time-sliced version with small
\delta t
. In practice, we pick
\delta t
such that the norm does not drop below a threshold, which for our examples we took to be 0.9999. Note that the rate of the norm decay in each segment depends on the initial condition. We observe a convergence of the resulting dynamics with decreasing value of
\delta t
. However, it should be noted that the limit of
\delta t\to 0
does not necessarily yield the exact many-particle dynamics.

reveals, that the error in every time-slice is linear in
\delta t

51
) into (
53

and the limit of infinitesimal time slices (

) as

\displaystyle\hat{U}^{TS}_{n,n^{\prime}}(t)

\displaystyle=\lim_{M\to\infty}\prod^{M}_{m=1}\delta_{nn^{\prime}}e^{-\rmi 2\kappa n^{2}t/Mj}\left[1-2\rmi\kappa\frac{t}{M}\left(1-\frac{n^{2}}{j^{2}}\right)\right]^{-1/2},{}

\displaystyle=\delta_{nn^{\prime}}e^{-2\rmi\kappa n^{2}t/j}\lim_{M\to\infty}\left(1-\frac{at}{M}\right)^{-M/2},

(54)

where
a=2\rmi\kappa\left(1-\frac{n^{2}}{j^{2}}\right)
. Using that

e^{x}=\lim_{M\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{x}{M}\right)^{M},

(55)

this reduces to

\displaystyle\hat{U}^{TS}_{n,n^{\prime}}(t)

\displaystyle=\delta_{nn^{\prime}}e^{-2\rmi\kappa(1+\frac{1}{2j})n^{2}/j}e^{\rmi\kappa t}.

(56)

The second exponential term is a constant real phase and does not affect the state dynamics. The other term has the same form as the exact solution, however, with a rescaled interaction parameter
\kappa_{eff}

5
Summary and Outlook

In summary, we have constructed a relatively simple semiclassical propagator which utilises interfering mean-field trajectories, which we have dubbed the Interfering Mean-Field (IMF) propagator. This method provides an approximation for the full time-evolved quantum state, and is able to effectively capture breakdown and revival phenomena. By extending the method with time-slicing, we are able to replicate even many-particle tunnelling to high accuracy. When an additional scaling is applied to the interaction coefficient, the constructed state is quantitatively very close to the true time evolution both for large time scales and far from the semiclassical limit.

Though this paper has focused on the application to the Bose-Hubbard dimer, the method straight-forwardly extends to other
\mathrm{SU}(2)
Hamiltonians. On a theoretical level the extension to
\mathrm{SU}(M)
systems, describing for example Bose-Hubbard systems with
M
sites, poses no significant challenge. However, the higher phase-space dimensions make a direct grid method as implemented here less feasible for a larger number of sites. For this case an evaluation of the phase-space integral using Monte-Carlo sampling could be used efficiently
[
24
]
.

Appendix A
Expectation Value Derivations

To begin we derive the action of the spin raising and lowering operators
\hat{J}_{\pm}=\hat{J}_{x}\pm\rmi\hat{J}_{y}
as well as
\hat{J}_{z}
on an unnormalised coherent state
||\zeta\rangle=e^{\zeta\hat{J}_{-}}|j,j\rangle
. The Hadamard lemmas will be useful for this exercise

\displaystyle e^{-\zeta\hat{J}_{-}}\hat{J}_{z}e^{\zeta\hat{J}_{-}}

\displaystyle=\hat{J}_{z}-\zeta\hat{J}_{-},

(58)

\displaystyle e^{-\zeta\hat{J}_{+}}\hat{J}_{-}e^{\zeta\hat{J}_{+}}

\displaystyle=\hat{J}_{-}-2\zeta\hat{J}_{z}-\zeta^{2}\hat{J}_{+},

(59)

\displaystyle e^{-\zeta\hat{J}_{-}}\hat{J}_{+}e^{\zeta\hat{J}_{-}}

\displaystyle=\hat{J}_{+}+2\zeta\hat{J}_{z}-\zeta^{2}\hat{J}_{-}.

(60)

Finding the action of the lowering operator on an unnormalised coherent state is straightforward:

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{-}||\zeta\rangle

\displaystyle=\hat{J}_{-}e^{\zeta\hat{J}_{-}}|j,j\rangle,{}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\left\{e^{\zeta\hat{J}_{-}}\right\}|j,j\rangle,{}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}||\zeta\rangle.

(61)

To find the action of the raising operator, we employ (
60
)

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{+}||\zeta\rangle

\displaystyle=\hat{J}_{+}e^{\zeta\hat{J}_{-}}|j,j\rangle,{}

\displaystyle=e^{\zeta\hat{J}_{-}}\left(\hat{J}_{+}+2\zeta\hat{J}_{z}-\zeta^{2}\hat{J}_{-}\right)|j,j\rangle,{}

\displaystyle=\left(2\zeta j-\zeta^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\right)||\zeta\rangle.

(62)

In a similar fashion we employ (
58
) to find the action of
\hat{J}_{z}
,

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{z}||\zeta\rangle

\displaystyle=\hat{J}_{z}e^{\zeta\hat{J}_{-}}|j,j\rangle,{}

\displaystyle=e^{\zeta\hat{J}_{-}}\left(\hat{J}_{z}-\zeta\hat{J}_{-}\right)|j,j\rangle,{}

\displaystyle=\left(j-\zeta\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\right)||\zeta\rangle.

(63)

With the above the expectation values of these three operators in normalised coherent states may be found. Defining the function

\displaystyle M(\zeta,\zeta^{*})=\langle\zeta||||\zeta\rangle=(1+\zeta\zeta^{*})^{2j},

(64)

the expectation value of
\hat{J}_{-}
may be expressed as

\displaystyle\langle\hat{J}_{-}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\zeta||\hat{J}_{-}||\zeta\rangle}{M(\zeta,\zeta^{*})},{}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\zeta||\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}||\zeta\rangle}{M(\zeta,\zeta^{*})},{}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\frac{\partial M}{\partial\zeta}{},

\displaystyle=\frac{2j\zeta^{*}}{1+|\zeta|^{2}},

(65)

where we may move the partial derivative to the left of the bra state as this is only a function of
\zeta^{*}
. With similar reasoning, or by taking the complex conjugate, we find the expectation of the raising operator

\displaystyle\langle\hat{J}_{+}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{2j\zeta}{1+|\zeta|^{2}}.

(66)

The expectation value of
\hat{J}_{z}
is found as

\displaystyle\langle\hat{J}_{z}\rangle

\displaystyle=j\frac{1-|\zeta|^{2}}{1+|\zeta|^{2}}.

(67)

Noting that

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{x}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{J}_{+}+\hat{J}_{-}\right),\quad\hat{J}_{y}=\frac{1}{2\rmi}\left(\hat{J}_{+}-\hat{J}_{-}\right),

(68)

from (
61
) and (
62
) the action of
\hat{J}_{x}
and
\hat{J}_{y}
on an unnormalised coherent state is found to be

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{x}||\zeta\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(2\zeta j+(1-\zeta^{2})\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\right)||\zeta\rangle,

(69)

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{y}||\zeta\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\rmi}\left(2\zeta j-(1+\zeta^{2})\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\right)||\zeta\rangle,

(70)

and from (
65
) and (
66
) their expectation values in coherent states may be found as

\displaystyle\langle\hat{J}_{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{j(\zeta+\zeta^{*})}{1+|\zeta|^{2}},

(71)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{J}_{y}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{j(\zeta-\zeta^{*})}{\rmi(1+|\zeta|^{2})}.

(72)

For an arbitrary operator
\hat{A}
, we may use the action of the spin operators to find the coherent state expectation values of
A\hat{J}_{k}
and
\hat{J}_{k}A
, where
k=x,y,z,+,-
. These can be concisely summarised as

\displaystyle\langle\hat{A}\hat{J}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{J}_{k}\rangle\langle\hat{A}\rangle+\frac{(1+|\zeta|^{2})^{2}}{2j}\frac{\partial\langle\hat{J}_{k}\rangle}{\partial\zeta}\frac{\partial\langle\hat{A}\rangle}{\partial\zeta^{*}},

(73)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{J}_{k}\hat{A}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{J}_{k}\rangle\langle\hat{A}\rangle+\frac{(1+|\zeta|^{2})^{2}}{2j}\frac{\partial\langle\hat{J}_{k}\rangle}{\partial\zeta^{*}}\frac{\partial\langle\hat{A}\rangle}{\partial\zeta}

(74)

Appendix B
Derivation of the SCS-TDVP Equations

In this appendix we provide a derivation for the equations of motions for the parameters in the SCS-TDVP aaproximation.

We consider an initially coherent state
|\psi_{0}\rangle=c_{0}|\zeta_{0}\rangle
,
and make the ansatz

\displaystyle|\psi(t)\rangle=c(t)|\zeta(t)\rangle\text{ such that }|\psi(0)\rangle=c_{0}|\zeta_{0}\rangle

(75)

for the solution of the time-dependent Schrödinger equation.

The dynamical equations for the parameters can be derived via the Dirac-Frenkel variational principle
[
23
,
40
,
50
]
, by demanding that

\displaystyle\rmi\langle\delta\psi|\dot{\psi}\rangle=\langle\delta\psi|\hat{H}|\psi\rangle,

(76)

where
|\psi(t)\rangle
is of the from (
75
), and the variation
\langle\delta\psi|
is given by

\langle\delta\psi|=\delta c^{*}\frac{\partial\langle\psi|}{\partial c^{*}}+\delta\zeta\frac{\langle\psi|}{\partial\zeta}+\delta\zeta^{*}\frac{\langle\psi|}{\partial\zeta^{*}}.

(77)

Using the coherent-state equalities

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\langle\zeta|}{\partial\zeta}

\displaystyle=-\frac{j\zeta^{*}}{1+|\zeta|^{2}}\langle\zeta|

(78)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\langle\zeta|}{\partial\zeta^{*}}

\displaystyle=\langle\zeta|\Big[-\frac{j\zeta}{1+|\zeta|^{2}}+\hat{J}_{+}\Big]

(79)

this can be expressed as

\displaystyle\langle\delta\psi|

\displaystyle=\langle\zeta|\Big[\delta c^{*}-c^{*}\delta\zeta\frac{j\zeta^{*}}{1+|\zeta|^{2}}+c^{*}\delta\zeta^{*}\Big(-\frac{j\zeta}{1+|\zeta|^{2}}+\hat{J}_{+}\Big)\Big].

(80)

Inserting (
80
) into (
76
) yields a linear equation for the variations of
c,\zeta
and
\zeta^{*}
. Since these variations are mutually independent
[
50
]
, we may equate coefficients. The
\delta c
and
\delta\zeta
terms yield the same equation

\displaystyle\rmi\langle\zeta|\dot{\psi}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\zeta|\hat{H}|\psi\rangle,

(81)

while the
\delta\zeta^{*}
terms leads to

\displaystyle\rmi c^{*}\langle\zeta|\hat{J}_{+}|\dot{\psi}\rangle

\displaystyle=c^{*}\langle\zeta|\hat{J}_{+}\hat{H}|\psi\rangle.

(82)

The time derivative of the ansatz state
|\psi\rangle
in terms of the parameters
c
,
\zeta
, and
\zeta^{*}
is given by

|\dot{\psi}\rangle=\dot{c}|\zeta\rangle+c\dot{\zeta}\frac{\partial|\zeta\rangle}{\partial\zeta}+c\dot{\zeta}^{*}\frac{\partial|\zeta\rangle}{\partial\zeta^{*}},

(83)

which, using

\displaystyle\frac{\partial|\zeta\rangle}{\partial\zeta^{*}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{j\zeta}{1+|\zeta|^{2}}|\zeta\rangle

(84)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial|\zeta\rangle}{\partial\zeta}

\displaystyle=\Big[-\frac{j\zeta^{*}}{1+|\zeta|^{2}}+\hat{J}_{-}\Big]|\zeta\rangle,

(85)

becomes

|\dot{\psi}\rangle=\Big[\dot{c}-\frac{jc}{1+|\zeta|^{2}}\Big(\zeta^{*}\dot{\zeta}+\zeta\dot{\zeta}^{*}\Big)+c\dot{\zeta}\hat{J}_{-}\Big]|\zeta\rangle.

(86)

Inserting this into the
\delta c
coefficient equation (
81
), some algebra yields

\rmi\dot{c}=c\langle\hat{H}\rangle+\rmi\frac{cj}{1+|\zeta|^{2}}\Big[\zeta\dot{\zeta}^{*}-\zeta^{*}\dot{\zeta}\Big],

(87)

where we have used the abbreviation
\langle\hat{H}\rangle=\langle\zeta|\hat{H}|\zeta\rangle
to denote the expectation value of the Hamiltonian in coherent states.

Turning to the
\delta\zeta
coefficient equation, by inserting (
86
) we find

\rmi c^{*}\Big[\dot{c}-\frac{jc}{1+|\zeta|^{2}}\Big(\zeta\dot{\zeta}^{*}+\zeta\dot{\zeta}^{*}\Big)\Big]\langle\hat{J}_{+}\rangle+\rmi cc^{*}\dot{\zeta}\langle\hat{J}_{+}\hat{J}_{-}\rangle=cc^{*}\langle\hat{J}_{+}\hat{H}\rangle.

(88)

With (
74

\rmi\dot{c}\frac{2j\zeta c^{*}}{1+|\zeta|^{2}}+\rmi\frac{2jcc^{*}}{[1+|\zeta|^{2}]^{2}}\big[j\zeta(\zeta^{*}\dot{\zeta}-\zeta\dot{\zeta}^{*})+\dot{\zeta}\big]=cc^{*}\Big[\frac{2j\zeta}{1+|\zeta|^{2}}\langle\hat{H}\rangle+\frac{\partial\langle\hat{h}\rangle}{\partial\zeta^{*}}\Big].

(89)

Inserting (

Appendix C
Saddle point approximation for the IMF propagator for pure interaction

The integrand of
g_{m}(A)
(equation (
49
)) is highly oscillatory, motivating the use of the saddle point approximation
[
3
]
. To begin we assume
m\neq\pm j
. Recalling that
\beta
does not scale with
j
, we may write
g_{m}(A)
in the form

\displaystyle g_{m}(A)=\frac{2j+1}{2^{2j+1}}\binom{2j}{j-m}\int^{1}_{-1}e^{j\Phi(z_{0})}\rmd z_{0},

(93)

where

\displaystyle\Phi(z_{0})=(1+B)\ln(1+z_{0})+(1-B)\ln(1-z_{0})+\rmi A(z_{0}-B)^{2}.

(94)

and we have defined
B=m/j\in(-1,1)
, which we assume to be fixed. The dominant contributions of the integrand come from the maxima of
\Phi(z_{0})
for real
z_{0}
which lie within the integration bounds. We find those by evaluating the derivatives

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(z_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1+B}{1+z_{0}}-\frac{1-B}{1-z_{0}}+2\rmi A(z_{0}-B),{}

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime\prime}(z_{0})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1+B}{(1+z_{0})^{2}}-\frac{1-B}{(1-z_{0})^{2}}+2\rmi A.{}

From these we find the real saddle to be
z^{*}=B
. The general form of the approximation will be
[
3
]

\displaystyle g_{m}(A)=\frac{2j+1}{2^{2j+1}}\binom{2j}{j-m}\sum_{\text{saddle points }z^{*}}e^{j\Phi(z^{*})}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{-j\Phi^{\prime\prime}(z^{*})}}\Big[1+O(1/j)\Big].

(95)

Evaluating the first and second derivatives at the saddle point and inserting them into this formula yields

\displaystyle g_{m}(A)=

\displaystyle\frac{2j+1}{2^{2j+1}}\binom{2j}{j-m}e^{j(1+B))\ln(1+B)+j(1-B)\ln(1-B)}{}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\pi(1+B)(1-B)}{j[1-\rmi A(1-B^{2})]}}\Big[1+O(1/j)\Big].

(96)

For large
j
, Stirling’s approximation may be used to show

\displaystyle\frac{2j+1}{2^{2j+1}}\binom{2j}{j-m}\approx\frac{j}{2^{2j}}\cdot\frac{e^{-j[(1-B)\ln(1-B)+(1+B)\ln(1+B)-2\ln 2]}}{\sqrt{\pi j(1-B)(1+B)}}

(97)

which leads to our function becoming approximately

\displaystyle g_{m}(A)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{j}{2^{2j}}\cdot\frac{e^{2j\ln 2}}{j\sqrt{1-\rmi A(1-B^{2})}}\Big[1+O(1/j)\Big],{}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\rmi A(1-B^{2})}}+O(1/j).

(98)

Recalling that IMF propagator matrix elements are

\displaystyle U_{n,m}^{IMF}=\delta_{nm}e^{-2\rmi\kappa tm^{2}/j}g_{m}(2t\kappa),

(99)

in the large
j
limit this is approximated by

\displaystyle U^{IMF}_{n,m}(t;\text{large }j)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{nm}e^{-2\rmi\kappa tm^{2}/j}}{\sqrt{1-2\rmi t\kappa(1-\frac{m^{2}}{j^{2}})}}.

(100)

In the case of
m=j
,
B=1
and the function reduces to

\displaystyle g_{j}(A)=\frac{2j+1}{2^{2j+1}}\int^{1}_{-1}e^{jf(z_{0})}\rmd z_{0},

(101)

with

\displaystyle f(z_{0})=2\ln(1+z_{0})+\rmi A(z_{0}-1)^{2}.

(102)

In this case there is no saddle on the real axis. The integrand vanishes at the lower integral limit,
z_{0}=-1
, and it’s real part is maximal at the upper integration limit,
z_{0}=1
. Thus, the integral can be approximated by a Laplace method
[
3
]
, where the leading contribution comes from the upper limit of the integral, and we find

\displaystyle g_{j}(A)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{j}{2^{2j}}\frac{e^{jf(1)}}{jf^{\prime}(1)},{}

\displaystyle=1.

(103)

The case where
m=-j
follows in a similar fashion and yields the same result. Thus the
m=\pm j
elements follow the formula (
100
) and are identical to the true matrix elements.

\displaystyle\langle j,m^{\prime}|\hat{T}^{j}_{l,k}|j,m\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{2l+1}{2j+1}}\,C_{jmlk}^{jm^{\prime}}\,,
