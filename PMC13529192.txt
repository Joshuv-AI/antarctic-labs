Causal relationships between oral-gut microbiome and bone neoplasm-related phenotypes
Authors: Qinyu Zou, Fengyu Sun, Wantong Xu, Dan Peng, Zhongbiao Jiang, Hongchun Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The human oral and gut microbiota are the 4 largest microbial communities in the body and play crucial roles in maintaining homeostasis and influencing disease. Observational studies have suggested links between these microbiota and bone neoplasm-related phenotypes, but establishing causality has been challenging due to confounding factors and reverse causality. We conducted a bidirectional, 2-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) study to investigate evidence consistent with a potential causal association between the saliva and gut microbiota and various bone neoplasm-related phenotypes. Genetic instruments for saliva and gut microbiota were sourced from large genome-wide association studies. Inverse variance weighted was the primary MR method, supplemented by 4 other MR techniques. Sensitivity analyses, including MR-Egger regression, were performed to assess pleiotropy and heterogeneity. In the forward MR analysis, Veillonella parvula from the saliva microbiota was associated with a decreased risk of bone and connective tissue neoplasms (β: −0.236, 95% CI: [−0.275, −0.197], P = 8.20E−33). MR analyses identified genetically predicted associations between several microbial taxa and bone neoplasm-related phenotypes. Reverse MR analyses showed that genetic liability to bone neoplasm-related phenotypes was associated with variation in the composition of the oral (e.g., Order Bacteroidales, Rothia mucilaginosa) and gut microbiota (e.g., Class Methanobacteria, Genus Eubacterium oxidoreducens group). Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings, as no statistical evidence of substantial heterogeneity or directional horizontal pleiotropy was detected. This study provides genetic evidence supporting a bidirectional causal relationship between specific saliva and gut microbiota and bone neoplasm-related phenotypes. Our findings identify several microbial taxa as potential candidates for future biomarker development and therapeutic investigation in bone neoplasm-related phenotypes. However, these genetically informed associations require further mechanistic, experimental, and prospective clinical validation before clinical application.

1. Introduction

The gut and oral microbiota are two of the largest and most diverse microbial communities in the human body.[1] Increasing evidence suggests that the microbiota plays an important role in our bodies, and it can maintain homeostasis. In the past 2 decades, the microbiota has attracted considerable research interest because of its important role in human health and disease. Pathological changes are associated with multiple inflammatory conditions, such as periodontal disease and inflammatory bowel disease. Moreover, alterations in the microbiota may contribute to disease progression through multiple biological mechanisms.[2] It is well established that the oral cavity and gut are the 2 largest microbial habitats, playing an important role in microbiome-associated diseases. Even though the oral cavity and gut are continuous regions connected through the gastrointestinal tract, the oral and gut microbiome profiles are well-segregated due to the oral-gut barrier. When the barrier is destroyed, certain oral microorganisms may translocate to the gastrointestinal tract or other body sites under specific physiological or pathological conditions. Therefore, oral-to-gut and gut-to-oral microbial transmissions can shape and/or reshape the microbial ecosystem in both habitats, eventually modulating the pathogenesis of disease.[3,4] For example, people with periodontitis often change the composition and diversity of both their gut and oral microbiota, and periodontitis can impact systemic health through 2 mechanisms: the circulation of subgingival microbiota and its components, as well as the ingestion of saliva microbiota in people with periodontitis.[5] What’s more, oral and gut microbiota are involved in arthritis pathogenesis. Microbiome composition and its functional implications have been associated with the prevention of bone loss and/or reducing fracture risk. Recent studies have found that the composition of oral and gut microbiota has altered in patients with rheumatoid arthritis and osteoarthritis.[6] Increasing evidence suggests that host–microbiome interactions influence immune homeostasis through modulation of inflammatory pathways, immune cell activity, and systemic immune responses. Recent studies have further highlighted the importance of integrating genetic approaches, including Mendelian randomization (MR), to investigate potential genetically predicted associations between microbiome-derived factors, immune regulation, and disease susceptibility.[5,7,8]

However, the relationship between oral and gut microbiota and bone neoplasm-related phenotypes remains unclear. Several observational studies have attempted to clarify these connections, yet the findings may be susceptible to reverse causality and confounding variables, thereby complicating the causal determination. Nowadays, MR has been used as a prevalent methodological tool to analyze epidemiology by employing genetic instrumental variables (IVs) to assess the exposure-outcome evidence consistent with a potential causal association.[9] There is a random distribution of parental alleles to the progeny during the formation of meiotic gametes.[10] Therefore, although MR strengthens causal inference, it cannot completely eliminate all sources of bias or establish the underlying biological mechanisms.[11] In addition, the IVs used in MR analyses are required to fulfill the following assumptions[9]: the independence assumption; the relevance assumption; and the exclusivity assumption.

It will be difficult to uncover relationships between oral and gut microbiota and bone neoplasm-related phenotypes by clinical trials, and clinical translation remains challenging because prospective validation and mechanistic studies are still lacking. Therefore, this study pioneers a 2-sample MR approach, using relevant genome-wide association studies (GWAS) data from a large European population, to examine the evidence consistent with a potential causal association between the composition of oral and gut microbiota and types of bone neoplasm-related phenotypes. This study aimed to investigate genetically predicted associations between the oral and gut microbiota and bone neoplasm-related phenotypes using bidirectional MR. The findings may provide a foundation for future mechanistic investigations and translational research but should not be interpreted as establishing clinically applicable biomarkers or therapeutic targets.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data sources

We used data on genetic variants associated with univariate and multivariate traits of the saliva microbiota from a recent GWAS meta-analysis containing 610 unrelated adults from the Danish ADDITIONPRO cohort.[12] The ADDITION-PRO study (2009–2011) is a risk-stratified cohort designed to explore type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease risks and their underlying mechanisms among Danish adults, and it is a subsidiary study of the Danish arm of the ADDITION trial (baseline: 2001–2006), which assessed the effect of intensive multifactorial cardiometabolic treatment on incident cardiovascular events in people with screen-detected diabetes.[12] This cohort consists of older middle-aged individuals at various levels of risk of developing type 2 diabetes.[12] About 610 participants in total with information about the oral microbiota and genotype data were included in the analysis, and the association between 43 univariate and 1 multivariate oral bacterial feature and 5195,096 and 5374,467 human autosomal variants was tested, respectively.[12]

In addition, we use genotyping data associated with gut microbiome from 18,340 participants from 24 cohorts from the USA, Canada, Israel, South Korea, Germany, Denmark, the Netherlands, Belgium, Sweden, Finland, and the UK.[13] But microbial composition showed high variability across cohorts: only 9 out of 410 genera were detected in more than 95% of samples.[13] The core microbiota comprises 9 genera, of which 7 were previously identified as such, plus the genera Ruminococcus and Lachnoclostridium.[13] Of these 9 genera, the most abundant genus was Bacteroides, followed by Faecalibacterium, Blautia, and Alistipes.[13]

2.2. Instrumental variables

Instrumental variables (IVs) for both the oral and gut microbiome datasets were selected using a uniform suggestive significance threshold of P < 1.0 × 10−5. This threshold was consistently applied in all forward and reverse MR analyses to ensure methodological consistency and sufficient instrument availability. Then, F-statistics were calculated to evaluate the strength of the selected single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), with IVs considered robust when F-statistics exceeded 10. Independent SNPs were selected through linkage disequilibrium clumping (r2 < 0.001). Potential confounder-associated SNPs were identified through systematic screening using the PhenoScanner database (version 2). SNPs significantly associated with predefined confounding traits (P < 1 × 10−5) were manually reviewed and excluded before data harmonization.

2.3. Statistical analysis

We conducted genetic association studies to evaluate the relationships between saliva microbiota and gut microbiota, and bone neoplasm-related phenotypes. Each GWAS-derived phenotype was analyzed independently as a separate outcome rather than being combined into a single aggregated phenotype. Our approach included a comprehensive MR analysis utilizing 5 distinct methods: inverse variance weighted (IVW; MRE), MR-Egger, Weighted median, Simple mode, and Weighted mode. In this work, the primary method employed for MR analysis was IVW.[15] IVW is widely regarded for its robust statistical power compared to other MR methodologies, which presupposes the validity of all IVs and their complete independence from one another.[15] IVW aggregates the Wald ratio estimates from multiple SNPs, applying the inverse of their approximate variances as weights, and does not include an intercept term in its regression model.[15] In the absence of horizontal pleiotropy, the IVW approach employing properly selected IVs yields the most reliable causal estimates in MR analyses.[15] The workflow of our research is illustrated in Figure 1. We also executed sensitivity analyses to detect any underlying heterogeneity or pleiotropic biases, and reverse MR studies were also conducted.

3. Results

3.1. Selection of IVs

First, at both suggestive thresholds P < 1.0 × 10−5,[15] ultimate IVs applied for clumping and harmonization in forward/reverse MR analysis were identified in Tables S1.1, S1.2, Supplemental Digital Content 1 and Tables S2.1, S2.2, Supplemental Digital Content 2. We further assessed the strength of each instrumental variable using F-statistics. All exposures demonstrated F-statistics <10, which meets the conventional threshold for strong instruments in MR studies and minimizes the risk of weak instrument bias.

3.2. The bidirectional causal relationships between saliva microbiota and bone neoplasm-related phenotypes

Full results of forward MR analyses were displayed in Table S3.1, Supplemental Digital Content 3. The final included MR results between each pair of exposure and outcome met the criteria of a significant PIVW value (<[0.05/(43 × 4) = 2.91E−04]) and the agreement of plus or minus signs of β-values of 5 MR approaches, alongside no statistical evidence of directional horizontal pleiotropy. As displayed in Table 1 and Figure 2A, MR analyses provided genetically supported evidence consistent with a potential protective association between genetically predicted Veillonella parvula abundance and bone neoplasm-related phenotypes (β: −0.236, 95% confidence interval [CI]: [−0.275, −0.197], P = 8.20E−33).

3.3. The bidirectional causal relationships between gut microbiota and bone neoplasm-related phenotypes

Full results of forward MR analyses were displayed in Table S3.2, Supplemental Digital Content 3. The final included MR results between each pair of exposure and outcome met the criteria of a significant PIVW value (<[0.05/(196 × 4) = 6.37E−05]) and the agreement of plus or minus signs of β-values of 5 MR approaches, alongside no statistical evidence of directional horizontal pleiotropy. As displayed in Table 3 and Figure 2B, in forward MR analyses, we identified evidence consistent with a potential causal association between gut microbiota and multiple bone neoplasm-related phenotypes: Genus Bilophila to bone and connective tissue neoplasm (β: −0.993, 95% CI: [−1.400, −0.585], P = 1.80E−06), Genus Enterorhabdus to malignant bone neoplasm (β: 1.265, 95% CI: [0.721, 1.808], P = 5.10E−06), Family Bacteroidaceae to secondary malignant neoplasm (β: −0.396, 95% CI: [−0.590, −0.202], P = 6.10E−05), Genus Bacteroides to secondary malignant neoplasm (β: −0.396, 95% CI: [−0.590, −0.202], P = 6.10E−05) and Genus Lachnospiraceae ND3007 group to secondary malignant neoplasm (β: −0.376, 95% CI: [−0.463,-0.290], P = 1.30E−17).

Full results of reverse MR analyses were shown in Table S4.2, Supplemental Digital Content 4. In Tables S5.3 and S5.4, Supplemental Digital Content 5, there was no positive result of reverse MR estimates for a significant association between gut microbiota and bone neoplasm-related phenotypes.

4. Discussion

This study employed bidirectional MR to systematically investigate causal relationships among the saliva microbiota, gut microbiota, and various bone neoplasm-related phenotypes. These results support the possibility of genetically predicted associations that warrant further mechanistic and experimental validation. These findings not only provide genetic evidence for the role of microbiota in the pathogenesis of bone neoplasm-related phenotypes but also suggest that oral and gut microbes may influence bone neoplasm-related phenotype development through shared biological mechanisms.

To further explore these relationships, we conducted reverse MR analyses to assess whether genetic liability to bone neoplasm-related phenotypes is associated with variations in oral and gut microbiota composition. The results suggest that host genetic predisposition to bone neoplasm-related phenotypes is associated with alterations in microbial composition, supporting a bidirectional host–microbiome relationship.[13] This bidirectional pattern is consistent with previous MR studies demonstrating that both microbiome and host traits may influence each other, rather than operating in a strictly unidirectional manner.[17] Because reverse MR evaluates genetic liability rather than the presence of active disease, disease progression, or treatment effects, the underlying mechanisms remain highly speculative. One speculative hypothesis is that these genetic predispositions might be linked to underlying pathways – such as baseline immune dysregulation or inflammatory tendencies – that parallel tumor-associated systemic effects known to reshape microbial ecological structures.[18] Importantly, MR analyses are subject to limitations such as horizontal pleiotropy and cannot fully disentangle feedback loops or temporal dynamics between exposures and outcomes.[19]

Under conditions of impaired oral mucosal barrier function, opportunistic pathogens such as Fusobacterium nucleatum and Porphyromonas gingivalis can translocate to bone tissue via the digestive tract or hematogenous routes. Studies have shown that Fusobacterium nucleatum may activate the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway[20] through its virulence factor Fap2 protein (a known adhesin that binds to Gal-GalNAc),[21] leading to inflammatory bone resorption and osteoclast differentiation.[22] Additionally, gingipains produced by P gingivalis can degrade the extracellular matrix, enhancing the invasive capacity of tumor cells and creating conditions for their colonization in the bone microenvironment.[23] Also, oral pathogens can induce the host to produce a large number of inflammatory factors, creating a microenvironment conducive to tumor development. Simultaneously, IL-6 can induce VEGF expression, promoting tumor angiogenesis and providing a blood supply, which may support bone metastasis.[24]

Gut microbiota may influence the pathogenesis and progression of bone neoplasm-related phenotypes. Gut microbiota regulate the enterohepatic circulation of estrogen by expressing β-glucuronidase, influencing systemic estrogen levels.[25] Estrogen deficiency (OVX) has been shown to fuel bone metastases, since the declining production of the sex steroid by the ovaries and inflammatory tone associated with estrogen deficiency modify the bone microenvironment, mainly the osteoclasts, in such a way that cancer cell anchorage, survival, and osteolytic phenotype are stimulated.[26]

Recent studies suggest that close bidirectional communication exists between oral and gut microbiota, forming an “oral-gut-bone” regulatory axis. Oral microbiota such as Streptococcus spp. and Veillonella spp. can reach the gut via swallowing and hematogenous routes, altering gut microbial composition.[27] These microbiota can compete for nutrients, produce antimicrobial peptides, and alter local pH, thereby affecting the ecological balance of the gut microbiota.[28] Similarly, gut microbial metabolites can also influence oral microbial composition via the circulatory system, forming a bidirectional regulatory network. As key components of the mucosal immune system, the oral cavity and gut influence each other through shared mucosal immune mechanisms. Regulatory T cells (Tregs) induced by gut microbiota can migrate to the oral mucosa, suppressing local inflammatory responses.[29] Conversely, Th17 cells activated by oral microbiota can also reach the gut, enhancing mucosal barrier function. This cross-migration of immune cells provides a mechanistic basis for explaining how microbiota remotely regulate the development of bone neoplasm-related phenotypes. Gut microbiota can influence the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis via the gut–brain axis, modulating glucocorticoid levels.[30] Glucocorticoids can inhibit osteoblast activity and promote osteoporosis, facilitating the bone metastasis of tumor cells.

Veillonella parvula, which was associated with a reduced risk of bone neoplasm-related phenotypes in our study, is a lactate-utilizing commensal bacterium widely present in the oral cavity. By consuming lactate produced by other oral streptococci, Veillonella may contribute to metabolic homeostasis and reduce local acidification and inflammatory amplification within mucosal environments.[31] This ecological function may indirectly attenuate systemic inflammatory signaling and osteoclast activation, thereby exerting a potential protective effect on bone microenvironments. In contrast, Bilophila wadsworthia has been shown to expand under bile acid–rich conditions and is strongly associated with pro-inflammatory immune responses, particularly Th1 polarization and increased production of inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6.[32,33] These pathways are directly implicated in osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption, suggesting a plausible mechanism through which Bilophila may contribute to bone tumor-promoting microenvironments. Similarly, Enterorhabdus has been increasingly reported as a genus associated with gut dysbiosis and metabolic inflammation, although its functional role remains less well characterized.[34] Emerging evidence suggests that Enterorhabdus abundance correlates with disrupted short-chain fatty acid metabolism and impaired intestinal barrier integrity, which may facilitate systemic immune activation and indirectly influence bone remodeling processes. Collectively, these taxa-specific interpretations support the hypothesis that microbial dysbiosis may contribute to bone neoplasm-related phenotypes susceptibility through convergent inflammatory and immune-mediated pathways, rather than tumor-type-specific mechanisms.

Microbes such as Veillonella parvula in saliva and Bilophila wadsworthia in the gut may represent potential candidates for future investigation as biomarkers or therapeutic targets in bone neoplasm-related phenotypes. While microbiota-based biomarkers might theoretically offer advantages such as non-invasiveness, their actual reproducibility and diagnostic utility in bone neoplasm-related phenotypes remain a general hypothesis requiring robust disease-specific clinical validation.[35] For high-risk individuals, modulation of the microbiota represents a potential avenue for future investigation; however, whether such interventions can influence the development or progression of bone neoplasm-related phenotypes remains unknown and requires rigorous experimental and clinical validation. Although theoretical interventions such as microbiota modulation have been explored in other conditions,[36-38] their applicability to bone neoplasm-related phenotypes is currently unsupported by direct evidence. Whether modulating the microbiome could ever serve as an adjuvant approach remains a speculative, general hypothesis that strictly mandates extensive experimental and disease-specific clinical validation. However, it remains unclear whether microbial alterations precede disease onset or reflect systemic consequences of tumor development.

From a clinical perspective, our findings provide potential directions for future microbiome-based research in bone neoplasm-related phenotypes. The identified microbial taxa may serve as candidates for further investigation in biomarker development or therapeutic exploration. However, whether these microbial alterations represent early contributors to tumor initiation or secondary consequences of disease remains unclear. Therefore, prospective longitudinal studies integrating microbiome profiling with clinical outcomes are required to validate their predictive value. Future studies combining microbiome sequencing, host genomics, metabolomics, and immune profiling may further elucidate the biological pathways linking microbial taxa associated with bone neoplasm development. In addition, experimental models will be necessary to determine whether targeted modulation of specific microbial taxa can influence tumor-related immune responses or disease progression. Thus, our findings should be considered a foundation for hypothesis generation and future translational investigation rather than immediate clinical application.

This study has several limitations. A limitation of our study is the predominant reliance on GWAS datasets derived from individuals of European ancestry. While we selected exposure and outcome datasets with matching ancestral backgrounds to minimize bias arising from population stratification, ancestry mismatch, and differences in linkage disequilibrium structures, this approach inevitably restricts the generalizability of our findings. Microbiome composition and host–microbiome interactions vary substantially across populations due to differences in ethnicity, geography, and diet, which may alter the strength and direction of microbe-tumor associations.[39] Because sufficiently powered microbiome GWAS datasets for non-European populations remain currently limited, future replication in larger and ancestrally diverse GWAS resources is essential to confirm these findings and establish their broader applicability. In addition, although we implemented strict instrumental variable selection procedures, and all genetic instruments demonstrated adequate strength (F-statistics > 10), some microbial traits were instrumented by a limited number of SNPs, which may reduce robustness and increase susceptibility to residual weak instrument bias. Furthermore, while multiple sensitivity analyses (including MR-Egger regression, weighted median, and heterogeneity tests) were conducted, horizontal pleiotropy cannot be completely excluded and may still influence causal estimates.

In addition, due to the nature of summary-level GWAS data, stratified analyses by histological subtype (e.g., osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, or metastatic bone disease origin) were not available in the current study. Current microbial data are largely limited to broad taxonomic ranks, such as the genus and order levels (e.g., Fusobacteriales), lacking strain-level information. Functional differences between strains may be significant, and this resolution limitation could obscure important biological associations.[40] While MR analysis can infer causality, it cannot reveal specific biological mechanisms. Subsequent experimental studies are needed to validate how these microbes influence bone neoplasm-related phenotype pathogenesis through molecular mechanisms.[41] Future research should integrate multi-omics approaches and experimental models to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms and validate the translational applicability of microbiota-based strategies in the prevention and management of bone neoplasm-related phenotypes.

Future research should focus on the following areas: combining metagenomic, metabolomic, and transcriptomic data to comprehensively analyze the functional pathways through which microbiota influence bone neoplasm-related phenotypes[42]; and establishing humanized mouse models to validate the functions of specific microbes and their metabolites in bone neoplasm-related phenotype pathogenesis.[43]

5. Conclusion

This bidirectional MR study provides genetic evidence consistent with a potential causal association between specific oral and gut microbiota and bone neoplasm-related phenotypes. Key microbial features, such as saliva Veillonella parvula and gut genera Bilophila and Enterorhabdus, were significantly associated with bone neoplasm-related phenotype risk. Overall, this study provides genetically supported evidence consistent with potential causal associations between specific oral and gut microbial taxa and bone neoplasm-related phenotypes. Further mechanistic studies, experimental validation, and prospective clinical investigations are required to determine their biological significance and translational potential.
