1.
Introduction

The representation of integers as sums of three squares has a long history. Legendre (1797) and Gauss (1796–1801) independently showed that an integer
m
can be expressed as a sum of three non-negative squares if and only if
m
is not of the form
4^{k}(8\ell+7)\ (k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\dots\})
. Moreover, Gauss gave an explicit formula for the representation number

r_{3}(m)=\#\{(x,y,z)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{3}:m=x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}\}

in terms of Hurwitz class numbers.
By Siegel’s lower bound for the class numbers of imaginary quadratic fields
[
20
]
, it follows that for any
\epsilon>0,

r_{3}(m)\gg m^{1/2-\epsilon}.

A well-known conjecture related to the three-squares theorem is that every positive integer
n
satisfying

\displaystyle n\equiv 3\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 24)\quad\text{and}\quad 5\nmid n

(1.1)

can be represented as a sum of three squares of primes. Towards this conjecture, Blomer and Brüdern
[
3
]
established that every sufficiently large integer
n
satisfying (1.1) can be written as the sum of three squares of
P_{r},
where

r=\begin{cases}371,&\text{if}\ n\text{ is square-free},\\
521,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This result has been improved by various authors
[
2
,
15
,
6
,
21
]
.

In the present paper, we prove the following two theorems.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n
be a sufficiently large integer satisfying condition
(\ref{e35})
.
Then
n
can be represented as

n=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2},

where
x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}
is a
P_{67}
-number. In fact, the number of such representations is
\gg n^{1/2-\varepsilon}
for any
\varepsilon>0
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
n
be a sufficiently large integer satisfying condition
(\ref{e35})
.
Then
n
can be represented as

n=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2},

where each
x_{i}
is a
P_{27}
-number for
1\leq i\leq 3
. More precisely, the number of such representations is
\gg n^{1/2-\varepsilon}
for any
\varepsilon>0
.

Theorem
1.1
improves on a result of Waibel
[
21
]
, where
P_{72}
was obtained in place of
P_{67}
. Theorem
1.2
is obtained from the same weighted-sieve argument, but the final almost-prime estimate is applied to each coordinate separately. The proof combines the three-dimensional sieve, a Richert-type weighted sieve method introduced by Cai
[
6
]
with the level-of-distribution estimate arising from Waibel’s work on ternary quadratic forms.

We next discuss a related application of the same method to the conjectures of Sun. Sun proposed the following two conjectures in
[
18
]
and
[
17
]
respectively.

Conjecture 1.1
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. If
n\equiv 10\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 24)
, then
n
can be expressed as

n=x^{2}+y^{2}+(2^{a}3^{b})^{2},

where
x,y,a,b
are non-negative integers and
b>0
. If
n\equiv 5\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 12)
, then
n
can be expressed as

n=x^{2}+y^{2}+(2^{a}5^{b})^{2},

where
x,y,a,b
are non-negative integers and
a>0
.

Conjecture 1.2
.

Every integer
n\geq 2
can be expressed as

n=x^{2}+y^{2}+(2^{a}3^{b})^{2}+(2^{c}5^{d})^{2},

where
x,y,a,b,c,d
are non-negative integers.

Banerjee
[
1
]
showed that every sufficiently large integer
m
not of the form
4^{k}(8\ell+7)
for any
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
can be represented as
m=x^{2}+y^{2}+(2^{a}z)^{2},
such that
z
is a
P_{118}
and
x,y,a\in\mathbb{N}
. Using Waibel’s diagonal norm estimate
[
21
, Theorem 2]
together with a one-dimensional weighted sieve, we improve this related result as follows.

Theorem 1.3
.

Every sufficiently large integer
m
not of the form
4^{k}(8\ell+7)
for any
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
can be represented in the form

m=x^{2}+y^{2}+(2^{a}z)^{2},

where
x,y,a,z
are non-negative integers and
z
is a
P_{18}
-number. Moreover, the number of such representations is
\gg m^{1/2-\varepsilon}
for any
\varepsilon>0
.

Corollary 1.1
.

Every sufficiently large integer
m
can be represented in the form

m=x^{2}+y^{2}+2^{2a}+(2^{b}z)^{2},

where
x,y,a,b
are non-negative integers and
z
is a
P_{18}
-number.

Proof.

Write
m=4^{k}m^{\prime}
with
4\nmid m^{\prime}
. If
m^{\prime}=1
, then
k>1
for all sufficiently large
m
, and hence

m=(2^{k-1})^{2}+(2^{k-1})^{2}+(2^{k-1})^{2}+(2^{k-1})^{2},

which has the required form. If
m^{\prime}>1
, then one can choose
a\in\{0,1\}
such that

m^{\prime}-4^{a}\not\equiv 0,4,7\ ({\rm{mod}}\ 8)\qquad\text{and}\qquad m^{\prime}-4^{a}>0.

For sufficiently large

, the integer
4^{k}(m^{\prime}-4^{a})
is sufficiently large and is not of the form
4^{u}(8v+7)
. The result follows by applying Theorem
1.3
to
4^{k}(m^{\prime}-4^{a})
.
∎

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we collect the required estimates for theta series, the level of distribution supplied by Waibel’s work, and the sieve lemmas used later. In Section 3 we apply a Richert-type weighted sieve in dimensions three and one. The three-dimensional argument proves Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, while the one-dimensional argument proves Theorem
1.3
.

Notation and auxiliary lemmas

In this paper,

denotes a prime number.

Bold lowercase letters denote three-dimensional horizontal vectors, and their components are represented by the same letter with subscripts. For example,

To successfully apply Waibel’s mean-value result
[

Let

Q=\begin{pmatrix}2\ell_{1}^{2}&0&0\\

where
\ell_{i}

For an integer

[
