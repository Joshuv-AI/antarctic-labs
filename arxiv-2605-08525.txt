Model-Reference Adaptive Flight Control of the 𝟗𝟓 \boldsymbol{95} -mg Bee ++
Francisco M. F. R. Gonçalves, Conor K. Trygstad, Néstor O. Pérez-Arancibia

Abstract
We introduce a model-reference adaptive control (MRAC) architecture for high-performance positional tracking of the Bee ++ —a 𝟗𝟓 \boldsymbol{95} -mg insect-scale flapping-wing aerial vehicle. The suitability, functionality, and high performance of the proposed approach are demonstrated using data from real-time flight experiments.



I
Motivation

The
95
-mg

Bee
++
is shown in
Fig.
1
. This robot is driven by four unimorph piezoelectric actuators powered via a tether wire
[
3
]
. Due to the system’s scale and complex fabrication, the model describing the associated dynamics is subject to parameter uncertainty, mainly in the inertia matrix and the actuator mapping of the flyer. Furthermore, due to its low inertia, the
Bee
++
is greatly affected by stochastic and systematic disturbances during flight, including
power-wire
tension, gusts, and undesired aerodynamic forces produced by wing misalignment. Therefore, the implementation of advanced
attitude-control
methods for the execution of
high-speed
maneuvers—such as those in
[
1
]
and references therein—first requires the
Bee
++
to adapt its behavior to counteract disturbances and model uncertainty. As a step toward this objective, in this abstract, we introduce a
model-reference adaptive control
(MRAC) framework for
high-performance
position control of
flapping-wing
robotic insects that can be modeled as rigid bodies in the 3D space.

II
Adaptation and Stability Analysis

Using Newton’s second law and assuming a rigid body, the translational dynamics of the robot moving in the 3D space can be described as

\displaystyle m\boldsymbol{\ddot{r}}(t)=\boldsymbol{f}(t)-mg\boldsymbol{n}_{3}+\boldsymbol{d}(t),

(1)

where
m
is the mass of the robot;
\boldsymbol{r}=[r_{1}~r_{2}~r_{3}]^{T}
is the position of the robot’s
center of mass
(CoM) relative to
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}=\left\{\boldsymbol{n}_{1},\boldsymbol{n}_{2},\boldsymbol{n}_{3}\right\}
, as defined in
Fig.
1
;
\boldsymbol{f}
is the total force generated by the flapping wings of the robot;
g
is the acceleration of gravity; and,
\boldsymbol{d}
is the unknown aggregated disturbance affecting the flyer. We assume that this disturbance can be modeled as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{d}(t)=\boldsymbol{W}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{W}
is a constant
n\times 3
matrix of unknown parameters and
\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})
is a vector of
Gaussian-based

radial basis functions
(RBFs), in which the
i
th element is given by

\displaystyle\phi_{i}=\text{exp}\left(\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{c}_{i}\|^{2}_{2}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right),~~i\in\left\{1,2,\cdots,n\right\},

(3)

with
\boldsymbol{x}=[\boldsymbol{r}^{T}~\boldsymbol{\dot{r}}^{T}]^{T}
. The real vector
\boldsymbol{c}_{i}
and the real number
\sigma_{i}
define the center and bandwidth of
\phi_{i}
, respectively.

.
This
insect-scale

flapping-wing
aerial robot weighs
95
mg
and is driven by four unimorph piezoelectric actuators.
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}=\left\{\boldsymbol{n}_{1},\boldsymbol{n}_{2},\boldsymbol{n}_{3}\right\}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=\left\{\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\boldsymbol{b}_{2},\boldsymbol{b}_{3}\right\}
respectively are the inertial and
body-fixed
frames of reference used for kinematic modeling.

By design, the Bee
++
can only generate a force with magnitude
f(t)
in the
\boldsymbol{b}_{3}
direction, as defined in
Fig.
1
; however, similarly to the case presented in
[
3
]
, for the purpose of stability analysis, we assume that the attitude controller of the system is fast enough such that
f\boldsymbol{b}_{3}\approx\boldsymbol{f}
. Thus, for a given desired state
\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{d}}
, we define the tracking error to be minimized as
\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{e}}=\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{d}}-\boldsymbol{x}
and the corresponding stabilizing control law as

\displaystyle\hskip-7.74997pt\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{K}_{\text{p}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\text{e}}+\boldsymbol{K}_{\text{i}}\int_{0}^{t}\boldsymbol{r}_{\text{e}}(\zeta)d\zeta+\boldsymbol{K}_{\text{d}}\boldsymbol{\accentset{\scalebox{1.4}{$\mbox{.}$}}{r}}_{\text{e}}+m\boldsymbol{\ddot{r}}_{\text{d}}+mg\boldsymbol{n}_{3}-\boldsymbol{f}_{\text{a}},

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{K}_{\text{p}}
,
\boldsymbol{K}_{\text{i}}
, and
\boldsymbol{K}_{\text{d}}
are diagonal positive definite matrices, chosen as specified in
[
3
]
;
\boldsymbol{r}_{\text{e}}=\boldsymbol{r}_{\text{d}}-\boldsymbol{r}
is the position error, with
\boldsymbol{r}_{\text{d}}
smooth and bounded; and,
\boldsymbol{f}_{\text{a}}=\boldsymbol{\hat{W}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})
is an adaptive term included with the objective of canceling the unknown disturbance affecting the system. In this approach, an adaptive law updates
\boldsymbol{\hat{W}}
such that the linear combination of Gaussian kernels matches the unknown disturbance to be canceled. By substituting (
4
) into (
1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\hskip-7.74997pt\boldsymbol{\ddot{r}}_{\text{e}}=-\frac{1}{m}\left[\boldsymbol{K}_{\text{p}}\boldsymbol{r}_{\text{e}}+\boldsymbol{K}_{\text{i}}\int_{0}^{t}\boldsymbol{r}_{\text{e}}(\zeta)d\zeta+\boldsymbol{K}_{\text{d}}\boldsymbol{\accentset{\scalebox{1.4}{$\mbox{.}$}}{r}}_{\text{e}}\right]-\frac{1}{m}\left(\boldsymbol{d}-\boldsymbol{f}_{\text{a}}\right),

(5)

which, by defining
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\int_{0}^{t}\boldsymbol{r}_{\text{e}}(\zeta)d\zeta
, can be written as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\dddot{\xi}}=-\frac{1}{m}\left[\boldsymbol{K}_{\text{p}}\boldsymbol{\dot{\xi}}+\boldsymbol{K}_{\text{i}}\boldsymbol{\xi}+\boldsymbol{K}_{\text{d}}\boldsymbol{\ddot{\xi}}\right]-\frac{1}{m}\left(\boldsymbol{d}-\boldsymbol{f}_{\text{a}}\right).

(6)

Furthermore, (
6
) can be put into the
state-space
form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\accentset{\scalebox{1.4}{$\mbox{.}$}}{z}}(t)&=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z}(t)+\boldsymbol{B}\left(\boldsymbol{d}-\boldsymbol{f}_{\text{a}}\right),\end{split}

(7)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{z}&=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{T}&\boldsymbol{\dot{\xi}}^{T}&\boldsymbol{\ddot{\xi}}^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T};\\[3.87498pt]
\boldsymbol{A}&=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}_{3\times 3}&\boldsymbol{I}_{3\times 3}&\boldsymbol{0}_{3\times 3}\\[3.87498pt]
\boldsymbol{0}_{3\times 3}&\boldsymbol{0}_{3\times 3}&\boldsymbol{I}_{3\times 3}\\[3.87498pt]
-\frac{1}{m}\boldsymbol{K}_{\text{i}}&-\frac{1}{m}\boldsymbol{K}_{\text{p}}&-\frac{1}{m}\boldsymbol{K}_{\text{d}}\end{bmatrix};\\[3.87498pt]
\boldsymbol{B}&=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}_{6\times 3}\\[3.87498pt]
-\frac{1}{m}\boldsymbol{I}_{3\times 3}\end{bmatrix}.\end{split}

Then, considering (
7
), we define a
zero-disturbance
reference model of the closed-loop system with the form

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\accentset{\scalebox{1.4}{$\mbox{.}$}}{z}}_{\text{r}}(t)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{r}}(t).

(8)

Thus, the
modeling-error
dynamics are given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\dot{\tilde{z}}}&=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}+\boldsymbol{B}\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{\text{a}}-\boldsymbol{d}\right)\\
&=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}+\boldsymbol{B}\left[\boldsymbol{\hat{W}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{W}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})\right]\\
&=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}+\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{\tilde{W}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x}).\end{split}

(9)

From (
9
), it follows that an effective adaptive method for rejecting the disturbances affecting the system must produce a matrix
\boldsymbol{\hat{W}}
such that
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\boldsymbol{z}(t)=\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{r}}(t)
. To this end, we first define the Lyapunov function candidate

\displaystyle V=\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}^{T}\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}+\gamma^{-1}\text{tr}\left\{\boldsymbol{\tilde{W}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\tilde{W}}\right\},

(10)

where
\boldsymbol{P}
is a positive definite matrix and
\gamma
is a positive real number. Then, taking the time derivative of
V
, we obtain

\displaystyle\hskip-7.74997pt\begin{split}\accentset{\scalebox{1.4}{$\mbox{.}$}}{V}&=\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}^{T}\left[\boldsymbol{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{P}+\boldsymbol{PA}\right]\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}+\boldsymbol{\phi}^{T}\boldsymbol{\tilde{W}}\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}+\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}^{T}\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{\tilde{W}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}\\
&\hskip 11.62495pt+2\gamma^{-1}\text{tr}\left\{\boldsymbol{\accentset{\scalebox{1.4}{$\mbox{.}$}}{\tilde{W}}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\tilde{W}}\right\}\\
&=-\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}^{T}\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}+2\text{tr}\left\{\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}\boldsymbol{\phi}^{T}\boldsymbol{\tilde{W}}\right\}+2\gamma^{-1}\text{tr}\left\{\boldsymbol{\accentset{\scalebox{1.4}{$\mbox{.}$}}{\tilde{W}}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\tilde{W}}\right\}\\
&=-\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}^{T}\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}+2\text{tr}\left\{\boldsymbol{B}^{T}\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}\boldsymbol{\phi}^{T}\boldsymbol{\tilde{W}}+\gamma^{-1}\boldsymbol{\accentset{\scalebox{1.4}{$\mbox{.}$}}{\tilde{W}}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\tilde{W}}\right\},\end{split}

(11)

where
\boldsymbol{Q}
is a positive definite matrix that satisfies the equation
\boldsymbol{A}^{T}\boldsymbol{P}+\boldsymbol{PA}=-\boldsymbol{Q}
. Accordingly, by choosing the adaptation law

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\dot{\hat{W}}}=\boldsymbol{\dot{\tilde{W}}}=-\gamma\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}^{T}\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{B},

(12)

we enforce that
\accentset{\scalebox{1.4}{$\mbox{.}$}}{V}=-\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}^{T}\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}\leq 0
, which implies that the equilibrium
\left\{\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}^{\star},\boldsymbol{\tilde{W}}^{\star}\right\}=\{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{0}\}
is uniformly stable in the sense of Lyapunov. Therefore,
\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
.

Next, using
signal-chasing
analysis, we show the attractivity of
\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}^{\star}
. First, note that since
V(t)\geq 0
and
\accentset{\scalebox{1.4}{$\mbox{.}$}}{V}(t)\leq 0
,
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{V(t)}=V_{\infty}<\infty
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}\accentset{\scalebox{1.4}{$\mbox{.}$}}{V}(t)dt&=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}^{T}\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}dt\\
\Rightarrow V_{\infty}-V(0)&=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}^{T}\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}dt\\
\Leftrightarrow V_{\infty}-V(0)&\leq-\lambda_{\text{min}}\left\{\boldsymbol{Q}\right\}\int_{0}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}dt\\
\Leftrightarrow V_{\infty}-V(0)&\leq-\lambda_{\text{min}}\left\{\boldsymbol{Q}\right\}\|\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}\\
\Leftrightarrow\|\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}&\leq\frac{V(0)-V_{\infty}}{\lambda_{\text{min}}\left\{\boldsymbol{Q}\right\}}\\
\Rightarrow\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}&\in\mathcal{L}_{2}.\end{split}

(13)

and
\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
, from (

), it follows that
\boldsymbol{\tilde{W}}(t)\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}

\boldsymbol{\hat{W}}(t)\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}

After having shown that all the signals on the right in (
9
) are bounded, we conclude that
\boldsymbol{\dot{\tilde{z}}}\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
. Thus, all the hypotheses of

in
[
2
]
are satisfied and, therefore, it follows that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{0}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{as}\hskip 9.24994ptt\rightarrow\infty.

(14)

As a consequence, from (
12
), it also follows that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\dot{\hat{W}}}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{0}_{n\times 3}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{as}\hskip 9.24994ptt\rightarrow\infty.

(15)

In summary, all the signals of the
closed-loop
system remain bounded during operation. Additionally, as intended,
\boldsymbol{z}
converges to
\boldsymbol{z}_{\text{r}}
asymptotically.

III
Experimental Results

To assess and demonstrate the performance of the proposed MRAC approach relative to that obtained with the benchmark controller presented in
[
3

back-to-back

\boldsymbol{r}_{\text{d}}=[0~0~10]^{T}

Fig.
2
, both control schemes can robustly stabilize the position of the Bee
++
during flight. However, a simple comparison of the trajectories shown in
Figs.
2
(a) and (b)

mean
\pm
experimental standard deviation
(ESD) of the

(RMS) position errors corresponding to the five performed flights are

cm
,
0.87\pm 0.39
cm
, and
0.13\pm 0.03
cm
along the
\boldsymbol{n}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{n}_{2}
, and
\boldsymbol{n}_{3}
directions, respectively. For comparison, the values corresponding to the benchmark controller are
2.74\pm 0.63
cm
,
1.54\pm 0.26
cm
, and
0.20\pm 0.02
cm
along the
\boldsymbol{n}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{n}_{2}
, and
\boldsymbol{n}_{3}

in the
