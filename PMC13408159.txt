Green spectrofluorimetric method using nitrogen–phosphorus carbon quantum dots and Box–Behnken optimized salting-out assisted extraction for valbenazine determination in environmental samples
Authors: Ahmed Serag, Manal E Alosaimi, Maram H Abduljabbar, Alaa O Baryyan, Abdulaziz Al Khzem, Mansour S Alturki, Mohammed F Aldawsari, Atiah H Almalki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA novel, sensitive, and sustainable spectrofluorimetric method was developed for valbenazine determination in spiked environmental water samples by integrating nitrogen and phosphorus co-doped carbon quantum dots (N,P-CQDs) as fluorescent nanoprobes with salting-out assisted liquid–liquid extraction (SALLE). The N,P-CQDs synthesized via microwave-assisted pyrolysis exhibited quasi-spherical morphology, high quantum yield, and strong blue–green fluorescence (excitation/emission: 355/441 nm, Stokes shift: 86 nm). Valbenazine induced fluorescence quenching consistent with a static mechanism involving ground-state complex formation, as supported by temperature-dependent Stern–Volmer analysis and thermodynamic studies which revealed spontaneous exothermic binding. Critical analytical parameters including pH, N,P-CQDs concentration, buffer volume, and reaction time were systematically optimized to maximize fluorescence quenching efficiency. Besides, SALLE conditions including pH, acetonitrile volume, salt concentration, and centrifugation time were optimized through four-factor three-level Box–Behnken design, yielding a predictive model with high statistical validity (R2 = 0.9106, adjusted R2 = 0.8777) for efficient extraction from complex environmental matrices. Under optimized conditions, the method exhibited high linearity (0.02–4.0 µg/mL, R2 = 0.9998), sensitivity (LOD: 6.2 ng/mL), high accuracy (99.65% recovery), and precision (RSD < 2%). Successful application to river and tap water samples yielded recoveries of 94–103% with RSD below 5%. Comprehensive green analytical chemistry assessment using AGREE (0.64), CaFRI (82/100), BAGI (72.5/100), and WAC RGB 12 model (87.7% whiteness) indicated satisfactory environmental sustainability, low carbon footprint, and high practical applicability, with reduced organic solvent consumption and shorter analysis time relative to conventional LC–MS/MS and HPLC methods reported for structurally related pharmaceuticals. The method offers a green, cost-effective proof-of-concept analytical platform with demonstrated feasibility for potential future monitoring of emerging pharmaceutical contaminants in aquatic ecosystems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-63824-1.Keywords: Valbenazine, Carbon quantum dots, Fluorescence quenching, Salting-out assisted liquid–liquid extraction, Green analytical chemistrySubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences

Introduction

The increasing contamination of water resources by pharmaceutical compounds has become a major environmental concern worldwide1. These compounds, even at very low concentrations (nanograms to micrograms per liter), can harm aquatic organisms and potentially affect human health2,3. The main sources of pharmaceutical pollution include human excretion after medication use, improper disposal of unused medicines, discharge from pharmaceutical manufacturing plants, hospital wastewater, and agricultural runoff from fields fertilized with contaminated sewage sludge4,5. Conventional wastewater treatment plants are not designed to remove these pharmaceutical residues effectively, allowing them to pass through and contaminate rivers, lakes, and groundwater6,7. A recent large-scale study examining water samples from 258 rivers across 104 countries found pharmaceutical contamination in more than 25% of the locations tested, with the highest pollution levels in developing countries where wastewater treatment infrastructure is inadequate8. These pharmaceutical residues can cause harmful effects in aquatic life including reproductive problems, endocrine disruption, development of antibiotic-resistant bacteria, and toxic effects on fish and other organisms9. Although the concentrations in drinking water are typically very low, there is growing concern about the long-term health impacts from continuous exposure to these compounds10.

Valbenazine is a novel pharmaceutical agent approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration in 2017 for the treatment of tardive dyskinesia in adults, representing the first approved medication specifically indicated for this debilitating movement disorder11. Tardive dyskinesia is an involuntary movement disorder that develops following prolonged exposure to dopamine receptor blocking agents, particularly antipsychotic medications12. Valbenazine functions as a highly selective inhibitor of vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2), reducing dopamine availability in the synaptic cleft and normalizing dopaminergic signaling in motor pathways13,14. Clinical trials have demonstrated statistically significant improvements in abnormal involuntary movement scores with a favorable safety profile15. As a widely and increasingly prescribed medication, valbenazine may potentially enter environmental water systems through patient excretion pathways, improper disposal of unused medications, and pharmaceutical manufacturing effluents16. However, comprehensive environmental monitoring data regarding the occurrence, persistence, and ecotoxicological impacts of valbenazine in aquatic ecosystems remain critically lacking, representing a significant knowledge gap given the compound’s increasing prescription rates17.

Despite the clinical importance of valbenazine, limited analytical methodologies exist for its determination in pharmaceutical and environmental matrices. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) methods utilizing pre-column chiral derivatization have been developed exclusively for quantifying valbenazine metabolites in human serum and plasma, achieving sensitivity down to 0.244 ng/mL18. However, this method requires complex derivatization procedures, expensive instrumentation, and is limited to biological matrices. Stability-indicating HPLC methods employing C18 columns with UV detection at 210–285 nm have been also reported for bulk drug analysis and impurity profiling, with detection limits of 0.27 µg/mL and analysis times of 15–60 min19,20. These methods suffer from insufficient sensitivity for environmental trace analysis, high organic solvent consumption, and potential interferences at low UV wavelengths. On the other hand, spectrofluorimetric methods based on native fluorescence with excitation at 210 nm and emission at 310 nm in acidic, basic, and neutral media achieved detection limits of 0.025–0.044 µg/mL21. Similarly, derivative UV spectrophotometric methods at 205–230 nm demonstrated detection limits of 0.89–2.75 µg/mL22. While these spectroscopic methods offer simplicity and lower costs, the short excitation wavelengths (205–210 nm) are highly susceptible to severe spectral interferences from dissolved organic matter, humic substances, aromatic compounds, proteins, and other naturally fluorescing/UV-absorbing species ubiquitous in environmental waters. Furthermore, existing methods lack the sensitivity required for environmental monitoring and suitable pre-concentration strategies for complex water matrices. Interestingly, no analytical methods have been reported for valbenazine determination in environmental water samples, representing a critical analytical gap.

Carbon quantum dots (CQDs) represent a novel class of zero-dimensional carbon nanomaterials with particle sizes typically below 10 nm, exhibiting high optical properties including strong photoluminescence, high photostability, tunable emission wavelengths, and size-dependent quantum confinement effects23,24. Heteroatom doping, particularly co-doping with nitrogen and phosphorus, has emerged as an effective strategy to substantially enhance the photophysical properties and sensing capabilities of carbon quantum dots by introducing additional energy states and modulating their electronic structure25,26. Nitrogen and phosphorus co-doped carbon quantum dots (N,P-CQDs) synthesized through green hydrothermal or microwave-assisted methods demonstrate superior quantum yields ranging from 36 to 63%, enhanced fluorescence intensity, and improved chemical reactivity compared to undoped counterparts27,28. The incorporation of phosphorus dopants introduces phosphate functional groups that modulate electronic density and enhance charge transfer properties, while nitrogen doping creates additional defect sites that improve fluorescent emission29. N,P-CQDs have demonstrated remarkable analytical performance in pharmaceutical and environmental sensing applications, enabling sensitive detection of various analytes including metal ions, pharmaceutical compounds, pesticides, and environmental pollutants through fluorescence quenching or enhancement mechanisms30–32. These nanoprobes offer significant advantages over conventional analytical techniques including simple synthesis procedures, excellent biocompatibility, low toxicity, cost-effectiveness, rapid response times, high sensitivity with detection limits in the nanomolar to micromolar range, and minimal consumption of hazardous organic solvents33–37. The unique combination of superior optical properties, facile surface functionalization, and environmental compatibility positions N,P-CQDs as promising fluorescent sensing platforms for trace-level detection of emerging pharmaceutical contaminants in complex environmental water matrices.

While CQDs-based fluorescence quenching methods offer high sensitivity, their application to real environmental water samples requires efficient sample preparation techniques to overcome the challenges posed by complex matrices containing high concentrations of interfering substances, dissolved organic matter, and inorganic salts38. Salting-out assisted liquid–liquid extraction (SALLE) has emerged as a promising green analytical approach involving the addition of inorganic salts to induce phase separation between aqueous samples and water-miscible organic solvents such as acetonitrile39. This salt-induced phenomenon produces two distinct phases wherein target analytes partition into the upper organic phase while matrix interferents remain in the lower aqueous phase40. SALLE offers substantial advantages over conventional extraction methods including simplified operation, reduced consumption of toxic solvents, cost-effectiveness, rapid execution, high extraction recoveries of 60–99%, superior tolerance to high-salinity matrices, and direct compatibility with fluorescence detection systems41. Systematic optimization of SALLE performance requires careful evaluation of critical parameters including solvent type and volume, salt concentration, pH, vortex time, and centrifugation conditions. Hence, application of design of experiments methodologies, particularly Box–Behnken designs, enables comprehensive optimization to maximize extraction efficiency for pharmaceutical contaminants in diverse environmental water samples38.

Given the critical need for sensitive, cost-effective, and environmentally sustainable analytical methods for monitoring emerging pharmaceutical contaminants in aquatic ecosystems, the present study was designed with the following specific objectives: (1) to develop a highly sensitive spectrofluorimetric method based on fluorescence quenching of N,P-CQDs for valbenazine determination in environmental water samples; (2) to synthesize and comprehensively characterize N,P-CQDs via microwave-assisted synthesis evaluating their morphological, structural, and optical properties; (3) to systematically investigate the fluorescence quenching mechanism through Stern–Volmer analysis, and thermodynamic parameter calculations to elucidate N,P-CQDs-valbenazine binding interactions; (4) to optimize critical analytical parameters affecting fluorescence quenching efficiency including pH, N,P-CQDs concentration, incubation time, and ionic strength; (5) to optimize SALLE pre-concentration parameters using Box–Behnken experimental design for efficient valbenazine extraction from complex water matrices; (6) to comprehensively validate the developed method according to ICH Q2(R2) guidelines evaluating linearity, sensitivity, accuracy, precision, robustness, and selectivity; (7) to demonstrate practical applicability through analysis of valbenazine in diverse environmental water samples with spike-recovery studies; and (8) to conduct comprehensive sustainability assessment using green analytical chemistry metrics. The integration of N,P-CQDs fluorescent nanoprobes with SALLE sample preparation represents a novel analytical platform that addresses the existing methodological gap for valbenazine environmental monitoring while adhering to green chemistry principles and ensuring compatibility with resource-limited laboratory settings.

Experimental

Chemicals and reagents

Valbenazine reference standard (purity 98.78%) was obtained from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) and utilized without further purification for method development and validation. Citric acid monohydrate, urea, magnesium sulfate, sodium sulfate, sodium chloride, ammonium sulfate, sodium hydroxide, and hydrochloric acid were procured from El-Nasr Pharmaceutical Chemicals Co. (Cairo, Egypt). Acetonitrile (HPLC grade), methanol (HPLC grade), acetone (HPLC grade), and absolute ethanol (analytical grade) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Quinine sulfate dihydrate, phosphoric acid (85% w/v), and sulfuric acid were supplied by Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Distilled water was used throughout all experimental procedures including N,P-CQDs synthesis, fluorescence measurements, buffer preparation, and sample dilutions.

Britton-Robinson universal buffer solutions were prepared using equimolar concentrations (0.04 M each) of phosphoric acid, acetic acid, and boric acid in distilled water according to established procedures. pH adjustments ranging from 3.0 to 9.0 were achieved through controlled addition of 0.04 M sodium hydroxide solution with continuous monitoring using a calibrated pH meter. Stock solution of valbenazine (100 µg/mL) was prepared by accurately weighing the appropriate amount of reference standard and dissolving in 10 mL ethanol, then the solution was quantitatively transferred to a 100 mL volumetric flask and the volume was completed to the mark with distilled water. The solution was sonicated briefly to ensure homogeneity, then stored at 4°C protected from light in amber glass bottles.

Environmental water samples including river water from the Nile River (Cairo, Egypt) and tap water from laboratory facilities were collected in pre-cleaned amber glass bottles and transported to the laboratory under refrigerated conditions. All water samples were filtered through 0.45 μm membrane filters (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) to remove suspended particulates and stored at 4°C protected from light until analysis within 24 h of collection.

Instrumentation

Fluorescence measurements were conducted on a Jasco FP-6200 spectrofluorometer equipped with a 150 W xenon lamp and photomultiplier tube detector, with excitation and emission slit widths set at 5 nm. Data acquisition was performed using Spectra Manager software. UV–visible absorption spectra were recorded on a Shimadzu UV-1800 dual-beam spectrophotometer using UVProbe software. Solution pH was measured using a calibrated Jenway 3510 digital pH meter. Microwave-assisted synthesis utilized a Tornado M-25MK domestic microwave oven. N,P-CQDs characterization was performed using a JEOL JEM-2100 transmission electron microscope, Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90 dynamic light scattering analyzer, Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS5 FTIR spectrometer, and Bruker MultiRAM FT-Raman spectrometer.

Synthesis and characterization of N,P-CQDs

Synthesis of N,P-CQDs

N,P-CQDs were synthesized via a rapid microwave-assisted pyrolysis method according to previous reports28,42 with slight modifications. Briefly, citric acid (500 mg) and urea (500 mg) were dissolved in deionized water (10 mL) in a ceramic crucible with stirring until complete dissolution. Phosphoric acid (1 mL, 85%, w/v) was then added as the phosphorus dopant, and the solution was mixed thoroughly. The resulting clear solution was subjected to microwave irradiation at 900 W for 5 min in a domestic microwave oven. During heating, the solution underwent progressive dehydration, polymerization, and carbonization, changing from colorless to yellow, then brown, and finally forming a dark brown viscous product. After cooling to room temperature, the crude product was dispersed in deionized water (20 mL) and sonicated for 15 min to ensure complete dissolution. The resulting brown solution was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 20 min to remove larger carbonized particles and aggregates. The supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22 µm syringe filter to obtain a clear brown solution containing N,P-CQDs.

For purification and isolation in powder form, the filtered N,P-CQDs solution was subjected to precipitation by addition of cold absolute ethanol (1:3 v/v ratio). The precipitate was collected by centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 30 min), washed three times with cold ethanol to remove residual unreacted precursors and small molecular impurities, and dried under vacuum at 50° to obtain N,P-CQDs powder. A stock solution of N,P-CQDs (1 mg/mL) was freshly prepared by accurately weighing the dried powder and dissolving it in deionized water with brief sonication. Working solutions were prepared daily by appropriate dilution of the stock solution.

Characterization of N,P-CQDs

The morphology and particle size of the synthesized N,P-CQDs were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) operating at 200 kV accelerating voltage. Samples were prepared by depositing diluted N,P-CQDs solution onto carbon-coated copper grids and allowing complete evaporation. Particle size distribution and hydrodynamic diameter were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a particle size analyzer at 25°C with a scattering angle of 90°. Functional groups present on the N,P-CQDs surface were identified by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectroscopy in the range of 4000–400 cm-1 using KBr pellet technique. Raman spectroscopy was performed to investigate the carbon structure and crystallinity of N,P-CQDs using 532 nm laser excitation with a spectral range of 100–3500 cm⁻1 and an integration time of 10 s with 3 accumulations to identify characteristic D and G bands of carbon materials. UV–visible absorption spectra were recorded between 200 and 500 nm to investigate the optical absorption characteristics. Fluorescence emission spectra were obtained to assess the photoluminescence properties of N,P-CQDs. The quantum yield was calculated using quinine sulfate in 0.1 M H₂SO₄ as a reference standard (quantum yield = 0.54) according to the comparative method equation: Φx = Φst × (Ix/Ist) × (Ast/Ax) × (ηx/ηst)2, where Φ is the quantum yield, I is the integrated emission intensity, A is the absorbance at the excitation wavelength, and η is the refractive index of the solvent. Absorbance values were maintained below 0.1 across all measured concentrations to minimize reabsorption effects. Deionized water (η = 1.333) and 0.1 M H₂SO₄ (η = 1.336) were employed as solvents for N,P-CQDs and quinine sulfate, respectively.

Optimization of analytical parameters

To achieve maximum fluorescence quenching efficiency and optimal analytical performance, critical parameters affecting the N,P-CQDs-valbenazine interaction were systematically optimized using univariate approach. All optimization experiments were performed in triplicate using 10 mL volumetric flasks with a fixed valbenazine concentration of 3 μg/mL unless otherwise stated. The influence of pH on fluorescence quenching efficiency was investigated over the range 3.0–9.0 using Britton-Robinson buffer (1 mL) at different pH values. The buffer was added to 10 mL volumetric flasks, followed by addition of N,P-CQDs solution and valbenazine standard solution. The volume was completed to the mark with deionized water, mixed thoroughly, and allowed to stand at room temperature. The effect of buffer volume was studied by varying Britton-Robinson buffer volume from 0 to 2.0 mL at the optimal pH in 10 mL volumetric flasks. The optimal N,P-CQDs concentration was determined by testing final concentrations ranging from 20 to 100 μg/mL prepared by appropriate dilution of the 1 mg/mL stock solution. The reaction time was evaluated by monitoring fluorescence intensity at time intervals from 0 to 10 min after mixing all components at room temperature (25 ± 2°C). All fluorescence measurements were performed at λex = 355 nm and λem = 441 nm, and quenching efficiency was assessed by calculating F₀/F ratio, where F₀ and F represent fluorescence intensities of N,P-CQDs without and with valbenazine, respectively.

General analytical procedures and construction of calibration curves

Calibration curves were constructed under optimized conditions determined from the optimization studies. In separate 10 mL volumetric flasks, 1.0 mL of Britton-Robinson buffer (pH 6.0) was transferred, followed by addition of 0.6 mL of N,P-CQDs stock solution (1 mg/mL) to achieve a final concentration of 60 µg/mL. Different aliquots of valbenazine working standard solutions were then added to achieve final concentrations of 0.02, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0, and 4.0 µg/mL. The volume was completed to the mark with deionized water and mixed thoroughly. The reaction mixtures were allowed to stand at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) for 3 min to ensure complete interaction between N,P-CQDs and valbenazine. The fluorescence intensity (F) was measured at excitation and emission wavelengths of 355 nm and 441 nm, respectively, using excitation and emission slit widths of 5 nm. A blank solution containing 1.0 mL buffer and 0.6 mL N,P-CQDs stock solution (without valbenazine) was prepared similarly, and its fluorescence intensity (F0) was measured under identical conditions. Each concentration was measured in triplicate. The degree of fluorescence quenching was evaluated by calculating the F0/F ratio, where F0 represents the fluorescence intensity of N,P-CQDs in the absence of valbenazine and F represents the fluorescence intensity in the presence of valbenazine. The calibration curve was established by plotting F0/F values against the corresponding valbenazine concentrations. Linear regression analysis was performed to determine the slope, intercept, and correlation coefficient (r2). The linearity range, limit of detection (LOD), and limit of quantification (LOQ) were calculated from the calibration data according to ICH Q2(R2) guidelines.

SALLE optimization using Box–Behnken design

Prior to systematic optimization, preliminary investigations were conducted to select the most suitable salt and organic solvent for the salting-out assisted liquid–liquid extraction procedure. Different inorganic salts including magnesium sulfate, sodium sulfate, sodium chloride, and ammonium sulfate, as well as various water-miscible organic solvents including acetonitrile, methanol, and acetone were evaluated for phase separation efficiency and extraction recovery. Based on these preliminary studies, magnesium sulfate and acetonitrile were selected as the optimal salt and solvent, respectively.

A four-factor, three-level Box–Behnken design was employed for systematic optimization using Design-Expert® software (Version 11, Stat-Ease Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA). The independent variables investigated were pH (X1: 4–10), acetonitrile volume (X2: 200–1000 µL), magnesium sulfate concentration (X3: 2–6 mol/L), and centrifugation time (X4: 2–10 min). The design generated 27 experimental runs including 3 center point replicates. Extraction recovery (%) was selected as the response variable, and all experiments were performed in random order.

For the SALLE procedure, environmental water samples (5 mL) were transferred into 15 mL polypropylene centrifuge tubes and pH was adjusted using dilute hydrochloric acid or sodium hydroxide. Valbenazine standard solution was spiked to achieve 3 µg/mL final concentration. The specified volumes of acetonitrile and magnesium sulfate were added according to the experimental design, and the mixture was vortexed for 2 min. Phase separation occurred spontaneously, and the mixture was centrifuged at 4500 rpm for the specified time. The upper organic phase was collected and diluted with deionized water before fluorescence measurement. The experimental data were analyzed using multiple regression analysis to develop a second-order polynomial equation. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to evaluate model significance and individual factors at 95% confidence level. Model adequacy was assessed by evaluating R2, adjusted R2, predicted R2, and lack of fit test. Three-dimensional response surface plots were generated to visualize interaction effects and identify optimal conditions. The optimized conditions were validated experimentally in triplicate.

Analysis of environmental water samples

Environmental water samples including river water and tap water were collected to evaluate the practical applicability of the developed method. River water samples were collected from the Nile River, Cairo, Egypt, while tap water samples were obtained from the laboratory facility. All samples were filtered through 0.45 µm membrane filters and stored at 4°C until analysis within 24 h.

The environmental water samples were analyzed using the optimized SALLE procedure. Water sample aliquots (5 mL) were transferred into 15 mL polypropylene centrifuge tubes, and pH was adjusted to 8.5. The samples were spiked with valbenazine standard solutions at four concentration levels: 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 µg/mL. Anhydrous magnesium sulfate (MgSO4, 3.31 g, equivalent to 5.5 mol/L with respect to the initial aqueous phase) and 1.0 mL of acetonitrile were added, vortexed for 2 min, and centrifuged at 4500 rpm for 3 min. The upper organic phase (approximately 0.8 mL) was carefully collected and transferred into a 5 mL volumetric flask. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of Britton-Robinson buffer (pH 6.0) and 0.3 mL of N,P-CQDs stock solution (1 mg/mL) were added, and the volume was completed to 5 mL with deionized water, maintaining the same final concentrations of buffer and N,P-CQDs (60 µg/mL) as the optimized analytical procedure. The mixture was allowed to stand at room temperature for 3 min prior to fluorescence measurement at λex = 355 nm and λem = 441 nm. Matrix-matched calibration curves were constructed for each water sample type by preparing calibration standards in the respective matrices and subjecting them to the complete SALLE procedure. Recovery studies were performed in triplicate for each spiking level, and recovery percentages and relative standard deviations were calculated to assess method accuracy and precision.

Results and discussion

Characterization of N,P-CQDs

The synthesis of highly fluorescent N,P-CQDs was achieved through microwave-assisted pyrolysis of citric acid, urea, and phosphoric acid as carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus sources, respectively. This one-pot synthesis approach offers significant advantages including rapid reaction time, energy efficiency, and environmental sustainability compared to conventional hydrothermal methods. Preliminary optimization experiments were systematically conducted to maximize quantum yield and fluorescence intensity. Various precursor mass ratios of citric acid to urea (1:0.5 to 1:2, w/w), phosphoric acid volumes (0.5–2.0 mL), microwave irradiation powers (450, 600, 750, and 900 W), and irradiation times (3–10 min) were investigated. The optimization studies revealed that citric acid (500 mg), urea (500 mg), and phosphoric acid (1 mL) subjected to microwave irradiation at 900 W for 5 min produced N,P-CQDs with the highest quantum yield of 48.26 ± 1.23%, calculated using quinine sulfate as reference standard according to the comparative method. This value compares favorably with quantum yields of 36–63% reported in the literature for N,P-CQDs synthesized via analogous microwave-assisted and hydrothermal approaches27,28, confirming the high photoluminescence efficiency of the synthesized nanomaterial. To assess synthesis reproducibility, three independently prepared batches of N,P-CQDs were evaluated, yielding quantum yields of 48.26%, 47.83%, and 48.91% with an RSD of 1.12%, confirming satisfactory batch-to-batch reproducibility of the microwave-assisted synthesis procedure. Lower powers or shorter times resulted in incomplete carbonization, while excessive conditions led to over-carbonization and diminished quantum yields. The equimolar citric acid to urea ratio ensured optimal nitrogen incorporation, while phosphoric acid served as both phosphorus source and dehydrating agent that facilitated the carbonization process and enhanced the formation of surface functional groups.

The morphological characteristics and particle size distribution of the synthesized N,P-CQDs were comprehensively evaluated using TEM and DLS techniques. TEM analysis (Fig. 1A) revealed that the N,P-CQDs exhibited a quasi-spherical morphology with high monodispersity and uniform distribution without significant aggregation, indicating good colloidal stability. Particle size analysis from TEM images demonstrated that the N,P-CQDs had an average diameter of 4.35 nm with a standard deviation of 1.87 nm (Fig. 1B), based on measurements of 200 individual nanoparticles, indicating a relatively narrow size distribution within the quantum confinement regime. The histogram analysis showed that the majority of the particles fell within the 3–6 nm size range. Complementary hydrodynamic diameter measurements by DLS (Fig. S1) yielded a mean particle size of 4.41 nm with a standard deviation of 1.64 nm, a polydispersity index of 0.15 confirming narrow size distribution, and a zeta potential of -27.3 mV indicating satisfactory colloidal stability through adequate interparticle electrostatic repulsion. The minimal discrepancy between TEM and DLS measurements can be attributed to the presence of hydration layers and surface functional groups surrounding the N,P-CQDs particles in aqueous solution. Colloidal stability assessment over a 15-day storage period revealed negligible change in fluorescence intensity with RSD below 2%, confirming the long-term stability of the synthesized N,P-CQDs under ambient storage conditions. These morphological characteristics are highly favorable for fluorescence-based sensing applications, as uniform particle size distribution ensures consistent optical properties and reproducible analytical performance.

Structural characterization of the N,P-CQDs was performed using FTIR spectroscopy and Raman spectroscopy to elucidate the surface chemical composition and carbon framework structure. FTIR spectrum (Fig. 1C) displayed several characteristic absorption bands that confirmed the successful incorporation of nitrogen and phosphorus heteroatoms along with various oxygen-containing functional groups on the N,P-CQDs surface. The broad absorption band in the region of 3200–3600 cm−1 was attributed to O–H and N–H stretching vibrations, while bands at approximately 2950 cm−1 corresponded to aliphatic C–H stretching vibrations. The prominent peak at approximately 1560 cm−1 was assigned to C=O stretching vibrations, while absorption at approximately 1320 cm−1 were characteristic of C–N and P=O stretching vibrations, providing indirect spectroscopic evidence supportive of heteroatom co-doping. These assignments are consistent with previously reported FTIR characterization of N,P-CQDs synthesized from analogous precursor systems, where direct elemental confirmation by XPS has been demonstrated28,29. These surface functional groups contribute significantly to the water solubility, colloidal stability, and surface reactivity of the N,P-CQDs. Raman spectroscopy (Fig. S2) revealed two characteristic peaks at 1348 cm−1 and 1602 cm−1, corresponding to the D-band (defect band) and G-band (graphitic band), respectively. The intensity ratio ID/IG was calculated to be 1.1, indicating that the N,P-CQDs possess a predominantly amorphous carbon structure with numerous defect sites and heteroatom-induced distortions. This ratio greater than unity confirms successful heteroatom doping and the presence of abundant structural defects that serve as radiative recombination centers essential for strong photoluminescence.

The optical properties of the synthesized N,P-CQDs were systematically investigated through UV–visible absorption and fluorescence spectroscopy. The UV–Vis absorption spectrum (Fig. S3) displayed strong absorption below 250 nm, attributed to π → π* electronic transitions of aromatic C=C bonds in the graphitic core structure. Additionally, a shoulder band around 340 nm was observed, assigned to n → π* electronic transitions involving heteroatoms in surface functional groups such as C=O, C=N, and P=O bonds. Fluorescence spectroscopy revealed that the N,P-CQDs exhibited strong blue–green photoluminescence with excitation and emission maxima centered at 355 nm and 441 nm, respectively (Fig. 1D). The emission spectrum displayed a symmetrical Gaussian-shaped peak centered at 441 nm with a full width at half maximum of approximately 62 nm, indicating narrow emission bandwidth and homogeneous emission from well-defined fluorescent states. The large Stokes shift of 86 nm is highly advantageous for fluorescence-based sensing applications as it minimizes self-absorption effects and reduces interference from scattered excitation light. The superior optical properties, including strong fluorescence emission, large Stokes shift, narrow emission bandwidth, and high quantum yield, position these N,P-CQDs as good fluorescent nanoprobes for developing sensitive spectrofluorimetric methods for pharmaceutical and environmental analysis.

Fluorescence quenching mechanism investigation

The interaction between N,P-CQDs and valbenazine resulted in significant fluorescence quenching, as evidenced by the progressive decrease in fluorescence intensity upon successive additions of valbenazine (Fig. 2A). The fluorescence emission peak at 441 nm exhibited systematic quenching with increasing valbenazine concentrations from 0 to 4 µg/mL, while the peak position remained unchanged, indicating a direct interaction between the fluorescent probe and the analyte. Understanding the quenching mechanism is crucial for optimizing analytical performance and establishing the nature of molecular interactions. Fluorescence quenching can occur through various mechanisms including inner filter effect, Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET), dynamic quenching, and static quenching. The inner filter effect and FRET mechanism were initially excluded as potential quenching pathways based on the optical characteristics of the system and the lack of spectral overlap requirements. The UV–visible absorption spectrum of valbenazine (Fig. S4) demonstrates that the drug exhibits absorption exclusively below 310 nm, with negligible absorbance at both the N,P-CQDs excitation wavelength (355 nm) and emission wavelength (441 nm). The absence of spectral overlap between valbenazine absorption and the N,P-CQDs excitation/emission wavelengths unequivocally confirms that the inner filter effect does not contribute to the observed fluorescence quenching, and no correction was therefore required. FRET was similarly excluded given the absence of spectral overlap between donor emission and acceptor absorption. To distinguish between static and dynamic quenching mechanisms, UV–visible absorption spectroscopy was employed as a preliminary investigation. The absorption spectrum of N,P-CQDs in the presence of valbenazine (Fig. 2B) showed distinct changes compared to N,P-CQDs alone, exhibiting both hypochromic effect and hypsochromic shift in the absorption band around 340 nm. These spectral changes are characteristic indicators of ground-state complex formation, providing initial evidence that the quenching mechanism follows a static pathway rather than dynamic collisional quenching.

Temperature-dependent fluorescence measurements were performed at three different temperatures (298, 303, and 313 K) to further confirm the quenching mechanism. The Stern–Volmer equation (F0/F = 1 + Ksv[Q]) was applied to analyze the quenching behavior, where F0 and F represent the fluorescence intensities in the absence and presence of valbenazine, respectively, Ksv is the Stern–Volmer quenching constant, and [Q] is the quencher concentration. The Stern–Volmer plots (Fig. 2C) exhibited high linearity across all three temperatures with correlation coefficients exceeding 0.999, indicating a single predominant quenching mechanism. The calculated Ksv values were 4.24 × 105, 3.67 × 105, and 3.10 × 105 M-1 at 298, 303, and 313 K, respectively (Table 1). The progressive decrease in Ksv values with increasing temperature is characteristic of static quenching, as elevated temperatures destabilize ground-state complexes and reduce binding affinity. In contrast, dynamic quenching typically exhibits increasing Ksv values with temperature due to enhanced molecular collisions. These results are consistent with a static quenching mechanism involving the formation of a non-fluorescent ground-state complex between N,P-CQDs and valbenazine. where F0 and F represent the fluorescence intensities in the absence and presence of valbenazine, respectively, Ka is the binding association constant (M⁻1), and [Q] is the molar concentration of valbenazine (M). The plot of F0/(F0 − F) versus 1/[Q] (Fig. S5) yielded linear relationships at all three temperatures, with Ka values determined from the reciprocal of the slope. The binding constant (Ka) values were 5.16 × 105, 4.76 × 105, and 4.04 × 105 M⁻1 at 298, 303, and 313 K, respectively (Table 1), indicating moderate to strong binding affinity. These results support the conclusion that the fluorescence quenching follows a static mechanism involving the formation of a non-fluorescent ground-state complex between N,P-CQDs and valbenazine, as evidenced by the temperature-dependent decrease in Ksv values and the UV–visible spectral changes observed upon complex formation. It is acknowledged that time-resolved fluorescence lifetime measurements would provide additional confirmation of this mechanism; however, the two independent lines of experimental evidence presented are consistent with static quenching as the predominant pathway.

Thermodynamic parameters were determined to elucidate the nature of binding forces involved in the N,P-CQDs-valbenazine interaction. The negative Gibbs free energy values (ΔG) of − 32.60, − 32.95, and − 33.61 kJ/mol at 298, 303, and 313 K, respectively (Table 1), indicate that the binding process is spontaneous and thermodynamically favorable. The enthalpy change (ΔH) was calculated to be − 12.57 kJ/mol, while the entropy change (ΔS) was 67.19 J/(mol K). The positive ΔS value indicates increased randomness upon complex formation, possibly due to release of water molecules from hydration layers. The van’t Hoff plot (Fig. 2D) of ln(Ka) versus 1/T exhibited high linearity, validating the thermodynamic calculations and confirming the consistency of the binding model across the temperature range studied. However, these thermodynamic parameters are consistent with, and suggestive of, hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces as the predominant binding forces between N,P-CQDs and valbenazine; however, it is acknowledged that thermodynamic parameters alone do not conclusively establish the molecular nature of these interactions and should be interpreted as mechanistic inference rather than direct experimental evidence.

Optimization of analytical parameters

Systematic optimization of critical analytical parameters was performed to maximize fluorescence quenching efficiency and achieve optimal sensitivity for valbenazine determination. The parameters investigated included pH, buffer volume, N,P-CQDs concentration, and reaction time.

The effect of pH on fluorescence quenching efficiency was investigated over the range of 3.0–9.0 using Britton-Robinson buffer (Fig. 3A). The quenching efficiency, expressed as F0/F ratio, increased progressively from pH 3.0 to 5.0, reaching maximum values in the pH range of 5.0–7.0, followed by a gradual decrease at higher pH values. At acidic pH below 5.0, the reduced quenching can be attributed to protonation of carboxylic groups on the N,P-CQDs surface, which diminishes the negative surface charge and weakens electrostatic interactions with valbenazine. The optimal quenching at pH 5.0–7.0 suggests favorable ionization states of both N,P-CQDs carboxylic groups and valbenazine, facilitating hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions for complex formation. At alkaline pH above 7.0, deprotonation of valbenazine disrupts the electrostatic interaction with the negatively charged N,P-CQDs surface, resulting in weakened binding affinity and reduced quenching efficiency. pH 6.0 was selected as optimal. Buffer volume optimization (Fig. 3B) showed that F0/F ratio increased up to 1.0 mL, remained constant between 1.0–1.5 mL, and decreased at higher volumes due to increasing ionic strength that disrupts the electrostatic interactions between N,P-CQDs and valbenazine. Therefore, 1.0 mL was selected.

(A) Effect of pH on fluorescence quenching efficiency showing optimal performance at pH 6.0. (B) Effect of buffer volume showing optimal performance at 1.0 mL. (C) Effect of N,P-CQDs concentration showing plateau at 60–80 µg/mL. (D) Effect of reaction time demonstrating rapid equilibrium within 3 min.

The effect of N,P-CQDs concentration was evaluated from 20 to 100 µg/mL (Fig. 3C). The F0/F ratio increased with concentration from 20 to 60 µg/mL, reaching a plateau at 60–70 µg/mL, indicating saturation of available binding sites. Further increase above 80 µg/mL caused slight decrease, possibly due to aggregation-induced quenching or inner filter effects. Thus, 60 µg/mL was selected as optimal. Reaction time investigation (Fig. 3D) revealed that quenching reached maximum efficiency within 3 min and remained stable for at least 10 min, indicating rapid complex formation kinetics. The fast equilibrium confirms that static quenching involves spontaneous ground-state complex formation without requiring prolonged incubation. A reaction time of 3 min was selected, ensuring complete interaction while maintaining analytical throughput. These optimized conditions (pH 6.0, 1.0 mL buffer, 60 µg/mL N,P-CQDs, 3 min reaction time) were employed for all subsequent analytical measurements.

SALLE optimization using Box–Behnken design

Salting-out assisted liquid–liquid extraction was employed as a green and efficient sample preparation technique to pre-concentrate valbenazine from environmental water samples prior to fluorescence detection. Prior to systematic Box–Behnken optimization, preliminary screening experiments were conducted to select the most appropriate organic solvent and inorganic salt for the SALLE procedure. Three water-miscible organic solvents were first evaluated at a fixed salt concentration, yielding extraction recoveries of 93.2%, 72.4%, and 63.8% for acetonitrile, methanol, and acetone, respectively. Acetonitrile demonstrated superior extraction performance owing to its lower viscosity, favorable polarity for valbenazine partitioning, and efficient phase separation upon salt addition, and was therefore selected for further optimization. Subsequently, four inorganic salts were evaluated using acetonitrile as the extraction solvent, yielding extraction recoveries of 92.4%, 81.6%, 78.3%, and 67.9% for magnesium sulfate, sodium sulfate, ammonium sulfate, and sodium chloride, respectively. The superior performance of magnesium sulfate was attributed to its high ionic strength, exothermic dissolution behavior, and strong salting-out effect that effectively promotes phase separation between acetonitrile and the aqueous matrix. Accordingly, acetonitrile and magnesium sulfate were selected for subsequent Box–Behnken optimization. To systematically optimize the SALLE procedure and investigate potential interactions among extraction parameters, a four-factor three-level Box–Behnken design was implemented. The independent variables investigated were pH (A: 4–10), acetonitrile volume (B: 200–1000 µL), salt concentration (C: 2–6 mol/L), and centrifugation time (D: 2–10 min), while extraction recovery (%) was selected as the response variable. The experimental design matrix comprised 27 runs including three center point replicates (Table S1), with all experiments performed in random order to minimize systematic errors.

The reduced model was derived through backward elimination of the initial full quadratic model, whereby all terms with p values exceeding 0.05 were sequentially removed while hierarchical model principles were maintained. Centrifugation time (factor D) was non-significant across all associated terms including its main effect, two-way interactions, and quadratic term, and was therefore excluded from the final model.

Analysis of variance revealed high model significance (F-value = 27.65, p < 0.0001), while the non-significant lack of fit (p = 0.5925) confirmed adequate model fitting (Table 2). Model quality assessment (Table S2) showed R2 = 0.9106, adjusted R2 = 0.8777, and predicted R2 = 0.8372, indicating that 91.06% of variability in extraction recovery is explained by the model with good predictive capability. The reasonable agreement between adjusted R2 and predicted R2 (difference < 0.2) confirms model validity without overfitting. The adequate precision of 19.84, well above the threshold of 4, indicates high signal-to-noise ratio and reliable model discrimination. Model diagnostics through residual analysis validated these statistics. The normal probability plot (Fig. S6A) shows residuals aligned along the diagonal, confirming normal distribution. The predicted versus actual plot (Fig. S6B) demonstrates high correlation with minimal scatter around the 45° line. The residuals versus run plot (Fig. S6C) shows random distribution without systematic patterns, while the leverage plot (Fig. S6D) confirms absence of influential outliers, collectively validating model assumptions and reliability.

Individual factor effects were evaluated through ANOVA and main effect plots (Fig. 4). The coded equation coefficients provide direct comparison of relative factor importance. Acetonitrile volume (B: coefficient = + 7.75) exhibited the strongest effect (F-value = 71.89, p < 0.0001), indicating it is the most critical parameter for extraction efficiency. From a chemical perspective, larger acetonitrile volumes provide greater solvent capacity for valbenazine partitioning from the aqueous phase and facilitate more complete phase separation (Fig. 4B). The positive coefficient confirms that increasing acetonitrile volume consistently improves extraction recovery. Salt concentration (C: coefficient = + 5.56) showed the second strongest effect (F-value = 36.94, p < 0.0001), which is chemically rational as higher magnesium sulfate concentrations enhance the salting-out phenomenon by decreasing the solubility of acetonitrile in water through ionic interactions, thereby strengthening phase separation and forcing valbenazine into the organic phase (Fig. 4C). pH (A: coefficient = + 5.43) demonstrated significant positive effect (F-value = 35.31, p < 0.0001), attributed to ionization state changes of valbenazine (Fig. 4A). At higher pH, valbenazine becomes less protonated and more hydrophobic, increasing its affinity for the organic acetonitrile phase and improving extraction efficiency. Centrifugation time (D) showed no significant effect and was excluded from the final model, indicating that 2 min provides sufficient centrifugal force for complete phase separation, and longer times offer no additional benefit (Fig. 4D).

Two significant interaction effects were identified. The pH-acetonitrile volume interaction (AB: coefficient = + 3.49, F-value = 4.86, p = 0.0401) shows positive synergistic effect, suggesting that the combination of higher pH (promoting valbenazine hydrophobicity) and larger acetonitrile volume (providing greater extraction capacity) produces enhanced recovery beyond additive individual effects. The pH-salt concentration interaction (AC: coefficient = -8.10, F-value = 26.21, p < 0.0001) exhibited the largest coefficient magnitude but with negative sign, indicating strong antagonistic effect. This antagonism can be explained chemically: at higher pH, valbenazine is already predominantly hydrophobic and readily extracted, so additional salt provides diminishing benefits. Conversely, at lower pH where valbenazine is more hydrophilic, increased salt concentration is more effective in forcing phase separation. The desirability function optimization (Fig. S7) identified optimal conditions: pH 8.39, acetonitrile volume 993 µL, salt concentration 5.51 mol/L, and centrifugation time 2.58 min, with predicted recovery of 99.05%. Practical conditions (pH 8.5, 1.0 mL acetonitrile, 5.5 mol/L salt, 3 min centrifugation) yielded experimental recovery of 98.8 ± 1.6% (n = 3), confirming model reliability.

Method validation

The developed spectrofluorimetric method was comprehensively validated according to ICH Q2(R2) guidelines to evaluate its analytical performance and suitability for valbenazine determination in environmental water samples43. The validation parameters assessed included linearity, sensitivity, accuracy, precision, robustness, and selectivity.

Linearity was evaluated by constructing calibration curves under optimized conditions using seven concentration levels. The method exhibited high linearity over the concentration range of 0.02–4.0 µg/mL with a correlation coefficient (R2) of 0.9998, indicating strong linear relationship between fluorescence quenching (F0/F) and valbenazine concentration (Table 3). The regression equation was F0/F = 1.0150C + 1.0626, where C represents valbenazine concentration in µg/mL. The regression model was highly significant (F = 33,119.09, p = 9.51 × 10⁻11), with slope of 1.0150 (95% CI 1.0007–1.0293) and intercept of 1.0626 (95% CI 1.0327–1.0924), confirming statistical significance of both parameters. The residual standard error of 0.0210 indicates minimal scatter around the regression line. The intercept value of 1.0626 slightly exceeds the theoretical F₀/F value of unity at zero analyte concentration, which is attributable to minor background fluorescence contributions from the N,P-CQDs and buffer system and instrument baseline variability. This deviation is analytically negligible and does not compromise calibration accuracy, as confirmed by the mean recovery of 99.65%.

The limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ) were calculated as 3.3σ/S and 10σ/S, respectively, where σ is the standard deviation of the blank and S is the slope of the calibration curve. These values represent instrumental detection and quantification limits derived from calibration solutions prepared under optimized analytical conditions in accordance with ICH Q2(R2) guidelines. The LOD was determined to be 6.2 ng/mL and LOQ was 18.7 ng/mL, demonstrating sensitivity suitable for trace-level analysis. Method accuracy was assessed through recovery studies at three concentration levels within the linear range, with each level analyzed in triplicate. The mean recovery was 99.65 ± 1.27%, indicating high accuracy with minimal systematic error and close agreement between measured and true values. Precision was evaluated at two levels: repeatability (intra-day precision) and intermediate precision (inter-day precision). Repeatability was assessed by analyzing three different concentrations in triplicate on the same day under identical conditions, yielding a relative standard deviation (RSD) of 1.274%. Intermediate precision was determined by analyzing the same concentrations in triplicate over three consecutive days, with independent preparation of both valbenazine standard solutions and N,P-CQDs solutions on each day, resulting in RSD of 1.767%. Method robustness was evaluated by introducing small deliberate variations in critical parameters including buffer pH (± 0.2 units), N,P-CQDs concentration (± 5 µg/mL), and reaction time (± 1 min). The mean recoveries remained within acceptable range: 99.86 ± 1.23% for pH variation, 98.25 ± 1.07% for N,P-CQDs concentration variation, and 100.02 ± 0.75% for reaction time variation, confirming method robustness against minor operational changes.

Selectivity was assessed by investigating potential interference from common inorganic ions and pharmaceuticals frequently present in environmental water samples (Table S3). All interferents were tested at a tenfold excess concentration relative to valbenazine (analyte concentration: 3 µg/mL; interferent concentration: 30 µg/mL; analyte-to-interferent ratio 1:10), with tolerance limits defined as no interference (< 2% deviation in QE%), slight interference (2–5% deviation), and significant interference (> 5% deviation) relative to valbenazine alone. Before SALLE application, several species including Fe3⁺, Cu2⁺, PO₄3⁻, and carbamazepine showed significant interference with quenching efficiencies deviating more than 5% from valbenazine alone. Two additional environmentally relevant dissolved organic matter components, humic acid and fulvic acid, also produced interference before SALLE treatment (QE% = 79.84 ± 1.98% and 78.63 ± 1.87%, respectively). However, after SALLE pre-treatment, all tested interfering substances, including humic acid (QE% = 76.23 ± 1.31%) and fulvic acid (QE% = 75.97 ± 1.24%), showed quenching efficiencies within the acceptable tolerance limit, indicating that the SALLE procedure effectively eliminates matrix interferences and ensures method selectivity for valbenazine determination in complex environmental water matrices.

The analytical performance of the developed method compares favorably with previously reported methods for valbenazine determination. The LC–MS/MS method developed for biological matrices18 requires complex chiral derivatization and expensive instrumentation, limiting its applicability to environmental analysis. Stability-indicating HPLC–UV methods19,20 employ C18 or related columns with run times of 15–60 min and substantial organic solvent consumption (acetonitrile-based mobile phases), achieving LOD values of 0.27 µg/mL, considerably higher than the present method. Native fluorescence spectrofluorimetric methods21 achieved lower LOD values (0.025–0.044 µg/mL) in pharmaceutical matrices using short excitation wavelengths (210 nm), which are highly susceptible to interference from naturally fluorescing species in environmental waters, as discussed in the Introduction. Derivative UV spectrophotometric methods22 reported LOD values of 0.89–2.75 µg/mL with similarly limited environmental applicability due to short detection wavelengths. In contrast, the present method employs a longer excitation/emission wavelength pair (355/441 nm), substantially reducing susceptibility to environmental matrix interference, while achieving a competitive LOD of 6.2 ng/mL with simple, rapid SALLE-based sample preparation requiring minimal acetonitrile consumption (1 mL per sample). Unlike previously reported methods, which were validated exclusively for pharmaceutical or biological matrices, the present method demonstrates feasibility for environmental water matrices, representing its principal advantage and addressing a previously unmet analytical need.

Analysis of environmental water samples

The practical feasibility of the developed method was evaluated through a proof-of-concept investigation involving the analysis of valbenazine in spiked environmental water samples, including river water collected from the Nile River (Cairo, Egypt) and tap water obtained from laboratory facilities. Prior to spiking, both water matrices were subjected to the complete analytical procedure to assess background interference. Valbenazine was not detected in either unspiked matrix, with fluorescence signals remaining below the method LOD of 6.2 ng/mL, confirming the absence of endogenous background at the analyte signal and demonstrating the suitability of the SALLE procedure for effective matrix cleanup. Water sample aliquots (5 mL) were transferred into 15 mL polypropylene centrifuge tubes, and pH was adjusted to 8.5. The samples were spiked with valbenazine standard solutions at four concentration levels: 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 µg/mL. These levels were selected to fall within the validated linear range of the method and to ensure robust assessment of matrix compatibility as part of this proof-of-concept study. It is acknowledged that actual environmental concentrations of valbenazine in surface waters are expected to be considerably lower; however, the SALLE procedure can be readily adapted to achieve higher enrichment factors through increased sample volumes or incorporation of an evaporation-reconstitution step, extending method sensitivity to trace environmentally relevant concentration ranges. Following spiking, 1.0 mL of acetonitrile and magnesium sulfate (5.5 mol/L final concentration) were added, vortexed for 2 min, and centrifuged at 4500 rpm for 3 min. The upper organic phase was collected, diluted with deionized water, and subjected to fluorescence measurement. The spike-recovery experiments were designed to assess matrix compatibility and method feasibility rather than to demonstrate actual environmental occurrence of valbenazine in the studied matrices. It is acknowledged that comprehensive environmental monitoring would require analysis of a larger number of real-world samples across diverse collection points and time periods, which represents an important direction for future investigation.

Matrix-matched calibration curves were constructed for each water sample type by preparing calibration standards in the respective matrices and subjecting them to the complete SALLE procedure. The matrix effect was assessed by comparing the slopes of solvent-based and matrix-matched calibration curves, calculated as ME% = (slope matrix/slope solvent − 1) × 100. The solvent-based calibration slope of 1.0150 was compared with matrix-matched slopes of 0.9887 and 1.0023 for river water and tap water, corresponding to quenching signal suppression matrix effects of − 2.59% and − 1.25%, respectively. Both values fall within the accepted tolerance threshold of ± 15%, confirming that the SALLE pre-treatment effectively minimizes matrix interference and that matrix-matched calibration adequately compensates for residual matrix effects. Recovery studies were performed in triplicate for each spiking level, and recovery percentages and relative standard deviations were calculated to assess method accuracy and precision. The results demonstrated satisfactory method applicability for environmental water analysis (Table 4). For river water samples, the recovery percentages ranged from 94.12% to 102.64% across all spiking levels, with relative standard deviations (RSD) between 2.13% and 4.29%. The lower recoveries at the lowest concentration level (94.12% at 0.1 µg/mL) can be attributed to the increased complexity of the river water matrix and proximity to the method’s quantification limit. At higher spiking levels, recoveries improved progressively, reaching 98.56% at 1.0 µg/mL and 102.64% at 2.0 µg/mL, confirming accurate quantification across the concentration range. The precision improved with increasing concentration, as evidenced by decreasing RSD values from 4.29% to 2.13%, which is expected due to better signal-to-noise ratios at higher analyte levels.

Green analytical chemistry and sustainability assessments

The environmental sustainability and practical applicability of the developed method were comprehensively evaluated using multiple assessment tools including Analytical GREEnness metric (AGREE)44, Carbon Footprint Reduction Index (CaFRI)45, Blue Applicability Grade Index (BAGI)46, and White Analytical Chemistry (WAC) RGB 12 model47. The assessments primarily considered the complete sample preparation and detection workflow (SALLE extraction and fluorescence measurement), as the synthesized N,P-CQDs stock solution is used across numerous analytical determinations rather than being prepared individually for each sample.

The pictogram reveals high performance in Factor 4 (integration of analytical processes), Factor 6 (avoidance of derivatization), and Factor 9 (energy consumption), shown as green segments. Integration of salting-out extraction with fluorescence detection eliminates multiple separate steps, reducing energy and reagent requirements. The absence of chemical derivatization and low-power ambient-temperature operation contribute to environmental favorability. Moderate performance, indicated by yellow segments, was noted for Factor 1 (sample treatment), Factor 2 (sample size), Factor 5 (automation), Factor 7 (waste generation), Factor 8 (multi-analyte capability), and Factor 10 (renewable reagents). The main limitation was Factor 3 (in situ measurements), shown as red, as samples require laboratory analysis. Compared with conventional chromatographic methods reported for valbenazine, which typically require organic-solvent-intensive mobile phases and analysis times of 15–60 min19,20, the present method offers reduced solvent consumption and shorter analysis time, supporting a relative sustainability advantage, although it remains limited by manual operation and the requirement for laboratory-based analysis.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed and validated a proof-of-concept spectrofluorimetric method for valbenazine determination in spiked environmental water samples by integrating N,P-CQDs as fluorescent nanoprobes with SALLE for sample preparation. The N,P-CQDs exhibited quasi-spherical morphology with average diameter of 4.35 nm, high quantum yield of 48.26 ± 1.23%, and strong blue–green fluorescence with large Stokes shift of 86 nm. The fluorescence quenching behavior was consistent with a static mechanism involving ground-state complex formation, supported by temperature-dependent Stern–Volmer analysis and UV–visible spectral changes, with thermodynamic parameters consistent with hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces as predominant interaction types. Box–Behnken design-based SALLE optimization yielded a highly efficient extraction procedure, and the developed method demonstrated satisfactory analytical performance with wide linear range (0.02–4.0 µg/mL), LOD of 6.2 ng/mL, accuracy (99.65% recovery), and precision (RSD < 2%). Proof-of-concept application to spiked river water and tap water samples with recoveries of 94–103% confirmed method feasibility and matrix compatibility. Comprehensive green analytical chemistry assessment using AGREE (0.64), CaFRI (82/100), BAGI (72.5/100), and WAC RGB 12 model (87.7% whiteness) demonstrated satisfactory environmental sustainability, offering sustainability advantages over conventional chromatographic methods in terms of solvent consumption and analysis time.

Several limitations of the present study warrant explicit acknowledgment. The instrumental LOD of 6.2 ng/mL, while suitable for the proof-of-concept scope of this investigation, may require further improvement through higher enrichment factors to achieve sensitivity compatible with trace-level monitoring in pristine surface waters. Orthogonal confirmation of the spiked sample results by LC–MS/MS or a validated HPLC method was not performed, representing an important direction for future validation. Direct elemental evidence for nitrogen and phosphorus incorporation, such as XPS or EDS analysis, was not available, and the doping confirmation relies on indirect spectroscopic evidence consistent with previously reported characterizations of analogous systems. Furthermore, the proposed static quenching mechanism, while supported by two independent lines of experimental evidence, could not be fully confirmed in the absence of time-resolved fluorescence lifetime measurements. Future work should explore higher enrichment factors through adapted SALLE conditions, orthogonal method verification, and comprehensive environmental monitoring of valbenazine across diverse water matrices. The availability of a validated analytical method for valbenazine determination represents a prerequisite step toward future environmental risk assessment, including investigation of its occurrence, persistence, degradation pathways, and ecotoxicological impacts in aquatic ecosystems, which remain critically lacking and warrant dedicated future investigation.
