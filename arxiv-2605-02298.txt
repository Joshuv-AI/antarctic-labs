On the approximation of permutons
Balázs Maga

Abstract
We study the optimal rectangular-discrepancy approximation of permutons by finite permutations. We transfer bounds from discrepancy theory to this more restricted setup. Moreover, we show that superlinear approximation can occur only for permutons supported by graphs of measure-preserving functions, and demonstrate how the local regularity of this function obstructs approximability. We also consider the biased Brownian separable permuton and prove a lower bound on its approximation error by showing that its supporting measure-preserving function has Lipschitz points almost surely. Keywords : permutons, rectangular distance, star discrepancy, self-similarity, Brownian separable permuton

Introduction

A
permuton
is a probability measure on the unit square with uniform marginals. Permutons are the natural limit objects of permutations
[
11
]
, where convergence is defined via convergent substructure densities arising from sampling (cf. graphons and graph convergence). Permutons have received much attention in recent years, see for example
[
3
,
5
,
6
]
.

We denote the set of permutons by
\mathop{\mathrm{Perm}}
. The convergence of a sequence of permutations
\pi_{n}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(n)
to
\mu\in\mathop{\mathrm{Perm}}
is defined using pattern densities. The density
t(\sigma,\pi)
of a pattern
\sigma\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(k)
in
\pi\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(n)
for
k\leq n
is defined as the probability of
(\pi(i_{j}))_{j=1}^{k}
being order-isomorphic to
(\sigma(j))_{j=1}^{k}
for a uniform random increasing
k
-tuple
(i_{j})_{j=1}^{k}
in
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. For example, in the permutation
15342\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(5)
, five of the ten increasing 3-tuples are order-isomorphic to 132 (153, 154, 152, 132, and 142), hence
t(132,15342)=5/10.

Analogously, the density
t(\sigma,\mu)
of
\sigma
in
\mu\in\mathop{\mathrm{Perm}}
is defined as follows: taking an i.i.d.
k
-sample
(x_{j},y_{j})_{j=1}^{k}
, if
(i_{j})_{j=1}^{k}
is a permutation of
[k]
such that
(x_{i_{j}})_{j=1}^{k}
is an increasing sequence,
t(\sigma,\mu)
is the probability of
(y_{i_{j}})_{j=1}^{k}
being order-isomorphic to
(\sigma(j))_{j=1}^{k}
, see Figure
1
. (A random permutation sampled this way from
\mu
is called a
\mu
-random permutation
.)

A sequence of permutations
(\pi_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is convergent if
|\pi_{n}|\to\infty
and
t(\sigma,\pi_{n})
is convergent for each pattern
\sigma
. A fundamental result in permuton theory
[
11
]
is that a convergent permutation sequence determines a unique permuton whose pattern densities coincide with the corresponding limit densities. Conversely, we also know that any permuton
\mu
is the limit of a permutation sequence, conveniently proved by using
\mu
-random permutations and showing that they are very close to
\mu
with probability tending rapidly to one. This closeness is appropriately measured by the
rectangular distance
,
defined by

\mathop{d_{\square}}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{R=[a,b]\times[c,d]}|\mu(R)-\nu(R)|,

a metric which metrizes the topology induced by convergence in pattern densities and also the topology of weak convergence restricted to permutons. (For permutons, the supremum is actually a maximum.) By
[
11
, Lemma 4.2]
a
\mu
-random permutation of length
k
(or more precisely, the naturally associated permuton, properly defined in Section
1.1
) is at most
k^{-1/4}
apart from
\mu
in the rectangular distance with high probability. The proof given there actually guarantees distance
O((\log k/k)^{1/2})
asymptotically almost surely, already shown in
[
8
, Lemma 4.9]
without using the language of permutons.

In this paper, we study the following question originally asked by Bálint Virág and communicated to the author by Balázs Ráth: to what extent can the approximation errors coming from random permutations be improved by a more careful choice? In other words, how well a given permuton can be approximated in
\mathop{d_{\square}}
by finite permutations? This can be thought of as the permuton version of the central question of measure-theoretic discrepancy theory.

1.1
The main results

The permuton
\widehat{\pi}
naturally associated with
\pi\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(n)
is defined as the uniform measure on
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{i-1}{n},\frac{i}{n}\right]\times\left[\frac{\pi(i)-1}{n},\frac{\pi(i)}{n}\right]
, see Figure
2
. (We refer to these
n
grid squares as the
base squares
of either
\pi
or
\widehat{\pi}
.) We put

D_{n}(\mu)=\min_{\pi\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(n)}\mathop{d_{\square}}(\mu,\widehat{\pi}).

\mu
from the previous figure approximated by the permuton
\widehat{\pi}

\pi=12348765
. The rectangle witnessing
\mathop{d_{\square}}(\mu,\widehat{\pi})=1/8
is shown with green boundary. The best approximation is given by
\pi^{\prime}=12345768

.

Established results in measure-theoretic discrepancy theory
[
7
,
1
]
(to be explicitly stated in Section 2), partially coming from bounds given to Tusnády’s problem on combinatorial and geometric discrepancy
[
14
]
can be tailored to imply some bounds on
D_{n}
. Given the aforementioned results, the proofs are very simple: as we will see, approximation with finitely supported measures (as in discrepancy theory) has the same error decay rate as
D_{n}(\mu)
. We state the following theorem among the main results nevertheless for future reference.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any permuton
\mu
,

D_{n}(\mu)=O(\log^{3/2}n/n).

If
\mu
is absolutely continuous,

D_{n}(\mu)=\Omega(\log^{1/2}n/n).

If
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\mu
can be covered by finitely many curves that are monotone in both coordinates, then

D_{n}(\mu)=\Theta(1/n).

It is worth pointing out that the upper bound in Theorem
1.1
is significantly better than what is guaranteed by the standard approximation via
\mu
-random permutations discussed in the introduction.

The last statement of Theorem
1.1
shows that singular permutons allow better rates of approximation, though as a basic triangle inequality based argument shows error
D_{n}(\mu)=o(1/n)
can hold along a subsequence at best. (This is sharpened below in Theorem
1.3
.) Such superlinear approximation even along a subsequence is only possible for permutons carried by measure-preserving bijections:

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume that
D_{n}(\mu)=o(1/n)
along a subsequence for a permuton
\mu
. Then there exists a measure-preserving bijection
f:[0,1]\to[0,1]
defined almost everywhere such that
\mu=\mu_{f}
, i.e.
\mu
is the uniform measure on the graph of
f
.

The following theorem further refines the boundary of superlinear approximability and relates it to local regularity properties of
f
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume
\mu=\mu_{f}
for an almost everywhere defined measure-preserving bijection.

1.

If
D_{n}(\mu)=o(1/n)
along a subsequence then
D_{n}(\mu)=\omega(1/n)
along another subsequence.

2.

If
f
is Hölder-
\alpha
at a point
x
for some
0<\alpha\leq 1
, then
D_{n}(\mu)=\Omega(n^{-1/\alpha}).
In particular, if
f
is Lipschitz at a point
x
, then no superlinear approximation is possible.

3.

The previous statement is sharp up to logarithmic factor: for every
0<\alpha\leq 1
there exists an almost everywhere defined measure-preserving bijection
f
which is Hölder-
\alpha
at a dense set of points with Hausdorff dimension 1 and satisfies
D_{n}(\mu_{f})=O\left(\frac{\log n}{n^{1/\alpha}}\right)
along a subsequence.

We remark that for
\alpha=1
, the third statement is trivial even without the logarithmic factor, as demonstrated by
f(x)=x
. We further note that
D_{n}
can decay arbitrarily fast using similar constructions to what is given in the proof of the third statement of Theorem
1.3
, but of course we need to sacrifice regularity.

The Brownian separable permuton was introduced by
[
4
]
as the distributional limit of the uniform random separable permutation of length
n
. This concept was generalized in
[
2
]
. There, the biased Brownian separable permuton was introduced as a one-parameter family
(\mu^{p})
, and a universality result was proved: under mild conditions, uniformly random permutations from substitution-closed permutation classes converge to some
\mu^{p}
. This family of permutons attracted significant attention. As proved in
[
12
]
,
\mu^{p}
is almost surely supported by the graph of a measure-preserving function, and has self-similarity properties, implying a fractal structure. Capitalizing on this result, we prove a result about its pointwise regularity:

Lemma 1.4
.

Almost surely, the measure-preserving function whose graph supports the biased Brownian separable permuton
\mu^{p}
is Lipschitz at some points.

Lemma
1.4
, coupled with Theorem
1.3
directly yields the following theorem on the approximability of the biased Brownian separable permuton:

Theorem 1.5
.

For the biased Brownian separable permuton
\mu^{p}
almost surely
D_{n}(\mu^{p})=\Omega(1/n)
.

Note that the previous theorem does not tell the actual decay rate of
D_{n}(\mu^{p})
, we highlight this as an open question:

Question 1.6
.

For the biased Brownian separable permuton
\mu^{p}
, what is the almost sure decay rate of
D_{n}(\mu^{p})
?

Remark 1.7
.

We note that the construction proving the third statement of Theorem
1.3
and Lemma
1.4
both rely primarily on the self-similar structure of the corresponding permutons. Consequently, the statements of Lemma
1.4
and hence Theorem
1.5
are also valid for some other statistically self-similar permutons, such as the recursive separable permuton introduced in
[
9
]
as the permuton limit of a Markovian model of separable permutations, or the permutons associated with random automorphisms of the infinite rooted
d
-ary tree
[
13
]
. We omit the proofs as they are essentially identical to the proof of Lemma
1.4
. We take note of the fact that while for the latter of these two random permutons, Question
1.6
also seems difficult, for the former, the bound
D_{n}(\mu)=O(1/n)
along a subsequence follows straightforwardly from the compatibility of the self-similar structure with a regular grid.

1.2
Discussion of the choice of
\widehat{\pi}

We note that while it is customary to define
\widehat{\pi}
as we defined it, it is not canonical and the essence of permuton theory does not depend on this particular choice. Notably, instead of putting the uniform measure to each of the
1/n
grid squares corresponding to the permutation matrix of
\pi
, one could put there any appropriately rescaled permuton, not necessarily the same one for different squares, to get a large family of permutons naturally associated with
\pi
. Denoting this family by
\mathop{\mathrm{Perm}}(\pi)
, one finds that for any choice of
\widehat{\pi}_{n}^{\prime}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Perm}}(\pi_{n})
, the convergence
\pi_{n}\to\mu
in terms of pattern densities is equivalent to
\widehat{\pi_{n}}^{\prime}\to\mu
in
\mathop{d_{\square}}
. Thus passing to such an admissible alternative of
\widehat{\pi}
indeed makes no difference for the big picture. However, at least formally it certainly affects the study of superlinear approximations. In the most relaxed form one could define

D_{n}^{\prime}(\mu)=\min_{\pi\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(n)}\inf_{\widehat{\pi}^{\prime}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Perm}}(\pi)\text{ }}\mathop{d_{\square}}(\mu,\widehat{\pi}^{\prime}).

and study approximability in terms of
D_{n}^{\prime}
. Inspecting the proof, it is not difficult to see that Theorem
1.2
holds for
D_{n}^{\prime}
as well, however, the statements of Theorem
1.3
are no longer valid, displayed for instance by
f(x)=x
, for which
D_{n}^{\prime}(\mu_{f})=0
for every
n
. (It is simple to show that
D_{n}^{\prime}
vanishes for every
n
only for the monotone permutons.) Thus
D_{n}
and
D_{n}^{\prime}
indeed behave differently to some extent, we choose to study
D_{n}
for aesthetic reasons, such as the duality-flavored statements of Theorem
1.3
.

2
Preliminaries and notation

The set of probability measures on a set
S
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}(S)
. Then
\mathop{\mathrm{Perm}}\subset\mathcal{P}([0,1]^{2})
. We denote projections to the
x
-axis and
y
-axis by
\Pr_{x}
and
\Pr_{y}
, respectively. For a measure-preserving function
f
, the permuton
\mu_{f}
is the pushforward of the Lebesgue measure under
x\mapsto(x,f(x))
.

The central question of measure-theoretic discrepancy theory is that how well finitely supported uniform distributions can approximate a given measure. To quantify this, star discrepancy is usually defined between finite point sets and measures. Slightly abusing this language, we define the star discrepancy of measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}([0,1]^{2})
as

{\mathop{d_{\square}}}^{*}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{R=[0,x]\times[0,y]}|\mu(R)-\nu(R)|.

The following lemma is immediate from the inclusion-exclusion principle:

Lemma 2.1
.

For any
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}([0,1]^{2})

{\mathop{d_{\square}}}^{*}(\mu,\nu)\leq{\mathop{d_{\square}}}(\mu,\nu)\leq 4{\mathop{d_{\square}}}^{*}(\mu,\nu).

Consequently, the approximability rates in
\mathop{d_{\square}}
and
\mathop{d_{\square}}^{*}
coincide up to constant factor.

For a finite point multiset
P
we write

\mu_{P}=\frac{1}{|P|}\sum_{p\in P}\delta_{p}.

To conclude the preliminaries, we recall a few results of discrepancy theory which are relevant in our discussion. First, for the approximation of the Lebesgue measure, we have the following matching bounds on the rate of approximation:

Theorem 2.2

(Two-dimensional case of
[
10
]
)
.

For any
n
there exist
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in[0,1]^{2}
with

{\mathop{d_{\square}}}^{*}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}},\lambda\right)=O\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right).

Theorem 2.3

(
[
15
, Theorem 2]
)
.

There exists
c>0
such that for any
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in[0,1]^{2}
we have

{\mathop{d_{\square}}}^{*}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}},\lambda\right)\geq\frac{c\log n}{n}.

It should be noted that while
2.2
extends to higher dimensions
k
with
O(\log^{k-1}{n}/n)
, whether this is optimal is known as the Great Open Problem of discrepancy theory. On a related note, even in two dimensions it is unknown whether other measures can exhibit worse approximability properties, with the best known result being the following:

Theorem 2.4

(Two-dimensional case of
[
1
, Theorem 1]
)
.

For any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}([0,1]^{2})
and
n
there exists
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in[0,1]^{2}
with

{\mathop{d_{\square}}}^{*}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}},\mu\right)=O\left(\frac{\log^{3/2}n}{n}\right).

The following statement appears in substantially greater generality in
[
7
]
, allowing non-uniform finite distributions and concerning various norms of the discrepancy.

Theorem 2.5

(Two-dimensional case of
[
7
, Corollary 1]
)
.

For any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}([0,1]^{2})
absolutely continuous, there exists some
c(\mu)>0
such that for any
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in[0,1]^{2}

{\mathop{d_{\square}}}^{*}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}},\mu\right)>\frac{c(\mu)\log^{1/2}n}{n}.

3
Universal approximation

The rates given by Theorems
2.4
–
2.5
are precisely in line with the statement of Theorem
1.1
. To convert them to statements about
D_{n}
, we just need to convert point sets into permutons associated with permutations with small error in
\mathop{d_{\square}}
. To this end, abusing the notation, we identify a permutation
\pi
with the point set
\{(i/n,\pi(i)/n):i\in[n]\}\subseteq[0,1]^{2}
. The following statement is immediate from the definitions:

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
\pi\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(n)

1/n\leq\mathop{d_{\square}}(\widehat{\pi},\mu_{\pi})\leq 4/n.

A finite point set in
[0,1]^{2}
is
generic
if its points have pairwise distinct
x
and pairwise distinct
y
coordinates. For a generic point set
P
in
[0,1]^{2}
, the pattern
\pi(P)
it determines is straightforward to define: we move from left to right and read the height ordering to get a permutation, see Figure
3
. For a non-generic point set, or more generally for a multiset of points, ties may occur both in the left-to-right ordering and in the height ordering; we resolve all such ties arbitrarily.

Figure 3:
The first panel shows a generic point set
P
with
\pi(P)=53124
. The second and third panel shows how
\pi(P)
(as a point set) is obtained in the proof of Lemma
3.2
from
P
in two steps: first we regularize the
x
coordinates to obtain
P_{\rightarrow}
, then the
y
coordinates to obtain
\pi(P)
.

In general
\mu_{P}
and
\mu_{\pi(P)}
can be far from each other. However:

Lemma 3.2
.

Put
n=|P|
. If
{\mathop{d_{\square}}}^{*}(\mu_{P},\mu)<C/n
for a permuton
\mu
and some
C>0
, then

{\mathop{d_{\square}}}^{*}(\mu_{P},\mu_{\pi(P)})<\frac{2C+3}{n}

Proof.

Note that
\pi(P)
as a point set can be obtained by first horizontally displacing points without changing their relative order by
x
-coordinate, then vertically displacing them without changing their relative order by
y
-coordinate. Denote the intermediate set by
P_{\rightarrow}
(second panel of Figure
3
).

Consider
R=[0,x]\times[0,1]
. Putting
n_{S}(R)
for the number of points of
S
in
R
, we have

|n_{P}(R)-nx|<C,

while

|n_{P_{\rightarrow}}(R)-nx|\leq 1,

implying

|n_{P}(R)-n_{P_{\rightarrow}}(R)|<C+1.

(1)

As
P_{\rightarrow}
is obtained from
P
by moving points only along the
x
-axis in an order-preserving manner, the difference between the quantities
n_{P}(R),n_{P_{\rightarrow}}(R)
solely comes from points entering
R
or points exiting
R
, no points can cross the boundary in opposite directions simultaneously. This implies that
(
1
) is valid for any
R^{\prime}=[0,x]\times[0,y]
in place of
R
, as there is no flux between
R^{\prime},R\setminus R^{\prime}
either due to the horizontal displacements. Consequently,

{\mathop{d_{\square}}}^{*}(\mu_{P},\mu_{P_{\rightarrow}})<\frac{C+1}{n}.

We argue the same way for replacing
P_{\rightarrow}
by
\pi(P)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

For the first statement, consider the finite point set guaranteed by Theorem
2.4
. Using Lemma
3.2
, we can pass to a nearby permutation point set
\pi
without changing the error rate, which in turn is in the
O(1/n)
proximity of
\widehat{\pi}
by Lemma
3.1
.

The second statement follows immediately from Theorem
2.5
and Lemma
3.1
.

For the third statement cover
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\mu
with injective, coordinatewise monotone curves
(\gamma_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
, parametrized by the closed intervals
(I_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
. We can assume that these are ordered,
0<I_{1}<\dots<I_{k}
. We want these pieces to have disjoint ranges, so we put

A_{i}=I_{i}\setminus\gamma_{i}^{-1}\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}\gamma_{j}(I_{j}))\right).

Now
\gamma:\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}\to[0,1]^{2}
defined with
\gamma(x)=\gamma_{i}(x)
once
x\in A_{i}
is an injective function which is coordinatewise monotone restricted to each
A_{i}
, and
\gamma(\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i})=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\gamma_{i}(I_{i})\supseteq\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\mu
. Below
\gamma(I)
for an interval
I
is understood as
\gamma(I\cap\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i})
.

Fix now
n
.
We claim that for
j=1,2,\dots,n
there exists
s_{j}\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}
with

\mu(\gamma([0,s_{j}]))\in\left(\frac{j}{n}-\frac{1}{n^{2}},\frac{j}{n}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

Indeed, otherwise by interval-halving we can find a nested sequence of closed intervals
J_{m}
with length converging to 0 such that
\mu(\gamma(J_{m}))>\frac{2}{n^{2}}
for each
m
. Then for the common point
x
of these intervals
\mu(\gamma(x))\geq\frac{2}{n^{2}}
, which is impossible because uniform marginals imply that
\mu
has no atoms.

Put now
P=\{\gamma(s_{j})\}_{j=1}^{n}
. Note that by monotonicity, for any rectangle
R
and
i=1,2,\dots,k
there exists some interval
I_{i,R}
such that
R\cap\gamma_{i}(A_{i})=\gamma(I_{i,R})
and hence

\mu(R)=\mu\left(\gamma\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}I_{i,R}\right)\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu(\gamma(I_{i,R})),\quad\mu_{P}(R)=\mu_{P}\left(\gamma\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}I_{i,R}\right)\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{P}(\gamma(I_{i,R})).

Then by triangle inequality

\mathop{d_{\square}}(\mu,\mu_{P})\leq\sup_{R}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\mu(\gamma(I_{i,R}))-\mu_{P}(\gamma(I_{i,R}))|\right).

However, by the choice of
P
, for any
R,i

|\mu(\gamma(I_{i,R}))-\mu_{P}(\gamma(I_{i,R}))|\leq 2/n+2/n^{2}.

This implies
\mathop{d_{\square}}(\mu,\mu_{P})\leq 2k/n+2k/n^{2}
and then one can use Lemma
3.2
to replace
P
by
\pi(P)
and
3.1
to replace
\pi(P)
by
\widehat{\pi(P)}
to ultimately obtain
D_{n}(\mu)=O(1/n)
. The lower bound on approximability follows from the Statement 1 of Theorem
1.3
proved later:
O(1/n)
approximability along the full sequence rules out
o(1/n)
along a subsequence.
∎

4
Superlinear approximation along a subsequence

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Fix a subsequence
n_{1},n_{2},\dots
in accordance with the assumption of the theorem, that is
D_{n_{k}}(\mu)<\alpha_{k}/n_{k}
with
\alpha_{k}\to 0
.
By discarding certain entries of the sequence, we may assume that
\alpha_{k}
is decreasing and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}<\infty
. We may also assume that
\frac{n_{k+1}}{n_{k}}>2^{k}
. To ease notation, set
\mu_{k}=\widehat{\pi_{k}}
with
\pi_{k}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Sym}}(n_{k})
realizing
D_{n_{k}}
.

Recall that a base square of
\pi_{k}
is a grid square of size
n_{k}^{-1}\times n_{k}^{-1}
contained by the support of
\widehat{\pi_{k}}
. For
x\in[0,1]\setminus\mathbb{Q}
, let
Q_{k}(x)=I_{k}(x)\times J_{k}(x)
be the unique base square of
\pi_{k}
for which
x\in I_{k}(x)
. (For rational
x
, this would not be unique.) We say that the sequence
Q_{k}(x)
is
eventually nested
if
Q_{k+1}(x)\subseteq Q_{k}(x)
for sufficiently large
k
.

Claim 4.1
.

For almost every
x\in[0,1]
,
Q_{k}(x)
is eventually nested.

Proof of Claim
4.1
.

Consider a base square
Q_{k}=I_{k}\times J_{k}
of
\pi_{k}
. Define the rectangles
R_{k}^{1},R_{k}^{2}
to be non-overlapping with
Q_{k}
such that

I_{k}\times[0,1]=R_{k}^{1}\cup Q_{k}\cup R_{k}^{2}.

Plugging in these rectangles into the definition of
d_{\square}
verifies that for
i=1,2

\mu(R_{k}^{i})\leq\frac{\alpha_{k}}{n_{k}}.

(2)

Now consider base squares of
\pi_{k+1}
over
I_{k}
, the
x
-projections of these are
1/n_{k+1}
grid intervals. As
n_{k}
may not be a divisor of

,

, or

. Observe that

due to (

. To sum up, we conclude the following for

(and hence

:

•

on a set of measure at most

•

on a set of measure at most

•

on a set of measure at most
\frac{4\alpha_{k}}{n_{k}}
,

is contained in either

or

, hence it is disjoint from

.

•

in the remainder of

,
Q_{k+1}(x)\subseteq Q_{k}(x)

Hence in
I_{k}
,

apart from measure at most

.
Multiplying by

to take into account all possible choices of

,

fails in

at a set of measure at most

By the assumptions on
(\alpha_{k})
and
(n_{k})
, the resulting series is summable, thus by the Borel–Cantelli lemma, we have that

holds for almost every

∎

For almost every irrational
x
, whose existence is guaranteed by the claim, denote by

the minimal index from where

is nested. Since the intervals

are nested and have lengths

to be this point.

Observe that the function

is injective outside

.

After defining

arbitrarily on the remaining null set, let

be the pushforward of the Lebesgue measure under

. We claim that it is the weak limit of the sequence

. This would conclude the proof: as the limit is unique, we obtain

, which directly implies that

is a measure-preserving function, and by omitting the null-set

from its domain, we get a measure-preserving bijection due to our observation about

By the Portmanteau theorem, it suffices to show that for any open set

we have

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\mu_{k}(G)\geq\mu_{f}(G).

(3)

Fix

, and let

. If

, then
\mu_{f}(G)=0
and we have nothing to prove. Otherwise for any
\varepsilon>0
for every large enough integer

we can find

with the properties

•

\lambda(H^{\prime})>\lambda(H)-\varepsilon
,

•

.

(By taking

Now it is clear that

5
Approximation under regularity conditions

Our goal in this section is to prove Theorem
1.3
.
We will work with certain fractal constructions (Figure
4
). Given a sequence of integers
n_{1}<n_{2}<\dots
and
N_{k}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}n_{k}
, the
k
th level grid intervals are the ones with length
N_{k}^{-1}
in
[0,1]
. Denote the family of such intervals by
\mathcal{I}_{k}
. (For the sake of completeness,
\mathcal{I}_{0}=\{[0,1]\}
.) The subfamily containing those
I\in\mathcal{I}_{k}
for which the unique
I^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{k-1}
with
I^{\prime}\supseteq I
is such that

is denoted by
\mathcal{I}_{k,r}
. In this case, we say that
I
is a direct
r
-descendant of
I^{\prime}
. Finally,

F_{k_{0},r}=\bigcap_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}\bigcup\mathcal{I}_{k,r}.

The primary role of
k_{0}

is to guarantee a nonempty intersection.

A few steps of the described fractal scheme for
n_{1}=4,n_{2}=7,n_{3}=10
. With red, we see the first three initial segments of the infinite intersection defining

.

We obtain the following:

Lemma 5.1
.

If
k_{0}
is large enough to guarantee that

is nonempty, then for any

we have
\dim_{H}F_{k_{0},r}=1.

Proof.

The proof is a highly standard one in geometric measure theory, yet we include it for the sake of completeness. We define a probability measure
\nu
on
F_{k_{0},r}
inductively:
\nu(F_{k_{0},r}\cap I)=N_{k}^{-1}
for
k<k_{0}
and
I\in\mathcal{I}_{k}
, and for
I\in\mathcal{I}_{k,r}
with
k\geq k_{0}
, if
I
is the direct
r
-descendant of

then

\nu(F_{k_{0},r}\cap I)=\frac{\nu(F_{k_{0},r}\cap I^{\prime})}{\text{no. of direct $r$-descendants of $I^{\prime}$}}.

With this definition, it is immediate that
\nu
is consistently defined on sets of the form
F_{k_{0},r}\cap I
, and thus it extends uniquely to all Borel subsets of
F_{k_{0},r}
as a measure by Carathéodory’s extension theorem. It is also straightforward to check that we have for
k\geq k_{0}
and

\nu(F_{k_{0},r}\cap I)=\frac{1}{\text{size of $\mathcal{I}_{k,r}$}}\sim\frac{1}{(1-2r)^{k-k_{0}}N_{k}}.

We would like to apply the mass distribution principle, to which end we fix an interval
J\subseteq[0,1]
with
|J|<1/N_{k_{0}}
. There exists unique
k>k_{0}
such that

\frac{l}{N_{k}}\leq|J|<\frac{l+1}{N_{k}}

for some
l\in\{1,2,\dots,n_{k}-1\}
. Then
J
is contained in the union of at most
l+1
adjacent intervals of
\mathcal{I}_{k}
, hence the same holds for

as well. Consequently,

\nu(J\cap F_{k_{0},r})\leq\frac{l+1}{(1-2r)^{k-k_{0}}N_{k}}.

Thus mass distribution principle guarantees
\dim_{H}F_{k_{0},r}\geq d
once there exists
C
with

\frac{l+1}{(1-2r)^{k-k_{0}}N_{k}}\leq\frac{Cl^{d}}{N_{k}^{d}}.

Writing
l+1\leq 2l
, and replacing

by
n_{k}
it suffices to demonstrate the existence of
C
with

2(1-2r)^{-(k-k_{0})}\leq C\left(\frac{N_{k}}{n_{k}}\right)^{1-d}=CN_{k-1}^{1-d}.

(4)

Note that as
n_{k}
is a strictly increasing sequence,

grows superexponentially, i.e., for any

we have
N_{k}>a^{k}
once

is large enough. Thus the same holds for
N_{k-1}^{1-d}
. Thus (
4
) is satisfied for arbitrary

and

: even if we omit the factor
C
, the right hand side will eventually exceed the left hand side, and the finitely many exceptions can be handled by choosing
C
large enough.
∎

.

For

Claim 5.3
.

If

We will analyze

\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}
is larger than
r
, then there is 1 offspring, while if both are, then there are 2 offspring. If
X_{0}<r
, then there are no offspring. Then for the random number
Z(r)
of offspring,
Z(r)\to 2
in probability as
r\to 0
, hence
GW(r)
survives with positive probability, yielding a non-empty boundary with positive probability
p_{r}
. Taking countably many pairwise disjoint initial segments (such as
1,21,221,\dots
) shows that there exists an
(r,n_{0})
-good
\mathbf{i}
for some
n_{0}
if any of the countably many independent events with probability
p_{r}
occurs, which clearly happens almost surely.
