The Factors Affecting the Screening of New Colorectal Cancer Cases Between 2020 and 2022 in Isfahan Province
Authors: Elaheh Ebrahimi, Razieh Hassannejad, Masood Shekarchizadeh, Jamshid Najafan, Zamane Vafaei, Samaneh Mostafavi, Fateme Nouri, Reihaneh Zavar, Ali Darakhshandeh, Mohammadreza Khosravifarsani, Farzaneh Ashraf, Mehran Sharifi, Ali Akhavan, Nadia Najafizadeh, Alireza Sadeghi, Maryam Mirporian, Masoumeh Sadeghi, Nizal Sarrafzadegan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common neoplasms in Iran. The objectives of this study were to determine the factors affecting the screening of new colon cancer cases in Isfahan province.Methods:This present study is a cross-sectional study that was conducted within the framework of the Cardio Vascular Events in Breast and Colorectal Cancers (CIBC) study. CIBC is a multicenter prospective cohort study of newly diagnosed BC and CRC patients that has been started in Iran since 2019 and will be continued until 2024. In this study, all patients who were diagnosed with CRC were considered between January 2020 and January 2022. Information was collected through a questionnaire.Results:In total, 78 cases in our study had colon cancer, and 69 had rectal cancer. The majority of patients in our study were between ages of 45-75 (74.4%), married (97%), educated (77%), urban-based (89.4%), nonsmoker (79.5%) and nondrinker (97%), had insurance coverage (96.3%) and had normal levels of depression (52.5%), anxiety (54.4%) and stress (77.5%). Higher age (P = 0.034), higher anxiety score (P = 0.00) and history of colorectal surgery (P = 0.018) was associated with higher rates of screening behaviors especially colonoscopy.Conclusions:Understanding the importance of CRC screening is critical to improve population health. According to the results, higher age, higher anxiety, and a history of colorectal surgery were the main factors affecting participation in the CRC screening programs.

Introduction

Cancer is a huge global health issue that not only results in death and reduced fertility but also has a heavy financial and psychological cost. One of the most prevalent diseases, colorectal cancer (CRC) affects 1.23 million people annually (9.7% of all cancers) and ranks fourth globally in terms of cancer-related fatalities (608,000 cases, or 8% of all cancer deaths).[1] According to estimates, the incidence of CRC is 3-5 incidences per 100,000 in most Middle Eastern nations and 30-50 cases per 100,000 in northern America and Europe.[2] On the other hand, throughout the course of the next 20 years, it is regrettably anticipated that the annual incidence would rise in developing nations, particularly those in Asia.[3] The Iranian National Cancer Registry Report indicates that throughout the past 25 years, there has been an increase in the incidence of CRC, indicating a change in the disease’s distribution towards lower age groups in the nation.[4] This cancer is the third most common cancer in Iranian men (standardized incidence: 8.1-8.3 per 100,000) and the fourth most common cancer in women with a standardized incidence of 6.5 to 7.5 per 100,000.[5]

One of the screening procedures that may aid in the prevention of malignancy is the identification of cancer and precancerous polyps in asymptomatic persons.[6] According to earlier research, using screening techniques can cut down on CRC-related fatalities by roughly 60%.[7] Furthermore, the implementation of CRC screening programs in the US has resulted in a notable decline in the death rates from cancer.[8] According to current standards, screening for CRC should begin earlier for high-risk individuals and in the general population at 50 years of age and above.[9] A colonoscopy every 10 years, a sigmoidoscopy every five years, a barium enema every five years, or an occult blood test annually are among the screening tests that every man and woman 50 years of age or older must undergo in order to comply with the country’s regulations for disease prevention.[10] The gold standard for assessing the prognosis of CRC adenoma and colon carcinoma is colonoscopy screening.[11] Given the high prevalence of CRC in our country and expecting the rise of incidents in the next 20 years, prevention throughout screening is vital. In this study, we aimed to investigate the factors affecting the screening of new colon cancer cases between 2018 and 2020 in Isfahan province.

Methods

Study design and population

This study is a cross-sectional study that was conducted within the framework of the Cardio Vascular Event in Breast and Colorectal Cancer (CIBC) study. CIBC is a multicenter prospective cohort study of newly diagnosed BC and CRC patients has been started in Isfahan since 2019 and have continued until 2024. The study population was all CRC patients who were newly diagnosed between January 2020 and January 2022. In coordination with oncologist and in compliance with ethical principles and patient privacy, before starting any treatment patients were contacted to refer to the Cardiovascular Research Center and enter the study. The CIBC questionnaire was completed and information was collected through it.[12]

Ethical consideration

Ethics approval for the study was obtained from the institutional review board of the Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUI.MED.REC.1402.20). The Declaration of Helsinki was followed throughout the study.

Data analyses

SPSS software version 25 was used to analyze the data. Quantitative data were stated as a mean ± SD, whereas qualitative data were expressed as a frequency (percent). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was utilized to determine whether quantitative variables were normal. For statistical analysis, Pearson Chi-Square or Fisher’s Exact tests were applied. In all tests, a significance level of less than 0.05 was used.

Discussion

Our study was designed to evaluate factors affecting the screening of new colon cancer cases in our local province. Our study showed that higher age, higher anxiety score and history of colorectal surgery were associated with a higher percentage of screening behaviors especially colonoscopy. Other factors did not show any statistical significance.

Randomized controlled trials have demonstrated the effectiveness of CRC screening in lowering incidence and death rates.[16] In actuality, screening is even advised for average-risk populations by clinical standards and medical organizations.[17] A crucial factor in determining the effectiveness of any population-based screening program is screening uptake. Both the cost-effectiveness and mortality reduction of screening have been continuously linked to high participation rates.[18] According to a recent report by the European Commission, The American Cancer Society’s goal is to screen 75% of people at average risk of cancer, while a minimum uptake of 45% in the average-risk population is regarded as an acceptable target and a desirable rate is 65%.[19] Notwithstanding the extant literature and targeted guidelines, CRC screening rates continue to stray from these objectives, albeit with notable regional variations.[20] However, compared to other advised adult preventive interventions, CRC screening participation rates are noticeably lower. As a result, it is critical to pinpoint the factors that influence screening adoption and create intervention plans to encourage screening habits.

There have been conflicting reports on how gender affects screening participation. Generally, men appear to participate in CRC screening more frequently than women, yet variations have been observed based on the nation and screening method. Accordingly, a recent systematic review demonstrated that women were more likely to participate in occult blood-based screening programs conducted in Australia or Europe.[21] There is evidence from other research that men use endoscopies more frequently.[22] According to surveys conducted by the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) in the United States, males are more likely than women to use the CRC test.[23] Nonetheless, gender tended not to be evaluated in the pooled study results of our study.[2425]

The relationship between age and screening uptake has been the subject of numerous research. In the United States, older adults (over 65) who reported a peak at 75 years old and a decline at 80-85 years old had higher screening uptake.[26] In contrast to younger invitees, men and women 65 years of age or older showed a statistically significant increase in screening, according to a randomized trial conducted in Italy.[27] Age was also a significant factor in our study.

Numerous research has linked a low socioeconomic position (income, unemployment, education level, and place of residence) to a lower screening participation rate.[2829] BRFSS survey data consistently show that those with lower household incomes, those without health insurance, and the jobless have a lower prevalence of CRC screening.[28] Regardless of the screening technique employed, lower education—defined in various research as having fewer years of schooling or less than a high school education—has also been identified as a screening obstacle.[3031] In a European study with 953 persons at average risk, those with higher educational levels had a frequency of using CRC screening that was more than four times higher.[32] Contradictory findings have been observed in Europe, despite the fact that living in an urban environment has been linked in US studies to higher rates of screening uptake.[33] For instance, two Spanish studies[32] revealed no correlation between the region of residence and screening uptake or desire to be checked for CRC, although a Swedish study found increased screening uptake in rural areas.[34] In our study, we could not find any relationship with education, residence location and socioeconomic position in screening behaviors. This difference may be due to a lack of adequate education about the importance of colon cancer and the role of screening in its treatment, as the general public does not receive education on these issues.

Studies have demonstrated that married individuals are more likely to follow recommendations for healthy behavior.[35] In a large European study conducted in the UK, the authors discovered that married couples had higher screening attendance rates and were more likely to participate in screening programs than single people, even after adjusting for age and educational attainment.[36] Marital status was also a nonsignificant factor in our study. This difference may be because marriage has not played a role in the importance given to health in our society.

Higher screening participation rates have often been linked to a sense of increased risk of getting CRC. In one study, for instance, participants who indicated that their risk above the average risk population were more ready to undergo CRC screening than those who indicated that their risk was the same or lower.[3738] Furthermore, there is evidence linking unhealthy behaviors like smoking and inactivity to an increased sense of risk. In a similar vein, higher intention to engage has also been linked to the existence of comorbidities, anxiety, high body mass index, and gastrointestinal symptoms.[3940]

In the bivariate analysis of a systematic review, there was no significant difference in participation in CRC screening between individuals with and without depression and/or anxiety (72.0% vs. 68.0%). Similarly, after adjusting for sociodemographic and health-related factors, the study found that the odds of participating in CRC screening did not vary based on an individual’s depression status.[41] Although high anxiety score was a significant factor in our study.

There are a few studies in the Iranian population. Bidouei et al.[42] showed that there was no relationship between gender, health insurance status, family history of individuals and their knowledge about CRC with screening behaviors and also, employment, education and higher income had a positive effect on the perception of people toward CRC screening. In another systematic review, 367,248 subjects of 31 published articles were reviewed. According to the results, knowledge, income, being married and female, trust to physician and kind contact of health providers, counselling centers disorders were the main predictive factors for participation in the CRC screening program.[43]

Limitations

The lack of cooperation of patients and prevention of the continuation of the research participation process, failure to provide complete and accurate information are limitations of this project.

Conclusions

Understanding and incorporating patient preferences for CRC screening are critical to improve population adherence to CRC screening and to reach population health goals. Our study was designed to evaluate factors affecting the screening of new CRC cases in our local province. Our study showed that higher age, higher anxiety score, and history of colorectal surgery were associated with higher rates of screening behaviors, especially colonoscopy. Further studies in local situations and multicenter basis are requirerd in order to generalize our findings.
