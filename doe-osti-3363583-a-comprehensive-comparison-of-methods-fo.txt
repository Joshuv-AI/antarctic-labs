Abstract
Long-duration energy storage (LDES) could play a pivotal role in modern electricity grids. The
weather- and time-dependent nature of generation from variable energy (VE) technologies
introduces challenges for grid balancing, stability, and use optimization. LDES—along with
short-duration energy storage (SDES)—can help address these issues by shifting excess energy
to non-generation periods, which maximizes the cost-effectiveness and load matching. Modeling
LDES in production cost models (PCM) is particularly challenging due to the need for high
temporal resolution over extended optimization windows while preserving chronology, which
ensures the optimal energy storage operation over multiday periods. This report compares
traditional dispatch methods with advanced LDES dispatch strategies across different PCM
platforms and examines trade-offs and scalability. The comparison reveals that the traditional 1-
day optimization horizon within the PCM leads to inefficient utilization of LDES. In contrast,
extending the optimization horizon as much as possible significantly improves storage dispatch.
There is also promise in using state-of-charge (SOC) or end volume targets set by an external
model; however, this requires an additional modeling step and generally increases computational
burden. This report presents a comparison of these various methods across a variety of different
power systems, starting with small test systems and scaling up to large, country-wide
simulations. Overall, this research presents the trade-offs of various computational methods and
illustrates how LDES may play an important role in power systems of the future.

Executive Summary
Long-duration energy storage (LDES) could play a pivotal role in modern electricity grids. The
weather- and time-dependent nature of generation from variable energy (VE) technologies
introduces challenges for grid balancing, stability, and use optimization. LDES—along with
short-duration energy storage (SDES)—can help address these issues by shifting excess energy
to non-generation periods, which maximizes the cost-effectiveness and load matching. This
report evaluates various LDES dispatch methodologies and their impacts on system operations,
costs, and the associated computational challenges.

Electricity systems of the future will likely require effective coordination between LDES and
other power systems assets. However, modeling LDES in production cost models (PCMs) is
particularly challenging due to the need for high temporal resolution over extended optimization
windows while preserving chronology, which ensures optimal energy storage operations over
multiday periods. This report compares traditional dispatch methods with advanced LDES
dispatch strategies across different PCM platforms and examines trade-offs and scalability.

Our findings show that the traditional 1-day optimization horizon within the PCM leads to
inefficient utilization of LDES. In contrast, extending the optimization horizon as much as
possible improves storage dispatch and thus reduces total power systems costs.

Key findings:

 • Extended horizon method outperforms traditional approaches but at a
 computational cost: The extended horizon method showed significant improvements in
 LDES dispatch, leading lower system operation costs. However, it requires substantially
 more computational resources, which may limit its practical application in real-time grid
 operations. We did find that one way to utilize extended horizons while keeping
 computational burdens low is to employ additional aggregation in the look-ahead
 window. The extent to which look-ahead can be used without compromising the solution
 is system-dependent and requires fine-tuning.
 • Using a simple, exogenous model to feed state-of-charge (SOC) targets to a fully
 detailed PCM is promising: We also considered using exogenous models to feed SOC
 targets to the PCM, which performs nearly as well as some extended horizon methods,
 but requires an additional modeling step in the workflow. We explored using a simple
 price-taker model to set these exogenous targets, as well as a simplified, highly
 aggregated version of the annual PCM.
 • LDES reduces total generation costs through more cost-efficient dispatch: Although
 various dispatch methodologies performed better than others, LDES regardless performs
 a useful system service by aligning load with available generation, which increases
 utilization of the lowest marginal cost resources and reduces total generation costs. We
 also find that LDES has an extremely high capacity credit (it has a high level of
 availability during periods of high system net load) regardless of dispatch routine.
 • Computational challenges remain a barrier to scalability: While the extended horizon
 method mitigates some of the inefficiencies of traditional dispatch approaches, it
 introduces significant computational challenges, with longer solve times and increased

 v

 memory requirements. Further research is needed to make this method scalable for larger
 systems.
This report highlights the role that LDES can play in the affordability and stability of future
power systems. The comparison of dispatch methodologies across PCMs underscores the trade-
offs between computational efficiency and improved LDES performance. Future research could
focus on refining LDES dispatch strategies, developing more-sophisticated modeling techniques,
and addressing the computational challenges that limit the scalability of extended optimization
horizons. These advancements can contribute to cost-effective and reliable grid operations in
energy systems of the future.

1 Introduction
Long-duration energy storage (LDES) technologies could play a pivotal role in modernizing
electricity grids. The weather- and time-dependent nature of generation from variable energy
(VE) technologies introduces challenges for grid balancing, stability, and use optimization. As
electrical demand and prices increase, the ability of LDES to store and dispatch energy over
extended periods in a more cost-effective manner becomes increasingly critical. LDES
encompasses a range of technologies that can store energy from several hours to multiple days,
thus providing a reliable solution to bridge the gap between energy generation and consumption.

The energy system is evolving at a fast pace due in part to significant cost reductions in solar
photovoltaic (PV), wind power, and utility-scale batteries. These factors have led to an increase
in the share of VE sources in the United States and worldwide (EIA 2023; IRENA 2022).
Concurrently, declining costs for various energy storage technologies have spurred interest in the
role of storage as a grid asset, particularly in facilitating the adoption of variable energy sources
(Frazier et al. 2021). Among these technologies, lithium-ion batteries have seen the most
pronounced cost reductions, suggesting that near-term deployment of grid-scale storage
technologies will predominantly feature batteries with relatively short durations, typically less
than 10 hours (Denholm et al. 2021a). However, as VE continues to rise, longer-duration storage
technologies are being considered to address extended mismatches between energy supply and
demand. Longer-duration storage can also offer higher capacity value, which is associated with
its ability to supply energy during peak load periods and contribute to resource adequacy
requirements (Guerra 2021). Load-serving entities are beginning to understand the value of
LDES within their systems (Zhang et al. 2020; Guerra et al. 2024).

In the past decades, numerous studies have demonstrated operational synergies between solar PV
and diurnal storage (with durations less than 6 hours) due to the predictable daily patterns of
solar generation (Jorgenson et al. 2022; Denholm et al. 2021b; Denholm and Mai 2019). Short-
duration energy storage (SDES) systems charge during the day when solar power is abundant
and discharge during evening or morning load ramps when solar generation is not available.
However, the optimal dispatch strategies for LDES might be similar, but are less clear. A critical
consideration is the decision-making process regarding whether to use stored energy for intra-
day energy arbitrage or to reserve it for potentially higher-value periods in the future, such as for
inter-day energy arbitrage or reliability needs (Guerra et al. 2024). Production cost models
(PCMs) are simulation tools that can evaluate the potential role of such resources by optimizing
unit commitment and economic dispatch within an electricity system. However, PCMs typically
operate on short-term horizons, often one to two days, to align with day-ahead energy markets,
limiting their ability to fully capture the inter-temporal value of storage operation (Guerra et al.
2024).

Modeling LDES effectively is fundamental for optimizing its operation and maximizing its
benefits within power systems. However, several challenges arise in this endeavor, primarily due
to the need for high chronological temporal resolution and the complexity of power system
dynamics. LDES requires models that can capture detailed temporal variations in both energy
generation and consumption, encompassing not only daily cycles but also longer-term
fluctuations spanning multiple days or even weeks. Accurately representing the interactions

 1

between LDES and other power system components is inherently complex (de Guibert,
Shirizadeh, and Quirion 2020; Zhang et al. 2020; Bistline et al. 2020; Twitchell, DeSomber, and
Bhatnagar 2023). Models must account for complexities and interdependencies within the system
to provide reliable dispatch solutions. High-resolution models that accurately represent LDES
operations can be computationally intensive, making it a significant challenge to balance the
trade-offs between model accuracy and computational efficiency.

To better understand these various topics, this report sets out to examine the role of LDES in
future power systems and examine various methodologies for dispatch and valuation. The
contributions of this report are as follows:

 • Comprehensive evaluation of LDES dispatch strategies: The report assesses four
 dispatch methods—extended horizon, end volume targets, variable time steps, and stored
 energy value—across small-scale and large-scale power grid simulations, identifying the
 extended horizon method as the most effective for improving dispatch accuracy. There
 can be computational difficulties under extended horizons, but certain simplifications can
 improve outcomes.
 • System-wide comparison of LDES and SDES: Through extensive simulations on the
 U.S. zonal grid, the report highlights the operational value, capacity credit, and
 equivalent storage cycles of LDES compared to SDES, demonstrating the enhanced
 performance of LDES under various dispatch routines.
 • Methodological advancement in production cost modeling: By introducing the
 extended horizon method, the report improves the ability of PCMs to capture the inter-
 temporal value of LDES, offering a solution that reduces degeneracy and integrates
 seamlessly into large-scale systems.
 • Insights into the advantages of long-duration storage: The report provides a detailed
 analysis of the benefits LDES offers over shorter-duration storage, including higher
 capacity value during peak demand periods and increased utilization of the lowest-
 marginal cost resources on the system and contribution to enhanced grid reliability.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes various LDES dispatch
methods and the advantages and shortfalls of each method. Section 3 introduces the
mathematical formulation of PCM, LDES, and the proposed LDES dispatch methods. Section 4
presents the performance comparison of the four methods on both small-scale and U.S.-scale
power systems. In Section 5, we apply the same methods on a large power system, a possible
continental U.S. evolution through 2035 under four different scenarios. Section 6 concludes the
paper and proposes future research directions.

2 LDES Optimal Dispatch Modeling Methods Review
This report comprehensively evaluates four primary methods of LDES dispatch. These four
methods are discussed in detail in this section: (1) extended horizon, (2) end volume targets,
(3) variable time steps, and (4) stored energy value.

Each method aims to improve the accuracy of storage dispatch by better aligning storage
operations with system needs, while also addressing computational constraints. We then evaluate
these methods in terms of economic, operational, and computational metrics across two test

 2

power systems: a small-scale system (5-bus and Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers
[IEEE] Reliability Test System [RTS]) and a U.S. zonal grid.

2.1 Extended Horizon
A primary issue when modeling energy storage in day-ahead power system optimization models
is the draining of the storage devices at the end of the simulated time frame or optimization
horizon because no value is placed on storing energy for usage outside of that time frame. Many
studies include a period of one day for their optimization window (DOE 2021; Greg Brinkman,
Novacheck, and Ho 2021; Jorgenson, Hale, and Cowiestoll 2020). A simple solution to the
problem of end-of-period storage effects would be to extend the optimization horizon to consider
more than one day at time. For long-duration storage, this might be several days, a week, or a
month. However, increasing the optimization horizon often increases the number of variables
and equations in the PCM formulation, which can dramatically increase the computation time.
Additionally, some long-duration storage may require even longer operational time horizons,
such as months. Extending the optimization horizon quickly becomes increasingly difficult to
solve on all but the most trivial systems.

Another similarly straightforward approach to valuing storage beyond the optimization time
frame discussed in the previous paragraph is to add some foresight (also often called a look-
ahead window), as shown in Figure 1. This approach passes more information to the
optimization solver to present a more complete picture of the near-term future value of stored
energy. Often, the look-ahead window may be optimized at a less-resolved time resolution or
include fewer constraints than during the primary optimization horizon to maintain
computational tractability. Many studies include one (Brinkman, Novacheck, and Ho 2021;
Jorgenson, Hale, and Cowiestoll 2020; Steinberg et al. 2021) or two days (DOE 2021; Jorgenson
et al. 2022) for this look-ahead window. The end effects can then be reduced by passing the state
of charge (SOC) or energy volume to the next time frame or model run (Niet 2020; Henze,
Dodier, and Krarti 1997). One key limitation or condition of this approach is that the look-ahead
time frame should be longer than the expected operational time frame of the storage device (Niet
2020). Thus, the required look-ahead time frame depends on the energy capacity or duration of
the storage technologies to be modeled, and the system needs. Therefore, this approach may not
be practical for modeling long-duration or seasonal storage technologies due to the increased
computational requirements. In summary, the capability of this approach to modeling long-
duration and seasonal energy storage requires a better fundamental understanding of the
computational efficiency versus model fidelity trade-offs, including a more comprehensive
analysis of the impacts of different look-ahead windows on the value of energy storage across
different timescales.

2.2 End Volume Targets
The end volume targets method (Guerra et al. 2020; Deane, McKeogh, and Gallachoir 2013)
uses an exogenous storage dispatch model (perhaps using a simplified production cost approach
or price-taker assumptions) or a stochastic optimization model to optimize the operation of
storage technologies in what can be termed a medium- or mid-term (MT) simulation phase.
Then, the optimal storage dispatch from the simplified MT phase is input as SOC or energy
volume targets for the standard PCM run. These external targets may apply on a daily, weekly,
or even monthly basis, depending on the formulation of the PCM run. These targets, which could
be based on heuristics, are used as constraints to enforce the long-duration or seasonal dispatch
behavior of energy storage, while keeping the flexibility to optimize intra-day operation of the
storage devices in the unit commitment and economic dispatch model, as illustrated in Figure 2.
Note that this approach does not require changes to the standard power system production cost
modeling. Optimization of unit commitment and economic dispatch for 1 day-ahead plus 1 day
look-ahead is generally in line with current day-ahead electricity markets. The main drawback to
this approach is that the MT production cost, storage dispatch, or stochastic models may not
accurately represent power system details (transmission constraints, operating reserves, ramp
constraints, etc.) which could impact the deployment and operation of energy storage
technologies. This approach also requires an additional model solution, potentially increasing
total solve times but has well-documented use cases, particularly in handling hydropower
dispatch in Brazil, Norway, and the western United States (Guerra et al. 2020; Helseth et al.
2023).

 seasonal energy storage.

A previous study used a multi-model approach for the implementation of the end volume targets
method for a system that had long-duration or seasonal storage power capacity designed to
represent around 1% of the peak load of the power system, while different energy capacities or
discharge durations were evaluated, including 1 day, 2 days, 1 week, 2 weeks, and 1 month
(Zhang et al. 2020; Guerra et al. 2020). A PCM performed chronological simulation of annual
power system operations without any long-duration storage and using hourly temporal resolution
and a 1 day-ahead optimization window (365 sequential 1-day, i.e., 24-hour, runs for each year).
After this first PCM run, the resulting net load and locational marginal prices for a given region
were used to optimize the operation of a long-duration or seasonal storage device using a price-
taker model with hourly temporal resolution and a 1-year optimization window. Based on the
optimal storage dispatch from the price-taker model, SOC targets were estimated for the end of
each day. Finally, the long-duration or seasonal storage device was included in the PCM with
end volume targets represented as constraints to enforce the long-duration or seasonal dispatch of
the storage device while optimizing the hourly operation of the storage device based on the
optimal unit commitment and economic dispatch (second PCM run). The outputs were analyzed
to evaluate the corresponding total system value, including operational value and capacity value.

2.3 Variable Time Steps
The variable time step approach (de Guibert, Shirizadeh, and Quirion 2020) uses a variable time
step to reduce the complexity of the unit commitment and economic dispatch problems by
aggregating some consecutive hours, while keeping the associated chronology between time
steps. To this end, critical periods in which hourly temporal resolution is required (typically,
peak load or peak net load) are identified a priori and the remaining hours are aggregated based
on a given daily sub-sampling strategy, as shown in Figure 3. This variable time step approach
allows for the optimization of unit commitment and economic dispatch decisions over longer
optimization windows, 1- or multi-year time frames, using chronological time steps. Thus,
multiscale energy storage deployment and operation can be integrated into the economic and unit

 5

commitment dispatch formulations. However, it is difficult to identify critical periods ex ante.
For example, critical periods could depend on the deployment of energy storage and other
flexibility options. Moreover, this approach assumes that system operators will dispatch the
power system based on the unit commitment and economic dispatch optimization for the entire
year simultaneously, assuming perfect foresight of the power system generation capacity and
needs for the entire year. However, this assumption contrasts with the current electricity market
settings, typically taking place on a 1 day-ahead dispatch schedule. Moreover, the selected
critical periods could depend on the load, wind generation, and solar PV generation time series
or input data, which could vary across scenarios used in production cost modeling studies. Thus,
the implementation of the variable time step approach can be a challenge, particularly for
production cost modeling studies that involve sensitivities around load, wind generation, and
solar generation data. Note that the variable time step approach is a specific time-series
aggregation approach that preserves the chronology of the variable time steps. A more
comprehensive and generic overview of time-series aggregation approaches for modeling
energy systems is presented in the literature (Teichgraeber and Brandt 2022).

 Figure 3. Variable time-step approach for modeling long-duration and seasonal energy storage.
 Source: de Guibert, Shirizadeh, and Quirion 2020.

This approach has been applied to investment and dispatch power system models for France and
Germany (de Guibert, Shirizadeh, and Quirion 2020). First, seven critical periods were defined
based on the peak net load. The durations of these critical periods varied between 1 hour and 95
hours. The results in terms of system cost, renewable generation, storage operation, and
computational time were compared with those of the original model formulation which
employed chronological hourly resolution. The test showed that the variable time step approach
allows for a computational time reduction of up to 60-fold, while the error in total system cost,
renewable generation, and storage deployment and generation is equal or lower than 1%, 2%,
and 20%, respectively. However, 1% total system cost or production cost could be equivalent to
billions of dollars for large-scale power systems.

2.4 Stored Energy Value Method
The stored energy value method is a straightforward yet powerful approach for optimizing the
dispatch of LDES. This method assigns a marginal future value to the energy stored within the
system, expressed in monetary terms ($/MWh). This value represents the benefit of storing an
additional unit of energy, which can guide the charging and discharging decisions of storage
devices. The fundamental principle of the stored energy value method is to apply a value to the
stored energy, which determines when the storage device should charge or discharge. If the
marginal price of energy is lower than the assigned value of the stored energy (considering

 6

efficiency losses), the storage device charges to store the cheaper energy. Conversely, if the
marginal price is higher than the stored energy value, the device discharges to capitalize on the
higher energy prices. This dynamic helps ensure that the storage device operates optimally
without the need for long-term optimization horizons or complex variable time steps.

Figure 4. Stored energy value approach (applied at the end of the day) for modeling long-duration
 and seasonal energy storage.
One significant advantage of the stored energy value method is its simplicity and reduced
computational demand. Unlike methods that require extended optimization windows or time
aggregation, the stored energy value method operates effectively within standard PCMs without
adding significant computational overhead. This simplicity makes it an attractive option for real-
time energy storage dispatch, particularly in systems with high levels of VE and with LDES and
even seasonal storage. A case study involving high amounts of variable generation in Los
Angeles demonstrated the efficacy of the stored energy value method (Cochran et al. 2021). By
adding a value to the stored energy, storage devices were encouraged to charge using zero-
marginal-cost energy, such as excess generation from renewables, and maintain a higher SOC at
the end of the optimization window. This behavior was achieved without any foresight into
future system conditions.

However, the implementation of the stored energy value method is not without challenges. A
critical aspect of this method is determining the appropriate value to apply to the stored energy.
This value can vary significantly, depending on factors such as location, season, the prevalence
of zero-marginal-cost resources, and natural gas prices. An incorrect value can lead to
suboptimal dispatch, either causing the storage device to rarely discharge or to miss opportunities
for future value by holding onto stored energy. Additionally, there is a risk of "over-
dispatching," where the storage device continuously responds to fluctuating prices, potentially
leading to increased operational strain without providing commensurate benefits.

In conclusion, the stored energy value method provides a pragmatic and efficient approach for
LDES dispatch, balancing simplicity with operational effectiveness. While it requires careful
consideration of the value assigned to stored energy, its ability to operate within standard
production cost models and its effectiveness in real-time applications make it a valuable tool for
supporting reliability of modern power systems. Future research could focus on refining the
methodologies for determining stored energy value and exploring the long-term impacts of this
approach on system reliability and cost-efficiency.

4 Evaluation of Methodology
4.1 System Description
Although we have thus far identified several methods for dispatching LDES, the appropriate
approach might vary based on the questions to be answered. To better examine the tradeoffs
between the various methodologies, we perform simulations on two modified test power
systems: the PJM 5-bus (Fangxing Li and Rui Bo 2010) and the RTS Grid Modernization Lab
Consortium (RTS-GMLC) test system (Subcommittee 1979; Barrows et al. 2020). The PJM 5-
bus test system, as configured for this work has five buses, three load centers, six transmission
lines, and eight generators. For the purposes of this study, we include two solar PV facilities and
one wind facility, along with one SDES device (with 80% round-trip efficiency and 4 hours of
duration), and one LDES device (with 65% round-trip efficiency and 10 hours of duration). The
RTS-GMLC test system, which is an updated version of the RTS-96 test system, has 74 buses,
14 load centers, 121 transmission lines, and 159 generators. This includes 57 solar PV facilities
and 4 wind facilities, 1 SDES device (with 80% roundtrip efficiency and 4 hours of duration),
and 1 LDES device (with 65% roundtrip efficiency and 15 hours of duration). As discussed
previously, the integration of zero-marginal-cost VE resources provides opportunities and
challenges for long-duration storage dispatch, so we increased wind, solar PV, and energy
storage resources to create systems that have a 70%−90% VE annual energy mix based on the
annual load. Single-year synchronous weather data was used for wind and solar PV profiles. The
installed generation capacity is shown in Figure 5 for each system and for two cases: one
dominated by solar PV and the other by wind power. Moreover, regulation up and spinning
contingency reserve constraints were included for all tests systems and cases. For the PJM 5-bus
test system, regulation up reserves are 5% of load and spinning up reserves are the sum of 5% of
load, 10% of wind availability (forecast), and 4% of solar PV availability (forecast). Reserve
constraints for the RTS-GMLC tests system are based on the time series for each reserve product
provided by a previous study (Barrows et al. 2020).

4.2 Scenarios
The following storage modeling approaches were tested to evaluate their capabilities and
limitations: (1) extended optimization horizon, (2) end volume targets, and (3) stored energy
value. These methods were compared with the ideal solution approach, defined as a 1-year
optimization horizon with hourly resolution with perfect foresight. The solution of the linear
programing (LP) relaxation of the ideal case was also included as a reference, with a lower
bound on the production cost and upper bound on the production cost reductions, for cases where
the ideal solution is not available. However, the LP solution is likely infeasible/unrealistic
because of the relaxation of unit commitment constraints. Thus, the LP solution should not be
used to assess the performance of any dispatch approach. Additionally, the variable time step
approach was excluded because the implementation of this approach could depend on the input
data (load time series, wind generation, solar PV generation, and wind to solar PV generation
mix), which makes this approach less attractive from a practical viewpoint, as described
previously in Section 2.4.

We also evaluated computational and LDES operational aspects, including required CPU time,

4.3.1 Parameters Under Consideration:
Key parameters reported for each scenario are as follows:

 • TPC is an annual sum of startup-shutdown cost, fuel cost, and variable operation and
 maintenance cost. It is measured in billions of USD. The TPC forms most of the
 objective function (remaining part is the penalty associated with the dropped load) of the
 PCM.

 • Computational metrics such as maximum peak memory usage (measured in gigabytes
 [GB]) and compute time (measured in hours) quantify the computational resource
 requirements for different methodologies. As the name suggests, maximum peak memory
 usage corresponds to the maximum of peak memory amongst 12 months. The compute
 time is calculated by adding the computational time for all 12 months.

 • VE curtailment is calculated as a ratio of solar and wind energy curtailed to the available
 solar and wind energy. It is expressed as a percentage. Individual solar and wind

 15

 curtailment can be obtained by considering respective technology’s curtailment and
 available energy.

 • VE integration is estimated by the fraction of the energy requirement met by solar and
 wind generation. The energy requirement of the grid is the sum of load (including storage
 losses).

 • Energy storage is characterized by equivalent storage cycles, providing a metric to
 understand the extent of storage cycling. It is the ratio of the total energy discharged by
 storage to the energy capacity of the storage.

 • Storage performance is also evaluated by calculating the capacity factor-based capacity
 credit. Capacity factor-based capacity credit is the ratio of the total energy supplied by a
 storage device during critical period (in this case, top 10 net-load hours) to the maximum
 possible energy that could have been supplied by the same storage device during the
 critical period. The average capacity credit values in this report are the average of the
 capacity credits for individual storage categories.

4.4 Quantitative Results
This section summarizes the results for the tested LDES dispatch approaches, based on the
metrics defined in the previous section. First, based on the assumption of no transmission
constraints, we evaluated the potential system value of modeling LDES based on the traditional
(24 hours optimization window + 24 hours look-ahead horizon) and the ideal (8,760-hour
optimization window with no look-ahead) methods. Note that the ideal case assumes perfect
foresight of VE generation, load, and storage needs for the entire year and, therefore, does not
provide the desired level of realism but is a useful basis for comparison. Then, we compare the
performance of each dispatch method in terms of system value (operational value and capacity
value) and computational requirements (CPU time and peak memory usage). We then illustrate
how including transmission constraints and VE uncertainty could impact the performance of the
most promising LDES dispatch approach.

4.4.1 The Potential System Value of Modeling Long-Duration Energy Storage
First, we evaluate the potential value of LDES by comparing the ideal (ID) case and the
traditional (TR) cases, assuming perfect foresight. Overall, the ideal LDES dispatch has the
potential to reduce power system production cost by 4%−14%, relative to the traditional method
(shown in Figure 6). These cost reductions are mostly driven by fuel cost savings, which indicate
an effective use of LDES to displace expensive thermal generators, including peaking
conventional generators.

 16

  Figure 6. Production cost breakdown for PJM 5- bus (a) and RTS-GMLC (b) solar PV- and wind-
 driven test power systems.
The cost reduction as mentioned above is mainly driven by higher usage of lower-marginal cost
resources. In this case, VE is the lowest marginal cost resource ($0/MWh). Therefore, underlying
the results in Figure 6 is an increase in VE generation. As shown in Figure 7, ideal LDES
dispatch increases VE contribution by 1.2%−5% while decreasing VE curtailment by
1.3%−3.3% (using otherwise curtailed zero-marginal cost energy to charge storage). Thus, the
ideal LDES dispatch has the potential to facilitate the integration of VE by allowing a more
effective utilization of LDES devices by allowing for more inter-day VE shifting, while reducing
the need for power generation from thermal power plants. Note that there is high VE curtailment
for the wind-driven cases. This could be because the storage power capacities and durations
assumed in this study were not optimized for the cases evaluated in this study. However, the high
VE curtailment is consistent across the results from both traditional and ideal dispatch
approaches.

 17

Figure 7. VE integration and curtailment for PJM 5- bus (a) and RTS-GMLC (b) solar PV- and wind-
 driven test power systems
The ideal LDES dispatch approach also increases the LDES standard capacity credit (based on
the top 10 peak net load hours) from 75% to 97% and from 58% to 72% for the PJM 5-bus and
RTS-GMLC solar PV-driven test power systems, respectively. The results for wind-driven
systems are similar (an increase of the standard capacity credit from 42% to 68% and from 52%
to 86% for the PJM 5-bus and RTS-GMLC test power systems, respectively). Note that
regardless of the test system and the energy mix, the SOC-aware peak net load-based capacity
credit for LDES was always 100% for the ideal dispatch approach. In contrast, regardless of the
test system and energy mix, the ideal LDES dispatch approach reduces the standard capacity
credit of SDES. For example, the standard capacity credit of SDES is reduced from 75% to 73%
and from 79% to 67% for the PJM 5-bus and RTS-GMLC solar PV-driven test power systems,
respectively. Similarly, these standard capacity credit reductions are from 68% to 59% and from
64% to 28% for the PJM 5-bus and RTS-GMLC test power systems, respectively.

Note also that, regardless of the test system and the energy mix, the SOC-aware peak net load-
based capacity credit for SDES was greater than or equal to 73% for the ideal dispatch approach.

Further, regardless of the test system and energy mix, the ideal LDES dispatch approach
increases the standard capacity credit of total energy storage capacity (combined SDES and
LDES) (e.g., an increase between 8.8% and 15.7% on the standard capacity credit of the total
energy storage capacity).

Moreover, for the traditional dispatch approach, LDES provides between 92% and 100% of the
total required reserves during the top 10 net load hours, depending on the test system and energy
mix. Whereas the LDES reserve provision for the ideal dispatch approach is between 90% and
100%. Additionally, the ideal dispatch allows for a better utilization of the LDES (e.g., the
equivalent annual discharge cycles increase by up to 37% when compared with the traditional
dispatch). Note that these results are based on the assumption of perfect foresight of VE
generation, load, and storage needs for the traditional and ideal dispatch approaches. In practice,
these parametric uncertainties are gradually revealed and therefore the assumption of perfect

 18

foresight could result in underestimation of power system flexibility needs, which could impact
the estimated value of modeling LDES.

Overall, we find marked reductions in production cost from the ideal LDES dispatch case
compared to the traditional LDES dispatch case. However, as the ideal LDES dispatch is
computationally infeasible for real-world systems, we use this as an upper bound for the possible
value of LDES and can serve as a benchmark for comparing other dispatch routes, as discussed
below.

4.4.2 The Trade-Off Between Improved Representation and Computational
 Complexity in Modeling Long-Duration Energy Storage
Using the ideal and traditional dispatch methods as benchmarks, this section considers the
performance of other dispatch methods. Regardless of the test power system and the VE mix, the
extended optimization horizon or window of foresight and the end volume targets dispatch
approaches always reduce the production cost of the power grid when compared with the
traditional dispatch approach (1 day-ahead plus 1 day look-ahead), as illustrated in Figure 8. For
instance, using the traditional dispatch approach as a baseline, depending on the power system
and the VE mix, extending the look-ahead horizon reduced the production cost by 3.5% to
13.8%, while the end volume target dispatch approach reduced the production cost by 0.1% and
12.8%. Note that adding 1 week of extended look-ahead horizon (ELH) achieves basically the
same production cost reduction as the ideal case. Note that storage devices with longer duration
could require longer look-ahead horizons. On the other hand, the energy value dispatch approach
could increase or decrease the production cost depending on the system and the VE mix. In this
case, the long-duration storage tends to be underutilized and thus results in worse performance.

 19

 b), and RTS-GMLC (c and d) solar PV- and wind-driven test power systems.
Both the extended window of foresight and the end volume targets dispatch approaches increase
the standard capacity credit of LDES regardless of the power system and the VE mix, as shown
in Figure 9. This is also true for the SOC-aware peak net load-based capacity credit. On the other
hand, the standard capacity credit of SDES decreases or increases depending on the power
system and the energy mix, but the standard capacity credit of total energy storage capacity
(combined SDES and LDES) tends to increase. Additionally, for the extended window of
foresight dispatch approaches, LDES provides between 18% and 100% of the total required

 20

reserves during the top 10 net load hours, depending on the test system and energy mix. The
LDES reserve provision is between 18.9% and 100% for the end volume targets dispatch
approaches. As with the production cost, the energy value approach could increase or reduce the
standard capacity credit of LDES, depending on the power system and the VE mix. Therefore,
from the system value standpoint, the extended optimization horizon and the end volume targets
dispatch approaches can improve the representation of LDES in power system dispatch models.
On the other hand, the energy value approach requires more tuning to find the proper parameters
that allow for a better dispatch of LDES.

Another important aspect when evaluating LDES dispatch approaches is the scalability of the
scheduling approaches measured in terms of computational requirements (e.g., CPU time and
peak memory usage). We observed that for the extended optimization horizon, the CPU time
increases exponentially with the length of the look-ahead horizon (particularly for the RTS-
GMLC tests power system), as shown in Figure 10. This could limit the scalability of the
extended optimization horizon or window of foresight, particularly for real-world applications
involving large-scale power systems with thousands of power plants, transmission lines, and

 22

buses. In contrast, the CPU times for the end volume targets and the energy value approaches are
similar to that of the traditional approach. Similar findings were observed when the peak
memory usage metric was used to assess the performance of the LDES dispatch approaches.
Thus, the end volume target and the energy value approaches do not significantly increase the
computational requirements of PCMs. However, for the MT scheduling-based end volume target
method (EVT-LA-MT), the MT model can overestimate the flexibility provided by thermal fleet
as it is ignoring commitment constraints and start-up and shutdown costs, which could limit the
performance of this approach in terms of improved system value for power systems with
moderately sized thermal fleets. Similarly, the use of a flat SOC target (EVT-LA method) can
sometimes over- or underestimate the required amount of energy going into the next day, which
limits the performance of the EVT method in terms of improved system value. On the other
hand, there is a trade-off between the improved representation and the computational
requirements associated with the extended optimization horizon (ELH-3d, ELH-1w, and ELH-
1m approaches).

Overall, this set of tests has highlighted that each of these LDES dispatch formulations have
certain strengths and limitations that users should be aware of before deciding which one to
utilize. For instance, an extended horizon is a very effective tool to approximate the ideal LDES
operation but suffers from scaling issues on larger systems, which could be addressed by
temporal decomposition methods (Guerra et al. 2024). Energy value is not as computationally
intensive to solve, although it requires some tuning of the energy value parameter. Using a static
value, however, can lead the model to over- or underestimate the value of stored energy. This
shortcoming can be addressed by using a time-varying energy value, but it does not address the
need to come up with a more robust methodology to determine the energy value for a given
system. One method that does strike a balance between approximating the value of LDES and
computational scaling is EVT-LA-MT, but the end volume targets (energy targets) are only as
good as the representation of system operations in the MT planning model.

4.4.3 The Effects of VE Uncertainty on the Performance of LDES Dispatch
 Approaches
Wind and solar PV resources are not only variable but also uncertain due to the nature of weather
and climate impacts. The uncertainty associated with VE power output could have a significant
impact on the operation of dispatchable generation/storage assets, including LDES. System
operators usually perform VE forecasts to attempt to mitigate the impacts of VE uncertainty in
the operation of power grids with significant shares of wind and solar PV. We tested the
performance of the two LDES dispatch approaches (extended optimization horizon and end
volume targets) in view of VE generation uncertainty for the RTS-GMLC solar PV- and wind-
driven test power systems. To this end, we created a synthetic day-ahead forecast for solar PV
and wind generation by drawing from a normal error distribution, with respective mean errors of
3% and 6%, for each time period in the look-ahead window. For the RTS-GMLC solar PV-
driven system, the system-level normalized root mean square errors for the day-ahead forecasts
are 13.0% for wind and 3.5% for solar PV, and the system level normalized mean absolute errors
are 10.5% for wind and 2.2% for solar PV. Similarly, for the RTS-GMLC wind-driven system,
the system level normalized root mean square errors for the day-ahead forecasts are 11.9% for
wind and 3.4% for solar PV, and the system level normalized mean absolute errors are 9.5% for
wind and 2.1% for solar PV. These day-ahead forecast errors are comparable with those from
previous work (Hodge et al. 2018; Lew et al. 2013).

The RTS-GMLC test power system was tested using a copper plate transmission representation
in view of VE generation uncertainty with the following LDES dispatch methods: extended
window of foresight with 3 days look-ahead (ELH-3d) and the MT-based end volume target
method (EVT-LA-MT). The day-ahead VE forecast is used in different ways depending on the
dispatch method. For example, for the traditional 1 day-ahead plus 1 day look-ahead and ELH-3d
dispatch approaches, the forecast is used for the look-ahead windows only (e.g., assuming
perfect foresight for the 1 day-ahead window). For the EVT-LA-MT dispatch approach, the day-
ahead forecast is used for the MT model that provides the end volume targets, while the
traditional dispatch model assumes perfect foresight for the 1 day-ahead period and the VE
forecast for the 1-day look-ahead windows. With this accounting of uncertainty and for the
copper plate power system representation, the LDES dispatch approaches could reduce
production cost by 8.4% to 12.2% with respect to the traditional dispatch (see Table 2), which is
comparable with the results for the copper plate representation with perfect foresight.

In general, the two LDES dispatch approaches increase the standard capacity credit of total
energy storage capacity (combined SDES and LDES) when compared with the traditional
dispatch approach. Note that, regardless of the energy mix, the SOC-aware peak net load-based
capacity credit for LDES is greater than or equal to 84% for the ELH-3d and EVT-LA-MT
dispatch approaches (Table 2). Additionally, for the ELH-3d dispatch approach, LDES provides
between 74.3% and 80.9% of the total required reserves during the top 10 net load hours,
depending on the energy mix. In contrast, the LDES reserve provision is between 34.5% and
89.6% for the EVT-LA-MT dispatch approach. Depending on the model and the energy mix, for
the ELH-3d dispatch approach SDES provides between 13.7% and 23.3% of the total required
reserves during the top 10 net load hours. The SDES reserve provision is between 3% and 54.3%
for the EVT-LA-MT dispatch approach. These results, which assume perfect foresight for the
day-ahead period and a VE forecast for the look-ahead window, clearly demonstrate the value of
more appropriate LDES dispatch approaches in view of VE generation uncertainties. To fully

 25

capture the value of LDES dispatch approaches considering power system operational
uncertainty, load, wind, and solar PV forecasts across multiple timescales can be used to provide
the best information available to the dispatch algorithms.

number of time steps, increasing the level of aggregation is not fruitful beyond a certain limit.
This limit is decided by the characteristics of the system. For example, for a solar-driven system,

 32

look-ahead time-aggregation beyond 3-4 hours results in worse storage operations than the
traditional case. On the other hand, for a wind-driven system, a significant reduction in the
production cost can be obtained with lower solve times through high levels of look-ahead time
aggregation (up to 8 hours).

5 Understanding the Scalability of the Extended
Horizon Approach
The results in Section 4 demonstrate that using extended modeling horizons results in the most
cost-optimal dispatch of storage compared to the other methods considered here. However, we
also observe that extended modeling horizons can impact solvability and may not be feasible in
realistically sized power systems models. In order to evaluate the efficacy of longer horizons on
large power systems, we focus on a zonal power system that encompasses the entire continental
U.S. based on results from a national-scale capacity expansion model. This section discusses
methodology and results evaluating a future 2035 power system containing substantial amounts
of VE, SDES, and LDES.

5.1 Description of 2035 Scenarios
The Regional Energy Deployment System (ReEDS™) is the National Laboratory of the Rockies’
flagship capacity expansion model that uses least-cost optimization to simulate the evolution and
operation of the generation, transmission, and end-use demand technologies of the U.S. bulk
power system (Ho et al. 2021). Key inputs include existing and planned capacity, temporal and
spatial VE availability, state and federal policies, hourly U.S. load projections, technology-
specific cost projections (capital, operations and maintenance, fuel), and technology and
performance projections. ReEDS then optimizes total capital and operational system costs to
determine the portfolio of generation, storage, and transmission expansion that satisfy the
constraints for each solve year. ReEDS has been used for numerous studies exploring the
evolution of the electric power system (Denholm et al. 2022; Frazier et al. 2021; Ardani et al.,
n.d.; Gregory Brinkman et al. 2024; U.S. Department of Energy 2024)

ReEDS divides the contiguous United States into 134 balancing areas to perform its
optimization, as shown in Figure 17. These balancing areas exist as an aggregation of U.S.
counties, and thus can be aggregated into various transmission regions, as also shown in Figure
17. These regions are based on the Federal Energy Regulatory Committee Order 1000
transmission planning regions and the Electric Reliability Council of Texas. They are used in this
analysis to aggregate results to a regional level.

5.2 Description of Modeling Methodology
Given the future scenarios provided by ReEDS (Figure 18), we use the translation tool ReEDS-
to-X to create PCM scenarios in PLEXOS (Sanchez Perez et al. 2024). We did use two
simplifications to support our goal of examining the dispatch of storage technologies. First, we
categorized all batteries as either SDES or LDES based on their discharge durations. Then, we
adjusted power ratings and efficiency settings to represent a single simplified technology for
each. Additionally, the capacities of all LDES and SDES for each model region were aggregated
such that the total capacities of all batteries are represented by one LDES unit and one SDES unit
to streamline unit commitment and economic dispatch formulation. Furthermore, to isolate the
effects of changing look-ahead periods and time aggregation solely on SDES and LDES
technologies, some dispatchable technologies such as ReEDS hydro-powered generation units
were held constant using predefined dispatch curves.

 37

Results from Section 4 were used to inform the development of a suite of PCM modeling
algorithms to be tested on the four scenarios from Figure 18. Details on the four methodologies
are outlined in Table 8. The first three cases represent the “extended look-ahead” methods, where
each utilize different combinations of time-aggregated look-ahead windows. “NoAgg” represents

no aggregation. “3D2A” utilizes a 2-hour time aggregation in its 3-day look-ahead window.

simplified PCM-informed MT simulation stage that passes on its storage dispatch as SOC targets
(or “volume” targets for a hydropower reservoir analog). This MT simplified step used 1-week
steps with 2-hour aggregation. These targets are then passed to the detailed PCM employing a
single day of look-ahead with no time aggregation.

6 Conclusions
This report presents a comprehensive analysis of LDES dispatch methodologies, revealing their
importance in future power systems. However, optimizing LDES within PCMs remains complex
due to the need for extended optimization windows and significant computational requirements.
The traditional short-term dispatch models currently in use are not well-suited to handle the
extended timescales required for LDES. To address this gap, the report presents various
methodologies to improve the dispatch of LDES. Each approach brings unique benefits and
challenges. The comparative analysis of these methodologies demonstrates the limitations of
traditional short-horizon optimization approaches. Dispatch strategies that optimize storage
operations on a daily basis fail to fully account for longer-term energy needs, resulting in the
inefficient use of LDES.

One method that we consistently find leads to better dispatch for LDES is considering extended
horizons in the dispatch period or with look-ahead. In particular, this method reduces overall
system costs. This cost reduction was driven by the strategic dispatch of LDES during periods of
high demand and low renewable generation, displacing expensive peaker plants. However, these
benefits must be weighed against a significant increase in computation times—over 300%
compared to traditional dispatch methods—underscoring the need for balancing operational
improvements with computational feasibility.

The report suggests that time aggregation, which involves grouping non-critical hours together to
reduce the number of time steps in the optimization process, could alleviate some of these
computational burdens. However, this approach must be carefully implemented, as inappropriate
aggregation could result in suboptimal LDES operations, especially in solar-driven systems
where generation patterns are highly variable. We present the methods in a clear, transparent
format that should be adaptable to different types of operational models, regardless of specific
tools, although the implementation will vary across platforms.

The report identifies several areas where future research is needed to advance LDES dispatch
optimization. First, reducing the computational complexity of extended horizon optimization is
essential for making this approach scalable and practical for large systems. Future research could
focus on developing more efficient time aggregation techniques and exploring machine learning
algorithms to predict optimal dispatch patterns based on historical data and real-time system
conditions. Although this information is presented here, it may need adjustment or modification
to implement across tools. Additionally, as the role of LDES in the power grid continues to
grow, there is a need to explore new energy storage technologies beyond lithium-ion batteries.
Technologies such as hydrogen storage, thermal energy storage, and iron-air batteries offer
promising solutions for LDES and could be a priority for future research. These technologies
could provide the flexibility needed for multiday or seasonal energy shifting, especially in
systems with high shares of renewable energy. Finally, as power systems become more
integrated with multiple energy vectors—such as electricity, hydrogen, and thermal energy—
research could explore how LDES can provide value across these different domains. Future
studies could investigate how LDES can be optimized to enhance the resilience and flexibility of
multi-energy systems, ensuring that future power grids can be reliable and affordable. By
advancing modeling techniques, exploring new storage technologies, and addressing the

 47

complexities of system integration, LDES can become a key enabler of a resilient, reliable
energy system capable of meeting the challenges of tomorrow’s power grids.
