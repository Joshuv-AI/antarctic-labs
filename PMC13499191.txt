ABSTRACT
Key Points•Anti-CCRL2 ADC shows prominent antileukemic effect in high-risk MDS/AML, including TP53-mutated in vitro and in vivo models.•Anti-CCRL2 ADC demonstrates no significant toxicity against healthy hematopoietic cells and systemic toxicity in mice.

Introduction

Patients with high-risk myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) and secondary acute myeloid leukemia (sAML) continue to exhibit poor outcomes, especially in the presence of high-risk features, such as TP53 mutations or deletions.1, 2, 3 Thus, the development of effective therapies is urgently needed for these individuals. Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs)4, 5, 6 show prominent activity in lymphoid cancers7, 8, 9 and good-risk AML10,11; however, they demonstrate relatively limited efficacy in high-risk MDS/AML.12 As a result, there are currently no available effective ADCs to be used as single-agent therapies or incorporated in combinational therapies for patients with these disease subsets.

The atypical chemokine receptor C-C motif chemokine receptor-like 2 (CCRL2) is normally expressed in differentiated myeloid cells, contributing to the regulation of cell migration in inflammation sites.13, 14, 15 This surface receptor is upregulated in MDS and sAML, whereas normal hematopoietic progenitors demonstrate minimal expression.16 CCRL2 silencing suppresses MDS/sAML cell growth and sensitizes them to hypomethylating agents but does not affect the survival and clonogenicity of healthy CD34+ cells.16,17 We recently discovered that across MDS/AML subtypes, TP53-mutated MDS/AML and AML with erythroid features express the highest CCRL2 levels. Intriguingly, TP53 deletion in TP53 wild-type (WT) AML cells caused a direct prominent CCRL2 upregulation.18 The main downstream target of CCRL2 is interferon gamma signaling, which has been associated with AML clonal evolution, TP53 deletion, acquisition of erythroid features, and treatment resistance.18, 19, 20 Thus, CCRL2 is potentially a promising target in high-risk MDS/sAML.

In this study, we show that an ADC targeting CCRL2 exhibits significant antileukemic activity in high-risk MDS/AML, including TP53-mutated cells, but has limited effect against normal hematopoietic cells and no significant systemic toxicity in murine models.

Material and methods

Cell lines and reagents

TF-1, SKM1, SET2, and MV4-11 cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection. F36P, OCI-AML3, and MOLM13 cells were purchased from Leibniz Institute DSMZ.21 Culture conditions and reagents are described in the supplemental Material and methods.

ADC generation

ADC generation is described in the supplemental Materials and methods. High-performance liquid chromatography methods and drug-antibody ratio calculations were described previously.22,23

MTT (3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) assay

TF-1, F36P, SET2, MOLM13, MV4-11, and SKM1 cells were plated in 96-well plates (7500 cells per well) and treated for 6 days with anti-CCRL2, anti-CD33, anti-CD123, gemtuzumab, and immunoglobulin G2a (IgG2a) antibodies. For the pretreatment experiment, cells were treated with 500nM anti-CCRL2 antibody or IgG2a for 2 hours before exposure to conjugated IgG2a or anti-CCRL2 ADC. The MTT assay methodology is described in the supplemental Material and methods.

Flow cytometry analysis

Flow cytometry analysis is described in the supplemental Material and methods.

Immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy

Antibodies are described in the supplemental Materials and methods. Cell fixation, permeabilization, staining methods, and coverslip mounting to slides using Prolong Gold Antifade with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) have been described previously.24, 25, 26 Images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM700 confocal microscope, cropped and adjusted for contrast using the Zeiss Zen software.

Western blotting

Protein collection and western blotting were performed as previously reported18 and are described in detail in the supplemental Materials and methods.

CCRL2 and TP53 knockout (KO)

Transfection and transduction are described in the supplemental Materials and methods.

Colony formation assay

Clonogenic assays were conducted as previously reported and are described in detail in the supplemental Materials and methods.17,18

Patients and sample processing

Specimen collection and processing are described in the supplemental Material and methods.

Treatment of C57BL/6 mice

Treatment of C57BL/6 mice is described in the supplemental Material and methods.

Cell line xenografts

Luciferase+ TF-1, SKM1, and F36P cells were injected into 8- to 10-week-old female NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NSG) mice (106 cells per mouse).16,17 Disease burden monitoring and treatments are described in the supplemental Material and methods.

Patient-derived multihit TP53-mutated MDS/AML xenografts

Mononuclear cells (MNCs) collected from marrow aspirates of multihit TP53-mutated patients with MDS/AML were injected into sublethally irradiated (1.5 Gy) 8- to 10-week-old female NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl Tg(CMV-IL3, CSF2, KITLG)1Eav/MloySzJ (NSGS) mice (5 × 105 cells per mouse). Bone marrow aspirates and peripheral blood were collected from mice on day 30, and based on disease burden, engrafted mice (≥2% human CD45+ [hCD45+] cells in bone marrow) were randomized to receive conjugated IgG2a or anti-CCRL2 ADC (1 mg/kg). On day 55, peripheral blood, spleen, liver, and bone marrow were collected from mice to assess disease burden by flow cytometry and immunohistochemical analysis. Institutional animal care and use committee guidelines were followed.

Immunohistochemistry

After preparation of paraffin-embedded bone marrow, spleen, and liver sections, slides were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin stain.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare the statistical significance of 2 groups. One-way analysis of variance was used for comparisons among 3 or more groups. Kaplan-Meier analysis and the Mantel-Cox log-rank test were used to compare mice survival. Standard deviation was used to assess centrality and dispersion.

Results

Anti-CCRL2 ADC is cytotoxic against MDS/AML cell lines

To identify the best payload for the development of an ADC with high activity against high-risk MDS/AML, 5 commonly used ADC payloads (mertansine, monomethyl auristatin E, exatecan, 7-ethyl-10-hydroxycamptothecin, and pyrrolobenzodiazepine [PBD]) were compared, and PBD showed the highest cytotoxicity against TP53-mutated TF-1, F36P, and SKM1 cells expressing high levels of CCRL2 (supplemental Figure 1A-C). This effect was not affected by CCRL2 expression based on the comparison of CCRL2 WT or KO cells (supplemental Figure 1D). Because our primary objective was the selective killing of CCRL2-expressing malignant myeloid cells with minimal off-target toxicity, we selected the highly potent PBD payload, whose in vitro activity allowed us to attribute cytotoxicity to CCRL2-dependent antibody binding rather than to bystander killing. The PBD dimer SG319927–conjugated ADC loncastuximab has received approval from the US Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of high-grade B-cell lymphomas.28,29 Thus, an anti-CCRL2 ADC was developed by conjugating a monoclonal antihuman CCRL2 antibody with PBD. A PBD-conjugated IgG2a antibody was used as a control. Conjugations were confirmed by high-performance liquid chromatography (supplemental Figure 2A-B), showing a drug-antibody ratio of ∼3.7 (supplemental Table 1).

To assess the selective efficacy of anti-CCRL2 ADC, CCRL2 WT and KO TF-1, F36P, and SET218 cells were treated with 0 to 300 ng/mL of ADCs for 6 days. Anti-CCRL2 ADC had significantly higher cytotoxicity in WT cells compared with KO cells and cells treated with conjugated IgG2a (Figure 1A-B; supplemental Figure 2C). Doxycycline-inducible CCRL2 TF-1 cells, which were genetically engineered to express CCRL2 under doxycycline,17 were used. The activity of anti-CCRL2 ADC was significantly higher in cells treated with doxycycline compared with untreated cells (Figure 1C). Finally, to confirm CCRL2-selective cytotoxicity, TF-1, F36P, and SKM1 cells were pretreated with 500nM anti-CCRL2 antibody or an IgG2a isotype control, followed by exposure to anti-CCRL2 ADC. Anti-CCRL2 ADC shows higher cytotoxicity against TP53-mutated MDS/AML cell lines compared with gemtuzumab and PBD-conjugated ADCs targeting CD33 and CD123

Next, to compare the efficacy of ADCs that target CCRL2 with the efficacy of ADCs against other targets, PBD-conjugated ADCs targeting the well-described AML targets CD33 and CD123 were developed (supplemental Figure 3A-B). Cytotoxicity of the calicheamicin-conjugated ADC gemtuzumab and the PBD-conjugated anti-CD33, anti-CD123, and anti-CCRL2 ADCs was assessed in AML cell lines derived from patients with de novo/TP53-WT AML (MOLM13, OCI-AML3, and MV4-11) and MDS/AML cell lines (TF-1 erythroleukemia cells, F36P MDS–related erythroleukemia cells, and SKM1 TP53–mutated MDS/AML cells). Consistent with our previously published data, CCRL2 expression is significantly higher in MDS/AML cell lines with erythroleukemic features and TP53 mutations compared with AML cell lines derived from patients with de novo/TP53 WT AML (supplemental Figure 3C). The expression of CD33 was higher in F36P and TF-1 cells compared with the rest of the cell lines tested (supplemental Figure 3C), whereas the expression of CD123 was overall higher in de novo/TP53 WT AML cell lines compared with those exhibiting erythroleukemic features and TP53 mutations (supplemental Figure 3C).

AML cell lines derived from de novo/TP53 WT AML were overall more sensitive to ADC treatment compared with MDS/AML cell lines (Figure 2C; supplemental Figure 4). Particularly, the activity of gemtuzumab and the PBD-conjugated ADCs targeting CD33 and CD123 was found to be significantly decreased in TP53-mutated MDS/AML cell lines compared with AML cell lines derived from de novo/TP53 WT AML (Figure 2C; supplemental Figure 4). Anti-CCRL2 ADC showed cytotoxicity similar to or lower compared with cytotoxicity of gemtuzumab and PBD-conjugated ADCs targeting CD33 and CD123 in AML cell lines derived from de novo/TP53 WT AML (Figure 2C; supplemental Figure 4). However, within TP53-mutated MDS/AML cell lines, anti-CCRL2 ADC showed the highest cytotoxicity in TF-1 and F36P cells and the second highest cytotoxicity in SKM1 cells (Figure 2C; supplemental Figure 4).

To test the impact of TP53 deletion in the cytotoxic activity of these ADCs, isogenic TP53 WT and KO MOLM13 and MV4-11 cell lines were developed by CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing (supplemental Figure 5A). Treatment of WT and KO lines with gemtuzumab, anti-CD123, and anti-CCRL2 ADCs showed that TP53 deletion significantly decreased their sensitivity to all ADCs (Figure 2D; supplemental Figure 5B-C). However, the relative increase of 50% inhibitory concentration in TP53 KO cells compared with WT cells was significantly higher for gemtuzumab and anti-CD123 ADC compared with anti-CCRL2 ADC (Figure 2E).

Overall, the activity of anti-CCRL2 ADC appears to be higher compared with the other ADCs against TP53-mutated MDS/AML cell lines and appears less affected by TP53 deletion.

Anti-CCRL2 ADC induces apoptosis and suppresses clonogenicity in MDS/AML primary cells

Next, the activity of anti-CCRL2 ADC in primary MNCs from patients with MDS/AML was analyzed. Based on our published data supporting the conclusion of higher expression of CCRL2 in TP53-mutated MDS/AML and in myeloid neoplasms with erythroid features compared with de novo AML,16,18 4 patients with de novo/TP53 WT AML, 4 patients with TP53 WT MDS–related AML, and 10 patients with multihit TP53-mutated (complex karyotype and TP53 mutation with a variant allele frequency of ≥50%) MDS/AML, including 3 individuals with acute erythroid leukemia (supplemental Tables 2 and 3; Figure 3A), were included. Cells were treated with 20 or 40 ng/mL anti-CCRL2 ADC or 20 ng/mL conjugated IgG2a for 5 days. Treatment with 10 and 20 ng/mL anti-CCRL2 ADC increased apoptosis and suppressed clonogenicity compared with treatment with conjugated IgG2a (Figure 3B). Fluorescence in situ hybridization of isolated colonies from multihit TP53-mutated treated samples confirmed the presence of 17p and 5q deletions and trisomy 8 in isolated cells (supplemental Figure 6). To assess if CCRL2 expression is associated with the cytotoxicity of anti-CCRL2 ADC, the expression of CCRL2 in the surface of primary blasts was measured by flow cytometry (Figure 3C). Consistent with our previously reported results,16,18 acute erythroid leukemia blasts expressed the highest levels of CCRL2, and TP53-mutated MDS/AML blasts expressed relatively higher CCRL2 levels compared with de novo AML blasts (Figure 3C). Linear regression showed a modest positive correlation between CCRL2 expression and the apoptotic effect of anti-CCRL2 ADC, defined as the ratio of apoptosis induced by 40 ng/mL anti-CCRL2 ADC to apoptosis induced by 40 ng/mL IgG2a (Figure 3D). No significant correlation was observed between the apoptotic effect of anti-CCRL2 ADC and TP53 variant allele frequency among the 10 samples from patients with TP53-mutated MDS/AML.

Taken together, our results support the conclusion that anti-CCRL2 ADC promotes apoptosis and suppresses the clonogenicity of primary MDS/AML cells. Although de novo AML samples demonstrated similar cytotoxicity compared with MDS/AML and TP53-mutated samples, higher CCRL2 expression was associated with a better response. These findings suggest that the observed antileukemic activity in TP53-mutated MDS/AML and, particularly, erythroid leukemia is likely associated with higher target expression and not cell-intrinsic sensitivity to the ADC.

Anti-CCRL2 ADC does not affect healthy hematopoietic cells and does not cause systemic toxicity in vivo

Treatment with anti-CCRL2 ADC did not induce apoptosis (Figure 4A), affect differentiation (Figure 4B), or suppress the clonogenicity (Figure 4C) of healthy CD34+ cells. Given that CCRL2 is overexpressed in leukemias with erythroid differentiation and that its deletion decreases the survival and clonogenicity of erythroleukemic cells,18 the effect of anti-CCRL2 ADC on healthy erythroid progenitors was assessed. MNCs from 3 additional healthy bone marrow donors were treated with 100 ng/mL anti-CCRL2 or conjugated IgG2a for 5 days. To further characterize the safety profile of this agent in vivo, an antimouse CCRL2 ADC was developed (supplemental Figure 7A). Discussion

Patients with high-risk MDS/AML, including those with TP53 mutations and deletions, exhibit relatively poor outcomes because of a lack of effective therapies and a high incidence of treatment resistance.1, 2, 3 ADCs, including the PBD-conjugated loncastuximab, have prominent activity in hematologic malignancies,10,11,28,29 but currently available ADCs show relatively limited activity in high-risk MDS/AML and TP53-mutated disease.12 Thus, we developed an anti-CCRL2 ADC using the PBD payload and showed a significant CCRL2-selective, single-agent antileukemic effect in various MDS/AML models. Anti-CCRL2 antibody showed rapid internalization and lysosomal trafficking in MDS/AML cells and the ADC induced DNA damage within 24 hours of treatment.

Our studies demonstrated that cell lines derived from patients with MDS/AML with erythroid features and TP53 alterations are generally less sensitive to gemtuzumab and PBD-conjugated ADCs targeting CD33 and CD123 compared with cell lines from patients with de novo AML or good- to intermediate-risk disease. Consistently, TP53 deletion reduced the sensitivity of MDS/AML cell lines to ADCs. These findings align with the clinical responses observed in patients with high-risk MDS/AML treated with gemtuzumab.12 In contrast, anti-CCRL2 ADC exhibited consistent efficacy across different cell line subtypes, demonstrating higher activity in cells derived from patients with MDS/AML with erythroid features and TP53 mutations compared with gemtuzumab and PBD-conjugated ADCs targeting CD33 and CD123. Similarly, the cytotoxic effect of anti-CCRL2 ADC was less affected by TP53 deletion compared with other ADCs. These results suggest that the marked upregulation of CCRL2 in high-risk MDS/AML and TP53-mutated cells may offset their inherent resistance to drug toxin–mediated cell killing.

Analysis of anti-CCRL2 ADC activity in primary samples confirmed that it induces apoptosis and suppresses the clonogenic potential of MDS/AML cells. MDS/AML and TP53-mutated samples showed overall similar sensitivity to anti-CCRL2 ADC compared with de novo AML samples, with erythroleukemia samples showing the highest sensitivity. Notably, there was a modest positive association between CCRL2 expression and the apoptotic activity induced by the agent, consistent with findings reported for other ADCs targeting CD33.32, 33, 34 Our group recently demonstrated that TP53-mutated MDS/AML and AML with erythroid features exhibited the highest levels of CCRL2 expression across the spectrum of MDS/AML subtypes.18 These findings support the conclusion that the significant antileukemic effect of anti-CCRL2 ADC in TP53-mutated MDS/AML and, particularly, erythroleukemic samples is likely associated with the prominent target overexpression in these subsets and not with their cell-intrinsic sensitivity to the released toxin. On the contrary, de novo AML samples are probably more sensitive to toxin-mediated cell killing based on our cell line data, thereby counterbalancing their relatively low CCRL2 expression.

PBD-conjugated ADCs targeting CD33 and CD123 showed promising preclinical efficacy in high-risk AML.35,36 However, early-phase clinical trials revealed cytopenia and hepatotoxicity, which hindered further clinical development. Notably, not only gemtuzumab but also bispecific antibodies and chimeric antigen receptor T cells targeting CD33 have been associated with cytopenia.37, 38, 39 This suggests that these adverse effects may be related to the expression of CD33 and CD123 on the surface of healthy hematopoietic cells. Although transient elevations in liver enzymes have been observed with PBD payloads, there have been no reports of fatal hepatotoxicity.28,29 On the contrary, healthy stem and progenitor cells expressed minimal CCRL2 levels.16,17 Consistently, anti-CCRL2 ADCs demonstrated minimal impact on healthy hematopoietic cells and did not induce systemic toxicity or significant changes in blood counts or chemistry panels in C57BL/6 mice, similarly to recent findings from our group.40 If cytopenia or hepatotoxicity arise with anti-CCRL2 ADCs, fractionated dosing may be considered because this approach has enabled the safe and effective use of gemtuzumab. It should be noted, however, that activated differentiated cells, such as neutrophils, monocytes, and endothelial cells involved in inflammatory responses, express high levels of CCRL2,13, 14, 15 which could result in on-target, off-MDS/AML toxicities. This possibility should be carefully considered during the development of this therapeutic agent. Finally, although bystander killing is a recognized property of potent payloads because of their lipophilic DNA–crosslinking warheads, allowing diffusion after linker cleavage41 and payload release, clinical trials on PBD-conjugated ADCs support the conclusion that any potential bystander effect is tolerable.7,29,42 Our in vivo studies demonstrated that anti-CCRL2 ADC effectively suppressed leukemic growth in cell line xenograft models, resulting in improved survival of treated mice. Notably, 2 doses of the agent administered 20 days apart were well tolerated by NSG mice and led to a marked reduction in disease burden. Furthermore, bone marrow aspirates collected from NSGS mice engrafted with TP53-mutated MDS/AML cells enabled randomization to treatment with either anti-CCRL2 ADC or an isotype control and comparison of pre- and posttreatment samples. This approach revealed a significant antileukemic activity of anti-CCRL2 ADC. Despite the marked reduction in disease burden in vivo, most treated mice ultimately experienced disease progression and died from leukemia, suggesting that incorporating this agent into combination therapeutic regimens may be a more effective strategy, particularly for TP53-mutated disease.

Further studies in syngeneic MDS/AML models are required to solidify the efficacy and safety of anti-CCRL2 ADC, but this agent appears to be a promising candidate as a single agent or as part of combination therapies for high-risk MDS/AML, including TP53-mutated disease, especially given the lack of effective antibody-based therapies in these disease subsets. Similarly, conjugating PBD to antibodies targeting other surface antigens that are particularly overexpressed in high-risk MDS/AML and that show very low expression in healthy hematopoietic cells may be an attractive strategy to improve the outcomes of patients with these neoplasms.
