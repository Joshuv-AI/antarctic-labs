“3D” Techniques for Improving Safety in Posterior Knee Arthroscopy During Posterior Cruciate Ligament Avulsion Fracture Fixation: A Series of Key Technical Modifications

Zhichen Zou, Xinbei Dong, Chuanzheng Zhu, Qinglong Wang, Zhixue Wang

Abstract

Arthroscopic procedures in the posterior compartment of the knee are technically demanding and present an inherent risk of iatrogenic neurovascular injury because of the close proximity of the popliteal neurovascular bundle. This technical note aims to enhance surgical safety by describing a series of modifications termed the “3D” technique. The technique integrates 3 simple, practical, and reproducible modifications that improve visualization and tunnel accuracy while minimizing the risk of complications using only standard arthroscopic instruments.

Although knee arthroscopy has become a well‐established and widely performed technique, visualization and instrumentation of the posterior compartment remain challenging, particularly during posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) reconstruction or reduction and fixation of PCL avulsion fractures. Neurovascular injury represents the most devastating complication of these procedures, with an incidence rate of up to 9% reported in the literature., Therefore, minimizing the risk of iatrogenic neurovascular injury while maximizing surgical safety during arthroscopic procedures in the posterior compartment remains one of the foremost concerns for surgeons. To address this concern, we describe a series of technical modifications to enhance surgical safety, summarized as the “3D” techniques (with each modification beginning with the letter “D”).

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Patient Positioning and Conventional Procedures

After successful anesthesia induction, the patient is positioned supine on the operating table. A pneumatic tourniquet is applied to the proximal thigh of the affected limb. After standard sterilization and draping, conventional anterolateral and anteromedial knee portals are established. First, intra‐articular debridement is performed, and concomitant injuries, such as meniscal tears, are addressed accordingly. Subsequently, the 3 key modifications of the “3D” techniques are implemented as described below.

First D: Distalization of the Neurovascular Bundle

Two technical modifications are implemented to maximize the distance from the neurovascular bundle, thereby minimizing the risk of iatrogenic injury. The first modification involves placing the affected lower extremity in a figure‐of‐4 position and hyperflexing it to >100° (Figure ). Anatomical studies have confirmed that hyperflexion of the knee maximizes the distance between the neurovascular bundle and the posterior compartment, thus optimizing surgical safety., , Additionally, throughout the procedure, the irrigation pressure is appropriately elevated and maintained to achieve sustained capsular distention. This maneuver confers a dual benefit: it displaces the neurovascular bundle further posteriorly, thereby increasing the safety margin while simultaneously expanding the working space within the posterior compartment to optimize visualization. Increased irrigation pressure can be achieved either by elevating the saline bag (gravity‐fed system) or by adjusting the pump pressure settings (automated pump system).

Second D: Dual Posteromedial Portal

The arthroscope (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI) is introduced into the posteromedial compartment between the PCL and the medial femoral condyle via the transnotch approach. Under direct arthroscopic visualization, a standard posteromedial portal is established. Approximately 4 to 5 cm proximal to the standard posteromedial portal, a high posteromedial portal is established under spinal needle guidance (Figure ). Typically, the high posteromedial portal serves as the viewing portal, whereas the standard posteromedial portal functions as the working portal (Figure ). Compared with visualization achieved through the anterolateral or anteromedial portals, this dual posteromedial portal setup optimizes visualization and facilitates thorough debridement and exposure of the tibial attachment of the PCL under direct arthroscopic visualization. For PCL avulsion fractures, this approach also enables the distal extension of capsular dissection beyond the avulsion fragment, allowing adequate exposure of its inferior margin. The exposure achieved is sufficient to prepare for subsequent tunnel creation. The dual posteromedial portal setup enables surgical instrumentation of the posterior compartment under direct visualization, thereby reducing the risk of posterior capsular violation and iatrogenic neurovascular injury, thus enhancing overall surgical safety. Furthermore, this setup facilitates accurate localization of the tibial tunnel and minimizes coronal‐plane deviation of the tunnel aperture.

Third D: Depth‐Limited Drilling

Both PCL reconstruction and arthroscopic fixation of PCL avulsion fractures require the creation of a tibial tunnel. The depth‐limited drilling technique involves positioning the bow‐shaped drill guide (Smith & Nephew, Memphis, TN) at the intended tibial tunnel site before tunnel creation, with the guide sleeve advanced until it contacts the anterior tibial cortex. The remaining length of the sleeve from its junction with the bow‐shaped guide to its free end is then measured and recorded to determine the drilling depth (Figure ). The drill guide is then withdrawn from the joint, and the previously measured remaining length of the sleeve is reproduced extracorporeally (Figure ,). The guide pin is then inserted into the sleeve and secured such that its length slightly exceeds the measured depth, thereby allowing adequate penetration without risking overpenetration (Figure ). Tibial tunnel drilling is then performed. Under direct arthroscopic visualization, the guide pin exactly penetrates the posterior cortex and simultaneously reaches the preset depth limit (Figure ,), thereby preventing overpenetration that could cause capsular violation and neurovascular injury. This depth‐limiting technique prevents overpenetration of the guide pin during tunnel drilling, thereby avoiding iatrogenic injury to the popliteal neurovascular bundle. DISCUSSION

The reduction and fixation techniques for PCL avulsion fractures have been extensively described in the literature., , However, arthroscopic procedures in the posterior compartment of the knee present unique challenges owing to limited visualization and the close anatomical proximity of the popliteal neurovascular bundle. Although knee arthroscopy is generally considered a safe procedure, complications are not negligible. In a large‐scale analysis of 138,823 arthroscopic knee procedures, the overall complication rate was 7.53%, with PCL reconstruction carrying the highest rate at 26.38%. Similarly, arthroscopic management of PCL tibial avulsion fractures poses comparable technical challenges, with a reported complication rate of 12.5% in a recent systematic review. These findings emphasize the need for practical, reproducible safety modifications.

To address these challenges, we describe a series of technical modifications, each beginning with the letter “D.” This simple, mnemonic summary is intended to reduce the complication rate and enhance surgical safety. The 3D technique provides a comprehensive, step‐by‐step description encompassing patient positioning, portal establishment, and the technical details of tunnel drilling throughout the entire procedure. The first D enhances surgical safety through 2 simple modifications: increasing knee flexion and maintaining appropriate capsular distention. The second and third Ds—establishment of dual posteromedial portals and implementation of depth‐limited drilling—may slightly prolong the operative time; however, they significantly improve visualization of the posterior compartment and tunnel accuracy while reducing the risk of neurovascular injury. In summary, the “3D” technique integrates 3 simple, practical, and reproducible modifications—distalization of the neurovascular bundle, dual posteromedial portals, and depth‐limited drilling—to enhance safety during posterior knee arthroscopy. This approach improves visualization and tunnel accuracy while minimizing the neurovascular risk.
