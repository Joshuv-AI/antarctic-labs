Bayesian inference of sparsity in stable vector autoregressive processes
Sarah E. Heaps, Ian H. Jermyn, Yujiang Wang, Darren J. Wilkinson

Abstract
Advances in sensing technology have made it possible to collect large volumes of high-dimensional time-series data. In fields like genetics and neuroscience, key questions concern whether directed relationships between variables can be learned from these data. To this end, graphical vector autoregressions are a popular tool because zeros among the autoregressive coefficients and error precision matrix have natural interpretations in terms of Granger non-causality and contemporaneous conditional independence. In applications where system dynamics are subject to functional or structural constraints, assuming the process is stable can be advantageous. However, enforcing stability demands restricting the autoregressive coefficients to lie in a constrained space with a complex geometry called the stationary region. The resulting inferential challenges are compounded when sparsity is also a requirement. Working in the Bayesian paradigm, we tackle the problem of developing a prior that simultaneously enforces stationarity and sparsity through parameter expansion, constructing a spike-and-slab prior with support constrained to the stationary region. A mixture of G G -Wishart distributions provides a sparse prior for the error precision matrix. Computational inference is carried out using Metropolis-within-Gibbs, exploiting the No-U-Turn Sampler and reversible-jump steps. We demonstrate the inferential and predictive benefits of our approach through simulations and applications in macroeconomics and neuroscience.



Keywords—
Granger causality;
G
-Wishart prior; parameter expansion; stationarity; time-series analysis.

Introduction

Over recent years, advances in sensing technology have made it possible to collect large volumes of time-series data on many variables. In a diverse array of fields, such as systems biology
(
38
)
, genetics
(
37
)
, neuroscience
(

(

, there is substantial interest in how these data can be used to improve understanding of the interactions between variables. Exploiting the time-ordering of data,

y_{1}
is Granger causal for another process

if utilising information on the history of
y_{1}
improves the prediction of
y_{2}
. In this paper, we focus on Bayesian inference for the class of stable graphical vector autoregressions, which have an interpretation in terms of strong Granger causality
(
15

. This is formulated through conditional independence statements, which can be represented as a mixed graph
G

44

Vector autoregressions are a popular class of models for multivariate time-series data. An
m
-variate, zero-mean Gaussian process
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}
evolving according to a vector autoregression of order
p
, or
VAR
{}_{m}\text{(}{p}\text{)}
process, can be written as

\boldsymbol{y}_{t}=\mathnormal{\phi}_{1}\boldsymbol{y}_{t-1}+\ldots+\mathnormal{\phi}_{p}\boldsymbol{y}_{t-p}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t},

(1)

where the errors
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}
form an uncorrelated sequence of zero-mean Gaussian random vectors,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}|\mathnormal{\Sigma}\sim\mathrm{N}_{m}(\boldsymbol{0},\mathnormal{\Sigma})
. The parameters of the model therefore comprise the autoregressive coefficient matrices
\mathnormal{\phi}_{s}\in M_{m\times m}(\mathbb{R})
(
s=1,\ldots,p
) and the error variance matrix
\mathnormal{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{S}^{+}_{m}

M_{m\times n}(V)
denotes the set of
m\times n

\mathcal{S}^{+}_{m}

m\times m

\mathnormal{\phi}_{s}

\mathsf{\Phi}

\mathsf{\Phi}
and the inverse of the error variance matrix,
\mathnormal{K}=\mathnormal{\Sigma}^{-1}

Here, non-zero entries in the autoregressive structure correspond to directed edges in the graph and represent statements of Granger causality, while non-zero entries in the error precision matrix correspond to undirected edges, representing statements of contemporaneous conditional dependence. In the Bayesian paradigm, zeros can be accommodated through a sparse prior distribution, typically borrowing ideas from the Bayesian variable selection literature for the (unconstrained) coefficients
\mathsf{\Phi}\in M_{m\times m}(\mathbb{R})^{p}
and from the Gaussian graphical modelling literature for the positive definite error precision
\mathnormal{K}\in\mathcal{S}^{+}_{m}

Over the last decade, the modelling framework for graphical vector autoregressions has been extended to accommodate multiple realisations of multivariate time-series in hierarchical or panel vector autoregressions
(
10
;
29
)
. To reduce the computational burden of model fitting, there have also been extensions in a variety of directions, such as variational Bayes approaches as an alternative to Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)
(
4
)
. However, much of the focus has been on exploiting structural constraints in graphical vector autoregressions to achieve dimension reduction, for example by structuring the autoregressive coefficient matrices into a three-way tensor and then applying a tensor decomposition of reduced rank
(
17

33

Using the backshift operator
B
, defined such that
B\boldsymbol{y}_{t}=\boldsymbol{y}_{t-1}
, (
1
) can be written as
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}=(\mathnormal{I}_{m}-\mathnormal{\phi}_{1}B-\cdots-\mathnormal{\phi}_{p}B^{p})\boldsymbol{y}_{t}=\mathnormal{\phi}(B)\boldsymbol{y}_{t}
where
\mathnormal{I}_{m}
is the
m\times m
identity matrix and
\mathnormal{\phi}(u)=\mathnormal{I}_{m}-\mathnormal{\phi}_{1}u-\cdots-\mathnormal{\phi}_{p}u^{p}
,
u\in\mathbb{C}
, is termed the characteristic polynomial. A vector autoregression is stable if and only if all the roots of
\det\{\mathnormal{\phi}(u)\}=0
lie outside the unit circle. We refer to this subset of
M_{m\times m}(\mathbb{R})^{p}
as the stationary region, denoted
\mathcal{C}_{p,m}
. From a practical perspective, an important property of stationary processes is that the variances of forecasts do not grow without bound as the forecast horizon increases. Particularly in the life sciences, where system dynamics can be subject to functional or structural constraints, this is often a desirable property. This includes applications where interest lies in long-term forecasting or when studying a linear dynamic system which is thought to be at equilibrium. Stationarity can also be advantageous for the visualisation of results: for a broad class of graphical time series model that are piecewise stationary,
15
introduced various Granger-causal Markov properties that enhance graphical interpretation by relating separation properties on the graph to statements of conditional independence or Granger non-causality. Motivated by these arguments, stationarity is frequently stated as an assumption in the literature on graphical vector autoregressions. However, it is generally only enforced as a constraint over a simpler subset of
\mathcal{C}_{p,m}

(
23
)
or, as highlighted in
13
, not enforced as a constraint at all. Yet, because the number of parameters in
\mathsf{\Phi}
grows quadratically with dimension
m
, for a time-series of fixed length, there is typically increasing epistemic uncertainty in the values of the
\phi_{s,ij}
, as
m
or the order
p
get large, allowing posterior mass to spill outside the stationary region. The problem is particularly pronounced if the length of the time-series is short relative to
m
, which makes it difficult to infer, with near certainty, that the process is stationary
(
25
)
.

In principle, the solution to this problem is simple: develop a prior distribution for the autoregressive coefficient matrices which can simultaneously induce sparsity while constraining inference to the (full) stationary region. In practice, however, it is difficult to specify a prior with these properties which facilitates tractable computational inference due to the geometric complexity of the stationary region, compounded by the complications arising from allowing sparsity in the parameter space. The main contribution of this paper is to address precisely this challenge. Introducing an auxiliary parameter, we define a bijective mapping between the parameter-expanded set of stationary autoregressive coefficient matrices and a parameter-expanded set of unconstrained square matrices. This mapping is constructed in such a way that zeros in the unconstrained square matrices map to zeros in the autoregressive coefficient matrices, and vice versa. As a result, specification of a sparsity inducing prior for the unconstrained square matrices produces a sparsity inducing prior for the autoregressive coefficient matrices that is constrained to the stationary region. To allow sparsity in the error precision matrix, we adopt a prior comprising a mixture of
G
-Wishart distributions
(
42
;
3
)
which, to the best of our knowledge, has not been applied previously in the context of graphical vector autoregressions. Crucially, we show that computational inference for this reparameterised model requires only minor modifications to the algorithm that would be used for an analogous model that ignores the stationarity constraint. We describe an efficient Metropolis-within-Gibbs algorithm to sample from the posterior, which uses the No-U-Turn sampler
(
26
)
to draw from the full conditional distribution of the continuous parameters pertaining to the reparameterised autoregressive coefficient matrices, and the
graphical model determination algorithm
(
47
)
to draw from the full conditional distribution of the error precision matrix. Through an extensive simulation experiment, we highlight the benefits of enforcing stationarity, and suggest situations in which the benefit is likely to be most pronounced. We then consider two substantive applications using data from macroeconomics and neuroscience, which demonstrate the improved predictive performance and scientific insight that can be gained by fitting our stationary graphical vector autoregression.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. In Section
2

G
-Wishart priors for the error precision matrix. Section

4

Graphical vector autoregressions

2.1
The graphical model

Let
G=(V,E_{1},E_{2})
denote a mixed graph where
V=\{1,\ldots,m\}
denotes the set of vertices,
E_{1}\subseteq V\times V
is a set of directed edges and
E_{2}\subseteq\binom{V}{2}
is a set of undirected edges. We denote a directed edge from
a\in V
to
b\in V
by
a\longrightarrow b
and an undirected edge between
a\in V
and
b\in V
by
a\relbar\mkern-7.0mu\relbar\mkern-7.0mu\relbar b
. Two distinct vertices,
a
and
b
, can be unconnected or connected by any combination of a directed edge in either direction and an undirected edge. A stationary graphical
VAR
{}_{m}\text{(}{p}\text{)}
process (
1
) associated with the graph
G
, denoted by
VAR
\text{(}{p}\text{, }{G}\text{)}
, has parameters that are constrained by:

(i)

a\longrightarrow b\notin E_{1}
if and only if
\phi_{s,ba}=0
for all
s=1,\ldots,p
in which case we say that
y_{a}
is (strongly) Granger non-causal for
y_{b}
;

(ii)

a\relbar\mkern-7.0mu\relbar\mkern-7.0mu\relbar b\notin E_{2}
if and only if
K_{ab}=K_{ba}=0
in which case we say that
y_{a}
and
y_{b}
are contemporaneously conditionally independent;

for all
a\neq b
. It follows that non-zero entries in off-diagonal elements of
\mathnormal{\phi}_{s}
, for any

, lead to directed edges, reflecting Granger causality relationships, while non-zero entries in the off-diagonal elements of
\mathnormal{K}
correspond to undirected edges, reflecting contemporaneous conditional dependence structure.

2.2
Prior for autoregressive coefficient matrices

2.2.1
Parameter expansion on an unconstrained space

As our inferential objective is to characterise Granger causality structure, we consider priors for the autoregressive coefficient matrices
\mathsf{\Phi}
which encourage zeros only in the off-diagonal elements
\phi_{s,ij}
for
i\neq j
,
s=1,\ldots,p
. When stationarity is not enforced, a typical approach would introduce a vector of unknown hyperparameters
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}=(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{1}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{p}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}})
and adopt a prior of the form

\pi(\mathsf{\Phi},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})=\prod_{s=1}^{p}\pi(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{s})\prod_{i=1}^{m}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\pi(\phi_{s,ij}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{s}),

(2)

where
\pi(\phi_{s,ij}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{s})
for
i\neq j
is a Bayesian variable selection prior and the role of
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{s}
is to allow the data to influence the degree of selection, shrinkage, or both, at lag
s
. For example,
\pi(\phi_{s,ij}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{s})
might be a spike-and-slab prior in which the slab is a normal distribution, centred at zero, and the spike is either another zero-mean normal distribution with smaller variance
(
18
)
or an atom of probability at zero
(
30
)
. In these cases, the unknown hyperparameters
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{s}
would typically include the precision of the slab and the mixing probability. Alternatively,
\pi(\phi_{s,ij}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{s})
might be a continuous shrinkage prior, such as the horseshoe, in which case
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{s}
would typically include local and global shrinkage parameters
(
22
)
. A naive approach to modifying this prior to constrain inference to the stationary region would simply truncate the density (
2
) such that
\mathsf{\Phi}\in\mathcal{C}_{p,m}
. However, this would introduce an intractable normalising constant that depended on
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
, making the posterior distribution doubly-intractable and hence significantly complicating computational inference. In addition, even if
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}
was fixed, the geometric complexity of the stationary region
\mathcal{C}_{p,m}
would make it difficult to design an efficient proposal distribution for
\mathsf{\Phi}
that restricted most of its mass to
\mathcal{C}_{p,m}
.

Rather than truncating a prior that is designed for unconstrained Euclidean space, we present a more tailored solution. The basic idea is first to find an unconstrained reparameterisation of a stationary process and then to apply a sparsity-inducing prior over the reparameterised space.
25
uses an unconstrained reparameterisation of a stationary vector autoregression to construct a prior which restricts inference to the stationary region. However the new parameters are partial autocorrelation matrices where zeros do not have a graphical interpretation in terms of Granger non-causality. In order to construct a reparameterisation in which the meaning of individual zeros is preserved, we adopt an approach based on parameter-expansion
(
32
;
36
)
. Although originally introduced as an auxiliary variable technique to accelerate Gibbs sampling algorithms, parameter-expansion has been used more recently to aid specification of priors over constrained regions such as the Stiefel manifold
(
27
)
or spaces of rank-reduced positive semi-definite matrices
(
24
)
.

Introducing the auxiliary variable
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, we map
(\mathsf{\Phi},\nu)\in\mathcal{C}_{p,m}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
to
(\mathsf{Z},u)\in M_{m\times m}(\mathbb{R})^{p}\times(0,1)
, where
\mathsf{Z}=(\mathnormal{Z}_{1},\ldots,\mathnormal{Z}_{p})
, through

\mathnormal{Z}_{s}=\nu^{s}\mathnormal{\phi}_{s},\ \text{$s=1,\ldots,p$;}\hskip 9.24994ptu=\rho(\mathnormal{C}_{\phi}),

(3)

where
\rho(\cdot)
denotes the spectral radius and
\mathnormal{C}_{\phi}=\mathnormal{C}(\mathnormal{\phi}_{1},\ldots,\mathnormal{\phi}_{p})
is the companion matrix

\mathnormal{C}_{\phi}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathnormal{\phi}_{1}&\mathnormal{\phi}_{2}&\cdots&\mathnormal{\phi}_{p-1}&\mathnormal{\phi}_{p}\\
\mathnormal{I}_{m}&\mathnormal{0}&\cdots&\mathnormal{0}&\mathnormal{0}\\
\mathnormal{0}&\mathnormal{I}_{m}&\cdots&\mathnormal{0}&\mathnormal{0}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
\mathnormal{0}&\mathnormal{0}&\cdots&\mathnormal{I}_{m}&\mathnormal{0}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

It can easily be shown (
e.g.
by induction) that the reciprocals,
\eta_{\phi}
, of the non-zero eigenvalues of the companion matrix
\mathnormal{C}_{\phi}
are the roots of
\det\{\mathnormal{\phi}(\eta_{\phi})\}=0
and so the stability condition is satisfied if and only if
\rho(\mathnormal{C}_{\phi})<1
. If we introduce a corresponding companion form for the unconstrained matrices,
\mathnormal{C}_{z}=\mathnormal{C}(\mathnormal{Z}_{1},\ldots,\mathnormal{Z}_{p})
, it can similarly be shown that the reciprocals,
\eta_{z}
, of the non-zero eigenvalues of the companion matrix
\mathnormal{C}_{z}
satisfy

0=\det\left(\mathnormal{I}_{m}-\mathnormal{Z}_{1}\eta_{z}-\ldots-\mathnormal{Z}_{p}\eta_{z}^{p}\right)=\det\left\{\mathnormal{I}_{m}-\mathnormal{\phi}_{1}(\nu\eta_{z})-\ldots-\mathnormal{\phi}_{p}(\nu\eta_{z})^{p}\right\}.

If follows that all (non-zero) eigenvalues of
\mathnormal{C}_{z}
are of the form
\nu\lambda_{\phi}
where
\lambda_{\phi}=1/\eta_{\phi}
is an eigenvalue of
\mathnormal{C}_{\phi}
. Therefore, because
\nu>0
,
\rho(\mathnormal{C}_{z})=\nu\rho(\mathnormal{C}_{\phi})
and we can interpret
\nu
as a scaling factor which maps the Schur stable block matrix
\mathnormal{C}_{\phi}
to the block matrix
\mathnormal{C}_{z}
with unconstrained first row block. We can therefore write the inverse of (
3
) as

\mathnormal{\phi}_{s}=\frac{u^{s}\mathnormal{Z}_{s}}{\rho(\mathnormal{C}_{z})^{s}},\ s=1,\ldots,p;\hskip 9.24994pt\nu=\frac{\rho(\mathnormal{C}_{z})}{u}.

(4)

We note that the inverse mapping (
4
) is not defined when
\rho(\mathnormal{C}_{z})=0
, that is, when
\mathnormal{C}_{z}
is a nilpotent matrix. As we discuss in Section
2.2.2
, we will adopt a prior in which the diagonal elements of the
\mathnormal{Z}_{s}
are continuous and so
\rho(\mathnormal{C}_{z})>0
almost surely, making the mapping (
3
) almost surely bijective on the support of
(\mathsf{Z},u)
.

2.2.2
Prior over parameter-expanded reparameterisation

For building a prior distribution for the autoregressive coefficient matrices, the reparameterisation described in the previous section has three important theoretical properties. First, for any off-diagonal element at any lag
s=1,\ldots,p
,
z_{s,ij}=0
if and only if
\phi_{s,ij}=0
(
i\neq j
). Therefore by assigning a joint prior to
(\mathsf{Z},u)
which allows sparsity amongst the unconstrained
z_{s,ij}
(
i\neq j
), we can induce a distribution for
(\mathsf{\Phi},\nu)
such that the marginal prior for the
\phi_{s,ij}
has precisely the intended property of sparsity. Second, the Jacobian determinant of the transformation (
3
) can be calculated in closed form as
J\{(\mathsf{\Phi},\nu);(\mathsf{Z},u)\}=-\nu^{q-1}\rho(\mathnormal{C}_{\phi})
, where
q=m^{2}p(p+1)/2
; a proof can be found in Section S1
of the Supplementary Materials. This facilitates derivation of properties of the marginal prior induced for
\mathsf{\Phi}
, which can be used to guide the choice of prior for
u
, as we explain in Supplementary Section S2.
Finally, results from simulations strongly suggest that shrinkage increases with lag, which is consistent with the commonly-held belief that the importance of lagged variables decays with lag length. In Supplementary Section S3,
we present some theoretical indications that support these observations.

To each off-diagonal element in the set of unconstrained square matrices
z_{s,ij}
,
i\neq j
,
s=1,\ldots,p
, we assign a spike-and-slab prior, which is formulated by introducing an indicator variable
\gamma_{s,ij}
and a continuous
effect size
parameter
\tilde{z}_{s,ij}
and by writing
\mathnormal{Z}_{s}=\mathnormal{\Gamma}_{s}\circ\mathnormal{\tilde{Z}}_{s}
where
\circ
denotes the Hadamard product and the diagonal elements of the
\mathnormal{\Gamma}_{s}
are fixed at 1. Introducing hyperparameters
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}=(\tau,\vartheta,\omega)^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}
, we then adopt the prior

\pi(u,\mathnormal{\tilde{Z}}_{1},\ldots,\mathnormal{\tilde{Z}}_{p},\mathnormal{\Gamma}_{1},\ldots,\mathnormal{\Gamma}_{p},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})=\pi(u)\pi(\tau)\pi(\vartheta)\pi(\omega)\prod_{s=1}^{p}\pi(\mathnormal{\tilde{Z}}_{s}|\tau,\omega)\pi(\mathnormal{\Gamma}_{s}|\vartheta),

where
u\sim\mathrm{Beta}(a_{1},a_{2})
and, for
s=1,\ldots,p
,

\displaystyle\tilde{z}_{s,ij}|\tau

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{N}(0,\tau^{-1}),

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\tau

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{Gam}(b_{1},b_{2}),

\displaystyle i\neq j,

\displaystyle\gamma_{s,ij}|\vartheta

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{Bern}(\vartheta),

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\vartheta

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{Beta}(c_{1},c_{2}),

\displaystyle i\neq j,

\displaystyle\tilde{z}_{s,ii}|\omega

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{N}(\mu,\omega^{-1}),

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\omega

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{Gam}(e_{1},e_{2}),

\displaystyle i=1,\ldots,m,

where we will typically take
\mu=0
to encourage shrinkage of the diagonal effect sizes to zero. However, an alternative is to assign
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
a prior to allow more flexible shrinkage to a common, but unknown, value.

A computational advantage of formulating the spike-and-slab prior using a product of effect-size parameters and indicators is that it fixes the dimension of the continuous part of the parameter space, allowing all the effect-size parameters to be updated jointly using an efficient proposal distribution that exploits the gradient of their full conditional distribution; see Section
3
for further details.

2.3
Prior for error precision matrix

There have been various efforts to provide a sparse prior for the error precision matrix
\mathnormal{K}
in graphical vector autoregressions. Some approaches rely on a hyper-inverse Wishart prior for the error variance matrix
\mathnormal{\Sigma}
given its associated undirected graph. This distribution has an analytically tractable normalising constant only if the graph is decomposable, and so either inference has been limited to the space of decomposable graphs
(
41
)
or the calculations underpinning structural comparison have been approximated when graphs are not decomposable
(
11
;
34
)
. Other approaches map the error precision or stationary precision matrix to unconstrained Euclidean space through a Cholesky factorisation then assign sparse priors over the unconstrained space
(
18
;
43
)
. Although this greatly simplifies computational inference, the natural graphical interpretation of the pattern of sparsity is lost.

In order to avoid these limitations, we employ ideas from Gaussian graphical modelling, in which the problem of Bayesian inference for the sparsity structure of a precision matrix has been studied extensively; see
35
or
46
for recent reviews. In this literature, the
G
-Wishart distribution is often used as a conditional prior for the precision matrix, given the undirected graph, because it is conjugate to the Gaussian graphical likelihood. Specifying a marginal distribution over undirected graphs completes the prior by quantifying uncertainty in the sparsity pattern. Although the normalising constant of the
G
-Wishart density is also analytically intractable unless the graph is decomposable, its evaluation can be avoided during MCMC using ideas based on the exchange algorithm
(
39
)
; see Section
3
.

To this end, denote by
G_{2}
the subgraph associated with all vertices but only the undirected edges
E_{2}
. We then specify a joint prior over
(G_{2},\mathnormal{K})
as
\pi(G_{2},\mathnormal{K})=\pi(G_{2})\pi(\mathnormal{K}|G_{2})
in which
\mathnormal{K}|G_{2}\sim\mathrm{W}_{G_{2}}(d,\mathnormal{D})
. Here
\mathrm{W}_{G_{2}}(d,\mathnormal{D})
denotes the
G
-Wishart distribution with shape parameter
d>2
, inverse scale matrix
\mathnormal{D}
and density
\pi(\mathnormal{K}|G_{2})=|K|^{(d-2)/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}(\mathnormal{K}\mathnormal{D})\right\}/I_{G_{2}}(d,\mathnormal{D})
, in which
I_{G_{2}}(d,\mathnormal{D})
is the normalising constant. In the applications in this paper, we use a uniform distribution over the space of undirected graphs as the marginal prior for
G_{2}
or, equivalently, independent uniform
\mathrm{U}\{0,1\}
distributions for the presence of each undirected edge. However, our approach could easily be modified to accommodate other options.

3
Posterior inference through MCMC

Consider observations modelled as realizations from a stationary graphical vector autoregression of order
p
. The likelihood function associated with the observations at times
t=t_{1},\ldots,n
conditional on the preceding
p
observations is given by

p(\boldsymbol{y}_{t_{1}:n}|\boldsymbol{y}_{(t_{1}-p):(t_{1}-1)},\mathsf{\Phi},\mathnormal{K})=\prod_{t=t_{1}}^{n}p(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}|\boldsymbol{y}_{(t-p):(t-1)},\mathsf{\Phi},\mathnormal{K})

(5)

in which
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{y}_{(t-p):(t-1)},\mathsf{\Phi},\mathnormal{K}\sim\mathrm{N}_{m}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{p}\mathnormal{\phi}_{s}\boldsymbol{y}_{t-s}\,,\,\mathnormal{K}^{-1}\right)
. In principle, the joint distribution of any
n
consecutive observations is a zero-mean Gaussian distribution with analytically tractable variance matrix,
\mathnormal{V}_{0}
. Therefore we could take
t_{1}=p+1
and extend (
5
) to construct the joint distribution of observations
\boldsymbol{y}_{1:n}
. Unfortunately, the variance matrix
\mathnormal{V}_{0}
for
\boldsymbol{y}_{1:p}
would depend on the error precision matrix
\mathnormal{K}
in a manner that destroyed the semi-conjugacy of the
G
-Wishart prior. Modern algorithms for efficiently exploring the joint posterior for
(\mathnormal{K},G_{2})
exploit analytic simplifications that rely on the full conditional for
\mathnormal{K}
being a
G
-Wishart distribution. As such, we do not model the first
p
observations through the stationary distribution. The simplest alternative is to simply take
t_{1}=p+1
and condition on the first
p
observations or, roughly equivalently, to model the first
p
observations using a uniform distribution. This is the approach we take in the applications in this paper. However, if a time-series is particularly short, one might not want to lose completely the information contained in the first
p
observations. In this case, one could set
t_{1}=1
, augment the parameter space with
n_{1}\geq p
(latent) initial values,
\boldsymbol{y}_{-(n_{1}-1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{0}
, and assign a prior
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}\sim\mathrm{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\mathnormal{I}_{m})
for
t=-(n_{1}-1),\ldots,p-n_{1}
while modelling
\boldsymbol{y}_{p-n_{1}+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{0}
through (
5
). For brevity in the remainder of this section, we assume that we have set
t_{1}=p+1
and use the conditional likelihood
p(\boldsymbol{y}_{(p+1):n}|\boldsymbol{y}_{1:p},\mathsf{\Phi},\mathnormal{K})
.

We treat the likelihood as a function of the error precision matrix,
\mathnormal{K}
, and the parameters that determine the autoregressive coefficient matrices,
(\mathnormal{\tilde{Z}}_{1},\ldots,\mathnormal{\tilde{Z}}_{p},\mathnormal{\Gamma}_{1},\ldots,\mathnormal{\Gamma}_{p},u)
. Combining it with the prior described in Sections
2.2
-
2.3
through Bayes theorem yields the posterior distribution,
\pi(\mathnormal{\tilde{Z}}_{1},\ldots,\mathnormal{\tilde{Z}}_{p},\mathnormal{\Gamma}_{1},\ldots,\mathnormal{\Gamma}_{p},u,\boldsymbol{\vartheta},\mathnormal{K},G_{2}|\boldsymbol{y}_{1:n})
, which is proportional to

\pi(G_{2})\pi(\mathnormal{K}|G_{2})\pi(\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\pi(u)\left\{\prod_{s=1}^{p}\pi(\mathnormal{\tilde{Z}}_{s}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\pi(\mathnormal{\Gamma}_{s}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\right\}p(\boldsymbol{y}_{(p+1):n}|\boldsymbol{y}_{1:p},\mathsf{Z},u,\mathnormal{K})

\mathnormal{\phi}_{s}

Joint posterior for the mixed graph
G

Supplementary Section S5.2 presents an investigation into the joint posterior distribution for the mixed graph
G
underpinning the model. A core result is that when the degree of sparsity in
\mathsf{\Phi}
is small (
r=0.1
) or moderate (

), the posterior for the (Hamming) distance between

5
Applications

5.1
U.S. macroeconomic data

In this section we consider an application involving a quarterly time series of U.S. macroeconomic variables. We focus primarily on demonstrating the improvements in predictive performance gained by using a prior that enforces stationarity and allows sparsity.

The original dataset is described in
28
. After applying log-transformations and differencing, where appropriate, and then standardising all variables, the data are modelled as arising from a stationary, zero mean vector autoregression of order
p=4
. Following
28
, we construct a subset of
m=20
variables whose names and mnemonics can be found in Supplementary Table S7. Of these, interest lies in forecasting the first three – real gross domestic product (GDP251), the consumer price index (CPIAUCSL) and the Federal funds rate (FYFF) – with the remaining seventeen variables included for their potential forecasting value. The data run from quarter 1 of 1959 to quarter 4 of 2007. In order to assess the forecasting properties of various prior-model combinations, we use the data for the first
n=156
quarters for model-fitting, holding back the last 40 observations to allow assessment of out-of-sample predictive performance.

To this end, we consider a
VAR
{}_{20}\text{(}{4}\text{)}
process under five prior distributions. Except in the first case, where the error precision matrix
\mathnormal{K}
is fixed, the autoregressive coefficients
\mathsf{\Phi}
and
\mathnormal{K}
are taken to be independent
a priori
:

(i)

A Minnesota prior
(
12
)
where the error variance matrix
\mathnormal{\Sigma}
is replaced with a fixed (diagonal) estimate and
\mathsf{\Phi}
is given a multivariate normal distribution. Judicious choice of the prior mean and variance for
\mathsf{\Phi}
is intended to encourage shrinkage towards a simple set of low order univariate AR models.

(ii)

A semi-conjugate prior in which
\mathsf{\Phi}
and
\mathnormal{K}
are given multivariate normal and Wishart distributions, respectively.

(iii)

A prior for
\mathsf{\Phi}
that enforces stationarity coupled with a Wishart prior for
\mathnormal{K}
; neither distribution allows sparsity in the parameter space. The prior for
\mathsf{\Phi}
is induced through a prior on the partial autocorrelation matrices of the process
(
25
)
.

(iv)

A sparse prior that does not enforce stationarity. As in the simulation experiments, this comprises spike-and-slab priors for the off-diagonal elements of the
\mathnormal{\phi}_{s}
and a prior comprising a mixture of
G
-Wishart distributions for
\mathnormal{K}
.

(v)

Our sparsity-inducing, stationarity-enforcing prior.

Clearly, priors (i), (ii) and (iv) do not constrain inference to the stationary region, while priors (i)-(iii) do not allow sparsity. Only prior (v) removes both of these limitations. Full details of all prior specifications can be found in Supplementary Section S6.1.2.

Priors (i)-(iii) were used in an analysis of the same data in
25
, where direct sampling from the analytically tractable posterior under prior (i) and MCMC sampling under non-conjugate priors (ii) and (iii) is described in full. Under priors (iv) and (v), we ran two chains, each generating 25K samples from the posterior, following a burn-in of 25K draws, and thinning to every tenth iterate to reduce post-processing overheads. The usual graphical diagnostics gave no evidence of any lack of convergence. It is worth noting that under the three priors which did not enforce stationarity, no posterior samples lay within the stationary region under the two non-sparse priors, (i) and (ii), while all the samples under the sparse prior (iv) lay within the stationary region. This demonstrates the important regularising effect that sparsity can have, ruling out implausible regions of parameter space when stationarity is a credible assumption.

Assessment of the predictive performance of the five prior-model combinations was based on forecast horizons of
h\in\{1,2,4,8\}
time-steps, ranging from short-term, one-quarter ahead forecasts (
h=1
) to longer-term, two-year ahead forecasts (

). We use a number of proper scoring rules
(
19
)
to compare
h
-step ahead predictive distributions to the observations that materialised. At every

in the hold-out period the scores are based on the
h
-step ahead posterior predictive distributions at time

, which are then averaged. To assess forecasting performance for the three variables of interest individually we consider two widely-used scores: the continuous rank probability score (CRPS) and the logarithmic score. The energy score (ES), which is a multivariate generalisation of the CRPS, is then used to assess joint forecasts of the three variables of interest. All scores are negatively oriented, with small values indicating better performance. Further details on the scoring rules, along with additional comparisons, can be found in Supplementary Section S6.1.3.

Discussion

For many applications, it can be advantageous to assume that a time-series is stationary, for example when structural or functional constraints on the process mean the predictive variance should be bounded. In analyses based on graphical vector autoregressions, stationarity is frequently stated as a model assumption but rarely enforced as a constraint. In this paper we provide, to the best of our knowledge, the first methodology for constructing a prior for the autoregressive coefficients of a vector autoregression which simultaneously constrains inference to the stationary region and allows sparsity in the parameter space. This is achieved by introducing an auxiliary variable and then transforming the expanded set of autoregressive coefficient matrices to an expanded set of unconstrained square matrices through a mapping which preserves zeros. By assigning a sparse prior to the unconstrained square matrices, a sparse prior is induced for the autoregressive coefficient matrices that guarantees the process is stationary. Completing the prior specification with a mixture of
G
-Wishart distributions for the error precision matrix yields a prior for the parameters of a stationary graphical vector autoregression.

An MCMC scheme for computational inference is presented which combines proposals using NUTS for continuous model parameters with an efficient reversible jump sampler for the error precision matrix and its sparsity indicators. A link to software implementing this algorithm is provided in the Supplementary Material.

Through a comprehensive simulation experiment we demonstrate that when stationarity is a reasonable assumption, characterisation of posterior uncertainty in sparsity patterns is generally improved when a sparse prior that restricts inference to the stationary region is used. We posit that this may be due to improved prior-data agreement, which limits confounding between the spectral radius of the companion matrix
\mathnormal{C}_{\phi}
and the number of zeros it contains. In an application involving U.S. macroeconomic data, we demonstrate the improvements in predictive performance that can be gained by adopting a prior that limits inference to the stationary region, allows sparsity in the parameter space, or both, with the greatest improvement in predictive performance achieved in the third case. Finally, an application involving brain activity data demonstrates the scientific insights that can be gained from the posterior for the directional indicators defining the patterns of sparsity in our stationary graphical vector autoregression.

We emphasise that the ideas in this paper could be adapted readily to develop sparsity-inducing priors over other spaces subject to norm-like constraints. This includes the simplex, with implications for mixture modelling and model-based clustering.
