Introduction
The increasing penetration of electric vehicles (EVs) has prompted a rapid expansion of the EV
charging infrastructure across various environments, including residential areas, multiple-
dwelling units, public parking spaces, commercial establishments, and dedicated charging hubs.
As charging infrastructure is growing at a rapid pace, cybersecurity of these devices are critical
and should not be overlooked.

As charging stations evolved from simple power outlets to sophisticated networked devices, they
have become increasingly interconnected with vehicles, backend management systems, and even
the power grid itself. Although this interconnectedness enables advanced functionalities such as
remote monitoring, smart charging, and payment processing, it simultaneously introduces the
potential for cybersecurity vulnerabilities. These vulnerabilities not only pose risks to individual
users but also present potential threats to critical infrastructure, such as the stability and
reliability of the power grid, should these devices be manipulated at scale.

This report categorizes the top 10 security weaknesses in electric vehicle supply equipment
(EVSE) using a similar approach by the Open Worldwide Application Security Project
(OWASP) Top 10, and it describes example exploitations, methods for prevention, and related
common weakness enumerations (CWEs) for each category. The weaknesses listed in this report
could lead to security issues or vulnerabilities in EVSE if they are exploited by an attacker to
compromise the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of a system. This report also
recommends EVSE industry best practices and remediations for the identified weaknesses to use
as guidelines for their secure products and systems.

1 Top 10 Security Weaknesses in EVSE
OWASP is an open community dedicated to enabling organizations to develop, purchase, and
maintain trusted software. OWASP releases the OWASP Top 10 as an awareness document for
developers to use to ensure that their software does not contain these weaknesses or
vulnerabilities. The list is based on a survey from the community and data from developers. The
survey asks which vulnerabilities are believed to be important by the community but might not
be reflected in the data yet. The data collected from developers include the time period, the total
number of applications tested in the dataset, and the list of CWEs with counts of how many
applications contained that CWE.

This report provides the top 10 security weaknesses in EVSE by benchmarking the OWASP Top
10. After a thorough survey of reports and articles regarding EVSE security weaknesses, we
produced the top 10 security weaknesses in EVSE based on:

 • How frequent the weakness is reported.
 • How serious the potential impact of the weakness is if it is exploited.
 • How wide the potential impact of the weakness is if it is exploited.

1.1 Communication Network Security
Charging stations are interconnected with many elements throughout the network, including
vehicles, backend management systems, and mobile devices. This interconnectedness introduces
a wide range of attack surfaces and cybersecurity vulnerabilities. Further, the attacks through
these surfaces can spread to other elements throughout the network and could result in significant
consequences with large-scale deployments.

Various communication protocols—including Open Charge Point Protocol (OCPP), Open Smart
Charging Protocol (OSCP), Message Queuing Telemetry Transport (MQTT), and even
Modbus—are used to provide advanced functionalities, such as remote monitoring/control, smart
charging management, and payment processing. Many EVSEs, especially older models, might
use outdated or unencrypted communication protocols by default, such as unencrypted Hypertext
Transfer Protocol (HTTP) or Modbus. Even protocols designed for security, such as OCPP and
International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 15118, can have insecure implementations,
failing to properly use Transport Layer Security (TLS) or public key infrastructure (PKI). This
can lead to sensitive data and control commands being easily captured or intercepted by
attackers.

Insecure implementations of communication protocols can also lead to the exploitation of
firmware theft and manipulation. Attackers can manipulate proprietary firmware or operational
data, leading to the malicious reprogramming of chargers and the injection of malware into the
network. The consequences of these protocol-level vulnerabilities include widespread service
disruption, significant data theft, compromise of data integrity, unauthorized control over
charging sessions, and the potential for broader network intrusions.

1.1.1 Example Exploitations
 • Interception of communication packets: An attacker intercepts the communications
 between an EV charger and its backend system, between the EV and the charger, or

 2

 between a user and a server to attempt a man-in-the-middle attack. If the communications
 are unencrypted (e.g., using an insecure OCPP profile or unencrypted HTTP), the
 attacker could read sensitive user data or payment information, or it could manipulate
 charging commands. Further, these attacks could spoof Domain Name System (DNS)
 entries to redirect charger traffic to a rogue server. The impacts of these attacks include
 the theft of sensitive data (payment information, user credentials, vehicle identification
 numbers), unauthorized control over charging sessions (e.g., free charging, manipulated
 billing), or the injection of malicious commands.
 • Injection of excessive traffic or malformed request: An attacker floods an EV charger,
 a charging site, or the central management system with excessive traffic or malformed
 requests, overwhelming its resources and interrupting service to attempt a denial-of-
 service attack. This can be achieved by exploiting vulnerabilities in communication
 protocols or backend systems. The impacts of these attacks include disrupting charging
 services (no charging), leading to long queues, stranded vehicles, and lost revenue for
 operators, as well as undermining public trust. In severe cases, coordinated denial-of-
 service attacks on multiple chargers could destabilize the power grid, leading to
 blackouts.

1.1.2 Prevention
EVSE manufacturers should adopt communication protocols with strong security mechanisms,
follow proper implementation procedures, and validate implementations with conformance
testing. If EVSE manufacturers use open-source libraries, they should validate that the libraries
are securely implemented.

Disabling unnecessary network services and implementing strict access control to essential
services through firewalls and robust authentication mechanisms are recommended practices to
prevent cyberattacks through communication networks.

1.2 Authentication and Authorization
Attackers can leverage several weaknesses to access and control EVSE through improperly
configured authentication and authorization mechanisms. Authentication mechanisms are used to
validate the identity of a user, whereas authorization mechanisms determine which resources
users should have access to. Authentication weaknesses include devices that are shipped with
hard-coded credentials that are the same for each installation of the product and usually cannot
be changed by equipment administrators, whereas authorization weaknesses include improper
access controls where the equipment does not restrict or incorrectly restricts access to a resource
from an unauthorized user. Both weaknesses can lead to attacks where an attacker gains
unintended access to a system to disrupt services or leak sensitive data.

1.2.1 Example Exploitations
 • An EVSE manufacturer uploads code to a public GitHub repository with credentials to a
 public object storage service. The attacker searches the repository, finds the credentials,
 and steals all data from this endpoint.
 • An EVSE is deployed without changing the default administrative credentials. An
 attacker can log in with the default credentials and perform administrative functions on
 the equipment.
 • An EVSE is running a web server that is serving a database file containing the credentials
 of the administrative user. The directory on the web server does not have any
 permissions, so the attacker can download and decrypt the database file and obtain the
 administrative password.
 • An EVSE is running a web server that uses Hypertext Preprocessor (PHP) scripts to
 update the administrative credentials. The permissions for these scripts are improperly
 configured, and an attacker can run these scripts via a direct request and give themselves
 access to the system.

1.2.2 Prevention
Private and other sensitive information, such as passwords, should be stored in a protected file or
an encrypted database. Developers should use the principle of least privilege when determining
the access restrictions of users to reduce the attack surface. New EVSEs have default usernames
and passwords that are initially configured by the EVSE manufacturers. These default usernames
and passwords must be changed to a strong password upon installation to prevent improper
access to the chargers.

Developers should divide their applications into different areas for different access levels (e.g.,
(administrator, registered user, anonymous). Use role-based access control to enforce roles with
appropriate boundaries. Industry-standard cryptographic algorithms should be used to protect
locally stored credentials. Developers should take extra care to ensure that their applications use

 4

proper access control mechanisms on the server side on every page. Caching should be disabled
for pages containing sensitive information, and they should be accessible only with an active and
authenticated session token.

1.3 Firmware and Software
Firmware and software attacks allow adversaries to bypass traditional security measures and
potentially compromise entire EV charging systems. Firmware is a low-level software that
controls a device’s hardware, and software is a type of computer program that tells the
computer—in this case, an EV charging point—what to do. Attacks on an EV charger’s firmware
can disable chargers, alter basic functions, exfiltrate data, or enable remote control. Software can
harm computer systems and cause data theft. These vulnerabilities in unison could cause a loss of

 5

service for the EV chargers (Delarea 2024). Software and firmware attacks can also occur
through supply chain compromise.

1.3.1 Prevention
For these types of attacks, EV charging stakeholders should ensure that firmware and software
updates are securely signed and delivered to prevent an attacker from maliciously changing the
firmware to a modified one. Implementing a secure boot process ensures the integrity of the
charging station’s firmware and software. The secure boot process verifies the new firmware and
software by checking digital signatures against a database of trusted keys. If a signature does not
match a trusted key, the bootloader (a small program that initiates the startup process of a
computer, loading the operating system or other firmware into memory) will prevent the new
firmware or software from loading. Ultimately, this prevents unauthorized or compromised code
from being executed. Additionally, continuous firmware and software updates, secure
communication protocols, strong authentication protocols, and ongoing monitoring will aid in
keeping EV chargers secure.

1.3.2 Example Exploitations
 • Remote code execution: Remote unauthenticated users connected to the same local
 network as the EV charger controller can execute arbitrary commands with elevated
 privileges on affected devices. This vulnerability applies to cPH2 charging stations with
 eCharge Salia Plastic Leaded Chip Carrier (PLCC) firmware versions ≤2.0.4 produced by
 eCharge Hardy Barth GmbH. (Kaiser 2024). The web administrative interface runs on
 PHP with many of the PHP scripts being makeshift scripts. Lighttpd, a web server for
 high-traffic websites that is used in eCharge Hardy Barth EV chargers, is configured to
 enforce authentication, but it does not apply to vulnerable PHP scripts; therefore,
 unauthenticated remote command execution over the local area network is possible
      (Kaiser 2024).
 • Heap-based buffer overflow: If there is an integer overflow vehicle-to-grid transport
 protocol implementation, this vulnerability leads to a heap overflow. An attacker can run
 arbitrary code on the Linux process that can lead to bypassing the payment gate for
 charging, compromise private keys stored in the charging stations, and communicate with
 the vendor’s backend using OCPP by impersonating the compromised charging station.
 • Authorization bypass: An unauthenticated attacker can obtain the EV charger version
 and firmware upgrading history by knowing the charger identification. Once this is
 known, the attacker can use this information to see which current vulnerabilities the EV
 charger has based on its software or firmware version.

1.4 Physical Tampering and Unauthorized Access
Even if a charging station is situated behind a firewall, an attacker connecting physically via the
charging cable might gain direct access to internal services due to misconfigurations in the
charging station controller, underscoring the need for layered security that considers both
network and physical access vectors.

1.4.1 Prevention
Securing the ports of EVSE can prevent many physical attacks by narrowing the attack vectors
that a bad actor can use. Some ports, such as diagnostic ports or an Ethernet switch’s port, must
be open to debug problems or use the EVSE entirely. These ports need to be secured by
implementing some kind of authentication method or encryption of the traffic flowing through an
Ethernet switch. Other ports that are not being used can be disabled entirely to prevent attacks
through this vector.

Shielding the EV-EVSE charging cable reduces interference from outside sources. Although
most interference would be from unknown sources, leading to minor unintended outcomes, an
adversary might exploit unshielded cables to launch targeted attacks on EVs and EVSE.

1.4.2 Example Exploitations
 • Unauthorized access: In many cases, EVSE are constructed using many connected
 circuit boards communicating over a shared Ethernet network. By plugging into an
 unprotected Ethernet switch, bad actors can intercept inter-module traffic, much of which
 is unencrypted, leading to possible exploits being found or, even worse, payment PKI
 information being released. Unprotected Universal Serial Bus (USB) ports can be

 7

 exploited to copy logs of recent users’ authentication information and OCPP server log-in
 credentials.
 • Gaining privileged access via physical connection: An attacker gains privileged access
 to EVSE via physical connection—e.g., Joint Test Action Group (JTAG), human-
 machine interface, USB, local wireless, serial—to disable the EVSE.
 • Connection sniffing: An attacker gains access via ISO 15118 sniffing, EV-EVSE
 connection sniffing, or a man-in-the-middle attack.
 • Denial of service: Attackers can launch attacks wirelessly by exploiting an unshielded
 charging cable’s ability to ack as antennas. The attacker can inject a busy single into the
 EV and/or EVSE by sending a signal to the charging cable on a specific channel. The EV
 and/or EVSE will wait for the busy signal to resolve, which in some current protocols
 does not happen by default. In some cases, the charging session must be restarted, but in
 most cases, the EV and/or EVSE need to be manually reset, leading to the EVSE being
 offline until the reset can occur.

1.5 Backend Cloud Systems
Backend cloud management systems are responsible for processing consumption data,
authenticating users, managing billing, supervising EV charging, and controlling the
configuration of charging stations. Attacks on these backend systems or the cloud portals used by
customers can result in large data breaches and widespread operational disruption and
manipulation.

Vulnerabilities in backend systems frequently serve as initial access points for more extensive
attacks, allowing attackers to move laterally and escalate privileges within the operator’s or
manufacturer’s broader infrastructure. Attackers often gain this initial access by exploiting
traditional cyberattack techniques, such as phishing, leveraging generic software vulnerabilities,
or exploiting open port connections. The concentration of control and data in these systems
means that a successful attack can have disproportionately massive consequences across the
entire EV charging ecosystem.

1.5.1 Example Exploitations
 • Phishing and malware deployment: An attacker gains initial network access via
 phishing and use malware to gain access to the backend server, which can result in

 8

 consequences such as data theft or even system lock down. This attack type can result in
 widespread service outages, leading to potentially significant financial losses and damage
 to operator reputation and public trust.
 • Injection of malicious code or compromised firmware: An attacker gains access to the
 backend server by injecting malicious code or compromised firmware through an
 exposed application programming interface (API) endpoint or Structured Query
 Language (SQL) injection flaw in a web application.

1.5.2 Prevention
A high level of security for backend cloud systems—such as advanced threat intelligence,
anomaly detection, stringent access controls, or comprehensive data encryption—could prevent
them from attacks. Further, although technical controls are vital, the reliance on traditional attack
vectors like phishing indicates that human vulnerabilities remain a persistent entry point;
therefore, a comprehensive security strategy should integrate robust human-centric defenses,
including continuous security awareness training, strong email security protocols, and mandatory
multifactor authentication for all administrative and user access.

1.6 Payment Systems
The ability to exchange payment for electricity to charge an EV is the basic function of an
EVSE. Several mechanisms can be used to verify the customer and pass this information to the
charge network operator to process the payment, such as plug-and-charge, radio-frequency
identification (RFID) cards, or smartphone apps. All these systems rely on some sort of
authentication method, such as certificates using a PKI or a unique identifier (UID), such as
RFID UID cards. Although most of these methods are seen as secure, in practice there can be
some gaps in their security when they are incorrectly implemented.

1.6.1 Example Exploitations
 • Connection sniffing: Bad actors could steal valid certificates issued by a valid root
 certificate authority. The EVSE manufacturer would then revoke this certificate to
 prevent the certificate from being used. Due to inconsistent revocation polices, an EV
 could still see this certificate as being valid and pass payment and other personally
 identifiable information to the EVSE using this certificate, and a bad actor could intercept
 this information.
 • RFID cloning: A device could be used to copy RFID tags. These copy tags could be
 used at EVSE to charge an EV while the payment is charged to the customer associated
 with the original RFID tag.
 • Sensitive information storage: A bad actor with physical access to an EVSE could copy
 authorization caches from a microSD on the EVSE controller to bill any transaction to
 other customers by pulling an RFID UID from the microSD. These RFID UIDs should be
 scrubbed from the authorization cache.

1.6.2 Prevention
PKI is used for verification, encryption, and authentication throughout the EVSE infrastructure.
Although PKI has been used for many years in other protocols, such as HTTPS, the adaption to
charging infrastructure could result in implementation gaps on a large scale, especially with
conflicting policies and stakeholder interactions. Solutions to gaps include creating and
implementing a unified certificate policy for all root hierarchies, improving the certificate
revocation policies, and creating life cycle management.

The RFID authentication method typically includes a UID stored on the RFID card or other
RFID token. This UID is used by the payment system to bill the customer that is associated with
the UID, but these UIDs can be easily copied with an RFID device that is capable of reading an
RFID token and writing to a new card or token. Transitioning to plug-and-charge prevents the
potential of physical copying, enabling a more secure option of paying.

Some EVSE controllers are equipped with a microSD card to store payment authorization caches
for transactions completed with the EVSE. Although this cache has been seen to store
information used for logging or debugging, it also stores sensitive payment information, such as
information from other EVSE or RFID UIDs, which can be used to repeatedly bill a transaction.
These caches should be scrubbed of all payment information to prevent unauthorized billing

1.7 Web Applications and API
Many charging network operators provide mobile or web services to pay utilities, monitor
charging status, check charging station availability, or even remotely manage charging. These
services offer convenience and smart functionalities to users but introduce more attack surfaces
at the same time. Attacks through weak security of the web and mobile applications associated
with charging services may propagate to other systems or services, which can lead to serious and
widespread impact on overall charging systems.

Mobile applications used in EV ecosystem are also reported to have security vulnerabilities. Jun,
Cryar, and Markel (2024) investigated the security attributes and vulnerabilities of a sample of
mobile apps that support key user functions in the EV ecosystem and showed that all analyzed
apps have security risks, categorized as high or medium or both. Therefore, even if the charging
stations themselves possess some level of security, weak security in the associated web and
mobile applications can provide attackers with alternative pathways into the wider EV charging
ecosystem.

1.7.1 Example Exploitations
 • Gaining access using default credentials or a weak password policy: An attacker
 discovers an EV charger’s web interface or a backend server API endpoint that uses
 default credentials or has a weak password policy. They use publicly available
 information (such as hard-coded credentials) or brute force techniques to gain
 administrative access. Once authenticated, they can reconfigure charging station settings,
 access sensitive operational data, or retrieve proprietary information.
 • SQL injection: An attacker identifies a web application vulnerability, such as an SQL
 injection flaw, in the charge point operator’s user management portal or an API endpoint
 that handles user data. They exploit this flaw to gain unauthorized access to the backend
 database, exfiltrating vast amounts of sensitive customer data, including names, email
 addresses, payment information, and charging history.

1.7.2 Prevention
Developers of web interfaces or API should integrate security from inception by applying
secure-by-design principles, such as cyber-informed engineering, from the earliest stages of
development for all web applications, APIs, and firmware that interact with EVSE. This includes
considering security for all components, software, network configurations, and data management
processes from the outset. Strengthening authentication and authorization mechanisms, ensuring
secure data handling and storage, implementing robust input validation and error handling, and
ensuring secure configuration management are some measures that should be considered in
development.

All software, including third-party libraries and open-source components, should be regularly
reviewed for potential weaknesses. Automated tools can be used to scan for outdated
components with known vulnerabilities.

1.8 Integration With Power Grid
Connecting EV chargers to the grid creates vulnerabilities surrounding grid stability because of
the increased and potentially unpredictable electric load they place on the grid. Uncontrolled
charging can cause sudden load changes, creating a significant spike in demand and making it
difficult for grid operators to maintain stability. An attack on multiple chargers could lead to

 12

potential blackouts by destabilizing the grid. Further, if many vehicles are charging in the same
area, it could cause localized stress on the grid and lead to potential outages in that region.
Ultimately, integrating charging points with the grid increases the amount of cybersecurity risks
that can impact grid stability.

1.8.1 Example Exploitations
 • Oscillations in grid frequency: By switching a large group of EV chargers on and off
 from the grid simultaneously, attackers have the potential to induce grid oscillations.
 Attacks of this nature, if large enough, can destabilize the grid frequency and force
 generators to trip, which can lead to a power outage.
 • False data injection: These attacks tamper with the grid’s measurement by introducing
 malicious or false data into the EV charging system. This threatens power grid stability
 by causing load peaks or drops, which can lead to blackouts, if done at scale.

1.8.2 Prevention
Proactive monitoring and incident response can detect abnormal energy usage, unauthorized
charging, or suspicious network activity in real time and promptly and effectively manage
cybersecurity incidents, including those that could impact grid stability.

It is a good practice to follow established cybersecurity standards and regulations—such as the
National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) IR 8473 (Cybersecurity Framework
Profile for Electric Vehicle Extreme Fast Charging Infrastructure), ISO 27001, SOC 2 Type II,
and International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) 62443/62351—to prevent power grid
vulnerabilities because these frameworks provide a structured approach to managing
cybersecurity risks in critical infrastructure.

Additionally, it is good practice to implement smart charging techniques that optimize charging
schedules to align with periods of low demand, thus reducing the strain on the grid during peak
demand periods. Smart charging consists of load shifting and optimized charging schedules.
During load shifting, strain on the grid during peak demand periods is reduced by shifting EV
charging to off-peak hours. To optimize charging schedules, algorithms can improve charging
schedules to minimize energy costs and maximize the use of renewable energy sources.

1.9 Data Security and Privacy
Data security and privacy vulnerabilities in EVSE are primarily caused by weaknesses in how
sensitive information is collected, transmitted, stored, and managed across the interconnected
components in EV ecosystems. Insecure data transmission or data storage can result in data
breaches, leading to identity theft, financial fraud (e.g., unauthorized transactions using stolen
payment information), privacy violations, and reputational damage for the charging network
operator.

1.9.1 Example Exploitations
 • An attacker gains unauthorized access to a charging station or its backend system,
 potentially through insecure web interfaces or physical tampering. Then they manipulate
 the consumption data reported by the charger, falsifying meter readings to enable free
 charging or to generate incorrect invoices for legitimate users. The impacts of this attack
 include financial losses for operators, violations of legal regulations, and service
 interruptions affecting billing processes and interoperability with partner charging
 networks.
 • EV charging applications and stations collect precise location data, driving patterns, and
 charging habits. If these datapoints are exposed due to insecure storage or transmission,
 attackers can aggregate the data to create detailed profiles of individuals’ movements and
 daily routines. This information can then be used for surveillance, stalking, or other
 malicious activities.
 • An attacker exploits vulnerabilities in poorly secured smartphone applications that are
 used to manage customer charging sessions. This could involve insecure authentication,
 weak session management, or data leakage flaws, leading to unauthorized access to a
 user’s account.

1.9.2 Prevention
Data security vulnerabilities caused by insecure data transmission and storage can be prevented
by using end-to-end encryption, secure communication protocols, and data encryption. For
secure data storage and handling, it is a good measure to collect and store only the data that are
strictly necessary for the operation of the charging station and to limit the retention period of the
user data to the shortest time possible while still meeting legal and business requirements.

1.10 Supply Chain
This is a weakness introduced during the development, manufacturing, delivery, or integration
process. The supply chain for EV charging infrastructure involves numerous third-party
hardware manufacturers, software developers, and service providers, which allows for multiple
ways in which a bad actor could manipulate the supply chain. This complexity creates multiple
points where vulnerabilities can be introduced, such as insecure components and manufacturing
practices, unpatched third-party software and libraries, and poor vendor support and life cycle
issues.

1.10.1 Example Exploitations
 • Back-doored firmware from third-party vendor: A third-party vendor hides a
 backdoor in firmware and supplies it to an EVSE manufacturer. This compromised
 firmware can be used for the remote takeover of chargers, the manipulation of charging
 behavior, or the theft of data. This may also include unintentional exploitation in which a
 third-party includes a firmware backdoor for legitimate monitoring purposes but doesn’t
 document this protocol with the manufacturer.
 • Tampered or counterfeit chips with malicious embedded codes: An attacker
 compromises or replaces legitimate components, often at the manufacturing or
 integration stage, with tampered ones containing hidden, malicious logic or embedded
 code. Malicious chips can stealthily execute unauthorized functions, for example,
 charging behavior manipulation, payment and authentication compromise, network
 intrusion, firmware and OTA update alteration, etc.
 • Supply chain compromise of SDK or library: An attacker compromises a third-party
 SDK or open-source libraries used in EVSE firmware or backend services by injecting
 malicious code into the GitHub repository or a package distribution server. If the
 compromised codes are incorporated into the EVSE SW build, the chargers or backend
 systems that use it become infected. The injected code can perform stealthy, unauthorized
 transmission of sensitive information and unauthorized command execution from the
 central system, resulting in remote manipulation of charging sessions, disabling firmware
 updates, pushing rogue configurations, or pivoting into backend networks that manage
 EVSE operations.

1.10.2 Prevention
Preventing supply chain vulnerabilities in EVSE requires a comprehensive, multilayered strategy
that addresses both physical security and cybersecurity risks while considering broader logistical
dependencies and geopolitical factors. This includes:

 • Rigorously vetting vendors and performing in-field monitoring.
 • Having an understanding of a product’s software bill-of-materials (SBOM) and hardware
 bill-of-materials (HBOM).
 • Implementing secure-by-design principles or cyber-informed engineering across the
 supply chain.
 • Ensuring secure software and firmware management by implement strong code
 signing/verification, continuous auditing codes and logs, etc.
 • Complying with industry standards and regulations.

  Strategies to EVSE Industry

Related CWEs in each category of the top 10 security weaknesses in EVSE are listed in the

2.1 CWE-20: Improper Input Validation
Improper input validation usually occurs when a software application receives input or data but
fails to correctly verify that the input possesses the necessary properties for safe and accurate
processing. Common consequences of the vulnerability include altered program flow, reading
confidential data, unauthorized resource control, or even arbitrary code execution.

Figure 1 shows an example Python script that has improper input validation. The example code
assumes that user_id is numeric and does not check whether the value is numeric or not; thus,
the code crashes if an attacker sends a non-numeric value for the variable. If the code is
connected to a database, this attack could enable SQL injection. This can be remediated by
validating inputs, as illustrated in Figure 2.

 from flask import Flask, request

 app = Flask(__name__)

 @app.route('/user')
 def get_user():
 user_id = request.args.get('id') # No validation!
 return f"Looking up user with ID: {int(user_id)}" # Potential crash or injection

 app.run()
 Figure 1. Example Python code with CWE-20 with no input validation

Testing for improper input validation involves systematically feeding unexpected, malformed, or

automated security testing for improper input validation by feeding unexpected, malformed, or
malicious data for specific input validation or brute force random data for broader validation.

Cleartext transmission of sensitive information happens commonly when sensitive or security-
critical data are transmitted over a communication channel without encryption. This can lead to

2.3 CWE-494: Download of Code Without Integrity Check
This weakness refers to downloading executable code (e.g., Python modules, scripts, or binaries)
from a remote source and executing it without verifying its integrity or authenticity. An example
code shown in Figure 5 uses HTTP without encryption or authentication and runs downloaded
code without checking the integrity or authenticity.

To fix the issues and prevent the vulnerability, the script should download over HTTPS to
prevent man-in-the-middle attacks and perform a Secure Hash Algorithm (SHA)-256 hash check
to ensure no tampering of the code. A sample remediation of the code is illustrated in Figure 6.

 # Downloading and executing remote code without integrity check
 import urllib.request

 url = "http://example.com/remote_script.py" # Note: HTTP, no integrity
 response = urllib.request.urlopen(url)
 code = response.read().decode()

 exec(code) # Executing unverified code

2.7 CWE-639: Authorization Bypass Through User-Controlled Key
This vulnerability occurs when a system’s authorization functionality does not prevent one user
from gaining access to another user’s data or record by modifying the key value identifying the
data. Using a known charger’s identification, an adversary could retrieve EV charger version
information and firmware upgrade history. The attacker can use this information to find which
current vulnerabilities the EV charger has based on its software or firmware version. The code in
Figure 13 lacks an authentication mechanism; thus, an attacker can guess or discover the
charger’s identification.

2.8 CWE-77: Improper Neutralization of Special Elements Used in a
 Command (“Command Injection”)
The vulnerability occurs when a program constructs and executes system-level commands using
untrusted input and fails to sanitize or neutralize special characters (e.g., ;, &&, |, &). This allows
attackers to inject arbitrary commands, potentially leading to full system compromise. Tools
such as Bandit, Semgrep, Burp Suit, or OWASP ZAP can be used for testing command injection.

An example code with this vulnerability is illustrated in Figure 15. The code gets a user input for
the system command execution, and it allows an attacker to run arbitrary system commands. To
avoid the vulnerability, shell should not be invoked, command arguments should be kept
isolated, and inputs should be sanitized by using allowlists. An example remediation is shown in
Figure 16. The remedied code does not invoke a shell and passes arguments as isolated tokens.

2.9 CWE-294: Authentication Bypass by Capture-Replay
This vulnerability happens when an attacker captures a valid authentication message in transit—
such as a password, token, or session cookie—and reuses or replays it to gain unauthorized
access even without knowing the original credentials. The code in Figure 17 does not use
encryption for the log in, and an attacker can capture and resend the same request because the
requests are replayable in the code. To prevent replay, best practices include using traffic
encryption, token expiration, a unique identification, time window validation, and session
binding. The code in Figure 18 uses a time stamp in the token creation and a time window
validation.

 return "Login failed"

import hmac, hashlib, time
from flask import Flask, request, abort

app = Flask(__name__)
SECRET_KEY = b'my_secret_key'
TIME_WINDOW = 30 # seconds

def generate_token(username, timestamp):
  message = f"{username}:{timestamp}".encode()
  return hmac.new(SECRET_KEY, message, hashlib.sha256).hexdigest()

@app.route('/login', methods=['POST'])
def login():
  username = request.form.get('username')
  timestamp = request.form.get('timestamp')
  token = request.form.get('token')

  if not username or not timestamp or not token:
      abort(400)

  # Validate time window
  if abs(time.time() - float(timestamp)) > TIME_WINDOW:
 return "Request expired", 401

  # Validate token
  expected_token = generate_token(username, timestamp)
  if hmac.compare_digest(expected_token, token):
 return "Login successful"
  return "Invalid token", 403
 Figure 18. Example remediation of CWE-294 by using a time stamp to prevent replay

3 Summary and Conclusions
Potential impacts of security breaches in the EV charging ecosystem intensify as EV charging
stations evolve from stand-alone charging devices to integral parts of a larger network that
encompasses the EV itself, the charging network operator’s backend systems, and the power
grid. Similar to other complex interconnected infrastructures, a complex array of cybersecurity
vulnerabilities can pose risks to users, operators, and national infrastructure.

This report provides a list of 10 vulnerability categories for EVSE security based on statistics as
well as the severity and scope of the potential impacts. Each category has an example attack
scenario, potential impacts, methods for prevention, and related CWEs. This report also provides
example codes that have vulnerabilities related to EVSE security along with remediations, test
tools, and procedures. This will help increase workforce knowledge and advance the
cybersecurity maturity of the industry to recognize insecure coding practices.

For the safe, reliable, and trustworthy expansion of electric mobility, stakeholders across the
automotive, energy, and cybersecurity sectors should address the interconnected cybersecurity
vulnerabilities in EVSE with a comprehensive, multilayered security approach.
