Unraveling the roles of DKK1 and S100P in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma: Insights from machine learning, single-cell/spatial transcriptomics, and functional validation
Authors: Yanzhao Mao, Yuqi Zhang, Yupeng Zhang, Dongwei Lin, Yuelin Song, Xiaojiang Niu, Peihao Xv, Yuliang Wang, Zhigang Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Multi-omics integration and machine learning identified DKK1 and S100Pas PDAC prognostic genes.•scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics revealed distinct expression patterns in the tumor microenvironment.•Functional assays confirmed that DKK1 and S100P significantly promote PDAC cell proliferation and migration.

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is one of the most lethal malignancies worldwide. Despite ongoing advances in diagnostic and therapeutic techniques, the prognosis of PDAC remains poor. The 5-year survival rate is only about 13%, and PDAC is currently the third leading cause of cancer-related death [1,2]. Because early symptoms are often subtle or nonspecific, most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage and therefore miss the opportunity for curative treatment. In addition, the marked genomic heterogeneity of PDAC and its highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) contribute to limited sensitivity to conventional chemotherapy and radiotherapy. These challenges highlight the need to identify reliable biomarkers and molecular targets for more precise diagnosis and treatment.

Machine learning (ML), as an important branch of artificial intelligence, has become a useful tool for biomarker discovery and risk stratification [3]. By integrating large-scale omics data, ML can identify molecular features associated with clinical outcomes and improve the predictive performance of prognostic models. Meanwhile, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) makes it possible to investigate intratumoral heterogeneity at single-cell resolution and to characterize the functional states of different cellular populations within tumor tissues, including malignant cells, immune cells, and stromal cells [4]. Spatial transcriptomics (ST) further preserves spatial information, allowing researchers to examine cell-cell interactions and the spatial organization of the tumor microenvironment [5].

In this study, we established a multi-omics analytical framework by integrating bulk transcriptomic datasets, scRNA-seq, and ST data to systematically investigate candidate biomarkers and malignant epithelial heterogeneity in PDAC. Through LASSO and SVM-RFE-based feature selection, we identified four candidate genes, DKK1, S100P, NMU, and IGFBP3. External validation and survival sensitivity analyses supported DKK1 as the most robust prognostic marker, while S100P showed stable diagnostic performance and partial prognostic relevance. NMU and IGFBP3 were more cautiously interpreted as candidate genes associated with specific cellular states and microenvironmental variation. Single-cell analysis of inferCNV-defined malignant epithelial cells further revealed that these genes were linked to distinct malignant epithelial programs and dynamic state transitions. DKK1 was preferentially associated with high-plasticity, invasive, or proliferative epithelial states, whereas S100P showed broader epithelial expression and was also enriched in differentiated epithelial states. CellChat and RCTD-based spatial analyses suggested that DKK1-high and S100P-high epithelial states were embedded in distinct epithelial–microenvironmental communication contexts. Functional experiments further confirmed that knockdown of DKK1 or S100P significantly inhibited PDAC cell proliferation and migration. These findings highlight the clinical and biological relevance of DKK1 and S100P in PDAC and provide a refined framework for understanding malignant epithelial heterogeneity and tumor–microenvironment organization.

Materials and methods

Data collection

Five PDAC-related bulk transcriptomic datasets were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO): GSE15471, GSE16515, GSE22780, GSE55643, and GSE125158. To reduce platform-related heterogeneity and avoid potential information leakage from random splitting, the datasets were divided according to microarray platform. The GPL570-based datasets (GSE15471, GSE16515, and GSE22780; 103 tumor samples and 83 normal controls) were used as the training cohort for DEG screening and machine learning-based feature selection, whereas the GPL6480-based datasets (GSE55643 and GSE125158; 62 tumor samples and 21 normal controls) were reserved as an independent testing cohort to assess cross-platform robustness. Datasets were included if they contained human PDAC tissue samples, normal pancreatic controls, available expression matrices, and clear sample annotations. These datasets were selected to capture the common biological contrast between PDAC tumors and normal pancreatic tissues, rather than to preferentially include datasets with similar results. We obtained transcriptome data and clinical information for pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) using the “TCGAbiolinks” R package [6], including data from a total of 154 PAAD patients with survival information. scRNA-seq data was sourced from GSE205013 (27 PDAC tumor samples). ST data originated from GSE235315 (7 PDAC tumor samples with spatial coordinates).

Processing of bulk RNA-seq data

Integrated gene expression matrices were constructed from five GEO datasets (GSE15471, GSE16515, GSE22780, GSE55643, GSE125158) using R v4.3.1. Batch effects across datasets were corrected via ComBat-seq (sva v3.48.0) with platform-specific adjustment. The training set comprised GSE15471/GSE16515/GSE22780 (GPL570 platform: 103 tumors, 83 normals), while GSE55643/GSE125158 (GPL6480 platform: 62 tumors, 21 normals) formed the test set.

Differential gene expression analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using limma v3.56.2 with empirical Bayes moderation. Significance thresholds were set at |log2(fold-change)| > 2.0 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. DEG visualization included: Hierarchical clustering (pheatmap v1.0.12: Euclidean distance, complete linkage), Volcano plots (ggplot2 v3.4.2: red = upregulated, blue = downregulated).

Processing of scRNA-seq data

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis was performed using Seurat (v4.3.1). Cells with fewer than 300 detected genes, high mitochondrial gene content (>15%), low ribosomal gene content (<3%), or high hemoglobin gene content (>0.1%) were excluded. Genes detected in fewer than three cells, mitochondrial genes, hemoglobin genes and MALAT1 were removed before downstream analysis. Potential doublets were identified using scDblFinder with an FDR threshold of 0.05 and were removed before normalization, dimensionality reduction and clustering. UMAP plots before and after doublet removal were generated to visualize the effect of doublet filtering. After quality control and doublet removal, retained cells were log-normalized and scaled using Seurat. The top 2,000 highly variable genes were selected for PCA, followed by batch correction using Harmony and UMAP visualization. Cell clusters were identified using the Leiden algorithm. Major cell types were annotated by integrating cluster-specific marker genes, multiple canonical lineage markers and reference information from CellMarker 2.0, followed by manual curation. The canonical markers used for annotation included EPCAM, PROM1 and KRT19 for epithelial cells; CD3D, CD3E, CD3G, NCR1 and GNLY for T/NK cells; LST1, ITGAX, CD14, C1QB, CD68 and LYZ for myeloid cells; COL1A1, COL1A2, DCN and LUM for fibroblasts; PECAM1, VWF, RAMP2 and CLDN5 for endothelial cells; MS4A1, CD19 and CD79A for B cells; MZB1, JCHAIN and IGKC for plasma cells; TPSAB1, TPSB2 and KIT for mast cells; and RGS5, ACTA2 and MCAM for mural cells. Malignant epithelial cells were identified using inferCNV. Myeloid cells were used as reference diploid cells, and epithelial cells showing broad chromosomal CNV patterns were classified as malignant. Low-CNV epithelial cells were excluded from tumor epithelial downstream analyses. Subclustering, CytoTRACE, pseudotime and CellChat analyses were subsequently repeated using only inferCNV-defined malignant epithelial cells.

Malignant epithelial-state annotation and Hallmark enrichment analysis

Cluster-specific marker genes of malignant epithelial subclusters were identified using the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat. The expression patterns of representative marker genes were visualized using the ClusterGVis R package. Scaled expression values of selected marker genes were displayed as a heatmap to show transcriptional differences among malignant epithelial states. To characterize the biological programs of each malignant epithelial subcluster, Hallmark pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the enrichCluster function in ClusterGVis with Hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) as custom gene sets. Representative enriched pathways were integrated with the marker-gene heatmap using ClusterGVis. This analysis was used to annotate functional malignant epithelial states, including differentiated/gastric-like, invasive or ECM-remodelling, proliferative, inflammatory, secretory, ciliated-like, and EMT/stress-like epithelial programs. Bulk-derived Moffitt basal-like and classical-like gene sets were assessed using AddModuleScore only as supportive transcriptional program scores. These scores were interpreted as bulk-derived basal-like or classical-like transcriptional program activity, rather than definitive single-cell subtype assignments.

Cell–cell communication analysis

Cell–cell communication analysis was performed using CellChat to infer ligand–receptor communication programs among malignant epithelial cells and other major cell populations [7]. The human CellChat ligand–receptor database was used for communication inference. To evaluate communication programs associated with key-gene-high epithelial states, malignant epithelial cells were stratified according to DKK1 and S100P expression. For DKK1, epithelial cells with detectable DKK1 expression were defined as DKK1-high epithelial cells, whereas cells without detectable DKK1 expression were defined as DKK1-low epithelial cells. For S100P, which was broadly expressed across malignant epithelial cells, S100P-high and S100P-low epithelial cells were defined according to upper and lower expression quantiles, and intermediate cells were excluded from this comparison. CellChat analysis was then repeated using DKK1-high/DKK1-low or S100P-high/S100P-low epithelial groups together with other major cell populations. Incoming and outgoing signaling pathways associated with DKK1-high and S100P-high malignant epithelial cells were identified. Representative communication pathways, including MIF-, MK/MDK-, and SPP1-related signaling, were visualized. These pathways were interpreted as state-associated communication programs rather than direct molecular interactions mediated by DKK1 or S100P themselves.

CytoTRACE and pseudotime trajectory analysis

Pseudotime trajectory analysis was performed using Monocle 2 based only on inferCNV-defined malignant epithelial cells. These cells were reprocessed independently rather than projected from the full-cell UMAP. Briefly, malignant epithelial cells were extracted, normalized, and subjected to variable feature selection and dimensionality reduction before trajectory reconstruction. Highly variable genes and cluster-specific marker genes were used as ordering genes for Monocle 2 analysis. The root state was determined according to the trajectory state with the highest median CytoTRACE score and enrichment of high-plasticity or proliferative malignant epithelial subclusters, including Epi_VIM, Epi_CENPF and Epi_PCNA. Pseudotime was then oriented from this high-CytoTRACE root state toward more differentiated epithelial states. Arrows were added to the trajectory plots to indicate the inferred direction from lower to higher pseudotime.

Processing of spatial transcriptomic data

Spatial transcriptome (ST) sequencing analysis was performed using Seurat (v4.3.1) with the following workflow: Genes detected at ≥ 10 counts in ≥ 3 spots were retained as expressed, while spots containing < 200 expressed genes were filtered. Gene expression was normalized across spots using the SCTransform function, followed by dimensionality reduction via Principal Component Analysis (PCA) incorporating 30 principal components. Clustering analysis sequentially executed RunPCA, FindNeighbors (k = 20), FindClusters (resolution = 0.4), and RunUMAP functions. Visualization included cluster distributions (DimPlot), spatial tissue mapping (SpatialDimPlot), and gene-specific spatial patterns (SpatialFeaturePlot) using default parameters.

Integration of ST and scRNA-seq data

Spatial cell type deconvolution was performed using the RCTD module (v2.2.1) in SpaceXR, leveraging scRNA-seq data as reference [8]. Briefly, cell-type-specific expression signatures were computed by averaging transcriptional profiles across scRNA-seq clusters, after which spatial transcriptomes were modeled as linear combinations of these reference signatures. The observed RNA counts per spatial barcode were statistically decomposed into contributions from multiple cell types through maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) to infer cell type proportions per spatial pixel. An exhaustive combinatorial search then identified optimal cell type mixtures (single or multiple) within each location, ultimately generating spatially-resolved cell type maps anchored to the scRNA-seq atlas.

Spatial malignancy mapping

Malignant cells were identified using SpaCET v1.0.0 [9]. Pathologist-annotated H&E slides were registered to Visium images via 10x Loupe Browser (v7.1.0) to generate binary tumor masks. The deconvolution model was configured with PDAC core markers (KRT19/EPCAM/MUC1) and exclusion markers (PTPRC/COL1A1/DCN). Spots with malignancy probability > 0.7 and epithelial score > 2.0 (per SpaCET's internal metric) were classified as malignant.

Enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment analyses elucidated the biological significance of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) through integrated bioinformatics workflows. Gene annotation via the org.Hs.eg.db R package preceded Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses using clusterProfiler, characterizing enrichment across molecular function (MF), cellular component (CC), and biological process (BP) domains while interrogating metabolic/signaling pathways. Complementarily, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) leveraged the MSigDB database (v2023.1, accessed September 1, 2023; http://www.gsea-msigdb.org) to evaluate predefined gene sets for statistical enrichment in ranked DEG lists, establishing phenotype-gene set associations.

Selection of prognostic features

Machine learning-based feature selection identified pivotal pancreatic cancer biomarkers by applying LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) and SVM-RFE (Support Vector Machine-Recursive Feature Elimination) algorithms to previously derived DEGs. SVM-RFE, a supervised learning method, constructs an optimal hyperplane for classification tasks, leveraging feature importance weights to rank gene contributions. Concurrently, LASSO regression induces model sparsity through L1 regularization penalty, compressing regression coefficients toward zero via iterative optimization of the objective function (loss + L1 penalty), thereby selecting non-redundant predictive genes.

Validation of the prognostic features

Integrative biomarker validation was performed to identify core diagnostic signatures for pancreatic cancer. Candidate biomarkers were selected by intersecting genes identified by support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression using Venn analysis. The diagnostic accuracy of the selected biomarkers was evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis using the glmnet and pROC R packages, and area under the curve (AUC) values were calculated to quantify classification performance. Differential expression and diagnostic performance were further validated in an independent test cohort combining GSE55643 and GSE125158, which included 62 pancreatic cancer samples and 21 normal pancreatic samples. Prognostic relevance was assessed by Kaplan–Meier survival analysis using the survival package (v3.5–7) and survminer package (v0.4.9). Maximally selected rank statistics were used in the exploratory analysis to determine the optimal expression cutoff for each biomarker. Given that this data-driven approach may be affected by multiple-testing bias, the resulting optimal-cutoff survival results were interpreted cautiously. To evaluate robustness, additional sensitivity analyses were performed using median-expression and quartile-based cutoff strategies. For the median-based analysis, patients were divided into high- and low-expression groups according to the median expression value of each gene. For the quartile-based analysis, patients in the upper quartile were compared with those in the lower quartile. Kaplan–Meier curves were used to visualize survival differences, and log-rank tests were used to assess statistical significance. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Immune infiltration analysis

Immune cell profiling was conducted using CIBERSORT, a deconvolution algorithm quantifying the relative proportions of 22 immune cell subtypes within complex tissues based on gene expression signatures, enabling large-scale analysis of cellular biomarkers and therapeutic targets. This study applied CIBERSORT to evaluate associations between all training set samples and immune cell compositions, with subsequent visualization of pancreatic cancer-specific abundance patterns via corrplot and vioplot packages, including heatmap generation and statistical analysis of intercellular correlations and differential abundances (FDR < 0.05).

Correlation analysis between pivotal genes and immune infiltration levels

Correlation analysis was performed to investigate intrinsic associations between pivotal genes and immune infiltration levels using Spearman's rank-order correlation, with results visualized via the ggplot2 package.

Drug sensitivity analysis

To predict susceptibility to different drugs in PAAD patients within the high and low risk score groups, drug treatment sensitivity was assessed using the R package "oncoPredict" [10]. This analysis used expression matrices from the Cancer Drug Sensitivity Genomics (GDSC) database and drug treatment information as a training set. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) served as the primary indicator for evaluating the sensitivity of PAAD cancer cells drugs. Differences in IC50 between the high and low risk score groups were compared using the Wilcoxon test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Cell lines and culture conditions

Human cell lines (HPDE6-C7, BxPC-3 and Panc-1) were obtained from the Shanghai Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. All cell lines were subjected to comprehensive quality authentication, including morphological examination, post-thaw viability assessment, isoenzyme profiling, STR genotyping, and rigorous screening for mycoplasma, bacterial, and fungal contamination. Primary human pancreatic ductal epithelial cells (HPDE6-C7) and pancreatic adenocarcinoma cell line BxPC-3 were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Inc.) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Inc.). The Panc-1 pancreatic adenocarcinoma cell line was cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Inc.) with 10% FBS. All cell lines were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ (Thermo Scientific, Inc.). Media were refreshed every 48 h, and cells were subcultured at 80–90% confluency using 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, Inc.).

siRNA transfection

Small interfering RNAs (RiboBio, Inc.) were used to knock down DKK1 and S100P. The DKK1 siRNA sequences are 5′-GCCGGAUACAGAAAGAUCATT-3′ (siDKK1–1) and 5′-GUACCAAGCAUAGGAGAAATT-3′ (siDKK1–2). The S100P siRNA sequences are 5′-GGACUUCAGUGAGUUCAUATT-3′ (siS100P-1) and 5′-GCACGCAGACCCUGACCAATT-3′ (siS100P-2). The riboFECT CP Transfection Kit (RiboBio, Inc.) was used for the transfection of siRNA.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

RNA extraction and qPCR analysis were performed as follows: Total RNA was isolated using RNAisoPlus reagent (Takara Bio, Inc.). Extracted RNA underwent reverse transcription to cDNA via the PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix kit (Perfect Real Time, Takara Bio, Inc.). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) utilized 2 × M5 HiPer Realtime PCR Super mix (Polymerase Ltd.) on an Applied Biosystems StepOne™ Plus system. Relative gene expression was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as endogenous control. All data represent mean values from triplicate technical replicates. Primer sequences are detailed in Table S1.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail, and total protein was collected after centrifugation. Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein from each sample were loaded and separated by SDS-PAGE, followed by transfer onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against DKK1 (A2562, ABclonal), S100P (A20795, ABclonal), GAPDH (AC001, ABclonal), and β-actin (AC038, ABclonal). GAPDH or β-actin was used as the internal control. After three washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Protein signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence reagent and imaged with a chemiluminescence detection system. The relative protein expression levels were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to GAPDH or β-actin. This assay was used to validate the endogenous protein expression of DKK1 and S100P and to confirm siRNA-mediated knockdown efficiency in pancreatic cancer cells.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) viability assays

Cell viability was examined using a CCK-8 assay (Sevenbio, China, Inc.). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 4 × 103 cells per well in 200 μL medium for 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, 96 h, 120 h. The absorbance was detected at 450 nm after the cells were treated with 10% CCK-8 at 37 °C for 2 h. Cell viability was calculated as a ratio of optical density (OD) values of drug-treated samples to those of controls.

Migration assay

A Transwell assay was performed to assess PDAC cell migration using 8-μm pore inserts (Corning, Inc.). Briefly, 5 × 104 BxPC-3 cells or 3 × 104 Panc-1 cells suspended in 200 μL serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber, while 600 μL medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 48 or 72 h. Cells remaining on the upper surface of the membrane were removed, and migrated cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 30 min, washed three times with PBS, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet.

Colony formation assay

In the colony formation assay, tumor cells were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 10³ cells per well, with three replicates per group. After 14 days of culture, the culture medium was discarded. The cells were then gently rinsed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, and stained with 1% crystal violet for 10 min. Subsequently, the plates were rinsed with triple-distilled water. Colonies were photographed against a white background and counted to assess cellular proliferative capacity.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) images were obtained from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (version 2X, http://www.proteinatlas.org). For each target gene, we selected IHC images from human pancreatic cancer and normal pancreatic tissues. Images were downloaded manually, following the database’s usage guidelines.

Protein expression

Protein expression data were downloaded from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) Data Portal (https://cptac-data-portal.georgetown.edu) and the NCI Proteomic Data Commons (PDC). Normalized protein abundance values for the target genes were extracted from the harmonized pancreatic cancer cohort datasets. Both datasets were used in accordance with their respective data use policies and citation requirements.

Statistical analysis

In this study, bioinformatics analyses including statistical analysis and plotting were performed using R software (v4.3.1). All experimental procedures were independently repeated three times. Data conforming to a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). GraphPad Prism 8.3 was employed for experimental data analysis and graph generation. Comparisons between each experimental group and the control group were performed using the unpaired Student's t-test. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The significance levels in the statistical analysis are denoted as follows: * P < 0.05; ** P < 0.01; *** P < 0.001; **** P < 0.0001.

Quantitative deconvolution of immune cell infiltration profiles

Immune infiltration analysis based on the CIBERSORT deconvolution algorithm revealed distinct immune landscapes between normal pancreatic tissues and pancreatic carcinoma tissues. Figure S3A shows the relative proportions of 22 immune cell subsets in the two groups. Correlation analysis (Fig. S3B) indicated a positive correlation between CD4+ naïve T cells and naïve B cells (Spearman’s ρ = 0.59), while resting mast cells showed the strongest negative correlation with activated mast cells (ρ = −0.53). As further illustrated by the violin plots in Fig. S3C, pancreatic tumors exhibited significantly increased infiltration of M0 macrophages, M1 macrophages, M2 macrophages, and resting dendritic cells (p < 0.001). In contrast, memory B cells, CD8+ T cells, monocytes, resting mast cells, activated NK cells, and activated dendritic cells were significantly decreased in carcinoma tissues (p < 0.01).

LASSO and SVM-RFE based biomarker screening in pancreatic cancer

Two machine learning algorithms were used for feature selection to identify key biomarkers in pancreatic cancer. LASSO regression identified 11 significantly dysregulated genes, including FN1, IGFBP3, S100P, SLPI, SLC6A14, DKK1, LAMB3, MSLN, NQO1, NMU, and MMP7 (Fig. 2b-c). SVM-RFE further identified 15 candidate biomarkers: IGFBP3, SERPINB2, S100P, PLAC8, PM20D1, ANXA10, DKK1, COL8A1, CXCL5, NMU, TCN1, TEX11, CLDN18, GABRP, and ALB (Fig. 2d). Venn analysis showed that four genes, DKK1, S100P, IGFBP3, and NMU, were shared by both algorithms, suggesting that these genes may represent core biomarkers in pancreatic cancer (Fig. 2e).

Biomarker validation in external cohorts

ROC curve analysis showed that all four biomarkers had good diagnostic performance in the combined training set (GSE15471, GSE16515, and GSE22780). The AUC values were 0.905 for DKK1 (95% CI: 0.858–0.952), 0.905 for IGFBP3 (95% CI: 0.847–0.951), 0.883 for NMU (95% CI: 0.827–0.936), and 0.911 for S100P (95% CI: 0.856–0.956) (Fig. 2f). In the independent validation set (GSE55643 and GSE125158), DKK1 and S100P retained relatively good diagnostic performance, with AUC values of 0.782 (95% CI: 0.674–0.827) and 0.810 (95% CI: 0.705–0.902), respectively. By contrast, the performance of IGFBP3 and NMU declined, with AUC values of 0.571 (95% CI: 0.432–0.720) and 0.639 (95% CI: 0.507–0.763), respectively (Fig. 2g). Differential expression analysis further confirmed that DKK1 and S100P were significantly dysregulated in pancreatic cancer (p < 0.05), whereas IGFBP3 and NMU did not show significant differences (Fig. 2h-k). Survival analysis was first performed using optimal cutoffs determined by maximally selected rank statistics (Fig. 2l-o). High expression of DKK1, S100P, IGFBP3 and NMU was associated with shorter overall survival under this exploratory cutoff strategy. To reduce cutoff-dependent bias, we further performed sensitivity analyses using median-expression and quartile-based cutoffs. DKK1 remained significantly associated with overall survival across all three strategies (optimal cutoff, P = 0.00035; median cutoff, P = 0.0049; quartile-based cutoff, P = 0.00023), supporting its robust prognostic relevance. S100P showed significant survival stratification using the optimal cutoff (P < 0.0001) and quartile-based cutoff (P = 0.0075), but not the median cutoff, suggesting partial cutoff-dependent prognostic relevance. In contrast, IGFBP3 and NMU did not retain prognostic significance in the median- or quartile-based analyses (Fig. S6A-D). Together with their weaker diagnostic performance in the independent validation cohort, IGFBP3 and NMU were interpreted more cautiously as candidate genes associated with specific cellular states rather than robust prognostic biomarkers.

Functional enrichment analysis of key gene-associated pathways

To further clarify the potential biological functions of the key genes, we performed GSEA. The results showed that DKK1, S100P, NMU, and IGFBP3 were mainly enriched in signaling pathways associated with tumor proliferation and carcinogenesis. More specifically, all four genes were significantly enriched in KRAS signaling up, MYC targets V1 and V2, E2F targets, G2/M checkpoint, and mitotic spindle pathways, suggesting their possible involvement in cell cycle regulation and tumor cell proliferation. In addition, DKK1 and NMU were also significantly enriched in the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathway, indicating that they may be related to tumor cell migration and invasion. S100P was additionally enriched in angiogenesis-related pathways, whereas IGFBP3 showed significant enrichment in hypoxia-related signaling (Fig. S4A-D). Overall, these findings indicate that the key genes are closely associated with KRAS-driven oncogenic signaling, cell cycle activation, and other tumor-promoting biological processes.

Correlation analysis between key gene expression and immune cell infiltration patterns

To further investigate the relationship between the key genes and immune cell infiltration, we analyzed immune infiltration patterns in PDAC using CIBERSORT. The results showed that S100P expression was positively correlated with M0 macrophages, activated dendritic cells, CD4+ memory T cells, resting NK cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and memory B cells, while it was negatively correlated with γδ T cells, CD8+ T cells, naïve B cells, resting mast cells, and activated NK cells (Fig. S5A-B). DKK1 expression was significantly positively correlated with M0 macrophages and activated dendritic cells, but negatively correlated with CD8+ T cells and γδ T cells (Fig. S5C-D). NMU expression was positively correlated with M0 macrophages, CD4+ memory T cells, and activated dendritic cells, but negatively correlated with γδ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and naïve B cells (Fig. S5E-F). IGFBP3 expression was significantly positively correlated with M0 macrophages and CD4+ memory T cells, whereas it was negatively correlated with activated NK cells, naïve B cells, and CD8+ T cells (Fig. S5G-H). Further stratified analysis based on gene expression levels showed that, in the high-S100P expression group, the proportions of CD8+ T cells and naïve B cells were significantly decreased, whereas the proportions of resting NK cells and M0 macrophages were significantly increased. The other three genes showed a similar pattern. These findings suggest that the four key genes may be involved in the formation of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in PDAC.

Drug sensitivity prediction analysis based on key gene expression

To further evaluate the potential therapeutic relevance of the key genes, we used the oncoPredict package to predict drug sensitivity and compared the estimated IC50 values between the high- and low-expression groups. The results showed that high expression of IGFBP3 and DKK1 was associated with significantly lower predicted IC50 values for Dasatinib. The high-NMU expression group showed greater predicted sensitivity to Ulixertinib, while the predicted IC50 value of selumetinib was significantly reduced in the high-S100P expression group. Notably, both Ulixertinib and Selumetinib target the MAPK signaling pathway, suggesting that elevated expression of NMU and S100P may be associated with increased susceptibility to MAPK pathway inhibitors. Taken together, these findings indicate that different key genes may correspond to distinct patterns of drug sensitivity and may provide a basis for individualized therapeutic strategies (Fig. 2p-s).

Distinct communication landscapes of DKK1-high and S100P-high malignant epithelial cells

To further explore the communication context of key-gene-associated malignant epithelial states, we performed CellChat analysis after stratifying malignant epithelial cells according to DKK1 and S100P expression. In the DKK1-stratified analysis, DKK1-high malignant epithelial cells showed extensive inferred communication with multiple microenvironmental cell populations, including myeloid cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells and T/NK cells (Fig. 6a). Comparison of outgoing and incoming signaling strength indicated that DKK1-high malignant epithelial cells participated in both signal-sending and signal-receiving networks, suggesting that DKK1-high epithelial states are embedded in active epithelial–microenvironmental communication programs (Fig. 6b). We next examined the major signaling pathways associated with DKK1-high malignant epithelial cells. Incoming signaling analysis showed that DKK1-high epithelial cells received multiple signals from the tumor microenvironment, including MIF-, SPP1-, MK-, EGF-, GAS-, GALECTIN- and TWEAK-related pathways (Fig. 6c). Outgoing signaling analysis further showed that DKK1-high epithelial cells contributed to several communication pathways, including MIF-, MK-, CXCL-, ANNEXIN-, KIT- and VISFATIN-related signaling (Fig. 6d). Representative ligand–receptor visualization further highlighted SPP1- and MIF-related communication patterns in the DKK1-stratified network (Fig. 6i). These results suggest that DKK1-high malignant epithelial cells are associated with a communication environment involving inflammatory, extracellular matrix-related and myeloid-interacting signaling programs. We also performed CellChat analysis after stratifying malignant epithelial cells according to S100P expression. The S100P-stratified communication network showed that S100P-high malignant epithelial cells interacted with diverse microenvironmental cell populations, although the overall communication pattern differed from that observed in DKK1-high epithelial cells (Fig. 6e, f). Incoming signaling analysis showed that S100P-high epithelial cells received signals related to MK, PTN, SPP1, ANGPTL, IL1, PARs and VISFATIN pathways (Fig. 6g). Outgoing signaling analysis indicated that S100P-high epithelial cells were mainly associated with MIF-, MK- and SPP1-related signaling programs (Fig. 6h). Representative pathway visualization further showed MK- and MIF-related communication patterns in the S100P-stratified network (Fig. 6j). Spatial feature plots showed that DKK1 expression was relatively restricted and scattered, whereas S100P was broadly distributed across epithelial-rich regions (Fig. 7b). Discussion

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains one of the most challenging malignancies in clinical practice. Although notable advances have been made in imaging evaluation, perioperative management, and systemic therapy in recent years, the overall prognosis of patients with PDAC has not improved substantially. This poor outcome is driven by several factors. Most patients already have locally advanced or metastatic disease at the time of diagnosis. In addition, PDAC is characterized by aggressive biological behavior, marked intratumoral heterogeneity, and a profoundly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [11,2]. Taken together, these features often leave only a limited window for effective intervention. For this reason, identifying key molecules that reflect the biological nature of PDAC and may carry translational relevance remains an important objective in this field.

In the present study, we integrated bulk RNA-seq, scRNA-seq, and spatial transcriptomic data and combined them with machine learning analysis to identify four key genes associated with PDAC progression, namely DKK1, S100P, IGFBP3, and NMU. These genes were then evaluated further through survival analysis, cell-cell communication analysis, pseudotime inference, and in vitro experiments. The strength of evidence, however, was not the same for all four genes. DKK1 and S100P showed the most consistent associations across prognosis, expression patterns, spatial distribution, and functional validation, and therefore form the main focus of this study. By contrast, NMU and IGFBP3 appear to be more informative as candidate genes that reflect specific cellular states and microenvironmental variation, while their exact biological functions in PDAC still need to be established more directly.

At the bulk transcriptomic level, the four genes were initially identified through machine-learning screening. However, their validation strength was not equivalent. DKK1 and S100P retained more reliable diagnostic performance in the independent validation cohort, whereas IGFBP3 and NMU showed weaker external validation and should therefore be interpreted more cautiously. Survival sensitivity analyses further supported DKK1 as the most robust prognostic marker, while S100P showed partial cutoff-dependent prognostic relevance. Functional enrichment analysis further linked these genes to KRAS signaling, MYC targets, E2F targets, the G2/M checkpoint, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Since KRAS dysregulation is one of the central molecular events in PDAC and contributes not only to tumor proliferation but also to metabolic adaptation, stress responses, and microenvironmental remodeling, this pattern is biologically plausible and agrees with previous reports [12,13]. What seems more interesting, however, is that these genes are not merely linked to enhanced proliferation. Rather, they appear to mark a more invasive and plastic malignant program, one that may also be better adapted to microenvironmental pressure.

The single-cell results refined this picture further. Previous work has shown that the basal-like phenotype is generally associated with poorer differentiation, greater invasiveness, and less favorable clinical outcomes, and our results fit well within that framework [14,15]. Just as importantly, these genes were not broadly elevated across all epithelial cells. Instead, they were preferentially found in particular high-risk subgroups, suggesting that they may serve more as markers of malignant cellular states than as general epithelial features. The increased proportion of basal-like-related subpopulations in metastatic lesions also supports their connection with disease progression.

Pseudotime analysis added a dynamic perspective to malignant epithelial-state transitions. In the revised analysis, pseudotime was reconstructed using only inferCNV-defined malignant epithelial cells. High-CytoTRACE states, including Epi_VIM and proliferative epithelial subclusters such as Epi_PCNA and Epi_CENPF, were positioned toward the high-plasticity end of the trajectory. The four genes showed distinct temporal patterns rather than a shared dynamic profile. DKK1 was relatively enriched in earlier high-plasticity regions and decreased along pseudotime, whereas S100P gradually increased toward later trajectory regions, consistent with its association with more differentiated epithelial tumor states. These findings support the view that DKK1 and S100P mark different phases of malignant epithelial-state progression.

They may also be involved in different stages of tumor cell state transition. That point is particularly relevant in PDAC, where tumor cell plasticity is increasingly regarded as a major basis for metastatic spread, therapy resistance, and adaptation to local stress [1]. From this perspective, the genes identified here may be better understood as molecular clues linked to the evolution of malignant cell states rather than as isolated differentially expressed markers.

From a broader viewpoint, heterogeneity in PDAC is not confined to transcriptional states at the single-cell level. It is also reflected in tissue morphology and clinical behavior. Recent digital pathology models, including those reported by Takamatsu and colleagues, have shown that greater morphological heterogeneity is associated with earlier postoperative recurrence, and that different histological clusters correspond to distinct transcriptional features [16]. This is highly consistent with our own findings. It suggests that PDAC prognosis is shaped not simply by the upregulation or downregulation of individual genes, but by the way different malignant states, morphological modules, and microenvironmental components are assembled into a more invasive and adaptable whole. In that sense, the heterogeneity observed here across transcriptomic, single-cell, and spatial levels may offer a more biologically meaningful explanation for clinical outcomes.

Among the four genes, S100P showed strong diagnostic relevance and clear tumor-cell functional effects. Its expression was broadly detected in malignant epithelial cells and epithelial-rich spatial regions, and siRNA-mediated knockdown significantly suppressed PDAC cell proliferation and migration. However, its prognostic association was not uniformly retained across all cutoff strategies, suggesting that S100P should be interpreted as a diagnostically robust and biologically relevant marker with partial prognostic support rather than as a uniformly validated prognostic biomarker. Previous studies have demonstrated that S100P is frequently overexpressed in PDAC and can already be detected in precursor lesions such as PanIN, suggesting that it may be involved early in pancreatic tumorigenesis [17,18]. Functionally, S100P promotes tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, at least in part through extracellular binding to RAGE and subsequent activation of pathways such as MAPK and NF-kB. Our results were broadly in line with these reports [19]. S100P was enriched in tumor regions and malignant epithelial cells, its expression was higher in primary lesions than in metastatic lesions, and its knockdown significantly suppressed the proliferation and migration of PANC-1 and BxPC-3 cells. Existing studies have also suggested that non-coding RNAs such as miR-495 can negatively regulate S100P, implying that its regulatory network may be more complex than is currently appreciated ([20,21]; P․F․ [22]).

DKK1 showed a somewhat different pattern. Although it was also stable across our analyses, its significance appears to extend beyond intrinsic tumor cell proliferation. Increasing evidence suggests that DKK1 acts more as a bridge between aggressive epithelial states and the immunosuppressive microenvironment. Although DKK1 was initially regarded as an antagonist of the Wnt/b-catenin pathway, subsequent studies have shown that its role varies across organs and microenvironments and may even differ between metastatic sites [23,24]. Zhuang et al. reported in breast cancer models that DKK1 plays different roles in lung and bone metastasis, underscoring that it cannot be understood as a uniformly tumor-promoting molecule [25]. At the same time, the immunological evidence surrounding DKK1 has become increasingly compelling. Previous studies suggest that DKK1 can promote the expansion or recruitment of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and weaken the antitumor activity of CD8+ T cells and NK cells [23,26,27]. In our study, high DKK1 expression was associated with increased myeloid cell-related infiltration, enrichment in basal-like malignant epithelial subpopulations, and poor prognosis. Considered together, these findings support the idea that DKK1 may contribute to both the maintenance of aggressive epithelial states and the establishment of a locally immunosuppressive microenvironment in PDAC. Given that inhibitory strategies targeting DKK1 are already under active development(H [28].), its translational relevance appears relatively promising.

NMU seems to fit better into a different category, one more closely related to microenvironmental communication. Previous studies have suggested that NMU is not only a neuropeptide but also exhibits clear local paracrine and autocrine characteristics. Its receptors, NMUR1 and NMUR2, are found not only in tumor cells but also in various immune and stromal populations, making NMU better suited to being understood as a regulator of signal exchange between tumor cells and the microenvironment [29,30]. In PDAC, earlier work has linked NMU more strongly to invasion, migration, and metabolic reprogramming than to direct proliferative effects [31]. In our data, NMU was enriched in early pseudotime states, and was also present in tumor regions and the tumor-stroma interface. These features are consistent with a role in maintaining invasive states and mediating crosstalk with the surrounding microenvironment. Still, because direct functional validation is lacking, NMU is better regarded at present as a candidate worthy of further study rather than as a confirmed core driver.

IGFBP3 is the gene that requires the most cautious interpretation. In our study, IGFBP3 was more likely to be distributed in fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and the epithelial-stromal interface than in highly proliferative malignant epithelial subpopulations. This is important because it suggests that its significance may lie more in the microenvironment and tissue context than in directly reflecting epithelial aggressiveness. Previous reviews on tumor endothelial cell (TEC) heterogeneity have identified IGFBP3 as one of the more common TEC-related markers across multiple tumor types and linked it to processes such as immune regulation and extracellular matrix remodeling [32]. In PDAC, tumor-derived IGFBP-3 has also been implicated in cachexia through effects on the IGF-1/PI3K/AKT axis [33]. When these findings are considered together with large epidemiological studies suggesting that the relationship between IGFBP3 and cancer risk is not stable across contexts [34], it becomes difficult to view IGFBP3 as a simple linear tumor-promoting molecule. Based on both the literature and our own results, we would interpret IGFBP3 more cautiously, as a candidate associated with endothelial or stromal heterogeneity, tumor-host interaction, and morphological variation, rather than as a core driver with evidence comparable to that for S100P or DKK1.

In addition to tumor-intrinsic states, our analyses also suggest that these key genes are closely linked to the immune microenvironment. High expression of DKK1, S100P, NMU, and IGFBP3 was generally accompanied by increased infiltration of immunosuppressive components such as M0 macrophages and reduced infiltration of CD8+ T cells and gammad T cells. S100P, in particular, was also associated with increased Treg abundance and reduced activated NK cells. Although these observations remain correlative, together they point toward a recognizable pattern: tumors with high expression of these genes are more likely to exist in a microenvironment enriched in myeloid components and marked by weakened cytotoxic immune surveillance, a pattern commonly seen in aggressive PDAC [35].

CellChat analysis was reinterpreted as a characterization of communication programs associated with gene-high malignant epithelial states rather than direct evidence that the key genes themselves mediate ligand–receptor interactions. In the revised analysis, DKK1-high and S100P-high malignant epithelial cells were analyzed separately. DKK1-high epithelial states were associated with broader epithelial–microenvironmental communication programs, including MIF-, MK/MDK-, and SPP1-related pathways, whereas S100P-high epithelial states showed more selective associations with MK/MDK- and SPP1-related signaling contexts. Importantly, these pathways should not be interpreted as direct DKK1- or S100P-mediated ligand–receptor interactions. Rather, they indicate that DKK1-high and S100P-high epithelial states are embedded in distinct communication environments within the PDAC microenvironment. We also avoided interpreting high DKK1 expression as WNT-sender activity, because DKK1 is a secreted antagonist and modulator of canonical WNT signaling rather than a WNT ligand.

Spatial transcriptomic analysis further strengthened the tissue-level interpretation of the single-cell and CellChat findings. Instead of relying only on raw spatial expression of the four genes, we used RCTD-based deconvolution to map malignant epithelial and myeloid components within PDAC tissue sections. We then constructed CellChat-guided spatial epithelial–myeloid axis scores for MIF, MK/MDK, and SPP1 signaling. These analyses showed that DKK1-high regions were associated with higher epithelial–myeloid spatial axis scores, whereas S100P was more broadly distributed in epithelial-rich regions and showed selective associations with MK/MDK- and SPP1-related axes. Therefore, the spatial results provide supportive tissue-level evidence for distinct DKK1- and S100P-associated communication contexts, but do not prove direct ligand–receptor signaling mediated by these genes. Drug sensitivity prediction provided additional translational clues. Tumors with high IGFBP3 and DKK1 expression appeared more likely to be sensitive to dasatinib, whereas those with high NMU and S100P expression showed greater predicted sensitivity to ulixertinib and selumetinib, respectively. Notably, the latter two drugs target the MAPK pathway, which is especially relevant in PDAC because of frequent KRAS-driven activation. These findings are in line with the pathway enrichment results described above and suggest that PDAC subgroups defined by different key genes may have distinct therapeutic vulnerabilities. At the same time, this part of the study remains computational and cannot substitute for direct pharmacological validation. It is therefore better regarded as a source of hypotheses for follow-up work than as a definitive therapeutic conclusion.

Among the four genes, we selected S100P and DKK1 for experimental validation because they showed the strongest prognostic significance and were prominently enriched in aggressive epithelial states. In vitro knockdown experiments demonstrated that silencing either gene significantly reduced the proliferation and migration of PANC-1 and BxPC-3 cells. The functional validation should be interpreted within its experimental scope. Knockdown of DKK1 or S100P significantly reduced PDAC cell proliferation and migration, supporting their tumor-intrinsic roles in PDAC cells. However, these assays did not directly test immune regulation, epithelial–myeloid communication, or spatial microenvironmental remodeling. Therefore, the immune and spatial findings should be regarded as computationally inferred associations that require further mechanistic validation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the integration of multiple public datasets and the use of an independent GPL6480-based testing cohort strengthened the cross-platform validation of our findings, the external validation cohort remained relatively small, and retrospective public datasets are still susceptible to biases related to sample heterogeneity, platform differences, and data-processing variation. The reduced external validation performance of IGFBP3 and NMU also suggests possible overfitting of the diagnostic model; therefore, DKK1 and S100P should be considered the more reliable diagnostic candidates in the current study, whereas IGFBP3 and NMU require further validation. Second, although maximally selected rank statistics were used in the exploratory survival analysis, this cutoff strategy may introduce multiple-testing bias. We therefore added median- and quartile-based sensitivity analyses and interpreted the survival results cautiously. Third, the biological functions of IGFBP3 and NMU were not directly tested experimentally, and their roles in PDAC remain less certain. In addition, analyses of immune infiltration, ligand–receptor interactions, spatial communication axes, and drug sensitivity were mainly based on computational inference and do not directly prove physical ligand–receptor interactions, immune-cell functional regulation, or therapeutic sensitivity. Fourth, bulk-derived basal-like and classical-like gene sets were used only as supportive transcriptional program scores rather than definitive single-cell subtype assignments. Finally, although spatial transcriptomics provided important compartment-level information, higher-resolution spatial approaches and additional functional studies will be needed to define the precise cellular interactions associated with these genes. Serum levels of DKK1 and S100P were also not evaluated in this study. Although tissue-level protein evidence from HPA and CPTAC supported the upregulation of these proteins in PDAC tissues, circulating biomarker performance cannot be inferred directly from tissue expression. Future studies should measure serum DKK1 and S100P levels in prospective PDAC cohorts and assess their diagnostic and prognostic value, either alone or in combination with established serum markers such as CA19–9.Overall, this study identified DKK1, S100P, IGFBP3, and NMU as key genes associated with PDAC progression and established an integrative framework linking invasive epithelial states, tumor cell plasticity, immune-related microenvironmental remodeling, and potential therapeutic sensitivity. Among these genes, S100P and DKK1 stand out most clearly, not only because of their prognostic associations, but also because their functional effects could be demonstrated experimentally. NMU and IGFBP3, by comparison, point to more complex aspects of PDAC biology, including secretory communication, endothelial or stromal heterogeneity, and tumor-host interaction. Taken together, these findings add to the current understanding of PDAC and may help guide future efforts in biomarker-based stratification and mechanism-oriented therapeutic exploration.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this integrative multi-omics study identified DKK1, S100P, NMU, and IGFBP3 as candidate genes associated with PDAC progression. Among them, DKK1 showed the most robust prognostic association across multiple survival cutoff strategies, whereas S100P demonstrated stable diagnostic performance and partial prognostic relevance. Single-cell analysis of inferCNV-defined malignant epithelial cells revealed that these genes were not uniformly enriched in a single epithelial subtype, but instead marked distinct malignant epithelial programs and dynamic state transitions. DKK1 was more closely associated with high-plasticity, invasive, or proliferative epithelial states, while S100P showed broader epithelial expression and was also linked to differentiated epithelial tumor states. CellChat and spatial transcriptomic analyses further suggested that DKK1-high and S100P-high malignant epithelial states occupy distinct epithelial–microenvironmental communication contexts. Importantly, these communication patterns should be interpreted as state-associated microenvironmental features rather than direct ligand–receptor interactions mediated by DKK1 or S100P themselves. RCTD-based spatial deconvolution provided supportive tissue-level evidence linking these gene-associated epithelial states with epithelial–myeloid spatial organization. Functional experiments further confirmed that DKK1 and S100P promoted PDAC cell proliferation and migration. By contrast, NMU and IGFBP3 were more cautiously interpreted as biologically informative candidate genes associated with specific cellular states and microenvironmental variation, rather than as robustly validated prognostic biomarkers. These findings provide a refined framework for understanding malignant epithelial heterogeneity, spatial tumor–microenvironment organization, and candidate biomarker relevance in PDAC.
