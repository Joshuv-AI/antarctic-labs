Mechanistic study on the influence of organic matter on soil compressive strength and nonlinear resistivity
Authors: Rui Lv, Qiang Sun, Ziyun Liang, Xianghao Zha, Jingjing Nan, Feixing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The incorporation of organic matter into loess can promote carbon sequestration while also significantly affecting the soil pore structure and strength properties. To investigate the effects of organic matter on the mechanical and physical properties of soil, as well as their interaction mechanisms, experimental studies were conducted on organic soils with different organic matter contents (0%–6%) and water contents (12%–24%). Meanwhile, the real-time evolution of resistivity during the mechanical tests was monitored. The results indicate that the addition of an appropriate amount of organic matter can improve the mechanical properties of loess, whereas the compressive strength decreases with increasing water content. As stress increased, the resistivity of the specimens decreased rapidly, and then remained relatively stable after the elastic stage until failure. Crack generation during the deformation process altered the conductive pathways within the organic soil and became an important factor influencing resistivity variation. These findings provide important theoretical support for soil remediation and land resource utilization in loess regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-62608-x.

Introduction

Carbon neutrality has become a major global concern, and soil organic carbon plays an important role in the global carbon cycle. Increasing soil organic matter content can influence the stabilization process of carbon in soils and has significant implications for ecosystem carbon cycling1–6. However, this study does not involve quantitative evaluation of carbon sequestration capacity or analysis of long-term carbon stability. Instead, organic matter is used as a soil amendment material to investigate its effects on the mechanical behavior and physical response characteristics of loess. Organic matter is one of the most active components in soil and plays a key role in soil structure formation and stabilization. In the Loess Plateau region of China, soils are generally characterized by a loose structure and low organic matter content. The addition of exogenous organic matter can improve soil aggregate structure, pore distribution, and water retention capacity, thereby affecting soil mechanical properties and engineering stability7–9. In addition, as an important binding agent in soil aggregates, organic matter plays a significant role in soil fertility, water retention, and erosion control10–14.

Organic matter, as an important component of soil, plays a crucial role in the evolution of soil physical properties and mechanical behavior. In recent years, studies have demonstrated that the incorporation of an appropriate amount of organic matter can effectively enhance the compressive resistance of soil under stress conditions15,16. For example, suitable additions of synthetic or natural fibers can improve the structural stability of soils and reinforce clay, thereby increasing its compressive strength17. In addition, the incorporation of wheat straw can effectively control the radial deformation of soil samples and inhibit crack propagation, resulting in a gradual enhancement in the soil resistance to deformation with increasing axial stress18. Furthermore, lignin can significantly improve the mechanical properties of sandy loess through cementation and fiber-reinforcement effects, enabling the soil to exhibit higher compressive strength under stress conditions19.

Meanwhile, in recent years, various green soil stabilization techniques have attracted increasing attention, including microbially induced calcium carbonate precipitation (MICP), biopolymers, and zeolite-based treatments. These approaches can significantly enhance soil strength, reduce permeability, and control contaminant migration by regulating soil microstructure. However, their long-term durability, environmental adaptability, and engineering cost-effectiveness still require further verification20–22. Nevertheless, existing studies have mainly focused on the effects of a single organic material or a single stabilization method on soil properties. Systematic investigations into the synergistic mechanisms between organic matter and different reinforcing agents remain limited, particularly in terms of their coupled effects on the mechanical behavior and physical properties of soils. In addition to organic matter, water content is a key controlling factor governing soil mechanical behavior. Previous studies have shown that water content significantly affects the stress–strain response and volumetric deformation characteristics of soils and largely dominates the evolution of strength and failure modes23,24. For example, with increasing water content, the compressive strength of loess continuously decreases, and its failure mode gradually transitions from brittle to ductile behavior25, while in silty clays, increased water content weakens interparticle bonding, thereby reducing overall load-bearing capacity26. From a microstructural perspective, the mechanical response and structural evolution of clays are jointly controlled by water content and dry density, and are closely related to interlayer water distribution and system energy variations. Meanwhile, pore collapse and particle rearrangement constitute the primary deformation mechanisms, which are further influenced by water–mineral interactions and the ionic environment27,28. In addition, in soil improvement studies, organic–inorganic composite stabilization systems have been shown to significantly enhance strength and reduce permeability and contaminant migration by forming a dense microstructure, demonstrating a strong structure-controlling effect29. Furthermore, both organic matter and water content not only affect the mechanical behavior of soils but also significantly alter their physical response characteristics. Among them, electrical resistivity, as a sensitive physical parameter, can effectively reflect changes in internal soil structure and water distribution. Previous studies have demonstrated that soil resistivity is closely related to factors such as water content, temperature, pore water chemistry, and particle structure30–32. In addition, several studies have investigated the strength characteristics, initial resistivity, and real-time resistivity variations of cement-stabilized soils during failure under loading conditions, and further explored the relationships among stress, strain, and resistivity in soils33. However, research on the real-time resistivity response and underlying mechanisms of organic soils during deformation and failure under stress remains limited, leaving a significant gap in understanding the coupling between soil mechanical behavior and physical properties. Building on our previous study on the effects of water content and organic matter content on the initial electrical resistivity characteristics of loess, this work further investigates the real-time electrical resistivity response of organic soils under mechanical loading and its coupling with deformation behaviour. All stress–strain and resistivity data were obtained from newly conducted uniaxial compression tests, and no resistivity data from previous studies were reused. Compared with previous studies focusing mainly on static resistivity characteristics or the effects of single modification factors, this study examines the coupling between electrical resistivity evolution and mechanical damage from the perspective of deformation processes.

To achieve these objectives, this study conducted mechanical tests on organic soil samples to determine their ultimate failure characteristics. The investigation included an analysis of failure modes and associated deformation mechanisms, allowing for an assessment of the effects of organic matter content and water content on the mechanical behavior of loess. The potential influence of changes in physical properties on soil mechanics was also considered. Accordingly, real-time variations in the electrical resistivity of organic soils during deformation and failure were monitored, and the underlying mechanisms of these resistivity changes were analyzed. The primary aim of this study was to elucidate the influence of organic matter on both the mechanical and physical properties of soils, as well as the interactions between these two aspects of soil behavior.

Sample preparation and test procedure

Sample preparation

According to the experimental procedure shown in Fig. 1, the loess used in this study was collected from Lintong District, Xi’an City, Shaanxi Province, China. Fresh leaves of Firmiana simplex used as organic matter were collected from the campus of Xi’an University of Science and Technology (approximately 34.2239° N, 108.9598° E). During sample preparation, the freshly collected loess and organic matter were first oven-dried separately. The loess was dried at 105 °C for 12 h, while the organic matter was dried at 45 °C for 12 h. After drying, the materials were initially crushed using a rubber hammer and subsequently ground into uniform powders using a mechanical grinder, followed by sieving to ensure particle uniformity. Due to the irregular morphology of the pulverized Firmiana simplex leaf particles, no dedicated particle-size analysis was conducted. To prevent moisture absorption from the atmosphere, all powdered materials were sealed with plastic wrap during storage and handling. Subsequently, according to the experimental flowchart and the designed mixing ratios, the dried loess and organic matter were placed into a mortar and manually mixed thoroughly for approximately 1 min to ensure uniformity. Distilled water was then sprayed uniformly onto the mixture in several stages using a spray bottle according to the target water content, while the mixture was continuously turned and mixed by hand to ensure adequate moisture distribution throughout the soil sample. After mixing, the prepared samples were sealed in plastic bags and stored in a desiccator for 12 h to promote uniform water distribution and sufficient moisture absorption within the soil. After the equilibration period, all specimens were compacted into cylindrical samples with dimensions of φ38 mm × 50 mm (diameter × height) using a hydraulic jack under identical compaction conditions. The target dry density of all specimens was strictly controlled at 1.60 g/cm3 to ensure consistency and comparability of the initial stress state. It should be noted that, although the dry density was maintained constant, variations in water content (12–24%) and organic matter content (0–6%) may still result in differences in derived structural parameters, such as the initial void ratio and degree of saturation. These differences are considered inherent characteristics of the reconstituted organic matter–loess system rather than artifacts associated with specimen preparation. A total of five water-content groups were prepared, with water contents (w) of 12%, 15%, 18%, 21%, and 24%, respectively. For each water-content level, seven organic matter contents (c) were considered, namely 0%, 1%, 2%, 3%, 4%, 5%, and 6%. Four parallel specimens were prepared for each testing condition to ensure the reliability and repeatability of the experimental results. To clarify the use of the four replicate specimens, an independent repeated-testing scheme was adopted for each testing condition. Each specimen was first used to determine the initial electrical resistivity under static conditions and was subsequently subjected to a uniaxial compression test with simultaneous real-time resistivity monitoring. Therefore, both the mechanical response and the electrical response were obtained from the same specimen throughout the entire testing process, ensuring the consistency and comparability of the coupled electro-mechanical behavior. All reported results represent the mean values of four parallel specimens, and the corresponding standard deviations were calculated for statistical analysis. During specimen preparation, the mass of each specimen was strictly controlled at 100 g with a tolerance of ±0.1%, thereby ensuring the target dry density of 1.60 g/cm3. After compaction, all specimens were sealed with multiple layers of plastic wrap to minimize moisture loss prior to subsequent mechanical and electrical resistivity testing. The above procedure constitutes the preparation process of the loess–organic matter mixed specimens used in this study34.

Test procedure

As shown in the experimental procedure, electrical resistivity measurements and uniaxial compression tests were conducted on the prepared specimens. During the initial resistivity measurements, each specimen was sandwiched between two thin copper plates connected to a TH2811D precision LCR digital bridge. A frequency range of 50 Hz to 200 kHz was applied to investigate the frequency-dependent response of electrical resistivity. During each measurement, a time-lapse acquisition method was used to record resistivity values at 5 s, 10 s, 15 s, and 20 s after the start of testing. The average of these four readings was taken as the initial resistivity of each specimen. All resistivity data reported in this study were independently obtained from the present experiments, and no data from previous studies were reused. The instrument was calibrated prior to testing in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions to ensure measurement accuracy and repeatability. For real-time resistivity monitoring during uniaxial compression, a fixed frequency of 10 kHz was adopted to avoid the influence of frequency variation on the dynamic response. This frequency was selected based on preliminary tests, which indicated stable signal behavior and reduced polarization effects.

Uniaxial compression tests were performed using an SLB-1A stress–strain controlled testing system at a constant loading rate of 0.8 mm/min. Electrical resistivity signals were synchronously recorded throughout the entire loading process together with mechanical data. To ensure stable electrical contact and measurement reliability, copper electrodes were placed at both ends of the specimen, with porous stones positioned outside the electrodes to improve electrode–soil contact and reduce contact resistance effects. The specimen, electrodes, and porous stones were assembled as an integrated unit and fixed on the loading platform to maintain consistent boundary conditions. To ensure reproducibility, four identical specimens were prepared for each testing condition, and the averaged results were used for analysis. In this study, the resistivity of the soil samples was measured using a TH2811D wide-frequency LCR digital bridge tester, and the measurement accuracy of the testing system was ±0.2 Ω·m. The damage pattern of organic soil serves as an effective indicator of its stress characteristics, offering insights into the form of crack extension within the organic soil specimen. Figure 5 presents the typical damage characteristics of the specimen under pressure, considering various water content and organic matter content conditions. It is evident from the figure that distinct damage modes emerge across different water content and organic matter content settings, ultimately manifesting in three primary patterns. Observing Fig. 5, it becomes apparent that within the water content range of 12 %-18 % and organic matter content range of 0 %-3 %, the organic soil samples exhibit damage characterized by the formation of longitudinal linear cracks aligned with the stress direction, resulting in a “straight line” mode of specimen damage. Conversely, within the water content range of 21 %-24 %, alongside the formation of axial stress and parallel straight-line cracks, there is also the successive generation of cracks along the stress direction. In the range of 21 %-24 %, in addition to the formation of straight line cracks parallel to the axial stress, the successive generation of cross cracks along the straight line cracks to both sides of the extension of the final specimen damage pattern for the “cross” mode, and the greater the water content is, the more cracks there are. With w in the range of 12 %-18 % and c in the range of 4 %-6 %, the straight line cracks along the two sides of the organic specimen deviated in terms of the degree of specimen damage, resulting in the final destruction of the specimens to the “slump” mode. The final damage was in the “collapse” mode. Discussion

Stress‒strain curve analysis of organic soil under compression

According to Fig. 3 and Table 4, the stress‒strain curves of the organic soil samples with different organic matter contents as well as different water contents all increase to a peak, then decrease, and finally converge to an stable value. Here the stress-strain curve of organic soil is analyzed with w = 18 % and c = 2 % as an example, and according to the trend graph of the stress‒strain curve, the curve can be divided into four stages, as shown in Fig. 6.Fig. 6Stress-strain curve stage analysis diagram.

Effect of water content on the compressive strength of organic soils

The stress–strain peak values under different conditions were summarized, as shown in Fig. 7. The results indicate that the peak stress gradually decreased with increasing water content. According to Fig. 5, the failure patterns of the organic soil specimens also changed with increasing water content. For specimens with low organic matter content, the failure mode gradually transformed from a “straight” crack pattern to a “cross” crack pattern, whereas specimens with high organic matter content changed from a “collapse” mode to a “cross” mode. These variations may be related to the influence of water content on the internal structure and interparticle interactions of the soil.Fig. 7Stress-strain curves with moisture content peak stress plots.

Stress-strain curves with moisture content peak stress plots.

With increasing water content, pore water gradually filled the voids between soil particles and formed water films around the particle surfaces. These water films weakened the effective stress transfer and reduced the interparticle bonding strength, thereby decreasing the overall compressive resistance of the soil. In addition, the increase in water content enhanced the lubrication effect between particles, which reduced frictional resistance and weakened the structural stability of the soil41,42. Under high water-content conditions, the pore water pressure within the soil also increased significantly. Since water is nearly incompressible, the increase in pore water pressure offset part of the effective stress, resulting in a reduction in soil compressive strength43. Furthermore, the adsorption of water molecules on particle surfaces may weaken particle interactions and reduce interparticle friction and bonding forces, further decreasing the overall compressive resistance of the soil.

Strain resistance curve analysis of organic soil under compression

The initial resistivity characteristics of the organic soils are presented in Fig. 8. The results show that the resistivity first increased and then decreased with increasing organic matter content. The initial increase in resistivity may be attributed to the addition of a small amount of organic matter, which potentially enhanced the bonding between soil particles and increased the proportion of large pores, resulting in less continuous conductive pathways. As the organic matter content further increased, the resistivity gradually decreased. This phenomenon may be related to the gradual filling of large pores by excess organic matter, which could improve the continuity of conductive pathways and promote a more continuous distribution of pore water within the organic soil44. The results indicate that the resistivity decreased with increasing water content under both the initial state and the deformation failure state of the organic soils. This suggests that water content is one of the dominant factors influencing soil resistivity, regardless of the deformation state of the organic soil. During the compressive strength tests of the organic soils, the real-time evolution of resistivity during deformation was mainly related to changes in conductive pathway connectivity and pore water distribution within the soil45,46. Three main conductive pathways may exist in the organic soil samples: solid–solid conduction pathways between soil particles through the electrical double layer, solid–liquid conduction pathways involving soil particles and pore water, and liquid–liquid conduction pathways formed by continuous pore water within the pore spaces. The conductivity of these pathways differs significantly, with liquid–liquid conduction generally exhibiting higher conductivity than solid–liquid and solid–solid conduction pathways47.

As shown in Stage I of Fig. 11, before loading, the organic soil specimen consisted mainly of soil particles, pore water, and pore air. At this stage, the soil particles and pores were relatively uniformly distributed, and pore water was dispersed throughout the specimen. Mechanism diagram of resistivity change during deformation of organic soil.

As axial stress increased and the specimen entered Stage II, the specimen volume gradually decreased with increasing strain. Meanwhile, the original pores within the soil gradually compressed and closed, resulting in reduced porosity and closer contact between soil particles. During this process, the number of solid–solid conduction pathways may have increased. In addition, changes in pore structure and pore water redistribution may have enhanced the connectivity between soil particles and pore water, thereby increasing the contribution of solid–liquid conduction pathways. Furthermore, the increase in pore water connectivity may also have enhanced the contribution of liquid–liquid conduction pathways. Therefore, the increase in conductive pathway connectivity during this stage may explain the observed decrease in resistivity47,48.

With further strain development, as shown in Stage III of Fig. 11, the specimen gradually exhibited plastic deformation characteristics. During this process, new microfissures continuously developed within the soil, while the original pores and microcracks gradually connected under axial stress, forming microfissures approximately parallel to the loading direction. Under these conditions, pore water may have migrated into the newly formed microfissures, thereby altering the conductive pathway connectivity within the soil. The increased connectivity of pore water may have enhanced the contribution of liquid–liquid conduction pathways to the overall electrical conduction behavior. Since liquid conduction generally exhibits higher conductivity than particle-based conduction, the resistivity decreased rapidly during this stage49,50. However, it should be noted that the interpretation of conductive pathway evolution in this study was mainly inferred from macroscopic resistivity responses and failure characteristics, and direct experimental verification of the conductive pathways was not conducted. Therefore, the proposed mechanisms remain partly qualitative and inferential.

As shown in Stage IV of Fig. 11, with further stress increase, the axial microfissures generated in the previous stage gradually expanded laterally, resulting in specimen dilation. Further crack connectivity and pore water redistribution may have increased the continuity of conductive pathways within the specimen. Under these conditions, liquid–liquid conduction pathways may have contributed more significantly to the overall electrical conduction behavior of the soil. Consequently, the resistivity continued to decrease, although the reduction rate gradually slowed until specimen failure occurred51.

By integrating the stage characteristics shown in Fig. 11 with the statistical analysis results, it can be observed that the evolution of resistivity corresponds well with the damage development process of the soil. During Stages I–II, the specimens were dominated by compaction deformation, during which soil pores gradually closed and the connectivity of conductive pathways increased, resulting in a rapid decrease in resistivity. During Stages III–IV, cracks progressively initiated, propagated, and eventually interconnected, while resistivity continued to decrease at a gradually reduced rate. The Pearson correlation analysis and multiple linear regression analysis further demonstrated significant statistical relationships between resistivity parameters and peak stress. These findings suggest that resistivity responses can not only reflect internal structural adjustment and crack development within the soil but also possess potential for evaluating and monitoring its mechanical behavior. It should be noted that mathematical fitting of the complete resistivity–strain curves was not conducted in the present study. Nevertheless, the evolution of resistivity exhibited a clear correspondence with the compaction stage, plastic deformation stage, and failure stage of the specimens, indicating a strong coupling relationship between electrical responses and the deformation–damage development process of the soil.

Effect of organic matter on the compressive strength of organic soils

Fig. 12(a) and (b) show the variation in peak stress of organic soil with organic matter content under different water content conditions. The results indicate that under low water content conditions (below the plastic limit), the peak stress first increases and then decreases with increasing organic matter content. Under this condition, an organic matter content of approximately 1% corresponds to a relatively higher peak strength, indicating that an appropriate amount of organic matter can improve soil structure, whereas excessive organic matter may weaken the effective contacts between soil particles. When the water content exceeds the plastic limit, the sensitivity of peak stress to organic matter content is significantly reduced, and the variation in strength among different organic matter contents becomes much smaller. This indicates that the regulatory effect of organic matter on the mechanical properties of soil is inhibited under high water content conditions. These results further demonstrate that the influence of organic matter on soil strength is strongly dependent on water content. Its reinforcing effect gradually diminishes at high water contents, while water content becomes the dominant factor controlling the mechanical behaviour of the soil. Regarding the failure patterns (Fig. 5), under high water content conditions, the failure mode gradually changes from “cross-shaped cracks” to more “linear cracks” with increasing organic matter content, suggesting adjustments in the internal failure path, while the overall failure characteristics are still primarily governed by the high water content condition. It should be noted that, although all specimens were prepared at the same target dry density, variations in water content and organic matter content may still lead to differences in pore structure and saturation conditions. When the organic matter content was further increased, the peak stress began to decrease. Meanwhile, both the BET specific surface area and Langmuir specific surface area continued to decline (Fig. 14), with the most pronounced reduction observed at an organic matter content of 6%. This trend may indicate that excessive organic matter alters the surface characteristics of soil particles and modifies the pore-structure state. It should be noted that the interpretations regarding pore-structure evolution, crack propagation, conductive pathway variation, and lignin-induced strengthening are primarily inferred from the mechanical response, resistivity evolution, failure characteristics, and specific surface area measurements obtained in this study. Although BET analysis provides some experimental evidence regarding changes in pore-related characteristics, direct microstructural evidence from SEM, MIP, CT scanning, mineralogical characterization, or matric suction measurements is currently unavailable. Therefore, the proposed mechanisms remain largely interpretative and should be regarded as preliminary explanations rather than definitive conclusions. Future studies combining microstructural characterization and multi-scale experimental approaches are needed to further elucidate the evolution of pore structure, particle connectivity, and conductive pathways in organic matter-amended loess. Overall, the results suggest that an appropriate amount of organic matter may influence the compressive behavior of loess by modifying particle arrangements and pore-structure characteristics. The observed enhancement in strength may be associated with macroscopic processes such as particle rearrangement and structural adjustment,however, the underlying microscopic mechanisms require further verification. It should also be emphasized that the long-term environmental behavior and stability of organic carbon in soil are highly complex. The present study did not involve quantitative assessments of carbon cycling processes or carbon sequestration capacity. Therefore, no direct inference or extrapolation regarding carbon storage effects is made herein. The findings of this study provide a useful basis for understanding the mechanical behavior of organically amended loess and offer experimental evidence for investigating the coupling relationship between the physical and mechanical properties of soils. In addition, the evolution of resistivity during deformation indicates a potential relationship between electrical responses and soil structural evolution or damage development. This suggests that resistivity monitoring may have potential applications in characterizing soil deformation and failure processes. Nevertheless, its practical applicability in engineering monitoring requires further validation through field investigations and long-term experimental studies.

Conclusion

To investigate the effects of organic matter on the compressive behavior and electrical resistivity evolution of loess, uniaxial compression tests were conducted on organic soils with varying water contents and organic matter contents. Meanwhile, real-time resistivity responses were monitored using an LCR digital bridge during deformation.
