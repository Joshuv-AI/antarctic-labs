A characterization of the ellipsoid in terms of pairs of sections associated by a harmonic homology Thanks: I would like to thank to Jesús Jerónimo-Castro for the interesting conversations about this work.
Efrén Morales-Amaya

Abstract
. Let K K be a convex body in an affine chart of the n n dimensional real Projective space ℝ ​ ℙ n \mathbb{RP}^{n} , n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 , let H H be a hyperplane which is not a support hyperplane of K K and let p 1 , p 2 ∈ ℝ ​ ℙ n ∖ H p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathbb{RP}^{n}\setminus H be two distinct interior points of K K . In this work we prove that if for for every ( n − 2 ) (n-2) -plane l ⊂ H l\subset H , there exists a harmonic homology, with plane G G and center τ \tau , such that l ⊂ G l\subset G , τ ∈ H \tau\in H and which maps the hypersection of K K defined by aff { p 1 , l } \operatorname*{aff}\{p_{1},l\} onto the hypersection of K K defined by aff { p 2 , l } \operatorname*{aff}\{p_{2},l\} , then K K is an ellipsoid.

A

,

.

2.1.
Some affine notions and results

A body
K\subset\mathbb{E}^{d}
is origin symmetric if when ever
x\in K
, it follows that

-x\in K
. A body K is

or has a

if a translate of

is origin symmetric, i.e., if there is a vector

such that

is origin symmetric.

Let

be a hyperplane and let

be a unit vector
not parallel to

. A mapping

with respect to

, the point

lies on the line parallel to
u
through

x
, at equal distance from
G
, and on the opposite side of
G
from
x
;

u
and
G
are called the
direction
and the
hyperplane of the affine
symmetry
. A convex body
K\subset\mathbb{E}^{d}
is said to be

if

. In particular, if

is orthogonal
to

, we say that the body

is symmetric.

False Center of Symmetry of a convex body.
We say that the point

p\in\mathbb{E}^{d}
is a

of

if: (1)

is not a center
of
K
and (2) for every 2-plane
\Pi
passing through

, the section

\Pi\cap K
is centrally symmetric (in the British language the word center
is

but in this work we will adopt the American word).

In
[
12
]
it is proved that a convex body

,

, with an

center is an ellipsoid. This problem was solved in
[
1
]
for the case when the false
center is in the interior of
K
and in
[
3
]
was presented the solution for the other cases. Both
proofs are quite long and complicated. In
[
9
]
was given a short proof of the False Center
Theorem (FCT) and in
[
10
]
a generalization of the FCT was proposed.

2.2.
Some projective notions and results

Let
\mathbb{E}^{d}
be the
d
-dimensional Euclidian space. We complete

\mathbb{E}^{d}
to the
d
-dimensional real projective space
\mathbb{RP}^{d}
by
adding the hyperplane at the infinity

.
Two points
P_{1},P_{z}
on a line, for which the division ratio with respect to

harmonic with respect to
A
and
B

[
14
]
.
P_{1}
the

of

that the four points are

[A,B;P_{1},P_{2}=-1].

The definition requires that for such points the relation

P_{1}A:P_{1}B=-P_{2}A:P_{2}B

holds.

The

is formed by four points

connected by the six sides

L(A,B),L(A,C),...,L(C,D)
. These sides can be separated into three pairs of opposite sides,
which intersect in the three diagonal points

L(A,B),L(C,D)\textrm{ }\textrm{ in }P;\textrm{ }\textrm{ }L(A,C),L(D,B)\textrm{ }\textrm{ in }Q;\textrm{ }\textrm{ }L(A,D),L(B,C)\textrm{ }\textrm{ in }R.

The following theorem will be used in the proof of the Theorem
1
(see pag. 46 of
[
14
]
):

[Complete quadrangle Theorem]

On every side of a complete quadrangle
the two vertices are harmonic with respect to the points in which this side is intersected by the sides
of the diagonal triangle.

We repeat the dual version of Pappus’ theorem (see Sec. 2-7 and pag. 44 of
[
14
]
):

[Dual version of Pappus’ theorem]

Two lines are intersected by four lines of a pencil in points with same cross ratio
.

Let

\mathbb{E}^{d}
,

, i.e., a compact, convex set with non-empty interior.
The point

,

Under these circumstances, the hyperplane

[

]
.

Notice that if the point

with center

.

The False Pole Theorem.
We say that the point

.

Notice that if

is a false pole of

with respect to

]
:

[False Pole Theorem].

of

We observe that if

3.
Motivation

Let us consider the following question.

Question 1
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{E}^{d}
,
d\geq 3
, be a convex body and let
p_{1},p_{2}\in\operatorname*{int}K
,
p_{1}\not=p_{2}
.
Suppose that, for every
v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, there exists an affine reflection

S_{v^{\perp}}^{u}:\mathbb{E}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{E}^{d}
, for some direction
u
and hyperplane of
reflection
v^{\perp}
, such that

(1)

\displaystyle S_{v^{\perp}}^{u}\big([p_{1}+v^{\perp}]\cap K\big)=[p_{2}+v^{\perp}]\cap K.

What can we say about
K
?

One of the main motivations of this work is to study Question
1
, but not in the context of Affine Geometry,
rather in that of Projective Geometry. Later, we will answer Question
1
in the context of Affine Geometry and
see some reasons why that answer doesn’t work when we move from one geometry to another, and from there, we will deduce
the need to provide an answer in the projective context.

Next, we will show how the Question
1
arose. The following result is due to Rogers
[
12
]
(In fact, Rogers’ result is
more general but for our purposes the next formulation is more relevant):

[Rogers’ Theorem]

Let
K_{1},K_{2}\subset\mathbb{E}^{d}
,
d\geq 3
, be two convex bodies and let
p_{i}\in\operatorname*{int}K_{i}
,
i=1,2
.
Suppose that, for every
v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, there exists a translation

T_{u}:\mathbb{E}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{E}^{d}
, for some direction
u
, such that

(2)

\displaystyle T_{u}\big([p_{1}+v^{\perp}]\cap K_{1}\big)=[p_{2}+v^{\perp}]\cap K_{2}.

Then there exists a translation
T:\mathbb{E}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{E}^{d}
such that
T(K_{1})=K_{2}
.

There are two possibilities: 1)
T(p_{1})=p_{2}
and 2)
T(p_{1})\not=p_{2}
. Let us focus on case 2).
In such case we have the following:

Question 2
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{E}^{d}
,
d\geq 3
, be a convex body and let
p_{1},p_{2}\in\operatorname*{int}K
,
p_{1}\not=p_{2}
.
Suppose that, for every
v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, there exists a translation

T_{u}:\mathbb{E}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{E}^{d}
, for some direction
u
, such that

(3)

\displaystyle T_{u}\big([p_{1}+v^{\perp}]\cap K\big)=[p_{2}+v^{\perp}]\cap K.

What can we say about
K
?

Since the translation for a vector
u
between two parallel sections of
K
passing through
p_{1},p_{2}
can be interpreted as an
affine reflection, with respect to a hyperplane parallel to the section and with direction
u
, we can see that now we are on the conditions
of Question
1
, that is, The Question
1
and Question
2
are equivalent.

On the other hand, the next result, in the spirit of Rogers’ theorem, has a corollary (mentioned as a particular case) related to Question
1
,
notice that in this result were considered only orthogonal reflections, it was proved in
[
11
]
:

If for a pair of convex bodies
K_{1},K_{2}\subset\mathbb{E}^{d}
,
d\geq 3
, there exists a hyperplane

H
and two distinct points
p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathbb{E}^{d}\setminus H
such that for every
(d-2)
-plane

l\subset H
, there exists a reflection mapping the hypersection of
K_{1}
defined by

\mathrm{aff}\{p_{1},l\}
onto the hypersection of
K_{2}
defined by
\mathrm{aff}\{p_{2},l\}
, then the
reflection with respect to
H
maps
K_{1}
onto
K_{2}
.

In particular, if
K_{1}=K_{2}
, then
K_{1}
has
H
as hyperplane of
symmetry.

Answer to Question
1
.

By Larman-Tamvakis’ Theorem
[
4
]
,
K
is centrally symmetric with centre at the
mid-point of
[p_{1},p_{2}]
. Thus, if we take a system of coordinates with the origin at the mid-point
of the line segment
[q_{1},q_{2}]
, for all
v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, the relation

(4)

\displaystyle-([p_{1}+v^{\perp}]\cap K)=[p_{2}+v^{\perp}]\cap K,

holds. By (
3
) and (
4
) it follows that

(5)

\displaystyle-([p_{1}+v^{\perp}]\cap K)=S_{v^{\perp}}^{u}\big([p_{1}+v^{\perp}]\cap K\big).

The relation (
5
) implies that
[p_{1}+u^{\perp}]\cap K

is centrally symmetric, i.e., all the hypersections of
K
passing through
p_{1}
are centrally
symmetric. Since
p_{1}
is not the center of
K
,
p_{1}
is a false center of
K
and,
consequently, by virtue of the FCT,
K
is an ellipsoid.
\square

It is possible to interpret an affine reflection
S_{G}^{u}:\mathbb{E}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{E}^{d}

regarding the hyperplane
G
and the direction
u
as a harmonic homology

\Phi_{G}^{\tau}:\mathbb{P}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{d}
with hyperplane
G
and
center
\tau
, where
\tau
is the point in the hyperplane at infinite
H_{\infty}
defined by

.

Furthermore, it is possible to interpret that the parallel sections of
K
passing through points
p_{1}
,

p_{2}
are defined by hyperplanes that intersect at
H_{\infty}
. Thus, we see that Question

1
, which is
formulated in the affine context, can be extended naturally to Projective Geometry. This allows the
hyperplane

to be any hyperplane, which is not a supporting hyperplane of

, and not only the

.

However, as soon as we do this, we realize that we no longer have a projective version of the
Larman-Tamvakis theorem, which was the starting point of our answer to Question
1
.
That is why an original answer to the general question is necessary. The Theorem
1
does
precisely that: it establishes the statement that defines the general situation and provides a proof.
