Project Goals and Objectives
Understanding complex reaction pathways in systems governed by multi-scale collective interactions across
time and length scales remains a central scientific challenge. This project was guided by the hypothesis that
the interplay among oligomers, solvents, and active sites can be tuned by a suitable choice of solvation envi-
ronment and pore architecture in solid-acid catalysts to direct chemical transformations relevant to biomass
conversion. Zeolites and zeolite nanosheets were used as model platforms, allowing for the interaction of
macromolecules with the surface of the zeolite nanosheets and with smaller pores that host catalytically
active sites. To investigate these coupled phenomena, we employ a multi-scale computational framework
that integrates molecular-level descriptions with advanced sampling approaches to capture key physical and
chemical interactions. Our work through this project improved fundamental understanding of how reactants
and solid-acid catalysts interact in solvent-rich environments, thereby enabling the rational design of cat-
alytic systems that upgrade biomass with enhanced selectivity and energy efficiency. In addition, the project
developed advanced sampling methodologies critical for disentangling complex, reactive processes in multi-
component catalytic environments. This manuscript has resulted in 8 articles published in peer-reviewed
journals [1-8] and one manuscript currently under peer review.[9]

In order to provide the thermodynamic basis for the observed mixing behavior, we borrow concepts from the
regular solution theory. Using regular solution theory one can show that when w/kT (w is the interchange
energy) is 2.5 or larger both the random mixing theory and the quasi-chemical approximation predict phase
separation. If we make the assumption that PMF of phosphonium ions closely mimics the interchange
energy, we can use the minima in the PMF to rationalize the miscibility behavior. The calculated w/kT
for [P2222 ], [P4444 ], [P6666 ], [P8888 ], and [P12121212 ] for the chloride IL is 0.13, 0.48, 1.23, 3.64, and 9.26,
respectively. For the acetate IL the corresponding numbers are very similar (0.07, 0.47, 0.80, 3.04, and 9.58,
respectively). Based on this relatively simple analysis, we can recommend that systems with w/kT (using
minimum in PMF profile as w) smaller than 2.5 would be miscible, and 2.5 to 4.0 would lead to emulsion
formation, while larger than 4.0 would lead to formation of interfacial systems. (See Reference 2 for more
details.)

Effect of Solvent Quality on Structure & Dynamics of Lignin: Here, we examined the structure and dynam-
ics of lignin in water/methanol mixtures using all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Methanol,
one of the cheapest and readily available green solvents produced from biomass components by demethoxy-
lation, has been widely used in organosolv pretreatment and lignin depolymerization due to its low boiling
point and ease of recovery. Prior computational work on lignin conformation suggests that lignin under-
goes morphological transition due to the change of external conditions during solubilization. Morphological
transition can provide information regarding self-aggregation during fractionation and "solvolytically or
catalytically competent conformation" for depolymerization. Conformational changes of lignin can be elu-
cidated by the framework of "solvent quality". Here, we investigated the structure of two lignin types (linear
softwood and linear hardwood) in the binary mixtures of methanol and water. Thus we aimed to provide
detailed molecular insights into the solvation of lignin in methanol and their binary mixtures with water as
cosolvent at three different temperatures (300, 340, and 380 K). Through careful analysis of trajectory and
molecular interactions, we rationalize the role of solvent in the structure and dynamics adopted by the lignin

Monte Carlo Simulations with ab initio and Machine Learned Interatomic Potentials: One of the motivations
for developing ASE-MC suite was to enable Monte Carlo simulation with using system energies and forces
using DFT and MLIPs, allowing users to choose the software they prefer to calculate energies and forces. To
further enhance the flexibility of ASE-MC, another goal was to enable users to add custom MC moves with
a minimal amount of Python coding. As an example to demonstrate these features, we designed a custom
torsion move to sample the characteristic dihedral angle of biphenyl at 300 K. The software flexibility
is shown by sampling the dihedral angle using GPAW, Quantum Espresso, as well as the foundational
MACE model, MACE-MP0. To minimize how much Python a user would need to write to create a custom
MC move, we utilize Python inheritance to pass several attributes and methods that are common in MC
moves and are implemented in a MCMove object. This design strategy is consistent with how MoSDeF’s
mbuild package is used to design complicated molecular structures and how PyTorch is used to design
neural networks. Both GPAW and Quantum Espresso used the PBE exchange-correlation functional and
a 60 Ry plane-wave kinetic energy cutoff. Pseudopotentials for Quantum Espresso were taken from the
Standard Solid State Pseudopotential library (SSSP PBEsol Precision v1.3.0), whereas GPAW describes
core electrons using the projector augmented wave method, with initial atomic setups obtained from the
developers.

Espresso and GPAW is located around the minima of the potential energy surface at 40, 140, 220, and 320
degrees, with only a few observations in the local maxima at 0, 90, 180, 270, and 360 degrees. However,
the MACE-MP0 model overpredicts the energy barrier, compared to DFT at 90 and 270 degrees, where the
two aromatic rings are perpendicular to each other, by approximately 0.177 eV. Additionally, while there
is a small barrier at 0 and 180 degrees, where the aromatic rings lie parallel to each other, the barrier is
underpredicted by approximately 0.061 eV. This leads to significantly more sampling in the MC simulation
in the vicinity of 180 degrees. Additionally, not only does MACE-MP0 incorrectly predict the locations
of the minima on the potential energy surface, but it also indicates that the potential energy surface is not
symmetric between 0 and 180 degrees and 180 and 360 degrees. This is a shortcoming of the MACE-MP0
model, as clearly, for biphenyl, the potential energy surface should be identical from 0 to 180 degrees as
from 180 to 360 degrees, as shown by the DFT calculations. Although the MACE-MP0 model is trained on
a wide variety of systems and molecules as described in the original manuscript, refinement is necessary for
polyaromatic species. These simulations demonstrate that multiple DFT software packages and MLIPs are
compatible with ASE-MC. It is possible to implement custom MC moves with a few lines of Python, and
random number broadcasting is performed correctly within ASE-MC. (See Reference 8 for more details.)
