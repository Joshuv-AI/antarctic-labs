Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

This project provided funding to NREL/Tandem PV by EERE-SETO and has the goal of
improving perovskite module stability by at least a factor of 2 without sacrificing initial
efficiency. This will be demonstrated using 3”x3” minimodules and will be tested according to
the Innovation and Entrepreneurship Center (IEC) standards and demonstrated using outdoor
testing. A CRADA was established between NREL and Tandem PV to address the goals for the
funded effort.

Summary of Research Results:

Here were the tasks under the CRADA that supported a DOE award. All the tasks were
conducted. A description of the work and results are in the attached award report.

Task 1: Barrier materials identification and coating

Identify and test barrier materials and compatibility with perovskite cell materials

Description: This task focused on the following

 • Identify barrier material candidates (literature study)
 • Develop inks for deposition of the top candidates
 • Test compatibility and performance of top candidates.
 • Analysis used for this: (SEM), x-ray diffraction (XRD), Profilometry, UV-VIS
 transmission/reflection, conductivity, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS).

Multiple barrier materials were identified.

Barrier materials required the following properties:

 1. Prevent diffusion of cell stack elements. Diffusion may be measured by multiple
 methods, including but not limited to XPS depth profiling, Auger spectroscopy, and
 SIMS.
 2. No chemical interaction with cell stack materials, i.e., no change in functional properties.
 Changes to functional properties may be measured by multiple methods, including but
 not limited to UV-Vis absorption, 4-point probe, scanning electron microscopy (SEM),
 atomic force microscopy (AFM) roughness, and solar simulation of solar cells.
 3. Provide some conductivity (P2) or insulation (P3). Conductivity/insulation properties
 may be measured by multiple methods, including but not limited to test structures
 including diodes and transistors, hall measurements, 4-point probe measurements, and
 non-contact mobility measurements.

Solution processable without damaging the materials it is being deposited on. Damage may be
measured by multiple methods, including but not limited to UV-Vis absorption, 4-point probe,
profilometry, AFM roughness, and solar simulation of solar cells.

 2

Task 2: Module Development

Implement the barrier materials into functional modules

Description: This task is focused on the implementation of the identified barrier materials into
3”x3” mini modules. The barriers can be deposited locally in the scribe lines by, e.g., inkjet
printing, or can be applied as a uniform coating on top of the to-be-protected layers through, e.g.,
blade or slot-die coating. The size of the module allows for each module to have several cells
connected in series through their interconnects. Modules with and without barrier materials were
made so that a direct comparison can be performed. Both module types were optimized for
maximum power conversion efficiency (PCE) performance, and it is expected that the modules
with and without barriers will have very comparable efficiencies. This allows for a good
comparison of the stability of the modules. Even though the barrier inks developed in task #1 are
developed with the deposition techniques in mind, as part of this task it is expected that some
further optimization of the ink is required to achieve the appropriate wetting, adhesion, and
material quality.

Task 3: Durability testing

Perform timed degradation testing in controlled environment and outdoors.

Description: This task tests the modules made in task #2 under various environmental stress
protocols. Most importantly, the modules were tested according to standards in IEC 61215
(damp heat and thermal cycling). For these tests, the modules’ performance are measured before
the stress test and then tested again after the stress. Additionally, the modules are tested to
demonstrate how they behave under real-life conditions. When possible, modules are placed
under load and tested regularly over a period of 2000 hrs.

Task 4: Encapsulation

Collect literature on encapsulated perovskite cells for various device stacks and determine the
one best compatible with available equipment.

Description: This task focuses on identifying an encapsulation method that minimizes
degradation of the cell stack, i.e., the device layers. This task looks at glass-glass
encapsulation using encapsulant materials, e.g., EVA, and edge seals, e.g., butyl rubber with
getters. The identified material does not have to be the best material, but it should reduce
degradation to a level that allows for deconvolution of the degradation caused in the PV stack
versus degradation caused in the module interconnects. The encapsulation method tested uses
devices with the same stack as used for the modules in task #3.

Task 5: Stakeholder engagement

Conduct interviews with stakeholders and use acquired knowledge to maximize outcome of the

Description: For this task, several entities (manufacturers, suppliers, and end users) were

This allows us to prioritize the research and ensure that the final product matches best with what
can be done, what is available, and what is required to make this a commercial success.

Major Goals & Objectives 1:

The goal of this project is to improve perovskite module stability by passivating exposed scribe
lines. Our hypothesis is that scribe-induced degradation is a major culprit for the stability of
perovskite modules and through developed protection of the exposed area in scribes, we hope to
improve the operational lifetime of perovskite modules, by a factor of 2 without sacrificing
initial efficiency. This will be demonstrated using 1x3” mini modules and will be tested using JV
scans and maximum power point tracking (MPPT).

Project Results and Discussion2:

First Year (BP1) Review

In the first budget period of the scribe passivation project eight barrier materials were developed
and tested to be compatible with the perovskite active layer as well as the hydrothermal
liquefaction (HTL), emissions-to-liquids (ETL), and contact layers (TASK 1). These inks were
developed to be inkjet-printed for localized coatings to infill perovskite module scribes. We
developed three stressor tests that compared spin-coated barrier materials directly on perovskite
to compare the effectiveness of a barrier to reduce degradation and the conversion of the

perovskite to lead iodide. The three stressor tests developed were a water drop test, a heat test,
and a light soak test.

The water drop test involved dropping 12 L deionized (DI) water droplets directly on the
perovskite, or perovskite with a barrier layer, to visually test how quickly a conversion takes
place. DI water on perovskite converts the phase from black to yellow within 3 to 4 seconds. The
heat test involves placing perovskite samples directly on an 85C hot plate and comparing
conversion times of the black to yellow change. Similarly, the light soak test is placing
perovskite samples under an AM1.5 spectrum lamp (at about 0.7 suns) in ambient light and
timing the conversion.

Many barrier materials were able to slow the yellowing of the perovskite from the water drop to
the order of minutes, and many barrier materials were capable of effectively reducing
degradation for the light soak test, but many initial barrier materials were incapable of
significantly reducing degradation due to the 85C hot plate. Eventually we tested CYTOP, an
expensive, commercially made fluoropolymer. On its own, CYTOP performed well with all
three tests, but when layered in combination with a terpineol and polyethyleneimine (TERPEI)
solution, we got very effective results. The combination of CYTOP and TERPEI, in that order,
led to what we call a super barrier (SB).

The SB was extremely effective for all three tests. Water droplets were incapable of penetrating
through the barrier films and would slide off the film like a windowpane if the sample was tilted.
Furthermore, water drops left negligible damage if the droplet was left on the sample until it
dried. For both the heat and light soak tests, our developed SB was also extremely effective. To
the eye, there was an unnoticeable change to the SB sample after many hours of both the heat
and light soak tests.

Fig. 1 shows the difference between a control perovskite without a barrier layer and a perovskite
coated with our developed SB. Ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) percent transmission data was taken
to quantify the effectiveness of our barrier layer. Data was taken after 30 minutes of intense heat
of 140C to imitate potential lamination and packaging temperatures. Then data was taken again
after 160 hours at 85C. Fig. 1A and 1B clearly indicate with UV-Vis what is clear to the eye in
Fig. 1C; that the control, after being subjected to 85C for 160 hours, had completely converted
to a nearly transparent film, whereas the perovskite with our developed SB had not changed
phases. To quantify the change, the absolute value of the change in percent transmission was
calculated in comparison to after 30 min at 140C and after 160 hours at 85C. These values are
displayed in the table in Fig. 1, suggesting that the control had degraded eight times more than
our SB-coated sample.

Up to this point, the CYTOP, TERPEI SB was the most successful barrier layer that we had
developed. However, there are significant issues with this barrier layer beyond its experimental
capabilities. CYTOP is not only very expensive (estimated $6,000 for 250 mL) but also had
major lag times for shipping and therefore was concerning to be considered and applicable
material for large-scale production. At this point the project was halted and renegotiated.

Milestone 1 Review

For the first milestone, the first step of the proposal had the general goal to take a wider look at
materials and, rather than focus on localized inkjet printing, expand to doctor blading blanket
coatings of barrier materials. First and foremost, to better understand the potential options, an in-
depth literature review was conducted. An Excel spreadsheet of literature reports for potential
barrier materials used was shared with the DOE, and eight barrier materials from this list were
selected and tested. The selected barrier materials were from material classes including styrenes,
acrylates, carbonates, and siloxanes with an aim to identify a simpler/cheaper and effective
coating material.

We then evaluated the eight-barrier material’s ability to stabilize perovskite thin films under
degradation mode tests developed in BP1 of the project as described above. Bare perovskite
films were scribed to the indium tin oxide (ITO) surface both mechanically and by using a laser,

 7

then coated with each barrier material. Optical mapping was used to examine the scribe area. In
addition to the three stressor tests as described in BP1, a fourth test of the combined stressor of
heat and humidity was added.

Each of the barrier-coated films was placed under illumination of ~0.7 sun AM1.5 spectrum
under ambient laboratory atmosphere for at least 50 hours and evaluated for color loss, indicating
the time it takes for conversion from the perovskite phase to lead iodide. The films were also
subjected to elevated temperatures of 85C for at least 100 hours, evaluating the amount of color
loss or the time it takes for conversion from the perovskite phase to lead iodide. The third test
was a water drop test measuring the time it takes for the water to penetrate and convert the
perovskite phase to lead iodide. The 4th test was a combined heat and humidity test using a
homemade controlled humidity apparatus on a hot plate to imitate an 85/85 chamber. Each
degradation mode was evaluated with each barrier layer being compared to an uncoated
perovskite and a PMMA- coated sample as controls.

The results of the four barrier layer tests compared to the perovskite control gave insight on the
best barrier materials and which materials we wanted to select for the next milestone. Of the barrier
materials tested PS, PMMA, SiOx, and Al2O3 were selected to study further on perovskite modules.

Milestone 2 Review

Having tested eight barrier materials, it was clear that four were exceeding the others. While the
PEI/CYTOP SB worked well, cost and complexity issues led to the material’s elimination from
the study. The four barrier materials that are not only capable of passing our stressor tests but
also are also economical and commercially abundant are PS, PMMA, SiO x, and Al2O3. These
barrier materials were then tested on the performance of perovskite modules and how they affect
performance, if they infill or completely cover the exposed scribes, and their optical properties.

Perovskite solar cells and components of perovskite solar cells were exposed to the potential
barrier materials and their respective solvents. Each of the layers within a perovskite module is
susceptible to degradation due to the interaction with the barrier material or its solvent.
Perovskite submodules were built on 1”x3” substrates. Selected barrier materials were then blade
coated onto perovskite submodules and inspected for degradation. Most importantly, the PCE of
the module, using modules with a PCE floor of 10%, had to be maintained within 1% (absolute)
of the starting efficiency (for example, a 12% module must be above 11% after coating). The
stacks were then investigated by optical microscopy, optical profilometry, SEM, stylus
profilometry, and JV scans under solar simulation. Stylus and optical profilometry, as well as
SEM, were used to investigate the P3 and P4 scribes and confirmed they are filled with the
barrier materials with more than the minimum thickness of 50 nm. Optical constants were
measured by ellipsometry to identify the optical constants of the barrier films.

Module Barrier Layer Performance Test

The most important goal for milestone 2.2.1. was achieving a PCE within 1% of the pre-coated
performance. To satisfy this requirement, we made two separate batches of 10 1x3” IZO, semi-
transparent modules with seven submodules on each substrate as shown in Fig. 2. These 10
modules were then aged five days. On the fifth day, all the barrier materials were applied to their
respective modules. But before any barrier layer treatment was applied, all ten modules (70
submodules) were measured for pre-deposition measurements. Then, all the materials were
applied. Two modules were blade coated with PS, and two modules were blade coated with
PMMA. Two modules were given approximately 50 nm of Al2O3 by atomic layer deposition
(ALD). Two modules were given approximately 1000 nm of SiOx by thermal evaporation. The
last two modules were left uncoated without a barrier material to be measured as a control. The
two control modules remained in the same case as the blade-coated modules to experience the
same ambient environment through the process. After all the modules were coated with their
respective barrier materials, they were all measured again for post-deposition performance.

Fig. 2 shows before and after the deposition of the barrier materials into modules (TASK 2). On
each module are vertical and horizontal scribes. The vertical scribes are the P1, P2, and P3, used
to put two solar cells in series to create a submodule. The horizontal scribes are the P4 scribes,
which separate each module substrate into seven submodules. It is most important to note that
there is no visible degradation to any of the scribes after the application of the respective barrier

Proof of Infill: SEM of Barrier Materials in Module Scribes

We next aim to show that the barrier material is in contact with the scribe walls by either fully
filling the P3 and P4 or by showing a layer of the material at least 50 nm thick coating the walls
inside the scribes. Optical microscopy and optical profilometry were insufficient methods to
prove the infill of the scribe because both methods were imaging through the material. Stylus
profilometry was also insufficient to prove an infill of scribes because the coated layers followed
a similar geometry of the scribe since all the barrier layers were blanket coated. The best method
we found to show infill and complete scribe wall passivation was cross-sectional SEM.

Fig. 3 shows SEM images of the four barrier materials inside P2 and P3 scribes. These four
images show each barrier material completely covering the side walls of the scribe; therefore, no
perovskite is exposed. Fig. 3A shows the Al2O3 does not fill the scribe but maintains a precise
thickness and film uniformity around the edges of the scribe. Figs. 3B, 3C, and 3D show PMMA,
PS, and SiOx respectively. Each of these three barrier materials completely fills the scribe and
encases the scribe sidewall with the barrier material. Furthermore, each of the barrier layer films
in the SEM images maintains uniformity around the scribe corners to provide no ingress or
egress. The behavior and quality of the films displayed below are representative of their behavior
and quality throughout the entire 1x3” module substrate. The SEM images in Fig. 3 give the
evidence needed to prove that the scribes have been fully encased in their respective barrier
materials.

Barrier Films Optical Properties

We have shown that the barrier films do not affect PCE performance beyond 1% and that the
films are completely encasing the exposed perovskite in module scribes. The last requirement of
milestone 2 was to find the optical properties of the barrier films. Ellipsometry measurements
were taken to understand the barrier films’ refraction index, n, and extinction coefficient, k.
Films were blade-coated on blank 1x3” microscope slides, with an additional blank microscope
slide being used as the substrate baseline for the n and k calculations. Fig. 4A shows the four
barrier materials on blank glass microscope slides. From left to right, the barrier layers are Al2O3,
SiOx, PMMA, and PS. Aside from SiOx, all barriers are transparent. Future experiments will test
SiOx thicknesses to optimize percent transmission with module stability. Fig. 4B shows the
calculated n and k values from ellipsometry measurements using the Cauchy model for Al2O3,
the Cody Lorentz model for SiOx, the Cauchy model for PMMA, and the GenOsc model for PS.
The values shown in Fig. 4B represent the values at wavelength 371 nm and at 1596 nm. A
steady and gradual change between these values occurs within the wavelengths 372 nm to 1595
nm and is within the range of the listed values. The ellipsometry measurement calculations of n
and k show that SiOx has the highest refraction index and extinction coefficient.

Impact3:

Although much work has been done to address Perovskite Solar Cell (PSC) degradation, to
facilitate TandemPV’s semitransparent PSC module product, we must address degradation and
performance factors inherent to module interconnects (TASK 3).

Our product design uses a non-metallic top contact, so we do not expect metal-migration-
induced ion exchange at our P2 scribes; many of the most stable modules reported use
alternatives to metal electrodes such as ours (Yulia Galagan 2020, J. Phys. Energy 2 021004).
Recent work (ACS Appl Energy Mater. 2019, 2, 6097-6101) indicates that MAI, MAPbI3, and
FAPbI3 etch ITO interfaces, causing In3+ ions to leach into the perovskite layer in the form of
InI3. Therefore, we tested for P2-related degradation in our modules, especially contact
resistance increases that could indicate ion exchange at IZO-perovskite interfaces.

We blanket-coated barrier materials to protect perovskite materials that have been exposed by
the P3 scribe. The materials exposed by the P3 scribe can experience accelerated degradation due
to oxygen exposure, moisture ingress, and the release of volatile perovskite species in the
presence of heat or light. As seen in Fig. 5, the P3 scribe sees the formation of PbI 2 after over 24
hours of light soaking in the presence of oxygen and moisture.

Identify >2 encapsulation schemes

This task (TASK 4) was completed early in BP1 and looked at glass-glass encapsulation using
encapsulant materials, e.g., EVA, and edge seals, e.g., butyl rubber with getters. The identified
material aimed to reduce degradation to a level that allows for deconvolution of the degradation
caused in the PV stack versus degradation caused in the module interconnects. However, it was
decided that encapsulation using glass-glass edge seals was counterproductive to rapidly
assessing barrier materials. The goal of the barrier materials and scribe infilling was to further
slowdown the degradation beyond that of the encapsulation. Thus, to more rapidly assess barrier
and scribe infilling procedures, we elected to study devices and materials without full
encapsulation to really see the effects of the barrier when exposed to moisture or oxygen, which
skirted the outer encapsulation scheme.

Nonetheless, the milestone was completed by evaluating established encapsulation schemes and
their feasibility to be combined with scribe infilling.

Scheme one involved the conventional polyisobutylene edge seal to seal a back sheet glass onto
the substrate. This is a common scheme used in other thin film PV technologies like CdTe. The
polyisobutylene is impregnated with desiccant to slow the ingress of moisture. This seal provides
30-year protection when the edge seal is 1 cm wide.

Another concern is the polymer back sheet, which fills the void between the two pieces of glass
and holds the top of the perovskite module to that of the back glass. Traditional EVA polymers
used in this case have shown acetate degradation in perovskite materials but in our instance can
be replaced with polyolefins. It was determined and reported to DOE that scribe infills proposed
in this work should not alter the back sheet function in this scheme.

The second encapsulation scheme involves simple evaporation of silicon monoxide, which was
leveraging other work done in the Luther group at NREL. It was found that a 1-micron thermal
evaporation of silicon monoxide on the rear of a finished solar cell provides protection against
oxygen and moisture, among other things. It was presented that an encapsulation scheme like this
could either be the infill and encapsulation or could supplement the infill by further
encapsulating. This scheme is not limited to SiOx, as ALD oxides could also be deposited on top
of the finished device stack for encapsulation purposes.

Again, the goal of this task is to identify how the devices with infilled scribes would merge with
final device encapsulation. Both strategies are additive and not envisioned to affect the role of
the scribe infilling.
