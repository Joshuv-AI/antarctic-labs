Evaluating Harm Reduction Advice From Large Language Models

Khy Choo, Nathan Smith, Bernadette Ward, Robert M. Lundin

Abstract

ABSTRACTIntroductionLarge language models (LLM) and generative Artificial Intelligence are increasingly providing health information. We have applied a novel method to evaluate the harm reduction information generated by popular LLMs. We focused on the filtration of oral medications for injection and the availability of local harm reduction services.MethodsWe designed a scoring rubric to evaluate the accuracy of the information provided by each LLM and applied it to four unique, commonly used LLMs.ResultsGemini 2.5 Flash (87%) and ChatGPT‐4o (77%) scored higher than DeepSeek R1 Distill Llama 3.3 70B (22%) and Llama 4 (18%). Overall, both Gemini and ChatGPT‐4o gave comprehensive and accurate instructions for the use of micron filters for drug injection, while neither DeepSeek R1 nor Llama 4 provided instructions on their use. Gemini 2.5 and ChatGPT‐4o both scored highly for harm reduction general information and situational questions, while DeepSeek R1 and Llama 4 provided limited to no responses. Gemini 2.5 and ChatGPT‐4o both provided accurate, location‐specific information on local harm reduction services. Specific models also generated misinformation, further affirming the need for in‐person, nuanced discussions with harm reduction specialists.Discussion and ConclusionsIn this study, we presented a novel method for evaluating LLMs' potential effectiveness for harm reduction across common techniques and situations. Most of the tested LLMs provided reasonable information in a non‐judgmental manner, but they also provided misinformation, and some wouldn't answer questions due to built‐in safety parameters. The developed scoring rubric can be easily adapted to other harm reduction topics for ongoing evaluation.
Generative Artificial Intelligence is likely to be used by people seeking harm‐reduction information, but its reliability for specific information on injecting and localised resources remains uncertain.This study developed and trialled a scoring rubric for evaluating large language model responses to wheel‐filter and injecting‐related harm‐reduction prompts.Gemini 2.5 and ChatGPT‐4o provided more comprehensive wheel‐filter and local harm‐reduction information than Meta Llama 4 and DeepSeek R1.All evaluated systems had limitations, including incomplete responses, content restrictions, or potentially harmful misinformation.Generative Artificial Intelligence may become a useful adjunct to traditional harm‐reduction services and in areas where primary services are not located, but outputs require expert review. This scoring rubric can be easily adapted for other harm reduction topics.

Introduction

The use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in health care is advancing rapidly []. Since the introduction of Chat Generative Pre‐trained Transformer (ChatGPT) by OpenAI and the emergence of other generative AI chatbots, such as Google Gemini, Meta Llama and DeepSeek R1, their applications in a range of health care settings have been gaining momentum []. Large language models (LLM) utilised by generative AI can generate human‐like text, unlike rule‐based systems with fixed outputs [].

The benefits of generative AI in psychiatry are promising. ChatGPT has been found to provide informative and relevant responses on topics such as clozapine use [], electroconvulsive therapy treatment [], bipolar disorder [] and nicotine cessation []. Responses from ChatGPT‐4 and Llama 2 (LLM Meta AI) often display empathetic language, and their use has been largely supported by clinicians []. However, there are concerns about the dissemination of dangerous misinformation or conflicting answers, and about the inability of generative AI chatbots to consider consumers' emotional states and non‐verbal cues [].

Injection‐related infections are a common adverse outcome associated with injecting drug use []. The proportion of the Australian population who inject drugs has decreased in the last two decades, but among those who do inject, there has been an increase in injection‐related acute infections []. The use of wheel filters is associated with a reduction in crushed tablet injecting‐related infections []. While the importance of addressing the barriers to accessing health care for this population cannot be understated, harm reduction initiatives to prevent injecting‐related infections remain a priority.

There have been few studies examining the provision of harm‐reduction knowledge by generative AI chatbots. Given the self‐ and social stigma associated with seeking harm reduction interventions [], there is a need to evaluate the use of generative AI technology, as it may alleviate many barriers to accessing harm reduction information. Generative AI chatbots could play a supportive role in harm reduction and the management of substance use disorders by simulating real‐time human conversation. Rather than waiting for formal evaluations through published research, service providers could utilise tools to rapidly assess the validity of generative AI chatbots for specific harm‐reduction information.

The aim of this project was to develop and examine a service‐based evaluation tool for the use of generative AI chatbots in harm‐reduction services. Given the extra‐medical intravenous use of prescription tablets [] and the acceptability and effectiveness of filtration in reducing injecting‐related harms [], we chose to examine harm reduction information associated with the use of wheel/μm filters. Specifically, we developed and trialled a method to assess the accuracy of harm‐reduction information about wheel filters generated by generative AI systems.

Methods

The research team, which includes addiction and harm reduction specialists, developed 12 prompts (items) that included hypothetical situations using wheel filters, common risky drug use behaviours and location‐specific (Victoria, Australia) harm reduction information (see Table ). The prompts were categorised into comprehensiveness (general information, situational questions and resources), accuracy and misinformation (accurate and factual information), relevance (whether resources provided were appropriate to the local context), presence of disclaimers, language use (empathy and supportive language content limitations) [].

In the absence of an existing scale, two members of the research team independently and inductively categorised harm reduction information on the use of wheel filters and vein care available from peak harm reduction services [, , , , , ] into three key themes: (i) ‘general information’ (how to use, steps in using, choice of, reasons for); (ii) ‘situational’ (amount of drug injected, pre‐heating, blood‐borne viruses and vein care, filters and blockages, use with MS Contin); and (iii) ‘resource provision’ (how, when and where to get resources and support in specific areas) (see Appendix ). Consensus on these headings and sub‐headings was reached following consultation with a third author. To maintain topic integrity, specific scoring prompts were included under each sub‐heading. If the LLM generated information matched and was entirely correct 1 point was awarded (i.e., complete). If the information was partially correct 0.5 points were awarded (i.e., incomplete). Where information was absent or completely incorrect, 0 points were awarded. Possible heading scores were 28, 14 and 18 points respectively, for a total of 60 points.

Qualitative thematic analysis of generative responses was conducted to identify the use of empathetic, non‐judgmental language. This was tested with one AI and scored independently by two authors. Where there was disagreement, consensus was reached through a third author.

Publicly accessible versions of generative AI systems were utilised on default settings as new accounts with no previous history. Selected models were ChatGPT‐4o by OpenAI [], Gemini 2.5 Flash by Google [], Meta Llama 4 by Meta AI [] and DeepSeek R1 Distill Llama 3.3 70B accessed via Lambda [].

For consistency, all prompts were entered consecutively into the respective generative AI interfaces. Responses were collected during a 2‐week period in July 2025. Data were analysed by two unblinded research team members, verified against the harm reduction resources utilised and by addiction specialists within the service. Full transcripts and completed scoring sheets are available in Appendix .

Results

Comprehensiveness

The scores were Gemini 2.5 (87%), ChatGPT‐4o (77%), DeepSeek R1 (22%) and Llama 4 (18%) (see Table ). Specifically, Gemini 2.5 provided complete and appropriate lists of materials and steps required to use a wheel filter, receiving the highest scores for both general information questions and situational questions. ChatGPT‐4o provided some of the materials required for wheel filter use but omitted additional steps, such as priming the filter with sterile water. Gemini 2.5 and ChatGPT‐4o scored similarly regarding resource provision. Neither DeepSeek R1 nor Llama 4 provided instructions on wheel filter use, and limited information was provided for the situations prompted. None of the responses from the evaluated generative AI systems included links to available instructional videos demonstrating wheel filter use.

Accuracy and Misinformation

There were several examples of inaccurate results or misinformation in response to the prompt ‘Does the wheel filter reduce the amount of drug injected?’ from DeepSeek R1 and ChatGPT‐4o. The use of wheel filters for injecting drugs is ‘highly discouraged due to significant health risks’. Similarly, ChatGPT‐4o recommended use of a ‘1–2 mL’ syringe to ‘minimise leftover volume’ (see Table ).

Resource Relevance

ChatGPT‐4o and Gemini 2.5 provided a comprehensive list of harm reduction resources, drug and alcohol hotlines, in‐person harm reduction agencies, and locations where wheel filters can be obtained in Melbourne. In contrast, Llama 4 provided a limited list of harm reduction services. Some national harm reduction resources were provided, but no hotlines or in‐person organisations were listed when general harm reduction resources were queried. DeepSeek R1 did not recommend any harm reduction services where wheel filters could be obtained. Instead, non‐Australian resources, such as the North American Centre for Disease Control and Prevention website, were cited.

In response to location prompts, ChatGPT‐4o and Gemini 2.5 provided location‐specific information for two regional towns, including links to relevant websites and phone contacts of local harm reduction services. Llama 4 suggested Melbourne harm reduction services, and DeepSeek R1 did not provide any.

Disclaimers

Disclaimers regarding the limitations of educational information were provided by ChatGPT‐4o and Gemini 2.5 in response to the first prompt. ChatGPT‐4o recommended seeking further information at local services. Gemini 2.5 reported that ‘whilst harm reduction strategies decrease the harm of injecting drug use, it does not fully eliminate associated risks.’ No obviously identifiable disclaimer statements were provided by Llama 4 and DeepSeek R1 (see Table ).

Language Use

All four AI systems responded with unique empathic and supportive language. ChatGPT‐4o included phrases such as ‘you're not alone’ and ‘nonjudgmental and ... respect[ful]’ support is available. Statements such as ‘that's a great question—accessing harm reduction information is a smart and empowering step’ were also observed. Fewer empathetic statements were provided by Gemini 2.5 and Llama 4, but they did comment on the availability of ‘confidential and non‐judgmental’ and ‘helpful’ harm reduction support, respectively. Empathetic language was also used in response to situational prompts related to troubleshooting wheel filter use. This was evident in DeepSeek R1's response, ‘I'm really sorry to hear that you're experiencing discomfort.’ Reassuring language of ‘you're not alone’ was also generated by DeepSeek R1 (see Table ).

Content Limitations

ChatGPT‐4o and Gemini 2.5 provided responses to all prompts, with no restrictions encountered during the conversation. However, when the prompt ‘how to use a wheel filter for injecting drugs?’ was entered into Llama 4 and DeepSeek R1, no information could be generated (see Table ).

Discussion

To our knowledge, no evaluation of generative AI responses on wheel filters for harm reduction (let alone the development of a generic assessment tool) has been conducted. The large variation in the comprehensiveness of responses across the different language models tested may result from the LLMs' parameter settings and training methods underpinning the selected AI systems. In contrast to our findings, other studies have found that ChatGPT‐4o yielded generally satisfactory, clinician‐approved responses for basic information [, ], with statistically significantly higher‐quality responses than Gemini 2.5 []. However, the pace at which upgrades in generative AI performance capabilities occur may limit the generalisability of these findings over time.

We identified responses that, in some cases, were harmful (e.g., discouraging the use of wheel filters and using 1–2 mL syringes to minimise leftover volume). Wheel filters significantly decrease the risk of serious health complications by filtering impurities and bacteria from injecting drug mixtures []. Many tablets require more than 1–2 mL of water to sufficiently dissolve before injecting, and drugs such as morphine have a solubility of 30 mg/mL and are available in 100 and 200 mg strengths [].

Misinformation from generative AI systems has previously been reported []. Given that 10% of Australians report recent use of ChatGPT for health information, and a further 38% would consider doing so [], there is a need for increased clinical input in the formulation of responses for generative AI systems to ensure user safety when seeking harm reduction information and service support. This is also important because misinformation may reflect the inherent biases underpinning LLMs. Despite this, it was encouraging to see that the generative AI systems we tested included disclaimers and advised seeking medical help in many responses.

We found that location‐specific advice was limited in the AI responses. While greater improvement of generative AI technologies may be a means of reducing the barriers associated with accessing harm reduction information [], there needs to be adequate oversight of local service information, as there is a risk of exacerbating existing disparities in harm reduction information and service provision between metropolitan and rural communities.

This study had limitations. We only compared four generative AI systems, and the context‐specific prompts (with different types and degrees of risk) were limited. We did not follow up with chatbot questioning, so it is unclear how these generative AI systems would perform in a conversational setting. Furthermore, prompts may not fully replicate real‐world user questions, which may include slang and colloquial language, as they were crafted by our research team to be clear and concise. However, this study provides a baseline for future studies which should also include inter‐rater reliability metrics for improved consistency. Comparisons between generative AI technologies, online search engines and human input could also be investigated.

The exponential growth in the use of AI in health care means there is a significant time lag in research evaluations of its use. While it is not psychometrically validated, the principles underpinning this tool make it (mindful of the caveats above) easy to use in service settings, as it could be adapted for a range of harm reduction (or other) topics.

Conclusion

This study provides new insights into how generative AI systems can be applied in alcohol and other drugs. Importantly, the scale provides a baseline from which subsequent updates can be assessed. With greater clinical supervision and input, generative AI systems have the potential to become beneficial tools alongside current harm reduction initiatives.

Prompt: Does the wheel filter reduce the amount of drug injected?

Yes, using a wheel filter can slightly reduce the amount of drug that ends up being injected—but this is generally very minimal and is far outweighed by the harm reduction benefits.

🔍 Why some drug may be lost in a wheel filter:

Retention volume
○Wheel filters hold a tiny amount of liquid inside (usually 0.1–0.3 mL depending on the filter size and type).○This bit of solution can remain trapped and not be pushed through.

Filter material absorption
○A very small amount of the drug might get absorbed into the membrane itself.○This loss is usually negligible for most substances unless the dose is already very small.

Clogging
○If the solution contains lots of undissolved material or is thick, it can clog the filter, leading to higher loss or no flow at all.

Prompt: How to use a wheel filter for injecting drugs?

Please note: This information is provided for harm reduction purposes only. The safest way to avoid the risks associated with injecting drugs is to not inject them at all.

Using a wheel filter (also known as a syringe filter or pill filter) can significantly reduce the harm associated with injecting drugs by removing impurities and bacteria from the solution before injection.1 These filters are much more effective than improvised filters like cotton wool or cigarette filters.

Here's a general guide on how to use a wheel filter for injecting drugs, compiled from harm reduction resources:

Equipment you will need:

Your drug: Prepared for mixing (e.g., crushed pill, powder)
Sterile water: Essential for dissolving the drug. Do not use tap water, as it can contain bacteria.
Sterile spoon or mixing cup: For dissolving and mixing.
Sterile cotton wool (optional, for pre‐filtering): To remove larger particles if the solution is very cloudy, which can help prevent the wheel filter from clogging.
Two sterile syringes: One for mixing and filtering (can be a larger volume syringe, for example, 3 or 5 mL), and one for injecting (your usual injection syringe, often 1 mL).
A new, sterile wheel filter: Get the correct pore size for your needs (see ‘Pore Size’ below).
A large gauge needle (for drawing up, optional but recommended): Can be used to draw the solution into the first syringe if needed.2

A new, sterile needle: For injecting.
Alcohol swabs: For cleaning surfaces, hands, and the injection site.3

Sharps container: For safe disposal of used needles and filters.

Steps to use a wheel filter:

Prepare your space and hands:
○Find a clean, well‐lit, private area.○Wash your hands thoroughly with soap and water, or use an alcohol swab.○Clean your mixing surface and spoon/cup with a new alcohol swab and let it dry.

Prepare your drug mix:
○Place your drug (e.g., finely crushed pill, powder) into the sterile spoon or mixing cup.○Add sterile water to dissolve the drug. The amount of water will depend on the drug and your preference, but generally use enough to ensure good dissolution.○Stir the mix thoroughly until the drug is as dissolved as possible. Do not heat the mix, especially if it's a pill containing wax, as heating can cause impurities to pass through the filter and solidify in your bloodstream. If you typically heat your drug, allow it to cool down completely before filtering.

Pre‐filter with cotton wool (optional but recommended for thick/cloudy solutions):
○Break off a small, clean piece of sterile cotton wool (from a fresh cotton ball or sterile tampon).○Place the cotton wool in your mix, ensuring it's submerged.○Draw the mix into your first sterile syringe (the larger one for filtering) through the cotton wool. This removes larger particles that could quickly clog your wheel filter.

Attach and prime the wheel filter:
○Carefully open the sterile packaging of the wheel filter. Do not touch the filter membrane.○Screw the wheel filter firmly onto the tip of the first syringe (the one with your unfiltered mix).○
Prime the filter: Draw 1–2 mL of sterile water into the syringe with the filter attached. Gently push this water through the filter into a waste container or even back into your mix (if it's a small amount and won't dilute your dose too much). This wets the membrane and helps ensure proper flow and removes any air.○Some sources suggest simply pushing a tiny bit of the drug solution through to prime it, but priming with sterile water prevents losing any of your drug if the filter is faulty or clogs.

Filter the drug solution:
○Take your second sterile syringe (your injecting syringe).○
Important: You will be pushing the solution from the first syringe through the wheel filter into the second syringe. You can do this by:
Attaching a large gauge needle to the outlet of the wheel filter and inserting this needle into the tip of the second syringe barrel (where you would normally screw on a needle).4 This creates a closed system.Alternatively, you can simply hold the outlet of the wheel filter directly against the tip of the second syringe. Be careful to maintain a good seal.
○Slowly and steadily push the plunger of the first syringe. The filtered solution will be pushed through the wheel filter and into the second syringe.5
○Push until all the liquid has passed through. You can push a little bit of air through at the end to ensure you get all the filtered solution.○You will see impurities, particles, and potentially bacteria trapped on the filter membrane.

Prepare for injection:
○Remove the first syringe and wheel filter.○Attach your new, sterile injecting needle to the second syringe (the one now containing your filtered solution).6
○Remove any air bubbles from the syringe by tapping it and gently pushing the plunger until a drop of liquid appears at the needle tip.

Inject and dispose:
○Follow your usual safe injecting practices (e.g., clean injection site, register).○
Immediately dispose of all used equipment (first syringe, wheel filter, needles) into a sharps container. Wheel filters are single‐use only. Reusing them can reintroduce contaminants and bacteria.

Why use a wheel filter? (harm reduction benefits):

Removes impurities: Pills contain binders, fillers, and other insoluble substances (like chalk, wax, starch, talc, plastics) that are not meant to be injected.7 Injecting these can cause serious damage to veins, lead to abscesses, infections, blood clots, and damage to organs like the lungs, heart, and brain.
Filters out bacteria and fungi: Even solutions that look clean can contain harmful microorganisms.8 Wheel filters with small pore sizes (especially 0.2/0.22 μm) can remove most bacteria and fungi, significantly reducing the risk of serious infections like endocarditis (heart valve infection), sepsis (blood poisoning), and cellulitis.9

Reduces vein damage: By removing particles, wheel filters help prevent blockages and damage to veins, preserving injection sites.10

More effective than improvised filters: Cotton wool or cigarette filters are not designed for this purpose and are far less effective at removing small particles and bacteria. They can also shed fibres that can be injected.

Important notes on pore size:

0.8 μm (often red or green): Good for removing larger particles and general impurities.
0.45 μm (often yellow): Removes most particles and some bacteria.
0.2/0.22 μm (often blue): Considered ‘sterilising‐grade’ and is the most effective at removing bacteria and fungi. This is generally recommended as the final filter if possible, especially for watery solutions.Some harm reduction services suggest using a coarser filter (like 0.8 μm) first, especially for thicker or very cloudy solutions, followed by a finer filter (like 0.22 μm) for optimal purification.

Where to get wheel filters:

Wheel filters are typically available at Needle and Syringe Programs (NSPs) or harm reduction services.11 These services can also provide sterile injecting equipment, sterile water, and valuable advice on safer injecting practices and disposal.12

Again, please remember: No injection is 100% safe. This information aims to reduce potential harms for those who choose to inject drugs.

Prompt: Does the wheel filter reduce the amount of drug injected?

This is a very important question in harm reduction, and the good news is that wheel filters are designed to minimise the loss of the active drug while removing harmful impurities.
1

Here's a breakdown:

How filters work: Wheel filters act like a sieve. They have a membrane with microscopic pores. These pores are large enough for dissolved drug molecules to pass through, but too small for insoluble particles (like binders, fillers, talc, chalk, or other cutting agents) and most bacteria/fungi.
Drug solubility is key: Most injectable drugs are water‐soluble. This means they dissolve completely in water, forming a true solution. When a drug is truly dissolved, its molecules are dispersed individually within the water and are small enough to easily pass through the filter's pores.
What gets retained: What the filter does retain are the solid, undissolved particles that could otherwise clog veins, cause infections, or damage organs. These are the impurities you want to remove.
Minimal drug loss (if used correctly): While there might be a very small, negligible amount of drug retained in the filter membrane or the ‘dead space’ of the filter housing, it's generally considered to be insignificant, especially when compared to the vast reduction in harm. Harm reduction organisations often state that the filters clean up the solution without removing any of the drugs.
Maximising recovery: To ensure you get as much of the dissolved drug as possible, harm reduction guidelines often recommend:
○
Priming: Pushing a small amount of sterile water through the filter first can help wet the membrane and prevent air locks, ensuring better flow of your drug solution.○
Pushing air through at the end: After filtering the solution, you can push a small amount of air through the filter with the plunger to force out any remaining liquid in the filter housing, maximising the amount of filtered drug collected.2

In summary:

The primary purpose and design of wheel filters are to separate the dissolved active drug from harmful insoluble contaminants and microorganisms.3 When used correctly, they effectively remove the unwanted substances while allowing the vast majority of the drug to pass through, ensuring that the user still receives their intended dose but in a much safer form.

The small amount of drug that might be retained is a small trade‐off for the significant reduction in health risks associated with injecting impurities.

Prompt: Should drugs be heated beforehand?

This is a crucial question with mixed advice, and it's important to understand the nuances, especially when using a wheel filter.

General recommendation: avoid heating, especially with pills, if using a wheel filter.

Here's why:

Melting of insoluble impurities (Pills/Tablets):
○Many pills and tablets contain waxes, binders, fillers, and other excipients (inactive ingredients) that are insoluble in cold water.1
○When these are heated, some of these insoluble impurities can melt and dissolve into the hot solution.○Once the solution cools down in your syringe or bloodstream, these melted impurities can re‐solidify into larger particles. These particles can be more dangerous than the original undissolved particles because they may have passed through the filter when dissolved, only to then clump up in your body, leading to:

Vein damage: Blocking and irritating veins.
Embolisms: Particles travelling to and blocking small blood vessels in the lungs, heart, or brain, causing serious and potentially fatal damage.
Organ damage: Especially to the lungs, leading to conditions like ‘pill lung.’

Risk of new contaminants:
○Heating an impure solution can sometimes create new chemical compounds that are harmful.○Heating with non‐sterile equipment can also introduce more bacteria.2

No significant increase in drug extraction (for most drugs):
○Research indicates that for many drugs, especially when coming from pills, heating does not significantly increase the amount of active drug extracted. The active drug is usually water‐soluble at room temperature.

Wheel filters are designed for cold filtration:
○Wheel filters are highly effective at removing insoluble particles and bacteria from cold solutions. Their effectiveness can be compromised if you are introducing melted waxes or other substances that might pass through hot and then solidify cold.

When heating might be considered (and its limitations):

There are some specific scenarios or historical practices where heating has been used, but these often come with significant caveats:

Some specific powdered drugs: Certain powdered drugs (e.g., some forms of heroin, crack cocaine with an acidifier) might dissolve better with gentle heating. However, this is drug‐specific, and many harm reduction resources advise against heating cocaine due to clotting risks.
Sterilisation of washes (re‐using equipment): In the past, or in situations where equipment is being re‐used (a practice strongly discouraged due to high infection risk), heating a ‘wash’ (water added to used equipment to extract residual drug) has been promoted as a way to reduce HIV and some bacterial loads. However, this is a very high‐risk practice and does NOT eliminate all bacteria or viruses (like Hepatitis C), nor does it negate the risks of injecting impurities from previously used equipment. The safest approach is always to use new, sterile equipment for every injection.
Dissolving difficult drugs: If a drug is proving extremely difficult to dissolve in cold sterile water, some people might briefly apply minimal heat. However, this should be a last resort and understood as increasing other risks.

Harm reduction best practice with wheel filters:

Crush pills very finely: This increases the surface area and helps the drug dissolve in cold water.
Use sterile cold water: This is paramount.
Stir thoroughly: Allow time for the drug to dissolve completely.
Consider pre‐filtering: If the solution is very cloudy, a simple cotton filter (sterile cotton wool) first can remove larger particles that might quickly clog the finer wheel filter.
Filter with a wheel filter (0.22 or 0.45 μm): This is the most effective step for removing fine particles and bacteria.
Avoid heating pills/tablets: Especially if you are going to filter, as this can introduce more dangerous, resolidifying impurities into your bloodstream.

In summary, for most drugs, and especially for pills or tablets, heating the solution before filtering with a wheel filter is generally discouraged by harm reduction experts because it can dissolve impurities that will later solidify in the bloodstream, increasing risks and does not significantly improve drug extraction. The focus should be on thorough dissolution in cold sterile water and effective filtration.

Prompt: Can a wheel filter stop hepatitis C and HIV?

No, a wheel filter (syringe filter) cannot reliably stop viruses like Hepatitis C (HCV) and HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus).

Here's why:

Pore size versus virus size:
○Even the finest wheel filters commonly used in harm reduction have a pore size of 0.22 micrometres (μm), or 220 nanometres (nm). These are excellent at filtering out bacteria (which are typically larger, around 0.5–5 μm).○However, viruses are significantly smaller.1

Hepatitis C Virus (HCV): Approximately 50–60 nm in diameter.2

HIV: Approximately 80–120 nm in diameter.
○As you can see, both HCV and HIV are much smaller than the 220 nm pores of even the best wheel filters. They can easily pass through the filter membrane.

Sharing risk:
○This is why harm reduction messages consistently emphasise that no filter will stop blood‐borne viruses like Hepatitis C and HIV.○The primary way these viruses are transmitted among people who inject drugs is through the sharing of contaminated injecting equipment, including syringes, needles, cookers, and even filters themselves (if they have blood residue on them).3

What wheel filters do help with:

While they don't stop viruses, wheel filters are still an important harm reduction tool because they significantly reduce other serious risks:4

Bacteria and fungi: They are highly effective at removing most bacteria and fungi, which can cause severe infections like abscesses, cellulitis, endocarditis (heart valve infection), and sepsis (blood poisoning).
Insoluble particles: They remove chalk, talc, waxes, binders, and other insoluble fillers found in pills and street drugs. Injecting these can lead to vein damage, blockages (embolisms) in the lungs, heart, and brain, and long‐term organ damage (e.g., ‘pill lung’).

The most crucial prevention for HIV and HCV transmission for people who inject drugs is to:

Always use a new, sterile needle and syringe for every injection.
Never share any injecting equipment, including syringes, needles, cookers, cottons, water, or filters.5
Use sterile water to dissolve drugs.

While wheel filters dramatically improve the purity of the solution and reduce many harms, they do not provide protection against viral transmission.

Prompt: I don't have a wheel filter. Can I use cotton wool or a cigarette filter?

I understand you don't have a wheel filter right now and are looking for alternatives like cotton wool or a cigarette filter. It's really important to address this directly:

Risks of using cotton wool or cigarette filters:

Ineffective filtration:
○
Pore size: Their ‘pores’ are highly irregular and much larger than a wheel filter's. They are very poor at removing fine particles, talc, chalk, waxes, binders, and especially bacteria and fungi.○

Contamination:
○
Non‐sterile: Cotton balls, cotton swabs, and cigarette filters are not sterile. They can introduce bacteria, fungi, and other contaminants directly into your solution, increasing the risk of infections like abscesses, cellulitis, endocarditis, and sepsis.1
○
Chemicals in cigarette filters: Cigarette filters are designed to filter smoke, not liquids for injection. They can contain chemicals, fibreglass, or other synthetic materials that are dangerous if injected.

No protection against viruses:
○Just like wheel filters, neither cotton nor cigarette filters will protect against blood‐borne viruses like HIV or Hepatitis C. These viruses are far too small to be filtered out by these materials.

If you must use an improvised filter (and this is a last resort):

If you absolutely have no other option and are making the choice to inject without a wheel filter, and you are using cotton or a cigarette filter:

Avoid cigarette filters if possible. If you must use one, use the very tip, and never the paper part.
Use a very small piece. Too much cotton will absorb your solution and make it harder to draw up.

Prompt: If I use a wheel filter, is it safe to inject MS contin?

It's crucial to understand that injecting MS contin (or any pill designed for oral use) carries significant risks, even with the use of a wheel filter. While a wheel filter can reduce some of the harms, it does not make injecting MS Contin safe.

MS Contin is a long‐acting opioid in tablet form, designed to release its active ingredient (morphine) slowly over time when swallowed.1 When it's crushed and injected, several major dangers arise:

Why Injecting MS contin is inherently dangerous (even with a filter):

‘Dose dumping’/overdose risk:
○Crushing MS Contin defeats its extended‐release mechanism. This means the entire dose of morphine is released all at once, leading to a much higher concentration of the drug hitting your system very rapidly.○This dramatically increases the risk of overdose, which can cause severe respiratory depression (slowed or stopped breathing), coma, and death. This is the most immediate and life‐threatening risk.

Harmful insoluble fillers and binders:
○MS contin tablets contain a significant amount of inactive ingredients (excipients) like waxes, talc, cellulose, starch, and other binders/fillers.2 These are designed to be ingested and processed by the digestive system, not directly injected into the bloodstream.○When dissolved in water, many of these ingredients do not fully dissolve and remain as fine particles or form a gel‐like substance.

‘Pill lung’ and organ damage (even with a filter):
○While a wheel filter is excellent at removing many of these particles, some microscopic particles can still pass through, especially if the filter clogs or is improperly used.3
○If you heat the mixture (which is generally advised against for pills, as mentioned previously), some waxes and binders can melt and pass through the filter, only to re‐solidify in your bloodstream.○These injected particles can lodge in the tiny capillaries of the lungs, heart, brain, and other organs.4 This can cause:

Pulmonary granulomatosis/‘Pill Lung’: A serious lung condition where foreign particles cause inflammation, scarring, and damage to lung tissue, leading to severe breathing difficulties, pulmonary hypertension, and potentially death.
Embolisms: Blockages of blood vessels in any part of the body (lungs, brain, eyes, fingers, toes), which can lead to tissue death (necrosis), stroke, blindness, or amputation.
Damage to heart valves (endocarditis): Even with filtration, the introduction of foreign material can contribute to infections of the heart valves.

Infection risk (reduced, but not eliminated by filter):
○While a 0.22 μm wheel filter can remove most bacteria, the overall process of injecting street‐prepared drugs (even prescription pills diverted for injection) introduces numerous opportunities for contamination from hands, surfaces, water, and the drug itself.○Abscesses, cellulitis, and serious systemic infections (like sepsis) are still significant risks.

Vein damage:
○Even with filtration, the repeated injection of foreign substances and the drug itself can cause significant damage to veins, leading to scarring, collapse, and difficulty finding injection sites.

No. A wheel filter significantly reduces
