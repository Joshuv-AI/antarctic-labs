 TRANSFORMING CRITICAL
 MINERAL RECOVERY
 WITH ALTA RESOURCE
 TECHNOLOGIES

 Rare-earth elements (REEs) are essential for many electronic, energy
 and advanced defense technologies, and are required for key national
 defense systems as well as permanent magnets in cell phones.
 However, most of the mining and processing of REEs happens in
 China, and establishing a domestic supply chain is a priority.

 A research collaboration between Lawrence Livermore National
 Laboratory (LLNL) and Pennsylvania State University (Penn State) has
 generated a portfolio of intellectual property (IP), jointly owned by
 both organizations, that uses bacterial proteins to pick out critical
 metal ions. In FY25, LLNL licensed its interest in the joint IP to Alta
 Resource Technologies (Alta) for commercialization of the resulting
 technology to transform mineral separation.
 Image Credit: Garry McLeod/LLNL Alta Resource Technologies plans to develop the technology at scale
 — raising $5.1 million in seed funding in addition to nearly $1 million
 in grant funding from the federal government, including the
 Department of Defense’s Defense Advanced Research Projects
 Agency (DARPA) as of FY25. The result could decrease the footprint
 of mining and is an example of how LLNL’s mission-based work can
 impact U.S. economic competitiveness through technology transfer.

 BRIDGEBIO ONCOLOGY AND LLNL:
 ADVANCING CANCER TREATMENT
 WITH AI AND SUPERCOMPUTING
 In June 2025, a new cancer drug candidate developed by Lawrence
 Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL), BBOT (BridgeBio Oncology
 Therapeutics) and the Frederick National Laboratory for Cancer
 Research (FNLCR) demonstrated the ability to block tumor growth
 without triggering a common and debilitating side effect.

 In early clinical trials, the compound, known as BBO-10203, shown
 promise in disrupting a key interaction between two cancer-driving
 proteins — RAS and PI3Kα — without causing hyperglycemia (high
 blood-sugar levels), which has historically hindered similar
 treatments. Published in Science, the findings marked a major
 milestone for the collaboration, offering a potential breakthrough
 for patients with aggressive, treatment-resistant cancers.

 The discovery of BBO-10203 brought together Department of Energy
 (DOE) high-performance computing with artificial intelligence (AI) and
 biomedical expertise to accelerate drug discovery. LLNL leveraged its Graphic by Amanda Levasseur/LLNL
 Livermore Computer-Aided Drug Design (LCADD) platform —
 combining AI and machine learning with physics-based modeling —
 and world-class DOE supercomputing resources to simulate and
 predict drug behavior long before any compound is synthesized.

 LLNL AND BIOVIND
 ACHIEVE DIAGNOSTICS
 MILESTONE

 Technology designed to aid on-the-field diagnostics for military
 applications gained a wider reach in FY25 as Lawrence Livermore
 National Laboratory (LLNL) and BioVind, LLC achieved a milestone
 in their partnership: the exclusive licensing of LLNL pathogen
 diagnostics technology focused on oil and gas applications.

 The technology, called BioID, is a rapid and portable molecular
 diagnostics platform that can detect up to 18 target DNA or RNA
 sequences from a single sample in 30 to 60 minutes. BioID uses an
 isothermal amplification technique to detect pathogen nucleic acid.

 BioID is the result of more than 10 years of research, led by Larry
 Dugan, a biomedical scientist in the Biosciences and Biotechnology
 division, whose team developed technology that enables quick
 identification of pathogens such as bacteria, viruses and fungi that
 impact humans, animals and the environment.

 Pictured: BioID device instrument and consumables
 BioVind has focused on testing and developing a commercial
 application for BioID to detect corrosion and biofouling-associated
 microbes found in the oil and gas sector, such as in gas pipelines and
 storage facilities. Unlike other licensees that are only partially based
 on LLNL-developed technology, BioVind is unique in that it’s based
 exclusively on technology licensed from the Laboratory.

 LLNL AND CANAERY DEVELOP
 CUTTING-EDGE NEURAL
 INTERFACE TECHNOLOGY
 Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) and neurotechnology
 company Canaery have developed an advanced nose-computer
 interface (NCI) capable of enhancing the ability of scent-detection
 animals to simultaneously identify contraband such as explosives
 and narcotics, as well as other types of important scents such as
 biomarkers for neurological and infectious diseases.

 The team brought LLNL’s longstanding expertise in fabrication of
 flexible neural interfaces and biocompatible packaging together
 with Canaery’s transformative work on a novel NCI that could provide
 human-readable identification of scents detected by animals. The
 effort extended LLNL’s manufacturing capabilities as the Laboratory’s
 first neural interface created under a new high-density
 nanofabrication process, utilizing electron beam lithography capable
 of patterning nanometer-scale features.

 STARRIS: OPTIMAX SCALES
 LLNL MONOLITHIC TELESCOPE
 TECHNOLOGY FOR SPACE
 In August 2025, Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) and
 Starris: Optimax Space Systems signed a Cooperative Research and

 Starris will serve as the manufacturing partner that can scale

 VERNE AND LLNL
 DEMONSTRATE NOVEL
 HYDROGEN ENERGY
 PATHWAY

 In FY25, Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) and Verne,
 a San Francisco-based startup, demonstrated a novel pathway for
 creating high-density hydrogen through a research program funded
 by the Department of Energy’s ARPA-E.

 The demonstration validated that it is possible to efficiently reach
 cryo-compressed hydrogen conditions with liquid hydrogen-like
 density directly from a source of gaseous hydrogen; substantially
 reducing the energy input required, compared to methods that rely
 on energy-intensive hydrogen liquefaction.

 A variety of sectors are seeking increased electrical power and are
 not currently being served by the grid. Hydrogen can be delivered to
 these sectors and converted to electricity on-site through a fuel cell,
 engine or turbine. However, the cost of existing hydrogen distribution
 technologies has limited adoption. The novel hydrogen densification
 pathway that LLNL and Verne demonstrated produces cryo-
 compressed hydrogen without requiring a phase change, leading to
 50 percent energy savings relative to small-scale hydrogen
 liquefaction. This densification pathway is also more modular so the
 hydrogen distribution network can be further optimized; locating
 densification and distribution hubs closer to the points of use.
