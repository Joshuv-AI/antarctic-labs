Executive Summary
Objectives and Approach
Residential wood heating lacks real-time particulate matter (PM) monitoring technologies
capable of direct flue gas measurements that could support automatic controls. Acumentor LLC
developed "Octoring," an opacity-based sensor designed to monitor smoke and provide user
feedback. In this project, we evaluate its measurement accuracy, repeatability, and suitability for
automated combustion control. An Octoring unit was tested across non-catalytic cordwood,
catalytic cordwood, and pellet heaters during startup, high-burn, and low-burn phases.
Performance was evaluated against reference particle instruments and gravimetric filters using
Lin's Concordance Correlation Coefficient (CCC), Ordinary Least Squares correlation (R²), and
Bland-Altman analysis. 24-hour ambient tests under zero-PM conditions isolated temperature-
dependent drift from actual PM signals.

Key Findings
This study identified temperature-dependent drift as the dominant error source limiting opacity-
based PM sensor performance. 24-hour ambient testing was used to quantify the systematic
drift of 0.09-0.27 opacity units/°C over ambient conditions (15-40°C). During combustion
measurements, sensor temperatures reached 80°C, well beyond calibration ranges.
Performance varied predictably with concentration range and thermal conditions. Across all
tests, concordance ranged from very poor to good (CCC: 0.05-0.92, R²: 0.08-0.96), with poor
repeatability even within the same sensor unit. The device demonstrated its best performance
during a pellet heater high-burn, achieving good agreement with reference instruments (CCC =
0.92, R² = 0.84). Sensor-to-sensor variability was severe, including a 74-fold difference in bias
magnitude with opposite bias directions between units. Hardware limitations included
photodetector temperature sensitivity, absent automated zeroing, and durability concerns (two
sensors failed at <100 hours).

Conclusions and Recommendations
This research establishes the first comprehensive performance baseline for the Octoring, an
opacity-based PM monitoring system for residential wood combustion and provides a clear
roadmap for achieving commercial viability. While the device demonstrated proof-of-concept
capability, occasionally achieving reference-grade concordance (CCC = 0.92), further
development is needed before commercialization. Specifically, the critical development priorities
identified by this research are as follows:
 • Robust photodetector – Upgrade photodetector with lower temperature sensitivity
 sensors, integrated zeroing, and >1000-hour durability at operational temperatures.
 • Thermal management - Implement temperature-controlled housing or develop validated
 correction algorithms across 15 to 90°C operational range through systematic thermal
 characterization.

• Reference cell baseline stability - Incorporate dual-beam design for automated zeroing
 and drift monitoring to establish consistent measurement baselines.
• Optimized light source - Evaluate multiple wavelengths to balance sensitivity and
 stability; verify LED output remains constant across operational temperature range.
• Automated calibration - Integrate calibration coefficients into firmware with built-in
 validation routines to ensure accuracy and detect degradation.
• Operational durability - Redesign enclosure to prevent optical path obstruction (insects,
 debris, condensation); validate durability through >1000-hour field testing under realistic
 weather conditions.

1. Introduction
If operated poorly, wood burning heaters can produce large amounts of particulate matter (PM).
While several instruments are available to monitor PM, most are unable to sample directly from
a flue due to the high PM concentrations and temperatures. This limitation creates a need for
robust, real-time monitoring technologies capable of operating in undiluted combustion
environments to enable performance assessment and automated combustion control.

Opacity-based sensors offer a promising approach for direct smoke measurement from wood
heater stacks that can support combustion control. These sensors operate on the principle of
light extinction, transmitting a beam through the smoke source and measuring the reduction in
transmitted light intensity. Although opacity sensors have been widely applied in industrial stack
monitoring and some diesel exhaust applications, none have been thoroughly evaluated or
optimized for residential wood combustion smoke [1–5]. Wood smoke presents unique
challenges including highly variable particle characteristics (size distribution, composition,
morphology), transient combustion conditions across different burn phases (startup, high-burn,
burnout), and significant temperature fluctuations affecting both the sample and sensor
hardware [6].

The goal of this research is to provide a comprehensive evaluation of an opacity sensor for
direct, undiluted, high-temperature wood smoke measurement. This report presents results from
an 18-month collaborative research project to optimize and validate the "Octoring," an opacity-
based smoke sensing technology developed by Acumentor LLC for residential wood heater
applications. The primary objectives were to: (1) characterize Octoring measurement accuracy
and repeatability across diverse wood heater types and operating conditions, (2) identify
sources of measurement error and uncertainty, and (3) assess the device's suitability for real-
time PM monitoring and potential integration into automated combustion control systems.

Over the course of the project, an Octoring unit was used to measure smoke from a non-
catalytic cordwood, catalytic cordwood, and pellet heater across startup, high-burn, and low-
burn combustion phases. Device performance was assessed by comparing Octoring
measurements to reference-grade particle instruments and gravimetric filter samples using
concordance analysis, correlation metrics, and Bland-Altman agreement methods. Temperature-
dependent drift was characterized through 24-hour ambient tests to isolate sensor artifacts from
actual PM signals.

2. Methods
2.1 Smokeless Chimney Octoring Opacity Monitor
To assess the reliability of opacity sensors as a means of quantifying PM concentration from
heaters, Acumentor LLC supplied a smoke-monitoring device called an Octoring. The Octoring
(see Figure 2.1) is a chimney-mounted transmissometer designed for continuous smoke
opacity monitoring; it is an octagonal prism that is 8-inches (203 mm) tall and has a central
opening for unobstructed exhaust-gas flow that is 6-inches (152 mm) in diameter. On the inner
wall of the central opening are two 0.248 inch (6.3 mm) diameter holes positioned directly
across from each other; an ams-OSRAM TCS34725 RGB color sensor (with IR filter and
integrated white LED) is mounted behind one hole, while a 3 watt, 20mm PCB, 3535 XPE style
530 nm narrow-band green light source
(https://www.aliexpress.us/item/2255800880304876.html) is mounted behind the opposite hole
[7]. The pathlength between the sensor and the light source is approximately 5.94 inches (151
mm). This configuration enables continuous, real-time measurement of smoke opacity by
detecting light attenuation as exhaust passes through the optical path. The Octoring uses a 12
VDC/<1 amp power supply and wirelessly transmits measured data (up to 20 channels) at 1
Hz, with a range of 82 feet (25 meters). For this project, a K-type thermocouple was added to
monitor flue gas, and a T-type surface thermocouple was added to monitor sensor temperature.

The experiments and analysis described in this report incorporated the following subset of the
Octoring’s available data channels:
 • Red, Blue, Green, and Clear counts: Digital response (0 to 65535) from each of the 4
 color channels on the TCS34725 color sensor.
 • Lux: Luminous flux (in units of lux) on the TCS34725 color sensor, calculated with an
 empirical equation supplied by the sensor manufacturer.
 • Opacity: Reduction in lux (in units of percent), referenced to ambient conditions just
 before sampling wood heater smoke.

2.2 Wood Heaters Evaluated
Experiments were conducted with three wood heaters shown in Figure 2.2: a catalytic
cordwood heater (Blaze King Sirocco SC20.2), a non-catalytic cordwood heater (Quadra-Fire
Millennium 4300), and a pellet heater (Regency Greenfire GF55). The catalytic cordwood
heater has a 1.8 cu. ft (0.051 cu. m) firebox and was acquired new in 2023. The non-catalytic
cordwood heater has a 2.3 cu. ft (0.065 cu. m) firebox and was obtained used from the
manufacturer following quality control testing in 2025. The pellet heater was purchased new in
2024. Prior to use in these experiments, each heater was pre-conditioned by undergoing at
least 48-hours of operation at medium-to-high burn rates.

Chimneys for the catalytic and non-catalytic cordwood heaters were 6 inches in diameter
(internal) and comprised 65 inches (1.65 m) of single-wall stove pipe and 59 inches (1.5 m) of
1-inch (2.54 cm) double-wall insulated pipe. The overall height of the catalytic cordwood heater
and chimney measured from the top of the scale to the top of the Octoring was approximately
170.875 inches (4.34 m). The overall height of the non-catalytic cordwood heater and chimney
measured from the top of the scale to the top of the Octoring was approximately 166.5 inches
(4.23 m). The Octoring added an additional 12 inches (0.3 m) to the height of the chimneys.

The chimney for the pellet heater was 4 inches in diameter (internal) and comprised 66.5 inches
(1.7 m) of single-wall stove pipe and 78.75 inches (2 m) of 1-inch (2.54 cm) double-wall insulated
pipe. Due to the rear-facing exhaust port, the single-wall chimney was routed upwards using a
single-wall tee connector measuring 5 inches (12.7 cm) in height from the exhaust. The overall
height of the pellet heater and chimney measured from the top of the scale to the top of the
Octoring was 183.625 inches (4.7 m). An adapter that expands the chimney diameter from 4-inches
(10.16 cm) to 6-inches (15.24 cm) was added to the Octoring, increasing the pellet heater chimney
height by an additional 13.125 inches (0.33 m).

2.3 Fuel Specifications
2.3.1 Cordwood fuel composition and moisture content
All cordwood experiments used Douglas fir firewood purchased from All Seasons Firewood in
Santa Rosa, CA, in October 2023. This firewood was sourced from a single tree and seasoned
for at least one year before being cut into logs. After purchase, the firewood was stored on
shelves in the laboratory and further seasoned for an additional two years before use in these
experiments. The firewood arrived with little bark, and any remaining bark fell off during
handling and transportation, so no bark was present in any cordwood test fuel charges. Results
from a fuel analysis conducted in August 2024 by ALS Environmental (Tucson, AZ) are
summarized in Table 2.2.

Moisture content was measured using a Delmhorst BD-2100 meter equipped with default 5/16
inch (7.9 mm) uninsulated pins (see Figure 2.3) [8]. The meter, which measures dry-basis
moisture content in a range of 6–40%, required no correction when used with Douglas fir and
the default pins. The moisture content of the paper knots was not measured or controlled but
was confirmed to be dry to the touch. Samples for moisture measurement were taken at three
points along the length of each small and large wood piece, and three representative pieces of
kindling were similarly tested.

If the wood was chopped more than one week in advance of testing, moisture content was
rechecked using insulated pins (26Delmhorst model 1-E) that penetrate deeper than the default
5/16-inch (7.9 mm) depth (see Figure 2.3). These insulated pins were hammered into the wood
with a rubber mallet, and the readings were taken at depth. Because insulated pins yield
slightly lower readings than the actual value, a correction factor was applied based on a linear
fit to the manufacturer’s correction table (given by y = 1.0926x - 0.368 with R² = 0.9998) [8]. All
measured wood was stored in air-tight plastic bags after moisture testing until no more than
one hour before use. Table 2.3 summarizes the average moisture content of the prepared fuel.

2.3.3 Cordwood fuel preparation
Cordwood logs were approximately 16 inches long with varying diameters and split to size prior
to each experiment. Test fuel charges for each experiment included four components: (1)
crumpled pieces of butcher paper tied into loose knots (“paper knots”), (2) thinly-cut sticks of
Douglas fir cordwood (“kindling sticks”), (3) small Douglas fir cordwood logs (“small logs”), and
(4) large Douglas Fir cordwood logs (“large logs”). Figure 2.4 and Table 2.5 provide images and
a summary of test fuel components. Each component of the test fuel was prepared as follows:

 1. Paper knots: Undyed, unbleached butcher paper, was cut into 6-inch by 18-inch strips,
 weighing approximately 0.18 to 0.23 oz (5 to 6.5 g) each. Each strip was crumpled
 along its length and tied into a loose overhand knot. The moisture content of the starter
 paper was neither tracked nor controlled, but was it was dry to the touch prior to use.

 2. Kindling sticks: Up to 20 sticks were chopped from Douglas Fir cordwood logs for
 each experiment. The diameter of each stick was approximately 0.5 inches (12.7 mm),
 and the length was within 10% of the log length, 16 inches (406.4 mm). Moisture
 content was measured from three randomly selected sticks, and the average
 measurement was recorded.

 3. Small logs: Three to four small logs were chopped from Douglas Fir cordwood to meet
 a specified total fuel weight for the heater type (see Table 2.5). The logs lengths were
       within 10% of 16 inches (406.4 mm). Each log weighed between 0.46-1.5 lbs. (0.21-
 0.70 kg) and was approximately 2 to 3 inches (50.8 to 76.2 mm) in diameter. Shape
 (e.g., triangular or rectangular) of the logs was not controlled. Logs did not contain

2.4 Experimental Procedure
Experiments were conducted to evaluate the accuracy of the Octoring for three operating
phases and across three different heater types (see Table 2.6). The three operating phases
included cold startup, high-burn, and low-burn, and are defined as follows:

Cold startup: Ignition from room temperature (i.e., cold condition) through the establishment of
a robust fire inside the firebox.
 • Cordwood heaters: Includes kindling and paper knots; catalyst was bypassed (not
 engaged).
 • Pellet heater: The first 10-minutes of operation.

High-burn: Operation for at least 1-hr with controls adjusted to maximum heat settings; follows
cold startup or another high-burn.
 • Cordwood heaters: Includes addition of fuel to a well-established fire or bed of hot coals
 in the firebox. The catalyst is engaged.
 • Pellet heater: 1-hr of operation at the highest heat level.

Low-burn: Operation for at least 2-hrs with controls set to minimum or near-minimum heat
settings; follows a high-burn and no additional fuel is added for cordwood heaters.

Detailed heater testing procedures for each test phase are provided in the following
subsections.

2.4.1 Cordwood heater test procedure
At the start of each test day, the heater firebox was emptied and vacuumed. The heater was
not operated for at least 8-hours prior to testing to ensure the heater and chimney started at
room temperature. Air controls were set to maximize burn rate in accordance with the user
manuals (see Figure 2.5). For the catalytic cordwood heater, the bypass was set to “open” and
the catalyst was disengaged.

All prepared paper knots and kindling sticks were loaded into the firebox, with the kindling
arranged in a crisscrossed pattern and the paper knots evenly distributed throughout the
firebox. For the non-catalytic cordwood heater, two small logs were placed beneath the paper
and kindling to fill the larger firebox (see Figure 2.6). The fire was ignited using a butane lighter,
and the experiment began once three separate paper knots were lit (taking up to 1.4 minutes to
light).

The door remained cracked open during start-up and was closed when the kindling caught fire
(catalytic heater), or the kindling and two small logs caught fire (non-catalytic heater). Once the
paper knots and kindling were burning well, small logs were loaded into the firebox in a crossed
pattern (three for the catalytic heater and two for the non-catalytic heater). After the added
small logs began burning well, the door was closed or large logs were added to initiate a high-
burn phase. This startup procedure took approximately 10 minutes to complete.

The high-burn phase began when large logs were loaded into the firebox (a Type 1 or Type 2
charge, see Table 2.6) and the door was closed. Once the stove reached catalyst engagement

    Evaluation of a Smoke Opacity Sensor for Improving Residential Wood Heater Combustion | 15

temperature (see Figure 2.5b), the bypass was closed, and the catalyst was engaged (catalytic
heater only). If a Type 1 charge was used to initialize the high-burn, the phase ended after
about an hour of operation, and the test subsequently transitioned to the low-burn phase. If a
Type 2 charge was used to start the high-burn phase, the heater was operated for at least one
hour and then a reload charge of Type 2 (for the non-catalytic heater) or a Type 3 (for the
catalytic heater) was placed into the firebox and operated until fuel extinction (~2 to 2.25
hours). The door was immediately closed after the reload charge. Table 2.6 shows the fuel
charges for each loading event.

For the low-burn phase, the heater air control setting was set to the lowest or a lower position
after one-hour of operating on high. The non-catalytic heater was set to the ‘1’ position and the
Automatic combustion Control (ACC) was moved to the ‘front,’ following the instructions in the
user’s manual [10]. The catalytic heater air control dial was set to 60% to prevent the flame
from extinguishing. Figure 2.7 shows the control position for each cordwood heater. The low-

Po-210 static eliminator for up to 60 seconds, and then weighed on the ultramicrobalance to a
stable reading. Per ASTM E2515 requirements, filters were not exposed to the laboratory
environment for more than 2 minutes during this sequence. At least two replicate weight
measurements, spaced at least 60 minutes apart, were captured for each filter. If both weights
did not agree to within 0.0100 mg for post-test filters, and 0.0050 mg for pre-test filters, the filter
would be weighed at least one additional time.

2.6.3 Statistical analysis
Opacity and PM data collected at 1 Hz and averaged over 10-second intervals to account for
sampling time differences between stack-mounted Octoring and dilution-tunnel-sampled particle
instruments. Agreement between the Octoring and the PM instruments was assessed using
three complementary metrics: (1) Lin's Concordance Correlation Coefficient (CCC), which
quantifies both precision and accuracy relative to the line of perfect agreement (CCC >0.90
indicates acceptable agreement); (2) coefficient of determination (R²) from ordinary least
squares regression, which measures linear correlation strength; and (3) Bland-Altman analysis,
which evaluates systematic bias (median difference) and limits of agreement [12–16]. Because
the data was not normally distributed, limits of agreement were calculated as the 2.5th and
97.5th percentiles rather than mean ± 1.96×SD.

3. Results and Discussion
We evaluated the Octoring’s measurement drift and accuracy by measuring ambient opacity
and opacity of exhaust from three heater types: non-catalytic cordwood, catalytic cordwood, and
pellet. Opacity from the chimney was recorded for at least 24-hours when the heater was not
operating, and when each heater was operating over three different operating phases: start-up,
high-burn, and low-burn. Results from these experiments are discussed in the following
subsections.

3.1 Measurement Drift
Because the TCS34725 photodiode array is known to be temperature-sensitive, we investigated
the effects of sensor temperature on opacity and lux measurements [7]. Ambient opacity
measurements and calculated opacity (derived from lux) versus sensor temperature for two
different light sensors are shown in Figure 3.1 through Figure 3.4, along with linear trendlines
and corresponding slopes. Two sensors were evaluated because the first sensor failed partway
through the study. When the light sensor was replaced, the LED light source was preserved.

The results indicate that the Octoring's opacity and lux measurements suffer from hysteresis. As
shown in Figure 3.1 and Figure 3.2Figure 3.1, the temperature coefficient for Sensor 1 shifted
from 0.15% per °C to 0.09% per °C over a two-month period. The second experiment (Figure
3.2) shows significant hysteresis within the 24-hour test between 28°C and 38°C, resulting in an
overall lower slope.

For Sensor 2 (see Figure 3.3 and Figure 3.4), experiments conducted 2 months apart showed
more similar temperature coefficients (0.23 and 0.27 % per °C). However, during the second
experiment with Sensor 2 (Figure 3.4), we observed an abrupt negative shift of approximately
2% in opacity measurements. While the cause of this shift could not be determined, it was likely
not environmental since experimental conditions remained constant and subsequent
measurements continued following the same temperature slope at an offset of -2%. The
temperature coefficients for the pre-shift data and the post-shift data remained unchanged
(0.27% per °C).

The data also reveal discrepancies between opacity, which is determined by the firmware, and
opacity calculated from lux measurements (see Equation 2). For example, Figure 3.4 shows a
reported opacity near 2% at temperatures just above 20°C, while calculated opacity from lux
remains near 1%. This discrepancy suggests a potential calculation error in the firmware's
opacity algorithm. Although we attributed most of the hysteresis to photodetector temperature
sensitivity, the LED junction temperature remains a potential, unmeasured source of error. Due
to time constraints, this effect was not evaluated.

To address some of the hysteric behavior, we applied the linear temperature corrections
derived from to the ambient opacity measurements to wood heater experiments conducted
within the same week. Sensor temperatures during combustion measurements ranged from 40
to 80°C, substantially exceeding the 15 to 40°C range characterized during ambient testing.
Extrapolating temperature corrections across this temperature gap assumes linear sensor
response, which is inconsistent with the non-linear and hysteretic behavior observed within the
ambient temperature range (see Figure 3.1 through Figure 3.4). Consequently, our linear
temperature corrections do not capture the full magnitude of the error at operational conditions.

3.2 Measurement Accuracy and Repeatability

Octoring measurements were compared with laboratory-grade particle instruments (used as
reference) to assess device accuracy and repeatability across three combustion phases:
startup, high-burn, and low-burn. According to the Lambert-Beer law light intensity decreases
exponentially as smoke density increases [17]. Because opacity is a measure of light extinction,
it maintains a non-linear relationship with particulate concentration. Consequently, photodiode-
based sensors typically demonstrate the highest precision in the lower half of their range
(below 50% opacity), where the signal-to-noise ratio remains high. While the TCS34725
datasheet does not specify a maximum opacity threshold, industrial standards for dedicated
smoke monitors identify 80% opacity as the point where measurement uncertainty typically
exceeds acceptable limits [18]. As shown in the Appendix (Figure B-1), opacity measurements
in this study were primarily below 40% with a peak instantaneous measurement of 70%,
staying within a range likely characterized by sufficient light transmission for the detector.

Figure 3.5 through Figure 3.14 present time series comparisons of opacity, lux-derived opacity,
and reference PM concentrations for each heater type and operating phase. The top row of
figures show opacity, lux-derived opacity, and PM concentrations with test time. Parity plots
below each time series quantify agreement between the lux-calculated opacity and the PM
concentrations using Lin's Concordance Correlation Coefficient (CCC) and correlation using R²
from ordinary least squares regression. Bland-Altman plots, shown below the parity plots,
further quantify agreement the Octoring and the PM instruments. Due to firmware issues, we
compared the accuracy of the lux-calculated opacity with PM concentrations instead of using
the direct opacity measurement. Results and discussion evaluating the accuracy, repeatability,
and reliability of the Octoring for each operating phase are presented in the following
subsections.

3.2.1 Startup
During heater startup, the Octoring provided inconsistent measurements across the replicate
experiments. For the non-catalytic cordwood heater (Figure 3.5), the Octoring showed
moderate to poor agreement with the particle instruments across three startup tests using the
same sensor (Sensor 2). Concordance was consistently below the acceptable threshold (CCC:
0.26-0.75; threshold > 0.90), and correlation was weak to moderate (R²: 0.15-0.61). While the
Bland-Altman plots show median differences near zero, the consistent asymmetric fan shape
indicates persistent proportional bias, with Octoring accuracy decreasing as particle
concentrations increase. The limits of agreement varied substantially between experiments,
spanning 40-125 mg/m³, demonstrating poor test-to-test repeatability even with the same
sensor unit.

For the catalytic cordwood heater (Figure 3.6), the Octoring showed consistently poor
performance across all startup experiments (CCC: 0.14-0.27; R²: 0.42-0.64). These
measurements were collected before catalyst engagement, with Startups 1 and 2 using Sensor
1 and Startups 3 and 4 using Sensor 2. Both sensors performed inconsistently across the
replicate experiments. The Bland-Altman plots reveal severe proportional bias, with the
Octoring underestimating at higher concentrations across all four experiments. Additionally,
mean bias varied substantially between tests (8-25 mg/m³), indicating poor baseline stability
and day-to-day repeatability. This variable offset, combined with concentration-dependent
proportional bias, demonstrates that the device requires a stable calibration reference and
cannot be corrected with a single adjustment factor.

Results from the pellet heater startup phase (Figure 3.7) demonstrated critical limitations in
both repeatability and sensor-to-sensor consistency. Using Sensor 1, Test 1 achieved the best
performance observed across all startup experiments (CCC = 0.75, R² = 0.83) at low
concentrations (<10 mg/m³). However, Test 2 with the same sensor under similar conditions
showed substantially degraded performance (CCC = 0.42, R² = 0.54), demonstrating that even
the device's best-case performance cannot be reliably reproduced. Test 3, conducted with
Sensor 2, revealed extreme sensor-to-sensor variability, with severe negative bias (-37 mg/m³)
compared to Sensor 1's small positive bias (~0.5-0.8 mg/m³). Despite high correlation (R² =
0.91), Test 3 showed poor concordance (CCC = 0.38) with severe underestimation at higher
concentrations. This demonstrates why correlation (R²) alone is insufficient for assessing
measurement agreement. It should also be noted that odd sensor behavior was observed 30
minutes into Test 3, suggesting the sensor was beginning to fail.

3.2.3 Low-burn
Performance during low-burn phases (Figure 3.13 and Figure 3.14) showed poor concordance
(CCC: 0.17-0.75) at extremely low PM concentrations (0-3 mg/m³), with calculated opacity
ranging from 2-10%. At these concentration levels, measurements were likely dominated by
drift rather than actual PM signal. The non-catalytic cordwood heater displayed the highest R²
of 0.8 with a low concordance (CCC = 0.29), indicating systematic bias persists. The pellet
heater tests showed particularly poor performance (CCC: 0.25-0.28, R²: 0.08-0.14), with Bland-

    Evaluation of a Smoke Opacity Sensor for Improving Residential Wood Heater Combustion | 35

Altman plots revealing that measurement scatter exceeded the PM signal itself.

Notably, opacity never returned to 0% during the tail end (burnout) of the experiments and the
minimum observed value was 2.8%. This indicates a persistent positive offset likely attributed
to temperature or temporal drift and sensor baseline instability. The results demonstrate that
the device's useful lower detection limit is approximately 3-5 mg/m³, below which systematic
error may exceed the opacity being measured.

4. Conclusions and Recommendations
Evaluation of the Octoring opacity sensor across multiple residential wood heater types and
combustion phases revealed significant measurement limitations that prevent the device from
serving as a reliable PM monitoring instrument in its current state. The device showed highly
variable performance (Lin's CCC: 0.05-0.92, R²: 0.07-0.92) with poor repeatability between
sensors and heaters, even after applying a linear temperature correction for drift. Performance
was inconsistent across heaters, concentration ranges, and combustion phases. During the
startup and high-burn phases, the Octoring demonstrated significant proportional bias likely due
to accumulated temperature and temporal drift. It also severely underestimated (or missed)
several instantaneous increases in PM concentration. During the low-burn phases, the
proportional bias persisted and the baseline drift became more apparent as the minimum
opacity reached 2.8%, never returning to 0% like it was at the beginning of a testing day.

Ambient, 24-hr (zero-PM) testing identified temperature-dependent drift as a primary error
source, with opacity measurements changing 0.09-0.27 units/°C over the 15-40°C ambient
range. The observed hysteresis in temperature response indicates complex, path-dependent
thermal effects that may not be corrected with simple linear compensation. The hysteretic
behavior combined with baseline instability (non-zero startup values, occasional negative
readings), and lack of a zeroing function, limited measurement accuracy.

In addition to the systematic errors, the Octoring demonstrated some reliability issues as two
photodiode sensors failed after less than 100 operating hours. The sensor occasionally
reported an abrupt and negative shift in opacity measurements even though the current and
voltage supplied to the sensor remained constant.

Despite these challenges, this project demonstrated that opacity-based PM monitoring has
potential for residential wood heater applications, with the device occasionally achieving
acceptable performance (CCC up to 0.92) under favorable conditions at low concentrations.
Further, light sources and sensors were straightforward to exchange and firmware was easily
updated. Before deployment for automated combustion control applications, additional
advancements should be made to improve its repeatability, reliability, and durability.
Specifically, the Octoring should demonstrate consistent performance between tests,
predictable behavior across PM concentration ranges, operation over thousands of hours
without failure, and reliable identification of high-smoke events.

The insights gained from this work provide a roadmap for future development efforts, with
priority given to addressing temperature-dependent drift, improving sensor durability, and
developing robust calibration protocols. The following section provides more detailed
recommendations to support the Octoring with commercial development.

4.1 Recommendations
To improve accuracy, durability, and reliability of the Octoring, and advance it towards
commercialization, the following advances should be considered:

 • Alternative photodetectors should be considered that have lower temperature
 sensitivity, automated zeroing functions, and demonstrated durability with testing over

    Evaluation of a Smoke Opacity Sensor for Improving Residential Wood Heater Combustion | 38

 thousands of hours at higher temperatures.
• Implementing a dual-beam reference cell for automated zeroing at test start and
 periodically during operation would help establish consistent baselines.
• Active thermal management through sensor and LED housing should be considered to
 address both temperature-dependent and time-dependent drift. Alternatively, if a more
 reliable sensor is obtained, experiments could be conducted to develop correction
 algorithms for the full operational range (15-90°C).
• Streamlined calibration protocols with coefficients integrated into sensor software to
 reduce setup complexity and confirm accuracy before and after testing.
• Additional light sources that cover different wavelengths should be explored to identify
 the optimal wavelength(s) that balance signal stability and measurement sensitivity.
• Experiments should be conducted to evaluate the stability of the light source and
 confirm that the brightness is not impacted by temperature.
• Extended durability testing over thousands of hours under realistic operating and
 weather conditions to ensure commercial viability
• Design modifications should be considered to prevent obstruction of the light source by
 insects, dust, or other debris.
