 ABSTRACT

The quantification of 133Xe in the presence of its mother radionuclide 133mXe requires the full
quantification of both to perform the ingrowth correction for 133Xe. Due to the nature of both of these
radionuclides, the 133mXe requires significantly more time to quantify by High Purity Germanium (HPGe)
detectors due to lower production yields, lower gamma emission probabilities, and lower detection
efficiencies. This work shows that 133Xe and 133mXe quantification can be accelerated by measuring the
133m:133 activity ratio for a large batch of material and applying this activity ratio to assays of lower
activity subsamples of the same batch of material. Included in this report are derivations of the required
decay correction equations, and experiments using actual samples to validate the performance of these
equations. A detector calibration method is also shown that leverages this method as an alternative to
existing calibration methods for 133mXe quantification.

 INTRODUCTION
 Nuclear fission produces a complex array of fission products. In a nuclear detonation, elements that
are inert and have a poor affinity to other materials are released more easily than more reactive species.
As an inert element, xenon (Xe) and its fission products have become useful targets in monitoring nuclear
detonations because they migrate more easily than more chemically reactive species from detonation
sites. This is especially true of underground tests where inert fission products more easily emanate from
the ground into the atmosphere where detection is possible. Most Xe radionuclides are shielded from
production by stable nuclides of other elements (mass number <131). Multiple Xe radionuclides are
produced by fission but some are too short-lived to survive environmental transport to detection systems.
The four Xe radionuclide fission products of practical importance are: 131mXe (T1/2 = 11.84 d)1, 133Xe (T1/2
= 5.2475 d)1, 133mXe (T1/2 = 2.198 d)1, and 135Xe (T1/2 = 9.168 hr)1. Because each of these has a different
half-life, the measurement of these activity ratios is used as a chronometer to estimate the moment of
nuclear fission or detonation. Various systems (such as SAUNA, SPALAX, or Xenon International) have
been manufactured to measure all four of these Xe radionuclides so that through activity ratios, the
detected radionuclides can provide corroboration of nuclear detonations with other detected signals, such
as particulate (non-gaseous) radionuclides, seismic, hydroacoustic, and/or infrasonic waveform data.
 During the manufacture and commissioning of systems that measure fission product radioxenon,
calibration and validation standards are required to tune the system to provide accurate results. The Idaho
National Laboratory (INL) Noble Gas Laboratory (NGL) provide radioxenon gas standards used in
calibration and validation work. Radioxenon is obtained through neutron irradiation of isotopically
enriched xenon gas (130Xe131mXe, 132Xe133mXe+133Xe, 134Xe135Xe), from uranium fission product
off-gas (as a radiopharmaceutical 133Xe), or through decay of parent species (131I131mXe). These
materials are then diluted and blended with natural xenon or other gases to create gas samples that match
end-user specifications.
 One of the more difficult radioxenon isotopes to work with is 133mXe. It is impossible to make in a
radiometrically pure form because its decay produces 133Xe. When 133mXe is produced by neutron
irradiation, 133Xe is formed more readily than 133mXe leading to samples that are higher in 133Xe than
133m
 Xe (3m:3 activity ratio is approximately 0.15 for freshly irradiated samples). The excess 133Xe in
samples creates problems in the detection and quantification of 133mXe. The emission probability for the
characteristic gammas of these respective radionuclides favors 133Xe (37%, for 81.0 keV)1 detection over
133m
 Xe (10%, for 233.2 keV)1. Additionally, High Purity Germanium (HPGe) detectors, which INL NGL
uses for gamma spectroscopy, have a higher detection efficiency at 81 keV than at 233 keV (~80% vs.
42%, respectively). The combination of these effects results in an approximately 50:1 ratio for the
observed count rates for these two radionuclides (Table 1 provides details). This larger 133Xe count rate
will lead to elevated detector dead-time in high count rate samples or to vanishingly small 133mXe count
rates in low dead-time samples.

 This ingrowth relationship between 133Xe and 133mXe is a challenge because when 133mXe is present it
must be quantified completely for the ingrowth of 133Xe to be properly quantified. With a ~50:1 count rate
ratio, it can be very time consuming to wait for the 133mXe region of interest (ROI) to have enough counts
to quantify the 133mXe activity.
 This report proposes and demonstrates a quantification method to overcome some of the challenges
associated with working with 133mXe. The concept comes from the desire to exploit the ~50:1 count rate
imbalance observed in samples containing 133Xe and 133mXe. For a batch of material, the ratio of 3m:3 can
be calculated at any time once this ratio has been determined. By measuring the batch’s 3m:3 ratio when
the counting is easy (fast count rates balanced with low detector dead-time), this ratio can be used in
combination with the 133Xe net counts of an aliquot assay to calculate the 133mXe activity in low count rate
samples. The benefits provided by this method include a reduction in assay times for low activity samples
(by a factor of 50) or improving detection limits for 133mXe by a factor of 50. Implementation of this
method, however, is limited to laboratories equipped to work with and manipulate large batches of
radioactive material, what the INL NGL routinely does when aliquoting radioactive material for standards

production. It is anticipated that the benefits provided by this quantification method will result in higher
quality certified values for 133mXe and increased production capacity through reducing sample assay
durations.

 Quantification of a short-lived species like 133mXe requires more than merely a decay equation.
Radiation detection systems provide an array of data during an assay that must be used to calculate
sample activity. Among the data provided are: assay start time and date, detector live time, real time or
clock time, and a histogram of observed events (i.e., the spectrum). Detectors that have been calibrated
can provide energies to the observed spectral peaks. The user (often using software) must then fit the peak
areas to obtain net area counts (see Table 2 for summary of variables).

 When a radiometric spectrum is analyzed, the collection of observed events are reduced to a single
number: the net peak area (𝑁𝑁𝐴𝐴 ). When 𝑁𝑁𝐴𝐴 is divided by the live time (LT) a number that resembles the
sample count rate is the result. But if the radionuclide under analysis is short-lived, the ratio of net peak
area and the live time (𝑁𝑁𝐴𝐴 ⁄𝐿𝐿𝐿𝐿) will provide a number that gives the average count rate observed over the
assay. Over time a radioactive sample’s count rate will decrease. If the decrease in the sample count rate
becomes significant during the radiometric assay, simple analysis like dividing the net peak area by the
count time will lead to biased results. Figure 2 shows an example of a short-lived radionuclide sample (in
this case 135Xe) with a decreasing count rate during an assay. The average count rate (i.e., 𝑁𝑁𝐴𝐴 ⁄𝐿𝐿𝐿𝐿 shown
by the orange trace) is not equal to the initial count rate (green trace). A simple approximation to get
around this issue with quantifying short-lived radionuclides is to use the average count rate (𝑁𝑁𝐴𝐴 ⁄𝐿𝐿𝐿𝐿) as
the sample count rate, but to use the time of count as the midpoint of the count, rather than the start of the
count. If the decrease in decay rate with time was linear this would provide perfectly accurate results, but
due to the curvature of the exponential decay the moment that the count rate matches the average count
rate is not at the exact midpoint (0.5 of the count duration) but occurs at 0.484 of the count time (in this
case for 135Xe). The offset in mid-point count rates between the modeled midpoint count rate (tfrac = 0.5)
and 𝑁𝑁𝐴𝐴 ⁄𝐿𝐿𝐿𝐿 is –0.6%. Some other data sets were modeled that included 133Xe, 131mXe, and 133mXe and the
midpoint count rate offset was not identical but ranged from –1.2 to +0.5%. If a laboratory uses this
method for quantification, this offset can be eliminated if the calibration assays and the assays of
unknowns are always the same duration. This offset will be accounted for in the detector efficiency,
providing accurate results. If laboratory operations are not conducive to using uniform count times
between calibration and sample analysis, another approach can provide a more accurate determination of
the sample count rate. The approach discussed in the next section is better suited to a gas standards
production operation such as the INL NGL because count times are shortened and lengthened depending
on the count rate to achieve a uniform net peak area. Gas standards production involves quantifying
samples of very high and very low count rates that change the count durations significantly.

 Sample Self-Attenuation
 Gamma radiation is a type of high energy photon. Being a chargeless particle, it passes through solid
matter relatively easily compared to charged alpha or beta particles. Gammas can be shielded if enough
material is placed around it to stop the radiation penetration. The three characteristics that determine the
effectiveness of shielding are the energy of the gamma �𝐸𝐸𝛾𝛾 �, the atomic number (z) of the shielding
material, and the density of the shielding material (𝜌𝜌). Shielding is most effective when 𝐸𝐸𝛾𝛾 is low and z
and 𝜌𝜌 are high. Materials often used for shielding photon energy (x-rays and gammas) are lead (z=82, ρ =
11.34 gm/cm3) or bismuth (z=83, ρ = 9.78 gm/cm3). In the samples under analysis in this study, the

 4

matrix is xenon gas (z=54, ρ = variable), which is a relatively high z material. The energy of the
characteristic photons used to quantify radioxenon isotopes range in energy from 81.0 keV1 (133Xe) up to
249.8 keV1 (135Xe). The potential for self-attenuation losses are variable because samples have a varying
amount of xenon. Putting increasing amounts of xenon in a fixed volume container increases the density
(ρ) of the shielding material (xenon gas). Sample volumes typically range from almost zero up to eight
cubic centimeters (cm3) corresponding to ρ = 0 – 0.0714 gm/cm3 in a glass ampoule container (xenon
density at std. conditions [0°C and 1 atm] is 0.005894 gm/cm3 contained in a 0.66 cm3 ampoule).
 It is difficult to estimate whether the combination of xenon gas density and these photon energies will
result in significant sample self-attenuation theoretically. Fortunately, experiments were conducted to
determine the effect and the results have been published.3 The published attenuation factors give the
percent loss in count rate per cm3 of xenon added to the sample. Attenuation factors are shown in Table 3.

 EXPERIMENTAL VALIDATION OF EQUATIONS
 The Ingrowth During Acquisition (IDA) Equation
 The derived IDA correction [see equation (18)] was applied to a real-world sample that contained
133m
 Xe and 133Xe. This sample contained 26.6 Bq of 133mXe and 44.25 Bq of 133Xe. The sample was
assayed and the collected spectrum was saved every 20,000 seconds until the total assay time reached
approximately 350,000 seconds. This data set, when analyzed correctly, should show the same initial
133
 Xe activity for every collected spectrum regardless of how long the assay of the sample was. The data
was analyzed two different ways: 1) using the DDA correction equation that assumes no contribution to
the 133Xe activity from the decay of 133mXe, and 2) using the IDA correction equation which accounts for
ingrowth during acquisition. The 133Xe activity results are plotted in Figure 4.

Figure 4. Comparison of initial 133Xe activity calculated using two different methods, DDA and IDA.
Each datapoint is an activity value calculated from a saved spectrum. A spectrum was saved every 20,000
seconds during the 350,000 second assay. DDA does not account for ingrowth during acquisition, IDA
does account for ingrowth during acquisition. The sample used contained 26.6 ± 0.7 Bq 133mXe and 44.2 ±
0.6 Bq 133Xe at the start of the count. Error bars shown indicate the 1σ uncertainty.
 The results show there is a growing bias in the results calculated from the DDA, whereas the IDA
shows a constant initial 133Xe activity over the entire collection of assays. These data show that not
accounting for the ingrowth can lead to significant bias in the calculated result during long assays where
significant 133mXe is present.
 There are no explicit conditions where the use of IDA is recommended, but concerning the DDA
correction, it is recommended when 10% of the isotope under analysis decays during assay.4 When the
same data from Figure 3 is plotted as a function of % total 133mXe loss to decay (Figure 5), it becomes
clear that using the IDA correction is recommended for use when 10% of the 133mXe present has decayed
away.

 11

Figure 5. Real world sample data analysis; sample contained 26.6 ± 0.7 Bq 133mXe and 44.2 ± 0.6 Bq
133
 Xe. Calculated 133Xe activity results are plotted vs. % total 133mXe loss to decay. Error bars shown
indicate the 1σ total propagated uncertainty.
 Although the 10% parent isotope decay threshold for using the IDA correction makes sense from this
data, it is likely that other rules are needed to clarify the picture. Questions such as how the parent and
daughter half-lives relate to each other (are they both within the same order of magnitude, or vastly
different from each other), and how much of the parent isotope vs. how much of the daughter isotope is
present in the sample at the time of assay. While this information is available for the sample presented,
this type of system will require more study before general guidelines can be formulated.
 Activity of 133mXe without using 133mXe counts Equation (Indirect
 Method)
 Data was collected during a radioxenon standard production exercise. A 133m:133 ratio was
determined upon receipt of the material. A quantitative dilution of the received material produced a gas
with a nominal concentration of 200 mBq 133mXe/cm3 of xenon. Typical HPGe analysis of this sample
requires approximately 45 hours of assay time to collect >10,000 133mXe counts (~1% counting error
target). For this reason, four samples were produced and each was counted on four separate HPGe
detectors to maximize quantification of this standard. Each sample was assigned a numerical identifier,
and each sample was referred to as a Verification Bean (VB). To test the effectiveness of the newly
derived equation to calculate 133mXe activity using only the batch ratio and 133Xe counts, the count data for
these samples were saved after ~1 hour and ~3 hours of count time. The 45 hour assay of these samples
was conducted without any interruptions. After the assay was completed, three of the four verification
beans were counted a second time for 70 hours. The fourth VB was not counted a second time because
one of the detectors was needed for another activity. These second assays only resulted in ~7,000 133mXe
counts due to decay of the sample, but the data were still analyzed for comparison. All data were analyzed
using the “Direct” 133mXe assay method and the new method developed in this report, referred to as the
“Indirect” method, that uses the 3m:3 ratio and the 133Xe counts. The data are shown in Figure 6.

 12

Figure 6. Data comparison between analysis using the “Direct” 133mXe assay method and the “Indirect”
method using the 3m:3 Ratio and the 133Xe counts.
 The indirect method results in Figure 6 show a good match to the nominal activity of the mixture. The
uncertainties are small and the variation between the different detector results are within the error bars (1σ
total propagated uncertainty). This is true for the data points with as little as 1 hour accumulated counts.
The direct assay results in Figure 6 show large error bars and large variability at 1 hour and 3 hour assay
durations. At 45 hours the variability between samples is significantly reduced and the error bars are
much smaller. The direct results at 70 hours are similar in variability and error bar size as the direct
method 45 hours results.
 The relative count rates between 133mXe and 133Xe in these irradiated materials are expected to have an
approximately 50:1 3:3m count rate abundance discrepancy. If properly exploited, this method could
result in a reduction of the required count time by a factor of 50x. Figure 6 suggests it is possible to
perform two indirect quantification 1 hour assays on a single sample and have the same, or even better,
looking data than two direct quantification assays of 45 hour + 70 hour (=115 hours total). The average
required analysis time by direct quantification is 57.5 hours (115 hours divided by 2). Compared to the
average 1 hour by indirect methods, this seems to show that this method can achieve the theorized ~50x
reduction in assay time.
 Application of the Indirect Method to Detector Calibration
 The indirect method for quantifying 133mXe can be applied to detector calibration. In a conventional
detector calibration, a certified standard is used to quantify detector response and calculate the efficiency
of the detector in quantifying the radiation type. The best calibration methods rely on samples with
identical or similar sample matrix, container shape and size, and distance from the detector (geometry).
During a calibration assay it is best to allow significantly more counts to accumulate than is done during

 13

typical sample analysis, to reduce the contribution of uncertainty of counting to the sample. If a typical
sample would be assayed until 10,000 counts have been accumulated (~1% counting uncertainty), a
calibration assay should be assayed until 20,000-100,000 counts or more are accumulated (~0.7% to 0.3%
counting uncertainty).
 The following procedure outlines how the indirect method for quantification can be used for detector
calibration:
 1) Assay a batch of 133mXe to quantify the 3m:3 activity ratio
 2) The batch of 133mXe will be split to make samples containing count rates best suited to the
 geometries being calibrated. The sample count rates should not cause detector dead time above
 10% (and ideally should be under 5%).
 3) The split samples are then assayed in the calibration geometry until 20,000-100,000 counts have
 accumulated in the 133mXe ROI.
 4) The 3m:3 ratio and the 133Xe counts accumulated in the split samples can be used to perform the
 indirect quantification of 133mXe present in the sample. This is used as the sample’s certified
 value.
 5) The directly measured 133mXe counts in the ROI are used as the detector response data.
 6) The ratio between the indirect 133mXe certified activity and the directly detected 133mXe counts
 provides the detector efficiency.
 This procedure was used to calibrate three HPGe detectors at INL. The resulting calibration results
were used to analyze a set of direct 133mXe quantification data. These new results were compared against
results obtained with the previously established efficiencies used for daily quantification. This
comparison showed a negative bias of -4.7% (± 3.2%) in the Indirect calibration results. At this point, it is
difficult to make a definitive judgement as to which calibration is the most correct. More analysis is
required before one method of calibration is accepted in place of the other.
 We have demonstrated that the Indirect 133mXe quantification method can be used for detector
calibration and to quantify unknown samples in significantly less time. Significant time savings were also
expected during detector calibration, but the need for high count accumulation and multiple replicate
measurements, no real time savings were achieved by employing this method.
 This method is dependent on an existing 133Xe (ratio), 133mXe (ratio) and 133Xe (calibration count)
calibrations to determine the 133mXe (calibration count) efficiency. We believe that this first attempt at
using this method of calibration suffered from poor 133Xe calibrations during the ratio determination. With
poor data inputs, the data outputs were also poor. Therefore, we recalibrated the detectors for 133Xe assays
at distance (12 and 24cm) where the ratios are measured and saw the results improve. The 133mXe (ratio)
efficiency also played a role in the final calibration quality. Because of the difficulty of calibrating 133mXe,
the efficiency used in the 3m:3 activity ratio was taken from a multi-energy gamma calibration. The work
accomplished and presented here suggests that the indirect method is a viable method but requires good
supporting data (i.e., detector efficiencies).
 CONCLUSION
 This report provides a detailed derivation of the decay during acquisition (DDA) equation, ingrowth
during acquisition (IDA) equation, the 3m:3 activity ratio equation (with ingrowth), the indirect
quantification of 133mXe equation, and the 133Xe activity quantification equation by indirect 133mXe
method. The DDA and IDA equations are useful for analysis of 133Xe and 133mXe under any circumstances
where direct 133Xe and 133mXe net count data is available. The indirect 133mXe quantification equations are
not universally useful. They can be used in a situation where a batch of material is quantified for 3m:3
activity ratio first, and then this ratio is used in subsequent analysis of splits of this original material. Not
very many laboratories are able to operate in this way, but this is useful and applicable to laboratories that

 14

produce 133mXe in a large batch and produce subsequent splits and dilutions from that large batch, as is
done in a gas standards production operation.
