Executive Summary / Abstract
This project developed reliable and computationally efficient mathematical and machine-learning
methods for analyzing scientific data generated by U.S. Department of Energy leadership facilities. The
work was organized around two original research thrusts: (1) stochastic back-propagation and
numerical analysis for probabilistic neural networks, and (2) goal-oriented data assimilation for dynamic
experimental design. Over the three-year performance period, the project produced a connected set of
advances in uncertainty quantification, nonlinear filtering, state and parameter estimation, data-driven
stochastic control, reinforcement learning, transferable neural-network approximation, and score-based
generative data assimilation. The first thrust culminated in a sample-wise back-propagation framework
with rigorous convergence analysis for stochastic neural networks and a backward-stochastic-
differential-equation formulation for quantifying uncertainty in deep learning. The second thrust
produced kernel-learning and adaptive density-estimation filters, direct parameter-estimation methods,
stochastic-maximum-principle-based control and reinforcement-learning algorithms, and high-
dimensional score-based filters. In the final project period, this line of work advanced to a training-free
ensemble score filter that directly estimates score functions from ensembles and demonstrated
scalable nonlinear filtering in systems with up to one million state variables.
The project also demonstrated the value of these mathematical methods in quantum materials. A
stochastic optimization method was used to perform analytic continuation and analyze quantum Monte
Carlo data for the electron-doped single-band Hubbard model. The resulting study identified robust
charge-density-wave correlations with checkerboard and unidirectional components and provided
evidence supporting the single-band Hubbard model as a description of electron-doped cuprates. The
study was published in Nature Communications and used resources of DOE leadership computing
facilities. Beyond this completed application, the project established the estimation and control
components needed for future closed-loop materials experiments, including posterior-density tracking,
online parameter learning, and data-informed feedback decisions. The project trained graduate
students, supported internships at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, and disseminated results through
peer-reviewed publications and organized conference mini-symposia. Overall, the award advanced the
mathematical foundations and practical scalability of scientific machine learning while connecting new
methodology to a DOE-relevant materials-science problem.

Original Goals:
The project objective was to develop reliable and efficient machine-learning methods for processing complex,
high-dimensional data produced by leadership computing and experimental facilities. The research emphasized
mathematical structure, convergence and stability, uncertainty representation, and computational.
The original work plan contained two research thrusts:
1. Develop stochastic back-propagation methods for training probabilistic deep neural networks and establish
 convergence and stability analysis. The intended outcome was a principled training method for neural
 networks with injected stochasticity, together with guidance for selecting training parameters and evaluating
 prediction reliability.
2. Develop goal-oriented data assimilation methods for dynamic experimental design. The intended outcome
 was a mathematically rigorous scientific-machine-learning workflow in which streaming observations update
 probability distributions, reduce resampling degeneracy, and support selection of experimental actions that
 best advance a scientific objective.

2. Research Accomplishments
I. Methodology Development
2.1 Probabilistic Neural Networks and Uncertainty Quantification
The project developed a stochastic formulation of neural-network training in which the network dynamics are
interpreted through stochastic differential equations and the learning problem is treated as a stochastic optimal-
control problem. This formulation led to a backward-SDE method for quantifying uncertainty in deep learning and
to a sample-wise back-propagation algorithm based on an adjoint backward stochastic differential equation.
Unlike conventional chain-rule back-propagation applied to deterministic networks, the method incorporates the
stochastic terms required to correctly propagate sensitivities through neural networks with injected noise.
The associated numerical analysis established convergence of the sample-wise method and connected the
number of training iterations to network depth under stated regularity and convexity assumptions. Benchmark
numerical experiments supported the analysis and showed that stochastic neural-network training can provide
robust predictive behavior. These results supplied a missing mathematical foundation for probabilistic learning
and provided practical guidance for selecting discretization and training parameters.

2.2 Kernel-Learning and FBSDE Data Assimilation
For nonlinear data assimilation, the project developed a kernel-learning backward-SDE filter and a PDE-based
adaptive kernel method. The approach represents the evolving filtering density using adaptive kernel
approximations and uses forward-backward stochastic differential equations to connect the state dynamics,
observation updates, and posterior density. The kernel representation reduces the sample degeneracy that can
occur in Monte Carlo resampling and provides a meshfree route to estimating posterior probability densities.
The project also completed convergence analysis for the kernel-learning FBSDE filter. This analysis supplied
theoretical support for an algorithm that had previously been validated primarily through numerical experiments,
clarifying how density-estimation error, time discretization, and iterative updates contribute to local and global
convergence. Together, these developments advanced the reliability of nonlinear filtering and state estimation for
scientific systems.

2.3 Data-Driven Stochastic Control, Reinforcement Learning, and Parameter Estimation
The project connected data assimilation to decision-making through the stochastic maximum principle. A sample-
wise data-driven control solver was developed for stochastic optimal-control problems with unknown model
parameters. The method learns model parameters from data while simultaneously computing feedback controls,
thereby avoiding a strict separation between model identification and control design. A subsequent convergence
study analyzed an online data-driven feedback-control algorithm and provided mathematical support for its long-
time use.
The same stochastic-maximum-principle framework was extended to reinforcement learning in parameterized
environments. This work interpreted policy improvement through a Hamiltonian-based update and provided a
model-informed alternative to purely black-box reinforcement-learning procedures. A direct-filter parameter-
estimation method was also applied to the truncated Korteweg-de Vries model, demonstrating online recovery of
uncertain model parameters from observations. These advances supplied the estimation and control components
required for adaptive experimental workflows.

2.4 Transferable Neural Networks for Partial Differential Equations
The project developed a transferable neural-network method for solving families of partial differential equations.
Rather than pretraining a network separately for each target equation, the method constructs a reusable neural
feature space from a function-approximation perspective. Hidden-layer features are prescribed and tuned through
auxiliary functions, while the output weights are obtained using standard least-squares solvers. This design
separates feature construction from the specific PDE and enables the same feature space to be reused across
equations with different domains and boundary conditions.
Theoretical analysis characterized the quality and distribution of the resulting features, and numerical
experiments demonstrated strong transferability and high accuracy with substantially reduced training cost. The
approach is particularly relevant to leadership-facility workflows that require repeated solution of related forward
and inverse problems under changing parameters, geometries, or experimental conditions.

2.5 Score-Based and Ensemble Score Filtering
During the final project period, the team developed a score-based filter that embeds score-based diffusion models
within recursive Bayesian filtering. The method stores information about the evolving filtering density in its score
function and uses an analytical observation update to incorporate data while mitigating high-dimensional
degeneracy. This framework produced accurate nonlinear filtering results in challenging tracking problems and
established a bridge between generative diffusion models and data assimilation.
The work then advanced to an ensemble score filter (EnSF). EnSF eliminates neural-network training for score
estimation by using a mini-batch Monte Carlo estimator directly on the forecast ensemble. The analytical update
gradually introduces observational information into the score, and the reverse diffusion process generates
posterior samples. Tests on Lorenz-96 systems demonstrated reliable and efficient filtering for highly nonlinear
observations and state dimensions as large as one million. This result directly addressed the project goal of
reliable learning and inference for leadership-scale scientific data.

2.6 Integrated Methodological Contribution
Taken together, the project produced a coherent scientific-machine-learning pipeline: probabilistic neural
networks quantify predictive uncertainty; kernel and score-based filters assimilate observations and update
posterior distributions; direct filters estimate uncertain model parameters; and stochastic-control and
reinforcement-learning methods convert updated information into decisions. The methods share a common
mathematical foundation in stochastic differential equations, Bayesian inference, and stochastic optimal control.
This integration is important for leadership-facility science because it supports repeated cycles of simulation,
observation, uncertainty reduction, and decision-making.

II. Applications in Materials Science
2.7 Quantum-Materials Application: Electron-Doped Hubbard Model
The principal completed materials-science application addressed the electron-doped single-band Hubbard model,
a central computational model for understanding high-temperature superconducting cuprates. Quantum Monte
Carlo calculations generate imaginary-time correlation data, and extracting real-frequency response functions
requires analytic continuation, an ill-posed inverse problem that is highly sensitive to noise. Under this award, the
PI developed and applied an efficient stochastic optimization code to perform analytic continuation and to provide
an independent analysis alongside maximum-entropy and differential-evolution methods.
The study found robust charge-density-wave correlations with both checkerboard and unidirectional components
that were decoupled from spin-density modulations. The correlations were inconsistent with a simple weak-
coupling description based on Fermi-surface nesting, and their doping dependence agreed qualitatively with
resonant inelastic x-ray scattering measurements. These results provided evidence that the single-band Hubbard
model captures important properties of electron-doped cuprates. The work was published in Nature
Communications and used resources of DOE leadership computing facilities.

2.8 Enabling Framework for Dynamic Materials Experiments
The project’s data-assimilation and control developments were motivated by dynamic experimental design,
including materials experiments in which measurements arrive sequentially and experimental settings must be
updated in real time. The project completed the mathematical components needed for such workflows: posterior-
density estimation from streaming observations, state and parameter inference, uncertainty-aware surrogate
modeling, and feedback decisions based on the stochastic maximum principle. The score-based filters are
especially relevant when the material state is high-dimensional and the observation operator is strongly nonlinear.
The supplied annual reports document the Hubbard-model study as the completed materials-science application
during the performance period. They do not document a second end-to-end autonomous materials experiment
under this award. Accordingly, the project’s additional materials impact is stated here as an enabling
methodological foundation rather than as a completed experimental deployment.

3. Technical Effectiveness and Impact
3.1 Impact on Applied Mathematics and Scientific Machine Learning
The project advanced the mathematical analysis of probabilistic machine learning by providing convergence
results for stochastic back-propagation and kernel-learning filters. It also introduced score-based generative
methods into nonlinear filtering and demonstrated that high-dimensional posterior updates can be performed
without the severe degeneracy of conventional particle methods. The training-free EnSF reduced the
computational burden associated with repeatedly training score networks, making generative data assimilation
more practical for time-dependent scientific systems.

3.2 Impact on Scientific Data Analytics
The methods developed under the award improve the reliability of science-informed data analytics by coupling
prediction with quantified uncertainty and by allowing new measurements to update both states and model
parameters. The combination of filtering and feedback control supports the transition from passive data analysis

3.3 Technical Effectiveness, Feasibility, and Public Benefit
Technical effectiveness was demonstrated through peer-reviewed analysis and numerical experiments spanning
stochastic neural networks, nonlinear filtering, PDE approximation, dynamical-system parameter estimation, and
quantum-materials calculations. The project did not perform a formal economic analysis. Its practical feasibility is
instead reflected in reduced training requirements, reusable feature spaces, meshfree density approximations,
and scalable ensemble-based score estimation. These advances can improve the scientific return from publicly
funded computing and experimental facilities by accelerating inference, increasing robustness to uncertainty, and
enabling more informative use of measurements.

8. Conclusions
The project achieved its central purpose of developing mathematically reliable and computationally efficient
machine-learning methods for leadership-facility scientific data. It established rigorous stochastic back-
propagation and uncertainty-quantification methods, advanced kernel and score-based nonlinear filtering,
connected online inference to stochastic control and reinforcement learning, and introduced a reusable neural-
feature-space approach for PDEs. The final-year score-based and ensemble score filters significantly expanded
the feasible dimension and nonlinearity of data-assimilation problems while reducing or eliminating score-network
training.
The quantum-materials application demonstrated the scientific value of the methodology by using stochastic
optimization to analyze noisy quantum Monte Carlo data for the electron-doped Hubbard model. The work
produced a peer-reviewed materials-science result and illustrated how advanced mathematics can increase the
information extracted from DOE computing facilities. The project also trained graduate students and strengthened
university-national-laboratory collaboration. Collectively, these outcomes provide a strong foundation for future
uncertainty-aware, closed-loop scientific workflows that combine simulation, measurement, inference, and
control.
