Efficacy of yeast and yeast-derived products on growth performance and intestinal morphology of broiler chickens: a meta-analysis☆
Authors: Emmanuel Oluwabukunmi Alagbe, Olayiwola Adeola, Ayodeji Simeon Aderibigbe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe restriction of antibiotic growth promoter use in poultry production has increased interest in alternatives such as yeast and yeast-derived products. However, studies on yeast supplementation have reported inconsistent results. Hence, this study evaluated the effects of dietary yeast supplementation on the growth performance and intestinal morphology of broiler chickens using a meta-analysis and meta-regression approach. A systematic search conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA; 2020) guidelines across three databases found 1,397 records, and 41 controlled broiler studies that met the inclusion criteria. Subgroup analysis, meta-regression, heterogeneity, and robustness to publication bias tests were all performed. Yeast supplementation significantly improved body weight gain (mean difference (MD) = 72.8; 95% CI: 58.2 to 87.4; P< 0.001) and feed conversion ratio (MD = 0.055; 95% CI: 0.045 to 0.065; P< 0.001), indicating consistent benefits for growth performance and feed efficiency. Feed intake was not affected (MD = 3.57; 95% CI: −17.6 to 24.7; P= 0.741), suggesting that improvements in growth may be primarily driven by enhanced nutrient utilization rather than increased feed consumption. Intestinal morphology was also positively affected, with increased villus height (MD = 89.7; P< 0.001), improved crypt depth (MD = 24.1; P< 0.001), and a higher villus height-to-crypt depth ratio (MD = 1.57; P< 0.001), indicating beneficial effects on gut health. Subgroup analysis showed that Arbor Acres birds had the greatest improvement in villus height (P< 0.001). Meta-regression analysis revealed that yeast inclusion dose significantly influenced growth performance and intestinal villus height (P< 0.001). Publication bias assessment confirmed the robustness of the body weight gain, feed conversion ratio, villus height, crypt depth, and villus height-to-crypt depth ratio results. Overall, these findings support yeast supplementation as an effective feed additive in broiler chicken production, with consistent improvements in growth and intestinal morphology. Therefore, this study reinforces its potential role as an alternative to antibiotic growth promoters.Keywords: Broiler chickens, Growth performance, Gut health, Intestinal morphology, Saccharomyces cerevisiae

Introduction

The broiler chicken industry is the fastest growing sector of livestock production in the world, and plays a vital role in meeting the growing demand for high-quality, affordable animal protein. To sustain the growth of industry, especially under regulatory, biosecurity, and consumer pressures, there is a need for continuous refinement of nutritional strategies that can support the growth and health of broiler chickens (Gao et al., 2021; Perera and Ravindran, 2025). One of the most significant pressures has been the global movement to restrict and gradually phase out the use of antibiotic growth promoters (AGPs) in commercial poultry production (Korver, 2023).

After the landmark ban by the European Union (Korver, 2023), several other countries have adopted similar restrictions on AGPs. These measures are driven by increasing evidence that sub-therapeutic antibiotic use in livestock production may contribute to the emergence and dissemination of antimicrobial-resistant pathogens that pose a direct threat to animals, humans, and environmental health (Korver, 2023; Perera and Ravindran, 2025). Hence, the removal of AGPs has created a clear performance gap in poultry production, and identifying biologically effective, commercially viable, and regulatory-compliant feed additive replacements remains one of the greatest challenges the industry faces.

Yeast and yeast-derived products have shown promise as effective feed additives and have attracted sustained interest in the industry as potential alternatives to AGPs. This is due to the diverse portfolio of bioactive components in yeast as well as their established safety profile and compatibility with AGP-free poultry production (Fathima et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2025). Saccharomyces cerevisiae is the species most widely studied in this context, and it can be delivered to broiler chickens in several forms, including live probiotic yeast, autolyzed or hydrolyzed whole yeast, enzyme-treated yeast, purified cell wall fractions such as mannan-oligosaccharides and beta-glucans, and complex fermentation culture products containing both cellular material and fermentation metabolites (Perricone et al., 2022; Kiarie et al., 2022; Fathima et al., 2023). These yeast products interact with the gastrointestinal tract through complementary mechanisms, including modulation of mucosal immune response, production of beneficial microbiota, competitive exclusion of enteric pathogens, and protection of the intestinal epithelium (Fathima et al., 2023; Abd El-Ghany, 2025). All these properties suggest a solid mechanistic basis for the improvements in both growth performance and intestinal health of broiler chickens fed yeast-supplemented diets.

Despite this mechanistic rationale, published studies exploring dietary yeast supplementation in broiler chickens are characterized by considerable inconsistencies. Individual trials have shown improvements in body weight gain (BWG), feed conversion ratio (FCR), and intestinal morphology (Pascual et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2025). However, conflicting results also exist (Alizadeh et al., 2016; Taechanan et al., 2024). In addition, the direction and effect magnitude vary widely with yeast inclusion level, broiler breed, and health challenge status of broiler chickens (Fathima et al., 2023). Due to this variability, it is difficult for animal nutritionists to draw reliable conclusions from any single study. Moreover, this variability muddies the overall effectiveness of yeast supplementation when all the evidence is viewed as a whole.

Two published meta-analyses have attempted to address this topic. However, both had a relatively narrow focus. One evaluated a specific yeast fraction, yeast mannans (Salami et al., 2024), whereas the other focused exclusively on yeast-based probiotics (Ogbuewu et al., 2020). As a result, neither study captured the broader range of yeast and yeast-derived products currently used in broiler chicken nutrition. More critically, most quantitative reviews to date have concentrated on growth performance outcomes alone, without examining the intestinal morphological changes that are likely to support those responses. Whether consistent improvements in intestinal morphology contribute to the growth-promoting effects of yeast supplementation remains poorly understood. This is a meaningful gap, because understanding the basis of any growth performance response is necessary for interpreting how yeast products work in practice, not merely whether they work as feed additives for broiler chickens.

Therefore, a rigorous quantitative synthesis is needed to characterize the direction and magnitude of effect, to identify the conditions under which yeast supplementation is most consistently beneficial, and to quantify the degree of variability that remains after accounting for known moderating factors. As such, the objective of the current study was to address this gap through a systematic meta-analysis of published controlled trials that examine the effects of yeast supplementation on the growth performance and intestinal morphology of broiler chickens. We sought to determine the pooled effects of yeast supplementation on BWG, FCR, feed intake (FI), villus height (VH), crypt depth (CD), and the villus height-to-crypt depth (VH/CD) ratio, and to investigate the contributions of broiler breed and yeast dose to the heterogeneity observed across studies.

Materials and methods

Search strategy and screening

A systematic literature search was performed following the framework of Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 to identify studies on supplemental yeast and yeast-derived products in broiler chickens under controlled feeding conditions (Page et al., 2021). To ensure extensive coverage in the search, we used relevant keyword combinations. The databases PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science were searched using Boolean expressions that captured terms related to broilers, yeast supplementation, and key physiological outcomes, including growth performance and intestinal histomorphology. The search was restricted to peer-reviewed, full-text articles with a DOI, published in English between January 2015 and January 2026. The search was limited to studies published during this period to capture research conducted under contemporary commercial broiler production conditions, feeding standards, and yeast product formulations that more closely reflect current industry practices. This timeframe also coincides with a marked increase in peer-reviewed investigations into yeast supplementation as an AGP alternative, driven by global regulatory shifts that intensified interest in feed additives for poultry production. All retrieved records were imported into Mendeley for reference management, and duplicate entries were identified and removed using Mendeley and Excel. Study selection was then carried out in stages, including title, abstract, and full-text screening, in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines to ensure a transparent and replicable process.

Inclusion criteria

Studies were considered eligible based on predefined criteria to ensure relevance and comparability. Only controlled feeding trials involving broiler chickens (Gallus gallus domesticus), having clear reporting of breed (Ross, Cobb, or Arbor Acres), and the production phase were selected. Eligible interventions were limited to diets supplemented exclusively with yeast or yeast-derived products, including live yeast, active dry yeast, yeast mannans, yeast culture, yeast cell wall, hydrolyzed yeast, autolyzed yeast, yeast extract, yeast nucleotides, yeast polysaccharides, or brewer’s yeast.

Each study was required to include a control group fed a basal diet without yeast supplementation and to report at least one outcome related to growth performance or intestinal histomorphology. In addition, only experiments with a controlled experimental design, adequate replication (≥ 6), clearly defined duration, and complete statistical reporting were included. This included measures of central tendency, variability, and indicators of statistical significance, such as p-values or letter-based comparisons.

Exclusion criteria

Studies that did not meet the predefined criteria were excluded from the meta-analysis. Research conducted on layers, rodents, roosters, turkeys, guinea pigs, rabbits, pigs, ducks, quails, and fish, was not considered. Trials in which yeast was combined with other additives, minerals, or synthetic compounds were excluded to ensure that the effects of yeast supplementation could be evaluated independently.

Studies involving heat challenge, disease, or toxin challenges, such as coccidia or Eimeria infection, Salmonella exposure, or mycotoxin contamination, were excluded if they did not include both an appropriate control group and a yeast-supplemented group without challenge. In addition, in vitro, ex vivo, and cell culture studies were omitted unless supported by subsequent in vivo trials in broilers. Studies lacking adequate statistical reporting were also removed, including those without control groups, unclear sample sizes, or missing measures of variability required for effect size estimation. Non-English publications, conference abstracts, review articles, and incomplete or duplicate reports were excluded. Only studies that satisfied all inclusion criteria were retained for data extraction and subsequent analysis.

Statistics and data analysis

Means and standard deviations (SD) from the included studies were extracted and compiled for the meta-analysis. In cases where the standard deviation was not reported, it was calculated from the standard error of the mean by multiplying the standard error of the mean by the square root of the sample size. Effect sizes for growth performance and intestinal morphology indices were then calculated using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software (CMA; version 3, Biostat, Englewood, NJ, USA). These were calculated as the mean difference (MD) between treatment and control groups as detailed in Bilal et al. (2026). All analyses were conducted following the methodology described by Borenstein (2022). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. In the forest plots, interpretation of effect direction differed by outcome. For all indices, effect sizes on the right side (positive MD) indicate a benefit of yeast, whereas those on the left side (negative MD) indicate a benefit of the control. To ensure a consistent interpretation of effect direction across all outcomes, MD values were calculated as Yeast over Control for all indices except FCR and CD. For FCR and CD, MD values were calculated as Control over Yeast. This approach allows beneficial effects of yeast supplementation across FCR, CD, and all other evaluated indices to be expressed as positive effect sizes. For each outcome, the pooled effect estimate shown in the forest plot was presented with its 95% confidence interval (CI). The CI indicates the precision and uncertainty of the estimated overall effect. A narrower CI indicates greater precision, whereas a wider CI indicates greater uncertainty.

Results are presented as MD between the yeast treatment and controls with 95% CI using a random effects model. The studies in the analysis are assumed to be a random sample from a universe of potential studies. Robustness to potential publication bias was evaluated using the Classic Fail-safe N, which estimates the number of additional null studies required to render the overall effect non-significant. Where evidence of bias was indicated, Duval and Tweedie’s trim-and-fill method was applied to adjust the pooled effect size estimates.

Heterogeneity assessment

Heterogeneity was evaluated using three complementary statistics following Borenstein (2020, 2022). The Q statistic tested whether variability among studies exceeded that expected from sampling error alone, confirming the presence of heterogeneity, with α = 0.05 used as the threshold for significance. The I² (I-squared) statistic explained the proportion of total observed variance that is attributable to true between-study differences rather than sampling error. The τ² (Tau-squared) was estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method, representing the estimated variance of the true effect sizes across studies. It is also defined as the between-study heterogeneity (Borenstein, 2020, 2022).

Subgroup analysis and meta-regression

Subgroup analysis allowed comparison of mean effect sizes across breed categories (Cobb, Ross, and Arbor Acres). For the meta-regression analysis, there were two covariates, breed and yeast dose. The analysis examined whether breed and yeast dose explained a significant proportion of the between-study heterogeneity in the pooled effect estimates. Breed (Cobb, Ross, and Arbor Acres) was classified as a categorical moderator, while yeast dose was included as a continuous moderator. To avoid perfect multicollinearity, the categorical moderator, breed, was coded into dummy variables with Cobb designated as the reference group; thus, the coefficients represent differences in mean effect size relative to this reference. Subgroup effects were not evaluated for subcategories with fewer than three comparisons for each outcome because of low statistical power; consequently, subgroup analysis for the VH/CD ratio was not performed.

Results

Overview of included studies

The literature search conducted across PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science identified 1,397 records, and after the screening process, 41 studies met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the meta-analysis. These studies consisted of randomized controlled trials involving healthy broilers receiving yeast-supplemented diet throughout the treatment period. All included studies reported at least one of the primary outcomes of interest, including BWG, FI, FCR, VH, CD, or the VH/CD ratio. Effect of yeast supplementation on BWG, FI, and FCR of broiler chickens

Across the 41 included studies, a total of 75, 82, and 78 experiments provided usable data for BWG, FI, and FCR, respectively. Yeast supplementation was associated with improved BWG, the random effects model showed a significant increase compared with the control group. The pooled MD for BWG was 72.8 (95% CI: 58.2 to 87.4; P < 0.001), as shown in Fig. 2.

Effect of yeast supplementation on intestinal VH, CD, and VH/CD ratio of broiler chickens

Across the 41 included studies, a total of 35, 33, and 27 experiments provided usable data for intestinal VH, CD, and VH/CD ratio, respectively. Overall, yeast supplementation was associated with improved intestinal morphology.

In the pooled analysis, yeast supplementation significantly increased intestinal VH compared with the control group under the random effects model. The pooled MD for VH was 89.7 (95% CI: 48.9 to 130.5; P < 0.001), with individual study estimates ranging from −271.1 to 479.27 (Fig. 8). Yeast supplementation also improved intestinal CD. The pooled MD for CD was 24.1 (95% CI: 15.3 to 32.9; P < 0.001), as presented in Fig. 9. In addition, yeast supplementation improved the VH/CD ratio relative to the control group, with a pooled MD of 1.57 (95% CI: 0.8 to 2.4; P < 0.001). Individual study estimates for this outcome ranged from −0.69 to 6.02 (Fig. 10).

While this suggests a nominal improvement of 2.74 in the intestinal CD effect size for every one-unit increase in yeast dose, the relationship was not statistically significant (Z = 0.21, P = 0.832; Table 1). Consequently, we failed to reject the null hypothesis, indicating that there is no significant linear relationship between yeast dose and the effect size of CD when breed is held constant (Fig. 12B, C, and Table 1). For the VH/CD ratio, heterogeneity was observed among studies (Q = 352.8, df = 34, P < 0.001; I² = 90.4%; Table S12), explaining that variability among studies exceeded that expected from sampling error alone.

Assessment of publication bias and robustness

To assess the robustness of the findings, Rosenthal’s fail-safe N was calculated. The analysis indicated that 2,001, 8,729, 1,016, 2,090, and 1,438 missing studies with null effects would be required to overturn the observed results for BWG, FCR, VH, CD, and VH/CD ratio, respectively. These values were statistically significant (P < 0.001), indicating that the results for these outcomes are highly robust and unlikely to be affected by potential publication bias (Fig. S1; S3 – S6).

In contrast, the fail-safe N for FI was not statistically significant (P = 0.555), suggesting that this result is not robust and may be influenced by missing studies (Fig. S2A and S2B). The Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill method identified 15 potentially missing studies on the left side of the funnel plot. After adjustment, the pooled estimate for FI shifted from 7.10 to −26.46 (95% CI: −50.36 to −2.56). This change in both direction and magnitude indicates that the FI result is sensitive to potential publication bias and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

Published trials on dietary yeast supplementation in broiler chickens have produced inconsistent results, with wide variation in the direction and magnitude of effects across yeast inclusion levels, broiler breeds, and health challenge conditions (Fathima et al., 2023). Additionally, the meta-analyses conducted on this topic are limited in scope and have not address intestinal morphological outcomes. The present study, therefore, aimed to provide a broader quantitative synthesis of the effects of yeast and yeast-derived products on growth performance and intestinal morphology in broiler chickens. Additionally, it investigated the contributions of broiler breed and yeast dose to the heterogeneity observed across studies. Our results demonstrate that yeast supplementation confers meaningful improvements in broiler growth performance and intestinal morphology, while also addressing important aspects that individual studies cannot resolve on their own.

Characteristics and functions of yeast-derived products

Yeast-derived products used as feed additives in broiler chickens differ in composition and biological activity. Live yeast and active dry yeast contain viable Saccharomyces cerevisiae cells and may influence the intestinal environment through microbial interactions, metabolite production, and immune modulation (Gao et al., 2008). In contrast, brewer’s yeast is generally inactive and functions mainly as a source of protein, amino acids, vitamins, minerals, and yeast cell-wall components. Yeast culture contains yeast biomass, fermentation media, and metabolites such as peptides, organic acids, vitamins, and enzymes. Therefore, its effects may result from both yeast components and fermentation products rather than from viable yeast cells alone (Gao et al., 2008; Sun et al., 2020).

Furthermore, yeast cell-wall products consist mainly of mannans, mannan-oligosaccharides, β-glucans, and smaller amounts of protein and chitin (Ghosh et al., 2012; Pascual et al., 2020). Mannans may reduce the attachment of certain pathogenic bacteria to the intestinal epithelium, while β-glucans may modulate innate immune responses (Spring et al., 2000; Ghosh et al., 2012). On the other hand, hydrolyzed and autolyzed yeast products are produced by breaking down yeast cells using enzymes, thereby releasing and solubilizing intracellular compounds, including proteins, peptides, amino acids, nucleotides, and other soluble components (Chae et al., 2001; Tanguler and Erten, 2008). In addition, nucleotide-rich yeast products may support intestinal epithelial repair and immune cell activity, particularly during disease challenge (Leung et al., 2019; Lawal et al., 2024). Consequently, it is important to note that differences in yeast viability, processing method, active-component concentration, and inclusion level may contribute to variation in growth performance and intestinal health.

Nevertheless, although the studies included in the current meta-analysis used different yeast-derived products, these products share several nutritional and functional components. These components may support growth performance and intestinal health through similar mechanisms, including modulation of the gut microbiota, regulation of immune responses, maintenance of intestinal integrity, and provision of bioactive nutrients (Gao et al., 2008; Ghosh et al., 2012; Sun et al., 2020). Therefore, the pooled estimates from this meta-analysis reflect the average response of broiler chickens to dietary supplementation with the range of yeast-derived products evaluated in the included studies.

Effects on body weight gain and feed conversion ratio

The improvements in BWG and FCR observed in this meta-analysis align with previous findings. The meta-analysis conducted by Salami et al. (2024) revealed that supplementation with yeast mannan-rich fraction improved multiple growth performance parameters and reduced mortality. The positive effects on growth performance also demonstrate equivalence with in-feed antibiotics in terms of production outcomes. Additionally, the meta-analysis by Ogbuewu et al. (2020) focused specifically on probiotic yeast supplementation in broilers and found significant improvements in both FCR and BWG, while also identifying a dose effect, with birds receiving less than 10 g/kg dietary yeast outperforming those receiving higher levels. Those dose-response observations corroborate the subgroup findings of our present analysis, in which dose was identified as a significant moderator of BWG response.

At the level of individual studies, Hossain et al. (2025) demonstrated that live yeast supplementation at 600 mg/kg of diet produced an FCR of 1.63 compared with 1.83 in unsupplemented controls, alongside a higher BWG, without using antibiotics. Similar outcomes were reported by Hoque et al. (2021), who showed that yeast culture supplementation improved both BWG and FCR over the full production cycle (d 1 to 35), with the most pronounced effects appearing during the finisher phases, when voluntary feed intake is highest (Alagbe et al., 2026).

Effect on feed intake of broiler chickens

In contrast to BWG and FCR, FI did not show a consistent response in the present meta-analysis. The trim-and-fill procedure further shifted the pooled estimate from a slight positive effect to a negative value, indicating that studies reporting increased FI may be over-represented in the published literature. If the improvement in BWG were primarily driven by increased appetite and feed consumption, a corresponding and consistent rise in FI would be expected. The lack of such a pattern suggests that the beneficial effects of yeast supplementation in broilers are more likely mediated through improved nutrient absorption or better gut health, rather than through stimulation of feed intake.

This finding aligns with earlier reports. Pascual et al. (2020) observed that yeast cell wall supplementation in broilers did not affect final live weight but reduced FI while improving FCR, indicating that comparable or improved performance can be achieved with lower feed consumption. Such a pattern, where FCR improves alongside constant or reduced FI, suggests more efficient nutrient utilization rather than increased feed intake. Similarly, Hoque et al. (2021) reported improvements in BWG and FCR without corresponding changes in FI throughout the production cycle, supporting the view that the benefits of yeast supplementation may be mainly associated with gastrointestinal function rather than appetite stimulation.

Impact on intestinal morphology

The intestinal morphology results provide a biological basis for the improvements observed in growth performance. Nutrient absorption occurs along the villi, while the crypts are responsible for epithelial renewal. Measures such as VH, CD, and the VH/CD ratio are commonly used indicators of intestinal structure and function (Iji et al., 2001; Alagbe et al., 2026). Increased VH is associated with greater absorptive capacity, while reduced CD suggests lower epithelial turnover and maintenance requirements, which may allow more nutrients to be directed toward growth (Uni et al., 1998; Iji et al., 2001; Alagbe et al., 2026). Notably, early development of the intestine is intricately linked to improvements in nutrient use. As noted by Gaweł et al. (2025) and Li et al. (2022), improvements in villus development during the post-hatch period can enhance digestive capacity and support better growth outcomes. Therefore, the positive effects of yeast supplementation on VH, CD, and VH/CD ratio observed in this study are likely to contribute to the improved BWG and FCR.

The observed increase in VH, reduction in CD, and improvement in the VH/CD ratio are functionally relevant and contribute to the improvements in feed efficiency. These changes indicate a more favorable intestinal environment for nutrient digestion and absorption. Supporting this, Wang et al. (2022) reported that dietary yeast hydrolysate produced linear and quadratic increases in jejunal VH and VH/CD ratio, along with decreases in CD. These structural changes were accompanied by reduced concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α and interleukin-6, as well as increased expression of tight junction proteins such as zonula occludens-1, suggesting improved mucosal barrier function (Wang et al., 2022).

Furthermore, Gao et al. (2008) showed that yeast culture supplementation at 2.5 g/kg increased the VH/CD ratio in multiple intestinal segments and improved calcium and phosphorus digestibility, connecting morphological changes to better nutrient utilization. Similarly, Kyoung et al. (2023) reported increased VH/CD ratios across the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum, together with reduced inflammatory markers in the serum and shifts in cecal microbiota toward beneficial populations. Collectively, these findings indicate that yeast supplementation supports coordinated changes in intestinal morphology, barrier integrity, and microbial balance, which together may improve nutrient absorption and overall performance of broiler chickens.

Breed-specific responses in broiler chickens

Another notable finding was the breed-related variation in the intestinal response of broiler chickens to yeast supplementation. The increase in VH was most pronounced in Arbor Acres birds, which may reflect differences in baseline intestinal morphology that allowed greater responsiveness to yeast supplementation. Birds with relatively lower initial villus development may have had more capacity for trophic adaptation following dietary intervention (Cardeal et al., 2015; Yaqoob et al., 2021). This response is biologically plausible, as yeast components such as beta-glucans and mannan-oligosaccharides have been shown to promote enterocyte proliferation and maintain mucosal integrity (Fathima et al., 2023).

In addition, the improvement in intestinal CD was primarily driven by studies conducted in Ross broilers. Modern broiler strains have been intensively selected for rapid growth and increased feed intake, characteristics that impose substantial physiological demands on the gastrointestinal tract and may contribute to greater mucosal turnover (Ducatelle et al., 2023; Riber and Wurtz, 2024). However, this explanation should be interpreted with caution, as similar growth-related demands are also present in other commercial strains such as Cobb and Arbor Acres, yet the same overall pattern was not observed. This suggests that factors beyond growth rate alone, including strain-specific intestinal physiology, genetic differences in mucosal adaptation, or variation in study conditions, may influence the response to yeast supplementation. Additional studies directly comparing broiler breeds under comparable experimental conditions are needed to clarify these differences. Taken together, these patterns suggest variation in baseline gut function and nutrient utilization among commonly used commercial lines. Major broiler breeds such as Ross, Cobb, and Arbor Acres have been developed under different selection goals, including growth rate, feed efficiency, and carcass traits (Zhang and Aggrey, 2003; Shim et al., 2012).

The findings explained above have practical implications. Given that the broiler industry encompasses a diverse range of genetic lines, it is important to recognize that dietary yeast supplementation may elicit distinct responses across different breeds, even when inclusion levels are similar. Consequently, breed-specific nutritional evaluations are necessary to ensure optimal outcomes, rather than adopting a one-size-fits-all approach based on results from a single genetic line. Moreover, interactions between genetic background and dietary interventions are well documented in broiler research (Zhang et al., 2020; Panisson et al., 2022), and the response to yeast supplementation appears to follow this pattern.

Dose-response relationships

The identification of dose as a significant factor influencing BWG is consistent with previous studies examining optimal inclusion levels of yeast products. Higher levels of yeast supplementation were associated with reduced BWG, indicating that while appropriate inclusion levels can improve performance, exceeding this range may impair growth. In support of this, Ahiwe et al. (2020) reported improvements in BWG and FCR in broilers fed autolyzed whole yeast or yeast cell walls at inclusion levels between 1.0 and 2.0 g/kg, with responses remaining relatively consistent within this range. Similarly, Ogbuewu et al. (2020) observed a dose-dependent response at levels below 10 g/kg, with no additional benefits at higher inclusion rates. Sampath et al. (2023) further showed that the optimal yeast dose may vary depending on product type, production conditions, and bird age, all of which can influence the overall response. In the present study, dose was treated as a continuous moderator in the meta-regression model. However, it is important to note that expressing dose in g/kg of diet does not fully account for differences in product composition, as the concentration of bioactive components can vary among yeast products.

In contrast, yeast dose showed a positive linear relationship with intestinal VH, indicating that increasing inclusion levels progressively improved intestinal morphology. This differed from the negative linear relationship observed between yeast dose and BWG, suggesting that the intestinal and growth responses to yeast supplementation may be driven by different dose-dependent mechanisms. Yeast-derived components such as β-glucans and mannanoligosaccharides are known to promote intestinal development, including increases in VH and the VH/CD ratio in broilers (Gao et al., 2008; Kyoung et al., 2023). However, at higher inclusion levels, their immunomodulatory properties may also increase the metabolic demands associated with immune activity. Cox et al. (2010) reported that dietary β-glucan supplementation altered chemokine-cytokine balance in broilers but did not induce a strong immune response in the absence of a disease challenge, suggesting that the benefits of immune stimulation may be limited under healthy conditions. In addition, Davis (2022) demonstrated that immune activation in poultry can trigger proinflammatory cytokine cascades that redirect nutrient utilization away from growth and toward immune maintenance. Consequently, a greater proportion of nutrients and energy may be allocated to maintenance and immune function rather than tissue accretion, which may explain the lack of corresponding improvements in BWG at elevated inclusion levels even though higher yeast doses may continue to improve intestinal morphology.

Heterogeneity and publication bias

The heterogeneity observed across outcomes emphasizes the multifactorial nature of broiler chicken production and the complexity of yeast–gut interactions. Several additional sources of variation were not fully captured by the moderators included in this analysis, one of which is the health status of broilers. Yeast supplementation often produces stronger responses under challenge conditions than in healthy birds, as its mode of action, such as pathogen exclusion, immune modulation, and barrier support, may be more relevant when the gut is under stress.

Leung et al. (2019) reported that, in the absence of an Eimeria challenge, a nucleotide-rich yeast extract reduced jejunal VH, whereas under Eimeria challenge, yeast supplementation increased VH relative to challenged broiler chickens fed diets not supplemented with yeast. Similarly, yeast β-glucan supplementation increased endogenous antimicrobial peptides in broilers with necrotic enteritis, while effects were less pronounced in unchallenged birds (Tian et al., 2016). These findings suggest that the response to yeast may be influenced by health status. Consequently, studies conducted under controlled conditions may report smaller effects than those conducted in commercial settings where subclinical enteric challenges are more common, contributing to between-study variability.

Diet composition is another likely contributor. Factors such as energy and protein levels, ingredient sources, antinutritional components, and overall diet complexity can influence digestive activities in broilers and modify the response to yeast supplementation. For instance, birds fed corn–soybean diets may respond differently from those receiving more complex diets that include byproducts or alternative protein sources (Ajao et al., 2022; Attia et al., 2023; Ye et al., 2025).

Furthermore, the heterogeneity observed across the included studies in this meta-analysis is consistent with that reported in previous meta-analyses in poultry nutrition (Bilal et al., 2026; Riaz et al., 2026; Mbajiorgu et al., 2026). Such variability does not invalidate the findings but confirms that true effect sizes differ across study conditions. The pooled estimates should therefore be interpreted as the mean effect across a distribution of true effects rather than a single universal value. Overall, the evidence from this study supports the use of yeast as a feed additive to improve growth performance and gut morphology of broiler chickens.

On a similar note, the assessment of publication bias provides additional context for interpreting our results. The findings for BWG, FCR, VH, and CD appeared robust, as large numbers of hypothetical null studies would be required to alter the pooled estimates. This suggests that these outcomes are unlikely to be driven primarily by selective reporting. In contrast, the results for FI were less stable. The trim-and-fill adjustment produced a notable shift in the pooled estimate, indicating that the available literature on FI may be incomplete. This is plausible, as studies showing improved performance with little or no change in FI may be more likely to be published than those reporting negative effects, particularly for secondary outcomes such as FI.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this meta-analysis provides a comprehensive quantitative synthesis of the effects of yeast supplementation on growth performance and intestinal morphology in broiler chickens. Overall, the findings support yeast as both a biologically effective and practically relevant feed additive. Birds receiving yeast supplementation showed greater body weight gain, averaging 72.8 g more than birds fed control diets, alongside improvements in feed conversion ratio and intestinal morphology, including an increase in VH of 89.7 µm. The absence of a consistent effect on feed intake suggests that these performance benefits are more likely attributable to improved nutrient utilization and intestinal function rather than increased feed consumption.

The identification of broiler breed and inclusion dose as significant moderators suggests that responses to yeast supplementation are context-dependent rather than uniform across production systems. At the same time, after accounting for these factors, additional biological and management-related variables are likely to contribute to variation in outcomes. Collectively, these findings suggest that maximizing the benefits of yeast supplementation will require more targeted application strategies that consider dose and broiler genetic background rather than a one-size-fits-all approach. One limitation of this meta-analysis is that intestinal site could not be evaluated as a source of heterogeneity because few studies reported intestinal morphology measurements for the duodenum or jejunum.

Future research should focus on dose–response studies that evaluate multiple yeast inclusion levels rather than simple control and treatment comparisons. This approach would provide stronger evidence for identifying optimal supplementation levels and help determine whether responses are consistent across broiler breeds. Notably, only 44% of the studies included in this meta-analysis used graded yeast inclusion levels, indicating that current evidence remains limited in this area. In addition, studies conducted under commercial production conditions, that include higher flock densities are needed to strengthen the practical relevance of these findings, which are currently based largely on controlled experimental environments. Finally, incorporating approaches such as microbiome profiling, metabolomics, and intestinal transcriptomics could further clarify how yeast products interact with the gut milieu of broilers and contribute to achieving more predictable responses across production conditions.
