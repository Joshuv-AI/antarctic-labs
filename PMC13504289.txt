Chromatin accessibility classification of TAD boundaries discloses new architectural proteins
Authors: Karina Jácome-López, Leonardo Ledesma-Domínguez, Ayerim Esquivel-López, Rosario Pérez-Molina, Andrés Pénagos-Puig, Amaury Aguilar-Lomas, Juan-Carlos Gómora-García, Mayra Furlan-Magaril
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract3D genome organization is crucial to modulate gene expression. Topologically associating domain boundaries isolate genes and their regulatory elements within the same topological neighborhood, avoiding crosstalk between regulatory elements. Perturbation of domain boundaries causes aberrant genomic contacts and gene expression misregulation. CTCF and the cohesin complex are critical to form boundaries through loop extrusion. However, we still lack a complete understanding of what makes a boundary more effective at insulating genomic contacts than others. Here, we experimentally classified boundaries according to their chromatin accessibility as a proxy of protein occupancy in K562 human cells. Highly accessible boundaries are occupied by more proteins including CTCF, are more robust contact insulators, have a more conserved CTCF DNA-binding motif, and are more conserved across cell types. By exploring proteins enriched at boundaries with different accessibility, we found that CTCF and cohesin bind together with REST forming one module, while ZNF316 and EMSY form another, both occupying boundaries very frequently. CRISPR–Cas9 deletion of the ZNF316/EMSY binding region at a robust CTCF-free boundary increases inter-domain contacts, demonstrating that this module contributes to chromatin insulation. Our results emphasize the importance of protein combination and abundance in boundary strength and identify ZNF316/EMSY as a novel chromatin insulator module.

Introduction

Topologically associating domains (TADs) are structural units that contribute to the spatiotemporal control of gene expression by isolating the interactions between regulatory elements in the genome favoring the interaction between elements inside domains and restricting the frequency of inter-domain interactions [1, 2]. The isolation of genomic interactions is achieved through the action of domain boundaries that prevent cross-regulation between genomic elements [1–3]. Various reports have shown the importance of domain boundaries in maintaining proper gene expression patterns in diverse scenarios including development, cell differentiation, and DNA replication. In addition functional assays have shown that boundaries misfunction can lead to pathologies like cancer [4–8].

The human genome contains ∼3500–8000 domains covering >90% of the genome, depending on the resolution used. Their size varies from ∼30–40 kb to 3 Mb, with a median size of 720–840 kb [9, 10]. Fifty-five to seventy-five percent domains are preserved between human cell lines and 45%–76% are conserved between human and mouse [1, 9, 11]. In addition to mammals, there are other organisms in which domains have been described such as Drosophila melanogaster [12], Schizosaccharomyces pombe [13], the X chromosome of hermaphroditic Caenorhabditis elegans individuals [14], in the bacteria Caulobacter crescentus [15], and in Oryza sativa [16].

There is a high heterogeneity regarding the amounts of architectural proteins present at boundaries, histone modifications, and gene transcription. Domain boundaries are enriched in DNA-binding sites for proteins such as CTCF, cohesin, mediator, RNA Pol II, and general transcription factors [1, 17]. Also, boundaries are rich in genes that are expressed constitutively, enriched for H3K27ac, H3K4me3, H3K4me1, and H3K36me3 histone modifications, and they are also DNase I chromatin accessibility sites [1, 9, 18]. CTCF is a key factor in forming and maintaining domain boundaries and forming chromatin loops together with cohesin [19, 20]. Chromatin loop extrusion is implicated in loop and domain formation, where the cohesin complex runs through the chromatin fiber until it finds CTCFs bound in a convergent orientation, stabilizing loop anchors or domain boundaries [21–24].

The deletion of specific CTCF DNA-binding sites in boundaries results in gene expression pattern alteration via the incorrect formation of contacts between distal regulatory elements leading to developmental abnormalities [3, 4, 6, 7, 25]. Also, CTCF acute depletion causes domain disappearance from most genomic regions with ∼18% of domains remaining intact [19].

However, there are still many questions about the differences between boundaries in terms of their insulation capacity and their direct influence on transcriptional regulation. Thus, characterizing the identity and protein occupancy levels between domain boundaries is essential to discern whether there is a functional hierarchy in their insulating capacity. Here, we have classified domain boundaries by chromatin accessibility as a proxy for protein occupancy. We found that highly accessible chromatin boundaries are occupied by more proteins, are more robust contact insulators, have a more conserved CTCF DNA-binding motif, and are more conserved across human cell types. Also, we found a collection of proteins (CTCF, Cohesin, REST, ZNF316, and EMSY, among others) that occupy boundaries. Importantly, CTCF, Cohesin, and REST form a module that often binds together at boundaries. In contrast, ZNF316 and EMSY form another module that frequently binds together at boundaries, and the latter is prominent at boundaries without CTCF. Finally, we functionally demonstrate that ZNF316 DNA-binding motif edition of a region co-occupied by ZNF316 and EMSY leads to domain boundary disruption and increased inter-domain interactions, suggesting ZNF316/EMSY as a new module structuring chromatin and insulating interactions between regulatory elements.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

K562 cell line was obtained from ATCC (ATCC® CCL-243™), the cells were grown in Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium (IMDM) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) and maintained in 5% CO2 at 37°C.

Hi-C in nuclei experiments

Hi-C at different times of digestion enzyme were performed with 2 million of cells in two replicates. The fixation occurred during 10 min at 2% formaldehyde (Pierce™ 16% formaldehyde, methanol-free Thermo Scientific™) in supplemental medium. After that, glycine was added to 0.13 M final concentration and incubated first for 5 min at room temperature and then for 15 min on ice. The pellets were washed with cold PBS (phosphate buffered saline) buffer.

The cell lysis was performed in lysis buffer: 10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8, 0.2% Igepal, 10 mM NaCI, and 1× complete protease inhibitor (Sigma–Aldrich®) for 30 min on ice. Then the pellets were washed twice with lysis buffer and then with New England Buffer #2 (NEB2) at 1.25× concentration.

The pellets were incubated in 360 μl of 1.25× NEB2, 11 μl of 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate incubated at 37°C for 45 min with agitation 950 rpm. Then 75 μl of 10% Triton was added under the same incubation conditions as before. The digestion occurred with 1500 U HindIII (New England Biolabs®) and the samples were incubated at different time points: 0.25, 1, 3, and 12 h at 37°C 950 rpm.

The fill-in reactions were prepared by adding 1.5 μl each of 10 mM dCTP, dGTP, and dTTP, along with 37.5 μl of 0.4 mM Biotin-14-dATP (Invitrogen™) and 10 μl (50 U) of Klenow enzyme DNA Polymerase I, Large Fragment (New England Biolabs®). The mixtures were incubated at 37°C for 75 minutes, with intermittent agitation at 700 rpm (15 seconds of shaking every 30 seconds).

To each experiment 100 μl of 10× ligation buffer (New England Biolabs®), 10 μl bovine serum albumin (10 mg/ml), 25 U T4 DNA ligase (Invitrogen™), and 352 μl H2O were added. The ligation reactions were incubated overnight at 16°C.

For reverse crosslinking we added 50 μl proteinase K (10 mg/ml) and 10 μl RNase (10 mg/ml) and purified with phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol 25:24:1 (Invitrogen™). The DNA was sonicated in COVARIS S220 (Covaris, Inc.) with the following conditions: duty factor 10%, peak incident power (w) 140, cycles per burst 200, and H2O level 10–15 for 80 seconds.

The biotin removal and extreme repair were performed using 7 µg of sonicated DNA in a volume of 130 µl TLE, 16 μl of 10× ligation buffer NEB, 2 μl of 10 mM dATP, 15 U T4 DNA polymerase NEB (New England Biolabs®), and 7 μl H2O were added and incubated for 30 min at 20°C. Then we added 5 μl of 10 mM dNTPs, 4 μl of 10× ligation buffer NEB, 5μl of T4 Polynucleotide Kinase (New England Biolabs®) (10 U/μl), 1 μl Klenow large fragment (New England Biolabs®) (5 U/μl), and 25 μl H2O, this reaction was incubated for 30 min at 20°C. The DNA were selected using Ampure SPRI Beads (Beckman Coulter, Inc.) to get 300–600bp fragments. The pull-down was performed with 150 μl Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin C1 (Invitrogen™). The libraries were marked with TrueSeq DNA Adapters (Illumina, Inc.) and amplified using Phusion High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs®). The paired-end sequencing was performed in a HiSeq2500 (Illumina, Inc).

The Hi-C experiments in the cells edited by CRISPR–Cas9 were performed as described above, but with the following modifications: per experiment 5 millions of cells were used. For the chromatin digestion, we used 400 U of DpnII enzyme (New England Biolabs®) incubating overnight. The paired-end sequencing was performed in Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, Inc).

Restriction percentage of chromatin digestion

The chromatin temporal digestion and DNA extraction were performed as described in Hi-C experiments section. The evaluation of digestion percentage at chosen boundaries was performed with primers that flank a HindIII site. The qPCR reactions were made with 50 ng of DNA using the KAPA SYBR Fast qPCR kit (Kapa Biosystems). The restriction percentage was obtained with the formula: % Restriction = 100 – 100/2^[(CtR–CtC) Digested – (CtR–CtC) Undigested] [26].

CRISPR–Cas9-mediated edition

Two single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) were designing in CRISPOR [27]. The sgRNAs were cloned in the lentiCRISPRv2 (Addgene) vector based on lentiGuide oligo cloning addgene protocol. Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen™) was used to transfect 3 × 105 cells with 2 µg of vector (sgRNA-1 and sgRNA-2). The transfected cells were selected with puromicine. Clonal dilutions were made to isolate cells; we evaluated the deletions by end-point polymerase chain reactions (PCRs) and sequencing. See sgRNAs sequences in Supplementary Table S1.

RT-qPCR

Total RNAs were isolated using Invitrogen TRIzol™ reagent as user guide recommend. The RNAs were treated with RQ1 RNase-free DNase (Promega). The complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis were performed with SCRIPT cDNA Synthesis Kit (Jena Bioscience). The OAZ1 gene was used as housekeeping gene calibrator. The reactions were performed in the Applied Biosystem StepOne Real-Time PCR System using KAPA SYBR Fast qPCR kit (Kapa Biosystems). All primers used are available in Supplementary Table S1.

Hi-C data analysis

The paired-end raw fastq files were processed in HiC-Pro v2.11.1 with default parameters [28]. The alignment of the reads was performed with Bowtie2 v2.3.4.1 against the hg19 human genome. The HiC-Pro quality output files were visualized in MultiQC v1.7. The normalized ICE matrices were generated with same number of valid pairs and computed at 20-kb resolution for the Hi-Cs at different digestion time points, as well as for the Hi-Cs in the edited and control cells. The Hi-C replicates at each time point showed a similar QC profile (Supplementary Table S2). Also, the number of Hi-C contacts generated between regions at different genomic distances was plotted to verify that the cis decay was equivalent between replicates (Supplementary Fig. S1). Finally, the matrices were visually inspected to confirm similarities between replicates. Afterward all analysis was performed in the merged data sets of the replicates. The quality metrics for all Hi-C experiments presented are in Supplementary Table S2.

TAD boundary calling and accessibility classification

The domains were called with the measure of TAD-separation score in HiCExplorer v3.7.6 [29] at 20-kb resolution. The comparisons were made with false discovery rate and q-value threshold of 0.00005. The domains and boundaries were aggregated by their identification at each Hi-C time point using the exact coordinates. The insulation scores (IS) of boundaries were computed in TADtool with a window size of 100 000 bp and a TAD cutoff of 15 [30]. The corner scores of the domains were extracted from published data in K562 [9]. The TAD boundaries identified in this study are in Supplementary Table S3. Inter domain contacts or inter-TAD score represent the number of interactions that cross a boundary. These values were obtained at 20 kb of resolution with a window of 140 kb. The inter-TAD value was calculated as described [31]. To map our boundaries to the data set generated in Rao et al. [9], we took the 20-kb domain boundaries and mapped them to the domains called at 5-kb resolution. Next, we filtered those that overlapped with start or ends of the domains. For the boundaries occupied by CTCF, we located the nearest CTCF peak to the domain limit to obtain the 5-kb bin.

Boundaries and domain conservation across human cell types

Published Hi-C data sets for the cell lines GM12878, HUVEC, HMEC, IMR90, NHEK, K562, and HCT116 were used (see the “Data availability” section). HiCExplorer was used to identify domain boundaries as described above. The conserved boundaries across the different cell types were identified using a window ±125 kb from the start and end coordinate of the boundaries. Conserved domains between cell line types were identified using an interval of ±125 kb from their start and end coordinates. To be considered a conserved domain, the overlap must be at least 80%. The conserved boundaries and domains were identified using searchV2.1.pl (Supplementary data).

Fast-ATAC-seq processing data

Published Fast-ATAC-seq (SRR5657532, SRR5657533), Omni-ATAC-seq (SRR5657530, SRR5657531), and two standard ATAC-seq (SRR5657534, SRR5657535; GSM1782764, GSM1782765) data were downloaded (the “Data availability” section) [32, 33]. The fastq files were aligned with HISAT v2.1.0 against the hg19 genome version. The read coverage was computed with multiBamSummary in deepTools v3.5.0 [34]. The peaks were called with Genrich v0.6 removing the PCR duplicates and applying the default parameters. Peaks >1 kb were removed.

A/B compartments analysis

The PCA (principal component analysis) eigenvalues were computed with hicPCA in HiCExplorer for Hi-C matrices at 100 kb of resolution using the Lieberman parameter. H3K27ac and H3K4me3 histone modifications from ENCODE mapped to the hg19 genome were used to assign the active regions (see the “Data availability” section).

ChIP-seq data analysis

Two-hundred ninety six ChIP-seq data sets produced by ENCODE in K562 cell line were used (see the “Data availability” section). We analyzed the optimal IDR thresholded peak files processed based on the ENCODE processing pipeline using the hg19 genome. The identification of peaks located at boundaries were made in bedtools v2.29.0. The average signal score for CTCF, REST, ZNF316, and EMSY were made using bigwig files and processed with multiBigwigSummary in deepTools v3.5.0. The boundary classifications, with and without CTCF, were performed based on three different CTCF ChIP-seq sets (see the “Data availability” section).

RNA-seq data analysis

RNA-seq data sets produced by ENCODE in K562 cell line were used (see the “Data availability” section). The alignment bam files of two isogenic replicates processed using the hg19 genome version were analyzed with multiBamSummary in deepTools v3.5.0. The gene quantification files from same data set were analyzed to identify the gene expression level at boundaries.

Gene Ontology enrichment

The Gene Ontology enrichment analyses were made in gProfiler [35]. The default parameters were applied. The statistical domain scope used was the option for all known genes.

DNA-binding motif orientation analysis

The CTCF DNA-binding motifs were identified using the FIMO tool from MEME suite [36]. The CTCF sequence motif used was the reported in HOCOMOCO V11 database. The CTCF motifs were identified based on the DNA sequence from the ChIP-seq peaks. The extraction of motifs inside the boundaries was made with bedtools intersect. The boundaries of a total size of 20 kb were divided in 2-kb windows to map the strand motif orientation in forward and reverse. For each window, the frequency was calculated dividing the number of motifs, forward or reverse, between the total number of motifs for each type of boundaries. The same process was made for REST DNA-binding motif. For ZNF316, we processed the fasta ChIP-seq peak sequences to find a consensus motif with MEME suite; we used the HOCOMOCO V11 database to find the best possible motif sequence match. The DNA-binding motif orientation analysis was performed as described above for CTCF.

CTCF DNA-binding motif conservation analysis

The CTCF DNA sequence motif conservation was extracted from phastCons46way database. The motifs sequences were extended to 60 bp. The conservation score graphed corresponds to the mean of all nucleotides scores for each position.

Results

The degree of chromatin accessibility at domain boundaries reflects their insulation capacity

The structure and maintenance of chromatin domains are presumably due to the proteins occupying their boundaries [1, 18, 37]. We hypothesized that more robust boundaries have higher chromatin accessibility and therefore higher protein occupancy. Thus, domains with more chromatin-accessible boundaries should be more stable than those with fewer.

To determine whether accessibility indeed reflects more robust boundaries and domains, we experimentally explored whether domain boundaries exhibit different degrees of accessibility in the human K562 cell line. To do so, we performed Hi-C experiments varying the digestion time with a restriction enzyme. Restriction enzymes have been used to measure chromatin accessibility in different contexts before, as more accessible chromatin will be digested faster [38–41]. The incorporation of this digestion time course into the Hi-C enables us to directly assess boundary accessibility and TAD formation from the same experiments (Fig. 1A). We digested chromatin for 0.25, 1, 3, and 12 h to capture the most-accessible boundaries at the shortest incubation times. The results show that chromatin is indeed digested gradually over time. Chromatin digestion is readily observed from 0.25 h of incubation, and the percentage of digestion increases with longer digestion times (Fig. 1B). After digestion, we continued the Hi-C experiment and sequenced the generated libraries obtaining from ∼420 to 680 million read pairs and from ∼179 to 330 million valid pairs (Supplementary Fig. S1A and B, and Supplementary Table S2). We next built contact matrices from the different time points and called TAD boundaries using TAD-separation score at 20-kb resolution. The number of domains detected increased with the digestion time (Fig. 1C). Next, we classified boundaries based on their accessibility. The boundaries identified from 0.25 h of digestion are those with the highest accessibility, and we grouped them as type I. Boundaries identified from 1 h of digestion are type II, boundaries identified from 3 h are type III. Finally, boundaries identified only after 12 h of digestion are type IV, representing the least accessible boundaries detected (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. S2A).

Given that restriction enzymes have a specific DNA cutting site that could be wrapped in nucleosomes around CTCF binding sites and to confirm our chromatin accessibility classification by an orthogonal method, we used available Fast-ATAC-seq data to assess the accessibility of the boundaries. The results confirmed that our experiments with different restriction incubation times recapitulate different levels of boundary accessibility measured through ATAC, with type I boundaries having the most Fast-ATAC-seq signal and type IV boundaries having the least Fast-ATAC-seq signal (Fig. 1E). This was also confirmed using additional ATAC-seq data sets strengthening the accuracy of our TAD boundary accessibility classification (Supplementary Fig. S2C).

To analyze how accessibility relates to boundary insulation capacity, we computed the TAD-separation score for all boundaries [42]. The TAD-separation score of boundaries with higher accessibility displays more negative values than boundaries with less accessibility, reflecting they are more robust at insulating interactions from adjacent regions (Fig. 1F). This observation was corroborated by counting the inter-domain contacts (Inter-TAD) as explained in the diagram in Supplementary Fig. S2B. We found that the lower the accessibility, the more contacts cross the boundary (Fig. 1G). Finally, to further confirm our finding, we detected boundaries using the IS [30]. Type I boundaries have lower IS values, reflecting fewer interactions across them compared to type IV boundaries (Supplementary Fig. S2D).

Next, to circumvent the limitations of having boundaries called at 20-kb resolution and confirm that our accessibility classification holds at higher resolution, we mapped our domains and domain boundaries to a public and deeply sequenced Hi-C data set at 5-kb resolution [9]. We obtained their corner score and calculated their TAD-separation score. Using these 5-kb boundaries, we confirmed that the more accessible type I boundaries are more robust, with higher corner scores and lower separation scores than less accessible type IV boundaries (Supplementary Fig. S2E). In addition, we measured their Fast-ATAC-seq signal identifying once more that the type I boundaries are the most accessible boundary set also at 5-kb resolution (Supplementary Fig. S2E). This confirms that our boundary detection, accessibility classification, and conclusions regarding boundary strength are sustained using a higher-resolution Hi-C data set, reducing the boundaries to 5 kb.

To validate our genome-wide observations experimentally and locally, we explored the accessibility of selected type I and IV boundaries. To do so, we measured the digestion percentage of amplicons containing the boundaries, using the chromatin digested at different time points as a template for PCR reactions. The results show that the selected type I boundaries have higher restriction percentages at earlier time points than type IV boundaries, confirming they are more accessible (Fig. 1H and I). Additional type I and IV boundaries (seven in total) were tested by qPCR confirming that type I boundaries are more accessible (and digested first) than type IV boundaries (Supplementary Fig. S3A–D).

We next mapped the different types of boundaries to A and B compartments. We defined the compartments through a PCA using as a seed the signal for the H3K27ac histone mark to assign the A compartment. Each genomic window has a positive or negative PC1 value and in our analysis the positive values are directly related to the H3K27ac enrichment. Seventy-four percent of type I and type II boundaries are in the A compartment, whereas 67% and 66% of type III and type IV boundaries, respectively, percentages that are similar for all boundary types (Fig. 2A). The position of random genomic regions in compartments showed 53% regions located in A, 40% in B, and 7% found in the limit of compartments A/B. When plotting the PC1 values, we observed that type I–IV boundaries similar enrichment of H3K27ac and all of them have a significantly higher enrichment than random regions (Fig. 2B). These observations suggest that compartment assignments do not directly reflect chromatin accessibility by protein occupancy and that most boundaries reside in the A compartment. Also, it is well documented that CTCF is enriched at domain boundaries, which contributes to their formation together with the cohesin complex, as detailed above, and that CTCF binding is enriched in the A compartment [20–24]. Thus, a bias toward the A compartment is expected.

Next, we assessed CTCF abundance at our different boundary types using available ChIP-seq data (see the “Materials and methods” section). The average CTCF signal intensity is higher in type I boundaries compared to type IV (Fig. 2C). These results show that CTCF occupancy at boundaries might be partly responsible for increasing their accessibility.

We then assessed RNAs coverage at boundaries. Type I boundaries show more transcripts accumulation than type IV boundaries (Fig. 2D).

To better understand the complexity of type I–IV boundaries, we analyzed together the presence of CTCF, the TAD-separation score, and the PC1 compartment values generated using the H3K27ac histone mark as the seed (Fig. 2E). The category with more differences between boundary types is to have negative TAD-separation scores, localizing in the A compartment and having CTCF occupancy (−++ category). Thirty-four percent of type I boundaries show these features, while types II, III, and IV boundaries are less represented in this category (27%,17%,14%, respectively). Type I boundaries also have the highest proportion of all types in a category like the previous one but located in compartment B (−−+ category). Notably, there are two categories where the proportion of type IV boundaries is higher than the rest. Twenty-seven percent of type IV boundaries show negative TAD-separation score values, are in compartment B, and do not have CTCF occupancy (−−− category). Also, 13% of type IV boundaries show positive TAD-separation score values, are in compartment A and no CTCF occupancy (++− category), while only 2.4% of this category corresponds to type I boundaries. All boundary types share a similar proportion (∼36%) in an additional category with negative TAD-separation scores, located in A compartment and with no CTCF occupancy (−+− category), suggesting that other genomic features might contribute to the insulation capacity of the boundaries. Finally, we compared boundaries with the same characteristics regarding TAD-separation score, compartment assignment, and CTCF occupancy signal. The results show no significant differences in transcript accumulation between boundaries with the same attributes (except for type I and type IV boundaries in the −−− category) (Fig. 2F). These results suggest that the combination of CTCF signal intensity and transcript levels make a boundary more robust in structuring domains with type I boundaries having stronger accumulation of both features.

More conserved CTCF DNA-binding motifs are enriched in more accessible and robust TAD boundaries

As mentioned above, CTCF occupies domain boundaries and several reports have shown that domains disappear upon acute CTCF depletion [19]. In addition, the removal of CTCF DNA-binding sites results in boundary disruption and deregulation of gene transcription [6, 7, 43, 44]. However there are evidences of boundaries that are not affected by CTCF depletion and also there are boundaries that lack CTCF [11, 43].

To better understand the function of CTCF in boundary strength, we investigated in more detail CTCF occupancy at the different boundary types using the 5-kb boundaries. Using available CTCF ChIP-seq data, we identified the boundaries that have one or more CTCF peaks and boundaries that lack CTCF. Sixty-eight percent of type I boundaries have at least one peak of CTCF and this percentage gradually decreases for less accessible boundaries with 46% type IV boundaries occupied by CTCF (Fig. 3A). Most of the boundaries exhibit at least one peak of CTCF, however when comparing the number of CTCF peaks between type I and type IV boundaries, we found significantly more peaks at the type I boundaries (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. S4A).

We then measured chromatin accessibility using Fast-ATAC-seq data sets. Both type I and type IV boundaries with CTCF have higher accessibility than boundaries without CTCF (Fig. 3C). However, type I boundaries with or without CTCF show higher Fast-ATAC-seq signal overall compared to type IV, which is consistent with our boundary classification based on chromatin accessibility (Fig. 1E,F). The presence or absence of CTCF in the boundaries does not seem to result in more negative TAD-separation scores when comparing boundaries with the same accessibility degree (Fig. 3D). However, all type I boundaries are more efficient in blocking interactions across domains, regardless of the presence of CTCF (Fig. 3D and E). Taken together, our results suggest that in addition to CTCF other unknown factors can contribute to form domain boundaries. Also, for the boundaries occupied by CTCF, additional factors could make some boundaries more robust than others.

To determine whether the number of CTCF peaks drives boundary strength, several features including TAD-separation score, PC1 values, RNA read coverage, and the number of gene promoters comparing type I and IV boundaries with 0, 1, 2, 3, and so on number of CTCF peaks were analyzed. As already described, type I boundaries have a larger proportion of CTCF occupancy and this holds if having 1, 2, 3, or 4 CTCF peaks (Supplementary Fig. S4A). However, the number of CTCF peaks does not influence the TAD-separation scores. Nevertheless, a clear difference between type I and IV boundaries confirms that the more accessible boundaries are more robust insulators than the least accessible ones (Supplementary Fig. S4B). Fast-ATAC reads increase together with CTCF peak number however, type I boundaries display overall more accessibility reads than type IV (Supplementary Fig. S4C). The higher number of CTCF peaks at a boundary does not correlate with more positive PC1 values (compartment A) (Supplementary Fig. S4D), higher RNA read coverage (Supplementary Fig. S4E), or a greater number of gene promoters (Supplementary Fig. S4F). Thus, type I boundaries have more CTCF occupancy overall, chromatin accessibility, and insulation strength than type IV boundaries, and the number of CTCF peaks alone does not fully explain their insulation capacity.

Next, we explored the direction of CTCF DNA-binding motifs at the different boundary types to determine whether the directionality of CTCF binding is linked to boundary strength. It has been documented that the orientation of CTCF motifs is relevant to form chromatin loops and domains, as the convergent orientation of CTCF binding halts cohesin chromatin extrusion [21, 22]. Other studies have shown that inversion or disturbance of CTCF DNA-binding motif orientation results in decreased insulating capacity of the altered boundaries [43–46]. We identified the direction of CTCF motifs at type I–IV boundaries. Counting in 2-kb genomic windows along the 20-kb boundaries, we then plotted the frequency and orientation of the CTCF DNA-binding motif along the boundaries. The initial regions of type I boundaries (the first 10 kb of the total size of 20 kb) show a higher frequency of motifs in the reverse orientation, while the final sections of the boundary show a higher frequency of motifs in the forward orientation. Type II boundaries show a similar CTCF DNA binding site orientation distribution, but type III and type IV boundaries seem to have an even distribution of the CTCF DNA-binding motif orientation (Fig. 3F and G).

The CTCF DNA-binding motif is conserved among vertebrates [47]. We next analyzed the conservation level of the motifs embedded in the different boundary types based on the vertebrate conservation scores. We found that the CTCF motifs of type I boundaries are more conserved than the CTCF motifs of type II, III, and IV boundaries (Fig. 3H). This result suggests that more conserved CTCF DNA-binding motif might represent motifs with higher affinities or CTCF residence time than less conserved ones and this might contribute boundary strength together with the motif orientation (Fig. 3I).

TAD boundaries are occupied by CTCF, Cohesin, REST, ZNF316, and EMSY, among other proteins

Domain boundaries have been associated with several features and proteins. As mentioned, one of the main characteristics is the presence of CTCF and cohesin. In addition, there are reports that proteins such as YY1, RNA pol II, and open chromatin histone marks are also associated with domain boundaries [1, 18].

To further explore the proteome enriched at domain boundaries, we used public ChIP-seq data for 296 proteins in K562 cells and identified proteins occupying boundaries genome-wide (the “Data availability” section, Supplementary Table S4).

First, we counted the total number of proteins occupying boundaries (Supplementary Fig. S5A). Type I boundaries have more protein occupancy than types II, III, and IV boundaries. Type IV boundaries show the lowest protein occupancy. This result confirms that accessibility is a good proxy for protein occupancy. To identify which proteins are enriched at boundaries, we considered all boundaries classified as types I, II, III, and IV. As expected, and shown for the boundaries at 5-kb resolution above, CTCF was found in most type I boundaries (65%). The percentage of boundaries with CTCF decreased progressively in less accessible boundaries with only 47% of type IV boundaries occupied by CTCF (Supplementary Fig. S5B). Additionally, we identified a small percentage of boundaries without any of the proteins in the database used.

After CTCF, we found that the proteins that are most represented at type I, II, III, and IV boundaries are REST (RE1 Silencing Transcription Factor), followed by the zing finger protein ZNF316, the cohesin complex subunits SMC3 and RAD21 and EMSY (BRCA2 Interacting Transcriptional Repressor). There are no previous reports about the presence of REST, ZNF316, and EMSY at boundaries. We measured the enrichment of these proteins and found that they are more highly enriched at boundaries than at random regions (Supplementary Fig. S5C). We repeated this analysis in boundaries with and without CTCF. In the boundaries with CTCF, REST is the most prevalent protein. More than 75% of boundaries with CTCF show the presence of REST (Fig 4A). Interestingly, >45% of boundaries without CTCF are mainly occupied by ZNF316, suggesting that this protein may have a function in preserving a boundary in the absence of CTCF. Also, boundaries without CTCF are occupied by EMSY in at least 37% (Fig. 4B). Enrichment analysis of REST, ZNF316, and EMSY at the boundaries with and without CTCF showed that REST is enriched at boundaries with CTCF. In contrast, ZNF316 and EMSY show no statistical difference in boundaries with and without CTCF (Fig. 4C). Examples of type I and IV boundaries are shown together with the signal of CTCF, REST, ZNF316, and EMSY occupancy as well as the Fast-ATAC-seq signal (Fig. 4D).

To determine whether different protein combinations exist at the boundaries, we measured the presence of CTCF, REST, ZNF316, and EMSY at boundaries (Supplementary Fig. S5D and E). Of the boundaries with CTCF, the four proteins are present in ~42% of type I and type II boundaries. This proportion is lower at type III and type IV boundaries (∼32%). The second more representative combination of proteins is CTCF and REST. This combination is present in 31%–32% of type I–III boundaries and increases to 36% in type IV boundaries (Supplementary Fig. S5D). This combinatorial protein occupancy at boundaries shows that only a few boundaries are occupied by CTCF alone. This suggests that other proteins may contribute to boundaries insulation properties.

Among the boundaries without CTCF, we analyzed the combinatorial occupancy of the ZNF316, EMSY, and REST (Supplementary Fig. S5E). The combinatory occupancy by ZNF316 and EMSY is the second more representative; 27% of the type I boundaries without CTCF show ZNF316 and EMSY occupancy. This proportion decreases in boundaries with lower accessibility; however, it still being the most representative combination within each boundary classification type. The category with only ZNF316 occupancy represents between 8% and 12% of the type boundaries without CTCF. This does not discard the presence of other proteins at these boundaries, different to EMSY and REST. The most prevalent combination corresponds to other proteins because at least 45% of the boundaries lack CTCF, REST, EMSY, and ZNF316. Other enriched proteins such as NFIC, ATF2, ATF4, PKNOX1, and CEBPB may occupy these boundaries (Fig. 4B).

To confirm whether REST, ZNF316, and EMSY proteins are enriched at boundaries regardless of their accessibility level, we took the 5241 boundaries detected under the overnight digestion condition and iterated the analysis. As expected, CTCF was found at most of the boundaries (58%) (Supplementary Fig. S6A). However, other proteins were also present in these boundaries (99%) (Supplementary Fig. S6B). The protein occupancy of CTCF, REST, ZNF316, and EMSY is also recapitulated using the full boundary set (Supplementary Fig. S6C and D). It has been documented that, although CTCF is enriched at boundaries, just 15% of CTCF binding sites are located inside the boundaries, and this number varies depending on the Hi-C resolution [1]. We quantified the ChIP-seq peaks of the proteins CTCF, REST, ZNF316, and EMSY inside the boundaries. Just 10% of the total ChIP-seq peaks of CTCF are in boundaries; a similar percentage is displayed for REST and 5% for ZNF316 and EMSY (Supplementary Fig. S6E). These results support the idea that the insulation capacity of the boundary is due to the synergy of different elements.

Next, we analyzed the co-occupation between proteins at the boundaries considering co-binding if at least 1 bp of the ChIP-seq peaks overlap. As expected, CTCF co-localized with the cohesin complex subunits, and we found that CTCF and cohesin co-localize with REST very frequently. Interestingly, ZNF316 co-localizes primarily with EMSY without other factors (Supplementary Fig. S6F). Finally, to understand how regularly these proteins co-bind in the genome, we analyzed the co-occupancy of CTCF, REST, cohesin, ZNF316, and EMSY genome-wide in the same way as described for co-occupation at boundaries. We found that the profile found at boundaries is recapitulated at the ChIP-peak level genome wide, with CTCF, cohesin, and REST frequently found together and in a different protein module than ZNF316 and EMSY (Supplementary Fig. S6G). As ChIP-seq experiments are made in a population of cells, the question of whether these proteins are binding to the same single genome is still open for CTCF and REST, ZNF316, and EMSY. Single-cell and/or single-molecule experiments will be needed to fully answer this question.

Given that CTCF’s contribution to boundary strength lies in the orientation of its DNA-binding motifs, we also explored the orientation for the REST and ZNF316. First, we determined the number of the ChIP-seq peaks at the boundaries (Supplementary Fig. S7A). The number of peaks for these factors at boundaries is variable, ranging from 1 to 12 peaks per boundary. Then we identified the DNA-binding motifs for REST and ZNF316 (see the “Materials and methods” section) and their orientation. As for CTCF, the localization of the motifs was explored in windows of 2 kb across the 20-kb boundaries. The arrangement of motifs for ZNF316 and REST showed no apparent preference (Supplementary Fig. S7B). We then analyzed these proteins occupying both boundaries insulating a domain. We found that in many instances (>60%), CTCF, ZNF316, and REST occupy both boundaries forming a type I–I domain. A lesser proportion was found for less insulated type IV–IV domain boundaries (Supplementary Fig. S7C).

To explore whether REST, ZNF316, and EMSY are occupying loop anchors together with CTCF, we used previously reported loops in K562 cell line [9]. As expected, CTCF is present in most loop anchor regions (84%). REST, SMC3 and RAD21 proteins are also present in more than 60% of these genomic regions. ZNF316 is found at around 42% of loop anchors and EMSY appears less enriched with 34% (Supplementary Fig. S7D).

ZNF316 function has been described as a transcription factor [48]. EMSY has been described as a transcriptional repressor interacting with BRCA2 that is recruited to chromatin [49–52]. Our results suggest that ZNF316-EMSY module could also structure domains in the presence or absence of CTCF.

High accessibility boundaries are conserved across different cell types

To investigate whether boundary accessibility relates to specific gene functions and boundary conservation, we first classified domains according to their boundaries’ accessibility. We considered domains in which both boundaries are type I, II, III, or IV, as well as all possible combinations and identified their gene content. We focused on the proportion of 22% domains have type I boundaries at both sides and the 7% exhibit type IV boundaries (Supplementary Fig. S8A).

Domains with start and end type I boundaries (I–I) would represent the most stable domains since these boundaries isolate contacts more efficiently. On the contrary, we suppose that domains with start and end type IV boundaries (IV–IV) could be more dynamic. To explore the variety of genes located inside domains I–I and domains IV–IV, we identified the genes inside these domains, measured their expression levels, and determined their function by Gene Ontology. The expressed genes in domains I–I are involved in essential and constitutive cellular functions like DNA packing complex, chromatin, regulation of transcription, and signaling by G-protein-coupled receptors. Interestingly, also there are cell type-specific functions, for example hematopoiesis and immune system (Supplementary Fig. S8B). An example of this domain type houses one of the histone gene clusters and the BTN gene family, a subset of the immunoglobulins, all of which are highly expressed in K562s (Supplementary Fig. S8C). In contrast, another example of an I–I domain that contains genes that are not expressed in K562 cells harbors the olfactory receptor genes whose expression is tightly regulated during development (Supplementary Fig. S8C). The genes inside domains IV–IV that are transcribed display general ontological categories, and the non-expressed genes are for example genes whose function are related to epidermis and tissue development (Supplementary Fig. S8D). The developmental gene FOXP2 is in a domain with type IV boundaries at each side and it is not expressed in K562 cells (Supplementary Fig. S8E). We also measured the size of the different domain types and found that domains with type IV–IV boundaries are larger than type I–I domains (Supplementary Fig. S8F). Finally, we measured the proportion of expressed and non-expressed within domain and did not find a meaningful differences between domain types (Supplementary Fig. S8G).

Next, we assessed domain conservation across different human cell types. Several reports have shown that domain boundaries are conserved between cell types and species [1, 53, 54]. However, these vary greatly depending on the resolution of the Hi-C matrices [54]. To explore the conservation level of TADs with different accessibility degrees, we used published Hi-C data from different cell types and called domains and boundaries. We identified the conserved boundaries across different cell lines including GM12878, HCT116, HMEC, HUVEC, IMR90, NHEK, and K562, and classified them according to their accessibility type in K562 cells (Fig. 5A). We found that 54% of conserved boundaries are highly accessible type I boundaries, while only 19% are type IV (Fig. 5B).

Then we inferred protein occupancy at conserved boundaries and found that ZNF316, EMSY, and REST occupy >50% of type I and II boundaries together with CTCF (Fig. 5C). These results suggest that these factors could be participating in boundary formation also in other cell types.

Next, we analyzed domain conservation considering both boundaries and found 684 conserved domains across the studied cell lines (Fig. 5D). 37.6% of conserved domains correspond to I–I domains with type I boundaries on either side and only 1.8% correspond to IV–IV domains with type IV boundaries on either side (Fig. 5E).

Then we explored the gene content of conserved I–I domains. Gene Ontology enrichment analysis shows that these domains either contain highly expressed constitutive genes such the histone clusters and the kinase gene MAP3K1 (Supplementary Fig. S8C and Fig. 5F and G) or non-expressed genes that require tight gene expression regulation such as olfactory receptors-coding genes (Fig. 5H and I).

These results suggest that domains with highly accessible and robust boundaries are the most conserved between different cell types and isolate highly active transcriptional units of constitutive genes or genes that need strict gene expression regulation in certain tissues and that these structures are preserved in different cells even if they are not being expressed.

ZNF316 and EMSY participate in the formation and maintenance of domain boundaries

Our data suggest that ZNF316 might have a function in boundary formation as it is present in 76% of the boundaries detected (Fig. 6A). To explore whether ZNF316 per se can participate in boundary formation, we selected a high accessibility boundary without CTCF and designed RNA guides to eliminate its DNA-binding site by CRISPR–Cas9 technology. We generated a homozygous mutant with a 612-bp deletion containing the ZNF316 DNA-binding site. Our design edition also includes a ChIP-seq peak for EMSY that overlaps with ZNF316 occupancy (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. S9A and B). Next, we measured chromatin interactions using Hi-C. The Hi-C interaction matrix shows that the edited boundary does not disappear. However, inter-domain contacts that cross the edited boundary are gained. Also, the mutant exhibits a new interaction stripe absent in the control (Fig. 6C). The differential matrix shows that when the ZNF316 DNA-binding site is deleted at the selected boundary, there is a general local increase in contact frequency (Supplementary Fig. S9C). The TAD-separation score in the plotted region shows the affection in the edited boundary, as the control has a more robust score than the mutant (Fig. 6D and E). In contrast, the TAD-separation scores at a control region upstream of the edited area on the same chromosome behave similarly in both the control and mutant cells (Supplementary Fig. S9D). We next quantified the contacts made from the edited region in the WT and mutant cells and found a reduction in contacts arising from the edited region in the mutant compared to the WT (Supplementary Fig. S9E, above) in contrast to two control regions (one upstream of the edited genomic region and one in a different chromosome), from which the number of contacts is equivalent between the WT and mutant cells (Supplementary Fig. S9E, below).

Next, we measured the contact number from gene promoters of genes flanking the edited boundary, and contacts are more numerous in the mutant than in the WT. For example, the SLC7A11 (Solute Carrier Family 7 Member 11) and ELF2 (E74 Like ETS Transcription Factor 2) genes located upstream and downstream of the altered boundary present a gain in interactions in the mutant in contrast with the control (Fig. 6F and G). By performing a virtual 4C, the interaction profile shows increased contacts in the areas around the promoter, and some of these interactions are made with enhancers (Fig. 6H). We quantified the expression levels of these genes and observed in increased expression in the mutant compared to the control (Fig. 6I). Other genes surrounding the edited boundary also display more contacts in the mutant versus the control cells in contrast to a non-edited genomic region used as a control (Supplementary Fig. S9F). Our results show that the ZNF316/EMSY module contributes to the insulating function at a boundary without CTCF impeding interactions across domains.

Discussion

Domains are essential for organizing the genome in chromatin neighborhoods and regulating gene expression in time and space. Domain boundaries are critical regions that delimit and constrain chromatin contacts. Many reports have demonstrated that genetic boundary alterations can have a functional impact on gene expression regulation by either forming or restricting chromatin contacts between regulatory elements such as enhancers and promoters [3, 55].

In this work, we experimentally combined chromatin accessibility and Hi-C, to identify and classify domain boundaries according to their accessibility as a proxy for protein occupancy, to then describe in detail their genomic features. To do so, we modified the restriction enzyme incubation time points in the Hi-C assay, to gradually digest chromatin based on its accessibility. Finally, we identified TADs and classified the boundaries into four accessibility types. To give a detailed description of the genomic features of domain boundaries, we focused on the most accessible boundaries (type I) and contrasted them with the least accessible boundaries (type IV). Type I boundaries are occupied by more proteins and result in better insulators of chromatin contacts compared to type IV boundaries, with lower TAD-separation scores and fewer inter-TAD contacts crossing the boundary.

Although CTCF function in domain boundary formation and loop extrusion has been extensively documented [19, 21, 22, 25, 56–59], some reports show evidence of domain boundaries that are CTCF-independent [19, 43]. Analyzing CTCF occupancy considering our different boundary types, we found that 68% and 46% of type I and type IV boundaries, respectively, are occupied by CTCF. Interestingly, the TAD-separation score and the number of interactions blocked by type I boundaries with or without CTCF are equivalent, suggesting other factors contribute to boundary insulation properties besides CTCF.

The convergent CTCF motif orientation at loop or domain anchors is necessary to efficiently block the cohesin complex according to the loop extrusion model [21, 23, 24]. Boundaries with the highest accessibility showed this divergent pattern of CTCF binding motifs, in contrast with the lowest accessibility boundaries, suggesting that this arrangement is relevant to the insulating properties of the boundary. Interestingly, we found that the conservation score of the CTCF DNA-binding site in type I boundaries is higher than the motif found in type IV boundaries. This might reflect CTCF binding affinity or residence time at the more conserved motif giving the boundary more stability.

To characterize which other proteins occupy boundaries we explored ChIP-seq data in K562 cells and found that REST, ZNF316, and EMSY occupy >60% of the boundaries with CTCF. Our data also found SMC3 and RAD21 and other previously reported proteins occupying boundary elements [18]. CTCF and cohesin commonly co-localize with REST, and ZNF316 often co-localizes with EMSY at boundaries, constituting two independent protein modules.

The identification of distinct protein modules at domain boundaries—CTCF/cohesin/REST and ZNF316/EMSY—is consistent with the broader principle that transcription factors operate in combinatorial, context-dependent assemblies at regulatory genomic regions. performed a large-scale analysis of trans-acting factor colocalization in K562 cells using self-organizing maps trained on 128 ChIP-seq data sets, revealing that TFs form specific colocalization patterns driven either by independent DNA binding or by stable protein–protein interactions within complexes [60]. Their integrative proteomics approach demonstrated that CTCF, RAD21, and SMC3 consistently co-occur as a cohesive module and that additional zinc finger proteins frequently associate with this complex at genomic loci. Our finding that ZNF316 and EMSY form an independent module, frequently present at boundaries lacking CTCF, extends this logic, suggesting that alternative TF complexes can sustain boundary insulation through mechanisms that do not require canonical CTCF/cohesin loop extrusion machinery.

Finally, we functionally tested ZNF316 and EMSY function at boundaries by CRISPR–Cas9-mediated edition of a ZNF316 DNA-binding site in which both ZNF316 and EMSY are present at a selected highly accessible boundary without CTCF. The edition increases intra-domain contacts in contrast to the non-edited control. Also, there is an increase in the inter-domain contacts from the promoters surrounding the edited boundary, some with enhancers. For some of these genes, we detected an upregulation in gene expression in the mutant compared to the control. Our results show that the ZNF316-EMSY protein module contributes to domain boundary formation without CTCF.
