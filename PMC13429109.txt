Comparison of Academic Performance and Preference for Teaching Modality Among Health Professions Students Taught Physiology Modules in the Rwandan Public University
Authors: Abdullateef Isiaka Alagbonsi, Mojeed Akorede Gbadamosi, Mathieu Nemerimana, Makinde Vincent Olubiyi, Olufunke Onaadepo, Akaninyene Ubong Ime, Abdul-Rahuf Aderemi Feyitimi, Ruth Wanjiru Mungai, Florent Rutagarama, Danilo Milanes Zambrano, Noah Adavize Omeiza, Simiat Olanike Elias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile online learning offers flexibility and expanded access to educational resources, concerns remain regarding its ability to support the complex conceptual understanding required in physiology education. We compared academic performance and preferences for teaching modality among undergraduate health professions students at University of Rwanda (UR).MethodsA cross-sectional comparative study using historical cohorts was conducted among undergraduate students enrolled in Medicine, Dentistry, Pharmacy, and Clinical Psychology programmes at UR. Academic performance data from students who completed physiology modules during online teaching (2020–2021) were compared with those from students who completed equivalent modules during face-to-face teaching (2022 onward). Mean Assessment Scores (MAS) obtained from official academic records were analysed using factorial analysis of covariance, adjusting for sex and programme. Student preferences regarding teaching modality were assessed using a structured questionnaire and analysed using logistic regression models.ResultsA total of 528 students participated in the preference survey, while 516 students were included in MAS analysis. Overall, teaching modality was not significantly associated with MAS after adjustment for sex and programme (p=0.349). However, a significant interaction between teaching modality and academic programme was observed (p<0.001). Online teaching was associated with higher adjusted scores among medical students, whereas face-to-face teaching was associated with higher adjusted scores among dental students. No significant difference was observed among pharmacy students. Most students preferred face-to-face teaching, although preferences varied across programmes.ConclusionAcademic performance in physiology did not differ substantially across teaching modalities after adjustment for programme and sex. However, the association between teaching modality and performance varied across academic programmes, suggesting that programme context may influence how students benefit from different instructional approaches. Most students preferred face-to-face learning despite comparable overall academic outcomes. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because the comparison involved non-contemporaneous historical cohorts exposed to different educational, assessment, and contextual conditions.Keywords: academic performance, face-to-face teaching, health sciences, online teaching, physiology, Rwanda

Introduction

Digital technologies have transformed higher education globally and have accelerated the adoption of online learning across health professions education.1,2 Although online teaching offers flexibility, accessibility, and opportunities for self-directed learning, evidence regarding its effectiveness in supporting learning outcomes remains inconsistent. A meta-analysis of online and blended learning reported outcomes comparable to those achieved through traditional face-to-face instruction when effective instructional strategies were employed.3 However, subsequent studies in physiology and health professions education have produced mixed findings, with some reporting comparable academic performance and student satisfaction across modalities.4 In contrast, others have identified challenges related to engagement, practical learning experiences, and perceived learning effectiveness.5–11 The rapid transition to emergency remote teaching during the COVID-19 pandemic further intensified interest in understanding the educational consequences of online instruction, particularly in disciplines such as physiology that require integration of foundational scientific knowledge with clinical application.12–20 As Physiology is often perceived by students as a challenging discipline that requires understanding complex, dynamic relationships among biological systems, concerns remain regarding whether online learning environments can adequately support the depth of engagement required for effective physiology education,4,21,22 especially in this era when competency-based physiology curriculum is being encouraged in the East African region and elsewhere.19,20,23

At the University of Rwanda (UR), physiology teaching underwent substantial changes during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. During the pandemic period (2020–2021), the university implemented online teaching through the Moodle learning management system, which supported both synchronous and asynchronous learning activities.4 Following the resumption of normal academic activities in 2022, physiology teaching reverted predominantly to face-to-face delivery, despite the Higher Education Council of Rwanda approving blended learning modalities. Academic leaders and educators in many settings remain cautious about integrating online teaching into health professions education due to concerns about educational quality, student engagement, and the acquisition of practical competencies.24–29 In East Africa, these concerns are compounded by infrastructure challenges, including unreliable internet connectivity, inconsistent electricity supply, and limited access to digital devices.30,31 Although recent national initiatives have sought to strengthen Rwanda’s digital learning capacity, empirical evidence on the impact of online physiology teaching remains limited. A pioneering study among Rwandan health professions students reported that students perceived online teaching to be as effective as face-to-face instruction, with perceived effectiveness varying according to experiential factors and academic year but not programme.4 However, that study did not examine actual academic performance or preferences for teaching modality, thereby limiting its usefulness for evidence-based educational planning.

Comparability of assessment across teaching periods is an important consideration when evaluating educational outcomes. At UR, physiology modules are delivered in accordance with institutionally approved course specifications that define learning outcomes, assessment weightings, and examination formats. Although instructional delivery changed between the emergency remote teaching period (2020–2021) and the post-pandemic face-to-face period (2022 onward), institutional assessment policies, module objectives, and overall assessment structures were maintained.4 Nevertheless, differences in learning environments, examination administration, and pandemic-related contextual factors may have influenced student performance independently of teaching modality. Programme-specific differences may also influence the educational impact of teaching modality. Medical and dental students complete physiology through sequential modules distributed across multiple semesters, whereas pharmacy students undertake a comprehensive integrated physiology module covering all major physiological systems. Furthermore, programmes differ in workload structure, opportunities for practical application, and reliance on psychomotor and clinically oriented learning experiences.32–34 These differences influence how students engage with online and face-to-face learning environments and may contribute to programme-specific variation in academic performance. Consequently, we hypothesised that the relationship between teaching modality and academic performance would differ across academic programmes. To strengthen the regional data on physiology education, this study compared academic performance and teaching modality preferences among undergraduate health professions students at UR and determined whether the relationship between teaching modality and academic performance varies across academic programmes.

Methodology

Study Design, Population, and Sampling

This study employed a cross-sectional comparative design using historical cohorts. Undergraduate health professions students at UR who had completed at least one physiology module during either the online teaching period (2020–2021) or the face-to-face teaching period (2022 onward) were eligible for inclusion. Programmes included Bachelor of Science (BSc) in Clinical Psychology, Bachelor of Pharmacy (BPharm), Doctor of Dental Surgery (DDS), and Bachelor of Medicine and Bachelor of Surgery (MBBS). Students who had withdrawn from their programmes were excluded. Convenience sampling was used for the questionnaire survey, yielding a response rate of 96% (n = 528). Fourteen physiology lecturers also participated in the survey.

Academic performance data were extracted from official university records for students who completed physiology modules during the online teaching period (2020–2021) and from comparable cohorts who completed the same modules during the face-to-face teaching period (2022 onward). Throughout the study period, continuous assessment accounted for 40% of the final module score, and end-of-module examinations accounted for 60%. These records were used to obtain Mean Assessment Scores (MAS) for the academic performance analysis. Table 1 summarises the characteristics of the physiology modules included in the study and justifies their comparability. The academic performance analysis used MAS from Physiology I for MBBS and DDS students (the first physiology module completed in Year 1, Semester 1) and Human Physiology for BPharm students. Clinical Psychology students were excluded from the academic performance analysis because their physiology module included substantial anatomy content and covered only endocrine and neurophysiology topics, making it insufficiently comparable with the modules included from other programmes.

Variables

Primary Outcomes

Academic performance: Academic performance was assessed using MAS obtained from official university academic records maintained by the Office of the Deputy Dean for Undergraduates, following approval by the College Academic Council. The MAS represented the final approved module score awarded to each student following completion of all required assessments. Across the included programmes, final module scores were derived using a standardised institutional weighting system, with continuous assessment activities contributing 40% and end-of-module examinations contributing 60% of the overall score. Continuous assessment included quizzes, assignments, practical exercises, and other programme-specific formative assessments conducted during the semester. The approved final module score was used in the analysis to maximise comparability across cohorts and programmes. For the academic performance analysis, records of students who completed physiology modules during the online teaching period (2020–2021) were compared with those of comparable cohorts who completed the same modules during the face-to-face teaching period (2022 onward).

Ethical Approval, Data Collection Tool, and Procedure

Before commencement of the study, ethical approval was obtained from the University of Rwanda College of Medicine and Health Sciences Institutional Review Board (UR-CMHS IRB) (Approval No. 510/CMHS IRB/2024). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Before participation, the study objectives and procedures were explained to all participants, and written informed consent was obtained.

The questionnaire was piloted among 5% (n = 30) of eligible students to assess clarity, validity, and reliability. Feedback from the pilot resulted in refinements to several items, including clarifying students’ current academic level versus the academic year in which physiology was studied and specifying that academic performance data would be obtained from official university records rather than self-reported scores. Pilot participants were excluded from the main study. Following concept identification through a literature review, the questionnaire underwent content validation by eight subject-matter experts: five physiology educators, one curriculum designer, and two biostatisticians. The instrument demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.84). Test–retest reliability was assessed among pilot participants over a two-week interval and demonstrated excellent stability (Pearson’s r = 0.87; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.78–0.93). The final questionnaire comprised 43 items covering participant demographics, experiences with different teaching modalities, and preferences for teaching modalities. Only variables relevant to the objectives of the present study are reported herein (Supplementary Figure S1). Data were collected electronically using KoboToolbox. Students and lecturers completed the questionnaire using smartphones, tablets, or computers. Information collected included age, sex, academic programme, current academic level, academic year in which physiology was studied, pre-university educational background, experiences with different teaching modalities, and preferred mode of instruction.

Statistical Methods

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were summarised using means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges, as appropriate, whereas categorical variables were summarised using frequencies and percentages. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

For the academic performance analysis, a full-factorial analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) model was fitted with teaching modality, academic programme, and their interaction included as fixed factors. Sex was included as a categorical factor because of its potential association with academic performance. Assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed before analysis. Because Levene’s test indicated heteroscedasticity, bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap procedures with 1000 resamples were used to generate robust confidence intervals and significance estimates. Estimated marginal means with 95% confidence intervals were computed for each teaching modality and programme combination. Significant interaction effects were examined using programme-specific simple-effect analyses.

Teaching modality preference was analysed using binary logistic regression. Preference for online teaching was coded as 1 and preference for face-to-face teaching as 0. The questionnaire required participants to select one preferred modality and therefore did not include a “no preference” category. Variables associated with preference at p < 0.20 in bivariate analyses were entered into multivariable logistic regression models. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals were reported. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and Nagelkerke R2 statistics. All analyses followed STROBE guidelines.35

Teaching Modality Preference

Of the 528 students, 287 (54.4%) preferred face-to-face teaching and 241 (45.6%) preferred online teaching. We used binary logistic regression to identify factors associated with preferring online teaching. Table 5 presents Model 1 (student-level factors only). In univariable analysis, students in higher academic years (3–5) had lower odds of preferring online teaching compared to those in years 1–2 (crude OR = 0.49; 95% CI: 0.26–0.95; p = 0.033). Pharmacy students had higher odds of preferring online teaching than medical students (crude OR = 2.22; 95% CI: 1.29–3.82; p = 0.004). After adjusting for current academic level, pharmacy students maintained higher odds of preferring online teaching (adjusted OR [aOR] = 2.47; 95% CI: 1.38–4.42; p = 0.002). The association with academic year also remained significant (aOR = 0.46; 95% CI: 0.23–0.93; p = 0.030). The model showed acceptable fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow χ2(8) = 5.83, p = 0.666; Nagelkerke R2 = 0.075).

Discussion

This study compared academic performance and teaching modality preferences among health professions students who studied physiology through either online or face-to-face instruction. The principal findings were that: (1) teaching modality alone did not significantly affect academic performance, but its effect differed across academic programmes; (2) medical students performed better following online instruction, whereas dental students performed better following face-to-face instruction; (3) no significant difference in performance was observed among pharmacy students; and (4) pharmacy students demonstrated a significantly greater preference for online learning independent of lecturer-related characteristics. These findings show that the effectiveness of online learning is context-dependent, like the programme-specific curricular characteristics and learning requirements, rather than being universally beneficial or detrimental, and should be interpreted within the context of a historical cohort comparison.

In the global context, there are conflicting reports about how teaching modalities affect the academic performance of students in health sciences modules. For instance, students performed better in physiology courses delivered via blended mode than via traditional didactic mode, while performance in physiology courses taught solely online was not better than that taught via traditional didactic mode.3,5 In the USA, nursing students in their first year, who are not yet grounded in human biology, found it difficult to understand complex subjects like physiology using the online mode.6,7 In line with these studies, a more recent German study reported that preclinical students who studied physiology courses via face-to-face modality performed better in 14 out of 15 subjects than those who studied the courses via online mode.8 Savitha et al9 reported that the learning outcomes, pass percentage, and mean scores were not significantly different among Indian students who studied physiology using offline modality compared to those who did so online. Studies in Thailand10 and Bahrain11 also reported the same academic outcomes among students who learned physiology practical entirely online and those who learned it via traditional didactic methods. In our present study, the absence of a significant overall effect of teaching modality is consistent with previous studies conducted in India, Thailand, Bahrain, and other settings, which reported comparable learning outcomes between online and face-to-face physiology education.5–7 However, our finding that the relationship between teaching modality and performance varies across academic programmes adds an important dimension to the literature. It represents one of the first reports of programme-specific modality effects in the East African context.

Notwithstanding this peculiarity, we acknowledge that differences in the program-level teaching and assessment structures and motivation of the students might influence the MAS across programs. The observed crossover interaction suggests that the most effective teaching modality may differ across disciplines because of differences in curriculum structure, learning requirements, and opportunities for practical application. Medical students complete physiology through multiple sequential modules and may benefit from the flexibility, self-paced learning, and repeated exposure offered by online environments. In contrast, dental students may derive greater benefit from face-to-face teaching because of the close integration of physiology with practical, laboratory, and clinically oriented learning activities. Pharmacy students complete a comprehensive integrated physiology module and may therefore adapt equally well to either modality. These explanations remain speculative and require validation through future studies specifically designed to investigate programme-level mechanisms underlying modality effects.3,5–11

It is widely reported that nowadays students come to the universities digitally literate, making them prefer accessing learning materials at their own comfort and pace.36–38 Students’ perception of their learning experiences correlates with their successes in human biosciences, the subject’s teaching quality, content overwhelmingness, and level of support received by tutors.39 Recently, Indian nursing students preferred blended or face-to-face teaching of physiology modules to the online mode, even though they admitted to the comfort and effectiveness of the online mode. These students highlighted the advantages of face-to-face teaching to include higher interactions, concentration, and interest, better subject understanding and information, and easier note-taking.9 Also, students in Thailand preferred didactic physiology practical sessions to those delivered via online mode.10 A recent study among pharmacy students in Iraq40 and Saudi Arabia41 showed that they preferred face-to-face over online learning in a module-specific manner. For instance, they preferred online learning in courses perceived to involve less rigorous coursework (eg, medicinal chemistry, pharmacoeconomics, and pharmacology) than in those requiring patient contact (eg, hospital training, therapeutics, and pharmaceutics). Among those who preferred online learning, the majority preferred synchronous to asynchronous learning.40 Conversely, medical students in Bahrain were satisfied with the asynchronous online modality for cardiovascular physiology teaching via recorded PowerPoint, as it makes their studying flexible.11 In another study, students viewed recorded lectures (asynchronous online teaching) to be useful as a revision tool that is not a replacement for live lectures (synchronous online teaching).42 Elsewhere, many medical students reportedly found online asynchronous teaching equally or more effective than in-person lectures.4,43

In the present study, student preferences also appeared to vary by academic programme. Pharmacy students demonstrated a stronger preference for online learning, which may reflect the value of flexible access to recorded materials and self-directed review opportunities in a content-intensive curriculum. At UR, the online physiology instruction combined both synchronous elements, such as video conferencing, and asynchronous elements, including recorded lectures, curated digital resources, automated quizzes, and discussion forums. Conversely, medical students showed a stronger preference for face-to-face instruction, potentially reflecting the perceived benefits of direct interaction with lecturers and peers across multiple physiology modules.11,40–43 The face-to-face modality for Medicine and Dentistry students emphasized interactive activities, such as live lectures, small-group discussions, presentations, and practical demonstrations. This modality’s hands-on, interactive nature likely provides a significant advantage for Dentistry students, whose field is highly psychomotor and visual, and Medicine students who value the direct engagement for mastering complex physiological concepts. These findings highlight the importance of considering student preferences alongside objective learning outcomes when designing instructional strategies, and also suggest that preference is likely not for the modality per se, but for the specific learning activities it enables, which are perceived to be of differential value across academic disciplines. Therefore, it would be ideal to assess students’ preferences in an academic program and tailor their preferred teaching modality to their needs. This will avoid implementing the same teaching modality for students in different programs that will not have the same context in terms of the nature of their modules.

Although statistically significant, the interaction effect was modest in magnitude (partial η2 = 0.029), indicating a small effect size. Furthermore, programme-specific differences ranged from approximately 2.5 to 4.3 percentage points on a 100-point scale. While these differences may not translate into substantial changes in academic standing, the consistency of the observed pattern suggests that programme characteristics may influence how students respond to different instructional modalities.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the use of non-contemporaneous historical cohorts (online teaching during 2020–2021 and face-to-face teaching during 2022 onward) introduces the possibility of residual confounding. Although institutional assessment policies, learning outcomes, and overall assessment structures remained unchanged throughout the study period, students were exposed to varied educational, societal, and pandemic-related circumstances. Consequently, differences in academic performance cannot be attributed exclusively to programs and teaching modality, and factors such as pandemic-related stressors, differences in examination conditions, and temporal changes in student preparedness may have influenced outcomes.24–26,26 Second, convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias, although the high response rate (96%) reduces this concern. Third, the relatively small sample size among dental students reduced statistical precision and led to wider confidence intervals for programme-specific estimates. Fourth, student-level linkage to individual lecturers was not available, preventing the use of multilevel modelling to account for potential clustering by instructor. Fifth, teaching modality preferences were self-reported and may therefore be susceptible to recall or response bias.4 Finally, the study was conducted at a single public university, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other institutions and educational settings. Future studies using contemporaneous cohorts, longitudinal designs, or experimental educational interventions would provide stronger evidence regarding causal relationships between teaching modality and academic performance.

Strengths

The study also has several strengths. Academic performance was assessed using official university records rather than self-reported grades, thereby reducing information bias. The use of bootstrap procedures enhanced the robustness of statistical inference despite heteroscedasticity. The study included a large sample of students and represents one of the first investigations in East Africa to examine programme-specific differences in both teaching modality effectiveness and student preferences within physiology education.

Conclusion

Teaching modality alone does not independently influence academic performance in physiology among Rwandan health sciences students. However, the academic programme modifies this effect: Medicine students perform better online, Dentistry students perform better face to face, and Pharmacy students show no difference. Preference for online teaching is also programme dependent, being notably higher among pharmacy students. Therefore, academic leaders and policymakers should remove barriers to blended learning and tailor teaching strategies to programme-specific needs rather than adopting a one-size-fits-all approach. Future research should use contemporaneous cohorts or randomised designs to confirm these findings and explore educator perspectives on online teaching in African settings.
