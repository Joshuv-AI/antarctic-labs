Comparative study of TiO2 and graphene oxide enhancement of the removal of methylene blue dye using PVA/PEO hydrogels synthesized by gamma irradiation
Authors: Mai M. Mohamed, Asmaa Sayed, Dalia E. Hegazy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Water pollution from industrial effluents, particularly textile dyes, represents a significant environmental challenge. In this study, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA)/polyethylene oxide (PEO)/poly(acrylic acid) (PAAc) hydrogels were synthesized via gamma irradiation (30 kGy) and modified with titanium dioxide (TiO₂) and graphene oxide (GO) nanoparticles to enhance methylene blue (MB) dye removal. Comprehensive characterization using FTIR, XRD, AFM, SEM, and TGA confirmed the successful incorporation of the nanofillers and their influence on the structural and physicochemical properties of the hydrogels. The addition of TiO₂ and GO significantly improved the gel fraction from 65% (neat hydrogel) to 99% and 98%, respectively. Swelling behavior showed a pH-dependent response, with maximum swelling observed at pH 10 after 24 h, following the order: PVA/PEO/PAAc < PVA/PEO/PAAc/TiO₂ < PVA/PEO/PAAc/GO. Batch adsorption experiments were conducted under controlled conditions (pH 10, adsorbent dosage 0.1 g, initial MB concentration 20 mg/L, and contact time 24 h). The removal efficiencies reached 64%, 98%, and ~ 100% for PVA/PEO/PAAc, PVA/PEO/PAAc/TiO₂, and PVA/PEO/PAAc/GO, respectively. Adsorption kinetics were better described by the pseudo-first-order model, indicating that physisorption plays a dominant role in the rate-controlling step. Isotherm analysis showed good agreement with the Langmuir model, suggesting predominantly monolayer adsorption on relatively homogeneous surfaces. Overall, both TiO₂- and GO-reinforced hydrogels exhibited enhanced adsorption performance compared to the neat hydrogel. The superior performance of GO-based hydrogels is attributed to their higher surface area and abundance of oxygen-containing functional groups, which facilitate dye–adsorbent interactions. These findings demonstrate the potential of radiation-synthesized nanocomposite hydrogels as efficient materials for dye removal, while further studies are required to evaluate their performance under realistic wastewater conditions.

Introduction

Organic dyes are among the most polluted species that discharged into water as effluents of many industries such as tanning, painting, ceramics and electronics [1, 2]. The most determinative effect of dyes to environment is that they prevent the sunlight from passing deep into aquatic flora and Fauna and cause significant depletion in the green coverage layer [3]. Moreover, they are highly harmful to human health and can induce several acute diseases [4–6].

Due to their high water solubility and ease of interaction with negatively charged cell membrane surfaces, cationic dyes, such as Methylene blue, are more hazardous than anionic dyes and concentrate in the cytoplasm of cells [7]. Several methodologies are applied to eliminate dyes from aqueous solutions. They include chemical oxidation [8], photocatalytic degradation [9, 10], precipitation and coagulation [11], membrane techniques [12], biological treatment [13]and adsorption onto different sorbents [14–17].Generally, treatment by adsorption finds strong potential over other methods due to ease of application, abundancy of different sorbents, recyclability of sorbents and generation of no wastes or toxic materials [18, 19] .

On the other hand, hydrogel sorbents are being superior over other sorbents due to their great ability of absorbing and retaining huge amounts of adsorbates [20, 21].

Hydrophilic polymers are cross-linked chemically and/or physically to form hydrogels, three-dimensional networks with improved absorption and retention capabilities [22, 23]. They have ionic functional groups, allowing them to absorb and retain ionic dyes from wastewaters, like MB.

Recently, hydrogel nanocomposites are used in wastewater treatment for enhanced adsorption due to their ability to modify the physical and catalytic properties of nanocomposite by tuned the materials with tailored properties such as large surface area resulted from incorporation of nanoparticles inside hydrogel matrix [24], reactivity and morphology. Hydrogels are generally made from natural polymers or green polymer. Poly (vinyl alcohol) is a green polymer with pronounced importance in different fields, such as packaging [25, 26], pharmaceutical [27, 28] and industrial applications [29, 30].

In wastewater treatment, Poly (vinyl alcohol) has been combined with other constituents to give rise up excellent formulations for removal of different pollutants [31, 32]. In this regard, PVA/ Arabic gum hydrogels were prepared via gamma irradiation for removal of heavy metals from wastewater [33].Qudah et al. prepared some crosslinked poly(vinyl alcohol)-based hydrogels for removal of heavy metals from simulated solutions [34].

Additionally, by using gamma-induced radiation polymerization, poly (acrylic, polyvinyl alcohol) copolymer hydrogels with different PVA (polyvinyl alcohol) contents were created. This mixture was applied to the purifying of water [35].

However, combination of poly (vinyl alcohol) hydrogels with carbonaceous and inorganic materials into composite formulations for wastewater treatment application is considered a recent approach. Under visible light illumination, Liu et al. used an Axylan/PVA/TiO2 hybrid material as a photocatalyst for the photocatalytic decolorization of Astrazon brilliant red-4G and ethyl-violet dyes [36], Alireza et al. enhanced photocatalytic efficiency of 81.47% for oxytetracycline degradation (OTC) using gray-shaded titania. This modified titania demonstrated better visible-light photocatalytic activity compared to pristine P25, attaining 81.47% degradation efficiency during degradation tests [37]. In another work, SDS/PVA/ZnO hydrogel composite was used as an active catalyst for the photodegradation of reactive red azo dye [38].

Zinc oxide (ZnO) particle-loaded polyvinyl alcohol/cellulose nanofibril (PVA-CNF) aerogel nanocomposites were created, according to [39].Methylene blue (MB) was sequestered in aqueous solution using the aerogels through immediate adsorption and catalytic breakdown with sodium borohydride. On the other hand, a special type of water-soluble, aerobically biodegradable thermoplastics is called poly (ethylene oxide, or PEO). PVA’s main hydroxyl group has the potential to create a hydrogen bond with PEO’s ether oxygen [40]. Because acrylic acid can absorb more than five to ten times its weight in water, it was added because it is the foundation of a class of substances known as super absorbents [41]. The addition of AAc enhanced the polymer’s ionizable groups. In order to increase the interaction between the cationic groups of MB dye and the functionalities of adsorbents, nanocomposites comprise a lot of carboxyl groups [42]. One of the most likely functional adsorption materials that can now be used to clean many different sorts of contaminated water sources is graphene-based hydrogels [43]. Due to its low cost of mass production, great water dispersibility, high surface area, and strong adsorption capacity, Graphene oxide (GO) is currently one of the most often used inorganic cores in composite synthesis. GO is typically a two-dimensional, one atom thick carbonaceous substance. It has many hydroxyl, epoxy, carbonyl, and carboxyl COOH groups, as well as other hydrophilic oxygenated functional groups. These compounds increase GO’s miscibility with the polymer matrix and make it hydrophilic [44]. Titanium dioxide (TiO2) plays a predominant role in polymeric hydrogel synthesis due to its non-toxicity, low cost and inert chemical properties and can be used in removing several types of organic poll. The electron rich Titanium dioxide and Graphene oxide capable of interacting with copolymer containing acrylate carbonyl group [45], so enhancing the polymer properties and introducing high surface area to improve the adsorption process. Therefore, a new combination between the previously mentioned candidates is introduced as effective sorbents for MB dye. In this contest, the preparation of Poly vinylalcohol ( PVA) /Polyethylene oxide(PEO) /Polyacrylic acid (PAAc) hydrogels reinforced separately with Titanium dioxide (TiO₂) and Graphene oxide (GO) nanoparticles using gamma irradiation is discussed. Gamma radiation is an effective facile technique for inducing crosslinking by generating numerous radical species, such as hydroxyl (•OH) and hydrogen (•H) radicals. Following their attacking on the polymer chains, these radicals generate a number of polymer macroradicals that take part in both intra- and intermolecular free-radical recombination operations [46]. Finally, a stable crosslinked polymeric network was produced [47].The prepared polymeric formulations were characterized by FTIR, XRD and AFM analyses and their adsorption capacity towards methylene blue dye was verified under different operating conditions.

Materials and experiment

Materials

Acrylic acid (C3H4O2) AAc stabilized with 200 ppm MEHQ 99.55%, purchased from ACROS ORGANICS, New Jersy, USA. Poly (vinyl alcohol) (C2H4O)n PVA, purchased from BLULUX Laboratories (p) Ltd., INDIA, M.W. app. 14,000 LR [Laboratory Rasayan s.d.finf-chem Ltd, Boisar 401501]. Polyethylene oxide (PEO) Mw 400.000 was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Co. Methylene bisacrylamide MBAM was purchased from Oxford Laboratory Reagent, India, as an AR. Titanium dioxide TiO2 (nano-powder) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Co. with an average particle size of 21 nm. Graphene oxide (GO) was purchased from Qualikems Laboratory Reagent India (average size 1–3 μm and thickness 1–5 nm). A Cationic dye was used as a model for application: Methylene blue (MB) C25H30ClN3, M.W. = 407.99, purchased from Qualikems Fine Chemicals Pvt. Ltd., New Delhi − 110 060, absorption max = 663 nm. All chemicals were used as received without further purification. Irradiation of the samples was carried out using a 60Co gamma source in Egypt, with an average dose rate of 2.70 kGy/h and a total dose of 30 kGy, which is adequate for preparing a stable hydrogel.

Preparation of PVA/PEO/PAAc/TiO2 and PVA/PEO/PAAc/GO Nanocomposite hydrogels

A Simple preparation method was applied. First,1 g of PVA was dissolved in 50 ml of distilled water under continuous stirring at 70 °C for 2 h, while 1 g of PEO was dissolved in 50 ml of distilled water. The two solutions were then mixed. Subsequently, 7.5 ml of AAc was added to the PVA/PEO solution, and the mixture was stirred until a completely homogeneous solution was obtained. A high percentage of vinyl monomers in the copolymer solution may lead to the formation of a three-dimensional crosslinked hydrogel [48].

Methylene bisacrylamide (MBAM; 0.5 g) was added to the prepared polymer solution to enhance crosslinking; the total polymer concentration was adjusted to 9.5 wt%, and the solution was exposed to gamma irradiation at a total dose of 30 kGy. The swelled hydrogel with a gel fraction of approximately 65% was obtained. Nanocomposite hydrogels were prepared by adding different concentrations of TiO2 and GO (1,2,3 wt%) of the total polymer concentration of (9.5 wt %), and of composition 1:1:7.5 wt/wt of PVA: PEO: AAc in presence of 5.2% MBAM crosslinker. Ultrasonication was used during the preparation of nanocomposite hydrogels to ensure proper dispersion of the nanoparticles within the polymer matrix. The resulting solutions were poured into test tubes and irradiated with 60Co gamma rays to a total dose of 30 kGy. After the irradiation process, the hydrogel obtained was removed from the test tubes, cut into regular small disks, and dried in an oven at 60 °C. Characterization

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM), the surface images of hydrogels were imaged using a Flexaxiom nanosurf with a non-contact mode using an NCLR rectangular shape cantilever with a frequency of 9 kHz.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) was used to study the functional group in the wave number range of 4000–400 cm-1 using a Bruker Vertex 70 FTIR spectrophotometer equipped with a HYPERION TM series microscope (Bruker Optic GmbH, Ettlingen, Germany) and an ATR unit for solid samples measurement were used in this study.

Result and discussion

Gel % and swelling (%) studies

Various TiO₂ and GO nanoparticle concentrations (wt%) were incorporated into PVA/PEO/PAAc hydrogels via gamma irradiation at 30 kGy. The gelation and swelling behavior of the resulting nanocomposites were investigated as a function of nanoparticle content, pH, and time.

Effect of nanoparticle concentration on gel% and swelling%

Figure 1a and b illustrate the effect of different TiO₂ and GO concentrations on the gel content and swelling behavior of PVA/PEO/PAAc hydrogels, respectively. As the concentration of TiO₂ and GO increased, the gel % improved significantly from 65% up to 99.6% for TiO₂ and 97.3% for GO at 2 wt%. This increase is attributed to enhanced crosslinking density via covalent and hydrogen bonding between the polymer chains and nanoparticles.

The swelling behavior in Fig. 1b shows that the addition of nanoparticles initially enhances the swelling percentage due to increased water diffusion facilitated by surface charges on TiO₂ and functional groups on GO. However, further increases in nanoparticle concentration (particularly at 2–3 wt%) led to a decline in swelling. In TiO₂-based hydrogels, this may be due to “strong knot tightening” effects resulting from chelation with hydroxyl groups, which restrict polymer chain expansion [49]. In the case of GO, excessive loading may lead to dense entanglements and strong interactions between GO sheets and the hydrogel network, thereby impeding water uptake [50].

Figure 1c presents the swelling response of PVA/PEO/PAAc hydrogels in buffer solutions across a pH range of 2 to 11. At acidic pH (2–6), the carboxylic acid groups remain protonated, allowing hydrogen bonding between –COOH and –OH groups, thereby reducing swelling [51]. Swelling kinetics as a function of time

The time-dependent swelling behavior of PVA/PEO/PAAc, PVA/PEO/PAAc/TiO₂a (1 wt%), and PVA/PEO/PAAc/GOb (2 wt%) hydrogels at pH 10 is shown in Fig. 1d. All samples exhibited rapid swelling during the first 6 h, followed by a gradual approach to equilibrium by 24 h. The swelling trend followed the order: PVA/PEO/PAAc < PVA/PEO/PAAc/TiO₂a < PVA/PEO/PAAc/GOb, indicating that GO imparts a superior water-uptake capacity due to its abundant oxygen-containing functional groups [53].

Overall, the incorporation of both TiO₂ and GO nanoparticles significantly enhanced the gelation and swelling behavior of PVA/PEO/PAAc hydrogels; however, GO demonstrated superior swelling capacity and responsiveness. This can be attributed to the high surface area and rich oxygen-containing functional groups on GO, which promote stronger interactions with the hydrogel network and greater water uptake [54]. In contrast, TiO₂ contributed more effectively to increasing gel%, likely due to its ability to reinforce the crosslinked structure [55]. These differences suggest that GO-based hydrogels may be more advantageous for dye adsorption applications due to their higher swelling capacity, whereas TiO₂-based hydrogels offer enhanced structural stability.

Surface morphology and topography

Atomic force microscopy (AFM)

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) provides high-resolution three-dimensional surface topography and is widely employed to characterize the morphological features of hydrogel materials. It enables the evaluation of key surface parameters such as height (Z-axis), surface roughness (Ra), and texture evolution, which are essential for understanding structural changes within the hydrogel network [56–58]. AFM images of the dry hydrogels and their corresponding methylene blue (MB)-loaded samples are presented in Fig. 2, with the associated quantitative data summarized in Table 2. The neat PVA/PEO/PAAc hydrogel exhibited a relatively rough surface (Ra = 8.794 nm), indicating heterogeneous polymer distribution and surface irregularities. Following MB adsorption, the surface roughness decreased to 3.316 nm, accompanied by a slight increase in surface height, suggesting surface coverage by dye molecules and partial smoothing of surface features. The TiO₂-containing hydrogel displayed a smoother initial surface (Ra = 3.235 nm), reflecting uniform nanoparticle dispersion within the hydrogel matrix; after adsorption, the roughness further decreased to 1.214 nm, while the surface height increased significantly, indicating morphological changes associated with dye accumulation on the surface. In contrast, the GO-loaded hydrogel exhibited higher initial roughness (Ra = 7.22 nm), likely due to the layered structure and partial stacking of graphene oxide sheets. After MB adsorption, the roughness decreased to 2.189 nm, with a notable increase in surface height, indicating surface modification following interaction with dye molecules. The consistent reduction in surface roughness across all samples suggests effective surface coverage by MB; however, since AFM measurements were conducted on dry samples, the observed changes are limited to surface topography and cannot be used to directly infer adsorption mechanisms, multilayer formation, or adsorption capacity. Overall, the AFM results demonstrate that nanoparticle incorporation modifies the surface morphology of the hydrogels, and the differences observed between TiO₂- and GO-containing systems reflect variations in surface structure and texture that may influence adsorption behavior, as discussed in subsequent sections.

SEM investigation

The surface morphology of the swelled hydrogels PVA/PEO/PAAc, PVA/PEO/PAAc/TiO₂a, and PVA/PEO/PAAc/GOb was examined using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), and the micrographs are shown in Fig. 3. These images provide insight into the influence of nanoparticle incorporation on the hydrogel structure.

Fig. 3SEM of PVA/PEO/PAAc hydrogel (a), PVA/PEO/PAAc/TiO2a (b), and PVA/PEO/PAAc/GOb (c) nanocomposite hydrogels,

SEM of PVA/PEO/PAAc hydrogel (a), PVA/PEO/PAAc/TiO2a (b), and PVA/PEO/PAAc/GOb (c) nanocomposite hydrogels,

The pristine PVA/PEO/PAAc hydrogel (Fig. 3a) exhibited a relatively smooth surface with uniformly distributed pores, indicating a highly porous structure that favors water uptake and swelling. Upon incorporation of TiO₂ nanoparticles (Fig. 3b), the surface structure became notably denser and more compact. The pores observed in the native hydrogel largely disappeared, and the surface exhibited an irregular texture. This transformation suggests strong interactions between TiO₂ and the polymer matrix, thereby enhancing crosslinking density and reducing porosity. These changes are consistent with the reduced swelling and the improved gel fraction observed in TiO₂-containing hydrogels. In contrast, the GO-loaded hydrogel (Fig. 3c) retained visible small pores and a more textured surface compared to the TiO₂-based sample. The morphology suggests that GO incorporation helps maintain micro-porosity while simultaneously reinforcing the network. This supports the observed enhancement in swelling and dye uptake, driven by GO’s functional groups and layered structure.

The SEM and AFM analyses together provide a comprehensive picture of the morphological and structural impact of TiO₂ and GO nanoparticles on the hydrogel matrix. SEM revealed that TiO₂ incorporation leads to a denser, pore-suppressed surface, while AFM confirmed lower height and surface roughness, indicating reduced porosity and tighter network compaction. Conversely, GO incorporation preserved surface pores in SEM and increased surface height and roughness in AFM, supporting the presence of a more open, layered, and functionalized network structure. These findings reinforce that TiO₂-based hydrogels favor structural stability and crosslink density, whereas GO-based hydrogels promote higher swelling, porosity, and dye removal capacity.

FTIR analysis

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to investigate the structural interactions between the polymer components and the incorporated nanoparticles, particularly in terms of hydrogen bonding and possible complexation. The FTIR spectra of the prepared hydrogels are presented in Fig. 4. The spectrum of the PVA/PEO/PAAc hydrogel exhibits characteristic absorption bands corresponding to its constituent polymers. A broad band centered at approximately 3429 cm⁻¹ is attributed to the stretching vibration of hydroxyl (–OH) groups of PVA [59, 60]. The reduced intensity of this band compared to pure PVA is associated with the participation of hydroxyl groups in intermolecular interactions with PEO and PAAc [56, 57]. The absorption band at 1729 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the C = O stretching vibration of carboxylic acid (–COOH) groups in PAAc. The spectral region between 1000 and 1300 cm⁻¹ is mainly attributed to C–O–C stretching vibrations of PEO and PVA [58], with a characteristic peak observed at 1022 cm⁻¹ corresponding to the polymer backbone. The band at 2877 cm⁻¹ is assigned to aliphatic C–H stretching of PEO chains. Upon incorporation of TiO₂ and GO nanoparticles, specific spectral changes were observed. The band appearing near 842 cm⁻¹ is assigned to Ti–O–Ti vibrations, confirming the presence of TiO₂ within the hydrogel matrix. In contrast, the feature observed around 954 cm⁻¹ lies within the characteristic polymer vibration region and is therefore attributed to polymer backbone vibrations rather than being uniquely associated with nanoparticle incorporation. This distinction avoids overlap with polymer-related bands in the same spectral domain. Additionally, the –OH stretching band shifted to lower wavenumbers, indicating hydrogen bonding interactions between the polymer matrix and the nanoparticles. This shift is more pronounced in the GO-containing hydrogel due to the abundance of hydroxyl functional groups in GO. Similarly, the C = O stretching band at 1729 cm⁻¹ exhibited a slight shift and reduced intensity, suggesting interaction between carboxyl groups and the incorporated nanoparticles. These results confirm the successful incorporation of TiO₂ and GO and their interaction with the hydrogel network without ambiguity in peak assignment.

XRD measurements

To study the dispersion of nanoparticles within the nanocomposite hydrogel matrix, X-ray diffraction patterns were obtained. However, the XRD pattern of PVA/PEO/PAAc/TiO2a shows many sharp crystalline peaks at 2θ = 23, 18, 26, and 39 °, confirming the incorporation and fine dispersion of crystalline TiO2 nanoparticles in the hydrogel matrix 66. No characteristic peaks of GO were observed, as GO may have been exfoliated within the matrix [54].There is an increase in intensity of the peak at 2θ = 19.82° due to incorporation of GO content in the hydrogel matrix [60].

The XRD patterns reveal clear differences in crystallinity and nanoparticle dispersion among the samples. The TiO₂-containing hydrogel exhibits sharp, well-defined peaks, confirming the presence and good dispersion of crystalline TiO₂. In contrast, the GO-loaded hydrogel shows broad, low-intensity peaks, indicating a more amorphous structure. The absence of distinct GO peaks suggests that GO is highly dispersed or exfoliated within the polymer matrix. Overall, TiO₂ enhances crystallinity and structural reinforcement, while GO promotes matrix homogeneity through uniform dispersion.

EDX analysis.

Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX) was employed to determine the elemental composition of the prepared hydrogels. The EDX spectrum of the pristine PVA/PEO/PAAc hydrogel shows two dominant peaks corresponding to carbon (C) and oxygen (O), which are associated with the polymer backbone and the oxygen-containing functional groups of PVA, PEO, and PAAc. The absence of additional peaks confirms the purity of the hydrogel and the absence of inorganic additives [61]. For the PVA/PEO/PAAc/GO nanocomposite hydrogel, the EDX spectrum also exhibits strong C and O signals, with a noticeable increase in carbon intensity compared to the pristine hydrogel [62]. This observation is attributed to the carbon-rich structure of graphene oxide, confirming its successful incorporation into the polymer matrix. In the case of the PVA/PEO/PAAc/TiO₂ nanocomposite, additional peaks corresponding to titanium (Ti) were observed at approximately 4.5 and 4.9 keV, confirming the presence of TiO₂ nanoparticles within the hydrogel. The coexistence of C, O, and Ti peaks verifies the successful incorporation of TiO₂ into the polymer network. It should be noted that the presented results are based on EDX spectra and provide information on elemental composition only. Elemental mapping was not performed; therefore, conclusions regarding the spatial distribution or dispersion of nanoparticles are beyond the scope of this analysis.

TGA analysis

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and derivative thermogravimetry (DTG) were used to evaluate the thermal stability and decomposition behavior of the PVA/PEO/PAAc hydrogel and its nanocomposites with TiO₂ and GO. The corresponding TGA and DTG curves are presented in Fig. 7a and b, respectively.

In the TGA curves (Fig. 6a), all samples showed a gradual weight loss with increasing temperature, attributed to moisture evaporation, followed by thermal degradation of the organic polymer backbone [63]. The neat PVA/PEO/PAAc hydrogel exhibited the lowest thermal stability, with major weight loss beginning around 250 °C and completing near 500 °C. The incorporation of TiO₂ and GO significantly enhanced thermal resistance, delaying decomposition. The GO-based nanocomposite showed the most rapid weight loss beyond 300 °C, likely due to thermal degradation of oxygen-rich GO functional groups. Meanwhile, the TiO₂-containing hydrogel exhibited a more gradual decomposition profile, indicating improved structural integrity [64].

The DTG curves (Fig. 6b) further confirm the multistep degradation pattern. All hydrogels displayed three primary decomposition peaks. For PVA/PEO/PAAc, major peaks appeared at 270 °C and 340 °C, corresponding to side-chain degradation and backbone scission. In PVA/PEO/PAAc/TiO₂a, decomposition peaks shifted to 236 °C, 351 °C, and 452 °C, revealing enhanced thermal stability due to stronger interactions and crosslinking with TiO₂. Similarly, the GO-based hydrogel exhibited peaks at 270 °C and 382 °C, with a minor peak at the higher temperature, suggesting that layered GO structures delay complete degradation but initiate partial breakdown earlier.

Adsorption of MB

Effect of pH on dye adsorption

The solution pH is one of the most critical parameters governing adsorption processes, as it directly affects both the ionization state of the dye molecules and the surface charge of the adsorbent [69]. Figure 7 illustrates the effect of pH on the removal percentage (R%) of methylene blue (MB) by PVA/PEO/PAAc-based hydrogels reinforced with different TiO₂ loadings (Fig. 8a) and different GO loadings (Fig.8b).

For all investigated samples, MB removal efficiency increased markedly with increasing pH, reaching its maximum value at pH 10. At acidic pH values (pH 3), relatively low removal efficiencies were observed. This behavior can be attributed to the protonation of functional groups such as carboxyl (–COOH) and hydroxyl (–OH) groups on the hydrogel backbone, which leads to reduced electrostatic attraction toward the cationic MB molecules. In addition, strong hydrogen bonding under acidic conditions causes partial collapse of the hydrogel network, limiting dye diffusion into the internal adsorption sites and consequently lowering removal efficiency.

As the pH increased toward neutral and alkaline conditions, deprotonation of the functional groups occurred, resulting in a more negatively charged hydrogel surface. This enhanced electrostatic attraction between the negatively charged adsorption sites and the positively charged MB molecules significantly improved dye uptake. At pH 10, near-maximum removal efficiencies were achieved for all nanocomposite hydrogels, indicating favorable adsorption conditions and effective interaction between MB molecules and the active sites of the adsorbents.

This improvement can be attributed to the high specific surface area of the nanofillers and the presence of abundant surface functional groups, which provide additional adsorption sites and strengthen dye–adsorbent interactions. In particular, the charged metal oxide nanoparticles promote electrostatic interactions with MB, while GO contributes π–π interactions and hydrogen bonding, further facilitating dye adsorption. Among the investigated compositions, nanocomposite hydrogels containing 1 wt% TiO₂ and 2 wt% GO exhibited the highest MB removal efficiency, especially under alkaline conditions. The removal efficiency decreased progressively with increasing initial concentration for all investigated systems. The pristine PVA/PEO/PAAc hydrogel showed a pronounced decline from approximately 90% at 10 mg /L to about 31% at 50 mg /L, indicating rapid saturation of available adsorption sites. In contrast, the nanocomposite hydrogels exhibited significantly improved performance and greater resistance to concentration increase. The TiO₂-containing hydrogel-maintained removal efficiencies above 85% even at higher concentrations, while the GO-based hydrogel demonstrated the highest adsorption efficiency, decreasing only slightly from ~ 100% to ~ 90% across the studied range.

The observed decrease in removal efficiency at elevated concentrations is attributed to progressive occupation of active adsorption sites and increased competition among MB molecules. At low concentrations, the number of active sites exceeds the number of dye molecules, enabling nearly complete removal. As concentration increases, the fixed number of adsorption sites becomes limiting, resulting in reduced percentage removal. The superior performance of GO-reinforced hydrogels can be attributed to their high surface area and abundant oxygen-containing functional groups, which enhance electrostatic attraction and π–π interactions with MB molecules. Similarly, TiO₂ incorporation improves adsorption through additional surface-active sites and stronger dye–adsorbent interactions.

To further elucidate the adsorption mechanism, the equilibrium data were fitted using the Langmuir and the Freundlich isotherm models (Fig. 11a and b). The corresponding isotherm constants and statistical fitting parameters are summarized in Table 3. The Langmuir model, which assumes monolayer adsorption onto a homogeneous surface, showed excellent agreement with the experimental data, as indicated by high correlation coefficients (R² > 0.99). The calculated maximum monolayer adsorption capacities (Qₘ) confirmed that nanocomposite hydrogels possess significantly higher adsorption capacities compared to the pristine hydrogel [69]. The GO-based system exhibited high adsorption affinity (high Ka), indicating a strong interaction between MB molecules and the adsorbent surface.

The Freundlich model (Fig. 11b), which describes heterogeneous multilayer adsorption, also demonstrated excellent fitting for all systems. The Freundlich constant (Kf) was highest for the GO-containing hydrogel, reflecting superior adsorption capacity. Additionally, the values of the heterogeneity parameter (n) were greater than unity for all adsorbents, confirming favorable adsorption behavior. The simultaneous good fitting to both models suggests that MB adsorption involves predominant monolayer coverage with contributions from surface heterogeneity.

Conclusion

In this study, PVA/PEO/PAAc-based nanocomposite hydrogels incorporating TiO₂ and graphene oxide (GO) were successfully synthesized via gamma irradiation–induced copolymerization at a total dose of 30 kGy. The incorporation of nanofillers improved the physicochemical properties of the hydrogel network, including gel fraction and swelling behavior, with the highest swelling observed under alkaline conditions (pH 10) after 24 h.

Comprehensive characterization (FTIR, TGA, AFM, XRD, SEM, and EDX) confirmed the successful incorporation of TiO₂ and GO within the polymer matrix and revealed distinct structural roles for each filler. GO contributed to increased surface roughness, porosity, and availability of functional groups, whereas TiO₂ enhanced crystallinity and thermal stability of the hydrogel system. The adsorption performance toward methylene blue (MB) dye demonstrated a clear improvement upon nanofiller incorporation, following the trend: PVA/PEO/PAAc < PVA/PEO/PAAc/TiO₂ < PVA/PEO/PAAc/GO. Under the investigated conditions (pH 10, 24 h, 20 mg/L MB), the maximum removal efficiencies reached 64%, 98%, and ~ 100%, respectively. Isotherm analysis showed better agreement with the Langmuir model, suggesting predominantly monolayer adsorption behavior, while kinetic data were more consistent with a pseudo-first-order model, indicating that physisorption plays a major role in the adsorption process. Overall, GO was more effective in enhancing adsorption performance due to its high surface area and abundant oxygen-containing functional groups, whereas TiO₂ primarily contributed to improved structural integrity and thermal resistance. These results highlight that the selection of nanofiller can be tuned depending on whether adsorption efficiency or material stability is the primary target.

Limitations and future perspectives

Despite these promising results, several limitations should be acknowledged. The adsorption experiments were conducted under controlled batch conditions using a single model dye (methylene blue), which may not fully represent the complexity of real wastewater systems containing mixed pollutants. Future work should therefore focus on evaluating the performance of these hydrogels in real wastewater matrices, assessing their recyclability and long-term stability, and employing advanced characterization and modeling approaches to better understand the adsorption mechanisms and optimize material design for scalable applications.
