Overview of systematic reviews and meta-analyses on problematic video game use and gaming disorder in adolescents and young adults

Jason M Nagata, Christiane K Helmer, Oliver H Huang, Sahana Nayak, Kevin Bao, Stephanie Zhang, Kevin Handoko, Elizabeth J Li, Megan A Moreno, Jason M Lavender

Abstract

SummaryBackgroundProblematic video game use and disordered gaming are increasingly recognized adolescent health concerns.MethodsThis overview summarizes assessment, epidemiology, risk factors, health outcomes, and treatment of problematic and disordered gaming among adolescents and young adults. PubMed, Web of Science, and PsycInfo were searched for reviews published from 9/1/2016 to 5/26/2026. At least two reviewers screened and extracted data. Risk-of-bias assessments were summarized. This review is registered with the International Platform of Registered Systematic Review and Meta-analysis Protocols (INPLASY #202670067).FindingsOf 98 unique records, 51 were included. Prevalence varied by region, sociodemographics, and diagnostic definitions, with gaming disorder or internet gaming disorder affecting 6–10% of adolescents and young adults worldwide. Earlier gaming initiation, maladaptive gaming patterns, poorer mental health, and controlling parenting were associated with increased risk. Problematic gaming was linked to adverse physical, sleep, neurobiological, and mental health outcomes. Cognitive behavioral therapy showed the strongest therapeutic evidence. Evidence certainty was low-to-moderate.InterpretationThe findings highlight the importance of early identification of problematic and disordered gaming among youth who begin gaming young, or experience mental health symptoms, social difficulties, or limited parental support. Future research should refine diagnostic criteria, evaluate treatments through randomized trials, and clarify developmental mechanisms.FundingJMN was supported by the National Institutes of Health and Rise Together, a donor-advised fund sponsored and administered by the National Philanthropic Trust.Keywords: Video games, Gaming disorder, Internet gaming disorder, Problematic video game use, Adolescent, Youth

Introduction

Video games are now a central part of adolescent life: more than 90% of adolescents play video games. Contemporary video games encompass a wide array of potential benefits, including social connection, cognitive engagement, physical activity (e.g., exergaming), and educational enrichment. Yet, a subset of adolescents engage in gaming patterns that are maladaptive or impair functioning., Importantly, evidence suggests that potential harms are not uniform across all gaming, but depend heavily on specific content, contexts, and patterns of use. This more differentiated perspective reflects a broader shift in the digital health field, moving away from assumptions that all screen time or gaming is inherently harmful, and toward identifying precise mechanisms of risk, an approach highlighted in recent critiques of overly broad and monocausal technological explanations.

A major challenge has been the lack of consensus on definitions and measurement., Terms such as problematic video game use have been operationalized in multiple ways, and although the World Health Organization's International Classification of Diseases 11th Revision (ICD-11) now recognizes gaming disorder, the criteria remain debated, and the corresponding Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) construct, internet gaming disorder, has not been fully adopted., This highlights the need for a careful and critical synthesis of what is known and where uncertainties remain.

The objective of this review is to synthesize the current state of evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses on problematic video game use and disordered gaming in adolescents and young adults. We examine the history of the construct, current definitions and assessment tools, epidemiology, risk factors, associated mental and physical health correlates, and emerging strategies for prevention and treatment. Throughout, we highlight areas of consensus and ongoing controversy, identify gaps in measurement and theory, and outline priorities for future research and clinical guidance for pediatricians.

Concerns about video game use, particularly in youth, were first described in 1983 by Soper's report “Junk-Time Junkies: An Emerging Addiction Among Students.” Soper highlighted various factors that could contribute to the development of gaming addiction in students (). Research during the 1990s further investigated video game addiction, using the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), Third and Fourth Edition pathological gambling criteria as a framework to assess problematic video gaming., The advent of online games in the 2000s led to expanded interest in studying problematic video game use, particularly with regard to online video gaming. In 2013, the American Psychiatric Association (APA) added internet gaming disorder as a disorder for future research in section III of the DSM-5. In 2019, gaming disorder was included in the World Health Organization's 11th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11), though it did not become a fully ratified disorder until 2022., The ICD-11 also included the term hazardous gaming, which refers to a gaming pattern that may be harmful to the individual or others around them, but does not rise to the clinical diagnosis of gaming disorder. Notably, while internet gaming disorder and gaming disorder refer to clinical diagnoses, problematic video game use is a broader construct. It is sometimes used to refer solely to gaming that is harmful but subclinical. However, this term can also be a catchall for both clinical and nonclinical patterns of harmful gaming. In this review, we use disordered gaming as a term when referring to a clinical diagnosis, and problematic video game use (or problematic video gaming) as the broader term for excessive and/or maladaptive gaming behavior.

The DSM-5 characterizes internet gaming disorder as persistent online gaming causing significant impairment or distress over a 12-month period, with five or more of nine criteria (e.g., preoccupation, withdrawal symptoms; see ) required for a diagnosis. One major criticism of this polythetic approach is that it can lead to excessive diagnostic heterogeneity, such that two patients with the diagnosis could exhibit few overlapping symptoms. However, heterogeneity in symptom presentation is not unique to internet gaming disorder and is characteristic of most DSM-5 diagnoses. Many other diagnoses in the DSM-5 rely on threshold-based criteria composed of partially distinct symptoms. Additionally, some argue that certain criteria do not accurately distinguish between addicted and non-addicted disorders. For instance, gamers with and without gaming disorder endorsed playing video games to escape negative emotions. Importantly, beyond meeting individual criteria, a diagnosis requires significant distress and/or impairment. In contrast to the DSM-5, the ICD-11 classifies gaming disorder into two categories, predominantly online and predominantly offline, with the former involving persistent gaming conducted primarily over the internet and the latter involving persistent gaming that occurs largely without internet use. Both subtypes share three core features: impaired control over gaming, increasing prioritization of gaming over other life interests and daily activities, and continuation or escalation of gaming despite negative consequences. Consistent with the DSM-5 definition, the ICD-11 requires that these symptoms be evident for at least 12 months.

The diagnosis of disordered gaming has also been contentious due to high co-occurrence with other mental health concerns. Critics have argued that problematic gaming is better conceptualized as a maladaptive coping mechanism rather than a disorder unto itself, and that classifying problematic gaming as a mental disorder may spread moral panic and stigma. Moreover, the addiction model underlies the DSM-5 and ICD-11 criteria, yet alternate theoretical frameworks for gaming disorder have been proposed. However, these criticisms are not unique to disordered gaming: comorbidity is common across many psychiatric conditions, and maladaptive coping behaviors can develop into clinically significant disorders. Emerging evidence also suggests that for a subset of individuals, problematic gaming can be persistent and associated with functional impairment. Furthermore, framing disordered gaming as a source of “moral panic” risks understating the clinically significant impairment experienced by affected individuals, as diagnostic frameworks are intended to delineate thresholds of disorder rather than pathologize normative behavior. Taken together, these perspectives highlight ongoing debate while supporting the need for continued empirical investigation and nuanced conceptualization of disordered gaming.

Methods

Protocol and registration

The data reported are presented in accordance with the guidance of the Preferred Reporting Items for Overviews of Reviews (PRIOR). A formal protocol was retrospectively registered with the International Platform of Registered Systematic Review and Meta-analysis Protocols (INPLASY; INPLASY202670067; https://doi.org/10.37766/inplasy2026.7.0067). We followed an adapted version of PRIOR guidance to structure the review process, including predefined eligibility criteria and systematic search procedures. While not all elements of the guideline were applied rigidly, these adaptations were implemented to enhance methodological rigor and transparency, and we outline our criteria, search strategy, and study selection below.

Search strategy and selection criteria

We included peer-reviewed systematic reviews and meta-analyses examining problematic gaming, gaming disorder, and/or internet gaming disorder. Studies were categorized into six thematic areas: assessment tools, epidemiology and prevalence, risk and maintenance factors, health outcomes, mental health correlates, and prevention and treatment approaches. Eligible studies primarily focused on adolescents and young adults. No language restrictions were applied. We excluded studies that did not meet these population or study design criteria or did not report relevant outcomes.

We conducted systematic searches in PubMed, Web of Science, and PsycInfo for articles published between September 1, 2016, and May 26, 2026. Inclusion criteria and detailed search strategies, including specific terms and Boolean operators, are provided in . A PRISMA flowchart diagram with an overview of study inclusion across the six thematic searches is provided in .

All search results were independently screened by at least two reviewers per section. Initial searches were completed by Jason Nagata (JN), Stephanie Zhang (SZ), and Sahana Nayak (SN), with subsequent searches conducted by Christiane Helmer (CH). Following title and abstract screening, full texts of potentially eligible studies were retrieved and independently assessed for inclusion by section-specific reviewers (assessment tools: Kevin Handoko [KH], epidemiology and prevalence and risk/maintenance factors: SN; health outcomes: Kevin Bao [KB]; mental health correlates: CH; and treatment/prevention: SZ) and a second independent reviewer. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with JN serving as the adjudicator when consensus could not be reached.

Risk of bias and quality assessment

We extracted and reported risk of bias and quality assessments as conducted within the included systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Initial extraction was completed by KH and SZ and verified by CH, KB, and SN. Where available, the assessments for each study were summarized to provide context for the strength and limitations of the synthesized evidence in . Quality appraisal summaries and narrative certainties for each thematic area (e.g., assessment, epidemiology and prevalence) are detailed in .

To quantify overlap among the included systematic reviews and meta-analyses, we calculated the corrected covered area (CCA) index. Because the six thematic domains addressed distinct research questions and bodies of literature, CCA was calculated separately for each domain.

Role of the funding source

The funders had no involvement in study design, data collection, data analyses, data interpretation, or the writing of the report.

Results

Our search yielded a total of 98 unique records, of which 51 unique reviews met inclusion criteria following title/abstract screening and full-text assessment. Of the included reviews, 3 focused on assessment tools, 8 on epidemiology and prevalence, 8 on risk and maintenance factors, 11 on health outcomes, 8 on mental health correlates, and 15 on prevention and treatment approaches, with 2 reviews overlapping across two sections. Study characteristics, key findings, and quality assessments are summarized in . Of the 51 included reviews, 30 (59%) conducted a quality assessment. Narrative certainty across thematic domains ranged from low to low-moderate (). The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) tools were the most frequently used (6 reviews) for quality assessment, followed by the Newcastle Ottawa Scale (NOS) and Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB) variants (4 each). None of the reviews used the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework for quality assessment.

Overlap among included reviews was generally low. CCA indices indicated slight overlap for epidemiology and sociodemographics (CCA = 0.032), health outcomes (CCA = 0.047), mental health correlates (CCA = 0.018), and treatment and prevention (CCA = 0.040), moderate overlap for risk and maintenance factors (CCA = 0.088), and high overlap for assessment studies (CCA = 0.138), reflecting the shared psychometric validation literature across assessment reviews.

Assessment

Numerous measures have been developed to assess the criteria for disordered gaming and problematic video game use more broadly. The Video Game Addiction Questionnaire, Gaming Addiction Scale-7, 9-item dichotomous Internet Gaming Disorder Scale, Young Diagnostic Questionnaire, and Compulsive Internet Use Scale-14 cover a subset of the criteria in DSM-5 and ICD-11.
 summarizes the coverage of the DSM-5 and ICD-11 criteria of selected tools. Some are tools to screen for clinical internet gaming disorder (e.g., 9-item dichotomous Internet Gaming Disorder Scale), while others are continuous measures of the severity of problematic gaming (e.g., Video Game Addiction Questionnaire). provides a description of selected tools, including their components, scoring, and clinical cutoffs.

Two meta-analyses on DSM-5-based internet gaming disorder measures found that the Internet Gaming Disorder Scale–Short-Form (IGDS9-SF) was the most widely studied and validated., The IGDS9-SF includes all 9 DSM-5 criteria for internet gaming disorder, and a systematic review of its psychometrics reported adequate internal consistency and criterion validity.

A priority for future research would be to increase consistency of assessment across studies to enhance the evidence base for select measures. Few measures have been evaluated using clinical samples. Clinical practice incorporates observation and clinical interviews alongside screening tools, and further research is necessary to evaluate diagnostic criteria for disordered gaming and their alignment with clinical practice. While DSM-5 and ICD-11 criteria have been useful, a more robust theoretical framework for disordered gaming may facilitate advancements in assessment and treatment research.

Epidemiology and prevalence

Across all ages, two meta-analyses found the prevalence of gaming disorder to be 3.05% and 3.3%., A global meta-analysis of internet gaming disorder in adolescents and young adults between ages 8–28 across 33 countries found an overall prevalence of 9.9%, with rates of 8.8% in adolescents (age 8–18) and 10.4% in young adults (age 18–28). Another meta-analysis of 16 studies found the prevalence of internet gaming disorder to be 4.6% in adolescents ages 10–19, with higher rates in males (6.8%) compared to females (1.3%). Smaller samples were associated with higher prevalence of gaming disorder. Prevalence estimates also varied by geographic region, with some Asian study populations reporting higher prevalence estimates of gaming disorder., More stringent sampling methods were associated with lower prevalence estimates. When analyses were restricted to studies using more stringent sampling methods, such as representative or random cluster sampling, the overall prevalence of gaming disorder decreased from 3.05% to 1.96%. Finally, these meta-analyses included studies prior to 2013 when the internet gaming disorder criteria were first published, potentially conflating problematic video game use with internet gaming disorder.

Notably, problematic gaming was typically more common among males than among females., , , This may be due, in part, to video game use and longer gaming duration generally being more common in males, as well as greater social acceptability of gaming among males. Findings further suggest that maladaptive cognitive patterns in male adolescents, such as overvaluing the rewards of playing video games and higher urges to play video games, may explain the apparent sex disparities.

Problematic video game use may also differ by age. As onset is most common during adolescence and young adulthood, these developmental stages represent critical periods for prevention, detection, and early intervention. Video game use also typically increases as adolescents get older. The percentage of adolescents using video games for more than 2 h increases from 15.5% at ages 9–11 to 32.1% at ages 11–13. Adolescence is a period of heightened sensitivity in reward circuitry, and gaming disorder is associated with altered reward processing, which may be associated with increased risk in adolescents for internet gaming disorder and video game addiction., , Similarly, adolescence is a period of heightened vulnerability to internalizing symptoms and disorders, which are in turn associated with greater risk for gaming disorder.,

Regarding racial and ethnic differences, evidence suggests that problematic video game use may be more common among Black, Hispanic, and Native American adolescents compared to their White peers. Notably, past studies have shown that Black, Hispanic, and Asian adolescents have higher video game time overall compared to White peers.,, There are also disparities across geographic regions. For instance, East Asian study populations typically have higher rates of gaming disorder, perhaps because gaming is a particularly popular hobby in East Asian cultures.,

Disparities in the prevalence of problematic video game use have also been found for sexual and gender minority adolescents. This may partly reflect higher exposure to social exclusion due to their identity, which may be associated with greater digital activity. Video gaming may also serve as a coping mechanism for sexual and gender minority adolescents in response to minority status-related stressors. Sexual and gender minority adolescents have reported using video games to cope with stress and experience positive emotions.,,, , , ,

Finally, evidence suggests that there are disparities in problematic video game use by household income. Lower household income has been associated with greater overall and problematic video game use, potentially reflecting fewer financial resources to support other recreational activities. Reduced access to recreational spaces and extracurricular programming may increase reliance on digital activities, and higher levels of video game use in these contexts may increase risk for problematic video game use.

Risk/maintenance factors and correlates

Key risk factors for problematic video game use and disordered gaming, including demographic, mental health, parenting, and use-pattern factors, are summarized in . Early initiation of gaming is one important risk factor. For instance, adolescents who initiated weekly video game use before age five had a higher risk of problematic video game use compared to those who initiated after age 10. Preschoolers who initiated video game use were also 1.7 times more likely to be at high risk of internet gaming disorder. Initiating video game use at a younger age may reflect a stronger tendency for youth to associate gaming with their self-worth, with lower gaming-contingent self-worth proposed to underlie the relationship between earlier initiation and internet gaming disorder. Moreover, the type of video game use may also be associated with problematic gaming., For example, in a study of Dutch adolescents and young adults, highly problematic gamers were more likely to spend more time on online versus offline games.

Certain dispositional traits are also linked to problematic video game use. Previous literature found that more withdrawn behavior, social phobia, and neglect of social contacts were associated with internet gaming disorder, as were lower levels of belonging, self-esteem, and autonomy., Reciprocal processes may underlie such relationships. For instance, socially withdrawn or anxious individuals may be more likely to turn to video games to avoid traditional social interaction, and those with problematic video game use patterns may end up neglecting social connection and becoming more withdrawn.

Furthermore, parenting practices may also be related to problematic video game use. Along with using screens to reward or punish adolescent behavior, greater parental controlling mediation (i.e., using parental authority and pressure to exert control instead of explaining why things should be a certain way) has been found to be associated with greater problematic gaming behaviors, independent of gaming frequency. Consequently, controlling parental behaviors may unintentionally encourage adolescents to play video games as a compensatory source of autonomy, thereby potentially increasing the risk of problematic video game use and gaming disorder.,, ,

Health outcomes

Problematic gaming is associated with a range of adverse physical outcomes. For example, gaming and internet addiction were found to be associated with higher rates of obesity in adolescents, which may be related to unhealthy eating habits (e.g., poorer nutrition, emotional eating) and physical activity displacement. Gaming disorder has also been associated with a variety of physical symptoms, including joint and muscle pain, headache, vision problems, and sleep disturbances, which was the most frequently reported symptom. These symptoms were more frequently reported by teenagers.

Sleep is a particularly important area of concern in relation to problematic video game use. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that problematic gaming is associated with multiple forms of sleep disruption, including shorter sleep duration, poorer sleep quality, higher daytime sleepiness, and increased sleep problems. Additional sleep disturbances include delayed sleep schedules, sleep deprivation, and nocturnal awakenings. Notably, the effect sizes of these associations were greater than that of other digital media usage, which may reflect the more arousing nature of video games compared to other “passive” media. Problematic gaming was also more strongly associated with sleep duration in adolescents than in adults. This may be related to both late-night gaming behaviors and developmental susceptibility to delayed circadian rhythms during adolescence. Potential mechanisms of sleep disruption include displacement of sleep or other sleep promoting activities, such as exercise, with gaming, thereby reducing overall sleep duration. Blue light exposure from screens can also promote arousal and suppress nighttime melatonin secretion, disrupting normal sleep–wake cycles and increasing sleep latency. Physical symptoms related to gaming such as muscle pain can also disrupt sleep.

Problematic video game use has also been associated with differences in brain development and function. Individuals with gaming disorder,,,, exhibit altered neurobiological structure and function associated with emotion regulation, cognitive control, attention, reward processing, inhibition, self-identity, and several domains of cognitive functioning, comparable to substance addiction.,,, , , Functional neuroimaging reveals that internet gaming disorder is associated with elevated loss sensitivity in brain regions involved with reward circuitry and altered activity in regions responsible for self-monitoring, development of habitual behaviors, and decision-making. Structural brain differences associated with internet gaming disorder include decreased gray-matter volume and white-matter integrity in regions associated with impulse control, emotional regulation, and attention.,,, , , Structural and functional differences have also been found in regions involved in impulse control, identity formation, emotion regulation, and reward sensitivity in problematic gaming. These findings suggest that an imbalance between weakened cognitive control and a heightened reward system may be associated with both problematic gaming and internet gaming disorder.,,

Overall, problematic gaming is associated with poorer physical health outcomes. Problematic gaming is associated with physical symptoms and obesity, which may be related to unhealthy eating habits and displacement of physical activity. Problematic video game use and internet gaming disorder are also associated with poorer sleep quality and lower sleep duration through biological mechanisms and displacement of sleep time. Video game addiction and internet gaming disorder are associated with a variety of structural and functional differences in several brain regions involving reward circuitry and executive functioning.

Mental health correlates

Problematic gaming is closely intertwined with broader mental health and may act as both a correlate and potential risk factor for psychosocial difficulties. Among adolescents, problematic gaming is commonly associated with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, inattention, anxiety, depression, and behavioral and emotional problems. Lower self-control has also been linked to problematic gaming, suggesting that impulsivity may contribute to its development. Relatedly, both problem gambling and problematic gaming are consistently associated with conduct problems and depressive symptoms. In students and adolescents, problematic gaming has also been associated with increased risk of both nonsuicidal self-injury and suicidality, with anxiety identified as both a mediator and moderator of this relationship.

Evidence from the COVID-19 pandemic further highlights contextual factors that may exacerbate gaming-related harms. During this period, gaming disorder was associated with maladaptive coping regulatory styles, poor social support, higher academic stress, excessive use of social networks, and poorer mental health. Gaming disorder was also significantly associated with higher levels of depression, anxiety, stress, and insomnia, as well as lower quality of life and subjective well-being.

Importantly, emerging research suggests that these relationships are often bidirectional. Internet gaming disorder, for example, shows reciprocal associations with depression and aggression, indicating that symptoms of depression or aggression may both contribute to and co-occur with disordered gaming. Internet gaming disorder has also been linked to mood dysregulation and lower psychological well-being, life satisfaction, and academic performance.

Prevention and treatment

Contemporary evidence supports the application of multimodal, integrative approaches for the treatment of disordered gaming in adolescents. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) remains the most frequently studied form of intervention; however, recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicate that CBT is most effective when delivered as part of broader programs incorporating motivational, psychoeducational, and skills-based components (e.g., emotion regulation, self-efficacy), rather than as a standalone treatment., Across studies, CBT-based and related psychosocial interventions are associated with significant reductions in gaming disorder severity and gaming time, with pooled effect sizes indicating moderate to large improvements.,

Importantly, treatment outcomes vary depending on how they are measured. Meta-analytic data suggest consistent reductions in gaming time and disorder symptom severity, as well as moderate improvements in comorbid symptoms such as depression and anxiety. However, improvements in time spent gaming do not always correspond directly to functional recovery, such as academic performance or social functioning, which are less consistently measured across trials. This heterogeneity in outcome definitions remains a key limitation in the literature.

Parental involvement is a critical component of treatment in child and adolescent populations. Interventions that integrate parental psychoeducation and communication strategies are commonly included within CBT-based programs and may improve treatment engagement and adherence, even when additional symptom reduction is not consistently observed., In addition to structured interventions, consistent parental monitoring, structured routines, and early parental recognition of warning signs, such as social isolation, depressive symptoms, and low academic performance, play an important role in prevention.

A range of other non-pharmacological interventions has also been explored. These include behavioral and mindfulness-based interventions, as well as emerging modalities such as virtual reality and animal-assisted therapy, which may support improvements in emotion regulation and interpersonal functioning. Meta-analytic evidence suggests that non-pharmacological interventions overall are associated with significant reductions in gaming disorder severity, with psychotherapy-based approaches demonstrating the largest effects. Preventive efforts emphasize school-based and public health approaches, as well as digital well-being programs that promote healthy technology use and early risk identification.

Pharmacological treatments have been investigated primarily in the context of comorbid psychiatric conditions. Evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses suggests that several medication classes, including antidepressants (e.g., bupropion, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors), stimulants for attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, and other psychotropic agents, have been trialed, often in combination with psychosocial interventions.,, While some studies report improvements in gaming-related outcomes, effects are generally modest and appear to be mediated by improvements in underlying symptoms such as impulsivity, inattention, or depression rather than direct effects on gaming behavior itself. Combined treatment approaches (pharmacotherapy plus psychosocial interventions) may offer enhanced benefit compared to either modality alone in some populations, although further research is needed.

To translate the existing evidence base into actionable clinical practice, we propose a pragmatic clinical pathway () outlining a stepwise approach to screening, triage, initial management, and referral. This framework synthesizes current knowledge on risk factors, assessment tools, and treatment strategies to inform clinical decision-making despite ongoing limitations in standardization.

In sum, current evidence supports CBT-based, multimodal interventions with family involvement as first-line treatment, combined with psychosocial or pharmacologic strategies when clinically indicated. However, further high-quality, long-term studies using standardized definitions of both behavioral and functional outcomes are needed to establish clear, evidence-based treatment guidelines for adolescents with disordered gaming.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, heterogeneity in definitions of problematic video game use, gaming disorder, and internet gaming disorder, alongside the lack of standardized assessment and screening measures, diagnostic tools, and outcome metrics for problematic video game use and disordered gaming, hindered cross-study comparisons. As this review synthesized evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses with substantial heterogeneity in study designs, populations, and quality assessment approaches (), we did not assign strength-of-evidence ratings to individual risk factors or health outcomes, as doing so would risk oversimplification. Second, we did not search local-language databases, which precluded coverage of reviews not indexed in PubMed, Web of Science, or PsycInfo. Third, the evidence synthesized by most of the included reviews is cross-sectional, limiting causal inference. Together, these limitations highlight the need for uniform criteria, and longitudinal research to strengthen the evidence base.

Future directions

To enhance prevention and treatment efforts, future research must address several critical gaps. Consensus committees across disciplines could establish clinical and diagnostic standards, similar to approaches used in the Red Book, or through consensus-based research methods such as concept mapping. Such efforts could also inform future iterations of diagnostic manuals, including the next DSM revision, by incorporating standardized criteria for internet gaming disorder/gaming disorder to improve diagnostic consistency. Importantly, these challenges are not limited to gaming; other forms of problematic screen use, such as social media addiction and problematic internet use, face similar issues with definitions, diagnostic criteria, and measurement. Prior work has shown positive associations across multiple behavioral addictions, with problematic internet use, internet gaming disorder, and social media clustering primarily via a shared negative association with conscientiousness, while another study reported only weak interrelationships between internet gaming disorder and social media addiction despite shared links to mental health symptoms. Together, these findings suggest both commonalities and distinctions across maladaptive technology use behaviors, supporting the need for transdiagnostic assessment and prevention approaches.

Second, more research should focus on risk factors for disordered gaming. Future studies should consider the role of co-occurring mental health disorders, including depression and anxiety, to better understand their interactions with addictive behaviors. More research is also needed to identify and strengthen protective psychosocial factors, such as parental monitoring, self-regulation skills, and media literacy, which together may promote resilience and reduce susceptibility to disordered gaming. Future research should also examine potential gender differences in risk factors, symptom presentation, and intervention outcomes.

Third, well-designed, large-scale randomized controlled trials using standardized outcome measures are needed to evaluate the long-term efficacy of pharmacological and psychosocial treatments. Most studies utilize small samples and have short follow-up periods, often limited to immediate post-treatment or short-term follow-up (e.g., three months).,,, Additionally, as new technology interventions for disordered gaming have arisen, future studies should study the efficacy of these novel treatments. Technology-based interventions have been introduced to reduce gaming intensity and improve awareness, but their effectiveness remains under-researched and requires evaluation across diverse adolescent and young adult populations.

Fourth, neuroimaging research should employ longitudinal designs and multiple neuroimaging methods (e.g., structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and functional MRI, diffusion tensor imaging) to clarify the direction of associations between problematic video game use and brain function and structure, including underlying mechanisms., Previous studies have also found that video game addiction predicts and is predicted by alterations in reward circuitry, suggesting a potential bidirectional relationship. There is a need to elucidate how video game addiction may impact neurodevelopment and how pre-existing neurobiological differences may contribute to risk for problematic video game use.

Finally, at a broader systems level, policy and industry accountability must be addressed. Stronger regulatory frameworks are needed to ensure transparency in the design of games, particularly regarding features that contribute to addictive use., Collaboration with developers to implement ethical design elements, such as game-time limits, warning prompts, and limits on monetization, represents a promising strategy for reducing harm.
