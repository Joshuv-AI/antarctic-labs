Characteristics of older people who experience high-cost hospital care at the end of life and report low-satisfaction: Evidence of low-value care from bereaved carer surveys in England
Authors: Shuang Jin, Irene J Higginson, Anna E Bone, Simon N Etkind, Charles Normand, Peter May
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA significant proportion of healthcare costs is incurred in hospitals at the end of life (EoL), with a small group of patients accounting for most of the costs. Scarce resources should be allocated based on cost-effectiveness. High costs and low satisfaction with care suggest low-value care at EoL; however, there is little evidence regarding which patients receive such care.ObjectivesTo identify the characteristics of older patients who experience high-cost care but report low satisfaction with care at EoL.Design and methodsSecondary data analysis of mortality follow-back surveys: International Access, Rights, and Empowerment (IARE) studies. We surveyed the next of kin of older patients who received palliative care or died in hospital in England. Satisfaction with care at EoL and cost of hospital care calculated with quantity of services and unit costs. We used multinomial logistic regression to analyse factors associated with potentially low-value care.Results404 patients were included (mean [SD] age: 80.4 [8.5] years). During the last three months of life, 53.8% of the total hospital care costs were incurred by 20% of the patients. 36.6% of the respondents reported low satisfaction. Compared to patients with low-cost care and reporting satisfaction, patients who were 65-74 years old (relative risk ratio [RRR] = 3.2, 95% CI: 1.09 to 9.42), had low income (RRR = 4.12, 95% CI: 1.29 to 13.15) and with an illness duration of 1-12 months (RRR = 3.93, 95% CI: 1.37 to 11.21) were more likely to receive high-cost hospital care and report low satisfaction.ConclusionsBeing younger, on low incomes and with short illness duration are associated with high-cost, low-satisfaction hospital care at EoL. Identifying underlying mechanisms may enable targeted interventions to reduce high-cost, low-value care. Further investigation is needed to change the care trajectories of patients with these risk factors.Keywords: end of life, equity, health care costs, satisfaction, socioeconomic status, older patient, value of care

Introduction

Demand for healthcare is rising with more people surviving with treatable chronic health conditions. 1 Achieving the World Health Organization (WHO) target of universal health coverage by 2030 will require additional resources and better use of existing resources. 2 Evidence shows that a large proportion of healthcare costs are incurred at the end of life (EoL), primarily due to hospital care used by a minority of patients.3–7 It is important to ensure that this high level of spending represents high value care with benefits in terms of longer life, better quality of life and better experiences for patients and their families. 8 Value was defined by Porter and Teisberg as the health outcomes achieved per dollar spent. 9 In high value care, higher costs are justified by improved outcomes and experience. Current literature focuses more on cost rather than value in the context of end-of-life care.10,11

This study analysed hospital costs and satisfaction with care at EoL. Patient and family satisfaction does not cover all aspects of the value of care. However, low value care is more likely to be found where costs are high and satisfaction is low at the same time. This study (1) describes the distribution and composition of hospital care costs incurred in the last three months of life; (2) examines the relationship between the costs and satisfaction; (3) identifies patients who receive high-cost hospital care with low care satisfaction and reports the factors associated with high costs and dissatisfaction.

Methods

Study design and settings

This study is a secondary analysis using UK data from the two International Access Rights & Empowerment cross-sectional, mortality follow-back surveys (IARE and IARE II).12–14 The respondents were next of kin of deceased patients, identified by clinical teams based at two large hospitals in the UK (characteristics see Appendix 2). IARE targeted patients who were ≥65 years old, who had at least one contact with the participating hospital palliative care team, and who died 4-10 months prior to the survey date. IARE II targeted patients who were aged ≥65 years and died in the study hospitals 4-10 months before the survey date: they may or may not have had contact with the hospital palliative care team. Participants were excluded if their contact information was incomplete or if there was an ongoing legal case regarding the care received by the patients at the hospital where they died. IARE was conductued between November 2012 and August 2014. IARE II was conducted between February 2017 and January 2019.

We formatted the report in accordance with the STrengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline 15 and MORECare statement.16,17

Questionnaires and data collection

The questionnaires included questions regarding demographics and experiences at EoL. It also collected information on care utilisation during the last three months of life using the Client Service Receipt Inventory (CSRI). 18 The questionnaires were pre-piloted before IARE started. 12 Consenting respondents returned the self-completed questionnaires via pre-stamped envelopes. Patients’ medical information was also retrieved from medical records. The questionnaires in IARE and II were nearly identical. Given the similarity in recruiting criteria, we pooled the data in the analysis.

Outcome variables

We calculated the cost of hospital care by multiplying the quantity of services in the last 3 months of life by the corresponding unit cost (see Appendix 1). The primary source for unit costs was the 2022 report by the Personal Social Services Research Unit (PSSRU). 19 In cases of uncertainty, opinions were sought from health care professionals in the relevant fields. The IARE questionnaires did not include out-of-pocket expenses, so they could not be included in the cost estimates. The distribution of EoL costs is wide and skewed. 20 We classified hospital cost as high when it falls within the top 20% of the overall distribution which have been shown to represent over 50% of end-of-life costs. 7 All costs are presented in Pounds Sterling (GBP).

Satisfaction with care was assessed by asking respondents whether patients, families, and friends were satisfied with the care provided at EoL.

Explanatory variables

We selected explanatory variables based on previous reports and clinical experience.7,12,21,22 They included age, sex, race/ethnicity, financial status, social support, diagnosis, comorbidity, caregivers’ report on the duration of illness, and utilisation of informal care. In the surveys financial status was reported in three categories: living comfortably, doing alright, and just getting by or worse. We grouped patients into five categories based on disease trajectory and diagnosis: cancer; chronic organ failure; neurodegenerative diseases; acute diseases or sudden death (including acute infections, vascular events, and trauma); other multi-comorbidity and frailty (diagnosed by clinicians as having frailty or multi-comorbidity, or with ≥3 severe conditions); and miscellaneous (with non-severe diagnoses).23,24 Comorbidity burden was assessed using the original version of the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). 25

Analysis

Stata/MP 18.0 was used for all analyses. We investigated the missing data rate, patterns and potential mechanisms. Little’s test for missing completely at random (MCAR) was applied.26,27 The missing cost data were imputed with the mean of 20 values generated by multiple imputation using the predictive mean matching method, with age and sex as predictors. Other missing data were imputed with the mode (for categorical data) or the median (for ordinal data). We used the chi-square test to examine the relationship between high-cost hospital care and satisfaction. Multinomial logistic regression was used to examine the characteristics of patients who received high-cost, low-satisfaction care. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using: (1) Data imputed with an alternative answer for care satisfaction; (2) Median imputation for cost; and (3) Complete data. The margins command in Stata was used to generate the probability of types of care, given different levels of key variables.

Results

Sample characteristics

The IARE team distributed 1,216 surveys in London, of which 404 (33.2%) were completed and returned: 245 (32.4%) in IARE and 159 (34.6%) in IARE II. Missing data were scattered across the CSRI for hospital care (2.2–17.8%), satisfaction (6.9%), symptom burden (5.2%), and the explanatory variables (0–3.0%), with no discernible patterns. According to Little’s test, the data were likely missing completely at random (p = 0.73). Table 1 and Appendix 2 present the characteristics of the patients and respondents, respectively.

The distribution and composition of hospital care cost

Hospital care comprised 87.3% of the total formal care cost. Figure 1 illustrates the distribution and composition of mean hospital care costs across deciles. The 10th decile accounted for 33.2% of these costs, while the 9th accounted for 18.5%, indicating that approximately 50% of hospital costs were incurred by 20% of patients. The costs for outpatient care, including emergency care, were low across all deciles. Non-ICU inpatient care constituted most of the hospital costs for most patients. However, the increase in ICU care was prominent from the 8th to the 10th deciles. The mean hospital care costs in each decile are detailed in Appendix 3.

The relationship between hospital care cost and satisfaction with care at EoL

Based on the costs incurred in hospital and perceived satisfaction with care at EoL, patients were divided into four groups: (1) Those receiving low-cost hospital care and were satisfied at EoL (206 patients, 51%); (2) Those receiving low-cost hospital care and were not satisfied (118 patients, 29.2%); (3) Those receiving high-cost hospital care and were satisfied (50 patients, 12.4%); and (4) Those receiving high-cost hospital care and were not satisfied (30 patients, 7.4%). The chi-square test showed that whether a patient experienced high-cost hospital care was not related to their satisfaction with care (p = 0.875).

Characteristics of older patients receiving high-cost, low-satisfaction hospital care at EoL

Table 2 presents the results of the regression analysis. Patients who were young-old (age 65-74, RRR = 3.2, 95% CI: 1.09 to 9.42), had the lowest income (RRR = 4.12, 95% CI: 1.29 to 13.15), and those with a short illness duration (1 month to less than 1 year, RRR = 3.93, 95% CI: 1.37 to 11.21) were more likely to receive high-cost hospital care but with low satisfaction at EoL as opposed to receiving low-cost hospital care and being satisfied. Patients with an illness duration of less than one month were also more than three times as likely to receive high-cost, low-satisfaction care, although these results did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.08). The sensitivity analyses showed similar results (Appendices 5 to 7).

Discussion

Main findings

The sample for the IARE studies was deliberately drawn from older patients with complex needs, most of whom died in hospitals. Compared to decedents across the wider population this sample has a higher proportion of patients with high hospital care costs. Studies have shown that palliative care interventions for such patients are associated with lower cost care trajectories with better outcomes for patients and families.20,28,29 For these patients, hospital care comprised the majority of formal care costs and was mainly incurred in an inpatient setting. The distribution and composition of formal care align with findings from other studies.7,20 Notably, about 50% of the total cost was incurred by 20% of the patients. However, cost was not correlated with satisfaction. The group of patients experienced high-cost hospital care, but who were dissatisfied at EoL, is likely to include many with low-value care. We found three characteristics associated with this group of patients: young-old (65–74 years), low income, and a shorter illness duration.

It is notable that low income was associated with what is likely to be high-cost, low-value hospital care. There are several possible explanations for why those on low income are at greater risk of high-cost care and low satisfaction. Poorer people may have more difficulty in accessing timely care and may be less able to navigate the care system. A systematic review and meta-analysis indicated that patients with lower socioeconomic status had increased odds of receiving acute care services and experiencing hospital deaths, while they were less likely to receive specialised palliative care. 30 A recent review highlighted an evidence gap in literature regarding the relationship between socioeconomic position and inequity in end-of-life care. 31 However, emerging evidence suggests that addressing health inequities at a societal level is possible.32,33

There is ongoing concern that longevity will lead to higher healthcare cost, such as “care of the elderly is bankrupting the NHS”.34,35 This argument is based on the observation that older individuals account for a substantial portion of healthcare costs. However, the largest proportion of these costs is incurred during the EoL period, which affects decedents of all ages, and are higher for younger decedents.36,37 As shown in other studies, when adjusted for proximity to death, age was inversely associated with formal healthcare costs. 36 According to a PSSRU report, individuals over 80 years old experienced more frequent pain relief compared to younger patients, who were less likely to rate the quality of care as excellent. 38 This evidence supports our findings that the oldest old had a better chance of receiving higher-value hospital care.

We also found that a short duration of illness (1 month to <1 year) was associated with high-cost, low-satisfaction hospital care at EoL, while the shortest illness duration showed a weaker association and no statistical significance. The likely explanation for the apparently weaker effect of very short illness duration is that such people are unlikely to use high levels of service until the last month of life, and service use in this study is measured over the last three months. Longer illness duration (over one year) may be associated with slower disease progression and also may allow time to make plans to avoid low-value care at EoL, whereas for a short illness duration, there was sufficient time for low-value care but insufficient time to avoid it. An illness shorter than a month may lead to difficulties in coping and dissatisfaction but is not long enough to yield high costs.

Some respondents described the patients’ illness as shorter than 1 month, even though the patient had a chronic diagnosis. The duration of illness reflects the respondents’ understanding of the illness trajectory.

These results underscore the importance of information and expectations in end-of-life care. Older age and the presence of long-term illnesses afford patients and their families more opportunities to adjust their perceptions of treatment based on the information accumulated throughout the illness trajectory. 39 For example, patients are more likely to engage in advance care planning at an older age. 22 Patients’ and families’ understanding through effective communication and comprehensive information can be beneficial. 40 Communication is often neglected in medical practice, yet it can help patients avoid low-value care that does not align with their goals. 41 On the other hand, medical professionals should work within a multidisciplinary team to cultivate a better and shared understanding of patients’ prognoses. Fragmented care can hinder professional carers’ ability to grasp the full picture.

Interpretation and policy implications

Understanding the main risk factors for high-cost, low-value care allows service providers to design services to change care pathways. For example, specialist palliative care services have been shown to increase patient-centred outcomes with a cost-saving effect.11,29,42 Emergency department-initiated palliative care consultations for advanced cancer can also improve patients’ quality of life. 43 IARE did not record the “dosage” of hospital palliative care, thus it was unable to investigate its association with the value of care. However, evidence from other studies suggests that more palliative care resources should be allocated to patients who are at higher risk of high-cost, low-value care, i.e., young-old patients with low income and a shorter duration of illness.20,30 There could also be support in deprived areas to flag these patients to primary care providers and hospitals, and to provide information and aid advance care planning. Future studies should be carried out to test these service models.

Limitations

Low satisfaction with care is not a perfect indicator of low value care, but we would argue that its use in conjunction with costs of care provides useful insight into the circumstances where low value care is most likely to occur. Other studies of costs and outcomes for people near the end-of-life report lower costs and better experiences for patients whose care trajectories are carefully planned.23,44

Mortality follow-back studies can be subject to bias resulting from the way in which participants were recruited, reducing the generalisability of the findings. 45 In this study the focus was not on representing decedents in general, but rather to understand the trajectories and experiences of patients with serious and complex illness near the end of life. While disease modifying interventions as well as symptom management may be justified in some cases, the balance of the goals of treatment will be to achieve the best experience and quality of life for patients. The 33% response rate, although typical of follow-back studies, may introduce some non-response bias. The accuracy of proxy-reported information may be compromised. In our study, not all respondents were primary carers or present at the patient’s death. These factors, together with possible memory decline over several months and the presence of grief, may have reduced their ability to provide accurate service utilisation data.

IARE and IARE2 did not include detailed data on hospital procedures and the volume of palliative care received and did not record some important items such as advanced care planning. There is no evidence that these limitations introduce bias into the results, but they have limited the scope for some potentially interesting analyses. Missing data was a problem but probably did not lead to bias,17,46 since the missingness appeared to be MCAR and was addressed by applying multiple imputation and sensitivity analyses. Our 2 × 2 grouping strategy may have identified individuals who were more likely to incur high costs. Future studies should collect more data and employ continuous measurements for both costs and outcomes. This approach would yield a more nuanced understanding of value.

The sample sizes in IARE and IARE II were calculated for the original main research questions.12,14 Even when the data from both studies were combined, the sample was too small to provide more definitive results such as the effect of very short illness duration. However, the findings do suggest that very short illness duration may be associated with poor treatment choices, high hospital costs and low care satisfaction. Further studies of this group of patients may provide some useful guidance on how to avoid low value care. The small sample size and missing data also limited our ability to include more indicators of socioeconomic status.

Teisberg et al. 47 have warned that satisfaction surveys tend to ask patients, “How were we?”, while value-based care should instead focus on, “How are you?” However, satisfaction remains a useful metric as it aligns with individual values and captures information about the care process. In end-of-life care, the process itself can serve as the outcome. 48 Given the sample frame, the study did not include many decedents with very low costs, so our low-hospital-cost patients would have on average higher costs compared to decedents in general.

Although the sample was drawn from a population with complex palliative care needs at the end of life, our data did not specify the actual individual needs, which again limited our ability to measure value. This also introduced potential heterogeneity into the study population. However, we attempted to adjust for needs using need-related variables such as age, diagnosis, comorbidity burden, and cost of informal care. In future, routine assessment of palliative care needs among targeted populations may not only enhance patient care but also provide opportunities for similar studies. 49

Conclusion

A group of decedents is receiving high-cost, potentially low-value hospital care at EoL, typically those who are younger, have low incomes, and have a short illness duration. This highlights the importance of realistic expectations and good decision making in end-of-life care. It also suggests that healthcare inequity may hinder the efficient use of hospital resources. At the individual level, medical professionals should prioritise effective communication with these patients and their families, as well as within their multidisciplinary teams. At the systemic level, services should be developed to optimise the care pathway for patients at high risk of high-cost, low-value care. At the societal level, the healthcare system should invest in promoting healthy ageing and addressing healthcare inequities.
