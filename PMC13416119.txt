Age‐associated immune dysregulation links to disease severity in macrolide‐resistant Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia: Insights from a comparative juvenile–adult mouse model
Authors: Xuejun Li, Yudong Wang, Liyong Liu, Mengmeng Gong, Jinlong Wang, Yuqing Cao, Zhixin Sun, Fanliang Meng, Qiuyan Wang, Hongji Wu, Fei Zhao, Yan Ma, Yibai Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMacrolide‐resistant mycoplasma pneumoniae (MRMP) infections are more common and severe in children, indicating that age‐related immune dysregulation affects disease outcomes, yet the underlying mechanisms remain unclear and suitable pediatric models are lacking. This study compared juvenile and adult mice with MRMP infection to identify age‐dependent immune features linked to lung injury and establish a relevant animal model for pediatric research.MethodsThree‐week‐old (juvenile) and 8‐week‐old (adult) BALB/c mice were infected with MRMP. Pathological phenotypes included lung index, bacterial load, and lung histopathology scores. Serum inflammatory storm profiles were characterized using the Olink 48‐plex assay. High‐dimensional immune mapping of the spleen was performed by cytometry by time‐of‐flight mass spectrometry (CyTOF). Integrated analyses including sparse partial least squares‐discriminant analysis (sPLS‐DA), correlation heatmaps, and KEGG pathway enrichment were created to explore associations among differential immune subsets, inflammatory mediators, and M. pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) phenotypes.ResultsNo statistically significant differences in bacterial load were observed among the MRMP‐infected juvenile (Juv+MRMP) and MRMP‐infected adult (Ad+MRMP) groups (p > 0.05). However, the Juv+MRMP group exhibited significantly higher lung indices and more severe pulmonary histopathological damage, indicating a dissociation between bacterial burden and tissue injury. Olink analysis revealed significantly higher levels of serum chemokines (CCL2, CCL12, CCL22) and proinflammatory cytokines (IL‐17A, IL‐1β, IL‐27) in the Juv+MRMP group than in the Ad+MRMP group. CyTOF analysis revealed a starkly different immune landscape: Juvenile mice exhibited excessive innate immune activation, with significant increases in neutrophil and monocyte infiltration, as well as elevated proportions of M2 macrophages and NK cells (p < 0.05); while adaptive immune responses were impaired, characterized by reduced proportions of CD4+ T cells and Treg cells, and maintained a high reserve of naïve Th cells compared with those in adult mice, along with elevated proportions of activated Tc cells (CD69+ Tc) (p < 0.05). Correlation analysis revealed that age and infection status are key factors driving host immune variability. M2 macrophages and NK cells were potentially associated with lung tissue pathology scores. Pathogen load correlates with both initial immune cells (CD24+ naïve Th, CD24+ naïve Tc) and effector immune cells (neutrophils, monocytes, CD69+ Tc), which may contribute to a Th1‐type inflammatory response involving CCL2 and CCL4. Feedback‐upregulated Treg cells may also aid pathogen clearance by promoting IL‐10 expression. Leukocyte migration and granulocyte chemotaxis were potentially associated with age‐dependent differences in inflammatory immune responses between juvenile and adult mice post‐MRMP infection.ConclusionThe severity of pediatric MRMP infection may be correlated with age‐associated immune dysregulation—characterized by heightened innate activation with insufficient adaptive regulation—rather than by bacterial load alone. The three‐week‐old juvenile mouse model appears to recapitulate key features of MRMP infection in children and could potentially serve as a useful platform for mechanistic and intervention studies.Keywords: adaptive immunity, age‐associated susceptibility, inflammatory response, innate immunity, juvenile mice, macrolide‐resistant Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia

INTRODUCTION

Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) is a predominant causative agent of community‐acquired pneumonia (CAP) globally, with a detection rate as high as 40% in children and adolescents aged 5 years and older, 1 accompanied by an upward trend in infection rates. 2 Recent surveillance data from China reveal distinct seasonal and age‐related clustering patterns of MP infection. The annual positivity rate peaks in November (33.56%), with the highest incidence concentrated in children aged 6 to 7 years, demonstrating a consistent year‐on‐year upward trend. 3 Although most MP infections are self‐limiting, some patients—particularly those who fail antibiotic treatment—develop severe macrolide‐resistant M. pneumoniae (MRMP), posing a significant threat to pediatric health and public disease control efforts. Currently, the most pressing challenge in pediatric respiratory medicine is the global spread of MRMP. 4 Reports indicate that from July 2023 to May 2024, the national MRMP ratio in China surged from 80% to 93.02%—a rate of increase far exceeding the cumulative rise observed over the past decade (5.3%). 3

The spread of MRMP has led to frequent failures of standard first‐line treatments such as macrolide antibiotics, significantly prolonging the duration of fever and cough while substantially increasing the risk of severe pneumonia. Alternative treatments, such as doxycycline and quinolones, carry potential risks for children, including effects on dental development, joint cartilage, and cardiac toxicity, respectively. 5 , 6 , 7 This therapeutic dilemma highlights the need to move beyond antimicrobial strategies and to better understand host–pathogen interactions and host defense–driven injury during MRMP infection.

Clinical studies have revealed significant age‐associated heterogeneity in the severity of MRMP infection. Compared to adults, infants, young children, and preschoolers demonstrate higher susceptibility to MRMP infection and are more prone to developing severe hyperinflammatory, coagulation abnormalities, and multiorgan damage following infection. 8 , 9 This suggests that the host's age‐dependent state of immune development may be a key determinant of infection outcomes. However, current research on how age factors remodel the immunopathological characteristics of MRMP remains limited. Existing research has largely focused on pathogen analysis or utilized single‐age (typically adult) mouse models, lacking systematic comparisons of immune response differences between hosts of different ages (e.g., children vs. adults) under identical pathogen exposure. This gap hinders a mechanistic understanding of why children are more susceptible to severe MRMP. Consequently, developing animal models that accurately simulate human age‐associated differences in immune–inflammatory responses is crucial. BALB/c mice represent a classic animal model for establishing MP infection. 10 Notably, 3‐week‐old BALB/c juvenile mice, with their immature immune systems and lung development equivalent to that of approximately 2‐year‐old children, are considered ideal for modeling the immune status of human infants and young children. 11 , 12

In this study, we utilized MRMP strains cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen from the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention's Institute for Infectious Disease to infect 3‐week‐old (juvenile) and 8‐week‐old (adult) BALB/c mice. Because females exhibit stronger innate and adaptive (humoral and cellular) immune responses compared to males, 13 and studies have shown that after the COVID‐19 pandemic, the positive rate of MP in boys is lower than that in girls, 14 female BALB/c mice were used as the research subjects in this study. The aim was to systematically characterize differences between juvenile and adult mice in pathogen clearance, inflammatory levels, and immune cell response profiles during MRMP infection. Furthermore, using multidimensional correlation analysis, we aimed to investigate whether age‐associated dysregulation of the innate and adaptive immune system is closely linked to disease progression. This work seeks to explore the utility of the 3‐week‐old juvenile mouse model for pediatric MRMP research and to provide experimental evidence for the potential reasons behind more severe MRMP symptoms in pediatric patients, while offering potential opportunities for immunological intervention strategies targeting MRMP. A graphical summary of the study design is presented in Figure 1.

Macrolide‐resistant Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MRMP) infected both juvenile and adult mice, with results compared to normal juvenile mice. The findings revealed comparable bacterial loads between juvenile and adult mice, yet juvenile mice exhibited more severe lung damage and elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines. This phenomenon stems from age‐dependent dysregulation of the innate‐adaptive immune response. Three‐week‐old juvenile mice serve as a reliable model for studying MRMP infection in children.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethical statement

The study adheres to the relevant national guidelines on laboratory animal welfare and ethics. All animal experimentation protocols implemented have been approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the Institute of Chinese Materia Medica, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (approval no.: 2025B170).

MRMP strain

The strain was obtained from the liquid nitrogen cryopreserved collection of the National Institute for Communicable Disease Control and Prevention, Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, designated as ICDC‐BJCDC‐005. Its in vitro MIC values for erythromycin, azithromycin, and clarithromycin were 64, 4, and 64 μg/mL, respectively, all exceeding the 1 μg/mL resistance threshold, confirming it as an MRMP strain. After polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification and product sequencing, the 23S ribosomal DNA (rDNA) domain V sequence of the strain was obtained, with a length of 927 bp. Compared to the 23S rDNA domain V sequence of the MP strain, ATCC 29342 (GenBank: NR_077056.1) revealed an A2063G mutation.

Animals

SPF‐grade 3‐week‐old female Balb/c mice were randomly assigned to a normal juvenile group (Juv group, n = 3) and an infected juvenile group (Juv+MRMP group, n = 5). Additionally, 8‐week‐old female Balb/c mice were designated as the adult infected group (Ad+MRMP group, n = 3). Only female mice were used in this study to minimize the confounding effects of male‐specific hormonal fluctuations (e.g., testosterone) on the immune response to MRMP infection, while aligning with the current epidemiological characteristic that the positive rate is higher in girls than in boys. All animals were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Laboratory Animal License No.: SYXK(Jing)2022‐0029) and housed at the Animal Center of the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention. The animal housing conditions were maintained at 22 ± 2°C, 50% ± 10% relative humidity, and a 12‐h light–dark cycle. All mice had free access to food and water and underwent a 2‐day acclimatization period before the formal experimental procedures.

Model building

Two groups of infected mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and then infected with the identified MRMP strain via intranasal instillation (1 × 109 CFU/mL, 30 μL per dose, once daily) in an ultra‐clean workbench for 3 consecutive days. The Juv group received intranasal instillations of the same volume of saline solution using the same method. Criteria for successful model validation included (i) MP nucleic acid positivity in lung tissue using fluorescent PCR/quantitative PCR (qPCR) after infection, (ii) gross evidence of lung congestion/edema/consolidation at necropsy, and (iii) histopathological evidence of inflammatory injury on hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining. Body weight was monitored as a general health indicator but was not used as a required criterion for successful infection, particularly in juvenile mice.

Reagents and instruments

Olink Target 48 Mouse Cytokine Kit (catalog no.: 934002, Olink, part of Thermo Fisher Scientific, Uppsala, Sweden), LunaStain cell staining buffer (catalog no.: PB005, Polaris Biology, China), cisplatin reagent (catalog no.: PB002, Polaris Biology, China), Fc receptor blocking solution (catalog no.: 101319, Biolegend, USA), Ir‐DNA intercalator reagent (catalog no.: PB012, Polaris Biology, China), QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (catalog no.: 51306, QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), LunaAcq acquisition solution (catalog no.: PB011, Polaris Biology, China), 2 × TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix (catalog no.: B639273, Solarbio, Beijing, China), small animal anesthesia machine (model: R500IE, Ruowei, Shenzhen, China), BLS‐2 laminar flow cabinet (model: BLS‐2, Suzhou Antai Air Technology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China), paraffin microtome (model: RM2245, Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany), tissue homogenizer (model: DS‐1, Shanghai Jingxin, Shanghai, China), real‐time fluorescent quantitative PCR instrument (model: QuantStudio 5, Applied Biosystems, Waltham, WA, USA), Lunarion mass cytometer (Polaris Biology, China).

Sample acquisition

On day 7 postinfection (7 dpi), relevant specimens were collected for testing. First, mice were deeply anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of peripheral blood was collected via orbital venous plexus puncture. After standing at room temperature for 1–2 h, the blood was centrifuged. The supernatant serum was collected, aliquoted into sterile EP tubes, and promptly stored at −80°C for subsequent cytokine detection. After blood collection, the abdominal and thoracic cavities were incised under aseptic conditions. The spleen and lungs were completely dissected and removed. The harvested lungs were promptly weighed to calculate the lung index. The right lung was placed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for fixation, intended for subsequent histopathological examination. The left lung was rapidly immersed in liquid nitrogen for cryopreservation, reserved for bacterial load determination. After being rinsed with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), spleen tissue was directly placed into centrifuge tubes containing 1640 medium and rapidly homogenized through a cell strainer to prepare a lymphocyte mononuclear cell suspension. After cell counting, the suspension was resuspended and fixed, then cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen for subsequent immunophenotyping analysis.

Indicator detection

Lung index measurement

Mice were euthanized at 7 dpi. Whole lungs were promptly isolated and weighed to determine wet weight. The lung index was then calculated as follows: lung index = wet lung weight (g)/body weight (g) to assess the degree of inflammatory edema in lung tissue.

qPCR determination of bacterial load

Bacterial load detection and analysis were performed using quantitative real‐time PCR. Mouse left lungs were homogenized and inoculated into MPP medium, cultured at 37°C for 14–28 days to validate the infectivity of the model. Subsequently, lung tissue homogenate samples were directly used for DNA extraction and bacterial load detection. Total DNA was extracted from the lung homogenate using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (catalog no.: 51306, QIAGEN). Detection was performed using an MP‐specific quantitative real‐time PCR assay targeting the MPP‐specific sequence repMP1CARDS toxin gene (mpn372) (comparison of commercial and in‐house real‐time PCR assays used for the detection of MP). The reaction system employed 2× TaqMan PCR Master Mix (catalog no.: B639273, Solarbio, Beijing, China). For bacterial load quantification, a standard curve was constructed using concentration gradient nucleic acids from the ATCC 29342 strain; the nucleic acids were quantified using Qubit 4.0 (Thermo Fisher) prior to the preparation of a standardized dilution series (ranging from 107 copies to 1 copy), and each dilution was detected by the aforementioned qPCR assay to generate the standard curve. The standard curve is provided in Figure S1. In both positive control and blank control reactions, samples exhibiting specific amplification curves were determined to be MP positive, whereas those without detectable amplification curves were determined to be negative. MP load per gram of lung tissue was calculated based on the standard curve. Because the qPCR assay targeted the MP‐specific repMP1CARDS toxin gene (mpn372), which is uniquely expressed by MP, no additional housekeeping gene or normalization control was required for bacterial load quantification.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining and pulmonary tissue pathological scoring

Lung tissue was fixed with paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 3‐μm‐thick serial sections, which were subsequently stained using HE. Inflammatory cell infiltration and histopathological damage in the lungs were observed using a light microscope. Each slide was blinded and assessed for inflammatory severity according to predefined semiquantitative scoring criteria. 15

The pulmonary tissue pathology scoring criteria are as follows:

0, No significant abnormalities observed: no pathological changes were recorded. No enlargement of pulmonary alveoli was noted in lung tissue; no widening of the pulmonary interstitium was observed; no interstitial edema was present. No inflammation was seen around the bronchioles within the lungs; no hyperplasia of the tracheal lumen lining was noted; no swelling was observed; no structural abnormalities were detected in the tissue.

1, Minimal: small focal lesions visible under a microscope, affecting less than 10% of the tissue.

2, Mild: small, multifocal lesions are visible under a microscope, affecting 10% to 20% of the tissue.

3, Moderate: the lesion extends over a larger area or involves a greater number of lesions, exceeding the species' baseline prevalence. This may begin to affect organ function.

4, Moderate to severe: similar to grade 3, but the lesion involves more tissue (up to 75%), potentially affecting organ function.

5, Severe: changes affect nearly the entire tissue, highly likely to impair organ function.

Olink Target 48 Mouse Cytokine Kit for serum inflammatory cytokine detection

Serum inflammatory cytokines were detected using the Target 48 Mouse Cytokine Kit. The experimental setup included three internal controls (incubation control, extension control, detection control) and three external controls (negative control, sample control, calibrator) to ensure comprehensive quality control. Operational quality control requires that the standard deviation of NPX (relative protein units on a log2 scale) for incubation and detection controls be less than 0.2 in samples and less than 0.5 in external controls. Additionally, the NPX deviation of individual samples from the plate median must be less than 0.3. Quality control of the detection method includes evaluating the accuracy of the average concentration of sample controls (bias must be within ±30% of the known value) and its intra‐coefficient of variation (Intra‐CV <30%). The Intra‐CV of calibration standards must also be <30%. Data were ultimately converted from NPX values to concentrations using a four‐parameter logistic (4PL) standard curve and reported in pg/mL.

CyTOF mass spectrometry flow cytometry characterization of spleen immune profiling

High‐dimensional single‐cell profiling of mouse splenic immune cells was performed using mass cytometry. 16 Staining was performed according to Polaris Biology Mass Cytometry Staining Protocol as follows. Briefly, mouse spleen single‐cell suspension was filtered by 40‐μm cell strainer (catalog no.: 352340, Falcon, USA); all cells were uniformly dispersed. Cells were collected using centrifugation and then washed by LunaStain cell staining buffer once. Based on cell counting results, 3 × 106 cells were collected and stained with 10 μL of cisplatin reagent (198Pt cisplatin) for 5 min, and washed once with LunaStain cell staining solution. Subsequently, Fc receptor blocking solution was used for staining for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were incubated with heavy metal‐labeled mouse antibodies mix (Polaris Biology, China) for 30 min at room temperature and then washed twice with LunaStain cell staining buffer. Finally, cells were labeled with Ir‐DNA intercalator reagent (an iridium‐containing nucleic acid intercalator) for 10 min. After labeling, the cells were washed and adjusted to 5 × 105 cells per milliliter with LunaAcq acquisition solution. Cell acquisition was performed on Lunarion mass cytometer. Mass cytometry data were automatically converted to standard FCS 3.0 file during data acquisition. Manual gating was performed using FCS Express 7 software (De Novo Software, USA), with a detailed gating strategy as follows (Figure S2A–F):

Data cleaning and gating of valid cellular events: First, event length gating was performed, where the data from individual pushes were integrated into mass‐specific ion counts for discrete cellular “events,” with the minimum and maximum number of pushes set to 10–150 pushes (consistent with the standard setting of the Lunarion mass cytometer used in this study). Subsequently, the consecutive signal ratio (CSR) parameter, calculated through multifeature coupling to represent the proportion of consecutive valid signals within an event, was used to exclude abnormal events (cell debris and doublets/multiplets), which showed significant deviation from the main population in CSR.

Correlation analysis

Sparse partial least squares discriminant analysis (sPLS‐DA) was applied solely as an exploratory feature selection tool to identify key immune signatures associated with group differences, rather than as a predictive or classification model. To mitigate the risk of overfitting (considering the relatively small sample size, with n = 3–5 per group), parameter optimization was performed via fivefold cross‐validation combined with Q 2 statistics, and only components with Q 2 > 0.097 were retained to ensure model stability. Given the very limited sample size (n = 3–5 per group), permutation testing for sPLS‐DA was not performed, as permutation inference is known to be underpowered and unstable in such small‐sample settings, and cross‐validation is considered the primary and most robust approach to assess model generalizability in this context. After cross‐validation, sample distributions were visualized using score plots, and core immune features were selected based on the loading weights of the first and second components, which reflect the relative contribution of each feature to the multivariate structure rather than formal classification performance. Subsequently, the internal correlations among immune cells and inflammatory factors, along with their association networks with clinical phenotypes (bacterial load, lung index, pathological score), were visualized using a butterfly‐shaped heatmap and Mantel tests. Spearman's correlation analysis was performed to calculate the correlations between each omics feature and environmental factors, and multiple testing correction was implemented using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) procedure with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 to control the FDR across all pairwise correlation tests. Only correlation pairs with adjusted p < 0.05 and absolute correlation coefficient |r| > 0.5 were retained for network construction (Table S1). Finally, the differentially expressed immune cells identified through sPLS‐DA screening and their significantly associated inflammatory cytokines were integrated to form a differential feature set. This feature set was then subjected to GO/KEGG pathway enrichment analysis to reveal the underlying regulatory signaling pathways. This is an exploratory study. It should be explicitly noted that, given the small sample size (n = 3–5 per group) and the exploratory nature of these analyses, the sPLS‐DA findings and correlation networks constructed herein are hypothesis‐generating rather than confirmatory. The identified signatures represent candidate biomarkers and strongly associated multivariate correlates rather than definitive causal pathways or validated classification features, and their biological validity warrants independent validation in larger cohorts. All analyses were performed using Python, R, and relevant software packages (mixOmics, clusterProfiler, etc.).

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (χ¯ ± s). GraphPad Prism 8.0 software was used for graphical representation and basic statistical analysis. Primary comparisons were prespecified as juvenile control versus juvenile MRMP (infection effect in juveniles), and juvenile MRMP versus adult MRMP (age‐associated difference under infection). For continuous variables meeting normality assumptions, comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Post‐hoc pairwise comparisons employed Tukey's test when variances were homogeneous and the Games–Howell test when variances were heterogeneous. For non‐normally distributed continuous variables, Kruskal–Wallis H test was used for multigroup comparisons, followed by Dunn's test for pairwise comparisons. For high‐dimensional readouts (Olink 48‐plex cytokines and CyTOF immune subsets), p‐values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR within each data family, and both raw and adjusted values are reported. Correlation analyses used Spearman's correlation coefficient and Mantel tests. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with a 95% confidence interval.

RESULTS

Juvenile mice exhibit more severe clinical symptoms after infection

MRMP infection does not affect weight gain in adult and juvenile mice

Continuous monitoring of body weight changes in infected mice from day 1 of infection to day 7 postinfection (total 10 days) revealed no significant alterations in the body weight curve (body weight at infection expressed as a percentage of preinfection weight) for the Ad+MRMP group (Figure 2A), indicating that MRMP infection had no significant impact on the physiological state of adult mice. The body weight curves for the Juv and Juv+MRMP groups showed an upward trend (Figure 2A), indicating that MRMP infection did not affect body weight gain in juvenile mice.

Body weight, lung index, bacterial load, and histopathological damage in lung tissue among infected mouse groups. (A) Body weight curve. (B) Lung index. (C) Bacterial load expression. (D) Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining of lung tissue. (E) Histopathological score of lung tissue (*p < 0.05).

MRMP infection significantly increases the lung index in juvenile mice

At 7 dpi, the lung index in the Juv+MRMP group showed no significant change compared to the Juv group (p > 0.05; Figure 2B), whereas the Ad+MRMP group exhibited a reduced lung index (p < 0.05; Figure 2B), suggesting a potential delayed response to MRMP infection in juvenile mice. Furthermore, the lung index in the Juv+MRMP group was significantly higher than that in the Ad+MRMP group (p < 0.05; Figure 2B), suggesting that juvenile mice may experience more severe pulmonary damage following MRMP infection.

There is no significant difference in MRMP clearance capacity between juvenile and adult mice

Results of MRMP‐DNA load in lung tissue revealed no statistically significant differences between the Juv + MRMP and Ad+MRMP groups (p > 0.05; Figure 2C), suggesting that the elevated lung index in juvenile mice may not be directly attributable to bacterial load itself.

Juvenile mice infected with MRMP exhibit more severe pulmonary tissue pathology

HE staining revealed more severe histopathological damage in the lung tissue of the Juv+MRMP group (Figure 2D), primarily manifested as extensive inflammatory cell infiltration around the bronchioles. Semiquantitative analysis showed that the histopathological score in the Juv+MRMP group was significantly higher than that in the Juv group (p < 0.05; Figure 2E), indicating that MRMP infection caused more severe lung tissue damage in juvenile mice. Although the lung tissue pathology score in the Juv+MRMP group was numerically higher than that in the Ad+MRMP group, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05; Figure 2E), which may reflect limited sample size and interindividual variability.

Juvenile mice infected with MRMP exhibit a heightened systemic inflammatory response

Olink results showed that compared to the Ad+MRMP group, the Juv+MRMP group exhibited significantly elevated serum levels of CC chemokine ligands (CCL12, CCL2, CCL22, CCL4), macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (CSF1), CXC chemokine ligands (CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL9, CXCL11), interleukins (IL‐17A, IL‐1β), and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) (p < 0.05; Figure 3A–L). This suggests that juvenile mice exhibit a higher systemic pro‐inflammatory state in response to MRMP infection compared to adult mice. The KEGG bubble plot indicates that the IL‐17 signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, NF‐κB signaling pathway, and differences in toll‐like receptor signaling were linked to the age‐dependent inflammatory mediator profiles observed after MRMP infection (p < 0.05; Figure 3M). Additionally, differential expression was observed in two sets of immunoregulatory/suppressive factors, including CD274 and IL‐10, as shown in Figure S3A–G.

Immune cell profiles in the spleens of MRMP‐infected juvenile and adult mice exhibit significant age‐related differences

Juvenile mice predominantly rely on innate immune responses to resist infection

The UMAP plot derived from high‐dimensional analysis of CD45+ immune cells revealed a significantly dispersed distribution pattern in infected adult mice. In contrast, cells from normal juvenile mice and infected juvenile mice exhibited relatively concentrated distributions, particularly among CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and T cells (Figure 4A). Further analysis examined the proportional distribution of various immune cell types. Regarding adaptive immunity, compared to the Juv group, the Ad+MRMP group exhibited significantly elevated CD4+ T‐cell expression (p < 0.05; Figure 4B), whereas the Juv+MRMP group showed no significant change in CD4+ T‐cell expression (p > 0.05). Intergroup comparisons revealed that the proportion of CD4+ T cells in the Juv+MRMP group was significantly lower than that in the Ad+MRMP group (p < 0.05), whereas the proportion of T cells in the Juv+MRMP group was significantly higher than that in the Ad+MRMP group (p < 0.05; Figure 4B,C). However, the three‐in‐one heatmap revealed no significant differences between the two groups in CD4+ T cells or activation markers such as CD44 and CD4 on T cells (Figure 4H), reflecting an age‐dependent immune profile: juvenile mice exhibit impaired CD4+ T‐cell‐specific immune responses and dysregulated immune–inflammatory responses. There were no statistically significant differences in the proportions of CD8+ T cells, CD4+CD8+ DPT cells, B cells, or Ly‐6C+ B cells among the three groups (p > 0.05; Figure S4A–D).

In terms of innate immunity, compared to the Juv group, the Juv+MRMP group exhibited significantly increased proportions of M2 macrophages and NK cell counts (p < 0.05; Figure 4D,G), suggesting that juvenile mice initiated an innate immune response after infection. Furthermore, both M2 macrophage and neutrophil proportions were downregulated in the Ad+MRMP group (p < 0.01, p < 0.05; Figure 4D,E), indicating that adult mice may resist MRMP infection through mature immune regulatory mechanisms rather than excessive reliance on innate immunity. However, the neutrophil‐to‐monocyte ratio in the Juv+MRMP group was significantly higher than that in the Ad+MRMP group (p < 0.01, p < 0.05; Figure 4E,F), and the triple‐color heatmap primarily revealed a marked increase in the activation markers CD44 and Ly‐6G on neutrophils and monocytes in both groups (Figure 4H), suggesting that juvenile mice rely excessively on innate immune responses to combat infection. The proportion of dendritic cells (DCs) in the Juv group was higher than that in the Ad+MRMP group (p < 0.05), but there was no significant difference in the DC proportion between the Juv+MRMP and Ad+MRMP groups (p > 0.05; Figure S4F). Furthermore, no statistically significant differences were observed in eosinophil and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) expression among the three groups (p > 0.05; Figure S4E–G).

Potential defects in antigen presentation responses by DCs in juvenile mice

In terms of the proportion distribution of DC subsets, compared to the Juv group, the Juv+MRMP and Ad+MRMP groups showed a downward trend in pDC proportion, though this difference did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05; Figure 5B). This preliminary finding suggests that MRMP infection may influence pDC immune distribution, particularly in adult mice, potentially by regulating pDC activity to limit immune‐pathological damage driven by excessive activation of the associated type I interferon (IFN‐I) pathway. There were no significant differences between the Juv+MRMP and Ad+MRMP groups in the proportion distribution of pDCs, cCD1, other DCs, and cCD2 (p > 0.05; Figure 5B–E), and UMAP plots and triple‐combined heatmaps also revealed no significant differences between the two groups (Figure 5A,F), suggesting that juvenile mice may not have effectively initiated DC‐mediated key innate immune responses and antigen presentation programs. t‐SNE plots are shown in Figure S4H.

This indicates that adult mice tend to maintain immune homeostasis through active suppression following infection. Further comparison between the two infection groups revealed that the Ad+MRMP group exhibited a lower proportion of CD24+ naïve Th cells and a higher proportion of Treg cells compared to the Juv+MRMP group (Figure 6D,E). This preliminary finding suggests that juvenile mice possess a higher initial T‐cell reserve, with relatively insufficient immune suppression. Mobilization of the myeloid hematopoietic system in juvenile mice initiates innate immune responses

Further analysis examined changes in monocyte subsets, including classical monocytes (ncMo) and their upstream precursor cells (MDP‐ncMo and GMP‐ncMo). Results showed no significant differences in the proportion distribution of ncMo and their upstream precursor cells among the Juv, Juv+MRMP, and Ad+MRMP groups (p > 0.05). Further analysis of cellular counts revealed that compared to the Ad+MRMP group, the Juv group exhibited a significant increase in mature ncMo counts (p < 0.05; Figure 6H), with a trend toward increased upstream GMP precursor cells (p > 0.05; Figure 6I,J). In contrast, both ncMo and GMP precursor cell counts were markedly elevated in the Juv+MRMP group (p < 0.05; Figure 6G,H). This further confirms that juvenile mice may potentially rely on the innate immune system to combat infection, a mechanism achieved through the emergency mobilization of bone marrow hematopoietic function to expand monocyte production. Compared to adult mice, cell distribution in juvenile mouse subpopulations was relatively concentrated (Figures 6C and S5M), and no significant differences were observed in activation markers across cell subpopulations in the two groups (Figure 6M).

Correlation analysis between clinical phenotypes and specific immune parameters

Age‐dependent differences in immune responses: Infection exacerbates inflammatory reactions in juvenile mice

The sPLS‐DA score plot revealed two key dimensions of sample distribution (Figure 7A). PC1 (contribution rate 42%) completely separated the Ad+MRMP group from the Juv+MRMP group based on age, potentially indicating that age is the primary factor driving overall immune profile differences. PC2 (contribution rate 14%) effectively distinguished infection status within juvenile mice, separating healthy juveniles from infected juveniles. This preliminarily demonstrates that MRMP infection drives a unique immune response state in juvenile mice, even at the same age. Thus, age and infection represent the most critical intergroup differential factors in this study.

The top 20 feature variables with the highest contributions to sPLS‐DA PC1 and PC2 were extracted, and loaded weight plots were generated (Figure 7B,C). Results showed that in the age component (Figure 7B), immune cell expression in the Ad+MRMP group was concentrated in CD69+ Th, naïve Tc, Treg, CD4+ T, and B cells and extended to the left, indicating that adult mice exhibit an active yet controlled adaptive immune response following infection, characterized by activated/memory T cells, regulatory T cells, and B cells as core components. Moreover, against this age‐related backdrop, the Juv+MRMP group exhibited elevated expression of typical pro‐inflammatory factors such as IL‐6, IL‐1β, CXCL1, and CCL2, revealing a hyperinflammatory state in infected juvenile mice. This hyperinflammatory state was accompanied by significantly reduced Treg cell abundance in juvenile mice, which may suggest a potential association between insufficient Treg‐mediated regulation and uncontrolled innate activation. In the context of endogenous infection in juvenile mice (Figure 7C), healthy juvenile mice are characterized by CD24+ naïve Th and CD24+ naïve Tc cells, whereas infected juvenile mice exhibit T cells and CD69+ Tc cells as core populations. This suggests that despite the immaturity of the adaptive immune system in juvenile mice, MRMP infection can still drive the uncontrolled differentiation and expansion of their naïve T cells into effector/activated states.

Immune cells, inflammatory mediators, and clinical phenotypes exhibit strong correlations

At the multidimensional correlation analysis level, within the immune cell populations (left heatmap), CD4+ T cells exhibit positive correlations with CD8+ T cells and Treg cells (dense red regions), while showing negative correlations with monocytes and neutrophils (dense blue regions). Within the inflammatory factors (right heatmap), IL‐2 showed positive correlations with CXCL1, CXCL2, IL‐3, and IL‐1α (yellow dense areas), while exhibiting the negative correlation with Pdcd1lg2 (purple dense areas).

Mantel test results indicate the correlation between M2 macrophages, NK cells, and lung tissue pathology scores (p < 0.05; Figure 7D), suggesting that innate immune cells may be involved in the lung injury process. Multiple immune cells and cytokines showed strong correlations with bacterial load. At the immune cell level, CD24+ naïve Th, CD24+ naïve Tc, pDCs, and GMP‐cMonos exhibited significant correlations with bacterial load (p < 0.05; Figure 7D), suggesting that infection may trigger the early process of immune system transition from an initial reserve state to an effector state. The correlation between effector cells such as neutrophils, monocytes, and CD69+ Tc cells and bacterial load (p < 0.05; Figure 7D) indicates a potential association between these effector cells and bacterial load, and implies that innate and adaptive immunity may work together to contribute to pathogen elimination. The correlation between Tregs and bacterial load may suggest that the intense inflammation triggered by high bacterial load itself activates a negative feedback regulatory circuit, actively upregulating Tregs to prevent immune overactivation and tissue damage. At the level of inflammatory cytokines, pro‐inflammatory factors, including IL‐16, CCL2, CCL4, CCL12, CXCL11, and CXCL12, showed strong correlations with bacterial load (p < 0.05; Figure 7D), revealing an immune response characterized by a Th1‐type inflammatory response. Concurrently, the correlation between pro‐inflammatory or regulatory factors such as IL‐10, IL‐27, CCL22, IFNL2, and HGF, as well as immune checkpoint molecules like CD274 and Pdcd1lg2, and bacterial load (p < 0.05; Figure 7D) indicates a unique phenomenon: under excessive inflammatory conditions, the feedback activation of Tregs may potentially upregulate the expression of anti‐inflammatory factors. However, these findings are only observational speculations, and further experiments are needed to verify whether there is a clear causal relationship between immune cells, cytokines, and lung injury.

Enhanced leukocyte migration and granulocyte chemotaxis signatures were associated with the more severe lung pathology observed in juvenile mice relative to adults

To systematically characterize the cellular and molecular features distinguishing Juv+MRMP from Ad+MRMP during MRMP infection, a multidimensional “immune cells‐cytokine‐pathway” interaction network was constructed based on differentially expressed immune cells and inflammatory cytokines (Figure 7E). Network analysis revealed that neutrophils and monocytes were the primary sources of effector cells, forming dense connections that tightly link them to the core inflammatory factor cluster (middle layer). CCL2, CXCL2, IL‐1β, and CSF1 were identified as pivotal hub molecules linking upstream cells to downstream functions. These factors belong to the category of intrinsic pro‐inflammatory cytokines, suggesting that the characteristic “hyperinflammatory” state observed in juvenile mice postinfection may be driven by intrinsic immune cells through specific pro‐inflammatory factors, constituting a self‐amplifying inflammatory feedback loop. GO/KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that core factors driving age‐related differences were significantly enriched in pathways such as leukocyte migration and granulocyte chemotaxis. This suggests that pro‐inflammatory factors released by neutrophils and monocytes, along with the chemotactic networks they form, recruit innate immune cells to lung tissue, creating an uncontrolled inflammatory positive feedback loop. This pattern may contribute to the more severe lung injury observed in juvenile mice compared to adults.

DISCUSSION

The surge in MRMP prevalence has become a significant challenge to global pediatric health, particularly as children exhibit more severe clinical outcomes compared to adults. 4 , 17 Although age‐related immune differences may be underlying causes, the precise immunological mechanisms behind this susceptibility remain unclear due to the lack of a systematic comparative model involving exposure to the same drug‐resistant Mycoplasma strain. This study innovatively constructed a parallel infection model involving juvenile and adult mice to conduct an in‐depth analysis of this age‐dependent immune response. Our findings reveal a critical phenomenon: despite comparable lung bacterial loads between juvenile and adult mice, juvenile mice exhibit significantly more severe pulmonary pathological damage and systemic inflammation. This phenomenon of “decoupling between bacterial load and pathological damage” fundamentally challenges the traditional view that disease severity correlates linearly with microbial replication. Instead, our data support an immunopathogenic profile in pediatric‐like MRMP characterized by heightened innate activation coupled with insufficient adaptive immune regulation.

A key finding of this study is the dissociation between pathogen load and tissue damage. Previous research has often used bacterial load as the primary surrogate indicator for disease severity. 18 , 19 Clinical studies reveal that MP DNA load is significantly positively correlated with length of hospital stay, peak fever temperature, C‐reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), IL‐6, and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels. 20 However, this study found that while the MRMP‐DNA load in the lung tissue of juvenile mice was similar to that in adult mice, they exhibited a significantly elevated lung index and more severe pulmonary histopathological lesions. This suggests that immature hosts do not exhibit defects in controlling bacterial replication but rather show abnormalities in regulating organ damage associated with pathogen clearance. Correlation analysis further supports this perspective: pathological scores correlate strongly primarily with host innate immune markers (such as M2 macrophages and NK cells) rather than with bacterial load itself. This distinction is critical for pediatric clinical management, suggesting that in severe pediatric cases, treatment strategies relying solely on antibiotics to eradicate bacteria may be insufficient to halt disease progression primarily driven by immune‐mediated mechanisms.

Furthermore, experimental data revealed a deceptive covert progression in juvenile infections. Unlike adult models where infection typically causes weight loss, 21 MRMP‐infected juvenile mice maintained a positive growth trajectory. We hypothesize that robust anabolic drivers during early development mask the catabolic stress induced by infection. 22 This finding replicates the phenomenon commonly observed in clinical severe pediatric MRMP, where “happy hypoxemia” or mild systemic symptoms are dissociated from severe radiographic consolidation. 23 Therefore, this underscores the limitations of relying solely on general physiological parameters such as weight or behavior to assess the severity of illness in infants, young children, or preschoolers. Clinicians must remain highly vigilant for substantial lung injury and damage to other systemic organs, even in the absence of systemic deterioration in the patient.

At the cellular level, the immune profile of juvenile mice exhibits a dysregulated “hyperinflammatory” characterized by an excessive response in the innate immune component. Olink and CyTOF data analysis revealed a massive influx of neutrophils and monocytes, accompanied by elevated levels of chemokines such as CCL2, CCL12, and CCL22. Although neutrophil recruitment is a hallmark of MP pneumonia, 24 , 25 the inflammatory pathological damage observed in juvenile mice may suggest a potential defect in the resolution phase of inflammation. The correlation between pulmonary pathology and the abundance of M2 macrophages and NK cells elucidates that the reason of lung tissue injury is associated with hyperinflammation and the absence of effective repair, which appears inconsistent with previous studies, as M2 macrophages are typically linked to anti‐inflammation and tissue repair. 26 Although studies have shown that the notion that M1 macrophages are pro‐inflammatory and M2 macrophages are anti‐inflammatory is overly simplistic, cells exhibit extremely high plasticity in response to microenvironmental stimuli. 27 Their abnormal expansion in acute high‐cytokine environments may lead to pathological remodeling and fibrin deposition. 28 However, based on our phenotypic panel, the identified cell cluster is more appropriately described as a CD206+MERTK+F4/80+ macrophage subset, or an M2‐like population, rather than a functionally defined M2 macrophage state. Thus, the observed association with lung pathology should be interpreted as a correlation with a CD206‐enriched macrophage profile rather than direct evidence of pathogenic M2 macrophage activity. Similarly, although NK cell hyperactivation serves a defensive purpose, it may exacerbate epithelial damage in the fragile lungs of juvenile mice through antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity. 29

In contrast, the adaptive immune response in juvenile mice exhibits functional impairment, such as a specific arrest in T‐cell differentiation, characterized by a marked elevation of CD24+ naïve Th cells and CD69+ Tc cells, coupled with the absence of differentiated effector CD4+ T cells. CD69 is an early activation marker, 30 indicating that although juvenile mouse T cells can rapidly recognize MRMP antigens early on, they fail to further differentiate into the functional effector subsets (Th1/Th2) required for targeted clearance. This differentiation disorder forces the host to rely solely on nonspecific and destructive innate immune mechanisms. This stands in stark contrast to the response in adult mice, where balanced expansion of effector T cells promotes pathogen control while minimizing immunopathological damage. Moreover, the ultimate decisive factor driving disease severity in juvenile mice may be the absence of moderate immunosuppression. Data indicate a significant deficiency of regulatory Tregs in juvenile mice. Tregs are crucial for suppressing excessive inflammation and preventing immunopathology. 31 Although we observed compensatory upregulation of immune checkpoint molecules (PD‐L1/CD274) and IL‐10, potentially representing the body's attempt to limit the inflammatory cascade, these associations clearly proved insufficient in the context of absolute Treg deficiency. This simultaneous imbalance of “brake failure” (Treg deficiency) and “accelerator overdrive” (congenital hyperactivity) may underlie the mechanism of the severe MRMP “hyperinflammatory” phenotype, consistent with clinical reports of altered Treg function in children with severe pneumonia. 32 The correlation analysis revealed that the possible mechanism of this difference lies in the cascade of hyperinflammatory caused by innate immune cells such as neutrophils and monocytes driving leukocyte migration and granulocyte chemotaxis through specific pro‐inflammatory factors (CCL2, CXCL2, IL‐1β, and CSF1).

Correlation analysis ultimately revealed that age is the key factor driving this disparity. Innate immune cells are directly associated with pathological lung tissue damage, a consequence that could result from juvenile mice's potential excessive reliance on innate immunity leading to a hyperinflammatory response. 33 Despite comparable bacterial loads in both groups, the immune response patterns were markedly different: juvenile mice exhibit excessive inflammatory response, with their high bacterial load strongly correlated with extensive mobilization of cells from precursor to effector stages and elevated levels of multiple inflammatory cytokines. This indicates that the immune system was in an immature state characterized by excessive and poorly regulated activity. Under these conditions, potent Th1‐type inflammatory chemokines (IL‐16, CCL2, CCL4, etc.) form a self‐amplifying inflammatory positive feedback loop with effector cell expansion, constituting the dominant immune pattern in MRMP. 34 Simultaneously, the hyperinflammatory stimulates a compensatory increase in Tregs, which release IL‐10 and CD274 in an attempt to control the inflammatory response. However, the intensity of this feedback may be insufficient to curb the preexisting hyperinflammatory. 35 Notably, the proportion of Tregs in juvenile mice is significantly lower than that in adult mice, and this reduced abundance contributes to their insufficient regulatory capacity to curb the excessive inflammatory response—potentially one of the key reasons for the more severe lung damage observed in juvenile mice. Furthermore, reports indicate that prolonged exposure of Tregs to Th1, Th2, or Th17 cytokines can lead to the loss of their immunosuppressive activity, resulting in the production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines. 36 Therefore, the more severe lung tissue damage observed in juvenile mice may not stem from inadequate clearance capacity but rather from their excessive reliance on and overactivation of the innate immune system, resulting in greater immunopathological costs when achieving pathogen clearance efficacy comparable to that of adult mice.

It should be noted that CyTOF analysis in the present study was performed on splenocytes, implying that the statements herein regarding cellular recruitment and immune responses represent systemic immune signatures that may drive or parallel localized lung pathology rather than fully reflecting the localized microenvironment of the infected lung or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF). Future studies may benefit from supplementing immune profiling of lung tissues or BALF to more comprehensively characterize the localized immune response during MRMP infection. In addition, it is important to clarify that such correlation network analysis serves as an observational description, and further validation is required to establish the causal relationships between immune cells, inflammatory factors, bacterial load, and lung injury.

Thus, this study suggests 3‐week‐old BALB/c mice as a suitable model for investigating MRMP in children. Previous research has demonstrated that the lung maturity of 3‐week‐old BALB/c juvenile mice corresponds to that of a 2‐year‐old child, 12 providing a model basis for studying MPP in young children. By replicating key features of human diseases, this model exhibits controllable bacterial loads yet intense inflammatory responses, potentially offering a superior platform to adult mice for novel therapeutic development. The identification of specific immune targets (such as the PD‐1/PD‐L1 axis and Treg/Th17 balance) has opened new avenues for immunomodulatory interventions. For instance, therapies that enhance Treg function or selectively inhibit neutrophil recruitment could serve as potent adjuncts to macrolide antibiotics, potentially mitigating lung injury without compromising bacterial clearance.

The limitations of this study are as follows: first, focusing solely on the acute phase (7 dpi) prevents the assessment of long‐term sequelae (e.g., obstructive bronchiolitis, postinfection fibrosis), and longitudinal studies are needed to confirm whether early immune dysregulation leads to permanent structural remodeling. Second, the study has small sample sizes (e.g., n = 5 for infected juvenile mice and n = 3 for normal juvenile mice and infected adult mice), and the exclusive use of female mice may limit the generalizability of the findings to male populations; future studies will include male mice to explore sex differences in immune dysregulation during MRMP infection. Third, the lack of an uninfected adult control group hinders the distinction between intrinsic age‐related immune differences and MRMP‐induced immune changes, which cannot be addressed due to time and resource constraints. Finally, the absence of functional validation experiments (e.g., Treg adoptive transfer, Treg‐mediated immune regulation experiments) and clinical validation data means only correlational observations can be provided rather than definitive causal conclusions. Future studies will focus on supplementing functional validation experiments and large‐scale clinical observations to enhance the study’ s clinical translational value.

In summary, research indicates that susceptibility to MRMP in juvenile hosts results not merely from the immune system's inability to clear pathogens but rather from an immunopathological state of imbalance caused by innate‐adaptive dysregulation. The decoupling between pathogen burden and disease severity highlights the critical role of host immune responses in driving pathology. By elucidating the specific cells and factors involved in innate hyperactivity and adaptive dysregulation, we provide experimental rationale for shifting pediatric MRMP treatment from monotherapy with antimicrobials to combination therapy incorporating immune modulation.

CONCLUSION

Our research confirms that innate‐adaptive immune dysregulation, rather than bacterial load, is the main reason underlying increased susceptibility and disease severity in juvenile hosts during MRMP infection. This intrinsic defect in juvenile mice provides a basis for studying innate‐adaptive immune mismatch mechanisms, and 3‐week‐old juvenile mice may be considered a suitable model for investigating MRMP infection in children.
