EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
An extremely halophilic archaeon (Halobacterium sp., WIPP strain), isolated from WIPP halite,
was tested for its ability to grow in media containing various combinations of sodium and
magnesium chloride that influenced overall ionic strength and water activity. Halobacterium sp.
grew well in most tested salt combinations, and its growth appeared to depend more upon the
relative contribution of sodium to overall ionic strength than on other parameters. Growth rates
were highest when media consisted of at least 2 M NaCl and lowest when NaCl dropped below
20% of the ionic strength (usually less than 0.5 M). High magnesium concentrations ( 1 and 1.5
M MgCh) did not prevent Halobacterium sp. growth, as long as the sodium contribution was
sufficient. Water activity was not a definitive factor in determining growth, because this value
did not reach an extreme lower limit in the experimental media.
Because the tested parameters are interdependent, it is difficult to say which is the chief
determinant affecting the growth of this organism. However, comparisons between these results
and those in the literature suggest that growth is possible when a balance is achieved between
chaotropic (e.g., Mg 2+) and kosmotropic (e.g., Na+) solutes at a permissive water activity and
permissive pH. Chaotropic substances cause disruption in cellular structures, while kosmotropic
substances can balance these adverse effects. To test this theory, a "C/K index" was calculated
as the ratio of chaotropic to kosmotropic solutes in the growth media. This index was also
calculated for numerous other hypersaline environments from literature data. Results suggest
that a 3-dimensional "window of habitability" can be delineated by the chaotrope/kosmotrope
index, water activity, and pH.

In these experiments, this window is defined for halophiles. The fact that an extreme halophile
did not grow well, or even at all, in some of the media combinations suggests that a less
halophilic organism might not grow at all. This supports the argument that not all organisms will
be active under WIPP conditions.

1.0 INTRODUCTION

Although microorganisms are present in the Salado Formation, their activity under projected
WIPP conditions is far from certain. Even if the potentially adverse effects of radioactivity are
disregarded, the presence of high ionic strength, low water activity (aw< 0.75), and high
concentrations of chaotropic solutes (i.e., Mg 2+ or Ca2+) can significantly constrain microbial
activity.
Sodium concentration is the primary determinant of microbial community structure in saline
settings, with 15%, w/v, NaCl (2.57 M) representing the transition in community composition
from moderate to extreme halophiles (Andrei et al., 2012; Kimbrel et al., 2018). Thus,
hypersaline environments are dominated by members of the Euryarchaeal Class Halobacteria,
because of their ability to use the low energetic cost "salt-in" strategy for osmoregulation. These
"haloarchaea" have been the main organisms enriched from cultures of WIPP halite at high salt
concentrations and are the main organisms detected from other subterranean halites as well
(Vreeland et al., 1998; Vreeland et al., 2007; Swanson et al., 2012; Jaakkolla et al., 2016; Megaw
et al., 2019). After salinity, chaotropicity may be the next greatest limiting factor to microbial
activity (Hallsworth et al., 2003, 2007; dC Rubin et al, 2017; Lee et al, 2018). High
concentrations of chaotropic solutes, such as Mg 2+ or Ca2+, can destabilize proteins and affect
membrane structure (Hallsworth et al., 2003, 2007; Broering and Bommarius, 2008). Finally,
low water activity can effectively delimit a habitable zone, even though halophilic
microorganisms can do well at low values (Hallsworth et al., 2003; Grant, 2004; Williams and
Hallsworth, 2009; Stevenson et al., 2014). These three stressors are interrelated in many
hypersaline environments, and they can determine which life forms survive and thrive and which
do not.
In spite of these constraints on microbial survival and growth, repository performance
simulations must assume that all microorganisms are healthy and active in such environments, in
order to predict the worst-case scenario-i.e., the microbially influenced release of radionuclides
into the biosphere. A series of experiments is underway to determine the likelihood of survival
and growth of organisms isolated from the WIPP environment under various combinations of
ionic strength, water activity, sodium concentration, and magnesium concentration, but in the
presence of their preferred organic substrates. This report will present data from growth
experiments on the first of these organisms, Halobacterium sp. This organism was chosen for
study because it is an extreme halophile, it is commonly detected in subterranean salt deposits,
and it is capable of growth in both WIPP brines, GWB and ERDA-6 (Radax et al., 2001; Gruber
et al., 2004; Vreeland et al., 2007; Park et al., 2009; Gramain et al., 2011; Yildiz et al., 2012;
Swanson et al., 2012; Jaakkolla et al., 2016; Swanson and Reed, 2018). Therefore, if
Halobacterium sp. growth is affected, then the survival and growth of less halotolerant
organisms is even more uncertain. While pH extremes can also affect microbial growth, they
were not considered in the experimental tasks for this work; the assumption is made that the
immediate osmotic shock caused by exposure to brines will outweigh the effect of extreme pH.

2.2 Organism

Halobacterium sp. was originally isolated from WIPP halite at 4.71 M NaCl (Swanson et al.,
2012). For these experiments, it was grown in generic halophile broth (Table 1) containing its
optimum formula with 3.42 M NaCl and 0.1 M MgCb. At late log/early stationary phase,
Halobacterium sp. was subcultured (2% v/v) into triplicate tubes containing broth from each
series (Table 2).
2.3 Parameters measured

2.3.1 Growth
Growth was measured as the optical density (OD) at 660 nm using a GeneSys 50
spectrophotometer. The growth rate, k, was calculated as the change in OD during exponential
growth and is calculated in units per day for this organism:
        k = (OD,2-OD11)/(ti-t1), where
 t1 and ti are the times at which exponential growth begins and ends, respectively.

2.3.2 Ion concentrations
Cation (Na+, Mg 2+, K+, and Ca2+) concentrations in the growth media were verified using an
Agilent 7900 inductively-coupled plasma mass spectrometer (ICP-MS; Santa Clara, CA).
Calibration curves were generated from a commercial standard cation mix (High Purity
Standards; Charleston, SC), and indium was used as the internal standard. Chloride
concentrations were measured on a Dionex Ion Chromatography System (ICS)-6000
(ThermoFisher Scientific; Waltham, MA) with conductivity detection and also calibrated with a
commercial standard anion mix (High Purity Standards). These constituents were measured
before inoculating the media and again at stationary phase, to determine if ion concentrations
changed during cell growth.

2.4 Parameters calculated
Water activities and overall ionic strengths (all ions, not just Na+ and Mg 2+) were calculated with
the SpecE8 application in Geochemist' s Workbench version 14.0, using the Pitzer
thermodynamic database, FMT4 (Domski, 2020). Activities of broths were calculated at the
experimental incubation temperature (37°C), as temperature affects both water activity and
chaotropicity. Organic components were not included in these calculations. Geochemist's
Workbench calculations cannot be considered Quality Level 1 data, since the software has not
been formally qualified, although it is used extensively in the field of geochemistry. Therefore,
calculations were validated using the FMT4 database in EQ3/6 software (Wolery, 1992).

3.1.2 Variable Ionic Strength
In the variable ionic strength series of experiments, the concentration of MgCh remained
constant within each series ( 1 M, 1.5 M, 2 M MgCh), while the NaCl concentration varied. In
the 1 M and 1.5 M MgCh series, Halobacterium sp. grew best when NaCl concentrations were 2
Mand above (Figures 3 and 4). Thus, as Mg 2+ increases, more Na+ is needed to counter its
chaotropic effects, but it is only effective up to a certain point. In the 2 M Mg 2+ series, 2 M NaCl
was the highest concentration used, due to precipitation at higher concentrations (Figure 5).
Again, growth was best at this concentration, albeit very slow and with a much lower resulting
biomass than at lower Mg 2+ concentrations (note relative axis scale).

3.1.3 Comparison of Growth Rates and Extent of Growth
Growth rates and the overall extent of growth (as represented by the maximum optical density
value reached) were compared for all conditions (Appendix A; Figures 6 & 7). Growth rates
increased with increasing NaCl concentration, for all but the 2 M Mg 2+ series, but only up to a
certain point (~2.5 M NaCl) at which they began to decrease. Maximum OD followed the same
trend: an increase in OD with increasing [NaCl] up to a certain point (2 M NaCl) beyond which
values decreased. The exception is the 5 M constant ionic strength series, where the rate
plateaued between 2.75-4.25 M NaCl (0.75-0.25 M Mg 2+, respectively) before decreasing.

In the IM and 1.5 M Mg 2+ series, Halobacterium sp. grew faster at 2.5 M NaCl, but maximum
biomass occurred at 2 M NaCl. The reason for this is unknown. In broth Hof the 5 M constant
ionic strength series (no magnesium added), growth was slow but the overall biomass reached
was as high as in the other broths. In the 2 M Mg 2+ series, it was difficult to determine if growth
was exponential, especially in the O Na+ and 2 M Na+ curves, which appear almost linear. This
will be discussed later.

3.2 Cell morphological changes
Rounding up and enlargement of cells was observed in the lM Mg 2+ series at all Na+
concentrations below 2 M (Figure 10). Below 0.5 M Na+, as sodium concentration increased, so
did cell size (Figure 11 from older experiment series). The 2.5 Mand 3M Na+ series of
replicates resulted in rod shapes. At 2.5 M Na+, the rods were of varying lengths from
coccobacilli to long bacilli, and at 3M Na+, the rods were very long and narrow. Halobacterium
sp. can be pleomorphic-i.e., can present different morphologies under different environmental
conditions-but it usually takes on a rod shape under ideal conditions. When grown in WIPP
brines, cells are small and ovoid (coccobacilli).

Rounding up has been observed for other species of haloarchaea when exposed to low
concentrations of sodium (Abram and Gibbons, 1961; Mohr and Larsen, 1963) or divalent
cations, such as magnesium or calcium (Cohen et al., 1991). In those cases, when cation levels
dropped too low, the S (surface)-layer was destabilized and often shed, resulting in changes to
cell shape.
Lower water activities have been shown to cause rod-shaped Halobacterium cells to divide into
smaller spherical cells ( ~0.4 micron in diameter) with a concomitant loss of S-layer surface
proteins and a decrease in total surface area (Abram and Gibbons, 1961; Fendrihan et al., 2012).
This was proposed as a long-term survival mechanism for these organisms and is not true cell
growth. It is possible that the loss of turgor and morphological changes observed in these studies
are due to the loss of the S-layer and subsequent inability to maintain cell shape. It is also
possible that increases in OD and cell numbers at the highest magnesium concentrations were
due to cell splitting and miniaturization instead of true cell division and growth.
Regardless of the cause for morphological change, the change in surface area and the loss of the
surface protein S-layer can affect metal or actinide biosorption. S-layer loss and dispersal or
decrease in cell surface area could have been the reason for little to no bioassociation of
neodymium observed with Halobacterium sp. in GWB and pCH+ 9.5 brines, due to their high
Mg 2+ content (Swanson, 2019). In comparison, bioassociation in ERDA did occur, albeit more
slowly than for similarly tested bacteria.

3.3 Changes in ion concentration
Principal ions (Na+, Mg 2+, ct·, K+, and Ca2+) were measured in the growth media prior to starting
the experiments and at the end of cell growth (Figures 12-18). They were also analyzed in the
negative controls at the same time point as the final biotic samples were taken. In some
instances, there were notable increases in the ion concentrations of uninoculated broths during
the course of the experiments (see especially 5 M series, Figure 13). In other cases, ion
concentration decreased (see Na+ and Mg 2+ in the 1.5 M Mg 2+ series-Figure 15; see also Ca2+ in
the 2 M Mg 2+ series-Figure 16). Increases in concentration (in uninoculated broths) are thought
to be due to enhanced solubility of medium components over time at elevated temperature, while
decreases in concentration are due to precipitation with time.

4.0 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS
Sodium concentration, magnesium concentration, ionic strength and water activity are
interdependent parameters in many hypersaline settings, including the WIPP repository, making
it difficult to determine which single parameter has the most impact on halophile viability and
activity. Even so, Halobacterium sp. growth correlated best with the contribution of sodium to
the (Na++ Mg 2+) ionic strength and overall ionic strength (as calculated in Geochemist's
Workbench). Optimum growth rates were measured when the contribution of Na+ to (Na++
Mg 2+) ionic strength was above 45% and magnesium concentrations were 1 Mor below. When
sodium contributed between 20-40% of the ionic strength, rates were more scattered but
generally increased with increasing sodium content. Below 20% there was little to no growth.
This suggests that, as long as the ratio between Na+ and Mg 2+ is acceptable, then growth will
occur (Figure 21). The range for chloride was much narrower, with optimum growth occurring
when c1- made up approximately 45-50% of the ionic strength.

However, this conclusion is too simplistic. Although growth rates were lower as magnesium
concentrations increased, the overall extent of growth was still significant at 1.5 M. According
to the scale developed by Cray et al. (2013) for chaotropicity/k:osmotropicity, NaCl and KCl fall
within the kosmotropic range of solutes, while both MgCh and CaCh are chaotropes. While
chaotropes adversely affect the structure and function of cellular macromolecules, kosmotropes
have the opposite effect, although the exact mechanism behind this phenomenon is still not

As shown in Figure 22, the viability of Halobacterium sp. is constrained by both water activity
and the chaotrope to kosmotrope balance in the broths, suggesting that there is a window
delineated by water activity and the Log(C/K) value that may possibly define the limits of a
habitable environment. Broths in which no growth occurred are at a significant distance from all
others. Broths in which minimal growth occurred fall within a narrow range of log(C/K) values.
All broths were above the 0.585 absolute limit of water activity that can sustain life (Stevenson
et al., 2017).
Nevertheless, the data do not fit perfectly (see data points within dashed red circle). It appears
that the C/K border is not well defined and may be better represented by a range rather than an
absolute value, since microbial growth occurred in the 2 M Mg 2+ medium with 0.5 and 1 M Na+
[log(C/K) = 0.796 and 0.836, respectively] but not in I M Mg2+ broth with 0.5 M Na+ (0.826).
Although increases in OD in the 2 M Mg2+ broth were minimal, the increase in cell numbers was
confirmed with plate counts. However, cell splitting and miniaturization (although technically
not growth) would still result in an increase in CFU counts, since each "dwarf' cell would
theoretically give rise to an individual colony. True reproduction would need to be confirmed by
substrate utilization testing.
To test these constraints on other systems, comparisons were extended to other natural terrestrial
brines, including those from a German and British salt mine (Figure 23), several surficial
hypersaline bodies of water (Figure 24), and numerous deep sea hypersaline anoxic brines
(DHABs; Figure 25). Many of these bodies of water are of interest in astrobiological studies that
address the possibility of life on Mars. Water activities for all brines were calculated in
Geochemist's Workbench and EQ3/6, in order to provide some consistency throughout the data
sets (Appendix C). It should be noted that some of the calculated water activities differed
significantly from their reported values. In those cases, both values were graphed. Geochemist's
Workbench-calculated values are highly dependent upon chloride concentrations. The reported
values were, for the most part, obtained using water activity meters that measure the humidity
over a specific sample whose free water is at equilibrium with the air above.

However, growth did not occur in the brine seeps that were tested. In these cases, pH was most
likely prohibitive (4.5).

The experiments described in this report were conducted in broth designed to permit microbial
growth. WIPP brines contain other salts that were not added to the media, such as lithium
chloride and sodium tetraborate. There is little documentation in the literature about the
presence of either lithium or borate in other thalassohaline brines. Both are considered
chaotropes, and borate is a significant contributor to ionic strength and the high C/K ratio in
GWB. Boron is present in seawater and salt evaporite sequences of marine origin. Both boron
and lithium are commonly associated with clays in marine and evaporite systems, including
WIPP halite (Siegel et al., 1991).
WIPP brines also contain high concentrations of sulfate, which is a kosmotrope that likely has a
protective effect on microbial cells. Additionally, the concentrations of calcium and potassium
in the broths were much lower than in WIPP brines. Halobacterium sp. and other WIPP
haloarchaea grow slowly but well in 90-95% strength WIPP brines amended with organics, but
complete dissolution of brine and organic components is not readily achieved (Swanson et al.,
2013).

Finally, it should also be noted that the pH used in these experiments was slightly lower than
those measured for WIPP brines, and more importantly, these experiments were carried out
under aerobic conditions. Halobacterium sp. prefers circumneutral pH and does not grow well
above pH 8.5. pH optima for thalassohaline-derived haloarchaea tend to be between 7-8 and
correlate with the sodium concentration of the source environment (Oren, 2006; Bowers and
Wiegel, 2011). Halobacterium sp., like most haloarchaea, is also an obligate aerobe. There have
been no anaerobic extreme halophiles isolated from WIPP halite to date, nor from any other
subterranean halites at high ionic strength. However, the presence of extremely halophilic and
obligately anaerobic bacteria of the order Halanaerobiales has been discussed previously, since
these organisms have been isolated from Culebra groundwaters at the WIPP and DNA signatures
have been detected in Gorleben halite. More recently, Halanaerobia have been isolated from a
brine stream in a Scottish salt mine in media with 10% (w/v) NaCl (~ 1.71 M; Megaw et al.,
2019); thus, their presence at WIPP becomes more probable.

Microbial survival and growth in polyextreme environments are not unheard of. In fact,
saturated NaCl is only considered a moderately extreme environment by some (Lee et al., 2018).
Many have suggested that organisms present in brines are poly-extremists, capable of survival at
high salt and ionic strength and in the presence of high chaotropicity (Hallsworth, 2019).
Further, extremes of pH, temperature and pressure can also be found in deep sea hypersaline
brines.

That said, a salt deposit such as the Salado Formation is not the equivalent of a natural,
biologically active hypersaline body of water. The WIPP repository (and other salt-based
nuclear waste repositories), are man-made settings that impose different conditions, and possibly

new stressors, on organisms that might be present. The indigenous and introduced microbial
communities may not possess the ability to survive under those imposed conditions. In these
experiments, an indigenous extreme halophile was capable of growth in most of the tested media,
but it could not grow in all, even if it still survived. It stands to reason that a less halophilic
organism will have less chance of survival, let alone growth. Future work may focus on other
halophiles and less halophilic organisms and may extend to chaotropic brines containing calcium
as the dominant chaotrope, in order to simulate the presence of cementitious wastes.
