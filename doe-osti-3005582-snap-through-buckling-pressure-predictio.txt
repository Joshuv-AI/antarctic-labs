Abstract
Snap-through buckling is a nonlinear and dynamic instability that occurs in curved shell
structures such as domes, pressure vessels, and aerospace panels. Unlike classical
linear buckling, it involves a sudden transition between equilibrium states caused by
geometric nonlinearity and rapid strain-to-kinetic energy conversion (Timoshenko & Gere,
1961 [1]; Budiansky & Roth, 1962 [2]). This study investigates the snap-through behavior
of thin spherical caps using both implicit and explicit solvers in ANSYS Workbench. While
implicit analysis accurately captures quasi-static response, it struggles with convergence
near instability. In contrast, explicit LS-DYNA simulation naturally handles the nonlinear
dynamic event with minimal tuning and computational cost.
The onset of snap-through was identified through simultaneous spikes in equivalent
plastic strain and nodal pressure, validated against analytical predictions from
Timoshenko’s theory. Results show that the critical buckling pressure increases with
thickness (but not linearly) while modal analysis confirms a reduction in natural frequency
approaching instability. These trends suggest that structural health monitoring (SHM) can
be achieved experimentally by tracking strain gauge responses and frequency changes,
which provide practical early indicators of buckling. Overall, explicit dynamic analysis
efficiently captures the transient and energy-driven behavior of snap-through, offering
both accurate predictions and valuable insight for SHM applications in thin shell
structures.

1. Introduction
Snap-through buckling is a classic example of geometric nonlinearity, where structures
under compressive or pressure loads exhibit multiple stable configurations. When loading
surpasses a critical threshold, the structure experiences a sudden displacement jump, or
“snap,” accompanied by significant energy release and vibration [1,2, and Calladine (1989
[3]]. This phenomenon is particularly important in aerospace structures—such as aircraft
fuselage panels, morphing wings, rocket fairings, and pressure domes—where
lightweight, thin-walled designs are inherently susceptible to geometric instabilities. In
these applications, accurately predicting the snap-through pressure is essential to
ensuring both safety and performance.

Traditional linear buckling analysis, commonly implemented in finite element software,
cannot accurately capture snap-through behavior because it assumes infinitesimal
deflections and a constant stiffness matrix. In contrast, the nonlinear nature of snap-
through means that stiffness decreases significantly as deformation increases, creating
strong coupling between geometry, load, and displacement. Furthermore, because the
transition occurs rapidly, the response is inherently dynamic (i.e. transient motion and
oscillations that cannot be represented by static equilibrium equations alone).

require inclusion of the inertial term, Mü, in the full equation of motion. Because the static

frequency vibrations associated with instability and collapse. Consequently, dynamic or
explicit solvers are required to simulate this behavior accurately. Explicit solvers such as
LS-DYNA integrate the complete equation of motion, enabling realistic modeling dynamic
events that involve large deflections, contact, and rapid instability transitions.

This study focuses on the snap-through buckling of thin spherical caps, which serve as
idealized models for curved aerospace panels and pressure-loaded domes. The objective
is to compare the performance and predictive accuracy of implicit and explicit finite

element solvers in ANSYS Workbench for determining the critical buckling pressure, post-
buckling response, and associated changes in natural frequency.

As indicated by NASA [4,5], finite element modeling (FEM) is the preferred method for
predicting buckling in thin shell-like structures because it directly incorporates geometric
nonlinearity within the solver environment. This approach avoids the simplifying
assumptions—and often inaccurate results—of purely analytical solutions, providing a
more robust framework for assessing the nonlinear stability of complex aerospace
geometries.

2. Theoretical Background
2.1 Geometric and Material Nonlinearity
In linear elasticity, strain is assumed small, and displacements are proportional to applied
loads. However, for structures undergoing large deflections, nonlinear terms in the strain–
displacement relations become significant. For a thin shell, the nonlinear strain
components can be expressed as [1,2,3, Bathke [6]]:

two terms represent the symmetric, infinitesimal strain components used in linear
analysis, while the additional quadratic term, uk,iuk,j, accounts for geometric nonlinearity
by capturing the coupling between large displacements and rotations. This full Green –
Lagrange strain formulation remains valid for finite deformations and ensures accurate
representation of the change in length and curvature of shell elements. Such nonlinear
geometric effects dominate in thin-walled structures under compression or pressure
loading, where even minor imperfections can trigger significant instability and snap-
through behavior. The geometric nonlinearity inherent in snap-through buckling poses
significant challenges for analytical analysis, as demonstrated throughout this report.

2.3 Plastic Strain and Dynamic Effects
Plastic strain arises during buckling because geometric instability in the shell creates
localized stress concentrations that exceed the material’s yield strength – particularly near
regions of curvature reversal or constraint such as the crown or clamped rim. Although
snap-through buckling is fundamentally a geometric instability, the rapid redistribution of
stresses during collapse can drive localized yielding even when the overall applied load
remains below the uniform yield pressure [1,2,3]. As the cap deforms, bending and
membrane (through thickness) stresses intensify, leading to nonuniform strain energy
release and the onset of plastic flow in these concentrated regions.

Calladine (1989) [3] noted that geometric instability and material yielding often interact –
yielding modifies local stiffness and shifts the critical pressure but does not govern the
instability itself. Similarly, Budiansky and Roth (1962) [2] demonstrated that for shallow
spherical caps, snap-through can begin elastically, but once the structure passes its limit
point, local yielding occurs as stored strain energy converts rapidly into kinetic energy.
Timoshenko and Gere (1961) [1] also emphasized that plastic buckling arises when
inelastic effects reduce the effective tangent modulus, accelerating deformation in
localized areas. In summary, plastic strain during buckling should be viewed because of
the instability, not its cause. It represents the structure’s localized yielding as it transitions
dynamically between stable equilibrium states. This behavior underscores the necessity
of explicit dynamic analysis, which incorporates inertia and time-dependent effects to
accurately simulate the energy exchange and localized yielding that occur during snap-
through [1,2,3].

3. Numerical Simulation Methodology
3.1 Implicit Solver versus LS-DYNA Explicit in ANSYS
The Static Structural module in ANSYS employs an implicit solution scheme that
iteratively enforces equilibrium between internal and external forces, where
displacements are solved via matrix inversion (𝑢𝑢=𝐾𝐾-1 x F). Near a buckling point, however,
the global stiffness matrix becomes singular, preventing convergence as the structure
loses stiffness. To trace the response beyond this instability, methods such as the arc-
 3

length algorithm or other stabilization controls are used to follow the load–displacement
path through the unstable region and identify the limit point of buckling. While effective,
these techniques are computationally demanding, require careful parameter tuning, and
can be difficult to configure for highly nonlinear snap-through problems. In contrast, the
LS-DYNA solver utilizes an explicit central-difference time integration scheme, advancing
the solution in small time steps by updating displacements directly from accelerations
without matrix inversion. This approach efficiently handles large deformations, contact,
and dynamic instabilities such as snap-through buckling, avoiding the convergence
issues typical of implicit solvers. Although pressure–deflection (displacement–load) plots
can be generated in LS-PrePost to identify the limit point, this study focused instead on
determining the buckling pressure using equivalent plastic strain and middle-node
pressure. As part of future work, displacement–load plots could be developed for each
cap thickness to compare the traditional limit-point approach (Figure 1) [7] with the plastic
strain and nodal pressure methods used here.

3.2 Computational Cost Comparison
Implicit methods require matrix factorizations and equilibrium iterations at each load step,
scaling poorly with problem size. Explicit methods, though constrained by small time
steps, are more efficient for short-duration dynamic analyses. For snap-through events,
where convergence is often problematic, explicit solvers can complete the analysis in a
fraction of the time required by implicit methods—provided that the implicit solution can
converge at all.

3.3 Eigenvalue Buckling and Linear Predictions
Eigenvalue buckling analysis in ANSYS estimates the critical load by linearizing the
stiffness matrix around a preloaded equilibrium state. In this study, a 1 psi pressure load
is applied in the Static Structural module before running the Eigenvalue Buckling module.
While useful for identifying initial buckling modes, this linearization neglects geometric
nonlinearity and post-buckling path behavior, often leading to overestimated results. This
was the case with this analysis.

3.4 Modal Analysis and Frequency Tracking
As a structure approaches buckling, its effective stiffness decreases. Since natural
frequency, ωn, is proportional to the square root of stiffness over mass, a decrease in
stiffness leads to a measurable drop in frequency. This frequency softening can be
detected experimentally via vibration measurements or accelerometer data. A Modal
analysis is performed in ANSYS both at rest and under critical pressure. The comparison
between these two states quantifies stiffness degradation.

3.5 Vibration- and Strain-Based SHM of Spherical Caps
This frequency shift offers a non-invasive means to monitor stability in pressurized
systems. By tracking natural frequencies in real time, impending buckling can be
predicted before catastrophic collapse, making it valuable for aerospace and civil
applications.

Additionally, monitoring plastic strain with strain gauges provides a practical and highly
sensitive method for structural health monitoring (SHM) in critical engineering systems
such as aerospace, civil, and pressure-vessel structures. Strain gauges can measure
localized deformations and identify when a component transitions from elastic to plastic
behavior, which is often a precursor to buckling or permanent damage. By continuously
tracking strain evolution, engineers can detect abnormal strain accumulation that
indicates the onset of yielding, fatigue, or geometric instability. In thin-shell structures,
such as aircraft panels or spherical pressure caps, a sudden increase in measured strain
– corresponding to the plastic strain spike seen in simulations – can be used to predict
snap-through buckling or other instability-driven failures. When coupled with data

 5

acquisition and threshold-based alert systems, strain-gauge networks enable real-time
health assessment and can prevent catastrophic structural collapse by triggering
inspections or automated load reductions before critical instability occurs. This approach
aligns with modern SHM strategies that integrate simulation-based prediction with in-situ
sensing to improve safety, reliability, and maintenance efficiency of high-performance
structures.

4. Finite Element Analysis of the Spherical Caps
ANSYS 2025R1 was used to model the spherical caps in implicit and explicit solvers. A
Static Structural module and Eigenvalue Buckling module were used for implicit modeling,
and LS-DYNA was used for explicit modeling.

4.1 FEA Model Assumptions
The following assumptions were made to implicit and explicit modeling:

 - No strain rate effects considered
 - 3D shell elements used (SHELL181) because geometries are thin
 - No gravity applied to simulations
 - Idealized geometry is used, no imperfections implemented
 - No damping applied (may suppress snap-through and post buckling vibrations)

4.3 Boundary Conditions and Loading
The spherical caps were clamped at the rim, preventing displacement and rotation, and
subjected to a uniform external pressure applied to the outer surface (Figure 5). A linear
pressure-time relationship was used to simplify correlation between simulation time and
applied pressure, facilitating direct identification of the buckling point. For the 0.02-inch-
thick cap, the pressure ramped from 0 to 100 psi over 0.01 s, corresponding to a rate of
10 psi per millisecond; for the 0.04-inch and 0.06-inch caps, the final pressures were
increased to 250 psi and 500 psi, respectively, to ensure that buckling occurred within the
same time span. This linear loading scheme allowed straightforward identification of the
buckling pressure from the simulation time: for example, a snap-through event occurring
at 0.005 s would correspond to 50 psi (for 100 psi over 0.01 s), and one at 0.0078 s would

correspond to 78% of the total applied load (for other pressures). Extending the total load
duration (e.g., to 0.1 or 1 s) would yield identical results but would cause longer solve
times as the loading would be ramped a magnitude(s) slower.

4.4 Mesh for Implicit and Explicit Simulations
Both implicit and explicit simulations were conducted using 3D shell elements
(SHELL181) to evaluate performance and accuracy. Implicit analyses were performed
with both four-node and eight-node shells and showed no difference in results, only in
computational time. Therefore, all results presented use four-node linear shell elements,
which solved approximately two to three times faster than quadratic elements. Explicit
simulations also employed linear shell elements, as they are generally preferred for
dynamic analyses due to their stability and efficiency. A detailed discussion of element
formulation is beyond the scope of this report. For meshing, a mapped mesh was initially
used for implicit simulations under the assumption that it would improve accuracy and
results. However, when performing initial explicit simulations, an unstructured mesh was
also tested and there was no difference in results between the two meshes, but the solve
time of it was about 30-40% faster than the mapped mesh (top meshes in Figure 6).
Consequently then, the unstructured mesh was adopted for all explicit analyses. Mesh
sizes of 0.5, 0.25, and 0.125 inches were evaluated.

5.3 Alternative Buckling Estimation via Implicit Solver Non-
Convergence
An alternative method for estimating buckling pressure involved running a Static
Structural analysis at a fixed pressure ramp (100 psi total) to identify when the solver
failed to converge. The load step at which the solution diverged provided an
approximation of the critical pressure, typically aligning closely with the limit point found
in explicit simulations. The solver will stop at that diverged load (or time) and that load will
be closer to the actual buckling load (compared to the Eigenvalue Buckling module). For
example, Figure 8 below shows the time step that the 0.5-inch mesh for the 0.02-inch-
thick cap solve diverged at – which was 0.47 (47% of the loading). This means that at 47
psi, the implicit solver could not converge on the displacement solution.

5.4 Natural Frequency Comparison Pre- and Post-buckling
A modal analysis (natural frequency) was performed to determine the natural frequency
when the structure is at rest with no loading. Then, once the Static Structural
unconverged, a modal analysis was run again to determine the natural frequency at
buckling. The three cap thicknesses did demonstrate lowered natural frequencies when
buckling occurred.

The 0.02-inch-thick spherical cap’s stiffness was reduced roughly by half in the 0.5-inch
mesh because the first collapse mode was reduced from 1800 Hz to 900 Hz. Then, for
the 0.25-inch mesh, and 0.125-inch mesh, the collapse mode was more difficult to track
because the bubble modes along the outer rim dominated until the buckling modes moved
towards the center. After analysis, the two finer meshes resulted in a higher mode number
and higher frequency for the collapsing mode. This can be seen in Figure 9 and Figure
10. Refining the mesh, in this instance, did not aid in the results determination. The
reduction in natural frequency indicates a loss of structural stiffness as the shell
approaches instability. This correlation between frequency softening and stiffness
degradation provides a quantitative indicator that can be exploited for SHM applications.

5.5 Methodology for Determining Snap-Through Buckling Pressure
The snap-through buckling pressure for each spherical cap thickness was determined
using explicit dynamic simulations in ANSYS Workbench LS-DYNA. Two primary result
quantities were analyzed to identify the critical buckling (limit) point: Equivalent Plastic
Strain (EPS) and Nodal Pressure (PRESSURE).

During loading, EPS increases gradually and then exhibits a sudden spike at the onset of
snap-through as geometric instability causes rapid strain localization and energy release.
The first sight of plastic strain is when the cap begins to buckle, and the sharp spike is
when the crown (or middle) of the cap inverts. Nodal pressure was also used to
corroborate snap-through. As the pressure loading is applied to the cap, the cap
experiences compressive stress until buckling occurs. Then, when buckling occurs, the

 15

pressure slope changes direction and then becomes negative, indicating the loss of
compressive load-carrying capacity and dynamic inversion of the structure occurs. This
is shown in the Nodal Pressure figures throughout the report. The negative pressure
means the cap is then experiencing tensile stress as it buckles through. When snap-
through is complete, the cap vibrates which is also visible in the Nodal Pressure plots.
The point of this pressure reversal aligns closely with the EPS spike and energy release
observed in LS-DYNA, confirming snap-through.

This dual-parameter approach–tracking both EPS and Nodal Pressure–provides a
reliable way to identify the buckling pressure in explicit analyses, where convergence-
based criteria (implicit solvers) are not as applicable. The consistency of these markers
across multiple thicknesses confirms the robustness of the explicit method for nonlinear,
dynamic, shell instability problems. The predicted buckling pressure per spherical cap
thickness and mesh size using LS-DYNA explicit are shown below in Table 4 through
Table 7.

The three mesh sizes were used for estimating the buckling pressure for the 0.02-inch-
thick cap. The results resolutions used were Program Controlled, 1E-4, and 1E-5, where
each resolution is finer than the previous. By increasing the results resolution, the solve
time was increased. After simulating the 0.02-inch-thick cap, it was determined that the
0.25-inch mesh with 1E-4 resolution was the best balance for mesh and results resolution
while balancing solve time. As the mesh was refined, increased local behavior was
captured that was not necessary for determining the buckling pressure. So, mesh
refinement was not the deciding factor for buckling—result resolution was. A coarser
mesh with finer result resolution can be balanced for accurate results and solve time. The

5.6 0.02-Inch-Thick Cap Results
The onset of buckling is identified at the first appearance of plastic strain, which develops
at the outer rim due to the clamped boundary condition (see Figure 13). These early local
strains indicate the formation of bubble-type buckling modes (referred to as local buckling

 17

depressions in [1], but as bubble modes in modern FEA)–common in thin shells and often
initiated by boundary stiffness and minor imperfections [1-3]. As pressure increases (due
to the ramped loading to 100 psi), the cap continues to deform (and does not require more
pressure to snap-through buckle), with the rim yielding first and the center following as
snap-through progresses. The initial plastic strain therefore marks the beginning of snap-
through buckling, with snap-through completing once the middle of the cap inverts. Figure
14 through Figure 19 show the analytical process, with annotations, used to determine
the snap-through buckling pressure, where “t” denotes the cap thickness.

The 0.02-inch-thick cap results are presented in Figure 14 through Figure 27. The final
buckling pressure was determined to be 46 psi. The results resolution consists of Program
Controlled, 1E-4, and 1E-5. All results are averaged across the geometry.

 Figure 13. Plastic Strain Begins at Start of Snap-Through

The methodology described below provides an accurate estimate of the buckling
pressure, typically within 5–20% of the true value. The slight discrepancy arises from the
linear pressure ramp used during loading, which can over- or under-predict the actual
buckling pressure by incrementally overshooting the limit point as pressure continues to
rise with time. For example, as can be seen in Figure 14, plastic strain first appears at
0.005 seconds–corresponding to 50% of the applied load or 50 psi, based on the linear
ramp from 0 to 100 psi over 0.01 seconds. However, when an explicit simulation was
performed up to 50 psi at 0.01 seconds, the 0.02-inch-thick cap snapped through just
before the end of the loading period. Using the same time-to-pressure relationship, the
true snap-through buckling pressure was approximately 46 psi. To finally prove 46 psi as

 18

the true snap-through buckling pressure, a simulation was performed for 0.02 seconds
where from 0 to 0.01 seconds, the pressure was linearly ramped still, but from 0.01
seconds to 0.02 seconds, the final pressure remained applied. 46 psi was the lowest
pressure that would buckle the 0.02-inch-thick spherical cap.
This same approach was applied to determine the buckling pressure for the other cap
thicknesses. Generally, the thicker the cap, the greater the buckling pressure offset
between the first simulation, and the final simulation. Of course, it could be said that 46
to 50 psi is the general area of the buckling pressure for the 0.02-inch-thick cap, but the
purpose of this study was to determine the exact buckling pressure with FEA.

6. SHM of Spherical Caps
A structural health monitoring approach can be taken to either test or provide safekeeping
of the spherical caps from buckling. Monitoring the natural frequency of the spherical cap
using accelerometers provides a practical method for detecting the onset of buckling. As
pressure increases and the structure approaches instability, the cap’s stiffness decreases,
resulting in a measurable reduction in natural frequency. By placing small, high-sensitivity
accelerometers on the surface, preferably near the crown and along the meridional
direction(s), the dominant vibration modes can be tracked in real time (Figure 39). A
consistent downward shift in frequency indicates progressive softening of the structure,
while a sudden drop suggests proximity to the snap-through threshold. This approach
enables non-intrusive vibration monitoring of a spherical cap, making it valuable for
aerospace and pressure-vessel applications where early detection of instability is critical
to prevent catastrophic failure.

 38

 Figure 39. Accelerometer Placement on Spherical Caps for SHM

For SHM in industrial applications, strain gauges can be strategically placed along the
outer rim and of the spherical cap to measure the local strain that could develop due to
displacement and or pressurization because of the boundary restraint (Figure 40).
However, for SHM testing applications only, additional strain gauges can also be applied
to the crown (center) of the cap to measure strain during buckling and snap-through. For
industrial applications, it is undesirable to have a cap physically buckle because of the
safety implications. For controlled and safe SHM testing though, buckling and its
associated strain can be captured.

Under increasing pressure, both the outer rim and crown exhibit gradual strain growth,
followed by a distinct, rapid surge as the crown begins to invert. Tracking these strain
patterns provides a direct, localized indicator of instability, allowing operators to predict
when buckling and crown snap-through is imminent. When combined with frequency data
from accelerometers, strain measurements form a complementary monitoring system
capable of identifying both global stiffness loss and local yielding, offering a robust
approach to structural health monitoring in thin shell structures.

7. Conclusion
This study demonstrates that snap-through buckling of a spherical cap is a highly
nonlinear and dynamic phenomenon that cannot be accurately captured by traditional
linear methods. Explicit dynamic analysis using LS-DYNA effectively modeled the
instability event, while implicit solvers in ANSYS require advanced stabilization
techniques–such as the arc-length method, nonlinear controls, and mass stiffening – to
trace the unstable path or suppress dynamic effects. Linear eigenvalue buckling provided
only an initial approximation of the critical load and failed to represent post-buckling
behavior, but it was surprisingly close to analytical predictions.

Explicit LS-DYNA simulations produced reliable results but showed greater deviation from
analytical expectations as the spherical cap thickness increased. Explicit simulations also
deviated significantly from the linear eigenvalue approach (Eigenvalue Buckling Module
in ANSYS). In contrast, implicit solvers exhibited higher computational costs and
convergence challenges near instability points. The eigenvalue method, while useful for
preliminary design, overpredicted buckling pressures by as much as 60% for the three
thin caps, and then about 1400% overprediction for the 0.5-inch-thick cap. These findings
confirm that explicit solvers offer superior performance and reliability for capturing
nonlinear dynamic instabilities such as snap-through buckling.

Modal analysis further verified that the spherical cap’s stiffness decreases as it
approaches instability, as evidenced by reductions in natural frequency. These results
show that SHM techniques, including accelerometers to monitor frequency shifts and
strain gauges to measure localized strain, offer a practical way to detect and reduce the

 40

risk of buckling in thin shell structures. Overall, explicit dynamic analysis provides a robust
and computationally efficient means of simulating snap-through behavior, while implicit
solvers remain suitable for pre-buckling static response. These results establish a
foundation for future experimental validation and provide a framework for integrating
computational prediction with SHM methods to enhance the reliability of thin-shell
aerospace and pressure-vessel structures.

8. Future Work
Future work will focus on experimental validation of numerical predictions using physical
testing of spherical caps under controlled pressure loading. Additional studies could
examine the effects of damping, strain rate, and imperfections on the snap-through
buckling threshold. Coupling explicit dynamic simulations with real-time structural health
monitoring data will also enhance predictive accuracy and enable early-warning systems
for aerospace and pressure-vessel applications.
