 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Although current practices meet regulations for grout waste forms, development of sequestration materials
and techniques for contaminants of concern could expand the use of grout waste forms and reduce risk to
established processes from future compositional changes. There are contaminants in waste streams that are
not permanently and completely sequestered in current grout waste forms under certain conditions. These
contaminants include methyl mercury, 129I, 99Tc, and nitrate. Each of these species have minor fractions
that are not permanently and completely isolated from the environment and therefore potentially contribute
to long-term environmental impacts.

An approach of interest includes the technique of reducing porosity to result in reduced leachability of all
contaminants simultaneously. The current Savannah River Site (SRS) saltstone formulation was used to
evaluate the relationship between leachability and porosity reduced waste forms by altering the water-to-
binder ratio. Results indicated decreasing the water-to-binder directly reduced porosity and resulted in the
waste form’s ability to more effectively immobilize nitrate. This work was funded by the Department of
Energy Office of Environmental Management (DOE-EM) Technology Operations Office (TOO) Project
# HQ221818.

1.0 Introduction
Environmental regulations have set limitations for the leachability of hazardous constituents.1 Sorbents,
reactants, and other materials are currently being evaluated to reduce hazardous constituent leachability
from grout waste forms. Other approaches are also of interest, such as techniques to reduce porosity, that
could reduce leachability of all contaminants simultaneously and expand the applicability of grouts to
current and future waste streams.

The Saltstone Production Facility (SPF) at SRS receives low-level waste (LLW) salt solution from Tank
50H via the two salt solution receipt tanks (SSRT) for treatment and disposal. This waste stream is mixed
with a binder termed “premix” (a mixture of blast furnace slag and thermally beneficiated Class F fly ash)
in a continuous mixer to produce a flowable grout that is pumped to Saltstone Disposal Units (SDUs) in the
Saltstone Disposal Facility (SDF) for permanent disposition. Previous work has been conducted on the SRS
Saltstone waste form consisting of 45 wt % Class F fly ash (FA) 1, 45 wt % blast furnace slag (BFS) 2, and
10 wt % ordinary portland cement (OPC) 3 to evaluate important processing and performance properties,
such as porosity and leachability. In 2009, tests were conducted to evaluate the variation of permeability as
a function of water-to-binder ratio in SRS Saltstone (with OPC).2 Water-to-binder ratios selected for this
test were 0.55, 0.60 (baseline), and 0.65. The results of this previous work indicated that an increase in
water-to-binder ratio (0.65) resulted in an increase in permeability, even with little to no change in porosity
when compared to the baseline formulation (0.60) and the reduced liquid formulation (0.55). SRS Saltstone
Production Facility now operates with a cement-free formulation.3 Eliminating the presence of portland
cement presents an additional need for understanding porosity and leachability as a function of water-to-
binder ratio in cement-free waste forms to understand the leaching behavior of the modified waste form.

This study evaluates both the effect to water to binder ration and the presence of cement on the porosity of
saltstone. Overall, the objective of this work, funded by Department of Energy Office of Environmental
Management (DOE-EM) Technology Operations Office (TOO) Project # HQ2218181, was to develop new
techniques for sequestering contaminants of concern.4

2.1.1 Salt Solution Simulant Preparation
A 2-L batch of simulated salt solution was prepared by adding the reagents in the proportion given in
Table 2-3 to a volumetric flask partially filled with deionized (DI) water and mixed. Each chemical was
added in the order listed in the table and given time to dissolve. After dissolution, the stir bar was removed,
and the flask was filled to the 2-L mark with additional DI water.

2.1.2 Sample Preparation
Samples were prepared by adding the amount of salt solution in Table 2-4 to a pitcher. The salt solution
was stirred using an overhead mixer and the blended premix was slowly added to the salt solution. After
the premix was fully incorporated, the mixture was poured into three 2″ x 4″ molds and capped. The
mixture with a W/PM of 0.35 (SS35) was difficult to mix and did not fully incorporate the premix into the
mixture.

2.2 Porosity
The method to measure total porosity was developed during the experimentation performed to determine
the effect of formulation variability on porosity.7 Porosity is the percentage of total volume occupied by the
pore solution for the saturated case. For example, a grout which contains 45 % by volume of simulated salt
solution has a total porosity of 45 %. Porosity was measured on pieces obtained from the interior of the 2″
 11

x 4″ samples that had been cured for a minimum of 28 days. The pieces were oven dried at ~105 °C until
no further weight loss was measured. For these saltstone samples, the mass of water released during heating
to 105 °C is converted to a volume of pore solution by first dividing by the ratio of the mass of water to the
mass of simulant. This results in the mass of the simulant in the pores. The mass of simulant is then divided
by the density of the simulant, 0.73 and 1.23 g/mL for the simulant, resulting in the volume of the simulant
in the pores.

2.3 Leach Testing
Leach testing was performed using a modified version of the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)
Manual SW-846 Method 1315.8 The modifications to the Method included shortened leach times and
leaching one monolith for each formulation. This testing used 2″x 4″monoliths suspended in leach
containers using deionized water. Monoliths were leached for time intervals ranging from 7-hours to 168-
hours. Subsamples were captured during each monolithic exchange and analyzed. Select cations and anions
of the collected leachates were measured by inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectroscopy
(ICP-OES) and ion chromatography (IC), respectively. Results were used to calculate the leach rates and
effective diffusivities of sodium and nitrate ions. Leach rate incorporates diffusivity and other factors like
solubility, concentration gradients, and mass flow.

4.0 Conclusions
The objective of this work was to evaluate and develop new methods for sequestering contaminants of
concern, specifically by reducing porosity as a function of the water-to-binder ratio. The SRS Saltstone
waste formulation was used to evaluate the leachability of nitrate as a function of the water-to-binder ratio.

In this study, SRS Saltstone waste forms were prepared with water-to-binder ratios ranging from 0.35 to
0.65. The samples were evaluated for porosity and diffusivity. Results for porosity testing indicated that as
the water-to-binder ratio was reduced the leachability index in the waste forms as a function of the water-
to-binder ratio was calculated and exhibited a weak correlation. Results also showed that the total fraction
of nitrate released was higher for waste forms with higher water-to-binder ratios and higher porosity than
those with lower water-to-binder ratios and lower porosity.
