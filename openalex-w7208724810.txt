Comparable outcomes with reoperative versus primary total arch
replacement
Noriyuki Takashima1

· Tomoaki Suzuki1 · Hodaka Wakisaka1 · Kohei Hachiro1 · Yasuo Kondo1 · Masahide Enomoto1

Received: 3 July 2025 / Accepted: 27 July 2026

Abstract
Purpose Resternotomy during total arch replacement surgery is associated with adverse outcomes. We aimed to determine
the accuracy of this statement.
Methods From January 2008 to February 2021, 384 consecutive total arch replacement surgeries were performed at our
institution. Resternotomy was performed in 53 (14%) patients. We compared the operative outcomes between the first sternotomy and resternotomy groups.
Results The resternotomy group was younger, with a higher mean EuroSCORE II and higher prevalence of Marfan syndrome and chronic dissection. The median durations of operation, cardiopulmonary bypass, coronary ischemic time, selective antegrade cerebral perfusion, and circulatory arrest were significantly longer in the resternotomy group. The in-hospital
mortality rate was similar between the two groups (P= 0.50). According to a multivariable analysis, cardiopulmonary bypass
time (P< 0.001), female sex (P= 0.01) and emergency status (P< 0.001) emerged as independent predictors of in-hospital
mortality, whereas resternotomy did not. A Kaplan–Meier analysis showed no difference in long-term survival between the
two groups (Log-rank P= 0.30).
Conclusions Total arch replacement requiring resternotomy is often technically complex. Nevertheless, appropriate surgical
strategies have achieved outcomes as good as those of first sternotomy.
Keywords Total arch replacement · Resternotomy · Redo surgery · Aortic disease

Purpose
In the context of a globally aging population, including
Japan, the number of operations for thoracic aortic disease
has been increasing annually for the last 10 years [1]. Owing
to improvements in surgical techniques and perioperative
management, the number of patients undergoing repeat cardiovascular surgeries is also increasing. With the introduction of selective cerebral perfusion [2], a 4-branched graft
[3], BioGlue (CryoLife, Inc, Kennesaw, GA) [4], elephant
trunk [5], and frozen elephant trunk (FET) [6], the results

Previous studies have shown that the most common indication for reoperative TAR is the prior repair of an acute type

the outcomes between patients undergoing TAR requiring

Methods
From January 2008 to February 2021, 384 patients underwent TAR at Shiga University of Medical Science Hospital.
Hemiarch/partial arch replacements were excluded. Among
these patients, 53 (14%) had undergone previous cardiovascular surgery. Table 1 shows the type of primary operation
and the etiology of the present procedures in all patients.
The mean time interval between the primary and present
operations was 5.9 years (range, 0.1 – 39 years).
Medical records and operative reports were reviewed to
verify the collected data. The study patients were followed
up at the outpatient clinic or by telephone. The follow-up
rate was 96.7%. We compared the early operative and midterm outcomes between the two groups. The ethics committee of our institution approved the study (R2024-115) and
waived patient consent on the condition that the patients
were not identified.
We compared 331 patients who underwent first sternotomy and 53 patients who underwent resternotomy. The
primary endpoint was in-hospital mortality, and the secondary endpoint was late death and freedom from aortic
reoperation.

Surgical procedure
All surgeries were performed via a median sternotomy. The
details of the TAR at our institute have been reported previously [15, 16]. Temperature probes were placed to monitor
tympanic, rectal, and bladder temperatures. Brain oxygenation was monitored using INVOS 5100C (Somanetics,
Troy, MI, USA), and bilateral radial artery pressures were
monitored.
In resternotomy cases, a defibrillation pad or wet gauze
was applied to the body surface to allow for prompt defibrillation if ventricular arrhythmia occurred during sternotomy
or dissection of the pericardium.
A full sternotomy was performed using an oscillating
saw after removing the stainless steel wires from the sternum. The arterial cannulation site was chosen according to
preoperative CT and mainly used the ascending aorta, femoral artery, and axillary artery. Recently, we have preferred
peripheral cannulation before sternotomy in all patients.
Although infrequent, in cases where preoperative CT findings suggest that in situ reconstruction of the left subclavian artery would be difficult, a 9-mm Dacron graft (J-graft,
Japan LifeLine, Tokyo) may be anastomosed to the left
axillary artery in advance and used as an alternative inflow
route. During circulatory arrest, a cerebral perfusion circuit
is connected to the graft and used for selective cerebral perfusion via the left subclavian artery. The graft is ultimately
routed from the left thoracic cavity into the mediastinum
and anastomosed to the side branch of the four-branched
graft.
Myocardial protection was performed by the retrograde
infusion of cold blood cardioplegic solution, which was
introduced directly into the coronary sinus via right atriotomy. Selective antegrade cardioplegia may be used when
cardiac arrest cannot be achieved with retrograde cardioplegia alone or when retrograde administration is challenging.
In patients with prior coronary artery bypass grafting, patent grafts are identified to allow for occlusion during cardiac arrest. In particular, a right internal mammary artery
graft crossing the midline may pose a significant challenge
during re-entry. To avoid graft injury, we used pericardial

echocardiography ( VeriQC system, MediStim, Oslo, Norway) to identify the course of the graft before initiating dissection [17].
We typically initiate circulatory arrest at a tympanic
temperature of 25 °C and a core (bladder or rectal) temperature of 30 °C. However, if lower-body circulatory arrest
is expected to last longer than 30 min, then the target core
temperature is reduced to 25 °C to provide additional organ
protection [15].
Selective antegrade cerebral perfusion (SACP) is always
used in TAR with balloon-tipped catheters (SP-GRIPFLOW,
Fuji Systemes, Tokyo). A 14Fr balloon-tipped cannula was
inserted into the right brachiocephalic artery, and 12Fr cannulae were inserted into the left common carotid and left
subclavian arteries.
A sealed quadrifurcated Dacron graft was used for arch
replacement. Anastomoses were sequentially constructed
at the distal arch, proximal root, left subclavian artery,
left carotid artery, and right brachiocephalic artery. After
completion of the distal repair, antegrade systemic circulation was restarted through the side branch of the prosthesis,
and rewarming of the whole body to 36 °C was initiated.
Recently, in situ reconstruction of the left subclavian artery
was performed, followed by proximal aortic anastomosis
and the remaining two head vessels. After completion of
proximal anastomosis, coronary perfusion was restored via
cardiopulmonary bypass. Systemic rewarming and heartbeat adequate for weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass
(CPB) were successfully achieved in almost all cases after
arch vessel reconstruction.
Since 2015, we have used the frozen elephant trunk
(FET) technique with a Japan-made prosthesis (J-graft Frozenix®, Japan LifeLine, Tokyo) [18], when indicated. In
general, FET is avoided in cases of degenerative aneurysms
because of concerns regarding spinal cord ischemia. The
primary indications include acute dissections, with the goal
of reducing the distal false lumen, and chronic dissections
as part of planned staged repair, such as closure of entries
in areas where direct anastomosis is not feasible or in cases
involving extensive lesions.

Definitions of complications
In-hospital mortality was defined as death during index hospitalization. Postoperative stroke was defined as a newly
developed neurological deficit confirmed by CT. Prolonged
ventilation was defined as intubation for >24 h. Postoperative renal failure was defined as the need for hemodialysis.
The presence of new Q waves on electrocardiograms or an
unexpected need for CABG during the operation was classified as perioperative myocardial infarction. In accordance

with the definitions of the STS Adult Cardiac Surgery Database, composite major morbidity was defined as the occurrence of stroke, acute kidney injury, prolonged ventilation,
mediastinitis, or reoperation for any reason.

Statistical analysis
Continuous data are presented as the median ( interquartile
range [IQR] 25%–75%), and categorical data are presented
as numbers. The clinical characteristics of the two groups
were compared using Pearson’s χ2 test for categorical variables, unpaired t-test for normally distributed variables, and
Mann–Whitney U-test for skewed variables. A multivariate
logistic regression analysis was used to calculate the odds
ratios (OR) of risk factors for in-hospital mortality, accounting for resternotomy, age, sex, emergent status, FET, etiology, and the duration of operation and cardiopulmonary
bypass. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–
Meier method, and differences were compared using the
log-rank test. A multivariate Cox proportional hazards
regression analysis was performed to identify the factors
associated with survival. The results are presented as hazard
ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). All statistical tests were two-sided. The results were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. All analyses were performed
using SPSS version 25.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results
The preoperative characteristics are shown in Table 2. The
resternotomy group was younger and had a higher prevalence of Marfan syndrome than the first sternotomy group.
The euroSCORE II predicted in-hospital mortality rates of
primary and reoperation were 4.36% and 9.04%, respectively (P< 0.001). In the etiology of the present procedures,
the proportion of degenerative aneurysms was higher in the
first sternotomy group, and chronic dissection was higher in
the resternotomy group.
The operative details are listed in Tables 3 and 4. Peripheral arterial cannulation was performed in 40 patients (21
femoral and 19 axillary arteries), and 13 patients underwent
ascending aorta cannulation. Eight patients (15%) had injuries during dissection, but no lethal damage was observed.
The durations of operation, cardiopulmonary bypass, crossclamp, circulatory arrest, and selective cerebral perfusion
were significantly longer in the resternotomy group than
in the first group. The frequency of concomitant coronary
artery bypass grafting was higher in the first group, whereas
that of FET was higher in the resternotomy group.

Discussion
In Japan, the number of thoracic and thoracoabdominal
aortic repair procedures has been increasing annually [1,
19]. According to a report by the Japanese Association of
Thoracic Surgery, the in-hospital mortality rates with TAR
were 10.1% for acute dissection, 2.4% for chronic dissection, 3.0% for unruptured thoracic aortic aneurysm, and
21.8% for ruptured thoracic aortic aneurysm (TAA) [1]. On
the other hand, the report also showed arch TEVAR with
branch reconstruction and unruptured TAA at 2.8%, or with
ruptured TAA at 16.0% (the number of acute and chronic
aortic dissection cases being few, those were excluded) [1].
That report included primary and redo operations; therefore,
the specific outcomes of TAR after previous cardiovascular
surgery were unknown. In this study, the in-hospital mortality rate of TAR requiring resternotomy was 8% in the whole
cohort and 0% in the elective settings.
Few reports have compared primary and reoperative
procedures for aortic arch pathologies. Etz et al. reported
similar in-hospital mortality and long-term survival rates
between primary and reoperative arch surgery [11]. Diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and emergent
operations were significant risk factors for in-hospital mortality, and reoperation had no significant effect on overall
mortality [11]. In this study, age and emergency surgery
were identified as independent risk factors for long-term
survival.
Problems with previous cardiovascular surgeries include
re-entry injury and prolonged operation time, including
circulatory arrest and cerebral perfusion times. Hamid et
al. reported that the incidence of re-entry injury was low
(2.7%), but the mortality rate in patients with re-entry injury
was 26%, compared with 9% in those without re-entry injury
[20]. Preoperative CT, particularly with contrast enhancement, is crucial for evaluating surgical anatomy, especially

re-entry injury, and is also essential for determining appropriate cannulation sites [14, 20, 21]. In this study, the incidence of re-entry injuries was 15%. Although injuries to the
right atrium and prosthetic grafts occurred in some cases,
cardiopulmonary bypass was readily available through
peripheral cannulation, preventing fatal outcomes.
Although this was not the case in the present report, in
certain reoperations, the proximity of prosthetic grafts or
vital organs to the sternum may lead to catastrophic bleeding during resternotomy. Establishing cardiopulmonary
bypass in advance facilitates the management of large hemorrhages, and the addition of left ventricular apical venting
allows temporary circulatory arrest during sternal reentry.
Accordingly, while ascending aortic cannulation was initially the standard approach, the strategy has recently shifted
toward peripheral cannulation prior to sternotomy.
For peripheral cannulation, our initial choice is femoral
cannulation because it is technically the simplest method.
However, in many cases where the aortic condition is unfavorable and retrograde perfusion should be avoided, axillary
artery cannulation is recommended. Axillary cannulation
may be the optimal strategy for reducing perfusion-related
morbidity and adverse outcomes in operations involving
aortic arch pathologies, and it does not appear to increase
the procedural risk [22]. Furthermore, axillary cannulation
has been reported as a protective factor for survival in aortic
reoperation [11]. When in situ reconstruction of the left subclavian artery is challenging, anastomosis of a Dacron graft
to the left axillary artery can be employed for arterial cannulation, selective cerebral perfusion, and subclavian artery
reconstruction.
This report and previous reports [5, 23, 24] show that
prolonged CPB is a risk factor for in-hospital death. The
frozen elephant trunk (FET) procedure is effective in reducing the duration of operation and CPB time [25, 26]. Since
the commercially made prosthesis Frozenix was launched in
Japan in 2014, the FET procedure has been widely adopted.
We also used the FET technique for high-risk and dissection
cases, which contributed to shortening the operation time.
Furthermore, in this report, the resternotomy group had
a higher rate of chronic dissection, but this is because, at
our institution, hemiarch replacement is the first choice for
acute dissection [27]. The use of the FET technique allows
for proximalization of the distal anastomosis, thereby minimizing the extent of dissection, even in reoperative cases
with dense adhesions. Although FET use was not significantly associated with in-hospital mortality or long-term
survival in our study (Tables 6 and 7), it may offer technical advantages by facilitating distal anastomosis and reducing the extent of surgical dissection. These benefits could
contribute to reduced operative complexity and improved

perioperative safety of the procedure. Furthermore, this
benefit is not limited to reoperative settings and may also be
applicable to primary cases.
Volodos et al. performed the first hybrid aortic arch repair
in 1991 [28]. Since then, the number of hybrid repairs for
aortic arch pathologies has increased dramatically. Although
reports of debranching hybrid surgery or total endovascular
procedures for aortic arch pathologies are increasing, endovascular aortic arch repair involves numerous problems
[29, 30]. In particular, requiring zone 0 proximal landing
is associated with poor outcomes. The gold standard for
aortic arch repair remains open surgery, even if it requires
resternotomy.
The frequency of the Bentall procedure in our study
was relatively low compared with other reports. However,
according to the guidelines of the Japanese Circulation
Society [31], the procedure is indicated when the maximum
diameter is ≥55 mm or when it is <55 mm with an expansion rate of ≥5 mm within six months. The EACTS/STS
Guidelines also state that the procedure is indicated when
the lesion is at least 50 mm or larger [32]. The surgical procedures in our study were performed in accordance with
these recommendations. In addition, patients who underwent TAR with previous cardiovascular surgery during the
study period were included and were not excluded from the
analysis.
This study has several limitations. This was a retrospective study with relatively few patients in a single
center. The follow-up period was relatively short. Owing
to these limitations, the findings of this study should be
interpreted with caution and may not be directly generalizable to routine clinical practice. Nevertheless, our analysis
demonstrated no statistically significant difference in the
outcomes between patients undergoing re-sternotomy and
those undergoing first-time surgery (HR = 1.37, 95% CI,
0.75–6.37; P = 0.30 during follow-up). However, the confidence interval was relatively wide and included effect sizes
that could represent a clinically meaningful increase in risk.
Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.
The two groups were not propensity-matched for factors
such as age and aortic etiology; however, they were representative of patients presenting at a general hospital in Japan
over more than a decade. We also performed a propensity
score–matched analysis using age, sex, Marfan syndrome,
chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), emergency
status, peripheral artery disease, and etiology as matching
variables, resulting in 44 matched patients in each group.
After matching, there were no significant differences in
operative duration, cardiopulmonary bypass duration, or
postoperative complications compared to the unmatched
analysis. However, some selection bias related to the indication for resternotomy cannot be completely eliminated, and

the relatively small matched sample size limits the robustness of the propensity score–matched analysis.
Most resternotomy patients had previously undergone
ascending/hemiarch replacement for acute dissection. In
emergency conditions, we believe that a limited operation
may be performed safely, and arch operations can subsequently be undertaken electively by skilled surgeons. Consequently, there have been many cases of recurrent arch
replacement after ascending/hemiarch replacement at our
facility [27]. Although the composite morbidity rate was
relatively high, this was largely attributable to the inclusion
of prolonged mechanical ventilation (> 24 h) in the composite endpoint. At our institution, patients are weaned from
mechanical ventilation only after confirming the absence
of significant bleeding and other life-threatening complications. Consequently, the duration of postoperative mechanical ventilation may be prolonged in some cases. When
prolonged ventilation was excluded from the composite
endpoint, the composite morbidity rate decreased to 23% in
the first group and 25% in the resternotomy group, which we
believe represents an acceptable incidence of postoperative
complications. Many cases of resternotomy are complex,
both anatomically and technically; however, surgery can be
performed safely by performing a preoperative evaluation
and peripheral cannulation, which allows for the establishment of CPB in advance.
