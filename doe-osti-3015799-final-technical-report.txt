Executive Summary
This final technical report provides an archival summary of all research activities conducted under
DOE Award DE-SC0024642 at The University of Texas at Tyler during the period August 15, 2023
through August 14, 2024. The content of this report corresponds to the technical progress
documented in Year 1 and serves as the formal close-out of the UT Tyler performance site.
The project investigated three interconnected research objectives related to carbon dioxide
geological storage in deep saline aquifers: (1) the effects of reservoir heterogeneity and brine
properties on convective dissolution, (2) the alteration of reservoir flow properties due to rock-
fluid-CO 2 interactions, and (3) the development of computationally efficient surrogate models for
predicting flow property changes under uncertainty.
Through laboratory experiments using custom Hele-Shaw cells and comprehensive numerical
reservoir simulations, the project produced several findings. Experimental and simulation studies
demonstrated that permeability heterogeneity substantially influences CO 2 finger morphology and
dissolution patterns, with preferential flow toward high-permeability zones and enhanced sweep
in regions of greater connectivity. Higher average permeability was shown to accelerate the onset
of convective dissolution, which is favorable for safe CO 2 storage. Long-term simulations revealed
that dissolution trapping is the dominant sequestration mechanism, ultimately accounting for
approximately 80% of injected CO 2 over a 1,000-year horizon, while mineralization, though
representing a smaller mass fraction for siliciclastic reservoirs, plays a critical role in altering
reservoir geophysical properties.
Response surface method proxy models were successfully developed to characterize uncertainty
in carbonate mineralization reactions, identifying the calcite dissolution rate constant as the most
sensitive kinetic parameter. The project resulted in three peer-reviewed journal articles, multiple
conference presentations at national and regional venues, and provided training opportunities for
graduate and undergraduate researchers across engineering and science disciplines.
This document consolidates and finalizes all technical outcomes achieved at UT Tyler under this
award.

1. Introduction
The sequestration of carbon dioxide (CO 2 ) in deep saline aquifers is widely recognized as one of
the most viable long-term strategies for carbon management[1]. Deep saline aquifers are
particularly attractive storage targets due to their widespread geographic distribution, substantial
storage capacity estimated at thousands of gigatons globally, and the absence of competing
economic uses for the subsurface pore space[2].
When supercritical CO 2 (ScCO 2 ) is injected into a saline aquifer, it undergoes a sequence of
trapping mechanisms that evolve over time. Structural and residual trapping dominate in the near-
term following injection, but over decades to centuries, dissolution trapping, wherein CO 2
dissolves into the formation brine, and mineralization trapping , wherein dissolved CO 2 reacts with
host rock minerals to form stable carbonate precipitates, become progressively more
significant[1]. These latter mechanisms are generally considered more secure from a leakage risk
perspective, but they are also more difficult to predict accurately due to the complex and coupled
interactions among CO 2 , brine geochemistry, and host rock mineralogy.
The onset of convective dissolution is a particularly important phenomenon in the context of long-
term storage safety[3]. When dissolved CO 2 creates a density instability at the CO 2 -brine
interface, convective currents develop that transport dissolved CO 2 downward, away from the
caprock. A shorter onset time for this convective instability is desirable, as it accelerates the
removal of mobile CO 2 from the near-caprock region. The timing and dynamics of this process
are influenced by reservoir permeability, heterogeneity, brine composition, and pressure-
temperature conditions.
This project was designed to address critical knowledge gaps in these areas through an integrated
program of laboratory experimentation and numerical simulation, with the ultimate goal of
advancing both fundamental science and practical tools needed to support safe, effective CO 2
geological storage.

2. Project Objectives
The project was organized around three primary research objectives:
Objective 1: Evaluate the impact of reservoir heterogeneities and brine properties on CO 2
dissolution trapping. This objective focused on understanding how spatial variations in
permeability, the presence of fractures, and the type and concentration of dominant cations in
formation brine (Na+, Ca2+, Fe2+) influence the critical time and wavelength for the onset of
convective dissolution. Systematic experiments in custom Hele-Shaw cells and fine-scale
numerical simulations were employed to characterize these effects.
Objective 2: Evaluate the alteration of reservoir flow properties due to rock-fluid
interactions. The injection of ScCO 2 into a deep saline aquifer initiates complex precipitation and
dissolution reactions that depend on brine composition, mineral content, operating conditions,
and reservoir properties. This objective sought to characterize how these reactions modify
reservoir porosity and permeability over time, with particular attention to carbonate and basalt
reservoirs where alkaline Earth and ferrous cations can react significantly with injected CO 2 .

Objective 3: Develop advanced statistical surrogate models to predict flow property
changes. Given the computational expense of fully compositional reactive transport simulations
and the limited availability of detailed site characterization data during early project planning, this
objective aimed to develop robust, probabilistic surrogate models that efficiently predict changes
in reservoir porosity and permeability while accounting for uncertainty in mineral and brine
properties.

3. Technical Approach

3.1 Hele-Shaw Cell Experiments
Laboratory experiments were conducted using a modular custom-designed Hele-Shaw cell
apparatus to study CO 2 dissolution in porous media under controlled, reproducible conditions.
Three heterogeneous cells were prepared with varying average permeabilities and irregular
spatial permeability distributions created by packing glass beads of different sizes. This approach
generated complex permeability fields that mimic the heterogeneity expected at natural geological
storage sites. The transparent cell design enabled optical observation and image-based
quantification of CO 2 dissolution patterns throughout the experiments.

3.2 Spectrophotometric Analysis
A spectrophotometric method was employed to quantify spatial CO 2 concentration distributions
within the experimental cells. Analysis was performed independently across three color channels,
red, green, and blue, to identify the optimal correlation between pixel intensity and pH change
associated with CO 2 dissolution. Dissolved CO 2 mass was tracked continuously over time and
compared against analytical solutions for pure diffusion to calculate Sherwood numbers and
quantify the enhancement of mass transfer due to convective transport.

3.3 Numerical Reservoir Simulation
Comprehensive numerical simulations were carried out using a fully compositional reservoir
simulator capable of modeling multiphase flow, reactive transport, and mineral precipitation and
dissolution simultaneously. Simulations addressed both the convective dissolution process in
heterogeneous permeability fields and the long-term evolution, up to 1,000 years, of CO 2 trapping
mechanisms and reservoir flow properties. Stochastic permeability distributions were generated
to examine the influence of spatial variability and connectivity on CO 2 transport, with particular
attention to the role of high-permeability flow channels.

3.4 Response Surface Method for Surrogate Models
Proxy models were developed using the response surface method (RSM) to create
computationally efficient surrogate representations of the relationship between key input
parameters and quantities of interest. Input parameters included mineral composition (calcite,
dolomite, kaolinite content), brine chemistry (pH, cation concentrations), kinetic rate constants,
and injection conditions. Output quantities included CO 2 mineralization volume, injectivity index,
and changes in porosity and permeability. Quadratic response surfaces were fitted to data
generated by the full compositional simulator over numerous parameter combinations, enabling
rapid parametric analysis and uncertainty quantification.

4. Results and Discussion

4.1 CO2 Dissolution in Heterogeneous Porous Media (Objective 1)

4.1.1 Experimental Findings
Experiments conducted in three heterogeneous Hele-Shaw cells with varying average
permeabilities revealed several important findings regarding the role of formation heterogeneity
in governing CO 2 dissolution dynamics[4]. The CO 2 finger morphology observed in the
heterogeneous cells closely replicated patterns expected at natural geological storage sites. Due
to spatial permeability heterogeneity, finger movement was nonuniform, with preferential
migration toward high-permeability zones. Conversely, low-permeability regions exhibited
minimal dissolution, resulting in poor local sweep efficiency. These observations have direct
implications for estimating the overall rate and spatial extent of CO 2 dissolution in heterogeneous
reservoirs.
Cells with higher average permeability produced more vigorous vertical finger movement, while
cells with lower average permeability showed noticeably slower vertical transport. This differential
behavior was reflected in the morphology of the dissolved CO 2 regions: higher-permeability cells
produced pronounced finger-like dissolution structures dominated by vertical transport, while
lower-permeability cells produced plume-shaped dissolution regions where slower vertical
movement promoted lateral diffusive mixing. Higher permeability was also correlated with a faster
onset of convective dissolution, a finding with direct implications for storage safety, as earlier
onset accelerates the downward migration of CO 2 away from the caprock.
Spectrophotometric analysis demonstrated that the blue color channel provides the strongest
linear correlation with pH change, improving upon previous studies that relied on the gray channel.
A scaling relationship was established between the average finger growth rate and permeability,
providing a quantitative basis for relating observable dissolution dynamics to reservoir properties.
The maximum Sherwood number recorded was 6.8, observed in cells with the highest
permeability, quantifying the substantial enhancement of mass transfer due to convective
transport relative to pure molecular diffusion. The effects of heterogeneity on unsteady dissolution
flux were characterized, and the experimental results were expressed in terms of dimensionless
numbers applicable to other systems under comparable conditions.

4.1.2 Simulation Findings
Numerical simulations of CO 2 convective dissolution were performed using two heterogeneous
permeability fields possessing the same average permeability, and the results were compared
against a homogeneous baseline case[5]. The simulation results confirmed and extended the
experimental observations. Nonuniform finger movement was observed due to spatial
permeability heterogeneity, with preferential flow toward high-permeability zones. The simulations
also revealed uneven CO 2 concentration distributions within convective fingers, with local
concentrations varying in a manner consistent with the underlying permeability structure.
A critical finding was that the average CO 2 concentration after 154 minutes of dissolution was
higher in the homogeneous case than in either heterogeneous case. This result highlights the
importance of considering the full permeability distribution and connectivity channels, rather than
average permeability alone, when modeling CO 2 dissolution in geological storage formations.

 Page 5

Preferential flow paths were more pronounced in heterogeneous cases with high permeability
connectivity, underscoring the role of spatial structure in governing dissolution efficiency. The
proposed simulation methodology was demonstrated to be extensible to more complex
heterogeneous systems and different fluid flow regimes.

4.2 Reservoir Flow Property Alteration
 Long-term reservoir simulations spanning 1,000 years provided detailed insight into the
temporal evolution of CO 2 trapping mechanisms and their cumulative effects on reservoir
properties in a siliciclastic reservoir[6]. Although CO 2 is initially present as a mobile supercritical
phase following injection, residual and dissolution trapping progressively become the dominant
storage mechanisms. After approximately 684 years of simulation time, nearly all mobile CO 2 had
dissolved into the formation brine, a desirable outcome from a storage security perspective, as
mobile CO 2 trapped beneath caprocks carries the greatest risk of leakage through undetected or
reactivated fractures.
 Dissolution trapping was identified as the dominant long-term sequestration mechanism.
Within 300 years, approximately half of the injected CO 2 was stored via dissolution trapping, and
by 1,000 years this fraction reached approximately 80% of the total injected CO 2 . Residual
trapping was the second most significant mechanism, sequestering approximately 27% of injected
CO 2 at the 1,000-year mark. Mineralization trapping represented a comparatively smaller mass
fraction, approximately 2.97% at 1,000 years as expected for a siliciclastic reservoir, but was
found to be important because it produced measurable alterations in reservoir geophysical
properties. The inclusion of capillary pressure in the simulation substantially improved predicted
CO 2 dissolution rates across multiple time horizons and was identified as a significant factor
influencing both mineralization and dissolution trapping.
Mineral reaction analysis identified calcite and dolomite as the two primary minerals contributing
to carbonate precipitate formation. After 1,000 years of simulation time, kaolinite was the only
mineral undergoing net dissolution, while all other minerals precipitated due to their interactions
with injected CO 2 and the formation brine. At the end of the 10-year injection period, porosity was
reduced by 11.56% and reservoir permeability by 34.23%. This porosity-permeability reduction
was attributed primarily to halite precipitation concentrated near the wellbore region. Notably,
outside the near-wellbore zone, both porosity and permeability increased over the simulation
period, indicating that local near-wellbore conditions do not accurately represent broader reservoir
trends.
4.3 Surrogate Models and Uncertainty Analysis
The uncertainty analysis employing the response surface method provided valuable insights into
the sensitivity of carbonate mineralization to input parameters and validated the feasibility of
surrogate-based prediction for CO 2 storage applications[7]. Proxy models constructed using
quadratic response surfaces effectively captured the functional relationships between kinetic
parameters and key outputs including CO 2 injectivity and mineralization volume over extended
redistribution periods. These models performed well with a relatively small number of underlying
simulation runs, demonstrating their practical value as computationally efficient tools for decision-
making in CO 2 storage project planning and risk assessment.

 Page 6

The expanded parametric analysis enabled examination of the relationships among kinetic
parameters and process-controlling variables including CO 2 injection rate, brine pH, and initial
calcite content in the rock matrix. Calcite dissolution was identified as the primary mechanism
driving mineralization, with dolomite forming as a secondary precipitate through reactions
influenced by the availability of dissolved calcite, pH, and CO 2 concentration. The interplay
between these variables and the kinetics of both calcite and dolomite reactions was found to be
critical for accurately predicting long-term mineralization behavior.
The rate constant for calcite dissolution emerged as the most significant kinetic parameter, directly
controlling the overall reaction rate in carbonate systems. The uncertainty associated with this
parameter underscores the need for continued experimental characterization efforts to improve
predictive confidence. Both pH and initial calcite concentration exert significant control over
dolomite precipitation, while the CO 2 injection rate influences both calcite dissolution and dolomite
generation. No changes in quartz content were observed in any of the numerical experiments,
consistent with the generally slow kinetics of quartz reactions at the conditions considered.
The CO 2 injectivity index was demonstrated as an effective proxy for quantifying uncertainty
associated with carbonate mineral reactions. Tracking this index from field measurements or
laboratory tests provides a practical and operationally feasible approach for monitoring and
assessing mineralization-related uncertainty during active CO 2 storage operations.

5. Project Impact

5.1 Impact on the Principal Discipline
The project contributed to advancing fundamental understanding of CO 2 dissolution and
mineralization processes in deep saline aquifers, with implications for carbon capture and storage
technology development. The quantitative relationships established between permeability
heterogeneity and dissolution dynamics, and the demonstration that surrogate models can
effectively capture mineralization uncertainty, provide tools and insights directly applicable to
storage site characterization and risk assessment.

5.2 Interdisciplinary Impact
The project attracted significant interdisciplinary interest. Students enrolled in mechanical
engineering, electrical engineering, and computer science programs contributed to and benefited
from the research activities. The integration of machine learning techniques, numerical simulation,
and experimental methods created a cross-functional research environment that fostered
innovation and broad skill development across disciplines.

5.3 Educational and Training Impact
The Principal Investigator incorporated project-related content into undergraduate coursework
through an open-ended sustainability project in the Thermodynamics II course (CHEN 3302),
engaging undergraduate students in research related to carbon capture and storage. Independent
study opportunities were offered through a 3-credit graduate course (MENG 5399), culminating
in student presentations at the East Texas Research Conference and other internal venues. One
Master's student supported by the project successfully defended her thesis proposal during the

6. Conclusions and Lessons Learned

6.1 Technical Conclusions
The findings of this project collectively advance the understanding of CO 2 geological storage in
deep saline aquifers across multiple critical dimensions. The key conclusions are summarized
below:
 • Formation heterogeneity plays a critical role in determining CO 2 dissolution efficiency and
 storage safety. Permeability spatial distribution, not average permeability alone, governs
 dissolution finger dynamics, sweep efficiency, and the timing of convective onset. Higher-
 permeability formations facilitate faster convective dissolution onset, which is favorable for
 long-term storage security.
 • Dissolution trapping is the dominant long-term CO 2 sequestration mechanism in deep
 saline aquifers, progressively accounting for the majority of stored CO 2 over century-scale
 time horizons. The near-complete dissolution of mobile CO 2 within approximately 700
 years represents a critical safety milestone for storage security assessment.
 • Mineralization, while representing a smaller fraction of sequestered CO 2 by mass in
 siliciclastic reservoirs, has disproportionate importance due to its effects on reservoir
 geophysical properties. The spatial heterogeneity of mineral reactions, particularly the
 contrast between near-wellbore permeability reduction and broader reservoir permeability
 increase, must be considered in storage site characterization and long-term monitoring
 plans.
 • Capillary pressure is a significant factor influencing both mineralization and dissolution
 trapping and should be included in simulation models used for storage performance
 assessment and risk evaluation.
 • Response surface method proxy models can effectively and efficiently characterize the
 sensitivity of mineralization processes to input parameter uncertainty with a limited number
 of calibration runs. The rate constant for calcite dissolution was identified as the parameter
 most critically in need of further experimental quantification.
 • The CO 2 injectivity index provides a practical operational indicator for monitoring
 mineralization-related uncertainty in field applications and can serve as a real-time proxy
 for carbonate reaction progression.

6.2 Challenges and Lessons Learned
Recruitment of graduate students presented a notable challenge during the early phase of the
project, owing to the relatively recent establishment of the Chemical Engineering Department at
the original host institution and reduced graduate enrollment in adjacent departments due to
COVID pandemic. These challenges were addressed through targeted outreach using LinkedIn
and other professional platforms, as well as cross-departmental recruitment from mechanical
engineering, electrical engineering, and computer science programs. The interdisciplinary nature
of the resulting research team ultimately contributed positively to the project outcomes.
Research activities under this project were primarily concentrated during the first budget period.
The successful completion of the core experimental and simulation work during this phase
demonstrated the effectiveness of the integrated experimental-simulation approach adopted by
the project team and generated a strong body of results addressing each of the three project
objectives.
