A novel strategy for controlled release of sialic acid based on W1/O/W2 emulsion microencapsulation for gut microbiota modulation
Authors: Donglei Zheng, Mengle Li, Ying Qian, Xiangsong Chen, Jianglin Chen, Long Jin, Lili Cao, Min Pang, Jiaying Huo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sialic acid (Neu5Ac) possesses a wide range of bioactivities; however, its instability under acidic conditions prevents it from effectively reaching the intestine. This underscores the urgent need to develop a stable delivery system capable of protecting Neu5Ac from gastric degradation and achieving its controlled release in the intestine. To enhance the stability and achieve controlled release, Neu5Ac was encapsulated within water-in-oil-in-water (W1/O/W2) emulsion-based microcapsules in this work. S2 emulsions featured a “dual-chamber, tri-phase” structure, comprising a Neu5Ac-chitosan fluid gel (inner aqueous phase), maize oil (oil phase), and an external aqueous phase of 13% sodium starch octenyl succinate, 5% β-cyclodextrin, 1.5% sodium caseinate, and 0.5% carboxymethyl cellulose. Microstructural analyses confirmed spherical morphology with core-shell architecture and bimodal droplet distribution (1-10 μm). In addition, S2 emulsions exhibited an encapsulation efficiency (EE) of 92.44%, a zeta potential of −60.80 mV, a Sauter mean diameter (d3,2) of 1.337 μm, shear-thinning behavior and weak gel network stability. S2 emulsions were spray-dried to obtain S2 microcapsules. S2 microcapsules exhibited an EE of 89.43% and a d3,2 of 3.839 μm, while FT-IR and XRD verified physical encapsulation without covalent bonding. In vitro digestion, S2 microcapsules exhibited controlled release behavior during simulated intestinal digestion, with a Neu5Ac release rate of 85.61% after 120 min. In mice, microencapsulated Neu5Ac increased the relative abundance of Akkermansia, Lactobacillus, and Bifidobacterium. This system demonstrates promising potential for the co-delivery and controlled release of Neu5Ac, and shows great promise for expanding its applications in the food industry.

Introduction

Sialic acid (Neu5Ac) is a subset of nonulosonic acids, which is nine-carbon α-keto aldonic acids (Yang et al., 2021). The aqueous solutions of Neu5Ac are acidic because Neu5Ac can ionize H+. Neu5Ac can be acetylated, methylated, sulphated or phosphorylated to form different isomers, which performs diverse functions in organisms, such as enhancing intestinal absorption, promoting neural development, immunoregulation, regulating tumor microenvironment and skin-whitening (Yang et al., 2021). The reduced degree of sialylation in intestinal mucins compromises the colonization of gut microbes and the maintenance of intestinal homeostasis (Yao et al., 2022). Targeted supplementation of Neu5Ac can enhance the diversity of the gut microbiota and promote the colonization of beneficial bacteria in the intestine (Coker et al., 2021). However, Neu5Ac exhibits an accelerated degradation rate in environments with a pH < 3.0, resulting in the loss of its bioactivity (Zhu et al., 2020). This characteristic limits the application of Neu5Ac as a bioactive substance in the food industry. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop effective protection and delivery system that enable targeted and controlled release of Neu5Ac in the intestinal tract, thereby facilitating its application in the food industry.

In response to the poor stability of Neu5Ac and its premature release in the gastrointestinal environment, the development of food-grade delivery systems has become an important strategy for improving its bioaccessibility and functional efficacy. Current research has mainly focused on systems such as microspheres, microbeads, hydrogels, nanoparticles, and gastroretentive or mucoadhesive carriers, which can enhance the gastrointestinal stability and targeted release of bioactive compounds through encapsulation, adhesion, controlled release, and local retention (X. Sun et al., 2022). Among these systems, hydrogels are considered promising carriers for the food delivery of Neu5Ac because of their three-dimensional porous networks, good biocompatibility, high water-holding capacity, and tunable degradation properties (Ge et al., 2022; L. Xu et al., 2024). Recent studies have shown that composite hydrogels constructed from gelatin, pectin, or carboxymethyl cellulose in combination with whey protein can effectively encapsulate Neu5Ac and significantly reduce its release during simulated gastric digestion, thereby improving its digestive stability and delivery efficiency (L. Xu et al., 2024). In food applications, Neu5Ac delivery systems can be integrated into whey protein-based products, infant formula, and functional dairy products, providing a technical basis for improving the nutritional utilization and functional stability of Neu5Ac. However, their compatibility with different food matrices, scalability, in vivo release behavior, and efficacy in humans remain to be further validated (Ge et al., 2022; Lacomba et al., 2011; L. Xu et al., 2024).

Water-in-oil-in-water (W1/O/W2) emulsions have attracted increasing attention as potential carrier systems for Neu5Ac because of their capacity for encapsulation, protection, and controlled release. Compared with single emulsions, W1/O/W2 emulsions possess a multicompartment structure that encapsulates hydrophilic bioactive compounds in the inner aqueous phase, while the oil and external aqueous phases act as dual barriers to improve core protection, reduce leakage, and enable controlled release (Hu et al., 2022). However, their application is limited by their inherently metastable structure, which is mainly associated with the high interfacial energy generated by the large contact area between immiscible phases. During storage and processing, W1/O/W2 emulsions are prone to gravitational separation, flocculation, coalescence, Ostwald ripening, and water migration, leading to reduced encapsulation efficiency and uncontrolled release behavior (Kumar et al., 2022). To improve their stability, recent studies have focused on strengthening the interfacial layer and restricting droplet mobility. For example, cellulose nanocrystal/β-lactoglobulin complexes can form a secondary interfacial layer to enhance emulsion viscosity and stability (Wu et al., 2025), while gelation of the external aqueous phase using natural polysaccharides such as xanthan gum and pectin can provide a three-dimensional network that suppresses droplet migration and coalescence (Boudoukhani et al., 2024; Pang et al., 2024). Further stabilization can be achieved by inducing gelation in multiple phases, thereby improving structural integrity, controlled-release performance, and textural tunability (Varnaitė-Kapočė et al., 2025; Wenjuan Wang, Sun and Xia, 2023). In addition, emulsifiers such as polyglycerol polyricinoleate (PGPR), lecithin (LE), and polyglyceryl ester of fatty acid (PGFE) can enhance W1/O/W2 emulsion formation and physical stability by modulating the oil-water interfacial film, interfacial tension, and droplet structure (Altuntas et al., 2017; M. Zhang and Zhang, 2025). Nevertheless, W1/O/W2 emulsions remain susceptible to destabilization during long-term storage, suggesting that conversion into a solid matrix may be necessary for practical Neu5Ac delivery.

Microencapsulation offers such a strategy by entrapping the Neu5Ac-loaded emulsion or active core within protective wall materials, thereby reducing exposure to environmental stresses and improving storage stability. However, the microencapsulation performance of W1/O/W2 emulsions strongly depends on wall-material composition, interfacial structure, and the adaptability of the emulsion system to spray drying. Adachi et al. (2004) reported that W1/O/W2 emulsion microcapsules prepared with maltodextrin as the wall material showed a higher encapsulation efficiency than those prepared with gum arabic (0.825 vs. 0.668), indicating that wall-material type directly affects the preservation of emulsion structure and the extent of core-material leakage during spray drying. Furthermore, Berendsen et al. (2015) demonstrated that whey protein-polysaccharide interfacial systems, particularly WPI-CMC, improved droplet stability and enhanced the retention of active compounds in dried microcapsules. Hu et al. (2022) also found that introducing suitable gel-forming polymers into the inner aqueous phase reduced the migration of hydrophilic core materials into the external aqueous phase, thereby improving the encapsulation efficiency and reconstitution stability of W1/O/W2 emulsions and their spray-dried microcapsules. These findings suggest that the coordinated regulation of wall materials, interfacial layers, and the inner aqueous phase is critical for reducing core-material leakage and improving powder quality and encapsulation efficiency. Therefore, constructing W1/O/W2 emulsions with good droplet stability, appropriate viscosity, and compatibility with spray drying is a prerequisite for the efficient microencapsulation, protection, and controlled delivery of hydrophilic bioactive compounds such as Neu5Ac.

Building on the successful preparation of Neu5Ac-loaded water-in-oil emulsions (Pang et al., 2022), this study developed spray-drying-compatible W1/O/W2 emulsions as templates for Neu5Ac microencapsulation, aiming to overcome the limitations of W1/O/W2 emulsions in long-term storage and achieve the controlled release of sialic acid. Weak gelation was introduced into both the internal and external aqueous phases to reinforce the emulsion structure and improve its adaptability to spray drying, thereby enabling transformation into a solid matrix. In addition, 16S rRNA sequencing was employed to evaluate the potential effects of the resulting microcapsules on gut microbiota. Overall, this work proposes a feasible microencapsulation strategy for the intestinal controlled release of Neu5Ac and provides a technical basis for its potential application in functional foods.

Materials and methods

Materials

Sialic acid (Neu5Ac; purity: 95%) was provided by Wuhan Zhongke Guanggu Green Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Polyglycerol polyricinoleate (PGPR) and chitosan (CH; deacetylation degree is 95%), β-cyclodextrin (β-CD), pectin (PE) and carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC), sodium starch octenyl succinate (SSOS), lecithin (LE), polyglyceryl ester of fatty acid (PGFE), sodium caseinate (SC), urea, uric acid, α-amylase (>100 U/mg), pepsin (15,000 U/g), lipase (15-35 U/mg), pancreatin (200 U/mg), bile extract (bile acid content ≥60%), NaCl, NaOH, HCl, H2SO4, KCl, Na2CO3, KSCN and NaH2PO4 were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Maize oil (food grade) were purchased from a local supermarket. Unless otherwise specified, the materials and reagents mentioned above are of chemical purity grade.

Preparation of W1/O/W2 emulsions

Preparation of the internal aqueous phase (W1): Neu5Ac (5.0%, wt/vol; pH = 2.52 ± 0.21) was dissolved in water, followed by the addition of CH (2.0%, wt/vol) to obtain an Neu5Ac-CH aqueous solution (pH = 6.31 ± 0.33). CH induced gelation of the W1, thereby inhibiting emulsions destabilization phenomena such as Ostwald ripening and coalescence.

Preparation of the oil phase: PGPR (2.0%; wt/wt) was added to maize oil.

Preparation of the external aqueous phase (W2): The wall materials were dissolved in water according to the specified proportions to prepare W2. Preparation of water-in-oil (W1/O) emulsions: The W1 and oil phase were mixed at a ratio of 3:7 (wt/wt) and pre-stirred at 1500 rpm for 15 min until a homogeneous mixture was obtained. The mixture was then sheared using a high-speed homogenizer (FA25, FLUKO, Shanghai, China) at 22,000 rpm for 3 min, followed by high-pressure homogenization (AH-NANO, ATS, Jiangsu, China) at 150 MPa for 3 cycles to obtain the W1/O emulsions.

Preparation of W1/O/W2 emulsions: The W1/O emulsions and W2 were mixed at a ratio of 3:5 (vol/vol) and sheared using a high-speed homogenizer (FA25, FLUKO, Shanghai, China) at 16,000 rpm for 4 min to obtain a homogeneous mixture. Subsequently, the mixture was subjected to high-pressure homogenization (AH-NANO, ATS, Jiangsu, China) at 150 MPa for 3 cycles to obtain the W1/O/W2 emulsions.

Characterization of W1/O/W2 emulsions

Microstructure of W1/O/W2 emulsions

Following dilution with water to an appropriate concentration, the samples were examined using a microscope (ML31, Mshot, Guangzhou, China) with a digital camera.

Particle size distributions of W1/O/W2 emulsions

Particle size analysis of the W1/O/W2 emulsions was performed on a Mastersizer 2000 (Malvern, UK) with water (refractive index: 1.333) as the dispersion medium at an obscuration of 5-10%. The refractive index and absorption of W1/O/W2 emulsions were set to 1.467 and 0.1, respectively. The average particle size was characterized by the Sauter mean diameter (d3,2).

Zeta potential of W1/O/W2 emulsions

Zeta potential measurements were performed at 25 °C using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern, UK). The samples were diluted to appropriate proportion with water to avoid multiple scattering.

Determination of Neu5Ac concentration

The Neu5Ac content was subsequently measured using a Sialic Acid Content Assay Kit (BC5365, Solarbio, Beijing, China). The NeuAc in the samples (W1/O/W2 emulsions, microcapsules, or simulated digestive fluids) was extracted or diluted using the extraction solution, followed by centrifugation to obtain the supernatant (Supernatant A). The chromogenic reagent was added to the supernatant, and the mixture was incubated in a boiling water bath at 100 °C for 15 min. After cooling to room temperature, the mixture was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min to collect the supernatant (Supernatant B). Finally, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 560 nm using a microplate reader (Varioskan LUX; Thermo Scientific, MA, USA), and the Neu5Ac content was calculated by comparing with the standard and blank controls. Detailed procedures are available in the manufacturer’s instructions.

Encapsulation efficiency (EE) of W1/O/W2 emulsions and microcapsules

The EE of Neu5Ac in W1/O/W2 emulsions could be calculated using Eq. (1):(1)EE(%)=Etotal−EouterEtotal×100where Etotal (mmol) represents the amount of Neu5Ac added to form the W1/O/W2 emulsions or microcapsules, Eouter (mmol) represents the amount of non-encapsulated Neu5Ac in the water phase.

Rheological properties of W1/O/W2 emulsions

The rheological properties of W1/O/W2 emulsions were assessed using a DHR-3 controlled-stress rheometer (TA, UK) equipped with a cone-and-plate geometry (40 mm diameter, 2° angle) and a Peltier temperature control system. Measurements were conducted at 25 °C. The shear rate-scan was performed over a range of 0.01-100 s−1, while angular frequency-scan and oscillation strain-scan were conducted over a range of 0.1-150 rad/s (strain: 0.1%) and 0.1%-100% (frequency: 1 Hz), respectively.

Storage stability analysis of W1/O/W2 emulsions

The W1/O/W2 emulsions were transferred into cylindrical glass vials and stored at 25 ± 1 °C. Stability was quantified using a settlement index (SI). The SI of each sample was measured at two-day intervals, which reflected the extent of phase separation resulting from emulsion destabilization, with a lower value indicating better stability. The calculation formula was as follows.(2)SI(%)=HH0×100where H (cm) is the height of the destabilized phase and H0 (cm) is the total height of the W1/O/W2 emulsions.

Preparation of microcapsules by spray-drying

The W1/O/W2 emulsions were converted into microcapsules via spray drying using a B-290 spray dryer (BÜCHI, Switzerland). The inlet temperature, feed rate, aspirator rate, and atomizing air flow were preliminarily set at 145 ± 5 °C, 5 mL/min, 90%, and 500 L/h, respectively, and the outlet temperatures maintained at 85 ± 5 °C.

Characterization of microcapsules

Microstructure of microcapsules

The microcapsules were mounted on conductive adhesive tape, sputter-coated with gold at 9.0 Pa for 120 s, and observed using a high-resolution field-emission scanning electron microscope (Regulus 8230, Hitachi, Japan) at 2.0 kV with magnifications of 2000× and 5000×.

Particle size distributions of microcapsules

Particle size analysis of the microcapsules was performed on a Malvern Mastersizer 2000 (Malvern, UK). Air (refractive index: 1.000) was used as the dispersant. For the measurement, the refractive index and absorption of the particles were specified as 1.520 and 0.1, respectively (Jia et al., 2019).

Surface oil content (SOC) of microcapsules

The microcapsules were washed by petroleum ether (30-60 °C) and petroleum ether was collected in triangular bottles. The surface oil of microcapsules was obtained after evaporation of petroleum ether. The SOC of microcapsules could be calculated using Eq. (3):(3)SOC(%)=m2−m1m0×100where m0 (g) represents the weight of microcapsule, m1 (g) represents the net weight of triangular bottle, m2 (g) represents the weight of surface oil and triangular bottle.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) of microcapsules

The chemical characteristics of the microcapsules were analyzed by FT-IR spectroscopy using a Nicolet iS10 spectrometer (Thermo, USA) equipped with an ATR accessory, with spectra recorded over 400-4000 cm−1 using 64 scans at a resolution of 4 cm−1.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of the microcapsules

XRD analysis was performed on a X-ray diffractometer (PANalytical, Netherlands) with a Cu target. Scans were conducted from 5° to 70° (2θ) at a step size of 0.02° under ambient air conditions.

Determination of thermogravimetric curves for microcapsules

Thermogravimetric analysis of the samples was performed using a simultaneous thermal analyzer (STA449F5, Netzsch, Germany). The temperature program was set from 30 °C to 700 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min, with a N2 purge at a flow rate of 20 mL/min.

In vitro simulated digestion of microcapsules

The simulated digestive fluids and digestion procedure were prepared and conducted according to the INFOGEST 2.0 protocol established by Brodkorb et al. (2019) through the international consensus of the COST INFOGEST network. The compositions of simulated salivary fluid (SSF), simulated gastric fluid (SGF), and simulated intestinal fluid (SIF) were as follows: SSF (pH = 7.0) contained CaCl2 (1.5 mM) and salivary amylase (75 U/mL); SGF (pH = 3.0) contained CaCl2 (0.15 mM) and pepsin (2000 U/mL); and SIF (pH = 7.0) contained bile salts (10 mM), CaCl2 (0.6 mM), trypsin (100 U/mL) and lipase (60 U/mL). The digestion procedure was performed as follows. The sample was mixed with SSF at a ratio of 1:1 (wt/wt) and stirred at 120 rpm and 37 °C for 2 min to obtain the oral digestion product. The oral digestion product was then mixed with SGF at a ratio of 1:1 (vol/vol) and stirred at 120 rpm and 37 °C for 2 h to obtain the gastric digestion product. Finally, the gastric digestion product was mixed with SIF at a ratio of 1:1 (vol/vol) and stirred at 120 rpm and 37 °C for 2 h to obtain the intestinal digestion product.

Release behavior of FFAs in SIF

The pH of SSF was monitored and maintained at 7.0. The release rate of FFAs in SIF was determined according to the method described by Li and McClements (2010), and could be calculated using Eq. (4).(4)ReleaserateofFFAs(%)=VNaOH×CNaOH×Mlipid2Wlipid×100where VNaOH (L) and CNaOH (mol/L) are the volume and concentration of the NaOH solution consumed, respectively; Mlipid (g/mol) is the molecular mass of the triacylglycerol oil, Wlipid (g) is oil content of microcapsules.

Release behavior of Neu5Ac in vitro simulated digestion

After incubated in SSF and SGF, the HCl or NaOH solution was dropped to stop the reaction and reaction mixture was centrifuged (10,000 rpm, 30 min) to obtain clear liquid for measuring release rate of Neu5Ac. During incubated in SIF, reaction mixture was taken every 20 min and stopped reaction by HCl. Then it was centrifuged (10,000 rpm, 30 min) to obtain clear liquid for measuring release rate of Neu5Ac. Continuous sampling was performed throughout the experiment.

Animal grouping and treatment

All procedures involving animals were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Hefei University of Technology and were approved under ethical permit No. HFUT20250409001M. C57BL/6 mice (male, 4 weeks, 18-22 g), obtained from Jiangsu GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China), were acclimatized and maintained under SPF conditions. After 7 days of acclimatization, mice received Neu5Ac by oral gavage at a dose of 100 mg/kg (for microcapsules, the dose was calculated based on Neu5Ac content).

In vivo digestion of microcapsules

After a 24 h fasting period, the mice were orally gavaged with microcapsules at a dose equivalent to 100 mg/kg Neu5Ac. Mice were then euthanized by cervical dislocation at 1 h intervals, and intestinal contents were collected for determination of Neu5Ac concentrations.

16S rRNA sequencing

DNA was extracted from mouse cecal contents using the E.Z.N.A.® Stool DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, USA). The quality, concentration, and purity of DNA were assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo, USA), respectively. The V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified with primers 338F/806R (338F: 5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′, 806R: 5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) on a GeneAmp 9700 thermal cycler (ABI, USA). Amplified products were used to construct libraries with the TruSeq® DNA PCR-Free Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA). Libraries were quantified using Qubit® and qPCR, and qualified ones were sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, USA). Taxonomic annotation was performed using QIIME2 and the SILVA database.

Shannon and Chao1 indices were used as α-diversity metrics, whereas principal co-ordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis and weighted UniFrac distances was used to evaluate β-diversity. Statistical differences in Shannon, Chao1, and PCoA results were analyzed using Tukey’s test and the Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate. Key differential bacterial taxa were identified using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe). The effect sizes of differentially abundant taxa were determined by linear discriminant analysis (LDA) based on the Kruskal-Wallis test, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05 and an LDA score threshold of 4.

Statistical analysis

All the tests were performed independently in triplicate, and the results were recorded as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistics were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using IBM SPSS Statistics 26. Different lowercase letters (a-d) indicate significant differences among different groups (P < 0.05).

Results and discussion

Microstructure of W1/O/W2 emulsions

The structural features of emulsions are presented in Fig. 1A. The observation of smaller droplets within larger emulsion droplets confirmed the distinct compartmentalization of the W1/O/W2 system. The W1, oil phase, and W2 were separated by interfacial films, forming a distinctive “dual-chamber, tri-phase” structure characteristic of W1/O/W2 emulsions. W. Wang et al. (2025) also observed analogous structures when stabilizing W1/O/W2 emulsions with rice bran protein. The structural integrity of W1/O/W2 emulsions relies on the mechanical strength and viscoelasticity of the oil-water interface. Bello-Flores et al. (2014) confirmed that SSOS exhibits high viscosity, which contributes to enhancing the strength of the oil-water interface, thereby improving the structural stability of the emulsions. Furthermore, the interaction between natural polysaccharides and proteins also contributes to maintaining the structural stability of W1/O/W2 emulsions (Boudoukhani et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025). Nevertheless, the high viscosity of the external aqueous phase significantly increases shear resistance during the preparation process, leading to substantial homogenization impedance and ultimately resulting in uneven droplet size distribution. Particle size distributions of W1/O/W2 emulsions

The particle size distributions of the emulsions are presented in Fig. 1B. The W1/O/W2 emulsions exhibited a bimodal particle size distribution, and predominantly concentrated in the first peak region. The proportions of the first peak are 88.81% (S1), 92.24% (S2), 91.86% (S3), and 84.33% (S4), respectively. It was speculated that the particle size distribution was influenced by factors such as free oil, emulsifier, and flocculation (Barbosa and Garcia-Rojas, 2022). This observation aligned with the optical microscopy results of the emulsions-nonuniform droplet distribution leads to a bimodal particle size pattern (Fig. 1A). The interactions between SC and polysaccharides contribute to the formation of more uniform and stable droplets, with the interaction between SC and CMC showing a more pronounced effect (Liu et al., 2012; Oppermann et al., 2018). Saavedra Isusi et al. (2021) reported that the high viscosity imparted by pectin promoted the uneven adsorption of wall materials at the oil-water interface or the formation of bridging structures, thereby facilitating droplet aggregation and coalescence and ultimately leading to a more pronounced bimodal size distribution in S3. On the other hand, previous studies have demonstrated that SSOS exhibits potential as an emulsifier to stabilize the oil-water interface and increase the viscosity of W1/O/W2 emulsions (Bello-Flores et al., 2014; Timgren et al., 2013). Although Zhang et al., 2025 suggested that increasing the viscosity of the external aqueous phase can restrict droplet movement, thereby contributing to the stability of W1/O/W2 emulsions, excessively high viscosity may elevate the risk of droplets aggregation and excessive adsorption of the wall material, ultimately leading to changes in particle size distribution and an increase in the mean droplet size. According to Stokes’ law of sedimentation, emulsions with smaller particle sizes possess superior stability (Vallath and Shanmugam, 2022). The d3,2 of S1, S2, and S4 were 1.753, 1.337, and 1.996 μm, respectively, with the droplets predominantly distributing in the range of 1-10 μm (Fig. 1B and C). Velderrain-Rodríguez et al. (2019) reported that the presence of CMC in the external aqueous phase contributed to a reduction in droplet size, which may explain the lower d3,2 of S2 compared with S1. In addition, this particle size distribution allowed the droplets to remain relatively stable and prevented bridging flocculation (Y. Zhang et al., 2023). Among S1-S4, S2 (3.147 m2/g) exhibited a significantly larger specific surface area than S1 (1.720 m2/g), S3 (2.537 m2/g), and S4 (1.283 m2/g), which facilitated moisture removal during spray drying and thereby promoted the formation of microcapsules (Fig. 1D). This result can be attributed to the smaller and more uniform droplet size of S2 compared with S1, S3, and S4.

Zeta potential of W1/O/W2 emulsions

The magnitude of the zeta potential is often used as a key indicator to predict the long-term stability of emulsion systems. An absolute value of zeta potential greater than 30 mV ensures the long-term stability of the system through electrostatic repulsion (Kumar et al., 2022; Prichapan et al., 2021). Regardless of the formulation, the zeta potential of the W1/O/W2 emulsions was lower than −30 mV (Fig. 1E), indicating the negative surface charges on the droplets served as a key stabilizing factor, conferring greater stability to the W1/O/W2 emulsions. The zeta potential of S2 was lowest (−60.80 ± 0.90 mV; Fig. 1E), due to the introduction of CMC and SC that increased the amount of negative charges in wall materials. Likewise, analogous findings were noted in the study by Estévez et al. (2019).

Encapsulation efficiency of W1/O/W2 emulsions

The EEs of S1, S2, S3 and S4 were 77.62 ± 2.39%, 92.44 ± 1.94%, 75.27 ± 1.32% and 90.06 ± 2.45%, respectively (Fig. 1F), indicating the Neu5Ac was successfully encapsulated within the W1/O/W2 emulsions, mainly due to the loading effect of inner aqueous phase gelated by CH, which reduced the release in the process of W1/O/W2 emulsions preparation. This result is consistent with Guerrero et al. (2026), who reported that the three-dimensional network structure of CH and the interactions between bioactive compounds and the matrix facilitated the encapsulation and loading of bioactive compounds. Moreover, the interaction between SC and CMC was attributed as one of the reasons for the highest EE observed in formulation S2.

Rheological properties of W1/O/W2 emulsions

The viscosity of W1/O/W2 emulsions decreased with the increase of shear rate (0.02-1.00 s−1), performing a shear-thinning property (Fig. 2A). The elevated viscosities of S2 and S4 indicated that their stability may be enhanced, further suggesting that CMC contributed more substantially to the increase in viscosity than PE (Jeong et al., 2025). Angular frequency-scan revealed that the storage modulus (G′) of the emulsions exceeded the loss modulus (G″) across the measured range (0.1-10 rad/s), indicating that the elastic deformation mainly occurred. These results implied the establishment of a gel-like network with weak strength, arising from interfacial interactions among the wall materials (Fig. 2B). Consistent results were obtained from oscillation strain-scan. Prior to the critical strain value, G′ remained greater than G″, confirming the elastic behavior of emulsions (Fig. 2C). Previous studies have demonstrated that gelation-mediated restriction of dispersed-phase movement and migration represents an effective and feasible strategy for enhancing the stability of W1/O/W2 emulsions (L. Li et al., 2025). However, an overly robust gel network may compromise system fluidity and atomization efficiency, leading to enlarged droplets and powder particles, broader particle size distribution, and potential reductions in drying efficiency and powder quality (Serfert et al., 2013). In this study, the weak gel structure formed by SSOS, β-CD, SC, PE, and CMC transformed the continuous aqueous phase into a viscoelastic matrix. This matrix restrained the mobility of the O/W droplets through hydrodynamic resistance and network encapsulation, thereby suppressing Ostwald ripening, coalescence, and phase migration, and ultimately enhancing the stability of the W1/O/W2 emulsions (Boudoukhani et al., 2024; Varnaitė-Kapočė et al., 2025).Fig. 2Rheological behavior and SI of W1/O/W2 emulsions. Stability of W1/O/W2 emulsions

The practical application of W1/O/W2 emulsions is predominantly limited by their instability, which arises from the combined action of gravitational separation, molecular migration, and Ostwald ripening (L. Li et al., 2025). Emulsion stability was evaluated based on the SI. The order of stability was S2 > S4 > S1 > S3 (Fig. 2D), which aligned with the predictions from the rheological analysis. It is noteworthy that higher emulsions viscosity leads to greater stability, suggesting that the enhanced viscoelasticity of the external aqueous phase contributed to improving the stability of W1/O/W2 emulsions (M. Li et al., 2020).

Microstructure of microcapsules

The surface morphology of microcapsules is closely related to their physicochemical properties and functionality, playing a crucial role in the encapsulation of Neu5Ac. No visually discernible differences were observed among the microcapsule samples (Fig. 3A). The microcapsules displayed a spherical morphology with some surface indentation, while no obvious cracks were observed (Fig. 3A). This indicated that the external aqueous phase of the W1/O/W2 emulsions was dehydrated to form a shell wrapped with W1/O droplets (Akbarbaglu et al., 2019). The surface indentation was possibly attributed to the shrinkage of the wall materials in the early drying stages (Korin et al., 2025). Furthermore, the high total solid content of the formulations in this study led to uneven cooling in the later drying phase, thus resulting in surface wrinkling of the microcapsules (Chew et al., 2018). Particle size distributions of microcapsules

A low-intensity peak was observed in the range of 0.1 μm to 1.0 μm (Fig. 3B), attributed to the fine particles formed by free wall material (Zhang et al., 2025). The proportions corresponding to this first peak were 4.08%, 5.23%, 3.50%, and 4.46%, respectively, which were considered negligible in the analysis. Ignoring this sub-peak, the particle size distributions of the microcapsules exhibited a unimodal pattern, with a size range of 1.5-60.0 μm (Fig. 3B). Compared to the W1/O/W2 emulsions, the d3,2 of the microcapsules increased by 3.414 μm, 2.502 μm, 2.291 μm, and 2.014 μm, respectively. This was attributed to the higher total solid content and the thicker shell, which caused the water evaporation rate to exceed the diffusion rate of water within the shell, consequently leading to larger particle sizes (Figs. 1C and. 3C) (Littringer et al., 2013). Notably, S2, containing both CMC and SC, exhibited the smallest d3,2 (3.839 μm), suggesting that this particular wall-material combination favored the production of microcapsules with reduced size and uniform distribution. Y. Wang et al. (2024) demonstrated that sodium carboxymethyl cellulose can interact with proteins to form films with favorable mechanical properties, which may support W1/O/W2 emulsion formation and enhance emulsion stability during spray drying, ultimately facilitating microcapsule formation.

Surface oil content and encapsulation efficiency of microcapsules

SOC and EE are key parameters for assessing the performance of microencapsulation. S2 exhibited the optimal encapsulation effect, with a SOC of 2.25% and an EE of 89.43% (Fig. 3D and E). Additionally, the SOC (3.59%) and EE (84.31%) of S4 were significantly higher than those of S1 and S3 (Fig. 3D and E), indicating that the incorporation of CMC improved the encapsulation effectiveness. This enhancement was attributed to the fact that CMC contributed to more uniform shell formation and increased the mechanical strength of the microcapsule wall (Yanfei Li, Zhang, Zhao, Lv, & Liu, 2024). Compared with the W1/O/W2 emulsions (Fig. 1F), the EE of the microcapsules (Fig. 3E) decreased by 4.32%, 3.01%, 7.64%, and 5.75%, respectively, which was due to structural changes during shell formation and the disruptive effects of high temperature and negative pressure experienced during the spray-drying process (F. Zhang et al., 2025). Furthermore, the favorable encapsulation results observed for S2 and S4 suggested that the pores on the surface of microcapsules did not adversely affect the encapsulation performance.

FT-IR spectroscopy analysis

All microcapsule samples exhibited characteristic absorption peaks of Neu5Ac-CH and maize oil (Fig. 4A and B), confirming that these two components were integrated at the molecular level within the same particle (Guo et al., 2024). A sharp and intense absorption peak at 1724 cm−1 was observed for the C=O in maize oil (Fig. 4A). This characteristic peak was retained in all microcapsule samples, accompanied by a shift toward higher wavenumbers (1743 cm−1; Fig. 4B). This indicated that no hydrolysis or oxidation of the oil phase occurred during the encapsulation process (Agu et al., 2024). The absorption peaks in the range of 2800-2900 cm−1 were attributed to C-H vibrations, corresponding to long-chain alkanes in maize oil, as well as glycosidic units in Neu5Ac and CH (Fig. 4A). However, in the microcapsules, the C-H absorption peaks appeared as double peaks (Fig. 4B), suggesting that the oil phase was encapsulated by polysaccharides. Nevertheless, this spectral change alone cannot demonstrate the formation of covalent bonds between them (Bao et al., 2024). Three weak peaks observed at 955-1190 cm−1 were assigned to the C-O-C structures in the pyranose rings of Neu5Ac and CH (Fig. 4A and B). The peak intensity of this group decreased, but it was still clearly visible in the microcapsules, indicating that the 3D network of the wall material retained the intact glycosidic backbone after spray drying. The broad peaks in the range of 3025-3680 cm−1 resulted from the overlapping contributions of O-H stretching vibrations and hydrogen-bond network vibrations (Fig. 4A and B). Compared to pure Neu5Ac-CH, the peak slightly shifted to lower wavenumbers, and the half-width increased (Fig. 4A and B). This phenomenon suggested that the introduction or exposure of hydrophobic chains may disrupt the original intermolecular hydrogen-bonding network of polysaccharides and promote their adsorption and rearrangement at the oil-water interface. The resulting interfacial layer, reinforced by hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and steric effects, can enhance interfacial film rigidity and thereby reduce droplet coalescence during storage (Dickinson, 2018; Sweedman et al., 2013; T. Xu et al., 2023). The weak peak at 1645 cm−1 was assigned to H-O-H bending vibrations of bound water (Fig. 4B). Its intensity increased with the total uronic acid content in the wall materials, indicating that this peak may serve as a rapid indicator of the binding capacity of hydrophilic microdomains within the microcapsules. In summary, FT-IR spectra showed no new absorption peak or significant peak shift, confirming that maize oil was physically embedded within the Neu5Ac-CH matrix. The rearrangement of hydrogen bonds and retention of the ester carbonyl peak further indicated that the microcapsules possessed a complete oil-phase encapsulation structure and potential oxidative barrier function (Xie et al., 2024).Fig. 4The properties of microcapsules. XRD patterns analysis

In the XRD pattern of Neu5Ac-CH, the diffraction peak at 2θ = 11.62° corresponded to Neu5Ac, while the peak at 2θ = 20.30° was consistent with CH (Fig. 4C). The diffraction peaks for S1, S2, S3, and S4 were observed at 2θ = 18.90°, 18.95°, 18.80°, and 18.82°, respectively (Fig. 4D). No new crystalline phase or distinct diffraction peaks inherited from Neu5Ac, CH, or Neu5Ac-CH were detected, suggesting that the core material was effectively encapsulated and predominantly present in an amorphous or molecularly dispersed state within the wall-material matrix, rather than existing as crystalline aggregates (Ding et al., 2022), which was consistent with the results obtained from FT-IR analysis.

Thermogravimetry analysis

The TG curves of S1-S4 can be divided into three stages. The first stage (25-200 °C, weight loss: 5.24%) corresponded to the evaporation of moisture and the degradation of SSOS and SC (Lai et al., 2021; C. Sun and Zhong, 2022). The second stage (200-320 °C, weight loss: 37.16%) was attributed to the decomposition of PE, β-CD, and CMC in the wall materials (Einhorn-Stoll et al., 2007; Priya et al., 2019; Trotta et al., 2000). The third stage (320-520 °C, weight loss: 40.7%) involved the decomposition of β-CD (Trotta et al., 2000), maize oil and the Neu5Ac-CH complex (Fig. 4E). The distinct differences between the TG profiles of S1-S4 and those of Neu5Ac-CH and maize oil indicated that microencapsulation enhanced the thermal stability of Neu5Ac (Fig. 4E).

Release behavior of the microcapsules

The in vitro release behavior of active ingredients in microcapsules serves as a crucial indicator for evaluating their digestive properties and functional modulation capacity. Due to the encapsulation of Neu5Ac within the oil phase and wall materials, its release in both SSF and SGF was minimal, but substantial in SIF, with the release rate exceeding 85.0% (Fig. 5A). To investigate the release process of Neu5Ac in SIF, the simulated intestinal digestion was monitored, and the results were presented in Fig. 5B and C. The release rate of FFAs in SIF rapidly increased within the first 20 min and then gradually slowed down, and S2 exhibited a lower FFAs release rate than S1, S3, and S4. This kinetic feature was highly consistent with the findings of Kulandasamy et al. (2023). This phenomenon can be attributed to the microcapsules prepared by spray drying, which have smaller oil droplet sizes and higher specific surface areas, providing abundant binding sites for lipase and significantly accelerating the initiation phase of lipolysis (Khan et al., 2024). At the end of digestion, the FFAs release rate of S1, S2, S3 and S4 were 72.62%, 67.59%, 70.37% and 70.09%, respectively, with no significant difference (Fig. 5B). The release behavior of Neu5Ac in SIF was similar to that of FFAs, and the release rates of Neu5Ac in SSF were 88.01%, 85.61%, 88.24% and 90.26%, respectively (Fig. 5C). Notably, the release of Neu5Ac exhibited a significant delay compared to that of FFAs. Taking S2 as an example, the release of FFAs began to plateau after 40 min of SIF treatment, whereas the release of Neu5Ac started to plateau after 60 min of SIF treatment (Fig. 5B and C). SC could slow down the release of FFAs and Neu5Ac, due to the disordered agglomeration of SC affected by low pH of SGF, wrapping the oil and the inner aqueous phase, preventing the enzymatic hydrolysis of oil phase, thus reducing the release of Neu5Ac (Binsi et al., 2017). To further evaluate the controlled-release properties of microencapsulation, the levels of Neu5Ac in the mouse small intestine and cecum were quantified following oral administration of free Neu5Ac or Neu5Ac-loaded microcapsules (Fig. 5D and E). In the small intestine, Neu5Ac concentrations began to rise 2 h post-administration, with microcapsules enhancing levels relative to Neu5Ac. Notably, S2 exhibited the most pronounced effect, achieving a 2.35-fold increase over the Con group (Fig. 5D). In the cecum, Neu5Ac levels started to increase 4 h post-administration, and microcapsules significantly elevated concentrations compared with free Neu5Ac, with S2 again showing the greatest enhancement (1.74-fold vs. Con group; Fig. 5E). These findings demonstrated that microencapsulation effectively protects Neu5Ac, enabling its passage through the acidic gastric environment to the small intestine and cecum. Collectively, the microcapsules developed herein present considerable potential for improving Neu5Ac bioavailability and supporting intestinal health.Fig. 5The release behavior of Neu5Ac. Effects of microcapsules on gut microbiota

The gut microbiota plays a pivotal role in intestinal health; concurrently, Neu5Ac demonstrates close links to both the intestinal flora and the integrity of the gut barrier (Yao et al., 2022). In this study, S2, which demonstrated optimal EE and sustained-release performance, was selected for subsequent experiments to investigate the beneficial effects of microencapsulated Neu5Ac on the gut microbiota (Fig. 6A). PCoA analysis revealed distinct separations among the healthy mice (Con), Neu5Ac, and S2 groups (Fig. 6B and C). Although neither Neu5Ac nor S2 significantly affected the Shannon index of the gut microbiota, both increased the Chao1 index (Fig. 6D and E). Additionally, taxonomic analysis results were shown in Fig. 6F–H. At the phylum level, Neu5Ac and S2 elevated the relative abundances of Actinobacteria, Deferribacteres, and Verrucomicrobiota; at the genus level, they enhanced the relative abundances of beneficial bacteria such as Akkermansia, Lactobacillus, and Bifidobacterium. To further elucidate the beneficial effects of microencapsulated Neu5Ac on the gut microbiota, LEfSe analysis was performed on the differential microbial taxa. The results indicated that, compared with the Con group, administration of S2 significantly increased the relative abundances of Akkermansia, Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, Bacteroides, Ligilactobacillus, and Turicibacter. Notably, the most pronounced change was observed for Akkermansia, which exhibited the highest LDA score of 4.76 (Fig. 6I). These changes improved gut microbiota composition, which may help regulate host metabolism and maintain intestinal microecological balance and barrier function (Sanders et al., 2019). Notably, compared to Neu5Ac, S2 exhibited a superior ability in promoting the enrichment of these favorable taxa. Although previous studies have shown that Neu5Ac can modulate the gut microbiota (Y. Wang et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2026), the potential contribution of the wall materials in S2 cannot be ignored. Considering the controlled-release behavior of S2 in the mouse intestine (Fig. 5D and E), its superior modulatory effect may be attributed to the targeted delivery and sustained intestinal release of microencapsulated Neu5Ac, which may have promoted the colonization and proliferation of beneficial bacteria and thereby improved gut microbiota composition (Wang et al., 2025). Furthermore, studies indicated that a deficiency of Neu5Ac in the intestine can lead to compromised mucosal barrier integrity, microbial dysbiosis, and increased susceptibility to intestinal inflammation (Yao et al., 2022). Conclusion

This study successfully demonstrated the use of W1/O/W2 emulsions to load Neu5Ac to prepare microcapsules, aiming to enhance its stability and sustained release performance. The W1/O/W2 emulsions formulated with wall materials comprising 13% SSOS, 5% β-CD, 1.5% SC, and 0.5% CMC, and emulsifiers comprising 1.5% PGFE and 1.5% LE, showed excellent stability. This was attributed to the weak gel network formed by the wall materials, which inhibited emulsion destabilization. The optimized emulsion had the smallest d3,2 value of 1.337 μm, the largest specific surface area of 4.983 m2/g, a zeta potential of −60.80 mV, and an EE of 92.44%. The microcapsules obtained by spray drying W1/O/W2 emulsions showed well-defined morphology and high encapsulation efficiency (89.43%). The in vitro simulated digestion experiment showed that the microcapsules were able to protect Neu5Ac from degradation in the oral and gastric environments, and facilitated its sustained release during the initial 80 min of intestinal digestion. By 120 min of simulated intestinal digestion, the release rate of Neu5Ac reached 85.61%. These results demonstrated sustained release effect for Neu5Ac, highlighting their potential for controlled release applications in food and pharmaceuticals. Furthermore, the study found that the interaction between SC and CMC positively influenced the encapsulation performance, improving both EE and the sustained release behavior. Building on this, the investigation was extended to assess the impact of microencapsulated Neu5Ac on the gut microbiota, which demonstrated that it facilitated the colonization and enriched the abundance of Akkermansia, Lactobacillus, and Bifidobacterium, thereby contributing to improved intestinal health. These findings suggested that the W1/O/W2 emulsions and microcapsules developed in this study exhibited significant potential for the loading and controlled release of Neu5Ac, thereby offering a feasible approach for its intestinal-targeted delivery and application in functional foods.
