Beyond
Direct Two-Electron Oxidation: High-Potential
Formaldehyde Oxidation on Palladium Still Generates Hydrogen Atoms

Ruizhi Li, Yufei Li, Xiaosong Hu, Tao Gao, Yujie Sun

Abstract

Electrochemical oxidation
is typically regarded as incompatible
with hydrogen intermediate formation, particularly at highly positive
potentials where surface hydrogen species are expected to be rapidly
oxidized. Formaldehyde oxidation reaction (FOR) on palladium represents
a prominent example: low-potential FOR has been associated with hydrogen
generation, whereas high-potential FOR is commonly interpreted as
a direct two-electron oxidation to formate that bypasses hydrogen
intermediates. Here we challenge this conventional view using a Janus
palladium membrane electrode (J-Pdm) that spatially decouples
hydrogen generation, absorption, permeation, and detection. By monitoring
the hydrogen oxidation reaction (HOR) current on the electrochemically
isolated detection side of J-Pdm, we directly quantify
hydrogen atoms generated during FOR under alkaline conditions. Unexpectedly,
permeated hydrogen remains detectable over a broad FOR potential window
up to 1.5 V vs RHE (reversible hydrogen electrode), demonstrating
that high-potential FOR on Pd is not a hydrogen-free oxidation process.
Coulometric measurements confirm hydrogen uptake within the Pd lattice,
with H/Pd ratios reaching up to 0.57. Spectroscopic and computational
results support a sequential hydrogen-generating oxidation mechanism
in which formaldehyde-derived intermediates undergo favorable C–H
activation, while the resulting hydrogen atoms compete among absorption
into the Pd lattice, recombination to H2, and oxidation
to protons. FOR-generated hydrogen atoms can be utilized for styrene
hydrogenation in an organic phase, establishing anodic hydrogenation
as a viable electrosynthetic strategy. These findings provide new
mechanistic understanding of formaldehyde oxidation on palladium and
also demonstrate J-Pdm as a powerful platform for detecting
and utilizing transient hydrogen intermediates in anodic reactions.

Introduction

Hydrogen (H2) is a central
chemical feedstock and a
promising green energy carrier because it can store renewable energy
and release energy without CO2 emission. At present, however, most industrial H2 is still
produced from fossil resources, motivating the development of more
sustainable routes driven by renewable energy. Electrocatalytic water splitting offers an appealing pathway for
green H2 production under mild conditions, yet its broad
implementation remains limited by the large voltage input required
for the overall reaction.
−

 A major source of this inefficiency
is the anodic O2 evolution reaction (OER), which is kinetically
sluggish and produces O2 as a low-value product.
−

 To address this limitation, extensive efforts have been devoted
to replacing OER with thermodynamically more favorable oxidation reactions,
such as the oxidative valorization of biomass-derived molecules,
−

 alcohols,
−

 aldehydes,
,
 amines,
−

 urea,
−

 and other small molecules. Nevertheless, in most cases H2 is produced only at the
cathode, while the anodic process primarily serves as an energy-saving
or value-generating counter reaction. From the perspective of maximizing
hydrogen output and electron economy, a more attractive strategy would
be to produce H2 from both electrodes while simultaneously
realizing a useful anodic oxidation reaction. Formaldehyde oxidation
reaction (FOR) is particularly appealing in this context. In alkaline
media, formaldehyde can be partially oxidized to formate, a value-added
C1 product, while releasing hydrogen equivalents at an
exceptionally low thermodynamic potential (a). Prior works have shown that coupling
FOR with HER can enable dual H2 production from both the
anode and cathode at much lower voltage than conventional water electrolysis.
−

These advances highlight FOR as
an attractive anodic alternative
to OER and suggest that formaldehyde may serve not only as an oxidizable
substrate but also as a source of reactive hydrogen species. Palladium
membrane electrodes offer a unique opportunity to interrogate and
utilize such hydrogen species. Owing to the exceptional ability of
Pd to absorb and transport atomic hydrogen, hydrogen atoms generated
on one side of a Pd membrane can enter the Pd lattice, diffuse through
the metal, and emerge on the opposite side. In our recent work, we exploited this property to demonstrate electrocatalytic
dual hydrogenation, in which hydrogen atoms generated from low-potential
FOR on a Pd membrane anode permeated through the membrane and drove
organic hydrogenation in an electrochemically isolated chemical chamber.

Despite this promise, a fundamental mechanistic
question remains
unresolved: does FOR on Pd actually generate hydrogen atoms under
anodic conditions, especially at highly positive potential? Many studies
of alkaline formaldehyde oxidation proposed that methylene glycolate
dehydrogenation produces adsorbed hydrogen atoms, which can either
recombine to form H2 (a) or undergo electrochemical oxidation to
protons.
−

 The classical works of van den Meerakker and Bindra concluded that on Group
VIII metals such as Pt and Pd, formaldehyde oxidation proceeds without
simultaneous H2 evolution because adsorbed hydrogen is
rapidly oxidized. This conclusion has strongly influenced the conventional
view that Pd, as an excellent hydrogen oxidation reaction (HOR) electrocatalyst,
should not permit detectable hydrogen generation during anodic FOR.
Moreover, high-potential FOR has often been interpreted as a direct
two-electron oxidation process to formate that bypasses hydrogen intermediate
formation altogether (b). This interpretation is largely
based on the absence of gaseous H2 as a product, rather
than on direct detection of transient hydrogen atoms. As a result,
it has remained unclear whether hydrogen atoms are truly absent at
high anodic potentials during FOR (one direct 2e–-oxidation step) or whether they are formed but consumed too rapidly
through HOR (two consecutive 1e–-oxidation steps).

Herein, we address this long-standing ambiguity using a Janus palladium
membrane electrode (J-Pdm) specifically designed to decouple
hydrogen generation, hydrogen absorption, hydrogen permeation, and
hydrogen oxidation. As shown in (c), in this architecture, FOR occurs on one
side of J-Pdm under alkaline anodic conditions, while the
opposite side of J-Pdm is electrochemically isolated and
configured to detect permeated hydrogen atoms through HOR current.
Because only hydrogen atoms absorbed into and transported through
J-Pdm can contribute to the HOR signal on the detection
side, this platform provides an unambiguous electrochemical readout
of hydrogen atom generation during FOR. Unlike conventional gas-collection
measurements, which can miss hydrogen atoms that are rapidly oxidized
at the FOR interface, our J-Pdm platform directly probes
the competing pathway in which hydrogen atoms enter the Pd lattice
before interfacial oxidation. Using this design, we demonstrate that
formaldehyde oxidation on Pd indeed generates hydrogen atoms under
alkaline conditions, even at fairly positive anodic potential up to
1.5 V vs RHE (reversible hydrogen electrode). These results show that
high-potential FOR on Pd is not simply a hydrogen-free, direct 2e–-oxidation process. Instead, hydrogen-generating elementary
steps persist over a broad anodic potential range of FOR, while the
fate of the generated hydrogen atoms is governed by competition among
(i) recombination to H2, (ii) electrochemical oxidation
on Pd (i.e., HOR), and (iii) absorption/permeation through the Pd
membrane. Remarkably, hydrogen absorption into Pd can remain kinetically
competitive with HOR even though Pd is widely recognized as an efficient
HOR electrocatalyst. By providing direct, quantitative electrochemical
evidence for permeated hydrogen atoms generated from anodic FOR, together
with theoretical computation, this work establishes J-Pdm as a powerful platform for resolving transient hydrogen intermediates
in oxidation reactions and opens new opportunities for coupling value-added
anodic oxidation with hydrogen utilization chemistry.

Results and Discussion

Control electrochemical studies were first conducted to identify
suitable conditions for subsequent J-Pdm experiments. Formaldehyde
oxidation was negligible in either acidic, or neutral electrolytes,
or on bare glassy carbon (Figure S1), whereas
Pd in alkaline electrolyte showed pronounced FOR activity, confirming
that both Pd and alkaline conditions are required. Increasing KOH
concentration enhanced FOR current (Figure S2), but high alkalinity also accelerated spontaneous Cannizzaro disproportionation
of formaldehyde (Figures S3–S8).
Therefore, 1 M KOH was selected to balance catalytic activity and
substrate stability. The HCHO concentration was then optimized in
1 M KOH (Figure S9), where increasing HCHO
from 1 mM to 0.5 M improved the oxidation current, while 1.0 M HCHO
led to decreased activity and more severe background formate formation
(Figures S10–S13). Based on these
combined studies, 0.5 M HCHO in 1 M KOH was selected as the standard
FOR electrolyte for subsequent J-Pdm studies.

With
the optimized FOR conditions established, we next constructed
the J-Pdm platform illustrated in c to directly probe hydrogen intermediates
generated during anodic formaldehyde oxidation. In this architecture,
a thin Pd membrane electrode (0.025 mm) simultaneously serves as both
the working electrode and the hydrogen-selective transport medium
separating two electrochemically isolated compartments. The HCHO-containing
side functions as the FOR chamber, where formaldehyde oxidation can
proceed through competing pathways, including direct two-electron
oxidation to formate (step 1 in c), hydrogen-generating one-electron oxidation
(step 2), hydrogen recombination to H2 (step 3),
local electrochemical oxidation of surface-adsorbed H* (step 4),
and hydrogen absorption into and permeation through J-Pdm (step 5). The opposite side of J-Pdm serves as
the HOR chamber, where permeated hydrogen atoms are oxidized under
independently controlled anodic polarization (step 6). Therefore,
any anodic current detected in this HOR chamber must originate from
hydrogen atoms generated from formaldehyde oxidation in the FOR chamber.
To improve FOR activity while maintaining efficient hydrogen transport,
the FOR-facing side of J-Pdm was electrodeposited with
Pd nanoparticles. As compared in Figure S14, moderate Pd deposition enhanced FOR current, whereas excessive
deposition decreased activity, likely due to reduced mass transport
and/or impeded hydrogen accessibility through the membrane. Accordingly,
a deposition charge of 1 C from Pd2+ precursor was adopted
for all the J-Pdm electrodes.

We then optimized the
operating potential in the HOR chamber to
ensure efficient oxidation of hydrogen atoms permeating from the FOR
chamber. For these experiments, the FOR chamber was operated galvanostatically
at constant anodic current densities of 5, 10, 20, 30, and 40 mA cm–2, while the HOR chamber contained 0.5 M H2SO4 and the HOR-facing side of J-Pdm was biased
at different potentials from 0.5 to 1.5 V vs RHE with an interval
of 0.2 V for 20 min. As shown in a–e, the detected HOR current density
generally increased as the applied HOR potential became more positive,
confirming that higher anodic bias more effectively oxidizes permeated
hydrogen species. At relatively low FOR current densities of 5 and
10 mA cm–2, the HOR responses at 1.1, 1.3, and 1.5
V vs RHE were comparable, suggesting that hydrogen oxidation was sufficiently
fast under these conditions and that the measured current became primarily
limited by the rate of hydrogen generation and permeation through
the Pd membrane. For example, when 10 mA cm–2 was
applied in the FOR chamber, the HOR current reached approximately
8 mA cm–2 at high HOR potentials, indicating that
a large fraction of the FOR-generated hydrogen flux could be captured
and oxidized on the detection side. In contrast, at higher FOR current
densities of 20, 30, and 40 mA cm–2, the dependence
of the HOR response on the applied detection potential became more
pronounced. Under these larger hydrogen-flux conditions, lower HOR
potentials were insufficient to fully oxidize the permeated hydrogen,
whereas 1.5 V vs RHE consistently produced the highest HOR current
density. This trend indicates that, as the rate of hydrogen generation
and loading in the FOR chamber increases, a stronger anodic driving
force is required in the HOR chamber to effectively oxidize those
permeated hydrogen atoms.

The maximum HOR current densities obtained upon the application
of different FOR current densities are summarized in f. The HOR current increased
monotonically with increasing applied FOR current density, reaching
approximately 26 mA cm–2 when the FOR chamber was
operated at 40 mA cm–2. Importantly, sustaining
this FOR current requires a highly positive anodic potential of approximately
1.4 V vs RHE in the FOR chamber, as shown by the corresponding chronopotentiometric
measurement in Figure S15. This observation
indicates that FOR-derived hydrogen capable of permeating through
the Pd membrane is still generated even at strongly anodic FOR potentials,
which will be further discussed in the subsequent paragraphs.

To further validate the origin of the detected HOR signal, control
experiments were performed under the selected FOR/HOR operating potentials
(Figure S16). Negligible current was observed
in either chamber in the absence of HCHO, whereas the addition of
0.5 M HCHO immediately initiated FOR and was also accompanied by the
development of a substantial HOR current at the opposite Pd surface.
This direct temporal correlation between HCHO addition, FOR, and the
subsequent HOR response, together with the absence of measurable HOR
current prior to HCHO addition, rules out an appreciable contribution
from background electrochemical processes associated with the Pd membrane
or pre-existing hydrogen stored within the membrane. These results
therefore further confirm that the detected HOR current originates
from hydrogen generated during FOR and subsequently transported through
the Pd membrane.

Having established the appropriate HOR-side
potential and verified
the FOR-dependent origin of the detected HOR current using our J-Pdm platform, we next employed this strategy to investigate how
the generation and permeation of FOR-derived hydrogen intermediates
evolve as a function of the applied FOR potential. Since OER becomes
appreciable on Pd electrodes above ca. 1.6 V vs RHE in 1 M KOH (Figure S17), the FOR-side potential was systematically
varied from 0.3 to 1.5 V vs RHE with an interval of 0.2 V. In these
experiments, the FOR chamber was operated potentiostatically in 1.0
M KOH containing 0.5 M HCHO, while the opposite HOR chamber was held
at 1.5 V vs RHE to promote efficient oxidation of hydrogen atoms permeating
through the Pd membrane. To assess whether the detected hydrogen flux
was affected by the electrolyte environment and HOR kinetics on the
detection side, the HOR chamber was independently tested using 0.5
M H2SO4, 1.0 M KPi, and 1.0 M KOH as the electrolytes.

As shown in a–c, increasing the applied FOR potential initially increased
both the FOR current density and the corresponding HOR current density,
indicating that the flux of FOR-derived hydrogen entering and permeating
through the Pd membrane initially increases with the anodic driving
force. In 0.5 M H2SO4, for example, the HOR
current density increased from ca. 4 mA cm–2 at
an FOR potential of 0.3 V vs RHE to a maximum of ca. 17 mA cm–2 when the FOR potential increased to 0.7 V vs RHE
(a). Upon further
increasing the applied FOR potential, however, the detected HOR current
gradually decreased. Importantly, measurable HOR currents were still
observed at FOR potentials above 1.0 V, including ca. 8.8 mA cm–2 at 1.3 V and ca. 2 mA cm–2 at 1.5
V, indicating that FOR-derived hydrogen remains detectable even at
highly positive anodic potentials.

The dynamic coupling between FOR and hydrogen permeation
was further
examined by periodically switching FOR on and off (d). Specifically, FOR was maintained
for 10 min and then switched off for 10 min, with the on/off operation
repeated for six consecutive cycles. Upon initiation of FOR, the HOR
current developed at the opposite side of the Pd membrane, whereas
interruption of FOR caused the HOR current to rapidly return to the
baseline. This response was reproducibly recovered over multiple consecutive
on/off cycles without an apparent progressive loss, demonstrating
a reversible temporal correlation between FOR-derived hydrogen generation
and its permeation through the Pd membrane. The structural stability
of the Pd membrane under the reaction conditions was further evaluated
by XRD (Figures S18 and S19) and SEM/EDS
(Figures S20–S24) before and after
electrolysis. The characteristic diffraction peaks of metallic Pd
were retained after electrolysis without the appearance of a detectable
crystalline palladium oxide phase, although minor changes in surface
morphology and an increase in the oxygen content were observed. These
results indicate that the Pd membrane largely retains its metallic
crystalline structure, with only limited surface compositional and
morphological changes during electrolysis. Together with the negligible
background current observed in the absence of HCHO (Figure S16) and the reproducible FOR/HOR response (d), these observations
collectively indicate that sustained or irreversible oxidation of
the Pd membrane does not make an appreciable contribution to the detected
HOR current under the present operating conditions.

With these
results in hand, we next considered the origin of the
decreasing HOR response at higher FOR potentials. This decline suggests
that, although hydrogen intermediates continue to be generated during
FOR, a smaller fraction of the generated hydrogen is ultimately transported
through the Pd membrane and detected at the HOR-facing interface.
Several competing pathways may account for this decreased fraction
of hydrogen detected by HOR. Specifically, on the FOR-facing side
of J-Pdm, the generated H* can either enter the Pd lattice
or recombine to form H2 before permeation. In addition,
at increasingly positive FOR potentials, surface H* can undergo local
direct electrochemical oxidation (H* + OH– →
H2O + e–). Therefore, not all hydrogen
intermediates generated during FOR are necessarily transported through
the Pd membrane and detected as HOR current. Consequently, the detected
HOR current only represents the fraction of FOR-derived hydrogen that
is successfully transported through the Pd membrane and electrochemically
captured at the HOR-facing side, rather than the total amount of hydrogen
intermediates generated during FOR. Accordingly, the decrease in HOR
current at higher FOR potentials does not necessarily indicate the
disappearance of hydrogen-intermediate formation but rather reflects
increasing competition between hydrogen permeation and alternative
hydrogen-consumption pathways.

Similar potential-dependent trends
were obtained when neutral KPi
or alkaline KOH was used in the HOR chamber (b,c). In fact, the maximum HOR current densities
showed only modest electrolyte-dependent variation (e). These results demonstrate
that high-potential FOR on Pd is not a purely hydrogen-free, direct
two-electron oxidation process. Instead, hydrogen-generating steps
persist across a broad anodic potential window (up to 1.5 V vs RHE),
while the fate of the generated hydrogen progressively shifts from
Pd-lattice absorption/permeation toward local electrochemical oxidation
at the FOR interface.

Next, we sought to quantify how much of
the hydrogen atoms generated
from FOR could be stored within the Pd lattice under anodic polarization.
The hydrogen amount in J-Pdm was expressed as the H/Pd
ratio and determined using a coulometric discharge approach.
−

 In these measurements, constant-current FOR electrolysis was conducted
at 10, 20, 30, and 40 mA cm–2 until the Pd membrane
reached a steady hydrogen-loaded state, as indicated by the plateaued
oxidation current in the HOR chamber (Figure S25a). The FOR electrolysis was then stopped, and the HOR side was biased
at 1.5 V vs RHE in 0.5 M H2SO4, a condition
identified above to efficiently oxidize permeated hydrogen species.
Under this anodic discharge condition, hydrogen stored within the
Pd lattice was oxidized and depleted from J-Pdm (Figure S25b), and the corresponding oxidation
charge was integrated to determine the amount of absorbed hydrogen
(Figure S25c). As summarized in a, appreciable hydrogen
loading was achieved in J-Pdm under anodic FOR conditions.
The calculated H/Pd ratios increased from 0.40 at an applied FOR current
density of 10 mA cm–2 to 0.49, 0.50, and 0.51 at
20, 30, and 40 mA cm–2, respectively. This trend
indicates that increasing the rate of FOR-driven hydrogen generation
promotes hydrogen uptake into the Pd lattice, although the H/Pd ratio
begins to level off at higher current densities. To further evaluate
whether the discharge electrolyte influenced the measured hydrogen
content, analogous measurements were performed using 1.0 M KPi and
1.0 M KOH as the electrolyte of the HOR chamber while maintaining
the FOR current density at 40 mA cm–2. Comparable
H/Pd ratios were obtained in these electrolytes as well, reaching
0.574 in 1.0 M KPi and 0.567 in 1.0 M KOH (b). These results confirm that the substantial
hydrogen loading stored in J-Pdm is not limited to acidic
HOR detection conditions but instead reflects genuine hydrogen absorption
into the Pd lattice during FOR. To the best of our knowledge, it represents
the first quantitative determination of hydrogen content in Pd generated
under anodic polarization (i.e., FOR). It should be noted that the
measured H/Pd ratios are lower than those typically obtained during
cathodic hydrogen loading from water reduction on Pd, which is not
surprising given the different hydrogen-generation environment. Under
cathodic conditions, hydrogen is continuously supplied directly through
water reduction at the Pd electrode surface. In contrast, under anodic
FOR conditions, hydrogen formation/absorption must compete against
its local electrochemical oxidation to protons at the J-Pdm interface. Nevertheless, the ability to achieve H/Pd ratios beyond
0.57 under anodic FOR conditions demonstrates that a significant fraction
of FOR-generated hydrogen can enter and accumulate within the Pd lattice,
providing a quantitative basis for the hydrogen permeation and downstream
hydrogenation chemistry described below.

To determine whether those hydrogen atoms generated
from FOR could
be directly utilized for synthetic hydrogenation in an organic phase,
we selected styrene hydrogenation in methanol as a model reaction.
Styrene was chosen because it is poorly soluble in water and therefore
represents a typical hydrophobic organic substrate. In addition, its
hydrogenation produces ethylbenzene as the sole hydrogenation product,
allowing straightforward quantification of conversion and hence Faradaic
efficiency (FE).
,
 Meanwhile, a deposition charge
of 5 C from Pd2+ precursor was adopted for the Pd that
faces the chemical chamber to enhance the substrate adsorption. As
shown in a,
a three-compartment electrochemical palladium membrane reactor was
employed for this study. The FOR chamber was charged with 1.0 M KOH
and 0.5 M HCHO, while the chemical chamber contained 50 mM styrene
in methanol. Constant-current FOR electrolysis was performed at current
densities ranging from 5 to 40 mA cm–2 to drive
hydrogen generation, permeation through the Pd membrane, and final
utilization in styrene hydrogenation in the chemical chamber. Each
reaction was terminated after passing the theoretical amount of charge
required for complete styrene hydrogenation. As plotted in b and S26, styrene hydrogenation proceeded successfully
under all investigated FOR current densities, confirming that hydrogen
generated during anodic formaldehyde oxidation remains chemically
accessible for downstream hydrogenation in an organic phase. At an
applied FOR current density of 5 mA cm–2, more than
43 mM ethylbenzene was produced, corresponding to a Faradaic efficiency
(FE) of 86%. To further explore whether the permeated hydrogen atoms
are directly responsible for styrene hydrogenation, an additional
control experiment was performed by applying 0 V vs Fc+/0 (Fc: ferrocene) to the chemical chamber while maintaining FOR at
5 mA cm–2. The applied potential was calibrated
against the Fc+/0 redox couple (Figure S27). Under these conditions, time-dependent product analysis
revealed substantially suppressed styrene hydrogenation, with only
∼6% ethylbenzene formed after 8 h (Figure S28). Meanwhile, a sustained anodic current was recorded at
the chemical-facing Pd surface throughout the 8 h electrolysis (Figure S29), indicating continuous electrochemical
consumption of the permeated hydrogen under applied anodic polarization.
We therefore attribute the suppressed hydrogenation to competition
between electrochemical oxidation of the permeated hydrogen and its
chemical utilization for styrene hydrogenation. In contrast, under
open-circuit conditions, the permeated hydrogen remains available
at the chemical-facing Pd surface for the hydrogenation of styrene
to ethylbenzene.

As the applied FOR current density increased, both
ethylbenzene
production and FE gradually decreased. This trend is consistent with
the mechanistic results described above: at higher anodic current
densities and more positive FOR potentials, a larger fraction of the
generated hydrogen intermediates is consumed by local electrochemical
oxidation at the FOR interface before it can enter and permeate through
the Pd lattice. Nevertheless, the observation of ethylbenzene formation
even at elevated FOR current densities confirms that FOR-generated
hydrogen can be transported across J-Pdm and used for organic
hydrogenation under anodic operating conditions.

To further
probe the surface species formed during FOR and provide
spectroscopic support for the hydrogen-involved oxidation pathway,
potential-dependent in situ FTIR spectroscopy was performed under
different conditions. Spectra were collected from open-circuit conditions
to 1.5 V vs RHE (). In 1.0 M KOH without HCHO, only the characteristic vibrational
features associated with interfacial or adsorbed water were observed
(Figure S30).
,
 In contrast, upon the addition of 0.5 M HCHO, two characteristic
bands at ca. 1589 and 1346 cm–1 increased progressively
with increasing potential (a). These bands are assigned to the asymmetric and
symmetric OCO stretching vibrations, respectively, of formate-related
species, indicating the progressive formation of formate during FOR
as the applied potential becomes more positive.
−

 Importantly, a weak feature near
ca. 1904 cm–1 emerged at higher anodic potentials.
This band is tentatively assigned to a Pd–H stretching vibration,
suggesting the formation of a hydrogen-involved surface state during
FOR.
−

 Although this assignment should be treated cautiously because of
the weak intensity of the band, its appearance under anodic FOR conditions
is consistent with the electrochemical detection of permeated hydrogen
and the coulometric evidence for hydrogen loading in J-Pdm. To further examine this assignment, isotope-labeling experiments
were performed using deuterated formaldehyde (Figure S31). With 0.5 M DCDO, the formate-related bands shifted
to ca. 1572 and 1322 cm–1, respectively, while the weak feature at ca. 1904 cm–1 was no longer resolved as a distinct band. This behavior is consistent
with the formation of Pd–D species upon H/D substitution, with
the corresponding Pd–D stretching vibration expected in the
ca. 1340 cm–1 region based on the reported isotope
shifts of Pd–H/Pd–D vibrations. The resulting Pd–D vibration would therefore substantially
overlap with the DCOO-related band at ca. 1322 cm–1, making it difficult to resolve as an independent feature. Direct
comparison of the spectra collected at 0.7 V vs RHE further supports
this interpretation, showing the loss of the distinct ca. 1904 cm–1 feature together with the isotope-induced shifts
of the formate-related bands (b). Together, these in situ FTIR results support the
conclusion that high-potential FOR involves both formate formation
and hydrogen-associated surface intermediates, rather than proceeding
exclusively through a hydrogen-free direct two-electron oxidation
pathway.

To further support the above experimental results, density functional
theory (DFT) calculations were performed using Pd(111) and hydrogen-enriched
PdH0.6(111) surfaces as model catalysts (Figure S32). Pd(111) was selected as the representative surface
because the XRD pattern of the Pd membrane is dominated by the (111)
reflection, and Pd(111) is also the most thermodynamically stable
low-index facet of fcc Pd that has been widely used in theoretical
studies of hydrogen adsorption and hydride formation on palladium. For the Pd(111) slab, the bottom two atomic
layers were fixed to represent the bulk lattice, whereas the upper
two layers were fully relaxed. To model hydrogen-enriched palladium
under FOR conditions, a nonstoichiometric PdH0.6(111) surface
was constructed from the Pd(111) slab. Because palladium hydrides
are characterized by disordered hydrogen distributions, a combined
random-loading and ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) approach was
used and the detailed description of DFT computations can be found
in the Supporting Information.

We
first examined the energetics of C–H bond activation,
the key hydrogen-generating elementary step during formaldehyde oxidation.
Under neutral conditions, molecular formaldehyde (HCHO) and hydrated
formaldehyde (HCH­(OH)2) were both considered. As presented
in a, HCHO
exhibits an energy barrier of 0.642 eV for the homolytic cleavage
of its C–H bond on Pd, while a much higher energy barrier (1.604
eV) was computed on PdH0.6. b compares the C–H bond activation
profiles of HCH­(OH)2 on Pd versus PdH0.6, both
of which exhibit large energy requirements, 1.500 and 1.604 eV, respectively.
These results indicate that it is less likely to observe hydrogen
atom release from either HCHO or HCH­(OH)2 on Pd under neutral
conditions, in agreement with the experimental results. In contrast,
remarkably different computational results were obtained under alkaline
conditions. As shown in c, the deprotonated methylene glycolate intermediate HCH­(OH)­O– was examined, because this species is expected to
dominate in strongly basic media. The
calculated free-energy profiles show that C–H bond cleavage
is quite favorable for HCH­(OH)­O– on Pd under alkaline
conditions, with an activation barrier of 0.514 eV. In fact, the C–H
bond activation of HCH­(OH)­O– on PdH0.6 was calculated to be spontaneous without an observable barrier.
These results indicate that the alkaline intermediate HCH­(OH)­O– is intrinsically more susceptible to hydrogen-generating
C–H activation. This trend is consistent with the experimental
observation that formaldehyde oxidation on Pd is strongly promoted
under alkaline conditions, whereas activity is negligible in neutral
electrolyte.

We next examined the possible fate of hydrogen
intermediates generated
after the C–H bond cleavage of HCH­(OH)­O– under
alkaline conditions on Pd. Two kinetically competing pathways were
considered: (i) absorption of surface hydrogen into the subsurface
Pd lattice and (ii) recombination of surface hydrogen atoms to evolve
molecular H2. As shown in , hydrogen absorption into the subsurface
region of Pd proceeds with a low activation barrier of 0.225 eV, whereas
H2 formation requires a much higher barrier of 1.668 eV.
These calculations indicate that once hydrogen atoms are generated
during alkaline FOR, their absorption into Pd is kinetically more
favorable than recombinative H2 evolution. This preference
for subsurface hydrogen absorption provides a theoretical basis for
the experimentally observed hydrogen permeation across J-Pdm and the substantial hydrogen loading quantified within the Pd membrane
under anodic FOR conditions ().

Because C–H bond activation of HCH­(OH)­O– was calculated to proceed spontaneously on PdH0.6, we
next evaluated whether the resulting carbon-containing intermediates
could be stabilized on the hydrogen-enriched Pd surface. Figure S33 compares the adsorption strengths
of several representative intermediates such as *HC•OHO– (the barrierless C–H bond cleavage
product of adsorbed HCH­(OH)­O– on PdH0.6), *HCOOH, and *HCOO– on PdH0.6. Among
these species, *HC•OHO– shows
the strongest adsorption stability (−6.209 eV), indicating
a particularly strong interaction between this intermediate and the
hydrogen-rich Pd surface. This result suggests that although PdH0.6 can strongly promote the initial hydrogen-generating C–H
activation step, the resulting strongly bound carbonaceous intermediates
may accumulate on the surface in the absence of an external driving
force. Such surface accumulation would limit sustained spontaneous
formaldehyde conversion. Under electrochemical FOR conditions, however,
anodic polarization can continuously oxidize these adsorbed intermediates
toward more weakly bound products like HCOOH and HCOO–, thereby regenerating active Pd sites and maintaining catalytic
turnover. This interpretation is consistent with the observation that
spontaneous formaldehyde conversion is not sustained over extended
reaction times (Figure S34), whereas electrochemical
FOR proceeds continuously under applied potential.

To further
clarify whether hydrogen-generating oxidation remains
viable under anodic polarization, we investigated the potential-dependent
energetics of HCH­(OH)­O– oxidation on both Pd and
PdH0.6 under alkaline conditions. In this proposed sequential
pathway, HCH­(OH)­O– first undergoes C–H bond
cleavage to generate a surface hydrogen intermediate, followed by
subsequent oxidation of the carbon-containing intermediate toward
formate. As shown in a, the C–H activation barrier on Pd decreases markedly
with increasing applied potential. At U = 0 V, this
step requires an activation barrier of 0.541 eV. When the potential
is increased to 0.3 V vs RHE, the barrier decreases to 0.214 eV; while
at potentials above 0.6 V vs RHE, C–H cleavage becomes nearly
barrierless. This trend indicates that anodic polarization not only
promotes formaldehyde oxidation thermodynamically but also facilitates
the initial hydrogen-generating C–H activation step kinetically.
A similar potential-dependent analysis was then performed on the hydrogen-enriched
PdH0.6 surface. In contrast to metallic Pd, C–H
bond cleavage on PdH0.6 proceeds spontaneously without an observable
activation barrier across the investigated potential range (b), further confirming
that hydrogen-rich Pd surfaces strongly facilitate hydrogen generation
from HCH­(OH)­O–.

We then examined the subsequent oxidation step following C–H
activation. On Pd, this process is thermodynamically uphill at U = 0 V, with a calculated reaction free energy of 0.991
eV. Increasing the applied potential progressively stabilizes the
oxidized products and lowers the energetic requirement associated
with the second oxidation step. Consequently, the free-energy penalty
for converting *HOCHO– + *OH to *HCOO– + H2O decreases markedly with increasing potential, indicating
that the two-electron oxidation pathway becomes increasingly accessible
under anodic conditions. The same potential-promoted trend is observed
on PdH0.6, where the reaction free energy decreases from
0.158 eV at U = 0 V to −0.442 eV at U = 0.3 V vs RHE and becomes increasingly favorable at higher
anodic potentials. Together, these calculations demonstrate that anodic
polarization facilitates both C–H activation and subsequent
oxidation in the sequential FOR pathway. Importantly, the hydrogen-generating
pathway remains energetically accessible over the full potential window
examined.

Conclusions

In summary, this work reveals that formaldehyde
oxidation on palladium
remains a hydrogen-generating process even under highly anodic conditions
where it has conventionally been regarded as a direct, hydrogen-free
two-electron oxidation pathway. By using a Janus palladium membrane
electrode that spatially decouples formaldehyde oxidation from hydrogen
detection, we directly quantified permeated hydrogen atoms generated
during FOR over a broad potential window up to 1.5 V vs RHE. We demonstrate
that high-potential FOR proceeds through hydrogen-involved sequential
oxidation pathways, while the fate of the generated hydrogen atoms
is governed by competition between absorption into J-Pdm and electrochemical oxidation on the FOR side of J-Pdm. Our theoretical calculations further show that alkaline HCH­(OH)­O– undergoes favorable C–H activation on Pd and
that hydrogen absorption into subsurface Pd is kinetically preferred
over hydrogen recombination to yield H2. Potential-dependent
free-energy profiles further demonstrate that anodic polarization
promotes the sequential oxidation pathway without suppressing the
initial hydrogen-generating step. Beyond resolving the mechanistic
ambiguity of FOR, this study establishes J-Pdm as a powerful
platform for probing short-lived hydrogen intermediates during anodic
oxidation reactions. More broadly, the ability to generate, transport,
quantify, and utilize anodically produced hydrogen atoms opens new
opportunities for coupling aldehyde oxidative transformations with
downstream hydrogenation chemistry.
