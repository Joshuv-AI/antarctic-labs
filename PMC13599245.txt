Primary spring ligament repair through a navicular tubercle osteotomy in flexible progressive collapsing foot deformity: results of a case series
Authors: Tulgar Toros, Can Yener, Kubilay Erol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpring ligament complex (SLC) insufficiency is regarded as an important component of progressive collapsing foot deformity (PCFD). Reconstruction of this ligament complex is often favored over primary repair since the latter has not proven successful in chronic injuries. We present the clinical and radiographic outcomes of primary repair in PCFD performed using the navicular osteotomy technique which improves visualization and repair of SLC and compare the results with augmentation and reconstruction series reported in the literature.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 17 PCFD of 16 patients with flexible deformities who underwent lateral column lengthening, distal advancement of the posterior tibial tendon (PTT), gastrocsoleus recession, and primary SLC repair via the navicular osteotomy technique. Clinical (the European Society for Foot and Ankle Surgery (EFAS) daily- living and sports scores) and eight weight-bearing radiographic parameters were compared preoperatively and at final follow-up using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.ResultsMean age of patients was 31.5 years (range, 10–65) and mean follow-up time was 17.6 months (range, 12–29). EFAS daily-living (9.5→20.9) and sports (6.4→14.4) scores improved significantly (p < 0.001). All radiographic parameters also improved significantly (p < 0.001), including antero‑posterior (AP) Meary (19.5°→−1.3°), lateral Meary (25.1°→4.6°), AP talonavicular coverage (31.4°→3.3°) and lateral talonavicular coverage (17.1°→0.8°) angles.ConclusionPrimary SLC repair using the navicular osteotomy technique, combined with lateral column lengthening and PTT advancement restored talonavicular congruity with clinical and radiographic outcomes comparable to those reported for augmentation and reconstruction.Level of evidenceIV, retrospective case series.Keywords: Progressive collapsing foot deformity, Posterior tibial tendon insufficiency, Spring ligament repair, Navicular tubercle osteotomy

Introduction

Adult acquired flatfoot deformity (AAFD), more recently termed progressive collapsing foot deformity (PCFD), comprises a wide spectrum of ligament and tendon failures that result in significant deformity and disability [1]. When the deformity is flexible, surgical correction of PCFD includes a combination of osteotomies and soft tissue procedures aiming to obtain the correct alignment while preserving the mobility of the foot [2]. Traditionally, acquired flatfoot deformity has been attributed to dysfunction of the posterior tibial tendon (PTT), which is regarded as the main dynamic support of the medial longitudinal arch [3, 4]. Recent studies have demonstrated that loss of the integrity of the spring ligament complex (SLC) also plays a critical role in medial arch collapse and progression of the deformity [5]. The SLC is a quadrangular ligament connecting the sustentaculum tali of the calcaneus and the navicular, which constitutes the main ligamentous support of the talonavicular joint [5, 6]. It consists of three described bands, the two main ones being the superomedial calcaneonavicular ligament that supports the talonavicular joint from the medial side and the inferior calcaneonavicular ligament which prevents plantar subluxation of the talonavicular joint [7]. This ligament complex is considered the primary static stabilizer of the medial longitudinal arch of the foot [3] and a major restraint to talonavicular deformity. Moreover, it plays a major role in supplying concentric reduction of the talonavicular joint and is believed to supply kinetic coupling between the hindfoot and the forefoot [8, 9].

There is a growing interest among foot surgeons in addressing the SLC in PCFD surgery in order to obtain proper talonavicular reduction. Recent studies mainly focus on reconstruction rather than repair of the SLC during surgical correction of flexible pes planovalgus, indicating that direct repair has not proven successful [2, 7]. One of the reasons for the propensity towards reconstruction may be related to the deep and confined location of the spring ligament under the terminal portion of the posterior tibial tendon (PTT), its distal extensions and the navicular tubercle, hampering primary repair.

This article presents the clinical and radiographic results of 17 feet of 16 patients with PCFD in which primary repair of the SLC using the navicular osteotomy technique is included in the surgical procedure in order to facilitate visualization and direct repair of both components of the ligament [10]. It also aims to compare the results of primary repair of the SLC with augmented repair and reconstruction techniques presented in the literature. Our hypothesis is that primary repair of the spring ligament complex is comparably effective to augmented repair and reconstruction techniques in achieving talonavicular joint congruency under weight-bearing conditions.

This study was approved by the institutional review board at the authors’ institution. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients involved in this study. For the 2 participants under the age of 16, informed consent was obtained from their parents.

Materials and methods

This is a retrospective study of prospectively collected data on consecutively operated 17 symptomatic PCFDs of 16 patients between November 2013 and April 2025 who did not respond to conservative treatment. In an effort to standardize the patient group, only cases with symptomatic Johnson–Strom/Myerson [11, 12] stage 2B pes planovalgus (PCFD stage 1 A/1B/1 C) with flexible hindfoot valgus, medial longitudinal arch collapse and forefoot abduction detected on clinical evaluation and X-ray images were included.

All patients were operated at the same institution by a single surgeon, using the same set of procedures which included lateral column lengthening as described by Hintermann [13], distal advancement of the PTT insertion, and gastrocsoleus recession. Debridement, re-tensioning and bony fixation of the two major limbs of the attenuated spring ligament were included in the operative procedure.

Clinical evaluation included the European Foot and Ankle Society (EFAS) daily living (ADL) and sports scores taken preoperatively and at the last follow-up [14]. The radiological evaluation comprised measuring eight pre-determined radiological parameters on weight-bearing anteroposterior (AP) and lateral X-rays of the involved feet on preoperative and last post-operative X-rays by three orthopedic surgeons. Measurements were performed on a picture archiving and communication system (PACS) using electronic calipers. All the measurements were performed three consecutive times by each observer, and the average value of each parameter was recorded. The measured parameters included talocalcaneal (Kite) angle, talus-first metatarsal (Meary’s) angle, and talonavicular coverage angle on AP images (Fig. 1a); calcaneal inclination angle (calcaneal pitch), height (in centimeters) of the navicular and medial cuneiform from the ground (the line that connects the lowest part of the medial sesamoid and the lowest part of the calcaneal tubercle), talus-first metatarsal (Meary’s) angle and lateral talonavicular coverage angle [15, 16] (Fig. 1b). The lateral talonavicular coverage angle is defined as the angle between the longitudinal talar axis and a line perpendicular to the navicular joint surface on the lateral radiograph (normal value: 0°; increasing values reflect greater plantar/medial talonavicular subluxation) [17].

Surgical technique

Patients were positioned supine on the operation table with a silicone pad located under the buttock of the symptomatic extremity in order to facilitate access to the lateral side of the foot. This pad may be removed when working on the medial side.

Initially, a longitudinal incision at the plantar medial junction of the foot is created centered over the navicular tubercle. Subdermal soft tissues and superficial fascia covering the navicular tubercle and the terminal portion of the PTT are opened with sharp dissection (Fig. 2a, b). The level of osteotomy is marked with a K-wire and checked with fluoroscopy. Ideally the osteotomy line should lie at the base of the navicular tubercle in order to mobilize a thick segment of bone that includes all the direct attachment site of the PTT [10] (Fig. 2c, d). It also should be large and wide enough to hold at least one, preferably two 2.7 mm screws for fixation. Great care should be exerted to refrain from violating the talonavicular and naviculocuneiform joints and their capsular attachments.

a Medial longitudinal incision exposing the navicular tubercle and the distal portion of the PTT. b The distal portion of the PTT lies over the SLC, hampering direct visualization and repair. c The navicular osteotomy line is located at the base of the tubercle and runs in an oblique plane from dorsomedial to plantar lateral. It should not violate the talonavicular and naviculocuneiform joints and their capsules. d The osteotomized fragment should include all the direct attachment site of the PTT, and should be large enough to accommodate at least one, preferably two 2.7 mm screws

When the osteotomy is completed, the plantar and recurrent components of the PTT as well as peripheral soft tissue attachments are released and the navicular tubercle and distal part of the PTT are mobilized proximally. Removing the terminal portion and navicular tubercle from the operative field provides perfect visualization and access to the spring ligament and its calcaneal and navicular attachment sites (Fig. 3a, b).

a Complete visualization of the SLC from a limited incision can be achieved by removing the distal portion of the PTT and the navicular tubercle from the operative field. b Osteotomized navicular fragment, as visualized on fluoroscopy

In all cases, inspection of the spring ligament demonstrated attrition and incompetency in various forms. In younger individuals, loosening and elongation with attenuation were most prevalent; in advanced ages, fraying degeneration and ruptures were commonly observed. Similar to previous reports, we observed that the damaged segment of the SLC was mainly located at its distal portion, close to or at the navicular insertion site [18]. The proximal half of the ligament was well preserved compared to the distal part, capable of holding locked stitches for primary repair (Fig. 3a).

Following resection of the damaged segments of the spring ligament (Fig. 4a), the talonavicular joint is reduced and redundant ligamentous tissue is trimmed in order to adjust the proper tension of the ligament following repair (Fig. 4b). The superomedial and inferolateral components of the SLC are separately grasped with 2 or 3 sets of No. 2 braided nonabsorbable locking loop sutures and the free suture ends are retrieved from the trimmed edge of the ligament (Fig. 4c). The plantar and medial edges of the navicular body are then trimmed with a rongeur to create bleeding bony surfaces in order to promote healing at the bone-ligament interface. 2 or 3 sets of parallel bone tunnels within the navicular are drilled with K-wires, entering from the trimmed bony edges and exiting on the dorsomedial surface of the navicular body (Fig. 4d). The tunnels should be located at the proximal and dorsal half of the navicular bone, since the distal and plantar half of the bone will be kept for screw placement during fixation of the osteotomized navicular tubercle (Fig. 5). If a FDL tendon transfer is planned, this segment will also be used for tendon fixation (Fig. 6c, d). The free ends of the sutures are then passed through the bone tunnels with a Keith needle and clamped in pairs on the dorsal side of the navicular (Fig. 6a). Final fixation of the spring ligament is performed after lengthening of the lateral column in order to adjust the appropriate tension of the repair (Fig. 6b).

Schematic drawing showing the proximal dorsal placement of the bone tunnels for fixation of the spring ligament. The plantar distal half of the navicular body is used for navicular tubercle fixation

a Intraoperative picture showing the debrided distal end of the SLC, placement of the sutures holding both components of the ligament and their path within the bone. b Following lateral column lengthening, the talonavicular joint is reduced and the sutures are tightened and tied on the dorsomedial cortex of the navicular body. c If tendon transfer is planned, the FDL is visualized and prepared through the medial incision. A distally located bony tunnel is drilled through the navicular body for the FDL transfer in order to refrain from intersection of the tunnels drilled for the tendon and the SLC. d Fixation of the transferred FDL to the navicular body is performed by grasping sutures passing through the navicular body

The second step of the operation involves lateral column lengthening as described by Hintermann [13]. A longitudinal incision at the lateral side of the foot parallel to the dorsal edge of the peroneal sheaths is preferred rather than the classical oblique sinus tarsi approach, since vertically oriented incisions may cause difficulty in soft tissue closure following lateral column lengthening (Fig. 7a). Subcutaneous tissue is mobilized with sharp dissection in order to preserve the microcirculation of the thin and delicate soft tissue coverage on the lateral side of the heel. Terminal branches of the sural nerve should also be protected (Fig. 7b).

a Preferred longitudinal incision used for lateral column lengthening. b Peroneal tendon sheaths, fat pad overlying the sinus tarsi, FDB muscle mass and terminal branches of the sural nerve should be protected during dissection. c Peroneal tendons should be mobilized en bloc, preserving their sheaths. d Visualization of the lateral wall of the calcaneus after dissection

The fat pad overlying the sinus tarsi and the proximal attachments of the FDB muscle were mobilized carefully and folded dorsally. These soft tissues play a vital role in covering the osteotomy site. The peroneal tendon sheaths are mobilized en bloc without exposing the tendons from the lateral wall of the calcaneus using a mini chisel in order to prevent tendon laceration or post-operative tendon adhesions (Fig. 7c). The interperoneal tubercle may also be osteotomized and mobilized with the tendon sheaths (Fig. 7d). The contents of the sinus tarsi should not be resected for obtaining a better exposure; this practice may cause subtalar instability due to laceration of the subtalar ligaments [19].

Two mini Hohmann retractors are inserted at the lateral wall of the calcaneus (one at the base of the sinus tarsi and the other on the plantar cortex of the bone) in order to improve visualization of the osteotomy site and prevent soft tissue damage during osteotomy (Fig. 8a). After fluoroscopic control of its localization (Fig. 8b), a vertical osteotomy is performed with a small oscillating saw, oriented parallel to the inferior border of the posterior facet of the subtalar joint (Fig. 8c).

a Lateral wall of the calcaneus exposed for osteotomy with mini Hohmann retractors. b Osteotomy line may be marked with a K-wire and can be checked with fluoroscopy for correct placement. c A vertical osteotomy line located at the lowest point of the sinus tarsi is created for lateral column lengthening

The osteotomy line is opened 6 to 10 mm using the multiple chisel technique (Fig. 9a). Soft tissues preventing the separation of the plantar side of the calcaneus are released in order to obtain a more balanced opening at the dorsal and plantar sides of the osteotomy. The plantar fascia should not be released. Autogenous cortico-cancellous wedge-shaped bone grafts harvested from the iliac crest are inserted into the osteotomy site and fixed with 2 headless screws (Fig. 9b). We prefer to fix the osteotomy site with headless cannulated screws inserted from the distal lateral edge of the calcaneus, fixing the anterior and posterior segments of the osteotomized calcaneus (Fig. 9c). Plate fixation from the lateral wall of the calcaneus usually causes irritation of the peroneal tendons and may necessitate removal of the implants.

a Gradual distraction of the osteotomy line using multiple osteotomes inserted sequentially into the osteotomy site. b Insertion of the bone graft into the distracted osteotomy site. c Fixation of the bone block with 2 headless cannulated screws inserted through guide wires

If the peroneus brevis tendon becomes too tense following lateral column lengthening, we prefer to lengthen it from the musculotendinous junction using a separate proximal incision. Lengthening the peroneus longus should be avoided, as it has a significant role in supporting the medial arch of the foot by plantarflexion of the first ray and balancing the pull of the tibialis anterior muscle. When fixation is complete, the osteotomy site is covered with the preserved fat pad, FDB muscle belly and peroneal tendon sheaths. The skin and subcutaneous tissue are repaired in layers.

Back on the medial side, while holding the talonavicular joint in the reduced position, the stitches holding the two components of the spring ligament complex are tightened and tied on top of the navicular body (Fig. 6a, b). If the PTT is damaged, debridement and tubularization is performed. If transfer of the FDL to the navicular is planned, it may be fixed into a bony tunnel drilled at the distal plantar surface of the navicular body (Fig. 6d). The osteotomized navicular tubercle is advanced distally and plantarly for approximately 7 to 10 mm and fixed to the distal plantar half of the navicular body with two 2.7 mm threaded screws with or without washers (Fig. 10a, b,c). Subcutaneous fascia is stitched neatly to cover the navicular tubercle. Subdermal and dermal layers are covered in separate layers.

a The osteotomized fragment is distalized to adjust the proper tension of the PTT and fixed to the distal half of the navicular body with 2.7 mm screws with or without washers. b Intraoperative fluoroscopic image of the navicular and calcaneal fixations. c Illustration depicting SLC repair and navicular osteotomy fixation

Following correction of the alignment of the foot, Achilles tendon tightness is evaluated by passive dorsiflexion of the ankle joint. Any limitation of 10 degrees of dorsiflexion is considered for lengthening. Our preferred technique in this case series is a transverse Vulpius type of lengthening at the proximal end of the Achilles tendon [20].

All operated feet were kept on a below-knee cast brace for two weeks postoperatively. By that time, the superficial stitches were removed and a below-knee cast was applied and kept until the end of the 6th postoperative week. Patients were instructed to remain non-weight-bearing during this time period. Following the removal of the cast, the feet were protected in a walker brace for an additional four weeks while the patients were allowed to bear weight as tolerated. At the end of the 10th postoperative week, full weight-bearing with regular shoes was permitted. Sportive activities were restricted up to the 6th postoperative month.

Statistical analysis

Mean, standard deviation (SD), median, and range (minimum–maximum) are given as descriptive statistics. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC: two-way mixed effects, absolute agreement, three raters; three consecutive measurements for each) was used to analyze inter-rater and intra-rater reliability. Since inter- and intra-rater agreement was found to be above 0.80 for all study outcomes, the mean of the repeats was used for statistical analysis. Missing data were addressed with complete-case analysis. The normality of the pre- to postoperative differences was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Preoperative and postoperative values were compared using the paired-samples t-test for normally distributed parameters and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for non-normally distributed parameters. Statistical significance was assessed at p < 0.05, and all statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 25.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Seventeen symptomatic flatfeet of 16 patients who did not respond to conservative treatment and underwent surgery were included in the study. There were eight female and eight male patients (one male patient was operated on bilaterally, accounting for 17 feet) with an average age of 31.5 (range, 10–65) years. The patients were re-evaluated at an average of 17.6 months postoperatively (range, 12–29). In 2 cases, FDL tendon transfer to the navicular was also performed.

The final evaluation revealed that all patients developed a successful union at the calcaneal and navicular osteotomy sites. No nerve damage, peroneal impingement or implant irritation was observed. Although the patients reported clinical improvement in their pain and functional level and returned to normal daily activities, overcorrection of the AP Meary angle in some cases was encountered, which may be related to overlengthening of the lateral column and/ or overtightening of the SLC during repair.

Compared to pre-operative values, the EFAS ADL and sports scores showed significant improvements at postoperative controls (p < 0.001). The average preoperative EFAS scores were 9.5 and 6.4 points for ADL and sports scores, progressing to 20.9 and 14.4 points postoperatively at the last follow-up (Table 1). Compared to preoperative values, all postoperative measurements demonstrated statistically significant improvements (p < 0.001) (Table 2) (Fig. 11).

Discussion

The results of this study are consistent with the current literature, demonstrating similar corrections between spring ligament repair and reconstruction. In the systematic review by Rubin et al., the mean lateral Meary angle on weight-bearing X-rays improved from 20.0° to 7.1° after isolated repair and from 13.7° to 2.7° after augmented repair, and the AP Meary angle from 18.0° to 6.4° after augmentation [21]. Shih et al. reported the lateral Meary angle improving from 20.5° to 4.9° and the AP talonavicular coverage angle from 23.9° to 3.9° following lateral column lengthening, spring ligament repair and posterior tibial tendon plication [22]. In another cohort study in which the SLC was reconstructed by a peroneus longus autograft added to lateral column lengthening, Williams et al. demonstrated similar improvements in the AP Meary (18.5°→11.6°), AP talonavicular coverage (34.4°→19.8°) and lateral talonavicular coverage (12.0°→0.3°) angles [23]. These values are coherent with our results (AP Meary 19.5°→ −1.3°, lateral Meary 25.1°→4.6°, AP talonavicular coverage 31.4°→3.3°, lateral talonavicular coverage 17.1°→0.8°), indicating that the radiographic outcomes of the presented operation are comparable with those reported after repair, augmentation, and reconstruction techniques [21].

In this series, the SLC and the talonavicular capsule were found to be elongated and attenuated in all patients, supporting previous reports indicating that spring ligament insufficiency in flexible pes planovalgus is more typically a chronic degenerative phenomenon rather than an acute rupture, resulting from gradual ligament attrition and stretching [21]. Tendon fraying and tears were mainly observed in advanced ages, located at the distal end of the SLC close to the navicular insertion. The proximal part of the ligament was typically less damaged, capable of holding grasping sutures for primary repair.

We preferred to resect the damaged segment of the ligament and attach the healthy ligamentous tissue to the navicular with non-absorbable sutures passed through transosseous tunnels. This technique enables uniform anchorage through multiple fixation points and facilitates proper tensioning of the SLC. Multiple anchor sutures may also be used for fixation. The elongated SLC enables fixation of the healthy ligamentous edge to the navicular without a gap following resection of the damaged segment. The quality and strength of the proximal half of the ligament permitted successful repair in this series, so repair rather than reconstruction was performed. This may be due to the relatively younger age of the operated cases enrolled in this study compared to previous series presented in the literature. In elderly patients with poor ligament quality, reconstruction may be a more adequate alternative.

Owing to its degenerative nature in flatfoot deformity, the literature mainly focuses on reconstruction rather than primary repair of the SLC, since the latter is regarded as unsuccessful with unpredictable results in long-standing deformities and is only advised in acute ruptures in young adults [24]. This opinion may also reflect the limitations of a strong repair due to the deep and confined location of the SLC. During surgery, retraction of the tendon inferiorly only provides partial exposure of the superomedial component of the ligament and the medial talonavicular (TN) joint (Fig. 2b). Adequate visualization of both components of the SLC necessitates mobilization of the distal insertion of the PTT with or without the navicular tubercle. The encouraging results of spring ligament repair obtained in this study may be related to the improved visualization and access to the SLC using the navicular osteotomy technique. This technique enables perfect evaluation of the superomedial and inferolateral components of the SLC and provides easy access from a limited incision for a reliable repair. It also has the advantage of preserving the tendon body and the unique fibrocartilage transition zone at its bony insertion site during mobilization of the PTT. Both the tendon and the fibrocartilage zone heal with scar tissue when damaged, presenting inferior tissue strength and an increased rate of complications such as lengthening, diminished tendon function and rupture [25].

Degeneration of the PTT marked by tendon elongation is commonly reported in Johnson–Strom/Myerson stage II deformity [12, 26]. When the tendon is elongated but intact, physiologic tension can be obtained by imbrication or, more commonly, advancement of the distal insertion site of the tendon on the navicular bone [27]. Since the tendon body or tendon–bone junction never attains pre-injury strength following severance [25], we prefer to mobilize the distal portion of the PTT with the navicular tubercle. This practice has the added advantages of greater initial strength of bone-to-bone fixation compared to tendon-to-tendon and tendon-to-bone fixation, and a scar-free healing at the osteotomy site.

The PTT was found to be salvageable and functioning in the operated patients, so following debridement, re-tensioning by distal advancement of the insertion site was performed. In 2 cases, FDL tendon transfer was added to the medial-sided repair in order to support the weakened PTT. We have observed that forefoot derotation by lateral column lengthening further slackens the already stretched medial soft tissue stabilizers, necessitating re-tensioning of the PTT in every case in order to adjust the physiologic tension and restore the functional length. The primary insertion point of the posterior tibial tendon may also be redirected plantarly to yield a better lever arm and to enhance soft tissue support of the talonavicular joint complex [28, 29].

The navicular tubercle osteotomy technique does not complicate FDL tendon transfer. When planned, the distal end of the tendon may be inserted and fixed into a bony tunnel drilled at the lower distal quadrant of the osteotomy site (Fig. 6d), in order to refrain from intersection of the fixation tunnels drilled for the transferred FDL tendon and the SLC.

We also prefer to evaluate Achilles tendon tightness as the last step of the operation after restoring the alignment of the foot. Uncorrected deformities of the hindfoot may cause underestimation of the amount of lengthening that should be performed. Gastrocsoleus recession using the transverse Vulpius technique was our preferred technique for lengthening the Achilles tendon in all cases [20].

This study has some limitations. It is a retrospective study with a small sample size, a wide age range consisting mainly of young patients, and a limited follow-up period. Comparative clinical studies with larger patient groups, longer follow-up and prospective study designs may help us to comment more accurately regarding the role of spring ligament repair on talonavicular reduction and medial arch stability in the surgical correction of PCFD.

Conclusion

In this series of flexible progressive collapsing foot deformities, primary repair of the spring ligament complex through a navicular tubercle osteotomy, combined with lateral column lengthening, distal advancement of the posterior tibial tendon and gastrocsoleus recession, restored talonavicular congruity and produced significant improvements in clinical scores and in all weight‑bearing radiographic parameters. The navicular tubercle osteotomy improved exposure of both components of the ligament and allowed a direct repair without additional graft or augmentation material. These findings suggest that, in appropriately selected patients with adequate ligament quality, direct spring ligament repair is a viable component of PCFD correction. Prospective comparative studies with larger cohorts and longer follow‑up are needed to define its role relative to augmentation and reconstruction techniques.
