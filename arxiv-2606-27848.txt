1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background and main result

Let
\Omega
be a bounded connected domain with smooth boundary in a Riemannian manifold. We use the sign convention

\Delta u=-\mu u,\qquad\partial_{\nu}u=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

and write the Neumann spectrum as

0=\mu_{0}(\Omega)<\mu_{1}(\Omega)\leq\mu_{2}(\Omega)\leq\cdots.

The isoperimetric theory of Neumann eigenvalues begins with the Szegő–Weinberger inequality. Szegő proved in dimension two that, among simply connected planar domains of prescribed area, the disk maximizes the first nonzero Neumann eigenvalue
[
12
]
. Weinberger removed the planar and conformal restrictions and proved the
n
-dimensional Euclidean inequality

\mu_{1}(\Omega)\leq\left(\frac{\omega_{n}}{|\Omega|}\right)^{2/n}p_{n/2,1}^{2},\qquad\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

with equality if and only if
\Omega
is a ball
[
13
]
. Here
\omega_{n}
denotes the volume of the Euclidean unit ball and
p_{n/2,1}
is the first positive zero of the derivative of
x^{1-n/2}J_{n/2}(x)
.

Ashbaugh and Benguria then studied reciprocal sums of low Neumann eigenvalues. They proved the scale-sharp but non-optimal estimate

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\mu_{j}(\Omega)}\geq\frac{n}{n+2}\left(\frac{|\Omega|}{\omega_{n}}\right)^{2/n},\qquad\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

and formulated the following sharp conjecture
[
2
]
.

Conjecture 1.1

(Ashbaugh–Benguria)
.

For any bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with smooth boundary,

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\mu_{j}(\Omega)}\geq\frac{n(|\Omega|/\omega_{n})^{2/n}}{p_{n/2,1}^{2}}.

Equality holds if and only if
\Omega
is a ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Conjecture
1.1
was subsequently highlighted by Ashbaugh and by Henrot
[
1
,
9
]
. Related reciprocal-eigenvalue estimates and quantitative refinements include work of Hile–Xu, Nadirashvili, and Brasco–Pratelli
[
8
,
11
,
5
]
. A sharp partial result toward Conjecture
1.1
was obtained by Xia and Wang, who proved the
(n-1)
-term inequality

\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{\mu_{j}(\Omega)}\geq\frac{(n-1)(|\Omega|/\omega_{n})^{2/n}}{p_{n/2,1}^{2}},\qquad\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

with equality only for balls
[
14
]
. Recently, He, Li and Tang posted a proof of Conjecture
1.1
; a central feature of their argument is to retain the transplanted first eigenspace as one coupled finite-dimensional trial space rather than to estimate its coordinate functions separately
[
7
]
.

The Szegő–Weinberger inequality for
\mu_{1}
has also been studied in spaces of constant curvature. In the hyperbolic case, it was proved independently by Ashbaugh–Benguria and by Xu; Chavel observed that Weinberger’s method can be adapted to this setting
[
3
,
15
,
6
]
. Ashbaugh–Benguria also proved the corresponding sharp upper bound for domains contained in a hemisphere of the sphere
[
3
]
. Motivated by these extensions, Xia and Wang formulated the following non-Euclidean space-form analogue of Conjecture
1.1
for constant curvature
\kappa\in\{-1,1\}

[
14
]
.

Conjecture 1.2

(Xia–Wang)
.

Let
M^{n}_{\kappa}
be the simply connected
n
-dimensional Riemannian manifold of constant sectional curvature
\kappa\in\{-1,1\}
, and let
\Omega\subset M^{n}_{\kappa}
be a bounded connected domain with smooth boundary. In the case
\kappa=1
, assume that
\Omega
is contained in an open hemisphere. Let
B_{\Omega}\subset M^{n}_{\kappa}
be a geodesic ball such that
|B_{\Omega}|=|\Omega|
. Then

(1)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\mu_{j}(\Omega)}\geq\frac{n}{\mu_{1}(B_{\Omega})}.

Equality holds if and only if
\Omega
is isometric to
B_{\Omega}
.

Xia and Wang proved the corresponding sharp
(n-1)
-term inequalities in the Euclidean and hyperbolic cases; in particular, for
\Omega\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
they obtained

\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{\mu_{j}(\Omega)}\geq\frac{n-1}{\mu_{1}(B_{\Omega})},

with equality only for geodesic balls
[
14
]
. In positive curvature, Benguria, Brandolini and Chiacchio proved the corresponding harmonic-mean inequality for the first
n-1
nontrivial Neumann eigenvalues on domains contained in a hemisphere of
\mathbb{S}^{n}

[
4
]
. Related lower-order estimates have also been developed in rank-one symmetric spaces
[
10
]
.

The main result of this paper proves the conjecture of Xia–Wang.

Theorem 1.3
.

Conjecture
1.2
holds true.

1.2.
Proof strategy

The proof is a finite-dimensional, matrix-valued refinement of
Weinberger’s transplantation method. In the classical coordinatewise
Weinberger argument, and in the known sharp
(n-1)
-term estimates, the
transplanted coordinate functions are tested essentially one at a time. The
key point here is instead to keep the whole transplanted first eigenspace as
a single coupled
n
-dimensional trial space, and to estimate its mass and
stiffness forms simultaneously in the Loewner order. This is structurally
analogous to the recent Euclidean proof of He–Li–Tang
[
7
]
. The
present paper develops the corresponding argument in space forms; the main
new difficulties are the curvature-dependent radial monotonicity estimates
and, in the spherical case, the need to fold antipodally before applying
raywise rearrangement.

More precisely, we first choose a Weinberger center and transplant the

n
-dimensional first nonzero Neumann eigenspace of the comparison ball

B_{\Omega}
. This gives trial functions
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
with zero mean on

\Omega
. Let
\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{K}
be their mass and stiffness matrices, as
defined in (
2
). The trace form of the Ritz principle
(Lemma
2.1
) gives

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\mu_{j}(\Omega)}\geq\tr(\mathsf{K}^{-1}\mathsf{M}).

Thus the problem is reduced to obtaining a sharp lower bound for

\tr(\mathsf{K}^{-1}\mathsf{M})
.

The required bound comes from raywise rearrangement in geodesic polar
coordinates about the Weinberger center. On each ray, the radial
monotonicity properties of the transplanted profile compare the contribution
of
\Omega
with that of the centered ball of the same volume. After
integrating over the angular variable, these one-dimensional comparisons
become the matrix inequalities

\mathsf{M}\succeq aI+cZ,\qquad\mathsf{K}\preceq\lambda aI-\eta Z,\qquad\tr Z=0,

where
\lambda=\mu_{1}(B_{\Omega})
, the constants
a,c,\eta
are positive, and

Z
is a symmetric trace-free matrix measuring the angular imbalance of

\Omega
relative to the chosen center. The important feature is that the
same imbalance matrix appears in the two estimates with opposite signs.
Lemma
2.3
then converts these Loewner-order bounds directly into

\tr(\mathsf{K}^{-1}\mathsf{M})\geq\frac{n}{\lambda},

which yields the desired reciprocal-sum inequality.

In the spherical case an additional geometric issue has to be addressed.
Although
\Omega
is assumed to lie in some open hemisphere, the Weinberger
center produced by the centering argument need not be the center of that
hemisphere. To remove this obstruction, we use a radial profile, with
respect to the chosen center
p
, that is even under
t\mapsto\pi-t
. The
corresponding trial functions are odd under the antipodal map, while their
mass and stiffness densities are antipodally invariant. Hence the part of

\Omega
lying beyond the equator of
p
may be folded back into

B_{\pi/2}(p)
without changing the matrices
\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{K}
. After this
folding, the entire rearrangement argument takes place in the half-sphere
where
\sin t
is increasing, so that the same matrix mechanism as in the
hyperbolic case applies.

B_{R}(p)=\{x:d(p,x)<R\}

2.1.
Trace Ritz principle

Let

H^{1}_{\perp}(\Omega)=\left\{u\in H^{1}(\Omega):\int_{\Omega}u\,dv=0\right\}.

The positive Neumann spectrum is the spectrum of the Dirichlet form
\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}dv
on
H^{1}_{\perp}(\Omega)
with mass form
\int_{\Omega}u^{2}dv
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded connected smooth domain, and let
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\in H^{1}_{\perp}(\Omega)
be linearly independent. Define the mass matrix
\mathsf{M}=(\mathsf{M}_{ij})
and stiffness matrix
\mathsf{K}=(\mathsf{K}_{ij})
as

(2)

\mathsf{M}_{ij}=\int_{\Omega}P_{i}P_{j}\,dv,\qquad\mathsf{K}_{ij}=\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla P_{i},\nabla P_{j}\rangle\,dv.

Lemma 2.1
.

\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{K}
are positive definite and

(3)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\mu_{j}(\Omega)}\geq\tr(\mathsf{K}^{-1}\mathsf{M}).

Proof.

Let

V=\spanop\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\}\subset H^{1}_{\perp}(\Omega).

Positive definiteness of
\mathsf{M}
follows from linear independence. If a nonzero
u\in V
had zero stiffness, then
\nabla u=0
a.e. Since
\Omega
is connected,
u
would be constant. Because
u\in H^{1}_{\perp}(\Omega)
, this constant would be zero, contradicting linear independence. Hence
\mathsf{K}
is positive definite.

The Ritz values
\rho_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\rho_{n}
of the pair
(\mathsf{K},\mathsf{M})
are characterized by

\mathsf{K}v=\rho\,\mathsf{M}v
. By the min-max principle,

\mu_{j}(\Omega)\leq\rho_{j},\qquad j=1,\dots,n.

Consequently

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\mu_{j}(\Omega)}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\rho_{j}}.

The reciprocal Ritz values are the eigenvalues of
\mathsf{K}^{-1/2}\mathsf{M}\mathsf{K}^{-1/2}
; hence their sum is
\tr(\mathsf{K}^{-1}\mathsf{M})
.
∎

2.2.
One-dimensional rearrangement on rays

Let
L\in(0,\infty]
, let
w:(0,L)\to(0,\infty)
be locally integrable, and put

\mathcal{V}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}w(\tau)\,d\tau.

When
L=\infty
,
\mathcal{V}(L)
means
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{V}(t)
. For
y\in[0,\mathcal{V}(L))
, let
\tau(y)
be the inverse function of
\mathcal{V}
. We shall use the following elementary one-dimensional rearrangement fact.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
E\subset(0,L)
be measurable and let

y=\int_{E}w(t)\,dt<\mathcal{V}(L).

Let
\psi:(0,L)\to\mathbb{R}
be measurable and monotone. If
\psi
is nondecreasing, then

\int_{E}\psi(t)w(t)\,dt\geq\int_{0}^{\tau(y)}\psi(t)w(t)\,dt.

If
\psi
is nonincreasing, then

\int_{E}\psi(t)w(t)\,dt\leq\int_{0}^{\tau(y)}\psi(t)w(t)\,dt.

The same conclusions hold when
y=\mathcal{V}(L)<\infty
, with
\tau(y)=L
.

Proof.

Since

\int_{E}w(t)\,dt=\int_{0}^{\tau(y)}w(t)\,dt=y,

we have

\int_{E\setminus(0,\tau(y))}w(t)\,dt=\int_{(0,\tau(y))\setminus E}w(t)\,dt.

If
\psi
is nondecreasing, then

\displaystyle\int_{E}\psi(t)w(t)\,dt-\int_{0}^{\tau(y)}\psi(t)w(t)\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{E\setminus(0,\tau(y))}\bigl(\psi(t)-\psi(\tau(y))\bigr)w(t)\,dt+\int_{(0,\tau(y))\setminus E}\bigl(\psi(\tau(y))-\psi(t)\bigr)w(t)\,dt\geq 0,

because
t\geq\tau(y)
on
E\setminus(0,\tau(y))
and

t\leq\tau(y)
on
(0,\tau(y))\setminus E
. The nonincreasing case is obtained by reversing the
inequality in the same argument, and the endpoint cases
y=0
and

y=\mathcal{V}(L)<\infty
are immediate.
∎

2.3.
A matrix lemma

We adopt the Loewner order on the space of real symmetric matrices. For two such matrices
X,Y
, we write
X\succeq Y
if
X-Y
is positive semidefinite, and
X\preceq Y
if
Y-X
is positive semidefinite.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{K}
be positive definite symmetric
n\times n
matrices. Suppose that
a,c,\eta,\lambda>0
and that
Z
is a symmetric trace-free matrix such that

(4)

\mathsf{M}\succeq aI+cZ,\qquad\mathsf{K}\preceq\lambda aI-\eta Z.

Then

(5)

\tr(\mathsf{K}^{-1}\mathsf{M})\geq\frac{n}{\lambda}.

Moreover, if equality holds in (
5
), then

(6)

Z=0,\qquad\mathsf{M}=aI,\qquad\mathsf{K}=\lambda aI.

Proof.

Set

T=\lambda aI-\eta Z.

Since
T-\mathsf{K}\succeq 0
and
\mathsf{K}>0
,
T
is positive definite.
We have

(7)

\tr(\mathsf{K}^{-1}\mathsf{M})\geq\tr(T^{-1}\mathsf{M})\geq\tr\bigl(T^{-1}(aI+cZ)\bigr).

Indeed, if
X,Y\succeq 0
, then
\tr(XY)=\tr(Y^{1/2}XY^{1/2})\geq 0
.

Diagonalize
Z
. Let
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}
be its eigenvalues. Since
T=\lambda aI-\eta Z
and
aI+cZ
are affine functions of
Z
, they are diagonal in the same orthonormal basis, and so is
T^{-1}
.
The positivity of
T
gives
\lambda a-\eta z_{i}>0
for every
i
. Hence

\displaystyle\tr\bigl(T^{-1}(aI+cZ)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{a+cz_{i}}{\lambda a-\eta z_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{\lambda}+\frac{\lambda c+\eta}{\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{z_{i}}{\lambda a-\eta z_{i}}.

The function

\phi(z)=\frac{z}{\lambda a-\eta z}

is strictly convex on
(-\infty,\lambda a/\eta)
, because

\phi^{\prime\prime}(z)=\frac{2\lambda a\eta}{(\lambda a-\eta z)^{3}}>0.

Jensen’s inequality yields

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(z_{i})\geq n\phi\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}\right)=0.

This proves (
5
).

Assume now that equality holds in (
5
). Then equality must hold in every step of (
7
) and in Jensen’s inequality. Since
\phi
is strictly convex, equality in Jensen’s inequality forces
z_{1}=\cdots=z_{n}
. Since
\sum_{i}z_{i}=0
, this gives
Z=0
.

With
Z=0
, we have
T=\lambda aI
. Equality in the first inequality of (
7
) gives

\tr\bigl((\mathsf{K}^{-1}-T^{-1})\mathsf{M}\bigr)=0.

Here
\mathsf{K}^{-1}-T^{-1}\succeq 0
and
\mathsf{M}>0
, so
\mathsf{K}^{-1}=T^{-1}
and hence
\mathsf{K}=T=\lambda aI
. Equality in the second inequality of (
7
) similarly gives

\tr\bigl(T^{-1}(\mathsf{M}-aI)\bigr)=0.

Since
T^{-1}>0
and
\mathsf{M}-aI\succeq 0
, we get
\mathsf{M}=aI
. This proves (
6
).
∎

2.4.
Almost-everywhere equality of smooth domains

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
be open domains with smooth boundary in a smooth Riemannian manifold. If
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
agree up to a null set, then
D_{1}=D_{2}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
x\in D_{1}\setminus D_{2}
. Since
D_{1}
is open, a small geodesic ball
U
centered at
x
is contained in
D_{1}
. If
x
is an interior point of the complement of
D_{2}
, then
U\setminus D_{2}
has positive volume for
U
small. If
x\in\partial D_{2}
, the smoothness of
\partial D_{2}
gives local coordinates in which
D_{2}
is a half-ball; hence
U\setminus D_{2}
again has positive volume. This contradicts
|D_{1}\setminus D_{2}|=0
. Thus
D_{1}\subset D_{2}
. Interchanging the roles of
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
gives the reverse inclusion.
∎

3.
The hyperbolic case

Throughout this section,

s(t)=\sinh t,\qquad w(t)=s(t)^{n-1}.

About any point
p\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
, geodesic polar coordinates identify
\mathbb{H}^{n}\setminus\{p\}
with
(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and the metric is

(8)

ds_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}^{2}=dt^{2}+s(t)^{2}d\sigma^{2},

where
d\sigma^{2}
is the round metric on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. We write
\widetilde{\nabla}
for the gradient on the unit round sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. The volume element is

dv=w(t)\,dt\,d\sigma.

3.1.
The first eigenfunctions of a hyperbolic ball

We shall use the following standard spectral fact for geodesic balls in hyperbolic space.

Lemma 3.1

(First Neumann eigenspace of a hyperbolic ball)
.

Let
B_{R}\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
be a geodesic ball of radius
R
and let

\lambda=\mu_{1}(B_{R}).

The first nonzero Neumann eigenvalue of
B_{R}
has multiplicity
n
. Its eigenspace is spanned by functions

u_{i}(t,\xi)=f(t)\xi_{i},\qquad i=1,\dots,n,

where
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and where
f
is the regular solution of

(9)

f^{\prime\prime}+(n-1)\coth t\,f^{\prime}+\left(\lambda-\frac{n-1}{s(t)^{2}}\right)f=0,\qquad 0<t<R,

with boundary conditions

(10)

f(0)=0,\qquad f^{\prime}(R)=0.

After multiplication by a constant,
f
may be chosen so that

f(t)>0\qquad 0<t\leq R.

Moreover,

(11)

\lambda=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}\left(f^{\prime}(t)^{2}+(n-1)\frac{f(t)^{2}}{s(t)^{2}}\right)w(t)\,dt}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}f(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt},

and

(12)

f^{\prime}(t)>0\qquad 0<t<R.

Proof.

See Appendix
A
.
∎

Define the Weinberger extension

(13)

F(t)=\begin{cases}f(t),&0\leq t\leq R,\\
f(R),&t\geq R.\end{cases}

Then
F
is nondecreasing,
F^{\prime}(t)=0
for
t>R
, and
F(0)=0
. The function
F(t)/s(t)
is understood at
t=0
by taking its finite limit. The following monotonicity is the key radial result.

Lemma 3.2
.

The function

t\longmapsto\frac{F(t)}{\sinh t}

is nonincreasing on
(0,\infty)
, and it is strictly decreasing on
(R,\infty)
.

Proof.

For
t\geq R
,
F(t)=f(R)
, so
F(t)/\sinh t
is strictly decreasing. It remains to consider
0<t\leq R
.
Set

\gamma(t)=f^{\prime}(t)-\coth t\,f(t).

The desired assertion on
(0,R]
is equivalent to
\gamma(t)\leq 0
. Since
f(t)=a_{0}t+O(t^{3})
near
0
for some
a_{0}>0
, we have
\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\gamma(t)=0
. Also

\gamma(R)=f^{\prime}(R)-\coth R\,f(R)=-\coth R\,f(R)<0.

Suppose, for contradiction, that
\gamma(t_{0})>0
for some
t_{0}\in(0,R)
. Then
\gamma
attains a positive maximum at some
t_{1}\in(0,R)
, and hence

(14)

0=\gamma^{\prime}(t_{1})=f^{\prime\prime}(t_{1})+\frac{f(t_{1})}{s(t_{1})^{2}}-\coth t_{1}\,f^{\prime}(t_{1}).

Using (
9
) at
t_{1}
and eliminating
f^{\prime\prime}(t_{1})
by (
14
), one obtains

f^{\prime}(t_{1})-\frac{f(t_{1})}{\sinh t_{1}\cosh t_{1}}=-\frac{\lambda f(t_{1})\sinh t_{1}}{n\cosh t_{1}}<0.

Since

\coth t_{1}=\frac{\cosh t_{1}}{\sinh t_{1}}>\frac{1}{\sinh t_{1}\cosh t_{1}},

this implies

f^{\prime}(t_{1})-\coth t_{1}\,f(t_{1})<0,

contradicting
\gamma(t_{1})>0
. Thus
\gamma\leq 0
, and the proof is complete.
∎

Introduce the radial constants

(15)

A=\int_{0}^{R}f(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt,\qquad Q=\int_{0}^{R}f^{\prime}(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt,\qquad H=\int_{0}^{R}\frac{f(t)^{2}}{s(t)^{2}}w(t)\,dt.

By (
11
),

(16)

Q+(n-1)H=\lambda A.

3.2.
A hyperbolic Weinberger center

Lemma 3.3
.

There exists
p\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
such that, in geodesic polar coordinates centered at
p
,

(17)

\int_{\Omega}F(t)\xi\,dv=0\in T_{p}\mathbb{H}^{n}.

Equivalently, for every first spherical harmonic
\omega(\xi)=v\cdot\xi
,

\int_{\Omega}F(t)\omega(\xi)\,dv=0.

Proof.

Let

\Phi(\rho)=\int_{0}^{\rho}F(\tau)\,d\tau,\qquad J(q)=\int_{\Omega}\Phi(d(q,x))\,dv_{x}.

The function
J
is continuous. Indeed,
F
is bounded on bounded intervals,
\Phi
is locally Lipschitz, and
\Omega
is bounded. Moreover,
J
is proper on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
: since
\Omega
is bounded,
d(q,x)\to\infty
uniformly in
x\in\Omega
as
q\to\infty
; and since
F(t)=f(R)>0
for
t\geq R
, the primitive
\Phi(\rho)
grows linearly as
\rho\to\infty
. Hence
J
has a minimizer
p
by Hopf–Rinow.

For
x\neq p
, write
x=\exp_{p}(t\xi)
. The first variation formula for distance gives

\nabla_{p}d(p,x)=-\xi.

At the exceptional point
x=p
, the function
q\mapsto\Phi(d(q,p))
has directional derivative zero at
q=p
, because
F(0)=0
and
F(t)=O(t)
as
t\downarrow 0
, whence
\Phi(t)=O(t^{2})
. Fix a compact neighbourhood
U
of
p
and a number
D
such that
d(q,x)\leq D
for all
q\in U
and
x\in\Omega
. For
x\neq p
, the directional derivative in a unit direction is bounded in absolute value by
\|F\|_{L^{\infty}([0,D])}
. Since
\Omega
has finite volume, dominated convergence justifies differentiation under the integral sign. For every
v\in T_{p}\mathbb{H}^{n}
,

0=dJ_{p}(v)=-\left\langle\int_{\Omega}F(t)\xi\,dv,\,v\right\rangle.

This proves (
17
).
∎

3.3.
Ray estimates in the hyperbolic case

Let

(18)

Y_{R}=\int_{0}^{R}w(t)\,dt.

For a measurable
E\subset(0,\infty)
put

\mathcal{Y}(E)=\int_{E}w(t)\,dt.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
E\subset(0,\infty)
be measurable with
\mathcal{Y}(E)<\infty
, and set
y_{E}=\mathcal{Y}(E)
. Then

(19)

\displaystyle\int_{E}F(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt

\displaystyle\geq A+f(R)^{2}\,(y_{E}-Y_{R}),

(20)

\displaystyle\int_{E}\frac{F(t)^{2}}{s(t)^{2}}w(t)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq H+\frac{f(R)^{2}}{s(R)^{2}}\,(y_{E}-Y_{R}),

(21)

\displaystyle\int_{E}F^{\prime}(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq Q.

Proof.

Since
F
is nondecreasing,
F^{2}
is nondecreasing. By Lemma
2.2
,

\int_{E}F^{2}w\,dt\geq\int_{0}^{\tau(y_{E})}F^{2}w\,dt.

The function

\Psi(y)=\int_{0}^{\tau(y)}F(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt

is convex in
y
, because
\Psi^{\prime}(y)=F(\tau(y))^{2}
is nondecreasing. Moreover
\Psi(Y_{R})=A
and
\Psi^{\prime}(Y_{R})=f(R)^{2}
. Hence the tangent-line inequality for a convex function gives

\Psi(y_{E})\geq A+f(R)^{2}(y_{E}-Y_{R}),

which proves (
19
).

By Lemma
3.2
, the function
F^{2}/s^{2}
is nonincreasing. Lemma
2.2
gives

\int_{E}\frac{F^{2}}{s^{2}}w\,dt\leq\int_{0}^{\tau(y_{E})}\frac{F^{2}}{s^{2}}w\,dt.

Now

\Theta(y)=\int_{0}^{\tau(y)}\frac{F(t)^{2}}{s(t)^{2}}w(t)\,dt

is concave in
y
, with
\Theta(Y_{R})=H
and
\Theta^{\prime}(Y_{R})=f(R)^{2}/s(R)^{2}
. Therefore the tangent-line inequality for a concave function gives

\Theta(y_{E})\leq H+\frac{f(R)^{2}}{s(R)^{2}}(y_{E}-Y_{R}),

which proves (
20
). Finally,
F^{\prime}=f^{\prime}
on
(0,R)
and
F^{\prime}=0
on
(R,\infty)
, so (
21
) follows from nonnegativity of
F^{\prime 2}w
.
∎

3.4.
Proof in the hyperbolic case

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
be a bounded connected smooth domain, and let
B_{R}\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
be a geodesic ball such that
|B_{R}|=|\Omega|
. Then

(22)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\mu_{j}(\Omega)}\geq\frac{n}{\mu_{1}(B_{R})}.

Equality holds if and only if
\Omega
is a geodesic ball of radius
R
.

Proof.

Put
\lambda=\mu_{1}(B_{R})
and let
f,F,A,Q,H
be as above. Choose the point
p\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
given by Lemma
3.3
. In polar coordinates centered at
p
, write
x=\exp_{p}(t\xi)
,
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, and define

(23)

P_{i}(x)=F(t)\xi_{i},\qquad i=1,\dots,n.

At
t=0
we set
P_{i}(p)=0
. In normal coordinates
y=t\xi
centered at
p
, the regularity of the degree-one radial solution gives
f(t)=a_{0}t+O(t^{3})
, and hence
P_{i}(y)=a_{0}y_{i}+O(|y|^{3})
near
p
. Away from
p
, the profile
F
is piecewise smooth and is
C^{1}
across
t=R
because
f^{\prime}(R)=0
. Thus
P_{i}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
. By the choice of
p
, each
P_{i}
has zero mean over
\Omega
.

The functions
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
are linearly independent on
\Omega
. Indeed, suppose that
P_{b}:=\sum_{i}b_{i}P_{i}
vanishes a.e. on
\Omega
for some
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
P_{b}
is continuous and
\Omega
is open,
P_{b}
vanishes everywhere on
\Omega
. If
b\neq 0
, then away from the single point
p
its zero set is contained in the totally geodesic hypersurface determined by
b\cdot\xi=0
. Such a hypersurface has empty interior in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
and cannot contain the nonempty open set
\Omega
. Hence
b=0
.

Recall the definition of mass and stiffness matrices (
2
). By Lemma
2.1
,

(24)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\mu_{j}(\Omega)}\geq\tr(\mathsf{K}^{-1}\mathsf{M}).

For
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, let

E_{\xi}=\{t>0:\exp_{p}(t\xi)\in\Omega\}

and

Y(\xi)=\int_{E_{\xi}}w(t)\,dt.

By Fubini’s theorem,
E_{\xi}
is measurable and
Y(\xi)<\infty
for a.e.
\xi
. Since
|\Omega|=|B_{R}|
,

(25)

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}(Y(\xi)-Y_{R})\,d\sigma(\xi)=0.

Set

(26)

Z=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}(Y(\xi)-Y_{R})\,\xi\otimes\xi\,d\sigma(\xi).

Then
Z
is symmetric and, by (
25
),

(27)

\tr Z=0.

Let

(28)

a=\frac{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}{n}A,\qquad c=f(R)^{2},\qquad\eta=\frac{f(R)^{2}}{s(R)^{2}}.

First,

\mathsf{M}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left(\int_{E_{\xi}}F(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt\right)\xi\otimes\xi\,d\sigma(\xi).

By (
19
),

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}

\displaystyle\succeq\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left(A+c(Y(\xi)-Y_{R})\right)\xi\otimes\xi\,d\sigma(\xi)

\displaystyle=A\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\xi\otimes\xi\,d\sigma+cZ=aI+cZ,

where we used

(29)

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\xi\otimes\xi\,d\sigma=\frac{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}{n}I.

Second, since

\left\langle\widetilde{\nabla}\xi_{i},\widetilde{\nabla}\xi_{j}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}=\delta_{ij}-\xi_{i}\xi_{j},

the hyperbolic gradient decomposition gives

\displaystyle\mathsf{K}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left(\int_{E_{\xi}}F^{\prime}(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt\right)\xi\otimes\xi\,d\sigma(\xi)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left(\int_{E_{\xi}}\frac{F(t)^{2}}{s(t)^{2}}w(t)\,dt\right)(I-\xi\otimes\xi)\,d\sigma(\xi).

By (
20
) and (
21
),

\displaystyle\mathsf{K}

\displaystyle\preceq\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}Q\,\xi\otimes\xi\,d\sigma

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left(H+\eta(Y(\xi)-Y_{R})\right)(I-\xi\otimes\xi)\,d\sigma.

Using (
25
), (
26
), and (
29
), this becomes

\displaystyle\mathsf{K}

\displaystyle\preceq\frac{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}{n}QI+\frac{(n-1)|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}{n}HI-\eta Z

\displaystyle=\frac{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}{n}\bigl(Q+(n-1)H\bigr)I-\eta Z.

By (
16
),

(30)

\mathsf{K}\preceq\lambda aI-\eta Z.

Thus the hypotheses of Lemma
2.3
are satisfied. Hence

(31)

\tr(\mathsf{K}^{-1}\mathsf{M})\geq\frac{n}{\lambda}.

Combining (
24
) and (
31
), we obtain

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\mu_{j}(\Omega)}\geq\frac{n}{\lambda}=\frac{n}{\mu_{1}(B_{R})}.

This proves the inequality.

If
\Omega
is a geodesic ball of radius
R
, then

\mu_{1}(\Omega)=\cdots=\mu_{n}(\Omega)=\mu_{1}(B_{R})=\lambda,

so equality holds.

Conversely, suppose equality holds in (
22
). Then equality holds in both inequalities

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\mu_{j}(\Omega)}\geq\tr(\mathsf{K}^{-1}\mathsf{M})\geq\frac{n}{\lambda}.

In particular, equality holds in Lemma
2.3
. By its equality statement,

(32)

Z=0,\qquad\mathsf{M}=aI,\qquad\mathsf{K}=\lambda aI.

Since
Z=0
, the upper comparison (
30
) reads
\mathsf{K}\preceq\lambda aI
; by (
32
) equality holds in this comparison.

For a.e.
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, set

\displaystyle q(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{E_{\xi}}F^{\prime}(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt,

\displaystyle h(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{E_{\xi}}\frac{F(t)^{2}}{s(t)^{2}}w(t)\,dt,

\displaystyle\delta(\xi)

\displaystyle=Y(\xi)-Y_{R}.

By Corollary
3.4
,

\alpha(\xi):=Q-q(\xi)\geq 0,\qquad\beta(\xi):=H+\eta\delta(\xi)-h(\xi)\geq 0.

A direct subtraction of the formula for
\mathsf{K}
from its upper bound gives

(33)

\lambda aI-\mathsf{K}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left[\alpha(\xi)\,\xi\otimes\xi+\beta(\xi)(I-\xi\otimes\xi)\right]d\sigma(\xi).

The left-hand side is zero. The integrand in (
33
) is positive semidefinite. Taking traces yields

0=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left(\alpha(\xi)+(n-1)\beta(\xi)\right)d\sigma(\xi).

Therefore

(34)

\alpha(\xi)=0,\qquad\beta(\xi)=0\quad\text{for a.e. }\xi.

In particular,
q(\xi)=Q
for a.e.
\xi
, that is,

(35)

\int_{E_{\xi}}F^{\prime}(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt=\int_{0}^{R}f^{\prime}(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt\quad\text{for a.e. }\xi.

Since
F^{\prime}=f^{\prime}
on
(0,R)
,
F^{\prime}=0
on
(R,\infty)
, and
f^{\prime}(t)>0
for
0<t<R
, (
35
) forces

(36)

(0,R)\subset E_{\xi}\quad\text{up to }w(t)dt\text{-null sets, for a.e. }\xi.

Since
w(t)>0
for
t>0
, these are equivalently one-dimensional Lebesgue null sets. Thus
Y(\xi)\geq Y_{R}
for a.e.
\xi
. But (
25
) says that the average of
Y(\xi)-Y_{R}
is zero. Therefore

Y(\xi)=Y_{R}\quad\text{for a.e. }\xi.

Together with (
36
), this gives

E_{\xi}=(0,R)\quad\text{up to }w(t)dt\text{-null sets, for a.e. }\xi.

Hence
\Omega
agrees almost everywhere with the geodesic ball
B_{R}(p)
.

Finally, Lemma
2.4
upgrades this almost-everywhere equality to equality as domains. Thus
\Omega=B_{R}(p)
.
∎

4.
The spherical case

We realize
\mathbb{S}^{n}
as the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. For
E\subset\mathbb{S}^{n}
, write
-E=\{-x:x\in E\}
, where
x\mapsto-x
is the antipodal map.

Throughout this section,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{S}^{n}
is a connected smooth domain contained in an open hemisphere. Let
B_{R}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n}
be a geodesic ball such that
|B_{R}|=|\Omega|
. Since
\Omega
is contained in an open hemisphere,
0<R\leq\pi/2
. Put

\lambda=\mu_{1}(B_{R}),\qquad s(t)=\sin t,\qquad w(t)=s(t)^{n-1}.

About any point
p\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
, geodesic polar coordinates identify
\mathbb{S}^{n}\setminus\{p,-p\}
with
(0,\pi)\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and the metric is

(37)

ds_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}^{2}=dt^{2}+s(t)^{2}d\sigma^{2}.

The volume element is

dv=w(t)\,dt\,d\sigma.

4.1.
The first eigenfunctions of a spherical ball

Also, we shall use the following standard spectral fact for spherical caps of radius at most
\pi/2
.

Lemma 4.1

(First Neumann eigenspace of a spherical cap)
.

For
0<R\leq\pi/2
, the first nonzero Neumann eigenvalue of
B_{R}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n}
has multiplicity
n
, and its eigenspace is spanned by functions

u_{i}(t,\xi)=f(t)\xi_{i},\qquad i=1,\dots,n,

where
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and where
f
is the regular solution of

(38)

f^{\prime\prime}+(n-1)\cot t\,f^{\prime}+\left(\lambda-\frac{n-1}{s(t)^{2}}\right)f=0,\qquad 0<t<R,

with boundary conditions

(39)

f(0)=0,\qquad f^{\prime}(R)=0.

After multiplication by a constant,
f
may be chosen so that

f(t)>0\qquad 0<t\leq R.

Moreover,

(40)

\lambda=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}\left(f^{\prime}(t)^{2}+(n-1)\frac{f(t)^{2}}{s(t)^{2}}\right)w(t)\,dt}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}f(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt},

and

(41)

f^{\prime}(t)>0\qquad 0<t<R.

Proof.

See Appendix
A
.
∎

We shall also use the elementary lower bound

(42)

\lambda\geq n,

with equality when
R=\pi/2
. Indeed, let
h(t)=\sin t
. Then
h
solves the same radial equation with eigenvalue
n
. Written in Sturm–Liouville form,

(wf^{\prime})^{\prime}+\left(\lambda-\frac{n-1}{s^{2}}\right)wf=0,\qquad(wh^{\prime})^{\prime}+\left(n-\frac{n-1}{s^{2}}\right)wh=0.

Subtracting the two equations after multiplication by
h
and
f
, respectively, gives

\bigl(w(hf^{\prime}-fh^{\prime})\bigr)^{\prime}+(\lambda-n)wfh=0.

Integrating from
0
to
R
and using
f^{\prime}(R)=0
,
h^{\prime}(R)=\cos R
, yields

(\lambda-n)\int_{0}^{R}w(t)f(t)\sin t\,dt=w(R)f(R)\cos R\geq 0.

The integral on the left is strictly positive, because
w(t)>0
,
f(t)>0
, and
\sin t>0
for
0<t<R
. This proves (
42
). If
R<\pi/2
, then the right-hand side is strictly positive, so
\lambda>n
. If
R=\pi/2
, the right-hand side is zero, hence
\lambda=n
; in this endpoint case the regular radial solution is proportional to
\sin t
.

Introduce the radial constants

(43)

A=\int_{0}^{R}f(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt,\qquad Q=\int_{0}^{R}f^{\prime}(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt,\qquad H=\int_{0}^{R}\frac{f(t)^{2}}{s(t)^{2}}w(t)\,dt.

By (
40
),

(44)

Q+(n-1)H=\lambda A.

Define the half-sphere Weinberger extension

(45)

F_{+}(t)=\begin{cases}f(t),&0\leq t\leq R,\\
f(R),&R\leq t\leq\pi/2.\end{cases}

Lemma 4.2
.

The function
F_{+}
is nondecreasing on
[0,\pi/2]
, and

t\longmapsto\frac{F_{+}(t)}{\sin t}

is nonincreasing on
(0,\pi/2]
.

Proof.

The monotonicity of
F_{+}
follows from (
41
) and its definition. On
[R,\pi/2]
, the quotient
F_{+}(t)/\sin t=f(R)/\sin t
is nonincreasing. It remains to consider
0<t<R
.

Set

\gamma(t)=f^{\prime}(t)-\cot t\,f(t).

The desired assertion on
(0,R)
is equivalent to
\gamma(t)\leq 0
. Since
f(t)=a_{0}t+O(t^{3})
near
0
for some
a_{0}>0
, we have
\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\gamma(t)=0
. If
R<\pi/2
, then
\gamma(R)=-\cot R\,f(R)<0
; if
R=\pi/2
, then
\gamma(R)=0
.

Suppose, for contradiction, that
\gamma
has a positive value. Then it attains a positive maximum at some
t_{1}\in(0,R)
. At this point
\gamma^{\prime}(t_{1})=0
. Combining this identity with (
38
) gives

n\cot t_{1}\,\gamma(t_{1})+(\lambda-n)f(t_{1})=0.

Since
0<t_{1}<R\leq\pi/2
,
\cot t_{1}>0
, and by (
42
) we have
\lambda\geq n
. The last displayed identity is impossible when
\gamma(t_{1})>0
. Hence
\gamma\leq 0
, and the proof is complete.
∎

Finally define the antipodally even radial profile

(46)

F(t)=F_{+}\bigl(\min\{t,\pi-t\}\bigr),\qquad 0\leq t\leq\pi.

Then

(47)

F(\pi-t)=F(t),\qquad F(0)=F(\pi)=0.

4.2.
A spherical Weinberger center

Lemma 4.3
.

There exists
p\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
such that, in geodesic polar coordinates centered at
p
,

(48)

\int_{\Omega}F(t)\xi\,dv=0\in T_{p}\mathbb{S}^{n}.

Equivalently, for every first spherical harmonic
\omega(\xi)=v\cdot\xi
on the equator of
p
,

\int_{\Omega}F(t)\omega(\xi)\,dv=0.

Proof.

Let

\Phi(\rho)=\int_{0}^{\rho}F(\tau)\,d\tau,\qquad J(q)=\int_{\Omega}\Phi(d(q,x))\,dv_{x},\qquad q\in\mathbb{S}^{n}.

Since
\mathbb{S}^{n}
is compact,
J
has a minimizer
p
.

Near the two exceptional radii one has

F(t)=O(t)\quad(t\downarrow 0),\qquad F(t)=O(\pi-t)\quad(t\uparrow\pi),

because
f(t)=a_{0}t+O(t^{3})
at the pole and
F(t)=F_{+}(\pi-t)
near the antipode. Consequently
\Phi(t)=O(t^{2})
as
t\downarrow 0
and
\Phi(\pi)-\Phi(t)=O((\pi-t)^{2})
as
t\uparrow\pi
. Hence
q\mapsto\Phi(d(q,x))
is differentiable at
q=p
for the exceptional points
x=p
and
x=-p
, with derivative equal to zero there.

For
x\neq p,-p
, the map
q\mapsto d(q,x)
is smooth in a neighbourhood of
q=p
. Writing
x=\exp_{p}(t\xi)
, the first variation formula for distance gives

\nabla_{p}d(p,x)=-\xi.

For all nonexceptional
x
, the directional derivative in direction
v\in T_{p}\mathbb{S}^{n}
is bounded in absolute value by
\|F\|_{\infty}|v|
. Since the exceptional points have zero volume and
\Omega
has finite measure, dominated convergence justifies differentiation under the integral sign. For every
v\in T_{p}\mathbb{S}^{n}
,

0=dJ_{p}(v)=-\left\langle\int_{\Omega}F(t)\xi\,dv,\,v\right\rangle.

This proves (
48
).
∎

4.3.
Antipodal folding and ray estimates

Let
p
be the center given by Lemma
4.3
. For
x=\exp_{p}(t\xi)
with
t\in(0,\pi)
, define

(49)

P_{i}(x)=F(t)\xi_{i},\qquad i=1,\dots,n,

and set
P_{i}(p)=P_{i}(-p)=0
. Near
p
, normal coordinates give
P_{i}(y)=a_{0}y_{i}+O(|y|^{3})
. Near
-p
, write
r=\pi-t
and use
F(t)=O(r)
; the functions are again locally Lipschitz in normal coordinates at
-p
. Away from
p
and
-p
the profile is piecewise smooth and is
C^{1}
across the radii
R
,
\pi/2
, and
\pi-R
because
f^{\prime}(R)=0
and the even reflection has zero derivative at
\pi/2
from both sides. Hence
P_{i}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
. By Lemma
4.3
, each
P_{i}
has zero mean over
\Omega
.

If
x=\exp_{p}(t\xi)
and
t>\pi/2
, then

-x=\exp_{p}((\pi-t)(-\xi)).

Using (
47
), we obtain

(50)

P_{i}(-x)=-P_{i}(x).

Lemma 4.4

(Antipodal folding)
.

Let

\Omega_{+}=\{x\in\Omega:d(p,x)<\pi/2\},\qquad\Omega_{-}=\{x\in\Omega:d(p,x)>\pi/2\},

where the equatorial slice
\Omega\cap\{d(p,x)=\pi/2\}
is discarded. Define

(51)

\widehat{\Omega}=\Omega_{+}\cup(-\Omega_{-})\subset B_{\pi/2}(p).

Then the folding map, equal to the identity on
\Omega_{+}
and to the antipodal map on
\Omega_{-}
, is injective and measure preserving up to null sets. In particular,

(52)

|\widehat{\Omega}|=|\Omega|=|B_{R}|.

Moreover, if
G
is any integrable density satisfying
G(-x)=G(x)
, then

\int_{\Omega}G\,dv=\int_{\widehat{\Omega}}G\,dv.

Consequently,

(53)

\int_{\Omega}P_{i}P_{j}\,dv=\int_{\widehat{\Omega}}P_{i}P_{j}\,dv,\qquad\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla P_{i},\nabla P_{j}\rangle\,dv=\int_{\widehat{\Omega}}\langle\nabla P_{i},\nabla P_{j}\rangle\,dv.

Proof.

The equatorial slice is contained in a smooth hypersurface and has zero
n
-dimensional measure. Since
\Omega
is contained in an open hemisphere, it contains no pair of antipodal points. Hence the folding map is injective after the equatorial null set is removed; otherwise two distinct points of
\Omega
would be antipodal. The identity and the antipodal map are isometries, so the folding map is measure preserving on the two pieces. This proves (
52
) and the integral identity for every antipodally invariant density
G
. Finally, (
50
) implies that
P_{i}P_{j}
is antipodally invariant. Since the antipodal map is an isometry and
P_{i}\circ A=-P_{i}
for the antipodal map
A
,
\langle\nabla P_{i},\nabla P_{j}\rangle
is also antipodally invariant. This proves (
53
).
∎

For
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, let

E_{\xi}=\{t\in(0,\pi/2):\exp_{p}(t\xi)\in\widehat{\Omega}\}

and

Y(\xi)=\int_{E_{\xi}}w(t)\,dt,\qquad Y_{R}=\int_{0}^{R}w(t)\,dt.

By Fubini’s theorem,
E_{\xi}
is measurable for a.e.
\xi
. By (
52
),

(54)

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}(Y(\xi)-Y_{R})\,d\sigma(\xi)=0.

Set

(55)

Z=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}(Y(\xi)-Y_{R})\,\xi\otimes\xi\,d\sigma(\xi).

Then

(56)

\tr Z=0.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
E\subset(0,\pi/2)
be measurable and set
y_{E}=\int_{E}w(t)\,dt
. Then

(57)

\displaystyle\int_{E}F_{+}(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt

\displaystyle\geq A+f(R)^{2}\,(y_{E}-Y_{R}),

(58)

\displaystyle\int_{E}\frac{F_{+}(t)^{2}}{s(t)^{2}}w(t)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq H+\frac{f(R)^{2}}{s(R)^{2}}\,(y_{E}-Y_{R}),

(59)

\displaystyle\int_{E}F_{+}^{\prime}(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq Q.

Proof.

This is the same tangent-line argument as in Corollary
3.4
, now on the finite interval
(0,\pi/2)
. Namely,
F_{+}^{2}
is nondecreasing, while
F_{+}^{2}/s^{2}
is nonincreasing by Lemma
4.2
. Applying Lemma
2.2
and using the convexity of

\Psi(y)=\int_{0}^{\tau(y)}F_{+}(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt

and the concavity of

\Theta(y)=\int_{0}^{\tau(y)}\frac{F_{+}(t)^{2}}{s(t)^{2}}w(t)\,dt

applied at
y=y_{E}
gives (
57
) and (
58
). When
R<\pi/2
, the supporting lines are the ordinary tangent lines at
Y_{R}
. When
R=\pi/2
,
Y_{R}=\mathcal{V}(\pi/2)
is the right endpoint of the interval of admissible

; for the convex function
\Psi
and the concave function
\Theta
, the left derivatives at this endpoint determine the required supporting lines, so the same inequalities remain valid. Finally,
F_{+}^{\prime}=f^{\prime}
on
(0,R)
and
F_{+}^{\prime}=0
on
(R,\pi/2)
, so (
59
) follows from nonnegativity of
F_{+}^{\prime\,2}w
.
∎

4.4.
Proof in the spherical case

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{S}^{n}
be a connected smooth domain contained in an open hemisphere, and let
B_{R}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n}
be a geodesic ball such that
|B_{R}|=|\Omega|
. Then

(60)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\mu_{j}(\Omega)}\geq\frac{n}{\mu_{1}(B_{R})}.

Equality holds if and only if
\Omega
is a geodesic ball of radius
R
.

Proof.

Let
p
be the spherical center from Lemma
4.3
. Define
P_{i}
by (
49
). These functions are linearly independent on
\Omega
. Indeed, if
P_{b}:=\sum_{i}b_{i}P_{i}
vanishes a.e. on
\Omega
, then by continuity it vanishes on the open set
\Omega
. For
b\neq 0
, the zero set of
P_{b}
outside the two points
p
and
-p
is contained in the totally geodesic hypersurface determined by
b\cdot\xi=0
, and hence has empty interior. Thus
b=0
. Define
\mathsf{M}_{ij}
and
\mathsf{K}_{ij}
as in (
2
). By Lemma
2.1
,

(61)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\mu_{j}(\Omega)}\geq\tr(\mathsf{K}^{-1}\mathsf{M}).

By Lemma
4.4
, specifically (
53
), the matrices
\mathsf{M}
and
\mathsf{K}
may be computed over the folded set
\widehat{\Omega}\subset B_{\pi/2}(p)
.

Let

(62)

a=\frac{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}{n}A,\qquad c=f(R)^{2},\qquad\eta=\frac{f(R)^{2}}{s(R)^{2}}.

On
\widehat{\Omega}
the profile is
F_{+}(t)
, so

\mathsf{M}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left(\int_{E_{\xi}}F_{+}(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt\right)\xi\otimes\xi\,d\sigma(\xi).

By (
57
),

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}

\displaystyle\succeq\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left(A+c(Y(\xi)-Y_{R})\right)\xi\otimes\xi\,d\sigma(\xi)

\displaystyle=A\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\xi\otimes\xi\,d\sigma+cZ=aI+cZ.

For the stiffness matrix, using the gradient decomposition in (
37
),

\displaystyle\mathsf{K}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left(\int_{E_{\xi}}F_{+}^{\prime}(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt\right)\xi\otimes\xi\,d\sigma(\xi)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left(\int_{E_{\xi}}\frac{F_{+}(t)^{2}}{s(t)^{2}}w(t)\,dt\right)(I-\xi\otimes\xi)\,d\sigma(\xi).

By (
58
) and (
59
),

\displaystyle\mathsf{K}

\displaystyle\preceq\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}Q\,\xi\otimes\xi\,d\sigma

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left(H+\eta(Y(\xi)-Y_{R})\right)(I-\xi\otimes\xi)\,d\sigma.

Using (
54
), (
55
), and

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\xi\otimes\xi\,d\sigma=\frac{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}{n}I,

we get

\displaystyle\mathsf{K}

\displaystyle\preceq\frac{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}{n}QI+\frac{(n-1)|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}{n}HI-\eta Z

\displaystyle=\frac{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}{n}\bigl(Q+(n-1)H\bigr)I-\eta Z.

By (
44
),

(63)

\mathsf{K}\preceq\lambda aI-\eta Z.

Thus the hypotheses of Lemma
2.3
are satisfied, and hence

(64)

\tr(\mathsf{K}^{-1}\mathsf{M})\geq\frac{n}{\lambda}.

Together with (
61
), this gives

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\mu_{j}(\Omega)}\geq\frac{n}{\lambda}=\frac{n}{\mu_{1}(B_{R})}.

If
\Omega
is a geodesic ball of radius
R\leq\pi/2
, then
\mu_{1}(\Omega)=\cdots=\mu_{n}(\Omega)=\lambda
, so equality holds.

Conversely, suppose equality holds in (
60
). Then equality holds in Lemma
2.3
, and therefore

(65)

Z=0,\qquad\mathsf{M}=aI,\qquad\mathsf{K}=\lambda aI.

In particular, equality holds in the stiffness comparison (
63
).

For a.e.
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, set

\displaystyle q(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{E_{\xi}}F_{+}^{\prime}(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt,

\displaystyle h(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{E_{\xi}}\frac{F_{+}(t)^{2}}{s(t)^{2}}w(t)\,dt,

\displaystyle\delta(\xi)

\displaystyle=Y(\xi)-Y_{R}.

By Corollary
4.5
,

\alpha(\xi):=Q-q(\xi)\geq 0,\qquad\beta(\xi):=H+\eta\delta(\xi)-h(\xi)\geq 0.

Subtracting the formula for
\mathsf{K}
from its upper bound gives

\lambda aI-\mathsf{K}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left[\alpha(\xi)\,\xi\otimes\xi+\beta(\xi)(I-\xi\otimes\xi)\right]d\sigma(\xi).

The left-hand side is zero, and the integrand is positive semidefinite. Taking traces gives

0=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left(\alpha(\xi)+(n-1)\beta(\xi)\right)d\sigma(\xi).

Since
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
, it follows that

\alpha(\xi)=0,\qquad\beta(\xi)=0\quad\text{for a.e. }\xi.

In particular,

\int_{E_{\xi}}F_{+}^{\prime}(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt=\int_{0}^{R}f^{\prime}(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt\quad\text{for a.e. }\xi.

Since
F_{+}^{\prime}=f^{\prime}
on
(0,R)
,
F_{+}^{\prime}=0
on
(R,\pi/2)
, and
f^{\prime}(t)>0
for
0<t<R
, it follows that

(0,R)\subset E_{\xi}\quad\text{up to }w(t)dt\text{-null sets, for a.e. }\xi.

Since
w(t)>0
for
t\in(0,\pi/2)
, these are equivalently one-dimensional Lebesgue null sets. Thus
Y(\xi)\geq Y_{R}
for a.e.
\xi
. By (
54
), the average of
Y(\xi)-Y_{R}
is zero, hence
Y(\xi)=Y_{R}
for a.e.
\xi
. Therefore

E_{\xi}=(0,R)\quad\text{up to }w(t)dt\text{-null sets, for a.e. }\xi,

and consequently

(66)

\widehat{\Omega}=B_{R}(p)\quad\text{up to a null set}.

Assume first that
R<\pi/2
. Then
B_{R}(p)
and
B_{R}(-p)
are disjoint open balls. We claim that

\Omega\subset B_{R}(p)\cup B_{R}(-p).

Indeed, assume that some
x\in\Omega
is outside this union. Since
\Omega
is open, and since the boundaries of
B_{R}(p)
and
B_{R}(-p)
are smooth hypersurfaces, a small neighbourhood of
x
inside
\Omega
contains a subset
U
of positive measure outside
B_{R}(p)\cup B_{R}(-p)
. The folding map is the identity on the northern side and the antipodal map on the southern side, hence it is measure preserving on each side of the equator. After discarding the equatorial null set, the folded image of
U
has positive measure and lies outside
B_{R}(p)
, contradicting (
66
). Thus the displayed inclusion holds.

Because the two balls are disjoint open sets and
\Omega
is connected,
\Omega
is contained in exactly one of them. If
\Omega\subset B_{R}(p)
, then (
66
) gives
|\Omega\triangle B_{R}(p)|=0
. If
\Omega\subset B_{R}(-p)
, then applying the antipodal map to (
66
) gives
|\Omega\triangle B_{R}(-p)|=0
. Lemma
2.4
now gives equality as domains. Hence
\Omega
is a geodesic ball.

Assume next that
R=\pi/2
. By hypothesis there is an open hemisphere
\mathcal{H}
such that
\Omega\subset\mathcal{H}
. Since
|\Omega|
equals the volume of a hemisphere and
\Omega\subset\mathcal{H}
, we have
|\mathcal{H}\setminus\Omega|=0
, hence
|\mathcal{H}\triangle\Omega|=0
. Lemma
2.4
, applied to
\mathcal{H}
and
\Omega
, gives
\Omega=\mathcal{H}
. Thus
\Omega
is a hemisphere, which is a geodesic ball of radius
\pi/2
.
∎

.

The hyperbolic assertion is Proposition

Appendix A
First Neumann eigenspaces of geodesic balls

We prove Lemmas
3.1
and

4.1
. The proof is by separation of variables and
elementary Sturm–Liouville comparison.

Let

s(t)=\begin{cases}\sinh t,&\text{in }\mathbb{H}^{n},\\
\sin t,&\text{in }\mathbb{S}^{n},\end{cases}\qquad c(t)=\frac{s^{\prime}(t)}{s(t)},\qquad w(t)=s(t)^{n-1}.

Thus
c^{\prime}(t)=-s(t)^{-2}
. In the spherical case below we assume

0<R\leq\pi/2
. In geodesic polar coordinates on a ball
B_{R}
,

ds^{2}=dt^{2}+s(t)^{2}d\sigma^{2},\qquad dv=w(t)\,dt\,d\sigma,

and

\Delta=\partial_{t}^{2}+(n-1)c(t)\partial_{t}+\frac{1}{s(t)^{2}}\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}.

Let
Y_{\ell,m}
,
m=1,\dots,d_{\ell}
, be spherical harmonics of degree

\ell
on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, normalized so that

-\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}Y_{\ell,m}=\alpha_{\ell}Y_{\ell,m},\qquad\alpha_{\ell}=\ell(\ell+n-2).

In particular,

\alpha_{0}=0,\qquad\alpha_{1}=n-1,\qquad d_{1}=n.

Separation of variables reduces the Neumann problem on
B_{R}
to the
one-dimensional problems

(67)

-(w\varphi^{\prime})^{\prime}+\frac{\alpha_{\ell}}{s^{2}}w\varphi=\nu w\varphi,\qquad\varphi^{\prime}(R)=0,

with the regularity condition at
t=0
. Equivalently,

\varphi^{\prime\prime}+(n-1)c\varphi^{\prime}+\left(\nu-\frac{\alpha_{\ell}}{s^{2}}\right)\varphi=0.

For fixed
\ell
, write the corresponding eigenvalues as

\nu_{\ell,1}\leq\nu_{\ell,2}\leq\cdots.

For
\ell=0
,
\nu_{0,1}=0
, with constant eigenfunction. By the
standard one-dimensional Sturm–Liouville theory for this regular-singular
endpoint problem, the first eigenvalue in each angular sector is simple in
the radial variable, and its radial eigenfunction has no zero in
(0,R)
.
Moreover a regular eigenfunction in the
\ell
-th sector has expansion

\varphi(t)=at^{\ell}+O(t^{\ell+2})\qquad(t\downarrow 0).

Lemma A.1
.

For a hyperbolic ball of any radius
R>0
, and for a spherical cap with

0<R\leq\pi/2
,

\nu_{1,1}<\nu_{0,2},\qquad\nu_{1,1}<\nu_{\ell,1}\quad\text{for every }\ell\geq 2.

Hence the first nonzero Neumann eigenvalue of
B_{R}
is
\nu_{1,1}
.

Proof.

For the comparison with higher angular sectors, let
\ell\geq 2
. Since

\alpha_{\ell}>\alpha_{1}=n-1
, for every admissible nonzero
\varphi
,

\mathcal{R}_{\ell}[\varphi]=\mathcal{R}_{1}[\varphi]+(\alpha_{\ell}-\alpha_{1})\frac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}\frac{\varphi^{2}}{s^{2}}w\,dt}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}\varphi^{2}w\,dt},

where

\mathcal{R}_{\ell}[\varphi]=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}\left(\varphi^{\prime 2}+\frac{\alpha_{\ell}}{s^{2}}\varphi^{2}\right)w\,dt}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}\varphi^{2}w\,dt}.

Taking
\varphi
to be the positive radial ground state in the

\ell
-th sector gives

\nu_{\ell,1}=\mathcal{R}_{\ell}[\varphi]>\mathcal{R}_{1}[\varphi]\geq\nu_{1,1}.

It remains to compare the degree-one sector with the radial sector. Let

g
be an eigenfunction for
\nu_{0,2}
. Then

g^{\prime\prime}+(n-1)cg^{\prime}+\nu_{0,2}g=0,\qquad g^{\prime}(0)=g^{\prime}(R)=0.

Set
h=g^{\prime}
. Since
g
is nonconstant,
h\not\equiv 0
. Differentiating
the equation for
g
and using
c^{\prime}=-s^{-2}
, we obtain

h^{\prime\prime}+(n-1)ch^{\prime}+\left(\nu_{0,2}-\frac{n-1}{s^{2}}\right)h=0.

Equivalently,

-(wh^{\prime})^{\prime}+\frac{n-1}{s^{2}}wh=\nu_{0,2}wh.

Moreover
h(R)=g^{\prime}(R)=0
, and the regularity at the pole gives

h(t)=O(t)
. Multiplying the last equation by
h
, integrating by parts,
and using the vanishing of the boundary terms at
0
and
R
, we get

\int_{0}^{R}\left(h^{\prime 2}+\frac{n-1}{s^{2}}h^{2}\right)w\,dt=\nu_{0,2}\int_{0}^{R}h^{2}w\,dt.

Therefore

\nu_{1,1}\leq\mathcal{R}_{1}[h]=\nu_{0,2}.

If equality held, then
h
would minimize the degree-one Rayleigh quotient.
Hence
h
would satisfy the natural Neumann boundary condition

h^{\prime}(R)=0.

Together with
h(R)=0
, this gives zero Cauchy data for a nonsingular ODE
at
t=R
. Thus
h\equiv 0
, a contradiction. Hence

\nu_{1,1}<\nu_{0,2}.

The first nonzero Neumann eigenvalue is therefore
\nu_{1,1}
.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
f
be the positive radial ground state in the degree-one sector:

f^{\prime\prime}+(n-1)cf^{\prime}+\left(\nu_{1,1}-\frac{n-1}{s^{2}}\right)f=0,\qquad f(0)=0,\qquad f^{\prime}(R)=0.

Then, in the hyperbolic case for every
R>0
, and in the spherical case
for
0<R\leq\pi/2
,

f^{\prime}(t)>0\qquad 0<t<R.

Proof.

Put

\lambda=\nu_{1,1},\qquad m(t)=w(t)f^{\prime}(t).

The equation for
f
gives

m^{\prime}(t)=\left(\frac{n-1}{s(t)^{2}}-\lambda\right)w(t)f(t).

Also

m(0)=0,\qquad m(R)=0.

Since
f>0
and
w>0
on
(0,R)
, the sign of
m^{\prime}
is the sign of

\rho(t)=\frac{n-1}{s(t)^{2}}-\lambda.

In the hyperbolic case
s(t)=\sinh t
, and in the spherical case with

R\leq\pi/2
,
s(t)=\sin t
is strictly increasing on
(0,R)
. Hence

\rho
is strictly decreasing.

We claim that
\rho(R)<0
. If
\rho(R)\geq 0
, then
\rho(t)>0
for
all
0<t<R
. Hence
m^{\prime}(t)>0
on
(0,R)
, which contradicts

m(0)=m(R)=0
. Thus
\rho(R)<0
. Since
\rho(t)\to+\infty
as

t\downarrow 0
, there is a unique
\tau\in(0,R)
such that

\rho(\tau)=0
. Therefore

m^{\prime}>0\text{ on }(0,\tau),\qquad m^{\prime}<0\text{ on }(\tau,R).

It follows that
m(t)>0
on
(0,\tau]
. For
\tau<t<R
,

m(t)=-\int_{t}^{R}m^{\prime}(r)\,dr>0.

Thus
m(t)>0
for every
0<t<R
. Since
w(t)>0
, we obtain

f^{\prime}(t)=\frac{m(t)}{w(t)}>0\qquad 0<t<R.

∎

Proof of Lemmas
3.1

and
4.1
.

By Lemma
A.1
, the first nonzero
Neumann eigenvalue of
B_{R}
is the first eigenvalue in the degree-one
sector:

\mu_{1}(B_{R})=\nu_{1,1}.

The radial ground state in this sector is simple, while the degree-one
spherical harmonics on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
have dimension
n
and are spanned by
the coordinate functions

\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}.

Therefore the whole first nonzero Neumann eigenspace is

\spanop\{f(t)\xi_{1},\dots,f(t)\xi_{n}\}.

Thus the multiplicity is
n
, and the eigenfunctions have the form

u_{i}(t,\xi)=f(t)\xi_{i},\qquad i=1,\dots,n.

In the hyperbolic case
s(t)=\sinh t
and
c(t)=\coth t
, so the radial
equation is

f^{\prime\prime}+(n-1)\coth t\,f^{\prime}+\left(\lambda-\frac{n-1}{\sinh^{2}t}\right)f=0.

In the spherical case
s(t)=\sin t
and
c(t)=\cot t
, so the radial
equation is

f^{\prime\prime}+(n-1)\cot t\,f^{\prime}+\left(\lambda-\frac{n-1}{\sin^{2}t}\right)f=0.

In both cases, regularity at the pole gives
f(0)=0
, the Neumann boundary
condition gives
f^{\prime}(R)=0
, and the ground-state property gives

f(t)>0\qquad 0<t\leq R.

By Lemma
A.2
,

f^{\prime}(t)>0\qquad 0<t<R.

Finally, multiplying the radial equation

(wf^{\prime})^{\prime}+\left(\lambda-\frac{n-1}{s^{2}}\right)wf=0

by
f
, integrating over
(0,R)
, and using the regularity at
0
and
the boundary condition
f^{\prime}(R)=0
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{R}\left(f^{\prime}(t)^{2}+(n-1)\frac{f(t)^{2}}{s(t)^{2}}\right)w(t)\,dt=\lambda\int_{0}^{R}f(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt.

Hence

\lambda=\frac{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}\left(f^{\prime}(t)^{2}+(n-1)\frac{f(t)^{2}}{s(t)^{2}}\right)w(t)\,dt}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}f(t)^{2}w(t)\,dt}.

This proves both Lemma
3.1
and Lemma

4.1
.
∎
