Executive Summary
Load growth at the grid edge is driving increased attention to the distribution system and its
ability to enable customer technology adoption in an affordable and timely manner. Key
industry stakeholders, including electric utilities and regulators, can benefit from strategies to
manage and balance customer needs with infrastructure investments, such as demand
flexibility.

This report focuses on demand flexibility—the ability to reduce, shift, shed, generate, or
modulate loads in response to building and grid needs—to reduce the need for costly grid
upgrades by deferring investment needs and increase system reliability by shifting electricity
usage during periods of high risk. Specifically, we focus on the emerging characteristics of
business models for demand flexibility as a framework to understand how demand flexibility
programs generate value.

In this report, we focus on the life cycle of demand flexibility programs, which provides
information on value creation and describes the various implementation phases program
implementers navigate from initial program conceptualization through to program expansion
and replication to new customer segments and regions. This report characterizes the key
phases of the demand flexibility program life cycle, identifies existing challenges across the
program implementation phases, and describes lessons learned.

Key phases of the demand flexibility program life cycle

Program implementers navigate a series of phases to transform a program's initial concept into
a fully operational offering. Figure ES-1 provides an overview of the various phases across the
life cycle, including scaling activities informed by program implementation.

Challenges across program life cycle phases include:
 • Uncertainty about the impact of program design decisions (e.g., technology package
 implemented) on operations and scalability
 • Ensuring interoperability across multiple systems and devices operating across layers
 (e.g., interactions between building-side systems and utility-side systems, including
 Distributed Energy Resources Management Systems)

 • Developing customer relationship management practices that ensure continued
 program participation
 • Understanding which customer classes to prioritize for program scaling efforts

Life cycle lessons learned

Lessons learned to support program implementation and scaling include:
 • Developing contingency plans for delays in key program implementation activities
 • Designing program participation agreements to enable data collection over the life of the
 program
 • Aligning program design with state and regulatory priorities, such as those established

 • Leveraging the capabilities and resources of strategic partners to support scaling efforts

1. Introduction
Significant load growth is being driven by technology adoption at the grid edge, including
electric vehicles and high-performance heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC), as
well as larger loads such as data centers supporting artificial intelligence, and the increasing
electricity needs of the industrial sector (Pereira et al., 2025). Recent North American Electric
Reliability Council (NERC) estimates, released in 2024, show load growth is expected to lead to
a 15% increase in summer peak demand and 18% winter peak demand, over the next 10 years
for the U.S. and Canada (NERC, 2024). The pace and scale of expected load growth are
leading to increased attention on the electricity sector infrastructure and investments needed to
ensure it can provide service to customers in a reliable and affordable manner. At the grid
edge, distribution systems play a key role as the interface between customer loads and the
grid. Investments in distribution systems have increased in recent years and currently account
for the largest share of costs for U.S. investor-owned utilities. For instance, data from the
Edison Electric Institute for 2024 indicate that distribution system capital expenditures account
for 32% of total capital expenditures (EEI, 2024). This further emphasizes the need for
strategies that can enable additional load while managing the impact on affordability, such as
managing flexible loads through demand flexibility (EFI Foundation, 2024; Goldenberg et al.,
2018).

Demand flexibility is the ability of building electricity loads to change in response to building and
grid needs and objectives. Demand flexibility can drive affordability improvements by shifting
electricity consumption out of high-price periods, as well as by reducing the need for costly
infrastructure upgrades. Affordability benefits for customers result from a more cost-effective
use of utility infrastructure, which reduces the need for additional investments, such as
increased capacity to meet higher peak loads. Utility benefits derive from the ability to allocate
resources more effectively by being able to reduce, defer, or fully mitigate the need for
infrastructure upgrades (Schwartz & Leventis, 2020). Demand flexibility can cost-effectively
support grid reliability by reducing electricity demand in periods of high risk (Fox-Penner et al.,
2025; Jackson et al., 2021; LBNL, 2025; NERC, 2024; Norris et al., 2025; Pereira et al., 2025;
U.S. Department of Energy, 2024).

Building demand flexibility can be enabled by behind-the-meter distributed energy resources
(DERs) and results from responses to price-based signals (e.g., dedicated time-of-use rate
structures to incentivize customer behavior) or grid-based signals (e.g., grid operator requests
change load behavior to manage and mitigate operational challenges) aimed at delivering
benefits to the building occupants, owners, and the grid (Satchwell et al., 2021). DERs, in
combination with building controls, can deliver demand flexibility by reducing, shedding,
shifting, modulating, or generating load (See Table 1).

Recent studies have estimated the significant potential of demand flexibility to reduce system
costs and improve affordability (Murphy et al., 2024). For instance, the U.S. Department of
Energy estimated that demand flexibility could deliver system benefits worth $8-18 billion
annually by 2030 (Satchwell et al., 2021). Similarly, the Rocky Mountain Institute estimated that
demand flexibility from air conditioning and water heating could result in a ~8% decrease in
peak demand in the U.S. in the residential sector alone, valued at $13 billion per year (Bronski
et al., 2017). The Brattle Group estimated that cost-effective demand flexibility could reach
~20% of the U.S. peak load, resulting in $15 billion in annual benefits by 2030 (Hledik et al.,
2019).

In addition to the benefits and potential of demand flexibility, key industry stakeholders,
including state and utility decision-makers, are prioritizing demand flexibility. For example, a
recent survey by the Association of Energy Service Professionals found that 93% of the utilities
surveyed considered demand flexibility a medium-to-high priority for their business. Moreover,
49% of the utilities in the study indicated that they plan to implement a demand flexibility
program within the next three years (AESP, 2024). Similarly, an analysis by the Smart Electric
Power Alliance and the North Carolina Clean Energy Technology Center in 2024 found that 38
states took action to advance virtual power plants and support DERs, which can contribute to
demand flexibility (SEPA, 2025a). These actions include utilities proposing new or expanding
existing programs (e.g., demand response programs, electric vehicle managed charging

programs, and multi-technology programs), and commissions and legislatures developing
frameworks and assessing the role of existing policies.

In this context, business models play an important role in ensuring that the significant demand
flexibility potential is realized (Li et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2021). Business models offer a
conceptual framework to understand how demand flexibility programs generate value to
customers, program implementers, and other key stakeholders and partners. Specifically,
business models provide insights into how program implementers (e.g., utilities, aggregators,
third-party program managers, etc.) create, deliver, and capture value (see Table 2). Value
creation and delivery encompass the decisions, resources, and processes program
implementers use to ensure that customers effectively benefit from program participation. Value
capture encompasses program implementer decisions related to implementation and operation
costs and program revenues. Collectively, these elements provide information relevant to
understanding how demand flexibility program implementers combine technologies and
capabilities in innovative offerings that generate value, including increased affordability and
reliability (Hamwi et al., 2021; Teece, 2010; Zott & Amit, 2010).

2. The key phases of the demand flexibility program
 life cycle
Implementing demand flexibility requires effective management throughout a program’s life
cycle, including planning and design, implementation, operation, and scaling activities. Figure 1
illustrates the demand flexibility program life cycle.

Figure 1. Demand flexibility program life cycle

Planning and design: This includes activities related to conceptualizing and bringing together
all the elements to support demand flexibility program delivery. In this phase, program
implementers establish goals, select target customers, and develop value propositions for the
program. This can include identifying a group of partners with the capabilities and resources
needed to deliver the program. This phase also includes designing demand flexibility
technology packages and selecting cost-effective procurement channels. Program
implementers may develop custom solutions for novel applications or opt to utilize existing
technologies. This phase is designed to clarify how customers interact with the program’s
technologies and partners, shaping the customer journey. In some cases, regulatory approval
may be necessary, which can also be part of program planning and design.

Implementation: This phase brings program plans to the market. Program implementers
launch marketing and outreach efforts during this phase to engage and recruit customers. As
customers sign up to participate, program technologies are installed, and customers are
informed about their role and associated benefits as program participants. Program
implementers may encounter challenges during implementation that can require revisiting the
program design and the expected value proposition. For example, this could be due to changes
in the technologies available compared to those selected during the planning and design
phase.

Operation: This phase encompasses all activities necessary once the program is running.
Program implementers monitor performance against initial targets and program and customer
evaluation protocols. This may include collecting data and issuing reports to partners and
stakeholders, such as regulatory bodies. This phase also focuses on ensuring a positive
customer experience. This may require solving technical issues with technologies installed in
customer buildings, which may involve coordinating customers and contractors. During
operations, program implementers may identify issues and challenges that need resolution in
the near term or that can inform future programs and scaling efforts.

          Business Models for Scaling Demand Flexibility – Volume IV: Program Life Cycle | 6

Scaling: This phase encompasses activities that support program growth, including
transitioning from a pilot program to a full-scale offering, replicating program delivery in other
regions, and expanding program offerings to additional customer classes. Scaling involves
assessing previous experiences across planning and design, implementation, and operations to
identify lessons learned that can be incorporated into future programs. Program implementers
can inform future technology package decisions based on previous experience with technology
performance, cost-effectiveness, and customer experience. This phase can also involve
incorporating feedback and insights from partners and customers, as well as program
evaluation results, to inform future programs.

For each phase in the life cycle, program implementers need to effectively manage partners
and resources to deliver successful programs with clear value propositions for customers and
benefits for collaborating partners. Additionally, as demand flexibility programs progress
through their life cycle, stakeholder roles and responsibilities evolve, necessitating ongoing
efforts to ensure alignment and clarity on program implementation priorities. Similarly, within
each phase, different levels of maturity for demand flexibility activities can impact the scope of
each phase of a program’s life cycle. See Text Box 1 for a discussion on demand flexibility
maturity for key program capabilities.

3. Challenges
Through our analysis, we identified challenges associated with navigating the life cycle of the
demand flexibility program. We identified challenges related to program planning,
implementation, operation, and scaling.

3.1 Planning and design
Long lead time between program planning and implementation, such as for new
construction programs. Demand flexibility programs may involve a multi-year lag from
program conceptualization to implementation. This can impact the quality of the program and
the effectiveness of the installed technologies. For instance, technologies selected during
program design may become outdated by the time they are installed, especially in cases where
implementation is significantly delayed. This challenge is particularly relevant for new
construction programs, where delays or uncertainty in building developer schedules can impact
demand flexibility, technology installation, and program starting dates. Programs targeting
commercial loads may also need to plan for delays in implementation, resulting from the longer
time needed to conduct outreach and recruitment of commercial customers (CEE, 2021).

One of the program implementers interviewed focused on demand flexibility programs for new
construction, shared the challenge of long construction timelines for new homes. In one case,
the program implementer had to reassess the technologies initially selected for a residential
development that took over five years to complete. This added a layer of complexity to program
delivery and increased the resources needed for planning and design.

Changes in partners and the availability of technology can impact the program’s vision
and goals. The initial vision for a demand flexibility program may need to be adjusted due to
gaps in existing capabilities and resources. For example, this can result from structural
changes in program partners’ priorities or cost-effectiveness concerns identified during the
assessment of technologies to include in the program. Resulting changes to the program’s
vision and goals may reduce the value proposition for customers and partners, as well as
require program implementers to identify new program partners to deliver the program.

Visibility into the impact of certain program design decisions on operations and
scalability. Program implementers and their partners may lack sufficient expertise to
understand the impact of design decisions, such as technologies included in the program. For
example, one of the program implementers interviewed had challenges understanding the
benefits of cellular communications over WI-FI for their program. Initially, they selected WI-FI
due to their familiarity from previous applications. However, during operations, WI-FI resulted in
performance issues and the team had to transition to cellular devices.

Resources needed for effective planning. Program implementers may be responsible for
implementing innovative technologies and testing new stakeholder collaborations or customer
journeys. This can bring uncertainty to planning. In some cases, it may be unclear whether the
time and budget estimates are sufficient to complete planning and design activities. This can
impact cost-effectiveness and collaborations with program partners, ultimately impacting

program success and scaling prospects. For example, one of the program implementers
interviewed initially estimated that significant time and resources would be needed to develop
algorithms supporting the program’s transactive market design. However, they estimated that
less effort would be needed to develop customer interfaces, which ultimately required greater
time and resources than expected during the planning phase.

3.2 Implementation
Technology installation complexity. Technology installation can be a lengthy process.
Program implementers may need to obtain permits or submit interconnection applications
before installation (e.g., for distributed energy resources). This can increase costs and add
uncertainty to the implementation timeline. One of the program implementers shared the
challenge of finding qualified contractors who understand all the technologies included in their
technology package, consisting of distributed solar, storage, electric vehicle charging, and
microgrids.

Changes in regulatory requirements. Commission decisions can impact program design and
proposed value propositions, for the customer and the range of stakeholders engaged in
program delivery, for programs that require regulatory approval. Program implementers may
need to adapt their plans, which may sometimes change program goals, including eligible
technologies and targeted customer segments.

In other cases, implementing new regulatory requirements or codes can bring uncertainty to
program delivery. One of the program implementers shared their experience with successive
delays from their state to implement a standard to enable two-way communication between
devices (e.g., water heaters and HVAC systems) and the distribution grid. These delays
impacted the program’s technology selection process.

Insurance practices can impact the installation of technology. Demand flexibility programs
can be designed in ways that go beyond insurers' established risk underwriting practices. For
example, one of the program implementers interviewed was not able to obtain insurance
coverage to include batteries in a living space. The insurer found that the risk of fire could not

the unique needs of each customer class and the technologies needed to deliver demand

of interest from home renters while also designing a program that allows for a longer decision-
making process for commercial customers. In some cases, program implementers may also
need to consider the needs of customer subclasses. In the residential class, home renters may
lack interest due to split incentives, while home buyers may lack the interest or bandwidth to
incorporate additional information into their buying process.

3.3 Operation
Loss of connectivity. Program participants can get disconnected during program operation.
For instance, participants can lose WI-FI connection. Connectivity issues impact program
operations, as implementers lose control over participant technologies and are unable to gather
data. In some cases, these issues may be solved by program participants. However, program
implementers may need to send a technician on site to provide support for more complex
issues or when customers are unable to resolve the issue.

Data collection. Data collection supports program operations and is crucial for evaluating
program performance and calculating program metrics. Ensuring that the data collected is
accurate, complete, and obtained in a timely manner to meet operational, evaluation, and
reporting requirements can be a challenge.

Program participant continuity. Program participants who join the program during its
implementation phase may later choose to abandon it. For example, renters or homeowners
may move during the program's operations phase. Once new residents move in, they may not
be aware of the technologies installed, or the value proposition may not align with their needs
and values. Similarly, programs requiring customer response to demand flexibility events may
see a reduction in customer engagement over time as a result of customer fatigue
(Prakobkaew & Sirisumrannukul, 2025). This can lower the program’s demand flexibility
potential and reduce the value proposition for remaining customers and benefits for
stakeholders contributing to program delivery. See the report in this series focused on customer

         Business Models for Scaling Demand Flexibility – Volume IV: Program Life Cycle | 12

relationship management for lessons learned to improve program customer interaction,
relevant for participation continuity.

Changes in utility rates. New rate structures may be implemented throughout program
operations, which can impact customers' willingness to engage in demand flexibility. For
example, new rates can reduce the value of shifting consumption or alter the period applicable
to existing rates (e.g., modifying the time windows in time-of-use rates). These changes may
lead to the program’s perceived value no longer aligning with participant preferences.

Uncertainty about the performance of technology packages in the field. Program
implementers may face uncertainty regarding the actual performance of the technologies
included in the program. Programs encompassing multiple technologies often leverage
modelling analyses to inform potential outcomes expected during program operations.
However, modeling assumptions and data inputs may miss customer, building, or utility
characteristics that ultimately affect program operations. Similarly, even when the best
available inputs and assumptions are used, modeling tools and techniques may have
limitations that result in different outcomes during operations.

Lack of experience with demand flexibility enabling systems (e.g., DERMS). Program
implementers may need to develop or procure new systems to manage distributed energy
resources (e.g., distributed energy resource management systems – DERMS). Introducing a
new system carries the risk of designing it and implementing it in a way that does not fully
address program needs during the operational phase. One of the program implementers
interviewed shared that as a utility, they are still learning how to use DERMS and maximize the
value of available functionalities to deliver demand flexibility programs (SEPA, 2025b).

3.4 Scaling
Value of custom-developed technologies. Program implementers may need to develop
custom systems or dedicated technology packages to enable the program’s vision and goals.
Custom developments can require significant resources and time while also carrying the risk
related to their long-term value, such as their applicability and ability to scale. For instance,
custom solutions may lack functionalities or flexibility to introduce future use cases or support a
larger number of customers and devices. One of the program implementers interviewed shared
their challenge of developing technologies that can be easily replicated to scale programs.

Suitability of data collection and management practices. Demand flexibility programs
require comprehensive data collection and management practices. Requirements and
processes developed to ensure data is collected with sufficient granularity and in an
appropriate time frame may vary over time and necessitate adaptation as a result of new
program offerings, customer needs, or to mitigate issues identified during program operations.

Program cost-effectiveness at scale. Program implementers can lack visibility on the cost-
effectiveness of scaling efforts. For instance, changes to variable (e.g., installed technologies,
recruitment) and fixed costs (e.g., program administration, data warehousing) may be difficult to

estimate, resulting in uncertainty. Similarly, vendors supplying key program services and
technologies may adjust their pricing over time, which can impact program economics. One of
the program implementers interviewed expressed concern about the cost of metering for future
scaling efforts, particularly the expense associated with LTE or Wi-Fi extenders to ensure
connectivity.

Priority customer classes for scaling efforts. Program implementers may need to prioritize
customer classes when scaling demand flexibility offers. In these cases, it can be challenging
to ensure sufficient expertise to address the needs of the customers in the class selected. For
example, one program implementer interviewed shared that their capabilities are more aligned
to the needs of commercial customers, and they plan to prioritize commercial customers in
future scaling efforts. To prioritize residential customers, they recognize they would need to find
a partner to help them engage those customers effectively.

4. Lessons learned
The list below highlights lessons learned related to demand flexibility program life cycle,
informed by program implementer experiences.

4.1 Planning and design
Adapt programs to implement multiple technologies. Program implementers interested in
adapting existing programs to further enable demand flexibility may need to build new
processes and expertise to implement changes. For example, selecting new technologies to
incorporate (e.g., DERs, building controls) involves gathering data on their potential
performance, or, in some cases, developing new evaluation protocols to measure program
outcomes like cost-effectiveness. In other cases, program implementers may need to develop
new internal capabilities to operate new programs. Regulatory processes and local utility
objectives may also shape the structure and evolution of demand flexibility programs, including
technology selection, customer delivery, and pathway to scale. Text Box 3 describes steps that
utilities with existing programs can take to transition to offer demand flexibility programs.

Include site visits in program plans. Program implementers can leverage site audits to
mitigate unforeseen challenges during implementation activities, particularly for programs
pursuing novel approaches and implementing multiple technologies. Site audits can allow
program implementers to identify building space limitations and understand the presence of any
complementary or redundant building systems when considering technologies to be installed as
part of the program. One of the program implementers interviewed shared the value of site
audits as an important source of information on building conditions to guide technology
package design and implementation activities.

Identify insurance requirements for all the technologies and buildings in the program.
Program implementers can engage with insurers early in the design process to understand how
existing risk underwriting practices relate to each technology and building type. For example,
this can allow the program implementer to identify early on the types of certifications required
for battery storage systems, or the requirements for spacing between storage assets and
building units or between storage assets (Newland, 2024). Additionally, program implementers
can include strategies to minimize battery fire risk, such as following best practices in battery
cooling techniques and collaborating with technology providers to understand effective risk
mitigation techniques (U.S. Fire Administration, 2025).

Develop contingency plans for delays in key program implementation activities. Program
implementers can assess their program plan to identify key activities that are likely to be
affected by delays. Experience and industry practices can inform contingency plans and
timeline decisions to ensure program implementers design plans that create appropriate
expectations for all partners involved. For example, one of the program implementers shared
that whenever they design a demand flexibility program for new construction, they consider a
five-year program implementation timeline to account for typical delays in building construction.

Benchmark demand flexibility industry performance to understand implementation
timelines. Program implementers can reduce the uncertainty related to program progress and
potential challenges to delivery timelines by conducting market research focused on gathering
evidence from previous demand flexibility programs focused on similar customer segments,
building types, and technology packages. This can provide program implementers with
important information to develop program implementation timelines. For example, market
research may show the different timelines necessary to conduct education and outreach for
residential and commercial customers. It may also show different timelines associated with
building retrofit deliver, which may be dependent on contractor availability, and with building
construction, for programs targeting new construction.

4.2 Implementation
Align customer participation starting time. Program implementers can align program
participation starting time to the extent possible to enable a larger volume of participant data to
be collected to support program evaluation activities. For example, by aligning starting times,
program implementers can run comparisons across participating buildings to understand
program performance.

Design participation agreements to support data collection. Customer participation
agreements can impact the ability of program implementers to collect participation data.
Participation agreements can include a sunset date sufficiently far into the future to allow for
sufficient data collection, which can include the collection of device data, as well as customer
participation surveys to assess satisfaction and inform future programs.

Partner with stakeholders that can support program recruitment. Program implementers
can identify partners with portfolios of resources to participate in the program. This may include
property managers with installed EV chargers or thermostats. This can accelerate
implementation efforts and ease some of the effort and challenges associated with participant
recruitment.

Alignment between program priorities and state goals and commission requirements.
Alignment between program and state goals can facilitate implementation activities. For
example, a state with policy goals that identify demand flexibility as a resource to manage load
growth may contribute to a stronger network of contractors and vendors who are interested in
operating in the demand flexibility space, which can support implementation activities.

Work with contractors who can provide services to multiple buildings. General
contractors can contribute to increased efficiency when installing program technologies. For
example, general contractors will often have knowledge of other trade professionals needed to
support installation activities, such as electricians and HVAC technicians.

For example, one of the program implementers interviewed shared their perspective on the
benefits of working with a general contractor. In this case, they are leveraging a single general
contractor to work across multiple buildings that are being upgraded with similar technologies.
The alternative would have been to issue an RFP for each building being upgraded, which
would likely result in increased complexity from having to manage various contractors in
parallel.

Develop customized outreach materials for prospective program participants. Program
implementers can identify the information needs of different customer classes and subclasses
to increase communication effectiveness and support program recruitment. For example, for a
program targeting residential customers, program implementers may benefit by developing
messaging that addresses the concerns of renters while also identifying the messaging that is
most effective with homeowners.

4.3 Operation
Prioritize participants for engagement in demand flexibility events. Program implementers
running events as part of their program design can develop prioritization criteria to select the
participants or groups of participants that can more effectively deliver flexibility to address grid
needs. For example, if multiple participating customers are served by common infrastructure
(e.g., a group of buildings or units served by a chilled water loop), it may not be possible to
precool all the buildings at the same time. See the report in this series focused on customer
relationship management for lessons learned to improve program customers' interaction,
relevant to enabling a positive customer experience and participation in demand flexibility
events.

4.4 Scaling
Leverage strategic partners to support growth. Program implementers can identify partners
with resources and capabilities that are well-suited for scaling. For example, a non-utility
program implementer interviewed discussed the value of partnering with utilities to leverage
their capabilities for program scaling. In their case, they see the utility’s established connection
with customers as beneficial. This can also contribute to avoiding duplication of efforts; for
example, utilities have established grid operations and customer billing capabilities that can be
valuable for scaling demand flexibility.

Informed customers contribute to demand flexibility market growth. Demand flexibility
market development efforts are benefitting from a trend of customers generally having a greater
understanding of energy technologies and the benefits of participating in a demand flexibility
program. For example, one of the program implementers interviewed shared that they see
customers as increasingly more informed when it comes to building energy technologies,
including distributed storage and solar, compared to their historical experience engaging
customers in previous programs.

Shared customer relationship management can enable growth. Scaling demand flexibility
programs can benefit from a shared customer relationship approach (See section X). This
approach can contribute to ensuring that stakeholders are responsible for the program activities
and customer segments that best align with their capabilities. For instance, for a utility-led
program the utility may choose to manage relationships with large commercial customers,
leveraging existing relationships and account managers expertise. In this case, the utility may
opt to partner with a third-party organization experienced with residential sector demand
flexibility programs to communicate with residential customers. For example, one of the
program implementers interviewed shared that they see an opportunity for a less centralized
approach where trade allies are responsible for some aspects of customer relationship
management to support scaling.
