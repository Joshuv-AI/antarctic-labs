Enhancing ZTMAF through federated trust learning, post quantum cryptography integration and seamless cross domain authentication handover

Jeevan Yoganand, Riya Bansal V, Dishal L S, Jeipratha P N

Abstract

Vehicular Fog Computing (VFC) has a major role to play in facilitating low-latency and real-time communication in Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS). Nevertheless, there exist major issues in trust management, security, and cross-domain authentication, considering the dynamic nature of vehicular networks. The Zero-Trust Mobility-Aware Authentication Framework (ZTMAF) has been proposed to overcome issues in trust management, security, and cross-domain authentication. Nevertheless, there exist major issues in distributed intelligence, trust transmission, and handover, which have been addressed in this proposed framework. The proposed framework extends ZTMAF with three major advancements: Federated Trust Learning, PQC-inspired Secure Transmission, and Cross-Domain Authentication Handover. The proposed framework uses fog nodes to train a trust model in a distributed setting, allowing for scalability and privacy preservation. Additionally, Post-Quantum Cryptography inspired encryption would be utilized for secure trust transmission, as well as to remain compliant with future post-quantum encryption standards. A token-based handover mechanism will allow vehicles to make use of previously verified trust, thus lowering latency in cross-domain authentication. The proposed framework has been validated via multiple simulation scenarios, and its performance has been evaluated against different types of attacks (e.g., stealth, on/off, Sybil). The results of the evaluations show that trust stability has improved, that detection of behavior-based attacks has improved, and that the use of the token-based handover mechanism has reduced the authentication delay by 45%. The proposed framework represents a secure and scalable solution for next-generation vehicular networks, and it sheds light on the issues associated with detecting Sybil attacks as part of the process.

Introduction

With the integration of vehicular networks and Fog Computing, highly sophisticated Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS) can now be developed with the need for real-time communication, low latency, and high reliability (; ). Vehicular Fog Computing (VFC) enables the offloading of computations and communication with the surrounding Fog Nodes by the vehicles, which can be used in various applications like avoiding collisions and traffic management and providing autonomous driving assistance(); (). However, the high mobility of the vehicles and the lack of connectivity and heterogeneous trust domains are the significant security challenges ().

Traditional methods of authentication, like PKI and Blockchain-based systems, are facing challenges in the VFC environment due to the high computational overhead and the latency caused by the consensus mechanism of the Blockchain system. To overcome the challenges of traditional methods of authentication in the VFC environment, the proposed Zero-Trust Mobility Aware Authentication Framework (ZTMAF) uses a zero-trust model in which every node is continuously authenticated on the basis of contextual and behavioral characteristics.

Despite the advantages offered by ZTMAF, the framework has some limitations when used in vehicular networks. Firstly, the framework is limited to localized trust computation. This limits the framework's ability to use knowledge shared by more than one fog node. Secondly, the framework does not provide strong protection for the transmission of trust values. Thirdly, vehicles have to undergo repeated authentication when they enter a new fog domain. Fourthly, the evaluations performed on the framework are limited to basic attacks and do not completely show the effects of more complex attacks, such as stealth and on–off attacks.

To address the limitations of ZTMAF, this paper presents an improved framework based on federated learning, secure transmission of trust values, and reuse of authentication in multiple fog domains. The framework is based on Federated Trust Learning. With Federated Trust Learning, fog nodes will be able to collaboratively learn trust models using logistic regression. The nodes will use federated averaging to update the learned models.

For ensuring secure communication, a PQC-inspired encryption layer is added to the system, ensuring that trust values are securely transmitted. Although a full-fledged post-quantum computing approach is beyond the scope of this work, a simulation for secure transmission is included to analyze its viability and potential. Additionally, a Cross-Domain Authentication Handover is included, allowing vehicles to reuse trust tokens for different fog domains, resulting in improved system efficiency.

The major novelty of the proposed ZTMAF+ framework lies in the integration of four complementary implementation-level enhancements into the original Zero-Trust Mobility-Aware Authentication Framework. First, Federated Trust Learning enables collaborative trust model training across multiple fog nodes without exchanging raw vehicle data, thereby improving scalability and preserving privacy. Second, a PQC-inspired secure trust transmission layer protects the communication of trust information through simulated quantum-resistant mechanisms without claiming full post-quantum cryptographic implementation. Third, the proposed Cross-Domain Authentication Handover mechanism enables authenticated vehicles to seamlessly migrate between neighboring fog domains using reusable trust tokens, thereby reducing authentication latency and computational overhead. Finally, Temporal Trust Computation incorporates historical behavioral information into trust estimation, improving robustness against dynamic and sophisticated attacks. Collectively, these enhancements distinguish the proposed ZTMAF+ framework from the original ZTMAF by providing improved scalability, secure trust exchange, efficient authentication continuity, and stronger resilience against advanced vehicular attacks.

We validate the proposed system's performance using simulation results, which include a detailed analysis of vehicle behavior, trust evolution and many different attacks. The system simulations have been tested on the attack types of stealth, on–off, and Sybil. The results indicate that while the proposed system performs well to defend against behavioral attacks, identity-based attacks remain an issue and future research will be needed to improve performance in this area.

Unlike the original Zero-Trust Mobility-Aware Authentication Framework (ZTMAF), which primarily focuses on context-aware trust evaluation and mobility-aware authentication within individual fog domains, the proposed ZTMAF+ extends the framework through four implementation-level enhancements. First, Federated Trust Learning enables collaborative trust model 2 training across multiple fog nodes using federated averaging while preserving local data privacy. Second, a PQC-inspired secure trust transmission layer is incorporated to simulate the secure exchange of trust values and evaluate the feasibility of integrating post-quantum cryptographic workflows into vehicular fog environments. Third, a token-based cross-domain authentication handover mechanism enables authenticated vehicles to reuse previously verified trust information when transitioning between fog domains, thereby reducing authentication latency. Finally, the framework introduces temporal trust computation and evaluates its robustness against stealth, on–off, and Sybil attacks using a large-scale simulated vehicular dataset. These enhancements collectively improve the scalability, mobility support, and security evaluation capabilities of the original ZTMAF while maintaining compatibility with its zero-trust authentication model.

Design characteristics

The proposed design models the secure transmission of trust values through a PQC-inspired simulation layer. The system is architected to represent the potential integration of quantum-resistant cryptographic techniques such as lattice-based cryptography in future implementations. An important requirement of the proposed system is the integrity of the managed data during the entire process of trust management, which can be represented by the condition: Tidec=Ti. This equation ensures lossless reconstruction, and it is important to note that the trust value Ti originally assigned by an entity is reconstructed at the receiver after the simulated encryption and decryption process without alteration of the trust metric.

Implementation considerations

The PQC layer is implemented using simulated mechanisms rather than an actual post-quantum cryptographic mechanism. Instead, an abstraction of simulated functions represents the sequence of encryption, transmission, and decryption activities that would occur in a cryptographic layer. This allows the performance of the secure transmission workflow to be evaluated without the computational expense associated with implementing an actual post-quantum cryptographic mechanism. No standardized post-quantum cryptographic algorithms, such as CRYSTALS-Kyber or CRYSTALS-Dilithium, are implemented in this work, and therefore no claims are made regarding quantum-resistant security guarantees. The proposed layer should instead be interpreted as a simulation-level architectural abstraction for evaluating the integration of future post-quantum secure transmission within the ZTMAF+ framework.

To clarify the implementation scope, the proposed framework does not implement standardized post-quantum cryptographic algorithms such as CRYSTALS-Kyber, CRYSTALS-Dilithium, or other NIST-approved PQC schemes. Instead, the framework introduces a PQC-inspired secure trust transmission layer that emulates the architectural principles of quantum-resistant communication while maintaining compatibility with conventional cryptographic mechanisms. The primary objective of this layer is to demonstrate the feasibility of integrating future post-quantum security concepts into zero-trust vehicular fog computing environments rather than claiming complete quantum-resistant security. Consequently, the proposed framework should be interpreted as an implementation-oriented proof of concept that provides a migration path toward future standardized PQC deployment.

Implementation details

The proposed ZTMAF+ framework was implemented using an integrated simulation environment consisting of SUMO, NS-3, Python, and TraCI. SUMO was employed to generate realistic vehicular mobility traces, while NS-3 simulated the vehicular communication network, including vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) and vehicle-to-infrastructure (V2I) interactions. TraCI served as the communication interface between SUMO and NS-3, enabling real-time synchronization of vehicle positions, mobility events, and network behavior.

During each simulation interval, vehicle mobility information obtained from SUMO was transferred to NS-3 through TraCI, where trust computation and authentication decisions were performed. Each fog node periodically collected trust-related attributes, including communication behavior, packet forwarding statistics, authentication history, and mobility consistency. These attributes were processed by the Temporal Trust Computation module to calculate updated trust scores.

The Federated Trust Learning component was implemented using distributed local model training at individual fog nodes. Rather than exchanging raw vehicle data, only model parameters were periodically aggregated to construct a global trust prediction model while preserving data privacy. This federated learning process significantly reduced communication overhead and enabled scalable trust management across multiple fog domains.

The authentication workflow consists of vehicle registration, trust evaluation, authentication request verification, trust token generation, secure trust transmission, and cross-domain authentication handover. Vehicles with trust scores exceeding the predefined threshold are granted authentication tokens that can be securely reused during handover between neighboring fog domains, thereby minimizing repeated authentication latency.

The implementation was developed using Python for trust computation and machine learning, NS-3 for network simulation, SUMO for traffic simulation, TraCI for simulator synchronization, Scikit-learn for Logistic Regression and federated model training, NumPy and Pandas for data processing, and Matplotlib for visualization of experimental results.

This section describes the adversarial scenarios considered in the proposed framework. To comprehensively evaluate system robustness, three representative attack models are implemented: Stealth Attacks, on–off Attacks, and Sybil Attacks.

Stealth attack

A stealth attack is characterized by gradual and subtle malicious behavior intended to evade detection mechanisms by maintaining trust values close to those of legitimate vehicles.

Attack behavior

The attacker introduces minor modifications to behavioral parameters while maintaining an acceptable level of compliance with system rules, thereby avoiding abrupt behavioral changes that may trigger detection mechanisms.

ON–off attack

The on–off attack alternates between benign and malicious behavior to exploit temporal trust mechanisms.

Attack behavior

The attack alternates between periods of normal behavior for trust gain and malicious behavior for executing the attack.

Mathematical representation

Impact on trust

Due to temporal soothing: trust degradation is delayed and attacker retains moderate trust.

Challenges

This attack takes advantage of temporal averaging and the delay in the adaptation of trust, making the malicious behavior less conspicuous over short periods. The solution to this problem is the recognition of patterns over time and global learning mechanisms to effectively capture the patterns in the behavior.

Sybil attack

A party that wants to harm a system can perform something called a Sybil attack, where they create numerous (but fake) identities to gain control over the system as if they were actually multiple participants in that system. In this case, one malicious party creates many (virtual) identities representing “vehicles” and can make these identities act like participants in a network by complying with some aspects of the system to establish a stake in it over time and to build trust as more participants use their services. With multiple identities and trust-building activities, the malicious party gains the ability to manipulate the process of evaluating trust through their coordinated actions, thereby allowing them to influence trust levels without having to act maliciously individually. The distributed nature of the attack makes it very difficult for the malicious actions to be detected because the coordinated behavior will appear “legitimately” under most situations; this is especially true in behavior-based trust models.

where all identities relate to a single malicious entity.

Impact on trust

As a result, this permits dispersal of trust build up about multiple identities, thereby allowing malicious actors can successfully hide what their real intentions are by creating an appearance of legitimacy through dispersal type behaviors. The absence of distinctive behavior patterns makes it extremely difficult to identify malicious activities based solely on the actions exhibited, since this attack relies on taking advantage of vulnerabilities related to their respective identities, not showing distinct behavioral patterns. Trust can be wrongfully increased when several of these types of threat actors are collaborating with one another, which ultimately results in serious increases in false negatives. Therefore, this illustrates that behavioral based detection methods may be insufficient for detecting threats related to identity. Thusly, this leads to demonstrating the necessity for utilizing identity based detection methodologies to assist in preventing attacks against legitimate entities.

Evaluation metrics

The system is evaluated using multiple performance metrics derived directly from the dataset.

Trust metrics

Trust metrics provide the base upon which the evaluation framework has been built, with emphasis placed upon trust behavior and evolution within the network. The Trust Evolution metric provides insight into how trust values evolve, thus allowing understanding of vehicle behavior and how well the network adapts to changing trust values. The Trust Distribution metric, on the other hand, provides a comparison between trust values given to regular vehicles and those given to malicious vehicles, thus allowing a clear understanding of how effectively the network has been able to distinguish between entities based upon trust values.

Classification metrics

Standard classification metrics are used for the evaluation of the detection capabilities of a system using a complete dataset for the simulation (stored as a CSV file). The metric called “Precision” can be calculated by taking the total number of True Positives divided by the total number of True and False Positives, which indicates how many of the systems positive classifications were “True” (or actual) vs. total “Malicious” and “Non-Malicious” vehicles classified as “Malicious.” Recall can be computed by calculating the total number of True Positives divided by the sum of True and False Negatives, which can be used to evaluate how many of the total instances of “Malicious” vehicles were detected. In this example, the F1 Score will be calculated as the harmonic mean of precision and recall, with consideration for both false positive and false negative counts. This gives a comprehensive evaluation of how effective the detection system is at detecting fire in this case.

Detection accuracy

Detection accuracy for each attack type will be computed individually to provide an overall understanding of how well the system has done against a specific kind of threat. Detection accuracy for each attack type will be computed via the total number of attacks identified and divided by the total number of times that attack has occurred in the data set.

Confusion matrix

The confusion matrix is a concept that offers the basis for the visualization and evaluation of the performance of the classification system. The prediction results are categorized into four classes, namely, True Positives (TP), indicating the number of malicious vehicles correctly classified, True Negatives (TN), indicating the number of benign vehicles correctly classified, False Positives (FP), indicating the number of benign vehicles misclassified as malicious, and False Negatives (FN), indicating the number of malicious vehicles missed by the system. The confusion matrix provides an extensive evaluation of the strengths and weaknesses of the system with regard to all classification results.

Federated learning metrics

In terms of federated learning, evaluation occurs through a comparison of local trust values and global trust aggregates, and this gives a sense of how much individual perceptions by vehicles align with each other, providing a sense of stability in the models. The nature of convergence is also used to evaluate how effectively the federated learning process converges to a stable point, ensuring trust aggregation occurs effectively and in a timely manner.

PQC layer metrics

The integrity of the post-quantum cryptographic layer can be determined by finding the difference between the trust value before encryption, i.e., Tenc, and the trust value after decryption, i.e., Tdec. If this difference is zero, it confirms that there is no loss of information during reconstruction, i.e., no corruption or alteration of trust values occurs during the process of encryption and decryption. This is an important metric to ensure that confidentiality is not achieved at the cost of integrity.

Attack generation parameters

The attack scenarios were generated using configurable parameters to represent different malicious behaviors. Stealth attacks were generated by introducing behavior deviations that remain close to normal vehicle behavior, while on–off attacks were generated by alternating between normal and malicious behavior over successive simulation intervals. Sybil attacks were generated by allowing a malicious entity to represent multiple identities within the simulated network. These attack configurations were applied consistently across the simulation runs to ensure reproducible evaluation.

Dataset generation

The dataset used for evaluating the proposed ZTMAF+ framework was generated entirely through simulation using the integrated SUMO–NS-3 environment. SUMO was employed to generate realistic urban vehicular mobility traces, including vehicle positions, speeds, lane changes, and traffic density under varying mobility conditions. These mobility traces were continuously synchronized with the NS-3 network simulator through the TraCI interface to produce realistic communication events.

During each simulation interval, every vehicle generated multiple trust-related attributes, including packet forwarding behavior, communication success rate, authentication history, mobility consistency, neighbor interactions, and temporal trust information. These attributes were processed by the proposed Temporal Trust Computation module to calculate dynamic trust scores.

To evaluate framework robustness, multiple attack scenarios including malicious packet dropping, stealth attacks, on–off attacks, and Sybil attacks were injected into the simulation environment. Vehicles participating in these attacks produced abnormal behavioral patterns that were reflected in the computed trust scores.

Based on the computed trust values and authentication outcomes, each vehicle interaction was assigned an authentication label representing successful or unsuccessful authentication decisions. These labeled trust records were subsequently used for machine learning model training and evaluation. Overall, approximately 1.4 million simulation records were generated across multiple traffic densities and experimental configurations to evaluate authentication accuracy, trust prediction performance, scalability, and attack detection capability.

The dataset generation process incorporated vehicle mobility, trust-related attributes, network metrics, security-layer values, attack information, and authentication decisions. The generated records were used consistently across the evaluation scenarios to analyze trust evolution, attack detection, secure trust transmission, and authentication performance.

Simulation-based validation and generalizability

Simulation-based evaluation was adopted in this work because it enables controlled, repeatable, and scalable experimentation under a wide range of vehicular traffic conditions while avoiding the practical cost and safety challenges associated with large-scale real-world deployments. The combined SUMO and NS-3 simulation environment provides realistic representations of vehicle mobility and wireless communication, allowing comprehensive evaluation of authentication performance, trust computation, and attack resilience under diverse operating scenarios.

Although simulation provides a reliable platform for performance assessment, it cannot fully capture all real-world factors such as heterogeneous hardware, unpredictable wireless interference, environmental conditions, driver behavior, and deployment-specific infrastructure constraints. Consequently, the reported results should be interpreted as demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed framework within a realistic simulation environment rather than guaranteeing identical performance in operational intelligent transportation systems.

Nevertheless, the proposed ZTMAF+ architecture is designed using modular components, including Federated Trust Learning, Temporal Trust Computation, PQC-inspired secure trust transmission, and cross-domain authentication handover. These components can be integrated into practical vehicular fog computing infrastructures with appropriate adaptation to deployment-specific communication protocols and security requirements. Future work will therefore focus on validating the framework using real-world vehicular datasets, roadside infrastructure, and field experiments to further evaluate its robustness, scalability, and deployment feasibility.

Reproducibility

To facilitate reproducibility, all experiments were conducted using a fixed simulation configuration implemented in SUMO and NS-3. The vehicular environment consisted of multiple fog nodes supporting dynamic vehicle mobility, while trust evaluation was performed using the proposed temporal trust computation model and federated logistic regression classifier. Identical simulation parameters, attack injection settings, trust computation weights, and authentication thresholds were maintained across all experimental runs to ensure consistent evaluation. The generated dataset was processed using the Scikit-learn machine learning library, while federated model aggregation was performed using the Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm. All simulations were executed using the same software configuration and parameter settings, enabling consistent replication of the reported experimental results.

The complete implementation, including the simulation scripts, trust computation modules, federated learning framework, and dataset generation pipeline, is available in the project's public GitHub repository to support independent verification and future research.

Implementation and simulation parameters

The simulation uses a fixed duration of 200 simulation steps with three fog nodes and an authentication threshold of 0.6. Federated model updates are performed every 50 simulation steps, while malicious vehicles are generated with a probability of 10%. The trust representation consists of 17 features, and the implemented Ridge-based prediction model uses a regularization parameter of α = 1.0. These parameters are fixed across the evaluated simulation scenarios to maintain consistency between experiments.

Statistical validation

To statistically validate the authentication-delay improvement, a paired vehicle-level analysis was performed using the simulation output. Rather than treating individual simulation records as independent observations, vehicles that experienced both token-based and non-token-based authentication were used as the statistical units. A total of 271 vehicles satisfied this condition. The mean authentication delay was 0.3626 for non-token-based authentication and 0.1979 for token-based authentication, corresponding to a reduction of 45.43%. The 95% confidence intervals of the respective mean delays were [0.3603, 0.3648] and [0.1920, 0.2037]. A paired t-test confirmed that the reduction in authentication delay was statistically significant (t = 53.28, p < 0.001). The corresponding paired effect size was dz = 3.24, indicating a large practical effect. Bootstrap resampling of the vehicle-level paired observations produced a 95% confidence interval of [43.83%, 47.03%] for the percentage reduction. These results provide statistical support for the approximately 45% authentication-delay reduction reported by the proposed token-based handover mechanism ().

Limitations

Although the proposed ZTMAF+ framework demonstrates promising improvements in trust management, authentication efficiency, and attack detection, several limitations remain. First, the evaluation has been conducted entirely in a simulation environment using SUMO and NS-3. While these simulators provide realistic vehicular mobility and communication behavior, real-world deployments may introduce additional factors such as unpredictable wireless interference, hardware constraints, and environmental variations that are not fully captured in simulation.

Second, the integration of Federated Trust Learning introduces communication overhead due to the periodic exchange of model parameters between participating fog nodes. Although this overhead is significantly lower than transmitting raw vehicular data, it may still affect network performance in highly dynamic large-scale deployments.

Third, Temporal Trust Computation and distributed model aggregation require additional computational resources at fog nodes. Resource-constrained edge devices may therefore require optimized implementations or lightweight learning models to maintain real-time performance.

Finally, although the proposed framework demonstrates improved resilience against stealth, on–off, and Sybil attacks within the simulated environment, sophisticated identity-based attacks employing adaptive or collaborative adversarial strategies remain challenging. Future work will focus on validating the framework using real-world vehicular datasets, integrating standardized post-quantum cryptographic algorithms, and developing more efficient trust learning mechanisms suitable for large-scale intelligent transportation systems.

Conclusion and future work

Conclusion

In this paper, an enhanced trust-based authentication system was proposed for vehicular fog computing environments by extending the existing Zero-Trust Mobility Aware Authentication Framework (ZTMAF). In the proposed system, federated trust learning, PQC-inspired secure transmission, and cross-domain authentication handover are integrated into a single system to overcome the limitations of the existing systems in terms of scalability, security, and mobility support.

With the inclusion of federated learning in the proposed system, the global decision-making ability of the system can be improved while maintaining the privacy of the data with the help of the distributed fog nodes in the network. In the proposed system, the PQC-inspired secure transmission component models the encryption, transmission, and reconstruction of trust values and provides a simulation-level mechanism for evaluating their lossless reconstruction during communication. Moreover, the token-based handover component of the system ensures a low latency level during the process of authentication due to the reuse of the existing authenticated trust values.

The experimental analysis, based on an extensive simulation data set, proves the efficacy of the proposed framework in terms of precision in identifying malicious behavior, high performance in tackling on–off and stealthy attacks, and enhanced trust stability through federated aggregation. Moreover, it is observed that the proposed framework can reduce authentication delay by nearly 45% using token-based handover, thus proving its efficacy in heterogeneous scenarios. However, it is also seen that it is difficult for behavior-based trust models to handle Sybil attacks, where identity-based manipulation is a problem.

Thus, it can be stated that the proposed framework is an effective solution for next-generation ITS systems, considering its scalability, security, and adaptability in integrating distributed intelligence, security, and efficient authentication techniques for trust management in vehicular fog computing.

Future work

Future studies may be conducted to enhance the detection mechanism of the framework through identity-aware and graph-based approaches to future research. The enhanced detection mechanism would allow for greater identification of more complex links between vehicles and potential Sybil Attacks and would enhance the strength of the overall design.

Another aspect of strengthening the design will involve implementing complete post quantum cryptographic systems, using lattice based encryption methods, to assist researchers in evaluating the design's feasibility under real world conditions.

Moving forward, researchers may also look into expanding the federated learning components of the detected fraud system by incorporating more complicated models and architectures to include Deep Neural Networks (DNN), which would increase accuracy of the fraud detection system by allowing the system to adjust to changing patterns of fraud in real time.

Additionally, the effectiveness of the complete detection mechanism will need to be evaluated in large scale situations involving high density vehicular networks and the high frequency of handovers from one domain to another domain; however, the optimization of the overall framework using both energy aware and resource constrained optimization strategies would further improve its feasibility within the context of real world vehicular devices with lower computational resources.
