Emerging era of robotics and artificial intelligence in orthopaedic trauma surgery
Authors: Ronald Man Yeung Wong, Minghui Yang, Chun Sing Chui, Emil H. Schemitsch, Peter V. Giannoudis
Published: 2026

The Journal of Orthopaedic Translation (JOT) focuses on the rapidly advancing field of orthopaedic translation research, and recent interest and focus have been on robotics and artificial intelligence. In the past, JOT has had special issues and high-quality content related to orthopaedic biomaterials and devices, and other cutting-edge topics, showing commitment to progressing translational innovation in the field.

Robotics in orthopaedic trauma surgery

Robotics has been used in arthroplasty [[1], [2], [3]] and spine [4], but robotic-assisted orthopaedic trauma surgery is a recent breakthrough in the field of orthopaedic surgery. A surgical robot is a computerized system often with arm(s) to perform surgical tasks with instruments and used for assistance with surgical navigation. Surgical robots have the ability to assist surgeons by potentially simplifying procedures by guiding trajectories of fixation with a robotic arm. Furthermore, with the recent additional development of the capability of surgical robotics to execute a full closed reduction of fractures together with surgical navigation, we have entered a new era of their application in the field of medicine with cutting-edge technologies advancing surgical treatment whilst improving patient care. Their versatility to simultaneously support reduction and fixation during surgical procedures has been one of the great advancements of robotic assisted surgery in the past few years. Moreover, the fact that these procedures can be carried out with minimally invasive surgery signifies their impact in terms of facilitating faster recovery and earlier return to function of patients [5].

The field of orthopedic trauma and particularly reconstruction of pelvic and acetabulum injuries represents a great example of the impact of robotic assisted surgical reduction and fixation for the management of difficult musculoskeletal injuries. Indeed, fractures of the pelvis can be challenging and complex, especially for fragility fractures in the elderly where the bone is osteoporotic and can be prone to fixation failure, let alone the fact that the overall 1-year mortality in this patient population was found to be as high as 15.5% [6]. Schuijt HJ et al. reported that the main advantages of robotics in orthopaedic trauma include reduced operating time, use of fluoroscopy, and intraoperative blood loss, and increased screw placement accuracy [7]. Furthermore, a previous systematic review also showed that robotic-assisted fracture reduction and internal fixation with surgical navigation compared with robotic-assisted screw fixation led to even more effective fracture reduction, less soft tissue damage, better pain relief post-operatively, and quicker recovery [8]. These advantages may be particularly beneficial for older patients as traditional open reduction techniques can be invasive and lead to significant surgical insult, blood loss and systemic derangement in patients with less physiological reserve. Interestingly, a recent multicenter randomized clinical trial investigated the effect of robot-assisted fracture reduction on displaced pelvic fractures [9]. In the study, 92 adult patients with acute closed, displaced pelvic fractures (Tile Type B or C) were randomized to robotic closed reduction or manual closed reduction. Results showed that robotic closed reduction achieved improved reduction quality and reduced intra-operative fluoroscopic time and radiation exposure. At 12-weeks, there was no significant difference in terms of patient-reported outcomes.

Reconstruction of long bone injuries is another area where robotic assisted reduction and fixation of fractures has had an impact. It is well accepted that long bone fractures including the femur can pose surgical difficulties. Surgical fixation typically results in high rates of fracture healing but malunion is not uncommon and can reach approximately 30%, resulting in pain and disability [10]. Zhao C et al. showed that the use of robotic assisted fracture reduction for femoral shaft fractures led to a more precise fracture reduction and lower use of intra-operative fluoroscopy [11]. This allowed a postoperative femoral length discrepancy, and anteversion difference to be significantly smaller compared to the control group [11]. This technology may offer significant advantages for minimally invasive treatment. Noteworthy, to the authors knowledge, Professor Xinbao WU, Dr. Yu WANG and Dr. Chunpeng ZHAO had major roles in the development of the first fracture reduction robotic technology in the world [5].

However, although there are clear potential advantages of robotic-assisted closed reduction surgery, limitations exist and further research remains to be explored. For example, the fracture reduction robot is based on contralateral side mirroring as a template and therefore in the presence of bilateral or more severe injuries, the usage may be limited. Obviously, further research and evidence with the use of artificial intelligence to form a library of normal anatomy may tackle this problem. Another issue that merits further investigation is whether the use of robotic-assisted fracture fixation is actually cost-effective. In the future, clear indications and guidelines for the use of robotic-assisted surgery would be valuable, as surgeons can also determine which pelvic fractures would best benefit from the use of robotics. There is also currently a lack of level I evidence and randomized controlled trials to confirm the effectiveness of robotics for functional recovery in the long term. In the future, integration of artificial intelligence (AI) for the automatic planning of screw and implant positions, augmented reality for visualization and improved tactile feedback are research directions that may further enhance robotic systems.

AI in orthopaedic trauma surgery

Artificial Intelligence is transforming healthcare worldwide and much research has been emphasized in the field of orthopaedics including trauma [12], arthroplasty [13] and spine [14,15]. A recent systematic review showed that the field has shown exponential growth with deep learning approaches and machine learning methods dominating this area of research [16]. Amongst the most common applications are fracture detection and classification, followed by prediction and segmentation. For example, a previous study developed a convolutional neural network for fracture detection, classification and characterization of proximal humerus fractures on X-rays. However, despite overall accuracy for fracture detection of 94%, AI driven classification was still insufficient and also exhibited poor ability for characterization [17] demonstrating the need for further development and research. On the positive note, there are many study results showing artificial intelligence systems that have sensitivity and specificity above 90% [16]. Predictive models have also been investigated for complications and mortality and these have Area Under the Curve (AUC) typically 0.10-0.15 higher than traditional scoring systems [16]. To better improve patient counselling, studies have also been performed in these areas. For example, a recent investigation developed and externally validated a machine learning algorithm to predict 1-year mortality in proximal humerus fracture patients 65 years or older. A web application had also been developed, potentially allowing clinicians to have shared decision making and patient counselling when discussing various treatment options [18]. Similarly, machine learning-based prediction of short- and long-term mortality in older hip fracture patients has also been performed to aid in shared decision making [19].

Comparative evaluation of large language models (LLM) has also been conducted for orthopaedic related questions as these are increasingly used in healthcare for patient education and clinical decision support. A recent study evaluated and compared 3 state-of-the-art LLMs for hip fracture-related questions and actually identified that all LLMs were able to deliver accurate and comprehensive information on hip fracture care [20]. Even for sarcopenia, a disease commonly identified amongst fragility fracture patients [[21], [22], [23]], it was found that LLMs can potentially serve as a valuable tool in patient education and care. Nonetheless, human oversight is recommended to ensure safe and appropriate assessment and management [24]. Based on current research, the use of artificial intelligence has the potential to enhance trauma care with improved diagnostic accuracy, optimizing treatment selection, and identifying patients that are at high-risk of developing complications.

Although AI shows much promise, there are still many limitations as of now. In particular, there are currently inadequate reference standard assignments to train and test AI before translation clinically. Furthermore, in terms of diagnostic and therapeutic scenarios, there is still a need for more solid evidence for safety and verification [25,26]. In the future, research directions should develop AI systems that are accurate and valid for personalized medicine including prediction outcomes and surgical planning, that can integrate into the real-world setting successfully and responsibly.

It is obvious that the role of robotics and artificial intelligence in the field of medicine will only grow in the years to come. For all of us, the sooner we engage and become familiar with the potential applications of these technologies in our clinical practice, the better we will be equipped with knowledge and techniques to improve the care of our patients.
