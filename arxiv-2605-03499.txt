A Hierarchical Sampling Framework for bounding the Generalization Error of Federated Learning
Dario Filatrella, Ragnar Thobaben, and Mikael Skoglund

Abstract
We study expected generalization bounds for the Hierarchical Federated Learning (HFL) setup using Wasserstein distance. We introduce a generalized framework in which data is sampled hierarchically, and we model it with a multi-layered tree structure that induces dependencies among the clients’ datasets. We derive generalization bounds in terms of Wasserstein distance under the Lipschitz assumption on the loss function, by applying a supersample construction that allows us to measure the sensitivity of the algorithm to the change of a single node in the sampling tree. By leveraging the FL structure, we recover and strictly imply existing state-of-the-art conditional mutual information (CMI) bounds in the case of bounded losses. We also show that our bound can be applied together with Differential Privacy assumptions, to recover generalization bounds based on algorithmic privacy. To assess the tightness of our bounds, we study the Gaussian Location Model (GLM) and show that we recover the actual asymptotic rate of the generalization error .



I
Introduction

Federated Learning (FL) is a machine learning setting where multiple clients (or nodes) collaboratively train a global model
[
6
]
.
The term was introduced in 2016 by
[
11
]
, where the authors present the challenges and motivations of training models in a distributed manner,
and propose the FedAvg algorithm.

The two main motivations to train models in a distributed fashion are: (i) to distribute training resources for models that are exceptionally large and (ii) to distribute
datasets in the case of privacy concerns or communication constraints.

Motivation (i) is an engineering driven motivation, and for further details, we refer to
[
24
]
and
[
13
]
,
which provide frameworks for training DNNs in a distributed manner.
Motivation (ii) is what we aim to address with the random participation of clients.
The idea of having a complex hierarchical data source, with non i.i.d. points, first appeared in classical statistics, specifically in the field of sampling techniques, when, in 1965, Kish introduced the concept of
design effect

and
effective sample size

[
9
]
. There exist many classical references on sampling techniques, such as
[
3
]
.
These results predate the modern learning theory framework, and aim to measure the heterogeneity of the data through variance-like quantities
and indicators, such as the
intraclass correlation coefficient
, which are not compatible with modern learning theory approaches based on complexity measures and information theory.

While generalization bounds for supervised learning (SL) could be applied, as the FL setup can be seen as a "black box" SL setup,
they would lose the leverage of the FL structure, and, in some cases, they might also incur in clashing assumptions due to the non-i.i.d. nature of the data.
Despite those reasons calling for the development of generalization bounds tailored for FL, the literature
on this topic remains in its early stages.

In our work, we primarily address motivation (ii), leaving flexibility to incorporate constraints about the computational power of each node.
Our main contributions are:

•

A generalized framework that encompasses hierarchical sampling strategies and previous Federated Learning frameworks as special cases.

•

A generalization bound in terms of Wasserstein distance that holds for a wide range of loss functions and, in particular, is tighter than previous state-of-the-art CMI bounds for bounded losses.

•

A generalization bound under privacy assumptions on the local algorithms.

•

A case study of the Gaussian Location Model (GLM), where we show how our bound compares to the known generalization error.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows:.
Section
II
reviews the related literature on FL and generalization bounds. Section
III

introduces the notation and our framework. Section
IV
presents our main results,
including both Wasserstein distance bounds, Differential Privacy bounds, and a discussion on the implications of our results.
Then, Section
V
presents the case study of the GLM.
Finally, we include the proofs of every result and additional discussions in the appendix.

II
Related work

II-A

Generalization theory

The literature for generalization bounds in the centralized setting is extensive, and dates back to the 70s and 80s
starting with the PAC learning framework
[
17
]
and the Vapnik-Chervonenkis dimension
[
18
]
.
Those approaches are based on the complexity of the hypothesis class, and, thanks to
[
22
]
,
we know that those classical complexity measures are not sufficient to explain the generalization of modern deep learning models, where the number of parameters is often much larger than the number of training samples
and the learning algorithms play a key role in the generalization performance.
Subsequently, a new theory based on information measures was developed,
with results such as
[
20
]
, where the authors established a bound in terms of information measures between the data and the output hypothesis of the learning algorithm,
and has been improved numerous times, for example, in
[
2
]
.

In 2020
[
16
]
introduced the conditional mutual information (CMI) framework and the supersample construction, which we build upon in this work.
This approach has yielded tighter bounds and has been extended to a larger class of losses using
f
-divergences and
phi
-divergences

[
4
]
and
[
5
]
.

Of particular interest for our work is
[
14
]
which introduced a new approach
based on Wasserstein distance, which implies the CMI bound as a special case for bounded losses.
The Wasserstein distance is particularly interesting for its connection with optimal transportation and its ability to capture the geometry of the distributions

[
15
]
.

II-B

Federated Learning

Due to the recency of FL, with the first ideas of training local models and averaging them appearing in
[
10
]
for the perceptron model,
studies of the generalization performance of FL algorithms started to emerge in the literature only recently
[
21
]
, with the introduction of the concept of

participation gap
and
Out-of-sample gap
, which provide the foundations for our contributions.
In this early work, both theoretical bounds and empirical measurements are provided for the two gaps, using common image classification datasets.
Following this work,
[
8
]
introduces a hierarchical Bayesian structure with personalized models for each
client. They prove a convergence rate and a generalization bound for a locally convex model, with the latter being a very strong limitation.

An important aspect of FL is the communication structure, often summarized by the number of communication rounds (i.e. the number of times the nodes are allowed to exchange information).
In the special case of the
single round
or
one-shot
setting, it is possible to derive specialized generalization bounds
[
12
,
23
]
.

In
[
1
]
the authors introduce a mutual information bound for generic FL algorithms and then specialize it
to the case of models that can be represented as Bregman divergences, including a bound with communication constraints and multi-round algorithms.

In
[
7
]
the authors show that the generalization errors is lower when clients data sources are more heterogeneous, in the case of averaging local models,
but their limitations are related to the lack of leverage of the FL structure, which we discuss further when showing how to recover their bounds as a special case of our framework.

Overall, there is a lack of unified frameworks for FL that can ecompass different sampling strategies and communication structures.

III-B

Hierarchical Federated Learning setup

The Hierarchical Federated Learning setup is a supervised learning problem, where
\mathcal{Z}
is the data space,
\mathcal{W}
is the hypothesis space,
and
\ell:\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
is the loss function.
We denote the dataset by
\mu
and the learning algorithm by
P_{W|\mu}
, which is a (not necessarily) random kernel that maps the dataset to a distribution over hypotheses.
The federated nature of the setup is modeled by (i) the sampling structure of the dataset
\mu
and (ii) the assumptions on the algorithm
P_{W|\mu}
.
In particular, our dataset
\mu
is indexed by a tree with a fixed topology that represents a hierarchical sampling strategy, hence the name.
Given the constraints, we can index the nodes of the tree by their depth and position in the tree as follows: each node is indexed as

\mu_{l}^{i_{1:l}}
where
l
is the depth of the node and
i_{1:l}
is the position of the node in the tree as a sequence of positions
of its ancestors (i.e.
i_{1},i_{1:2},\ldots,i_{1:l-1}
are the ancestors of
i_{1:l}
). We also define
L
to be the depth of
the tree.

D
\mu_{1}^{1}
\mu_{2}^{1,1}
\mu_{2}^{1,2}
\mu_{2}^{1,n_{2}}
\mu_{1}^{2}
\mu_{2}^{2,1}
\mu_{2}^{2,2}
\mu_{2}^{2,n_{2}}
\mu_{1}^{n_{1}}
\vdots
\cdots
\cdots
\cdots

Fig. 1:
Hierarchical sampling structure

Formally the sampling is as follows:
the first layer’s nodes are sampled i.i.d. from a meta-distribution
D
, meaning
\mu_{1}^{i_{1}}\sim D
for all
i_{1}
. For the lower layers we have

\mu_{l}^{i_{1:l}}\sim\mu_{l-1}^{i_{1:l-1}}.

(1)

Consequently,
\mu_{L}^{i_{1:L}}\in\mathcal{Z}
is a data point,
\mu_{L-1}^{i_{1:L-1}}
is a distribution over data points, and so on.
We can then write the sampling of a single data point as
\mu_{L}\sim\mu_{L-1}\sim\cdots\sim\mu_{1}\sim D
, where
\mu_{l}
is a random variable that takes values in the set of nodes at depth
l
.
For simplicity we will also shorten the previous notation to
\mu_{L}\sim\mu_{1}
to indicate the whole chain of sampling from
L
to
1
.

The intuitive motivation behind this dataset construction is the following: the leaves represent the actual data points, while the intermediate nodes represent
clients or clusters, which are also assumed to be random, and in this setting they are indentified as distributions over the lower nodes (i.e. a cluster is a distribution over clients, a client is a distribution over data points).

Regarding the algorithmic assumptions, our main results hold for a general kernel
P_{W|\mu}
that takes as input the whole tree without imposing any constraint (such as privacy mechanisms or communication constraints).
Of course, such bounds can only get tighter when restricting to a smaller class of algorithms, but we are still able to recover the bounds of
[
19
]
and
[
7
]
.
Their constraints consist of imposing each client to only communicate an hypothesis
W_{l}^{i_{1:l}}\in\mathcal{W}
to the parent node, which will
then only act as an
aggregation rule
, which can be modeled by a Markov chain.
In section
IV-B
we will explore such setting, and derive generalization bounds under privacy assumptions.

For technical reasons discussed in the proof of Theorem
3
, we also assume constant branching factor at each layer,
i.e. we assume that at layer
l
each node
\mu_{l}^{i_{1:l}}
has
n_{l+1}
children
\mu_{l+1}^{i_{1:l+1}}
. We can then denote
N_{l}=\prod_{k=1}^{l}n_{k}
as the number of nodes at depth
l
.

Remark 1
.

While the hypothesis
W
clearly depends on the intermediate nodes
\mu_{l}^{i_{1:l}}
for
l<L
, those nodes are usually an abstraction (such as the type of client) and they are
not real data points that are used by the algorithm, which only uses leaf nodes.
We can then model this with the following independence assumption:

W\perp\mu_{l}^{i_{1:l}}\mid\left\{\mu_{l+1}^{i_{1:l+1}}\right\}_{i_{l+1}^{l+1}=1}^{n_{l+1}}.

(2)

We do not require this for our main results, but it is often implicitely assumed in the literature, for example in
[
7
,
23
]
.

Remark 2
.

With a slight modification we can assume that the sampling is dependent on the indices of the nodes, i.e.,
\mu_{l}^{i_{1:l}}\sim K(\mu_{l-1}^{i_{1:l-1}},i_{1:l})
, where
K
is a kernel.
In this case, the fixed depth becomes a non-real constraint, since we can just impose a certain node to output a deterministic copy until a leaf node is reached.
Having positionally dependent kernel will make it possible to recover results from
[
7
]
, but it is reasonable to assume symmetry among the nodes and
invariance of the learning algorithm to the ordering of the data points.

III-C

Generalization Error

Given a realization of the dataset
\mu
, the empirical risk of a hypothesis
w
is defined as

\hat{L}(w,\mu)=\frac{1}{N_{L}}\sum_{i_{1:L}}\ell(w,\mu_{L}^{i_{1:L}}),

(3)

and the population risk is defined as

L(w)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{L}\sim\mu_{L-1}\sim\cdots\sim\mu_{1}\sim D}[\ell(w,\mu_{L})].

(4)

The generalization error of an algorithm
P_{W|\mu}
is defined then as:

\text{gen}(P_{W|\mu})=\mathbb{E}_{W\sim P_{W|\mu},\mu_{L}\sim\mu_{L-1}\sim\cdots\sim\mu_{1}\sim D}[L(W)-\hat{L}(W,\mu)].

(5)

Proposition 3
.

[Generalization bound decomposition]
Under the above setup, the generalization error can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\Delta_{l,i_{1:l}}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{W,\mu}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{L}^{\mathrm{test}}\sim\mu_{l}^{i_{1:l}}}\!\left[\ell\!\left(W,\mu_{L}^{\mathrm{test}}\right)\right]

(6)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{n_{l+1}}\sum_{i_{l+1}}\mathbb{E}_{W,\mu}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{L}^{\mathrm{test}}\sim\mu_{l+1}^{i_{1:l+1}}}\!\left[\ell\!\left(W,\mu_{L}^{\mathrm{test}}\right)\right],

\displaystyle\bigl|\operatorname{gen}(P_{W|\mu})\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}\frac{1}{N_{l}}\sum_{i_{1:l}}\bigl|\Delta_{l,i_{1:l}}\bigr|.

(7)

Where
\mu^{\mathrm{test}}_{L}\sim\mu_{l}^{i_{1:l}}
is an independent test point with the same distribution as the leaf nodes rooted in
\mu_{l}^{i_{1:l}}
.

The decomposition in Proposition
3
is an intuitive result that allows for controlling the generalization of
the algorithm in terms of smaller "local" generalization errors measuring the difference in performance of parent-child nodes.

III-D

Supersample construction

We now introduce a supersample construction in the hierarchical FL setup, following the technique of
[
16
]
.
The idea is to construct a "ghost" dataset
\bar{\mu}
with the same statistical properties as the original dataset
\mu
, and measure the generalization
error in terms of an information measure between the algorithm output hypothesis when training on
\mu
or
\bar{\mu}
.
At the root layer
l=1
we sample
\tilde{\mu}_{1}^{i_{1}}=\left(\mu_{l,1}^{i_{1}},\mu_{l,2}^{i_{1}}\right)\sim\mathcal{D}^{\otimes 2}
.
For each node of the tree we sample
U^{i_{1:l}}_{l}
uniformly at random from
\{1,2\}
(so one decision variable per tree node)
and sample the rest of the supersample as follows:

\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{i_{1:l}}=\left(\mu_{l,1}^{i_{1:l}},\mu_{l,2}^{i_{1:l}}\right)\sim\left(\mu_{l-1,U^{i_{1}:l}_{l-1}}^{i_{1:l-1}}\right)^{\otimes 2}.

(8)

In this way we have the following conditional independence:

\mu_{l,1}^{i_{1:l}}\perp\mu_{l,2}^{j_{1:l}}\mid\mu_{l-1,U^{i_{1}:l}_{l-1}}^{i_{1:l-1}},

(9)

and the following distributional equivalence:

\mu_{l,1}^{i_{1:l}}\overset{d}{=}\mu_{l,2}^{i_{1:l}}\mid\mu_{l-1,U^{i_{1}:l}_{l-1}}^{i_{1:l-1}}.

(10)

We denote
\bar{U}_{l}^{i_{1:l}}
to be the flipped version of
U_{l}^{i_{1:l}}
, i.e.,
\bar{U}_{l}^{i_{1:l}}=3-U_{l}^{i_{1:l}}
.
We also denote by
\mu_{l}^{i_{1:l}}=\mu_{l,U^{i_{1}:l}_{l}}^{i_{1:l}}
the selected node at layer
l
and by
\bar{\mu}_{l}^{i_{1:l}}=\mu_{l,\bar{U}^{i_{1}:l}_{l}}^{i_{1:l}}
the ghost node at layer
l
.

Main results

In this section we present our main results. We start with the Wasserstein distance bound, together with some implications and discussion, and then move

IV-A

Wasserstein distance bounds

We assume that the loss function

Under the above hierachical sampling structure and supersample construction, and under the Lipschitz Assumption

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{W,\mu_{L}}\,\operatorname{gen}(W,\mu_{L})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2L_{\mathrm{Lip}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\frac{1}{N_{l}}\sum_{i_{1:l}}\Biggl|\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{i_{1:l}},\,U_{l}^{i_{1:l}}}\Biggr.

(12)

\displaystyle\Biggl.\mathbb{W}\!\left(P_{W\mid\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{i_{1:l}},\,U_{l}^{i_{1:l}}},P_{W\mid\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{i_{1:l}}}\right)\Biggr|,

where the Wasserstein distance is computed with respect to the same metric

Corollary 6
.

For bounded losses in
[0,1]
, we obtain:

\mathbb{E}_{W,\mu_{L}}gen(W,\mu_{L})\leq\sum_{l=1}^{L}\frac{1}{N_{l}}\sum_{i_{1:l}}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{i_{1:l}}}\sqrt{2I(W;U_{l}^{i_{1:l}}|\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{i_{1:l}})}.

(13)

This is our main result, which relates the generalization error with the Wasserstein distance between the distribution of the output hypothesis when conditioning on a single selected node and its ghost copy.

We observe two contrasting effects in the bound: on the one hand, the number of terms scales as
O(L)
, on the other hand, the more layers we have, the lower the intra-class variance
will be at each parent-child node, which should reduce the Wasserstein distance between the two distributions.
We also note that the first layers are the most impactful, which is reasonable since they introduce correlations among very large chunks of the dataset and it is an
inherent feature of the hierarchical sampling strategy.

Under the Lipschitz Assumption

IV-B

Privacy Bound

We now show that our framework is also suitable for deriving generalization bounds from differential privacy (DP) assumptions.
We consider a
single round
setting, where each node forms an hypothesis
W_{l}^{i_{1:l}}
by aggregating the hypothesis formed at the lower layer
l+1

(in the case of the last layer, those hypothesis would just be the data points). We can then encode the privacy of the whole FL algorithm by imposing local privacy constraints
on the aggregation steps as follows:

Assumption 12
.

Each aggregation algorithm
P_{W^{i_{1:l-1}}_{l-1}|W_{l}^{i_{1:l-1}}}
is
\epsilon_{l}
-differentially private, i.e.:

\frac{P_{W^{i_{1:l-1}}_{l-1}|W_{l}^{i_{1:l-1}}}(S|w)}{P_{W^{i_{1:l-1}}_{l-1}|W_{l}^{i_{1:l-1}}}(S|w^{\prime})}\leq e^{\epsilon_{l}}.

(15)

For any
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}
that are differing in a single data point.
This also implies
I(W^{i_{1:l-1}}_{l-1};W_{l}^{i_{1:l}})\leq\epsilon_{l}(e^{\epsilon_{l}}-1)
.

Intuitively, this condition implies that at any point in the tree, the output hypothesis of the parent does not reveal information about
the output hypothesis of the children, and therefore about the data points.

Theorem 13
.

Under the privacy Assumption
12
, loss bounded in
[0,1]
, and the same setup as Theorem
5
, the following holds:

\mathbb{E}_{W,\mu_{L}}gen(W,\mu_{L})\leq 2\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sqrt{\min(\epsilon_{l},\epsilon_{l}(e^{\epsilon_{l}}-1))}.

(16)

V
Gaussian Location Model

We now analyze the effectiveness of our bounds for the Gaussian location model, where data points are sampled hierarchically and are therefore correlated.
This particular model allows for exact computation of the generalization error, which allows us to compare our bounds with the correct baseline.

For simplicity, given that variances are fixed, we will identify Gaussian distributions with their mean, which will ease the notation.

We consider a sampling tree of depth
L
with root parameter
\theta
. At the first layer, we draw
n_{1}
independent samples

\mu_{1}^{i}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\theta,\sigma_{1}^{2}\bigr),\qquad i=1,\dots,n_{1},

(17)

and, recursively, at layer
\ell=2,\dots,L
, each node at layer
\ell-1
spawns
n_{\ell}
children sampled according to

\mu_{\ell}^{i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell}}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\mu_{\ell-1}^{i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell-1}},\sigma_{\ell}^{2}\bigr),\qquad i_{\ell}=1,\dots,n_{\ell}.

(18)

The hypothesis
W
is the average of all the leaf nodes, which can be written as:

W=\frac{1}{N_{L}}\sum_{i_{1:L}}\mu_{L}^{i_{1:L}}=\theta+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\frac{1}{N_{l}}\sum_{i_{1:l}}\epsilon_{l}^{i_{1:l}},

(19)

where
\epsilon_{l}^{i_{1:l}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{l}^{2})
are independent noise terms.

In this setting, we have that the true generalization error is given by:

\mathbb{E}_{W,\mu_{L}}gen(W,\mu_{L})=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\left(\sqrt{V+\Delta}-\sqrt{V-\Delta}\right),

(20)

where
N_{l}=\prod_{k=1}^{l}n_{k}
,
V=\sum_{l}\sigma_{l}^{2}
and
\Delta=\sum_{l}\frac{\sigma_{l}^{2}}{N_{l}}
.
Expanding with Taylor series around
\Delta=0
we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{W,\mu_{L}}gen(W,\mu_{L})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\left(\frac{\Delta}{\sqrt{V}}+O\left(\frac{\Delta^{3}}{V^{5/2}}\right)\right)

(21)

\displaystyle\approx\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\left(\frac{\sum_{l}\frac{\sigma_{l}^{2}}{N_{l}}}{\sqrt{\sum_{l}\sigma_{l}^{2}}}\right).

(22)

While, computing the Wasserstein bound with Theorem
5
gives:

\mathbb{E}_{W,\mu_{L}}gen(W,\mu_{L})\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\frac{\sigma_{l}}{N_{l}}.

(23)

We start by observing that in
L=1
case, our bound coincides with the true generalization error to
first order, up to a factor of
\sqrt{2}
.

Adding more layers introduces a larger gap, due to
\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sigma_{l}\geq\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sigma_{l}^{2}/\sqrt{\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sigma_{l}^{2}}

becoming looser.

In particular we also gain an insight on the scaling: The two bounds are the closest when the variance of a single layer dominates.

Specifically, in the homogeneous
\sigma_{l}=\sigma
case, we get that the true generalization error is given by:

\mathbb{E}_{W,\mu_{L}}gen(W,\mu_{L})\approx\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}\sum_{l}\frac{1}{N_{l}}}{\sqrt{L}\sigma}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma}{\sqrt{\pi L}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\frac{1}{N_{l}},

(24)

while our bound gives:

\mathbb{E}_{W,\mu_{L}}gen(W,\mu_{L})\leq\frac{2\sigma}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\frac{1}{N_{l}}.

(25)

Which means that the bound qualitatively captures the scaling with the number of data points but misses the scaling with the number of layers.
For this specific model, the bound from Lemma
11
yields
the same expression as Equation
25
.

VI
Discussion

We have presented an abstract formulation of the Hierarchical Federated Learning setup that encloses multiple existing frameworks.
We then derive a generalization bound in terms of the Wasserstein distance by assuming a Lipschitz condition on the loss function and by using a supersample construction.
This bound implies as special cases existing CMI bounds in the literature, but it is more general and can be applied to any hierarchical sampling procedure.
We also compared the bound with the true generalization error in the Gaussian Location Model, showing that it captures the scaling with the number of data points,
but misses the scaling with the depth of the sampling tree. We also showed that the framework is suitable for deriving generalization bounds from privacy assumptions, and we recovered
a generalization bound based on a local differential privacy assumption on the aggregation steps.
A possible future direction is to extend the framework to general tree structures, in order to enlarge the sampling procedures covered by our bound.
We acknowledge that our bound does not take into consideration the communication structure and/or computational limitations of each node. We addressed this problem
when proving the implication in Corollary
10
, showing that adding independence conditions to model privacy is straightforward
and can be further explored.

Extra preliminaries and definition

In this section we breifly overview some of the definitions and results that we will use for the proofs.

A-A

Information theory

Definition 14

(KL divergence)
.

Given two probability distributions
P
and
Q
over the same space
\mathcal{X}
, the Kullback-Leibler divergence from
Q
to
P
is defined as

D_{KL}(P||Q)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\log\left(\frac{dP}{dQ}(x)\right)dP(x),

(26)

where
\frac{dP}{dQ}
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
P
with respect to
Q
.

Definition 15

(Mutual information)
.

Given two random variables
X
and
Y
, the mutual information between them is defined as

I(X;Y)=D_{KL}(P_{XY}||P_{X}\otimes P_{Y}),

(27)

where
D_{KL}
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence.

Definition 16

(Conditional mutual information)
.

Given three random variables
X
,
Y
and
Z
, the conditional mutual information between
X
and
Y
given
Z
is defined as

I(X;Y|Z)=\mathbb{E}_{Z}[D_{KL}(P_{XY|Z}||P_{X|Z}\otimes P_{Y|Z})].

(28)

Definition 17

(Total variation distance)
.

Given two probability distributions
P
and
Q
over the same space
\mathcal{X}
, the total variation distance between
P
and
Q
is defined as

\|P-Q\|_{TV}=\sup_{A\subseteq\mathcal{X}}|P(A)-Q(A)|=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}|dP(x)-dQ(x)|.

(29)

Theorem 18

(Pinsker’s inequality)
.

For any two probability distributions
P
and
Q
over the same space
\mathcal{X}
, the total variation distance between
P
and
Q
is bounded by the square root of the KL divergence:

\|P-Q\|_{TV}\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}D_{KL}(P||Q)}.

(30)

Definition 19

(Differential privacy)
.

A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{W}
is said to be
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differentially private if for any two neighboring datasets
D
and
D^{\prime}
differing in at most one element, and for any output set
S\subseteq\mathcal{W}
,

\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D)\in S]\leq e^{\epsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})\in S]+\delta.

(31)

A-B

Wasserstein Distance

Definition 20

(Wasserstein distance as optimal transport cost)
.

Given two probability distributions
P
and
Q
over a metric space
(\mathcal{X},\rho)
, the Wasserstein distance of order 1 between
P
and
Q
is defined as

\mathbb{W}(P,Q)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(P,Q)}\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim\pi}[\rho(X,Y)],

(32)

where
\Pi(P,Q)
is the set of all couplings of
P
and
Q
, i.e., the set of all joint distributions
\pi
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
such that the marginals of
\pi
are
P
and
Q
respectively.

Theorem 21

(Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality)
.

The Wasserstein distance can also be expressed in dual form as

\mathbb{W}(P,Q)=\sup_{f:\text{Lip}(f)\leq 1}\left[\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}[f(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q}[f(Y)]\right],

(33)

where the supremum is taken over all 1-Lipschitz functions
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 22

(Wasserstein distance and total variation distance)
.

When the metric of the underlying metric space
(\mathcal{X},\rho)
is the discrete metric, i.e.,
\rho(x,y)=1
if
x\neq y
and
\rho(x,x)=0
, then the Wasserstein distance between two probability distributions
P
and
Q
is equal to the total variation distance between
P
and
Q
:

\mathbb{W}(P,Q)=\|P-Q\|_{TV}.

(34)

Substituting back we get:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}I(W,J_{k,j}\mid\tilde{Z}_{k,j})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}I(W,J_{k}\mid\tilde{Z}_{k})}

(76)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{nK}\sum_{k=1}^{K}I(W,J_{k}\mid\tilde{Z}_{k})}

(77)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{nK}\sum_{k=1}^{K}I(W_{k},J_{k}\mid\tilde{Z}_{k})}.

(78)

∎

Proof of the ordering of lemma
11
.

For simplicity, call
X=\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{i_{1:l}}
,
Y=(\mu_{L}^{i_{1:l}},\bar{\mu}_{L}^{i_{1:l}})
and
U=U_{l}^{i_{1:l}}
. We have that
U
is independent of
X
and
Y
and that
the following Markov chain holds:
X-(Y,U)-W
.
We start by expanding the following quantities:

\displaystyle I(U;W,Y|X)

\displaystyle=I(U;Y|X)+I(U;W|X,Y)=I(U;W|X,Y)

(79)

\displaystyle=I(U;W|X)+I(U;Y|X,W),

(80)

and

\displaystyle I(U;W,X|Y)

\displaystyle=I(U;X|Y)+I(U;W|X,Y)=I(U;W|X,Y)

(82)

\displaystyle=I(U;W|Y)+I(U;X|Y,W).

(83)

We have used the independence of
U
and the chain rule for the conditional mutual information to obtain the equalities. We notice that we have two expression for
I(U;W|X,Y)
, so we can write:

I(U;W|X)+I(U;Y|X,W)=I(U;W|Y)+I(U;X|Y,W).

(85)

We observe that
I(U;X|Y,W)=0
because of the following factorization:

P(U,X|Y,W)=\frac{P(W|Y,U,X)P(Y|U,X)P(U)P(X)}{P(W,Y)}=\frac{P(W|Y,U)P(Y|X)P(U)P(X)}{P(W,Y)},

(86)

which shows that
U
and

are independent given

W
. Therefore we get:

(87)

and since the CMI is non-negative we get the desired result:

(88)

The last step is the only inequality we have used, therefore the relaxation of the two bounds only differs by

Appendix C
Computations for the Gaussian Location Model

We start by observing that:

\mu_{L}^{i_{1:L}}=\theta+\sum_{l=1}^{L}Z_{l}^{i_{1:l}}.

(89)

Where
Z_{l}^{i_{1:l}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{l})
are independent Gaussian noise variables.
The hypothesis is given by
W=\theta+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\frac{1}{N_{l}}\sum_{i_{l}}Z_{l}^{i_{1:l}}
.
The test point is an independent leaf node sampled as
\mu^{\text{test}}_{L}\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,\sum_{l}^{L}\sigma^{2}_{l})
.
The population risk is then simply
|W-\mu^{\text{test}}_{L}|
, which is the absolute value of a Gaussian variable with variance:

\text{Var}(W-\mu^{\text{test}}_{L})=\text{Var}(W)+\text{Var}(\mu^{\text{test}}_{L})=V+\Delta,

(90)

where
\Delta=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\frac{\sigma^{2}_{l}}{N_{l}}
and
V=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sigma^{2}_{l}
.
We then get that
\mathbb{E}[|L(W,\mu^{\text{test}}_{L})|]=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\sqrt{\Delta+V}
.
For the empirical risk we need to compute the expectation of
|W-\mu_{L}^{i_{1:L}}|
. The inner part is a Gaussian variable and we can compute by observing that:

W-\mu_{L}^{i_{1:L}}=\sum_{l}^{L}\left(\bar{Z}_{l}-Z_{l}^{i_{1:l}}\right),

(91)

where
\bar{Z}_{l}=\frac{1}{N_{l}}\sum_{i_{l}}Z_{l}^{i_{1:l}}
.
Then:

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\bar{Z}_{l}-Z_{l}^{i_{1:l}})

\displaystyle=\text{Var}(\bar{Z}_{l})+\text{Var}(Z_{l}^{i_{1:l}})-2\text{Cov}(\bar{Z}_{l},Z_{l}^{i_{1:l}})

(92)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}_{l}}{N_{l}}+\sigma^{2}_{l}-2\frac{\sigma^{2}_{l}}{N_{l}}=\sigma^{2}_{l}-\frac{\sigma^{2}_{l}}{N_{l}}.

(93)

Therefore by summing over
l
we get
\mathbb{E}[|\hat{L}(W,\mu_{L}^{i_{1:L}})|]=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\sqrt{V-\Delta}
and the generalization error is:

\mathbb{E}gen(W,\mu_{L})=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\left(\sqrt{V+\Delta}-\sqrt{V-\Delta}\right).

(94)

For the Wasserstein bound, starting from the bound of theorem
5
we first notice that our loss
function has Lipschitz constant equal to 1.
We now aim to compute:

\mathbb{W}\left(P_{W|\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{i_{1:l}},U_{l}^{i_{1:l}}},P_{W|\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{i_{1:l}}}\right).

(95)

Conditional on
\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{i_{1:l}}
and
U_{l}^{i_{1:l}}=u
we can write:

W=\frac{1}{N_{l}}\left(\left(\sum_{i^{\prime}_{1:l}\neq i_{1:l}}\frac{N_{l}}{N_{L}}\sum_{i^{\prime}_{l+1:L}}\mu_{L}^{i^{\prime}_{1:L}}\right)+\frac{N_{l}}{N_{L}}\sum_{i^{\prime}_{l+1:L}}\mu_{L}^{i_{1:l},i^{\prime}_{l+1:L}}\right),

(96)

where
\mu_{L}^{i_{1:l},i^{\prime}_{l+1:L}}\sim\tilde{\mu}_{l,u}^{i_{1:l}}
are the leaf nodes of the subtree controlled by
U_{l}^{i_{1:l}}
.
Basically, we have taken out of the summation the subtree that is controlled by
U_{l}^{i_{1:l}}
.
The first part of the summation does not depend on
u
and we can just call it
A
. We observe that
W
is the sum of Gaussian variables, and is therefore a Gaussian. Computing its expected value:

\mathbb{E}[W|\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{i_{1:l}},U_{l}^{i_{1:l}}=u]=\mathbb{E}[A]+\frac{1}{N_{L}}\sum_{i^{\prime}_{l+1:L}}\mathbb{E}[\mu_{L}^{i^{\prime}_{1:L}}|\tilde{\mu}_{l,u}^{i_{1:l}}]=\mathbb{E}[A]+\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{l,u}^{i_{1:l}}}{N_{l}}.

(97)

We then notice that
P_{W|\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{i_{1:l}}}
is a mixture of
P_{W|\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{i_{1:l}},U_{l}^{i_{1:l}}=u}
for
u=1,2
, and using that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}(\frac{1}{2}P_{1}+\frac{1}{2}P_{2},P_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{W}(P_{1},P_{1})+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{W}(P_{2},P_{1})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{W}(P_{2},P_{1}),

(98)

combined with the fact that the Wasserstein distance between two Gaussians is the distance between their means, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}\left(P_{W|\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{i_{1:l}},U_{l}^{i_{1:l}}},P_{W|\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{i_{1:l}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left|\mathbb{E}[W|\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{i_{1:l}},U_{l}^{i_{1:l}}=1]-\mathbb{E}[W|\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{i_{1:l}},U_{l}^{i_{1:l}}=2]\right|

(100)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left|\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{l,1}^{i_{1:l}}}{N_{l}}-\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{l,2}^{i_{1:l}}}{N_{l}}\right|

(101)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2N_{l}}\left|\tilde{\mu}_{l,1}^{i_{1:l}}-\tilde{\mu}_{l,2}^{i_{1:l}}\right|.

(102)

Given that
\tilde{\mu}_{l,1}^{i_{1:l}}
and
\tilde{\mu}_{l,2}^{i_{1:l}}
are independently sampled from their ancestor, they have the same mean and variance equal to
\sigma^{2}_{l}
,
so we have
\tilde{\mu}_{l,1}^{i_{1:l}}-\tilde{\mu}_{l,2}^{i_{1:l}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,2\sigma^{2}_{l})
.
Therefore, taking the expected value we get:

\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{i_{1:l}},U_{l}^{i_{1:l}}}\left[\mathbb{W}\left(P_{W|\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{i_{1:l}},U_{l}^{i_{1:l}}},P_{W|\tilde{\mu}_{l}^{i_{1:l}}}\right)\right]\leq\frac{1}{2N_{l}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\tilde{\mu}_{l,1}^{i_{1:l}}-\tilde{\mu}_{l,2}^{i_{1:l}}\right|\right]=\frac{1}{2N_{l}}\sqrt{\frac{4\sigma^{2}_{l}}{\pi}}=\frac{\sigma_{l}}{\sqrt{\pi}N_{l}}.

(103)
