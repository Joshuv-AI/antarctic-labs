Abstract

 An innovative sorbent structure of trapped small amines in hierarchical nanoporous capsules
(HNC) embedded in porous electrospun fibers (PEF) was developed for direct air capture (DAC).
This involves tailoring both sorbent and PEF materials to achieve a compact system for DAC with
high capacity for CO2 at concentrations typically available in air and at near ambient conditions.
The sorbent material was prepared by an interfacial reaction to form a polyamide (PA) coating on
amine-loaded mesoporous silica. The optimized sorbent material exhibited CO2 capacity as high
as 4.88 mmol/g under humid DAC conditions and negligible loss (<1%) during 10 cyclic heating-
cooling operations. The PA-coated sorbent also showed fast adsorption and desorption kinetics,
with <20% t1/2 increase compared to uncoated sorbent. After incorporating the optimized sorbent
material into PEF, the structured sorbent had a CO2 capacity of approximately 4.0 mmol/g under
humid DAC conditions, with capacity loss of 0.17% per cycle and t1/2 increase less than 10%.

 A 4-step process was designed to capture CO2 from air using the optimized sorbent. Aspen
Adsorption Simulator was used to fit the experimentally measured breakthrough curves and extract
equilibrium and kinetic data. Techno-Economic Analysis (TEA) was conducted to calculate the
CO2 capture cost. For a DAC plant with CO2 productivity of approximately 3,000 tonne/year, the
levelized cost of CO2 capture was $612/tonne, with the largest contribution of 44.33% from the
fixed operation cost. Increasing CO2 productivity, while maintaining similar fixed operation cost,
is expected to significantly reduce the CO2 capture cost. A sensitivity study was also conducted
to understand the influence of total plant cost, sorbent cost, CO2 concentration in the feed, sorbent
mat lifetime, sorbent regeneration electricity, and adsorption blower pressure drop on the levelized
cost of CO2 capture, revealing a capture cost range of $520-870/tonne.

Executive Summary

 This report summarizes the carbon capture research and development conducted by The State
University of New York at Buffalo (UB) and GTI Energy (GTI) for award “DE-FE0031969: Direct
Air Capture Using Trapped Small Amines in Hierarchical Nanoporous Capsules on Porous
Electrospun Fibers” sponsored by the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE). The objective of this
project is to develop an innovative sorbent structure of trapped small amines in HNC embedded in
PEF for DAC. This involves tailoring both sorbent and PEF materials to achieve a compact system
for DAC with high capacity for CO2 at concentrations typically available in air and at near ambient
conditions.
 An innovative sorbent structure of trapped small amines in hierarchical nanoporous capsules
(HNC) embedded in porous electrospun fibers (PEF) was developed for direct air capture (DAC).
This involves tailoring both sorbent and PEF materials to achieve a compact system for DAC with
high capacity for CO2 at concentrations typically available in air and at near ambient conditions.
An interfacial polymerization process was developed, which utilized loaded amines inside
mesoporous silica and trimesoyl chloride (TMC) dissolved in organic solvents as the precursors,
to generate a polyamide (PA) coating layer on mesoporous silica and thus trap amines. Reaction
conditions, including TMC concentration, organic solvents, reaction time, etc., for interfacial
polymerization were optimized to effectively trap loaded amines, and cyclic heating-cooling
operation was conducted to evaluate the coating quality. Larger pore volume mesoporous silica
was also synthesized to increase amine loading and thus increase CO2 capacity.
 The optimized sorbent material exhibited CO2 capacity as high as 4.88 mmol/g under humid
DAC conditions and negligible loss (<1%) during 10 cyclic heating-cooling operations. The
optimized PA-coated sorbent also showed fast adsorption and desorption kinetics, with <20% t1/2
increase compared to uncoated sorbent. PEF fabrication conditions, including organic solvents for
dissolving core and shell polymers, voltage, distance from the nozzle to the collection panel, etc.
were adjusted to better incorporate HNC. After incorporating the optimized sorbent material into
PEF, the structured sorbent had a CO2 capacity of approximately 4.0 mmol/g under humid DAC
conditions, with capacity loss of 0.17% per cycle and t1/2 increase less than 10%.
 A techno-economic analysis (TEA) for the process design for a DAC system based on our
developed sorbent structure of trapped small amines in HNC embedded in PEF was conducted.
The process design included process description and major equipment sizing and energy and mass
balances in addition to scale-up research results and estimated capture cost. Aspen Adsorption
Simulator was used to fit the experimentally measured breakthrough curves and extract
equilibrium and kinetic data of the optimized sorbent. Our results indicated that for a DAC plant
with CO2 productivity of 3,000 tonne/year, the levelized cost of CO2 capture was $612/tonne, with
the largest contribution of 44.33% from the fixed operation cost. Increasing CO2 productivity,
while maintaining similar fixed operation cost, is expected to significantly reduce the CO2 capture
cost. A sensitivity study was also conducted to understand the influence of total plant cost, sorbent
cost, CO2 concentration in the feed, sorbent mat lifetime, sorbent regeneration electricity, and
adsorption blower pressure drop on the levelized cost of CO2 capture, revealing a capture cost
range of $520-870/tonne.

2. Experimental Methods, Assumptions, and Procedures

2.1. Preparation of Hierarchical Nanoporous Capsules

2.1.1. Synthesis of High Pore Volume Silica

 Triblock copolymer poly(ethylene oxide)-b-poly(propylene oxide)-b-poly(ethylene oxide)
surfactant P123 (EO20PO70EO20, Mv ~ 5800), sodium metasilicate, acetic acid, ammonium
fluoride, and 1,3,5-trimethylbenzene (TMB), were purchased from Aldrich and used as received.
Methanol (≥99.8%, Macron Fine Chemicals) were used as received. Deionized water was used in
all experiments. N2 used for calcination was Ultra High Purity (UHP) Grade N2 (Airgas, NI
UHP300, 99.999% N2).

 High porous volume mesoporous silica was prepared following the synthesis recipe reported
in the literature.20 In the reported synthesis, an emulsion was prepared by adding TMB (60 g) to a
homogeneous surfactant solution containing P123 (45.0 g), acetic acid (67.0 g), water (750 g), and
ammonium fluoride (0.9 g) at 45°C. To this emulsion, a solution of sodium metasilicate (46.5 g)
in water (600 g) preheated to 45°C was poured under vigorous stirring. The resulting white slurry
was then kept statically at 45°C for 24 h and aged at 100°C for another 24 h. The product was
collected by filtration, washed with DI water, and calcined at 560°C for 6 h. When replicating the
exact synthesis procedure for the high pore volume mesoporous silica, the batch was too large to
easily process with available equipment and led to samples with no accessible pores, so batches
were prepared at 1/20 and 1/10 of the reported size. The calcination conditions and template
removal are important for pore formation, but the article neglected to report all of the conditions
that are important for calcination, notably the ramp rate. Samples were subsequently prepared
using ramp rates of 0.25-1oC/min, different calcination gases, and an additional template extraction
step prior to calcination. In addition, template extraction via methanol was explored as an option
to controllably remove the template with an additional calcination step to ensure full template
removal.

2.1.2. Amine loading in Mesoporous Silica

 Mesoporous silica SBA-15 (ACS Materials, LLC), diethylenetriamine (DETA, 99%, Sigma-
Aldrich), tris(3-aminopropyl) amine (TAPA, 95%, TCI Chemicals), trimesoyl chloride (TMC,
98%, Sigma-Aldrich), anhydrous toluene (99.8%, Fisher Scientific), and methanol (≥99.8%,
Macron Fine Chemicals) were used as received. Synthesized high pore volume mesoporous silica
was also used as a substrate for amine loading.

 Amine-loaded sorbents were prepared by wet impregnation. Amines were dissolved in
methanol and mixed with SBA-15 or high pore volume mesoporous silica, stirred for 4 h at room
temperature (~25°C), then dried under vacuum at room temperature for 24 h to achieve desired
weight percentage of amine loading in mesoporous silica.

2.2. Preparation of Porous Electrospun Fibers with Loaded HNC

 Matrimid (Matrimid) was provided by Huntsman Advanced Materials. Poly(ethylene oxide)
(PEO) with molecular weight of 400,000 Da was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. The solvents
methanol (CH3OH, 99.8%) and dichloromethane (CH2Cl2, 99.5%) were purchased from Fischer-
Scientific.

 Fabrication of the fibers was based on previous literature reports that created sorbents
embedded in porous electrospun fibers.21,22 The fabrication process was shown schematically in
Figure 7.

 11

Figure 7. Schematic showing the process of fabricating porous electrospun fibers. SPAR: solvothermal
 polymer additive removal; ENNC: electrospun nanoﬁber/nanoparticle composites.

 Fibers were electrospun in CH2Cl2 solutions in different mass ratios of Matrimid: PEO:
sorbent. Sorbent was suspended in polymeric solutions, and a custom-designed electrospinning
apparatus, as shown in Figure 8, was used for electrospinning. Electrospinning solutions were
loaded into a syringe for electrospinning. The fibers were deposited on the aluminum sheets and
parameters of the voltage, collector plate distance, and flow rate were varied to gain an
understanding of how to operate the equipment. Ranges of variables included flow rates between
0.05 and 0.2 mL/min, voltages between 10 and 25 kV, and working distances between 5 and 20
cm with aluminum foil as the grounded collector. To wash samples, fibers were immersed in 15
mL methanol solutions and heated to 65°C for 12 h, until they were within 5% of the expected
mass, considering complete PEO removal. Samples were activated at 45°C under vacuum for 100
h before further tests.

2.3. Characterization of Sorbents

2.3.1. General Characterization

 The surface and cross-sectional morphology of HNC and PEF were characterized by Focused
Ion Beam Scanning Electron Microscope (FIB-SEM)-Carl Zeiss AURIGA. Transmission Electron
Microscopy (TEM, JEOL 2010F, 200 kV) was also used to characterize coating morphology.
Samples dispersed in ethanol were deposited on lacey carbon copper grids and dried before
imaging. Coating presence and thickness were assessed from multiple images. Fourier-transform

 12

Infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, BRUKER VERTEX 70) was performed to investigate the PA-coated
sorbents. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, TA Instruments SDT-650) was conducted under
flowing N2 (100 mL min-1) or air. For derivative TGA analysis, samples were heated at 3°C min-1
from 100-800°C under air after initial 1-h hold at 100°C under nitrogen. Sorbent materials were
also characterized by 77 K N2 adsorption isotherms collected on the 3Flex Physisorption Station
by Micromeritics Instrument Corporation. Sorbents were degassed at 60°C for 18 h under vacuum
before adsorption experiments. N2 used for TGA and adsorption isotherms was Ultra High Purity
(UHP) Grade N2 (Airgas, NI UHP300, 99.999% N2).

2.3.2. Sorbent Cyclic Stability Characterization

 Standard thermal cycling protocol for stability testing: (1) Regeneration at 100°C under N2
(100 mL min-1, 2 h, 10°C min-1 ramp) to ensure baseline; (2) Cooling to 30°C under N2; (3) CO2
adsorption from 15% CO2/N2 (100 mL min-1, 30 min); (4) Thermal desorption at 100°C under N2
(60 min). The 1-h desorption time was selected to provide extended thermal stress while ensuring
complete CO2 release. This protocol was repeated for 5-15 cycles, and the data was collected via
TGA.

2.4. Adsorption and Desorption Measurements

2.4.1. CO2 Adsorption Isotherm Measurements

 CO2 adsorption isotherms were measured at 30°C via Micromeritics 3Flex using pure CO2
with pressures ranging from 0-100 kPa. Samples were degassed at 60°C for 4 h under vacuum
before measurement.

2.4.2. Simulated DAC Performance Measurements

 DAC performance under dry conditions was evaluated using synthetic gas mixtures with
TGA. Standard procedure involved initial desorption at 100°C under N2 for 15 min to establish
baseline, followed by adsorption with 400 ppm CO2 in N2 at 30°C, and desorption with pure N2 at
30°C. Flow rates were maintained at 100 standard cubic centimeter per minute (sccm) throughout
all measurements. N2 used was UHP Grade N2, and synthetic gas used was a custom-blend 400
ppm CO2 in N2 cylinder (Airgas, 400 ppm CO2, balance N2).

 Humid DAC cycling was evaluated using a custom fixed-bed system with mass flow
controllers and continuous CO2 analysis (Li-Cor LI-850), as shown in Figure 9. Sorbent materials
(50 mg) were diluted 1:20 by mass with commercially available inert glass beads (150-210 μm)
and packed in a 4 mm ID stainless steel tube. During adsorption, synthetic air (400 ppm CO2 in
N2, Airgas) was humidified to 70% relative humidity and fed to the bed at 100 sccm and room
temperature (~23°C). Adsorption was conducted for 60 min, followed by regeneration using pure
N2 (UHP Grade, Airgas, NI UHP300, 99.999% N2) at 300 sccm for 3 h at different temperatures.
This protocol was repeated to assess DAC capacity an cycling stability under humid conditions
representative of atmospheric DAC operations.

2.5. Modeling and Process Simulation

 The process configuration and design basis for a 3,123 tonne/year CO₂ DAC system is
described below. It outlines the DAC process and plant layout. Figure 10 illustrates the block flow
diagram for the proposed DAC plant, while Figure 11 provides a breakdown of the process flow
diagram steps for one DAC cycle. The DAC system begins with adsorption blowers that deliver
ambient air to sorbent mats. A mat is defined as an array of beds operating under identical
conditions; in this system, there are 5 mats, each comprising 15 beds. The air blowers
simultaneously supply two mats for a duration of one hour during the adsorption phase. The time
for adsorption was selected as an optimum value achieving higher CO2 capture efficiency based
on experimental results and simulation results using Aspen Adsorption.

 14

 Figure 10. Full DAC schematic diagram for the proposed DAC plant.

 Upon completion of adsorption, the respective mat is sealed, and thermal regeneration is
initiated by heating the sorbent to 100°C. Concurrently, a vacuum is applied to facilitate desorption
of CO₂ and co-adsorbed water. The vacuum step is performed on one mat at a time, with each
desorption cycle requiring approximately 30 min. Consequently, two mats are desorbed per hour.
The vacuum level and period were also selected based on experimental results and an optimization
study done using Aspen Adsorption software.

 Since water constitutes a significant fraction of the desorbed stream, a primary condenser is
positioned upstream of the vacuum pump to condense the bulk of the water vapor. This step
improves pump efficiency and mitigates the risk of pump water overloading. Residual water vapor
exiting the vacuum pump is removed via a secondary condenser located downstream. The resulting

 15

CO₂-rich stream is subsequently compressed to the desired outlet pressure for downstream
utilization or storage. Capacity of equipment is estimated using Aspen Plus flowsheet.

 Following desorption, a cooling air blower directs ambient air through the regenerated mats
for 30 min, reducing their temperature to prepare them for the next adsorption cycle.
Simultaneously with the desorption step, the desorbed water and carbon dioxide are redirected
toward a neighboring mat, serving as a preheating step to recover and transfer sensible heat. This
strategy improves overall energy efficiency by reducing the thermal load during subsequent
regeneration.

 Figure 11 provides a detailed breakdown of the steps and their sequence in a DAC cycle. Each
step has a duration of 30 min, resulting in a total cycle time of 2.5 hours. The figure illustrates the
initial step for each mat in the system, with subsequent steps following sequentially every 30 min
as the mat transitions from one operating mode to the next. An optimization study was conducted
using Aspen Adsorption to optimize the time for each step in the DAC cycle.

3. Results and Discussion
3.1. High Pore Volume Mesoporous Silica Synthesis and Characterization

 In order to reach higher CO2 capacities for amine-functionalized sorbents, a higher loading of
amines in a substrate is necessary. Substrates that have higher pore volumes are ideal for this
purpose and enable higher amine loadings, often achieving the highest reported CO2 capacities.
After an investigation into literature, a mesoporous silica was found with one of the highest
reported pore volumes (2.69 cm3/g) as well as one of the highest reported CO2 capacities (11.8
mmol/g in 8% CO2) after being functionalized with amines.3 Using the synthesis recipe for the
silica provided in the supplementary information, samples of the mesoporous silica were prepared
and characterized.

 As mentioned previously, the calcination conditions and template removal are important for
pore formation, but the article neglected to report all of the conditions that are important for
calcination, notably the ramp rate. Samples were subsequently prepared using ramp rates of 0.25-
1oC/min, different calcination gases, and an additional template extraction step prior to calcination.
It was hypothesized that using N2 as the calcination gas would allow for some template to remain
in the structure and that performing another calcination step in air would remove the remaining
template. The prepared samples were evaluated for their pore characteristics via N2 adsorption at
77 K. As indicated in Table 4, this proved to be true with sample MS8.1 showing the next highest
pore volume of samples at 2.36 cm3/g followed by MS8 at 2.25 cm3/g. In addition, template
extraction via methanol was explored as an option to controllably remove the template with an
additional calcination step to ensure full template removal. The samples MS10 and MS11 showed
that this was beneficial, revealing that the highest pore volume was achieved by MS11 at 3.12
cm3/g, even greater than the literature report of 2.69 cm3/g.20

3.2. HNC and PEF Fabrication and Characterization

3.2.1. HNC Fabrication and Structure and Composition Characterization

 A PA coating was generated on the outer surface of mesoporous silica (SBA-15 and high pore
volume mesoporous silica) loaded with amines by interfacial polymerization to seal amines. Once
the silica pores were entirely filled, an interfacial reaction was performed to generate a dense
coating at the pore mouth. The underlying reaction is the Schotten-Baumann reaction, involving a
reactant containing an acid chloride group and another reactant with an amine group, where the
two functional groups react to synthesize an amide structure. This reaction can become a

 19

polymerization reaction using molecules such as TMC and amines, like DETA, which have
multiple available functional groups, resulting in thermally stable and dense coatings. With this
understanding, a coating was generated by reacting amine-loaded SBA-15 or high pore volume
mesoporous silica with a solution of TMC in hexane for a desired amount of time. During the
process of coating development, influence of different reaction parameters on the coating quality
was investigated. The purpose was to generate a coating which can seal amines in silica pores and
thus maintain consistent CO2 uptake during long-term thermal treatment. Different parameters
studied were concentration of TMC in hexane or tulene, amount of TMC that the amine-loaded
SBA-15 is exposed to, the reaction time, the stirring rate, and water/amine loading. Table 5 shows
the representative sorbents with PA coatings that were discussed below.

derivative TGA were performed on various components and sorbents (Figure 15a and b). For
convenience, samples were designated as TX-Y where X represents TMC concentration (wt.%)
and Y represents solution-to-sorbent mass ratio (e.g., T2-0.5x indicates 2 wt.% TMC with

TAPA/SBA-15 sample reveals new peaks around 3280 and 3360 cm-1 as the doublet for primary

Pure TAPA shows an evaporation range from 100-235°C with maximum mass loss rate at
approximately 200°C, confirming its high volatility at temperatures relevant to DAC regeneration.
Bare SBA-15 shows no significant mass loss across the entire temperature range (100-800°C),
serving as an inert support baseline. TAPA-loaded SBA-15 shows a broad feature similar to pure
TAPA centered around 180°C corresponding to TAPA thermal evaporation from the pore
structure.

 The T2-0.5x sample exhibits two additional broad decomposition features at approximately
215-350°C and 400-650°C that are absent in the uncoated material. These features align with those
observed in pure PA synthesized separately from TMC and TAPA (225-330°C and 420-650°C),
confirming the presence of PA coating in the T2-0.5x sample. The lower-temperature feature (215-
350°C) likely corresponds to decomposition of lightly crosslinked PA species and oligomers,
while the higher-temperature feature (400-650°C) is attributed to degradation of the more
extensively crosslinked PA network.24

 Notably, the initial TAPA evaporation peak (100-200°C) in T2-0.5x shows similar intensity
to the uncoated sample despite the presence of the coating. This suggests that at the elevated
temperatures used in TGA analysis, the PA coating becomes sufficiently permeable to allow rapid
TAPA diffusion and evaporation. The increased molecular mobility at high temperatures may
temporarily compromise the coating's barrier properties, allowing TAPA escape during TGA
analysis even though the coating effectively prevents amine loss under the milder conditions of
cyclic adsorption-desorption operation (100°C). This temperature-dependent permeability is
consistent with literature, where liquids such as water are observed to permeate significantly faster
as operating temperature increases.25 The coating's effectiveness is therefore best evaluated under
operationally relevant conditions rather than through high-temperature TGA analysis alone.

 Systematic comparison of derivative TGA across all coated samples (Figure 15c) reveals how
synthesis parameters affect coating formation and structure. T1-1x shows a weak PA
decomposition feature in the 215-350°C range, indicating limited coating coverage consistent with
insufficient solution volume despite adequate total TMC availability. T1-4x exhibits prominent,
sharp PA peaks centered at 215-260°C, attributed to a relatively uniform population of lightly
crosslinked PA oligomers formed when excess solution volume allows TMC penetration slightly
beyond the immediate pore mouth region. T2-0.5x displays a broader PA decomposition
distribution spanning 215-350°C, suggesting a more heterogeneous coating structure comprising
a highly crosslinked external barrier at the pore mouth with peripheral oligomeric species at the
coating-pore interface. This structural heterogeneity likely arises from the higher TMC
concentration (2 wt.% vs. 1 wt.%) driving faster interfacial polymerization that depletes reactants
before extensive pore penetration occurs, creating a sharp concentration gradient and
corresponding gradient in crosslink density. All samples retain the higher-temperature PA

 21

decomposition feature (400-650°C) corresponding to extensively crosslinked network regions,
with intensity scaling according to overall coating coverage: T2-0.5x ≈ T1-4x > T1-1x. The TGA
characterization thus provides direct compositional evidence for successful PA formation while
revealing that synthesis parameters systematically control coating coverage, structure, and
homogeneity, which subsequently determines thermal stability and capture performance as
discussed below.

3.2.2. Optimization of HNC Fabrication and Stability Evaluation

Effect of solution-to-sorbent mass ratio at constant TMC concentration

 We first investigated the effect of solution-to-sorbent mass ratio on coating formation using
fixed TMC concentration (1 wt.%) and 10-min reaction time. For 0.1 g sorbent (58.1 wt% TAPA
loading, 0.0581 g TAPA, 0.000309 mol), the TMC: TAPA molar ratios are: T1-1x (1.83:1), T1-
2x (3.66:1), T1-4x (7.33:1). All conditions have stoichiometric excess of TMC yet show vastly
different stability performance.

concentration (1 wt%): (a) cycling stability over 10 cycles (1-hour desorption at 100°C per cycle) and (b)

 Figure 16a shows cycling stability under accelerated conditions (1-h desorption at 100°C per
cycle). TAPA-loaded SBA-15 (uncoated sample) exhibits severe degradation from 2.37 to 1.41
mmol g-1 (40.6% loss). T1-1x shows moderate improvement: 1.40 to 0.90 mmol g-1 (35.7% loss).
T1-2x further improves: 1.57 to 1.28 mmol g-1 (18.3% loss). T1-4x shows excellent stability: 1.61
to 1.57 mmol g-1 (only 2.2% loss), an 18-fold improvement. The systematic improvement with
increasing solution volume occurs despite all samples having stoichiometric TMC excess
(TMC:TAPA ratios of 1.83:1 to 7.33:1), indicating that coating quality is limited by solution
distribution rather than chemical availability. This interpretation is directly supported by TGA
characterization (Figure 15b), which shows that the PA decomposition features (215-350°C and
400-650°C) increase systematically in intensity with solution volume: T1-1x < T1-2x < T1-4x.
The correlation between PA content (TGA) and cycling stability (Figure 16a) confirms that
increased solution volume produces more extensive coating coverage. Quantitatively, T1-4x
exhibits PA decomposition accounting for approximately 21% of total mass loss compared to only
16.7% for T1-1x, consistent with the 18-fold difference in stability performance.

 The solution volume dependence likely reflects improved wetting and TMC distribution
throughout the heterogeneous particle bed at higher volumes, as interfacial polymerization occurs
only where TMC solution contacts TAPA-loaded surfaces.26 Insufficient solution volume may
result in incomplete particle wetting or poor penetration into agglomerates, leaving unreacted pore
mouths that allow continued amine loss. The progressive improvement from T1-1x to T1-4x
confirms that coating uniformity across the particle ensemble, not chemical stoichiometry, limits
performance at low solution volumes.

 The varying initial capacities (1.40-1.61 mmol g-1) despite identical initial amine loading
(58.1 wt%) reflect the effects of the coating process on the final CO2 capture traits of the materials.
Reasons for the varying capacities include partial amine loss during coating procedure itself and
differing coating quality, leading to amine loss during the sorbent degassing. T1-1x (lower
capacity) suggests more amine evaporates before protective coating forms on all particles,
consistent with incomplete initial coverage. T1-4x (higher capacity) indicates better amine
preservation through rapid, uniform coating formation.

 However, enhanced stability comes with a kinetic trade-off. Figure 16b shows the time to
reach 95% CO2 capacity under simulated flue gas conditions (15% CO2, 30°C). Uncoated reaches
95% capacity in 6.5 min. T1-1x: 13.7 min (2.1x slower), T1-2x: 15.8 min (2.4x), T1-4x: 18.8 min
(2.9x). All coated samples exhibit biphasic kinetics: an initial rapid phase (0-5 min) followed by a
slower diffusion-limited phase that becomes more pronounced with increasing solution volume.
This behavior reflects sequential transport through two domains: the ultrathin PA coating (~20
nm) at pore mouths and the amine-filled mesoporous network (~10 nm pores, several μm depth).
For such sequential resistances, the overall rate is governed by the slower step. Given the ultrathin
nature of the coating relative to the pore depth, and the high viscosity of the amine-filled pores,
diffusion through the amine-filled pore network likely dominates the overall transport kinetics
rather than passage through the coating itself. This is consistent with the observation that the
kinetic penalty scales with solution volume (T1-1x < T1-2x < T1-4x): increased solution volume
produces more extensive coating coverage and reduced amine loading per particle (as evidenced
by TGA, Figure 15b), which may decrease the effective amine accessibility and increase the
average diffusion path length within the pore network. The modest kinetic penalties (2.1-2.9x

 23

slower) relative to the substantial stability improvements (18-fold for T1-4x) indicate that the
coating strategy successfully balances protection with CO2 transport efficiency.

 The progressive kinetic penalty with increasing solution volume (T1-1x: 2.1x, T1-2x: 2.4x,
T1-4x: 2.9x slower) correlates with the increased PA content observed in TGA analysis (Figure
15c). The more pronounced PA decomposition features in T1-4x compared to T1-1x suggest
greater overall coating mass and potentially more extensive pore mouth coverage. This enhanced
coating coverage, while beneficial for amine retention, increases the diffusional resistance for CO2
transport to amine sites within the pores. The biphasic kinetics becomes more pronounced at higher
solution volumes, consistent with more complete pore mouth encapsulation that restricts gas access
to the internal amine-filled pore network. The trade-off between stability (improved by more
complete coating) and kinetics (slowed by increased diffusion resistance) is evident in the
systematic trends across T1-1x, T1-2x, and T1-4x. While T1-4x achieves excellent stability (>97%
retention), the 2.9-fold kinetic penalty makes it less attractive for applications requiring rapid
cycling.

Effect of TMC concentration at constant TMC-to-TAPA mass ratio

 To decouple concentration effects from total TMC amount, we compared T1-1x (TMC/TAPA
molar ratio: 1.83:1), T2-0.5x (TMC/TAPA molar ratio: 1.83:1), and T10-0.1x (TMC/TAPA molar
ratio: 1.83:1). This maintains constant total TMC amount in solution while varying local
concentration, isolating kinetic effects on coating quality.

 Figure 17a shows T2-0.5x achieves superior stability: 1.33 to 1.31 mmol g-1 (1.5% loss), a

1.62 to 1.14 mmol g-1 (29.5% loss), barely better than uncoated. The divergence despite identical

formation. At moderate concentration (2 wt.%), interfacial polymerization proceeds at controlled
rates allowing uniform network formation. The exothermic reaction and HCl evolution are
manageable as heat dissipates through solution while HCl escapes gradually without disrupting
coating.27,28 At 10 wt.% (10x concentrated, 1/10 volume), the same total TMC reacts much faster.

Rapid polymerization generates intense localized heating (estimated 20-30°C rise based on
adiabatic calculations) combined with vigorous HCl evolution that physically disrupts forming
polymer. This creates structural defects (micropores, cracks, incomplete crosslinking) providing
amine leaching pathways during thermal cycling. This parallels PA membrane literature showing
excessive monomer concentration increases defect density. 27,28

 The fact that T10-0.1x shows similar kinetics to T2-0.5x (18.2 min vs 17.8 min) despite poor
stability supports the defect hypothesis: CO2 bypasses coating through defect pathways
(maintaining reasonable kinetics) while defects permit amine evaporation (poor stability). This
asymmetric selectivity occurs because CO2 diffusion needs only small pathways while amine
retention requires complete encapsulation. T2-0.5x's moderate initial capacity (1.33 mmol g-1 vs
1.61 mmol g-1 for T1-4x) reflects some amine loss during coating due to reduced solution volume
(7.5 mL vs 60 mL), but this is compensated by exceptional stability and superior kinetics.

 Figure 17b shows T2-0.5x reaches 95% capacity in 17.8 min (1.3x slower than T1-1x),
compared to T1-4x at 18.8 min. T2-0.5x's kinetic advantage over T1-4x despite similar stability
results from coating localized at pore mouths without extensive oligomerization within pores. At
2 wt.% with 0.5x volume (7.5 mL), polymerization is rapid and confined to external surfaces.
Limited solution volume restricts TMC penetration time, minimizing oligomer formation within
pores. The amine phase retains low viscosity, allowing rapid CO2 diffusion once gas traverses the
thin external coating.

 T2-0.5x shows less biphasic uptake than T1-4x, with gradual transition between phases,
suggesting thinner/more permeable coating allowing sustained uptake. T1-4x's abrupt transition
indicates distinct layers (external coating and viscous oligomer-enriched region) creating
pronounced diffusion barrier.

 T2-0.5x emerges as optimal due to 98.5% retention (27-fold improvement), 1.3-fold kinetic
penalty, requiring only 7.5 g solvent per 0.1 g sorbent. It balances sufficient solution volume for
uniform particle wetting and complete surface coverage, moderate TMC concentration enabling
controlled polymerization without defects, minimal volume limiting oligomer formation and
maintaining favorable kinetics, and practical, scalable synthesis.

 Fabrication of the co-axial based fibers was carried out. Based on previous results and success

axial electrospinning. The core solution consisted of 0.3 g PEO mixed with 0.3 g SBA-15 in 10

varied to increase the silica loading in the composite fibers. Initially both solutions were spun at

3.3. DAC Performance of Hierarchical Nanoporous Capsules

 Based on systematic optimization, sample T2-0.5x was selected for comprehensive DAC
performance evaluation under both controlled simulated conditions and authentic atmospheric
conditions to validate practical deployment readiness. Figure 27a shows the cycling stability under
simulated dry DAC conditions (400 ppm CO2/N2, 30°C adsorption for 8 hours, 100°C desorption

 30

with 10-minute hold). The coated T2-0.5x material demonstrated exceptional stability over 15
consecutive cycles with capacity remaining constant at 0.97 ± 0.01 mmol/g throughout the entire
cycling test. The consistent capacity across all 15 cycles confirms that the coating maintains its
integrity and protective function under realistic thermal cycling that mimics field deployment,
where minimizing regeneration time and energy is economically important. The absolute capacity
under DAC conditions (0.97 mmol/g) is lower than under accelerated cycling at 15% CO2 (Figure
18) due to the thermodynamic limitation of capturing CO2 from dilute streams. At 400 ppm (0.04%
CO2), the driving force for adsorption is much lower than at 15% CO2, resulting in incomplete
saturation of all available amine sites even after 8 h of exposure. This behavior is typical for amine
sorbents and reflects the fundamental challenge of DAC where low CO2 concentrations limit
achievable working capacities. The optimized HNC was also tested for Dac under humid
conditions (Figure 27b) and showed an initial CO2 capacity of 3.1 mmol/g (calculated by
integrating the breakthrough curves). Also, it showed excellent stability over cyclic operation,
demonstrating almost constant capacity over 5 cycles under humid DAC conditions.

3.6.7. Levelized cost of capture

 To calculate the estimated levelized cost of capture (LCOC), the capital and O&M costs were
used, and the methodologies established in the DOE NETL baseline reports30, 31 were followed.
The levelized capture cost was estimated, applying the global economic assumptions in Table 15.
The current capture cost is approximately $612.25 per tonne of CO₂. As shown in Table 19, the
dominant contributor to this cost is the fixed operating expenses, with a significant share coming
from operating labor. If the plant capacity is increased while maintaining the same number of
personnel, the fixed labor cost would be distributed over a larger amount of CO₂ captured. This
would reduce the fixed-cost contribution per tonne and therefore lower the overall capture cost.
For example, if the plant productivity were doubled to approximately 6,000 tonne/year while
maintaining the same number of operating laborers, the estimated capture cost would decrease by
$117/tonne, assuming a linear increase in other costs.

 The tornado diagram shown in Figure 40 illustrates the results of a sensitivity study on LCOC
for the DAC system. The red arrow simplifies the direction of increase of the studied parameter.
The analysis evaluates the influence of key technical and economic parameters by varying each
within the specified uncertainty ranges while holding the others constant.

 Among the parameters, total plant cost emerges as the dominant driver of LCOC, producing
the widest variation in capture cost. This indicates that capital investment exerts the strongest
leverage on project economics and highlights the importance of accurate cost estimation in early-
stage project assessment. Sorbent regeneration electricity also has a significant impact, shifting the
capture cost by nearly ±$40–60 per tonne of CO₂. This TEA assumes an inlet air concentration of
328 ppm CO₂. If actual site conditions provide slightly higher CO₂ concentrations, production
would increase, and the overall capture cost would decline. Conversely, even a small decrease in
concentration—while keeping all other equipment and operating parameters unchanged—would
reduce production and noticeably raise the capture cost. These results highlight the dual
importance of process energy efficiency and site-specific CO₂ concentrations in determining the
overall feasibility of DAC deployment.

 By contrast, parameters such as sorbent cost, mat lifetime, and adsorption fan pressure drop
display comparatively smaller effects on the LCOC within the studied ranges. While these factors
should not be neglected, their economic weight is less critical than capital expenditure and energy
requirements.

4. Summary and Future Steps

 An innovative sorbent structure of trapped small amines in HNC embedded in PEF was
developed for DAC. This involves tailoring both sorbent and PEF materials to achieve a compact
system for DAC with high capacity for CO2 at concentrations typically available in air and at near
ambient conditions. An interfacial polymerization process was developed, which utilized loaded
amines inside mesoporous silica and TMC dissolved in organic solvents as the precursors, to
generate a PA coating layer on mesoporous silica and thus trap amines. Reaction conditions for
interfacial polymerization were optimized to effectively trap loaded amines, and cyclic heating-
cooling operation was conducted to evaluate the coating quality. Larger pore volume mesoporous
silica was also synthesized to increase amine loading and thus increase CO2 capacity.

 The optimized sorbent material exhibited CO2 capacity as high as 4.88 mmol/g under humid
DAC conditions and negligible loss (<1%) during 10 cyclic heating-cooling operations. The
optimized PA-coated sorbent also showed fast adsorption and desorption kinetics, with <20% t1/2
increase compared to uncoated sorbent. PEF fabrication conditions were adjusted to better
incorporate HNC. After incorporating the optimized sorbent material into PEF, the structured
sorbent had a CO2 capacity of approximately 4.0 mmol/g under humid DAC conditions, with
capacity loss of 0.17% per cycle and t1/2 increase less than 10%.

 TEA for the process design for a DAC system based on our developed sorbent structure of
trapped small amines in HNC embedded in PEF was conducted. Aspen Adsorption Simulator was
used to fit the experimentally measured breakthrough curves and extract equilibrium and kinetic
data of the optimized sorbent. Our results indicated that for a DAC plant with CO2 productivity of
3,000 tonne/year, the levelized cost of CO2 capture was $612/tonne. A sensitivity study was also
conducted, revealing a capture cost range of $520-870/tonne.

 The next phase would be a bench-scale development including the following:

 ▪ Sorbent material scaleup
 ▪ Structured sorbent scaleup
 ▪ Design and construction of the test unit with integrated components
 ▪ Sorbent mat configuration optimization for process scaleup
 ▪ Demonstrate DAC performance by integrated testing for 3-6 months
 ▪ Perform TEA and sensitivity studies and determine operating conditions for low-cost
 DAC
