Ipsilateral adrenal metastasis with adrenal and residual renal vein tumor thrombus from ESC-RCC 6 years post-nephrectomy: A case report
Authors: Longchao Chen, Zhigang Xu, Chuanhua Zhong, Hu He, Fangyu Li, Yuanyuan Yang, Hao Tang, Jinhong Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEosinophilic solid and cystic renal cell carcinoma (ESC-RCC) is a rare, indolent renal tumor. We report a 57-year-old female who underwent left radical nephrectomy with adrenal sparing for ESC-RCC in 2018. Six years later, she developed a solitary ipsilateral adrenal metastasis with tumor thrombus in the adrenal and residual renal veins. Pazopanib was discontinued. Robotic adrenalectomy and venous thrombectomy succeeded. Pathology confirmed metastatic ESC-RCC. At one-year follow-up, no recurrence. This case shows ESC-RCC can produce late adrenal metastases with venous thrombus, and resection achieves favorable outcomes.Keywords: ESC-RCC, Ipsilateral adrenal metastasis, Tumor thrombus, Robotic adrenalectomy

Introduction

ESC-RCC is a novel entity recognized in the 2022 WHO classification, accounting for approximately 0.2% of renal tumors.1 It is characterized by solid and cystic architecture, eosinophilic cytoplasm, perinuclear halos, and frequent TSC1/TSC2 mutations.2,3 Although generally indolent with low metastatic potential,4 its long-term natural history remains incompletely defined. We present an exceptional case of late ipsilateral adrenal metastasis from ESC-RCC with tumor thrombus in the adrenal and residual renal veins, occurring 6 years after radical nephrectomy.

Case presentation

A 57-year-old female was incidentally found to have a left upper pole renal mass in October 2018. Abdominal contrast-enhanced CT showed a 100 × 80 × 80 mm left renal mass with heterogeneous enhancement and no lymphadenopathy (Fig. 1). Chest CT was normal. Preoperative CT showed no adrenal invasion, and intraoperatively the tumor had a clear boundary from the adrenal gland. She underwent left laparoscopic radical nephrectomy with ipsilateral adrenal sparing at another hospital.

Abdominal contrast-enhanced CT. (A) Axial and (B) coronal sections showing a heterogeneously enhanced left upper pole renal mass with a normal left adrenal gland.

Pathology revealed tumor cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasmic granules (Fig. 2). Immunohistochemistry: CK(+), CK20(scattered+), CK7(±), CD117(−), P504S(−), PAX8(+), RCC(−), CD10(+), TFE3(−), Ki-67(5%), MelanA(−). Diagnosis: ESC-RCC, pT2aN0M0. The original pathology report from the outside hospital confirmed all surgical margins were negative, including the renal vascular and ureteral margins (R0 resection). The diagnosis was rendered by experienced pathologists at the outside hospital. The morphological and immunohistochemical features (CK20 scattered+, CK7±, CD117-, PAX8+, CD10+, TFE3-, MelanA-) are highly characteristic of ESC-RCC and effectively exclude most other eosinophilic renal neoplasms. She received adjuvant immunotherapy with interferon-alpha and interleukin-2 at the outside hospital in 2018. Of note, this cytokine-based regimen has never been shown to provide a survival benefit in the adjuvant setting for renal cell carcinoma in randomized phase III trials,5,6 and the patient did not meet the contemporary high-risk criteria for adjuvant pembrolizumab defined by the KEYNOTE-564 trial.7,8 She was followed for 3 years. Two years before the current presentation, she underwent right renal cystectomy.

Pathological specimen showing tumor cells with abundant eosinophilic punctate cytoplasmic granules.

In March 2025, a left adrenal mass was incidentally found during a gallstone checkup. CT showed a 61 × 53 × 50 mm left adrenal mass with heterogeneous enhancement and central necrosis, invading the adrenal vein and residual renal vein with tumor thrombus (Fig. 3). Hormonal evaluation was non-functional. No other recurrence was noted.

Abdominal contrast‑enhanced CT at recurrence (March 2025). (A) Coronal section showing a 61×53×50 mm left adrenal mass with heterogeneous enhancement. (B) Tumor thrombus extending into the adrenal vein. (C) Tumor thrombus in the residual renal vein.

Pazopanib (800 mg QD) was initiated but discontinued due to severe anorexia and nausea. Since the metastasis was solitary, left adrenalectomy was planned. In April 2025, she underwent robotic-assisted left adrenalectomy with adrenal vein and residual renal vein thrombectomy (Fig. 4). The postoperative course was uneventful, and she was discharged on day 5. At one-year follow-up, CT showed no recurrence, and the patient remained asymptomatic.

Intraoperative and gross specimen photographs. (A) Exposure of the left adrenal mass. (B) Exposure of the left adrenal vein and left renal vein. (C) Exposure of the left renal vein and left ovarian vein. (D) Complete resection of the tumor and tumor thrombus. (E) Gross specimen – external view. (F) Gross specimen – cut section.

Pathological examination of the resected specimen revealed a 7 × 6 × 5 cm grey-white solid mass. A vessel attached to the mass contained tumor thrombus. Microscopically, the tumor had both cystic and solid areas. Solid areas showed cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasmic granules, round to oval nuclei arranged in nests and sheets, and eosinophilic bodies. Immunohistochemistry showed variable positivity for PAX-8, CK7, vimentin, and CD10, and negativity for α-inhibin, CgA, Melan-A, and syn (Fig. 5). These findings confirmed a renal origin, and the final diagnosis was metastatic ESC-RCC.

Pathological and immunohistochemical examination of the adrenal metastasis. (A) Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining showing solid areas of tumor cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm (×100). (B) Higher magnification demonstrating eosinophilic cytoplasmic granules and round to oval nuclei arranged in nests and sheets (HE, ×200). (C) Immunohistochemistry showing diffuse nuclear positivity for PAX‑8 (×100). (D) Higher magnification confirming PAX‑8 expression (×200), supporting renal origin. The tumor cells were negative for α‑inhibin, CgA, Melan‑A, and syn (not shown).

Discussion

This case demonstrates that ESC-RCC, despite its indolent behavior, can produce a late, anatomically complex ipsilateral adrenal metastasis with tumor thrombus in the adrenal and residual renal veins six years after radical nephrectomy. To our knowledge, such a pattern has not been reported for ESC-RCC, challenging the assumption that long-term disease-free survival equates to cure and supporting extended surveillance.

The patient underwent adrenal-sparing radical nephrectomy based on negative preoperative imaging and a clear intraoperative plane. This aligns with evidence favoring selective over routine adrenalectomy. A meta-analysis of 11,736 patients found that upper-pole location does not predict adrenal involvement, and routine adrenalectomy offers no survival benefit over adrenal-sparing surgery (HR 0.89; P = 0.43), with a negative predictive value of 98.5% for preoperative imaging.9 For early-stage tumors with normal adrenal imaging, routine adrenalectomy confers no prognostic benefit. Thus, the initial adrenal sparing was appropriate, and the late metastasis reflects inherent tumor biology rather than surgical failure.

When the adrenal metastasis developed, it was solitary but with venous thrombus. Pazopanib was not tolerated. For solitary metastatic RCC, surgery remains the standard. Prospective data show that RCC adrenal metastases have a 5-year cancer-specific survival of 55.9% after resection, significantly better than other histologies.10 Another study reported 5-year overall survival of 74.4%.11 The successful robotic thrombectomy in this case supports the feasibility of minimally invasive approaches even with venous thrombus.

The molecular profile of ESC-RCC (frequent TSC1/TSC2 mutations2,3) suggests susceptibility to mTOR inhibitors. A phase II basket trial of everolimus in TSC1/TSC2-altered tumors demonstrated clinical benefit.12 This represents a rational option for advanced or unresectable disease.

We acknowledge that this case has diagnostic limitations: no independent pathology review was performed, and ancillary molecular testing (e.g., FH immunohistochemistry or TSC1/TSC2 sequencing) was not carried out. While the morphological and immunohistochemical features strongly support ESC-RCC, these limitations should be considered when interpreting the diagnosis. We encourage routine subspecialty pathology review and molecular correlation in future cases to more reliably exclude other eosinophilic renal neoplasms.

We also acknowledge that the adjuvant therapy administered at the outside hospital—interferon-alpha combined with interleukin-2—does not align with current evidence-based standards. Several randomized phase III trials have demonstrated that adjuvant cytokine-based immunotherapy offers no disease-free or overall survival benefit over observation in patients with resected renal cell carcinoma,5,6 and contemporary reviews have concluded that adjuvant interferon and tyrosine kinase inhibitors were ineffective in this setting.13 By contemporary standards, this pT2aN0M0 patient would not have met the high-risk criteria for adjuvant pembrolizumab as defined by the KEYNOTE-564 trial (which included pT2 with Grade 4 or sarcomatoid features, T3 or higher, node-positive disease, or M1 NED)7,8; moreover, the KEYNOTE-564 evidence pertains primarily to clear cell renal cell carcinoma14 and has not been established for ESC-RCC. The decision to treat adjuvantly with cytokines reflects practice patterns from an earlier era and should not influence clinical management for similar patients today.

Limitations include single case report, ongoing follow-up, and lack of genetic testing.

Conclusion

This case provides the first evidence that ESC-RCC can metastasize to the ipsilateral adrenal gland with venous tumor thrombus as late as six years after radical nephrectomy. Complete surgical resection achieved no recurrence at one year, supporting curative-intent surgery for solitary adrenal metastases even with venous thrombus. Extended surveillance is warranted for ESC-RCC.
