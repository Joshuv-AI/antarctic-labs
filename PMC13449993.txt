Protocol for transcriptional profiling of fiber type and regeneration markers in mouse Tibialis Anterior muscle
Authors: Zuleyha Yildirim, Aurélia Noll, Jean-Christophe Amé, Françoise Dantzer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySkeletal muscle integrity and function depend on fiber-type composition and regenerative capacity. Evaluating both components in normal and injured tissues provides valuable insights into subtle declines in muscle function and recovery. We describe a protocol for the transcriptional analysis of the major fast-twitch type II myofibers and regeneration-related genes in normal and local-injured mouse Tibialis Anterior skeletal muscles. We describe steps for integrating freeze-induced injury, sample collection, tissue dissociation, RNA extraction, and gene expression analysis by quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR).Subject areas: Cell Biology, Molecular Biology, Tissue Engineering

Before you begin

While substantial effort has gone into myofiber typing and regeneration profiling using immunohistochemistry coupled with automated image analysis, these approaches remain technically demanding, requiring intensive optimization. In addition, we observed that their ability to accurately analyse subtle, non-penetrant phenotypes, such as those identified in Parp3-deficient mice or associated with aging, remain constrained. To overcome these limitations, we have successfully implemented an RT-qPCR-based protocol that captures the transcriptional landscape underlying muscle fiber type composition and myogenic regeneration through a target-driven gene signature strategy.

The protocol below describes the specific steps for freeze-induced Tibialis Anterior (TA) skeletal muscle damage and sample collection throughout regeneration that can be used in both young and old mice. Then we describe steps to analyse target-driven gene expression changes associated with fiber type specification and tissue regeneration. While optimized for TA muscles, this workflow can be adapted for other skeletal muscles, experimental models, or human muscle samples to investigate age-associated transcriptional adaptations or modest alterations of fiber type composition and muscle regeneration.

Innovation

In our hands, this protocol provides the most straightforward and rapidly standardized methodology to reveal minor or modest alterations in myofiber type composition, skeletal regeneration or muscle integrity. The target-driven transcriptional signature of fast-twitch type myofibers combined with ongoing regeneration markers and markers of fiber maturity by RT-qPCR offers a fast and affordable methodology for a refined quantitative assessment of muscle function and integrity that can be missed using Eosin-Hematoxylin (H&E) histology.

Step-by-step method details

Freeze-induced TA muscle injury

Timing: 15–20 min per mouse (2 muscles)

Freeze injury is described to induce inflammation-dominant tissue injury. Since age-related muscle pathologies are often driven by chronic and dysregulated inflammation, the freeze-injury based protocol better mimics the biological environment of aging tissues. Moreover, freeze-induced tissue damage elicits a robust regeneration demand, thereby being more appropriate when a subtle phenotype needs to be highlighted.

Note: The extent of injury should be adjusted and standardized according to the duration of the freeze-thaw cycle and the number of repeats. A freeze duration of 15 sec is recommended for analyses up to 14 days post-injury. A pilot study might be necessary to evaluate the optimal duration of freezing and the number of cycles with your animal model. In our experimental conditions, we used two freeze-thaw cycles in total. In addition, the appropriate kinetic of regeneration response can be verified by H&E staining of muscle sections to identify myofibers with centrally located nuclei used as robust indicators of recent myofiber regeneration.

RNA extraction from TRIzol-homogenized Tibialis Anterior muscle using the RNeasy Mini Kit

Timing: 60–75 min for 12 samples

The extraction of total RNA from TRIzol-homogenized TA skeletal muscle biopsies is performed using silica-membrane spin columns (RNeasy Mini Kit, Qiagen), with TRIzol used as the lysis buffer instead of the chaotropic lysis buffer RLT supplemented with β-mercaptoethanol. Trizol gives higher yield and better integrity for biopsies fibrous and rich in connective tissues like skeletal muscles (TS4).

Expected outcomes

The expression levels of the early regeneration markers (Myh3, Myh8, Desmin and Vimentin) characterizing sustained ongoing regeneration, increase in conditions of age-related or modest pathological muscle decline as detected in Parp3-deficient TA (Figure 4). Markers of myofiber maturity (Myoz1, Myoz2, Myoz3 and Tnni2) also tend to increase in the context of a modest muscle deterioration, indicative of substantial muscle regeneration.

Moreover, both sets of markers display a specific expression signature in the context of acute injury-induced regeneration. In response to freeze injury, the expression levels of the early regeneration markers Myh3, Myh8 and Vimentin first notably increase at day 4 post-injury when regeneration culminates and then gradually decline up to day 14 post-injury when regeneration terminates (Figure 5A). In contrast the expression level of Desmin transiently declines at day 7 and returns to control levels by day 14. Over the same time frame, the expression levels of the indicators of myofiber maturity (Myoz1, Myoz2, Myoz3, Tnni2) decline at day 4 post-injury, maintain a reduced level up to day 7 post-injury and then gradually increase again when muscle repair has advanced (Figure 5B).

In conclusion, when no obvious signs of altered injury-induced regeneration are seen using standard H&E staining of TA cryosections, the analysis of early and terminal muscle regeneration-related genes allows a more detailed analysis for differences in profile dynamics.

Also subtle chronic regeneration, occurring without major impact on muscle integrity and with only a modest increase in centrally nucleated myofibers (CNMs), can be revealed through enhanced expression of muscle regeneration-related genes and/or modified expression of fast-twitch type II genes.

Limitations

A first limit is that this protocol is optimized for RNA extraction and gene-expression analysis from Tibialis Anterior (TA) muscle, a tissue that is rich in contractile proteins and difficult to lyse. Although homogenization in TRIzol combined with column-based purification yields RNA of sufficient quality for RT-qPCR, this workflow may produce lower RNA integrity than phenol-chloroform phase separation, especially if samples partially thaw during handling or homogenization. In addition, the use of TRIzol in place of the RLT + β-mercaptoethanol buffer deviates from the manufacturer’s recommended lysis conditions, and extraction efficiency may vary between laboratories. RNA yield and purity depend strongly on tissue size, freezing quality, and homogenization efficiency, and small variations in these steps can introduce variability across samples. Likewise, reverse transcription efficiency may vary with RNA integrity, affecting absolute Ct values. Quantification using the ΔΔCt method assumes stable expression of housekeeping genes across conditions, which must be experimentally validated for each study.

A second limit is that the protocol relies on RNA extracted from whole TA muscle without microdissection or cell-type separation. Consequently, the transcriptional signals originate from a mosaic of cell types including myofibers, satellite cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells or infiltrating immune cells. This may present a limitation, particularly for the regeneration markers Desmin also expressed in activated satellites and Vimentin detected in activated satellites, fibroblasts, endothelial and immune cells. To address this limitation, it is crucial to study the combination of regeneration markers in parallel, to ensure robustness in the interpretation of the results.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

TRIzol-based homogenization of fibrous skeletal muscle typically yields RNA with optimal A260/A280 ratios (∼1.8–2.1). However, suboptimal A260/A230 ratios (<1.8) can be observed due to residual phenol or guanidinium thiocyanate carryover inherent to TRIzol extraction. This issue is further amplified in samples with low RNA concentrations, where trace contaminants disproportionately affect absorbance-based purity measurements. This approach is therefore not recommended for applications requiring high RNA purity such as RNA-seq.

Potential solution

Avoid TRIzol saturation by maintaining a consistent tissue-to-reagent ratio and preventing excessive tissue loading during homogenization. Conversely, ensure sufficient RNA yield, as very low RNA concentrations can lead to unreliable A260/A230 ratios despite preserved RNA integrity.

Problem 2

When analysing fiber type markers, 18 S rRNA was identified as the most stable reference gene under our experimental conditions as it exhibited the lowest variability across the samples tested. However, due to its very high abundance, 18 S may not be suitable for normalization when target genes are weakly expressed, as large Ct differences can reduce ΔCt robustness. Reference gene suitability should therefore be evaluated according to the Ct range of the genes of interest.

Possible solution

Commonly used housekeeping genes for skeletal muscle include Gapdh, Actb, Cmas, Hprt, Rpl13a, Bm2… provided their stability is validated under the experimental conditions. Using one or more mRNA reference genes with moderate Ct values improves ΔCt calculation robustness and enhances the biological relevance of normalization. To align with MIQE standards, we recommend that all reference genes undergo stability validation using geNorm, NormFinder, or BestKeeper. This step is crucial for minimizing technical bias in gene expression studies.

Problem 3

After three Precellys homogenization cycles using ceramic beads, fibrous skeletal muscle samples may remain partially undissociated, with visible tissue fragments persisting in the TRIzol lysate.

Possible solution

If incomplete dissociation is observed after three cycles, an additional short homogenization cycle can be performed. To prevent overheating and RNA degradation, it is essential to place the tubes on ice between each Precellys cycle and allow sufficient cooling before restarting homogenization. Excessive or continuous cycling without cooling should be avoided, as heat accumulation and mechanical shear can compromise RNA integrity.

Problem 4

When skipping the traditional chloroform phase separation step, users need to be aware of potential contamination with genomic DNA, proteins, lipids, debris which may affect RNA quality (purity and yield).

Possible solution

To mitigate these risks, it is important to use a TRIzol-compatible column kit, to include a DNase treatment step, to validate RNA quality combining 28 S:18 S ratio assessement and Bioanalyzer, and to include no-RT and/or H20 controls in the qPCR reaction.
