Look Again Before You Abstain: Budgeted Conformal Evidence Acquisition for Reliable Vision-Language Models
Jian Xu, Delu Zeng, John Paisley, Qibin Zhao

Abstract
Large vision-language models (LVLMs) hallucinate: they assert visual details that the image does not support. A principled remedy is selective prediction with a distribution-free guarantee—verify each claim and abstain when the claim is not grounded, so that the hallucination rate among asserted claims is provably bounded. We show, however, that this guarantee is bought at a brutal price: to keep the hallucination rate below 5 % 5\% on a balanced object-existence benchmark, a state-of-the-art conformal filter must abstain on more than 80 % 80\% of claims. We argue that abstention is wasteful when more visual evidence is cheaply available, and introduce Budgeted Conformal Evidence Acquisition (BCEA) , which replaces the binary answer/abstain decision with a three-way choice: answer, abstain, or acquire additional visual evidence by re-examining the image (zooming, cropping, or applying a claim-specific intervention) under a bounded compute budget. We make two observations. First, acquisition that is plugged naively into a calibrated filter breaks the statistical guarantee—realized risk overshoots the target by up to 17 17 points—because the acquisition step destroys the exchangeability that conformal calibration relies on. Second, folding the entire acquisition policy into the score function and re-calibrating on post-acquisition scores restores the finite-sample guarantee while still recovering coverage. BCEA further uses structured, claim-type-specific interventions; e.g., a single horizontal-flip intervention lifts spatial-relation verification from chance ( 0.57 0.57 AUROC) to 0.77 0.77 , where the global ungrounding score used by prior work is useless. Across the POPE benchmark and COCO-constructed existence and spatial-relation claims, on four open VLMs (LLaVA-1.5, Qwen2.5-VL, LLaVA-NeXT, InternVL2), BCEA controls the hallucination rate at the target level and consistently improves coverage over a guaranteed-abstention baseline.



1
Introduction

Large vision-language models (LVLMs) produce fluent, helpful descriptions of images, but they also
hallucinate
: they assert objects, attributes, and relations that are not present in the visual input
(
Leng et al. 2024
;
Huang et al. 2024
;
Guan et al. 2024
)
. For deployment in settings that demand reliability—medical, scientific, assistive—fluency is not enough; what is needed is a
guarantee
that, whatever the model says, the rate of unsupported claims stays below a user-specified level.

Conformal prediction offers exactly this kind of guarantee, and a recent line of work brings it to LVLMs. The strongest instance,
ConfLVLM

(
Li et al. 2025
)
, treats each generated detail as a hypothesis, scores it with a cheap uncertainty heuristic, and filters out claims whose score falls below a conformally calibrated threshold; the result is a finite-sample, distribution-free bound on the factual error rate of what remains. This is principled and attractive. But it inherits the central weakness of selective prediction:
to be safe, it abstains
. In our experiments, guaranteeing a hallucination rate below
5\%
forces such a filter to abstain on more than
80\%
of all claims (Figure
1
). A reliable model that refuses four out of five questions is of limited use.

We start from a simple observation:
abstention is wasteful when more evidence is cheaply available.
When a person is unsure whether a small object is present, they look closer; they do not give up. LVLMs admit the analogous move—re-encoding a zoomed crop, or re-scoring the claim under a targeted visual intervention—at the cost of a few extra forward passes. The question is whether this “look again” step can be added
without forfeiting the statistical guarantee
that motivated selective prediction in the first place.

Two existing literatures each solve half of this problem. Conformal LVLM filters
(
Li et al. 2025
;
Tayebati et al. 2025
)
provide the guarantee but never acquire evidence—they only drop claims. Evidence-acquisition methods such as
ReCoVERR

(
Srinivasan et al. 2024
)
do gather more evidence to rescue abstained questions, but they rely on heuristic risk-tolerance thresholds and provide no distribution-free guarantee; moreover they acquire
textual
evidence (LLM-generated follow-up questions) rather than re-examining the pixels. No method does both—acquire visual evidence
and
retain the guarantee—and, as we show, naively combining the two is not safe.

We introduce
Budgeted Conformal Evidence Acquisition (BCEA)
. BCEA replaces the binary answer/abstain decision with a three-way action—answer, abstain, or
acquire
—and makes three contributions.

(1) Acquisition that is naive breaks the guarantee.

Calibrating a conformal threshold on a claim’s base score and then, at test time, replacing that score with a higher post-acquisition score is the obvious thing to do, and it is wrong. Acquisition changes the score distribution, breaking the exchangeability between calibration and test on which conformal validity rests. Empirically, naive acquisition raises coverage but pushes realized risk up to
13
points past the target (Table
1
). We make this failure precise.

(2) Re-calibration on post-acquisition scores restores it.

The fix is to treat the entire acquisition policy as part of the score function and calibrate on the post-acquisition scores, so that calibration and test claims pass through an identical pipeline and exchangeability is recovered. We prove this restores the finite-sample guarantee (Theorem
1
), and show empirically that BCEA then controls risk at the target level while still improving coverage over guaranteed abstention.

(3) Structured interventions beat global ungrounding.

The grounding score used by prior conformal filters—roughly, how much the claim’s likelihood drops when the image is removed—works for object existence but
fails
for relational claims: removing the image lowers “
A
is left of
B
” and “
A
is right of
B
” equally. We show that cheap, claim-type-specific interventions recover the lost signal; a single horizontal-flip intervention lifts spatial-relation verification from
0.57
to
0.77
AUROC.

Finally, we give a tight
characterization
of when acquisition helps: guaranteed coverage at a fixed risk is a single point on the score’s ROC curve, so acquisition raises coverage at every risk level if and only if it improves the ROC (Theorem
2
)—a measurable criterion that explains why model-guided crops beat a uniform grid and why per-claim budget allocation, by contrast, does not help. We validate all of this on object-existence and spatial-relation claims from COCO and the POPE benchmark, across three open VLMs, with head-to-head baselines.

Related Work

Hallucination in LVLMs.

Object hallucination is measured by benchmarks such as POPE
(
Li et al. 2023
)
, AMBER, and, for free-form generation, THRONE
(
Kaul et al. 2024
)
; HallusionBench
(
Guan et al. 2024
)
separates language hallucination from visual illusion, and TUBench
(
He et al. 2024
)
probes unanswerable questions. Mitigation methods are largely heuristic: contrastive decoding (VCD
(
Leng et al. 2024
)
, HALC), over-trust penalties (OPERA
(
Huang et al. 2024
)
), and preference optimization (RLHF-V, V-DPO). None provide a statistical guarantee on the residual hallucination rate.

Conformal and selective prediction for generation.

Conformal factuality
(
Mohri and Hashimoto 2024
)
and conformal abstention for LLMs
(
Yadkori et al. 2024
)
bound the error rate of text generation by filtering or backing off claims. For LVLMs,
ConfLVLM

(
Li et al. 2025
)
provides finite-sample factuality guarantees by hypothesis-testing each claim with global uncertainty heuristics, and CAP
(
Tayebati et al. 2025
)
learns adaptive conformal abstention thresholds for LLMs/VLMs. All of these only
drop
claims. We add an acquisition action and show the resulting validity subtlety and its fix.

Evidence acquisition and active perception.

ReCoVERR

(
Srinivasan et al. 2024
)
reduces over-abstention by collecting LLM-generated follow-up QA as evidence, under a heuristic risk tolerance and without distribution-free guarantees. Zoom/crop methods such as ViCrop
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
and CropVLM improve fine-grained accuracy but target accuracy, not guaranteed risk; our CLIP-guided acquisition adopts the same zoom idea as a verification tool and adds the guarantee.

Conformal validity under adaptivity.

That applying a fixed, label-independent transformation to both calibration and test points preserves exchangeability is known: test-time augmentation can be folded into a conformal predictor without losing coverage
(
Shanmugam et al. 2025
)
, and conformal risk control has been combined with instance-level stopping under a
compute
budget for LLM reasoning
(
Wang et al. 2026
)
and with joint calibration–test thresholds for selective risk
(
Xu, Guo, and Wei 2025
)
. Our theory is an instance of this principle; our contribution is to (i) identify that
visual
evidence acquisition silently violates the guarantee when this principle is ignored—the natural and tempting deployment—and (ii) bring acquisition into the pixels via structured, claim-type interventions. To our knowledge BCEA is the first to couple guaranteed VLM hallucination filtering with visual evidence acquisition.

4.1
Evidence-Sufficiency Scores

Global ungrounding.

Following the intuition behind prior conformal filters, a claim is grounded if its likelihood depends on the image. With
\ell(v)=\frac{1}{|c|}\sum_{t}\log p_{\theta}(c_{t}\mid v,\text{prompt})
the mean token log-likelihood of claim
c
under visual input
v
, the
global
score is

s_{\text{glob}}(x,c)=\ell(x)-\ell(\varnothing),

(2)

where
\varnothing
is a blank (all-black) image. A hallucinated claim barely changes when the image is removed (
s_{\text{glob}}\approx 0
); a grounded claim drops sharply.

Region and intervention scores.

s_{\text{glob}}
throws the whole image away and cannot tell
where
the evidence is, nor distinguish claims that depend on visual
structure
. We replace the blank reference with a targeted intervention
T
chosen by claim type,
s_{T}(x,c)=\ell(x)-\ell(T(x))
: region masking (
T
blacks out the queried object’s support) for existence/attribute claims, and a horizontal flip (
T(x)=\text{flip}(x)
) for left/right relational claims, under which a
correct
directional claim becomes false (so
s_{T}>0
) while an
incorrect
one becomes true (so
s_{T}<0
).

4.2
The Acquisition Action

When
s(x,c)
is too low to assert but not clearly ungrounded, BCEA
acquires
: it forms a set of
B
zoomed views
\{x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(B)}\}
of
x
(a coarse grid of overlapping crops, requiring no ground-truth localization) and forms the post-acquisition score

s_{\text{acq}}(x,c)=\max\!\big(\ell(x),\ \max_{b\leq B}\ell(x^{(b)})\big)-\ell(\varnothing).

(3)

For a true object, some crop magnifies it and raises its likelihood; for a hallucinated object, no crop provides support. The maximization is the source of both the benefit and the danger analyzed next.

4.3
Why Naive Acquisition Is Invalid—and How to Fix It

Let
\tau_{\alpha}
be calibrated on the base scores
s_{\text{glob}}
. The tempting deployment is to assert iff
s_{\text{acq}}(x,c)\geq\tau_{\alpha}
: acquire evidence, keep the old threshold. This is invalid. Conformal validity requires the test score and the calibration scores to be exchangeable draws from one distribution; but the threshold was calibrated on the distribution of
s_{\text{glob}}
, whereas the deployed decision uses
s_{\text{acq}}
, and
s_{\text{acq}}\geq s_{\text{glob}}
by construction (Eq.
3
). The acceptance region is systematically enlarged for
all
claims, including hallucinated ones, so the realized risk exceeds
\alpha
.

Proposition 1

(Naive acquisition is anti-conservative)
.

Since
s_{\text{acq}}\geq s_{\text{glob}}
pointwise, asserting on
\{s_{\text{acq}}\geq\tau_{\alpha}\}
with
\tau_{\alpha}
calibrated on
s_{\text{glob}}
accepts a superset of
\{s_{\text{glob}}\geq\tau_{\alpha}\}
. The level-
\alpha
guarantee certified for the latter does
not
transfer to this larger set, whose selective risk need not be bounded by
\alpha
; whenever the extra accepted claims contain label-
0
claims at a rate above
\alpha
, the realized risk exceeds
\alpha
.

The same monotonicity explains why budget helps and why it must be paid for by re-calibration.

Proposition 2

(Monotone acquisition)
.

Let
s_{\mathcal{A}}^{(B)}(x,c)=\max(\ell(x),\max_{b\leq B}\ell(x^{(b)}))-\ell(\varnothing)
. Then
s_{\mathcal{A}}^{(B)}
is non-decreasing in
B
pointwise, so at any
fixed
threshold
\tau
the accepted set is nested in
B
. Hence both the coverage
\Pr[s_{\mathcal{A}}^{(B)}\geq\tau]
and the
false-acceptance probability

\Pr[y{=}0,\ s_{\mathcal{A}}^{(B)}\geq\tau]
are non-decreasing in
B
.

More budget lets more true claims clear a fixed bar—but also more false ones, so the risk creeps up unless
\tau
is re-raised. Re-calibrating
\tau
on
s_{\mathcal{A}}^{(B)}
at each budget restores risk control, and empirically still nets a coverage gain (Figure
6
). The fix, then, is to make calibration and test traverse an identical pipeline. Treat the acquisition policy
\mathcal{A}
(which views to form and how to aggregate them, Eq.
3
) as part of the score, and calibrate the threshold on the post-acquisition scores of the calibration claims.

Theorem 1

(Acquisition-adaptive validity)
.

Fix an acquisition policy
\mathcal{A}
that maps each
(x,c)
to a post-acquisition score
s_{\mathcal{A}}(x,c)
using only
(x,c)
and the model (not the calibration labels). If the calibration and test claims are exchangeable, then choosing
\tau
by the Clopper-Pearson fixed-sequence procedure on
\{s_{\mathcal{A}}(x_{i},c_{i})\}_{i\in\text{cal}}
and asserting on
\{s_{\mathcal{A}}\geq\tau\}
controls the selective risk at level
\alpha
with probability
1-\delta
on the test claims.

Proof sketch.
Applying the fixed map
\mathcal{A}
to every claim preserves exchangeability of the pairs
(s_{\mathcal{A}}(x_{i},c_{i}),y_{i})
. The result is then standard split-conformal selective risk control with score
s_{\mathcal{A}}
in place of
s
.
\square

Theorem
1
instantiates a known principle—folding a fixed, label-independent transformation into the conformal pipeline keeps coverage
(
Shanmugam et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2026
)
. We state it because the
failure mode it rules out
(Proposition
1
) is, for visual acquisition, both easy to commit and empirically severe, as we show next.

Theorem
1
requires only that
\mathcal{A}
not peek at calibration labels. Crucially, this permits acquisition that is
sequential and adaptive within a claim
.

Proposition 3

(Sequential within-claim acquisition)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
form views one at a time and decide whether to acquire another or stop, where each decision (the next view and the stopping time
\kappa\leq B
) is a function of the current claim’s observations alone. Then
s_{\mathcal{A}}
is a fixed measurable function of
(x,c)
, and the conclusion of Theorem
1
holds verbatim.

That is, “look, judge, look again, stop when confident” is admissible without any correction, because the stopping rule never consults calibration labels; the entire random budget expenditure is absorbed into a single deployed score. When the
policy class
itself is tuned on calibration data (e.g. selecting
B
or a stopping threshold to maximize coverage), validity is preserved by a held-out split or a Learn-then-Test correction over the finite policy grid
(
Angelopoulos et al. 2025
)
. The practical prescription is simply:
calibrate on the scores you will actually deploy.

A second, orthogonal source of optional stopping is the
calibration stream
itself: one may keep collecting labeled claims and stop when coverage looks good. The fixed-sample Clopper–Pearson bound is then invalid (it assumes
n
is fixed in advance). Replacing it with a betting confidence sequence restores validity at
every
sample size simultaneously.

Proposition 4

(Anytime-valid risk control)
.

Fix a threshold
\tau
and let
E_{1},E_{2},\dots\in\{0,1\}
be the error indicators of accepted calibration claims (i.i.d.
\mathrm{Bernoulli}(R(\tau))
by Lemma
1
). For predictable bets
\lambda_{t}\in[0,1/r)
,
M_{t}(r)=\prod_{i\leq t}\big(1+\lambda_{i}(r-E_{i})\big)
is a non-negative supermartingale under
R(\tau){=}r
, so
C_{t}=\{r:\ \max_{r^{\prime}\geq r}M_{t}(r^{\prime})<1/\delta\}
is a
(1{-}\delta)
upper confidence sequence for
R(\tau)

(
Jiang and Yu 2024

Ramdas et al. 2023
)
. Selecting the smallest
\tau
whose confidence-sequence upper bound is

controls the selective risk at level
\alpha

uniformly over all

Unlike Theorem
1

4.4
When Does Acquisition Help?

Theorem
1
guarantees
safety
but says nothing about whether acquisition
improves
anything. We now characterize the coverage gain exactly. Let
\pi=\Pr[Y{=}1]
, and for a score
S
let
\mathrm{TPR}_{S}(\tau)=\Pr[S\geq\tau\mid Y{=}1]
and
\mathrm{FPR}_{S}(\tau)=\Pr[S\geq\tau\mid Y{=}0]
trace the ROC curve
\mathrm{ROC}_{S}
.

Proposition 5

(Coverage at fixed risk is an ROC point)
.

The maximum coverage of a selective rule on
S
subject to selective risk
\leq\alpha
is attained at the ROC point on the ray
\mathrm{FPR}/\mathrm{TPR}=\rho(\alpha):=\tfrac{\pi\alpha}{(1-\pi)(1-\alpha)}
, and equals
C_{S}(\alpha)=\big(\pi+(1-\pi)\rho(\alpha)\big)\,\mathrm{TPR}^{\star}_{S}(\alpha)
, where
\mathrm{TPR}^{\star}_{S}(\alpha)
is the largest TPR on
\mathrm{ROC}_{S}
along that ray.

Theorem 2

(Acquisition helps iff it improves the ROC)
.

For scores
S
(pre-acquisition) and
S^{\prime}=s_{\mathcal{A}}
(post-acquisition),
C_{S^{\prime}}(\alpha)\geq C_{S}(\alpha)
for
every

\alpha\in(0,1)
if and only if
\mathrm{ROC}_{S^{\prime}}
dominates
\mathrm{ROC}_{S}
pointwise. A sufficient condition is that
S^{\prime}
be more informative about
Y
than
S
in the Blackwell sense (its class-conditional likelihood ratio is a monotone refinement of that of
S
).

This converts the vague “does acquisition help” into a measurable, falsifiable criterion, and predicts the experiments: uniform-grid acquisition lifts the ROC modestly (AUROC
0.82\!\to\!0.86
) and CLIP-guided more (
\to\!0.88

2
then forces the coverage ordering at

<

\tfrac{(1-\pi)\,\mathrm{FPR}_{s_{\mathcal{A}}}(\tau_{\alpha})}{C}

Why we do
not
claim budget allocation.

A natural extension is to spend a fixed total budget unevenly across claims; under diminishing returns the optimum is greedy water-filling on marginal gains (a standard exchange argument, App.). Empirically this gives
no
improvement over uniform allocation on existence claims (
\leq 0.4
coverage points, within CI; App.). The reason is structural: with a single global threshold, an extra look raises the scores of true and false boundary claims alike, and at allocation time their labels are unknown—so any label-agnostic allocation is, to first order, coverage-neutral. By Theorem
2
, allocation can help only by making the
aggregate
score more informative, which requires heterogeneous and
predictable
per-claim informativeness; absent that, uniform is optimal. We therefore do not claim allocation as a contribution—a negative result we think is clarifying.

6
Discussion and Limitations

Our study uses four backbones (LLaVA-1.5, LLaVA-NeXT, Qwen2.5-VL, InternVL2) and two working claim families (existence, left/right); building a principled per-claim-type intervention library is the natural next step. The method also has a clear scope boundary: evidence sufficiency can only certify what the backbone can perceive. Counting, relative size, above/below, color attributes, and color–object
binding
are all near chance for these backbones (
s_{\text{glob}}
AUROC
0.50
–
0.58
), and no intervention we tried (instance masking, vertical flip, grayscaling) recovers a usable signal, so coverage collapses to zero on those families. The binding result is telling: our score cannot certify whether “the red
cup
” is correctly bound precisely because the backbone itself does not reliably make that distinction. This is the faithful behavior: where a
7
–
8
B VLM genuinely cannot perceive a property, our scores report
ungrounded
rather than fabricate confidence, and the guaranteed filter abstains. BCEA adds signal only where the backbone has signal to localize (existence, left/right) and a truth-flipping, in-distribution intervention exists. Coverage gains were moderate under a uniform grid but improve markedly once crops are model-guided (Table
4
); larger budgets and richer interventions are a further lever. We deliberately do
not
claim per-claim budget allocation: it is coverage-neutral here for the structural reason in the theory section, a clarifying negative. The framework is otherwise general: any black-box LVLM, any claim-type intervention, any exchangeable calibration set.

From probed claims to free-form generation.

A genuine gap separates our motivation from our evaluation. The motivation is hallucination in
free-form
description, but we evaluate
probed, atomic
claims—existence and spatial claims built from COCO ground truth, and POPE’s yes/no queries. This buys clean labels and controlled difficulty, but is a step removed from a model rambling in open generation, where claims are self-generated and entangled (the regime
ConfLVLM
and THRONE target via claim decomposition). Because BCEA’s score and guarantee are defined
per claim
and are agnostic to how the claim was produced, the framework should compose with a claim parser applied to free-form output; but whether acquisition helps as much on self-generated claims, and how parser noise interacts with the conformal calibration, are open questions we have not tested. We present BCEA as a mechanism validated under controlled probing, not yet as an end-to-end free-form pipeline.

7
Conclusion

Guaranteed selective prediction makes LVLMs reliable but over-cautious. BCEA shows that the model can
look again
instead of giving up—acquiring cheap visual evidence to rescue claims—provided the acquisition policy is folded into the score and the conformal threshold is re-calibrated on what is actually deployed. Naive acquisition silently breaks the guarantee; re-calibration restores it and still improves coverage. Together with structured, claim-type-specific interventions that see what global ungrounding cannot, this turns a binary answer/abstain filter into a budgeted, guaranteed, evidence-seeking one.

By construction

(sequential within-claim acquisition).

Let the policy reveal views

Proposition

(monotone acquisition).

For each

Proposition

With

Theorem

Allocation (not claimed; for completeness).

Were per-claim gains
g_{i}
non-decreasing and concave,
\max\sum_{i}g_{i}(b_{i})
s.t.
\sum_{i}b_{i}\leq B_{\text{tot}}
would be solved by greedily incrementing
\arg\max_{i}\Delta_{i}(b_{i})

\Delta_{i}(b)=g_{i}(b{+}1)-g_{i}(b)
: for any optimal
