Medial premotor cortex as a potential reservoir of reward expectancy signals influencing the primate frontoparietal network
Authors: Keisuke Sehara, Masashi Kondo, Yuka Hirayama, Teppei Ebina, Masafumi Takaji, Akiya Watakabe, Ken-ichi Inoue, Masahiko Takada, Tetsuo Yamamori, Masanori Matsuzaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reward expectancy plays an important role in modulating cortical activity associated with motivation, selection, and preparation of subsequent goal-directed movements. However, it is unclear how it is represented in the primate frontoparietal cortex in a form that is separable from activity for motor execution. Here, using common marmosets performing a classical conditioning task with auditory cues, we conducted wide-field one-photon calcium imaging of frontoparietal and auditory cortices while recording orofacial and body movements. Reward expectancy that was separable from licking, eye movement, and hand movement was represented in the medial part of the dorsorostral premotor cortex (mPMdr), but not the central part. Granger causality analysis revealed information flows from mPMdr to widespread frontoparietal areas beyond its functional subnetwork, specifically during the delay period before reward delivery. Our results suggest that in non-human primates, mPMdr serves to retain reward expectancy to modulate the frontoparietal network for subsequent movements.

Introduction

Sensorimotor transformation is one of the most important functions of the cerebral cortex. In particular, in both primates and rodents, the premotor cortex (PM) serves as a center for integrating sensory and cognitive signals, selecting actions, and preparing movements1–7. In primates, the PM is spatially segregated into multiple subareas with different functions. The rostral part of the dorsal PM (PMdr or pre-PM) connects densely with the prefrontal cortex and is associated with cognitive processes, while the caudal part of the dorsal PM (PMdc) connects strongly with M12,8. In sensory stimulus-triggered reaching tasks, an information flow from PMdr to PMdc is apparent3,9. In addition, sensorimotor transformation is strongly modulated by reward expectancy, a cognitive signal representing the anticipation of future outcomes and associated with a sensory cue after learning. Reward expectancy is strongly related to motivation and affects the selection of the following action and its kinematics10–12. The region posterior to the arcuate sulcus of the macaque, corresponding to caudal premotor regions, has been shown to represent reward expectancy related to a following saccade13,14. Furthermore, regardless of the sensory modality of the cue, reward expectancy is represented in the macaque PM even during a period in which no specific action is required for the reward to be delivered10,15. Reward expectancy is also represented in the supplementary motor area (SMA) and supplementary eye field (SEF)16,17. Thus, the dorsal higher-order motor areas may carry a common reward-expectancy signal associating sensory inputs with motor outputs. However, previous electrical recording studies have not clarified how the reward expectancy is spatially distributed across these areas, and whether and how it may affect the activity of other sensorimotor areas.

In mice, the spatial resolution of observations affects how reward expectancy signals appear to be distributed across dorsal cortical areas. In our previous study using a classical conditioning task with two auditory cues assigned to different reward probabilities, two-photon imaging of different cortical areas revealed stronger single-cell-level representation of reward expectancy in the dorsomedial part of the secondary motor cortex (M2 or PM) than in M1, the dorsolateral part of M2, or the medial prefrontal cortex18. By contrast, when wide-field one-photon calcium imaging was used for observations, no strong representation of reward expectancy was detected on the dorsal cerebral cortex18,19. This apparent dichotomy is thought to derive from the fact that movement-related activity dominates dorsal cortical activity in mice, irrespective of whether it is task-related or not19–23. However, in the cerebral cortex of non-human primates, movement-related activity is less dominant in both the prefrontal and primary visual cortices, regions that have been the main focus of recent primate studies21,24–27. We reasoned that by using wide-field imaging4,18,19,28, which captures the local-averaged neuronal activity of somata, dendrites, and axons in the superficial layer, the spatial specificity of reward-expectancy representation could be made readily visible in the cortex of non-human primates, unlike in mice. Wide-field imaging enables monitoring of activity over large cortical areas while retaining spatial resolution on the order of hundreds of micrometers and temporal resolution of tens of milliseconds28,29. These properties make it well-suited for examining activity across distributed neocortical areas and identifying small subregions with distinct activity profiles in behaving animals. Considering both electrophysiological findings in macaques, including those from PM, SMA, and SEF, and our previous two-photon imaging results in mice, we hypothesized that the medial premotor cortex in primates may represent information related to reward-predicting cues. Importantly, we tested this hypothesis in a broad exploratory manner by surveying activity across dorsal premotor regions accessible within the imaging field, rather than by directly targeting SMA and SEF.

In this study, we used the common marmoset (Callithrix jacchus), a small New World monkey whose lissencephalic cerebral cortex is suitable for fluorescence imaging9,30,31. We introduced two large glass windows to survey for reward expectancy-related activity across multiple cortical areas, including PM, primary motor cortex (M1), primary and secondary somatosensory cortex (S1 and S2, respectively), posterior parietal cortex, and primary auditory cortex (A1). This approach allowed us to identify task-related activity patterns across regions within the accessible imaging field, including areas near the medial premotor cortex. We also used three cameras to monitor orofacial and body movements. Using these procedures, we identified a functionally defined subregion exhibiting strong cue-related activity. This subregion was located in the medial part of the dorsal premotor cortex (PMdr) and extended toward the lateral portion of area 6M, a medial frontal motor-related region in primates, potentially overlapping with regions corresponding to the SMA and pre-supplementary motor area (pre-SMA)32. For clarity, we refer to this functionally defined region as medial PMdr (mPMdr) throughout this study. By constructing a regression model to explain the neuronal activity according to task-related and movement-related variables18,19, we found that activity in this region strongly represented reward expectancy-related signals. Furthermore, Granger causality analysis suggested that the reward expectancy represented in mPMdr transiently becomes predictive of activity in other cortical areas across the frontoparietal cortex. These findings indicate that a spatially restricted premotor subregion can transiently influence widespread cortical networks, highlighting a flexible mechanism for context-dependent modulation of sensorimotor processing in primates.

Results

Head-fixed marmosets show anticipatory licks during a classical conditioning task

We trained two adult marmosets (Marmoset 1 and Marmoset 2) to perform a classical conditioning task under head-fixation (Fig. 1a). Two sinusoidal tones were associated with different probabilities of reward delivery: Tone A (16 kHz) was associated with a high (70%) reward probability (cue A trials), whereas Tone B (6 kHz) was associated with a lower (30%) reward probability (cue B trials) (Fig. 1b). In each trial, either of the tones was randomly presented for 2 s (cue period), followed by a 1 s delay period, after which the reward was delivered according to the reward probability associated with the tone. After 52 sessions for Marmoset 1 and 37 sessions for Marmoset 2, we conducted a craniotomy over the frontoparietal and superior temporal cortices and injected adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) carrying a tetracycline-inducible jGCaMP8m expression cassette under the control of the human synapsin (hSyn) promoter, resulting in pan-neuronal expression30,33–37 (Fig. 1c). For this, we used the mosaic AAV vector system AAV2.1, which is reported to achieve stable and intense expression of the target proteins in primates38. After virus injection, we set two glass windows over the craniotomy: the first one (14.2 × 7.2 mm) over the frontoparietal cortex and the second one (8.2 × 4.2 mm) over the superior temporal cortex, including the lateral sulcus (Supplementary Fig. 1a–c, e). We determined the border between M1 and the caudal part of the dorsal PM (PMdc or 6DC) by conducting intracortical micro-stimulation (ICMS) prior to virus injection (Supplementary Fig. 1c, e)9,39. The demarcations of dorsal neocortical areas were inferred relative to this ICMS-defined border, with reference to the marmoset atlas9,31,40. Auditory areas were estimated after we confirmed GCaMP expression; we examined calcium responses through the second glass window while we presented sinusoidal tones of 1–24 kHz. The location of A1 was estimated on the basis of the resulting tonotopy map and the location of the lateral sulcus30,41,42 (Supplementary Fig. 1d, f).

After GCaMP expression reached a sufficiently high level, we conducted one-photon imaging through the two glass windows (Supplementary Fig. 1b) in ten sessions (one per day) for Marmoset 1 and nine sessions (one per day) for Marmoset 2. In these imaging sessions, both animals showed a gradual increase in anticipatory licks around the middle of the cue period, with these further increasing during the delay period (Fig. 1d, e). The lick rate returned to the baseline level around 6 s after the onset of the tone presentation. The anticipatory lick rates around the reward timing were higher in cue A trials than in cue B trials (Fig. 1d, e). These results imply that the animals successfully learned this classical conditioning task: they expected the reward after the auditory cue presentation, and recognized the difference in the reward probabilities associated with the two cues.

Calcium responses of the frontoparietal and primary auditory cortices during the classical conditioning task

During the imaging sessions, GCaMP fluorescence was simultaneously recorded from the two glass windows (Fig. 2a, b). We focused on the changes in cortical activity after the tone presentation (Fig. 2c, d and Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). In the analyses, the imaging frames were spatially down-sampled fourfold, from 1024 × 1024 (15.8 µm/pixel) to 256 × 256 pixels (63.2 µm/pixel). To examine the differences in activity changes across multiple cortical areas, we defined twelve regions of interest (ROIs) (Fig. 2a, b), selecting locations that were present in both animals at comparable cortical positions and exhibited qualitatively similar cue-dependent activity patterns. We also included areas previously implicated in different aspects of sensory- and motor-related processing. Two ROIs were set for PMdr and two for PMdc because the medial parts of these areas, mPMdr and the medial part of PMdc (mPMdc), appeared to show prominent increased activity during the cue and delay periods in comparison with the central part of PMdr (cPMdr) and the central part of PMdc (cPMdc) (Fig. 2c, d and Supplementary Fig. 2a–d). We also set rostral (rA1) and caudal (cA1) ROIs in A1 because they showed opposite tone selectivity during the cue period (Fig. 2c, d and Supplementary Fig. 2a–d). The ROI for S2 was set at its most lateral part (lS2) to capture responses related to tongue movement31,43–45. For the other areas, one ROI per area was assigned (area 8c [A8c], M1, S1, PE, and the anterior intraparietal cortex [AIP]) (Fig. 2a, b). To examine the potential roles of these ROIs in the representation of reward-predicting cues, we further narrowed down our focus to their responses during the cue and delay periods. The ROIs in mPMdr, mPMdc, and lS2 showed significant increases in activity during both the cue and delay periods, across both cue A and cue B trials, and in both marmosets (Supplementary Fig. 2e, f). The ROIs in M1, PE, and S1 also showed significant increases during the cue period, but this finding was consistent across both cue types only in Marmoset 2, being observed only in cue B trials in Marmoset 1. However, during the delay period, these regions exhibited significant increases in activity with both cue types in both marmosets (Supplementary Fig. 2e, f). On the basis of these results, we focused subsequent analyses on these six ROIs, next examining their cue preference using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (auROC). In both marmosets, the responses in all six ROIs were larger for cue A trials than for cue B trials during more than half of the delay period (Fig. 2e).

It did, however, remain equally possible that the observed cue-specific differences in calcium activity merely reflected the cue-specific difference in timing and/or modulation of the lick behavior of the animals (Fig. 2f). We thus further selected those trials that did not contain lick events between 0.4 and 3.2 s after the cue onset (i.e., up to 200 ms after the reward timing). mPMdr activity was larger for cue A trials than for cue B trials at some time points of the delay period in Marmoset 1, and during the whole of the delay period in Marmoset 2 (Fig. 2g). In Marmoset 2, the lick behavior immediately after the reward timing did not differ between cue A and cue B trials (Fig. 2h). Overall, these results suggest that the activity of mPMdr during the cue and delay periods was related to reward-predicting cues, rather than to a general arousal or drive-related process13,14.

Changes in orofacial and limb movements during the classical conditioning task

It is possible that mPMdr activity during the cue and delay periods might reflect cue-related movements of other body parts, including eyes, limbs, and face, and therefore that the difference in mPMdr activity between cue A and cue B trials during the delay period might merely reflect differences in these movements. To address this issue, we extracted eye, hand, and facial movements from the three videos and measured foot movement using an accelerometer (Fig. 3a). Facial movements were extracted as the deviation of 18 key-points on the face from their mean positions, and were represented in terms of their principal components (PCs). The changes in some traces of the extracted behavioral parameters often appeared to align with the occurrence of individual trials (Supplementary Fig. 3), and the time courses of trial-averaged movements indicated that many dimensions of the animal’s behavior were in fact phase-locked to the task events (Fig. 3b–d). During the delay period, movement differences between cue A and cue B trials were observed in different sets of behavioral parameters from the two animals: in eye positions and face movement PCs (PC1 and PC2) for Marmoset 1, and in hand speed and face movement PC (PC5) for Marmoset 2 (Fig. 3b–d). The contributions of movements and the reward-predicting cue to cortical activity

To clarify the contributions of hand, foot, eye, and facial movements, along with licking, and task events (i.e., cue presentation and reward delivery) on the activity of each cortical area, we employed a ridge regression model (see Materials and Methods for details). In our design matrix, the input variables (regressors) to the model consisted of the time series of flags for cues (cue A or cue B trials) and the flags for being rewarded, plus all the motion-related parameters from our behavioral tracking (Fig. 4a). Time-shifted versions of motion-related parameters were also included in the design matrix to account for the preparatory and feedback activity related to movements. All these different parameters were used to predict the entire time course of the calcium response for each imaging pixel (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 4a). A ten-fold cross-validation model was used, and the cross-validation explained variance (cvR2) was computed to evaluate the performance of each model18,19. On average, 22.9%–64.4% of the total fluctuations in all the activity time series in each of the twelve ROIs were explained by this linear encoding model (Fig. 4b–d). It should be noted that the considerable variability in the explained variance across ROIs might be largely driven by differences in activity before cue onset, rather than task-related responses. Therefore, we defined the baseline activity of each trial as the mean activity during the 1 s window before the cue onset and examined the performance of the models in predicting the trial-to-trial fluctuation in baseline activity and baseline-subtracted trial-related activity during the following 6 s window after the cue onset. The explained variance in baseline fluctuations ranged from 9.47% to 51.6%, whereas the variability in the baseline-subtracted trial-related activity ranged from 56.4%–82.6% across ROIs (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). We also examined the unique contributions of the distinct sets of variables used in the model (Supplementary Fig. 4d). These unique contributions were computed by calculating the reduction in the explained variance (∆cvR2) after time-shuffling the set of variables to be examined. As expected from their calcium responses, A1 areas showed large cue-related ∆cvR2 values (Supplementary Fig. 4d). Large hand-related unique contributions were detected mainly in M1, S1, S2, and PE (Supplementary Fig. 4d), and a large lick-related unique contribution was noted in the lateral side of A8c, S1, and S2 (Supplementary Fig. 4d). Eye-related unique contributions were prominent around A8c in Marmoset 1 and a part of the posterior parietal cortical areas in both animals (Supplementary Fig. 4d), presumably reflecting the activity related to saccadic eye motion44,46. A slight foot-related unique contribution was observed in the medial side of M1, S1, and PE. The spatial patterns of the representations of these movements are in line with the somatotopy of the motor and sensory cortices in non-human primates, supporting the validity of our regression modeling. By contrast, the cue- and reward-related unique contributions were, overall, low in the cortical areas, except for A1 (Supplementary Fig. 4e–h).

Reward-predicting cues are represented in mPMdr

So far, our analysis of the variance explained by the encoding models has been based on the signals from the full-duration imaging sessions. This approach may have underestimated the contribution of task-related events that appeared at a very limited number of timepoints in each imaging session. We therefore examined the time courses of the representations of cue A- and cue B-related variables using 1 s time bins aligned with the cue onset. In both marmosets, spot-like patterns of large unique contributions were observed in the vicinity of mPMdr during the cue and delay periods of the trial (Fig. 4e), while more diffuse patterns were detected over a large fraction of somatosensory-motor areas after the reward timing. We then examined the amount of information related to reward-predicting cues by computing the unique contribution of cue-related variables in the selected ROIs during the cue and delay periods (0–3 s period from the cue onset). Although the cue-related unique contribution was detected in many ROIs, it was consistently larger in mPMdr than in all other ROIs except for rA1 and cA1, with statistically significant differences being observed in seven ROIs across the two animals (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 5a).

We also compared the differences between cue A- and cue B-related unique contributions in the form of the ∆Variance (Cue A – Cue B) (Fig. 4g, h). This difference in unique contributions during the cue and delay periods was larger in mPMdr than in all other ROIs except for cA1, with statistically significant differences being observed in four ROIs across the two animals (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Fig. 5b). These results suggest that even when we accounted for the contribution from the marmoset’s movements, mPMdr held a significant fraction of information related to reward-predicting cues.

The differences in these measures between mPMdr and the other ROIs were unlikely to result solely from variations in GCaMP expression across the field of view, because the variability in unique contributions during the cue and delay periods was mainly accounted for by ROI positions, rather than by expression-level variability (Supplementary Fig. 6a–d).

It was also possible that regressor interdependence in our design matrices, which may be high across our task- and behavior-related parameters, could have biased the results of our analysis based on linear encoding models. Although we expect our Bayesian framework, combined with cross-validation and the estimation of unique contributions of variable sets, to be robust to multicollinearity and observation noise19,47, we also assessed whether multicollinearity in the design matrices affected the estimation of cue-related unique contributions. To quantify the multicollinearity, we computed the variance inflation factor (VIF) for each regressor, a standard measure of linear dependence among predictors. The VIF values were generally very large (>1000), particularly for hand- and eye-related regressors in both animals, indicating substantial mutual dependence among regressors (Supplementary Fig. 7a). To determine whether such multicollinearity influenced the model performance, we filtered the regressors to reduce their mutual dependence (Supplementary Fig. 7b). We fitted linear regression models based on these restricted design matrices (restricted models) in the same way as we did for the original full-size models, and found that performance did not largely differ between the restricted and full-size models (Supplementary Fig. 7c, d). Although the representation of reward-predicting cues in four ROIs in Marmoset 1 and two ROIs in Marmoset 2 was higher with the restricted model than with the full-size model, it was not significantly different in mPMdr (Supplementary Fig. 7e). We thus concluded that the linear regression modeling approach took sufficient care of the multicollinearity in our design matrices. In addition, the trends of the activity and model performance were similar across the two marmosets; thus, in the following analyses, we combined the results from the two marmosets unless it is stated otherwise.

mPMdr does not represent a purely sensory stimulus

We further addressed the possibility that the cue-related unique contribution in mPMdr may be explained solely in terms of pure sensory responses. We did this by performing a separate set of auditory stimulation experiments on the same day as we conducted 7 out of the 19 classical conditioning experiments (Fig. 5a). In these experiments, we presented the animals with task-relevant (16 and 6 kHz) and task-irrelevant (1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 kHz) 500 ms tones, none of which were associated with reward delivery, and delivered the reward at random times (Fig. 5a). We found that the lick rate in response to tone presentation was higher after the end of the cue A tone than after the end of the non-cue tones (Fig. 5b), suggesting that the animals’ reward expectancy to the cue A tone persisted even in this task context. At 8 kHz, neither animal showed significantly different responses from those at 1 kHz (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). These results indicate that a higher tone frequency does not uniformly lead to stronger lick responses. Consistent with the behavioral persistence of the animals’ lick responses to cue A tones during the auditory stimulation experiments, the calcium responses in mPMdr during the 1–3 s period after the tone onset were larger after the cue A tone than after the non-cue tones, whereas no significant differences between the two types of tones were observed in rA1 and cA1 during this period (Fig. 5c). By contrast, during the 500 ms tone presentation period, a period in which rA1 and cA1 showed significant tone-type-dependent responses, no significant differences were observed in the calcium responses of mPMdr to the different types of tones, implying that mPMdr contains no immediate stimulus-dependent information (Fig. 5c). To remove the effects of movements from these calcium responses, we used linear encoding models in a manner similar to the method we employed for the classical conditioning task. The unique contributions of the tone variables during the 0–0.5-s and 0.5–3-s periods after tone onset still differed between mPMdr and A1 (Fig. 5d, e). Thus, the amplitude of the activity of mPMdr did not simply reflect that of A1, which may have included the animal’s engagement with task-related tones48. We also examined the calcium responses to reward delivery, and found that the responses in mPMdr were not consistently larger or smaller than those in the other ROIs shown in Fig. 2e (Fig. 5f–h), indicating that the activity of mPMdr did not specifically reflect attention to novelty, such as the unexpected reward17. These results are consistent with our hypothesis that mPMdr maintains task-relevant representations rather than purely sensory ones.

We further validated the temporal aspects of movement-related modulation on neuronal activity (Supplementary Fig. 9a–e). Most of the model weights for hand, foot, and eye movements that exhibited relatively large peaks increased before the movement timing and then peaked afterward, presumably reflecting the time courses of their corresponding sensory and motor activity (Supplementary Fig. 9a–d). By contrast, the time course of the weight for the pupil diameter was similar across all areas and had a characteristic transient peak that occurred before the time of change in pupil diameter (Supplementary Fig. 9e). The time course of lick-related weights varied: S1 and lS2 showed large peak values (Supplementary Fig. 9a), consistent with the robust lick-related representations in these areas. On the other hand, in cPMdr, cPMdc, M1, A8c, and AIP, either no increases or only slight increases in the weights were observed before the onset of individual lick events, and the weight became negative within 0.5 s after the lick onset (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Together, these findings suggest that movement-related representations differ across cortical areas in both their somatotopic organization and temporal dynamics.

Finally, we examined how effective connectivity (that is, the direction and magnitude of the inferred functional connectivity)49 varied during the task. We calculated pairwise Granger causality (GC)18 between the 12 ROIs by fitting vector autoregression models to their time series50,51 (Supplementary Fig. 10a). The models were well-behaved and explained more than 90% of the variability in the activity of the individual ROIs (Supplementary Fig. 10b–d). Of the five time periods we examined, the estimated GC was prominent during the delay period (Fig. 7e and Supplementary Fig. 10e), during which mPMdr showed statistically significant Granger-causal influence on ten other ROIs, including M1, cPMdc, and cPMdr, while it received significant Granger-causal influence only from cPMdc (Fig. 7e and Supplementary Fig. 10e). Among all 12 ROIs, mPMdr showed the largest negative In–Out degree (i.e., the number of statistically significant source ROIs minus the number of statistically significant target ROIs)52 during this period (Fig. 7f). Collectively, our results indicate that although the patterns of functional subnetworks remained stable throughout the session, the levels of effective connectivity within and between them were modulated according to the phase of the trial. Although it should be noted that Granger causality should be interpreted as a measure of statistical predictability rather than as a measure of causal relationship, our analysis suggests that mPMdr-specific activity during the delay period of the trial becomes transiently associated with activity throughout the frontoparietal areas, beyond the borders of functional subnetworks (Fig. 7g).

Discussion

In the current study, we reveal that reward-predicting cues are strongly represented in mPMdr during cue and delay periods, although we examined only two marmosets. In all recent studies that compared representations between cognitive (or task-related) variables and movements in the macaque21,24–27, the cognitive tasks required goal-directed movements to achieve a rewarded state. By contrast, we here used a classical conditioning task to minimize the cortical activity and movements that may be related to decision making, planning, and preparation of goal-directed movement. Our results also suggest that mPMdr was not particularly responsive to unexpected rewards or pupil dilation, which may reflect arousal and attention. In our linear encoding models, the lick-related weight in mPMdr did not ramp up towards the lick onset, consistent with the notion that the primate premotor cortex responsible for licking and jaw movement is on the ventral side53,54. Therefore, it seems reasonable to consider that the unique contribution of the cue-related variables in mPMdr mainly represented the reward expectancy in the current task, rather than cue-triggered attention, motivation, or preparation for the following licking, although some contribution of these factors and unmeasured movements may also be possible.

Interpretation of the recorded calcium signals

The recorded signals mainly reflect the population activity of neurons transduced with AAV2.1 under the human synapsin I promoter, which drives expression in both excitatory and inhibitory neurons in the cortex36,37. Because one-photon calcium imaging predominantly detects signals from superficial cortical layers, the contribution from deeper layers is relatively small28,29. Although calcium imaging is more sensitive to increases in firing than decreases in it, decreases in fluorescence reliably reflect decreases in spiking55. The development of wide-field one-photon calcium imaging allows the capture of population activity across large cortical areas, facilitating mesoscale analysis of cortical dynamics.

Representation of reward-predicting cues in mPMdr

Given the gradual cytoarchitectonic transitions within medial area 6 in the marmoset32, we consider mPMdr to be a functionally defined region that may span beyond traditional areal boundaries, rather than a strictly delimited anatomical field. Area 6M is located medially to PMdr and PMdc, and its rostral and caudal portions were shown to anatomically correspond to pre-SMA and SMA, respectively32. However, neither pre-SMA-like nor SMA-like neuronal activity has been identified in marmosets.

Previous studies using eye-movement tasks reported that subsets of neurons in the SEF and SMA show activity that increases during the post-saccade period and extends up to reward delivery. This has been interpreted as reflecting reward expectancy following a required action16,17. However, these paradigms did not manipulate reward magnitude or probability. Other studies using classical conditioning paradigms reported that subsets of premotor neurons responded preferentially to reward-predictive cues rather than to non-reward cues, although the detailed spatial distribution of such neurons remains unclear10,15. Yet another body of work introduced cues associated with different reward outcomes and showed that neural activity during the delay period is modulated by expected reward, with stronger effects being observed in premotor areas than in the frontal eye field, and in the rostral part of SMA than in SEF13,14. In these delay-based paradigms, reward-related modulation was less evident during the post-movement period.

Building on these previous findings, we employed a conditioning task that bridges these paradigms by introducing cues associated with different reward probabilities. Under this design, we found that putative reward expectancy-related signals during the cue and delay periods were more strongly represented in mPMdr than in cPMdr and PMdc. Importantly, this activity pattern remained evident after removing lick-related components using our encoding models, suggesting that these signals cannot be fully explained by the measured movement-related activity. While weaker cue-related components were also detectable in other frontoparietal regions (Fig. 4f), the effect was most prominent in mPMdr, suggesting a distributed representation with a gradient across regions. In this context, the earlier latency observed in mPMdr may reflect its relative position within the processing hierarchy, rather than a fundamentally distinct encoding.

Compared with previous studies in macaques, our results highlight both similarities and differences across premotor subregions. SEF and SMA activity have been primarily associated with post-action or outcome-related signals, and pre-SMA neurons were shown to maintain task-relevant sensory information across delay periods56 and to support visuo- and audio-motor associations in humans2,57,58. Our results on marmoset mPMdr indicate that it robustly represents reward-predictive information during the cue and delay periods, prior to movement execution, which aligns with the functional properties reported in these electrophysiological studies on macaque premotor subregions. In addition, our wide-field imaging approach allowed us to obtain a continuous spatial map of this representational region across the frontoparietal cortex.

Putative hub-like functions of mPMdr over the frontoparietal networks

Our functional correlation mapping showed that mPMdr and cPMdr participated in distinct functional subnetworks. This is paralleled by previous anatomical findings on the marmoset area 6M: neurons in area 6M innervate M1 more strongly than do PMdr neurons59, and PMdc receives more inputs from area 6M than does PMdr60. Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) also demonstrated that a functional connectivity-defined cluster that mainly included M1 also included the middle parts of PMdr and PMdc, whereas another cluster connecting with the intraparietal areas included area 8c and the lateral parts of PMdr and PMdc61. Considering that this fMRI experiment was conducted in a lightly anesthetized state, these resting-state functional clusters could be considered to be independent of any movement-related modulations, and therefore presumably reflected intrinsic functional modules in the marmoset neocortex. Considered alongside these previous reports, our results add to the idea of intrinsic functional modules that retain their basic structure when the animal undergoes cognitive tasks. In addition, our analysis of GC showed that during the delay period, mPMdr activity was useful to predict activity in a wide range of somatosensory and motor areas, beyond the borders of the functional subnetwork that contains mPMdr.

Together, these findings suggest that mPMdr serves as a hub-like node that represents task-relevant contextual information and influences activity across distributed sensorimotor networks. This interpretation is consistent with previous human imaging studies suggesting that PMdr serves as an interface between motor and cognitive networks, with cognitive control-related activity extending in the rostromedial direction62. Although these studies did not distinguish medial from more central portions of PMdr, our results suggest that the medial component (mPMdr, as defined here) may be particularly involved in representing such contextual information prior to movement execution.

This interpretation is supported by anatomical evidence from macaques showing that PMdr is interconnected with prefrontal areas and the SMA63, suggesting a network architecture that may enable the integration of cognitive and motor-related signals. In line with this, previous studies have shown that reward expectancy can influence activity in motor-related regions, including M164, and that dorsal sensorimotor cortices can encode effort cost and integrate it with stimulus value65–68. Although our task did not explicitly manipulate effort, the cue-dependent activity observed in mPMdr is consistent with the idea that this region contributes to the integration of motivational and contextual information relevant for action preparation.

Our results, therefore, suggest that medial portions of PMdr may play a role in linking reward-predictive cues to action preparation by representing contextual information prior to movement onset. Future studies that directly manipulate mPMdr activity will be necessary to test its causal contribution to this process.

Motor representation in the marmoset dorsal neocortex

In non-human primates, it is still not completely understood how movement-related activity is represented across widespread cortical areas, including both motor and non-motor regions, and how it relates to other neural activity associated with the task. Our finding that non-task-relevant movements were widely represented in not only M1, S1, and S2, but also PMdr and A8c, is consistent with a macaque study that showed task-irrelevant movement, in addition to relevant cognitive signals, to be strongly represented in area 8a, which is located anterior to PMdr24. At the same time, our finding that movements did not dominate A1 during the tone presentation is consistent with studies in macaques and marmosets that show that bodily movements do not strongly affect V1 activity during visual stimulus presentation25,27. The strength of motor representation in M1, S1, and lS2 corresponds well with the somatotopy revealed by ICMS-based mapping and sensory mapping43,45,46,69,70. Taken together, it is likely that in non-human primates, the effects of movements on neocortical activity depend on the modalities of individual areas.

By contrast, the time courses of the model weights for pupil diameter were relatively similar across all imaging areas (Supplementary Fig. 9e). The transient increase in pupil diameter after the onset of cue presentation may reflect cue-triggered arousal and an increase in attention, patterns that are regulated in part by adrenergic and cholinergic neurons71,72. Considering that these neurons broadly project their axons over the cortex and that their cortical activity preceded the pupil dilation73, it seems reasonable to presume that they modulated pupil diameter and dorsal neocortical activity in different latencies.

Unexpectedly, we observed a robust reduction in the activity of cPMdr and A8c after the lick onset, without an increase during the delay period (Supplementary Fig. 2c–f). PMdr and A8c connect with intraparietal areas, including AIP, and have been considered to specifically process ocular and/or visual information. In fact, the cPMdr, A8c, and AIP ROIs belonged to the same cluster according to the correlation-based metrics and the movement-related encoding model weights (Fig. 7c, d). It is possible that in our current study, the state of the animal, presumably related to licking and/or reward consumption, resulted in suppression of activity in these areas during the response period of the trial. This possibility is supported by the finding that locomotion negatively modulates activity in marmoset V127. The functional relevance of these movement-related modulations of cortical activity, particularly those in the negative direction, remains to be further examined.

Technical specifics and limitations of the marmoset research

The combined field of view (FOV) of the two windows used in the present study was 136.9 mm2 (14.2 × 7.2 mm + 8.2 × 4.2 mm). To our knowledge, this is the largest FOV to have been used for one-photon calcium imaging of the mammalian brain. The combination of a wide field of view and spatial resolution of a few hundred micrometers enabled us to investigate both functional and effective connectivity—analytical approaches commonly used in human fMRI studies49—between subareas of the frontoparietal and auditory cortices. The temporal resolution (tens of milliseconds) of the calcium imaging also enabled us to investigate how connectivity patterns changed or remained stable across task-related periods, an investigation that is challenging to perform with fMRI.

We expect that it would be possible to achieve even larger imaging fields by installing multiple one-photon microscopes with different tilt angles, as has already been demonstrated for two-photon imaging of the mouse brain74. Higher and primary auditory cortices have been simultaneously imaged, and the activity of axonal projections connecting these areas can also be imaged30,75. In future studies, it should be possible to examine the cellular basis of end-to-end information flow in the marmoset neocortex, from the processing of visual or auditory inputs in primary sensory areas, through the representation of cognitive processes in cortical association areas, to the motor areas where integrated information is converted into movement outputs, including vocalization and other social interactions of marmosets75,76.

On the flipside, the less pronounced sulcal structures of the marmoset neocortex present a drawback, in that cortical areas cannot be distinguished by morphological landmarks that are easily detectable on visual inspection. Failure to take into account animal-to-animal differences in the sizes and positions of individual neocortical areas would lead to erroneous attributions of activity to underlying neocortical areas. To alleviate such issues, careful registration of each brain to the standard reference brain is necessary, possibly by conducting MRI77. Furthermore, the repertoires of the motor and cognitive tasks available for marmosets are rather limited compared with those available for macaques; it is necessary to develop behavioral paradigms that fit well with the ethology of marmosets78,79.

Limitations of the current study

The present study has several limitations. First, although the main activity patterns were broadly consistent across both animals, the fact that there were only two marmosets may limit the generalizability of our findings and could have led to an underestimation of representations in the encoding models or an overrepresentation of animal-specific roles of mPMdr in the current task. In addition, the recordings did not fully extend into more medial frontal motor-related regions, including area 6M, partly because of the practical constraints on imaging window placement near the midline. Although we determined the borders of cortical areas on the basis of ICMS mapping and stereotaxic coordinates, MRI-based analyses have revealed substantial inter-individual variability in cranial and cortical landmarks80. Therefore, the anatomical borders defined in the present study should be regarded as approximations rather than definite full-precision demarcations, and our conclusions rely primarily on functional properties rather than precise areal demarcation. Accordingly, the mPMdr as defined here may partially overlap with adjacent medial frontal areas, including area 6M, as noted in the Introduction. Future studies combining MRI-based registration with analyses in a larger number of animals, as well as broader coverage of medial frontal areas, are required to determine whether the region showing strong cue-related activity extends further anteriorly (area 8a) and medially (area 6M)81, or whether additional functionally distinct regions are present.

Second, the task design included only two reward probabilities (70% and 30%), which precludes reliable estimation of trial-by-trial value signals. Thus, although the observed activity may be consistent with a graded representation such as expected value, we interpret it more conservatively as reflecting reward expectancy in a broad sense. In addition, while the auditory stimulation experiments indicate that mPMdr activity did not simply increase with higher cue sound frequency, it will be important to test whether similar activity patterns are preserved when the frequencies assigned to Tone A and Tone B are reversed.

Third, the behavioral monitoring relied only on video-based and tactile-based tracking. Therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility that some of the observed cue-related activity in mPMdr reflects muscle activity not captured by our measurements. In particular, expectancy-related signals may still be partially confounded by preparatory motor activity, including subtle orofacial movements that these measures may not fully capture.

Finally, we used one-photon calcium imaging, which lacks single-cell resolution and detects population-averaged signals. This means that precise spike timing could not be estimated, resulting in limited temporal resolution. Future studies using two-photon calcium imaging or single-neuron recordings, ideally with broader coverage including medial frontal areas, will be necessary to reveal the activity dynamics of the individual neurons underlying the area-averaged signals observed here.

Methods

Animals

We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. All work involving animals followed the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Nonhuman Primates in Neuroscience Research established by the Japan Neuroscience Society. All experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Tokyo (Protocol number P20-079). Humane endpoints were predefined based on signs of distress and general health status. Animals were monitored regularly, with frequent monitoring after experimental procedures. The monitored signs included impaired food or water intake, self-injurious behavior, abnormal posture, respiratory distress, pain-related vocalization, and rapid body weight loss (>20% within a few days). Animals showing these signs received supportive care (e.g., fluids, analgesics, antibiotics, anti-inflammatory treatment, thermal support, or oxygen supplementation). If recovery was not achieved, animals were to be euthanized by intraperitoneal overdose of sodium thiopental (≥100 mg/kg).

One male (Marmoset 1) and one female (Marmoset 2) common marmoset (Callithrix jacchus) were used in the present study. The animals were laboratory bred, and when the habituation started, their ages were 25 and 21 months, respectively, and they each weighed approximately 350 g. Both marmosets were housed in a room with other marmosets in individual cages, allowing them to communicate audibly. The animals were kept under a 12:12-h light-dark cycle with the light phase starting at 8 AM. Neither animal had been used for any other experiments prior to the present study.

Virus production

The AAV plasmid of the human synapsin I promoter (hSyn)-tetracycline-controlled transactivator 2 (tTA2s) was described previously35,39,82. The plasmid for tetracycline response element (TRE)-jGCaMP8m was constructed based on the TRE3G-GCaMP6f vector33, by replacing GCaMP6f with jGCaMP8m from pGP-AAV-syn-jGCaMP8m-WPRE (a gift from the GENIE Project, Addgene plasmid 162375)34. The plasmids were packaged using the AAV2.1 mosaic-capsid vector system to achieve stable and high expression of the genes38. The virus particles, AAV2.1-hSyn-tTA2S and AAV2.1-TRE3G-jGCaMP8m38, were produced using the helper-free triple transfection procedure, purified by affinity chromatography (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL), and concentrated by ultrafiltration (Amicon Ultra-4 10 K MWCO; Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). Viral titer was determined by quantitative PCR using Taq-Man technology (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA).

General handling and training schedule

Before they underwent headplate implant surgery, the marmosets were first habituated on a daily basis to the experimenters, to wearing a jacket, and to water restriction. During this phase, the animals were positively reinforced, on a regular basis, by being provided with small portions of sweet foods and 7%–10% sucrose water. Habituation to head-fixation and the training setup started 12 days after headplate implantation for Marmoset 1 and 12 days after for Marmoset 2. Classical conditioning training started once each animal could sit calmly in the setup for more than 5 min. This required nine habituation sessions for both animals and began 27 days after surgery for Marmoset 1 and 21 days after for Marmoset 2. The animals performed the behavioral task 1–5 days per week, during which their body weight was maintained at approximately 90% of their normal level by restriction of food and water. Additional water was occasionally given to the animals after behavioral sessions, to ensure that their water intake per day was not below 5–10 mL. The behavior of the animals was monitored daily, including their food and water intake, vigor, and physical condition, to ensure their well-being. On the off-duty days, the food and water restrictions were weakened to allow them to return to their normal weight. The behavioral sessions were carried out once per day.

The classical conditioning pre-training sessions started by using 100% reward probabilities for both cues. The probabilities were then lowered to 70% (cue A) and 30% (cue B) after a robust anticipatory lick behavior appeared across sessions (Marmoset 1, 36 sessions; Marmoset 2, 38 sessions). Craniotomy, intracortical micro-stimulation (ICMS), and virus injection into each animal were conducted after differential anticipatory lick behavior was confirmed for the two tones (Marmoset 1, 52 sessions after introducing the difference in reward probabilities; Marmoset 2, 37 sessions). Food and water restrictions were lifted several days before these surgical procedures (Marmoset 1, 11 days; Marmoset 2, three days). Classical conditioning training, along with food and water restriction, was restarted 10 days after the surgery for Marmoset 1 and 28 days after for Marmoset 2. A set of imaging sessions was started after we confirmed that viral expression was sufficiently high (Marmoset 1, 38 days after virus injection; Marmoset 2, 37 days).

General surgical procedures

The surgeries were conducted in the following order: headplate implantation, craniotomy and durotomy, intracortical microstimulation (ICMS), AAV injection, and glass window implantation. Detailed procedures for each step are described in the following sections. All the surgeries and viral vector injections were carried out under aseptic conditions, as described previously9,35,39. Each marmoset was placed in a stereotaxic instrument (SR-6C-HT; Narishige, Tokyo, Japan), 0.8%–4.0% isoflurane anesthesia at a flow rate of 1 L/min was maintained. The saturation of percutaneous oxygen (SpO₂), pulse rate, and rectal temperature were monitored throughout surgery. In the perioperative period, the following medicines were intramuscularly (i.m.) administered: ampicillin (16.7 mg/kg of body weight; Nippon Zenyaku Kogyo, Fukushima, Japan) as an antibiotic, carprofen (Remadile®, 4.4 mg/kg; Zoetis, Parsippany, NJ) as an anti-inflammatory agent, and maropitant (Cerenia®, 1000 mg/kg; Zoetis) as an antiemetic. To avoid dehydration, 10 mL of Ringer’s solution and riboflavin (Bisulase®, 0.02 mL/mL Ringer solution; Toa Eiyo, Fukushima, Japan) were subcutaneously infused. Post-operative i.m. administration of ampicillin and carprofen was conducted on a daily basis for several days (the exact durations of administration are described in the specific sections below).

Headplate implantation

In the headplate implantation procedure, we depilated and sterilized the scalp, incised it with an external application of lidocaine, and removed the connective tissue to expose the skull. After small screws were anchored to the skull, a headplate was attached to the skull with dual-cured adhesive (Bondmer lightless®, Tokuyama Dental, Tokyo, Japan) and dental resin cement (Estecem II®; Tokuyama Dental, and Superbond®; Sun Medical, Shiga, Japan). Daily post-operative i.m. administration of ampicillin and carprofen was conducted for three days.

Craniotomy and durotomy

Craniotomy and durotomy for the dorsal and temporal cortices were carried out under anesthesia, and the medications described above were administered with additional intramuscular administration of dexamethasone (Decadron®; 0.5 mg/kg; Sandoz Pharma, Tokyo, Japan) and subcutaneous administration of 20% D-mannitol (2 g/kg; Yoshindo Inc., Toyama, Japan) to prevent cerebral edema. After the surgery, the exposed cortex was covered with silicone elastomer (Kwik-Sil®; World Precision Instruments, Sarasota, FL) and further covered with dental resin cement (Superbond®). For Marmoset 2, post-operative ampicillin and carprofen were administered i.m. for one day between the craniotomy and the subsequent ICMS experiment. No additional post-operative injection was administered to Marmoset 1 because the ICMS experiment, including administration of ampicillin and carprofen, was performed the next day.

ICMS

ICMS was conducted in a similar way to that described previously9,31,35 to identify the boundaries between the primary motor cortex (M1), premotor cortex (PM), and somatosensory cortices. During the ICMS experiment, we anesthetized each marmoset with ketamine (Ketalar®, initial dose 15 mg/kg; additional dose 5 mg/kg; Daiichi Sankyo Propharma, Tokyo, Japan) and xylazine (Seractal®, initial dose 0.75 mg/kg i.m.; additional dose 0.25 mg/kg; Beyer Pharma Japan, Osaka, Japan), and administered atropine (0.050 mg/kg i.m.; FUSO Pharmaceutical Industries, Osaka, Japan) for sialoschesis to prevent respiratory obstruction. A silver reference electrode was immersed in the cerebrospinal fluid on the exposed cerebral surface, then a tungsten microelectrode (impedance 0.5 MΩ, diameter 100 μm; World Precision Instrument) was inserted into the cerebral cortex to a depth of 1.8 mm. Electrical pulses were controlled with an electrical stimulator and isolator (SEN-3301 and SS-203J, Nihon Kohden, Tokyo, Japan). Fifteen 0.2-ms cathodal pulses of 333 Hz were applied. We increased the stimulation currents from 10 to 100 μA in steps of 10 μA until a body-part movement was detected by visual inspection. After the experiment, the exposed cortex was re-covered with silicone elastomer and dental resin cement. For Marmoset 2, post-operative ampicillin and carprofen were administered i.m. for one day between the ICMS experiment and the subsequent virus injection experiment. No additional post-operative injection was given to Marmoset 1 because the virus injection experiment was performed the next day.

We determined the rostrocaudal position of the PMdc-M1 border according to the current threshold required for movement generation. Using this border and information about the distance from the midline, the marmoset cortical map40 was overlaid on top of the ICMS map. Alignment of the cortical map with the imaging FOV was performed according to the vasculature patterns of the neocortical surface. It should be noted that we did not account for animal-to-animal variability in the size and shape of individual neocortical areas. The area annotation may therefore have been less precise as it moved farther away from the PMdc-M1 border. For example, a central part of the most posterior region visible through the imaging glass window, which we inferred to be the AIP, likely also contained other intraparietal areas. Thus, the AIP activity related to eye movements likely reflected activity in the lateral and caudal intraparietal areas, which are strongly involved in visual information processing.

AAV injection and glass window implantation

Mineral oil (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) was backfilled into a Hamilton syringe (25 μL, 702RN, Hamilton Company, Reno, NV) and a quartz pipette with an outer tip diameter of approximately 30 μm (Sutter Instruments, Novato, CA) was prepared. The viral solution containing rAAV2.1-TRE3-jGCaMP8m (1.05 × 10¹³ vector genomes [vg]/mL) and rAAV2.1-hSyn-tTA2 (1.75 × 10¹³ vg/mL) was front-loaded with a syringe pump (KDS310; KD Scientific, Holliston, MA). This viral solution was vertically injected into each site at a depth of 500 μm from the cortical surface at a rate of 0.10 μL/min to a total amount of 0.50 μL. The pipette was maintained in place for 5 min before being slowly withdrawn. Multiple injections were conducted in 1.6–1.7 mm spacings, resulting in 52 (Marmoset 1) or 53 (Marmoset 2) injection sites to cover the areas corresponding to the two glass-window implants. The glass-window implant was built with a rectangular glass coverslip (15 × 8 mm and 9 × 5 mm, thickness No. 1, Matsunami glass, Osaka, Japan), and a custom-made outer frame (0.4 mm width)35 was attached to the exposed dorsal and temporal cortices. The inner sizes of the glass windows were 14.2 × 7.2 mm and 8.2 × 4.2 mm for dorsal and temporal cortices, respectively. The thickness of the coverslips was approximately 0.15 mm. The gap between the implant and the cranium was infused with Kwik-sil silicone elastomer, and the implant was then fixed with dental resin cement (Super Bond). Post-operative ampicillin and carprofen were administered i.m. daily for two days for Marmoset 1 and for three days for Marmoset 2. Dexametazone was also administered i.m. post-operatively to prevent cerebral edema (Marmoset 1, 0.5 mg/kg i.m. for two days; Marmoset 2, 1.0 mg/kg i.m. for one day).

Behavioral apparatus and training

The head- and body-restrainer apparatus was as described previously9,35. The marmosets wore a jacket and were seated on the head-fixation frame (O’Hara & Co., Tokyo, Japan). The pitch- and yaw-angles of the frame were adjusted to compensate for the angle mismatch between the surface of the glass windows and the optical perpendicular. The apparatus was placed in a sound-attenuation box (O’Hara & Co.) for training sessions, or a custom-made imaging booth covered with black cloth for imaging experiments, to prevent any unwanted environmental cues. The interior of the sound-attenuation box or imaging booth was illuminated by a white ambient light (40–60 lux) at the ceiling of the box or booth; this helped in restricting the animals’ pupil dilation to observable ranges. Auditory stimuli were delivered from a full-range speaker (FE126NV2, Fostex, Tokyo, Japan) placed behind the head of the animal. To mask environmental sounds, white noise (~70 dB at the position of the animal’s head) was generated by a custom-made electric circuit and was constantly presented during all the training and imaging sessions. Reward delivery was controlled with a solenoid valve (HNB1-M5-DC12V, CKD corporation, Aichi, Japan). The animal’s lick behavior was detected by a custom-made lick sensor83 that connected the footrest with the lick spout.

Our classical conditioning paradigm was similar to the one described previously18: rewards (unconditioned stimulus) were delivered in a probabilistic manner, depending on the tone being presented as the cue (conditioned stimulus). A lick spout was placed in front of the animal’s mouth, and a drop of sweet water (7%–10% sucrose) was given as the unconditioned stimulus (reward). One of two auditory cues (16 kHz or 6 kHz, cue A or B, respectively; 80 dB sound pressure level (SPL) at the position of the animal’s head) was presented for 2 s as the conditioned stimulus, and after a delay of 1 s, the reward was delivered at a probability corresponding to the auditory cue. The animals were allowed to lick freely at any time, i.e., no punishment was given in response to lick behavior irrelevant to the reward delivery. Inter-trial intervals were 8 ± 2 s. The reward probability was set at 0.7 (70%) for cue A and 0.3 (30%) for cue B. Each block of 20 trials consisted of: (i) cue A with reward (7 trials); (ii) cue A without reward (3 trials); (iii) cue B with reward (3 trials); (iv) cue B without reward (7 trials). The order of the trials was shuffled for every other block.

During auditory stimulation experiments, the animals were presented with 0.5 s sinusoidal tone stimuli. The experiments proceeded in blocks: each block of stimuli consisted of sequential presentations of 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, and 24 kHz tones (80 dB SPL at the position of the animal’s head) at 2 ± 1 s intervals, followed by a delivery of a fixed amount of reward. The order of the sinusoidal tone stimuli within each block was pseudo-randomized. Typically, each imaging session contained 15–20 blocks of stimuli.

A NI-DAQ (USB-6363) and LabVIEW software (National Instruments, Austin, TX) were used to control and monitor the behavioral tasks, at a 1 kHz sampling rate for training and at 10 kHz for the imaging experiment. Stimulus sounds (sinusoidal tones) were generated as analog waveforms by the NI-DAQ and a custom-written LabVIEW program. The sinusoidal tone waveforms and the white noise outputs were mixed using an audio mixer and were delivered from the speaker through a custom-made audio amplifier.

Wide-field one-photon Ca2+ imaging

Wide-field one-photon Ca2+ imaging was conducted with a custom-made zoom-variable microscope equipped with an air objective lens (Plan-NEOFLUAR Z 1.0×; numerical aperture 0.25; Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The FOV size was set to fit the full extents of the dorsal and temporal glass windows. A set of 470 nm and 405 nm LEDs (M470L5 and M405L4; Thorlabs, Newton, NJ) and a filter set (MDF-GFP, Thorlabs) were used for the imaging experiments. We used the same set of dichroic mirrors and emission filters for the two excitation lights. Illumination light at 405 nm was used to detect an isosbestic fluorescence point4,19 for the correction of hemodynamic contamination. The excitation light intensity under the objective was set to 9.0 mW for the 470 nm light and 5.0 mW for the 405 nm light.

A scientific CMOS (sCMOS) camera (ORCA-Fusion; Hamamatsu Photonics, Shizuoka, Japan) with a resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels (15.8 µm/pixel) was used as a photodetector. Images were acquired at a frame rate of 40 Hz. The excitation light was switched from frame to frame with a microcontroller (Arduino Mini) and a custom script, resulting in a 20 Hz frame rate for each excitation light. Each LED illumination and frame acquisition timing from the sCMOS camera was input to the DAQ device performing the behavioral task control and analog data acquisition. Note that at the time of imaging, the two glass windows were not positioned in parallel, and there was a difference in their angles (Marmoset 1, ~20 degrees; Marmoset 2, <5 degrees). We were thus unable to align the optical axis precisely perpendicular to both of the glass windows at the same time. Nevertheless, we believe that this issue had little impact on our results, because we observed sharp neocortical vasculature boundaries and robust area-specific calcium responses.

Behavioral tracking

To monitor the body movements of the animals, three high-speed CMOS cameras (DMK33UP1300, Imaging Source, Bremen, Germany) and IC Capture acquisition software (Imaging Source) were used. The three cameras recorded the animals’ eyes, face, and left-side upper/lower body at sampling rates of 200, 60, and 30 frames/s, respectively. Objective lenses (f = 35 mm, MVL35M23 and f = 12 mm, MVL12M23, Thorlabs; f = 3 mm, 89,341 Edmund optics, Barrington, NJ) with a focal length adjuster ring (CMV05, Thorlabs) were used to obtain an appropriate FOV for each camera. The custom-written LabView behavioral-task program generated and recorded frame triggers for the three cameras simultaneously. To obtain a uniformly illuminated image from each camera, an array of 56 LEDs (940 nm, OSI5LA5453B; OptoSupply, Hong Kong, China) was mounted at the back of each camera.

In addition to the lick detector, a three-axis accelerometer (KXTC9-2050, Kionix, Ithaca, NY) was affixed to the footrest to monitor hindlimb activity. Signals from these electric sensors were recorded using the LabView behavioral-task programs.

Data acquisition

The signals recorded by the LabView behavioral-task program included: (i) periods and frequencies of sinusoidal tone presentation; (ii) the TTL signals reported by the lick detector circuit; (iii) the signals generated by the accelerometer affixed to the footrest; (iv) the frame acquisition clocks (30, 60, and 200 Hz) for behavioral videography; and (v) the timings of 470 nm and 405 nm LED flashes, corresponding to the timing of frame acquisition for wide-field calcium imaging. For wide-field calcium imaging, HCImage software (Hamamatsu Photonics) was used to grab and store frames. The data were exported as TIFF files for later analysis. IC Imaging Control software (Imaging Source) was used to acquire behavioral videography. The resulting videos were recorded in motion-JPEG format and subsequently exported to MPEG4 format.

Preprocessing of wide-field imaging data

The original imaging data consisted of frames of emission signals resulting from the alternating 405 nm and 470 nm excitation lights. These two channels were split into separate TIFF files and then processed using a pipeline including motion correction, temporal down-sampling, and baseline estimation. First, motion correction was performed based on the NoRMCorre algorithm84 to create pixel-by-pixel correspondence across imaging sessions from the same animal. The frames were then spatially down-sampled fourfold, from 1024 × 1024 to 256 × 256 pixels. The fluorescence baseline was estimated by taking the eighth percentiles of 30 s sliding windows positioned every 15 s. The resulting baseline levels were interpolated and smoothed (30 s sliding window averaging) before being used to calculate the ∆F/F values of the signals.

Hemodynamics correction of the 470 nm-excited signals was based on a previous report85 with slight modifications. The 405 nm- and 470 nm-excited signals from each pixel (in the 256 × 256-pixel frame) were bandpass-filtered using the 4–7 Hz frequency range, which corresponds to the range of the heart rate of awake marmosets86. Linear regression was performed using the bandpass-filtered signals to estimate the pixel-by-pixel contribution of the 405 nm-excited (hemodynamic) signals to the 470 nm-excited signals. Finally, hemodynamics-corrected GCaMP signals for each pixel were computed by subtracting the 405 nm-excited signals, multiplied by the regression coefficient computed earlier, from the 470 nm-excited signals.

To alleviate the possible effects of acquisition noise, we applied a two-dimensional Gaussian filter (1-pixel standard deviation) to the imaging data, followed by a pixel-by-pixel zero-phase temporal low-pass filter (4.5 Hz cutoff, second-order Butterworth filters applied from both directions). ROIs (each with an area of 10 × 10 pixels within the 256 × 256-pixel frame) were manually and arbitrarily defined over the frontoparietal cortex on the basis of the registered area annotation. Care was taken to ensure that the ROI locations on the atlas matched between the two animals, and that cue- and reward-dependent modulation was clearly observed in the given area.

Preprocessing of behavioral data

The output of the lick detector, sampled at 10 kHz, was binarized to ON or OFF at a threshold of 2.2 V. Each ON period was considered to be a raw lick event. Neighboring raw lick events with an interval shorter than 30 ms or duration shorter than 50 ms were considered to be due to “bouncing” and were merged into a single event. The resulting events were removed if they had durations longer than 350 ms, because they were likely to be due to the animal’s hand holding the lick spout. The onsets of the remaining lick events were used as the timings of individual licks.

For the output of the accelerometer on the footrest, which was sampled at 10 kHz, the first derivative of the signal was initially computed to remove any slow offset drift. The root mean-square (RMS) of the signal was then estimated by computing its 100 ms sliding window standard deviation.

DeepLabCut version 2.187,88 was used to detect the animals’ behavior from the videography. For each type of key-point estimation, a single model was trained and used for all the behavioral videos of the two animals. In general, estimations with a likelihood lower than 0.9999 were dropped, and the corresponding data points were treated as “missing”. The datasets were prepared using the same sampling rate as their source videos (i.e., eye, 200 Hz; hand, 30 Hz; face, 60 Hz) and were stored for later analysis.

For the detection of eye motion, a single DeepLabCut model was trained on 161 annotated images from 10 videos. An ellipse was fitted manually to the pupil, and 36 distinct key points were annotated along its perimeter for use as the key points to be estimated. The images underwent rigorous augmentation: we generated 20 images per annotated frame using the Python imgaug module. After the DeepLabCut model-based inference step, an ellipse based on the estimated 36 landmarks for each video frame was recovered using a least-squares approach. We defined the center of the ellipse as the position of the eye, and the diameter of the pupil was computed as double the length of the ellipse’s semi-major axis. To remove any erroneous estimation (typically due to occlusion of the eye by a finger or blinking activity), each time point was used only when the x- (horizontal-) and y- (vertical-) coordinates of the eye, as well as the pupil diameter, all lay within the 1–99th percentile range. From these data (horizontal/vertical/diameter, in pixels, at 200 Hz), the per-frame motion of the eye (dHorizontal and dVertical, in pixels/frame) and the per-frame net speed of the eye (V, in pixels/frame, non-negative) were also computed.

For the detection of hand motion, a single DeepLabCut model was trained on 126 annotated images from seven videos. A bounding rectangle was set around the left hand of the animal, and its geometrical center was annotated as the position of the hand. The images were subjected to vigorous augmentation procedures, and 20 images were generated for each annotated frame using imgaug. After estimation of the x- (horizontal-) and y- (vertical-) coordinates of the hand position at each frame (Horizontal and Vertical, in pixels, at 30 Hz), the per-frame motion of the hand (dHorizontal and dVertical, in pixels/frame) and the net speed (V, in pixels/frame, non-negative) were computed.

To detect face motion, we fine-tuned the “primate-face” model (Clair Witham, Center for Macaques, MRC Harwell, UK) in DeepLabCut Model-Zoo. The landmarks were adapted for marmoset faces under our experimental rig. It should be noted that our annotations for the ears (key-points #2–7, #20–25 of the original model) and the indents (key-points #11 and #17) did not correspond to the original annotation scheme. In addition, because of limitations with our dataset, the cheek (key-points #14) and lip (key-points #51–54) were poorly annotated, and the “neck nape” (landmark #55) was not annotated at all. We annotated 36 images extracted from six previously published videos89 and 60 images from six videos of the two animals used in this study. Note that image augmentation was not used for the training of this model. Furthermore, because the area around the animal’s mouth and lower jaw was often masked by the lick spout, only the key points at or above the level of the cheek were used for analysis. For each video (i.e., for each imaging session), two-dimensional deviations from the session-mean position were computed for each key-point at each frame. Face motion components were orthogonalized using principal component analysis (PCA). The scores of the first 18 PCs, which explained 94.17% ± 0.54% (mean ± SD, Marmoset 1, n = 10 sessions) and 94.03% ± 0.45% (Marmoset 2, n = 9 sessions) of the variance of the total deviations, were stored and used in later analysis.

Computing class-balanced averages

The trials of our classical conditioning task consisted of 2 × 2 conditions: cue A vs. cue B trials and rewarded vs. non-rewarded trials. These conditions were marginally balanced but conditionally unbalanced. For example, cue A trials consisted of ~70% rewarded trials and ~30% non-rewarded trials, whereas cue B trials included ~30% rewarded and ~70% non-rewarded trials. Simply taking averages of these unbalanced trials could lead to unwanted overrepresentation of the majority (e.g., rewarded trials in cue A trials) and underrepresentation of the minority (e.g., non-rewarded trials in cue A trials). To alleviate this issue, we performed a balancing procedure, using inversely weighted averaging to compute class-balanced means. For each trial-aligned time point t, we have four different conditions: (i) the mean of cue A rewarded trials, MA+(t); (ii) the mean of cue A non-rewarded trials, MA-(t); (iii) the mean of cue B rewarded trials, MB+(t); and (iv) the mean of cue B non-rewarded trials, MB-(t). Encoding models

We used linear encoding models with ridge regression19,47 with slight modifications. Instead of using trial-aligned peri-event time histograms (PETHs), we used one whole imaging session over its full duration (i.e., including both trial time and inter-trial intervals) to estimate and validate a single set of encoding models.

The task-related columns of the design matrix included cue-related and reward-related variables. Cue A trials and cue B trials were encoded separately as cue-related variables. Encoding of trials included time-to-time bias terms; individual timepoints in the trial (i.e., frames 1, 2, …, 160, starting from the tone onset and up to +8 s after the tone onset) were represented as separate columns for each of the tone types in the design matrix. Reward was represented (only in the timepoints corresponding to rewarded trials) as a set of time-to-time bias terms from +3 s to +8 s after the tone onset. Together, the diagonal representations of cue A trials, cue B trials, and rewards comprised the task-related columns of the design matrix.

The motion-related columns of the design matrix included: Lick (lick counts), Hand/Foot (hand Horizontal, hand Vertical, hand dHorizontal, hand dVertical, hand V, foot sensor RMS), Eye/Pupil (eye Horizontal, eye Vertical, eye dHorizontal, eye dVertical, eye V, pupil diameter), and Face (face deviation PC scores). These motion-related variables were resampled to the 20 Hz imaging frame rate. Because the face PC scores presumably contained a non-negligible amount of information overlapping with the other behavior variables, they were decorrelated from the other behavior variables using QR decomposition before being added to the design matrix. To account for the preparatory and feedback activity corresponding to the behavioral output at each time point, time-shifted versions (from –20 frames to +20 frames, 41 columns in total per variable) of each behavior-related variable were also included.

Pixel calcium activity was normalized by computing the Z-scores. To speed up the calculation of the encoding models, singular value decomposition (SVD) was applied, and the largest 8500 components, corresponding to >99% of the total variance, were used for the analysis. On the side of the design matrix, all columns were Z-scored prior to the analysis. A linear model was fitted using ridge regression to predict each singular-value component (i.e., each row vector of the product of the right singular matrix and the singular value matrix) based on the design matrix of the session. The pixel-by-pixel prediction in ∆F/F values was subsequently recovered by multiplying by the spatial components (i.e., the left singular matrix), and by referring to the mean and standard deviation values of each pixel.

Ten-fold cross-validation was used: 90% of the timepoints were used to estimate a linear model including its intercept term, and the fitting performance of each model was validated by computing the coefficient of determination for the remaining 10% of the timepoints (cross-validation R2, or cvR2). Throughout this study, out of the 10% of timepoints, only those timepoints corresponding to trials (i.e., where the tone-related variables had non-zero values) were used to compute cvR2 values. To compute the unique contribution of a specific set of variables, the columns corresponding to the variables of interest were first temporally shuffled, and then the reduction in cvR2 values in the resulting linear model (∆cvR2) was calculated. Each ∆cvR2 value was rectified to ensure that it had a non-negative value. To alleviate sampling bias, five different splits of ten-fold cross-validation were performed, and their mean value was reported.

When analyzing the responses of the auditory stimulation experiments, a 6-s trial was defined for each of the different stimulus types (1 , 2 , 4 , 6 , 8 , 12 , 16 , and 24 kHz tones, and reward delivery) in the same way as for the classical conditioning experiment. The unique contributions of cue A-, cue B-, and non-cue tone-related variables were computed for the 3 s time window after stimulus onset. When computing the cvR2 values, imbalances in the number of trials were taken into account by using only those trials and timepoints where the tones of concern were presented; given NA, NB, and NN as the number of cue A, cue B, and non-cue-tone trials, respectively, the cvR2 values were computed separately for cue A-, cue B-, and non-cue tone-related variables by using the corresponding NA × 3 s, NB × 3 s, or NN × 3 s after the stimulus onset of the corresponding trials.

Separation of trial-related variability in the assessment of model performance

To gain insights into the source of the ROI-to-ROI variability in the performance of our linear encoding models, we separated the trial-related variability in ∆F/F, on the basis of which we computed cvR2 values, into two mutually exclusive components: trial baselines and baseline-subtracted trial responses. For each trial, the baseline period was defined as the 1 s period before the cue onset, and the mean activity during this period was defined as the baseline activity. Thus, for N trials in a single session, we computed N baseline values from the original data, and N values from the predicted data based on our “full” linear encoding models, and computed cvR2 values based on these N samples. The baseline-subtracted trial responses were computed by subtracting the baseline values computed above from the original ROI traces. For each trial, the 0–6 s period after the cue onset was used to compute the extent to which our linear encoding models explained the time-to-time variability of the baseline-subtracted trials. Thus, for each session with N trials recorded at the frame rate r, we collected a pair of N × 6 × r samples, one from the original data and one from the encoding-model prediction, and computed cvR2 values based on these samples.

Assessing the effects of expression-level fluctuations

We assessed the effects of GCaMP expression-level fluctuations using linear mixed-effect models. The models were fitted to predict the level of contribution at each pixel (in terms of ∆cvR2) provided by sets of parameters in the linear encoding models, based on the ROI annotation of the pixel (fixed effects) and its GCaMP expression level (random effects). The expression level was estimated from the across-session mean fluorescence in response to excitation at the 405 nm wavelength, which corresponds to the isosbestic point of calcium-bound and calcium-free GCaMP proteins. Z-scores of the expression levels across all the pixels in the two glass windows were computed and used for all the sessions. For each session, linear mixed-effect models were fitted using the single-session ∆cvR2 values of pixels from the 12 selected ROIs (mPMdr, mPMdc, cPMdr, cPMdc, M1, PE, S1, A8c, lS2, AIP, rA1, and cA1). The fraction of variance explained by each parameter was computed as the reduction in R2 values (∆R2) in response to shuffling the set of parameters of interest. For example, ∆R2 for an ROI was computed using the data where all the pixel-to-ROI annotations in the input table were shuffled. When computing ∆R2 for the interaction terms, the correspondences between ∆cvR2 (of the encoding models) and expression levels were shuffled.

Hierarchical clustering

For the clustering of calcium activity, trial-aligned PETHs were generated for the 12 ROIs. The 1 s window before tone onset was considered as the baseline, and the mean activity during this period was subtracted for each trial. The signals were divided into three periods based on the time after the tone onset: cue (0–2 s), delay (2–3 s), and response (3–6 s). For clustering of movement-related unique contributions, all the weights assigned to all the movement-related variables were used for each ROI. For each of the four categories of signals (cue-period activity, delay-period activity, response-period activity, and movement-related unique contributions), the values pooled across all the trials from all the imaging sessions were concatenated, and Pearson’s correlation coefficient, R, was computed for each pair of ROIs. Agglomerative (bottom-up) hierarchical clustering was performed using the value (1 – R) as the distance metric. Weighted averaging of correlation coefficients was used as the linkage method. The arbitrary threshold R = 0.4 was used to define clusters.

Granger causality analysis

The unique contribution of a subset of ROIs in vector auto-regression (VAR) models was used as an estimate of Granger causality. The time series of the 12 selected ROIs was used as the vector time series of the model. To reduce their potential seasonality due to repetition of trials, the time series were transformed by first-order differencing of the original time course. We set five time windows in accordance with the analysis based on hierarchical clustering (numbers relative to the tone onset): pre-trial (–4 to –1 s), cue (0 to 2 s), delay (2 to 3 s), response (3 to 6 s), and post-response (6 to 9 s). The VAR models were fitted for each of the time windows from each session, using the same set of algorithms as for the fitting of the encoding models. The orders of the VAR models were determined in a session-by-session manner prior to fitting using the Multi-Variate Granger Causality (MVGC) toolbox51. For each session, we first estimated the optimal orders of each full model based on the Bayesian information criterion and the Akaike information criterion, considering the smaller of the two as the model order. We took the minimum value of the five model orders as the optimal model order in a session and re-constructed the models for the five time windows. Weak stationarity of all the resulting VAR models was confirmed on the basis of their coefficient matrices, by assessing that the largest of the absolute values of roots (ρ) of their characteristic equations were less than one. The MVGC toolbox was used for this procedure.

In addition to the full model, we prepared reduced models to estimate the effects of subsets of ROIs on inferring the time courses of the other ROIs. The reduced models were prepared by fitting the VAR models to the vector time series, where the time courses of the corresponding ROIs were shuffled. Ten-fold cross validation was performed on a trial-by-trial basis, and the cvR2 values from inferring each ROI time series were computed for each VAR model using all the time points of the corresponding time window. To provide an estimate of Granger causality from this subset to the other ROIs, the unique contribution of a given subset of ROIs was computed as a ∆cvR2 value by subtracting the cvR2 of the reduced model from the value of the full model. We repeated the cross-validation splits five times and report the mean values. A pair of ROIs was considered “effectively connected” if the unique contribution of the source ROI to the variability of the target ROI was found to be significant based on the resample test (see below), using a significance level of α = 0.05. The In–Out degree for each ROI was computed by subtracting the number of effective outbound connections (i.e., the number of connections for which this ROI works as the source) from the number of effective inbound connections (i.e., the number for which this ROI works as the target). A modified version of the “Digraph chord chart” library of MATLAB was used when plotting the effective connections (https://www.mathworks.com/matlabcentral/fileexchange/121043-digraph-chord-chart).

Statistics and reproducibility

To calculate the PETHs of the calcium activity, the mean of the 1 s pre-cue activity of each trial was considered as the baseline and was subtracted from the subsequent trial-aligned trace. No baseline subtraction was performed for PETHs of behavior-related parameters. For the results of the classical conditioning task, class-balanced within-session average traces were computed (Marmoset 1, n = 10 sessions; Marmoset 2, n = 9 sessions). The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was performed for each time point along the trial to compare the two trial conditions (cue A vs. cue B, or rewarded vs. non-rewarded). The significance level threshold was set to α = 0.05. Information on all statistical tests is provided as Supplementary Data files.

To test the statistical significance of ROI-by-ROI comparison, a resampling-based testing25,90 was applied. For each session, we prepared N pairs of values, with N being the number of sessions (data vs. chance for single-ROI comparison, data 1 vs. data 2 for between-ROI comparison). To test whether the mean difference between the two sets of values was statistically significant, the sampling distribution of the difference was estimated by sampling from the values with replacement, without distinguishing between the two classes under consideration, randomly assigning them class annotations, and computing the mean difference between the two classes. This resampling procedure was performed for 30,000 iterations, and we examined whether the value derived from the original data lay outside of the 95-percentile range of the resampled distribution. To compute chance-level ∆cvR2 values for each ROI from each session, we used the full-model cvR2 values based on the five different iterations (i.e., cross-validation splits). By selecting one of the five values as the reference, we computed the ∆cvR2 values of the other four full models, and defined their mean as the chance-level ∆cvR2. To compare values between ROIs, we performed multiple paired resampling tests as described above, with Holm–Bonferroni correction. The significance level threshold was set to α = 0.05. Information on all statistical tests is provided as Supplementary Data files.
