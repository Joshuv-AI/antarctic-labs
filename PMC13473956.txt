Peripheral blood mononuclear cell proteomic profiling reveals cytoskeletal- and chromatin-associated protein signatures in autism spectrum disorder
Authors: Marlieke Lisanne van Erp, Wasana Yuwattana, Chayanit Poolcharoen, Thanit Saeliw, Sittiruk Roytrakul, Natchaya Vanwong, Valerie W. Hu, Pon Trairatvorakul, Weerasak Chonchaiya, Tewarit Sarachana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeWe aimed to identify protein patterns in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) associated with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) diagnosis and clinical heterogeneity and to explore their relevance to biological processes implicated in ASD.MethodsPBMC proteomic profiles were examined in a Thai cross-sectional cohort of 191 children with ASD and 106 typically developing (TD) controls. Mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) was performed to identify differentially expressed proteins (DEPs). Exploratory classification performance was assessed by ROC analysis across clinical subgroups. Associations between DEPs and behavioral and cognitive symptoms were evaluated using mixOmics multivariate integration. DEPs were further assessed for overlap with ASD-associated genes and previously reported ASD blood and brain proteomic datasets, and functionally annotated using protein–protein interaction and enrichment analyses.ResultsThirty-nine annotated proteins were differentially expressed between children with ASD and TD controls, while a subset was associated with heterogeneity within ASD. Several DEPs were encoded by known ASD-associated genes and overlapped with proteins previously reported in ASD blood and brain studies. Functional analyses identified enrichment of cytoskeletal and chromatin-associated pathways, including an actin-centered protein interaction network. Multivariate integration analyses revealed associations between these proteins and behavioral, social, emotional, sensory, and cognitive phenotypes, while subgroup analyses indicated subtle molecular heterogeneity within ASD.ConclusionPBMC proteomic profiles identified molecular signatures associated with ASD diagnosis and clinical heterogeneity in this discovery cohort. Exploratory network analyses highlighted cytoskeletal- and chromatin-associated proteins, supporting further evaluation of peripheral proteomics as a tool for investigating ASD biology and biologically informed stratification.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a group of early-onset neurodevelopmental disorders characterized by social communication deficits and restricted or repetitive behaviors. It presents with highly variable phenotypes and frequent co-occurring conditions, including attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), anxiety, intellectual disability, epilepsy, sleep disorders, gastrointestinal issues, and immune dysregulation (Besag, 2018; Bougeard et al., 2021; Careaga et al., 2010; Meltzer and Van de Water, 2017; Robinson-Agramonte et al., 2022; Sauer et al., 2021). Approximately 0.6–1% of the global population is diagnosed with ASD (Salari et al., 2022; Zeidan et al., 2022), with U.S. rates as high as 1 in 36 children (Maenner et al., 2023). The disorder carries major socioeconomic costs (Cakir et al., 2020; Rogge and Janssen, 2019), yet treatment remains limited in part due to late diagnosis. Diagnosis typically relies on Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) (American Psychiatric Association, 2013) criteria and tools such as the Childhood Autism Rating Scale, Second Edition (CARS-2) (Schopler et al., 2010), the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule Second Edition (ADOS-2) (Lord et al., 2000), or the Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised (ADI-R) (Lord et al., 1994). Biomarkers are an attractive avenue for early and cost-effective ASD diagnosis, but progress has been hindered by substantial phenotypic heterogeneity resulting in modest effect sizes and inconsistent biomarker signatures (Siegel and Beaulieu, 2012). Identifying biologically informed ASD subgroups and their associated molecular signatures may improve understanding of ASD heterogeneity and support future efforts toward more precise stratification and intervention strategies.

To overcome this challenge, researchers are working to better understand how genetic signatures relate to specific clinical phenotypes. A previous study demonstrated an association between gene expression profiles and clinical severity (Hu et al., 2009), while another showed subgrouping by clinical traits revealed biologically meaningful differences more effectively than gene expression data alone (Yuwattana et al., 2025). Although ASD has a strong genetic basis (heritability 40–80%), gene expression is shaped by epigenetics, copy number variations, environmental exposures, and other modifiers (Kanlayaprasit et al., 2024; Kasitipradit et al., 2025; Rylaarsdam and Guemez-Gamboa, 2019; Saechua et al., 2024; Saeliw et al., 2022, 2023; Thongkorn et al., 2023), leaving up to 85% of cases idiopathic (Casanova et al., 2020).

Proteins directly reflect active biological processes and therefore represent both promising biomarkers and essential readouts of pathophysiology. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) offer an easily accessible, minimally invasive resource of protein-level information and appear particularly relevant to ASD. Although PBMCs are typically resting cells unless an immune response is triggered (Kleiveland, 2015), immune abnormalities have been reported in a subset of children with ASD. These include atypical activation, cytokine, oxidative-stress, and regulatory patterns across several cell types and assay systems, in some cases associated with symptom severity (Ahmad et al., 2018a;Ahmad et al., 2018b; Ahmad et al., 2017; Ahmad et al., 2017; Enstrom et al., 2010; Inga Jácome et al., 2016; Meltzer and Van de Water, 2017; Molloy et al., 2006; Nadeem et al., 2019; Robinson-Agramonte et al., 2022; Sallam et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2019; Warren et al., 1987). Moreover, immune-related proteins are expressed in the developing and mature nervous system, where they contribute to the establishment and modulation of synaptic connectivity (Boulanger, 2009). Experimental evidence further suggests mechanistic connections between immune imbalance and neural development; for example, reduced CD4 T-cell numbers during neurodevelopment can disrupt microglial maturation and drive ASD-like behaviors related to anxiety, motivation, and learning (Pasciuto et al., 2020). Together, these findings suggest that immune dysfunction may be a central component of ASD pathophysiology rather than merely a comorbidity. However, most studies linking immune alterations to ASD have focused on limited molecular or cellular aspects, leaving substantial gaps in our understanding of proteomic changes within immune cells and their relationship to clinical heterogeneity. To address this, we investigated the association between protein expression in PBMCs and ASD phenotypes, hypothesizing that dysregulated protein levels reflect variation in behavioral and cognitive traits through immune-related processes.

Methods

Participant inclusion and autism assessment

This cross-sectional study included Thai children aged 2–18 years of both sexes, either with Thai parents or from Chinese families living in Thailand for at least three generations. Before ASD assessment by an expert, children were screened online for psychological traits and risk for ASD. Parents/caretakers completed the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) (Goodman, 1997) for emotional, behavioral, and social functioning, and the Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers (M-CHAT) (Robins et al., 2001, 2014) for children <4 years (≥3 items or ≥2 critical items indicating risk) or the Pervasive Developmental Disorder Screening Questionnaire (PDDSQ) (Pornnoppadol et al., 2002) for children ≥4 years (score ≥18) to assess risk for ASD. Eligible children were referred for ASD and IQ assessment by a developmental and behavioral pediatrician and a psychologist. Intellectual ability was measured with the Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence, Fourth Edition (WPPSI-IV) (Wahlstrom et al., 2018) (<6 years) or Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence, Second Edition (WASI-II) (Wechsler, 2011) (≥6 years). Verbally fluent participants ≥6 years with IQ ≥ 80 were assessed with CARS-2 High-Functioning Version (CARS-2-HF); younger children, those with lower IQ, or with communication difficulties were assessed with CARS-2 Standard Version (CARS-2-ST) as suggested in the CARS-2 manual. Medical history from the past 6 months was also recorded. Children were assigned to the TD group if ASD was excluded by expert assessment based on DSM-5 (American Psychiatric Association, 2013) and CARS-2 (Schopler et al., 2010) criteria, or to the ASD group if diagnosis was confirmed. CARS-2-ST and CARS-2-HF assess comparable items, as presented in Supplementary Data S1. Likewise, PDDSQ and M-CHAT were integrated by matching items of corresponding domains: social interaction, communication, restricted/repetitive behaviors, sensory issues, thinking style, and cognition (Supplementary Data S1).

Participant exclusion criteria

Children were excluded if their parents or caretakers did not consent or if they had developmental delays due to causes other than ASD. This included childhood disintegrative disorder, genetic syndromes (e.g., Down, Rett, Prader-Willi, velocardiofacial, neurocutaneous), visible birth defects, neurological disorders with known causes (e.g., cerebral palsy, brain tumors, congenital brain malformations, neuromuscular disorders), psychiatric disorders (e.g., schizophrenia, depression, bipolar disorder, anxiety), prenatal alcohol or drug exposure, birth complications (<37 weeks gestation, <2500 g, severe oxygen deprivation, cerebral hemorrhage), congenital hypothyroidism, or environmental deprivation.

ASD subgrouping

This study included 191 children with ASD and 106 TD controls. ASD subgroups were defined via unsupervised machine learning on clinical phenotypes as described by Yuwattana et al. (2026), using k-means and k-medoids clustering. Three subgroups were identified: ASD_sub1 (n = 81) with severe social, cognitive, and hyperactive symptoms; ASD_sub2 (n = 57) with mild symptoms; and ASD_sub3 (n = 53) with moderate symptoms but severe anxiety/insecurity and hyperactivity. Male-to-female ratios ranged 78–89%:11–22% (Fig. 1a), reflecting typical ASD prevalence (Halladay et al., 2015; Loomes et al., 2017). ASD_sub1 children were slightly younger (Fig. 1b), and all ASD subgroups had lower IQ than TD (as expected), with the lowest in ASD_sub1 (Fig. 1c). The TD group included children with IQ levels ranging from extremely low to very superior, with the vast majority in the average or low-average range. Sample collection and protein extraction

After CARS-2 evaluation, 6 mL of peripheral blood was collected from each participant. TD and ASD samples were processed concurrently under identical conditions by the same personnel using a standardized protocol. Samples were randomized across extraction and measurement runs to avoid confounding group assignment with batch effects. PBMCs were isolated using HiSep™ (LSM 1077), washed twice with PBS, counted, and resuspended in 500–750 μL GENEzol™. Chloroform was added to separate phases, retaining proteins in the supernatant after RNA and DNA removal. Proteins were precipitated with isopropanol, washed with cold acetone, and solubilized in lysis buffer (7 M urea, 2 M thiourea, 4% CHAPS, 100 mM DTT, protease inhibitors) before storage at −80°C. Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay. To assess protein integrity and verify consistent protein loading, one sample from each extraction day was analyzed by Coomassie-stained SDS-PAGE, followed by densitometric analysis of total lane intensities using ImageJ. For downstream analyses, PBMC proteins were aliquoted to 20 μg in 50 μL lysis buffer.

Mass spectrometry

NanoLC-MS/MS was performed using an Ultimate3000 Nano/Capillary LC System (Thermo Scientific, UK) coupled to a ZenoTOF 7600 mass spectrometer (SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA). Five μg of protein was digested in-solution: dissolved in 10 mM ammonium bicarbonate (AMBIC), reduced with 5 mM DTT (60°C, 1 h), alkylated with 15 mM IAA (RT, 45 min, dark), and digested with trypsin (1:20, 16 h, 37°C). Samples were dried, resuspended in 0.1% formic acid, and 1 μL injected. Peptides were enriched on a μ-Precolumn (C18 Pepmap 100) and separated on a 75 μm × 15 cm C18 column at 60°C with a 5–55% acetonitrile gradient (0.3 μL/min, 30 min). Mass spectrometry data were acquired on a ZenoTOF 7600 system operating in positive ion mode. Ion source parameters were set as follows: ion source gas 1 at 8 psi, curtain gas at 35 psi, CAD gas at 7 psi, source temperature at 200°C, and spray voltage at 3300 V. Data-dependent acquisition (DDA) was performed using a cycle time of 3.0 s, with the top 60 most abundant precursor ions selected per MS1 survey scan at an intensity threshold of 150 cps. Dynamic exclusion and dynamic collision energy were enabled, and MS/MS spectra were collected over a mass range of 100–1800 m/z with a 50-ms accumulation time and Zeno trap enabled. A declustering potential of 80 V was applied. To monitor analytical performance, a tryptic BSA digest was injected as a standalone QC standard at the start of each analytical day, and solvent blank injections were performed every 10 samples to minimize carryover. Samples were analyzed in triplicate.

Mass spectrometry data processing

Raw mass spectrometry data were processed in MaxQuant (Tyanova et al., 2016) with Andromeda against the UniProt Homo sapiens (UniProt Consortium, 2025), downloaded on 9 December 2024. The precursor peptide tolerances were set to 20 ppm for the first search and 10 ppm for the main search. Trypsin/P digestion was specified with up to two missed cleavages. Carbamidomethylation was set as a fixed modification, and methionine oxidation and N-terminal acetylation were set as variable modifications, with a maximum of five modifications per peptide. Peptides shorter than seven amino acids or larger than 4600 Da were excluded. Proteins were quantified using unique and razor peptides, with at least two peptides required per protein. Advanced ratio estimation and match between runs were enabled, and the false discovery rate (FDR) was set at 1%. Other parameters were kept at default settings.

Potential contaminant proteins were incorporated into the search space automatically by MaxQuant using the built-in contaminants FASTA file. UPS1 standard protein sequences were included as internal standard proteins. The resulting ProteinGroups.txt file was subsequently imported into Perseus (Tyanova et al., 2016) for downstream analysis. Entries annotated as potential contaminants were excluded unless they matched UPS1 standard proteins, and reverse hits were also removed from the dataset.

Proteins were further processed in MetaboAnalyst version 6.0 (Pang et al., 2024). A group-wise filter was applied, retaining proteins if they contained valid measurements in at least 50% of samples in one or more clinical groups, to minimize excessive imputation and reduce the influence of sparsely quantified proteins. Because quantitative proteomic datasets may contain a mixture of missing completely at random (MCAR), missing at random (MAR), and missing not at random (MNAR) values (Lazar et al., 2016), multiple missing value strategies were evaluated, including no imputation, k-nearest neighbours with variable selection (kNN-var), mechanism-integrated group-wise pre-imputation (MGpI; Du et al., 2024), and quantile regression imputation of left-censored data (QRILC). Differential expression patterns were compared across methods to assess robustness. QRILC was selected as the primary approach because missing values in label-free LC-MS/MS data are commonly attributed to low-abundance proteins below the detection limit (left-censored MNAR), and QRILC has been shown to preserve underlying data structure and multivariate relationships under this mechanism (Wei et al., 2018). Triplicate intensity values were averaged prior to downstream statistical analyses.

Data visualization, functional enrichment, and overlap analyses

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed in R using the FactoMineR package (Lê et al., 2008). Pairwise comparisons between groups were conducted in R using independent t-tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing (adjusted p < 0.05 considered significant). All primary analyses were performed using the full cohort to maximize statistical power and preserve biological heterogeneity. To assess potential age and sex effects, post hoc sensitivity analyses were performed using age- and sex-matched groups, with 47 male and 6 female participants included per comparison. The mean age ± standard deviation (SD) was 7.6 ± 3.4 years in ASD_sub1, 8.8 ± 4.0 years in ASD_sub2, 8.8 ± 4.2 years in ASD_sub3, and 8.7 ± 4.1 years in TD controls; the corresponding mean IQ ± SD values were 52.9 ± 15.0, 73.7 ± 21.4, 74.9 ± 18.5, and 90.6 ± 11.3, respectively.

Heatmaps of clinical phenotype scores were created in R using the pheatmap package (version 1.0.12) (Kolde, 2018). Box plots for differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) between ASD subgroups were generated using ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016) and reshape2 (Wickham, 2007) in R. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated using the pROC (Robin et al., 2011) package in R to explore the classification performance of selected proteins. Area under the curve (AUC) values, along with 95% confidence intervals, were calculated to quantify classification performance between ASD subgroups and TD controls. Functional enrichment analyses and protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were assessed using the STRING database (Szklarczyk et al., 2023; von Mering et al., 2005). Enrichment p-values reported by STRING were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure within each annotation category (e.g., Gene Ontology Biological Process, Molecular Function, Cellular Component, Reactome, and KEGG pathways), as implemented by the STRING platform. Identified proteins were overlapped with previously identified DEPs from serum (Mesleh et al., 2023; Schwarz et al., 2011), PBMCs (Shen et al., 2019), plasma (Shen et al., 2018; Tang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2023), dried blood spots (T. Lee et al., 2025), and post-mortem brain tissues (Fatemi et al., 2024, 2025) of children with ASD and adults. DEPs were overlapped with genes listed in the SFARI Gene database (list updated 23 October 2025; accessed 30 October 2025; http://gene.sfari.org). To assess enrichment of ASD-associated risk genes among DEPs, Fisher's exact test (one-sided, alternative = "greater") was performed in R using the detected proteome as the background.

Integration of proteomic and clinical data

Integration of proteomic and clinical data was conducted in R using the mixOmics Data Integration Analysis for Biomarker Discovery using Latent Variable Approaches for Omics Studies (DIABLO) framework for supervised multiblock sparse Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (sPLS-DA) (Rohart et al., 2017). DIABLO integrates multiple datasets measured on the same samples (or individuals) by constructing latent components that maximize the covariance between data blocks while simultaneously discriminating predefined outcome groups. Sparse variable selection through Lasso penalization enables the identification of a correlated multi-omics signature linking proteomic profiles with clinical and behavioral phenotypes. For the ASD versus TD DIABLO model, the dataset was randomly partitioned into training (75%; 143 ASD and 80 TD) and testing (25%; 48 ASD and 26 TD) sets. To investigate heterogeneity within ASD, a separate DIABLO model was constructed using only ASD participants, who were randomly partitioned into training (75%; ASD_sub1 = 59, ASD_sub2 = 44, ASD_sub3 = 40) and testing (25%; ASD_sub1 = 22, ASD_sub2 = 13, ASD_sub3 = 13) sets while preserving subgroup proportions. Model performance was evaluated via repeated 5-fold cross-validation (50 repeats) using maximum, centroid, and Mahalanobis distances. Bar plots were created to visualize the importance of variables in driving the data structure, using the mixOmics plotLoadings() function. Strong correlations (|r| > 0.6) between clinical and proteomic data were visualized in circos plots, using the circosPlot() function.

Digital cytometry

Immune cell proportions were estimated using CIBERSORTx (Newman et al., 2019), which deconvolutes bulk RNA-seq data using reference signature matrices. A published single-cell RNA-seq–derived signature matrix of PBMCs from healthy children (Vance et al., 2026) was obtained and formatted for use in CIBERSORTx using R. Bulk RNA-seq data were generated as previously described (Yuwattana et al., 2026) for 220 participants (150 ASD, 70 TD) on the DNBSEQ™ platform (BGI Genomics, Thailand), with an average of 20 million paired-end reads per sample. For deconvolution analysis, RNA-seq data were restricted to participants overlapping with the proteomics dataset, resulting in a final cohort of 63 ASD_sub1, 43 ASD_sub2, 41 ASD_sub3, and 69 TD individuals.

Results

Proteomic profiling of ASD versus TD: DEPs and subgroup differences

Prior to LC-MS/MS analysis, protein integrity was assessed by Coomassie-stained SDS-PAGE and densitometric analysis. We identified no evidence of substantial loading differences or protein degradation; representative gels are given in Supplementary Figure S1. Subsequently, PBMC protein samples were subjected to LC-MS/MS to identify protein signatures associated with ASD clinical subgroups and TD. A total of 2548 proteins were detected, of which 53 proteins met the inclusion criteria (<50% group-wise missing values). A two-component PCA of these proteins suggested distinct protein expression patterns between ASD and TD (Fig. 2a), but not between males and females (Fig. 2b), across age ranges (Fig. 2c) or intelligence levels (Fig. 2d). Pearson's correlation analyses of four randomly selected proteins showed no clear linear relationship between age or IQ and protein expression, although these exploratory analyses do not exclude residual age- or IQ-related effects across the full protein set (Supplementary Figure S2). Post hoc age- and sex-matched sensitivity analyses showed highly concordant effect directions between the original and matched analyses: all ASD_sub2–TD and ASD_sub3–TD proteins, and 37 of 39 ASD_sub1–TD proteins, showed the same direction of change. ASD subgroup comparisons were also largely consistent, with 35–38 of 39 proteins showing the same direction. Proteins distinguish ASD from TD but not between clinical subtypes

We next explored the classification performance of our 39 DEPs using ROC analyses for each ASD subgroup versus TD controls. Top-performing proteins with AUC values greater than 0.8 for distinguishing each ASD subgroup from TD controls in this discovery cohort are shown in Fig. 4a–c and their overlap across subgroups is illustrated in Fig. 4d. In total, 21 proteins showed AUC values greater than 0.8 for distinguishing one or more ASD subgroups from TD controls in this discovery cohort. Of these, KRT1 showed the strongest discrimination for ASD_sub2, PCK2 for both ASD_sub2 and ASD_sub3, and ACTA2, DPYSL5, H1-2, POTEF, and SLAMF9 for ASD_sub3. Note that these proteins also demonstrated moderate discrimination (AUC = 0.7–0.8) in the remaining ASD subgroups (Supplementary Data S5), suggesting they are relatively stronger markers of the indicated subgroup rather than subgroup-specific markers. ROC analyses comparing each ASD subgroup against the remaining ASD subgroups showed limited discriminatory performance (AUC = 0.5–0.6) (Supplementary Data S6). Thus, individual proteins showed exploratory classification potential for distinguishing ASD from TD but limited ability to discriminate among ASD clinical subtypes. As all analyses were performed in the discovery cohort, all reported classification performance requires independent validation and should be considered exploratory. Nevertheless, the moderate differences in classification performance across clinical subgroups prompted us to investigate whether integrated molecular and clinical features could better capture the underlying heterogeneity of ASD.Fig. 4Exploratory ROC analysis of selected proteins in the discovery cohort. Protein signatures associated with autism heterogeneity differ from those associated with diagnosis

To identify proteomic patterns associated with clinical heterogeneity within ASD, we applied the multivariate DIABLO framework implemented in mixOmics. Repeated cross-validation of the sPLS-DA model indicated optimal performance with two components (lowest overall and balanced error rates; Supplementary Figure S3), which were therefore used in all subsequent analyses. The ASD-only DIABLO model achieved a prediction error rate of 22.9% and a balanced error rate of 27.2%, indicating moderate discrimination between clinically defined ASD subgroups (Supplementary Table S2). Notably, the model correctly classified most ASD_sub1 participants (21/22, 95.5%), whereas ASD_sub2 and ASD_sub3 were frequently misclassified as each other. No participants from either subgroup were classified as ASD_sub1, suggesting that ASD_sub1 represents the most distinct subgroup in the integrated feature space.

To identify the variables contributing most strongly to subgroup separation, we examined the protein loading plots from the integrated sPLS-DA model (Fig. 5a). Although ASD_sub1 exhibited the highest classification accuracy, the strongest individual protein contributors were associated with ASD_sub2. Specifically, Tcr-alpha, YDJC, and RCOR1 showed the largest absolute loadings and contributed most strongly to the separation of ASD_sub2 from the other ASD subgroups. To further explore the relationship between these proteins and clinical phenotypes, we generated a circos plot depicting strong correlations (|r| > 0.60) between proteins and clinical measures (Fig. 5b; Supplementary Data S7). Strong negative correlations were observed between RCOR1, Tcr-alpha, and YDJC and visual response (CARS7; r = −0.61, −0.68, and −0.63, respectively). Tcr-alpha additionally showed strong negative correlations with emotional response (CARS3; r = −0.65), relating to people (CARS1; r = −0.63), and imitation (CARS2; r = −0.60). Positive correlations above the threshold were also observed between Tcr-alpha and prosocial behavioral traits, including a tendency to voluntarily help others (SDQ20; r = 0.61) and being helpful when others are hurt, upset, or feeling ill (SDQ9; r = 0.60).

To determine whether the proteins associated with ASD heterogeneity also contributed to ASD diagnosis, we next applied DIABLO to an integrated ASD-versus-TD model and examined the proteins contributing most strongly to group separation. The ASD-versus-TD model showed low internal classification error in this discovery cohort, with a prediction error rate of 1.4% and a balanced error rate of 1.0%, correctly classifying all TD participants and all but one ASD participant (Supplementary Table S3).

Examination of the loading profiles revealed a distinct set of contributing proteins, with ACTBL2, ACTB, TMEM179, and UIMC1 among the strongest drivers of ASD–TD separation (Fig. 5c). Collectively, these findings indicate that protein signatures associated with ASD diagnosis differ, at least in part, from those associated with clinical heterogeneity within ASD. Although integrated proteomic profiles separated ASD from TD in this discovery cohort, within-ASD variation appeared to involve distinct molecular features linked to specific behavioral domains.

The circos analysis further demonstrated that proteins driving diagnostic separation were not necessarily those most strongly associated with individual clinical features (Fig. 5d; Supplementary Data S8). For example, TMEM179 contributed substantially to ASD–TD separation despite showing only moderate correlations with behavioral measures, whereas ACTB and ACTBL2 showed stronger associations across multiple symptom domains. These findings suggest that protein signatures associated with ASD diagnosis and phenotypic heterogeneity are overlapping but distinct.

Peripheral protein networks in autism implicate cytoskeletal and chromatin remodeling functions

To explore the biological relevance of our 39 DEPs in ASD, we investigated their functional relationships, molecular functions, and biological processes using protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis in STRING. Out of the 39 DEPs, 38 proteins were successfully mapped in STRING, while one protein, Tcr-alpha, could not be identified and was excluded from the analysis. STRING enrichment p-values were FDR-corrected within each annotation category as implemented by STRING.

The resulting network showed a significantly higher number of interactions than expected by chance (adjusted p = 0.000616), suggesting biological connectivity among the DEPs. The network was significantly enriched for proteins functioning as structural constituents of the cytoskeleton (GO:0005200; adjusted p = 0.0057) and structural molecule activity (GO:0005198; adjusted p = 0.00084) (Fig. 6a and b). Within the cytoskeleton-related cluster, key interacting DEPs included ACTBL2, ACTB, KRT9, KRT14, and ARPC2. Notably, two neuronal-related enrichments were also observed in this network: structural constituent of postsynaptic actin cytoskeleton (GO:0098973) and glutamatergic synapse (GO:0098978) (Fig. 6c and d). The structural molecule activity cluster consisted of MACF1, KRT14, ACTB, ARPC2, ACTBL2, KRT1, KRT9, PI3, H3-3B, H2BC15, and H1-2. This network was significantly enriched for several biological processes, including intermediate filament cytoskeleton organization (GO:0045104), organelle organization (GO:0006996), cytoskeleton organization (GO:0007010), intermediate filament organization (GO:0045109), and chromatin remodeling (GO:0006338) (Fig. 6e and f). Furthermore, overlap of proteins was found with disease–gene association (brain disease; DOID:936) and tissue expression (sense organ tissue expression; BTO:0000202). However, neither group showed significant protein–protein interaction (PPI) enrichment (adjusted p = 0.538 and adjusted p = 0.164, respectively), indicating that the observed proteins do not exhibit more interactions among themselves than expected for a random set of proteins of similar size and connectivity, and therefore do not form a clearly interconnected biological network. Computational deconvolution reveals no evidence of immune cell proportion differences

To determine whether the observed proteomic differences could be explained by shifts in immune cell composition rather than aberrant intracellular processes, we performed computational deconvolution of RNA-seq data. No significant differences in relative immune cell type proportions were observed either between ASD subgroups or between ASD and TD controls (Supplementary Data S9). These findings do not suggest major differences in relative immune cell composition, although absolute cell numbers and functional cellular states could not be assessed using this approach.

Sensitivity analyses

To evaluate the influence of residual contaminants, differential expression analyses were repeated after excluding keratin proteins and F2. No proteins lost or gained statistical significance after FDR correction compared with the original analysis (Supplementary Data S10). Protein–protein interaction analysis continued to show significant network enrichment, although cytoskeletal pathway enrichment was no longer observed (Supplementary Figure S4).

Cytoskeletal processes across blood proteomic studies

We compared our DEPs with previously reported blood-derived DEPs in ASD, spanning studies of serum, PBMCs, plasma, and dried blood spots. These included 39 DEPs identified in serum by Schwarz et al. (2011), 248 in serum by Mesleh et al. (2023), 41 in PBMCs by Shen et al. (2019), 24 and 51 in plasma by Shen et al. (2018) and Tang et al. (2025), respectively, 45 in plasma by Zhang et al. Limited overlap of proteins across previous studies may reflect methodological differences between studies, including variations in sample type, cohort characteristics, and analytical approaches, rather than a true absence of shared molecular alterations. Therefore, we next assessed whether protein networks showed convergence at the level of Gene Ontology terms or pathways by performing STRING analyses on each list of DEPs. We observed overlap at the functional level, with protein clusters related to the molecular function structural constituent of cytoskeleton (GO:0005200) reported in both dried blood spots and PBMCs; networks associated with cytoskeletal organization (GO:0007010) detected in plasma, dried blood spots, and serum; and proteins involved in intermediate filament organization (GO:0045109) reported in dried blood spots (Table 1). In contrast, no cytoskeleton-related pathways or biological processes were significantly enriched among the DEPs reported by Zhang et al. (2023). Differentially expressed proteins correspond to autism risk genes

To further explore the biological relevance of our DEPs to ASD, we next investigated whether the genes encoding our DEPs have been previously associated with the disorder. The updated SFARI Gene Database contained 1255 genes, of which 124 were represented among the 1808 detected proteins with known gene names. Among the 39 DEPs, seven overlapped with genes listed in the SFARI database. A one-sided Fisher's exact test using the detected proteome as the background indicated that this overlap was greater than expected by chance (odds ratio = 3.09, p = 0.01) (Fig. 7). Table 2 provides an overview of the 7 DEPs with corresponding genes that have been previously associated with ASD. AHDC1 stands out with a notably high confidence score, supported by at least 34 reports linking it to ASD. ACTB also has a high confidence score, with at least 11 reports associating it with the disorder. UIMC1 is considered a strong candidate gene for ASD, with 6 supporting reports. Suggestive evidence is found for involvement of ATXN2, H3-3B, KMT2B, and MACF1 in ASD. These genes include pleiotropic and syndromic ASD-associated genes; therefore, their overlap should be interpreted as pathway-level biological convergence rather than ASD-specific biomarker evidence. Discussion

As summarized in Fig. 8, this study aimed to identify PBMC proteomic signatures associated with ASD diagnosis and clinical heterogeneity and to evaluate their relationship with ASD-associated molecular processes. We identified marked proteomic differences between ASD and TD individuals, more subtle signatures associated with heterogeneity within ASD, and multiple DEPs encoded by ASD-associated genes. Together, these findings suggest that PBMC proteomes contain molecular signatures associated with ASD diagnosis and heterogeneity in this cohort and may support future biologically informed stratification studies. More broadly, PBMC protein profiles are increasingly being investigated as accessible peripheral molecular readouts in central nervous system disorders, including Parkinson's disease, supporting the rationale for evaluating PBMC-derived biomarkers across neurological conditions (M.S. Lee et al., 2025). In ASD, such peripheral molecular signatures may ultimately complement broader multimodal biomarker frameworks that integrate molecular, behavioral, neuroimaging, and other biological data, although further validation in independent cohorts is required before clinical application (Agrawal and Agrawal, 2025).Fig. 8Overview of analytical workflow and key findings. The biological relevance of our findings is supported by the identification of interconnected protein networks and multiple DEPs encoded by ASD-associated genes. Exploratory functional enrichment analyses highlighted cytoskeletal organization and chromatin remodeling, processes with established roles in immune-cell activation and neurodevelopment. For example, dynamic actin remodeling is required for immune cell motility, antigen presentation, and the formation and stabilization of the immunological synapse during T cell activation (Bunnell et al., 2001; Dustin and Cooper, 2000; Fritzsche et al., 2017). Growing evidence points to a contributory role of T cell dysregulation in ASD-related behavioral symptoms (Moreno et al., 2025). Our enrichment results extend beyond cytoskeletal organization to include chromatin remodeling, consistent with evidence that T cell activation involves widespread, coordinated changes in chromatin accessibility and higher-order chromatin interactions that regulate gene expression programs central to T cell biology (Bediaga et al., 2021). We observed no comparable chromatin-remodeling enrichment in prior plasma- and serum-based proteomic studies; this lack may be attributable, at least in part, to the nuclear nature of many proteins involved in this process, which limits their detectability in circulating biofluids.

ACTB (β-actin) occupied a central position within the cytoskeletal network. Beyond its structural role, ACTB participates in chromatin remodeling complexes that regulate chromatin accessibility and gene expression (Mahmood et al., 2021), consistent with evidence that immune activation involves coordinated cytoskeletal and transcriptional remodeling (Bediaga et al., 2021). Altered ACTB levels have also been reported in PBMCs of individuals with Baraitser–Winter syndrome, where they were linked to defective T-cell synapse formation (Bar-On et al., 2025; Rivière et al., 2012; Verloes et al., 2015). Together with reports of altered ACTG1 and ACTN1 expression in plasma from children with ASD (Shen et al., 2018), these findings support broader involvement of actin-associated pathways in ASD.

Several DEPs with roles in cytoskeletal organization were encoded by genes listed in ASD-associated databases, consistent with previous studies implicating cytoskeletal processes in ASD biology (Chang et al., 2018; Gąssowska-Dobrowolska et al., 2022; Griesi-Oliveira et al., 2018; Joensuu et al., 2018). These findings are also consistent with systems-level studies showing convergence of inherited and de novo variants on shared molecular pathways (An et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2026; Parikshak et al., 2016). Cytoskeletal processes also play key roles in neurodevelopment and synaptic function (Cingolani and Goda, 2008), and disrupted actin dynamics have been reported in ASD-derived stem cells and in developmental disorders caused by ACTB mutations (Cuvertino et al., 2017; Griesi-Oliveira et al., 2018).

Several identified DEPs, including ARPC2 and MACF1, have previously been reported in ASD post-mortem brain tissue (Fatemi et al., 2024, 2025). Although keratins also overlapped with published brain datasets, these proteins are susceptible to contamination in mass spectrometry-based proteomics and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

In addition, the identified network contained annotations related to glutamatergic synapse components, which is consistent with known roles of actin dynamics in synaptic function (Durand et al., 2012; Rojas, 2014); however, these annotations were inferred from peripheral proteins and should not be interpreted as direct evidence of synaptic pathology.

Outside the ACTB network, several proteins remained noteworthy. DPYSL5, a regulator of synaptic function (Desprez et al., 2023), was elevated in both PBMCs from children with ASD and previously reported ASD brain tissue (Fatemi et al., 2024), with the highest levels observed in the subgroup characterized by severe anxiety/insecurity and hyperactivity. DDX17 and ATXN2, two interacting proteins previously implicated in ASD, also showed strong associations with clinical phenotypes. Together, these findings suggest that molecular heterogeneity in ASD extends beyond cytoskeletal pathways alone.

Finally, proteins associated with ASD heterogeneity differed from those primarily distinguishing ASD from TD. Tcr-alpha, YDJC, and RCOR1 (REST corepressor 1) were associated with variation in clinical phenotypes. RCOR1, a core component of the CoREST chromatin remodeling complex, is essential for neuronal migration and differentiation in the cerebral cortex (Fuentes et al., 2012; Garay et al., 2016). Although measured in PBMCs, chromatin remodeling complexes operate across multiple cell types (Clapier et al., 2017), suggesting that RCOR1-associated variation may reflect epigenetic mechanisms relevant to ASD heterogeneity.

Increasing evidence suggests that neurodevelopmental disorders involve complex interactions between the nervous and immune systems, with immunological phenotypes observed both prenatally and throughout development (Kim et al., 2024). For example, a subset of individuals with ASD exhibits transient behavioral improvements during fever (Curran et al., 2007; Grzadzinski et al., 2018). Together, these observations highlight the importance of investigating neuroimmune interactions as a potential contributor to biological heterogeneity in ASD. Although the present study cannot establish causal relationships, the identification of interconnected peripheral protein signatures, including proteins involved in cytoskeletal organization and chromatin remodeling, supports further investigation of immune-cell states and their potential contribution to ASD heterogeneity. Such efforts may help clarify how immunological processes intersect with neurodevelopmental pathways and whether they contribute to symptom variation across individuals with ASD. A deeper understanding of these pathways may eventually help clarify whether immune-cell regulatory mechanisms contribute to ASD symptom variation and whether they represent future translational targets (Kim et al., 2024).

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, recent evidence indicates that specific monocyte and T-cell subpopulations are altered in children with ASD and are associated with symptom severity (Breece et al., 2025). Although no differences in relative immune cell proportions were detected by computational deconvolution, this approach cannot capture absolute cell numbers or functional cellular states. Future studies integrating proteomics with flow cytometry, complete blood counts, and functional immune phenotyping may clarify how immune-cell states contribute to ASD heterogeneity and whether peripheral molecular signatures relate to neurocognitive function.

Second, comparisons were limited to ASD and typically developing controls, precluding assessment of specificity relative to other neurodevelopmental conditions. Although no significant linear relationships between IQ and protein expression were observed and age- and sex-matched sensitivity analyses yielded similar results, residual confounding by IQ or other unmeasured factors cannot be excluded, particularly given the close relationship between cognitive ability and ASD phenotypic heterogeneity.

Third, ASD subgroup definitions were based on behavioral measures later used in correlation analyses; thus, these associations should be considered exploratory and require longitudinal validation in independent cohorts. Similarly, DEP selection, ROC analyses, and DIABLO modeling were conducted within the same discovery cohort, so internal classification estimates should not be interpreted as externally validated diagnostic accuracy.

Finally, proteome coverage reflected a conservative filtering strategy that prioritized consistently quantified proteins, resulting in a smaller but high-confidence dataset. Reduced proteome depth may nevertheless have limited detection of additional ASD-relevant pathways. Moreover, highly abundant structural proteins, including keratins, are susceptible to contamination and pre-analytical variation, meaning that apparent blood biomarkers may sometimes reflect handling artifacts rather than disease biology (Geyer et al., 2017). Accordingly, the cytoskeletal enrichments reported here should be interpreted cautiously. Sensitivity analyses excluding keratin proteins and F2 did not alter differential-expression results, but the specific cytoskeletal pathway enrichments were attenuated, indicating that these functional annotations should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive evidence of ASD-related biology. Future studies should validate the identified protein signatures and pathway-level findings in independent cohorts using complementary targeted approaches, such as immunoassays or targeted mass spectrometry, to establish their reproducibility and potential clinical utility.

Conclusion

PBMC proteomic profiles identified molecular signatures associated with ASD diagnosis and clinical heterogeneity. Exploratory network analyses highlighted proteins involved in cytoskeletal organization and chromatin remodeling, supporting the potential relevance of peripheral proteomics to ASD biology and its utility for biologically informed patient stratification.
