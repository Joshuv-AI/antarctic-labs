Executive Summary
Utility-scale photovoltaic (PV) plants are highly digitalized assets that continuously
generate large volumes of operational data through supervisory control and data
acquisition (SCADA) systems. Despite this richness of data, solar operations centers
are often “data-rich but information-poor,” lacking automated, scalable approaches to
translate raw measurements into actionable diagnostics. This project developed and
validated algorithms and tools to automate the detection, diagnosis, and benchmarking
of faults, failures, and underperformance across PV fleets using existing
instrumentation.
During the initial project period, research focused on near-real-time detection of
balance-of-system (BOS) faults—including string, combiner, and tracker outages—using
hybrid physics-based and machine-learning models. These methods demonstrated high
detection rates (>50% true positive rate in most plants) with low false positive rates
(<10%) when validated against aerial infrared inspections. Automated site-setup
routines dramatically reduced engineering effort, enabling plant models to be
established in minutes rather than days.
In the extended phase of the project, methods were expanded beyond fault detection to
plant-level underperformance analysis and industry-wide benchmarking through the

Solar Utilization, Performance, and Reliability (SUPER) platform. SUPER standardizes
data quality control, expected power modeling, loss categorization, and key
performance indicator (KPI) calculation across all participating plants, enabling fair
comparisons across owners, technologies, and vintages. As of 2026, SUPER includes
over 9 GWac across 120+ plants, providing statistically robust insights into performance
loss rates, inverter downtime, and capacity degradation.

Key findings include:
 • A fleet-wide performance loss rate of approximately −0.7%/year, consistent
 with—but slightly worse than—common financial assumptions.

 • Inverter downtime averaging ~2–3%, materially higher than the ~1% commonly
 assumed in project finance models.
The project culminated in a scalable analytical framework, publicly disseminated
methodologies, and a commercially deployed benchmarking platform that enables
continuous improvement in PV performance and reliability across the industry.

 1. Background
Background and Relation to Current Literature
This section summarizes recent peer-reviewed journal papers, conference proceedings,
and technical reports relevant to SCADA-based PV fault detection/diagnostics,
underperformance analytics, and fleet benchmarking. The intent is to position the
project alongside the current state of the art, clarify points of alignment and
differentiation, and document how external R&D has been leveraged to accelerate
development and improve methodological rigor.

SCADA-based PV fault detection and diagnostics
Similarities to this project. Recent literature increasingly emphasizes fault detection
using existing operational data streams (SCADA) to avoid high-cost or low-frequency
inspections. Review work [1] surveys thermography and AI approaches, underscoring
the same practical drivers addressed here: early detection, reduced energy loss, and
scalable workflows across diverse PV assets. In addition, multiple recent SCADA-
focused frameworks [2] highlight the value of higher-resolution operational indicators
and automated analytics for reducing mean time to detection.

Differences from this project. Many published SCADA-only approaches focus on
plant-level energy balances, generic anomaly scores, or classification of a limited set of

fault modes, often at smaller (behind-the-meter) scales or with instrumentation not
consistently available at utility plants. By contrast, this project targets utility-scale
balance-of-system (BOS) fault modes (e.g., string/combiner/tracker outages) and is
designed to function with the common denominator of utility-plant telemetry. A second
differentiator is explicit use of plant architecture and peer comparisons (e.g., combiner-
to-inverter populations) to localize DC-field abnormalities, rather than relying solely on
global residuals or black-box predictors.

How the literature is leveraged. The project draws on the literature’s consensus that
normalization for weather/operating regime is essential, anomaly persistence logic
reduces false alarms, and interpretability is critical for operational adoption. These
lessons are operationalized via hybrid expected-power modeling, multi-day persistence
requirements, and diagnostic features designed to map alarms to actionable BOS
causes.

Expected power modeling and weather normalization
Similarities to this project. A major trend in PV analytics is improved irradiance and
power modeling using satellite products, nowcasting/forecasting, and machine learning
to reduce reliance on ground sensors and site visits. This aligns directly with the
project’s underperformance and benchmarking workflows, where expected power must
be computed consistently across many plants. The broader forecasting literature [3] and
related hybrid ML forecasting work) reinforces the value of combining physics-based
structure with data-driven residual learning under variable cloud conditions.
Differences from this project. Many publications emphasize prediction (forecasting)
accuracy at short horizons. In contrast, this project requires a long-term “expected
power” model that is used to compare against actual performance for long-horizon KPI
estimation (performance index, degradation, loss disaggregation). Accordingly, model
selection is driven not only by mean error but also by bias, robustness to missing data,
and comparability across sites, seasons, and vintages. Another difference is the explicit
coupling of normalization uncertainty into downstream loss categories (e.g., inverter
downtime uncertainty from missing channels), rather than treating expected power as a
single deterministic baseline.

How the literature is leveraged. The project leverages the rapid maturation of satellite
meteorological products and open-source PV modeling (e.g., pvlib) to standardize
inputs at scale, then evaluates multiple model/data-source combinations to identify
viable substitutes for ground instrumentation. This directly accelerates onboarding
(reduced dependence on site met stations) and improves benchmarking fairness
(consistent weather sources and modeling choices applied across the fleet). The same
body of work informs quality-control filters (e.g., cloud screening) so that model
residuals are more diagnostic of equipment/operations issues than of weather artifacts.

Fleet benchmarking
Similarities to this project. The literature increasingly recognizes that benchmarking
requires harmonized data definitions, rigorous QC, and transparent KPI formulations.
The IEA PVPS Task 13 report on technical KPIs (2024) [4] emphasizes standardization
of definitions and data processing as prerequisites for fair comparisons. In parallel, the
NREL PV Fleet Performance Data Initiative final report (2025) [5] demonstrates the
value of large, aggregated operational datasets for estimating performance loss
distributions and climate-dependent trends. Standards and taxonomy work (e.g., the
Orange Button O&M taxonomy presented by Hansen et al., 2024) [6] likewise targets
interoperability across the PV lifecycle.
Differences from this project. Many benchmarking publications focus on aggregated
energy reporting (monthly/annual) or emphasize statistical summaries without providing
an end-to-end operational pipeline. This project’s benchmarking work (SUPER) is
designed around higher-resolution, inverter-level SCADA data, enabling separation of
downtime, curtailment, clipping, and system-loss components with clearer operational
pathways for mitigation. Additionally, the project couples performance benchmarking
with reliability benchmarking (maintenance-record-based failure rates), which is less
commonly integrated in a single analytics framework in the open literature.

How the literature is leveraged. The project leverages these community efforts by
aligning KPI terminology and data structures with emerging best practices (e.g.,
standardized KPI definitions and O&M taxonomies) so that results are comparable
across organizations and over time. Public fleet initiatives and standards inform the
project’s choices for quality control, missing-data handling, and reporting conventions,
while the project contributes back by demonstrating a practical, scalable
implementation: a consistent pipeline and an anonymized benchmarking interface that
converts research concepts into repeatable operational practice.

 2. Project Objectives
Motivation and Signiﬁcance
As solar penetration increases, ensuring the reliability, availability, and financial
performance of PV assets is critical for grid operators and asset owners as well as the
public benefit of clean, affordable, reliable, and safe energy. Unlike conventional
generation, PV plants are geographically distributed, remotely operated, and composed
of thousands of small, repeating components. This structure creates both a challenge
and an opportunity: faults are often subtle and difficult to detect, yet they frequently
manifest as repeatable patterns in SCADA data.

Multiple industry studies have shown that a significant fraction of lost energy in PV
plants arises from BOS faults—such as blown fuses, disconnected strings, or stuck
trackers—that are often inexpensive to repair once identified. However, traditional
detection approaches rely heavily on manual inspections or aerial infrared scans, which
are costly and infrequent.

Project Goals
This project addressed these challenges by developing automated solutions that:

 1. Detect subtle and major sources of underperformance in near real-time
 2. Reduce false positives relative to existing commercial tools

 3. Minimize manual engineering effort through automated site configuration
 4. Scale from individual plants to fleet-wide benchmarking and reliability analysis

Summary of Key Achievements
The original scope (three-year base period plus one-year no-cost extension) focused on
fault detection and validation. A two-year extension broadened the scope to include
performance and reliability benchmarking through a tool called SUPER (Solar Utilization
Performance and Reliability Benchmarking).

Physics-Based Modeling and S-Metrics
For each modeled combiner, PVLib produces expected Isc and Voc. Measured data
captures Vmp and Imp. From these coordinates, two features are extracted from the IV
curve, as seen in Figure 4. The two blue dotted lines are the S-metrics, which quantify
deviations in current-voltage behavior indicative of faults. A fault causing a reduction in
Vmp and/or Imp will appear as a change in the slope of one or both of the S-curves. Isc
and Voc provide reference points to detect deviations in Vmp/Imp.

Figure 4. Reconstructed low-fidelity I-V curve and S-metric definitions.

Fault Detection Methodology
Faults are detected by comparing distributions of S-metrics at the combiner level
against inverter-level populations. Persistent deviations over multiple days are required
to trigger alarms, improving robustness. To implement the persistence requirement, a
smoothing window was applied which requires a fault to be detected 4 out of 5 days
before triggering an alarm.

Figure 5. Implementation of smoothing window to reduce false alarms

Sensitivity Tuning and ROC Analysis
Detection sensitivity is tunable, allowing operators to trade-off between high detection
rates with more false positives and lower detection rates with fewer false positives. The
receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve shows the true positive rate versus the
false positive rate for a range of sensitivity settings. The user can select which point on
the curve to operate based on their desired sensitivity. A better algorithm will have a
curve closer to the upper left of the plot (high TPR and low FPR).

Figure 6. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for fault detection.

Validation Against Aerial Infrared Inspections
To quantify the accuracy of the developed method, aerial IR scan-detected faults were
used as the ground truth. For each IR scan dataset, SCADA data from the same time
period was used so that the faults would match. Eleven IR-SCADA matched datasets
from nine plants were used as ground truth to measure the accuracy of the algorithm.
Results:

 • Overall average true positive rate > 50% with false positive rate <10%

 • 6 of the 11 validation datasets achieved 50% TPR with 10% FPR.

Figure 7. Detection performance versus IR ground truth.
Detection accuracy is also a function of the size of the fault relative to the size of the
combiner box, since the combiner box current is the primary input to the algorithm. A
combiner box typically has around 20 strings, therefore if one string is completely
faulted, the combiner box would lose 5% of its capacity. To measure the detection
threshold in terms of faulted capacity, intentional faults were introduced by blocking
sunlight from modules by covering them with fabric. The intentional faults ranged in
magnitude from 1 to 6% of a combiner box. In sunny weather (clear sky index above 0.7
on average for the smoothing window of 5 days), the algorithm detected half of the
faults at 3% magnitude and near 80% of the faults at 4% magnitude. In cloudy weather
(clear sky index below 0.7 for the 5-day window), the accuracy is reduced.

Figure 8. Impact of fault magnitude and weather on detection rate.

Underperformance Detection and Expected Power Modeling
 1. Expand Diagnostic Capabilities and Data Multimodality
 2. Multi-modal data approach demonstrated and plan to implement into remote
 operations centers developed

Underperformance detection differs from fault detection in that it measures gradual
reduction of capacity and includes factors that impact an entire plant. Therefore, peer-
to-peer comparison is not used, rather performance over time and versus absolute
reference models are utilized. Underperformance analysis compares actual power
against expected power normalized for weather and design. Expected power models
vary depending on the type of analysis they are used to perform. In benchmarking,
physics-based models are used to generate an ideal expected power that represents
how the plant should operate given its design specifications, as an absolute reference.
This Ideal Expected Power is explained in more depth later in the benchmarking
section. In this section, we evaluate data-driven models to generate expected power
tuned to the as-built plant to detect subtle changes in performance. This type of
expected power is a relative reference, and is used to detect site-wide degradation,
soiling, or irregular performance as the plant capacity reduces over time.

Figure 10. Underperformance detection using expected power and performance index.

Creating an accurate expected-power model is essential. The expected-power model
captures the impacts of weather and normal or predictable plant behavior. Downstream
analysis includes loss identification/disaggregation. A better expected-power model
reduces noise in the performance index (actual divided by expected, as shown in Figure
10) and gives a more accurate assessment of plant health. The major components to
expected power are the model and weather data.

Performance and Reliability Standardization and KPI’s
 1. Analyze Performance Data

The project evolved and broadened from fault detection to general performance and
reliability benchmarking, filling a gap in the industry of quantifying overall PV plant
performance and loss factors.

Why Benchmarking Matters
Industry studies consistently show PV plants underperforming P50 estimates by 5–8%,
yet monthly reporting lacks the granularity to diagnose causes, according to kWh
Analytics [7]. By comparing large, anonymized datasets across diverse locations,
technologies, manufacturers, EPCs, and O&M practices, benchmarking helps answer
fundamental questions such as typical performance levels, the magnitude and drivers of
different loss sources, and common component failure rates and associated costs. This,
in turn, allows stakeholders to quantify the impact of upfront design decisions, OEM and
EPC selection, and site-specific factors such as technology, design, location, age, and
vintage.

Effective benchmarking relies on consistency and transparency, with identical analytical
methods applied to all datasets using inverter-level raw data, standardized quality
control, satellite weather inputs, performance models, and clearly defined KPIs. When
methods, definitions, and formulas are shared openly and paired with collaboration
through user groups, benchmarking enables strong data-driven feedback loops that
inform better upfront design, optimize maintenance strategies, improve prediction
models, and accelerate industry knowledge sharing and improvement.

Loss Categorization
The approach taken in SUPER is to generate an ideal, best-case expected power for
each plant. Then the difference between ideal expected and actual energy is labeled
and quantified into loss categories. The Ideal Expected Energy is the theoretical
maximum. The full glossary of terms, losses, and KPI definitions is posted publicly on
the SUPER website at (https://super.epri.com/public/en/glossary.html).

Ideal Expected Energy: Theoretical energy produced given the plant’s nameplate
rating, design, and weather. Ideal expected energy is adjusted for the actual weather
the plant experienced, including irradiance and temperature. A physics-based model of
the plant is used to calculate expected power using plant specifications such as
clipping/curtailment level, module temperature coefficient, module faciality, crystalline or
thin film, ground coverage ratio, tilt, azimuth, and tracking. A generic inverter loss model
simulates AC losses. No additional losses are included.

Currently, SUPER quantifies losses due to:

Fixed System Loss: Conversion efficiency loss inherent to the plant design and
construction, not as a result of degradation over time. This baseline/constant loss is
measured by average efficiency during the first 1-2 years of operation. Factors that may
contribute to fixed system loss include module nameplate error, module mismatch,
wiring and other ohmic loss, orientation error/uneven terrain, tracking error, self-
shading, etc. Clipping and long-term curtailment (due to a permanent setpoint) are not
counted as fixed system loss, since they “normal” or intended in the design. Clipping
and long-term curtailment are quantified separately.

Variable System Loss: Conversion efficiency losses that accumulate over time while a
plant is in operation. Variable system loss can come from module degradation, soiling,
faults and failures of modules and wiring components, shade from vegetation, tracker
controller drift, etc.

Inverter Downtime: Energy lost due to inverter power outages.

Irregular Performance: Energy lost due to a temporary and severe reduction in power.
Potential causes include snow cover, offline combiners, severe shading, significant
maintenance. Inverter outages and curtailment are quantified separately and not
included in irregular performance.

Short-term Curtailment: Energy lost due to a reduction of capacity by AC setpoint,
lasting hours to days. Times of short-term curtailment are detected by flat peaks in the
AC power at levels lower than the typical setpoint.

Ongoing work is being done to further disaggregate system loss and irregular
performance into stuck tracker loss, tracker error/self shading, inverter temperature
derating, snow, and soiling.

Data requirements
SUPER benchmarking requires a standardized set of time-series data, metadata, and
contextual plant information so that performance and reliability can be evaluated
consistently across owners and sites. Time-series data includes inverter-level power,
energy, and reactive power measurements and tracker position data when available. In
addition to time-series channels, the analysis requires site configuration information
such as inverter and module types, location, DC and AC nameplate per inverter, ground
coverage ratio, and other design details. These inputs are paired with formatting rules,
quality checks, and metadata templates so that datasets can be mapped to a common
naming scheme and processed automatically within the SUPER workflow. Finally,
maintenance records are used for benchmarking component failures.

A data transfer method was designed to minimize manual effort for both participants and
EPRI while maintaining security and repeatability. Under the SUPER approach,
participants can use the SUPERXfer script to gather, format, and transmit data from

reduced data onboarding time from weeks to ~8 hours on average per participant. The

 • Runs in the participant’s own environment to gather, format, and transmit data to
 EPRI.
 • Standardizes channel formatting before transfer to support a consistent

 • Saves the most recent timestamp transferred so the workflow can resume from
 where it left off.
 • Supports flexible execution intervals, allowing automated transfers to run daily,

 • Reduces recurring manual effort by enabling incremental catch-up transfers

Fleet-wide results show:

Actual performance averages ~81–82% of ideal expected energy (defined in the loss
categorization section). Fixed system losses account for roughly 10%, while variable
system losses increase with plant age, and inverter downtime contributes additional loss
with relatively high uncertainty. The remaining “irregular performance” category captures
a mix of effects—such as stuck trackers, shading, inverter temperature derates, soiling,
and snow—that are actively being further quantified and disaggregated.

Figure 17. Energy Loss Waterfall

Plant-to-plant spread is significant across each major loss category. The benchmarking
results highlight both typical values and significant variability across the fleet. Fixed
system losses dominate on average, while inverter downtime, curtailment, irregular
performance, and variable losses show tighter distributions but still meaningful outliers.
This variability underscores the importance of benchmarking to distinguish systematic
trends from site-specific issues. Some of the spread in variable system loss is due to

plant age, where older plants accumulate more loss. For this reason, the performance
loss rate is analyzed using the capacity index (defined next).

Figure 18. Energy Loss by category and by plant

The Ideal Capacity Index is a measure of power generating capability, similar to a
capacity test. Unlike a capacity test, it is calculated daily throughout the plant's life
rather than only once at the beginning. The ideal capacity index is the ratio of actual
energy to ideal expected energy during uninhibited operation of a plant. This metric
captures the state of health of the plant when it is operating regularly, without downtime,
short-term curtailment, irregular performance, clipping, or long-term curtailment.
Capacity index differs from system loss in that system loss measures the overall energy
impact of the system efficiency and is dependent on the DC/AC ratio. Times of clipping
and long-term curtailment are removed from capacity index calculations but not system
loss.

Across the fleet, capacity index declines gradually with age, reflecting the combined
effects of module degradation and accumulating system-level issues. Significant
plant-to-plant scatter remains, indicating differences in design quality, operating

conditions, and maintenance effectiveness.

Figure 19. Plant capacity versus age
The fleet-wide performance loss rate averages −0.7% per year, with both the mean and
median aligned at this value. The interquartile range spans approximately −0.3% to
−1.0% per year, capturing variability due to technology, environment, and operational
practices. These results are broadly consistent with industry expectations but highlight a
non-trivial population of faster-degrading plants.

Figure 20. Distribution of performance loss rate

Estimated inverter downtime varies depending on how missing data are interpreted,
ranging from 1.1% (best case) to 3.1% (worst case), with a middle-case estimate near
2.2%. When restricting analysis to plants with low uncertainty (±1% or less), average
energy loss due to inverter downtime is approximately 1.7% of ideal energy. Remaining
uncertainty is primarily driven by the lack of plant-level power or energy data needed to
fully separate communication outages from true power outages.

Figure 21. Inverter downtime uncertainty range

Tracker availability is quantified by examining tracker position tags and determining
whether a tracker is moving or stuck in one position throughout the day. Therefore, an
unavailable tracker is one that is not moving. Preliminary results indicate median tracker
availability of roughly 90–95% across the analyzed plants. Compared to inverter
downtime, tracker availability exhibits a wider uncertainty band, driven largely by
missing or incomplete tracker position data. Data collection is ongoing, and availability

estimates are expected to tighten as more sites and higher-quality signals are

incorporated.

Figure 22. Tracker availability and uncertainty range

Losses can be disaggregated by inverter, module, and tracker model, enabling direct
comparison of performance and reliability across equipment types. Clear differences
emerge among models, with some inverter populations exhibiting systematically higher
downtime than the fleet average. This capability supports data-driven feedback to
owners, OEMs, and EPCs on technology selection and long-term reliability risk.

Component failure rates were calculated using a large, multi-owner dataset of over
125,000 maintenance records from 57 utility-scale PV plants. The data was
systematically categorized using a large language model (LLM). Maintenance records
were standardized by component and sub-component and classified by action type, with
the analysis intentionally focused on corrective events (replace/repair) to enable
consistent measurement of component reliability. After excluding scheduled and
preventative maintenance, protection actions, and extreme weather-related events, the
final dataset comprised approximately 12,000 failure events, providing a robust
foundation for technology- and component-level reliability benchmarking. Failure rates
were calculated by component, OEM, and vintage.

Failure rates were weighted by cost and grouped by plant age. Then the total costs
were compared against a literature estimate of corrective maintenance. The literature
estimate was found by averaging estimates from the DOE Solar Photovoltaic System
Cost Benchmarks (https://www.energy.gov/cmei/systems/solar-photovoltaic-system-

(subscription required: https://power-and-

combination of predictions and survey results of O&M providers.

For full inverter replacements, the SUPER average is much lower than industry
estimates, while for the rest of the system, the SUPER estimate is in line with literature
estimates. In the SUPER database, only around 3% of inverters undergo a full
replacement by year 10. Just over 1% are replaced by year 5 and 2% by year 7. This is
much lower than the replacement rate for the typically assumed 10-15 year inverter
lifetime. The feedback we received from industry is that inverters are most often
repaired piecewise rather than full replacements. Reasons for a full replacement are a
catastrophic fire caused by a subcomponent or external failure.
Additional potential reasons for SUPER estimates being low overall are that SUPER
plants are relatively young, whereas literature estimates give lifetime cost averages. In
addition, SUPER costs do not have a labor component yet.

 4. Signiﬁcant Accomplishments and Conclusions
This award delivered a practical, scalable pathway for turning utility-scale PV SCADA
and maintenance records into actionable insights on fault detection, underperformance,
and fleet benchmarking. The work is significant because it reduces reliance on
infrequent, labor-intensive inspections; improves the timeliness and consistency of
diagnostics across large portfolios; and establishes common KPI definitions and
processing methods needed for industry-wide comparability.

Signiﬁcant accomplishments
 • Validated BOS fault detection from standard SCADA signals. Demonstrated
 that string/combiner/tracker fault modes can be detected with operationally useful
 true-positive rates while maintaining low false positives, enabling earlier
 intervention and reduced recoverable energy loss.
 • Automated site setup and configuration. Reduced plant onboarding and
 engineering effort by inferring repeated hardware configurations (e.g., strings per
 combiner) from data, which is essential for scaling analytics across
 heterogeneous fleets.
 • Benchmarked against third-party tools and inspection ground truth. Direct
 comparison to aerial infrared inspections and commercial APR (advanced pattern
 recognition) software clarified where the new methods add incremental value.
 • Established an end-to-end benchmarking pipeline (SUPER). Implemented
 standardized QC, expected-power normalization, loss categorization, and KPI
 computation across many plants, enabling “apples-to-apples” comparisons.
 • Produced fleet-level performance and reliability insights. Quantified
 distributions of performance loss rate, downtime, and component failure rates
 using multi-owner datasets—information that is difficult to obtain from single-site
 studies yet critical for planning, financing, and O&M strategy.

Challenges encountered and lessons learned
 • Data completeness and semantic consistency are the main scaling
 bottlenecks. A lesson learned is to invest early in metadata validation and
 automated QC, otherwise analysis time is dominated by integration issues.
 • Normalization uncertainty propagates into KPI uncertainty. Lesson:
 benchmarking should report uncertainty bounds (especially for downtime and

 curtailment) when communications outages cannot be cleanly separated from
 power outages.
 • Fleet benchmarking requires standard definitions. Lesson: publishing KPI
 definitions and loss taxonomies is as important as model development because
 comparability is foundational to learning across owners and technologies.

Pivots and changes in scope
The project scope expanded from fault detection and validation to include performance
and reliability benchmarking via the SUPER platform. This pivot was significant because
early results showed that many owners need not just alarms for discrete faults, but also
standardized, finance-relevant KPIs that quantify “how a plant is doing” over months to
years; The largest practical barrier to broad impact was not a single detection model,
but a scalable pipeline that harmonizes data ingestion, quality control, and consistent
performance normalization across many sites.

Conclusions
Overall, the award demonstrated that scalable, data-driven PV performance
management is achievable using the signals already available at most utility plants,
provided that strong QC, standardized definitions, and uncertainty-aware benchmarking
are built into the workflow. The delivered methods and platform infrastructure enable
continuous identification of recoverable losses, clearer attribution of underperformance
drivers, and data-driven feedback on equipment and O&M practices—while also
clarifying the remaining industry needs around missing signals, consistent metadata,
and more granular loss disaggregation.

 5. Path Forward
Moving forward, our objective is to expand SUPER’s commercial deployment while
continuing to enhance its capabilities. At present, SUPER is supported by 11 funders
and comprises 9 GW of data. Enhancements to SUPER include:

 - Further disaggregation of losses including stuck trackers, soiling, snow,
 and inverter temperature derates.
 - API access to the results.
 - Incorporation and analysis of serial defects and extreme weather events.
