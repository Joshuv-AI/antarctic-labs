Loop expansion in polymer field theory: application to phase separation
Kiyoharu Kawana, Kyosuke Adachi

Abstract
Liquid-liquid phase separation underlies phenomena ranging from protein condensate formation to the phase coexistence of synthetic polymers. Although the random phase approximation (RPA) is widely used to predict such phase behavior, its quantitative accuracy for binodals of polymer solutions, particularly outside the high-density regime, remains incompletely characterized. Here, we develop a field theoretic loop expansion in homopolymer systems by identifying the inverse polymer density ρ − 1 \rho^{-1} as the Planck constant ℏ \hbar in quantum field theory. We calculate the leading-order and next-to-leading-order corrections to the RPA free energy, denoted as RPA+ and RPA++, respectively. Testing the binodal predicted by the RPA+ against molecular dynamics simulations of bead-spring chains with Gaussian pair interactions, we find that the RPA+ qualitatively improves the dilute-phase coexistence density over the RPA, while the critical point error remains comparable to that of the RPA. Our results establish the loop expansion as a systematic route for refining the RPA-based binodal predictions for polymer phase separation.



I
Introduction

Liquid–liquid phase separation of disordered protein polymers has been recognized as a fundamental principle of intracellular organization, underlying the formation of membraneless compartments such as nucleoli and stress granules
6
;
2
;
3
;
8
.
Quantitative prediction of phase behavior observed in experiments

;
7

14
;
30
.
Apart from molecular dynamics simulations of such models, field-theoretic approaches have been employed for numerical sampling of equilibrium states
16
;
15
;
25
, as well as for analytical calculation of binodals with low computational cost
17
;
21
.

The random phase approximation (RPA), obtained by the Gaussian (i.e.
1
-loop) approximation for fluctuations around the mean-field saddle point within the field-theoretic approach
12
;
17
, has been a powerful analytical tool to predict polymer phase separation.
The RPA has been extensively applied to polyelectrolyte solutions
5
;
23
;
29
;
9
and solutions of polyampholytes or charged disordered proteins
21
;
20
;
25
.
Refinements of the RPA, such as renormalization of the Kuhn length
19
, have also been proposed to improve the prediction accuracy.

Despite such developments, the RPA-based methods for predicting phase behavior have been applied mainly to systems with long-range electrostatic interactions, while short-range repulsive and attractive interactions are often incorporated at the mean-field level
21
;
34
.
Though the RPA should be accurate at high polymer densities even for systems with short-range interactions
11
;
17
, it is unclear how well it can predict the binodal curve, which is determined by the free energy in a wide range of densities.
To develop an analytical framework applicable to both long-range and short-range interactions, and to systematically improve the accuracy of the RPA, it is natural to go beyond the Gaussian approximation and consider higher-order corrections
26
;
27
.

In quantum field theory, the loop expansion corresponds to an expansion in powers of the Planck constant
\hbar
, which clearly indicates that the limit
\hbar\rightarrow 0
corresponds to the classical (or saddle-point) limit.
This naturally raises the question: What is the expansion parameter in the field-theoretic approach in polymer systems?
In the following, we explicitly show that the inverse of the polymer density
\rho^{-1}
plays the role of
\hbar
.
This correspondence implies that the RPA is increasingly accurate in the high density limit
\rho\rightarrow\infty
, as anticipated from the previous studies
11
;
17
.

Once we understand the correspondence
\hbar~\leftrightarrow~\rho^{-1}
, it is clear how the RPA can be systematically improved: the loop expansion leads to the higher-order corrections to thermodynamic quantities in powers of
\rho^{-1}
.
In this paper, we identify the diagrams corresponding to the leading-order (LO) and next-leading-order (NLO) corrections, and evaluate these diagrams explicitly.
For comparison with the conventional RPA, we refer to the LO and NLO analyses as RPA+ and RPA++, respectively.
We then test the binodals predicted by the RPA+ against molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of homopolymer systems with short-range interactions.
We find that the RPA+ qualitatively improves the RPA prediction of the dilute-phase density, bringing it to the same order of magnitude as observed in simulations.
On the other hand, the prediction near the critical point is not improved by the RPA+, indicating the need for incorporating alternative methods, such as renormalization-group method, that can effectively resum higher-loop corrections.
Taken together, our results establish the loop expansion as a systematic route to refine RPA-based binodal predictions for polymer phase separation.

Section
VI

II
Single polymer system

In the field theory approach for equilibrium polymers, the information of monomer correlation functions in a single polymer
35
is important.
Let us summarize the fundamental results in a single polymer system.
In the following, we denote a point in
D
-dimensional space and a momentum vector simply by
x=(x^{1},\cdots,x^{D})
and
p=(p_{1},\cdots,p_{D})
, respectively.

The single-polymer partition function is defined by

\displaystyle Q[\phi]\coloneqq\frac{1}{V}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}\int d^{D}x_{i}\right)\exp\left(-\beta\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}u(r_{l})+\sum_{l=1}^{N}\phi(x_{l})\right)~,

(1)

where
\beta=(k_{B}T)^{-1}
is the inverse temperature,
N
is the total number of monomers, i.e. degree of polymerization,
r_{l}=x_{l+1}-x_{l}
,
u(x)
is a general interaction between adjacent monomers, and
-\phi(x)
is an external potential.
Throughout this paper, we choose the Gaussian chain

\displaystyle\beta u(x)=\frac{Dx^{2}}{2b^{2}}~,

(2)

where
b
is a bond length, as often used in literature
11
;
17
.

By introducing the monomer density

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{S}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta^{(D)}(x-x_{i})=\int\frac{d^{D}k}{(2\pi)^{D}}e^{ik\cdot(x-x_{i})}~,

(3)

we can also express the external potential term as

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi(x_{i})=\int d^{D}x~\hat{\rho}_{S}(x)\phi(x)~.

(4)

Here, the subscript
S
means “single” polymer.
Then, we can introduce the
n
-point density correlation functions as

\displaystyle F^{(n)}_{\rm free}(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{Q[\phi]}\frac{\delta^{n}Q[\phi]}{\delta\phi(X_{1})\cdots\delta\phi(X_{n})}\bigg|_{\phi=0}

(5)

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{\rho}_{S}(X_{1})\hat{\rho}_{S}(X_{2})\cdots\hat{\rho}_{S}(X_{n})\rangle_{\rm free}~,

(6)

where
\langle\cdots\rangle_{\rm free}
means the expectation value in the free single-polymer system.
By using Eq. (
3
), this becomes

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{N}\cdots\sum_{i_{n}=1}^{N}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\int\frac{d^{D}k_{j}}{(2\pi)^{D}}\right)\left\langle e^{i\sum_{j=1}^{n}k_{j}\cdot(X_{j}-x_{i_{j}})}\right\rangle_{\rm free}~.

(7)

The corresponding Fourier mode is

\displaystyle\tilde{F}_{\rm free}^{(n)}(k_{1},\cdots,k_{n})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\int d^{D}X_{j}e^{-ik_{j}\cdot X_{j}}\right)F^{(n)}_{\rm free}(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{N}\cdots\sum_{i_{n}=1}^{N}\left\langle e^{-i\sum_{j=1}^{n}k_{j}\cdot x_{i_{j}}}\right\rangle_{\rm free}~.

(8)

After a bit calculation (Appendix
A
), we obtain the following expression:

\displaystyle\tilde{F}_{\rm free}^{(n)}(k_{1},\cdots,k_{n})

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{D}\delta^{(D)}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}k_{j}\right)\frac{1}{V}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{i_{1}=0}^{N-1}

\displaystyle\cdots\sum_{i_{n}=0}^{N-1}\exp\left(-\frac{b^{2}}{2D}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{n}k_{j}\cdot k_{j^{\prime}}\mathrm{min}(i_{j},i_{j^{\prime}})\right)~,

(9)

where we have replaced
i_{j}-1\rightarrow i_{j}
.
Note that the delta function reduces to

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{D}\delta^{(D)}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}k_{j}\right)\quad\rightarrow\quad V\times\delta_{\sum_{j=1}^{N}k_{j},0}~.

(10)

in a finite volume system.

A graphical representation of the connected correlation function
G^{(n)}_{\rm free}
.

We can also introduce the connected density correlation functions and their Fourier modes by taking functional derivatives of
\log Q[\phi]
:

\displaystyle G^{(n)}_{\rm free}(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\delta^{n}\log Q[\phi]}{\delta\phi(X_{1})\cdots\delta\phi(X_{n})}\bigg|_{\phi=0}~,

(11)

\displaystyle\tilde{G}_{\rm free}^{(n)}(k_{1},\cdots,k_{n})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\prod_{j=1}^{N}\int d^{D}X_{j}e^{-ik_{j}\cdot X_{j}}\right)G^{(n)}_{\rm free}(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})~.

(12)

Factoring out the momentum-conservation factor,
(2\pi)^{D}\delta^{(D)}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}k_{j}\right)/V
, we express these Fourier modes as

\displaystyle M\tilde{F}_{\rm free}^{(n)}(k_{1},\cdots,k_{n})\equiv\rho(2\pi)^{D}\delta^{(D)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}\right)\tilde{f}_{\rm free}^{(n)}(k_{1},\cdots,k_{n})~,

(13)

\displaystyle M\tilde{G}_{\rm free}^{(n)}(k_{1},\cdots,k_{n})\equiv\rho(2\pi)^{D}\delta^{(D)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}\right)\tilde{g}_{\rm free}^{(n)}(k_{1},\cdots,k_{n})~,

(14)

where
M
denotes the total number of polymers and
\rho=M/V
is the polymer density.
In particular, one can check that the zero mode is

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{\rm free}^{(n)}(0,\cdots,0)=N^{n}~

(15)

by the definition (
9
).
In this paper, we represent the connected correlation function
G^{(n)}_{\rm free}
graphically as Fig.
1
.

Since these correlation functions are defined by the same functional
Q[\phi]
, there exist rigorous relations among them

, which enable us to express the connected correlation functions
\{G_{\rm free}^{(n)}\}
by the non-connected ones
\{F_{\rm free}^{(n)}\}
.
In Appendix
B
, we summarize these relations for up to
n=4
.

III
Equilibrium polymers

Let us consider the equilibrium system of homopolymers.
A Hamiltonian of
M
polymers is introduced by

\displaystyle H_{M}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{p_{\alpha i}^{2}}{2m}+V_{M}(\{x_{\alpha i}\})~,

(16)

where
\alpha,\beta,\cdots~(i,j,\cdots)
are the indices of polymers (monomers).
A polymer potential is

\displaystyle V_{M}(\{x_{\alpha i}\})=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}u(r_{\alpha i})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{(\alpha,i)\neq(\beta,j)}v(x_{\alpha i}-x_{\beta j})~,

(17)

where
r_{\alpha i}\coloneqq x_{\alpha i+1}-x_{\alpha i}
and
\sum_{(\alpha,i)\neq(\beta,j)}
denotes the sum over all different monomers.
Note that the effects of the solvent are supposed to be included in the two-body potential
v(x)
effectively.
For the following discussion, we also introduce the total monomer density function

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{\alpha=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta^{(D)}(x-x_{\alpha i})~.

(18)

without any subscript.

III.1
Polymer field theory

Below, we explain the field theoretic approach in the equilibrium polymer system
16
;
17
;
15
.
We assume
v(0)<\infty
in the following.

The canonical partition function is defined by

\displaystyle Z\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{(2\pi mk_{B}T)^{\frac{D}{2}MN}}{M!}\left(\prod_{(\alpha,i)}\int d^{D}x_{\alpha i}\right)\exp\left(-\beta V_{M}(\{x_{\alpha i}\})\right)

(19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{z^{M}}{M!}\left(\prod_{(\alpha,i)}\int_{V}d^{D}x_{\alpha i}\right)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\beta\sum_{\alpha=1}^{M}\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}u(r_{\alpha l})-\frac{\beta}{2}\int d^{D}x\int d^{D}y\hat{\rho}(x)v(x-y)\hat{\rho}(y)\right)~,

(20)

where
z=e^{\frac{\beta}{2}Nv(0)}(2\pi mk_{B}T)^{\frac{D}{2}N}
.
In the following, we neglect the factor
e^{\frac{\beta}{2}Nv(0)}
in
z
by shifting the origin of energy per monomer.
Substituting the functional identity

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\int{\cal D}\rho\delta(\rho(x)-\hat{\rho}(x))

\displaystyle={\cal N}\int{\cal D}\rho\int{\cal D}\phi~e^{i\int d^{D}x(\rho(x)-\hat{\rho}(x))\phi(x)}~,

(21)

we obtain

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=Z_{\rm ideal}\int{\cal D}\rho\int{\cal D}\phi~e^{-S[\rho,\phi]}~,

(22)

\displaystyle S[\rho,\phi]

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta}{2}\int d^{D}x\int d^{D}y\rho(x)v(x-y)\rho(y)

\displaystyle\quad-i\int d^{D}x\rho(x)\phi(x)-M\log Q[-i\phi]~,

(23)

where

\displaystyle Z_{\rm ideal}=\frac{(2\pi mk_{B}T)^{\frac{D}{2}MN}}{M!}V^{M}~

(24)

corresponds to the partition function of ideal gas, and
Q[-i\phi]
is the single-polymer partition function (
1
) with an imaginary external potential.
Note that the free-energy density of ideal polymers is

\displaystyle f_{\rm ideal}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-T\frac{\log Z_{\rm ideal}}{V}=T\rho\log\left(\frac{\rho}{(2\pi mk_{B}T)^{\frac{D}{2}N}}\right)~.

(25)

We assume that the Fourier mode
\tilde{v}(k)
of
v(x)
is greater than zero for
{}^{\forall}k
.
Then we can perform the path-integral of
\rho(x)
as

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=Z_{\rm ideal}\int{\cal D}\phi e^{-S_{\phi}}~,

(26)

\displaystyle S_{\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\beta}\int d^{D}x\int d^{D}y\phi(x)v^{-1}(x-y)\phi(y)-M\log Q[-i\phi]~,

(27)

where
v^{-1}(x)
is the inverse function of
v(x)
:

\displaystyle\int d^{D}yv(x-y)v^{-1}(y-z)=\delta^{(D)}(x-z)~.

(28)

Equation (
26
) allows us to perform the saddle point approximation for
M\rightarrow\infty
.
The saddle point of
\phi(x)
is determined by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\int d^{D}yv^{-1}(x-y)\phi(y)=

\displaystyle-i\frac{M}{Q[\phi]}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}\int d^{D}x_{i}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta^{(D)}(x-x_{i})\right)\exp\left(-\beta\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}u(r_{l})-i\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi(x_{i})\right)~.

(29)

In particular, for
\phi(x)=\phi=constant
, this becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{v}(0)^{-1}}{\beta}\phi=-i\frac{MN}{V}\quad\therefore\quad\phi=\phi_{s}=-i\beta\tilde{v}(0)N\rho~,

(30)

where
\tilde{v}(0)=\int d^{D}xv(x)
.

Introducing a fluctuation by

\displaystyle\phi(x)=\phi_{s}+\chi(x)~,

(31)

the partition function (
26
) is now written as

\displaystyle Z=

\displaystyle Z_{\rm ideal}e^{VN^{2}\beta\frac{\tilde{v}(0)}{2}\rho^{2}+M\log Q[i\phi_{s}]}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\int{\cal D}\chi~\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{2}\int d^{D}x\int d^{D}y\chi(x)\Gamma^{(2)}(x-y)\chi(y)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=3}^{\infty}\frac{(-i)^{k}}{k!}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\int d^{D}x_{i}\right)MG_{\rm free}^{(k)}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{k})\chi(x_{1})\cdots\chi(x_{k})\bigg)~,

(32)

where

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(2)}(x)=k_{B}Tv^{-1}(x)+MG^{(2)}_{\rm free}(x)~

(33)

corresponds to the kinetic term for
\chi(x)
.
Note that we can put the zero mode of
\chi(x)
zero since it only provides an overall factor in
\langle e^{-i\int d^{D}x\hat{\rho}_{S}(x)\chi(x)}\rangle_{\rm free}
.
Equation (
32
) is the polymer field theory we utilize in the following discussion.
It is also straightforward to obtain similar field theories for more general polymer systems, such as heteropolymers
21
;
25
;
34
.

Let us here summarize relevant thermodynamic quantities.
The Helmholtz free energy is

\displaystyle F=Vf=-\beta^{-1}\log Z~,

(34)

by which we can calculate the chemical potential as

\displaystyle\mu=\frac{\partial F}{\partial M}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\rho}~.

(35)

The pressure and isothermal compressibility are given by

\displaystyle p=-\frac{\partial F}{\partial V}=-f+\rho\mu~,\quad\kappa_{T}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k_{B}T}\frac{\partial p}{\partial\rho}=\frac{\rho}{k_{B}T}\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial\rho}=\frac{\rho}{k_{B}T}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\rho^{2}}~.

(36)

The critical point
(\rho,T)=(\rho_{c},T_{c})
is determined by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p}{\partial\rho}=\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial\rho^{2}}=0\quad\leftrightarrow\quad\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial\rho}=\frac{\partial^{2}\mu}{\partial\rho^{2}}=0~.

(37)

For
T<T_{c}
, the system exhibits a first-order phase transition, which implies that there exists two (or multiple) densities that predict the same pressure
p(\rho_{l})=p(\rho_{h})
and chemical potential
\mu(\rho_{l})=\mu(\rho_{h})
.
Hence, a binodal curve is determined by the simultaneous solution of

\displaystyle p(\rho_{l})=p(\rho_{h})~,\quad\mu(\rho_{l})=\mu(\rho_{h})~

(38)

for a given temperature
T

9
;
22
.

III.2
Random phase approximation

The RPA corresponds to a
1
-loop approximation in field theory language.
Namely, the
1
-loop free energy is

\displaystyle f_{\rm RPA}

\displaystyle=f_{\rm ideal}+\frac{N^{2}\tilde{v}(0)}{2}\rho^{2}+\frac{k_{B}T}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\log\Gamma^{(2)}\right)

(39)

\displaystyle=f_{\rm ideal}+\frac{N^{2}\tilde{v}(0)}{2}\rho^{2}+\frac{k_{B}T}{2}\int\frac{d^{D}k}{(2\pi)^{D}}\log\left(1+\beta\rho\tilde{v}(k)\tilde{g}_{\rm free}^{(2)}(k)\right)~,

(40)

where we have omitted a constant term.
By taking the
\rho
derivative, the chemical potential is

\displaystyle\mu_{\rm RPA}=

\displaystyle k_{B}T\log\left(\frac{e\rho}{(2\pi mk_{B}T)^{\frac{DN}{2}}}\right)+N^{2}\tilde{v}(0)\rho

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d^{D}k}{(2\pi)^{D}}\frac{\tilde{v}(k)\tilde{g}_{\rm free}^{(2)}(k)}{1+\beta\rho\tilde{v}(k)\tilde{g}_{\rm free}^{(2)}(k)}~,

(41)

and the pressure is given by
p_{\rm RPA}=-f_{\rm RPA}+\rho\mu_{\rm RPA}
.

Here, we should comment on the convergence of these integrations.
For a short-range potential such as a Gaussian potential
\tilde{v}(k)\propto e^{-a^{2}k^{2}}
, these integrations are strongly convergent and there is no need for the renormalization.
On the other hand, for a long-range potential such as
\tilde{v}(k)\propto k^{-2}
, these integrations are divergent for
D\geq 2
, and such UV divergences must be subtracted (or renormalized) to obtain finite results.
Such a subtraction, however, does not change thermodynamic properties of the system.

IV
Beyond random phase approximation

IV.1
Loop expansion

Here, we clarify that the expansion in powers of
\rho^{-1}
corresponds to loop expansion, i.e., it corresponds to
\hbar
in quantum field theory.
To this end, we express the action of
\chi(x)
by the Fourier modes as

\displaystyle S[\chi]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d^{D}k}{(2\pi)^{D}}\tilde{\chi}(k)^{*}\tilde{\Gamma}^{(2)}(k)\tilde{\chi}(k)~

\displaystyle-\rho

\displaystyle\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}\frac{(-i)^{n}}{n!}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{d^{D}k_{i}}{(2\pi)^{D}}\tilde{\chi}(k_{i})\right)\tilde{g}^{(n)}_{\rm free}(\{k_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})(2\pi)^{D}\delta^{(D)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}\right)~,

(42)

where we have used Eq. (
14
).
From this expression, one can obtain the propagator as

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{\chi}(k)^{*}\tilde{\chi}(q)\rangle=\frac{(2\pi)^{D}\delta^{(D)}(k-q)}{\tilde{\Gamma}^{(2)}(k)}=\frac{(2\pi)^{D}\delta^{(D)}(k-q)}{k_{B}T\tilde{v}(k)^{-1}+\rho\tilde{g}_{\rm free}^{(2)}(k)}~,

(43)

and see that each vertex is proportional to
\rho
.
By these results, we can examine how each Feynman diagram contributes to the free energy.
Let us focus on the
n
th-order vertex
\rho\tilde{g}_{\rm free}^{(n)}
and denote the number of vertices (propagators) by
V_{n}~(\mathrm{P})
.
Since each propagator is constructed from
2
legs, we have a relation

\displaystyle nV_{n}=2\mathrm{P}~.

(44)

Then, the
\rho
dependence is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\rho^{V_{n}}}{(\tilde{\Gamma}^{(2)})^{\mathrm{P}}}\sim\rho^{V_{n}-P}=\rho^{-\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)V_{n}}\quad\text{for }\rho\rightarrow\infty~,

(45)

which clearly indicates that higher-loop diagrams are suppressed by a power of
\rho^{-1}
.
In addition, one can also check that the exponent in Eq. (
45
) is the same as the number of loop integrations: let us denote the number of loop integrations by
L
.
Since there are effectively
(n-1)
momentum integrations for each vertex and each propagator preserves the momentum [see Eq. (
43
)], we have

\displaystyle L=(n-1)V_{n}-\mathrm{P}+1=1+\left(\frac{n}{2}-1\right)V_{n}~,

(46)

where
+1
in the first equality represents the total momentum conservation, and we have used Eq. (
44
).
Now Eq. (
45
) is written as
\rho^{-(L-1)}
which clearly indicates that
\rho^{-1}
corresponds to
\hbar
in quantum field theory.

When a diagram contains multiple types of vertices, Eq. (
45
) is generalized to

\displaystyle\rho^{-\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}(\frac{n}{2}-1)V_{n}}~.

(47)

In particular, the LO corrections correspond to

\displaystyle(n,V_{n})=(4,1)~,\quad(3,2)~,

(48)

and the NLO corrections are

\displaystyle(n,V_{n})=

\displaystyle(6,1)~,\quad(4,2)~,\quad(3,4)~,

\displaystyle(3,2)+(4,1)~,\quad\text{and}\quad(3,1)+(5,1)~.

(49)

In general, the problem is equivalent to counting the number of partitions of
2(L-1)
in the form

\displaystyle\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}(n-2)V_{n}=2(L-1)~.

(50)

Below, we explicitly calculate the LO and NLO corrections.

Leading order corrections (RPA+)

Comparison with molecular dynamics simulations

V.1
Model and simulation protocol

In the following, we consider three dimensional systems (
D=3
).
To test the predictions of the RPA and RPA+ against simulations, we performed MD simulations of a simple homopolymer solution model with implicit solvents.
Each polymer consists of
N=30
monomers connected by harmonic bonds with spring constant
3k_{B}T/b^{2}
.
Non-bonded interactions between monomers are given by a superposition of Gaussian pair potentials of short-range repulsion and attraction:

v(r)=u_{1}e^{-r^{2}/(4{a_{1}}^{2})}-u_{2}e^{-r^{2}/(4{a_{2}}^{2})},

(54)

where
a_{i}>0
and
u_{i}>0
(
i=1,2
) represent the interaction range and strength, respectively.
In the following, we use reduced units (
b=k_{B}T=m=1
, where
m
is the mass of a monomer) and fix
a_{1}=1
,
a_{2}=2
, and
u_{2}/u_{1}=0.1
.
We vary
u_{1}
as the effective inverse temperature of the system to examine the phase diagram in the
\rho
–
u_{1}
plane.
Note that, with these parameter sets, the Fourier transformation
\tilde{v}(k)
is positive for any
k
, particularly
\tilde{v}(0)>0
, which satisfies a condition for thermodynamic stability

;
15
.

To sample equilibrium configurations, we used Langevin dynamics simulations.
We set friction constant
\gamma=1
and timestep
\Delta t=0.01
.
All the simulations were performed with HOOMD-blue
1
.
As often used for studying phase separation in polymer models
4
;
10
;
33
;
18
;
13
, we applied a slab geometry with simulation box of aspect ratio
L_{x}:L_{y}:L_{z}=5:1:1
under periodic boundary conditions, containing
M=400
polymers.
We set the polymer density as
\rho b^{3}=2\times 10^{-3}
.
For each value of
u_{1}/k_{B}T\in\{0.16,0.18,\ldots,0.28,0.30\}
, we first relaxed the system through a short volume ramp and then recorded totally
200
snapshots every
10^{6}
steps.
We discarded the first
20
snapshots for equilibration and used the remaining
180
as equilibrium configurations.

\rho_{h}

was obtained by averaging over the outermost (i.e., farthest from the center-of-mass)
6

We further took time average over the
180

In Figs.

4

and

The bottom panels [Figs.

(55)

with

V.3
Comparison of binodal curves

Figure
5
summarizes the comparison between the theoretical and simulated binodals.
As seen from the logarithmic-scale plot [Fig.
5
(a)], the binodal predicted by the RPA (black dotted line, Sec.
III.2
), reaches over the low-density region
\rho<10^{-5}
and differs by an order of magnitude from the dilute-phase density observed in MD simulations (red circles).
In contrast, the prediction of the binodal based on the RPA+ (blue solid line, Sec.
IV.2
) is closer to the observation and reasonably in the same order of magnitude.
This indicates that the lowest-order correction to the RPA qualitatively improves the prediction of thermodynamic properties.

As seen from the linear-scale plot [Fig.
5
(b)], prediction of the binodal near the critical point is not improved significantly by the RPA+, compared to the RPA.
This suggests that higher-order corrections are increasingly important near the critical point, and we need other techniques such as renormalization group to incorporate critical fluctuations and precisely estimate the critical point.

VI
Conclusion and discussion

In this paper, we developed a field-theoretic loop expansion of the free energy for polymer systems and clarified that it amounts to a systematic expansion in powers of
\rho^{-1}
, with the inverse polymer density
\rho^{-1}
playing the role of the Planck constant
\hbar
in quantum field theory.
We identified the leading-order (RPA+) and next-to-leading-order (RPA++) corrections and evaluated the RPA+ binodal for homopolymer solutions with short-range Gaussian interactions.
Compared with MD simulations, the RPA+ qualitatively improved the dilute-phase density predicted from the RPA by bringing it to the same order of magnitude as observed in simulations.
On the other hand, the critical point was not substantially improved, suggesting the need for more sophisticated methods to account for the critical fluctuations.

A further comment is in order before concluding this paper.
For a purely repulsive case, i.e.,
u_{2}=0
in Eq. (
54
), the homopolymer solution is well-mixed and should not exhibit phase separation, but the RPA predicts a spurious binodal with a critical point (
u_{1c}\approx 0.09
for
N=30
).
The leading-order correction (RPA+) does not cure this issue and still predicts a binodal line.
This indicates that, while systematic, the
\rho^{-1}
expansion is not always qualitatively correct over the interaction parameter range.
It is therefore an important and interesting challenge to devise a method for systematically improving the
\rho^{-1}
expansion so as to recover qualitatively correct predictions in such polymer systems.
Besides, extending the present framework to heteropolymer phase separation relevant to biomolecular condensates is also an interesting direction for future work.

Appendix A
Monomer correlation functions

The exponent in Eq. (
8
) can be written as

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}k_{j}\cdot x_{i_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}k_{j}\cdot\left(\sum_{l=1}^{i_{j}-1}r_{l}+x_{1}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}k_{j}\cdot x_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{i_{j}-1}k_{j}\cdot r_{l}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}k_{j}\cdot x_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}k_{j}\cdot r_{l}\theta(i_{j}-1-l)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}k_{j}\cdot x_{1}+\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}K_{l}\cdot r_{l}

(57)

where

\displaystyle K_{l}(\{i_{j}\})\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{n}k_{j}\theta(i_{j}-1-l)~.

(58)

Thus, each
r_{l}
integration leads to

\displaystyle{\cal N}\int d^{D}r_{l}e^{-\frac{D}{2b^{2}}r_{l}^{2}-iK_{l}(\{i_{j}\})\cdot r_{l}}=e^{-\frac{b^{2}}{2D}K_{l}(\{i_{j}\})^{2}}~.

(59)

Namely, we obtain

\displaystyle F^{(n)}_{\rm free}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{N}\cdots\sum_{i_{n}=1}^{N}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\int\frac{d^{D}k_{j}}{(2\pi)^{D}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left(i\sum_{j=1}^{n}k_{j}\cdot x_{j}-\frac{b^{2}}{2D}\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}K_{l}(\{i_{j}\})^{2}\right)(2\pi)^{D}\delta^{(D)}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}k_{j}\right)~,

(60)

where
(2\pi)^{D}\delta^{(D)}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}k_{j}\right)
comes from the translation invariance.
By definition,

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}K_{l}(\{i_{j}\})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{n}k_{j}\cdot k_{j^{\prime}}\theta(i_{j}-1-l)\theta(i_{j^{\prime}}-1-l)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{n}k_{j}\cdot k_{j^{\prime}}\mathrm{min}(i_{j}-1,i_{j^{\prime}}-1)~,

(61)

which leads to Eq. (
