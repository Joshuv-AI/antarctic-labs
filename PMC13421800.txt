Systematic dissemination of study findings from local to national stakeholders: a case of a social network-based study on HIV self-testing and linkage among fishermen in Kenya
Authors: Benard O Ayieko, Edwin D Charlebois, Lila A Sheira, Sarah A Gutin, Phoebe Olugo, Jayne Lewis-Kulzer, Zachary A Kwena, Harsha Thirumurthy, Kawango Agot, Carol S Camlin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDisseminating research findings to potential beneficiaries such as study participants, community members, and stakeholders is a critical component of participatory research. While there is general agreement that dissemination is critical, documented examples of comprehensive dissemination activities are few in the literature. We present a concrete example of a multi-level stakeholder dissemination process from a successful HIV self-testing and linkage to treatment and prevention intervention, describe the dissemination framework used to guide activities, document the process required to carry out the dissemination, and provide qualitative observations and feedback from the process. Findings are from direct engagement in discussions with participants, community members, sub-County and County health managers, and the National AIDS & STI Coordinating Program (NASCOP) leadership during the community engagement and dissemination process. Using Stakeholder Theory and Diffusion of Innovations Theory as guides, Stakekeepers (research institutions, local Institutional Review Boards, the National Sexually Transmitted Infection & HIV Control Program), Stakewatchers (County and sub-County Health Management Teams who act as proxies or intermediaries who protect the interests of real stakeholders) and Stakeholders (such as participants and health facilities, i.e.: Beach Management Units, community members and study participants) felt that the engagement and dissemination process improved upon dissemination of research findings to key stakeholders over usual dissemination efforts. The process of engaging real Stakeholders, Stakewatchers and Stakekeepers brings on board both service recipients, policymakers, and implementers of public health interventions and maintained supportive community relationships and positive views of engagement in research. We provide a concrete example, and lessons learned from a successful multi-level research results dissemination process that can serve as a model for future dissemination efforts. The trial was registered at Clinical Trials.gov on February 26, 2021 (registration #NCT04772469).KEYWORDS: Dissemination, social networks, HIV self-testing, fishermen, Kenya

Introduction

Disseminating research findings to potential beneficiaries such as study participants, community members, and stakeholders is a critical component of community-based participatory research (Israel et al., 2019; Leung, 2004; McDavitt et al., 2016). Even when stakeholders are involved in collective partnerships to generate insights about research findings, opportunities to develop programmes and interventions that are responsive to specific cultural determinants of health may be missed (McDavitt et al., 2016). Policy decision-making and effective public health interventions are often based on research findings. As such, a lack of or poor dissemination of research findings can directly compromise the direction of critical policy decisions or public health programmes (Bassler et al., 2016).

Face-to-face dissemination of research findings with stakeholders is rated by researchers as impactful on practice and policy, yet it only constitutes 40% of all the dissemination outlets, e.g. publication in scientific journals, academic conferences, press releases, etc., thereby limiting access to information for persons not able to access alternative dissemination channels (Knoepke, Ingle, Matlock, Brownson, & Glasgow, 2019; Shato et al., 2024). Addressing both individual and modifiable barriers to access and use of research findings through dissemination better aligns the efforts of researchers with those of the beneficiaries, such as practitioners and government (Shato et al., 2024). In addition to the ethical responsibility that researchers have to study participants, the dissemination of research findings to local communities also strengthens the researchers’ long-term engagement with communities for subsequent intervention projects. Based on these benefits that accrue from adequate dissemination of research findings, it is critical for the processes to be embedded as an integral part of research project planning and development (Ondenge et al., 2015). While there is general agreement on these principles as goals, documented examples of comprehensive dissemination activities enacting these ideals are few in the literature. We therefore present a concrete example of a novel multi-level stakeholder dissemination process from a successful HIV self-testing and linkage to treatment and prevention intervention in a global health setting (C. S. Camlin, 2023), describe the dissemination framework used to guide the activities, document the process required to carry out the dissemination, and provide qualitative observations and feedback from the process.

Research context

The Owete study (NCT#04772469) was a randomised control trial to test an HIV self-testing and linkage to treatment and prevention intervention among mobile fishermen in a high-priority HIV setting in western Kenya, which was implemented between July 2020 and March 2023. The intervention strategy was to recruit and train highly socially connected men (named ‘promoters’ by the study) to distribute HIV self-tests and provide linkage support to men in their close social networks. In intervention clusters, promoters were given oral fluid-based HIV self-tests to offer to their network members as well as monetary vouchers that incentivized the network members to seek clinic-based confirmatory testing after self-testing. In control clusters, promoters were asked to distribute referral vouchers within their network that encouraged clinic-based HIV testing or free HIV self-test pickup at those clinics. The study found that this approach significantly increased men's HIV testing and linkage to local clinics within three months of testing (C. Camlin et al., n.d.). However, although we hypothesised that the social network approach would also increase ART and PrEP use, the study did not see an immediate impact on ART uptake or PrEP (Sheira et al., 2025). The study used longitudinal qualitative and quantitative assessments to identify the pathways of intervention action and understand how a social network-based approach with support for clinic linkage affected HIV testing, ART and PrEP uptake, and retention among men. Dissemination of research findings was an important and pre-specified part of the research plan.

Development of the dissemination plan

To develop the dissemination plan, we used a combination of Stakeholder Theory (Donaldson & Preston, 1995) and Diffusion of Innovations Theory (Turner, 2007) to guide the identification of key stakeholders and communication channels in the dissemination process. Stakeholder Theory explains that entities such as research enterprises need to identify different categories of stakeholders (defined as any individual, group, or organisation that can influence an organisation) and consider their interests. Stakeholder Theory asserts that stakeholders (who are divided into real Stakeholders, Stakewatchers and Stakekeepers) will support research enterprises if they receive or are assured of value in return. The real Stakeholders (in our setting, participants and health facilities) have an actual stake in a research enterprise and possess a legitimate claim, power, and influence, and the research has a responsibility and moral obligation towards them. The Stakewatchers (county and sub-county health officials and national AIDS control programmes), on the other hand, may not truly have a stake in the research themselves but act as proxies or intermediaries who protect the interests of real stakeholders. The Stakekeepers (IRBs, the press and the media) are independent regulators who have no stake in the research enterprise but exert some external controls and regulations. The research enterprise has no responsibility, power, or moral obligation to attend to the well-being of the Stakewatchers and Stakekeepers, who hold power and external controls over the research.

Diffusion of Innovations Theory seeks to explain how new ideas, practices, or products spread within a social system (such as a country) over time (Turner, 2007). The theory identifies five key elements: the innovation itself, communication channels through which information about the innovation is shared, time it takes for adoption to occur, and the social system where the innovation is introduced (Turner, 2007). Individuals within the system adopt innovations at different rates influenced by factors such as the innovation's relative advantage, compatibility with existing values, complexity, trialability, and observability. The structure of the Owete intervention, through the utilisation of social network central promoters, created a community of early adopters. In our qualitative analyses, we found that Promoters did act as early adopters, showing high levels of engagement and enthusiasm for the study (Okore et al., 2024) and testing for HIV before asking other men in their networks to use the tests, thereby acting as role models for other men (Olugo et al., 2024). We used Stakeholder Theory and Diffusion of Innovations Theory to choose and organise multiple communication channels in providing study results to study participant promoters who were early adopters and proponents of the intervention. In addition, using theory to guide the incorporation of detailed dissemination plans and approaches in our research plans prior to the research start further facilitated dissemination.

Methods

Target levels selected were the key or real Stakeholders (study participants, health facility staff), the Stakewatchers (County health management team, sub-County health management team, and National AIDS and STI Control Program (NASCOP)), and the Stakekeepers (IRBs, the press, and media) (see Figure 1).

Stakeholders theory applied in the dissemination plan.

In the description that follows, findings are from direct engagement in discussions with participants, community members, sub-County and County health managers, and the National AIDS & STI Coordinating Program (NASCOP) leadership during the community engagement and dissemination process. The findings were from participant-generated feedback and meeting notes and minutes. Analysis was done qualitatively at synthesis meetings held after the dissemination process and was structured and informed by Stakeholder Theory.

Results

Phase-1 pre-intervention activities

One critical feature of our dissemination plan was the development of a strong bi-directional relationship with the community, organisational entities, and policy makers well in advance of the dissemination activities (Table 1). A large group of stakeholders was needed because each group played unique and non-overlapping roles. The research team held meetings (study introduction, study activity planning, study initiation, monthly monitoring implementation) with the beach/community leadership to discuss community health challenges, overall makeup, organisation, and functioning of the community. These relationships helped to inform the design and implementation of the research, and the pre-existing relationships provided an established channel for subsequent dissemination activities.

Community entry process

After the study was approved by the research funding institution IRB (UCSF IRB:19-28205) and local IRB (KEMRI IRB: SERU/SSC/677), we sought approval from the National Commission for Science, Technology and Innovation (NACOSTI/540927). After the three approvals, we sought the Kenya Ministry of Health County Health Management Team and the sub-County Health Management Team approvals before we went down to the Beach Management Units and health facilities to introduce the study and get their buy-in.

Beach management unit (BMU) and community support engagement

Beach Management Units are legally recognised community organisations (under governance within the Kenya Ministry of Fisheries) that bring together community members (e.g. fishers, fish traders, boat owners and other beach stakeholders) who traditionally depend on fisheries activities for their livelihoods. The Beach Management Units co-manage the fisheries resources within their area of jurisdiction with the government and other stakeholders. We had a two-hour initial introductory meeting with BMU officials from each of the three beaches, where we obtained information regarding the best time to meet with different kinds of fishermen; i.e. nocturnal versus daytime fishermen. We were informed that the best time to find the ‘Omena’ (Sardines) fishermen was in the morning immediately after they dock at the shore at around 6–8 am and before they go back to the lake at around 3 pm. However, those specialising in fishing ‘Mbuta’ (Nile perch) and ‘Ngege’ (Tilapia) could be found at about mid-day since they go fishing early in the morning. The Beach Management Units were very accommodating and supportive, and after the entry process, the Beach Management Units referred us to one of their reliable peer educators to help with study mobilisation and tracing of fishermen. They also made sure our Research Assistants had private spaces for participant interviews. These various meetings and consultations took approximately 18 h (see Table 1).

Ministry of health engagement

The County Health Management Team (CHMT) allowed us to work within the county and after the community entry meeting, the team designated the County AIDS/STI Coordinator to introduce us to the Sub-County Health Management Teams and asked them to support our work within MOH facilities within the sub-County (Table 2). After the meeting with the sub-County Health Management Team (SCHMT), the team assigned the sub-County AIDS/STI Coordinator and the sub-County Health Records Information Officer to introduce us to the facilities working within the three study beach communities. The sub-County Health Records Information Officer and the Facility Health Records Information Officer supported our data manager in getting information on the participants who linked to the facility for services after self-testing for HIV within the community. Due to the significant support we received from the community and other stakeholders during the study implementation, we had broader than usual community engagement. These various meetings and consultations took approximately six hours (see Table 1).

Phase-2 study interim progress update process

In August 2022, we had a three-hour meeting per beach that reached 146 study participants (Beach 1 – 22, Beach 2 – 50 and Beach 3 – 74 participants), and 90 Beach Management Unit officials/stakeholders at the three beaches within the beaches’ community halls, and in December 2022, we had three-hour meetings with both the county and sub-county health management teams at the Sub-County and County offices to update them on the study progress and the preliminary study findings and study progress at the 3-month follow-up visit. In both cases, the Beach Management Units and Health Management Team officials supported community mobilisation and provided community halls for the meetings. At the County Health Management Team and Sub-County Health Management Team mid-term progress update meetings, the sub-County and the County Laboratory managers re-committed to work closely with our Laboratory Technologist. They also asked other Laboratory staff at the study facilities where viral load sample collection for the study viral load testing was being done to continue supporting the study for the remaining study period. The sub-County and County Health Records Information Officers also indicated that they would continue to support our Data Manager in working closely with the facility's Health Records Information Officers in abstracting linked study participants’ data from the facility’s Electronic Medical Record System. The feedback received from the interim progress update with the Sub-County Health Management Team and the County Health Management Team helped us revise our medical data abstraction standard operating procedures to reflect how we would continue with the abstraction from the facility's Electronic Medical Record System as opposed to hard copy forms (we initially planned to flag the files for our study participants to help the health care workers identify the study participants). Thus, the interim progress update helped maintain engagement and relationships with stakewatchers. These various meetings and consultations took approximately 24 h (see Table 1).

Phase-3 the dissemination process

Community and county dissemination planning meetings

In February 2024, we carried out the dissemination planning meetings. We started by separately meeting the Beach Management Unit officials from the three study beach communities and the County Director of Health and informed them about the anticipated study dissemination process. These officials helped set up venues and dates for meeting their members and the study participants within the three beach communities. We also involved the Beach Management Unit officials and other beach community members to provide venues, chairs, and snacks/meals as part of community empowerment during the dissemination exercise.

BMU and county dissemination meetings

We met with a total of 384 participants and 39 Beach Management Unit officials; all the meetings were conducted in the Luo language by the study site investigator and the study coordinator. Once again, Beach Management Unit officials supported with community mobilisation and provided community halls for the meetings. We also met with 15 health managers at the sub-County, including the sub-County health departmental heads, i.e. Sub-County AIDS and STI Coordinator, Medical Laboratory Coordinator, Health Promotion Coordinator, and Public Health Nurse, among others. At the County level, we had a total of 22 heads of departments, including the County Director of Medical Services, AIDS & STI Coordinator, Health Promotion Coordinator, Public Health Officer, Medical Laboratory Coordinator, Nursing Officer, Pharmacist and others in attendance. During the meetings, there were questions from the County Health Management Team members, such as: what were the main factors for the low PrEP uptake seen, whether the promoters created an impact, and whether participants appreciated the study, the benefit of the study to the community, the method of disposal of the used HIV self-testing kits, reported prior use of HIV self-testing, and sustainability of the monetised voucher (incentives) to enhance testing. Community and policymakers were concerned about sustainability and scale-up during the dissemination process. These various meetings and consultations took approximately 15 h (see Table 1).

Recommendations from the participants, beach management unit officials, sub-county health management team and county health management team

During the dissemination meetings with the study participants and the Beach Management Units, the stakeholders at the community level explained that they were very happy that we came back to them with the study findings and feedback, unlike previously when studies would be conducted in the community and the researchers would just disappear, never to be seen. They indicated that this is the right way to do it, and it would make communities embrace research. During the Sub-County Health Management Team dissemination meetings, the stake-watchers indicated that it was important for studies to disseminate study findings the way we did, so that the findings can be used in improving services to the community, sub-county and the county level. The County Health Management Team’s participants reiterated the importance of sharing research findings with the community and the participants who contributed to the findings; this was important because it improves policy development and increases the use of evidence-based interventions and programmes to improve public health. This is unlike previously when they would just hear of, or read about, the findings from media and publications, yet the studies were done within their communities. They observed that many researchers fail to return to the communities to inform them about the research findings; therefore, they miss out on future community support for further research and programmes. Additionally, questions were raised around the applicability of the study approach to newer HIV prevention and treatment options such as long-acting PrEP and long-acting injectable ART treatment. Furthermore, county health management officials questioned whether monetised vouchers to enhance HIV testing were sustainable and whether the Promoter strategy had created an impact in the community. The local stakeholders asked the researchers to share the study findings with higher levels of health administration, where decisions are made. This was seen as a way of increasing the chances of the gaps identified being addressed to the benefit of the community.

National dissemination process

National dissemination planning meetings

We had a planning meeting with the National AIDS & STI Coordinating Program (NASCOP) leadership to hold a national dissemination meeting in March 2024. The National HIV/AIDS Coordinator assigned the Research Manager at NASCOP to help us plan for the national dissemination meeting. NASCOP further provided the conference hall for the dissemination meetings and helped mobilise other national interest groups for the dissemination meetings.

The national dissemination meeting

A total of 27 people attended the meeting in-person and six virtually. Members present included high-level policymakers such as National AIDS & STI Coordinating Program Managers, representatives of the Deputy Director of Medical Services office, the Technical Advisor for Care and Treatment, representatives of the Monitoring & Evaluation Office and Nairobi County Mental Health Office, the Head of National HIV Reference Laboratory office, Heads of organisations dealing with adolescents and young people, representatives from United States Agency for International Development, and the Clinton Health Access Initiative. These various meetings and consultations took approximately 8 h (see Table 1).

Plenary feedback after the dissemination

The National AIDS & STI Coordinating Program leadership and other national and international partners underscored the importance of sharing findings from studies conducted locally at the national level, rather than seeing the results disseminated only at international conferences before they are informed. The NASCOP leadership committed to inviting the researchers to make the presentation to the larger HIV testing services/PrEP technical working group at their next meeting for consideration during policy revisions, which were underway. Additionally, the NASCOP leaders expressed interest in considering some of the findings, particularly the low level of PrEP adherence, to be included as areas to be assessed in the Kenya National Population-based HIV Impact Assessment Cycle 2. The process of integrating study findings into national guidelines and the Kenya National Population-based HIV Impact Assessment is ongoing. National partners at the meeting also stressed the need to use a more objective measure for assessing ART adherence, as opposed to self-report, in future research.

Discussion

We describe a novel multi-level comprehensive, theory-informed, research dissemination process for a successful global health intervention. A large group of stakeholders was needed in this context, but they each played unique roles and did not duplicate functions or responsibilities. The observations and feedback we received from all dissemination levels indicated that the various stakeholders felt the process improved upon the dissemination of research findings to key stakeholders over the usual dissemination of research findings to stakeholders, stake-keepers, and stake-watchers. This approach engaged sectors with a critical stake in the arenas of policy making and implementation of public health interventions and maintained supportive community relationships and positive views of engagement in research. Throughout, recommendations were addressed to hone the research process and fostered bi-directional dialogue. The success of the process relied on multiple researcher-community and researcher-public health/government relationships that were developed over the course of the research. This structured dissemination process is also the first step in developing a tool that could be used by other projects to engage similarly with stakeholders (such as participants and health facilities), stake-keepers (research institutions, local Institutional Review Boards, and national policy bodies), and stake-watchers (health officials who act as proxies or intermediaries who protect the interests of real stakeholders).

Taken together, we provide a concrete example and lessons-learned from a successful multi-level research results dissemination process that can serve as a model for future dissemination efforts. While the process described may be most applicable to Southern and Eastern Africa, we believe the model used here is applicable and can be adapted to various settings. Key findings from the evaluation of the dissemination process point to the benefits of a theory-guided approach, the added value of preparatory planning and engagement, interim stakeholder engagement activities, and a broad multi-level results dissemination that includes sustainability discussions.
