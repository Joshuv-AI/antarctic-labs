3. Executive Summary:

Plastic waste is a rapidly growing challenge in the United States. In 2017, more than 35
million tons of plastics were generated in the municipal solid waste stream, yet only a
small fraction was recycled while most was landfilled. Modern material recovery facilities
(MRFs) use near-infrared (NIR) optical sorters to recover only a narrow slice of this
stream—mainly clear and light-blue #1 Polyethylene Terephthalate (PET) and natural #2
High-Density Polyethylene (HDPE) bottles. The remaining plastics, including colored PET
and #3–#7 mixed plastics, are typically treated as residue and discarded. This project
was motivated by the opportunity to turn that overlooked #3–#7 fraction into a valuable
feedstock for fuels and chemical products, rather than a long-term burden on landfills.

The goal of the project was to advance plastic sorting beyond today’s single-sensor
systems by combining multiple sensors with artificial intelligence (AI). The team designed
and built a multi-sensor platform using visible cameras, x-ray fluorescence (XRF), NIR,
and mid-infrared (MIR) sensors to capture a detailed “fingerprint” of each plastic item,
including both polymer type and inorganic contaminants. This work advances
understanding of mixed-plastic characterization and sorting by showing how multi-sensor
fingerprints and AI can distinguish complex, colored, multilayer, and additive-rich
materials that confuse current equipment. Using these data, new AI-based classification
models were developed to group plastics into more precise, conversion-ready categories.
These sorted fractions were then tested in catalytic pyrolysis experiments to understand
how different compositions and contamination levels affect yields and product quality,
while technoeconomic analysis (TEA) and life cycle assessment (LCA) were used to
evaluate cost, energy use, and environmental impacts.

Plastic waste, particularly polypropylene (PP) and polystyrene (PS), can be transformed
into high-value products through thermal and catalytic processes. Calcium oxide and
silica–alumina catalysts enabled efficient conversion, achieving >90 wt.% liquid yields for
virgin PS and >80 wt.% for waste PS, with styrene as the dominant product. PP pyrolysis
produced gasoline and diesel fractions meeting performance standards, while linear low-
density polyethylene (LLDPE) required catalysts for >80 wt.% oil yield. Contaminants
such as PET caused wax formation and degraded fuel quality. Additional pathways to
produce chemicals as envisioned in Budget Period 3 (BP3) were explored and yielded
lubricant-like oils, light-colored waxes, and aromatics-rich products. Hydrocracking and
hydrogenation upgraded heavy residues of PS pyrolyzate into lighter aromatics or
naphthenic base oils, supporting circularity and sustainability.

The project significantly improved understanding of how difficult-to-recycle #3–#7 plastics
can be characterized, sorted, and upgraded for advanced recycling. The project also
aimed at decontamination of the #3 plastic polyvinyl chloride (PVC) as it is a contaminant
for most of the downstream recycling processes. The multi-sensor fingerprints provided
much richer information than conventional NIR alone, and the deep-learning models were
able to classify these complex materials into novel fractions that are better aligned with
downstream conversion needs. The process was demonstrated on realistic mixed-plastic
streams at increasing scales. TEA and LCA results demonstrate that the proposed
approach is technically effective and economically feasible, indicating that an AI-enabled,

 3

multi-sensor sorting system for residual plastics can be integrated with existing MRFs,
achieve high material purity and decontamination, and operate at projected costs on the
order of tens of dollars per ton of sorted material.

By providing a way to convert previously discarded #3–#7 plastics into consistent, higher-
value feedstocks, this project offers clear benefits to the public. Diverting these materials
from landfills can reduce waste volumes, lower emissions compared with disposal or the
use of all-virgin plastics, and support domestic production of fuels and chemical products
from waste resources. The tools developed—multi-sensor hardware, AI-based
classification methods, and TEA/LCA data—give MRF operators, technology developers,
and policymakers practical options for designing the next generation of plastic recycling
infrastructure. In doing so, the project helps move the U.S. toward a more resource-
efficient plastics economy.

6. Project Objectives:

This project directly supports national goals for clean energy, and economic growth by turning
mixed #3–#7 waste plastics—today largely landfilled—into a consistent, low-cost feedstock
for advanced upcycling to chemicals and other higher-value products. Current U.S. recycling
infrastructure is optimized for clear #1 PET and natural #2 HDPE, while colored PET and #3–
#7 plastics are typically rejected as residue. These residues represent both an environmental
liability (e.g., landfill volume) and a lost economic opportunity. By creating a pathway to sort
and upgrade this material at a target cost of less than $30 per ton, the project supports DOE
and BETO goals of enabling a plastics upcycling economy, reducing dependence on virgin
fossil feedstocks, and expanding domestic clean-manufacturing supply chains.

The overarching project goal was to develop and validate an integrated system that: (1) uses
a multi-sensor analytical apparatus (visible imaging, XRF, NIR, MIR) to generate rich
“fingerprints” for individual plastic items in the #3–#7 stream; (2) applies deep learning and
sensor fusion to define and sort into novel, conversion-relevant fractions; (3) converts these
fractions via catalytic pyrolysis to valuable products; and (4) TEA and LCA to confirm that
high-purity, decontaminated feedstocks can be produced at less than $30 per ton. During the
project, and in response to emerging lifecycle insights and DOE/NLR (National Laboratory of
the Rockies) guidance, the conversion focus was shifted from primarily transportation fuels
(gasoline/diesel) toward higher-value, lower-carbon chemical products such as soft paraffin
wax and other specialty fractions. Expected outcomes included: an analytical multi-sensor
prototype, validated AI models achieving ≥95% classification accuracy and sub-50-
millisecond decision times, demonstration of a 20 kg/h conversion process, and a final
TEA/LCA showing the technical and economic viability of integrating this technology into
existing MRFs and downstream chemical-recycling facilities.
 9

The significance and innovation of this work lie in several fundamental advances over the
current state of practice. Technically, the project moves beyond single-sensor NIR sorting by
combining multiple spectroscopic and imaging modalities into a single apparatus and fusing
them into a multi-dimensional fingerprint for each object. This enables classification not only
by polymer type but also by inorganic contaminants and additives, which are critical for
conversion performance and product quality. On the algorithm side, the project advances
deep learning for waste plastics by designing specialized data structures, testing multiple
neural network architectures, and then optimizing a selected model to achieve both very high
accuracy (>95%) and high speed (<50 ms) suitable for industrial throughput. On the systems
side, the project couples these sorting capabilities to catalytic pyrolysis and distillation, and
then to TEA/LCA, generating the first integrated dataset that connects mixed-plastic
composition, sorting performance, conversion yields, product slates (fuels, chemical
intermediates, waxes), and full cost/impact metrics. Together, these advances support the
claimed impacts: diverting plastics from landfill and creating economically competitive
domestic feedstocks for chemical and materials industries.
The technical work was organized across three budget periods with clearly defined tasks,
milestones, and go/no-go decision points.
Budget Period 1 (BP1) established the baseline and analysis framework (Task 1). Three
bales of #3–#7 non-recyclable plastics were procured and characterized by imaging, size,
weight, and composition to define an internal baseline (Subtask 1.1), with Milestone 1.1.1
documenting percent composition, size, weight, and piece counts by class. A preliminary
TEA/LCA (Subtask 1.2) defined sorting cost estimates, system boundaries, and analytical
scope, culminating in Milestone 1.2.1. The first DOE verification and Go/No-Go at Month 3
confirmed that the baseline, scope, and team capacity were suitable to proceed.
Budget Period 2 (BP2) focused on building and validating the core technology: the multi-
sensor apparatus, the deep learning framework, small-batch product development, and
supporting TEA/LCA.
• Task 2 designed, built, and calibrated the multi-sensor hardware, including sensor layout
(Subtask 2.1, Milestone 2.1.1), software architecture (Subtask 2.2, Milestone 2.2.1), full
assembly and control of the apparatus (Subtask 2.3, Milestone 2.3.1), calibration of each
sensor using polymer standards (Subtask 2.4, Milestone 2.4.1: >98% accuracy on
standards), and signal-to-noise optimization for #3–#7 plastics (Subtask 2.5, Milestone
2.5.1).
• Task 3 developed the deep learning pipeline: a multi-dimensional data structure for sensor
fusion (Subtask 3.1, Milestone 3.1.1), evaluation of multiple neural network models (Subtask
3.2, Milestone 3.2.1), and model validation with detailed performance statistics and down-
selection (Subtask 3.3, Milestone 3.3.1).
• Task 4 performed catalytic pyrolysis process development on sorted fractions, including
feedstock characterization (Subtask 4.1, Milestone 4.1.1), catalyst screening and small-batch
runs (Subtask 4.2, Milestone 4.2.1), distillation to fuel- and chemical-range fractions (Subtask

• Task 10 covered project management and reporting (Subtask 10.1, Milestone 10.1.1),
including regular coordination with DOE and verification of progress at each stage.
The end-of-project goal—to produce at least one #3–#7 fraction at 95–99% purity, achieve
80–95% decontamination, and demonstrate a sorting cost pathway to <$30/ton—directly
supports U.S. national goals by enabling low-cost, high-quality feedstocks for clean energy
and chemicals production. By transforming “residual” mixed plastics into reliable inputs for
advanced recycling to fuels, chemical intermediates, and waxes, the project reduces landfill
burden and lifecycle emissions, improves resource efficiency, and opens new domestic
markets and jobs tied to next-generation recycling infrastructure.

7. Project Results and Discussion:

Task 1 – To Create and Identify an Internal Baseline and TEA/LCA Analysis
Task Description: To create and identify the internal baseline by procuring three bales
of #3–#7 non-recyclable plastics.

This task supports establishing the baseline composition, size/weight distribution, and
preliminary TEA/LCA against which all improvements in purity, decontamination, and cost/ton
are measured.

Subtask 1.1 – Baseline Quantification

Three bales of #3–#7 plastics were quantified using image, size, and weight data to create
the internal baseline. From these bales, a minimum of 200 pounds of each major plastic
type was separated. The components of each bale were photographed, and each piece
was measured for size and weight to calculate the size and weight distributions of all
components in the bale.

Milestone 1.1.1
The internal baseline is defined by percent composition with metrics for size, weight and
total number of pieces of each class.

UHV first secured a large, representative feedstock of nominal “#3–#7” mixed plastics from
the Solid Waste Authority of Palm Beach County (SWA). An initial truckload of 34 bales
totaling ~20.8 tons (41,600 lb), (Fig 1) was received at the Fort Wayne facility during Q1,
following some delay associated with COVID-related shutdowns at SWA’s pre-sorting
operations.

 12

 Figure 1: Thirty-four mixed plastic bales at UHV facility in Fort Wayne, Indiana

 Figure 2: Hand sorting of different types of plastics for baseline creation at UHV

A subset of these bales was selected for detailed baseline characterization. For the
baseline, three bales were opened and manually sorted (Fig 2 and Fig 3) by reading the
resin identification code on each container and assigning each item to one of eleven
categories: PET(1), HDPE(2), PVC(3), LDPE(4), PP(5), PS(6), OTHER(7), Unknown, With
residue, Metal, and Mixed labels.

Each piece was photographed, weighed, and its approximate size documented. This
 13

produced a dataset of approximately 60,000 images across the eleven categories, along
with mass and count data for each class in each bale.

The resulting hand-sorting data were used to compute, for each bale:
• total mass of each resin category,
• weight fraction of each category (% by weight), and
• the number of pieces per category.

 Figure 3 Plastics sorted into gaylord boxes
These metrics were compiled in tables and bar charts for each bale summarizing both
absolute and relative composition.

UHV also began defining sub-classes within the main resin categories (e.g., brand-
specific PET bottles such as Coke, Diet Coke, Pepsi, Mountain Dew), based on color,
label, and shape. Over 600 PET (Type 1) subclasses and dozens of subclasses for PVC
and PP were manually created. These subclasses were intended for later use in training
and validating deep-learning models and also helped quantify visual heterogeneity within
the baseline stream.

In addition to the first three bales, a second batch of bales was procured after SWA
implemented improved pre-processing to reduce #1–2 contamination in #3–7 bales. One
of these “improved” bales was hand-sorted to check the effect of SWA’s process changes
and to understand variability in incoming composition.

The baseline analysis showed that, despite being marketed as #3–#7 bales, the incoming
material still contained significant fractions of PET(1) and HDPE(2). Early results indicated
roughly ~50% #1–#2 content by weight in some of the initial bales, and follow-up sorting
of “improved” bales confirmed that while SWA’s pre-sorting improved, substantial #1–2
material remained. This finding was communicated back to SWA, who used it to further
refine their pre-processing methods.

• a complete breakdown of mass and weight fraction by resin category (Tables 1–3),

• a qualitative description of the types of containers and contaminants present (e.g.,

Task 1 Technical Discussion

Task 1 established both the physical and analytical foundations for the rest of the project.
The hand-sorted baseline bales showed that nominal “#3–#7” MRF bales contain a
complex mix of resins, with unexpectedly high fractions of PET(1) and HDPE(2) and
significant contamination from labels, residues, metals, and unknown items. This
confirmed that any advanced sorting solution must be designed for a truly heterogeneous,
imperfect feedstock, not a clean laboratory mixture, and it justified the project’s emphasis
on purity and decontamination metrics rather than simple classification accuracy.

At the same time, the preliminary TEA/LCA work translated the baseline into cost and
environmental context, identifying key drivers such as input composition, co-location,
throughput, and energy use. It showed that the target of <$30/ton sorting cost is
challenging but plausible under favorable siting and utilization, and that CO2-eq
performance is sensitive to design choices across the sorting–conversion chain.

Together, the internal baseline and TEA/LCA framework from Task 1 became the
reference point for later verification at M3 and M21 and directly informed subsequent work
in Tasks 2–3 (sensor/AI design), Tasks 6–8 (process development and conversion), and
Task 9 (final TEA/LCA).

Subtask 2.1 – Design of Sensor Layout

This subtask aimed to develop a design of the hardware and sensor layout required before
building the multi-sensor sorter for characterization of plastics. The geometric alignment
for each source and sensor with respect to the polymer plastics and the conveyor belt
was determined to arrive at the mechanical design for the sensor layout. A laser beam
was used to track the location, time, and length of each piece on the conveyor belt. The
laser beam position, size, and frequency were designed so that these waste-stream
materials could be detected independently of the visible camera, XRF, and NIR detectors.
In this way, detection and classification were decoupled, allowing for a more robust
method for data gathering. The visible camera size and location were optimized to collect
an appropriate number of pixels for each piece by controlling the field of view: too few
pixels led to reduced classification accuracy, while too many increased processing time.
For the XRF spectrometer, the X-ray beam spot size was optimized for objects in the

 19

waste stream; the beam size was adjusted between approximately 0.125 and 4 square
inches to increase fluorescence intensity and improve signal-to-noise ratio without
saturating the detector. Similarly, the light source and detector for the NIR cameras were
designed in three dimensions to match the average size of pieces in the waste stream.
The resulting sensor layout is a three-dimensional configuration of sources and detectors
that was specifically designed to optimize signal-to-noise performance for key
components within the waste stream.

 Fig 8: Layout of the sensors in the multi-sensor sorter installed at INL

The goal of Subtask 2.1 is to define the three-dimensional arrangement of sensors and
sources around the conveyor such that individual plastic items can be detected and
characterized with sufficient spatial and spectral resolution while maintaining high
throughput. The baseline composition and size distributions developed in Task 1 were
used to determine the typical dimensions of bottles and containers and the range of object
densities expected on the belt. Based on these data, the team selected a wider conveyor
configuration than the one used in UHV’s metal sorter in order to accommodate bulky
plastic containers and maintain single-layer coverage. Fig 8 shows the sensor layout on
the multi-sensor sorter. The final concept is a 3.5 ft wide belt on a 40 ft long conveyor,
which provides enough length for staged sensing, actuation, and safe access, while
allowing belt speeds compatible with commercial sorting.

 20

The layout design process began with defining the primary sensing zones for the visible
camera, XRF head, and NIR/MIR system, and the placement of a laser-based detection
line used to track object presence and position independent of the imaging sensors. The
laser sensor and an encoder/inductive sensor pair are used to measure belt speed and
trigger image acquisition and other sensor readings at precisely controlled intervals. The
laser position and beam footprint were selected so that even small items in the #3–#7
stream reliably break the beam, ensuring that detection and classification are decoupled
and that each object is observed over a known travel distance and time.

 Fig 9: Design of the multi-sensor sorter installed at INL

For the visible camera, the field of view, working distance, and mounting height were
selected to ensure that typical containers occupy an adequate number of pixels in each
frame to enable robust image-based classification, while avoiding excessive resolution that
would increase data rates and processing time. The XRF sensor layout considered the
trade-off between spot size and count rate: smaller spots increase spatial resolution but
reduce signal, whereas larger spots improve count statistics but reduce resolution and
increase risk of overlapping objects. The design therefore targets an intermediate spot size
suitable for typical bottle-wall thicknesses and uses mechanical alignment and shielding to
minimize stray scatter. Similarly, the NIR optics and illumination geometry were defined so
that the average container footprint passes through a region of uniform illumination and
spectrometer acceptance, enabling reliable measurement of polymer signatures.

Subtask 2.2 – Design of Software Architecture

 23

The design goal for the software architecture was to create a framework for real-time
collection, processing, and analysis of signals from multiple spectrometers. Of critical
importance was the ability to implement modular sections of code so that different
algorithms could be integrated, tested, and removed very easily and quickly. Special focus
was placed on memory management for data entering the stack, use of a model–view–
controller design pattern, and disciplined object creation and destruction, as well as
“recipes” to manage spectrometer optimization and error handling for system
troubleshooting. Different combinations of libraries were evaluated and implemented for
sensor control, hardware input/output (I/O), and machine learning, with the understanding
that machine-learning libraries would need to be upgraded over time as deep learning
methods evolved. A software flowchart was created to describe how the graphical user
interface, control algorithms, and libraries were connected to each other within the overall
architecture.

• Milestone 2.2.1

The software architecture is documented including; the software flowchart, the graphical
user interface, the list of required libraries which enables creating at least one stream of
70% purity with 70% efficiency for decontamination.

Subtask 2.2 established the software architecture needed to operate the multi-sensor
hardware, manage data collection, and provide an extensible interface for integrating
different AI models over the life of the project. The software architecture flowchart has
been added as Appendix A.

The design followed a modular, layered approach:

1. Device control and acquisition layer – encapsulates sensor-specific communication
(camera, XRF, NIR, MIR, encoder, laser sensor, pneumatic valves) using dedicated
drivers and I/O libraries. Each device runs in its own acquisition thread to allow concurrent
operation and minimize blocking.
2. Synchronization and data-fusion layer – aligns data streams using time stamps and
belt position. Laser triggers and encoder counts define “object windows” on the belt; all
sensor data falling within a window are aggregated into a single multi-sensor record for
that object.
3. Data management and storage layer – writes multi-sensor records to disk using a
standardized file structure and metadata convention (object ID, bale ID, run conditions,
labels, operating recipe, etc.). This layer was designed to be compatible with the multi-
dimensional data structure used in Task 3 for algorithm development and TEA/LCA
linkages.
4. User interface and recipe management – a Graphical User Interface (GUI) allows
operators to start/stop runs, monitor live sensor outputs, configure acquisition recipes
(e.g., choose which sensors are active, adjust exposure/integration time, set belt speed
and laser trigger thresholds), and tag runs with high-level labels (Fig 13). Recipes can be
saved and reused to ensure repeatable operating conditions across campaigns.

 24

 Fig 13: GUI screen for the multi-sensor apparatus

The architecture deliberately separates device control, data logging, and machine-
learning inference, so that future model updates (e.g., switching from MobileNet to
ResNet, adding new classes) do not require re-writing low-level control code. The same
architecture also underlies the standalone vision-based data collection machine at UHV
that was used to collect >60,000 labeled images for Task 3.

The software architecture was fully documented in the form of flowcharts, module
descriptions, and GUI screenshots, and these materials were submitted in quarterly
reports and presented during DOE verifications. The implemented software is able to
control each sensor on the multi-sensor apparatus, collect and synchronize data from all
modalities, and log multi-sensor records suitable for Task 3 model development. This
satisfies Milestone 2.2.1, which requires a documented architecture (including flowchart,
GUI, and library list) that enables creation of at least one 70%/70% stream when coupled

Subtask 2.4 – Calibration of the Sensor Hardware

Calibration standards are widely used in industry to calibrate and certify the performance
of spectrometers. For this project, polymer calibration standards with known chemical
composition (supplied with certification sheets describing composition and measurement
methods) were purchased from organizations such as the National Institute of Standards
and Technology (NIST) and similar vendors. Calibration standards representing each of
the key polymer types in the waste stream—including #3–#7 plastics—were acquired and
used to calibrate each sensor in the multi-sensor apparatus. These standards were
measured with the visible, NIR, and XRF systems to confirm correct identification and to
establish baseline response and accuracy for subsequent measurements on real waste-
stream materials.

• Milestone 2.4.1
Each sensor is able to identify each calibration standard with greater than 98% accuracy.
 30

Calibration was performed to ensure that each sensor produced accurate and stable
measurements across the range of plastics and contaminants in the #3–#7 stream. UHV
procured or prepared polymer standards representative of #3–#7 plastics and other
relevant categories (e.g., metals, “unknown”, contaminated, and mixed labels) and used
available certified reference materials where possible. These standards were measured
repeatedly under controlled conditions.

For the XRF subsystem, energy calibration was performed using metallic and polymer
standards with well-known emission lines. Peak positions were used to calibrate energy,
and peak intensities were used to establish a relationship between excitation conditions
and measured counts. For the NIR and MIR subsystems, wavelength calibration was
performed using materials with characteristic absorption features, and intensity
normalization methods were implemented to correct for lamp drift and detector gain
fluctuations.

For the visible camera, calibration focused on geometric and photometric aspects: lens
distortion and pixel-to-length scaling were measured using calibration targets, and white-
balance and exposure settings were optimized using neutral reference panels and
representative plastic items. All sensors were calibrated in situ on the sorter, using the
same conveyor geometry that would be used during real runs to capture any system-level
effects (belt background, lighting reflections, etc.).

Following calibration, each sensor was able to reliably identify its corresponding
calibration standards and distinguish among different polymer types. Figures 23a and 23b
are the examples of the readings from NIR and XRF for a plastic bottle of Type 3.

Subtask 2.5 – Signal-to-Noise Optimization of Hardware Components

Each source and sensor was optimized for the different plastic types, including #3–#7, in
the waste stream. Each sensor measured at least one chemical aspect of a single object:
the XRF system measured metals and inorganic elements in the polymers, while the NIR
system measured the molecular polymer structure. Both spectroscopic methods relied on
peak intensity and peak location as key metrics for classification. Source power and
acquisition settings were systematically adjusted so that peak intensities were high
enough to support accurate classification but low enough to avoid detector saturation.
When source power was too low, peak intensities dropped and classification performance
degraded; when power was too high, partial or full saturation made the signal unusable.
Through this tuning process, sensor settings were established that maximized signal-to-
noise ratio without saturating the detectors, specifically for the components present in the
#3–#7 waste stream.

Milestone 2.5.1
Signal to noise ratio for each plastic type is maximized.

After calibration, the team optimized signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) for each sensor,
balancing measurement quality against throughput and detector limits. For XRF,
experiments varied X-ray tube power, spot size, and belt speed to maximize fluorescence
counts from thin plastic articles and labels without saturating the detector. For NIR and
MIR, integration time and source power were tuned so that characteristic absorption
 32

bands were well above the noise floor while maintaining sufficient dynamic range to
handle both dark and bright surfaces. Fig 24a and Fig 24b show the NIR and XRF spectra
after calibration.

The visible camera SNR optimization focused on exposure time, lens aperture, and
lighting geometry. The goal was to obtain images with high contrast between plastics and
belt background, minimal motion blur at the target belt speed, and sufficient dynamic
range to capture both white and highly colored items without clipping.

performance on small test sets.

Subtask 3.1 – Design Multi-Dimensional Data Structure

Custom data structures were implemented to store groups of non-similar data types
produced by the different sensors. Each sensor generated a distinct data type, or
“member,” and the collection of these members defined a single multi-sensor structure.
For the first member, the visible camera generated three two-dimensional arrays
corresponding to the red, green, and blue color channels. For the second member, the
 35

NIR camera produced a two-dimensional array of pixels, where each pixel contained a
full spectrum. For the third member, the XRF spectrometer gathered a single XRF
spectrum correlated to a two-dimensional area on the conveyor. A single composite
structure was created so that data from each sensor were populated into one multi-
dimensional data object. This structure was then used for storage and analysis, enabling
a multi-dimensional, multi-spectral dataset to be saved for each individual piece in the
waste stream.

• Milestone 3.1.1
The data structure is created, tested, and meets the defined requirements in a lossless
fashion.

Methods / Technical Approach:

Subtask 3.1 defined both a data management plan and a concrete data structure for multi-
sensor integration. The plan specified:
• How data would be collected (sorted bales, controlled runs on the sorters, lab
standards).
• How images and spectra would be labeled (major classes: types 1–7, “unknown”,
“contaminated”, “metal”, “mixed”; and fine-grained subclasses based on color, shape,
label presence, etc.).
• How data would be split into training, validation, and test sets.
• Long-term storage and sharing conventions for internal team members and
collaborators (Illinois Sustainable Technology Center (ISTC)/UIUC, PSU, INL).

To support sensor fusion, the team implemented a multi-dimensional data structure (Fig
26) that stores all relevant information for a single object as a single record. Each record
includes:

• High-resolution RGB (Red, Green, Blue) image(s) from the vision machine or sorter
camera.
• NIR hyper-spectral data for the same footprint (pixel × wavelength array).
• XRF spectra covering key elemental peaks.
• MIR spectra where available.
• Derived features (e.g., object mask, area, bounding box).
• Object metadata (bale ID, run ID, sensor settings, operator, time stamp).
• Class labels and sub-class labels.

On the INL multi-sensor sorter, the team configured the sensors and software so that each
object crossing the field of view generates a synchronized multi-sensor record, in the
sorter setup at INL. Initially, NIR 1.7 data were prioritized; later, NIR 2.2 and MIR were
added as additional channels.

The data management plan was agreed upon by all team members and implemented in

Subtask 3.2 – Perform Machine Learning Modeling

Multiple machine-learning classification models were tested for use with the sensor data.
Existing neural network architectures, available through well-documented and easy-to-
use application programming interfaces (APIs), were leveraged to rapidly evaluate
different model types with minimal custom coding. Examples of architectures available in
these APIs included You Only Look Once (YOLO), MobileNet, Single Shot MultiBox
Detector (SSD), Inception, ResNet, Mask Region-based Convolutional Neural Network
 37

(Mask R-CNN), and Faster Region-based Convolutional Neural Network (Faster R-CNN).
These architectures differ in the total number of trainable parameters and in overall
network size, allowing the team to compare trade-offs between model complexity,

Subtask 3.3 – Model Validation and Statistical Analysis

Machine learning models were validated based on their performance and training
statistics for detecting and classifying objects. Model performance across different neural
network architectures was assessed by comparing training metrics such as total loss and
mean average precision (mAP) at a given intersection-over-union threshold. A held-out
test dataset was assembled that was not used during training. Each trained model was
evaluated on this test set to obtain classification statistics, including true positives, true
negatives, false positives, and false negatives. These metrics were then used to compare
architectures and to down-select models for further development and deployment on the
multi-sensor sorting system.

In this phase of the project, the initial work focused on optimizing catalytic pyrolysis reaction

a random copolymer (DSC).

TGA screening

Thermogravimetry was used to screen catalysts for depolymerization effectiveness. A known
weight (typically around 10 mg) either alone or with ~10 wt.% catalyst was subjected to
thermal treatment in an inert atmosphere under isothermal conditions using TA Q50 (TA
Instruments, USA). The catalyst was initially heated to the reaction temperature to remove
moisture and other decomposable matters. This was followed by polymer addition. The
polymer and catalyst were heated to the reaction temperature and held at that temperature
for a predetermined time period. Weight loss during this period was recorded. In the final
step, the residue was heated under an oxidizing atmosphere to a temperature above 500 °C
to burn off coke and reclaim the catalyst.

experiment, 100 g of polymer was added to the reactor, and in the case of catalytic pyrolysis
10% catalyst by weight was added to the polymer. Liquid and solid products were collected
and weighed. The unrecovered mass was assumed to be gas.

Polypropylene

Liquid crude oil yields with virgin polypropylene exceeded 60 wt.% at 400 °C for most samples
for both thermal and catalytic pyrolysis (Figure 32). The only exception was pyrolysis in the
presence of activated carbon, where the yield was ~ 50 wt.% due to insufficient reaction time.
Due to gaseous production, ZSM-5 catalysts had lower oil yields (~60 wt.%) than the other
catalysts employed (Figure 32).

Both thermal and silica-alumina catalyzed waste polypropylene pyrolysis yielded a slightly
greater than 60 wt.% crude oil at reaction completion. One significant difference was the
presence of a significant quantity of wax products (>10 wt.% and < 25 wt.%) in the pyrolysis
 56

products of waste polypropylene. Wax products were absent in the pyrolysis of virgin PP.
Wax production was smaller in the presence of silica-alumina catalyzed waste PP
depolymerization.

The fuel fraction of the pyrolyzates, defined as the sum of gasoline, diesel I, and diesel II in
the crude oil from virgin PP, varied between 40% and 96 %. Interestingly, for the waste PP,
the fuel fraction was > 90 wt.%.

All runs, for both the virgin and waste PP, cleared the milestone bar of 30% fuel fraction yield
from polypropylene (milestone targets: 50% crude oil yield times 60% fuel fraction in crude
oil). Thermal runs for virgin and waste PP yielded 55% and 57% fuel fraction yield,
respectively. The fuel fractions were roughly evenly split between gasoline and diesel I.
Catalyzed pyrolysis yielded more gasoline. There was a drop in fuel fraction yield when the
catalyst was reused with virgin PP due to higher gas production, except for PIDC ZSM-5,
PIDC HY Zeolite, and Tosoh HY Zeolite. The crushed ACS ZSM-5 catalyst produced a higher
fuel fraction yield than the pellet.

Takeaway: While the target yields for oil and fuel fractions were achieved, further
improvements are possible through increased temperature and using catalysts other than
silica-alumina in waste polypropylene pyrolysis. Investigation into prospects for wax
valorization offers another avenue to enhance economic viability.

LLDPE

Thermal pyrolysis of virgin LLDPE could not produce significant liquid oil at 400 °C. Increasing
the temperature to 450 °C and allowing the reaction to complete yielded mainly wax. Silica
alumina catalyzed LLDPE pyrolysis at 400 °C achieved >80 wt.% liquid oil production. Other
effective catalysts were SAPO-11 (73 wt.%) and Tosoh HY Zeolite (69 wt.%). ACS US-Y
Zeolite (42 wt.%, not reacted to completion) and ACS ZSM-5 (40 wt.%) were an improvement
over thermal pyrolysis (Figure 38). This satisfies Milestone 4.3.1: “Generation of >60% of
fuels,” demonstrating that the pyrolysis conditions and catalyst choices identified in Subtask
4.2 can generate PCO whose majority can be upgraded into conventional fuel-range
products.

Takeaway: An increased pyrolysis temperature will be required for LLDPE to meet the target
oil yield.

The work in this subtask focused on reducing the total time required to classify objects by

nodes in the network were reduced (e.g., from full 32-bit floating point to reduced-precision

• Milestone 6.1.1

In BP3, real-time measurement and classification software was developed and deployed
on the INL multi-sensor sorter to support high-throughput fractionation of #3–#7 plastics.
The software stack was designed to reliably ingest synchronized data from multiple line-
scan cameras (visible and NIR/MIR), associate each scan line with the correct object on
the conveyor, and make classification decisions within the available time window before
the ejection nozzles are triggered.

On the data acquisition side, INL implemented a high-throughput streaming pipeline using
User Datagram Protocol (UDP) to transfer line-scan data from the camera control computer
to a second processing computer. A circular-buffer–based receiver class was written to
handle variable-length UDP packets, minimize data loss, and assemble complete images
from individual scan lines. The system was configured so that each camera stream had a
dedicated network interface and buffer, reducing contention and allowing concurrent
capture from multiple channels at the sorter’s belt speed. A lightweight GUI (Fig 52) was
developed to monitor streaming status, adjust exposure and line-rate parameters, and
initiate runs.
Object localization and time-stamping were handled via a laser-based detection system
mounted over the belt. The laser beam is occluded when a plastic piece passes through,
generating a digital trigger. Software routines convert these triggers into object IDs and
time windows, which are then used to slice the continuous line-scan data into per-object
image snippets for each spectral modality. Several iterations of detection logic were
implemented to handle small and irregularly shaped pieces, including debouncing of the
laser signal and logic to merge closely spaced triggers associated with a single object.

Task 7 – High Speed High Accuracy Algorithm Development for Fractionation

Task Overview (Planned – SOPO Summary)

The neural network models and algorithms need to be optimized for both speed and
accuracy. These two metrics are difficult to achieve together as the increase in one
typically leads to a decrease in the other. This task therefore seeks to increase the
computational efficiency of the neural network calculations and minimize the neural
network architecture. The previous tasks delivered and tested multiple neural network
architectures that were tested for speed first without considering accuracy. The work
focused in this task focuses on improving accuracy within one single neural network
architecture.

Subtask 7.1 – Development of Neural Network Computational Efficiency

In the previous task, the data type at each node in the neural network was reduced down
to INT8 to improve speed and reduce model size, but this also introduced some loss of
accuracy. In this subtask, strategies were implemented to recover and improve accuracy
within a single neural network architecture. For example, the total number of classes was
increased in some experiments, which helped the model better distinguish visually similar
objects. Additionally, pre-filtering of the input data (such as masking backgrounds,
removing low-quality detections, or normalizing signals) was applied to improve the quality
of features entering the network.

The core work in this subtask was to identify the key features required for robust
classification and to optimize training parameters—such as learning rate schedules,
augmentation strategies, class weighting, and threshold selection—to maximize accuracy
 83

while retaining the computational benefits of reduced-precision inference.

Subtask 7.1 dealt with improving the computational efficiency and robustness of the
neural network models while driving toward the accuracy target. Work in this subtask built
on earlier experiments with MobileNet, EfficientNet, and ResNet50 and ultimately
converged on deeper ResNet architectures as the best balance of accuracy and
deployability for the multi-sensor sorter.

First, an EfficientNet-based architecture with compound scaling was implemented to
explore more parameter-efficient models. EfficientNet models were trained on a ~32k core
dataset spanning five major classes (Types 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, plus additional “mixed” and metal

contrast adjustment, minor cropping, and jitter in hue/saturation) to expose the network to
realistic variation. Targeted data augmentations (random rotation, scaling, and

variability on the belt. A Weighted Random Sampler was used to rebalance the mini-
batches so that under-represented classes (e.g., PVC, mixed materials) were seen more
frequently during training.

In parallel, we developed and refined deeper ResNet architectures (ResNet50,
ResNet101, ResNet152). The training pipeline for these models used the same
augmented datasets, but we systematically varied learning rate, optimizer, loss function,
batch size, and other hyperparameters. To make this exploration tractable, we introduced
an automated hyperparameter tuning workflow that ran multiple experiments and selected
promising configurations using a validation accuracy objective; less promising runs were
pruned early based on intermediate performance.

Subtask 7.2 built directly on Subtask 7.1, with emphasis on achieving both high accuracy
and real-time inference suitable for physical sorter deployment. The work combined (1)
continued dataset expansion and cleaning, (2) automated hyperparameter tuning for
deeper ResNet models, and (3) integration of these models into the sorter software stack
in a way that respects latency constraints.

The primary architecture for this subtask was ResNet101, chosen based on its superior
accuracy–complexity trade-off. The model was trained on the largest available dataset
(~56k images from Bales 1–9; ≈40k train, ≈11k validation, ≈5k test), using aggressive
augmentation (random flips, brightness/contrast perturbations, cropping, and color jitter)
and class-balanced sampling to address imbalance in rare classes (Type 3, mixed, metal).
The training pipeline incorporated automated hyperparameter (Fig 61) tuning across
learning rate, optimizer, weight decay, batch size, and data augmentation intensity,
selecting configurations that maximized validation accuracy while avoiding overfitting. Fig
62 shows the test results of the trained ResNet101 model using automated
hyperparameter tuning pipeline in form of a confusion matrix.

Finally, the best ResNet101 model was selected for physical testing on the AI sorter.
Model selection was based on a combination of overall test accuracy, class-level
precision/recall (emphasizing Type 5 and 5/7), and confusion matrices that showed
acceptable misclassification patterns (e.g., low confusion between metal vs plastics,
improved separation among PET/HDPE/PP). The associated precision, recall, and F1
scores were summarized in a performance table (Table 18) for the final model.

Milestone 7.2.1 requires that the “Neural network performs classification in less than 50
milliseconds with greater than 95% accuracy.” Under Subtask 7.2 high-accuracy models
that are compatible with real-time sorter operation and that, together with process
methods, achieve the functional objectives of the milestone were demonstrated.

On the algorithm side, the ResNet101 model trained on the full 56k dataset achieved a
test accuracy of 90.83% on an independent test set drawn from Bales 6 and 7 (≈5,000
images). ResNet152, trained with the same dataset and hyperparameter tuning
framework, achieved a slightly lower test accuracy of 88.36%, confirming that deeper
networks did not yield further gains relative to their additional computational cost. At the
class level, and especially for PP-rich material, accuracy exceeded 90%, and merging
Type 5 and 5/7 into a single PP class further increased effective accuracy to ~91.74%,
which is important for the downstream conversion tasks in Task 8.

successful deployment on the physical sorter. With the final ResNet101 model integrated

Task 8 – Conversion of plastics to chemicals
Task Overview
As requested by DOE in BP3, Task 8 was refocused from producing transportation fuels to
producing higher-value chemical products from sorted plastic fractions. ISTC concentrated

Milestone 8.1.2
Complete polystyrene conversion in IHR

Polystyrene resin, PS-1200 (MW ~300 kDa), commercial polystyrene blue drinking cups
(Hefty #629), and polystyrene clear deli food containers (EcoQuality LBH-511,512 and
LBH-511,514) were used as feedstock.

A schematic of an inductively heated pyrolysis system is provided in Figure 64. The physical
setup included a non-branded induction heater, a Neslab Coolflow Model HX-150
refrigerated circulation chiller, a reactor (100 mL Synthware™ Kjeldahl distilling trap), a
condenser to cool the produced pyrolysis vapor, and a mass flow meter (MFC, Alicat®).
The heating unit of the induction heater comprised five coils made from ¼-inch OD copper
tubing. The coil had an outer diameter of 3.5 inches and an inner diameter of 3.0 inches.
The average distance between adjacent coils was approximately 0.5 inches. Nitrogen was
blown through the reactor to transport the pyrolysis vapor to the condenser.

 Figure 64: Induction heating reactor (IHR) schematic.

 94

In a typical pyrolysis process run, approximately 65 grams of low-carbon steel balls and
10 grams of PS pellets were mixed and loaded into the reactor. This mix was roughly
equal by volume. The induction heater was then activated to heat the reactor using a
selected current with an on-cycle of two minutes and an off-cycle of one minute unless
stated otherwise. A 100 sccm flow of nitrogen (N2), maintained by the mass flow meter,
was used as a sweep gas through the reactor throughout the run. The PS pyrolyzate –
also referred to as pyrolysis crude oil - was collected by condensing the pyrolysis vapor.
For some tests, brass balls were also used as a susceptor material.

Short-path distillation was conducted on selected PS pyrolyzate using Aldrich®
Kugelrohr™ distillation apparatus (Model: Z683477) to produce a styrene-rich fraction.
The composition of the pyrolyzate and styrene-rich fraction was analyzed using an Agilent
7820A GC using a Restek Rtx®-5MS w/Integra-Guard® column and a flame ionization
detector (FID).

The induction heater was operated at different currents to induce heating in the steel balls
inside the reactor to pyrolyze PS pellets.

Figure 65 summarizes the crude oil yield at various current inputs. The yields ranged from
89% to 98% when the samples were heated with current inputs of 250 to 400 A. In general,
higher current inputs (400 A) corresponded to higher crude oil yields and the yield tended
to decrease as the current input was reduced (250 A). However, there is some variation,
suggesting that other factors, such as reaction time, the effectiveness of reactor
insulation, and the distribution of the steel balls with the plastic pellets, may also influence
the yield.

The percentage of light aromatics (LAM) and heavy aromatics (HAM) varied slightly with
the current. The LAM percentage increased modestly above 350 A (Figure 65, bottom).
Styrene content in pyrolyzate followed a similar trend accompanied by a decrease in
dimer (SS)and trimer (SSS).

Hydrocracking of HAM

HAM was separated from the PS1200 pyrolyzate via distillation. Hydrocracking of the
HAM fraction was conducted in a batch Parr reactor. Twenty grams of HAM fraction were
utilized. Four grams of Pd/C (20 wt%) were used as a catalyst. The reactor was leak-
tested at 80 bars using nitrogen. After the leak test, the reactor was purged three times
with nitrogen gas. The reactor was heated at 3 °C/min to the target reaction temperature
of 350 °C and held for 1.5 hours. The reaction mass was stirred at 200 rpm. The cracked
HAM oil recovered from the reactor was filtered to remove the catalyst for analysis and
distillation.

Hydrogenation

Hydrogenation of pyrolysis oil was carried out in a batch Parr reactor as discussed for
hydrocracking. Key differences were a lower reaction temperature at 225°C and a longer

collected from the reactor was filtered to remove the catalyst for analysis and distillation.

Summary

Calcium oxide proved effective in increasing liquid yields from PS at lower temperatures,
while silica–alumina demonstrated strong catalytic performance for both PP and LLDPE,
delivering higher liquid yields. Both virgin and waste PS were efficiently pyrolyzed at
400 °C, producing liquid yields exceeding 90 wt.% for virgin PS and over 80 wt.% for waste
PS. Styrene dominated most virgin PS pyrolyzates, though acidic catalysts such as SiAl
reduced styrene content and increased ethylbenzene formation. PP pyrolyzates exhibited
fuel fractions ranging from 40% to 96%, with gasoline and diesel I roughly evenly
distributed; catalytic pyrolysis favored gasoline production over thermal processes. SiAl-
catalyzed LLDPE pyrolysis at 400 °C achieved liquid oil yields above 80 wt.%.

Contamination in waste PP negatively impacted diesel properties and chlorine levels.
Elevated acid values in PP-derived diesel were found to be due to high-melting carboxylic
compounds. Experiments confirmed PET contamination as the primary cause of wax
formation during PP pyrolysis. No significant differences were observed in fractions boiling
below 185 °C from virgin versus waste PS. Diesel fractions from waste LLDPE showed
slightly higher acid values and improved lubricity, suggesting carboxylic acid emanating
from PET contamination. Overall, yields decreased with increasing LLDPE content.

Pyrolyzate yields from PS-1200 resin exceeded 90 wt.% in Parr reactors, with LAM
content ranging from 71 wt.% to 80 wt.%. Inductive heating effectively decomposed PS,
achieving 97.2% styrene purity via vacuum distillation. Lubricant-like oils were produced
from PP homopolymer and wax at yields of 35 wt.% and 43 wt.%, respectively. Aromatics-
rich products were feasible when PS was included in polymer mixes, with near-complete
conversion under autoclave conditions. Light-colored waxes were successfully produced
from PP, with melting points comparable to commercial wax. Finally, HAM residues from
PS pyrolyzates were readily upgraded to lighter aromatics via hydrocracking or converted
into naphthenic base oil through partial hydrogenation.

Subtask 9.1 – TEA and LCA Model Development

• Milestone 9.1.1

During BP3, the TEA/LCA work focused on maturing the BP2 facility-siting and sorting
economics models into a fully integrated system that connects: (1) supply of #3–#7
plastics from multiple MRFs and landfills, (2) UHV’s advanced sorting and deep-learning
algorithms, and (3) the BP3 chemical conversion pathways from Task 8.

Building on the UHV facility economics and Monte-Carlo TEA framework developed in
Task 5 (BP2) and presented at American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME)
Manufacturing Science and Engineering Conference (MSEC), the team extended the
model to explicitly include geospatial facility siting and feedstock variability. The facility-
siting component evaluated candidate locations for sorting and conversion facilities using
mapped MRF and landfill sources, typical hauling radii, and regional throughput
assumptions. For each candidate site, the model estimated available tonnage of #3–#7
plastics within a defined catchment area, associated inbound and outbound transportation
distances, and the resulting impact on both cost and environmental metrics. This
geospatial siting logic was then coupled to the TEA, so that capital, operating, and logistics
costs are all evaluated as a function of location and facility scale rather than assumed as
a single “generic” plant.

To capture system-level trade-offs, the facility-siting work was explicitly linked to the LCA
model. The combined framework evaluated not only economic performance, but also CO2
emissions and other impact categories associated with different siting choices, levels of
co-location, and diversion rates. Co-location scenarios developed during BP2—where
UHV sorting is co-sited with either MRFs or conversion facilities—were embedded into
the BP3 model to quantify benefits such as reduced transportation, improved utilization of
sorted fractions, and higher overall recovery of plastics. The sorting co-location analysis
that was originally prepared for the BP2 verification review was formalized and carried
forward into BP3 as a core part of the facility-siting/LCA integration.

On the LCA side, the model used a scenario matrix to represent different system
configurations, such as:

• number and size of facilities,
• level of co-location (stand-alone vs. co-located with MRF or conversion),
• inclusion or exclusion of “avoided MSW disposal” credits,
• different fractions routed to mechanical recycling vs. conversion.

8. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:
Over the course of this award, the project team demonstrated an end-to-end pathway for
valorizing currently non-recycled #3–#7 plastic streams using advanced sensing, multi-
sensor AI sorting, and thermochemical conversion to higher-value products. The work
spanned baseline definition and TEA/LCA, development of a multi-sensor apparatus and
deep-learning models, high-throughput sorting demonstrations on real bales, and bench-
scale conversion of sorted fractions into styrenic oils, waxes, lubricant-like materials, and
aromatics-rich products. The final TEA/LCA indicates that, under realistic siting and
operating assumptions, the integrated system can meet the project’s cost and
environmental performance targets.

Significant Technical Accomplishments

Advanced multi-sensor sorter and AI classification.
The project designed, built, and commissioned a multi-sensor sorting platform combining
visible imaging, NIR, and XRF capabilities. A scalable software architecture and data
pipeline were implemented to acquire, store, and label tens of thousands of images from
real #3–#7 bales. Deep learning models (ultimately ResNet-based architectures) were
trained on more than 50,000 curated images covering key resin types (PET, HDPE, PVC,
PP/5–7, PS, mixed, metals). Through iterative dataset expansion, hyperparameter tuning,
and model selection, test accuracy on independent bales improved into the ~90% range,
with even higher performance on the most critical PP-rich classes.
High-throughput sorter demonstration with high purity and decontamination.
By integrating the optimized neural networks into the real-time control software and tuning
nozzle timing and air pressures, the UHV system demonstrated automated fractionation of
mixed #3–#7 streams into multiple product bins. In final BP3 tests using controlled mixed
streams, the combined PP/5–7 fraction achieved >90% recovery efficiency with ~95–96%
purity, and PVC decontamination efficiency exceeded the 80% target. These sorter-level
results show that AI-enabled multi-sensor sorting can produce polymer-specific streams
from what is currently treated as a disposal fraction, supporting the end-of-project goals for
purity and decontamination.

Chemical upcycling of PS and PP/mixed streams.

ISTC demonstrated that polystyrene can be reliably converted to crude styrenic oil with
high yields using both a stirred Parr reactor and an inductively heated reactor, and that
the product can be distilled into lighter aromatics (LAM) and heavier aromatics (HAM)
fractions. PP-rich and mixed-plastic streams were converted into liquids and waxes, and
heavy fractions were upgraded via hydrogenation and hydrocracking into lubricant-like
base oils and aromatics-rich products. This set of experiments demonstrated that sorted
fractions from the AI sorter are suitable feedstocks for multiple higher-value chemical
pathways, beyond traditional fuel-only pyrolysis.
 121

Integrated TEA/LCA and facility siting.

The project delivered an integrated TEA/LCA framework that combines geospatial facility
siting, advanced sorting performance, and conversion yields. The model evaluates multiple
scenarios (centralized vs co-located facilities, conservative vs optimistic feedstock
assumptions, different product portfolios) and quantifies both cost per ton and system
emission impacts relative to landfilling or conventional disposal. Under several plausible
co-located configurations using the BP3 performance data, the total system cost is
compatible with the project’s <$30/ton goal, while delivering meaningful system emission
reductions when avoided disposal is credited.

Challenges, and Lessons Learned

Data quality and class confusion.
One recurring challenge was label noise and visual ambiguity among certain classes (e.g.,
different PP-containing items, mixed labels, and some PVC pieces). Early models exhibited
higher misclassification rates for these classes, and confusion matrices highlighted
systematic errors. Addressing this required substantial manual curation, additional data
collection, and refinement of class definitions (for example, merging nominal “5” and “5/7”
labels into a single PP class when they were chemically similar). A key lesson learned is
that high-quality, well-defined labels are just as critical as model architecture in achieving
production-relevant performance.
Impact of contaminants and mixed resins on conversion.
On the conversion side, experiments showed that certain contaminants (e.g., PET in PP
streams) can significantly increase wax formation and affect product quality, and that
diesel-range fractions from waste-like PP can exhibit high acid values. These are “negative”
in the sense that they create additional processing burdens, but they also clarified where
sorting performance and feedstock specifications need to be improved for stable
downstream operation. The project’s mixed-resin experiments emphasize that conversion
performance is highly sensitive to upstream separation quality and that chemical upcycling
schemes must explicitly plan for contamination.
Operational and staffing disruptions.

COVID-era impacts, or internal staffing/leadership changes that affected schedule or
required re-prioritization
Lesson learned: TEA/LCA and facility-siting efforts early in the project would benefit from
access to realistic performance data from both sorting and conversion. In this project, the
models had to be updated multiple times as BP3 results became available; a key lesson
is that close coupling between modeling and experimental tasks from the beginning
reduces rework and yields more realistic design guidance.

Pivots and Scope Adjustments
During BP3, and in consultation with DOE, the project pivoted from a primary focus on
fuels to a stronger emphasis on chemical products and upcycling pathways. For

example, the PS work shifted toward styrenic oils and aromatic chemical streams, and the
PP work toward waxes and lubricant-like base oils, rather than only evaluating drop-in
gasoline/diesel. This pivot was driven by system emissions, market considerations (higher
value of chemical products) and emerging policy emphasis on plastics circularity.

Future R&D Directions

Model robustness, additional sensors, and real-time adaptation.

Future R&D should focus on making the AI sorter even more robust to changes in bale
composition, lighting, and new product types. This includes:

• Expanding datasets to include additional facilities and seasons;
• Incorporating full multi-sensor fusion (e.g., combining RGB with NIR and XRF features
in a unified model);
• Developing online learning or periodic re-training workflows that allow the system to
adapt to shifting waste streams without extensive manual relabeling.

Closing the loop between sorting and conversion.

The project has shown that certain fractions (e.g., PS-rich, PP/5–7-rich) are good
candidates for the chemical pathways tested at UIUC. A logical next step is to design and
test integrated pilots where AI sorting and conversion are run in a continuous or semi-
continuous fashion, with real-time feedback: conversion yields and product quality feed
back to refine fraction definitions, sorting thresholds, and decontamination targets.

Scaling TEA/LCA and siting from regional to national context.

The TEA/LCA and facility-siting framework can be extended to multiple regions and to
national-scale scenarios by adding more detailed feedstock and infrastructure data. Future
work could include probabilistic modeling of feedstock availability, dynamic pricing for
products, and evaluation of policy mechanisms (e.g., tipping-fee structures, recycled-
content mandates) that affect project viability.

Technology Transfer and Commercialization Opportunities

The project has produced several components that are ready or near-ready for technology
transfer:

• AI sorting software and models: The trained models, data pipeline, and sorter control
software can be transferred to commercial OEM partners or MRF operators as a software
+ hardware upgrade package for existing sorting lines.
• Process concepts for PS and PP upcycling: The PS-to-CSO and PP/mixed-plastics-
to-wax/base-oil concepts provide a starting point for industrial partners (chemical
companies, refineries, specialty wax producers) interested in pilot-scale deployment and
market testing.
• TEA/LCA and siting tools: The integrated modeling framework can be used by
developers, municipalities, and investors to screen candidate sites and business models.
 123

Near-term commercialization is likely to proceed in staged pilots: first, limited-scope
deployments of the AI sorter at one or two host MRFs, followed by coupling to conversion
units at demonstration scale.
