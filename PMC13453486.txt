Nanosonosensitizers in antibacterial sonodynamic therapy: mechanism exploration, activity enhancement strategies, and current status of antibacterial applications☆

Introduction

In recent years, the global spread of multidrug-resistant bacteria has accelerated. “Superbugs,” such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), have evolved diverse resistance mechanisms through genetic mutations, including altering cell wall and membrane permeability to block drug entry and upregulating efflux pump activity to accelerate drug efflux. Coupled with the widespread overuse and improper prescription of antibiotics, these factors have exacerbated the selection pressure, accelerating the emergence and global spread of drug-resistant bacteria [1], [2]. Although the modification or structural optimization of antibiotics has shown improved antibacterial efficacy against bacterial infections, the process from discovery to clinical trials is costly and lengthy, making it unlikely that these new antibiotics will be widely translated into clinical practice in the near future. Additionally, many bacterial species form resilient biofilms on surfaces and host tissues, encased in self-secreted extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) composed of proteins, exopolysaccharides, and extracellular DNA. The structural barrier created by EPS significantly impedes the penetration and efficacy of conventional antibiotics, resulting in suboptimal treatment outcomes [3].

To address these challenges, non-antibiotic antimicrobial strategies have emerged as promising alternatives. Among these, exogenous stimulus-responsive therapies such as photodynamic therapy (PDT) and sonodynamic therapy (SDT) have attracted extensive attention due to their durable potential and remarkable efficacy [4]. However, while PDT can generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) via photosensitizers to kill bacteria, its penetration depth in biological tissues is limited to a few millimeters to one centimeter, thereby reducing its efficacy against deep-seated infections. In contrast, SDT utilizes Low‑intensity ultrasound (US) to activate sonosensitizers, enabling ROS production in tissues several centimeters deep. The ROS include singlet oxygen (1O2), hydroxyl radicals (·OH), and superoxide anions (O2−·). These species then trigger lipid peroxidation in cell membranes, disrupt membrane integrity, cause leakage of cellular contents, lead to DNA strand breaks, and promote denaturation of essential functional proteins, thereby inhibiting bacterial proliferation and causing bacterial death [5]. Therefore, SDT not only ensures broad‑spectrum antibacterial efficacy and reduces the risk of resistance development through ROS generation, but also exhibits unique advantages in treating deep‑tissue infections [6].

While SDT shows mechanistic advantages, its therapeutic efficacy depends largely on the performance of sonosensitizers. Conventional organic sonosensitizers, such as Chlorin e6, protoporphyrin, and curcumin, exhibit excellent biocompatibility and structural tunability, but they often suffer from limitations in chemical stability, bioavailability, and rapid clearance [5], [7]. Inorganic nanosonosensitizers, on the other hand, offer higher ROS generation efficiency and improved stability. In a narrow sense, inorganic nanosonosensitizers are specifically defined as inorganic nanomaterials that possess intrinsic sonosensitizing activity, generating ROS directly through US-triggered electronic transitions or piezocatalytic effects to exert antibacterial functions [3]. Broadly speaking, even if the overall particle size of a material exceeds 100 nm, it can still be considered within the scope of inorganic nanosonosensitizers, provided that its core functional domains—such as bandgap‑engineering interfaces, piezoelectric response regions, or surface defect layers—remain at the nanoscale, thereby endowing the system with enhanced acoustic cavitation effects and improved electron–hole (e−-h⁺) separation efficiency [8]. Representative inorganic nanoantibacterial agents include metal‑based NPs such as Au and Ag2O2, transition metal oxides (e.g., TiO2, ZnO), and carbon‑based nanomaterials (e.g., graphene, carbon nanotubes) [2]. Furthermore, when inorganic nanoparticles (NPs) serve merely as inert or synergistic carriers for small‑molecule organic sonosensitizers, the resulting constructs are essentially organic–inorganic hybrid nanosonosensitizers [9]. Such hybrid systems not only enable effective drug delivery to infected sites, but also potentiate antibacterial efficacy by lowering the cavitation threshold and amplifying cavitation effects [5], [10]. In addition, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), as organic-inorganic hybrid materials, have also shown promising antibacterial activity. Through design strategies such as morphology control, heterojunction construction, and defect engineering, these nanomaterials synergistically enhance US‑induced cavitation effect and piezoelectric effect, enabling efficient generation of ROS under US excitation. This provides a new platform for highly effective antibacterial therapy, thereby achieving potent antimicrobial outcomes [11].

Currently, existing reviews on antibacterial sonodynamic therapy (aSDT) have systematically surveyed this field from multiple perspectives, including the underlying sonochemical and sonomechanical mechanisms, the categorization of various sonosensitizers, and the progress in nanomedicine toward improving delivery efficiency and multifunctionality of sonosensitizers. In recent years, some reviews have also begun to highlight emerging material systems such as piezoelectric sonosensitizers, as well as the synergistic integration of SDT with other therapeutic modalities. However, a comprehensive and integrated summary of strategies to enhance sonocatalytic efficiency—specifically morphology regulation, defect engineering, phase engineering, and interface engineering—from a materials science perspective remains notably scarce. Moreover, most existing reviews are largely confined to single disease contexts, such as dental or skin infections, and a systematic overview of aSDT applications across a broader spectrum of bacterial infectious diseases—including osteomyelitis, myositis, periodontitis, and chronic gastritis—is still lacking. To address these gaps, this review first provides a systematic elucidation of the underlying mechanisms of aSDT, tracing the cascade from US triggering and sonosensitizer activation to ROS generation and subsequent ferroptosis‑like bacterial death. Then, from a materials‑science standpoint, it summarizes a range of strategies for boosting sonocatalytic efficiency via rational sonosensitizer design. Finally, it consolidates recent progress in the application of aSDT to various infectious diseases. Collectively, this review aims to offer systematic insights and a valuable reference for the translation of aSDT from mechanistic studies towards clinical practice.

Mechanisms of inorganic nanosonosensitizer-mediated aSDT

SDT is an antibacterial modality that generates ROS efficiently at infection sites through US‐activated sonosensitizers, which promote interactions with surrounding O2 or H2O, or exploit endogenous chemical signals in the infected microenvironment (e.g., H2O2, acidic pH) to catalyze redox reactions that produce ROS [3]. The generated ROS first overwhelm the bacterial antioxidant defence system, which includes the enzymatic antioxidants superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and thioredoxin (TRX), as well as the non‑enzymatic antioxidant glutathione (GSH), all of which scavenge intracellular ROS to maintain redox homeostasis [12], [13]. Once these defenses are breached, ROS cause lipid peroxidation of bacterial membranes, oxidative inactivation of proteins, and oxidative damage to nucleic acids, compromising membrane integrity and genetic material structure [4], [11], [14]. Sui et al. [15], using adaptive evolution screening, found that SOD gene‑knockout mutations significantly enhanced Escherichia coli (E. coli) tolerance to ferroptosis‑like death, confirming SOD as a critical defense line against oxidative damage. Wang et al. [16] applied synthetic CuFeS2 nanozymes to E. coli and MRSA, and demonstrated that these nanozymes synergistically promote the irreversible accumulation of lipid peroxides by depleting intracellular GSH and interfering with respiratory metabolism, thereby confirming the central role of GSH depletion in ferroptosis‑like death.

ROS subsequently attack Fe–S clusters in the bacterial respiratory chain, leading to electron transport chain disruption, inhibition of ATP synthesis, and release of free Fe2+. Strotmann et al. [17], using electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy on E. coli respiratory complex I, directly demonstrated that treatment with 1 mM H2O2 selectively abolished the characteristic EPR signal of the binuclear Fe–S clusters N1b, providing direct evidence that ROS attack Fe–S clusters in the respiratory chain. Sedláček et al. [18], using Paracoccus denitrificans as a model and paraquat to impose superoxide stress, found that NADH dehydrogenase and succinate dehydrogenase activities were markedly reduced; importantly, the inactivated dehydrogenases could be fully restored by anaerobic incubation in a mixture containing 2‑mercaptoethanol, sulfide, and ferrous ions, directly proving that Fe–S clusters are the targets of superoxide attack. Shen et al. [19] designed a ferrous polysulfide aqueous solution (Fe(II)Snaq) system and, through respiratory chain complex I/II activity assays, observed significant inhibition of Complex I and Complex II. Pang et al. [20] constructed a siderophore‑sonosensitizer‑Fe3+ self‑assembled nanoplatform (FSP) and, using activity assays for respiratory chain complexes I–IV, confirmed that the enzymatic activities of all four complexes were markedly suppressed.

The liberated Fe2+ generate highly toxic ·OH via the Fenton reaction (Fe2+ + H2O2 → Fe3+ + ·OH + OH−), while a portion of Fe3+ can be reduced to Fe2+ by reducing substances such as O2−·, forming an oxidative amplification cycle that exacerbates lipid peroxidation and iron homeostasis imbalance, ultimately leading to the collapse of bacterial energy metabolism (Fig. 1) [14]. Shen et al. [19], by comparing the bactericidal effects of Fe2+ and Fe3+, found that Fe2+ is the key direct bactericidal factor, whereas Fe3+ showed almost no effect under the same conditions; EDTA chelation experiments confirmed that chelation of Fe2+ significantly inhibited bactericidal activity, corroborating iron dependence. Sui et al. [15], using a malachite green decolorization assay, demonstrated that the phloroglucinol–Fe3+ complex enhanced ·OH production in the Fenton reaction by 2.2‑fold, and that intracellular ·OH levels were more than 230‑fold higher than in the control group, directly confirming that the Fe2+‑driven Fenton reaction is the core mechanism for ·OH generation and amplification of oxidative damage. Pang et al. [20], using a FerroOrange probe, detected a significant increase in intracellular Fe2+ after FSP treatment, and further confirmed the iron‑dependent ferroptosis‑like pathway through concentration‑dependent rescue of bacterial survival by Ferrostatin‑1 and attenuation of bactericidal efficacy by EDTA.

Concurrently, the mechanical shock waves and microjets generated by US cavitation physically disrupt the three‑dimensional structure of biofilms, damage the EPS secreted by bacteria, enhance the penetration of sonosensitizers and drugs, and thereby improve bacterial clearance efficiency [3]. Furthermore, while directly killing bacteria, SDT releases pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), such as lipopolysaccharide and peptidoglycan, via bacterial lysis, and also releases damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as ATP and high mobility group box 1, due to cavitation or ROS-induced host cell damage. Su et al. [21] designed a biodegradable oxygen–generating metal–phenolic network (MnPM) system and, through in vivo experiments, demonstrated that SDT exposes bacterial–associated antigens (BAAs), while Mn2+ released from MnPM activates the cGAS–STING pathway in antigen–presenting cells, thereby enhancing the immune response to BAAs. PAMPs and DAMPs collectively activate host pattern recognition receptors, initiating multiple downstream immune signaling pathways (e.g., cGAS–STING, NF–κB, cAMP/cGMP–PKG), promoting the phagocytic activity of immune cells (such as macrophages and neutrophils) and the secretion of inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL–1β, TNF–α), generating an antibacterial immune synergistic effect that aids in eliminating residual pathogens and preventing infection recurrence (Fig. 1) [4], [14]. Li et al. [22] constructed an emodin (ED)‑conjugated Mn‑doped TiO2 nanorod array (TOM) system and, using in vitro macrophage polarization assays and an animal model of osteomyelitis, verified that the ROS generated by this hybrid coating under US effectively disrupt bacterial membranes while activating cAMP and cGMP–PKG pathways, driving macrophage polarization towards the M1 phenotype, which exhibits potent phagocytic capacity against MRSA. Xu et al. [23] developed an iron‑based covalent organic framework nano‑adjuvant (CFCP) system and showed, through in vivo experiments, that the combination of enhanced SDT and interference with iron metabolism increased the release of bacterial double‑stranded DNA (dsDNA); the immunogenic dsDNA promoted dendritic cell maturation via STING activation, amplified neutrophil immunostimulation through IFN‑β, and enhanced bacterial antigen presentation, which in turn triggered humoral immunity in B and T cells, conferring long‑term anti‑infection protection.

The cascade effects of these multiple mechanisms collectively shift bacteria from reversible oxidative stress to irreversible lethal damage, constituting the complete execution program of ferroptosis‑like death. This enables efficient, multi‑targeted elimination of drug‑resistant bacteria and biofilm‑associated infections.

In summary, inorganic nanosonosensitizers convert external energy input (e.g., US) into sonodynamic effects or utilize endogenous chemical signals in the infection microenvironment to catalyze redox reactions, both of which collectively promote the targeted generation of ROS. This overcomes the bacterial oxidative defense system, and, in combination with the Fenton reaction, cyclically amplifies oxidative damage to bacteria while simultaneously activating host signaling pathways, collectively achieving the antibacterial effect [24]. Thus, ROS generation is central to the antibacterial action of SDT. In this section, we will discuss how inorganic nanosonosensitizers mediate SDT to produce ROS upon ultrasonic stimulation, and explore the potential antibacterial mechanisms of SDT.

Mechanisms of ROS generation

As an emerging non-invasive antibacterial strategy, aSDT leverages low-intensity US to activate sonosensitizers enriched at the infection site, synergistically eliminating bacteria through physicochemical dual effects, demonstrating particularly significant advantages against drug-resistant bacteria and biofilms. Its mechanism can be summarized into two major aspects: the chemical killing by ROS and the physical adjuvant killing by ultrasonic cavitation. Among these, ROS generation primarily originates from two key effects, namely the ultrasonic cavitation effect and the piezoelectric effect.

Ultrasonic cavitation effect

Ultrasonic cavitation effect serves as the fundamental basis for the physicochemical effects generated by aSDT. When the pressure oscillation amplitude of US exceeds a critical threshold, it induces bubble formation in the liquid medium. Subsequently, these microbubbles undergo growth and violent collapse, a phenomenon known as acoustic cavitation, with the microbubbles also referred to as cavitation bubbles (Fig. 1) [25], [26]. Based on the dynamic behavior of cavitation bubbles, cavitation can be classified into inertial cavitation (transient cavitation) and non-inertial cavitation (stable cavitation). Inertial cavitation generates localized high temperatures (thousands of K), high pressures (exceeding 1000 atm), shock waves, and sonoluminescence upon violent collapse of cavitation bubbles, whereas non-inertial cavitation primarily induces microstreaming and shear stress through stable oscillations [27], [28].

Photocatalytic effect

During inertial cavitation collapse, the energy accumulated inside the bubble is released in the form of extremely short light pulses, a phenomenon known as sonoluminescence, whose spectral range can cover the ultraviolet to visible region (approximately 200–800 nm) [29]. Although the photon energy generated by sonoluminescence may theoretically induce direct photolysis reactions, under actual sonodynamic conditions its intensity is insufficient to efficiently produce ROS. Beguin et al. [30] exposed clinical US contrast microbubbles to therapeutic US (1 MHz) and, using a photomultiplier tube for detection, demonstrated that these microbubbles generate multibubble sonoluminescence under clinically relevant US conditions. The primary role of sonoluminescence is to activate sonosensitizers, indirectly generating ROS through energy transfer. Sonoluminescence can excite semiconductor-type sonosensitizers (e.g., TiO2). When the photon energy emitted by sonoluminescence exceeds the band gap of the semiconductor (the band gap of TiO2 is approximately 3.2 eV), valence band (VB) electrons are excited to the conduction band (CB), forming e−-h⁺ pairs (Fig. 1) [31]. Yang et al. [32] confirmed this mechanism in their study of carbon‑doped TiO2. Subsequently, the photogenerated electrons react with surface-adsorbed O2 to generate O2−·, while the photogenerated holes can oxidize H2O or hydroxide ions to produce ·OH [33]. These two ROS serve as the basis for subsequent cascade reactions.

Thermal catalytic effect

The local “hot spots” formed during inertial cavitation collapse can reach temperatures as high as several thousand Kelvin, causing a sharp temperature rise on the surface of sonosensitizers [11]. This extremely high-temperature environment can promote ROS generation by two pathways. On the one hand, it can induce a pyrolytic effect: high temperatures directly pyrolyze water vapor molecules inside cavitation bubbles, generating hydrogen radicals (·H) and ·OH (Fig. 1) [34], [35]. In the presence of O2, ·H rapidly reacts with O2 to form hydroperoxyl radicals (·OOH), which further convert to H2O2. Troia et al. [36] systematically investigated ROS generation and H2 evolution by sonicating homogeneous concentrated simple salt solutions. They confirmed that collapsing cavitation bubbles act as local “hot spots”, reaching extreme temperatures exceeding 10,000 K and pressures above 10 MPa. These extreme conditions induce sonochemical splitting of water molecules within the bubbles, producing ·H and ·OH radicals. On the other hand, it can produce a thermally enhanced catalytic effect: even if the pyrolysis temperature is not reached, the local high temperature significantly accelerates the kinetic processes of catalytic reactions on the sonosensitizer surface, promoting thermal excitation of charge carriers, thereby synergistically enhancing ROS generation efficiency [37]. This pathway typically serves as a complementary and enhancing mechanism to the photocatalytic pathway.

Piezoelectric effect

In addition to the aforementioned cavitation-dependent mechanisms, the piezoelectric effect represents another independent and important pathway for ROS generation. For piezoelectric materials with non-centrosymmetric crystal structures (e.g., BaTiO3, Bi2MoO6, etc.), the periodic mechanical stress generated during US propagation directly induces lattice deformation, producing a polarization electric field (piezopotential) inside the material [38], [39]. This built-in electric field acts as an efficient driving force that promotes the separation and migration of e−-h⁺ pairs on or near the material surface while effectively suppressing their recombination. The separated electrons and holes undergo redox reactions with surface-adsorbed O2 and H2O, respectively, generating ROS such as O2−· and ·OH (Fig. 1) [40]. Zhu et al. [41] designed and synthesized piezoelectric tetragonal barium titanate (T‑BTO) NPs. Using finite element modeling simulations, they confirmed that US exposure induces a built‑in electric field within T‑BTO that modulates the band alignment; they further verified the generation of ROS, including O2−· and ·OH, under US irradiation. This pathway does not rely on cavitation luminescence or high temperatures and represents an important physicochemical conversion route independent of conventional photocatalytic mechanisms, offering new possibilities for the application of aSDT in complex environments.

The above three pathways are not mutually exclusive. They often coexist and act synergistically during aSDT, with their dominance depending on the type of sonosensitizer material (semiconductors, piezoelectric materials, etc.), US parameters, and microenvironmental conditions.

Auxiliary antibacterial mechanism

The antibacterial effect of aSDT relies not only on the chemical oxidation by ROS, but the multiple physical effects of ultrasonic cavitation itself also produce a powerful adjuvant killing effect, representing a potent and rapid killing mechanism independent of biochemical pathways [11].

Mechanical damage

During inertial cavitation, the shock waves and high-speed microjets released upon bubble collapse can directly act on the bacterial cell wall and cell membrane, causing physical tearing and rupture [42]. This mechanical damage disrupts both the thick peptidoglycan layer of Gram-positive bacteria and the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria, leading to leakage of cellular contents and achieving a direct bactericidal effect (Fig. 1). Fitriyanti et al. [43] systematically investigated the failure mechanisms of bacterial surfaces under impact loading generated by ultrasonic cavitation using explicit dynamic finite element simulations. Using E. coli and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) as model organisms, they identified three primary failure modes—perforation, bursting and a transitional type. The simulation results showed excellent agreement with existing experimental data, confirming that cavitation‑generated shock waves (with pressures up to 100 MPa) and high‑speed microjets (with velocities exceeding 100 m/s) induce differential physical damage depending on the cell wall structure of the bacterium, thereby providing theoretical guidance for optimizing US sterilization strategies.

Furthermore, the mechanical energy from inertial cavitation—including shock waves and microjets—is not limited to physical disruption of biological structures; the localized extreme energy released upon bubble collapse can also directly act on small molecules in the surrounding medium. Studies have shown that this mechanical energy can directly cleave H2O to generate ·OH and activate dissolved O2 to produce 1O2, enabling radical generation without reliance on any chemical sensitizers [44]. Based on this understanding, He et al. [44], in a study on sonocatalytic mechanisms, constructed CO2‑loaded nanocarriers as exogenous cavitation nuclei; the mechanical energy released through enhanced inertial cavitation significantly improved the efficiency of ROS generation.

Sonoporation

Sonoporation is a specialized form of cavitation, referring to the transient increase in cell membrane permeability under US exposure [45]. During non-inertial cavitation or the early stages of inertial cavitation, the microstreaming and shear stress generated by bubble oscillation can induce the formation of reversible micropores on the cell membrane, thereby greatly enhancing the transmembrane transport efficiency of sonosensitizers, antibiotics, or other therapeutic drugs, allowing them to accumulate inside bacterial cells and achieving a synergistic effect (Fig. 1). Zhao et al. [46] investigated a porcine model of suppurative arthritis using US‑triggered microbubble destruction (UTMD) and found that UTMD induces increased permeability of bacterial aggregates, confirming the existence of sonoporation. Following combined treatment with amikacin and UTMD, no bacteria were detected in the synovial fluid of the porcine model, indicating a remarkably potent antibacterial effect. This demonstrates that UTMD enhances antibiotic efficacy by increasing permeability through sonoporation.

Thermal effect

The localized high temperatures generated by cavitation hot spots, as well as the sonothermal effect of certain sonosensitizers (e.g., Au NPs) under US exposure, can cause a local temperature rise in bacteria (Fig. 1) [47]. This thermal effect can induce protein denaturation on the bacterial surface and inactivation of key metabolic enzymes, thereby weakening the bacterial defense mechanisms and making them more susceptible to subsequent oxidative attack by ROS. Lee et al. [48] systematically investigated the inactivation efficacy of E. coli K12 suspended in apple juice following treatment with manothermosonication (MTS, 400 kPa/59 ℃), thermosonication (TS, 100 kPa/59 ℃) and manosonication (MS, 400 kPa/55 ℃). The results showed that MTS achieved a 5‑log reduction within 1.4 min, whereas TS required 3.8 min and MS required 2.5 min to achieve the same level of inactivation. By comparing the inactivation efficiencies of thermally assisted treatments (TS and MTS) with those of MS, the authors confirmed a significant synergistic effect between US and heat.

In summary, the antibacterial mechanism of aSDT is a complex, multi-factor synergistic process. It begins with US excitation of sonosensitizers, efficiently generating ROS through various pathways such as sonoluminescence, pyrolysis, and piezoelectric catalysis, thereby achieving chemical disruption of key bacterial biomolecules. Simultaneously, it exploits the mechanical shock, sonoporation, and thermal effects of cavitation to directly kill bacteria at the physical level and enhance drug penetration. This synergistic attack strategy, in which chemical and physical mechanisms complement each other, constitutes a unique advantage of aSDT over conventional antibiotics, enabling it to exert significant efficacy against drug-resistant bacteria and recalcitrant biofilm infections.

Inorganic nanosonosensitizer-mediated strategies for enhancing sonodynamic catalytic activity

Although SDT, as a non-invasive physical therapeutic modality with high tissue penetration capability, has shown great potential for the treatment of bacterial infections, inorganic nanosonosensitizers such as TiO2, BaTiO3 and ZnO still have certain limitations in practical applications [49]. First, from the perspective of the disease microenvironment, the hypoxic microenvironment at the infection site greatly restricts the electron-driven reduction of O2 to O2−·, resulting in ROS yields far below theoretical values [50]. Meanwhile, limited endogenous H2O2 content and high concentrations of GSH not only fail to provide sufficient substrates for the sonocatalytic process, but also consume the limited ROS, further compromising the sonodynamic therapeutic efficacy [51], [52]. Second, considering the intrinsic properties of sonosensitizers, conventional inorganic sonosensitizers typically possess wide band gaps, which prevents them from efficiently utilizing the broad-spectrum sonoluminescence generated by US excitation. Moreover, the energy conversion efficiency of individual photons is low, leading to poor e−-h⁺ separation efficiency. Furthermore, the generated e−-h⁺ pairs are highly prone to rapid recombination, all of which limit subsequent ROS generation [53], [54]. Additionally, many materials exhibit unsatisfactory hot electron conversion capabilities, resulting in inefficient thermal excitation [55]. Finally, the microjets and shock waves generated during cavitation bubble collapse continuously erode the sonosensitizer surface, causing structural damage and detachment of active components, thereby affecting their cycling stability and biosafety [56]. To address these limitations, researchers have recently developed a series of strategies to enhance sonodynamic catalytic activity, focusing on: (1) low e−-h⁺ separation efficiency; (2) rapid e−-h⁺ recombination rate; (3) hypoxic microenvironment at the infection site; and (4) cycling stability and biosafety of sonosensitizers. In this section, we will systematically elaborate on various activity enhancement strategies and their application progress in the antibacterial field from four aspects: morphological engineering, defect engineering, phase engineering and interface engineering, and novel synergistic enhancement strategies.

Morphological engineering

As mentioned above, the ultrasonic cavitation effect is the fundamental basis for the physicochemical effects generated by SDT. Ultrasonic cavitation produces cavitation bubbles, which subsequently trigger inertial or non-inertial cavitation. Among these, the formation (nucleation) of cavitation bubbles is a prerequisite for initiating the ultrasonic cavitation effect. In a homogeneous system without solid particles, cavitation bubbles must form through homogeneous nucleation, which requires overcoming an extremely high energy barrier. In the SDT system, however, the introduced sonosensitizer nanomaterials provide preferential nucleation sites for bubbles, i.e., heterogeneous nucleation occurs [56]. Solid surfaces (especially hydrophobic, porous, or rough surfaces) significantly reduce the energy barrier required to form cavitation bubbles of critical size. The geometric factor f(θ) satisfies 0 ≤ f(θ) ≤ 1. Here, θ denotes the equivalent contact angle of a gas bubble at the solid–liquid interface, rather than the contact angle of a water droplet. For a hydrophobic surface, the water droplet contact angle satisfies θwater>90°, which corresponds to a bubble equivalent contact angle of θ=180°-θwater<90°. Under this condition, f(θ) is significantly reduced, leading to a substantial decrease in the nucleation energy barrier, and thus cavitation bubbles preferentially form on hydrophobic surfaces [57]. Moreover, surface roughness can further increase the apparent contact angle of hydrophobic surfaces, while porous structures can create a low-energy-barrier environment favorable for nucleation [58], [59]. Both effects contribute synergistically to further lowering the nucleation energy barrier and promoting cavitation occurrence. Therefore, heterogeneous nucleation serves as the foundation and promoting mechanism of the ultrasonic cavitation effect. Optimizing the surface characteristics of sonosensitizers, such as particle size (affecting the effective radius of curvature), surface roughness (increasing the density of nucleation sites), porosity (providing pre-existing gas nuclei), and wettability (regulating gas-solid interface stability), is one of the key strategies for enhancing SDT efficacy [60]. Sviridov et al. [61] synthesized mesoporous silicon NPs via electrochemical etching of crystalline silicon wafers combined with high-energy ball milling. The mesoporous morphology provided abundant sites for gas nucleus entrapment, and the cavitation energy index was several times higher than that of pure water. Under a US intensity of approximately 1 W/cm2, bacterial viability decreased sharply, while the NPs themselves were almost non-toxic at the studied concentration of about 1 mg/mL. Wang et al. [62] synthesized piezoelectric materials in three morphologies—BTO nanofibers (abbreviated as NPs), BTO nanocubes (NCs) and BTO nanorods (NRs)—via a two‑step hydrothermal method. The band structures of the three morphologies were mapped using UV–vis spectroscopy combined with Tauc plot analysis and ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (Fig. 2a). The results revealed that BTO NPs exhibited a narrower bandgap (3.09 eV) than BTO NCs (3.22 eV) and BTO NRs (3.11 eV). Combined with transient photocurrent response and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy measurements, these findings further confirmed that BTO NPs possessed the optimal e−-h⁺ separation efficiency and the lowest charge‑transfer resistance. In piezoelectric catalytic antibacterial experiments under US alone (Fig. 2a), BTO NPs achieved a bactericidal rate of 90.17% against E. coli, significantly outperforming BTO NRs (81.90%) and BTO NCs (76.72%). Ma et al. [63] constructed an urchin‑like copper‑based metalloporphyrin liposomal nanosystem (FA–L–CuPP). This urchin‑like morphology endows the material with a high specific surface area and a rough surface, which favor heterogeneous nucleation of cavitation bubbles under US. Molecular orbital distribution calculations revealed that strong intramolecular charge transfer is likely to occur in the CuPP complex under US irradiation, providing sufficient energy to the surrounding O2 and H2O for the generation of multiple ROS, including 1O2, ·OH, and O2−·.

For the piezoelectric effect, its physical essence lies in the mechano-electric conversion of non-centrosymmetric materials under ultrasonic mechanical stress, following the piezoelectric constitutive equation P=dij∙ε, where P is the polarization intensity, dij is the piezoelectric strain coefficient and ε is the strain [64]. In a US field, the energy first acts on the liquid medium and is then transferred to the NPs via viscous forces. At the nanoscale, a reduction in particle size can enhance the coupling efficiency with the surrounding medium, allowing more efficient conversion of mechanical US energy into lattice strain, which in turn strengthens the piezoelectric polarization field and promotes e−/h⁺ separation. However, as the particle size continues to decrease, the dij itself may become size-dependent and eventually vanish due to structural phase transitions. This interplay between enhanced ε and reduced dij gives rise to a nuanced, size-dependent evolution of piezoelectricity. It is well established that ferroelectric order in dense nanoceramics is suppressed below a critical grain size due to domain wall pinning and lattice distortion. For instance, Zhao et al. [65] prepared dense nanocrystalline BaTiO3 ceramics with grain sizes of 30, 50 and 100 nm and found that ferroelectricity was completely lost when the grain size was reduced to 10–30 nm. Similarly, Roelofs et al. [66] fabricated lead titanate nanocrystals ranging from 15 to 200 nm and observed no piezoelectric response signal for grains smaller than 20 nm, indicating a transition from the ferroelectric (non‑centrosymmetric) to the superparaelectric (centrosymmetric) phase, with a critical size of approximately 4–14 nm. Importantly, the disappearance of ferroelectricity does not necessarily imply the complete loss of piezoelectricity, as the latter originates primarily from strain‑induced lattice deformation rather than domain switching. Indeed, Xu et al. [67] fabricated ultrasmall BaTiO3 NPs with a particle size of approximately 6.7 nm and integrated them into a polyvinyl alcohol-agarose hydrogel. Under US irradiation, the nanoscale size morphology significantly enhanced the piezoelectric built-in electric field, promoting effective separation of e−-h⁺ pairs. H2O and O2 were catalyzed by these charge carriers to generate ROS, achieving over 90% inactivation of S. aureus within 10 minutes in vitro (Fig. 2b). These studies suggest that, beyond morphological and roughness regulation, the piezoelectric polarization field induced by US strain can be maximized by tuning particle size at the nanoscale; however, particular attention should be paid to identifying the optimal size range that maintains the non‑centrosymmetric crystal phase while minimizing particle size to maximize coupling efficiency [68]. In addition, Sahiner et al. [69] investigated BaTiO3 NPs of three different sizes (50, 200 and 500 nm) by comparing their piezoelectric output and antibacterial activity, and found that the 50 nm particles generated the highest piezoelectric voltage, yet their antibacterial activity was effective only against Gram‑negative bacteria. After surface modification with polyethyleneimine (PEI), the isoelectric point of PEI@BaTiO3 increased from pH 3–4 to approximately pH 10, conferring broad‑spectrum antibacterial capability, with MIC values reduced by about four‑fold compared with unmodified particles. This study confirmed that, within the tested range, 50 nm exhibited the optimal piezoelectric performance for BaTiO3, but also demonstrated that piezoelectric performance is not the sole determinant of antibacterial efficacy; surface charge state exerts an equally important influence on bactericidal activity.

In summary, morphological engineering enhances ROS production by regulating the particle size, surface roughness, porosity, etc., of sonosensitizers, thereby improving SDT efficacy. Studies have shown that sonosensitizers with nanosheet (NS), nanorod, hollow, or mesoporous morphologies exhibit higher specific surface areas and richer surface-active sites, thus reducing the probability of e−/h⁺ recombination. However, morphological engineering strategies also face numerous challenges in practical applications. First, the controllable preparation of nanomaterials with complex morphologies still needs improvement. Second, the mechanism by which morphology influences sonocatalytic performance lacks systematic theoretical models for guidance, and the matching relationship between different morphological materials and US parameters requires further exploration. Moreover, nanomaterials with high specific surface areas may pose risks of non-specific adsorption and potential long-term toxicity in vivo. Therefore, this review synthesizes that future research directions should focus on: (1) developing green and scalable synthetic processes to achieve precise morphological control; (2) establishing a theoretical framework for the structure-activity relationship linking morphology, band structure, and sonocatalytic activity; and (3) exploring biodegradable morphological engineering strategies to reduce the risk of long-term retention in the body while ensuring therapeutic efficacy.

Defect engineering

According to solid-state band theory, only when the photon energy generated by US excitation exceeds the band gap can electrons be excited to the CB, leaving holes in the VB to form e−-h⁺ pairs. Therefore, the efficiency of e−-h⁺ separation, as well as their recombination rate, greatly influences the efficiency of ROS generation. To address this, defect engineering has been proposed as a strategy to enhance sonocatalytic activity. Defect engineering is a materials design strategy that involves the controllable introduction, modulation or repair of defects—such as oxygen vacancies, which can act as electron traps to capture photo‑ or sono‑generated electrons, effectively suppress e−-h⁺ recombination and prolong carrier lifetime—into the crystal lattice of a material, thereby systematically altering its physicochemical properties (including electronic structure, band alignment, carrier dynamics and surface activity) [70]. At its core lies the purposeful introduction of atomic‑scale lattice defects to modulate the band structure of semiconductor materials, thereby optimizing carrier excitation and separation [71]. On the basis of this tuning strategy, the present review classifies defect engineering into two categories: intrinsic point defects and extrinsic point defects.

Intrinsic point defects are defects generated within a crystal lattice by thermal motion of lattice atoms or external energy inputs (such as high‑energy irradiation, quenching and mechanical deformation), and primarily include anion vacancies and cation vacancies. Their defining feature is that they do not alter the overall chemical composition of the material. Notably, intrinsic point defects are not limited to those produced under thermodynamic equilibrium via thermal motion; they can also be introduced by non‑equilibrium means. In sonosensitizer design, oxygen vacancies and sulfur vacancies are of particular interest, because they can effectively suppress carrier recombination by capturing electrons and serve as reactive sites. With respect to anion vacancies, oxygen vacancies, as a representative example, can introduce local defect levels within the semiconductor bandgap that act as donor levels, effectively narrowing the bandgap and thereby enhancing the efficiency of US energy absorption by the material [72]. Dong et al. [73] synthesized non‑stoichiometric TiO1+x NRs via a hydrothermal method and conjugated them to the surface of engineered E. coli. By tuning the Ti/O ratio, they introduced oxygen vacancies into the crystal lattice. These vacancies act as e−-h⁺ trapping centers, significantly suppressing carrier recombination, which endows TiO1+x with substantially higher sonocatalytic activity than pristine TiO2. Under US excitation, the efficient generation of ROS enables precise regulation of bacterial abundance by inhibiting the uncontrolled proliferation of E. coli. Liu et al. [7] significantly narrowed the band gap of ZnCuW layered double hydroxide (ZnCuW-LDH) from 3.29 eV to 1.80 eV via acid etching treatment, substantially improving the material's ultrasonic energy absorption efficiency. However, the introduction of defect levels into semiconductor materials has a dual impact on their photocatalytic performance. On the one hand, an appropriate amount of surface defects or doping levels can act as shallow trapping centers, temporarily capturing photo‑generated electrons or holes and slightly delaying their recombination, thereby prolonging the effective carrier lifetime and promoting surface catalytic reactions [74]. On the other hand, excessive bulk defects can act as Shockley–Read–Hall (SRH) recombination centers, accelerating carrier recombination and shortening the carrier lifetime. This effect can be described by the relationship between the carrier recombination lifetime τ and the defect concentration Nd: 1/τ=1/τ0+k∙Nd, where τ0 is the intrinsic recombination lifetime and k is the defect-assisted recombination coefficient [75]. Thus, defect concentration plays a critical role in modulating catalytic performance: at relatively low concentrations, the beneficial effect of promoting charge separation dominates, and performance improves with increasing defect density; beyond a certain threshold, however, SRH recombination takes over, leading to a decline in performance. This competitive mechanism dictates the existence of an optimal defect concentration—the optimum balance between enhancing charge separation and suppressing recombination [76]. He et al. [77] demonstrated that modulating the oxygen vacancy concentration in BaTiO3 (VO-BaTiO3) via thermal reduction significantly enhanced charge density and ROS yield under US irradiation, with the BTO-400 sample treated at 400 ℃ exhibiting optimal piezocatalytic activity and sonodynamic antibacterial performance (Fig. 2c). Sulfur vacancies represent another important category of intrinsic point defects, particularly garnering attention in layered transition metal dichalcogenides. Chen et al. [78] constructed a sulfur-vacancy and boron-loaded MoS2 (VS-B/MoS2) catalyst through a dual-defect strategy combining boron doping with sulfur vacancies. The synergistic effect of sulfur vacancies and boron doping disrupted the centrosymmetric structure of MoS2, achieving a piezoelectric coefficient of 35.1 pC N−1, which is 6.27 times higher than that of VS- MoS2 containing only sulfur vacancies, and exhibited significant bactericidal performance against E. coli upon ultrasonic activation.

Extrinsic point defects are generated by the introduction of foreign impurity atoms or ions. Compared with intrinsic defects, they offer more precise controllability and are mainly employed in two forms: metal doping and non‑metal doping. Metal doping (e.g., with Cu, Fe, Co, Zr, etc.) can synergistically enhance sonocatalytic activity through two mechanisms. First, the dopant atoms induce lattice distortion and local polarization, thereby strengthening the piezoelectric response of the material. Second, doping is accompanied by the introduction of oxygen vacancies, which further suppress e−/h⁺ recombination [79]. Wu et al. [8] introduced extrinsic point defects into BaTiO3 by Cu doping: Cu2+ partially substitutes for Ti4+ sites. On one hand, the ionic radius mismatch induces lattice distortion that enhances the piezoelectric response; on the other hand, to maintain charge balance, a large number of oxygen vacancies are introduced as intrinsic point defects. These oxygen vacancies act as electron traps, effectively capturing electrons generated by US excitation and thereby significantly suppressing e−/h⁺ recombination. Han et al. [80] introduced extrinsic point defects into non‑piezoelectric brookite TiO2 by Ba2+ doping. Substitution of Ba2+ for Ti4+ causes pronounced lattice distortion owing to the ionic radius mismatch, inducing polarization in the originally centrosymmetric TiO2 lattice and thus imparting piezoelectricity to the material from scratch. Meanwhile, Ba2+ doping also introduces oxygen vacancies that promote charge separation. This doping‑induced piezoelectrification strategy demonstrates that extrinsic point defects can convert materials lacking intrinsic piezoelectric activity into efficient piezocatalysts. As a result, the material achieved efficient ROS generation during piezocatalytic processes and attained a 100% antibacterial rate against E. coli at low doses. Regarding nonmetal element doping (e.g., S, N, C, etc.), dopant atoms typically substitute oxygen sites in the lattice, and the introduced impurity energy levels can alter the position of the VB maximum or CB minimum of the material, thereby modulating its redox potential. Lei et al. [81] introduced non‑metal extrinsic point defects into BaTiO3 by sulfur doping: S2− partially substitutes for O2− sites. On one hand, the narrowing of the bandgap lowers the energy threshold required for US excitation; on the other hand, the introduction of an appropriate amount of oxygen vacancies increases local lattice asymmetry, thereby enhancing the piezoelectric response. The narrowed bandgap and enhanced piezoelectric response act synergistically to promote e−/h⁺ separation efficiency. At the optimal doping level, the material achieved an antibacterial efficiency of 97.12% against S. aureus. (Fig. 2d).

In summary, intrinsic point defects—exemplified by oxygen and sulfur vacancies—improve the intrinsic sonocatalytic activity and charge separation efficiency of materials by modulating the band structure and providing reactive sites. Extrinsic point defects, such as metal and non‑metal dopants, enhance sonosensitivity and multifunctional synergistic therapeutic effects through the introduction of impurity energy levels. However, a comparison of their optimization effects reveals that intrinsic defects, while directly enhancing sonodynamic performance, offer relatively limited precision and stability in regulation. Extrinsic defects, although enabling more versatile functional modulation, alter the overall chemical composition of the material and may introduce non‑radiative recombination centers or raise biosafety concerns. Each approach offers distinct advantages, and in practice, they are often employed in combination in sonosensitizer design to achieve comprehensively improved performance. In summary, defect engineering modulates the band structure of sonosensitizers through the introduction of oxygen vacancies, sulfur vacancies, and element doping, thereby improving e−-h⁺ separation efficiency and prolonging carrier lifetime, which in turn increases ROS production and enhances antibacterial efficiency. Nevertheless, it should be noted that the type and concentration of defects have a significant impact on catalytic efficiency, and thus precise control of defect density still requires in-depth investigation [82]. Therefore, this review proposes that future research should focus on establishing quantitative structure-activity relationship models linking defect type and density to sonocatalytic activity, as well as developing in situ and dynamic defect characterization techniques, so as to achieve precise defect regulation and real-time monitoring of therapeutic efficacy.

Phase engineering and interface engineering

Phase engineering is a strategy to enhance sonodynamic antibacterial performance by modulating different crystalline phases or crystalline/amorphous states of sonosensitizers with the same chemical composition [85]. For piezoelectric materials, crystalline phases with non-centrosymmetric structures (e.g., tetragonal and rhombohedral phases) generate polarization electric fields due to lattice distortion under US, thereby driving e−-h⁺ separation. In contrast, the piezoelectric response of centrosymmetric phases (e.g., cubic phase) approaches zero. Therefore, researchers tend to select or construct non-centrosymmetric crystalline phases of piezoelectric materials to enhance sonocatalytic activity. Wu et al. [86] subjected a non-centrosymmetric potassium bismuth titanate (KBTO) piezoelectric nanocatalyst to ferroelectric polarization engineering to obtain PKBTO, introducing lattice strain and widening the band gap. Compared with the non-polarized control, PKBTO showed a 260.2% increase in 1O2 yield under US excitation. Further loading with the antimicrobial peptide Brilacidin tetrahydrochloride (BriTE） yielded BKBTO, which achieved an in vitro bactericidal efficiency exceeding 95% against multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (MDR P. aeruginosa). For transition metal dichalcogenides, taking MoS2 as an example, the 1T phase of MoS2 exhibits an octahedral coordination structure, possessing both metallic conductivity and strong piezoelectricity; whereas the 2H phase features a trigonal prismatic coordination structure and is a semiconducting phase. In sonodynamic antibacterial applications, the metallic-phase MoS2, due to its high electrical conductivity and stronger ultrasonic energy absorption efficiency, significantly outperforms the 2H phase MoS2 in US-induced ROS generation. Although the 2H phase MoS2 has relatively lower sonocatalytic activity, its semiconducting nature provides unique band engineering advantages in photo-sonodynamic combination therapy [87]. Zhao et al. [83] prepared multiphasic molybdenum disulfide NSs with sulfur defects (1T/2H MoS2−x NSs) through a synergistic strategy combining phase engineering and defect engineering. They found that the material exhibited optimal piezocatalytic activity when the 1T phase content was 35.2%, achieving complete inactivation of 6.52 log10 CFU/mL of ampicillin-resistant E. coli within 35 minutes, whereas conventional 2H phase MoS2 only achieved an inactivation effect of 2.61 log10 CFU/mL, indicating that the formation of 1T/2H heterophase junctions significantly improved charge separation efficiency and ROS yield (Fig. 2e). Modulation of crystalline/amorphous states has recently become a research hotspot in the field of sonocatalysis. For non-piezoelectric materials, constructing crystalline/amorphous heterophase structures can also create a built-in electric field via interfacial band offset, directly driving charge separation and thus enhancing sonocatalytic activity without relying on piezoelectricity [88]. Furthermore, the built-in electric field at heterophase junctions can convert O2 into O2−· through the electron reduction pathway or generate O2 through the water oxidation pathway, alleviating the hypoxic microenvironment at the infection site [88]. Meng et al. [89] constructed two-dimensional lateral anatase-rutile TiO2 phase junctions, where the two crystalline phases form an atomic-level heterophase interface within the same NS. Due to differences in work function and band alignment between anatase and rutile, a built-in electric field directed from anatase to rutile is generated at the interface, which promotes e−-h⁺ separation and efficiently generates O2 via the water oxidation reaction, achieving approximately 2.4 times the O2 evolution performance of pristine anatase TiO2 NSs.

Unlike phase engineering, which focuses on modulating different crystalline phases of the same chemical composition or the heterophase interfaces between different crystalline phases of the same material, the core of interface engineering lies in constructing different types of functional interfaces to generate built-in electric fields. In sonodynamic catalysis, heterojunctions and Schottky junctions are the core strategies of interface engineering. A heterojunction is formed when two semiconductor materials with distinct band structures are brought into contact. Owing to the difference in their Fermi levels, electrons diffuse from the higher to the lower energy level, creating a space‑charge region at the interface and inducing band bending. This band bending drives US‑generated electrons and holes to migrate in opposite directions, thereby effectively suppressing their recombination [90]. On the basis of the relative band alignment (i.e., the positions of the conduction and VBs) of the two semiconductors, heterojunctions are classified into three main types: Type Ⅰ, Type Ⅱ and Z‑scheme. In a Type Ⅰ heterojunction, the conduction and VB edges of the two semiconductors exhibit a nested configuration. Both photo‑generated electrons and holes migrate to the narrower‑bandgap material, confining the carriers within the same material and leading to low separation efficiency, which is generally unfavorable for interfacial catalytic reactions [91]. To date, Type Ⅰ heterojunctions have received relatively little attention in the context of sonocatalytic antibacterial applications, whereas Type Ⅱ and Z‑scheme heterojunctions have become research foci owing to their more efficient charge separation capabilities. In a Type Ⅱ heterojunction, the conduction and VBs of the two semiconductors assume a staggered configuration. Under US excitation, electrons migrate from the material with a more negative CB edge to that with a more positive CB edge, while holes migrate in the opposite direction, thereby achieving spatial e−-h⁺ separation pair and effectively suppressing carrier recombination [92]. Liu et al. [92] prepared a La2(WO4)3/CuWO4 Type Ⅱ heterojunction via a two-step hydrothermal method. Under US excitation, electrons migrated from La2(WO4)3 to CuWO4, while holes migrated in the opposite direction, significantly suppressing e−-h⁺ recombination. However, the spatial charge separation in Type Ⅱ heterojunctions comes at the cost of reduced redox potentials: electrons migrate to the material with weaker reducing capability, while holes migrate to the material with weaker oxidizing capability. Z‑scheme heterojunctions, by contrast, preserve both the strong reducing and strong oxidizing capabilities of the two semiconductors through an interfacial recombination mechanism. The charge transfer pathway follows a Z‑shaped pattern: electrons from the CB of material A recombine with holes from the VB of material B at the interface, leaving material A with strongly oxidizing holes in its VB and material B with strongly reducing electrons in its CB [93]. For example, the Cu-MOF/TiO2 Z-scheme heterojunction prepared by Liu et al. [94] exhibited high levels of O2−· and ·OH under US irradiation, and photoluminescence spectroscopy showed that carrier recombination was significantly suppressed. Both S‑scheme heterojunctions and Z‑scheme heterojunctions achieve the coexistence of strong oxidizing and strong reducing capabilities through interfacial charge recombination. However, S‑scheme heterojunctions place greater emphasis on the driving role of the built‑in electric field and band bending at the interface in governing charge transfer, and can thus be regarded as a specific manifestation of Z‑scheme heterojunctions in solid‑state interfacial systems [95]. Wei et al. [95] fabricated hollow BaTiO3 NPs via template etching and in situ assembled zinc tetrakis (4-carboxyphenyl)porphyrin (ZnTCPP) to form an S-scheme heterojunction. Both experimental and theoretical evidence demonstrated that the US-triggered piezoelectric polarization field enhanced the interfacial electric field and band bending. In addition to the above heterojunctions based on band alignment, if piezoelectric materials are further introduced, the built-in electric field can synergize with the piezopotential generated by the piezoelectric effect under US excitation, jointly driving efficient charge separation and directional migration. Wang et al. [84] prepared a metal-deficient TiO2 with rich oxygen vacancies and BaTiO3 heterojunction (DTO/BTO). The oxygen vacancies narrowed the band gap of TiO2, while the unpaired spin electrons of Ti atoms induced a spin polarization effect that accelerated the migration of ultrasonic piezoelectric charges in BTO and enhanced the surface sonocatalytic efficiency, achieving 99.82% killing of S. aureus and inhibiting biofilm formation under US irradiation (Fig. 2f). In addition, the combination of piezoelectric materials with MOFs can also construct heterojunctions. Pan et al. [96] designed a MoS2@pCu-MOF type Ⅱ heterojunction nanocomposite scaffold (where pCu-MOF stands for phosphate-based Cu-MOF). By promoting e−-h⁺ separation, it generated ·OH and 1O2 independent of H2O2 levels under US excitation. Furthermore, interfaces rich in nanopores or featuring special topological structures can act as gas nucleation sites, significantly lowering the cavitation threshold and enhancing cavitation effects[95]. This physical mechanism complements the chemical mechanisms based on bandgap engineering and piezoelectric effects, and together they synergistically enhance the therapeutic outcome of sonocatalysis.

In addition, when a metal contacts a semiconductor, the difference between the work function of the metal and the electron affinity of the semiconductor leads to the formation of a Schottky barrier at the interface. Under US, sonogenerated electrons are rapidly injected into the metal, while holes remain in the semiconductor VB, achieving spatial separation of electrons and holes [97]. Noble metal deposition (e.g., Au, Ru, etc.) not only forms Schottky junctions but also exploits the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) effect to enhance sonocatalytic performance. The excitation of this effect relies on resonant coupling between sonoluminescence‑generated photons and free electrons in the metal. Specifically, Boyd et al. [98], through bubble dynamics modelling combined with finite‑difference time‑domain optical simulations, predicted that sonoluminescence photons can resonantly couple with ultraviolet plasmonic modes of metals, providing a theoretical basis for SPR excitation by sonoluminescence. Furthermore, Park et al. [99], using in situ Bragg coherent X‑ray diffraction imaging, experimentally demonstrated that under 532 nm light irradiation (corresponding to the SPR absorption peak of Au), the SPR effect in Au/TiO2 heterojunctions significantly enhances ROS generation and thereby amplifies the catalytic outcome. In addition, Huang et al. [100], in their study on the TiO2–Au sonosensitizer system, explicitly noted that the SPR effect of Au can amplify the local electromagnetic field and enhance the US responsiveness. However, the aforementioned studies have each independently verified only fragments of the overall physical picture—from sonoluminescence to SPR‑amplified catalysis—and direct experimental evidence for the complete mechanism remains lacking. In particular, the transient processes of SPR excitation in noble metals by US and the spectral matching efficiency warrant further in‑depth investigation. Moreover, the enzyme-like activities of noble metals (e.g., CAT-like activity) can catalyze the decomposition of H2O2 to generate O2, effectively overcoming the limitation of the hypoxic environment at the infection site on ROS generation [101]. Yuwen et al. [102] developed a MnCO3-Au Schottky heterojunction sonosensitizer. The Schottky junction formed at the Mn-Au interface promoted the separation of US-excited e−-h+ pair and suppressed carrier recombination, achieving a bacterial inactivation efficiency of 99.92% in a mouse model of implant-associated S. aureus biofilm infection, while MnCO3 gradually degraded in the acidic infection microenvironment, exhibiting good biocompatibility. Ma et al. [103] constructed a Schottky heterojunction by combining oxygen‑vacancy‑rich BaTiO3 (BTOv) with ruthenium (Ru), denoted as BTOv‑Ru, achieving efficient electron injection from BTOv to Ru and hole retention in BTOv, while the CAT-like activity of Ru catalyzed H2O2 to generate O2 for self-supplied oxygen.

In summary, phase engineering and interface engineering enhance sonocatalytic activity from two dimensions: the intracrystalline lattice and the external interface, respectively. In practical applications, phase engineering modulates the band structure and carrier migration characteristics of the material itself to drive e−-h⁺ separation, while interface engineering guides the directional migration of carriers and effectively suppresses their recombination through the construction of heterojunctions and Schottky junctions. The two approaches complement each other to achieve favorable therapeutic outcomes in aSDT [104]. In view of the high-temperature and high-pressure conditions required for the selective synthesis of crystalline phases, as well as the need for precise control of heterojunction interfacial contact and band alignment, future research should focus on developing phase modulation synthesis methods under mild conditions to reduce preparation complexity and energy consumption, and explore high-throughput screening technologies to improve the efficiency of screening crystal phase-interface combinations and identify optimal combinations. In addition, synergistic strategies combining phase engineering and interface engineering should be actively explored to further enhance the catalytic efficiency of sonosensitizers.

Novel synergistic enhancement strategies

Currently, SDT, owing to its deep tissue penetration, low invasiveness, and non-antibiotic dependence, has become a powerful tool against bacterial infections, especially drug-resistant bacteria and biofilm-associated infections. However, the hypoxic infection microenvironment and the excessive inflammatory response that may be induced by ROS while killing bacteria mean that focusing solely on optimizing the structure of sonosensitizers has certain limitations in improving the antibacterial efficacy of SDT. In recent years, researchers have begun to pay more attention to the synergistic enhancement of multifunctional material systems, constructing novel platforms that can actively modulate the infection microenvironment and achieve multifunctional synergistic antibacterial effects by integrating sonosensitizers with carriers, catalysts, or functional molecules possessing specific biological functions. This section mainly summarizes three representative directions: (1) compounding inorganic nanosonosensitizers with hydrogel delivery systems, utilizing the injectability, bioadhesivity, and US-responsive degradability of hydrogels to achieve on-demand release of sonosensitizers at the infection site; (2) introducing nanozyme catalytic systems to synergize with SDT to enhance ROS generation efficiency by mimicking the catalytic activities of natural enzymes (e.g., CAT-like, peroxidase-like, etc.); (3) using MOFs as carriers to load functional molecules, leveraging their tunable pore sizes to coordinate ROS generation with the release of functional molecules, thereby achieving synergistic antibacterial effects between SDT and functional molecules.

Construction of inorganic nanomaterial-hydrogel delivery systems

Although conventional inorganic nanosonosensitizers exhibit good sonocatalytic activity and stability, they still have certain limitations in antibacterial applications, such as lack of active targeting and poor biocompatibility [49]. To address these issues, researchers have encapsulated inorganic nanosonosensitizers in hydrogel matrices to construct US-responsive local delivery systems [105]. Such systems typically consist of two components: a polymer hydrogel backbone with a three-dimensional network structure (e.g., chitosan, gelatin, alginate, etc.) and inorganic nanosonosensitizers dispersed in the gel [106]. The hydrogel achieves efficient loading of sonosensitizers through physical entrapment and electrostatic adsorption. After injection, it forms a local depot at the infection site, prolonging the retention time of the sonosensitizers. In addition, the hydrogel backbone can degrade in response to the infection microenvironment or external US stimulation, enabling on-demand release of sonosensitizers and auxiliary drugs [107]. Lao et al. [105] constructed a sonosensitizer–hydrogel platform by physically mixing ytterbium (Yb3+)-doped Bi2WO6 NSs (BWO‑x%Yb NSs) with a poly(vinyl alcohol)-based hydrogel matrix. The three‑dimensional porous network structure of the hydrogel provides a uniformly dispersed loading environment for the BWO NSs, effectively preventing NS aggregation, while its hydrophilic framework facilitates the absorption and exchange of wound exudate. Under US irradiation, the BWO‑x%Yb NSs embedded in the hydrogel efficiently generate ROS through a sonodynamic process, achieving a 100% clearance rate against MRSA and E. coli. Meanwhile, the hydrogel matrix can be gradually degraded in response to pH changes in the infected microenvironment or to US stimulation, enabling on‑demand release and metabolism of the sonosensitizer. Zhang et al. [108] developed an injectable thermoresponsive hydrogel based on ultrathin high‑entropy hydrotalcites (UHE‑HTs), which physically encapsulated nicotinamide mononucleotide (PFN). Upon injection, the hydrogel undergoes a sol–gel phase transition at body temperature, forming an in situ local drug reservoir. In the early stage of infection, US triggers UHE‑HTs to rapidly generate 1O2 to kill drug‑resistant bacteria; after US cessation, the hydrogel scaffold clears residual ROS from the wound through its oxido‑reductase‑like catalytic activity (Fig. 3). Luo et al. [109] incorporated gold nanoparticle‑decorated barium titanate (BaTiO3@Au) piezoelectric NPs into a thermoresponsive double‑network hydrogel via physical doping. This hydrogel endows the system with temperature‑sensitive softening, on‑demand thermal shrinkage, high mechanical strength and favorable tissue adhesion. US‑triggered sonopiezoelectric effects generate a polarization electric field in BaTiO3, producing ROS on demand to eliminate bacteria; concurrently, the hydrogel undergoes volume shrinkage under near‑infrared laser irradiation, actively drawing the wound edges together.

Nanozyme catalytic systems

Limited by the hypoxic state of the infection microenvironment and the limited endogenous H2O2 content, conventional inorganic nanosonosensitizer-mediated SDT generates limited ROS, and high concentrations of GSH further consume the ROS produced. Nanozymes, an emerging class of functional materials that have received extensive attention in recent years, can specifically overcome the above bottlenecks. Nanozymes are nanomaterials with natural enzyme-mimicking catalytic activities. Upon US excitation, they can act as sonosensitizers to mediate SDT for ROS generation while exerting multiple enzyme-like catalytic effects, such as CAT-like, peroxidase-like, and GSH oxidase-like activities, actively modulating O2 supply, H2O2 conversion, and GSH consumption in the infection microenvironment, thereby synergistically enhancing ROS generation efficiency [110], [111]. Specifically, the peroxidase-like activity of nanozymes converts H2O2 into ·OH, and the CAT-like activity catalytically decomposes endogenous H2O2 in the infection microenvironment into O2, thereby alleviating hypoxia and continuously supplying substrates for SDT [112]. Moreover, nanozymes containing metals such as Mn, Fe, and Cu exhibit GSH oxidase-like activity, which disrupts bacterial redox homeostasis through GSH depletion, synergistically amplifying the killing effect of SDT [113]. More importantly, nanozymes with sonocatalytic activity can also achieve improved e−-h⁺ separation efficiency through interface engineering, and synergize with their enzyme-like catalytic effects to form a synergistic interaction between enzymatic catalysis and sonocatalysis under US excitation, achieving efficient amplification of ROS. Zheng et al. [114] designed and synthesized a US‑activatable bimetallic PtRu alloy nanozyme (PR) with a Pt/Ru atomic ratio of 80:20, which was predicted and optimized through density functional theory (DFT) calculations. This nanozyme exhibits both oxidase‑like and peroxidase‑like activities. On the one hand, its peroxidase‑like activity catalyses the conversion of H2O2 into ·OH for chemodynamic therapy (CDT). On the other hand, under US irradiation, interfacial defects in the alloy promote the e−-h⁺ separation, synergistically generating ·OH and 1O2. This synergy between sonodynamic and chemodynamic processes enables precise and spatiotemporally controllable ROS generation. Sun et al. [106] conjugated polydopamine‑modified Prussian blue nanocages (PPCN) with Pt nanozymes and antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), and loaded the resulting complex into a gelatin/alginate hydrogel (HGel). The CAT‑like activity of the Pt nanozymes catalyses the decomposition of H2O2 into O2, effectively alleviating the hypoxic microenvironment of diabetic infected wounds; subsequently, US‑induced 1O2 achieves efficient sonodynamic sterilization under enhanced O2 supply (Fig. 4). In addition, some MOFs also possess enzyme-like catalytic activities, capable of catalyzing the conversion of endogenous H2O2 into oxygen, thereby alleviating hypoxia in the infection microenvironment, or directly catalyzing ROS generation using the multienzyme activities of metal ions in the MOF framework. Su et al. [21] designed biomimetic metal–organic framework (PCN‑224)‑coated MnO2 hydrangea‑like NPs (MnPM). Upon reaching the acidic biofilm microenvironment rich in H2O2, the MnO2 component converts H2O2 into O2, providing an ample oxygen substrate for US‑triggered SDT and thereby enhancing the generation of 1O2.

Metal-organic framework carrier systems

As mentioned above, to address the issues of low e−-h⁺ separation efficiency and the tendency for rapid e−-h⁺ recombination pair in most inorganic nanosonosensitizers, strategies such as morphological engineering, defect engineering, and heterojunction construction are commonly employed to enhance the catalytic activity of sonosensitizers. With increasing research on MOFs, it has been found that MOFs, as crystalline porous coordination polymers self-assembled from metal ions/clusters and organic ligands through coordination bonds, possess large specific surface areas, high porosity, structural tunability, and good biocompatibility. They can serve as ideal nanocarriers for loading sonosensitizers and auxiliary functional molecules. Their pore structures can efficiently load antibiotics or natural antibacterial molecules, and synergize with strategies for optimizing the intrinsic structure of sonosensitizers to achieve enhanced antibacterial effects through both ROS generation and drug action under US irradiation [115]. Zhu et al. [116] constructed a BTO@ZIF‑8/CIP piezoelectric nanoplatform, in which BTO serves as the core to provide piezoelectric effects, while ZIF‑8 acts as the shell to load CIP. Under US irradiation, the BTO–ZIF‑8 heterostructure enhances piezocatalytic ROS generation. Meanwhile, the acidic degradation of ZIF‑8 releases CIP for synergistic bactericidal action and Zn2+ to promote tissue repair. As ZIF‑8 degrades, ROS production gradually decreases from a high to a low level, enabling dynamic regulation of the therapeutic process. In addition to organic sonosensitizers, the encapsulation through MOF pores also enables efficient loading of antibiotics, antimicrobial peptides, iodine, and natural antibacterial drugs, along with pH-responsive release and synergistic antibacterial effects. Ávila-Márquez et al. [117] constructed a MIL‑53 MOF from Fe3+ and terephthalate, and achieved efficient loading of penicillin G through multiple interactions with the MOF channels and Fe sites, including coordination, π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding. The system exhibits first‑order kinetic controlled release via Fickian diffusion in PBS, and shows excellent inhibitory activity against a broad range of sensitive and penicillin‑resistant uropathogenic strains. Xu et al. [118] encapsulated artemisia argyi essential oil (AAEO) into ZIF‑8 MOF. The porous structure of the MOF protects the natural antibacterial components, effectively addressing the issues of poor water solubility and high volatility of the essential oil. In the acidic microenvironment of bacterial infected wounds, the ZIF‑8 framework dissociates and releases AAEO along with Zn2+. AAEO disrupts bacterial membrane integrity, while Zn2+ acts synergistically to exert antibacterial effects, achieving pH‑responsive on‑demand release. Gao et al. [119] constructed a biomimetic neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticle system (NM@PCN-TIG) using porphyrin-based MOF (PCN-224) as a carrier for tigecycline. Under US irradiation, ROS were generated via SDT and the antibiotic was released on demand, effectively clearing extensively drug-resistant bacteria (Fig. 5). Wang et al. [120] constructed a HA@PCN‑CLT system by coating hyaluronic acid (HA) onto porphyrinic MOF (PCN‑224) and loading celastrol (CLT). The HA shell prevents premature leakage of CLT, and upon degradation by hyaluronidase overexpressed in MRSA biofilms, the surface charge switches from negative to positive, facilitating penetration of the system through the biofilm. Meanwhile, the acidic microenvironment promotes the disassembly of the PCN framework, enabling on‑demand release of CLT. Under US irradiation, the system generates 1O2 for SDT, while the physical effects of US accelerate CLT release and diffusion. These two mechanisms act synergistically to achieve efficient eradication of both planktonic and biofilm‑embedded MRSA. Furthermore, MOFs can serve as ideal platforms for enzyme immobilization, protecting the structural stability of enzymes through pore encapsulation. Yu et al. [121] encapsulated CAT into ZIF‑8, which significantly improved its activity compared with the free enzyme under harsh conditions (pH 11, 50 °C and organic solvents), and subsequently grew Ag NPs in situ on the surface. The resulting composite exhibited potent antibacterial activity against E. coli, S. aureus and P. aeruginosa through the synergistic effects of enzymatic catalysis and Ag+ release, and also displayed good biocompatibility. Zhou et al. [122] constructed aptamer-synergistic multienzyme (ASCM) nanocatalysts using bimetallic CeCo MOF as a carrier loaded with copper NPs, SOD, and functionalized aptamers. The material exhibited four types of enzyme-like activities (SOD, CAT, POD, and GPx), killing bacteria and alleviating wound hypoxia by generating highly toxic ·OH and O2, achieving >99% inactivation efficiency against MRSA and P. aeruginosa.

Regarding the physical mechanism by which low‑intensity US triggers the release of cargo from MOF pores, we have surveyed recent studies and found that current research predominantly focuses on demonstrating US‑induced release, while the underlying mechanisms remain largely unexplored. For instance, Ahmed et al. [123] investigated the release behavior and therapeutic efficacy of the antitumour drug doxorubicin loaded in NH2‑Fe‑BDC under acidic tumor‑mimicking conditions and US stimulation, and demonstrated the necessity of US for drug release. Nevertheless, two hypotheses have been proposed. The first hypothesis posits that the mechanical forces generated by acoustic cavitation—including microscopic shock waves and high‑speed microjets—can directly disrupt the MOF structure or induce irreversible changes in pore size, thereby releasing the cargo. In support of this, Wan et al. [124], in their study on US‑triggered release of temozolomide from UiO‑66‑NH2 for glioblastoma therapy, explicitly attributed the release to cavitation effects. Moreover, a model study on hydrogen‑bonded organic frameworks showed that US can break non‑covalent bonds to trigger release[125]. In a study on Co‑MOF, Liu et al. [126] demonstrated that US can cleave interchain hydrogen bonds, exfoliating micron‑sized bulk crystals into NRs—direct evidence that US can irreversibly alter the size and morphology of MOFs by disrupting non‑covalent bonds (hydrogen bonds). The second hypothesis suggests that US energy can cleave specific chemical bonds between the drug and the carrier, leading to release. Sun et al. [127] thoroughly explored the adsorption mechanisms of ibuprofen and naproxen on UiO‑66 and UiO‑66‑NH2, and identified multiple weak interactions—including hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, anion–π interactions and Lewis acid–base complexation (coordination bonds)—as the main forces governing drug–MOF interactions. Combined with the finding by Liu et al. that US can cleave hydrogen bonds in Co‑MOF, it is reasonable to infer that the mechanical forces from US may similarly act on the non‑covalent bonds (e.g., hydrogen bonds, π–π stacking) between drugs and MOFs, thereby triggering on‑demand release. In addition to US‑triggered release, the release profile of MOF‑loaded drugs can also be regulated during the synthesis stage by tuning the pore size to achieve the desired release kinetics in specific physiological environments. For example, Ávila‑Márquez et al. [117] compared the release behavior of penicillin G from MIL‑53 (average pore size 10.43 nm) and a composite material with larger pores (average pore size 13.11 nm) in simulated body fluids, and found that the larger‑pore material facilitated more efficient release. In summary, current research on US‑triggered drug release from MOFs remains largely at the stage of phenomenological validation, with limited mechanistic understanding. Particularly in the context of antibacterial applications, several key issues warrant systematic and in‑depth investigation: the structure–activity relationship between US parameters and MOF architecture, the differential susceptibility of various weak interactions to US, and the synergistic interplay between US‑triggered release and SDT. We anticipate that future studies combining in situ characterization techniques with theoretical simulations will provide a more comprehensive and detailed elucidation of the underlying mechanisms.

In summary, for the performance optimization of inorganic nanosonosensitizers, morphological engineering, defect engineering, phase engineering and interface engineering, as well as novel synergistic enhancement strategies, can all effectively improve sonodynamic antibacterial efficiency. These strategies constitute a multi-level regulatory system spanning from the atomic/electronic scale to the microstructure and then to the macroscopic functional system [104]. Among them, defect engineering and phase engineering optimize the band structure and carrier behavior by altering the intrinsic electronic structure of the material [7]. Interface engineering enhances e−-h⁺ separation efficiency and prolongs carrier lifetime by constructing heterojunctions or Schottky junctions to generate built-in electric fields. Meanwhile, hydrogel delivery systems, nanozyme catalytic systems, and MOF carrier systems achieve active modulation of the microenvironment through local depots, mimicking multienzyme catalysis, or loading functional molecules, thereby synergistically enhancing the antibacterial effect of SDT [95]. Although significant progress has been made with the above strategies, in clinical applications, attention still needs to be paid to the precise regulation of ROS levels and further improvement of therapeutic selectivity. When ROS are controlled at an appropriate level, good antibacterial effects can be achieved; however, when their level is too high, they may induce oxidative stress and cause damage to normal tissues [128], [129]. Therefore, future research should focus more on how to rapidly amplify ROS activity when bacterial killing is required, while promptly reducing the concentration after sterilization to restore the redox homeostasis of the microenvironment. In addition, strategies such as optimizing low-intensity US parameters, infection microenvironment-responsive design (e.g., acidic pH, overexpressed H2O2 and enzymes), and immunomodulation should be employed to achieve treatment targeting, thereby reducing oxidative stress damage to normal tissues [130].

Applications of inorganic nanosonosensitizer-mediated SDT in infectious diseases

Skin infections and wound healing

The skin serves as the first line of defense against the invasion of exogenous pathogens. Disruption of its barrier function (e.g., extensive burns) often leads to bacterial colonization and biofilm formation, while infections with drug-resistant bacteria (especially MRSA) significantly increase patient mortality and treatment difficulty. SDT, with its non-invasive nature and strong tissue penetration, offers a new therapeutic approach for refractory burn wound infections. Of particular note, when infections spread to deep tissues and form abscesses, conventional light‑responsive or microenvironment‑responsive drug delivery systems often suffer from limited therapeutic efficacy due to inadequate tissue penetration. SDT, with its non‑invasive nature and excellent tissue penetration capability, offers a new therapeutic paradigm for refractory deep‑seated infections. Xu et al. [131] designed a US‑driven manganese‑based nanoparticle delivery system (AUR@MnOx@PEG, abbreviated as AMP) to address the pathological challenges of deep bacterial clearance and the hypoxic microenvironment that severely restricts SDT efficacy in MRSA‑infected wounds. The system utilizes the CAT‑like activity of MnOx nanoflowers to catalyse the decomposition of overexpressed H2O2 in the infected microenvironment to generate O2, effectively alleviating wound hypoxia. Meanwhile, under US irradiation, MnOx acts as a sonosensitizer to mediate SDT for efficient ROS generation, which acts synergistically with the released non‑antibiotic antibacterial agent olaquindox (AUR), thereby achieving efficient MRSA clearance and promoting wound healing (Fig. 6a, Table 1). Song et al. [132] developed a high‑entropy MXene (TVNMC) sonosensitizer composed of Ti, V, Nb, Mo and C to address the clinical challenges of deep abscess elimination and the limited efficacy of SDT alone in MRSA infections. Leveraging the broad‑spectrum absorption properties of high‑entropy MXene, the system efficiently generates ROS under US triggering for SDT‑mediated sterilization, while simultaneously producing local hyperthermia via photothermal effects under NIR‑II laser irradiation, synergistically enhancing antibacterial efficacy. The synergistic action of US and NIR‑II laser overcomes the limitation of insufficient ROS production by SDT alone in deep infections, achieving near‑complete eradication of MRSA biofilms and abscesses, effectively promoting infected wound healing, and showing no obvious side effects in mouse tissues (Table 1).

The healing of diabetic foot ulcers (DFU) is triply hindered by a hyperglycemic environment, persistent infection, and excessive oxidative stress. SDT not only effectively eliminates infection but also balances antibacterial and anti-inflammatory needs by modulating ROS levels, opening a new avenue for DFU treatment [133]. Huang et al. [128] designed a piezoelectric BiVO4/fullerene heterostructure to address the pathological features of MRSA persistent infection and excessive ROS accumulation in diabetic wounds. The system exploits the piezopolarization effect of BiVO4 to narrow the bandgap and enhance charge separation under US irradiation, enabling efficient ROS generation that achieves 99.9% MRSA elimination. Upon cessation of US, the fullerene component mediates a ROS‑scavenging function that spontaneously switches the system to an antioxidant state, neutralizing excess ROS in the wound and thereby avoiding damage to nascent tissue from excessive oxidative stress. In an MRSA‑infected diabetic wound model, this on‑demand ROS regulation strategy—actuated by a US on/off switch—effectively suppressed bacterial proliferation, alleviated inflammation and promoted angiogenesis, leading to accelerated wound closure within 14 days (Table 1). Li et al. [134] developed a lipase‑activated MnO2 nanoflower theranostic platform (Mn‑TC NPs) to address the multiple challenges of deep biofilm infection, hypoxic microenvironment severely limiting SDT efficacy, and the lack of real‑time infection monitoring in diabetic wounds. In this system, the TCPP sonosensitizer is grafted onto the surface of MnO2 nanoflowers; upon ester bond hydrolysis in the biofilm‑rich lipase microenvironment, TCPP fluorescence is restored, enabling real‑time imaging of infected wounds. Meanwhile, MnO2 catalyses the conversion of H2O2 to O2, relieving hypoxia and enhancing the sonodynamic efficacy of US‑activated TCPP. This strategy achieved a 94.0% reduction in biofilm biomass and 99.9% bacterial clearance, accelerating diabetic wound healing through biofilm eradication and angiogenesis promotion (Table 1). Sun et al. [135], addressing the clinical challenge of concurrent bacterial infection and delayed healing in diabetic wounds, demonstrated for the first time that unmodified zirconium‑based MOF (UiO‑66) can serve as a novel inorganic sonosensitizer for diabetic wound infection therapy. The study revealed that under US irradiation, UiO‑66 achieves effective e−-h⁺ separation through its wide‑bandgap structure, generating ROS to kill bacteria. Concurrently, the porous structure of UiO‑66 facilitates wound exudate absorption and gas exchange, promoting tissue repair. In a diabetic mouse model of infected wounds, UiO‑66 combined with US treatment significantly accelerated wound closure, achieving the dual functions of antibacterial therapy and tissue regeneration (Table 1).

Osteomyelitis

Osteomyelitis is an inflammatory disease of bone and bone marrow caused by pathogenic microorganisms, often accompanied by bone destruction, necrosis, and impaired new bone formation. Conventional systemic antibiotic therapy, on the one hand, limits effective drug delivery to the lesion due to compromised local blood supply, resulting in insufficient concentration; on the other hand, long-term use readily induces drug-resistant bacteria. SDT, owing to its deep tissue penetration capability, exhibits unique advantages in the treatment of deep bone infections [146]. Li et al. [22] developed a multifunctional nanoplatform integrating sonodynamic and immunomodulatory functions—TiO2–Mn–Emodin (TOMPE)—by constructing emodin (ED)-conjugated Mn‑doped TiO2 nanorod arrays on titanium surfaces, to address the triple pathological barriers in osteomyelitis: poor drug delivery due to insufficient blood supply in deep bone tissue, biofilm and abscess formation by MRSA, and local immunosuppression induced by excessive ROS in the infected microenvironment. In this system, Mn doping introduces oxygen vacancies that significantly narrow the bandgap of TiO2 and enhance e−-h⁺ separation under US excitation; meanwhile, emodin, as a natural sonosensitizer, synergistically amplifies ROS generation under US, directly disrupting bacterial membranes. More importantly, the platform activates the cAMP/cGMP–PKG signaling pathway to induce macrophage polarization towards the M1 phenotype, endowing them with robust phagocytic capacity against MRSA. In a rat model of osteomyelitis, the platform effectively eradicated MRSA biofilm infection, alleviated inflammation and promoted bone tissue repair (Fig. 6b, Table 1). Li et al. [136] designed a trehalose‑functionalized multifunctional nanoplatform (TMZ) to address the core challenges of MRSA immune evasion and post‑antibiotic recurrence in refractory osteomyelitis. The platform achieves selective recognition and efficient targeting of MRSA through trehalose functionalization. Under US irradiation, TMZ generates ROS to disrupt bacterial membranes and induce immunogenic bacterial death, releasing bacterial DNA and antigens. Simultaneously, TMZ undergoes pH‑responsive degradation in the acidic osteomyelitis microenvironment, enabling controlled release of Mn2+ ions. The released Mn2+ cooperates with bacterial components to activate the cGAS–STING pathway in dendritic cells, promoting innate immune activation, dendritic cell maturation and efficient antigen presentation, which in turn elicits pathogen‑specific adaptive immune responses and establishes durable immunological memory. In a mouse model of MRSA‑induced osteomyelitis, TMZ significantly reduced bacterial burden, activated systemic antibacterial immunity and effectively prevented recurrence, showing superior efficacy over conventional antibiotics without inducing resistance (Table 1). Zhao et al. [137] constructed a multifunctional nanoplatform, PCN‑222(Mn)–Cur, using a manganese‑based porphyrinic MOF (PCN‑222(Mn)) as the sonosensitive core and modifying its surface with curcumin (Cur), to address the intertwined pathological features of persistent bacterial infection, chronic inflammation and impaired bone regeneration in MRSA‑induced osteomyelitis. In this platform, the MnN4 active sites of PCN‑222(Mn) endow the material with dual enzyme‑mimetic activities—SOD and CAT—forming a cascade antioxidant reaction that effectively scavenges excess ROS in the infected microenvironment, alleviates oxidative stress and mitigates inflammation. Meanwhile, curcumin, as a natural sonosensitizer, mediates SDT under US irradiation to rapidly generate large amounts of ROS for efficient bacterial killing. Notably, the combination of rapid ROS burst from SDT and sustained release of curcumin—which possesses long‑lasting antibacterial activity—achieves synergistic and prolonged antibacterial effects by inhibiting regrowth of residual bacteria (Table 1).

Chronic gastritis

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) is classified as a Group I carcinogen by the World Health Organization and is a major causative factor for chronic gastritis, peptic ulcers, and gastric cancer. Conventional antibiotic-based triple/quadruple therapies face the problems of increasing drug resistance and gut microbiota dysbiosis. SDT, utilizing the non-invasive and deep-penetrating capability of US combined with targeted enrichment of sonosensitizers in the gastric mucosa, offers a novel antibiotic-independent strategy for H. pylori eradication. Fan et al. [138] designed a sonodynamic nanocomplex, PtCu3‑PDA@AIPH@Fucoidan (PPAF), to address the multiple pathological barriers in H. pylori infection, including impaired drug penetration by the gastric mucus barrier, increasing antibiotic resistance and biofilm formation that limits antibiotic efficacy. This system comprises a polydopamine (PDA)-modified inorganic sonosensitizer PtCu3, an alkyl radical generator AIPH and a targeting ligand fucoidan. The fucoidan moiety endows PPAF with good hydrophilicity and negative surface charge, facilitating penetration of the gastric mucus barrier and enabling targeting of fucoidan‑binding proteins on the surface of H. pylori. Under US stimulation, PPAF not only generates abundant ROS through PtCu3‑mediated sonodynamic processes, but also utilizes the thermal decomposition of AIPH to produce oxygen‑independent alkyl radicals, overcoming potential hypoxia in the gastric infection microenvironment. Meanwhile, the N2 generated from AIPH decomposition enhances cavitation effects, aiding PPAF NPs in penetrating the mucus layer and disrupting biofilm integrity (Fig. 6c, Table 1). Luo et al. [139] developed a pH‑responsive theranostic nanoprobe to address the clinical challenges of lacking real‑time bacterial localization and achieving spatially precise activation of SDT in H. pylori infection therapy. The probe incorporates manganese porphyrin (MnTCPP) into hollow mesoporous iron oxide NPs (HMIN) and is coated with an H. pylori‑targeting polymer. Upon acidic pH activation at the gastric infection site, MnTCPP is released from HMIN, enabling precise bacterial localization; subsequently, US triggers MnTCPP to efficiently generate 1O2, achieving spatially accurate SDT (Table 1). Yu et al. [140] designed a pH‑responsive ROS nanogenerator, Fe‑HMME@DHA@MPN, to address the multifaceted challenges of increasing antibiotic resistance, disruption of gut microbiota by conventional therapies and insufficient H2O2 in the infection microenvironment in H. pylori infection. This system consists of an acid‑responsive metal–phenolic network (MPN) shell and a mesoporous metal–organic nanostructure core (Fe‑HMME) loaded with dihydroartemisinin (DHA). Under gastric acidic conditions, the MPN shell degrades, and the released Fe(II) decomposes H2O2 in the infection microenvironment via Fenton/Fenton‑like reactions to generate O2, providing oxygen substrate for HMME‑mediated sonodynamic processes and enhancing 1O2 generation. Simultaneously, DHA, as a hydroperoxide source, enhances the peroxidase‑like activity of the nanosystem to generate ·OH, compensating for the insufficiency of H2O2 in the infection microenvironment (Table 1).

Periodontal and root canal infections

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease driven by plaque biofilms. Pathogenic bacteria—such as Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) and Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans—form structured biofilms sub-gingivally and, through the secretion of virulence factors and activation of excessive host immune responses, lead to periodontal pocket formation, progressive alveolar bone resorption and ultimately tooth loosening or loss [147]. Because bacteria within biofilms are encased in EPS and reside in a hypoxic, low‑pH microenvironment, conventional mechanical debridement and systemic antibiotic therapy often fail to completely eliminate deep‑seated pathogens and carry the risk of inducing antibiotic resistance [148]. SDT, leveraging the tissue‑penetrating capability of US and sonosensitizer‑mediated ROS generation, can effectively disrupt biofilm structures, eradicate deep‑seated pathogens and modulate local inflammatory responses, offering a promising strategy for periodontitis treatment [149]. Li et al. [141] developed a lipopolysaccharide (LPS)‑targeting piezoelectric sonosensitizer (Ti/Mn‑MOFs‑P‑L) by modifying piezoelectric bimetallic Ti/Mn‑MOF NSs with phenylboronic acid (PBA) and L‑arginine (L‑Arg), to address the pathological features of periodontitis—including the predominance of anaerobes (70%–90%) in deep periodontal pockets and the intertwined vicious cycle of infection and inflammation. The PBA moiety covalently couples to cis‑diol groups of LPS on the surface of Gram‑negative bacteria via boronate ester bonds, achieving chemical targeting and precise drug accumulation at periodontal pathogens. The 3d orbital electrons of Mn hybridize with the Ti–O framework energy levels, effectively narrowing the bandgap of Ti‑MOFs and optimizing sonodynamic efficiency. Under US conditions, Ti/Mn‑MOFs mediate SDT to generate ROS, which oxidize L‑Arg and enable controlled release of NO. Owing to its relatively long half‑life and large diffusion radius, NO exerts both bactericidal and anti‑inflammatory effects under the regulation of US on/off switching, achieving a synergistic effect of transient bacterial killing and sustained anti‑inflammation. Moreover, this targeting strategy selectively eliminates pathogens while preserving the oral commensal microbiota, thereby avoiding dysbiosis (Fig. 7a, Table 1). Liu et al. [142] synthesized Zn(OH)F/CaF2 heterojunction nanomaterials via a hydrothermal method to address the clinical need for simultaneous deep bacterial killing and alveolar bone repair—a challenge that conventional therapies for periodontitis struggle to meet. The formation of the heterojunction significantly narrows the bandgap of the material and enhances internal electron transfer. Under US irradiation, the system leverages the overexpressed H2O2 in the inflammatory microenvironment to further promote electron transfer, thereby augmenting catalytic activity (Table 1). The material exhibited significant antibacterial efficacy against P. gingivalis and S. aureus under US irradiation, while the released trace elements Ca2+ and Zn2+ promoted tissue repair and osteogenic differentiation. After treatment, bone destruction was significantly reduced and gingival tissue remained intact.

The complexity of the root canal system anatomy and the formation of biofilms are the main reasons for root canal treatment failure. Enterococcus faecalis (E. faecalis) is the most commonly detected pathogen in root canal reinfection; its dense biofilm exhibits strong resistance to conventional sodium hypochlorite irrigation and antibiotic therapy. SDT, owing to the cavitation effect and microstreaming of US within the root canal, can penetrate complex structures such as lateral canals, achieving thorough eradication of intracanal infections [133]. Jian et al. [143] designed single‑phase bismuth ferrite (Bi2Fe4O9, BFO) NSs to address the dual challenges in root canal therapy: the complex anatomy of the root canal system that hinders thorough debridement by conventional instruments, and the high tolerance of E. faecalis biofilms to routine irrigants such as sodium hypochlorite. The two‑dimensional morphology of BFO NSs endows them with excellent piezocatalytic capability, enabling in situ generation of H2O2 under US stimulation. The generated H2O2 further drives the Fe2+/Fe3+‑mediated Fenton reaction in BFO, accelerating disruption of the membrane structure of E. faecalis biofilms. This system achieves efficient bacterial killing at a US power of only 60 W—a power level that can be readily delivered by clinical routine ultrasonic irrigation devices used in root canal therapy (Table 1). Pan et al. [144] constructed an Al3+‑doped SrTiO3/TiO2 nanotube coating (Al‑STNT) on titanium implant surfaces to address the clinical challenge of eliminating peri‑implant plaque biofilms in the confined oral environment and the need for revision surgery following implant failure. Al3+ doping introduces oxygen vacancies and distorts the SrTiO3 lattice; under US stimulation, the piezoelectric effect overcomes the bandgap barrier, significantly enhancing ROS generation. This nanocoating not only efficiently disrupts bacterial biofilms and suppresses their metabolism, but also endows the implant with osteogenic activity, promoting the formation of stable osseointegration between the implant surface and the alveolar bone (Table 1).

Myositis

Bacterial myositis is a deep muscle infection caused by pathogenic microorganisms, which can lead to systemic myalgia and multifocal myositis. Due to the deep location of the infection and the complex anatomical structure, conventional antibiotic therapy often fails to achieve effective drug concentrations. SDT, with its US tissue penetration capability, overcomes this limitation [146]. Wei et al. [145] constructed a TH@FeM precursor consisting of an Fe3+–ZnTC MOF loaded with tetrahydroxyphenyl porphyrin (TH). The NPs, approximately 187 nm in size, could extravasate through vascular endothelial gaps into infected tissues; the increased vascular permeability associated with inflammation further facilitated passive accumulation at the infection site. Upon reaching the infected site, the high concentration of GSH reduced Fe3+ to Fe2+, causing the MOF structure to collapse. The released porphyrin molecules—TH and ZnTC—then underwent in situ self‑assembly via π–π stacking and electrostatic interactions to form larger TH/ZnTC bis‑porphyrin heterojunctions. The increased size hindered their re‑entry into the circulation through endothelial gaps or renal filtration, thereby prolonging retention at the infected site and enabling local high‑concentration enrichment. During this process, the released Fe2+ drove CDT, while the in situ formed S‑scheme heterojunction promoted efficient e−-h⁺ separation under US excitation, significantly enhancing the yield of 1O2. Composed of organic porphyrin molecules and metal ions, the system could be cleared via hepatic and renal metabolism after completing therapy, demonstrating favorable biosafety. (Fig. 7b, Table 1). Pang et al. [20] constructed the first carrier‑free iron–siderophore–sonosensitizer nanoplatform (FSP) via covalent conjugation of a catechol siderophore with the sonosensitizer Purpurin 18, followed by coordination self‑assembly with Fe(III) ions, to address the clinical challenges in multidrug‑resistant biofilm infections—including limited antibiotic efficacy, poor penetration of the biofilm physical barrier, and a hostile microenvironment (low pH, hypoxia) that further compromises therapeutic outcomes. This system exploits the bacterial siderophore uptake machinery for precise targeting and active internalization, and achieves deep penetration through pH‑responsive assembly/disassembly behavior. Under US irradiation, the platform synergistically combines sonocatalysis and the Fenton reaction to generate a burst of ROS. Concurrently, iron overload and ROS synergistically induce ferroptosis‑like death in bacteria, ultimately achieving complete cure in a mouse model of pyomyositis (Table 1).

In summary, given the inherent advantages of inorganic nanosonosensitizers in terms of stability and sonocatalytic efficiency, inorganic nanosonosensitizer‑mediated SDT exhibits significantly greater potential than conventional antibacterial therapies for the treatment of various types of infectious diseases. However, its clinical translation still faces formidable challenges concerning tissue toxicity and long‑term metabolic fate. Current studies indicate that conventional inorganic nanomaterials (e.g., TiO2) generally suffer from non‑degradability; their long‑term, non‑specific accumulation in healthy tissues may not only result in suboptimal therapeutic efficacy but also pose potential toxicity risks, severely hampering their clinical translation [150], [151]. Therefore, the development of inorganic nanosonosensitizers with favorable biodegradability and clearance capability has become a critical direction for promoting their clinical translation. In recent years, researchers have introduced defects into the MOF framework through strategies such as defect engineering, which not only significantly enhances ROS generation efficiency but, more importantly, enables such defect‑engineered MOFs (e.g., D‑MOF(Ti)) to be degraded and metabolized out of the body after completing therapy, without off‑target toxicity [150]. Similarly, the degradable p–n junction sonosensitizer N‑CD@LiFePO4, after completing SDT, can slowly degrade into non‑toxic components such as Li+, Fe3+ and PO43−, while the ultra‑small N‑CDs are cleared via the renal excretion pathway [152]. These studies provide important insights for the clinical translation of inorganic nanosonosensitizers—namely, by incorporating degradable structural units or employing defect engineering strategies in material design, it is possible to endow materials with metabolizability while maintaining sonocatalytic activity, thereby effectively circumventing the safety risks associated with long‑term in vivo retention. Nevertheless, systematic investigations into the degradation kinetics, metabolic pathways and long‑term biosafety of inorganic nanosonosensitizers in vivo remain rather limited. Future efforts should systematically integrate biodegradability and metabolic clearance mechanisms into material design, and establish comprehensive in vivo fate evaluation systems, so as to propel inorganic nanosonosensitizers from the laboratory towards clinical application.

Summary and outlook

The ever-increasing bacterial resistance has prompted exploration of alternative therapeutic modalities beyond traditional antibiotic therapy. Among these, ROS-based strategies have attracted widespread attention due to their broad-spectrum antibacterial activity and low resistance potential. Compared with PDT, SDT exhibits more pronounced advantages in treating deep-seated infections owing to its tissue penetration capability. This review provides a detailed introduction to the antibacterial mechanisms of SDT, strategies for enhancing SDT, and recent applications of inorganic nanosonosensitizer-mediated SDT in infectious diseases. In the section on the antibacterial mechanisms of SDT, we detail: (1) the generation of ROS mediated by inorganic nanosonosensitizers; (2) the mechanical damage, thermal effects and sonoporation induced by ultrasonic cavitation; and (3) the mechanisms by which ROS cause bacterial damage. In the section on enhancing sonodynamic catalytic activity, focusing on the core challenges faced by inorganic sonosensitizers—low e−-h⁺ separation efficiency, short carrier lifetime, the hypoxic microenvironment at the infection site, as well as the cycling stability and biosafety of sonosensitizers—we summarized several regulatory strategies based on optimizing the structure of sonosensitizers: morphological engineering, defect engineering (e.g., oxygen vacancies, element doping), and phase engineering and interface engineering (e.g., construction of heterojunctions and Schottky junctions). These methods effectively lower the cavitation threshold, promote cavitation bubble formation, suppress carrier recombination, and enhance the material's efficiency in absorbing ultrasonic energy, thereby significantly improving ROS generation efficiency. Furthermore, with the exploration of sonosensitizer functions and research on other multifunctional materials, it has been found that using hydrogel delivery systems, nanozyme catalytic systems, and MOF carrier systems—through local depots, mimicking multienzyme catalysis, or loading functional molecules—enables active modulation of the microenvironment, thereby synergistically enhancing the antibacterial effect of SDT and, to some extent, addressing the issues of cycling stability and biosafety of inorganic nanosonosensitizers. In the section on applications of SDT in infectious diseases, we mainly covered five aspects: skin infections and wound healing, periodontal and root canal infections, osteomyelitis, myositis, and chronic gastritis. It can be observed that inorganic nanomaterials, owing to their physicochemical stability, can adapt to complex infection microenvironments such as gastric acid and biofilms. Moreover, from the combination applications of inorganic nanosonosensitizers with novel materials such as hydrogels and MOFs, we find that the targeting and dynamic regulation of SDT have been significantly improved.

In future research, we should shift from a narrow focus on enhancing the performance of sonocatalytic materials toward a holistic approach that integrates safety, targeting, and clinical feasibility. The primary goal is to develop standardized evaluation procedures to assess the catalytic activity of sonosensitizers, ROS yield, and long-term biocompatibility of the materials in vivo. Second, attention should be paid to the development of in situ and real-time characterization techniques, enabling dynamic monitoring of ROS generation in complex biological matrices through innovative tools, while also observing the distribution and metabolism of nanomaterials in vivo, thereby precisely guiding preclinical material system research and enhancing treatment specificity. In addition, the concentration control of ROS requires particular attention. High concentrations of ROS can exert sufficient killing effects on pathogenic bacteria, but if ROS accumulate in normal tissues, they can also cause non‑selective damage to healthy cells. Of particular note, the translation from the currently widely used small, homogeneous rodent infection models to heterogeneous human clinical applications faces numerous fundamental challenges. First, significant anatomical (e.g., skin thickness, tissue acoustic properties), immunological (e.g., differences in innate and adaptive immunity), metabolic and infection microenvironment (e.g., oxygen partial pressure, pH) disparities between rodents and humans render efficacy and safety data validated in mouse models difficult to extrapolate directly to humans [153]. Second, infectious foci in rodent models are typically highly homogeneous—standardized strains, standardized inoculum sizes and fixed infection sites—whereas the pronounced heterogeneity of human diseases, including inter‑individual variability (age, underlying diseases, immune status), lesion microenvironment differences (biofilm formation, necrotic tissue, local blood flow) and the diversity of drug‑resistant strains, imposes more stringent demands on the universality of SDT [154]. Third, the distribution, metabolism and clearance of nanomaterials in vivo may differ by orders of magnitude between rodents and humans (e.g., hepatic/splenic uptake rates, renal clearance half‑life), and most existing studies on inorganic nanosonosensitizers lack systematic validation in large animal models such as non‑human primates [155]. In light of these translational barriers, we recommend that preclinical studies progressively incorporate more clinically relevant infection models and establish cross‑species equivalent conversion methods for US parameters to improve the accuracy of efficacy prediction. Given the pronounced heterogeneity of infectious lesions, material evaluation stages should include validation using multiple clinically isolated strains and mixed‑infection scenarios. Furthermore, cross‑species pharmacokinetic comparative studies should be increased to enable extrapolation of efficacy and toxicity from animals to humans. Meanwhile, with the rapid development of artificial intelligence, the future development of high-performance nanosonosensitizers will inevitably benefit from the deep integration of materials science and AI. AI-based predictive models and high-throughput screening technologies are expected to accelerate the rational design of sonosensitizers with tailored electronic structures and optimized band gap properties. Moreover, the standardization and intelligent regulation of US exposure parameters are crucial to ensure the reproducibility and efficacy of treatment across different clinical settings.

In summary, inorganic nanomaterial-mediated SDT has demonstrated great advantages and potential in the field of combating drug-resistant bacterial infections. Further development of this therapy requires not only the construction of more efficient nanomaterials, but also standardized evaluation procedures, advanced characterization techniques, a cross-species translational validation framework, and the systematic integration of artificial intelligence. We believe that future aSDT will achieve more scientific, precise, and intelligent applications.
