Spreading speeds for prey–predator systems in a shifting environment: a short proof
Zhucheng Jin

Abstract
This note is concerned with the spreading speed of the predator component in a class of prey–predator reaction–diffusion systems with spatiotemporal heterogeneity depending on a moving variable. The main difficulty is that the full system lacks a direct comparison principle. We establish a pointwise estimate showing that the prey density is close to its carrying capacity wherever the predator density is sufficiently small. This allows us to compare solutions of the system with those of scalar Fisher–KPP equations in shifting environments. Consequently, we obtain an explicit formula for the spreading speed of the predator. In particular, for nonmonotone shifting environments, locking and nonlocal pulling phenomena may occur. Key words . spreading speeds; shifting environments; prey-predator systems 2020 Mathematical Subject Classification . 35K45, 35K57, 92D25

Introduction

In this paper, we investigate spreading speeds for the following general reaction-diffusion systems in a shifting environment

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=d\partial_{xx}u+uf(x-c_{1}t,u,v),&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R},\\
\partial_{t}v=\;\partial_{xx}v+vg(x-c_{1}t,u,v),&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R},\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\;\;v(0,x)=v_{0}(x),&x\in\mathbb{R},\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
c_{1}>0
is the shifting speed of the environment and
d>0
is the diffusion rate of the prey.
A typical example of (
1.1
) is a Lotka–Volterra prey–predator system.
For notational simplicity, we normalize the diffusion coefficient of the predator to be one. Indeed, a system with diffusion terms
d_{1}\partial_{xx}u
and
d_{2}\partial_{xx}v
, where
d_{1},d_{2}>0
, can be reduced to the present form by a standard time rescaling argument.
Our main goal is to determine the spreading speed of the predator species when the prey is initially uniformly established. The detailed assumptions of
f
and
g
are given in the next section.

The concept of the spreading speed was introduced by Aronson and Weinberger
[
1
]
in the context of a scalar reaction–diffusion equation to describe the spatial expansion of a solution front over time. The celebrated example is the Fisher–KPP equation in a homogeneous environment,

\partial_{t}u=\partial_{xx}u+u(r-u),\quad\text{ for }t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}.

For the solution
u
of this Fisher–KPP equation with compactly supported initial data, it follows from
[
1
]
that there exists a constant
c_{*}:=2\sqrt{r}
such that

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\sup_{x\geq ct}u(t,x)=0,\;\;\forall c>c_{*}\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\inf_{0\leq x\leq ct}u(t,x)>0,\;\;\forall c\in(0,c_{*}).

(1.2)

The constant
c_{*}
is called the spreading speed. In (
1.2
), the speed
c_{*}
is linearly determined.
Since then, the spreading speed of various reaction-diffusion equations/systems and their generalizations in heterogeneous environments have been extensively studied, see for example
[
4
,
10
,
14
]
and the references therein.

Shifting environments arise naturally in population dynamics when the spatial distribution of favorable habitats changes over time, for instance due to climate warming or other large-scale environmental changes. Such models were introduced in
[
22
]
and
[
2
]
.
To model such effects, a simple way is to assume that the growth or interaction rates depend on the moving variable
x-c_{1}t
, where
c_{1}
is the velocity of the moving habitat. Beyond biological modeling, the shifting heterogeneity can also arise in a triangular system that is partially decoupled, see
[
12
,
15
]
and the discussion in Section 2.1 of
[
13
]
. The propagation dynamics of equations/systems in shifting environments have been extensively studied in
[
7
,
12
,
13
,
16
,
18
,
20
,
21
]
. See also
[
24
,
25
]
for the general theory on these problems. We refer the reader to the survey article
[
23
]
and the references therein.
Unlike homogeneous media, the spreading speed may depend on the velocity and the profile of the moving habitat. As shown in
[
3
,
20
]
and
[
17
, Chapter 6]
, some intriguing phenomena may occur, for instance, locking and nonlocal pulling phenomena, where the spreading speed is not linearly determined.
The locking phenomenon means that the spreading speed of the species equals the shifting speed, while the nonlocal pulling phenomenon means that the spreading speed is not linearly determined by the leading edge, which can be enhanced by the presence of a good habitat at large spatial scales.

More recently,
[
13
]
and
[
18
]
give a complete characterization of the spreading speed for scalar KPP equations in shifting environments with nonmonotone growth rate functions.
The nonmonotone shifting environments are significant in biological modeling, since many biological processes depend unimodally on temperature
[
8
]
. The lack of monotonicity in the shifting environments introduces additional difficulties in characterizing the propagation dynamics.

For prey-predator systems, the analysis becomes even more delicate. The full system generally lacks a comparison principle, and the growth rate of the predator depends on the prey density, which is affected by predation. Hence, the methodology for scalar equations cannot be applied directly. For prey-predator systems in homogeneous environments, the spreading speed was studied in
[
9
,
10
]
. Recently the propagation dynamics of prey-predator systems in shifting or nonautonomous environments were investigated by
[
7
,
16
,
11
]
.

The main purpose of this note is to provide a reduction from the prey-predator system to an associated scalar Fisher–KPP equation in a shifting environment, where the environmental profile may be nonmonotone. The local favorable patches may affect the selection of speed. This reduction allows us to characterize how a nonmonotone shifting environment affects the predator invasion speed. This reduction is obtained through a pointwise estimate showing that the prey density is close to its carrying capacity wherever the predator density is small, see Lemma
4.1
below.

The pointwise estimate was introduced in
[
11
]
for time heterogeneous prey-predator systems. In contrast, the present problem contains a moving spatial heterogeneity, and the speed selection is governed by the interaction between the predator front and the shifting environmental profile. We prove that the predator component has exactly the same spreading speed as the scalar KPP equation with effective growth rate
g(x-c_{1}t,1,0)
. Here, we emphasize that
g(x-c_{1}t,1,0)
can be nonmonotone.
The locking and nonlocal pulling mechanisms discovered for scalar KPP equations in shifting environments persist at the level of predator invasion in a non-cooperative prey-predator system.
Our main result also confirms the conjecture about the locking case stated in
[
16
, Remark 3]
, where a Hamilton–Jacobi approach was used to study a Lotka–Volterra prey-predator model with a monotone conversion rate function.

Finally, we mention the work of Bramson
[
6
]
, which employs probabilistic arguments to establish the logarithmic delay in the position of solutions to Fisher–KPP equations relative to the position of the traveling front with minimal wave speed. For prey-predator systems, characterizing such a correction remains a challenging open problem.
The argument used in this note does not seem applicable to addressing this issue.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we present the assumptions and state our main results. In Section 3, we recall the spreading speed results of KPP equations in shifting environments and prove some basic results. In Section 4, we prove the spreading speed theorem. In
Section 5, we prove the asymptotic behaviors of our problem and display a concrete example.

2
Assumptions and main results

2.1
General assumptions

Now, we state the assumptions on
f
and
g
.

Assumption 2.1

Assume that
f:\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy:

(i)

For every
R>0
,
z\mapsto f(z,u,v)
and
z\mapsto g(z,u,v)
are bounded and uniformly continuous on
\mathbb{R}
, uniformly for
(u,v)\in[0,R]^{2}
. Moreover,
f
and
g
are Lipschitz continuous in
(u,v)\in[0,R]^{2}
, uniformly with respect to
z\in\mathbb{R}
;

(ii)

(
Predation type
) For each
u>0
,
z\in\mathbb{R}
, the map
v\mapsto f(z,u,v)
is decreasing. For each
v>0
,
z\in\mathbb{R}
, the map
u\mapsto g(z,u,v)
is increasing.

(iii)

(
Monostable
)
f(\cdot,1,0)\equiv 0
and
\inf_{z\in\mathbb{R}}f(z,u,0)>0
for all
u\in[0,1)
.

(iv)

(
Strong KPP
) For each
u\geq 0
, the map
v\mapsto g(z,u,v)
is nonincreasing uniformly in
z\in\mathbb{R}
.

(v)

The limits
\lim_{z\to\pm\infty}g(z,1,0)
exist and

\inf_{z\in\mathbb{R}}g(z,1,0)>0.

Denote

r^{\pm}:=g(\pm\infty,1,0).

(2.1)

Remark 2.2

We have
r^{\pm}>0
. In this note, it is not necessary to require
z\mapsto g(z,1,0)
to be monotone. We are interested in the case

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}g(z,1,0)>\max\{r^{+},r^{-}\}.

Next we impose the dissipative assumption.

Assumption 2.3

Assume that there exist constants
\overline{V}>0
and
\delta>0
such that

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}g(z,1,\overline{V})\leq 0\quad\text{ and }\quad\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathbb{R},\;0\leq u\leq\delta\end{subarray}}f(z,u,\overline{V})>0.

Assumption 2.4

Let
\overline{V}
be chosen as in Assumption
2.3
. Let
u_{0}
and
v_{0}
be two bounded and continuous functions satisfying

0<\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}u_{0}(x)\leq u_{0}(x)\leq 1,\quad 0\leq v_{0}(x)\leq\overline{V},\quad v_{0}\not\equiv 0,\quad\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}.

and suppose that
v_{0}
is compactly supported.

Remark 2.5

Assumption
2.3
provides an admissible upper bound for the predator population and guarantees the persistence of the prey component at low density. Together with the monotonicity assumptions, it yields the invariant region stated in Lemma
3.3
below.

2.2
Main results

We consider only rightward propagation, since leftward propagation can be treated by the change of variable
x\mapsto-x
. We introduce the definition of rightward spreading speed, which will be referred to simply as the spreading speed.

Definition 2.6

Let
(u,v)
be a solution of (
1.1
) with initial data
(u_{0},v_{0})
satisfying Assumption
2.4
. The quantity
c^{*}>0
is called the rightward spreading speed of
v
if

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\sup_{x\geq ct}v(t,x)=0,\qquad

\displaystyle\forall c>c^{*},

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\inf_{0\leq x\leq ct}v(t,x)>0,\qquad

\displaystyle\forall c\in(0,c^{*}).

To state our main theorem, we first introduce an eigenvalue problem,

\partial_{zz}\phi+g(z,1,0)\phi=\Lambda\phi,\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.2)

Let us define the generalized principal eigenvalue
\Lambda_{1}
of (
2.2
) as follows:

\Lambda_{1}:=\Lambda_{1}(g)=\inf\left\{\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}:\;\;\exists\psi\in C^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}),\psi>0,\partial_{zz}\psi+g(z,1,0)\psi\leq\Lambda\psi\text{ in }\mathbb{R}\right\}.

(2.3)

Several notions of generalized principal eigenvalues are analyzed in
[
5
]
.
We refer to
[
5
]
,
[
13
, Proposition 3.1]
and
[
18
, Proposition 4.2]
for some basic properties of
\Lambda_{1}
.

Now we state our main theorem.

Theorem 2.7

Let Assumptions
2.1
,
2.3
and
2.4
be satisfied and
c_{1}>0
. Let
(u,v)
be the solution of (
1.1
). Then the spreading speed of
v
is given by

c_{v}^{*}=\begin{cases}2\sqrt{r^{+}},&\text{ if }0<c_{1}\leq 2\sqrt{r^{+}},\\
c_{1},&\text{ if }2\sqrt{r^{+}}\leq c_{1}\leq 2\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}},\\
\frac{c_{1}}{2}-\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}-r^{-}}+\frac{r^{-}}{\frac{c_{1}}{2}-\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}-r^{-}}},\quad&\text{ if }2\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}}\leq c_{1}\leq 2(\sqrt{r^{-}}+\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}-r^{-}}),\\
2\sqrt{r^{-}},&\text{ if }c_{1}\geq 2(\sqrt{r^{-}}+\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}-r^{-}}),\end{cases}

where
r^{\pm}
and
\Lambda_{1}
are defined in (
2.1
) and (
2.3
) respectively.

Remark 2.8

Note that the map
c_{1}\mapsto c_{v}^{*}
is nonmonotone.
When
c_{1}\in(2\sqrt{r^{+}},2\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}})
, the predator propagates at the same velocity as the moving habitat, namely, the locking phenomena. However, for
c_{1}\in\big(2\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}},2(\sqrt{r^{-}}+\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}-r^{-}})\big)
, the nonlocal pulling phenomenon occurs, where distant favorable regions enhance the spreading speed.

Remark 2.9

If
\Lambda_{1}=r^{+}>r^{-}
, then the second case (locking) in Theorem
2.7
is eliminated. This situation has been investigated in
[
16
]
. In case that
\Lambda_{1}=r^{-}>r^{+}
, the third case (nonlocal pulling) is eliminated. When
\Lambda_{1}=r^{-}=r^{+}
, both the second and the third cases are eliminated.

2.3
Asymptotic behaviors

As a consequence of Theorem
2.7
,
we have the asymptotic behavior of solutions at the leading edge.

Corollary 2.10

Let the assumptions of Theorem
2.7
be satisfied. Then we have

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\sup_{x\geq(c_{v}^{*}+\eta)t}\big\{\left|u(t,x)-1\right|+\left|v(t,x)\right|\big\}=0,\forall\eta>0.

(2.4)

The spreading speed result above does not by itself determine the asymptotic state behind the invasion front. To describe the convergence in the interior spreading region, we impose additional assumptions on the limiting ODE systems, which are close to
[
10
, Assumption 2.5]
.

Assumption 2.11

Assume the following limits exist:

\lim_{z\to\pm\infty}f(z,u,v)=f_{\pm}(u,v)\text{ and }\lim_{z\to\pm\infty}g(z,u,v)=g_{\pm}(u,v)\;\;\text{ locally uniformly for }(u,v)\in[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty).

For the limiting system, we impose that the vector fields
(f_{+},g_{+})
(respectively
(f_{-},g_{-})
) has a unique zero
(u^{*}_{+},v^{*}_{+})
(respectively
(u^{*}_{-},v^{*}_{-})
) in the rectangle
[\beta,1)\times(0,\overline{V}]
, where
\beta:=\min\{\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}u_{0}(x),\delta\}
and
\delta
and
\overline{V}
are given in Assumption
2.3
.

In general, the asymptotic behavior behind the front is not clear, which may depend to a large extent on the dynamical behavior of the following limiting ODE systems

\begin{cases}u_{t}=uf_{+}(u,v),\\
v_{t}=vg_{+}(u,v),\end{cases}

(2.5)

and

\begin{cases}u_{t}=uf_{-}(u,v),\\
v_{t}=vg_{-}(u,v).\end{cases}

(2.6)

Assumption 2.12

Assume that (
2.5
) and (
2.6
) admit a strict Lyapunov function. More precisely, suppose that

(i)

There exist strictly convex functions

\Phi_{+},\Phi_{-}\in C^{2}\big([\beta,1)\times(0,\overline{V}],\mathbb{R}\big),

such that
\Phi_{+}
attains its minimum at
(u_{+}^{*},v_{+}^{*})
, while
\Phi_{-}
attains its minimum at
(u_{-}^{*},v_{-}^{*})
. And the functions
\Phi_{+}
and
\Phi_{-}
satisfy

(uf_{+}(u,v),vg_{+}(u,v))\cdot\nabla\Phi_{+}(u,v)\leq 0,\;\;\forall(u,v)\in[\beta,1)\times(0,\overline{V}],

and

(uf_{-}(u,v),vg_{-}(u,v))\cdot\nabla\Phi_{-}(u,v)\leq 0,\;\;\forall(u,v)\in[\beta,1)\times(0,\overline{V}].

(ii)

Let
(u^{+}(t),v^{+}(t))
and
(u^{-}(t),v^{-}(t))
be the solutions of (
2.5
) and (
2.6
) with initial data
(\hat{u}_{0},\hat{v}_{0})\in[\beta,1)\times(0,\overline{V}]
, respectively. For
i\in\{+,-\}
. For each
(\hat{u}_{0},\hat{v}_{0})\in[\beta,1)\times(0,\overline{V}]
, assume that we have:

if
\Phi_{i}(u^{i}(t),v^{i}(t))=\Phi_{i}(\hat{u}_{0},\hat{v}_{0})
for all
t\geq 0
, then

(\hat{u}_{0},\hat{v}_{0})=(u^{*}_{i},v^{*}_{i}).

Then we have the following convergence result.

Corollary 2.13

Let the assumptions of Theorem
2.7
and Assumptions
2.11
and
2.12
be satisfied.
Let
(u,v)
be the solution of (
1.1
). Assume
d=1
. Then the following statements hold true:

(i)

if
c_{1}\geq 2\sqrt{r^{+}}
, then for all
\eta\in(0,c_{v}^{*}/2)
,

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\sup_{0\leq x\leq(c_{v}^{*}-\eta)t}\big\{\left|u(t,x)-u^{*}_{-}\right|+\left|v(t,x)-v^{*}_{-}\right|\big\}=0;

(2.7)

(ii)

if
0<c_{1}<2\sqrt{r^{+}}
, then for all
\eta\in(0,c_{1}/2)
,

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\sup_{0\leq x\leq(c_{1}-\eta)t}\big\{\left|u(t,x)-u^{*}_{-}\right|+\left|v(t,x)-v^{*}_{-}\right|\big\}=0,

(2.8)

and for all
\eta\in(0,(c_{v}^{*}-c_{1})/2)
,

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\sup_{(c_{1}+\eta)t\leq x\leq(c_{v}^{*}-\eta)t}\big\{\left|u(t,x)-u^{*}_{+}\right|+\left|v(t,x)-v^{*}_{+}\right|\big\}=0.

(2.9)

Remark 2.14

From the formula of
c_{v}^{*}
given in Theorem
2.7
, we have the fact:

\begin{cases}c_{v}^{*}=c_{v}^{*}(c_{1})\leq c_{1}&\text{ if }c_{1}\geq 2\sqrt{r^{+}},\\
c_{v}^{*}=c_{v}^{*}(c_{1})>c_{1}&\text{ if }c_{1}<2\sqrt{r^{+}}.\end{cases}

Corollary
2.13
shows that, in the invaded region of the predator, the solution converges to the positive coexistence equilibrium selected by the limiting environment seen in the moving frame
x-c_{1}t
. The asymptotic state depends on the relative position between the predator front (
x=c_{v}^{*}t
) and the shifting environmental interface (
x=c_{1}t
).

The assumption

is used only in the Lyapunov argument for the limiting systems. It can be removed in special cases; see example (

).

This corollary does not describe the fine asymptotics near

or

. We refer to
[
19

for the logarithmic correction results of KPP equations in shifting environments.

3
Preliminaries

3.1
Scalar KPP equations in shifting environments

We first recall a very recent result
on the spreading speed of the scalar Fisher–KPP equation in a shifting environment, established by
[
13
,
18
]
.
Let us focus on the following equation

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}w=\partial_{xx}w+w\left(g(x-c_{1}t)-w\right),&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R},\\
w(0,x)=w_{0}(x),&x\in\mathbb{R},\end{cases}

(3.1)

where
c_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
and the initial function
w_{0}
is nonnegative, nontrivial, bounded, continuous and compactly supported on
\mathbb{R}
.
Assume that the function
g
is continuous and positive on
\mathbb{R}
with

g^{\pm}:=g(\pm\infty)>0.

Similarly to (
2.2
) and (
2.3
), we define
\lambda_{1,g}
as the generalized principal eigenvalue of the following eigenvalue problem:

\phi_{yy}+g(y)\phi=\lambda\phi,\quad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}.

Namely,

\lambda_{1,g}=\inf\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}:\;\;\exists\psi\in C^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}),\psi>0,\psi^{\prime\prime}+g(y)\psi\leq\lambda\psi\text{ in }\mathbb{R}\right\}.

With these notations, the exact spreading speed
c^{*}
of (
3.1
) is characterized by the following theorem. Note that the spreading speed of (
3.1
) is completely determined by quantities
g^{+}
,
g^{-}
,
c_{1}
and
\lambda_{1,g}
. Different approaches to its proof can be found in
[
13
,
18
]
. The dependence of the spreading speed
c^{*}
on
c_{1}
has been illustrated in
[
13
,
18
]
for various scenarios.

Theorem 3.1
(
[
13
,
18
]
)

Let
w
be a solution of (
3.1
). Then the rightward spreading speed of
w
is given by

c^{*}=\begin{cases}2\sqrt{g^{+}},&\text{ if }c_{1}\leq 2\sqrt{g^{+}},\\
c_{1},&\text{ if }2\sqrt{g^{+}}\leq c_{1}\leq 2\sqrt{\lambda_{1,g}},\\
\frac{c_{1}}{2}-\sqrt{\lambda_{1,g}-g^{-}}+\frac{g^{-}}{\frac{c_{1}}{2}-\sqrt{\lambda_{1,g}-g^{-}}},\quad&\text{ if }2\sqrt{\lambda_{1,g}}\leq c_{1}\leq 2(\sqrt{g^{-}}+\sqrt{\lambda_{1,g}-g^{-}}),\\
2\sqrt{g^{-}},&\text{ if }c_{1}\geq 2(\sqrt{g^{-}}+\sqrt{\lambda_{1,g}-g^{-}}).\end{cases}

Remark 3.2

From
[
18
, Theorem 2.13]
, the same conclusion also holds for the general KPP reaction terms.

3.2
Invariant region and a Liouville-type lemma

Now we prove that the solution of (
1.1
) is invariant in a rectangle.

Lemma 3.3

Let Assumptions
2.1
,
2.3
and
2.4
be satisfied. Denote
\beta:=\min\{\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}u_{0}(x),\delta\}
. Then

\beta\leq u(t,x)\leq 1,\;\;0\leq v(t,x)\leq\overline{V},\quad\forall t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}.

Proof.
Although the full system does not admit a comparison principle, scalar comparison can still be applied to each component separately. We first observe that
0
is a subsolution for each component equation.
Since
0
satisfies
v
-equation for any
u
and initial function
v_{0}\geq 0
, we obtain
v(t,x)\geq 0
for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Similarly, we have
u(t,x)\geq 0
for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
. From the monotonicity of
f
and
g
, we have

f(z,1,v)\leq f(z,1,0)=0\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{R},v\geq 0\;\text{ and }\;g(z,u,\overline{V})\leq g(z,1,\overline{V})\leq 0\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{R},u\in[0,1].

Hence,
u=1
is a supersolution of
u
-equation for any
v\geq 0
and
v=\overline{V}
is a supersolution of
v
-equation for any
u\in[0,1]
. Together with the initial condition in Assumption
2.4
, this yields
(u,v)(t,x)\in[0,1]\times[0,\overline{V}]
for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Next we prove that
u
has a positive lower bound for positive initial data. By Assumptions
2.1
and
2.3
, the constant function
u=\beta
satisfies

\partial_{t}\beta-\partial_{xx}\beta\leq\beta f(x-c_{1}t,\beta,\overline{V})\leq\beta f(x-c_{1}t,\beta,v),\quad\forall t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R},v\in[0,\overline{V}].

Thus, the comparison principle for scalar equation implies that
u\geq\beta>0
. The proof is complete.

Next we state a Liouville-type lemma, which plays an important role in the proof of pointwise estimate.

Lemma 3.4

Let
u
be a bounded entire solution of

\partial_{t}u=d\partial_{xx}u+u\tilde{f}(x-c_{1}t,u,0),\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

where
\tilde{f}(\cdot,u,0)
is a locally uniform limit of translations of
f(\cdot,u,0)

and satisfies the monostable condition inherited from Assumption
2.1
(iii).
If

0<\beta\leq u(t,x)\leq 1\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{2},

then
u\equiv 1
.

Proof.
Denote
w(t,x):=u(t,x+c_{1}t)
and
m:=\inf_{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}w(t,x)
. Assume
m<1
. There exists a sequence
\{(t_{n},x_{n})\}_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
w(t_{n},x_{n})\to m
as
n\to+\infty
. Define
w_{n}(t,x):=w(t+t_{n},x+x_{n})
. By parabolic estimates, up to a subsequence, it follows that

\begin{split}\lim_{n\to+\infty}w_{n}(t,x)=\tilde{w}(t,x)\quad&\text{ locally uniformly in }(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\\
\lim_{n\to+\infty}\tilde{f}(x+x_{n},w_{n},0)=\hat{f}(x,\tilde{w},0)\quad&\text{ locally uniformly in }(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\end{split}

and
\tilde{w}
satisfies

\partial_{t}\tilde{w}=d\partial_{xx}\tilde{w}+c_{1}\partial_{x}\tilde{w}+\tilde{w}\hat{f}(x,\tilde{w},0).

(3.2)

Note that
\tilde{w}(0,0)=m\in[\beta,1)
and
(0,0)
is the global minimum point.
Thus we have

\partial_{t}\tilde{w}(0,0)=0,\;\;\partial_{x}\tilde{w}(0,0)=0\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\partial_{xx}\tilde{w}(0,0)\geq 0.

By Assumption
2.1
(iii)
, it follows that
\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\hat{f}(x,m,0)>0
. This contradicts (
3.2
). We have
m=1
, namely
w\equiv 1
, and hence
u\equiv 1
. This proves the lemma.

4
Proof of the spreading speed

To prove Theorem
2.7
, we first derive the following pointwise estimate, which plays an important role in the lower estimate of spreading speed of
v
.

Lemma 4.1

Let the assumptions of Theorem
2.7
be satisfied. For each
\alpha>0
, there exist constants
M_{\alpha}>0
and
T_{\alpha}>0
such that

1-u(t,x)\leq\alpha+M_{\alpha}v(t,x),\quad\forall t\geq T_{\alpha},\forall x\in\mathbb{R}.

Proof.
Argue by contradiction and suppose that there exist sequences
\{x_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}
,
\{t_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{R}
with
t_{n}\to+\infty
as
n\to+\infty
, and some constant
\alpha_{0}>0
such that

1-u(t_{n},x_{n})>\alpha_{0}+nv(t_{n},x_{n}),\;\;\;\;\forall n\geq 1.

(4.1)

Set

u_{n}(t,x):=u(t+t_{n},x+x_{n})\text{ and }v_{n}(t,x):=v(t+t_{n},x+x_{n}).

Note that
(u_{n},v_{n})(t,x)
satisfies

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u_{n}=d\partial_{xx}u_{n}+u_{n}f\big(x+x_{n}-c_{1}(t+t_{n}),u_{n},v_{n}\big),&\forall t>-t_{n},x\in\mathbb{R},\\
\partial_{t}v_{n}=\partial_{xx}v_{n}+v_{n}g\big(x+x_{n}-c_{1}(t+t_{n}),u_{n},v_{n}\big),&\forall t>-t_{n},x\in\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

By parabolic estimates and Assumption
2.1
(i)
, up to a subsequence, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to+\infty}u_{n}(t,x)=\tilde{u}(t,x)\quad\text{ locally uniformly in }(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to+\infty}v_{n}(t,x)=\tilde{v}(t,x)\quad\text{ locally uniformly in }(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to+\infty}f\big(x+x_{n}-c_{1}(t+t_{n}),u_{n},v_{n}\big)=\tilde{f}\big(x-c_{1}t,\tilde{u},\tilde{v}\big)\quad\text{ locally uniformly in }(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to+\infty}g\big(x+x_{n}-c_{1}(t+t_{n}),u_{n},v_{n}\big)=\tilde{g}\big(x-c_{1}t,\tilde{u},\tilde{v}\big)\quad\text{ locally uniformly in }(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

The parabolic estimates ensure that
(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})(t,x)
satisfies for all
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\tilde{u}=d\partial_{xx}\tilde{u}+\tilde{u}\tilde{f}\big(x-c_{1}t,\tilde{u},\tilde{v}\big),\\
\partial_{t}\tilde{v}=\partial_{xx}\tilde{v}+\tilde{v}\tilde{g}\big(x-c_{1}t,\tilde{u},\tilde{v}\big).\\
\end{cases}

Letting
n\to+\infty
in (
4.1
) and using the boundedness of
u
, we obtain

\tilde{v}(0,0)=0
. The strong maximum principle applied to the
\tilde{v}
-equation implies that
\tilde{v}\equiv 0
. Thus,
\tilde{u}
satisfies

\partial_{t}\tilde{u}=d\partial_{xx}\tilde{u}+\tilde{u}\tilde{f}\big(x-c_{1}t,\tilde{u},0\big),\quad\forall(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Moreover, Lemma
3.3
gives
\tilde{u}\geq\beta>0
.
Then the Liouville-type Lemma
3.4
gives
\tilde{u}\equiv 1
. However, (
4.1
) yields

1-\tilde{u}(0,0)\geq\alpha_{0}>0
.
This contradiction completes the proof.

We now prove Theorem
2.7
using Lemma
4.1
and Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
2.7
.

Upper estimate:

We first show the upper estimate of the spreading speed of
v
. Since
u\in[\beta,1]
, the solution
v
satisfies the following differential inequality

\partial_{t}v\leq\partial_{xx}v+vg(x-c_{1}t,1,v)\quad\forall t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}.

Note that the reaction term
vg(\cdot,1,v)
satisfies the KPP condition.
The comparison principle, together with Theorem
3.1
and Remark
3.2
, yields

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\sup_{x\geq ct}v(t,x)=0,\quad\forall c>c^{*}_{v}.

Lower estimate:

Let us prove the lower estimate of spreading speed of
v
. From Assumption
2.1
and Lemma
3.3
, there exists a constant
L>0
such that

g(z,u,v)\geq g(z,1,0)-L(1-u)-Lv,\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{R},\;\;(u,v)\in[\beta,1]\times[0,\overline{V}].

Lemma
4.1
implies that for each
\alpha>0
small enough, there exist some
M_{\alpha}>0
and
T_{\alpha}>0
such that
v
satisfies

\partial_{t}v\geq\partial_{xx}v+v\bigg(g(x-c_{1}t,1,0)-L\alpha-LM_{\alpha}v-Lv\bigg),\;\;\forall t\geq T_{\alpha},x\in\mathbb{R}.

We denote

r_{\alpha}(x-c_{1}t):=g(x-c_{1}t,1,0)-L\alpha\;\;\text{ and }\;\;h_{\alpha}:=LM_{\alpha}+L.

Let
w_{\alpha}(t,x)
solve

\partial_{t}w_{\alpha}=\partial_{xx}w_{\alpha}+w_{\alpha}\bigg(r_{\alpha}\big(x-c_{1}(t+T_{\alpha})\big)-h_{\alpha}w_{\alpha}\bigg),\;\;\forall t>0,x\in\mathbb{R},

(4.2)

equipped with initial data
w_{\alpha}(0,x)
, where
w_{\alpha}(0,\cdot)
is nonnegative, nontrivial, continuous and compactly supported on
\mathbb{R}
. By Lemma
3.3
and the strong maximum principle for the
v
-component of the equation (
1.1
), we have
v(t,x)>0
for all
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
. We can choose
w_{\alpha}(0,x)
small enough such that
w_{\alpha}(0,\cdot)\leq v(T_{\alpha},\cdot)
. The comparison principle implies that
w_{\alpha}(t,x)\leq v(t+T_{\alpha},x)
for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Now we introduce an eigenvalue problem. Define

r^{\pm}_{\alpha}:=r_{\alpha}(\pm\infty)=g(\pm\infty,1,0)-L\alpha.

Let
\Lambda_{1,\alpha}
be the generalized principal eigenvalue of following eigenvalue problem:

\phi_{yy}+r_{\alpha}(y)\phi=\Lambda\phi,\quad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}.

Equivalently,

\Lambda_{1,\alpha}=\inf\left\{\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}:\;\;\exists\psi\in C^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}),\psi>0,\psi_{yy}+r_{\alpha}(y)\psi\leq\Lambda\psi\text{ in }\mathbb{R}\right\}.

Let us choose
\alpha>0
small enough such that
r^{\pm}_{\alpha}>0
and
\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}}r_{\alpha}(y)>0
.
For each sufficiently small
\alpha>0
, applying Theorem
3.1
and Remark
3.2
to (
4.2
) with
g(\cdot)
replaced by
r_{\alpha}(\cdot)
, we obtain that

c^{*}_{\alpha}=\begin{cases}2\sqrt{r_{\alpha}^{+}},&\text{ if }c_{1}\leq 2\sqrt{r_{\alpha}^{+}},\\
c_{1},&\text{ if }2\sqrt{r_{\alpha}^{+}}\leq c_{1}\leq 2\sqrt{\Lambda_{1,\alpha}},\\
\frac{c_{1}}{2}-\sqrt{\Lambda_{1,\alpha}-r_{\alpha}^{-}}+\frac{r_{\alpha}^{-}}{\frac{c_{1}}{2}-\sqrt{\Lambda_{1,\alpha}-r_{\alpha}^{-}}},\quad&\text{ if }2\sqrt{\Lambda_{1,\alpha}}\leq c_{1}\leq 2\left(\sqrt{r_{\alpha}^{-}}+\sqrt{\Lambda_{1,\alpha}-r_{\alpha}^{-}}\right),\\
2\sqrt{r_{\alpha}^{-}},&\text{ if }c_{1}\geq 2\left(\sqrt{r_{\alpha}^{-}}+\sqrt{\Lambda_{1,\alpha}-r_{\alpha}^{-}}\right).\end{cases}

By the comparison principle for (
4.2
), we obtain that for each given
\alpha>0
,

\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\inf_{0\leq x\leq ct}v(t+T_{\alpha},x)\geq\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\inf_{0\leq x\leq ct}w_{\alpha}(t,x)>0,\quad\forall c\in(0,c^{*}_{\alpha}).

(4.3)

The time shift
T_{\alpha}
can be removed since the arbitrariness of
c\in(0,c^{*}_{\alpha})
.
Thus, for all
\alpha>0
sufficiently small, we have

\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\inf_{0\leq x\leq ct}v(t,x)>0,\quad\forall c\in(0,c^{*}_{\alpha}).

(4.4)

Finally, let
\alpha\to 0^{+}
. Note that
r_{\alpha}^{\pm}
is continuous with respect to
\alpha\geq 0
and
r_{\alpha}^{\pm}\to r^{\pm}
as
\alpha\to 0^{+}
. Here
r^{\pm}
is defined in (
2.1
). From the definition of generalized principal eigenvalue, we have

|\Lambda_{1,\alpha}-\Lambda_{1}|\leq\|r_{\alpha}(\cdot)-r(\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}=L\alpha\to 0\;\;\text{ as }\alpha\to 0^{+},

where
r(\cdot):=g(\cdot,1,0)
and
\Lambda_{1}
is defined in (
2.3
).
Since
c_{\alpha}^{*}\to c_{v}^{*}
as
\alpha\to 0^{+}
, for every
c^{\prime}\in(0,c_{v}^{*})
there exists
\alpha^{\prime}>0
sufficiently small such that
c^{\prime}<c_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{*}
.
Hence, it follows from (
4.4
) that we have

\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\inf_{0\leq x\leq ct}v(t,x)>0,\quad\forall c\in(0,c^{*}_{v}).

This completes the proof.

5
Asymptotic behavior and application

5.1
Proof of Corollaries
2.10
and
2.13

As a consequence of Theorem
2.7
, we can first establish the large-time behavior of solutions at the leading edge.

Proof of Corollary
2.10
.

By Theorem
2.7
, it suffices to prove that

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\sup_{x\geq(c_{v}^{*}+\eta)t}\left|u(t,x)-1\right|=0,\quad\forall\eta>0.

By contradiction, for each
\eta>0
, we suppose that there exist sequences
\{t_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and
\{x_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
with
x_{n}\geq(c_{v}^{*}+\eta)t_{n}
and
t_{n}\to+\infty
as
n\to+\infty
such that

\limsup_{n\to+\infty}u(t_{n},x_{n})<1.

(5.1)

Denote
(u_{n},v_{n})(t,x):=(u,v)(t+t_{n},x+x_{n})
. By standard parabolic estimates,
we have

\lim_{n\to+\infty}(u_{n},v_{n})(t,x)=(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})(t,x)\quad\text{ locally uniformly in }(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

and

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\tilde{u}=d\partial_{xx}\tilde{u}+\tilde{u}\tilde{f}\big(x-c_{1}t,\tilde{u},\tilde{v}\big),\\
\partial_{t}\tilde{v}=\partial_{xx}\tilde{v}+\tilde{v}\tilde{g}\big(x-c_{1}t,\tilde{u},\tilde{v}\big).\\
\end{cases}

From the definition of spreading speed, we have
\tilde{v}(0,0)=0
. By the strong maximum principle, we obtain
\tilde{v}\equiv 0
. Then
\tilde{u}
satisfies

\partial_{t}\tilde{u}=d\partial_{xx}\tilde{u}+\tilde{u}\tilde{f}(x-c_{1}t,\tilde{u},0),\quad\forall(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Note that
\tilde{u}\geq\beta
from Lemma
3.3
.
By the Liouville-type Lemma
3.4
, we obtain
\tilde{u}\equiv 1
, which contradicts
(
5.1
). The proof is complete.

Next we prove Corollary
2.13
. The proof is close to
[
10
, Theorem 2.6]
and
[
16
, Theorem 4]
.

Proof of Corollary
2.13
.

Let us first prove (
2.7
). By changing the coordinate
x\mapsto-x
, from Theorem
2.7
, we know that there exists
c_{l}>0
such that

\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\inf_{-(c_{l}-\eta)t\leq x\leq 0}v(t,x)>0,\quad\forall\eta>0.

Choose
\delta\in(0,c_{l})
. Then for all sufficiently small
\eta>0
, there exist
T=T(\eta,\delta)>0
and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that

v(t,x)\geq\varepsilon_{0}\quad\text{ for all }t\geq T\text{ and }-\delta t\leq x\leq(c_{v}^{*}-\frac{\eta}{2})t.

If (
2.7
) fails, then there exist
\varepsilon_{1}>0
and a sequence
\{(t_{k},x_{k})\}_{k\geq 1}
with

0\leq x_{k}\leq(c_{v}^{*}-\eta)t_{k}
and
t_{k}\to+\infty
as
k\to+\infty
such that

\left|u(t_{k},x_{k})-u^{*}_{-}\right|+\left|v(t_{k},x_{k})-v^{*}_{-}\right|\geq\varepsilon_{1}\quad\forall k\geq 1.

(5.2)

Denote
(u_{k},v_{k})(t,x):=(u,v)(t+t_{k},x+x_{k})
.
Then, up to a subsequence, we have

v_{k}(t,x)\geq\varepsilon_{0},\qquad\forall(t,x)\in\Omega_{k},

where

\Omega_{k}:=\{(t,x):t+t_{k}\geq T,\;\;-\delta(t+t_{k})\leq x+x_{k}\leq(c_{v}^{*}-\eta/2)(t+t_{k})\}.

For every compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have
K\subset\Omega_{k}
for all sufficiently large
k
.
From Lemma
3.3
, we have

0<\beta\leq u_{k}(t,x)\leq 1\text{ and }\varepsilon_{0}\leq v_{k}(t,x)\leq\overline{V},\quad\forall(t,x)\in\Omega_{k},

(5.3)

where
\beta
and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
are independent of
k\geq 1
.
By parabolic estimates, possibly along a subsequence, we have

\lim_{k\to+\infty}(u_{k},v_{k})(t,x)=(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})(t,x)\quad\text{ locally uniformly in }(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Since
c_{1}\geq 2\sqrt{r^{+}}
, we have
c_{v}^{*}\leq c_{1}
. Then we obtain

x_{k}-c_{1}t_{k}\leq(c_{v}^{*}-\eta-c_{1})t_{k}\leq-\eta t_{k}\to-\infty\quad\text{ as }k\to\infty.

Hence,

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to+\infty}f(x+x_{k}-c_{1}(t+t_{k}),u_{k},v_{k})=f(-\infty,\tilde{u},\tilde{v})=f_{-}(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})~~\text{ locally uniformly in }(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to+\infty}g(x+x_{k}-c_{1}(t+t_{k}),u_{k},v_{k})=g(-\infty,\tilde{u},\tilde{v})=g_{-}(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})~~\text{ locally uniformly in }(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Note that
(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})
satisfies

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\tilde{u}=d\partial_{xx}\tilde{u}+\tilde{u}f_{-}\left(\tilde{u},\tilde{v}\right),&\forall(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\\
\partial_{t}\tilde{v}=\partial_{xx}\tilde{v}+\tilde{v}g_{-}\left(\tilde{u},\tilde{v}\right),&\forall(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.\\
\end{cases}

The estimate (
5.3
) yields

\beta\leq\inf_{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\tilde{u}\leq\sup_{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\tilde{u}(t,x)\leq 1\text{ and }\varepsilon_{0}\leq\inf_{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\tilde{v}\leq\sup_{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\tilde{v}(t,x)\leq\overline{V}.

Moreover, (
5.2
) gives

\left|\tilde{u}(0,0)-u^{*}_{-}\right|+\left|\tilde{v}(0,0)-v^{*}_{-}\right|>\varepsilon_{1}.

(5.4)

Since
\tilde{v}>0
, the strong maximum principle applied to
\tilde{u}-
equation implies
\tilde{u}<1
.
Using
d=1
, Assumption
2.12
and Claim 7.1 in
[
10
]
, we obtain

(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})(t,x)\equiv(u^{*}_{-},v^{*}_{-}).

(5.5)

This contradicts (
5.4
) and completes the proof of (
2.7
).

The proofs of (
2.8
) and (
2.9
) are very similar. The only difference is the choice of the sequences
\{(t_{k},x_{k})\}
and hence the limiting system satisfied by
(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})
.
In the proof of (
2.8
), the sequence satisfies

x_{k}-c_{1}t_{k}\to-\infty
, and hence the limiting system is governed by
(f_{-},g_{-})
.
In the proof of (
2.9
), the sequence satisfies

x_{k}-c_{1}t_{k}\to+\infty
, hence the limiting system is governed by
(f_{+},g_{+})
.
The same Lyapunov argument then yields the desired contradiction.
The proof is complete.

5.2
The Lotka-Volterra prey-predator model

We now illustrate the result with the following Lotka–Volterra prey–predator system:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=d\partial_{xx}u+au(1-u-pv),&t>0,\ x\in\mathbb{R},\\
\partial_{t}v=\partial_{xx}v+v(b(x-c_{1}t)u-1-v),&t>0,\ x\in\mathbb{R},\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\quad v(0,x)=v_{0}(x),&x\in\mathbb{R},\end{cases}

(5.6)

where
u
and
v
denote the densities of the prey and the predator, respectively. The constant
d>0
is the diffusivity of the prey,
a>0
is the intrinsic growth rate of the prey, and
p>0
measures the predation rate. The function
b(x-c_{1}t)
describes the conversion rate of consumed prey into predator biomass. When
b
is monotone, the spreading speed of (
5.6
) was studied in
[
16
]
.

Example 5.1

Let
d,a,p
be positive constants and let
b:\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)
be bounded and uniformly continuous. Set

b_{\sup}:=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}b(z),\qquad r(z):=b(z)-1.

Assume that the limits

r^{\pm}:=\lim_{z\to\pm\infty}r(z)

exist and that
\inf_{z\in\mathbb{R}}r(z)>0
.
We require

\beta_{0}:=1-p(b_{\sup}-1)>0.

Let

\overline{V}:=b_{\sup}-1.

Then

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}g(z,1,\overline{V})=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\{b(z)-1-\overline{V}\}\leq 0.

Moreover, choosing
0<\delta<\beta_{0}
, we obtain

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathbb{R}\\
0\leq u\leq\delta\end{subarray}}f(z,u,\overline{V})=a\inf_{0\leq u\leq\delta}\{1-u-p\overline{V}\}>0.

Hence Assumption 2.3 is satisfied.

Let the initial data
u_{0},v_{0}\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R})
satisfy

0<\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}u_{0}(x)\leq u_{0}(x)\leq 1,\qquad 0\leq v_{0}(x)\leq\overline{V},\qquad v_{0}\not\equiv 0,

and assume that
v_{0}
is compactly supported. Then Assumptions 2.1, 2.3 and 2.4 are satisfied. Therefore Theorem 2.7 applies to (
5.6
), and the spreading speed of the predator is given by the formula in Theorem 2.7 with

g(z,1,0)=b(z)-1=r(z).

In particular, if
r^{+}<\Lambda_{1},

then the locking phenomenon occurs for

2\sqrt{r^{+}}<c_{1}<2\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}}.

If

2\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}}<2(\sqrt{r^{-}}+\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}-r^{-}}\;),

then the nonlocal pulling phenomenon occurs for

2\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}}<c_{1}<2(\sqrt{r^{-}}+\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}-r^{-}}\;).

Furthermore, let

b_{\pm}:=b(\pm\infty),\qquad u_{\pm}^{*}:=\frac{p+1}{pb_{\pm}+1},\qquad v_{\pm}^{*}:=\frac{b_{\pm}-1}{pb_{\pm}+1}.

Then
(u_{\pm}^{*},v_{\pm}^{*})
are the unique positive equilibria of the limiting Lotka–Volterra systems

\begin{cases}u_{t}=au(1-u-pv),\\
v_{t}=v(b_{\pm}u-1-v).\end{cases}

For
i\in\{+,-\}
, define

\Phi_{i}(u,v):=b_{i}\int_{u_{i}^{*}}^{u}\frac{\xi-u_{i}^{*}}{\xi}\,d\xi+ap\int_{v_{i}^{*}}^{v}\frac{\eta-v_{i}^{*}}{\eta}\,d\eta,\qquad u>0,\ v>0,

where
b_{+}=b(+\infty)
and
b_{-}=b(-\infty)
. Then
\Phi_{i}
is strictly convex and attains its minimum at
(u_{i}^{*},v_{i}^{*})
. A direct computation shows that

\displaystyle(uf_{i}(u,v),vg_{i}(u,v))\cdot\nabla\Phi_{i}(u,v)

\displaystyle=b_{i}(u-u_{i}^{*})f_{i}(u,v)+ap(v-v_{i}^{*})g_{i}(u,v)

\displaystyle=-ab_{i}(u-u_{i}^{*})^{2}-ap(v-v_{i}^{*})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 0,

where

f_{i}(u,v)=a(1-u-pv),\qquad g_{i}(u,v)=b_{i}u-1-v.

Thus the limiting ODE systems admit strict Lyapunov functions.

Consequently, the convergence conclusions in Corollary 2.13 apply to (
5.6
). Moreover, for this particular Lotka–Volterra model, the restriction
d=1
is not needed. Indeed, the Lyapunov function is separable:

\Phi_{i}(u,v)=\Psi_{1,i}(u)+\Psi_{2,i}(v).

The same argument used in the proof of Lemma 4.1 in
[
9
]
can be applied to prove that the limiting entire solutions are equal to the coexistence states for every
d>0
.
