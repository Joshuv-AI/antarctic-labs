Geometric Coastline Localization using Vision-Language Models Journal: Neurocomputing
Rafia Malik, Bernhard Pfahringer, Karin Bryan, Mark Dickson, Eibe Frank

Abstract
Coastline detection in remotely sensed imagery is a task that may benefit from automation, but machine learning-based approaches typically annotate individual pixels and neither model nor evaluate coastline geometry explicitly: generation of a vector-based representation is delegated to a post-processing step, if considered at all. This formulation relegates coastline geometry, the primary representation used in coastal change analysis, to a secondary artifact rather than the learning objective. Independent of that representational choice, coastlines used in practice are defined by geomorphic proxies such as vegetation lines, dune toes, or cliff edges rather than the instantaneous land-water boundary commonly used in pixel-based segmentation approaches. In this work, we revisit coastline extraction from a representation perspective and formulate the task as geometric boundary localization. Using the New Zealand Coastal Change Dataset (NZCCD) and high-resolution aerial imagery from Land Information New Zealand (LINZ), we develop CoastlineVLM-7B, a vision-language model (VLM) built on the GeoChat-7B/LLaVA-1.5 architecture that jointly performs coastline presence detection, proxy-type classification, and coastline grounding. Crucially, grounding a coastline with this model yields a thin coastline boundary represented as a polyline rather than a dense segmentation mask. We evaluate the approach against widely used deep learning-based segmentation models under strict one-pixel boundary supervision. Experimental results show that coastline extraction, under strict one-pixel supervision, is more effectively evaluated using geometric distance metrics rather than pixel-overlap metrics such as Intersection over Union (IoU). This observation motivates the use of a geometry-oriented evaluation framework. We show that CoastlineVLM-7B improves geometric alignment with the ground-truth coastlines compared to segmentation-based approaches, reducing worst-case geometric error and improving global boundary matching as measured by Hausdorff distance (reduced from 37.74 m to 31.84 m) and Earth Mover’s Distance (reduced from 21.12 m to 17.32 m). These results indicate that output representation is a critical design choice in coastline extraction, and that geometry-oriented learning, combined with the semantic reasoning capabilities of vision-language models, aligns well with how coastlines are defined and evaluated in operational coastal monitoring.

Introduction

Coastlines are a fundamental input for coastal hazard assessment, erosion monitoring, and long-term coastal change analysis
[
4
]
. In operational settings, coastlines are used as vector geometries for transect-based measurements, spatial analysis in geographic information systems (GIS), and expert interpretation
[
27
,
23
]
. In contrast to this operational use, most deep learning approaches to coastline detection formulate the task in pixel space, typically as a land-water segmentation problem
[
24
,
28
,
33
]
, and extract the final coastline through post-processing operations such as geometric contour extraction, skeletonization, and line simplification
[
10
,
25
]
.

This formulation introduces a representation mismatch between the learning objective and the geometric coastline products used in coastal analysis. Segmentation models are optimized to classify pixels
[
16
,
21
,
6
,
32
]
, whereas coastal monitoring workflows require accurate geometric boundaries. As a result, segmentation metrics such as intersection-over-union (IoU) or pixel accuracy poorly reflect coastline localization error, particularly when evaluating thin boundaries in high-resolution imagery. In addition, the vectorization stage introduces heuristic decisions such as thresholding, contour selection, and smoothing
[
10
,
25
]
, which vary across coast types and reduce reproducibility.

Another form of mismatch arises from the inconsistent use of the terms shoreline and coastline in the literature. While shoreline often refers to the instantaneous land-water boundary, coastal science typically defines coastlines using geomorphic proxies such as the vegetation line, dune toe, or cliff edge
[
4
]
. These proxies provide more stable indicators of coastal morphology and are widely adopted in operational monitoring datasets. For example, the New Zealand Coastal Change Dataset (NZCCD)
[
29
]
and the Victorian Coastal Monitoring Program (VCMP)
[
7
]
dataset explicitly define coastlines using proxy-dependent indicators rather than the instantaneous land-water boundary, reflecting how coastlines are interpreted in practical coastal analysis.

In this work, we revisit coastline extraction from a representation perspective and formulate the problem as geometric boundary localization. Instead of learning dense segmentation masks and deriving coastlines through post-processing, we train a vision-language model to localize the coastline boundary directly while simultaneously reasoning about coastline presence and proxy type. The proposed model, CoastlineVLM-7B, is built on the GeoChat-7B architecture
[
13
]
, which extends the LLaVA-1.5 multimodal framework
[
15
]
with object-grounding capabilities for remote sensing imagery. While GeoChat grounds objects using fixed bounding boxes, we generalize this capability to represent coastlines as polylines, which enables direct localization of thin coastline boundaries. CoastlineVLM-7B combines visual features with textual descriptions of coastline proxies to reduce ambiguity in complex coastal environments. Our qualitative results indicate that conventional segmentation models often misidentify roads, vegetation edges, or muddy water boundaries as coastlines, highlighting the limitations of such approaches.

To avoid the conceptual mismatch between pixel-based metrics and the evaluation of coastline localization approaches, we adopt a geometry-oriented evaluation framework based on distance metrics that quantify alignment between extracted and expert-generated reference coastlines. We measure geometric alignment using Chamfer distance
[
2
]
, Hausdorff distance
[
11
]
, Fréchet distance
[
9
]
, and the Earth Mover’s Distance
[
22
]
. Experimental results on our Coastline-Instruct dataset show that, under strict one
-
pixel supervision, CoastlineVLM-7B achieves improved geometric alignment with the reference coastline as measured by these distance-based evaluation metrics.

Our contributions are:

We revisit coastline extraction from a representation perspective and formulate the task as geometric boundary localization, highlighting the limitations of segmentation-centric approaches for coastline extraction.

2.

We introduce a new multi-task vision-language model, CoastlineVLM-7B, that jointly performs coastline presence detection, proxy-type classification, and coastline grounding.

3.

We propose a geometry-oriented evaluation framework for coastline localization using distance-based boundary metrics.

4.

We demonstrate that vision-language reasoning improves geometric coastline localization in complex coastal environments.

2
Related Work

2.1
Coastline Detection in Remote Sensing

Coastline extraction from remote sensing imagery is a fundamental task for coastal monitoring, erosion assessment, and hazard management
[
10
,
24
]
. A comprehensive review by Boak and Turner
[
4
]
emphasizes that coastal boundaries are defined using geomorphic proxies such as vegetation lines, dune toes, or cliff edges rather than the instantaneous land-water interface. Such proxy-based definitions are widely used in coastal geomorphology and monitoring applications.

Traditional coastline detection methods are based on spectral thresholding
[
28
]
, edge detection
[
10
]
, and object-based image analysis applied to aerial or satellite imagery
[
24
]
. More recently, automated workflows have been developed to detect coastlines in large volumes of satellite data. For example, CoastSat enables large-scale coastline monitoring using publicly available satellite imagery and cloud-based processing platforms
[
30
]
. Despite these advances, many approaches focus primarily on identifying the land-water interface rather than proxy-based coastlines used in coastal change studies.

Most deep learning approaches formulate coastline detection as a semantic segmentation problem
[
24
,
28
]
. Convolutional neural network architectures such as fully convolutional networks
[
16
]
, U-Net
[
21
]
, and DeepLabV3+
[
6
]
have been widely applied to segment land and water regions in remote sensing imagery. More recently, transformer-based segmentation architectures, such as SegFormer
[
32
]
, have been introduced to capture long-range spatial dependencies in visual scenes. Although these models improve segmentation accuracy compared to traditional approaches, they still produce raster outputs rather than explicit coastline geometries.

Some studies approach coastline detection indirectly by identifying coastal proxy indicators that correlate with shoreline position. For example, convolutional neural networks have been used to detect coastal vegetation edges from aerial imagery as proxies for coastline position
[
20
]
. Transformer-based architectures have also been used to capture long-range spatial dependencies when predicting coastal boundaries from remote sensing imagery
[
33
]
. However, these methods still treat coastline detection as a pixel classification problem and derive coastline boundaries through post-processing of segmentation outputs.

2.2
Geometric Boundary Extraction and Thin Structure Modelling

Beyond coastline detection, computer vision researchers have extensively studied the extraction of thin geometric structures from images. Examples include road network reconstruction
[
17
,
3
]
and building boundary extraction
[
1
]
. These problems share a common challenge: the target structures are spatially thin and geometrically constrained, making them difficult to evaluate using region-based segmentation metrics alone.

In remote sensing, building extraction research provides a representative example. Early deep learning approaches treated building detection as a semantic segmentation task using fully convolutional networks
[
16
,
21
]
. However, segmentation outputs often produce jagged or fragmented boundaries that require additional geometric regularization. Recent studies therefore explore approaches that explicitly model geometric primitives such as vertices, edges, or polygons. For instance, Polygon-RNN identifies object boundaries by sequentially regressing polygon vertices
[
5
]
. More recent work reconstructs building geometry using feature lines and topological reasoning to generate polygonal representations
[
31
]
. These approaches demonstrate that explicitly modelling geometric structure can improve alignment with manually delineated boundaries.

Similar challenges arise in other thin-structure extraction problems, which have often been addressed using segmentation-centric learning. For example, DeepRoadMapper reconstructs road topology from aerial imagery by converting pixel-wise predictions into structured road graphs
[
17
]
. In remote sensing, segmentation-based approaches have also been used to identify rivers and surface water boundaries from satellite imagery
[
12
]
. A more geometry-aware formulation is found in medical imaging, where DeepVesselNet predicts vessel centrelines and bifurcations to explicitly model vascular structure
[
26
]
.

The challenges addressed in these studies are closely related to coastline localization, where the target structure is also a spatially thin boundary. Prior work in domains such as building extraction and vessel analysis highlights that segmentation outputs alone may be insufficient for modelling thin geometric structures, motivating formulations that explicitly represent boundary geometry, as pursued in this work.

2.3
Vision-Language Models in Remote Sensing

Recent advances in multimodal learning have introduced vision-language models capable of integrating visual perception with natural language reasoning. Early large-scale models such as CLIP demonstrated the ability to learn joint visual-text representations from large image-text datasets
[
19
]
. Subsequent work extended these ideas to instruction-following multimodal systems. LLaVA combines a visual encoder with a large language model to enable multimodal reasoning over images using natural language prompts
[
15
]
.

In the remote sensing domain, several studies have adapted vision-language models to geospatial imagery. GeoChat is a multimodal model designed for visual reasoning in Earth observation imagery that extends the LLaVA framework using remote sensing training data and tasks
[
13
]
. Other recent work explores multimodal large language models for satellite image interpretation and geospatial reasoning
[
35
,
34
]
.

The use of multimodal models for explicit geometric boundary localization has received limited attention in remote sensing applications, where work on vision-language models has largely focused on high-level semantic reasoning tasks such as captioning, scene description, or question answering. While recent vision-language models demonstrate strong semantic reasoning capabilities for remote sensing imagery, they have rarely been applied to structured geometric localization tasks. Our work takes a step toward closing this gap in the context of coastline extraction by investigating whether direct geometric boundary localization can be effectively combined with multimodal reasoning over coastal proxy indicators.

Each dataset entry follows a conversational instruction format compatible with instruction-tuned vision-language models, allowing the dataset to support unified multi-task supervision within a single multimodal training framework. 3.2
Task Formulation

The Coastline-Instruct dataset supports a multi-task instruction-following formulation for coastline analysis and features the following three tasks:

Task I: Coastline Presence Detection.

The model predicts whether a coastline proxy is visible in the image using a binary question-answer format.

Task II: Proxy-Type Classification.

The model predicts the geomorphic proxy defining the coastline (e.g., vegetation line, dune toe, or cliff edge). This task introduces semantic reasoning about coastal morphology.

Task III: Coastline Grounding.

The model localizes the coastline as a polyline, represented by a text sequence of normalized image coordinate pairs in the form
[[x_{1},y_{1}],\ldots,[x_{K},y_{K}]]
, where each coordinate lies in the range
[0,100]
. This enables direct prediction of coastline geometry without requiring post-processing.

3.3
CoastlineVLM-7B Framework

Our proposed model,
CoastlineVLM-7B
, is built on the GeoChat-7B architecture, which itself extends the LLaVA-1.5 multimodal framework. LLaVA-1.5 couples a CLIP vision encoder and a Vicuna large language model via a multimodal projector that projects CLIP’s visual embeddings into Vicuna’s token embedding space. Vicuna is a LLaMA
-
based, decoder
-
only (i.e., autoregressive) transformer trained with cross
-
entropy and instruction
-
tuned for conversational use.

Figure

shows the overall CoastlineVLM-7B framework. It consists of three main components:

1.

extracts visual features from input aerial imagery.

2.

Multimodal Projector:
maps visual embeddings into the language model token space.

3.

Large Language Model:
generates textual responses conditioned on visual features and task prompts.

Through instruction tuning, the model learns to generate responses corresponding to coastline presence detection, proxy classification, and coastline grounding tasks.

Predictions are evaluated using geometric distance metrics.

3.4
CoastlineVLM Training Setup

The CoastlineVLM-7B model is fine-tuned using instruction-based supervision. Each training sample consists of an input image paired with a task prompt and the corresponding ground-truth response. The CLIP vision encoder is kept frozen during training to maintain stability and reduce overfitting given the relatively small dataset. Only the multimodal projector and LoRA adapters in the language backbone are fine-tuned during instruction tuning. Training is performed on four NVIDIA A100 GPUs, with inference conducted on a single A100 GPU. The full training configuration for the CoastlineVLM localization experiments is summarized in Table
2
. We report results across four random seeds. The seed controls stochastic aspects of training such as model initialization, data shuffling, and dropout, while the dataset splits remain fixed. A validation set (Hawke’s Bay region) is used during training for monitoring performance and selecting model checkpoints. Final results reported in this paper are evaluated on the held-out West Coast test set. Unless otherwise stated, CoastlineVLM-7B results correspond to the seed-42 model.

3.5
Segmentation Baselines

To provide a comparison with conventional pixel-wise methods, we train four semantic segmentation models for coastline boundary detection. The evaluated architectures include U-Net, U-Net++, DeepLabV3+, and SegFormer, which are widely used architectures for semantic segmentation in remote sensing imagery. All baseline models follow the training configuration described in the SWED dataset benchmark study
[
18
]
, including the same loss formulations and optimization settings. They are trained and evaluated using the same data splits and preprocessing pipeline as CoastlineVLM to ensure a consistent comparison.

During training, coastline polylines from the NZCCD dataset are rasterized into 1-pixel-wide binary boundary masks that serve as segmentation ground-truth. Since segmentation models produce raster outputs, an additional post-processing step is required to convert predicted masks into vector coastline geometries. At inference time, predicted segmentation masks are post-processed using skeletonization to extract a single-pixel centerline for geometric evaluation.

3.6
Polyline Representation and Fixed-K Resampling

Coastlines are represented as polylines with variable numbers of vertices. During dataset preprocessing, ground-truth coastlines are simplified using the Ramer-Douglas-Peucker (RDP) algorithm
[
8
]
and capped at a maximum of
K\leq 50
vertices to satisfy the context-window constraints of LLaVA-1.5. As a result, training polylines remain variable in length but are bounded by this upper limit.

For evaluation, geometric distance metrics can be sensitive to differences in vertex sampling density along the curve. To ensure consistent and fair comparison across methods, both predicted and ground-truth coastlines are uniformly resampled to a fixed number of vertices (
K=50
) using arc-length interpolation. This normalization is applied to outputs from both CoastlineVLM and the segmentation baselines prior to computing geometric metrics.
By enforcing a consistent vertex representation during evaluation, our geometric evaluation metrics are computed under consistent geometric conditions and are not influenced by differences in vertex sampling density.

The detailed arc-length resampling procedure is described in
B
, which provides the full mathematical formulation.

3.7
Evaluation Metrics

To evaluate localization accuracy, we adopt a geometry-based evaluation framework based on distance metrics that quantify geometric alignment between predicted and reference coastlines. Five complementary metrics are used to capture different aspects of geometric boundary alignment: Chamfer distance (average proximity), Modified Average Hausdorff distance (directional deviation), Hausdorff distance (worst-case error), Earth Mover’s Distance (global structural alignment), and Fréchet distance (ordered curve similarity). Table
3
provides a concise summary of these metrics and their objectives. The formal mathematical definitions are provided in
A
.

We also consider three custom tolerance-based metrics that measure the percentage of predicted coastline points located within fixed spatial tolerances (e.g., 5 m, 10 m, and 20 m) of the ground-truth coastline. These metrics quantify how much of the predicted coastline lies within predefined distance thresholds.

For the first two tasks, which are classification tasks, we report the F1 metric. In the case of the second task, we report both Micro-F1 and Macro-F1.

4.3
Comparison with Segmentation Baselines

To better interpret the above results, we contrast them with standard pixel-based metrics commonly used in semantic segmentation research. A representative example is Intersection over Union (IoU). However, coastline extraction in our setting is fundamentally a geometric localization problem.

Pixel-based metrics require exact pixel correspondence, which makes them unsuitable for evaluating thin one-pixel boundaries. If a pixel from an extracted coastline is close to a pixel from the ground-truth coastline, but the two pixels are not identical, this does not contribute to increasing the IoU.

Segmentation baselines, especially UNet, yield the highest scores when evaluated using pixel-overlap (IoU) criteria, but their predictions are more fragmented, which leads to increased geometric localization error. In contrast, the proposed VLM formulation predicts the coastline directly as a geometric curve, enabling more consistent alignment with the ground-truth coastline. This behavior is reflected in Table
5
, where CoastlineVLM-7B achieves the best Hausdorff distance and Earth Mover’s Distance while remaining competitive on average Hausdorff distance.

5
Limitations

Although the proposed approach demonstrates improved global coastline geometry, several limitations remain. First, coastline polylines are simplified during dataset preparation to a maximum of
K=50
vertices to maintain stable token lengths for the vision-language model. This simplification may limit geometric detail for highly curved coastlines. Second, the Coastline-Instruct dataset focuses on coastal environments within New Zealand, and further evaluation across diverse coastal morphologies will be necessary to assess broader geographic generalization. Third, the proxy-type distribution in the dataset is imbalanced, with vegetation and cliff proxies dominating the training data. While our proxy-balancing experiment indicates that improved class balance can further enhance performance, additional data collection for underrepresented proxy types would likely further improve performance.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

We presented CoastlineVLM-7B, a multimodal vision-language model for coastline localization from high-resolution aerial imagery. Unlike conventional segmentation-based approaches that treat coastline detection as a pixel-wise classification problem, the proposed method formulates coastline extraction as a geometric boundary localization task and predicts coastline geometry directly as a polyline. To train and evaluate the model, we introduced the Coastline-Instruct dataset, which combines aerial imagery with geomorphologically defined coastline proxies and instruction-based supervision for coastline detection, proxy-type reasoning, and geometric coastline grounding within a unified multimodal framework.

Experimental results on the West Coast test set demonstrate that CoastlineVLM-7B produces more coherent global coastline geometry compared with segmentation baselines, achieving the lowest Hausdorff distance and Earth Mover’s Distance among the evaluated models. While conventional segmentation architectures remain competitive under strict local tolerance thresholds, the proposed vision-language formulation provides improved global structural alignment with the ground-truth coastline.

Future work will investigate approaches to further improve the geometric representation of coastline boundaries in vision-language models. The current dataset focuses on New Zealand coastlines, and expanding the dataset to additional coastal environments and proxy types may improve generalization across diverse coastal morphologies. Improving the balance of proxy-type distributions through targeted data augmentation or resampling strategies may also enhance geometric localization accuracy. More broadly, integrating multimodal reasoning with structured geometric prediction may enable vision-language models to address a wider range of thin-boundary extraction problems in Earth observation, such as rivers and road networks.

Appendix A
Geometric Distance Metrics

This appendix provides the formal definitions of the geometric distance metrics used to evaluate coastline boundary alignment. The formulations follow standard definitions used in geometric matching and computer vision literature
[
2
,
11
,
9
,
22
]
.

Let the predicted coastline and ground-truth coastline be represented as two ordered sets of
K
resampled points:

A=\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K},\qquad B=\{b_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K},

where
a_{i},b_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
denote coastline coordinates in meters.

All distances are computed in meters after resampling both predicted and ground-truth coastlines to a fixed number of
K
arc-length equidistant points, except for Fréchet distance in the task-wise polyline grounding evaluation, which is computed in normalized coordinate space and scaled by the diagonal length of the normalized
100\times 100
coordinate plane.

A.1
Chamfer Distance

The symmetric Chamfer distance measures the average nearest-neighbour distance between two point sets. It is defined using directed components:

d_{A\rightarrow B}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\min_{1\leq j\leq K}\|a_{i}-b_{j}\|

(1)

d_{B\rightarrow A}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\min_{1\leq i\leq K}\|b_{j}-a_{i}\|

(2)

The symmetric Chamfer distance is then computed as

d_{CD}(A,B)=\frac{1}{2}\left(d_{A\rightarrow B}+d_{B\rightarrow A}\right)

(3)

A.2
Modified Average Hausdorff Distance

The Modified Average Hausdorff distance captures directional average boundary deviation by taking the maximum of the directed mean nearest-neighbour distances:

m_{A\rightarrow B}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\min_{1\leq j\leq K}\|a_{i}-b_{j}\|

(4)

m_{B\rightarrow A}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\min_{1\leq i\leq K}\|b_{j}-a_{i}\|

(5)

The symmetric Modified Average Hausdorff distance is defined as

d_{AH}(A,B)=\max(m_{A\rightarrow B},\,m_{B\rightarrow A})

(6)

A.3
Hausdorff Distance

The Hausdorff distance captures the worst-case boundary deviation between two point sets. The directed components are defined as

h_{A\rightarrow B}=\max_{1\leq i\leq K}\min_{1\leq j\leq K}\|a_{i}-b_{j}\|

(7)

h_{B\rightarrow A}=\max_{1\leq j\leq K}\min_{1\leq i\leq K}\|b_{j}-a_{i}\|

(8)

The symmetric Hausdorff distance is then given by

d_{H}(A,B)=\max(h_{A\rightarrow B},\,h_{B\rightarrow A})

(9)

A.4
Discrete Fréchet Distance

The discrete Fréchet distance measures similarity between two ordered curves while preserving point order along the curves. It is defined as

d_{F}(A,B)=\min_{\alpha,\beta}\max_{t}\|A(\alpha(t))-B(\beta(t))\|,

(10)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are order-preserving mappings along the two polylines.

A.5
Earth Mover’s Distance

Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD) measures global structural correspondence by computing the optimal transport cost between two point sets. Let
\pi
denote the permutation minimizing the total transport cost:

\pi^{*}=\arg\min_{\pi}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\|a_{i}-b_{\pi(i)}\|

(11)

The EMD is then defined as

d_{EMD}(A,B)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\|a_{i}-b_{\pi^{*}(i)}\|

(12)

Appendix B
Uniform Arc-Length Resampling

To ensure consistent evaluation, both predicted and ground-truth polylines are uniformly resampled to a fixed number of vertices
K
using arc-length parameterization.

Let a polyline be defined as an ordered sequence of vertices:

P=\{p_{0},p_{1},\dots,p_{N-1}\},\quad p_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

The cumulative arc-length along the polyline is computed as:

s_{i}=\sum_{t=0}^{i-1}\|p_{t+1}-p_{t}\|

with total length

L=s_{N-1}

We then define
K
uniformly spaced arc-length positions

t_{k}=\frac{k}{K-1}L,\quad k=0,\dots,K-1

For each target arc-length
t_{k}
, the corresponding polyline segment
[p_{j},p_{j+1}]
satisfying

s_{j}\leq t_{k}\leq s_{j+1}

is identified. The resampled vertex is obtained via linear interpolation:

p(t_{k})=(1-\alpha)p_{j}+\alpha p_{j+1}

where

\alpha=\frac{t_{k}-s_{j}}{s_{j+1}-s_{j}}

This procedure produces a uniformly spaced polyline with exactly
K
vertices.
