Sylvester’s Theorem and Reduced Linear Systems From Three-Factor Products to Small Dynamical Models Lecture notes for an upper-level course in numerical analysis
James M. Hyman, ORCID 0000-0001-5247-5794

Abstract
Large systems of linear equations often contain far fewer active degrees of freedom than their ambient dimension suggests. A particularly transparent instance occurs when a large matrix factors as 𝐀 = 𝐓𝐒𝐖 , \mathbf{A}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}, where 𝐓 ∈ 𝔽 m × n \mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times n} , 𝐒 ∈ 𝔽 n × n \mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n} , 𝐖 ∈ 𝔽 n × m \mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times m} , and m ≫ n m\gg n . The action of 𝐀 \mathbf{A} then passes through an n n -dimensional intermediate space. Sylvester’s theorem relates the nonzero eigenvalues of a product 𝐗𝐘 \mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y} to those of the reversed product 𝐘𝐗 \mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X} . Applied cyclically, it shows that the nonzero spectrum of the large matrix 𝐀 \mathbf{A} is completely determined by either of the small matrices 𝐁 = ( 𝐖𝐓 ) ​ 𝐒 , 𝐂 = 𝐒 ⁡ ( 𝐖𝐓 ) . \mathbf{B}=(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T})\mathbf{S},\qquad\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}). This article develops the theorem from first principles and explains the geometry behind the factorization. The result yields exact reduced systems, both for linear algebraic equations and for first-order linear ordinary differential equations. Worked examples carry this through in detail: spectral reduction, reconstruction of full-space eigenvectors, reduced solution of shifted systems, and exact integration of a three-dimensional ODE through a two-dimensional model. A separate section takes up singular values. Those of 𝐀 \mathbf{A} , 𝐁 \mathbf{B} , and 𝐂 \mathbf{C} do not agree in general, though they do when the bases are orthonormal, a case the same theorem settles once it is applied to 𝐀 ∗ ​ 𝐀 \mathbf{A}^{*}\mathbf{A} . The closing sections separate exact factorization from projection-based approximation, and flag what the theorem leaves undetermined: zero-eigenvalue structure, conditioning, and transient behavior. The material is classical. What this article adds is one continuous development, from first principles through to the limits of the theorem, pitched for an upper-level undergraduate course.

A matrix may be large because the state has many components, yet its interactions may depend on only a few combinations of those components. Suppose

How does this exact structure relate to approximate projection-based model reduction?

The central tool is a theorem usually associated with James Joseph Sylvester. In modern form, it states that
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}
have the same nonzero eigenvalues, including algebraic multiplicities, even when the two products have different dimensions. Sylvester wrote about the “latent roots” of reversed matrix products in 1883
(
Sylvester, 1883
)
. The determinant identity used today is often called Sylvester’s determinant theorem or Sylvester’s determinant identity.

The computational motivation is equally important. A dense
m\times m
matrix requires
m^{2}
stored entries, and standard dense eigenvalue algorithms require on the order of
m^{3}
arithmetic operations. By contrast, the factors in
equation

2
require about
2mn+n^{2}
entries. Forming
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}
costs on the order of
mn^{2}
, after which the principal spectral work occurs in dimension
n
. For
m\gg n
, this is a decisive reduction. Similar considerations motivate the broad field of reduced-order modeling
(
Antoulas, 2005
;
Benner et al., 2015
;
Quarteroni et al., 2016
)
.

The distinction between two uses of the word
reduction
will be maintained throughout.

Definition 1.1

(Exact factor reduction)
.

An exact factor reduction begins with a matrix that actually satisfies

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}.

All identities derived from this equality are exact.

Definition 1.2

(Projection-based approximation)
.

Given a general full matrix
\mathbf{L}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times m}
, a projection method chooses trial and test maps and constructs a smaller matrix
\mathbf{L}_{r}
so that

\mathbf{L}\approx\mathbf{T}\mathbf{L}_{r}\mathbf{W}.

The equality between the lifted reduced operator and the original operator is generally approximate.

Sylvester’s theorem gives exact information about
\mathbf{T}\mathbf{L}_{r}\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{L}_{r}
. It does not, by itself, guarantee that
\mathbf{L}_{r}
accurately approximates the spectrum or trajectories of
\mathbf{L}
. That requires additional analysis of the chosen reduced space.

2.2
Eigenvalues and characteristic polynomials

For a square matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{F}^{k\times k}
, an eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
satisfies

\mathbf{M}\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}

for some nonzero vector
\mathbf{v}
. The eigenvalues are the roots of the characteristic polynomial

p_{\mathbf{M}}(\lambda)=\det(\lambda\mathbf{I}_{k}-\mathbf{M}).

(7)

In practice one almost never forms a characteristic polynomial to compute eigenvalues. Its coefficients can be extremely sensitive to perturbation, so recovering accurate roots from them is unreliable even in modest dimensions. Determinant identities remain valuable as proof tools. That is how they are used here: to establish spectral equivalence, not to compute anything.

2.3
Schur complements

The proof of Sylvester’s determinant identity uses a standard block determinant formula. If
\mathbf{P}
is invertible, then

\det\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{P}&\mathbf{Q}\\
\mathbf{R}&\mathbf{S}\end{pmatrix}=\det(\mathbf{P})\det(\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{R}\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{Q}).

(8)

If
\mathbf{S}
is invertible, then

\det\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{P}&\mathbf{Q}\\
\mathbf{R}&\mathbf{S}\end{pmatrix}=\det(\mathbf{S})\det(\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{R}).

(9)

Both identities follow from block Gaussian elimination. Factor the block matrix into block triangular factors, then use the fact that a block triangular determinant is the product of its diagonal blocks. Having two formulas for the same determinant is the entire mechanism of the proof in
section

3
. Standard references for matrix analysis and computation include
Horn and Johnson (2013)
and
Golub and Van Loan (2013)
.

3
Sylvester’s theorem

3.1
The determinant identity

Theorem 3.1

(Sylvester’s determinant identity)
.

Let

\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times n},\qquad\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times m}.

Then

\det(\mathbf{I}_{m}+\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y})=\det(\mathbf{I}_{n}+\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}).

(10)

Proof.

Consider the block matrix

\mathbf{K}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{m}&\mathbf{X}\\
-\mathbf{Y}&\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

(11)

Using the upper-left block
\mathbf{I}_{m}
in the Schur-complement formula gives

\det(\mathbf{K})=\det(\mathbf{I}_{m})\det(\mathbf{I}_{n}+\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X})=\det(\mathbf{I}_{n}+\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}).

Using the lower-right block
\mathbf{I}_{n}
instead gives

\det(\mathbf{K})=\det(\mathbf{I}_{n})\det(\mathbf{I}_{m}+\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y})=\det(\mathbf{I}_{m}+\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}).

The two expressions are equal because they are both
\det(\mathbf{K})
.
∎

The theorem is striking because the two determinants can have different sizes. One is
m\times m
, the other
n\times n
, yet they agree exactly.
Corollary

3.2
will explain why. The extra
m-n
eigenvalues of
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}
are all zero, so the matching eigenvalues of
\mathbf{I}_{m}+\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}
equal one and contribute nothing to the determinant.

3.2
The characteristic-polynomial form

Corollary 3.2

(Rectangular product theorem)
.

Suppose
m\geq n
. Then

\det(\lambda\mathbf{I}_{m}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y})=\lambda^{m-n}\det(\lambda\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}).

(12)

Consequently,
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}
have the same nonzero eigenvalues, including algebraic multiplicities.

Proof.

For
\lambda\neq 0
, factor
\lambda
from each determinant:

\displaystyle\det(\lambda\mathbf{I}_{m}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle=\lambda^{m}\det\!\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}-\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{m}\det\!\left(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{m-n}\det(\lambda\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}),

where
theorem

3.1
is applied with

\mathbf{X}
replaced by
-\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{X}
. Both sides of

equation

12
are polynomials in
\lambda
, so equality for all nonzero
\lambda
implies equality for every
\lambda
.
∎

(Terminology and history)

Sylvester’s 1883 paper used the language of latent roots and emphasized that reversing the order of two factors preserves the nonzero spectral information

3.3
The eigenvector mapping

The determinant proof establishes algebraic multiplicities. A direct eigenvector proof explains the geometry.

Proposition 3.4

(Eigenvector transfer)
.

Let
\lambda\neq 0
.

(a)

If
\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}
, then

\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}
and

\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v})=\lambda(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}).

(b)

If
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{u}=\lambda\mathbf{u}
, then

\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{u}\neq\mathbf{0}
and

\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{u})=\lambda(\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{u}).

Proof.

Suppose
\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}
. If

\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}
, then the left side is zero, which would imply
\lambda\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}
. Since
\lambda\neq 0
, this contradicts
\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}
, so
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}
, and

\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v})=\lambda\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}.

The reverse statement is analogous.
∎

3.4
Why zero is different

The theorem does not say that the two products have identical behavior at zero. If
m>n
, the larger product has at least
m-n
additional zero eigenvalues. Even when both products are square of the same size, their Jordan blocks at zero can differ.

Example 3.5

(Different null structures)
.

Let

\mathbf{X}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{Y}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Then

\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Both matrices have only the eigenvalue zero, but the first is a nonzero nilpotent matrix with a Jordan block of size two, whereas the second is the zero matrix.

The detailed relation between the elementary divisors of
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}
, including their zero Jordan blocks, was analyzed by
Flanders (1951)
. For reduced-system analysis, the most frequently used part is the exact agreement of the nonzero spectrum.

(The reduced matrices)
.

The matrices

and

\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{M}

Proposition 4.3

(When
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{C}
are similar)
.

If either
\mathbf{S}
or
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}
is invertible, then
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{C}
are similar.

Proof.

If
\mathbf{S}
is invertible, then

\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{S})\mathbf{S}^{-1}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{S}^{-1}.

If
\mathbf{M}

\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{M}^{-1}(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{S})\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{M}.

\mathbf{C}

A worked linear algebra example

The following example is small enough to compute by hand but large enough to show that
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{C}
need not be equal.

Example 5.1

(A three-dimensional operator reduced to dimension two)
.

Let

\mathbf{T}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{S}=\begin{pmatrix}-2&0\\
0&-3\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{W}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&1\end{pmatrix}.

(23)

Then

\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
1&2\end{pmatrix}.

(24)

The two reduced matrices are

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{S}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-2&0\\
-2&-6\end{pmatrix},

(25)

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{M}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-2&0\\
-3&-6\end{pmatrix}.

(26)

They are different matrices, but both are triangular with eigenvalues
-2
and
-6
.

The full matrix is

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}=\begin{pmatrix}-2&0&0\\
0&-3&-3\\
-2&-3&-3\end{pmatrix}.

(27)

A direct determinant calculation gives

\det(\lambda\mathbf{I}_{3}-\mathbf{A})=\lambda(\lambda+2)(\lambda+6).

(28)

Meanwhile,

\det(\lambda\mathbf{I}_{2}-\mathbf{B})=\det(\lambda\mathbf{I}_{2}-\mathbf{C})=(\lambda+2)(\lambda+6).

(29)

Equation

15
becomes

\lambda(\lambda+2)(\lambda+6)=\lambda(\lambda+2)(\lambda+6).

The extra factor
\lambda
is the inactive dimension in the full space.

5.1
Reconstructing full eigenvectors

For
\lambda=-6
,
equation

26
gives

\mathbf{C}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}=-6\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

By
proposition

4.5
, lift with
\mathbf{T}
:

\mathbf{x}_{-6}=\mathbf{T}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

(30)

Multiplying by
\mathbf{A}
confirms this:

\mathbf{A}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\\
1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
-6\\
-6\end{pmatrix}=-6\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

For
\lambda=-2
, an eigenvector of
\mathbf{C}
is

\mathbf{v}_{-2}=\begin{pmatrix}4\\
-3\end{pmatrix},

which lifts to

\mathbf{x}_{-2}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{v}_{-2}=\begin{pmatrix}4\\
-3\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

(31)

Direct multiplication verifies
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{-2}=-2\mathbf{x}_{-2}
.

The zero eigenvalue requires separate treatment. Solving
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
gives, for example,

\mathbf{x}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
-1\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

(32)

This vector belongs to
\operatorname{null}(\mathbf{W})
, as predicted by
equation

6
.

5.2
What the example teaches

The example separates three ideas that are sometimes conflated.

1.

The matrices
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{C}
can differ entry by entry.

2.

Their characteristic polynomials are nevertheless identical.

3.

Beyond what the reduced matrices already contain, the large matrix
\mathbf{A}
contributes only additional zero eigenvalues.

For an
m\times m
problem with
m
in the millions and
n
in the tens or hundreds, the same algebra replaces an infeasible full spectral calculation by a manageable reduced one.

Factorizations of this shape arise in two ways. They can be built deliberately, by projecting a large operator onto a chosen low-dimensional subspace, which is the subject of
section

11
. They also arise structurally, whenever the components of a large state interact only through a small number of aggregated quantities, so that the coupling passes through
\mathbf{W}
before returning through
\mathbf{T}
. The shifted systems of
section

10
, where a low-rank interaction sits on top of uniform decay, are of the second kind.

6.2
The reduced matrices place no upper bound on the norm

This is not a matter of small discrepancies. Both reduced matrices can be held completely fixed while the norm of
\mathbf{A}
grows without bound.

Example 6.1

(Fixed spectrum, unbounded norm)
.

Take
m=2
,
n=1
, and

\mathbf{T}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{W}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\kappa\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{S}=\begin{pmatrix}1\end{pmatrix},

with
\kappa
real. Then
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{I}_{1}
, so

\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{S}=\begin{pmatrix}1\end{pmatrix}

for every
\kappa
. The full matrix is

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\kappa\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

whose eigenvalues are
1
and
0
, again for every
\kappa
. Its singular values are

\sqrt{1+\kappa^{2}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad 0.

Either ordering gives them. Forming
\mathbf{A}^{*}\mathbf{A}
leads to a characteristic equation, while the reversed product

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}1+\kappa^{2}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}

is diagonal and gives the nonzero value by inspection. Choosing the convenient ordering is the same move the rest of this article makes with
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
.
The reduced matrix reports the same thing at every
\kappa
, while
\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert_{2}
grows without bound.

A reduced model therefore does not determine the conditioning of the full operator, which has to be established separately from the factors themselves. The reduced spectrum is not entirely silent: since
\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert_{2}
is at least the spectral radius, and that radius is shared with
\mathbf{B}
, the reduced eigenvalues bound
\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert_{2}
from below. They place no bound on it from above, which is what
example

6.1
exploits.

6.3
When the singular values do agree

There is one important case in which singular values transfer exactly. The statement below is classical: it is the invariance of singular values under unitary transformation
(
Horn and Johnson, 2013
, Section 2.6)
, extended to a rectangular isometry. The proof is given anyway, because it is another application of
corollary

3.2
, this time to
\mathbf{A}^{*}\mathbf{A}
rather than to
\mathbf{A}
.

Proposition 6.2

(Orthonormal bases preserve singular values)
.

Suppose the columns of
\mathbf{T}
are orthonormal, so that
\mathbf{T}^{*}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
, and take
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{T}^{*}
. Then
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{S}
, and the nonzero singular values of
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{T}^{*}
are exactly the nonzero singular values of
\mathbf{S}
.

Proof.

Because
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{T}^{*}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
, the matrix
\mathbf{M}
is the identity, so
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{S}
. For the singular values,

\mathbf{A}^{*}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}^{*}(\mathbf{T}^{*}\mathbf{T})\mathbf{S}\mathbf{T}^{*}=\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{S}^{*}\mathbf{S})\mathbf{T}^{*}.

Apply
corollary

3.2
with

\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{S}^{*}\mathbf{S}),\qquad\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{T}^{*}.

Then
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{A}^{*}\mathbf{A}
and

\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{S}^{*}\mathbf{S}
, so the two have the same nonzero eigenvalues. Taking positive square roots gives the statement.
∎

Remark 6.3

(Biorthogonality is not enough)
.

The condition
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
by itself does not give this conclusion, as
example

6.1
shows. It does force
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{S}
. Two hypotheses share the work in
proposition

6.2
. Orthonormal columns,
\mathbf{T}^{*}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
, cancel the inner factor of
equation

33
, leaving
\mathbf{A}^{*}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{W}^{*}(\mathbf{S}^{*}\mathbf{S})\mathbf{W}
. The pairing
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{T}^{*}
then collapses the outer factors: by
corollary

3.2
applied once more, the nonzero eigenvalues of
\mathbf{W}^{*}(\mathbf{S}^{*}\mathbf{S})\mathbf{W}
are those of
(\mathbf{S}^{*}\mathbf{S})(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{*})
, and
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{T}^{*}
forces
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{*}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
. Neither hypothesis can be dropped. Taking
\mathbf{T}=(1,0)^{\mathsf{T}}
,
\mathbf{W}=(1,3)
, and
\mathbf{S}=(1)
gives both
\mathbf{T}^{*}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{I}_{1}
and
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{I}_{1}
, yet
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{*}=10
, so the one nonzero singular value of
\mathbf{A}
is
\sqrt{10}
rather than
1
. An oblique biorthogonal pair leaves
equation

33
with oblique outer factors.

6.4
Why this matters in practice

Three consequences deserve attention.

Condition numbers do not transfer. The sensitivity of a full linear system cannot be read off a reduced matrix, and the same holds for least-squares problems, where sensitivity is governed by singular values rather than by eigenvalues.

Errors can grow when a reduced solution is lifted. That growth is measured by
\left\lVert\mathbf{T}\right\rVert
, a quantity invisible to both
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{C}
.
Section

13
returns to the point.

The reduction strategies surveyed in
section

11
are themselves built from singular values. Proper orthogonal decomposition selects directions by the singular values of a snapshot matrix, and balanced truncation ranks states by Hankel singular values
(
Antoulas, 2005
)
. Neither is a spectral construction. This is one reason orthonormal bases are preferred whenever a reduced model has to be trusted quantitatively.

7
Reduced algebraic systems

7.1
Applying the full operator without forming it

Given
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{m}
, compute
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}
in three steps:

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x},

(34)

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{q},

(35)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{r}.

(36)

No
m\times m
matrix is required. For dense factors, the cost is on the order of
mn+n^{2}
operations rather than
m^{2}
. If
\mathbf{T}
or
\mathbf{W}
is sparse or has fast transforms, the savings can be larger.

7.2
Powers of the large matrix

Proposition 7.1

(Powers through the reduced matrices)
.

For every integer
k\geq 1
,

\mathbf{A}^{k}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}^{k-1}\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{C}^{k-1}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}.

(37)

Proof.

For the first identity, the case
k=1
is
equation

2
. If it holds for
k
, then

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}^{k}\mathbf{A}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}^{k-1}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}^{k-1}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T})\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}^{k}\mathbf{W}.

The proof of the second identity is analogous, grouping

\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{C}
.
∎

Repeated application of
\mathbf{A}
therefore reduces to repeated application of either
\mathbf{B}
or
\mathbf{C}
. For example,

\mathbf{A}^{k}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{B}^{k-1}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}).

(38)

The full dimension appears only in the first extraction and the final lifting.

7.3
Shifted linear systems and the resolvent

The matrix
\mathbf{A}
is singular whenever
m>n
, so a system

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}
may have no solution or many solutions. A more useful problem is the shifted system

(\mu\mathbf{I}_{m}-\mathbf{A})\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b},

(39)

where
\mu\neq 0
.

Theorem 7.2

(Reduced resolvent formula)
.

Assume
\mu\neq 0
and
\mu\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{B}
is invertible. Then

(\mu\mathbf{I}_{m}-\mathbf{A})^{-1}=\frac{1}{\mu}\mathbf{I}_{m}+\frac{1}{\mu}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}(\mu\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{B})^{-1}\mathbf{W}.

(40)

Proof.

Let

\mathbf{R}=\frac{1}{\mu}\mathbf{I}_{m}+\frac{1}{\mu}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}(\mu\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{B})^{-1}\mathbf{W}.

Then

\displaystyle(\mu\mathbf{I}_{m}-\mathbf{A})\mathbf{R}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}_{m}-\frac{1}{\mu}\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}(\mu\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{B})^{-1}\mathbf{W}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{\mu}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}(\mu\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{B})^{-1}\mathbf{W}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}_{m}-\frac{1}{\mu}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\left[\mathbf{I}_{n}-\frac{1}{\mu}\mathbf{B}\right](\mu\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{B})^{-1}\mathbf{W}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}_{m}-\frac{1}{\mu}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}+\frac{1}{\mu}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}_{m}.

Because
\mu\mathbf{I}_{m}-\mathbf{A}
is square, a right inverse is the inverse, so
\mathbf{R}=(\mu\mathbf{I}_{m}-\mathbf{A})^{-1}
. The formula is a low-rank case of the Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury identity, the standard tool for inverting a matrix that differs from an easily inverted one by a low-rank term.
∎

The theorem converts
equation

39
into the following procedure:

1.

Form the reduced right-hand side
\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{b}
.

2.

Solve

(\mu\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{B})\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{g}.

(41)

3.

Reconstruct

\mathbf{x}=\frac{1}{\mu}\mathbf{b}+\frac{1}{\mu}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{z}.

(42)

Example 7.3

(Solving a shifted system through dimension two)
.

Use the matrices from
example

5.1
, take
\mu=1
, and let

\mathbf{b}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
2\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

The reduced right-hand side is

\mathbf{W}\mathbf{b}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
2\end{pmatrix}.

Since

\mathbf{I}_{2}-\mathbf{B}=\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\
2&7\end{pmatrix},

we solve

\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\
2&7\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{z}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
2\end{pmatrix}

to obtain

\mathbf{z}=\begin{pmatrix}1/3\\
4/21\end{pmatrix}.

(43)

Then

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1/3\\
10/7\\
-26/21\end{pmatrix}.

Direct substitution verifies

(\mathbf{I}_{3}-\mathbf{A})\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}.

The only linear solve was
2\times 2
.

7.4
Matrix functions

The same power identity reduces analytic functions of
\mathbf{A}
. For background on matrix functions, see
Higham (2008)
.

Proposition 7.4

(Analytic matrix functions)
.

Suppose
f
has a power series about the origin whose radius of convergence exceeds the spectral radius of
\mathbf{A}
, equivalently of
\mathbf{B}
, since the two share their nonzero eigenvalues. Define

g(z)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{f(z)-f(0)}{z},&z\neq 0,\\[6.0pt]
f^{\prime}(0),&z=0.\end{cases}

(44)

Then

f(\mathbf{A})=f(0)\mathbf{I}_{m}+\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}g(\mathbf{B})\mathbf{W}.

(45)

Proof.

Write
f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}z^{k}
. Then

f(\mathbf{A})=a_{0}\mathbf{I}_{m}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}\mathbf{A}^{k}.

Using
equation

37
,

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{A})

\displaystyle=a_{0}\mathbf{I}_{m}+\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}\mathbf{B}^{k-1}\right)\mathbf{W}

\displaystyle=f(0)\mathbf{I}_{m}+\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}g(\mathbf{B})\mathbf{W}.

∎

For the matrix exponential, define

\varphi_{1}(z)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\mathrm{e}^{z}-1}{z},&z\neq 0,\\[6.0pt]
1,&z=0.\end{cases}

(46)

Then

\mathrm{e}^{t\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{I}_{m}+t\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\varphi_{1}(t\mathbf{B})\mathbf{W}.

(47)

This formula will reappear in the ODE analysis.

8
Exact reduction of first-order linear ODEs

Consider the autonomous system

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}(t)=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t),\qquad\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W},\qquad\mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{x}_{0}.

(48)

There are two natural reduced variables. They lead to
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{C}
, respectively.

8.1
Reduction by extracted observables

Define

\mathbf{q}(t)=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}(t)\in\mathbb{F}^{n}.

(49)

Then

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{q}(t).

(50)

The initial condition is

\mathbf{q}(0)=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}_{0}.

(51)

Equation

50
is an exact closed system for the extracted variables, and it holds for every initial state
\mathbf{x}_{0}
with no restriction whatever.

Once
\mathbf{q}(t)
is known, the full derivative is

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}(t)=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{q}(t),

(52)

so

\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{x}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{q}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

(53)

Because

\mathbf{q}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{t\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}_{0},

this integral reproduces
equation

47
.

8.2
Reduction by lifted coordinates

Suppose
\mathbf{T}
has full column rank and the initial condition belongs to
{T})
. Write

\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{y}_{0}.

(54)

Because
{T})
is invariant under
\mathbf{A}
by
corollary

4.4
, every term of the series for
\mathrm{e}^{t\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{x}_{0}
lies in that subspace, so the solution remains there for all
t
. Write

\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{y}(t).

(55)

Substitution into
equation

48
gives

\mathbf{T}\mathbf{y}^{\prime}(t)=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{y}(t)=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y}(t).

Because
\mathbf{T}
has full column rank,

\mathbf{y}^{\prime}(t)=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{y}(t),\qquad\mathbf{y}(0)=\mathbf{y}_{0}.

(56)

The full solution is reconstructed simply by

\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{T}\mathrm{e}^{t\mathbf{C}}\mathbf{y}_{0}.

(57)

The two reduced states have different meanings:

\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}\quad\text{contains extracted observables,}\qquad\mathbf{y}\quad\text{contains coefficients in the columns of }\mathbf{T}.

(58)

If
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{y}
, then

\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{y}.

(59)

When
\mathbf{M}
is invertible, the two reduced coordinates are related by a change of basis, and
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{C}
are similar.

The two reductions also differ in what they require. The observable reduction
equation

50
needs no assumption on
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, because
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}
is defined for every full state. The lifted reduction
equation

56
applies only when
\mathbf{x}_{0}
lies in
{T})
. Outside that subspace there is no
\mathbf{y}_{0}
to start from.

8.3
The biorthogonal decomposition of a trajectory

If
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
, then
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{W}
is a projection. Decompose

\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{x}_{0}+(\mathbf{I}_{m}-\mathbf{P})\mathbf{x}_{0}.

(60)

The second term,
(\mathbf{I}_{m}-\mathbf{P})\mathbf{x}_{0}
, lies in
\operatorname{null}(\mathbf{W})
, so it is annihilated by
\mathbf{A}
and holds its initial value for all
t
. It is this whole component that stays fixed, not any single coordinate of
\mathbf{x}
. The active component evolves in
{T})
. The trajectory splits accordingly:

\mathbf{x}(t)=(\mathbf{I}_{m}-\mathbf{P})\mathbf{x}_{0}+\mathbf{T}\mathrm{e}^{t\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}_{0}.

(61)

This formula cleanly separates inactive directions from reduced dynamics.

9
A worked ODE example

Example 9.1

(A three-dimensional ODE solved in dimension two)
.

Let

\mathbf{T}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{W}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{S}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\
0&-2\end{pmatrix}.

(62)

Here

\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{I}_{2},

(63)

so
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{S}
. The full matrix is

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1&0\\
0&-2&0\\
-1&-1&0\end{pmatrix}.

(64)

Consider

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x},\qquad\mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{T}\begin{pmatrix}a\\
b\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a\\
b\\
a+b\end{pmatrix}.

(65)

Because the initial state lies in
{T})
, write

\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{y}
. The reduced problem is

\mathbf{y}^{\prime}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{y},\qquad\mathbf{y}(0)=\begin{pmatrix}a\\
b\end{pmatrix},

(66)

or, componentwise,

\displaystyle y_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-y_{1}+y_{2},

(67)

\displaystyle y_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-2y_{2}.

(68)

The second equation gives

y_{2}(t)=b\mathrm{e}^{-2t}.

(69)

Using an integrating factor in the first equation, and fixing the constant so that
y_{1}(0)=a
,

y_{1}(t)=(a+b)\mathrm{e}^{-t}-b\mathrm{e}^{-2t}.

(70)

Lifting with
\mathbf{T}
gives

\mathbf{x}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}(a+b)\mathrm{e}^{-t}-b\mathrm{e}^{-2t}\\
b\mathrm{e}^{-2t}\\
(a+b)\mathrm{e}^{-t}\end{pmatrix}.

(71)

Direct differentiation verifies
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}
.

9.1
Spectral interpretation

The eigenvalues of
\mathbf{S}
are
-1
and
-2
. Sylvester’s theorem predicts that the nonzero eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
are
-1
and
-2
. Because
m-n=1
, the full matrix has one additional zero eigenvalue:

\operatorname{spec}(\mathbf{A})=\{0,-1,-2\}.

(72)

The zero eigenvector
\mathbf{e}_{3}=(0,0,1)^{\mathsf{T}}
lies outside
{T})
and belongs to
\operatorname{null}(\mathbf{W})
. It represents an inactive component that remains constant. The chosen initial condition in
equation

has no inactive component, so only the two decaying modes appear in
equation

.

9.2
A general initial condition

For an arbitrary

\mathbf{x}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{10}\\
x_{20}\\
x_{30}\end{pmatrix},

the projector is

\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{W}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
1&1&0\end{pmatrix}.

(73)

The inactive part is

(\mathbf{I}_{3}-\mathbf{P})\mathbf{x}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
x_{30}-x_{10}-x_{20}\end{pmatrix}.

(74)

It remains constant, while the active variables evolve according to
\mathbf{S}
. This gives the full solution

\mathbf{x}(t)=(\mathbf{I}_{3}-\mathbf{P})\mathbf{x}_{0}+\mathbf{T}\mathrm{e}^{t\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}_{0}.

(75)

10
Shifted systems, forcing, and stability

A pure low-rank generator
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}
has at least
m-n
zero eigenvalues. Therefore the origin cannot be asymptotically stable for

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}
when
m>n
. In applications, the low-rank interaction often appears together with full-space decay:

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(-\gamma\mathbf{I}_{m}+\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W})\mathbf{x},\qquad\gamma>0.

(76)

Theorem 10.1

(Spectrum of a shifted low-rank system)
.

Let

\mathbf{J}=-\gamma\mathbf{I}_{m}+\mathbf{A}.

Then

\det(\lambda\mathbf{I}_{m}-\mathbf{J})=(\lambda+\gamma)^{m-n}\det\!\left((\lambda+\gamma)\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{B}\right).

(77)

Hence the spectrum consists of

-\gamma\quad\text{with multiplicity at least }m-n,

(78)

and

-\gamma+\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{B}),\qquad j=1,\dots,n,

(79)

with multiplicities inherited from
\mathbf{B}
. The two lists overlap when
\mathbf{B}
is singular, since each zero eigenvalue of
\mathbf{B}
contributes a further copy of
-\gamma
. The exact multiplicity of
-\gamma
is
m-n
plus the algebraic multiplicity of zero in
\mathbf{B}
.

Proof.

Use
equation

15
with
\lambda+\gamma
in place of
\lambda
:

\displaystyle\det(\lambda\mathbf{I}_{m}-\mathbf{J})

\displaystyle=\det((\lambda+\gamma)\mathbf{I}_{m}-\mathbf{A})

\displaystyle=(\lambda+\gamma)^{m-n}\det((\lambda+\gamma)\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{B}).

∎

Corollary 10.2

(Stability criterion)
.

Assume
\gamma>0
. The system in
equation

76
is asymptotically stable if and only if

\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\operatorname{Re}\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{B})<\gamma.

(80)

Proof.

By
theorem

10.1
the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{J}
are
-\gamma
together with
-\gamma+\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{B})
. Because
\gamma>0
, the first has negative real part. The others do exactly when
\operatorname{Re}\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{B})<\gamma
for every
j
.
∎

The criterion reduces an
m
-dimensional stability test to an
n
-dimensional eigenvalue calculation. It settles asymptotic behavior as
t\to\infty
and nothing beyond that.
Section

13
returns to what eigenvalues leave undetermined about transient growth.

Example 10.3

(Stability from the reduced matrix)
.

Use
\mathbf{B}
from
equation

25
, whose eigenvalues are
-2
and
-6
, and take
\gamma=1
. Then the full shifted matrix

\mathbf{J}=-\mathbf{I}_{3}+\mathbf{A}

has eigenvalues

-1,\qquad-3,\qquad-7.

The eigenvalue
-1
comes from the inactive dimension; the other two are obtained by shifting the reduced eigenvalues by
-1
.

10.1
Forced systems

Consider

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(-\gamma\mathbf{I}_{m}+\mathbf{A})\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{f}(t).

(81)

The extracted variable
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}
satisfies

\mathbf{q}^{\prime}=(-\gamma\mathbf{I}_{n}+\mathbf{B})\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{W}\mathbf{f}(t).

(82)

This equation is exact for every forcing function. Once
\mathbf{q}
is found, the full state satisfies

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=-\gamma\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{f}(t).

(83)

Suppose
\mathbf{T}
has full column rank, as in the lifted-coordinate reduction of
section

8
, so that
\mathbf{T}
is injective. If the forcing also lies in the lifted subspace,
\mathbf{f}(t)=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{h}(t)
, and the initial condition belongs to
{T})
, then cancelling
\mathbf{T}
is legitimate and the lifted coordinates satisfy

\mathbf{y}^{\prime}=(-\gamma\mathbf{I}_{n}+\mathbf{C})\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{h}(t).

(84)

11
From exact factorization to projection-based model reduction

The preceding reductions are exact because the full matrix is assumed to factor through an
n
-dimensional space. Most model-reduction problems begin differently: a general large matrix is given, and one seeks a useful low-dimensional approximation. Projection methods provide the bridge
(
Antoulas, 2005
;
Benner et al., 2015
;
Quarteroni et al., 2016
)
.

11.1
Trial and test spaces

Consider the full system

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{x},\qquad\mathbf{L}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times m}.

(85)

Choose a trial basis

\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times n},\qquad\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{T})=n,

(86)

and approximate

\mathbf{x}(t)\approx\mathbf{T}\mathbf{y}(t).

(87)

Choose a test map

\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times m}

(88)

that is usually normalized so that

\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{I}_{n}.

(89)

The residual of the approximation is

\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{y}^{\prime}-\mathbf{L}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{y}.

(90)

A Petrov–Galerkin condition requires

\mathbf{W}\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{0}.

(91)

Using
equation

89
gives

\mathbf{y}^{\prime}=\mathbf{L}_{r}\mathbf{y},\qquad\mathbf{L}_{r}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{T}.

(92)

If
\mathbf{T}
has orthonormal columns and
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{T}^{*}
, this is a Galerkin projection. More general choices yield Petrov–Galerkin methods
(
Quarteroni et al., 2016
;
Benner et al., 2015
)
.

11.2
The lifted reduced operator

The reduced matrix acts in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
. To compare it with a full-space operator, define

\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{L}_{r}\mathbf{W}.

(93)

This matrix has the exact factor form studied earlier, with
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{L}_{r}
. Because
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
, Sylvester’s theorem gives

\det(\lambda\mathbf{I}_{m}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}})=\lambda^{m-n}\det(\lambda\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{L}_{r}).

(94)

The nonzero eigenvalues of the lifted approximation are exactly the nonzero eigenvalues of the reduced matrix, with the same multiplicities. If
\mathbf{L}_{r}
is singular its zero eigenvalue is not among them.

The statement is useful, but it is easy to over-read:

\operatorname{spec}_{\neq 0}(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}})=\operatorname{spec}_{\neq 0}(\mathbf{L}_{r})

(95)

is an exact algebraic identity, whereas

\operatorname{spec}(\mathbf{L}_{r})\approx\operatorname{spec}(\mathbf{L})

(96)

is an approximation claim that requires justification. The first is free: it holds for any
\mathbf{T}
and
\mathbf{W}
with
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
, whatever subspace they happen to describe. The second carries the entire modeling risk, and Sylvester’s theorem says nothing about it.

11.3
When the reduction is exact

Theorem 11.1

(Exact invariant-subspace reduction)
.

Suppose
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
and there is a matrix
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
such that

\mathbf{L}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}.

(97)

Then

\mathbf{W}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{S}.

(98)

For every initial condition
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{y}_{0}
, the full solution of

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{x}
is

\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{T}\mathrm{e}^{t\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{y}_{0}.

(99)

Proof.

The proof has two steps. The first is a short computation. The second constructs a
candidate solution, verifies that it satisfies the same initial-value problem as

\mathbf{x}
, and then appeals to uniqueness. That pattern is worth recognizing: it
identifies
\mathbf{x}
without ever integrating the full system.

Multiplying
equation

97
by
\mathbf{W}
gives

\mathbf{W}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{S}.

If
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{y}_{0}
, set

\mathbf{z}(t)=\mathbf{T}\mathrm{e}^{t\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{y}_{0}.

Then
\mathbf{z}(0)=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{y}_{0}=\mathbf{x}_{0}
, and

\mathbf{z}^{\prime}(t)=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathrm{e}^{t\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{y}_{0}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{T}\mathrm{e}^{t\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{y}_{0}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{z}(t),

where the middle step is
equation

97
. So
\mathbf{z}
solves the same initial-value problem as
\mathbf{x}
, and the solution of a linear initial-value problem is unique, giving
equation

99
.
∎

11.4
Measuring the defect of an approximate subspace

For a projected matrix

\mathbf{L}_{r}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{T},

define the invariance residual

\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{T}-\mathbf{T}\mathbf{L}_{r}.

(100)

If
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{0}
, the trial space is invariant and the reduction is exact on that space. If
\left\lVert\mathbf{R}\right\rVert
is small, the trial space is nearly invariant in the chosen norm. This residual is one practical check on a spectral reduced model, though trajectory and output accuracy may require more specialized error estimates.

Example 11.2

(A projection that preserves the algebra and loses the dynamics)
.

Keep the trial and test maps of
example

9.1
,

\mathbf{T}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{W}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{I}_{2},

and replace the exact factorization by a general operator that does not factor through
{T})
:

\mathbf{L}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&-1&-1\\
0&-2&-1\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\operatorname{spec}(\mathbf{L})=\{-1,-2,1\}.

(101)

The matrix is triangular, so its eigenvalues are the diagonal entries, and the full system
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{x}
has one growing mode.

The projected matrix and its characteristic polynomial are

\mathbf{L}_{r}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{T}=\begin{pmatrix}-2&-2\\
-1&-3\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det(\lambda\mathbf{I}_{2}-\mathbf{L}_{r})=(\lambda+1)(\lambda+4).

(102)

Lifting the reduced matrix back into the full space gives

\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{L}_{r}\mathbf{W}=\begin{pmatrix}-2&-2&0\\
-1&-3&0\\
-3&-5&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det(\lambda\mathbf{I}_{3}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}})=\lambda(\lambda+1)(\lambda+4),

(103)

which is
equation

94
with
m-n=1
. The algebra delivers what it promised.

The modeling claim does not. Against
\operatorname{spec}(\mathbf{L})=\{-1,-2,1\}
, the reduced matrix keeps one eigenvalue, introduces
-4
, and loses
1
altogether. A reader who stopped at
equation

95
would conclude that every mode decays, when one of them grows. The habit built in
section

10
, where a reduced spectrum settles asymptotic stability, was earned there by an exact factorization and is not available here.

The invariance residual records the gap. From
equation

100
,

\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{T}-\mathbf{T}\mathbf{L}_{r}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\\
4&6\end{pmatrix},\qquad\left\lVert\mathbf{R}\right\rVert_{F}=2\sqrt{13},

(104)

of rank one and supported entirely in the third component. The eigenvector of
\mathbf{L}
for
\lambda=1
is a multiple of
(-1,-1,3)^{\mathsf{T}}
, while
{T})
is the plane
x_{3}=x_{1}+x_{2}
. The residual points at the one direction the trial space cannot represent.

Changing only the trial space repairs the reduction. With

\mathbf{T}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}^{\prime}=\mathbf{I}_{2},

the same construction gives

\mathbf{L}_{r}^{\prime}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{T}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&-1\\
0&-2\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{R}^{\prime}=\mathbf{0},

(105)

with
\operatorname{spec}(\mathbf{L}_{r}^{\prime})=\{-1,-2\}
, two exact eigenvalues of
\mathbf{L}
.
Equation

97
holds, so
theorem

11.1
applies verbatim. The theorem was the same in both calculations. What changed was the subspace.

One caution survives the repair. The corrected trial space is invariant, and it still omits the growing mode, which lies outside
{T}^{\prime})
. Exactness on a subspace is a statement about that subspace, not about the behavior a reduced model was built to capture.

11.5
How reduced spaces are chosen

The theorem does not choose
\mathbf{T}
and
\mathbf{W}
. Common strategies include:

•

eigenvectors or invariant subspaces associated with important modes
(
Golub and Van Loan, 2013
)
;

•

proper orthogonal decomposition from state snapshots
(
Benner et al., 2015
)
;

•

Krylov subspaces designed to match moments or transfer functions
(
Antoulas, 2005
)
;

•

balanced truncation based on controllability and observability
(
Antoulas, 2005
)
;

•

reduced basis methods for parameter-dependent equations
(
Quarteroni et al., 2016
)
.

These methods address the modeling question: which low-dimensional subspace retains the behavior of interest? Sylvester’s theorem addresses a different algebraic question: once an operator is written in lifted reduced form, how are its nonzero eigenvalues related to those of the reduced matrix?

Computational workflow

A practical implementation should preserve the factorization rather than assemble
\mathbf{A}
.

12.1
Reduced spectral analysis

Given
\mathbf{T}
,
\mathbf{S}
, and
\mathbf{W}
:

1.

Form

\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}.

2.

Form either

\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{S}\quad\text{or}\quad\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{M}.

3.

Compute the eigenvalues of the

reduced matrix.

4.

Interpret these

values as the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
inherited from

, and append exactly

further zeros for the inactive dimensions. The total multiplicity of the zero eigenvalue is
m-n
plus the algebraic multiplicity of zero in
\mathbf{B}
.

5.

Reconstruct selected full eigenvectors using
\mathbf{T}\mathbf{v}
from eigenvectors of
\mathbf{C}
, or
\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{u}
from eigenvectors of
\mathbf{B}
.

6.

Check the full residual without forming
\mathbf{A}
:

\rho=\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x})-\lambda\mathbf{x}\right\rVert}{\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert}.

(106)

12.2
Cost

For dense factors, forming
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}
costs approximately
O(mn^{2})
, and the reduced eigenvalue problem costs

. Storing the factors costs
O(mn+n^{2})
, whereas storing a dense full matrix costs
O(m^{2})
. Applying
\mathbf{A}
to one vector costs
O(mn+n^{2})
. These estimates explain why the factor form is attractive when

.

A numerical implementation should still consider scaling and conditioning. Forming
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}
may lose accuracy when the columns of
\mathbf{T}
or rows of
\mathbf{W}
are poorly conditioned. In projection methods, orthonormal or biorthogonal bases are often preferred for this reason
(

;
Trefethen and Bau, 1997
)
. For conditioning questions the two are not interchangeable, as

6

13
Limitations and cautions

Sylvester’s theorem is exact, but the conclusions it supports are specific. Several common overextensions should be avoided.

13.1
The zero eigenvalue is not fully described

The nonzero characteristic factors agree exactly. At those eigenvalues more agrees than the characteristic polynomial records. Applying
Horn and Johnson (2013, Theorem 3.2.11.1)
to the two groupings of
section

4
shows that
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{B}
, and
\mathbf{C}
carry the same number of Jordan blocks of each size at every nonzero eigenvalue. The zero eigenvalue may have different geometric multiplicity, nilpotent structure, and Jordan block sizes. If long-time behavior depends on generalized zero modes, the reduced characteristic polynomial alone is insufficient. The work of
Flanders (1951)
gives the classical refined description.

13.2
Eigenvalues are not singular values

The singular values of
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{X}
generally differ, so the theorem does not preserve operator norms, condition numbers, energy amplification, or least-squares sensitivity.
Section

6
develops this point in full, including the one case in which singular values do transfer.

13.3
Equal eigenvalues do not imply equal transient behavior

A nonnormal matrix can exhibit substantial transient growth even when every eigenvalue lies in the left half-plane. Two matrices with the same eigenvalues can have very different eigenvectors, pseudospectra, and short-time amplification. A stability conclusion based on eigenvalues should therefore be separated from a claim about transient response. Because
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{B}
, and
\mathbf{C}
need not share singular values, the reduced matrices carry no guarantee here either. Standard numerical linear algebra references discuss conditioning and nonnormality in greater depth
(
Trefethen and Bau, 1997
;
Golub and Van Loan, 2013
)
.

13.4
Lifting can amplify errors

Suppose an approximate reduced eigenvector
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}
is lifted as

\widehat{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{T}\widehat{\mathbf{v}}
. If
\left\lVert\mathbf{T}\right\rVert
is large or
\mathbf{T}
is poorly conditioned, small reduced errors may produce large full-space errors. Similarly, an extraction map
\mathbf{W}
of large norm can magnify measurement or rounding errors. Conditioning is the relevant quantity when a map is inverted, and norm is the relevant one for direct amplification.

13.5
Rank deficiency can reduce the active dimension further

Although
n
is the nominal reduced dimension,

\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A})\leq\min\{\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{T}),\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{S}),\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{W})\}.

(107)

If any factor is rank deficient, the true active dimension may be smaller than
n
, and
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{C}
will have additional zero eigenvalues.

13.6
A reduced model must be judged by its purpose

In an approximate projection method, preserving selected eigenvalues may be important, but it may not be the primary objective. A reduced model might instead be designed to reproduce an output, a transfer function, a time interval, a parameter range, or a conserved quantity. Sylvester’s theorem explains the internal spectrum of the lifted reduced operator; it does not replace application-specific validation.

15
Conclusion

The four questions of
Section

1
now have answers. The nonzero eigenvalues of the large matrix are exactly those of either small matrix (
Section

3
,
Section

4
), and the extracted variable
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}
evolves without reference to the full state (
Section

8
). A shifted large system reduces to an
n\times n
solve (
Section

7
). The same algebra describes the lifted operator of a projection method, though not whether that operator approximates the original (
Section

11
).

The factorization

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}

reveals that a large operator acts through an
n
-dimensional channel. Sylvester’s theorem converts this geometric fact into an exact spectral statement:

\det(\lambda\mathbf{I}_{m}-\mathbf{A})=\lambda^{m-n}\det(\lambda\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{B})=\lambda^{m-n}\det(\lambda\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{C}).

The two small matrices have complementary meanings. The matrix

\mathbf{B}=(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T})\mathbf{S}
governs the extracted observables

\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}
, while

\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T})
governs coefficients in the lifted subspace

\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{y}
. The intertwining identities

\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{W},\qquad\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{C}

make these interpretations precise.

For exact factorizations, the reduction is not heuristic. It gives exact nonzero eigenvalues, exact reduced ODEs, exact formulas for powers and analytic matrix functions, and exact low-dimensional solutions of shifted linear systems. What it does not give is equally definite. Singular values, condition numbers, and transient behavior are not determined by the reduced matrices, and have to be established from the factors themselves. For projection-based model reduction, the same theorem explains the relation between a reduced matrix and its lifted full-space representation. The separate question of whether that representation approximates the original full operator must be answered by the quality of the trial and test spaces, residual analysis, and application-specific validation.

Two directions are left open. The fine structure at the zero eigenvalue, described by
Flanders (1951)
, is not developed here, and neither is a quantitative bound relating the invariance residual of
equation

100
to the accuracy of the reduced spectrum. Both are within reach of a reader who has worked the exercises.

The practical principle is simple, and so is its boundary:

When a large linear system acts through a small intermediate space, analyze its spectrum and its exact trajectories inside that space, and use the lifting and extraction maps to connect the reduced results to the full variables. Norms, conditioning, and transient growth must be read from the maps themselves.
