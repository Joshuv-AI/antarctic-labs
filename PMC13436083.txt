Validation of Checklist to Evaluate Emergency Medicine Competencies of Medical Students in Simulation Training
Authors: Nico Tannemann, Mohamed Esmail, Marius Gebauer, Frank Herbstreit, Thorsten Brenner, Cynthia Szalai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSimulation-based training is crucial for acquiring emergency medicine competencies during medical education. In Germany, teaching is largely competency-based and guided by the National Competence Based Catalogue of Learning Objectives for Undergraduate Medical Education (NKLM) 2.0. Given the diversity of clinical scenarios and varying student expertise, a standardized assessment instrument is required for objective and reproducible performance evaluation. Moreover, such instruments are essential for educational research.MethodsWe developed a checklist aligned with NKLM 2.0 and established emergency medicine guidelines. Content validity was ensured through expert panel review. The checklist assessed three domains (evaluation, background, and action) corresponding to key elements of the ISBAR framework. The instrument was applied to 265 medical students across different semesters and multiple simulation scenarios using purposive sampling and including all students from two semesters willing to participate. Construct validity was examined using factor analysis. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s α, and interrater reliability by calculating variance attributable to rater and station effects.ResultsAfter exclusion of one item with Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin estimator of 0.45, exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses supported a three-factor structure, with items loading on evaluation (CFA-loadings 0.831–0.901), background (0.582–0.736), and action (0.491–0.759). The CFA showed good model fit (CFI = 1.0, TLLI = 1.014, RMSEA < 0.001, SRMR = 0.045). Factors were moderately correlated (0.735–0.882), suggesting related dimensions of a common construct. Rater and station effects accounted for 14.2% of total variance. Cronbach’s α was 0.730 overall, and 0.721 (evaluation), 0.557 (background), and 0.604 (action), respectively.ConclusionAlignment with NKLM 2.0 supported content validity, while factor analysis confirmed the proposed construct structure. Overall internal consistency and acceptable rater-related variance as well as factor-correlation indicate that the checklist is a valid and reliable instrument for assessing emergency medicine competencies in undergraduate simulation training, though subscore interpretation remains limited due to low internal consistency of individual domains.Keywords: validation checklist, emergency medicine, simulation

Background

Training in emergency medicine is a core component of medical education.1 Medical students must acquire not only theoretical knowledge but also clinical reasoning skills, structured assessment strategies, and non-technical skills.1 Simulation-based training is therefore a commonly used tool to facilitate experiential learning in a safe environment.2

In many curricula, emergency medicine teaching is delivered longitudinally across multiple courses and semesters, addressing students at different stages of training and with varying levels of clinical experience.1 Accordingly, a broad range of emergency scenarios is used to match learners developmental levels and progressively increasing competencies.2 This curricular diversity requires assessment tools that are not limited to single scenarios or specific levels of expertise but can be applied consistently across different clinical cases and learner stages. We put emphasis on the construction of teaching formats such as simulation training and assessment tools in line with the National Competence Based Catalogues of Learning Objectives for Undergraduate Medical Education (NKLM) 2.0. The NKLM 2.0 defines learning objectives in different fields of medicine, including emergency medicine as an interdisciplinary speciality.3

The NKLM emphasises clinical skills such as assessment of the patient’s physical condition using basic and extended monitoring techniques, taking a relevant medical history, and appropriate physical examination. Moreover, the NKLM 2.0 requires awareness of the emergency situation and generating a differential diagnosis based on patient presentation.3 Furthermore, a primary survey using the ABCDE algorithm and knowledge about subsequent diagnostic measures are part of crucial competencies as well as the planning of further management and structured handover using the ISBAR (Identification, Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) approach.3 Finally, the knowledge about intra- and prehospital secondary circumstances such as emergency teams is demanded in the NKLM 2.0.3 These skills are all combined to establish a diagnosis and initiate acute emergency management of the patient.

Robust and valid assessment tools are essential to ensure that learning objectives are met and that student performance can be evaluated in a fair, transparent, and reproducible manner.4 Contemporary medical curricula are increasingly shaped by the principles of competency-based medical education, which clearly defines competencies that integrate knowledge, skills, and professional attitudes as primary aims of medical education.5 The assessment of performance is not just a tool for grading but a tool for documenting competence development and ensuring alignment between learning objectives, teaching activities, and evaluation strategies.6

Since the introduction of the NKLM 2.0 in Germany, there is no validated assessment tool specifically developed to be consistent with this emergency medicine curriculum. The development of an appropriate validation tool to assess student’s performance in simulation training aligned to the NKLM 2.0 appears meaningful. Moreover, there are few validated assessment tools for simulation-based training for medical students in general. The Queen’s Simulation Assessment Tool (QSAT) for instance is validated for postgraduate emergency medicine trainees in resuscitation simulation.7 Another assessment tool developed by Zoller et al is validated for interprofessional teams of 4th year medical students, paramedics and emergency physicians and only covers one distinct prehospital emergency scenario.8 Furthermore, this assessment tool measures the performance of teams of three participants.

Therefore, we aimed to develop an objective and generalizable checklist which could assess individual students’ emergency medicine competencies across different training levels strictly aligned with the NKLM 2.0. Part of the development of the items was to emphasize the importance of clinical reasoning as this is a crucial part of clinical practice.9,10 However, the checklist should still be reliable, valid, be useable for raters with varying levels of experience and still applicable to multiple clinical scenarios. Beyond its educational function, validation of this instrument is essential to its psychometric soundness.4,11 A validated and reliable assessment tool reduces measurement error and increases the precision of performance estimates.12 This enhances power in subsequent research studies using the instrument.

Materials/Methods

Construction of the Assessment Tool

We aimed to develop a standardized assessment tool to measure emergency medicine competencies of medical students in a simulation setting. Our overarching objective was to assess emergency care competence across different clinical scenarios. Therefore, following an extensive literature review and in consideration of current clinical guidelines to ensure content validity, ten key items representing core components of emergency competence were developed (see Table 1 and Supplementary File 1: Emergency Medicine Simulation Training Checklist).13 We aimed to form a checklist development panel composed of members of different levels of emergency medicine training as well as medical education competence. The development process therefore involved two senior consultant anesthesiologists, one being a senior emergency medicine physician, and the other holding a Master of Medical Education. Furthermore, the panel also included a resident anesthesiologist with prehospital emergency physician certification. Lastly, we invited two students who already underwent the Block Practicum emergency medicine and work as student assistants in emergency medicine teaching. This inclusion accounted for the student’s perspective. Both medical students were also trained paramedics. All of the physicians were chosen from the Department of Anaesthesiology and Intensive Care Medicine of the University Hospital Essen because they take actively part in medical student education.

Items of the Assessment Tool and Their Aspects

Since the ISBAR framework14,15 is an internationally recognised tool for structured clinical communication and reasoning,16 we aimed to adapt our assessment tool and its items to align with this format. Hence, we constructed the items to oriented to the dimensions of the ISBAR framework. We combined the dimensions “situation” and “assessment” because either of the items “recognition of medical emergency” and “presentation of appropriate primary diagnosis” require cognitive processes of reflecting and evaluating the clinical situation and the collected data respectively. We therefore settled on four dimensions.

The items represent complex and broad clinical processes. Scores were assessed on a partial-credit scale of 0–2 point. The rater decided whether the item is not accomplished (0 points), partly accomplished (1 point) or completely accomplished (2 points) for every item.

Study Collective and Simulations

We tested our questionnaire in different collectives of (n= 265) medical students from the Medical Faculty of the University Duisburg-Essen across two semesters. To assess the checklist’s applicability across different stages of medical training, we included two student cohorts, whose scenarios varied in difficulty and requirements accordingly. The selection process was purposive. We aimed to include all the participants enrolled in the two collectives, with lack of consent being an immediate exclusion criterion. Assignment to the clinical scenario was random. The first collective (N = 67 and N = 104) consists of medical students in the Block Practicum emergency medicine in the summer semester 2021 and the winter semester 2021/2022 respectively. These students were in fourth year of their six-year medical training.

The second cohort included sixth‑year students who had already completed their written medical exams and participated in the Skills Training and Simulation course in summer semester 2021 (N = 40), and winter semester 2021/2022 (N = 54). This course is geared to teach and refresh clinical skills in the beginning of their final-year clinical rotations. We developed 20 scenarios to assess their emergency medicine competencies in different clinical settings and different emergency situations. For further information regarding the scenarios, please see Supplementary File 2 (Exemplaric Scenario: Anaphylactic Shock). Scenarios were assessed by a separate examiner. The raters were resident physicians from the department of Anesthesiology and Intensive Care Medicine of the University Hospital Essen. Specific training or calibration of raters was not logistically possible due to the large pool of residents and the numerous varied student seminars offered in the department. All raters had access to a teaching app which provided a list of learning objectives for each scenario, general information on teaching structure, and details of the courses.9,17 Checklist scores were recorded electronically using Lime Survey® in real time, the scores were stored digitally afterwards.

Verbal and written informed consent were obtained from all participants. Data was anonymized, and all possible identifying markers were removed. This study was approved by the Ethics committee of the Faculty of Medicine of the University Duisburg-Essen (21–9914-BO). The work has been carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical Methods

To describe the basic metrics, we calculated the number and proportion of students graded with 0, 1, and 2 points for each item. As the score of the students is not normally distributed, we show the median, first and third quartile of the scores for each station and the total score. Besides ensuring content validity, we statistically checked for construct validity, internal consistency and Inter-Rater-Reliability.

We tested for construct validity using an exploratory factor analysis (EFA). Therefore, we tested the applicability of an EFA for the present data and calculated Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) criterium for every item and excluded items with an KMO < 0.5.18 Since our data is ordinally scaled, we used polychoric correlation for construction of the correlation matrix.19 Furthermore, we performed the Bartlett test for sphericity.20 We created a Scree plot and visually analysed it to determine the number of factors for the EFA. Furthermore, we conducted a parallel analysis21 for the Scree plot22 and calculated Eigenvalues and chose a cut-off for Eigenvalues > 1.23 In combination with our theoretical concept of three dimensions after exclusion of above-mentioned item (evaluation, background, action), we decided to use a three‑factor EFA with principal axis factoring. We then calculated the model fit and defined the fit indices Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) < 0.08,24 Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) < 0.08,24 Tucker Louis Index > 0.924 as acceptable. We furthermore calculated the Bayesian information criterion (BIC). Additionally, we assessed the model fit for a one- and two-factors approach.

After our theoretical structure was confirmed by the EFA, we conducted a confirmative factor analysis (CFA) using the same collective as for the EFA to assess the actual fit of our model and factor loadings. We set the same thresholds as in the EFA and additionally calculated the comparative fit index (CFI) with an acceptable result >0.95.24 In addition, we estimated the Cronbach’s α as measure for the internal consistency of the whole construct as well as for each factor. Since the factor “evaluation” only has two items, we further assessed the composite reliability as a more robust measure for factors with only two items.25

We then estimated the interrater reliability. Since each student was examined in exactly one station and each station had one separate examiner, our study has a nested design and we estimated interrater reliability by calculating the variance explained by the rater and the station using a linear mixed model as a measure for the interrater consistency.26

The statistical analyses were performed using the R software (Version 4.4.3)27 with the psych package28 for the EFA, Cronbach’s α and the correlation matrix. The CFA was performed using the lavaan package.29,30 We used the package lme431 for the linear mixed models to calculate interrater reliability and the package semTools32 for the composite reliability. The visualisation was carried out with the ggplot2 package.33

Discussion

Content Validity

The development of our assessment tool is guideline based. To ensure content validity. We constructed our assessment tool to ensure that the defined competencies were strictly aligned with the learning objectives of NKLM 2.0.3 This catalogue defines the different dimensions of emergency medicine competencies. Since communication skills are included in the objectives, we decided to implement the item “Students introduction” in the checklist. This item should also help the student to immerse themselves in the simulation and reenforce their role as caregiver. It also served to communicate the student’s roles and competencies to the other team members of the simulation such as the nurse, as well as informing the simulation patient of the various members of the interprofessional team. In an emergency situation ascertaining an appropriate relevant medical history and performing a relevant clinical examination are essential parts of assessing and treating patients, hence these competencies were crucial to the checklist.3 The items “recognition of emergency situation” and “presentation of appropriate primary diagnosis” are derived from the (PR_E-)AUD2IT scheme.34 In an emergency it is important to become aware of the situation’s severity as this decides within which timeframe specific diagnostic and therapeutic measures must be implemented. The primary diagnosis is crucial in deciding which further diagnostic and therapeutic measures should be implemented. The ABCDE algorithm is a central part of nearly all emergency medicine guidelines35,36 and is included as a learning objective of the NKLM 2.0.3 Therefore, we believed that assessing the application of the algorithm is an indispensable component of our assessment tool. Further recommendations for continued management is a part of the well‑validated ISBAR scheme and crucial in the handover process, as it alerts the new caregiver as to the prioritization of further management goals.37

Besides the evidence‑based alignment to the course concept and checklist tool, an evaluation by experts is an important part to ensure content validity.38 After the development of the checklist items we presented the draft the panel described above for consideration and feedback. The final draft was deemed satisfactory to assess the key competencies, we wanted to address.

Construct Validity

We tested construct validity using exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmative factor analysis (CFA). All our items except “Student introduction” met the requirements for these analyses. We excluded the aforementioned item for its Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin estimator of 0.45 which is inacceptable.18 This might be explained by its characterization as a formal aspect in the ISBAR scheme37 rather than a hard emergency medicine skill. Nevertheless, this item facilitates the student’s initial approach to the patient. The Bartlett test for sphericity was highly significant, so we moved on to assessing the number of factors for the exploratory factor analysis. The parallel analysis as gold standard suggests a 3-factor model.39,40 The scree plot substantially declines after the third factor, even though the graphical interpretation is subjective, this is compatible with a 3-factor model as well.40 Although the Eigenvalue for the 3-factor model is 0.96 and therefore slightly under the threshold of 1, this criterion is known to be less valid than the parallel analysis40 and the eigenvalue does not substantially undercut the threshold, also aligning with a 3-factor model. After excluding the dimension “identification” because its item had an unacceptable KMO, the model no longer aligns as closely with the ISBAR scheme. However, the three factors “evaluation”, “background”, and “action” remain. The ISBAR items for assessment and situation were condensed into “evaluation”, reflecting its cognitive component of interpreting and reflecting on the clinical situation.

Regarding the model fit for a 1-, 2-, and 3-factor model, the BIC is the only fit index with a better result for the 1- and 2-factor model. This can be explained by a higher model complexity due to a larger number of factors.41 The RMSR, RMSEA and TLI indicate an excellent fit of the 3-factor model and distinctly favour this model. Furthermore, the explained variance of the model is 40.2%, the explained variance in humanities is commonly as low as 50–60%.42 However, the cutoff for explained variance is not fixed to a certain threshold.43 The factor loadings ranged from 0.354 to 0.912. Commonly, a threshold of 0.4 is considered as a sufficient loading.44 Despite the fact that four of the tested items undercut this limit, many authors suggest that 0.3 is enough to demonstrate satisfactory loading.43,44

Besides these psychometrical parameters, a key strength of this model is that the items clustered as theoretically predicted. We hypothesized that our main construct of emergency medicine competencies measured in the checklist is dividable into the subcategories “evaluation”, “background”, and “action”. The category evaluation lays focus on the cognitive capacity of drawing conclusions from previously derived information using the items “recognition of emergency situation” and “presentation of appropriate diagnosis”. Appropriately, data from the (PR_E-)AUD2IT scheme used in the structured treatment of critical ill patients, recognition of critical condition and the developing a differential diagnosis are also both included in the interpretation category.34 The category “background” includes the items “relevant medical history”, “assessment of clinical condition”, and “appropriate clinical examination” align with the background component of ISBAR acronym. The items “appropriate diagnostic investigations”, “call for appropriate assistance”, and “therapeutic measures” as well as “plan for further management” are comprised in the “action” subcategory.

The EFA revealed three factors. Factor 1 comprised “recognition of emergency situation” and “presentation of appropriate diagnosis”. Factor 2 included “relevant medical history”, “assessment of clinical condition”, and “appropriate clinical examination”. Factor 3 consisted of “appropriate diagnostic investigations”, “call for appropriate assistance”, “therapeutic measures” and “plan for further management”. This grouping fits perfectly with our previously introduced concept of the three factors “evaluation”, “background”, and “action” and confirms it empirically. We conducted a confirmative factor analysis to reveal the actual loading of the checklist items. The loadings of the CFA range from 0.491 for the item “call for appropriate assistance” and 0.901 for “recognition of emergency situation”. All factor loadings are highly significant.

All factors “evaluation”, “background”, and “action” are correlated (R2 > 0.7 for each factor) indicating that they are subdimensions with emergency medicine competencies being the common main dimension. The Cronbach’s α of the separate factors ranges from 0.557 to 0.721. While the factor “evaluation” shows acceptable Cronbach’s α of 0.721 and therefore acceptable internal consistency, the factors “background” (Cronbach’s α 0.557) and “action” (Cronbach’s α 0.604) show low reliability. This might be attributed to individual loadings < 0.4 and few items per factor. Factors with less than 5 items tend to have low results for Cronbach’s α.45 This is true for all our factors as the competency “evaluation” has two items, “background” has three items, and “action” has four items. Furthermore, several items represent broad, multi-step clinical processes, such as initiating therapeutic measures according to the ABCDE scheme or initiating further diagnostics. On the other hand, some items are conceptually overlapping, namely the assessment of the patient’s clinical condition, the performance of an appropriate physical examination, and the taking of relevant medical history, whereas the last two items are necessary to assess the patient’s clinical status. Moreover, our checklist focuses on clinical reasoning as a part of the treatment process. Clinical reasoning itself is a complex, multi-dimensional, and heterogeneous concept. These reasons might contribute to a low internal consistency of these two factors, indicating that the checklist is not valid for assessing individual subdimensions of emergency medicine competency.13,46 As we aimed to construct an easy-to-use checklist, a compromise between feasibility and number and specificity of items and hence reliability was sought. Despite the above-mentioned limitations, the Cronbach’s α for the entire construct is 0.73 and indicates internal consistency.

Interrater Variability

Due to the design of our study, where each station had its own rater and every student was rated by exactly one rater, we could not directly assess interrater reliability. We therefore calculated the variance accounted to the rater and the station respectively in relation to the overall variance using a linear mixed model. 14% of the variance was attributable to rater/station effects. It is known that there is a high impact of the rater on the grading of students in medical educational training.47 Although there is no fixed threshold for this, our results do not seem unusual. Some authors report a variance of up to 44% due to rater effects.48 However up to 12% has also been reported.49 It is important not to forget that a separation between the rater and stations effect is not possible, as the stations and raters are totally confounded. We can conclude that the effects of the rater and station are within an adequate range indicating a sufficient generalizability of our scoring tool.

Limitations

The development of the checklist items was done in two iterations firstly from an author then secondly after being reviewed from the panel mentioned above. The process may have benefitted from a formal Delphi consensus; however, this was limited due to lack of resources. The authors, maintain that content validity was ensured due to rigorous alignment with the learning objective catalogue recommended by state training authorities, which is accepted practice in medical education research.38

The authors actively decide to limit the items to 10 to preserve useability of the checklist without overwhelming the rater. It may be argued that assessment additional is competencies is necessary in an emergency training scenario.

As already mentioned, it was not possible to directly calculate interrater reliability. A study design with more than one rater per student could have shown relevant additional information. Furthermore, we conducted the EFA and CFA in the same collective, the study could have benefitted from a CFA in a different collective since performing an EFA and CFA on the same collective might lead to model overfitting. Moreover, an analysis and comparison of the subgroups could have been meaningful, however, the number of participants was too low in certain subgroups.

Lastly, it is also debatable that the first draft should have been assessed and based on results a new draft should be developed. The authors decided that based on the results obtained for this draft, the checklist is appropriate for use for assessing training scenarios at multiple levels of training.

Conclusion

We developed an assessment tool for simulations in emergency medicine education for medical students of different experience levels. By creating the items in alignment with current guidelines and the NKLM 2.0, then conferring with an internal expert panel to evaluate the items, content validity was maintained. The checklist was tested on 265 medical students across two semesters, with EFA showing that the items cluster into three factors within one overall construct. This reflects our theory-guided assumption of three subdimensions: “evaluation”, “background” and “action” oriented on the ISBAR approach. We excluded the “identification” dimension due to its unacceptable KMO and condensed situation and assessment to the factor “evaluation”. The CFA confirmed these findings indicating empirical support of the theoretical construct. While the internal consistency was low for the individual factors, it was sufficient for the overall construct indicating that an assessment of individual subdimensions of emergency medicine competency is not valid using the checklist. Interrater/interstation reliability estimated by variance components was within an acceptable range for medical educational data. Therefore, we conclude that the checklist is valid for use in assessing emergency training scenarios across multiple stages of medical training with untrained raters from the department of anesthesiology and intensive care medicine. In further studies, the generalizability of the checklist to other institutions or emergency medicine specialties might be examined once the specific learning objectives for each theme have been predetermined.
