 Abstract

 Global supply chains for critical minerals and materials are complex, evolving networks of
countries, products, production stages, and trade relationships. Existing analytical approaches
are limited by fragmented data and static network representations that do not capture the dynamic
production dependencies linking raw materials, intermediate products, and final goods across mul-
tiple countries. Trade and production statistics provide only a partial view of domestic production,
inventories, and material flows, making it difficult to identify indirect sourcing pathways, hidden
dependencies, and embedded foreign exposures.

 This paper introduces the Supply Chain Exposure Network Tracking and Risk Assessment
(SENTRA) framework, a modular graph-based computational framework for constructing, analyz-
ing, and forecasting dynamic supply chain networks. As the first paper in a three-part method-
ological series, it establishes the computational foundation of SENTRA by constructing a temporal
attributed multi-relational graph whose nodes represent product–country pairs and whose edges
encode observed trade and within-country value-chain relationships. Statistical estimation and
constrained optimization recover latent production, final demand, and product input dependency
coefficients while enforcing economic accounting constraints. Graph-derived exposure measures
quantify direct, transshipment, value-chain, and multi-hop supply chain dependencies indepen-
dently of the forecasting model. A temporal graph forecasting architecture based on a relational
graph neural network then forecasts the evolution of the graph under mass-balance constraints
with distribution-free conformal uncertainty quantification. Validation on the global aluminum sup-
ply chain shows that the learned graph representations recover economically meaningful supply
chain structure, accurately forecast out-of-sample trade relationships, and produce well-calibrated
prediction intervals. Subsequent papers apply this computational foundation to exposure assess-
ment, disruption analysis, and scenario-based policy analysis, and extend the framework to multi-
material supply chain modeling and decision support.

Keywords: supply-chain networks; value–chain estimation; graph neural networks; spa-
tiotemporal forecasting; optimal transport; constrained estimation; conformal prediction;
critical minerals.

1 Introduction

The security of critical mineral and material supply chains has become a central concern
for economic competitiveness, national security, and the global energy transition (Barlock
et al. 2024; U.S. Department of Energy 2023; U.S. Government Accountability Office
2024). Modern supply chains are globally distributed and highly interconnected, linking
extraction, processing, manufacturing, and final consumption across multiple countries
and industries through complex global value chains (Baldwin and Lopez-Gonzalez 2015;
Baldwin and Freeman 2021). Recent disruptions arising from the COVID-19 pandemic,
geopolitical conflicts, trade restrictions, natural disasters, and transportation bottlenecks
have demonstrated that vulnerabilities often originate far beyond immediate trading part-
ners and cascade through these interconnected networks (Benoit et al. 2022; Amaral et
al. 2022). These developments have underscored the need for analytical methods capa-
ble of characterizing, monitoring, and forecasting the evolution of strategic supply chain
networks in support of policy and industrial decision making.
 A broad range of analytical approaches has been developed to study supply chains, in-
cluding international trade statistics, input–output models, material flow analysis, network
science, optimization models, statistical monitoring systems, and, more recently, machine
learning and artificial intelligence methods. Input–output models provide macroeconomic
perspectives on production and trade dependencies by representing inter-industry re-
lationships within national and global economies (Leontief 1936; Miller and Blair 2009;
Timmer et al. 2015). Material flow analysis offers physically consistent accounting of re-
source flows across industrial systems and has recently incorporated probabilistic meth-
ods for estimating uncertain material stocks and flows (Pauliuk and Heeren 2020; Zhu
et al. 2026). Network-based approaches characterize structural properties such as con-
nectivity, centrality, and systemic fragility (Korniyenko, Pinat, and Dew 2017), while opti-
mization models support operational decision making under uncertainty (Aqlan and Lam
2016). More recently, graph neural networks have demonstrated promising capabilities
for learning complex relational dependencies and forecasting supply chain behavior (Wan
and Jiang 2026).
 Despite these advances, important methodological gaps remain. Most existing ap-
proaches address only one aspect of the supply chain and rarely provide an integrated
computational representation capable of supporting both inference and prediction. Eco-
nomic accounting frameworks, such as input-output models and Social Accounting Ma-
trices (SAMs), extend the national accounting framework to represent inter-industry pro-
duction and trade relationships but are not designed to model and predict supply chain
network. Material flow analysis focuses primarily on accounting and reconciliation rather
than prediction, while optimization methods generally require predefined scenarios and
often rely on proprietary firm-level information (Aqlan and Lam 2016). Statistical monitor-
ing systems detect emerging disruptions through changes in observed indicators but re-
main largely descriptive and retrospective (Benoit et al. 2022; Amaral et al. 2022). Recent
graph neural network approaches improve predictive performance but generally assume

that the underlying supply chain network is already known and complete, without esti-
mating unobserved production, demand, or other latent supply chain quantities (Wan and
Jiang 2026). Existing methodologies rarely integrate heterogeneous observations into a
unified computational framework capable of simultaneously constructing, estimating, and
forecasting evolving supply chain networks.
 To address these limitations, we introduce the Supply Chain Exposure Network
Tracking and Risk Assessment (SENTRA) framework, a modular computational frame-
work for constructing, analyzing, and forecasting temporal supply chain networks for criti-
cal minerals and materials. SENTRA models global supply chains as temporal attributed
multi-relational graphs constructed from heterogeneous observations describing produc-
tion, trade, value-chain relationships, participating countries, and contextual observations.
The temporal graph is the central computational representation through which latent sup-
ply chain quantities are estimated, graph-derived exposure measures are computed, and
future network evolution is forecast. This graph-centric architecture is modular, and ac-
commodates additional analytical models, contextual observations, and decision-support
capabilities as the broader SENTRA framework evolves.
 This paper is the first in a three-part series describing the SENTRA framework. Part I
establishes the computational and statistical foundation through temporal attributed multi-
relational graph construction, latent-quantity estimation, graph-derived exposure mea-
sures, and spatiotemporal graph forecasting 1 . Part II applies this computational foun-
dation to exposure assessment, anomaly detection, including the detection of potential
tariff and sanctions circumvention, disruption and vulnerability analysis, resilience, and
scenario-based policy analysis. Part III extends the SENTRA framework to multi-material
supply chain modeling through hierarchical transfer learning, cross-supply-chain reason-
ing, scalable computation, and enhanced decision-support capabilities for strategic policy
and industrial decision making. Future research will continue to expand the SENTRA
framework through the integration of complementary analytical methods, including graph-
based, statistical, econometric, and other relevant approaches within its modular compu-
tational framework.
 Part I makes four principal methodological contributions. First, we develop a method-
ology for constructing a temporal attributed multi-relational graph that integrates hetero-
geneous observations into a unified representation of critical mineral and material supply
chains. The resulting graph captures bilateral trade relationships, within-country value-
chain transformations, and their evolution over time. Second, we develop a latent-quantity
estimation methodology that recovers unobserved supply chain quantities, including do-
mestic production, final demand, and upstream product dependencies, by combining het-
erogeneous observations with physically consistent supply–demand constraints. Third,
we develop graph-derived exposure measures that quantify direct and indirect dependen-

ongoing research directions, and future extensions of SENTRA.

2 SENTRA Framework Overview

SENTRA framework is a modular computational framework for constructing, analyzing,
and forecasting global supply chain networks. SENTRA represents supply chains as
evolving networked systems in which production, trade, processing, manufacturing, and
end use are interconnected across multiple countries and stages of the value chain. By
integrating heterogeneous observations into a common temporal graph representation,
the framework enables reconstruction of hidden supply chain dependencies, estimation
of latent quantities, prediction of future network evolution, and quantitative assessment of
supply chain exposure and systemic risk. The framework is designed to address several
strategic supply chain questions that can be grouped into four fundamental categories:
 1. Hidden dependencies: Which products remain exposed to foreign upstream risk,
 though they appear to be sourced domestically or from allied countries?
 2. Network exposure: How do direct and indirect dependencies spread through multi-
 stage supply chains, and which downstream products or industries are most exposed
 to upstream disruptions? and can anomalous trade patterns reveal potential tariff or
 sanctions circumvention?
 3. Forecasting and scenario analysis: How will supply chain networks evolve under

 3

 changing economic conditions, disruptions, or policy interventions, and how do these
 changes alter future exposure?
 4. Policy evaluation and optimization: Which domestic and trade policies most ef-
 fectively strengthen supply chain resilience, reduce strategic dependencies, and im-
 prove supply security, and how can their effectiveness be quantitatively evaluated?

2.1 Computational Architecture
SENTRA is organized as a modular computational architecture consisting of three in-
teracting components: Data Integration, Network Intelligence, and Decision Support (Fig-
ure 1). These components share a common temporal attributed multi-relational graph that
serves as the common computational representation across the framework. The modular
design enables individual analytical components to evolve while preserving the common
graph representation.
  • Data Integration: The data integration component combines heterogeneous obser-
 vations describing international trade, domestic production, processing, manufactur-
 ing, value-chain relationships, participating countries, and contextual observations.
 Statistical estimation methods reconcile incomplete observations and estimate latent
 supply chain quantities while enforcing supply–demand accounting constraints. The
 resulting temporal attributed multi-relational graph provides a unified representation
 of the evolving supply chain.
  • Network Intelligence: The network intelligence component operates on the tempo-
 ral attributed multi-relational graph to characterize supply chain structure, compute
 graph-derived exposure measures, identify hidden dependencies, detect disruptions
 and anomalies, and forecast network evolution. Part I develops graph-derived expo-
 sure measures and a graph forecasting architecture as the initial analytical capabil-
 ities of this component. The modular architecture accommodates additional analyt-
 ical methods operating on the common graph representation, enabling the network
 intelligence capabilities of SENTRA to evolve without changing the underlying com-
 putational framework.
  • Decision Support: The decision-support component integrates the analytical capa-
 bilities of SENTRA to support strategic policy and industrial decision making. These
 capabilities include exposure assessment, continuous risk monitoring, scenario anal-
 ysis, early-warning indicators, policy evaluation, and other application-specific anal-
 yses. Part II develops these capabilities through exposure assessment, anomaly
 detection, disruption analysis, vulnerability analysis, systemic risk assessment, re-
 silience analysis, and scenario-based policy analysis. Part III extends the decision-
 support capabilities of the framework through multi-material supply chain modeling,
 hierarchical transfer learning, cross-supply-chain reasoning, scalable computation,
 and enhanced decision-support capabilities.

3 Data

Supply chain networks are characterized by heterogeneous observations that differ in
their spatial and temporal resolution, product (or materials) coverage, and degree of com-
pleteness. No individual data source provides a complete description of the production,
transformation, and movement of products through a global value chain. International
trade statistics generally exclude domestic production and consumption, production statis-
tics are often incomplete or vary in temporal and geospatial resolution, and many interme-
diate manufacturing activities are not directly observed. SENTRA treats the available data
as partial observations of an underlying supply chain network whose complete state must
be inferred. To construct this representation, SENTRA integrates three broad classes
of information: observations describing international trade, observations describing pro-
duction and value-chain transformations, and contextual observations such as firm-level
information, economic indicators, geopolitical events, transportation and logistics data,
environmental conditions, social factors, institutional conditions, and policy changes that
influence supply chain dynamics. The framework is designed to accommodate multiple
data sources within each category, allowing new observations to be incorporated as they
become available.
 1. Trade observations: For this study, bilateral trade observations are obtained from
 Global Trade Tracker (GTT), which provides monthly import and export statistics for
 more than 140 reporting countries beginning in 1988. Current analysis focuses on ob-
 servations from 2010 onward. Available mirror statistics are used to infer trade involv-
 ing non-reporting countries, increasing geographic coverage to over 200 countries.
 Country-specific tariff classifications also provide more granular product resolution for
 selected commodities than is available from more aggregated trade datasets, while
 the monthly reporting frequency enables the network to incorporate recent changes
 in global trade patterns.
 2. Production and value-chain observations: Trade data provide information on cross-
 border material flows but do not capture production and transformation within national
 economies. We therefore incorporate production statistics together with value-chain
 process information to obtain a more complete representation of supply chain ac-
 tivity. Production volumes for raw materials and selected intermediate products are
 obtained from the U.S. Geological Survey (2025), providing observations of mine pro-
 duction and intermediate materials such as smelter’s output where available. Value-
 chain transformations specify the admissible relationships between input and output
 products. We represent these relationships using linear process maps that connect
 raw materials, intermediate products, and end-use products through successive pro-
 cessing stages. Each trade classification and reported production quantity is then

 6

 linked with a corresponding process node, providing the structural basis for construct-
 ing the supply chain graph. Alternative process representations could be incorpo-
 rated within the same framework without changing the underlying graph formulation.
  3. Contextual observations: Supply chains are shaped by a broad range of economic,
 geopolitical, environmental, social, and institutional factors that extend beyond mate-
 rial production and trade. The SENTRA framework is designed to incorporate these
 observations as contextual node and edge attributes within the temporal attributed
 multi-relational graph. Although contextual observations are not incorporated in the
 methodology presented in Part I, they provide an important extension of the frame-
 work. Part II and Part III incorporates contextual observations, including tariff sched-
 ules, to support exposure assessment, disruption analysis, and scenario-based pol-
 icy analysis. Future extensions will accommodate additional contextual observations,
 including firm-level information, transportation and logistics infrastructure, additional
 policy measures, environmental indicators, and near-real-time monitoring data, as
 they become available.

4 Temporal Graph Construction and Latent-Quantity Es-

SENTRA integrates heterogeneous observations of international trade, domestic produc-
tion, and value-chain transformations to construct a country-agnostic temporal attributed
multi-relational graph. The framework is designed to accommodate contextual obser-
vations as additional node and edge attributes in future analytical modules. The graph
provides a unified representation of the supply chain in which country-product pairs form
the nodes, while trade flows and value-chain transformations define complementary edge
types. Quantities that are not directly observed, including domestic production, final de-
mand, and input dependencies, are treated as latent and estimated during graph con-

struction.
 We first define nodes and the two edge types in Section 4.1, then the two estimators
required to populate the edge weights and node attributes that are not directly observed:
product input dependency coefficients in Section 4.2 and latent domestic-production and
final-demand quantities in Section 4.3.

dependency, domestic production, final demand, and product dependency metrics are
generally not observed and are treated as latent quantities. Estimating these quantities
populates the value-chain edges, completing the graph representation required for the
exposure analysis and forecasting methods developed in the following sections.

4.2 Estimating the product input dependency coefficient ipq
Method 1. Direct Estimation: The product input coefficient, ipq , quantifies the contri-
bution of input product p to the production of product q. The metric defines the weights
associated with value-chain edges in the graph and therefore determines how depen-
dencies spread between successive stages of the supply chain. Because these relation-

and production data. The estimator aggregates country-local import-shares into a global
export-weighted aggregation. We use two estimators. Method 1 (this subsection) is a
direct evaluation; Method 2 in Section 4.3 iterates it jointly with the production estimate,
using the Method 1 result to initialize the first iteration.

4.3 Estimating latent production and final demand
Construction of the value-chain edges (3) requires estimates of domestic production, Pqk ,
which are unavailable for most product-country pairs. These quantities, together with

5 Graph-Based Network Exposure Measures

The graph constructed in Section 4 makes hidden supply chain dependencies computable

The exposure measures defined in Equations (15) to (17) and (24) are deterministic func-

weights. The spatiotemporal graph forecasting framework described in Section 6 esti-

mates the future graph Gt+1 , from which the corresponding exposure measures are com-

6 Spatiotemporal Graph Forecasting

Having constructed the temporal attributed multi-relational graph, the next step is to fore-
cast the graph over time. Given a sequence of historical graph snapshots, the objective is
to predict the node attributes and relationship weights defining the graph at the next time
step while satisfying the supply–demand accounting constraints introduced in Section 4.3.
The graph-derived exposure measures defined in Section 5 are then evaluated on the
forecast graph to obtain future supply chain exposure. Several graph neural network ar-
chitectures can be used to forecast the graph evolution, including graph convolutional
networks (Kipf and Welling 2017), graph attention networks (Veličković et al. 2018), and
GraphSAGE (Hamilton, Ying, and Leskovec 2017). They differ primarily in how informa-
tion is aggregated across graph relationships. In this work, the graph forecasting model
is implemented using a relational GraphSAGE architecture for three reasons specific to
supply chain graphs:
First, the aggregation weights carry economic meaning: GraphSAGE’s aggregation
weights are prescribed rather than learned, allowing them to be defined directly from the
observed edge values normalized by destination. Thus, information propagates through
the graph according to observed trade shares and production relationships rather than
learned attention coefficients. This preserves the interpretation of edge weights as sup-
ply shares and remains consistent with the conservation accounting introduced in Sec-
tion 4.3. A graph attention network instead learns its mixing weights as feature-based
attention; even when edge weights are supplied as features they enter through a learned
softmax rather than serving directly as the mixing coefficients, so its aggregation need
not coincide with the observed supply shares or with the conservation accounting of Sec-
tion 4.3
Second, it is light and scalable: The mean aggregator adds no attention parameters,
reducing the risk of overfitting, and its localized neighborhood aggregation, optionally with
neighbor sampling, scales to large graphs (Hamilton, Ying, and Leskovec 2017; Ying et
al. 2018), which matters as the framework expands to many materials and countries.
Third, it generalizes inductively: Because it learns aggregation functions rather than
node-specific embeddings, it applies unchanged to nodes and entire graph snapshots
never seen in training (Hamilton, Ying, and Leskovec 2017), which is essential here,
where we forecast future months’ graphs and expect to add new countries and products
over time.

6.4 Multi-task decoder
From the node embeddings {hv }, the decoder predicts the node attributes and relation-
ship weights defining the forecast graph Gbt+1 through parallel multilayer-perceptron heads:
1. Edge existence. For a candidate ordered pair (u, v) a logistic head scores the prob-
 ability π̂uv = σ(gex (hu , hv , euv )) that a trade edge exists next month (link prediction), so
 that the formation and disappearance of trade relationships are modeled, rather than
 only changes in existing trade flows.
2. Edge flow (residual on a persistence prior). The flow head predicts a log-space
 correction to a lag-1 persistence prior, log Tbuv = log Tuv (t) + gflow (hu , hv , euv ), gated by
 the existence probability π̂uv . Predicting a residual anchors the model to the strong
 persistence of trade and concentrates learning on changes.
3. Non-root production. A node-level head predicts domestic production for processed
 products, log Pbvk = anchorvk + gprod (hv ), where the anchor is a smoothed (multi-month
 moving average) of recent production—a choice that damps spurious single-month
 seasonal swings.
4. Root production. A separate head handles extraction-stage products (e.g. ore) that
 have no modeled value chain inputs products.
5. Auxiliary node forecast. A head predicts node-level import/export totals, supervised
 with a bidirectional consistency loss, providing the marginal constraints used by the
 mass-balance projection.

6.7 Uncertainty quantification
Split conformal, and why it fits. A flagged exposure means little without a sense of how
uncertain the underlying forecast is, we apply conformal prediction to the point forecasts
(Vovk, Gammerman, and Shafer 2005; Lei et al. 2018), which turns any forecaster into
calibrated intervals using only a held-out sample of its own past errors. Calibration pro-
ceeds as follows: on a calibration set disjoint from training, compute the forecast residual
at each point; the empirical quantiles of those residuals set the interval widths for future
forecasts. Nothing is assumed about the shape of the error distribution or the correctness
of the model. The payoff is a distribution-free, finite-sample guarantee: if calibration and
test residuals are exchangeable, the realized coverage is at least 1 − α up to a 1/(ncal + 1)
term, for any predictor and any sample size. This is exactly what a monitoring system
needs, intervals that are honest by construction rather than by a parametric leap of faith.
Mondrian stratification. Trade flows are strongly heteroskedastic: large, stable flows
are generally more predictable than small, intermittent ones, so a single global interval
would be at once too wide for the former and too narrow for the latter. We therefore use
Mondrian (group-wise) conformal calibration (Vovk et al. 2003), partitioning trade edges
into strata b(u, v) by product and flow magnitude and calibrating within each. Because
forecasts and residuals live in log space, the band is multiplicative in the level, and we
keep it two-sided to capture skew:

is controlled by the distributional drift between calibration and test residuals, formally a

age, consistent with the near-nominal empirical coverage reported in Section 7. Second,
for streaming deployment we replace the fixed quantile with adaptive conformal inference

the bands after a run of misses and tightening them after a run of hits; ensemble time-
series variants such as EnbPI (Xu and Xie 2021) are equally compatible. The stratified
split-conformal bands of Equation (35) are thus the fixed-sample instrument we evaluate,
and their adaptive form is the online instrument the monitor runs.

7 Validation on the Aluminum Supply Chain

This section evaluates (i) the learned graph representations, (ii) the forecasting perfor-
mance, and (iii) the uncertainty quantification. We validate the proposed graph construc-
tion and forecasting architecture on the aluminum supply chain using monthly data from
2010–2024. The graph consists of the value chain from bauxite and alumina through
unwrought aluminum to semi-fabricated forms and downstream components. The fore-
casting architecture is trained on data through December 2022; observations from 2023–
2024 are held out and never seen during training. This section provide methodological
evidence that the learned graph representations are meaningful, the forecasting architec-
ture is predictive, and the associated uncertainty estimates are well calibrated.

7.1 An interpretable supply chain latent space
A point-accurate forecaster need not have learned anything economically meaningful; it
could merely extrapolate existing trade relationships. We therefore ask a stronger ques-
tion: does the encoder’s latent space organize countries by their functional role in the
supply chain? This is a probe of interpretability, and a prerequisite for trusting the model’s
behavior under perturbation.
Experiment: We feed the actual monthly trade snapshots for the most recent quarter
(Oct–Dec 2024) through the trained encoder and read back the per-node latent states;
for each country we average its states over the products it actively trades, yielding one la-
tent vector per country. Independently, we label each country by its dominant export role,
computed from the product composition of its 2024 aluminum exports across four supply
chain stages—upstream (bauxite/alumina), primary smelter (unwrought, slab, billet, in-
got), converter / end market (semi-fabricated and finished goods), and scrap (secondary
aluminum). Restricting to the 117 countries with more than $30M in annual aluminum
trade, we project the latent vectors onto the two linear-discriminant directions that best
separate the four role labels and measure how well roles are recovered by the latent
geometry alone, via per-role 5-nearest-neighbor purity (the fraction of each country’s five
nearest neighbors sharing its role). In Figure 4 a country’s position is the role-composition
simplex (corner = pure role) and its color blends the four role colors by export share, so
mixed economies appear in intermediate hues and locations.
Result: Roles are almost perfectly linearly separable in the latent space: same-role 5-NN
purity is 100% for upstream producers, 100% for primary smelters, 98% for converters / end
markets, and 95% for scrap exporters (Figure 4). The encoder was trained only to forecast
trade flows and node quantities—it was never given role labels—yet it has discovered the
stage structure of the value chain, placing bauxite/alumina exporters (Guinea, Australia,
Brazil), primary smelters (Russia, the Gulf states, Iceland, Canada), and downstream
converters (Germany, Italy, the United States) in distinct regions, with hub and dual-role
economies (e.g. the United States, which is both a major scrap exporter and a large end

7.3 Prediction of new trade relationships
A forecast that only re-weights existing flows misses the events that matter most for policy:
the formation of a new trade relationship, which is the visible signature of rerouting and
transshipment. We therefore test the model on births, defined as a trade flow present at
month t but absent at t − 1 (new or re-opened), above a $100k value floor. The lag-1
persistence baseline cannot recover these events by construction because every trade
relationship absent at t − 1 is assigned zero existence probability and zero trade value.

signed existence probability above 0.5 to 99.8% of them (Figure 7a,b), while the lag-1 prior
recovers none. This is not a saturated head that predicts existence everywhere: discrim-

 7.4 Coverage of conformal bands
 We assess the magnitude-stratified conformal bands of Section 6.7 by their empirical
 coverage on the held-out period (Figure 6). Global coverage on 2024 is 93.1% against a
 95% target, and by construction, band width increases from large, stable trade flows to
 smaller, more intermittent trade flows, confirming that the Mondrian stratification captures
 heteroscedastic forecast uncertainty rather than imposing a single global interval width.

8 Discussion and Future Research

This paper establishes the computational graph foundation of the SENTRA framework.
Heterogeneous observations are integrated to estimate latent quantities (Sections 4.2
and 4.3) and construct a temporal attributed multi-relational graph representing trade and
value-chain relationships (Section 4). Graph-derived exposure measures characterize
multi-stage supply chain dependencies (Section 5), while the graph forecasting framework
estimates the future evolution of the temporal graph (Section 6).
 The framework is deliberately modular. The temporal attributed multi-relational graph
serves as the common computational representation linking data integration, network in-
telligence, and decision-support capabilities. Exposure measures are deterministic func-
tions of the graph, while graph forecasting estimates its future evolution. This separation
enables individual analytical components to be refined or replaced without modifying the
underlying graph representation. Additional graph-based, statistical, econometric, and
other analytical methods can be incorporated within the same computational framework
while preserving a consistent representation of the supply chain network.

8.1 Current limitations
The current implementation relies on several modeling assumptions that should be inter-
preted as approximations rather than ground truth. These limitations primarily reflect the
current estimation methods and implementation rather than the underlying computational
architecture.
 • The production estimator (Equation (7)) represents production through estimated
 product input dependency coefficients ipq , but it does not model value added at each
 stage explicitly. Labor, energy, capital, and other sources of value added are therefore
 absorbed implicitly into the recovered production estimates. This approximation may
 introduce bias where value added represents a substantial share relative to embod-
 ied inputs. Introducing an explicit value-added term into the supply-demand balance
 constraints is an interesting area of future research.
 • The quadratic program selects the minimum-magnitude solution satisfying the ac-
 counting constraints. This conservative choice can underestimate domestic produc-
 tion in transit economies with limited processing activity, where recovered production
 becomes a small residual. The estimates are therefore most reliable when anchored
 by observed production data. Future research could also apply Bayesian and other
 statistical methods to derive uncertainty distributions for these estimates when ob-
 served data is unavailable.
 • In the current framework trade flows are represented by the value of trade instead
 of physical quantities, because quantities are not as consistently available as values.
 Without information on traded volumes, changes in trade values cannot be separated

 28

 into price and quantity effects, limiting the interpretation of value-based shocks.
  • The current implementation relies on manually constructed process maps that define
 admissible value-chain relationships within each supply chain. Extending SENTRA
 to additional critical minerals and strategic supply chains requires maintaining these
 underlying process representations as products, technologies, and manufacturing
 pathways evolve. Ongoing research at Argonne is investigating artificial intelligence
 methods for automatically constructing and updating process maps from heteroge-
 neous information sources. These capabilities are expected to substantially improve
 the scalability of SENTRA to additional materials and industrial sectors.
  • The current implementation models individual material supply chains independently.
 Although the existing framework already relies on high-performance computing, scal-
 ing the framework to multiple interconnected material supply chains will substantially
 increase graph size, computational requirements, and the complexity of cross-supply-
 chain reasoning. Part III addresses these challenges by developing a unified multi-
 material graph that supports hierarchical transfer learning and cross-supply-chain
 reasoning across interconnected critical mineral supply chains. These developments
 will continue to leverage leadership-class high-performance computing resources,
 including systems at the Argonne Leadership Computing Facility (ALCF), to support
 large-scale graph construction, forecasting, and network intelligence.
  • The current implementation focuses primarily on trade, production, and value-chain
 observations. Although the SENTRA framework is designed to incorporate contex-
 tual observations as node and edge attributes, many important drivers of supply
 chain behavior, including firm-level information, transportation infrastructure, policy
 changes, geopolitical events, environmental conditions, and other contextual fac-
 tors, are not represented in the current implementation. Future research will de-
 velop methods for integrating these heterogeneous observations into the temporal
 attributed multi-relational graph to improve supply chain characterization, forecast-
 ing, and decision-support capabilities.

8.2 Future research directions
The current estimators are deliberately transparent and computationally simple. The
framework does not require these particular estimators; it requires only that node and
edge quantities be estimated consistently before they are assembled into the temporal
attributed multi-relational graph. This separation allows individual analytical components
to evolve without modifying the underlying computational framework.
 Future research should incorporate complementary graph-based, statistical, econo-
metric, and other analytical methods at both the node and edge levels. Examples include
gravity and structural models for bilateral trade, state-space and dynamic-factor models for
production and demand, econometric estimation of substitution, value added, and pass-
through within value chains, and complementary graph-based methods for network infer-
ence and forecasting. Because graph construction and forecasting operate on estimated

 29

node and edge quantities rather than on specific estimation procedures, improvements to
these analytical components extend directly to graph-derived exposure measures, fore-
casting, and subsequent decision-support capabilities.
 The framework presented in Part I establishes the computational foundation for the
broader SENTRA research project. Part II applies this computational foundation to expo-
sure assessment, anomaly detection, including the detection of potential tariff and sanc-
tions circumvention, disruption and vulnerability analysis, resilience, and scenario-based
policy analysis. Building on the graph-derived exposure measures introduced in Sec-
tion 5, it develops methods for identifying rerouting behavior, tariff circumvention, and
concentrated sourcing risks, while incorporating selected contextual observations, includ-
ing trade and policy measures, to support supply chain assessment. Part III extends the
framework to multi-material supply chain modeling through hierarchical transfer learning,
cross-supply-chain reasoning, scalable computation, enhanced decision-support capabil-
ities for strategic policy and industrial decision making, and the incorporation of a limited
set of contextual observations that enhance the analytical capabilities of the unified multi-
material graph. These extensions require additional modeling components, including em-
pirically estimated substitution elasticities and constrained reallocation mechanisms, to
move beyond forecasting toward scenario analysis and structural policy evaluation.

8.3 Conclusion
This paper presents the methodological foundation of the SENTRA framework. The
framework combines latent-quantity estimation, temporal attributed multi-relational graph
construction, graph-derived exposure measures, and graph forecasting into a unified com-
putational framework for analyzing supply chains from heterogeneous observations. Val-
idation on the global aluminum supply chain demonstrates that the learned graph repre-
sentations capture economically meaningful supply chain structure, that the graph fore-
casting framework accurately predicts future trade relationships, and that the associated
uncertainty estimates are well calibrated.
 More broadly, the principal contribution of SENTRA is the separation of data integra-
tion, network intelligence, and decision-support capabilities through a common temporal
attributed multi-relational graph. This modular architecture allows individual estimation
methods, analytical models, and forecasting approaches to evolve independently while
preserving a consistent computational framework for supply chain analysis. As additional
data sources and complementary analytical methods become available, they can be in-
corporated within the same computational framework without changing the underlying
graph representation.
