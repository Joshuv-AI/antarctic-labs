Capital Adequacy as a Driver of Profitability: Panel Evidence from Nepalese Wholesale and Retail Microfinance Institutions

Basu Dev Lamichhane, Satyanarayan Choudhary, Bharat Ram Dhungana, Manika Shrestha

Abstract

AbstractBackgroundCapital adequacy is essential for the sustainable operation of microfinance institutions (MFIs). MFIs can expand their outreach to low-income and marginalized people if the institutions are sustainable.MethodsThis study is based on secondary data collected from the annual reports of the selected microfinance institutions. A descriptive and causal comparative research design was used. The study sampled 10 microfinance institutions from 57 MFIs, including wholesale and retail. The independent variables were capital adequacy ratio, leverage ratio, size, growth, inflation, and type. The dependent variables were return on assets (ROA) and return on equity (ROE). The purposive sampling method was used.ResultsThe panel regression model observed that the regression coefficients for SIZE and GDP growth in the fixed-effect panel regression model, and CAR, LR, SIZE, GDP growth, and TYPE are statistically significant for ROA. This study did not find a statistically significant effect on ROE. Profitability of microfinance institutions is not only determined by the sum of capital and investments but also through the strategic balance between financial stability and operational efficiency. High levels of capital adequacy necessitate restrained management to avoid diminishing returns. The regression coefficient for TYPE is statistically significant; the profitability of Retail MFIs and Wholesale MFIs is different.ConclusionCapital structure, asset size, and leverage are determinants of the profitability of microfinance institutions. One remarkable result is that the type of microfinance institutions is statistically significant; the profitability of retail MFIs and wholesale MFIs is different. Wholesale MFIs are more capitalized but less profitable than retail MFIs. This implies that capital position does not necessarily translate into higher profitability. Retail business models in terms of financial performance may not necessarily work in wholesale business models. Capital adequacy is a significant determinant of the type of MFI profitability in Nepal. Policymakers should not apply a one-size-fits-all regulation but develop diverse supervisory and capital adequacy frameworks for wholesale and retail MFIs. This study is particularly useful for regulators and microfinance institutions for policy formulation.Keywords: Capital adequacy, MFIs, profitability, regulatory institution, sustainability

Introduction

MFIs offer financial services to the unbanked population and play a significant role in socio-economic transformation of low-income people. Expanding financial services, livelihood improvement, and poverty reduction through productive investment and collateral-free lending are key attributes of microfinance (
). Sustainability of MFIs largely depends on prudential regulations and capital adequacy that ensure MFIs’ ability to withstand both expected and unexpected losses (
). Preserving strong capital adequacy originates from supervisory and capital structure perspective that enables sustained operation of MFIs’ lending (

).

CAR confirms the financial stability of MFIs, especially in fostering financial prudence. Profitability, loan portfolio quality, and institution size are important elements of CAR among MFIs (
). The profitability of MFIs strengthens capital growth through expanded capital structures (
). Adequate capital levels are shaped by institutional alignment, profitability, supervisory requirements, and operational effectiveness (

Capital adequacy supports economic stability in the banking and microfinance sectors. Financial institutions must maintain a minimum level of capital, proportional to their risk-weighted assets, to protect shareholders and preserve complete certainty (
). The CAR is supervisory tool used to assess microfinance institution’s ability to maintain healthy status (
). CAR consists of two main components: Tier 1 capital and Tier 2 capital, contributing to safeguarding against insolvency conditions (
).

The need to preserve adequate capital is emphasized in frequent supervisory guidance, including the Basel Accords, international standards for economic sector regulation (
). Regulatory institutions adopt capital adequacy norms tailored for MFIs to improve financial inclusion while reducing total risk (
). CAR is indicator of a financial institution’s stability and flexibility, measuring its capital adequacy relative to its risk-weighted assets (
).

Microfinance institutions exhibit high CAR levels, thereby supporting the sequence between capital adequacy and stability. There are positive associations between profitability and CAR. Liquidity and credit quality matter considerably in determining profitability (
).

It is essential to assess whether profitability underwrites the development of CAR through firming internal capital assets, or if capital necessities themselves overwhelm profitability due to preventive financial illiquidity (
). Limited leverage due to high capital requirements can constrain MFIs’ ability to offer competitive loan products and reduce their focus on high-return lending, further affecting profitability (
). Understanding this dynamic is crucial for aligning financial regulation with the growth and durability of MFIs in Nepal.

Given the importance of capital adequacy in microfinance institutions, it has been necessary to study various aspects, the impact, and the status of the capital adequacy norms and standards in the context of Nepal. Various questions arise about the subjects such as (i). What is the current status of the capital adequacy ratio, leverage ratio, size, GDP growth, inflation, and type on profitability of Nepalese MFIs? (ii). What is the relationship between capital adequacy ratio, leverage ratio, growth, inflation, and type on profitability of Nepalese MFIs? (iii). What is the impact of the variables, capital adequacy ratio, leverage ratio, growth, inflation, and type on the profitability of Nepalese MFIs? The aim of this study is to evaluate the impact of capital adequacy ratios on the profitability of microfinance institutions in Nepal.

Literature review

Theoretical review

A theoretical review summarizes the main concepts associated with capital adequacy and profitability of microfinance institutions. Modern portfolio theory (MPT) was established by Markowitz in 1952. It is found on the impression that a stockholder can conceive an “effective” selection that exploits probable returns aimed at an assumed equal level of risk. This model is primarily applicable to MFIs that emphasize expanding economic assets to mitigate risk. Intended for MFIs, preserving a CAR remains important for managing risks. It includes credit, liquidity, and operational risks that affect operational performance. MPT advises that MFIs can expand their credit selections and secure adequate principal to cushion against probable risks (
). This theory guides MFIs to maintain CAR which is essential to long-term stability and dependable effectiveness.

The risk-based capital adequacy is established by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision in 1999. It highlights the economic institutions to preserve capital in balance with the risks related to credit, market, and operational (
). MFIs analyze the essential capital created on the institution’s risk-weighted assets to assist in determining the minimum capital required to absorb potential losses. The supervisory system safeguards against monetary shocks, prevents liquidation, and utilizes investors’ funds in the productive sector (
). Sustaining CAR, MFIs meet supervisory norms while ensuring stability among investors.

Agency theory was advanced by Michael Jensen and William Meckling in 1976. It examines the relationship between principals (owners) and agents (managers). This theory advises that executives may remain incentivized to take extreme risks or engage in activities that benefit them individually. A resilient CAR can help to maintain adequate equity capital to attract potential borrowers, thereby reducing the need for risky liability support. By preserving an adequate CAR, directors are less likely to incur extreme risks (
). This reduces the likelihood of conflict among executives and proprietors as joint gatherings are incentivized to preserve the institution’s financial health. A strong CAR serves as a catalyst in bringing to the forefront the benefits of managers and proprietors, encouraging long-term effectiveness. It fosters collaborative working efficiency and sustainability of organization.

Research methodology

Research design

This research uses the descriptive and causal-comparative investigation method. The data were used to evaluate the impact of the capital adequacy ratio on profitability in Nepalese microfinance institutions and to inform future research. A causal-comparative investigation design was applied to assess the effects of capital adequacy ratios on profitability in Nepalese microfinance institutions. The study followed a quantitative approach.

Population and sample, and sampling design

In Nepal, there are 57 registered MFIs as of May 2025. For this study, 10 microfinance institutions were purposively selected based on their significant presence, influence, and financial diversity. The sampling is structured to capture both wholesaler and retailer microfinance institutions, reflecting different operational scales and business models. Specifically, FMLBSL, SKBLBSL, and RSDCLBSL are categorized as wholesaler microfinance institutions, accounting for 30 percent of the total sample. These wholesalers primarily provide bulk loans to smaller microfinance institutions or cooperatives rather than directly to individual clients.

The remaining 70 percent of involves seven retail microfinance institutions, including CLBSL, NULBSL, FMLBSL, GBLBSL, MMLBSL, NMBLBSL, and NADEPLBSL. Those institutionss involve direct end customers, provided they are limited to entities and collections for investment decisions.

This sampling method safeguards a representative sample of large-scale, extensive creditors and smaller-scale, retail-oriented microfinance breadwinners, enabling a comprehensive and proportional examination of the capital adequacy ratio (CAR) and its impact on profitability across diverse administrative categories within Nepalese microfinance institutions.

Nature and sources of data

The main source of the data was secondary, comprising the annual reports of the selected microfinance institutions. They provided the thorough financial statements essential for analyzing capital adequacy and profitability. Furthermore, the research combined data and institutions, drawing on both published and unpublished sources. Applicable resources such as academic journals, newspapers, and magazines were studied to capture broader industrial tendencies and background context. Additionally, annual reports of concerned MFIs and and Nepal Rastra Bank NRB are important sources of consistent financial data and regulatory information.

Data collection instruments and procedures

The data collected from the annual reports of the selected institutions and from reports published by the Nepal Rastra Bank were organized and analyzed in Excel. The use of suitable data collection methods enhanced the credibility and value of the research outcomes.

Data analysis tools and methods

Financial and statistical techniques, such as mean, standard deviation, correlation, and multiple regression, were used to analyze data from the sampled institutions’ annual reports.

Discussion, conclusion, and policy implications

Discussion

This research explored the effect of capital adequacy on the profitability of Nepalese microfinance institutions. Capital adequacy shows a weak correlation with profitability. Leverage ratio exhibits a negative association indicating increased leverage reduces profitablity and raises the risks. Likewise, size of total assets positively and significantly associated with profitability of MFIs. GDP growth has positive relationship with profitability whereas inflation has a negative association with profitability of MFIs. These findings underscore the urgent need for a comprehensive method for capital structure, liquidity management, and debt operations to restore financial performance.

The inverse relationship between capital adequacy and profitability validates earlier findings. A high level of capital adequacy strengthens financial stability and constrains lending capacity and operational efficiency, thereby weakening profitability (
;
). It implies that excessive capital adequacy increases opportunity costs, as funds allocated to capital are not accessible for income-generating undertakings (
).

The panel regression model observed that regression coefficients for SIZE and GDP growth in the fixed-effects panel regression model, and CAR, LR, SIZE, GDP growth, and TYPE are statistically significant for ROA. This finding is consistent with the

Leverage ratio indicates significant positive influences on profitability (

Profitability of microfinance institutions is not only determined by the sum of capital and investments, but also through the strategic balance between financial stability and operational efficiency. High levels of capital adequacy necessitate restrained management to avoid diminishing returns. The regression coefficient for TYPE is statistically significant; the profitability of Retail MFIs and Wholesale MFIs is different. This finding aligns with the study made by
 that type of MFIs is related to social and commercial interests.

Capital adequacy is critical for financial security; excessively high levels can reduce profitability. Microfinance institutions must balance capital cushions with lending and investment activities. Leverage must be used carefully, as excessive reliance on debt can intensify risk without offering improved returns. A deliberate approach to capital management, liquidity planning, and debt operations helps institutions achieve sustainable and efficient financial performance.

Conclusion

The capital structure, asset size, and leverage are the determinants of the profitability of microfinance institutions. One interesting result is that the type of microfinance institution is statistically significant; the profitability of retail MFIs and wholesale MFIs is different. It indicates that wholesale MFIs are more capitalized while less profitable than retail MFIs. This implies that capital position does not necessarily explain into higher profitability. Retail business models in terms of financial performance may not necessarily work in wholesale business models. The impact of capital position on profitability depends on the business model to a certain extent. The unobserved factors, which include portfolio quality and operating efficiency, explain nearly all variations in profitability. Capital adequacy is a significant determinant of the type of MFI profitability in Nepal.

Policy implications

The effect of capital adequacy on profitability is contingent on the institutional business model. Policymakers should not apply a one-size-fits-all regulation but develop diverse supervisory and capital adequacy frameworks for wholesale and retail MFIs. The institutional business model approach enables microfinance institutions to achieve profitability and financial resilience and ultimately promote sustainable financial inclusion in Nepal. This study concentrates on a small sample of 10 Nepalese MFIs over 10 years, mainly in regard to capital adequacy and a specific set of firm-specific and macroeconomic determinants of profitability. Future research could extend the sample size, add other governance and risk factors, and conduct a rural-urban analysis. This study is particularly useful for regulators and microfinance institutions for policy formulation.
