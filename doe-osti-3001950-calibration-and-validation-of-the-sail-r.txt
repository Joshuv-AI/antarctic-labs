 Executive Summary
Calibration of a weather radar is not a simple process, as is well described and documented in several
articles (Chandrasekar, V, L Baldini, N Bharadwaj, and PL Smith. 2015. "Calibration procedures for
global precipitation-measurement ground-validation radars." URSI Radio Science Bulletin 2015(355):
45–73, https://10.23919/URSIRSB.2015.7909473.). Direct hardware-based characterization is called
calibration whereas indirect check with other sources such as disdrometer, or comparison with other
radars or indirect means, is called validation. The validation process has its own measurement error and
the comparison should fall within its limit. In the following we describe calibration and validation
processes for the SAIL radar. Based on the comparison of calibration and validation, an addition of 2 dB
is suggested for the SAIL radar observations, with a nuanced Zdr calibration correction, as presented. If a
fixed Zdr calibration is preferred, a bias addition of 0.5 dB is suggested.

1.0 Calibration of the SAIL Campaign CSU X-band Radar
 Using a Target Simulator
 During the week of September 10, 2023, a target simulator was set up near the Colorado State University
 (CSU)-CHILL radar facility, with the goal of obtaining an absolute calibration of the X-band radar that
 CSU deployed in the Crested Butte, Colorado ski area as part of the U.S. Department of Energy
 Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) User Facility’s Surface Atmosphere Integrated Field
 Laboratory (SAIL) campaign.

 Figure 1. CSU X-band radar target simulation set-up.

 The instrument setup had a 430 m baseline between the target and the radar (well beyond the
 1.8 m-diameter antenna far-field) and an elevation angle of ~1.5 deg. The radar was set up to scan a
 10 deg azimuthal sector at 13 elevations with 0.2 deg spacing. Each elevation was scanned in both
 clockwise and counterclockwise directions for a total of 26 PPIs per measurement cycle.

 During the measurement cycle the target simulator records the incoming radar waveforms and sends back
 to the radar a synthesized version with controlled amplitude and phase values simulating a polarimetric
 target located at a predetermined distance.

 Figure 2. Shape of the X-band radar short pulse for both polarizations.

 The target simulator was set up to produce a target echo at 7 km with a reflectivity value of 39 dBZ, a
 velocity value of 5 m/s, a spectral width of 0.5 m/s2 , a differential phase of -120 deg, and a differential
 reflectivity of 0 dB. A summary of the simulated target polarimetric values and plots of the radar

well-formed antenna main beam in all the measured variables. The 1.5 deg elevation angle together with

2.0 Reflectivity and Differential Reflectivity Validation of the
 SAIL X-band Scanning Radar (XSAIL)
 This document provides a comprehensive overview of the calibration process applied to radar reflectivity
 (Z) and differential reflectivity (Zdr) measurements acquired from the scanning X-band weather radar in
 SAIL. The precise measurement of Z and Zdr holds significant importance in microphysical retrievals,
 particularly for hydrometeor classification and quantitative precipitation estimation (QPE). The crucial
 role of these measurements in different applications emphasizes the need for precise calibration. In
 practice, radar measurements can be subject to systematic biases originating from factors like hardware
 and system design. As a result, regular calibration checks are needed to guarantee the accuracy of
 retrievals and the radar system's reliability.

 2.1 Reflectivity Calibration
 The use of naturally occurring volumetric targets for calibration is a well-established approach. [1]
 Raindrops, as reference targets, offer the advantage of directly accounting for antenna properties in the
 calibration process. One of the most widely adopted methods relies on surface-based disdrometer
 observations. Here, drop size distributions (DSDs) measured by disdrometers are converted into radar
 reflectivity through electromagnetic scattering computations. This approach involves comparing time
 series or distributions of calculated reflectivities from a disdrometer to the observed reflectivities from the
 radar system collocated at the disdrometer location.

 Figure 5. Location of X-band scanning radar and ARM instrumentation site in SAIL.

 A significant source of uncertainty in this method relates to the vertical variability of reflectivity profiles,
 which can arise from factors like turbulence, wind shear, evaporation, drop breakup, and coalescence.
 Consequently, it is important to focus on radar observations close to the ground, where disdrometers are
 positioned.

 3

Figure 5 shows the location of the XSAIL radar and ARM disdrometer site (38.9562o N, 106.9879o W).
The disdrometer is a laser disdrometer that samples drops at a 60-second interval. An inherent challenge
in comparing the two sets of instantaneous observations lies in the substantial disparity in sample
volumes. The radar averages precipitation over a considerably larger volume in each pulse compared to
the disdrometer. To address this, DSD samples are integrated into 2-minute samples. For instance, the
disdrometer at the ARM site is located 7.5 km from the XSAIL radar, with the radar’s 1o beam height at
the disdrometer location positioned at 130 meters above ground level. This proximity makes the ARM
disdrometer an ideal instrument for radar calibration.

series of reflectivity computed from DSD measurements using T-Matrix scattering computations.

2.2 Differential Reflectivity Calculation
A commonly employed method for determining the system bias in Zdr is the "bird bath" scan in which the
radar is oriented vertically in rain and rotated through a full 360o in azimuth. The measured Zdr values are
then averaged across the entire 360o rotation to eliminate azimuthal dependencies. Therefore, any
remaining biases would primarily arise from differences between the horizontal (H-pol) and vertical

 5

(V-pol) channels of the radar. Deviations from 0 dB in the measured Zdr indicate the presence of bias, as
detailed in [2]. Note that this technique is predominantly applied in radars configured for vertical pointing
scans. However, the XSAIL radar was not configured for such scan type, so an alternative approach is
employed in this analysis. The bias can be estimated by evaluating radar measurements of Zdr under
conditions where the Zdr estimate should inherently be near zero or can be taken to be near zero.
[1, 2] These conditions include two types of hydrometeors: light rain (drizzle) and dry snow. Since
raindrops in drizzle are spherical, at low incident angles, the intrinsic Zdr should theoretically be 0 dB, as
the horizontal and vertical polarization returns would be the same. The same holds for dry snow
aggregates. The calibration process begins by identifying regions of dry snow (for winter-time
precipitation) or drizzle (for summer-time precipitation) from the lowest PPI scan, which is not
significantly blocked by the surrounding terrain, focusing on areas with correlation coefficient (ρhv)
exceeding 0.99 and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) surpassing 10 dB. The radar range is fixed from 5 km to
35 km. Furthermore, within these regions, Z values ranging from 0 to 30 dBZ are selected. All these
together ensure that we are selecting data from strong radar returns in dry snow or drizzle. In rain events,
care should be taken to avoid contamination from the melting layer, which can be achieved by restricting
data below environmental freezing height. Subsequently, a scatter density plot consisting of Zdr and Z
samples can be constructed. Ideally, when Z approaches 0 dBZ, the Zdr also should be 0 dB. Any
deviation from that can be taken as the Zdr bias. Another approach is to construct histograms of Zdr
samples in dry snow or drizzle. The histogram should exhibit a Gaussian-shaped distribution centered on
0 db. Any deviation of the distribution mean from 0 db is indicative of the bias.
