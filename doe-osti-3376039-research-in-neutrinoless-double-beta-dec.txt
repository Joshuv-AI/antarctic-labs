Abstract. The M AJORANA D EMONSTRATOR recently concluded its search for neutrinoless double-beta decay. This technical
report covers the contributions of the PI Tedeschi for the data production including the development of a run database and imple-
mentation of the file blinding scheme, as well as the analysis of the cosmic ray intensity measured by the detector veto panels.
The experiment operated an array of up to 40.4 kg of germanium detectors, 27 kg of which were isotopically enriched in 76 Ge and
housed inside a compact shield consisting of lead and copper at the Sanford Underground Research Facility (SURF) in Lead, SD.
The experiment achieved a world leading energy resolution of 0.12% FWHM at 2039 keV, one of the lowest background rates in
the region of the 0νβ β Q-value, 15.7 +1.4 25
 −1.3 cnts/(FWHM t y), and set a 0νβ β half-life limit of T1/2 > 8.3×10 yrs based on its full
active exposure of 65 kg yr, resulting in limits on the effective neutrino mass of mβ β < 113-269 meV.

 INTRODUCTION

In two-neutrino double-beta decay (2νβ β ), the nucleus emits 2 β particles and 2 ν e conserving lepton number. It
is an allowed second-order weak process that occurs in nature, although its rate is extremely low. Half-lives for this
decay mode have been measured at ∼1019 years or longer in several nuclei. [1] Neutrinoless double-beta decay,
0νβ β , where only the 2 β particles are emitted with no neutrino, violates lepton number conservation and hence
requires physics beyond the Standard Model. One can visualize 0νβ β as an exchange of a virtual neutrino between
two neutrons within the nucleus. In the framework of the SUL (2)× U(1) Standard Model of weak interactions, the
first neutron emits a right-handed anti-neutrino. However, the second neutron requires the absorption of a left-handed
neutrino. In order for this to happen, the neutrino must have mass so that it is not in a pure helicity state and the
neutrino and anti-neutrino have to be indistinguishable. That is, the neutrino would have to be its own antiparticle, as
suggested by Majorana. [2]
 The decay rate for 0νβ β can be calculated assuming the exchange of Majorana neutrinos interacting with left
handed V-A weak currents. It is given as

 THE MAJORANA DEMONSTRATOR

The M AJORANA D EMONSTRATOR (MJD) was developed as a critical test of experimental techniques used in the
search for neutrinoless double beta decay in germanium via the reaction 76 Ge→76 Se+e− +e− . High purity germanium
crystals (HPGe) were enriched to a high isotopic fraction of 76 Ge and placed within a graded shield on the 4850 level
of the Sanford Underground Research Facility as shown in Figure 1. Special attention was paid to the radiopurity
of all experimental parts to keep the background signal as low as possible. For example, the cryostats and structural
components were fabricated from ultralow background copper that was electroformed underground. The cryostats
were surrounded by a lead shield and scintillating panels acting as a veto for cosmic muons. The shield volume was
surrounded by 25 cm of polyethelyne to moderate neutrons and the entire volume was purged with nitrogen gas to
reduce the radon contribution.
 The D EMONSTRATOR accumulated 80 TB of data resulting in 65 kg-yrs of active exposure during the course of
operations from 2015-2021. The HPGe detector waveforms were recorded by digitizers developed for the GRETINA
experiment, with a sampling frequency of 100 MHz and 14 bits of resolution. Detector calibration was performed
weekly using 228 Th line sources inserted along calibration tracks that wrap around each cryostat. The exposure-
weighted average FWHM at 2039 keV across all PPC detectors, including broadening due to gain drift and energy
nonlinearities, is 2.52 ± 0.08 keV. [4, 5] The data were automatically transferred to the National Energy Research
Scientific Computing Center (NERSC) for a statistically blind analysis with restricted access to 75% of the data.
The digitized waveforms from the “open" 25% of the data were used to calculate event energies and pulse-shape
discrimination (PSD) parameters used to reject likely backgrounds.

 Figure 2 shows the final energy spectrum obtained, with the effect of data cuts visible. The rejection of multisite
events is achieved via the parameter “AvsE" which represents a comparison between the maximum amplitude (A) of
a waveform current pulse and the total energy (E) where multisite events have lower A for a given E than single-
site events. [6] Alpha particles impinging on the passivated surface experience increased charge trapping such that
this charge is collected slowly, increasing the slope of the falling tail of the waveforms relative to bulk events. The
“Delayed Charge Recovery" (DCR) parameter is a measure of this slope and is used to eliminate surface events. [7]
Finally, events with a partial charge deposition in the transition layer between the n-type surfaces and the detector
bulk experience energy degradation, and have waveforms with a slow-rise component. We developed the late charge
(LQ) parameter, which is the integral of uncollected charge after a waveform has reached 80% of its maximum value
to identify these events. The slow-rise component of these waveforms increases LQ, and we cut events that fall 5σ
above the center of the parameter distribution. [8]
 The spectrum is dominated by 2νβ β below Qβ β =2039 keV, and near Qβ β most events are removed by the various
cuts. Unblinding of data proceeded in a staged fashion with basic data quality assurance checks performed at each
stage. No changes to analysis parameters or run selection were made after opening the energy window 1950 –
2350 keV which contains Qβ β (inset for Figure 2 denoted by dot-dash lines).

 The M AJORANA D EMONSTRATOR has completed it’s search for neutrinoless double beta decay where the p-
type point contact detectors provided excellent energy resolution. These detectors are now being combined with the
GERDA detector system [9] to form the LEGEND experiment with the eventual goal of 1 tonne of enriched material

covering the mass range predicted for the inverted ordering of neutrino masses. [10]

 COSMIC RAY MUON RATE

 Student Mentoring

The M AJORANA D EMONSTRATOR recorded underground muon signals for almost seven years in order to collect
enough data to measure the slant depth dependence at a depth of one mile underground. The long data-taking cycle
allowed time for undergraduate and new graduate students to learn about nuclear physics analysis techniques and
develop significant contributions to the project. B. McClain (2016-2017), an undergraduate computer science major

developed a recursive "shooting-method" algorithm to determine the depth of rock sampled by each muon hodoscope
using a topological map of the land surrounding SURF. B. McClain now works for a software firm. While an under-
graduate (2017-2019), B. Ranson completed checks of the D EMONSTRATOR geometry ensuring that the Monte Carlo
simulation dimensions were as close to the as-built dimensions as possible. During this effort, B. Ranson created the
correct rotation and translation of coordinates that connected the position and orientation of the M AJORANA D EMON -
STRATOR to the Yates Shaft and subsequently to the surface coordinates used in the slant-depth determination. The
full Monte Carlo simulation and analysis was performed by F. Adams as a M.S. project (2021-2023). Much of this
work was done remotely due to the pandemic. F. Adams developed scripts to runs the hundreds of millions of events
using the NERSC Cori supercomputing system. He analyzed both the real and MC data, and developed a novel ap-
proach to calculating the muon detection efficiency for the slant-depth distribution. USC undergraduate C. Fairchild
(2022-2023) analyzed scintillator hit combinations involving the veto side-panels with the top, bottom, and opposite
side panels. The results indicated that the statistics were too low, or coincident panels too close, to remove background
effectively, thus are not included in the work. These investigations were an important part of the exploration of hits
and the effectiveness of the muon coincidence requirements. Finally, as a beginning Ph.D. graduate student, A. Boldy
(2024) was introduced to the CERN Root analysis suite to perform systematic checks of the MJD data series to rule
out any potential bias in our data selection and cleaning processes.

 Cosmic Ray Intensity

The cosmic muon rate is a well known quantity, [11] thus its measurement serves as a validation of the understand-
ing of the mile-deep overburden of material at SURF and the operation of the M AJORANA D EMONSTRATOR veto-
shield detectors. While the cosmic ray cross section on Earth’s surface is described by a cos2 θ distribution, for
underground experiments the muon intensity decreases exponentially with the amount of rock traversed - known as
slant-depth. Slant-depths are measured in kilometers of water equivalent depth (km w.e.) for comparison of sites
with different depths and rock overburdens. The M AJORANA Collaboration published a total muon flux rate of
(5.31 ± 0.17) × 10−9 µ/s/cm2 [12] consistent with expectations and a previous measurement in the Davis Cavern,
verifying the average density of the rock overburden as 2.86±0.11g/cm3 . [13, 14] Data collection has continued
since the time of this initial measurement, and the increased statistics and additional analysis allow for a fully differ-
ential measurement by using combinations of scintillating veto detectors as muon hodoscopes, thus allowing direct
comparison to underground cosmic-muon measurements worldwide. The muon intensity as a function of slant depth
for underground labs is shown in the left panel of Figure 3. The right hand panel shows the measurements from SURF
at different slant depths, including the integrated value of the current work (solid circle) described in this report. The
horizontal bar on the MJD data point represents the range of slant depths in the data set, and the muon intensity as a
function of slant depth is covered in detail below.

 Run Database and File Blinding

The M AJORANA D EMONSTRATOR generated approximately 80,000 data files for detector calibration, commission-
ing, and physics data runs. The large number of data files presented a challenge, since keeping track of run properties
was difficult with traditional capabilities of computer operating system directories. Instead, a combination of Unix
bash, Python, and C++ applications initiated data transfers, executed analysis programs, and stored the processing
status in a CouchDB database. The database provided a method to categorize and access the runs in terms of many
attributes such as run type (calibration, test, background data), run quality and access (open, blind) and to create run
lists for analysis. The ability to store and display a wide variety of objects in the database — such as text log files
and images as attachments within individual run records — allowed the database to be used by the collaboration for
daily operational checks as well as archiving the run information. Storing processing logs in the database allowed for
quality checking of mass-reprocessing tasks.
 The D EMONSTRATOR run database implementation allowed access to the database in three ways: via a web
browser, via operating system scripts, and through C++ analysis program utilities. Collaboration members viewed
the data file status through a web browser on a daily basis to keep track of file transfers, data processing, and data
quality. Additionally, the database was used by automated programs (cron-jobs) that query the database via operating
system level scripts. For example, automated production scripts checked the database for files that needed processing
and updated the file status and quality fields when the analysis programs completed.
 The run database was used to account for the β β (0ν)-decay runs that were covered by the statistical blind analysis
approach adopted by M AJORANA [17]. The M AJORANA D EMONSTRATOR blindness scheme called for cycles of
31 hours of open data followed by a period of 93 hours of blinded data to ensure a 25% open/blind ratio. File access
control lists were used to limit the analysis to the small subset of “open" data files using the logic shown in Fig. 5.
When a raw file arrived on the NERSC disks, its access was changed from undefined to closed. Only a very restricted
set of users had access to these files. During production the raw data files were processed and analysis parameters,
such as energy, were calculated. During production of the data, the access value of the run files was changed to either
“to_be_blinded" or “to_be_opened" based on the run data type provided by ORCA. If the run data type corresponded
to a β β (0ν)-decay run, then it was marked as “to_be_blinded" while all other data types were set to “to_be_opened".
 Files to be “blinded" were set to either “blind" or “to_be_opened" according to the 31/93 (open/blind) ratio, and
“to_be_opened" raw files had their permissions changed to read access and their access value set to “open".
 Open data were used by the collaboration to check the health of the system and develop analysis cuts. These cuts
were then applied in a staged approach to the blinded data during the unblinding process used for published results.
