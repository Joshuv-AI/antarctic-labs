In Situ Copolymerized Zwitterionic-Oligoether Gel Electrolytes: Balancing Li Transport versus Li-Mediated Cross-Links
Authors: Sajal Arwish, Monika Schönhoff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gel polymer electrolytes (GPEs) are promising candidates for the improvement of safety and electrochemical performance of lithium-ion batteries; however, achieving a simultaneous balance between high ionic conductivity and mechanical robustness remains a major challenge. Here, we report GPEs based on Li salt in dimethylformamide (DMF) formed via in situ copolymerization of 2-methacryloyloxyethyl phosphorylcholine (MPC) and poly­(ethylene glycol) methyl ether methacrylate (PEGMA), designed to balance ion transport and structural integrity through different functional units. Ethylene oxide (EO) segments facilitate Li+ coordination and transport, while zwitterionic MPC units introduce physical cross-links, which enhance mechanical strength and favor salt dissociation. By systematically varying the salt concentration (discussed as the EO/Li ratio) in poly­(MPC-co-PEGMA) GPEs, we observe a transition from a transport-optimized to a coordination-dominated regime. Increasing salt content strengthens Li+–polymer interactions due to competition for coordination sites, leading to reduced ionic diffusivity but an increased apparent Li+ transference number (up to ∼0.42 at EO/Li ≈ 3–2.5). Ionic conductivity exhibits a maximum (∼3.2 mS cm–1 at EO/Li ≈ 9), reflecting an optimal balance between charge carrier density and mobility. Raman spectroscopy indicates MPC-induced reduced Li-anion coordination, which favorably contributes to enhanced charge carrier density. Investigating several compositional series, we identify a compositional regime combining optimized Li transport and Li-mediated cross-linking for mechanical strength. At high salt loadings, further increasing MPC content elevates the gel modulus via enhanced cross-link density while suppressing Li-anion aggregation, suggesting a dual role of MPC in mechanical reinforcement and ion dissociation. These findings highlight the importance of balancing ion coordination and zwitterionic cross-linking in designing mechanically robust, yet highly conductive gel polymer electrolytes for advanced lithium battery systems.

Introduction

In recent years, there has been an urgent demand for sustainable energy storage, and the rapid transition toward e-mobility has significantly intensified the need for safer, efficient, high energy density storage systems. Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) currently play a dominant role in this field due to their high energy density, long cycle life, and widespread application in portable electronics as well as the rapidly growing electric vehicle market. Despite substantial technological progress, conventional liquid electrolytes used in LIBs still pose serious safety concerns, including leakage, flammability, and potential thermal runaway. − To overcome these challenges, polymer electrolytes (PEs) have emerged as promising alternatives to traditional liquid electrolytes because of their improved safety, good processability, mechanical stability, and potential compatibility with high energy battery systems. The earliest report in the field of polymer electrolytes dated back to 1973, when Fenton and co-workers demonstrated that alkali metal salts can be complexed with poly­(ethylene oxide) (PEO) to form ionically conductive polymer matrices. Since then, polymer electrolytes have been extensively investigated for solid-state lithium batteries.

Among different classes of polymer electrolytes, gel polymer electrolytes (GPEs) have gained particular interest, since the benefits of liquid and solid electrolytes can be combined. Specifically, they exhibit liquid-like ionic conductivity while keeping mechanical robustness and safety characteristics of solid materials. , In general, GPEs consist of a polymer matrix swollen with liquid organic solvents and lithium salts and were first reported by Feuillade and Perche.

Traditionally, poly­(ethylene oxide) (PEO) and its derivatives have been the most extensively studied polymer hosts for lithium-ion conduction owing to the strong solvating ability of ether oxygen atoms toward Li+ ions. − However, PEO-based polymer electrolytes still suffer from relatively low lithium transference number and low ionic conductivity at room temperature. These limitations primarily arise from ion pairing and the coupled motion of Li+ ions with the polymer chains. − To overcome these limitations, several strategies have been explored, including copolymerization with flexible segments, incorporation of fillers, and blending with low-molecular-weight plasticizers.

As an alternative strategy to mitigate strong Li+ and anion interactions and improve ion transport, the incorporation of zwitterionic (ZI) components has recently emerged as an effective approach. Zwitterionic polymers have attracted increasing interest because they can form three-dimensional solid frameworks within electrolyte systems while maintaining reasonable ionic conductivity. The zwitterionic functional groups present along the polymer backbone can significantly influence Li+ transport behavior. Depending on the specific chemical structure of the zwitterionic moieties, these groups may interact strongly with Li+ ions, which can either facilitate or hinder their mobility. Among various polyzwitterionic materials investigated, poly­(2-methacryloyloxyethyl phosphorylcholine) (p­(MPC)) has demonstrated particularly favorable compatibility with lithium salt systems. In p­(MPC)-based electrolytes, Li+ ions can perform dual roles: they act as charge carriers responsible for lithium-ion conduction while simultaneously serving as physical cross-linking agents that bridge adjacent p­(MPC) chains, thereby forming self-supporting gel electrolytes (ionogels). However, the use of p­(MPC) as the only polymer host can restrict effective Li+ mobility due to strong interactions between Li+ ions and the zwitterionic groups. Therefore, diluting the zwitterionic units with other coordinating polymer segments may be a promising approach. In this context, ethylene oxides as strongly coordinating segments can effectively compete with p­(MPC) for Li+ coordination. Consequently, investigating copolymerized PEO–p(MPC) systems is of significant interest, as such architectures may balance mechanical robustness with enhanced ionic conductivity through rational material design.

Besides the polymer host, the choice of solvent also plays a crucial role in the ionic transport and electrochemical stability of gel polymer electrolytes. Among the various cosolvents investigated as plasticizers, N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) offers several advantageous properties, including high polarity, low viscosity, high boiling point, high relative permittivity, and moderate binding energy with cations. , These characteristics make DMF a promising solvent for promoting an efficient ion dissociation and transport. However, it has also been reported that DMF can exhibit instability toward lithium–metal anodes, resulting in poor cyclic stability. Recent studies have explored strategies to mitigate these limitations. For example, Zhang et al. demonstrated that DMF-based electrolytes combined with appropriate cosolvents can generate stable localized interfacial environments that suppress side reactions at the lithium metal anode and improve cycling stability. Similarly, Womble et al. reported that increasing the salt-to-solvent ratio in DMF-based electrolytes significantly improves cyclic lifetimes and gravimetric capacities. These studies highlight the continued relevance of DMF as a solvent when it is combined with appropriate electrolyte design strategies.

Building on our previous work demonstrating Li+-ion-induced cross-linking in MPC-based ionogels, the present study systematically investigates photoinitiated in situ polymerized poly­(MPC-co-PEGMA) gel electrolytes as a new class of ion-conducting materials. Here, we examined the influence of varying lithium salt concentration and MPC fraction on ionic transport and mechanical robustness of the gels. To elucidate the structure–transport relationships in these materials, ionic conductivity measurements and pulsed-field-gradient (PFG) NMR diffusion experiments are employed to probe overall charge transport and individual ionic species mobility. These measurements are complemented by Raman spectroscopy to analyze anion coordination, rheological characterization of the gel network, and NMR titration studies to examine ion–polymer interactions. From these results, we have analyzed the coordination behavior and mobility of ions to optimize the material design in terms of optimized ion transport combined with mechanical strength.

Experimental Section

Materials

Lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)­imide (LiTFSI, 99.95% trace metals basis, Sigma-Aldrich) was dried at 100 °C and 10–6 hPa overnight, using a turbomolecular pump system (HiCube 30 Eco DN 40 ISO-KF; Pfeiffer, Aβlar, Germany). Dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich) was dried over molecular sieves, and both were subsequently stored in an argon-filled glovebox (GS Glovebox Systemtechnik). 2-Hydroxy-2-methylpropiophenone (HMPP, 97%, Sigma-Aldrich), 2-methacryloyloxyethyl phosphorylcholine (MPC, 97%, Sigma-Aldrich), and poly­(ethylene glycol) methyl ether methacrylate (PEGMA, average M n 500, with 900 ppm MEHQ as an inhibitor, Sigma-Aldrich), were used as received and stored in a refrigerator.

Copolymer Gel Preparation

In an argon-filled glovebox, first mPEGMA was added to a glass vial, containing LiTFSI and the cosolvent (DMF) to form a solution containing salt and monomer in the desired ratios. Generally, the cosolvent (CS) to ethylene oxide (EO) ratio was kept constant at EO/CS = 1, while other compositional parameters were varied. The resulting mixture was stirred at 25 °C until it became homogeneous. For in situ copolymerization, the MPC monomer was added and allowed to stir at 80 rpm for 6–7 h. The photoinitiator (HMPP) was then added to the solutions (1% w/w with respect to the total monomers amount). The HMPP-containing precursor solutions were then stirred at 80 rpm for 30–40 s. NMR Quartz glass tubes (Wilmad) were then filled with the precursor solutions for Raman and PFG-NMR experiments. They were then exposed to a custom-built UV/vis irradiation box, which contained two UV LEDs (RSW-P03-365-0 Roschwege GmbH, Greifenstein, Germany; nominal wavelength 365 nm, 3 W each) and two further LEDs (WEPRB3-S1, Winger Electronics GmbH & Co. KG, Dessau-Roßlau, Germany; 440–450 nm) for irradiation-induced in situ polymerization. Reference gels without MPC were prepared following the same protocol as described above except without the addition of the MPC monomer. Rheology

An MCR 101 rheometer (Anton Paar) was used for rheological measurements, which was equipped with a Peltier element for temperature control and a PP-25 plate plane geometry with a diameter of 25 mm. The gap was lowered until a normal force ∼5N was reached, ensuring a suitable contact to the sample. In addition, prior to measurement, samples were left to relax completely for approximately 10 min. Small angle oscillatory shear experiments were conducted with the shear strain amplitude kept within the linear viscoelastic region at 5%. Frequency sweeps were performed successively at different temperatures ranging from 25 to 115 °C in order to allow an expansion of the frequency range by time–temperature superposition (TTS).

Conductivity

Conductivity measurements were performed at room temperature in a setup consisting of a closed sample cell (TSC70) which is mounted on a Microcell HC measuring stand (both rhd instruments, Darmstadt, Germany). The gel precursor was placed inside the cells within the glovebox and then exposed to the UV Lamp as described above. The cell constant was calibrated by using a KCl solution standard with a known conductivity of 1.413 mS/cm at 25 °C (Mettler-Toledo GmbH, Lienen, Germany). The temperature during impedance measurements was controlled using the integrated Eurotherm temperature control unit of the measurement system. The desired measurement temperature was set and regulated through the temperature controller module in the WinDETA software. Samples were allowed to equilibrate for 900 s at each temperature prior to data acquisition to ensure thermal stability throughout the measurement. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed by an Alpha-A Frequency Analyzer (Novocontrol, Montabaur, Germany) in a frequency range from 1 Hz to 100 MHz using an AC voltage of 10 mV. The resistance R value was extracted at the intercept (x-axis) in a Nyquist plot, and conductivity values were calculated by using formula σ = k/R. The conductivity results were reported as the average of three replicate measurements on two different batches for each composition.

NMR Spectroscopy and Pulsed-Field Gradient NMR

NMR spectra and diffusion experiments were performed on an AVANCE Neo 400 MHz, an AVANCE Neo 300 MHz, or an AVANCE III HD 400 MHz NMR spectrometer (all from Bruker, Rheinstetten, Germany), equipped with gradient amplifiers, using either a gradient probe head with selective radiofrequency inserts for 1H or 19F (“Diff30” with a maximum gradient strength of 12 T/m, Bruker) or a broad band diffusion probe head (DIFF BBO, Bruker) with a maximum gradient strength of 17 T/m. For measurements at room temperature (25 °C), the temperature was controlled by an air stream and calibrated with a Pt100 thermocouple (Greisinger electronics). The self-diffusion coefficients of each nucleus under study were measured by PFG-NMR in a stimulated echo pulse sequence, recording a series of spectra with gradient strength, g, as variable parameter. The diffusion coefficients D for a distinct resonance were obtained from the decay of the signal intensity I with g, employing the Stejskal–Tanner equation. 1 I=I0exp(−γ2δ2g2D(Δ−δ3))

Here, γ represents the gyromagnetic ratio of the observed nucleus and I 0 the signal intensity at g = 0. The effective gradient pulse length duration δ was set to a constant value between 1 and 2 ms with g being incremented in subsequent spectra up to a maximum of 1 T/m. The observation time Δ was set to 50 to 200 ms, depending on the nature of the specific sample and the observed nucleus. The diffusion coefficients are obtained as the average of three different batches for each composition. Errors are given as the standard deviation plus a deviation of 5% accounting for potential systematic errors.

For NMR titrations, a 200 mM solution of LiTFSI in DMF was prepared to which varying amounts of MPC or PEGMA were added to estimate the binding strength of the two polymer hosts to Li ions. In single-pulse experiments, the 7Li chemical shift was observed at room temperature (25 °C) by using an external reference of 1 M LiCl in D2O.

Raman Spectroscopy

The same samples employed for PFG-NMR were also used to record the Raman spectra on a spectrometer (MultiRAM, Bruker, Rheinstetten), which was equipped with a laser of 263 mW and a wavelength of 1064 nm and operated at ambient temperature. The Raman spectra were recorded in the wavenumber range of 30–3600 cm–1 with a resolution of 0.5 cm–1. The obtained spectra were then baseline corrected and normalized to the integral of the TFSI Raman band region from 700 to 800 cm–1. Voigt profiles (60% Lorentzian and 40% Gaussian) were employed for spectral decomposition, using OPUS software (Bruker, Rheinstetten). Free parameters included the peak intensity, width, and peak position for two deconvoluted peaks at ∼742 cm–1 and 748 cm–1 for free and Li-coordinated TFSI, respectively. The results were averaged over three different batches for each composition.

Li-Ion Binding Affinity

It is interesting to take the relative coordination strength of Li to either of the polymers (MPC, PEGMA), respectively, into account. Therefore, we investigated Li-ion binding through NMR titration experiments. The 7Li chemical shift in LiTFSI-DMF solution is determined upon addition of specific amounts of the coordinating polymer, see Figure . A 1M LiCl solution in D2O serves as external reference. Since the chemical shift indicates the electron shielding effect of specific nuclei, a shift toward lower magnitude is indication of strong interaction of Li ions with the coordinating groups in the polymer host. PEO is well-known to strongly coordinate with Li ions through chelation by its ether oxygen atoms. However, it is interesting to see that the phosphorylcholine group O­(P) in MPC also strongly associates with Li+ ions. In Situ Copolymerized MPC-PEGMA Ionogels: Variation of Li Salt Fraction

Diffusion Coefficients

A first series of copolymerized ionogels is prepared with a broadly varying EO to Li molar ratio ranging from 52 to 2.5 (r = 0.019 to 0.4), while the ratios of EO/CS = 1, EO/MPC = 106.2, and MPC/CS = 0.0094 are kept constant (see green squares in Scheme and compositions written in green color in Table S1). The salt content is varied in this series to observe the effect of low to high salt concentration on diffusion and transport properties in copolymerized gels. PFG-NMR experiments on 1H, 19F, and 7Li nuclei yield the diffusion coefficients of Li, anions, and DMF, respectively, see Figure a. There, with increasing salt content, the diffusion coefficients of all nuclei are decreasing in copolymerized gels. It is a typical behavior observed in many salt-in-polymer systems that an increase in Li-ion concentration results in reduced segmental motion, enhanced viscosity, and formation of large ion clusters, leading to lower diffusion coefficient values of all ionic constituents. − At low salt content, i.e., EO/Li = 52, the diffusion coefficients of Li, TFSI anions, and cosolvent are differing significantly. However, with increasing salt content (from around r = 0.15, i.e., EO/Li = 6), the diffusion of Li nuclei almost approaches that of TFSI. In the high salt region, the diffusion coefficients of Li and TFSI ions are very similar (D Li ≈ D TFSI), as Li in this regime is not sufficiently coordinated by EO units, and the system is considered dominated by Li-TFSI interactions.

To analyze the influence of MPC in copolymerized gels, some of the compositions are prepared as reference gels without MPC, again varying the ratio of EO/Li, while keeping the ratios of EO/CS = 1 and MPC/CS = 0 (see dark blue circles in Scheme and compositions written in blue color in Table S1). It can be seen (open data points in Figure a) that the diffusion coefficients of each nucleus (1H, 19F, and 7Li) are decreasing with an increasing salt content in PEGMA homopolymer gels, similar to the copolymer gels. Comparing the compositions with and without MPC, Figure a shows differences only at low Li concentration (EO/Li = 52). Here, the diffusion coefficients of all species in gels without MPC (open data points) are significantly higher than in the gel with MPC (full data points). Furthermore, in this regime, the diffusion coefficient of Li is far more affected by the presence of MPC than those of the anion and solvent. Quantitatively, for gels with EO/Li = 52, the diffusion coefficient of Li decreases by about 60%, whereas the anion and solvent decrease by only 34% and 24%, respectively. In comparison to the PEGDA homopolymer, the MPC-containing gels have a higher total polymer content, which is expected to contribute to an overall reduction in diffusion. However, with the addition of 0.5 MPC groups to 52 EO units per Li+ ion, the total polymer content - and consequently the total concentration of coordination sites - is not significantly increased. Therefore, while the additional MPC content may promote a minimal viscosity enhancement, the drastic reduction of Li diffusivity indicates the formation of strong Li-MPC coordination. It is remarkable that such a strong MPC coordination of Li is found in spite of a large excess of EO groups over MPC (MPC/Li = 0.5, EO/Li = 52). However, a strong MPC-Li coordination is consistent with our previous study of pMPC homopolymer gels, which showed that an MPC/Li ratio of 0.5 marks the onset of Li+-ion-mediated cross-linking of pMPC homopolymers, connected with a decrease in the fraction of free Li ions. In conclusion, at low salt content, Li ions are capable of acting as cross-linkers between MPC groups even in the presence of competing oligoether segments.

On the other hand, in the high salt concentration regime (EO/Li < 6) (see Figure a), there is no strongly pronounced MPC-induced change in diffusion coefficients. For example, at EO/Li = 5 (r = 0.19), the diffusivities decrease only by 14%, 9%, and 4% for Li, anions, and solvent, respectively, as compared to reference gels. At these low EO/Li ratios, the number of ether oxygen groups available per Li ion is close to or even below the typical Li coordination number, which is ∼4–6, such that TFSI can be expected to contribute to the Li coordination shell. Moreover, the number of MPC groups per Li ion is very small in these compositions (≈0.05), which is insufficient to establish an effective cross-linking network.

Thus, while MPC strongly reduces diffusion at low salt concentration through the formation of Li-mediated cross-links, at high salt concentration, the small amount of MPC present cannot significantly alter ion transport. These observations indicate that the main factor reducing diffusion in the MPC-containing systems is the formation of Li-mediated cross-links due to strong Li-MPC coordination interactions.

The Nernst–Einstein equation describes an ideal electrolyte and is generally not valid in the case of concentrated electrolytes, as these are subject to ion–ion correlations. Therefore, determining transference numbers from self-diffusion coefficients via PFG-NMR is an approximation under the assumption that ion correlations are negligible. Figure b shows the Li apparent transference number for both gel series. It is worthwhile to note that it increases with the decreasing ratio of EO to Li. The highest value observed is ∼0.42 for compositions labeled CP-12 and CP-13. Similarly, for the gels without MPC (open blue stars), the apparent Li transference number is also increasing with increasing salt concentration. Again, as already observed in the diffusion data, there is a significant difference in Li transference number for composition EO/Li = 52 for gels without and with MPC (CP6R and CP6, respectively). This again highlights the influence of additional MPC groups (0.5 per Li), which strongly coordinate Li and form cross-links, thus reducing the transference number, since a fraction of the Li+ ions are immobilized, acting as cross-links. In comparison, at high salt content, the difference in t Li * is not very pronounced. Apparently, the low fraction of MPC (less than 6% with respect to Li) is not sufficient for inducing cross-links from Li ions coordinated between different zwitterionic units. In connection with Li being undercoordinated by EO groups, most probably, a mixed coordination environment of Li persists in this regime. In the next section, we will shed light on Li coordination by Raman spectroscopy of the anion.

Li-Anion Coordination

Raman spectra are recorded to observe any changes in Li-TFSI coordination behavior. For quantification of the fractions of free and Li+-coordinated anions, Raman spectra were deconvoluted by Voigt profiles (see Figure S2). From the integrals of the Raman bands of free TFSI– species (A f) and coordinated TFSI– species (A c), the integral ratio A c/(A c + A f) is determined. Provided that both species have the same Raman scattering coefficient, this integral ratio reflects the fraction of Li-coordinated anions. Previous studies have demonstrated the validity of this condition for TFSI ions in an ionic liquid environment, , leading to a widespread application of this method in literature. − In the present work, we investigate the TFSI anion in an organic solvent, which should modify the Raman scattering coefficients, since it is known to depend on the polarizability of the solvent. Here, we apply the above procedure under the assumption that the decrease of the Raman scattering coefficient in DMF as compared to ionic liquid is equal for both Raman bands investigated. The integral ratio is plotted in Figure a for copolymerized ionogels with a varying EO to Li molar ratio ranging from 52 to 2.5 while keeping the ratio of EO/CS = 1 constant. For clarity, the blue shaded area in Figure represents the low salt content regime, defined by the possibility of full Li+-ion coordination by ether oxygens, i.e., EO/Li > 6. At the lowest salt concentration, i.e., EO/Li = 52, the fraction of coordinated TFSI is already small ≈0.08, which is consistent with the large excess of polymer coordination sites (52 ether oxygen atoms and about 0.5 MPC groups) available for Li solvation. Surprisingly, the coordinated TFSI fraction decreases further when the salt concentration is increased from EO/Li = 52 to 9, reaching a minimum of ∼0.02. Based purely on binding equilibria, an increase in salt concentration would normally promote Li-TFSI coordination due to the reduced number of EO units available per Li. The opposite trend observed here therefore suggests a restructuring of the Li coordination environment.

To fully understand this behavior, the coordinated fraction of TFSI is calculated for the reference gel series (without MPC; see open data points in Figure a). For the composition EO/Li = 52, the coordinated TFSI fraction is ∼0.04 for the gel without MPC, only half the value as in the gel with MPC. Surprisingly, MPC addition does not seem to replace TFSI in the Li coordination shell. Instead, MPC seems to promote TFSI coordination to Li. For an explanation, we refer to our previous study on MPC homopolymer-based ionogels, where we found that at MPC/Li+ = 0.5, the TFSI ions are still involved in Li coordination, which results in a heterogeneous coordination environment (see Figure b), where Li+ ions are shared by MPC and anions in a mixed coordination. In summary, considering that fully oligoether-coordinated Li+ ions do not require coordination by anions, the presence of Li-based MPC-cross-links apparently promotes the occurrence of Li-coordinated anions, increasing the population of coordinated TFSI at low salt content. Besides, this low salt regime agrees to the regime where cross-linking dominates transport, as discussed in the context of the diffusion results (Figure a). In conclusion, these results imply that Li-induced cross-linking does not necessarily require that TFSI leaves the Li coordination shell completely (see Figure b). Now, the decreasing trend of coordinated TFSI fraction in the low salt regime can be explained from counting MPC coordination sites per Li. The number of MPC per Li decreases from 0.5 (EO/Li = 52) to 0.25 (EO/Li = 26). This decrease also decreases the probability of finding TFSI anions in a mixed coordination, ultimately resulting in a decrease of the coordinated TFSI fraction. This decrease continues down to EO/Li = 9 with MPC/Li = 0.08.

On the other hand, in the high salt regime, i.e., below EO/Li = 9 (unshaded area in Figure ), the coordinated TFSI fraction is again larger, with a sharp rise between r ∼0.12 and 0.15. At larger r, oligoether coordination is not sufficient to saturate Li ions. For example, N polymer, defined as the total number of EO and MPC units per Li, reaches ≈5.05 (5 EO units and 0.05 MPC units) for the concentrated gel with an EO/Li ratio of 5. As the number of EO and MPC units per Li+ decreases, Li+ ions cannot be fully solvated by polymer chains alone, which increases the probability of transient Li+-TFSI– coordination and leads to the sharp increase of the coordinated TFSI fraction. In addition, while DMF might additionally play a role in Li coordination, from the sharp rise of coordinated TFSI, it can be concluded that Li-EO is the dominant coordination, only limited by the availability of oligoethers in the unshaded regime. Additional indirect evidence for enhanced ion association at a high salt concentration regime can be seen in diffusion data (Figure a), as TFSI and Li diffusion coefficient start approaching each other toward the concentrated salt region, consistent with increased correlated motion between cations and anions.

Ionic Conductivity

The ionic conductivities of the copolymerized ionogel series are measured at room temperature (25 °C) and are depicted in Figure . The increase in the salt content enhances the ionic conductivity, until at EO/Li = 9, a maximum is reached, followed by a decrease in dependence on conducting salt concentration. This is a common feature in many electrolyte materials. − Generally, at low salt concentration, the number of charge carriers increases with added salt, and hence, the conductivity increases. At a higher salt concentration, the decrease in the conductivity reflects a viscosity enhancement, i.e., reduction of the mobility of charge carriers, since salt acts as a weak cross-linker and vibrational spectroscopic studies have shown the formation of the ionic clusters, pairs, or aggregates at very high salt concentrations. − The conductivity results are in good agreement with the diffusion data (Figure a), as at high salt (EO/Li = 6, 5, or 3) the diffusion coefficients of Li and F nuclei become similar, indicating the existence of ionic pairs.

Room-temperature ionic conductivities for poly­(MPC-co-PEGMA) gels with varying Li salt concentration (see green squares in Scheme and compositions written in green color in Table S1).

It should be noted that the maximum conductivity is observed at an EO/Li ratio = 9, while the highest Li apparent transference number occurs at an EO/Li ratio = 3–2.5. A figure of merit combining both parameters is given by the plot of σ·t Li *, as presented in Figure S3 in Supporting Information. Consequently, the gain in apparent transference number does not compensate for the loss in conductivity, and σtLi decreases beyond EO/Li ≈ 9.

Finally, from all of the above findings, it can be concluded that in copolymerized gels modulating the EO to Li ratio offers a high Li apparent transference number at high salt, and a high conductivity value of ∼3.2 mS cm–1 can be achieved in the medium salt range, around the saturation of Li by oligoether coordination. Importantly, the presence of a low fraction of MPC does not impair ion transport; nevertheless, the beneficial Li-mediated cross-links in the low salt regime result in a reduction of the apparent Li transference number. In the following, we systematically explore the role of MPC content in the copolymerized gel system.

Variation of MPC Content at Low Salt Fraction

In this series, the MPC fraction in the copolymer is varied while keeping both the EO/CS and EO/Li ratios constant. The MPC content is a relevant parameter, because our previous study has demonstrated that MPC plays a crucial role in the formation of noncovalent cross-links, which are responsible for the mechanical reinforcement of the gels. By adjusting the MPC/Li ratio, we aim to clarify how MPC-mediated interactions influence ion diffusion and transport properties. Two series with high and low salt content are selected: one highly diluted system (i.e., low salt, EO/Li = 52) and one concentrated gel (i.e., high salt, EO/Li = 3).

The diffusion and transport data for the copolymerized gels at constant EO/Li = 52 with varying MPC/Li ratios (red symbols in Scheme and compositions highlighted in red in Table S1) are presented in Figure S4, and a detailed discussion is provided in the Supporting Information. Overall, the results demonstrate that highly diluted copolymerized gels do not provide an optimal balance between the ion transport and mechanical integrity. The gels were mechanically very weak and showed a low transference number, especially at high MPC content. Through the combination of high MPC content and low salt concentration, which are both desired properties, Li transport as well as mechanical strength become impaired. An optimized lithium salt concentration is therefore essential to generate sufficient noncovalent cross-linking while simultaneously maintaining favorable lithium transport properties. Based on these findings, an electrolyte composition concentrated in Li salt is selected for further systematic variation of MPC content to evaluate its influence on diffusivity, conductivity, and mechanical strength.

Variation of MPC Content at High Salt Fraction

Realizing the relevance of Li cross-links, we now turn to copolymer gels with high salt content (EO/Li = 3). Note that this salt concentration is higher than the one at maximum conductivity, which is a purposeful choice in order to provide sufficient Li for both functionalities, i.e., a mobile charge carrier as well as a cross-linker. This choice also makes use of an increased transference number (Figure b). To this end, copolymerized ionogels are prepared with the varying comonomer content of MPC, resulting in an MPC to Li molar ratio ranging from 0 to 0.08. Again, the ratio EO/CS = 1 is kept constant (see purple symbols in Scheme and compositions written in purple color in Table S1). The room-temperature self-diffusion coefficients of each nucleus (1H, 19F, and 7Li) are displayed in Figure a. The values of all species decrease with an increasing content of MPC. The Li transference numbers are estimated from eq and are given in Figure b. In spite of a slight monotonic decrease with increasing p­(MPC) content, which signifies the Li+ interaction with the O­(P) unit of p­(MPC), the values remain relatively high in the range 0.41 to 0.38.

There is a strong decrease in the coordinated TFSI fraction with increasing MPC/Li ratio, which suggests that MPC’s phosphate oxygens coordinate Li+ strongly, competing with TFSI ion coordination. The corresponding room-temperature ionic conductivities are given in Figure b. From MPC/Li = 0.03 to 0.05, there is a slight initial increase in conductivity. This may be due to the fact that at this concentrated composition (EO/Li = 3) Li coordination still involves a small degree of ion pairing at low MPC content, which decreases with the increasing MPC to Li ratio, as seen in Figure a, thus enhancing conductivity. With a further increase of p­(MPC) content, there is a leveling off of the conductivity (Figure b), and it remains at a rather high level. In this regime, probably ionic cross-linking induced by strong binding of Li+ ions to p­(MPC) and reduced segmental motion of the polymer compensate for any further MPC-induced salt dissociation which could further increase the charge carrier density. Therefore, in this regime, both effects of MPC groups involving Li ions, i.e., liberation of Li-TFSI interactions and Li+-ion-mediated cross-link formation, are well-balanced. Interestingly, the conductivity reached here (2.5–3 mS cm–1) is on the same order as the maximum values found in the first series (Figure ), where the best conducting compositions were found with EO/Li = 9, however, at a lower comonomer fraction of MPC. It can be concluded from the above findings that a high MPC content, which supports the formation of mechanically stronger gels, can be afforded if it is combined with an optimized Li salt concentration. In such compositions as in this last series, a small fraction of Li is involved in cross-linking, while a sufficiently large fraction of free Li ions contributes to conductivity. Finally, rheology is performed on these salt-concentrated and high-MPC-content-containing gels to demonstrate their mechanical strength.

Gel Formation

Rheological experiments using small amplitude oscillatory shear were performed on the gel series concentrated in salt with a varying MPC to Li ratio (see purple symbols in Scheme and compositions written in purple color in Table S1) to observe the effect of MPC on the mechanical strength of the gels. In rheology, time–temperature superposition (TTS) is used to extend the range of viscoelastic data such as the dynamic modulus by shifting curves measured at different temperatures along the time or frequency axis. Since increasing temperature accelerates molecular motion, relaxation occurs faster and curves are shifted to shorter times (i.e., higher frequencies). Consequently, the temperature-dependent shift factor reflects the thermorheological behavior of the material and describes how temperature affects relaxation processes. Here, we performed T-dependent shear rheology and applied TTS to enhance the accuracy of determining the elastic modulus in the rubbery plateau in a master curve corresponding to room temperature.

Rheological spectra at room temperature are shown in Figure . Spectra at elevated temperatures were treated in the following way: By first applying a horizontal shift to the frequency scale a T = ω (T ref)/ω­(T) in order to account for temperature-driven changes in network relaxation dynamics and then a small vertical shift b T = G′ (T ref)/G′(T), that accounts for changes in the magnitude of the viscoelastic response. Physically, b T primarily reflects temperature-dependent changes of the entropic chain elasticity for any polymeric system. Specifically for dynamic networks, temperature-driven changes of the cross-link number density further affect the magnitude of the plateau modulus beyond the effect of temperature alone. In this way, the data could indeed be superimposed across temperatures, see Figure .

The values of both log a T and log b T used to construct the master curves are reported in Table S2 of the Supporting Information. Regarding the variation of log b T, the factor shows only modest changes with temperature and composition compared with the horizontal shift factor, indicating that temperature primarily affects the relaxation time scales rather than causing large changes in the modulus level. To further assess the origin of the vertical shift factor, we compared the experimentally determined log b T values with the theoretical entropic scaling expected from rubber elasticity, where the modulus is proportional to absolute temperature (G ∝ T). For example, at 55 °C, the experimentally determined value of b T (0.89, i.e., log (b T) = −0.05), is in excellent agreement with the corresponding entropic factor T ref/T (0.90). This close correspondence indicates that the temperature dependence of the storage modulus is largely governed by the expected entropic elasticity of the network, with negligible contributions from temperature-induced changes in network connectivity.

In the superimposed spectra, a rubbery plateau is clearly observed, even if it is not visible in the data at all individual temperatures. The incomplete superposition of the rheological data in the low-frequency regime suggests that the material does not exhibit thermorheologically simple behavior. Although satisfactory superposition is obtained for the storage modulus, the loss modulus does not superimpose well over the investigated temperature range. This observation suggests that there are two dissipative relaxation processes. One is controlled by chain dynamics, and the other is controlled by dissociation of the Li cross-links and is characterized by different activation energies and cannot be described by a single shift factor. Therefore, the material does not exhibit fully thermorheologically simple behavior.

The plateau modulus values G p ′ are extracted from the plateau regime at a temperature of 25 °C. The significant increase of the plateau modulus with the increasing molar ratio of MPC to Li (Figure ) indicates that p­(MPC) is indeed forming highly cross-linked networks, involving Li ions. As described further above, Li+ has a strong binding affinity with ZI units comparable to binding to PEGMA chains. Generally, the rubbery plateau modulus of a gel can be expressed as G p ′ = ρRT/M x , where ρ is the polymer density and M x the molecular weight of strands between cross-links. Since ρ/M x can be interpreted as the cross-link density, G p ′ is directly proportional to the cross-link density of the Li+-ion network. Therefore, gels with high plateau modulus values are also highly cross-linked. The enhanced cross-linking interpreted from the increase of the modulus in Figure corroborates the lowering of the average Li+ diffusivity observed with the increasing content of p­(MPC) (see Figure a). More Li+ cations are noncovalently coordinated with increasing p­(MPC), resulting in a decrease in the number of mobile Li+ cations and an increase in the Li+-ion-mediated cross-link density of the gels. Interestingly, the remarkable increase in modulus is not compromised by a loss in conductivity; therefore, these gel compositions provide a good compromise between ion mobility and mechanical strength, achieved by tailoring the relative content of MPC and Li salt in order to promote the dual role of Li as a cross-linker and simultaneously as a free charge carrier.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings reveal that the interplay of different component ratios (EO, Li, and MPC) within in situ copolymerized MPC-PEGMA gels can significantly contribute to a change in diffusivity, Li transport, conductivity, and mechanical strength of the gels. There are three main important aspects that we learned from our investigated system.

First, by examining some of the gels with and without MPC (see green and blue series in Scheme ), we infer that in the high salt regime the presence of MPC does not drastically change the diffusivity and Li transport. This has allowed us to design copolymerized gels with improved mechanical strength, based on the beneficial property of MPC to form cross-linked networks with Li ions. Second, the effect of the Li salt concentration (see green squares in Scheme ) in in situ copolymerized gels has shown that with increasing salt content the diffusivity of all nuclei decreases, reflecting the general electrolyte property of an ion mobility reduction with increasing salt. It is interesting to see, however, that the Li transport number increases with addition of the salt, reaching a maximum value of ∼0.42 for EO/Li ratios of 3 and 2.5, i.e., occurring beyond the conductivity maximum (∼3.2 mS cm–1) around EO/Li = 9.

At the end, with modulating the MPC content in a highly salt-concentrated gel (see purple symbols in Scheme ), we have observed a slight decrease in diffusivity with an increase in MPC content because of an increase in cross-link density of the gels. However, with enhanced mechanical strength due to Li-mediated cross-links, the conductivity remains high. Here, MPC plays an important role in breaking LiTFSI clusters, which enhances conductivity and can thus counterbalance the conductivity loss associated with Li-MPC cross-link formation. At high salt content, there is a sufficient Li-ion concentration, which allows both functionalities of Li+, i.e., contributing to the formation of noncovalent zwitterionic cross-links, as well as acting as a free charge carrier. Furthermore, after analyzing the results from rheology, a significant increase in modulus is observed for gels with a higher amount of MPC (MPC/Li = 0.08), demonstrating an increased cross-linking density of the gels. Thus, the copolymer gels with a high amount of Li salt such that enough Li ions are mobile and also available for cross-linking with MPC units result in highly conductive and simultaneously mechanically strengthened gel electrolytes.

The current study highlights the importance of the optimization and tuning of various components and their molar ratios within in situ synthesized copolymer gels. This approach of working along different compositional lines can help achieve an optimal balance among different parameters, mainly Li transport, conductivity, and mechanical strength.
