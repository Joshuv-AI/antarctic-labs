Executive Summary
Phase modifiers are low molecular weight molecules added to solvent extraction separations to
improve the physical performance of the separation system, for example by increasing the
concentration of metal ions that can be separated or improving the compatibility of the two
solvents. This work explored using phase modifiers as a simple way to increase selectivity for rare
earth elements in solvent extraction. Two key hypotheses were developed and validated in this
work: that phase modifiers influence the stability and configuration of rare earth–diglycolamide
assemblies through hydrogen bonding and solvent polarity, and that certain modifiers can
kinetically favor extraction of rare earths by altering interfacial organization. Major
accomplishments include the deployment of the SAFE database for solvent extraction data,
completion of over 2,300 high-throughput measurements using the LANL Super Separator, and
the discovery that alcohol-based phase modifiers can enhance separation performance without
altering inner-sphere complexation. Machine learning approaches linked changes in extraction
behavior to micelle formation, and detailed experimental studies revealed how phase modifier
identity, concentration, and structure impact aggregation, selectivity, and extraction rates. These
findings collectively establish new principles for controlling rare earth separations by manipulating
supramolecular assembly and interfacial chemistry in organic phases.

Project Goals and Research Questions
 Current rare earth extractants display marginal selectivity for individual rare earth elements
despite decades of ligand design studies. We are striving to understand how to harness the
properties of the organic solution itself to significantly enhance separations. Instead of focusing
on the metal-ligand bonds of the extracted rare earth complexes, we are pursuing a novel
separations principle for rare earths wherein altering the properties of the organic solution by
introducing phase modifiers improves the separations selectivity by (1) tuning the equilibrium
thermodynamic selectivity for particular rare earth ions through solute-solvent interactions in the
outer coordination spheres of the extracted complexes in the bulk organic phase, and (2) shifting
the balance of the rare earth phase transfer kinetics at the aqueous/organic interface.

Hypothesis 2: Phase modifiers that create stable secondary structures at the aqueous/organic

preferences and the species transported across the interface

Task 1. Developing rational control of equilibrium rare earth complex-solvent interactions
 Rather than designing more selective extracting ligands, our approach to rethinking how to
approach separations of critical rare earth elements seeks to alter the nature of the organic diluent
in order to generate selectivity where current extracting ligands are not selective. However, the
number of rare earth elements, acidities, and phase modifier composition that need to be studied
pose too large an experimental space for individual experimentation. To address this we
established a workflow that used the LANL Super Separator to measure lanthanide distribution
ratios in liquid-liquid extractions across a wide range of compositions. The workflow enables 13
partitioning parameters to be systematically and rigorously tested, namely phase modifier identity,
phase modifier concentration, lanthanide identity, lanthanide concentration, extractant identity,
extractant concentration, acid identity in the aqueous phase, acid concentration in the aqueous
phase, the ionic strength of the aqueous phase, mixing rate, temperature, organic phase identity,
and contact time. Across 2352 individual extraction experiments we evaluated key partitioning
variables that were expected to be important for rare earth extraction. Distribution ratios for
TODGA extraction of lanthanides in nitric acid were quantified for four lanthanides (Nd3+, Eu3+,
Dy3+, Ho3+) with three phase modifiers (1-octanol, tributylphosphate (TBP), and di-2-
ethylhexylbutanamide (DEHBA), Figure 2) as a function of lanthanide identity, lanthanide
concentration, aqueous nitric acid concentration, and phase modifier identity. The organic phase
composition (30 mM TODGA in n-dodecane with a phase modifier), system temperature (25 ℃),
contact time (30 min), and pressure (0.8 bar) were all held constant. The results of these 2352
experiments (Figure 3) highlighted the impact of the phase modifier. For example, nearly
equivalent distribution ratios were observed for Nd3+, Eu3+, Dy3+, and Ho3+ for all experimental
conditions tested when the phase modifier was either 1-octanol or DEHBA. This is interesting

length influence the organic phase structure, solvation environment, and extraction behavior of

lanthanide distribution relative to unmodified TODGA from Tb on.

Varying isomerization of octanol
 Since the length of the alkyl chain of the alcohol altered the rare earth selectivity in only a
few cases, we also investigated how octanol isomers affected bulk properties in TODGA systems.
The focus was on organic-phase capacity, resistance to third-phase formation, and polar network
connectivity. Collectively, the results from these studies demonstrate that the position of the -OH
group on the octanol backbone directly tunes organic-phase polarity, hydration, acid uptake, and
aggregation behavior. The variations in aggregation behavior and the onset of third phase
formation indicated that this series of alcohols would be important to use in our attempts to probe
the second hypothesis regarding the effect of phase modifiers on the kinetics of phase transfer as
they should influence the phase transfer kinetics.
 Measurements of organic-phase hydration and nitrate uptake showed strong isomeric
dependence. At fixed TODGA and phase modifier loading, 1-octanol consistently yielded the
highest organic-phase water content, followed by 2-octanol and then 3-octanol. This ordering
persisted at both 5 and 30 vol% of phase modifier. The magnitude of water uptake increased with
phase modifier loading for all isomers, but the gap between isomers widened at 30 vol%, showing
that the accessibility of the -OH group dictates hydrogen-bond network formation within the
organic phase.
 Comparing the behaviors of the organic phases in contact with 0.5 M HNO3 and 0.5 M
LiNO3 showed that acid-mediated hydration plays a major role for 1-octanol, which exhibits larger
increases in [H2O]org (Figure 7) in the HNO3 case. In contrast, 3-octanol showed nearly identical

Task 2. Regulating the kinetic components of rare earth extraction selectivity
 The second overarching goal of this project is to understand and improve the separations
of rare earths using phase modifiers to alter their extraction kinetics and affect separations of
particular lanthanides. The kinetic systems studied have been selected based on the measurements
for the equilibrium systems described above, and they concentrate on the effects of phase modifiers
on the reactions at the liquid-liquid interfaces of these solvent extraction systems. Our principal
tools for the kinetics centered work were molecular simulations, interfacial tensiometry, and real-
time monitoring of the progress of solvent extraction processes using a unique approach to
operando UV-Visible spectroscopy coupled with highly efficient mixing.

similar systems have been studied on two other occasions, only a few solution compositions had
been examined, the conclusions of the two studies did not agree, and neither study proposed a

phases was studied over a wide range of solution compositions (Figure 13). The data showed clear
signatures of a distinct, chemically controlled extraction pathway that has rate limit steps for the

The Effects of Phase Modifiers on the Phase Transfer Kinetics and Extraction Mechanism
 The addition of phase modifiers to the TODGA/dodecane organic phase has dramatic
impacts on the extraction kinetics. We focused on the commonly used modifier 1-octanol for its
relevance and impact and extended the study to its isomers 2-octanol and 3-octanol in order to
understand the effects of branching on the extraction mechanism. We also were intrigued by the
effects of TBP and monoamides as phase modifiers uncovered by the experiments at LANL.
Because these molecules are amphiphilic, they preferentially adsorb at the interface and could alter
the mechanism described above. With the exception of systems that use TBP as a phase modifier,
the equilibrium studies discussed above indicated that the inner sphere coordination environment
of the organic phase lanthanide complexes at equilibrium does not change when phase modifiers
are introduced. Speciation measurements conducted as part of the kinetic studies with TBP,

 14

N,N-dihexyloctanamide (DHOA), 1-, 2-, and 3-octanol and octanenitrile confirm this. However,
different phase modifiers affected the phase transfer rates and extraction mechanisms differently.

 The kinetics of the systems containing the phase modifiers DHOA, 3-octanol, and
octanenitrile each show little change from the TODGA extraction system without phase modifiers
even though each of these phase modifiers alters the properties of the bulk organic phase and
displays surface active properties. For example, in the case of DHOA, the limiting rate laws for
the TODGA systems containing 0.25 M DHOA and 1.0 M DHOA match the unmodified case, but
the constants for individual steps in the kinetic mechanism presented above change modestly
depending on the concentration of DHOA. 3-Octanol, on the other hand, displays little effect on
the rate law or rate constants in the extraction direction, but it increases the rate of the reverse
reaction modestly though the overall rate law, and presumably the mechanism, does not change.
These phase modifiers are the least surface active of the set of phase modifiers we studied.
 In contrast, 1-octanol and TBP and generally increased the lanthanide extraction rates and
slowed down the reverse partitioning rates, except at the highest concentrations of 1-octanol
studied (30 vol%) where both the forward and reverse phase transfer reactions slowed dramatically
(Figure 14). These phase modifiers have the highest surface activity of the surfactants we studied,
and both molecules drive changes in the extraction mechanism compared to the unmodified case.
In both cases, fewer TODGA molecules and nitrate ions are involved in the final rate determining
step of their mechanisms, and a loss of the plateau regions in the forward and reverse directions
also suggest a simpler mechanism at high phase modifier (30 vol%) concentrations. But the reason
for these differences in their mechanisms does not appear to be the same for both systems. In the
TBP case, a different complex is formed in the organic phase as compared to the unmodified
system (Figure 15), so the specific kinetic mechanism is expected to be different. In contrast,
spectroscopic measurements of the octanol systems indicate that the final lanthanide coordination
environment is similar between the unmodified system and all three octanol systems. Nevertheless,
