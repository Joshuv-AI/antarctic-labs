Glass Surfaces
Rendered Hydrophobic by Silylation
and Sol–Gel Coatings: Optical, Wetting, and Chemical Stability

Attila Ábrahám, Kinga Kovács, Lenke Jula-Kócs, Norbert Nagy, Attila Sulyok, Beáta Szolnoki, Dániel Zámbó, Zoltán Hórvölgyi, Emőke Albert

Abstract

This study presents a comparative evaluation of three
strategies
for hydrophobizing glass surfaces: direct surface silanization with
dimethyldichlorosilane (DMDClSi), the application of a hybrid silica
coating (H-SiO2), and the use of a hexamethyldisilazane
(HMDS)-modified silica coating (P-SiO2/HMDS). The glass/H-SiO2 and glass/P-SiO2/HMDS coatings were prepared by
a sol–gel process and deposited onto glass substrates via dip-coating
using alcohol-based precursor sols. All surface treatments resulted
in hydrophobic surfaces, with advancing water contact angles exceeding
90° and very low contact angle hysteresis (∼1°),
indicating uniform and stable water-repellent surfaces. Surface free
energy analysis confirmed the predominantly nonpolar character of
the modified surfaces, with the lowest values observed for the DMDClSi-treated
samples (18 ± 1 mN m–1). Both silica-based
coatings also increased the light transmittance of the glass substrates
compared to the bare glass, achieving maximum transmittance (Tmax) of ≈99.6–99.7% and average transmittance
increases of 6.0–6.5% (400–800 nm), together with controlled
porosity (34–35%) and uniform thickness values (127 nm). Ellipsometric
porosimetry revealed marked differences in pore structures between
the silica-based coatings: the glass/H-SiO2 coating exhibited
a high-surface-area porous network (1124 m2 cm–3, 38% porosity), whereas the glass/P-SiO2/HMDS coating
showed higher porosity (48%) with larger mesopores and a lower specific
surface area (245 m2 cm–3), indicating
a more open porous structure. The stability of hydrophobic and optical
properties was investigated under combined chemical and thermal stress
using a three-factor Box–Behnken design. The results identified
pH as the main degradation parameter, while temperature and exposure
time acted as accelerating factors. Under alkaline–thermal
conditions, degradation of Si–CH3 groups was identified
as the primary mechanism responsible for hydrophobic failure in the
glass/H-SiO2 system, as confirmed by ATR-FTIR analysis.
These results suggest that the high-surface-area pore network of the
glass/H-SiO2 coating is more susceptible to the loss of
Si–CH3 functionalities. Wettability measurements
demonstrated that the glass/DMDClSi surface exhibited the highest
resistance to degradation throughout the investigated design space.
Among the silica-based coatings, the glass/P-SiO2/HMDS
sample showed markedly improved hydrophobic stability compared to
glass/H-SiO2, retaining water-repellent properties under
most conditions and undergoing substantial hydrophilization only under
the most severe alkaline–thermal exposure. Light transmittance
measurements showed significant optical degradation for glass/H-SiO2 (Tmax ≈ 92–93%) over a wide range
of conditions, whereas the glass/P-SiO2/HMDS coating maintained
high optical stability (Tmax ≥ 99.1%) across most
of the investigated pH-temperature–time combinations, with
deterioration observed only under the most severe conditions. Although
the DMDClSi treatment provided the highest wettability stability,
the glass/P-SiO2/HMDS coating combined high optical stability
with durable hydrophobicity, and improved resistance to environmental
stress.

Introduction

The hydrophobization of
glass surfaces has gained significant attention
due to its critical role in developing self-cleaning windows, anti-fogging
coatings, drag reduction systems, glass displays, and protective cover
glasses for electronic devices.
−

 However, the practical implementation
of treated glass faces substantial challenges, particularly regarding
the simultaneous achievement of preserving intrinsic optical transparency
and ensuring durable water repellency.
−

 The fundamental trade-off
between the surface modification necessary for superhydrophobicity
and the optical clarity expected from glass represents a critical
design challenge that has limited the widespread implementation of
these technologies.
−

 Recent comprehensive studies have further emphasized
that superhydrophobic and multifunctional coatings are increasingly
designed not only for water repellency, but also for additional functionalities
such as anti-icing, anticorrosion, self-cleaning, and environmental
protection performance, highlighting their broad technological relevance
and application-driven development trends.
,,−

 Furthermore, the development of transparent multifunctional interfaces
has increasingly relied on advanced hybrid and composite material
concepts that integrate surface chemistry and nanoscale structural
design within a single coating structure. Glass substrates can be rendered hydrophobic through various distinct
methodologies, primarily either by directly functionalizing the pristine
glass surface itself or by applying a secondary coating that inherently
possesses or is subsequently modified to exhibit water-repellent properties
(e.g., hybrid silica coatings containing nonpolar functional groups
integrated into the matrix). These surface
modification strategies typically include silanization-based molecular
grafting, which enables the formation of low-surface-energy self-assembled
monolayers on hydroxylated glass surfaces; sol–gel-derived hybrid silica coatings that combine optical
transparency with tunable surface functionality;
,
 plasma-assisted functionalization, which allows efficient surface
activation and subsequent hydrophobic modification; laser-assisted surface texturing for the generation of
controlled micro- and nanoscale roughness; and nanostructure-assisted surface engineering based on hierarchical
sructures that promote superhydrophobic behavior while preserving
optical performance.
,
 Among these approaches, silanization
remains one of the most widely employed methods because organosilanes
can form covalently attached self-assembled layers on hydroxylated
glass surfaces while simultaneously reducing surface free energy.
Early studies demonstrated that chlorosilanes and alkylsilanes can
generate densely packed hydrophobic monolayers on silica-based substrates,
providing a molecular-level route toward controlled wettability modification
and long-term alteration of surface wetting behavior.
,
 By carefully optimizing the nanoscale surface structure, thickness,
and refractive index of such coatingsparticularly silica-based
onesit is possible to develop bifunctional surfaces that simultaneously
exhibit antireflective (AR) properties with high light transmittance
(>95%) and superhydrophobicity with water contact angles exceeding
150°.
,
 Achieving this dual functionality
is a delicate process, as it requires minimizing light scattering
while maintaining the high apparent contact angles necessary for water
repellency.
,
 The deposition of a silica coating
with an optimized thickness and refractive index on a transparent
glass substrate can further enhance its light transmittance. In addition, functionalizing the glass surface
to be hydrophobic enables the development of a bifunctional coating
that simultaneously exhibits AR and water-repellent properties.
,,,,
 Recent advances in hybrid composite coatings
have shown that the incorporation of organic and inorganic building
blocks within nanostructured silica matrices enables simultaneous
optimization of transparency, wettability, and mechanical stability. The durability of these bifunctional properties
on glass in practical applications presents another significant challenge.
Environmental factors such as UV exposure, temperature variations,
mechanical stress, and prolonged chemical contact can lead to differential
degradation of optical and wetting properties.
,,
 Studies have shown that while the optical
properties of treated glass may be partially recoverable through thermal
treatment, wetting property changes from surface chemistry modifications
are often irreversible. Long-term stability
testing has revealed that coatings can maintain superhydrophobic and
antireflective properties on glass for at least one year under outdoor
weathering conditions, though mechanical properties may be insufficient
for abrasive wear.
,
 In this context, recent advances
in mechanically robust coating structures have demonstrated that enhancing
interfacial adhesion and designing hierarchical structures can significantly
improve resistance to abrasion and long-term environmental degradation,
which is essential for real-world applications. Hybrid silica-based materials are particularly promising
for developing AR and hydrophobic coatings on glass, as their high
chemical compatibility with the substrate allows the formation of
bifunctional surfaces in a single preparative step without additional
surface modification.
,
 Hybrid silica systems combine
the excellent optical transparency and chemical stability of inorganic
silica networks with the tunable functionality of organic moieties,
making them attractive candidates for next-generation multifunctional
coatings. Beyond their structural versatility,
hybrid silica systems are increasingly recognized as sustainable and
environmentally friendly alternatives to fluorinated coatings, enabling
per- and polyfluoroalkyl substance-free surface engineering and aligning
with current trends in green chemistry and eco-conscious materials
design. These materials can achieve remarkable
optical performance, with transmittance values reaching up to 99.8%
while maintaining water contact angles of 90° or higher.
,
 The use of intrinsic hydrophobic functionalities, such as Si-CHn units or trimethylsilyl groups, provides more stable water
repellency compared to surface-applied fluorination. Recent studies have further demonstrated that organically
modified silica networks can be designed to provide long-term hydrophobicity
while maintaining excellent optical properties and improved environmental
compatibility. In addition, polymer–silica
hybrid architectures have emerged as a versatile platform for tailoring
interfacial properties through molecular-level control of network
composition and crosslinking density. Current research has identified several key design principles for
achieving durable transparent superhydrophobic glass: utilizing nanostructures
smaller than the visible-wavelength-scattering threshold to minimize
light scattering, implementing hierarchical roughness for mechanical
resilience, and designing architectures that decouple mechanical support
from functional topography to enable independent optimization of each
property.
,,,
 These design strategies are strongly supported by
recent advances in multifunctional coating systems, where combined
chemical–structural engineering enables simultaneous optimization
of optical clarity, mechanical stability, and wetting behavior, reflecting
the current trend toward integrated advanced material platforms. Furthermore, recent studies have highlighted
that achieving mechanical robustness remains one of the key limitations
of superhydrophobic coatings, and that failure often originates from
nanoscale damage accumulation and interfacial delamination under stress. Advanced coating technologies increasingly employ
multiscale composite structures and hybrid material concepts to overcome
this limitation while preserving transparency and surface functionality.
,
 Recent reports have also demonstrated that combining hierarchical
roughness with chemically integrated low-surface-energy functionalities
can improve both durability and multifunctionality without relying
on environmentally persistent fluorinated compounds.
,
 However, significant gaps remain in understanding the long-term
stability mechanisms and developing standardized durability testing
protocols for these multifunctional glass surfaces.
,

To address these challenges, this study systematically compares
three distinct approaches for preparing hydrophobic glass surfaces,
including direct silanization and two silica-based coating strategies.
While numerous studies have reported individual hydrophobic or antireflective
glass treatments, direct comparisons of fundamentally different hydrophobization
routes under identical environmental stress conditions are still limited.
The novelty of the present work lies in the combined evaluation of
surface chemistry, optical performance, wettability, and durability
using a unified experimental framework. By combining optical characterization,
surface chemical analysis, wettability measurements, and accelerated
durability testing across systematically varied chemical (pH 2, 7,
and 12), thermal (24.9–59.9 °C), and temporal (2–24
h) conditions, we systematically investigate the interplay between
surface chemistry, coating structure, optical performance, and hydrophobic
stability. Particular emphasis is placed on evaluating the effectiveness
of different hydrophobization strategies in preserving transparency
and water repellency under environmental stress. The findings contribute
to the rational design of durable, transparent, and multifunctional
glass surfaces for optical and protective applications.

grade and used without further purification.

Ammonium hydroxide (25%, a.r., Lach-Ner) was

of the as-deposited coatings, while the Piranha solution was prepared

Ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ·cm, purified
with a Millipore
Simplicity 185 filtration system) and diiodomethane (99+%, Acros Organics)

Microscope glass slides
(76 × 26 × 1 mm, Thermo Scientific,
Menzel-Gläser) and silicon wafers (Siegert Wafer GmbH, (100),
p-type, prime grade) were used as solid substrates.

Synthesis of Precursor Sols

To obtain
hybrid silica coatings, a sol from two precursor molecules (TEOS and
MTES) was synthesized. First, TEOS was mixed with an aqueous hydrochloric
acid solution (0.06 M, pH = 1.3) and ethanol, then the mixture was
stirred for 1 h at 60 °C. The molar ratio of TEOS:H2O:EtOH was 1:10:7.7. In another beaker, CTAB was dissolved in a mixture
of ethanol and MTES and stirred for 30 min at room temperature. The
molar ratio of MTES:CTAB:EtOH was 1:0.2:1. The TEOS:MTES molar ratio
was 1. After stirring, the two mixtures were poured together and stirred
for a further 15 min (400 rpm) at room temperature. The resulting
suspension was labeled as H-SiO2.

Besides the hybrid silica coating, an alternative silica sol
containing Pluronic PE 10500 was synthesized and used for the preparation
of a second type of silica coating (P-SiO2). This silica
sol was synthesized by the acid-catalyzed controlled hydrolysis of
TEOS. The molar ratio of the components was TEOS:2-propanol:HCl:H2O:PE10500 = 1:14.49:0.01:5.61:0.01. In the first step, the
Pluronic PE 10500 surfactant and a 0.1 M hydrochloric acid solution
were added to the alcoholic medium, and it was stirred (400 rpm) until
the complete dissolution of the surfactant (ca. 30 min) at 25 °C.
TEOS was added to this solution, and the mixture was stirred for further
30 min (400 rpm).

Preparation of the Samples

The overall
sample preparation strategy was designed to obtain two coating types
on glass substrates (glass/H-SiO2 and glass/P-SiO2/HMDS) with closely similar optical and wetting properties. Therefore,
on the basis of our previous experience and preliminary experimental
results, the specific sols, processing parameters, environmental conditions,
and post-treatment steps described in the following subsections were
optimally selected and applied.

Preparation of the Sol–Gel Coatings

Microscope glass slides and silicon wafers were used as solid substrates
for layer deposition. Prior to film deposition, glass substrates were
washed with an aqueous detergent solution (1 wt %), rinsed with ultrapure
water, and neutralized with a dilute sulfuric acid solution (10 wt
%). After that, they were wiped with 2-propanol-impregnated cotton,
then rinsed with 2-propanol and ultrapure water. Silicon substrates
were cleaned with 2-propanol and ultrapure water. Then the substrates
were dried at 70 °C in a drying oven (Memmert UNB 400).

Prior to layer deposition, the silicon substrates were treated with
O2 plasma (10 min, 0.4 mbar, 300 W) using a radiofrequency-generated
low-pressure plasma system (SmartPlasma 10, Plasma Technology GmbH)
coupled with an oxygen concentrator (DeVilbiss Healthcare 525 KS)
in order to enhance surface adhesion and wettability toward the alcoholic
precursor solutions.

Sol–gel coatings on glass and silicon
substrates were prepared
from the precursor sols by the dip-coating method (dip coater, Plósz
Mérnökiroda Kft., Hungary) at room temperature. Cleaned
and dried substrates were immersed in the precursor sol and pulled
out at a constant speed of 5 cm min–1 (H-SiO2 sol) or 1.5 cm min–1 (P-SiO2 sol). The deposited H-SiO2 coatings were annealed in
a muffle furnace (Nabertherm B170) at 400 °C for 2 h with a heating
rate of 13.3 °C min–1. In the case of P-SiO2 coatings, colloidal aging was applied in order to strengthen
their structure. The as-deposited coatings were placed in a sealed
desiccator above 250 mL of a 2 M aqueous ammonium hydroxide solution
and exposed to aqueous ammonia vapor for 2 h at ambient temperature
(22–26 °C). The volume of the desiccator was 11 L. The
treated samples were annealed in a muffle furnace (Nabertherm B170)
at 480 °C for 1 h with a heating rate of 15.0 °C min–1. Finally, the samples were immersed in ultrapure
water for 30 min and dried at 70 °C.

Silylation Methods

Silylated glass
and silicon substrates were prepared by using a 1% v/v% DMDClSi solution
in n-hexane. Prior to silylation, glass substrates
were immersed in Piranha solution (a 3:1 mixture of H2SO4 and H2O2) for 5 min and then rinsed
with ultrapure water. Before hydrophobization, the silicon substrates
were subjected to plasma treatment (O2 plasma, 10 min,
0.4 mbar, 300 W). Subsequently, both glass and silicon substrates
were immersed in the silylating agent solution for 1 h. After the
reaction, samples were rinsed with n-hexane which
was followed by heat treatment at 150 °C for 30 min.

Silylated
silica and glass (for comparison) surfaces were obtained by HMDS vapor
treatment. Prior to silylation, the porous samples were kept in 0.01
M HCl solution for 5 min in order to induce the hydrolysis of alkoxy
groups still present on the surface. The silica and glass (for comparison)
samples were kept in a desiccator (ca. 1 L) containing 0.5 mL of HMDS
reagent for 6 h. Then the samples were heat-treated at 350 °C,
for 1 h, with a heating rate of 5 °C/min.

Ellipsometric Porosimetry (EP)

Ellipsometric porosimetry was used to determine the open porosity,
pore size, and pore size distribution of the glass/H-SiO2 and glass/P-SiO2/HMDS samples. Measurements were carried
out using a Semilab Sopra EP-12 ellipsometric porosimeter. EP is a
well-established technique for the characterization of porous thin
films, providing information on open porosity, specific surface area,
pore size, and mechanical strength. The method combines vapor adsorption
with spectroscopic ellipsometry, enabling the adsorption process to
be monitored stepwise. During the measurements, vapor of the adsorptive
material condenses within the pore network, resulting in a change
in the effective refractive index. Analysis of the adsorption–desorption
isotherms allows the determination of sample porosity, while the pore
size distribution can be calculated using the modified Kelvin equation
for mesopores and the Dubinin–Radushkevich model for micropores.
The SiO2 coatings were characterized using 2-PrOH as the
adsorptive material. Prior to the measurements, the samples were heated
at 175 °C for 15 min and then vacuumed. The 2-propanol adsorption–desorption
isotherms were recorded at 24 °C. In addition, EP enables the
determination of the optical properties of the samples at zero relative
pressure of the adsorptive material, similar to conventional spectroscopic
ellipsometry. Measurements were performed at an angle of incidence
of 60° over a wavelength range of 248.5–971.5 nm. The
coating thickness and effective refractive index (at 632.8 nm) were
determined using the Cauchy dispersion model. The specific surface
area values were calculated according to the BET method, assuming
a molecular cross-sectional area of 0.388 nm2 for 2-PrOH.

X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)

To characterize the surface coating, the elemental composition
in the surface range was examined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy
(XPS) using an Al anode. Spectra were obtained by a special cylindrical
mirror analyzer with retarding field (type DESA 150, Staib Instruments
Ltd.) that provided a constant energy resolution of 1.5 eV. After
taking a spectrum from the freshly prepared surface, further spectra
were recorded after applying a gentle low-energy ion bombardment (Ar,
1 keV, 78° angle of incidence) to remove possible surface contamination
from the environment. The chemically different subcomponents of each
XPS lines were determined with a peak fitting algorithm with Shirley
background subtraction and Gaussian/Lorentzian fitting.

Abrasion Resistance Test

The abrasion
resistance was evaluated using a Coating Hardness Kit (Summers Optical,
a division of EMS Acquisition Corp.) in accordance with ISO 9211-4:2012. A stack of 32 layers of cotton cheesecloth (MIL
CCC-C-440) was prepared and used as the abrasive medium. The head
of the aluminum abrasion tester was covered with the cheesecloth pad
and loaded with a force of 5 N. The tester was then manually moved
across the surfaces of the glass/H-SiO2 and glass/P-SiO2/HMDS samples. A total of 100 rubbing strokes were applied
to each sample, consisting of 50 forward and 50 backward passes. After
the abrasion test, the sample surfaces were visually inspected and
compared to evaluate abrasion-induced damage and abrasion resistance
of the coatings.

UV–Vis Spectroscopy

Optical Properties of the As-Prepared Samples:
Ellipsometric Porosimetry

The pore structure of the glass/H-SiO2 and glass/P-SiO2/HMDS coatings was characterized
by ellipsometric porosimetry using 2-propanol as the adsorptive at
24 °C. The glass/H-SiO2 sample exhibits a steep adsorption
step in the relative pressure range of p/p0 ≈ 0.40–0.55
with only minor hysteresis between the adsorption and desorption branches
(a), indicating
a relatively homogeneous mesoporous network with a narrow pore size
distribution. The limited extent of hysteresis suggests efficient
pore emptying and the absence of pronounced pore-network effects.
In contrast, the glass/P-SiO2/HMDS sample (b) displays a type IV isotherm
with a pronounced H2-type hysteresis loop according to the IUPAC classification.
Capillary condensation occurs at substantially higher relative pressures
(p/p0 ≈ 0.80–0.95), while the broad hysteresis
loop indicates the presence of interconnected mesopores, pore constrictions,
and significant pore-network effects associated with a structurally
heterogeneous porous structure.

using the Lorentz–Lorenz equation (). The ellipsometric porosimetry results

than the glass/H-SiO2 coating (n = 1.259), indicating a
higher pore fraction within the coating. This is consistent with the
total porosity values calculated using the Lorentz–Lorenz equation,
which are 38% for glass/H-SiO2 and 48% for glass/P-SiO2/HMDS. The corresponding open porosity values are 37% and
43% for the glass/H-SiO2 and glass/P-SiO2/HMDS
samples, respectively (). These results suggest differences in pore accessibility
and connectivity within the two coating systems. This interpretation
is supported by the mesopore radii obtained from the adsorption and
desorption branches of the isotherms. While the glass/H-SiO2 sample exhibits identical adsorption and desorption mesopore radii
(2.1 nm), indicating a relatively uniform mesoporous network, the
glass/P-SiO2/HMDS coating shows a pronounced difference
between the adsorption and desorption mesopore radii (15.4 nm and
7.3 nm, respectively). Such behavior suggests the presence of pore-network
effects and possible pore constrictions, which may limit access to
part of the pore volume. The pore size distributions further highlight
the structural differences between the two coatings. The glass/H-SiO2 sample is characterized by micropore radii of 1.0 nm (adsorption)
and 0.5 nm (desorption), together with a relatively narrow mesopore
size distribution centered at approximately 2.1 nm. In contrast, the
glass/P-SiO2/HMDS coating exhibits micropore radii of 0.5
nm (adsorption) and 0.7 nm (desorption), while the mesoporous pore-size
range is shifted toward substantially larger pore sizes. These results
demonstrate that the HMDS-modified coating possesses a broader pore
structure with significantly larger mesopores than the hybrid silica
coating. The specific surface area also differs markedly between the
two samples. The glass/H-SiO2 coating exhibits a high BET
specific surface area of 1124 m2 cm–3, whereas 245 m2 cm–3 was obtained for
the glass/P-SiO2/HMDS coating. This decrease is consistent
with the substantial increase in characteristic pore size, as larger
pores provide a lower internal surface area per unit volume than smaller
pores. Consequently, although the HMDS-modified coating possesses
a higher overall porosity, its accessible internal surface area is
considerably lower than that of the hybrid silica coating. Overall,
the ellipsometric porosimetry results demonstrate that the glass/H-SiO2 coating consists of a relatively homogeneous micro- and mesoporous
network with high specific surface area, whereas the glass/P-SiO2/HMDS coating is characterized by higher total porosity, substantially
larger mesopores, and more complex pore accessibility. These structural
differences are reflected in the refractive index, porosity, specific
surface area, and pore size distribution values determined for the
two coating systems (). Although minor differences exist between the optical and
porosity-related parameters obtained from ellipsometric porosimetry
and UV–Vis spectroscopy, these deviations mainly arise from
the different measurement principles and evaluation methodologies
of the two techniques, while both methods lead to the same overall
conclusions regarding the structure–property relationships
of the investigated coatings.

Abrasion Resistance of SiO2-based
Coatings

To evaluate the abrasion resistance of the prepared
coatings, glass/H-SiO2 and glass/P-SiO2/HMDS
samples were subjected to a standardized abrasion test according to
ISO 9211-4:2012. The test conditions
were selected based on category C of the environmental durability
classification, which corresponds to coatings intended for outdoor
applications where high abrasion resistance is not a primary requirement.
This category was chosen to provide a representative moderate-severity
level for assessing the mechanical durability of the coatings.

Visual inspection was performed at regular intervals during the abrasion
process (every 5 cycles, i.e., 10 rubbing strokes) to monitor the
onset and progression of surface changes. Both coating types remained
fully adhered to the substrate after 50 abrasion cycles (i.e., 100
rubbing strokes), with no visible delamination observed in either
case. The glass/H-SiO2 sample showed the first signs of
surface degradation after approximately 10 rubbing cycles, where minor
scratch formation became visible on the coating surface. In contrast,
the glass/P-SiO2/HMDS coating did not exhibit any detectable
delamination or cracking of the thin film when inspected to the naked
eye; however, a slight decrease in surface intensity (fading) was
observed after approximately 10 cycles.

The evaluation of the experimental soaking data (–) and the Box–Behnken regression model ( and ) clearly identifies pH (X1) as the
dominant factor determining
the stability of all investigated coatings. Under neutral conditions
(pH 7), all surfaces retained high advancing contact angles (ΘA ≈ 101–104° for glass/DMDClSi, 84–87°
for glass/H-SiO2, and 76–87° for glass/P-SiO2/HMDS), whereas acidic media caused moderate deterioration,

As discussed in our previous work, the decrease in water contact angles for silica-based coatings can
be attributed to two main mechanisms. Firstly, water molecules penetrate
the boundary layer of the solid matrix during soaking, slightly reducing
the hydrophobic character of the surface. Secondly, in strongly acidic
or alkaline media, hydrolysis of the siloxane bridges generates polar
silanol groups, which orient toward the polar water molecules, thereby
increasing the surface hydrophilicity. This effect is particularly
pronounced for glass/H-SiO2 and glass/P-SiO2/HMDS, leading to a significant decrease in contact angle values.
In the case of the glass/DMDClSi sample, only a thin layer of surface-bound
Si–O–Si linkages is formed during silylation, without
the development of an extended siloxane network. Consequently, hydrolysis
plays only a minor role in the reduction of the contact angle, and
the slight decrease observed is mainly attributable to the penetration
of water into the boundary layer and to limited chemical heterogeneity
within the ultrathin surface film.

These trends are quantitatively
confirmed by the regression coefficients
( and ). The strongly negative linear pH terms (β1) for both ΘA and ΘR confirm
that increasing alkalinity is the primary driver of wettability loss.
This effect is especially pronounced for glass/H-SiO2 and
glass/P-SiO2/HMDS, while glass/DMDClSi shows only weak
pH sensitivity, consistent with its higher chemical resistance. The
positive intercepts (β0) follow the same ranking
as the initial hydrophobicity of the samples, further supporting the
experimental observations. Temperature (X3) acts as a secondary
but significant destabilizing factor. Experimentally, higher temperatures
reduced both ΘA and ΘR and increased
contact angle hysteresis (–). In the regression
model, this is reflected by negative linear temperature coefficients
(β3) and, in some cases, positive quadratic terms
(β33), indicating nonlinear thermal effects. The
interaction between pH and temperature (β13) is strongly
negative for the silica-based coatings, demonstrating that alkaline
and thermal stresses act synergistically to accelerate surface degradation.
Soaking time (X2) alone has a weaker and nonmonotonic influence.
While the linear time terms (β2) are slightly positive
near the design center, the negative quadratic coefficients (β22) indicate curvature and progressive degradation at longer
exposure times. This agrees with the experimental finding that prolonged
soaking becomes critical mainly under extreme pH or high-temperature
conditions. Correspondingly, the interaction terms β12 (pH–time) and β23 (time–temperature)
show that extended soaking time intensifies the destabilizing effects
of chemical and thermal stress, particularly for glass/H-SiO2 and glass/P-SiO2/HMDS sample types ( and ). Receding contact angles (ΘR) exhibit even stronger
sensitivities than advancing contact angles, reflecting the high responsiveness
of ΘR to surface heterogeneity and damage. Both experimental
data and regression coefficients show that ΘR decreases
more rapidly than ΘA under alkaline and thermal stress,

extreme conditions, glass/H-SiO2 and glass/P-SiO2/HMDS samples develop pronounced CA hysteresis (up to 20–30°),
whereas glass/DMDClSi preserves consistently low hysteresis, indicating
a more homogeneous and stable surface, whereas the high hysteresis
of the silica-based coatings suggests that the surface has undergone
partial chemical and morphological degradation (–).

The changes observed in the equilibrium (Young) contact angle and
the corresponding work of adhesion (Wadh) further support
the conclusions drawn from the advancing and receding contact angle
measurements (–). For all investigated samples,
a decrease in ΘYoung was accompanied by a systematic
increase in Wadh, consistent with the Young–Dupré
relationship. The glass/DMDClSi surface exhibited only moderate changes
across the experimental conditions. Its Young contact angle remained
relatively high (77–102°), while the work of adhesion
increased only from the initial value of 60 mN m–1 to a maximum of approximately 90 mN m–1 under
the most aggressive soaking conditions (). These results confirm the high chemical
stability of the DMDClSi-modified surface and indicate that only limited
changes occurred in the surface chemistry during soaking. In contrast,
the silica-based coatings showed a much stronger response to the soaking
conditions. For the glass/H-SiO2 sample, ΘYoung decreased from the initial value of 95° to as low as 6–14°
under alkaline conditions, accompanied by a substantial increase in
Wadh to 144–145 mN m–1 (). Similarly, the
glass/P-SiO2/HMDS coating exhibited a decrease in ΘYoung from 93° to 0–26°, while the adhesion
work increased from 70 mN m–1 to 139–146
mN m–1 (). The nearly complete disappearance of the Young contact
angle and the simultaneous increase of the work of adhesion toward
the theoretical limit predicted by the Young–Dupré relationship
indicate the development of highly hydrophilic surfaces and, in the
most severe cases, complete wetting.

The dependence of ΘYoung and Wadh on
pH, temperature, and soaking duration follows the same trends observed
for the advancing and receding contact angles. Neutral conditions
generally preserved relatively high ΘYoung and low
Wadh values, whereas acidic media caused moderate changes
and alkaline environments led to the most pronounced deterioration.
Elevated temperatures further accelerated these effects, particularly
for the silica-containing samples. The strong increase in adhesion
work under alkaline and thermal stress indicates the formation of
polar surface functionalities, most likely silanol groups generated
by hydrolysis of siloxane bonds, which enhance water–surface
interactions. Consequently, the evolution of ΘYoung and Wadh provides additional quantitative evidence that
the silica-based coatings undergo substantial chemical degradation
under aggressive soaking conditions, while the DMDClSi-modified glass
retains a significantly higher level of hydrophobicity and interfacial
stability.

Overall, the combined experimental–model analysis
demonstrates
that pH is the primary driver of wettability loss, temperature is
the main accelerating factor, and soaking time modulates these effects
through nonlinear and interaction effects. The most severe degradation
occurs when all three variables reach their extreme levels simultaneously,
particularly for silica-based coatings, while the DMDClSi-modified
glass surface exhibits the highest resistance across the investigated
design space. Although small deviations are observed under extreme
conditions, the model reliably reflects the overall trends, quantitatively
representing the influence of pH, soaking time, and temperature on
contact angle evolution and surface stability.

Importantly, the chemical modifications identified by FTIR provide
direct evidence for the mechanism underlying the wetting behavior
discussed in . While AFM analysis
indicated that changes in surface roughness do not correlate with
the observed wettability degradation, the FTIR results demonstrate
that the loss of hydrophobicity is primarily driven by chemical alterations
of the surface, namely the degradation of Si–CH3 groups under alkaline conditions (soaking at pH 12 and 59.9 °C
for 13 h).

Conclusions

This work presents a comprehensive
comparative study of three distinct
methodologies for hydrophobizing glass substrates: direct silanization
(glass/DMDClSi), and the application of two functional coatingshybrid
silica (glass/H-SiO2) and hexamethyldisilazane-modified
silica (glass/P-SiO2/HMDS). All surface treatments yielded
hydrophobic surfaces, with advancing water contact angles exceeding
90° and very low contact angle hysteresis (∼1°),
confirming chemically and morphologically homogeneous water-repellent
surfaces. Surface free energy analysis further corroborated the hydrophobic
character of the investigated systems, with the lowest values observed
for the DMDClSi-modified surface and a clear dominance of the dispersive
component over the polar component, consistent with the contact angle
trends and the presence of nonpolar surface functionalities. In addition,
both silica-based coatings (glass/H-SiO2 and glass/P-SiO2/HMDS) significantly enhanced the light transmission of the
glass substrates, achieving maximum transmittance values above 99%,
together with well-defined porosity (34–35%) and uniform layer
thicknesses of 127 nm. Ellipsometric porosimetry revealed clear structural
differences between the silica-based coatings. The glass/H-SiO2 coating exhibited a relatively homogeneous mesoporous network
with a narrow pore size distribution centered at ∼2 nm and
only minor hysteresis, whereas the glass/P-SiO2/HMDS coating
displayed a type IV isotherm with a pronounced H2-type hysteresis
loop, indicating a more heterogeneous interconnected mesoporous structure
with larger pores, pore constrictions, and pore-network effects. These
EP-derived differences were reflected in the determined optical and
structural parameters. The HMDS-modified coating showed a lower refractive
index (n = 1.225 vs 1.259), higher total and open porosity (48% vs
38% and 43% vs 37%), but a lower specific surface area (245 vs 1124
m2 /cm3), confirming larger, less surface-dense
pores compared to the compact high-surface-area H-SiO2 network.
The durability of the hydrophobic functionality and optical properties
was systematically evaluated under combined chemical and thermal stress
through soaking experiments designed according to a three-factor,
three-level Box–Behnken framework. The results identified pH
as the dominant degradation parameter, while temperature and exposure
time acted as synergistic accelerators. Under strongly alkaline–thermal
conditions, loss of Si–CH3 functionalities was identified
as the primary mechanism controlling hydrophobic failure of the glass/H-SiO2 sample, as confirmed by ATR-FTIR analysis. This chemical
transformation, rather than morphological roughening, was shown to
be the key factor controlling wettability loss. The evolution of light
transmittance under soaking conditions further clarified the degradation
behavior. The glass/H-SiO2 coating exhibited pronounced
optical deterioration across a broad range of alkaline–thermal
conditions, with reduced maximum transmittance (Tmax ≈
92–93%) and negligibleaverage transmittance increase values
(ATI ≈ 0–1%), highlighting strong synergistic effects
of pH, temperature, and exposure time. In contrast, the glass/P-SiO2/HMDS system showed excellent optical stability across most
of the investigated conditions, preserving high transparency (Tmax ≥ 99.1%; ATI ≈ 5.7–6.2%) with minimal
sensitivity to environmental variables, with degradation occurring
only under the most severe alkaline–thermal conditions (pH
12, 13 h, 59.9 °C). Wettability stability tests further revealed
a clear performance hierarchy among the investigated coatings. The
glass/DMDClSi surface exhibited the highest resistance to wetting
degradation across the entire experimental domain, maintaining relatively
high advancing contact angles and low hysteresis even under strongly
alkaline and elevated temperature conditions,showing substantially
greater resistance to wetting degradation than both silica-based coatings.
Among the silica-based systems, glass/P-SiO2/HMDS showed
markedly improved hydrophobic stability compared to glass/H-SiO2, retaining water-repellent properties under most investigated
conditions and undergoing severe wetting loss only under the most
extreme alkaline–thermal exposure. The glass/H-SiO2 system exhibited the lowest wetting stability, including complete
wetting under the most severe conditions. When optical performance
and wettability are evaluated simultaneously, a clear functional trade-off
emerges. The glass/DMDClSi coating provides superior wetting resistance
but lacks the porosity-driven optical enhancement. The glass/H-SiO2 coating offers high surface area but suffers from poor stability
in both optical and wetting properties under environmental stress.
In contrast, the glass/P-SiO2/HMDS system represents the
best overall compromise, maintaining high transparency (>99%) and
beneficial optical properties under most conditions while preserving
acceptable hydrophobic performance, except under extreme alkaline–thermal
exposure. Importantly, the observed functional behavior can be directly
correlated with the EP-derived pore structure and the ATR-FTIR results.
The dense, high-surface-area network of glass/H-SiO2 is
more susceptible to hydrolysis-induced loss of Si–CH3 groups and subsequent silanol formation, leading to rapid degradation
of hydrophobicity. In contrast, the more open mesoporous structure
of glass/P-SiO2/HMDS contributes to improved retention
of both optical transparency and water repellency under most investigated
conditions.

Overall, the combined structural, chemical, optical,
and mechanical
analyses demonstrate that no single coating optimizes all properties
simultaneously. However, the glass/P-SiO2/HMDS coating
delivers the most balanced performance among the investigated systems
by combining high transparency, porosity-driven optical enhancement,
and comparatively robust hydrophobic stability. Although all coatings
eventually degrade under extreme alkaline–thermal conditions,
the HMDS-modified system significantly extends functional stability
relative to glass/H-SiO2, while glass/DMDClSi provides
the benchmark for maximum wetting resistance. These results highlight
the critical role of surface chemistry and pore structure in developing
multifunctional coatings where optical performance and interfacial
stability must be simultaneously optimized. The combination of excellent
optical transparency (Tmax > 99%), low contact angle
hysteresis,
and enhanced resistance to environmental degradation makes the glass/P-SiO2/HMDS coating particularly attractive for applications requiring
both optical clarity and long-term water repellency. Potential application
areas include self-cleaning architectural and automotive glazing,
protective cover glasses for electronic displays, optical sensors,
photovoltaic cover plates, and antifouling transparent surfaces exposed
to outdoor environments.
