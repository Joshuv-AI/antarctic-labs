Parent-delivered Hand–arm Bimanual Intensive Training Including Lower Extremity for young children with bilateral cerebral palsy in Vietnam: protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Mai Ngoc Doan Nguyen, Huynh Thien Nhan Le, Trieu Vy Nguyen, Thanh Phuong Do, Geoffroy Saussez, Yannick Bleyenheuft
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCerebral palsy (CP) is a leading cause of long-term disability for children in Vietnam and across the world, affecting both gross and fine motor skills as well as daily independence. Intensive therapy has been shown to significantly improve motor outcomes. Moreover, parental involvement in therapy contributes positively to child development. Hand–Arm Bimanual Intensive Therapy Including Lower Extremities (HABIT-ILE) is a structured intervention that targets motor function of the upper and lower extremities through intensive, goal-directed practice. This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of a parent-delivered HABIT-ILE programme in young children with CP in Vietnam, using a randomised controlled trial comparing onsite and online groups of intervention.Methods and analysisA total of 48 children aged 2–6 years diagnosed with bilateral CP will be randomly assigned to one of two intervention groups: parent-delivered HABIT-ILE conducted either in a rehabilitation centre or at home. Participants will be paired based on their age and functional levels using the Gross Motor Function Classification System Expanded and Revised and the (mini-)Manual Ability Classification System before randomisation. Each group will receive an identical 50-hour intervention over 2 weeks, delivered by parents under the supervision of HABIT-ILE experts. Primary outcomes include the Gross Motor Function Measure-66 and the Melbourne Assessment 2. Secondary outcomes will consist of Vietnamese versions of the West-African version of the ACTIVLIM-CP questionnaire, Paediatric Evaluation of Disability Inventory, Canadian Occupational Performance Measure and parental quality of life with World Health Organization Quality of Life Brief Version questionnaire. Assessments will occur twice at baseline with 2 weeks in between (T0 and T1), postintervention (T2, 2 weeks after T1) and 3-month follow-up (T3). Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) is a widely accepted term referring to a spectrum of motor impairments caused by non-progressive brain injury or malformation occurring during early development.1 CP may affect both gross and fine motor skills and may impair trunk control and postural stability of children.2 3 The primary purpose of rehabilitation in children with CP is to minimise the impact of physical disability, improve independence in daily activities and enhance the quality of life for both the children and their families, who play a key role in the process.4

According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s 2022 epidemiological data, it is estimated that 1–4 children out of 1000 have CP worldwide. The regional rate of CP in low- and middle-income countries is up to 3.4 per 1000 (95% CI 3.0 to 3.9) live births, which is twice as high as the regional rate 1.5 per 1000 (95% CI 1.4 to 1.6) from high-income countries (HICs).5 In Vietnam, national data on CP incidence are lacking, but hospital and provincial reports offer partial insights. For instance, the National Children’s Hospital reported that 765 children were newly diagnosed with CP in 2017.6 In the Vietnamese Ministry of Health’s 2008 guidelines for the care of children with CP, it was estimated that approximately 2 per 1000 live children have CP, accounting for 30–40% of all childhood disabilities diagnosed in Vietnam.7 Along with the additional burden of associated impairments,8 rehabilitation for children with CP faces numerous challenges in the Vietnamese context.6 These challenges stem from various factors, including the cost of treatment, as CP requires long-term care. Additionally, there is a shortage of therapists to meet the extensive needs of children requiring training,9 and rehabilitation facilities are limited and often concentrated in central urban areas.10 Therefore, it is essential to develop a rehabilitation programme that is both effective and accessible for children and their families. Such a programme should address the unique needs of Vietnamese children with CP and be designed to overcome the barriers.

Traditionally, CP has been managed through neurorehabilitation based on neurodevelopmental methods.11 However, the effectiveness of these methods is still hotly debated.11 According to systematic reviews of interventions for preventing and treating children with CP, intensive interventions based on motor skill learning have proven to be highly effective in enhancing functional abilities and promoting neuroplasticity.12 13 Additionally, parental support is essential to optimise child development, target parenting skills and protect parental mental health.14 15 Such intensive intervention programmes that are conducted daily for several weeks are based on repetitive tasks practice solely focusing on voluntary motor control (hands off), gradually increasing in difficulty of the performed activities in a motivational context to reach functional goals identified by children and their caregivers.16 17 Such interventions have shown significant benefits in motor and functional outcomes, correlated with neuroplastic changes.18–20 Among these interventions, Hand–Arm Bimanual Intensive Therapy Including Lower Extremities (HABIT-ILE)21 includes a therapeutic focus on both the upper and lower extremities, with a constant stimulation of trunk control.

HABIT-ILE intervention is part of the recommended interventions to improve motor function of the upper and lower extremities in HICs with significant improvements shown in the scientific literature for children with unilateral and bilateral CP, aged from 6 months to 18 years old.18 22 23 It is usually delivered with therapists in a rehabilitation centre, in the form of camps of 50–90 hours of therapy in 2 weeks, depending on age and CP subtypes (5–9 hour a day).18 19 24 HABIT-ILE is increasingly applied to examine its transferability across diverse contexts and countries. In low- and middle-income settings, it has been implemented in the Republic of Benin, West Africa, for young children with bilateral CP.19 In Australia, participatory HABIT-ILE programmes, such as Preschool HABIT-ILE, include caregiver coaching and are delivered in groups of 4–6 children for 3 hours per day over 10 days (30 hours), supplemented by 10 hours of caregiver-led home practice, for a total dose of 40 hours.25 In Belgium, a home-based telesupervised model has been tested in a pilot study in which caregivers provided 3 hours of training per day for 5 days (15 hours) with remote video supervision during the first and last 30 min of each session. Caregivers reported feeling adequately supported, demonstrating that HABIT-ILE principles could be effectively transferred and sustained in the home environment with a remote supervision.22

Recognising the potential of HABIT-ILE within the Vietnamese context, with the opportunity to bring large changes and independence in daily life activities in a short period of time, this paper will describe the study protocol for implementing parent-delivered HABIT-ILE in Vietnam with the aim to provide effective and accessible approaches that empower parents to deliver targeted training.

More than assessing the transferability and efficacy of such parental involvement in HABIT-ILE camp, this randomised controlled trial (RCT) aims to test two different approaches of this parent-delivered intervention: one delivered in a rehabilitation centre, with families managing to travel to the rehabilitation centre; and the other delivered at the participant’s home, with a remote supervision by the therapeutic team. Both interventions aim to optimise motor function and enhance independence in daily activities for children with CP, tailored to their specific contexts.

Aims and hypotheses

Broad aim

This RCT will be conducted in Vietnam, involving 48 children aged 2–6 with bilateral CP. The RCT aims to evaluate the effectiveness of the parent-delivered HABIT-ILE programme, implemented both in a rehabilitation centre and at children’s homes, compared with the baseline ‘non intensive’ period. The primary outcomes will focus on improvements in gross motor skills, assessed using the Gross Motor Function Measure-66 (GMFM-66) and upper extremities unimanual motor function, assessed with the Melbourne Assessment 2 (MA2). Secondary outcomes will assess changes in the performance of daily activities using Vietnamese versions of the West-African version of the ACTIVLIM-CP questionnaire (ACTIVLIM-CP-WA) and the Paediatric Evaluation of Disability Inventory (vPEDI), performance and satisfaction about the self-determined goals with the Canadian Occupational Performance Measure (COPM) and parental quality of life with World Health Organization Quality of Life Brief Version questionnaire (WHOQOL-BREF). These primary and secondary outcomes will be tested at four testing sessions: twice at baseline with a 2 weeks interval (T0 and T1 just before the intervention), postintervention (T2; 2 weeks after T1) and at 3-month follow-up (T3; 3 months after T1).

Methods

Study design

This longitudinal RCT in Vietnam will compare a parent-delivered HABIT-ILE intervention, which involves 5 hours of therapy per day for 10 days (total of 50 hours), implemented both in a rehabilitation centre or at home setting, as well as a baseline control measure of 2 weeks receiving no intensive period of rehabilitation. Patient and public involvement

Children with CP and their parents were not involved in the development of the study design or methodology. However, they will actively engage in establishing the children’s individual functional goals, and parents will receive tailored coaching from the supervisors. Following the intervention, individual progress reports will be disseminated to the participants to support the ongoing application of these goals to their daily lives.

Randomisation process

Before the first assessment, children will be paired based on their levels at the GMFCS E&R, MACS or mini-MACS, and their age. Each pair will be randomly assigned to the rehabilitation centre or the home-based settings, using the website www.randomizer.org. Personnel involved in participant recruitment will not have access to the allocation sequence before randomisation. This randomisation process will be conducted by the first and the last two authors (NMND, GS and YB).

Sample size

For this RCT, the sample size has been calculated based on the changes in the GMFM-66, with a 95% confidence level (alpha=0.05) and 90% statistical power (beta=0.10). Based on the parameters reported by Bleyenheuft et al in 2017,18 a SD of 6.4 and a minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 7 points are assumed. Using the standard formula for comparing means between two independent groups, 18 participants per group are needed. Accounting for an estimated 25% dropout rate, the final required sample size is 24 participants per group, resulting in a total of 48 participants.

Status of the protocol

The study is currently in the second period of intervention delivery phase.

Blinding procedure

Trained assessors will administer the primary outcome measures, the GMFM-66 and MA2. These assessments will be video recorded for later scoring by trained raters blinded to both group allocation and the timing of the assessments. The primary author will anonymise all data, ensuring confidentiality. Analyses will be conducted using these coded group allocations, maintaining objectivity throughout the research process. Unblinding procedure for video analyses is not planned.

Study intervention

Non-intensive phase

This study protocol includes two phases of non-intensive rehabilitation. A first 2 weeks baseline and the second one during the 12 weeks follow-up period. During non-intensive phases, children will receive their usual care non-intensively. A non-intensive care can be defined as receiving less than 30 hours of training within a 2-week period.26 Activities during this phase may include: (1) usual care provided by caregiver without structured training from a therapist; (2) passive manual therapy or assisted training from a therapist, such as assisted range of motion exercises, stretching, splinting or casting; and (3) general fitness activities not directed at a specific functional goal. Services may occur in various settings including clinics, hospitals, homes or special schools, and the frequency ranges from multiple times a week to a few sessions distributed over months.

HABIT-ILE

HABIT-ILE is an intensive programme combining hand-arm bimanual activities with a constant concomitant stimulation of the lower limbs and trunk control.21 Built on the knowledge about motor skill learning, HABIT-ILE incorporates functional task-oriented activities designed to promote voluntary movement control and enhance motor skills in children with CP.27 28 Throughout the whole programme, the complexity of the proposed activities gradually increases based on self-determined goals and the actual motor and functional abilities (shaping). The ‘intensity’ principle refers to the level task repetition (motor engagement time) and the high therapeutic dosage (eg, 5 hour/day, 2 weeks). Activities are performed following a ‘hands off’ practice, meaning the therapist becomes less involved in directly manipulating the children’s body, emphasising active participation and voluntary motor control based on problem-solving/trial-error strategies. Therapists play an important role by specifically designing and adapting therapeutic environments to promote the learning of new abilities. As motivation is crucial for maintaining engagement and promoting learning, activities are performed in a child-friendly context including games and leisure activities. The functional aspect with self-determined goals ensures the programme aims to improve skills that are practical and applicable in daily life.21 29

Eligibility to fulfil requirements of HABIT-ILE intervention: Having at least one trained supervisor in charge of the intervention.

A typical HABIT-ILE programme

Among different possibilities for dosage, HABIT-ILE interventions are mainly provided in a 2 weeks camp with small groups from 6 to 12 children and delivered in a camp. Children engage in full days of therapeutic personalised activities designed and implemented by interventionists (mainly occupational therapists or physiotherapists), with constant supervision and coaching made by the supervision team (specifically trained occupational therapists and/or physiotherapists). Parents are involved in the process of intervention based on the discussion about self-determined goals at the admission testing session, in addition to everyday discussion at the end of therapy days. The treatment plan is constantly revised and adapted by the supervision team, with a daily team meeting dedicated to discussing treatment goals and plan modifications for the next day.21

Parent-delivered HABIT-ILE

In this study, parents will systematically be involved as HABIT-ILE intervention providers, supervised by HABIT-ILE trained supervisors, a method named as ‘parent-delivered HABIT-ILE’. Parent-delivered HABIT-ILE will be organised in two settings: at home or in a rehabilitation centre. An additional team of three to four local Vietnamese therapists will be involved in the rehabilitation process to communicate with parents/caregivers, ensure understanding of the desired activities and sustain motivation of children/caregivers.

Before the intervention, parents/caregivers will be trained about the basic principles of HABIT-ILE, emphasising the role of becoming a child’s playmate during the intervention and promote movement repetition by motivating/playful activities rather than exercises based on directive interactions or punishment, plus safety indications. In addition to this 3 hours training, a HABIT-ILE user guide will also be provided.

At the beginning of every therapeutic day, parents will receive a therapeutic programme containing pictures and information about the therapeutic focus for the day: movement trained for the different self-determined goals, instructions for organising therapeutic environment (type of objects, tables, sitting/standing material), instructions about spatiotemporal features of the desired activities and ways to organise activities in a playful context.

At the end of each day, activities are reviewed and adjusted based on a team meeting including the supervision team and local therapists. In the rehabilitation centre setting, supervision and therapists’ interactions are conducted in person, whereas in the home setting, it is provided through two daily video conferences of 30 min each. Location

Assessments will be conducted at the Rehabilitation Department of An Binh Hospital or the Rehabilitation Department of the University of Medicine and Pharmacy at Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. The intervention will take place in the rehabilitation department of An Binh Hospital for the onsite group, while the online group will receive the intervention at home.

Adverse events and safety

Adverse events will be monitored daily through verbal check-ins with parents or caregivers at both rehabilitation centre and home settings, with all incidents documented. Minor events (eg, pain, tripping, soreness, bruises) will be noted during routine follow-ups. Safety measures will include therapist supervision at the rehabilitation centre and caregiver training for home-based delivery. Activities will be conducted in safe, structured environments with gradual task progression, built-in rest periods and culturally adapted materials. Remote supervision will be provided daily for home-based participants. All events will be centrally logged to ensure continuous monitoring and adherence to the study protocol.

Screening and descriptive measures

All participating children will be classified using the following two classification systems.

GMFCS-ER was developed in 2007, it helps to classify the ability to perform gross motor functions and locomotor activities in children with CP.30 This classification includes five age ranges, and the age groups are: under 2 years, 2–4 years, 4–6 years, 6–12 years and 12–18 years of age and five levels described as:

Level I: Able to walk without limitations;

Level II: Can walk with limitations (eg, balance, endurance limitations);

Level III: Can walk using a hand-held mobility device (may use additional support, such as wheeled mobility, for longer distances, outdoors).

Level IV: Self-mobility is limited—may be transported in a manual wheelchair or may use powered mobility.

Level V: Transported in manual wheelchair.31

Manual Ability Classification System and mini- Manual Ability Classification System

Two classification systems describe how children with CP use their hands to manipulate objects in daily activities: the Mini-MACS for children aged 1–4 years, and the MACS for children aged 4–18 years. Abilities are ranked on a five-level scale based on the child’s ability to initiate independently and the need for support or adaptations to manipulate objects. The descriptions are relevant to how the child manipulates age-appropriate objects including five levels:

Level 1: Handles objects easily and successfully.

Level 2: Handles most objects, but with somewhat reduced quality and/or speed of achievement.

Level 3: Handles objects with difficulty;

Level 4: Handles a limited selection of easily managed objects in simple actions;

Level 5: Does not handle objects and has severely limited ability to perform even simple actions.32

Demographic questionnaire

Demographic information to be collected from the child includes age, sex, place of residence, comorbidities, usual therapeutic interventions. Information from the caregiver includes age, sex, educational level, current occupation and household income.

Data collection and data management

Each child will be assessed at four time points: twice at baseline with a 2-week interval (T0 and T1, just before the intervention), postintervention (T2; 2 weeks after T1) and at 3-month follow-up (T3; 3 months after T1). Standardised motor function tests will be administered to each child at every time point, with the assessment process video recorded. Both primary outcomes will be scored blindly. Meanwhile, parents or caregivers will complete the corresponding questionnaires in the form of written reports. To promote retention, families will be contacted by phone to remind them of upcoming appointments. For participants who discontinue or deviate from the protocol, the reasons will be documented, and attempts will still be made to collect outcomes at all scheduled follow-up time points.

Data will be recorded on paper forms and de-identified entered into an electronic database on a secure, password-protected hard drive. Physical documents will be kept in a locked cabinet, with access restricted to authorised personnel. Due to minimal risks, trial conduct will be monitored internally by the two principal investigators through regular meetings.

Outcome

Primary outcomes

GMFM-6633 34 is a 66-item scale that assesses five aspects of gross motor function: lying and rolling; sitting; crawling and kneeling; standing; and walking, running and jumping. During the assessment, the child performs each item, and the entire session is video recorded to allow for scoring. GMFM-66 requires at least one standardised assessment session, with scoring conducted by trained raters based on the recorded videos. Raw scores are then entered into the Gross Motor Ability Estimator-2 software, which converts them into a Rasch-based interval score ranging from 0 to 100, providing a reliable and sensitive measure of a child’s current gross motor capacity.35 The GMFM-66 is a measurement tool with good responsiveness, as indicated by a high standardised response mean (SRM).36 A MCID of 1.58 points has been reported for the GMFM-66, indicating that a change of this magnitude reflects a clinically meaningful improvement in gross motor function.37

MA238 is a reliable and valid tool used to assess upper limb movement quality in children aged 2.5–15 with neurological impairments.39 It evaluates four key areas: range of motion, accuracy of reaching and placement, dexterity of grasp, release and manipulation, and fluency of movement. The 14-item test involves children reaching for, grasping, releasing and manipulating simple objects while their performance is video recorded for scoring later. The test is administered separately for each hand, and the performance is video recorded to allow for scoring. The MA2 demonstrates strong psychometric properties, including high interrater and intrarater reliability (intraclass correlation coefficients >0.90), good internal consistency, established construct and discriminant validity and the large SRM values (1.70–2.00) confirm the responsiveness of the MA2.40 41

Secondary outcomes

The ACTIVLIM-CP-WA questionnaire42 is an adapted version of the original ACTIVLIM-CP,43 which was designed to assess overall performance in daily life activities among children with CP. The ACTIVLIM-CP-WA was built on the parents’ perception and involves 31 items covering the domain of self-care, mobility and domestic life of the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health, Child and Youth version (ICF-CY). The 31 items of the ACTIVLIM-CP-WA include 21 items for preschoolers (2–6 years), 23 items for children aged 6–12 years and 19 items for teens (12–19 years). Parents are instructed to estimate their child’s difficulty in performing the activities as mentioned in the questionnaire without technical or human help, on three-level responses: impossible (the child is unable to perform the activity alone), difficult (the child is able to perform the activity alone but experiences some difficulties), easy (the child is able to perform the activity alone without any difficulty). To enable parents/caregivers in Vietnam to use this assessment tool, the initial English version has been translated into Vietnamese following Beaton’s cross-cultural adaptation guidelines. The results will be calculated using the official scoring software available on the scale’s website (https://www.rehab-scales.org).

vPEDI44 is a translated and culturally adapted version of the original PEDI,45 an interview-based tool designed to assess functional performance in children. vPEDI is divided into three parts. This study applied Part I of vPEDI, which focuses on assessing the functional skills of children across three domains: self-care (73 items), mobility (59 items) and social function (65 items). Each item is scored on a binary scale: 0 (unable/limited) or 1 (capable). Raw scores in each of the three domains will be calculated separately, with higher scores indicating a greater number of functional skills that the child can perform independently.

The COPM46 is a client-centred outcome tool that enables individuals to identify and prioritise daily challenges that limit their participation in everyday life.47 It focuses on activity performance across three areas: self-care, leisure and productivity. Five of the most important goals are then rated using a 10-point scale for both performance and satisfaction. The total score is calculated as the average of the performance and satisfaction scores.

WHOQOL-BREF quality of life assessment48 49 is a self-administered questionnaire that includes 26 questions about individuals’ perceptions of their health and mental state in the 2 weeks prior. Answers to the questions are scored on a 1–5 Likert scale, where 1 represents ‘disagree’ or ‘strongly disagree’ and 5 represents ‘strongly agree’ or ‘extremely agree’. WHOQOL-BREF consists of four domains, each with specific aspects: physical health, psychological, social relationships, environment. WHOQOL-BREF scores are calculated for each domain on a scale from 0 to 100.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis will be conducted using SPSS software. Baseline outcome measures between groups will be tested using t-tests (or non-parametric equivalent analysis) for variables presenting verified normality and homoscedasticity. To evaluate changes over time as well as the group×time interaction, two-way repeated measures analysis of variance will be used with a 2 (groups)×4 (test sessions) design. When assumptions of normality or homoscedasticity are not met, linear mixed models will be applied as appropriate. Additional analyses are planned to include effect size estimation notably based on the MCID. All analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle.

Discussion

This protocol targets preschool children aged 2–6 years diagnosed with bilateral CP. In Vietnam, there is a significant shortage of healthcare resources and experience in organising family-centred therapy programmes or intensive rehabilitation programmes specifically designed for children with CP. Recognising the potential benefits of HABIT-ILE intensive therapy, we aim to investigate the effectiveness of the HABIT-ILE approach for children with CP in Vietnam. Unlike the typical HABIT-ILE approach, which does not systematically include parental involvement during training sessions, this study engages parents as active therapy providers under the supervision of HABIT-ILE experts, referred to as parent-delivered HABIT-ILE. This approach will be implemented both in an intervention rehabilitation centre and at home.

The study anticipates that if parent-delivered HABIT-ILE demonstrates significant improvements across various outcomes (functional abilities, performance of activities of daily living), it could positively influence the developmental trajectory of each child. This could result in meaningful impacts across all three domains of the ICF-CY in the short, medium and long term, compared with a non-intensive phase.

This study is the first to emphasise the role of parents/caregivers in delivering HABIT-ILE contrasting both onsite and home-based groups in young children with CP from 2 to 6 years old. Parent-delivered HABIT-ILE may offer cost-effective therapeutic options that would expand access to effective intensive intervention by combining therapist-guided and parent-led training, either in clinical settings or at home. Through this study, we hope to provide additional training opportunities for children with CP and contribute to the diversity of rehabilitation strategies available to clinicians.

We expect that, in case of positive outcomes, parent-delivered HABIT-ILE could not only transform the approach to caring for children with CP but also reduce the economic burden on the healthcare system associated with this population.

Ethics and dissemination

The study has been approved by the Ethics Committee in Biomedical Research at the University of Medicine and Pharmacy, Ho Chi Minh City (Approval No. 726/HĐĐĐ-ĐHYD, issued on 13 June 2024). The full trial protocol is available in the ClinicalTrials.gov registry (NCT06593158; https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06593158), first posted on 19 September 2024. Any important modifications to the protocol will be submitted to the Ethics Committee for approval before implementation and updated on the clinical trial registry. Participants will be fully informed about the study procedures and voluntarily sign a written informed consent form prior to enrolment. This documented approval will ensure that parents/caregivers understand the study and agree to participate. All personal data will be anonymised, treated with strict confidentiality and used solely for research purposes. No compensation or additional care is planned. Participants will continue to receive routine rehabilitation and standard clinical care. The study results will be disseminated through publication in peer-reviewed journals and presentations at scientific conferences. After the study is published, a summary of the results will be provided to participating children and their families. Individual results will also be shared with each participant.
