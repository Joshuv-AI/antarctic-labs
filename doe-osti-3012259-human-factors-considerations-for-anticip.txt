 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
 The objective of this report is examine anticipated advanced reactor designs
 that incorporate high levels of autonomy in operation of advanced reactor
 systems or facilities, and to begin identifying the human factors engineering
 (HFE) considerations relevant to those designs. The insights gained will help
 identify areas where U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) may need to
 develop or update review guidance to adequately address HFE considerations
 essential for safe operation. The adequacy of existing guidance, along with
 recommendations for updates or new guidance, may be addressed in future
 reports. We use the term “landscape analysis” to describe the review, analysis,
 and characterization of publicly available information on the anticipated future
 state of the industry regarding particular technologies or operational concepts.
 This landscape analysis considers the potential range of advanced reactor
 automation concepts that may be used for monitoring and controlling nuclear
 systems and facilities, including the potential for highly automated, near
 autonomous, or fully autonomous operation of advanced nuclear reactors.
 Nine reactor concepts were characterized by their design characteristics and
 concept of operation (ConOps) with respect to level of autonomy. The analysis
 presented in this report considers how aspects of advanced reactor design such as
 design simplicity and passive safety may enable higher levels of automation.
 Simple designs may have fewer actions needed to maintain operability, may have
 fewer active components, and hence are likely to have fewer human actions. In
 addition to design simplicity, passive safety features and inherent safety
 characteristics such as natural circulation also reduce the number of active
 components that need to be monitored and maintained and the actions that
 humans need to take to maintain safety functions. These characteristics, and
 others, may be considered as technical arguments when considering if a
 particular design is safe to use in a highly automated or autonomous manner.
 We describe the levels of autonomy for each reactor design in the context of
 elements of a ConOps presented in the draft Interim Staff Guidance,
 “Development of Scalable Human Factors Engineering (HFE) Review Plans,”
                 (DRO-ISG-2023-03, NRC, 2023a; NRC, 2022) which include:
 • Plant Mission and Goals
 • Agent’sa Roles and Responsibilities
 • Staffing, Qualifications, and Training
 • Management of Plant Conditions:
 - Normal Operations
 - Off-Normal Conditions and Emergencies
 - Maintenance and Modifications
 - Tests, Inspections, and Surveillances

a
 Modern approaches to human-automation interaction use multi-agent teams for monitoring and controlling complex systems
 (O’Hara, Fleger, Desaulniers, Green, Seymour, & D’Agostino, 2021; O’Hara, Higgins, Fleger, & Ki, 2020; O’Hara &
 Higgins, 2010). System agents can include personnel and automation or a combination of both.

 v

 The design characteristics informed evaluation of the human-automation
interaction characteristics of the ConOps. The ConOps were evaluated
hierarchically with plant mission and goals informing tasks and functions needed
for normal and off-normal operations, maintenance, and testing and surveillance
operations. Agents’ roles and responsibilities and training and qualifications were
evaluated for each function for the different operating conditions and plant
activities.
 Finally, we reviewed the reactor concepts analyzed in the landscape analysis
to identify human factors considerations related to autonomy based on their
proposed design characteristics and ConOps. These include situation awareness,
trust and automation reliability, workload, teaming, training, skill and knowledge
retention, and novel human error types, which are discussed based on
characterizations of autonomy for anticipated concepts.
 Based on a review of publicly available information including designer
websites, promotional materials, publicly available presentations, and
preapplication documents, there are several general themes and conclusions
related to autonomy and several human factors considerations that emerge.
 One key overarching gap is a clear description of what a human operator’s
role and authority will be when they serve a monitoring and “backup” function.
The human-automation interaction literature and experience from other industries
underscore that when operators do not have an active role in day-to-day
operations, they may struggle to maintain vigilance and situation awareness.
Moreover, situations in which a human is needed as backup are likely to be the
most complex and difficult to plan for, further complicating the process of
effectively preparing the human to act as backup. This can be addressed with
careful thought put into the tasking, human-systems interfaces, and training;
however, these considerations are not yet documented in publicly available
materials for most designs. To make a determination of reasonable assurance of
safety, it will be critical for NRC staff to understand the human’s role in future
design applications proposing near-autonomous operation, as variability in
human performance can be highest when human involvement is infrequent.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 1.1. Background
 The technical staff of the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) are responsible for the review
and evaluation of the human factors engineering (HFE) programs of applicants for construction permits,
operating licenses, standard design certifications, combined operating licenses and license amendments.
Advanced nuclear reactor technologies present new opportunities and new challenges for both the NRC
staff and the nuclear industry. The NRC staff anticipates that reactor developers will propose novel
approaches to reactor operations, such as increased reliance on automation including near-autonomous
operation for safety-significant systems, structures, and components (SSCs). The purpose of this research
is to better understand the anticipated use of automation and the level of autonomy in the nuclear industry.
 Broadly speaking, this report is guided by the following questions:
 • WHAT is being automated (e.g., what systems and functions)

 • WHEN is it being automated (e.g., normal operations, off-normal/emergency operations)

 • HOW is it being automated (e.g., levels, functions, processes, and modes)

 • WHERE is it being automated (e.g., main control room, local control station, remote operations
 center)

 Increased automation is highly desirable for a range of functions that can lead to improved work
systems, lower human errors and reduced operational costs. However, there are tradeoffs that must be
considered with increased use of automation and near-autonomy. We use the term “landscape analysis”
to describe the review, analysis, and characterization of publicly available information on the anticipated
future state of the industry regarding particular technologies or operational concepts.
 The objective of the landscape analysis in this report is to describe what is anticipated in terms of
advanced reactor designs that incorporate automation and increased levels of autonomy in advanced
reactor systems or facilities, and use the results to begin to identify the associated human factors issues
and considerations. The analysis considers the potential range of advanced reactor automation concepts
that may be used for monitoring and control of nuclear systems and facilities, including the potential for
high levels of autonomy in one or several nuclear reactor designs.
 1.2. Definition and Key Considerations
 It is difficult to define autonomy within the nuclear industry because many different characterizations
are used (NRC, 2023a). For the purposes of this landscape analysis, relevant literature was consulted
including research areas in 1) nuclear power operations, 2) systems engineering, 3) human factors and
human-automation, and 4) industries such as aviation and aerospace. A summary of relevant process
control and industry definitions is presented in Appendix A. Although a variety of working definitions are
used, three converging attributes of the term autonomy emerged:
 • Independent (i.e., free from outside control; not depending on another's authority)

 • Adaptive (i.e., having an ability to change to suit changing conditions)

 1

 • Self-governing (i.e., exercising control over one's own affairs).

 In addition to the converging themes listed above, several other keywords or phrases emerged,
highlighting the wide variety of definitions and meanings that are attributed to autonomy. For
completeness, we list them here:
 • predetermined

 • without human intervention (including humans unable to disable the system)

 • varying conditions of autonomy (e.g., under conditions of uncertainty)

 • decision versus control autonomy

 • artificial intelligence (AI) that may learn, evolve, adaptive, and generative

 • the notion of autonomy failing to compensate for failures.

 Although this analysis draws from a broad range of literature and industries, it does not explicitly
consider autonomy in the context of large light water reactors (LLWRs). This is intentional. LLWRs
represent a mature technology with well-established operational paradigms, regulatory frameworks, and
human-system integration practices. As such, the introduction of high levels of autonomy into LLWRs is
not currently a primary focus of innovation or regulatory interest. Instead, autonomy-related research and
development efforts are more prominently centered on advanced reactor designs, including microreactors
and other non-light water technologies, where novel operational concepts (e.g., remote monitoring,
reduced staffing, and factory fabrication) are being explored. These emerging designs present unique
challenges and opportunities for autonomy that differ significantly from those in LLWRs, and thus are the
primary focus of this landscape analysis.
 Taking into consideration these myriad meanings, a nuclear-specific definition of autonomy should be
practical, while also flexible enough to encompass a range of concepts of autonomous operations that are
likely to be employed in the nuclear industry. A preliminary definition put forth in SECY-24-0008
Enclosure 1 titled “Technical, Licensing, and Policy Considerations for Factory-Fabricated Micro-
Reactors” (NRC, 2024a) is:
 “Autonomous systems are considered those able to perform their task and achieve their functions
 independently (of the human operator), perform well under significant uncertainties for extended
 periods of time with limited or nonexistent communication, with the ability to compensate for
 failures, all without external intervention.”
 Although levels of autonomy were not formally assessed at the workshop on Human Factors for
Remote Operations in January 2024 (Morrow et al., 2025), the workshop findings indicate that most
vendors are unlikely to aim for fully autonomous operation of a facility. Some systems may be capable of
full autonomy, but the operation of the entire facility is not anticipated to be fully autonomous. Likewise,
we do not consider an autonomous system to mean unattended operations. While it may be the case that
an autonomous reactor system does not require human intervention, such systems are still anticipated to
be attended with human intervention possible. Thus, this landscape analysis focuses on high levels of
autonomy, including fully automated systems that may enable near-autonomous operation.
Mapping Traditional Conceptualizations of Automation to Levels of Autonomy

 The human factors implications for autonomy demand an understanding of how humans and
automation are expected to interact to perform safety functions under varying conditions. The human-

automation literature provides two key concepts to characterize human-automation interaction. These are
levels of automation (LOA) and flexible allocation.
 Most literature on human-automation interaction focuses on LOA. LOA describes roles of automation
alongside the roles of humans, ranging from fully manual to fully automatic. Several LOA models have
been put forth (e.g., Sheridan, 2002; Endsley and Kaber, 1999) with some describing full autonomy as the
final gradation (e.g., Billings, 1991).
 In their document offering guidance for reviewing a nuclear operator’s interface with automation,
O’Hara and Higgins (2010) characterize five levels each with examples of applicable nuclear power plant
(NPP) functions in current large light-water reactors (LLWRs; Table 1). Note that while full autonomy
might apply to the reactor protection system (RPS; level 5), startup functions may consist of a human-
automation combination (level 3).

 Beyond LOA, O’Hara and Higgins (2010) provide four other key dimensions of automation that are
likely implicated under levels of autonomy. These are:
 • Processes of Automation: How the automation achieves its objective through information
 processing must be considered (e.g., control logic, AI etc.).

 • Modes of Automation: The preferred mode must be stipulated, that is, a selection made from the
 different ways the automation may perform the function (e.g., the Reactor Mode switch in a
 boiling water reactor).

 • Reliability of Automation: Considerations pertaining to automation failure either partially or
 entirely compromising its capacity to perform the function.

 • Functions of Automation: The information processing activities that apply to the automation,
 ranging from 1) information acquisition, 2) information analysis, 3) decision and action
 selection, as well as 4) action implementation (i.e., also see Parasuraman, Sheridan, and Wickens,
 2000). Each of these ‘functions’ may have differing LOAs.

 Flexibility of allocation is a key characteristic in a nuclear operator’s interface with automation. In
practice, most real-world automation functions are dynamic in nature and involve flexible function

 3

allocation. Take the simple example of automotive cruise control. The function of maintaining speed is
allocated to the human until the human allocates it to the automation by turning on the cruise control and
setting the speed it should maintain.
 Flexible allocation can take the form of adaptive automation and adaptable automation. Adaptive
automation refers to the “dynamic, real-time change in the degree of automation a system employs that is
triggered by conditions such as poor task performance and high operator workload” (O’Hara et al., 2020).
Adaptable automation is also proposed as a way to manage workload but one that is manipulated by the
human (Sauer and Chavaillaz, 2017). In other words, while they are both flexible allocation of function
strategies, the former is carried out via automation whereas the latter is determined by the human. The
automotive cruise control example could be characterized as adaptable automation. Most of the levels of
automation described in the O’Hara and Higgins (2010) taxonomy (i.e., Table 1) are on the lower end of
the automation spectrum.
 In addition to the conceptualizations of automation, there are three additional considerations:
 1) Design attributes that influence the extent of human involvement
 2) AI and autonomy
 3) Autonomy as distinct from remote operations.
Design Attributes that Influence the Extent of Human Involvement

 One of the key features of Generation IV advanced reactors is a simpler design with fewer systems,
structures, and components (e.g., valves), making them easier to operate and maintain, and with less
opportunity for plant upsets (World Nuclear Association, 2021). Many designs also possess passive, as
opposed to active, safety features, in which the hazard has been reduced by design thereby improving the
safety margins (IAEA, 1991). Passive safety features involve systems that are guided by physics and
natural forces, natural circulation, or gravity flow (Fisher, 2020). Though passive, these systems still have
elements of engineering design that need to be considered (such as assumptions about conditions needed
for heat transfer that go into the engineering design) or may involve active components such as valves
that may require human oversight and/or actions. The International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA, 2009)
describes different categories of passive safety systems in the Passive Safety Systems and Natural
Circulation in Water Cooled Nuclear Power Plants (note that concepts for water cooled system can be
adapted for other coolant types). These systems may lend themselves to autonomous operation, because
the reactor can safely shut down without human action. Westinghouse’s AP1000 was the first simplified
reactor design to obtain NRC approval (NRC, 2011).
 A related concept is inherent safety characteristics, which the IAEA defines as, “safety achieved by
the elimination of a specified hazard by means of the choice of material and design concept” (IAEA,
1991). The most common example of an inherent safety characteristic is a negative temperature
coefficient of reactivity.
 Simplicity of design is important for human factors considerations, because fewer plant systems
require fewer personnel actions. For example, advanced concepts of operations that rely on gas or natural
convection to cool the core, instead of traditional methods such as forced water cooling, means that
circulatory pump systems are no longer required.
 The likely increase in levels of autonomy in advanced reactor systems may change the role of humans
from performing control actions to supervising automated control actions. Even in cases where full
autonomous operation is possible, it may remain necessary to allow for human intervention as a backup to
an automated system (e.g., defense in depth). For example, the AP1000 still uses active systems
proactively to prevent the need to rely on the passive systems. In this case, human actions may be needed
to activate automation as a preventative measure rather than for event mitigation. How operational

 4

authority is shifted from the automation to the human must be clearly defined and trained in a site’s
concept of operations (ConOps), similarly to the current practice in the operating LLWR fleet.
 A multifaceted approach to understanding how best to successfully incorporate human supervision is
necessary, with several outstanding considerations to address. Research over the last four decades has
documented drawbacks when introducing highly automated systems absent human factors consideration.
These considerations are elucidated further in Section 4 of this technical report.
AI and Autonomy

 In its AI strategic plan from 2023, the NRC defines AI as “a machine-based system that can go
beyond defined results and scenarios and has the ability to emulate human-like perception, cognition,
planning, learning, communication, or physical action” (Dennis et al., 2023). While many experts
consider AI as a key component for autonomous operations (e.g., the NRC’s strategic plan for AI
considers that AI may enable autonomous operations [Dennis et al., 2023]), AI is not strictly necessary to
achieve high levels of autonomy. Rather, it might be more appropriately conceived as enabling AI-
supported autonomy. For example, deterministic methods have been applied such as programmable logic
controllers and distributed control systems. These rule-based approaches (e.g., using if-then logic) to
operations technology work well under many conditions, and when paired with simplicity, passive safety
features, and inherent safety characteristics, near autonomy could potentially be achieved with
deterministic methods. Still, some reactor developers are considering AI in their ConOps, and some may
be considering AI as a means to achieve near-autonomy and in some cases full autonomy.
 It is important to note that while AI is not necessary to achieve a high level of autonomy, careful
consideration of its safe use in any level of automation is key. The International Electrotechnical
Commission (IEC) recently issued several standards that provide guidance on AI systems such as life
cycle processes (ISO/IEC, 2023), assessment of machine learning (ML) classification performance
(ISO/IEC, 2022), and ways to identify opportunities to develop AI (ISO/IEC, 2024). Together, these
standards provide a valuable framework with which to consider different aspects of data and algorithm
development.
 The NRC has been working with industry and increasing its readiness for advanced reactor concept
applications for several years (Moe, 2017; Owusu, Holbrook, and Sabharwall, 2018; Samanta, Diamond,
and O’Hara, 2019). Notably, there is currently no distinction in AI regulatory guidance for legacy versus
advanced reactors, including those with near-autonomy or advanced automation (Dennis et al., 2023).
Autonomy as Distinct from Remote Operations

 Remote and autonomous operations share similarities in that both are dramatic departures from the
traditional status of the existing NPP fleet, in which operators have licensing stipulations that require
them to be onsite and present in control rooms, per 10 Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) 50.54(k) and
(m). Remote and autonomous operations both seek to reduce the number of operators and other personnel
required to be onsite at the plant. However, a keen distinction between the two is that remote operations
seek to relocate the operators to an offsite centralized facility, thereby reducing the number of operators
needed onsite per reactor, whereas autonomous operations seek to minimize the need for operators
altogether (NRC, 2024a). Autonomous systems may be represented by a continuum of automation
capabilities whereby the extreme end of that spectrum is autonomously controlled reactivity (Hughes
Green and Morrow, 2024). In addition, while a combination of remote and autonomous operations may be
present in some anticipated advanced reactor ConOps (e.g., autonomy onsite with remote monitoring), it
is possible to have one without the other.

 2. LANDSCAPE ANALYSIS METHODOLOGY
 The landscape analysis evaluates advanced reactor design concepts based on a combination of design
characteristics that are relevant to level of autonomy including size, simplicity, and passive and inherent
safety features. These design characteristics inform further analysis of the automation considerations
based on the design’s ConOps. The overall approach followed is illustrated in Figure 1. Key steps in this
approach are described next.

 2.1. Step 1: Screening of Reactor Concepts
 Advanced reactor concepts were screened to be included in our analysis if they:
 • Are currently under active development.

 • Are past initial conceptual design (i.e., the design is sufficiently defined such that the ConOps can
 be understood at a high level).

 • Have publicly available information regarding their ConOps (i.e., whether from a vendor website,
 publicly available technical reports, or were discussed at the 2024 NRC Workshop on Human
 Factors Considerations for Remote Operation of Nuclear Facilities (NRC, 2024b).

 2.2. Step 2: Characterization of Reactor Concepts
 Next, reactor concepts were categorized by their design characteristics. As noted in an IAEA report
(IAEA, 2024a), there are many (i.e., over 60) different advanced reactor designs being developed. As a
result, an effective way of characterizing these reactors is by grouping them into categories of reactor
technology, reactor size and design features such as simplicity, passive safety features, and inherent safety
characteristics:
 • Reactor technology. We note the technology type.

 1) integral pressurized water reactors (iPWRs)

 2) high-temperature, gas-cooled reactors (HTGRs)

 3) liquid-metal reactors (LMRs) – for the purpose of this report Molten Salt Reactors are in this
 category

 • Reactor size. We note size in terms of megawatt electric (MWe) and megawatt thermal (MWth)

Agent’s Roles and Responsibilities

 The roles and responsibilities for staffing and automation were considered generally and then in more
detail in the context of the functions and tasks that are required for normal, abnormal, and maintenance
operations. This section focuses on high level descriptions of the LOA, and where there are details for a
specific function, that will be considered separately in the respective section. For example, if a developer
describes that the reactor will provide automatic load following, the details about the human and
automation roles will be documented under normal operations.
 While this section outlines high-level descriptions of levels of automation (LOA) and associated
responsibilities, it is important to emphasize that autonomous systems and human operators should be
viewed as complementary agents rather than replacements for one another. In safety-critical domains such
as nuclear operations, humans often serve as a critical backup to automation, particularly in scenarios
involving unexpected conditions, degraded system performance, or failure recovery. Conversely,
automation can serve as a backup to human performance by supporting workload management, reducing
human error, and maintaining operational continuity during periods of reduced staffing or high cognitive
demand. This mutual support model is essential for ensuring system resilience and should be explicitly
considered in the design and evaluation of roles and responsibilities across all operational modes.

 General information about staffing and training were evaluated including the overall level of staffing
and roles covered, location of staff, and training and qualifications that will be required. Specific details
about how tasks and functions will influence staffing and training considerations are provided in the
subsequent sections described next. This section also includes details on where automation occurs, as the
extent and location of automation directly impact staffing models and training needs. For example, highly
automated systems may reduce the number of staff required on-site but increase the need for specialized
training in remote monitoring, diagnostics, or supervisory control. Similarly, automation of specific
functions, such as maintenance or surveillance, may shift the required skill sets or qualifications for
personnel supporting those areas. Understanding where and how automation is implemented is therefore
essential to determining appropriate staffing levels, role definitions, and training programs.

3.1.1.1. Radiant Nuclear: Kaleidos

 The Radiant Nuclear Kaleidos is a micro-HTGR reactor that would provide approximately 1.2 MWe
and 3 MWth (Radiant Industries, 2023). The Kaleidos reactor will use tri-structural isotropic (TRISO)
fuel (i.e., capable of withstanding very high temperatures), helium gas coolant, and prismatic graphite
blocks. The design of the reactor plans to be transportable in nature and consist of a size envelope that
allows it to fit in a single shipping container for transportability via roadway, railway, or sea (Figure 2).

Figure 2. Graphical illustration of the Kaleidos reactor (adapted from Radiant Industries, 2023).

 The Kaleidos reactor concept credits its simplicity due to its small size combined with inherent safety
characteristics and passive safety features for ensuring safety. Radiant Nuclear highlights key differences
between Kaleidos and traditional LLWRs in their preapplication materials. For heat management,
whereas LLWRs rely on active coolant, Kaleidos uses passive cooling. For radioactive coolant, LLWRs
require active containment and monitoring and Kaleidos uses helium gas, which is chemically and
radiologically inert (i.e., inherently safe; e.g., NRC, 2003). For corrosion concerns, LLWRs require active
inspections whereas Kaleidos helium gas coolant is noncorrosive in nature (i.e., inherently safe). For fuel
containment, LLWRs rely upon fuel rods2 with cladding (i.e., offering passive safety) while Kaleidos uses
TRISO fuel that is inherently safe. Reactivity control will remain active, being controlled through drums
(Radiant Industries, 2023).

2
 Traditional LLWR fuel rods have cladding which helps contain radionuclides. Radiant uses TRISO fuel, which is a type of
 advanced reactor fuel that has its own type of layers (e.g., silicon carbide layers) to keep the radionuclides contained within
 the fuel.

 13

 The primary mission for the Kaleidos reactor concept would be electricity generation. With this, there
are several use cases identified by Radiant Nuclear that have been documented in their preapplication to
the NRC (Radiant Industries, 2023).
 The primary mission and goal for the Kaleidos reactor concept would be to provide electricity to
areas that are not currently served by the traditional electrical grid (i.e., that are currently being powered
by diesel generators). The application of Kaleidos, therefore, may serve regions for temporary (e.g.,
disaster relief) or permanent (e.g., remote regions) use.
 A second mission and goal may be to provide electricity to the primary electrical grid as a means of
enabling peak-shaving for base power generation as a way of allowing for lower baseload utilization that
optimizes cost.
 A third mission and goal includes providing supplemental electricity capacity for renewable energy
sources (e.g., solar and wind) whose outputs are conditional to weather conditions. Thus, the Kaleidos
reactor intends to provide greater stability to electricity output, making up for the variability in renewable
sources. It is worth noting, based on insights from the NRC Workshop on Human Factors Considerations
for Remote Operation of Nuclear Facilities (NRC, 2024b; Morrow et al., 2025), that some utilities are
considering ways to include microreactors3 like Kaleidos within a broader portfolio of other electricity
generating resources (e.g. renewables and/or fossil fuels).
 A final goal identified by Radiant Nuclear consists of supplying additional electricity to the grid in
high-consumption regions for enhanced grid stability (NRC, 2024b; Morrow et al., 2025).
Agent’s Roles and Responsibilities

 The Kaleidos reactor concept plans to rely largely on automation for control (e.g., load following), as
well as remaining safety functions. Radiant Nuclear highlights that Kaleidos plans to use telemetry and
ML for monitoring incoming data of plant health to enable automated decision making during normal and
off-normal conditions. For example, in the event of an unsafe condition occurring, safety automation will
shut down the reactor.
 Kaleidos does not plan to require manual action for ensuring safety. They plan to accomplish
monitoring functions using trained operations personnel located remotely at a centralized facility that is
geographically distant from the reactor site during normal operations and for monitoring reactor health,
physical barriers, and exclusion zones (Radiant Industries, 2024). Remote personnel would also be
responsible for startup and shutdown evolutions. In the event that manual intervention is required,
operators would have authority from the remote facility (e.g., during an unexpected emergency
condition). The onsite personnel would be responsible for the security and physical access to the reactor.
Refueling would be accomplished from a central manufacturing facility. As a result, when refueling is
needed (i.e., every five years), each Kaleidos reactor would require personnel to manually transport the
reactor to the manufacturing facility.

 Detailed staffing, qualification, or training plans for Kaleidos are not publicly available at this time.
Though, some limited information on staffing was shared by Radiant Nuclear at the NRC Workshop on
Human Factors Considerations for Remote Operation of Nuclear Facilities (NRC, 2024b; Morrow et al.,
2025). The monitoring of Kaleidos reactors from a remote centralized facility would not follow existing
staffing plans per 10 CFR Part 50.54(m). Rather, Radiant Nuclear indicated that a single operator would

3.1.2.2.           X-energy: Xe-100

 The Xe-100 is an 80 MWe (200 MWth) per-unit pebble bed reactor, which is helium cooled. The
design intent is to include a plant with four (4) modules totaling 320 MWe.
 The Xe-100 is described as a simple and modular design but includes more active components and

safety features are expected to reduce the complexity and number of actions required compared to an
existing LLWR.
 Inherent safety characteristics include a large negative temperature coefficient, which limits reactor
power and TRISO particle fuel, which is fully encapsulated in a ceramic core (X Energy, 2022a, 2025).
Passive safety features for the Xe-100 include passive decay heat removal through conduction, natural
convection and radiation (X Energy, 2025).

 Promotional material on the Xe-100 website5 indicates that it can be used for either electricity or
process heat. It is also designed to load follow from 40% – 100% and is designed for online refueling.
 Specific applications for the Xe-100 are not described in detail, and it isn’t specified directly how
high a temperature can be provided for process heat (the helium coolant temperature is 750 degrees
Celsius).
 New functions that must be supported are load following and managing multiple modules and unit
configurations due to online refueling.

3.2.1.2. Oklo Aurora Powerhouse

 The Oklo Aurora Powerhouse microreactor is a 15 MWe (can be scaled to 50 MWe) liquid metal

3.2.2.1. Flibe Energy, Inc.: LFTR

 The Flibe Energy, Inc. LFTR is a small modular graphite-moderated, thermal-spectrum reactor that
expects to produce approximately 250 MWe per unit for electricity production (Figure 11).

Figure 11. Graphical illustration of the Flibe Energy, Inc. LFTR (adapted from Flibe Energy Presentation
at NRC Workshop, January 31, 2024 [Hanson, 2024]).

 The LFTR concept uses a mixture of fluoride salts that are liquified to provide a means for fission to
occur within the reactor. The reactor core contains two different regions: 1) feed and 2) breed. These
regions support fuel recycling via thorium fuel cycle. As fission occurs within the reactor, the uranium
releases one neutron which becomes absorbed by thorium in the liquid fuel. The thorium then decays to
become protactinium. The protactinium then decays to become uranium, which can then be used for the
next fuel cycle. To manage this fuel lifecycle, the LFTR would contain a separate storage unit for
recycled fuel, which would later be directed to the reactor to create fission. A stated advantage of this
process would be to minimize waste and reduce the cost of refueling.
 The LFTR concept leverages both inherent safety characteristics and passive safety features (IAEA,
2021). For instance, the chemically stable Flibe (i.e., mixture of fluoride salts) coolant and passively
cooled containment and heat removal systems would provide the capability to stabilize the reactor without
the need for manual action or active safety systems. The reactor design includes a drain at the bottom of
the vessel. In the event where the LFTR requires powering down, the salt plug for the drain would melt
and the fluid salt would drain into a passively cooled containment vessel for decay heat removal. In any
case, the LFTR concept includes the capability for manual operation of control rods to support certain
plant evolutions like reactor shutdown (IAEA, 2021).

 The primary mission of the LFTR concept would be electricity production (IAEA, 2021). It would be
capable of producing baseload electricity, as well as load follow.

 29

 In addition to electricity production, the LFTR would be capable of supporting additional missions
such as providing thermal energy for industrial processes. Though, specific details regarding LFTR’s
support of additional missions has not been disclosed at this time.

 There is limited information regarding proposed staffing levels, qualifications, or training for the
LFTR concept.

 There are limited available details on management of normal operations. A description of the design
in an IAEA report (2021) specifies that reactivity would be controlled by a human operator; though, the

 There are no publicly available details on management of off-normal or emergency operations.

3.2.2.2. Kairos Power: Fluoride Salt-Cooled, High Temperature Reactor (KP-FHR)

 The KP-FHR small modular reactor concept is a type of Molten Salt Reactor, using TRISO fuel and
Flibe coolant to provide approximately 140 MWe to the electrical grid. A demonstration for the FHR
through the Hermes test reactor is targeted to be operational by 2027. At the time of this report, a non-
nuclear test unit has been used for demonstration of molten salt operations. On December 14, 2023, the
NRC issued a construction permit for the Hermes test reactor (Kairos Power LLC, 2023b). On November
21, 2024, the NRC issued construction permits for the Hermes 2 Units 1 and 2 test reactors (Kairos Power
LLC, 2024a, 2024b).
 The KP-FHR concept credits the use of inherent safety characteristics and passive safety features,
with its safety systems fully passive (Kairos Power LLC, 2018). For instance, the use of TRISO fuel is
designed to withstand very high temperatures (i.e., 1600 degrees Celsius) and the Flibe coolant is
chemically stable, which mitigates the potential for fuel damage scenarios and enables simplicity in the
design through a reduced number of needed safety systems. Further, containment is managed through
multiple inherently safe barriers, such as its TRISO fuel and fuel pebble bed, that provide safety without
the need for active safety systems or manual operator action.

 The primary systems of the KP-FHR consist of 1) the reactor system, 2) the primary heat transfer
system, 3) the intermediate heat transport system, 4) the power conversion system (Kairos Power LLC,
2018, Figure 12). Together, these systems enable converting the heat energy produced through fission
generation from the reactor system to produce electricity at the power conversion system.

 The primary mission of the KP-FHR concept is expected to be electricity generation.

3.2.2.3. TerraPower: Natrium

 Natrium is a 345 MWe (840 MWth) sodium cooled fast reactor that is designed to be paired with a
large energy storage system that is capable of providing up to 100-500 MWe for up to 5.5 hours.
 The design is described in promotional materials as simple. Passive safety features include passive
cooling using gravity and thermal convection, and inherent safety characteristics include a negative
temperature coefficient of reactivity. The “energy island” is described as completely decoupled from the
nuclear systems allowing the energy conversion and energy storage parts of the systems to be operated
independently of the nuclear systems.

 34

 In addition to electricity generation, the Natrium design is intended to provide additional applications
and flexibility compared to LLWRs by including a large energy storage system. The decoupling of the
nuclear island (NI) from the energy conversion and storage functions (called the energy island, or EI) is
intended to provide flexibility at a reduced cost. Promotional material describes that the design “enables
carbon free electricity, heat and steam” which implies that it is intended to be used for process heat or
other applications where steam is needed.
 The design is not intended to load follow directly but achieves flexibility in responding to grid
demand by diverting energy to the storage system. Natrium’s report on Regulatory Management of
Natrium Nuclear Island and Energy Island Design Interfaces (TerraPower, LLC, 2022) describes how the
storage system will be managed.
 Many of the functions associated with plant output (such as diverting energy to storage versus the
grid or configuring the system to provide heat or steam) plan to be managed on the EI rather than the NI.
Agent’s Roles and Responsibilities

 The Natrium Human Factors Engineering Concept of Operations whitepaper (TerraPower, LLC,

done an initial allocation of functions based on OE and have identified several specific functions that plan
to be fully automated along with criteria for determining whether other functions are automated or shared
or manually controlled. There are no high-level descriptions of the LOA nor details about which functions
the human will monitor versus having active control and whether the operator will have the authority to
intervene on those functions. The publicly available information in the list provided in TerraPower, LLC.
(2023) does not contain a comprehensive list of typical normal and abnormal operating functions.

 The staffing concept for the Natrium reactor includes the following on-site personnel: Shift Manager,

proximity of the nuclear control room. There are currently no additional details on the training or

 4. HUMAN FACTORS CONSIDERATIONS WITH LEVELS OF
 AUTONOMY
 The goal of human factors research within a nuclear setting is to understand human capabilities and
limitations in order to design systems that maximize human and system efficiency, reliability, safety, and
health. The NRC HFE review is a comprehensive evaluation that ensures the integration of human factors
into the design and operation of nuclear facilities thereby reducing the likelihood of accidents and human
error.
 We begin this section with a brief discussion of the reactor design ConOps broadly categorized into
two groups by levels of autonomy (Section 4.1). The purpose of grouping the anticipated reactor designs
identified in this report is to help summarize the concepts and aid understanding. However, these
groupings should be considered broad, and there may be some concepts that do not neatly fall into either
category, or that encompass features of both.
 For each group, we describe a variety of features with corresponding potential for human
involvement. With this backdrop, a high-level overview of human factors considerations is introduced
that may be pertinent to autonomous concepts in nuclear operations. It should be noted that in addition to
the ConOps that are currently under development, we also consider hypothetical and anticipated designs
that the NRC are likely to encounter in the future.
 4.1. Anticipated ConOps with Levels of Autonomy
 Sections 2 and 3 of this landscape analysis describe the role of highly automated systems in ConOps
already under development for future advanced reactors. Broadly speaking, based on the categorizations
generated from the summary findings in Section 3.4, these concepts are representative of two groups of
advanced reactor types, entailing:
 1) Microreactors with near-autonomous operation. These are small, simple and scalable designs
 that are readily transportable, and with no planned important human actions for ensuring safety.
 Startup and shutdown may be performed onsite, but otherwise operators will likely conduct
 remote monitoring. Control functions would typically be carried out without human intervention.
 What is unclear from available information is what tasks are required of personnel during off-

 and fulfilling safety functions, such as through credited actions in mitigating plant events.
 2) Small advanced reactors with greater automation than LLWRs but not autonomous. These may

 will likely be some onsite personnel to support daily operations with operators acting as backup

 4.2. Introduction to Human Factors Considerations for Autonomous
 Systems
 While Table 6 presents two generalized ConOps categorizations for advanced reactor designs for the
purpose of summarizing the concepts identified via the landscape analysis, the main source of variability
for human factors implications stems from levels of autonomy. Generally, the smaller and simpler the
reactor concept, the higher the expected level of autonomy in the design and the lower the expected level
of human involvement. Most designs call for some level of human intervention during abnormal and
emergency conditions, which are more cognitively burdensome than normal operations. For the small
advanced reactor concepts, there may be greater automation used to perform plant functions (e.g.,
reactivity control) across different plant conditions (e.g., normal or off-normal) compared to LLWRs, but
the human role may be more manual in nature compared to the microreactor concepts. With this, the
personnel responsible for reactor control may be located onsite, yet with potentially fewer staff compared
to LLWRs. Here we present a brief introduction to human factors considerations that have been identified
as most relevant to the anticipated ConOps identified in the landscape analysis. For the most part, these
are perennial human factors issues that exist when humans are required to work within any type of highly
automated environments. We consider both prospective designs and hypothetical representations and
suggest potential treatment considerations across the ConOps categorizations.
 The relevant human factors considerations for new ConOps with high levels of automation and
autonomy are listed below. We referenced NUREG/CR-7126 "Human-Performance Issues Related to the
Design and Operation of Small Modular Reactors" (O’Hara, Higgins, and Pena, 2012) because this report
addresses the unique human factors and performance considerations essential for the successful
deployment and operation of SMRs, and there are parallels with autonomous concepts, for example in the
areas of mission, safety features, staffing and training, and multi-site considerations.
 1) Simple designs with passive safety features and inherent safety characteristics may have fewer
 actions that need to be carried out in general, which may reduce the number of actions that a
 human must take, even without automation. The lack of human involvement may have
 implications for skill and knowledge retention and situation awareness.
 2) Multi-unit sites that may be supporting multiple missions at once, or be in different operational
 states, will require careful consideration for how training, procedures, and HSIs will support
 adequate situation awareness (NUREG/CR-7126 Sections 6.2.1 Multi-unit Operations and
 Teamwork and 6.4.9 HSI Design for Multi-unit Monitoring and Control; O’Hara, Higgins, and
       Pena, 2012).
 3) The specific responsibilities of personnel will need to be determined. This includes what specific
 authority personnel will have if automation fails or what specific maintenance tasks are needed to
 ensure reliable operation of passive systems. The roles and responsibilities for personnel in each
 plant design will inform staffing levels (including considerations for teaming), qualifications,
 training, procedures, and HSIs needed to safely and reliably monitor and control these plants.
 4) For designs with high levels of autonomy, vigilance decrement is a concern (e.g., Sheridan,
 2002). Training, procedures and HSIs will need to be carefully considered to address vigilance
 concerns along with situation awareness.
 5) Staffing levels will need to consider workload demands needed for safely and reliably
 monitoring and controlling multiple plants, which may be in different operational conditions and
 supporting different missions (NUREG/CR-7126 Sections 6.3.2 Staffing Models & 6.3.3 Staffing
 Levels; O’Hara, Higgins, and Pena, 2012).
 6) There may be new staffing positions required to support new missions, perform tasks onsite,
 troubleshoot automation, or maintain passive systems. Such positions should be defined through

 45

 determining the roles and responsibilities of personnel and automation (NUREG/CR-7126
 Section 6.3.1 New Staffing Positions; O’Hara, Higgins, and Pena, 2012). The roles and
 responsibilities of personnel and their interaction with automation will need to consider impacts
 to teaming.
7) Depending on the roles and responsibilities of personnel (e.g., to provide backup support for
 automation), training programs may need to ensure personnel develop and retain competencies
 in the automation itself to safely and reliably monitor and control the plant. Training should
 ensure personnel have adequate mental models of the algorithms and processes used by the
 automation to know when intervening is necessary; this consideration therefore applies to
 ensuring adequate situation awareness and calibrated trust.
8) With higher levels of automation and minimal active control required by personnel, there may be
 greater demands on trust in automation. Depending on the efficacy of the training, procedures,
 and HSIs, there can be risk of mis-calibrated trust (e.g., over trust, also known as complacency)
 in automation due to less experience afforded by personnel in actively performing tasks manually
 on a routine basis.
9) For advanced reactors that place personnel in a more active role, there may be workload
 considerations, especially if personnel are supervising multiple units under different operating
 conditions or supporting multiple missions. For instance, there may be increases in mental
 workload during times where the operator is required to perform load following tasks on one or
 more units while also monitoring the performance of other units performing automated functions.
10) For ConOps that place personnel remotely and geographically distant from the reactor, loss of
 communication between the control facility and HSIs with the reactor may have serious
 consequences to personnel’s ability to restore the reactor to a safe state in the event automated
 functions require human intervention. The ConOps should consider situations in which there may
 be a loss in automated safety function and a loss in communication to the HSIs and facility.
 Failures like this may impact situation awareness, workload, and may lead to novel human
 errors.
11) If AI features become embedded into plant functions like surveillances, it will be important to
 understand the impacts of its reliability, such as how AI features influence personnel trust.
12) There is limited function allocation guidance to support the review of automation decisions with
 higher levels of automation that may be potentially adaptable or adaptive depending on certain
 plant conditions, as well as considering passive systems (NUREG/CR-7126 Section 6.2.3
 Function Allocation Methodology to Support Automation Decisions; O’Hara, Higgins, and Pena,
 2012).
13) Resiliency and recovery from unexpected operational conditions must be explicitly considered
 in autonomous and highly automated designs. In scenarios where automation fails, degrades, or
 encounters unanticipated conditions, operators may be required to rapidly re-engage with the
 system and restore it to a safe state. This can be particularly challenging if operators have had
 limited involvement in routine operations, leading to reduced familiarity or skill degradation.
 Human-system interfaces, procedures, and training programs should be designed to support
 effective re-engagement, including clear pathways for manual control, diagnostic support tools,
 and scenario-based training that emphasizes recovery from automation failures. These
 considerations are essential for ensuring that personnel can respond effectively under time
 pressure or uncertainty, and that the system as a whole remains resilient to unexpected events.

the relevant human factors concepts that must be considered for safe, efficient, and reliable operations.
We now offer a deeper analysis of each of the human factors considerations under examination in sub-
sections 4.2.1 – 4.2.7 and importantly, offer insights for human factors concepts as a function of our

4.2.1. Situation Awareness
 Situation awareness (SA) refers to how people proactively allocate their limited attentional resources
to understand what is going on around them (Endsley, 2000). SA has become one of the most important
topics of human factors in recent decades with the advent of advanced automated systems, especially in
process control industries such as nuclear power operations. This is because nuclear plants present an
environment in which multiple units of information are in flux simultaneously, each with corresponding
demands on attention.
 Compared to traditional operations, reductions in SA along with vigilance decrement are likely the
most critical considerations for near-autonomous and highly automated nuclear power operations. This is
because against a backdrop of automated control functions that typically require little or no prior
engagement, the demands for real-time decisions when malfunctions occur (i.e., the human is used as the
failsafe) produce a cognitive burden that can overwhelm the operator (Warm, Dember, and Hancock,
2019). Humans are ill-suited for signal detection (i.e., monitoring), and it is well documented that
vigilance decrement occurs as quickly as 10 minutes for personnel who are monitoring system behaviors
and do not have any active tasks to perform (e.g., Dring, 2018; Sheridan, 2002). Automation is typically
better suited to handle tedious, long-duration monitoring tasks while also mitigating errors that may stem
from fatigue, distraction, or cognitive overload. This provides the benefit of freeing operators to focus on
higher-level supervisory functions that may be necessary for safety and to improve overall plant
performance. Additional demands to SA will occur for advanced ConOps that require operators to
monitor multiple units, support multiple missions at once, or be in different operational states.
 To ensure acceptable levels of SA, the plant-wide distribution of automated and human-performed
tasks should be evaluated as part of the design and testing process through function allocation and task
analysis. For example, some small advanced reactor designs are considering automation for long-term
monitoring tasks, such as those exceeding 20 minutes, due to the known vigilance decrement effects in
human operators. Given that most control functions for reactor concepts on the microreactor end of the
spectrum are planned to occur without human involvement, maintaining SA for these reactors may prove
to be more difficult than with designs closer to small advanced reactors. However, from a regulatory
standpoint, a microreactor design that provides adequate defense in depth without the reliance on
significant human actions necessary to ensure safety (i.e., credited operator actions) poses less risk and
therefore there may be less overall impact of reduced SA. Last, for any autonomous concept, well-
designed automation with corresponding high-usability HSIs can enhance SA by synthesizing multi-
source information and highlighting critical parameters.
4.2.2. Trust and Automation Reliability
 Trust is a key determinant in an operator’s willingness to rely on automated processes (Dzindolet et
al., 2003). This is because should automation fail, this can have significant negative implications, and
nuclear operators bear legal responsibility for any incidents, with or without any automation. Reliability
of the system is an important determinant of trust, formed via repeated successful use and demonstration.
A great deal of research points to lower reliability producing less user trust and dependence on the
automation (Madhavan and Wiegmann, 2007; Wickens and Dixon, 2007; Wilkison, Fisk, and Rogers,
2007).
 Trust and automation reliability are challenging issues for near-autonomous or highly automated
operations because there is currently no OE to draw from, and no proven track record to ensure

 47

performance or reliability. Higher levels of automation with minimal active control required by personnel
will place greater demands on automation trust. Automation is imperfect and depending on the efficacy of
the training, procedures, and HSIs, there may be an increased risk of miscalibrated trust (i.e., over- or
under-trust) in automation due to less experience afforded by personnel that actively perform tasks
manually. However, unwarranted distrust in automation may lead operators to intervene inappropriately
and cause errors. Appropriate reliance produces the optimal human-system outcomes whereas over-trust
or distrust can lead to failures and accidents. Last, if AI features become embedded into plant functions, it
will be important to understand how these AI features influence personnel trust. For instance, some
reactor designs including novel ConOps aspects like refueling while remaining online, may use AI for on-
line monitoring and predictive maintenance. Ensuring that personnel trust is appropriately calibrated
based on the reliability of these systems will likely be essential for these novel situations.
 Concepts closer to microreactors may require a higher level of trust compared to small advanced
reactors because they incorporate more autonomous features reducing the need for human involvement.
However, microreactors with simpler designs that emphasize passive and inherent safety features are
likely to use less control automation than reactor designs closer to small advanced reactors. Consequently,
these designs may necessitate less trust due to the reduced reliance on active automation.
4.2.3.      Workload
 Workload refers to the mental demand put on human operators. Automation is typically designed with
the goal of lessening human workload but there can be unintended consequences on mental load
(Parasuraman and Mouloua, 1996). The application of HFE during function requirement analysis and
function allocation can help achieve desired outcomes and guard against both underloading (which can
result in boredom, reduced attention and a loss of SA), and overloading should the automation fail to
create its intended effect, and humans must compensate (Czaja and Nair, 2012). With increased use of
automation in nuclear operations, workload demands shift from operators possessing a deep, intimate
knowledge of the plant systems, to needing a deep knowledge of the automation systems and
corresponding interface.
 The main take-away regarding workload burdens in highly automated and near-autonomous
environments can be summarized as concerns around expectations of operators to troubleshoot high-level,
complex and interconnected system failures after little direct engagement with the systems. This may be
exacerbated if personnel are supervising multiple units under different operating conditions and
potentially supporting multiple missions. For instance, there may be increases in mental workload during
times when the operator is required to perform load following tasks on one or more units while also
monitoring the performance of other units performing automated functions. Simple designs with passive
safety features and inherent safety characteristics may have fewer actions that need to be carried out in
general, which may reduce the number of actions that a human must take even without automation. There
is limited function allocation guidance to support the workload review of automation decisions with
higher levels of automation that may be potentially adaptable or adaptive depending on certain plant
conditions, as well as considering passive systems (NUREG/CR-7126 Section 6.2.3; O’Hara, Higgins,
and Pena, 2012).
 Higher levels of autonomy for concepts more aligned with microreactors means that operator
workload will be reduced during normal operations compared to small advanced reactors which may
present challenges with system engagement and SA. Additionally, while the HSI design for microreactor
concepts will primarily support effective interaction with multiple automated systems, for concepts more
aligned with small advanced reactors, the HSI will likely need to balance manual controls with
automation to ensure the operators can readily switch between different control modes.

 48

4.2.4.      Teaming
 Teaming involves human-human, human-system, and system-system interactions to achieve desired
outcomes. The success of the current civil fleet is more than a function of technological innovation and
technical skills. It is also a result of highly successful human-human teaming such as shared SA,
teamwork, leadership, and communication (Wang et al., 2020), that together have afforded dependable
normal operations and reduced the risk of human error. Human-autonomy teaming research is still in its
infancy and limited, and all the different ways that humans will use fast-paced emerging autonomous
technologies is unknown (O’Neill et al., 2020).
 While the current commercial fleet of reactors rely on crews of human operators to control the plant,
new plant designs with high automation or near-autonomy will involve radically different forms of
teaming. Generating “symbiosis” and designing cooperative automation will make clear precisely what
each team agent will contribute (human or digital), how they will work together, and how they will evolve
together (Bruni, Freiman, and Riddle, 2023). The human operator should be able to anticipate what the
automation will do next, and the automation should anticipate and facilitate operator decisions. A shift
from a crew to sole human operator in a team may add undesirable pressure or weight to the roles and
responsibilities of the operator. For ConOps that place personnel remotely and geographically distant
from the reactor, a loss of communication between the control facility and HSIs with the reactor may
impact personnel’s ability to restore the reactor in a safe state in the event automated functions require
human intervention. The ConOps should consider situations in which there may be a loss in automated
safety function and a loss in communication to the HSIs and facility.
 Compared to concepts closer to small advanced reactors, concepts more aligned with microreactors
will likely require higher degrees of system-system teaming than human-system teaming, and smaller
crews anticipated for microreactors may place an additional workload burden on decision-making during
abnormal events.
4.2.5.      Training
 Training serves to harmonize the relationship between the human user and the technology (Popov et
al., 2021). Given that ConOps with levels of autonomy presents a departure from current operational
procedures, this too means that training programs will necessarily change. Under current conditions,
operators are required to have periodic continuing (requalification) training, which includes operating and
written exams, in accordance with their approved training program (per regulation 10 CFR 50.120 and 10
CFR 55.59). Training requirements will be determined by a top-down process. First, plant designers must
consider the mission of the facility and its design simplicity to accomplish the mission. Second, this will
bound the safety functions, which will in turn determine the roles and responsibilities that must be
fulfilled. Last, the tasks assigned to the humans will inform the staffing qualifications, which will
determine the training program required. Consistent with current light water reactor training programs,
personnel should have adequate mental models of the algorithms and processes used by the automation to
know when intervening is necessary; this consideration therefore applies to ensuring adequate situation
awareness and calibrated trust with autonomous systems.
 With new ConOps, training requirements may either increase or decrease depending on a variety of
factors including simplicity of design, whether control room simulation training is necessary (i.e., some
microreactor designs may not contain an on-site control room), and the population to be trained. It is
feasible that total time training is similar for microreactors and small advanced reactors concepts, but that
the training content is qualitatively different. For example, compared to small advanced reactors, concepts
closer to microreactors may require extensive digital automation training, recognition of automation
failures and corresponding cyber training. For small advanced reactors, more emphasis may be placed on
emergency intervention training. Another important training consideration entails the cross-training of
personnel. Training programs will need to consider the cognitive aspects of different jobs and tasks when
being combined to be fulfilled by a specific role. Such cognitive aspects that may need to be addressed

 49

with training include the necessary education and prerequisite knowledge, regulatory requirements
associated with a job’s position, number of tasks, degree of cognitive complexity, and risks. In addition,
training for experienced nuclear ROs may need to account for interference effects when learning new
ConOps. Conversely, new generations who are digital natives may be more comfortable with automation
but may need to focus more on retention of nuclear systems knowledge in training.
4.2.6. Skill and Knowledge Retention
 Retaining job skills and knowledge is a critical consideration because any move toward autonomy
will coincide with a move away from human skills and experience related to the automated tasks. The
human factors literature has documented for several decades the fact that experts can quickly become
novices when shifted from manual-based job roles to supervising highly automated environments
(McLeod, 2022).
 Retaining job skills and knowledge is a critical consideration because any move toward autonomy
will coincide with a move away from human skills and experience related to the automated tasks. Skills
decay for any new personnel roles with levels of autonomy will have to be understood and mitigated.
Additionally, less engagement with plant operations may mean that systems are not known as well by the
operators. This runs counter to using humans as ‘failsafes’ in which intimate system knowledge must be
achieved and maintained. One of the most important ingredients for nuclear power systems safety is a
human possessing expert knowledge of the system.
 In comparison to small advanced reactors, skill and knowledge retention may be more challenging for
concepts more closely aligned with microreactors, due to their reduced complement of staff and fewer
human actions. Further, some advanced reactor concepts may operate for longer periods before requiring
to refuel, which may have implications to skill and knowledge retention resulting from reducing task
frequency. In addition, given the scaled down workforce, it may be the case that less individuals will be
hired and trained to possess the requisite knowledge, with fewer expertise training pipelines. Those that
do become a single point of failure should they retire, resign, pass away, or become absent for any reason.
Similarly, unlike traditional plants with decades of OE, developers of ConOps with higher levels of
automation and autonomy may not yet be fully aware how users will optimally interact with the system,
and this may result in a very few number of persons that have an understanding of how the system works.
4.2.7. Novel Human Error Types
 New advanced reactor ConOps with higher levels of autonomy will mean that there are new human-
system interactions and new HFE considerations that have not yet been observed, affording opportunities
to make new mistakes. Insights can be gained from previous examples, such as use of computer-based
procedures introducing new fault modalities not present in traditional paper-based procedures. These
include the human operator failing to complete tasks successfully when the system is degraded, and
failures to recover from input errors to the system (Boring, Gertman, and Le Blanc, 2011). Further, the
decades of OE for existing ConOps will not apply, and trial-and-error is not a learning tool widely
employed in nuclear power operations.
 While design simplicity may be one of the key attributes of ConOps with higher levels of automation
and autonomy, some systems will become more complex, especially if AI is present. Together, this will
bring about novel human errors with interactivity. Boring et al. (2022) identified an absence of predictive
analytical tools for advanced technologies, including important human factors considerations such as
vigilance decrement, skills loss and human-machine teaming. They also stated that safety predictions for
autonomous AI are needed. Taken together, there is a need for tools that might help identify the risk
associated with novel human error types in new technologies with higher levels of autonomy.
 Unexpected behaviors of automated systems such as unclear decision states present new ways
humans are required to respond; while automation may be generally good enough that it decreases human
engagement, out-of-the-loop performance represents a new type of human error such as delayed

 50

responding (i.e., the human may be slower to respond and less able to assess automation edge cases). This
is especially important for time-critical actions which are actions requiring rapid response—typically
within seconds to minutes—to prevent or mitigate accident progression such as reactor trips, emergency
cooling system activation, station blackouts or containment isolation (Sidelinger, 2007). This can be
addressed with careful thought put into the tasking, HSIs, and training, however these considerations are
not yet documented in publicly available materials for most designs. Further, situations in which a human
is needed as backup are likely to be the most complex and difficult to plan for, further complicating the
process of effectively preparing the human to act as backup.
 It is feasible that concepts more aligned with small advanced reactors, that largely employ operators
to act in backup and failsafe modes during off-normal and emergency situations, present more
opportunities for novel human errors, because one key overarching gap is a clear description of what a
human operator’s role and authority will be when they serve in this function. Concepts closer to
microreactors and that employ passive and inherent safety features may have less complications in this
regard. The human automation interaction literature and experience from other industries underscore that
when operators do not have an active role in day-to-day operations, they may struggle to maintain
vigilance and situation awareness. This can be addressed with careful thought put into the tasking, HSIs,
and training; however, these considerations are not yet documented in publicly available materials for
most designs.

 5. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION
 The purpose of this document was to assess the degree to which reactor designers and vendors plan to
incorporate autonomy and high levels of automation into advanced reactor designs and to identify human
factors considerations that the NRC will need to be ready to assess. Based on a review of publicly
available information including designer websites, promotional materials, publicly available
presentations, and preapplication documents, there are several general themes and conclusions related to
autonomy and several human factors considerations that emerge.
 First, very few of the designs have descriptions in publicly available material that are detailed enough
to comprehensively identify the human factors related to planned autonomy. While many concepts had
high level descriptions of the general level of automation, most did not break down the plant’s functions
to a level of detail beyond the high level ConOps dimensions, nor did they describe the human and
automation agent’s authorities and cognitive functions that they perform. This is expected, given the stage
of development that many of these designs are in. For systems that have levels of automation that utilize
shared functions to a large degree, it will be necessary to see more detailed breakdowns of plant functions
to assess the human factors issues related to agent’s roles and responsibilities. It should be noted that
NRC is currently developing guidance to help clarify expectations in these areas, such as guidance for
submittals related to the ConOps and function allocation requirements associated with the proposed 10
CFR Part 53 rulemaking.
 Second, none of the designs reviewed explicitly stated that they plan to have a design completely
unattended by human operators, but several anticipate high levels of autonomy. Many concepts describe
near autonomous operation, with the reactor systems being mostly automated and human operators
monitor and only intervene when necessary. A couple of concepts did not have enough detail to fully
evaluate their level of autonomy and how they plan to be monitored or supervised by human operators.
 The microreactors had simpler designs and were more likely to plan for near autonomy than the small
advanced reactors. All the concepts, regardless of size, described that they were achieving high levels of
autonomy largely through simplicity, passive safety features and inherent safety characteristics. The
small, advanced reactors were more likely to have high automation compared to LLWRs but still allocate
many normal and abnormal functions to be shared between humans and automation. Most concepts
described their safety functions as not requiring human intervention, and many described that they also
did not require active control.
 Most concepts included new missions in their ConOps (e.g., providing steam or heat instead or in
addition to electricity) and new applications (e.g., load following, microgrid, supporting integrated
systems or industrial processes). With a few exceptions, there was very little detail on how new missions
and applications would be managed by personnel. Issues like managing multiple outputs and coordination
with external systems were not described in detail for most concepts. Only one concept reviewed included
AI/ML as part of their ConOps, and none planned to use AI/ML for control automation. The one concept
that planned to use AI/ML described using it for online monitoring and maintenance decisions. Several
other concepts described using predictive maintenance, which will likely partly include data-driven
automation methods like AI/ML but did not explicitly state so.
 Though several human factors issues emerged and are summarized in Section 4 and in Table 6, one
key overarching gap is a clear description of what a human operator’s role and authority will be when
they serve a monitoring and “backup” function. The human automation interaction literature and
experience from other industries underscore that maintaining vigilance and situation awareness is a
challenge when humans are not actively engaged in the operation of a system. Further, situations in which
a human is needed as backup are likely to be the most complex and difficult to plan for, further
complicating the process of effectively preparing the human to act as backup. This challenge should be
addressed with care through function allocation, task analysis, design of HSIs, training programs, and
human performance monitoring.
