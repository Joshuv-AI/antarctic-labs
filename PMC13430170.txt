Recognizing the azygos lobe: A case series detailing presentations and clinical implications
Authors: Salma Alkhatib, Diego Marin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Discovery of an azygos lobe is an infrequently encountered anomaly that can be recognized on chest imaging, occurring in approximately 1.2% of patients. The azygos lobe is formed from abnormal migration of the azygos vein, leading to the formation of an azygos fissure and corresponding azygos lobe. This variant is asymptomatic, however, it has clinical implications because of its ability to be mistaken as various pulmonary pathologies. Recognition of this rare finding is essential to prevent misdiagnosis, and to avoid complications during thoracic procedures such as vascular injury. This case series details 3 presentations of an incidental azygos lobe, identifying the presentation, and describing the clinical and surgical significance of its discovery.

Introduction

An azygos lobe is a rare, often incidentally found, anatomical variant which results from the anomalous course of the azygos vein during embryological development. In the general population, the estimated prevalence is approximately 1.2%, with increasing detection due to modern computed tomography (CT) scans [1]. During embryological development, the azygos vein typically courses over the apex of the right lung and enters the mediastinum. If this development fails, the azygos vein can instead penetrate the upper portion of the right lung, forming an abnormal pleural fold called the mesoazygos which leads to the creation of the azygos fissure [2]. Due to this, a small portion of lung parenchyma becomes isolated, and is referred to as the azygos lobe. The azygos lobe, however, is not a true pulmonary lobe because it notably lacks its own bronchus and segmental anatomy [3].

Azygos lobes are typically asymptomatic and found incidentally on chest imaging. Recognition of this anatomical variant is important, however, as it may be mistaken for pathological imaging findings such as pulmonary nodules, bullae, or pneumothorax [2]. Additionally, surgeons must have awareness of this anomaly, as there is an increased risk of vascular injury if the azygos vein is not recognized prior to thoracic procedures such as lung resections [4]. Clinicians should therefore be aware of this anatomical variant to prevent potential complications. We present 3 cases of incidentally identified azygos lobes, emphasizing their clinical presentation and relevance in practice.

Case presentation

Case 1

A 64-year-old male presented to the clinic with a 3-week history of a cough. He had a past medical history including hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and an umbilical hernia. Subsequent workup included a CT with contrast, which revealed slight coronary artery calcifications but was otherwise unremarkable for pulmonary nodules or consolidations. Additionally, an incidental azygos lobe and fissure was apparent, as seen in Fig. 1. The thin, curvilinear white line formed by the azygos fissure is seen extending from the apex of the right lung to the level of the azygos arch. The azygos fissure transverses medially and extends to the mediastinum, characteristic of a type C azygos lobe morphology. The lung has no associated volume loss or architectural distortion. Case 2

A 79-year-old female presented to the hospital with altered mental status, difficulty swallowing, and repeated emesis. She had a past medical history of cerebrovascular accident, deep vein thrombosis, Alzheimer dementia, and a hip fracture. She developed acute hypoxemic respiratory failure and was ultimately admitted and required intubation. Investigative work-up included a chest CT, which revealed patchy consolidative and ground-glass opacities in the right upper and lower lung, consistent with multifocal pneumonia and/or aspiration. Additionally, an incidental finding of the azygos fissure was recognized, as illustrated in Fig. 2. CT imaging revealed the characteristic white, curvilinear azygos fissure vertically oriented and traveling to the mediastinum. Case 3

A 59-year-old male was seen in the pulmonary clinic for follow-up of a recent chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) exacerbation. His past medical history was notable for COPD and Grave’s disease. Due to his smoking history, a CT scan was ordered during his work-up, which revealed an azygos lobe, as demonstrated in Fig. 3. The white, curvilinear appearance of the azygos fissure travels vertically from the mediastinal aspect of the right upper lung and extends superiorly, indicative of a type C azygos lobe. There is no associated volume loss or architectural distortion, supporting an incidental finding. Discussion

The azygos lobe represents an anatomical variant resulting from abnormal embryological development of the azygos vein. Notably lacking its own bronchial and segmental anatomy, the azygos lobe is not a true accessory lobe. In this case series, the 3 azygos lobes were identified incidentally through CT scans which were performed for unrelated conditions. This is consistent with existing literature demonstrating that azygos lobes are typically asymptomatic and are an incidental finding [1]. In all instances, the azygos lobe had no clinical manifestations and required no management or intervention.

There are 3 variants of azygos lobes which have been described in the literature, depending on morphology of the fissure. Type A is seen as a horizontal fissure, cutting the lateral portion of the lung between the apex and a point about 2 cm above. Type B presents as a vertical fissure dividing the apex into 2 halves. Type C is a vertical fissure that starts from the mediastinal part of the lung and travels to the upper edge of the root of the lung [5]. The most frequently encountered fissure morphology is type A, followed by type B, and then type C [6]. In this case series, all patients presented with type C morphologies.

Recognition of this anatomical variant is critical during radiological interpretation of imaging. On CT imaging, abnormal migration of the azygos vein forms the azygos fissure which presents as a white, curvilinear division from the lung apex to the mediastinum, separating the azygos lobe from the rest of the right upper lobe. Additionally, the azygos lobe typically has no associated parenchymal damage or architectural changes. The azygos lobe requires accurate identification as it may be misinterpreted for various pathologies such as pulmonary nodules, bullae, abscesses, or pneumothorax [2]. CT imaging of pulmonary nodules is distinct from an azygos lobe as nodules characteristically present as a discrete, rounded opacity within the lung parenchyma, with no associated fissure or vascular anomaly [7]. Notably, nodules can present anywhere within the lung parenchyma while the azygos lobe is confined to the right upper lobe. Pulmonary bullae can be distinguished from azygos lobes on CT by their appearance as air-filled, subpleural cystic spaces measuring greater than 1 cm with thin walls. Unlike an azygos lobe, pulmonary bullae lack an azygos fissure and are often associated with emphysematous changes [8]. CT findings of a pneumothorax characteristically demonstrate pleural air accumulation, leading to separation of the visceral pleural from the parietal pleura [9]. The pathognomonic finding of the azygos lobe is visualization of the azygos vein coursing within an accessory azygos fissure in the right upper lobe. In all these cases, the proper identification of the azygos lobe on CT using its characteristic imaging features ensured accurate diagnosis and prevented unnecessary workup.

A study conducted in Jordan evaluated the awareness and recognition of this anatomical variant among residents at a teaching institution. Of the 227 surgical and radiology residents included in the study, only half of the radiologists could correctly report the finding, with none of the other residents recognizing the anomaly [10]. Although the study had limitations such as location and sample size that restricted its generalizability, the findings still suggest a lack of awareness of the azygos lobe among medical trainees, emphasizing the necessity for greater exposure and education regarding this anatomical variant.

Clinically, awareness of the azygos lobe is particularly relevant for thoracic procedures. The anomalous course of the azygos vein may lead to increased risk of vascular injury if not properly identified and documented prior to thoracic procedures such as lung resections or video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery [1]. Thorough review of imaging preoperatively is critical to maintain patient safety and prevent potential complications.

Overall, this case series emphasizes the importance of recognizing the azygos lobe as a benign anatomical variant with potential implications for imaging interpretation and procedural planning. Improved awareness of this anomaly among clinicians can help prevent misdiagnosis, reduce unnecessary workup, and ensure patient safety and optimize patient care.

Conclusion

An uncommon and generally benign anatomical variant, the azygos lobe is most frequently encountered incidentally during imaging. All 3 cases within this series presented an asymptomatic azygos lobe that was unrelated to the initial condition, and none required additional treatment or management. These cases highlight the importance of recognizing the azygos lobe during clinical practice and describe the significance in proper diagnosis preoperatively to reduce complications. Greater awareness and familiarity of this variant among clinicians can help prevent misinterpretation, avoid unnecessary diagnostic interventions, and minimize the risk of procedural complications.
