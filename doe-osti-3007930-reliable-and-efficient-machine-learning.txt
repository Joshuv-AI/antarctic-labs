 Abstract/Excutive Summary
 Modern scientific experiments generate massive amounts of data at a pace
much faster than humans can manually analyze. While machine learning has
revolutionized commercial data analysis (such as recommending movies or rec-
ognizing faces), applying these tools to complex scientific discovery is challeng-
ing because scientific answers must be precise, interpretable, and adhere to
physical laws. The research under this project aims to develop new mathe-
matical tools and computer algorithms specifically designed for scientific appli-
cations. Major progress has been made in automatically cleaning and decon-
structing messy experimental data, analyzing the visual information of physical
phenomena, determining the underlying physical variables, and providing rig-
orous mathematical analysis of interesting algorithms and concepts widely used
in machine learning.
 This project addressed the critical gap between our ability to generate mas-
sive scientific data and our ability to extract interpretable information from
it. We established mathematical foundations for Scientific Machine Learn-
ing (SciML) aimed at effective data analytics and automated discovery. Our
work focused on three core objectives: (1) developing reliable feature extrac-
tion methods for dynamic high-dimensional data, (2) establishing mathematical
foundations for discovering dynamics via neural networks, and (3) creating rig-
orous optimization techniques for these models.
 Key outcomes come from two fronts. On the practical side, they include
the development of algorithms that significantly enhance the extraction of sig-
nals from field data, as well as the capability to handle situations that exhibit
smooth variations or physical stretching due to temperature changes. They also
include the creation of an automated framework for discovering fundamental
state variables from raw experimental data, demonstrating the ability to iden-
tify intrinsic physical dimensions without prior knowledge of the governing laws.
On the theoretical front, the research results in theoretical advances in Optimal
Transport, a widely used notion in SciML, specifically regarding functions with
fixed-size nodal sets, provide sharp bounds relevant to uncertainty quantifica-
tion. Meanwhile, the outcomes also include the establishment of convergence
theories for nonlocal gradient descent methods, enabling robust optimization
with noisy data in high-dimensional settings commonly encountered in scientific
modeling.
 The project also helps creating opportunities to train the next generation
of researchers, equipping them with the necessary technical skills for today’s
workplace and preparing them for future advances.

2.1 Advanced Feature Extraction: Non-Negative Matrix Factorization
 (NMF) for Scientific Data
Background and Significance Non-Negative Matrix Factorization (NMF) is a pow-
erful tool for decomposing mixed signals, such as separating the diffraction patterns of
individual chemical components from a mixture. However, standard NMF assumes that
the signal of a component remains identical throughout an experiment. In real-world sci-
entific experiments, such as temperature ramps, this assumption fails. Materials expand
with heat (that is, thermal expansion), causing their diffraction peaks to shift or ”stretch.”
Standard NMF interprets this stretching as a new, incorrect chemical component. Devel-
oping algorithms that respect these physical constraints is critical for accurate real-time
data analysis at user facilities.

Two-Stage Algorithm for Smoothly Varying Data Publication: A fast two-stage
algorithm for non-negative matrix factorization in smoothly varying data (Acta Cryst. A,
2023)[3].
 We addressed the challenge of analyzing time-series or temperature-series data where
changes occur gradually.
 − Methodology: We developed a ”Two-Stage” algorithm designed for dense data
grids. The first stage employs an alternating non-negative least-squares (ANLS) frame-
work, utilizing an active set method and a warm-start strategy to rapidly approximate the
solution. The second stage employs an interior point method to refine the solution and
accelerate local convergence.
 − Findings: Benchmark tests on both synthetic and real-world diffraction data demon-
strated that this two-stage approach achieves higher precision and computational efficiency
compared to existing algorithms like NENMF. It effectively utilizes the ”smoothly varying”
nature of the data to constrain the solution space.
 In short, our algorithm is more effective in obtaining high-precision solutions. More-
over, our algorithm has the potential to be applicable to more problems through suitable
extension using several variants of NMF, which could be further investigated in the future.

Stretched NMF for Physical Signal Variability Publication: Stretched non-negative
matrix factorization (npj Computational Materials, 2024).[4].
 In this work, we address a more complex challenge that arises when the physical sig-
nals themselves deform (stretch) along an independent axis (e.g., 2θ in diffraction) due to
changing environmental conditions.

 5

 − Methodology: We introduced STRETCHEDNMF, a novel model that incorporates a
”stretching factor matrix” into the factorization. This allows every component to have a
unique stretching scale at each time point. We solved this non-convex problem using a
combination of discretization and Block Coordinate Descent algorithms.
 − Findings: The STRETCHEDNMF model successfully disentangled signal variability
caused by thermal expansion from actual compositional changes. An enhanced version,
SPARSE-STRETCHEDNMF, leveraged signal sparsity for accurate data analysis. As an illustra-
tive application, the method was demonstrated to be highly effective in analyzing powder
diffraction data, accurately identifying chemical components even when their signatures
were shifted due to the thermal expansion.

2.2 Automated Discovery of Physical Laws
Publication: Automated discovery of fundamental variables hidden in experimental data
(Nature Computational Science, 2022).[1]

Background and Significance The ultimate goal of scientific discovery is to find the
mathematical laws that govern the universe. However, before deriving a formula, one
must first identify the relevant state variables (e.g., ”force” or ”mass”). Standard machine
learning in physics typically assumes these variables are known. A true ”AI Scientist” must
be able to observe a phenomenon and determine the minimum set of state variables required
to describe it without human intervention. This capability is essential for exploring new
physical systems where the governing physics are unknown.

Unsupervised Variable Discovery from Video We tested the hypothesis that the
intrinsic dimension (ID) and state variables of a dynamical system can be extracted purely
from high-dimensional observational data (video).
 − Methodology: We proposed a data-driven framework that first estimates the in-
trinsic dimension of the data manifold to determine how many variables exist. It then uses
a specifically designed neural network architecture to encode the high-dimensional video
into a low-dimensional latent space that represents these variables.
 − Findings: Our algorithm successfully identified the correct number of state variables
across multiple dynamical systems, such as the angle and angular velocity of a pendulum,
directly from raw pixel data. Crucially, the ”Neural State Variables” it discovered were
shown to be mathematically related to the known physical variables.

2.3 Mathematical Foundations of Optimization and Analysis
Background and Significance The success of SciML methods relies heavily on solving
optimization problems. These problems are often ”non-convex,” filled with local minima
where algorithms can get stuck. In scientific applications, data is also frequently corrupted
by high-frequency noise. To build reliable tools for science, we must provide rigorous
mathematical guarantees that our algorithms will converge to the correct answer despite
these challenges.

Nonlocal Gradient Descent and Gaussian Smoothing Publication: Convergence
analysis for a nonlocal gradient descent method via directional Gaussian smoothing (Com-
putational Optimization and Applications, 2025).[5]
 We investigated methods to help optimization algorithms sense the broader landscape
rather than getting trapped in small, noisy fluctuations. The analysis focused on the Non-
local Gradient Descent method that uses Directional Gaussian Smoothing (DGS). Instead
of following the slope at a single point, this method calculates a smoothed gradient over a

 Our major finding included a rigorous convergence theory that proves this method

Optimal Transport and Uncertainty Quantification Publication: Minimizing
Optimal Transport for Functions with Fixed-Size Nodal Sets (Journal of Nonlinear Sci-
ence, 2023).[2]
 Quantifying error (uncertainty) is as important as finding the answer. We explored some
theoretical questions related to optimal transport, a widely used tool in data analysis and
machine learning. The work targeted some mathematical foundations of the Wasserstein
distance, a metric used to measure the difference between probability distributions, and the
nodal set, which connects to the underlying geometric, functional, and spectral properties.
 More specifically, we studied the minimization of the optimal transport cost for func-

 8

tions involving a fixed number of nodal sets (i.e., zero sets). This provided a precise setup
for studying the connections between the two. We demonstrated that in some cases, the
problem enjoys an explicit and elementary solution for the optimal transport cost on the
line and the circle, which are all non-branching spaces. The solution enables us to further
explore the relationship between the geometric complexity of a function and the cost of
relocating its mass.
 Our complete solution to the OT minimization yields a sharp lower bound for the
transport cost, at least in the simple setting, effectively creating ”uncertainty principles”
for the Wasserstein distance. This work provides the theoretical bedrock for understanding
how geometric constraints in data affect our ability to assess errors in high-dimensional
models. An exciting aspect of the work is that it offers a conjecture for more complex and
multidimensional cases, which may provide new avenues for applications of OT in UQ and
mathematical modeling.

2.4 Discussions on research findings
This project successfully established new mathematical frameworks and computational
tools for analyzing scientific data. By addressing the specific challenges of scientific data,
such as physical constraints (stretching), the need for interpretability (variable discovery),
and the impact of noise and ruggedness of loss landscapes (optimization), we have enhanced
the capability of scientific learning to support genuine scientific discovery. The algorithms
developed are directly applicable to user facility data, and the theoretical results provide
the necessary confidence for their deployment.
