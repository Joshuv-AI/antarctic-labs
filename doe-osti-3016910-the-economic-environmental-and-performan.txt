3. Executive Summary: The Pollinator Habitat Aligned with Solar Energy (PHASE)
project studied the impacts of co-location of pollinator plantings at large-scale solar
facilities (10 MW or larger). The project focused on the ecological benefits, economic
considerations, and PV performance impacts of co-located pollinator vegetation through
field research at seven operating solar sites. Ecological field data were collected over
multiple growing seasons to evaluate trends in pollinator abundance and diversity,
vegetation establishment, and consequent PV performance. This research informed the
development of the Solar Pollinator Vegetation Implementation Manual, the Cost
Comparison Tool, Solar Seed Selection Tool, and Habitat Assessment Module to assist
developers in considering pollinator plantings at large-scale solar facilities. Moreover, to
assess the impact of co-locating pollinator plantings with solar facilities on bird and bat
occupancy and activity at the test sites, acoustic and ultrasonic recorders were installed
to identify the species present based on their vocalizations. This data is being used to
provide insight into the extent to which solar pollinator plantings may be providing
secondary benefits to both local resident bird and bat species, as well as to migratory
birds and bats.

5. Background: The Energy Resources Center (ERC) is an interdisciplinary public
service and research organization within UIC’s College of Engineering that works to
improve energy efficiency and the environment. The ERC facilitated the formation of the
Rights-of-Way as Habitat Working Group (ROWHWG) in 2015 to build capacity for
pollinator conservation across the energy and transportation sectors. Today, more than
400 organizations from the U.S. and Canada are engaged in the ROWHWG, which
provides educational and networking opportunities, leverages expertise and resources
across sectors, and serves as a central point of coordination and information-sharing for
pollinator conservation on energy and transportation lands. Moreover, the ERC
assembled more than 40 energy companies and transportation agencies to develop the
first nationwide Candidate Conservation Agreement with Assurances (CCAA) for the
monarch butterfly. The agreement is unprecedented in its size and cross-sector
participation and is expected to provide millions of acres of pollinator habitat across the
U.S., including at renewable energy facilities. The ERC serves on multiple regional and
national committees as a “go-to” organization for unbiased and accurate information on
energy technologies and habitat conservation on working landscapes.
 The ERC has partnered with the U.S. Department of Energy’s (USDOE) National
Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) and Argonne National Laboratory (Argonne) on
this project to leverage the existing work of the Innovative Site Preparation and Impact
Reductions on the Environment (InSPIRE) program. The InSPIRE program investigates
the agricultural, environmental and economic aspects of solar co-location with
agriculture and pollinator plantings, including: (1) the effects of native vegetation on
beneficial pollinators, (2) the extent of nearby agriculture that could benefit either
directly or indirectly, 1 and (3) the performance of different native vegetation types at
ground-mounted PV installations. 2 To date, InSPIRE field research has focused
primarily on small-scale pollinator plantings at PV facilities less than 5 MW in
Massachusetts, New York, Minnesota, Oregon, Idaho, and Arizona. This project will
expand the research to encompass large-scale solar installations (≥10 MW) in
additional states.
 NREL is the lead organization for the InSPIRE project and a founding member of
the Agriculture and Solar Together: Research Opportunities (ASTRO) network, which
serves as a working group of national labs, non-profits, universities, and solar
developers as part of the InSPIRE program. NREL is a leading research voice on co-
location of agriculture and solar, development of low-impact site preparation guidelines,
and development of pollinator plantings at PV facilities. NREL is well-positioned to
ensure this project builds upon these past successes and utilizes similar modelling and
testing protocols. Similarly, Argonne has extensive expertise evaluating ecosystem
services of pollinator-friendly solar practices as part of the InSPIRE program and avian
interactions at utility-scale PV facilities in collaboration with the Multiagency Avian-Solar

6. Project Objectives: The co-location of pollinator plantings at large-scale solar
facilities has gained interest from energy companies, state and local governments, and
others. Still, there exist barriers to adoption due to uncertain costs, benefits, and
operational risks. In particular, the impact of scale and configuration of pollinator
plantings are not well understood in terms of ecological benefits, installation and
operational costs, and photovoltaic (PV) performance impacts. This project
addresses uncertainties that the solar industry has about co-locating pollinator plantings
at large-scale solar facilities to enable informed decisions on if, when, and how to
incorporate pollinator plantings into solar projects. The team leveraged existing
research, collaborative partnerships, and industry and other stakeholder involvement to
achieve the following objectives: (1) research co-location and scalability of pollinator
plantings at seven solar facilities with capacities greater than or equal to 10 MW, namely
ecological benefits, economic considerations, and PV performance impacts; (2) create
comprehensive implementation guidance and decision tools to assist solar developers
and other stakeholders when considering pollinator plantings at large solar facilities; and
(3) engage solar industry partners and share findings with broader industry
stakeholders. Deliverables include the Solar Pollinator Vegetation Implementation
Manual, the Cost Comparison Tool, the Solar Seed Selection Tool, and the Habitat
Assessment Module. These tools are available in the online information hub and have

 Page 5 of 20

been distributed to the solar industry, conservation partners, state and local
governments, and other stakeholders through presentations at industry conferences
and meetings, webinars, and academic publications.
 The project aimed to study the ecological benefits, economic considerations, and
PV performance impacts of co-locating pollinator plantings at large-scale solar facilities
through field research at seven operating solar sites across the Midwest in Illinois,
Indiana, Michigan, and Wisconsin.
 Pollinator plantings were introduced at four solar facility sites during the first year
of the study and the remaining sites in Year 2. Trends in ecological impacts and PV
performance were monitored throughout the entire project, but pollinator plantings can
take up to 4 years to fully establish and provide maximal ecosystem benefits, even with
best management practices. For the pollinator planting sites, we treated data collected
during Year 1 as control data to compare to Year 2 and Year 3 data collected when the
plantings had become more established. For comparison, the ecological observation
team surveyed agriculture reference sites in similar regions to the ecovoltaics sites. The
ecological team also observed restored prairies in the Midwest as an optimal habitat
case.
Ecological Evaluation
This project studied the ecological effects on the diversity and abundance of native
pollinator insects, birds, and bats associated with co-locating pollinator plantings at
large-scale solar facilities. We found that where pollinator plantings are established at
solar facility test sites, the observed abundance of pollinator insects and bats increased
over time. We also found that there was an increased diversity of pollinator insects and
birds.
For all of the solar facility test sites, we coordinated with site partners regarding planting
seed mixes and the site planting configurations. The solar facility test site managers
were responsible for planting and maintaining the pollinator plantings. In addition to
evaluating changes in pollinator diversity and abundance over time at each site,
soybean agricultural reference sites were considered as a baseline. Reference sites in
Champaign County, IL and Cass County, IN are in a similar landscape setting to that of
the solar facility test site, but without pollinator plantings, and are 1 km or farther from
the test site, which is greater than the foraging distance of most native pollinators. Thus,
they are unaffected by solar sites with established habitat. Different sampling methods
capture different types of pollinators, so we used multiple sampling methods to best
capture changes in bee community abundance and diversity. The ecological team
visited each site 4-6 times per growing season, depending on the site-specific ecology
of pollinator insects. During each site visit, the ecological team completed an
observational walk-through of transects, targeted timed hand-net sampling, and setting
of pan traps and/or pitfall traps. At least one of these methods was employed at each
solar facility test site and agricultural reference sites where applicable. The specific
methods and numbers of transects, and locations of transects/equipment are detailed in
each site field research plan.

We conducted pollinator transect research in the soybean fields with pan trap/pitfall trap
following the same protocols used at the solar facilities, once site access agreements
were in place. The goal of this research was to quantify the numbers and types of
pollinators present in the adjacent agricultural fields for comparison.
To study the impact of co-locating pollinator plantings and solar facilities on bird and bat
occurrence at and use of the solar facility test sites, we set up acoustic and ultrasonic
recorders at all sites to identify the types of birds and bats utilizing the sites through
their vocalizations. The acoustic bird monitoring equipment collected species-specific
avian vocalization data year-round, both before and during the establishment of
pollinator plantings. The ultrasonic bat monitoring equipment was used to collect
species-specific bat vocalizations. These data were used to provide insight into the
extent to which solar pollinator plantings may be providing secondary benefits to both
local resident bird and bat species, as well as to migratory birds and bats. Moreover,
opportunistic bird and bat presence surveys were conducted to supplement the
monitoring data by confirming avian/bat presence and identifying species using the
sites.
We coordinated with site partners regarding seed mixes to be planted and the site
planting configurations. In 2021, seed mixes were planted at Solar Farm 2.0, Prairie
Wolf, Point Beach, Logansport, Electric City, and Riverstart. In 2022, seed mixes were
planted at the Bellflower, and Riverstart was re-seeded. In order to study the vegetation
present over time after seeding, data were collected in each distinct seed mix. Each
seed mix had three separate transects for study (one on the northern edge of a panel
row, one on the southern edge of a panel row, and one in between rows). Each transect
included 6-8 quadrats of data collection, evenly spaced throughout the length of the
transect. Quadrats were placed on the ground to define the boundary of observation.
Present plant species were identified and categorized as either part of the original seed
mix or as invasive species. The percentage bare ground cover in the quadrat was
visually assessed. In total, 24 quadrats were studied per distinct seed mix. Data was
recorded on paper and consolidated in online spreadsheets. All monitoring and survey
data on pollinator insects, birds, bats, and percent cover are stored on the secure UIC
ERC server.
Economic Evaluation
This project developed the Solar Pollinator Vegetation Implementation Manual, the Cost
Comparison Tool, Solar Seed Selection Tool and a Habitat Assessment Module to assist
solar developers in making decisions regarding vegetation and pollinator management
on large solar sites.
This research informed the development of the Solar Pollinator Vegetation
Implementation Manual for solar facilities and three online tools to assist developers in
considering pollinator plantings at large-scale solar facilities: the Cost Comparison Tool,
Solar Seed Selection Tool, and Habitat Assessment Module. The Solar Pollinator
Vegetation Implementation Manual is intended to provide a support guide to teams
tasked with planning and implementing pollinator vegetation on utility-scale solar
projects. It was developed by gathering input on content and feedback from the IAG,

 Page 7 of 20

TAG, and other stakeholders. The Manual highlights key constraints for planning
vegetation, goals for vegetation establishment and maintenance, site considerations
from preparation to decommissioning, and guidance for contracting. The Solar Pollinator
Vegetation Implementation Manual was developed by gathering feedback from the IAG,
TAG, and other stakeholders. The Cost Comparison Tool provides an easy-to-use online
system for site owners and developers to evaluate the economic costs and benefits of
including pollinator plantings at a solar facility based on the unique variables of a
specific location and planting configuration. The Solar Seed Selection Tool builds upon a
native plant tool, NativeBrowser, which allows a user to select plants for specific site
conditions based on several factors, including EPA ecoregions, soil moisture, deer
resistance, sun exposure, soil pH, and salt tolerance. Updates to the Solar Seed
Selection Tool include the addition of variables important to solar development, such as
plant height, bloom timing, days to germination, and approximate cost. This tool
incorporates data for approximately 8,000 species of plants for the ecoregions currently
included in Native Browser. This tool is accessible via the project’s online information
hub. The third tool is the Habitat Assessment Module, including solar-specific updates to
apply the pollinator scorecard developed by the Rights-of-Way as Habitat Working
Group (ROWHWG) to solar facilities. The pollinator scorecard has biases toward
vegetation that is not compatible with photovoltaic energy facilities. To address this, the
Habitat Assessment Module provides guidance for evaluating and monitoring the
efficacy of pollinator vegetation practices using a version of the scorecard tailored
specifically to solar photovoltaic land use, helping to identify management challenges
and opportunities. An online information hub contains the Solar Pollinator Vegetation
Implementation Manual, Cost Comparison Tool, Solar Seed Selection Tool, and Habitat
Assessment Module.

PV Performance Evaluation
For all the solar facility test sites, we coordinated with site partners regarding power
production data. System Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) data was obtained from
site partners, containing 5-minute interval current readings at the inverter level over the
study area containing the dataloggers. The PV performance team visited each site in 6-
month intervals. During each site visit, all UIC ERC equipment and sensors were
visually inspected, and sensor readings were collected using PC400 software. From
2021 to 2024, SD card data was collected from onsite Campbell dataloggers. SD card
data was copied to a laptop onsite, verified for completeness, then a blank SD card
replaced the old one. Each site housed two separate dataloggers along the arrays. The
locations of all dataloggers are detailed in each site field research plan and intended to
capture differences in seed mixes. The goal of this research was to quantify the impact
of pollinator plantings on the performance of photovoltaic arrays. With the electrical
current data in Amps and the site specification of voltage at the inverter level, the power
production was calculated. Dataloggers recorded measurements every 5 minutes from
sensors monitoring front and back of module solar irradiance, back of module
temperature, air temperature, relative humidity, and wind speed.

statement of project objective (SOPO) regarding industry advisory group members,

Industry Advisory Group (IAG)
17 IAG and 21 TAG members agreed to participate in the advisory groups and provide
insight on the opportunities and barriers to co-locating solar energy with pollinator
vegetation. The IAG/TAG reviewed research plans for all 7 sites. An outreach plan was
developed and distributed to the IAG leaders and research team for feedback. Yearly
data summaries were presented to the IAG and TAG.

Research Plans
Seven research plans were written incorporating feedback from the IAG/TAG. Each site
has a research plan addressing PV performance, pollinator, avian, bat, and seed mix
research at the solar test facility. Research plans were periodically updated to reflect
activities on site.

Environmental Data Collection
Environmental data equipment was installed at 6 of the 7 solar facility test sites to
capture site conditions underneath the solar panels. The 6 sites were chosen because
they had a similar single axis tracking system site design. Data collected by the
environmental tripods included back of module panel temperature, air temperature,
relative humidity, light irradiance in watts per square meter underneath the panels, and
wind speed underneath the panels at two locations in each solar facility. Site locations
were selected based on different seed mixes, different panel design configurations, and
proximity to perimeter of solar facility depending on the different site configurations. The
environmental data collection equipment monitored the solar irradiance being reflected
by the vegetation to the underside of the panels, as well as the relative humidity
underneath the panels influenced by the evapotranspiration of the vegetation. Site
layouts were all single axis tracking systems and had a mix of bifacial and monofacial
panels, allowing the team to analyze potential vegetation albedo impacts. By the end of
2021, across 4 sites, 8 tripods were deployed to capture data. All sensors were verified
to be functioning during installation. The final tripods were installed at the remaining 3
project sites in the fall of 2022. The environmental data download was verified with the
first SD card replacement.

Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition Data (SCADA)
The project team collected SCADA system data from all six of the solar facilities where
environmental data was being collected. Data collected from the solar facilities were
inverter DC input, inverter AC output, site back of module temperature sensors, panel tilt
angle, meteorological station data (wind speed, air temperature, and relative humidity at
15 feet), combiner box current and voltage where available. Along with the collection of
the SCADA system data, the project team met with the solar facility operations team to
discuss unplanned outages and technical issues to ensure that equipment faults were
not being included in the analysis of the data. The goal of the solar performance
research is to determine the operating capacity factor of each inverter and determine
influencing factors on performance. The team identified a linear relationship between
panel temperature and air temperature and is working to identify deviations from
expected panel temperature as a dependent variable of air temperature. The team
identified that relative humidity underneath the panels has an inverse relationship to the
panel temperature. When the vegetation research is published the project team will
compare that data to the capacity factor and humidity under the panels to further the
evapotranspiration and albedo research. The vegetation team is quantifying floristic
quality index, percent ground cover, vegetation height, and flower abundance at each of
the solar facilities. The performance team will utilize this data to see if vegetation height
and flower abundance has a positive or negative impact on under panel relative
humidity and under panel irradiance. One major uncertainty that has been identified in
determining influencing factors on panel temperature is inverter clipping causing
increased panel operating temperatures. Inverter clipping in general seems to limit the
amount of efficiency gains that may be captured by evapotranspiration cooling but could
create potential of reduced degradation of panel cells.

Ecological Data Collection
Bird and bat monitoring tripods were installed at all seven solar facilities along with
pollinator observational transects, and a characterization of the vegetation. Pan, pitfall,
and netting trapping were conducted at four of the solar facilities. Bird monitoring
identified increased species diversity at solar facilities compared to agricultural
reference sites but did not have the capability of determining abundance of species. Bat
monitoring identified increased bat activity at the solar facility compared to reference
sites. Initial pollinator impact was an observed increase in pollinator abundance
compared to conventional turf grass and traditional agriculture. There was an identified
pollinator diversity compared to soybean agriculture but was comparable to
conventional turf grass. Sample data is being compared to pollinator planting data being
collected. A primary objective is to identify when vegetation management events are
occurring on site and the impact of pollinator presence on site. Over mowing can have
an impact on native bee nesting resources and prevent vegetation flowering decreasing
food availability for pollinators. Another suspected reason for solar facilities increasing
pollinator abundance is due to land use change from row crop agriculture. The reduced
use of pest controls like pesticides may decrease impact on pollinator communities.

Pollinator Plantings
Pollinator plantings were established underneath solar panels at all 7 sites by the end of
2021. Additionally, one site will implement a second seed mix in fall 2022. By
September 2022, all planned vegetation had been installed in buffer areas and
underneath solar panels. Pollinator plantings were evaluated using percent bare ground
cover analysis, floristic quality index, vegetation height, floristic abundance, and
evaluation of species on site classified as seeded plant, native recruit, and invasive
vegetation. The project team also conducted interviews with site operators discuss the
vegetation management practices and what actions they have taken to support
vegetation establishment. Reseeding was done at two of the solar facilities. The site
maintained the pollinator vegetation, conducting mowing and spot herbicide application
as needed. Pollinator seed mixes were evaluated to determine the presence of seeded
vegetation compared to native recruits and invasive species to determine which seed
mixes were more likely to establish at a solar facility. Vegetation monitoring and timing
of vegetation maintenance were identified by the project team as the most crucial
components of seed mix success and prevention of invasive species.

8. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions: Over the course of the project the
team was able to complete all of the milestones detailed in the statement of project
objectives. The project team collected four years of ecological and performance data at
the seven solar facilities capturing the establishment timeframe of the pollinator
vegetation. The project team created four tools for solar industry use when considering
pollinator vegetation. The project team is developing two more journal articles that will
summarize the data collected from the pollinator and vegetation research, and from the
research focused on PV production and environmental conditions around the panels.
The project results did indicate greater abundance of bird and bat use of solar facilities,

increased pollinator diversity and abundance compared to the soy bean fields, and
identified vegetation that successfully established from the planted seed mixes. The
project also evaluated the impact that the vegetation has on the solar facility’s power
production. With the data collected the research team will be able to evaluate the
comparative power production of bifacial vs monofacial solar panels, vegetation height
impact on evapotranspiration, and floristic quality index impact on ground albedo.

9. Path Forward: The project intent was to capture the ecological value of each of the
seven solar sites during the establishment phase of pollinator habitat development.
Following a land use change from row crop agriculture to photovoltaic energy
production, the team was able to identify ecosystem services benefits such as
increased bird diversity, bat abundance, and pollinator abundance. The research team
identified that seed mix establishment is a challenge for large scale solar facilities and
best practices regarding vegetation management activities need to be refined. In
addition, all planted seeds were not necessarily present on site and a review of seed
mix vegetation success should be conducted to identify vegetation that is not
compatible with solar energy production.
This project only captured ecosystem services provided by pollinator vegetation in the
establishment phase. Tactics such as vegetation surveys to identify invasive species
and mowing at the identified correct time and frequency are crucial to successful
vegetation establishment. Invasive species need to be managed with spot herbicide
treatments or mowed before the vegetation can go to flower. Mowing too late can result
in the spread of invasive species throughout the site and over-mowing can prevent
planted vegetation from being able to establish. These specific windows of vegetation
management activities can be difficult to implement on a utility-scale. The Solar
Pollinator Vegetation Implementation Manual describes these nuances and highlights
the importance of monitoring and maintaining vegetation. Additional research is needed
to evaluate whether ecosystem services continue to increase over time and to
determine when ecosystem services reach a saturation level of benefit to pollinators
and wildlife.

Software
The Solar Pollinator Vegetation Implementation Manual describes goals, considerations,
and best practices for planning, designing, installing, and maintaining pollinator habitats
at large-scale solar sites. The Cost Comparison Tool allows users to compare the costs
of different vegetation configurations on utility-scale solar facilities. The Solar Seed
Selection Tool helps identify suitable species for solar plantings. The Habitat
Assessment Module builds on the Rights-of-Way as Habitat Working Group pollinator
scorecard to evaluate the habitat establishment at solar facilities.

between the research efforts and the development of tools and guidelines for industry
use. In addition, the ERC led the research on the economic effects and performance

ROWHWG to ensure expert industry feedback is incorporated throughout the project.
