Motivators and barriers to influenza vaccination among healthcare workers
Authors: Zaid Al-Hamdan, Mohammad Al Hamdan, Hala Bawadi, Awab Al Hamdan, Majdi M Alzoubi, Khalid Al Mugheed, Amany Anwar Saeed Alabdullah, Sally Mohammed Farghaly Abdelaliem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimed to identify the key motivators and barriers influencing influenza vaccination uptake among healthcare workers -and to examine the behavioral, social, and practical factors associated with vaccination acceptance and future vaccination intentions. A cross-sectional survey was conducted to assess the social and behavioral drivers (BeSD) of influenza vaccination among healthcare workers. A total of 523 Jordanian healthcare workers employed in healthcare institutions across the three regions of Jordan were recruited. Descriptive statistics, chi-square tests and Univariable logistic regression analyses were used. A total of 523 healthcare workers participated in the study, with 48.0% reporting receipt of the influenza vaccine within the previous three years. Awareness of influenza vaccination was high (96.2%), although only 24.1% considered its benefits very important and 31.7% perceived it as very safe. Previous vaccination experience was the strongest predictor of motivation to vaccinate (OR = 24.38, 95% CI: 15.33–38.76). Confidence in vaccine benefits (OR = 16.97, 95% CI: 10.95–26.29), vaccine effectiveness (OR = 15.08, 95% CI: 9.70–23.42), social responsibility (OR = 11.33, 95% CI: 7.51–17.09), and satisfaction with vaccination services (OR = 6.60, 95% CI: 4.45–9.79) were significantly associated with motivation to receive influenza vaccination (all p < .001). Influenza vaccination uptake among healthcare workers in Jordan remains suboptimal despite high awareness. Belief in vaccine benefits, effectiveness, and safety, along with previous vaccination experience, social influences, and vaccine accessibility, were key determinants of uptake and motivation. Targeted interventions addressing misconceptions and strengthening vaccine confidence may improve vaccination coverage among healthcare workers.KEYWORDS: Health care workers, influenza vaccination, Jordan

Introduction

Influenza is a highly contagious respiratory disease that continues to pose major public health challenges worldwide, particularly among vulnerable populations such as healthcare workers (HCWs), pregnant women, and older adults. HCWs are at increased risk of exposure to influenza viruses because of their close and frequent contact with patients, which may compromise both their own health and patient safety.1 Annual influenza vaccination is therefore strongly recommended for HCWs as an effective strategy to reduce influenza transmission, prevent complications, decrease mortality, and lessen the burden on healthcare systems.2–5

Despite the proven benefits of vaccination, influenza vaccine uptake among HCWs remains suboptimal in many countries, including Jordan. Previous studies conducted in Jordan have shown that many HCWs have limited knowledge regarding influenza transmission, disease severity, and the protective role of vaccination.6–9 Misconceptions are also common, including beliefs that the influenza vaccine can cause influenza infection or that vaccination is only necessary for high-risk groups. In addition, attitudinal and practical barriers such as concerns about vaccine effectiveness and safety, misinformation, and needle phobia continue to negatively influence vaccination uptake.4,10–12

Although awareness of respiratory infectious diseases and vaccination increased during the COVID-19 pandemic, important gaps still remain regarding HCWs’ understanding of seasonal influenza vaccination, particularly in relation to vaccine safety, the importance of annual immunization, and confidence in vaccine effectiveness.13 Furthermore, most previous Jordanian studies focused primarily on either knowledge or vaccination rates separately, with limited attention to the combined influence of knowledge, attitudes, and perceived barriers on vaccine uptake among HCWs across different healthcare settings.

Therefore, this study aimed to identify the key motivators and barriers influencing influenza vaccination uptake among healthcare workers in Jordan and to examine the behavioral, social, and practical factors associated with vaccination acceptance and future vaccination intentions.

Method

Study design and sampling

A cross-sectional survey was conducted among healthcare workers in Jordan. A total of 1,015 healthcare workers were invited to participate in the study, of whom 523 completed the survey, yielding a response rate of 51.5%. Participants comprised physicians, nurses, pharmacists, dentists, and other allied healthcare workers recruited from major hospitals across the northern, central, and southern regions of Jordan. The minimum required sample size was estimated at approximately 279 participants using a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and an anticipated prevalence of 50%, assuming a target population of 1,015 healthcare workers. The 50% anticipated prevalence does not represent the expected influenza vaccination rate; rather, it was selected as a conservative estimate because the true prevalence of the primary outcome (i.e., influenza vaccination uptake) among the target population was uncertain. Using a prevalence of 50% maximizes the required sample size and is a standard approach in sample size estimation when no reliable prior estimate is available.14 To increase the precision of the estimates and ensure adequate representation of different healthcare professions and geographic regions, recruitment continued until 523 participants were enrolled. Therefore, 244 participants were recruited beyond the minimum required sample size. Healthcare institutions were purposively selected from Jordan’s three geographical regions to enhance regional representation, including 4 institutions from the central region, 3 institutions from the northern region, and 1 institution from the southern region. Eligible healthcare workers, including physicians, nurses, pharmacists, dentists, and allied healthcare professionals. These factors should be considered when interpreting the study findings. The inclusion criteria were1: currently employed as a healthcare worker in a healthcare institution and2 willingness to participate in the study. All eligible healthcare professionals in the participating institutions were at least 23 y of age, reflecting the characteristics of the study population rather than an eligibility criterion. Individuals who were not directly involved in healthcare services, students or trainees, and healthcare workers who were unavailable during the data collection period or declined participation were excluded from the study.

Eligible healthcare workers who met the inclusion criteria and were available during the data collection period were approached by trained research assistants and invited to participate in the study. Participation was entirely voluntary.

Data collection process

The study was conducted between March and April 2024 after obtaining ethical approval from the Ministry of Health Institutional Review Board (MOH-IRB), Jordan (Approval No. MOH-IRB 265–2-2024). Ethical approval was obtained from the MOH-IRB because the study was conducted in Ministry of Health hospitals, where the MOH-IRB serves as the competent ethical review authority and is required to approve research involving Ministry of Health facilities and personnel. Therefore, separate ethical approval from the authors’ affiliated institution was not required. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Permission to conduct the study was also obtained from the participating healthcare facilities before data collection.

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained research assistants. This method may have introduced social desirability bias, as participants could provide socially acceptable responses regarding vaccination behaviors and attitudes. To minimize this bias, participants were assured that responses were anonymous, confidential, and would be used only for research purposes. Research assistants maintained a neutral approach and emphasized that there were no right or wrong answers.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Participation was voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. No personal identifiers were collected, and all data were securely stored and reported in aggregate form to maintain confidentiality. The survey was done in Arabic. All data collectors are exposed to a data training program that includes research ethics, data collecting tools, and form and data input orientation.

Questionnaire

The Behavioral and Social Drivers (BeSD) questionnaire for influenza vaccination was adapted from previously validated BeSD instruments developed for childhood vaccination and COVID-19 vaccination.15 The Social and Behavioral Drivers (BeSD) tool is a set of four major components designed to give a holistic understanding of the information and attitudes that affect health behaviors, specifically vaccination uptake. The components are: What people believe and feel: This includes attitudes of vaccine safety, effectiveness and need, as well as emotional reactions such as fear or faith in vaccines. Social processes: This component examines the influence of social networks, peer pressure and cultural values on an individual’s decision to get a vaccine. Motivation: The personal factors that motivate the decision to vaccinate, for example the desire to protect, protect others, are assessed. Practical issues such as the availability of the vaccine, ease, and any barriers to immunization such as cost or time are also taken into consideration. The adaptation process involved modifying the wording of the original items to specifically address seasonal influenza vaccination among healthcare workers while maintaining the conceptual domains of the BeSD framework, including knowledge, attitudes, social influences, and practical barriers related to vaccination. Items that were specific to childhood or COVID-19 vaccination contexts were revised to reflect influenza vaccine recommendations, annual vaccine uptake, perceived effectiveness, safety concerns, and accessibility among HCWs.

The adapted questionnaire was reviewed by a panel of experts in public health, nursing, infectious diseases, and epidemiology to assess content relevance, clarity, and appropriateness for the Jordanian healthcare context. Based on their feedback, minor modifications in wording and phrasing were made to improve clarity and cultural suitability.

A pilot test was conducted on a small sample of healthcare workers who were not included in the final study sample to evaluate the clarity, comprehensibility, and feasibility of the questionnaire. Feedback obtained during the pilot phase resulted in additional minor revisions to several items.

The questionnaire was originally prepared in English and then translated into Arabic using a forward – backward translation process by bilingual experts to ensure linguistic accuracy and conceptual equivalence. Any discrepancies between the original and translated versions were resolved through discussion among the research team and translators.

To assess the reliability of the study instrument, the internal consistency of the questionnaire domains was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients. The results demonstrated acceptable to good reliability across the study domains, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.72 to 0.89, indicating satisfactory internal consistency of the questionnaire items.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 23. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were used to summarize participant characteristics and study variables. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using chi-square tests. Univariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine factors associated with influenza vaccine uptake, motivation to receive influenza vaccination, and confidence in vaccine benefits. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical significance was set at p < .05. BeSD items were scored using Likert-scale responses, with each response assigned a numerical value. Items within each domain were summed or averaged to calculate domain scores, and negatively worded items were reverse-coded. Higher scores indicated more positive attitudes, knowledge, and acceptance of influenza vaccination.

Result

A total of 523 healthcare workers completed the interview and were included in the analysis. The mean age of participants was 37.7 y (SD = 8.8). Most participants were female (67.5%), resided in the central region of Jordan (66.2%), and reported no chronic diseases (78.4%). Nearly all participants (97.6%) held a bachelor’s degree or higher. Nurses represented the largest professional group (44.6%), followed by physicians (23.1%), pharmacists (10.0%), and dentists (6.3%), while 15.0% did not report their profession. Approximately half of the participants (49.7%) had 10 y or more of professional experience, and about half were employed in public hospitals (Table 1).

Confidence in the benefits of the influenza vaccine varied according to profession, years of practice, and workplace setting. General practitioners, specialists, and dentists showed higher confidence in vaccine benefits than other professions, although these associations were not statistically significant. In contrast, healthcare workers in non-core health professions were significantly less likely to perceive the vaccine as beneficial (OR = 0.56, p = .017). Healthcare workers with 1–4 y of experience were significantly more likely to report moderate to high confidence in vaccine benefits (OR = 2.58, p < .05). Regarding workplace setting, healthcare workers employed in health centers were significantly more likely to report confidence in vaccine benefits (OR = 1.53, p = .024), whereas hospital employees showed a tendency toward lower confidence, although this association was not statistically significant (OR = 0.72, p = .059). Discussion

Research on the Behavioral and Social Drivers (BeSD) of influenza vaccination among healthcare workers in Jordan suggest that various elements that pertain to cognition, emotions, social and practical considerations have a significant effect on their motivation and confidence in the benefits of vaccines. These findings are consistent with other findings that suggest the complex interplay between cognitive, social, and logistical factors that influence vaccination behavior in healthcare workers.

Perceived risk and confidence in vaccines

A key factor for motivation to get vaccinated was perceived personal risk of catching influenza. Healthcare workers with moderate and high levels of concern about contracting the virus had a significantly higher motivation to vaccinate (OR: 2.82, p < .001), which is consistent with studies finding that perceived susceptibility often leads to preventive health behaviors.16,17 This is in line with the health belief model that indicates that people will most probably be willing to take preventive steps including vaccination in case they think they are at risk of falling sick Also, the HCWs who expected to become moderately or very ill with influenza were more likely to be vaccinated (OR: 2.03, p < .001). This is in line with research which has indicated perceived illness severity as an inducement to change health behaviors.11,18 Conversely, in Jordan, there was a difference in the HCWs confidence on the vaccine effectiveness. To illustrate, HCWs who perceived effectiveness for themselves were 15.08 times more likely to want to get vaccinated and those who perceived it for others were 6.5 times more likely (both p < .001). This is in line with research around the world that has indicated that perceived vaccine effectiveness is a key factor in vaccine uptake18,19 particularly among HCWs, who are concerned with both their health and the possible risk to patients16,19

Social processes and norms

Moreover, healthcare workers who anticipated that a contract of influenza would cause them to be “moderately or very sick” were more likely to seek vaccination (OR: 2.03, p < .001). This finding is consistent with research that suggests perceived illness severity is a driving force for health behavior change adoption15. On the other hand, a difference was identified in Jordan in the level of confidence of the healthcare workers in the effectiveness of the vaccine. For example, those who viewed the vaccine as being very effective for themselves were 15.08 times more likely to want to be vaccinated while those who viewed the vaccine as being effective for others were 6.5 times more likely (both p < .001). This aligns with studies conducted around the world which have shown that the perceived effectiveness of vaccines is one of the key factors in vaccine acceptance,15 particularly for healthcare workers, who are aware of their own health and of potential risk to patients.16

Practical issues and accessibility

Practical issues (especially involving access to vaccines and satisfaction with services) were important in promoting vaccination. HCWs who knew where to get the vaccine were more than three times as likely to be motivated to get vaccinated (OR: 3.26, p < .001), which is consistent with previous research showing that logistical simplicity is essential in the uptake of vaccination. Additionally, the quality of the vaccination service had a significant effect on motivation with satisfied HCWs being 6.6 times more likely to get vaccinated (p < .001). This stresses the importance of healthcare experiences in shaping continued health behaviors, per discussions in literature stressing the importance of positive experiences of service in driving trust and accepting public health efforts.20 The lack of vaccination services on site, which has been suggested to be linked with a higher uptake, is an important gap in many health facilities in Jordan, suggesting an opportunity for focused interventions to address logistical challenges.8

Implications for public health policy

The results of the study suggest that specific strategies that address cognitive and practical barriers might help to improve influenza vaccination rates for healthcare workers in Jordan. Educational efforts that focus on the real risks and benefits of vaccination would be a way to possibly eliminate misconceptions and increase the perceived effectiveness and safety of vaccines. Approaches that are culturally aware, including those which involve religious and community leaders could increase social approval of vaccination, consistent with WHO recommendations for areas where community leadership is strong.15 Making vaccines readily accessible, potentially through on-site programs at hospitals and health centers, could further reduce practical barriers, consistent with findings from settings in healthcare systems where logistical access is associated with higher rates of vaccination among healthcare workers.21,22

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the use of convenience sampling, although it increased the diversity of the sample, may have introduced selection bias and limited the representativeness of the study population, thereby restricting the generalizability of the findings to all healthcare workers in Jordan. Second, data were collected through face-to-face interviews, which may have introduced social desirability bias, as participants could have provided socially acceptable responses regarding influenza vaccination attitudes and practices. Third, healthcare workers from different professional groups were analyzed collectively; differences in influenza exposure risk and vaccination perceptions across occupational categories were not assessed and may have masked profession-specific patterns. Finally, the study relied primarily on univariable analyses to examine associations between participant characteristics and influenza vaccination outcomes. Consequently, the observed associations may be subject to confounding, as potential confounding variables were not simultaneously controlled for in a multivariable model. Therefore, the findings interpreted as associative rather than independent relationships.

Conclusion

The research highlights the importance of developing a holistic approach that considers the beliefs and social factors of healthcare workers, as well as the accessibility needs, in order to increase vaccine acceptance. These results imply that customized interventions aimed at these particular obstacles as well as motivations in Jordan, might promote vaccination rates among healthcare professionals and promote better public health outcomes. Tailored education on the effectiveness of vaccines, support from healthcare and community leaders, and improved access to vaccination through on-site vaccination programs could be effective in improving vaccination rates among HCWs. Such strategies are necessary to enhance the protection of the public health and to minimize the spread of influenza in the healthcare environment.
