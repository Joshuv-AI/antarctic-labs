Non-Noetherian Bass and Betti numbers
Mohsen Asgharzadeh and Elham Mahdavi

Abstract
. This paper investigates the vanishing and non-vanishing of Betti and Bass numbers for non-finitely generated modules. We prove that for d d -dimensional Cohen–Macaulay local rings, every non-zero 𝔪 \mathfrak{m} -torsion module satisfies β d ​ ( M ) ≠ 0 \beta_{d}(M)\neq 0 , and we establish the Betti number behavior of the injective hull E R ​ ( k ) E_{R}(k) . We study tor-rigidity for H 𝔪 d ​ ( R ) H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R) . We also provide partial positive answers to Schoutens’ question on whether the vanishing of some Betti number of a big Cohen–Macaulay algebra forces the Cohen–Macaulay property of R R . For the absolute integral closure R + R^{+} , we establish both Tor and Ext results. On the Tor side, we prove that β i ​ ( R + ) = 0 \beta_{i}(R^{+})=0 for some i > 0 i>0 implies regularity in a series cases. On the Ext side, we prove that μ i ​ ( R + ) = 0 \mu_{i}(R^{+})=0 for some i > d i>d forces regularity for Gorenstein domains of prime characteristic, and we obtain analogous results for graded normal domains of dimension 2 2 and also for quotient and isolated singularities in any dimension. 1.
Introduction

The study of
Betti numbers

\beta_{i}^{R}(M)=\dim_{k}\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(k,M)
and
Bass numbers

\mu^{i}_{R}(M)=\dim_{k}\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{i}(k,M)
is fundamental to commutative algebra, providing deep insights into the structure of modules and rings. For finitely generated modules over regular local rings, the Auslander–Buchsbaum formula and the theory of dualizing complexes completely govern the behavior of these invariants. However, when modules are not finitely generated, or when the base ring is singular, classical results often fail and new phenomena emerge. This paper systematically investigates the vanishing and non-vanishing of both Tor and Ext in these broader contexts, with particular emphasis on modules arising from local cohomology, injective hulls, and the absolute integral closure.

The absolute integral closure of an integral domain
R
, denoted by
R^{+}
, is the integral closure of
R
inside an algebraic closure of its fraction field. This construction plays a central role in tight closure theory, perfectoid algebras, and the study of singularities in positive and mixed characteristic. A fundamental question, raised by Bhatt, Iyengar, and Ma, asks whether Tor-vanishing for
R^{+}
forces regularity:

.

(See
[
7
]

2.
Tensor product and Cohen–Macaulay variations

This section collects results on when the tensor product of two modules being (generalized) Cohen–Macaulay forces the same property on the factors.

Question 2.1
.

(See
[
11
, Question 1.1]
).
Let
R
be a complete intersection. Suppose
M\otimes_{R}N
is maximal Cohen–Macaulay. When does it follow that
M
or
N
is maximal Cohen–Macaulay?

Remark 2.2
.

(See
[
11
, Theorem 1.4]
).
A partial answer is suggested by the following: if
M
is maximal Cohen–Macaulay and
\dim R>\operatorname{codim}(R)
, then
N
is maximal Cohen–Macaulay.

The next example demonstrates that the generalized Cohen–Macaulay property is not preserved under tensor products, in the sense that both factors need not be generalized Cohen–Macaulay even if their tensor product is.

Example 2.3
.

Let
R=k[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]]
with
n\geq 3
. Set
M=R/I
with
I=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})+(y_{1})\cap(y_{2},y_{3})
, and
N=R/J
with
J=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})+(x_{1})\cap(x_{2},x_{3})
. Then
M\otimes_{R}N=R/(I+J)
. The module
M
is not equidimensional, as there exists

\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Ass}(M)=\operatorname{Ass}(M)\setminus\{\mathfrak{m}\}=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})+(y_{1}),(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})+(y_{2},y_{3})\}

such that
\dim(R/\mathfrak{p})\neq\dim(R/I)
. Similarly,
N
is not equidimensional over punctured spectrum. By
[
10
, Example 9.5.7(1)]
, if
M
were generalized Cohen–Macaulay then
\dim(R/\mathfrak{p})=\dim(M)
for all
\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Ass}(M)\setminus\{\mathfrak{m}\}
and
M_{\mathfrak{p}}
would be Cohen–Macaulay. Hence
M
and
N
are not generalized Cohen–Macaulay, but
M\otimes_{R}N=R/(I+J)=R/\mathfrak{m}
is generalized Cohen–Macaulay.

Proposition 2.4
.

Suppose
R
is a complete Cohen–Macaulay domain with an isolated singularity. Let
M
and
N
be
R
-modules with
\dim M=\dim N=\dim R
. If
M\otimes_{R}N
is generalized Cohen–Macaulay, then both
M
and
N
are generalized Cohen–Macaulay.

Proof.

Since
R
is a domain and both modules have full dimension, we have
\operatorname{Supp}(M)=\operatorname{Supp}(N)=\operatorname{Spec}(R)
. For any non-maximal prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Supp}(M\otimes_{R}N)\setminus\{\mathfrak{m}\}=\operatorname{Spec}(R)\setminus\{\mathfrak{m}\}
, the localization
(M\otimes_{R}N)_{\mathfrak{p}}\cong M_{\mathfrak{p}}\otimes_{R_{\mathfrak{p}}}N_{\mathfrak{p}}
is Cohen–Macaulay over
R_{\mathfrak{p}}
by
[
10
, Ex. 9.5(1)]
.
Recall that
R_{\mathfrak{p}}
is regular, and then any maximal Cohen-Macaulay
R_{\mathfrak{p}}
-module is free. Thus,

(M\otimes_{R}N)_{\mathfrak{p}}\cong M_{\mathfrak{p}}\otimes_{R_{\mathfrak{p}}}N_{\mathfrak{p}}
is free. It turns out that

N_{\mathfrak{p}}
and

M_{\mathfrak{p}}
are free (see
[
6
, 3.4.7]
), hence they are Cohen–Macaulay. Now take any
\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Ass}(M)\setminus\{\mathfrak{m}\}
. We see that
\mathfrak{p}=(0)
. Applying
[
9
, Ex. 9.5.7(b)]
, we obtain that
\dim(R/\mathfrak{p})=\dim M
for every
\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Min}(\operatorname{Supp}(M))\setminus\{\mathfrak{m}\}
, and that
M_{\mathfrak{p}}
is Cohen–Macaulay for every
\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Supp}(M)\setminus\{\mathfrak{m}\}
. These conditions precisely characterize
M
as generalized Cohen–Macaulay. By symmetry, the same conclusion holds for
N
.
∎

Proposition 2.5
.

Suppose
R
is a complete hypersurface domain and
M,N
are
R
-modules with
\dim M=\dim N=\dim R
. If
M\otimes_{R}N
is generalized Cohen–Macaulay, then both
M
and
N
are generalized Cohen–Macaulay.

Proof.

The argument closely parallels the preceding observation, with the isolated singularity hypothesis replaced by a theorem of Huneke–Wiegand
[
21
, 3.1]
: over a hypersurface ring, if
M\otimes_{R}N
is Cohen–Macaulay, then both
M
and
N
are Cohen–Macaulay. Since
M\otimes_{R}N
is generalized Cohen–Macaulay, for every non-maximal prime
\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Spec}(R)\setminus\{\mathfrak{m}\}
, the localization
(M\otimes_{R}N)_{\mathfrak{p}}
is Cohen–Macaulay over the local hypersurface ring
R_{\mathfrak{p}}
. Applying the Huneke–Wiegand result locally yields that both
M_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
N_{\mathfrak{p}}
are Cohen–Macaulay. The remainder of the proof follows identically to the previous case: one shows that
\operatorname{Ass}(M)\setminus\{\mathfrak{m}\}=\{(0)\}
and similarly for
N
, after which the criterion from
[
9
, Ex. 9.5.7(b)]
ensures that
M
and
N
are generalized Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a regular local ring and let
M\otimes_{R}N
be a balanced big Cohen–Macaulay
R
-module.
Then
M
is balanced big Cohen–Macaulay if and only
N
is.

Proof.

Since
R
is regular, every balanced big Cohen–Macaulay module is flat (see
[
19
, Page 61]
). Thus
M\otimes_{R}N
is faithfully flat.
By symmetry, assume
M
is balanced big Cohen–Macaulay. In particular,
M
is faithfully flat.
Suppose
0\longrightarrow A\longrightarrow B
be an inclusion.
We need to show the exactness of

A\otimes N\longrightarrow B\otimes N
. Then tensoring
0\longrightarrow A\longrightarrow B
with
M\otimes N
gives the inclusions

(i)

Suppose
0\longrightarrow A\otimes(M\otimes N)\longrightarrow B\otimes(M\otimes N)
,

(ii)

0\longrightarrow(A\otimes N)\otimes M\longrightarrow(B\otimes N)\otimes M.

But
M
is faithfully flat, so

0\longrightarrow A\otimes N\longrightarrow B\otimes N
is exact.
Hence
N
is flat.
If

N\otimes L=0,
for some module
L
,
then

M\otimes(N\otimes L)=(M\otimes N)\otimes L=0.
Since
M\otimes N

is faithfully flat, we get

This shows
N
is is faithfully flat, and so balanced big Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

Example 2.7
.

Flatness of
M\otimes_{R}N
does not imply that both
M
and
N
are flat. Let
R
be a domain with fraction field
Q
, and fix
0\neq r\in R
. Set
M=Q\oplus R/rR
and
N=Q
. Then

M\otimes_{R}N\cong(Q\oplus R/rR)\otimes_{R}Q\cong Q,

which is flat over

is not flat because its direct summand

is torsion.

Proposition 2.8
.

(i)

Suppose
A
and
B
are Tor-independent
R
-modules such that
\beta_{1}(A)=\beta_{1}(B)=0
. Then
\beta_{1}(A\otimes_{R}B)=0
.

(ii)

Suppose the pair
(B,C)
is Ext-independent and that
\mu_{1}(C)=\beta_{1}(B)=0
. Then
\mu_{1}(\operatorname{Hom}(B,C))=0
.

Proof.

(i) Since
A
and
B
are Tor-independent, there is a spectral sequence

\operatorname{Tor}_{p}^{R}(A,\operatorname{Tor}_{q}^{R}(B,k))\Longrightarrow\operatorname{Tor}_{p+q}^{R}(A\otimes_{R}B,k)

(see
[
26
, Theorem 10.59]
). Again by
[
26
, Theorem 10.31]
, there is an exact sequence

\operatorname{Tor}_{2}^{R}(A\otimes_{R}B,k)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Tor}_{2}^{R}(A,B\otimes_{R}k)\longrightarrow A\otimes_{R}\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{R}(B,k)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{R}(A\otimes_{R}B,k)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{R}(A,B\otimes_{R}k)\longrightarrow 0.

Now
B\otimes_{R}k\cong k^{\,t}
for some
t\in[0,\infty]
. Consequently
\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{R}(A,B\otimes_{R}k)=0
. From the displayed exact sequence,
\beta_{1}(A\otimes_{R}B)=0
.

(ii)
Since
(B,C)
is Ext-independent,

\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{i}(B\otimes_{R}P,C)=\{0\}

for all
i\geq 1
whenever
{}_{R}P
is projective.
Then there is a third quadrant spectral sequence (see
[
26
, Theorem 10.62]
):

\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{p}\bigl(\operatorname{Tor}_{R}^{q}(B,k),C\bigr)\Rightarrow_{p}\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{n}\bigl(k,\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(B,C)\bigr).

By
[
26
, Theorem 10.33]
, there is an exact sequence

0\longrightarrow E_{2}^{1,0}\longrightarrow H^{1}(\operatorname{Tot}(M))\longrightarrow E_{2}^{0,1}\xrightarrow{d_{2}}E_{2}^{2,0}\longrightarrow H^{2}(\operatorname{Tot}(M)).

Now
B\otimes_{R}k\cong k^{\,t}
for some
t\in[0,\infty]
. Consequently

E_{2}^{1,0}=\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{1}\bigl(\operatorname{Tor}_{R}^{0}(B,k),C\bigr)=\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{1}\bigl(k^{\,t},C\bigr)=0
. Similarly,

E_{2}^{0,1}=\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{0}\bigl(\operatorname{Tor}_{R}^{1}(B,k),C\bigr)=\operatorname{Hom}_{R}\bigl(0,C\bigr)=0
.
From the displayed exact sequence,
\mu_{1}(\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(B,C))=0
.
∎

3.
Weakly tor-rigid

This section is divided into two subsections:

3.1.
Weak tor-rigidity and big Cohen–Macaulay algebras

This subsection focuses on tor-rigidity and big Cohen–Macaulay algebras, proving that Schoutens’ question has a positive answer for tor-rigid algebras.

Question 3.1
.

(Schoutens,
[
29
, End of §2]
).
Suppose
S
is a big Cohen–Macaulay algebra over a local ring
R
. If
\operatorname{Tor}_{n}^{R}(k,S)=0
for some
n\geq 1
, does it follow that
R
is Cohen–Macaulay?

First, recall that vanishing of all Betti numbers does not imply flatness:

Example 3.2

(Bartijn–Strooker
[
8
]
)
.

Let
R=k[[x,y,z]]
and let
\varphi
be a free module of infinite countable rank. Define
\Omega:=\varphi+(x,y)\widehat{\varphi}
. Then
\{x,y,z\}
is an
\Omega
-sequence,
\{z,x,y\}
is not an
\Omega
-sequence, and
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(k,\Omega)=0
.

The following extends Example 3.2, and simplifies a result of Huneke (see
[
20
, 9.1]
).

Observation 3.3
.

If
R
is a regular local ring and
M
is a big Cohen–Macaulay module, then
\beta_{i}(M)=0
for all
i>0
.

Proof.

Since
R
is regular, the residue field
k
admits a finite free resolution

0\longrightarrow F_{d}\longrightarrow F_{d-1}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow F_{0}\longrightarrow k\longrightarrow 0.

Tensoring this resolution with
M
yields a complex whose homology is precisely
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(k,M)
. Because
M
is big Cohen–Macaulay, the Koszul complex on any system of parameters is exact on
M
, which is equivalent to the vanishing of all positive
\operatorname{Tor}
’s with the residue field; hence
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(k,M)=0
for every
i>0
. Consequently, the Betti numbers
\beta_{i}(M)=\dim_{k}\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(k,M)
vanish for all
i>0
.
∎

Definition 3.4
.

An
R
-module
S
is called
weakly tor-rigid
if
\operatorname{Tor}_{j}^{R}(R/I,S)=0
for some parameter ideal
I
and some integer
j
implies
\operatorname{Tor}_{j+1}^{R}(R/I,S)=0
.

Concerning Question 3.1, he proved the desired claim when
n\leq 2
. Here, we deal with the case
n=3
, and the general case seems being analogous.

Theorem 3.5
.

Suppose
S
is a big Cohen–Macaulay, weakly tor-rigid and
\operatorname{Tor}_{3}^{R}(S,k)=0
. Then
R
is Cohen–Macaulay.

Proof.

There exists an exact sequence

0\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow F_{1}\xrightarrow{\varphi}F_{0}\longrightarrow S\longrightarrow 0,

where
M=\operatorname{Syz}_{2}(S)
and
F_{0},F_{1}
are free modules (not necessarily of finite rank). As observed in
[
29
, 2.5]
, it suffices to prove that
IS\cap R=I
for some parameter ideal
I
.
Consider the two short exact sequences arising from the free resolution:

0\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow F_{1}\longrightarrow E:=\operatorname{Im}(\varphi)\longrightarrow 0,

0\longrightarrow E\longrightarrow F_{0}\longrightarrow S\longrightarrow 0.

Tensoring these with
\overline{R}:=R/I
yields the exact sequences

(i)

0\longrightarrow\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{R}(E,\overline{R})\longrightarrow M/IM\longrightarrow F_{1}\otimes_{R}\overline{R}\longrightarrow E\otimes_{R}\overline{R}\longrightarrow 0,
and

(ii)

0\longrightarrow\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{R}(S,\overline{R})\longrightarrow E\otimes_{R}\overline{R}\longrightarrow F_{0}\otimes_{R}\overline{R}\longrightarrow S\otimes_{R}\overline{R}\longrightarrow 0.

Now tensor the short exact sequence
0\to I\to R\to R/I\to 0
with
S
to obtain

\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{R}(R/I,S)\longrightarrow S\otimes_{R}I\longrightarrow S.

Since
S
is big Cohen-Macaulay,
I
is generated by an
S
-sequence. Hence, the map
S\otimes_{R}I\to S
is injective, so
\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{R}(R/I,S)=0
. Moreover, the natural map
I\otimes_{R}S\to IS
is an isomorphism. Applying tor-rigidity, we also get
\operatorname{Tor}_{2}^{R}(R/I,S)=0
. Assuming
\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{R}(R/I,E)=\operatorname{Tor}_{2}^{R}(R/I,S)=0
, the first tensored sequence reduces to

\zeta:=0\longrightarrow M/IM\longrightarrow F_{1}\otimes_{R}\overline{R}\longrightarrow E\otimes_{R}\overline{R}\longrightarrow 0.

From this we deduce
\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{R}(M,k)=\operatorname{Tor}_{3}^{R}(S,k)=0
, and consequently
\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{\overline{R}}(M/IM,k)=0
by
[
29
, 2.1]
. Hence
M/IM
is flat over
\overline{R}
, (see
[
22
, 22.3]
). Now, by
\zeta
we deduce
E\otimes_{R}\overline{R}
has finite flat dimension over
\overline{R}
.
Since
\overline{R}
is zero-dimensional, the flat dimension of
E\otimes_{R}\overline{R}
over
\overline{R}
is at most dimension of the ring, then it is actually flat over the artinian ring
\overline{R}
.
By

0=\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{R}(S,\overline{R})\longrightarrow E\otimes_{R}\overline{R}\longrightarrow F_{0}\otimes_{R}\overline{R}\longrightarrow S\otimes_{R}\overline{R}\longrightarrow 0.

we see

is actually flat over

. Recall that flat modules are torsion-free, thus, annihilator
of

as an

-module is

.
In other words,

. This ensures that

is generated by an
R
-sequence, as

I
is
S
-regular sequence.
By definition,

∎

Remark 3.6
.

If

is Cohen–Macaulay and

is a big Cohen–Macaulay module, then

is weakly tor-rigid.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{x}=x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
be a system of parameters for

. Since

\mathbf{x}
is an

Fact 3.7
.

(See
[

, Theorem VI.10]
). Suppose there exists

such that

By residual dimension we mean:

The following completes the proof of Theorem

:

Theorem 3.8
.

Assume
R
is an isolated singularity and
S
is an
R
-algebra such that
S
is big Cohen–Macaulay and
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(S,k)=0
for some

. Then
R
is Cohen–Macaulay.

Proof.

By
[
12
, 1.1]
,
\operatorname{Tor}_{j}^{R}(S,k)=0
for all
j\geq i
. Now recall from Fact
3.7

that
\mathrm{pd}_{R}(S)<\infty
, here we may pass to some syzygies of
S
, but this has no effect on finiteness of projective dimension. These enable us to use a result of Bartijn and Strooker
[
8
, 4.1]
(also, see the updated version presented in
[
27
, 7.4]
). This implies that
\mathrm{depth}(R)-\mathrm{depth}(S)=\mathrm{resdim}(S)
. But

is big Cohen–Macaulay, so
\mathrm{depth}(S)=\dim(R)
. Hence,

Thus
\mathrm{resdim}(S)=0
. Therefore
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(S,k)=0
for all

.
In particular,
\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{R}(S,k)=0
.
By
[

, Proposition 2.5]
,
R
is Cohen–Macaulay.
∎

Proposition 3.9
.

Suppose
R
is a
3
-dimensional local ring and
M
is a reflexive module such that
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(M,k)=0
for some integer
i>0
. Then
\operatorname{Tor}_{j}^{R}(M,k)=0
for all
j>i
.

Proof.

We may assume
M\neq 0
. Every reflexive module over a local ring is canonically isomorphic to its double dual, so we may write
M\cong N^{*}
for some module
N
. Assume without loss of generality that
M^{*}\neq 0
. Take a partial free resolution
F_{1}\to F_{0}\to N\to 0
of
N
, where
F_{0}
and
F_{1}
are free modules not necessarily of finite rank. Since the dual of a free module is flat (indeed,
(\oplus R)^{*}\cong\prod R
is flat over any coherent rings, and recall that in our setup
R
is noetheian and so coherent), dualizing the resolution yields an exact sequence

0\longrightarrow N^{*}\longrightarrow F_{0}^{*}\longrightarrow F_{1}^{*}\longrightarrow\operatorname{Tr}(N)\longrightarrow 0,

where
\operatorname{Tr}(N)
denotes the Auslander transpose of
N
. This gives isomorphisms
\operatorname{Tor}_{j+2}^{R}(\operatorname{Tr}(N),k)\cong\operatorname{Tor}_{j}^{R}(N^{*},k)
for all
j\geq 0
. Recall
N^{\ast}=M^{\ast\ast}=M
. From
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(M,k)=0
, we obtain
\operatorname{Tor}_{i+2}^{R}(\operatorname{Tr}(N),k)=0
. Since
\dim R=3
, we have
i+2\geq\dim R
, so the result of
[
12
, 1.1]
applies to yield
\operatorname{Tor}_{j}^{R}(\operatorname{Tr}(N),k)=0
for all
j\geq i+2
. Translating back via the isomorphism above, we get
\operatorname{Tor}_{j}^{R}(N^{*},k)=0
for all
j\geq i
. Since
N^{\ast}=M^{\ast\ast}=M
, and therefore
\operatorname{Tor}_{j}^{R}(M,k)=0
for all
j\geq i
.
∎

Remark 3.10
.

One has
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(\prod_{j}M_{j},k)=\prod_{j}\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(M_{j},k)
, because noeherian rings are coherent (see
[
14
, 3.2.26]
). Thus, if for some
j_{0}
we know
\beta_{i}(M_{j_{0}})
is nonzero, then
\beta_{i}(\prod_{j}M_{j})\neq 0
.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
M
be a torsionless module over a
d
-dimensional local ring
R
. If
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(k,M)=0
for some
i\geq d-1
, then
\operatorname{Tor}_{j}^{R}(k,M)=0
for all
j\geq d
.

Proof.

Let
\oplus R\to M^{\ast}\to 0
be exact. Then
M\subseteq M^{\ast\ast}\hookrightarrow(\oplus R)^{\ast}=\prod R=:F
, gives an embedding
0\to M\to F
into the flat module
F
. Let
X
denote the cokernel, so we have a short exact sequence

0\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow F\longrightarrow X\longrightarrow 0.

Applying
\operatorname{Tor}_{*}^{R}(k,-)
and using the flatness of

, we obtain isomorphisms
\operatorname{Tor}_{n+1}^{R}(k,X)\cong\operatorname{Tor}_{n}^{R}(k,M)
for all
n\geq 0
. In particular,
\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(k,X)\cong\operatorname{Tor}_{d-1}^{R}(k,M)=0
, where the vanishing follows from the hypothesis since
i\geq d-1
. By the result of
[
12
]
, the vanishing of
\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(k,X)
forces
\operatorname{Tor}_{d+n}^{R}(k,X)=0
for all
n\geq 0
. Translating back via the isomorphism above yields
\operatorname{Tor}_{d+n-1}^{R}(k,M)=0
for all
n\geq 0
, which is precisely the desired vanishing for all
j\geq d
.
∎

Remark 3.12
.

The torsionless assumption is important. Indeed, suppose

is regular of dimension

. Then

satisfies
\beta_{d-1}(E_{R}(k))=0
, while
\beta_{d}(E_{R}(k))\neq 0
.

Failure of weak rigidity and examples

This subsection provides a detailed analysis of
\beta_{1}(H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R))
for a specific non-Cohen–Macaulay ring, illustrating the failure of rigidity.

Example 3.13

(Lichtenbaum,
[
17
]
)
.

Let
R=\frac{k[x,y]}{(x^{2},xy)}
and
M=R/\mathfrak{p}
, where
\mathfrak{p}:=(x)
is a prime ideal. Since
\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Spec}(R)\setminus\{\mathfrak{m}\}
and
\dim(R)=1
, it follows that
M
is a Cohen–Macaulay module, as multiplication with any
r\in\mathfrak{m}\setminus\mathfrak{p}
gives an injection
R/\mathfrak{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle r}}{{\longrightarrow}}R/\mathfrak{p}
. In particular,
M
is maximal Cohen–Macaulay. Note that
x\in\sqrt{yR}
because
x^{2}=0\in yR
. Hence
\sqrt{yR}=(x,y)
, so
y
is a system of parameters. It remains to recall from
[
17
, 3 in Page 226]
that
\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{R}(M,R/yR)=0
, but
\operatorname{Tor}_{2}^{R}(M,R/yR)\neq 0
.

Let us check the non-vanishing property of
\beta_{d}

for our favorite table
\{H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R),E(R/\mathfrak{m}),R^{+}\}
, with the convenience that

R^{+}:=\oplus_{\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Ass}(R)}(R/\mathfrak{p})^{+}
.

Example 3.14
.

Suppose
k=\bar{k}
, and let
R:=\frac{k[x,y]}{(x^{2},xy)}
. The following holds:

(i)

\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{R}(H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R),k)\neq 0
.

(ii)

One has

\beta_{1}(E(R/\mathfrak{m}))\neq 0
.

(iii)

One has

\beta_{1}(R^{+})\neq 0
,

Proof.

(i) First note that
R
is not Cohen–Macaulay since
\operatorname{depth}(R)=0\neq 1=\dim(R)
. Indeed,
H^{0}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)=xR\neq 0
. Consider the beginning of a free resolution of
k
:

(F_{\bullet},d_{\bullet}):=\cdots\longrightarrow R^{3}\xrightarrow{\begin{pmatrix}x&y&0\\
0&0&x\end{pmatrix}}R^{2}\xrightarrow{(x\;\;y)}R\longrightarrow k\longrightarrow 0,

which may be checked by hand that is exact.
Recall that
\sqrt{(y)}=\mathfrak{m}
, hence
y
is a system of parameters. Therefore
H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)=\frac{R_{y}}{\operatorname{im}(\rho)}
, where
\operatorname{im}(\rho)=\operatorname{im}(R\to R_{y})
, see
[
9
, Corollary 2.2.21]
. Now
R_{y}=\{\frac{r}{y^{n}}\mid r\in R,\ n\geq 0\}
. Since
xy=0
in
R
, we have
\frac{x}{y}=0
, and hence

R_{y}=\left\{\frac{1+a_{1}y+\cdots+a_{n}y^{n}}{y^{m}}\mid a_{i}\in k,\ m,n\geq 0\right\}.

Tensoring
F_{\bullet}
with
H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)
gives
(H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R))^{\oplus 3}\xrightarrow{A}(H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R))^{\oplus 2}\xrightarrow{(x\;\;y)}H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)
. Therefore

\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{R}(H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R),k)=H_{1}((H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R))^{\oplus 3}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varepsilon_{2}}}{{\longrightarrow}}(H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R))^{\oplus 2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varepsilon_{1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)).

Let us compute this homology. First,
\ker(\varepsilon_{1})=\{\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\mid\alpha x+\beta y=0\}
, where
\alpha,\beta\in H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)
,
and recall that

\operatorname{im}(\varepsilon_{2})=\bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
\beta\\
\gamma\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x&y&0\\
0&0&x\end{pmatrix}\mid\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)\bigg\}=\bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}\alpha x+y\beta+0\gamma\\
0\alpha+0\beta+x\gamma\end{pmatrix}\bigg\}.

Then

(a)

\ker(\varepsilon_{1})=\{\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\mid\beta y=0\}
, since

xH^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}=0
.

(b)

\operatorname{im}(\varepsilon_{2})=\operatorname{im}(A)=\bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}y\beta\\
0\end{pmatrix}\mid\beta\in H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)\bigg\}
, as
x\gamma=0
.

In particular,

\bullet

\binom{0}{\frac{1}{y}+\operatorname{im}(R)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\in}}\ker(1_{H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)}\otimes d_{1}),
but

\bullet

\binom{0}{\frac{1}{y}+\operatorname{im}(R)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\notin}}\operatorname{im}(1_{H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)}\otimes d_{2}).

Therefore
\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{R}(H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R),R/\mathfrak{m})=H_{1}(\frac{R_{y}}{\operatorname{im}(R)}\otimes_{R}F_{\bullet})\neq 0
.

(ii) Let
F_{\bullet}
be as (i), and set
A:=k[[X,Y]]
. Then there is a ring homomorphism
A\to R
. By
[
10
, Lemma 3.16]
,
0\neq\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(R,E_{A}(k))
, is injective and its associated prime is
\mathfrak{m}
. By Matlis decomposition theorem,

\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(R,E(k))=\oplus_{t}E_{R}(R/\mathfrak{m})
for some
t>0
.
Then, we show
\beta_{1}(\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(R,E(k)))\neq 0
. Recall that
\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{R}(R/\mathfrak{m},\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(R,E_{A}(k)))
is isomorphic to
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{1}(R/\mathfrak{m},A),E_{A}(k))
, see
[
14
, 3.2.1]
. Thus it suffices to show
E_{1}:=\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{1}(R/\mathfrak{m},A)\neq 0
. We apply
\operatorname{Hom}(-,A)
to
F_{\bullet}
, and we obtain

A\xrightarrow{\binom{x}{y}}A^{2}\xrightarrow{\begin{bmatrix}x&0\\
y&0\\
0&x\end{bmatrix}}A^{3}.

We denote the boundary of this complex by
\partial_{\bullet}
. For
(x,y)\in A^{2}
, we observe

\begin{bmatrix}x&0\\
y&0\\
0&x\end{bmatrix}\binom{x}{x}=\begin{pmatrix}x^{2}\\
xy\\
x^{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix}=0.

Thus
\binom{x}{x}\in\ker(\partial_{2})
, but
\binom{x}{x}\notin\operatorname{im}(A\xrightarrow{(x\;y)}A^{2})=\{\binom{ax}{ay}\mid a\in A\}
. Hence the complex is not exact, so
\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{1}(R/\mathfrak{m},A)\neq 0
. Therefore
\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{R}(R/\mathfrak{m},E_{R}(k))\neq 0
, and consequently
\beta_{1}(E_{R}(R/\mathfrak{m}))\neq 0
.

(iii) Recall that
\operatorname{Ass}(R)=\{\mathfrak{m},(x)\}
. Since
R^{+}=\oplus_{\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Ass}(R)}(R/\mathfrak{p})^{+}=k\oplus(k[[y]])^{+}
, and

, we see that
\beta_{1}(R^{+})=1+\beta_{1}((k[[y]])^{+})\neq 0
, as desired claim. ∎

Proposition 3.15
.

Let
R
be a Cohen–Macaulay ring and
M
a non-Cohen–Macaulay module of dimension
d_{0}<d-1:=\dim(R)-1
and depth
t\leq d-2
. Then
H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)
is not rigid.

Proof.

Since
R
is Cohen–Macaulay, the flat resolution of
H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)
is given by

0\longrightarrow R\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}R_{x_{i}}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow R_{x_{1}.\cdots.x_{d}}\longrightarrow H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)\longrightarrow 0,

where
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}
is a system of parameters. Tensoring with
M
gives a complex whose homology is

\operatorname{Tor}^{R}_{i}(M,H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R))=H^{d-i}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M).

Suppose for contradiction that it is tor-rigid. Then
\operatorname{Tor}^{R}_{1}(M,H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R))=H^{d-1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)=0
by Grothendieck’s vanishing theorem (see
[
9
, 6.1.2]
), as
d_{0}<d-1
. On the other hand,
\operatorname{Tor}^{R}_{d-t}(M,H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R))=H^{t}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)\neq 0
, since
t=\operatorname{depth}(M)
(see
[
9
, 6.2.7]
). Therefore
H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)
is not rigid.
∎

4.
Consequences of (non-)vanishing of
\beta_{d}(M)

4.1.
Non-vanishing of
\beta_{d}(M)

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.3
, i.e., the non-vanishing of
\beta_{d}(M)
for
\mathfrak{m}
-torsion modules over Cohen–Macaulay rings, and explore consequences for the injective hull
E_{R}(k)
.

Definition 4.1
.

An
R
-module
M
is called
\mathfrak{m}
-torsion
if
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)=M
, i.e., for each
m\in M
there exists
n>0
such that
\mathfrak{m}^{n}m=0
.

Remark 4.2
.

Every module of finite length is
\mathfrak{m}
-torsion. Any direct limit of
\mathfrak{m}
-torsion is again
\mathfrak{m}
-torsion. In particular,
any artinian module is
\mathfrak{m}
-torsion.
The module
\oplus_{\mathbb{N}}E_{R}(k)
is a non-artinian module which is
\mathfrak{m}
-torsion.

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose
(R,\mathfrak{m})
is a Cohen–Macaulay local ring of dimension
d
. Let
0\neq\Omega
be an
\mathfrak{m}
-torsion module. Then
\beta_{d}(\Omega)\neq 0
.

Proof.

Assume, toward a contradiction, that
\beta_{d}(\Omega)=\dim_{k}(\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(k,\Omega))=0
, i.e.,
\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(k,\Omega)=0
. We first establish that
\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(L,\Omega)=0
for every finite-length module
L
. This follows by induction on the length
\ell(L)
: the case
\ell(L)=1
reduces to
L\cong k
, which holds by the assumption. For
\ell(L)>1
, choose a maximal proper submodule
L^{\prime}\subsetneq L
, so
\ell(L^{\prime})=\ell(L)-1
and
L/L^{\prime}\cong k
. The induced long exact sequence in Tor from
0\to L^{\prime}\to L\to k\to 0
gives
\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(L^{\prime},\Omega)\to\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(L,\Omega)\to\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(k,\Omega)
, where the left and right terms vanish by the induction hypothesis and the initial assumption, respectively; hence
\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(L,\Omega)=0
. Now consider any artinian module
A
, expressed as a direct limit
A=\varinjlim A_{i}
of its finitely generated submodules
A_{i}\subseteq A
. Since
A
is artinian, each
A_{i}
has finite length, so the preceding paragraph yields
\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(A_{i},\Omega)=0
for all
i
. As Tor commutes with direct limits, we obtain
\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(A,\Omega)=\varinjlim\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(A_{i},\Omega)=0
. Since
R
is Cohen–Macaulay, choose a system of parameters
\mathbf{x}=x_{1},\dots,x_{d}
. Then the local cohomology module
H_{\mathfrak{m}}^{d}(R)\cong H_{\mathbf{x}}^{d}(R)
is artinian, and the isomorphism
\Omega\cong\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(H_{\mathbf{x}}^{d}(R),\Omega)
from
[
4
, Proposition 7.8]
applies. But because
H_{\mathfrak{m}}^{d}(R)
is artinian, the vanishing result just proven gives
\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(H_{\mathfrak{m}}^{d}(R),\Omega)=0
, which forces
\Omega=0
, contradicting the hypothesis that
\Omega
is non-zero. Therefore
\beta_{d}(\Omega)\neq 0
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

Concerning the other Betti numbers
\beta_{i}(\Omega)
, one cannot expect a similar statement. The following well-known fact illustrates this phenomenon.

Fact 4.5
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a regular local ring and let
E=E_{R}(R/\mathfrak{m})
denote the injective hull of the residue field. Then

\beta_{i}(E)\neq 0
if and only if
i=\dim R.

Proof.

Since
R
is regular, the injective dimension of
E
is precisely
\dim R
. The minimal injective resolution of
E
therefore has length
\dim R
. Applying Matlis duality to this resolution yields a minimal free resolution of the completion
\widehat{R}
over itself. As
\widehat{R}
is regular of dimension
d=\dim R
, its
d
-th Betti number is non-zero while all lower Betti numbers vanish; the same conclusion thus holds for
E
.
∎

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a
d
-dimensional Cohen–Macaulay local ring and let
0\neq M
be an
R
-module. If
\beta_{d}(M)=0
, then
\beta_{d+1}(M/\Gamma_{\mathfrak{m}}(M))\neq 0
if and only if
0pt(M)>0.

Proof.

Suppose first that
0pt(M)>0
. By
[
28
, Theorem V.2]
, we have
\beta_{d+1}(M)=0
.
Since
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)=0
, we have

\beta_{d+1}(M/\Gamma_{\mathfrak{m}}(M))=\beta_{d+1}(M)=0
.
Now, assume that
0pt(M)=0
.
By
[
28
, Theorem V.2]
, we have
\beta_{d+1}(M)=0
.
Consider the short exact sequence

0\longrightarrow\Gamma_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow\overline{M}:=M/\Gamma_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)\longrightarrow 0.

Applying
\operatorname{Tor}_{*}^{R}(-,k)
gives the induced connecting homomorphism

0=\operatorname{Tor}_{d+1}^{R}({M},k)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Tor}_{d+1}^{R}(\overline{M},k)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(\Gamma_{\mathfrak{m}}(M),k)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(M,k)=0,

yielding an isomorphism

\beta_{d+1}(\overline{M})=\beta_{d}(\Gamma_{\mathfrak{m}}(M))\quad(\ast)

Since
0pt(M)=0
,
we know
0\neq\Gamma_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)
is
\mathfrak{m}
-torsion, so it follows immediately from Theorem
4.3

that
\beta_{d}(\Gamma_{\mathfrak{m}}(M))\neq 0
.
In view of
(\ast)
,
\beta_{d+1}(M/\Gamma_{\mathfrak{m}}(M))\neq 0
.
∎

Over regular rings, Corollary
4.6
can be polished more:

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a
d
-dimensional regular local ring and let
M
be an
R
-module. Then

\beta_{d}(M)\neq 0
if and only if
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)\neq 0.

Proof.

(\Leftarrow)
Suppose
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)\neq 0
. Then there exists a non-zero element
x\in M
annihilated by
\mathfrak{m}
, yielding an injection
0\to R/\mathfrak{m}\xrightarrow{\varphi}M
. Let
C
denote the cokernel, so we have a short exact sequence
0\to k\to M\to C\to 0
. Since
\operatorname{gldim}(R)=d
, we have
\operatorname{Tor}_{d+1}^{R}(k,C)=0
. Applying
\operatorname{Tor}_{*}^{R}(k,-)
gives the exact segment

0=\operatorname{Tor}_{d+1}^{R}(k,C)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(k,k)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(k,M).

Because
\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(k,k)\cong k\neq 0
, it follows that
\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(k,M)\neq 0
, and hence
\beta_{d}(M)\neq 0
.

(\Rightarrow)
Conversely, suppose
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)=0
. Write
M=\bigcup_{i\in I}M_{i}
as a direct limit of its finitely generated submodules. Since each
M_{i}\subseteq M
, we have
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{m}}(M_{i})\subseteq\Gamma_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)=0
, so
0pt(M_{i})>0
for every
i
. By the Auslander–Buchsbaum formula applied over the regular local ring
R
, each
M_{i}
has projective dimension strictly less than
d
; hence
\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(k,M_{i})=0
for all
i
. Taking direct limits and using the commutation of Tor with direct limits yields
\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(k,M)=0
, so
\beta_{d}(M)=0
.
∎

4.2.
Matlis reflexive Modules and local cohomology

This section contains further results on Matlis reflexive modules and the relationship between Betti numbers and local cohomology, including a proof that
\beta_{d}(M)=0
implies
H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M^{\vee})=0
.

By
(-)^{\vee}
we mean the Matlis dual.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a
d
-dimensional local ring and let
M
be an
R
-module such that
\beta_{d}(M)=0
. The following holds:

(i)

If

\beta_{d}(M)=0
, then
H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M^{\vee})=0
.

(ii)

If

\mu_{d}(M)=0
then
H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)=0
.

Proof.

(i) The condition
\beta_{d}(M)=0
means
\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(k,M)=0
. By using the isomorphism
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(k,M)^{\vee}\cong\operatorname{Ext}^{i}_{R}(k,M^{\vee})
, we obtain
\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{d}(k,M^{\vee})=0
. Since
H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M^{\vee})\cong\varinjlim_{n}\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{d}(R/\mathfrak{m}^{n},M^{\vee}),
and each
\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{d}(R/\mathfrak{m}^{n},M^{\vee})
is a subquotient of a direct sum of copies of
\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{d}(k,M^{\vee})=0
(for more details, see the argument of Theorem
4.3
), it then follows that
H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M^{\vee})=0
, as claimed.

(ii)
The condition
\mu_{d}(M)=0
means
\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{d}(k,M)=0
.
It follows by the same trick as part (i) that
\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{d}(R/\mathfrak{m}^{n},M)=0
for each
n
. This implies that
H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)=0
.
∎

Corollary 4.9
.

Let
R
be a regular local ring of dimension
d
and let
M
be
\mathfrak{m}
-torsion-free. Then
H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M^{\vee})=0
.

Proof.

Since
M
is
\mathfrak{m}
-torsion-free, we have
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)=0
. By Proposition
4.7
, this implies
\beta_{d}(M)=0
. The result then follows immediately from the previous proposition.
∎

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
R
be a regular local ring of dimension
d
. If
\mu_{d}(M)=0
and
M
is Matlis reflexive, then
H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)=0
.

Proof.

The condition
\mu_{d}(M)=0
means
\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{d}(k,M)=0
. By local duality, this is equivalent to
\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(k,M^{\vee})=0
, i.e.,
\beta_{d}(M^{\vee})=0
. Applying the preceding proposition to
M^{\vee}
, we get
H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}((M^{\vee})^{\vee})=0
. Since
M
is Matlis reflexive,
(M^{\vee})^{\vee}\cong M
, and therefore
H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)=0
.
∎

Proposition 4.11
.

Let
R
be a
d
-dimensional regular local ring and let
M
be a Matlis reflexive and complete module. If
\mu_{d}(M)=0
, then
\dim(\operatorname{Supp}(M))<d
.

Proof.

Since
R
is complete, every Matlis reflexive module admits a short exact sequence

0\longrightarrow F\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow A\longrightarrow 0,

where
F
is finitely generated and
A
is artinian (see
[
14
, page 92, Ex. 6]
). The hypothesis
\mu_{d}(M)=0
means that
\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(k,M)=0
. For a regular local ring,
\operatorname{Tor}_{d}^{R}(k,M)\cong\operatorname{Ext}^{d}_{R}(k,M)
, so we have
\operatorname{Ext}^{d}_{R}(k,M)=0
, equivalently
H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)=0
. Applying the long exact sequence in local cohomology to
0\to F\to M\to A\to 0
gives
H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(F)=0
, since
H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(A)=0
because
A
is artinian and
d>0
. By Grothendieck’s vanishing theorem,
\dim(F)<d
. Therefore,

\dim(\operatorname{Supp}(M))=\max\{\dim(\operatorname{Supp}(F)),\dim(\operatorname{Supp}(A))\}<d,

as both
F
and
A
have support of dimension strictly less than
d
.
∎

Corollary 4.12
.

If
R
is d-dimensional, then
\beta_{d}(E_{R}(k))\neq 0
.

Proof.

Suppose not. Then
\operatorname{Tor}_{d}(E_{R}(k),k)=0
. By Proposition
4.8
,
H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(E_{R}(k)^{\vee})=0
. But
E_{R}(k)^{\vee}\cong\widehat{R}
, and therefore
H^{d}_{\mathfrak{m}}(\widehat{R})\neq 0
, a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 4.13
.

Suppose
R
is Cohen–Macaulay. Then
\beta_{i}(E_{R}(k))=0\iff i\neq\dim(R)
.

Proof.

Similar to before, note that
E_{R}(k)^{\vee}\simeq\widehat{R}
and
\widehat{R}
is Cohen–Macaulay, and
H^{i}_{\mathfrak{m}}(\widehat{R})\neq 0\iff i=\dim(R)
.
∎

Remark 4.14
.

Is the Cohen–Macaulay hypothesis really needed? Yes: if the equivalence holds, then
\operatorname{depth}(R)=\dim(R)
.

Proposition 4.15
.

Let
R
be a complete local ring and let
M
be a nonzero Matlis reflexive module of dimension
r\neq 1
. Then
H^{r}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)\neq 0
.

Proof.

If
r=0
, then
M
is artinian, whence
H^{0}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)=M\neq 0
. Now assume
r>0
. Since
R
is complete, every Matlis reflexive module admits a short exact sequence

0\to F\to M\to A\to 0,

where
F
is finitely generated and
A
is artinian (see
[
14
, page 92, Ex. 6]
). Recall that , with
F\neq 0
, as
r>0=\dim(A)
. Consequently,
\dim(M)=\dim(F)=r
. Applying the long exact sequence in local cohomology,
to the above sequence yields

H^{r-1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(A)\longrightarrow H^{r}_{\mathfrak{m}}(F)\longrightarrow H^{r}_{\mathfrak{m}}(M).
One has
H^{r-1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(A)=0
by Grothendieck’s vanishing theorem because

(here, we used the assumption

Thus, we obtain an inclusion

∎

Example 4.16
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a
1
-dimensional complete local integral domain with field of fractions
Q
. Then
\dim(Q)=1
, and the short exact sequence

0\longrightarrow R\longrightarrow Q\longrightarrow Q/R\longrightarrow 0

exhibits
Q/R\cong H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)
as an artinian module, so
Q
is Matlis reflexive. However,
Q
is injective, whence
H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(Q)=0
. This shows that the assumption

in the proposition is necessary. Moreover, since

is injective, we have
\mu_{1}(Q)=0
and
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{R}(R/\mathfrak{m},Q)=0

Remark 4.17
.

A natural question arises: how can one construct a Matlis reflexive module that is not a direct sum of a finitely generated module and an artinian module? Here, we present a such module. Consider to any
1
-dimensional complete local ring
(R,\mathfrak{m})
. In this setting, by using a result of Matlis, we know
\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{R}(E(k),R)
is non-zero, so there exists a non-split short exact sequence

0\longrightarrow R\longrightarrow K\longrightarrow E(k)\longrightarrow 0.

The middle module
K
is then Matlis reflexive, yet it is neither artinian nor noetherian, and in particular it cannot be decomposed as a direct sum of a finitely generated module and an artinian module.

5.
The Absolute Integral Closure: Bass and Betti Numbers

This section is devoted to the absolute integral closure and contains our main results on both Tor and Ext for
R^{+}
.

5.1.
Tor Results for
R^{+}

We start with the following auxiliary result.

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose
R
is Cohen–Macaulay and
M
is a module with
0pt(M)=\dim(R)-1
. Then
\beta_{1}(M)\neq 0
.

Proof.

Assume to the contrary that
\beta_{1}(M)=0
, i.e.,
\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{R}(M,k)=0
. Let
\underline{x}=x_{1},\dots,x_{d-1}
be both an
M
-sequence and an
R
-sequence, which exists because
0pt(M)=d-1
and
R
is Cohen–Macaulay. As observed in
[
29
, Lemma 2.1]
, we have

\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{\overline{R}}\!\left(\frac{M}{\underline{x}M},k\right)\cong\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{R}(M,k)=0,

where
\overline{R}=R/(\underline{x})
. By a standard argument, the vanishing of
\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{\overline{R}}(\overline{M},k)
implies
\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{\overline{R}}(\overline{M},L)=0
for every finite-length
\overline{R}
-module
L
. In particular, choosing
y
to be a system of parameters for the one-dimensional Cohen–Macaulay ring
\overline{R}
, we get

0=\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{\overline{R}}\!\left(\overline{M},\frac{\overline{R}}{y\overline{R}}\right)=\ker(\overline{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle y}}{{\longrightarrow}}\overline{M}).

This forces multiplication by
y
on
\overline{M}
to be injective. It follows that
0pt(\overline{M})=1
. Consequently,
0pt(M)=0pt_{\overline{R}}(\overline{M})+(d-1)=d
, contradicting the hypothesis that
0pt(M)=d-1
. Therefore
\beta_{1}(M)\neq 0
.
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

Suppose
R
is a
d
-dimensional Cohen–Macaulay local ring containing
\mathbb{Q}
. If
d>2
, then
\operatorname{Tor}_{1}^{R}(R^{+},k)\neq 0
.

Proof.

For
d=3
, the result follows from the preceding proposition. Indeed,
R^{+}
is a direct limit of normal rings, each of which satisfies Serre’s condition
(S_{2})
, so
0pt(R^{+})\geq 2
. Since
R^{+}
is not big Cohen–Macaulay (see
[
19
, Ex. 7.2]
), we must have
0pt(R^{+})=\dim(R)-1=2
. The proposition then yields
\beta_{1}(R^{+})\neq 0
. For
d>3
, the result is established in
[
23
]
.
∎

Example 5.3
.

Suppose
R
is a
2
-dimensional regular local ring. Then
\operatorname{Tor}_{+}^{R}(R^{+},k)=0
. Indeed, this is easy to see
0pt_{R}(R^{+})=2
.
Recall from
[
8
, 4.1]
that
0=\mathrm{depth}(R)-\mathrm{depth}(R^{+})=\mathrm{resdim}(R^{+})
. Therefore
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(S,k)=0
for all
i>0
. Alternatively, by the additional completeness assumption, one may use
[
22
, 23.1]
and deduce that
R\to R^{+}
is flat, because direct limit of flat modules is flat, and
R^{+}
is a direct limit of local Cohen-Macaulay rings.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
R
be a quotient singularity containing
\mathbb{Q}
. If
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(R^{+},R/\mathfrak{m})=0
for some
i>0
, then
R
is regular.

Proof.

By definition, a quotient singularity is of the form
R=k[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]^{G}
for a finite group
G
acting linearly on
A:=k[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
. Recall that
R\subseteq A
is an integral extension, and so,
R
is normal. Since
R
is normal, every finite extension of
R
splits (see
[
23
, Lemma 2.1]
). Hence the inclusion
A\hookrightarrow R^{+}
splits, so
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(A,k)
is a direct summand of
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(R^{+},k)=0
. Thus
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(A,k)=0
as well. Since
A
is finitely generated as an
R
-module, it has finite flat dimension over
R
. By the Gruson–Raynaud theorem,
A
has finite projective dimension over
R
. As
A
is Cohen–Macaulay, the Auslander–Buchsbaum formula implies that
A
is flat over
R
. Since
A
is regular, it follows from
[
10
, 2.2.12(a)]
that
R
is regular.
∎

Discussion 5.5
.

(Reduction to complete rings).
Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be an excellent local domain. There are three natural
objects associated to
R
, namely

\{(\widehat{R})^{+},\widehat{R}\otimes_{R}R^{+},\widehat{R^{+}}\}.

It may be worth to finding conditions for which there is an
injective, surjective, or isomorphisms between these.
For example, let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be an excellent analytically irreducible local domain.
Assume that

\widehat{R}\otimes_{R}R^{+}

is a domain. Then there exists a natural injective homomorphism

\widehat{R}\otimes_{R}R^{+}\hookrightarrow(\widehat{R})^{+}.
Indeed, let
K:=\operatorname{Frac}(R)
and fix an algebraic closure
\overline{K}
of

K
. Since

R^{+}=\varinjlim_{L/K}\overline{R}_{L},

where
\overline{R}_{L}
denotes the integral closure of
R
in a finite field

\widehat{R}\otimes_{R}R^{+}\cong\varinjlim_{L/K}\bigl(\widehat{R}\otimes_{R}\overline{R}_{L}\bigr).
Since
R
is excellent, each ring

\widehat{R}\otimes_{R}\overline{R}_{L}

is normal. Moreover, it is finite over
\widehat{R}
, hence integral over

\widehat{R}
. Therefore

\widehat{R}\otimes_{R}\overline{R}_{L}

is contained in the integral closure of
\widehat{R}
inside an algebraic
closure of
\operatorname{Frac}(\widehat{R})
, namely
(\widehat{R})^{+}
.
Consequently,

\widehat{R}\otimes_{R}\overline{R}_{L}\subseteq(\widehat{R})^{+}

for every finite extension
L/K
. Passing to the direct limit yields

\widehat{R}\otimes_{R}R^{+}=\varinjlim_{L/K}\bigl(\widehat{R}\otimes_{R}\overline{R}_{L}\bigr)\subseteq(\widehat{R})^{+}.
Hence there is a natural injective homomorphism

\widehat{R}\otimes_{R}R^{+}\hookrightarrow(\widehat{R})^{+}.

The following completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
R
be a homogeneous Cohen–Macaulay domain of dimension
d
over an algebraically closed field of characteristic
0
. Assume that
R
is of finite Cohen–Macaulay type. If

\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(R^{+},k)=0

for some
i>d
, then
R
is a UFD, and of multiplicity at most two.

Proof.

By the classification of Eisenbud–Herzog (see
[
18
, Theorem 16.6]
), the ring
R
is isomorphic to one of the following:

(i)

k[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]

\exists n\geqslant 0
; (ii)
k[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]/(x_{0}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2})

\exists n\geqslant 0
;

(iii)

k[x]/(x^{m})

\exists m\geqslant 1
;  (iv)
k[x,y]/(xy(x+y))
;  (v)
k[x,y,z]/(xy,yz,xz)
;

(vi)

k[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]/I_{2}\!\begin{pmatrix}x_{0}&\cdots&x_{m-1}\\
x_{1}&\cdots&x_{m}\end{pmatrix}
for some
m\geqslant 1
;

(vii)

k[x,y,z,u,v]/I_{2}\!\begin{pmatrix}x&y&u\\
y&z&v\end{pmatrix}
; and (viii)
k[x,y,z,u,v,w]/I_{2}\begin{pmatrix}x&y&z\\
y&u&v\\
z&v&w\end{pmatrix}
.

Since
R
is a domain, cases (iii)–(v) cannot occur. Case (i) is regular, and hence
R
is a UFD.

By
[
18
, theorems 16.2 and 16.4]
, the rings in (vii) and (viii) are quotient singularities. Moreover, by
[
18
, p. 291]
, the ring in (vi) is isomorphic to

k[u^{m},u^{m-1}v,\ldots,v^{m}],

and hence is also a quotient singularity. Therefore Corollary
5.4
shows that

\operatorname{Tor}_{j}^{R}(R^{+},k)\neq 0

for all
j>0
. Thus these cases are excluded.
It remains to consider case (ii), namely the
A_{1}
-singularity

R=k[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]/(x_{0}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2}).

When
d=2
, the result follows from Corollary
5.4
; indeed, by a theorem of Esnault–Herzog, such a ring is a quotient singularity. Alternatively, one may use
[
23
, Theorem (A)]
.
Assume now that
d\geq 3
. It is well known that

\operatorname{Cl}(R)=0

whenever
d\geq 4
; see
[
13
, Section 11]
. Hence
R
is a UFD.
The remaining case is
d=3
. In this case

R\cong k[x,y,z,w]/(xw-yz),

and

\operatorname{Cl}(R)\cong\mathbb{Z}

by
[
13
, 11.3]
. Thus
R
is not a UFD.
Let
\mathfrak{\mathfrak{}}p=(x,y)
, which generates
\operatorname{Cl}(R)
. Following
[
25
, Example 2]
, one can construct a module-finite normal domain extension

A\cong R\oplus\mathfrak{p}^{(-2)}\,\eta

contained in
R^{+}
, where
\eta\in R^{+}
is an element without perfect square in
R
, we may apply more restriction on it (see
[
25
, Example 2]
).
On the other hand, as observed in
[
23
]
, the assumption

\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(R^{+},k)=0

implies

\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(A,k)=0.
Now, recall that
\mathfrak{p}^{(-2)}\eta
is a direct summand of
A
. Thus,
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(\mathfrak{p}^{(-2)}\eta,k)=0.
This shows
\mathfrak{p}^{(-2)}\eta
has finite projective dimension, as it is finitely generated as an
R
-module. Recall that
\mathfrak{p}^{(-2)}\eta\cong\mathfrak{p}^{(-2)}
as an

R
-module.
Set
M:=\mathfrak{p}^{(-2)}=(\mathfrak{p}^{(2)})^{\ast}
. It is easy to see the following properties; a)
\operatorname{pd}(M)<\infty
,
b)
\operatorname{End}_{R}(M)=\overline{R}=R
is a projective
R
-module, and
c)
M
is reflexive.
Then
M
is a locally Gorenstein module (see e.g.,
[
3
, Fact 3.5]
). In particular it is Cohen-Macaulay, but then
A
is Cohen-Macaulay, which is not (see
[
25
, Example 2]
).
Therefore the three-dimensional quadric hypersurface cannot occur. Hence
R
is a UFD.
To complete the proof, recall that

e(k[\underline{x}]/(f))=\deg(f)
,
where
f
is homogeneous.
∎

Observation 5.7
.

(i)

Let
A=\frac{k[x,y,z]}{(f_{1},f_{2})}
be a
2
-dimensional ring. Assume that
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{A}(A^{+},k)=0
. Then
(f_{1},f_{2})=(f)
for some polynomial
f
.

(ii)

Suppose that
f
is a binomial,
f=x^{a_{1}}y^{b_{1}}z^{c_{1}}-x^{a_{2}}y^{b_{2}}z^{c_{2}}
. In this case
R=k[x,y,z]/(f)
is a toric ring.
For simplicity, consider
f=x^{3}-zy
. Define a
k
-algebra homomorphism
\pi:k[x,y,z]\longrightarrow k[u_{1},u_{2}]
by
x\longmapsto u_{1}u_{2}
,
y\longmapsto u_{1}^{2}u_{2}
, and
z\longmapsto u_{1}u_{2}^{2}
. Then
\pi(x^{3}-zy)=(u_{1}u_{2})^{3}-(u_{1}u_{2}^{2})(u_{1}^{2}u_{2})=0
. Hence
x^{3}-zy\in\ker(\pi)
. Since
\ker(\pi)
is a height one prime ideal of the UFD ring
k[x,y,z]
, it is principal. Therefore
\ker(\pi)=(x^{3}-zy)
. Consequently,
R=\frac{k[x,y,z]}{(x^{3}-zy)}\hookrightarrow k[u_{1},u_{2}]
. Since
k[u_{1},u_{2}]
is normal, we obtain an inclusion
R\subseteq k[u_{1},u_{2}]\subseteq R^{+}
.
Now assume that
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(k,R^{+})=0
. Because
k[u_{1},u_{2}]
is a direct summand of
R^{+}
(by the usual splitting argument), it follows that
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(k,k[u_{1},u_{2}])=0
. Hence
k[u_{1},u_{2}]
has finite flat dimension as an
R
-module. Since
k[u_{1},u_{2}]
is Cohen–Macaulay, the Auslander–Buchsbaum formula implies that
R
is regular. But
R=\frac{k[x,y,z]}{(x^{3}-zy)}
is not regular, a contradiction. Therefore
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(k,R^{+})\neq 0
.

(iii)

More generally, suppose
H\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{n}
is an affine semigroup. Define the semigroup ring

R:=\mathbb{Q}[H]:=\bigoplus_{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in H}\mathbb{Q}\,x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}].

Suppose
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(k,R^{+})=0
for some
i>0
. Then we know
R
is normal, which implies that
H
is a normal semigroup. Recall that a normal semigroup satisfies
H=H_{1}\cap\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{t}
with
H_{1}
is positive provided. Since by assumption
H\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{n}
, it follows that
H
is positive. Hence
H
is normal and positive. Such semigroups are called
full
. For every full semigroup we know that
k[H]
is a direct summand of a polynomial ring (for more details, see
[
10
, Section 6.1]
).

5.2.
Ext Results for

This subsection contains our main Ext-vanishing results for the absolute integral closure.
Here is the reason that explains why we study the condition
\mu_{j}(R^{+})=0
only for

.
The results here are motivated from
[

,

.

Example 5.8
.

Let

be any two dimensional local ring. Then
\mu_{2}(R^{+})\neq 0
.

Proof.

In this case

is direct limit of normal rings, and so it big Cohen-Macaulay. This shows Ext-grade of

is two, and so
\mu_{2}(R^{+})\neq 0
.
∎

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a Gorenstein local ring of prime characteristic with isolated singularity. If
\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{i}(R/\mathfrak{m},R^{+})=0

, then

is regular.

Proof.

It follows from
[
28
, Page 226]
,

has finite injective dimension, here we need Cohen-Macaulayness of

[

, any injective module over a Gorenstein ring is of finite flat dimension. Thus

has finite flat dimension. In particular,
\operatorname{Tor}^{R}_{i}(R/\mathfrak{m},R^{+})=0
for all sufficiently large

. Recall from
[

, Corollary 3.5]
that this forces

to be regular.
∎

Remark 5.10
.

One has
\operatorname{Ext}^{d}_{R}(R/\mathfrak{m},R^{+})\neq 0
for complete
R
under the above hypotheses. Suppose not. By the Cohen structure theorem,

is module-finite over a regular ring

. But

. Then by Proposition

,

. A theorem of Hochster
[

says that

, a contradiction.

Proposition 5.11
.

Let
R
be an
\mathbb{N}
-graded normal domain of dimension

, finitely generated over an equicharacteristic zero field

. If
\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{i}(k,R^{+})=0

is regular.

Proof.

Normal rings of dimension two are Cohen-Macaulay, and satisfies Serre condition
(R_{1})
. Since
\dim(R)
it is of isolated singularity. Now, by
[

, Page 226]
,
R^{+}
has finite injective dimension. Since
\mathbb{Q}\subseteq R
, and in view of
[

, Lemma 2.1]
, we know

splits. Thanks to our vanishing assumption, it follows that
\operatorname{Ext}^{i}_{R}(R/\mathfrak{m},R)=0
, so

is Gorenstein, because by
[

]
Bass numbers are only nonzero in the range

, i.e.,

has finite flat dimension. Finally, we use
[

, Theorem A]
to deduce that

is regular.
∎

Proposition 5.12
.

Let
R
be a local ring of dimension

with a quotient and isolated singularity containing

. If
\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{i}(k,R^{+})=0
for some

, then

Proof.

Proposition 5.15
.

Suppose
R
is
d
-dimensional
F
-pure with isolated singularity and
\operatorname{char}R=p>0
and
\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{i}(R/\mathfrak{m},R^{\infty})=0
for some
i>d
. Then
R
is regular.

Proof.

Since purity behave well with respect to direct limit, and
R
is F-pure, we see
0\to R\to R^{\infty}
is pure. Now, the above fact implies that

\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{i}(R/\mathfrak{m},R)\subseteq\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{i}(R/\mathfrak{m},R^{\infty})
.
So
\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{i}(R/\mathfrak{m},R)=0
.
Again, by
[
24
]
,
R
is Gorenstein. In particular, it is Cohen-Macaulay. This and
\operatorname{Ext}_{R}^{i}(k,R^{\infty})=0
enable us to use
[
28
, page 226]
, and deduce that
R^{\infty}
has finite injective dimension.
By Gorenstein property, we see
R^{\infty}
has finite flat dimension. In particular,
\operatorname{Tor}_{i}^{R}(R/\mathfrak{m},R^{\infty})=0
for some
i
. By
[
1
, 3.6]
,
R
is regular.
∎
