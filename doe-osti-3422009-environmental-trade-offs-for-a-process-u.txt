 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 Phase II work is meant to develop an advanced understanding of how production of a renewable
 diesel blendstock from lignocellulosic biomass feedstocks via aerobic respiration at different
 production scales affects emissions of criteria pollutants and greenhouse gases (GHGs), local and
 regional air quality. The results could inform decisions by stakeholders (e.g., industry,
 community, air quality planners, investors) regarding relationships and tradeoffs between
 multiple environmental impacts and the challenges and opportunities for the design and
 deployment of a sustainable biofuel supply chain.

 CRADA benefit to DOE, Participant, and US Taxpayer:

 • Uses the laboratory’s core competencies

 Summary of Research Results:

 Task 1: Update the Aspen Plus process models for the biochemical conversion of sugars to
 hydrocarbon pathway for two feedstocks (i.e., INL uniform feedstock blend (INL 2014) and corn
 stover).

 a) The updated models will include automation features to allow users to select one of the
 three biorefinery sizes and one of the two lignin uses for the conversion process. The
 three biorefinery sizes start with NLR’s standard design size of 2,000 dry metric tons of
 biomass inputs per day (dmtd), increasing to the range of typical petroleum refiners (e.g.,
 9000 dmtd) The Aspen Plus model will provide users with two options for lignin uses;
 options under consideration include production of process heat and electricity with excess
 electricity to be exported to the grid and production of pellets as a by-product for sale.
 The two variations of lignin uses are expected to yield insights into how by-products may
 affect the “net” GHG emissions, fossil energy consumption, and air permitting levels
 (e.g., due to differences in carbon, air pollutants, energy balances of the conversion
 process).
 b) Develop a manuscript to document how the process design for renewable diesel
 blendstock (RDB) production from 2 selected feedstocks at 3 scales with 2 different uses
 of lignin could affect process outcomes such as product yield. (Publication 1)

 Task 1 Results

 The Aspen Plus models for the biochemical conversion pathway were updated and expanded to
 handle two feedstocks (corn stover and the INL uniform blend). Three different biorefinery
 scales (2000, 5100/5200, and 9100 dry metric tons/day) were incorporated. An automation
 feature was added to allow switching between two lignin-use scenarios:

 • Lignin combusted for on-site heat and power with surplus electricity exported.
 • Lignin diverted to produce lignin pellets as a co-product.
 These variations formed 12 total “design permutations.” The updates are documented in [3]
 where we compare the resulting process outputs (e.g., sugar yields, energy balances) across these
 permutations. The differences in feedstock composition and scale influenced the flowrates of
 sugar intermediates, residual solids (including lignin), and process heat and power demands.

 Multiple feedstocks, scales, and lignin uses were introduced to capture realistic commercial
 scenarios and to explore how by-products (electricity or pellets) influence overall energy
 balances and subsequent emissions (e.g., net GHG and criteria air pollutants).
 The automation helps stakeholders rapidly evaluate different combinations without needing
 separate process models for each scenario.

 and lignin use.

 Task 2: Estimate biorefinery emission of air pollutants regulated under the Clean Air Act’s New
 Source Review (NSR) and Title V programs and assess the applicability of relevant federal air
 regulations to the biochemical conversion process.

 a) Following the protocol documented in NLR’s technical reports, (see
 https://www.NLR.gov/docs/fy16osti/62547.pdf for an example), NLR will review and
 assess the applicability of federal air regulations (New Source Performance Standards
 (NSPS) and National Emission Standards from Hazardous Air Pollutants (NESHAP)
 codified in Title 40, Parts 60, 61, and 63 of the Code of Federal Regulations) for the
 biorefinery and each unit operation for the 12 design permutations. The air pollutants to
 be evaluated include nitrogen oxides (NOx), sulfur dioxide (SO2), carbon monoxide (CO),
 volatile organic compounds (VOCs), particulate matter (PM, PM2.5 and PM10), lead
 (Pb), hydrogen sulfide (H2S), and hazardous air pollutants (HAPs)
 b) Estimate potential to emit (PTE) for air pollutants, which are regulated under the NSR
 and Title V permit programs and under applicable NSPS and NESHAP rules. Although
 GHG emissions are not regulated for biorefineries, our PTE analysis will include the
 calculation of gross emissions of three GHGs, i.e., CO2, CH4, and N2O.
 i. Our PTE estimates will utilize best available information, such as material and
 energy balances from the Aspen Plus process model, EPA’s AP-42 emission factors
 and RACT/BACT/LAER clearinghouse data; where those sources do not provide the
 emission estimates we need, we will investigate emission factors from existing air
 permits or permit applications for biofuel facilities, petroleum refineries or other
 analogous sources.
 ii. Since the boiler is the biorefinery’s key contributor to multiple air pollutants
 regulated under the New Source Review program, NSPS, and NESHPA, NLR will
 attempt to improve the accuracy of emission estimates for the boiler employed in the
 design case through gathering information from multiple sources, including air
 permits, open literature, boiler manufacturer’s test data, and experimental data from
 combustion labs. Because some information will only be made available after
 developing a relationship (i.e., is not already public data), some travel for site visits
 will be required.
 iii. The PTE of regulated air pollutants will be estimated for all 12 design
 permutations, although NLR will investigate the feasibility of reducing computational
 burden for instance through scaling emissions based on biorefinery size and whether
 feedstock type affects the magnitude or type of air pollutants likely to be emitted from
 the biorefinery. The insights from this effort will inform the decision on the number
 of model runs for air quality impact (refer to Task (4)).
 c) Determine whether the biorefinery per its design developed in Task 1 is expected to meet
 the potentially applicable federal air regulations (for 12 design permutations). If the
 biorefinery does not meet certain applicable regulations, NLR will evaluate control options
 that are required to ensure the facility complies with potentially applicable federal rules.

 4

 d) Adjust the Aspen Plus model to include emissions control technologies required for
 compliance with federal regulations.
 e) Assess siting/sizing strategies based on the PTE estimates and the major soruce thresholds
 under NSR and Title V programs. For instance, if PTE estimates indicate VOC or NOx
 emissions are above 50 but below 100 tons per year, siting a biorefinery in a serious or severe
 or extreme ozone non-attainment area will require a major source permit under the NSR
 program. However, siting the biorefinery in other areas is not expected to trigger major
 source permitting requirements due to its VOC or NOx emissions.
 f) Develop a manuscript documenting the regulatory analysis, potential air pollutant
 emissions (PTE) and how size, feedstock type, lignin use could affect permitting
 requirements and whether the biorefinery design would need to adopt additional emission
 controls to meet applicable federal regulations (Publication 2).

 Task 2 Results

 The team examined potential emissions of regulated pollutants under the Clean Air Act (e.g.,
 NOx, SO2, CO, VOC, PM, H2S, HAPs), focusing on the 12 design permutations (two
 feedstocks, three scales, two lignin uses).

 In [4] we systematically identified potentially applicable federal standards (NSPS, NESHAP) by
 reviewing each major unit operation (pretreatment, fermentation, boilers, etc.).

 Potential-to-Emit (PTE) was calculated using:

 • Process flow data from the Aspen Plus models.
 • Emission factors from AP-42 and (where unavailable) from other permits or boiler stack
 data.

 The boiler was highlighted as the primary emitter for multiple pollutants. We gathered additional
 emission-factor data from industry sources to better capture the effects of unconventional
 biomass fuels (lignin, sludge, off-gases). Results showed that all large-scale cases could exceed
 major-source thresholds for certain pollutants, indicating that additional controls or siting
 strategies might be required. The Aspen Plus model was then adjusted to incorporate emission
 control technologies (e.g., flue gas desulfurization, selective non-catalytic reduction, baghouses),
 and the PTE was re-estimated.

 We documented which regulations would be triggered depending on the plant’s scale and
 location and whether the facility could qualify as a minor or major source under NSR/Title V.

 5

 Quantifying the PTE for each design scenario is essential to determine whether the facility
 triggers major source permitting. Because many of these biochemical operations are novel, the
 standard boiler emission factors in AP-42 were updated with more specific data from air permits,
 stack tests, and open literature, thus refining the accuracy of PTE estimates.

 In addition, we compiled empirical emissions data for unconventional biomass-based fuels (e.g.,
 lignin, sludge, biogas)[2]. By integrating these more specific emission factors, we improved the
 accuracy of boiler PTE estimates, addressing gaps where AP-42 data may not fully capture the
 unique fuel composition in a biochemical biorefinery.

 stover feedstock at a plant scale of 2,000 dmtd, 5,200 dmtd, and 9,100 dmtd. The top panels show

 Task 3: Investigate the tradeoffs between net GHG emissions, air pollutant emissions, and level
 of air permitting required for the biorefinery designs that considered different feedstocks, lignin
 utilization and biorefinery scale options.

 a) Estimate net GHG emissions including credits where applicable (e.g., electricity sold to
 the grid of product displacement for lignin utilization) for each biorefinery design
 permutation.
 i. Within budget and scope, we will evaluate methods for emission allocation
 among primary product and by-products and the implications of displacing
 marginal vs. Average electricity on carbon credits biorefinery could recieve. The
 analysis will take into consideration the variations in carbon intensity of the
 displaced electricity based on the locations of the biorefinery.
 b) Analytically and visually explore the tradeoff space of the primary outcomes (net GHG
 emissions, emissions of regulated air pollutants) based on the 12 design permutations
 and, where feasible, varying other process parameters or design choices (e.g., how much
 electricity to use for plant needs vs. selling to the grid). The aim is to identify process
 designs that minimize the primary outcomes; if minimization of all emissions is not
 possible simultaneously, the analysis will illuminate the tradeoffs based on process
 design choices.
 c) Conduct Aspen Plus process modeling to minimize air pollutant emissions beyond that
 required to comply with federal regulations and with the goal of reducing air emissions to
 avoid being subject to major source permitting requirement. Additional control
 technologies will be added in the Aspen Plus process model. PTE will be re-calculated
 based on their documented control efficiency.
 i. This analysis will only consider 4 designs of a 2000 dry metric tons per day
 (dmtd) biorefinery. We focus on just the 2000 dmtd size because our previous
 analysis suggests that a larger biorefinery will likely require a major source permit
 even with additional pollutant controls.
 d) Develop a manuscript to document the tradeoffs between level of air permitting required
 (major/minor sources permitting), net GHG and non-GHG air pollutant emissions
 (Publication 3).

 Task 3 Results

 Building on the updated process models and PTE estimates, the analysis focused on “net” GHG
 emissions (CO2, CH4, N2O) while including coproduct displacement credits (electricity export
 or lignin pellets) and comparing them with regulated air pollutant emissions (e.g., NOx, SO2,
 PM).

 In [1] we show how each scenario’s net emissions vary depending on lignin usage and scale. We
 explore how certain designs can minimize GHG at the cost of higher PM or NOx, thus
 highlighting a tradeoff.

 7

 Additional emission controls beyond basic federal compliance (for the 2000 dmtd scale) were
 tested to see if the biorefinery could stay under major-source thresholds while also limiting GHG
 emissions. The smaller capacity (2000 dmtd) was chosen for deeper optimization because larger
 plants invariably had PTE above major-source thresholds even with extra controls.

 The goal was to find designs with acceptable regulatory status (minor source vs. major source)
 while also achieving low net GHG emissions. We demonstrate that diverting lignin to pellets can
 reduce certain criteria pollutants but might increase grid electricity purchases, thus affecting net
 CO2. These tradeoffs help identify whether a minimal-emissions scenario is feasible for both
 GHG and air pollutants.

 Figure 3 illustrates how net emissions (including coproduct credits) change across the
 permutations, and it captures the tradeoffs between pollutant control and GHG reduction.

 Figure 3: “Gross and net: A) greenhouse gas and B) criteria air pollutant emissions for a
 hypothetical biorefinery processing 2000 dry metric tons per day of two types of herbaceous
 biomass feedstocks (corn stover and uniform feedstock blend [UFB]) to renewable diesel in
 Georgia and Iowa. Two different uses of lignin are contrasted: lignin combusted to generate
 excess electricity exported to the grid and lignin used to produce pellets. The results presented in
 this figure are reported on a functional unit basis (per MJ of fuel produced), which considers
 displacement credits from coproducts.”[1]

 Task 4 Results

 We applied an air-quality screening approach—building on prior “Phase 1” feedstock-production
 analysis—to see how the marginal PM2.5 or other pollutants from the biorefinery (Task 3
 results) compare with upstream feedstock-production emissions.

 Although the core results are less extensively showcased in the main net-GHG tradeoff paper [1],
 the analysis indicates that site-level PM2.5 near the biorefinery can sometimes exceed that from
 feedstock fields, highlighting the significance of local controls.

 A “hotspot analysis” identified the prime contributors across the entire supply chain, focusing
 on the boiler for the biorefinery stage and field machinery or fertilizer-related emissions for the
 feedstock stage. The final outcome was a recommendation that controlling boiler and pelletizing-
 lignin operations could substantially reduce local air-quality impacts, sometimes rivaling or
 surpassing feedstock production impacts.

 Combining feedstock production and conversion-facility analysis shows which segment is the
 largest contributor to air-quality impacts (PM2.5 or ozone precursors). Highlighting these
 hotspots helps direct R&D or policy measures toward the processes most in need of improved
 controls.
