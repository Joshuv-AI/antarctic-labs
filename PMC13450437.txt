Working-memory effects of prefrontal tDCS across baseline cognitive-severity groups in schizophrenia: A secondary analysis of a randomized double-blind sham-controlled trial
Authors: Alberth Patricio Muñoz-Gualan, Sergio Padilla, Verónica Romero-Ferreiro, Sergio Cardona-Bejarano, Manuela Martín-Bejarano García, Álvaro Moleón-Ruiz, Roberto Rodriguez-Jimenez, Lorena García-Fernández
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCognitive impairment is common in schizophrenia and remains poorly responsive to pharmacological treatment. We examined working-memory change after prefrontal transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) across baseline cognitive-severity groups and tested whether baseline severity modified response.MethodsThis complete-case secondary analysis included 120 of 173 randomized participants (62 active tDCS, 58 sham) who completed the MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery before and after 10 bifrontal tDCS sessions. K-means clustering of seven baseline domain T-scores identified cognitive-severity groups. Treatment effects were assessed using descriptive comparisons and adjusted mixed-effects models. Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied jointly across 14 domain-by-group hypotheses, with Holm correction as a sensitivity analysis. To address circularity, groups were re-derived without baseline working memory, and moderation was tested using the time × treatment × group interaction.ResultsThe groups differed mainly in overall cognitive severity rather than profile shape. The active-versus-sham difference in working-memory change was 3.68 T-points (95% CI, 1.57–5.79) in the greater-impairment group and 2.59 T-points (95% CI, 0.90–4.28) in the less-severe-impairment group. Working memory survived joint false-discovery-rate correction in both groups, but Holm correction only in the greater-impairment group. No other domain survived joint correction. After reclustering without baseline working memory, the time × treatment × group interaction was not significant (estimate, 0.93 T-points; 95% CI, −1.71 to 3.56; p = .4915).ConclusionsPrefrontal tDCS showed a working-memory signal across both cognitive-severity groups, but baseline severity did not significantly modify response. The groups should be interpreted as positions along a cognitive-severity continuum rather than distinct profiles or subtypes.Keywords: Schizophrenia, Neuromodulation, tDCS, Working memory, Cognition

Introduction

Cognitive impairment is a core and enduring feature of schizophrenia, affecting multiple domains and representing one of the strongest determinants of functional outcome, disability, and quality of life (McCutcheon et al., 2023; Howie et al., 2026). Deficits in working memory, processing speed, attention, learning, and executive functioning are often present early in the course of illness, remain relatively stable over time, and show limited responsiveness to antipsychotic treatment (Dienel et al., 2022; Starzer et al., 2024). As a result, cognitive dysfunction remains a major unmet therapeutic need in schizophrenia, despite substantial advances in the pharmacological management of psychotic symptoms (Dienel et al., 2022; Kahn and Keefe, 2013).

Non-pharmacological interventions aimed at modulating neural plasticity and prefrontal network function have therefore attracted increasing interest (Michalopoulou et al., 2025). Among these approaches, transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) is a promising neuromodulatory technique because it can modulate cortical excitability, influence synaptic plasticity, and engage prefrontal circuits involved in cognitive control (Boudewyn et al., 2020; Yamada et al., 2022; Yamada and Sumiyoshi, 2021). In addition, compared with more resource-intensive neuromodulatory approaches, tDCS is relatively inexpensive, portable, well tolerated, and suitable for repeated multi-session protocols, making it a potentially scalable intervention for cognitive impairment in schizophrenia (García-Fernández et al., 2025a).

Clinical studies of tDCS in schizophrenia have reported improvements in several cognitive outcomes, including cognitive insight, higher-order neurocognitive performance, and working memory, although findings have not been uniform across trials (Chang et al., 2019; Lisoni et al., 2022; Smith et al., 2015; Narita et al., 2020; García-Fernández et al., 2025b; Schwippel et al., 2025; Kostova et al., 2020; Ciullo et al., 2021). Meta-analytic evidence also suggests that multi-session prefrontal tDCS may exert modest pro-cognitive effects in schizophrenia, but with substantial between-study heterogeneity (Narita et al., 2020; García-Fernández et al., 2025b). This variability may reflect differences in stimulation parameters, montage, treatment duration, outcome measures, concomitant interventions, and sample characteristics. Importantly, it may also reflect clinically meaningful heterogeneity in baseline cognitive status, which is rarely incorporated into trial design or analysis.

Schizophrenia is characterized by marked interindividual in the overall severity of cognitive impairment (McCutcheon et al., 2023; Howie et al., 2026; Koo et al., 2023). Patients may present with relatively circumscribed deficits, globally reduced cognitive performance, or comparatively preserved cognition. Such heterogeneity is particularly relevant for neuromodulation studies, because baseline cognitive vulnerability may influence both the magnitude and detectability of treatment-related change. Across neuromodulatory and cognitive-intervention research, lower baseline performance has been associated with larger measurable gains, although this pattern may also reflect regression to the mean, ceiling effects, or greater room for change (Zhi et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2023; Roncero et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). Baseline cognitive severity should therefore be evaluated as a potential effect modifier using formal interaction tests rather than inferred from separate within-group significance tests.

Beyond baseline heterogeneity, an additional question concerns the cognitive specificity of tDCS effects. Rather than producing broad, nonspecific cognitive enhancement, prefrontal stimulation may preferentially affect domains that depend on dorsolateral prefrontal and frontoparietal circuitry (Vergallito et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2021; Masina et al., 2021; Ramaraju et al., 2020; Wischnewski et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2025). Working memory consistently impaired in schizophrenia and depends critically on prefrontal network integrity, and contributes to broader cognitive integration and functional outcome (Nielsen et al., 2017; Van Snellenberg et al., 2016; Kukuła et al., 2025; Ruf et al., 2017). Therefore, working memory may represent a sensitive domain for detecting prefrontal tDCS-related cognitive change and a plausible contributor to changes in global neurocognitive performance.

Within this framework, the present study used data from a randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled trial of prefrontal tDCS in schizophrenia to examine working-memory response across data-driven levels of baseline cognitive severity. The study therefore aimed to characterize the robustness of the working-memory treatment signal across baseline cognitive-severity groups without interpreting these groups as distinct cognitive subtypes.

Materials and methods

This study reports a prespecified secondary analysis of a randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled clinical trial evaluating the cognitive effects of transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) in patients with schizophrenia (García-Fernández et al., 2025a).

Briefly, participants were adults meeting DSM-5 criteria for schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder (American Psychiatric Association, 2013), clinically stable at enrollment, and receiving stable antipsychotic treatment. Exclusion criteria included neurological disorders, substance use disorder within the previous six months, or contraindications to non-invasive brain stimulation. All participants provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the local ethics committee and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The complete design, eligibility criteria, stimulation protocol, and primary outcomes of the parent trial have been described previously (García-Fernández et al., 2025a).

The present analysis included the 120 participants with complete baseline and immediate post-intervention MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB) assessments. Of these, 62 received active tDCS and 58 received sham stimulation. Because participants without paired MCCB assessments were excluded, the present analysis followed a complete-case approach and did not constitute a strict intention-to-treat analysis. Participant flow, treatment allocation, and reasons for missing assessments are also reported in the original publication, including the corresponding CONSORT flow diagram.

Randomization and blinding

Participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive either active or sham tDCS using a computer-generated allocation sequence. Randomization was performed independently of the assessment team. Participants, treating clinicians, and outcome raters were blinded to treatment allocation throughout the study.

tDCS intervention

The tDCS protocol followed that of the parent randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled trial (García-Fernández et al., 2025a). Briefly, active stimulation consisted of 2 mA direct current delivered for 20 min per session over 10 consecutive weekday sessions using a bifrontal DLPFC montage. The anode was placed over the left DLPFC and the cathode over the right DLPFC, corresponding to F3 and F4 according to the international 10–20 EEG system, consistent with protocols commonly used in previous schizophrenia trials (García-Fernández et al., 2025a; Klem et al., 1999; Kennedy et al., 2018; Gomes et al., 2018). Sham stimulation used the same electrode placement and device configuration, including initial ramp-up/ramp-down procedures and brief stimulation to mimic the cutaneous sensations of active tDCS, without sustained current delivery.

To improve the transparency and reproducibility of intervention reporting, the protocol used in the parent trial was retrospectively mapped onto the Report Approval for Transcranial Electrical Stimulation (RATES) framework (Nejati et al., 2025). Because RATES was published after completion of the parent trial, it was used only as a retrospective reporting framework and not to guide the original protocol. Device characteristics, electrode configuration, stimulation parameters, sham procedures, session schedule, and available safety and tolerability information are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Cognitive assessment

Cognitive performance was assessed at baseline and immediately after completion of the stimulation protocol using the MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB) (Nuechterlein et al., 2008; Kern et al., 2008; Kern et al., 2011; Rodriguez-Jimenez et al., 2015; Siu, 2008). Domain scores were expressed as age- and sex-adjusted T-scores. MCCB domain T-scores were used to characterize baseline cognitive performance and to examine domain-level cognitive change. The main nonsocial cognitive domains were processing speed, attention/vigilance, working memory, verbal learning, visual learning, and reasoning and problem solving.

Social cognition was retained for descriptive charanterization and exploratory domain-level analyses, but was excluded from the global neurocognitive composite. Accordingly, the global neurocognitive composite was computed as the mean of the six nonsocial MCCB domain T-scores. Change scores were calculated as post-intervention minus baseline values.

Baseline cognitive severity grouping

To summarize interindividual variation in baseline cognitive performance, k-means clustering was applied to the seven baseline MCCB domain T-scores. Solutions with two, three, and four groups were evaluated. The preferred solution was selected using the silhouette coefficient, the Calinski–Harabasz index, and the Davies–Bouldin index.

The two-group solution produced the most favorable relative validation indices. Inspection of the cluster centroids indicated that the two groups differed mainly in overall cognitive performance and showed broadly parallel patterns across most domains. The groups were therefore interpreted as representing different degrees of baseline cognitive impairment rather than qualitatively distinct cognitive profiles or cognitive subtypes. They were designated the greater cognitive impairment group and the less severe cognitive impairment group. Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses summarized demographic, clinical, and cognitive variables according to treatment allocation and cognitive-severity group. Continuous variables are reported as means and standard deviations, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages.

Unadjusted Welch's t-tests were used to provide descriptive comparisons of cognitive change between active and sham stimulation within each cognitive-severity group. Between-group standardized effect sizes were expressed as Hedges' g with 95% confidence intervals.

Multiplicity-controlled inference was based on linear mixed-effects models fitted separately within each cognitive-severity group for each of the seven MCCB domains. Each model included fixed effects for time, treatment group, and the time × treatment interaction, with a participant-specific random intercept. Models were adjusted for age, sex, and chlorpromazine-equivalent antipsychotic dose (Leucht et al., 2016; Woods, 2003). The time × treatment interaction estimated the active-versus-sham difference in cognitive change within each severity group.

The complete exploratory multiplicity family comprised 14 domain-by-cognitive-severity-group hypotheses, corresponding to the seven MCCB domains evaluated within the two k-means-derived severity groups. Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction was applied jointly across the 14 time × treatment tests. Holm-adjusted p-values were additionally calculated as a conservative sensitivity analysis. Unadjusted Welch's tests and ANCOVA models were not considered separate confirmatory hypothesis families.

As an adjusted robustness analysis, ANCOVA models were fitted using the post-intervention cognitive score as the dependent variable, treatment allocation as the main predictor, and the corresponding baseline cognitive score, age, sex, and chlorpromazine-equivalent antipsychotic dose as covariates.

To address potential circularity between baseline working-memory performance, cognitive-severity-group assignment, and subsequent working-memory change, the k-means procedure was repeated after excluding baseline working memory. The remaining six baseline MCCB domain T-scores were z-standardized, and solutions with two, three, and four groups were evaluated using the same internal validation criteria. Working-memory effects were then re-estimated within the re-derived cognitive-severity groups. Formal effect modification was assessed in this sensitivity analysis using a linear mixed-effects model including time, treatment, the re-derived cognitive-severity group, all lower-order interactions, and the time × treatment × re-derived cognitive-severity-group interaction, with a participant-specific random intercept and adjustment for age, sex, and chlorpromazine-equivalent antipsychotic dose. A corresponding ANCOVA model including the treatment × re-derived cognitive-severity-group interaction was conducted as an additional robustness analysis.

All statistical tests were two-sided. Nominal p-values, multiplicity-adjusted p-values, effect estimates, and 95% confidence intervals are reported as appropriate.

Results

Analytic sample and previously reported overall cognitive effects

The parent randomized trial included 173 participants. The present complete-case secondary analysis comprised 120 participants with paired baseline and immediate post-intervention MCCB assessments: 62 allocated to active tDCS and 58 allocated to sham stimulation. The remaining 53 randomized participants did not contribute to this secondary analysis because complete paired MCCB assessments were unavailable. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as complete-case rather than strict intention-to-treat estimates.

As previously reported in the parent trial, active tDCS was associated with greater improvement than sham stimulation in working memory (estimated marginal mean difference, 2.716 T-points; p < .001) and in the standard global neurocognitive composite (estimated marginal mean difference, 1.289 T-points; p = .007) (García-Fernández et al., 2025a). The analyses below evaluate the domain-level effects across baseline cognitive-severity groups and should not be interpreted as replacing the overall treatment-effect estimates from the parent trial.

K-means-derived baseline cognitive-severity groups

The K = 2 solution provided the most favorable internal validation indices among the evaluated solutions, with a silhouette coefficient of 0.255, a Calinski–Harabasz index of 53.45, and a Davies–Bouldin index of 1.42. The comparatively modest silhouette coefficient indicated limited separation between groups and supported interpreting the solution as a cognitive-severity gradient rather than as evidence of discrete cognitive subtypes.

The greater cognitive impairment group included 52 participants, of whom 25 received active tDCS and 27 received sham stimulation. Mean baseline MCCB T-scores ranged from 31.7 to 38.4 across domains. The less severe cognitive impairment group included 68 participants, of whom 37 received active tDCS and 31 received sham stimulation. This group showed higher baseline performance in the six nonsocial cognitive domains, with mean T-scores ranging from 44.5 to 51.3. Baseline social-cognition scores were similar in the two groups (38.4 and 38.6, respectively), further indicating that the k-means solution predominantly represented differences in overall nonsocial cognitive severity rather than distinct domain configurations (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table S2).

The descriptive correspondence between the k-means solution and the MCCB-based clinical classification was strong (Table 1). All 46 participants classified as having marked cognitive deficit were assigned to the greater cognitive impairment group, whereas all 22 participants classified as having preserved cognition were assigned to the less severe cognitive impairment group. Of the 52 participants with mild-to-moderate cognitive deficit, 6 were assigned to the greater impairment group and 46 to the less severe impairment group. The association between the two classifications was strong (χ2 = 98.38, p < .001; Cramér's V = 0.91), although the classifications were not considered formally equivalent because they contained different numbers of categories.

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics according to cognitive-severity group and treatment allocation are reported in Supplementary Table S3. Within the greater cognitive impairment group, baseline working-memory performance was higher in the active group than in the sham group (38.96 versus 32.85 T-points; p = .026). Within the less severe cognitive impairment group, chlorpromazine-equivalent antipsychotic dose was lower in the active group than in the sham group (410.32 versus 566.15 mg/day; p = .028). These imbalances supported emphasizing the adjusted mixed-effects and ANCOVA estimates.

Domain-level treatment effects within the primary cognitive-severity groups

In the greater cognitive impairment group, mean working-memory improvement was 4.68 T-points after active tDCS and 1.00 T-point after sham stimulation. The descriptive between-group difference in change was 3.68 T-points (95% CI, 1.57 to 5.79; p = .0012), corresponding to a large standardized effect (Hedges' g = 1.003; 95% CI, 0.424 to 1.581).

The adjusted mixed-effects model supported this working-memory effect (time × treatment p = .0002; joint Benjamini–Hochberg FDR-adjusted p = .0028; Holm-adjusted p = .0028). The corresponding ANCOVA estimate was consistent with the mixed-model result (p = .0014).

In the less severe cognitive impairment group, mean working-memory improvement was 3.27 T-points after active tDCS and 0.68 T-points after sham stimulation. The descriptive between-group difference was 2.59 T-points (95% CI, 0.90 to 4.28; p = .0034), corresponding to a moderate standardized effect (Hedges' g = 0.692; 95% CI, 0.200 to 1.183).

The adjusted mixed-effects model also favored active tDCS in this group (time × treatment p = .0041; joint FDR-adjusted p = .0287). However, the result did not remain statistically significant under the more conservative Holm correction (Holm-adjusted p = .0533). The corresponding ANCOVA estimate was directionally consistent (p = .0184).

No other MCCB domain survived joint Benjamini–Hochberg correction across the complete family of 14 domain-by-group tests. In particular, visual learning in the greater cognitive impairment group showed nominal evidence of a treatment effect in the mixed model (p = .0356) and ANCOVA (p = .0321), but the result did not survive either FDR correction (p = .1661) or Holm correction (p = .4272) and was therefore not interpreted as robust. Full domain-level estimates are reported in Supplementary Table S4.

Accordingly, although the within-group working-memory estimate was numerically larger in the greater cognitive impairment group, the primary analysis did not establish that baseline cognitive severity modified the magnitude of the treatment effect. The within-group estimates should therefore be interpreted as evidence that the working-memory signal was observed across both cognitive-severity groups, rather than as evidence that one group responded significantly better than the other.

Sensitivity analysis excluding baseline working memory from group derivation

After excluding baseline working memory from the clustering variables, the K = 2 solution again provided the most favorable internal validation indices, with a silhouette coefficient of 0.263, a Calinski–Harabasz index of 53.66, and a Davies–Bouldin index of 1.415. The re-derived greater cognitive impairment group included 50 participants, comprising 25 active and 25 sham participants. The re-derived less severe cognitive impairment group included 70 participants, comprising 37 active and 33 sham participants (Supplementary Table S5).

In the adjusted mixed-effects analyses, active tDCS remained associated with greater working-memory improvement than sham stimulation in the re-derived greater cognitive impairment group (estimated difference, 3.56 T-points; 95% CI, 1.55 to 5.57; p = .0005) and in the re-derived less severe cognitive impairment group (estimated difference, 2.63 T-points; 95% CI, 0.93 to 4.34; p = .0024).

The formal time × treatment × cognitive-severity-group interaction was not statistically significant (estimated interaction, 0.93 T-points; 95% CI, −1.71 to 3.56; p = .4915). Thus, the sensitivity analysis provided no evidence that baseline cognitive severity modified the working-memory treatment effect.

The ANCOVA models produced consistent findings. Active tDCS was associated with higher post-intervention working-memory scores in the greater cognitive impairment group (adjusted difference, 3.95 T-points; 95% CI, 1.75 to 6.15; p = .0004) and in the less severe cognitive impairment group (adjusted difference, 2.56 T-points; 95% CI, 0.65 to 4.47; p = .0086). The treatment × cognitive-severity-group interaction was again not statistically significant (estimated interaction, 1.39 T-points; 95% CI, −1.30 to 4.08; p = .3103).

Using the complete family of 14 domain-by-group comparisons after reclustering, the working-memory effect survived joint Benjamini–Hochberg correction in both the greater cognitive impairment group (FDR-adjusted p = .0073) and the less severe cognitive impairment group (FDR-adjusted p = .0423). Under Holm correction, the effect remained significant in the greater impairment group (p = .0073) but not in the less severe impairment group (p = .0786). No other cognitive domain survived multiplicity correction.

Overall, the sensitivity analysis reduced concern that the observed working-memory result was produced solely by including baseline working memory in the original clustering procedure. However, the nonsignificant interaction tests provided no evidence that baseline cognitive severity was a treatment-effect modifier.

Discussion

This secondary complete-case analysis examined working-memory response to prefrontal tDCS across data-driven levels of baseline cognitive severity in schizophrenia. Three findings are relevant. First, k-means clustering identified two groups that differed predominantly in overall cognitive performance rather than in the shape of their cognitive patterns. Second, active tDCS was associated with greater working-memory improvement than sham stimulation within both cognitive-severity groups. Working memory was the only MCCB domain that survived joint Benjamini–Hochberg correction across the 14 domain-by-group comparisons, although the effect in the less severe impairment group did not survive the more conservative Holm correction. Third, repeating the clustering procedure without baseline working memory produced similar within-group estimates, while the formal interaction was not statistically significant. These results support a working-memory treatment signal but do not establish that baseline cognitive severity modifies the response to tDCS.

The interpretation of the k-means solution requires particular caution. The comparatively modest silhouette coefficient and the broadly parallel cluster centroids indicate limited separation between groups. Moreover, social-cognition scores were nearly identical, whereas the principal differences involved a general shift in the six nonsocial MCCB domains. The groups are therefore more appropriately understood as representing greater and less severe cognitive impairment along a continuum rather than distinct cognitive profiles or biological subtypes. The descriptive correspondence with the MCCB-based clinical categories supports the clinical interpretability of this severity gradient, but it does not provide independent evidence of qualitatively different cognitive phenotypes or differential treatment responsiveness (McCutcheon et al., 2023; Howie et al., 2026; Koo et al., 2023; Nuechterlein et al., 2008; Kern et al., 2008; Kern et al., 2011; Rodriguez-Jimenez et al., 2015).

Within this framework, working memory represented the clearest domain-level treatment signal. The parent trial previously reported improvement in both working memory and the global neurocognitive composite after active tDCS (García-Fernández et al., 2025a). The present analyses extend those findings by showing that, among the individual MCCB domains examined across cognitive-severity groups, working memory was the only outcome that remained significant after joint false-discovery-rate correction. This result should not be interpreted as demonstrating an absence of broader cognitive benefit, given the previously reported effect on the global composite. Rather, it suggests that working memory provided the most statistically robust and reproducible domain-specific signal within the current exploratory subgroup framework.

A working-memory effect is biologically plausible given the bifrontal stimulation montage used in the parent trial. Working memory depends on coordinated activity within dorsolateral prefrontal and frontoparietal networks, which are consistently altered in schizophrenia (Nielsen et al., 2017; Van Snellenberg et al., 2016; Vergallito et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2021; Masina et al., 2021; Ramaraju et al., 2020; Wischnewski et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2025). Modulation of these networks may therefore be more readily detected using tasks that directly engage short-term information maintenance and manipulation than using cognitive outcomes that depend on broader or more distributed neural systems. Nevertheless, the present study did not include neurophysiological, neuroimaging, or electric-field measures capable of demonstrating that the observed cognitive change was mediated specifically through modulation of these circuits. The mechanistic interpretation should consequently remain provisional.

The multiplicity framework is also important for interpreting the strength of the evidence. The working-memory effect survived joint Benjamini–Hochberg correction in both cognitive-severity groups, but under Holm correction it remained significant only in the greater impairment group. Other nominal findings, including visual learning in the greater impairment group, did not survive either correction and should not be interpreted as evidence of additional domain-level efficacy. Thus, the findings are most appropriately described as a working-memory signal observed across both severity groups, with stronger statistical evidence in the greater impairment group, rather than as proof of two independently confirmed subgroup effects. Similarly, the numerically larger estimate among participants with greater impairment does not demonstrate that these participants responded better than those with less severe impairment.

The sensitivity analysis excluding baseline working memory from group derivation addresses an important source of potential circularity. Because baseline working memory contributed to the original clustering solution, participants with lower scores could have been preferentially assigned to the greater impairment group and subsequently shown greater apparent improvement because of regression to the mean or greater room for change. Re-deriving the groups without working memory produced materially similar treatment estimates, reducing concern that the finding depended entirely on direct mathematical coupling between the grouping variable and the outcome. However, this analysis cannot eliminate regression to the mean, ceiling effects, measurement variability, or other consequences of baseline-dependent subgrouping. Furthermore, the nonsignificant time × treatment × cognitive-severity-group interaction in the sensitivity analysis provided no evidence that treatment effects differed between the re-derived groups. Baseline cognitive severity should therefore not yet be considered a validated predictor or moderator of tDCS response (Zhi et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2023; Roncero et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025).

These findings nevertheless have implications for the design of future cognitive neuromodulation studies. Baseline cognitive performance may be useful as a prespecified stratification variable, particularly to improve balance between treatment groups and to reduce unexplained variability. Such stratification should be distinguished from using baseline severity to select patients for treatment. Demonstrating clinical utility would require adequately powered prospective trials in which the interaction between baseline cognitive severity and treatment is specified in advance as the principal test of effect modification. Future studies should also assess whether changes in working memory are sustained beyond the immediate post-intervention period and whether they translate into improvements in everyday functioning, occupational performance, or quality of life.

Several strengths of the present study should be acknowledged, including the randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled design of the parent trial, use of the standardized MCCB, adjustment for relevant demographic and pharmacological covariates, joint control of the domain-by-group multiplicity family, and the sensitivity analysis addressing the inclusion of working memory in the clustering procedure. However, the study also has important limitations. This was a secondary analysis, and the parent trial was not prospectively stratified or powered to test moderation by cognitive severity. The analysis included 120 of the 173 randomized participants and therefore followed a complete-case rather than strict intention-to-treat approach, creating potential for selection bias if participants with complete cognitive assessments differed systematically from those excluded. Some baseline imbalances remained after subgrouping, including baseline working-memory performance in the greater impairment group and chlorpromazine-equivalent dose in the less severe impairment group, although adjusted models produced consistent estimates. The k-means solution showed modest internal separation and may be sample dependent. In addition, the sample size limited the precision and statistical power of interaction tests, and a nonsignificant interaction should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that treatment effects are identical across severity groups. Finally, outcomes were assessed immediately after the intervention, precluding conclusions about durability or functional relevance.

In conclusion, prefrontal tDCS was associated with a reproducible working-memory signal across participants with different levels of baseline cognitive impairment. The findings remained materially similar when cognitive-severity groups were re-derived without baseline working memory, reducing concern about direct circularity. However, the available interaction analyses did not support baseline cognitive severity as a treatment-effect modifier, and the groups should be interpreted as points along a cognitive-severity continuum rather than distinct cognitive subtypes. Prospective, adequately powered trials with prespecified moderation analyses, longer follow-up, and functional and neurobiological outcomes are needed to determine the clinical relevance and reproducibility of the working-memory effect.
