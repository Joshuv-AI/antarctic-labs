rGO/RuO₂/PPy nanocomposite: a promising platform for repeatable, selective, long-term stable, and room-temperature TEA sensing with p-n transition
Authors: Maryam Azad, Mohammad Akbarzadeh Pasha, Hossain Milani Moghaddam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study reports the successful synthesis of a novel ternary nanocomposite (NC) comprising reduced graphene oxide, ruthenium dioxide, and polypyrrole (rGO/RuO2/PPy) by in-situ chemical oxidative polymerization of PPy in the presence of hydrothermally prepared binary rGO/RuO2 NC. The samples were characterized by SEM, EDX, X-map, XRD, FTIR, Raman, XPS, BET, and TGA techniques. The prepared NCs were utilized as chemo-resistive sensing layers for the detection of triethylamine (TEA). Under similar conditions, the rGO/RuO2/PPy NC exhibited improved TEA sensing capability, with enhanced sensitivity compared to rGO/RuO2 NC. The rGO/RuO2/PPy NC showed the room temperature response of 16% against low concentration of 20 ppm TEA (75% against 100 ppm) with excellent selectivity toward TEA as compared with ammonia, toluene, ethanol, methanol, acetone and methane. Moreover, the rGO/RuO2/PPy NC demonstrated ultrafast response/recovery times of 2.5/6 s against 20 ppm TEA at 150 °C. Additionally, a temperature-dependent p- to n-type behavior transition was observed upon exposure of rGO/RuO2/PPy NC to TEA at a turning temperature of 150 °C. rGO/RuO2/PPy NC displayed exceptional properties such as good selectivity, considerable stability, high response value, and fast response time, making it a promising candidate for TEA gas sensor applications.Keywords: Gas sensing, Polypyrrole, Reduced graphene oxide, Ruthenium dioxide, Ternary nanocomposite, TriethylamineSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

Toxic gases, such as TEA are found in both industrial production and daily life, posing a serious environmental concern. TEA is a volatile organic compound (VOC) that has been used as a solvent, catalyst, and raw material in various industrial processes1–4. It is also an important marker of food spoilage, especially in fish and seafood. The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) has recommended 25 ppm as the permissible exposure limit (PEL) for TEA5–9. TEA is a hazardous chemical due to its high flammability and its ability to form explosive vapor–air mixtures, making accidental release a significant safety concern. Exposure to TEA vapors can cause irritation of the respiratory tract, leading to symptoms such as coughing and breathing difficulty. Direct contact with the liquid or its vapors may result in skin irritation, chemical burns, and eye damage, including temporary visual impairment. Prolonged or repeated exposure has been associated with adverse effects such as headache, dizziness, and reduced work performance. In addition to its impact on human health, TEA poses environmental risks; its release into terrestrial or aquatic systems can disrupt ecosystems and harm aquatic organisms. Therefore, monitoring TEA is essential for effective risk management in both occupational and environmental contexts10,11.

Interest in chemical gas sensors has increased markedly, particularly for semiconductor metal oxide (SMO)-based systems capable of rapid, sensitive, and portable detection of amine vapors including TEA. These sensors operate through surface redox interactions between TEA molecules and chemisorbed oxygen species, resulting in measurable changes in electrical resistance that define the sensor response12. Key performance metrics include sensitivity, selectivity, response and recovery times, stability, and limit of detection. Sensitivity reflects the magnitude of signal change with analyte concentration, while selectivity denotes the ability to distinguish TEA from interfering gases such as ammonia or ethanol. Fast response and recovery, along with long-term stability, are essential for reliable real-time monitoring, particularly in industrial safety applications13. Recent advances in nanostructuring, doping, and hybrid material design have significantly enhanced sensor performance. Nanostructured metal oxides possess high surface-to-volume ratios and appropriate surface chemical functionalities, which allow enhanced adsorption of gas molecules and improved sensor response14. Moreover, the addition of noble metals has been found to improve the catalytic activity and the selectivity toward amine compounds15–17.

Graphene has been widely utilized in chemical and biochemical sensors due to its exceptional mechanical strength, high surface area, and proper room-temperature electron mobility. As a modified version of graphene, graphene oxide (GO) is enriched with oxygen functional groups, making it further suitable for gas sensing applications due to its multiple active surface sites. The reduction of GO can be achieved by chemical reducing agents or thermal treatment. Graphene, GO, rGO, and their composites have been employed in fabrication of flexible sensors18–24. The gas-sensing properties of graphene-based sensors can be improved by metal oxide nanoparticles (NPs) such as Co3O425, RuO226,27, SnO228, WO329, In2O330, CuO31, Fe2O332, and TiO233. The resulting sensing system demonstrates improved sensitivity, selectivity, response/recovery times, and detection limit toward aromatic compounds due to their larger surface area, and high carrier mobility at room temperature (RT) as well as their outstanding mechanical behavior and thermal conductivity19. Zhang et al.28 developed a TEA sensor based on Ag/SnO2/rGO NC whose good response was obtained at 100 ppm concentration of TEA, a high temperature of 220 °C, and a relatively long recovery time. Wei et al.34 synthesized a TEA sensor by combining MoO3 nanobelt and RuO2 NPs with improved sensitivity and selectivity, at a high temperature of 260 °C. Ma et al.35 reported the use of Ag-decorated MoO3 for TEA sensing. The fast response/response times of this sensor was achieved at a TEA concentration of 50 ppm and 220 °C.

Conductive polymers, such as PPy and polyaniline (PANI) are promising candidates for the next generation of sensors. These polymers demonstrate excellent features including high sensitivity, room temperature operation, feasible large-scale production, and easy adhesion into wearable devices. Particularly, PPy has received great attention due to its exceptional properties such as high conductivity, biocompatibility, biodegradability, stability, and work efficiency under ambient conditions36–38. PPy has been synthesized in different structures such as nanoparticles38, nanoribbons39, and nanofibers40. The PPy/rGO sensor developed by Ly et al.41 showed a response of 15% at 500 ppb for NH3 gas. Based on Tiwari et al.42, the addition of rGO to PPy not only enhances the sensitivity of NH3 sensor, but also improves the response time. Sun et al.43 synthesized a thin film sensor based on PPy-WO3 NC with high response rate of 680% against 100 ppm TEA at room temperature. However, that study has not addressed the full recovery and recovery time.

This work, for the first time, reports the synthesis of a ternary NC by combining rGO, RuO2 and PPy to detect TEA at RT. Interestingly, rGO/RuO2/PPy NC demonstrates unusual sensing behavior with a switching transition from p-type to n-type response against TEA by increasing operating temperature. Thus, this nanohybrid enjoys the advantages of both types of sensors. This novel ternary NC exhibited good room-temperature sensitivity, ultra-fast response/recovery times at higher temperatures, proper selectivity toward similar compounds, and long-term stability, offering a solution to an optimal sensor for TEA gas.

Experimental

Materials

Analytical grade of graphite powder (Merck, Germany), sodium nitrate (NaNO3, Merck, Germany), sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 98%, Merck, Germany), potassium permanganate (KMnO4, Merck, Germany), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 35%, Merck, Germany), Ruthenium (III) chloride (RuCl3, Merck, Germany), Pyrrole monomer (C4H5N, Merck, Germany), iron (III) chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O, Merck, Germany), and ethanol (C2H6O, 99%, Merck, Germany) were used as received.

Characterization

The structural phase and surface chemistry of the samples were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a X’Pert Pro (Netherlands) and a Bruker Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectrometer (model AVATAR, USA), respectively. Raman spectra were recorded in ambient condition by employing Raman spectroscopy (MIRA DS, Metrohm, Switzerland) with a laser wavelength of 532 nm. A scanning electron microscope (FESEM, MIRA3TESCAN-XMU, Czechia) equipped with an energy dispersive X-ray spectroscope (EDX) was employed to explore the morphology and particle size. A Bes Tec (Germany) system was used to examine the chemical state of the surface of samples by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis using Al Kα (1486.6 eV) X-rays at a pressure of 10− 10 mbar. The thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed by STA 503 (BAHR Co, Germany) from 25 to 700 °C with heating rate of 10 °C min− 1 in air. N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms were obtained using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method with a BELSORP analyzer, following sample degassing by a BEL PREP VAC II (BEL, Japan).

Synthesis of GO

GO was produced from pure graphite powder using a modified hummer’s method. In a typical procedure, graphite powder (1 g) and NaNO3 (1 g) were added into 25 mL of H2SO4 and stirred for 10 min in an ice bath. KMnO4 (3 g) was slowly added to the solution over 6 min and the mixture was stirred for 2 h. Then, the temperature of the solution was raised to 35 °C followed by the addition of 90 mL of distilled water. To reduce excess KMnO4, 3 mL of H2O2 was slowly added to the solution and stirred for 10 min. The solution was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm (9168 × g) for 10 min. The resulting precipitate was washed three times with 5 M HCl solution for 10 min each to remove residual manganese ions and other inorganic impurities generated during the oxidation process. Finally, the suspension was subsequently washed several times with deionized water until neutral pH was reached and then dried in an oven at 50 °C for 48 h.

Synthesis of rGO/RuO2 NC

Hydrothermal method was adopted for preparation of the rGO/RuO2 binary NC. First, 40 mg of RuCl3 was dissolved in 60 mL of distilled water for 15 min under magnetic stirring. Then, 30 mg of GO was sonicated in 10 mL of distilled water for 10 min and added to the first solution. The solution was ultrasonicated for another 10 min and transferred to an autoclave, where it was heated at 200 °C for 12 h26. The precipitate was subsequently separated by centrifugation, and washed three times with distilled water and ethanol, and dried at 70 °C. The final product was calcined at 300 °C for 2 h. The abbreviation “GRu” denotes rGO/RuO2 nanocomposite.

Synthesis of rGO/RuO2/PPy NC

The rGO/RuO2/PPy NC was prepared by in situ chemical oxidative polymerization. In a typical procedure, GRu (30 mg) was added to 50 mL of distilled water and ultrasonicated for 20 min. Then, 80 mg of FeCl3.6H2O was added to the solution under magnetic stirring for 1 h, followed by the addition of 20 µL of pyrrole monomer. The solution was stirred for 24 h at room temperature33. Finally, the precipitate was collected and dried at 50 °C for 12 h. The abbreviation “GRuP” denotes rGO/RuO2/PPy nanocomposite. Schematic diagram of the preparation process for GRu and GRuP NCs.

Gas sensing test

The gas sensing behavior of the synthesized nanomaterials was explored using a resistive sensor system (Nano GST-300 Solarsense), as described in our previous report31. This system comprises a cylindrical chamber containing two comb-like electrodes (with dimensions of 13 × 7 mm) made of platinum on a glass substrate. The synthesized NCs were completely dispersed in ethanol and then deposited on the electrode by drop casting. The sensor response was defined as R (%) = (|Ra – Rg|)/Ra×100, where Ra and Rg denotes the electrical resistance of sensor layer upon exposure to air and target gas, respectively. The gas sensing tests were conducted at a constant relative humidity of 35%. A heater inside the chamber adjusts the operating temperature. Response and recovery times are defined as the time taken by the sensor to achieve 90% of the total resistance change against the target gas and air, respectively.

Physicochemical characteristics of synthesized GRu and GRuP NCs

Microstructure morphology and composition

Figure 2a-c presents the FE-SEM images of synthesized GO, GRu, and GRuP nanomaterials. Figure 2a refers to the GO sample, which exhibits the expected plate-like morphology. Figure 2b shows the binary GRu nanocomposite, where RuO2 nanoparticles densely decorate the rGO nanosheets. Figure 2c presents the ternary GRuP nanocomposite, in which tiny particle-like features (attributed to RuO2 and PPy) are distributed across the plate-like rGO segments.

FE-SEM image of (a) GO, (b) GRu, (c) GRuP, and (d) EDX spectrum and X ray elemental mapping (X-map) of GRuP NC.

Figure 2d shows the EDX spectrum of the GRuP nanocomposite together with the corresponding elemental X-maps. As expected, this figure confirms the presence of carbon (36.95%), nitrogen (10.84%), oxygen (41.58%), and ruthenium (10.63%) in the composite. The absence of other detectable elements indicates the high purity of the synthesized nanocomposite. Stoichiometrically, oxygen is expected to be several times more abundant than ruthenium, which is consistent with these results. The density of nitrogen signals is much lower than that of carbon, which may be attributed to the presence of carbon in both rGO and the polymer. Furthermore, the elemental maps indicate a uniform spatial distribution of all detected elements.

Phase structure and surface chemistry

Figure 3a shows the XRD patterns of GO, pure PPy, GRu, and GRuP NCs. The XRD pattern of GO exhibits a sharp peak at 2θ = 11.15°, characteristic of the (001) plane of GO. The XRD pattern of pure PPy shows a broad diffuse halo, indicating its amorphous or poorly crystalline nature, as reported elsewhere42. The XRD pattern of GRu NC, also presented in Fig. 3a, displays peaks at 2θ = 28.09°, 35.2°, 54.4° and 67.07°, corresponding to the (110), (101), (211) and (112) crystal planes of tetragonal structure of RuO2, respectively. The main GO peak disappears, while a weak peak emerges at 2θ = 25.35°, corresponding to the (002) plane of rGO, indicating the reduction of GO to rGO. The GRuP NC shows weaker diffraction peaks compared to GRu pattern, which can be attributed to the presence of amorphous PPy as well as to the lower relative content of rGO and RuO2 in the nanocomposite44. The average RuO2 crystallite sizes in the GRu and GRuP nanocomposites, calculated using the Debye–Scherrer equation (D = kλ/β Cosθ; D: crystallite size, k = 0.9, λ = 1.54 Å, and β denotes the full width at half maximum of the diffraction peak) are 17.5 and 14.5 nm, respectively.

(a) XRD patterns of GO, pure PPy, GRu and GRuP, (b) FTIR spectra, and (c) Raman spectra of GRu and GRuP NCs.

The surface chemistry of GRu and GRuP NCs were characterized by FTIR as shown in Fig. 3b. In the FTIR spectrum of GRu the peaks at 3454, 1572, 1113, 781, and 467 cm− 1 can be attributed to–OH, C = C, C-O stretching vibration, C-C stretching and Ru-O stretching vibration, respectively. Beside the similar peaks, the spectrum of the GRuP showed a new peak at 1415 cm− 1 referring to vibration of N-C indicating the presence of PPy polymer; while the peak at 640 cm− 1 belongs to vibrational mode of RuO227,45.

Raman spectra of GRu and GRuP NCs are also presented in Fig. 3c. Accordingly, the peak at 1573 cm− 1 is related to the C = C backbone stretching of GO (G band), while the one at 1335 cm− 1 can be assigned to the D band (disorder induced band). The Raman spectrum of GRu also shows a peak at 2675 cm− 1, belonging to the 2D band, characteristic of graphene sheets restacking. Disappearance of this peak in GRuP NC is an indicator of homogeneous dispersion of rGO nanosheets on the matrix of this composite which is favorable for sensing performance. As a measure of defects and degree of disorder, the ratio of D to G band intensity (ID/IG) increases from 0.41 in GRu to 0.85 in GRuP NC46. The higher ID/IG ratio of GRuP can be attributed to the presence of amorphous carbon from the PPy structure after pyrolysis47, as well as increasing the defects in the structure of rGO through the synthesis process of this NC.

The surface chemistry of GRuP NC was further explored by XPS analysis, as shown in Fig. 4. As expected, the scan survey spectrum (Fig. 4a) indicated the presence of C, Ru, O, and N on the surface layer of GRuP NC. Figure 4b-d shows the high resolution XPS spectra of the elements observed along with deconvoluted peaks. The XPS spectrum of Ru 3d overlapped C 1s, as reported elsewhere48. According to Fig. 4b, the main C 1s peak at the binding energy of 284.6 eV confirms the dominant sp2 C = C of rGO sheets, and the peaks of Ru 3d5/2 (281.8 eV) and Ru3d3/2 (285.3 eV) support the existence of RuO248,49. The XPS spectrum of O 1s (Fig. 4c) has more confirmed the existence of RuO2 in the NC. O 1s spectrum is composed of Ru-O-Ru (529.77 eV), Ru-O-H (530.15 eV), H2O (531.29 eV), HO-C = O (532.3 eV), C = O (533.12 eV), and C-OH (533.7 eV) peaks48,50. The high-resolution N 1s spectrum can be curve-fitted into three peaks, the most dominant one is assigned to pyrrolic N 1s (399.6 eV), while the other two are related to graphitic N 1s (401.2 eV) and pyridinic N 1s (398.05 eV), confirming the partial covalent bonding of PPy to GO46.

Figure 5a,b presents the BET analysis of GRu and GRuP NCs. The isotherms exhibit a standard type IV behavior, characteristics of mesoporous materials. Moreover, the existence of a hysteresis loop of type H3 refers to slit shaped pores and plate-like particles which is expected due to presence of rGO sheets. As summarized in Table 1, the specific surface area of GRu and GRuP NCs are 79.88 and 15.99 m2g− 1, respectively. The reduction of pore volume and surface area of GRuP compared to GRu is due to the presence of amorphous polymer51. The mean pore diameter of the synthesized NCs shows the mesoporosity of this material which is the best condition for gas sensing application.

The thermal stability of GRuP NC was evaluated by TGA, as shown in Fig. 5c. A slight weight loss of about 0.5% between 50 and 140 °C is attributed to evaporation of adsorbed water27. An additional weight loss of 2.6% was observed between 140 and 280 °C, which may be due to the removal of adsorbed species or surface functional groups from the nanocomposite. The weight loss continues up to 560 ᵒC, reaching 26.4%, which can be attributed to the decomposition of rGO and PPy. Overall, the TGA results indicate that the GRuP NC is thermally stable at moderate temperatures. This thermal stability is sufficient for the present sensing application, since its operating temperature range is considerably lower than the temperatures at which rGO-based52 and PPy-containing43 NCs typically undergo significant thermal degradation (~ 300 °C and above).

The DTG curve is the derivative of TGA curve and provides more information about the thermal degradation behavior of the GRuP NC. As shown in Fig. 5c, a small DTG peak appears below 150 °C, which is associated with the loss of physically adsorbed moisture and residual volatile species. In addition, two distinct DTG peaks are observed in the temperature range of 250–500 °C. These peaks (at 320 and 480 °C) correspond to the maximum weight-loss rates and the most pronounced mass-loss events in the TGA curve, and they are attributed to the degradation of PPy and rGO, respectively. Above 500 °C, the TGA and DTG curves remain relatively stable, indicating that most thermally labile components have already decomposed. The residual mass, composed mainly of RuO2 nanoparticles, persists within the examined temperature range.

Sensing behavior

Effects of operating temperature and gas concentration

The gas-sensing performance of GRu and GRuP sensor layers against 20 ppm TEA at different working temperatures are illustrated in Fig. 6. As can be seen in Fig. 6a, GRuP shows a response rate of %15.9 with response/recovery times of 31.5/399.5 s, while GRu exhibits no response at room temperature. By raising the temperature (Fig. 6(b)) and approaching the optimal working temperature, the response rate of GRu NC increased to its maximum value of %2.8 at 150 ᵒC, which declined by further temperature elevation. The sensing response of GRuP towards 20 ppm TEA gas at different temperatures (from 20 to 300 ᵒC) is shown in Fig. 6c, which demonstrates remarkably different behavior. As TEA is a reducing gas, GRuP NC acts as a p-type sensor with decreasing response through temperature growth in the range of 20–100 °C. Reversely, it shows an n-type behavior, and increasing response with temperature elevation in the range of 150–300 °C. At a high temperature of 150 °C and with activation of RuO2 (as the optimal operating temperature of semiconducting metal oxide sensors is high) the response value increases with increasing temperature13. This decrease/increase in the gas response value with temperature rising and the p-type to n- type sensing behavior transition will be discussed further. Figure 6e shows the response/recovery time of GRuP sensing layer at different temperatures. As seen, the response/recovery time decreases with temperature increase and reaches the lowest value at 150 °C (2.5s/6s).

The relationship between sensor responses and the TEA concentrations at RT was presented in Fig. 7. As seen the response rate exceeds %100 at 200 ppm concentration of TEA gas. Recyclability, long-term stability and selectivity

a series of experiments were carried out at room temperature to investigate the repeatability, stability, and selectivity of GRuP sensor. The response of sensor toward 20 ppm TEA in six successive cycles were recorded which exhibited a stable response, as shown in Fig. 8a. To evaluate the long-term stability of the sensor, sensing tests were performed during four weeks on 20 ppm of TEA at room temperature. As shown in Fig. 8b, the gas response was well maintained for 28 days, with no significant decrease. This indicates the good long-term stability of the GRuP NC-based sensor. The selectivity of gas sensing is an important parameter for their practical use. Figure 8(c) compares the responses of GRuP sensor upon exposure to TEA and other VOCs, including ammonia, toluene, ethanol, methanol, acetone and methane, at a concentration of 20 ppm and room temperature. Clearly, the GRuP sensor shows a selective response to TEA vapor with a response of %15.9, much higher than that of the other gases (even in comparison to similar gases like NH3), indicating its proper selectivity.

Proposed sensing mechanism

In recent years, several studies have reported n-p or p-n or even n-p-n type transition sensing behavior against different gases (such as H2, NO2, methanol)57, and a similar behavior is observed in the present work. The GRuP sensor exhibits a p-type to n-type response transition at 150 ᵒC, that can be attributed to the coexistence of different types of semiconductor materials in the NC. The electron depletion layer and hole accumulation layer models can be used to explain the sensing mechanisms of n-type and p-type semiconducting sensors, respectively. RuO2 is known as an n-type semiconductor, so electrons are its dominant charge carriers58, whereas rGO and PPy behave as p-type materials, where charge transport occurs mainly through holes59.

In the present sensor, p-type behavior with a decreasing response below 150 °C and an increasing n-type response above 150 °C was observed. Therefore, it can be inferred that hole conduction dominates below this turning temperature, whereas electron conduction becomes dominant at higher temperatures. Figure 9 shows a schematic of the proposed sensing mechanism in the present sensing layer.

Schematic of proposed sensing mechanism and p-type to n- type transition for GRuP sensor.

When the oxygen molecules of the air react with the surface of the n-type/p-type sensor material, they convert to O2−, O− and O2− species (depending to operating temperature) by receiving free electrons on the surface of the material. After TEA gas molecules (a reducing agent) enter and react with adsorbed oxygen species on the surface, electrons are released, which reduces the thickness of the electron depletion layer (hole accumulation layer), and accordingly the resistance of the sensor decreases(increases) for n-type material (p-type material). When the temperature increases, the electron density and mobility in RuO2 increases, which increases the number of extra electrons in the NC and decreases the number of holes due to neutralization13. With increasing temperature from room temperature to 150 °C, the sensor response decreases due to the decrease in the number of holes as charge carriers. At temperatures above 150 °C, the sensor demonstrates p-n transition due to the neutralization of holes and a further increase in the number of electrons changes the charge carriers from holes to electrons60.

It is generally accepted that metal oxide semiconductors (like RuO2) exhibit their sensing properties at high temperatures and are inactive at intermediate or room temperatures13,34. PPy is intrinsically a p-type conducting polymer because charge transport occurs through its conjugated π-electron system. In chemically synthesized PPy, FeCl₃ acts primarily as an oxidizing agent during polymerization, generating oxidized PPy chains containing polarons and bipolarons61. Thermal treatment of PPy, at sufficiently high temperature (~ 150 °C and above), can induce dedoping and structural changes that reduce the concentration of polarons and bipolarons, thereby lowering the hole concentration and weakening p-type conductivity. This effect may persist even after residual dopant species (Cl− ions) are no longer detectable, because backbone rearrangement or partial degradation can also contribute to conductivity loss62–64. Without sufficient hole carriers, electrons become the dominant charge carriers, resulting in a shift toward n-type behavior.

Conclusion

In this study, the sensing characteristics of rGO/RuO2/PPy NC towards TEA gas were investigated. It was observed that by adding polypyrrole to rGO-RuO2, the NC had a good response at room temperature with a value of %15.8, and the response time and recovery time were 31.5 s and 399.5 s, respectively. Also, the hybrid shows a fast response time of 2.5 s and a p-type to n-type transition at 150ᵒC, which can give the NC the advantage of both types of sensors in different conditions. It can also be observed through various tests that the NC not only had a good response at room temperature, but also had proper selectivity, repeatability and stability. The developed rGO/RuO2/PPy NC offers a promising solution to an optimal sensing layer against TEA gas.
