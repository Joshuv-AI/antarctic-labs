Protocol for haplotype-resolved genome comparison and allele-specific expression analysis across multiple tissues and individuals
Authors: Chenyu Shi, Shikai Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHaplotype-resolved genome comparison and allele-specific expression (ASE) analysis are essential for understanding how genomic diversity drives phenotypic variation. However, performing these analyses reproducibly across multiple tissues and individuals remains challenging. Here, we present a protocol for haplotype-resolved genome comparison and ASE analysis, extended to enable ASE profiling across multiple tissues and individuals. We describe steps for identifying and comparing haplotype-divergent sequences and alleles. We then detail procedures for detecting variants from RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data and identifying ASE genes.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Liu et al.1Subject areas: Sequence analysis, Genetics, Genomics

Before you begin

The protocol below describes the specific steps for haplotype sequence comparison and precise allele identification. Furthermore, we have expanded this protocol to allow for accurate ASE analysis across multiple tissues and individuals. This protocol can be adapted for any diploid organism with available haplotype-resolved assemblies and RNA-seq data.

Innovation

1.Quantitative HDS (haplotype divergent sequence) pipeline. While high-quality haplotype-resolved genomes are increasingly available, a standardized comparative framework is still lacking. This protocol pioneers a quantitative pipeline to define, identify, and extract haplotype divergent sequences. It further enables the systematic correlation of HDS spatial distribution with diverse genomic features, such as transposable elements (TEs), at the whole-genome level.

Quantitative HDS (haplotype divergent sequence) pipeline. While high-quality haplotype-resolved genomes are increasingly available, a standardized comparative framework is still lacking. This protocol pioneers a quantitative pipeline to define, identify, and extract haplotype divergent sequences. It further enables the systematic correlation of HDS spatial distribution with diverse genomic features, such as transposable elements (TEs), at the whole-genome level.

2.Precision allele identification framework. This workflow refines allele pairing by integrating macro-synteny, functional consistency, and stringent sequence divergence thresholds. This multi-layered filtering ensures highly accurate ortholog matching, providing a robust foundation for subsequent comparative and evolutionary analyses.

Precision allele identification framework. This workflow refines allele pairing by integrating macro-synteny, functional consistency, and stringent sequence divergence thresholds. This multi-layered filtering ensures highly accurate ortholog matching, providing a robust foundation for subsequent comparative and evolutionary analyses.

3.Transcriptome-based phasing and ASE analysis workflow. By leveraging multi-tissue transcriptomic data, the workflow enables accurate SNP calling, individual-specific pseudo-genome construction, and subsequent haplotype phasing. This innovative approach circumvents the traditional dependency on highly contiguous phased genome assemblies, establishing a highly accessible and reliable pipeline for quantifying allele-specific expression (ASE). Consequently, it provides a foundation for large-scale ASE and allele-specific quantitative trait locus (aseQTL) studies across diverse tissues and populations.

Variant detection from RNA-seq data

Timing: 4–5 h

For individuals without a pre-existing haplotype-resolved genome, we provide a comprehensive pipeline for SNP detection and haplotype phasing using multi-tissue RNA-seq data from the same individual. This transcriptome-based approach effectively targets functionally active and transcribed genomic regions; however, it inherently restricts SNP detection to expressed genes, and can potentially lead to allele dropout in the phenomena such as extreme allele-specific expression or lowly expressed genes. Using multi-tissue RNA-seq data for SNP detection is a good strategy for capturing genes that might be silenced in a single tissue and reducing tissue-specific expression biases. (Additional mitigation strategies are discussed in the limitations section). Following SNP detection, non-biallelic variants are explicitly dropped. To construct the individual-specific pseudo-genome, heterozygous SNPs are subsequently masked, whereas homozygous variants are used to replace the corresponding loci in the reference genome (executed via Script_Scy/rnaseq/02.SNP_filter.sh). Then RNA-seq datasets are re-aligned to this reference. This establishes a highly accurate foundation for subsequent haplotype phasing and ASE quantification.

Note: The RNA-seq reads were mapped to the C. sikamea hapA reference genome using STAR in 2-pass mode. Uniquely mapped reads were subsequently processed according to GATK Best Practices for RNA-seq to call SNPs. Raw SNPs were hard-filtered using GATK VariantFiltration to exclude variants matching the following criteria: QD < 2.0 and FS > 30.0. SNPs were retained only if they met the following stringent thresholds: (1) a minimum read depth of 10 (DP ≥ 10) in at least 20% of the sampled tissues; and (2) an overall genotype Call Rate (CR) ≥ 50% across all combined tissues. Finally, this pipeline ultimately product a full set of high-quality SNPs (designated as ∗.snp2mask.vcf.gz), from which the heterozygous subset (designated as ∗.snp2ase.vcf.gz) was specifically extracted for downstream allele-specific expression quantification.

Identification and visualization of ASEGs

Timing: 5 min

Here, we describe the steps for detecting allele expression among each RNA-seq dataset by using phASER. Subsequently, a binomial test was performed to determine the presence of ASEGs. To account for multiple comparisons, the resulting p-values were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, ensuring a false discovery rate of 0.05.

Expected outcomes

This protocol yields a comprehensive genome-wide landscape of haplotype divergent sequences alongside a curated catalog of expressed alleles, suitable for downstream evolutionary and functional interpretation. From the whole-genome alignment approaches (Part 1), researchers can expect positional coordinate tables (BED-like format) that strictly categorize genomic regions as syntenic, structurally rearranged, or unaligned. Visualizations generated via plotsr will display chromosome-wide structural similarities and macroscopic rearrangements between the haplotype genomes (Figure 1). By integrating these coordinates with Transposable Element annotations, users will generate correlation matrices and spatial distribution plots mapping HDS density against TE accumulation across the whole genome (Figure 2). Furthermore, the synteny-based allele identification pipeline (Part 2) produces tabular lists of high-confidence allele pairs. These tables include paired gene IDs, exact sequence similarity scores (derived from EMBOSS needle), and matched functional annotations (via EggNOG), ensuring that only orthologous sequences with <5% protein-level divergence are retained for comparative analysis.

For individuals lacking a pre-existing phased genome, the variant calling pipeline (Part 3) generates high-confidence, quality-controlled VCF files derived from multi-tissue RNA-seq data following stringent GATK filtering. Utilizing these SNPs, the protocol outputs individual-specific pseudo-genome assemblies (FASTA format) designed to substantially reduce reference mapping biases in highly polymorphic species. Building upon this customized alignment foundation, the allele-specific expression (ASE) pipeline (Part 4) yields sample-specific allele read-count matrices extracted via phASER. Subsequent statistical assessment produces the final tables of Allele-Specific Expression Genes (ASEGs), detailing the exact read counts, read number imbalances, and binomial test p-values (FDR-corrected) for every candidate gene across each sampled tissue.

Overall, this protocol enables the systematic characterization of how haplotype divergence influences allelic variation and transcriptomic regulation by integrating comparative genomics with individualized transcriptomics. By combining these approaches, researchers can accurately pinpoint structural variations and assess their direct impact on allele-specific expression imbalances. This framework provides a robust foundation for investigating the relationship between gene expression and genomic diversity, as well as for advancing applications in allele-specific expression quantitative trait locus (aseQTL) mapping.

Limitations

The success and reliability of this protocol heavily depend on the continuity and accuracy of the initial genome assemblies. If the inputted haplotype-resolved genomes are highly fragmented or contain significant assembly errors (e.g., collapsed repeats or false duplications), the whole-genome alignment in Part 1 will inherently produce false-positive divergent sequences, and Part 2 will fail to accurately pair true alleles.

Furthermore, the RNA-seq-based variant calling and pseudo-genome construction (Part 3) are strictly limited by the expression profiles of the sampled tissues. Because variants are called exclusively from transcribed regions, any genes that are silenced or expressed at extremely low levels across all sampled tissues will not have SNPs called. Consequently, these unexpressed regions will remain unphased in the resulting pseudo-genome.

Finally, the ASE identification relies on stringent read depth thresholds (a minimum of 10 reads in at least one allele). Therefore, this protocol is mechanically biased toward moderately to highly expressed genes. ASE dynamics in genes with low baseline expression will be systematically excluded from the final results, which may underrepresent allele imbalance in certain regulatory genes or specific developmental stages. When whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data are available for the same individual or its parents, integrating them for the initial variant calling step represents the most robust mitigation strategy to capture extreme ASE.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

plotsr fails to generate the synteny visualization or produces an empty/distorted plot (Part 1, Step 1).

Potential solution

This is almost exclusively caused by formatting errors in the required genome.txt configuration file or ID mismatches. Ensure that the chromosome/scaffold IDs in your input FASTA files exactly match the IDs present in the SyRI output.

Problem 2

An unexpectedly low number of putative alleles are retained after the EggNOG and EMBOSS needle filtering steps (Part 2, Step 5 & 6).

Potential solution

The filtering parameters may be too stringent for a highly polymorphic species. Ensure that translated protein sequences (or CDS) rather than raw genomic nucleotide sequences are used in EMBOSS needle. If the inputs are correct, consider relaxing the requirement for identical functional annotations, as alternative splicing or slight truncation in one haplotype can alter the EggNOG annotation string. You may also slightly increase the protein divergence threshold (e.g., from 5% to 10%) to account for naturally high allelic variation.

Problem 3

GATK VariantFiltration or the custom genotype filters remove nearly all detected SNPs (Part 3, Step 8).

Potential solution

First, note that the pseudogenome generation pipeline assumes the individual is homozygous for all alleles, and non-biallelic variants are explicitly dropped in protocol/02.SNP_filter.sh. The hard-filtering thresholds or the Genotype Call Rate (CR ≥ 50%) might be too aggressive if your RNA-seq data exhibits highly variable coverage across loci. Check the overall mapping rate and depth of your STAR alignments. If the depth is generally low, you must combine more tissue samples from the same individual prior to variant calling to increase the statistical power. Alternatively, you can temporarily lower the depth requirement (DP ≥ 10) to DP ≥ 5 to rescue valid SNPs located in moderately expressed genes.

Furthermore, to definitively establish the reliability of RNA-seq-based variant calling and fine-tune these filtering parameters for your specific study system, we recommend conducting a pilot validation. As demonstrated in our previous work,1 researchers can perform paired whole-genome re-sequencing and multi-tissue RNA-seq on a single representative individual. Benchmarking the RNA-seq-derived SNPs against the gold-standard WGS variant call set explicitly confirms the feasibility and accuracy of the transcriptome-based pipeline, ensuring that the selected filtering thresholds effectively balance sensitivity and specificity.
