Caspase‐mediated cleavage events hidden by secondary proteolysis during apoptosis
Authors: Fatemeh Fotouhi, Erik Gomez‐Cardona, Jainilkumar Patel, Olivier Julien, Howard Fearnhead
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCaspases are cysteine proteases that cleave specific proteins to control a range of cellular processes including cell death, inflammation, and differentiation. Proteomic approaches, like N‐terminomics, have been central to identifying both cleaved proteins and where they are cleaved. We recently reported N‐terminomics on differentiating and dying C2C12 myoblasts, identifying many new caspase substrates and cleavage sites. Here, we present a further analysis of this dataset demonstrating that there are caspase substrates “hidden” in our and in other N‐terminomics datasets. We propose that for a subset of proteins, caspase cleavage is followed by removal of two amino acids from the neo‐N‐terminus by a dipeptidyl peptidase (DPP). As the peptide sequence of the neo‐N‐terminus is used to identify the cleavage site, this hides the caspase‐mediated cleavage event. The implications of this are that N‐terminomics datasets can be used to reveal hitherto unidentified caspase substrates and provide more information on cell‐fate decisions. In C2C12 myoblasts, we have shown that cleavage of “hidden” substrates in apoptotic myoblasts that is absent from differentiating myoblasts (which also contain active caspase‐3), suggesting that cleavage may identify apoptotic cells. A better understanding of the interplay between caspases and other proteases and their sequential action on caspase substrates may allow further elucidation of cell fate decisions.

INTRODUCTION

Caspases are cysteine proteases that control a range of cellular processes including cell death, inflammation and differentiation, typically by cleaving specific proteins (Connolly et al., 2014). The cleavage sites are described using Schechter‐Berger nomenclature in which amino acids are numbered relative to the scissile bond: Pn…P1–P1’…Pn′ (Schechter & Berger, 1967). One the defining characteristics of caspases is a very strong preference for aspartic acid (D) in the P1 position, while different preferences for amino acids in the P4‐P2 positions confer distinct substrate specificities on different caspases (Pop & Salvesen, 2009).

The role of caspases in diverse cellular functions has made the identification of caspase substrates important for understanding how caspases control different cellular processes (Julien & Wells, 2017). To this end, researchers have used a range of proteomic approaches to find caspase substrates (Enoksson et al., 2007; Hanna et al., 2023; Impens et al., 2010; Lee et al., 2004; Stoehr et al., 2013; Turowec et al., 2014). N‐terminomics is one such approach and is a powerful proteomic technology for identifying protease substrates and the cleavage site, and has been used to great effect in caspase research (Julien et al., 2016). For example, subtiligase N‐terminomics is used to label newly generated protein N‐termini revealed by proteolytic cleavage with a biotinylated peptide containing an amino‐butyric acid mass‐tag. Labeled peptides are enriched using streptavidin‐based affinity purification before using mass spectrometry to assign to identify the P1′…Pn′ peptide sequence (Wiita et al., 2014). The P1′…Pn′ sequence is used to identify the cleaved protein and to infer the cleavage site. When the inferred P1 amino acid is D (Asp) then the protein is a putative caspase substrate. “Forward” N‐terminomics describes the labeling of neo termini generated in cells and has the advantage of identifying cleavage events occurring during changes in cell behavior but has limited power to identify the protease responsible for each proteolytic event. In contrast “reverse” N‐terminomics describes the labeling of neo termini after a specific protease has been added to a cell lysate. In this case there is more information about the protease involved, but less about biological relevance. Together, these approaches have been very successful in identifying hundreds of caspase substrates and explaining how caspases control a range of different caspase‐regulated processes.

We recently reported a forward N‐terminomics comparison of differentiating and dying myoblasts (Gomez‐Cardona et al., 2024). This revealed important aspects about caspase activity and caspase substrates in death and differentiation, as well as identifying many new caspase substrates (P1 = D) associated with both cell fates. However, further analysis of this dataset leads us to now propose that using P1 = D to identify caspase substrates does not identify all caspase substrates cleaved during apoptosis, and that there are missing caspase substrates hidden in N‐terminomics datasets. These “hidden” substrates undergo an initial caspase cleavage event at an aspartic acid residue (D). Our new data indicate that caspase cleavage is followed by a second cleavage by either dipeptidyl protease‐8 or ‐9 (DPP8/9); DPP activity removes two amino acids (Xaa‐Pro) from the N‐terminus of the caspase cleavage product, so the N‐terminomics data shows cleavage of these proteins at P1 = proline, not aspartic acid. As a result, these caspase substrates have been missed up to this point. The data also explains why one important caspase substrate (BCL2L1) first identified by a candidate approach has never been previously identified in N‐terminomics datasets.

The broad implications of this research are that (1) analysis of N‐terminomics datasets can reveal interplay/relationships between different proteases and (2) that apoptosis involves sequential proteolysis of specific proteins by cysteine (caspases) and serine proteases (DPPs). In addition, we only observed “hidden” substrates in apoptotic myoblasts, not in differentiating myoblasts (which also contain active caspase‐3), suggesting that cleavage of “hidden” substrates identifies apoptotic cells, and that their cleavage perhaps plays a role in deciding between differentiation and death for a cell.

RESULTS

Hidden substrates

Forward N‐terminomics of dying and differentiating cells detects more than just caspase‐mediated proteolysis (defined by P1 = D; Figure 1a). We therefore analyzed all cleavage events (P1 = Xaa) in apoptotic and differentiating myoblasts from our dataset (Figure S1; (Gomez‐Cardona et al., 2024)). Analysis of the apoptotic cells showed that when P1 = P, the sequence logo for P6‐P3 positions resembles D‐X‐X‐D, which is typical of a caspase‐3 cleavage site. There were 31 proteins in the apoptotic C2C12 cells where P1 = P and P3 = D (Figure 1c; Table 1). This cleavage pattern was clearly associated with the apoptotic cell population but not with the undifferentiated myoblasts, differentiating myoblasts (which also have active caspase‐3), or differentiated cells (myotubes and reserve cells). The total number of unique cleavages was similar for all five groups, so the increase in the apoptotic cells of substrates with the P1 = P and P3 = D motif cannot be explained by more events being detected in apoptotic cells. Broad spectrum inhibitors of cysteine and serine proteases are added at the lysis step suggesting that the pattern of cleavage was generated in the cells and not post‐lysis. In addition, analysis of our reverse N‐terminomics dataset generated by adding caspase‐3 to myoblast lysates identified only three proteins showing P1 = P and P3 = D (Table S1). These results support the idea that the P1 = P/P3 = D signature was generated in apoptotic cells and that it is not an artifact of cell lysis.

Of the 31 P1 = P/P3 = D peptides identified (Table 1; mass spectra in Table S2), three peptides mapped to reported caspase‐3 cleavage sites in known caspase substrates (Filamin A, BCL2L1 and DDX47). Six mapped to new cleavage sites in previously identified caspase substrates. Twenty two peptides mapped to new caspase cleavage sites in proteins not previously identified as caspase substrates. Five were cleaved in both live cells and apoptotic cells, while 25 were cleaved only in apoptotic cells. One was cleaved only in live cells. This is a strikingly different distribution compared to proteins cleaved at P1 = D (out of 272 P1 = D substrates, 210 were present in both live and dead cells with 32 detected in only live cells and 30 detected only in dead cells). Multivariate hypergeometric analysis showed that the probability that the different distributions of substrates between live and apoptotic cells for P1 = P/P3 = D and P1 = D is due to a sampling error is very small (p = 1.45 × 10−27). These data suggest that the pattern of cleavage was not by chance and was generated by a Proline specific activity in the apoptotic cells.

We hypothesized that two step cleavage could generate such a pattern (Figure 1d): first cleavage by caspase‐3 at aspartic acid generating a C‐terminal fragment whose N‐terminus is Xaa‐Pro, followed by cleavage by a Dipeptidyl peptidase (DPP). DPPs, such as DPP8/DPP9, cleave two amino acids from the N‐terminal of protein if the second amino acid is proline (Cui et al., 2022), or less commonly alanine (Bjelke et al., 2006; Maes et al., 2005). In the N‐terminomic workflow the P1 amino acid is inferred from the P1′…Pn′ peptide sequence, so DPP activity removing two amino acids hides the caspase cleavage event.

Data is available from several other N‐terminomic studies of apoptotic cells using similar subtiligase‐based approaches to one that we employed (Wang & Julien, 2023). We therefore asked whether these data sets contained evidence of this proteolytic activity (P1 = P and P3 = D), and if so, whether they identified any of the same proteins identified in our C2C12 cell data set. Studies using four different cell lines (Jurkat, MM1S, U266, and DB) treated with various apoptotic stimuli (staurosporine, etoposide, cisplatin, doxorubicin, TRAIL) were analyzed to identify proteins cleaved where P3 = D and P1 = D, and the list of proteins compared to those identified in our data set (which is apoptosis induced by serum withdrawal). Eighty‐five cleaved proteins were identified where P3 = D and P1 = P and showed varying degrees of conservation of P4‐P4’ amino acids between human and mouse cells (Figure 1e). Thirty‐five proteins showed 100% conservation between mouse and human. Seven other proteins conserved the P1 = P, but the P3 aspartic acid was replaced with glutamic acid, which can also be cleaved by caspase‐3 (Seaman et al., 2016). Fourteen substrates did not conserve P4‐P4′ between the two species. There are also 14 proteins cleaved after Ala with a P3 = D in the C2C12 N‐terminomics dataset and the Degrabase data set (Tables S3 and S4), adding additional weight to the hypothesis. We therefore set out to test if any of the “hidden” substrates were cleaved by caspase‐3 and in apoptotic cells.

Corroborating the identification of new substrates

To corroborate the N‐terminomics data we selected three substrates to investigate further: JUND, POL1RC(RPAC1), and MTSS1. These proteins were all cleaved in C2C12 cells, and cleavage at the same site was also detected in three (JUND and POLR1C) or two (MTSS1) human cell lines (Figure 1e). To do this we incubated HEK293T cell lysates prepared from cells overexpressing epitope tagged versions of the proteins (N‐terminal 3× FLAG tag and C‐terminal HA tag) with purified recombinant mouse caspase‐3. Cleavage of the proteins was then assessed by immunoblotting.

We found that JUND, MTSS1, and POLR1C were all cleaved by recombinant caspase‐3, and mutation of the putative caspase cleavage site (JUND: TVPD → TVPA; MTSS1: TVPD → TVPA; and POLR1C: HTTD → HTTA) reduced the level of caspase‐3 mediated cleavage (Figure 2). In the case of JUND, mutating D242 did not abolish the appearance of the predicted fragment. However, a double mutation of D242A plus D248A did, suggesting that caspase‐3 preferentially cleaves at D242 but that it can also cleave at D248. In the case of both JUND and MTSS1, caspase‐3 generated an additional, unpredicted fragment (~30 kDa for JUND; ~60 kDa for MTSS1), suggesting that both proteins contain other unidentified caspase cleavage site(s). Mutating D81 and D85 in JUND to alanine abolished the appearance of the 30 kDa band, identifying an additional site of caspase‐3 cleavage (Figure S2). In addition to these three new substrates, we also confirmed earlier reports that BCL2L1 is cleaved at D61 (Figure S3).

We next tested if the proteins were cleaved in cells undergoing apoptosis. HEK293T cells overexpressing epitope tagged proteins were treated with either etoposide or TPCK (Fabian et al., 2009; Fabian & Fearnhead, 2010) to activate caspase‐3 and induce apoptosis. Protein cleavage was then assessed by immunoblotting. This revealed that the proteins were also cleaved in apoptotic cells (Figure 3). Both JUND and MTSS1 showed a cleavage pattern like that seen in the cell‐free system (Figure 2), with the appearance of a fragment of predicted size that disappeared when aspartic acids at the cleavage sites were mutated. POLR1C showed decreased levels of the full‐length protein after treatment that were reduced by mutating the aspartic acids at the predicted site. However, a fragment was not detected in the treated cells, suggesting that it may be unstable or that additional cleavage removes the HA tag (there is a putative caspase cleavage site toward the C‐terminal of the protein). Similar results were observed in HeLa cells. We then immunoprecipitated JUND from etoposide treated HEK293T cells using an anti‐HA antibody and performed mass spectrometry (workflow shown in Figure S4) to identify the cleavage sites in apoptotic cells. Peptides mapping to the neo‐N termini generated by cleavage at D242 and P244 were identified, confirming the cleavage pattern seen in mouse cells (Figure 3d).

Testing if DPPs process a product of caspase activity

Weaver et al. showed that caspase cleavage can generate new substrates for the N‐end rule pathway, resulting in the rapid loss of proteins after caspase cleavage (Weaver et al., 2017). To test if DPP activity was processing the products of caspase activity in cells we took advantage of this idea. HEK293T cells were transfected with plasmids encoding a Δ62 BCL2L1 mutant (the C terminal fragment generated by caspase cleavage), in which the alanine residue after the proline was mutated to arginine, which makes it a substrate for the N‐end rule pathway if it is processed by a DPP (A64R mutant; Figure 4a). If this protein is cleaved by DPP, the N‐terminal amino acid is arginine, and the protein will be rapidly removed by the N‐end rule pathway. Treating cells expressing the Δ62/A65R mutant with the DPP inhibitors Talabostat or 1G244 increased the level of the protein detected in cells (Figure 4c) showing that a product of caspase cleavage is a substrate for a DPP activity. We next tested siRNAs against DPP8 and DPP9. Control or specific siRNAs were transfected into HEK293T with the A64R mutant and protein levels assessed 48 h later. Cells transfected with the control siRNA showed low levels of the A64R protein, but transfection with siRNA against either DPP8 or DPP9 increased the levels (Figure 4d,e). The similar effect of pharmacological inhibitors and DPP siRNAs indicates that the A64R assay reports DPP activity and that a product of caspase cleavage is a DPP substrate for both DPP8 and DPP9.

Caspase cleavage of JUND abolishes its transactivation activity

The caspase cleavage site in JUND lies between its transactivation domain and its DNA binding domain. To test if cleavage affected the activity of JUND, we co‐expressed either full length JUND, JUND1‐244 (mimics N‐terminal fragment generated by caspase cleavage); Δ243 (JUND243‐341; mimics C terminal fragment generated by caspase cleavage) or Δ245 (JUND245‐341; mimics DPP proteolysis of Δ243; Figure 5a) with a luciferase reporter of AP1 activity. As expected, overexpression of full length JUND increased luciferase activity (Figure 5b). In contrast, all truncation mutants significantly reduced the basal AP1 activity in the cells (Figure 5b), suggesting that caspase cleavage of JUND not only abolishes transactivation activity of JUND but may also generate dominant negative fragments.

We also investigated whether mutants that mimic DPP proteolysis altered the function of BCL2L1 fragments generated by caspase cleavage. BHK cells were co‐transfected with either empty pcDNA3.1 vector, a Δ62 mutant (the C terminal fragment generated by caspase cleavage), a Δ64 mutant (mimics DPP proteolysis of Δ62) or Δ62/A64P truncation mutant plus a GFP plasmid. In the Δ62/A64P mutant the alanine 64 is replaced with proline (so N‐terminal Ser‐Pro‐Ala becomes Ser‐Pro‐Pro; Figure 5c) to prevent cleavage by DPP (Weiss et al., 2025). The number of green‐fluorescent cells and the percentage of small rounded green cells (apoptotic cells) was then assessed after 48 h. All three truncation mutants significantly (p < 0.01) reduced the number of green‐fluorescent cells compared to vector control by ~50% (Figure S5) and the increased proportion of apoptotic cells (Figure 4d, p < 0.01).

DISCUSSION

N‐terminomics is a powerful proteomic approach because it both enriches for the neo‐N‐termini generated by proteolysis, improving substrate discovery in complex mixtures, and also identifies the cleavage sites (Wiita et al., 2014). Despite its advantages, there are limitations: the subtiligase labels unblocked N‐termini so termini that are blocked in the cell after caspase cleavage are underrepresented (Hanna et al., 2023). In addition, although engineered subtiligases have broad specificity (Weeks & Wells, 2018), the N‐terminal amino acid sequence affects labeling efficiency, so some substrates are easier to detect than others. Even if labeled, Arg and Lys rich sequences, very short, highly hydrophobic, or poorly ionizing N‐terminal peptides are less likely to be identified by mass spectrometry, further limiting substrate identification.

Here we show that our own N‐terminomics datasets and those published by others contain previously unknown caspase substrates that are “hidden” by secondary proteolysis. Our use of broad‐spectrum cysteine and serine protease inhibitors during cell lysis, together with the observation that the secondary proteolysis is seen in the forward but not in the reverse N‐terminomics datasets, makes it unlikely that the secondary proteolysis is an artifact of cell lysis. Instead, it is more likely that the secondary proteolysis occurred in the intact cells and so reflects the biology/biochemistry of the cells.

All three of the proteins (JUND, MTSS1, and POLR1C) chosen to corroborate the N‐terminomics data showed cleavage by purified recombinant caspase‐3, and in apoptotic cells consistent with the mass spectrometry data. In addition, the P4‐P4′ sequence that contains the DXP motif is conserved in mouse and human for all three proteins (and for 64/85 proteins). Together, the data show that these three proteins are cleaved in several different cell types induced to die by several different apoptotic stimuli. This result supports the conclusion that these three, and by extension the other substrates reported here, are “hidden” caspase substrates cleaved during apoptosis.

JUNB is related to JUND and was previously reported as being cleaved at aspartic acid 134 by caspases at a site conserved between human and mouse (P4‐P4′: GFAD↓GFVK). This cleavage does not generate a DPP substrate and it is not found in JUND. Nor is the conserved TVPD↓VPSF JUND cleavage site we report here found in JUNB. These data point to different types of caspase‐mediated regulation of two Jun‐family members: one involving secondary proteolysis by a DPP and one not.

Despite the caspase cleavage of BCL2L1 being well‐attested (Clem et al., 1998; Fujita et al., 1998) and functionally important (Jonas et al., 2004; Ofengeim et al., 2012), it has never been previously identified by N‐terminomics. Our data suggest that this is because rapid processing by a DPP‐like activity, “hides” the caspase‐mediated cleavage. It is currently unclear if the removal of the two N terminal amino acids after caspase cleavage further modifies BCL2L1 activity.

Our data also suggest that these “hidden” substrates are detected only in apoptotic cells and not in cells undergoing caspase‐dependent differentiation. Previous investigations have identified substrates that are cleaved to bring about specific apoptotic changes, but the consensus is that there are no specific “death substrates” but that there is a large set of redundant cleavage events that can cause death. This makes discriminating between substrates that induce non‐death fates and those that produce death very challenging. For example, cleavage of ICAD, a key event in the archetypal apoptotic change of DNA‐laddering, is also necessary for myoblast differentiation (Larsen et al., 2010). This issue is underscored by proteomic approaches (Gomez‐Cardona et al., 2024; Conde‐Rubio et al., 2021) that show a very large overlap between the proteins cleaved by caspases in non‐lethal and lethal contexts. Therefore, the very strong association of the “hidden” substrates with apoptosis but not with differentiation is striking. It suggests that cleavage of the “hidden” substrates may identify apoptotic cells and that their cleavage could be used as a marker.

DPP7, 8, and 9 are intracellular serine proteases (Cui et al., 2022) that process short peptides and proteins (Finger et al., 2020; Geiss‐Friedlander et al., 2009; Justa‐Schuch et al., 2016; Wilson et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2015). Our data testing the expression of a BCL2L1 Δ62 mutant in the presence of two DPP inhibitors (Talabostat, which inhibits DPP7, 8, and 9, and 1G244, which inhibits DPP8 and 9 but not 7) excludes a role for DPP7. The siRNA data implicate both DPP8 and DPP9, which is consistent with the similar structure and substrate specificity of these enzymes (Ross et al., 2018). Previous reports have documented around 100 DPP8/9 substrates using a range of approaches, none of which are identified as DPP substrates in by our data. Most of these reported substrates are proteins cleaved at an MXP motif (Zhang et al., 2015), indicating two‐step processing by methionine aminopeptidase (MAP) followed by DPP. These events occur co‐translationally at the N‐terminus of the nascent polypeptide chain, whereas the DPP‐mediated cleavage events we have identified are contingent on caspase‐3 activity. This difference explains the lack of overlap between the two sets of DPP substrates.

In conclusion, N‐terminomic data sets can reveal interplay/relationships between different proteases, and we show that apoptosis involves sequential proteolysis of specific proteins by cysteine (caspases) and serine proteases (DPP8/9) that can “hide” caspase cleavage products. As a result, we identified new caspase substrates whose cleavage strongly correlates with cell death rather than with other non‐death fates induced by caspases.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Analysis of N‐terminomics data sets

N‐terminomics data from DEGRABASE (https://wellslab.ucsf.edu/degrabase/; Degrabase_1.0.xlsx) and from C2C12 cells (Gomez‐Cardona et al., 2024) (Modified_forward_data‐2.xlsx) were analyzed in R Studio. The C2C12 data were filtered to identify peptides labeled with Butyric acid (Abu). The data were then further filtered to identify all substrates cleaved where P1 = P and P3 = D across all five different cell populations (myoblast, differentiating (live), apoptotic (dead), myotubes, and reserve cells). The accession number was used to identify the gene name using UniProt. The accession number of the human protein was converted to the corresponding mouse accession number (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/orth). The list of gene names was then used to identify genes common to apoptotic Jurkat, MM1S, DB, U266 cell data reported in Degrabase. To analyze Degrabase, the data were filtered first on “Perturbagen” to remove data from untreated cells. Data from treated cells were then filtered on P1 = P and P3 = D. A similar approach was taken to identify cleavage products where P1 = A and P3 = D. Sequence logos for amino acids at cleavage sites were plotted using ggseqlogo package in R.

Immunoblotting

Fifty to 100 μg cell lysate was mixed with Laemmli buffer and incubated at 95°C for 5 min. Proteins were resolved on 8%–15% acrylamide gel depending on the protein size and transferred (100 V, 1–2.30 h depending on the size of the proteins of interest) onto a nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was blocked in 5% skim milk in PBS + Tween 20 (0.1%; PBST) for 1 h and then incubated with the primary antibody (1:1000 in PBST) overnight at 4°C. The membrane was washed three times (3 × 10 min) with PBST and then incubated with the secondary antibody (1:10,000 in PBST) for 1 h at room temperature. After three washes with PBST, the membrane was scanned by Li‐Cor Odyssey Scanner. Primary antibodies: anti‐HA antibodies (#3724—Cell Signaling Technologies). Secondary antibodies: IRDye® 800CW Goat anti‐Rabbit IgG, 926‐32211.

Mouse caspase‐3 expression and purification

Recombinant His‐tagged mouse caspase‐3 (pET23b‐mouse casp‐3‐His) was expressed in BL21 (DE3) pLysS Escherichia coli. Transformed E. coli were plated on Mueller‐Hinton agar with 100 μg/mL ampicillin and 25 μg/mL chloramphenicol at 37°C overnight. One colony was added to 10 mL of LB media (Fisher Scientific) with ampicillin and chloramphenicol. The starter culture was used to inoculate 1.5 L of LB broth, supplemented with ampicillin and chloramphenicol, at 37°C until the optical density reached 0.6. Protein expression was induced for 14 h at 20°C with 0.5 mM IPTG (Fisher Scientific). Cell pellets were collected by centrifugation 6000 × g for 30 min at 4°C and the pellet was resuspended in 45 mL of lysis buffer (100 mM NaCl and 100 mM Tris pH 8.0). Cells were lysed by high‐pressure homogenization (Avestin EmulsiFlex C3), then centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C. The clarified protein supernatant was passed through a 1 mL Ni2+ affinity column (Cytiva Inc.) and eluted with a linear gradient of buffer (250 mM NaCl, 200 mM imidazole, 100 mM Tris pH 8.0). The eluted protein fractions were assessed for purity using SDS‐PAGE and the mouse caspase‐3 fractions were pooled.

Site‐directed mutagenesis

Site‐directed mutagenesis was performed using PrimeSTAR® Max DNA Polymerase (R045A, Takara Bio) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Following PCR, the reaction was treated with DpnI (R0176, NEB) to digest the template plasmid and purified using a NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean‐up Kit (740609, Macherey‐Nagel). Desired mutations were introduced with primers listed in Table S6, and all mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Eurofins Genomics).

Culturing HEK293T, HeLa, and BHK cells

HEK293T, HeLa, and BHK cells (EACC) were maintained at <70% confluency in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM, Sigma‐Aldrich, D5796) supplemented with 10% FBS (Sigma‐Aldrich, D7524) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma‐Aldrich, P4333).

Over expressing epitope tagged proteins in HEK293T and HeLa

pcDNA3.1 constructs for candidate proteins were purchased from Genescript with an N terminal FLAG and a C terminal HA tag (see Table S5 for sequences). Chemically competent E. coli cells (Stellar cells; #636763, Takara Bio) were transformed with plasmid DNA by heat shock and selected on antibiotic‐containing agar plates. Individual colonies were expanded in liquid culture, and plasmid DNA was purified using a silica membrane‐based miniprep kit following the supplier's protocol. Purified plasmids were quantified spectrophotometrically and used directly for mammalian cell transfection experiments. HEK293T, Hela or BHK cells were reverse transfected with the constructs using polyethylenimine (PEI; ratio 3:1 of PEI:DNA) and the cells cultured for a further 48 h. Cells were then harvested and lysed (lysis buffer: 50 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 10 mM KCl, 5 mM EDTA, 0.1% Triton, 100 μM PMSF, 10 mM DTT, 1 μg/mL Cytochalasin B). Lysates were incubated on ice for 10 min, were vortex mixed, and centrifuged (20,000 × g, 20 min, 4°C). In some experiments, cells were treated with TPCK (100 μM; 4 h) or Etoposide (40 μM; 48 h) to activate caspase‐3 and induce apoptosis before cell lysates were prepared. In these experiments, the floating (apoptotic) cells and adherent cells were pooled and then lysed.

Cleavage of proteins by purified recombinant caspase‐3

Cell lysates were incubated with 200 nM purified recombinant mouse caspase‐3 for 1 h at 4 or 37°C and the cleavage of the proteins assessed by immunoblotting (see above).

Assessing the effect of DPP inhibitors and siRNAs on the levels of the BCL2L1 Δ62 A64R mutant

To test the DPP inhibitors, HEK293T cells were transfected with a plasmid encoding the BCL2L1 Δ62 A64R mutant with a C terminal HA tag. Cells were treated with Talabostat (2 μM) or 1G244 (10 μM) for 16 h before being harvested 48 h after transfection. Protein levels were then assessed by immunoblotting. To test siRNAs, HEK293T cells were seeded and cultured for 24 h prior to transfection. For each well, 0.3 μg BCL2L1 Δ62 A64R plasmid DNA and MISSION® esiRNAs targeting human DPP8 (Merck‐EHU075121) and DPP9 (Merck‐EHU158471) were diluted in 100 μL jetPRIME® buffer (final esiRNA concentration of 100 nM). The mixture was gently mixed by pipetting. JetPRIME® transfection reagent (Polyplus‐transfection, Cat. No. 114‐15) was vortexed for 5 s and briefly centrifuged before use. Then, 2 μL of jetPRIME® reagent was added to the DNA/esiRNA mixture, vortexed for 1 s, and briefly centrifuged. The transfection complexes were incubated at room temperature for 15 min. The transfection mixture was then added dropwise to HEK293T cells maintained in complete serum‐containing medium. Following transfection, cells were incubated for 48 h before being harvested and lysed. Protein lysates were collected, and protein expression levels were subsequently analyzed by immunoblotting.

Luciferase assays for JUND transactivation activity

HEK293T cells were transfected with the indicated JUND expression constructs and pGL4.44 [luc2P/AP1 RE/Hygro] reporter construct which contains luciferase under the control of an AP1 promoter (E411A‐Promega). Forty‐eight hours after transfection, cells were lysed and 25 μg of total protein was used in triplicate for firefly luciferase activity using the One‐Glo Luciferase Assay System (E6110‐Promega) in a PerkinElmer Victor microplate reader. The firefly luciferase activity was measured for each sample and normalized using the vector‐transfected cells before calculating the mean fold change and standard deviation for three independent experiments.

Assessing cell death induced by overexpression of BCL2L1 mutants

BHK cells were transfected using PEI as outlined above except the cells were co‐transfected with a plasmid encoding eGFP (4 parts test DNA: 1 part eGFP DNA). After 48 h, cells were imaged with an EVOS5000 and six fields (200–1200 cells) captured. The percentage of GFP positive apoptotic cells (small, rounded cells; circularity >0.85) was then assessed using imageJ (Clem et al., 1998).

Statistical analysis

Multivariate hypergeometric distribution test was used to assess whether the observed results were due to sample size. All other data were assessed using ANOVA with Tukey's post‐hoc test after a Shapiro–Wilks test for normality. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
