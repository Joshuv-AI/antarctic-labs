Chiral Twin-Electrodes for Amplifying the Faradaic Current of Enantiomers via Redox Cycling
Authors: Sorasak Klinyod, Zikkawas Pasom, Gerardo Salinas, Patrick Garrigue, Véronique Lapeyre, Stéphane Reculusa, Chularat Wattanakit, Alexander Kuhn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Redox cycling (RC) is often used to amplify the faradaic current associated with the electrochemical transformation of redox-active species. For a given concentration, this allows a considerable increase in sensitivity and an improvement in the detection limit of analytical devices. However, all previous reports have focused only on the detection of achiral molecules, e.g., ferrocene derivatives. In this contribution, we report an original setup composed of two metal electrodes encoded with chiral features, facing each other to form a microchannel. The presence of the same chiral feature on the two parallel-oriented electrodes enables the preferential cyclic transformation of a specific enantiomer. It will be more easily oxidized at the anode with the right chirality and can subsequently diffuse across the microchannel to be reduced back into the starting enantiomer at a cathode with an identical chiral feature. As a function of the width of the microchannel, this cycle is repeated multiple times, significantly enhancing the overall current in terms of chiral recognition. Herein, we illustrate this fundamental concept with mesoporous Pt–Ir alloy electrodes, imprinted with either L-DOPA or D-DOPA as transducers constituting the microchannel. The latter allows an amplification of the analytical signal of DOPA as a model compound by more than one order of magnitude and with an enantiodiscrimination efficiency reaching 90%. Thus, the proposed strategy opens up very interesting perspectives for chiral-sensing applications.

Introduction

In recent years, the synthesis or discrimination of chiral compounds has played an important role in various applications, in particular for the pharmaceutical industry, as the two enantiomers of a chiral compound can exhibit very different biological effects. For example, L-epinephrine (L-EP) is pharmacologically active in emergency medical treatments, including bronchospasm, anaphylaxis, and laryngeal, while D-epinephrine (D-EP) does not have a biological effect. , Another example is 3,4-dihydroxy-L-phenylalanine (L-DOPA), which can affect dopamine levels in the medical treatment of Parkinson’s disease, while its antipode (D-DOPA) is pharmacologically inactive. Therefore, the development of powerful strategies for both asymmetric synthesis and enantioselective recognition of chiral compounds is crucial in modern chemistry.

Recently, asymmetric synthesis of chiral compounds has been successfully demonstrated with mesoporous chiral metal electrodes, for instance, chiral-imprinted platinum (C-Imp-Pt) , and chiral-imprinted nickel (C-Imp-Ni). Employing such chiral electrodes for the reduction of ketone derivatives, including acetophenone and adrenalone, allows for the production of chiral compounds with an enantiomeric excess (% ee) of up to 80%. ,, The features of these chiral metal electrodes can also be advantageously used for the separation and detection of enantiomers, and their chemical and structural stability can be further enhanced when the pure metal is replaced by an alloy. , Therefore, a mesoporous chiral-encoded Pt–Ir alloy was considered to be a good candidate for the present study.

From an electroanalytical perspective, enhancing the sensitivity is commonly associated with increasing the magnitude of the amperometric response arising from the oxidative or reductive transformation of an analyte. In this context, a very attractive approach to signal amplification at a constant analyte concentration is based on successive oxidation and reduction cycles of the same molecule in a loop. This concept is analogous to the operating principle of scanning electrochemical microscopy (SECM), where redox cycling generates a measurable current, which is dependent on the probe-surface distance. − This distance-dependent sensitivity can be advantageously used in sensing applications via the concept of redox cycling (RC). RC is based on multiple sequential redox reactions of a small amount, or even a single molecule, of an analyte in a micro- or nanogap between the generating and collecting electrodes. The smaller the gap between the two electrodes, the higher the RC efficiency, similar to SECM and AFM-SECM techniques. , This is attributed to the ability to repeatedly reoxidize and rereduce the same redox-active species.

Accordingly, nanogap twin electrodes (∼40 nm to ∼70 nm) in a parallel configuration were developed in the past as transducers for the electrochemical detection of single molecules, such as ferricyanide and ferrocene derivatives. ,, Such devices are in general able to measure extremely low concentrations, leading to a current response in the femtoampere (10–15 A) range, which is close to the detection limit at room temperature. To further improve the sensitivity, porous coaxial twin electrodes allow for increasing the electrochemically active surface area, thereby enhancing the RC efficiency. These previous reports highlight the power of the RC concept for detecting extremely low concentrations of analytes. However, all of these RC experiments were focusing on the detection of achiral molecules. Consequently, we propose herein, as an important extension of this concept, the strategy of chiral redox cycling (c-RC), based on the parallel face-to-face arrangement of two mesoporous chiral-encoded Pt–Ir films, forming a microchannel containing the analyte. The currents generated by a specific enantiomer, which has a molecular structure that fits the chiral sites encoded in the metal matrix, can be amplified by more than an order of magnitude compared to the signal measured for the opposite enantiomer. This first set of proof-of-principle experiments opens up very interesting perspectives for the further development of enantioselective sensing devices with a considerably enhanced performance.

Experimental Section

Reagents and Materials

Hexachloroplatinic acid hexahydrate (H2PtCl6.6H2O), hexachloroiridic acid hydrate (H2IrCl6.xH2O), polyethylene glycol hexadecyl ether (Brij C10) 3,4-dihydroxy-L-phenylalanine (L-DOPA), and 3,4-dihydroxy-D-phenylalanine (D-DOPA) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Hydrochloric acid (HCl) was purchased from Quality Reagent Chemical (QRëC). Moreover, all chemicals were used without further purification. Milli-Q water with a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ·cm was used to prepare both the Pt–Ir plating gel and the reaction solution. Furthermore, gold-coated glass slides were used as a support for the electrochemical deposition of the Pt–Ir alloy.

Preparation of a Mesoporous Chiral Pt–Ir Alloy on Gold-Coated Glass Slides via Electrochemical Deposition

Electrochemical deposition of chiral-imprinted Pt–Ir films was performed with a conventional three-electrode setup using a Metrohm μAutolab Type III potentiostat. A clean gold-coated glass slide (0.50 cm–2), Pt mesh, and Ag/AgCl were used as working, counter, and reference electrodes, respectively. Prior to using the working electrode, it was cleaned with isopropyl alcohol under ultrasonication for 15 min. Then, the slide was washed with Milli-Q water several times and dried under N2 flow. After that, the Pt–Ir plating gel was prepared by mixing 29 wt % of metal precursors (H2PtCl6.6H2O: H2IrCl6.xH2O = 85:15), 28 wt % of Milli-Q water, and 43 wt % of Brij C10 and the chiral template, either D-DOPA (D-Imp) or L-DOPA (L-Imp), with a chiral molecule to metal salt molar ratio of 0.04. Then, the resulting paste was continuously mixed for 15 min and incubated at 40 °C for 30 min. This cycle (mixing and incubating) was repeated three times to obtain a homogeneous plating gel. Subsequently, the gel was positioned on a clean gold-coated glass slide, and the Pt mesh and Ag/AgCl electrodes were positioned on top of it. Then, chronoamperometry was performed at −0.05 V. After passing the desired charge density, the synthesized mesoporous chiral Pt–Ir electrode was washed with Milli-Q water under N2 atmosphere overnight to eliminate the chiral template and Brij C10 molecules, as illustrated in Scheme S1. Additionally, mesoporous nonimprinted Pt–Ir electrodes (N-Imp) were synthesized in the same way, but without adding the chiral template to the Pt–Ir plating gel.

In the next step, two slides, modified with mesoporous Pt–Ir films, imprinted either with L-DOPA (L-Imp) or D-DOPA (D-Imp), as well as nonimprinted mesoporous electrodes (N-Imp), were combined in a sandwich configuration by fixing them face-to-face at a given distance with the help of an intercalated thin plastic sheet at the borders. They are denoted as LL-Imp, DD-Imp, and NN-Imp sandwiches, respectively.

Characterization

The morphology of the prepared mesoporous Pt–Ir electrodes was visualized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) with a JEM-ARM200F (JEOL) microscope operating at 200 kV. In addition, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) on a HITACHI TM-1000 was employed to illustrate the cross section of the designed electrodes. Moreover, a scanning electron microscope equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDX) on a JSM-7610F (JEOL) field-emission electron microscope was used to map the element distribution of a mesoporous Pt–Ir film. In order to investigate the gradual elimination of organic template from the mesoporous Pt–Ir films during the washing step, ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectroscopy was used to follow the absorption band of the washing solution.

Electrochemical Studies

The electrochemical characterization and the chiral discrimination studies were performed using either a Metrohm μAutolab Type III or Autolab PGSTAT204 potentiostat equipped with a classic three-electrode setup using the mesoporous Pt–Ir, Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl), and platinum mesh as working, reference, and counter electrodes, respectively. The electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) of the prepared electrodes was evaluated by cyclic voltammetry (CV) in the potential range from −0.25 to 1.25 V (vs Ag/AgCl) with a scan rate of 100 mV s–1 in 0.5 M H2SO4 at room temperature.

The chiral recognition ability of the mesoporous chiral Pt–Ir layers was verified by differential pulse voltammetry (DPV) with a step potential of 10 mV, modulation amplitude of 50 mV, modulation time of 50 ms, and interval time of 500 ms. DPVs were recorded in 4 mM of either D-DOPA or L-DOPA with 50 mM HCl as supporting electrolyte.

In order to choose the right supporting electrolyte composition for the redox cycling, CVs in 4 mM DOPA with various concentrations of HCl, including 0.05, 0.10, 0.50, and 1.00 M, were performed using a Metrohm μAutolab Type III connected with Pt, Ag/AgCl (sat. KCl), and platinum mesh as working, reference, and counter electrodes, respectively. After determining the appropriated supporting electrolyte, the redox reactions were further studied on the mesoporous nonimprinted Pt–Ir electrodes with different scan rates of 5, 10, 25, 50, 100, and 200 mV s–1 in order to characterize the redox processes on these porous electrodes.

Redox Cycling with the Chiral Twin Electrode

The redox cycling reaction occurring in the narrow gap between the two chiral electrodes was investigated with either a Metrohm μAutolab Type III or Autolab PGSTAT204 potentiostat in the two-electrode mode, with one mesoporous Pt–Ir electrode connected as the working electrode and the second one as a combined counter/reference electrode. Prior to performing the redox cycling reaction in the microgap, the system was studied with linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) in 4 mM DOPA with 1 M HCl as supporting electrolyte with potential differences applied between the two electrodes of the sandwich ranging from 0.0 to 0.5 V (vs Ag/AgCl). After identifying the potential difference where DOPA is electroactive, chronoamperometry was carried out by applying a certain constant potential difference between the two electrodes constituting the chiral twin-electrode for a duration of 300 s to monitor the amperometric response of the chiral analytes.

Results and Discussion

Improving the detection of a chiral redox-active analyte can be in principle achieved by using the redox cycling (RC) concept based on a thin-layer cell composed of two electrodes encoded with the appropriate chiral information. When a sufficiently high potential difference is applied between the two facing chiral electrodes, the enantiomer, which has a conformation corresponding to the chiral sites encoded in the pores of the two working electrodes, will preferentially interact with the anode to be oxidized. The generated oxidized molecules can then cross the microchannel by diffusion and will be reduced back at the chiral cathode. This reaction sequence repeats multiple times, depending on the gap size, thus enhancing the signal of a given chiral analyte by increasing the faradaic current density (as illustrated in Scheme ). The main ingredients of these experiments are, of course, the chiral-imprinted electrodes constituting the channel walls. They are elaborated by following a previously described procedure, illustrated in Scheme S1. After the electrodeposition step, the organic templates (surfactant and chiral compounds) are removed by rinsing the electrodes and leaving them in Milli-Q water. UV–vis spectroscopy can be used to follow the elution of the organic molecules as a function of washing time, indicating a completely template-free mesoporous electrode after 12 h (Figure S1). After this step, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were employed to study the morphology of the prepared electrodes.

Schematic Illustration of the Redox Cycling Reactions of L-DOPA and D-DOPA Occurring in the Microgap between the Two Mesoporous Pt–Ir Electrodes in the Three Different Sandwich Devices, Namely LL-Imp, NN-Imp, and DD-Imp

The mesoporous Pt–Ir phase grown on the surface of the gold-coated glass slide has a thickness of around 500 nm (Figure S2a). Moreover, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) indicates a mesoporous structure with around 5 ± 1.0 nm pore diameter when analyzing statistically a subset of 20 pores (Figure S2b). , Furthermore, SEM-EDX was performed to investigate the elemental distribution on a mesoporous nonimprinted Pt–Ir film generated with a deposition charge density of 6 C cm–2 (Figure S3). The results indicate a homogeneous distribution of Pt and Ir throughout the sample, with atomic percentages of 97.3% and 2.7%, respectively (Table S1). This finding agrees well with a previous publication, which reports atomic percentages of Pt (97.0%) and Ir (3.0%) for a mesoporous Pt–Ir film generated with a deposition charge density of 6 C cm–2. The as-synthesized mesoporous Pt–Ir electrodes were then employed as working electrodes for the reversibility test of the chiral redox couple, and were also used as construction elements of the chiral twin-electrode device.

Reversibility of the Chiral Redox Couple

The perfect reversibility of the redox couple is another crucial ingredient for the RC system. It allows transformation of the reduced form by oxidizing it at the anode and reducing the product back at the cathode to the original form. This will occur multiple times in a short period within the small gap between the two electrodes, operating in a kind of generator-collector mode, , amplifying the analyte response. In the present work, the reversible redox transformation of DOPA was chosen as a model reaction for the RC process.

In order to investigate the reversibility of the DOPA redox couple, cyclic voltammograms (CVs) were recorded with a commercial Pt electrode in 4 mM DOPA with different HCl concentrations, including 0.05, 0.1, 0.5, and 1.0 M, at a scan rate of 50 mV s–1 (Figure S4). When increasing the HCl concentration from 0.05 M to 0.1, 0.5, and 1 M, a slight shift in the oxidation potential of the reduced form of DOPA is observed. This is consistent with findings from a previous report. However, the reduction potential of the oxidized form of DOPA shifts more significantly (from 370 mV to 530 mV) (Table S2). This expected shift of the reduction potential toward more positive values may be attributed to the increasing concentration of protons, which are required to reduce the quinone form of DOPA back to DOPA. However, the shift does not follow the 60 mV/pH unit changes, expected from the Nernst equation. This might be attributed to the fact that the Nernst equation uses proton activity, which is not equal to proton concentration when the system is operated in a high-ionic-strength solution. , Moreover, catechol-type molecules, like the DOPA/DOPA-quinone system might not be fully reversible, especially in strong acid. The positive shift of the reduction potential, combined with an insignificant shift of the oxidation potential decreases the overall potential difference (ΔE) between the two redox peaks from 280 mV to 110 mV (Table S2). A well-defined and symmetrical CV was recorded when using 4 mM DOPA in 1.0 M HCl as supporting electrolyte. Therefore, the RC process was further studied with these conditions.

As mentioned above, introducing porosity into the structure of the two working electrodes can increase the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) and improve the sensitivity of the detection process. ,, Consequently, the reversible DOPA redox couple was further studied with mesoporous nonimprinted Pt–Ir electrodes. The N-Imp film was used as a working electrode in a conventional three-electrode setup to examine the reversibility of the DOPA redox couple at the porous electrode. The CV profiles of a 4 mM DOPA solution in 1.0 M HCl were recorded as a function of the scan rate (in a range between 5 and 200 mV s–1) (Figure S5a). The characteristic oxidation and reduction reactions were observed at 640 mV and 560 mV, respectively, yielding a ΔE of 80 mV. This value is 30 mV lower when using the mesoporous Pt–Ir. This might be attributed to the higher specific surface area and the confinement effect of the mesoporous electrode, which enhances the analyte detectability compared to the flat electrode. − This can be considered as an important advantage of using mesoporous metal films for the recycling reaction. In addition, the peak currents of the oxidation and reduction reactions are directly proportional to the square root of the scan rate (Figure S5b), which is characteristic of a redox couple present in solution and arriving at the electrode by diffusion, in agreement with the Randles–Ševčik equation. These observations confirm that using a mesoporous Pt–Ir electrode, which has a higher electrochemically active surface area (ECSA), will improve the sensitivity of the detection.

Apart from N-Imp electrodes, films of either mesoporous L-DOPA- or D-DOPA-imprinted Pt–Ir (L-Imp or D-Imp) have also been synthesized via coelectrodeposition of Pt and Ir, in the presence of the surfactant and one of the two enantiomers (either L-DOPA or D-DOPA), acting as mesoporogen and chiral template, respectively.

Recognition Ability of Imprinted Electrodes

To further verify the possibility of enantiodiscrimination with these electrodes, DPV measurements were performed in a potential window from 0.35 to 0.65 V to compare the currents of the mesoporous L-Imp and D-Imp electrodes. The DPV curves reveal characteristic oxidation peaks for L-DOPA and D-DOPA at approximately 520 mV vs Ag/AgCl (Figure a and b). As anticipated, the L-Imp electrode shows a significantly higher anodic current density for L-DOPA than for D-DOPA (Figure a).

DPVs of L-DOPA (black) and D-DOPA (red) electrooxidation recorded with (a) L-DOPA imprinted mesoporous Pt–Ir (L-Imp) with an ECSA of 4.2 cm2 and (b) D-DOPA imprinted mesoporous Pt–Ir electrodes (D-Imp) with an ECSA of 3.2 cm2. The ECSA was evaluated by cyclic voltammetry in sulfuric acid.

In contrast, the D-Imp film preferentially oxidizes D-DOPA, as reflected by a higher current density than that for the opposite enantiomer (Figure b). These findings confirm the successful incorporation of chiral characteristics into the mesoporous Pt–Ir structure, even after the removal of the chiral template. In the next step, mesoporous N-Imp, L-Imp, and D-Imp electrodes were employed as transducers in a sandwich configuration to facilitate the RC within a small gap.

Validation of the Redox Cycling Process

To verify the RC in the microgap between the two parallel working electrodes, a twin-electrode thin-layer cell was built by fixing two individual commercial Pt or mesoporous Pt–Ir electrodes in a parallel, face-to-face configuration, separated by a microgap of 84 ± 1 μm. The gap was fixed by using plastic sheets placed at the edges, which served as spacers (Figure a–c). The RC process was then studied within the microgap of this two-electrode setup. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was first performed with this sandwich to determine the potential difference required to initiate RC in a solution containing 4 mM L-DOPA and 1 M HCl. The results show that the onset of the RC process occurs at a potential difference of approximately 350 mV, with the current increasing progressively as the applied voltage increases (Figure d). Based on these LSV data, a potential difference of 425 mV was selected for the subsequent redox cycling experiments, as the corresponding current is reasonably high, but not too high to avoid damaging the chiral features of the mesoporous Pt–Ir film. For potential differences above 500 mV between the two sandwich electrodes, the mesoporous films start detaching partially from the supporting electrode (gold-coated glass slide), as illustrated in Figure S6. Under these optimized conditions, the RC process for enantioselective reactions was systematically investigated by comparing the RC efficiency between twin commercial flat Pt and mesoporous Pt–Ir electrodes.

To demonstrate the advantages of the mesoporosity of the two working electrodes, the amperometric responses in terms of current density (J), produced by the flat Pt and the mesoporous Pt–Ir sandwich, have been compared for an identical micro distance in 4 mM DOPA with 1 M HCl as the supporting electrolyte (Figure e). It was found that the averaged current density produced by the twin NN-Imp setup (∼498.8 μA cm–2) over 300 s was approximately 52 times higher compared to a twin flat Pt electrode (∼9.6 μA cm–2). Introducing a porous feature enhances the interaction of the analyte with the electrode surface, thereby improving the efficiency of the system. This might be considered as a first good argument for using the two mesoporous metal films as building blocks of the transducer device for improving its sensitivity.

Moreover, the RC process occurring in two twin NN-Imp devices with gaps of 84 ± 1 μm and 538 ± 5 μm has also been studied in 1 M HCl containing 4 mM L-DOPA to characterize the amplification ability of the RC process as a function of the interelectrode distance. These results demonstrate that increasing the microgap from 84 to 538 μm adversely affects the current response, resulting in a lower current density generated by the analyte. Specifically, the current density decreases by approximately a factor of 7.7 when the microchannel height is increased to 538 μm compared to the NN-Imp device with an 84 μm gap, which is quite consistent with the theoretically predicted decrease by a factor of 6.4. After this first illustration of the beneficial amplification effect of the RC in the gap, even without using chiral-encoded electrodes, the next step introduces chiral information into both electrodes of the sandwich. In principle, the faradaic current density, associated with the transformation of the matching enantiomer, is expected to be significantly enhanced, whereas its antipode should generate significantly lower current responses, although discrimination cannot be absolute because the individual electrodes also do not show absolute selectivity (Figure ).

Enantioselective Enhancement of the Chiral RC Signal

Beyond the general advantage of conventional RC systems, incorporating chiral features into both electrodes should enable enantioselective RC, further enhancing the analytical performance of such devices. An increase in current density is expected for the enantiomer whose chirality matches the chiral information imprinted in the mesoporous Pt–Ir films. Consequently, twin mesoporous L-DOPA-imprinted (LL-Imp), D-DOPA-imprinted (DD-Imp), and nonimprinted (NN-Imp) Pt–Ir films were employed to design the corresponding transducer sandwiches. Their RC efficiencies in 4 mM of either L-DOPA or D-DOPA in 1 M HCl were systematically evaluated using chronoamperometry. In a D-DOPA solution, the twin DD-Imp electrode exhibits a markedly higher capability for D-DOPA cycling than the NN-Imp and LL-Imp configurations. This is evidenced by the very different averaged current density recorded over a period of 300 s, being 627.1 μA cm–2 for DD-Imp, 372.1 μA cm–2 for NN-Imp, and 27.2 μA cm–2 for LL-Imp, as illustrated in Figure a. Conversely, L-DOPA undergoes preferential cycling within the microchannel of the LL-Imp sandwich device, yielding an averaged current density of 906.7 μA cm–2. This value is significantly higher than those obtained with the NN-Imp (428.4 μA cm–2) and DD-Imp electrodes (42.1 μA cm–2), as depicted in Figure b. Moreover, the enantioselective enhancement of DD-Imp in D-DOPA solution over a short period (25 s of interval time) was investigated by dividing the J value obtained with DD-Imp by that of LL-Imp. It was found that the J ratios gradually increase with time and saturate after 100 s (Figure c, red dots). Mirror-image results were observed when the analyte solution was switched from D-DOPA to L-DOPA with the LL-Imp sandwich (Figure d, black dots). This indicates that at the initial state of the RC process, the oxidized form of the analyte needs time to diffuse from the anode to the cathode for reduction, and the reduced form then diffuses back to the anode for reoxidation. These events occur multiple times, increasing the faradaic current, which saturates for longer times.

Additionally, a quite identical J ratio was observed when comparing the J value of NN-Imp with either the one of LL-Imp in D-DOPA (Figure c, blue dots) or the one recorded with DD-Imp in L-DOPA (Figure d, blue dots) solutions. This implies that an achiral device does not improve the sensitivity for a specific enantiomer, but has a better performance than the electrode sandwich imprinted with the wrong enantiomer. This can be attributed to a structural mismatch between the chiral analyte and the microscopic architecture of the mesoporous imprinted matrices, which slows down its conversion and consequently decreases the current generated by the RC system. The enantioselectivity for D-DOPA is around 89% for DD-Imp and −94% for LL-Imp. Conversely, for L-DOPA, the selectivity is 94% in LL-Imp and −89% in DD-Imp, revealing an almost mirror-symmetric behavior (Figure e and f), with an average absolute EEF value of 92%.

These observations demonstrate that the current response of a specific chiral analyte can be selectively amplified by more than one order of magnitude compared to its antipode. The enhanced current density is attributed to an improved conversion efficiency of the enantiomer whose chiral configuration matches the chiral architecture embedded in the mesoporous Pt–Ir matrices of the electroanalytical device. This loss of amplification might be attributed to the blocking of the mesopores by organic species, leading to a gradual decrease of the ECSA, confirmed by CV, as shown in Figure S7. Nevertheless, the enantioselectivity of the reused transducers remains significant, with amplification factors still exceeding one order of magnitude. To reactivate the chiral transducer after the c-RC process, a potential-induced surface regeneration concept, as proposed in a previous publication, might be employed to eliminate physisorbed organic compounds and recover the device’s efficiency.

Considering the practical use of the twin-mesoporous chiral transducers, the limit of detection (LOD) value needs to be estimated by following eq . 6 LOD=3.3σS where S and σ are the values of the slope of the calibration curve (Table S3 and Figure S8) and the standard deviation of the blank signal (Table S4), respectively. For the tested LL-Imp device, this leads to a LOD of 40.6 μM.

To understand whether the redox cycling efficiency is concentration dependent, we studied the performance of the LL-Imp device close to the LOD (42 μM of L-DOPA and D-DOPA; Figure S9). The average current density of L-DOPA and D-DOPA is 84.7 μA cm–2 and 33.7 μA cm–2, yielding an enantiomeric amplification factor of 2.5. Even though the amplification decreases significantly when the L-DOPA concentration is lowered, enantioselective amplification is still observed.

An interesting question that arises after this demonstration of amplified enantiodiscrimination is whether it is possible to determine the ratio of two enantiomers with such a device. To allow for the detection of the individual chiral compounds in a mixture of L-DOPA and D-DOPA with varying ratios, the electrodes would need to be 100% selective for a specific enantiomer, like the ones coated with chiral polymers that we have used in some of our previous contributions. However, the present mesoporous chiral Pt–Ir can by definition not be 100% selective for a single enantiomer because the imprinted electrodes not only have chiral recognition sites but also unselective parts, especially at the outermost surface of the electrodes, as illustrated by the DPVs of Figure . Nevertheless, it would be possible to combine two RC devices imprinted with opposite enantiomers (LL-Imp and DD-Imp sandwich) in the same analytical platform and expose them to the same solution. Comparing the two c-RC currents would then allow one to calculate the ratio of the two enantiomers in the mixture. Accordingly, the overall c-RC strategy is a promising approach for advanced chiral sensing applications based on a concept that has not been investigated so far.

Conclusion

We report the development of a chiral sensing strategy based on an enantioselective current enhancement by redox cycling (c-RC). This can be achieved by encoding chiral information into two mesoporous metal electrodes, positioned face-to-face to form a microgap in which redox-active chiral molecules are oxidized at one electrode and reduced back at the opposite electrode multiple times. This leads to a very significant enantioselective amplification factor. The LL-Imp transducer selectively oxidizes and reduces L-DOPA with an amplification factor of around 20 compared to that of the DD-Imp device, and the latter shows a very similar enhancement factor when operating in a D-DOPA solution. As expected, this discrimination is not observed for an achiral mesoporous sensing device (NN-Imp). The proposed strategy, which, to the best of our knowledge, has not been explored before, opens up a new avenue for extending the applications of mesoporous chiral metal films to the field of redox cycling for the further development of fully operational enantioselective sensing platforms with significantly enhanced performance.
