Where does excessive beta in Parkinson's disease originate? Insights from a case of unilateral cortico-pallidal pathway dysfunction
Authors: Leonardo Rigon, Valentina D'Onofrio, Laura Ludovica Grassi, Dario Ciprietti, Andrea Landi, Angelo Antonini, Andrea Guerra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plain language summary Detecting the source of abnormal beta oscillations in Parkinson's Disease: evidence from a case of impaired connection between cerebral cortex and basal ganglia. In Parkinson's Disease, a key feature is an exaggerated, oscillatory activity in the brain called the “beta rhythm”, which occurs in the circuit, particularly relevant for motor functions, connecting the cortex (the outer brain layer) and the basal ganglia (the deeper structures). Scientists have long debated whether the exact source of this pathological activity was located in the cortex or in the basal ganglia. This report describes a critical clue from an unexpected event: a person with Parkinson's disease developed temporary swelling (edema) that affected both the sensorimotor cortex and the basal ganglia. Notably, this event was limited to one side of the brain and occurred in a subject who carried a medical device that enables recordings of the beta rhythm from inside the basal ganglia. In this peculiar case, the excessive beta rhythm on the affected side was completely suppressed and only returned once the swelling in the overlying sensorimotor cortex had resolved, even though the edema in the basal ganglia itself had recovered earlier. This unique observation suggests that the integrity of the cortex is essential for the pathological beta rhythm to occur in Parkinson's Disease, lending strong support to the theory that this disruptive electrical rhythm is generated in the cortex before spreading to deeper brain structures such as the basal ganglia.

Dear Editor,

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disorder primarily affecting the basal ganglia-thalamo-cortical circuitry, and abnormally hypersynchronous oscillations in the beta frequency range (13–30 Hz) within the cortical-subcortical motor network represent a pathophysiological hallmark of the disease.1,2 Local field potentials (LFP) and electroencephalography recordings have consistently demonstrated their presence in deep brain stimulation (DBS) target structures (including the globus pallidus internus (GPi)) as well as in the coherence between basal ganglia (BG) and sensorimotor cortex (SMC).3,4 Importantly, hypersynchronous beta activity correlates with motor symptom severity and is suppressed by both dopaminergic medication and DBS,3,4 making it a valuable biomarker that has been recently translated into novel adaptive DBS systems.

However, the source of pathological beta activity in PD remains debated. 5 While early evidence suggested that abnormal beta oscillations originate in the BG and secondarily spread to the SMC, subsequent studies proposed the opposite hypothesis.3,6 Indeed, functional connectivity analyses between cortex and BG provided a higher likelihood for cortical beta driving into the BG rather than the opposite.1,3,6,7 Accordingly, enhancing beta oscillations at the SMC through non-invasive brain stimulation worsened bradykinesia severity in PD patients. 8

Recent advancements in DBS technology have enabled the chronic recording of LFPs via implanted electrodes, allowing continuous monitoring of beta dynamics in target structures, thus providing a unique window on PD neurophysiological abnormalities.

We describe the case of a PD patient implanted with a sensing-enabled DBS system who developed perioperatively severe unilateral cerebral edema, unintentionally providing a lesional model of transient, unilateral cortico-BG pathway dysfunction. We analyzed longitudinal changes in chronic LFP recordings and contextualized them to the progressive edema resolution. Specifically, brain CT scans were obtained upon clinical indication at post-operative Days 1-5-7-13-34 and presence/absence of structural lesions in the BG and SMC was visually assessed by trained neurologists (LR and VD) and confirmed by a senior member of our team (AG). LFP recordings were obtained concomitantly, while impedances were routinely measured to ensure circuit integrity. Accurate lead placement was consistently confirmed using LeadDBS (details in Supplementary Materials and Supplementary Figure 1). This research was completed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration and approved by the Local Ethics Committee of Padua Province. The patient gave written informed consent for case publication.

A 55-year-old Caucasian man was referred to GPi-DBS for PD complicated by refractory fluctuations. He had no further relevant medical history.

Sensing-enabled Medtronic Percept™ PC neurostimulator with SenSight™ directional leads (B33005) were implanted under general anesthesia. No immediate complications occurred and the patient fully recovered from anesthesia by the evening. Post-operative head CT showed mild bilateral frontal pneumocephalus without major intracranial bleeding or edema. The day after surgery, LFP recordings were performed as part of routine investigations at our center. Five days after surgery, the patient developed left hemiparesis. Urgent head CT revealed massive right peri-lead edema and pneumocephalus, initially treated with intravenous mannitol plus steroids and eventually required surgical evacuation. Chronic LFP recordings were maintained active over time (Figure 1A) while neuroimaging and BrainSense™ indefinite streaming (IS) acquisitions were repeated at four postoperative timepoints. Notably, throughout the entire observational period stimulation was kept off while dopaminergic medications were unchanged.

As massive right-hemispheric edema developed on post-operative Day-5, beta activity remained markedly suppressed in the homolateral (right) GPi, while gradually reappearing in the left GPi. Crucially, beta power suppression in the right GPi persisted despite radiological resolution of pallidal edema at post-operative Day-13. Then, as the right SMC edema resolved, beta activity re-emerged (post-operative Day-34, Figure 1B); concomitantly, left hemiparesis completely recovered. In the acute phase, the systematic assessment of parkinsonian symptom severity was limited by the patient's hemiparesis and potentially unstable clinical conditions. However, we observed slight improvement in rigidity and disappearance of rest tremor in the left limbs. Eventually, when formal assessment of parkinsonism returned feasible (post-operative Day 34) the MDS-UPDRS III score in the OFF condition was comparable to that before surgery (Supplementary Table 2).

The Mann-Kendall analysis showed a trend of beta power increase over time in the right GPi (p<0.001), while no significant changes were observed in the left (unaffected) GPi (p=0.72). In the right GPi, 5-day regression slopes of beta power were close to zero until post-operative Day-12, consistent with persistent beta suppression, then became positive after Day-13, mirroring beta re-emergence. Conversely, in the left GPi beta appeared to fluctuate throughout the observational period (Figure 1A). In contrast to the differential changes in beta activity, gamma power dynamics were similar between sides (Figure 1B).

In this report, we present a unique case of iatrogenic, unilateral, transient cortico-pallidal pathway dysfunction occurring in a patient implanted with a sensing-enabled DBS system.

In the left (unaffected) GPi, chronic beta recordings showed a reduction in beta power during the first postoperative days, reflected by a negative regression slope, followed by a progressive rebound (positive slopes). This pattern might be compatible with a mild postoperative microlesional effect, consistent with prior findings. 9

A different pattern emerged in the right (affected) GPi. Chronic LFP recordings revealed a substantial suppression of pallidal beta activity after the onset on post-operative Day-5 of extensive edema spanning from the SMC through the BG. This suppression was more prominent and persistent than in the contralateral (unaffected) hemisphere and showed a significant monotonic trend over time. Crucially, beta power remained suppressed despite radiological resolution of the pallidal edema (post-operative Day-13) and only recovered once the cortical edema resolved (post-operative Day-34). The temporal coupling between cortical recovery and beta reappearance supports the idea that SMC integrity is essential for pathological beta activity to be generated in PD.

Importantly, while beta activity was markedly suppressed in the affected GPi, gamma activity remained stable and exhibited comparable temporal patterns across hemispheres. This finding demonstrates the frequency specificity of changes in oscillatory dynamics observed in our patient. Notably, pallidal gamma activity is thought to arise predominantly from local microcircuitry, 10 potentially rendering it less susceptible to disruptions in network connectivity. The preservation of gamma activity therefore supports the idea that beta suppression did not reflect a generalized loss of signal, but rather a selective disruption of the cortico-pallidal beta-generating network.

By its nature, this case report of an unexpected, severe surgical adverse event could not follow a pre-established study protocol. Rather, assessments were performed strictly in accordance with standard clinical practice, inevitably leading to methodological limitations. First, we cannot entirely rule out that postoperative lead migration might have affected the LFP recordings. However, despite observing some displacement following edema reabsorption, both leads were consistently localized within the target. Notably, the right lead was confirmed to be within the posterior GPi even on postoperative Day 5, when beta activity was reduced. Moreover, we investigated brain activity solely through longitudinal LFP recordings. We acknowledge that more advanced investigations, including cortico-subcortical (i.e., EEG-LFP) coherence analyses, would have been most informative in this context. However, these techniques fall beyond the scope of this report, which is based exclusively on standard clinical DBS assessments.

In conclusion, hypersynchronous beta oscillations are an established neurophysiological signature of PD, yet the main source of this activity remains debated. We presented a case of unilateral, delayed-onset brain edema, serving as an incidental lesional model of transient cortico-pallidal pathway dysfunction, where a frequency-specific suppression of beta activity appeared after the lesion, persisted beyond nuclear edema resolution and lasted until SMC recovery. Overall, our findings indicate that SMC integrity is essential for the emergence of exaggerated beta oscillations within the cortico-basal ganglia loop in PD, and support the hypothesis of a cortically driven beta activity toward the BG.1,6 Although the conclusions derived from a single case must be interpreted with caution, our report contributes to the ongoing debate on the source of beta hypersynchrony in PD, highlighting the crucial role of the cerebral cortex in pallidal oscillatory dynamics.
