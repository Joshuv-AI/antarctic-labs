Purpose of the Technical Assistance

• The purpose of this technical assistance is to quantify the potential impact of developing designated
 sites on Kentucky's electricity load growth, providing insights at both the state and county level
 considering the next 10 years.

• This analysis should explicitly incorporate and address key project uncertainties by developing various load
 growth scenarios that account for:

 • Development scale: The total area (square footage) developed across the state within the study's time
 horizon.

 • Site specificity: The precise location of the sites that achieve successful development.

 • Sector variability: The specific business or industrial sectors (and their associated load profiles) that will
 ultimately occupy each developed zone.

• Geospatial analysis: The technical assistance will include a geospatial analysis of the load forecasting
 results to map the impact of each one of the developed scenarios on the distribution of electrical load and
 visualize potential spatial impacts and provide insights that enable the Kentucky Energy and Environment
 Cabinet to anticipate possible constraints on the grid.

• The need for a site-specific analysis comes from the understanding that conventional econometric (top-
 down) load forecasting models cannot sufficiently isolate or predict the discrete load increases
 resulting from the development of these unique and targeted economic sites.

 Purpose of the Technical Assistance

 • In addition, the study will generate scenario-based load time series by county across

 realistic grid planning scenarios and, consequently, guide more effective

 Overview of Load Forecasting Methods
  ECONOMETRIC METHOD (Top-Down)

  • Combines economic theory and statistical techniques to estimate relationships between aggregated energy
 consumption (i.e., yearly) and different factors such as:

 • Population growth

 • Business and industrial growth

 • Income and employment

 • Weather and climate

 • Electricity prices.

  END-USE METHODS (Bottom-Up)
  • These methods try to establish the demand consumption by end-use (i.e., each color of the figure Residential Load Profiles
 at the bottom). Often consists of a two-step process: Disaggregated by End-Use
 1. Technology adoption modeling
 • Considers historical adoption, technoeconomic factors, stock turnover, etc.
 • Determines the customer adoption rate and/or nameplate capacity.
 2. Energy modeling
 • Considers weather, customer behavior, historical load shapes.
 • Generates peak demand levels and/or load profiles.

INPUTS: ComStock/ResStock
 The ComStock and ResStock datasets were filtered to isolate
 normalized time series load profiles representative of typical
 commercial buildings within Kentucky.

 This filtering process yielded a final set of 49 distinct building
 load profiles for use in the analysis.

 • The dataset contains information about 7 different buildings
 type groups: Education, Food Service, Healthcare Lodging
 Mercantile and Warehouse/Storage.

 • Each building type group contain different subcategories as
 illustrated in the figures.

 To characterize these profiles, the following key metrics are
 presented:

 • Building size: average and standard deviation of modeled
 floor area.

 • Energy intensity: average and standard deviation of site
 energy intensity.

 The ComStock time series dataset for Kentucky is publicly
 available as well as the corresponding metadata.

 INPUTS: Synthetic Industrial Profiles

 To overcome the lack of normalized industrial load data, a synthetic profile generation
 methodology was developed utilizing two primary sources:

 • Load shape proxy (hourly data): Hourly time series data for emissions and energy
 inputs (e.g., boilers, furnaces) were extracted from the U.S. Environmental Protection
 Agency’s Clean Air Markets Program Data for the year 2018. This data serves as a
 high-resolution proxy for industrial activity patterns.

 • The samples are predominantly for industries with high utilization factors1.
 Representing industries with low utilization factors might cause errors in peak
 demand estimation.
 Examples of Synthetic Normalized Industrial Profiles

 • Quality control: Missing data points were corrected using a 1-week moving buffer
 and interpolation.

 • Scale and intensity (annual data): Real-world annual electricity consumption and
 floor area data for various Kentucky industries were sourced from the U.S. Department
 of Energy Industrial Training and Assessment Centers (ITAC) program.

 Profile Integration: Both datasets were merged using corresponding North American
 Industry Classification System codes to derive a set of calculated hourly energy
 intensity time series.

Details About Monte Carlo Simulation Setup

 • Total area development through the horizon was estimated to
 be between 30% and 60% of the total mapped area. This was
 estimated based on recent development of sites supported by
 financial incentives from state-level programs.
 • Each Monte Carlo simulation generated 100 different scenarios:
 • Given some specific sites with a large amount of area available,
 which might be difficult to completely develop in one year, the
 simulation was carried out considering steps of two years.
 • Each Monte Carlo scenario keeps the chronology in the
 developed sites across the 10 years of horizon.
 • The kind of business that can be developed at each site has been
 constrained to the higher-probability kind of business that might
 be developed according to the metadata in the Kentucky’s
 ZoomProspector dataset.
 • Each kind of business has a set of possible normalized time
 series that are pondered according to the site area.

RESULTS: Baseline – Comments
The visualizations demonstrate the localized impact of developing the mapped economic sites, a load addition that conventional econometric
models cannot capture.

Moderate overall state impact:

• At the aggregate state level (top-left plot), the new economic development sites scenarios (represented by the average blue line and the
 minimum to maximum range shaded area) show an impact, but it is not drastic when compared to the existing, underlying ReEDS
 terawatt-hour (TWh) econometric forecast (dashed red line).

• The uncertainty band (blue shaded area) is relatively narrow compared to the total TWh scale.

County-level detail (local impact and uncertainty): When zooming into specific counties, the analysis suggests that the impact of the
economic development sites tends to be highly localized and significant for certain regions.

• Counties like Boone County and Bullitt County show a notably larger blue shaded area (minimum to maximum range). This wide range
 indicates a much higher degree of uncertainty in future load, driven by the varying of economic sites that might be developed and the
 types of businesses that will be established on those.

 • In Boone County, the average blue line exhibits a greater deviation from the ReEDS econometric baseline. This suggests that a
 considerable amount of the Monte Carlo scenarios result in significant load additions, consistently elevating the county's projected
 energy demand.

 • Conversely, in Bullitt County, the average line is closer to the baseline, indicating that only a select number of high-impact scenarios
 substantially elevate the load above the econometric projection.

 • This variance in the average outcome is consistent with the input data: the larger potential load increase and greater overall uncertainty in
 Boone County aligns with it having the highest mapped area of economic development sites in the inventory.

RESULTS: Baseline – Comments

County-level detail (local impact and uncertainty) continued:

• Moderate/low-impact counties: Counties like Jefferson County, Fayette County, and Campbell County show a narrower shaded area.

• While the scenarios elevate the average load above the ReEDS baseline, the relatively smaller width of the minimum to maximum range
 suggests lower uncertainty or less volatility from the selection and timing of sites in these particular counties.

Significance: Results deliver projected impacts to electricity demand which may inform evaluation of potential programs which incentivize
economic development.

• While econometric methods capture general trends, only a site-specific, bottom-up approach can adequately reveal the magnitude and
 geospatial uncertainty on the electricity demand caused by the development of such sites. This will help to inform integrated resource
 planning as well as transmission and distribution planning.

Key Takeaways

  • Load growth scenarios across the state of Kentucky are estimated in response to speculative
 economic development and related incentives; results are mapped to counties.

  • A novel method was applied which constrains demand forecasts according to realistic
 potential economic development, according to location and types of load. This advances
 understanding of potential siting, primary drivers of load growth, and related uncertainty which, in
 turn, enables anticipation of grid constraints and improved grid planning.

  • The methodology consider uncertainty from inputs including
 • Total developed area over time
 • Location and time of development across sites
 • Business type by location and business-specific load profiles

Limitations and Future Work

 • The highly integrated methodology requires clear documentation and interpretation of assumptions related to each data
 input.
 • The assumptions from econometric-based demand projections and the dataset of economic development sites must be consistent to avoid double counting load
 additions or any other inconsistencies (i.e. assumed weather years for load time-series of different inputs).

 • In this study, it was assumed that conventional econometric (top-down) load forecasting from ReEDS did not account for potential discrete load increases
 resulting from the development of the economic sites mapped in the Kentucky ZoomProspector website.

 • Results near the end of the forecast horizon have lower statistical robustness, not only due to increased temporal
 uncertainty, but also because of some inherent limitations of the method.
 • The model cannot anticipate new economic development sites over time; it is constrained to those included in the input data. Therefore, as the simulation makes
 progress developing sites, it has fewer potential sites to select in subsequent iterations. This reduces the robustness of the stochastic assessment.

 • Consequently, this methodology should be applied to planning horizons for which the expected total developed area by the end of the horizon is substantially less
 than the total potential area at the beginning. This study assumed 30% to 60% of the total area of the dataset would be developed area at the horizon’s end.

 • There is a lack of open-source datasets of normalized industrial load profiles for different industries and for different
 weather years.
 • Load forecasting results would benefit from increasingly standardized, informative inventories of economic development
 sites. This could include consistent reporting of site location, area, recommended uses or business types, existing or
 planned infrastructure, zoning, and more.
