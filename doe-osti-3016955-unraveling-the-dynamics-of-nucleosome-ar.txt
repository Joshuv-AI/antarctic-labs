Project Overview

The organization of genomic DNA into chromatin is a fundamental determinant of genome
stability, regulation, and cellular function. Nucleosomes, the basic repeating units of chromatin,
assemble into higher-order structures whose organization and heterogeneity remain difficult to
characterize using conventional ensemble-averaged techniques. A key need in the field is the
development of experimental approaches capable of directly visualizing nucleosome assemblies
and their structural variability at the single-molecule level.

This LDRD Lab-Wide project focused on establishing and evaluating atomic force microscopy
(AFM)–based approaches for the characterization of nucleosome assemblies. The work
emphasized experimental workflows for preparing, imaging, and assessing multi-nucleosome
systems, rather than isolated single nucleosomes. Through method development and
exploratory measurements, the project demonstrated the feasibility of applying scanning probe
microscopy to investigate chromatin-relevant assemblies and provided preliminary insight into
the strengths and limitations of this approach for future quantitative studies. Results and
lessons learned from this effort were disseminated to the broader scientific community through
multiple national conference presentations, helping to position LLNL for continued work in
chromatin and genome organization research.

Mission Impact

This research contributed to LLNL missions by advancing experimental capabilities in high-
resolution imaging and biophysical characterization of complex biological systems. The
development and evaluation of AFM-based workflows for nucleosome assemblies strengthened
Laboratory expertise in scanning probe microscopy, supported cross-disciplinary collaboration
between biosciences and materials science, and enhanced LLNL’s visibility in the broader
structural biology community. The outcomes of this work are relevant to DOE and NNSA
missions that depend on a fundamental understanding of genome organization, biomolecular
structure, and complex biological processes, and they provide a foundation for future
programmatic research and external collaborations.

Abstract
Chromatin organization plays a central role in genome regulation, stability, and cellular
function, yet the structural heterogeneity of nucleosome assemblies remains challenging to
characterize using conventional ensemble techniques. This LDRD Lab-Wide project sought to
develop and assess atomic force microscopy (AFM)–based approaches for the visualization and
characterization of nucleosome assemblies at the single-molecule level. The project focused on
experimental workflows for sample preparation, imaging, and qualitative analysis of multi-
nucleosome systems. Through method development and exploratory measurements, this work
established the feasibility of applying scanning probe microscopy to chromatin-relevant
assemblies, identified key technical challenges, and laid the groundwork for future quantitative
studies. Project outcomes were disseminated through multiple national conference
presentations, contributing to capability development and external visibility for LLNL in
chromatin and genome organization research.
Background and Research Objectives
The packaging of genomic DNA into chromatin enables meters of DNA to be compacted within
the nucleus while maintaining accessibility for essential processes such as transcription,
replication, and repair. At the core of chromatin structure are nucleosomes, which consist of
DNA wrapped around histone protein octamers. While high-resolution structural methods have
provided detailed information on individual nucleosomes, understanding how nucleosomes
assemble into higher-order structures and how structural heterogeneity emerges remains an
open challenge.
Many existing techniques for studying chromatin rely on ensemble-averaged measurements
that obscure heterogeneity and transient structural states. Single-molecule approaches,
including scanning probe microscopy, offer a complementary route for directly visualizing
individual nucleosome assemblies. Atomic force microscopy, in particular, provides the ability
to image biological samples under near-physiological conditions with nanometer-scale
resolution, making it well suited for studying heterogeneous chromatin systems.
The primary objectives of this project were to (1) establish experimental workflows for AFM-
based imaging of nucleosome assemblies, (2) assess the feasibility of resolving structural
features of multi-nucleosome systems, and (3) identify technical limitations and optimization
strategies relevant to future chromatin studies. As the project progressed, additional objectives
emerged related to instrument optimization, cross-disciplinary consultation, and dissemination
of preliminary findings to the broader scientific community.
Scientific Approach and Accomplishments
The scientific approach of this project centered on the development and evaluation of AFM-
based methods for imaging nucleosome assemblies. Experimental efforts focused on sample
preparation strategies for in vitro nucleosome assembly, surface immobilization approaches
compatible with scanning probe microscopy, and optimization of imaging parameters to
improve resolution and reproducibility.

Methodological guidance for in vivo and in vitro nucleosome assembly was provided through
collaboration with colleagues experienced in chromatin biochemistry, enabling the preparation
of samples suitable for AFM characterization. Imaging experiments explored a range of
scanning probe configurations and operating conditions to balance resolution, stability, and
sample integrity. Input from materials science expertise informed considerations of probe
geometry, surface interactions, and complementary electron microscopy observations relevant
to improving scanning probe performance.

Instrument reliability and performance were supported through troubleshooting and
maintenance efforts that addressed operational issues encountered during the project.
Together, these activities resulted in the establishment of practical workflows for AFM-based
imaging of nucleosome assemblies and generated preliminary qualitative observations
regarding assembly morphology and heterogeneity. While the project did not culminate in a
finalized quantitative dataset, it successfully demonstrated feasibility, clarified technical
challenges, and defined clear pathways for future refinement and application.
Mission Impact
This project strengthened LLNL’s capabilities in single-molecule biophysics and high-resolution
imaging by advancing AFM-based approaches applicable to complex biological assemblies. The
work fostered collaboration across disciplines, integrating biosciences, materials science, and
instrumentation expertise. The methodologies and expertise developed through this project are
broadly applicable to future research in genome organization, biosecurity, and biomolecular
systems.
Conclusion
This LDRD Lab-Wide project established a foundation for AFM-based characterization of
nucleosome assemblies and highlighted both the potential and the technical challenges of
applying scanning probe microscopy to chromatin systems. Future work may build on these
efforts by integrating quantitative analysis, complementary structural techniques, and
expanded biological models. The workflows and insights developed here provide a pathway for
continued research, potential external collaborations, and follow-on funding opportunities in
chromatin and genome organization studies.
