 1. INTRODUCTION
The Inner Reflector Plug (IRP) of the Spallation Neutron Source (SNS) was replaced during the SNS
shutdown period beginning 25 November 2025. This IRP, IRP-2, was first placed in service in May 2018,
and was in use for 42.5 GW-hrs of operation, somewhat longer than its original design life of 30 GW-hrs,
or even a refined estimate of 33 GW-hrs for the water moderator poison plate and 39 GW-hrs on the
decoupled-poisoned hydrogen moderator decoupler (Gallmeier, Lu, and Iverson 2018). The time-integrated
power history for IRP-2 is tracked in Figure 1. The IRP is replaced due to the burn-up of neutron

 Figure 1. Accumulated power history for IRP-2.

poisons and decouplers used to tailor neutron time and energy distributions to match the needs of the
neutron scattering instruments. The nature of this burn-up implies a significant change in performance
over the lifetime of the IRP as represented by those time and energy distributions, especially toward
the end of its service life. This report documents measurements of spectral intensity on the SNS direct
geometry spectrometers (Stone et al. 2014)—ARCS, (Abernathy et al. 2012) SEQUOIA, (Granroth
et al. 2010; Granroth, Vandergriff, and Nagler 2006) HYSPEC, (Shapiro et al. 2006) and CNCS (Ehlers
et al. 2011)—near the IRP-2 End-of-Life (EOL).

 2. CHARACTERIZATION EQUIPMENT
2.1 NOWB
The measurements described here were carried out using a portable “nED-on-Wheels” cart, locally called
a NOW cart, specifically NOWB. This data acquision system is built around a standard SNS Digital Signal
Processing (DSP) module and an Optical Distribution Board (ODB). Event-mode TTL timing signals can
be fed into input pins on the ODB and will be digitized to appear as tracked “beam monitor” events in
the resulting SNS-formatted NeXuS datafiles. The NOWB cart can additionally control and scan through
instrument control Process Variables (PVs) on the local SNS instrument.

2.2 MONITOR LND 2238
At SNS, we have used an LND beam monitor, serial number LND-2238, throughout the life of the facility
for beam characterization purposes. This monitor was measured to have an absolute efficiency of 1.35E-4
counts per neutron at 1.8 Å (25 meV) (Iverson et al. 2006) when operated at 1000 V with lower level
discrimination at 1.38 V, using an ORTEC 142PC preamplifier and an ORTEC 590 SCA amplifier with
coarse gain 100 and fine gain at two marks less than 2.5. All of the electronics supporting the LND-2238
detector are assembled in a mini-NIM bin, and the discrimitated TTL output is fed into the NOWB ODB
as described above.

2.3 MONITOR ORDELA 4511N 69
An unused beam monitor style detector, ORDELA model 4511N serial number 69, was used to characterize
some of the neutron beams in question. It is a physcially small form factor, and is thus able to fit into
a variety of sample environments as required. Additionally, it was thought to be of quite low absolute
efficiency, permitting characterization of beams that would otherwise pose significant instantaneous
countrate problems. Unfortunately, there was no documentation of the efficiency available prior to this
round of measurements. However, measurements on ARCS and SEQUOIA, described below, permit cross
calibration. This monitor is a so-called “SNS style beam monitor,” which uses an in-house developed beam
monitor controller to provide high voltage and lower-level discrimination and send an event to an SNS
data acquisition system. As will be described below, this monitor was found to have an absolute efficiency
of (1.5 ± 0.4)E-7 counts per neutron at 25 meV by comparison to the ARCS BM1 beam monitoer, as
described in in Section 3.1, and an absolute efficiency of (2.2 ± 0.2)E-7 by comparison to the SEQUIOA
BM1 beam monitor, as described in in Section 3.2, for a weighted average of (2.06 ± 0.18)E-7, and a
dead-time constant of 1.45 µs when operated at 1000 V with a lower level discriminator setting of 520 mV.

 3. INSTRUMENTS
3.1 ARCS
ARCS is a wide angular-range, direct-geometry, time-of-flight chopper spectrometer (Abernathy
et al. 2012) at the SNS. We measured incident spectra made mono-energetic by the action of a Fermi
chopper using a physically small, low-efficiency beam monitor-style detector (Ordela Model 4511N serial
number 69), operated in counting mode, mounted in the sample position at 13.6 m flight path, feeding
pulses to an SNS data acquisition system (the NOWB cart), which controlled its own time-of-flight
counting, the ARCS data acquisition system counting, and the ARCS chopper control system to cycle
through a set of incident energies. A physically small monitor was necessary as the air thimble in which it
was mounted is 100 mm diameter. Simultaneous measurements were made with the permanently installed
ARCS beam monitor, an ORDELA Model 4770N, believed to be serial number 2. A third monitor, also an
ORDELA 4770N, believed to be serial number 3, is also in place for these measurements as a downstream
monitor. The ARCS upstream monitor and the monitor in the sample position see “the same beam;”
there is no separate collimation or beam modification in between them, and both are large enough to
fully intercept the beam, ensuring that wavelength-based divergence differences are not relevant, and
enabling cross-calibration (the ARCS downstream detector does not intercept the full beam divergence,
so cannot be similarly treated). Measurements were performed at incident energies Ei of 20, 50, 100, 250,
and 500 meV as part of IPTS 36116. Further details appear in Table 1. As noted in Table 1, our portable

3.4 CNCS
The Cold Neutron Chopper Spectrometer (CNCS) is a direct-geometry cold-neutron time-of-flight chopper

 4. SUMMARY
The measurements detailed above will serve as a snapshot of performance for these instruments as of
EOL for IRP-2. We anticipate repeating these measurements in 2026 at the Beginning-of-Life for IRP-3.
Additionally, we note the following observations, which require follow-up:
 1. Figure 4 shows several fundamentally different features calling for explanation and further study.
 There are background “clouds” at specific time-of-flight values that do not change between detector
 position—we believe these to be related to fast neutrons or gammas coming through the T0
 chopper. There are additional clouds that do not appear in the same TOF for different detectors,
 nor even at all for some of the detectors. These two different types of clouds are seen on both
 ARCS and SEQUOIA, and in measurements not reported here were more strongly suppressed with
 discrimination than were the desired thermal neutrons. We wish to more fully explore these features,
 especially with regards to optimal discrimination setting for beam monitor detectors.
 2. All energies on ARCS and SEQUOIA should be measured for longer to support better statistics.
 3. The discrepancy between HYSPEC’s reported sample position and the flight path necessary to
 convert the observed time-of-flight to the known energy should be investigated and resolved.
 4. ARCS and SEQUOIA show asymmetric peak bases, and in some cases satellite peaks, in the
 installed instrument beam monitors. These should be better understood.
 5. HYSPEC shows chopper leakage at a level somewhat higher than anticipated. One side is routinely
 trimmed by a careful bandwidth chopper setting. This should be understood, documented, and
 corrected if possible.
 6. The CNCS measurements indicated significant background in the temporary detector, which could
 add 20–25% to the apparent monoenergetic peak counts. We believe this likely to come from
 scattering off of the 14 T magnet assembly in place during these measurements, just downstream
 of the detectors. While this is not necessarily impacting instrument performance, it should be
 understood.
 7. At current SNS performance levels, the upstream beam monitors installed on ARCS and SEQUOIA
 are experiencing some level of saturation. This should be characterized, so that appropriate data
 correction and/or equipment replacement can happen.
 8. The ORDELA 4511N 69 monitor used for portable characterization is estimated to have an
 efficiency 2.5% that of the currently installed SEQUOIA BM1, thought to be ORDELA 4770N 2,
 which was measured to have efficiency 8.69E-6 at 25.3 meV. ORDELA 4511N 69 would then have
 an efficiency of(2.2±0.2)E-7 counts per neutron at 25.3 meV. However, this number is uncertain
 because of uncertainty in the identify of the SEQUOIA BM1 monitor. Additionally, similar
 measurements taken on ARCS provide a slightly different value of (1.5±0.3)E-7 counts per neutron
      at 25.3 meV.
 9. There is some ambiguity about which beam monitors are installed on SEQUOIA. This should be
 resolved.
