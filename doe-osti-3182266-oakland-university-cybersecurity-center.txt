Abstract

This report summarizes the outcomes of Award DE-CR0000023, “Oakland University
Cybersecurity Center,” a 31-month project funded by the U.S. Department of Energy Office of
Cybersecurity, Energy Security, and Emergency Response (CESER). The project addressed
cybersecurity risks facing small and medium-sized manufacturers (SMMs) transitioning to
Industry 4.0.

The project integrated customer discovery, applied research, and cybersecurity training
development. A total of 51 cybersecurity assessments identified significant gaps in baseline
practices, incident response, and workforce capability. Research efforts produced a scalable
mitigation framework tailored to SMM environments, and workforce analysis identified persistent
talent gaps.

Eight cybersecurity training modules were developed and deployed via Oakland University’s
Professional and Continuing Education (PACE) platform. All objectives were completed, with
98.93% federal budget utilization and cost share exceeding requirements.

The project establishes a scalable model for strengthening cybersecurity resilience and
workforce capacity across U.S. manufacturing supply chains.

1. Executive Summary
Award DE-CR0000023, the "Oakland University Cybersecurity Center," was a 31-month effort
funded by the U.S. Department of Energy Office of Cybersecurity, Energy Security, and
Emergency Response (CESER) and led by Automation Alley in partnership with Oakland
University. Subcontracted research and assessment work was performed by Fraunhofer USA,
SMFS, Inc. dba GRIMM Cyber, and SensCy. The project ran from June 1, 2023 through
December 31, 2025.
The project was designed to address the cybersecurity threats facing small and midsize
enterprises (SMEs), including small to midsize manufacturers, as they transition to Industry 4.0.
SMEs operating in or adjacent to the energy sector face an expanding attack surface as they
integrate Information Technology (IT) with Operational Technology (OT), adopt connected
cyber-physical systems, and bring IoT and cloud platforms into legacy production environments.
They typically lack dedicated cybersecurity staff, formal incident response programs, and
access to manufacturer-relevant workforce training. The Oakland University Cybersecurity
Center was established to close those gaps through three integrated lines of effort: customer
discovery and primary research with Michigan manufacturers; development of a manufacturer-
focused cybersecurity training curriculum; and delivery of that curriculum at scale through a
sustainable online platform.
Over the period of performance, the project completed all SOPO scope. SensCy delivered
cybersecurity assessments to Michigan manufacturers and conducted intensive year-long
support engagements with three companies, generating measurable improvement in
cyberhealth scores. Fraunhofer USA produced research on protecting the energy systems and
industrial controls used by manufacturers, including incident response practices and a tested
cybersecurity strategy framework, culminating in a published white paper for SME owners and
policymakers. GRIMM Cyber delivered an industry white paper on cybersecurity talent gaps
based on dozens of interviews with experts in automotive, ICS/OT, and critical-infrastructure
cybersecurity. Automation Alley convened four Integr8 Roundtables, each producing a publicly
disseminated playbook, and integrated the project into its Integr8 Summit. Oakland University
developed, certified, and launched eight cybersecurity training modules on its Professional and
Continuing Education (PACE) platform, supported by a joint marketing campaign reaching more
than 4,000 manufacturing companies. The first learner cohort enrolled in December 2025.
All project metrics were met (8 of 8 modules; 4 of 4 Integr8 Roundtables; 2 of 2 white papers).
Federal expenditures totaled $1,978,577 against an approved federal budget of $2,000,000
(98.93% utilization). Cost share contributions totaled $525,298 against a committed amount of
$500,000, exceeding the cost share requirement by $25,298. Automation Alley and Oakland
University have committed to continuing Center operations beyond the federal period of
performance.

2. Introduction and Background
The cybersecurity threat to American manufacturing has shifted from an IT problem to an
existential business problem, and small and midsize manufacturers are highly exposed. Chubb
Group reported in 2023 that 62% of all cyberattacks now target small and midsize businesses,
even though most lack dedicated security teams or incident response plans [1]. INCYBER News
estimates that the move to Industry 4.0, which connects older operational technology to modern
IT, IoT, and cloud platforms, expands a manufacturer's attack surface by approximately four
times [2]. A 2025 Infosecurity Magazine analysis found that 7 in 10 SMEs still operate without a

formal cybersecurity framework, leaving them exposed to phishing, ransomware, and supply-
chain compromise [3]. The May 2021 Colonial Pipeline ransomware attack illustrated the
stakes. A single compromised VPN password halted the largest fuel pipeline in the United
States for six days, triggered a $4.4 million ransom payment, caused fuel shortages at more
than 10,000 gas stations, and demonstrated how a single breach in this segment can ripple
through grid operations, fuel supply, automotive production, and public safety [4].
The exposure problem is matched by a capability problem. SMEs face structural barriers to
building cybersecurity capability, including tight budgets, lean IT teams, aging infrastructure, and
limited access to skilled cybersecurity professionals. The customer-discovery work performed
under this Award quantified those barriers in the Michigan manufacturing population. SensCy
assessed Michigan manufacturers across the period of performance using its proprietary
cyberhealth score, finding that the average company scored 577 on a scale where 800
represents a sound cybersecurity posture, that 90% of assessed companies were not following
baseline cybersecurity practices, and that only 35% had a formal incident response plan in
place. GRIMM Cyber's parallel workforce study, drawing on dozens of one-on-one interviews
with experts in automotive, ICS/OT, and critical-infrastructure cybersecurity, identified a
persistent talent shortage compounded by three factors: limited training and education programs
in cyber-physical systems, inconsistent hiring prerequisites across employers, and a rapid pace
of technological change that outstrips the existing curricula. GRIMM further found that industry
consistently demands hands-on practical experience, OT-specific knowledge, and soft skills
(communication, problem-solving, collaboration), all of which are in short supply in the existing
cybersecurity workforce.
The Oakland University Cybersecurity Center was created to close these gaps through an
industry-aligned model. Automation Alley, Michigan's Digital Transformation Insight Center,
partnered with Oakland University as the academic curriculum lead. The team was
supplemented by three cybersecurity vendors performing distinct customer-discovery roles
alongside Automation Alley's Integr8 Roundtables: SensCy on primary manufacturer
assessments, Fraunhofer USA on applied research and framework development, and GRIMM
Cyber on workforce talent analysis. The project grounded its curriculum in real customer-
discovery findings and delivered training through Oakland University's Professional and
Continuing Education (PACE) platform.

3. Project Objectives
The project objectives were defined in the Statement of Project Objectives (SOPO). As stated in
the SOPO:
"Automation Alley (AA) will create a Cybersecurity Center to address the cybersecurity threats
for manufacturers transitioning to Industry 4.0. This Cybersecurity Center will help small and
midsize manufacturers transition to Industry 4.0 (I4.0) to improve their productivity, growth and
employment. This center will focus on these companies to break the barriers preventing them
from making this transition as well as increasing the supply chain for talent. Large
manufacturers in the energy sector face the unprecedented growing cybersecurity challenges to
protect the nation from disinformation threats, and secure digital assets including the transition
to industry 4.0."
The SOPO directed the project to support manufacturers in three areas:
 1. Adopt a risk-based security mindset that ties business criticality to defense strategies.

5. Approach
5.1 Task 1.0: Project Management
Automation Alley led overall project management. Within 30 days of award, Automation Alley
submitted a Project Management Plan (PMP) and Data Management Plan (DMP), both of which
were approved by DOE on June 29, 2023, satisfying the project's Go/No-Go decision point and
authorizing initiation of subsequent tasks. Project management activities throughout the period
of performance included regular meetings with Oakland University and the subcontractor team,
quarterly Research Performance Progress Reports (RPPRs) and Federal Financial Reports
(SF-425) submitted via EERE PMC, ongoing risk and budget review, and coordination of all
closeout activities. A No-Cost Time Extension (NCTE) was approved in July 2024 to
accommodate Task 3 schedule pressure stemming from Oakland University staffing transitions.

5.2 Task 2.0: Customer Discovery

Customer discovery combined primary research (direct engagement with Michigan
manufacturers) with secondary research (literature synthesis and applied framework
development). The work was organized across three subcontractor streams plus the four
Integr8 Roundtables led by Automation Alley. Findings from all four streams were synthesized
continuously by Automation Alley and shared with Oakland University to inform curriculum

5.2.1 SensCy: Cybersecurity Assessments and Intensive Support
SensCy conducted cybersecurity assessments using its proprietary SensCy Score, a numeric
measure of organizational cyberhealth derived from the NIST Cybersecurity Framework's six
pillars: Identify, Detect, Protect, Respond, Recover, Govern. Scores range from 0 to 1,000+,
with 800+ representing a strong cybersecurity posture. SensCy executed 51 assessments with
Michigan manufacturers over the period of performance and conducted intensive, contracted
year-long engagements with three small manufacturers to validate remediation approaches and
track score progression.

5.2.2 Fraunhofer USA: Applied Research and Mitigation Framework
Fraunhofer USA executed a seven-task research scope addressing primary and secondary
cybersecurity research for SME energy companies transitioning to Industry 4.0.
The first phase (Tasks 1 and 2) analyzed current cybersecurity methods and threats.
Fraunhofer's security researchers identified assets in SME OT/IT infrastructure, assessed
security posture, and quantified risks using the open-source FAIR methodology. The team
analyzed established security risk mitigation strategies, including NSA security risk mitigation
strategies, ISO 31000, IEC 62443-2-1, the NIST Risk Management Framework, NIST SP 800-
82, and SANS Top 20 Controls. Threat models were developed for production, transmission,
distribution, operation, and network areas.
The second phase (Tasks 3 and 4) focused on energy delivery systems and incident response.
Fraunhofer interviewed partnered companies, created a model IT-OT infrastructure of energy
delivery systems, and evaluated the effectiveness of current security measures. The team also
researched existing incident response policies and processes, including NSA security risk
mitigation, ISO 31000, IEC 62443-2-1, the NIST Risk Management Framework, NIST SP 800-
82, SANS Top 20 Controls, and MITRE ATT&CK for ICS, analyzing whether existing policies
adequately addressed access controls, network security, software security, incident response,
security assessments, and audits.
The third phase (Tasks 5, 6, and 7) developed and validated the mitigation framework.
Fraunhofer developed mitigation strategies and frameworks for operational application
development security, operational incident response handling, business continuity management,
risk management, vulnerability and patch management, change management, and other
product-specific requirements. The team also identified automated security monitoring and
incident response solutions leveraging AI and machine learning to align with Industry 4.0
adoption. The completed framework was validated through two SME case study scenarios.

5.2.3 GRIMM Cyber: Cybersecurity Workforce Talent Gap Analysis
GRIMM Cyber conducted dozens of one-on-one interviews with experts in automotive
cybersecurity, Industrial Control Systems (ICS) and Operational Technology (OT) cybersecurity,
and critical-infrastructure cybersecurity. The analysis combined practitioner survey results, a
SWOT skills assessment, and detailed industry feedback on each of Oakland University's eight
proposed training courses and produced an industry white paper synthesizing findings and

Oakland University led curriculum development. Training modules were designed, hands-on lab

campus for the Cybersecurity Center. All modules were certified and integrated into Oakland
University's Professional and Continuing Education (PACE) platform. Each module includes
guided lab sessions and access to the lab infrastructure via VPN, allowing learners to complete
lab assignments asynchronously.

partner presentations from GRIMM, Fraunhofer, and SensCy, and hands-on technical
demonstrations of a deepfake detector, a digital twin of a robot cell, and digital twin–integrated
industrial controllers developed under the curriculum.

6. Results and Major Accomplishments
6.1 Customer Discovery: SensCy Assessment Results
Across 51 cybersecurity assessments conducted by SensCy with Michigan manufacturers:
 • The average initial SensCy Score was 577, well below the 800-point threshold
 considered indicative of a sound cybersecurity posture.
 • 90% of assessed companies were not following baseline cybersecurity practices, leaving
 them exposed to phishing and ransomware.
 • Only 10% of assessed companies scored at or above the 800-point threshold.
 • Among companies that adopted recommended cybersecurity best practices, scores
 improved by an average of 81% within the first 12 months.
 • Foundational gaps identified included: only 35% had a formal cybersecurity incident
 response plan, only 31% updated their response plans annually, and significant
 deficiencies were found in patch management and penetration testing.

 • Positive trends among assessed manufacturers included: 82% restricting user rights
 through Principle of Least Privilege, 98% limiting administrative rights to designated

 their Wi-Fi.

6.2 Customer Discovery: Fraunhofer USA Findings
Fraunhofer's research interviews with SME IT professionals identified a consistent pattern of
foundational gaps that persisted even where basic security controls were nominally in place:
 • Poor network and endpoint security was identified at 73% of companies surveyed, linked
 to risks of unauthorized access and malware infection.
 • Multi-factor authentication (MFA) was absent in 78% of cyber-physical system (CPS)
 deployments, materially increasing exposure to credential-based attacks.
 • Inadequate data encryption and backup security was found at 58% of companies,
 exposing sensitive data to theft and irrecoverable loss.
 • Many companies operated outdated legacy systems, lacked comprehensive incident
 response plans, and relied on third-party vendors without sufficient oversight.
Fraunhofer's recommended mitigation strategies, embedded into the published framework,
included:
 • Robust password policies and enforced MFA
 • Regular vulnerability scans
 • Enhanced incident response plans with regular testing
 • Comprehensive data encryption and backup
 • Secure development practices
 • Agile Patch Management
 • IT/OT-specific incident response playbooks
 • Ongoing cybersecurity awareness training for employees and management
The framework was designed to be flexible and scalable for SMEs and was tested with two
SME case study scenarios.

6.3 Customer Discovery: GRIMM Cyber Findings
GRIMM Cyber's interviews with subject-matter experts from automotive product security teams,
ICS/OT professionals, and critical-infrastructure providers produced three consistent findings:
 • Industry stakeholders strongly emphasized the importance of hands-on lab work using
 real-world tools, software, and hardware as the most effective preparation for
 practitioners.

 • Industry leaders expressed strong interest in participating as guest speakers and
 curriculum contributors, indicating an opportunity for closer academia-industry
 collaboration.
 • Soft skills, including leadership, critical thinking, communication, and problem-solving,
 were repeatedly identified as essential and consistently in short supply alongside
 technical expertise.
GRIMM's curriculum-specific recommendations, used by Oakland University to refine the
training modules during development, included:
 • Accredited degree programs blending mechanical and electrical engineering with
 computer science
 • Inclusion of real-world business scenarios in lab activities
 • Mandatory final project presentations
 • Industry guest speakers
 • Table-top incident response exercises
 • Current standards and regulations content
GRIMM's full white paper additionally provided a SWOT analysis of the cyber-physical systems
security workforce, a detailed inventory of desired knowledge and skills (including Threat
Analysis and Risk Assessment, regulatory compliance, and incident response planning), and
module-by-module feedback informing Oakland University's curriculum design.

12. Conclusions, Lessons Learned, and Sustainability
12.1 Conclusions
The Oakland University Cybersecurity Center project successfully achieved its objectives over
the 31-month period of performance (June 1, 2023 – December 31, 2025). All major project
tasks were completed: project management infrastructure was established, customer discovery
research was conducted across 51 Michigan manufacturers, eight cybersecurity training
modules were developed and certified, and the modules were launched on Oakland University's
PACE platform with hybrid delivery options. The project established a sustainable foundation for
continued cybersecurity workforce development in Michigan's manufacturing sector and
produced research deliverables that contribute to the broader understanding of cybersecurity
readiness, workforce gaps, and mitigation strategies for small and medium-sized manufacturers.

12.2 Sustainability Plan
Automation Alley and Oakland University have established a sustainability plan to continue
operation of the Cybersecurity Center beyond the period of performance:
 • Oakland University will continue to offer cybersecurity training modules through the
 PACE platform, with ongoing enrollment open to Michigan manufacturers and the
 broader workforce community.
 • Automation Alley will maintain the cybersecurity landing page at
 https://automationalley.com/cyber-security-center/ and continue promotional activities
 through the weekly newsletter and manufacturing ecosystem partnerships.
 • Both organizations are exploring partnerships with Michigan community colleges and
 workforce development agencies to expand access to the training programs.
 • Automation Alley will leverage relationships established through this project to support
 cybersecurity awareness among small and medium-sized manufacturers in Michigan.
