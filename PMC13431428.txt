Interpreting Perineural Esketamine for Analgesia After Total Knee Arthroplasty: Methodological and Reporting Considerations [Letter]
Authors: Zijin Zhou, Yanxin Li, Jun Lu
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We read with interest the randomized, double-blind trial by Cheng et al comparing ultrasound-guided femoral nerve block with ropivacaine alone versus ropivacaine plus perineural esketamine in 60 patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty.1 The authors reported a lower visual analogue scale (VAS) score at 10 hours and reduced 24-hour sufentanil consumption with esketamine, concluding that the regimen could serve as a safe analgesic option. The opioid-consumption finding appears statistically robust, but several internal reporting and analytical issues limit the strength of the claims regarding improved pain control, safety, and a specifically perineural mechanism.

Inconsistent Pain-Assessment Timing and Unadjusted Repeated Testing

The VAS schedule is inconsistent across the article. The abstract lists assessments at 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 hours; the Outcome Measurements section lists 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 hours; and Table 2 reports 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 hours. Thus, the 24-hour assessment specified in the main Methods is absent, whereas a 10-hour assessment not specified there is included. This discrepancy is consequential because 10 hours is the only time point showing a between-group difference (P=0.026). Clarification against the time-stamped trial registry and protocol is needed to establish the prespecified schedule and to recover the omitted 24-hour result, if collected. The six VAS time points were analyzed using separate Mann–Whitney tests without a declared primary VAS time point, a global longitudinal test, or an adjustment for repeated testing. An illustrative Bonferroni adjustment across the six comparisons would change P=0.026 to P=0.156. Bonferroni correction is not the only possible approach, particularly because repeated measurements are correlated, but the present series of isolated tests does not estimate an overall treatment trajectory or a group-by-time interaction.2,3 A mixed-effects model, repeated-measures model, or prespecified area-under-the-curve analysis, followed by multiplicity-controlled visit-specific contrasts, would provide a more coherent assessment. Until such an analysis is reported, the isolated 10-hour result should be considered exploratory rather than evidence of consistently improved postoperative pain.

Incomplete Reporting and Statistical Reproducibility

The Methods define time to first PCA bolus as the duration of femoral nerve block analgesia and state that PCA bolus use was recorded over six postoperative intervals. Neither time to first request nor interval-specific PCA use is reported. Moreover, the Discussion states that sensory block duration was not significantly prolonged, but no sensory-duration data or corresponding analysis appear in the Results. These outcomes are important for interpreting the reduction in total sufentanil consumption and should be reported with denominators, effect estimates, and confidence intervals. The reported sufentanil values, 108.52±10.71 versus 96.30±12.85 micrograms, yield a mean difference of 12.22 micrograms. A conventional independent-samples calculation gives P approximately 0.00018 and an approximate 95% confidence interval of 6.1 to 18.3 micrograms, supporting a statistical difference. The concern is therefore not that the opioid finding disappears, but that its clinical meaning cannot be fully evaluated without first-request time, bolus counts, rescue analgesia, and a prespecified clinically important difference.

Some categorical results also require clarification. Table 3 reports postoperative nausea and vomiting in 7/30 versus 5/30 patients, with the latter percentage written as 16.3% rather than 16.67%. The reported P=0.467 is not reproduced by common two-sided analyses of this 2×2 table (Pearson chi-square P approximately 0.52; Fisher exact P approximately 0.75). The authors should identify the exact test and any continuity correction used, and verify the underlying counts and P value.

Safety, Blinding, and Mechanistic Interpretation

The Methods prespecify hypotension, hypertension, respiratory depression, nausea, vomiting, and urinary retention, yet Table 3 reports only postoperative nausea and vomiting. The statement that adverse-event rates were low therefore cannot be audited across the full prespecified safety set. With only 30 participants per group, absence of a severe event would also remain compatible with an underlying event probability of approximately 9.5% at the one-sided 95% level. Complete group-specific reporting is needed before describing the regimen as safe.

The designation “double-blind” also warrants clarification. The article explicitly states that the outcome assessor was blinded, but it does not clearly identify whether participants, the anesthesiologist performing the block, postoperative care personnel, and data analysts were blinded or how visually identical preparations were maintained. In addition, without an intravenous esketamine comparator or plasma concentration measurements, the trial cannot distinguish a local perineural effect from systemic absorption. Motor block, quadriceps strength, ambulation, and falls were not measured, although these outcomes are central to femoral nerve block within enhanced-recovery pathways. Consequently, the data support reduced 24-hour sufentanil consumption, but do not by themselves establish an improved overall pain trajectory, a local perineural mechanism, motor-sparing recovery, or a favorable safety profile.

We suggest that the authors reconcile the prespecified VAS schedule, report all recorded analgesic and adverse-event outcomes, clarify the blinding procedures, and provide a longitudinal pain analysis with treatment effects and confidence intervals. These additions would preserve the credible opioid-sparing signal while appropriately narrowing the clinical and mechanistic conclusions.
