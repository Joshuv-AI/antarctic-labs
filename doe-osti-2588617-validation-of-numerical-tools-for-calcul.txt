 ABSTRACT
 The Southwest Experimental Fast Oxide Reactor (SEFOR) was an experimental sodium-
cooled fast breeder reactor operated from 1969 to 1972 with experiments designed to measure
Doppler reactivity feedback in a wide temperature range from around 350 °F to temperatures
approaching the melting point of mixed oxide fuel of around 5000 °F, providing valuable data
for code validations. Co-supported by the Department of Energy (DOE) Fast Reactor Program
(FRP) and the DOE Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS) program,
the SEFOR benchmark project focused on using the experimental data to validate numerical
tools that are used in industry and academia to design and license sodium-cooled fast reactors
(SFRs). By the end of FY-25, substantial progress was achieved in the SEFOR benchmark
study. A variety of numerical tools commonly used for modeling SFRs were applied to develop
models for SEFOR core configurations I-D, I-E, I-I, and I-J. These included Monte Carlo codes
such as MCNP, Serpent, and Shift; deterministic codes such as the legacy Argonne Reactor
Computation (ARC) suite and the high-fidelity NEAMS code Griffin; and the system analysis
code SAS4A/SASSYS-1 (SAS). Using these models, both SEFOR zero-power experiments and
power-ascending tests were successfully simulated. Comparisons were performed against
experimental measurements of core criticalities, reflector worth, kinetics parameters (Λ/βeff),
isothermal reactivity feedback (from 350 °F to 760 °F at zero power), and power-ascending
reactivity feedback (as power increased from 0.4 MW to 17 MW). In general, these
comparisons demonstrated very good agreement between numerical results and experimental
data. In Fiscal Year 26 (FY-26), the SEFOR benchmark project will continue to address the
modeling issues identified in FY-25. Effort will focus on the simulation of reactivity insertion
transients in SEFOR core II using the ARC/SAS model. Future work will also focus on
incorporating BISON into the SEFOR core modeling process to enable the first multiphysics
simulations of the isothermal tests based on the MOOSE framework.

1 Introduction
 The United States Department of Energy’s Office of Nuclear Energy (DOE-NE) Fast
Reactor Program (FRP), under the Advanced Reactor Technologies (ART) portfolio, supports
development of key technical elements needed by industry for enabling future large-scale
commercialization of fast reactors in the United States. An important area supported by this
program is leveraging data from past legacy experimental facilities, such as EBR-II, FFTF and
TREAT, to validate computational tools and methods used by industry for the design and
licensing of advanced Sodium Fast Reactors (SFRs).
 The Southwest Experimental Fast Oxide Reactor (SEFOR) reactor was operated from 1969
to 1972. During this period, a three-phase experimental program was carried out to measure the
Doppler reactivity feedback under various reactor conditions: Phase I - reactor startup and zero-
power tests, Phase II - steady state power ascending tests, and Phase III - reactivity insertion
transient tests [1-5]. These experiments produced unique sets of measured Doppler reactivity
feedback in a wide temperature range from around 350 °F to temperatures approaching the
melting point of mixed oxide fuel of around 5000 °F, providing valuable data for code
validations.
 In Fiscal Year 2023 (FY-23), a new SEFOR benchmark effort was launched. The goal was
to validate reactivity feedback calculations for mixed oxide (MOX) fueled SFRs using reactor
physics codes in the Argonne Reactor Computation (ARC) code suite [6] and the legacy
SAS4A/SASSYS-1 (SAS) reactor system and safety analysis code [7]. Both codes are
developed at Argonne National Laboratory (ANL) and are widely used by industry stakeholders
for modeling SFRs.
 The benchmark activities described in this report were also conducted as part of the
OECD/NEA SEFOR benchmark, developed under the Working Party on Scientific Issues and
Uncertainty Analysis of Reactor Systems SFR expert group (WPRS SFR-UAM) [8]. The expert
group focused on applying best-estimate plus uncertainty (BEPU) methodologies to assess the
reliability of software and codes used for modeling Sodium Fast Reactors. In addition, ANL
and the French Alternative Energies and Atomic Energy Commission (CEA) have expanded
collaboration efforts to extend the SEFOR benchmark with additional cases to be published in
a future report.
 In FY-25, the U.S. DOE Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS)
program also supported the use of the SEFOR experimental data to validate Griffin, a high-
fidelity, multiphysics capable, reactor physics code, for SFRs applications. This report will also
cover the research activities performed under this program.
 This document is the first joint milestone report summarizing SEFOR-related research
conducted under both the FRP program and the NEAMS program. It covers reactor physics
modeling and simulation of the SEFOR zero-power tests and SEFOR power ascending tests.
As the modeling and simulation continue through FY-2026, a second joint milestone report is
planned to document verification results across all three experimental phases using software
widely used in industry and supported by FRP, along with advancements in applying the
NEAMS high-fidelity Multiphysics tools to modeling SEFOR zero-power tests.

2 SEFOR Core Specifications and Experiments for Measuring Reactivity
  Feedback
 2.1 SEFOR Reactor Core Description
 The Southwest Experimental Fast Oxide Reactor (SEFOR) was an experimental sodium-
cooled fast breeder reactor constructed in Cove Creek Township, Washington County,
northwest Arkansas. It operated from 1969 to 1972, during which the US General Electric
Company (GE) and the West Germany Karlsruhe Laboratory (KFK) jointly conducted
systematic experiments.
 SEFOR was a fast spectrum, 20 MWt, sodium-cooled reactor fueled with mixed PuO2-UO2.
It was designed as a pool-type reactor, with primary coolant entering the vessel through a single
top inlet nozzle (Figure 2-1). From there, the coolant flowed radially across the top, then down
through the downcomer, which was an annular region between the vessel wall and the shroud
wall, before turning upward at the vessel bottom. It then entered the 109 hexagonal channels
through orifices, cooled the fuel assemblies, and exited the vessel through a single outlet at the
top. SEFOR was also equipped with an auxiliary cooling system operated in parallel, with its
own inlet and outlet connections as shown in the figure. Reactor cooling was provided by forced
circulation of liquid sodium in the primary loop and heat was transferred through an
intermediate heat exchanger to the secondary loop which was located outside the reactor
building [5, 9]. During steady state operations at ~ 20 MW, the primary sodium temperature
was about 700 °F at the inlet and 820 °F at the outlet.
 The SEFOR core was housed in a concrete-shielded nitrogen-atmosphere within the reactor
building. Core criticality was controlled by ten movable nickel reflectors positioned outside the
reactor vessel, moving vertically as shown in Figure 2-2. For shutdown, all reflectors were
pushed down below the reactor active fuel region, providing about 10$ shutdown worth. During
normal operations, two reflector positions were adjusted to fine-tune core criticality while the
others were either fully down below the active core or fully up to cover the fuel region.
 Radiation shielding was achieved through both the reflectors and different shielding layers
inside the reactor building. Radially, all shielding layers were positioned outside the reactor
vessel and cooled by nitrogen gas. Axially, shielding above the core was provided by a neutron
shield layer within the vessel and the vessel head, while the lower shield consisted of a thermal
shielding layer in the vessel and a shielding plug beneath the vessel [5].

 Core Geometry
 SEFOR could accommodate up to 109 hexagonal fuel assemblies. Figure 2-3 a) shows the
cross section of a standard fuel channel, which contained six fuel rods arranged around a central
structure rod and reinforced with six steel side rods. The rods contained ~18.7% fissile
plutonium (Pu-239 and Pu-241) and were held in position by a hex sleeve surrounding the
central rod. In addition to the Standard fuel Assemblies (SA), several variants were used in
constructing critical core configurations, such as the one illustrated in Figure 2-2. These variants
included substitutions for standard fuel rods:
 1) Guinea Pig (GP) rods with higher fissile Pu concentration of 25.0%,
   2) B4C rods,
 3) instrumented fuel rods with thermocouples embedded in the standard fuel rod,
 4) steel rods, and,
 5) void fuel position (missing fuel rods).
 A “Drywell” channel, which was mostly empty, was also placed at the core center as (central
channel labeled in Figure 2-2) to house experimental devices such as the Fast Reactivity
Excursion Device (FRED) for transient tests.

SEFOR. Each rod incorporated nickel reflectors at both ends, depleted UO2 insulation layers
separating the MOX fuel into segments. A top spring to hold all the different components

measuring reactivity feedback from the fuel Doppler effect. Instrumented fuel rods have two
springs to divide the fuel.

 The available literature did not provide sufficient information to develop a fully detailed 3-
D full-core model of SEFOR. Within the WPRS UAM-SFR framework, the SEFOR benchmark
was defined based on a reference RZ model originally developed in 1970s [1, 11]. The geometry
of this RZ model shown in Figure 2-4, was constructed such that the radial dimensions
preserved the volumes of the different core regions, while the axial dimensions were selected
to form distinct homogenized zones.
 In the WPRS benchmark specification, the RZ model was preserved outside the active core,
with homogenized materials in those regions adopted directly from the legacy RZ model.
Within the active core, however, the detailed geometry of individual fuel channels was
explicitly represented, taking advantage of modern reactor physics codes that are capable of
handling complex geometries, and delivering more accurate physics descriptions. The
recommended radial dimensions from the WPRS SEFOR benchmark, along with selected
engineering dimensions reported in the literature, are summarized in Table 2-1, while the
corresponding axial dimensions for different rod types are tabulated in Table 2-2.

 The dimensions specified in the WPRS benchmark correspond to components at room
temperature (294 K). In contrast, the RZ core dimensions shown in Figure 2-4 assume the core
is at 350 °F. In this benchmark study, and in accordance with the WPRS benchmark
specifications, it is assumed that regions outside the active core region remain unchanged and
are not influenced by the conditions in the active core. Therefore, their dimensions are held
constant under all modeled conditions. The dimensions in the active core need to be adjusted
according to the core temperatures as modeling the SEFOR active core region thermal
expansion is critical when comparing the calculated reactivity feedback effects with SEFOR
experimental data. Details will be discussed in Section 4 of this report.

 Critical Core Configurations
 Experiments were conducted in two SEFOR core configurations. The first configuration
(SEFOR core I) used BeO tightener rods at the center of each fuel assembly, while in the second
core configuration (core II), the BeO rods were replaced with stainless steel rods, resulting in a
harder neutron spectrum compared to core I. For core I, zero-power experiments were carried
out in seven different core loadings, designated Core I-A through I-I (no Core I-G), with their
core maps shown in Figure 2-6. Subsequent power-ascending tests were performed in Core I-
J, with the corresponding core map also provided in the same figure.
 The first critical core loading in SEFOR was Core I-A, which had an excess reactivity of 9
cents with all reflectors raised and sodium at 350 F. Core I-B was derived from Core I-A by
adding fuel rods to calibrate reflector worth. However, reactivity worth measurements revealed
that the reactivity worth of several fuel rods (used in both I-A and I-B) was ~40% lower than
expected, which later attributed to a plutonium deficit in those rods. Core I-C was subsequently
constructed after replacing the deficient rods with rods containing the correct Pu concentration,
yielding an excess reactivity of 1.01$ under the same reflector and sodium conditions for Core
I-C.
 Core I-D was the first critical configuration with all 108 fuel channels filled, with an
estimated excess reactivity of 1.8$. In this configuration, zero-power experiments including
noise measurements for kinetic parameter l/β as well as measurements of the material worth
and fission rate distributions were all successfully performed.
 Core I-E introduced the GP rods and first complete set of isothermal tests were performed
to measure reactivity feedback from fuel Doppler. Its excess reactivity was estimated to be 3.4$
with all reflectors up in the core and sodium temperature at 350 °F. In this core configuration,
the reflector worth was also calibrated at two temperatures.
 Core I-F was characterized by a symmetric loading of the GP rods and B4C rods, while Core
I-I was the first configuration containing Instrumented Fuel Assembly (IFA), enabling direct
measurements of fuel centerline temperatures. Isothermal tests were repeated in Core I-I and
the reflector worth were also calibrated with this configuration. Core I-J was formed by

 12

replacing a neutron absorber rod in Core I-I with a stainless-steel rod. Power ascending tests
were performed in this configuration, with additional reactivity provided by replacing the
neutron absorber with the stainless steel, compensating for the negative reactivity feedback
encountered during power increase [1].
 This milestone report focuses on modeling and simulating zero-power experiments and
power ascending tests conducted in SEFOR Core I-E, Core I-I and Core I-J, with modeling
work for SEFOR transient tests in Core II addressed in future studies.

 2.2 SEFOR Zero-Power Experiments
 In SEFOR, critical core loadings were formed by loading fuel assemblies in an
approximately cylindrical pattern. Critical loadings were achieved with support from both
numerical simulations using simple numerical models and ZPR-3 mockups. A series of zero-
power experiments were conducted in these critical core configurations, including reflectors
worth calibration, measurements of the material reactivity worth, foil activation for mapping
fission rate distributions, noise method to determine kinetic parameters, and measurements on
reactivity coefficients (flow, pressure, temperature) at zero fission power. This report focuses
specifically on reflector worth calibration and the determination of temperature reactivity
coefficients, in particular isothermal tests designed to measure reactivity feedback as the core
temperature was uniformly increased.
 During SEFOR experiments, neutron fluxes were monitored by two He-3 detectors located
in the central channel and two BF3 neutron detectors located below the reactor core. The
counting rates from the in-core He-3 detectors were corrected for detector dead time (using the
paralyzable model) at low count rates. The detector was switched to current mode at higher
count rates. No dead time corrections were applied to the BF3 detector signals, as their counting
rates were low in zero-power experiments.

 Isothermal Tests
 Isothermal tests were conducted in both Core I-E and I-I to determine the temperature
coefficients of reactivity in SEFOR. During these tests, the primary coolant was gradually
heated from 350 °F to 760 °F using a primary loop trace heating system. In the I-E experiments,
after reaching 760 °F, sodium was cooled to about 700 °F and was then reheated back to 760
°F. Coolant inlet and outlet temperatures were monitored by resistance temperature detectors
(RTDs), while the in-vessel coolant temperature was measured by thermocouples (T/Cs). The
uncertainties assigned to the RTD measurements were ± 2.5 °F, and the uncertainties for the
T/Cs were much smaller and were about ± 0.5 °F [13].
 The heating rate was about 20 °F per hour. Figure 2-9 shows the RTDs and T/Cs recordings
from the first 22 hours of the isothermal tests, demonstrating good convergence of temperatures
within the core. Notably, T/C readings were consistently about 15 °F lower than those from
RTDs. The differences were likely due to the different calibration methods in T/C and RTDs.
 In the isothermal tests, the radial reflector positions were adjusted to compensate for the
negative reactivity feedback developed during the test. Reactor power was kept small at about
600 W. Coolant temperatures and reflector positions were recorded every 15 minutes. The
experimental data for Core I-E and I-I are listed in Table 2-17, Table 2-18, and Table 2-19 in
which the temperature readings were taken by the in-vessel T/C.
 Ref. [1] and [13] detailed the reflector positions in the isothermal tests. For tests performed
in Core I-E, reflectors #5 and #10 were initially fully withdrawn during the first 34
measurements, while reflector #8 was used to compensate for the reactivity loss as core
temperature increased. All other reflectors were fully raised. Reflector #5 remained down until
test #116, after which it was positioned at 50 cm inside the core. A total of 283 tests were
conducted, during which coolant temperature increased steadily from 350 °F to 759.75 °F at
test #177. The coolant was then cooled down and reheated to 759.75 °F during the remaining
tests in the rest of the tests.
 For Core I-I, 40 tests were carried out. Reflectors #5 and #10 were initially down while
reflector #6 was positioned at 31 cm inside the core during the first 9 measurements. Starting
at test #10, reflector #6 was raised, followed by reflector #10 after test #21, and Reflector #3
after test #26.
 Additional 38 experimental data points for the isothermal tests performed in Core I-E were
also reported in in Ref. [14] and were tabulated in Table 2-18. There were very similar results
from those reported in Ref. [1] as shown in Figure 2-10. In this benchmark, the data set with
283 measurement points were used for validating numerical simulations.

coolant in the reactor core. Typically, the reactivity feedback due to core thermal expansion

is used to quantify the reactivity feedback caused by Doppler broadening of nuclear cross

 Overall, the fitted constants were found to be very sensitive to the choice of experimental

to employ SEFOR experimental data to validate numerical tools for modeling reactivity

 2.3 Power Ascending Steady State Tests
 In the SEFOR experiments, static tests were conducted in Core I-J to determine reactivity
feedback effects associated with power increases [3]. The reactor power was raised stepwise
from 0.4 MW to 17.5 MW, with reflectors adjusted to offset the negative reactivity feedback at
higher power levels. During the tests, the average coolant temperature over the core was
maintained at approximately 760 °F, while the inlet coolant temperature varied between 400 °F
and 745 °F. Outlet coolant temperatures were varied correspondingly and were monitored
continuously.
 Fuel temperature increases above the sodium coolant temperature were measured using
thermal sensors installed in the two IFAs. Similarly, RTDs were used to monitor the coolant
inlet and outlet temperatures. TCs were monitoring the coolant temperature in the inner vessel.
The uncertainties of these measures were ± 2.5 °F for RTDs and ± 0.5 °F for TCs.
 Flow rates were measured in both the primary and auxiliary loops, and the reactor thermal
power and the average coolant temperature were determined from the measured flow and
temperature data. Figure 2-12 to Figure 2-14 present the measured flow rates, coolant
temperatures and fuel temperatures for power levels up to 17.5 MW, while the experimental
data were tabulated in Table 2-20 and Table 2-21.
 Reactivity feedback was inferred from the reflector position adjustments required to
maintain core criticality at each power step, as shown in Figure 2-15. In this figure, the curve
labeled “rho_Doppler” represents reactivity changes reported in Ref. [3] , corrected to account
for an estimated fuel axial expansion contribution of about -0.5 cents/MW, and the estimated
contribution from sodium density change. The curve “rho_all” shows the measured reactivity
change without these corrections. The reported uncertainty in reactivity feedback is about 2.0
cents, or 5% when greater than 2 cents. The uncertainty in the estimated power is about 0.3
MW or 5% when larger.

3 Numerical Tools for Modeling SEFOR Reactor
 3.1 ARC Reactor Physics Codes: MC2-3, DIF3D and PERSENT
 The Argonne Reactor Computation (ARC) code suite [6] is a series of codes that have been
widely used in academia and industries for modeling various types of fast reactor, including
Sodium Fast Reactors (SFRs), fast spectrum Molten Salt Reactors (MSRs), Lead-cooled Fast
Reactors (LFRs) as well as other Small-Modular Reactors (SMRs) and Micro-Reactors (MRs)
with a fast energy neutron spectrum. When coupled with other safety analysis codes such as
SAS4A/SASSYS-1 [7] developed at ANL(Figure 3-1), the ARC suite can simulate fast reactor
core behavior under both steady state operation and dynamic transient conditions.

 Figure 3-1 Flow diagram of ANL ARC ecosystem for modeling fast reactors.

 Within the ARC suite of codes, MC2-3 [16] is used for fast reactor cross-section generation,
DIF3D [17] is used for full core analysis using diffusion or transport theory, REBUS [18] is
used for fuel depletion and management, and PERSENT [19] is used for perturbation and
sensitivity analysis. PERSENT can also calculate reactor kinetics parameters and reactivity
feedback coefficients. Results from these codes typically include detailed flux and power

 40

distributions of the reactor core, safety parameters of the reactor core and fuel compositions
after reactor operation, all of which are essential for fast reactor modeling and design.
 In this benchmark analysis, MC2-3 was used to generate 33-g cross sections. MC2-3 is a
multigroup cross section code designed primarily for fast reactor analysis. It processes ENDF/B
nuclear data, performs high-fidelity multigroup transport calculations to capture the energy self-
shielding effects, and solves the neutron slowing-down equation to collapse the continuous-
energy data into multigroup form. MC2-3 can model fuel pins, fuel assemblies or infinite
medium. For improved spatial accuracy, MC2-3 also couples with TWODANT [20] and
performs 2-D SN ultrafine-group transport calculation, accounting for neutron leakage and
generating region-dependent multigroup cross sections.
 DIF3D has multiple neutron diffusion or transport solvers for calculating full core fluxes
and power distributions. For SEFOR analysis, DIF3D-VARIANT [21] was employed, which
solves the P1-Pn neutron transport equation using the variation nodal method. DIF3D supports
a variety of spatial geometries, from 1-D slab or cylindrical to 3-D cartesian, or 3-D hexagonal-
z geometry. DIF3D-VARIANT, in particular, supports 2-D and 3-D cartesian as well as 2-D
hexagonal and 3-D hexagonal-z geometry using a semi-structured grid. Oftentimes the structure
grid is aligned with the fuel assembly and can be uniform or non-uniform.
 PERSENT (PERturbation and SENsitivity for Transport) is based on the variational nodal
method and uses DIF3D-VARIANT to compute the forward and adjoint fluxes required for
perturbation calculations. In SEFOR work, PERSENT was used to calculate sensitivity
coefficients and the uncertainties of the Doppler reactivity feedback due to nuclear data
uncertainties using the first-order perturbation theory. It was also used to calculate reactivity
feedback coefficients for SAS to simulate power ascending tests.

 3.2 System Analysis Code: SAS4A/SASSYS-1
 SAS4A/SASSYS-1 (SAS) is a system analysis code developed by ANL to simulate the
accident and transient behavior in liquid metal cooled fast reactors (LMFRs), particularly SFRs.
The code includes models for representing reactor core kinetics, thermal hydraulics in heat
transfer systems, reactor control systems and more, enabling comprehensive analyses of the full
reactor dynamics. Extensive validation of the code has been performed against integral system
tests, such as against the EBR-II Shutdown Heat Removal Tests [22] and the FFTF Passive
Safety Tests [23].
 Some of the code’s unique features include single-pin characteristic channel models for
rapid system level transient evaluations, and full-plant coolant system models including
primary and intermediate heat transport systems. It also includes models for both oxide and
metallic fuels, and has reactor point kinetics models integrated with reactivity feedback effects
to model reactor dynamics.

 3.3 NEAMS Meshing Tool: MOOSE-Reactor-Module
 To simulate the SEFOR reactor using the NEAMS reactor physics codes, the reactor mesh
was first constructed using the Multiphysics Object Oriented Simulation Environment
(MOOSE) [24] Reactor Module [25]. This open-source meshing toolkit is designed to
streamline the creation of finite element meshes for nuclear reactor cores and integrates directly
with MOOSE-based applications. The Reactor Module supports both Cartesian and hexagonal

 41

lattice geometries and enables hierarchical mesh construction, from individual pin cells to fuel
assemblies, and ultimately full-core models. It also offers flexible axial extrusion features for
3-D geometries. Advanced meshing features, such as boundary layer biasing and symmetry-
based mesh trimming, are also supported to reduce computational cost. Additionally, the
module utilizes “reporting IDs”, extra element integer tags, to label mesh regions (e.g., pins,
assemblies, axial planes). These tags assist with material assignments, depletion tracking, and
post-processing. Meshes generated using the Reactor Module can be used directly in MOOSE-
based applications or exported in Exodus II format for use in other finite element solvers.

 3.4 NEAMS Reactor Physics Code: Griffin
 Griffin [26] is a finite-element-based reactor physics application built on the MOOSE
multiphysics framework, jointly developed by ANL and INL. It is designed for high-fidelity
neutronics analyses in advanced reactor systems, with multiphysics coupling considered from
the outset of development. Griffin can perform steady-state calculations and time-dependent
transient simulations, including reactor kinetics and accident scenarios. It supports full-core
analyses with detailed physics.
 Griffin solves the linearized Boltzmann transport equation for neutrons using a variety of
numerical methods. It employs multigroup energy discretization and offers several schemes for
the angular/distribution variable, including discrete ordinates (SN), spherical harmonics
expansion (PN), and the diffusion approximation (a low-order limit of transport). For spatial
discretization, Griffin uses finite element methods (FEM) on unstructured meshes, supporting
both continuous and discontinuous Galerkin formulations. Acceleration techniques such as
Coarse Mesh Finite Difference (CMFD) are available to speed up iterative transport solves.

4 Phase I: Modeling Zero Power Experiments
 4.1 Numerical Models and Simulation
 As discussed in Section 2.1.1, the SEFOR reactor was modeled by replacing the active core
region of the original RZ model with detailed geometries for each fuel assembly channel.
Modern reactor physics codes‒ whether relying on Monte Carlo methods or high-fidelity
deterministic approaches using an unstructured mesh‒ can simulate reactor core physics in far
greater details and with higher accuracy compared with the legacy study in the 1960s [1].
 In this benchmark study, Monte Carlo reference models were first developed with both
Serpent [27] and Shift [28] to calculate the Doppler reactivity feedback and to provide reference
solutions for deterministic methods. Two deterministic codes were then used for modeling Core
I-E and Core I-I: 1) a DIF3D- VARIANT model which homogenizes regions within each fuel
subassembly and 2) a Griffin model which explicitly represents the detailed geometry of fuel
assembly similar to Monte Carlo models. The following subsections provide details for these
models.

modeling discrepancies of the ambiguous downcomer region (DC_IV and DC_OV) between

investigating the potential discrepancies among the different models.

 Monte Carlo methods have been deployed to calculate reactivity feedback arising from
Doppler broadening of nuclear cross sections and to provide reference solution for the
deterministic method. Initially, the MCNP code was used to set up various Monte Carlo models.
However, MCNP does not support interpolation of temperature-dependent cross section
libraries and requires auxiliary subroutines or codes to compute Doppler reactivity feedback
when temperatures differ from those in pre-processed libraries. Although NJOY can preprocess
temperature dependent libraries, this approach demands significant preprocessing efforts and
extensive computer memory.
 In contrast, both Serpent and Shift can perform on-the-fly Doppler broadening.
Accordingly, consistent Monte Carlo models using Serpent or Shift were developed to simulate
the SEFOR isothermal experiments and provide reference solutions for deterministic methods.
Serpent 2 is a Monte Carlo code developed by VTT Research Institute in Finland [27]. Serpent
offers two options for modeling the temperature dependence of the nuclear cross sections on
the fly. The first is a Doppler-broadening pre-processor which was activated using the “TMP”
card in the material definition [30]. Below the energy threshold of the unresolved resonance
region, the Doppler-broadening pre-processor adjusts nuclear cross sections by analytically
integrating the Doppler broadening formula. The second method is a stochastic method that
samples the motion of target nucleus on-the-fly. It can be enabled using the “TMS” card [31].
In both methods, the Doppler-broadening is applied only for energies below the unresolved
resonance threshold. For this benchmark analysis, both the TMP card and the TMS card were
tested for Core I-E. And for Core I-I, only the TMP card was employed.
 On the other hand, Shift, a Monte Carlo code developed by ORNL, used a different
approach to generate the problem-dependent Doppler-broadened cross sections. It applies a
finite-difference broadening scheme for one-dimensional nuclear cross sections and unit-base
linear interpolation for two-dimensional moderator scattering data library [32]. Shift was
applied to model Core I-E. In Shift model, the DC_OV region was modeled as a separate
cylindrical layer, as illustrated in Figure 4-5. This Monte Carlo core mapping model was
referred as “Model D”, as it differs slightly from the previous model A to model C. As Griffin
is able to handle the cylindrical core boundary, the Griffin neutronic model is consistent with
the Shift reference model.

 ARC Reactor Physics Model
 Two codes were employed to develop deterministic models for the SEFOR reactor. First,
MC2-3 was used to generate 33-g multigroup cross sections. These cross sections were then
read by DIF3D-VARIANT, to perform full-core calculations to determine neutron flux
distributions and power profiles.
 The multigroup cross-section generation in MC2-3 followed a two-step procedure and
produced output in ISOTXS format. In the first step, a series of transport calculations were
carried out using simple 1-D cylindrical models or 0-D material mixtures to obtain energy self-
shielded cross sections for various regions of the reactor core, using a fine energy grid with
over 1000 energy groups. These detailed cross sections were then applied to an approximate
RZ model of the full SEFOR reactor core. The TWODANT code was used to perform the
neutron transport calculation using this RZ geometry. Fluxes from the transport calculations

 47

included both neutron leakage and spatial self-shielding effects. In the second step, the flux
distributions from TWODANT were used by MC2-3 to condense the fine-energy grid cross
sections to the ANL 33-group structure.
 Microscopic cross sections were generated for both Core I-E and Core I-I across
temperatures ranging from 350 °F to 750 °F, with increments of 50 °F. The benchmark model
defined 18 homogenized zones for Core I-E as shown in Table 4-3. Previously homogenized
regions in the Monte Carlo model‒ such as the bottom grid plate, core top (Na_steel),
Downcomer inside (DC_IV) and outside the vessel (DC_OV), radial reflectors and radial
shields‒ were retained as separate homogenized regions. An infinite homogeneous mixture was
used when calculating cross sections for these regions.
 For the fuel assemblies, cross sections were homogenized within each individual assembly.
Corresponding to the different axial segments in a fuel rod or an IFA rod, different 1-D
cylindrical models were created in MC2-3 to approximate each heterogeneous lattice

 For the subsequent Griffin simulations, a split-mesh configuration was used to optimize
parallel computational performance especially related to the memory requirement. The
workflow involves configuring the mesh split setup prior to running simulations in Griffin. This
process is managed through the Mesh block in Griffin's input files. The Griffin executable is
utilized to execute the mesh splitting. Once the pre-split mesh is created, the workflow
transitions to simulation mode by substituting the Exodus file with the split .cpr file and
activating the “parallel_type=distributed” option to enable distributed processing during the
simulation.

Chebyshev quadrature for discretizing the angular space. For the multiphysics analysis, the
angular quadrature was specified with three polar angles and four/six azimuthal angles. The
finite element shape functions for the angular fluxes (primal variables) were chosen as
MONOMIAL with first-order shape functions, and the maximum scattering anisotropy order
was set to 2.
 To enhance computational efficiency, the “using_array_variable” option was enabled,
allowing angular fluxes for each group to be stored via MOOSE’s ArrayVariable system. This
approach reduces the number of computational kernels and minimizes overall computational
cost. Additionally, the “collapse_scattering” option was activated, enabling in-group scattering
sources to be directly formed within the scattering kernels, further reducing simulation runtime.
 For the cases that used CMFD acceleration, the “prolongation_type” was set to be
multiplicative. This configuration updates the fine mesh flux using the coarse mesh solution in
a multiplicative manner, influencing convergence behavior and allowing for performance
tuning. A maximum diffusion coefficient of 1 was specified using the
“max_diffusion_coefficient” parameter. If this limit is reached, a warning is issued, and the
value is clipped. Users must verify proper convergence of the Richardson iteration when this
maximum diffusion coefficient is applied, as restricting the diffusion coefficient can adversely
affect convergence. The Richardson outer iteration was configured with an absolute tolerance
of 10-6, a relative tolerance of 10-6, and a maximum of 1000 iterations. The inner linear solver
used GMRES, with a maximum of 1000 iterations per solve.

 Monte Carlo models were also used to compute the total reactivity feedback and to compare
against experimental results from the isothermal tests. Core geometries at different temperatures

 Similarly, reactivity feedback at different isothermal states were computed using Griffin

thermal expansion coefficients.
 In fast reactor safety analyses, particularly for SFRs, reactivity feedback effects are often
presented through independent coefficients. For instance, fuel Doppler coefficient accounts for
reactivity change due to temperature-dependent nuclear reactions, the radial core expansion
coefficient accounts for reactivity change due to radial core expansion, fuel density coefficient
accounts for change due to fuel pin axial expansion, sodium density coefficient accounts for
change due to temperature dependence of sodium density, and etc. These coefficients are
typically assumed independent and the sum of them provide the total reactivity feedback.
 In this benchmark study, the ARC deterministic model evaluated the reactivity feedback in
the isothermal tests from four primary mechanisms: Doppler, fuel axial expansion, core radial
expansion and sodium density change. The total reactivity feedback was then determined as the
sum of these four contributions.
 Figure 4-19 compares the calculated results with the experimental data for both Core I-E
and Core I-I, using the Serpent Monte Carlo model and ARC deterministic model. Both
approaches showed good agreements with the experimental measurements of the total reactivity
feedback. Furthermore, the ARC calculations breakdown of feedback contributions indicates
that in SEFOR, Doppler feedback is the dominant mechanism, followed by radial core
expansion. Sodium density changes and fuel axial expansions in both Core I-E and Core I-I
only provide small reactivity feedback. All four feedback components were found to be
negative for both core configurations.
 The calculated total reactivity feedback from the SEFOR isothermal tests, obtained using
the Monte Carlo models, the ARC model as well as the Griffin neutronic models, are compared
with experimental measurements in Figure 4-20. For both Core I-E and Core I-I, excellent
agreement is observed among the different numerical models and with the experiment data,
which demonstrates the accuracy of modern reactor physics codes in predicting reactor
feedback effects.

5 Phase II: Modeling Power Ascending Tests
 5.1 Numerical Models and Simulation
 To simulate the power ascending tests performed in Core I-J, an ARC model, based on the
ARC model previously developed for Core I-I, was developed for Core I-J to compute the
fission power distribution. A SAS model of the SEFOR core was developed to account for the
heat transfers as reactor power increased from 0.4 MW to 17.2 MW. For each power ascending
test, the ARC model computed fission power distribution, which was then passed offline to the
SAS model to determine the fuel temperature distribution. Reactivity feedback coefficients
were obtained using PERSENT/DIF3D-VARIANT based on the ARC model. With fuel
temperatures obtained from SAS model and reactivity feedback coefficients from PERSENT,
the total reactivity feedback associated with each test was determined. The following
subsections describe these models in detail.

 SAS4A/SASSYS-1 Model

ascending tests (power from 0.4 MW to 17 MW), the average coolant temperature remained

variations across power levels were primarily governed by the fuel thermal expansion.

 An iterative process was developed to determine the appropriate fuel-clad gap width for
each test, channel, and axial node:
 1) Fuel pin and cladding dimensions at 760 °F were obtained from the benchmark
 specifications and applied to the initial SAS model.
 2) After running the model, the predicted average fuel temperatures were used to update
 the fuel outer radii via the thermal expansion correlation indicated in Table 5-3 .
 3) The updated geometry was re-entered into SAS, and the model was run again.
This process was repeated until convergence, typically requiring four to five iterations. Figure
5-4 presents an example of the converged fuel pin radius for power ascending test #42. As
expected, the axial radius profile followed the fission power distribution, with a maximum
variation of about 2%.

agreement for the calculation and measured fuel temperatures in the IFA rods.

 5.2 Phase-II: Power Ascending Test Results
 The power ascending tests were simulated using the ARC/SAS model. Reactivity
coefficients calculated by PERSENT, together with fuel-average temperatures in each channel
calculated by the SAS model, were used to compute the reactivity feedback from sodium
density change, fuel axial expansion and fuel Doppler. Feedback from radial core expansion
was neglected in most cases as the inlet coolant temperature varied only slightly between 700
°F to 760 °F (cases below 700 °F were excluded from comparison).

axial expansion and fuel Doppler separately for all the testing cases. As shown in Figure 5-10

compared to that from fuel Doppler.

6 Summary and Conclusions
 By the end of FY-25, substantial progress was achieved in the SEFOR benchmark study. A
variety of numerical tools commonly employed in modeling sodium fast reactors were applied
to develop models for SEFOR core configurations I-D, I-E, I-I, and I-J. These included Monte
Carlo codes such as MCNP, Serpent, and Shift; deterministic codes such as the legacy ARC
suite and the high-fidelity NEAMS code Griffin; and the system analysis code
SAS4A/SASSYS-1.
 Using these models, both SEFOR zero-power experiments and power-ascending tests were
successfully simulated. Comparisons were performed against experimental measurements of
core criticalities, reflector worth, kinetics parameters (Λ/βeff), isothermal reactivity feedback
(from 350 °F to 760 °F at zero power), and power-ascending reactivity feedback (as power
increased from 0.4 MW to 17 MW). In general, these comparisons demonstrated very good
agreement between numerical results and experimental data.
 Key findings and conclusions drawn from this benchmark study include:
 1) The calculated from various numerical models all matched experimental data for
 Core I-E within 250 pcm. Variations among the modeling approach, such as Monte
 Carlo versus deterministic methods, different ENDF library versions, different Monte
 Carlo methods, different ways of modeling the core downcomer region, were generally
 within this same range, with the exception of ENDF/B-VIII.0, which produced that
 were 500 pcm less than those obtained with other libraries.
 2) Very good agreements observed for reflector worth calibrations and kinetics parameters
 using the Monte Carlo models.
 3) Despite the differences in calculating , all numerical models are highly accurate in
 calculating the reactivity feedback in the isothermal tests, with deviations < 3% from
 the experimental data.
 4) Good agreement between the the calculated reactivity feedback using the ARC model
 and the reactivity feedback calculated from Monte Carlo reference model (isothermal
 tests) and experiment confirmed that the modeling approach for reactivity feedback in
 the reactor safety analysis code (SAS model) is accurate. For SEFOR, the total reactivity
 feedback was found to be dominated by Doppler broadening, radial core expansion, fuel
 axial expansion and sodium density change. Importantly, these components can be
 evaluated as independent effects, and the total is their sum.
 5) On the thermal hydraulic side, the SAS model accurately predicted the measured
 increase in fuel temperature recorded by thermocouples in the IFA rods.
 6) The ARC/SAS model provided reliable estimates of total amount of reactivity feedback
 during power ascending tests. These calculations typically agree with the experiment
 value within its experimental uncertainty (1σ).
 Overall, with good agreement observed in this benchmark analysis, the following user
experiences emerged on using the different numerical codes, developing appropriate numerical
models as well as applying experimental data for validations:
 1) The use of Griffin to model SEFOR demonstrated the feasibility of developing a fully
 heterogeneous model for Core I-E and Core I-I. Meshes were directly generated using

 89

 the MOOSE Reactor Mesh Module, eliminating the need for external meshing tools.
 Despite the complexity of solving the 33-group discrete ordinates neutron transport
 equation on a large mesh, Griffin completed the calculations within a reasonable
 timeframe given to the available high-performance computational resources. This effort
 also serves as part of Griffin’s code validation, specifically for calculating reactivity
 feedback in a sodium fast reactor.
 2) MC2-3 was used to generate multigroup cross sections for both the ARC model and
 Griffin models. While there is good overall agreement with its reference Monte Carlo
 model, larger discrepancies of the calculated power from the reference model appeared
 in fuel assemblies containing neutron absorbers. These seem to relate to the accuracy of
 the multigroup cross sections generated for the homogenized region. Different ways of
 transforming the hexagonal geometry into the 1-D cylindrical geometry were used in
 this benchmark study. This issue will be investigated further in future studies.
 3) The single channel model with iterated fuel pin model proved to be capable of
 simulating the SEFOR power ascending tests adequately. However, this methodology
 is unlikely to be applicable for modeling reactivity insertion transient, where more
 advanced models may be required in future studies.
 4) In this benchmark study, while there is plenty of experimental data available from
 literature, inconsistencies were encountered across different sources. In several cases,
 the published values were not raw experimental data, but corrected results based on
 assumptions that lacked independent validation.
 Looking into FY-26, the SEFOR benchmark project will continue to address the modeling
issues identified in FY-25. Effort will focus on the simulation of reactivity insertion transient
tests in SEFOR using a similar ARC/SAS model. Future work will also focus on incorporating
BISON into the SEFOR core modeling process to enable the first multiphysics simulations of
the isothermal tests based on the MOOSE framework.
