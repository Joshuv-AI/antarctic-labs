Jacobi exceptional orthogonal polynomials for extended Scarf I potentials with position-dependent mass
C. Quesne

Abstract
We show that the Scarf I potential problem in a position-dependent mass background of the type m ⁡ ( α , x ) = ( 1 + α ​ sin ⁡ x ) − 2 m(\alpha;x)=(1+\alpha\sin x)^{-2} , 0 < α < 1 0<\alpha<1 , can be solved by using a point canonical transformation mapping the corresponding Schrödinger equation onto that of the Scarf I potential with constant mass. The inverse point canonical transformation then provides some exactly-solvable rational extensions of the Scarf I potential with positive-dependent mass associated with X m X_{m} -Jacobi exceptional orthogonal polynomials of type I, II, or III. The Scarf I potential problem with position-dependent mass is shown to exhibit a deformed shape invariance property in a deformed supersymmetric framework. Such a property is also valid for extended potentials of type I and II. The results are illustrated with a simple example.



Keywords: exceptional orthogonal polynomials, point canonical transformation, deformed supersymmetric quantum mechanics, deformed shape invariance

Introduction

A few years ago, new sets of orthogonal polynomials, generalizing the classical orthogonal polynomials of Hermite, Laguerre, and Jacobi
[
1
]
, were introduced and called exceptional orthogonal polynomials (EOPs)
[
2
,
3
]
. They form orthogonal and complete polynomial sets with a positive weight function although there are some gaps in the sequence of their degrees in contrast with classical orthogonal polynomials
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
. Soon after that, it was shown that they may occur in bound-state wavefunctions of rationally-extended well-known quantum potentials, constructed by Darboux transformations in the context of shape invariance in supersymmetric quantum mechanics
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
.

Another topic that has recently received a lot of attention is that of position-dependent mass (PDM) in quantum systems because it has a lot of applications in several fields, ranging from the study of inhomogeneous materials to energy density many-body problems
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
. It is worth noting that the PDM presence in the Schrödinger equation may alternatively be interpreted as reflecting a curvature of the underlying space or a deformation of the canonical commutation relations
[
17
]
.

A difficulty that occurs in such an approach comes from the momentum and mass operator noncommutativity, which gives rise to an ordering ambiguity in the kinetic energy representation. To cope with this difficulty, von Roos proposed a general two-parameter form of the kinetic energy operator
[
18
]
. A special case of the latter, which is very often used, is that of the Mustafa-Mazharimousavi ordering
[
19
]
.

In contrast with the case of standard Schrödinger equations, for with many exactly-solvable rational potentials connected with EOPs have been built (see, e.g.,
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
), for generalized Schrödinger equations there have been less attempts of constructing exactly-solvable rational potentials connected with EOPs. Among them, one may quote some extensions of the quantum oscillator and Kepler-Coulomb problems in curved space
[
27
]
, those of an oscillator-shaped quantum well confined in a cavity between two infinite walls
[
28
]
, those of a radial harmonic oscillator
[
29
]
, as well as some extensions
[
30
]
of previously known potentials in a PDM background
[
31
]
and some families of asymmetric parabolic potentials associated with different kinds of PDM
[
32
]
.

The aim of the present paper is to provide an additional example of exactly-solvable potentials with PDM, whose wavefunctions can be expressed in terms of
X_{m}
-Jacobi EOPs.

In section 2, we review the problem of a Scarf I potential in a
m(\alpha;x)=(1+\alpha\sin x)^{-2}
background and prove its exact solvability by mapping it onto that of a Scarf I potential in a constant mass background by means of a point canonical transformation (PCT). In section 3, we then make use of the known rational extensions for the latter problem connected with
X_{m}
-Jacobi EOPs to build similar extensions for the former one. The deformed supersymmetric properties of the resulting potentials are then discussed in section 4. A simple example is presented in section 5. Finally, section 6 contains the conclusion.

2
The Scarf I potential in a
m(\alpha;x)=(1+\alpha\sin x)^{-2}
background

On using the Mustafa and Mazharimousavi ordering
[
19
]
, in units wherein
\hbar=2m_{0}=1
, a PDM Schrödinger equation can be written as

\biggl(-m^{-\frac{1}{4}}(\alpha;x)\frac{d}{dx}m^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\alpha;x)\frac{d}{dx}m^{-\frac{1}{4}}(\alpha;x)+V(x)\biggr)\psi^{(\alpha)}_{n}(x)=E^{(\alpha)}_{n}\psi^{(\alpha)}_{n}(x),

(2.1)

where
m(\alpha;x)
is the dimensionless form of the mass function
m_{0}m(\alpha;x)
and
V(x)
is the potential. In the present paper, we assume that

m(\alpha;x)=(1+\alpha\sin x)^{-2},\qquad 0<\alpha<1,

(2.2)

and

\displaystyle V(x)

\displaystyle=V(x;A,B)=[A(A-1)+B^{2}]\sec^{2}x-B(2A-1)\sec x\tan x,

\displaystyle\qquad A-1>B>0,\qquad-\frac{\pi}{2}<x<\frac{\pi}{2},

(2.3)

is a Scarf I potential
[
33
]
.

With such a choice of PDM, equation (
2.1
) may alternatively be interpreted as a deformed Schrödinger equation

[\pi_{x}^{2}+V(x;A,B)]\psi^{(\alpha)}_{n}(x;A,B)=E^{(\alpha)}_{n}(A,B)\psi^{(\alpha)}_{n}(x;A,B),

(2.4)

written in terms of a deformed momentum operator

\pi_{x}=-{\rm i}\sqrt{f(\alpha;x)}\frac{d}{dx}\sqrt{f(\alpha;x)},\qquad f(\alpha;x)=1+\alpha\sin x,\qquad 0<\alpha<1.

(2.5)

Equation (
2.4
) can be solved by mapping it onto a constant-mass Schrödinger equation

\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{du^{2}}+U(u)\right)\phi_{n}(u)=\epsilon_{n}\phi_{n}(u)

(2.6)

by a PCT
[
34
,
35
]
, i.e., by some changes of variable and of function

u(\alpha;x)=av(\alpha;x)+b,\qquad v(\alpha;x)=\int^{x}\frac{dx^{\prime}}{f(\alpha;x^{\prime})},

(2.7)

\phi_{n}(u(\alpha;x))\propto\sqrt{f(\alpha;x)}\psi^{(\alpha)}_{n}(x;A,B).

(2.8)

The two potentials and their corresponding energy eigenvalues are related by

V(x;A,B)=a^{2}U(u)+c,\qquad E^{(\alpha)}_{n}(A,B)=a^{2}\epsilon_{n}+c.

(2.9)

In (
2.7
) and (
2.9
),
a
,
b
, and
c
denote three arbitrary real constants.

For the choice of
f(\alpha;x)
made in (
2.5
), we obtain

v(\alpha;x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}\arctan\frac{\tan(x/2)+\alpha}{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}.

(2.10)

Hence, on taking

a=\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}},\qquad b=2\arctan\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha}{1+\alpha}}-\frac{\pi}{2},

(2.11)

we get

u(\alpha;x)=2\arctan\frac{\tan(x/2)+\alpha}{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}+2\arctan\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha}{1+\alpha}}-\frac{\pi}{2},

(2.12)

showing that
u
varies in the interval
-\frac{\pi}{2}<u<\frac{\pi}{2}
. From (
2.12
), it results that

\tan u=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}(\tan x+\alpha\sec x),\qquad\sec u=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}(\sec x+\alpha\tan x),

(2.13)

or, conversely,

\tan x=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}(\tan u-\alpha\sec u),\qquad\sec x=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}(\sec u-\alpha\tan u).

(2.14)

With such a choice of variable, the potential
V(x;A,B)
, defined in (
2.3
), becomes

V(x;A,B)=(1-\alpha^{2})U(u;A^{\prime},B^{\prime})+c,

(2.15)

where
U(u;A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
is another Scarf I potential

U(u;A^{\prime},B^{\prime})=[A^{\prime}(A^{\prime}-1)+B^{\prime 2}]\sec^{2}u-B^{\prime}(2A^{\prime}-1)\sec u\tan u,

(2.16)

whose parameters
A^{\prime}
,
B^{\prime}
are defined in terms of
A
,
B
by the equations

\begin{split}&A^{\prime}(A^{\prime}-1)+B^{\prime 2}=\frac{1}{(1-\alpha^{2})^{2}}\{(1+\alpha^{2})[A(A-1)+B^{2}]+2\alpha B(2A-1)\},\\
&B^{\prime}(2A^{\prime}-1)=\frac{1}{(1-\alpha^{2})^{2}}\{2\alpha[A(A-1)+B^{2}]+(1+\alpha^{2})B(2A-1)\},\end{split}

(2.17)

while the additional constant
c
is given by

c=-\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha^{2}}\{\alpha[A(A-1)+B^{2}]+B(2A-1)\}.

(2.18)

In order to solve (
2.17
) for
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
, it is useful to introduce the combinations of parameters

\Delta_{1}=\left(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{(A-B)(A-B-1)}{(1+\alpha)^{2}}\right)^{1/2},\qquad\Delta_{2}=\left(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{(A+B)(A+B-1)}{(1-\alpha)^{2}}\right)^{1/2}.

(2.19)

Then
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
can be shown to be given by

A^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}(\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+1),\qquad B^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}(\Delta_{2}-\Delta_{1}),

(2.20)

and, as a consequence of (
2.19
), they satisfy the condition
A^{\prime}-1>B^{\prime}>1
. It is worth observing that
c
may be rewritten in terms of
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
as

c=\alpha\{\alpha[A^{\prime}(A^{\prime}-1)+B^{\prime 2}]-B^{\prime}(2A^{\prime}-1)\}.

(2.21)

From the known eigenvalues

\epsilon_{n}(A^{\prime})=(A^{\prime}+n)^{2},\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots,

(2.22)

of the Scarf I potential
U(u;A^{\prime},B^{\prime})

[
33
]
, and the constants
a
and
c
defined in (
2.11
) and (
2.18
) or (
2.21
), respectively, we get for the eigenvalues (
2.9
) of the PDM Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle E^{(\alpha)}_{n}(A,B)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha^{2})n^{2}+(1-\alpha^{2})(\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+1)n+\frac{1}{4}(1+\alpha)^{2}\Delta_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(1-\alpha)^{2}\Delta_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}(1-\alpha^{2})(\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}+\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2})+\frac{1}{4}(1-2\alpha^{2}),\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots,

(2.23)

provided the corresponding wavefunctions are normalizable on
\left(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right)
, which will indeed be the case.

Such wavefunctions can be expressed as

\psi^{(\alpha)}_{n}(x;A,B)=\lambda[f(\alpha;x)]^{-1/2}\phi_{n}(u;A^{\prime},B^{\prime}),

(2.24)

where
\phi_{n}(u;A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
are the well-known Scarf I wavefunctions
[
8
]

\begin{split}\phi_{n}(u;A^{\prime},B^{\prime})&={\cal N}^{(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})}_{n}(1-\sin u)^{\frac{1}{2}(A^{\prime}-B^{\prime})}(1+\sin u)^{\frac{1}{2}(A^{\prime}+B^{\prime})}\\
&\quad\times P_{n}^{(A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2},A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2})}(\sin u),\\
{\cal N}^{(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})}_{n}&=\left(\frac{(2A^{\prime}+2n)n!\Gamma(2A^{\prime}+n)}{2^{2A^{\prime}}\Gamma\left(A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}+n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}+n+\frac{1}{2}\right)}\right)^{1/2},\end{split}

(2.25)

written in terms of Jacobi polynomials, and
\lambda
is the constant
(1-\alpha^{2})^{1/4}
, arising from the change of normalization from

to

\displaystyle\psi^{(\alpha)}_{n}(x;A,B)

\displaystyle={\cal N}^{(\alpha)}_{n,A,B}[f(\alpha;x)]^{-\frac{1}{2}(\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+2)}(1-\sin x)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}

\displaystyle\quad\times(1+\sin x)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}P_{n}^{(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})}(t),\qquad t=\frac{\alpha+\sin x}{1+\alpha\sin x},

(2.26)

with

{\cal N}^{(\alpha)}_{n,A,B}=\left(\frac{(1-\alpha)^{\Delta_{1}+1}(1+\alpha)^{\Delta_{2}+1}(\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+1+2n)n!\Gamma(\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+1+n)}{2^{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+1}\Gamma(\Delta_{1}+n+1)\Gamma(\Delta_{2}+n+1)}\right)^{1/2},

(2.27)

and
n=0
, 1, 2, ….

3
Rational extensions of the Scarf I potential in a
m(\alpha;x)=(1+\alpha\sin x)^{-2}
background

Rational extensions of the Scarf I potential connected with Jacobi EOPs and a constant mass have been constructed as partners of conventional potentials in the framework of SUSYQM
[
7
,
8
,
10
]
. We will consider here more specifically the case of one-step SUSYQM, leading to
X_{m}
-Jacobi polynomials. By applying the inverse of the PCT presented in section 2, we will build from them rational extensions of the Scarf I potential in the
m(\alpha;x)
background and study some properties of the latter.

The rational extensions of the Scarf I potential
U(u;A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
belong to three different types I, II, and III, according to the kind of seed function that is used to construct the partner. Such seed functions and their corresponding energies can be written as

\begin{split}&\varphi^{\rm I}_{m}(u;A^{\prime},B^{\prime})=\phi_{m}(u;B^{\prime}+\tfrac{1}{2},A^{\prime}-\tfrac{1}{2}),\qquad e^{\rm I}_{m}(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})=(B^{\prime}+m+\tfrac{1}{2})^{2},\\
&\quad A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}>m+\tfrac{1}{2},\\
&\varphi^{\rm II}_{m}(u;A^{\prime},B^{\prime})=\phi_{m}(u;\tfrac{1}{2}-B^{\prime},\tfrac{1}{2}-A^{\prime}),\qquad e^{\rm II}_{m}(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})=(B^{\prime}-m-\tfrac{1}{2})^{2},\\
&\quad A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}>m+\tfrac{1}{2},\\
&\varphi^{\rm III}_{m}(u;A^{\prime},B^{\prime})=\phi_{m}(u;1-A^{\prime},-B^{\prime}),\qquad e^{\rm III}_{m}(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})=(A^{\prime}-m-1)^{2},\\
&\quad A^{\prime}+B^{\prime},A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}>m+\tfrac{1}{2},\qquad m\text{\ even},\end{split}

(3.1)

where

\phi_{m}(u;A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\propto(1-\sin u)^{\frac{1}{2}(A^{\prime}-B^{\prime})}(1+\sin u)^{\frac{1}{2}(A^{\prime}+B^{\prime})}P_{m}^{(A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2},A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2})}(\sin u),

(3.2)

in accordance with (
2.25
).

To obtain for the partner some rationally-extended Scarf I potential with given
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
parameters, we have to start from a conventional potential with different parameters
\bar{A}^{\prime}
,
\bar{B}^{\prime}
, which depend on the type considered. The results read

\begin{split}U_{0}(u)&=U(u;\bar{A}^{\prime},\bar{B}^{\prime}),\\
U_{1}(u)&=U(u;\bar{A}^{\prime},\bar{B}^{\prime})-2\frac{d^{2}}{du^{2}}\log\varphi_{m}^{i}(u;\bar{A}^{\prime},\bar{B}^{\prime})\\
&=U^{(m)}_{\rm ext}(u;A^{\prime},B^{\prime})=U(u;A^{\prime},B^{\prime})+U^{(m)}_{\rm rat}(u;A^{\prime},B^{\prime}),\end{split}

(3.3)

where

U^{(m)}_{\rm rat}(u;A^{\prime},B^{\prime})=2\left\{z\frac{\dot{g}_{m}^{(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})}}{g_{m}^{(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})}}-(1-z^{2})\left[\frac{\ddot{g}_{m}^{(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})}}{g_{m}^{(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})}}-\left(\frac{\dot{g}_{m}^{(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})}}{g_{m}^{(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})}}\right)^{2}\right]\right\},\qquad z=\sin u.

(3.4)

Here a dot denotes a derivative with respect to
z
, and we have for the three different types

\begin{split}\text{(I)\ }&\bar{A}^{\prime}=A^{\prime},\quad\bar{B}^{\prime}=B^{\prime}-1,\quad g_{m}^{(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})}(z)=P_{m}^{\left(-A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2},A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}-\frac{3}{2}\right)}(z),\\
&\quad A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}>m-\tfrac{1}{2},\\
\text{(II)\ }&\bar{A}^{\prime}=A^{\prime},\quad\bar{B}^{\prime}=B^{\prime}+1,\quad g_{m}^{(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})}(z)=P_{m}^{\left(A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}-\frac{3}{2},-A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}\right)}(z),\\
&\quad A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}>m-\tfrac{1}{2},\\
\text{(III)\ }&\bar{A}^{\prime}=A^{\prime}+1,\quad\bar{B}^{\prime}=B^{\prime},\quad g_{m}^{(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})}(z)=P_{m}^{\left(-A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2},-A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}\right)}(z),\\
&\quad A^{\prime}+B^{\prime},A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}>m-\tfrac{1}{2},\quad m\text{\ even}.\end{split}

(3.5)

It is worth noting that on going from
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
to
(\bar{A}^{\prime},\bar{B}^{\prime})
,
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
in (
2.19
) are changed into

(\bar{\Delta}_{1},\bar{\Delta_{2}})=\begin{cases}(\Delta_{1}+1,\Delta_{2}-1)&\text{for type I},\\
(\Delta_{1}-1,\Delta_{2}+1)&\text{for type II},\\
(\Delta_{1}+1,\Delta_{2}+1)&\text{for type III}.\end{cases}

(3.6)

For types I and II, the two partners
U_{0}(u)
and
U_{1}(u)
are strictly isospectral, so that the spectrum of the latter is given by

\epsilon_{n}^{(\rm ext)}(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})=(A^{\prime}+n)^{2},\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots,\qquad\text{for type I or II},

(3.7)

whereas, for type III, the inverse of the seed function being normalizable, we get an extra bound state below the spectrum of
U_{0}(u)
, hence

\epsilon_{n}^{(\rm ext)}(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})=(A^{\prime}+1+n)^{2},\qquad n=-m-1,0,1,2,\ldots,\qquad\text{for type III}.

(3.8)

The wavefunctions of
U_{1}(u)
corresponding to
n=0
, 1, 2, …, can be obtained from

\phi^{(\rm ext}_{n}(u;A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\propto\frac{{\cal W}(\varphi^{i}_{m}(u;\bar{A}^{\prime},\bar{B}^{\prime}),\phi_{n}(u;\bar{A}^{\prime},\bar{B}^{\prime})\mid u)}{\varphi^{i}_{m}(u;\bar{A}^{\prime},\bar{B}^{\prime})},\qquad i=\text{{\rm I}, {\rm II}, or {\rm III}},

(3.9)

where
{\cal W}(\varphi^{i}_{m},\phi_{n}\mid u)
denotes the Wronskian of the functions
\varphi^{i}_{m}
and
\phi_{n}

[
36
]
. From Eqs. (
2.25
), (
3.1
), (
3.5
), and standard properties of Wronskians, they can be written as

\phi^{\rm ext}_{n}(u;A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\propto\frac{\phi_{0}(u;A^{\prime},B^{\prime})}{g^{(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})}_{m}(z)}Q^{(m)}_{n}(z;A^{\prime},B^{\prime}),\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots,

(3.10)

where

\displaystyle Q^{(m)}_{n}(z;A^{\prime},B^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\left(A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\right)g^{(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})}_{m}(z)P_{n}^{(A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2},A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}-\frac{3}{2})}(z)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}(1-z)\biggl[(n+2A^{\prime})g^{(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})}_{m}(z)P^{(A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}+\frac{3}{2},A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2})}_{n-1}(z)

\displaystyle\quad-(m+2B^{\prime}-1)g^{(A^{\prime},B^{\prime}+1)}_{m-1}(z)P^{(A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2},A^{\prime}+B4-\frac{3}{2})}_{n}(z)\biggr],

(3.11)

\displaystyle Q^{(m)}_{n}(z;A^{\prime},B^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\left(A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\right)g^{(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})}_{m}(z)P^{(A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}-\frac{3}{2},A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2})}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}(1+z)\biggl[(n+2A^{\prime})g^{(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})}_{m}(z)P^{(A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2},A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}+\frac{3}{2})}_{n-1}(z)

\displaystyle\quad-(m-2B^{\prime}-1)g^{(A^{\prime},B^{\prime}-1)}_{m-1}(z)P^{(A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}-\frac{3}{2},A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2})}_{n}(z)\biggr],

(3.12)

\displaystyle Q^{(m)}_{n}(z;A^{\prime},B^{\prime})

\displaystyle=[2B^{\prime}-(2A^{\prime}+1)z]g^{(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})}_{m}(z)P^{(A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2},A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2})}_{n}(z)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}(1-z^{2})\biggl[(n+2A^{\prime}+2)g^{(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})}_{m}(z)P^{(A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}+\frac{3}{2},A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}+\frac{3}{2})}_{n-1}(z)

\displaystyle\quad-(m-2A^{\prime})g^{(A^{\prime}-1,B^{\prime})}_{m-1}(z)P^{(A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2},A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2})}_{n}(z)\biggr],

(3.13)

for types I, II, and III, respectively. In the first two cases, it is a
(m+n)
th-degree polynomial in

th-degree polynomial in

&\quad+\frac{1}{4}(1-\alpha)^{2}\Delta_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(1-\alpha^{2})(\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}-\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2})+\frac{1}{4}(1-2\alpha^{2}).\end{split}

(3.15)

(3.17)

and

P_{m}^{(\Delta_{1}-1,-\Delta_{2}-1)}(t)&\text{for type II},\\
P_{m}^{(-\Delta_{1}-1,-\Delta_{2}-1)}(t)&\text{for type III},\end{cases}

(3.18)

resulting from (
2.15
) and (

), with

for types I, II, and III, respectively. They constitute orthogonal and complete families of polynomials (i.e., EOPs) on

4
Deformed supersymmetric properties

It is well known that the Scarf I potential
U(u;A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
, defined in (
2.16
), is a shape invariant potential in the context of SUSYQM
[
33
]
. As a result, the transformed potential
V(x;A,B)
of Eq. (
2.3
) is deformed shape invariant in the context of deformed SUSYQM
[
37
]
.

This means that on taking the ground-state wavefunction
\psi^{(\alpha)}_{0}(x;A,B)={\cal N}^{(\alpha)}_{0,A,B}[f(\alpha;x)]^{-\frac{1}{2}(\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+2)}(1-\sin x)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}(1+\sin x)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}
of the deformed Schrödinger equation (
2.4
) as the seed function, the corresponding superpotential

W_{\alpha}(x;A,B)=-f\frac{d}{dx}\log\psi^{(\alpha)}_{0}(x;A,B)-\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime},

(4.1)

where a dash denotes a derivative with respect to
x
, can be written as

W_{\alpha}(x;A,B)=\lambda\tan x+\mu\sec x,

(4.2)

with

\lambda=\frac{1}{2}[\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+1+\alpha(\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2})],\qquad\mu=\frac{1}{2}[\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}+\alpha(\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+1)].

(4.3)

It allows us to consider a pair of first-order differential operators

A^{\pm}_{\alpha}(A,B)=\mp\sqrt{f}\frac{d}{dx}\sqrt{f}+W_{\alpha}(x;A,B),

(4.4)

in terms of which the starting deformed Hamiltonian
H_{0}=\pi_{x}^{2}+V_{0}(x)
,
V_{0}(x)=V(x;A,B)
, can be written as

H_{0}=A^{+}_{\alpha}(A,B)A^{-}_{\alpha}(A,B)+E^{(\alpha)}_{0}(A,B).

(4.5)

The partner of
H_{0}
is

\displaystyle H_{1}=A^{-}_{\alpha}(A,B)A^{+}_{\alpha}(A,B)+E^{(\alpha)}_{0}(A,B)=\pi_{x}^{2}+V_{1}(x),

\displaystyle V_{1}(x)=V_{0}(x)+2f\frac{d}{dx}W_{\alpha}(x;A,B).

(4.6)

On writing
A=A^{(0)}
,
B=B^{(0)}
, so that
V_{0}(x)=V(x;A^{(0)},B^{(0)})
and
\Delta_{1}=\Delta_{1}^{(0)}
,
\Delta_{2}=\Delta_{2}^{(0)}
, one finds that

V_{1}(x)=V(x;A^{(1)},B^{(1)})-\alpha\left[(1+\alpha)\Delta_{1}^{(0)}-(1-\alpha)\Delta_{2}^{(0)}+\alpha\right],

(4.7)

where

\begin{split}A^{(1)}(A^{(1)}-1)+(B^{(1)})^{2}&=A^{(0)}(A^{(0)}-1)+(B^{(0)})^{2}+(1+\alpha)^{2}\Delta_{1}^{(0)}+(1-\alpha)^{2}\Delta_{2}^{(0)}\\
&\quad+1+\alpha^{2},\\
-B^{(1)}(2A^{(1)}-1)&=-B^{(0)}(2A^{(0)}-1)+(1+\alpha)^{2}\Delta_{1}^{(0)}-(1-\alpha)^{2}\Delta_{2}^{(0)}+2\alpha,\end{split}.

(4.8)

and
A^{(1)}
,
B^{(1)}
correspond to
\Delta_{1}^{(1)}=\Delta_{1}^{(0)}+1
,
\Delta_{2}^{(1)}=\Delta_{2}^{(0)}+1
. Hence, the potential
V_{0}(x)=V(x;A,B)
exhibits a deformed shape invariance property: up tp some additive constant, its partner
V_{1}(x)
is similar in shape and differs only in the parameters
A^{(1)}
,
B^{(1)}
that appear in it. The explicit form of
A^{(1)}
and
B^{(1)}
in terms of
A^{(0)}
and
B^{(0)}
is determined in the appendix.

Such a deformed shape invariance property enables us to construct a hierarchy of Hamiltonians

H_{i}=A^{+}_{\alpha}(A^{(i)},B^{(i)})A^{-}_{\alpha}(A^{(i)},B^{(i)})+\sum_{j=0}^{i}\varepsilon_{j},\qquad i=0,1,2,\ldots,

(4.9)

where

\begin{split}&A^{\pm}_{\alpha}(A^{(i)},B^{(i)})=\mp\sqrt{f}\frac{d}{dx}\sqrt{f}+W_{\alpha}(x;A^{(i)},B^{(i)}),\\
&\varepsilon_{0}=E^{(\alpha)}_{0}(A,B),\\
&\varepsilon_{i}=(1-\alpha^{2})(\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+2i)=2\lambda-2\alpha\mu+(1-\alpha^{2})(2i-1),\qquad i=1,2,\ldots,\end{split}

(4.10)

and

W_{\alpha}(x;A^{(i)},B^{(i)})=(\lambda+i)\tan x+(\mu+i\alpha)\sec x.

(4.11)

Such Hamiltonians are associated with a set of potentials
V_{i}(x)
,
i=0
, 1, 2, …, in such a way that

H_{i}=\pi_{x}^{2}+V_{i}(x),

(4.12)

where

V_{i}(x)=V(x;A^{(i)},B^{(i)})-i\alpha[\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}+\alpha(\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+i)].

(4.13)

One may also write

H_{i+1}=A^{-}_{\alpha}(A^{(i)},B^{(i)})A^{+}_{\alpha}(A^{(i)},B^{(i)})+\sum_{j=0}^{i}\varepsilon_{j}.

(4.14)

In other words, the first-order operators
A^{\pm}_{\alpha}(A^{(i)},B^{(i)})
fulfil a deformed shape invariance condition

\displaystyle A^{-}_{\alpha}(A^{(i)},B^{(i)})A^{+}_{\alpha}(A^{(i)},B^{(i)})=A^{+}_{\alpha}(A^{(i+1)},B^{(i+1)})A^{-}_{\alpha}(A^{(i+1)},B^{(i+1)})+\varepsilon_{i+1},

\displaystyle\qquad i=0,1,2,\ldots.

(4.15)

Finally, one may note that the energy eigenvalues of the starting Hamiltonian
H_{0}
can be directly found from the
\varepsilon_{i}
’s as

E^{(\alpha)}_{n}(A,B)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}.

(4.16)

3.16

3.16

6
Conclusion

In the present paper, we have shown that the problem of the Scarf I potential in a
m(\alpha:x)=(1+\alpha\sin x)^{-2}
background can be easily solved by mapping the corresponding Schrödinger equation onto that of the Scarf I potential with constant mass by means of a PCT. In addition, we have proved that the well-known shape invariance of the Scarf I potential in SUSYQM gives rise to a deformed shape invariance property for the considered PDM problem in deformed SUSYQM.

We have also taken advantage of the knowledge of rational extensions of the Scarf I potential connected with
X_{m}
-Jacobi EOPs of type I, II, and III to transform them into rational extensions for the corresponding PDM problem. These results have been analyzed in the deformed SUSYQM framework and the extended Scarf I potentials of type I and II have been shown to be endowed with a deformed shape invariance property.

Considering multi-indexed rational extensions and corresponding orthogonal polynomials would be a very interesting topic for future investigation.
