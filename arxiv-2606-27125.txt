The Ising Model Coupled to 2D Gravity: Critical Partition Function
Maurice Duits, Nathan Hayford, Seung-Yeop Lee

Abstract
We prove that the differential of the log of the partition function for the 2 2 -matrix model with quartic interactions converges in a certain double-scaling regime to the differential of the 𝝉 \bm{\tau} -function for the ( 3 , 4 ) (3,4) string equation. This confirms the convergence of the critical Ising model on random surfaces to the ( 3 , 4 ) (3,4) topological minimal model, which was stated in the works of Douglas and Shenker, Brézin and Kazakov, and Gross and Migdal. Our analysis is based on a steepest-descent analysis of a Riemann-Hilbert problem associated to a family of biorthogonal polynomials. New features in the matching problem in the construction of local parametrices appear.



1. Introduction

In this work, we will consider the integral

Z_{n}(\tau,t,H;N):=\iint\exp\bigg(N\tr\Phi(X,Y;\tau,t,H)\bigg)dXdY,

(1.1)

where each integration is carried out over the space of
n\times n
Hermitian matrices, and

\Phi(x,y;\tau,t,H):=\tau xy-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}-\frac{te^{H}}{4}x^{4}-\frac{1}{2}y^{2}-\frac{te^{-H}}{4}y^{4}.

(1.2)

This integral is called the
partition function
for the
2
-matrix model, and is well-defined when
0<\tau<1
,
t>0
, and
H\in\RR
. For given
N>0
, the partition function is an analytic function of
t
, and as such can be analytically continued to the complex
t
plane, with a branch point at
t=0
. We are particularly interested in
Z_{n}
when
t<0
. We recall from
[
23
]
that the
genus
0
free energy:

F_{0}(\tau,t,H):=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\log\frac{Z_{N}(\tau,t,H;N)}{Z_{N}(\tau,0,0;N)},

(1.3)

also admits an analytic continuation to
t<0
, up to a special critical surface (shown in Figures
1.1
,
1.2
), and undergoes a
3^{rd}
order phase transition at the point

\tau=\tau_{c}:=\frac{1}{4},\qquad t=t_{c}:=-\frac{5}{72},\qquad H=H_{c}:=0,

(1.4)

which confirms the conjectured results of Kazakov and Boulatov
[
37
,
12
]
.
Our main result concerns how, in a special scaling limit around this point,
\log Z_{n}
converges to the
\bm{\tau}
-function for the
(3,4)
string equation
, which is a higher order Painlevé-type equation.

The relation between matrix integrals like (
1.1
) and Painlevé equations (which we shall refer to as
critical phenomena
) has been studied extensively for almost 40 years. The interest in this relation stemmed from the connection of these critical phenomena to the minimal models of conformal field theory coupled to topological gravity. In the mid-80s, Kazakov and Boulatov
[
37
,
12
]
demonstrated that matrix models held promise in predicting what came to be known as the
Kniznik Polyakov Zamolodchikov (KPZ) scaling relations
[
38
]
. Several years later, the seminal works of Douglas and Shenker
[
21
]
, Brézin and Kazakov

[
13
]
, and Gross and Migdal
[
33
,
34
]
, showed how in certain ‘double scaling’ limits, the partition function for a given matrix model will converge
to a
\bm{\tau}
-function for the corresponding Painlevé equation, which could then be thought of as a partition function for the corresponding ‘topological minimal model’. These ideas were developed more analytically in the works of Fokas, Its, and Kitaev
[
28
,
29
]
, where the importance of the isomonodromy approach was elucidated in the case of the
1
-matrix model. Also introduced in their work was a
Riemann-Hilbert problem
(RHP) for the associated orthogonal polynomials, which allowed for rigorous asymptotic analysis via the Deift-Zhou method
[
19
]
. These results caused a surge in activity in the study of random matrices, Painlevé equations, and orthogonal polynomials. The techniques introduced in
[
19
]
were applied soon thereafter to prove local universality results for the Hermitian ensembles
[
18
,
40
]
. Using these techniques, analytical analyses related to the appearance of Painlevé equations, in particular Painlevé II, were found by Bleher and Its
[
11
]
, and were shown to be universal by Claeys and Kuijlaars
[
17
]
. Results on the Painlevé I critical point of
[
28
,
29
]
were finally obtained by Duits and Kuijlaars
[
25
]
, and later on by Bleher and Deaño
[
8
]
(see also the more recent
[
7
,
9
]
). However, a rigorous analysis of a critical phenomena in the
2
-matrix model as studied in the early physics literature was never performed, although new critical phenomenon related to Painlevé II in the
2
-matrix model were indeed studied by Duits and Geudens
[
22
]
.

This kind of analysis also attracted interest in enumerative combinatorics. It can be shown that, as
N\to\infty
, provided

are sufficiently small,

\frac{1}{N^{2}}\log\frac{Z_{N}(\tau,t,H;N)}{Z_{N}(\tau,0,0;N)}\sim\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}\frac{F_{g}(\tau,t,H)}{N^{2g}},

(1.5)

F_{g}(\tau,t,H)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{Z}_{n,g}(\tau,H)\left(\frac{-t\tau}{4(1-\tau^{2})^{2}}\right)^{n},\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathcal{Z}_{n,g}(\tau,H)=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{g,n}}Z_{\pi}(\tau,H),

(1.6)

and where

is the set of all
connected, labeled,
4

[

for the exact details of this correspondence). By tuning the parameters

, one is able to study the behavior of the critical Ising model on quadrangulations (which are dual to
4
-regular maps) of genus

with infinite vertices were carried out by the groups of Albenque, Ménard, and Schaeffer
[

[

1.1.
(3,4)
string equation & tau-function

Our main result is given in terms of a special solution to the
(3,4)
string equation
. This equation reads

\begin{cases}0=\frac{1}{2}V^{\prime\prime}-\frac{3}{2}UV+\frac{5}{2}\eta V+\mu,\\
0=\frac{1}{12}U^{(4)}-\frac{3}{4}U^{\prime\prime}U-\frac{3}{8}(U^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{3}{2}V^{2}+\frac{1}{2}U^{3}-\frac{5}{12}\eta\left(3U^{2}-U^{\prime\prime}\right)+\nu,\end{cases}

(1.7)

where
U=U(\eta,\mu,\nu)
,
V=V(\eta,\mu,\nu)
are meromorphic functions of the variables
(\eta,\mu,\nu)
, and
{}^{\prime}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}
. This string equation is augmented by a collection of compatible PDEs, which govern the dependence of
U,V
on
\eta,\mu
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial U}{\partial\mu}

\displaystyle=-2V^{\prime},

(1.8)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial V}{\partial\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}U^{\prime\prime\prime}-UU^{\prime},

(1.9)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial U}{\partial\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}\left[-\frac{1}{6}UU^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{8}(U^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{1}{4}U^{3}-\frac{1}{2}V^{2}-\frac{5}{9}\eta\left(3U^{2}-U^{\prime\prime}\right)+\frac{4}{3}\nu\right],

(1.10)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial V}{\partial\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}\left[\frac{1}{12}U^{\prime\prime}V-\frac{1}{4}U^{\prime}V^{\prime}+\frac{5}{16}U^{2}V-\left(\frac{5}{3}\eta+\frac{1}{4}U\right)^{2}V-\mu U\right].

(1.11)

Although these equations look quite complicated, it has been shown that:

Proposition 1.1

(
[
35
]
, Theorem 1.1)
.

Under the identification

\nu=t_{1},\qquad\qquad\mu=t_{2},\qquad\qquad\eta=t_{5},

there exist polynomials
H_{1},H_{2},H_{5}
in the variables
(P_{1},P_{2},P_{3},Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3};t_{1},t_{2},t_{5})
such that the equations

\frac{\partial H_{a}}{\partial P_{k}}=\frac{\partial Q_{k}}{\partial t_{a}},\qquad\qquad-\frac{\partial H_{a}}{\partial Q_{k}}=\frac{\partial P_{k}}{\partial t_{a}},

(1.12)

a=1,2,5
,
k=1,2,3
are equivalent to the equations (
1.7
), (
1.8
)–(
1.11
). Moreover, for any
a,b=1,2,5
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\{H_{a},H_{b}\}:=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\left(\frac{\partial H_{a}}{\partial P_{k}}\frac{\partial H_{b}}{\partial Q_{k}}-\frac{\partial H_{a}}{\partial Q_{k}}\frac{\partial H_{b}}{\partial P_{k}}\right),

(1.13)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial H_{a}}{\partial t_{b}}-\frac{\partial H_{b}}{\partial t_{a}}.

(1.14)

In other words, the
(3,4)
string equation can be characterized as a completely integrable (non-autonomous) Hamiltonian system. The explicit form of these Hamiltonians is given in Appendix
B
, both in the Darboux coordinates and evaluated on a solution to the string equation. An important observation is that one can define the following
closed
differential

{\bf d}\log\bm{\tau}(\eta,\mu,\nu):=H_{1}d\nu+H_{2}d\mu+H_{5}d\eta,

(1.15)

whose primitive we refer to as the
\bm{\tau}
-function. This function is well-defined up to a multiplicative constant.

Another important characterization of Equation (
1.7
) is as the set of isomonodromy deformations of a certain linear differential equation with rational coefficients, or alternatively as the solution to a certain Riemann-Hilbert problem, which is given in Appendix
B
. This characterization was demonstrated in
[
35
]
, and in
[
36
]
, a special solution to this equation was shown to exist asymptotically for a particular set of
Stokes multipliers
:

\displaystyle s_{1}

\displaystyle=0,\qquad s_{2}=-1,\qquad s_{3}=0,\qquad s_{4}=0,\qquad s_{5}=1,\qquad s_{6}=-1,\qquad s_{7}=0,

\displaystyle s_{-1}

\displaystyle=0,\qquad s_{-2}=1,\qquad s_{-3}=-1,\qquad s_{-4}=0,\qquad s_{-5}=0,\qquad s_{-6}=1,\qquad s_{-7}=0.

(1.16)

More precisely, the solution to this model RHP with the above Stokes data (which indeed appears in the present work) was shown to be asymptotically free of singularities in a particular sector of the
(\eta,\mu,\nu)
-space in
[
36
]
. In a sense, the solution studied there is analogous to the
tritronquée
solution of Painlevé I:

u^{\prime\prime}(x)=6u(x)^{2}+x,

(1.17)

which appears in the study of the
1
-matrix model. Indeed, it was also demonstrated in
[
36
]
that
\bm{\tau}(\eta,\mu,\nu)
limits to the tau function for this solution of the Painlevé I equation in a certain regime.

The main result of this work can now be stated slightly more precisely:
in a certain double-scaling regime, the partition function (
1.1
) converges to
\bm{\tau}(\eta,\mu,\nu)
.
Of course, since we have only defined
\bm{\tau}(\eta,\mu,\nu)
up to a multiplicative constant, we can only state this convergence in terms of the differential of the partition function.

Remark 1.1
.

The (3,4) minimal model coupled to gravity.

It is by now folklore that the Ising critical point is characterized by the
(3,4)
minimal model of conformal field theory
[
31
]
. This field theory has central charge
c=\frac{1}{2}
, and contains operators
\mathbb{I}
,
\epsilon
, and
\sigma
,
which we refer to as the
identity
,
thermal
, and
spin
operators, respectively. These operators have scaling dimensions

\Delta_{\mathbb{I}}=0,\qquad\qquad\Delta_{\epsilon}=\frac{1}{2},\qquad\qquad\Delta_{\sigma}=\frac{1}{16}.

(1.18)

The KPZ formula
[
38
]
provides a formula for how the scaling dimensions change upon coupling to quantum gravity:

\hat{\Delta}_{\bullet}=\frac{\sqrt{1-c+24\Delta_{\bullet}}-\sqrt{1-c}}{\sqrt{25-c}-\sqrt{1-c}},\qquad\qquad\bullet\in\{\mathbb{I},\epsilon,\sigma\}

(1.19)

The “dressed” weights are

\hat{\Delta}_{\mathbb{I}}=0,\qquad\qquad\hat{\Delta}_{\epsilon}=\frac{2}{3},\qquad\qquad\hat{\Delta}_{\sigma}=\frac{1}{6}.

(1.20)

The parameters
\eta,\mu,\nu
‘couple’ to the operators
\epsilon,\sigma,\mathbb{I}
, respectively, in the sense that correlation functions of the fields
\epsilon,\sigma,\mathbb{I}
may be computed by taking appropriate derivatives of the
\bm{\tau}
-function.

1.3. Associated biorthogonal polynomials & Riemann-Hilbert problem

The analysis of the partition function (
1.1
) and its multiscaling limit is made possible through its connection to a family of

biorthogonal polynomials
. Let us briefly summarize this connection; we refer the reader to
[
23
]
Section 1.2 for details. We define
two families of monic polynomials
\{P_{n}(z)\}
,
\{Q_{n}(w)\}
via the relation

\int_{\Gamma_{t}}\int_{\Gamma_{t}}P_{n}(z)Q_{m}(w)\exp\left[N\Phi(z,w;\tau,t,H)\right]dzdw=h_{n}(\tau,t,H;N)\delta_{nm},

(1.28)

where
\Gamma_{t}:=\{xe^{i\theta}\mid x\in\RR,\theta=\arg(t^{-1/4})\}
, and is given the orientation which increasing with
x
. The partition function (
1.1
) is related to these polynomials via the relation

Z_{n}(\tau,t,H;N)=n!\frac{C_{n,N}}{\tau^{-\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}}\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}h_{k}(\tau,t,H;N),

(1.29)

where
C_{n,N}
is a constant depending only on
n,N
, which is not important to us. Note that the right hand side of the above expression makes sense
for complex
t
, and thus can be used as a definition for the analytic continuation of
Z_{n}
. We are particularly interested in the regime when
t<0
, and so from here on we fix
t
to be negative, and take as a definition
\Gamma_{t}:=\Gamma
to be the contour starting from
e^{3\pi i/4}\cdot\infty
and ending at
e^{-\pi i/4}\cdot\infty
, which corresponds to analytic continuation of
Z_{n}
through the upper half-plane. Let us observe that we can redefine
\Gamma
in any compact subset of
\CC
and still obtain the same family of biorthogonal polynomials (and thus the same analytic continuation). We will make use of this fact later on.

We will make use of some of the special properties of the biorthogonal polynomials
\{P_{n}(z)\}
,
\{Q_{n}(w)\}
. These polynomials satisfy finite-term recurrence relations
[
43
,
37
]
:

\displaystyle zP_{n}(z)=P_{n+1}(z)+R_{n}P_{n-1}(z)+S_{n}P_{n-3}(z),

(1.30)

\displaystyle wQ_{n}(w)=Q_{n+1}(w)+\tilde{R}_{n}Q_{n-1}(w)+\tilde{S}_{n}Q_{n-3}(w),

(1.31)

and

\displaystyle R_{n}=R_{n}(\tau,t,H;N),\qquad

\displaystyle\qquad S_{n}=S_{n}(\tau,t,H;N),

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{n}(\tau,t,H;N)=R_{n}(\tau,t,-H;N),\qquad

\displaystyle\qquad\tilde{S}_{n}(\tau,t,H;N)=S_{n}(\tau,t,-H;N)

are the
recurrence coefficients
. We also frequently encounter the quantity

f_{n}=f_{n}(\tau,t,H;N):=\frac{h_{n}(\tau,t,H;N)}{h_{n-1}(\tau,t,H;N)},

(1.32)

which we will also refer to as a recurrence coefficient. As is well-known (cf.
[
43
,
37
,
12
]
), these recurrence coefficients satisfy
the following non-linear polynomial equations:

\displaystyle\tau S_{n}

\displaystyle=te^{-H}f_{n}f_{n-1}f_{n-2},

(1.33)

\displaystyle\tau\tilde{S}_{n}

\displaystyle=te^{H}f_{n}f_{n-1}f_{n-2},

(1.34)

\displaystyle\tau R_{n}

\displaystyle=f_{n}[1+te^{-H}(\tilde{R}_{n+1}+\tilde{R}_{n}+\tilde{R}_{n-1})],

\displaystyle=N\tau e^{N\Phi(z,w)}\left[-\frac{P_{n-1}(z)Q_{n}(w)}{h_{n-1}}+\frac{\tilde{R}_{n}P_{n}(z)Q_{n-1}(w)}{h_{n}}+\sum_{j=0}^{2}\frac{\tilde{S}_{n+j}P_{n+j}(z)Q_{n+j-3}(w)}{h_{n+j}}\right],

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial w}K_{n}(z,w)

\displaystyle=N\tau e^{N\Phi(z,w)}\left[-\frac{P_{n}(z)Q_{n-1}(w)}{h_{n-1}}+\frac{R_{n}P_{n-1}(z)Q_{n}(w)}{h_{n}}+\sum_{j=0}^{2}\frac{S_{n+j}P_{n+j-3}(z)Q_{n+j}(w)}{h_{n+j}}\right].

Proof.

We only prove the first identity; the second follows from similar arguments. Let
V(z;t):=\frac{1}{2}z^{2}+\frac{t}{4}z^{4}
,
and define functions

\psi_{n}(z)=\frac{P_{n}(z)}{h_{n-1}}e^{-NV(z;e^{H}t)},\qquad\qquad\phi_{n}(w)=Q_{n}(w)e^{-NV(z;e^{-H}t)},

(2.13)

which satisfy

\iint\psi_{n}(z)\phi_{n}(w)e^{N\tau zw}dzdw=\delta_{nm}.

(2.14)

Then, define the semi-infinite vectors

\Psi(z):=\langle\psi_{0}(z),\psi_{1}(z),\cdots\rangle^{T},\qquad\qquad\Phi(w):=\langle\phi_{0}(w),\phi_{1}(w),\cdots\rangle^{T},

(2.15)

and the semi-infinite matrix

\Pi_{n}:=\text{diag }(\underbrace{1,\cdots,1}_{n},0,\cdots),\qquad\qquad\Pi_{n}\psi_{j}(z)=\psi_{j}(z)\cdot\bm{1}_{j<n},\qquad\Pi_{n}\phi_{j}(w)=\phi_{j}(w)\cdot\bm{1}_{j<n}.

(2.16)

With this notation, we can write
K_{n}(z,w)
as

K_{n}(z,w)=e^{N\tau zw}\Psi^{T}(z)\Pi_{n}\Phi(w).

There exist matrices
A,B
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\Psi(z)

\displaystyle=A\Psi(z),\qquad\qquad z\psi_{j}(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}A_{jk}\psi_{k}(z),

\displaystyle w\Phi(w)

\displaystyle=B\Phi(w),\qquad\qquad w\phi_{j}(w)=\sum_{k\geq 0}B_{jk}\phi_{k}(w).

Let us compute the matrix element
B_{jk}
. Integrating by parts,

\displaystyle B_{jk}

\displaystyle=\iint\psi_{j}(z)w\phi_{k}(w)e^{N\tau zw}dzdw=\frac{1}{N\tau}\iint\psi_{j}(z)\phi_{k}(w)\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(e^{N\tau zw}\right)dzdw

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N\tau}\iint\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\psi_{j}(z)\right)\phi_{k}(w)e^{N\tau zw}dzdw=-\frac{1}{N\tau}A_{kj}.

In other words,

B=-\frac{1}{N\tau}A^{T}.

(2.17)

So,

\displaystyle\left(w+\frac{1}{N\tau}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)\Psi^{T}(z)\Pi_{n}\Phi(w)

\displaystyle=\Psi^{T}(z)\Pi_{n}B\Phi(w)+\frac{1}{N\tau}\Psi^{T}(z)A^{T}\Pi_{n}\Phi(w)=\Psi^{T}(z)\left[\Pi_{n},B\right]\Phi(w),

where
\left[\Pi_{n},B\right]:=\Pi_{n}B-B\Pi_{n}
is the matrix commutator. It follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}K_{n}(z,w)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(e^{N\tau zw}\Psi^{T}(z)\Pi_{n}\Phi(w)\right)=N\tau e^{N\tau zw}\left(w+\frac{1}{N\tau}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)\Psi^{T}(z)\Pi_{n}\Phi(w)

\displaystyle=N\tau e^{N\tau zw}\Psi^{T}(z)\left[\Pi_{n},B\right]\Phi(w).

Now, the matrix
B
can be computed by appealing to the recurrence relation for the polynomials
Q_{n}(w)
:

B_{jk}=\begin{cases}1,&j=k+1,\\
\tilde{R}_{k},&j=k-1,\\
\tilde{S}_{k},&j=k-3,\\
0,&\textit{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2.18)

So, we obtain that

\frac{\partial}{\partial z}K_{n}(z,w)=N\tau e^{N\tau zw}\left[-\psi_{n-1}(z)\phi_{n}(w)+\tilde{R}_{n}\psi_{n}(z)\phi_{n-1}(w)+\sum_{j=0}^{2}\tilde{S}_{n+j}\psi_{n+j}(z)\phi_{n+j-3}(w)\right].

Re-expressing
\psi_{k}(z)
,
\phi_{k}(w)
in terms of the biorthogonal polynomials completes the proof.
∎

We now make use of this formula to prove Proposition
2.2
.

Proof.

Proposition
2.2
: Formula (
2.5
)
.
Obviously, we have that
\Omega_{\tau}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\log Z_{n}(\tau,t,H;N)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\log Z_{n}(\tau,t,H;N)

\displaystyle=\frac{n(n-1)}{2\tau}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\log h_{n}(\tau,t,H;N)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n(n-1)}{2\tau}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{h_{k}}\iint P_{k}(z)Q_{k}(w)\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}e^{N\Phi(z,w)}dzdw

\displaystyle=\frac{n(n-1)}{2\tau}+\iint\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{P_{k}(z)Q_{k}(w)}{h_{k}}\right)Nxye^{N\Phi(z,w)}dzdw

\displaystyle=\frac{n(n-1)}{2\tau}+\iint\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{N}{2}x^{2}y\right)K_{n}(x,y)dzdw

\displaystyle=\frac{n(n-1)}{2\tau}-\frac{N}{2}\iint x^{2}y\frac{\partial}{\partial x}K_{n}(x,y)dzdw.

By Lemma
2.2
, we can rewrite

in terms of a finite number of biorthogonal polynomials. By applying the recurrence relations (

which can be evaluated explicitly by biorthogonality.
∎

In principle, one can apply identical calculations to obtain formulae for

We consider the following alternative family of monic biorthogonal polynomials:

where

\hat{\Phi}(x,y,\varsigma,\mathfrak{t},h):=\varsigma xy-\frac{e^{-h}}{2}\mathfrak{t}x^{2}-\frac{e^{h}}{2}\mathfrak{t}y^{2}-\frac{1}{4}x^{4}-\frac{1}{4}y^{4}.

These biorthogonal polynomials satisfy the recurrence relations

xp_{n}(x)=p_{n+1}(x)+r_{n}p_{n-1}(x)+s_{n}p_{n-3}(x),\qquad yq_{n}(y)=q_{n+1}(y)+\tilde{r}_{n}q_{n-1}(y)+\tilde{s}_{n}q_{n-3}(y).

We write
\mathfrak{h}_{n}=\mathfrak{h}_{n}(\varsigma,\mathfrak{t},h;N)
to denote the dependence
of
\mathfrak{h}_{n}
on the parameters of this model, and similarly for the recurrence coefficients. We can in fact relate the recurrence coefficients and norming constants of these polynomials to those of the ones define by (
1.28

to obtain a simpler formula for the logarithmic derivatives of the partition function. To this end, we present the following lemma:

Lemma 2.3
.

The following relations between the norming constants
\mathfrak{h}_{n}
and

\displaystyle h_{n}(\tau,t,H;N)=t^{-(n+1)/2}\mathfrak{h}_{n}\left(\tau t^{-1/2},t^{-1/2},\frac{1}{2}H;N\right).

(2.20)

Furthermore, the following relations between the recurrence coefficients hold:

\displaystyle R_{n}(\tau,t,H)

\displaystyle=t^{-1/2}e^{-H/2}r_{n}\left(\tau t^{-1/2},t^{-1/2},\frac{1}{2}H\right),\quad\tilde{R}_{n}(\tau,t,H)=t^{-1/2}e^{H/2}\tilde{r}_{n}\left(\tau t^{-1/2},t^{-1/2},\frac{1}{2}H\right),

(2.21)

\displaystyle S_{n}(\tau,t,H)

\displaystyle=e^{-H}t^{-1}s_{n}\left(\tau t^{-1/2},t^{-1/2},\frac{1}{2}H\right),\quad\tilde{S}_{n}(\tau,t,H)=e^{H}t^{-1}\tilde{s}_{n}\left(\tau t^{-1/2},t^{-1/2},\frac{1}{2}H\right).

(2.22)

Proof.

The proof of each of the above identities follows from a simple change of variables argument. For instance, by substituting

\displaystyle\varsigma=\tau t^{-1/2},\quad\mathfrak{t}=t^{-1/2},\quad h=\frac{1}{2}H,\qquad x=t^{1/4}e^{H/4}z,\quad y=t^{1/4}e^{-H/4}w

in the definition of
\mathfrak{h}_{n}

\mathfrak{h}_{n}\left(\tau t^{-1/2},t^{-1/2},\frac{1}{2}H;N\right)=t^{(n+1)/2}\int P_{n}(z)Q_{n}(w)e^{N\Phi(z,w;\tau,t,H)}dzdw=t^{(n+1)/2}h_{n}(\tau,t,H;N),

which yields the first identity.
∎

We additionally will need a formula for the derivatives of
\mathfrak{h}_{n}
in

and

Lemma 2.4
.

The following identities hold:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathfrak{t}}\log\mathfrak{h}_{k}(\varsigma,\mathfrak{t},h;N)=-\frac{N}{2}\left[e^{-h}(r_{k}+r_{k+1})+e^{h}(\tilde{r}_{k}+\tilde{r}_{k+1})\right],

We sketch the proof of the first identity only; the second follows from identical arguments. By direct calculation,

We also establish the following lemma, which will be useful later on in exchanging
n

Lemma 2.5

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[A_{n}+nr_{n-1}\right],

\displaystyle=Ne^{-h}\mathfrak{t}s_{n}+N[r_{n}r_{n-1}r_{n-2}+r_{n}s_{n-1}+(r_{n+1}+r_{n}+r_{n-2}+r_{n-3})s_{n}+r_{n-2}s_{n+1}].

(2.26)

We prove the ‘untilded’ identities; the identities with tildes follow from identical calculations. First, observe that, using the recurrence relations for the biorthogonal polynomials, at order

\chi_{n+1}=\chi_{n}+r_{n}.

On the other hand, let us write

\displaystyle p_{n}^{\prime}(x)=np_{n-1}(x)+A_{n}p_{n-3}(x)+\OO(x^{n-5}).

Since we can express the monomials

\displaystyle x^{n-1}=p_{n-1}+\chi_{n-1}p_{n-3}+\OO(x^{n-5}),\qquad\qquad x^{n-3}=p_{n-3}+\OO(x^{n-5}),

we find that

\displaystyle p_{n}^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=nx^{n-1}-(n-2)\chi_{n}x^{n-3}+\OO(x^{n-5})

\displaystyle=n\left(p_{n-1}+\chi_{n-1}p_{n-3}+\OO(x^{n-5})\right)-(n-2)\chi_{n}\left(p_{n-3}+\OO(x^{n-5})\right)+\OO(x^{n-5})

\displaystyle=np_{n-1}+\left(n\chi_{n-1}-(n-2)\chi_{n}\right)p_{n-3}+\OO(x^{n-5})

\displaystyle=np_{n-1}+\left(2\chi_{n}-nr_{n-1}\right)p_{n-3}+\OO(x^{n-5}),

\displaystyle A_{n}\mathfrak{h}_{n-3}

which completes the proof.
∎
