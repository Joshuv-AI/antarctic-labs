Astragaloside IV in hepatocellular carcinoma: repolarizing macrophages to reprogram the tumor microenvironment

Peng Ma, Xin-da Lu, Jian-li Han, Jia-jia Du

Abstract

The establishment of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) represents a central mechanism underlying the failure of conventional therapies and resistance to immunotherapy in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), as the most abundant immune cell population within this microenvironment, play a pivotal role in establishing immunosuppression, promoting angiogenesis, and driving therapeutic resistance through their polarization toward the M2 phenotype. Astragaloside IV (AS-IV), the principal bioactive constituent of the traditional Chinese medicine Astragalus membranaceus, has recently been recognized for its remarkable immunomodulatory properties. This review systematically summarizes recent advances in understanding the multi-dimensional mechanisms through which AS-IV modulates M2 macrophage polarization. At the signaling pathway level, AS-IV undermines the molecular basis of macrophage polarization by inhibiting signal transducer and activator of transcription 3/6 (STAT3/STAT6) phosphorylation and modulating phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B/mammalian target of rapamycin (PI3K/Akt/mTOR) and AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) signaling cascades. At the metabolic level, it influences the metabolic reprogramming of macrophages, thereby altering their functional phenotypes. At the epigenetic level, it may interfere with processes such as histone modifications, thereby attenuating the “memory” of M2 polarization. This multi-layered regulation promotes the reprogramming of TAMs from a pro-tumorigenic M2 phenotype toward an anti-tumor M1-like phenotype, thereby systematically remodeling the HCC immune microenvironment—restoring effector T cell function, reducing immunosuppressive cell infiltration, inhibiting angiogenesis, and ameliorating the fibrotic barrier. This review aims to provide a systematic framework for understanding the immunomodulatory mechanisms of AS-IV and offer a theoretical foundation for the development of novel natural product-based immunotherapeutic strategies for HCC.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), a highly prevalent malignant tumor associated with substantial morbidity and mortality worldwide, represents a major global public health challenge. According to recent global cancer statistics, HCC accounts for approximately 800,000 deaths annually, ranking as the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally (). Despite remarkable advances in HCC treatment strategies over the past decade—including refinements in surgical techniques, optimization of local ablation modalities, and the emergence of systemic therapies represented by immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs)—the overall prognosis of patients remains dismal, with a 5-year survival rate hovering around 18% (). The core of this therapeutic dilemma lies in the high heterogeneity and complex biological characteristics of HCC, particularly the profoundly immunosuppressive microenvironment it establishes.

The HCC tumor microenvironment (TME) constitutes a dynamic ecosystem comprising diverse cell types and soluble mediators. Within this system, the functional state of immune cells dictates the tumor’s immune response profile and therapeutic responsiveness. Among these, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs)—the most abundant population of tumor-infiltrating immune cells, accounting for approximately 30%–50% of the total immune infiltrate—exert a critical and dual regulatory role in governing tumor immune responses (). Under normal physiological conditions, macrophages possess the capacity to recognize and eliminate abnormal cells; however, under the continuous shaping of the TME, they predominantly polarize toward the M2 phenotype, alternatively termed the “alternatively activated” phenotype. These M2-type TAMs not only lose their anti-tumor functions but also actively establish and maintain an immunosuppressive state by secreting immunosuppressive factors such as interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), expressing immune checkpoint molecules such as programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1), and depleting arginine in the microenvironment through metabolic reprogramming—thereby promoting tumor growth, angiogenesis, invasion, and metastasis (). Numerous clinical studies have confirmed that high-density infiltration of M2-type macrophages in tumor tissue is significantly associated with poor prognosis, increased risk of early recurrence, and shorter overall survival in HCC patients, rendering them an attractive therapeutic target ().

Nevertheless, the clinical translation of TAM-targeted strategies faces substantial challenges. Traditional approaches, such as macrophage depletion or blockade of their recruitment, often yield limited efficacy and may compromise physiological macrophage functions (). Consequently, the development of strategies capable of precisely reprogramming TAM polarization states—driving their transition from the pro-tumorigenic M2 phenotype toward the anti-tumor M1-like phenotype—has emerged as a cutting-edge Frontier in tumor immunotherapy research in recent years (). This “re-education” rather than “depletion” strategy aims to restore immune surveillance function within the TME while avoiding systemic immune toxicity.

Against this backdrop, natural compounds derived from traditional medicine have regained considerable attention owing to their multi-target actions, relatively favorable safety profiles, and long history of clinical application (). Astragaloside IV (AS-IV), one of the principal bioactive constituents extracted from the dried roots of Astragalus membranaceus, belongs to the cycloartane-type tetracyclic triterpenoid saponins. Previous pharmacological research has primarily focused on its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, cardioprotective, and direct anti-proliferative effects on tumor cells (). However, with advances in immunometabolism and tumor immunology research, accumulating evidence has revealed that AS-IV possesses significant immunomodulatory functions, particularly the ability to precisely modulate the macrophage polarization process. In the context of HCC, AS-IV has demonstrated unique potential in ameliorating the immunosuppressive microenvironment by regulating TAM function ().

Despite its promising immunomodulatory properties, the clinical translation of AS-IV is constrained by its suboptimal pharmacokinetic profile. Following oral administration, AS-IV exhibits low systemic bioavailability (approximately 7.4% in rats) due to poor aqueous solubility and limited intestinal permeability (). Furthermore, its distribution to liver tissue, while detectable, may not achieve therapeutically effective concentrations without dose escalation or formulation optimization. These exposure limitations represent a critical translational barrier that must be addressed through advanced delivery strategies.

This review aims to systematically elucidate and comprehensively explore the molecular mechanisms, functional effects, and clinical translation prospects of AS-IV in remodeling the immunosuppressive microenvironment of HCC by regulating TAM polarization. Through this systematic analysis, we aim to provide a solid theoretical foundation and novel insights for the development of AS-IV-based immunotherapeutic strategies for HCC.

Biological characteristics and functions of M2-type macrophages in the HCC immune microenvironment

Polarization drivers and heterogeneity

The HCC microenvironment drives monocyte recruitment to tumor sites and their subsequent polarization toward the M2 phenotype through the secretion of a diverse array of chemokines and cytokines, forming a complex signaling network (). Among these, the chemokine C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2) and its receptor C-C chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2) play a central role in monocyte recruitment (). CCL2 secreted by tumor cells and stromal cells establishes a concentration gradient that guides peripheral blood monocytes across the endothelial barrier and into tumor tissue. Additionally, CXCL12 (stromal cell-derived factor-1α) also participates in this process through its receptor C-X-C chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4) (). Once within the TME, these monocytes undergo polarization under the continuous stimulation of specific cytokines.

Key cytokines driving M2 polarization include colony-stimulating factor-1 (CSF-1), interleukin-4 (IL-4), IL-10, and interleukin-13 (IL-13). CSF-1 not only promotes monocyte survival and proliferation but also induces the expression of M2-associated genes through activation of downstream signaling pathways such as phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt) (). IL-4 and IL-13 are classical M2 polarization inducers; they upregulate the expression of M2 markers such as arginase-1 (Arg1) and mannose receptor through activation of the Janus kinase 1/signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (JAK1/STAT6) signaling axis (). IL-10, conversely, induces macrophages to produce more IL-10 via activation of the STAT3 pathway, forming a self-reinforcing immunosuppressive loop (). These signaling pathways do not operate independently but rather constitute a cross-talking network that collectively determines the final macrophage phenotype.

In recent years, the application of single-cell RNA sequencing technology has profoundly advanced our understanding of TAM heterogeneity. Studies have demonstrated that TAMs in HCC do not represent a homogeneous M2 population but rather a continuous spectrum encompassing multiple functional subsets. For instance, Zheng et al. identified at least three major macrophage subsets in HCC via single-cell analysis: 1) TREM2+ lipid-associated macrophages, which highly express lipid metabolism-related genes and may be associated with tumor lipid accumulation and immunosuppression; 2) SPP1+ macrophages, which highly express osteopontin and are implicated in tissue repair, fibrosis, and immune regulation; and 3) CXCL10+ macrophages, which express higher levels of chemokines and may possess enhanced immune cell recruitment potential (). This spatiotemporal heterogeneity suggests that targeted therapies may require precise design tailored to distinct tumor stages and individual patient characteristics.

HCC-specific features of the macrophage microenvironment

While many aspects of M2-TAM biology are shared across solid tumors, the hepatic microenvironment confers several unique features that distinguish HCC-associated macrophages from those in other malignancies. These HCC-specific characteristics have important implications for the design and interpretation of macrophage-targeted therapies, including AS-IV-based strategies.

Chronic inflammation–fibrosis–carcinogenesis axis

A defining feature of HCC is that most cases arise on a background of chronic hepatitis, fibrosis, and cirrhosis. Activated hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) secrete abundant TGF-β, IL-6, and ECM proteins that promote and maintain M2-like TAM polarization (). Unlike most other solid tumors, the fibrotic liver thus provides a pre-existing immunosuppressive niche established long before tumor initiation. This has two major implications: the macrophage-suppressive network is embedded in a chronically sustained wound-healing program, and therapeutic strategies aimed solely at TAMs may be insufficient because HSCs and the fibrotic stroma continuously replenish M2-polarizing signals. For AS-IV, this suggests that maximal efficacy may require concurrent targeting of HSC activation and fibrotic stroma remodeling. The reported anti-fibrotic activity of AS-IV in hepatic fibrosis models therefore represents an important, yet underexplored, component of its anti-TAM activity in HCC ().

Gut–liver axis

HCC patients frequently exhibit gut microbiota dysbiosis, increased intestinal permeability, and elevated portal vein levels of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (). The liver is uniquely exposed to gut-derived microbial products via the portal circulation, and LPS signaling through TLR4/NF-κB in hepatic macrophages drives a distinctive activation state that reinforces the M2-like phenotype. This sustained low-grade endotoxemia contributes to the immunosuppressive microenvironment and may blunt immunotherapy efficacy. Preliminary evidence indicates that AS-IV can protect the intestinal barrier and modulate gut microbiota composition (); however, whether these gut-directed effects contribute to its anti-TAM activity in HCC is unknown. Investigating whether AS-IV reduces portal LPS levels and thereby dampens TLR4-driven macrophage polarization represents an important future direction.

Abnormal bile acid metabolism

Bile acids are dysregulated in chronic liver disease and HCC, functioning not only as detergents but also as signaling molecules through FXR and TGR5 (). TGR5 is expressed on macrophages, and its activation by bile acids suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokine production while promoting an M2-like, tissue-protective phenotype. Sustained TGR5 signaling may therefore reinforce the immunosuppressive macrophage compartment in HCC. Whether AS-IV modulates bile acid composition or directly interferes with TGR5/FXR signaling in TAMs has not been investigated and constitutes a significant knowledge gap. This is particularly relevant because bile acid profiles vary considerably depending on HCC etiology (viral, metabolic, alcohol-related), potentially creating patient-specific differences in macrophage polarization pressure.

Kupffer cells versus monocyte-derived TAMs

A critical feature of the hepatic macrophage compartment is its dual developmental origin. Liver-resident Kupffer cells (KCs), originating from yolk-sac precursors, are radio-resistant, self-renewing, and express high levels of CD163 and TIM-4. In contrast, monocyte-derived TAMs (Mo-TAMs) recruited via CCL2/CCR2 signaling tend to express CCR2, CD11b, and higher levels of PD-L1 (). Functionally, Mo-TAMs are generally considered the more immunosuppressive and pro-tumorigenic subset, whereas KCs may retain tissue-protective and phagocytic functions. Whether AS-IV differentially regulates these two populations is currently unknown. This distinction carries direct therapeutic relevance: CD206/CD163-targeted nanocarriers—commonly proposed for macrophage-directed drug delivery—may preferentially deliver AS-IV to Kupffer cells rather than to the more pathogenic Mo-TAMs, potentially limiting efficacy or causing off-target effects. Conversely, if AS-IV exerts stronger effects on Mo-TAMs, CCR2-targeted delivery systems may be more appropriate. Resolving this question requires single-cell-resolution studies comparing the transcriptomic and functional responses of KCs and Mo-TAMs to AS-IV treatment in HCC models.

Multi-dimensionality of core immunosuppressive mechanisms

M2-type TAMs efficiently establish and maintain the tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment through a repertoire of multi-dimensional, synergistic mechanisms, which can be broadly categorized into direct suppression, metabolic interference, and physical barriers.

Direct suppressive mechanisms

Cytokine-mediated suppression represents the most canonical immunosuppressive mechanism employed by M2-type TAMs. The high levels of IL-10 they secrete can inhibit dendritic cell (DC) maturation and antigen presentation function, downregulate the expression of major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC-II) molecules and co-stimulatory molecules, and thereby impair T cell activation (). Concurrently, IL-10 can directly suppress the capacity of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and T helper 1 (Th1) cells to produce effector cytokines such as interferon-γ (IFN-γ). TGF-β is another potent immunosuppressive factor; it not only inhibits the proliferation and cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells but also serves as a key driver of naïve CD4+ T cell differentiation into regulatory T cells (Tregs) (). Tregs further secrete IL-10 and TGF-β within the TME, thereby amplifying the immunosuppressive effect.

The expression of immune checkpoint molecules constitutes a direct pathway through which M2-type TAMs mediate T cell exhaustion. These cells highly express PD-L1 on their surface; when PD-L1 binds to PD-1 on T cells, it transmits inhibitory signals, leading to T cell proliferation arrest, diminished effector function, and ultimately exhaustion or apoptosis (). In addition to PD-L1, co-inhibitory molecules such as B7-H4 (VTCN1) are also expressed on M2-type macrophages, further suppressing T cell function through interactions with as-yet-unidentified receptors on T cells ().

Metabolic interference mechanisms

Metabolic competition within the TME represents an immune escape mechanism that has attracted considerable attention in recent years, with M2-type macrophages playing a significant role therein. Their highly expressed arginase-1 (Arg1) hydrolyzes L-arginine in the microenvironment into ornithine and urea (). L-arginine is essential for T cell activation and proliferation, serving as a critical substrate for metabolic reprogramming and nitric oxide synthesis. Arginine depletion directly leads to downregulation of the T cell receptor ζ chain, arresting T cells in the G0-G1 phase and preventing effective proliferation and cytotoxic function. The product of Arg1, ornithine, serves as a precursor for polyamine synthesis, and polyamines have also been shown to exert immunosuppressive effects.

Furthermore, the glycolytic activity of M2-type macrophages produces large quantities of lactate, which—together with lactate produced by tumor cells—contributes to TME acidification (pH values as low as 6.0–6.5) (). This acidic environment exerts multiple negative effects on immune cell function: it inhibits the cytotoxicity and cytokine production of T cells and NK cells; impairs the antigen processing and presentation capabilities of dendritic cells (DCs); and may promote the stability and suppressive function of Tregs. Lactate itself can also act as a signaling molecule, inhibiting the immune activation function of antigen-presenting cells through its receptor GPR81.

Physical barriers and cell-cell interactions

In addition to secreted factors, M2-type TAMs also construct physical and functional barriers through interactions with other stromal cells. They engage in close crosstalk with cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs): CAFs secrete factors such as CCL2 and CSF-1 to recruit and polarize macrophages, while macrophages activate CAFs by secreting factors such as platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), promoting extracellular matrix deposition and fibrosis (). This dense fibrotic stroma not only constitutes a physical barrier that impedes cytotoxic T cell infiltration but also its components (such as collagen and hyaluronic acid) can themselves transmit inhibitory signals.

Synergistic effects in promoting tumor progression

The pro-tumor functions of M2-type TAMs extend far beyond immunosuppression; they participate in virtually every aspect of tumor progression and form an intimate symbiotic relationship with tumor cells.

Angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis

Tumor growth and metastasis are highly dependent on the formation of new blood vessels. M2-type TAMs serve as critical “helper cells” for tumor angiogenesis (). They secrete a variety of pro-angiogenic factors—including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), fibroblast growth factor-2 (FGF-2), PDGF, and TGF-β—directly stimulating endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and lumen formation. Spatially, these macrophages often aggregate around tumor blood vessels, forming the so-called “perivascular” phenomenon, supporting vascular stability and function through direct cell contact or paracrine signaling. Additionally, they secrete matrix metalloproteinases (such as MMP-2 and MMP-9) and ephrins, degrading vascular basement membranes and remodeling the extracellular matrix to pave the way for angiogenesis. Similarly, they also promote tumor lymphangiogenesis by secreting factors such as VEGF-C, creating conditions for lymphatic metastasis.

Invasion and metastasis

Tumor invasion and metastasis represent a multi-step, complex process in which M2-type macrophages play a driving role. They directly promote tumor cell proliferation by secreting mitogens such as epidermal growth factor (EGF) (). More importantly, they can induce epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in tumor cells by secreting various cytokines (such as TGF-β, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-6 (IL-6)) and proteases. EMT enables tumor cells to lose intercellular junctions and polarity, acquiring the migratory and invasive capabilities of mesenchymal cells—a critical step in distant metastasis. Macrophage-derived proteases (such as MMPs and cathepsins) degrade the extracellular matrix and basement membrane, opening channels for tumor cell invasion. Some studies have even observed that macrophages form fusion cells with tumor cells or exchange substances through tunneling nanotubes, which may directly transfer metastatic potential.

Maintenance of cancer stem cell niches

Cancer stem cells (CSCs)—a small subpopulation of tumor cells with self-renewal, multi-differentiation, and drug resistance capabilities—are considered the root of tumor recurrence and metastasis. M2-type TAMs maintain and support CSCs by creating specialized microenvironmental niches (). For instance, IL-6 secreted by them can activate the STAT3 signaling pathway in CSCs, a critical pathway for maintaining stemness. Macrophages also influence CSC metabolism and drug resistance by secreting mediators such as prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) and nitric oxide (NO). In certain tumors, a bidirectional positive feedback loop exists between macrophages and CSCs: CSCs secrete factors to recruit and polarize macrophages, while polarized macrophages in turn maintain CSC properties.

Therapeutic resistance

M2-type TAMs are associated with multiple forms of therapeutic resistance. In chemotherapy, they can secrete cytokines to protect tumor cells from apoptosis or activate survival pathways such as nuclear factor κ-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) and STAT3 (). In radiotherapy, macrophages help tumor cells repair DNA damage and resist radiation-induced cell death by secreting mediators such as nitric oxide synthase (NOS) and PGE2. Particularly importantly, in ICI therapy, M2-type macrophages are recognized as one of the key factors underlying primary or acquired resistance. They not only directly inhibit T cells through molecules such as PD-L1 but also establish compensatory immunosuppressive networks by recruiting other immunosuppressive cells such as Tregs and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). Therefore, targeting M2-type macrophages holds promise as a breakthrough for overcoming existing therapeutic resistance and improving treatment efficacy.

Complexity of the molecular regulatory network

The acquisition and maintenance of the M2 phenotype depend on a complex intracellular molecular regulatory network involving precise regulation at multiple levels—signal transduction, metabolic reprogramming, and epigenetic modifications.

Core signaling pathways

JAK/STAT pathway

This is the core pathway that responds to extracellular signals and initiates M2 gene transcription. IL-4/IL-13 activate JAK1/JAK3 through receptors, which in turn phosphorylate STAT6. Phosphorylated STAT6 forms dimers, translocates to the nucleus, and binds to the promoter regions of M2-associated genes (such as Arg1, Retnla, and Mrc1), driving their expression (). IL-10 and CSF-1, on the other hand, primarily exert their polarization effects by activating STAT3. STAT3 not only directly promotes M2 gene expression but also reinforces the M2 phenotype by inducing the expression of transcription factors such as IRF4 and inhibiting IRF5.

PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway

This pathway integrates growth factor, cytokine, and nutrient signals and serves as a central regulator of cell growth, survival, and metabolism. In M2 polarization, PI3K/Akt activation promotes the M2 phenotype through multiple mechanisms, including activation of downstream mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1)—a major driver of protein synthesis and metabolic reprogramming (). Inhibition of mTORC1 can block IL-4-induced M2 polarization, suggesting its critical role in phenotypic regulation.

AMPK pathway

AMPK is the cellular energy sensor. Unlike the pro-inflammatory effects of AMPK activation in M1 polarization, the activity and function of AMPK in the context of M2 polarization are more complex. Some studies suggest that AMPK activation may indirectly influence M2 polarization by inhibiting mTORC1 or regulating mitochondrial function (). AMPK is closely linked to cellular metabolic status and represents a key node connecting metabolism and immunity.

Metabolic reprogramming

Macrophage polarization is accompanied by profound metabolic remodeling; metabolic status is both a consequence of polarization and, conversely, influences and stabilizes the cellular phenotype. M1 macrophages primarily rely on glycolysis for rapid ATP production—even under aerobic conditions (the Warburg effect)—accompanied by the pentose phosphate pathway and a truncated tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle (). In contrast, M2 macrophages are metabolically characterized by enhanced oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and fatty acid oxidation (FAO). This metabolic preference enables M2 macrophages to utilize long-chain fatty acids more efficiently as an energy source, supporting their tissue repair and immunoregulatory functions. This process is regulated by peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) and its coactivator peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ coactivator 1α (PGC-1α). PPARγ directly promotes the expression of genes related to fatty acid uptake and oxidation and acts synergistically with STAT6 to reinforce the M2 transcriptional program.

Epigenetic regulation

Epigenetic modifications provide “memory” for macrophage polarization, enabling cells to maintain specific functional states even after the initial stimulus has disappeared. Histone modifications represent an important aspect of this. For instance, M2-associated gene loci are often accompanied by increases in activating histone marks such as H3K4me3 (trimethylation) and H3K27ac (acetylation), as well as decreases in the repressive mark H3K27me3 (). These modifications alter chromatin accessibility, facilitating transcription factor binding. The activities of histone deacetylases (HDACs) and histone methyltransferases/demethylases are regulated by polarization signals. DNA methylation also plays a role in polarization; M2 polarization may lead to demethylation of specific gene promoter regions, thereby permitting their sustained expression. Interestingly, intermediate products of cellular metabolism—such as acetyl-CoA, α-ketoglutarate, and S-adenosylmethionine—serve precisely as substrates or cofactors for histone modifications and DNA methylation, thus tightly linking cellular metabolism to epigenetic regulation.

Notably, this metabolic-epigenetic coupling is central to the sustained maintenance of the M2 phenotype. Within the TME, specific metabolites act as substrates or cofactors for epigenetic enzymes, establishing a self-reinforcing loop. For instance, α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), a TCA cycle intermediate, functions as an obligatory co-substrate for ten-eleven translocation (TET) DNA demethylases and JmjC-domain histone demethylases, both of which promote M2-associated gene programs (). Conversely, the M2 metabolic signature—characterized by elevated fatty acid oxidation (FAO) and glutamine utilization—drives the intracellular accumulation of acetyl-CoA and S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), which respectively fuel histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and histone/DNA methyltransferases (). These enzymatic activities reinforce an open chromatin conformation at M2 marker loci (e.g., Arg1, Mrc1) and maintain their transcriptional competence across cell divisions. AS-IV, by disrupting FAO and altering the intracellular pools of acetyl-CoA and α-KG, may deprive these epigenetic enzymes of their essential substrates, thereby destabilizing the chromatin landscape that sustains M2 polarization memory.

Molecular characteristics and pharmacological activities of AS-IV

Chemical structure and pharmacokinetic features

AS-IV is a cycloartane-type tetracyclic triterpenoid saponin extracted and isolated from the dried roots of the Chinese medicinal herb Astragalus membranaceus. Its chemical name is 3-O-β-D-xylopyranosyl-6-O-β-D-glucopyranosylcycloastragenol, with a molecular formula of C41H68O14 and a molecular weight of 784.97 (). Its chemical structure comprises a hydrophobic cycloartane triterpenoid aglycone and two hydrophilic glycosyl moieties (one xylose and one glucose) linked via glycosidic bonds. This amphipathic structure enables it to interact with the phospholipid bilayer of cell membranes and may modulate membrane protein function, serving as a critical structural basis for its multi-target pharmacological activities. The multiple hydroxyl groups and glycosyl moieties in its structure also confer relatively good aqueous solubility, although oral absorption remains challenging.

Pharmacokinetic studies indicate that AS-IV is absorbed slowly and incompletely following oral administration, with a low absolute bioavailability (approximately 2.2%–5.3%) (). This is primarily attributed to its relatively large molecular weight, low lipophilicity, instability in the gastrointestinal tract, and potential first-pass effects. Following absorption, AS-IV is widely distributed throughout the body but primarily accumulates in the liver, kidneys, and lungs—consistent with the liver being its primary metabolic organ and its potential hepatoprotective and renoprotective targeting properties. In plasma, AS-IV exhibits a high protein binding rate, which influences its free drug concentration and volume of distribution.

The metabolic transformation of AS-IV in vivo is relatively complex, primarily involving phase I metabolic reactions mediated by the hepatic cytochrome P450 enzyme system (particularly CYP3A4 and CYP2C9), including hydroxylation and deglycosylation, which yield multiple metabolites (). Among these, deglycosylation products (such as cycloastragenol) may still retain partial biological activity. AS-IV and its metabolites are ultimately excreted primarily via renal elimination in urine, with small amounts eliminated through biliary and fecal pathways. Its elimination half-life varies across studies but is generally relatively short, suggesting that frequent administration or sustained-release formulations may be required to maintain stable plasma concentrations.

To improve the bioavailability and targeting of AS-IV, the development of nano-delivery systems has emerged as a prominent research focus in recent years. For instance, encapsulating it in liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles (such as poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) nanoparticles), solid lipid nanoparticles, or mesoporous silica nanoparticles can significantly enhance its aqueous solubility, protect it from degradation by gastrointestinal enzymes, and enable passive accumulation at tumor sites through the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect (). More advanced strategies involve the development of actively targeted nanocarriers, such as modifying nanoparticle surfaces with mannose (targeting macrophage mannose receptors), RGD peptides (targeting tumor vascular endothelial integrin αvβ3), or folic acid (targeting tumor cells with high folate receptor expression) to achieve precise delivery to the TME or specific cell types (). Furthermore, stimuli-responsive drug delivery systems (such as pH-sensitive, enzyme-sensitive, or reactive oxygen species-sensitive carriers) can leverage the unique characteristics of the TME to achieve controlled drug release at target sites, further enhancing therapeutic efficacy and reducing systemic toxicity ().

Despite promising preclinical results, nano-delivery systems for AS-IV remain at the preclinical stage, and several bottlenecks hinder clinical translation. First, carrier immunogenicity poses a significant concern: many nanocarriers can activate complement or trigger hypersensitivity upon repeated dosing, potentially worsening immune-related toxicities in HCC patients. Second, the metabolic fate and long-term toxicity of nanocarrier degradation products remain poorly characterized, particularly under impaired hepatic clearance. Third, large-scale manufacturing with reproducible physicochemical properties presents substantial technical and regulatory hurdles. Moreover, commonly used targeting ligands (e.g., mannose, galactose) are not truly TAM-specific—their receptors (CD206, etc.) are also expressed on normal tissue macrophages (e.g., Kupffer cells, alveolar macrophages) and even some non-macrophage cells. This off-target accumulation risks reprogramming homeostatic macrophages and disrupting immune surveillance. Future development should prioritize identifying more specific TAM markers, designing dual-targeting or stimuli-responsive systems, and rigorously evaluating off-target effects in relevant preclinical models.

Pharmacokinetic challenges in populations with hepatic impairment

The clinical translation of AS-IV is further complicated by the fact that many HCC patients have underlying cirrhosis or hepatic insufficiency, which substantially alters drug-metabolizing enzyme activity. CYP3A4 and CYP2C9, key enzymes in AS-IV metabolism, are often downregulated in cirrhotic livers, potentially reducing first-pass clearance and raising systemic exposure. Concurrently, reduced hepatic blood flow, portosystemic shunting, and hypoalbuminemia can further alter drug distribution and free fraction, making the pharmacokinetic profile of AS-IV highly unpredictable in this population. Even modest drug accumulation in patients with compromised hepatic function may exacerbate toxicities or disturb liver-resident macrophage homeostasis. These considerations highlight the need to evaluate AS-IV pharmacokinetics and safety margins in preclinical models of hepatic impairment and to develop delivery systems that minimize reliance on hepatic metabolism.

Multi-level pharmacological activities

AS-IV exhibits a broad spectrum of pharmacological activities; its actions are not limited to a single target but rather exert effects through a multi-dimensional, multi-level mechanistic network, which confers unique advantages in the treatment of complex diseases such as cancer.

Direct anti-tumor effects

Numerous in vitro and in vivo studies have confirmed that AS-IV exerts direct growth-inhibitory and pro-apoptotic effects on various tumor cell types. In HCC cell lines (such as HepG2, Huh7, and SMMC-7721), AS-IV treatment inhibits cell proliferation in a dose- and time-dependent manner (). The mechanisms underlying its induction of apoptosis involve: 1) Mitochondrial pathway: downregulating the expression of anti-apoptotic proteins Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL, upregulating the expression of pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and Bak, leading to decreased mitochondrial membrane potential, cytochrome c release, and subsequent activation of the caspase-9 and caspase-3 cascade; 2) Death receptor pathway: under certain conditions, it may upregulate the expression of Fas/FasL and activate caspase-8; 3) Endoplasmic reticulum stress pathway: inducing the expression of endoplasmic reticulum stress markers such as GRP78 and CHOP, and activating caspase-12.

AS-IV can also induce cell cycle arrest, causing cells to become arrested in G0/G1 phase or G2/M phase. The mechanism is related to upregulating the expression of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors p21 and p27 while simultaneously downregulating the expression of cyclins (such as cyclin D1 and cyclin E) and cyclin-dependent kinases (such as CDK4 and CDK2) (). Additionally, AS-IV significantly inhibits the invasion and metastatic capabilities of HCC cells. This is closely associated with its downregulation of the expression and activity of MMPs (particularly MMP-2 and MMP-9), upregulation of the expression of their endogenous inhibitors tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases-1 (TIMP-1) and tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases-2 (TIMP-2), and reversal of the EMT process (upregulating E-cadherin and downregulating N-cadherin, vimentin, and transcription factors such as Snail) ().

Basal immunomodulatory activities

AS-IV exerts a bidirectional regulatory effect on the immune system—that is, it enhances immunity when immune function is suppressed and inhibits immunity when inflammation is excessive—consistent with the traditional Chinese medicine theory of “strengthening the righteous and dispelling the pathogenic.”

Regulation of T cells

AS-IV can promote the differentiation of naïve CD4+ T cells toward Th1 cells, manifested by upregulated T-bet expression and increased IFN-γ secretion, while simultaneously inhibiting differentiation toward T helper 2 (Th2) and regulatory T (Treg) cells. This contributes to establishing a cellular immune response favorable for anti-tumor immunity ().

Regulation of DCs

As the most professional antigen-presenting cells, the maturation state of DCs determines the efficiency of T cell activation. AS-IV can promote DC maturation, upregulate the expression of surface MHC-II molecules and co-stimulatory molecules (CD80, CD86, CD40), and enhance their IL-12 secretion capacity, thereby more effectively activating naïve T cells ().

Anti-inflammatory effects

In various acute and chronic inflammation models, AS-IV demonstrates significant anti-inflammatory effects. The mechanisms involve inhibiting the activation of the nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway, reducing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6), and suppressing the expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) ().

Tumor immune microenvironment-specific regulation

This represents the most cutting-edge and translationally promising direction in AS-IV anti-tumor research. Unlike traditional chemotherapeutic agents that primarily target tumor cells, AS-IV can act on multiple stromal cells within the TME—particularly immune cells—thereby systematically altering the tumor ecosystem.

Reprogramming TAMs

As will be elaborated in subsequent sections of this review, AS-IV can effectively inhibit macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype and may promote their shift toward an M1-like phenotype. This represents one of the core mechanisms by which it reverses the immunosuppressive microenvironment ().

Regulation of MDSCs

MDSCs represent another important class of immunosuppressive cells. Preliminary studies suggest that AS-IV may reduce the recruitment and expansion of MDSCs in tumors and attenuate their ability to suppress T cell function by inhibiting such as the STAT3 pathway ().

Improving tumor vascular function

AS-IV possesses vascular protective effects. In the tumor context, it may reduce abnormal angiogenesis by inhibiting VEGF secretion from M2-type TAMs and potentially promote vascular “normalization,” improving tumor perfusion and hypoxia, thereby facilitating immune cell infiltration and drug delivery ().

Regulation of CAFs

CAFs are the main component of the tumor stroma. Studies suggest that AS-IV may inhibit CAF activation by interfering with signaling pathways such as TGF-β/Smad, reducing excessive extracellular matrix deposition, and thereby lowering the physical barrier and ameliorating immunosuppression ().

Molecular mechanisms of AS-IV in regulating macrophage polarization

Precise intervention at the signaling pathway level

The regulation of macrophage polarization by AS-IV is first manifested in its precise intervention in key signaling pathways, which serve as bridges connecting extracellular stimuli with the conversion of intracellular gene expression programs (; ).

Regulation of the JAK/STAT pathway

The JAK/STAT pathway is the central pathway for cytokine signal transduction, and the sustained activation of STAT3 and STAT6 is critical for driving M2-type gene expression. Studies have demonstrated that AS-IV can effectively interfere with this pathway.

Inhibition of STAT3 activation

In macrophage M2 polarization models induced by IL-10, CSF-1, or tumor cell conditioned medium, AS-IV pretreatment significantly reduces the phosphorylation level of STAT3 at the Tyr705 site and inhibits its nuclear translocation (). Phosphorylated STAT3 dimers, upon entering the nucleus, act as transcription factors that directly bind to the promoter regions of M2 marker genes such as Arg1 and Mrc1 (CD206). AS-IV intervention blocks this process, leading to decreased transcription levels of downstream M2-associated genes. Mechanistically, AS-IV may: 1) Inhibit the activity of upstream JAK family kinases (such as JAK1 and JAK2); 2) Enhance the activity of protein tyrosine phosphatases (such as SHP-1 and SHP-2), leading to STAT3 dephosphorylation; 3) Upregulate the expression of suppressor of cytokine signaling (SOCS) family proteins (such as SOCS1 and SOCS3). SOCS proteins exert negative feedback inhibition on JAK/STAT signaling by competitively binding to JAK or receptors and promoting protein ubiquitination and degradation.

Inhibition of STAT6 activation

For the more classical M2a-type polarization induced by IL-4 or IL-13, AS-IV similarly demonstrates inhibitory effects. It can reduce STAT6 phosphorylation, thereby interfering with the IL-4Rα/JAK/STAT6 signaling axis. This leads to significant downregulation of STAT6-dependent M2a signature genes (such as Arg1, Fizz1, and Ym1) (). The inhibition of STAT6 by AS-IV may be related to its regulation of upstream signaling molecules or its effect on the DNA-binding capacity of STAT6.

Regulation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway

The PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway integrates growth factor, cytokine, and nutrient signals and serves as a core regulator of cellular metabolism, growth, survival, and autophagy; it is also closely associated with macrophage polarization.

Multi-node inhibition

AS-IV can inhibit the activation of multiple key nodes in this pathway. Studies have found that it reduces the phosphorylation of the PI3K catalytic subunit p110 and regulatory subunit p85, decreasing their membrane recruitment and activity (). Consequently, the phosphorylation of its downstream key kinase Akt at the Thr308 and Ser473 sites is also inhibited. Akt inactivation further leads to reduced activity of its downstream effector mTORC1, manifested by decreased phosphorylation levels of mTOR and its downstream targets p70S6K and 4E-BP1.

Functional implications

Inhibition of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway exerts multiple effects on macrophage polarization. First, mTORC1 is a major driver of protein synthesis and metabolic reprogramming; its inhibition alters cellular metabolic status, which in turn determines the functional phenotype of macrophages. Second, this pathway engages in crosstalk with STAT3 and other molecules; inhibiting PI3K/Akt may indirectly affect STAT3 activity. Finally, this pathway also regulates cellular autophagy, and the autophagic process participates in regulating macrophage inflammatory responses and polarization.

Regulation of AMPK signaling

AMPK is the cellular energy sensor; it is activated during nutrient deprivation, promoting catabolism to generate energy while inhibiting anabolism. The activation state of AMPK is complexly associated with macrophage polarization.

Activation of AMPK

Multiple studies have demonstrated that AS-IV can induce the phosphorylation and activation of AMPK in macrophages (). The mechanisms may involve: 1) Directly activating AMPK by influencing mitochondrial function or increasing the AMP/ATP ratio; 2) Acting through upstream kinases such as LKB1 or CaMKKβ.

Role in polarization regulation

The effect of AMPK activation on polarization is context-dependent. In the context of M1 polarization, AMPK activation is generally considered to have anti-inflammatory effects. In the context of M2 polarization, AMPK activation induced by AS-IV may inhibit the M2 phenotype through the following mechanisms: 1) Phosphorylating and inhibiting mTORC1, which echoes the aforementioned PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway inhibition; 2) Regulating downstream transcription factors such as PGC-1α (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ coactivator 1α), affecting mitochondrial biogenesis and fatty acid oxidation—the latter being the preferred energy source of M2 macrophages; 3) Modulating NADPH oxidase activity, influencing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which can act as signaling molecules affecting polarization.

Crosstalk among JAK/STAT3, PI3K/Akt/mTOR, and AMPK pathways

These signaling pathways engage in extensive crosstalk that governs macrophage polarization. The JAK/STAT3 and PI3K/Akt/mTOR axes positively regulate each other through reciprocal activation, while AMPK serves as a negative regulator of both by inhibiting mTORC1, promoting STAT3 degradation, and dampening PI3K/Akt signaling. This crosstalk shapes AS-IV’s effects: coordinated inhibition of STAT3 and PI3K/Akt may synergistically disrupt their mutual reinforcement, and concurrent AMPK activation could further amplify suppression via feed-forward loops. However, potential antagonism must also be considered—sustained AMPK activation may trigger compensatory Akt activation, and prolonged STAT3 inhibition could upregulate other JAK family members. The multi-target nature of AS-IV thus represents both an advantage (coordinated suppression) and a challenge (unpredictable compensatory responses). Elucidating the precise dynamics of this pathway network under AS-IV treatment warrants systems-level investigation using phosphoproteomics and computational modeling.

Other related pathways

AS-IV may also influence other pathways related to macrophage function and polarization, such as:

NF-κB pathway

Under pro-inflammatory stimulation, AS-IV typically exhibits an inhibitory effect on NF-κB activation, thereby reducing the production of pro-inflammatory factors (). However, in the context of M2 polarization, its effect on NF-κB may be more complex, as certain M2-inducing signals may also involve non-canonical NF-κB pathways.

MAPK pathway

Studies suggest that AS-IV may modulate the phosphorylation levels of ERK, p38 MAPK, and JNK, kinases involved in regulating cytokine production and cellular function ().

Regulatory role in metabolic reprogramming

Macrophage polarization is inseparable from profound remodeling of metabolic status. By intervening in key metabolic pathways and enzyme activities, AS-IV influences the energy and substrate metabolism of macrophages, thereby altering their functional phenotype.

Regulation of the AMPK/mTOR axis and energy sensing

As previously mentioned, AS-IV activates AMPK and inhibits mTORC1; this regulatory axis represents the core switch of cellular energy metabolism. AMPK activation promotes catabolic pathways (such as fatty acid oxidation—note that the effect of AMPK on glycolysis in macrophages is complex), while mTORC1 inhibition reduces anabolism (such as protein and lipid synthesis). This shift in metabolic state may render macrophages no longer supportive of the sustained tissue repair and immunoregulatory functions required by the M2 phenotype, as the maintenance of M2 function requires active anabolism to produce large quantities of secreted proteins and membrane receptors.

Intervention in mitochondrial function and oxidative phosphorylation

M2 macrophages rely on efficient oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) to generate energy. AS-IV exerts regulatory effects on mitochondrial function and may influence OXPHOS through the following mechanisms: 1) Regulating mitochondrial membrane potential and ROS production; 2) Affecting the activity of electron transport chain complexes; 3) Influencing mitochondrial biogenesis through the AMPK/PGC-1α axis (). Altering the efficiency of OXPHOS may affect the energy supply and functional stability of M2 macrophages.

Inhibition of fatty acid oxidation

Fatty acid oxidation (FAO) is the primary energy source of M2 macrophages, and its enhancement is one of the hallmarks of M2 metabolic reprogramming. The FAO process is transcriptionally regulated by PPARγ and its coactivator peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ coactivator 1α (PGC-1α). AS-IV may indirectly downregulate the expression or activity of PPARγ and PGC-1α by inhibiting upstream signals such as STAT6 and PI3K/Akt/mTOR, thereby reducing fatty acid uptake (e.g., downregulating CD36) and the expression of FAO-related enzymes (e.g., CPT1), limiting the energy metabolism of M2 macrophages ().

Effects on amino acid metabolism

Arginine metabolism represents a key metabolic node in macrophage polarization. M1 macrophages highly express iNOS, metabolizing arginine into citrulline and nitric oxide; in contrast, M2 macrophages highly express Arg1, metabolizing arginine into ornithine and polyamines. By downregulating Arg1 expression through the inhibition of such as the STAT6 pathway, AS-IV may alter the metabolic flow of arginine in the TME, reduce the production of immunosuppressive polyamines, and potentially relatively increase arginine available for the iNOS pathway—contributing to the shift toward an M1-like phenotype ().

Potential regulation at the epigenetic level

Epigenetic modifications provide plasticity and long-term memory for the functional state of macrophages, raising the intriguing possibility that stabilizing a shift away from the M2 phenotype may require epigenetic reprogramming. It is important to emphasize that no direct experimental evidence (e.g., ChIP-seq, ATAC-seq, or quantitative histone modification assays) currently demonstrates that AS-IV alters the macrophage epigenome. The following subsections therefore present testable hypotheses regarding how AS-IV might indirectly influence the epigenetic landscape of macrophages through metabolic and enzymatic intermediates.

Metabolite availability and epigenetic modifications

Intermediate products of cellular metabolism serve as substrates or cofactors for many epigenetic modification enzymes, forming a well-documented metabolism-epigenetics axis. Based on this axis, we hypothesize that AS-IV-induced metabolic changes could theoretically impact the following:

Acetyl-CoA and histone acetylation

Acetyl-CoA serves as a substrate for histone acetyltransferases (HATs). Because AS-IV may influence glycolysis and fatty acid metabolism, it is plausible that intracellular acetyl-CoA levels and distribution could be altered, potentially affecting histone acetylation marks such as H3K9ac and H3K27ac—marks generally associated with gene activation (). This remains speculative; direct measurement of acetyl-CoA pools and global or locus-specific acetylation in AS-IV-treated macrophages has not been reported.

α-Ketoglutarate and succinate

These TCA cycle intermediates function as cofactors or inhibitors of histone and DNA demethylases. If AS-IV significantly shifts the metabolic profile of macrophages, it could theoretically alter the ratio of these metabolites, thereby influencing histone and DNA methylation levels. Again, this is an inference and has not been experimentally verified in the context of AS-IV.

S- adenosylmethionine (SAM)

SAM is the primary methyl donor for DNA and histone methylation. AS-IV might affect the methionine cycle or folate metabolism, leading to altered SAM levels and consequent changes in global or locus-specific methylation. This possibility awaits experimental testing.

Potential effects on epigenetic modification enzymes

A more proximal link between AS-IV and the epigenetic machinery may exist through its known modulation of certain signaling pathways that converge on chromatin-modifying enzymes. For example, AS-IV has been shown to activate AMPK (), which can increase NAD+ levels and thereby activate SIRT1, an NAD+-dependent class III histone deacetylase. SIRT1 deacetylates both histones and non-histone proteins (such as NF-κB and STAT3), participating in the regulation of inflammation and metabolism. Therefore, we hypothesize that AS-IV-mediated AMPK activation could enhance SIRT1 activity, potentially leading to altered acetylation of specific histones or transcription factors. However, whether AS-IV actually changes SIRT1 activity or histone acetylation in macrophages has not yet been demonstrated. Additionally, AS-IV-mediated inhibition of the JAK/STAT pathway could theoretically reduce the recruitment of downstream epigenetic co-activators, indirectly affecting chromatin states. These mechanistic links remain to be experimentally tested.

Future directions for experimental validation

To move from inference to evidence, future studies should combine genome-wide and locus-specific epigenetic profiling, including ChIP-seq for activating (H3K4me3, H3K27ac) and repressive (H3K27me3) histone marks and ATAC-seq in AS-IV-treated versus vehicle-treated macrophages under M2-polarizing conditions. Candidate M2- and M1-associated loci identified by transcriptomics (e.g., Arg1, Mrc1, Il10, Tnf, Il12b) could then be validated by ChIP-qPCR and bisulfite sequencing for promoter/enhancer histone modifications and DNA methylation changes. To test the proposed metabolism-epigenetics coupling, pharmacological or genetic perturbation of key metabolic nodes—such as glycolysis (2-DG), fatty acid oxidation (etomoxir), or SIRT1 (EX-527)—could be used to determine whether these interventions abrogate AS-IV-induced transcriptional and phenotypic shifts. Finally, adoptive transfer or serial re-stimulation experiments would help assess the stability of the phenotypic shift in the absence of continued treatment, thereby providing evidence for underlying epigenetic memory.

Changes at the cellular functional level

The aforementioned molecular, metabolic, and epigenetic changes ultimately converge into significant alterations in macrophage phenotype and function.

Distinction between inhibition of M2 polarization and true M1 induction

Most current data demonstrate that AS-IV reduces M2 marker expression (CD206, CD163, Arg1) under M2-inducing conditions and partially increases selected M1-like markers in vitro (; ). However, these findings largely reflect the inhibition of de novo M2 polarization—that is, the prevention of macrophages from acquiring or maintaining an M2 phenotype under M2-polarizing stimuli—rather than the active conversion of pre-existing, fully differentiated M2-like TAMs into M1-like anti-tumor macrophages (). Whether established tumor-resident M2 TAMs are actively converted into M1-like macrophages in vivo remains unproven (). AS-IV may primarily inhibit the maintenance of the M2 state rather than directly induce M1 polarization; the observed M1-like changes may be secondary to reduced M2-associated signaling or alterations in the tumor microenvironment. This distinction should be kept in mind when interpreting the functional changes described below ().

Downregulation of M2 marker expression

Macrophages treated with AS-IV exhibit significantly reduced expression of surface markers CD206 (mannose receptor) and CD163 (hemoglobin scavenger receptor) under M2 polarization stimulation, and intracellular arginase-1 activity and protein levels are also markedly decreased ().

Shift of secretory profile toward pro-inflammatory/anti-tumor direction

AS-IV treatment reduces the ability of macrophages to secrete M2-associated immunosuppressive/chemotactic factors such as IL-10, TGF-β, CCL17, and CCL22. Concurrently, under certain experimental conditions, it may promote the production of M1-associated pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-12 and TNF-α. However, whether this represents true M1 polarization or merely a partial acquisition of selected M1-like features remains unclear and may depend on specific stimulation contexts, dosages, and experimental models ().

Changes in phagocytic function and antigen presentation capacity

The phagocytic function of M2-type macrophages is generally considered to focus on clearing apoptotic cells and debris, with relatively weak antigen presentation capacity. The phenotypic shift induced by AS-IV may enhance the phagocytic ability of macrophages against tumor cells or apoptotic tumor cells and may upregulate the expression of MHC-II molecules and co-stimulatory molecules (such as CD80 and CD86), thereby improving their efficiency in presenting tumor antigens to T cells and promoting the initiation of anti-tumor immune responses ().

Altered ability to directly regulate tumor cell behavior

The conditioned medium of macrophages with an M1-like phenotype demonstrates reduced, or even potentially inhibitory, effects on tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and migration. This is related to the decreased growth-promoting factors and increased pro-inflammatory factors in their secretory profile ().

In summary, AS-IV forms a multi-level regulatory network by intervening in core signaling pathways such as JAK/STAT, PI3K/Akt/mTOR, and AMPK, influencing macrophage metabolic programs, and potentially regulating epigenetic states—ultimately modulating macrophage polarization by suppressing M2-associated functions and partially promoting M1-like features with anti-tumor potential. This functional modulation serves as the cornerstone for the subsequent systematic remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment.

Systemic remodeling of the immune microenvironment mediated by AS-IV

The regulation of TAM polarization by AS-IV is not an isolated event but rather the initiating point that triggers systemic, cascading remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment. This remodeling encompasses functional and interactional changes in virtually all immune cells and stromal components within the microenvironment () ().

Restoration and activation of the adaptive immune system

Restoration of CD8+ cytotoxic T cell function

CD8+ T cells represent the primary effector cells of anti-tumor immunity, but they frequently exist in a state of functional exhaustion or anergy within the HCC immunosuppressive microenvironment. Through TAM reprogramming, AS-IV alleviates CD8+ T cell suppression via multiple mechanisms:

Improving the cytokine environment

Reducing the production of inhibitory cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β while potentially augmenting cytokines that promote T cell activation and Th1 differentiation, such as IL-12, thereby creating favorable conditions for the survival and functional execution of CD8+ T cells ().

Alleviating immune checkpoint-mediated exhaustion

M2-type TAMs represent a major source of PD-L1 within the TME. AS-IV downregulates PD-L1 expression on TAMs, reducing the opportunity for sustained PD-1 activation on T cell surfaces, thereby helping to reverse T cell exhaustion and restore their proliferative capacity and ability to produce effector molecules (such as IFN-γ, granzyme B, and perforin) ().

Relieving metabolic suppression

By downregulating Arg1 expression in TAMs, AS-IV alleviates arginine depletion within the TME, thereby helping to restore the metabolic adaptability and function of T cells.

Promoting T cell infiltration and survival

The altered macrophage secretory profile may affect the chemokine network—for example, reducing chemokines that recruit Tregs such as CCL22 while potentially augmenting chemokines that recruit effector T cells such as CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11—thereby altering the composition and spatial distribution of T cells within the tumor (). Animal experiments have demonstrated that following AS-IV treatment, the infiltration density of CD8+ T cells in tumor tissue significantly increases, and their effector function is enhanced.

Remodeling of CD4+ helper T cell subset balance

CD4+ T cells play a critical role in orchestrating anti-tumor immune responses. The microenvironmental changes induced by AS-IV favor anti-tumor Th1 responses while simultaneously suppressing immunosuppressive Th2 and Treg responses.

Promoting Th1 differentiation

The relative increase in IL-12 levels and the attenuated inhibitory environment are conducive to the differentiation of naïve CD4+ T cells into IFN-γ-secreting Th1 cells under stimulation by antigen-presenting cells such as DCs, thereby enhancing cellular immune responses ().

Inhibiting Th2 differentiation

IL-4 and IL-13 secreted by M2-type TAMs are key drivers of Th2 differentiation. By inhibiting M2 polarization, AS-IV reduces the source of these cytokines, thereby indirectly suppressing Th2 responses—which are generally associated with humoral immunity and certain pro-tumorigenic effects.

Limiting tregs proliferation and function

The induction, expansion, and functional maintenance of Tregs are highly dependent on TGF-β and IL-10. By reducing the production of these factors by TAMs, AS-IV directly attenuates the survival environment of Tregs (). Concurrently, altered macrophages may reduce CCL22 secretion, thereby decreasing chemotactic recruitment of Tregs. The relative reduction in Treg numbers and diminished function further alleviate the suppression of effector T cells.

Activation of the innate immune system

Restoration of natural killer cell function

NK cells represent the first line of anti-tumor defense in the innate immune system, but their function is similarly suppressed in the HCC microenvironment.

Relieving inhibitory signals

TGF-β secreted by M2-type TAMs is one of the main inhibitory factors for NK cell function; it can downregulate the expression of NK cell activating receptors (such as NKG2D) and inhibit their cytotoxicity and cytokine production. By reducing TGF-β production, AS-IV helps restore the killing potential of NK cells ().

Providing activation signals

Cytokines such as IL-12, IL-15, and IL-18 produced by M1-type macrophages can effectively activate and maintain NK cell function. The macrophage phenotypic transition induced by AS-IV may increase the production of these cytokines, providing positive stimulation for NK cells.

Effects on MDSCs

MDSCs represent another heterogeneous cell population with potent immunosuppressive capacity. Close interactions and positive feedback loops exist between TAMs and MDSCs.

Interfering with recruitment and expansion

TAMs can secrete factors such as CCL2 and CSF-1 to recruit MDSC precursors and promote their expansion. By inhibiting M2-type TAMs, AS-IV may reduce the secretion of these factors, thereby limiting the accumulation of MDSCs at tumor sites ().

Weakening immunosuppressive function

The suppressive function of MDSCs partially depends on the activation of such as the STAT3 pathway. The inhibitory effects of AS-IV on these pathways may similarly extend to MDSCs, reducing their production of Arg1, iNOS, and ROS, and weakening their ability to suppress T cells and NK cells.

Normalization of the tumor vascular system and extracellular matrix

Inhibition of abnormal angiogenesis

HCC is a highly vascularized tumor, and abnormal neovascularization not only provides nutrients for tumor growth but also constitutes physical and functional barriers to immune cell infiltration.

Reducing pro-angiogenic factors

M2-type TAMs are a major source of pro-angiogenic factors such as VEGF, FGF-2, and PDGF. By inhibiting M2 polarization, AS-IV directly cuts off an important supply source of these factors, thereby inhibiting abnormal endothelial cell proliferation and angiogenesis ().

Promoting vascular normalization

Studies suggest that certain immunomodulators, while inhibiting excessive angiogenesis, may induce structural and functional “normalization” of remaining blood vessels—manifested as increased pericyte coverage, reduced vascular permeability, and improved perfusion. Although direct evidence for AS-IV in this regard still needs to be accumulated, theoretically, by regulating TAMs and improving hypoxia, it may contribute to the formation of vascular networks more conducive to immune cell migration and drug delivery ().

Amelioration of the fibrotic barrier

HCC often occurs against a background of chronic liver disease (such as cirrhosis), accompanied by significant fibrosis and extracellular matrix remodeling, forming physical barriers and generating immunosuppressive signals.

Regulating the macrophage-hepatic stellate cell axis

Activated hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) are the main cells producing extracellular matrix. M2-type TAMs activate and maintain the activated state of HSCs by secreting factors such as TGF-β and PDGF. Conversely, activated HSCs also secrete factors to recruit and polarize macrophages. By inhibiting M2-type TAMs, AS-IV may break this positive feedback loop, reducing activation signals to HSCs ().

Reducing matrix deposition

TGF-β is the most potent pro-fibrotic factor. By reducing TAM-derived TGF-β, AS-IV may directly attenuate the fibrotic process. Additionally, M2-type TAMs themselves secrete certain matrix remodeling enzymes. Phenotypically transformed macrophages may alter the balance between MMPs and their inhibitors, affecting matrix degradation and deposition, thereby reducing matrix density and facilitating immune cell infiltration ().

Indirect alterations of tumor cell properties

Disruption of CSCs niches

CSCs are resistant to conventional therapies and are considered the root of recurrence and metastasis. M2-type TAMs provide microenvironments that maintain stemness and self-renewal for CSCs by secreting factors such as IL-6, PGE2, and nitric oxide.

Cutting off support signals

After AS-IV inhibits M2-type TAMs, the secretion of these supporting factors is reduced, potentially causing CSCs to lose niche support and be forced to transition to a more differentiated, treatment-sensitive cell state ().

Enhancing immune system recognition of CSCs

M1-like macrophages may enhance phagocytosis and antigen presentation of CSCs, thereby stimulating specific immune responses against CSCs.

Sensitization to conventional therapies

The systemic improvement of the TME by AS-IV may enhance the efficacy of chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and even radiotherapy.

Improving drug delivery

Vascular normalization and matrix remodeling may increase the penetration and distribution of chemotherapeutic/targeted drugs within tumor tissue ().

Reversing immunosuppression-induced therapeutic resistance

The efficacy of many conventional treatments partially depends on the participation of the immune system (e.g., immunogenic cell death). The restored immune surveillance function mediated by AS-IV may enhance the anti-tumor immune responses induced by these treatments, generating abscopal effects and immune memory ().

Synergistic induction of cell death

AS-IV itself has the effects of inducing tumor cell apoptosis and endoplasmic reticulum stress, potentially producing synergistic effects with certain chemotherapeutic drugs.

In summary, AS-IV leverages TAMs as a critical fulcrum to pry the entire HCC immune microenvironment from an “immunosuppressive” state toward a “permissive” or even “activated” state. This systemic remodeling not only restores anti-tumor immune responses but also improves the physical and metabolic microenvironment of the tumor, creating favorable conditions for the combined application of multiple therapeutic strategies.

Translational potential and challenges of AS-IV in HCC therapy

Synergistic potential as an immunotherapy sensitizer

ICIs, represented by PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, have revolutionized the treatment landscape of HCC, but the objective response rate remains limited (approximately 15%–20%), and primary or acquired resistance persists (). The tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment, particularly the enrichment of M2-type TAMs, represents a critical contributor to the “cold tumor” phenotype and ICI resistance. Therefore, the combination of AS-IV with ICIs has a strong theoretical synergistic basis ().

Mechanisms of synergistic action

Converting “cold tumors” to “hot tumors

The premise of ICI action is the presence of a certain number of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) within the tumor, albeit with suppressed function. By reversing TAM polarization, augmenting effector T cell infiltration, and enhancing T cell function, AS-IV can effectively increase immune cell infiltration and inflammatory signals in tumors, providing the necessary immune cell basis and environmental conditions for ICIs to exert their effects (). Preclinical studies have demonstrated that AS-IV pretreatment or combination therapy can significantly increase tumor sensitivity to PD-1 antibodies.

Overcoming multiple immunosuppressive mechanisms

ICIs primarily block the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway, but tumors may compensate by upregulating other immune checkpoints (such as cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), lymphocyte-activation gene 3 (LAG-3), and T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3)) or augmenting the function of other suppressor cells such as Tregs and MDSCs. The systemic immunomodulatory effects of AS-IV can simultaneously act on these compensatory mechanisms—for example, reducing Treg recruitment and attenuating MDSC function—thereby preventing or delaying the development of resistance ().

Regulating PD-L1 expression on TAMs

Intriguingly, while inhibiting M2 polarization, AS-IV may selectively downregulate immunosuppressive PD-L1 expression on M2-type TAMs without affecting or potentially augmenting the expression of necessary co-stimulatory molecules on antigen-presenting cells, thereby more precisely releasing the immune brake rather than comprehensively inhibiting immune checkpoints ().

Combination potential with other immunotherapies

In addition to combination with ICIs, AS-IV may also synergize with adoptive cell therapies, cancer vaccines, and other immunotherapeutic modalities.

Adoptive cell therapies

Such as chimeric antigen receptor T cells (CAR-T) or TIL therapies. The improved TME mediated by AS-IV (reduced inhibitory factors, improved vasculature and stroma) may facilitate the survival, infiltration, and functional persistence of infused T cells at tumor sites ().

Therapeutic vaccines

AS-IV enhances DC function and antigen presentation, potentially augmenting the efficiency of tumor vaccines in eliciting specific T cell responses ().

Strategic considerations for combining AS-IV with first-line HCC agents

Despite strong theoretical synergies, the translation of AS-IV-based combinations requires careful consideration of timing, dose ratios, and potential pharmacological antagonism, particularly given AS-IV’s modulation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, which overlaps with the mechanisms of several first-line HCC drugs ().

Anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy

Optimal timing and sequence

Preclinical evidence indicates that AS-IV pretreatment can convert an immune “cold” tumor into a “hot” one, suggesting that a lead-in period of AS-IV monotherapy followed by ICI administration may maximize synergism (). Concurrent administration, while potentially more convenient, might prematurely expose reinvigorated T cells to an incompletely remodeled TME, risking suboptimal activation or even T-cell exhaustion. Future clinical trials should explore both sequential (AS-IV → ICI) and concurrent regimens with integrated biomarker analysis (e.g., CD8+ T-cell infiltration after lead-in).

Dose ratio

No optimal dose ratio has been established; however, preclinical models suggest that moderate immunomodulatory doses of AS-IV (capable of M2→M1 polarization without systemic immunosuppression) should be combined with standard ICI doses. Overly high AS-IV doses could theoretically suppress necessary immune checkpoints on effector cells or increase immune-related adverse events (irAEs). Clinical combination studies should include AS-IV dose escalation with pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic monitoring.

Antagonistic risks

AS-IV’s ability to downregulate PD-L1 on M2-TAMs may theoretically reduce the number of PD-L1 targets available for anti-PD-L1 antibodies, potentially diminishing drug occupancy and efficacy (). However, this effect is likely offset by the global increase in CD8+ T-cell infiltration and function. A more relevant risk is the overlapping toxicity: AS-IV-enhanced immune activation could increase the incidence or severity of irAEs when combined with ICIs, necessitating careful safety monitoring ().

Multitargeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs)

Overlap in PI3K/Akt/mTOR inhibition

Lenvatinib and sorafenib inhibit VEGFR and FGFR, both of which signal partly through the PI3K/Akt/mTOR axis (). AS-IV also suppresses this pathway via multiple upstream targets (e.g., PTEN upregulation, PI3K inhibition) (). Combined inhibition may result in:

- Potentiated anti-angiogenic and anti-tumor effects, through deeper suppression of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway and more complete blockade of compensatory signaling.
- Potential antagonism or excessive toxicity: If the pathway is overly suppressed, normal tissue homeostasis (e.g., hepatic regeneration, immune cell signaling) may be compromised, leading to increased adverse effects such as fatigue, hepatotoxicity, or impaired wound healing. Additionally, some TKIs may increase hypoxia in the TME, which could paradoxically promote M2 polarization—an effect that AS-IV may partially counteract through macrophage reprogramming ().

Strategic framework

Preclinical experiments should systematically evaluate the effect of AS-IV on lenvatinib/sorafenib IC50 values in HCC cell lines under normoxic and hypoxic conditions, as well as the net immune-modulatory outcome in co-culture systems. A staggered schedule (e.g., AS-IV after TKI-induced vascular normalization) may warrant exploration. At present, no clinical evidence supports combination, and the risk-benefit ratio must be clarified before proceeding.

Development strategies for novel delivery systems

The inherent pharmacokinetic limitations of AS-IV (low oral bioavailability, lack of targeting) represent one of the major bottlenecks in its clinical translation. The development of advanced delivery systems is critical to enhancing its efficacy and safety.

Nano-delivery systems

Types

Including liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles (PLGA, chitosan), solid lipid nanoparticles, mesoporous silica nanoparticles, nanoemulsions, and others. These systems can effectively improve the water solubility of AS-IV, protect it from degradation, and achieve passive targeting using the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect of solid tumors ().

Advantages

Prolonging blood circulation time, increasing drug accumulation at tumor sites, and reducing exposure to normal tissues, thereby potentially improving the therapeutic index.

Active targeting strategies

Modifying the surface of nanocarriers with targeting ligands to achieve cell- or tissue-specific delivery.

Targeting TAMs

Modifying with mannose, galactose, or phosphatidylserine analogs to achieve specific drug accumulation within TAMs by leveraging the highly expressed mannose receptors, scavenger receptors, or Tim-4 receptors on TAM surfaces—improving local concentration, enhancing reprogramming effects, and reducing systemic impacts ().

Targeting tumor vasculature

Modifying with RGD peptides to target integrin αvβ3, which is highly expressed on tumor neovascular endothelium.

Targeting tumor cells

Modifying with folic acid to target tumor cells with high folate receptor expression.

Stimuli-responsive drug release systems

Designing “smart” carriers that respond to the unique characteristics of the TME to achieve precise drug release at target sites.

pH-sensitive

The TME and TAM lysosomes are weakly acidic. Using pH-sensitive chemical bonds (such as hydrazone bonds) to link drugs, or selecting pH-sensitive carrier materials (such as PEGylated liposomes), for rapid drug release in acidic environments ().

Enzyme-sensitive

Certain enzymes (such as MMPs and cathepsins) have excessively high activity at tumor sites or within TAMs. Designing peptide segments specifically cleaved by these enzymes to link drugs or serve as part of nanocarriers.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS)-sensitive

ROS levels are elevated in the TME and within activated immune cells. Using materials containing ROS-sensitive bonds such as thioketals.

Dual/multiple responsiveness

Combining two or more response mechanisms for more precise control.

Combination drug delivery nano-platforms

Constructing nano-platforms for co-delivery of AS-IV with ICIs (such as anti-PD-1 antibodies) or other small molecule inhibitors (such as STAT3 inhibitors) to achieve spatiotemporally synchronized synergistic therapy, simplifying dosing regimens, and potentially altering drug distribution and pharmacokinetics through carrier properties ().

Challenges in fibrotic/cirrhotic liver: penetration, long-term safety, and immunogenicity

The clinical application of nano-delivery systems for AS-IV in HCC must also address the pathology of fibrotic/cirrhotic livers. First, dense extracellular matrix and elevated interstitial pressure severely limit nanoparticle penetration, causing perivascular trapping that compromises drug delivery to deeper tumor regions. Second, the long-term fate and toxicity of carrier materials remain poorly characterized; PLGA degradation can release acidic byproducts that exacerbate local inflammation, while inorganic nanoparticles may accumulate in Kupffer cells and hepatocytes, disrupting metabolic and immune functions. Third, repeated administration of targeted nanocarriers carries immunogenicity risks, including anti-polymer antibody production, complement activation-related pseudoallergy, and accelerated blood clearance—effects likely amplified in patients with chronic liver inflammation. Addressing these challenges requires optimizing nanocarrier design for enhanced stromal penetration, evaluating biocompatibility in fibrotic liver models, and exploring biomimetic strategies that minimize off-target recognition while preserving TAM-specific accumulation.

Safety and toxicity

The clinical translation of AS-IV for HCC requires thorough safety evaluation. Preclinical studies show a favorable safety profile, with an LD50 exceeding 80 mg/kg (i.v.) in rodents, suggesting a wide therapeutic window (). However, several concerns merit attention, particularly given that most HCC patients have underlying cirrhosis and compromised hepatic function.

First, high-dose AS-IV has been associated with mild, reversible hepatotoxicity in some animal models, manifesting as transient ALT/AST elevations and subtle histopathological changes—raising concerns for patients with pre-existing hepatic impairment.

Second, AS-IV’s immunomodulatory effects carry theoretical risks of immune-related adverse events, especially when combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors. Excessive M1-like macrophage activation could potentially trigger cytokine release syndrome or exacerbate autoimmunity in susceptible individuals.

Third, long-term safety in cirrhosis remains poorly characterized. Cirrhotic livers exhibit altered drug-metabolizing enzyme activity, compromised biliary excretion, and disrupted sinusoidal architecture, all of which may alter AS-IV pharmacokinetics and toxicity. Dedicated studies in cirrhotic models and patients with Child-Pugh A/B cirrhosis are warranted.

Finally, potential drug-drug interactions must be assessed, given AS-IV’s modulation of pathways (e.g., PI3K/Akt, AMPK) that intersect with those targeted by concurrent therapies (e.g., sorafenib, lenvatinib, ICIs). Comprehensive toxicological assessments in relevant disease models are essential before clinical evaluation.

Conclusion and prospects

A central feature of the HCC immunosuppressive microenvironment resides in the predominant role of M2-type TAMs, and targeting their phenotypic modulation has emerged as a pivotal therapeutic strategy. This review systematically summarizes current evidence indicating that AS-IV, as a multi-target immunomodulatory agent, can modulate this process through multi-level mechanisms. These concerted effects modulate macrophage polarization by suppressing M2-associated functions and partially promoting M1-like features with anti-tumor potential, which in turn contribute to the remodeling of the immune microenvironment. This multi-level regulatory network exemplifies the scientific rationale underlying the traditional Chinese medicine concept of “holistic modulation.” Looking ahead, the development of Astragaloside IV as an immunotherapeutic sensitizer, particularly when combined with ICIs, holds substantial translational potential. However, several challenges must be addressed, including overcoming pharmacokinetic limitations, developing targeted delivery systems, and undergoing rigorous clinical validation.
