The locus coeruleus calcitonin receptor can Be engaged by amylin and calcitonin gene-related peptide to suppress feeding without inducing nausea
Authors: Samantha M. Fortin, Marcos J. Sanchez-Navarro, Jiayin Hu, Maggie Zhou, Carlie Zhang, Marisa C. Petticord, Brandon Alonso, Savanna A. Cohen, Matthew R. Hayes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Efforts to fully characterize the diversity of mechanisms underlying energy balance control have led to the identification of atypical sites of action for metabolic signals. The locus coeruleus (LC), a major noradrenergic nucleus of the brain, has recently been shown to regulate aspects of food intake and energy expenditure. We use complementary pharmacological, behavioral, immunohistochemical, and genetic approaches in both rats and mice to demonstrate the role of LC calcitonin receptors (CTR) in feeding behavior. LC neurons robustly express CTRs that can be pharmacologically and chemogenetically activated to potently inhibit food intake and body weight without inducing nausea or changes in autonomic physiology including heart rate, body temperature, and gastric emptying. We next examined the ability of amylin and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), two endogenous anorectic peptides that signal through the CTR, to modulate feeding through signaling in the LC. RNAscope analysis revealed that LC CTRs are in fact capable of responding to amylin and CGRP, as they co-express RAMP1, and microinjections of either peptide to the LC induces anorexia without nausea. Together, these findings identify LC CTRs as a previously unrecognized neural substrate through which amylin and CGRP signaling suppress feeding, with direct relevance to the mechanisms underlying emerging amylin-based obesity therapeutics.

Introduction

Energy balance is coordinated by distributed neural circuits that regulate feeding, energy expenditure, and adaptive behavioral responses. It is now appreciated that widespread nuclei of the brain serve as entry points and relay stations for the integration of peripheral metabolic cues with central representations of internal state and environmental context to coordinate energy balance [1]. Recently, the locus coeruleus (LC), a nucleus classically implicated in arousal, vigilance, and stress responding [2], has established itself as a contributor to feeding behavior. Optogenetic and chemogenetic studies in mice have shown that activation of LC norepinephrine neurons attenuates feeding behavior [3,4] and pharmacological manipulation of classical feeding receptors, including those for the hormones glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and ghrelin, in the LC bidirectionally control appetite [5,6]. Together, these findings position the LC as an integrative node where metabolic signals, the extent of which is still unknown, shape feeding behavior.

The calcitonin receptor (CTR) is abundantly expressed in the LC [7,8], suggesting that this nucleus is positioned to respond to multiple members of the calcitonin peptide family, including calcitonin, calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), and amylin. Amylin is a well-established anorectic hormone that regulates energy balance and exerts its effects through amylin receptors, which are formed by heterodimerization of the CTR with one of three receptor activity-modifying proteins (RAMP1-3) [9,10]. Activation of these receptor complexes suppresses food intake, delays gastric emptying, and reduces postprandial glucagon secretion [9,10], making amylin an effective therapy for both diabetes and obesity [[11], [12], [13]]. More recently, Dual Amylin and Calcitonin Receptor Agonists (DACRAs), including cagrilintide, have demonstrated weight-loss efficacy comparable to current GLP-1 receptor agonist therapies [11,14]. Because DACRAs activate multiple CTR-containing receptor complexes, including the AMY1 receptor (CTR:RAMP1), understanding the anatomical distribution and functional roles of these receptors within the brain is of increasing therapeutic importance) [10,15,16]. In addition to amylin, the AMY1 receptor is activated by CGRP) [10,15,16]. CGRP signaling has an increasingly recognized role in feeding suppression and aversion [8,[17], [18], [19], [20]], although these studies have implicated CGRP-expressing neurons rather than directly establishing CGRP as the critical neurotransmitter mediating these effects. CGRP also signals through the canonical CGRP receptor composed of the calcitonin receptor-like receptor (CLR) and RAMP1 (CLR:RAMP1) [10]. Because the LC expresses the molecular components required for both AMY and CGRP receptor complexes, it may represent an important yet understudied site through which calcitonin-family peptides influence ingestive behavior. Defining these central sites of action is critical for understanding the mechanisms through which current and next-generation CTR agonists produce their therapeutic effects [[21], [22], [23], [24], [25]].

Here, we use pharmacological and chemogenetic approaches to show that LC CTR activation suppresses feeding in the mouse and rat. We demonstrate that LC CTRs co-express the necessary RAMPs to permit amylin and CGRP signaling, and that pharmacological delivery of either CTR ligand to the LC suppresses food intake. Importantly, LC CTR manipulations do not recruit illness-like behaviors or autonomic impairments that are observed under other conditions of LC engagement [5,26,27] or following CTR agonism in other areas of the brain [9], [28], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35]. Together, these findings position the LC as a critical and dissociable node for calcitonin receptor signaling in the central control of feeding behavior.

Materials and methods

Study design

Studies were designed to define the role of the LC CTR in the control of feeding behavior. We first used pharmacological and chemogenetic approaches in rats and mice to determine whether LC CTR activation suppresses food intake and body weight in chow-fed and high fat diet-fed animals. We then assessed the capacity of endogenous CTR ligands, amylin and CGRP, to modulate feeding via LC signaling. We used RNAscope in situ hybridization to determine co-expression of CTR with RAMPS in LC noradrenergic neurons and cFos immunohistochemistry to test excitation of LC cells by central delivery of amylin and CGRP. Finally, we examined whether the anorectic effects of LC CTR activation are accompanied by illness-like or autonomic responses including changes in heart rate, core body temperature, and gastric emptying. All testing was conducted 10 days after implantation of guide cannula and/or telemetry devices and four weeks after virus delivery. All experiments utilized separate cohorts of animals tested in a within-subjects, Latin-square design, with each treatment separated by 72 h. In the experiments conducted using a between-subject design, animals were randomly allocated to their relative treatment conditions. Treatment order was randomly selected for all within-subjects work. All biological tissues/samples were processed in a blinded fashion. Behavioral data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. See Supplemental Methods for more details.

Animals

Rat studies used adult male Sprague–Dawley rats (Charles River) and mouse studies used adult male C57BL/6 or Calcr- Cre knock-in mice (CTRCre; bred from Jax 037,028) and wildtype controls. All animals were individually housed in a temperature- and humidity-controlled satellite vivarium on a 12-hour reverse light/dark cycle and had ad libitum access, except where noted, to water and standard chow (5001 LabDiet) or a 60% high fat diet (D12492; Research Diets). All procedures were approved by the Institute of Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Pennsylvania.

Results

Chemogenetic activation of LC CTRs suppresses food intake in mice

To examine if LC CTRs contribute to food intake control, we used analyzed food intake and body weight changes following chemogenetic activation of CTR–expressing LC neurons. Experimental mice (Calcr-cre mice injected with an excitatory DREADD virus; CTRDq) injected with DCZ had reduced cumulative food intake at all measured time points relative to vehicle injected CTRDq mice and genetic control mice (wild type mice injected with an excitatory DREADD virus; CtrlDq) (Figure 1A). Analysis of body weight change 24 h after drug manipulation revealed a significant genotype × drug interaction, suggesting that chemogenetic activation of LC CTR cells influenced body weight change in a genotype-dependent manner (Fig 1B). Assessment of feeding microstructure revealed time-dependent effects on meal size and meal number. Average meal size was suppressed for 6 h following DCZ in the CTRDq mice (Fig 1C). Cumulative meal number showed a different temporal profile, with a significant genotype × drug interactions at 3, and 6 h, reflecting a delayed alteration in meal number suppression that is lost by 24 h (Fig 1D). Finally, under high-fat diet conditions, cumulative intake displayed significant main effects of genotype and drug at 1 h, with genotype × drug interactions emerging at 3 h and persisting through 24 h (Fig 1E). Under high-fat diet conditions, chemogenetic activation of CTR–expressing LC neurons did not have a significant effect on body weight (Fig 1F). Pharmacological activation of LC CTRs suppresses food intake in rats and mice

We next wanted to examine if pharmacological activation of LC CTRs would recapitulate the food intake suppressive effects observed with chemogenetic activation of the LC CTR. In rats, sCT administration significantly reduced cumulative chow intake at all post-injection timepoints (Figure 2A) and was accompanied by a reduction in body weight at 24hr (Fig 2B). Analysis of feeding microstructure revealed that sCT robustly decreased average meal size across all measured timepoints (Fig 2C) and cumulative meal number from 1 to 6 h (Fig 2D), indicating coordinated effects on both the size and frequency of meals. Similarly, in mice, sCT significantly suppressed cumulative chow intake at 3–24 h timepoints (Fig 2E) and reduced 24 h body weight (Fig 2F). Under high-fat diet conditions in rats, sCT markedly reduced cumulative intake across timepoints (Fig 2G), demonstrating efficacy across dietary contexts (Fig 2H). Despite strong effects on intake, body weight changes were modest and variable. The LC expresses receptor complexes to permit amylin and CGRP signaling and both amylin and CGRP activate the LC

To phenotype CTR expressing cells in the LC, RNAscope was conducted in mouse and rat (Figure 3A–B). Analysis revealed that in both species, most LC CTR-expression is on noradrenergic neurons (79% and 91.8% of CTR cells in mouse and rat, respectively, co-express DBH; Fig 3C). Furthermore, most LC CTRs express RAMP1 (90.3% of CTR cells in mouse and 74.2% of CTR cells in rat; Fig 3D) to permit amylin and CGRP signaling. We also analyzed co-expression of CTR with RAMP2 or RAMP3 in the mouse and rat (Fig 3D). In mouse, RAMP2 was expressed on 53.2% of CTR cells and RAMP3 was expressed on 5.5% of CTR cells. In the rat, we observed a different RAMP expression pattern than in the mouse. RAMP2 and RAMP3 were expressed 90.4% and 40.1% of CTR cells, respectively. To investigate the potential for CGRP to signal through not only the AMY1 receptor (CTR:Ramp1), but also the CLR + Ramp1, we ran RNAscope in the rat (Fig 3E). Of the LC DBH-expressing neurons in the LC, 30.8% of the cells expressed the CLR together with RAMP1 (Fig 3F). Expression of the receptor complexes that permit amylin and CGRP signaling in the LC suggests a site of action for these ligands directly within the LC. LC amylin suppresses food intake in rats and mice

To examine if observed amylin receptors in the LC contribute to food intake regulation, we took a pharmacological approach in rats and mice. In rats, administration of salmon calcitonin (sCT) or amylin directly to the LC significantly reduced cumulative chow intake across all post-administration timepoints examined (1–24 h) relative to vehicle. At 6 and 24 h the food intake suppression by sCT was more potent than amylin (Fig A). Intake-suppressive effects of sCT were accompanied by a change in body weight, with sCT producing a reduction in body weight relative to vehicle or amylin (Figure 4B). In mice, treatment-dependent effects on cumulative chow intake emerged at later timepoints. Both sCT and amylin significantly reduced cumulative intake at 3 and 6 h relative to vehicle, whereas at 24 h only sCT remained different from vehicle (Fig 4C). Body weight responses in mice paralleled intake effects, with sCT producing significant weight loss relative to vehicle (Fig 4D). Analysis of feeding microstructure in mice revealed time- and treatment-dependent effects. Average meal size was significantly reduced at intermediate timepoints, with sCT reducing meal size relative to vehicle at 3 and 6 h timepoints, and amylin reducing meal size relative to vehicle only at 3 h (Fig 4E). In contrast, at the selected doses, cumulative meal number was more broadly affected. Salmon calcitonin reduced meal number relative to vehicle at all timepoints examined (1–24 h), whereas amylin reduced meal number at later timepoints (3–24 h) (Fig 4F). LC CGRP suppresses food intake in rats

Given the presence of CGRP receptors (CTR:RAMP1 and CLR:Ramp1), we examined if LC delivery of CGRP could suppress food intake. CGRP produced modest, time-dependent effects on feeding behavior (Figure 5A). Analysis of cumulative chow intake revealed no effect of CGRP at the earliest timepoint, followed by a significant reduction in intake at intermediate timepoints and a trend toward reduced intake at the latest interval. CGRP significantly reduced body weight relative to vehicle treated animals at 24 h (Fig 5B). In contrast to sCT and amylin, CGRP did not significantly alter average meal size at any timepoint examined (Fig 5C). Instead, the reductions in food intake were driven by changes in meal frequency (Fig 5D). CGRP significantly reduced cumulative meal number at later timepoints, with no effect observed at earlier intervals. LC CTR signaling is not associated with measures of avoidance or nausea

To assess whether feeding suppression induced by calcitonin-family peptides or activation of CTR–expressing LC neurons is associated with nausea-related behavior, we examined conditioned flavor preference and kaolin intake. In the conditioned preference assay, the aversive agent lithium chloride produced a robust aversive response, significantly reducing flavor preference relative to both control and CTRDq mice treated with DCZ. In contrast, DCZ did not alter flavor preference in either genotype, and no difference was observed between CtrlDq-DCZ and CTRDq-DCZ groups, indicating that chemogenetic activation of LC CTR neurons does not induce conditioned aversion (Figure 6A). Across peptide treatments including sCT (Fig 6B), amylin (Fig 6C), and CGRP (Fig 6D), no evidence of nausea-related behavior was observed. LC delivery of all drugs failed to increase kaolin intake relative to vehicle at any post-administration timepoint (1–24 h). LC CTR signaling is not associated with abnormal autonomic signaling

To determine whether sCT alters physiological or behavioral processes that could confound interpretation of feeding-related effects, we assessed thermoregulation, cardiovascular activity, drug absorption, and locomotion following LC delivery of sCT. Salmon calcitonin produced a mild increase in body temperature that was not significant when area under the curves for vehicle- and sCT-treated conditions were compared (Figure 7A). Heart rate was not affected by sCT administration, with no differences observed in heart rate AUC relative to vehicle (Fig 7B). To assess whether sCT influenced gastric emptying or systemic absorption, plasma acetaminophen levels were measured following oral administration. AUC values did not differ between vehicle and sCT conditions, indicating that sCT did not alter acetaminophen absorption (Fig 7C). Finally, analysis of locomotor behavior revealed no differences in cumulative activity between vehicle- and sCT-treated conditions at any timepoint examined (Fig 7D). Discussion

The role of the LC in the control of feeding behavior is only beginning to be appreciated, despite the long-standing recognition of this nucleus as a critical regulator of arousal, autonomic function, and stress responsiveness. Separately, although central amylin and calcitonin signaling have been studied for decades, the precise neural substrates through which these peptides suppress food intake remain uncharacterized. The present study identifies the LC as a previously unrecognized site at which pharmacological and chemogenetic activation of CTR signaling robustly suppresses food intake. These findings provide mechanistic insight into the hindbrain circuits that can be engaged by calcitonin-family peptides and therapeutic CTR agonists to influence ingestive behavior.

Here we show that the LC is a site of CTR expression at which pharmacological and chemogenetic activation robustly suppresses food intake, extending the central amylin/calcitonin literature beyond the canonical area postrema–nucleus tractus solitarius axis. Prior anatomical and binding studies have reported dense CTR-like immunoreactivity and calcitonin-family ligand binding in the LC in both rodents and primates, consistent with the presence of functional CTR-containing receptor complexes in this nucleus [10], [15], [36]. Building on this foundation, we demonstrate that both pharmacological activation of LC CTRs using sCT and chemogenetic activation of LC CTR–expressing neurons robustly suppress food intake. Notably, these effects, which were consistent between rats and mice, were accompanied by reductions in meal size and meal number at the doses tested. Future dose–response studies using lower, subthreshold doses will help determine the degree to which LC CTR signaling influences meal architecture. The accompanying reductions in 24-hour body weight are likely attributable, at least in part, to the robust suppression of food intake and the resulting reduction in gastrointestinal contents. These findings identify the LC as a novel hindbrain node through which these peptides can engage noradrenergic circuitry to regulate ingestive behavior.

An important conceptual distinction of the present work is that our experiments establish the LC CTR as a pharmacologically and chemogenetically recruitable site of action that produces robust hypophagia. These findings do not establish that endogenous LC CTR signaling is required for physiological meal regulation, a question that will require future receptor-selective antagonist, conditional receptor deletion, or inhibitory chemogenetic approaches. Rather, our results identify the LC as a previously unrecognized neural substrate that can be engaged by calcitonin receptor agonists to suppress food intake. Defining the central circuits recruited by pharmacological CTR agonism is of particular translational importance given the rapidly expanding clinical development of these compounds for obesity and metabolic disease.

CTR signaling in the brain is incredibly complex. There are two known splice variants of the CTR (CTRa/b) and three RAMPS (1–3), resulting in at least 6 different amylin receptor subtypes [10], [15], [37]. Historically, localization and function of central CTR subtypes has been hampered by availability of RAMP antibodies [[36], [38]] and lack of CTR subtype-specific agonists and antagonists. Recent advances using RNAscope in situ hybridization have begun to resolve these limitations by enabling high-sensitivity, cell-type–specific detection of CTR and Ramp transcripts, allowing more precise mapping of functional receptor complexes in the CNS [39]. Using this approach, we demonstrate that LC neurons express the molecular machinery required for both amylin and CGRP signaling, including CTR together with Ramp1–3 as well as the CLR together with Ramp1. Our results provide strong evidence that multiple CTR-based receptor complexes are present within this nucleus.

Consistent with this molecular profile, we find that both amylin and CGRP suppress feeding when injected into the LC. Anorectic effects are more pronounced for amylin, suggesting ligand- and receptor-complex–specific signaling biases. Such differences may reflect distinct CTR:RAMP stoichiometries, signaling efficacies, or downstream coupling, questions that remain unresolved but are beginning to be addressed by recent genetic studies, including RAMP2 and RAMP3 knockout models, which highlight nonredundant roles for specific RAMPs in metabolic regulation [40], [41]. The abundance of the AMY1 and AMY3 receptor complexes in the LC is particularly interesting given that cagrilinitide, an amylin analog being explored for the treatment of obesity, induces weight loss through these receptors [42]. The nuances of AMY1 and AMY3 signaling have yet to be determined. Additionally, whether the AMY2 receptor, shown here to be expressed in the LC but also expressed in the hypothalamus [[43], [44], [45]], contributes to food intake control is unclear, due in part to RAMP2 knockout mice being embryonic lethal [40]. Furthermore, the impact of co-expression of multiple AMY receptors on feeding behavior, or co-expression of AMY and CGRP receptors, has not been investigated but is relevant for the LC where we observe overlapping receptor expression patterns. Defining the precise mechanism through which these agents act is essential for understanding their therapeutic effects and side–effect profiles, and for guiding the rational development of next-generation compounds that more selectively engage beneficial anorectic circuits while minimizing aversive outcomes.

An important finding of the present study is that the anorectic effects of LC CTR activation are preserved in animals maintained on a high-fat diet, a metabolic state commonly associated with resistance to many endogenous satiety signals [46]. Both pharmacological activation of LC CTRs and chemogenetic engagement of LC CTR–expressing neurons continued to suppress food intake in HFD-fed animals, indicating that this circuit remains responsive despite diet-induced obesity and chronic positive energy balance. The preservation of LC CTR–mediated feeding suppression suggests that CTR signaling within the LC may bypass or remain insulated from diet-induced adaptations that limit the effectiveness of other anorectic pathways. From a translational perspective, this resilience highlights LC CTR circuits as a promising target for anti-obesity therapeutics, as sustained efficacy in obesogenic states is a key determinant of clinical utility.

Activation of LC CTR signaling by sCT, amylin, or CGRP produced minimal adverse physiological or illness-like effects, despite robust suppression of food intake. Specifically, calcitonin-family drug delivery to the LC did not increase kaolin intake (i.e. pica), markedly delay gastric emptying, or induce profound changes in heart rate or core body temperature—measures commonly used to index visceral malaise and autonomic stress in rodents [[47], [48]]. These endpoints were selected based on our prior work showing that activation of LC GLP-1 receptors with exendin-4 produces clear autonomic and illness-like effects, including delayed gastric emptying, cardiovascular and thermoregulatory changes, and increased kaolin consumption, consistent with engagement of visceral stress pathways [5]. Both native amylin and sCT analogs have been reported to increase energy expenditure in rodents [[49], [50], [51]]. LC CTRs do not, however, appear to be involved in autonomic regulation. Also, in contrast to LC GLP-1R stimulation [5], we did not observe any pica following SCT or amylin delivery to the LC. This effect is consistent with CTR signaling elsewhere, as comparable doses and CTR activation in other areas of the brain do not cause aversion or sickness-like behaviors that are indicative of nausea [[52], [53], [54], [55]]. We also did not observe pica or the formation of a CTA with LC CGRP administration although CGRP signaling has been attributed to pain, aversion and malaise-related responses [17,19]. The absence of comparable effects following LC CTR and GLP-1R activation suggests that although both calcitonin-family and GLP-1 receptor ligands converge anatomically within the LC, they recruit distinct downstream circuits and physiological outputs. The autonomic and aversive effects of LC engagement do not appear to be an obligatory consequence of LC activation but instead reflect differences in ligand pharmacology and the neural circuits engaged. Furthermore, our data support the conclusion that LC CTR–mediated anorexia reflects a hypophagic mechanism rather than a secondary consequence of autonomic activation or visceral illness, highlighting a potentially advantageous therapeutic profile for CTR-targeting agents.

Despite clear evidence that LC CTR engagement can suppress feeding, the mechanism(s) by which pharmacological CTR ligands access and recruit this nucleus remains incompletely defined. Similarly, a central open question is how native amylin reaches the LC in vivo, and to that extent, why the LC expresses the CTR. The LC sits within a highly vascularized and densely interconnected pontine hub that integrates interoceptive and arousal signals [27]. Penetrance of peripherally secreted amylin across the blood brain barrier into the LC has not been demonstrated with fluorescently labeled molecules [39] but remains a possibility. Indeed, radioactively labeled amylin can cross the blood brain barrier [56], however the exact mechanism for amylin transport across the blood brain barrier is not yet defined. The existence of a central amylin source is also worth considering [9]. Accumulating evidence suggests that amylin secretion in the brain is sexually dimorphic, with the most occurring in lactating dams [57], however, there also appears to be upregulation in central amylin production by diet and leptin status in males [58]. Investigating sexual dimorphism in LC amylin signaling will be pursued in future studies and is supported by known sex differences in both amylin signaling [57], [58] and LC signaling [[59], [60], [61]].

It is likely that LC CGRP receptors are the targets of centrally produced peptide, as CGRP produced in the periphery does not cross the blood brain barrier [62]. Potential sources of central CGRP include spinal trigeminal nucleus, parabrachial nucleus, raphe nuclei, thalamus, cerebellum, or neurons of the LC itself [10]. Given that these areas project to the LC [[63], [64],], the contribution of individual projections will be interesting to study. Additionally, which cell types and output projections of the LC are necessary and sufficient for CTR-mediated hypophagia also remains to be determined. We show that CTRs are densely expressed on DBH + neurons of the LC. Where these neurons project and what they potentially co-release beyond norepinephrine, is uncharacterized. It is known that LC circuitry is cellularly and functionally heterogeneous and as evidenced by our studies, CTR engagement biases LC ensembles differently than GLP-1R signaling. Recent advances in intersectional genetic approaches provide powerful tools to resolve how distinct receptor-defined ensembles contribute to behavior [65]. These approaches will be critical for delineating the downstream circuitry through which CTR signaling selectively suppresses feeding without engaging the aversive or autonomic outputs typically associated with broader LC activation.

Together, the current studies emphasize the importance of identifying brain-region–specific CTR signaling mechanisms that preserve anorectic efficacy while avoiding engagement of malaise-related pathways, an issue of direct relevance for the therapeutic development of calcitonin-family drugs. Current work is aimed at examining the translational relevance of LC CTRs to the weight loss following amylin analog therapies (i.e. cagrilintide, eloralintide) being developed to treat obesity. In addition, the present findings place LC CTR signaling at the intersection of metabolic control and broader brain-state regulation. The LC is increasingly recognized as a critical node in numerous pathological conditions, including Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder [66], underscoring the relevance of understanding how neuromodulatory signals shape LC function across physiological and disease contexts. Within this complex neurochemical landscape, our data demonstrate that LC CTR engagement suppresses food intake and body weight while largely sparing aversive and autonomic side effects, highlighting a previously unrecognized facet of LC biology. Importantly, despite the pharmacological complexity of calcitonin-family receptors, the robust anorectic, and weight-lowering effects of amylin make it an especially attractive target for anti-obesity and anti-diabetic drug development. By defining a novel LC-based site of action for CTR signaling, this work advances mechanistic understanding of central amylin pathways and provides a framework for developing next-generation therapeutics that harness the metabolic benefits of LC CTR activation while minimizing adverse effects.
