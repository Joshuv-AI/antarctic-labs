PrismNet: Viewing Time Series Through a Multi-Modal Prism for Interpretable Power Load Forecasting
Yuxuan Chen, Shuo Dai, Ruoyi Xu, Haipeng Xie

Abstract
Load forecasting plays a pivotal role in the safe and stable operation of power systems. Conventional deep learning methods often struggle to adapt to few-shot scenarios frequently encountered in industrial applications. Existing multi-modal approaches typically overlook domain-specific cross-modal semantic alignment and lack sufficient mechanism interpretability. To address these challenges, this study proposes PrismNet, an interpretable multi-modal framework for power load forecasting. First, a multi-modal augment module integrates text and image modalities to strengthen load time series representations, empowering the model with few-shot learning capabilities. Subsequently, we design a Partial Information Decomposition (PID) guided multi-modal contrastive learning (CL) mechanism to achieve domain-specific cross-modal semantic alignment. This process elucidates the intrinsic interactions among modalities and offers a new lens for interpretability. Extensive experiments on real-world public datasets demonstrate that PrismNet outperforms strong deep learning and multi-modal baselines, particularly in few-shot settings, while providing a trustworthy and interpretable solution for safety-critical electric load scenarios. Our code is available at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/PrismNet-9DFC.



I
Introduction

Electric power is a fundamental energy source underpinning modern industry, economic growth, and daily life. Accurate power load forecasting is essential for optimizing resource allocation
[
1
]
, improving unit commitment strategies, and ensuring the secure and stable operation of the power system
[
2
]
.

Electric load forecasting methods can be broadly categorized into statistical modeling and data-driven approaches based on artificial intelligence. Statistical modeling primarily relies on autoregressive time series paradigms, such as ARIMA, exponential smoothing. These statistical models are often combined with additional pre-processing and post-processing steps to solve short-term load forecasting tasks in early studies
[
3
,
4
]
. While such methods are theoretically straightforward and lightweight, they heavily depend on data stationarity and pronounced trend or seasonal patterns, and are incapable of capturing long-term dependencies and nonlinear characteristics inherent in power load time series.

Data-driven approaches based on artificial intelligence have become mainstream in recent years. Early methods centered on recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which are adept at capturing temporal dependencies and inter-variable relationships
[
5
]
. With the advent of the Transformer architecture
[
6
]
, attention-based models have advanced rapidly for time series forecasting, giving rise to numerous variants
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
that have been widely applied to electric load forecasting
[
10
,
11
]
. However, despite the impressive results of Transformer-based deep learning models, their cross-entity generalization and performance in few-shot settings remain limited, making them ill-suited to the data-scarce scenarios common in industrial practice.

More recently, driven by advances in multi-modal large models, researchers have explored leveraging auxiliary modalities to achieve more accurate and more data-availability–robust forecasting. Specifically, LLM-based forecasting has been widely applied to industrial scenarios such as electric load, traffic flow, weather etc. Some approaches recast time series forecasting as prompt-based inference, converting sequences into textual prompts for an LLM and letting the LLM output predictions
[
12
,
13
]
. This bypasses large-scale retraining; however, pre-trained LLMs often struggle to capture fine-grained temporal dynamics. Other studies fine-tune or retrain parts of the encoder and decoder layers to encode numerical information effectively and exploit the model’s prior knowledge for few-shot learning (FSL)
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
. Nevertheless, the inherent modality gap between numerical signals and textual representations continues to limit performance.

Beyond text, researchers have explored image-based representations for time series forecasting. Image modalities (e.g., line plots and grayscale maps) can explicitly preserve temporal dependencies and spatial correlations. Forecasting is then performed by embedding images into a vector space with pretrained visual encoders and combining them with Transformer-based architectures
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
. Nevertheless, since large vision models are optimized for image-specific features, their transfer to numerical modalities remains limited.

Most prior work emphasizes using auxiliary modalities to boost prediction accuracy, but often neglects cross-modal semantic alignment, especially the alignment of domain-specific knowledge. This omission becomes more pronounced in industrial applications, where model interpretability is indispensable. Without clear interpretability, the internal mechanisms through which multi-modal inputs enhance forecasting remain obscure. Such opacity undermines confidence in the results and hampers adoption in safety-critical electric-load scenarios.

In this context, we propose PrismNet, a foundation model for interpretable multi-modal power load forecasting. The name is inspired by an optical prism that separates light into its constituent wavelengths; analogously, PrismNet leverages textual and visual views to uncover deeper patterns in raw time series data, yielding more comprehensive and explainable predictions. The framework comprises three components:
1) Multi-modal Augment
module converts the raw load series into auxiliary text and image modalities.
2) Tri-Modality Encoding
embeds all three modalities into a shared vector space and performs forecasting with a cross-attention Transformer.
3) Interpretable Alignment
module achieves domain-specific cross-modal semantic alignment via multi-modal contrastive learning. As shown in Fig. 1, unlike conventional deep models and prior multi-modal approaches, our method uses text and image to strengthen the representation of time series modality. Moreover, grounded in partial information decomposition, we reveal the underlying mechanisms of multi-modal interactions, providing a bridge between implicit learning paradigms and industry-friendly interpretability.

The main contributions are threefold:

1) We introduce PrismNet, an interpretable multi-modal framework for power load forecasting that integrates text and image to strengthen load time series representations.

2) We develop a multi-modal contrastive learning strategy grounded in partial information decomposition, which elucidates the intrinsic interactions among modalities and offers a new lens for interpretability.

3) Extensive experiments on real-world public datasets demonstrate that PrismNet outperforms strong deep-learning and multi-modal baselines, while achieving superior few-shot learning capability and improved model interpretability.

II
Related Works

II-A

Deep Learning-based Approaches

This section focuses on deep learning-based approaches for time series forecasting. Early methodologies, including Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), demonstrated strong capabilities in capturing temporal and inter-channel dependencies
[
20
]
. More recently, the field has been dominated by Transformer-based architectures. Seminal works in this area include Informer
[
8
]
, which introduced an efficient ProbSparse self-attention mechanism, Autoformer
[
7
]
, which leveraged series decomposition and an auto-correlation mechanism, and FEDformer
[
9
]
, which innovatively applied attention in the frequency domain. More recent innovations like DLinear
[
21
]
achieve efficient forecasting with a simple linear structure, while PatchTST
[
22
]
adapted the Transformer encoder to process time series patches. These state-of-the-art methods have found widespread application in various industrial domains. Guo, et al.
[
5
]
proposed a BiLSTM-based framework for load forecasting via multi-task learning. Zhao, et al.
[
10
]
and Wang, et al.
[
11
]
respectively utilized a hybrid attention Transformer and a Bayesian Transformer for multi-energy load forecasting. However, despite their significant success, these deep learning-based methods still exhibit limitations in cross-domain generalization and performance under data scarcity, making them difficult to adapt to the few-shot scenarios prevalent in industrial applications.

II-B

Multi-Modal for Time Series

The integration of multi-modal analysis has recently emerged as a promising direction for time series forecasting. While early works like MIF-DNQR
[
23
]
demonstrated the benefits of fusing multi-source information for probabilistic load forecasting, their scope was confined to integrating heterogeneous time series data. The advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) has paved the way for text-enhanced forecasting. Approaches like PromptCast
[
12
]
and TRACE
[
13
]
formulated the task using natural language prompts. However, their efficacy was hindered by the intrinsic domain gap between numerical series and textual representations. To address this, GPT4TS
[
24
]
and LLM4TS
[
15
]
re-trained tokenizers on time series data to enhance the LLM’s understanding of the numerical modality. Building on this, TimeLLM
[
16
]
and UniTime
[
14
]
leveraged pre-trained prior knowledge for successful few-shot forecasting. A key limitation, however, is that these methods primarily focused on refining time series embeddings rather than explicitly aligning representations across different modalities.

Beyond text, image has been explored as another modality to enrich time series analysis. VisionTS
[
17
]
employed a pre-trained visual autoencoder for zero-shot forecasting, however this method ignored the inter-variable interactions. TimeMixer++
[
18
]
utilized multi-scale frequency representations from images to improve the learning of temporal patterns. TimeVLM
[
19
]
employs a frozen large vision-language model (VLM) to obtain image embeddings, which are then used for multi-modal fusion forecasting. Nevertheless, existing methods suffer from two key limitations: inadequate multi-modal representation alignment and opaque fusion mechanisms that hinder interpretability, which is a significant barrier to deployment in safety-critical industrial applications.

III
Problem Statement

This study investigates the problem of power load forecasting, formulated as a classical time series prediction task. Let the historical load observations be represented as a sequence with
l
time steps and
d
channels:

Fig. 2:
The overall framework of PrismNet

{{X}_{1:l}}=\{{{x}_{1}},{{x}_{2}},...,{{x}_{l}}\}\in{{R}^{d\times l}}

(1)

where
x_{t}
denotes the observation at time step
t
.

In addition, we perform multi-modal augmentation on the load time series to obtain auxiliary Text and Image modalities:

\begin{cases}T=LL{{M}_{\text{finetuned}}}({{X}_{1:l}})\\
I=BilinearInterp(Conv(FFT({{X}_{1:l}})))\end{cases}

(2)

Our goal is to learn an end-to-end function that takes
({{X}_{1:l}},T,I)
as input and outputs the predicted load values for the next
h
steps.

{{\hat{X}}_{l:l+h}}=\{{{\hat{x}}_{l}},{{\hat{x}}_{l+1}},...,{{\hat{x}}_{l+h}}|{{X}_{1:l}},T,I\}

(3)

We consider forecasting tasks across multiple load scenarios, including user-level, building-level, and regional-level load forecasting within distribution networks. The prediction horizons cover both short-term and long-term scales.

IV
Methodology

The overall framework of the proposed model, PrismNet, is shown in Fig. 2. PrismNet predicts power load from multiple modalities while remaining interpretable. The framework comprises three components:

•

Multi-modal Augment.
The raw load time series is converted into a text modality via an instruction-finetuned LLM, and into an image modality via temporal convolution followed by bilinear interpolation.

•

Tri-Modality Encoding.
We obtain embeddings for all three modalities and then employ a cross-attention Transformer as the forecaster.

•

Interpretable Alignment.
A PID-guided multi-modal contrastive learning paradigm aligns domain-specific knowledge across modalities and reveals the underlying mechanisms of multi-modal collaboration.

IV-A

Multi-Modal Augment

Text Modality.
To obtain a textual representation of the load series, we prompt pretrained LLMs to describe a segment of the time series. We adopt an instruction-tuning paradigm so that the LLM output follows a fixed template (Fig. 3, left). The textual view is composed of three parts:

T={{T}_{\text{task}}}+{{T}_{\text{stat}}}+{{T}_{\text{knowledge}}}

(4)

Here,
{T}_{\text{task}}
gives a brief description of the forecasting task (e.g., subject, resolution, and other metadata);
{T}_{\text{stat}}
summarizes statistics of the segment (seasonality, trend, min/max, mean, kurtosis, skewness, etc.); and
{T}_{\text{knowledge}}
encodes domain knowledge, such as load-specific patterns leveraged from the LLM’s pretrained priors knowledge to provide additional cues for downstream forecasting.

Image Modality.
We first extract frequency features via the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT):

FFT({{X}_{1:l}})[k]=\sum\nolimits_{t=0}^{l-1}{{{x}_{t}}\cdot{{e}^{-2\pi ikt/l}}}

(5)

where
k
is the (discrete) frequency index.

We then concatenate the FFT features with the original series and pass them through one 1-D convolution and two 2-D convolutions to obtain a reshaped feature map. To match the image size typically seen during CLIP pretraining, we resize it with bilinear interpolation:

I(u,v)=\sum_{i=0}^{1}\sum_{j=0}^{1}w_{ij}\,I(x_{i},y_{j})

(6)

where
(x_{i},y_{j})
are four neighboring integer coordinates of
(u,v)
, and
w_{ij}
are determined on relative distances.

After resizing, we normalize values to [0,255] and obtain the image modality
I\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{B\times H\times W}}
(we set
H\times W=224\times 224
)

Negatives construction.
Because different modalities exhibit substantial domain gaps, naive feature alignment may cause negative transfer. We therefore perform semantic alignment via contrastive learning, which requires abundant positive/negative pairs. For text, we construct negatives by:

•

Context swapping: replace metadata in the description (e.g., turn a user-level sequence into “Distribution network …”).

•

Semantic tampering: corrupt numeric statements (e.g., alter seasonality to “The seasonality is
<
22
>
”).

For images, we construct negatives by:

•

Patch swapping: crop an image and swap its patches.

•

Color jittering: randomly perturb brightness, contrast, and saturation.

Multi-modal augment and negatives construction

IV-B

Tri-Modality Encoding

Time Series Encoding.
We first patchify the load sequence and apply normalization. The normalized patches are then fed into a multi-head self-attention layer
Att{{n}_{\text{MH-self}}}(\cdot)
with a residual connection to capture temporal dependencies:

Att{{n}_{\text{MH-self}}}({{\bar{X}}^{i}})=Attention({{\omega}_{q}}{{\bar{X}}^{i}},{{\omega}_{k}}{{\bar{X}}^{i}},{{\omega}_{v}}{{\bar{X}}^{i}})

(7)

{{\tilde{X}}^{i}}=Att{{n}_{\text{MH-self}}}({{\bar{X}}^{i}})+{{\bar{X}}^{i}}

(8)

Here,
{{\bar{X}}^{i}}
denotes the
i
-th normalized patch segment, and
{{\omega}_{q}}
,
{{\omega}_{k}}
,
{{\omega}_{v}}
are learnable linear projections.

Finally, a feed-forward projection produces the time series embedding:

{{h}_{X}}=FCL({{\tilde{X}}^{i}})

(9)

Text Encoding.
We use a frozen GPT-2 to tokenize and represent the raw text. GPT-2 is a Transformer decoder with causal multi-head self-attention, which enforces that each token attends only to previous tokens and itself:

{{\bar{T}}^{i}}=Att{{n}_{\text{MMH-self}}}({{T}^{i}})+{{T}^{i}}

(10)

{{\tilde{T}}^{i}}=FFN({{\bar{T}}^{i}})+{{\bar{T}}^{i}}

(11)

We then attach a trainable Transformer-based encoder to adapt the textual features to the forecasting task:

{{h}_{T}}=Encode{{r}_{\text{prompt}}}({{\tilde{T}}^{i}})

(12)

Image Encoding.
Prior studies
[
19
,
17
]
treat the image corresponding to each segment as an independent input, failing to capture potential temporal dependencies across segments. We follow a standard temporal consistency design from video modeling: a group of
N
images
I\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{B\times N\times H\times W}}
is fed to a pretrained CLIP image encoder to obtain frame-wise embeddings
{{Z}_{I}}\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{B\times N\times 512}}
. We then attach a trainable GRU module to model within-group dependencies and produce the image representation
{{h}_{I}}\in{{\mathbb{R}}^{B\times N\times{{H}_{\text{hidden}}}}}
:

h_{t}^{I}=GRU({{z}_{t}},h_{t-1}^{I}),\text{ }{{h}_{I}}=[h_{1}^{I},...,h_{N}^{I}]

(13)

Forecaster.
We adopt a cross-modal multi-head attention decoder followed by a linear projection head. The time series embedding serves as queries, while the fused text-image representation provides the keys and values, yielding the final multi-step predictions.

IV-C

Interpretable Alignment

Recent multi-modal studies on power-load forecasting often rely on simple fusion schemes
[
19
,
25
]
, while overlooking cross-modal semantic alignment, particularly domain-specific knowledge. This misalignment hinders cross-modal compositional reasoning. To address this, we design a multi-modal contrastive learning strategy that aligns representations across modalities and enhances model interpretability.

Our design is inspired by partial information decomposition. PID posits that the information about a target can be decomposed into contributions induced by different factors
[
26
]
. We argue that the integrated gains achieved through interactions among modalities naturally accord with the PID perspective.

Let
\{{{M}_{1}},{{M}_{2}},...,{{M}_{n}}\}
denote a set of modalities,
Y
the prediction target, and
Info(\cdot)
the informative content provided by the n-modal ensemble. Then
Info(Y;{{M}_{1}},{{M}_{2}},...,{{M}_{n}})
can be viewed as the aggregate of three interaction types:
uniqueness
,
redundancy
, and
synergy
.

1) Uniqueness. Modality
{{M}_{i}}
may contribute information that no other modality
{{M}_{j}},i\neq j
provides. Let
{{U}_{i}}
denote the unique contribution of modality
i
.

2) Redundancy. Two modalities
{{M}_{i}}
and
{{M}_{j}}
can carry the same or overlapping information. We use
R
to denote the redundant contribution.

3) Synergy. The joint use of
{{M}_{i}}
and
{{M}_{j}}
can reveal additional information about
Y
that is unavailable from any single modality. We denote this cross-modal synergistic contribution by
S
. In the three-variable case, the interaction diagram in Fig. 4 illustrates that synergy is not the set-theoretic union of modalities; rather, it corresponds to genuinely novel information that emerges only from their combination
[
27
]
.

Fig. 4:
PID for three-variable system

In PrismNet, we consider three modalities—time series, text, and image—together with the prediction target
Y
, forming a
4
-variable PID system. Compared with 3-variable case, the 4-variable PID is more involved and introduces entangled information terms. The 4-variable PID CL has not been addressed
[
27
]
, but in our scenario we can simplify the standard 4-variable system based on the derivation below.

Lemma 1:
The information provided by a single modality and by a set of modalities can be expressed as combinations of redundancy
R
, uniqueness
U
, and synergy
S
:

\begin{cases}Info(Y;M_{i})=R+U_{i}\\
Info(Y;M_{1},...,M_{n})=R+S+\sum{U_{i}}\end{cases}

(14)

Definition 1:
When PID involves more than two modalities, the system becomes complex and
entangled information terms
arise. We define the entangled information between modalities
i
and
j
as
E{{I}_{ij}}
, which aggregates all cross-terms mixing redundancy and synergy related to
i
and
j
(e.g., the redundancy shared by the pairwise synergies {X,T} and {X,I}, denoted {X,T}{X,I}; see Appendix A, Fig. A2)

Corollary 1:
In 4-variable system
{{M}_{X}}
,
{{M}_{T}}
,
{{M}_{I}}
,
Y
, the information provided by different modality combinations admits the following simplifications (Proof is given in Appendix A)

\begin{cases}Info(Y;M_{X},M_{T})=S_{XT}+U_{T}+Info(Y;M_{X})+EI_{XT}\\
Info(Y;M_{X},M_{I})=S_{XI}+U_{I}+Info(Y;M_{X})+EI_{XI}\\
\begin{aligned} Info(Y;M_{X},M_{T},M_{I})={}&S_{XTI}+S_{XI}+S_{XT}+EI_{XTI}\\
&+Info(Y;M_{X})+U_{T}+U_{I}\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(15)

Assumption 1:
Let
{{f}_{\theta}}(\cdot)
denote the embedding function of a modality. We assume that, once the model is ideally trained, the embedding
f_{\theta}^{*}
preserves all the information carried by that modality. Formally,

Info(Y;f_{\theta}^{*}(M))\approx Info(Y;M)

(16)

Lemma 2:
Given Assumption 1 and Lemma 1, we obtain:

Info(Y;f_{\theta}^{*}({{M}_{i}}))\approx R+{{U}_{i}}

(17)

Assumption 2.
The auxiliary modalities
T
and
I
, together with their negatives
T^{\prime}
and
I^{\prime}
, have the capacity to collaborate with
X
to accomplish task
Y
. This is a mild assumption: even in unimodal settings it holds; otherwise the total available information would be insufficient for prediction.

Corollary 2.
Under Assumption 2, when training is adequate, the fused information can approximate the target
Y
. Combined with Lemma 2, we obtain:

\displaystyle Info(h_{F};f_{\theta}^{*}(M_{X}),f_{\theta}^{*}(M_{T}),f_{\theta}^{*}(M_{I}))

(18)

\displaystyle\approx Info(Y;f_{\theta}^{*}(M_{X}),f_{\theta}^{*}(M_{T}),f_{\theta}^{*}(M_{I}))

\displaystyle\approx Info(Y;X,T,I)

where
h_{F}
is the fused representation. The first approximation follows from Assumption 2 and the second from Lemma 2.

From the expressions above and Corollary 1, the last term aggregates all synergistic information. Meanwhile, by Lemma 2, the information contributed by any single modality decomposes into redundancy and uniqueness. This motivates our design: we adopt the classic
InfoNCE
loss to measure similarity between positive/negative pairs. Cross-modal losses capture redundancy
R
, while losses between the fused representation and each unimodal representation capture synergy
S
:

{\small\begin{cases}{{\mathcal{L}}_{rdn}}={{\alpha}_{1}}{{{\hat{I}}}_{NCE}}({{h}_{X}},{{h}_{T}})+{{\alpha}_{2}}{{{\hat{I}}}_{NCE}}({{h}_{X}},{{h}_{I}})\\
{{\mathcal{L}}_{syn}}={{\beta}_{1}}{{{\hat{I}}}_{NCE}}({{h}_{F}},{{h}_{X}})+{{\beta}_{2}}{{{\hat{I}}}_{NCE}}({{h}_{F}},{{h}_{T}})+{{\beta}_{3}}{{{\hat{I}}}_{NCE}}({{h}_{F}},{{h}_{I}})\end{cases}}

(19)

The meaning of Eq. (19) is straightforward:
{{\mathcal{L}}_{rdn}}
pulls positive pairs together, encouraging the model to learn shared, consistent signals.
{{\mathcal{L}}_{syn}}
encourages each modality to contribute to the final fusion; if a modality (e.g., text) helps align the fused representation with others, the model tends to preserve and exploit it, reflecting multi-modal synergy.

Finally, we regard uniqueness
U
as crucial for downstream forecasting. Rather than optimizing it directly with a contrastive loss, we preserve it indirectly via the task loss, as
{{\mathcal{L}}_{prediction}}
forces the model to retain modality-specific cues that benefit prediction. Our overall objective is:

{{\mathcal{L}}_{total}}={{\mathcal{L}}_{prediction}}+{{\lambda}_{1}}{{\mathcal{L}}_{rdn}}+{{\lambda}_{2}}{{\mathcal{L}}_{syn}}

(20)

V-D

Interpretability Analysis

PrismNet leverages PID-guided multi-modal contrastive learning to align domain-specific semantics across modalities, offering a mechanistic lens into how multi-modal interactions emerge. We present a case study under the few-shot setting on the Electricity dataset (Fig.
7
).

•

Multi-modal augmentation yields richer knowledge representations.
As shown in Part A, we visualize via t-SNE the embeddings of: i) unimodal time series, ii) time series + image fusion, iii) time series + text fusion, and iv) tri-modal fusion. Image fusion emphasizes salient internal patterns, helping preserve prototypical load characteristics such as daily periodicity. Text fusion enriches the representation, yet the overall distribution becomes overly dispersed, suggesting the introduction of noise. In contrast, the tri-modal representation provides more comprehensive knowledge while avoiding distribution shift.

•

PID guidance balances redundancy and synergy.
Semantic alignment of domain knowledge is essential to fully exploit multi-modal information. Under few-shot settings, distribution shift limits coverage of full-range temporal patterns, as shown in Part B. PID-guided contrastive learning explicitly balances redundancy and synergy: redundancy is reflected by the overlap between multi-modal and unimodal embeddings, whereas increasing synergy tends to “push” the multi-modal representation outward, thereby expanding knowledge coverage.

•

Unlocking synergy from domain knowledge.
From Parts A and B, the direction in which text expands unimodal embeddings mirrors the effect of the
{{\lambda}_{2}}
term in PID guidance, suggesting that synergy is largely contributed by the text modality. To validate this hypothesis, we visualize token-level attention weights from the encoder following GPT-2, as shown in Part C. The model attends strongly to numerical tokens; more importantly, it effectively leverages content in the
domain knowledge
field (e.g., “seasonality exceeding 0.75”), which differs from the few-shot estimate of 0.726 (Part B). Such externally supplied domain cues synergize with time series features to support downstream forecasting, improving predictive accuracy.

VI
Conclusion

In this study, we introduce PrismNet, an interpretable multi-modal framework for power load forecasting. By augmenting load time series representations with complementary textual and visual cues, PrismNet improves knowledge coverage beyond the limited unimodal. Moreover, the proposed PID-guided multi-modal contrastive learning enforces domain-specific cross-modal semantic alignment, yielding a principled interpretability perspective on multi-modal interactions. Extensive evaluations on real-world public datasets demonstrate consistent gains over strong deep-learning and multi-modal baselines, with notable improvements under few-shot scenarios, supporting PrismNet as a trustworthy solution for safety-critical electric load scenarios.

VI-B

Contrastive Learning for Power Load

We adopt InfoNCE loss in multi-modal contrastive learning to measure the similarity between positive and negative pairs. For a batch with
N
paired samples, the objective is to maximize the similarity of each positive pair while minimizing its similarity to the remaining
N-1
negatives.

Taking the time series and text modalities as an example, we first compute the loss with the load time series as the anchor and the text as the target:

{{\mathcal{L}}_{X\to T}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}{\log\frac{\exp(-D(e_{i}^{x},e_{i}^{t})/\tau)}{\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{N}{\exp(-D(e_{i}^{x},e_{j}^{t})/\tau)}}}

(B1)

Here,
e_{i}^{x}
and
e_{i}^{t}
denote the embeddings of the i-th time series and text samples, respectively, and
\tau
is the temperature parameter that controls the emphasis on hard negatives.

We then compute the symmetric loss, using text as the anchor and the time series as the target:

{{\mathcal{L}}_{T\to X}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}{\log\frac{\exp(-D(e_{i}^{t},e_{i}^{x})/\tau)}{\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{N}{\exp(-D(e_{i}^{t},e_{j}^{x})/\tau)}}}

(B2)

The overall contrastive objective aggregates two directions:

{{\hat{I}}_{NCE}}={{\mathcal{L}}_{X\to T}}+\eta{{\mathcal{L}}_{T\to X}}

(B3)

where
\eta\in(0,1)
denotes a weighting coefficient.
