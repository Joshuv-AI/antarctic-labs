Completion Report: Neutron Irradiation and PIE of ZrC Coated Surrogate Particle Fuel in
 the IN-pile Steady-state Extreme Temperature (INSET) Testbed (#4603)

 PI: Brandon Wilson (Oak Ridge National Laboratory)
 Collaborators: Eddie Lopez Honorato, Emily Hutchins, Katherine Montoya, Bryan Conry,
 J. Wesley Jones, and N. Dianne Bull Ezell

1. Introduction

Tristructural-isotropic (TRISO) and other coated particle fuels have been identified as the preferred fuel
configuration for several advanced reactor concepts for terrestrial use and space reactors. Although much
is known about the performance of traditional TRISO fuel particles, the expanded envelope of operating
conditions introduced by some of the new reactor designs requires the fabrication of coated particle fuels
with different coating configurations (such as the use of ZrC coatings) and performance characterization at
temperatures as extreme as 2400°C. Therefore, there is an important need to study the performance and
operating limitations of these new fuel coatings under exposure to high temperatures and neutron
irradiation. Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL), a leading institution on coated particle fuel
fabrication, recently developed the IN-pile Steady-state Extreme Temperature (INSET) testbed, which can
be used to heat materials above 2000°C in a nuclear reactor to test both high-temperature and radiation
effects on materials. In this Super Rapid Turnaround Experiment (RTE) project, we used INSET to study
the performance of ZrC coated surrogate fuel particles exposed to thermal cycling above 2000°C under
neutron and gamma irradiation.
Coated nuclear particle fuel consists of a uranium-bearing kernel coated with one or several layers of
ceramics to act as a protective coating. New coated particle configurations have been proposed for use
under operating conditions well beyond those tested for the traditional TRISO fuel under the Advanced Gas
Reactor Fuel Development and Qualification (AGR) Program. ORNL recently fabricated ZrC coated
particle fuel samples that are expected to withstand multiple thermal cycles up to 2400°C. Material
properties tend to change when exposed to neutron irradiation as well as temperatures this high (> 2000°C),
so it is important to test the impact of both neutron irradiation and extreme temperatures on the
microstructure and mechanical properties of both the pyrolytic carbon (PyC) and ZrC layers of the fuel
particle. Surrogate fuel samples with ZrC coatings were fabricated at ORNL and used in the INSET testbed,
which is capable of thermal cycling the fuel samples during irradiation in a nuclear reactor. INSET can
provide both transient and steady-state temperatures above 2000°C and can be exposed to a neutron fluence
using the Ohio State University Research Reactor (OSURR) ex-core 9.5″ vertical dry tube or other research
reactors with large irradiation ports. To test the coatings, two tests were conducted with INSET: an out-of-
pile high-temperature-only test and an in-pile high-temperature reactor irradiation test. These two tests
enabled the effects of temperature and radiation on the coating microstructure and performance to be
characterized independently. This study of the performance of PyC and ZrC under high temperatures and
under neutron irradiation resulted in the deconvolution of the radiation and high-temperature effects on the
particles.

2. Experimental Setup

INSET was used to create a harsh environment to test the ZrC coated particle fuel at high temperatures
(above 2000°C) and under reactor irradiation. INSET is a vacuum furnace, with an aluminum 6061 body

(low neutron activation material), powered with a resistive graphite heating element inside. The graphite
heating element sits upon a high-temperature ceramic insulator (MgO) inside the furnace, surrounded by
high-reflectivity molybdenum sheets (see Figure 1 below). Graphite fiber insulation surrounds the entire
heated region. A multipurpose flange is used on the top of INSET to allow power cables, a vacuum line,
and thermocouples to penetrate into the furnace. Using an Elektro-Automatik DC power supply, 2.5 kW of
power is passed through the graphite heating element (50 A at 50 V), enabling the furnace to ramp from
room temperature to over 2000℃ in around 3 min. Two type C thermocouples are used to monitor the
temperature inside the heated region, and two type K thermocouples are used to monitor the furnace wall
temperature.

 INSET furnace.

The ZrC particles were packed into graphite crucibles and placed inside the INSET furnace. For both
experiments (high-temperature-only and high-temperature with radiation), four sets of particles of different
ZrC stoichiometry were made to analyze how different stoichiometries fared under high temperature and
radiation. The particles were loaded into the INSET furnace and ramped up to 2200°C in under 5 min. The
temperature was held for 30 min before allowing it to cool back to room temperature. These 30 min high-
temperature transient tests were repeated four times for a total time of 2 h at temperatures above 2200℃.
After these tests, the particles were removed and sent for analysis. Samples from the same fuel batch were
then loaded into INSET, and the furnace and assembly were taken to The Ohio State University for
irradiation in the OSURR.
At the OSURR, INSET was lowered down into the 10″ dry tube irradiation facility, which sits next to the
reactor core. The reactor was run at 450 kW for 14 h, resulting in a total neutron fluence of
7 × 1016 n⋅cm−2⋅s−1 on the particles and a gamma dose of 150 MRad. During the irradiation, the temperature
of the furnace was ramped up to 2200°C seven times, and the total time at 2200°C was 80 min. The gamma
heating from the reactor caused the furnace to heat faster and cool more slowly, resulting in longer wait
times between transients as compared with the out-of-pile tests.
After irradiation at the OSURR, the samples were placed in storage for 3 months to give short-lived neutron-
activated isotopes time to decay away before handling. After the decay time, an ORNL team retrieved the
samples from INSET and shipped them back to ORNL to the Low Activation Materials Development and
Analysis (LAMDA) laboratory to conduct post-irradiation examination (PIE) on the samples. Scanning
electron microscopy, electron backscatter diffraction, and transmission electron microscopy with energy

dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) were used at LAMDA to characterize and determine the condition of the
surrogate fuel coatings after the combined effects irradiation.

3. Results

Examination of the ZrC coating layers revealed that all the samples survived the high-temperature transients
and reactor irradiation without cracking or ablating away. The absence of cracking or mass reduction in the
layers means that the fission products cannot escape the particles under these harsh conditions, but of more
importance, outside harsh environmental conditions, such as hot hydrogen or other superheated working
fluids, cannot ingress into the particles and destroy them.
The PIE of the coated particles showed grain growth after thermal cycling and irradiation in INSET from
their as-fabricated condition. An example of one set of particles is shown in Figure 2. The different colors
represent different grains; the top image is the as-fabricated coating, and the bottom image is the high-
temperature, irradiated sample. The as-fabricated condition had mean grain areas in the range of 0.17–
0.25 µm2. Post treatment, the mean grain area increased to 16–25 µm2. All four different ZrC compositions
saw an increase in grain growth from the high-temperature transients (2200°C) from the INSET testing.
Additionally, the high-temperature, reactor-irradiated samples showed even larger grain growth, indicating
that radiation damage increases the grain growth properties even more in the ZrC layer. When the grain
sizes increase, the mechanical properties (i.e., the fragility) of the coatings decrease. Large grain size growth
can induce cracks and fractures in the coating that would enable fission products to escape or enable outside
environmental conditions inside the particle, which could be detrimental to the particle.

Figure 2. Example of ZrC grain size for the (a) as-fabricated condition and (b) post-irradiation condition.

The as-fabricated microstructures of the four different ZrC coating stoichiometries all had increased grain
growth from the high-temperature, irradiated conditions. However, there was variation in mean grain area
among the irradiated conditions, indicating that grain growth occurred at different rates. There may be a
growth advantage related to grain boundary composition and/or carbon distribution in this ZrC
stoichiometry regime that promoted abnormal grain growth. All samples were hypostoichiometric with
varying amounts of free carbon. The least hypostoichiometric microstructure (less ideal ZrC, with less
carbon than zirconium) exhibits the slowest grain growth (smaller grains result in better mechanical

properties). Prior work suggests that this behavior may be attributable to differences in carbon distribution
in the as-fabricated microstructure as a function of ZrC stoichiometry.
Focused ion beam (FIB) lift-outs of the ZrC coating were taken from the most hypostoichiometric
(C/Zr = 0.78) and closest to stoichiometric (C/Zr = 0.89) as-fabricated and irradiated batches. To capture
compositional variations within the layers, three windows were created in the FIB lift-outs. Each location
was imaged to check for increased porosity or agglomerations. Within each lift-out, EDS measurements
were taken approximately 550 nm into the ZrC layer and approximately 15 µm into the PyC layer. The
signals of interest were zirconium, carbon, and oxygen.
Carbon was expected to migrate to the grain boundaries during irradiation, but targeted line scans showed
only a small increase in carbon concentration within the grains themselves. Based on the bright-field
images, certain microstructural features were more commonly observed across the off-stoichiometric
samples. Minimal porosity was observed in both samples. When present, it was more commonly found at
the grain boundaries and was more prominent closer to the PyC interface.
For the full results, we currently have two papers under review in the Journal of Nuclear Materials that
cover both the high-temperature-only and the high-temperature with radiation data on these ZrC coated
particles.
Lessons Learned

  ZrC coatings survived high-temperature transients up to 2200°C along with reactor irradiation
 without cracking or ablating away.
  Thermal cycling up to 2200°C causes grain growth in the ZrC layer of TRISO fuel particles
 (grain growth in the layer reduces the mechanical properties of the coating).
  Thermal cycling plus reactor irradiation causes additional grain growth, compared with high-
 temperature-only, in the ZrC layer.
  The stoichiometry of the ZrC matters in regard to grain growth. The more hypostoichiometric the
 layer (the less carbon to zirconium), the smaller the grain growth (potentially advantageous
 mechanical properties; less chance of cracking and fracture).
