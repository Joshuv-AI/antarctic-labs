 ABSTRACT

 This report presents the fission product mass balance for the AGR-5/6/7
TRISO fuel irradiation experiment. The fission product inventories deposited on
capsule components outside of the fuel (e.g., stainless-steel shells, graphite
holders, Grafoil disks, and associated hardware) were quantified as part of the
post-irradiation examination (PIE) to assess the performance of this fuel.
Comparisons were made between these inventories and depletion calculations,
non-destructive measurements of fuel fission product inventories in the intact
fuel compacts, prior AGR experiments, and results from among each of the five
distinct AGR-5/6/7 capsules. The data serve as estimates of the condensable
fission products released from the fuel during irradiation. Excluding Capsule 1
(which experienced accidental damage during irradiation) and Capsule 3 (which
was tested at very high irradiation temperatures of >1300°C), the results indicate
that the AGR-5/6/7 fuel performed comparably to fuel from earlier AGR
experiments. The mass balance results were also used to estimate the number of
particles with in-pile SiC failures. Subject to the assumptions made in these
estimates, and excluding Capsules 1 and 3, the in-pile SiC failure rates for AGR-
5/6/7 are comparable to those observed in AGR-2.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 This report focuses on the post-irradiation examination (PIE) quantification of condensable fission
products released from the AGR-5/6/7 fuel compacts during irradiation in the Advanced Test Reactor
(ATR) at Idaho National Laboratory (INL). These data are combined with the compact inventory
measurements collected from non-destructive gamma scanning of the compacts, which is often referred to
as the mass balance for the irradiation experiment.
 1.1 Program Background
 The Advanced Gas Reactor (AGR) fuel development and qualification program was established to
provide a baseline tristructural isotropic (TRISO) fuel qualification dataset to support licensing,
deployment, and operation of TRISO-fueled high-temperature gas-cooled reactors (HTGRs) in the United
States (Petti et al. 2010; Demkowicz and Hunn 2020; Gougar et al. 2020). To achieve these goals, the
program includes fuel fabrication, irradiation of TRISO fuels and high-temperature materials (e.g.,
graphite), safety testing and PIE, fuel performance modeling, and fission product transport and source
term research (Mitchell 2022).
 The AGR program has conducted four distinct fuel irradiation experiments in ATR at INL. The first
of these irradiation tests, designated AGR-1, began in ATR in December of 2006 and ended in November
2009. This experiment was primarily to act as a shakedown test of the multi-capsule test train design and
to provide early data on fuel performance. AGR-1 fuel kernels were produced on an engineering scale at
BWX Technologies, Inc. (BWXT), but the TRISO coatings and cylindrical fuel compacts were fabricated
on a laboratory scale at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL). The AGR-1 PIE was completed at
ORNL and INL, and the final report was published in 2015 (Demkowicz et al. 2015).
 The second irradiation test, AGR-2, started in ATR in June 2010 and ended in October 2013. The
AGR-2 irradiation test in ATR was designed to provide fuel performance data for coated particles
fabricated on an engineering-scale pilot line using a coater with an internal chamber diameter of 150 mm
(6 inches). In AGR-2, the kernels and TRISO particles were fabricated on the engineering scale at
BWXT. The particles were overcoated and the compacts were fabricated at the laboratory scale at ORNL.
The final AGR-2 PIE report was published in 2021 (Stempien et al. 2021).
 AGR-3/4, a single irradiation that combined what had originally been conceived as the third and
fourth tests, was conducted to support the refinement of fission product transport models and to assess the
effects of sweep gas impurities on fuel performance and fission product transport. The experimental
portions of AGR-3/4 PIE have been completed, but the fission product transport modeling and extraction
of transport parameters from the data are still in progress.
 The AGR program has completed its final irradiation experiment: AGR-5/6/7. This was an irradiation
of 194 fuel compacts, each containing thousands of TRISO-coated fuel particles, housed within five
independent capsules in ATR (Pham et al. 2025). All components of this fuel were fabricated at
engineering scale at BWXT. This included the kernels, TRISO coatings, and fuel compacts.

 1.2 AGR-5/6/7 Fuel and Purpose
 The AGR-5/6/7 TRISO-coated particles contained fuel kernels composed of a heterogeneous mixture
of uranium carbide and uranium oxide, often abbreviated as UCO. Nominally, the 235U enrichment was
specified at 15.5 wt%, and the kernel diameters were specified at 425 ± 10 μm. The fuel specification
called for coating thicknesses nominally as follows: buffer 100 ± 15 μm, inner pyrolytic carbon (IPyC)
40 ± 4 μm, silicon carbide (SiC) 35 ± 3 μm, and outer pyrolytic carbon (OPyC) 40 ± 4 μm (Marshall
2017). All fuel compacts were approximately 25-mm long and 12.3-mm in diameter. The volumetric
packing fraction of TRISO particles in the fuel compacts were nominally 25% for Capsules 2, 3, and 4,
and 40% for Capsules 1 and 5. Major fuel characteristics and dimensions are summarized in
INL/EXT-21-64221, Rev. 1 (Pham et al. 2025, Demkowicz et al. 2026).
 The intent of the AGR-5/6/7 PIE was to collect performance data on this engineering scale fuel,
compare those data to results obtained in the earlier experiments of fuel produced at smaller scales (e.g.,
AGR-1 and AGR-2), and determine whether any new phenomena arose to challenge the existing
understanding of UCO TRISO fuel irradiation performance (Stempien 2020).
                                  1.3     AGR-5/6/7 Test Train
 The AGR-5/6/7 irradiation test train consisted of five independently instrumented and controlled
capsules that utilized three capsule design types. A schematic diagram of the axial cross section of the
assembled test train is shown in Figure 1. The test train was loaded into ATR with Capsule 1 at the
bottom and Capsule 5 at the top. Figure 2 shows schematic radial cross sections of the five different
capsules. Each capsule had a stainless-steel shell, serving as the experiment pressure boundary. Capsules
1, 2, 4, and 5 each had a single graphite holder with a hollow center to reduce graphite mass and graphite
volumetric heating (Collin 2018). Capsule 1 did not have any through-tubes, but Capsules 2 through 5
each had four through-tubes to accommodate gas lines and thermocouple (TC) leads from capsules below
them in the test train. Capsule 3 had two graphite holders: an inner holder for the fuel compacts and an
outer holder with through-tubes.

 1.4 Irradiation and Mass Balance
 The AGR-5/6/7 irradiation spanned nine ATR cycles (162B–168A) over two and a half years and a
total of approximately 360.9 effective full-power days with an end-of-irradiation (EOI) of July 22, 2020,
at 1:10 a.m. MST. Online measurements of fission gas release from Capsule 1 indicated significant rates
of TRISO fuel particle failure that were much higher than expected (Pham et al. 2025). PIE showed that
the fuel failure was caused by issues related to capsule design and was not due to inherently poor fuel
performance (Stempien, Palmer and Pham 2022; Stempien and Cai 2023; Kancharla et al. 2025). The
design error allowed the Capsule 1 fuel holder to be offset inside the stainless-steel shell, which generated
higher temperatures in the fuel and the nearby TCs in portions of the capsule. Temperatures were hot
enough to cause eutectic melting of the TCs, which led to dispersal of Ni and other transition metals from
the TCs and into the Capsule 1 fuel, resulting in the fuel failure. In-pile fission gas measurements suggest
approximately 15 particle failures in Capsule 3, four in Capsule 2, and none in Capsules 4 and 5 (Pham
and Scates 2022; Pham et al. 2025).
 After the capsule disassembly (Stempien and Cai 2023), as in prior AGR PIE (Demkowicz et al.
2013; Stempien et al. 2018; Stempien and Demkowicz 2020), in addition to the fuel compacts, all the
other capsule components besides the fuel were analyzed with gamma spectrometric and radiochemical
methods to quantify the inventory of key fission products that had been released from the fuel compacts
and deposited on those components. This analysis included (1) the metal capsule components, including
the stainless-steel capsule shells, stainless-steel capsule heads, through-tubes (composed of stainless-steel
tubes inside molybdenum tubes, also called sleeves), and other pieces of metallic hardware (e.g.,
thermocouples and gas lines); (2) the IG-430 graphite fuel holders; and (3) the Grafoil disks and spacers
(made of graphite or zirconia) from the top and bottom ends of the capsules. These data provide
information on the total inventory of fission products released from all fuel compacts in each capsule, and
when combined with the compact inventory data from non-destructive gamma scanning, constitute what
is commonly referred to as the mass balance.

 2. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS AND PROCESS NOTES
 The experimental methods were the same as those used in previous AGR PIE mass balance activities,
with detailed procedures documented in earlier reports (Demkowicz et al. 2013; Stempien et al. 2018;
Stempien and Demkowicz 2020). The only major difference here was that the larger diameter of the
stainless-steel test train shell necessitated acid-leaching in the air-cell at the Hot Fuels Examination
Facility (HFEF). A brief summary of these methods is provided below. The analyses were conducted
either at the Analytical Laboratory (AL) or at HFEF at the Materials and Fuels Complex (MFC).
Appendix A lists all the parts assayed in the course of this work along with their AL log numbers and AL
report numbers.
 2.1 Metallic Hardware
 All the metallic components of the AGR-5/6/7 irradiation capsules, including stainless-steel shells,
through-tubes, and other pieces of metallic hardware (e.g., thermocouples, gas lines, and tungsten parts)
were leached in near-boiling (~90°C) 8M nitric acid in the air cell (also called the decontamination cell)
at HFEF at MFC at INL according to HFEF-LI-0228 (INL 2022). This document was developed
specifically for leaching metallic AGR-5/6/7 hardware at HFEF. The acid leaching approach was used to
dissolve condensable fission products from the metallic components in the test train into solution for
analysis by radiochemical methods. Knowing the mass of the leach solutions was important; therefore, the
masses of all the parts and the solutions were measured before and after leaching via an Ohaus balance
installed in the cell. Direct measurements of gamma-emitting fission products on the metallic hardware
are complicated by neutron activation of the hardware producing gamma rays that interfere with
measurements of gamma-emitting fission products. Two aliquots of each leach solution were transferred
from HFEF to AL for gamma spectrometry and Sr separation and gas proportional counting. The results
from each set of duplicate aliquots were averaged to give final results.
 In some cases, during disassembly, the stainless-steel through-tubes slid out of their molybdenum
sleeves, and the sleeves remained in the graphite holders. In other cases, both the stainless-steel through-
tubes and the molybdenum sleeves were recovered together. The recovered pieces were leached at HFEF.
Any pieces of the through-tubes or sleeves that remained in the holders were gamma counted with the
holders during non-destructive exams via the Precision Gamma Scanner (PGS) (Walker, Stempien and
Glagolenko 2025). The holders were then sent to AL for burn-leach analysis, and any through-tubes,
sleeves, or other metallic components (e.g., TCs and gas lines) that were still embedded in the graphite
were leached along with any of the ash-like remains of the holder following oxidation of the graphite
holder in air at 750°C.
 Capsules 3 and 4 were the first to be leached at HFEF. Photographs of the process were taken through
the HFEF hot cell window, and Figure 3 and Figure 4 show selected photos from these capsules. The top
and bottom bulkheads, through-tubes, gas-lines, and any recovered Capsule 3 TCs were placed in the
same leach vessel (a stainless-steel container with two spouts). The tungsten heaters were placed in a
different container and leached separately from the shell and bulkheads. Nitric acid was added until it
covered all the components. The leaching vessels and their contents were then heated on a hot plate.
Similarly, the top and bottom bulkheads, through-tubes, gas-lines, and any recovered Capsule 4 TCs were
placed in the leach vessel. A small, stainless-steel basket (not pictured) was inserted. The basket
contained some smaller metallic pieces (primarily from the through-tubes) that were broken but recovered
during the disassembly process. Then nitric acid was added until it covered all the components. The
Capsule 4 leach vessel and its contents were also heated on a hot plate. Two 10-mL aliquots of each of the
acid solutions were taken by stirring up the parent solution and extracting the aliquots using a calibrated
pipette. These aliquots were then transferred to the AL at MFC for radiochemical analysis (i.e., gamma

 4

spectroscopy for gamma-emitting nuclides and ion separation and gas proportional counting for
quantification of beta-emitting Sr-90).
 When the through-tubes from Capsules 3 and 4 were leached, many of them had the molybdenum
sleeve intact. This molybdenum reacted exothermically with the nitric acid to produce molybdate
precipitates. A red fume was also visible at the time. After the aliquots were taken and transferred to
separate containers, the solid precipitates were recovered from the leach vessels, then dried and weighed.
In total, 290.06 g and 316.72 g of solid precipitates were recovered from the Capsule 3 and 4 vessels,
respectively. Additional steps were taken to assess the precipitates. Two containers of solids from each of
Capsules 3 and 4 were containerized and transferred to AL, and each container had between 1–2 g of the
solids. These were gamma counted at AL, and to estimate the total gamma-emitter inventory in the solid
precipitates, the results were adjusted for the fact that only small samples of all the precipitates recovered
were analyzed. A table in Appendix C summarizes the masses of the precipitate samples that were
analyzed relative to the total masses of the precipitates that were recovered.

 Next, the Capsule 1 metal hardware consisting of the top and bottom bulkheads, capsule shell, and
any recovered TCs or gas lines were leached. Capsule 1 did not have through-tubes. The wave spring was
misplaced between disassembly and leaching. Thus, it was not leached. Only four of the TCs were

It was decided to leach the TC along with its molybdenum sleeve along with the other Capsule 1
hardware. No precipitates were noted after this operation.
 Given the stainless-steel through-tubes in Capsules 2 and 5 were fitted with outer sleeves made of
molybdenum, a revision to HFEF-LI-0228 was issued before leaching these in the air cell at HFEF. This
revision stipulated that any parts made of molybdenum would be leached separately from other metal
components, the leaching step would only be 30–60 seconds, and the solution would not be heated. The
molybdenum-bearing components were loaded into baskets and dipped in acid for 30–60 seconds. This
allowed the recovery of fission products from these components without generating precipitates.

 2.2 Graphite Holders
 All graphite holders were non-destructively gamma scanned on the PGS in HFEF at INL to determine
their inventories of gamma-emitting fission products (Walker, Stempien and Glagolenko 2025). In that
work, the measured inventories of gamma-emitting fission products in the holders were compared to the
inventories predicted from physics calculations by (Sterbentz 2020).
 The holders were then transferred to AL for further analysis. Given the widespread damage to fuel in
Capsule 1 caused by accidental dispersion of nickel from overheated TCs, the Capsule 1 holder was not
transferred to AL because any analysis of that holder would not have given results relevant to inherent
fuel performance. The holders from Capsules 2 through 5 were transferred from HFEF to AL. To
facilitate the transfer of the holders, the graphite holder from Capsule 2 and the inner and outer graphite
holders from Capsule 3 were sectioned at HFEF to make them shorter. The Capsule 2 holder and the
Capsule 3 outer holder were each cut in half, yielding 4-in.-long pieces. The Capsule 3 inner holder was
cut into three approximately 2.67-in.-long pieces so that it could be transferred via pneumatic rabbit
between HFEF and AL. A vacuum and cyclone separator were used to recover any fines generated from
the cutting operations so that they could be analyzed as well. Given the considerably lower gamma

 6

activity of the holders from Capsules 4 and 5, these were not cut, and they were transferred intact using
shielded “pigs.”
 At AL, all holders and pieces of holders were gamma counted using a spectrometer in Cell 4. These
gamma counts were conducted to supplement the non-destructive gamma counts completed using the
PGS at HFEF. With a lower background activity, the Cell 4 spectrometer may have a lower detection
limit for some nuclides. After gamma counting, the graphite holders were put in quartz beakers and
oxidized in air in the Cell 5 muffle furnace at 750°C until all the carbon had been consumed. The graphite
pieces from the same holder were oxidized together, and following oxidation, the remnants were leached
together. The leaching process was carried out using 8M HNO3, which was heated to just below boiling
and then poured into the quartz beaker. The solution was left to sit for 30 minutes before being carefully
poured into a clean, tared poly bottle, ensuring the solid residuals remained in the oxidation ware. The
leachate was analyzed by gamma spectroscopy. To quantify beta-emitting Sr-90, the leachate went
through an ion separation, followed by gas proportional counting. As stated earlier, the holders often had
gas lines and thermocouples embedded in them that could not be removed without risking significant
damage to the holders. These metal pieces remained as solids after oxidation and leaching. To see if there
were any residual fission products on the post-holder-oxidation metals, these remaining solids were also
gamma counted.
 2.3 Grafoil Disks and Spacers
 Capsules 1, 2, 4, and 5 each had one top spacer (IG-430 graphite for Capsule 1 and zirconia for the
others), one bottom spacer (zirconia), two top Grafoil disks, and two bottom Grafoil disks. Capsule 3 had
one top spacer (zirconia), one bottom spacer (zirconia), four top Grafoil disks, and four bottom Grafoil
disks. Upon disassembly of each capsule, any retrievable Grafoil disks and spacers were shipped to AL
for gamma counting. The appendices of this report list each sample and what components were
represented by each sample. Additionally, the disassembly and metrology report discusses items that were
accidentally damaged during disassembly, such as the Capsule 2 graphite holder (Stempien and Cai
2023). Following gamma counting, the carbonaceous parts were oxidized in air and then leached in acid.
The leachates were analyzed by ion separation and gas proportional counting. Ceramic spacers were
gamma counted and then leached directly (with no intermediate oxidation step). The leachates from the
spacers were analyzed by gamma spectrometry and ion separation and gas-proportional counting. The
leachates from the foils were not analyzed by gamma spectrometry on the assumption that the pre-
oxidation gamma counting of the pieces is quantitative, and post-oxidation spectrometry of the leachate
would be unnecessarily duplicative.
 3. CAPSULE MASS BALANCE RESULTS
 3.1 Data
 All fission product activities measured in these exams were decay-corrected back to the EOI (July 22,
2020, 1:00 a.m. MDT) plus one day (July 23, 2020, 1:00 a.m. MDT) using Equation (1):
𝐴𝐴 = 𝐴𝐴𝑜𝑜 𝑒𝑒 −𝜆𝜆𝜆𝜆 (1)
where A is the activity at time t, Ao is the activity at t = 0, and λ is the decay constant (λ = ln(2)/t1/2, where
t1/2 is the half-life). Isotope half-lives were taken from the ENDF/B-VII.1 library (Chadwick et al. 2011).
All the components in a given capsule could conceivably have fission products from any of the many fuel
compacts that were irradiated in that capsule. Thus, the methods employed in this work to analyze the
fission products on the test train components only provide information on the fission product behavior in
the capsule overall, meaning fission-product data cannot be linked to a specific compact within a capsule.
To compute the fraction of a given nuclide’s inventory measured on the components (sometimes referred
to as the measured-to-calculated ratio [M/C]), the decay-corrected measured fission product values were
divided by the inventories predicted by physics calculations to have been produced in each capsule and

 7

adjusted to the EOI + one day (Sterbentz 2020). The average behavior of a compact in a capsule could be
inferred by multiplying the computed capsule fraction by the number of compacts in a given capsule. The
number of particle-equivalent inventories represented by a capsule fraction can be computed by
multiplying the capsule fraction by the estimated number of particles in the capsule. In instances where no
activity was detected for a given nuclide, minimum detectable activities (MDAs) were reported. Like the
measured activities, these MDAs were also decay-corrected and are denoted by “<.” These MDAs were
considered an upper bound on the activity of a given nuclide.
 Between the quantitative gamma scanning by PGS, the gamma scanning at AL, gamma spectrometry
conducted on the post-oxidation leachate, and the post-burn-leach gamma counting of any solid residuals,
the graphite holders from Capsules 2–5 were analyzed multiple times. Similarly, other samples (e.g., the
Capsule 2 bottom spacer) were assessed both by gamma spectrometry of the solid samples and the
leachates of those samples. Where there were multiple analogous results from different methods, the
highest value was taken from among those different analyses to produce a conservative result. When both
measurable values and MDA were available, the measurable values were used in preference over the
MDA. Based on prior experience comparing gamma results measured by gamma counting the solid
samples with those from post-oxidation leachate, burn-leach recovery of fission products is often
incomplete, which results in lower activities typically being observed in leached samples. Sr-90 was only
measured from leachates.
 For the metal components leached at HFEF, two aliquots of each solution were transferred to AL for
analysis. The results were averaged from each pair of aliquots. Where there were multiple pairs of
aliquots for multiple leach operations (e.g., shell and heads; through-tubes; heaters) the averages were
added to give a total inventory leached from the operations conducted at HFEF.

 3.3 Capsule 2
 The graphite holder was gamma scanned on the PGS at HFEF and again at AL. Then, the holder was
burned and leached at AL and the leachate was analyzed by gamma spectrometry and by separation and
gas proportional counting for Sr-90. The results, expressed in units of compact fraction, are shown in
Table 3. Among the measurements, the PGS results generally showed the highest activities, except for Cs-
137. This discrepancy suggests that the Cs-137 levels at AL may have been biased high due to hot cell
contamination. To ensure a conservative approach, the highest measured activities from among all of the
different methods were used for totaling each fission product.
 The gamma results from leach solutions were typically lower than those from the solid, pre-leach
sample. This has been common throughout AGR PIE, and is believed to be due to incomplete recovery of
nuclides during the burn-leach process (Stempien and Demkowicz 2020). Past AGR PIE observations
have shown that Sr-90 has transport and release behavior in the fuel similar to Eu-154, and the recovery
of Eu-154 may cautiously be used as a proxy to infer Sr-90 recovery. In this case, the Eu-154 activity
detected in the leached solution was 27% of the activity measured by the PGS. A nearly identical ratio of
the Eu-154 content in the post-burn leachate to PGS was reported in the AGR-2 Capsule 2 mass balance
(Stempien and Demkowicz 2020). While this correction was not applied here in this work, if one were to
assume the Sr-90 recovery from the leachate was also 27%, the Sr-90 measured in the leachate could be
divided by 0.27 to attempt to correct for incomplete recovery.
 In the residual solids gamma-counted after the burn-leach operation, 92% of the Eu-154 activity
measured in the PGS was detected, for example. These residual solids are the remnants of through-tubes,
TCs, and gas lines. Given their irregular shapes, the residual solids were counted in an uncalibrated
geometry and should not be considered quantitative, though they do suggest significant amounts of
condensable fission products were held up on the metal parts embedded in the holder. The PGS values are
quantitative, and the residuals are not needed for the mass balance of the gamma-emitting fission
products.

 3.7 Comparison of Graphite Holder Gamma Results
 Most often, the solid-state gamma measurements (i.e., the scans on PGS and the pre-burn counts at
AL) yielded the highest values. To assess the isotope recovery from the burn-leach process across all the
capsules, the gamma-emitting inventories measured from burn-leach were ratioed to those measured from
solid-state gamma, as shown in Table 15.

 18

 As the table shows, excluding the entries where an MDA was reported, the burn-leach solutions
recovered between 15% and 72% of fission products measured via solid-state gamma. There were two
instances where the holder burn-leach results were above the detection limit while the solid-state gamma
results were below detection: Eu-154 in the holders of Capsules 4 and 5. In AGR-3/4, there were similar
instances where Eu-154 was detectable via burn-leach methods but not via PGS (Stempien and Cai 2026).
 The Ag-110m from Capsule 4 had the lowest apparent recovery of only 2.53 × 10⁻². This low value is
attributed to considerable Ag-110m activity in the residual metallic solids remaining after burn-leach of
the Capsule 4 (Table 9). A similarly low burn-leach recovery of Ag-110m was seen for the AGR-2
Capsule 3 graphite holder at 0.05 (Stempien and Demkowicz 2020). To re-emphasize, as long as the
solid-state gamma is quantitative, this is not a problem. However, under-recovery would be undesirable
for quantifying Sr-90, which could not be independently quantified without gas proportional counting.
 The burn-leach method is the only means of quantifying Sr-90, making it impossible to directly assess
whether all the Sr-90 was recovered from a given piece of hardware. AGR PIE observations indicate that
Sr-90 transports and is retained or released similarly to Eu-154. It may be informative to use the Eu-154
recoveries estimated in Table 15 to infer Sr-90 recovery. The two meaningful Eu-154 recovery
measurements—from the Capsule 2 graphite holder and the Capsule 3 inner graphite holder—showed
burn-leach recoveries of 26.8% and 68.2% of the corresponding solid-state values, respectively. For
comparison, the Eu-154 recovery was 26% from AGR-2 Capsule 2. Consequently, the Sr-90 values
determined from burn-leach may be biased low for Capsule 2 and the inner graphite holder from Capsule
3. For the remaining capsules—Capsule 3 outer, Capsule 4, and Capsule 5—a meaningful Eu-154
recovery ratio could not be established due to MDA limitations. Nevertheless, given the consistently low
Eu-154 recoveries observed in the other capsules and in the previous AGR-2 campaign, the Sr-90 values
for these capsules could also be biased low. To be consistent with that prior work, no attempts to correct
the Sr-90 were made in this work, but the data needed to make such a correction are included below in

 4. DISCUSSION
 4.1 Cesium Mass Balance
 Fission-product Cs is well retained by intact SiC layers, and significant Cs release only occurs in
conjunction with failed or defective SiC coatings. This includes failed or defective TRISO particles,
which necessarily have failed, or defective SiC coatings in addition to failed or defective PyC coatings.

 19

Cs-134 is typically the preferred indicator of cesium release from fuel, as it is less susceptible to bias from
hot-cell contamination compared to the longer-lived Cs-137.
 The following in-pile failures and as-fabricated defects will contribute to the Cs measured outside of
the fuel on the capsule components: the dispersed uranium fraction (DUF), the exposed kernel fraction
(EKF), the SiC defect fraction (SDF), in-pile SiC failures, and in-pile TRISO failures. The DUF, EKF,
and SDF were reported as averages and as 95% confidence intervals for the different compact batches in
AGR-5/6/7 (Marshall 2020). Using the number of in-pile TRISO failures estimated from the fission-
product monitoring system (FPMS), the Cs inventory measured outside of the fuel in each capsule, and
the as-fabricated defect fractions, the number of in-pile SiC failures per capsule can be estimated by
applying some key assumptions. In the discussion that follows, a failed-SiC particle is considered as a
separate and distinct class from a failed TRISO particle, and likewise, a failed TRISO particle is distinct
from a failed-SiC particle.
 The first assumption involves the amount of Cs released from a compact with a certain defect or
failure and the temperature dependence of that release. In the prior AGR-3/4 experiment, the capsule-
component inventories of Cs were compared to the Cs inventories measured from radial deconsolidation-
leach-burn-leach (DLBL) of AGR-3/4 fuel compacts, and the two were generally complementary
(Stempien and Cai 2024). In that AGR-3/4 work, which had 20 designed-to-fail (DTF) fuel particles per
compact and four compacts per capsule, about 80 particle equivalents of Cs were accounted for between
the analysis of the capsule components and the destructive examinations of the compacts. This gave some
confidence that analyzing the capsule components is a reasonable estimate of the amount of Cs released
from the fuel; however, it may not be a suitable means of enumerating the number of particles that were
responsible for releasing that Cs. A major conclusion from that work was that approximately 35%–80%
of the Cs from the DTF particles was retained in the fuel compact for irradiation temperatures between
about 850°C and 950°C. For irradiation temperatures from 989°C–1376°C, the amounts of Cs measured
in the radial DLBLs of these AGR-3/4 fuel compacts indicated notably lower Cs retention, ranging from
3%–24% of the DTF inventory (Stempien and Cai 2024).°
 In AGR-5/6/7 Capsule 3, an average compact had a time-average volume-average (TAVA)
temperature of 1313°C and a time-average (TA) peak temperature of 1432°C. With such high irradiation
temperatures in AGR-5/6/7 Capsule 3, one could assume 3% retention of Cs based on the prior AGR-3/4
work. The fuel in Capsules 2, 4, and 5 had much lower irradiation temperatures: the Capsule 2 average
TAVA temperature was 833°C and the average TA peak was 948°C;a Capsule 4 had an average of 857°C
TAVA temperature and a 970°C TA peak; Capsule 5 had a 756°C average TAVA temperature and an
864°C TA peak. In those temperature ranges, the Cs retention in a compact with a SiC or TRISO failure
could be 35%–80% of that particle’s inventory, based on AGR-3/4 results. Indeed, the analysis of AGR-
5/6/7 Compact 2-7-4 (TAVA = 836°C) by DLBL revealed 0.592 particle equivalents of Cs-134 retained
in the fuel matrix with one confirmed SiC failure, which corresponds to 59% retention and 41% release of
the predicted Cs-134 inventory of that failed-SiC particle (Stempien and Cai 2025). As of this writing,
none of the compacts to undergo DLBL at INL have given information on the amount of Cs from a failed
SiC or failed TRISO particle that might remain in a compact after irradiation at the low irradiation
temperature of ~750°C. Thus, it is possible the retention of Cs from a hypothetical defective or failed SiC
or TRISO particle could be higher than 80% for the irradiation temperatures in Capsule 5. To attempt to
correlate the number of Cs-releasing particles per capsule with the Cs inventory measured on the capsule
components, the capsule component inventory can be divided by the projected release fractions based on
the DLBL data described above and then compared to the as-fabricated defect rates and the number of in-
pile TRISO failures estimated from online fission gas monitoring.

a Note that Capsule 2 had higher temperature uncertainties (Pham and Hawkes 2022), and its irradiation temperatures may have

 been influenced by imperfect centering of the holder within the stainless-steel shell (Hawkes, Pham and Otani 2024).

 20

 The second assumption is that the number of in-pile TRISO failures estimated via FPMS is correct,
though it may be an over- or underestimate of the true value. FPMS can most accurately quantify in-pile
TRISO failures for fuel with low defect fractions and few in-pile failures. In AGR-3/4, each capsule had
80 DTF particles, and quantifying the number of failed particles via FPMS proved difficult, with wide
uncertainty bands (Collin et al. 2018). For example, the FPMS-based estimates for the number of failed
DTF particles specifically in AGR-3/4 Capsule 3 ranged from as few as 53 to as many as 164. With only
80 DTF particles per capsule, a result of 164 failures suggested on the upper end of the FPMS analysi is
unrealistically high because it suggests failure of ~80 driver TRISO particles (a failure fraction of ~1% in
the capsule), which had the same properties as the AGR-1 TRISO particles and which demonstrated
extremely low in-pile failure rates (Demkowicz and Hunn 2020).
 The third key assumption is that as-fabricated dispersed uranium, exposed kernels, and SiC defects
release the same fraction of Cs as in-pile SiC and TRISO failures. With those three assumptions, the
number of in-pile SiC failures in a capsule is equal to the Cs inventory on the capsule components
adjusted for holdup in the fuel, minus the contributions from dispersed uranium, exposed kernels (which
are discrete instances of integer numbers of kernels per capsule), SiC defects (discrete instances of integer
numbers of particles per capsule), and in-pile TRISO-failures (discrete instances of integer numbers of
particles per capsule). Several values were used for the assumed retention of Cs in failed or defective fuel
to give a few different estimates. The quantities needed for these calculations are given in the tables in
Appendix B.
 From the logic explained above, the FPMS estimates on in-pile TRISO failure, the as-fabricated
defect rates and related quantities given in Appendix B, and the total Cs-134 quantities measured outside
of the fuel from the present report, the estimated number of in-pile SiC failures per capsule are given in
Table 16. In-pile SiC failures are estimated here for all the capsules under several different scenarios for
the retention of Cs in as-fabricated defects and in-pile failures. The first scenario assumes no Cs retention
(100% release) from any failed or defective SiC or TRISO particles. The second scenario assumes
relatively low Cs retention, with 59% retention from failed or defective particles or SiC coatings in
Capsules 2, 4, and 5 and 3% retention from the hotter Capsule 3 fuel. The third scenario assumes
relatively high retention of 80% of the Cs from defective or failed SiC or TRISO particles in Capsules 2,
4, and 5 and 24% Cs retention Capsule 3. In the fourth scenario, the assumed retention of Cs in the
Capsule 5 fuel was increased until the in-pile SiC failure estimate became > 0, which required a minimum
Cs retention of 88%.
 Capsule 1 components outside of the fuel were found to have 2.40E+3 particle equivalents of Cs-134.
Given the wide temperature range of fuel in this capsule and the lack of a reasonable estimated TRISO
failures from FPMS, it is not possible to estimate the number of TRISO and SiC failures. Furthermore,
this fuel cannot be used to assess performance given the accidental Ni dispersion from overheated
thermocouples. The actual number of particles with SiC or TRISO coatings broken by this accidental Ni
dispersion is not clear and likely exceeds 2.40E+3.
 As stated earlier, the fuel in Capsule 3 had high irradiation temperatures, with some compacts having
TAVA temperatures of >1360°C and time-averaged peak temperatures of >1420°C. The instantaneous
peak temperatures for about 20% of the fuel in Capsule 3 exceeded 1450°C for several months (Pham et
al. 2025). The total amount of Cs-134 recovered from the Capsule 3 components was approximately 48
particle equivalents. For reference, the TRISO failure estimate based on in-pile fission gas measurements
was about 15 particle failures in Capsule 3 (Pham and Scates 2022; Pham et al. 2025). Table 16 gives a
variety of permutations for estimating the number of in-pile SiC failures. At the bottom of the table,
assuming the 15 in-pile TRISO failures, the in-pile SiC failure estimate ranges from 30 to 45 particles.
Given the high irradiation temperature in this capsule, the low-compact retention cases, which estimated
30 to 31 in-pile SiC failures may be more accurate.

 Capsules 2 and 4 had nominally 25% packing-fraction compacts with similar irradiation temperatures

 4.2 Silver Mass Balance
 Figure 5 illustrates the Ag-110m M/C ratios for each capsule. These M/Cs were determined by
summing the activities in the compacts (measured via PGS [Walker, Stempien, Glagolenko 2025]) and
the activities measured on the capsule components (from the present work) and dividing by the total Ag-
110m inventories in the capsules from depletion calculations. The results range from 0.64 to 0.85. Given
the historical propensity for Ag diffusion through intact TRISO coatings, the data from Capsule 1 may not
be as strongly affected by the rampant damage caused by Ni dispersion in Capsule 1. Thus, they are
included here.
 While irradiation temperatures in AGR test trains often peaked toward the center of the test train, this
is not always the case. Reviewing the Ag-110m mass balances from AGR-1, AGR-2, and AGR-3/4 (and
their analogous bar charts), indicates that higher total Ag-110m M/Cs occur in capsules away from the
axial ends of the test train. That same pattern is repeated here, and it may be dominated more by spatial
biases in the physics calculations than actual transport of Ag within the experiment, especially in
Capsules 4 and 5.
 Very little Ag-110m was measured on the components of Capsule 5, accounting for only 1.68E-4 of
the capsule’s total Ag-110m inventory. Capsule 5 had the lowest irradiation temperature of all the AGR-

 24

5/6/7 capsules, and very little Ag-110m release was expected. If this is a quantitative representation of all
the Ag-110m to have been released from the Capsule 5 fuel, then the compact PGS, which averaged 0.73,
may signal nearly 100% retention of Ag-110m in the Capsule 5 fuel. That would mean the physics
depletion calculations overestimated the production of Ag-110m in Capsule 5. DLBL of Compact 5-1-2
allowed a comparison between the non-destructive PGS of the intact compact and gamma counting of
individual particles from the compact (Stempien and Cai 2025). In that work the average particle M/C
was 0.85, compared to a PGS-based compact M/C of 0.80. Those data from Compact 5-1-2 suggest that
the relationship is consistent between the gamma measurements and the computed inventories. Given the
available data, the most likely explanation is that Ag-110m production is overestimated in the depletion
calculations.
 Capsule 2 had high Ag-110m inventories on the test train components, with about 42% of the
predicted Capsule 2 inventory of Ag-110m being measured on the capsule hardware. On average, the Ag-
110m compact M/C from PGS of the Capsule 2 compacts was 0.21, with some compacts having Ag-
110m M/Cs of about 0.01. DLBL and particle gamma counting corroborated these low M/Cs (Cureton et
al. 2025). Adding the measured Ag-110m from the compacts with the inventory measured from the
Capsule 2 hardware gives an overall Ag-110m mass balance of 0.64. Prior work suggests that Ag-110m is
overpredicted in capsules near the top or bottom of the ATR core. Based on AGR-3/4, this over prediction
may be more pronounced in capsules below the ATR core centerline for capsules irradiated in the
Northeast Flux Trap position at ATR. It is possible that the Ag-110m production was overestimated in
Capsule 2. It is also possible that Ag-110m was under-recovered from the test train components; however,
neither of these explains the significant apparent release of Ag-110m from the fuel to the capsule
components.
 Thus, there was clearly significant release of Ag-110m from the Capsule 2 fuel compacts; however,
that quantity of Ag-110m release was not expected based on the relatively low average irradiation
temperature of 833°C in Capsule 2 (Hawkes 2022). While the AGR-5/6/7 thermal model showed that
Capsule 2 had the second lowest TAVA irradiation temperature (Hawkes 2022), the temperature
uncertainties in Capsule 2 were higher than in Capsules 3, 4, and 5 (Pham and Hawkes 2022), and non-
uniform gas gaps in Capsule 2 may have resulted in peak temperatures in a small amount of the fuel
exceeding 1100°C in that capsule for a short period of the irradiation (Hawkes, Pham, and Otani 2024). A
temperature of 1150°C has been previously noted as having significantly higher Ag-110m release rates in
PIE (Hunn et al. 2015), but the current thermal analyses do not suggest that a significant portion of the
fuel in Capsule 2 had TAVA irradiation temperatures that hot. Thus, the current Ag-110m Capsule 2 mass
balances cannot be explained solely by the available irradiation-temperature information; however, when
compared to the body of prior AGR work, the amount of Ag-110m measured outside of the Capsule 2
fuel indicates that the Capsule 2 irradiation temperatures were hotter than predicted by the thermal model.
 Capsule 4 had a computed fuel irradiation temperature slightly higher than Capsule 2 (Hawkes 2022);
however, Capsule 4 had a much lower amount of Ag-110m on the capsule components than Capsule 2.
For Capsule 4, a Ag-110m fraction of 1.90E-2 was measured on the capsule components. Its compacts
had an average Ag-110m M/C of 0.81. It appears that very little Ag-110m was released from the fuel in
Capsule 4. Interestingly, Capsule 3, which experienced the highest irradiation temperature and had fuel
morphologies characteristic of very high irradiation temperatures (Krajewska-Travar and Stempien 2026),
was found to have little Ag-110m release. The capsule fraction of Ag-110m measured outside the
compacts was 8.5E-2 (see Table 7), and its average compact Ag-110m M/C was 0.76 (Walker, Stempien,
and Glagolenko 2025).

 5. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS
 Components of the AGR-5/6/7 irradiation test train—including the stainless-steel capsule shells and
bulkheads, through-tubes and molybdenum sleeves, thermocouples, gas lines, IG-430 graphite holders,
Grafoil disks, and assorted spacers—were analyzed for key gamma-emitting fission products (Ag-110m,
Cs-134, Cs-137, Ce-144, Eu-154, and Sb-125) and beta-emitting Sr-90. These measurements, combined
with previous non-destructive gamma scanning of fuel compacts via the Precision Gamma Scanner
(PGS), constitute the fission product mass balance for the AGR-5/6/7 experiment. The principal findings
are summarized below.
 Cesium Mass Balance and In-Pile Failure Estimates. Cesium (particularly Cs-134) can be used as
an indicator of SiC coating integrity, as significant release is associated with failed (i.e., fuel that failed
during irradiation) or defective (i.e., fuel with defects at the time of fabrication prior to irradiation) SiC
layers. Based on the Cs-134 inventories measured on the capsule components, combined with in-pile
fission gas measurements, as-fabricated defect fractions, and AGR-3/4 data on the amount of Cs that
might be retained in defective fuel, the following in-pile failure estimates were made in an attempt to
bound the possible range of in-pile SiC failures in each capsule. Several key assumptions factored into
these estimates, and the uncertainties in these estimates may be significant:
 • Capsule 1 exhibited massive fuel damage (≥2.4 × 10³ TRISO and SiC failures) caused by
 eutectic melting of thermocouples and subsequent nickel dispersion into the fuel. This failure was
 a consequence of a capsule design error—specifically, an offset graphite holder that led to locally
 elevated temperatures—and is not representative of intrinsic fuel performance.
 • Capsule 2 is estimated to have experienced between one and four in-pile TRISO failures (based
 on prior fission gas analyses). From various combinations of assumptions on as-fabricated defect
 rates, and employing the results from the Capsule 2 mass balance reported here, Capsule 2 was

 29

 estimated to have experienced zero to seven in-pile SiC failures in addition to the aforementioned
 TRISO failures.
 • Capsule 3 was designed to explore fuel performance margins at elevated temperatures (average
 TAVA temperature of 1313°C, with some compacts exceeding TAVA temperatures of 1360°C).
 Capsule 3 experienced approximately 15 TRISO failures (based on prior fission gas analyses) and
 30 to 45 in-pile SiC failures. Considering that this capsule was operated well beyond nominal fuel
 temperatures for the duration of the irradiation, these results indicate reasonably good fuel
 performance.
 • Capsule 4 was estimated to have zero in-pile TRISO failures, based on fission gas monitoring.
 With the Capsule 4 Cs-134 mass balance returning only 0.84 particle equivalents across all the
 capsule components, and applying the assumptions discussed on the retention of Cs in low
 irradiation-temperature fuel, it was estimated that there were zero to two in-pile SiC failures.
 • Capsule 5 was estimated to have experienced zero to one in-pile SiC failures; however, these
 estimates are sensitive to assumptions about Cs retention at this capsule's low average TAVA
 temperature of 756°C. A Cs retention fraction of ~87% in the compact matrix was required to
 yield a physically meaningful (i.e., non-negative) SiC failure estimate of zero. A Cs retention of
 88% had to be assumed for the accounting to suggest the possibility of one in-pile SiC failure.
 Excluding Capsule 1 (accidental damage) and Capsule 3 (elevated temperature testing), the AGR-
5/6/7 in-pile TRISO failure rates from fission gas analyses and the SiC failure rates estimated in the
present work are comparable to those observed in AGR-2.
 Silver Mass Balance. The total measured Ag-110m inventory (compacts plus capsule components)
ranged from 0.64 to 0.85 of the calculated inventory across the capsules. This suggests that the physics
depletion calculations overestimated the production of Ag-110m in the experiment in general, or a
significant amount of Ag-110m was not quantified. It is conceivable that some Ag-110m was not
completely recovered from the metallic capsule components or was present on small capsule components
(e.g., the Capsule 1 wave spring) that were not recoverd during test train disassebly, for example. Based
on AGR-3/4, Ag-110m production over predictions may be more pronounced in capsules below the ATR
core centerline in the Northeast Flux Trap position at ATR, and the AGR-5/6/7 results are similar in that
regard. Capsule 5, with the lowest irradiation temperature in AGR-5/6/7, showed the smallest measurable
Ag-110m release to the capsule components (1.7 × 10⁻⁴ M/C, equal to 13.7 particle-equivalents)—the
lowest measurable release observed across all AGR campaigns. Capsule 2 exhibited an anomalously high
Ag-110m component fraction (~42% of the calculated capsule inventory) despite having the second-
lowest nominal computed irradiation temperature in AGR-5/6/7. This observation, together with elevated
Eu-154 and Sr-90 inventories on Capsule 2 components, suggests that actual irradiation temperatures in
Capsule 2 were higher (by perhaps 200°C) than predicted by thermal models, possibly due to an offset
graphite holder. Further investigation of Capsule 2 temperatures is ongoing.
 Europium and Strontium Mass Balance. The total measured Eu-154 inventory (i.e., the sum of the
compact gamma scans and capsule component inventories) across all capsules ranged from approximately
0.79 to 0.82 of calculated values, indicating a consistent overprediction bias in the depletion calculations
observed in previous AGR experiments. Capsule 3, with the highest irradiation temperatures in AGR-
5/6/7, had the highest Eu-154 and Sr-90 inventories on the capsule components (Eu-154 M/C = 2.09 ×
10⁻²; Sr-90 M/C = 3.31 × 10⁻²), consistent with the established finding from combined AGR data that Eu-
154 and Sr-90 releases accelerate at irradiation temperatures beyond about 1200°C. Capsules 4 and 5,
irradiated at lower temperatures (average TAVA 857°C and 756°C, respectively), exhibited Eu-154
component fractions of 2.2 × 10⁻⁷ and 1.1 × 10⁻⁶, respectively—among the lowest values measured across
all AGR experiments. In contrast, the mass balance for Capsule 2 showed a capsule fraction of 3.1 × 10⁻4
for Eu-154, which is an indication of potentially higher fuel temperatures than originally computed there.

 30

 Overall Assessment. Despite the difficulties in exactly enumerating in-pile TRISO and SiC failures
in AGR-5/6/7, the fission product mass balance indicates that engineering-scale UCO TRISO fuel
fabricated at BWXT performed comparably to fuel from earlier AGR experiments produced at smaller
scales. Excluding the anomalous results from Capsule 1 (due to accidental thermocouple-induced
damage) and Capsule 3 (due to intentional above-normal irradiation temperatures), the in-pile TRISO and
SiC failure rates are acceptably within the performance expectations for TRISO-fueled HTGR
applications. The amounts of Ag, Eu, and Sr released from Capsule 2 all suggest Capsule 2 had higher
irradiation temperatures (potentially on the order of 200°C hotter) than initially computed assuming
perfect centering of the components in the capsule. Additional thermal modeling has shown it is possible
that the Capsule 2 graphite holder was offset and may have increased temperatures for a small fraction of
the Capsule 2 fuel, but the thermal analysis alone does not currently suggest capsule-wide temperatures
were much hotter for the duration of the irradiation. The other point of interest is that the Ag-110m
release from Capsule 3 (which has been verified as having very high irradiation temperatures) was
relatively low in comparison. Lastly, the mass balances indicate that the production of Ag-110m an Eu-
154 from fission is overpredicted, and this should be considered when drawing conclusions on the
retention of these nuclides in other phases of AGR-5/6/7 PIE.
