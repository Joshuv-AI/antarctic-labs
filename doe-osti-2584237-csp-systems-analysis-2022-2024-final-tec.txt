Executive Summary
The goal of the Concentrating Solar Power (CSP) Systems Analysis CORE project is to provide
timely and accurate cost data to assess the current state of CSP technologies as well as predict
performance and cost for precommercial and emerging technologies. The National Renewable
Energy Laboratory (NREL) maintains several open-source modeling tools that provide core
stakeholders with common platforms for CSP systems analysis. This project augments these
tools with new technologies, updates and improves existing models, and answers user questions.

Several tasks are recurring and focus on ongoing maintenance of existing tools, including
updating cost assumptions, engaging with international organizations, and addressing questions
from the user base of established NREL tools. This project updated costs for key CSP
components and frequently deployed systems by updating user assumptions in the CSP modules
of the System Advisor Model (SAM), NREL’s largest software library for the techno-economic
analysis of energy technologies. Specifically, the team added new physical trough collectors to
SAM’s library of options, and we updated the molten-salt power tower for the 2023 Annual
Technology Baseline, an electricity technology cost database managed by NREL, to reflect
current technology cost and performance. These efforts resulted in a conference paper that shows
plant sizes and markets under which parabolic trough solar thermal can be most competitive
when compared to natural-gas costs in some parts of the United States. Additionally, we resolved
all user questions for SolTrace and SolarPILOT, two CSP performance characterization tools,
over the course of the project. The release of SolTrace version 3.4 contains several significant
new capabilities and improvements, including updated help documentation, a Python scripting
interface, improved surface intersection calculations on complex surfaces, and surface import
functionality. Finally, NREL maintained active participation in the international CSP community
via its status as the operating agent for SolarPACES Task I and as an active participant in the
International Energy Agency’s Task 36 (Carnot Batteries) and Task 64 (Solar Process Heat).

The remaining tasks in the project focused on developing new models and technologies for
SAM. The first extension was the supercritical carbon dioxide (sCO2) power cycle, an emerging
option for CSP. We published a journal article Solar Energy describing the techno-economic
optimization of “Generation 2” (Gen2) molten-salt power towers integrated with the sCO2
recompression cycle 1, along with a conference paper detailing our modeling approach and the
design-point performance of the SCO2 recompression with a high-temperature recuperator
bypass cycle 2. We constructed a modeling framework to analyze and optimize both the
configuration and design of sCO2 cycles integrated with Gen3 single-pass flat-curtain falling
particle technology. We are using this model to investigate the sensitivity of system design-point
metrics on design parameters and plan to publish results in the next cycle of this project.

The second collection of model extensions in SAM in this project focused on the emerging
application of concentrating solar to industrial process heat (IPH). We added IPH technology
models in SAM for molten-salt tower and molten-salt linear Fresnel solar field technologies to
complement the existing physical trough and direct-steam linear Fresnel IPH models.

Pumped thermal energy storage (PTES) technology shares thermal energy storage and power
cycle concepts with CSP. Therefore, we integrated design-point PTES models into SAM’s PTES
module, which models the plant as a price-taker, showing the hourly effect of prices and thermal
degradation. A article in the Journal of Engineering for Gas Turbines and Power summarizes the
development of new off-design modeling methodology and plant control strategy for an ideal-gas
Brayton cycle PTES system 3. Our proposed off-design control strategy enables PTES to operate
robustly and deliver the required power under a wide range of off-design scenarios.

The final development of this project is a new model for a particle receiver and thermal energy
storage technology for demonstrating next-generation, “Generation 3” (Gen3), CSP technology,
modeled after the Gen3 Particle Pilot Plant being developed by Sandia National Laboratories.

further work is completed to add catch-and-release obstructions to the receiver particle pathway

1 Introduction
The Concentrating Solar Power (CSP) Systems Analysis CORE project estimates the current
market cost of CSP subsystems and technologies for use by the Department of Energy (DOE)
Program and within NREL analysis tools. The project addresses upgrades to NREL’s System
Advisor Model (SAM) related to CSP, as well as development of new modules within SAM to
expand the types of CSP systems that can be simulated and new tools for evaluating CSP
subsystem performance or optimizing CSP system layouts, for example, SolarPILOT and
SolTrace. The project evaluates the potential cost and performance of new CSP-relevant
technologies and generally includes assessment of CSP technologies in support of the DOE
subprogram.

A primary goal of the CSP System Analysis project is to provide timely and accurate cost data to
the DOE to assess the current state of CSP technologies as well as predict performance and cost
for pre-commercial and emerging technologies that may impact the industry. Prior work in this
task has produced technology cost reports and roadmaps for DOE and highlighted promising
research paths that could reduce overall CSP cost, such as the supercritical CO2 (sCO2) power
cycle and molten-salt trough systems.

CSP Systems Analysis produces modeling tools to evaluate the performance of CSP systems,
subsystems, or components, and journal articles, conference papers, technical reports and
databases to disseminate information to the CSP industry. CSP technology costs are estimated
and tracked using these tools as well as analysis of literature and industry sources. The CSP
modeling tools and NREL analysis results are used by researchers; government, financial, and
industry analysts; and other CSP stakeholders.

The purpose of this final technical report is to summarize the key accomplishments of the past
three years of this project. The project was divided into seven separate tasks, and in what
follows, we describe the background, objectives, key accomplishments, and path forward for
each task within the project, with one section of this report summarizing each task.

 1

2 CSP Cost Tracking
This task estimates the current market cost of CSP subsystems and technologies for use by the
DOE Program and within NREL analysis tools. NREL are one of the few organizations that track
CSP costs and subsystems, over long periods of time for the U.S. Organizations like the German
Aerospace Center (DLR) and the International Renewable Energy Association (IRENA) are vital
at highlighting cost breakdowns of parabolic trough systems and power tower systems
(Dieckmann et al. 2017; “Renewable Power Generation Costs in 2023” 2024) and provide
representative costs.

Prior work on this topic produced technology cost reports and roadmaps for DOE and
highlighted promising research paths that could reduce overall CSP cost such as sCO2 power
cycle (Mehos, Turchi, Vidal, Wagner, Ma, et al. 2017a) and molten-salt trough systems.

2.1 Project Objectives
This task focuses on updating costs for key CSP components and frequently deployed or
emerging systems. First, we compile performance and cost metrics for commercially available
parabolic trough collectors. This information is important because troughs are a commonly
deployed CSP technology, and they are also somewhat modular, so there are several active
designs and developers. Next, we update molten-salt power tower system costs for SAM and use
in NREL’s Annual Technology Baseline (ATB). This technology is the lone CSP representative
in the ATB, so it is important to use current performance and cost assumptions. Finally, we
investigate the cost of parabolic trough collector (PTC) technology for process heat applications.
This study considers the influence of scale on PTC costs and evaluates the effect of international
steel prices on total cost. In total, this task provides critical market and cost information to CSP
stakeholders.

2.2 Project Results and Discussion
2.2.1 Updated Parabolic Trough Costs in SAM
For Task 1 FY22, we undertook research to find cost and performance data for updating the
assumptions in SAM’s PTC library. The aim of the task was to update the cost and performance
of parabolic trough systems and related subcomponents. A prior parabolic trough survey was
undertaken to determine an estimate for the installed cost ($/m2). The survey with contacts in the
CSP industry (greater than 3 data points) found no significant change in the SAM default value
of $150/m2. Discussions with CSP developers have found no further improvement in the solar
field costs, or other systems.

The next focus was to update the SAM library with performance characteristics of commercially
available and developing PTCs. This includes metrics such as length, aperture area, aperture
width, and the incidence angle modifier (IAM), which would allow the ‘Physical Trough’
module to utilize a developer’s trough characteristics to model the annual performance of the
trough in SAM. This was in response to the industrial survey, where we received some feedback
from industry that the commercial parabolic trough options in SAM are 5-10 years old and need
updated.

2.2.2 Updated Power Tower Costs in SAM
In FY23, we focused on making significant updates to the ATB 2023 CSP data and projections.
For the 2023 release, the Base year was 2021. The SAM, CSP default parameters in 2022.11.21
have changed. This includes a larger solar field (1,348,316 m2 instead of 1,278,150 m2),
increased number of heliostats (9,339 heliostats), and a taller tower (height of ~195 m instead of
190 m). Parameters such as the solar multiple of 2.4 and a 10-hour thermal energy storage (TES)
system have remained constant. The default CSP case built in SAM, now includes an ‘Electric
HTF Heater’, which has the capability to electrically heat the hot tank (e.g., resistively heat the
molten salt) via grid electricity. It is worth mentioning that the SAM inbuilt direct normal
irradiance (DNI) for the locations used in the ATB (e.g., Daggett, CA, Phoenix, AZ and Abilene,
TX have all improved. As a result of these changes, the capacity factor of the baseline modelled
system increased to 66.6% compared to 65% in Daggett, CA.

2.2.3 Updated Benchmark Costs for Solar Industrial Process Heat From
 Parabolic Trough Concentrators
In FY24, the team was able to finalize and present the paper “Parabolic Trough Collector Cost
Update for Industrial Process Heat in The United States” (Akar and Kurup 2024) at the EuroSun

4

2024 conference in Limassol. A summary of the paper and completion of the milestone is

The primary cost driver and the focus of this analysis was the PTC over a range of sizes. The
cost of the installed solar field did not account for site-specific TES. The analysis utilized
updated generic HTF, mobilization, and integration costs associated with connecting the solar
industrial process heat (SIPH) field and the TES to the industrial site. The levelized cost of heat
(LCOH) is calculated based on these updated costs.

Design for Manufacturing and Assembly (DFMA) was employed to update the material costs
and machining steps associated with the SunBeam design, first for 510 SCAs, and then for 90
and 12 SCAs. In collaboration with Solar Dynamics, updated quotes for purchased
components—such as trackers, receiver tubes, and mirrors—were obtained for each of the three
field sizes. The installed costs were further adjusted by including site integration and heat
exchanger costs to estimate the LCOH, which was then compared to a site utilizing natural gas.

The 510, 90, and 12 SCAs represent large, medium, and small SIPH fields, respectively, with
solar field powers of 225 MWth, 40 MWth, and 5 MWth. The power output for each field size is
based on the 7.67 kWh/m2/day Direct Normal Irradiance (DNI) of Daggett, CA, and is designed
with a solar multiple (SM) of 2.3. This configuration is designed to integrate with a 6-hour TES
system.

The results from the study indicate that the installed cost for large solar fields (510 SCAs,
approximately 804,000 m²) has increased from $120/m² (2020$) to $184/m² (2023$) due to post-
pandemic inflation and rising steel prices from supply chain disruptions. For medium-sized SIPH
fields (90 SCAs, approximately 142,000 m²), the analysis estimates an installed cost of $197/m²
(2023$). For smaller SIPH fields (12 SCAs, approximately 19,000 m²), the cost increases to
$297/m² (2023$). These cost estimates reflect the 2023 steel prices in the United States. Figure 2
provides a breakdown of the estimated U.S. installed costs by component for the three solar field
sizes.

The current analysis indicates the steel components have the highest cost share followed by the
mirror panels and the receivers. While unit cost steel components correspond to ~52-54% of the
installed cost of 510 SCAs and 90 SCAs, this share can jump to 67% for the 12 SCAs (Figure
3).

Design changes can have a positive impact on the installed solar field cost of the PTC systems.
Considering 50%–60% of the total installed solar field cost comes from the material cost, any
design change that requires less parts and materials in the structural frame can lower the installed
cost significantly. Another important factor that affects the cost of manufacturing is the change
in process. As an example, replacement of stamping with roll forming or increased automation in
manufacturing and assembly such as robotic arm riveting would reduce the total installed cost of
the PTCs.

 9

Between 2020 and 2022, the installed field cost of parabolic trough collectors increased
significantly, from approximately $120/m² to $184/m², largely due to the surge in steel prices
during the COVID-19 pandemic (Figure 5). Key structural components of parabolic trough
systems, such as space frames, pylon supports, and support arms, are heavily reliant on steel.
During the pandemic, disruptions in supply chains and surging demand led to unprecedented
increases in steel prices across the globe, with the U.S. Producer Price Index (Figure 6) for iron
and steel mills peaking in 2022. This escalation in steel prices directly contributed to the rise in
estimated total installed cost. However, if steel prices had remained at pre-pandemic levels, the
expected installed field cost would have been approximately $132/m², representing only a
modest increase over the baseline due to inflation and minor component updates (Figure 5). This
cost variance highlights the disproportionate impact of steel on parabolic trough construction
economics.

2.3 Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
We added new PTCs to SAM library of PTC options in the physical trough power generation and
process heat models. The new PTCs represent recent improvements by PTC developers, and
including them in SAM allows stakeholders to analyze state-of-the-art solar thermal technology.

We updated the molten-salt power tower for the 2023 ATB to reflect current technology cost and
performance. The electric sector uses ATB to represent baseline performance of commercially
available power generation technology. For some stakeholders in that sector, the ATB may be
their primary source of CSP information, so it is important that the ATB numbers are up to date.

We applied DFMA methodology to update material costs and machining processes, improving
the overall design and cost efficiency of the SunBeam system. We then worked with Solar
Dynamics to obtain updated quotes for critical components (e.g., trackers, receiver tubes, and
mirrors) for various field sizes. We published at the EuroSun 2024 conference (Akar and Kurup
2024) our results that show plant sizes and markets under which solar thermal can be most
competitive when compared to natural gas costs in some parts of the U.S.

Data collection is a challenge because there are a small number of developers, and few are
willing to share their technology cost numbers. There are also only a few technology providers,
and 2022 through 2024, no CSP plants have been built in the U.S. We produced meaningful
results by leveraging DFMA® expertise, building trust with developers, and participating in
international solar thermal events. Future collaborative events and technology standardization
efforts will improve understanding and dissemination of baseline solar thermal costs.

2.4 Path Forward
There are multiple ongoing and future efforts related to cost modeling of CSP systems, with a
focus on the tower technology. Through the Heliostat Consortium, the team is conducting a
bottom-up cost analysis of SunLite, a heliostat design developed by SolarDynamics.
Additionally, we intend to revisit the cost assumptions for specific subsystems of the molten-salt
tower technology. The plan for next year is to revisit the cost curves for the tower, receiver, and
heliostat field specifically, which will influence both the CSP plant cost assumptions in the ATB
and SAM default costs.

3 International Engagement
3.1 Background
Finally, the CSP Systems Analysis project includes outreach and engagement with the
international CSP community. NREL serves as Task I Operating Agent in (SolarPACES). This
task addresses the design, testing, demonstration, evaluation, and application of CSP systems,
and supports an international project database for commercial CSP systems as well as the
development of acceptance test procedures and standards for CSP systems. The active
international sector provides valuable markets for US CSP developers and knowledge that can be
applied to systems in the United States. CSP plays in an international marketplace; understanding
international activity is essential to the health and relevance of the U.S. CSP industry as well as
the viability of domestic CSP projects.

Additionally, within the International Energy Agency (IEA) Technology Collaboration
Programmes of Solar Heating and Cooling (SHC) and SolarPACES, a new task in 2020 for
‘Solar Process Heat’ was established. ‘Solar Process Heat’ is known as Task 64 in the SHC and
is part of the continuation of Task IV in SolarPACES. NREL has been part of Task 64 from the
start of 2020. This task focuses on SIPH solutions for buildings and industry in the low to
medium temperature range (from 50 °C up to about 400 °C) and aims to help increase the
deployment of commercially ready solutions.

3.2 Project Objectives
Generally, the objective of the international task is to represent U.S. solar thermal in the
international community. As the SolarPACES Task I Operating Agent, NREL guides
international activities in standards development and CSP value analysis. In IEA Task 64, the
NREL role as a participant is to provide data, knowledge sharing and collaboration with the other
organizations. NREL’s role will help strengthen the DOE and Solar Energy Technologies Office
(SETO) position to influence the IEA SHC tasks on Solar IPH. The roles will also support
NREL’s efforts to establish itself as a leader in SIPH, disseminate the results of our work, and
track developments in the SIPH industry.

In IEA 36, which focuses on Carnot Batteries, the project team was invited to contribute to a new
IEA annex titled “Power-to-Heat and heat integrated Carnot Batteries for Zero-Carbon
(industrial) heat supply”. The Annex brought together experts from industry and academia with
the aim of identifying and evaluating the role of heat-integrated Carnot Batteries in the energy
transition. Electrically charged thermal energy storage systems and heat integrated Carnot
batteries can decouple the availability of renewable electricity and (waste) heat from the heat
and/or electricity demand in a cost effective and environmentally friendly way. They offer
excellent opportunities to provide storage and flexibility to the electricity market and
simultaneously integrate a large share of renewable electricity in the heating sector. Thus,
electrically charged thermal storage systems & Carnot Batteries can be implemented as flexible
sector coupling solutions, providing flexibility to the electricity grid and to decarbonize the heat
supply.

Although a significant number of studies have investigated the development and integration of
storage supported power-to-heat solutions in the context of sector coupling, the analysis is
typically done for a specific technology implementation in a specific use case. The same is valid
for heat integrated Carnot Batteries. Within the framework of Task 36 – Carnot Battery, several
implementations of the Carnot Battery were classified, assessed and use cases and barriers were
identified, focusing on the role of electricity-to-electricity storage only. At the time, in this
electricity-only context, several pilot plants and plant-scale facilities were deployed, and
significant scientific data was generated over the course of Task 36. An important outcome of
Task 36 was the realization that heat integration and heat extraction from Carnot Batteries offers
an enormous potential to provide the needed flexible sector coupling and decarbonize the heating
sector. Although first information on such heat integrated systems has been gathered within the
framework of Task 36, a systematic techno-economic and environmental assessment of this
potential and the flexibility in design for power vs. heat was not yet characterized: this was the
goal of the new IEA Task.

4 Model and Software Support
4.1 Background
NREL maintains several tools and models that serve core stakeholder CSP systems analyses. At
the center of the CSP Systems Analysis task is SAM, a system performance and financial model
to estimate the cost of energy from CSP and a range of other energy technologies (Kesseli et al.
2019). SAM is used to support SETO program efforts such as SunShot (Mehos et al. 2016) and
CSP Gen3 cost modeling (Augustine, Kesseli, and Turchi 2022) and is employed in many of the
CSP Systems Analysis projects, as well as being seen as an industry standard modelling tool.

5 sCO2 Power Cycle Design and Analysis
5.1 Background
The sCO2 power cycle was chosen for the Gen3 CSP effort as the best option for increasing CSP
power cycle efficiency to 50% at the target turbine inlet temperature of >700 °C (Mehos, Turchi,
Vidal, Wagner, and Ma 2017). There are a variety of sCO2 cycle configurations that have
different attributes like cycle efficiency, temperature difference across the heat input,
complexity, and cost. Prior work at NREL developed design and performance models for the
simple, recompression, and partial cooling sCO2 cycles. The models are written in C++,
available as open-source code, and accessible to users through a Python scripting interface 6.
These configurations offer their own advantages and disadvantages. Subsequent analysis (Neises
and Turchi 2019) of the integration of these configurations with CSP described a trade-off
between the recompression cycle offering higher efficiency and the partial cooling cycle offering
a larger temperature difference across storage (and consequently less expensive storage).

Additional configurations offering trade-offs between efficiency, cost, complexity, and heat
input temperature difference have been proposed, and recent literature from the industry shows
interest in the “recompression with preheat” (RC-PH) cycle that expands the temperature
difference across storage (Le Moullec et al. 2019). This configuration is less efficient than the
partial cooling cycle but offers simpler cycle architecture. Another issue for power cycle
performance is the ambient temperature. Recent research at NREL and the literature also shows
the sCO2 recompression cycle off-design performance has a strong dependence on ambient
temperature (Neises 2020). At ambient temperatures warmer than the design point, the cycle can
experience a significant drop-off in capacity as well as the expected thermal efficiency penalty.
Finally, Gen3 particle systems may offer significantly different system design trade-offs than
Gen2 molten-salt systems, because particles can operate stably over a large temperature
difference and Gen3 systems potentially have low-cost TES and receiver components (C. Ho,
University of Adelaide, et al. 2021).

5.2 Project Objectives
Prior work shows that the CSP industry has not settled on a dominant sCO2 power cycle design.
Furthermore, the power cycle cannot be studied in isolation, because its operation impacts the
cost, operating parameters, and performance upstream in the TES tanks and receiver that must be
considered. In addition, performance can be impacted by ambient temperature, so the optimal
cycle design, especially compressor and air cooler parameters, are highly dependent on the
climate and time-of-deliver electricity pricing schedule. Understanding how the power cycle
configuration and design assumptions influence plant techno economics and cycle design options
that can optimize plant performance is important to both characterize CSP systems that will
operate in hot (with large temperature variations), arid environments that restrict the plant to air-
cooling and to help sCO2 component researchers understand which components improvements
create the most value for CSP integrations. Finally, Gen3 particle systems present new trade-offs,

5.4 Techno-Economic Optimization of Annual Performance
The goal of this subtask is to optimize the design of a sCO2 recompression cycle, especially
design parameters related to heat rejection via air-cooling, to maximize the technoeconomic
return of a Gen2 molten-salt power tower plant employing the sCO2 cycle. This task builds on
prior work by combining i) sCO2 design-point optimization and parametric models, ii) a sCO2

 25

off-design performance model, and iii) SAM’s molten-salt power tower (MSPT) technology
model to capture the interaction between cycle design, cost, off-design performance, temporal
electricity pricing, and ultimately technoeconomic metrics that consider plant revenue (e.g.,
IRR). We describe our background, methodology, results, conclusions, and future work in detail
in a peer-reviewed journal paper (Neises 2023). The following summarizes key outcomes.

We present a modeling framework to evaluate the influence of cycle design, cost, and off-design
on the techno-economic performance of a molten-salt power tower integrated with a sCO2
recompression cycle. Our off-design cycle model uses a steady approach that fixes shaft speeds
and controls compressor inlet pressure (via inventory control) and the air-cooler fan power to
optimize cycle performance. Because some off-design solutions that require optimizing multiple
free parameters require solution times on the order of minutes, we must pre-process a sample of
off-design cases and then use a surrogate model during the annual simulation in SAM. The paper
presents a surrogate model that uses a novel heuristic approach to correct the resulting
interpolation when the cycle reaches its capacity limit at ambient temperatures which are hotter
than the design point. The surrogate model compares well with the detailed off-design model and
accurately finds the maximum HTF flow rate above which the cycle power becomes insensitive
to HTF mass flow rate and the HTF cold temperature increases above the design value.

Using the SAM model, we evaluate the sensitivity of the system performance to cycle design-
point ambient temperature, air-cooler approach temperature, air-cooler fan power, and
recuperator conductance. Then we simulate annual performance where the electricity pricing is
set by a “duck curve” schedule. Annual simulation results in Figure 8 show that while a cycle
designed at 30°C suffers capacity degradation at 45°C, its combined advantages of 1) higher
efficiency during most hours of the year and 2) lower cost results in a favorable IRR compared to
cycles designed at hotter temperatures. This analysis does not consider potential incentives
(beyond duck curve pricing, which doesn’t always correspond to the highest prices at the highest
temperatures) for generating nameplate capacity during the hottest periods. We recommend that
future studies extend the off-design control methods studied; for example, by adding shaft speed
or inlet guide vane control. Overall, we found that 1) our off-design model that shows capacity
degradation at hot ambient temperatures is consistent with the most recent off-design literature,
especially works published by entities closest to industry and 2) this paper contributes an
important examination of the influence of cycle design-point on MSPT techno-economic
performance, especially design point ambient temperature.

5.5 Design-Point Model of a Recompression Cycle With Recuperator
 Bypass
We developed a design-point model for the sCO2 recompression with high-temperature
recuperator (HTR) bypass power cycle in our sCO2 modeling platform to complement the
existing simple, recompression, and partial cooling cycle models. This cycle, shown in Figure 9,
splits off high-pressure CO2 from the HTR inlet and sends it to a “bypass” heat exchanger where
it is heated by the HTF stream leaving the primary heat exchanger. The CO2 outlet of the bypass
heat exchanger is then mixed with the high-pressure CO2 outlet of the HTR. The recompression

 27

with HTF bypass cycle can decrease the HTF temperature outlet while maintaining a simpler
cycle layout than the partial cooling design. The cycle has been investigated for use in coal fired
power plants (Mecheri 2018), waste heat recovery (Alfani et al. 2019), and CSP applications (Le
Moullec et al. 2019; Alfani et al. 2024).

 Figure 11. sCO2 recompression with HTR bypass cycle layout sCO2
We solve the recompression with HTR bypass cycle in a similar manner to the basic
recompression cycle with a few added assumptions. The recuperators are solved iteratively, as
with the recompression cycle; the only change is the HTR high pressure mass flow rate is
reduced, depending on the bypass fraction. The sCO2 bypass outlet temperature is assumed to be
equal to the HTR high pressure outlet (or offset by a constant value) to minimize the exergy loss
in the second mixer. The HTF PHX inlet and outlet temperature are defined by user inputs, so
the HTF mass flow rate is calculated to equal the CO2 heat transfer in the PHX. Then, we
calculate the bypass heat exchanger HTF outlet temperature using the known HTF mass flow
rate and PHX HTF outlet temperature.

The bypass fraction cannot be optimized to maximize cycle efficiency like the recompression
fraction, because it will always optimize to zero. Instead, the bypass fraction is a user input that
lets the user trade cycle efficiency for a larger HTF temperature difference. As such, the cycle
has five free parameters: compressor inlet temperature, recompression fraction, LTR
conductance, HTR conductance, and bypass fraction. When we run a parametric sweep with
these design parameters for Gen3 HTF inlet temperatures, we find that the recompression bypass
cycle does not achieve meaningfully better combinations of HTF outlet temperature, cycle
efficiency, and cycle cost than the baseline recompression cycle. This result occurs because
reducing the total recuperator conductance in the recompression cycle tends to have a similar
effect. Recent research by shows a preference for the recompression cycle versus the
recompression cycle with bypass (Alfani et al. 2024). However, different applications and design
parameters may lead to different outcomes, and it is valuable to have a model of this cycle. The
bypass may have value in providing better control at off-design or startup, but that analysis is

 28

beyond the scope of our modeling. Finally, removing the recompressor results in a simple cycle
with bypass configuration that shows interesting results in our subsequent sCO2-Gen3
integration study. We published a peer-reviewed paper at the 2024 Supercritical Carbon Dioxide
Power Cycle Symposium that describes our modeling methodology in detail and presents model
validation against the literature (Brown and Neises 2024).

The goal of this task is to understand how sCO2 cycle design influences design-point techno-
economics of Gen3 systems employing single-stage flat-curtain falling particle receivers and
two-tank particle TES. Our past research completed a similar analysis for Gen2 molten-salt
power towers that showed the less efficient partial cooling cycle may result in the lowest LCOE
because it allows for a larger temperature difference across TES, thereby reducing TES cost
(Neises and Turchi 2019). Gen3 systems introduce different trade-offs, as they offer the potential
for low-cost TES but utilize higher temperatures that cause more expensive power cycle and heat
exchanger components.

In this analysis, we model the six sCO2 cycle configurations shown in Figure 13: simple
recuperated (with optional recuperator bypass), recompression (with optional high temperature
recuperator bypass), partial cooling, and turbine split flow. We perform a parametric sweep of
cycle design parameters (as applicable): main pressure ratio, intermediate pressure ratio,
recompression fraction, bypass fraction, recuperator conductance fraction, and total recuperator
conductance to encapsulate the full design space. Then, we integrate each case from the
parametric sweep into a system-level CSP design point model that uses the cycle efficiency, cost,
and HTF temperatures to design the CSP system, simulate design point performance, and
calculate CSP component, system, and total installation costs. The performance of each case is
compared by calculating the cost per net power produced. The cost is the total system installed
cost, including heliostat field, tower, receiver and storage. The net system power subtracts
particle conveyance parasitic losses, which vary depending on thermal power and HTF
temperature difference. Figure 14 illustrates the models and data exchange we use to calculate
system design-point performance.

Preliminary results show that optimal cycle configurations that result in the lowest system
installed cost tend to trade cycle efficiency for lower cost cycle components and lower cost TES.
In fact, when filtering recompression and partial cooling cycle designs from parametric results,
the resulting optimal designs usually correspond to the lowest nonzero value of recompression
split fraction available in the parametric sweep. For the baseline case, the turbine split flow cycle
has the lowest $/kWe of all configurations. It achieves this by producing the largest HTF
temperature difference of all cases, which saves TES and HTF costs, and minimizes conveyance
parasitic losses. The next most optimal cases are the simple w/ bypass and simple cycles
respectively. Both cycles have higher efficiency than the turbine split flow case, with smaller
HTF temperature differences. The higher efficiency saves solar field and tower costs, and the
simplified layout of the cycle saves power cycle costs as well. It is worth noting that our current
baseline case uses aggressive cost targets for the PHX and heliostat field that result in a relatively
cheap cost of thermal energy generation. We except that as we increase the cost of components
that are sized inversely to thermal efficiency, cycles with higher thermal efficiency will become
more competitive.

5.8 Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
We published a journal paper describing the techno-economic optimization of Gen2 molten-salt
power towers integrated with the sCO2 recompression cycle. This paper presents new
methodology to correct interpolated off-design cycle results for cycle capacity limitations on hot
days and defines a modeling framework to assess the influence of cycle design parameters on
hourly annual performance. Our results show that cycles designed at the hottest ambient
temperatures earn the most revenue during the hottest days, but this benefit cannot overcome the
ability of cycles designed at colder ambient temperatures to achieve higher thermal efficiencies
during colder ambient temperatures. Overall, cycles designed at 30°C have a favorable internal
rate of return compared to cycles designed at 45°C for our assumptions. Scenarios where plants
have an incentive to produce nameplate capacity during the hottest days of the year may still
benefit from the 45°C design. This study shows the value in including off-design cycle
performance and hourly annual simulations to optimize cycle design for well-understood solar
thermal technologies.

We developed design-point cost and performance models of the recompression with high-
temperature recuperator bypass and turbine split flow sCO2 cycle configurations. We published
a conference paper detailing our modeling approach and the design-point performance of the
bypass cycle. Industry and the literature have proposed this cycle as an option for applications
because it increases the HTF temperature difference for heat input without adding
turbomachinery to the recompression cycle.

 35

We constructed a modeling framework to analyze and optimize both the configuration and
design of sCO2 cycles integrated with Gen3 single-pass flat-curtain falling particle technology.
We are using this framework to assess how key cycle design inputs like pressure ratio, flow
splits, and conductance influence cycle efficiency, temperature difference across heat input, and
cost. Then, we pass these high-level cycle design parameters to the Gen3 system model to
calculate the required solar field size, tower height, TES size and cost, lift cost, and particle
conveyance parasitic losses. The system model reports the total installed cost per net power
(subtracting unique conveyance parasitic losses) that we can minimize to find the optimal cycle
design. DOE and NREL agreed to extend this analysis into FY25 to leverage the modeling
framework to investigate additional parametric scenarios like CSP component particle cost
sensitivity.

As we discuss in more detail in the Gen3 section of this report, our single-pass flat-curtain falling
particle receiver thermal loss model shows that at target high flux design-point temperatures (e.g.
1400 suns), the receiver thermal efficiency is relatively insensitive to the temperature difference
across the particles. At these conditions, the competing effects of increasing the temperature
difference, 1) reduced mass flow reducing the curtain opacity and 2) less radiative heat loss from
a colder average particle temperature, tend to balance out. However, at lower design flux values,
the effect of decreased opacity dominates, and the thermal efficiency decreases with increasing
particle temperature difference. Gen3 developers understand this effect and are developing
“catch-and-release” multistage particle curtains to mitigate it. However, our receiver thermal
efficiency model currently doesn’t include multistage capabilities. As such, our analysis
currently fixes the receiver thermal efficiency, which implies a high flux at design.

5.9 CSP Path Forward
The sCO2 power cycle remains a potential option for future Gen2 and Gen3 power plants.
Ongoing commercial and government test campaigns should provide clearer expectations of
cycle component performance, especially the turbomachinery. As broad interest in the sCO2
cycle grows and the CSP community continues to develop novel receiver and storage
technology, it is important to continue development of tools and research that establish baseline
cycle and system expectations. As such, NREL’s FY25-27 Systems Analysis project includes a
sCO2 task. Our first objective is to complete sCO2-Gen3 design-point integration analysis and
submit to peer-review journal. Then, we will develop a UI to make the performance code,
currently only available via scripting, easier to use and accessible to a broader user base. Next,
we will research off-design cycle behavior for Gen3 cycle designs. Prior work developed a
detailed off-design model of the recompression cycle. In the FY25-27 Systems Analysis project,
we will update this off-design methodology based on the past five years of progress in the
research community, and we will apply these updates to the optimal cycle configuration in our
sCO2-Gen3 design-point study (which likely will not be the recompression cycle). Finally, we
will analyze the optimal G3P3-sCO2 system design considering cycle off-design influence on
annual performance. We will also pursue opportunities to validate the sCO2 model with
measured system data. Finally, as noted in more detail in the Gen3 section, a multistage falling
particle receiver model would add fidelity sCO2-Gen3 integration studies.

6.1 Background
Thermal energy for industrial processes is another potential market for concentrating solar
technology. IPH accounts for 8% of primary U.S. energy consumption (Schoeneberger et al.

6.6 Packed Bed Solid Media Thermocline in SAM
We added a packed bed solid media TES model in SAM. This technology consists of a tank
filled with a porous packed bed of low-cost solid media (e.g. rocks, spheres, etc.). The HTF (e.g.
oil) pass through the porous packed bed and exchanges heat with the TES via convective heat
transfer. The technology offers the potential for low cost, compact alternative to other storage
options, such as two tank systems (J. D. McTigue, Markides, and White 2018), although it may
exhibit more temperature degradation and require more complex controls. When charging, hot
HTF enters the tank and heats the solids closest to the inlet. As the solids heat, the thermal
gradient travels down the tank. The HTF exits the tank on the other end at a colder temperature.

 41

During discharge, cold HTF enters the opposite side of the tank and exits at a hotter temperature.
Figure 19 shows a schematic of the process during charge, where the thermal gradient moves
towards the hot inlet during the charging duration.

6.7 Single Owner and Commercial Owner Financial Models for
 Process Heat in SAM
We modularized SAM financial model methods so that the methods can be used with either
electricity or heat as the input and return the correct calculations and labels. The IPH
implementation calculates electricity purchases required to operate the CST equipment, includes
them as an operating expense in the financial calculations, and reports them in the cash flow.
Then, we added Single Owner and Commercial Owner financial models for the physical trough,
power tower, and molten-salt Fresnel IPH models.

The Single Owner model represents a “heat as a service” model where a CST developer sells
heat at a contracted “power purchase agreement” (PPA) price, analogous to the power generation
implementation where the CSP plant sells electricity at a PPA price. Heat as a service has been
proposed as a business model that approximates conventional thermal energy pricing structures
and moves the capital outlay to the CST developer.

The Commercial Owner model represents a scenario where the thermal process owner owns and
operates the CST as a mechanism to reduce existing thermal energy purchases.

Both models report net present value (NPV), while the Single Owner model reports internal rate
of return (IRR) and the Commercial Owner reports payback period. These metrics can help users
determine when a specific project or set of cost and financial assumptions is economical. Both
financial models calculate total installed costs from component level cost inputs, electricity
purchases, taxes, depreciation, and incentives.

6.8 Dispatch Optimization for Process Heat in SAM
Dispatch optimization is valuable when the value of the plant output varies on an hourly scale. In
a scenario where a process owner purchases CST heat to replace natural gas, the breakeven price
for the CST heat is correlated with the natural gas price, and natural gas prices typically do not
vary over the dispatch timescale of solar-thermal-charged TES (i.e. approximately daily).
However, in the scenario where CST competes with electric heat, then the breakeven price is
correlated with electricity prices which can have significant hourly variation. As such, dispatch
optimization is important to understand how solar thermal compares to and interacts with electric
heating options.

As prior work, we developed dispatch optimization for power generation models in SAM
(Wagner et al. 2017; Hamilton and Neises 2023), and we extended those routines to add dispatch
optimization for process heat. We tested the IPH dispatch model using simple, contrived time-of-
day heat pricing signals that assign a cost multiplier of 2 for morning hours and a multiplier of
0.01 for afternoon hours. This schedule incentivizes the system to generate during morning hours
whenever possible, but the system also earns a much smaller amount of revenue by generating
heat during afternoon hours. Figure 26 shows a comparison between the baseline dispatch and
dispatch optimization. The baseline case prioritizes heat delivery to the offtaker and not revenue,
so it delivers heat during the day and into the early evening hours until it exhausts TES. In
contrast, the dispatch optimization model prioritizes heat delivery in the morning hours.
However, in summer months with abundant solar resource, the dispatch model knows that it can
fully charge the four hours of TES for the morning and still deliver some heat in the afternoon at
a lower price multiplier. Overall, the system with dispatch optimization delivers around 9% less
heat but earns significantly more revenue. Consequently, the dispatch optimization system has a
higher LCOH but significantly better NPV and IRR.

 48

 Figure 28. Example of baseline dispatch versus dispatch optimization for hourly heat price
 multiplier of 2 in the AM hours and 0.01 in the PM hours
Dispatch optimization fits best with financial models that include temporal pricing. The Single

7 Electric Thermal Energy Storage
7.1 Background
Electric Thermal Energy Storage (ETES) is a grid-scale electric storage technology that uses
electricity to charge thermal energy storage when there is abundant electricity and then uses a
power cycle to generate electricity from the power cycle when there is demand for electricity.
PTES technologies generally use a heat pump to create thermal reservoirs when there is excess
electricity supply and then use the thermal reservoirs to power a heat engine to generate
electricity when there is demand for electricity. In this way, PTES offers dispatchable power and
rotational inertia. PTES requires up-front capital cost to purchase the heat pump and heat engine,
but it can add storage duration at low-cost relative to conventional batteries by simply increasing
the size of the thermal reservoirs (J. D. McTigue, Farres-Antunez, Sundarnath, et al. 2022).
Consequently, PTES has potential as a “long duration” (e.g. 6+ hours) energy storage (LDES)
technology with modeled round-trip efficiencies typically in the range of 55-70% for utility-scale
systems.

CSP has important overlaps and synergy with ETES technology, so it is likely that improvement
and deployment of ETES technology will benefit CSP. For example, ETES and CSP systems
both utilize TES and a thermoelectric power cycle - only the charging source, solar thermal
versus electricity, is fundamentally different. Furthermore, both systems leverage dispatchability
to maximize profits. CSP and ETES can also coexist in a hybrid plant where both electricity and
solar thermal sources charge the TES. Consequently, the research, commercialization,
deployment, and operational experience gained from PTES should benefit solar thermal, and vice
versa.

NREL has developed cost and performance models for ETES systems with ideal gas Brayton
cycle heat pumps in MATLAB and Python (J. D. McTigue et al. In Press) and estimated a
levelized cost of storage (LCOS) for the system at 0.12±3 cents/kWh and lifetime costs that are
competitive with lithium-ion batteries at durations of longer than 4 hours. NREL also developed
an ETES dispatch model in SAM that uses high-level models, like look-up tables and regression
models, to represent component performance (Neises et al. 2022). The ETES model optimizes
plant dispatch to maximize plant revenue (Hamilton and Neises 2023) and calculate plant
technoeconomic return given high-level design parameters like system cost, round-trip
efficiency, start-up time, switchover time, and storage thermal losses. This project is designed to
broaden the types of ETES configurations in SAM and add fidelity to the performance models.

7.2 Project Objectives
This task leverages the detailed component models and dispatch models developed by NREL to

reasons:

 • It uses molten-salt two-tank TES, similar to CSP technology,
 • Air-Brayton cycles employ relatively standard components and a non-toxic working

 • The technology has received significant private-sector investment.

7.3 Project Results and Discussion
7.3.1 PTES Design-Point in SAM
This subtask aims to leverage detailed PTES models in MATLAB to populate design-point
inputs for the SAM PTES model. NREL previously developed a detailed thermodynamic model
of ideal-gas Brayton cycle PTES in the MATLAB framework (J. D. McTigue, Farres-Antunez,
White, et al. 2022). This open-source model 7 can evaluate the techno-economic performance of
several PTES cycles and storage fluids and can be used for parametric studies and multiobjective
design-point optimization (J. D. McTigue, Farres-Antunez, J, et al. 2022). This is referred to as
the ‘detailed’ model due to several features, including: (1) calculating real gas properties (even
‘ideal gases’ exhibit a variation in properties over large temperature and pressure ranges); (2)
calculating heat exchanger geometry to facilitate off-design calculations and cost estimates; (3)
using iterative procedures to find component sizes for the system to deliver a desired power input
and output; (4) calculating exergy destruction in each component, including complex
mechanisms such as fluid mixing in storage tanks; (5) using iterative procedures to obtain steady
off-design operation for a range of off-design scenarios (such as variations in part load, ambient
temperature, and tank temperatures); (6) incorporating multiobjective design optimization
options.

The detailed PTES model inputs can be categorized into three groups:

 1. Cycle configuration: variables that define the type of cycle:
 o Working fluid – e.g., nitrogen
 o Storage materials – e.g., nitrate molten salts and hexane
 o Cycle type – e.g., Joule-Brayton cycle
 o Ambient temperature
 2. Component specifications: variables that define the performance of components:
 o Polytropic efficiencies of compressors and expanders
 o Performance maps of compressors and expanders (for calculating off-design
 behavior)
 o Effectiveness and pressure losses in heat exchangers
 o Heat exchanger structure (e.g. shell-and-tube) for calculating off-design behavior

7
 https://github.com/NREL/solar-PTES/tree/Kavin

 54

 o Efficiencies of pumps, fans, motors, and generators
 3. Cycle operating points: variables that will be used to define the state points around the
 cycle
 o Charge compressor inlet temperature and pressure
 o Charge compressor outlet temperature (from which outlet pressure is found) or
 compressor outlet pressure (from which outlet temperature is found).
 o Initial temperatures of hot and cold storage fluids
 o Working fluid mass flow rate in charge and discharge, from which charge and
 discharge powers are calculated, as well as component sizes
 o Discharge duration, from which is found the charge duration and energy
 capacities.
It is notable that having defined the cycle configuration and component performance, only a few
variables are required (3a, 3b, and 3c above) to define all cycle state points for both charge and
discharge. This is because the cycle is heavily constrained by two main factors:

 • A closed cycle is used, therefore the fluid properties (e.g. pressures and temperatures) at
 the ‘end’ of the cycle must equal those at the ‘start’. For example, if the pressure losses in
 each heat exchanger are known, as well as the compressor pressure ratio, then the turbine
 pressure ratio is constrained by this required.
 • At the design point, the cycle must be in a steady state – that is, the storage fluids must be
 in the same state at the end of the discharging cycle as they are at the start of the charging
 cycle. This ensures that the performance of every charge-discharge cycle is the same and
 that the cycle could theoretically charge and discharge continuously without any external
 actions being performed, such as resetting the storage tank volumes or temperatures.
Due to the large number of options and inputs that are possible to the MATLAB model, we
developed a simplified interface that reduced the inputs to high-level inputs in the SAM PTES
model. Users can then simulate the PTES design-point performance (i.e. round-trip efficiency
and power ratings). Then, we created a SAM macro that automatically loads the outputs from the
MATLAB model into SAM using the json file created by the MATLAB model.

 55

 Figure 29. Screenshot of PTES macro implementation in SAM

MATLAB requires users to purchase a license, so we cannot create an automatic interface with
SAM, and users without a license will not be able to use the MATLAB model. To enable PTES
design-point calculations in the open-source code base, we developed a simplified modelling
approach based on knowledge obtained while developing the MATLAB model. The simplified
model is implemented in SAM’s underlying C++ code base so that SAM’s User Interface (UI)
can directly access it. Simplifications include the use of constant gas properties (such as heat
capacity) and the use of an effectiveness and pressure loss factor to model the heat exchangers.
Assuming fixed effectiveness and pressure loss factor enables the cycle state points to be
calculated quickly without iteration. (Unlike the MATLAB model, which designs physical
dimensions of the heat exchangers for the charge cycle, then uses that design to calculate heat
transfer performance and pressure drops in the discharge cycle design point.

This process captures the slight difference between charge and discharge at design, but it adds
significant complexity to both the heat exchanger model and the system design iteration.) This
approach also simplifies calculation of the system sizing and performance. Once the cycle state
points are known, the specific power input and output are easily obtained, from which the round-
trip efficiency is calculated. The working fluid mass flow rate to give the desired power output
can then be calculated, as well as storage tank volumes, and turbomachinery power ratings.
(Whereas in the ‘detailed’ MATLAB model, obtaining a desired power output requires iteration
on the working fluid mass flow rate, which affects heat transfer and pressure losses in the heat
exchanger, and therefore affects state points and the round-trip efficiency. These effects tend to
be minor and can be neglected for simplified models but are worth incorporating when
considering techno-economic optimization or off-design operation).

 Figure 31. LCOS for 100 MWe discharge pumped thermal energy system as a function of charge

The results in Figure 29 all assume the same average electricity price for charging. In an actual
market system, the electricity price changes with demand, and many markets in sunny climates
see midday PV generation driving low prices. The results imply that systems that can charge
faster than they discharge can take advantage of lower electricity costs in the middle of the day,
resulting in a lower average electricity price for charging. Faster charging rates may be achieved
via ‘inventory control’ – that is, increasing the mass flow rate in the system to increase the power
input. However, to keep the turbomachinery operating close to its design point, the system
pressure must also be increased. The system therefore must be designed to manage higher
pressures which will likely lead to larger capital costs. A larger motor may also be required.

 58

It is unclear what effect these design enhancements have on capital cost. Therefore, we calculate
the ‘allowable increase in capital cost,’ i.e., how much the capital cost is allowed to increase if
lower electricity prices can be accessed to keep the LCOS constant. To lower overall LCOS, the
decrease in electricity price for charging must outweigh the increase in capital costs. Figure 30
shows what this new average electricity price for charging would have to be for a PTES with a
2:1 charge-to-discharge time ratio compared to a baseline system with a 1:1 ratio. The figure
shows multiple curves, each of which assumes a different original electricity price for charging
for the baseline system. For example, if the baseline system is purchasing electricity for charging
at 0.05 $/kWh, and switching to a faster charging rate could drop the purchased electricity price
by half to 0.025 $/kWh, the same or lower LCOS could be achieved if the capital costs do not
increase by more than ~70%.

SAM’s dispatch model optimizes PTES charging and discharging to maximize profit from
buying and selling electricity. In turn, the model requires “price taker” curves that represent the
cost of electricity as a function of the time of day. It should be noted that the price taker curves
are a function of the electricity demand and other generators on the grid for a given region.
Hence, each price curve is time and location specific, making development of a “representative”
price taker curve quite challenging. In addition, studies suggest the potential for PTES to be
competitive with conventional batteries when the storage duration exceeds 6-10 hours. The 2023
U.S. grid does not require these storage durations, consequently utility scale electricity prices
typically do not exhibit the magnitude of spread between high and low prices to enable revenue-
positive operation of a PTES system with a 50-60% round-trip efficiency. As such, when we use
hourly pricing schedules from utility data or databases of grid modeling results like Cambium,
we find the PTES is often idle for long periods of time. As a result, the system has low revenue
and financial metrics are poor. While artificially amplifying the electricity pricing schedule
would mitigate this issue (and perhaps be a first approximation of possible future grid scenarios),
it would not be appropriate for the journal paper analysis that we envisioned for this subtask.

 59

This finding is not a meaningful datapoint on the feasibility of PTES, but rather it reflects current
modeling limitations and lack of clarity about current and future incentives for energy storage.
PTES offers value beyond electricity pricing arbitrage that SAM’s performance and dispatch
model currently cannot calculate. For example, PTES can earn capacity credits and provide
rotational inertia, and the value and operational requirements for these services typically is
determined on a case-by-case basis. Additionally, continued deployment of instantaneous
generators may result in future grids with more opportunity for longer duration electricity
arbitrage. Finally, PTES has intriguing potential to incorporate into a hybrid system with power
heat generation sources and heat consumers. In the former, other heat generation sources like
solar thermal can provide low-cost heat and leverage the PTES power cycle during idle periods
(J. McTigue et al. 2020). In the latter, PTES can send waste heat from the inefficiency of its
round-trip energy conversion to a process that needs the heat (Neises and McTigue 2025).

Finally, we discovered during this subtask that PTES off-design behavior, e.g., part-load of
thermal degradation of the TES, is not well understood in the literature. However, it is an
important piece to fully understand how PTES will function as an energy storage device. As
such, DOE and NREL re-scoped the second year of the PTES task to focus on off-design
modeling of the ideal-gas Brayon cycle PTES cycle.

7.3.3 sCO2 PTES
Relative to ideal-gas Brayton cycles, development, and eventual deployment of sCO2 cycle
technology is even more relevant to solar thermal technologies because next-generation solar
thermal power would benefit from its potential high efficiency and techno-economic feasibility
at smaller capacities. Several companies are pursuing PTES concepts based on CO2 heat
engines. Echogen is developing transcritical sCO2 PTES and built an approximately 200 kWt
proof-of-concept system as part of the ARPA-E Days program (Echogen Power Systems, n.d.).
MAN Energy Systems are developing a “tri-generation” system that aims to develop a co-

 60

generation system that leverage the hot- and cold- reservoirs of sCO2-based PTES in addition to
its electricity generation (MAN Energy Systems, n.d.). Energy Dome built a 2.5 MWe demo of
their technology that stores ambient temperature and pressure gaseous CO2 when the system is
fully discharged and pressurized CO2 when the system is charged. While Energy Dome
technology shares similarities with both PTES and liquid air energy storage (Energy Dome, n.d.),
it employes turbomachinery, TES, and heat exchangers of interest to the CSP and PTES
communities.

We chose to model a CO2-PTES system that utilizes a recuperated simple cycle configuration,
reaches compressor outlet temperatures of around 360°C, and uses ice storage as the cold
reservoir (Echogen, n.d.). Because the CO2-PTES system is highly constrained and its full
system model requires real-fluid CO2 properties and several implicit relationships, we developed
modeled this system in Engineering Equation Solver (EES).

We started with the heat pump cycle:

 • First, we set the maximum pressure, compressor outlet temperature, and evaporating
 temperature. With a given compressor isentropic efficiency, we can solve for the
 compressor inlet.
 • Next, we assume for the moment (we override this assumption in the full system model)
 that the expander outlet is known, so with a given expander isentropic efficiency we can
 solve for the expander inlet.
 • Finally, we assume a realistic recuperator hot side approach temperature and guess the
 temperature at which the high-pressure stream exits the recuperator and enters the air-
 cooler. We override the latter assumption in the full system model to conserve system
 energy over a complete charge/discharge cycle.
Then we added the heat engine:

 • First, we set the maximum pressure, condensing temperature, pump inlet conditions, and
 turbine inlet temperature. With given isentropic efficiencies, we can solve for the
 turbomachinery outlets.
 • Next, we set the hot TES heat exchanger approach temperature. This constraint will fix
 the recuperated thermal power. Because the high-pressure stream has higher specific heat
 than the low-pressure stream, the approach temperature must be larger enough that the
 low-pressure stream is always hotter than the high-pressure in the recuperator. Currently
 we set this approach manually, so the solution is more robust, and we iterate on approach
 temperature as necessary to ensure feasibility.
Finally, we add system-level constraints to replace assumptions in the heat pump model:

 • First, we specify that the expander inlet must be an approach temperature hotter than the
 design ambient temperature, so that we can guarantee the air-cooler can achieve the inlet
 conditions.
 • Finally, we calculate the heat rejection required to balance the difference between charge
 energy and discharge energy.

exergy loss between the hot TES and CO2 between charge and discharge. For example,

power cycle. Furthermore, moving a recuperator between the air-cooler and the expander inlet
allows us to remove the constraint that the expander inlet is warmer than ambient. In turn, this
will let us set the expander outlet to subcooled conditions and ensure that hot ambient days don’t
require an extreme pressure ratio to reject heat.

7.3.4 PTES Off-Design
Many factors can cause the PTES system to operate in off-design conditions, and typically,
operating away from the design point will reduce the performance – e.g. round-trip efficiency
and power output. Relevant factors include the ambient temperature, storage tank temperatures,
and the power cycle mass flow rate. Understanding the impact of these off-design parameters is
important for several reasons:

 • Off-design parameters will have an instantaneous impact on efficiency and power
 input/output.

 63

 • There are also longer-term consequences, which may impact the performance of future
 cycles. For example, higher ambient temperatures can change the temperature of the
 storage tanks which then compromises the performance of future cycles.
 • The storage tanks contain fluid that has strict operating temperature limits (e.g. the
 molten salt must not freeze) so it is important to control the system in such a way that
 these limits are not exceeded.
 • Similarly, off-design operational points can significantly change the system’s mass flow
 rate and pressure, and it is important that pressure limits are not exceeded.
 • The plant operator may have target power output or input and needs to understand how to
 achieve these objectives given current off-design conditions and how this behavior will
 impact the ability to meet future requirements.
In summary, understanding and managing off-design operations is important because many
factors create off-design conditions that typically reduce performance. Numerous approaches
exist for managing these off-design scenarios, and a single off-design event can have
consequences that last many cycles.

The ‘detailed’ MATLAB model was extended to calculate off-design performance for range of
conditions, such as part-load, varying ambient temperature, and varying storage tank
temperature. Turbomachinery pressure ratio and efficiency were calculated from performance
maps. Heat exchanger geometry was calculated at the design point, and the off-design heat
transfer and pressure loss was calculated by finding the fluid properties, Nusselt number and
friction factor along the length the heat exchangers. An iterative procedure was used to find the
system pressure that enabled the cycles to ‘close’ – i.e., have the final state conditions at the
beginning and end of the cycle. A second level of iteration is employed to vary the working fluid
mass flow rate to obtain the desired power input or output (additional iteration is required since
changing mass flow rates affects heat exchanger pressure losses). This methodology is illustrated
in Figure 32.

 64

 Figure 34. Information flow diagram for PTES off-design solution

We then developed a two-step strategy to manage a range of off-design scenarios while
minimizing the impact on system performance and storage tank temperatures. The control
strategy first uses inventory control to manage the mass flow rate in the thermodynamic cycles,
which facilitates load following. With inventory control, the mass flow rate and pressure are
varied to keep the turbomachinery close to their design point: this keeps the efficiency and cycle
temperatures close to their design value. As an example, Figure 33 shows part-load operation of
the heat engine, which does not demonstrate significant changes in efficiency over a wide range
of part-load conditions. Inventory control therefore facilitates part-load operation and load
following.

 65

 Figure 35. Modeled part-load cycle output (left) and efficiency (right) using inventory control

The second step of the strategy manages the storage fluid mass flow rates: these are varied to
ensure the molten salt is maintained at its design temperature. This maximizes efficiency and
minimizes problems with salt freezing or degradation. The cold storage fluid mass flow rate is
varied so that cold tanks have the same state-of-charge as the hot tanks. This leads to variations
in cold fluid temperature, but these variations are found to be acceptably small. (The alternative
scenario was investigated, in which cold fluid temperatures were kept at the design point, but this
leads to the hot and cold tanks becoming ‘unbalanced’ which requires a larger energy penalty to
undo).

The off-design methodology and control strategy were published in the Journal of Energy
Storage (J. McTigue and Neises 2024). In that work, the ambient temperature and storage tank
temperatures are moved ±50 °C from the design values and the impact on power, duration, and
tank temperatures is quantified. Results demonstrate that the proposed control strategy is stable
and self-correcting – that is, storage temperatures converge on stable values after two-to-three
charge-discharge cycles. When inputs return to design values, the system returns to its design
point after two charge-discharge cycles. For example, Figure 33 shows a sudden change in
ambient temperature which affects the performance of the heat rejection equipment and can
therefore impact cycle temperatures. The control strategy keeps hot tank temperatures constant.
Cold tank temperatures change but quickly stabilize and then return to their design values when
ambient temperatures return to normal.

7.4 Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
We developed two technoeconomic models to calculate thermodynamic state points of the
charge and discharge cycles, round-trip efficiency, and capital cost. The first model uses
detailed, dimensional heat exchanger models and considers off-design effects to calculate system
performance metrics, and users must run the model via MATLAB and load results into SAM.
The second simplifies component design and estimate higher-level performance metrics like
round-trip efficiency, and users can run this model from SAM’s UI. We integrated these models
with the SAM PTES annual simulation tool that models the plant as a price-taker, showing the
hourly effect of prices and thermal degradation.

We developed a design-point thermodynamic model of a sCO2 PTES configuration. This
configuration employs TES around the same temperatures as CSP trough plants and has a cold
store that is near ambient temperatures, making it an intriguing option for hybridization with
CSP. Our results show that the efficiency of a simple recuperated cycle is approximately 5-10
percentage points worse than industry suggests, and we found that advanced designs employ
multiple serial stages of TES to offset flow capacitance differences in the recuperator. We were
unable to complete a model of the advanced design. Finally, we found that it is challenging to
develop sCO2 PTES models in explicit equations with a broad range of inputs for free
parameters because the system uses a fluid with rapidly varying properties and is highly
constrained and complex. Adding sCO2 PTES to SAM would be simplified by selecting specific
plant characteristics and limiting the available free design parameters.

We published a journal paper exploring new off-design modeling methodology and plant control
strategy for an ideal gas Brayton cycle PTES system (J. McTigue and Neises 2024). This topic is
important because PTES systems are highly constrained by TES temperatures and often
experience off-design conditions like varying ambient temperature, load, and TES heat losses.
Our proposed off-design control strategy enables PTES to operate robustly and deliver the
required power under a wide range of off-design scenarios.

We found that lack of clarity about potential PTES contracts and operating incentives prohibit
meaningful assessment of annual hourly system modeling, because current grid pricing and most
modeled future grid pricing does not present enough arbitrage opportunity for the system to
generate a positive return on investment when arbitrage is the only value stream. However,
system design-point models still return valuable information and design-point metrics like LCOS
and round-trip efficiency are useful indicators. While grid-scale operations remain uncertain,
modeling the direct coupling of an instantaneous electricity generator, PTES, and load can
provide insight into PTES techno-economics.

7.5 Path Forward
PTES remains a relevant technology for future long-duration energy storage deployments,
because it offers the potential to add storage duration at relatively low cost. The models we
developed in this project provide important new capabilities to model PTES system designs and
performance. The United States has many independent utilities, systems operators and potential
customers for electricity storage, so the future payment structures for potential LDES
installations remains uncertain and is an area for future work. The LDES National Consortium is

 68

working to identify new and/or existing markets, revenue streams, and use cases for LDES
through the convening of public and private stakeholders 8.

While PTES companies likely are focused on developing the first commercial offerings of their
core technology and not lower TRL system concepts, PTES hybridized with solar thermal and
PTES as combined heat and power are concepts that can utilize unique PTES characteristics like
low cost TES, low capacity factor power block, and availability of waste heat For example,
McTigue and Ma recently studied an ideal gas PTES-CSP hybrid design that uses a heat pump to
charge high- and low- temperature particle TES and implements a high-temperature particle
receiver that can utilize the PTES discharge cycle (J. McTigue and Ma 2025). They find designs
achieving PTES round-trip efficiencies > 60% and CSP conversion efficiencies > 40% when
particle temperatures are hotter than 800 and 900°C, respectively. Future work on system costing
and plant operations would help set cost and market targets. Future work on hybridization of
sCO2 PTES configurations that have the cold store near ambient temperatures may identify
systems with smaller differences between PTES and CSP discharge cycles.

8.1 Background
The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) selected particles (C. Ho, University of Adelaide, et al.
2021) as the heat transfer and energy storage medium for a demonstration of Gen3 CSP
technology (Mehos, Turchi, Vidal, Wagner, Ma, et al. 2017b). Particle technology was selected
over other pathways (i.e., molten salt (liquid) and gas) due to its possible reduction in cost and
complexity of solar thermal systems. Particles can operate at temperatures over 1000 °C without
degradation or need for hermetic containment which enables higher efficiency power cycles (i.e.,
supercritical CO2) and a wider temperature range for IPH applications (C. Ho, Sment, et al.
2021).

Researchers at Sandia National Laboratories have developed CSP particle-based techno-
economic system models for evaluating the performance and costs of their novel falling-particle
receiver technology (C. K. Ho et al. 2016; B. H. Mills et al. 2022; B. Mills et al. 2020). These
models use a combination of publicly available software (e.g., SolarPILOT) and in-house models
and correlations. However, Sanda’s techno-economic particle-based CSP system models are
private (i.e., not open source), uses a scripting equation-based interface (i.e., Engineering
Equation Solver), and require manually updating input files for solar field performance. The
latter uses SolarPILOT to model the falling particle receiver aperture as a rectangular flat plate
receiver with acceptance angles to approximate the impact of the SNOUT. To address these
limitations, this task’s objective was to create a Gen3 particle-based CSP system model within
the SAM software framework which includes adding the free-falling particle receiver with
SNOUT geometry to SolarPILOT for solar field characterization.

Based on conversations with Sandia, we identified capabilities, challenges, and gaps within their
existing system modeling efforts that this project could address to improve the SAM

implementation of a particle-based CSP system. First, we identified the characterization of the
heliostat field targeting the SNOUT receiver design, shown in Figure 35, as the single largest
subtask required for modeling particle-based CSP systems within SAM. The SNOUT design
improves receiver performance by reducing the impact of wind has on the free-falling particle
curtain. However, the SNOUT reduces the heliostat field view of the curtain, specifically for
heliostats near the field boundaries. The major challenge with this modeling effort is the
characterization of the solar flux profile onto free-falling particle curtain. This work requires first
a characterization of the solar flux profile through the SNOUT aperture. Then, we must project
the aperture flux profile onto the free-falling particle curtain which lies some distance deeper into
the cavity.

Another major challenge we identified is understanding how the Gen3 particle-based system will
scale within SAM, specifically the free-falling particle receiver. Historically, SAM’s CSP
systems have been able to scale over a wide range of receiver thermal powers. However, the
Gen3 particle-based system is a cavity receiver with a directional field. This optical constraint
limits the receiver thermal power that is economically achievable without introducing additional
receivers, e.g., Sandia’s proposed three receiver particle system (B. Mills et al. 2022).
Additionally, the free-falling particle receiver design has a limited curtain height―as it increases,
the particles accelerate and the density of the curtain decreases (K. Kim et al. 2009). As the
particle curtain density decreases, the curtain transmissivity increases, and incoming solar flux
can pass through the curtain irradiating and heating the receiver back wall of which can be
problematic if it results in back wall temperatures that exceed material limits. This behavior
motivates Sandia’s catch-and-release trough design that collects particles and restarts the curtain
over the front edge (J.-S. Kim et al. 2019).

8.3.2 Heliostat Field Layout Method
SolarPILOT’s heliostat layout method starts by generating all possible heliostat locations based
on the field boundaries and layout pattern (i.e., radial stagger or cornfield). Next, it uses the
Hermite series integral to determine each heliostat’s efficiency (including intercept) and
estimates the power delivered to the receiver over a subset of time periods (based on user
specific inputs). Once SolarPILOT determines the individual heliostat efficiencies and energy
delivered to the receiver, it orders them by a user’s choice metric and eliminates the worst
performing heliostats until the target receiver power is achieved at design point conditions.
Unfortunately, the Hermite method was never developed to account for obstructions between the
heliostats and the receiver absorbing surface, i.e., SNOUT and aperture.

To update SolarPILOT’s layout method, we had to modify the Hermite Series Integral to account
for the free-falling particle receiver geometry. The Hermite Series Integral is a relatively
complex analytical method that was originally implemented within DELSOL3 (Kistler 1986).
Our approach to modify this method was to change the “size of the receiver” (which defines the
integral bounds within the Hermite method) for each heliostat depending on its specific view of
the receiver’s aperture and SNOUT window. The SNOUT window refers to the aperture created
by the forward edges of the SNOUT. We calculate the “viewable” receiver size by projecting the
receiver’s aperture and the SNOUT window onto the heliostat’s image plane (a plane that is
orthogonal to heliostat-to-receiver vector). This projection will result in one of three outcomes;
the receiver’s aperture will be either 1) completely within, 2) completely outside, or 3) partially
overlap the SNOUT window projection. If completely within, the SNOUT does not block the
heliostat’s view of the aperture, and the receiver size used in the Hermite does not change. If

 74

completely outside, the SNOUT completely blocks the view of the receiver aperture and the
heliostat’s intercept efficiency is zero. If the aperture projection partially overlaps the SNOUT
window projection, then the SNOUT partially blocks the view of the receiver’s aperture. In this
case, we use the Sutherland-Hodgeman polygon clipping algorithm to determine the overlap
polygon bounding points. Then, we project these bounding points back onto the receiver plane
along the heliostat-to-receiver vector. This final projection gives us the “viewable” receiver
aperture height and width that goes into the Hermite series integral calculation.

By using the modified Hermite method, SolarPILOT can identify poorly performing heliostats
and remove them from the final field layout. Figure 39 presents SolarPILOT’s resulting heliostat
field with and without accounting for SNOUT during the layout calculations. When the SNOUT
is not accounted for, the resulting field has heliostats located on the extreme peripherals of the
receiver result in low individual efficiency, e.g., 8.3%. Additionally, the layout not accounting
for the SNOUT results in a 4% shortage in design receiver power. The new layout method
removes these low efficiency heliostats and adds heliostats to the back of field to achieve the
desired receiver power. However, the SNOUT layout does have a slightly lower solar field
efficiency because the SNOUT limits the heliostats field and requires heliostats to be located
further from the receiver resulting in greater optical losses.

8.3.3 Heliostat Aimpoint Methods
For this project, we focused on two heliostat aimpoint methods for the free-falling particle
receiver, referred to as “simple” and “image size priority.” The simple aimpoints method points
all the heliostats at the center of the receiver, in this case the receiver’s aperture. This method is
straightforward to implement; however, it creates a spot of very high solar flux concentration at
the center of the receiver (e.g., 5,500 kW/m2 or greater depending on heliostat performance
assumptions). The image size priority method orders heliostats by image size in descending order
and positions their images onto the receiver at the lowest flux concentration point while bounded
by a user-specified offset distance from the receiver’s edge. The biggest advantage of this
aimpoint strategy is the flux is evenly distributed across the receiver surface which results in a

 75

lower peak flux experienced by the receiver (e.g., 1,500 kW/m2). Without accounting for the
SNOUT limiting the view of the receiver, this aim point method would result in heliostats
pointing at areas of the receiver that could not be viewed by the heliostat resulting in increased
spillage (see left image in Figure 40). This problem worsens as heliostats are positioned closer to
the receiver, as can be seen by the low heliostat efficiencies in the first 6 rows in Figure 40. To
address this, we refactored the image size priority algorithm to account for the limited view of
the receiver through the SNOUT. Now the algorithm is limited to placing the heliostat image
only within the area of the receiver viewable by the individual heliostat. If the heliostat image is
bigger than the receiver viewable area, the image is placed in the center of the viewable area,
thereby minimizing spillage. We saw approximately a 2% increase in receiver power after
updating the image size priority aimpoint method for a constant field layout; however, this
increase in receiver power would be sensitive to the SNOUT geometry and receiver design
power.

8.3.4 Hermite Method Verification
Previously described free-falling particle receiver flux characterization work uses the Hermite
method (analytical) (Kistler 1986). To verify this implementation, we created a method to
translate the SolarPILOT’s free-falling particle receiver geometry parameters into SolTrace
geometry. SolTrace is a Monte Carlo ray-tracing (numerical method) software developed by
NREL (Wendelin, Dobos, and Lewandowski 2013). This implementation required special
attention because of the SNOUT that sits in front of the receiver aperture. To correctly calculate
the intercept efficiency, we needed to identify which incoming rays enter the receiver aperture.
This required the addition of a SolTrace virtual stage that is located at the receiver aperture. This
virtual stage allows SolarPILOT (by calling SolTrace and post-processing results) to determine
the exact number of rays reflected by the heliostat field that pass through the aperture. Before
this change, SolarPILOT could only determine if a ray intersected the receiver geometry. For this
new receiver geometry, this previous implementation is problematic because if a heliostat
reflected ray intersects the SNOUT surface it would be counted as a receiver hit thereby resulting
in artificially higher intercept efficiency. Figure 41 presents an example of the free-falling

 76

particle receiver geometry with a SNOUT modeled within SolTrace after exporting from
SolarPILOT.

We validated the updated Hermite method against SolTrace’s Monte Carlo ray-tracing method.
To get good agreement between the two flux calculation methods, we had to impose specific
surface properties to the SNOUT, cavity walls, and particle curtain for the SolTrace method to
model the surfaces as the Hermite method models them. The Hermite method cannot account for
secondary reflections and assumes all solar flux entering the aperture reaches the curtain. For the
SolTrace simulation, we assumed the SNOUT’s reflectivity was zero, the cavity surfaces had a
reflectivity of one, and the particle curtain reflectivity was 1 minus the user’s input for receiver
absorptance. Additionally, the particle curtain surfaces were treated as a reflection surface rather
than a refraction surface (which would enable curtain transmissivity).

8.3.6 Receiver Thermal Modeling
We develop an open cavity free-falling particle receiver thermal model with a suitable level of
detail for annual simulations in SAM. As this work proceeded in parallel with the initial
development of the SAM compute module, we first created a standalone particle receiver model
in Python outside of the SAM framework. Having the receiver model in a standalone and
callable form facilitates initial model development, testing, and comparison with Sandia’s
results. The code avoided the use of any special packages, solvers, etc. so that it is easily
translatable to C++ for use in SAM.

 81

The challenge in developing a meaningful reduced-order model for the falling particle receiver is
the uncertainty in advective losses, which dominate the total thermal losses from the receiver by
a significant margin (B. Mills et al. 2022). The advective losses arise from complex air flow
patterns resulting from wind effects, downward air entrainment by the particle curtain, and
upward buoyancy-induced currents. To our knowledge, there are no simple approaches that can
adequately predict these losses from first principles. Sandia uses computational fluid dynamics
(CFD) simulations to estimate the advective losses for their specific receiver designs. Sandia’s
reduced-order receiver models then assume a set of functional forms defining the advective loss
coefficient with no wind (hadv) and an adjustment factor (fwind) accounting for wind speed and
wind direction. In the most recent work (González-Portillo et al. 2023), these functions contain
eight total adjustable coefficients, which are determined by minimizing the difference in the
predicted efficiency from the reduced-order model and that from the CFD model over a range of
aperture sizes, incident solar power, particle mass flow, wind speed, and wind direction
conditions.

Correspondingly, these fits are specific to the receiver geometry (e.g., curtain location in the
cavity, relative sizing of the curtain and aperture), SNOUT geometry, other inputs used in the
CFD simulations (e.g., particle properties, inlet temperature, etc.), and the particular set of
assumptions used in the reduced order model when fitting the coefficients (e.g., curtain flux
uniformity, temperature uniformity, etc.) and cannot be expected to retain their predictive
validity for receiver designs, sizing, or operating conditions beyond those simulated in Sandia’s
CFD models. In the discussion below we utilize Sandia’s best-fit advective loss expressions;
however, we plan to implement three different options in SAM: (1) Sandia’s expressions, (2) a
user-provided constant advective loss coefficient, and (3) a user-provided lookup-table of
advective loss coefficients as a function of conditions.

Our starting point for a reduced-order falling particle receiver thermal model is a 1D model
originally developed by Sandia (González-Portillo et al. 2023) using best-fit parameters to
describe advective losses as discussed above. The model solves 1D mass, momentum and energy
balances for the particle curtain in the height dimension, and an energy balance at the back wall
accounting for radiative exchange with the curtain and external conduction loss. The model
assumes a fixed rate of increase in curtain thickness with particle fall distance based on
experimental data and calculates curtain optical properties as a function of local solids volume
fraction and curtain thickness using an approximate analytical model (González-Portillo et al.
2021). Additional key assumptions in this reduced-order model include:

 • Uniform curtain irradiation, uniform particle temperature and back wall temperature
 along the width dimension (at any height position).
 • The curtain size is equal to the aperture size.
 • The curtain exchanges radiative energy with only the cavity wall section at the same
 height position.
 • The fraction of the radiosity (total outgoing energy) at the front of the particle curtain that
 escapes the receiver aperture is an empirical coefficient fit from the CFD model results.
 The cavity walls in front of the particle curtain are not included in the 1D model.
We first re-created Sandia’s 1D model and verified that our results matched those produced by
Sandia at 189 sets of aperture size, incident solar power, mass flow, wind speed, and wind

 82

direction conditions published in (González-Portillo et al. 2023), as shown in Figure 46. Very
slight deviations compared to Sandia’s 1D model (left side of Figure 46) are simply the result of
truncation in the published best-fit advective loss coefficients.

We then created a second model implementation that relaxes the assumptions listed above to
capture the effects of 2D solar flux variability on the particle curtain (and thereby more
completely couple the receiver thermal model to the level of detail in incident flux distributions
available from SolarPILOT within SAM), and to generalize the radiative exchange model to
remove the additional empirically-fit parameter in assumption (4) and thereby improve the
robustness of the radiative transfer model to variations in geometry. We account for 2D incident
solar flux variation and 2D variation in particle curtain temperature by expanding the 1D model
to a quasi 2D model assuming no mixing between different width sections of the particle curtain.

The updated radiative exchange model calculates view factors between each section of the
discretized particle curtain and the aperture based on geometrical parameters (curtain size,
aperture size, aperture position, distance between the aperture and the curtain), and includes an
additional single surface representing the front wall between the aperture and particle curtain.
The updated model formulation is general enough to allow each element of the particle curtain to
exchange energy with any element of the back wall; however, for now we’ve retained
assumption (3) above for computational efficiency assuming a relatively small distance between
the particle curtain and the back wall. This model is more computationally expensive than
Sandia’s 1D model because it requires calculation of numerous view factors and an iterative
approach for convergence between particle/wall temperature solution profiles and radiative
exchange in the full cavity. As such, we will assess the balance of computational time and model
results once the model is implemented in C++ in SAM. The updated reduced-order model does
not produce substantially better (or worse) agreement with Sandia’s CFD results compared to
that shown in Figure 46 when evaluated at the same conditions, but adds generality for
simulation parameters outside those simulated in Figure 46.

The quasi-2D receiver model solves 1D mass and momentum balances for each curtain width
subsection assuming a fixed rate of increase in curtain thickness with particle fall distance. This
parameter can be adjusted by the user but, by default, is set to the value used by Sandia based on

 83

experimental data (González-Portillo et al. 2023). Curtain reflectance and transmittance are
calculated for each discretized element based on local curtain thickness and solids volume
fraction using an approximate analytical model (González-Portillo et al. 2021). 1D particle
curtain energy balances are solved in the particle fall direction for each discretized width element
and include incident solar energy, advective loss, and radiative loss. Energy balances for each
back wall element account for incident solar energy (after transmission through the curtain), IR
radiative exchange and external conduction loss. As previously discussed, the thermal losses are
heavily dominated by advection, and thus the advective loss coefficient is a critical parameter.
This coefficient can either be specified by the user in SAM, or calculated internally as a function
of wind speed, wind direction, particle velocity, and particle temperature using correlations fit by
Sandia to a large set of CFD simulations (González-Portillo et al. 2023). The internally
calculated particle curtain transmittance and advective loss coefficient can each be adjusted via
user-defined multipliers to evaluate sensitivity to these parameters.

The radiative exchange model is a typical surface exchange model, adapted to account for the
partially transmissive particle curtain. The domain includes 3*(nxny)+2 elements, with (nxny)
discretized elements representing each the front of the curtain, back of the curtain, and back wall,
with two additional elements representing the full aperture and the full passive wall between the
aperture and the front of the curtain. View factors between all discretized elements are calculated
based on the position of the element and the geometry. Thus, for example, each particle curtain
element has a slightly different view factor to the aperture depending on location. For a given
temperature solution, the radiative exchange between elements in the enclosure can be written in
a set of 3*(nxny)+2 linear equations. The solution iterates between this set of radiative exchange
equations and energy balance solutions until temperature convergence. The formulation is
generic and could therefore be extended in the future to include, for example, more discretization
in the front wall or addition of the SNOUT with additional view factor calculations.

Computational expense was initially prohibitive for an annual solution in SAM and thus multiple
strategies to reduce computational time were implemented. First, solution caching was added so
that the receiver class can store a set of the most recently calculated mass flow and temperature
solutions and thereby prevent re-calculation of identical solutions during system-level controller
iterations. Second, the model originally took numerous iterations to identify conditions at which
the receiver could not reach the target outlet temperature because it required successive

 86

tightening of lower/upper bounds on mass flow to a small tolerance. To alleviate this problem,
we assume that the receiver efficiency increases monotonically with mass flow, and thus an
upper bound on particle exit temperature can be approximated from the combination of the
current lower bound of particle mass flow (if known) and the receiver efficiency at the current
upper bound for particle mass flow (if known). Iterations are stopped when this upper bound on
particle exit temperature falls below the temperature target. Finally, the radiative exchange
model was adapted to solve using “groups” of elements rather than at the same resolution as the
curtain and back wall discretization. Each discretized element has a different temperature, but
groups of elements emit and receive energy reflected from other surfaces as if they were a single
element. This slightly smears out the spatial resolution of IR radiative exchange, but significantly
reduces the number equations in the radiative exchange model and has a dramatic effect on
solution time with only a minor effect on the total annual thermal output from the receiver as
shown in Figure 50. All solutions in Figure 50 use 20-by-10 (height x width) discretization in the
particle curtain but differ in how these elements are grouped within the radiative exchange
solution.

8.3.10 Multireceiver Systems
In the last year of this project, the Gen3 CSP task was expanded to include multireceiver
implementation within SAM. Previous work conducted during the Gen3 CSP gas pathway,
created multireceiver modeling capability within SolarPILOT. For this project, we utilize this
work and expanded it to include free-falling particle receiver technology. SolarPILOT uses a
mixed-integer linear program (MILP) to evaluate heliostat selection during layout design and
performance evaluation. This approach results in slightly longer solution times compared to a
single receiver system; however, the times are not detrimental to SAM implementation.

For the heliostat layout design problem, an initial layout is generated for all possible heliostat
locations within the field boundaries using the assumed layout method (either radial stagger or
cornfield). Each heliostat’s performance is evaluated for each receiver to calculate a proxy “cost
of energy.” The design MILP minimizes the proxy cost of heliostats included in the solar field
while meeting each receiver’s thermal power requirements. This problem determines which
heliostats will be used in the field design and assigns those heliostats to specific receivers. Figure
57 presents the heliostat-receiver assignments for a 3-receiver falling particle system with a total
thermal capacity of 150 MWt and the east and west receivers ordinated 60 degrees from north.

 Figure 59. Heliostat-receiver assignments for a three-receiver falling particle system

For the heliostat field performance evaluation problem (i.e., heliostat field size is fixed), the
MILP problem is modified to maximize power to the receivers. The performance evaluation
MILP has one constraint that majorly impacts heliostat-receiver assignments. This constraint

 94

requires the relative receiver power fractions to remain constant for off-design conditions. For
example, if all receivers are designed to have the same capacity, including this constraint will
result in heliostat assignments that maintain constant power across all receivers, shown in Figure
58a. This constraint results in some degraded optical performance, especially during early
morning and late evening hours when one side of the field is subject to lower efficiencies and the
other side is subject to higher efficiencies. Without this constraint, the MILP chooses the
heliostat-receiver combinations that produce the greatest power which can result in a receiver
receiving higher than design power, shown in Figure 58b. This issue of over-fluxing a single
receiver becomes worse for systems with different receiver capacities.

For both these cases, the heliostat-receiver assignments change with solar position. For the SAM
implementation, we added an additional operating mode where the heliostat-receiver
assignments are fixed for all time periods. This operating mode should have slightly better
optical efficiencies than the constant power operation but not subject any single receiver to over
fluxed conditions.

unique inputs for each receiver. Additionally, we have added a Boolean variable defining if the
receivers are duplicates of each other. When this Boolean is True, all receiver geometric
variables are assumed to be equal to the first element of the receiver input arrays. This reduces
the number of inputs required to be defined by the user when modeling multireceiver systems
with identical receivers. The user is still required to provide unique receiver azimuth angles and
tower offset positions of the receivers when modeling identical receivers because these two
receiver parameters define the orientation and location of the receivers and must be unique in a
real system.

For multiple receiver systems, the specific targeted receiver by each heliostat becomes an extra
degree of operational freedom. We previously explored the heliostat-receiver assignment options

 95

already existing within SolarPILOT. We implemented a multireceiver aimpoint strategy that we
feel provides the SAM particle tower model with an adequate estimate of heliostat field
performance without the complexity of dynamically changing receiver assignments through
time. After the heliostat field is determined (either by SolarPILOT or imported by the user), our
approach simulates the design point solar position and assigns heliostats to receivers based on the
receiver’s power fraction provided by the user.

Once heliostats are assigned to receivers, that decision is fixed for all solar positions simulated
here by after. This approach also fixes the number of heliostats and therefore the heliostat field
area assigned to each receiver throughout the annual simulations. This assumption significantly
decreases the modeling complexity of multireceiver system annual operations and thereby the
computational expense. However, in a real system, there exist situations where plant operators
would want to re-assign heliostats to different receivers to maximize thermal production. For
example, if a receiver is operating at a low-efficiency point or down for maintenance, heliostats
assigned to the affected receiver that can view adjacent receivers could be used to increase
adjacent receiver’s output thereby reducing the impact of low-efficiency or outage receiver.
Quantifying the impact of these heliostat-receiver reassignment decisions will be important for
the commercialization of multireceiver tower systems; however, it is out of scope for this work.

We implemented and tested all the methods for SAM users to interact with SolarPILOT for the
multireceiver systems. These method include: 1) SolarPILOT generates the heliostat field and
predicts field performance and receiver flux profiles, 2) user provides specific heliostat positions
and SolarPILOT predicts field performance and receiver flux profiles, 3) user provides flux
maps, field efficiency maps, and heliostat positions and SolarPILOT calculates land area and
heliostat field area, 4) user provides land area and heliostat area as well as everything specified
in option 3 (SolarPILOT is not needed), and 5) SolarPILOT optimizes the tower and receiver
geometry. Option 1 is the traditional workflow of SolarPILOT and did not require much
additional work to achieve in the multireceiver context.

We did have to create an approach for the heliostat-receiver assignment problem over the course
of the annual simulation, discussed in the previous paragraph. Options 2 and 3 required
modifications to SolarPILOT’s multireceiver field design mixed-integer linear programming
(MILP) approach. For these cases, we fixed heliostat placement decisions so SolarPILOT could
not remove any heliostats that the user specified; therefore, SolarPILOT multireceiver MILP was
only making decisions on heliostat-receiver assignments. Options 3 and 4 required us to modify
the user input structures to handle multireceiver inputs. For example, field efficiency maps were
scaled to include multiple field efficiencies, consistent with the number of receivers, for each
solar position (i.e., a field efficiency for each receiver at each solar position). Option 5 required
an updated optimization variable formulation for multireceiver systems which will be discussed
next.

In other CSP tower-based SAM models (i.e., molten-salt systems), users can enable SolarPILOT
to optimize tower height, receiver height, and receiver diameter while generating the heliostat
field and its associated performance. We implemented a SolarPILOT optimization method
specific for multireceiver Gen3 falling particle systems. The design variables are tower height,
aperture height/width, and receiver azimuth. The latter of which is only a variable for
multireceiver systems. For this optimization, we assume that aperture is square, the receivers are

 96

identical, and that the receivers are positioned symmetrically about the north-south axis (i.e., the
west receiver’s azimuth is the negative value of the east receiver’s azimuth). These assumptions
simplify the tower-receiver optimization problem while still providing useful solutions to SAM
users. While implementing and testing the tower-receiver sizing optimization, we discovered that
if a multireceiver field cannot achieve design power, SolarPILOT fails by returning NAN values
for optical power and only a few random heliostats (i.e., 2-5) within the field. This behavior is
undesirable when using optimization algorithms that may explore poorly designed heliostat
fields.

To address this issue, we implemented a catch method when heliostat field design problem is
determined to be infeasible. In this catch method, we keep all initial heliostats and calculate field
performance based on the optimal heliostat-receiver assignments. With this approach,
SolarPILOT can continue to calculate the optical power which allows SolarPILOT’s
optimization algorithm to evaluate the performance of poorly designed fields without crashing.
Design instances that cannot achieve design optical power are penalized within the objective
function; thereby, incentivizing the optimization algorithm to stay in the feasible design space.

8.3.12 Receiver Defocus Methodology
In typical single-receiver models in SAM, the CSP system controller/solver provides the weather
conditions, HTF cold inlet temperature, and the fraction of the heliostat field focused on the
receiver at any point in time. A collector/receiver class calls the individual field and receiver
performance models at the provided conditions, and returns the receiver operational state, field
parasitic loads, and receiver performance (mass flow, exit temperature, efficiency, thermal
output, etc.) back to the controller/solver. This quarter, the original single-receiver particle
collector/receiver class was replaced with an updated version capable of handling multiple
receivers. The new structure (Figure 59) splits the controller-provided overall heliostat field
focus fraction between the individual fields, calls each individual field and receiver performance
model, aggregates the outputs and returns only the combined receiver output to the system
controller/solver. The code is generalized to accept any number of receivers, but has only been
tested substantially with one, two, or three receivers. The model currently neglects receiver
startup time and/or energy requirements; however, we plan to incorporate startup requirements in
the upcoming quarter.

 Figure 61. New multireceiver particle collector/receiver class
Figure 60 illustrates an example of simulated thermal output and receiver efficiency in SAM for
a case with three receivers at orientations of 0° (north, N), 114° (southeast, SE), and -114°
(southwest, SW). As expected, the east-most facing receiver operates further into the afternoon
hours due to high afternoon efficiency of the east-side heliostats. Similarly, the west-most facing
receiver starts up earlier in the morning due to higher morning efficiency of the west-side

 97

heliostats. However, the west-facing receiver efficiency is more sensitive to wind conditions
from the predominantly westerly wind at the simulated Daggett CA site, and commonly operates
at lower efficiency than the north or southeast receiver.

 Figure 62. Thermal output and receiver efficiency from an example three-receiver simulation

Under typical operation with a field focus fraction of unity the multireceiver simulation is
relatively straightforward – each individual receiver solves for the mass flow required to achieve
the target particle exit temperature, and if the target exit temperature cannot be achieved under
current conditions, the individual receiver is shut off. However, the behavior becomes more
complicated when the system controller requires part of the heliostat field to be defocused
because the storage capacity and power cycle cannot accept the full receiver output. In this case
the collector/receiver class must decide how to defocus individual heliostat fields in accordance
with the overall controller-provided signal. This decision is complicated by several factors: (1)
the incident power and thermal performance differs for each receiver, (2) the behavior of
receiver exit temperature vs. mass flow rate is non-monotonic (Figure 61) owing largely to
changes in particle curtain absorptivity with mass flow, and (3) the minimum focus fraction at
which any receiver can achieve the target exit temperature is unknown and depends on the day,
time, weather conditions, particle inlet temperature, etc.

 98

  Figure 63. Example particle exit T vs. mass flow rate and incident thermal power (design point
 incident power = 50 MWt)

There are multiple possible strategies that could be employed to split the focus fraction between
the individual heliostat fields. The system controller iterates to find a focus fraction that can
balance mass flow between the receiver(s), TES, and power cycle; thus, any split of the focus
fraction between receivers that keeps all receivers operating at the target temperature is
functionally equivalent at the system level (with only slight differences in heliostat field parasitic
loads). However, given the behavior illustrated in Figure 61, it is not always possible for one or
more receivers to meet the target exit temperature at low incident power. Solutions that shut
down one (or more) receivers to reduce the total mass flow incur additional receiver startups that
are likely operationally undesirable and can lead to discontinuities in the mass flow vs. focus
fraction response that lead to errors in system-level mass and energy balances. To avoid these
challenges, the strategy implemented in SAM this quarter prioritizes keeping each receiver
operating (if the receiver could achieve the target exit temperature at full focus) even if
individual receivers produce under-temperature output at low focus fractions.

A logic-based heuristic was developed to iterate over attempted splits in the focus fraction to
minimize the occurrence of under-temperature operation while promoting a smooth aggregated
mass flow vs. focus fraction response to facilitate system convergence. The heuristic starts with
an initial guess for focus fractions for each receiver, based on the relative mass flow rates at full
focus. The mass flow in any receiver is set to the mass flow required to achieve the target
particle exit temperature (if possible). If the receiver can’t achieve the target temperature, then
the mass flow in that receiver is simply fixed to the product of the mass flow at full focus and the
heliostat field focus fraction. If the solution does not produce the target exit temperature from all
receivers, then a new guess is created by adjusting the focus fractions of individual receivers up
or down (with constant average focus fraction), with different logic for these adjustments
governing cases where: (1) at least one receiver achieved the exit temperatures, (2) no receiver
achieved the exit temperature, or (3) at least one receiver produced a net loss of energy. The best
solution after the allowed number of iterations was defined to be that which keeps the most
receivers operating with positive efficiency at the highest combined particle exit temperature.

8.3.13 Particle Receiver Inlet Temperature Sensitivity
Various sCO2 cycle configurations have implications on the particle temperature difference, TES
sizing, cycle and TES cost, and receiver performance. In the last quarter we investigated the
sensitivity of receiver performance to particle inlet temperature conditions. A nominal 50 MWt
north-facing receiver was selected with an aperture 6.51 meters in both height and width, a
curtain size with 1,400 kW/m2 average incident solar flux, and a target hot particle exit
temperature of 775°C. At design-point conditions, the predicted receiver performance was
relatively insensitive to particle inlet temperature over a wide range of wind conditions (Figure
63).

As with any receiver, decreasing the inlet temperature decreases the HTF mass flow (for a given
thermal power and exit temperature), and generally decreases the heat loss simply because a
larger fraction of the receiver is at a lower temperature. However, changes in mass flow in the
falling particle receiver have implications on particle curtain transmittance and absorptance, and
the competing effects of lower average temperature vs. higher curtain transmittance at low
particle inlet temperature produce a slight local maximum in receiver efficiency at moderate inlet
temperatures. A traditional liquid-phase receiver HTF has a conceptually analogous decrease in
internal heat transfer coefficient with a decrease in mass flow, but typically a much smaller effect
on receiver performance.

 100

 Figure 65. Receiver efficiency and mass flow rate as a function of inlet temperature, wind speed,
 and wind direction at design point incident solar power conditions (59 MWt)
At off-design low-power conditions, the sensitivity of the receiver efficiency to particle inlet
temperature increases substantially. In addition to decreasing the receiver efficiency under part-
load conditions, low particle inlet temperatures restrict the range of off-design conditions over
which the receiver can operate. “Missing” points in Figure 64 indicate conditions under which no
mass flow can reach the target 775°C exit temperature. For example, at the worst-case wind
direction (315°) and 50% design incident power (30 MWt) the receiver could only operate when
particle inlet temperatures exceeded 575°C, while at 75% of design incident power (44 MWt) the
receiver could operate with inlet temperatures as low as 200°C. The locations of these effective
operational cutoffs depend on the aperture and curtain sizing, and the effective minimum
turndown ratio increases as the size of the curtain decreases for a given thermal capacity.

Figure 66. Receiver efficiency and mass flow rate as a function of inlet temperature, incident solar
 power (design point incident power = 59 MWt) and wind direction with a 5 m/s wind speed

Figure 65 illustrates the annual receiver output from the SAM particle system model for a 50-
MWt north-facing receiver and Daggett, CA TMY2 weather conditions. At the base case
aperture size from Figure 63 and Figure 64 (6.51 m, 1400 kW/m2 average design solar flux) the
receiver produced approximately 18% less thermal output annually with a design inlet
temperature of 200°C than 600°C. With a smaller aperture size and higher incident flux
concentration the reduction in annual output decreased to approximately 7%.

8.3.14 Receiver Heat Loss for Field Generation
Falling particle receiver performance is greatly impacted by advection and radiative losses out of
the receiver’s aperture. When SolarPILOT generates a heliostat field layout, it does not model
the thermal performance of the receiver but instead relies on a receiver thermal loss per surface
area user input parameter to appropriately size the field to overcome receiver losses and achieve
design power. Historically, SAM’s molten-salt tower models have relied on users to provide a
good estimate of this parameter so that SolarPILOT can size the heliostat field appropriately. For
the Gen3 particle system model, we wanted to improve on this approach by allowing the receiver
thermal model to directly calculate this heat loss term for SolarPILOT. To implement such an
approach, we moved the receiver class initialization to occur before SolarPILOT is called. Figure
66 presents an information flow chart of SAM’s receiver thermal model and SolarPILOT
initialization process. During the receiver class initialization, it estimates the design-point
performance based on a uniform flux across the aperture assumption.

This design-point performance provides an estimate of heat loss per aperture area that can be
passed to SolarPILOT to inform heliostat field layout. This approach works well if the receiver
(and aperture) size is known a priori which is not the case when the user wants to use
SolarPILOT tower and receiver sizing optimization method. In this case, we still initialize the
receiver class and estimate heat loss before SolarPILOT by using the user provided receiver
dimensions as initial guesses. SolarPILOT runs its optimization method to determine receiver
aperture dimensions and then SAM re-initializes the receiver class given these new aperture
dimensions and re-estimates the receiver heat loss term. This updated heat loss term is passed
back to SolarPILOT where it generates the field design and performance map based on the
optimized variables values. This second iteration approach ensures that SolarPILOT
appropriately sizes the heliostat field given the best aperture size and estimated heat loss term.
By implementing the receiver thermal model to calculate the heat loss per surface area term for
SolarPILOT, we were able to remove this input parameter from the model thereby reducing
burden on users to accurately estimate this term outside of SAM or reducing the modeling error
by users unaware of this parameter’s importance.

8.4 Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
Within this project, we generalized and implemented the free-falling particle receiver with
SNOUT into SolarPILOT to enable its use in a full system SAM model. This required modifying
the Hermite analytical methods to account for the SNOUT impeding the view of the receiver
aperture. This Hermite method modification enabled SolarPILOT’s existing layout and aimpoint
methods to be used within SAM system model. We verified the modifications to the Hermite
method using SolTrace (numerical ray-tracing software) with good agreement. We replicated
previously developed 1D free-falling, flat-curtain particle receiver thermal model and expanded
it to a quasi 2D model that enables 2D representation of incident solar flux (coming from
SolarPILOT), curtain particle temperature, and cavity back wall temperature. This removed some
of the 1D modeling assumptions and adds generality for SAM receiver thermal model. We
integrated both the updated SolarPILOT methods and novel quasi 2D receiver thermal model
into SAM as well as particle specific balance of plant subcomponents (i.e., particle thermal
energy storage bins, particle lifts, particle-to-sCO2 primary heat exchanger, and sCO2 cycle
performance) to create an annual simulation model that can be used for techno-economic
analysis.

In addition, we updated system subcomponent cost models to be consistent with previous work
on particle-based system modeling. We compared SAM’s performance and LCOE prediction
with published results. We updated SAM GUI to include the newly developed particle-based
CSP system model. Lastly, we expanded the particle-based system model to include multiple
receivers on a single tower which was not a trivial task because multireceiver systems have more
operational degrees of freedom that must be handled during the annual simulation, e.g., receiver
start-up and shutdown procedures and heliostat-receiver assignments. In total, the new
capabilities in SAM and SolarPILOT enable stakeholders to assess the techno-economic
performance of emerging Gen3 particle technology in an easy-to-use framework that enables
straight-forward comparison to other CSP and renewable technologies.

One of the biggest challenges that faces our SAM model implementation of Gen3 particle-based
CSP is the estimation of advection losses. As previous work shows, these losses can have a
significant impact on the free-falling particle receiver performance and there is currently no way
to estimate these losses using first principles. These losses must be derived from either
experimental results or high-fidelity modeling (i.e., CFD), neither of which can be easily
generalized. This limits how well our SAM implementation can predict particle-based CSP
performance across a wide range of receiver thermal ratings.

8.5 Path Forward
There are several opportunities to add important capabilities to the Gen3 model developed in this
project. The receiver thermal model should be extended to include cascading particle flow (i.e.,
“catch-and-release” troughs) to increase the residence time of the particles and maximize the
particle curtain opacity in the peak flux region. This design feature could improve the scalability
of the technology and enable the receiver to operate over a wider range of mass flowrates. The
receiver thermal model should be extended to include other passive wall temperatures (i.e.,
cavity walls and SNOUT surfaces). This modeling extension would enable the receiver thermal
model to predict unsafe operating conditions (i.e., over flux conditions) and, when combined
with a heliostat field flux characterization model, users could evaluate aimpoint strategies to

 104

mitigate these unsafe conditions. Lastly, our receiver thermal model should be validated against
G3P3 on-sun testing data. This will ensure that our performance model can accurately represent
the G3P3 test facility being developed and tested at Sandia National Laboratory. This validation
effort would not resolve previously stated challenges with advection losses; however, it would
provide us with a better understanding of these losses at the G3P3 scale.
