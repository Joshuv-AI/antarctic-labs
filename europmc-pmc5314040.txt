The two cases hereafter described are considered worthy of
report because of the comparative rarity of the uncomplicated
c?ndition. A fracture of the talus (astragalus) is not uncommon.
It is of great importance, particularly if unrecognised, because
the disability is then so great, while, if treated, an exceedingly
good and useful ankle is the result. A fracture-dislocation of
the talus, however, although rare, is usually recognised as a
Serious injury to the ankle joint. In the majority of cases of
fracture of the talus there is an associated fracture of the
calcaneus or of the fibula, or an associated dislocation at the
ankle joint.
Anatomy of the Talus.?The talus is divided into a head,
a neck, and a body. It is situated between the tibia above and
the calcaneus below, and is grasped firmly on either side by the
two malleoli. It receives the whole weight of the body. The
head is the anterior part of the bone and presents a large
articular surface, facing forwards. The neck is the constricted
Part behind the head. It bears the interosseous groove which
therefore narrows it and gives attachment to the strong inter-
?sseous ligament which binds the talus to the calcaneus. Small
nutrient foramina penetrate the neck, to convey branches of
the dorsalis pedis artery and its tarsal branch. The body is
quadrilateral, with four surfaces and a posterior border. On
the posterior border there is a groove in which the flexor
hallucis longus runs and on either side of which are two
tubercles, medial and lateral, the latter being well developed
and giving attachment to part of the external lateral ligament
?f the ankle joint.
The outer casing of the bone is a thin shell, the main bulk
?f the bone being composed of cancellous tissue. Fractures
?f this bone, both direct and indirect, are usually caused by
considerable violence. Three varieties are described: fracture
of the neck, fracture of the body, and fracture of the posterior
Process. The body fracture is usually a comminuted one,
without marked displacement of the fragments, while the
fracture of the posterior process is often only a supposition, the

An operation was carried out on 20th December 1926. A semi-

the tendo achilles divided by splitting it as for a lengthening operation.
The displaced body of the talus was defined in the hollow of the
medial side of the heel with the flexor hallucis longus tendon

difficulty in healing, probably owing to the compound nature of the
first injury, but eventually the patient made a wonderfully complete
recovery. His movements were much limited but there was great
improvement effected by this partial talectomy.
Case II.?J. S., aged 37. Admitted to Ward 7, Royal Infirmary,
10th January 1928.
Complaint?Pain and stiffness in his left ankle and foot.
History.?The patient jumped off an 8-foot wall in the dark, on
22nd November 1927, and landed feet first in a rubble ditch. He
felt a sudden sharp pain in the left ankle on landing and was unable
to bear any weight on the affected foot. A right-angled splint was
applied, but the pain continued even without movement. It was on
for four days and massage was instituted, but the pain did not improve
and he was sent for further advice to the Infirmary. On examination,
the left ankle was seen to be swollen and slightly discoloured, and
the bony landmarks obscured by cedema. The hollows on either side
of the tendo achilles were filled up forwards to the malleoli. Active
and passive movements were possible in all directions but much
limited and with much pain. An X-ray showed that there had been
an oblique fracture through the neck of the talus, the body being
afterwards dislocated completely backwards and rotated on its transverse
axis, so that the fractured surface lay behind the lower part of the
tibia and fibula, facing downwards, and closely adherent to them.
On the 14th of January an operation was performed by the author.
A lateral J-shaped incision was made after the manner of Kocher.
The peronei tendons being pulled forwards and upwards, the foot
strongly inverted, and the ankle joint opened, the displaced fragment

It is interesting to reconstruct the accident and to determine
the exact cause of the fracture. There must always, of course,
ln a. fracture of the talus, be an approximation of the tibia and
the calcaneus, but to result in fracture of the neck this approxi-
mation must be produced while the foot is in a dorsiflexed
Position, so that the neck is nipped between the anterior edge
?f the tibia and the fore part of the upper surface of the calcaneus.
The narrow neck of the bone is perhaps further weakened by
the presence of many nutrient foramina, so that it is somewhat
easily susceptible to comparatively mild degrees of violence.

The chief immediate symptoms are similar to a Potts
fracture: swelling and pain on weight-bearing or flexion of the
foot, but the deformity differs in that there is an irregular filling
up of the hollows on either side of the tendo achilles posteriorly*
although this may be more accentuated on one side or the
other, while the tenderness is over the talus and not the
malleoli. Crepitus is usually absent. The foot is held in the
plantar flexed position to relax the tendo achilles as much as
possible, but there appears to be little tendency to either valgus
or varus deformity.
The fracture is very commonly overlooked, even in the
existence of good X-rays, but a consideration of the cause and
the symptoms should put one on the look-out for such an injury-
In the existence of displacement, the injury is a serious one,
and there is apt to be a permanent disability, particularly if the
error is not soon reduced. Stalig has collected 122 of these
cases, and in 50 the fragment was removed. Eight required
amputation, and he states that in most of them the mobility
of the ankle joint was permanently impaired. Permanent
ankylosis is apparently very common. The injury may easily
be converted into a compound one by a pressure-necrosis from
the fragment pressing on the skin, so making the outlook more
immediately serious.
The consensus of opinion in regard to those fracture-
dislocations of the talus is that the best treatment is to remove
the displaced fragment if seen late, but that it may be possible
to reduce the error if recognised within a day or two of the
receipt of the fracture.
Under a general anaesthetic, traction is put on the foot with
the ankle joint and the knee flexed to a right angle. The tendo
achilles is thus relaxed while the space is opened up for the
receipt of the fragment which may be manipulated back to its
normal site with finger and thumb. The foot may be further
flexed so that the receptive place is enlarged.

To achieve replacement of the fragment it hitherto seems
have been considered necessary to carry out a tenotomy of
the tendo achilles. The writer considers, however, that all the
relaxation required can be secured by flexion of the knee, and,
this position does not permit of replacement, then removal
?f the fragment should be carried out.
A lateral J-shaped incision down the back of the fibula and
below the lateral malleolus gives a good access to the fragment
and does not lead to any operative trauma to the important
structures on the medial side of the achilles tendon. There
does not appear to be any necessity to remove the head and
neck of the bone as their retention does not produce any foot
deformity.
A few days of absolute immobility is essential and then
Passage and active and passive movements are instituted,
during the convalescence a lateral iron and a medial T strap
are important adjuncts to the treatment in preventing any
tendency there may be to a lateral deformity.
