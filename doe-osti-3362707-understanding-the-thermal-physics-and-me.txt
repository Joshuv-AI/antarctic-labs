 1. Abstract

This project focused on understanding the effect of thermal history on the microstructural
development during metal Big Area Additive Manufacturing (m-BAAM), here using wire-arc
additive manufacturing (WAAM). Overheating and anomalous cooling rates in turn can cause
inconsistencies in the microstructure, leading to sudden failure when used in safety-critical
applications. In other words, flaw formation in m-BAAM parts is governed by the thermal
history – intensity and spatial distribution of heat inside the part during printing. The thermal
history is a complex function of the part shape and process settings such as welding energy,
path taken by the welding torch for deposition (tool path), wire feed rate, among others.
To ensure consistent part quality, it is essential to predict thermal history and, subsequently,
microstructural development which control part performance. Consequently, this project used
graph theory approaches to predict thermal history and characterized the microstructures to
corroborate this thermal history, as they are sensitive to temperature excursions and cooling.
Top-to-bottom variations in microstructure (grain size, phase content) and hardness were
determined. For single-bead walls, microstructure was well correlated to thermal histories.
However, triple-bead walls were more microstructurally complex at a scale not captured by
graph theory approaches, owing to the complicated thermal histories of multiple-pass
techniques.

 2. Technical Description
This project sought to correlate the microstructures observed within wire-arc additively
manufactured (WAAM) structures and measured and calculated cooling rates. The material was
a low-carbon steel (ER70S-6), and the parts were fabricated by collaborators at IIT-Hyderabad.
Two walls were built, one using a single-track weld bead, and one using a triple-weld bead. The
former was straight, while the latter was S-shaped (Figure 1).

Thermocouples were installed at the base (holes are visible in Figure 1(b)) to monitor
temperature changes during processing, and Graph Theory approaches were used to model the
temperature profiles (see Section II). For each wall, a vertical section was cut out and sectioned,
with microstructures examined after appropriate metallurgical preparation. An example is shown
in Figure 2. The objective is to correlate aspects of the microstructure with local cooling rates (or
undercoolings), which influence microstructural scale and phase formation. For the single-wall
experiment, local cooling rates were extracted from the cooling curves calculated using Graph
Theory; these are overlaid on continuous cooling transformation diagrams in Figure 3. It should
be noted that cyclic heating occurs, as already-deposited layers heat back up upon subsequent
layer deposition. If the temperature reaches the austenite regime, then microstructural evolution
“restarts” and generates new features. Thus, the final cooling rate is of consequence, and
considered here. In general, for this the cooling rates decreased as building heights increased
(Figure 4(a)). Grain size distributions were determined from electron backscatter diffraction
analysis. One expects finer grain sizes at higher cooling rates. Figure 4(b) shows that there is a
higher fraction of finer grains closer to the substrate, qualitatively in agreement with Figure 4(a).
Further, the pearlite spacing can reveal variations in undercooling (spacing is inversely
proportional to undercooling). Here we are focused on undercooling, as theoretical models of
lamellar spacing of eutectoid transformations are generally isothermal; in general, higher cooling
rates result in greater undercooling. The lamellar spacings were measured for different heights,
and again there is good agreement supportive of higher cooling rates closer to the substrates,
shown by the Graph Theory models (Figure 4(c)).

In summary, for the single-bead wall, the microstructural analysis strongly supported the Graph
Theory modeling of the cooling rates, indicating at least a qualitatively good thermal modeling
scenario.

Triple-bead Wall
The thermal modeling on the triple-bead wall was significantly more complicated. The
microstructural variations we observed suggest that thermal variations were on a more localized
scale, making spatially-resolved quantification of the thermal history unfeasible. Nevertheless,
we were able to qualitatively describe thermal histories and explain microstructural evolution.
Further, we were able to show that ultrasonic investigations revealed large-scale microstructural
periodicity, arising from processing.
Figure 5 shows a compilation of optical microscopy images. The optical microscopy images
revealed different zones of microstructure formation. Within a single layer with a 2.1 mm layer
thickness, several regions exist where different types of grains, including equiaxed polygonal
ferrite grains and columnar grains, were formed. The triple weld bead boundaries can be seen on
each layer cross-section view with large columnar grains on the boundary of the layer (bead
boundary) and finer polygonal grains at the layer center. This trend, as shown in Figure 5, is
repeatedly formed due to the layer-by-layer triple-weld bead deposition. To gain a more detailed
understanding, higher-resolution optical and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis was
performed on selected regions within individual layers (Figure 6, regions I–V). These
investigations enabled a more precise distinction of localized grain size variations, grain
morphology transitions, and the effects of overlapping beads on microstructural refinement. Five
distinct microstructural regions were identified across the wall cross-section, each characterized
by differences in grain morphology and scale. At the melt pool boundaries and along the bead
interfaces, coarse columnar grains were predominant. Optical and SEM imaging of these areas
confirmed the presence of a banded structure, where elongated columnar grains were
interspersed with finer grains. On the other hand, the layer/melt pool center consists of uniform,
polygonal ferrite grains.

Region I corresponds to the central bead boundary (where the final bead is deposited at the
center), where large columnar grains are oriented opposite to the build direction, consistent with
heat dissipation away from the last-deposited track, interspersed with acicular ferrite grains.
Region II, the transition zone, shows the gradual disappearance of columnar grains and the
emergence of larger polygonal ferrite grains. Toward the midsection of the melt pool, Region III
is characterized by uniformly shaped, smaller polygonal grains. Region IV, located in the overlap
zones between adjacent beads, exhibited the finest polygonal ferrite grains, indicating
microstructural refinement due to reheating and remelting. At the outer edges of the wall (Region
V), large columnar grains develop, oriented toward the wall center along the thermal gradient,
with thin bands of finer grains consisting of predominantly acicular ferrite, interspersed between
them.
The combined effects of thermal gradients, solidification dynamics, and crystallographic growth
selection control the formation of the five microstructural regions in the WAAM-fabricated ER70S-
6 wall. At the bead boundaries and outer edges (Regions I and V), the thermal field is strongly
directional, with heat rapidly conducted away toward the surrounding solid material. This steep
thermal gradient promotes the growth of elongated columnar grains, aligned opposite to the build
direction and toward the center along the direction of maximum heat extraction. Such orientation
patterns are a characteristic outcome of fusion-based additive processes, where the solidification
front is dominated by the thermal gradient rather than random nucleation. In this region, which is
affected by the solid-state transformation occurring in non-equilibrium conditions during multipass
deposition of layers, the previously solidified metal re-austenite, and during rapid cooling,
austenite transforms to different types of ferrite, starting from allotriomorphic ferrite, then
Widmanstatten ferrite, and eventually acicular ferrite, which nucleates from inclusions.
The crystallography of low-carbon steels further highlights the formation of the five distinct
microstructural regions. For BCC structures, the <100> orientation exhibits the fastest growth
kinetics under thermal gradients during solidification. Grains aligned with <100> parallel to the
heat flow direction therefore advance more rapidly and overgrow neighboring grains of less
favorable orientation. This competitive growth leads to the development of columnar grains whose
<100> axes are nearly parallel to the thermal gradient. At the same time, the relatively large
molten pool volume produced during deposition on top of a previously solidified layer extends the
solidification time at the bead boundaries. The slower cooling rate in these regions enhances
grain coarsening, yielding the large columnar grains observed along layer boundaries.
Moving inward to the transition zone (Region II), the thermal gradient weakens, and cooling rates
are reduced, creating conditions that favor the nucleation of larger polygonal ferrite grains. This
transformation is consistent with the columnar-to-equiaxed transition (CET) zone. At the center of
the melt pool (Region III), heat dissipation into surrounding solid material leads to relatively higher
cooling rates and more uniform solidification, and more importantly, less effected by subsequent
thermal cycles. These conditions result in finer, equiaxed polygonal ferrite grains, characterized
by reduced growth competition and limited preferred orientation. In the bead overlap regions
(Region IV), the material undergoes multiple thermal cycles due to successive passes of the
welding torch. This repeated reheating and partial remelting of overlapping regions promotes
grain refinement, producing the finest ferrite grains across the cross-section. The overlap zones,
therefore serve as sites of microstructural refinement, driven by localized cyclic thermal exposure.
This behavior can be further explained in terms of the ratio of the temperature gradient (G) to the
solidification rate (R), which controls the solidification morphology. A high G/R ratio, as present at
bead boundaries and wall edges, favors columnar growth, whereas a reduction in G or an
increase in R shifts conditions toward equiaxed or polygonal grain formation. Toward the bead
center, where thermal gradients are weaker and cooling rates are relatively higher, the G/R ratio
decreases, leading to the nucleation of finer polygonal ferrite grains. In the bead overlap zones,

repeated reheating and partial remelting impose additional thermal cycles, promoting refinement,
which explains the exceptionally fine ferrite grains found in Region IV. Thus, the combined
influence of heat flow direction, crystallographic orientation, extended solidification times, and
G/R-controlled solidification stability explains the formation of five distinct regions and the
arrangement of columnar and polygonal ferrite grains within one single layer and also repeated
trend of this type of microstructure along the build direction.
Additional factors contributing to the repeated pattern of microstructure formation with large
columnar grains on bead/layer boundary and polygonal ferrite grains in bead/layer center include
the imposed dwell time between layers and the time delay between deposition of successive
tracks within each layer. The 15-minute interlayer dwell time allowed partial cooling, followed by
reheating from subsequent deposition, which promoted partial remelting and grain growth. In
certain cases, this cyclic heating produced banded structures and periodic variations in
microstructure and grain size along the build height, as confirmed by non-destructive ultrasound
testing (Figure 7). Similarly, within each layer, the delay between the first and last deposited beads
subjected earlier tracks to additional thermal cycles, further refining grains and modifying their
orientations. The combination of solidification physics, interlayer reheating, and deposition
sequence thus explains the recurring arrangement of columnar and polygonal grains, as well as
the five distinct regions identified in the microstructural analysis.
Electron Backscatter Diffraction (EBSD) was done in collaboration with PNNL (Nicole Overman).
The analysis was carried out on the x–y cross-section of a representative sample, covering a total
scanned area of approximately 2.2 × 3.4 mm². A montaged EBSD orientation map of this region
is presented in Figure 8. The results confirmed the microstructural features observed in optical
and SEM imaging (Figure 6), highlighting clear differences within a single deposited layer: coarse
columnar grains were prevalent along the melt pool/ layer boundaries, while fine equiaxed
polygonal ferrite grains dominated at the melt pool center.

 Two key trends emerge from the results presented in Figure 9. First, a pronounced
increase in hardness from 250 HV to over 350 HV was observed in the region corresponding to
the heat-affected zone of the EN8 steel substrate (Figure 9(a)). This high hardness can be
attributed to the hardening caused by melt pool penetration and localized heat treatment of the
substrate. Beyond this region, the hardness values recorded along the build height exhibit only a
gradual increase when data from different wall positions (bottom, mid-height, and top) are plotted
together.

The second notable feature is the fluctuation in hardness within individual layers, ranging from
160 HV to 190 HV, most clearly illustrated in Figure 9(b) for a representative mid-wall sample.
This variation reflects the periodic grain structure generated during WAAM processing. Hardness
is reduced in regions dominated by coarse columnar grains near the melt pool or layer boundaries,
while the centers of layers, which contain finer polygonal ferrite, show comparatively higher
hardness. Superimposed on this layer-wise fluctuation is a gradual upward trend in hardness
along the build direction, which corresponds to an increasing volume fraction of pearlite phase
formed on grain boundaries (Figure 10(c)). As pearlite is a harder constituent, its enrichment at
grain boundaries enhances the overall hardness response of the wall. Quantitative phase fraction
analysis on three cross sections of wall with corresponding SEM images (Figure 10(a,b)) confirms
a ~9% increase in pearlite content from the bottom to the top of the build, consistent with the
observed hardness evolution.

Graph Theory Approach to Thermal Modeling

Graph Theory was used to develop rapid thermal simulation techniques for WAAM using the
discrete Green’s function (GF) method. This method is semi-analytical, which is distinct from
fully numerical methods such as the finite element or finite difference methods. Specifically, the
discrete GF method is analytical in time, which provides that the method is stable for timesteps
of any size, and the precision is independent of the time step size. In contrast, finite element
and finite difference methods require many small timesteps to control stability and precision,
Previous work with the discrete GF method demonstrated for thermal simulation of the Laser
Powder Bed Fusion process (LPBF). For that process, the method provides part-scale thermal
simulations that are computationally faster than traditional numerical methods, at comparable
precision, and the goal of that work was to open a path to better control of additive
manufacturing processes by understanding thermal history during the build. The present project
extends the method to WAAM processes. The specific WAAM technique studied was wire-arc
process with inert gas shielding.
Several advances to the discrete GF method were carried out to adapt the method to WAAM.
Because the torch speed for WAAM is much slower than the laser scan speed for LPBF, a slow-
moving source with the Goldak double-ellipsoid shape was applied. The slow torch speed also
required that the tool path be carefully considered in the simulation, and a key advance in the
work was to create a meshing procedure independent of the toolpath. This approach speeds up
the thermal simulation, and has the potential for future work to include optimization of the
toolpath without adding significantly to the computing time required. Another advance is the
introduction of node placement on grids with variable node density. Previous work with the
method involved grids of uniform density. The use of lower node density away from the build
surface, where temperature gradients are low, greatly reduced the simulation computation time
with small impact on simulation precision. Specifically, with these advances the discrete GF
method provided thermal simulations of comparable precision to that of a commercial finite
element code, but much faster, at one-third to one-tenth the time required.
Future work with the method is planned in several areas made possible by faster thermal
simulations. Feed-forward control is pre-planning of machine settings for the build, such as
laser power, tool path, and interlayer dwell time, by running sufficient thermal simulations to
identify optimal conditions for the build to maximize part quality. Feed forward control will be
explored to control the metallurgy of the finished part, as determined by correlating post-build
characterization with temperature history of each layer as it is deposited.

Conclusions
This study presented a comprehensive characterization of the microstructural evolution and
mechanical property variation in ER70S-6 low-carbon steel produced by CMT-based wire arc
additive manufacturing. Both a single-bead wall and a triple-weld bead wall were built. For the
triple-bead wall, a 15-minute interlayer dwell time, and an S-curved scan path was used, enabling
a systematic investigation of intra- and inter-layer temperature gradients. Through EBSD analysis,
hardness mapping, and microstructural quantification, the walls were shown to develop distinct,
spatially periodic features directly linked to the thermal history imposed by the chosen deposition
strategy.
The major findings of this study can be summarized as follows:
 • For the single-wall build, microstructural evolution matched well with Graph Theory models
 of thermal histories, showing that relatively local temperatures can be adequately

 predicted using Graph Theory, thereby reducing thermal modeling computational times
 and expenses
 • For the triple-bead weld, thermal gradients were more challenging to predict
 • In the triple-bead wall, significant grain-size heterogeneity formed between bead/layer
 boundaries and centers. EBSD revealed coarse columnar ferrite grains exceeding 150 μm
 at boundaries transitioning to fine polygonal ferrite grains (20–50 μm) at layer centers.
 • Localized crystallographic texture developed at bead/layer boundaries, with a maximum
 M.U.D value of 4.46, driven by higher G/R ratios that favored directional solidification and
 columnar grain growth, whereas layer centers solidified under more equiaxed conditions.
 • Periodic hardness fluctuations were observed across approximately 12 consecutive
 layers, reflecting the alternating microstructural regions; harder regions coincided with
 smaller grains and lower hardness related to larger grains.
 • Pearlite content increased by ~9% along the build height, contributing to a gradual rise in
 hardness. Pearlite formed exclusively at bead/layer centers, where cooling was slower,
 whereas bainite formed at layer boundaries due to higher cooling rates, as confirmed by
 optical and SEM observations.
 • The periodic microstructural and mechanical pattern was observed all along the build
 direction and arose from three interacting main factors:
 o (i) the prolonged 15-minute interlayer dwell time,
 o (ii) time delays inherent to the S-curved scan path, and
 o (iii) overlapping adjacent weld beads.
 • Together, these conditions produced cyclic reheating, reinforced thermal gradients, and
 repeated transitions in cooling behavior along the build direction.

Overall, these results demonstrate that thermal history, governed by both interlayer dwell time
and intra-layer deposition sequencing, exerts a dominant influence on microstructure and
mechanical response in WAAM-fabricated low-carbon steel. The emergence of a reproducible
periodic microstructural pattern underscores the critical importance of controlling dwell time, bead
overlap, and scan strategy when uniform properties are required. More broadly, this work shows
that intentional thermal management offers a powerful pathway for tailoring microstructural
gradients, reducing heterogeneity, and enabling location-specific property design in large-scale
WAAM steel components. These insights provide a valuable foundation for future optimization of
WAAM processing strategies aimed at enhancing structural reliability and performance.
