Feedback Motion Planning for Stochastic Nonlinear Systems with Signal Temporal Logic Specifications
Liqian Ma, Zishun Liu, Glen Chou, Yongxin Chen

Abstract
We study feedback motion planning for continuous-time stochastic nonlinear systems under signal temporal logic (STL) specifications. We propose a framework that synthesizes control policies for chance-constrained STL trajectory optimization problems, with the goal of ensuring that the closed-loop stochastic system satisfies a given STL formula with high probability (e.g., 99.99%). Our approach is based on a predicate erosion strategy that transforms the intractable stochastic problem into a deterministic STL trajectory optimization problem with tightened STL formula constraints. The amount of erosion is determined by a probabilistic reachable tube (PRT) that bounds the deviation between the stochastic trajectory and an associated nominal trajectory. To compute such bounds, we leverage contraction theory and feedback design, and develop several tracking controllers. This yields a complete feedback motion planning pipeline which can be implemented by numerical optimizations. We demonstrate the efficacy and versatility of the proposed framework through simulations on several robotic systems and through experiments on a real-world quadrupedal robot, and show that it is less conservative and achieves higher specification satisfaction probability than representative baselines.

Robotic systems operate under uncertainty and they must satisfy precisely stated behavioral requirements under safety- and mission-critical constraints.
To reason about such requirements rigorously, practitioners employ
formal specifications
that capture desired properties as statements over system trajectories.
While classical safe-set specifications only encode spatial constraints (the state must never enter an unsafe region)
[
38
]
, many real-world tasks are inherently
spatio–temporal
: they involve deadlines, sequencing, or persistence requirements (e.g., “visit region A before B and remain there for at least
\tau
seconds”).

Signal Temporal Logic
(STL) provides an intuitive way to specify a wide range of these high-level properties in continuous time and over real-valued signals
[
30
]
. Therefore, it is well-suited to applications in continuous-time dynamical systems, such as robotics
[
35
,
32
,
5
,
43
,
42
]
.

In this context, verification asks whether a given closed-loop system satisfies an STL formula
[
40
,
21
,
28
]
, whereas
control synthesis
aims to
design
control strategies that guarantee satisfaction of the STL specification. The latter is crucial for deploying dependable autonomy at scale and is the focus of this work. Multiple approaches to control synthesis under STL specifications have been developed in recent years.
One prominent line of research encodes satisfaction at sampled time instants by introducing binary variables, and then solves a mixed-integer convex program
[
39
,
41
]
. However, this type of encoding cannot handle nonlinear dynamics or nonlinear predicates.
Another line of work formulates control synthesis as a continuous optimization by encoding the STL specification via its robustness metric, replacing the nonsmooth
\min/\max
operators with differentiable surrogates, and solving the resulting problem using standard nonlinear programming solvers
[
13
,
22
,
32
,
14
]
, but the resulting nonconvex optimization problems can suffer from convergence to local minima.
Beyond sampled-time formulations, direct continuous-time synthesis methods have also been studied. Early approaches address restricted fragments of STL and typically exclude nested temporal structure (specifications where temporal operators are composed within one another)
[
23
,
24
]
, while more recent work extends control barrier function (CBF) based synthesis to nested STL formulas
[
57
]
by encoding satisfaction via backward reachability, a step that can become computationally demanding and hence challenging to scale with system dimension. Moreover,
[
19
]
leverages graphs of convex sets for continuous-time temporal-logic path planning, but does not enforce that the planned trajectories are feasible for the underlying nonlinear dynamics.

In real-world dynamical systems, unmodeled dynamics and external perturbations are unavoidable, so STL control synthesis methods commonly treat uncertainty as unknown-but-bounded disturbances and reason about the worst case. Representative approaches include robust model predictive control (MPC) that propagates disturbance in robustness scores through linear system evolution
[
41
]
, interval semantics using Hamilton-Jacobi reachability
[
50
]
, and learning-based methods using adversarial training
[
54
]
.

In many practical settings, disturbances are better modeled as unbounded stochastic noise (e.g., sensor or actuator noise) rather than unknown-but-bounded inputs. Under such uncertainty, methods that focus on worst-case bounds become overly conservative, as they must account for extreme, low-probability events. We therefore adopt a probabilistic formulation, seeking to design controls that ensure the STL specification holds with high probability (e.g.,
\geq 99.99\%
). While probabilistic approaches exist, they exhibit significant limitations when scaled to continuous-time or nonlinear systems. For instance,
[
10
,
55
]
translate chance constraints onto individual predicates, but are restricted to discrete-time linear systems. Other works
[
52
,
28
]
use probabilistic reachable sets to tighten predicates; however, this reduction relies on the union bound (Boole’s inequality), which breaks down in continuous time where STL satisfaction requires an intersection over uncountably many time instants. Recently,
[
56
]
addressed continuous-time STL by utilizing the unscented transform to approximate uncertainty propagation. Because this is an approximation, it lacks formal probabilistic guarantees and is fundamentally limited to a restricted fragment of STL with affine predicates. Consequently, efficiently solving motion planning problems for continuous-time stochastic systems under general STL specifications remains a significant open challenge.

In this paper, we address this gap by proposing a systematic feedback motion planning framework that maps the original stochastic problem to a deterministic STL trajectory optimization (STLTO) problem with tightened STL formulas. The key idea is to construct an nominal trajectory, design a feedback tracking controller around it, and use a
probabilistic reachable tube
(PRT) to bound the stochastic deviation from the nominal trajectory. This tube is then used to erode each predicate in the STL formula so that satisfaction of the tightened deterministic specification implies high-probability satisfaction of the original chance-constrained specification. Our contributions are summarized as follows.

•

We introduce a
continuous-time STL erosion strategy
that systematically transforms chance-constrained STL trajectory optimization problems into deterministic ones.

•

We develop a
complete pipeline for feedback control synthesis under probabilistic STL specifications
, enabling practitioners to design feedback controllers for stochastic systems with rigorous probabilistic guarantees.

•

We demonstrate the scalability and applicability of our framework through extended experiments on various robotic systems in both simulation and real world, and show that it is less conservative and achieves higher specification satisfaction probability than baselines.

Problem Formulation

In this section, we first present the dynamic model and the syntax and semantics of STL. Then, we formulate the stochastic STL trajectory optimization problem.

II-A

Dynamic Model

We consider the continuous-time stochastic system

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=f_{t}(X_{t},u_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+g_{t}(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}

is an

[
58

to guarantee the existence of a solution to (
1

is bounded for all

.

Assumption 1
.

For the stochastic system (
1

\sigma>0
such that
g_{t}(X)g_{t}(X)^{\mathsf{T}}\preceq\sigma^{2}I_{n}
for any

\dot{x}_{t}=f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t}),

. We use

to denote the state reached at time

II-B

STL Syntax and Semantics

We use STL
[
30
]
to specify the desired spatio-temporal properties of the system trajectory
\bm{x}
.
STL uses logical operators (negation
\neg
, conjunction
\wedge
, disjunction
\vee
) and temporal operators (
always

\square_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}
,
eventually

\lozenge_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}
,
until

\mathsf{U}_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}
) to recursively define specifications. The syntax of an STL formula is given as

\varphi:=\pi{}\mid{}\neg\varphi{}\mid{}\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2}{}\mid{}\varphi_{1}\vee\varphi_{2}{}\mid{}\\
\square_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}\varphi{}\mid{}\lozenge_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}\varphi\mid{}\varphi_{1}\mathsf{U}_{[t_{1},t_{2}]}\varphi_{2}{},

(3)

where
\pi:=(\mu_{\pi}(x)\geq 0)
is a predicate defined by a function
\mu_{\pi}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
\varphi,\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}
are STL formulas, and
0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}<\infty
define time intervals which the temporal operators consider. An STL formula
\varphi
is recursively constructed using this syntax.

The Boolean semantics of an STL formula
\varphi
are defined over a system trajectory
\bm{x}

[
30
]
, which we summarize in Definition
1
.

Definition 1

(Boolean semantics)
.

We write
(\bm{x},t)\models\varphi
to denote that
\bm{x}
satisfies
\varphi
at time
t
.
Let
I=[t_{1},t_{2}]\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
t+I:=\{\,t+\tau:\tau\in I\,\}
.
The semantics are given recursively by

\displaystyle(\bm{x},t)\models\pi

\displaystyle\ \Leftrightarrow\ \mu_{\pi}(\bm{x}(t))\geq 0,

\displaystyle(\bm{x},t)\models\neg\varphi

\displaystyle\ \Leftrightarrow\ \neg\big((\bm{x},t)\models\varphi\big),

\displaystyle(\bm{x},t)\models\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle\ \Leftrightarrow\ (\bm{x},t)\models\varphi_{1}\ \wedge\ (\bm{x},t)\models\varphi_{2},

\displaystyle(\bm{x},t)\models\varphi_{1}\vee\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle\ \Leftrightarrow\ (\bm{x},t)\models\varphi_{1}\ \vee\ (\bm{x},t)\models\varphi_{2},

\displaystyle(\bm{x},t)\models\square_{I}\varphi

\displaystyle\ \Leftrightarrow\ \forall\tau\in t+I,\ (\bm{x},\tau)\models\varphi,

\displaystyle(\bm{x},t)\models\lozenge_{I}\varphi

\displaystyle\ \Leftrightarrow\ \exists\tau\in t+I,\ (\bm{x},\tau)\models\varphi,

\displaystyle(\bm{x},t)\models\varphi_{1}\mathsf{U}_{I}\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle\ \Leftrightarrow\ \exists\tau\in t+I,\ (\bm{x},\tau)\models\varphi_{2}\ \wedge

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\forall\tau^{\prime}\in[t,\tau],(\bm{x},\tau^{\prime})\models\varphi_{1}.

STL admits a
robustness (quantitative)
semantics that assigns a real score to each formula and trajectory segment
[
9
]
.
Intuitively, a positive value of robustness indicates satisfaction, and a negative value indicates a violation, with the magnitude reflecting the margin of satisfaction/violation. The quantitative semantics are introduced in Definition
2
.

Definition 2

(Quantitative semantics)
.

The robustness of
\varphi
with respect to
\bm{x}
at time
t
is a real value
\rho_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)\in\mathbb{R}
defined recursively:

\displaystyle\rho_{\pi}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle:=\mu_{\pi}(\bm{x}(t)),

\displaystyle\rho_{\neg\varphi}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle:=-\,\rho_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t),

\displaystyle\rho_{\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2}}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle:=\min\{\rho_{\varphi_{1}}(\bm{x},t),\ \rho_{\varphi_{2}}(\bm{x},t)\},

\displaystyle\rho_{\varphi_{1}\vee\varphi_{2}}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle:=\max\{\rho_{\varphi_{1}}(\bm{x},t),\ \rho_{\varphi_{2}}(\bm{x},t)\},

\displaystyle\rho_{\square_{I}\varphi}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle:=\min_{\tau\in t+I}\ \rho_{\varphi}(\bm{x},\tau),

\displaystyle\rho_{\lozenge_{I}\varphi}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle:=\max_{\tau\in t+I}\ \rho_{\varphi}(\bm{x},\tau),

\displaystyle\rho_{\varphi_{1}\mathsf{U}_{I}\varphi_{2}}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle:=\max_{\tau\in t+I}\ \min\!\Big\{\rho_{\varphi_{2}}(\bm{x},\tau),\ \min_{\tau^{\prime}\in[t,\tau]}\rho_{\varphi_{1}}(\bm{x},\tau^{\prime})\Big\}.

Finally, Boolean satisfaction is recovered from the sign of robustness:

(\bm{x},t)\models\varphi\ \Leftrightarrow\ \rho_{\varphi}(\bm{x},t)\geq 0.

For the ease of analysis, we assume that all STL formulas are converted into a negation-free form, i.e., without explicit negation operators. Such a conversion is always possible by first converting the formula into Negation Normal Form
[
9
]
and, if necessary, introducing new predicates with reversed inequalities
[
3
,
41
, Proposition 2]
. We also assume that the horizon of the formula is bounded. Specifically, the horizon of an STL formula refers to the minimum time duration over which a trajectory must be defined to fully evaluate the formula’s satisfaction. This formula horizon must be shorter than the overall horizon of the motion planning problem.

II-C

Stochastic STL Motion Planning Problem

Given a dynamic system and a desired STL specification, the goal of motion planning is to derive a control input signal
\bm{u}
which can result in a trajectory
\bm{x}
satisfying the specification. This can be effectively formulated as a trajectory optimization problem, where the satisfaction of the STL specification is added as a constraint.

First consider the deterministic STLTO problem. Given a trajectory
\bm{x}
of system (
2
) with initial state
x_{0}
, we say
\bm{x}
satisfies a STL specification
\varphi
if
(\bm{x},0)\models\varphi
. The goal of deterministic STLTO is to derive a control input curve
\bm{u}
which can minimize some cost while ensuring the constraint
(\bm{x},0)\models\varphi
is satisfied
[
39
,
11
]
.

However, such a definition of specification satisfaction can be overly restrictive for a stochastic trajectory
\bm{X}
of system (
1
). Since the stochastic noise can go unbounded, the stochastic state
X_{t}
can be unbounded as well, which leads to inevitable violation of the STL specification. Therefore, we consider the probabilistic satisfaction of an STL specification in the stochastic trajectory optimization problem. Given a user-defined probability tolerance
\delta\in[0,1]
, a STL specification
\varphi
, we say the trajectory
\bm{X}
controlled by
\bm{u}
satisfies
\varphi
with probability
1-\delta
if

\mathbb{P}\left((\bm{X},0)\models\varphi\right)\geq 1-\delta,

(4)

where
\delta
is usually a small value. For example, if we choose
\delta=10^{-4}
, then the constraint requires that the resulting stochastic trajectory
\bm{X}
satisfies
\varphi
with probability at least
99.99\%
.

Given the stage cost function
\mathcal{L}_{t}(X_{t},u_{t})
, the terminal cost
\Phi_{T}(X_{T})
, and the initial state
X_{0}=x_{0}
, the stochastic trajectory optimization problem under STL specifications can be formulated as the following stochastic optimization problem with the chance constraint (
4
)

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{X,u}}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{L}_{t}(X_{t},u_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\Phi_{T}(X_{T})\right\}

(5a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}=f_{t}(X_{t},u_{t})\mathrm{d}t+g_{t}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(5b)

\displaystyle\bm{X}(0)=x_{0},

(5c)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left((\bm{X},0)\models\varphi\right)\geq 1-\delta,

(5d)

\displaystyle u_{t}\in\mathcal{U}.

(5e)

To the best of our knowledge, no existing method systematically solves trajectory optimization for
continuous-time
stochastic systems under
general
STL specifications with explicit high-probability guarantees. The difficulty comes from the need to enforce a trajectory-level chance constraint over uncountably many time instants while simultaneously handling the logical and temporal structure of STL specifications. This motivates the following problem.

Problem 1

(Chance-constrained STL trajectory optimization)
.

Given the stochastic STLTO in (
5
) with risk level
\delta\in(0,1)
and STL formula
\varphi
over horizon
[0,T]
, develop a scalable algorithm that computes a control
\bm{u}
while ensuring the chance constraint (
5d
) holds.

At a high level, our solution strategy is as follows. We first replace the original stochastic STL constraint by a deterministic one through
predicate erosion
: each predicate in the STL formula is tightened by a margin large enough to absorb stochastic tracking error. To compute this margin, we associate each stochastic trajectories with their nominal deterministic trajectory and bound their deviation by a PRT. We then design a feedback tracking controller so that the closed-loop system is contracting, which allows us to derive a tight PRT. The next three sections develop these ingredients in order: predicate erosion and deterministic reduction in Section
III
, contraction-based PRT construction in Section
IV
, and feedback controller design in Section
V
.

III
Stochastic Trajectory Optimization with STL Constraints

The stochastic optimization problem (
5
) is fundamentally challenging due to the presence of chance constraints. Even in the simpler case of safe-set constraints, where (
5d
) is replaced by
\mathbb{P}\left(X_{t}\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{safe}},\forall t\right)\geq 1-\delta
and there are no temporal requirements, ensuring continuous-time satisfaction remains challenging. Existing approaches for stochastic trajectory optimization under safe-set constraints typically seek to derive tractable
deterministic
surrogates for the expectations and probability constraints
[
33
,
34
,
53
,
26
]
. When STL specifications are introduced, this complexity is exacerbated due to the coupling of spatial and temporal requirements.

To effectively solve Problem
1
, we introduce
predicate erosion
, a systematic strategy that transforms the chance-constrained formulation (
5
) into a tractable, deterministic trajectory optimization problem.

III-A

Predicate Erosion Strategy

We interpret the deterministic system (
2
) as the nominal, noise-free counterpart to the stochastic dynamics (
1
). Given the same initial state and control curve
\bm{u}
, we refer to the resulting state trajectories of (
2
) and (
1
) as the
nominal trajectory

\bm{x}
and the
stochastic trajectory

\bm{X}
, respectively. We say
\bm{x}
and
\bm{X}
are associated trajectories. The intuition underlying our approach is that a stochastic trajectory
\bm{X}
will fluctuate around its deterministic counterpart
\bm{x}
with high probability.

Building upon this intuition, we extend the
predicate erosion
strategy
[
28
]
to the continuous-time domain to efficiently handle the stochastic STLTO problem (
5
). Consider a predicate function
\mu_{\pi}(\cdot)
defining a valid region via its superlevel set
\mathcal{C}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\mu_{\pi}(x)\geq 0\}
. We construct a smaller set
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}\subset\mathcal{C}
by systematically eroding the boundary of
\mathcal{C}
. The core insight is that if the nominal state
x_{t}
lies in the eroded set
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
, the corresponding stochastic state
X_{t}
is guaranteed to reside within the original set
\mathcal{C}
with high probability.

This erosion mechanism establishes a tractable, deterministic proxy for the original stochastic STL constraint. Rather than directly evaluating intractable chance constraints over the stochastic trajectory, we constrain the nominal trajectory to satisfy a tightened STL specification, wherein every constituent predicate is replaced by its eroded counterpart. Consequently, if the deterministic trajectory satisfies this eroded specification, the stochastic trajectory will satisfy the original STL formula with the desired high probability. As intuitively illustrated in Fig.
1
, this process translates to shrinking goal regions and expanding obstacle boundaries. By planning against this deterministic proxy, we systematically solve the stochastic STLTO problem while retaining rigorous probabilistic guarantees.

To formalize our method, we introduce the following definitions. Definition
3
states the stochastic deviation of the stochastic trajectory with respect to the deterministic trajectory.

Definition 3
.

Let
\bm{X}
and
\bm{x}
denote the associated stochastic and deterministic trajectories in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, respectively.
The stochastic fluctuation at time
t
is

e_{t}:=X_{t}-x_{t},

and the stochastic deviation is the weighted norm of the fluctuation with a weight (metric) matrix
M_{t}\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{n}
:

\|e_{t}\|:=\|X_{t}-x_{t}\|_{M_{t}}.

The fluctuation trajectory is
\bm{e}=\bm{X}-\bm{x}
.

The reason why we consider a weighted norm
\|\cdot\|_{M_{t}}
is that the standard Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|
may become conservative when the system is high-dimensional
[
51
]
. The choice of
M_{t}
will be discussed in Section
IV
. We use
probabilistic reachable tube (PRT)
to characterize the trajectory-level evolution of the stochastic fluctuation.

Definition 4
.

Given associated trajectories
\bm{X}
and
\bm{x}
, a finite horizon
[0,T]
, a probability level
\delta\in(0,1)
, a positive definite matrix
M_{t}\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{n}
and a radius curve
r_{\delta,t}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, define for each
t\in[0,T]
the closed ellipsoid

E_{t}:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ \|y\|_{M_{t}}\leq r_{\delta,t}\}.

The indexed family
\bm{E}:=\{E_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
is a PRT for
e_{t}=X_{t}-x_{t}
with probability
1-\delta
if

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{e}\in\bm{E}\right):=~\mathbb{P}\left(e_{t}\in E_{t},\ \forall t\in[0,T]\right)\\
=~&\mathbb{P}\left(\|X_{t}-x_{t}\|_{M_{t}}\leq r_{\delta,t},\ \forall t\in[0,T]\right)\geq 1-\delta,\end{split}

(6)

That is, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the entire fluctuation trajectory remains inside the tube
\bm{E}
over
[0,T]
.

Remark 1
.

The construction of a tight PRT will be discussed in Section
IV
. In general, due to underactuation and limited control authority, the system trajectories may not be sufficiently contractive to suppress stochastic deviations, so disturbances can accumulate over time. Consequently,
r_{\delta,t}
is typically a non-decreasing function of
t
, and
\bm{E}
is an expanding tube.

To derive the predicate-erosion strategy, we extend the predicates in the STL syntax (
3
) to
time-varying
predicates, since
E_{t}
(and thus
\mathcal{C}\ominus E_{t}
) depends explicitly on
t
.

Definition 5
.

Let
\mu_{\pi}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
be a time-varying predicate function.
The time-varying atomic predicate at time
t
is

\pi_{t}:\ (\mu_{\pi}(x,t)\geq 0).

The time-varying STL syntax is obtained from the standard STL syntax (
3
) by replacing atomic predicates with
\pi_{t}
.
Its Boolean semantics are defined accordingly by

(\bm{x},t)\models\pi_{t}\ \Leftrightarrow\ \mu_{\pi}(\bm{x}(t),t)\geq 0.

Using the PRT as Definition
4
, we can erode a predicate in the STL formula
\varphi
to get a tightened and time-varying predicate.

Proposition 1

(Predicate erosion)
.

Let
\pi
be a (time-invariant) predicate with superlevel set
\mathcal{C}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\ \mu_{\pi}(x)\geq 0\}
.
Given a PRT
\bm{E}=\{E_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
, define the time-varying tightened predicate
\tilde{\pi}_{t}
by

(\bm{x},t)\models\tilde{\pi}_{t}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ x_{t}\in\mathcal{C}\ominus E_{t}.

Then, for every
t\in[0,T]
and every
\bm{e}\in\bm{E}
,

(\bm{x},t)\models\tilde{\pi}_{t}\ \Longrightarrow\ (\bm{x}+\bm{e},t)\models\pi.

Proof.

Fix
t\in[0,T]
and
\bm{e}\in\bm{E}
, so
e_{t}\in E_{t}
.
If
(\bm{x},t)\models\tilde{\pi}_{t}
, then by definition
x_{t}\in\mathcal{C}\ominus E_{t}
, i.e.,
x_{t}\oplus E_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
.
Since
e_{t}\in E_{t}
, it follows that
x_{t}+e_{t}\in\mathcal{C}
, equivalently
(\bm{x}+\bm{e},t)\models\pi
.
∎

Remark 2
.

In practice, one can use the largest
E_{t}
to erode the predicate which results in a time-invariant
\tilde{\pi}
. This avoids the need to introduce time-varying predicates but may lead to slightly more conservative results (the eroded superlevel set becomes smaller). We use time-varying predicates in the theoretical results for completeness.

Proposition
1
extends
[
28
, Proposition 1]
to the continuous-time setting and to the time-varying predicates in Definition
5
.
The next theorem shows that a continuous-time probabilistic STL constraint can be reduced to a deterministic one by eroding every predicate in the STL formula using Proposition
1
.

Theorem 1

(STL formula erosion)
.

Let
\bm{X}
and
\bm{x}
be associated stochastic and deterministic trajectories, and let
\bm{E}
be a
1-\delta
PRT as in Definition
4
.
Consider an STL formula
\varphi
constructed from predicates
\pi^{1},\dots,\pi^{m}
with superlevel sets
\mathcal{C}^{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}^{m}
.
Construct the eroded formula
\tilde{\varphi}
by replacing each atomic predicate
\pi_{i}
with the time-varying predicate
\tilde{\pi}_{i,t}
whose superlevel set is
\mathcal{C}^{i}\ominus E_{t}
, leaving all logical and temporal operators unchanged.
Then

(\bm{x},0)\models\tilde{\varphi}\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathbb{P}\left((\bm{X},0)\models\varphi\right)\ \geq\ 1-\delta.

Proof.

By the PRT definition (
6
), it suffices to prove the following deterministically: for every fluctuation trajectory
\bm{e}\in\bm{E}
,

(\bm{x},0)\models\tilde{\varphi}\ \Longrightarrow\ (\bm{x}+\bm{e},0)\models\varphi.

For convenience, we prove the following
stronger
claim:

(\bm{x},t)\models\tilde{\varphi}\ \Longrightarrow(\bm{x}+\bm{e},t)\models\varphi,\ \forall t\in[0,T],\ \forall\bm{e}\in\bm{E}.

(7)

Since the STL formulas are recursively defined, we prove (
7
) by structural induction on
\varphi
.
(We assume
\varphi
is negation-free as stated in Section
II
.)

Base case (atomic predicates).

For any atomic
\pi_{i}
and its erosion
\tilde{\pi}_{i,t}
, Proposition
1
yields

(\bm{x},t)\models\tilde{\pi}_{i,t}\ \Rightarrow\ (\bm{x}+\bm{e},t)\models\pi_{i}\quad\text{for all }t\text{ and all }\bm{e}\in\bm{E}.

Induction step.
Assume (
7
) holds for
\varphi
and
\psi
and their eroded versions
\tilde{\varphi}
and
\tilde{\psi}
. We verify it for compositions:

1.

Logical operators.

•

Conjunction: If
(\bm{x},t)\models\widetilde{(\varphi\wedge\psi)}
then
(\bm{x},t)\models\tilde{\varphi}
and
(\bm{x},t)\models\tilde{\psi}
. By the induction hypothesis (IH),
(\bm{x}+\bm{e},t)\models\varphi
and
(\bm{x}+\bm{e},t)\models\psi
, hence
(\bm{x}+\bm{e},t)\models\varphi\wedge\psi
.

•

Disjunction: If
(\bm{x},t)\models\widetilde{(\varphi\vee\psi)}
then
(\bm{x},t)\models\tilde{\varphi}
or
(\bm{x},t)\models\tilde{\psi}
. The IH implies the corresponding satisfaction for
(\bm{x}+\bm{e},t)
, hence
(\bm{x}+\bm{e},t)\models\varphi\vee\psi
.

2.

Temporal operators.
Let
I=[t_{1},t_{2}]
with
0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq T
.

•

Always: If
(\bm{x},t)\models\widetilde{\square_{I}\varphi}
, then for all
\tau\in t+I
,
(\bm{x},\tau)\models\tilde{\varphi}
. By IH, for all
\tau\in t+I
and any
\bm{e}\in\bm{E}
,
(\bm{x}+\bm{e},\tau)\models\varphi
. Hence
(\bm{x}+\bm{e},t)\models\square_{I}\varphi
.

•

Eventually: If
(\bm{x},t)\models\widetilde{\lozenge_{I}\varphi}
, there exists
\tau\in t+I
such that
(\bm{x},\tau)\models\tilde{\varphi}
. By IH, for the same
\tau
,
(\bm{x}+\bm{e},\tau)\models\varphi
, hence
(\bm{x}+\bm{e},t)\models\lozenge_{I}\varphi
.

•

Until: If
(\bm{x},t)\models\widetilde{\varphi\mathsf{U}_{I}\psi}
, there exists
\tau\in t+I
such that
(\bm{x},\tau)\models\tilde{\psi}
and, for all
\tau^{\prime}\in[t,\tau]
,
(\bm{x},\tau^{\prime})\models\tilde{\varphi}
. By IH,
(\bm{x}+\bm{e},\tau)\models\psi
and
(\bm{x}+\bm{e},\tau^{\prime})\models\varphi
for all
\tau^{\prime}\in[t,\tau]
. Hence
(\bm{x}+\bm{e},t)\models\varphi\mathsf{U}_{I}\psi
.

By induction, (
7
) holds for all subformulas and thus for
\varphi
.
Finally, using
\mathbb{P}\left(\bm{e}\in\bm{E}\right)\geq 1-\delta
from Definition
4
, we obtain

(\bm{x},0)\models\tilde{\varphi}\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbb{P}\left((\bm{X},0)\models\varphi\right)\ \geq\ 1-\delta.

∎

At a high level, the STL erosion mechanism separates
randomness
from
logic
:
the PRT as Definition
4
bounds the fluctuation
e_{t}=X_{t}-x_{t}
in a time-varying tube
E_{t}
, while predicate erosion (Proposition
1
) shrinks each predicate set
\mathcal{C}_{i}
to its robust counterpart
\mathcal{C}_{i}\ominus E_{t}
so that satisfaction is preserved under any
e_{t}\in E_{t}
. By compositionality of STL semantics, this predicate-level robustness lifts to the full formula (Theorem
1
), yielding the guarantee

(\bm{x},0)\models\tilde{\varphi}\Rightarrow\mathbb{P}\left((\bm{X},0)\models\varphi\right)\geq 1-\delta
.

III-B

Deterministic Surrogate of Stochastic STLTO

The predicate erosion strategy indicates that the chance constraint in (
5
) can be enforced conservatively by requiring the associated
deterministic
trajectory
\bm{x}
to satisfy the eroded formula
\tilde{\varphi}
under the deterministic dynamics. This observation leads to a deterministic surrogate of the stochastic STL trajectory optimization problem, obtained by replacing the chance constraint with
(\bm{x},0)\models\tilde{\varphi}
:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x},\,\bm{u}}\quad

\displaystyle J_{d}(\bm{x},\bm{u})\;=\;\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{L}_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t\;+\;\Phi_{T}(x_{T})

(8a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{t}\;=\;f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t}),

(8b)

\displaystyle\bm{x}(0)=x_{0},

(8c)

\displaystyle(\bm{x},0)\models\tilde{\varphi},

(8d)

\displaystyle u_{t}\in\mathcal{U},\qquad\forall t\in[0,T].

(8e)

Unlike the intractable stochastic formulation (
5
), the surrogate problem (
8
) is deterministic and can be solved using standard trajectory optimization techniques
[
17
,
13
,
14
]
. Let
\{x_{t}^{*},u_{t}^{*}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
denote an optimal solution to (
8
). However, applying the feedforward control
u_{t}^{*}
to the stochastic system (
1
) in a purely open-loop manner allows stochastic deviations to accumulate. This results in an excessively large PRT, which requires overly aggressive predicate erosion and renders the deterministic surrogate (
8
) infeasible. To maintain a tight PRT and preserve feasibility, it is essential to employ a closed-loop feedback controller. Specifically, we augment the nominal control with a state-feedback tracking controller that restricts the deviation of
X_{t}
from its nominal counterpart
x_{t}^{*}
. The composite control input applied to the stochastic system is thus formulated as:

\textstyle u_{t}=K_{t}(X_{t};x_{t}^{*},u_{t}^{*})+u_{t}^{*},

(9)

where
K_{t}(\cdot;x_{t}^{*},u_{t}^{*}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a tracking controller satisfying
K_{t}(x_{t}^{*};x_{t}^{*},u_{t}^{*})=0
. The design of
K_{t}(\cdot;x_{t}^{*},u_{t}^{*})
is detailed in Section
V
.

Algorithmically, this establishes a two-stage synthesis framework. In the offline phase, we solve (
8
) to obtain the nominal trajectory and feedforward control
\{x_{t}^{*},u_{t}^{*}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
. During the online execution phase, the actual stochastic system (
1
) is driven by the composite policy (
9
). Crucially, as we formally establish in Theorem
2
,
X_{t}
and
x_{t}^{*}
remain valid associated trajectories under this closed-loop scheme. Consequently, the predicate erosion guarantees hold, ensuring that the online stochastic system satisfies the original STL specification with the desired high probability.

Theorem 2

(Deterministic surrogate of stochastic STLTO)
.

Consider the stochastic system (
1
) and its associated deterministic system (
2
) under Assumption
1
.
Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
and let

be a

4
.
Construct the eroded STL formula

as in Theorem

.
Let

be a feasible solution of (

Then any

satisfies the chance constraint in the stochastic problem (

(\bm{x^{*}},0)\models\tilde{\varphi}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbb{P}\left((\bm{X},0)\models\varphi\right)\geq 1-\delta.

Consequently, any feasible solution of (

) yields a control policy
\bm{u}
as (

) that is feasible for the stochastic STLTO (

).

Proof.

Define

. The solution

of (

controlled by

) can thus be expressed as:

\begin{split}\dot{x}^{*}_{t}&=f_{t}^{cl}(x_{t}^{*},u_{t}^{*};x_{t}^{*},u_{t}^{*})\\
\mathrm{d}X_{t}&=f_{t}^{cl}(X_{t},u_{t}^{*};x_{t}^{*},u_{t}^{*})\mathrm{d}t+g_{t}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}W_{t}\end{split}

(10)

Since

and the same

and

are associated trajectories with respect to the closed-loop transition function

,

).
∎

The feasibility of the predicate-erosion strategy depends on the tightness of the PRT radius profile

as in Definition

. If

IV
A Tight Bound on PRT

In this section, we develop a tight bound
r_{\delta,t}
on the PRT defined in Definition
4
.

IV-A

Contraction Theory

Under the composite control law (
9
), the feedback term
K(X_{t};x_{t}^{*})
suppresses the deviation between the stochastic state
X_{t}
and the nominal state
x_{t}^{*}
. This closed-loop regulation tightens the resulting PRT. More specifically, the tube radius
r_{\delta,t}
, which establishes the probabilistic bound on the stochastic deviations, is fundamentally governed by the contraction properties of the closed-loop dynamics. To formalize and analyze this behavior for nonlinear systems, we leverage
contraction theory
.

Definition 6

(Contracting System)
.

A deterministic system formulated as (
2
) is said to be
c_{t}
-contracting if for any two trajectories
x_{t}
,
y_{t}
of (
2
),
\exists c_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
and
M_{t}\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{n}
such that:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|x_{t}-y_{t}\|_{M_{t}}^{2}\leq 2c_{t}\|x_{t}-y_{t}\|_{M_{t}}^{2}

(11)

holds at time
t\in[0,T]
. Here,
c_{t}
is called the contraction rate and
M_{t}
is the called contraction metric. In particular, the system is said to be strongly contracting if
c_{t}<0
holds for all
t
.

Remark 3
.

While the most general, geometric formulation of contraction theory is defined on Riemannian manifolds
[
48
]
, we adopt a different formulation in Definition
6
that avoids involving Riemannian geometry. This setting is fully sufficient for the scope of this paper and avoids unnecessary complexity. For an extended theoretical analysis based on Riemannian manifolds, we refer the reader to
[
27
]
.

The contracting systems have many intriguing properties. One property is that a contracting system with arbitrary contraction rates
c_{t}
has a counterpart with contraction rate exactly equal to
0
. This property allows analysis on the special case
c_{t}\equiv 0
to be smoothly generalized to general cases, and will be useful in Section
IV
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
x_{t}
be a deterministic trajectory of (
2
). Suppose that it is contracting with the rate
c_{t}
and the metric
M_{t}
as defined in Definition
6
. Then for the trajectory
y_{t}=e^{\int_{0}^{t}c_{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau}x_{t}
, its dynamics is contracting with the rate
c_{t}^{y}\equiv 0
and the metric
M_{t}^{y}=M_{t}
.

Proof.

See
[
15
]
.
∎

For the closed-loop system (
10
) under the control policy (
9
), a contraction rate of
c_{t}\geq 0
implies that the stochastic trajectory
X_{t}
may locally diverge from the nominal trajectory
x_{t}^{*}
. Such divergence fundamentally undermines the objective of the tracking controller. Consequently, we require that the feedback policy
K(\cdot;x_{t}^{*})
is appropriately designed to render the closed-loop dynamics
f_{cl}

strongly contracting
.

Assumption 2
.

The closed-loop system (
10
) is strongly contracting with
c_{t}<0
under the control policy (
9
).

We introduce the feedback controller design in Section
V
to ensure that Assumption
2
is satisfied.

Existing works based on the Incremental Stability Analysis
[
37
,
6
,
49
]
have shown the strong relationship between the bound on the PRT and the contraction condition of the system. However, the bounds established in these existing works are overly conservative when the probability level
1-\delta
is very high (e.g., 99.99%), and are not on the trajectory level, thus cannot be utilized in the STL erosion strategy. Next, we develop a trajectory-level bound on the PRT that is tight even when
\delta
is very small.

IV-B

Tight Bound on Probabilistic Reachable Tube

Based on the contraction condition, we use a martingale-based method to derive a tight bound
r_{\delta,t}
on the PRT in Definition
4
.

The family of martingales can be treated as stochastic analogs of barrier functions
[
46
]
, and is a common approach to bound the probability of a stochastic trajectory staying in a region
[
4
]
.
Based on the
c
-martingale
[
46
]
widely used in robotics, we introduce a novel generalization dubbed Affine martingale (AM).

Definition 7

(Affine Martingale)
.

For a continuous stochastic process
Y_{t},~t\in[0,T]
, a nonnegative differentiable function
B(Y,t):\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is said to be an affine martingale (AM) of
Y_{t}
if there exist
a_{t}\in\mathbb{R},b_{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that for all
t\leq T
and as
\mathrm{d}t\to 0
:
\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(B(Y_{t+\mathrm{d}t},t+\mathrm{d}t)|Y_{t}\right)-B(Y_{t},t)}{\mathrm{d}t}\leq a_{t}B(Y_{t},t)+b_{t}.

When
a_{t}\equiv 0
, the AM reduces to the classical
c
-martingale. This type of martingale first appeared in
[
20
, Chapter 3]
, and Definition
7
slightly generalizes it to time-varying coefficients. Similar with existing martingale-based methods, one can utilize supermartingale theory to bound the probability of
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
staying in certain sets. This is formalized in the following lemma and its proof can be found in
[
25
, Lemma 4.1]
.

Lemma 2
.

Consider a continuous-time stochastic trajectory
Y_{t},~t\in[0,T]
. Let
B(Y,t)
be an AM of
Y_{t}
with coefficients
a_{t},b_{t}\geq 0
.
Define
\widetilde{B}(Y_{t},t):=B(Y_{t},t)\xi_{t}+\int_{t}^{T}b_{\tau}\xi_{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau
,
where
\xi_{t}:=e^{\int_{t}^{T}a_{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau}
. Then given any
\overline{B}>0
and the set
\mathcal{Y}_{t}:=\{Y_{t}:~\widetilde{B}(Y_{t},t)\leq\overline{B}\}
, it holds that
\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{t}\in\mathcal{Y}_{t},\forall t\leq T\right)\geq 1-\frac{B(v_{0},0)\xi_{0}+\int_{0}^{T}b_{\tau}\psi_{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau}{\overline{B}}.

One may wonder how to construct an AM that can probabilistically bound the fluctuation of the stochastic trajectory. The answer lies in a novel function named Averaged Moment Generating Function (AMGF), which was initially proposed in
[
1
]
and fully exploited in
[
15
,
25
]
. The definition of AMGF is as follows:

Definition 8
.

Given
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the averaged moment generating function (AMGF) is defined as

\textstyle\mathbb{E}_{X}\Phi(X)=\mathbb{E}_{X}\mathbb{E}_{\ell\in\mathcal{S}^{n-1}}e^{\lambda\left<\ell,X\right>},

(12)

where
\mathcal{S}^{n-1}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|x\|=1\}
is the unit sphere in the
\mathbb{R}^{n}
space, and
\Phi(X)=\mathbb{E}_{\ell\in\mathcal{S}^{n-1}}e^{\lambda\left<\ell,X\right>}
is defined as the kernel of the AMGF. Moreover, given
M\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}
, the weighted version of
\Phi(X)
is defined as
\Phi_{M}(X)=\Phi(M^{1/2}X)
.

The kernel of weighted AMGF has many intriguing properties. The following lemma lists some of them that are useful in this paper. Its proof directly follows
[
25
]
.

Lemma 3
.

Consider the function
\Phi_{M}(X)
in Definition
8
, where
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
M\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}
, then it holds that:

1.

The value of
\Phi_{M}(X)
only depends on
\|X\|_{M}
.

2.

\Phi_{M_{1}}(X_{1})\leq\Phi_{M_{2}}(X_{2})
if
\|X_{1}\|_{M_{1}}\leq\|X_{2}\|_{M_{2}}
.

3.

Given any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
\Phi_{M}(X)\geq(1-\varepsilon^{2})^{\frac{n}{2}}e^{\varepsilon\|\lambda X\|_{M}}.

Equipped with AMGF, we are able to construct AM for nonlinear stochastic systems, and derive a tight bound for the PRT. The bound is demonstrated in the following lemma.

Theorem 3
.

Consider a trajectory
X_{t}
of the closed-loop stochastic system (
10
) with the control policy (
9
),
and its associated nominal trajectory
\{x_{t}^{*},u_{t}^{*}\}_{t\in[0,T]}

and

hold for the closed-loop system (

Given the terminal time

,
\delta\in(0,1)
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\Delta t>0
, define
\overline{\sigma}_{t}=\sqrt{\|M_{t}\|}\sigma
where
\sigma
is as Assumption
1
,
k=\lceil\frac{t}{\Delta t}\rceil
,
\psi_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}c_{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau

is the contraction rate introduced in Definition

,
\Psi_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\overline{\sigma}_{\tau}^{2}e^{-2\psi_{\tau}}\mathrm{d}\tau

,
\varepsilon_{1}=\frac{\log(\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon^{2}})}{\varepsilon^{2}},~\varepsilon_{2}=\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}
, and:

r_{\delta,t}=(\sqrt{e^{2\psi_{t}}\Psi_{t}}+\sqrt{\Psi_{t}^{\Delta t}})\sqrt{\varepsilon_{1}n+\varepsilon_{2}\log\frac{2T}{\delta\Delta t}}.

(13)

Then it holds that

\mathbb{P}\left(\|X_{t}-x_{t}\|_{M_{t}}\leq r_{\delta,t},\forall t\leq T\right)\geq 1-\delta.

(14)

Proof.

We start with the special case where
c_{t}=0
. For associated trajectories
X_{t}
and
x_{t}

e_{t}=X_{t}-x_{t}
, define
\hat{e}_{t}=M_{t}^{1/2}e_{t}

\beta_{t}=f_{cl}(X_{t},u_{t}^{*},t;x_{t}^{*})-f_{cl}(x_{t}^{*},u_{t}^{*},t;x_{t}^{*})

and
\mathcal{A}(V_{t})=\frac{\mathbb{E}(V_{t+\mathrm{d}t}|V_{t})-V_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}
. Obviously,
\mathrm{d}e_{t}=\beta_{t}\mathrm{d}t+g_{t}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}W_{t}
. Then, by Ito’s Lemma, the evolution of

can be extracted as

\begin{split}\textstyle&\mathcal{A}(V_{t})=Z_{1}+Z_{2},~\text{where:}\\
&Z_{1}=\left<\frac{\partial V_{t}}{\partial\hat{S}_{t}},\frac{\mathrm{d}M_{t}^{1/2}}{\mathrm{d}t}e_{t}+M^{1/2}_{t}\beta_{t}\right>\\
&Z_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left<\nabla^{2}V_{t},g_{t}(X_{t})g_{t}(X_{t})^{\mathsf{T}}\right>.\end{split}

(15)

Z_{1}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\Phi_{M_{\tau}}(\bar{x}_{\tau}-x_{\tau})|_{\tau=t}

\Phi_{M_{\tau}}(\bar{x}_{\tau}-x_{\tau})

\pm
) as

. Therefore,

.

\begin{split}Z_{2}=&\tfrac{1}{2}\left<\nabla^{2}V_{t},g_{t}(X_{t})g_{t}(X_{t})^{\mathsf{T}}\right>\\

\leq&\tfrac{\lambda^{2}\|M_{t}\|\sigma^{2}}{2}V_{t}\leq\tfrac{\lambda^{2}\overline{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}{2}V_{t}.\end{split}

Combining the bounds on

and

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}(V_{t})\leq\tfrac{\lambda^{2}\overline{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}{2}V_{t},\end{split}

(16)

which implies that
V_{t}=\Phi_{M_{t}}(X_{t}-x_{t})
is an affine martingale with

and
b_{t}\equiv 0
. Therefore, for every
\overline{r}>0
and any

In this section, we focus on the design of feedback tracking controllers for the nominal trajectory generated by (

Let

be a nominal state-control trajectory pair solving the deterministic surrogate problem (

u_{t}=u_{t}^{*}+K_{t}(X_{t};x_{t}^{*},u_{t}^{*}),

(27)

where

V-A

Trajectory Tracking via Time-Varying LQR

We first consider a Time-Varying Linear Quadratic Regulator (TVLQR) constructed along the nominal trajectory
(\bm{x}^{*},\bm{u}^{*})
. Linearizing the deterministic dynamics (
2
) along the nominal plan gives the time-varying Jacobians

A_{t}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\bigg|_{x_{t}^{*},u_{t}^{*},t},\qquad B_{t}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\bigg|_{x_{t}^{*},u_{t}^{*},t}.

Letting
\tilde{x}_{t}=x_{t}-x_{t}^{*}
and
\tilde{u}_{t}=u_{t}-u_{t}^{*}
denote the tracking error and control deviation, respectively, the local error dynamics are approximated by

\dot{\tilde{x}}_{t}=A_{t}\tilde{x}_{t}+B_{t}\tilde{u}_{t}.

Given positive semidefinite state penalty
Q_{t}\succeq 0
, positive definite input penalty
R_{t}\succ 0
, and terminal penalty
Q_{f}\succ 0
, the TVLQR gain is obtained from the continuous-time differential Riccati equation

-\dot{S}_{t}=S_{t}A_{t}+A_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}S_{t}-S_{t}B_{t}R_{t}^{-1}B_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}S_{t}+Q_{t},\quad S_{T}=Q_{f}.

(28)

The resulting feedback law is

\tilde{u}_{t}=-K_{t}\tilde{x}_{t},\quad K_{t}=R_{t}^{-1}B_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}S_{t},

or equivalently,

u_{t}=u_{t}^{*}-K_{t}(X_{t}-x_{t}^{*}).

The closed-loop linearized dynamics are

\dot{\tilde{x}}_{t}=A_{\mathrm{cl},t}\tilde{x}_{t},\qquad A_{\mathrm{cl},t}=A_{t}-B_{t}K_{t}.

We use the Riccati solution itself as the contraction metric candidate, i.e.,
M_{t}:=S_{t}.

To characterize the induced contraction rate, consider the quadratic energy

V_{t}=\tilde{x}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}S_{t}\tilde{x}_{t}.

(29)

Combining the closed-loop dynamics with (
28
) yields

\begin{split}\dot{V}_{t}&=\tilde{x}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\big(\dot{S}_{t}+S_{t}A_{\mathrm{cl},t}+A_{\mathrm{cl},t}^{\mathsf{T}}S_{t}\big)\tilde{x}_{t}\\
&=-\tilde{x}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\big(Q_{t}+K_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}K_{t}\big)\tilde{x}_{t}.\end{split}

(30)

To extract a contraction rate, we compare
\dot{V}_{t}
with
V_{t}
. A contraction rate
c_{t}
is valid if

\dot{V}_{t}\leq 2c_{t}V_{t}

(31)

for all tracking errors
\tilde{x}_{t}
. Equivalently,

-\tilde{x}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\big(Q_{t}+K_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}K_{t}\big)\tilde{x}_{t}\leq 2c_{t}\,\tilde{x}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}S_{t}\tilde{x}_{t}.

(32)

Let
z=S_{t}^{1/2}\tilde{x}_{t}
, then for any
z_{t}
the following inequality should hold

\frac{z_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}S_{t}^{-1/2}\big(-Q_{t}-K_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}K_{t}\big)S_{t}^{-1/2}z_{t}}{z_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}z_{t}}\leq 2c_{t}

(33)

Hence the local contraction rate can be taken as

c_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\max}\!\Big(S_{t}^{-1/2}\big(-Q_{t}-K_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}R_{t}K_{t}\big)S_{t}^{-1/2}\Big),

(34)

which is non-positive when
Q_{t}\succeq 0
and
R_{t}\succ 0
. Since
S_{t}
is continuous and positive definite on the compact interval
[0,T]
, there exist global matrices
M_{1},M_{2}\succ 0
such that

M_{1}\preceq S_{t}\preceq M_{2},\qquad\forall t\in[0,T].

(35)

These bounds can then be used in Theorem
3
. The TVLQR construction is straightforward and computationally efficient, but it can be conservative due to the implicit metric induced by the Riccati solution.

V-B

Time-Varying CCM Tracking Controller

To reduce conservativeness beyond TVLQR tracking, we also consider a time-varying control contraction metric (TVCCM) construction.
Linearizing along the nominal trajectory
(x_{t}^{*},u_{t}^{*})
gives the differential dynamics

\delta\dot{x}=A_{t}\delta x+B_{t}\delta u,

(36)

where

A_{t}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\bigg|_{x_{t}^{*},u_{t}^{*},t},\qquad B_{t}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\bigg|_{x_{t}^{*},u_{t}^{*},t}.

The goal of TVCCM synthesis is to construct a differentiable dual metric
W_{t}\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{n}
and a differential feedback matrix
Y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
such that the closed-loop differential dynamics are contracting with rate
c<0
. Defining

M_{t}=W_{t}^{-1},\qquad K_{t}=Y_{t}W_{t}^{-1},

the continuous-time dual contraction condition
[
44
]
is

-\dot{W}_{t}+A_{t}W_{t}+W_{t}A_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}+B_{t}Y_{t}+Y_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}B_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}\preceq-2cW_{t}.

(37)

To improve the tightness of the resulting probabilistic tube, we further seek metrics that are well-conditioned and small in the task-relevant state projection. If the STL predicates are defined mainly in the position coordinates, then these coordinates are the most relevant for erosion (e.g., if the predicates consider goal regions or obstacle avoidance). We therefore introduce a common upper bound matrix
\bar{W}\succ 0
and scalar
\bar{\beta}>0
such that

I\preceq W_{t}\preceq\bar{W},\qquad\bar{W}\preceq\bar{\beta}I,\qquad\forall t\in[0,T],

(38)

and penalize
\bar{\beta}
together with the projected metric size

\mathrm{tr}(P\bar{W}P^{\mathsf{T}}),

where
P
selects the state coordinates appearing most prominently in the STL predicates, such as position states.

Once
(W_{t},Y_{t})
is synthesized, the tracking controller is implemented as

u_{t}=u_{t}^{*}+K_{t}(X_{t}-x_{t}^{*}),\qquad K_{t}=Y_{t}W_{t}^{-1}.

The induced contraction metric is then
M_{t}=W_{t}^{-1}
,
which can be used directly in the PRT bound of Section
IV
. Compared with TVLQR, TVCCM offers more freedom in shaping the metric and the contraction rate, and therefore can lead to tighter PRTs.

V-C

Coupling Between Nominal Planning and Feedback Design

As the readers might have noticed, there is an intrinsic coupling between nominal trajectory optimization and feedback design. The tightened STL constraint in (
8

r_{\delta,t}
, since this radius determines the amount of predicate erosion. However,
r_{\delta,t}
is itself determined by the contraction rate and contraction metric of the tracking controller through Theorem
3

For TVLQR, a natural practical strategy is to alternate between these two steps. One first selects an initial erosion profile, solves (
8
) to obtain a nominal trajectory, synthesizes the TVLQR tracker along that trajectory, computes the resulting bound
r_{\delta,t}
, and then resolves the nominal planning problem with the updated erosion. This process is repeated until the bound used in planning dominates the bound certified by the resulting controller, i.e., the final planning erosion must be larger than the controller-induced tube.

If TVCCM is synthesized only along a given nominal trajectory, the same alternating strategy applies. However, TVCCM also admits a less coupled alternative. Inspired by
[
44
]
, one may prescribe a constant target contraction rate
c<0
and synthesize a time-varying dual metric and differential controller together with the uniform metric bounds (
38
).
Assume that the continuous-time TVCCM condition (
37
) is feasible with this prescribed rate
c
. Since
M_{t}=W_{t}^{-1}
, the metric bounds imply

\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}I\preceq M_{t}\preceq I,\qquad\|M_{t}\|\leq 1,\qquad\forall t\in[0,T].

Therefore,
\overline{\sigma}_{t}
in Theorem
3
satisfies

\overline{\sigma}_{t}=\sqrt{\|M_{t}\|}\sigma\leq\sigma,

and the constant contraction rate specialization of Theorem
3
yields the metric-space tube bound

r_{\delta,t}^{M}\leq\frac{\sigma\!\left(\sqrt{1-e^{2ct}}+\sqrt{e^{-2c\Delta t}-1}\right)}{\sqrt{-2c}}\sqrt{\varepsilon_{1}n+\varepsilon_{2}\log\frac{2T}{\delta\Delta t}}.

(39)

Consequently, the largest metric radius over the full horizon is

\bar{r}_{\delta}^{M}=\frac{\sigma\!\left(\sqrt{1-e^{2cT}}+\sqrt{e^{-2c\Delta t}-1}\right)}{\sqrt{-2c}}\sqrt{\varepsilon_{1}n+\varepsilon_{2}\log\frac{2T}{\delta\Delta t}}.

(40)

This bound is independent of the particular nominal trajectory and can therefore be computed before solving the nominal planning problem, provided the subsequent TVCCM synthesis is feasible with the prescribed
(c,\bar{\beta})

M_{t}\succeq\bar{\beta}^{-1}I
, any metric tube

implies

\frac{1}{\bar{\beta}}\|e_{t}\|^{2}\leq\|e_{t}\|_{M_{t}}^{2},

and hence the Euclidean tube bound

\|e_{t}\|\leq\sqrt{\bar{\beta}}\,\|e_{t}\|_{M_{t}}\leq\sqrt{\bar{\beta}}\,r_{\delta,t}^{M}.

(41)

This bound can be directly used to apply predicate erosion. The price paid for this decoupling is additional conservatism relative to trajectory-dependent TVCCM synthesis.

VI
Numerical Optimization

We defined the stochastic STL trajectory optimization problem, introduced predicate erosion, derived the contraction-based PRT bound, and formulated feedback tracking controllers. The goal of this section is to convert these ingredients into
computable numerical algorithms
. To do so, we introduce support times, discretize the nominal trajectory optimization problem, and then describe how the tracking controller constructions are implemented numerically on the support grid.

VI-A

Support-Time Discretization

The deterministic surrogate problem (
8
) is infinite-dimensional. We therefore choose support times

0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{N}=T,\qquad h_{k}:=t_{k+1}-t_{k},

and represent the continuous state and control trajectories by the discrete variables

x_{k}:=x(t_{k}),\quad u_{k}:=u(t_{k}),\quad k=0,\ldots,N.

The nominal plan is thus encoded by the support-point sequences

\bm{x}_{0:N}:=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{N}),\quad\bm{u}_{0:N-1}:=(u_{0},\ldots,u_{N-1}).

Any consistent transcription of the dynamics can then be used. For instance, one may employ direct shooting, trapezoidal collocation, or Hermite-Simpson collocation
[
17
]
. We keep the presentation agnostic to the specific scheme and denote the resulting dynamics map by

x_{k+1}=F_{k}(x_{k},u_{k}),\qquad k=0,\ldots,N-1,

where
F_{k}
is induced by the chosen integration or collocation method.

The STL formulas are also evaluated over the support times. More precisely, the STL formulas are interpreted over the support states
\bm{x}_{0:N}
or
\bm{X}_{0:N}
. Each atomic predicate is evaluated at the support states, and the temporal operators are enforced over the corresponding support-time indices lying in the specified time intervals. Since bounded-horizon STL depends only on finitely many time instants once a support grid is fixed, this yields a finite-dimensional constraint system suitable for numerical optimization.

After predicate erosion, the remaining dynamics constraint is deterministic, so the continuous-time problem (
8
) can be handled by standard trajectory-optimization transcription techniques. For standard transcriptions such as Runge–Kutta and collocation methods, the resulting finite-dimensional nonlinear program approximates the original continuous-time optimal control problem, and under suitable regularity assumptions, local solutions of the discretized problem converge to local solutions of the continuous-time problem as the mesh is refined
[
8
,
16
]
. In practice, discretization error can be further controlled by mesh-refinement procedures based on residual estimates
[
36
,
17
]
.

VI-B

Trajectory Optimization under STL Specifications

There are several ways to turn the support-time STL constraints into a numerical optimization problem. Mixed-integer formulations introduce binary variables and are exact for suitable classes of systems and predicates
[
3
,
39
]
, but they become difficult to scale to nonlinear systems and long horizons. Another option is to replace the max/min operations in the robustness semantics with smooth approximations.
Along this line,
[
13
]
replaces the nonsmooth
\min
and
\max
operators in STL robustness by differentiable surrogates, which is suitable for gradient-based optimization but generally introduce approximation error. In this work, we instead adopt the exact smooth reformulation proposed in
[
14
]
. At a high level, this method augments the planning problem with auxiliary variables so that the
\min
and
\max
can be represented by additional smooth nonlinear constraints without changing the feasible set of the support-time STL problem. This choice lets us use standard nonlinear programming solvers while retaining exactness.

Using this reformulation, the support-time nominal trajectory optimization problem can be written as

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x}_{0:N},\,\bm{u}_{0:N-1},\,z}\quad\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}L_{t_{k}}(x_{k},u_{k})+\Phi_{T}(x_{N})

(42a)

s.t.

\displaystyle x_{0}=x_{0}^{\mathrm{given}},

(42b)

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=F_{k}(x_{k},u_{k}),\qquad k=0,\ldots,N-1,

(42c)

\displaystyle u_{k}\in\mathcal{U},\qquad k=0,\ldots,N-1,

(42d)

\displaystyle(\bm{x}_{0:N},z)\ \text{satisfy smooth encoding of }\tilde{\varphi}.

(42e)

Here
z
collects the auxiliary variables introduced by the smooth STL transcription. By construction, (
42e
) is equivalent to enforcing the eroded STL specification on the support trajectory. The resulting finite-dimensional problem is a smooth nonlinear program and can be solved by off-the-shelf NLP solvers, such as SNOPT
[
12
]
.

along the nominal support trajectory. We then integrate the continuous-time Riccati equation (

) backward in time numerically from
t=T
to
t=0
to obtain support-time matrices

S_{k}\approx S(t_{k}),\qquad k=0,\ldots,N.

The feedback gains at the support times are computed as

K_{k}=R_{k}^{-1}B_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}S_{k}.

During rollout, these gains can be applied through zero-order hold or interpolation between consecutive support times. The sampled Riccati matrices also provide the support-time metric values

M_{k}:=S_{k},

from which numerical lower and upper metric bounds can be extracted for use in the PRT computation.

VI-C
2
TVCCM

For TVCCM, we discretize the continuous-time synthesis problem (
37
) on the support-time grid. Let

A_{k}:=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\bigg|_{x_{k}^{*},u_{k}^{*},t_{k}},\qquad B_{k}:=\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\bigg|_{x_{k}^{*},u_{k}^{*},t_{k}}.

Approximating the metric derivative in (
37
) by a finite difference,

\dot{W}_{t_{k}}\approx\frac{W_{k+1}-W_{k}}{h_{k}},

we obtain the sampled contraction constraints

-\frac{W_{k+1}-W_{k}}{h_{k}}+A_{k}W_{k}+W_{k}A_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}+B_{k}Y_{k}+Y_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}B_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\preceq-2cW_{k},

(43)

for
k=0,\ldots,N-1
. To control the metric size and conditioning, we also impose

I\preceq W_{k}\preceq\bar{W},\qquad\bar{W}\preceq\bar{\beta}I,\qquad k=0,\ldots,N.

This leads to a semidefinite program of the form

\displaystyle\min_{\{W_{k},Y_{k}\},\,\bar{W},\,\bar{\beta}}\quad

\displaystyle\bar{\beta}+w\,\mathrm{tr}(P\bar{W}P^{\mathsf{T}})

(44a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:sampled_tvccm_lmi}\text{ holds for }k=0,\ldots,N-1,

(44b)

\displaystyle I\preceq W_{k}\preceq\bar{W},\qquad k=0,\ldots,N,

(44c)

\displaystyle\bar{W}\preceq\bar{\beta}I,

(44d)

where
w>0
is a weight to adjust the priority of the two cost terms. We want to control both of the two cost terms because the radius of the PRT projected on the task-relevant coordinates depends on both the lower bound and the upper bound of the metric. The semidefinite program can be solved by any off-the-shelf solver, such as MOSEK
[
2
]
.

After solving (
44
), we recover the metrics and feedback gains via

M_{k}=W_{k}^{-1},\qquad K_{k}=Y_{k}W_{k}^{-1}.

The gains are then applied between support times by zero-order hold or interpolation.

IX
Conclusion

We present a feedback motion planning framework for continuous-time stochastic nonlinear systems under STL specifications. The central idea is to convert the original chance-constrained STL trajectory optimization problem into a deterministic surrogate by eroding the STL predicates using a PRT around a nominal trajectory. To make this reduction practical, we developed contraction-based PRT bounds together with feedback tracking controllers, and showed how these ingredients can be implemented with numerical optimization.
The resulting framework provides a systematic way to combine high-level temporal-logic planning with feedback control and probabilistic guarantees. The experiments demonstrate that the method scales across multiple simulation benchmark systems, is less conservative and achieves higher specification satisfaction probability than baselines, and transfers to a real quadrupedal platform. Future work will investigate tighter integration between planning, feedback synthesis, and uncertainty quantification.

-A

Details on Chance-Constraint Handling in Baselines

We consider two representative stochastic STL baselines on a canonical double-integrator reach-avoid task. Although our method is formulated for continuous-time stochastic systems and provides a continuous-time trajectory-level guarantee, both baselines considered here are discrete-time methods. Therefore, for this comparison only, we discretize the double-integrator SDE and enforce the STL formula on the resulting support grid. This yields a simpler problem than the continuous-time chance-constrained STL problem addressed by our method. The purpose of this subsection is to make explicit how each baseline converts the STL chance constraint into deterministic optimization constraints.

0&0&1&0\\

Q_{d}=0.02^{2}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta t+\frac{\Delta t^{3}}{3}&0&\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2}&0\\
0&\Delta t+\frac{\Delta t^{3}}{3}&0&\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2}\\
\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2}&0&\Delta t&0\\
0&\frac{\Delta t^{2}}{2}&0&\Delta t\end{bmatrix}.

Let

P=\begin{bmatrix}I_{2}&0_{2\times 2}\end{bmatrix}

be the position projection, so that
p_{k}=Px_{k}
. Let
p_{\mathrm{goal}}
and
R_{\mathrm{goal}}
denote the center and radius of the goal disk, and let
p_{\mathrm{obs}}
and
R_{\mathrm{obs}}
denote the center and radius of the circular obstacle. We define the reach-avoid predicates

\displaystyle\pi_{\text{goal}}(k)

\displaystyle:=\left(\|PX_{k}-p_{\mathrm{goal}}\|_{2}\leq R_{\mathrm{goal}}\right),

\displaystyle\pi_{\text{obs}}(k)

\displaystyle:=\left(\|PX_{k}-p_{\mathrm{obs}}\|_{2}\geq R_{\mathrm{obs}}\right),

where
\pi_{\text{goal}}
encodes reaching the goal region and
\pi_{\text{obs}}
encodes avoiding the obstacle. The discrete-time reach-avoid STL formula is

\varphi_{\mathrm{RA}}:=\lozenge_{[0,N]}\pi_{\text{goal}}\;\wedge\;\square_{[0,N]}\pi_{\text{obs}}.

(54)

The chance constraint is therefore

\mathbb{P}\left((X_{0:N},0)\models\varphi_{\mathrm{RA}}\right)\geq 1-\delta,

(55)

where
\delta\in(0,1)
is the desired risk level.

1
STL-structure based chance decomposition (STLCD)

The first baseline, which we denote STL-structure based chance decomposition (STLCD), follows the chance-constraint decomposition used in
[
10
]
. The original method is developed for affine predicates so that each atomic predicate chance constraint can be converted into a linear deterministic constraint. Since our experiments use circular predicates and solve the resulting problems with nonlinear programming, we keep the same STL-level risk decomposition but convert the circular predicate chance constraints using Gaussian confidence balls.

For a fixed open-loop input sequence, the predicted state distribution satisfies

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{0}

\displaystyle=x_{0},

\displaystyle\Sigma_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=A\bar{x}_{k}+Bu_{k},

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k+1}

\displaystyle=A\Sigma_{k}A^{\mathsf{T}}+Q_{d}.

(56)

Thus
Y_{k}:=PX_{k}
is Gaussian with mean
\bar{p}_{k}=P\bar{x}_{k}
and covariance
S_{k}=P\Sigma_{k}P^{\mathsf{T}}
. For any risk level
\eta\in(0,1)
, define the scalar position uncertainty radius

r_{k}(\eta):=\sqrt{\chi^{2}_{2}(1-\eta)\,\lambda_{\max}(S_{k})},

where
\chi^{2}_{2}(1-\eta)
is the
(1-\eta)
-quantile of the chi-square distribution with two degrees of freedom. Then

\mathbb{P}\left(\|Y_{k}-\bar{p}_{k}\|_{2}\leq r_{k}(\eta)\right)\geq 1-\eta.

Consequently, the following deterministic constraints are sufficient for the two predicate-level chance constraints used in this reach-avoid task:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\pi_{\text{goal}}(k)\big)\geq 1-\eta

\displaystyle\Leftarrow\|\bar{p}_{k}-p_{\mathrm{goal}}\|_{2}\leq R_{\mathrm{goal}}-r_{k}(\eta),

(57)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\pi_{\text{obs}}(k)\big)\geq 1-\eta

\displaystyle\Leftarrow\|\bar{p}_{k}-p_{\mathrm{obs}}\|_{2}\geq R_{\mathrm{obs}}+r_{k}(\eta).

(58)

We next decompose the STL chance constraint. Let

\varphi_{\text{goal}}:=\lozenge_{[0,N]}\pi_{\text{goal}},\qquad\varphi_{\text{obs}}:=\square_{[0,N]}\pi_{\text{obs}}.

Choose outer risk allocations
\delta_{\text{goal}},\delta_{\text{obs}}\geq 0
satisfying

\delta_{\text{goal}}+\delta_{\text{obs}}\leq\delta.

It is sufficient to impose
\mathbb{P}(\varphi_{\text{goal}})\geq 1-\delta_{\text{goal}}
and
\mathbb{P}(\varphi_{\text{obs}})\geq 1-\delta_{\text{obs}}
. In the baseline implementation, we use the uniform outer allocation

\delta_{\text{goal}}=\delta_{\text{obs}}=\frac{\delta}{2}.

For the always component, the union bound gives the predicate-level constraints

\mathbb{P}\big(\pi_{\text{obs}}(k)\big)\geq 1-\eta_{\text{obs},k},\qquad\sum_{k=0}^{N}\eta_{\text{obs},k}\leq\delta_{\text{obs}}.

With uniform allocation over time,
\eta_{\text{obs},k}=\delta_{\text{obs}}/(N+1)
, and (
58
) gives

\|\bar{p}_{k}-p_{\mathrm{obs}}\|_{2}\geq R_{\mathrm{obs}}+r_{k}\!\left(\frac{\delta_{\text{obs}}}{N+1}\right),\qquad k=0,\ldots,N.

(59)

For the eventually component, the goal only needs to be reached at one time instant. The resulting deterministic goal constraint is

\exists j\in\{0,\ldots,N\}:\quad\|\bar{p}_{j}-p_{\mathrm{goal}}\|_{2}\leq R_{\mathrm{goal}}-r_{j}(\delta_{\text{goal}}).

(60)

Under the uniform outer allocation, the predicate-level risks become

\eta_{\text{obs},k}=\frac{\delta}{2(N+1)}
.
Hence, the uniformly allocated deterministic constraints are

\displaystyle\|\bar{p}_{k}-p_{\mathrm{obs}}\|_{2}\geq R_{\mathrm{obs}}+r_{k}\!\left(\frac{\delta}{2(N+1)}\right),\;k=0,\ldots,N,

(61)

\displaystyle\exists j\in\{0,\ldots,N\}:\quad\|\bar{p}_{j}-p_{\mathrm{goal}}\|_{2}\leq R_{\mathrm{goal}}-r_{j}\!\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right).

(62)

This baseline allocates risk at the STL-component level and then across the support times of the always operator.

Discrete-time probabilistic reachable tube (DPRT)

The second baseline, which we denote discrete-time probabilistic reachable tube (DPRT), follows the probabilistic tube-based tightening approach of
[
52
]
. Assume a stabilizing feedback gain
K
is given. We decompose the stochastic state into a nominal component and an error component,

X_{k}=z_{k}+e_{k},

and apply the controller

u_{k}=v_{k}+K(X_{k}-z_{k})=v_{k}+Ke_{k}.

Then

\displaystyle z_{k+1}

\displaystyle=Az_{k}+Bv_{k},

(63)

\displaystyle e_{k+1}

\displaystyle=A_{\text{cl}}e_{k}+w_{k},\qquad A_{\text{cl}}:=A+BK,

(64)

with
z_{0}=x_{0}
and
e_{0}=0
.

The baseline constructs a probabilistic reachable tube for (
64
). For Gaussian disturbances, choose a disturbance confidence region

\mathcal{W}_{\beta}:=\left\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:w^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{d}^{-1}w\leq\chi^{2}_{4}(\beta)\right\}.

Choosing
\beta=1-\delta/N
gives a union-bound trajectory confidence level of at least
1-\delta
. Starting from
\mathcal{E}_{0}=\{0\}
, propagate the error reachable sets by

\mathcal{E}_{k+1}=A_{\text{cl}}\mathcal{E}_{k}\oplus\mathcal{W}_{\beta},\qquad k=0,\ldots,N-1.

(65)

Then the tube
\mathcal{E}:=\mathcal{E}_{0}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{E}_{N}
satisfies
\mathbb{P}(e_{0:N}\in\mathcal{E})\geq 1-\delta.

Define the projected tube radius

r_{k}^{\mathrm{tube}}:=\max_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{k}}\|Pe\|_{2}.

If the nominal trajectory satisfies the predicates tightened by
r_{k}^{\mathrm{tube}}
, then every trajectory whose error remains in the tube satisfies the original predicates. For the circular goal and obstacle, this gives

\displaystyle\|Pz_{k}-p_{\mathrm{goal}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq R_{\mathrm{goal}}-r_{k}^{\mathrm{tube}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\pi_{\text{goal}}(k),

\displaystyle\|Pz_{k}-p_{\mathrm{obs}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq R_{\mathrm{obs}}+r_{k}^{\mathrm{tube}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\pi_{\text{obs}}(k).

Therefore the chance constraint (
55
) is conservatively enforced by the deterministic STL constraints

\displaystyle\exists k\in\{0,\ldots,N\}:\|Pz_{k}-p_{\mathrm{goal}}\|_{2}\leq R_{\mathrm{goal}}-r_{k}^{\mathrm{tube}},

(66)

\displaystyle\|Pz_{k}-p_{\mathrm{obs}}\|_{2}\geq R_{\mathrm{obs}}+r_{k}^{\mathrm{tube}},\qquad k=0,\ldots,N.

(67)

In contrast to the predicate-level baseline, this method does not allocate risk through the STL syntax. The entire probability budget is used to construct a trajectory-level tube, and the STL formula is then enforced deterministically on the tightened predicates.
