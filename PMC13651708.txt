Infrared Illumination-Induced
Responsivity Enhancement
in Photoelectric Tunable-Step Terahertz Detectors

Ran Chen, Matthew Tan, Harvey E. Beere, Jonathan P. Griffiths, David A. Ritchie, Wladislaw Michailow

Abstract

Quantum phenomena in two-dimensional electron gases (2DEGs)
provide
new opportunities for high-responsivity far-infrared detection. A
prime example is photoelectric tunable-step (PETS) terahertz (THz)
detectors, which use the in-plane photoelectric (IPPE) effect as a
sensitive photonic detection mechanism. Here, we show that the terahertz
sensitivity of this quantum photodetection mechanism can be dynamically
tuned by using infrared illumination. We leverage the persistent photoconductivity
(PPC) effect in AlGaAs/GaAs-based heterostructures to tune the device
performance by adjusting the electron density and mobility of the
channel. Our approach enables optimization of the responsivity for
different operating modes and dynamic control over the output impedance
of PETS terahertz detectors of approximately one order of magnitude.
We provide an explanation for the underlying physics that advances
the understanding of quantum photodetection phenomena in 2DEGs. Our
findings pave the way to in situ tunable optoelectronics combining
concepts from the infrared and terahertz spectral regions.

Introduction

The terahertz (THz) region (f = 0.1–10
THz) has received significant attention in recent years due to its
unique capabilities in applications such as ultrafast wireless communication,
,
 medical imaging
,
 and non-destructive testing.
,
 The advancement of THz technology critically relies on sensitive
detection of terahertz waves. Among the various approaches, devices
based on two-dimensional electron gases (2DEGs), such as field effect
transistors (FETs), have emerged as a promising platform for THz detection
over the past few decades.

The idea of employing FETs for THz
detection originated from the
proposal of using plasmonic mixing by Dyakonov and Shur in 1996. Since then, FETs based on a wide range of material
systems, including gallium arsenide,
−

 silicon,
−

 and gallium nitride,
,
 have been explored as THz detectors
utilizing plasmonic mixing as the underlying mechanism.
,
 Other mechanisms responsible for the generation of a THz photoresponse
in 2DEG-based THz detectors have also been demonstrated, including
photo-thermoelectric effect,
,
 collision-less electron
heating, photon-assisted tunneling, bolometric, and
photovoltaic effects.

In 2022,
a novel, quantum, collision-free mechanism, the in-plane
photoelectric (IPPE) effect, was demonstrated.
,
 Devices exploiting the IPPE effect for THz detection are called
photoelectric tunable-step (PETS) THz detectors. The detection mechanism
is illustrated in : by applying two different voltages onto independently biased
gates, the bottoms of the conduction band under the left and right
gates shift, respectively, and an electrically tunable potential step
is artificially created within the channel. Under terahertz illumination,
the antenna on top of the detector couples the incident terahertz
radiation into the 2DEG and amplifies the electric field within the
gap between the two gates. Electrons near the artificially created
potential step absorb photons, which leads to a net electron flow
of quantum origin within the 2DEG from the high- to the low-electron-density
area. This results in a strong photoresponse of the device generated
under zero source-drain bias.

The first PETS detectors with a bow-tie antenna
on top showed a
strong photoresponse to ∼1.9 THz radiation in an AlGaAs/GaAs
high electron mobility transistor heterostructure at 9 K. Subsequent studies further improved the responsivity
of PETS THz detectors by optimizing devices’ dimensions and
revealed that the IPPE effect in AlGaAs/GaAs heterostructures persists
up to 75 K, significantly extending its practical applicability. Besides single-pixel antenna geometries, including
dipole,
−

 bow-tie, and X-wing antennas, high-sensitivity PETS THz detectors
with metasurface structures were recently demonstrated. The observation of the IPPE effect in an AlGaN/GaN
heterostructure shows its universality across different material systems
supporting a 2DEG.

A comparison
of the performance of PETS THz detectors exploiting
the IPPE effect with that of other state-of-the-art THz and sub-THz
detectors is presented in . Superconducting hot-electron bolometers (HEBs) show
excellent performance with high responsivity, low noise equivalent
power (NEP), and short response time in the THz range.
,
 However, expensive cryogenic temperature operation is required since
SHEBs are inherently limited by the superconducting transition temperatures
(T
c), for example, T
c = 8.5 K for 10 nm MgB2 film,
T
c = 9.9K for NbN film. By contrast, PETS THz detectors offer a more
cost-effective solution since they are capable of operating at much
higher temperatures, up to 75 K for AlGaAs/GaAs-based devices, with potential to operate at even higher temperatures
on other material systems. Photonic detectors based on intersubband
or intersublevel transitions, including quantum well infrared photodetectors
(QWIPs) and quantum dot detectors (QDDs), exhibit large responsivities
and fast response speeds
−

 but require an applied source-drain
bias and exhibit a rapid decay of the responsivity above liquid helium
temperatures. For instance, an InAs/GaAs quantum ring intersublevel
detector, formed by postepitaxy thermal annealing of quantum dots,
demonstrates a high responsivity of 25 A/W under 1.82 THz radiation
at 5.2 K, which drops significantly to 2.1 A/W at 10 K, with no detectable
signal observed at 12 K. On the contrary,
PETS THz detectors operate without source-drain bias, and the decay
of responsivity with increasing temperature is much smaller.

Various FET-based detectors relying on alternative
detection mechanisms
have been reported, e.g., silicon-based FETs utilizing plasmonic mixing,
graphene-based devices exploiting the photothermoelectric effect,
and others.
,,−

 Among detectors based on 2D electron systems, the novel PETS THz
detectors are highly promising devices, as the in-plane photoelectric
effect underpinning their operation was shown to provide a substantially
larger response than other, previously
known mechanisms, including plasmonic mixing and photothermoelectric
effects. Since their discovery in 2022, the field of PETS detectors
has been rapidly developing. Nowadays,
experimentally achieved responsivity values surpass the performance
of other detectors at comparable frequencies and operating temperatures. For example, a dipole-antenna PETS THz detector
achieved a photovoltage responsivity of 2.5 kV/W to ∼2 THz
radiation at 10 K, which is notably higher
than the 240 V/W responsivity of a bilayer-graphene FET under comparable
conditions, that relied on plasmonic mixing. More recently, a quantum metasurface-based PETS detector achieved
a responsivity of 2.7 A/W at 10 K, exceeding that of single-pixel
devices by more than 20 times. Although
some devices exhibit comparable or even higher responsivities, they
operate at significantly lower frequencies; for example, a responsivity
of 106 V/W was reported in an AlGaAs/GaAs-based quantum
point contact operating at 0.11–0.17 THz based on collision-less
electron heating. Achieving high-sensitivity
detection with FET-based THz detectors above 1 THz remains challenging,
as the responsivity drops and the noise-equivalent power rises with
frequency.
,
 For broadband detectors, the
decay was found to be approximately ω–3 with
frequency for antenna-coupled FETs
,
 and, in some
cases, an even steeper ω–7 dependence was observed. This further highlights the highly
promising performance of PETS THz detectors based on the IPPE effect,
which is predicted to operate across the entire THz range.
,

Despite the outstanding performance and rapid development
of detectors
based on the IPPE effect, a fundamental question remains unresolved:
how does the performance of PETS THz detectors depend on the electron
density in the 2DEG channel? In AlGaAs/GaAs heterostructures, the
electron density can be tuned in situ via the persistent photoconductivity
(PPC) effect, a unique phenomenon associated with the photoionization
of DX centers, in which optical illumination under low-temperature
conditions (typically T < 100 K) induces a long-lived
increase in electron density that persists for extended periods ranging
from hours to days.
−

 The PPC effect provides a convenient and continuous method of modulating
the electron concentration in 2DEGs over a wide range, as demonstrated
in refs
,
.

In this work,
we systematically investigate the dependence of the
IPPE effect on the electron density in the 2DEG by exploiting the
PPC effect in an AlGaAs/GaAs-based PETS THz detector. Incremental
infrared illumination with small doses enables the controlled tuning
of electron density and mobility in the 2DEG channel. Our results
reveal the existence of an optimal illumination dose that maximizes
detector performance, providing a practical strategy for the performance
optimization of PETS THz detectors through infrared illumination.
This study also elucidates the underlying mechanisms by which infrared
illumination modulates the IPPE effect in AlGaAs/GaAs-based two-dimensional
electron systems.

Device Design

A bowtie-antenna PETS THz detector as
shown in a,b
is fabricated from an AlGaAs/GaAs
high electron mobility heterostucture. The layer structure will be
discussed later in detail, in c. The 2DEG is approximately 77 nm beneath the top
surface, which is estimated from the electron density vs gate voltage
data shown in Supporting Information Section 1. The 2DEG is characterized by Hall measurements and features an
electron density of 2.3 × 1011 cm–2 and a mobility of 5.4 × 105 cm2/V s in
the dark at 1.5 K.

The PETS THz detector represents a dual-gated antenna-coupled
FET
with the two gates shaped in the form of a bowtie antenna. The antenna
confines incident radiation into a narrow channel, created within
the heterostructure by mesa etching. The two Ti/Au wings of the bowtie
antenna serve as the two gates of the detector, with one gate being
solid, while the other gate having a cutout that narrows the metal
down to a narrow bridge at the antenna center. Annealed AuGeNi ohmic
contacts far away from the active region serve as sources and drains
for electron transport in the 2DEG. The dimensions of the bowtie antenna
on top of the device are identical to those in ref .

Experimental Setup

The PETS THz detector is placed
in a cryostat, which enables it
to be excited by THz radiation from the top (antenna side) using a
quantum cascade laser (QCL) and illuminated with an infrared LED from
the backside, through the 500 μm GaAs semi-insulating substrate.
The QCL used emits at a wavelength of
λ = 159.38 μm (1.881 THz emission frequency).

The
detector and the QCL are cooled down to ∼6 and ∼18
K, respectively, in two liquid helium continuous-flow cryostats. A
cylindrical multimode waveguide system (annealed copper waveguides,
4.6 mm inner diameter) delivers and focuses the THz radiation from
the QCL to the front surface of the detector. The QCL is electrically modulated by a square wave with a frequency
of 772 Hz and a duty cycle of 2.14 %, enabling measurements of the
photoresponse using a lock-in technique.

The infrared (IR) illumination
is realized using an LED operated
at room temperature with an IR center wavelength of λ = 940
nm (E = 1.32 eV). A 1 mm diameter hole throughout
the copper support of the sample enables unobstructed access for illumination
of the detector back side; see Supporting Information Section 2. The substrate-side illumination ensures homogeneous
excitation of the 2D electron system, preventing a shadowing effect
from the gates that would otherwise occur in the case of top-side
IR illumination.

Interpretation

The change in carrier density after
illumination is mostly attributed
to the existence of deep-level donor impurities in semiconductors.
These deep levels are known as DX centers,
−

 which are complexes involving a donor atom (D) and an anion vacancy
(X), e.g., Si–VAs for Si-doped Al
x
Ga1–x
As.
,
 The most successful model to explain the DX center is the “negative-U
model” proposed by Chadi and Chang,
,
 supported by ref , and further analyzed in ref . The DX center is a highly localized and negatively charged
center resulting from the reaction
3
[ph6c01501_m003.webp]2d0→d++DX−
where d0 and d+ represent
neutral and ionized four-fold-coordinated substitutional donors. The
density of DX centers is, at most, one half the density of the donor
atoms. In highly doped samples, DX centers may be formed by capturing
two electrons

4
[ph6c01501_m004.webp]d++2e−→DX−
The ionization and capture processes of DX
centers go through two steps
5
[ph6c01501_m005.webp]DX−⇆d0+e−⇆d++2e−
where the intermediate neutral d0 state is thermodynamically unstable but plays a role in all carrier
capture and emission processes of DX centers. An important property of DX centers is that, once ionized, their
electron capture rate is thermally activated and strongly suppressed
at liquid helium temperatures. This gives rise to a persistent photoconductivity
effect: once the electron density is changed due to DX center ionization,
it remains in the non-equilibrium state for a long time (hours to
days).
−

As illustrated in a, the equilibrium charge carrier density n
0 in the 2DEG decreases slightly at the beginning before
rising up steeply to a plateau at around 200 s, and then stays at
the plateau until ∼1500 s. Beyond 1500 s, n
0 gradually increases until it saturates. The overall
increase in n
0 is attributed to the ionization
of DX centers, from which electrons are released and tunnel through
into the 2DEG channel, resulting in an overall rise in electron density.
However, the presence of a plateau between ∼200 and ∼1500
s, as well as the two-stage growth behavior of n
0, suggests that additional mechanisms may be involved that
require further explanation.

First, we exclude parallel conduction
through the doped layer as
a possible cause of the observed behavior. In AlGaAs/GaAs high-electron-mobility
transistor heterostructures such as the one we use here, see c, such a phenomenon could occur and may lead to abrupt levelling-off
of the electron density in the 2DEG,
similar to what is observed here at ∼200 s. However, magnetotransport
measurements on a reference Hall bar from the same wafer show that
no parallel conduction is present, even under high gate bias and after
red-light illumination. Only one 2DEG channel around 77 nm below the
top surface is observed; see Supporting Information Section 1.

We now present an explanation of the observed behavior
based on
DX-center excitation in combination with a backfilling of Si donor
levels that occur through three stages as the illumination dose is
increased, which is shown in d.

For an AlGaAs/GaAs heterostructure, typical
observed thresholds
of photon energies for changing the photoconductivity of a 2DEG are
1.9 eV corresponding to the bandgap of Al
x
Ga1–x
As (where x ∼ 0.3), 1.5 eV corresponding to the bandgap of GaAs, 1.1
eV corresponding to DX center activation, and 0.6-0.8 eV corresponding
to other traps, very likely in the GaAs buffer layer.
−

 Here, we illuminate the sample with a photon energy of 1.32 eV,
which is smaller than the GaAs and Al
x
Ga1–x
As bandgap energies. In this
case, two photoexcitation processes, called A and B can occur, as
shown in a,b.
Process A corresponds to the excitation of electrons from the 2DEG,
with relaxation into the doped barrier region. Process B corresponds
to DX-center excitation within the barrier region.

After cooling
in the dark and before illumination, the sample is
in thermal equilibrium. The DX centers are not ionized, while part
of the Si donors are ionized and contribute to the creation of the
electrons in the 2DEG (State A in d). As the sample starts to be illuminated,
both processes A and B will occur simultaneously.

In process
A, electrons are photoexcited, and these photoexcited
electrons will experience the electric field acting on them due to
the tilt of the conduction band edge. Those photoexcited electrons
that go lower into the heterostructure (to the right in the figure)
will relax and return to the 2D electron gas. Those electrons that
go to the left in the figure will accumulate in the potential minimum
created by the ionized silicon donors, which are positively charged.
While the electrons will not be captured again by the DX centers,
whose capture rate is thermally activated and suppressed at 6 K, they
will compensate some of the ionized Si shallow donors. Simultaneously,
the near-infrared LED will ionize DX centers, as shown by process
B, thus creating electrons in the conduction band of the doped region.
Some of these electrons will relax into the conduction-band minimum
of the doped donor region. Other electrons relax into the triangular
quantum well.

At low illumination doses (<100 s), the threshold
voltage initially
shifts to more positive values corresponding to a reduction of the
electron density in the channel (State A to B in d). At this stage, process
A is dominant since most electrons photoexcited from the DX centers
will be captured by the ionized Si shallow donors. Therefore, after a small amount of photoexcitation, this
will reduce the electron density in the 2DEG (a). However, the scattering is reduced, as
the ionized Si shallow donors are neutralized after illumination.
The lower electron density results in an overall reduction in remote
impurity scattering and leads to increased mobility.

From ∼200
s, the ionization of the DX centers, process B,
becomes the dominant process. More electrons are released from the
DX centers. Some of them tunnel through the barrier into the 2DEG,
which is compensated by the decrease of electrons by process A, hence
keeping the electron density in the channel constant over a certain
range of illumination doses (from ∼200 to ∼1500 s).
However, the plateau does not mean that the system is stationary,
because with every additional illumination pulse, more DX centers
become ionized in the doped region, and the quasi-Fermi levels for
electrons and holes start to drift apart more and more. Hence, in
the doped region, there are more and more positively charged DX centers,
and simultaneously there are more electrons that are filling back
the ionized silicon donors. At some point, most of the ionized silicon
donors contributing to the 2DEG will be filled back, and the electrons
will start to fill states in the conduction band edge of the parabolic
minimum. Soon after that, however, they will start to tunnel through
into the 2DEG, now notably increasing the 2D electron gas density,
thanks to the large number of ionized DX centers, which manifests
itself in the threshold voltage shifting back again to more negative
values.

As the illumination dose keeps increasing, more and
more impurities/DX
centers become ionized in the doped region, and the contributing electrons
accumulate slowly in the 2DEG from ∼1500 s until saturation
∼105 s (State B to C in d). The larger amount of positively charged
impurities in the doped region now pulls the conduction band edge
down in the region from 10 to 50 nm, resulting in a steeper and thinner
triangular potential barrier at the surface facing the gate metal.
This reduces the size of the tunneling barrier from the surface to
the conduction band minimum formed in the doped region, resulting
in a higher electron tunneling rate. As a result of that, the gate
leakage starts to occur at lower gate voltage and keeps increasing
from that point, as seen in b.

Attention is now directed to the magnitude
of the observed photoresponse
in .

The strength of the in-plane photoelectric current depends physically
on the chemical potentials under the positively and negatively biased
gates, and experimentally also on the
mobility of the electrons in the channel. Illumination of the sample
with the infrared LED hardly affects the conditions of the 2DEG under
a strongly positively biased gate. Under such high positive gate voltages,
when the 2D electron gas operates in enhancement mode, it is known
that the density of electrons does not depend anymore on whether the
sample has been illuminated or not. Moreover,
as under the positively biased gate the electron density is already
very high, any change of the electron density induced by additional
donor ionization will not significantly alter the parameters of the
2DEG under that gate. The highest photoresponse is observed close
to the threshold of the device, and correspondingly, the position
of the photoresponse shifts together with the threshold voltage of
the device. The main reason for the change in the photoresponse magnitude
is related to the scattering and, hence, the mobility of electrons
under the negatively biased gate.

At low illumination doses
(<100 s), the electron density decreases,
but the photoresponse stays the same or slightly increases. The backfilling
of the ionized silicon donors reduces the scattering in the channel,
hence enhancing the mobility under the negatively biased gate, which
in turn results in a slight increase of the observed photoresponse,
despite a reduction of the density/absolute value of the threshold
voltage.

At the region from around 200 to 1500 seconds, just
enough DX centers
are ionized to create the optimal regime of photovoltage detection.
Electrons excited from the DX centers compensate the ionized Si shallow
donors, reducing the scattering and improving the mobility. Therefore,
the photovoltage response increases to the optimal value, as any electrons
photoexcited by the THz radiation experience the least amount of scattering.

The maximum photocurrent response occurs at larger illumination
doses around 105 s, while the photovoltage actually decreases
in this region. This is because the photocurrent is affected much
stronger by changes in the resistance of the channel than the photovoltage.
As presented in , the significant increase in conductance until ∼105 s due to the illumination of the LED continues to increase the photocurrent,
even when the photovoltage starts to decrease.

Finally, after
around 105 s of equivalent illumination
time, any further increase in illumination causes only a decrease
in both I
ph and V
ph. Here, too many DX centers or deep impurities become ionized,
and together with the increasing gate leakage, there may also be accumulation
of mobile charges within the doped donor layer that create hopping
conduction. Any charge accumulation within the donor layer will screen
the incident THz radiation, and if there is any onset of parallel
conduction, this will also reduce the IPPE effect by essentially short-circuiting
the 2DEG as a detection element. The increasing number of ionized
DX centers (i.e., charged remote impurities) will also reduce the
mobility and increase gate leakage in the device. Correspondingly,
beyond 105 s, both photovoltage and photocurrent responses
start to decay.

It should be noted that besides the above-described
mechanisms,
there is also some charge transfer into the gate metal. As the 2DEG
and gates are galvanically connected through the gate circuits, a
change in the electron density in the 2DEG also leads to a change
in charge on the gate metal. However, this effect has little impact
on the band structure: due to the very large density of states in
the gate metal, the Fermi level within the gate and at its interface
with the semiconductor remains essentially unchanged upon near-infrared
illumination. The device characteristics are determined by the band
structure changes within the semiconductor, which has a much smaller
density of states.

Discussion and Conclusion

We have shown that infrared
illumination is an effective method
to dynamically tune the responsivity and output impedance of PETS
THz detectors over approximately one order of magnitude. We explained
the results using the physics of the persistent photoconductivity
effect and the interplay of different donor types. We found that there
is an optimal illumination dose that gives the highest terahertz sensitivity
and that dose is different depending on whether the detector operates
in photocurrent or photovoltage mode. The IR illumination method provides
an additional degree of freedom for tuning PETS detector performance
that cannot be achieved solely by tuning the two gate voltages and
facilitates matching of the device to external readout circuits.

The demonstrated mechanism is highly valuable for scaling up AlGaAs/GaAs-based
detector technology to focal plane arrays. In detector arrays, it
is very important to minimize pixel-to-pixel deviation of sensitivity
in order to produce a reliable 1D or 2D image, e.g., in a cooled high-sensitivity
terahertz camera chipan issue that microbolometer cameras
still suffer from. While the sensitivity of a single PETS detector
can be tuned using gate voltages, this approach is not suitable for
scaling up, as individual fine-tuning of the gate voltages on each
pixel is either not possible or very complicated. Our approach provides
an elegant solution to this problem: a 2D array of AlGaAs/GaAs-based
detectors can be mounted on top of a plate of infrared micro-LEDs,
allowing the same gate voltages to be applied to all detectors within
the array, with controlled infrared pulses ensuring that the sensitivity
of all pixels is set equal. Such an approach will enable in situ calibration
of AlGaAs/GaAs-based camera chips, while also mitigating against the
performance of individual pixel parameters drifting apart with time.

One challenge in the practical implementation of this tuning mechanism
is the persistent nature of DX center ionization at cryogenic temperatures.
This leads to a risk of overdosing the device with IR illumination
if the detector is illuminated accidentally above the optimal dose.
To reset ionized DX centers of the device, a thermal cycling process
would be needed. This challenge can be practically solved by placing
calibration devices on the same chip away from the active THz detection
area/focal plane array, allowing the density vs illumination dose
curves to be measured in situ after cooldown using pulsed IR illumination.
This allows the key characteristics to be identified, i.e., the illumination
dose that results in the sudden threshold voltage shift and the illumination
dose where the density stops increasing. This calibration can be done
on off-camera calibration units but on the same substrate. Thereafter,
the dose to be applied to the infrared LEDs backing the THz detector
array is known. In addition, instead of aiming to reach the dose yielding
maximum responsivity, in practical operation, it is sensible to aim
for a slightly lower infrared illumination dose in order to avoid

the electron density with time, as electrons are recaptured by ionized
DX centers, a process that is slow at cryogenic temperatures but still
occurs on time scales of hours to days. At periodic intervals, an
in situ recalibration of the camera can be done, where small additional
pulses of infrared radiation are applied to those pixels whose sensitivity
or density appears to have drifted away to restore uniform responsivity
across the focal plane array. Such periodic internal recalibrations
are common in, e.g., modern oscilloscopes. These issues can be solved
in practical device implementations via device firmware and internal
calibration lookup tables.

The demonstrated method is a useful
tool for in situ tuning of
all optoelectronics relying on AlGaAs/GaAs heterostructures. This
performance optimization method can be exploited to facilitate scalable
terahertz optoelectronic devices and arrays that utilize physical
concepts from both the infrared and terahertz spectral ranges.
