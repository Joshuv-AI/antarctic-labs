Silphium perfoliatum flavonoids alleviate LPS-induced injury via enhancing intestinal antioxidant capacity, barrier function, and regulating cecal microbiota in broilers
Authors: Guanglong Liu, Wei Guo, Tengyuqi Li, Yuhan Wang, Kabelo Anthony Makatjane, Hongzhi Lin, Jiayin Xu, Fang Zhang, Haizhu Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effects of Silphium perfoliatum L. flavonoids (SPF) on growth performance, intestinal morphology, antioxidant capacity, anti-inflammatory responses, gut barrier function, and cecal microbiota in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-challenged broilers. 250 one-day-old Arbor Acres broilers were randomly assigned to five groups: control (C), LPS (L), and LPS + SPF at 100, 200, or 300 mg/kg (SL1, SL2, SL3), with five replicates of 10 birds. Broilers in the LPS and SPF groups received 0.5 mg/kg LPS intraperitoneally on days 16, 18, and 20, while the control group received an equal volume of saline. SPF mitigated LPS-induced growth impairment, reducing feed-to-gain ratio (F/G) and improving average daily gain (ADG), villus height (VH), and villus height to crypt depth ratio (VH/CD) relative to the LPS group (all P < 0.001). SPF supplementation decreased malondialdehyde (MDA, P < 0.001), increased glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px, P < 0.001) and total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD, P = 0.011), elevated interleukin-4 (IL-4, P = 0.03), enhanced interleukin-10 (IL-10, P < 0.001), and reduced tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α, P < 0.001). SPF downregulated jejunal Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), myeloid differentiation factor 88 (MyD88), nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB), and TNF-α mRNA expression (all P < 0.001) and upregulated tight junction genes zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and occludin (OCLN, both < 0.001), and claudin-1 (CLDN1, P = 0.002). SPF ameliorated LPS-induced decreases in cecal microbial diversity and abundances of beneficial bacteria including Lactobacillus and Ruminococcus. These results indicate that dietary SPF effectively improves growth, gut health, antioxidant status, and anti-inflammatory responses in LPS-stressed broilers, supporting its potential as a natural alternative to antibiotics in poultry production.Keywords: Oxidative stress, Gut barrier function, TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB, Antioxidant capacity, Microbial diversity

Introduction

With the ongoing acceleration of scale-intensive development in animal husbandry, fundamental changes have occurred in farming practices, production efficiency, and industrial layout. The broiler industry, characterized by a short growth cycle, high feed conversion efficiency, and controllable rearing costs, has become a key pillar of the global poultry sector, ensuring a stable supply of poultry meat. However, while scale-intensive farming enhances production efficiency, it also poses a range of challenges to the rearing environment and animal health. Studies have shown that external stressors can induce the generation of free radicals, particularly reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) (Chen et al., 2014). These free radicals can cause cellular damage and functional impairments by triggering lipid peroxidation, protein carbonylation, DNA damage, and other mechanisms (Hafez and Attia, 2020).

In intensive poultry production, broilers are almost inevitably exposed to Gram-negative bacteria, and antibiotics are commonly used for prevention, treatment, and growth promotion. However, the rising prevalence of antibiotic resistance has become a significant global challenge for both human and animal health (Roth et al., 2019). Oxidative stress (OS) is a critical factor in the initiation and progression of broiler stress responses induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (Hu et al., 2023). In experimental models of inflammation, LPS is frequently used because it elicits robust inflammatory responses (Shi et al., 2024). LPS-induced oxidative stress can trigger inflammatory reactions and impair broiler growth performance (Lu et al., 2008; Liu et al., 2023). Mechanistically, LPS activates Toll-like receptor 4 and induces ROS production, leading to pyroptosis and disruption of the intestinal barrier (Liu et al., 2024). Consequently, identifying effective natural antioxidants has become a major focus of current research and application.

Plant extracts containing bioactive compounds, including essential oils, tannins, and flavonoids, hold great potential as alternatives to antibiotics (Vanessa Valenzuela-Grijalva et al., 2017). Flavonoids are naturally occurring compounds with diverse phenolic structures and represent one of the most widely distributed classes of phytochemicals in the plant kingdom, primarily found in vegetables, fruits, cereals, roots, herbs, as well as tea and wine products (Tan et al., 2022). As important secondary metabolites, flavonoids possess multiple biological functions, including antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory activities. They have been shown to effectively improve intestinal health, enhance immune function, optimize growth performance in broilers (Abd El-Hack et al., 2023), modulate gut microbiota (Guo et al., 2026), and reduce disease incidence.

In this study, Silphium perfoliatum L., a perennial herbaceous plant of the Asteraceae family native to North America, was used. Traditionally, it has been employed for medicinal purposes by Native American tribes, and its roots are known for their high nutritional value and water-retention properties (Kowalski, 2009). The plant can serve ornamental, apicultural, and medicinal purposes, and it can also be utilized as forage or a plant-based energy source (Haag et al., 2015). As a plant with considerable development potential, one of the core values of S. perfoliatum L. lies in its abundant flavonoid content (Kowalski, 2009), a characteristic that has been confirmed by numerous domestic and international studies and has recently become a research hotspot. Dietary supplementation with S. perfoliatum L. has been shown to improve growth performance and immune function in Micropterus salmoides (Tang et al., 2024), enhance the immunity of Penaeus vannamei, and promote the growth of beneficial gut microbiota in shrimp (Du et al., 2023). Extracts of S. perfoliatum L. have also been demonstrated to significantly ameliorate high-fat diet-induced non-alcoholic fatty liver disease in mice by promoting lipid metabolism, reducing fat accumulation, and effectively protecting against liver injury associated with lipid deposition and inflammation (Xu et al., 2024). These functions highlight the potential of flavonoids from S. perfoliatum L. as a promising feed additive.

Although some studies have investigated Silphium perfoliatum L. and its extracts, the specific effects of its flavonoid extracts on broilers remain largely unexplored. The present study aimed to evaluate the effects of dietary supplementation with Silphium perfoliatum L. flavonoids on growth performance, antioxidant capacity, intestinal barrier function, and gut microbiota in broilers subjected to LPS-induced stress. This research seeks to elucidate the potential of these flavonoids in mitigating oxidative stress, providing both effective strategies and theoretical insights for alleviating oxidative damage in broilers.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and broiler management

A total of 250 healthy one-day-old Arbor Acres broiler chicks with similar body weights were randomly assigned to five groups. The C and L groups were fed a basal diet, and the SL1, SL2, and SL3 groups received the basal diet supplemented with 100, 200, or 300 mg/kg of Silphium perfoliatum L. flavonoids (SPF), respectively. Broilers in the L, SL1, SL2, and SL3 groups were intraperitoneally injected with 0.5 mg/kg LPS on days 16, 18, and 20, whereas the C group received an equivalent volume of physiological saline. Each group consisted of five replicates with 10 chicks per replicate.

The trial lasted for 21 days, during which birds had ad libitum access to feed and water. The relative humidity in the rearing facility was maintained at 60-70%, and the temperature was gradually decreased from 30°C on day 1 to 22°C on day 21. A photoperiod of 23 hours of light and 1 hour of darkness was applied throughout the trial. Arbor Acres broilers were obtained from Jilin Dexiang Animal Husbandry Co., Ltd., flavonoids from Shaanxi Fengqiwu Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and LPS from Changchun Lebang Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Sample collection

On day 21 of the trial, broilers were euthanized, and three birds were randomly selected from each replicate for further analysis. After humane euthanasia, the birds were dissected to collect a 2-cm segment of the jejunum, which was fixed in paraformaldehyde for hematoxylin and eosin staining to observe intestinal morphology. The jejunal lumen was then rinsed with physiological saline, cut open using sterile scissors, and mucosal samples were scraped with sterile slides. The collected mucosa was transferred into cryogenic vials, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently stored at −80°C until analysis. Cecal contents were collected into cryogenic vials, immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C for subsequent examination.

Growth performance

The broilers were weighed after fasting on days 1, 16, and 21 of the experiment. During the entire experimental period, daily feed intake was recorded, and the average daily feed intake (ADFI), average daily gain (ADG), and feed-to-gain ratio (F/G) were calculated.

Intestinal morphology analysis

Jejunal segments fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde were processed into paraffin-embedded sections and stained with HE. Villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) were measured under a light microscope (DM-1000, Leica Microsystems Inc., Germany), and the villus height to crypt depth ratio (VH/CD) was calculated.

Antioxidant enzyme activities and inflammatory cytokines

An appropriate amount of jejunal mucosa was homogenized on ice at a ratio of 1:9 (w/v) in homogenization buffer. The supernatant was collected and used to determine the following parameters: total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD), malondialdehyde (MDA), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) using commercial assay kits. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits were employed to measure jejunal mucosal inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-4 (IL-4), interleukin-10 (IL-10), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). All assay kits were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China), and all procedures were performed strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Intestinal gene expression

Approximately 50 mg of jejunal mucosa was placed into a 2 mL sterile centrifuge tube, and total RNA was extracted using a TRIzol RNA extraction kit (TAKARA, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration was determined using a DHS NanoPro-One micro-spectrophotometer (Dinghaoyuan, China). The target gene was synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., and the primer sequences are shown in Table 2. The total reaction volume for reverse transcription-quantitative PCR was 25 μL. Amplification was performed on a CFX Opus96 system (Bio-Rad, USA) with the following cycling program: initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, denaturation at 95°C for 5 s, and annealing/extension at 60°C for 30 s, with fluorescence signal acquisition during the 60°C step. Gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method, with β-actin serving as the internal reference gene.

16S rRNA analysis

Genomic DNA from intestinal samples was extracted using a commercial kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and quality of the extracted DNA were assessed using a spectrophotometer. The V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 515F and 806R with sample-specific barcodes. PCR cycling conditions were as follows: 98°C for 1 min; 30 cycles of 98°C for 10 s, 50°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s; final extension at 72°C for 5 min. Each 15 µL reaction contained Phusion High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix, 0.2 µM each primer, and approximately 10 ng template DNA. Paired-end reads were merged using FLASH (v1.2.11), quality-filtered using fastp (v0.23.1), and chimeric sequences were removed using vsearch (v2.16.0) against the Silva database. PCR products were evaluated by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel. Subsequently, PCR products were purified using magnetic beads, and equimolar amounts of purified PCR products were pooled and thoroughly mixed. The pooled PCR products were quantified, and the target bands were recovered.

Sequencing libraries were constructed from the purified PCR products. The quality and concentration of the constructed libraries were assessed using Qubit fluorometry and quantitative PCR. Qualified libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using paired-end 250 bp (PE250) reads. Raw sequencing data were processed using the DADA2 plugin within QIIME2 to perform denoising, yielding high-resolution amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and feature tables. Taxonomic annotation was conducted using QIIME2 against the Silva 138.1 database.

To normalize sequencing depth across samples, the data were rarefied to the sample with the lowest sequencing depth. Subsequent alpha- and beta-diversity analyses were performed based on the normalized data. Relative abundance distributions were visualized using histograms plotted with the SVG function in Perl. Heatmaps were generated using the pheatmap() function in R. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) were performed and visualized using the ade4 and ggplot2 packages in R. Differential microbial community composition was further analyzed using the linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) software.

Statistical analysis

Data were organized and processed using Microsoft Excel. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27. Homogeneity of variance was assessed before conducting one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For comparisons among groups, Welch’s t-test was applied when variances were unequal. Post hoc multiple comparisons were performed using Tukey’s test or the Games-Howell test, as appropriate. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Antioxidant capacity and inflammatory cytokines

The effects of dietary SPF on antioxidant capacity in the jejunal mucosa of LPS-challenged broilers are illustrated in Fig. 2. Dietary supplementation with SPF reduced the content of MDA in the jejunal mucosa of broilers induced by LPS (P < 0.001). Supplementation with 300 mg/kg SPF increased the content of GSH-Px in the LPS-induced jejunal mucosa of broilers (P < 0.001). Supplementation with 100 mg/kg SPF increased the content of T-SOD induced by LPS (P = 0.011). Compared with the C group, the activity of CAT in all other groups decreased (P = 0.004). The effects of dietary SPF supplementation on jejunal mucosal inflammatory cytokines in LPS-challenged broilers are shown in Fig. 3. Compared with the C group, LPS challenge increased the IL-1β content in the jejunal mucosa (P < 0.001). Dietary SPF supplementation decreased TNF-α content in the jejunal mucosa (P < 0.001). Dietary supplementation with 100 and 200 mg/kg SPF increased the IL-4 content in the jejunal mucosa of LPS-challenged broilers (P = 0.03). In addition, supplementation with 200 mg/kg SPF increased the IL-10 content (P < 0.001) induced by LPS challenge.

Cecal microbiota

Considering the superior effects observed with 200 mg/kg SPF in the previous experiments, cecal microbiota analysis was conducted on the C, L, and SL2 groups. The relative abundances at the phylum level are shown in Fig. 6. Compared with the C group, the L group showed a significant decrease in Bacillota abundance (P = 0.003) and a significant increase in Bacteroidota abundance (P = 0.003), with Thermodesulfobacteriota exhibiting an increasing trend. In the SL2 group, the abundances of Bacillota and Bacteroidota were closer to those of the C group. The relative abundances at the family level are shown in Fig. 7. Compared with the C group, the L group exhibited a significant increase in Bacteroidaceae abundance (P = 0.005), but Lactobacillaceae and Ruminococcaceae abundances decreased. Compared with the L group, SL2 supplementation increased the abundances of Bacteroidaceae, Lactobacillaceae, and Ruminococcaceae.

Discussion

In the present study, LPS challenge led to a decline in growth performance of broilers, which is consistent with previous findings (Chen et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2026). Previous studies have shown that dietary supplementation with flavonoids can improve the average daily gain and feed-to-gain ratio of broilers (Wu et al., 2024; Akter et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2025). The growth inhibition caused by LPS may be related to a reduction in the release of hypothalamic appetite-stimulating neuropeptides, leading to decreased feed intake (Yousefi et al., 2021).

Villus height and crypt depth are commonly used indicators for assessing small intestinal function (Zhang et al., 2019). Generally, lower crypt depth, higher villus height, and increased VH/CD are associated with improved intestinal structure and function, which facilitates nutrient digestion and absorption, enhances disease resistance, and supports better growth performance (Luo et al., 2021). In the present study, dietary SPF supplementation increased the VH/CD, suggesting that SPF can alleviate LPS-induced intestinal injury. LPS-induced changes in jejunal morphology observed in this study are consistent with previous reports (Wang et al., 2023). Supplementation with SPF significantly improved the LPS-induced reduction in VH and VH/CD, which aligns with earlier findings (Prihambodo et al., 2020). These results indicate that SPF may mitigate LPS-induced intestinal damage by improving intestinal morphology.

SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px reduce free radical concentrations by catalyzing their reduction, thereby protecting cells from oxidative damage (Jing et al., 2020). The present study demonstrated that LPS challenge decreased the antioxidant capacity of broilers, consistent with previous findings (Song et al., 2024), indicating that LPS suppresses antioxidant enzyme production and exacerbates oxidative stress. Compared with the LPS group, dietary SPF significantly increased T-SOD and GSH-Px levels in the jejunum and reduced MDA content, suggesting that SPF enhances the antioxidant capacity of broilers by upregulating antioxidant activity, in agreement with previous studies (Zhou et al., 2019; Shi et al., 2022). Compared with the LPS-challenged group, supplementation with SPF did not significantly affect CAT activity in any group, although an upward trend was observed.

A typical immune response to harmful stimuli is inflammation, which serves as a key mechanism for defending against pathogens and initiating tissue healing and repair (Jomova et al., 2025). The expression levels of inflammatory cytokines reflect the effectiveness of the immune response. In this study, LPS challenge increased the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the intestines of broilers, consistent with previous findings (Xing et al., 2023). Dietary supplementation with SPF significantly elevated anti-inflammatory cytokine levels and reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine expression (Sun et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2024).

TLR4 is an important pattern recognition receptor that detects pathogenic bacteria that have entered the bloodstream and activates downstream signaling pathways. Upon activation, the MyD88-dependent pathway triggers NF-κB signaling and upregulates multiple inflammatory cytokines (Tang et al., 2021). Previous studies have reported that LPS increases TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB mRNA expression in the intestine (Zhang et al., 2022a). In the present study, LPS challenge elevated mRNA expression of TLR4, MyD88, and NF-κB, thereby exacerbating the inflammatory response. Flavonoids from other plants, such as Trifolium pratense flavonoids, have been shown to alleviate inflammatory damage in mice by modulating the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway (Feng et al., 2024), and sea buckthorn flavonoids have been reported to downregulate LPS-induced TLR4, NF-κB, and IL-1β mRNA expression (Zhi et al., 2024). Our results indicate that dietary SPF supplementation significantly reduced the mRNA levels of TLR4, MyD88, NF-κB, and TNF-α, suggesting that SPF may mitigate intestinal inflammation in broilers through regulation of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway. Future studies should investigate these effects at the protein level to further elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Intestinal permeability is closely associated with tight junction proteins (Turner et al., 2014), which are primarily composed of Claudin, Occludin, and ZO-1 (Chen et al., 2023). Previous studies have shown that during inflammatory responses, various cytokines can infiltrate the intestinal tissue, causing intestinal lesions, damaging the mucosa, and impairing barrier function, which is accompanied by reduced mRNA expression of Occludin, ZO-1, and Claudin-1 (Zhang et al., 2022b). LPS challenge has also been reported to decrease protein expression levels of ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-1 (Chang et al., 2020). In the present study, LPS-induced stress significantly decreased mRNA levels of ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-1 in the intestines of broilers, indicating that LPS impairs intestinal barrier function and increases permeability by downregulating these genes. Dietary supplementation with SPF significantly upregulated mRNA expression of ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-1 in LPS-challenged broilers, consistent with previous reports (Tu et al., 2025). These results suggest that SPF may repair intestinal barrier function and alleviate stress-induced intestinal injury by enhancing the expression of tight junction-related genes.

The composition of the intestinal microbiota has a profound impact on animal health and production performance. A stable gut microbiota facilitates nutrient absorption and metabolism, whereas dysbiosis can lead to negative outcomes such as diarrhea and impaired nutrient utilization (Rao et al., 2024). Previous studies have reported that LPS-induced stress reduces microbial diversity in the cecum of broilers (Wang et al., 2025). Our research indicates that LPS challenge significantly increased the relative abundance of Bacteroidota and decreased that of Bacillota in the intestinal microbiota of broilers. The abnormal increase in Bacteroidota may be associated with the induction of inflammation (Zhao et al., 2022). Dietary supplementation with SPF restored the relative abundances of Bacillota and Bacteroidota toward levels observed in the control group, suggesting that SPF may help reestablish the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio, which is linked to reduced opportunistic infections (Liu et al., 2025).At the family level, LPS challenge significantly increased the abundance of Bacteroidaceae in the cecal contents, consistent with phylum-level observations, but the abundances of Lactobacillaceae, Ruminococcaceae, and Oscillibacter decreased. Supplementation with SPF promoted the recovery of these beneficial taxa, including Lactobacillaceae, Lactobacillus, Ruminococcaceae, Ruminococcus, and Faecalibacterium. Previous studies indicate that Ruminococcaceae are primarily responsible for degrading polysaccharides and fibers, producing short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) that support intestinal health (Hooda et al., 2012). Lactobacillus exerts antimicrobial effects and modulates immune function (Yu et al., 2015), and Faecalibacterium possesses anti-inflammatory properties, enhances intestinal barrier integrity, produces butyrate (He et al., 2021), and can ameliorate renal dysfunction and inflammation while reducing serum levels of multiple uremic toxins (Li et al., 2022). SPF may confer intestinal protection by promoting the growth of these beneficial microbes. Consistently, flavonoids have been reported to enhance beneficial bacteria while inhibiting the growth of pathogenic microbes (Shi et al., 2020).

Alpha diversity analysis revealed that the Chao1 and Shannon indices of the L group were significantly lower than those of the C group, whereas the SL2 group showed an increasing trend relative to the L group. Beta diversity analyses, including PCoA and NMDS, indicated that the microbial community structure of the L group diverged from the C group, and the SL2 group exhibited a trend of convergence toward the C group. These results suggest that dietary SPF supplementation may modulate host health by regulating cecal microbial diversity. LEfSe analysis identified Agathobaculum as the dominant taxon in the L group. Agathobaculum is a butyrate-producing intestinal bacterium (Ahn et al., 2016). Butyric acid is the primary metabolite of butyrate-producing bacteria and one of the main SCFAs. Butyrate plays a key role in regulating intestinal energy metabolism and maintaining gut homeostasis, regulatory effects in extra-intestinal organs and tissues (Du et al., 2024). In the gut, it is predominantly produced through the fermentation of dietary fiber and can supply energy to the host via fatty acid oxidation, serving as a primary energy source for intestinal epithelial cells (Liu et al., 2017). Its accumulation may represent an adaptive microbial response to environmental stress, aiming to maintain intestinal homeostasis. In the SL2 group, the intestinal microbiota was significantly enriched with Bacilli, Lactobacillales, and Faecalitalea. The predominant members of Lactobacillales are lactic acid bacteria, which can modulate the composition of the intestinal microbiota, enhance mucosal barrier integrity, and exhibit significant synergistic effects with natural bioactive compounds in immune regulation (Ji et al., 2025). Faecalitalea primarily produces butyrate (Ma et al., 2020) and is often used in probiotic formulations to enhance intestinal barrier function (Elokil et al., 2024). Previous studies have shown that quercetin intervention can alleviate LPS-induced inflammatory responses and help maintain intestinal function, partly by modulating the composition of the gut microbiota, especially through the enrichment of SCFA-producing bacteria (Feng et al., 2023). Our results indicate that supplementation with SPF can adjust the cecal microbiota structure and promote the abundance of beneficial bacteria, thereby mitigating LPS-induced immunological stress in broilers, consistent with previous findings (Tian et al., 2026).

This study demonstrates that dietary supplementation with Silphium perfoliatum L. flavonoids can mitigate the negative effects of LPS-induced stress in broilers. The supplementation improved feed efficiency, enhanced intestinal antioxidant and anti-inflammatory capacity, and promoted intestinal morphology, microbiota balance, and barrier integrity. These effects are likely mediated through inhibition of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby suppressing inflammatory responses and alleviating oxidative stress-induced damage. These findings provide a theoretical basis for the development of novel plant-derived feed additives to support poultry health under stress conditions.
