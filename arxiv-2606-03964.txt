Informational completeness of qubit measurements and IC preservability of qubit channels: Characterization and Quantification
Jatin Ghai 1,2,, Arindam Mitra 3,

Abstract
Informationally complete (IC) measurements are a useful class of measurements, as their outcome statistics uniquely determine an unknown quantum state. Hence, they are important for certain tasks such as quantum state tomography, quantum process tomography, etc. In this work, we study the quantification of informational completeness for arbitrary quantum measurements by introducing and characterizing a faithful measure for it. We explicitly evaluate the informational completeness of qubit symmetric informationally complete (SIC) measurements and show that it is an upper bound for all qubit minimal informationally complete measurements. Furthermore, by introducing a faithful measure, we try to quantify and characterize the ability of an arbitrary quantum channel to preserve informational completeness of any IC measurement when the channel acts on it in the Heisenberg picture. We call this measure informational completeness-preservability (IC preservability) of quantum channels. After studying its properties, we establish its relation to another quantity, namely, the absolute output coherence of a quantum channel, which quantifies the minimum amount of coherence (w.r.t. an arbitrary incoherent basis) that can always be obtained from the output of that channel. Finally, we illustrate our results with an example from light-matter interaction scenarios. Thus, in this work, not only do we try to provide a quantitative framework for studying both the informational completeness of quantum measurements and the ability of quantum channels to preserve it, but we also try to offer key insight into the conceptual relation between informational completeness and quantum coherence.



I
Introduction

Quantum measurements play a central role in quantum mechanics. They serve as a bridge between the abstract mathematical description of a quantum system and the actual information about the quantum system that can be accessed in practical experiments. Thus, the characterization of quantum systems is fundamentally based on the extraction of information through measurement. In many quantum information-processing tasks, such as quantum state tomography and quantum process tomography, it is necessary to perform measurements whose outcome statistics are sufficient to uniquely determine a completely unknown quantum state. This class of measurements is known as
informational complete
(IC) measurements
D’Ariano et al. 2004
;
Busch 1991
;
D’Ariano et al. 2005
. For an unknown quantum state, the statistics generated by an informationally complete measurement contain sufficient information to uniquely determine that unknown state. Consequently, informationally complete measurements are necessary for both the foundational exploration of the quantum theory, as well as, for the practical side of quantum theory in experimental settings. Among informationally complete measurements, a special class of measurements is
symmetric informationally complete
(SIC) measurements, also known as SIC-POVMs (Symmetric Informationally Complete Positive Operator-Valued Measures)
Renes et al. 2004
;
Samuel and Gedik 2025
. A SIC measurement in a
d
-dimensional Hilbert space consists of
d^{2}
rank-one measurement operators with equal pairwise overlaps. Besides the convenient symmetrical structure, SIC-POVMS also have practical importance. They are very useful for quantum-state tomography
Wootters and Fields 1989
;
Scott 2006
, and has applications in entanglement detection
Shang et al. 2018
, dimension witnessing
Brunner et al. 2013
, device-independent randomness generation
Andersson et al. 2018
, and the certification of quantum measurement devices
Tavakoli et al. 2020
;
Mironowicz and Pawłowski 2019
.

If white noise is mixed with an informationally complete measurement, it may remain informationally complete
Mitra and Ghai 2026
. But the addition of white noise makes it less robust. In other words, its ability to discriminate between quantum states is decreased. Therefore, a natural question is how to quantify the informational completeness of a given quantum measurement, and such an investigation has not been widely conducted yet to the best of our knowledge. Since neither informationally complete nor incomplete measurements form a convex set
Heinosaari and Ziman 2011
;
Watrous 2018
, measures such as robustness
Uola et al. 2015
;
Skrzypczyk et al. 2019
and weight
Uola et al. 2020
are generally not well-defined or well-behaved. Therefore, in this work, we are
motivated
to quantify the informational completeness of quantum measurements. Now, when a quantum channel acts on quantum measurements in the Heisenberg picture, it may decrease the quality of informational completeness or destroy it. Therefore, it is important to study the informational completeness preservability of quantum channels, and such an investigation also serves as another
motivation
behind this work. In this work, we also demonstrate the relation of informational completeness preservability with absolute output coherence of a quantum channel, which offers key insight into the conceptual relationship between informational completeness and quantum coherence. It should also be mentioned that apart from a few general results, we mostly restrict ourselves to qubit systems in this work.

The rest of the work is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
we discuss the necessary preliminaries. We discuss our main results in Sec.
III
. More specifically, in Sec.
III.1
we study the quantification of
information completeness
for quantum measurements and explore its properties. In Sec.
III.2
, we quantify the
informational completeness preservability
of quantum channels and try to characterize it. In Sec.
III.3
, we define and study the
absolute output coherence
of a qubit channel and establish its relation with IC preservability of the same qubit channel (see Theorem
4
). In Sec.
III.4
, we illustrate our results with an example from light-matter interaction scenarios. In Sec.
IV
, we summarize our results and discuss future directions.

II.1
Quantum Measurements and Channels

A quantum measurement, or positive operator-valued measure (POVM), on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is described by a set of positive semidefinite operators

A=\{A(x)\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\}_{x\in\Omega_{A}}
, satisfying the normalization condition
\sum_{x\in\Omega_{M}}A(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}}
, where
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}}
denotes the identity operator on
\mathcal{H}
Heinosaari and Ziman 2011
. The set
\Omega_{A}
denotes the possible outcomes of the measurement, while
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
corresponds to the space of linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
. In this work, we restrict ourselves to finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. Each operator
A(x)
is referred to as a POVM element of the measurement
A
. A measurement
A
is called projective if every POVM element satisfies
A(x)^{2}=A(x),\penalty\ \forall\penalty\ x\in\Omega_{A}
. In this work, only measurements with finitely many outcomes are considered. When a measurement
A
is performed on a quantum state
\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
, where
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
denotes the set of density operators on
\mathcal{H}
, the probability of observing outcome
x
is given by the Born rule
p(x)=\text{Tr}[\rho A(x)]
. We denote the set of all measurements on
\mathcal{H}
by
\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})
. For qubits specifically, we denote their Hilbert space as
\mathcal{H}_{Q}
.

Quantum channels describe generic physically realisable deterministic transformations between quantum states. Mathematically, a quantum channel
\Lambda:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})
is represented by a completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) linear map
Heinosaari and Ziman 2011
. In the Schrödinger picture, the channel acts on density operators, mapping states on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
to states on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
. With any linear map

\Lambda:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})
, there exists
another associated map, known as its dual map
\Lambda^{\dagger}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
,
defined through the relation

\text{Tr}[Y^{\dagger}\Lambda^{\dagger}(X)]=\text{Tr}[(\Lambda(Y))^{\dagger}X]
, for all
X\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})
and
Y\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
Watrous 2018
. The dual map corresponds to the description of the quantum channel in the Heisenberg picture and in this picture it transforms the measurements in
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{K})
to the measurements in
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{H})
. More specifically, if
A=\{A(x)\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})\}
is a measurement on the output space of a channel
\Lambda
, then the transformed measurement on the input space in the Heisenberg picture is given by
\Lambda^{\dagger}(A):=\{\Lambda^{\dagger}(A(x))\}
. The set of all quantum channels from
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
to
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})
is denoted by
\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
. Given two linear maps
\Lambda_{1}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{2})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
and

\Lambda_{2}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
,
their composition is written as
\Lambda_{1}\circ\Lambda_{2}
and is defined by
(\Lambda_{1}\circ\Lambda_{2})(\rho):=\Lambda_{1}(\Lambda_{2}(\rho)),\penalty\ \forall\penalty\ \rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
. If both maps are quantum channels, then
\Lambda_{1}\circ\Lambda_{2}\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H}_{1})
. For two channels
\Lambda\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
and
\Lambda^{\prime}\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K}^{\prime})
, we say that
\Lambda
is a post-processing of
\Lambda^{\prime}
, if there exists a channel
\Theta\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{K}^{\prime},\mathcal{K})
such that
\Lambda=\Theta\circ\Lambda^{\prime}
. In this case, we write
\Lambda^{\prime}\succeq_{postproc}\Lambda
. The relation
\succeq_{postproc}
defines a preorder on quantum channels and is commonly referred to as the
post-processing preorder
Heinosaari and Miyadera 2017
. A linear map
\Lambda:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})
is said to be Hermitian-preserving trace preserving (HPTP) linear map if for arbitrary
X\in Herm(\mathcal{H})
we have
\Lambda(X)\in Herm(\mathcal{K})
and
\text{Tr}[\Lambda(X)]=\text{Tr}[X]

Watrous 2018
. Here,
Herm(\mathcal{H})
is a set of Hermitian matrices in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
. In the case, when
\Theta
is just an HPTP map in the relation
\Lambda=\Theta\circ\Lambda^{\prime}
the preorder is denoted as
\Lambda^{\prime}\succeq_{asymp}\Lambda
Mitra and Ghai 2026
. Given a quantum channel
\Lambda
, a linear map
\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K}^{\prime})
is called a statistical morphism if for any POVM
A=\{A(x)\}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{K}^{\prime})
, there exists another POVM
A_{\mathcal{N}}=\{A_{\mathcal{N}}(x)\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{K})\}
such that
Buscemi 2018
;
Buscemi 2012
;
Buscemi 2016
for all
\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle\text{Tr}[\mathcal{N}\circ\Lambda(\rho)A(x)]=\text{Tr}[\Lambda(\rho)A_{\mathcal{N}}(x)].

(1)

II.2
Informationally Complete Measurements

Informationally complete (IC) measurements are an important class of quantum measurements
D’Ariano et al. 2004
;
Busch 1991
;
D’Ariano et al. 2005
. Their defining feature is that for an arbitrary quantum state, the measurement statistics is sufficient to uniquely determine that underlying quantum state. In other words, for an informationally complete measurement
A=\{A(x)\}
, if
\text{Tr}[\rho A(x)]=\text{Tr}[\omega A(x)]\penalty\ \forall x\in\Omega_{A}
hold for a pair of quantum states
\rho,\omega\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
then
\rho=\omega
must hold by definition. For a quantum system described by a
d
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, an IC measurement requires at least
d^{2}
POVM elements. Formally, a POVM
A=\{A(x)\}_{x=1}^{N}
where
N\geq d^{2}
, is informationally complete if its elements span the full operator space
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
. Consequently, any operator
X\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
has a decomposition of the form

X=\sum_{x=1}^{N}c_{x}A(x).

(2)

In general, all coefficients
\{c_{x}\}
need not be non-negative, even when
X
itself is positive semidefinite. In general, the POVM elements
\{A(x)\}
of an IC measurement form an overcomplete basis of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
, implying that the decomposition in Eq. (
2
) is not unique. If a measurement is not informationally complete, it is informationally incomplete. A particularly important subclass of IC measurements is
minimal informationally complete measurements
, for which the number of POVM elements is exactly
d^{2}
. In this case, the POVM elements are linearly independent and form a basis for
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
, ensuring that the expansion in Eq. (
2
) is unique. Minimal informationally complete measurements are useful for describing the dynamics of finite dimensional quantum systems using probability distribution
Yashin et al. 2020
.

II.3
Symmetric informationally complete measurements

Within minimal informationally complete measurements, another important subclass is formed by
symmetric informationally complete
(SIC) POVMs. A SIC POVM
A
in dimension
d
consists of
d^{2}
subnormalized rank-one operators
Renes et al. 2004

A(x)=\frac{1}{d}\ket{\psi_{x}}\bra{\psi_{x}},\forall x\in\{1,\ldots,d^{2}\}
, satisfying the symmetry condition
|\langle\psi_{x}|\psi_{y}\rangle|^{2}=\frac{1}{d+1},\qquad\forall\penalty\ x\neq y
, together with the normalisation relation
\sum_{x}A(x)=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}}
. Since a SIC POVM contains exactly
d^{2}
linearly independent POVM elements, it is minimal by construction. SIC POVMs are conjectured to exist in every finite dimension
Renes et al. 2004
;
Samuel and Gedik 2025
. Explicit analytical constructions are known for several dimensions
Appleby et al. 2017
;
Appleby et al. 2018
;
Appleby et al. 2022
;
Bengtsson et al. 2025
, while numerical solutions for higher-dimensional cases have also been reported in the literature
Appleby et al. 2018
;
Scott 2017
.

For qubit systems (
d=2
), a SIC POVM consists of four subnormalized rank-one operators
M(x)=\frac{1}{2}\ket{\psi_{x}}\bra{\psi_{x}},\forall x=\{1,2,3,4\}
, where the corresponding pure states form the vertices of a regular tetrahedron on the Bloch sphere. In terms of Bloch vectors, an SIC measurement can be expressed as

\displaystyle A(x)=\frac{1}{4}(\mathbbm{1}+\textbf{r}(x).\boldsymbol{\sigma}),

(3)

where the Bloch vectors
\textbf{r}(x)\in\mathbbm{R}^{3}
for
x=1,2,3,4
satisfy
\textbf{r}(x).\textbf{r}(y)=-\frac{1}{3}
. Furthermore, since
\sum_{x}A(x)=\mathbbm{1}
, we obtain
\sum_{x}\textbf{r}(x)=0
.

One convenient choice of such Bloch vectors following the vertices of a regular tetrahedron is the following:

\displaystyle\textbf{r}(1)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1,1,1),\qquad\textbf{r}(2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1,-1,-1),

(4)

\displaystyle\textbf{r}(3)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(-1,1,-1),\qquad\textbf{r}(4)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(-1,-1,1).

(5)

II.4
Qubit Channel Parametrization

In the Bloch sphere picture, a qubit density matrix can be written as
\rho=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbbm{1}+\textbf{r}.\boldsymbol{\sigma})\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}_{Q})
, where
\textbf{r}=(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})
is the Bloch vector of the quantum state
\rho
and it satisfies
|\textbf{r}|\leq 1
. Also,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3})
is the vector of three Pauli matrices. One can define a
1\times 4
vector
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\rho}
associated with the quantum state
\rho
such that
(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\rho}):=(1,\textbf{r})
. Then, an arbitrary linear map
\Lambda:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{K})
acting on qubit states can be represented by a real
4\times 4
matrix
\mathbf{T}_{\Lambda}
through

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\Lambda(\rho)}=\mathbf{T}_{\Lambda}(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\rho})=(\mathbf{T}_{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\rho}^{T})^{T},

(6)

where
T
denotes the transpose. If
\Lambda
is a CPTP map, this matrix has the block form
Braun et al. 2014
;
King and Ruskai 2001
;
Beth Ruskai et al. 2002

\displaystyle\textbf{T}_{\Lambda}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0_{1\times 3}\\[5.69054pt]
\textbf{t}^{T}_{\Lambda}&M_{\Lambda}\end{pmatrix},

(7)

where
M_{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
,
\textbf{t}_{\Lambda}=(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Equivalently, the action on the Bloch vector can be written as:

\displaystyle\textbf{r}^{\prime}=M_{\Lambda}(\textbf{r})+\textbf{t}_{\Lambda}.

(8)

where
\textbf{r}^{\prime}
is the block vector of
\Lambda(\rho)
.
For any given matrix
M_{\Lambda}
, there exist two orthogonal matrices
O_{1}
and
O_{2}
such that singular value decomposition of
M_{\Lambda}
can be written as

\displaystyle M_{\Lambda}=O_{1}M_{\Lambda_{D}}O_{2}.

(9)

For any
3\times 3
orthogonal matrix
O
, either
O
(proper rotation) or
-O
(improper rotation) is in
SO(3)
. Accordingly,
M_{\Lambda}=O_{1}M_{\Lambda_{D}}O_{2}
with
M_{\Lambda_{D}}=D
if both
O_{1}
and
O_{2}
are in
SO(3)
, or,
M_{\Lambda_{D}}=-D
if only one of
O_{1}
and
O_{2}
is in
SO(3)
where

\displaystyle D=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&0&0\\
0&\lambda_{2}&0\\
0&0&\lambda_{3}\end{pmatrix}.

(10)

Here
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}
are the signed singular values of
\Lambda
. Since every rotation in
SO(3)
corresponds to a unitary transformation on the qubit Hilbert space, the channel
\Lambda
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Lambda=\mathcal{U}\circ\Lambda_{D}\circ\mathcal{V},

(11)

where
\mathcal{U}(\rho)=U\rho U^{\dagger}
, and
\mathcal{V}(\rho)=V\rho V^{\dagger}
, for suitable unitary operators
U
and
V
, and
\Lambda_{D}
is the quantum channel with diagonal
M_{\Lambda_{D}}
.

II.5
Characterization of Quantum Coherence

For a fixed reference basis
\{\ket{i}\}
of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, a quantum state is said to contain no
coherence
if it is diagonal in that basis. Explicitly, incoherent states are of the form
\omega=\sum_{i}p_{i}\ket{i}\bra{i}
, where
p_{i}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i}p_{i}=1
and the reference basis
\{\ket{i}\}
is known as incoherent basis
Streltsov et al. 2015
;
Baumgratz et al. 2014
. Any quantum state that cannot be expressed in this form possesses a non-zero amount of coherence.

It is important to emphasize that quantum coherence is basis-dependent. A state that is incoherent with respect to one reference basis may become coherent when represented in a different basis.

If
\mathcal{I}
denotes the set of all incoherent density operators, then a CPTP map
\Lambda
is called an incoherent operation if it has Kraus decomposition

\Lambda(\rho)=\sum_{i}K_{i}\rho K_{i}^{\dagger},

(12)

such that,

\frac{K_{i}\rho K_{i}^{\dagger}}{\text{Tr}[K_{i}\rho K_{i}^{\dagger}]}\in\mathcal{I},\quad\forall\rho\in\mathcal{I}.

(13)

Any valid measure of coherence
\mathcal{C}(\rho)
, under the standard axiomatic approach to resource theories, is postulated to satisfy the following properties
Streltsov et al. 2015
;
Streltsov et al. 2017
: (1)
\mathcal{C}(\rho)\geq 0
, with
\mathcal{C}(\rho)=0
iff
\rho\in\mathcal{I}
, (2)
\mathcal{C}(\rho)\geq\mathcal{C}(\Lambda(\rho))
for every incoherent operation
\Lambda
, (3)
\mathcal{C}(\rho)\geq\sum_{i}p_{i}\,\mathcal{C}(\tilde{\rho}_{i})
where
p_{i}=\operatorname{Tr}\left(K_{i}\rho K_{i}^{\dagger}\right)
with

\tilde{\rho}_{i}=\frac{K_{i}\rho K_{i}^{\dagger}}{p_{i}}
, and the Kraus operators satisfy
\frac{K_{i}\omega K_{i}^{\dagger}}{\text{Tr}[K_{i}\omega K_{i}^{\dagger}]}\in\mathcal{I}\forall\omega\in\mathcal{I}.
, (4)
\mathcal{C}(\sum_{i}p_{i}\rho_{i})\leq\sum_{i}p_{i}\mathcal{C}(\rho_{i})
.

Any functional satisfying the above properties is referred to as a
coherence monotone
. Several such measures have been proposed in the literature
Streltsov et al. 2017
. One of such measures is the family of distance-based coherence measures, defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\rho)=\min_{\omega\in\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{D}(\rho,\omega),

(14)

where
\mathcal{D}
is a suitable distance measure between quantum states.

Generally, the distance
\mathcal{D}
is required to be contractive under completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) maps and to satisfy the joint convexity property. In other words, for any quantum channel
\Lambda
, and for arbitrary quantum states
\rho,\rho^{\prime},\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
,
\mathcal{D}(\Lambda(\rho),\Lambda(\omega))\leq\mathcal{D}(\rho,\omega)
and
p\mathcal{D}(\rho,\omega)+(1-p)\mathcal{D}(\rho^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\geq\mathcal{D}(p\rho+(1-p)\rho^{\prime},p\omega+(1-p)\omega^{\prime})
must hold.

This condition ensures that
C
satisfies the above-mentioned properties.

II.6
Optical Master Equation

The interaction of a quantum system with the environment results in dissipation and decoherence. In the open system framework, after tracing out the environmental degrees of freedom, the system evolution is generally non-unitary. Such dynamics are typically irreversible and are governed by master equations. The solution of such a master equation is called a dynamical map which is a set of quantum channels parametrized by time.

Both the quantum system and the environment evolve under the total Hamiltonian of the form

H_{\mathrm{tot}}=H_{S}+H_{B}+H_{I},

(15)

where
H_{S}
is the system Hamiltonian,
H_{B}
is the bath Hamiltonian, and
H_{I}
is the interaction Hamiltonian. The interaction is of the form,

H_{I}=\sum_{\alpha}A_{\alpha}\otimes B_{\alpha},

(16)

where
A_{\alpha}
are system operators and
B_{\alpha}
are bath operators.
.

For a system weakly coupled to a large bath, under Born, Markov, and rotating-wave approximations, the evolution of the system is governed by the Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad (GKSL) equation of the form
Breuer and Petruccione 2002

\frac{d\rho}{dt}=-i[H_{\mathrm{eff}},\rho]+\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}\left(L_{k}\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{k}^{\dagger}L_{k},\rho\}\right),

(17)

where
H_{\mathrm{eff}}
is an effective Hamiltonian
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
,
L_{k}
are Lindblad (or jump) operators,
\gamma_{k}\geq 0
are decay rates, and
\{X,Y\}=XY+YX
denotes the anticommutator.

A common model for the bath is a continuum of harmonic oscillators,

H_{B}=\sum_{\mu}\omega_{\mu}b_{\mu}^{\dagger}b_{\mu},

(18)

where
b_{\mu}
and
b_{\mu}^{\dagger}
are bosonic annihilation and creation operators satisfying

[b_{\mu},b_{\nu}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{\mu\nu},\qquad[b_{\mu},b_{\nu}]=[b_{\mu}^{\dagger},b_{\nu}^{\dagger}]=0.

(19)

In quantum optical systems, a system is considered to be an atom or a molecule, while the environment is typically modeled as a continuum of electromagnetic field modes. The interaction between the system and the environment is given by dipole interaction as
Breuer and Petruccione 2002

\displaystyle H_{I}=-\mathbf{D}.\mathbf{E},

(20)

where
\mathbf{D}
is the dipole operator of the system while
\mathbf{E}
is the electric field operator of the environment. The interaction between the system and the reservoir gives rise to irreversible processes such as spontaneous emission, absorption, and dephasing.

For example, consider a two-level system with ground states
\ket{0}
and excited state
\ket{1}
. The Pauli operators can be written as

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}=\ket{1}\bra{0}+\ket{1}\bra{0},

\displaystyle\quad\sigma_{2}=-i\ket{1}\bra{0}+i\ket{1}\bra{0},

\displaystyle\quad\sigma_{3}=

\displaystyle\ket{1}\bra{1}-\ket{0}\bra{0}

(21)

.
The system Hamiltonian is thus given as
H_{S}=\frac{1}{2}\omega_{10}\sigma_{3}
, where
\omega_{10}
is the transition frequency for the given two-level system. Neglecting the free evolution part, the optical master equation can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{d\rho(t)}{dt}=\gamma_{0}(N+1)\left(\sigma_{-}\rho(t)\sigma_{+}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\sigma_{+}\sigma_{-},\rho(t)\right\}\right)

\displaystyle+\gamma_{0}N\left(\sigma_{+}\rho(t)\sigma_{-}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\sigma_{-}\sigma_{+},\rho(t)\right\}\right).

(22)

Here,
N=\left(\exp\left(\frac{\hbar\omega_{10}}{k_{B}T}\right)-1\right)^{-1}
is the Planck distribution, where
k_{B}
denotes the Boltzmann constant,
T
is the temperature of the thermal bath and

\displaystyle\sigma_{+}=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{1}+i\sigma_{2}),\qquad\sigma_{-}=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{1}-i\sigma_{2})

(23)

The parameter
\gamma_{0}
is the spontaneous emission rate, and

\gamma=\gamma_{0}(2N+1)

(24)

is the total emission rate, incorporating both thermally induced emission and absorption processes. For more detail, we refer the reader to
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
.

III.1
Quantifying informational completeness quantum measurements

Here, we investigate the quantification of the informational completeness of a quantum measurement. We begin with the following definition:

Definition 1
.

Given a quantum measurement

A=\{A(x)\}_{x=1}^{n}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})
, we define its informational completeness as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}(A):=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})\\
\rho\neq\rho^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\sum_{x\in\Omega_{A}}\Big|\text{Tr}[\rho A(x)]-\text{Tr}[\rho^{\prime}A(x)]\Big|}{||\rho-\rho^{\prime}||_{1}},

(25)

where
||.||_{1}
denotes the trace norm.

In Eq. (
25
), infimum is used instead of minimum as
\rho^{\prime}
can vary in the set
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})\setminus\rho
which is not close and therefore, cannot be compact where the symbol “
\setminus
” denotes the different between two sets. Now, we begin with proving the unitary invariance of the above measure.

Lemma 1
.

For an arbitrary measurement
A\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})
, its informational completeness
\mathfrak{D}(A)
is unitary invariant, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}(U^{\dagger}AU)=\mathfrak{D}(A),

(26)

for any unitary matrix
U
.

Proof.

From Eq. (
25
), we can write

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}(U^{\dagger}AU)=

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})\\
\rho\neq\rho^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\sum_{x\in\Omega_{A}}\Big|\text{Tr}[\rho U^{\dagger}A(x)U]-\text{Tr}[\rho^{\prime}U^{\dagger}A(x)U]\Big|}{||\rho-\rho^{\prime}||_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})\\
\rho\neq\rho^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\sum_{x\in\Omega_{A}}\Big|\text{Tr}[U\rho U^{\dagger}A(x)]-\text{Tr}[U\rho^{\prime}U^{\dagger}A(x)]\Big|}{||\rho-\rho^{\prime}||_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})\\
\rho\neq\rho^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\sum_{x\in\Omega_{A}}\Big|\text{Tr}[U\rho U^{\dagger}A(x)]-\text{Tr}[U\rho^{\prime}U^{\dagger}A(x)]\Big|}{||U(\rho-\rho^{\prime})U^{\dagger}||_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})\\
\rho\neq\rho^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\sum_{x\in\Omega_{A}}\Big|\text{Tr}[U\rho U^{\dagger}A(x)]-\text{Tr}[U\rho^{\prime}U^{\dagger}A(x)]\Big|}{||U\rho U^{\dagger}-U\rho^{\prime}U^{\dagger}||_{1}},

(27)

where in the second line we have used the cyclicity of the trace, in the third line we have used the fact that the trace norm of an operator is invariant under unitary conjugation. Now, an arbitrary
\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
,
if and only if

\rho_{U}:=U\rho U^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
and
\rho-\rho^{\prime}\neq 0\iff\rho_{U}-\rho^{\prime}_{U}\neq 0
for arbitrary
\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
, and for an arbitrary unitary matrix
U
, as unitaries are invertible. Thus, we can write

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}(U^{\dagger}AU)=

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho_{U},\rho_{U}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})\\
\rho_{U}\neq\rho_{U}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\sum_{x\in\Omega_{A}}\Big|\text{Tr}[\rho_{U}A(x)]-\text{Tr}[\rho_{U}^{\prime}A(x)]\Big|}{||\rho_{U}-\rho_{U}^{\prime}||_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}(A).

(28)

∎

Now, we prove the faithfulness of this measure in the following proposition:

Proposition 1
.

For a generic quantum measurement
B=\{B(x)\}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})
, the informational completeness
\mathfrak{D}(B)\geq 0
and
\mathfrak{D}(B)=0
if and only if
B
is informationally incomplete.

Proof.

From Eq. (
25
), it is clear that both numerator and denominator are non-negative. Hence,
\mathfrak{D}(B)\geq 0
for an arbitrary measurement
B
. Now,
B=\{B(x)\}
is informationally incomplete then by definition there exists a pair of state
\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
such that
\text{Tr}[\rho A(x)]=\text{Tr}[\rho^{\prime}A(x)]\penalty\ \forall x]\in\Omega_{A}
. Hence, the numerator
\sum_{x}|\text{Tr}[(\rho-\rho^{\prime})A(x)]|=0
. But as
\rho\neq\rho^{\prime}
denominator cannot be zero. Hence,
\mathfrak{D}(B)=0
.

Now, we have to prove that if
\mathfrak{D}(B)=0
then
B
is informationally incomplete. At first, we observe that if
X\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
is a traceless Hermitian operator, i.e.,
X^{\dagger}=X
and
\text{Tr}[X]=0
such that
||X||_{1}=1
then there exists a pair of states
\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
such that
X=\frac{\rho-\rho^{\prime}}{||\rho-\rho^{\prime}||_{1}}
where
\rho\neq\rho^{\prime}
. We use this
observation
in the following steps:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}(B)=

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})\\
\rho\neq\rho^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\sum_{x}\Big|\text{Tr}[\rho B(x)]-\text{Tr}[\rho^{\prime}B(x)]\Big|}{||\rho-\rho^{\prime}||_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})\\
\rho\neq\rho^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\sum_{x}\Big|\text{Tr}[Y(\rho,\rho^{\prime})B(x)]\Big|}{||Y(\rho,\rho^{\prime})||_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})\\
\rho\neq\rho^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\sum_{x}\Big|\text{Tr}[X(\rho,\rho^{\prime})B(x)]\Big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}),X^{\dagger}=X\\
\text{Tr}[X]=0,||X||_{1}=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{x}\Big|\text{Tr}[XB(x)]\Big|

(29)

where in the second equality
Y(\rho,\rho^{\prime})=\rho-\rho^{\prime}
, in third equality
X(\rho,\rho^{\prime})=\frac{Y(\rho,\rho^{\prime})}{||Y(\rho,\rho^{\prime})||_{1}}
(therefore,
X(\rho,\rho^{\prime})^{\dagger}=X(\rho,\rho^{\prime})
,
\text{Tr}[X(\rho,\rho^{\prime})]=0
, and
||X(\rho,\rho^{\prime})||_{1}=1
) and in fourth equality we have used above-mentioned observation and the fact that the set
\mathbbm{X}(\mathcal{H}):=\{X\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})|X^{\dagger}=X,\text{Tr}[X]=0,||X||_{1}=1\}
is compact (therefore, one can replace infimum with minimum). Note that
||X||_{1}=1\Rightarrow X\neq 0\penalty\ \forall X\in\mathbbm{X}(\mathcal{H})
. Now, if
\mathfrak{D}(B)=0
then there exists an
X^{*}\in\mathbbm{X}(\mathcal{H})
such that
\sum_{x}\Big|\text{Tr}[X^{*}B(x)]\Big|=0
which implies
\text{Tr}[X^{*}B(x)]=0\forall\penalty\ x\in\Omega_{B}
which implies that the set
\{B(x)\}
cannot span
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
as
X\neq 0
. Hence,
B=\{B(x)\}
is informationally incomplete.

∎

From Proposition
1
, we conclude that the measure
\mathfrak{D}
is faithful and non-negative. Next, let us focus our attention on the qubit SIC measurements.

Theorem 1
.

If a qubit measurement

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}(A)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}.

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\textbf{s}-\textbf{s}^{\prime}).\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\frac{\textbf{d}.\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{2},

(31)

\textbf{d}=\textbf{s}-\textbf{s}^{\prime}\in\mathbbm{R}^{3}

\displaystyle||\rho-\rho^{\prime}||_{1}=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\Big[\sqrt{(\rho-\rho^{\prime})^{\dagger}(\rho-\rho^{\prime})}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\text{Tr}[\sqrt{(\textbf{d}.\boldsymbol{\sigma})(\textbf{d}.\boldsymbol{\sigma})}]}{2}

\displaystyle\frac{\text{Tr}[|\textbf{d}|\mathbbm{1}_{Q}]}{2}=|\textbf{d}|.

\displaystyle\frac{\text{Tr}[\sqrt{(\textbf{d}.\boldsymbol{\sigma})(\mathbbm{1}+\textbf{r}(x).\boldsymbol{\sigma})}]}{8},\quad\forall\penalty\ x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\text{Tr}[\sqrt{(\textbf{d}.\boldsymbol{\sigma})(\textbf{r}(x).\boldsymbol{\sigma})}]}{8}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}(A)=

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{|\textbf{d}|}=1}\frac{1}{4}\sum_{x}\Big|\hat{\textbf{d}}.\textbf{r}(x)\Big|

Since

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\hat{\textbf{d}}.\textbf{r}(x)=0.

Separating the above sum into positive and negative contributions gives

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\\
\hat{\textbf{d}}.\textbf{r}(x)\geq 0\end{subarray}}\hat{\textbf{d}}.\textbf{r}(x)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\\
\hat{\textbf{d}}.\textbf{r}(x)<0\end{subarray}}\hat{\textbf{d}}.\textbf{r}(x)=0.

(36)

Define
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\\
\hat{\textbf{d}}\textbf{r}(x)\geq 0\end{subarray}}\hat{\textbf{d}}.\textbf{r}(x):=p\geq 0

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\Big|\hat{\textbf{d}}.\textbf{r}(x)\Big|=2p.

(37)

Now consider

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\Big(\hat{\textbf{d}}.\textbf{r}(x)\Big)^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\sum_{x}\hat{\textbf{d}}_{i}\hat{\textbf{d}}_{j}\textbf{r}_{i}(x)\textbf{r}_{j}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\Big(\sum_{i}\hat{\textbf{d}}_{i}^{2}(\textbf{r}_{i}(x))^{2}+2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\\
i<j\end{subarray}}\hat{\textbf{d}}_{i}\hat{\textbf{d}}_{j}\textbf{r}_{i}(x)\textbf{r}_{j}(x)\Big).

\displaystyle\,\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\\

(47)

∎

Theorem 2

Let

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}(A)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}},

where inequality is saturated when

Proof.

Consider a general four outcome qubit measurement qubit measurement

, which can be written as

\displaystyle A(x)=\frac{1}{2}\Big(a(x)+\textbf{b}(x).\boldsymbol{\sigma}\Big),\penalty\ \forall x.

\displaystyle\sum_{x}a(x)=2,\penalty\ \sum_{x}\textbf{b}(x)=0,\penalty\ |\textbf{b}(x)|\leq a(x),\penalty\ \forall\penalty\ x.

(50)

Note that if

is informationally incomplete

and hence, the theorem is trivially satisfied. Therfore, we assume

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}(A)=\min_{\hat{|\textbf{d}|}=1}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x}\Big|\hat{\textbf{d}}.\textbf{b}(x)\Big|=:\frac{\Delta}{2}.

(51)

Clearly, here,
\Delta=\min_{\hat{|\textbf{d}|}=1}\sum_{x}\Big|\hat{\textbf{d}}.\textbf{b}(x)\Big|
. Now choose
\hat{\textbf{d}}

\displaystyle\hat{\textbf{d}}\perp\textbf{b}(i),\textbf{b}(j),

(52)

for some

\hat{\textbf{d}}.\textbf{b}(j)=0

Using

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\hat{\textbf{d}}.\textbf{b}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{\textbf{d}}.\textbf{b}(k)+\hat{\textbf{d}}.\textbf{b}(l)

\displaystyle\hat{\textbf{d}}.\textbf{b}(k)=-\hat{\textbf{d}}.\textbf{b}(l),

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\Big|\hat{\textbf{d}}.\textbf{b}(x)\Big|=2\Big|\hat{\textbf{d}}.\textbf{b}(k)\Big|.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Delta\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\Big|\hat{\textbf{d}}.\textbf{b}(x)\Big|=2\Big|\hat{\textbf{d}}.\textbf{b}(k)\Big|=\Big|\hat{\textbf{d}}.(\textbf{b}(k)-\textbf{b}(l))\Big|

k<l\end{subarray}}|\textbf{b}(k)-\textbf{b}(l)|^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,l\\
k<l\end{subarray}}(|\textbf{b}(k)|^{2}+|\textbf{b}(l)|^{2}-2\textbf{b}(k).\textbf{b}(l))

\displaystyle 3\sum_{x=1}^{4}|\textbf{b}(x)|^{2}-2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,l\\
k<l\end{subarray}}\textbf{b}(k).\textbf{b}(l).

Moreover,

\displaystyle 0=\Big|\sum_{x}\textbf{b}(x)\Big|^{2}=

k<l\end{subarray}}\textbf{b}(k).\textbf{b}(l)=-\frac{\sum_{x}|\textbf{b}(x)|^{2}}{2}.

Substituting Eq. (

We now determine when the inequalities are saturated. From Eq. (

\displaystyle\textbf{b}(k).\sum_{x}\textbf{b}(x)=

\displaystyle 0,\qquad\forall\penalty\ k

\displaystyle 0,\qquad\forall\penalty\ k.

Using Eq. (

\displaystyle d=

Finally, from Eqs. (

III.2
Informational completeness preservability of Quantum Channels

In this section, we explore the quantification of the informational completeness preservability of a quantum channel. We begin with the following definition:

Definition 2
.

Given a quantum channel
\Lambda\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H})
, we define its informational completeness preservability (or IC- preservability) as

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}(\Lambda):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}A=\{A(x)\}\\
A\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})\end{subarray}}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})\\
\rho\neq\rho^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\sum_{x}\Big|\text{Tr}[\rho\Lambda^{\dagger}(A(x))]-\text{Tr}[\rho^{\prime}\Lambda^{\dagger}(A(x))]\Big|}{||\rho-\rho^{\prime}||_{1}}.

(73)

Note that Eq. (
73
) can equivalently be written as

\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}(\Lambda)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}A=\{A(x)\},A\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{D}(\Lambda^{\dagger}(A)).

(74)

Remark 1
.

From Eq. (
74
), using Lemma
1
and the fact that for any given measurement
A=\{A(x)\}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})
and a unitary conjugation channel
\mathcal{V}
,
\mathcal{V}(A)=\{VA(x)V^{\dagger}\}
, where
V
is a unitary matrix, is also a valid measurement, it immediately follows that the IC-preservibility of a quantum channel is invariant under unitary pre and post-processing. That is,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}(\Lambda)=\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}(\mathcal{U}\circ\Lambda\circ\mathcal{V}),

(75)

where
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
denote unitary conjugation channels.

Proposition 2
.

For a generic quantum channel
\Lambda\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H})
, its IC-preservability
\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}(\Lambda)\geq 0
and
\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}(\Lambda)=0
if and only if
\Lambda^{\dagger}(A(x)
is informationally incomplete for all
A=\{A(x)\}\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})
.

Proof.

As
\Lambda
is a quantum channel,
\Lambda^{\dagger}(A)
is a valid measurement for any valid measurement
A\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})
. From Eq. (
74
) and Proposition
1
we observe that

\displaystyle\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(\Lambda)=

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}A=\{A(x)\},A\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{D}(\Lambda^{\dagger}(A))

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}(\Lambda^{\dagger}(B))

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 0,

(76)

where
B\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})
is an arbitrary measurement. Next, if
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(\Lambda)=0
then as
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}(\Lambda)\geq 0
, we get
\mathfrak{D}(\Lambda^{\dagger}(A))=0
for any
A\in\mathscr{M}(\mathcal{H})
, From Proposition
1
we can conclude that
\Lambda^{\dagger}(A)
is informationally incomplete for any
A\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{H})
. Conversely, if
\Lambda^{\dagger}(A)
is informationally incomplete for any
A\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{H})
then, again from Proposition
1
we get
\mathfrak{D}(\Lambda^{\dagger}(A))=0
for any
A\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{H})
. Hence, from Eq. (
74
), we have

III.3
Absolute output coherence of Quantum Channels and its relation with IC preservability

In this section, to establish a conceptual relationship between information completeness and quantum coherence, w define another important quantity, namely the absolute output coherence of a quantum channel as:

Definition 3
.

Given a qubit channel
\Lambda
, we define its
absolute output coherence
as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}(\Lambda):=\min_{E=\{\ket{e},\ket{e^{\perp}}\}}\max_{\rho}\mathcal{C}_{E}(\Lambda(\rho)),

(103)

where
\mathcal{C}_{E}
denotes a valid measure of quantum coherence
Streltsov et al. 2017
w.r.t. an arbitrarily chosen incoherent basis
E=\{\ket{e},\ket{e^{\perp}}\}
.

Remark 2
.

\mathfrak{C}
refers to the minimum amount of coherence (w.r.t. respect of an arbitrarily chosen incoherent basis) that can be obtained from the output of a given qubit channel for a suitable input state. This definition can be generalized for quantum channels acting on an arbitrary finite-dimensional Hilbert space. Furthermore, just like the IC-preservability of a channel, from the above definition, it follows immediately that the absolute output coherence is also invariant under unitary pre and post-processing. Explicitly,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}(\Lambda)=\mathfrak{C}(\mathcal{U}\circ\Lambda\circ\mathcal{V}),

(104)

where
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
are unitary channels. Now, it is easily seen that for unitary channels,
\mathfrak{C}
is maximum and equal to that of identity channel. It is also easy to see that
\mathfrak{C}
is zero for the completely dephasing channel w.r.t. an arbitrary basis. Furthermore, note that
\min
function and
\max
function do not commute as it can be easily seen that
\max_{\rho}\min_{E=\{\ket{e},\ket{e^{\perp}}\}}\mathcal{C}_{E}(\Lambda(\rho))=0
by choosing
E
as the eigen basis of
\Lambda(\rho)
.

Note that in Eq. (
103
), the coherence measure can be arbitrarily chosen, in general. From now on we restrict ourselves to distance-based coherence measurement defined in Eq. (
14
) where the distance between two arbitrary qubit states
\rho,\rho^{\prime}
is chosen to be
||\rho-\rho^{\prime}||_{1}
. We now establish the bounds on the absolute output coherence of a quantum channel.

Lemma 4
.

For a given qubit channel
\Lambda
, its absolute output coherence is lower-bounded as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}(\Lambda)\geq|\lambda_{2}|

(105)

where the signed singular values associated with the given channel
\Lambda
satisfies the ordering
|\lambda_{max}|=|\lambda_{1}|\geq|\lambda_{2}|\geq|\lambda_{3}|=|\lambda_{min}|
.

Proof.

As mentioned above, the measure of coherence
Streltsov et al. 2015
;
Streltsov et al. 2017
is chosen to be

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{E}(\Lambda(\rho))=\min_{\rho^{\prime}_{E}\in\mathcal{I}_{E}}\mathcal{D}(\Lambda(\rho),\rho^{\prime}_{E}),

(106)

where
\mathcal{I}_{E}
denotes the set of incoherent states with respect to the basis
E=\{\ket{e},\ket{e^{\perp}}\}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}_{E}=p\ket{e}\bra{e}+(1-p)\ket{e^{\perp}}\bra{e^{\perp}}.

(107)

In this work, we choose the distance measure
\mathcal{D}
to be the trace distance. Explicitly,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\Lambda(\rho),\rho^{\prime}_{E})=||\Lambda(\rho)-\rho^{\prime}_{E}||_{1}.

(108)

Consequently, the absolute output coherence of an arbitrary quantum channel can be written as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}(\Lambda):=\min_{E=\{\ket{e},\ket{e^{\perp}}\}}\max_{\rho}\min_{\rho^{\prime}_{E}\in\mathcal{I}}||\Lambda(\rho)-\rho^{\prime}_{E}||_{1}.

(109)

Using Eq. (
90
), the trace distance can be expressed as

\displaystyle||\Lambda(\rho)-\rho^{\prime}_{E}||_{1}=|(M_{\Lambda_{D}}(\textbf{s})+\textbf{t})-\textbf{s}^{\prime}_{E}|,

(110)

where
s
and
\textbf{s}^{\prime}_{E}
denote the Bloch vectors corresponding to
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}_{E}
, respectively.

Now, if

\displaystyle\ket{e}\bra{e}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbbm{1}+\hat{\textbf{e}}\cdot\sigma}{2},

(111)

\displaystyle\ket{e^{\perp}}\bra{e^{\perp}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbbm{1}-\hat{\textbf{e}}\cdot\sigma}{2},

(112)

\displaystyle\textbf{s}_{E}=(2p-1)\hat{\textbf{e}}.

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}(\Lambda):=\min_{\hat{\textbf{e}}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{s}\\

where

\displaystyle|(M_{\Lambda_{D}}

\displaystyle(\textbf{s})+\textbf{t})-(2p-1)\hat{\textbf{e}}|

\displaystyle 2|\mathsf{P}_{\hat{\textbf{e}}^{\perp}}(M_{\Lambda_{D}}

\hat{\textbf{w}}
denote the unit vector perpendicular to it. We can always find a unit vector of the form
\hat{\textbf{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2},0)
such that projection of

in
1-2
plane is orthogonal to
M_{\Lambda_{D}}(\hat{\textbf{x}})=(\lambda_{1}x_{1},\lambda_{2}x_{2},0)

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{s}\\
|\textbf{s}|\leq 1\end{subarray}}|\mathsf{P}_{W}(M_{\Lambda_{D}}(\textbf{s}))|

\displaystyle\geq|\mathsf{P}_{W}(M_{\Lambda_{D}}(\hat{\textbf{x}}))|

\displaystyle=|M_{\Lambda_{D}}(\hat{\textbf{x}})-(M_{\Lambda_{D}}(\hat{\textbf{x}}).\hat{\textbf{w}})\hat{\textbf{w}}|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{|M_{\Lambda_{D}}(\hat{\textbf{x}})|^{2}-(M_{\Lambda_{D}}(\hat{\textbf{x}}).\hat{\textbf{w}})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\lambda_{1}^{2}x_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2}x_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq|\lambda_{2}|\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=|\lambda_{2}|.

(125)

Since this holds for an arbitrary plane
W
, it holds for the minimum
\hat{\textbf{e}}
in Eq. (
132

\hat{\textbf{e}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle\geq\min_{\hat{\textbf{e}}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{s}\\
|\textbf{s}|\leq 1\end{subarray}}|\mathsf{P}_{\hat{\textbf{e}}^{\perp}}(M_{\Lambda_{D}}(\textbf{s}))|

\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{s}\\
|\textbf{s}|\leq 1\end{subarray}}|\mathsf{P}_{\hat{\textbf{e}}^{\prime\perp}}(M_{\Lambda_{D}}(\textbf{s}))|

\displaystyle\geq|\lambda_{2}|.

(126)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathfrak{D}}(\Lambda)\leq\mathfrak{C}(\Lambda).

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}(\Lambda)\leq\max_{i\in\{1,2,3\}}|\lambda_{i}|=:\lambda_{max}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that the signed singular values associated with the given channel
\Lambda

\lambda_{max}=\lambda_{1}
.

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}(\Lambda)=

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\textbf{e}}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textbf{s}\\

IV
Conclusion

In this work, we have tried to characterize and quantify the informational completeness of qubit measurements by introducing a faithful measure for it and then studying some of its properties. We have explicitly calculated its numerical value for an arbitrary qubit SIC-POVM and have demonstrated that the numerical value of this
informational completeness
measure for any qubit minimal IC measurement is upper bounded by that of an SIC. Next, for an arbitrary channel, we have defined a quantity called
informational completeness-preservability
that quantifies its ability to preserve informational completeness for any IC measurement when it acts on it in the Heisenberg picture. Just as in the case of
informational completeness
, we proved certain useful properties of it. We then calculated bounds on the
informational completeness-preservability
of a channel in terms of signed singular values associated with that quantum channel. Then, we have defined and studied
absolute output coherence
of the given channel and have demonstrated that the
absolute output coherence
of a qubit channel is lower bounded by its
informational completeness-preservability
, thus establishing a conceptual relationship between informational completeness and quantum coherence. Finally, we have illustrated our results with examples from light-matter interaction scenarios.

Our work opens up several avenues for future research. The majority of the results in this work are proved for qubit systems, and therefore, it would be interesting to generalize the results for higher-dimensional systems. It is also important to investigate how the measure of
informational completeness
relates to performance in some quantum information-theoretic tasks. It would also be interesting to study the further characterization of
absolute output coherence
for higher-dimensional systems as well.
