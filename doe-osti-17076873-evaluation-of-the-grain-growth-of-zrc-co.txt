 ABSTRACT

An important challenge in space nuclear propulsion is the development of fuels and materials that can
withstand temperatures up to 2,400°C with flowing hydrogen. Zirconium carbide has been selected as a
possible candidate for use in coated particle fuels, ceramic matrix composites, and environmental barrier
coatings in this application. Initial tests at high temperature showed considerable grain growth of ZrC
coatings produced by chemical vapor deposition. Because this change in microstructure could have an
important effect on the physical properties and performance of this material, it is important to understand
how ZrC changes with temperature and time and whether there are microstructures or compositions more
resilient to these extreme conditions.

In this work, two sets of studies were performed designed to provide a greater understanding of the grain
growth of ZrC. In the first experiments, two types of ZrC with similar microstructures were heat treated at
1,900°C for up to 5 h. Grain size, texture, grain boundary character, and grain boundary orientation were
studied using electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD), and composition was obtained using Raman
spectroscopy. This first set of experiments showed that grain size and grain boundary character had
limited effect on ZrC grain growth. Conversely, the sample with higher concentration of localized excess
carbon, as detected by Raman spectroscopy, had lower grain growth (see Attachment). In the second set
of tests, the Out-of-Pile Surrogate Testbed for Extreme Temperatures was used to heat treat four ZrC
coatings at 2,200°C for 2 h under argon. EBSD was conducted to evaluate both the initial and evolved
microstructures—specifically, the grain size, grain boundary character, and orientation. Samples with
varying stoichiometries were selected to determine the effect of composition on the growth rate of the
samples. Once again, the ZrC with localized excess carbon was identified as the most resilient to grain
growth, having similar grain size to the ZrC in the lower-temperature (1,900°C), longer-duration (5 h)
test. This investigation provided insight into how the as-fabricated composition and microstructure of the
ZrC layer evolves under high temperatures and helped identify a ZrC more resilient to grain growth.

 1. INTRODUCTION

There is an interest in developing nuclear thermal propulsion for deep space exploration and for traveling
to Mars due to double to triple efficiency in specific impluse in comparison with tradiational chemical
rockets. Nuclear thermal propulsion rockets operate by flowing hydrogen through a superheated (>
2,000°C) nuclear reactor core by using the heat from the reactor to energize the propellant and expel it out
the nozzle. The combination of extreme temperatures, corrosive hot hydrogen, and radiation
embrittlement causes the reactor core materials to corrode and degrade rapidly. Additionally, nuclear
thermal rockets ramp in power and temperature in less than 1 min, going from around 20°C to over
2,200°C. One important challenge in space nuclear propulsion (SNP) is developing materials that can
withstand multiple restarts and short burns at 2,400°C with flowing hydrogen. During the Nuclear Engine
for Rocket Vehicle Applications (NERVA) project, several ceramic and metallic coatings were tested to
protect fuel against mass loss. Among these materials, ZrC was identified as a possible candidate for this
application.

Zirconium carbide has promising thermomechanical and corrosion properties that would make it a
suitable candidate to protect the fuel elements [1, 2]. The fabrication processes for ZrC using chemical
vapor deposition can result in varying stoichiometries and microstructures due to perfectly stoichiometric
ZrC existing in a narrow range [3]. Depending on this initial composition, the mechanical properties can
be weakened, making the coating more susceptible to failure [3]. This adds to complexity in determining
which ZrC is optimal for the coating application. The temperature range (T > 1,900°C) has shown grain
growth in ZrC, and the addition of rapid thermal ramps can induce significant stress on the coatings,

 1

leading to potential failures. Testing the ZrC under transient conditions is crucial to determining the
adequate coating for SNP applications.

The investigation presented explores the effect of high temperature on the microstructures of four ZrC
coatings with different initial C/Zr ratios. The first set of experiments, included as a journal paper in
Appendix A, studied the grain growth of two ZrC coatings heat treated at 1,900°C for up to 5 h. In the
second set of tests, four ZrC coatings were heat treated up to 2,200°C for 2 h. Changes in microstructure
and grain boundary character and composition were studied using electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD)
and Raman spectroscopy. These studies provided a deeper understanding of ZrC grain growth and helped
identify microstructures and compositions more resilient to high temperatures.

 2. EXPERIMENTAL METHOD

2.1 MATERIALS

Four sample sets of particles were selected to compare the relative stoichiometric compositions to
determine the grain growth. The samples were previously fabricated at Oak Ridge National Laboratory
via fluidized bed chemical vapor deposition (FB-CVD). The selected samples each had different
fabrication conditions that were suspected to contribute to the different growth behavior [4, 5]. The
particle design was composed of 524 µm diameter yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) kernels, a 49.8 µm
dense pyrolytic carbon layer, and (lastly) a ZrC layer with an average thickness of 21 µm [4]. The
reported C/Zr ratio is presented for each sample in Table 1.

2.2 HEAT TREATMENT

The particles were first placed in individual graphite crucibles with a ZrC powder bed. The inner
crucibles containing the samples were then placed into the outer graphite crucibles, sealed, and then
embedded into the graphite foam of the furnace, as shown in Figure 1. After the assembly of the Out-of-
Pile Surrogate Testbed for Extreme Temperatures (OUTSET) furnace, samples were placed under
vacuum to eliminate oxidation during testing. The furnace was then ramped from room temperature to
around 2,200°C in under 5 min and was held at that temperature for 30 min. After the isothermal hold, the
furnace was turned off and allowed to cool to room temperature. This thermal cycle was repeated three
more times for a total of 2 h. This heat treatment was intended to represent the sample going through four
nuclear thermal propulsion engine burns.

2.3 EBSD ANALYSIS

EBSD was performed using a TESCAN MIRA3 XMH SEM with an EDAX Velocity EBSD detector.
EBSD maps were acquired with step sizes ranging from 0.05 µm to 0.2 µm due to varying grain size.
Data analysis was performed using MATLAB’s MTEX microstructural analysis toolbox [6]. A 5°
misorientation threshold was used to segment grains from the raw data.

After heat treatment, the relative differences between the coatings are significant. As shown in Table 2,
after heat treatment, samples 21, 35, 41, and 43 show mean grain areas of 9.265, 14.24, 13.67, and
18.79 µm2, respectively. Here, there is a clear trend in grain growth that corresponds to the C/Zr
stoichiometric ratio. ZrC-21—which is the most off-stoichiometric, Zr-rich sample (C/Zr = 0.78)—
exhibits the slowest grain growth of all characterized coatings. Conversely, the most stoichiometric
coating, ZrC-43 (C/Zr = 0.89), exhibits the fastest grain growth by far. These two coatings were
characterized previously under different heat treatment conditions, and these results are consistent with
this past work [7]. Both ZrC-35 and ZrC-41, for which grain growth had not previously been
characterized, have nearly identical C/Zr ratios (0.84 and 0.85, respectively) and correspondingly exhibit
nearly identical intermediate grain growth rates between the two stoichiometric extremes in this study.
Overall, these results suggest that the grain growth of ZrC particle fuel coatings under very-high-
temperature conditions is dictated by stoichiometric composition.

Figure 3 summarizes the grain boundary characterization in each ZrC coating before and after heat
treatment. As shown in Figure 3a, samples ZrC-21 and ZrC-43 exhibit a strong preference for grain
boundaries misoriented about the [111] axis, at roughly 4 multiples of a random distribution (MRD).
These samples were characterized previously, and this boundary character, in conjunction with the
prevalence of 60° misorientation in the as-fabricated coatings (Figure 3d) misorientation angle
distribution, is characteristic of Σ3 coincident site lattice (CSL) grain boundaries, which are characterized
by a 60° rotation about the [111] axis in face-centered cubic materials. Interestingly, the [111] grain
boundary character is also present for ZrC-35 and ZrC-41, though it is less frequent than in the former
two coatings (~2.5 MRD). Similar to the as-fabricated grain areas, based on this result, there is not a clear
correlation between grain boundary twinning and ZrC stoichiometric composition. Despite this limitation,
there is noticeable CSL character in all as-fabricated coatings. Figure 3c shows the number fraction of all
Σ3 CSL grain boundary segments for all samples and the respective changes after heat treatment. As
predicted, ZrC-21 and ZrC-43 have nearly identical and the highest as-fabricated Σ3 fractions; however,
they trend differently with grain growth after heat treatment. The Σ3 fraction decreases by ~70% in
ZrC-21 and only by 55% in ZrC-43. This suggests that the Σ3 CSL boundaries may be more stable in a
near-stoichiometric composition, like ZrC-43, and may be annihilated at a higher rate in an off-
stoichiometric composition, like ZrC-21. For the coatings with very similar stoichiometry, the Σ3 fraction
evolves very similarly; ZrC-35 and ZrC-41 decrease by 40% and 38%, respectively, once again

 5

suggesting a correlation between the stoichiometric C/Zr ratio in ZrC particle fuel coatings and grain
growth behavior at high temperatures.

 4. SUMMARY

Four sample sets were successfully heat treated and remained intact during the four thermal cycles. EBSD
analysis suggests that grain growth is strongly related to the as-fabricated composition. Samples with
similar C/Zr ratios exhibited similar grain growth rates, whereas the more off-stoichiometric samples
differed significantly. In particular, it was observed that localized carbon excess, even in

 6

substoichiometric ZrC, could hinder grain growth. Further work needs to be performed to cover a wider
range of compositions and study the effect of excess carbon on the mechanical properties of these
samples.

Abstract
Interest in coated particle fuel for space nuclear propulsion (SNP) has expanded in recent years
due to successful demonstrations of the resiliency of the coatings to extreme environments. For
SNP applications, the coating layer for the particle design needs to be able to withstand exposure
to high-temperature hydrogen during operating conditions. Zirconium carbide has been proposed
as a protective layer; however, it is important to understand ZrC’s behavior at high temperature to
ensure adequate protection to the fuel. In this study, surrogate ZrC coated particles were heat
treated at 1,900°C for up to 300 min to examine how the microstructure evolves when exposed to
high temperature. Scanning electron microscopy and electron backscatter diffraction were used to
determine grain size and grain boundary character and orientation to determine the degree of
change in the ZrC layer post heat treatment. Raman spectroscopy provided insight as to how the
as-fabricated stoichiometry of each sample contributed to differences in the grain growth behavior.
Despite the as-fabricated samples showing a similar initial grain size and grain boundary character,
the samples with a higher amount of localized excess carbon exhibited smaller grain areas and
slower growth rates when exposed to 1,900°C. This investigation provided insight into how the
as-fabricated microstructure of the ZrC layer (specifically grain size) evolves under high
temperature, as this can affect the performance of the fuel under operating conditions.

1. Introduction
 Nuclear thermal propulsion is a promising candidate for space nuclear propulsion (SNP)
technology. One proposed fuel type for SNP is coated fuel particles, where the design is composed
of an actinide-bearing fuel kernel with a protective coating layer [1, 2]. The fuel type for SNP
applications needs to be able to withstand operating conditions of high temperatures (up to 2,426°C
for multiple burns) and be corrosion resistant to the high-temperature hydrogen propellant [3, 4].
Zirconium carbide is a promising candidate to act as the protective coating layer due to its high
melting point, mechanical strength, and corrosion resistance to fission products [5, 6]. Previous
irradiation and heat treatment studies have shown significant grain growth in the ZrC layer at high
temperatures and that composition could affect the performance of the fuel [7, 8]. Determining the
optimal microstructure and composition of the ZrC for SNP applications is crucial for success
during operation.
 The fabrication of ZrC using chemical vapor deposition can be complex because there is a
narrow range for stoichiometric ZrC and because there are a variety of coating parameters that can
be altered to control the microstructure and composition [9-11]. The mechanical and thermal
properties have been shown to be dependent on the stoichiometry of ZrC—more specifically, the
amount of carbon present [6, 7, 12-15]. High amounts of carbon in the ZrC can potentially limit
the amount of grain growth at high temperatures [6, 7, 16]. Exposure to high temperatures can
induce grain growth in the ZrC, and this can potentially increase the propensity for cracking due
to a reduction in fracture toughness and mechanical strength [6, 17]. It has previously been
observed that ZrC undergoes considerable grain growth when heat treated at temperatures above
1,900°C and that composition might play an important role in controlling the grain growth at these
temperatures [5-8]. However, other factors, such as initial grain size and grain boundary character,
have not been discussed or studied in detail. The evaluation of these factors could provide vital
information in understanding grain growth of ZrC as well as information on the type of
microstructure and composition that could provide better performance at these extreme
temperatures.
 In this study, two different ZrC coatings with varied stoichiometry were heat treated to provide
insight on how the initial microstructure and composition of the ZrC layer evolves at high
temperature. The investigation explored the potential for the as-fabricated microstructure—
including grain size, texture, grain boundary character, and stoichiometry—to alter the grain
growth behavior at high temperature and subsequently affect the performance of the fuel. The ZrC
coated particles were exposed to 1,900°C for varying times to determine how the grains evolve as
a function of time. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and electron backscatter diffraction
(EBSD) analysis were implemented to evaluate the evolved microstructure. Raman spectroscopy
was implemented to identify variations in the as-fabricated stoichiometry to rationalize the
differences in growth rates.

2. Material and Sample Preparation
2.1 Materials
 The ZrC coatings were fabricated at Oak Ridge National Laboratory within a 5 cm inner
diameter fluidized bed chemical vapor deposition (FB-CVD) coating system. The full surrogate
particle fuel architecture comprised (on average) 524 µm diameter yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ)
kernels, initial coating of a dense pyrolytic carbon (PyC) layer with an average thickness of 49.8
µm, and (lastly) a ZrC layer with an average thickness of 21 µm [18]. For both coatings
characterized in this work, the thickness was measured to be consistent, which ensured similar

edge-constraint effects on the grain growth. Further details on the FB-CVD system and the coating
parameters are available in Fray et al. [12]. The selected samples were deposited at 1,500°C with
a gas ratio of 1.2 CH4/Cl2 but with difference in the Zr metal precursor used [12]. The
stoichiometric ratio of carbon to zirconium is a crucial component of characteristic differences in
behavior between different ZrC materials, as observed in previous work [6]. Table 1 shows the
stoichiometric ratios for the two samples considered in this study, which are named ZrC-45C and
ZrC-47C in accordance with their carbon content.

2.2 Sample preparation
 Cross sections of the surrogate particles were prepared for metallographic sample preparation
of the ZrC coatings. Particles were mounted on a Ted Pella PELCO 15000 lapping fixture using
Crystalbond 509 Clear metallographic wax. This fixture enables grinding to a specified distance
using an attached micrometer. The samples were ground to near midplane using 320 grit SiC paper
and then ground to midplane on 600 grit SiC paper. Once the samples were at midplane, the YSZ
kernels were removed using tape, leaving behind a half-shell of inner pyrolytic carbon (IPyC) and
ZrC. Samples were removed from Crystalbond with acetone. Half-shells were mounted in Struers
PolyFast hot mounting material with ground face down. The mount was then cut and remounted
at a 90° angle. Then, the half-shells were ground on a semi-automatic grinder/polisher using a
9 µm diamond suspension and Struers DP-Lubricant Yellow on a Struers MD-Allegro disc.
Grinding continued until the half-shell was at midplane, resulting in a quarter-shell to ensure that
measurements were taken at midplane. Samples were ground on a separate MD-Allegro disc with
6 µm diamond suspension. Samples were then polished on an MD-Largo disc with 3 µm diamond
suspension and lubricant until an even plane was reached. A final polishing step of 0.05 µm
colloidal silica solution on a Struers MD-Chem polishing disc. After each grinding or polishing
step, samples were cleaned in an ultrasonic bath of isopropanol for 3 min.

2.3 Sample characterization
 Samples were heat treated in a Netzsch F3 449 Jupiter simultaneous thermal analyzer (STA)
(Netzsch; Selb, DE) in a graphite crucible over a graphite powder bed. Isothermal testing was
conducted at 1,900°C under flowing argon with a ramp rate of 20°C/min. The STA was then
allowed to cool to room temperature. The samples underwent heat treatments for 30, 90, and
300 min.
 SEM and EBSD were performed using a TESCAN MIRA3 GMH SEM and with an Oxford
Symmetry EBSD detector as well as a TESCAN MIRA3 XMH SEM with an EDAX Velocity
EBSD detector. EBSD maps were collected at varying magnifications for all samples, with step
sizes ranging from 0.05 µm to 0.2 µm, depending on the magnification and grain size of the sample.
Quantitative analysis of the EBSD data was conducted using MATLAB’s MTEX microstructural
analysis toolbox [19] by using a 5° misorientation threshold to segment grains from the raw data.

 A-3

The number of grains analyzed for each condition ranged from ~300 grains after extensive grain
growth to over 10,000 grains in the as-fabricated condition.
 A Renishaw inVia Qontor with a 532 nm wavelength laser and a 50× lens was used to obtain
Raman spectra of the ZrC coatings. Raman spectroscopy images were obtained using an automated
high-speed optically encoded sample stage for XY mapping and a 1 µm step size with five spectra
accumulations for each step. The obtained spectra were processed using the WiRE 5.5 software to
obtain peak position and intensity of the signals of interest.

3. Results
3.1. Microstructural analysis
3.1.1. As-fabricated
 Figure 1 presents a comprehensive summary of the microstructural characterization for both
ZrC coatings in the as-fabricated condition prior to any secondary heat treatment. Figure 1a shows
an area-weighted histogram of all the grain areas from both microstructures. The mean area of
ZrC-45C is 0.228 µm2, whereas the mean area of ZrC-47C is 0.167 µm2. Though ZrC-47C has
slightly smaller grains on average, the microstructures are qualitatively very similar, as shown in
the raw EBSD data in Figure 1b and Figure 1d. Furthermore, grain texture can be calculated from
the mean Euler orientation of every grain in the microstructure; these results are shown via inverse
pole figures (IPFs) in Figure 1c and Figure 1e for ZrC-45C and ZrC-47C, respectively. These plots
are colored according to multiples of a random distribution (MRD), and neither microstructure
shows any grain orientation exhibiting > 1.5 MRD, suggesting random texture in both
microstructures.

 Characterization of the grain boundaries in both coatings further supports that the starting
microstructures are similar despite their different stoichiometric compositions. Figures 1f–g show
IPFs representing the crystallographic misorientation axes for all grain boundaries in ZrC-45C and
ZrC-47C, respectively. These distributions of grain boundary character in the as-fabricated ZrC
coatings are almost identical, showing a strong prevalence for boundaries misoriented about the
[111] axis at > 3 MRD. Moreover, the misorientation angle distribution for both microstructures
shows a peak around 60° (Figure 1h). This suggests twinning Σ3 coincident site lattice (CSL) grain
boundaries, which are characterized by a 60° rotation about the [111] axis in face-centered cubic
materials and appear to be equally prevalent in both ZrC coatings. Overall, despite their different
stoichiometric compositions, both ZrC-45C and ZrC-47C have similar as-fabricated
microstructures.

3.1.2: High-temperature microstructural evolution of ZrC coatings
 Despite the demonstrable similarity between the initial microstructures summarized in
Figure 1, the two ZrC coatings exhibit starkly different grain growth behavior. Figure 2a shows
the mean grain area as a function of time for each sample. After heat treatments of 30, 90, and 300
min at 1,900°C, ZrC-45C exhibits mean grain areas of 8.91, 9.00, and 13.8 µm2, respectively. ZrC-
47C grows at a significantly faster rate, exhibiting mean grain areas of 10.0, 12.6, and 25.9 µm2 at
these respective conditions. To investigate whether a difference in grain orientation is playing a
role, Figures 2b–d show grain texture as a function of heat treatment time for ZrC-45C, and Figures
2e–g show the same for ZrC-47C. Overall, there is little difference in grain orientation evolution
between the two samples despite the significant difference in grain area.

microstructures exhibit slight differences in their evolution. After initial grain growth, there are

ZrC-45C shows increasing grain boundary texture toward the [111] direction with grain growth.

grain boundary misorientation angle is also a key component of the grain boundary character, and
as shown in Figure 3d–f, there is little significant difference between the misorientation
distributions with increasing heat treatment time. Overall, although there is indeed a quantifiable
difference between the two ZrC coatings in their grain boundary evolution, this relatively minor
difference in grain boundary character is unlikely to have caused the grains in ZrC-47C to grow
twice as large as those of ZrC-45C under the same conditions.

3.1.3: Spatial distribution of grain area
 Thus far, results have shown that the vastly different grain growth behavior between the two
ZrC coatings cannot be easily attributed to any clear differences in independent grain morphology
metrics or grain boundary character. However, one observation that is consistent across multiple
areas for the collected EBSD maps is that ZrC-45C tends to develop a rind of small grains along
the outside of the coating (i.e., the outer interface with the substrate). Figure 4 shows representative
EBSD micrographs of ZrC-45C (Figure 4a) and ZrC-47C (Figure 4b) after the 300 min heat
treatment at 1,900°C. The IPyC and substrate sides of the coating, which can be deduced from the
curvature, have been labeled for clarity. Clearly, ZrC-45C exhibits an abnormally high fraction of
small grains close to the outer curvature of the coating, whereas grain area is more uniform in
ZrC-47C.

Figure 7. Representative EBSD maps of (a) ZrC-45C and (b) ZrC-47C after the 300 min heat
treatment, with the inner curvature (IPyC) and outer curvature (epoxy) denoted.

 To quantify and contrast this phenomenon between the two ZrC samples, Figure 5 shows a
contoured probability density map to provide a visualization of the correlation between radial
distance from the IPyC interface (x-axis) and grain area (y-axis). The data for ZrC-45C and ZrC-
47C are shown in Figure 5a and 5b, respectively, for each of the heat treatment conditions (as
labeled). The radial distance is normalized for simplicity, and the plotting convention is shown in
Figure 5c. Once again, both samples are similar in their as-fabricated condition; they both exhibit
peak density at a normalized radial distance of ~0.15 with a grain area of ~0.08 µm2. As grain
growth proceeds, the two coatings diverge in their spatial dependence on grain area. As observed
qualitatively, ZrC-45C develops a strong correlation between small grains and a high normalized
radial distance from the IPyC layer; that is, a prevalence of small grains along the outer rind. The
opposite trend is observed for ZrC-47C, where the correlation becomes more diffuse as grain
growth proceeds and there is no strong radial dependence on grain area. Unlike the individual grain
morphology and boundary metrics, these spatially dependent phenomena introduce a stark
difference between the two samples with grain growth and will be discussed in a subsequent
section.

3.2 Stoichiometry
3.2.1 Raman analysis of as-fabricated samples
 Figure 6 shows the Raman spectra of ZrC-45C (Figure 6a) and ZrC-47C (Figure 6b). It is
possible to observe two distinctive regions between 200–900 cm−1 and 1,400–1,800 cm−1.
Stoichiometric ZrC, with its NaCl crystal structure, is Raman inactive because all its atoms have
an inversion symmetry that does not allow first-order Raman scattering [20]. However, the
presence of defects, such as vacancies, can break the local symmetry, allowing defect-induced
Raman signals [20, 21]. The signals observed at 210 and 280 cm−1 and between 400 and 700 cm−1
have been assigned to acoustic and optical phonons, respectively [20, 21]. These originate from
the presence of carbon vacancies, suggesting the formation of a substoichiometric ZrC structure.
Additionally, the signals between 1,400 and 1,700 cm−1 correspond to the presence of carbon,
originating from the E2g symmetry in-plane stretching mode of graphite and defect-induced double
resonant scattering [22-24].
 Figure 7a shows that for ZrC-45C, defect-induced signals were identified up to around 6 µm
from the PyC interface. Beyond 7 µm, the defect-induced signals almost disappear, suggesting the
formation of a structure closer to stoichiometric ZrC. Beyond 10 µm, the carbon signal is clearly

 A-9

observed, suggesting the formation of a ZrC+C structure. Similarly, Figure 7b shows that ZrC-47C
also had the presence of defect-induced Raman signals below 800 cm−1 up to 10 µm beyond the
PyC interface. Contrary to ZrC-45C, sample ZrC-47C showed the presence of defect-induced
signals and carbon closer to the outer surface of the coating. These results suggest that both
coatings had a substoichiometric composition and a carbon-rich area closer to the surface of the
coating.
 Figure 7 and 8 show the Raman spectroscopy imaging of ZrC-45C and ZrC-47C, respectively.
Both maps show the intensity ratio of the C/ZrC signals as a measure of the concentration of carbon
in the coatings. Similar to what was identified in Figure 6, it is possible to detect that both coatings
contained a higher concentration of carbon toward the outer surface of the coating. In particular,
ZrC-45C showed a larger concentration of carbon up to 10 µm inside the coating, as shown in
Figure 6.

4. Discussion
 In the as-fabricated condition, ZrC-45C exhibits slightly larger grains than ZrC-47C (Figure
1a). Previous literature has shown that excess carbon tends to retard grain growth in materials due
to solute segregation to grain boundaries, resulting in solute drag [7]. Therefore, this difference in
as-fabricated grain size (although minimal) may be attributable to redistribution and segregation
of excess carbon in the higher-carbon-content ZrC-47C, resulting in solute drag and smaller grain
areas during the initial nucleation and growth of the ZrC coating. Although the comparison
between as-fabricated grain size can be rationalized in terms of well-studied solute segregation
effects, the evolution of grain areas during high-temperature heat treatment cannot. As shown in
Figure 2a, ZrC-47C, despite having an overall higher carbon content, grows at a significantly faster
rate than ZrC-45C. After 300 min at 1,900°C, ZrC-47C exhibits a mean grain area almost twice as
large as that of ZrC-45C, suggesting that solute drag is not a significant factor affecting grain
growth.
 As discussed in Sections 3.1 and 3.2, the as-fabricated microstructures are very similar in terms
of their starting grain size, texture, and grain boundary character, providing no immediate
explanation for the significant difference in grain growth behavior. However, as shown in
Figure 5a, throughout the grain growth process, ZrC-45C shows a strong correlation between grain
area and radial distance across the coating, as a rind of small grains is consistently present at the
outside of the coating. This is not observed in ZrC-47C, as reflected by the decreasing correlation
with grain growth shown in Figure 5b. This phenomenon is a distinct difference between the two
microstructures that manifested after rapid grain growth at the high temperature tested in this study
and is correlated to a difference in the distribution of the as-fabricated carbon content between the
two coatings.
 As shown in Figures 6 and 7, both samples show a distinct concentration of carbon along the
outer curvature of the coating (top of the image, epoxy interface). However, based on the analysis
of peak intensities, the high-carbon region is larger and more intense in ZrC-45C than it is in
ZrC-47C. Although this difference does not play a role in the fine-grained as-fabricated coatings,
it is likely that this is the factor contributing to the significant difference in grain growth. Although
ZrC-45C has a lower stoichiometric carbon concentration, the Raman analysis shows that it has a
high concentration of localized excess carbon at the outer edge of the coating relative to ZrC-47C.

 A-11

Accordingly, grain boundaries close to the outer edge of the coating would be expected to undergo
solute segregation-induced drag, retarding their growth rate. As the rest of the microstructure
grows normally over time at 1,900°C, grains surrounded by these solute-rich boundaries are
pinned, prohibiting growth beyond the outer rind and significantly reducing the overall growth rate
within the already dimensionally constrained ZrC coating. This is consistent with the observed and
unexpected slow growth of ZrC-45C as well as the increasing correlation with growth between
grain area and radial distance from the IPyC in this sample (Figure 5a).
 The increase in grain size observed in this study can provide insight into how the coating will
perform under high temperature. The ZrC coating will contribute to the mechanical stability of the
particle configuration during operation. Changes in grain size are suspected to alter the mechanical
properties of the coating, specifically the elastic modulus [7, 13, 16]. Vasudevamurthy et al.
observed that after heat treatment at 800°C–1,250°C, all samples exhibited significant grain
growth, and those with excess carbon had a reduction in elastic modulus and hardness [7].
Additionally, ZrC with excess carbon that formed during fabrication displayed a similar decrease
in the mechanical properties at high temperatures [9, 16]. Understanding how the processing
conditions affect the stoichiometry of the sample allows for more control of producing a ZrC
coating that can withstand extreme conditions.

5. Conclusion
 Initial microstructural characterization of the as-fabricated ZrC coatings had shown similar
grain boundary type and size despite small differences in stoichiometry. Heat treatments at 1,900ºC
had displayed significant grain growth for all exposure times with different growth rates and final
areas for each sample. The CSL fraction and grain boundary misorientation angle data identified
quantifiable differences that could contribute microstructurally to the high-temperature behavior.
A spatial dependence for the grain area as a function of distance from the IPyC layer was identified,
showing that an accumulation of smaller grains remained on the outer portion of the coating.
Raman analysis identified a ZrC+C structure where a carbon-rich area formed radially during
fabrication near the outer portions of the layer. This excess carbon is suspected of contributing to
the slower growth rate. These results helps to understand how the as-fabricated microstructure and
composition of these layers both contribute to how the ZrC coatings will perform at high
temperatures.
