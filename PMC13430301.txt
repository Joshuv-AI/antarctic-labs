Association Between Physical Performance and Depressive Symptoms in Older Adults: A Retrospective Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Yun Wang, Yi-Wen Chen, Ming-Zhao Qin, Jing Wang, Hui Ye, Lili Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo explore the relationship between physical function and depressive and anxiety symptoms among older adults, and to determine whether poorer physical function is associated with higher levels of depressive symptoms.Patients and methodsThis retrospective analysis included data from the comprehensive geriatric assessments of 491 patients aged ≥65 years who visited the Geriatric Outpatient Clinic or were hospitalized at the Geriatric Department of Beijing Tongren Hospital between November 2021 and December 2023. After excluding cases with incomplete data, 443 participants were included in the final analysis. Physical function was assessed using grip strength, the Timed Up and Go Test (TUGT), 4-meter walk speed, the five-time sit-to-stand test, the three-posture test, and the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB). Depressive and anxiety symptoms were evaluated using the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) and the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS). Correlations between physical function and depressive and anxiety symptoms were analyzed, and factors associated with depressive symptoms were examined.ResultsThe prevalence of depressive and anxiety states was 15.6% and 12.0%, respectively. Participants with depressive symptoms had significantly lower SPPB scores compared with those without (5.83 ± 2.70 vs 8.68 ± 2.14, P < 0.05). SPPB scores were moderately negatively correlated with depressive symptoms (r = −0.404, P < 0.05). Logistic regression showed that higher SPPB scores were associated with lower odds of depressive symptoms (OR = 0.557, 95% CI: 0.411–0.754), whereas longer TUGT time (OR = 1.123, 95% CI: 1.018–1.238) and higher anxiety scores (OR = 1.273, 95% CI: 1.185–1.368) were associated with higher odds. ROC analysis showed that SPPB score was associated with depressive symptoms (AUC = 0.859), with sensitivity of 0.86 and specificity of 0.67 at the optimal cutoff of 8.5. TUGT and anxiety scores also showed associations with depressive symptoms (AUC = 0.844 and 0.891, respectively; both P < 0.05).ConclusionDeclining physical function was associated with depressive symptoms among older adults. Within comprehensive geriatric assessments (CGA), routine SPPB evaluation alongside depressive symptom monitoring may help identify vulnerable older individuals and maintain intrinsic capacity.

Introduction

The global population is aging rapidly, prompting a paradigm shift in how societies approach healthy aging. In 2015, the World Health Organization (WHO) released the World Report on Ageing and Health, which redefined healthy aging as the process of developing and maintaining the functional ability that enables well-being in older age.1 This report emphasized the need to move beyond disease-focused models toward promoting intrinsic capacity—the composite of an individual’s physical and mental capabilities across the life course. Subsequently, the WHO introduced the Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) model,2 centered on the assessment and promotion of five key domains of intrinsic capacity: physical mobility (the ability to walk, climb stairs, rise from a seated position, and maintain balance to perform daily activities); mental health (emotional well-being, resilience to stress, and the absence of depressive symptoms that impair quality of life); cognitive function (memory, attention, problem-solving, and decision-making for independent living); vitality (energy and stamina supporting activity and overall well-being); and sensory abilities (vision and hearing for safe navigation, communication, and social connection). These five dimensions are key to maintaining older adults’ intrinsic capacity and enable better prediction of adverse health outcomes, including falls, functional decline, and chronic disease progression.3 Clinically, this requires identifying measurable and feasible indicators within these domains—such as changes in gait speed or mood symptoms—that can signal impending declines in overall well-being.

Among these domains, psychological health plays a pivotal role in healthy aging, and depression is one of the most common mental health problems among older adults. Depression can lead to reduced social participation, decreased interaction with family and friends, increased feelings of loneliness, and heightened risk of poor health outcomes.4 There is a well-recognized link between physical fitness and physical and mental aging in older adults. Maintaining good physical fitness can yield a range of benefits, including reduced cardiovascular risk, decreased disability and frailty, improved metabolic markers, and enhanced quality of life.5 Exercise physiology research further indicates that physical activity can improve older adults’ physiological function and physical capacity while alleviating clinical symptoms of depression.6,7

That physical activity alleviates depressive symptoms is well established.8,9 However, most of the evidence derives from trials involving specific volunteer populations; it remains unclear which older adults specifically experience both poor physical function and depressive symptoms in real-world clinical settings. A recent longitudinal study by Mengist et al found that among community-dwelling older adults, those with poor physical function and weak grip strength were 81% more likely to develop depressive symptoms (OR = 1.81), while those with depressive symptoms were 70% more likely to experience subsequent declines in physical function (OR = 1.70).10 However, this study did not examine whether physical function measures commonly used in comprehensive geriatric assessments—such as the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) and Timed Up and Go Test (TUGT)—are associated with the psychological state of older patients.

The Current Study

The present study focuses on two interconnected domains of intrinsic capacity: mobility and psychological health. Using comprehensive geriatric assessment data from hospitalized and outpatient older adults, we examined associations between objective physical performance measures—particularly SPPB scores—and self-reported symptoms of anxiety and depression. Specifically, we evaluated bivariate correlations between motor performance and affective symptoms and conducted multivariable analyses to identify independent predictors of depressive symptoms. We hypothesized that poorer physical performance would be associated with higher levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms, and that mobility-related metrics would emerge as significant predictors of depressive symptoms.

Methods

Participants

A retrospective analysis was conducted on the geriatric comprehensive assessment data of 491 elderly patients aged ≥65 years (65–99 years, mean age 81.82±6.74 years) who visited the Geriatric Outpatient Clinic and were hospitalized at the Geriatric Department of Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University, from November 2021 to December 2023. A total of 443 cases with complete data were ultimately included in this study. All participants signed informed consent forms following the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University (approval No. TRECKY 2021–210).

Inclusion criteria: (1) Age ≥65 years; (2) Able to communicate normally, with adequate visual and auditory function, and capable of cooperating to complete all components of the geriatric comprehensive assessment; (3) No history of mental illness or family history of mental illness; (4) Voluntarily agreed to undergo the geriatric comprehensive assessment.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Severe cognitive impairment; (2) Lack of independent functional capacity and communication ability; (3) History of anxiety and depressive symptoms, currently taking oral antidepressants, or accompanied by severe mental disorders; (4) Severe organ dysfunction or end-stage diseases that prevent completion of the geriatric comprehensive assessment.

Based on age groups, participants were divided into two groups: 65–84 years old and ≥85 years old.11,12

Medical History Collection and Evaluation

A comprehensive geriatric assessment is conducted by a trained and certified medical team, which records the patient’s age, sex, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), medical history, medications, history of falls, and educational level (divided into: primary, secondary and above, primary education includes elementary and junior high school, while secondary and higher education includes high school, vocational school, and above), living arrangements (classified as: living alone or not living alone; living alone includes: living alone or residing in a nursing home, while not living alone includes: living with a spouse, children, or a caregiver). The Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB)

SPPB comprises three components. (1) Balance test: participants maintain three standing postures — side-by-side stand, semi-tandem stand, and tandem stand — each held for 10 seconds. (2) Five-time sit-to-stand test (FTSST): using an armless chair with a seat height and width of 44 cm, participants cross their arms over the chest and complete five consecutive sit-to-stand movements as quickly as possible without using arm support; time is recorded with a stopwatch (in seconds). (3) Four-meter gait speed: participants walk six meters at their usual pace, and the time from the 2-meter mark to the 5-meter mark is recorded with a stopwatch to calculate gait speed over 4 meters (m/s). Grip Strength Test

Grip strength was measured using the Camry EH101 grip strength meter. Participants stood quietly, gripped the grip strength meter for 5 seconds. The test was performed twice, with a 15-second interval between each test. Both hands were tested, and the highest value was taken as the grip strength value.

Timed Up and Go Test (TUGT)

On a flat surface, use a chair with a backrest, 45 cm in height. The participant sits on the chair, and a stopwatch is used to record the time (in seconds) it takes for the participant to stand up from the chair, walk 3 meters at their usual pace, turn around, walk back to the chair, and sit down.

Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) and Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS)

In this study, the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) and the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) were used to assess anxiety and depressive symptoms, respectively. Participants were asked to complete each of the 20 items based on their situation over the previous week using the following 4-point scale: “1” indicates none or a little of the time; “2” indicates some of the time; “3” indicates a good part of the time; “4” indicates most or all of the time. Raw scores were summed and multiplied by 1.25 to obtain a standardized score. For both scales, a cut-off score of 50 was applied, with scores ≥50 indicating the presence of anxiety or depressive symptoms. Higher scores reflect a greater tendency toward anxiety or depressive symptoms. To minimize measurement and interviewer bias, questionnaires were completed independently by participants whenever possible. For individuals with visual impairment, severe fatigue, or low literacy, trained researchers read the questionnaire items aloud verbatim without interpretive prompting, maintaining strict standardization to avoid influencing responses.

Both the SAS and SDS have demonstrated robust reliability and validity in Chinese populations. Regarding the SDS, prior research in Chinese populations has reported a Cronbach’s α of 0.847.15,16 For the SAS, a validation study reported a Cronbach’s α of 0.733 and a one-week test-retest reliability of 0.62.17

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 22.0). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD and compared between groups using independent-samples t-tests. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were expressed as number (percentage) and compared using the Pearson chi-square test. Spearman correlation analysis was used to evaluate the association between physical function and psychological status; correlation coefficients were interpreted as follows: ≤0.35 indicating weak correlation, 0.36–0.70 indicating moderate correlation, and 0.71–1.0 indicating strong correlation. Binary logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with depressive symptoms, with results presented as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multicollinearity among explanatory variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) derived from linear regression models, with VIF <10 considered indicative of no significant multicollinearity. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the sensitivity and specificity of physical performance measures and anxiety scores for discriminating depressive symptom status. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Preliminary Analyses

This study initially evaluated 491 cases, of which 443 ultimately met the inclusion criteria with complete data. The average age was 81.82±6.74 years, with 291 male patients and 152 female patients. Fifty-three patients (12.0%) met the diagnostic criteria for anxiety, while 69 patients (15.6%) met the criteria for depressive symptoms. Additionally, five patients had a depressive symptoms scale score of 48.5, which was close to but did not yet meet the diagnostic criteria for depressive symptoms.

Comparison of Differences Between the Two Age Groups

Comparisons between the two age groups revealed significant differences in body mass index (24.19 ± 2.86 vs 23.37 ± 3.26, P = 0.014), living alone status (15.13% vs 29.01%, P = 0.010), grip strength (25.28 ± 8.71 vs 22.33 ± 6.92, P = 0.001), TUGT time (12.77 ± 5.68 vs 14.97 ± 6.84, P < 0.001), SPPB score (8.86 ± 2.25 vs 7.51 ± 2.68, P < 0.001), and aCCI score (4.65 ± 3.64 vs 5.98 ± 2.04, P < 0.001). The prevalence of depressive symptoms was slightly lower in the ≥85 years group than in the 65–84 years group; however, the between-group difference did not reach statistical significance. Discussion

Population aging has become an increasingly pressing challenge worldwide. In response, the World Health Organization has launched the “Healthy Aging” initiative. Existing research confirms that biological age, rather than chronological age, influences self-care capacity. However, evidence linking psychosocial factors to intrinsic capacity remains insufficient. Among the five ICOPE domains, mental health issues in older adults—particularly depressive symptoms—are receiving growing attention.18

The data for this study were derived from a comprehensive geriatric assessment. The prevalence of depressive symptoms among the included older patients was 15.6%, with a higher rate in women than in men. This finding is comparable to those reported by Patino and Dickens19,20 but slightly lower than that reported by Corneliusson.21 A study on intrinsic capabilities in older adults conducted by Ma revealed that 14.2% of participants exhibited impaired psychological capacity.22 Given that the present study included only outpatients and inpatients who were capable of independent living and able to cooperate with the comprehensive geriatric assessment, it excluded individuals who were bedridden long-term or had substantially reduced mobility that precluded completion of the mobility assessment. Thus, the true prevalence of depressive symptoms among older adults is likely higher.

Compared with depressive symptoms, the prevalence of anxiety in this study was relatively low. This may reflect age- and sex-related differences in the clinical presentation of anxiety and depressive symptoms. Some evidence suggests that anxiety disorders typically emerge in young adulthood or middle age, characterized by pathological worry and persistent or episodic feelings of unexplained fear, dread, tension, and restlessness, often accompanied by a sense of impending doom or even a fear of dying. In contrast, older adults are more likely to present with depressive symptoms. The prevalence of depressive symptoms among community-dwelling older adults ranges from less than 1% to 5%, but rises to 13.5% among those requiring home care. Approximately 25% of older adults with chronic illnesses experience depressive symptoms, while among nursing home residents the prevalence ranges from 25% to 50%.23,24

The oldest-old — variably defined in the literature as those aged 85 or 90 years and above.11,12 — are more likely to be affected by physical disability, poor nutrition, frailty, and depression. The 85-year threshold was adopted in this study to specifically identify and represent this population. The prevalence of depressive symptoms was slightly lower in the ≥85 years group, but the difference was not statistically significant. Possible explanations include a healthy survivor effect (older survivors may be more resilient) and a tendency to somatize psychological distress, leading to underreporting. Importantly, our findings suggest that physical performance, rather than chronological age, is a stronger correlate of depressive symptoms. These interpretations remain speculative given the limited sample size in the oldest-old group, and causality cannot be inferred.

Depressive symptoms are closely related to quality of life in older adults and represent one of the major contributors to frailty, falls, and disability in this population.25 The medical and economic costs associated with depressive symptoms have become a substantial burden on society and families.26 The focus of intervention in older adults should shift from alleviating existing depressive symptoms toward preventing their onset and identifying effective self-help strategies. Clinically, identifying and reducing modifiable risk factors for depressive symptoms is essential. Most known risk factors, such as socioeconomic status, family circumstances, insufficient social support, and adverse life events, are difficult to modify. Evidence suggests that physical inactivity is a risk factor for depressive symptoms, and that maintaining physical function through regular activity is an effective strategy for preventing depressive episodes.27,28 Conducting SPPB assessments to evaluate functional capacity in older adults is both warranted and clinically meaningful.29 In this study, SPPB scores were obtained through comprehensive geriatric assessments. Given that the LPP group was significantly older, the observed differences in grip strength, TUGT, and aCCI between the LPP and NPP groups are likely attributable to age. Significant differences in the prevalence of anxiety and depressive symptoms were also found between the two groups. Spearman correlation analysis showed a moderate correlation between SPPB scores and depressive symptoms, whereas the association with anxiety symptoms was weak, despite reaching statistical significance. These results suggest that physical function may be less clinically related to anxiety than to depression. Evidence suggests that depressive symptoms in older adults often manifest as fatigue, psychomotor disturbances, and sleep problems, with physical symptoms being particularly prominent in this population.30 It has been proposed that declines in physical function may impair self-esteem, exacerbate negative self-appraisal, and precipitate depressed mood, while also potentially contributing to cognitive decline that may progress to depressive symptoms.31,32 Currently, treatment for depression relies primarily on pharmacotherapy and psychotherapy; however, most patients who receive treatment do not achieve complete remission and frequently experience symptom recurrence. Prevention of depressive symptoms is therefore of particular importance. Although existing evidence has not conclusively demonstrated that exercise alone can effectively treat depressive symptoms—likely because most studies have enrolled patients with established depression, in whom exercise rehabilitation alone has limited efficacy—exercise-based interventions are nonetheless beneficial for maintaining physical function. Shifting the focus toward earlier identification, screening older adults through comprehensive geriatric assessments or SPPB, and promoting mobility and preservation of activities of daily living may be associated with a lower burden of depressive symptoms in this population; however, further prospective studies are needed to establish causality.

In this study, the depressive symptoms group differed significantly from the non-depressive symptoms group across several characteristics, including gender, living situation, educational attainment, grip strength, SPPB scores, TUGT time, and anxiety. After adjusting for gender, living situation, educational attainment, and grip strength, binary logistic regression analysis showed that higher SPPB scores were associated with a reduced risk of depressive symptoms, whereas prolonged TUGT time and higher anxiety scores were associated with a significantly increased risk. This finding is noteworthy because it suggests a significant association between physical function and depressive symptoms. Therefore, regardless of age, cultural background, or living situation, strategies aimed at maintaining or improving physical function in older adults may be considered as part of comprehensive approaches to depressive symptom management.

ROC curve analysis showed that SPPB (optimal cut-off: 8.5 points), TUGT (best sensitivity at 17s, best specificity at 20s), and anxiety score (cut-off: 40 points) effectively discriminated between older adults with and without depressive symptoms, further supporting their close associations with depression. However, given the relatively small sample size, these cut-off values should be interpreted with caution and are not intended as diagnostic thresholds. Nevertheless, these findings underscore the importance of integrating physical and psychological assessments into routine geriatric care, thereby facilitating early identification and self-management education. Future larger-scale studies are warranted to validate these associations and explore intervention strategies.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences between physical performance, anxiety, and depressive symptoms. Second, despite adjusting for multiple covariates, the possibility of residual confounding due to unmeasured variables (e.g, chronic pain, sleep disorders, polypharmacy, and socioeconomic factors) cannot be excluded, which may have introduced bias into our estimates. Third, the relatively small sample size limits the generalizability of our findings, data were collected from a single center, which may further affect external validity. Additionally, the exclusion of bedridden and severely mobility-impaired individuals may have led to an underestimation of the true prevalence of depressive symptoms. Future prospective studies with larger, more diverse samples are warranted to confirm and extend our results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, poorer physical function was significantly associated with depressive symptoms in older adults. SPPB assessment may help identify older adults with a higher likelihood of depressive symptoms, and regular monitoring of physical performance could inform clinical attention to mental health. However, given the retrospective cross-sectional design of this study, causal relationships cannot be established. Future large-scale, prospective studies are warranted to clarify whether improving physical function can lead to better psychological outcomes.
