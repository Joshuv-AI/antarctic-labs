Impact of Higher Versus Lower Blood Pressure Targets on Pulmonary Vascular Hemodynamics During Intensive Care After Out‐of‐Hospital Cardiac Arrest
Authors: Shira K. Hendin, Rasmus P. Beske, Jesper Kjaergaard, Jacob E. Møller, Henrik Schmidt, Simon Mølstrøm, Simon Schneekloth, Martin A. S. Meyer, Laust E. W. Obling, Christian Hassager, Johannes Grand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHemodynamic management after out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is critical, yet the impact of vasopressor‐driven mean arterial pressure (MAP) targets on pulmonary circulation and right ventricular (RV) function remains unclear.MethodsIn this substudy of the randomized, double‐blinded BOX trial, comatose OHCA survivors were allocated to low (63 mmHg) or high (77 mmHg) MAP targets. Pulmonary artery catheters (PAC) were used for serial hemodynamic assessment for 48 h after Intensive Care Unit admission. The primary endpoint was calculated pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), secondary endpoints included pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP), pulmonary artery pulsatility index (PAPi), and RV cardiac power output (RV‐CPO)—a measurement of RV pumping function.ResultsAmong 730 included patients (median time randomization to PAC insertion 1.3 h), mPAP was consistently higher in the high‐MAP group (mean difference 1.11–1.71 mmHg, 95% CI range 0.12–2.59). Calculated PVR was transiently lower in the high‐MAP group during the first 24 h (mean difference −0.16 to −0.30, 95% CI range −0.31 to 0.11), before converging between groups. RV‐CPO was lower in the low‐MAP group throughout the observation period (mean difference 0.01–0.04 W [95% range 0.00–0.07], with the largest difference at 48 h. PCWP decreased in both groups but was significantly lower in the low‐MAP group during the first 12 h (mean difference 1.06–1.40 mmHg, 95% CI range 0.25–2.38).ConclusionsIn comatose OHCA survivors, targeting a higher MAP increased pulmonary artery pressures, PCWP, RV‐CPO, heart rate, and cardiac output. The proportionally greater increase in cardiac output over pulmonary artery pressures resulted in a decreased calculated PVR.Trial Registration ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03141099.Editorial CommentIn this secondary analysis of a subgroup in the BOX out of hospital cardiac arrest treatment trial for oxygen level targets, blood pressure treatment target levels, higher or lower were analyzed, including central circulatory outcomes, using a pulmonary artery catheter. The higher blood pressure target group had accompanying higher pulmonary artery pressures and cardiac output, with initially a small reduction in calculated pulmonary vascular resistance.

Introduction

Out of hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is often followed by a postresuscitation circulatory syndrome characterized by transient myocardial dysfunction, systemic vasoplegia, and inflammatory activation [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. In comatose survivors admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU), hemodynamic instability is common, necessitating pharmacological support to maintain adequate perfusion pressure [3, 7]. Although evidence guiding postresuscitation hemodynamic management is limited, observational studies consistently associate systemic hypotension with poor outcomes [6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. While restoration of mean arterial pressure (MAP) is a central component of postresuscitation care, the physiological consequences of vasopressor‐mediated MAP elevation beyond the systemic circulation remain incompletely characterized.

Vasopressors are routinely administered to counteract hypotension after return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) [7, 14]. In the BOX trial (Blood Pressure and Oxygen Targets in Post Resuscitation Care), targeting a higher MAP (77 mmHg vs. 63 mmHg) required substantially higher doses of vasopressors and resulted in increased cardiac output (CO), heart rate (HR), and systemic vascular resistance, without improvement in survival or neurological outcome [15, 16], consistent with earlier pilot studies [17, 18, 19]. These findings suggest that higher MAP targets, attained through increased vasopressor support, induce measurable alterations in cardiovascular physiology, yet the downstream effects on other circulatory domains critical to cardiac performance—particularly the pulmonary circulation—remain unknown.

The pulmonary circulation is a low‐pressure, low‐resistance system that plays a central role in gas exchange and right ventricular (RV) loading conditions [20]. Pulmonary vascular tone is tightly regulated and sensitive to changes in oxygen tension, inflammatory mediators, intravascular volume, and neurohumoral activation [21, 22]. Following cardiac arrest, ischemia reperfusion injury, and systemic inflammation may impair pulmonary vascular function, potentially altering pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) and RV function during the early postresuscitation period [23, 24]. Even modest changes in PVR may therefore have significant consequences for RV afterload and pumping efficiency [22].

Vasopressor agents, such as norepinephrine and dopamine, primarily increase systemic arterial pressure through α‐adrenergic‐mediated vasoconstriction, but may influence pulmonary vascular tone through combined α‐ and β‐adrenergic effects, while also enhancing RV contractility [25, 26, 27, 28]. While the effects of vasopressor‐driven MAP targets on systemic circulation has been studied thoroughly, its effects on pulmonary hemodynamics in postcardiac arrest patients has not been systematically evaluated. Notably, despite the frequent use of pulmonary artery catheterization in this population, clinical data linking MAP targets to invasive measures of pulmonary vascular load and RV function remain scarce.

The present study was designed as a mechanistic substudy of the BOX trial to investigate the effects of higher versus lower vasopressor‐mediated MAP targets on pulmonary hemodynamics in comatose OHCA patients during the early ICU phase. Using invasive pulmonary artery catherization, effects on calculated pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) were assessed as the primary endpoint. Secondary endpoints included pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP), pulmonary artery pulsatility index (PAPi), and right ventricular cardiac power output (RV‐CPO)—a measurement of RV pumping function.

Methods

Study Design, Setting and Patients

This study is a post hoc analysis of the BOX trial [15, 29]—a randomized, controlled two‐center trial employing a double‐blinded design to compare two MAP targets (63 and 77 mmHg). The main trial also included open‐label interventions evaluating oxygenation and fever prevention strategies [15, 29, 30, 31]. All patients received device‐controlled temperature management, targeting 36°C for 24 h before controlled rewarming at 0.5°C per hour until 37°C. Following this, patients were randomized to device‐based fever prevention for comatose patients for either 12 or 48 h, corresponding to total intervention durations of 36 or 72 h, respectively [31].

The study was conducted at two tertiary cardiac care centers in Denmark between March 2017 and December 2021, enrolling 789 adult comatose patients who had survived an OHCA of presumed cardiac origin (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03141099).

Randomization to MAP targets was achieved by modifying the calibration factor of the blood pressure monitoring system, as described previously [32]. Inclusion and exclusion criteria for the BOX trial are provided in the primary publications and Supporting Information materials [16]. For this substudy, only patients with placement of a pulmonary artery catheter (PAC) were eligible (Figure 1). Details of this predefined substudy are published previously [29].

Screening and randomization. A total of 789 patients were included in the BOX‐trial. Of these, 59 patients were excluded from this substudy due to various reasons. Of the remaining 730 patients, 362 were assigned to high blood pressure (MAP = 77 mmHg) and 368 to low blood pressure (MAP = 63 mmHg). MAP, mean arterial pressure; PAC, pulmonary artery catheter. These data have been published in a previous article [16].

Prehospital data were collected systematically according to the Utstein guidelines [33].

The study protocol, including the use of PACs for research purposes, was approved by the regional ethics committee (Ref. nr.: H‐16033436). Informed written consent was obtained from both a legal representative and an independent physician not involved in the trial. If a patient regained consciousness, additional consent was obtained directly from the patient.

Study Procedures

Patients were enrolled upon arrival to the ICU, and within 4 h of the cardiac arrest. The blood pressure intervention was initiated immediately upon enrolment and continued until termination of invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring. A standard balloon‐tipped PAC was inserted under ultrasound guidance via the internal jugular or subclavian vein, and advanced to the pulmonary artery as soon as possible. The blood pressure targets were achieved using a stepwise approach, administering fluids and vasoactive drugs.

Hemodynamic evaluations, including thermodilution‐based CO measurements, were performed at T 0—defined as the moment when hemodynamic monitoring was initiated, and the core temperature had reached the target of 36°C. The PVR (normal range < 2.0 Wood Units [WU]) was calculated using variables from the right heart catheter, which is the gold standard for measuring PVR. The formula used was PVR = (mean pulmonary artery blood pressure (mPAP, mmHg) − pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP, mmHg))/CO (L/min). When PCWP (normal range 4–12 mmHg) measurements were unavailable and transpulmonary gradient was normal, PCWP was estimated as pulmonary diastolic artery pressure (PAPdia) minus 3 mmHg. Pulmonary artery pulsatility index (PAPi) was calculated as the ratio of pulmonary artery pulse pressure to right atrial pressure (normal range > 0.9). RV‐CPO (normal range 0.20–0.27 W), a measurement of the RV pumping function, was calculated as (mPAP × CO)/451.

The PAC was removed at intensive care unit (ICU) discharge or after 72 h unless continued use was clinically indicated. Postcardiac arrest care protocols applied at the participating institutions have been described previously [15, 29], and are further outlined in the Supporting Information.

Monitoring

Arterial blood pressure was continuously monitored via a catheter placed in either the radial or brachial artery. Central venous pressure (CVP) was obtained through the proximal port of the PAC, and the pulmonary pressures were measured through the distal port.

At the Copenhagen site, a 7.5F triple‐lumen Swan‐Ganz catheter with a thermistor and balloon tip (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA, USA) was used. CO was determined by thermodilution (infusion of cold isotonic glucose solution into the catheter), and the final value calculated as the mean of three consecutive measurements, provided the variability between them did not exceed 10% [34].

At the Odense site, a Continuous Cardiac Output (CCOmbo) PAC system (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA, USA) connected to a Vigilance II monitor was used, and hemodynamic data were digitally transmitted to a computer at 2‐s intervals. Continuous CO was measured using intermittent heating of the blood, with resulting temperature changes detected by a thermistor at the distal end of the catheter [35].

Interobserver variability within our research group has previously been evaluated, demonstrating minimal bias and high reproducibility [36]. Prior studies have demonstrated strong agreement and high accuracy and precision between different CO assessment methods [36]. Hemodynamic parameters were indexed for body surface area.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the difference in calculated PVR between the MAP groups during the first 48 h, evaluated using a repeated‐measures mixed‐effects model at predefined time points (T0, T6, T12, T24, T36, and T48).

Secondary outcomes included RV‐CPO, PCWP, and PAPi. Previously published data on cardiac index (CI), heart rate, and mPAP was revisited to investigate potential associations [16, 29].

Statistical Analyses

Patient characteristics were summarized using counts and percentages for categorical variables, and medians with interquartile ranges for continuous variables. For group comparisons of continuous variables, Wilcoxon rank sum test was employed. Categorical variables were compared using chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests.

Differences in pulmonary hemodynamic measures over the first 48 h between treatment groups were analysed using a linear mixed‐effects model, including fixed effects for time, intervention, and their interaction. An unstructured covariance matrix accounted for within‐subject correlation due to repeated measurements. This model accommodates missing data under the assumption of missing at random. Results were presented as estimated overall group differences with corresponding 95% confidence intervals and p‐values.

A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.0 (R Core Team, 2025).

Pulmonary Circulation

At the time of PAC insertion, after initiation of the MAP intervention achieved through differential vasopressor dosing, mPAP was 24.8 mmHg (95% CI 23.9–25.6) in the low‐MAP group and 26.5 mmHg (95% CI 25.7–27.4) in the high‐MAP group (p = 0.003). Calculated PVR at the time of PAC‐insertion was 2.84 WU (95% CI 2.56–3.13) in the low‐MAP group, and 2.54 WU (95% CI 2.26–2.83) in the high‐MAP group (p = 0.15). Calculated PVR decreased during the first 36 h in both groups (−1.13 WU [95% CI −0.28 to −0.50] and −0.77 WU [95% CI −0.48 to −1.05]). At 12 h, calculated PVR was lower in the high‐MAP group (mean difference −0.16 WU [95% CI −0.32 to 0.00; p = 0.05]). This difference persisted the first 24 h (mean difference −0.17; 95% CI −0.31 to −0.03; p = 0.015) after which no significant difference was found (Table 2).

No statistically significant difference in PaCO2 or PaO2 between the two MAP‐groups was observed during the observation period (Supplementary Table S1).

In the early postresuscitation phase (the period from PAC insertion to t 6), RV‐CPO was below normal for both groups (0.10 W (95% CI 0.9–0.11) in the low‐MAP group versus 0.12 W (95% CI 0.11–0.13) in the high‐MAP group), but gradually increased over time. RV‐CPO was consistently 12%–24% higher in the high‐MAP group compared with the low MAP target group (mean between‐group difference 0.01–0.04 W [95% ranging from 0.00 to 0.07]), with the greatest separation noted at 48 h after PAC insertion (low‐MAP group: 0.17 W [95% CI 0.14–0.19] vs. high‐MAP group 0.21 W [95% CI 0.18–0.23; p = 0.021]) (Figure 2).

PCWP was initially elevated in both groups (16.9 mmHg [95% CI 16.2–17.6] in the low‐MAP group vs. 18.3 mmHg [95% CI 17.6–18.9] in the high‐MAP group), and gradually decreased during the observation period (low‐MAP group PCWP between 14.3 and 15.6 mmHg [95% CI ranging from 13.7 to 16.2] and high‐MAP group PCWP between 16.7 and 14.9 mmHg [95% CI ranging from 14.2 to 17.3]) (Figure 2). During the first 12 h, PCWP was significantly higher in the high MAP target group compared to the low MAP group (mean between‐group difference between 1.06 and 1.40 mmHg with 95% CI ranging from 0.25 to 2.38) (Table 2).

In both MAP groups, PAPi was reduced (low‐MAP group mean 1.49–2.22 [95% CI range 1.31–1.78] vs. high‐MAP group mean 1.51–1.78 [95% CI range 1.32–1.91]) during the first 6 h and subsequently normalized. No significant differences between the groups were detected at any time point during the observation period.

Vasopressor Support During Intervention

Cumulative vasoactive drug exposure over the first 48 h is summarized in Table S2 and has been reported previously [16]. Nearly all patients (98% in the high MAP target group vs. 95% in the low‐MAP group) received norepinephrine, with no difference in overall use between groups. However, median cumulative norepinephrine doses were significantly higher in the high‐MAP group compared with the low‐MAP group (43 mg [q1–q3: 21–79 mg] vs. 21 [q1–q3: 8–43 mg], p < 0.0001). A greater proportion of patients in the high‐MAP group received dopamine at least once during the first 48 h of ICU admission (194 [53%] vs. 156 [42%], p = 0.002). Consistent with this, median vasopressor load was higher in the high‐MAP group (47 [q1–q3: 25–84] vs. 23 [q1–q3: 11–45], p < 0.0001).

Discussion

In this post hoc substudy of the BOX trial, we assessed the hemodynamic effects of two MAP targets (77 mmHg vs. 63 mmHg) on the pulmonary circulation in comatose survivors of cardiac arrest, achieved through differential vasopressor use. The main findings are: (i) calculated PVR was higher in the low MAP group during the first 24 h, (ii) mPAP was modestly higher in patients allocated to the high MAP target, (iii) RV‐CPO was consistently lower in the low MAP group, and (iv) PCWP decreased over time but was significantly lower in the low MAP group within the first 12 h.

This is, to our knowledge, the first large, randomized clinical trial to investigate the pulmonary hemodynamic effects of high vs. low MAP target in humans. These findings provide new insights into the pulmonary vascular consequences of postresuscitation vasopressor therapy and complement systemic hemodynamic data from the BOX hemodynamic substudy [16].

This trial finds that a higher MAP with increased doses of norepinephrine leads to decreased calculated PVR. Prior clinical investigations of blood pressure targets after OHCA have primarily focused on systemic hemodynamics. The BOX hemodynamic substudy demonstrated that higher MAP targets were associated with increased norepinephrine dose, higher CI and HR, but no difference in stroke volume [16]. Small‐scale clinical observations have reported limited changes in stroke volume with norepinephrine, but systemic vascular resistance and HR were increased [37]. Experimental studies have described heterogeneous pulmonary vascular responses to catecholamines. Norepinephrine, the predominant vasopressor in this trial, may increase pulmonary vascular tone through α‐adrenergic stimulation, but also enhance RV contractility via β1‐receptor activation [27, 28]. Our findings extend this evidence by demonstrating that pulmonary pressures also rise with a higher vasopressor‐driven MAP target, while calculated PVR only showed a temporal pattern of minute elevation in the low‐MAP group, possibly explained by doses being either too high or too low. Importantly, RV‐CPO was consistently lower with the low MAP target, suggesting that the systemic hemodynamic and vasopressor profile translates into measurable differences in RV workload. This adds a pulmonary dimension to previous findings, indicating that the balance between systemic vasoconstriction, pulmonary vascular tone, and RV adaptation is sensitive to vasopressor dose and perfusion pressure targets.

Potential Mechanisms

The observed reduction in calculated PVR at higher vasopressor‐driven MAP targets could house a methodological rather than physiological explanation. Although patients in the higher MAP group received more norepinephrine and had higher systemic vascular resistance [16], the apparent decrease in calculated PVR may simply reflect the limitations of the conventional PVR formula, calculated as (mPAP − PCWP)/CO. This equation is an approximation derived under assumptions of steady flow and a simplified linear pressure–flow relationship, and therefore does not fully account for the pulsatile nature of the pulmonary circulation. In particular, when CO increases primarily because of a higher HR rather than an increase in stroke volume, calculated PVR may decrease even if true pulmonary vascular tone is unchanged or even increased. This is relevant in light of our previous findings showing that the higher MAP target increased CO mainly through HR, while stroke volume remained unchanged [16]. Under such conditions, if pulmonary artery pressure and wedge pressure remain relatively stable, or even if pulmonary pressures rise modestly, the denominator of the equation may increase disproportionately compared with the numerator, resulting in a lower calculated PVR despite no true fall in pulmonary vascular resistance. Thus, the lower calculated PVR in the higher MAP group should be interpreted cautiously, as it may represent a mathematical consequence of flow‐dependent estimation rather than a genuine reduction in right ventricular afterload.

The higher RV‐CPO observed in the higher MAP target group was likely, at least partly, driven by the increase in HR, which increased CO and, together with the higher mPAP, mechanically increased RV‐CPO. As RV‐CPO is inherently flow dependent, this finding should not necessarily be interpreted as improved intrinsic right ventricular function, but may instead reflect greater hemodynamic work performed by the right ventricle under higher vasopressor support.

An alternative explanation for the observed increase in mPAP without a corresponding rise in calculated PVR observed in the high‐MAP group may relate to the augmented venous return and right ventricular preload. Increased venous return to the right atrium and ventricle could result in a higher cardiac output [37]. The consequent increase in pulmonary arterial blood volume may promote recruitment and distension of pulmonary vessels, manifesting as a reduction in calculated PVR accompanied by parallel increases in mPAP and PCWP. The consistently lower RV‐CPO in the low MAP group is likely related to lower HR and reduced systemic venous return [37]. This finding could be explained by increased β‐adrenergic effects on RV contractility and forward flow in the high MAP group and might be worsened by impaired pulmonary vascular autoregulation and postresuscitation inflammation.

Since a varying degree of myocardial dysfunction was present in most patients, the increased blood flow and concurrently increased PCWP may put a strain on the left ventricle (LV), further contributing to the increase in mPAP through backwards failure of the LV. The decrease in PCWP over time in both groups is consistent with gradual recovery from postresuscitation myocardial dysfunction and redistribution of intravascular volume.

Clinical Implications

Our findings suggest that targeting a higher MAP after OHCA through increased vasopressor use modestly increases pulmonary pressures while only discretely decreasing PVR and increasing RV workload. However, whether these physiologic differences translate into any physiological or patient outcomes is uncertain. The main BOX trial found no difference in mortality or neurological outcomes between MAP strategies, indicating that the pulmonary and RV effects observed here are unlikely to confer a net clinical benefit at the tested MAP ranges. Nevertheless, in selected patients with preexisting RV dysfunction, pulmonary hypertension, or acute right‐sided failure, careful consideration of MAP targets and vasopressor dosing may be warranted.

The patients with severe hemodynamic instability and patients requiring mechanical circulatory support have been excluded from the BOX‐trial. Thus, it can be speculated whether increasing MAP with the resulting increased RV pre‐ and afterload and increased LV preload may be different in patients with more severe acute RV or LV failure. Since no clinical benefit was seen in the overall study, these hemodynamic findings could support a strategy of using as little vasopressors as possible to keep MAP > 63 mmHg.

Limitations

This substudy has several limitations. First, the observed differences in pulmonary hemodynamics were modest in absolute magnitude, raising questions about their clinical significance. Finally, placement of the PAC prior to initiation of the intervention would have allowed for more robust baseline comparisons. Additionally, this substudy included consecutive patients from two tertiary cardiac centers, which may have resulted in a somewhat selected cohort of OHCA patients. Compared with other international OHCA cohorts, our population contained a higher proportion of men, patients with ST‐elevation myocardial infarction and presentation with a shockable rhythm. In addition, 7% of patients had to be excluded, further contributing to selection bias and limiting external validity. Nevertheless, enrolling 93% of 800 critically ill patients in an acute setting constitutes, to our knowledge, one of the most comprehensive datasets with invasive hemodynamics available in this field.

Conclusions

In comatose OHCA survivors, targeting a higher MAP through increased vasopressor support modestly increased pulmonary artery pressures and PCWP, while decreasing calculated PVR. RV‐CPO was consistently lower in the low MAP group, indicating that vasopressor‐mediated higher MAP targets augment RV workload and afterload.
