 1. ABSTRACT
 New material innovations are needed for the extreme environments of next generation, hydrogen-fueled
turbine engines. Ceramic matrix composites (CMCs) offer high-temperature capability but are susceptible to
degradation in high-temperature water vapor. Pratt & Whitney’s (P&W) baseline silicon carbide (SiC)/SiC CMC
was initially applied to design a component in a hydrogen-fueled engine, verifying the existence of a design space
and extracting boundary conditions to inform testing parameters. Several material innovations, including two
fibers, interface coatings (IFC), and self-healing matrices (SHM), were investigated to improve high-temperature
performance in water vapor. A boron-doped pyrocarbon (B-PyC) IFC and a SHM matrix with layers of zirconium
nitride (ZrN) or zirconium diboride (ZrB2) were developed to fabricate minicomposites with either standard Hi-
Nicalon™1 Type S (HNS) fibers or new Tyranno®2 SA4 fibers (SA4). The B-PyC IFC is functional but lacks in
providing improved durability. A matrix consisting of thick layers of SiC and thin layers of ZrB2 shows promise
as a SHM that can effectively seal matrix cracks in this extreme environment. Minicomposites with HNS fibers
generally outperform those with SA4 fibers.

 2. INTRODUCTION
 This report describes the work performed under a 2022 United States Department of Energy Office of Fossil
Energy and Carbon Management initiative (DE-FOA-0002613). P&W proposed the development of a 2,700°F-
class SiC/SiC CMC with enhanced water vapor resistance. This composite would be an enabling technology for
future hydrogen turbine engine hot section components applicable to both industrial and aero gas turbines, using
mixed fuels or pure hydrogen. The higher temperature and higher water vapor content of a hydrogen-fueled
turbine engine is a unique environment that is expected to challenge the engine materials’ life, particularly CMCs.
The project introduced three main innovations to push the temperature capability of CMCs towards 2,700°F and
provide the CMC with higher resistance to water vapor environments.
 The first innovation was introduction of a new SiC fiber with a doped polycrystalline microstructure SA4.
This new generation of fibers offer larger SiC grain size as well as doped grain boundaries that provide better
thermal stability and oxidation resistance over HNS [1, 2]. In addition, the strength of this fiber has been
demonstrated to be comparable to that of HNS [3].
 The high-velocity, water vapor-rich environment is very aggressive to the surface of the composite systems,
both with and without an environmental barrier coating [4, 5, 6]. The high concentration of water vapor will also
degrade the life of the composite as water vapor ingresses into the composite microstructure through cracks and
pores to corrode the fiber-matrix interface [7, 8].
 The second innovation was to enhance the resistance of the fiber/matrix interfacial region to water vapor by
controlling the amount of boron using a B-PyC instead of the traditional boron nitride (BN) IFC. In an over-stress
situation of a component in a hydrogen-fueled turbine, a crack will propagate through the matrix, reaching the
fiber/matrix interface and oxidation will ensue much more rapidly than in a hydrocarbon-fueled turbine. A
traditional BN IFC might rapidly oxide into boron trioxide and quickly flux the SiC fiber and matrix in that
location, whereas the resulting glass from a B-PyC might rapidly form primarily as SiO2 and be more stable;
therefore, the goal was to decrease the amount of boron.
 The third innovation was the addition of zirconium (Zr)-containing layers as part of either the IFC or the
matrix. Upon oxidation of the matrix crack faces or IFC, the Zr-containing layers oxidize to form a borosilicate
glass enriched with Zr, intended to plug the matrix crack. The presence of Zr in borosilicate glasses, in nuclear
waste glasses for instance [9], has been shown to increase their resistance to water corrosion.
 The outline for the project is shown as a graphical abstract in Figure 1. Test conditions were obtained from
the Hydrogen Steam-injected Intercooled Turbine Engine (HySIITE) concept and scaled to a high-pressure
turbine CMC vane component. For rapid material development, minicomposites comprising a single tow of SiC
fiber were used as the primary test specimens. Minicomposites are relatively quicker to process, allowing for an
iterative approach of fabricating various process conditions of the Zr-containing IFC and matrix layers, and
testing in water vapor environments at relevant temperatures.

 3. OBJECTIVES AND SUCCESS CRITERIA
 The overarching objective of the project was to expand the temperature and operational envelope of SiC/SiC
composites to 2,700°F in high water vapor environments, enabling new applications, including hydrogen-fueled
turbine technology. The project scope focused on introducing several advanced technologies, such as employing
new SiC fiber technology with higher-temperature capabilities, incorporating a B-PyC interface layer and
applying Zr-based layers at or near the fiber-matrix interface to mitigate water-induced oxidation effects.
Additionally, the project aimed to develop rapid testing and evaluation capabilities for these extreme
environments.
 Phase 1 of the project was exploratory in nature (Tasks 2 through 5), addressing key risks identified at the
project's inception (Figure 2). The initial step involved validating the approach and ensuring the relevance of the
test conditions by computing boundary conditions for a conceptual engine vane developed by P&W as part of a
prior Advanced Research Projects Agency - Energy (ARPA-E) project, Contract DE-AR0001561 (Task 2).
Subsequently, five batches of minicomposites were produced (Task 3). A novel testing methodology was
developed to evaluate the produced minicomposites in high water vapor environments (Task 4). This testing
facilitated updates to the oxidation models. By the end of Phase 1, all identified risks were addressed except one:
the controlled deposition of the ZrN layer in the IFC around individual fibers was unsuccessful and deposition
inside the composite proved more challenging than anticipated. The maturity of the deposition process was
insufficient to successfully infiltrate full composite preforms as initially planned in Phase 2.

 Figure 2. Gannt Chart for the Project Showing Replan After Phase 1

 Phase 2 of the project was restructured to address the ZrN deposition challenge by focusing on improving the
deposition process for the Zr layer in the tows of the minicomposites. The approach mirrored that of Phase 1.
Three additional batches of minicomposites were produced (Task 6) using two distinct chemistries. One of these
chemistries successfully achieved the intended multilayer deposition of Zr-based compounds through two
minicomposite runs. They were subsequently tested in high water vapor environments (Task 7), and the oxidation
models were further refined based on these tests (Task 8). However, a fourth batch of minicomposites was not
produced in Phase 2, and the completion of testing in Task 7 and final calibration of the oxidation models in Task
8 was only partially completed due to run execution difficulties and delays. Note that eight runs were completed
as initially planned in the program, but a ninth run could have been carried out, time permitting.
 The program management task (Task 1) was successfully executed throughout the project.

 4. METHODS AND PROCEDURES
 All minicomposite specimens were fabricated through chemical vapor infiltration (CVI) in a state-of-the-art
reactor at P&W. Each CVI batch consisted of an IFC run and a matrix densification run. B-PyC and SiC layers
were deposited with standard precursor gases. Zr-containing layers make use of the chlorinator on the reactor. Zr
pellets are loaded into the chlorinator and chlorine (Cl2) flows through the bed to form zirconium chloride (ZrCl4)
gas that flows into the reactor chamber. A typical CVI run contained a graphite tool with a few dozen
minicomposites and Si wafers for characterization (Figure 3). The minicomposite tool was placed in either a
vertical orientation as shown in Figure 3 or a horizontal orientation for more uniform deposition. In each run, half
of the minicomposites were made of HNS fiber and the other half were made of SA4 fiber. Testing and
characterization were conducted on both types of minicomposites in each run.

 Figure 3. Schematic of a Typical Chemical Vapor Infiltration Reactor Loading

 Si wafers were analyzed for composition of the various CVI layers using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy
(XPS) and for CVI layer thickness using optical or scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Minicomposites also
underwent SEM for CVI layer thickness to assess differences in deposition throughout the reactor. Additionally,
the minicomposites underwent transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to measure CVI layer composition and
structure. Mechanical testing of minicomposites consisted of:
 • Room-temperature tensile tests to establish whether the IFC was producing baseline mechanical
 performance
 • High-temperature monotonic tensile tests
 • Stress-rupture tensile tests in water vapor.
 A water vapor stress-rupture rig was constructed for testing minicomposites in near 100% (1 atm) water vapor
at temperatures up to 1,540°C. The setup consisted of a hydrogen generator, a hydrogen torch, ceramic shielding
to prevent the specimen from experiencing pesting conditions, and a SiC susceptor used to heat the specimen
(Figure 4). The hydrogen torch was tested for durations of up to 100 hours. Stress-rupture testing was conducted

 5. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

5.1 COMPONENT BOUNDARY CONDITIONS IN HYDROGEN-FUELED GAS TURBINE
 The goal of Task 2 was to determine the component surface conditions (surface temperature, gas composition,
gas flow rate, and gas pressure) for a SiC-SiC CMC turbine vane in a hydrogen-fueled gas turbine engine. Work
was split between two subtasks: flow scaling work to generate analytical inlet boundary conditions and
component-level work to evaluate vane material conditions. The P&W Advanced Concepts group provided
turbine vane inlet gas conditions based on a snapshot cycle design for the conceptual HySIITE (Figure 5), which
is based on prior ARPA-E work. To evaluate component-level conditions in a HySIITE 2nd-stage turbine vane
(2V) application, the Hot Section Engineering sub-team performed a scaling exercise based on analysis of an
existing CMC vane developed for a PW308 engine used to demonstrate the technology.

Figure 5. Surface Temperature Contours for a Ceramic Matrix Composite 2nd-stage Turbine Vane in the HySIITE
 Concept

 As a first step in scaling the heat transfer coefficients (HTCs) from the PW308 engine analysis, a Nusselt
scaling parameter was established based on differences in geometry and cycle behavior between the conceptual
HySIITE and the PW308 engines. Considering core size differences alone (assuming similar operating conditions
and similar turbine airfoil design Mach), the geometric scale factor in the conceptual HySIITE accounts for a 15%
increase in heat transfer to the CMC 2V relative to the PW308 engine.
 Beyond geometric scaling, the team also applied a secondary scaler to the heat transfer coefficients to account
for the difference in gas path fluid chemical composition between the PW308 engine cycle and the HySIITE cycle
(from conventional gas path combustion byproducts to the H2O-heavy composition in HySIITE). The scaling
factor was fully decomposed into terms consisting of fluid property ratios, which are all functions of gas path
temperature, pressure, and gas composition. Ultimately, due to the higher water mass in the gas path, the fluid
properties component of this scaler in the conceptual HySIITE was found to account for another 37% increase in
heat transferred to the CMC 2V.
 The 2V-adjacent heat transfer coefficients (geometric and gas composition scaler) for the conceptual HySIITE
were found to be significantly higher than that of the conventional PW308 engine (with approximately 1.58x
being the product of the two scaling factors). Considering the HTC scaling factors and the gas composition from
cycle design, component surface conditions from PW308 engine were scaled to HySIITE conditions to generate
surface maps of HTCs and surface temperature (Figure 5). Peak surface temperatures for a CMC component
without an environmental barrier coating were found to be around 1,380°C. Results were used to guide
experimental conditions for stress-rupture testing of minicomposites detailed in Section 5.7.

5.2 OVERVIEW OF CHEMICAL VAPOR INFILTRATION FABRICATION OF MINICOMPOSITES
 Eight batches of minicomposites were fabricated in an iterative manner to develop the material B-PyC IFC
with Zr-containing layers. Batches 1 through 3 were used to develop a recipe for the B-PyC IFC. The targeted
composition was a doping of about 10 to 15 at% B with a thickness of a few hundred nm. The recipe used in the
third run successfully reached these targets and resulted in high-strength minicomposites with a functional IFC.
The composition for this recipe was used for the remaining Batches 4 through 7 with some modifications to the
time on gas and resulting thickness. Additionally, Batches 1 through 5 attempted to deposit ZrN as a second IFC
layer. This proved to be difficult due to the highly reactive nature of ZrCl4 with ammonia (NH3). ZrN process
conditions (namely temperature and gas flows) were varied in each run to promote deposition, but ZrN was
unable to be deposited as an IFC layer on fibers, although ZrN was observed on Si wafers in these runs. Layers
deposited on the wafers are more akin to chemical vapor deposition (CVD) rather than CVI, as there is no
infiltration aspect to deposition on wafers like that of a fibrous preform. To overcome this processing challenge,
Batches 6 through 8 consisted of a single-layer B-PyC IFC and a multilayer matrix consisting of alternating layers
of SiC and a Zr-containing layer, either ZrN or ZrB2. ZrB2 was added as an alternative layer material to ZrN in
Phase 2 because it was thought that ZrCl4 might be less reactive with boron trichloride (BCl3) than with NH3, and
the addition of the boron in the matrix near the zirconium would facilitate the stabilization of the glass in that
location.

5.3 CHARACTERIZATION OF BORON-DOPED PYROCARBON
 CVI B-PyC was deposited from precursor gases of methane (CH4) and BCl3. The temperature was fixed for
all batches and was chosen to be sufficiently high for the cracking of methane needed to deposit carbon. The key
variable in Batches 1 through 3 was the molar flow ratio of BCl3 to CH4. Batch 1 had a high ratio of BCl3 to CH4
and resulted in high roughness and an excess B-doping of about 50 at%. As a result, this IFC was not functional,
and the minicomposites were brittle and low strength. From TEM, it was observed that the B-PyC IFC layer
consisted of grains of boron carbide (B4C) embedded within B-PyC (Figure 6). The B-PyC regions had the
targeted 10 to 15 at% B. Composition was measured by TEM electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS). In
Figure 6, the image labeled (A) shows the SEM overview image of the minicomposite, while image (B) is a
higher-magnification SEM image showing rough B-PyC IFC deposited on fibers and the location of the lamella
used for TEM. Image (C) is a TEM image showing grains of B4C embedded in the B-PyC IFC layer.

5.4 CHARACTERIZATION OF ZIRCONIUM NITRIDE INTERFACE COATING
 Batches 1 through 5 attempted to deposit ZrN as an IFC layer on tooled minicomposites. The tooling is
representative of CMC panels or parts and removes the fiber tow of the minicomposite from the bulk flow of

5.8 MODELING STRESS-RUPTURE BEHAVIOR OF MINICOMPOSITES IN WATER VAPOR
5.8.1 Oxidation Model Background
 The numerical oxidational model combines the micro-mechanical model of Callaway and Zok [10] with
oxidation degradation mechanisms of Parthasarathy et al. [11] The original intent of the model was to capture the
degradation of a SiC-SiC minicomposite with the dual-layer IFC comprising B-PyC and ZrN. Additional features
described at the end of Section 5.8.1 were added to account for the self-healing matrix consisting of alternating
layers of SiC and ZrB2. However, the project ended before these features could be calibrated with experimental
data.
 For the micro-mechanics portion, the model considers individual filaments in the loading direction within a
homogenized SiC matrix. Individual filaments spanning the full length of the model geometry are split into small
segments and randomly assigned Weibull strengths. Similarly, the matrix spanning the full width is split into

 5-20

Final Report Pratt & Whitney DE-FE0032225

small segments along the length and assigned Weibull strengths from a separate distribution. Four input
parameters govern these strengths: the Weibull modulus and the Weibull reference strength for both fiber and
matrix. The sliding stress is the fifth input parameter, which governs the slip lengths and crack opening
displacements (COD). The five parameters are obtained from calibration to room temperature tensile testing and
fiber push-in testing (sliding stress) performed in Tasks 3 and 4.
 For the oxidation degradation portion of the model, governing equations from TAP are incorporated into the
micromechanics model to mathematically describe the inward diffusion of oxidants down matrix cracks and
subsequent attack of the interface coating and fiber resulting in a degradation of fiber strength. Two key
temperature-dependent parameters are needed for this portion of the model: oxidation kinetics of the SiC matrix
and strength degradation of the fiber. The former is well-described by Deal-Grove SiC oxidation laws and values
from the literature are used [8]. The latter requires a fit of experimental stress-rupture failure times and residual
strengths in the case of 100-hour runouts. This homogenized parameter depends on the complex oxidation
behavior of the IFC and the fiber. Therefore, the parameters used in this study are unique to the B-doped
pyrocarbon IFC system with a SiC matrix.
 With this calibration, the oxidation model can simulate stress-rupture conditions as a function of temperature,
stress, and partial pressure of H2O. The key time-dependent mechanisms at play in this model are COD, crack
sealing through crack face oxidation, matrix external surface oxidation reducing cross-sectional area, and fiber
strength degradation. Key model outputs are the time at which cracks seal and the residual stress-strain response
or the failure time if rupture occurs before a 100-hour runout in the simulation. Creep, which occurs at the high
temperatures of interest, is not included in this model.
 For the matrix consisting of alternating layers of SiC-ZrB2, additional features were added to the model but
were not calibrated. First, a second set of oxidation rate parameters for the matrix was added to account for
oxidation and crack sealing of the ZrB2. ZrB2 appears to oxidize more quickly than SiC, which allows for rapid
formation of a Zr-B-Si-O glass that plugs matrix cracks, limiting fiber and IFC degradation. Second, the matrix
strength was given a time dependency. As the exterior SiC surface oxidizes, the scale can become a critical flaw
that cracks at some critical scale thickness or upon crystallizing to a phase such as cristobalite. This allows for
degradation, and possibly failure, to occur after sealing of the initial matrix cracks formed upon loading.

 The model results indicate that a high PH2O environment severely accelerates the failure of the CMC system
from Phase 1 relative to dry air conditions. The two key mechanisms at play here are:
 • The volatilization of SiO2 in the presence of H2O (e.g., a switch from passivating, parabolic oxidation
 kinetics of SiC to non-passivating, para-linear oxidation kinetics of SiC)
 • Increased oxidation rates of SiC due to the higher permeability of H2O in SiO2 than O2 in SiO2.
 The former mechanism of switching to para-linear kinetics means that crack sealing time increases or may not
occur at all due to the recession of SiO2 along the matrix crack. The latter mechanism of increased oxidation rates
of SiC in H2O means that fibers degrade faster, leading to shorter stress-rupture lifetimes. To improve life, matrix
cracks must be sealed quickly relative to the rates of fiber oxidative degradation, and the cracks must remain
sealed to prevent further degradation. The addition of ZrB2 to SiC has been shown to undergo rapid oxidation
with large increases in weight and volume in water vapor at high temperatures, whereas SiC alone in these
conditions recesses and drops in weight [12]. This oxidation behavior of ZrB2-SiC is key to quickly sealing matrix
cracks and forming a stable Zr-rich oxide that keeps the crack plugged, preventing further ingress of oxidants to
the fiber surface.

5.9 CAPITAL COST ANALYSIS
 The Technology Readiness Level (TRL) of the SiC/SiC technology at P&W was assessed at TRL 3-4 (e.g.,
validation of properties at laboratory scale) at the start of the project. Concurrently with this project, the
manufacturing infrastructure for CMCs within P&W has experienced significant growth. However, the specific
technologies related to interface coating and the deposition of Zr-based layers were exploratory in nature and are
not part of P&W’s baseline material. This approach was minimally explored at the project’s inception and
continued to present challenges.
 Several technologies, including B-doped pyrocarbon, high temperature water vapor testing of
minicomposites, and aspects of modeling, have been readily integrated into P&W’s technology portfolio and used
in higher TRL technology developments. As a result, these technologies do not require additional capital
investment.

 5-24

Final Report Pratt & Whitney DE-FE0032225

 At the same time, valuable insights were gained in the deposition of less commonly studied layers within the
community, such as ZrN and ZrB2. This specific technology remains at TRL 2 to 3 and will require further
process development. Due to the high reactivity of ZrCl4, it is unlikely that an efficient solution can be developed
to infiltrate preforms in a large reactor. An alternative approach would involve identifying a Zr precursor that is
more suitable for deposition in a large reactor. This option would necessitate the addition of a gas cabinet and

of Zr metallic chips and Cl2 gas.
 In conclusion, while further development of the process would result in some additional equipment
modifications, it is not expected to demand significant capital investment at the laboratory scale. However, much
larger investments will be required to transfer the process to the larger reactors.

 6. CONCLUSIONS
 This project demonstrated that ZrN and ZrB2 are difficult materials for use in CVI processing. However, some
significant developments and learnings related to high-temperature water vapor stress-rupture performance have
been achieved.
 1. A functional IFC of B-PyC was quickly developed and repeatedly produced with uniform thickness
 and composition. HNS minicomposites with this IFC routinely had higher strength and failure strain
 than SA4 minicomposites. Further developments are needed to obtain composite behavior on SA4
 minicomposites.
 2. ZrCl4 precursor gas for ZrN or ZrB2 was shown to be highly reactive and difficult for controlled CVI
 deposition in tooled CMC preforms. ZrCl4 is more suitable for CVD-like processes such as in the
 later stages of CVI matrix densification in CMCs where depositing is primarily occurring near the
 exterior surface.
 3. ZrB2 showed promising characteristics of a self-healing matrix material. ZrB2 has a higher rate of
 oxidation at high temperature than SiC and undergoes a large volume expansion upon oxidation to
 plug matrix cracks (Pilling-Bedworth Ratio is approx. 2.7). Zr-B-O glass readily dissolves SiC from
 the matrix. If the ZrB2 layers are too thick, the matrix can undergo excessive oxidation and
 dissolution resulting in rapid stress-rupture failure. It was determined that thin layers of ZrB2 are
 needed for optimal self-healing behavior and long stress-rupture lifetimes in high-temperature water
 vapor environments.
 4. A new approach was developed for tensile and stress-rupture tests using H2/O2 torch. The water vapor
 content near the specimen was close to saturation in water vapor. The oxidation of SiC in those
 conditions appeared very rapidly and suggested that an environmental barrier coating may be required
 to mitigate this external attack and benefit from the internal oxidation protection from the Zr-bearing
 layers.

 7. RECOMMENDATIONS
 This project was successful in determining relevant test conditions in a hydrogen-fueled turbine engine and
developing process conditions needed to deposit a B-PyC interface and ZrB2 matrix layers. Stress-rupture testing
of minicomposites from Batch 8 with thin ZrB2 matrix layers was limited to short test durations of 10 hours.
Testing at longer durations up to 100 hours on the thin-layered ZrB2 minicomposites is the first step to study the
long-term stability of the Zr-B-Si-O glass that forms as the matrix crack plug. From there, the number of ZrB2
layers and thickness of these layers can be further optimized to improve stress-rupture performance. Next, the
process should be scaled up to fabricate panels with a CVI matrix comprising alternating layers of SiC and ZrB2.
Self-healing behavior is more effective in panels where the diffusion length of oxidants down matrix cracks is
significantly longer than in a single tow minicomposite. Fibers near the exterior surface in the outer plies of
panels may be oxidized during the matrix self-healing process, but those fibers in the interior would be expected
to remain largely intact and unoxidized. The application of an environmental barrier coating might be beneficial.
 Additional improvements in performance in high-temperature water vapor environments could be explored
from alternative IFC materials and designs. B-PyC is a functional IFC as demonstrated with the high strength of
HNS minicomposites at room temperature but is still readily oxidized at high temperatures. Future alternative
materials that can be processed through CVI, yield a functional IFC, and are durable are desired. Many materials
have proved to satisfy the first two requirements (e.g., B-PyC is processable and functional but not durable), but
finding one that satisfies all three has proven to be a great challenge to the CMC community for decades.
