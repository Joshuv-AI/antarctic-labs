Hybrid Versus Moderate Rough Implants in Periodontally Compromised Patients: A Randomized Controlled Split‐Mouth Study
Authors: Anton Dank, Daniël Wismeijer, Pieter van Elsas, Joao Mendes Tribst, Naichuan Su, Albert Feilzer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroTo compare clinical conditions and the implant marginal bone level after 1 year following placement of hybrid and moderate rough implants in the mandible of periodontally compromised patients using standardized radiographs.MethodsIn a multicenter study (three centers), thirty‐nine periodontally compromised patients were randomly treated in a split‐mouth study with both hybrid and moderate rough implants in the mandible at the premolar and molar site. In each patient, two tissue‐level implants were inserted, and a healing cap was placed. After 3 months, the implants were restored with a FPD. The primary outcome consisted of radiographic assessments performed at 1‐year follow‐up.ResultsThirty‐six patients completed the study. Both types of implants showed a survival rate of 94.4% ± 3.8% (95% CI 87.0%–100%); all lost implants failed to achieve osseointegration. There is no significant difference found in the change of the bone level over time within the first year between the two groups (p = 0.25 for mesial pocket bone, p = 0.99 for distal pocket bone, p = 0.98 for mesial proximal bone and p = 0.68 for distal proximal bone).ConclusionAfter one year, no statistically significant radiographic and clinical differences were observed between hybrid and moderately rough implants. However, peri‐implant diseases often manifest after a longer period. This underscores the importance of extended follow‐up. A subsequent evaluation is planned at the three‐year mark.

Introduction

The first endosseous titanium implants, placed in the 1960s, were machined implants with a minimal surface roughness [1]. These had high long‐term survival and success rates in the mandible but somewhat lower rates in the maxilla [2]. To enhance implant success and reduce the osseointegration period, various techniques have been employed to increase titanium surface roughness. A distinction is made between minimally rough (surface roughness (Sa) ≈ 0.5–1.0 μm) and moderately rough (Sa ≈ 1.0–2.0 μm) titanium surfaces, with minimally rough preferred for contact with soft tissues and moderately rough for contact with bone [3]. Minimally rough implants are easier to clean and maintain, which is crucial for long‐term implant health. Moderately rough is showing an improved osseointegration behavior [4]. Today, most clinicians prefer implants with a moderately rough surface roughness [5].

A major drawback of dental implantology is the potential for peri‐implant complications, which remain difficult to manage effectively [6]. Prevention should therefore be a priority, especially in high‐risk patient populations, for example, periodontal susceptible patients and smokers. The design and physiochemical characteristics of a dental implant surface determine the attraction for bacterial adhesion and colonization. A correlation exists between implant surface roughness and bacterial adhesion [7, 8, 9]. To benefit from advantages of both minimal rough and moderate rough implants, hybrid implants have been introduced. These have a moderate rough surface in the apical and corpus section, while the marginal section has a minimal rough surface. It was hypothesized that such hybrid implants can reduce the incidence of peri‐implantitis in the periodontal compromised and smoking patients, and consequently could be of benefit in high‐risk patients. The survival rate of hybrid implants has been evaluated in edentulous patients and in partially edentate patients, but not in periodontally compromised partially edentate patients [10, 11, 12]. Therefore, we planned to conduct a multicenter randomized controlled trial evaluating hybrid implants in partially edentulous periodontally compromised individuals. The study's primary objective was to compare marginal bone level changes during the first year after implant placement. Secondary objectives included evaluating implant survival, implant stability, and marginal peri‐implant soft‐tissue parameters (plaque accumulation, bleeding on probing, and probing pocket depths).

Materials & Methods

Study Design

The present study was designed to compare treatments using moderate rough and hybrid tissue level implants (Southern Implants 4.0 × 10.0 mm) in the posterior mandible of periodontally compromised patients in a multicenter split‐mouth randomized controlled clinical trial. Three clinics were participating in this study: the department of Reconstructive Oral Care of the Academic Centre for Dentistry Amsterdam (ACTA) and two affiliated dental implant clinics in The Netherlands (in Dieren and Roermond resp.). At each center, one experienced clinician performed all surgical interventions, prosthetic procedures, and clinical observations.

The study was approved by the medical ethical committee (NL63989.029.19) and registered in the Dutch trial register (NL8439).

Research Cohort Size

The primary outcome (changes in radiographical implant marginal bone level after one year) was used to determine the power of the study. We used the statistical software program G*power to determine the number of subjects needed based on two‐tailed pair‐sample t‐test [13]. We assumed that the effect size of the outcome between the two groups is medium (i.e., 0.50), and considered that the minimal relevant change in marginal bone level between the two studied implant types was 0.50 mm. Therefore, the standard deviation of the difference was assumed to be 1 [14]. 34 subjects were needed to achieve 80% power to detect a statistically significant difference (α = 0.05). Compensating for a possible drop‐out rate of 20%, 43 subjects were required in total.

Patient Selection

Periodontally compromised patients with two single partially edentulous areas in the mandibular premolar and molar region in the 3rd and 4th quadrants were eligible for inclusion in the study.

Patients were referred between March 2020 and March 2024 to one of the three participating clinics by their respective general practitioner. Following a thorough review of medical and dental histories, description of the clinical procedures and financial implications, followed by an explanation of the study, the patients had a 2‐week window to decide if they wanted to participate in the study or not. After agreement, a signed informed consent was obtained.

Inclusion Criteria

To be eligible to participate in this study a patient needed to be periodontally compromised implying that they had a history of proximal attachment loss of > 5 mm in > 30% of teeth present and were enrolled in a supportive periodontal therapy (SPT) program after active periodontal treatment. Planning

The subject screening included a routine clinical and radiographical evaluation after taking a standardized panoramic radiograph, mandatory to plan the implant surgery. A clinical and radiographical periodontal examination was executed to determine the periodontal status of the patient. An impression of both the maxilla and mandible was made with polyether impression material (Impregum Penta, 3 M ESPE, Seefeld, Germany). Based on these impressions, a digital prosthetic driven planning was made for both implant locations, and custom jigs were printed (3Shape Model Builder and coDiagnostiX, 3Shape A/S, Denmark; Rapidshape P30+ printer, Rapid Shape GmbH, Baden‐Württemberg, Germany; Straumann PPro surgical guide 1000 g resin, Straumann Holding AG, Basel, Switzerland). If the residual bone volume of the alveolar inferior processus needed to be assessed, a ridge mapping procedure was performed as described by Ten Bruggenkate [15]. A CBCT‐scan was taken when the ridge mapping procedure gave insufficient clarity. As this was not mandatory, it was strictly judged by the investigator's clinical insight in accordance with the ALARA‐principle. Individualized Xray jigs were manufactured based on the impressions and the implant jig.

Surgical Procedures

Preoperatively, chlorhexidine 0.12% mouthwash was given. Postoperatively, patients continued with chlorhexidine rinsing twice daily for 7 days. No antibiotics were prescribed. Patients were advised to use Acetaminophen when necessary and reported back if used. They were asked to follow a soft diet for six weeks post‐surgery. Intermittent extra‐oral cooling with a cold pack was furthermore advised for 48 h. All surgeries were performed under local anesthesia (Articaine hydrochloride 4% with Epinephrine 1:100000, Ultracain DS, Sanofi‐Aventis, France).

A locked computer software program was used to randomly assign the designated implant to either the 3rd or 4th quadrant after implant osteotomy (CastorEDC, Castor, The Netherlands). A dental nurse, not involved in the study, was responsible for this procedure. Patients were not informed about the allocation result. Within the study, the clinician could not be blinded, as the surface of the implant could be visualized during placement.

After a crestal incision and intrasulcular incision at the adjacent teeth, a buccal and lingual full thickness mucoperiostal flap was reflected. The shape of the bone ridge was analyzed, and sharp ridges were removed. The implant osteotomy was performed according to the Southern Implant surgical drilling protocol. During the surgery, the pre‐manufactured non‐restrictive surgical guide with the ideal prospective implant crown was used for implant placement. All osteotomies were done under excessive cooling with sterile saline solution and a speed of 1000 rpm. To facilitate bone chip removal, osteotomies were conducted with intermittent movement applying moderate pressure. The osteotomies were identical for both implant procedures as the macro‐design of both implants is identical. The implant was placed at a speed of 30 rpm at equicrestal position, both mesial and distal even with the alveolar crest. Thus, the junction from implant surface to implant polished neck was placed on bone level. If necessary, the bone was flattened. The distance between the implants and the adjacent teeth was at least 1.5 mm. The insertion torque was measured with a manual torque wrench or with the surgical unit. Primary implant stability was assessed by insertion torque measurement and the absolute absence of axial or rotational movement by manipulation of the implant mount before removal. During implant placement, peak insertion torque was recorded in Newton centimeters (Ncm). The Implant Stability Quotient was also measured at implant placement (Osstell ISQ 100500, Osstell, Götenborg, Sweden). If implant stability was not reached, the patient was excluded from the dataset of the project but was treated and completed within the same protocol after four months of healing. Immediately after surgery, the pre‐made customized implant position transfer jig was used to pick up an impression coping from the implant to register the implant position. All implants were placed non‐submerged (one stage). The height of the screw‐retained healing abutment depended on local soft tissue thickness. Both wounds were closed tension free with Polypropylene 6–0 suture (Prolene, Johnson and Johnson MedTech, United States). Per‐operative clinical photographs were obtained. Routine oral hygiene (brushing) at the implant site was not allowed until sutures were removed after 7 days. At this time point an intraoral radiograph with an individual radiographic jig was obtained. Furthermore, the healing process was reviewed, and the patient was given oral hygiene instructions to clean the healing abutment carefully with a soft (surgical) manual toothbrush.

Prosthetic Procedures

Six weeks after implant surgery, oral hygiene was checked through visual inspection of plaque on the healing caps and the condition of the peri‐implant mucosa. If plaque was noticed, the patient received oral hygiene reinforcement. After 3 months (delayed loading protocol), a final full arch open tray impression using a custom acrylic resin impression tray and polyether impression material (Impregum Penta, 3 M ESPE, Seefeld, Germany) was taken at implant level and of the opposite arch. In absence of a stable Maximum Intercuspal Position (MIP), the maxillary‐mandibular relationship was recorded with registration material (Futar D, Kettenbach Dental, Germany). The final full zirconia FPDs (Vita YZST, Vita Zahnfabrik H. Rauter GmbH & Co. KG, Germany) were cemented in the dental lab to Southern Implants passive abutments with resin cement (Multilink, Ivoclar Vivadent AG, Lichtenstein). The FPD's were not stained or glazed, but only highly polished. Before delivery of the restorations, interproximal contact points were checked with metallic articulating foil (Shimstock 8 μm, Bausch, Germany). Necessary adjustments were made chairside with diamond drill (maximum 10 000 rpm) and water spray. After the adjustments, the zirconia surface was polished carefully with Zirconia Twist Dia polishing kit (Katana, Kuraray Noritake Dental, Japan). The occlusion was designed to have minimal occlusal contact on the implants: a shimstock was put in between the implant FPD and the antagonist under MIP. The shimstock was required to be pulled out with a light drag without tearing. If the shimstock tears, excessive contact was present. Therefore, a colored articulating paper (Arti‐Check micro‐thin 40 μm, Bausch, Germany) was needed to check the location of the contact. Adjustments were required until light drag is obtained with the shimstock. Lateraltrusive, mediotrusive, and protrusive movements also were required to be carefully evaluated. During these movements, no contact on the FPDs was present. If any contact was present in these movements, it was considered as an interference and further adjustment was required. The occlusal scheme was kept the same as the start of the patient treatment. This means anterior guidance with posterior disclusion and canine disclusion or group function, or partial group function in lateral excursive movements.

All FPD's were screw retained. The internal abutment screws were torqued to 35 Ncm according to the manufacturer's guideline with a manual torque wrench. Tightening of the abutment without implant movement or pain was considered a sign of clinical osseointegration. The fit of the FPD's was checked manually by the absence of resistance when tightening the abutment screw. The connection of the FPD's with the implants was doublechecked on an intra‐oral radiograph. The screw‐access hole was closed with sterile PTFE tape and sealed with an opaque restoration material (Filtek Supreme XTE, 3 M ESPE, Seefeld, Germany). Impressions of the new situation were taken from both arches with polyether impression material (Impregum Penta, 3 M ESPE, Seefeld, Germany).

Maintenance

Patients received a thorough oral hygiene instruction using both an electric toothbrush and SuperFloss (Oral B, Proctor and Gamble, Iowa City, Iowa, United States) and were scheduled for routine maintenance recalls every 6 months with the dental hygienist of the section of Reconstructive Oral Care of ACTA or affiliated clinic.

Radiographic Assessments

Radiographic assessment was performed according to the standard protocol (paralleling technique, 70 kV, 7 mA, 0.250 s). There was no extra radiation exposure during this study. An intra‐oral periapical radiograph was taken directly after implant placement (T0), 2 months after FPD delivery (T2, baseline) and after 12, 24 and 36 months of loading (T12, T24, T36), with a phosphor plate (Durr Dental, Bietigheim‐Bissingen, Germany) and an X‐ray device with a square tube (Planmeca, Helsinki, Finland) using the long‐cone paralleling technique and a pre‐made jig. All radiographs were taken with the same kV and mA. For each patient, an individualized full‐arch X‐ray jig was made to standardize each radiograph. The jig was designed with the picked‐up implant position on the master model. The inclination of the implants was transferred to the individualized X‐ray aiming device to be sure the position of the X‐ray is parallel to the placed implants (Figure 3).

Individualized X‐ray aiming device.

The phosphor plate was inserted vertically into the slot. The locator ring was fitted onto the indicator rod opposite the film packet. The assembly was then positioned in the mouth. The X‐ray tube was positioned in the locator. The vertical distance from the mesial and distal end point of the coronal flat part (without windings) of the implant to the pocket and proximal bone is measured to the nearest 0.1 mm. The pocket bone is defined as the dense radiopaque bone in contact with the implant. The proximal bone is defined as the highest more radiolucent bone still in contact with the implant. The change in mean marginal bone level, including both pocket bone and proximal bone changes, was calculated for each implant and presented for both implant types at each time point. The calibration was performed using the implant diameter as a reference, according to the exact dimensions provided by the manufacturer. To assess the reliability of the radiographic examination, three outcome assessors made independent blinded measurements. Although one of the assessors was also one of the three clinicians, all radiographic data were stored and analyzed in a fully anonymized and randomized manner to ensure double blinding.

All radiographs were randomly numbered, and the time point was blinded for both examiners. The radiographs were displayed in a software program (Emago, Oral Diagnostic Systems, The Netherlands) on a 19‐in. LCD screen (HP Compaq LA2205wg, USA) in a darkened room. The screen resolution is 1024 pixels. The measuring tool of the software was used to perform the measurements, taking the calibration into account. Contrast, brightness, and zoom are standardized for all radiographs.

Statistical Analysis

Interclass correlation coefficient (ICC) analysis (two‐way random and absolute agreement) was performed to assess the inter‐rater agreement between the three reviewers.

For statistical analysis, the SPSS statistical package (SPSS 29.0 IBM, New York, USA) and the lmerTest package (R‐Studio 4.2.1 Version 2025.05 1 + 5 + 13 RStudio Team, Boston USA) were used. In all analyses, a statistically significant level of 0.05 is chosen.

The survival rate is the percentage of implants in the study group that remain functional after one year. This rate includes immediate, early, and late implant failures.

To compare clinical assessments between the two groups for ordinal data including bleeding on probing and amount of plaque, Wilcoxon signed rank tests were used. For continuous ratio data, that is, PPD, paired sample t‐tests were used.

A modified intention‐to‐treat (mITT) analysis was performed. Patients were excluded if intraoperative findings revealed that the protocol‐defined procedure could not be completed as assigned, including requiring guided bone augmentation or inability to achieve primary implant stability. For all other aspects of the study, the ’normal’ principle was applied [16].

To assess the effect of implant types and time and their interaction on the outcomes of radiographic analyses, multilevel linear regression analyses were performed due to the multilevel data structure (i.e., each individual patient was measured at multiple time points). For each outcome, we first performed a multilevel linear regression analysis with random intercept only (model 1), and then a multilevel linear regression analysis with random intercept and random slope (model 2). The −2 log‐likelihood values were used to compare the fit between the two models based on Chi‐square tests. The model with significantly lower −2 log‐likelihood was used as the final model per outcome. In each multilevel regression analysis, we corrected for the confounders including clinical center, smoking (non‐smoking vs. smoking) and the position of the implant (premolar vs. molar).

Results

Patients

A total of 39 eligible subjects were included from March 2020 to March 2024. Due to the difficulty in recruiting patients, we decided to terminate the inclusion after four years. The actual sample size in the analysis was 36, which is larger than the minimum required sample size in the sample size calculation without adjusting for the drop‐out rate (i.e., 34). This indicates that our sample size in the analysis is sufficient.

Implant Survival Rate

Four implants became mobile and failed to osseointegrate, resulting in early implant loss. Two of these implants demonstrated relatively high insertion torque values (50 Ncm). Furthermore, in both cases, a non‐vital adjacent natural tooth was detected during the diagnostic evaluation of the reported pain symptoms. Consequently, these adjacent teeth underwent endodontic treatment. The two other implants, both placed in the same patient, failed to osseointegrate in low‐density type D4 bone. This indicated a survival rate of 94.4% ± 3.8% (95% CI 87.0–100%) for the type H group and 94.4% + 3.8% (95% CI 87.0–100%) for the type MR group.

Implant Insertion Torque

Type H implants were inserted with a mean torque value of 38.6 ± 9.5 Ncm (95% CI, 35.4–41.8). One implant showed an insertion torque value lower than 20 Ncm. 13 implants were placed with a value above 40 Ncm (36%).

For type MR implants we calculated a mean insertion torque value of 37.2 ± 9.1 Ncm (95% CI, 34.1–40.3). Also, one implant showed a value lower than 20 Ncm. 10 implants were inserted with a value above 40 Ncm (28%).

Implant Stability Quotient

ISQ values of the type H osseointegrated implants at implant placement ranged from 45 to 86 with a mean 76.3 ± 9.5 (95% CI, 72.9–79.6). For the type MR the values ranged from 43 to 86 with a mean 72.9 ± 11.7 (95% CI, 68.8–76.7). Inter‐Observer Correlation

Radiographic measurements were done by three independent observers. The Inter‐observer intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for distal pocket bone between the 3 raters is 0.709 (95% CI: 0.606–0.783), indicating moderate agreement. ICC for mesial pocket bone between the 3 raters is 0.556 (0.439–0.650), indicating moderate agreement. ICC for distal proximal bone is 0.751 (0.621–0.830), indicating good agreement. ICC for mesial proximal bone is 0.570 (0.379–0.698), indicating moderate agreement.

Radiographic Marginal Bone Level Changes

For the mesial pocket bone there was no significant difference in the outcome between the H and MR groups, averaged across the four time points (regression coefficient: 0.062 (95% CI: −0.231; 0.354), F(1,234.615) = 0.170, p = 0.681, Table 2) when adjusting for center, smoking and position of implants. Discussion

The present study revealed that both hybrid and moderate rough implants perform similarly after 1 year when placed in the posterior mandible of periodontal compromised patients.

When we examine the baseline characteristics, we observe that the variable implant site location almost entirely matches between both study groups. This makes them highly comparable. The same applies to smoking; both groups have an equal number of smokers. It is well‐established that smoking has a negative impact on the outcome of oral implantology treatments [17]. However, only mild smokers (8 cigarettes/day or less) were included in this study.

The implant survival rate is 94.4%, equal for both study groups. All four implants failed before achieving osseointegration. For two of the four implants, the exact cause of failure remains unclear. It is possible that the relatively high insertion torque and/or the non‐vital status of the adjacent teeth contributed to the outcome. For the other two implants, the low bone density appears to have been a contributing factor. The survival rate is favorable and similar to the findings reported by Roccuzzo et al., 2022. They treated eighty‐four periodontal compromised patients and reported an overall implant survival rate of 93% after 20‐years [18].

In general, the implants of both groups were placed with relatively high insertion torque. Several explanations may account for this phenomenon. First, the use of a tapered implant, as applied in this study, may contribute to the observed effect. Tapered implants possess a more cutting design, which enables them to achieve higher insertion torques, even in softer bone. Second, anatomical differences in bone density between regions play a significant role. The mandible generally consists of denser bone types, namely type D1 and D2, compared to the maxilla, which more often presents with softer bone types such as D3 and D4. Lastly, specific regions within the mandible, particularly the posterior area near the second molar, are characterized by a predominant cortical bone structure and reduced vascularization, which may further influence implant stability and insertion torque. It seems that high insertion torque is more likely to introduce bone compression and consequently bone necrosis. But in their systematic review and meta‐analysis, Lemos et al., 2021, concluded that a high insertion torque does not affect implant survival rate or marginal bone loss [19].

From the moment of implant placement to the delivery of the suprastructure, the ISQ gradually increases for both implant groups. Only small differences are observable in this regard. The achieved ISQ values are clinically considered satisfactory and consistently higher than those reported in the study by Gehrke et al., 2023 [20].

Radiographically, significant differences over time are observed for mesial pocket bone, mesial proximal bone and distal pocket bone when averaging both study groups, but not for distal proximal bone. This phenomenon could tentatively be explained by several factors. One possible explanation is that in periodontal compromised patients with attachment loss, implants placed posterior to an end‐position tooth may encounter a dorsal slope. This is particularly in premolar regions where the implant is positioned closer to the adjacent tooth. In addition, the presence of the anatomically stable linea obliqua externa might play a role. Another contributing factor could be the influence of the Curve of Spee. As implants are placed more posteriorly, they may be inserted with a slight mesial angulation to follow this curve according to preoperative planning and the interoperative used non‐restrictive surgical guide. The patient's maximal mouth opening may have been a contributing factor, especially in cases involving the most posterior molar positions. Consequently, the smooth, supra‐bony portion of a tissue‐level implant, especially on the mesial aspect, comes into infra‐bony contact upon placement. In order to ensure correct positioning of the distal part of the implant, this may contribute to unavoidable biological bone loss in the mesial area. Considering the significant differences observed among smokers in the mesial proximal and mesial pocket measurements, smoking may be a contributing factor that strengthens this effect. However, these explanations remain hypothetical and would require further investigation to confirm.

As we are comparing premolars with molars, the question arises if this can influence our results? Kolte et al. 2021 stated, based on a retrospective clinical and radiographic analysis, that premolar implant sites demonstrate a greater propensity for marginal bone loss than molar implant sites [21]. In the present study, no significant differences in marginal bone loss were observed between different positions of the implants within the lower arch.

Like the prospective study of Serrano et al. 2022, no significant differences in clinical outcomes were observed after 1 year at hybrid and moderate rough implants placed in periodontal compromised patients [22]. It is important to note that in the study by Serrano et al., bone‐level implants were used, in contrast to the tissue‐level implants placed in the present study, which represents a fundamentally different implantological concept for both. For periodontal compromised patients, Quirynen et al. reported no decisive influence of implant surface roughness on clinical outcomes under adequate maintenance, including no clear clinical advantage of specific surface configurations such as hybrid designs in terms of long‐term marginal bone stability [23].

A greater amount of distal proximal marginal bone loss was observed in implants placed at the RoerMondzorg clinic compared with those placed at the ACTA clinic, although no clear scientific rationale could be identified for this difference.

The current study did not account for the influence of soft‐tissue thickness on marginal bone level during the surgical procedures. Van Eekeren et al. 2017 concluded that when tissue‐level implants are used and the implant‐abutment connection is 2.5 mm above the crestal bone level, there is no statistically significant difference in crestal bone change [24]. The implants used in this study all feature a 1.8 mm polished neck, which in theory may lead to bone remodeling, particularly on the mesial aspect of the implants, as described above. However, as tissue‐level implants were used, the surgical protocol permitted only a single placement approach, namely equigingival positioning.

Three implants received extra autologous bone chips on the vestibular aspect. As the implants were fully enveloped by the native bone and only autologous particles were used preventively, it may be assumed that this did not affect the outcome.

Limitations

At the start of the current study, the used implants were newly designed by Southern Implants company upon request of our research group; the type H was, in fact, even a prototype implant. Although all three surgeons are highly experienced, there is always a certain learning curve when using new drill protocols. Chrcanovic et al. reported that clinician experience is associated with implant failure rates, suggesting an experience‐dependent improvement in clinical outcomes [25]. This factor should nonetheless be taken into consideration when interpreting the overall results. However, when comparing the intergroup outcomes, this does not represent a confounding factor, as the drilling protocols for both implant types were identical.

A caveat when quantifying and making statements about marginal bone level changes is that, as is customary in the scientific literature, these are often based on two‐dimensional images. From other peri‐implantitis studies, we know that in most cases, it involves a three‐dimensional defect [26]. Monje et al. reported in a cone‐beam computed tomography study, in which 158 implants with peri‐implantitis were analyzed, that the most prevalent bone defect morphology was an infraosseous two‐ to three‐wall defect [27]. Therefore, it is important to exercise caution when drawing conclusions. However, it may be stated that the combined use of clinical and radiographic data allows for an adequate diagnosis without the need for three‐dimensional imaging, in accordance with the ALARA principles.

The current article only describes a one‐year follow‐up period. However, peri‐implant diseases typically manifest clinically after a longer observational period, as their onset and progression are generally time‐dependent and often become evident several years after implant placement [28, 29].

Nevertheless, early marginal bone loss may serve as a predictor of long‐term peri‐implant pathology, with a significantly increased risk of peri‐implantitis when early bone loss exceeds thresholds of 0.5 to 1 mm, particularly in the presence of additional risk factors such as a history of periodontal disease prior to implant treatment [26, 30].

Conclusion

After one year, no statistically significant radiographical and clinical differences were observed between hybrid and moderately rough implants in periodontal compromised patients. However, peri‐implant diseases often manifest after a longer period. This underscores the importance of extended follow‐up. A subsequent evaluation for the current patient group is planned at the three‐year mark.
