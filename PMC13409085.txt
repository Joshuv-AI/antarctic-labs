Clinical and cost-effectiveness of an acute medical unit model in Singapore: protocol for a pilot prospective cohort study
Authors: John Tshon Yit Soong, Jose M Valderas, Derek Bell, Mihir Ghandi, Nicholas Graves, Marcus Ong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHealthcare systems face increasing strain from population ageing, multimorbidity and rising emergency department (ED) attendances. Acute medical units (AMUs) have been adopted internationally to improve patient flow and reduce hospital length of stay, but prospective evidence on their clinical and economic effectiveness remains limited, particularly in Singapore. This study aims to evaluate the clinical outcomes and cost-effectiveness of an AMU model compared with usual care in the general ward at a Singapore tertiary hospital.Methods and analysisThis is an ongoing single-centre prospective observational cohort study conducted at the National University Hospital, Singapore. Adults admitted non-electively under medical services, whose first admission ward is either the AMU or a general ward, will be recruited and followed for 6 months after index discharge. Recruitment commenced in September 2024 and will continue through December 2025, with final follow-up expected in July 2026. The primary endpoint is total acute-care bed-days within 6 months of index admission, including bed-days accrued during the index admission and any subsequent non-elective acute-care admissions within 6 months of the index admission date. Secondary endpoints include ED reattendance, unplanned readmission, mortality, patient-reported outcomes (EuroQol Five-Dimension Five-Level, WHO-Five Well-being Index, Patient Activation Measure-13, Patient Health Questionnaire-9, Barthel Index and Rapid Estimate of Adult Literacy in Medicine-Short Form), healthcare utilisation and healthcare costs. Because allocation to AMU or general ward care is determined by routine service processes rather than randomisation, comparative analyses will use propensity score inverse probability weighting to reduce measured confounding. Repeated patient-reported outcomes will be analysed using mixed-effects models. The economic evaluation will estimate incremental costs and quality-adjusted life years associated with AMU care compared with usual care over a 6-month period.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval has been granted by the National Healthcare Group Domain Specific Review Board, Singapore (Reference: 2023/00590). Written informed consent will be obtained from participants or their legally authorised representatives. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations and stakeholder engagement and will inform the design of a future multicentre evaluation.Trial registration numberNCT07536035.

Introduction

Healthcare systems worldwide face increasing pressure from population ageing, multimorbidity and rising demand for urgent and unscheduled care. By 2030, one in six people globally will be aged 60 years or older, with ageing accelerating particularly rapidly in East and Southeast Asia, Latin America and Africa compared with historical trajectories in Western Europe.1 2 Emergency department (ED) attendances also vary substantially across health systems, averaging 27 visits per 100 population annually in Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development countries, with peaks of 50 per 100.3

In Singapore, the share of residents aged 65 years and older increased from 12.4% in 2014 to 19.9% in 2024 and is projected to reach 24.1% by 2030. Alongside rising multimorbidity, this has increased demand for acute care, particularly among older adults with complex needs. Public hospital ED attendances increased by an average of 5.6% annually between 2005 and 2016, exceeding population growth, with the sharpest increases among older adults.4 5 Healthcare systems therefore need to adapt acute and chronic care delivery to changing population needs.6

Acute medical units (AMUs) have been introduced internationally as a service model for early assessment, investigation and treatment of adults with acute medical illness. AMUs are dedicated receiving wards intended to provide rapid senior review, early diagnostics, multidisciplinary assessment and timely discharge or transfer, usually within 24–72 hours. Core features include a close interface with ED services, consultant-led care, structured multidisciplinary input and a focus on patient flow.7

International evidence suggests AMUs may improve clinical and operational outcomes. Systematic reviews report reductions in hospital length of stay of 0.3–2.62 days, in-hospital mortality of 0.1%–8.8% and non-elective readmissions of 0.7%–5.2%, alongside improved patient flow.7 These gains are thought to reflect process reliability, including earlier senior review, same-day diagnostics, structured handovers and defined discharge criteria,8 thereby rendering the model scalable in health systems constrained by bed capacity and workforce availability. The specialty has become embedded within healthcare systems in the UK,9 Australia10 and parts of Europe,11 but evidence remains limited by retrospective designs, implementation heterogeneity and incomplete evaluation of patient-centred and economic outcomes.

In Singapore, AMUs have become increasingly established across public hospitals, but prospective evidence on their effectiveness and cost-effectiveness remains lacking. Local retrospective studies suggest reductions in readmissions and inpatient stays: one tertiary-centre study reported a 1.4%–2.2% reduction in readmissions relative to diagnosis-matched general ward (GW) controls,12 while an ED-based acute medical team evaluation reported average savings of one inpatient day and lower patient charges.13 However, longer-term service utilisation, patient-reported outcomes and cost-effectiveness have not been comprehensively evaluated. A prospective evaluation is therefore needed to assess the comparative impact of AMU care within Singapore, including downstream acute-care utilisation, patient-reported outcomes and costs.

Objectives and hypothesis

The primary aim of this study is to evaluate the clinical outcomes and cost-effectiveness of an AMU model compared with usual GW care among adults admitted with acute medical illness at a Singapore tertiary hospital. We hypothesise that, compared with usual GW care, AMU care will be associated with lower acute-care bed-day use over 6 months and improved service and patient-reported outcomes, without higher mortality or unplanned readmission.

Methods

Study design and setting

This is an ongoing single-centre prospective observational cohort study conducted at the National University Hospital, Singapore, an academic medical centre with over 1200 beds. Recruitment commenced in September 2024 and is planned to continue until December 2025; final follow-up is expected in July 2026. Participants will be followed for 6 months after index hospitalisation. The study will compare patients whose first admission ward is the AMU with those admitted first to a GW.

Allocation to AMU or GW care is determined by routine clinical and operational processes rather than by the study team. Patients with acute medical illness are assessed in the ED or specialist clinic and referred for non-elective medical admission when inpatient care is required. Bed allocation follows the usual hospital workflow and may be influenced by AMU and GW bed availability, clinical suitability for AMU care, specialty-specific care needs, infection control or isolation requirements, time of admission, staffing and operational capacity, illness severity and clinician judgement. The AMU is covered by a teaching service consultant on a 7-day roster and accepts patients every day, both within and outside routine working hours.

These allocation processes may introduce selection bias and residual confounding. Measured confounding will be addressed using propensity score inverse probability weighting with prespecified baseline covariates, while recognising that unmeasured operational and clinician-level factors may remain.

Fidelity will be assessed using routinely captured operational indicators. Fidelity metrics will be summarised monthly and reviewed during monitoring meetings.

GW group

The comparison group comprises patients admitted directly from the ED or specialist clinic to a GW as the first inpatient ward during the index admission under the Division of Advanced Internal Medicine. These patients will receive usual inpatient medical care in accordance with routine GW practice, including medical review, investigation, treatment, discharge planning and follow-up as determined by the treating team.

After discharge, participants in both groups will continue to receive usual follow-up care through primary care, specialist outpatient clinics or other services as determined by their treating teams.

Participants (inclusion/exclusion criteria)

Eligible participants will be adults aged 21 years or older who have a non-elective medical admission from the ED or specialist clinics and whose first admission ward during the index admission is either the AMU or a GW.

Patients will be approached for recruitment before discharge from the index admission. As this recruitment strategy may miss patients who die during admission, are transferred early, or are discharged before approach, the study team will maintain a screening log of all potentially eligible patients, including, where available, reasons for non-recruitment, to assess potential selection bias.

Endpoints (primary and secondary)

The primary endpoint is total acute-care bed-days within 6 months of index admission, including bed-days accrued during the index admission and any subsequent non-elective acute-care admissions within 6 months of the index admission date. Total acute-care bed-days within 6 months was selected as the primary endpoint because AMU care may affect both the duration of the index admission and subsequent acute hospital utilisation. Index length of stay alone would not capture possible downstream consequences such as early readmission, the transfer-of-care burden or recurrent non-elective admissions. Similarly, 30-day readmission captures only one component of postdischarge utilisation and does not account for the duration or intensity of subsequent acute-care episodes. A 6-month cumulative bed-day endpoint, therefore, provides a broader measure of acute-care resource use and aligns with the study’s clinical and economic evaluation objectives. Because death may reduce the opportunity to accrue subsequent bed-days, all-cause mortality will be reported separately, and sensitivity analyses will examine the impact of death as a competing event.

Sample size calculation

This is a pilot prospective cohort study designed primarily to estimate recruitment feasibility, follow-up completeness, data variability and key effect size parameters to inform a future multicentre study. The planned sample size is based on the feasibility of recruitment within the study period and the need to obtain sufficiently precise estimates of outcome distributions and between-group differences.

Our preliminary data suggest that the mean utilisation of acute bed-days in the AMU was approximately two fewer days than in the GW group. We are considering a conservative estimate of an absolute reduction of 1.5 acute bed-days as the minimum clinically meaningful difference for the novel model of care under the AMU. A sample size of 220 patients in a 3:1 allocation ratio (165 in the AMU and 55 in the GW group) will be required to detect a difference of 1.5 days in acute bed-days between the AMU and GW groups to achieve a power of 85% at a 5% two-sided type-I error rate, assuming a SD of 3 days and variance inflation factor of 10% among the exposure and confounder variables (estimated from the preliminary data).

Statistical analysis

General approach

Baseline characteristics will be summarised descriptively by study group. Continuous variables will be reported using means and SD or medians and IQRs, as appropriate, and categorical variables will be reported as counts and percentages.

Clinical effectiveness

Given the observational design, the primary comparative analyses will use inverse probability weighting (IPW) based on propensity scores to reduce measured confounding arising from non-random allocation to AMU or GW care. Propensity scores for AMU admission will be derived using a Generalised Linear Model (GLM) with a binomial distribution, including all matching variables (age, gender, Simple Segmentation Tool (SST), National Early Warning Score, primary diagnosis group, primary diagnosis severity score, Charlson Co-morbidity Score, Clinical Frailty Score and Barthel Index). IPWs will then be calculated and used to weight all effectiveness analysis models.

The SST14 is a prospective tool based on the Bridges to Health population segmentation model.15 It was developed in Singapore to segment the population into meaningful categories of health, social and environmental needs,14 enabling reproducible assessment of patients’ normative needs. The tool has been validated in the local ED setting16 and internationally.17

Covariate balance before and after weighting will be assessed using standardised mean differences, with values below 0.10 considered indicative of acceptable balance where appropriate. The distribution of estimated propensity scores will be examined by study group to assess overlap and the plausibility of the positivity assumption. The distribution of inverse probability weights will also be inspected to identify extreme weights. Stabilised weights will be used in the primary weighted analyses. If extreme weights are observed, sensitivity analyses using truncated weights will be considered to assess the robustness of findings. Sensitivity analyses will explore the robustness of the primary findings to alternative model specifications and, where feasible, to confounding adjustment approaches, including covariate-adjusted weighted models and propensity score matching. These analyses will be interpreted as supportive because this pilot study is not designed to provide definitive causal estimates.

The primary endpoint will first be summarised descriptively by study group. Weighted regression models will then be used to estimate the association between AMU care and total acute-care bed-days. Because bed-day data may be skewed or overdispersed, the final model family will be selected after examining the outcome distribution, dispersion and model assumptions. Candidate models include weighted linear regression for estimating mean differences and count models, such as Poisson or negative binomial regression, where appropriate. Results will be reported as adjusted between-group differences or ratios with 95% CIs, depending on model specification.

Binary secondary endpoints, such as any ED reattendance, will be analysed using weighted generalised linear models with an appropriate link function (identity, log or logistic), with weights applied via IPW. Count outcomes, such as the number of readmissions, will be analysed using weighted Poisson or negative binomial regression, depending on dispersion. Mortality will be analysed descriptively and, where event counts permit, using weighted regression models. Because death may preclude subsequent hospital utilisation, sensitivity analyses will examine the influence of death on the primary bed-day endpoint.

Analyses of patient-reported outcomes will be exploratory and will focus on estimated between-group differences over time, uncertainty intervals, response rates and the completeness of repeated measurements. A random participant-level effect will be included to account for within-person correlation across repeated assessments.

Cost-effectiveness analyses

The economic evaluation will estimate incremental costs and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) associated with AMU care compared with usual GW care over 6 months, from a healthcare system perspective. Cost data will be derived from hospital billing and administrative sources and will include inpatient, outpatient, ED and medication-related costs. Participant-reported non-medical costs, including transport and caregiving time, will be collected separately and, where feasible, analysed as indirect or out-of-pocket costs.

Health outcomes for the cost-utility analysis will be derived from EQ-5D-5L responses collected at discharge, 3 months and 6 months. Utility values will be estimated using an appropriate value set and used to derive QALYs over the follow-up period. Incremental cost-effectiveness ratios will be estimated where appropriate, with uncertainty intervals, and probabilistic sensitivity analysis will characterise decision uncertainty.

Where appropriate, mixed-effects multivariable models will be used for repeated continuous and categorical outcomes, incorporating baseline and time-varying covariates and allowing for missing data at random.18 If this assumption appears violated, alternative approaches, including multiple imputation, will be explored. A decision tree or state-based model may be used to summarise the adjusted intervention effects on costs and QALYs. Modelling will follow the relevant ISPOR19 and CHEERS20 guidance. As this is a pilot study, the economic evaluation will primarily assess feasibility, estimate parameters and inform a future definitive economic evaluation.

Missing data

The extent, timing and pattern of missing data will be described separately for routinely collected administrative outcomes and questionnaire-based patient-reported outcomes. Where appropriate, analyses will assume data are missing at random and use multiple imputation for missing covariates and outcomes. The imputation model will include the study group, baseline covariates, observed outcome data and variables associated with missingness, where available. Sensitivity analyses will assess the robustness of the findings to alternative assumptions about missing data, particularly for patient-reported outcomes at 3 and 6 months. Complete-case analyses will also be reported where appropriate.

Subgroup analyses

Prespecified exploratory subgroup analyses will be undertaken by diagnostic group and by selected baseline complexity measures, including the SST category, where sample size permits. These analyses will be interpreted with caution because the study is not powered to detect definitive subgroup effects.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design of this protocol. The study includes patient-reported outcome measures to capture health-related quality of life, well-being, depressive symptoms, patient activation, functional status, health literacy and care experience where applicable. Findings on questionnaire completion, participant burden, follow-up retention and missingness will be used to refine patient-facing materials and outcome selection for a future multicentre study. Patient-facing summaries of results will be developed for dissemination where feasible.

Ethical approval

The study received ethical approval from the National Healthcare Group Domain-Specific Review Board (Reference Number: 2023/00590), which serves as the ethics board for the participating institution. The clinical research coordinator (CRC) will approach eligible participants and provide them with detailed information sheets outlining the study’s aims and roles. The CRC will obtain written informed consent from patients. Caregivers or legally appointed representatives will be approached if the patient is unable to provide informed consent, in accordance with ethics-approved procedures. To ensure confidentiality and anonymity, participants’ data will be coded using unique serial numbers. Participants will be reimbursed SGD 25 as a token of appreciation for sharing their valuable insights.

Discussion

Demand for acute care is rising faster than population growth, increasing pressure on health systems. Acute medicine has emerged as a service model to improve the quality, efficiency and value of acute care through early assessment, multidisciplinary management and timely transition to the most appropriate care setting.

AMUs were developed to provide coordinated assessment, investigation and treatment for unscheduled medical admissions at the interface of emergency, primary and inpatient care. Although AMUs are now established in the UK and have been adopted internationally, the evidence base remains limited by retrospective designs, confounding, variation in AMU design and implementation and limited cost-effectiveness data.

This pilot study is the first prospective evaluation of AMU care in Singapore and is intended to inform a future multicentre study. It will assess clinical, operational, patient-reported and economic outcomes, including ED reattendance, acute-care bed-day use and patient costs, while generating feasibility data on recruitment, follow-up, outcome completeness, baseline patient characteristics, normative needs, fidelity to core care processes and service utilisation. Although conducted in a Singapore tertiary hospital, the evaluative framework and contextual insights may inform AMU evaluation in other health systems.

The core AMU components evaluated are early senior generalist review, expedited diagnostics, structured multidisciplinary input, close interface with the ED and early discharge or transfer planning. These are broadly consistent with AMU models internationally. Singapore-specific features include local bed management processes, relationships between AMU and specialist/general ward teams, 7-day allied health input, follow-up through polyclinics and specialist outpatient clinics and local operational pressures. Describing both core components and contextual features will support comparison with AMU and related acute-care models elsewhere.

A major strength of the study is its pragmatic design. In this setting, an observational evaluation is more appropriate than a single-centre randomised trial because acute medicine models are complex service interventions shaped by local operational pressures, staffing arrangements and patterns of patient flow. A trial conducted within a single institution would have limited ability to capture variation in these contextual determinants or to assess how the model’s core components function in routine care.21 The study therefore focuses on real-world clinical and cost-effectiveness. Comparability between groups will be improved using propensity score inverse probability weighting based on prespecified baseline covariates, including age, sex, comorbidity, frailty, diagnosis group, physiological acuity and normative needs.

Another strength of the study is the inclusion of patient-reported and economic outcomes alongside service utilisation measures. The broad outcome set reflects the exploratory purpose of a pilot study and permits assessment across multiple domains, including quality of life, well-being, mood, patient activation, functional status and health literacy. This approach should help identify which measures are feasible, responsive and most informative for a future multicentre study. However, it also introduces the possibility of conceptual overlap between measures, increased participant burden, missing data and multiple comparisons. For this reason, response rates, patterns of missingness and correlations between measures will be examined to guide refinement and prioritisation of endpoints in the definitive study.

Several limitations should be recognised. First, residual confounding may persist because AMU versus GW allocation is not randomised and may be influenced by unmeasured factors such as bed availability, time of admission, staffing, clinician judgement, social circumstances and operational pressures. IPW can reduce measured confounding but cannot address unmeasured determinants of allocation or outcome. Second, recruitment before discharge may introduce survivor or selection bias because patients who die early, are rapidly transferred, are discharged before approach or are unable to provide consent may be under-represented; screening logs will assess representativeness where data are available. Third, as a single-centre pilot study in a Singapore tertiary hospital, findings may not generalise to hospitals with different AMU structures, staffing, bed management or postdischarge pathways. Finally, the broad patient-reported outcome set may increase respondent burden and missing data; feasibility outcomes will guide refinement for a future multicentre study.

Overall, this pilot study will provide prospective evidence on the clinical, patient-reported, operational and economic outcomes of AMU care in Singapore, while establishing the methodological foundation for a larger multicentre evaluation.
