Executive Summary

The mid-Atlantic offshore region of the United States shows great promise for large-scale,
geologic carbon sequestration of carbon dioxide (CO2) emitted from the industry and population-
dense northeastern portion of the country. Previous research estimated CO2 storage resources
of 150 to 1150 gigatons in broad geologic structures off the Delaware, Maryland, New Jersey,
New York, Virginia coastal outer continental shelf. The EXPLORE ACS (Exploring Potential
Long-term Offshore Regional Atlantic Carbon Storage) project progressed on those prior
learnings by characterizing multiple real-world carbon storage sites selected within the region
and defining the infrastructure necessary for construction of CO2 pipelines and injection fields in
the offshore mid-Atlantic outer continental shelf. The two-year Department of Energy (DOE)
project was a collaborative effort led by Battelle along with Rutgers, TRC, Aker/Entr, Lamont
Doherty Earth Observatory, and TGS, with technical advice from Carbonvert, Enbridge, and
Amrize. This project has defined geological factors for carbon storage, infrastructure design
options for carbon storage, and a stakeholder assessment for offshore mid-Atlantic carbon
storage, and development options for carbon storage.
Geologic Factors for Carbon Storage
Geologic factors were analyzed for the study area, showing important trends to consider for
carbon sequestration. Thick (>1000 feet) sandy rock layers are present at depths of 3,000 to
10,000 feet below sea level on the mid-Atlantic outer continental shelf. However, these
reservoirs vary according to depositional environments. Key findings on geological factors for
carbon storage along the mid-Atlantic outer continental shelf area are summarized as follows:
• Common risk segment maps were made based on geological parameters (depth, thickness,
 caprock), operational restrictions (seabed features, environmentally sensitive areas), and
 cost factors (distance to shore, water depth). The maps indicate large areas favorable for
 carbon sequestration (Figure ES-1).

• Detailed geological models were developed for three storage scenarios: 1.) Great Stone
 Dome, 2.) Indian River Shallow, and 3.) Indian River Deep. These three scenarios reflect the
 geological setting along the mid-Atlantic outer continental shelf where carbon storage
 projects may be sited.
• The models illustrated that geological trends are important considerations for carbon storage
 project development in the mid-Atlantic offshore. The Great Stone Dome and Indian River
 Deep models include the Mississauga Sandstone at the base of the model, Logan Canyon
 Formation as the carbon storage reservoir, and the overlying Dawson Canyon Shale (Figure
 ES-2).

 Figure ES-2. Stratigraphic Column of the Baltimore Canyon Trough (MRCI).
• The main targets of interest at the Great Stone Dome site are the deltaic sands, which have
 good lateral continuity and reservoir properties. The overall porosity and permeability in the
 Logan Canyon 2 ranges from 1% to 42%, with maximum permeability of 5.7 Darcies and a
 mean of 51 millidarcies (mD). In the Logan Canyon 3, porosity ranges from 1% to 36%,
 while permeability reaches a maximum of 1.5 Darcies and has a mean of 37 mD.
• The Indian River Shallow model is located along the Delaware-Maryland shoreline and
 represents a nearshore structural model with less well data available as only three wells are
 present in the model domain. The main reservoir target is the fluvial channels of the
 Wastegate Formation (equivalent to the Mississauga Formation which underlies the Logan
 Canyon Formation). The fluvial channels of the Wastegate Formation were modeled
 perpendicular to the paleoshoreline. The porosity and permeability in the Wastegate
 Formation range from 5% to 35% and 0.1 mD to 1,000 mD with a mean of 37 mD. The
 Potomac I (Logan Canyon 3 equivalent) has porosity and permeability averages and ranges
 of 7% to 37% and 0.1 mD to 1,757 mD, respectively, with a mean of 45 mD.

• The Indian River Deep geological model located 50 miles off the Delaware/Maryland border
 was based on a seismic and stratigraphic model created by Rutgers University to predict the
 environments of deposition. The mean permeability for potential CO2 storage intervals was
 75 to 183 mD. The high permeability sections in these zones are largely restricted to the
 deltaic facies in the half of the model closer to the current day shoreline. The site represents
 an area with no well control, which is a challenge in many areas of the offshore mid-Atlantic.
• Preliminary safety and monitoring options were outlined for carbon storage operations in the
 mid-Atlantic offshore scenarios based on site-specific geological, seabed, ocean, and
 logistical features. Options like low-impact seismic surveys, baseline environmental
 monitoring, and three-dimensional (3D) seismic monitoring would facilitate safe project
 development.
Infrastructure Design Options for Carbon Storage
A key part of the project was infrastructure design for CO2 transport and injection along the mid-
Atlantic offshore. Battelle completed reservoir simulations to determine injection system
configurations, sustainable CO2 injection rates, injection pressures, and well locations. TRC
completed source and pipeline transport analysis for the region to connect industrial sources
and pipeline transport both onshore and offshore. Aker/Entr completed the preliminary design of
the offshore CO2 injection system, wellheads, manifolds, and metering/safety systems.
• Reservoir simulations were completed for two CO2 injection scenarios to illustrate
 deployment options for CO2 storage along the offshore mid-Atlantic. The numerical modeling
 concluded that an eight-well field in two clusters at the Great Stone Dome scenario could
 maintain a yearly rate of 17 MMT/year over 30 years with wellhead pressures of 3000 psi or
 less. Injection rates per well varied from 0.9 to 3.8 MMT/year, reflecting the geological
 variations in the marine deltaic sediments. Total injection volume was 500 MMT. The
 simulation CO2 saturation area covered an area of 14.1 by 8.8 miles or 80,000 acres.
• The Great Stone Dome injection system design site was defined by Aker based on the eight
 well injection configuration. A 125-mile pipeline was specified to transport the CO2 to the
 offshore field consisting of eight injection wells located in 200 feet of water (Figure ES-3).
 The wells were grouped into two clusters, referred to as the SW and SE pads, which were
 spaced approximately 6.7 miles apart.
• The Great Stone Dome injection field was specified as a subsea system with central
 manifolds and cluster wells. The CO2 pipeline is tied into the manifold which is connected to
 the subsea trees via rigid spools. An umbilical from shore provides power and
 communication to the field and ties into the manifold which distributes the services to the
 subsea trees by means of electrical and fiber optic flying leads. The wellhead provides the
 main pressure containing conduit from the subsurface to the seabed. A subsea manifold
 receives CO₂ from the pipeline and distributes it to the subsea trees. The manifold design is
 based on a 10-inch main header that runs through the structure and branches out to each of
 the four injection tree slots. To enable flow control, the manifold includes several valves,
 which can be manually operated by remote operated vehicle or remotely actuated via a
 subsea control module. The manifold also distributes umbilical services to the trees.
• Subsea injection trees were considered the best option to minimize visual disturbance and
 costs. Subsea injection trees are installed on the seafloor and are equipped with valves and
 sensors that enable the safe and controlled injection of CO₂. These systems require external
 power and communication, typically delivered via a dedicated umbilical, while CO₂ is
 supplied through a pipeline. The trees can be deployed as standalone units connected
 directly to the pipeline tied back to a subsea manifold. Current subsea tree configurations
 derive from oil and gas applications, with adaptations for CO₂ service.

• The Indian River Shallow scenario simulated a well drilled onshore, then laterally offshore
 18,000 ft. This well was able to sustain 2.5 MMT/year injection rate for 30 years at wellhead
 pressures less than 3000 psi. Total injection mass was 75 MMT, and the simulation showed
 that the CO2 would remain in state waters 3 nautical miles offshore.
• The Indian River Shallow CO2 storage system design was specified based on a single
 injection well drilled on shore and completed offshore laterally into state waters. The well
 was designed to accommodate 2.5 MMT/year CO2 injection. The well would require larger
 diameter casing to accommodate larger 5-inch diameter injection tubing necessary to inject
 2.5 MMT/year CO2. The well includes a lateral section 18,000 ft long to extend offshore into
 state waters. The injection section would be vertical from 5,700 to 7,000 ft deep. A
 preliminary cost estimate of $13 million was based on recent Battelle drilling work and front-
 end engineering design (FEED) studies for CO2 storage projects.
• Offshore CO₂ transport routes were completed by TRC based on preliminary technical and
 economic evaluations of pipeline and compression systems, including requirements for
 delivering CO₂ at appropriate pressures for offshore injection. Regulatory and permitting
 considerations were integrated throughout to ensure all proposed scenarios were consistent
 with state and federal requirements and addressed stakeholder interests.
• Two routes for a pipeline and compression system were specified to gather CO2 from the
 large CO2 point sources in the region for delivery to potential shoreline/coastal locations in
 Delaware, Maryland, and New Jersey. This study also accounted for the necessary
 compression to deliver the CO2 at 1,500 psi to the locations. TRC’s proprietary software,
 Integra Link Route (IL Route)™, was used to determine the most feasible routes.

Stakeholder Assessment for Offshore Mid-Atlantic Carbon Storage
Stakeholder engagement for the EXPLORE-ACS project consisted of regional outreach,
education, and knowledge sharing on key aspects of carbon storage development for the mid-
Atlantic offshore areas. Outreach materials developed for the EXPLORE-ACS project included a
project website, fact sheets, and informational presentations.
A technical advisory committee was established for the EXPLORE-ACS to provide feedback on
carbon storage development options project including Enbridge (pipeline/midstream),
Holicm/Amrize (cement industry), Carbonvert (CCS developer), DOE-NETL, and EQT
(energy/natural gas producer). Meetings were held with the technical advisory committee in
February 2025 to discuss the CO2 storage scenarios and December 2025 to discuss
infrastructure designs. The committee had concerns with offshore construction costs, uncertain
energy policy, managing injection well pressures, pipeline construction costs, public opposition
to development, schedule for construction, pipeline crossing points along shorelines, and the
overall timeline of developing carbon storage for the mid-Atlantic.
Development Options for Carbon Storage
The EXPLORE-ACS project was able to identify options for developing carbon capture and
storage (CCS) along the mid-north Atlantic offshore that would be technically feasible,
economic, and minimize impacts on the environment. These options would help to facilitate
public acceptance, but this remains a concern for development of new infrastructure projects
along the east coast. However, many stakeholders suggested that large infrastructure projects
like bridges, tunnels, and ports are not unusual along the Atlantic Coast and existing industry
could accommodate CCS construction activities. In addition, CCS infrastructure would have
minimal visibility after initial construction, which is a major impediment to many offshore projects
like wind energy.
The project benefited from several Technical Advisory Committee and project team meetings to
review options for carbon storage in the region. Key items identified in these meetings for
successful development of carbon sequestration projects along the offshore Atlantic are listed
below.
• Stratigraphic Test Wells- The members of the team meeting were unanimous in asserting
 how important stratigraphic test wells are to confirm geotechnical properties, caprock
 integrity, and geological trends. These wells are especially important in areas without well
 controls 15 to 30 miles offshore where carbon sequestration projects are most likely to be
 sited.
• Scalable Carbon Sequestration systems- Having options to develop scalable carbon
 sequestration systems ranging from 0.2 to 20 MMT CO2/year is important to providing
 flexibility in deployment, building confidence in offshore storage.
• Near-term and Long-term Carbon Sequestration Options- Realistically, smaller carbon
 sequestration systems are more likely to be successfully developed in the near term. The
 Indian River Shallow site provides options for near-term projects up to 2.5 MMT/year with a
 short pipeline, onshore drill site, and storage footprint in state waters. Larger, long-term
 carbon sequestration systems may piggy-back off these early projects.
• Outreach with Key Stakeholders- Addressing social concerns in early stages is vital to
 ensuring project success. Keys to project success include establishing a local presence,
 local business engagement, boots on the ground, and clear benefits for local communities.

• Regulation Clarity- Projects would benefit from federal and state clarity on regulations
 related to offshore carbon sequestration. An integrated approach to permitting would be
 beneficial to balancing timelines. Early contact with permitting agencies would be critical to
 ensuring projects meet state and federal requirements.
• Establishing Confidence in Carbon Sequestration Operations- Field projects need to
 validate geological models, validate reservoir simulations with monitoring data, demonstrate
 flow/pressure controls, establish release mitigation measures, and confirm safety practices.
• Economic Factors for Carbon Sequestration Project Development- Cost modeling for
 test wells, seismic acquisition, economic analysis for scenarios, and cost benefit analysis
 would aid in defining costs for developing CO2 storage along the mid-Atlantic offshore.

A rough timeline of 7 to 10 years was estimated for carbon sequestration development along the
Atlantic offshore (Figure ES-4). There is value in continued development of carbon
sequestration resources in the region to provide carbon management options for new industry
development, as there are no other carbon storage options on the order of hundreds of gigatons
in the northeast U.S. The mid-Atlantic offshore carbon storage systems outlined in this project
would provide certainty on carbon management options for large, long-term industrial
investments in the northeast U.S.

 Figure ES-4. Rough timeline for CCS deployment in the study area.
Early adopters may benefit from several aspects of offshore carbon sequestration along the
mid-Atlantic outer continental shelf like access to preferred lease block areas with high CO2
storage resources, pipeline right of ways, offtake locations, and the ability to leverage results of
the EXPLORE-ACS project. A “de-risking” strategy would aid in addressing project risks as
financial investments increase over time during carbon storage development. Scalable options
are also advantageous, where additional injection wells and pipelines could be added to the

CO2 storage system over time. Near-term options exist to drill wells at an onshore wellpad, then
laterally offshore into state waters. Offshore injection system construction in federal waters
would require more infrastructure and time, but these options would benefit from economies of
scale since large injection fields may be feasible in these areas.
Overall, the EXPLORE‑ACS project demonstrates that offshore carbon storage development
along the mid‑Atlantic is technically feasible and can be advanced in a staged, scalable manner
that aligns near‑term deployment with long‑term storage capacity. By identifying realistic
development pathways, defining site‑specific options that minimize environmental and social
impacts, and outlining regulatory, economic, and operational considerations, the project
provides a practical framework for transitioning from regional storage resource estimates to
implementable CCS projects. The analyses presented reduce uncertainty for early movers,
support proactive engagement with regulators and stakeholders, and establish a foundation for
de‑risked investment in offshore carbon sequestration. Collectively, the outcomes of this study
position the mid‑Atlantic offshore region as a critical, long‑term carbon management option for
sustaining industrial activity and decarbonization efforts in the northeastern United States.

1.0 Introduction

This final technical report presents a summary of work completed for the project Supporting
Communities and Industry for Mid-Atlantic Offshore Carbon Storage Hub (FE0032407), which
has been designated EXPLORE-ACS (Exploring Potential Long-Term Offshore Regional
Atlantic Carbon Storage). The project is part of the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) National
Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL) funding opportunity announcement 2799 for Regional
Initiatives to Accelerate Carbon Capture, Utilization, and Storage (CCUS) Deployment. The
objective of the DOE 2799 program is to identify and address the challenges facing commercial
deployment of carbon capture, transport, and storage in specific regions of the United States.
The overall objective of the EXPLORE-ACS project was to establish a foundation for carbon
capture and storage (CCS) in selected parts of the Atlantic outer continental shelf (OCS) from
Virginia to Massachusetts by addressing technical challenges, environmental factors, and
stakeholder concerns. There is a need to develop the Atlantic offshore storage hub to enable
mitigation of “stranded” carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions from sources along the eastern coast
and Appalachian Basin, and help achieve regional, national, and international climate goals.
Project task objectives are listed in Table 1-1. The research project included seven tasks to
address technical and non-technical aspects of carbon storage hub development, data sharing,
infrastructure analysis for linking sources to offshore storage, technology transfer, and
stakeholder outreach. The project team included Battelle (prime), Aker, Lamont Dougherty Earth
Observatory, Maryland Geological Survey, TRC Companies, TGS, Holcim, and Carbonvert. In
addition, the project benefited significantly from technical advisors from BOEM, Enbridge, and
EQT. The two-year project started in April 2024 and concluded in March 2026. This summary
report describes results for technical tasks completed under the project.

1.1 Background
The mid-Atlantic offshore study area in this project encompasses nearly 171,000 square
kilometers (km²) along the mid-Atlantic states of Virginia, Maryland, Delaware, New York, New
Jersey, and Pennsylvania (Figure 1-1). The study area comprises three major subregions:
Georges Bank Basin (GBB), Long Island Platform (LIP), and Baltimore Canyon Trough (BCT).
The project study area extends from within 10 kilometers (km) to 300 km offshore,
encompassing the inner continental shelf to portions of the continental slope. Water depths in
the mid-Atlantic continental shelf grade gently from zero depth along the shoreline to depths of
100 to 200 meters (m) near the transition to the continental slope. Along the continental slope,
water depths plummet more than 2,000 m into the North Atlantic Basin.

 Figure 1-1. Assessed Mid-Atlantic storage resources from the Mid-Atlantic Carbon Sequestration
 Research Project (2019).

The mid-Atlantic offshore region of the United States shows great promise for the large-scale
carbon sequestration of CO₂ emitted from the industry and population-dense northeastern
portion of the country. This project seeks to advance a prior U.S. Department of Energy–
supported study also led by Battelle, known as the Mid-Atlantic U.S. Offshore Carbon Storage
Resource Assessment Project (MAOCSRAP), which broadly determined the nature and size of
the region’s CO₂ storage resource potential (Cumming et al., 2017; Fukai et al., 2020; Gupta et
al., 2021). Under this previous research, geological carbon storage resources on the order of
150 to 1,136 gigatons were calculated within broad geologic structures off the coasts of
Delaware, Maryland, New Jersey, and New York, with the highest concentration of storage
volume assessed to be within the Baltimore Canyon Trough area (Figure 1-1).

EXPLORE-ACS Final Technical Report 2 March 2026

Led by Battelle, the prior project was conducted by a team of public and private entities with
expertise in offshore geology and resources for the study region, including the state geological
surveys of Delaware, Maryland, and Pennsylvania; the U.S. Geological Survey–Woods Hole
Coastal and Marine Science Center; Rutgers University; Harvard University; Lamont-Doherty
Earth Observatory at Columbia University; and international partners. The storage resource
assessment was completed for a broad region offshore of the U.S. East Coast, from
Massachusetts to Virginia.
This prior project included extensive analysis of legacy seismic, well-log, core, and
biostratigraphic data in the mid-Atlantic study area. Along the Atlantic Outer Continental Shelf
(OCS), 45 deep exploration wells were drilled between 1976 and 1984, and more than 45,000
linear kilometers of two-dimensional (2D) seismic data were acquired to evaluate the presence
of oil and gas resources in deep rock formations. Additional deep onshore wells along the
Atlantic Coast also provide datasets that help define the geologic setting of the region. Although
this exploration effort did not identify economic hydrocarbon resources, the resulting datasets
outlined deep rock layers with properties suitable for carbon sequestration.
The Mid-Atlantic Carbon Storage Resource Assessment Project digitized, reprocessed, and
analyzed these legacy datasets using modern techniques and completed laboratory testing on
rock cores previously collected in the region. Geotechnical testing, geophysical log analysis,
mud log interpretation, drilling reports, and well testing were also incorporated. Additional
geologic research related to deep rock stratigraphy, depositional environments, geochemistry,
and biostratigraphy further advanced understanding of offshore storage potential in the
mid-Atlantic OCS (Miller, 2017; Miller et al., 2018; Schmelz et al., 2020; Schmelz et al., 2024).
Together, these efforts provide a comprehensive dataset comprising thousands of data fields
that support assessment of CO₂ storage options in the region.
The project team from the prior assessment calculated prospective storage resources ranging
from approximately 150 to 1,136 megatons for the combined storage zones, indicating that
mid-Atlantic offshore formations could store decades of CO₂ from industrial sources in the
northeastern United States. Preliminary reservoir simulations completed under that effort
suggested that injection rates exceeding 1 million metric tons of CO₂ per year may be
achievable in select deep formations. However, the project did not evaluate the infrastructure
required to access these storage resources, including CO₂ transportation pipelines, offshore
injection field designs, site-specific monitoring approaches, or integration with existing offshore
activities.
Building directly on these prior findings, the EXPLORE-ACS project sought to characterize
multiple real-world carbon storage sites within the study area and to evaluate options for routing
CO₂ pipelines from inland emission sources to offshore injection wellfields. Many large industrial
facilities are located along the Delaware River, Hudson River, and Chesapeake Bay. While
there is no active oil and gas production along the mid-Atlantic OCS, the region contains
numerous existing uses, including shipping lanes, major ports, trans-Atlantic
telecommunications cables, military installations, and population centers. As a result, a key
challenge for offshore CO₂ transport and storage development in this region is integrating new
infrastructure with existing features while minimizing environmental impacts and effects on local
communities.

2.1 CO2 Storage Scenarios
Selecting an appropriate site is critical for maturing a storage project towards commercialization.
An appropriate offshore site must be located within an economically viable distance of onshore
CO2 take-off points, allow unobstructed site access, and be able to store the volumes required
over the project’s lifespan, at acceptable injection rates. All of this must be accomplished while
complying with regulatory requirements and minimizing the impact on the environment and
people. Finding specific sites entails studying and distilling numerous relevant datasets, at
regional to site-level scales, to find locations that meet the criteria mentioned above. The
National Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL) has summarized this process, as seen in
Figure 2-1 (NETL, 2017).

There are many similarities between the search for hydrocarbon resources and the search for
CO2 storage sites. It therefore makes sense to utilize best practices developed in the oil and gas
industry and apply those methodologies. Aside from the Storage Resources Management
System (SRMS) mentioned in Figure 2-1, other oil and gas techniques used by carbon storage
scientists include common risk segment mapping, a useful technique for site

exclusive domain of the petroleum industry, has been greatly advanced by the large volume of
projects and modeling techniques that have developed during the long, worldwide search for
hydrocarbon resources.

2.1.1 Site Screening/Selection
Common risk segment mapping is a method for optimal site selection adapted from
hydrocarbon exploration and applied to carbon storage. Key factors controlling the viability of a
project are defined and spatially mapped into regions of varying risk. Risk maps (segments) are
then mathematically “stacked”, resulting in a holistic, map-view representation of overall relative
project risk (Bump et al., 2021) (Figure 2-2). Advantages of the common risk segment method
are that it provides an intuitive, objective, and shared visual representation of risk which a
variety of stakeholders can quickly understand, likely explaining why this method has become a
best practice for carbon storage exploratory efforts.

For the EXPLORE-ACS project, three critical risk segment maps were defined: subsurface,
cost, and operational restrictions. Individual risk segments can incorporate more than one
variable. Subsurface common risk segment map inputs consisted of regional grids of storage
volume capacity, overburden depth to the Logan Canyon Formation reservoir targets, and
thickness grids for the regional caprock (Figure 2-3). Information comprising the subsurface
common risk segment map was predominantly derived from products produced by the prior Mid-
Atlantic offshore carbon storage assessment (Cumming et al., 2017; Fukai et al., 2020), with
more recent key contributions by researchers at project partner Rutgers (Miller et al., 2017;
Miller et al., 2018; Schmelz et al., 2020; Schmelz et al., 2024).

The cost common risk segment map (Figure 2-4) uses inputs of water depth and distance from

2.2 Refining/Maturing Storage Scenarios
The Atlantic OCS carbon storage systems are Cretaceous age mixed siliciclastic rocks of the
Logan Canyon Formation and Wastegate Formation capped by the overlying Dawson Canyon
Shale (and the equivalent Navesink Formation). The common risk segment mapping has been
utilized to select three candidate sites (Figure 2-9). The Great Stone Dome (GSD) and Indian
River Deep (IRD) models are representative of offshore carbon storage systems of the Atlantic
with high storage potential according to the common risk segment (CRS) maps, and the Indian
River Shallow (IRS) site which has moderate storage potential and is a nearshore scenario to
assess a lateral onshore-offshore drilling scenario. To understand the reservoir performance,
static earth models (SEMs) were created for these sites so that injection simulations could be
performed.
The Atlantic SEMs are guided by a conceptual understanding of the carbon storage systems
combined with structural constraints from well formation tops and reservoir characteristics from
well log analysis. The conceptual models are based on previous sequence stratigraphic studies,
2D seismic interpretations, thin-section analysis, core analysis, well-log analysis, and analog
formations which supplement characteristics of the fluvial channels and the connectivity of the
delta. Additional data such as stratigraphic test well and three-dimensional (3D) seismic
acquisition at the model sites would be necessary to validate the reservoir properties and
connectivity of the SEMs. An overview of the SEM workflows and details of each SEM is
provided in the following sections.

2.2.1 Overview of SEM Workflows, Data Availability, and Key Assumptions
The three SEMs have similar conceptual models and follow similar workflows depending on the
data available at each site. The GSD and IRD models developed include the Mississauga
Sandstone at the base of the model, Logan Canyon Formation (specifically sequences 2 and 3)
as the carbon storage reservoir, and the overlying Dawson Canyon Shale. Similarly, the
Wastegate Formation (which is equivalent to the Mississauga) is the reservoir at IRS, as it is the
appropriate depth for supercritical injection of CO2 and has promising reservoir parameters.
The structural frameworks of the sites are based on sequence stratigraphic studies (Miller et al.,
2017; Miller et al., 2018; Baldwin et al., 2022) and current stratigraphic models (this study), 2D
seismic horizons, and formation tops of the major sequence boundaries.
While data at GSD feature an abundance of 2D seismic lines, horizon picks, and eight wells
(seven of them with core data), more assumptions were required for the data-sparse IRD and
IRS sites. Structural surfaces for IRD and IRS relied on the seismic horizons since the IRD
model has no legacy wells in the model domain and IRS has four wells with spontaneous
potential (SP) logs in the model domain. The data utilized were originally gathered from a prior
resource estimate project (Battelle et al., 2019; Cumming et al., 2017; Fukai et al., 2020).
After the structural framework was created, environment of deposition (EOD) logs, lithologic
facies logs, and porosity log workflows were applied to the models. These workflows varied
depending on the data availability of each site. In general, the EOD logs were used to inform
the population of the lithologic facies within each EOD, and the lithofacies logs were created
based on a density-neutron plot analysis with a lithologic overlay. Then, porosity-permeability
regressions were created for each lithology from core data. Porosity values of the core data
range from approximately 1% to 33%, while permeability values range from <0.01 mD to 3,500
mD. Effective porosity logs were calculated by the density porosity log or a compaction-porosity
curve when a porosity log was not available (Van Sickel et al., 2004). The permeability of each
lithology was calculated by the porosity-permeability equation.
Key geologic assumptions were made in order to create representative SEMs to investigate
whether the east coast can be a successful carbon storage hub. Some of the key assumptions
are noted below:
Structural Framework – Although there is a relatively high data coverage of 2D seismic lines,
these lines haven’t been depth converted which creates uncertainty in the accuracy of the tie
between the well log data with the seismic horizon picks. Additionally, high resolution 3D
seismic data have not been collected at the Atlantic, which further restricts our understanding of
the spatial and temporal trends and reservoir properties of the Cretaceous rocks.
Environments of Deposition – Many studies have analyzed the stratigraphy (including the
biostratigraphy) of the well data and detailed stratigraphic models have been created to guide
the trends of the SEM, but assumptions on the stratigraphic history have been made.
Additionally, the valley fills have not yet been mapped in the 2D seismic and have not been
directly tied to the fluvial channels modeled. As a result, the fluvial channels that have been
mapped are representative and require validation in future work. Additionally, deltas have quite
a range of bioturbation and wave-reworking that can occur which can increase or decrease
reservoir compartmentalization. These spatial trends can’t be accurately made until additional
seismic acquisition and interpretations are made.
Lithologic and Petrophysical Property Distributions – Although analog data are supplemented to
understand the spatial distributions of the lithologic facies, additional sedimentological and

diagenetic studies of existing core and acquisition of 3D seismic could help to validate model

2.2.2 Conceptual Model of the EXPLORE-ACS Sites
The reservoir quality rock of these Atlantic EXPLORE-ACS scenarios includes the Early
Cretaceous shallow marine deltaic sandstones and their nearshore fluvial equivalents. These
deposits generally thicken seaward and were deposited during a time of increased
accommodation space from sediment loading, compaction, and a rise in sea level (Kominz et
al., 1998; Kominz et al., 2008). The region is located on a passive margin and as a result is
structurally stable. The structure is also gently dipping at all three SEMs, with a maximum dip of
2.71° at the GSD. Growth and conjugate faulting occurred throughout and is typically in
proximity to the present-day slope (Grow and Sheridan, 1988; Baldwin et al., 2022). In addition,
one salt diapir has been identified on seismic and is marked as a no-flow boundary (Baldwin et
al., 2022).
EOD logs were developed from Rutgers University from past sedimentological and stratigraphic
studies (Poag, 1985; Steinkraus, 1981; Steinkraus et al., 1985a; Steinkraus et al., 1985b;
Steinkraus and Bebout, 1985) and the current study. Five primary depositional environments
were assigned in each of the three models: fluvial, estuarine/coastal plain, inner delta shelf, mid-
to outer-delta shelf, and a bathyal zone. Some of these logs are shown in Figure 2-10. Typically,
the highest quality reservoir zones (clean sand and calcareous sand lithofacies) correspond to
the fluvial, estuarine, and inner-shelf depositional environments. Figure 2-11 shows a 3D model
of some of the various depositional environments and their proximity to each other. The
paleoshoreline is northeast-southwest (Baldwin et al., 2022), so the deltaic lithofacies are
modeled parallel to the paleoshorelines, and the fluvial channels are modeled perpendicular to
the paleoshoreline.
The fluvial and shallow marine sandstones are typically medium- to coarse-grained poorly
sorted with quartz as the major grain type with minor amounts of calcite grains and feldspar
grains that are subangular to subrounded. The cements can be variable, with low to high
amounts of calcitic and dolomitic cement, iron oxide rich cement, and minor amounts of clays.
Thin-section observations show that high amounts of calcitic and dolomitic cements cause low
porosity. The porosity is mostly from intergranular and grain/cement dissolution with minor
porosity from molds, intragranular pores, and fractures (Battelle et al., 2019). Some sedimentary
structures such as cross laminations, tidal bundles, and mudcracks have been identified, but
EOD logs and lithologic facies interpretations relied mostly on well log data, seismic data, and
stratigraphy.
The lithologic facies which populated the various EOD logs were based on a cross-plot analysis
of well logs and a review of the existing thin-section and petrophysical core data from the prior
Mid-Atlantic U.S. Offshore Carbon Storage Resource Assessment project (Battelle, et al., 2019;
Alberty, 1994). A lithologic facies log was generated by grouping the density and neutron logs
into standard-defined bins for clean sand, calcitic sand, dolomitic sand, and dolomite (Figure 2-
12). In addition, mudstones were identified from well logs based on the volume of shale (Vsh)
cutoff of 80% and density porosity (DPHI) <14% since they aren’t easily identifiable from
density-neutron cross plot templates. After the lithologic facies were assigned, the binned core
porosity and permeability data points of each of the facies were used to create facies-specific
porosity-permeability transforms. This resulted in reasonable permeability logs tied to the core
data. Grouping these core measurements into defined bins provided a higher definition set of
lithologically defined porosity-permeability transforms that were used to populate the 3D model
(Figure 2-13).

2.3 Risking and Monitoring, Verification, and Accounting Plans per Site
This section presents a preliminary assessment of risks and monitoring options associated with
development of CO2 storage for the three EXPLORE-ACS scenarios identified by the project
team. The risk assessment was based on approaches utilized by other offshore carbon storage
projects. Similarly, the monitoring plan was based on experience documented in other offshore
carbon storage projects along with the geological and environmental setting at the three
scenario locations identified along the mid-Atlantic OCS.

2.3.1 Risks and Risk Mitigation Plans for EXPLORE-ACS Sites
Data availability plays a strong role in the ability to correctly perceive risks, while the physical
site location greatly influences the likely risk profile. The candidate sites comprise a spectrum of
scenarios in this regard. The Great Stone Dome (GSD) site has relatively good legacy data
availability for risk analysis, is located in medium water depths (~64 m) above a shallow marine
(deltaic) set of reservoirs, but is a significant distance (187 km) from the nearest likely onshore
CO2 takeoff point. The Indian River Deep (IRD) site has a much sparser available dataset, is in

EXPLORE-ACS Final Technical Report 31 March 2026

similar water depths (45 m) also over dominantly shallow marine reservoirs, but is about half the
distance to projected CO2 takeoff at 87 km. Indian River Shallow (IRS) is located very close to
shore, within Delaware state waters. IRS has a few relatively close legacy onshore wells to
guide risking, and is expected to be dominated by reservoirs deposited in an onshore (fluvial)
environment.
Battelle utilized a few different approaches to evaluate site risk. The first was the “Features,
Events, & Processes” (FEP) method (Savage et al., 2004; Maul et al., 2005), which is a
structured methodology that systematically categorizes and evaluates factors that can influence
CO₂ storage performance, integrity, and safety. Also utilized was the Failure Modes and Effects
Analysis (FMEA) process (Ben-Daya, 2009), which is a proactive framework to identify, analyze,
and mitigate potential failures or risks. FMEA is often used in industries where safety/reliability
are critical, one of those being offshore oil and gas development, a close relative of offshore
carbon storage. FMEA ranks identified risks for severity by estimating a “risk priority number”
(RPN), which is the product of the severity (Sv), frequency of occurrence (Oc), and chance of
detection (Dt).
The risks affecting offshore carbon storage projects can be broadly grouped as geological,
operational, regulatory/legal, or socioeconomic in nature. Within each category, specific risks
can be further defined. Key geological risks include the potential for CO₂ leakage, lack of
sufficient storage volume and /or injectivity to meet project goals, and induced seismic events.
Leakage is the most critical geologic risk, since it potentially has environmental and legal
ramifications. Leakage can occur through the caprock, via fault activation, or through
faulty/degraded wellbores, allowing CO₂ to migrate out of the intended storage reservoir, and
possibly even to the sea bottom, causing environmental concerns. The risk of inaccurate
assessment of storage volumes and injectivity can have a large impact on project economics,
and may be ascertained from limited hard data. Induced seismic events can be triggered by
fault activation, as injection and reservoir pressures increase. The vast majority of such events
are imperceptible by humans even in onshore situations, much less many kilometers offshore,
hence induced seismicity is not seen as a major risk for offshore storage.
Operational risks consist mainly of concerns over infrastructure integrity (pipelines,
compressors, injection wells, etc.) and flow assurance issues. Corrosion in pipelines due to
carbonic acid formation from the interaction of CO2/H2O can occur, potentially creating leak
points. Pipeline leakage of CO2 between the capture facility and the onshore takeoff point could
be life-threatening in populated areas. Pipeline corrosion offshore can make repairs difficult and
very expensive; a shut-in pipeline halts project revenue completely. Additionally, offshore
pipelines can have problems with flow assurance. Flow assurance refers to the reliable and
continuous transport of CO₂ from capture facilities to offshore storage sites. Temperature drops
along the pipeline route can cause solid CO₂ hydrates (similar to ice) to form, leading to
blockages. In summary, offshore CCS pipelines face unique challenges due to the physical
properties of CO₂, extreme environmental conditions, and long transport distances.
Regulatory/legal risk refers to delays due to lengthy and complicated approval processes, or
potential legal liability during or after the project life. BOEM is currently formulating regulations
to manage carbon storage in the OCS. With the rollout of new regulations, expected in late
2025, it is reasonable to assume that initial permitting will be slow, as BOEM scales the learning
curve of a new process. Long-term legal liability should be less of an issue with offshore carbon
storage, since the area is unpopulated and has no private landowners. However, it would be
unwise to think that a significant offshore CO2 leak would have no possible legal consequence.

EXPLORE-ACS Final Technical Report 32 March 2026

Socioeconomic risks include factors impacting project return on investment or public support.
Higher than predicted costs or insufficient revenue could limit project investments. Public
opposition to the pipeline, particularly from the capture facility to the onshore takeoff point, could
greatly delay or kill a project. Market or policy changes could impact project revenue or viability
and threaten a project.

2.3.2 Operational Safety Plan for EXPLORE-ACS Sites in the OCS
The EXPLORE-ACS project is proposing three candidate offshore CO2 injection sites: Great
Stone Dome (GSD), Indian River Deep (IRD), and Indian River Shallow (IRS) (Figure 2-7). The
former two sites are located in waters of the OCS, i.e., waters greater than 3 nautical miles
(1.62 km) offshore. The latter site, IRS, is designed to drill and inject from an onshore location
into offshore reservoirs located under Delaware state waters (< 3 nautical miles or 1.62 km
offshore). The location of an offshore site determines the governing regulatory authorities. For
carbon storage projects operating in the OCS (i.e., GSD and IRD), a characterization and safety
plan is required by the federal regulatory bodies governing that area, which are the Bureau of
Ocean Energy Management (BOEM) and the Bureau of Safety and Environmental Enforcement
(BSEE). These agencies currently share responsibilities in regulating oil and gas activities for
the United States OCS and are also being given oversight in regulating and facilitating carbon
sequestration activities there. Projects located in state waters (i.e., the IRS site) are regulated
by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), unless the state in question has been approved
for primacy over its own carbon storage projects. Since the state of Delaware does not have
primacy at the time of this writing, the EPA is expected to regulate carbon storage at the IRS
site.
Since the IRS site proposes drilling/injection from onshore Delaware into offshore reservoirs
under Delaware state waters, that project will also require permits related to drilling/injection
activities onshore, both from the state of Delaware and the EPA.
BOEM/BSEE are currently developing regulations that will govern carbon storage in the OCS.
Those regulations are expected to be greatly influenced by existing ones for oil and gas
development, with specific modifications accounting for the unique aspects of carbon storage.

2.3.3 EXPLORE-ACS Site Risking and Mitigation
Work in Subtask 3.1 identified three primary carbon storage candidate sites: Indian River
Shallow (IRS), Indian River Deep (IRD), and Great Stone Dome (GSD). Offshore storage sites
generally have unique risk profiles due to the variety of variables involved, whether they relate
to geology, location, data density, water depth, or myriad other factors. This section covers
Battelle’s risk assessment of each individual candidate site, and steps that can be taken to
mitigate those risks.

2.3.3.1 Great Stone Dome Risk/Mitigation Summary
GSD is located in ~64 meters of water, 100 km offshore. This is the most data-rich site selected,
benefiting from dozens of legacy oil company exploratory wells that were drilled in the 1970s-
1980s. Exploratory drilling was unable to find economic volumes of hydrocarbons, and
subsequent federal policy prevented further exploration efforts. The legacy dataset is high-
quality, with cores, well logs and a substantial 2D seismic database. As the name suggests, the
site is a massive structural dome with an area of 500 km2. Target Cretaceous reservoir zones of
the Logan Canyon Formation drape over the top of the feature and are capped by the regional
Dawson Canyon Shale. Injected CO2 is expected to remain trapped within this giant closed
volume for millennia, just as hydrocarbons do in many areas of the world.
GSD risks were evaluated and are summarized in Table 2-13. The highest risk was judged to be
operational, specifically accidents and/or repairs. Unlike most existing or planned offshore
carbon storage projects, EXPLORE-ACS has no offshore service industry for drilling/injection
operations, because there is no existing petroleum industry there. Accidents or equipment
failures must not only contend with that issue, but also with the relatively remote offshore
location. Those two factors combined could cause extended and expensive operational pauses
to occur.
Most other noteworthy GSD risks appear to be related to pipelines. Analysis of potential
onshore takeoff points by partner TRC showed that locations at the mouths of either Delaware
or Chesapeake Bays were most feasible, while more northerly takeoffs closer to GSD (i.e., in
New York/New Jersey) were likely to face strong public opposition. Utilizing the optimal
southern takeoffs to supply CO2 to GSD would require the longest and most expensive pipeline
of any storage candidate, at 200 km. The long distance also increases chances for flow
assurance, corrosion problems, and leakage risk. Repairs are complicated by the remote
location, and lack of nearby offshore service industry. Mitigation of corrosion/failure risk has
been addressed by several measures in analog projects. For example:
 • CO2 dehydration prior to injection was utilized in Japan’s Tomakomai CCS project
 (Gaurina-Međimurec et al., 2018),
 • Corrosion inhibitors to extend CO2 pipeline life have been used in Texas enhanced oil
 recovery projects,

EXPLORE-ACS Final Technical Report 40 March 2026

 • Real-time pipeline integrity monitoring using fiber optics has been used for U.S. Midwest
 CCS projects (Rodosta et al., 2018),
 • Smart-pigging is used in the Alberta Carbon Trunk Line in Alberta, Canada, to monitor
 the condition of the CO2 pipeline there (Nuttall and MacGregor, 2024).

Pipeline-related costs are believed to be a moderate risk to project viability. Mitigation of the
cost risk could be accomplished by seeking federal subsidies for the pipeline, sharing financial
risk with partner(s), or both. The 45Q tax credits for carbon storage may be insufficient to
provide acceptable returns, due not only to the expensive offshore pipeline, but also a difficult
and costly onshore pipeline segment from the capture facility to the takeoff point, and relatively
expensive offshore drilling and facilities.

Another significant risk is the chance for “lawfare” against the onshore pipeline needed to take
captured CO2 to the takeoff point. Numerous onshore natural gas pipelines have been subject
to intense legal battles, typically sponsored by well-funded environmental non-governmental
organizations (NGOs). Lawfare risk is highest for the onshore portion of the pipeline and can be
minimized by using existing rights of way (ROWs). Exploring the use of alternate modes of CO2
transport (i.e., barge, etc.) is also recommended to determine if that would be a viable option in
a worst-case scenario in which an onshore pipeline isn’t possible.

2.3.3.2 Indian River Deep Risk/Mitigation Summary
The risk profile of IRD is very similar to GSD, differing mainly in those risk areas affected by the
relative lack of legacy well control (Table 2-14). Containment and volumes/injectivity are seen
as higher risk due to the lack of hard data confirming sand quantity/quality, caprock thickness
and distribution, and the presence of significant faults. Even though the major depositional
sequences seen at GSD do trend southward to IRD, local-scale changes in facies have been
seen which make this location higher risk until a stratigraphic test well is drilled. The well should
include a robust data acquisition plan, including a complete suite of well logs, core, and core
analysis. Dynamic simulation of an earth model constructed using the new stratigraphic well
information and 3D seismic analysis will help inform/mitigate the risk of potential leak-prone
faults or interruptions in seal presence, while also firming up estimates of the volumetric and
injective capacity of the reservoirs. Cost risk at IRD is viewed as moderate, but slightly lower
than at GSD due to the shorter pipeline distance to optimal takeoff points identified. Mitigations
recommended are identical to GSD, i.e., federal subsidies and/or risk-sharing partnerships.
The related risks of lawfare and public acceptance mentioned for GSD remain at IRD.
Repurposing existing pipelines or building in existing ROWs is the best way to avoid such
problems, and if that is impossible, evaluating the impact of alternate CO2 transport modes is
advisable.

2.3.3.3 Indian River Shallow Risk Summary
Due to its location, IRS has a unique risk profile among the candidate sites (Table 2-15). The
original motivation for selecting an onshore-to-offshore site was to save on drilling and
infrastructure costs, which it does by eliminating expensive offshore drilling and platforms,
subsea wellheads, long pipelines, etc. However, along with those cost savings come tradeoffs,
such as greater public awareness and concern due to a visible injection site onshore, and the
relatively close location of an injected CO2 plume (within state water, ~3.5 miles offshore) to
onshore population centers.
Liability/lawfare risk is estimated as marginal (the highest rating) due to the elevated chance of
long-term liability for a CO2 plume which will eventually move shoreward and be located under
highly-populated areas. Associated with that will be a long-term need for monitoring, as the
plume and AoR continually move onshore, which will increase monitoring costs. The public
acceptance risk is seen as moderate to nearly marginal, since the onshore drilling/injection site
will be high-profile compared to remote offshore drilling/injection operations. Volumes and
injectivity are the next highest rated (moderate) risk. Legacy well control is greater than at IRD,
but still sparse. The blanket-like deltaic sands relied upon as the main injection targets for
GSD/IRD are too shallow at IRS, where the target is the relatively untested Potomac Formation,
i.e., the onshore equivalent of the deepest offshore reservoir, the Logan Canyon 3. Potomac
sediments are derived from a fluvial environment of deposition, whose sands tend to be less
homogenous or continuous than the deltaic sands that are targets at GSD and IRD. Although
legacy well logs show potential for sands with properties favorable for injection, shale-filled
channels and fine-grained overbank deposits could form lateral barriers in fluvial rocks, which
would hinder permeability and injectivity.

2.4 Monitoring, Verification and Accounting Plans
Offshore geological storage of CO₂ requires continuous, site-specific monitoring to ensure safe
and permanent containment. Since the first large-scale injections at the Sleipner field in 1996
and Snøhvit in 2008, various technologies and pilot projects have been tested worldwide.
Projects such as Snøhvit, Northern Lights, Endurance, K12-B (a pilot-scale project), Rotterdam
Opslag en Afvang Demonstration (ROAD), and Goldeneye in the North Sea (STEMM-CCS
Project Consortium, 2020), along with Pre-Salt in Brazil and Tomakomai in Japan, have
contributed to the development of best practices and informed technology selection under

EXPLORE-ACS Final Technical Report 47 March 2026

diverse geological and operational conditions (Arts and Winthaegen, 2005; Bull et al., 2015;
IEAGHG, 2015; Dean and Tucker, 2017; Melo et al., 2018; Dean et al., 2020; Lichtschlag et
al., 2021; Ndlovu et al., 2024). Monitoring objectives are generally grouped into three
categories: containment assurance (ensuring CO₂ remains within the designated storage
complex), conformance assurance (verifying that the CO₂ behaves as predicted in reservoir
models), and contingency monitoring (addressing deviations from expected performance).
Monitoring strategies must be able to detect, attribute, and quantify any leakage and assess
its potential environmental impact (Wimmer et al., 2011; Vinnes et al., 2024; Kano et al.,
2024). Before injection begins, a baseline seabed survey must be conducted to characterize
natural environmental conditions and background CO₂ levels. Throughout injection and into
the post-injection phase, monitoring must be adjusted based on observed site behavior and
risk assessments. Monitoring frequency may decrease over time if conformance is
demonstrated, but additional surveillance may be required if anomalies are detected.
In terms of the types of monitoring tools used, it is sometimes convenient to classify them
into three groups. The first group comprises deep-focused methods, which are specifically
designed to monitor the reservoir and its overburden; these methods provide critical insights
into the movement of the CO₂ plume and capture pressure variations that could impact the
storage integrity. The second group encompasses shallow-focused methods, which
concentrate on surveying the seabed, sediments, and water column to detect any signs of
leakage, thereby playing a key role in the early identification of potential environmental risks.
The third group consists of well-based methods, involving tools installed in injection or
observation wells to continuously track downhole conditions and integrity of the wells.
Most of the monitoring methods commonly used in CCS projects were developed and
applied by the oil and gas industry for both onshore and offshore operations. While some of
these methods are versatile enough to be used in either environment, others are specifically
designed for one setting (Table 2-17).
From a regulatory perspective, the United States currently lacks finalized federal regulations
specifically addressing monitoring, verification and accounting practices for offshore sub-
seabed CO₂ storage. Unlike regulations in Europe, Japan, and Australia, U.S. regulatory
measures are not based on the London Protocol. However, legislative updates and agency
actions have begun to establish a foundational framework. In 2018, BOEM further supported
early offshore CO₂ transportation and storage efforts by issuing best management practices.
Under the 2021 amendment to the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA), the
Department of the Interior is authorized to issue leases for CO₂ sequestration on the OCS,
with BOEM managing leasing and site assessments, and BSEE slated to regulate
operational safety and monitoring, verification and accounting once the rules are finalized.
This section provides a technical overview of monitoring, verification and accounting
technologies applicable to the potential storage sites selected for the Mid-Atlantic CCS
project. It reviews offshore CCS monitoring practices from around the world, analyzes
lessons learned from major international projects, and evaluates a specific monitoring
scheme for the study areas chosen for this project. The objective is to identify the most
effective and widely used monitoring tools and outline a framework that spans the entire life
cycle of the storage project—from pre-injection baseline establishment, through injection, to
post-injection observation. The assessment goals included the following items:

 • Development of effective monitoring strategies for assessing CO₂ behavior

 2.4.1 Difference in Monitoring, Verification and Accounting Between
 Onshore/Offshore
 Monitoring technologies for CO₂ storage share a number of principles across both onshore
 and offshore settings, especially in deep-focused applications where the geological behavior
 of reservoirs and overburden is comparable. However, the operational context, monitoring
 priorities, and practical constraints differ significantly between the two environments.
 Onshore monitoring is often driven by regulatory and public concerns, especially the
 protection of USDWs and impacts on populated areas. Atmospheric and soil gas monitoring,
 land surface displacement tracking, and detailed aquifer assessments are essential in
 onshore projects. Shallow-focused monitoring systems on land are influenced by strong
 seasonal and diurnal changes, complex hydrogeology, and anthropogenic activity, making
 baseline establishment and anomaly detection more challenging.
 Offshore, logistical complexities and higher operational costs limit the frequency of
 monitoring, particularly for downhole and cross-well methods. Nonetheless, seismic
 techniques benefit from more consistent subsurface coverage and repeatability. In shallow
 monitoring, offshore monitoring benefits from the continuity between sediments and
 seawater, enabling acoustic and chemical detection of leakage via bubble streams, pH
 changes, or seabed deformation. Unlike onshore settings, protection of USDWs is generally
 not a concern, as offshore sub-seabed aquifers are typically saline.
 Surface displacement monitoring (e.g., InSAR, satellite imagery) is widely used onshore due
 to land coherence and infrastructure access. Offshore, such methods are less applicable,
 and alternatives such as seabed displacement sensors or global positioning system (GPS)
 on platforms face reliability and calibration challenges.
 Instrumentation access and infrastructure also differ. Onshore, monitoring wells are easier
 and cheaper to drill, and land-based sensor arrays can be widely deployed. Offshore, well
 spacing is broader and dedicated monitoring wells are limited, constraining options for high-
 resolution subsurface investigation. Some differences between onshore and offshore
 monitoring, verification and accounting practices are summarized in Table 2-18.

Sleipner Monitoring Plan
At Sleipner, where CO₂ injection in the North Sea off Norway began in 1996 and continues
to this day, the monitoring strategy is designed to manage the risks of CO₂ migration through
geological seals, wellbores, or beyond the storage complex. The core approach is based on
time-lapse (4D) seismic surveys, repeated extensively to track the CO2 plume and detect
any sign of upward or lateral leakage. Complementing this deep-focused method, well-based

monitoring—including continuous wellhead pressure measurements—has been in use since
injection started to provide ongoing insights into downhole conditions. Over the years,
additional research initiatives have integrated potential-field methods and shallow-focused
tools to further refine the understanding of CO₂ behavior. Although the publicly available
dataset spans from 1994 to 2013 (Table 2-19), it is important to note that this project
remains active, and the additional seismic repeats—largely driven by both commercial
operational needs and scientific research—continue to enhance the overall reliability and
performance of the monitoring regime at Sleipner.

Snøhvit Monitoring Plan
At the North Sea Snøhvit site, CO₂ injection began in 2008 at a rate of approximately 0.8 Mt
per year, with a total of 23 Mt planned over the project’s projected 30-year lifespan. The
primary goals are to keep injection pressure below fracture thresholds—thus preserving the
mechanical integrity of the reservoir and its caprock—and to monitor the CO₂ plume for
possible upward migration. Since the injection of CO₂, continuous downhole and wellhead
pressure measurements have been implemented. As shown in Table 2-20, monitoring efforts
from 2003 to 2013 focus on deep-focused techniques, including repeated 3D seismic
acquisitions and continuous well-based pressure and temperature measurements. Additional
shallow-focused surveys—such as multibeam echosounding, water column sampling, and
seabed sampling—have been carried out, but these complement the core deep-focused
approach. Overall, the Snøhvit plan provides high-resolution tracking of the injected CO₂ and
ensures that reservoir pressures are carefully managed, thereby reducing the risks of
unintended migration or mechanical failure.

ROAD Monitoring Plan
The ROAD project off the coast of the Netherlands is currently paused, and CO₂ injection
has not yet commenced. Despite this, the monitoring program is well-developed with primary
emphasis on detecting potential leakage through wellbores. Previous studies have assessed
localized pressure anomalies, barrier integrity, and potential fault-related migration, with
results feeding into updated risk assessments. The plan will be updated at least six months
before the start of injection, again within four years and nine months after injection begins,
and every five years thereafter.
Once operations commence, a combination of deep-focused, well-based, and shallow-
focused monitoring technologies will be deployed across all project phases. 3D seismic
surveys are expected to be carried out during and after CO₂ injection, and potentially beyond
project closure, to detect and assess the long-term migration behavior of the injected CO₂.
Wellhead pressure and temperature will be monitored continuously both before and during
the injection phase, while downhole pressure monitoring is expected to begin continuously
once injection starts (Table 2-21). Seabed gas and sediment sampling will be performed
regularly throughout the injection period to determine the presence of any CO₂ leakage near
the seafloor. Wellbore integrity assessments will be conducted at various points across the
project’s lifespan to confirm mechanical stability and containment performance. In addition to
the pre-injection and injection phases, the monitoring program also includes a structured
post-injection plan that addresses the transition to well abandonment and eventual transfer
of responsibilities to the competent authority.

Goldeneye Monitoring Plan
The Goldeneye North Sea full-chain CCS project was designed to capture CO₂ emissions
from an operational gas-fired power plant in Peterhead (UK), with the intention to store
between 10 and 20 million tonnes of CO₂ over a 10-year period. However, the project was
cancelled due to funding constraints. Despite this, a monitoring plan was developed, closely
aligned with a comprehensive risk assessment to address a range of potential concerns
including CO₂ release at the seabed, sub-sea or platform blowouts, lateral migration to
nearby fields or legacy wellbores, and the movement of dissolved CO₂ within the formation.
The monitoring strategy focused on demonstrating containment and conformance across
baseline, injection, and post-injection phases, integrating both deep- and shallow-focused
components. For deep-focused monitoring, the plan included time-lapse 3D seismic surveys
and downhole seismic – vertical seismic profiles (VSPs). Continuous data acquisition from
broadband seismometers beneath the platform and GPS installations was also included to
track any structural or geodynamic changes during injection, although not planned for post-
injection.

Tomakomai Monitoring Plan
The Tomakomai CCS demonstration project in Japan represents a full-chain, pilot-scale
initiative aimed at capturing CO₂ from a hydrogen production facility and storing it in two
distinct offshore reservoirs near Tomakomai Port. Between 2016 and 2018, the project
successfully achieved its storage target of 100,000 tonnes of CO₂ per year, transitioning
afterward into a post-injection monitoring phase. A key focus of the project’s monitoring plan
is to characterize reservoir behavior, ensure early detection of potential leakage, and
crucially, to investigate seismic activity, given Japan’s naturally high seismicity.
The monitoring program at Tomakomai incorporates multiple surveys of deep-focused, well-
based, and shallow-focused inspection (Table 2-23). A combination of 2D and 3D seismic
surveys was utilized to image the storage reservoirs, all conducted via ocean bottom cables
(OBCs). These cables also support a significant component of the seismicity monitoring,
which includes detection of both natural earthquakes and microseismic events. To
complement this, the project employs four permanent Ocean Bottom Seismometers (OBSs)
and downhole sensors installed in observation wells. This setup enables continuous
geophysical monitoring across both the injection and post-injection phases.
Continuous measurements of wellhead and downhole pressure and temperature were
maintained throughout the injection period and continue in the post-injection phase.
Additionally, seabed imaging, sediment sampling, and water column monitoring are
conducted on a permanent or continuous basis, offering a comprehensive view of the
shallow subsurface and marine environment.

K12-B Monitoring Plan
The K12-B project, located in the Dutch sector of the North Sea, was developed as a
research-focused CO₂ storage initiative, with particular emphasis on assessing the potential
of CO₂-enhanced gas recovery. From 2004 to 2013, the project reinjected over 80,000
tonnes of CO₂ into the depleted gas field. The site benefits from a thick Zechstein evaporite
formation, which serves as an excellent upper and lateral seal, significantly limiting the risk
of migration through the caprock. Given this favorable geological containment, the main risk
identified is migration via wellbores, making wellbore integrity the central focus of the
monitoring program. The monitoring approach is research-oriented, involving selective
deployment of well-based techniques over time rather than a continuous operational
framework. Monitoring activities have included downhole and wellhead pressure
measurements, well integrity checks, chemical tracer injection, and downhole fluid sampling
(Table 2-24).

Northern Lights Monitoring Plan
The Northern Lights project located in the North Sea is fully prepared to begin CO₂ injection,
with the first phase scheduled to start in 2025. The project has developed a monitoring
strategy focused on ensuring long-term containment, environmental protection, and
regulatory compliance throughout the CO₂ storage lifecycle (Table 2-25). The monitoring
program will include 3D seismic surveys and microseismic monitoring, with the latter
expected to be essential for detecting induced seismicity. Continuous wellhead and
downhole pressure and temperature monitoring will be used to assess injection performance
and detect any irregular pressure behavior that might signal migration or leakage. Well
integrity is a major focus, particularly given the reliance on long-term injectivity and the
proximity to legacy wellbores in the North Sea. Regular downhole fluid sampling will be
employed to track geochemical evolution and verify conformance to expected storage

Pre-Salt Monitoring Plan
The Pre-Salt project in Brazil’s Santos Basin is currently the largest CO₂ injection initiative

plume within the reservoir. In addition, pressure and temperature monitoring systems are
installed downhole, enabling real-time data acquisition to assess injection performance and

Well-based methods are always a mainstay of any offshore monitoring program, calling for

2.5.3 Indian River Shallow Monitoring Plan
Indian River Shallow (IRS) is a novel storage concept of injecting CO2 from onshore wells
into offshore reservoirs. Because injection will be in state (not federal) waters, it is expected
that the EPA will have primacy over Class VI injection, rather than BOEM. The IRS storage

saline injection into Lower Cretaceous Wastegate Formation reservoirs (Figure 2-31). Due to

groundwater wells, as the CO2 plume moves updip and into shallower depths. A
combination of 3D seismic, and quite possibly, monitoring wells may be necessary to
monitor the injected plume, while complying with regulatory requirements. Given water
depths of less than 10 meters and abundant nearshore obstacles, the seismic data will most
certainly need to be acquired using an OBC system. A semi-permanent ocean bottom node
system for induced seismic events is recommended and may also be required, depending

Additional research on the subsurface geology for the northeast Atlantic OCS includes
understanding the spatial distribution of the EODs and of carbonate cementation of the
sandstone. For this data integration into Petrel, analog basins were utilized to complement
current observations and interpretations of the BCT Logan Canyon.
For the fluvial and estuarine EODs, the straight to anastomosing Orinoco Delta of Venezuela
was selected for its abundance of wide and shallow channels, which the channels of the
BCT are interpreted to be based on well logs and seismic signatures.
For the inner and outer shelf EODs, the analogs include the Scotian Basin in Canada and
the Rhone Delta in the Mediterranean, which are both wave-dominated systems formed in a
passive margin with similar diagenesis. A wave-dominated delta system is characterized by
sediments being carried parallel to shore which oftentimes has large, interconnected sand
bodies that are parallel to the shore. Their general morphologies and diagenetic trends have
been recorded for integration for the SEM.
According to the Logan Canyon Formation of the Scotian Basin in Canada, diagenesis of
these rocks are difficult to predict stratigraphically. However, variations in permeability are
related to the burial depth, lithofacies, grain size, and sediment supply. The shelf EOD has
late secondary porosity that is likely from deep basinal fluid migration and alteration, and
these diagenetic trends are elongated down dip (Zhang et al., 2015). The calcitic and
dolomitic lithologies of the shelf EOD at the BCT are also present with the well logs, which
further support their similarities. As a result, these analog trends were input into the SEM in
Petrel.
In association with the data inventory, a survey was completed on 31 key offshore CCS
projects and technical proposals worldwide, analyzing their geographical distribution,
operational status, reservoir characteristics, CO₂ sources, injection rates, monitoring
techniques, and associated costs. The goal of this work is to take key learnings from other
pertinent offshore CCS projects so that they may be applied to our current feasibility study of
the offshore Atlantic.
The data presented in this report were collected through a comprehensive review of publicly
available technical reports from different companies like Equinor, Total, Eni, ExxonMobil,
and Petrobras. Additionally, information was gathered from official company websites,
conference presentations, and peer-reviewed articles. These references are documented in
the project summary database in Excel. Most of the data was collected in August 2024, and
additional geocharacterization and monitoring data were added in March 2025.
The database was developed to compile, organize, and analyze the collected data. It
encompassed information on baseline monitoring techniques, geological characterization
methods, and summaries of various offshore CCS projects worldwide. The monitoring,
verification, and accounting (MVA) data provided insights into project-specific practices
designed to detect potential CO₂ leaks, while the geocharacterization data detailed the
techniques used to assess storage complex properties. Additionally, the database included a
summary of essential technical parameters such as reservoir type, geological age, depth,
porosity, permeability, and cap rock characteristics.
Based on this organized information, charts, maps, and tables were developed from scratch
to provide a visual understanding of the key findings. These visual aids were created

tables may reflect information from different projects, depending on the completeness of the

Project Status
The status of the 31 offshore CCS projects evaluated varies widely. About half are still in the
planning phase, which involves conceptualizing the project, setting objectives, evaluating
potential sites, and considering technical, financial, and regulatory factors. Nearly a quarter
are in the technical review phase, where detailed site assessments, risk analysis, modeling,
and simulations are conducted to assess the project's safety, efficiency, and feasibility. At
this stage, engineering designs are often reviewed, and compliance with regulations and
standards is verified.
Approximately 10% of the 31 projects are operational, including notable examples like
Sleipner in the North Sea, which has been injecting CO₂ since 1996, and Snøhvit in the
Norwegian Sea, active since 2008. Also, Brazil's Pre-Salt project has used CO₂ injection for
EOR since 2008, while Japan's Tomakomai project is currently in post-injection monitoring.
The remaining projects were canceled due to financial constraints, regulatory challenges, or
technical challenges.

mechanisms. Together, these data types enable the construction of detailed SEMs of the

Storage Complex Characteristics
The properties of the reservoirs and caprocks of each of the offshore CCS projects have
been recorded and analyzed where data were publicly available. Porosity values of
reservoirs range from 12% to 35%, though the most common range is between 12% and
20%, indicating moderate storage capacity. High-porosity reservoirs (above 30%) are less
frequent but provide superior CO₂ storage potential. Permeability values vary widely, from
80 mD to 10,000 mD, with most projects reporting permeability within the 80 to 1,000 mD
range. This suggests that many reservoirs have high permeability, which is beneficial for
efficient CO₂ injection and distribution within the storage formation.

Reservoir thickness varies significantly among offshore CCS projects, with documented
values ranging from 68 m at Snøhvit to 274 m at the Northern Endurance project in the North
Sea. The average reservoir thickness across projects is approximately 170 m. Most
reservoirs consist primarily of sandstones, although carbonate reservoirs are present in
some cases, such as in the Pre-Salt project in Brazil. The depth of reservoirs ranges from
900 m, as seen in the Sleipner project in the North Sea, to 2,820 m in the Viking project, also
in the North Sea. In other regions, such as Japan, the top of the reservoir is found at 1,200
m, while offshore southern Australia, it is located at 1,500 m. Most reservoirs are located at
depths of approximately 1,400 m (Figure 3-6). The thickness of the caprock formations,
which play a critical role in preventing CO₂ leakage, ranges from 70 m at Sleipner to as
much as 2,000 m in Brazil’s Pre-Salt formations. These sealing formations mainly consist of
shale, with evaporites present in some cases, such as in the Pre-Salt project in Brazil,
Northern Endurance and the Viking CCS project in the North Sea.

CO₂ Sources, Injection Rates and Injection Wells Required

Offshore CCS projects typically source CO₂ from industrial and energy sectors characterized
by high emissions. Major contributors include petrochemical plants, power plants, steel
production facilities, cement manufacturing sites, and oil refineries. Collectively, these
industries account for a significant proportion of global CO₂ emissions, making them prime
candidates for large-scale carbon capture initiatives.
Petrochemical plants and power plants alone generate nearly half of the captured CO₂ in
offshore CCS projects, while industries such as steel production, cement manufacturing, and
refineries contribute around 26%.
On the other hand, injection rate data were plotted for 29 out of the 31 projects, based on
data availability. These cumulative injection rates range from 0.1 to 8 MMT/year. Most of the
projects have injection rates between 0.1 and 2 MMT/year, as these values are influenced
by reservoir properties, well design, geomechanical stability, and overall storage capacity.

Higher injection rates are less common and typically depend on factors like reservoir

Monitoring Techniques
Offshore CCS projects employ a variety of monitoring techniques to ensure CO₂ remains
retained. These methods are categorized into deep-focused, well-based direct
measurements, and shallow-focused monitoring strategies. Monitoring activities span the

EXPLORE-ACS Final Technical Report 74 March 2026

entire lifecycle of a project, from pre-injection (baseline) through injection to post-injection
phases. The following information outlines some of the monitoring techniques employed in
six projects, including Sleipner, Snohvit, Northern Lights, and Endurance in the North Sea,
Tomakomai in Japan, and Pre-Salt in Brazil.
Shallow-focused monitoring methods provide information from the near seabed, seabed and
water-column. These tools are classified into geophysical, chemical, and biological. The
monitoring methods used in this category include sediment sampling to measure CO₂
concentrations, seabed imaging to identify potential gas migration, and water column
sampling, which involves analyzing dissolved CO₂ levels at various depths to facilitate early
leak detection. According to the data collected, sediment sampling and water column
sampling (bubble stream chemistry) are the most popular, employed in four projects each.
These methods are essential for detecting potential leakage near the seabed. Techniques
such as seabed bathymetry and imaging are used in three projects, while conductivity-
temperature-depth profiles and multibeam echo sounding are less common, appearing in
one or two projects.
Deep-focused monitoring methods provide insights into the reservoir and deeper
overburden. These techniques aim to detect unexpected CO2 migration out of the primary
storage reservoir, movement into the overburden, possible secondary reservoirs, and out of
the storage complex, which could trigger leakage. Tools used include time-lapse 3D seismic
imaging to track CO₂ plume migration and seabed gravity measurements to detect density
changes within the reservoir. Microseismic monitoring is also employed to confirm that CO₂
follows expected migration pathways without inducing seismic activity. In this regard, the
most widely used method across all six projects, according to the data collected, is 3D
seismic (time-lapse), which is applied in all six projects. Other commonly used techniques
include seabed gravity and controlled-source electromagnetic (CSEM), highlighting their
importance in detecting subsurface changes.
Well-based direct measurements focus on evaluating pressure changes within the reservoir
and injection wells. Monitoring fluctuations in well pressure is crucial for detecting potential
CO₂ leaks and ensuring storage integrity. According to the data collected, the most
frequently used techniques include wellhead pressure and temperature measurements,
which are applied in all six projects, and downhole pressure and temperature
measurements, used in five. These methods are essential for monitoring reservoir
conditions. Less common techniques, such as well integrity assessments and downhole fluid
sampling, are each employed in one project.

3.3.1 Gravity/Magnetic Analysis of Great Stone Dome (LDEO)
In support of the EXPLORE-ACS project, Lamont Doherty Earth Observatory (LDEO)
completed analysis of constraints on the geological Great Stone Dome structure and
composition from new high-resolution aeromagnetic and gravimetric data. New high-
resolution aeromagnetic and gravimetric survey data over the GSD revealed positive
magnetic and gravity anomalies that are offset by < 5 km, with the former showing a north-
south elongation. A combination of geopotential modeling, seismic, and well data
established a 2 km uplift of sediments above the GSD with a diameter 56% larger than
previous estimates. Modeling and seismic interpretations suggest the GSD is a dike swarm
with a southern extension. The significant uplift on the northern, southern and western flanks
form potential structural closures for CO2 storage, with the western flank situated ~20 km
closer to emission sources than previously thought.
The extent of the GSD can be observed by a positive anomaly in existing magnetic and
gravity surveys due to its strong susceptibility and high density, respectively (e.g., Zietz and
Gilbert, 1980; Maus et al., 2009; USGS, 2022), however previous interpretations of these
data have been hampered by poor resolution and the dilution of signals caused by the
adjacent East Coast Magnetic Anomaly (ECMA) (e.g., Turney et al., 2025b). Additionally, the
overall structure of the GSD is relatively unknown due to limited drill core data and
incoherent seismic reflections.
A new high-resolution aeromagnetic and gravimetric survey was conducted above the GSD
(Figure 3-8) as part of an independent project funded under Grantham Foundation
award GFPE CU23-1713 (Turney et al., 2025a). 2D and 3D forward and inverse modeling
were subsequently conducted using Geosoft Oasis Montaj to provide new insights into the
structure of the GSD and future CO2 storage targets.
The airborne magnetic and gravity data were collected at an altitude of ~200 m and line
spacing of ~1000 m. A Scintrex CS-3 Cesium vapor magnetometer was used with a
resolution of 0.01 ± 0.002 nT, operating range of 15000 to 105000 nT, sampling rate of 20
Hz, iMar iCorus X gravimeter generated low-noise longwave gravity data with a sampling
rate of 1.0 Hz, and gravity noise on the final grid of < 1.5 mGal (RMS) after passing through
a 100-s low-pass filter.

Initial 2D forward modeling was guided by well data, depth estimates from Lippert (1983),
and seismic information after Klitgord and Scheider (1994), Triezenberg et al. (2016) and
Schmelz et al. (2020). Susceptibilities (a measure of the degree to which sediments become
magnetized in response to an applied magnetic field) and densities for sediments were taken
from Dobrin and Savit (1988). The GSD was assigned a susceptibility of 3 x 10-3 and density
of 2.9 to 3.0 g/cm3 based on lamprophyre samples from Mobil well 544-1 and measured
values from Rao (2008), Hrouda et al. (2016), and Baesso et al., (2022).
Gravity data inversion and 3D modeling were conducted using wire-framing of 2D forward
models to constrain the GSD structure and produce a relief map of the top of the GSD. 2D
Werner deconvolution (Werner, 1953) and 3D standard Euler deconvolution (Thompson,
1982) were applied to the magnetic data identify the shallowest sources of magnetism,
including the GSD top. The Euler solutions are given as structural indices which increase in
complexity from 1 = dike to 2 = cylinder to 3 = sphere. Forward and inverse models and
depth-to-source analyses were combined to iteratively constrain the shape of the GSD.
Results showed a positive magnetic and gravity anomaly located above the GSD with a
diameter of ~15 km (Figure 3-9), although the former is elongated N-S with a width of ~25
km. The gravity anomaly was offset from the magnetic anomaly by 4 to 5 km north and
northwest above the GSD.

the dome, the modelled GSD body has a width of ~13 km which narrows to ~4 km towards
its greatest depth of > 18 km. The magnetic susceptibility inversion results (Figure 3-10) are
indicative of a mafic composition of the GSD, supporting the lamprophyre observations from
well samples (e.g., Jansa and Pe-Piper, 1988). This modeling and bedding in seismic
sections also suggest evidence of interbedded intrusions or a dike swarm for the GSD into
adjoining sediments, rather than a solid igneous stockwork structure.

4.1.2 CO2 Quality and CO2 Pipeline Safety
Kinder Morgan has established specific quality requirements for CO₂ transported through its
pipelines to ensure safety, efficiency, and compatibility with EOR operations. The key
specifications are as follows:

• CO₂ Purity: Minimum of 95 mole percent CO₂.
• Water Content: No free water; vapor-phase water content must not exceed 30 pounds per
 million cubic feet (MMcf).
• Hydrogen Sulfide (H₂S): Maximum of 20 parts per million by volume.

EXPLORE-ACS Final Technical Report 85 March 2026

• Total Sulfur: Maximum of 35 parts per million by weight.
• Temperature: Must not exceed 120°F.
• Nitrogen (N₂): Maximum of 4 mole percent.
• Hydrocarbons: Maximum of 5 mole percent; hydrocarbon dew point must not exceed -
 20°F.
• Oxygen (O₂): Maximum of 10 parts per million by weight.
• Glycol: No liquid glycol; if present, it must not exceed 0.3 gallons per MMcf.
CO2 pipeline safety has emerged as a critical issue in CO2 pipeline permitting projects.
Kinder Morgan quality, as described above, is the industry standard for CO2 pipeline
transport to maintain CO2 pipeline integrity and utilized for CO2-EOR since the 1970s. CO2
storage projects may require more specialized CO2 purity specifications based on materials
used in pipeline and injection components (Parfomak, 2007; IEA, 2014; Wetenhall, 2014).
Accordingly, while the Kinder Morgan standards likely cover the majority of sources
contributing CO2 to the EXPLORE ACS pipeline, the CO2 gas quality standards should be
established for each source contributing to the pipeline to address any source-specific
contamination concerns. Due to the varying chemical compositions of CO2 captured from
different industrial sources, standards are often negotiated for specific source-to-sink
projects.
Current Regulations Governing CO2 Pipeline Safety

The Pipeline and Hazardous Materials and Safety Administration (PHMSA) is responsible for
the development and promulgation of regulations addressing CO2 pipeline safety (Current
CO2 pipeline safety regulations are found at 49 CFR Parts 190, 192, and 195) as
summarized below:
49 CFR Part 190 (Pipeline Safety Enforcement and Regulatory Procedures) outlines the
procedural regulations for the enforcement of pipeline safety laws under the jurisdiction of
PHMSA (2019). It establishes the framework for administrative practices, including
inspections, investigations, and enforcement actions related to pipeline safety. The
regulation details the processes for issuing notices of probable violations, corrective action
orders, and penalties for non-compliance. It also provides guidelines for public participation,
operator hearings, and judicial reviews. Essentially, Part 190 ensures that pipeline operators
comply with federal safety standards and provides mechanisms for addressing violations.
49 CFR 191 (Transportation of Natural Gas and Other Gas by Pipeline; Annual, Incident,
and Other Reporting) establishes the reporting requirements for pipeline operators
transporting natural gas, hazardous liquids, or CO2. It mandates operators to report pipeline
incidents, such as leaks, ruptures, injuries, fatalities, or property damage, to PHMSA.
Operators must also submit annual reports detailing pipeline mileage, safety-related
conditions, and maintenance activities. The regulation includes provisions for immediate
notification of significant incidents and outlines the format and timelines for reporting. Part
191 ensures transparency and accountability in pipeline operations, enabling PHMSA to
monitor safety trends and enforce compliance effectively. Note that 49 CFR 191 applies to
pipelines transporting gaseous CO2, not liquid or supercritical CO2.
49 CFR Part 192 (Transportation of Natural Gas and Other Gas by Pipeline: Minimum
Federal Safety Standards) sets the minimum safety standards for the design, construction,
operation, and maintenance of pipelines transporting natural gas and other gas types. It
covers requirements for materials, welding, pressure testing, corrosion control, and pipeline
integrity management. The regulation also includes provisions for emergency response, leak
detection, and safety measures in high-consequence areas (HCAs), such as densely

populated or environmentally sensitive regions. Part 192 ensures the safe and reliable
operation of gas pipelines to protect public safety, property, and the environment. 49 CFR
192 applies to pipelines transporting gaseous CO2, not liquid or supercritical CO2.

4.1.3 Overview of Post-Combustion Carbon Capture Technologies
As part of the project, a preliminary assessment of adding carbon capture to existing industry
in the northeastern U.S. was completed. There are a diverse range of CO2 sources in the
region, so many different capture approaches may be required to implement CCS. CCS
technologies for power plants and cement kilns in the US fall into three main categories:
post-combustion capture, pre-combustion capture, and oxy-fuel combustion, along with
storage and utilization methods. Post-combustion carbon capture using amine sorbents is
the most common carbon capture technology in the US for the following reasons:
Proven Commercial Viability: Amine-based systems, such as those using
monoethanolamine (MEA), have been in use for decades and are well-established in the US
for post-combustion CO₂ capture. The systems are widely deployed in industries such as
power generation, cement production, and chemical manufacturing.
High CO₂ Capture Efficiency: Amine systems can capture up to 90% of CO₂ from flue gas
streams, making them highly effective for reducing emissions.
Flexibility: Amine-based systems are suitable for retrofitting existing facilities, which is
critical for older coal and natural gas plants in the US.
The carbon capture process using amines as sorbents generally consists of three steps:
Step 1. Absorption: Flue gas containing CO₂ is passed through an absorber column (also
called a scrubber) where it comes into contact with an aqueous amine solution (e.g., MEA,
diethanolamine [DEA], or other amine blends). The amine chemically reacts with CO₂ to
form a soluble compound, effectively removing CO₂ from the gas stream.

EXPLORE-ACS Final Technical Report 90 March 2026

Step 2. Regeneration: The CO₂-rich amine solution is sent to a stripper column (or
regenerator), where it is heated. The heat breaks the chemical bond between the amine and
CO₂, releasing pure CO₂ gas. The regenerated amine solution is recycled back to the
absorber for reuse.
Step 3. CO2 Purification, Compression, and Storage: The captured CO₂ is processed to
remove water and impurities and then compressed and prepared for transport to storage
sites (e.g., geological formations) or for utilization in industrial processes (e.g., enhanced oil
recovery, chemical production).
Based on their widespread existing use and proven effectiveness, amine sorbents are
recommended as the primary carbon capture method for the majority of CO₂ sources in the
northeastern U.S; however, further source-level assessments are necessary to ensure
optimal technology fit across the region’s diverse industrial landscape.

4.2.1 CO2 Pipeline Transport Analysis
This section presents proposed offshore CO₂ transport routes based on preliminary technical
and economic evaluations of pipeline and compression systems, including requirements for
delivering CO₂ at appropriate pressures for offshore injection. Regulatory and permitting
considerations were integrated throughout to ensure all proposed scenarios are consistent
with state and federal requirements and address stakeholder interests. Additional guidance
for routing CO2 pipelines for CCS projects was referred to in the analysis (Righetti, 2017;
Serpa, 2011; Wallace, 2015).
The objective of this task was to develop appropriate transport and injection systems. The
design assumed CO2 is transported via pipeline (onshore and offshore) although additional
transportation methods may be studied. Two storage sites were assessed in further detail
through dynamic reservoir modeling to assess reservoir injectivity: A) Indian River Shallow
(Maryland coastal site) and B) Great Stone Dome (New Jersey site). The Indian River
Shallow CO2 storage site is a nearshore option with storage simulated in state waters, while
the Great Stone Dome CO2 storage site is located approximately 50 to 70 km offshore in 30
to 60 m water depth. A third storage site, Indian River Deep, is located offshore Maryland in
federal waters but was not dynamically simulated for injectivity assessment.
The project aimed to define an efficient, feasible and safe onshore and offshore proposal for
transporting CO2. Due to the nature of the fields, different solutions may be suitable for the
Maryland and New Jersey sites. The intent was to identify development possibilities for both
locations and required infrastructure to safely inject CO2 into the reservoir.
Two routes for a pipeline and compression system were specified to gather CO2 from the
locations identified in Task 1 for delivery to shoreline/coastal locations — one in New Jersey
and the other in Maryland. This study also accounted for the necessary compression to

4.2.2 CO2 Injection Reservoir Simulations
Reservoir simulations were completed for two CO2 injection scenarios to illustrate
deployment options for CO2 storage along the offshore Mid-Atlantic (Figure 4-6). The
objective of the reservoir simulations was to use numerical modeling to design a well field at
the Great Stone Dome scenario for yearly rate of 15 to 20 MMT/year and 2.5 MMT/year at
the Indian River shallow scenario. The simulations were run for 30 years of injection and a
50-year post-injection period to evaluate the size and shape of pressure and CO2 saturation
extents. A safety threshold of 90% of rock fracture gradient was set to ensure the
simulations are limited to safe injection pressures.

 Figure 4-6. Location map for Indian River Shallow and Great Stone Dome reservoir simulations.

The reservoir simulations were developed with computer programs that simulate the CO2
injection process with numerical equations. The simulations specified a three-dimensional
gridded array of geotechnical parameters based on the geological models described in the
EXPLORE-ACS Task 3 Summary Report. CO2 injection simulations depict both the
geological rock properties and the subsurface conditions in terms of pressure, temperature,
fluid saturation, and boundary features.
The simulations were completed with the CMG-GEM multi-phase flow simulator to model
CO2 injection into deep saline rock formations. The CO2 simulations account for the multi-
phase behavior of CO2 mixed with the deep saline fluids. The simulations involved many
assumptions because they are simplified versions of reality designed to predict the pressure,
CO2 saturation areas, injection rates, and optimal wellfield designs. The simulations also
helped to understand data gaps and uncertainties in the subsurface. The simulations
completed under the EXPLORE-ACS project were based on limited well control. In addition,

the simulations were not calibrated to field injection pressure falloff data or baseline
hydraulic measurements which would provide more confidence in simulation results.
Therefore, the results should be considered preliminary pre-feasibility level output.

4.3 CO2 Storage System Design
Designs for the CO2 storage system were developed for the Indian River Shallow and Great
Stone Dome sites. The designs were based on the reservoir simulation results that specified
the injection well locations, injection pressures, and flow rates. In addition, the design was
linked to the CO2 pipeline described in Section 4.1.

4.3.1 Great Stone Dome CO2 Storage System Design
Most of the effort for the CO2 storage system design was focused on the Great Stone Dome
site, since it is more complicated with 17 MMT/year CO2 injection rate and eight injection
wells located further offshore. This was focused on based on the significantly high storage
potential and detailed characterization efforts. Aker Solutions conducted the offshore
infrastructure evaluation to support CO₂ injection at the Great Stone Dome. Aker Solutions

EXPLORE-ACS Final Technical Report 112 March 2026

completed the design for suitable offshore system development options and associated
equipment including:

• Evaluation of subsea and surface injection solutions,
• Assessment of development options and field architectures,
• Design of main offshore equipment, and
• Development of order-of-magnitude cost estimates for the proposed solutions.
The study assessed system-level architecture, field layout options and equipment design
with the goal of defining a robust, flexible and cost-effective technical solution for injecting
CO2 at Great Stone Dome. Much of the infrastructure is applicable to other locations along
the Atlantic outer continental shelf because water depth and seabed conditions are similar to
the Great Stone Dome location.
The base case development assumed eight injection wells located approximately 200 km
offshore from the off-take point in 60 to 70 meters of water depth. The design includes a 16
inch pipeline that brings the CO2 to the injection site where the wells are grouped into two
four-well clusters (SW and SE pads), spaced approximately 10.8 km apart. An average
annual injection rate of 17 MMT was projected over a 30-year operational life.
Two main options were investigated for injecting CO2 offshore: subsea injection and platform
based systems. It was concluded that a subsea development strategy is more cost efficient
at these water depths. Subsea systems are less intrusive and offer greater flexibility and
scalability. The study evaluated several field layout options: daisy chain, satellite wells,
clustered wells with central manifolds, and template-based systems. Among these, clustered
well configurations with a central manifold were found to provide a good balance between
operational flexibility, system complexity and cost. Cluster topologies offer compact layouts
with wells located in the vicinity of manifolds that distribute the CO2 from the pipeline.
The subsea field design was based on 7 inch subsea trees (5 inch option are also available)
with a 5 1/2 inch tubing hanger interface to accommodate the required downhole tubing size.
The manifolds featured four slots and a single 10 inch header for CO2 distribution. The
manifold also distributed the umbilical services to the trees via electric and optic flying leads.
Distance to shore (200 km) can be challenging from a control and communication
perspective. An all-electric system was specified to reduce umbilical cost, however further
work is required to define the best power transmission solution and umbilical design.
The design included high level cost estimates (Class 5). For the Aker Solutions scope the
major cost contributor was the umbilical from shore followed by the subsea injection trees
and wellheads. In future phases, the location of the control room and umbilical termination
should be considered to reduce the umbilical length and cost. Aker Solutions considers the
Mid-Atlantic Hub Offshore Storage Hub a feasible initiative comparable to other CO2 injection
projects developed across the world and will support further development of this project.

4.3.3 Great Stone Dome Offshore Injection System Selection
Two main approaches were considered for defining the offshore injection system: subsea
injection trees and surface injection platforms. These alternatives represent different design
philosophies and are suited to specific field conditions and project requirements. The
following sections describe both systems and their advantages and trade-offs in the context
of CO₂ injection.
Subsea Injection Tree System- Subsea injection trees are installed on the seafloor and are
equipped with valves and sensors that enable the safe and controlled injection of CO₂.
These systems require external power and communication, typically delivered via a
dedicated umbilical, while CO₂ is supplied through a pipeline. The trees can be deployed as
standalone units connected directly to the pipeline, tied back to a subsea manifold, or
integrated into a larger template structure. Several types of injection trees are available on
the market (Figure 4-24). Current subsea tree configurations derive from oil and gas
applications, with adaptations for CO₂ service. However, vendors are actively working on
standardization and modularization initiatives aiming to simplify and optimize the designs for
CO₂ injection.

 Figure 4-24. Typical subsea injection trees.

Surface Injection System- Surface injection trees are installed on platforms that house all
other equipment necessary to safely inject CO₂. The injection platform consists of three main
components: 1) topside equipment, 2) substructure supporting the topside, and 3) a pile that
penetrates the seabed and acts as a foundation (Figure 4-25). The substructure is designed
according to several technical factors in which topside weight and water depth play a crucial

role. For the case of Great Stone Dome (65 m water depth), a four-legged jacket has been
used as the reference design (Figure 4-26). Typically, the platform is unmanned and
controlled via an umbilical.

Installation methods- The main differentiator between subsea and surface tree solutions is

increase overall costs, making the dry tree solution more expensive than the subsea
alternative on a per-well basis.

perform well intervention. However, the main difference is that wet systems require a high

of the dry tree. These factors make the intervention campaigns for subsea trees more
complex and costly.

Maintenance- Maintenance and repair operations on surface facilities are significantly
simpler than those required on a subsea system. It is worth noting that subsea trees have
high redundancy and reliability. Critical components are also designed to be retrievable at
subsea with minimum downtime. It is also important to note that surface equipment can be
exposed to severe weather conditions, but in general it is considered that maintenance and
repair cost for subsea equipment is higher.
Collisions and Area Congestion- Offshore platforms are generally more intrusive and have
a higher impact on other marine activities. Considering there are other offshore
developments in the proximity of the Great Stone Dome field, a subsea alternative offers
more flexibility to design the field considering other ocean users.
Technical Readiness- Surface and subsea trees for CO2 injection are being developed
based on their oil and gas predecessors and the technology presents high readiness in both
cases.
Field Design Flexibility- Subsea systems present a smaller footprint and are easily
expandable leading to more flexible field designs.
Visual Impact- Subsea equipment has no visual impact, as all infrastructure is installed on
the seabed. In contrast, topside systems can be 20 to 25 m above sea level.
Carbon Footprint- The additional structures and installation operations required for surface
equipment result in a higher carbon footprint, making subsea solutions more favorable in this
category.
Recommendation and Way Forward- Aker Solutions has conducted an evaluation of
subsea and surface tree solutions for the Great Stone Dome case. Based on the available
data and perceived project drivers, subsea systems are recommended due to their lower

Daisy Chain- In a daisy chain, subsea trees are connected to a main pipeline that follows the
position of the wells. For this topology to be effective, the location of the wells should allow
the pipeline to be laid without sharp turns or complex installation corridors. To connect the
trees to the main pipeline, short-length spools and inline tees (ILTs) are commonly used. The
main pipeline usually terminates in a pipeline end termination (PLET), which can serve as an
expansion hub if additional wells are added in the future (Figure 4-28). Pig receiving facilities
are typically connected to the PLET to facilitate maintenance operations. This is a simple
and effective field architecture for satellite wells that minimizes the number of required
structures. However, if the well locations are spread in a non-linear pattern, this configuration
tends to result in longer pipeline lengths.
Advantages of the daisy chain arrangement include:

• Ability to drill vertically into the reservoir with optimal well location
• Simpler commissioning and pigging operations
• Low-cost option.
Disadvantages of the daisy chain arrangement include:

• Jack-up rig needs to move to every location
• Long flowline length
• Low reliability. Highly dependent on single pipeline servicing all the wells
• Low flow control
• Low flexibility and potential for expansion
• Occupies a large area.

Further Considerations for CO2 Storage System Construction

Well Locations- The number and location of wells is crucial in defining an effective field
architecture. The current design assumes lateral drilling is possible and feasible. This
assumption needs to be confirmed in future study phases. Considering the current well
locations, the distance between the manifold and wells is approximately 65 to 70 m. It is

Monitoring and Bleed-Off Wells- Monitoring wells may be necessary to track CO2 migration
in the formation. These wells are typically located on the outskirts of the field to monitor CO2
migration outside pre-established formation boundaries. Monitoring wells are usually not
connected to the injection pipeline but will require power and signals. In addition, depending
on the geologic conditions, bleed-off wells may be necessary to bleed off excess water and
maintain reservoir pressure.
Area Congestion- There are several offshore wind projects, legacy wells and submarine
cables close to the Great Stone Dome site. The final field layout needs to take into account
the existing infrastructure and minimize the number of potential cable crossings. It is also
recommended to avoid routes used by maintenance and supply vessels servicing the
offshore wind fields as there can be a risk for heavy dropped objects.
CO2 Supply- Further discussion is needed to clarify the regularity of CO₂ supply and the
associated operational scenarios, (turndown cases, shutdowns, cold restarts, etc.). These
conditions may introduce additional system requirements that have not yet been evaluated.

4.3.6 Great Stone Dome CO2 Storage System Control System
The control system architecture was based on remote control of the subsea equipment via an
umbilical from the onshore control room (Figure 4-45).

 Figure 4-45. Control system onshore-offshore architecture.

All-Electric versus Electro-Hydraulic Control System- The offshore equipment can be
controlled using either an all-electric architecture or a conventional electro- hydraulic system.
The main difference between the two systems is valve actuation. An all-electric system uses
electric actuators, whereas a hydraulic system requires hydraulic fluid to operate the valves.
Electro-hydraulic systems have been the standard technology for the majority of oil and gas
developments. This system requires hydraulic power unit (HPU) and tubes in the umbilical to
supply control fluid to the offshore equipment. As step-outs and water depth increase, hydraulic
systems become more complex and difficult to operate. In these systems, the umbilical requires
tubes to supply the hydraulic fluid to the offshore equipment, increasing its cost.
All-electric systems use power cores in the umbilical to actuate the offshore equipment.
Umbilical cost tends to be lower, but electric actuators are more expensive than their hydraulic
counterparts and traditionally have presented a lower maturity level. Electric systems have also
faced challenges related to fail-safe mechanisms in safety functions. However, recent
development and standardization programs are changing these notions of all-electric systems
and can lead to system simplifications, lower cost and higher operability. For this study, Aker
Solutions recommends an all-electric system due to the long step-out distance and associated
umbilical cost.
Power and Communications- The proposed power distribution system faces challenges
related to step-out and distance to well locations. The following considerations were made:

• The control room is located at the Indian River Power Plant (approximately 200 km from the
 Great Stone Dome site)
• The system is based on segregated power-on-copper cables and fiber optic communication
• The maximum power consumption per tree is assumed to be 950 W
• Manifolds do not have SCMs and do not require electric power
• Traditional fiber optic communication is feasible as there is no bandwidth-intensive
 equipment.

Theoretically, a +/-66kV DC system with 70 mm2 power conductors would be sufficient to
transfer the required power over 200 km. However, a detailed power and system analysis is
required to define the optimal system solution.

Onshore Controls Equipment Master Control Station- The Master Control Station (MCS)
serves as the central control and monitoring hub for the onshore and offshore equipment. The
MCS is installed in the control room and requires HVAC facilities and controlled ambient
temperature. It features a rack design with dual redundant industrial PCs that operate on hot
standby, ensuring no single point of failure. An internal programmable logic controller (PLC) acts
as a watchdog to monitor the MCS performance. The MCS offers a graphical user interface
(GUI) that provides trending, logging, reporting, and alarm capabilities. This GUI can be
mirrored on a remote operator workstation at a different location.
Electrical Power Unit- The electrical power unit (EPU) provides power to the control system. It
consists of two segregated channels (EPU A/B) to eliminate single points of failure (Figure 4-
48). Each EPU includes relays and contactors on the input lines controlled by the emergency
shutdown (ESD) system. Due to the low power requirements of the controls equipment for the
injection system, it is possible to fit the components of the EPU in a cabinet.

 Figure 4-48. MCS dashboard (left and middle) and EPU (right).

ESD/PSD Nodes- The ESD/PSD (process shutdown) systems control system shutdowns. The
ESD signals are hardwired to the UPS unit, disconnecting power in case of an emergency and
generally affecting the entire injection system. The PSD signals are broadcasted to the MCS
that executes a discrete command sequence normally dedicated to control specific equipment
on the network. ESD/PSD triggers and actions are developed in detailed engineering as part of
cause and effect studies.
Umbilical Termination- The power and control umbilical is terminated onshore and connected
to the control equipment. Electric and fiber optic junction boxes bring the required services to
the umbilical termination for further distribution to the offshore system.
Subsea Control Module- The Subsea Control Module (SCM) is the main offshore control
equipment and is located on the injection trees. The SCM manages offshore power and
communication, executes the commands sent by the onshore MCS, sends feedback signals
and system health data. The design of the SCM is dependent on the control system vendor and
usually includes:

• Subsea Electronic Module: A sealed, nitrogen-filled electronics chamber operating at 1
 atmosphere that converts incoming power to the required DC voltages for instrumentation
 (typically 24 V and 5 V)

• Communications Interface: Acts as a network router enabling high- speed data transfer
 and control
• Power & Signal Distribution System: Internally routes power and communication signals to
 instruments and sensors

SCMs are typically built as modular and stackable assemblies:

• Components are isolated from external pressure using sealed canisters and dielectric fluid
• Internal metal plates house electrical connectors and circuit channels
• Electrical and fiber optic connectors for EFLs (electrical flying leads)/OFLs (optical flying
 leads)
• Mounting base with mechanical latching for subsea installation/retrieval.

4.3.7 Great Stone Dome CO2 Storage System Power and Signal Umbilical
The power and signal umbilical runs from the onshore control room to the offshore wells and
includes power and fiber optic cores. These elements are helically laid around a central filler to
maintain structural balance and ensure uniform stress distribution.
The umbilical cross-section depends on the number and size of power and fiber optic bundles.
Power cables are rated for the required transmission voltage and are typically jacketed with a
thermoplastic insulation layer. To maintain spacing and protect internal components, umbilicals
include polymer fillers and an armoring layer. The entire assembly is enclosed in an external
sheath, usually made of polyurethane or polyethylene, which provides resistance to abrasion,
seawater corrosion, and mechanical impact. In this study, hydraulic tubes are not required. For
this study, a representative umbilical cross-section was assumed (Figure 4-49). The
configuration includes:

• 2off primary copper conductors and 1off spare (70 mm2 with XLPE insulation each)
• 2off fiber optic bundles (typically 48 fibers organized in 4×12 arrangements)
• Fillers, armoring and outer sheath (polypropylene or PE).
The estimated outer diameter of the umbilical is approximately 100 to 120 mm (including armor
and sheathing).

4.3.9 Indian River Shallow CO2 Storage System Design
The Indian River Shallow CO2 storage system design was specified based on a single injection
well drilled on shore and completed offshore laterally into state waters. The well was designed
to accommodate 2.5 MMT/year CO2 injection. Preliminary cost estimates were made based on
recent Battelle drilling work and Front End Engineering Design (FEED) studies for CO2 storage
projects. The Indian River Shallow CO2 injection well would be a complicated well completion,
and a detailed well design by a drilling engineer would be recommended prior to any well
construction.
Figure 4-55 shows a preliminary well design for the Indian River Shallow CO2 storage system.
The well would require larger diameter casing to accommodate larger 5-inch diameter injection
tubing necessary to inject 2.5 MMT/year CO2. The well was designed to protect any shallow
groundwater resources per U.S. EPA Class VI UIC requirements. Thus, there are sections of
surface and intermediate casing cemented to surface. The well includes a lateral section 18,000
ft long to extend offshore into state waters. The injection section would be vertical from 5700 to
7000 ft deep.

4.4 Summary
A thorough review of existing infrastructure, including pipelines, offshore infrastructure
requirements, and related facilities, was conducted to assess the feasibility of CO2 capture,
transport and storage in the East Coast region. This analysis integrated technical,
environmental, and regulatory factors, considering routing constraints such as wind installations,
submarine cables, shipping lanes, and right of ways. The evaluation of candidate onshore
transfer sites led to the selection of Dagsboro, Delaware, as the most promising location for
onshore CO2 transfer, based on its readiness and existing infrastructure.
The project also addressed the critical issue of CO2 quality and pipeline safety, adopting
industry-standard specifications to ensure safe and efficient transport. Reservoir simulations
using dynamic reservoir modeling tools confirmed the feasibility of sustainable injection rates at
the Great Stone Dome and Indian River Shallow site, while highlighting the impact of geological
variability and the need for further data collection and model calibration to reduce uncertainties.
These findings underscore the importance of ongoing monitoring and adaptive management to
optimize future injection operations, in addition to subsea infrastructure planning.
Overall, the findings support regional and national climate goals by providing a viable solution
for mitigating stranded CO2 emissions from major industrial sources along the eastern US
seaboard. The integration of technical, environmental, and stakeholder considerations
throughout the planning process positions the East Coast region as a viable option for future
carbon capture, transport and storage initiatives.

5.3 Lease Block Resource Analysis
Analysis of offshore lease blocks was completed to depict the value of offshore lease blocks for
carbon sequestration developers and leaseholders in the region. The OCSLA provides the U.S.
Department of the Interior (DOI) BOEM and BSEE authorization to regulate the development of

EXPLORE-ACS Final Technical Report 146 March 2026

mineral resources (including hydrocarbon resources) on the OCS. The Infrastructure Investment
and Jobs Act of November 2021 amended the OCSLA and authorized DOI to administer leases,
easements, and rights-of-way on the OCS that “provide for, support, or are directly related to the
injection of a carbon dioxide stream into sub-seabed geologic formations for the purpose of
long-term carbon sequestration.” Under the authority defined in the OCSLA, the DOI BOEM
may permit the use and sequestration of CO2 on existing oil and gas leases on the OCS. As of
fall 2025, BOEM was finalizing their rulemaking for carbon sequestration in federal waters.
The BOEM OCS leasing process includes four phases: (1) planning and analysis, (2) lease
issuance, (3) site assessment, and (4) construction and operations. Offshore lease blocks on
the Atlantic coast are 3 miles by 3 miles square areas covering 5,760 acres. These blocks are
oriented north-south and are delineated out to the BOEM planning areas 200-300 nautical miles
offshore. BOEM’s oil and gas property appraisal process includes extensive geological,
geophysical, engineering, economic and probabilistic analyses. Blocks are sold to the highest
qualified bidder via a proposed notice of sale, notice of sale, and lease sale. The value of an
offshore lease block for carbon sequestration is uncertain, especially in the offshore Atlantic
region. For reference, a lease sale in the Gulf of America completed in December 2024
awarded a total of 299 leases on tracts covering 1,659,224 acres with total high bids accepted
of $372,460,408, or $1.3 million per lease block.
Currently, there are no remaining oil and gas leases in the Atlantic area. There has been no
offshore oil and gas production along the east coast of the U.S. Two Presidential Memoranda
were issued in January 2025 under Section 12(a) of the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, to
protect all U.S. OCS areas off the East coast from future oil and natural gas leasing. As of fall
2025, a total of 27 offshore wind leases had been issued by BOEM along the east coast in
larger tracts of lease blocks ranging from 46,000-183,000 acres (~8-32 lease blocks). These
offshore wind lease tracts follow the BOEM lease block areas. Large CO2 storage projects along
the east coast would likely cover many lease blocks with a focus on the subsurface similar to oil
& gas lease blocks. Consequently, the lease block is a useful metric to evaluate offshore CO2
resources.
The EXPLORE-ACS study area was analyzed for carbon storage resources in BOEM lease
block areas. This analysis provides information for developers on the potential CO2 storage
mass per lease block in comparison to the value of lease blocks for lease sales in relation to
carbon storage credits and other financial decisions.
The analysis was based on BOEM Atlantic lease block maps and CO2 storage resource
estimates from the Mid-Atlantic Carbon Storage Resource Study (Fukai et al., 2020; Gupta,
2021). Figure 5-1 summarizes statistics for the lease blocks in the EXPLORE-ACS study area.
There are 7,703 lease blocks in the EXPLORE-ACS study area with suitable geology for carbon
sequestration. The mean storage estimate per lease block was 4.6 MMT. Several hundred
lease blocks had storage resources of 8 to 12 MMT per block. Conversely, 30% of the lease
blocks had less than 3 MMT per block storage resource, which may be uneconomic to develop.
The resource estimates include conservative efficiency factors, which assume only a small
portion of the rock pore space may be utilized for CO2 storage. In practice, it would likely be
possible to store much more concentrated mass of CO2 near injection wells. For example,
reservoir simulations for EXPLORE-ACS Indian River Deep scenario indicated 40-50% CO2
saturation near injection wells and the ability to store 510 MMT CO2 in a 126 square mile area
(~14 lease blocks). This would suggest up to 36 MMT CO2 storage per lease block in practice.
With 45Q EPA tax credits at $85 per ton CO2 sequestered, a single lease block may have up to
$3 billion in tax credits. Of course, much of this value would be necessary for exploration,
injection well construction, pipeline, CO2 capture, and CO2 compression.

7.0 Conclusions and Carbon Storage Development
 Options

This section presents key conclusions and development options for carbon storage along the
mid-Atlantic offshore. The project made substantial progress in defining geological factors,
pipeline transport, CO2 storage infrastructure, and stakeholder concerns. Multiple options for
CO2 storage development were also outlined by the project team, since many different
pathways may be necessary to establish CCS in the region.

7.1 Key Conclusions for Carbon Storage along the Mid-Atlantic Offshore
Key conclusions related to geological factors and infrastructure design options for the mid-
Atlantic offshore are listed below. The project team found that geological factors are likely to
have a large impact on the location and CO2 storage system design. Infrastructure design
options were outlined for CO2 pipeline transport and injection systems that emphasize safety
and minimize impacts to the environment and local communities.
Geologic Framework for Carbon Storage
The project team compiled and analyzed geotechnical information for the offshore mid-Atlantic
including seismic survey data, geophysical well logs, rock core tests, and depositional trends
from geological conceptual models. The project team identified three representative CO2
storage scenarios to assess representing the study area:
 1. Great Stone Dome- located 60 miles offshore of New Jersey along the Great Stone
 Dome geological structure where eight exploration wells were drilled. This site was
 considered for a large-scale CO2 storage scenario at 15-20 MMT/year.
 2. Indian River Shallow- located along the Delaware/Maryland shoreline where a well may
 be drilled horizontally to enable offshore carbon storage without an offshore wellpad.
 This site was evaluated for a single source storage scenario of 1-2.5 MMT/year.
 3. Indian River Deep- located 40-50 miles off the Delaware/Maryland coast. This site was
 evaluated as a potential storage site with little well control.
The project team advanced the understanding of geological factors on carbon storage to a more
local level. This work concluded that geological factors have a significant impact on the design
of CO2 storage systems for the offshore Atlantic.
Infrastructure Design Options for Carbon storage
A pre-feasibility assessment of infrastructure design options for developing CO2 storage was led
by Aker, Battelle, and TRC. Overall, the project team determined that offshore CO2 storage is
feasible in the region, and options are available to minimize the impacts of construction onto the
environment and local communities.
 • There are >50 industrial sources in the region with CO2 emissions of 70 MMT CO2 per
 year. Many of the sources are located along the Delaware River and Hudson River
 industrial corridors, where there are few options for CO2 storage. Additional large carbon
 sources in the Midwest may benefit from CO2 storage resources along the Atlantic
 offshore but require regional CO2 pipelines to reach the coast.

EXPLORE-ACS Final Technical Report 152 March 2026

 • Pipeline transport options are present in the region. Following existing natural gas
 pipeline right of ways may provide the most feasible routes for CO2 pipelines.
 • Shoreline offtake points for CO2 pipelines may be a challenge for public acceptance,
 especially during construction. However, the pipelines would be buried and have minimal
 long-term impact on the environment,
 • Subsea well completions, seabed manifolds, and low-impact seismic survey/monitoring
 options are available to minimize the visual and environmental impact of CO2 storage
 infrastructure.
 • The shoreline crossing/offtake point for a CO2 pipeline will be a key part of CO2 storage
 system. There are options to locate pipeline offtakes at areas where transatlantic
 communication cables or offshore wind energy transmission cables are located.

7.2 Development Options for Carbon Storage along the Mid-Atlantic Offshore
Based on the Task 3 and Task 5 results, development options were compiled for CCS along the
mid-Atlantic offshore. The development options also reflect input from the project team meetings
on technical issues and infrastructure. In addition, development options were informed by
several technical advisory group meetings with CCS development companies,
pipeline/midstream industry, energy companies, and other stakeholders.
Table 7-1 summarizes. The CCS development options identified in this project reflect both near-
term, smaller projects that may be completed within several years, along with larger CCS
systems that would require onshore CO2 pipelines, multiple capture facilities, offshore CO2
pipelines, and multiple injection wells. Other options include characterization of deep rock layers
in areas with no test wells.
Establishment of mid-Atlantic offshore carbon storage resources may require 7-10 years of
continued planning and execution to fully develop. However, these resources would support
carbon management options for new and existing industry along the northeast U.S. including
power, refineries, cement plants, data centers, and chemical plants. There are also near- term
options to develop carbon sequestration injection wells in state waters. It would likely require
several different parties to develop larger CCS projects in the region like industrial sources,
capture companies, compression, CO2 pipeline transport, well drilling, and regulatory activities.
The EXPLORE-ACS project was able to identify options for developing CCS along the mid-north
Atlantic offshore that would be technically feasible, economically viable, and minimize impacts
on the environment. These options would help to facilitate public acceptance, but this remains a
concern for development of new infrastructure projects along the east coast. However, many
stakeholders suggested that large infrastructure projects like bridges, tunnels, and ports are not
unusual along the Atlantic Coast and existing industry could accommodate construction
activities necessary to develop CCS. In addition, CCS infrastructure would have minimal
visibility after initial construction, which appears to be a major impediment to many offshore
projects like wind energy.

The project benefited from several Technical Advisory Committee and project team meetings to
review options for CS in the region. Key items identified in these meetings for successful
development of carbon sequestration projects along the offshore Atlantic are listed below.
 • Stratigraphic Test Wells- The members of the team meeting were unanimous in
 asserting how important stratigraphic test wells are to confirm geotechnical properties,
 caprock integrity, and geological trends. These wells are especially important in areas
 without well controls 15-30 miles offshore where CS projects are most likely to be sited.
 • Scalable CS systems- Having options to develop scalable carbon sequestration
 systems ranging from 0.2-20 MMT CO2/year is important to providing flexibility in
 deployment, building confidence in offshore storage.
 • Near-term and Long-term Carbon Sequestration Options- Realistically, smaller CS
 systems are more likely to be successfully developed in the near-term (<5 years). The
 Indian River Shallow site provides options for near-term project up to 2.5 MMT/year with
 a short pipeline, onshore drill site, and storage footprint in state waters. Larger, long-
 term carbon sequestration systems may piggy-back off these early projects.
 • Outreach with Key Stakeholders- Addressing social concerns in early stages is vital to
 ensuring project success. Keys to project success include establishing a local presence,
 local business engagement, boots on the ground community engagement, and clear
 benefits for local communities.
 • Regulation clarity- Projects would benefit from federal and state clarity on regulations
 related to offshore CS. An integrated approach to permitting would be beneficial to
 balancing timelines. Early contact with permitting agencies would be critical to ensuring
 projects meet state and federal requirements.
 • Permit Coordination- An offshore carbon storage project will require streamlining and
 integration of permitting activities and schedules for pipelines, environmental monitoring,
 CO2 storage, early pore space access strategy, and other construction. Another
 coordination aspect of CCS is having carbon capture and storage timelines aligned.
 • Establishing Confidence in CCS Operations- Field projects need to validate
 geological models, validate reservoir simulations with monitoring data, demonstrate
 flow/pressure controls, establish release mitigation measures, and confirm safety
 practices. Other validation items include seismic interpretation calibration to well data,
 depositional model assumptions, and seals/containment characterization.
 • Cost Certainty and Economic Factors for CS Project Development- Additional
 certainty in project costs, economic factors, and financing options are necessary to
 substantiate carbon storage development feasibility along the mid-Atlantic offshore. Cost
 modeling STW, seismic, Economic analysis for scenarios, cost benefit analysis.
Figure 7-1 shows a potential timeline for Atlantic offshore carbon storage development. Initial
efforts would focus on permitting and outreach to lay the foundation for exploration activities like
seismic surveys and stratigraphic test well drilling. This would be followed by baseline
environmental monitoring and planning for injection field construction. CO2 injection operations
may start in 7-10 years under an ambitious schedule.
A timeline duration of 7-10 years is similar to other offshore energy projects. Much of the
timeline is contingent upon clear federal and state rules for offshore carbon sequestration. Lead
times for materials and equipment for system construction work are 18-24 months, so
procurement would need to be coordinated during project development. Much of this equipment
would need to be sourced from companies in the Gulf Coast region.
While this is a longer timeline than smaller onshore CCS projects, the time would allow
continued stakeholder informational and education to gradually introduce the concept of carbon

sequestration to the northeast U.S. There is also value in continued development of CS
resources in the region to provide carbon management options for new industry development in
the region, as there are no other carbon storage options on the order of 100s of gigatons in the
northeast U.S. The mid-Atlantic offshore carbon storage systems outlined in this project would
provide certainty on carbon management options for large, long-term industrial investments in
the northeast U.S.

Early adopters may benefit from several aspects of offshore carbon sequestration along the
mid-Atlantic outer continental shelf.
 • Access to preferred lease blocks with the highest CO2 storage resources and lowest
 risks for project development.
 • Potential ability to repurpose strategic gas pipeline right of ways for CO2 transport.
 • Access to CO2 storage field sites located nearest to sources and offtake locations.
 • Opportunities to shape policy for CO2 storage at state and/or federal levels.
 • Ability to utilize data and results from EXPLORE-ACS project and leverage knowledge
 from other offshore CO2 storage projects in the Gulf Coast region.
There are many risks related to uncertain costs, community opposition to development,
geological variability, and lack of regulation precedents. Therefore a “de-risking” strategy would
aid in addressing project risks as financial investments increase over time during carbon storage
development. Scalable options are also advantageous, where additional injection wells and
pipelines could be added to the CO2 storage system over time.

There are a variety of development options for carbon storage along the offshore mid-Atlantic.
Near term options exist to drill wells at an onshore wellpad, then laterally offshore into state
waters. These options would also require less pipeline to transport CO2 from a source to the
injection well. Offshore injection system construction in federal waters would require more
infrastructure and time, but these options would benefit from economies of scale since large
injection fields may be feasible in these areas.
