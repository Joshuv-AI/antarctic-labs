Executive Summary
The program is a multi-institutional effort led by the University of Wisconsin-Madison Engine Research
Center, partnered with WM International and the University of Central Florida, aiming to revolutionize
combustion technology for medium-duty commercial vehicles. The project’s primary objective is to
develop a high-efficiency mixing controlled compression ignition (MCCI) combustion strategy utilizing
propane and propane/DME blends. This development is crucial because current propane-fueled spark-
ignited (SI) engines operate at substantially lower efficiencies (BTE of 31.5%) than comparable modern
diesel engines (37.1%). The program goal is to achieve an efficiency greater than 37.1% and enable a
15% reduction in CO2 emissions compared to current diesel engines, along with a greater than 5%
reduction in total cost of ownership.
The core technical challenge involves overcoming propane’s low cetane number, which resists auto-
ignition, especially at low loads, while simultaneously developing a robust fuel system capable of
handling these liquefied gaseous fuels at extremely high injection pressures. The project addressed this
by coupling engine modeling, fundamental chemical kinetics studies, and hardware development. A
high-pressure propane/DME fuel system, featuring an improved dual pump architecture, was
successfully developed, fabricated, and installed in the test cell, demonstrating operation at injection
pressures exceeding 1100 bar, which is necessary for effective mixing controlled combustion.
Furthermore, high-flow nozzles were specifically designed and calibrated for the direct-injection system.
To address the challenge of achieving stable ignition on a low cetane fuel like propane under low-load
conditions, Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) optimization identified an innovative solution: exhaust
rebreathing. This strategy uses a custom-designed camshaft to re-open the exhaust valve during the
intake stroke, trapping hot residual gases to preheat the charge. Simulations demonstrated that this
mechanism, combined with an optimized injection system, enables stable MCCI combustion. Unassisted
propane operation required highly elevated intake conditions (180 kPa intake pressure and 90 °C intake
manifold temperature) for stability; however, with the rebreathe cam installed, stable compression
ignition was achieved at typical engine intake conditions (125 kPa intake pressure and 45 °C IMT).
Validated CFD models project that this optimized system achieves an indicated specific fuel
consumption (ISFC) near 180 g/kW-hr, corresponding to a BTE greater than 38% and achieving a 16%
reduction in Brake Specific CO2 (BSCO2) compared to the baseline LPG SI engine. Engine testing using
the final hardware confirmed stable compression ignition operation on neat propane with combustion
phasing similar to conventional diesel fuel.
Complementing the engine development, fundamental research was conducted using a high-pressure
shock tube facility to collect Ignition Delay Times (IDTs) for various propane/DME blends across high
pressures (up to 110 bar) and relevant temperature ranges. These data were used to modify and
validate chemical kinetic mechanisms, resulting in a computationally efficient reduced mechanism of 63
species for use in engineering CFD codes. Additionally, alternative ignition methods, such as ceramic
glow plugs and spark plugs, were tested under energy-assisted compression ignition (EACI) strategies,
both proving effective in enabling split-injection combustion at normal intake temperatures. Economic
analysis suggests that while the MCCI powertrain hardware costs approximately $3,300 more than an SI
engine, the projected fuel savings due to efficiency gains result in a quick payback period of roughly 1.5
years (about 1500 hours).

1. Introduction
The program, a collaboration led by the University of Wisconsin-Madison Engine Research Center with
partners including WM International and the University of Central Florida, is developing an ultra-low
CO2 engine architecture for medium-duty commercial vehicles. The project addresses the significant
performance gap between existing low-carbon fuels and conventional diesel technology. While propane
and dimethyl ether (DME) offer inherent greenhouse gas (GHG) reduction potential due to their lower
carbon content, current state-of-the-art propane-fueled engines rely on spark-ignited (SI) combustion,
achieving a Brake Thermal Efficiency (BTE) of 31.5%. This is substantially below a comparable modern
diesel engine (~37.1%). The core objectives are to develop a Mixing Controlled Compression Ignition
(MCCI) combustion strategy capable of achieving a BTE greater than 37.1%, thus enabling a 15%
reduction in CO2 emissions compared to current diesel engines, and achieving a greater than 5%
reduction in total cost of ownership.
Achieving stable MCCI operation using low cetane fuels like propane introduces two primary technical
challenges:
 1) the design of a suitable high-pressure direct-injection (DI) fuel system capable of operating at
 injection pressures needed for high load MCCI combustion;
 2) ensuring stable auto-ignition, especially at low-load conditions where propane is resistant to
 ignition, leading to potential misfire.
The program addresses these through a coupled approach integrating hardware development, engine
experiments, and extensive computational optimization. Approaches considered to boost ignition
include glow plugs, spark plugs, and exhaust rebreathing to trap hot residuals within the cylinder.
Fundamental ignition delay measurements collected via high-pressure shock tube experiments are used
to validate and refine chemical kinetic models, which are then integrated into Computational Fluid
Dynamics (CFD) studies to optimize the combustion chamber and injection hardware for robust MCCI
operation across a range of propane/DME blends.
Significant achievements have been realized in developing the specialized hardware and
computationally optimizing the combustion process. A high-pressure propane/DME fuel system,
featuring an improved dual pump architecture, was successfully developed, fabricated, and installed,
demonstrating injection pressures above 1100 bar necessary for mixing controlled combustion. High-
flow nozzles were also designed and calibrated for the direct-injection system.
To manage propane’s low reactivity, particularly at low-load conditions, the project successfully
developed and installed an exhaust rebreathing camshaft, which re-opens the exhaust valve during the
intake stroke to trap hot residual gases. Unassisted propane operation required highly elevated intake
conditions (180 kPa intake pressure and 90 °C intake manifold temperature) for stability; in contrast, the
system with the rebreathe cam achieved stable compression ignition on neat propane at normal intake
conditions (125 kPa intake pressure and 45 °C intake manifold temperature), with combustion phasing
similar to conventional diesel fuel. Simulation results, combining the understanding from CFD
simulation, metal engine experiments, and optical engine experiments, demonstrated that the
optimized combustion system with exhaust rebreathing enables stable MCCI combustion, with an
estimated BTE greater than 38%, resulting in a 16% reduction in Brake Specific CO2 (BSCO2) compared
to the baseline LPG SI engine, exceeding the program’s targeted 15% reduction goal.
Additionally, high-pressure shock tube experiments measured Ignition Delay Times (IDTs) for various
propane/DME mixtures up to 110 bar, providing crucial data used to improve chemical kinetics models

 7

and develop a computationally efficient reduced mechanism of 63 species. Alternative Energy-Assisted
Compression Ignition (EACI) using either glow plugs or spark plugs was also tested and found to allow
stable split-injection combustion at normal intake temperatures. Economically, analyses show that
despite the estimated increase in hardware cost of approximately $3,300 over SI engines, the efficiency-
based fuel savings are expected to yield a rapid payback period of roughly 1.5 years (approximately 1500
hours).

3. Technical Discussion
The program was executed in five tasks. Detailed findings for each Task are discussed below.

Fuel System Development (Task 1)
A fuel system bench was constructed to support engine testing on a Cummins 6.7 L research engine
platform with propane-DME fuel mixtures. The fuel system bench work comprised: (1) bench hardware
including pumps, heat exchangers, regulators; (2) controls to start, operate at specified pressures, and
safely shutdown; and, (3) a rework of the fuel injector to provide a new nozzle to accommodate higher
injection flow rates required in the use of these fuels. Tasks 1 and 2 were accomplished in Year 1, and
Task 3 was completed in Year 2 of the project.
Fuel Bench. Fuel supply hardware shown in Figure 1 incorporated a fuel tank, a delivery fuel pump (DFP),
a high-pressure fuel pump (HPFP), pressure regulators, monitoring pressure sensors, and flow control
valves. The system was designed to evacuate the fuel after shutdown back to the fuel tank via a
dedicated pump and bypass valves. The fuel tank supply and return were secured with electronically
controlled valves to ensure the system was isolated after use. An oil lubrication circuit was used to
provide lubrication to the crank and roller followers of the HPFP.
The bench featured equipment to measure the rates of injection, that is the injected flow in mm3/ms
per command pulse to the injector. The equipment was used to calibrate injectors across the range of
operation and provide UWM a table of injector pulse width as a function of desired flow and set
pressure at the common rail.

 Figure 1 Fuel bench schematic and build.

Control System. The control system used in the fuel bench served three main functions: (1) provide
initial air evacuation ahead of introducing the Propane/DME fuel mixture; (2) provide a safe startup, run,
shut-down, and evacuation sequence; and, (3) provide control over the high-pressure common rail.
The control system was housed on a secure enclosure mounted to the fuel bench and provided
communication with the UWM engine controller and lab system via CAN. WMI provided a
communication protocol to monitor the bench data and diagnostics via a GUI interface.
Rail pressure control was provided via control over the HPFP metering valve. The bench functions as a
‘return-less’ system, where the flow through the pump matches the flow required by the injector.

Propane-DME Model Development (Task 2)
This research was conducted at the University of Central Florida (UCF)'s High-Pressure Extended Range
Shock Tube for Advanced Research (HiPER-STAR) facility [ 1, 2]. The facility is capable of conducting high-
pressure experiments, reaching pressures of up to 1000 atm. Generally, shock tubes are considered
ideal devices for replicating combustion test conditions (temperature, T5, and pressure, P5 in shock tube
reference) in a controlled environment. Some of our previous works provided details on shock tube
utilization for IDT measurements [3,4,5]. PCB (113B26) piezoelectric pressure transducers record the
pressure traces during the experiments. The test location is 1 cm away from the end wall in the test
section of the shock tube. Six equidistantly spaced pressure transducers are connected to Agilent
53220A time interval counters on the driven side. The arrival of the incident shock triggers these timer
counters, and the time difference is used to calculate shock velocity. The uncertainty of IDTs was
calculated using equation 1, [6,7]. Where the temperature uncertainty was derived from the T5/T1 shock
equation for the lowest temperature and highest temperature for each mixture, and averaged out. The
pressure uncertainty was set to 3% and the shock uncertainty was from the velocity measured by the
timer counters, which is influenced by temperature [1].

experimental time-zero, considered when the core of the reflected shock passes by the test section, to
the time when OH* attains a peak value (τ in Figure 13) [11].
Shock tube experiments assume a homogeneous, distributed, quiescent gas behind the reflected shock,
with test times on the order of a few milliseconds. This will enable the direct comparison of
experimental data from shock tube experiments with kinetic model predictions, assuming constant
internal energy and volume. The experiments are during the microsecond time scale; therefore, the heat
loss process is negligible, which justifies the adiabatic assumption for modeling. The chemical kinetic
mechanisms used in this work are Aramco3.0 [11], NUIG [12], and Dames et al. [13]. Most of the
analysis in this work was carried out with the Dames et al. [13] model, as it is well-validated for DME-
propane mixtures for pressures up to 20 bar.

delay times. With higher pressures, ignition is more rapid for a 100% propane mixture. The experiments
do not show significant pressure dependency for neat DME at the conditions studied. For the 60/40

ignition occurred at lower temperatures. As mentioned earlier, DME and propane complement each
other very well, allowing for a broad temperature range between 754 and 1022K at 60 and 80 bar—a
much wider range for testing compared to the neat mixtures.

A sensitivity analysis was conducted using the method described by Dames et al. [13] model shown in
Figure 16 to understand the discrepancies between the model and experiments. A sensitivity analysis
was conducted for the high-temperature case (1057 K and 89 atm), which exhibited the maximum
deviation from experiments. The top 20 reactions at the time when 50 % of DME was consumed are
shown in Figure 8. The most sensitive reaction is H2O2 (+M) = 2OH (+M). This reaction is well-studied in
the literature, and its rates are well-established. However, the second most sensitive reaction was
CH3OCH3 + HO2 = CH3OCH2 + H2O2, which is the H-abstraction from DME by the HO2 radical. This is
because the mixture under investigation contains 50% propane in the fuel, and propane undergoes H-

 22

abstraction at high temperatures to produce C3H7 radicals. The C3H7 produced then reacts with
oxygen, forming HO2 radicals. The larger amount of HO2 in the reaction pool makes this H-abstraction
by HO2 one of the most sensitive reactions for the DME/propane mixture. The rate constant for this
reaction is from the theoretical work by Mendes et al. [14] and has an estimated uncertainty factor of
2.5. Other sensitive reactions include H-abstraction by OH and O2. These rates were estimated by
Curran and Fisher [15] and are prone to significant uncertainties. The IDT for this mixture is also
sensitive to a few pressure-dependent reactions of DME intermediates, such as CH2OCH2O2H + O2 =
O2CH2OCH2O2H and CH3OCH2 + O2 = CH3OCH2O2. Both rates are from Burke et al. [16], which utilized
many assumptions to derive these rates using the theoretical calculations from Yamada et al. [17] and
the high-pressure rate from Li et al. [18].
To understand the effect of sensitive reactions, the reaction CH3OCH2 + HO2 = CH3OCH2 + H2O2 was
modified within its uncertainty, and the A-factor was multiplied by 2.5 times. The resulting IDT
predictions are shown in Figures 9 and 10. The agreement has improved considerably, indicating that
further analysis is required for the sensitive reactions outlined in this work. Figure 17, shows the
comparisons of the experiments with the modified Dames et al. mechanism.

Reduced Mechanism
A model reduction was applied using the modified Dames mechanism, which has 120 species. The
method used for model reduction was a directed relation graph with error propagation (DRGEP), DRG
with path flux analysis (DRGPFA), both DRGEP and DRGPFA with sensitivity analysis, and a full-scale
sensitivity analysis (FSSA) was applied at the end. All methods had the same targets selected: CO, CO2,
OH, H2O, and temperature, and they were all important in the combustion of DME/C3H8. For the
targeted species, the absolute tolerance was set at 0.1, and the relative tolerance was set to 10%. For
temperature, the relative tolerance was set to 200 K, while the relative tolerance was 20%. For all
tolerances, the values of the produced skeletal mechanism were compared to the profile of the detailed
mechanism. This allowed for a total comparison of differences in values over time till ignition. For the
selected species, these included the targeted species, as well as DME and C3H8. The conditions for the
model reduction are listed in Table 6.

Chemkin-Pro allows multiple operations to be run back-to-back, and in the reduction results, target
tolerances between the original mechanism and skeletal mechanism are given. At the beginning of the
reduction, DRGEP gave a reduced mechanism with the least differences in target tolerances. From here
on, multiple iterations of DRGEP and DRGPFA were conducted together. After completion, all reduced
mechanisms from both methods were compared, and the one with the least target tolerance was
chosen. Then, this process was repeated multiple times, as the reduced mechanism was reduced by one
or two species each time. When the reduced mechanism reached 68 species, iterations of DRGEP and
DRGFA + sensitivity were conducted. At 65 species, FSSA was conducted to reach 63 species.
The DRGPFA method differs from other model-reduction methods in one crucial way. This is because the
DRGPFA is based on multi-generational flux analysis, whereas the DRG and DRGEP only use one-
generational direct flux/reaction rates [25]. Before flux analysis can be applied, a directed graph is first
created, which can also be represented as a matrix. Each node in the directed graph represents a
species, and if a reaction connects two species, a line between those nodes represents that reaction.
Species that are important for the combustion process are more internally woven and are in the center
of the graph node. In contrast, less critical species can be spotted around the edges of the directed
graph. Then, a random edge node is selected, and its interaction coefficient is found using path flux
analysis. Flux is mole per time in its chemical path. The interaction coefficient represents the production
and consumption of one target species, A, by another species, B, which is defined by the selected edge
node. These values are used to find the first two-generational reaction coefficients. Then, using the two
first-generation production and computational interaction coefficients, two additional consumption and
production coefficients are found by incorporating a third reactant. These are referred to as the second-
generation interaction coefficient for production and consumption. The four interaction coefficients are
used to get one threshold value, which is used to determine if a species should be removed or not [33].
A DRGPFA + sensitivity analysis conducts a DRGPFA and then a sensitivity analysis, addressing an issue
from the DRG, DRGEP, and DRGPFA. After DRGPFA, for example, Chemkin-Pro provides a target
tolerance/error; however, the influence of this error on each component in a specific numerical feature
cannot be directly obtained, which results in unimportant species and their related reactions remaining
in the mechanism. However, a DRGPFA is used to remove these species. This is achieved by having a
species A and determining its coefficient of species, which is defined as its error on target parameters,
such as the temperature between the original and reduced mechanisms. [26]. The mechanism was
reduced to 63 species.

High-pressure fuel system

Due to the unique properties of propane and DME relative to traditional compression-ignition fuels, a
custom high-pressure fuel system was developed for this work. The fuel pump is a pneumatically driven
liquid pump (Haskel DSTV-150). The stock fluoro-rubber (VitonTM/FKM) seals used in the wetted section
were replaced with ethylene propylene rubber (EPR) seals to ensure compatibility with DME. An air
amplifier system (HighPressure Technologies AS-SPLV2-10GH) boosts compressed building air from 90-
100 psig to 180 psig and feeds the pump. The output fuel pressure is PID controlled by a digital pressure
regulator (Proportion Air PSR4PN). Since propane and DME are liquefied gases, bleeding the fuel system
safely was an important consideration when designing the fuel system. Fortunately, the building exhaust
system was easily accessible, and excess fuel was routed into the building exhaust as they both have low
inhalation toxicities [28,29]and do not act as GHGs.
Figure 32 shows a schematic of the fuel system. The optical engine propane-DME fuel system utilizes a
dead-headed design where low-pressure fuel does not recirculate back to the tank from the injector or
common rail. This reduces the complexity of a liquefied gas fuel system because there is no longer a
requirement for back-pressure regulated return lines to the tank to keep the fuel liquefied.

Technical Details – Energy-Assisted Compression-Ignition Experiments
High speed pressure and simultaneous schlieren and OH* chemiluminescence imaging were acquired
using propane, DME, and propane-DME blends at over a range of conventional direct-injection
compression-ignition (DICI) and energy-assisted compression ignition (EACI) operating conditions. The
DICI experiments focused on both single injection and double injection strategies using propane and
propane-DME blends of 10%, 20%, and 30% DME by mass, hereafter referred to as 90-10, 80-20, and 70-
30, respectively. The single-injection results for an intake temperature sweep were used to establish the
intake conditions needed for the double injection experiments. The single-injection results showed that
an intake temperature of at least 180 °C was required for pure propane to obtain complete combustion.
For double-injection experiments, results indicated that pilot heat release varied significantly with DME
blend percentage and pilot timing, but the ideal pilot timing remained the same between the blends.
The EACI experiments assessed the impacts of DME blend percentage, intake temperature, global
equivalence ratio, and in-cylinder density on split-injection EACI operation. Results indicated that for the
intake conditions investigated, intake temperature has minimal impact on EACI engine operation with a
glow plug as the ignition assistant. However, increased global equivalence ratio significantly improved
combustion efficiencies. The impacts of in-cylinder density and global equivalence ratio were further
investigated using a spark plug as an ignition assistant for EACI combustion. Results indicated that
increased global equivalence ratios allow for increased jet-to-jet propagation, due to the increased local
equivalence ratios, allowing for improved performance at high in-cylinder densities. Suggesting that
global equivalence ratio may play a significant role in split-injection EACI operation.
Experimental details

Ignition assistants

For the EACI experiments, two COTS ignition assistants were investigated: a COTS ceramic glow plug
(Beru CGP003) and a COTS resistor spark plug (Brisk MR12L Silver). A previous investigation has shown
comparable EACI performances between the two different ignition assistant devices albeit with higher

cycle-to-cycle variability for the spark plug. The in-cylinder location relative to the assisted fuel jet is
shown in Figure 42.

The COTS ceramic glow plug was operated at a constant input power of 75 W, which was controlled by a
DC power supply (BK-Precision). The input power selected is the highest input power achievable with
this glow plug while maintaining a reasonable operation lifetime and achieves a surface temperature of
~1450 K under motored operation at 1200 RPM. The COTS resistor spark plug was coupled with a smart
inductive spark coil (AEM 30-2853) and was sparked only once per cycle. A previous study found that a
spark timing command of 2 degrees before the start-of-injection (SOI) for the pilot injection resulted in
the best EACI split-injection operation.
Results – Reacting measurements

Experimental results are presented in two sections, reflecting the differing operating strategies. The first
section examines how DME blend percentage and pilot-injection timing influence conventional DICI
operation, in which ignition is governed solely by fuel chemistry. The second section evaluates how DME
blend percentage, intake temperature, global equivalence ratio, and in-cylinder density affect split-
injection EACI operation. In this mode, local energy deposition from an ignition assistant is used to ignite
one or more of the directly injected fuel jets, enabling the use of low-reactivity fuels in compression-
ignition engines under challenging in-cylinder thermodynamic conditions.

injections. The single injection experiments included variations in injection timing, intake temperature,
and fuel energy. For the double-injection experiments intake conditions were held constant for all runs
at the same conditions used for the single-injection fuel energy sweep.

Results for ignition delay for the intake temperature sweep were calculated based both on the
measured in-cylinder pressure and using the OH* chemiluminescence emission. The start-of-combustion
from pressure (SOC) was calculated using the method developed by Groendyk [34] which selects SOC as
the crank-angle of the minimum integrated difference between the average motored pressure and the
fired-cycle pressure. This method is sensitive and can detect slight changes in pressure due to the slow
heat release of more advanced, premixed pilot injections. Figure 44 shows the ignition delay results
versus intake temperature. For the propane-DME blends, the ignition delay decreases significantly with
increasing intake temperature. At the highest intake temperature of 200 °C, ignition delay for the 70-30
and 80-20 blends approaches 1 ms, which is starting to approach the values typical for diesel
combustion.
The ignition delay results for DME shown in Figure 44 have almost a constant ignition delay irrespective
of the intake temperature. This is likely due to the chemical delay making up only a small portion of the
overall delay which is dominated by the physical delays associated with the fuel injection and mixing
process. This contrasts with the results for propane and the propane blends which are controlled mainly
by the chemical kinetics of the ignition process.

As show in Figure 61, the glow plug and spark plug assisted ignition approaches had very similar

probe was developed to compare the performance of laser-assisted ignition to the previously studied

Nd:YAG laser was focused with a custom probe (Designed by Seamus Doyle and Prof. David Rothamer),
approximately the same size as the ceramic glow plug, to breakdown the fuel-air mixture in-cylinder.
The probe focuses the laser beam with an anti-reflection coated molded aspheric lens (LightPath
354996) with a 4.5 mm focal length and a theoretical working distance of 3.5-mm to generate the laser-

positions at a pulse repetition rate of 6 kHz (1 pulse every 1.2 CAD at an engine speed of 1200 RPM). A
removeable head insert was designed to position the point of laser breakdown at approximately the
same position as the spark plug’s gap shown as shown in Figure 42.
Due to the laser not being synched to engine crank angle, this resulted in inconsistent timing of the laser
pulses relative to the injection process of the pilot injection, causing high cyclic variability in the
combustion process. Despite these troubles, this was largely a proof-of-concept exercise, the ability to
use laser assisted ignition as an alternative ignition assistance approach for split-injection EACI operation
was demonstrated.

Combustion Optimization (Tasks 4)
Introduction, Objectives, and Modeling Approach
The objective of this program is to develop a high-efficiency Mixing Controlled Compression Ignition
(MCCI) combustion strategy utilizing propane and propane/DME blends. This development is crucial for
transitioning medium-duty commercial engines to lower-carbon fuels while achieving a Brake Thermal
Efficiency (BTE) greater than 37.1% and enabling a 15% reduction in CO2 emissions compared to current
diesel engines. The computational fluid dynamics (CFD) effort was tasked with computationally
optimizing the combustion system hardware specifications, including the fuel injector nozzle and the
piston bowl geometry, to ensure robust and stable MCCI combustion of neat propane across key engine
operating conditions.

Model Setup and Validation
The CONVERGE commercial CFD software [36] version 3.0.27 was used to perform the sector
compression ignition (CI) simulations. One partial engine cycle was simulated using the SAGE
combustion model with detailed chemistry and automated mesh refinement (AMR). The simulations
were run from intake valve closure (IVC) to exhaust valve opening (EVO). Propane chemical kinetics
were simulated using the 63 species, 393 reaction mechanism being developed as part of the project by
the University of Central Florida (UCF) team. The 4 species, 12 reaction mechanism of Kong et al. [37]
was added for NOx emissions predictions. The well-established Lagrangian drop – Eulerian fluid (LDEF)
approach was used to model the fuel sprays. Best practices recommended model settings are used.
The Hiroyasu-NSC model is used for soot predictions. Acetylene (C2H2) was used as the soot formation
species.
The rate of injection (ROI) was based on the measurements of the WM International team, as part of the
project. The initial conditions were calculated based on the experimental engine data taken by the
University of Wisconsin, Engine Research Center (ERC) team, along with 0-D calculations. The base cell
size is 1mm. The grid contain approximately 140,000 cells at IVC. Temperature AMR is used with a
minimum cell size of 0.25mm. Velocity AMR has a minimum cell size of 0.125mm. A 0.25mm embedded
cone grid is used at the fuel injection nozzle. Table 17 shows the temperature boundary conditions.

injection system specifications. The previously validated closed cycle sector CFD model was used.
One of the main objectives of the project is to develop robust strategies for mixing controlled
compression ignition (MCCI) combustion of propane. The base measured engine data and model results

approach to achieve stable combustion with short ignition delay times. Although mixture confinement

avoids the near TDC injection and mixing challenges posed with narrow IA nozzles.

4. Conclusion and Summary
The successful completion of this program marks a significant advancement in the development of ultra-
low CO2 engine architectures for medium-duty commercial vehicles. By transitioning from conventional
spark-ignited propane combustion to a high-efficiency Mixing Controlled Compression Ignition (MCCI)
strategy, the project has successfully addressed the efficiency gap that previously limited the
environmental benefits of propane and dimethyl ether (DME). The multi-institutional effort led by the
University of Wisconsin-Madison has effectively demonstrated that a low-carbon fuel like propane can
meet and exceed the performance standards of modern diesel engines while significantly reducing
greenhouse gas emissions.
The project utilized an integrated approach combining fundamental research, computational
optimization, and hardware innovation. Fundamental chemical kinetics studies provided crucial ignition
delay data for propane/DME blends at pressures up to 110 bar, which facilitated the creation of a
computationally efficient 63-species reduced mechanism for engine modeling. Simultaneously, the
development of a high-pressure fuel system capable of exceeding 1100 bar injection pressure provided
the necessary hardware foundation for mixing controlled combustion.
The key technical accomplishment was overcoming the low reactivity of propane, which traditionally
resists auto-ignition. The implementation of an exhaust rebreathing strategy—enabled by a custom-
designed rebreathe camshaft—successfully utilized hot residual gases to achieve stable compression
ignition at normal intake conditions. This innovation eliminated the need for the extreme intake heating
previously required for unassisted propane operation. Furthermore, alternatives such as glow plug and
spark-assisted ignition were proven effective, offering additional flexibility for managing ignition under
challenging low-load conditions and cold start.
The final performance evaluations and GT-Power modeling confirmed that the program met its core
objectives. The optimized system achieved a Brake Thermal Efficiency (BTE) greater than 38%,
surpassing the target for diesel-like efficiency (37.1%). The technology projected a 16% reduction in
Brake Specific CO2 (BSCO2) compared to baseline LPG spark-ignited engines, exceeding the program's
15% reduction goal.
Economically, the propane MCCI architecture represents a compelling value proposition for commercial
fleet operators. While the estimated initial hardware costs are approximately $3,300 higher than a
spark-ignited engine, the substantial fuel savings result in a rapid payback period of roughly 1.5 years.
Long-term analysis suggests a Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) improvement of 13% over a ten-year
duration. In summary, this program has established a robust, computationally validated, and
experimentally proven pathway for high-efficiency, low-carbon propane power in the medium-duty
transport sector.
