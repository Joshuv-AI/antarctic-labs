Executive Summary

This report summarizes Phase 1 of a project involving the design of a Floating Hydrogen
Production and Dispensing Barge destined for the Port of San Francisco (SF). The H2 Barge
is designed to produce renewable H2 at the rate of ~ 530 kg/day, storing 512 kg of
hydrogen at 517-bar, allowing fast refueling of hydrogen fuel cell vessels and land-side
hydrogen delivery trailers for distribution into the nascent SF hydrogen ecosystem. The
broader considerations that impacted the H2 Barge design are also described. An account
is given of a new review process formulated by the United States Coast Guard (USCG) to
review this first-of-its-kind maritime implementation of hydrogen technology. The
immediate goals of the H2 Barge Project are to 1) demonstrate the feasibility, viability and
methods of hydrogen production, storage and fueling in a maritime context, 2) help shape
(where needed) and navigate the required local, state and federal regulatory gauntlet and
3) catalyze a “green hydrogen ecosystem” (both marine and landside) with locally
produced renewable hydrogen at the San Francisco waterfront.

A summary is also given of the modeling and experimental activity of Phase 1 directed to
the development of science-based refueling protocols for large marine Type IV 250-bar
hydrogen tanks. Combined modeling and experimental studies are reported of the filling
of large (28 kg) 250-bar Type IV hydrogen tanks of the type being deployed on early
hydrogen ferries, such as the MV Sea Change. The primary question was to determine
how such tanks can be successfully filled (state of charge greater than 97%) within 45
minutes without exceeding the 82 °C temperature limit historically set for such tanks. The
studies show that a gas injector is needed avoid thermal stratification during filling which
can result in potential hot spots. Pre-cooling of the hydrogen was found to be essential in
most cases, as ambient conditions greatly affect the need for a pre-cooling to achieve the
45-minute fill time. Pre-cooling cannot be supplied by nearby water, such as that found in
nature (bays, lakes, rivers, etc.) because pre-cooling cooling below 0 °C was found to be
necessary to avoid excessive compression heating. The experimental results afforded a
calibration of the engineering model (SOFIL) for these large 250-bar tanks, which now
enables using SOFIL to predict volume-averaged hydrogen filling temperatures to an
accuracy of +/- 2.7°C for these tanks. The model can therefore be used to evaluate
potential scenarios for development of a standardized fueling methodology for ferries
utilizing large Type-IV tanks of the type examined here.

1. Background:

 After successfully completing the feasibility study of SF-BREEZE ferry and subsequently
 building the Water-Go-Round Hydrogen Fuel Cell Ferry (which was renamed as Sea
 Change) in San Francisco, the necessity was felt for having focused H2 generation and
 refueling infrastructure on the waterfront to refuel the hydrogen ferries in San Francisco.
 The urgent need was also supported by the progress made by the Nautilus Project, under
 which a hydrogen fuel cell auxiliary power system was being designed for retrofitting on
 Hornblower’s 600 passenger Discover Zero Ferry. The idea was to design, permit, build,
 and operate a forward-looking green hydrogen production and distribution station. At the
 time of the Project’s conception, the use of gaseous hydrogen for propulsion in maritime
 was in its infancy (and remains so as of the date of this report) and, therefore, it was
 proposed that such an infrastructure should be placed temporarily and which could be
 moved if needed to another location. The US-Department of Energy liked the idea and
 awarded a Hornblower-led team a competitive H2@Scale grant to design, build and deploy
 a barge-based hydrogen production, compression, storage and distribution facility at the
 San Francisco Waterfront. This report summarizes Phase I of the project, which included H2
 Barge design, experiments and modeling to support developing science-based refueling
 protocols for 250-bar marine hydrogen tanks, and an initial engagement with the United
 States Coast Guard to develop a regulatory approach to the H2 Barge. The report
 summarizes the land-based site selection and permitting issues associated with the barge
 as well as preliminary activities undertaken to establish a green hydrogen ecosystem in San
 Francisco.

2. Introduction

 Hydrogen is a commodity gas of continuing interest for transportation when used with
 hydrogen energy conversion devices such as internal combustion engines (ICEs) and fuel
 cells1. Hydrogen fuel cells in particular are finding deeper and wider utility in providing zero-
 emissions electrical power due to their higher thermal efficiency (especially at partial load),
 dramatically lower noise, reduced maintenance and associated costs, and emissions-free
 operation at the point of use1. In addition, because fuel cell modules can be easily
 combined together, this “modularity” enables power architectures that can be more
 efficiently matched to the power consumption profile of a given application, improving
 overall energy efficiency.
 Most major auto manufacturers have designs for light-duty fuel-cell vehicles, with several of
 these vehicles now available to the public for sale or lease2. Beyond light-duty vehicles, fuel-
 cell buses have been in service already for many years3, fuel-cell construction lighting has
 been demonstrated4,5, tens of thousands of fuel-cell forklifts are now in routine use6 and the
 first light rail systems using hydrogen fuel cells are now operational7. In the last few years,
 the feasibility of using hydrogen fuel cells to replace marine diesel engines in maritime
 applications has been shown, for example in high-speed ferries8 and oceanographic

3. Project Objectives:

 The project had three overall objectives:
 I. To demonstrate the feasibility and viability of hydrogen production, storage, and fueling
 in a maritime context, establishing robust science-based protocols, procedures, operating
 parameters, and attendant training materials for the safe and routine generation and
 storage of electrolyzed hydrogen, and handling of water-to-water and water-land hydrogen
 and fuel-cell power transfer.

 II. To catalyze a “green hydrogen ecosystem,” via localized production of renewable
 hydrogen at the San Francisco Waterfront, encompassing San Francisco and the
 surrounding Bay Area for both maritime and landside users; and

 III. Most importantly, to establish the solid technical foundation required to sustainably
 continue objectives I and II well past a successful demonstration period.

5. Site Preparation

5.1. Site Selection
 The project organized a Hydrogen Barge (HB) Site Permitting Team in charge of identifying
 and selecting the site for the HB deployment and navigating the regulatory issues. After
 establishing a range of criteria and reviewing a number of potential locations, the Team
 selected Pier 68 (Wharf 3) at the Port of San Francisco (POSF) as the best candidate location.
 This site is shown in Figure 5.1.

 Figure 5.1: Site chosen for the H2 Barge, Wharf 3 at Pier 68 of the Port of San Francisco.

 This site has sufficient power and water utilities for HB operations and is protected from strong
 Bay waves and currents. The site is located within a long-established maritime-focused
 industrial area. It is within a gated compound located at a safe distance from the immediate
 public, which reduces noise and similar concerns. Yet, the site’s location is close enough to
 central San Francisco (in the developing Mission Bay neighborhood) to enable educational
 and public tours and visits. The location offers excellent freeway access for hydrogen trailers
 coming to the site, and the site is generally compatible with the hydrogen trailer refueling
 and vessel refueling activities planned for the HB. The site was deemed well-suited for
 regulatory approval.

 The site assessment confirmed the viability of the location. Water and land access is good.
 Mooring options are available for the barge, which allow for approach by vessels to be
 refueled. The dock is sufficient to accommodate truck access. Sufficient power is also
 available, although the mid-dock transformer is in poor condition, whereas the northernmost
 transformer is in good condition. The better transformer is also located at the preferred
 position on the dock for mooring and vessel approach. The depth of the water at the site was
 sufficient for H2 vessel approach and refueling activities.

6. H2 Barge Design

The first step in the overall HB design was to define the performance attributes. It was decided
the HB would produce approximately 500 kg per day of renewable hydrogen available at 517
bar pressure. Of the 500 kg/day production, 200 kg/day would be dedicated to hydrogen
maritime vessels. The remaining 300 kg/day would be devoted to landside uses (motive or
stationary) for hydrogen ecosystem development. Renewable electricity is available at the Port
of San Francisco from the SF Public Utilities Commission, and that electricity will be used for
water electrolysis and charging the batteries of battery hybrid vessels coming to the HB.

Design work on the HB began with the selection of the type of high-pressure hydrogen storage
tanks holding the ~ 500 kg of hydrogen. After technical review, it was decided to use ~ 520-
bar rated Type III storage tanks. These were chosen because of their excellent track record, the
ability to fuel them faster (due to higher temperature rating), and the absence of a weight
requirement that would motivate going to lighter Type IV hydrogen tanks. To provide
redundancy and good access to all the manifolding hardware, it was decided that the HB
hydrogen storage system would consist of two identical hydrogen storage “pods” (~ 250 kg
each). Also, the hydrogen pods assembled from 25 type III 517-bar rated hydrogen storage
tanks (10.25 kg each) will be enclosed in shipping containers, providing protection against the
maritime environment. After analysis, it was also decided that, in an overall sense, the HB would
be a 517-bar system. The decision was made for a 517-bar pressure rating because this pressure
is more typical of standard hydrogen hardware (tubes, valves, etc.), which would make any repairs
and service more convenient and cost effective.

The HB hydrogen production will be based on the 1.2 MW Nel Hydrogen MC250 water
electrolyzer, which consists of a containerized electrolyzer, with a supporting power system and
HVAC equipment. The electrolyzer can produce 500 kg of hydrogen per day. The dimensions
of these electrolyzer components were given to Glosten for design purposes and to construct a
3D model of the HB. Also required is a compressor. There was analysis given on the choice
between 450-bar-rated and 517-bar-rated compressors. The decision was to settle on a 517-bar
compressor from PDC Machines (Model PDC-13-1250-7500) which can handle the throughput
of 500 kg per day of hydrogen. This compressor technology was also incorporated into the
growing 3D model of the HB. We discussed with equipment manufacturers regarding
marinization needs of their equipment for deployment in San Francisco port. No additional
marinization was needed for the equipment on barge other than the existing marine grade paint

                                                                                    Page 21 of 109

and containerized solution for electrolyzer and storage system. We also explored providing a
containerized solution for compressor to keep it in clean and extend maintenance intervals,
though the compressor doesn’t need any additional marinization for the current project
environment.

The HB will also be delivering electricity to charge the batteries of battery-electric vessels on SF
Bay. It was originally thought that one of the components on the HB would be the 100-kW fuel
cell, called the maritime fuel cell (MarFC) unit deployed at the Scripps Institution of
Oceanography. However, history with the MarFC suggested that this elderly unit would require
refurbishment or replacement in order to be used for HB operations. The decision was made to
design the HB to accommodate the MarFC (or its replacement, TBD) while at the same time
exploring other fuel-cell options. In the meantime, the primary source of electricity for charging
the batteries of battery electric vessels will be a power stanchion that brings renewable 480 VAC
shore power onto the HB.

Bringing the different HB components together in a safe and technically sound way required that
the team identify and capture the overall HB design attributes as well as the various interfaces
(electrical, mechanical, water) between the components and the overall system cooling
requirements. After analysis, at a high-level, the HB will have a notional 160 ft x 40 ft footprint.
The decision was made to have flexible connections which can accommodate tidal variations.
Electrical systems involve 12.4 KV for electrolysis and 480 VAC for barge facilities and charging
systems and other control/monitoring activities. To support both the Site (permitting) and
Technical Teams Moffat & Nichol, an engineering design firm with extensive experience with pier
engineering issues at the Port of SF, was retained. Thus, Sean Hart from Moffat & Nichol joined
the Site and Technical Teams, both to support the Port’s Maritime and Engineering staffs and to
help ensure that the detailed technical requirements for the site and barge are reciprocally
understood and well-coordinated between the Site and Technical Teams.

7.1. Regulatory
7.1.1. US Coast Guard
The USCG is the maritime security, search and rescue, and law enforcement service branch of
the United States Armed Forces. The USCG has ultimate approval authority over the H2 Barge,
from the various offices associated with USCG Headquarters in Washington DC, as well as the
local Sector SF branch. The USCG is involved at the beginning and end of the approval process,
evaluating the design initially, and then inspecting the H2 Barge when it is built. We give an
account of this engagement because it was a direct product of the H2 Barge design including
the hazardous area and setback plans ensuring compliance with NFPA 2. This account hopefully
aids others in pursuing such projects in the future.

Although the USCG has approval jurisdiction, the H2 Barge was unlike any prior project the USCG
had encountered. Nonetheless, the H2 Barge is subject to USCG authority, and they will assess
all elements relevant to the safety and security of the facility, port, and associated waterways
before authorizing its operation within the marine transportation system. However, there are no
existing USCG standards relating to such a floating platform handling gaseous hydrogen.
Furthermore, as there is no existing USCG policy relating to the design review of the H2 Barge,
it was necessary to create a USCG Design Basis Agreement process specifically for the H2 Barge.
Generating that review process took time and engaged many offices within the USCG, including:
the Office of Operating and Environmental Standards (CG-OES), Office of Port and Facility

                                                                                   Page 35 of 109

Compliance (CG-FAC), Office of Design and Engineering Standards (CG-ENG), Marine Safety
Center (CG-MSC), Liquified Gas Carrier National Center of Expertise (LGC NCOE), and the
Captain of the Port (COTP) at Sector San Francisco. All the above offices agreed to cooperatively
assess the safety and security of the H2 Barge design. The COTP is the USCG Sector Commander,
in this case, Sector San Francisco.

The overall USCG review process is geared to minimize safety-related risk for a project which
falls under Coast Guard COTP Authority, but for which regulations governing explicit safety
standards do not exist. The first step is for the USCG to see what is being proposed and give the
project team some initial feedback. Then, the project team, considering USCG comments,
resubmits the description for deeper USCG review along with a proposal for which standards the
project will adhere to. The USCG reviews how the hardware is designed, how it will be operated
from a safety perspective, and the suitability of the proposed standards. The USCG then issues
a statement outlining any safety concerns raised in the design and operations reviews.
Additionally, the USCG will comment on the suitability of the standards in terms of minimizing
risk. The project then submits the final design that addresses concerns and is compliant with
indicated standards, the USCG reviews the plans, and if they are following the standards, a letter
of acceptance is issued which allows the people to start building. This is all the “upfront”
review. When the project is built, the USCG also would supervise certification testing, and
inspect the project before approving entry into service.

Even before the definition of a regulatory pathway, a basic question needed to be answered by
the USCG: would they review the H2 Barge as a “vessel” or as a “facility?” The H2 Barge does
not have a propulsion system but it is floating on the water, fixed to a pier and produces, stores
and dispenses hydrogen. After a comprehensive review of the regulations, and several
discussions with various USCG personnel, in May of 2022 the project team submitted a Design
Basis Summary document to the USCG Office of Commercial Vessel Compliance (CG-CVC). The
Design Basis Summary described the project background, purpose, general description
(equipment, dimensions, etc.), the mooring arrangement, a concept of operations, and a
regulatory applicability matrix. The team proposed that the H2 Barge be classified as a
‘designated waterfront facility’ as defined in 33 CFR Chapter I, Subchapter L, Part 126, and not
as a “vessel.”

The USCG (including the seven offices) conducted a comprehensive review of the design
summary and the applicable laws and regulations and in May of 2023 (a year later) sent a letter

                                                                                    Page 36 of 109

to the project team that they agreed that the project would be designated as a “waterfront
facility” and would be subject to USCG authority and jurisdiction in accordance with the Ports
and Waterways Safety Act (PWSA), 46 U.S.C. §§ 70001-70036, and the regulations promulgated
thereunder in 33 CFR part 160, including authority in 33 CFR § 160.109. Confirmation was
received from the USCG that such a designation as a waterfront facility would not prevent future
towing of the H2 Barge in the event it needed to be repositioned, although such a move would
not be routine.

8.1. Introduction
8.1.1. Description & Scope
 The floating hydrogen barge aims to address the logistical challenges of hydrogen by
dispensing on-site generated hydrogen to fuel cell powered ferries. A key technical challenge
for refueling these first-generation marine vessels is the absence of a standardized refueling
protocol for ferries that store hydrogen at 250-bar.

SWITCH Maritime recently completed a demonstration project in the Bay Area using the flagship
Sea Change Ferry12. As per the report, the Sea Change Ferry stores 246 kg of hydrogen in ten
Type-IV tanks located on the top deck of the ferry - 8 x Hexagon Magnum 26’’ x 225’’ + 2 x
Hexagon Magnum 26’’ x 95’’. The properties of these tanks are:
 ● Nominal Working Pressure : 250 bar
 ● Storage Capacity: Each of the larger tanks has a capacity of 28 kg of hydrogen
 (minimum water volume ~ 1520 L), while the smaller tanks each hold 11 kg
 (minimum water volume ~ 571 L).

In 2023, as part of the Nautilus Project13 Hornblower Group Company received the Design Basis
Letter from the United States Coast Guard (USCG) Marine Safety Center to retrofit an existing
diesel-battery hybrid passenger ferry with a hydrogen-fuel cell system. The intent of the retrofit
is to test and evaluate hydrogen technology as a new approach to reducing reliance on diesel
fuel for motive and auxiliary power. The ferry to be retrofitted, named Discover Zero, intends to
store 168 kg of hydrogen at the Nominal Working Pressure of 250 bar. The Compressed
Hydrogen Storage System (CHSS) comprises 6 tanks of 28 kg storage capacity (exclusively
utilizing the larger tanks used in the Sea Change Ferry from the same tank manufacturer).

8.1.3. Technical Approach for the development of the fueling methodology
 Creating a standardized hydrogen refueling protocol involves developing a set of
standardized procedures and parameters to safely and efficiently fill the Compressed Hydrogen
Storage System (CHSS) of a hydrogen vessel. This requires collaboration among several
stakeholders, including vessel OEMs, infrastructure providers, and representatives from
standardizing organizations along with the necessary scientific knowledge.

The primary objective of this section of the project focused on developing the required scientific
knowledge to aid the development of a standardized refueling protocol for H25 class. This
includes details on the evolution of the thermal field during the filling process. For Type-IV tanks,
this is very important to avoid damage to the tank due to overheating during the filling process.
Such knowledge currently exists for Type-IV hydrogen storage tanks up to a maximum water
volume of 350 liters19. Other tanks tested in the past for which detailed scientific knowledges
exists include the light duty vehicle tanks- 36 liters (Type IV, 700 bar), 40 liters (Type III, 700 bar)
as well as various heavy-duty vehicle hydrogen tanks of 244 liters (Type IV, 700 bar), 350 liters
(Type IV, 500 bar), 322 liters ( Type III, 350 bar), and 165 liters (Type IV, 700 bar). The maximum
aspect ratio (length-to-width) of the tanks tested is 5.3. In the case of the ferries discussed in the
previous section, the Compressed Hydrogen Storage System (CHSS) comprises a tank at
Nominal Working Pressure of 250 bar with water volume of ~ 1520 L and aspect ratio of 8.6. To
safely fill these tanks, it is necessary to verify the applicability of the current knowledge to this
large storage tank.

The primary backbone of a refueling protocol is a reliable thermodynamic model that can predict
the maximum gas temperature, pressure and state-of-charge during the filling. The existing
thermodynamic models have been validated for tanks utilized in Light- and Heavy-Duty Vehicles.

The technical approach for this study leveraged insights from prior extensive research on smaller
tanks, to verify, validate and/or update the model as needed to ensure safe and efficient filling
for the marine vessels. This will be achieved through a three-part approach:

1. Computational Fluid Dynamics Simulation: 3-D

2. Experiments: This provides the measurements for

3. Process Simulations: This consists of a simplified
 process model (relative to detailed CFD), which can
 be executed on a regular computer, to simulate the filling behavior across a broad spectrum
 of conditions. This encompasses simplifying assumptions and empirical correlations that
 necessitate experimental measurements to enhance reliability and accuracy. A dependable
 process model can subsequently be employed to determine optimal filling profiles and
 control parameters, such as Average Pressure Ramp Rate and others essential for a
 standardized fueling protocol.

The work has been centered on the development of a reliable thermodynamic model. In addition
to this, detailed Quantitative Risk Assessment (QRA) needs to be performed for a standardized
refueling protocol.

conventional fueling methods.

8.2. Parameters governing the Refueling Process
 The thermal field that evolves within a tank during the refueling process is intricate and
 involves the interplay between several parameters. A brief overview of these parameters are
 provided below:

8.2.1. Compressed Hydrogen Storage System Properties: Type, Size, and Orientation
 Tank Type: The choice of tank material plays an important role in the evolution of the
internal thermal field as well as the allowable temperature limits during filling and emptying
processes. The different types of cylinders widely used in the industry are:
- Type I : fully metallic cylinder - most widespread technology
- Type II : metallic cylinders with a composite wrapping
- Type III : metallic (usually aluminum) liner cylinder with composite wrapping
- Type IV : polymer liner with composite wrapping
The gravimetric energy density increases from Type-I to Type IV cylinders. The thermal behavior
in Type I and II cylinders are less restrictive compared to the composite cylinders and are well
understood. For Type III and IV cylinders, relatively poor heat transfer properties of the liner

                                                                                     Page 46 of 109

material significantly affects the temperature behavior during the filling process. These tanks
(Type III and IV) usually have a temperature limit of -40 to 85oC.
The Type-IV tank that has been approved by ABS is the focus of this work.

Size: The tank size (storage capacity) is primarily governed by the application needs. Prior work
performed under the HyTransfer Project21 has shown the aspect ratio, defined as the ratio of
internal length (L) and internal tank diameter (D) is an important property of tank geometry that
needs to be considered to avoid thermal stratification during filling and emptying processes. The
tank focused in this work has an aspect ratio ~ 8.

Orientation: For this work the tank has been installed horizontally as is the case of the ferries.

8.2.2. Injector Diameter
 The thermal behavior within the tank is influenced by the injector's internal diameter and
orientation9,22,23. The internal diameter determines the injection speed for a given mass flow rate,
while the orientation affects the flow field inside the tank. The tank focused in this work does not
come with an on-the tank valve (OTV) from the manufacturer. To select an injector, following
approach has been taken:
 ● Prior learnings from the HyTransfer Project9 showed that for hydrogen tanks with aspect
 ratio in the range of 3 - 5.6, the gas velocity at point of injection should not exceed 100
 m/s for a long period of time to prevent the formation of horizontal temperature gradient.
 For the present setup, some selections were narrowed down based on preliminary process
 simulations that considered the transient nature of mass flow rate during filling as well as
 estimated pressure drop in the delivery line.
 ● Preliminary CFD simulation was performed to understand the potential behavior of the
 selected injector for a reference condition. The details are provided in the CFD section
 later.

 ○ Internal Diameter : 5 mm
 ○ Orientation of the injector : Axial
 ○ Length of the injector inside the tank : 100 mm

8.2.4. Initial tank gas condition: Pressure, Temperature and Wall Condition

 These parameters represent the initial conditions before fueling and are important inputs
for models.
Pressure: From the literature, it is well established that when initial pressure in the tank increases,
the issue of temperature elevation decreases. Additionally, a low initial pressure results in a
longer development of the thermal effect and for identical mass flow rate can result in higher gas
temperature at the end of filling. The manufacturer specifies a minimum operational pressure of
7 bar for the tank. To ensure experimental conditions accurately reflect real-world scenarios, a
minimum initial pressure of approximately 10 bar was employed. This conservative value
provides a safety margin to the ferry operator.

Temperature and Wall Condition: Usually in automotive application, the compressed hydrogen
storage systems are protected from the outdoor weather by a protective cover (as in some Class-
8 trucks) and/or by positioning the tanks based on design. However, in the currently operating
Sea Change Ferry and ferries of similar design, the storage tanks are located on the top deck
without any cover. This exposes the tanks to solar radiation that can potentially result in higher
temperature on the top surface with respect to the lower surface. To test this type of scenario,
in the test setup no weather shed was put on the tank. The tested conditions include
homogeneous conditions (where the mean internal gas temperature in the top and bottom
sections is within a 4oC) as well as thermal stratification conditions (where the top surface of the
tank is higher than the bottom surface as well as internal gas temperatures in the top section
exceeds the bottom section by 4oC)

8.3.3. Thermal stratification during refueling

 Thermal stratification during refueling can cause localized high temperatures, which
should be avoided. The injector diameter governs fluid velocity at the tank inlet that affects the
flow and thermal behavior within the tank. Buoyancy, due to temperature differences, can cause
vertical thermal stratification, as colder, denser gas moves down and warmer, lighter gas rises.

Thermal stratification within the tank must be prevented for several reasons. First, it can lead to
localized hot spots exceeding the 82°C threshold, even when the average gas temperature is
below the limit. Second, monitoring gas temperature becomes more complex with stratification,
compared to a uniform temperature distribution where a single sensor suffices.

Two cases are simulated and compared, one with no injector and another case with the injector
(Internal diameter: 5 mm and length: 100 mm). For these two cases, following conditions were
considered:
 ● Ambient Temperature : 25oC

 ● Dispenser Delivery Temperature : 25oC

Summary
CFD simulations provided key insight on the impact of using an injector during refueling. The
results showed that using an injector can maintain a homogeneous temperature distribution in
the tank, enabling efficient heat transfer across the available surface area and potentially avoiding
damage due to overheating. While CFD is a powerful tool for simulating fluid flow, it has its own
set of challenges, including extensive computational resource requirements (long simulation
times and significant computing resources, especially for 3-D geometries). For example, the 3-D
simulation of the 45-minute refueling process took almost 3 months to complete. CFD alone
cannot efficiently evaluate the multiple cases required for developing a refueling methodology
within a reasonable timeframe. Therefore, an experimental setup discussed in the following
section was developed to investigate various scenarios using key insights from the CFD results.

For a closure on the topic of injector, two specific issues need to be addressed by the
experiments:
1. Verify the observed CFD simulation result for the 5 mm injector for APRR of 7 bar/min (Fill
 Time: ~43 minutes)
2. Verify the thermal behavior within the tank for a range of flow rate (range of fill time): Given
 prior observations [Ref. 9-11, refer to footnotes in prior section], it is important to verify the

                                                                                       Page 55 of 109

 thermal behavior within the tank across a range of flow rates (fill times). The reported
 observations indicate that for a specific injector, a velocity range exists that promotes a
 homogeneous thermal distribution during refueling. Velocities below this range can lead to
 buoyancy-dominated hot spots in the upper tank section. Conversely, high velocities can
 cause jet impingement on the tank liner, resulting in thermal stratification. Therefore, an
 optimal injector geometry will enable a homogeneous gas temperature distribution over a
 practical filling duration.

● The experiments demonstrated that achieving a successful fill within the maritime industry's

 optimum injector.
● Ambient conditions and the lack of weather protection for storage tanks greatly affect the
 need for a pre-cooler to achieve a 45-minute fill time. The filling campaign demonstrated that

 arrangement is recommended for a fast fill.

                                                                                    Page 87 of 109

● Thermal stratification can occur in storage tanks without weather protection, due to the upper
 section of the tank heating up more from solar radiation exposure than the bottom section.
 This can lead to higher gas temperature inside the tank relative to the ambient temperature.
 This can affect the maximum temperature inside the tank during filling. To ensure a safe filling,
 ‘Hot Soak’ conditions should be defined appropriately.

8.5. Technical Approach 3: Process Simulations
 The development of a standard protocol is a lengthy and complex process, requiring
modeling tools and experimental validation. This process can take up to three or four years, as
exemplified by the HyTransfer32 project, and can be very expensive. However, the use of
validated modeling tools for simulating the filling process can significantly accelerate protocol
development, reduce costs, and limit experimental campaigns. To achieve this, only validated
models should be utilized.

For development of the protocols for light and heavy-duty vehicles, several organizations have
developed thermodynamic models to estimate the temperature and pressure in the hydrogen
tanks during filling and defueling. This includes - Air Liquide’s SOFIL, Engie’s Hyfill, NREL’s
H2FillS, and Wenger’s H2-Fill. In the framework of the PRHYDE Project7, a benchmark study has
been performed33, that gives details on each model. These engineering models run in a few
minutes, as they have a much lower computational cost than CFD models. To do so reliably, they
utilize certain simplifying assumptions and empirical correlations derived from experimental
results. Based on prior works, these models have been tuned and validated with experimental
results for cylinders of smaller volumes - 36 liters (Type IV, 700 bar), 40 liters (Type III, 700 bar)
as well as various heavy-duty vehicle hydrogen tanks of 244 liters (Type IV, 700 bar), 350 liters
(Type IV, 500 bar), 322 liters (Type III, 350 bar), and 165 liters (Type IV, 700 bar). The maximum
aspect ratio of the tanks tested is 5.3. For tanks within their validated range, all models are in
good general agreement. However, the reliability of these simplified models needs to be verified
when applying to filling tanks that are outside of the model’s current validation range.

The goal of this technical approach:
● Assess the applicability for filling the ferry tanks based on the experimental results.

8.5.1. Brief Overview of the Modelling Tool: SOFIL
 The SOFIL model, developed by Air Liquide R&D since 2010, is a sophisticated tool for
predicting the thermal behavior of gas storage tanks. It operates under the assumption that the
temperature and pressure of the gas inside the tank is homogeneous (prior and during the filling),
while the temperature inside the wall is considered one-dimensional in the thickness. This
necessitates an optimal injector for the tank to maintain uniform temperature distribution during
filling and prevent thermal stratification. Therefore, the HyTransfer Project recommended a
minimum gas velocity of 5 m/s for the tested tanks. For tanks with an aspect ratio exceeding 3,
the gas velocity at the injection point should not surpass 100 m/s for extended periods. The 0D-
gas/1D-wall approach has been shown to accurately predict both gas as well as tank wall
temperatures when compared to experimental results for previously tested tanks35,36.

8.5.2. Assessment of the previously validated model with the experimental result
 Following the experimental campaign, the measured parameters (dispenser pressure and
temperature) were used as input into the model. The estimated gas temperature, pressure, and
tank wall temperature were compared with the recorded experimental measurement. Figure 8.29
shows the comparison for Test-ID-7. In this test case, homogeneous temperature distribution
was present prior to filling. The ambient temperature was 22.8 OC and a successful filling was
achieved within 43 mins without using pre-cooling. The predicted gas pressure at the end of

8.5.3. Model Updates and resulting performance
 To improve the predictability of the SOFIL toolkit for this tank, several
updates/modifications were attempted and compared with the experimental measurements.
Based on these, a combination of the following updates provided the best fit to the experimental
measurements:
● Thermophysical properties of the tank liner and composite: Using the data for Type-IV tanks
 generated during the HyTransfer Project improved the estimation to some extent. The results
 shown in the previous section utilized the values reported in the PrHyde Project reports.
● Internal Heat Exchange: In the Nusselt number correlation, described in the previous section,

 this work. These values were estimated to be 0.01 and 0.035, respectively.
● External Heat Exchange: On the external surfaces of the tanks considered, radiative heat
 exchanges were modelled, in addition to natural convection exchanges. From the

The significant improvement in the reliability of the thermodynamic model is clearly evident from

 ● For conditions with a homogeneous temperature distribution before filling, Test-ID-1, 5

during the filling process.

Summary
This section showed that engineering models such as SOFIL can, with calibration, reach an
accuracy of +/- 2.7 OC for the estimation of the volume average gas temperature during filling of
hydrogen tanks. These models can run in a few minutes, and can be used to evaluate several
potential scenarios that are needed for development of standardized refueling protocols under
the following conditions:

                                                                                    Page 98 of 109

1. The simplified thermodynamic model needs to be well validated for the tanks and fueling
 conditions being considered. Section 5.2 showed that using models for tanks outside the
 validated range can have wide discrepancies.
2. The underlying assumption of temperature and pressure homogeneity needs to be
 considered. During filling, the occurrence of thermal stratifications can be well mitigated by
 proper On-Tank Valve design, while the weather-related thermal stratification issue in the
 modeling can be mitigated by considering an additional margin (either in the initial gas
 temperature through the hot-soak assumption or the final gas temperature estimation).

Overall Summary and Recommendations

The primary focus of this work has been to support the development of a fueling methodology
for ferries utilizing large Type-IV tanks (storage capacities of 28 kg). The performance target for
filling the tank includes achieving a successful fill within 45 mins, desired maximum filling duration
by the maritime industry. Currently, no standardized refueling protocol exists for this application.
A reliable thermodynamic model supported by Computational Fluid Dynamics Simulations and
experiments has been developed in this work to analyze numerous potential scenarios and aid
the development of fueling methodology.

The key finds from the study are listed below:
 ● Within the maritime industry's preferred 45-minute fill time, successful filling of these
 larger tanks is achievable without tank damage from overheating, over-pressurization, or
 overfilling, provided the tank is equipped with an optimal injector.
 ● Computational Fluid Dynamics simulations showed that for lower injection (or gas inlet)
 velocities, thermal stratification can occur resulting in potential hot spots. This expands
 the use of this previous knowledge to tanks with larger dimensions. This analysis guided
 the use of an axial injector with 5 mm internal diameter and 100 mm length.
 ● Ambient conditions and the lack of weather protection for storage tanks greatly affect the
 need for a pre-cooler to achieve a 45-minute fill time. The filling campaign demonstrated
 that fast filling within 45 minutes can be achieved without pre-cooling when both the
 ambient and internal gas temperatures before filling are below 25 OC. For warmer
 conditions, a pre-cooling arrangement is recommended to achieve a fast fill.
 ● The engineering model (SOFIL) has been updated and it can reach an accuracy of +/- 2.7

12. Current Status

Step 1 of the USCG review (Figure 7.4) was initiated on June 15, 2023 with the USCG, via a
submission to Sector San Francisco of the following required items, namely a “regulatory matrix”
which describes the guiding principles for H2 Barge design (i.e., NFPA 2, NFPA 70 and CGA G-
5.5), the General Arrangements, the Hazardous Area plan, the Setback Distances plan, a
hydrogen systems P&ID, an electrical one-line diagram and a control block diagram. In the
December 2023 timeframe, the project received a Go-decision from the DOE based on the
technical progress made on the design, the receptivity of the local San Francisco Bay Area
community, and encouraging interactions with the USCG. Hornblower’s continued leadership in
latter Phases was conditional and subject to the project’s ability to secure a sustainable business
model for project Phase 2 (equipment assembly and deployment) and Phase 3 (operations for a
one-year demonstration period and beyond). Owning to a number of factors, including: (i) the
inability to secure a mutually beneficial fueling agreement with a maritime customer; (ii) the
erosion in project economics stemming from deteriorating Low carbon Fuel Standard (LCFS)
credit prices and uncertainty regarding the Project’s ability to access a Clean Hydrogen
Production Tax Credit under Section 45V; and (iii) the financial difficulties of its parent company,
Hornblower Energy determined it could not effectively lead the project beyond Phase I in
January 2024. DOE considered the administrative challenges associated with finding a new
project lead with cost-share funding and decided to suspend the H2 Barge Project after successful
completion of Phase I.

The project team is currently seeking alternative funding sources and business models that will
allow completion of Phases II (construction) and Phase III (deployment) of the H2 Barge.

13. Summary:

We have designed a Floating Hydrogen Production and Dispensing Barge to be located at the
Port of San Francisco. The “H2 Barge” can produce renewable H2 at the rate of 531kg/day, storing
512 kg of 517-bar high-pressure H2, allowing fast refueling of hydrogen fuel cell vessels and land-
side hydrogen delivery trailers for distribution into the nascent land-based SF hydrogen
ecosystem. The facility can also provide renewable power for recharging the batteries of electric
vessels. Pier 68/Wharf 3 at the Port of SF provides a very suitable home for the H2 Barge, with
physical, electrical and fresh-water infrastructure that can ably support safe and secure H2
production and refueling operations. The site also provides the required vessel maneuvering
space, highway access for H2 trailers, all within compliance of NFPA 2 safety regulations.

The design enabled the USCG to generate a review process for the activity. After internal
deliberations with the various USCG offices, it was determined the H2 Barge would be reviewed
as a “facility” or a “permanently moored craft” for regulatory purposes. Work will continue
seeking funding for Phase 2 and 3 of the activity, which will involve full engagement with the
USCG regulatory process, final H2 Barge design, approval of the H2 Barged design and planned
fueling operations, construction and demonstration.

Another result of this work has been to support the development of a fueling methodology for
ferries utilizing large Type-IV tanks (storage capacities of 28 kg). The performance target for filling
the tank includes achieving a successful fill within 45 mins, desired maximum filling duration by
the maritime industry. Currently, no standardized refueling protocol exists for this application. A
reliable thermodynamic model supported by CFD simulations and experiments has been
developed in this work to analyze numerous potential scenarios and aid the development of
fueling methodology.

The key finds from the experimental and modeling study are:

 ● Within the maritime industry's preferred 45-minute fill time, successful filling of these
 larger tanks is achievable without tank damage from overheating, over-pressurization, or
 overfilling, provided the tank is equipped with an appropriate injector that mitigates
 temperature variations that can lead to “hot spots.”
 ● CFD simulations showed that for lower injection (or gas inlet) velocities, thermal
 stratification can occur resulting in potential hot spots. This expands the use of this

                                                                                        Page 106 of 109

 previous knowledge to tanks with larger dimensions. This analysis guided the use of an
 axial injector with 5 mm internal diameter and 100 mm length.

● Ambient conditions and the lack of weather protection for storage tanks greatly affect the
 need for a pre-cooling to achieve a 45-minute fill time. The filling campaign demonstrated
 that fast filling within 45 minutes can be achieved without pre-cooling when both the
 ambient and internal gas temperatures before filling are below 25°C. For warmer
 conditions, a pre-cooling arrangement is recommended to achieve a fast fill. Pre-cooling
 cannot be supplied by nearby ambient water, such as that found in nature (bays, lakes,
 rivers, etc.).

● The engineering model (SOFIL) has been calibrated against experiment and can reach an
 accuracy of +/- 2.7°C for the estimation of the volume average gas temperature during
 filling of hydrogen tanks. The model can be used to evaluate potential scenarios for
 development of fueling methodology. However, the underlying assumption of
 temperature and pressure homogeneity needs to be considered. During filling, the
 occurrence of thermal stratifications can be well mitigated by proper On-Tank Valve
 design, while the weather-related thermal stratification issue can be mitigated by
 considering an additional margin (either in the initial gas temperature through the hot-
 soak assumption or the final gas temperature estimation).
