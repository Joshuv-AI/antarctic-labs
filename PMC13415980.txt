Primary Large Cell Neuroendocrine Carcinoma of the Bladder Successfully Treated With Radical Cystectomy Alone: A Case Report
Authors: Shun Aoki, Takashi Kawahara, Yuri Nishino, Hideki Tsukada, Takayuki Hirano, Genta Iwamoto, Shusei Fusayasu, Jun‐ichi Teranishi, Kazuhide Makiyama, Hiroyuki Hayashi, Hiroji Uemura, Masatoshi Moriyama, Junichi Ohta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPrimary large‐cell neuroendocrine carcinoma of the bladder is highly invasive and has a poor prognosis. However, early curative treatment may lead to a good prognosis.

Introduction

Neuroendocrine carcinoma is most often found in the lungs and the gastrointestinal tract. Most patients with bladder cancer are diagnosed with urothelial carcinoma; neuroendocrine carcinoma accounts for less than 1% of cases [1, 2]. Large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC) is a rare type of neuroendocrine carcinoma of the bladder [3, 4, 5]. According to the current WHO classification, neuroendocrine carcinomas of the bladder comprise small cell carcinoma and the rarer large cell type, which differ in cell size and nuclear morphology. To date, fewer than 50 cases have been reported, and the largest systematic review identified 39 patients [2].

Primary LCNEC of the bladder is usually diagnosed in the advanced stages of the disease and has a poor prognosis [6]. Currently, there is no established treatment for primary bladder LCNEC, and previous reports have described combined treatment with surgery and chemotherapy [7].

In this study, we report a case of primary LCNEC of the urinary bladder treated with cystectomy alone without recurrence. This case is noteworthy because long‐term recurrence‐free survival was achieved with radical cystectomy alone, without perioperative chemotherapy, which is rarely reported for this aggressive tumor.

Case History/Examination

A 76‐year‐old woman presented to our hospital in October 2020 with gross hematuria that had persisted for one month. Her medical history included hypertension, no smoking history, and no symptoms other than gross hematuria. Blood samples showed no abnormal findings and urinalysis showed atypical cells (5–9 per high‐power field); urine cytology of the same specimen was class II (negative for malignancy). Cystoscopy revealed a 3 cm papillary tumor on the left wall of the bladder.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment)

Pelvic MRI scan showed a 3 cm‐sized hypodiffusion lesion on the left lateral wall of the bladder with no obvious bladder muscle layer invasion (Figure 1). A thoracoabdominal pelvic CT scan showed no lymph node metastasis or distant metastasis. Transurethral resection of the bladder tumor was performed in October 2020, and a histopathological examination revealed highly atypical urothelial cells infiltrating the bladder muscle layer, with numerous mitotic figures (29 per 10 high‐power fields) and rosette‐like structures. Tumor necrosis was present. Immunohistochemical staining of the tumor cells with synaptophysin and chromogranin A was positive (Figure 2). The antibodies used were synaptophysin (clone MRQ‐40, rabbit monoclonal; Roche Diagnostics/Ventana Medical Systems; ready‐to‐use) and chromogranin A (clone K2H10, mouse monoclonal; Roche Diagnostics/Ventana Medical Systems; ready‐to‐use). Because the diagnosis of LCNEC had already been established from the characteristic morphology (large pleomorphic cells with a high mitotic count and necrosis) together with positive synaptophysin and chromogranin A staining, Ki‐67 and CD56 were not examined as part of the routine diagnostic workup; lymphovascular invasion could not be assessed in the TURBT specimen. The patient was finally diagnosed with primary mixed bladder neuroendocrine carcinoma consisting of urothelial carcinoma and LCNEC (approximately 80% LCNEC and 20% urothelial carcinoma, estimated from the synaptophysin‐positive area) (T2N0M0). Although preoperative MRI showed no obvious muscle‐layer invasion, the TURBT specimen demonstrated infiltration into the muscle layer, confirming muscle‐invasive disease (cT2).

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy was considered, but we decided to perform immediate laparoscopic radical cystectomy plus ileal conduit diversion in January 2021, together with a standard bilateral pelvic lymphadenectomy (internal iliac, external iliac, and obturator regions; 20 lymph nodes removed).

Conclusions and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The surgical margins were negative, and no residual carcinoma or lymph node metastasis was found in the bladder specimen (final pathological stage, pT0N0); therefore, postoperative chemotherapy was not performed. At 3 years and 6 months postoperatively, there was no evidence of recurrence or distant metastasis. Postoperatively, the patient was followed with plain computed tomography every 3 months, and the most recent evaluation, in July 2024, showed no recurrence (Figure 3). This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE 2023 criteria.

Discussion

Neuroendocrine carcinoma is mainly found in the lungs and gastrointestinal tract, with primary bladder involvement being rare [1, 3, 4, 5]. Although the mechanism of development of bladder LCNEC is still unknown, it is thought to be derived from multifunctional stem cells in the bladder mucosa, and this mechanism is thought to allow bladder LCNEC to coexist with urothelial carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, and squamous cell carcinoma [5]. As with other rare and aggressive variant histologies of the bladder, such as squamous cell carcinoma, the prognosis is generally poor and management remains challenging.

LCNEC is diagnosed by histopathological and immunohistological examination; histopathological features include large cells with pleomorphism, abundant cytoplasm, and coarse‐grained chromatin. Bladder LCNEC may show structures such as rosettes, palisading patterns, and trabecular architecture. Immunohistochemistry has high sensitivity and specificity in neuroendocrine carcinoma when it shows expression of synaptophysin, chromogranin A, CD56, and Ki‐67 index > 40% [8, 9, 10, 11]. Based on these criteria, we diagnosed bladder LCNEC associated with urothelial carcinoma.

Bladder LCNEC is a rare disease with no established standard of care, but almost all previously reported cases have been treated with radical total cystectomy with chemotherapy [11]. Chemotherapy regimens for LCNEC are based on those for lung neuroendocrine carcinoma, with etoposide and platinum‐based regimens being the most common [5, 9]. Because no randomized trials specific to bladder LCNEC exist, treatment recommendations are largely extrapolated from other neuroendocrine carcinomas, such as small cell lung cancer [12, 13, 14, 15]. Surgical resection followed by neoadjuvant chemotherapy has been reported to improve overall survival in patients with localized bladder LCNEC [9]. Gupta et al. [16] reported that the overall survival of patients with localized mixed bladder LCNEC was 38.8 months in their radical cystectomy‐only group and 86.8 months in their combined chemotherapy group, and the overall prognosis for bladder LCNEC is a 1‐year survival rate of less than 55% and a 3‐year survival rate of less than 25%, with a mean survival of less than 3 months for patients with metastasis. Postoperative survival of bladder LCNEC is related to the T stage at the diagnosis, with median survival of 23.9 and 16.0 months for patients with T1–2 and T3–4 tumors, respectively [2]. In that systematic review, early T stage (T1–2), the absence of distant metastasis, and multimodality therapy were associated with better overall survival [2]; our patient shared the favorable factors of localized T2 disease without metastasis, which may account for the prolonged recurrence‐free survival despite the omission of chemotherapy.

The patient underwent immediate total cystectomy because of concern that preoperative chemotherapy would delay radical resection; because bladder LCNEC is a highly aggressive tumor and the evidence supporting chemotherapy is limited, early radical surgery was judged to be the preferable option. Because there was no residual tumor in the resected specimen, adjuvant chemotherapy was not administered. Because no postoperative chemotherapy regimen with established evidence could be offered for this disease, the decision to forgo adjuvant chemotherapy was made together with the patient after a full explanation of the available options. As mentioned above, the prognosis of patients treated with surgical resection alone is worse than that of patients who receive surgical resection combined with chemotherapy; however, this patient is currently doing relatively well. This may be due to the fact that the patient had T2 disease at the diagnosis, without lymph node or distant metastasis, and had no residual tumor in the resected specimen. Nevertheless, this single case should not be interpreted as evidence that radical cystectomy alone is sufficient for bladder LCNEC. Given the aggressive nature of this tumor and the high reported risk of systemic relapse, the favorable course here may reflect a biologically favorable subset, and the omission of chemotherapy should be considered with caution. Accumulation of further cases is needed to define the optimal management strategy. Molecular or genomic profiling (e.g., next‐generation sequencing) was not performed in this case. Molecularly, neuroendocrine carcinomas of the bladder frequently harbor TP53 and RB1 alterations and appear to share a common clonal origin with co‐existing urothelial carcinoma, and immune‐checkpoint inhibitors have been proposed as an emerging option for high‐grade neuroendocrine carcinoma [17, 18]. Beyond immune‐checkpoint inhibitors, these molecular features are prompting the evaluation of targeted and combination strategies in high‐grade and extrapulmonary neuroendocrine carcinoma. For example, immune‐checkpoint inhibitors have been combined with cabozantinib in the ICONIC trial (NCT03866382); lurbinectedin has shown anecdotal activity in treatment‐refractory metastatic small‐cell/neuroendocrine carcinoma, with a prospective study ongoing (NCT06228066); and the SWOG S2012 trial (NCT05058651) is a randomized phase 2/3 study evaluating induction platinum/etoposide chemotherapy with or without atezolizumab in metastatic extrapulmonary small‐cell carcinoma [19, 20, 21].

Primary LCNEC of the bladder is extremely rare, and its prognosis is poor because the disease is often advanced at the time of the diagnosis. However, if there is no metastasis or residual tumor after cystectomy, patients may have a good prognosis even after radical cystectomy alone. However, given the aggressive nature of bladder LCNEC and the fact that this is based on a single case, this observation should be interpreted with caution and confirmed by the accumulation of further cases.
