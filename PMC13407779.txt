Pleomorphic Adenoma of the Palate with Bizarre Ductal and Myoepithelial Cells: A Case Image
Authors: Katsutoshi Hirose, Masato Nakaguro, Kohei Kawamura, Taisuke Mori, Satoru Toyosawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPleomorphic adenomas (PAs), the most common salivary gland tumors, are comprised of ductal epithelial and myoepithelial cells within a stromal background. A rare subset of PAs exhibits bizarre cells and 12q amplification involving HMGA2 and MDM2; in reported cases, bizarre cells have been confined to the myoepithelial component. We report the first case of a PA with bizarre-cytological features in both the ductal and myoepithelial components. Case presentationA 75-year-old woman presented with a palatal mass. Histology revealed a well-circumscribed biphasic tumor with marked nuclear pleomorphism in both luminal ductal and non-luminal myoepithelial cells. Despite the alarming atypia, mitotic activity was low, and neither necrosis nor invasive growth was observed. Immunohistochemically, luminal bizarre cells exhibited ductal characteristics, whereas non-luminal bizarre cells exhibited myoepithelial characteristics. Tumor cells were negative for androgen receptor and HER2 and showed a p53 wild-type expression pattern. The Ki-67 labeling index was < 5%, and the bizarre cells rarely tested positive. Molecular analysis demonstrated amplification of HMGA2 and MDM2, whereas no other pathogenic variants related to salivary gland tumors were detected. Complete excision was performed; no evidence of recurrence or metastasis was observed after 10 months. ConclusionMarked cytological atypia of bizarre cells in PA, even when involving ductal cells, may not necessarily indicate malignancy. This case highlights the value of integrating clinicopathological and genetic findings to better define PAs with bizarre cells and prevent overdiagnosis of carcinoma based on atypia alone.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12105-026-01953-9.Keywords: Salivary glands, Pleomorphic adenoma, 12q, HMGA2, MDM2

Pleomorphic adenoma (PA), the most common salivary gland tumor, is comprised of ductal epithelial and myoepithelial cells within a stromal background and is arranged in various morphological patterns [1]. Although PAs are benign, they may contain neoplastic cells with varying degrees of atypia [1, 2]. Bishop et al. [2] proposed a PA subset based on an analysis of salivary gland tumors with tumor cells showing high-grade nuclear atypia termed “bizarre cells.” This subset is characterized by 12q amplification involving HMGA2 and/or MDM2, bizarre cells with myoepithelial properties, and biphasic tumor architecture [2]. To date, only 13 cases of PA featuring bizarre cells have been reported (Supplementary Tables 1, 2); in all cases, the bizarre cells were confined to the myoepithelial component [2–5]. Herein, we report the first case of palatal PA with bizarre-cytological features in both the ductal epithelial and myoepithelial components.

A 75-year-old woman was referred to Osaka University Dental Hospital for painless swelling of the left side of her hard palate persisting for 6 months. She had no history of smoking or alcohol consumption, or notable medical history. Intraoral examination revealed a bluish mass (Fig. 1a). Imaging showed a well-circumscribed, ovoid submucosal mass measuring 14 × 13 × 12 mm (Fig. 1b) and pressure-induced resorption of the adjacent maxillary alveolar bone (Fig. 1c). Based on a clinical diagnosis of PA or a low-grade malignant salivary gland tumor, an incisional biopsy and complete excision were performed.

Macroscopically, the cut surface of the surgical specimen had solid whitish and yellowish areas (Fig. 2a, b). Histologically, the whitish areas corresponded to a well-circumscribed proliferation of tumor cells adjacent to the salivary glands, without a distinct capsule (Fig. 2c). The yellowish areas corresponded to macrophage aggregates (data not shown). The tumor showed a proliferation of tumor cells, forming cystic and ductal structures (Fig. 2d). The tumor was composed of an admixture of bilayered ducts, myoepithelial cells, and stroma. Ductal and myoepithelial cells were commonly arranged as bilayered ductal structures, and the luminal ductal cells often exhibited abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm with mild nuclear enlargement (Fig. 2e, f). Bizarre cells were scattered within the inner luminal epithelial layer (Fig. 2f) and in areas of sheet-like proliferation of non-luminal myoepithelial cells (Fig. 2g). The bizarre cells contained large, irregularly shaped nuclei with smudgy chromatin and, occasionally, nuclear vacuoles. The overall mitotic rate was low (~ 1/10 HPF), and the bizarre cells lacked mitotic activity. Focal mucous cells staining positive for mucicarmine were identified (Fig. 2h). No increase in mitotic figures, atypical mitosis, necrosis, lymphovascular invasion, or perineural invasion were present.

These bizarre cells exhibit large, irregularly shaped nuclei with smudgy chromatin and occasional nuclear vacuoles, but mitoses are absent. h Mucous cells in the luminal layer (inset: mucicarmine stain)

Immunohistochemical analysis (Fig. 3) revealed strong expression of CK AE1/AE3 and CK7 in ductal epithelial cells and weak expression of these in myoepithelial cells. Myoepithelial-cell markers (p63, p40, SMA, and calponin) highlighted preservation of the biphasic architecture. Luminal bizarre cells were strongly positive for CK AE1/AE3 and CK7 and negative for myoepithelial-cell markers, supporting their ductal cell characteristics. Conversely, non-luminal bizarre cells were weakly positive for CK AE1/AE3 and CK7 and positive for myoepithelial-cell markers, supporting their myoepithelial cell characteristics. The Ki-67 labeling index was lower than 5% throughout the tumor. Similar positivity rates were observed in both ductal and myoepithelial cells. However, most bizarre cells tested negative. The tumor cells were negative for androgen receptor and HER2. They exhibited a p53 wild-type expression pattern.

RNA-based targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) was performed using a custom panel designed to detect fusions, single-nucleotide polymorphisms, insertions/deletions, and expression levels of 27 genes relevant to salivary gland tumors [1] (Supplementary Table 3). It revealed a high expression level of HMGA2 (Fig. 4a). Similarly, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) demonstrated amplification of HMGA2 without rearrangement (Fig. 4b). Immunohistochemistry for HMGA2 revealed diffusely positivity in the tumor cells, including the bizarre cells (Fig. 4b). Although MDM2 was not included in the custom NGS panel, FISH confirmed MDM2 amplification, and immunohistochemistry showed that the tumor cells, including the bizarre cells, were strongly positive for MDM2 (Fig. 4b). These findings support the presence of 12q amplification in bizarre cells as well. Targeted NGS did not identify other pathogenic variants commonly found in PAs or carcinoma ex-PA [1].

In the left panels, HMGA2 break-apart FISH reveals amplification without rearrangement (5’-red centromeric side, 3’-green telomeric side). IHC showing high HMGA2 protein expression in both ductal and myoepithelial cells (inset shows a bizarre cell). In the right panels, FISH analysis shows MDM2 amplification (green signals) relative to the chromosome 12 centromere control (red signals). IHC shows high MDM2 protein expression in both ductal and myoepithelial cells (inset shows a bizarre cell)

Based on these observations—a biphasic tumor with low proliferative activity, mucinous metaplasia, and an HMGA2 alteration—a final diagnosis of PA with bizarre ductal and myoepithelial cells was established. Ten months after surgery, no recurrence or metastasis was observed, but authors acknowledge the limited follow-up for this tumor type. The subset of PAs with bizarre cells appears to have a favorable prognosis, suggesting that the presence of these cells may indicate a benign process rather than malignancy [2–5] (Supplementary Table 1). The present case also suggests that the marked nuclear atypia of these bizarre cells, even when present in ductal cells, is not necessarily indicative of malignancy, and may be a significant diagnostic pitfall on preoperative biopsy. Detailed clinical, morphological, and genetic analyses may improve our understanding of the biological behavior of PAs and help to avoid a misdiagnosis of carcinoma based on cytological features alone.
