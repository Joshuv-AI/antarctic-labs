Nonlinearities and Switch‐Like Behavior in Gene Expression: From Genetics to Biochemistry and Back
Authors: Reiner A. Veitia, James A. Birchler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMany developmental and physiological outcomes depend on transcriptional thresholds or switches. The transcriptional output, ultimately measured as mature RNA, is determined by a series of molecular steps that can introduce thresholds, bottlenecks, or amplification mechanisms. Their combination can convert quantitative changes in regulator abundance into more‐than‐proportional changes in mature RNA production. Such nonlinear, S‐shaped/sigmoidal input–output relationships may account, at least in part, for the haploinsufficiency (HI) of transcription factors (TFs) and chromatin regulators. Here, we link genetic evidence for dosage sensitivity to biochemical nonlinearity in the context of transcription regulation. Without aiming to be exhaustive, we discuss several sources of sigmoidicity, such as classical cooperative DNA binding and transcriptional synergy. We also analyze how chromatin factors, three‐dimensional nuclear organization, and transcriptional condensates can generate thresholds. In addition, we explore the roles of epigenetic hysteresis and molecular memory. Finally, at the systems level, we consider how nonlinearities can emerge from regulatory networks that convert graded changes in regulator abundance into stable alternative states. Overall, this paper supports the view that HI of TFs and chromatin regulators does not rely on a single mechanism. Rather, HI is a phenotypic consequence of the disruption of diverse molecular and network architectures that translate dosage perturbations into discrete biochemical and phenotypic outcomes.

Introduction

Transcription factors (TFs) are central actors of gene regulation. By binding DNA motifs within cis‐regulatory elements (e.g., promoters and enhancers) and recruiting or antagonizing the transcriptional machinery, TFs convert diverse inputs into changes in RNA output. However, TF occupancy is only an upstream variable in a multi‐step process that includes chromatin remodeling, enhancer–promoter communication, pre‐initiation complex assembly (PIC), promoter opening, promoter escape, early elongation and subsequent RNA production and processing [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. These steps are further modulated by post‐translational modifications that can alter the interaction strength, activity and localization of regulatory proteins [1, 6]. Thus, transcriptional output emerges from a hierarchy of coupled molecular events, any of which can introduce bottlenecks, thresholds or amplification mechanisms.

On the genetics side, extensive work has shown that transcriptional regulators are very sensitive to dosage perturbation. Early attempts to mimic aneuploid effects (missing or extra chromosomes) on genome‐wide expression revealed that changing regulatory dosage can extensively modulate target‐gene expression in trans. For instance, various genetic screens in Drosophila recovered modifiers that increased or decreased the expression of a particular locus, later characterized as TFs, signal‐transduction components, and chromatin proteins [7, 8]. Classical genomic‐balance studies established that both increased and reduced chromosomal dosage, relative to balanced ploidy, were often detrimental. These effects are substantially produced by dosage‐sensitive multi‐subunit regulatory machineries, including TFs, chromatin regulators, and protein complexes [9].

Moreover, dosage‐series experiments in maize show that monosomies (one missing chromosome), trisomies (one extra chromosome), and tetrasomies can increase the expression of some targets, decrease that of others, or affect them in the same direction in both reduced‐ and increased‐dosage states. Not surprisingly, disomic haploids (one supernumerary chromosome in a haploid genome) show stronger effects, illustrating that greater imbalances can produce stronger transcriptomic perturbations [10, 11].

Human trisomy datasets are consistent with this picture. When TFs located on the trisomic chromosome are themselves significantly dosage‐altered, their putative targets can show both positive and negative responses, again indicating that TF dosage effects impact network and stoichiometric contexts leading to nonlinear input–output relationships [12]. Many “TF disorders” are gene‐dosage disorders [13, 14]. Consistently, genome‐wide analyses and curated datasets show that genes encoding TFs tend to be haploinsufficient (HI) [15]. That is, decreasing their gene‐product dosage by about 50% cannot ensure a normal phenotype. Furthermore, experimental modulation of TF levels (e.g., SOX9) demonstrates that even modest reductions in protein levels can produce more‐than‐proportional reductions in transcriptional output [16]. To complicate matters, even in homeostasis, the effective transcriptional input (e.g., TF abundance, activity, nuclear localization, and cofactor availability) depends on signals and cues which are not fixed in time [17, 18].

These observations raise a mechanistic question: How can the effect of a nominal twofold reduction in the dosage of a transcriptional/chromatin regulator be amplified at a subset of critical targets?

Dominance, HI and Nonlinearities

HI and, dominance in general, reflect a nonlinear relationship between genotype and phenotype, so that the phenotype of the heterozygote does not map at the midpoint between those of the parental homozygotes [19, 20]. In phenomenological terms, a simple way to reflect these genetic dominance effects in the context of transcription is to represent a sigmoidal input–output relationship by a Hill‐type function: where S is the upstream input (e.g., TF concentration/activity), Vs is the maximal effective synthesis/processing capacity (plateau/ceiling) and Ks corresponds to the half‐activation point. Thus, S=Ks places the system near the transition between the rising regime and saturation and n is a compound effective Hill exponent. A higher n implies a sharper transition (Figure 1). For instance, if the physiological TF concentration lies right above this transition zone, i.e., around K s , a 50% reduction in gene dosage can move down the system across the threshold separating low and high transcriptional states, resulting in amplified effects at the phenotypic level (Figure 1). A complementary way to understand the HI phenomenon is to consider not only the mean level of gene product, but also the temporal stability of its production. Indeed, Cook, Gerber and Tapscott proposed that HI need not reflect only a static requirement for more than 50% of the normal gene‐product level. In their model, each allele switches independently between inactive and active states, so that diploidy buffers random and transient interruptions of expression [21]. Reducing copy number can increase the amplitude and duration of downward excursions and cause product concentration to fall transiently below a functional threshold. A key nonlinear feature of the model is that, under unstable expression kinetics, repeated activation events are required to accumulate enough product to cross this threshold, making the input–output relationship sigmoidal. Gene copy number strongly modulates this sigmoid: two alleles generate a steeper and more predictable response than one allele [21].

Although these copy‐number changes can lead to approximately linear changes at the biochemical level, their consequences need not be linear at the level of RNA production or target gene activity. Decreasing S in the dosage‐sensitive regime induces a much larger decrease in transcriptional output than expected from gene dosage alone. (B) The biological consequence is that the dosage‐altered state is not intermediate between two parental or reference states. This nonlinear behavior provides an explanation for genetic dominance and HI.

In the context of HI and as shown by Naqvi et al. [16], most targets of the TF SOX9 are poorly sensitive to dosage variation. However, a minority, particularly those linked to the HI phenotype, show pronounced dosage sensitivity. This shows that the regulatory parameters, in particular the effective K s values, of different promoters/enhancers differ substantially [16]. Similarly, PAX6 illustrates differential threshold sensitivity, because heterozygous loss of function is sufficient to cause aniridia and other ocular phenotypes, yet tissue‐specific studies show that different ocular structures have distinct dosage requirements [22]. These two examples illustrate a recurrent theme: a master regulator may control many targets, but only a subset of target promoters/enhancers sit close enough to a threshold at which a 50% decrease in dosage matters. The following sections examine molecular mechanisms that can generate threshold‐like behavior at several stages of gene expression and its regulation.

Promoter/Enhancer‐Level and Early Transcription Sources of Nonlinearity

Many genes exhibit nonlinear input–output behavior because promoter and enhancer activation arise from a sequence of events. Because each step has its own kinetics and some may become rate‐limiting, gene expression output can remain low across a range of TF input and then rise sharply once a limiting requirement is satisfied. This multi‐step architecture can therefore generate nonlinear responses even without DNA‐binding cooperativity [2].

Classical Cooperativity

The notion of classical cooperativity entails that binding of a TF molecule increases the equilibrium affinity of another molecule. In this regime, as S (e.g., TF concentration/activity) increases, cooperative stabilization makes it disproportionately easier to populate the promoter/enhancer, leading to a productive configuration (Figure 2). For instance, Shively et al. have shown that cooperativity explains in vivo binding specificity of Cbf1p and Tye7p in yeast [23]. However, systematic measurements suggest that for many yeast TFs, cooperativity (especially involving the same type of TF molecules) contributes only modestly to in vivo occupancy [24]. This points to the existence of other sources of nonlinearity that will be dealt with below.

Some examples of mechanisms generating nonlinear transcriptional responses. (A) Homotypic cooperativity. Binding of one TF molecule to a cis‐regulatory element can favor the recruitment or stabilization of additional molecules of the same TF on adjacent sites. In the schematic, three TF molecules are already bound cooperatively, while an additional TF is poised to bind an empty neighboring site (“attracted” by DNA and the bound TF molecules, represented by dashed green lines). Such positive coupling between identical bound factors increases the probability of promoter/enhancer occupancy over a narrow input range. (B) Transcriptional synergy. Several DNA‐bound TF molecules (mediated by cooperativity or not) can act collectively to recruit or stabilize a molecular platform, represented here by Mediator, p300 and RNA polymerase II. In this case, the nonlinearity does not arise from cooperative DNA binding itself, but from multivalent functional interactions between bound activators and the transcriptional machinery.

DNA‐Mediated/Residence‐Time/Nucleosome‐Mediated Cooperativity

DNA binding of one TF molecule to DNA can also modify the shape/dynamics of the DNA and increase binding affinity of another TF, even if they do not display protein–protein interactions in solution [25]. For example, interactions between TWIST1 and ALX4 cannot form a complex in solution, but their interaction is stabilized on a long DNA motif called “Coordinator” [26]. In addition, it is known that DNA can transmit allosteric signals that increase TF binding affinity [27]. Recent large‐scale interaction mapping has shown that many TF pairs preferentially bind composite arrangements with defined spacing/orientation, providing a biochemical basis for DNA‐facilitated/mediated/guided TF cooperativity [28]; for more details, see also [25]. Linked to this is the idea of kinetic cooperativity or residence‐time cooperativity. A typical example concerns the interaction between the glucocorticoid receptor (GR) and the architectural chromatin protein HMGB1. Using live‐cell FRET and FRAP, Agresti et al. (2005) showed that GR and HMGB1 do not form a detectable complex in the nucleoplasm. They interact only when bound to chromatin and this interaction reduces each partner's mobility, that is, it extends their residence time on chromatin [29]. Another study using fast single‐molecule tracking in living cells shows that the interaction of transcriptional coactivators with TF activator domains might increase TF dwell time on chromatin and that the length of the activation domains can control chromatin‐bound fractions and residence times [30]. Thus, binding of a TF can also stabilize binding of another TF molecule (of the same type or not) such that the promoter spends longer in a PIC‐competent configuration, increasing the probability that subsequent binding/recruitment steps occur before dissociation. It is worth emphasizing that, loosely speaking, classical cooperativity preferentially stabilizes the promoter/enhancer occupied state, whereas kinetic cooperativity stabilizes its persistence and productive use, even when TF‐promoter/enhancer occupancy is weakly or not cooperative.

Finally, in indirect cooperativity, the binding of multiple TFs near one another makes them compete with nucleosomes, fostering nucleosome‐mediated cooperativity, which has been documented in vivo and in vitro [31].

Transcriptional Synergy

Even if the recruitment of TF molecules to a promoter/enhancer is independent, multivalent interactions with a protein platform can foster sigmoidicity. In contrast to cooperativity, transcriptional synergy refers to the more‐than‐proportional increase in transcriptional output produced when several DNA‐bound TFs act together to recruit a macromolecular platform or the basal transcriptional machinery itself [32, 33]. In this view, each DNA‐bound activator TF contributes a favorable interaction energy to recruitment. These contributions are approximately additive in terms of free energy, but their effect on binding strength is multiplicative because of the relationship ΔG° = −RT ln K. Thus, if one activator–machinery contact corresponds to an association constant K, n similar contacts can generate, loosely speaking, an effective affinity proportional to K n (disregarding a series of lower order penalties). As a consequence, only highly occupied promoter/enhancer configurations would be efficient at recruiting macromolecular platforms and/or the transcriptional machinery itself [33].

On experimental grounds, it is known that TF activation domains and coactivators such as p300 (and Mediator) contain multiple interaction surfaces, so productive assemblies can be stabilized by many weak contacts with DNA‐bound molecules. Specifically, it has been proposed that p300 is an integrator of combinatorial TF inputs because single TF‐p300 interactions are insufficient to drive chromatin binding and activation [34]. This is a typical example of synergistic/multivalent/velcro binding. In yeast, single‐molecule fluorescence work has shown that Mediator occupancy is nonlinear with respect to activator concentration, which does not result from the cooperative binding of activators to DNA but from the simultaneous interaction of multiple DNA‐bound molecules with Mediator [35]. An interesting example involving NF‐κB/RelA illustrates the continuum between cooperativity and synergy. Clusters of κB sites, including weak sites that are poorly active in isolation, can collectively enhance RelA occupancy in vivo, indicating a form of multisite or context‐dependent cooperative recruitment. However, the transcriptional consequence is better described as synergistic: Combinations of weak and strong κB sites drive reporter and endogenous gene expression more effectively than expected from individual sites alone [36].

The beta‐interferon enhancer action remains a useful mechanistic example of synergistic behavior [32] and references therein. Specifically, viral induction of IFNB1 requires the assembly of NF‐κB, IRF‐family factors, ATF‐2/c‐Jun, and HMG I(Y) on a compact enhancer, followed by recruitment of CBP/p300, chromatin modifiers/remodelers, and the basal transcriptional machinery [37]. Structural studies have shown that the complete enhanceosome architecture can generate a continuous nucleoprotein surface supposed to be competent for coactivator recruitment [38]. This illustrates why transcriptional synergy (irrespective of whether the cis‐regulatory elements were bound cooperatively or not) relies on the creation of interaction surfaces that recruit coactivators, remodelers, and other macromolecular complexes.

Whatever the source of sigmoidicity, in a Hill‐like formalism, the steepness parameter n summarizes how strongly promoter output increases once cooperative/synergistic interactions take place. V s reflects a throughput ceiling due to biophysical and biochemical constraints [39].

This promoter/enhancer‐centered view also helps interpret genomic‐balance observations. A TF does not act in isolation: it competes or cooperates with cofactors. Consequently, changing the dosage of one TF could produce more‐than‐proportional effects on target genes because the dosage perturbation alters the stoichiometry of multimolecular activating or repressive complexes. In such cases, the relevant biochemical variable is not only absolute TF abundance, but the ‘ratio’ of the TF to its partners and to the number of genomic binding opportunities. Indeed, the relationship between a TF and its predicted or validated targets from co‐expression data is not always preserved under, say, aneuploidy. For instance, a positive regulator can produce an inverse (negative) effect when its own dosage is changed in an imbalanced genome due to such stoichiometric alterations [10, 40, 41]. Similarly, recent rapid‐depletion experiments in yeast have shown that removing individual TFs can produce both up‐ and down‐regulation among known target genes [42]. Rather than being paradoxical, this outcome is consistent with the idea that the sign and magnitude of a TF effect depend on promoter context and quantitative relationships with other regulatory molecules.

Potential Promoter‐Proximal Kinetic Checkpoints

Many metazoan genes accumulate RNA polymerase II in a promoter‐proximal paused state, creating a checkpoint between initiation and productive elongation. Transition into productive elongation depends on P‐TEFb/CDK9‐dependent phosphorylation events. The key pause‐release substrates include elongation regulators such as NELF and DSIF/SPT5, while Pol II CTD (carboxy terminal domain) phosphorylation accompanies productive elongation and contributes to downstream processes, including RNA processing [43]. This process is supported by in vivo data in Drosophila [44]. From the perspective of thresholds, a relevant control variable could be the effective, promoter‐engaged pool and activity of P‐TEFb rather than its total abundance. A substantial fraction of P‐TEFb can be sequestered in an inactive reservoir (e.g., the 7SK snRNP), and switching between inactive 7SK‐associated P‐TEFb and active, chromatin‐associated P‐TEFb complexes can become a regulatory step. For instance, recruitment factors such as BRD4 and the super elongation complex (SEC) can facilitate the reconstitution of active P‐TEFb from 7SK‐related or dissociated states, so that local recruitment/assembly can control functional availability [45]. This does not guarantee switch‐like behavior but might contribute to it when (i) productive output is primarily limited by the transition to elongation; (ii) the control variable is the promoter‐engaged P‐TEFb rather than total P‐TEFb and (iii) productive release requires satisfying multiple coordinated requirements such as availability of active P‐TEFb, efficient recruitment at the paused complex, and sufficient kinase action on the relevant substrates.

Chromatin, Enhancer–Promoter Communication and Nuclear Hubs

Chromatin conformation can not only threshold gene expression but also create a form of memory. This is so because the DNA elements required for promoter activity can be at least partly occluded by nucleosomes and higher‐order chromatin packing. Pioneer TFs [46] and ATP‐dependent remodelers lower these barriers by promoting local chromatin opening that exposes hidden DNA motifs. In mammals, SWI/SNF‐family remodelers are central players in pioneering and chromatin opening. They promote local decompaction and cooperate with other remodelers to reposition or evict nucleosomes and stabilize accessible states [47]. The nonlinear aspect arises because chromatin opening relies on a series of events. If a promoter/enhancer must undergo several coupled transitions (nucleosome displacement/repositioning, deposition/removal of histone marks, etc.), as we will see below, then transcriptional output can remain low until enough, if not all, of these requirements are satisfied and then rapidly increase. Such intricate processes can produce reinforcing or antagonistic loops. In short, chromatin is not only an accessibility filter, it can also act as a dynamic regulatory module allowing or enhancing the conversion of graded inputs into threshold‐like responses.

Chromatin Accessibility and Memory

Once chromatin is transcription‐permissive, histone PTMs contribute further to promoter competence through the coordinated action of writers and readers. p300/CBP are prominent writers that deposit marks such as H3K27ac (and related acetylations) at active promoters and other regulatory regions. As mentioned above, recent papers discuss their ability to function as interaction‐rich scaffolds that integrate TF inputs [34, 48]. In parallel, SET1/MLL‐family complexes are major writers of the H3K4 methylation system (H3K4me1/2/3). H3K4me3 is linked to specific phases of transcription, including initiation and promoter‐proximal control [49]. Interestingly, crosstalk can make these marks mutually reinforcing. For instance, histone H3 acetylation can increase accessibility of the H3 tail to methyl readers and writers, thereby coupling H3 acetylation to MLL1‐dependent methylation. This has been described as an acetylation‐mediated chromatin switch [50]. More generally, chromatin opening and histone acetylation can expose DNA and nucleosomal surfaces that favor the recruitment or activity of additional chromatin regulators. Acetylated chromatin can also recruit bromodomain‐containing readers, such as BRD4 and related factors, which may help retain coactivators and transcriptional machinery at active regulatory regions. In specific contexts, acetylation writing and reading can participate in positive‐feedback mechanisms involving p300 and acetyl‐lysine readers [51]. Thus, reader–writer–remodeler feedback loops provide a plausible route to threshold‐like responses when the reinforcing interactions are sufficiently strong (Figure 3). In such an architecture, transcription would remain low until an input S is sufficient to push the system across an opening/maintenance threshold [52].

Such chromatin transactions could also give rise to hysteresis, which implies that for the same input S, the promoter can be ON or OFF depending on its recent history (Figure 4). Hysteresis is expected to occur when the open chromatin state relaxes back to the closed state sufficiently slowly relative to changes in the input S. Slow reversal can reflect delayed nucleosome reassembly or slow re‐establishment of repressive chromatin organization [53]. In such cases, the input level required to initially open chromatin and establish a permissive state is higher than the input required to maintain that state once it has been established. Thus, for intermediate values of S, the promoter can remain responsive during a decreasing‐input trajectory but remain OFF during an increasing‐input trajectory, producing a history‐dependent behavior (Figure 4). In the context of HI, a reduced regulator dosage may be limiting for establishing a permissive chromatin state than for maintaining one already in place, explaining why expression of some genes can be more affected than that of others. Hysteresis provides a form of molecular memory [54].

Hysteresis in chromatin‐mediated promoter activation. (A) A sufficiently strong input S drives the transition to an open/ON state with high transcriptional output. If the reverse transition is slower than the initial activation step, the promoter state at a given input S would depend not only on the current input level but also on its recent history. (B) This history dependence could produce, in principle, a hysteresis loop. During an increasing‐input trajectory, the promoter switches ON only when S exceeds a relatively high activation threshold, Son. During a decreasing‐input trajectory, there is a high probability of remaining in an ON state until S falls below a lower deactivation threshold, Soff. (C) Hysteresis can act as a form of molecular memory. If input S briefly drops after the promoter has entered a permissive chromatin state, slow chromatin reversal can allow the promoter to remain accessible, or to reactivate rapidly when the input returns. The duration and functional impact of this memory depend on how long the permissive chromatin state persists relative to the timescale of input variations.

Within our phenomenological analysis, chromatin opening and mark accumulation are expected to lower the activation threshold. This is consistent with promoter nucleosome‐depleted regions being central determinants of access for TFs, cofactors, and Pol II [55]. The effective steepness of the response would be expected to increase with the strength of reinforcing loops (writer–reader–remodeler feedback). The ceiling may be constrained by how fast those open promoters can cycle through productive initiation events under a permissive chromatin state [55].

The biochemical action of chromatin regulators can be distributed over many loci but the phenotypic consequences of dysregulation can be locus‐ or tissue‐specific. Rubinstein‐Taybi syndrome caused by CREBBP or EP300 variants is a paradigm of dosage sensitivity affecting histone acetyltransferase/coactivator function [56]. Because CBP/p300 proteins both write acetylation marks and act as multivalent coactivator scaffolds, partial loss of activity can lower local acetylation, weaken BRD4/cofactor recruitment, and shift a subset of enhancers away from a permissive threshold rather than uniformly reducing transcription. This locus‐selective view is supported by genome‐wide analyses showing that partial or complete reduction of CBP/p300 activity does not affect all genes equally. The specific responses are strongly shaped by enhancer/promoter context and probably by the availability of compensatory regulatory inputs [57].

Erasers provide a complementary component of this framework. H3K27 demethylases such as UTX/KDM6A and DNA demethylation enzymes such as TET1/2/3 can help remove repressive chromatin or DNA‐methylation states. In developmental disorders, the importance of such eraser activities is illustrated by KDM6A in Kabuki syndrome and by the broader class of Mendelian disorders of the epigenetic machinery [58, 59]. Several heterozygous mutations of TET3 have been predicted to result in loss of function, suggesting HI. However, for some variants, the possibility that a truncated protein could be produced and exert a dominant‐negative effect cannot be excluded [60]. Thus, at present, direct evidence that eraser‐dependent mechanisms generate non‐additivity is more limited than for some writer‐reader systems.

Other examples provided by human genetics show that a heterozygous loss of genes encoding chromatin‐related proteins can affect the expression of only a subset of loci. For instance, CHD8, an ATP‐dependent chromatin remodeler implicated in autism, has dosage‐sensitive transcriptional and chromatin‐accessibility targets in pluripotency and neural differentiation models. Thus, CHD8 heterozygosity produces cell‐type‐ and stage‐dependent effects during cortical development [61, 62]. ARID1B and other BAF‐complex genes similarly illustrate how partial loss of a chromatin‐remodeling component can perturb selected developmental outputs rather than simply reduce global transcription [63].

Enhancer–Promoter Contacts and the 3D Genome

In metazoans, enhancers and promoters frequently reside within 3D neighborhoods organized through chromatin loops and domain boundaries [4]. The cohesin complex (a protein ring) and CTCF play an essential role in this process [4]. Dosage deficiencies in CTCF and cohesin components alter genome organization and gene expression and can lead to neurodevelopmental phenotypes (among others) [64]. Experimental evidence involving relocated enhancers across several chromosomal positions relative to a fixed promoter in mouse embryonic stem cells shows that the transcriptional effect of an enhancer depends on enhancer–promoter contact probabilities via a sigmoidal scaling relationship [65]. Moreover, architectural insulation by CTCF can in principle change promoter sensitivity in ways consistent with a nonlinear coupling between contact probability and transcription, and it has also been shown that enhancer strength influences sensitivity to CTCF‐mediated insulation [65]. Recent work supports the idea that cohesin‐mediated 3D contacts (together with distance constraints) can tune enhancer effects on the expression of genes with strong distal enhancers (> 50 kb away) [66]. Other analyses show that long‐range enhancer‐controlled genes are more sensitive to cohesin depletion compared with short‐range cases, consistent with a model in which cohesin/CTCF‐dependent organization is often stronger for some long‐range or insulated loci, which makes productive encounters rare [67]. Besides, it is known that tissue‐specific genes are recruited to hubs (see below), fostering cooperative interactions among multiple enhancers and promoters. However, recent work using a B cell leukemia cell line shows that CTCF depletion can impair hub formation while gene expression changes remain modest. This can be explained by rewired enhancer–promoter interactions, suggesting that contact multiplicity matters and that different 3D architectures can produce similar expression levels [68]. This “contact multiplicity as cooperative inputs” notion implies that once a promoter engages enough regulatory inputs in a time window, downstream steps can be driven more effectively than by any single contact.

In sum, by forming boundaries and anchoring cohesin‐dependent loops, CTCF can reduce inappropriate enhancer‐promoter contacts, favor contacts within insulated neighborhoods, and thereby modulate the multiplicity of regulatory contacts available to a promoter. Because transcription scales nonlinearly with contact probability [65], even modest CTCF/cohesin‐dependent changes in contact frequency can have large effects when a promoter operates in the steep part of the nonlinear input–output relationship. This view is consistent with the neurodevelopmental phenotypes mentioned above caused by dosage deficiencies in CTCF and cohesin‐related proteins [64].

Transcriptional Hubs and Condensates

There is growing evidence that hubs containing strong regulatory loci, particularly super‐enhancers and highly active promoters, enriched in Mediator, BRD4, Pol II and TFs, often display properties consistent with biomolecular condensation ([69] and references therein). From our perspective, their formation and stability can be thresholded: multivalent interactions (e.g., via TF activation domains, coactivator intrinsically disordered regions, bromodomain–acetyl‐lysine binding, Pol II CTD interactions) could produce, in principle, a regime in which below a critical effective concentration condensate assembly is unstable and short‐lived but becomes more stable and persistent above it (Figure 5). In other words, condensate nucleation would require reaching a critical local density of components, and a small increase in TF/coactivator availability can cause a drastic increase in the probability of forming and maintaining a transcriptionally competent hub. This nucleation‐like behavior was proposed as a phase‐separation model for transcriptional control, linking super‐enhancers to high cooperativity/Hill‐like behavior [70, 71].

The figure illustrates how hub/condensate assemblies can provide a context‐dependent mechanism for threshold‐like gene activation. (A) Below a critical local concentration of TFs and cofactors, enhancers and promoters from different genes remain mostly dispersed. (B) When the local concentration of several players increases beyond a threshold, enhancers and promoters can become enriched within a shared transcriptional hub or condensate, thereby increasing the effective probability of productive enhancer–promoter communication and transcription.

Linked to the above, once a hub forms, it can further enrich the very components that promote productive transcription—Mediator, BRD4, Pol II, associated factors and enhancers—thereby generating positive feedback. Recent quantitative work shows that acetylated chromatin provides a platform that enables BRD4 clustering below the concentration that would be required for condensation. Once acetylation and BRD4 engagement pass a locus‐specific threshold, BRD4 assemblies become much more likely and persistent, amplifying the recruitment of the transcriptional machinery [69]. Another example is provided by YAP, which can form sub‐micron condensates at endogenous levels in response to signaling/mechanical cues. Such condensates are stabilized by partner TFs such as TEAD1, recruit coactivators such as BRD4, and are enriched at active (super) enhancers [72].

Finally, condensates concentrate TFs, various cofactors, and the enzymes that modify them through PTMs, increasing their local concentrations. This could favor a phenomenon called zero‐order ultrasensitivity because the concentration of such proteins can partially saturate the modifying and demodifying enzymes, leading to very steep switch‐like responses [1, 73].

Present evidence suggests that condensates/hubs are a mechanistically plausible source of thresholding and ultrasensitivity rather than a universal mechanistic explanation [74]. Indeed, some authors note that condensates can be activating, neutral, or even inhibitory depending on context [70] and references therein.

Systems‐Level Nonlinearities

Before closing, it is worth emphasizing that TFs and chromatin regulators may also generate nonlinearity at the level of entire gene‐regulatory networks, not only at individual promoters or enhancers. TFs can directly activate their own expression and/or form cross‐repressing modules that sustain or repress competing transcriptional outcomes [75, 76]. These architectures are particularly relevant during cell fate decisions, where initially graded differences in TF concentration can eventually be converted into stable, mutually exclusive outcomes.

Bistability provides a way in which nonlinear regulatory circuits can convert graded differences in regulator abundance into discrete, self‐maintained responses. In one simple architecture, a master regulator activates its own expression through positive feedback. If this feedback is sufficiently strong, the circuit can generate two stable states separated by an unstable threshold: below this threshold, regulator levels decay toward a stable low‐expression state, whereas above it, self‐activation reinforces regulator expression and drives the system toward a stable high‐expression state associated with a different cellular outcome. Another architecture involves mutual repression between two regulators. This generates two alternative stable attractors separated by an unstable boundary. Thus, both configurations can create switch‐like regulatory decisions in which initially modest quantitative differences are amplified into stable alternative states [76, 77].

The molecular nonlinearities discussed throughout this paper can be embedded within higher‐order regulatory circuits. In this setting, halving a regulator dosage can weaken autoregulatory reinforcement or cross‐inhibitory effects, reduce the ability to cross a required threshold, or shrink the parameter space over which the “correct” state is stable. This framework can explain why HI of some TF and chromatin regulators can produce selective developmental phenotypes: only those cells with target circuits operating near a network threshold will be strongly affected. Thus, HI can also arise from disrupted network‐level decision‐making. Systems‐level effects provide a complementary explanation linking nonlinearities to genetic dominance [76, 77].

Conclusions

Nonlinearity in transcription regulation can emerge from various sources. Their combined contributions can transform graded inputs into switch‐like transcriptional outputs. In a phenomenological Hill‐type description, the parameters provide a useful way to summarize this complexity. The region around Ks defines the input range over which transcription becomes strongly responsive. Different target loci controlled by the same regulator may therefore have different effective Ks values. This can help explain why some of them remain buffered after a reduction in TF dosage, whereas others are strongly affected [16].

Although less firmly established, co‐transcriptional processing and post‐transcriptional steps [78] may provide additional sources of nonlinearity in gene‐expression output. Indeed, capping, splicing, and 3′‐end cleavage/polyadenylation could act as gates by determining what fraction of nascent transcripts becomes export‐competent mRNA. This deserves experimental testing.

Nonlinear steps described above do not act in isolation. Interestingly, when moderately sigmoidal stages are arranged in series, their transition zones can align such that a perturbation in the upstream input traverses the sensitive range of several successive modules simultaneously. Under these conditions, this cascading can sharpen the overall input–output relationship beyond what any single step would produce.

Sigmoidal behavior and bistability can provide complementary and non‐exclusive explanations for incomplete penetrance (not all individuals carrying a disease‐causing variant display the associated phenotype). After a reduction in gene dosage, when the physiological level of a regulator lies near a transition zone, small fluctuations that would remain buffered under normal conditions can be amplified in some individuals and lead to marked phenotypic consequences because of the influence of modifier genes or the environmental. This can also lead to cell‐to‐cell variability. In some cells the threshold may be reached in time, thereby leading to their engagement in the appropriate developmental pathway or the expected physiological response, whereas others could fail to do so. If the proportion of cells responding properly is below a specific threshold, an abnormal phenotype can appear.

Before closing, it is worth mentioning that HI can be conditional, being revealed only when physiological challenges increase the demand placed on a dosage‐sensitive regulator. Steroidogenic factor 1 (SF‐1/NR5A1) provides a clear example. In mice, Nr5a1 heterozygosity produces a relatively preserved/compensated adrenal function under basal conditions, but stress exposes the insufficiency. Indeed, Sf‐1 +/− animals have abnormal adrenal development and a blunted corticosterone response when subjected to restraint or fasting stress [79]. In short, various physiological cues such as stress, fasting, overnutrition, high/low glucose or high or low‐fat feeding can alter the biochemical environment in which TFs and chromatin regulators operate. In the present framework, such signals and cues can alter the effective Ks or the maximal attainable output of a critical subset of targets leading to HI. In addition, we have focused here on cases where only a wild‐type allele is left in the heterozygote. However, a genotype involving one wild‐type allele and one hypomorphic allele can behave functionally as HI. For instance, several heterozygous NR5A1 missense variants identified in cases of 46, XY differences of sex development or 46, XX ovarian insufficiency lead to proteins with partial transcriptional activity in reporter assays. Importantly, for some variants, co‐transfection experiments did not detect inhibition of the wild‐type SF‐1 protein, arguing against a dominant‐negative mechanism. These observations support the idea that partial reduction of total TF activity (wild‐type + hypomorph) may be insufficient to reach the activation threshold required at specific loci [80, 81].

Genetics and biochemistry provide complementary information. Genetics identifies which regulators are dosage‐sensitive and which phenotypes result from a reduced gene product dosage. Biochemistry shows that HI is not based on a single mechanism.
