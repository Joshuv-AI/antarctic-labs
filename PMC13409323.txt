Antipsychotic Drug Interactions and Short‐Term Mortality Risk in People With Dementia: A National Cohort Study
Authors: Edward C.Y. Lau, Grace H. Shim, Chin Hang Yiu, Sarah N. Hilmer, Yun‐Hee Jeon, Weisi Chen, Christine Y. Lu, Edwin C.K. Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antipsychotics are often prescribed in the management of behaviors and psychological symptoms of dementia. While the potential harms of antipsychotic use in people with dementia have been established, little is known about the risk associated with antipsychotic drug–drug interactions (DDIs). This study aims to examine the patterns of antipsychotic DDIs and their association with 90‐day mortality among people with dementia using antipsychotics. This retrospective cohort study utilized Australian nationwide linked 2021 Census and Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme data. People with dementia aged ≥ 65 years who initiated a single antipsychotic (risperidone, quetiapine, aripiprazole, haloperidol, or olanzapine) between August 2021 and July 2022 were included. Antipsychotic DDIs were defined by Micromedex® Drug‐Reax, and were stratified by number, severity, and pharmacological mechanism. Mortality risk was assessed using modified Poisson regression and confounding was addressed with overlapped weighting. Among 9064 incident antipsychotic users, 6913 (76.3%) were exposed to ≥ 1 DDI, with the majority of them classed as “major” severity (n = 6709). Nervous system drugs were the most commonly interacting agents. When compared to those without antipsychotic interactions, those with ≥ 1 DDI had a 43% increased risk of 90‐day mortality (adjusted relative risk [aRR]: 1.43; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.22–1.69), while those with ≥ 2 DDIs had an 80% increased risk (aRR: 1.80; 95% CI: 1.49–2.17). Overall, antipsychotic DDIs are common among older adults with dementia and are associated with increased risk of 90‐day mortality. As such, medication review and regular monitoring is important when initiating antipsychotics in this vulnerable population.

Dementia is a complex neurodegenerative disease with growing prevalence, currently accounting for more than 57 million cases worldwide and 10 million new cases annually. 1 In Australia, dementia is now the leading cause of death, affecting an estimated 433,000 people and contributing significantly to the health burden among older adults. 2 Beyond cognitive decline, up to 90% of people with dementia may also experience changed behaviors, often referred to as behaviors and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD). 3 While non‐pharmacological strategies are preferred in the management of changed behaviors, antipsychotics may be prescribed when non‐pharmacological treatment fails. 4

Antipsychotics are used by approximately one‐fifth of people with dementia, ranking as the third most commonly prescribed subclass of psychotropic among older people with dementia living in Australia. 5 However, the use of antipsychotics is associated with serious adverse events, including stroke, falls, and mortality. 6 , 7 The risk of adverse events is influenced by other clinical and sociodemographic factors; for example, those of older age or with more comorbidities are at additional risk of hospitalization and mortality. 8

Due to the high prevalence of multimorbidity in this population, 9 polypharmacy is common in people with dementia. 10 , 11 The risk of antipsychotic‐related adverse events may be further exacerbated by the concurrent use of other medications. 8 As medication burden increases, so does the likelihood of antipsychotic drug–drug interactions (DDIs), which have been shown to affect more than a quarter of antipsychotic users with dementia in previous research. 12 While one study reported that such DDIs increase the risk of mortality in residential aged care settings, its generalizability was limited by a small sample size and exclusive inclusion of residential aged care home residents. 13 To date, no population‐based cohort study has investigated the risk of antipsychotic DDIs in people with dementia. Therefore, this study aims to investigate (i) patterns of antipsychotic DDIs among incident antipsychotic users with dementia, and (ii) the mortality risk associated with these exposures.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The reporting of this study follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for cohort studies. 14 This study was deemed to have negligible risk and was exempted from ethical approval by The University of Sydney Human Research Ethics Committee.

Data sources

This cohort study utilized nationwide data sourced from the Person Level Integrated Data Asset (PLIDA) provided by the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS). 15 The study employed linked datasets from the 2021 Census, the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS), and the National Death Index. The 2021 Census was conducted on August 10, 2021, and provides information on the total population of Australia; variables in the present study that were derived from the 2021. Census include education level, selected self‐reported medical conditions, assistance with activities of daily living, language spoken at home, living conditions, remoteness, country of birth, and Socio‐Economic Indexes for Areas (SEIFA) (measured according to the Index of Relative Socioeconomic Advantage and Disadvantage). The PBS provides access to subsidized prescription medications for Australian citizens, permanent residents, and overseas visitors from certain countries. 16 , 17 Each PBS item code corresponds to a specific medication, dosage, and formulation, and was matched to the corresponding Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) Classification code. Combination medications were broken down into individual active ingredients in the present study. The National Death Index provided information on the date of death.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study included older adults aged 65 years and older with dementia who were incident users of antipsychotic medications. Dementia was defined as either a self‐ or proxy‐reported diagnosis in the 2021 Census and/or the dispensing of any antidementia medication (including acetylcholinesterase inhibitors or memantine) between 2016 and 2021. Incident antipsychotic users were defined as individuals who had risperidone, quetiapine, aripiprazole, haloperidol, or olanzapine dispensed during the 12‐month period following the 2021 Census (August 1, 2021–July 31, 2022). These five agents were selected as they are the antipsychotics recommended for the management of BPSD in Australian clinical guidelines, 18 contributing to over 95% of antipsychotics dispensed to people with dementia. 12 A class‐based washout was conducted where individuals with any antipsychotic dispensing in the 12 months prior to the study period (August 1, 2020–July 31, 2021) were excluded to ensure the cohort comprised new users. Individuals dispensed multiple antipsychotics during the study period were excluded to avoid confounding from within‐class drug interactions and to account for potential medication switching. Individuals using multiple antipsychotics were likely to have different therapeutic indications than those with only one. In addition, individuals receiving palliative care were excluded to reduce confounding arising from differences in underlying illness severity and treatment intent, while individuals with a date of death preceding their index date were excluded as these records likely reflect administrative data anomalies. The study flowchart is provided in Figure S1 .

Antipsychotic drug interactions and mortality

Antipsychotic DDIs were defined as the dispensing of an interacting medication 30 days before and/or after the incident dispensing of the index antipsychotic during the study period (August 1, 2021–July 31, 2022), as illustrated in Figure S2A . 19 For clarity, the present study excludes people with multiple different antipsychotics dispensed in the study period; thus, antipsychotic‐antipsychotic combinations were excluded. The index date for individuals exposed to antipsychotic DDIs was either the dispensing date of the antipsychotic or the interacting medication, whichever occurred last. The index date for individuals unexposed to an antipsychotic DDI was the date of the antipsychotic dispensing date. Figure S2B illustrates the possible scenarios for classifying antipsychotic drug interactions and the determination of index dates. 19 The follow‐up period was defined as the 90 days following the index date or until death, whichever occurred first. All‐cause mortality was defined as death from any cause during the 90‐day follow‐up period.

Antipsychotic DDIs were identified using Micromedex® Drug‐Reax, 20 a comprehensive and widely used drug interaction database with high accuracy. 21 Only interactions classified as moderate, major, or contraindicated in severity were included in the analysis. Minor interactions were excluded due to their limited clinical significance and low likelihood of requiring substantial treatment modification. According to Micromedex, moderate interactions may worsen the patient's condition and/or require therapy adjustment; major interactions may be life‐threatening and/or require medical intervention; and contraindicated interactions indicate that concurrent use should be avoided in all circumstances. 20 Pharmacological mechanisms of antipsychotic DDIs were classified as pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic interactions. Pharmacokinetic interactions included those that alter the concentration of antipsychotics or their interacting drugs, such as those affecting clearance by Cytochrome P450 enzymes. Pharmacodynamic interactions included those that modify the clinical effects of the antipsychotic or their interacting drugs, such as additive sedative effects or QT prolongation.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics of the study population, as well as the incidence and severity of the interaction. The most common interacting drug classes identified using the ATC first‐level classification, as well as the five most frequently interacting drugs for both typical and atypical antipsychotics, were identified and reported. Incidence rates for all‐cause mortality were calculated separately for individuals with and without antipsychotic DDIs.

The associations between 90‐day mortality and four exposures were examined: (i) the presence of any antipsychotic DDI, (ii) the number of antipsychotic DDIs, (iii) the highest severity of antipsychotic DDI, and (iv) the pharmacological mechanism underlying the antipsychotic DDI. Relative risks of 90‐day all‐cause mortality were estimated using modified Poisson regression. Potential confounders were selected based on existing literature and clinical relevance. 12 , 13 These included age, sex, living arrangements (private dwelling with others, private dwelling alone or non‐private dwelling), SEIFA, country of birth, level of education, language spoken at home, requiring assistance with daily activities, remoteness, number of medications, and comorbidities assessed using the weighted Rx‐Risk score. 22 Rx‐Risk score, 22 a validated comorbidity index commonly used to adjust for confounding in observational studies utilizing medication claims data, and number of medications were included to account for differences in baseline disease severity and overall clinical complexity between exposure groups. PBS dispensings in the 90‐day period prior to the index date were used to calculate number of medications and weighted Rx‐Risk score. Definitions of these variables are provided in Table S1 . Confounding was addressed using overlapped weighting, which is a propensity score weighting method developed to balance the covariates between groups and to reduce the bias and variance created by extreme propensity scores. 23 Covariate balance between exposure groups after weighting was assessed using absolute standardized mean differences (SMD), with a threshold of ≤ 0.1 indicating adequate balance. 24 Propensity scores were estimated using generalized boosted models (GBM), 25 with separate models fitted for each specific interaction exposure definition. Overlap weights were subsequently derived from these propensity scores. In the outcome analyses, a robust sandwich variance estimator was used to account for the weighting. The balance of covariates was presented using Love plots in Figures S3–S6 . Detailed weighted baseline characteristics are provided in Tables S2–S5 . Survival was visualized using a cumulative incidence graph. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1.

Sensitivity analysis

In the primary analysis, the index date for individuals exposed to a DDI within 30 days following an incident antipsychotic dispensing was defined as the date of the interacting drug dispensing to minimize immortal time bias. However, to account for time‐related bias, we conducted a sensitivity analysis in which all individuals were assigned the incident antipsychotic dispensing date as the index date. In this analysis, individuals who were dispensed an interacting drug within the 30‐day period after incident antipsychotic use were reclassified into the no‐interaction group. The incident antipsychotic dispensing date was then uniformly applied as the index date for the entire cohort to test the robustness of the primary results. Furthermore, as distinct classes of antipsychotics exhibit varying interaction mechanisms and are prescribed in specific clinical situations, an additional subgroup analysis was performed. Antipsychotic exposures were stratified into three mutually exclusive categories: (i) risperidone, (ii) other atypical antipsychotics, and (iii) typical antipsychotics, to further explore differences in mortality risk across antipsychotic classes.

RESULTS

Cohort characteristics

Overall, 9064 incident users of a single antipsychotic agent with dementia were included in the final analytical sample. Majority of them initiated atypical antipsychotics, including risperidone, olanzapine, aripiprazole, and quetiapine (n = 7955; 87.8%). The median age of the participants was 84 (interquartile range [IQR]: 79–89), 5029 (55.5%) were female, and 7479 (83%) indicated they required assistance with activities for daily living. The median Rx‐Risk score and number of medications used in the 90‐day period before the index date were 10 (IQR: 8–12) and 8 (IQR: 5–11) respectively. Although most participants were living with other people in a private dwelling, over one‐third were residing in non‐private dwellings (n = 3517, 38.8%), such as residential aged care homes. A quarter of participants resided in areas that were the most socioeconomically disadvantaged (n = 2282, 25.2%). Detailed baseline characteristics are reported by number of interactions in Table 1 .

Antipsychotic drug interactions

Overall, 6913 (76.3%) incident antipsychotic users were exposed to at least one antipsychotic DDI. Among those with at least one antipsychotic DDI, the number of people with a single interaction (n = 3241/6913, 46.8%) was comparable to those with two or more (n = 3672, 53.1%). Nearly all participants exposed to an interaction had one that was classified as major severity (n = 6709, 97.0%). The majority of these individuals used atypical antipsychotics (n = 5931, 85.8%), with one‐seventh using typical antipsychotics (n = 982, 14.2%). Pharmacodynamic interactions were the most common underlying mechanisms (n = 6668, 96.5%), and 1891 of these individuals were also exposed to pharmacokinetic interactions. Of the incident antipsychotic users with at least one interaction, 245 (3.5%) were exposed exclusively to pharmacokinetic interactions.

Nervous system drugs (ATC N), such as antidepressants, antidementia drugs, and opioids, were the most commonly implicated drug classes in interactions involving both typical (n = 917; 93.3%) and atypical antipsychotics (n = 5443; 91.8%). Among atypical antipsychotic users, donepezil, mirtazapine, escitalopram, buprenorphine, and sertraline were the most commonly interacting drugs. Among typical antipsychotic users, morphine, oxycodone, metoclopramide, buprenorphine, and mirtazapine were the most commonly interacting drugs. Risk of mortality

The highest mortality rate among all drug interaction exposures was observed in those with contraindicated interactions (n = 252, 58.3%). Compared to those without any antipsychotic DDIs, antipsychotic users who had at least one interaction were at a 43.3% higher risk of 90‐day all‐cause mortality (adjusted relative risk [aRR] 1.43, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.22–1.69, P < 0.001) (Figure 1 , Table 3 ). When stratified by the number of antipsychotic DDIs, those with at least two interactions were 79.8% more likely to experience 90‐day mortality than those who had no interactions (aRR 1.80, 95% CI: 1.49–2.17, P < 0.001) while no statistically significant difference was observed in those with only one (aRR 1.15, 95% CI: 0.94–1.39, P = 0.171) (Figure 2 , Table 3 ). The estimated number needed to harm for at least one antipsychotic DDI was 23 (95% CI: 16.7–40) (Table S6 ).

Regarding interaction severity, major interactions were associated with a 25.3% increase in the risk of mortality (aRR: 1.25, 95% CI: 1.03–1.53, P = 0.026), while contraindicated interactions were associated with four times the risk of mortality (aRR: 3.99, 95% CI: 3.24–4.92, P < 0.001) compared to those with no or moderate interactions (Figure 3 , Table 3 ). Being exposed to pharmacodynamic (PD) interactions only was associated with an increased risk of mortality (aRR: 1.72, 95% CI: 1.42–2.08, P < 0.001), while no statistically significant difference was found in those with pharmacokinetic (PK) interactions (both PK interaction only or PK interaction with PD interaction) (Figure 4 , Table 3 ).

Sensitivity analysis

Similar results were observed when reclassifying individuals exposed to a DDI only in the 30‐day period after incident antipsychotic use into the no‐interaction group. Detailed results are reported in Table S7 . In the stratified sensitivity analysis, the mortality risk associated with DDIs was highest among typical antipsychotic users, with a similar trend observed in users of other atypical antipsychotics. Notably, among risperidone users, the presence of a DDI was not associated with an overall increase in all‐cause mortality; increased risk in this subgroup was only observed in those exposed to contraindicated DDIs. DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first population‐based cohort study to investigate the risk of mortality associated with antipsychotic drug interactions among people with dementia. Over three‐quarters of incident, antipsychotic users with dementia in our study were exposed to at least one interacting drug. In contrast, a previous study of residents of aged care homes reported that 46.0% of their cohort (n = 604) had at least one interacting medication with their antipsychotic. 13 This lower proportion may reflect differences in how interactions were defined, the inclusion of prevalent antipsychotic users rather than incident users, and a shorter window for capturing potential interactions.

The risk of 90‐day all‐cause mortality was 43% higher among those exposed to at least one antipsychotic DDI. The estimated number needed to harm was 23, calculated from the adjusted risk difference of 90‐day mortality; this indicates that one additional death would be observed for every 23 patients exposed to an antipsychotic DDI. The risk observed was lower than that observed in a previous study. 13 This difference is likely attributable to the longer follow‐up period used in that study and its focus on residential aged care settings, where residents tend to be frailer and have limited physiological reserve, making them more susceptible to adverse outcomes. Consistent with previous findings, 13 we also observed that a greater number of interactions was associated with an increased risk of mortality, demonstrating a dose–response relationship.

Most antipsychotic drug interactions identified in this study were classified as major in severity, indicating combinations that can place individuals at greater risk of potentially fatal effects and for which medical intervention is recommended. 20 The severity of interactions was strongly associated with the risk of mortality, where major interactions were associated with a 25% higher risk of mortality within 90 days, and individuals exposed to contraindicated interactions experienced nearly a fourfold increased risk of mortality.

The most frequently interacting agents were nervous system drugs (ATC N), including opioids, antidementia drugs, and antidepressants. These reflect the prescribing patterns in people with dementia, 5 where these agents are commonly prescribed to manage pain, cognitive, behavioral, and psychological symptoms of dementia. 26 However, concurrent use of these agents with an antipsychotic increases the risk of central nervous system depression and increased anticholinergic side effects. 20 Although antipsychotic use alone is associated with an increased risk of adverse cardiovascular events, 6 the vast majority (86.8%) of individuals exposed to at least one antipsychotic DDI in our study encountered combinations known to increase the risk of cardiovascular complications, including stroke, cardiac arrest, QT interval prolongation, and Torsades de Pointes. These interactions may further elevate the risk of serious adverse cardiac events in a population already at elevated baseline risk.

Among participants with at least one antipsychotic drug interaction, pharmacodynamic (PD) interactions were more common than pharmacokinetic (PK) interactions. Although this distribution likely reflects the predominance of PD interactions indexed within the Micromedex database, 20 PD interactions in the present study were associated with a 72% increased risk of mortality. In contrast, PK interactions did not demonstrate a statistically significant association with mortality compared to non‐users. This difference may be attributed to the smaller sample size within the PK‐only cohort, which potentially limited the statistical power required to detect an association. Furthermore, specific PK mechanisms, such as increased drug clearance, may reduce antipsychotic exposure, 20 potentially lowering the likelihood of antipsychotic‐related adverse events. Consequently, while our findings demonstrate a robust association between PD interactions and mortality, further research with larger cohorts is warranted to elucidate the clinical significance of pharmacokinetic interactions.

Clinical implications

The incidence of 90‐day mortality in people with dementia was high among antipsychotic users in the present study, which further reinforces the importance of non‐pharmacological interventions in the management of changed behaviors in people with dementia. 27 Such approaches should be implemented before antipsychotic therapy is considered, especially when there is no immediate risk of harm. 27 Antipsychotics should be reserved for situations in which non‐pharmacological strategies have proven insufficient, 27 and clinical guidelines recommend using the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration possible, ideally not exceeding 12 weeks. 18 Shared decision‐making with patients and caregivers is essential to weigh the risks and benefits of treatment, particularly in the context of comorbidities and polypharmacy.

Polypharmacy is common in people with dementia, 10 , 11 and is associated with an increased risk of DDIs. 28 The present study found that antipsychotic DDIs further increase the risk of mortality among antipsychotic users with dementia. This elevated risk may be partially driven by cumulative anticholinergic burden. Previous studies have shown that up to 15% of people with dementia are exposed to a high baseline anticholinergic burden. 11 The use of an antipsychotic with strong anticholinergic properties, such as olanzapine and quetiapine, 29 combined with other interacting medications, can compound this burden and lead to poorer therapeutic outcomes. These findings strongly reinforce established geriatric prescribing guidelines, which suggest limiting the concurrent use of multiple psychotropic and anticholinergic agents. 30 , 31 Therefore, a comprehensive medication history must be carefully considered when prescribing antipsychotics. Medication reviews prior to initiation and during treatment can help identify potentially inappropriate medication use and assist in the reduction of DDIs. 18 , 32 In the case of unavoidable antipsychotic interactions, regular monitoring during treatment is recommended. 18

Strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first nationwide cohort study to examine the incidence of antipsychotic DDIs among older adults with dementia and their association with mortality. The large, nationally representative cohort, including individuals across all care settings, enhances the generalizability of the findings. To minimize prevalent user and immortal time biases, the study included only new users of antipsychotics during the specified study period, with follow‐up initiated from individual index dates. Antipsychotic DDIs were defined with Micromedex, a highly accurate database. 21 The risk of mortality was adjusted using propensity score weighting, achieving adequate covariate balance between exposure groups and across various antipsychotic DDI exposures, thereby strengthening the validity of the results.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the definition of dementia involved self‐ or proxy‐reported dementia diagnoses, which may include individuals without a confirmed clinical diagnosis. Furthermore, the PBS dataset provides information only on dispensed medications; therefore, it is not possible to ascertain actual treatment adherence. Additionally, data regarding dosage, indication, and duration of treatment were unavailable. While the PBS covers the majority of medications used in Australia, 17 private prescriptions and over‐the‐counter medications are not captured by the system and were therefore excluded from the present study. While these factors may contribute to group misclassification, such errors are likely to bias the results toward the null. In addition, our findings are likely influenced by confounding by indication or quality of care. Typical antipsychotics are often prescribed in more acute clinical situations and are not recommended first line in Australian guidelines for older people with BPSD, 30 creating a non‐causal pathway that may explain the higher mortality risk observed in this subgroup compared to atypical antipsychotic users. Conversely, while we excluded palliative care patients to minimize confounding by indication due to fundamental differences in goals of care and underlying mortality risk, this necessary exclusion limits the generalizability of our findings to end‐of‐life populations. While the risk of immortal time bias was considered and managed by adjusting the index date for those who received an interacting agent within 30 days of incident antipsychotic dispensing, and supported by a sensitivity analysis, some residual time‐related bias may remain. This residual bias is likely anti‐conservative. Furthermore, although we adjusted for a comprehensive range of sociodemographic and clinical variables, residual confounding by unmeasured factors remains possible. This may have contributed to an anti‐conservative bias. Lastly, while all‐cause mortality is an important clinical endpoint, we were unable to ascertain specific clinical outcomes, such as arrhythmias, falls, and hospitalizations, due to data limitations. Future studies utilizing linked clinical datasets are needed to explore these specific endpoints.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, antipsychotic drug interactions are common in incident antipsychotic users with dementia, and are associated with increased risk of 90‐day mortality. These findings highlight the need for judicious use of antipsychotics and the importance of regular comprehensive medication review and monitoring when antipsychotics are indicated.
