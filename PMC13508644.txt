High-risk single-refractory and double-refractory chronic lymphocytic leukemia: feasibility and impact of alloHCT
Authors: Joseph Kauer, Isabelle Krämer, Patrick Derigs, Maximilian Felix Blank, Sascha Dietrich, Julia Meissner, Niklas Kehl, Mirco Julian Friedrich, Maria-Luisa Schubert, Anthony Dick Ho, Anita Schmitt, Guido Kobbe, Carsten Müller-Tidow, Michael Schmitt, Peter Dreger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Patients with CLL with adverse genetics refractory to cBTKi or BCL2i or both, have a poor prognosis independent of prior chemoimmunotherapy.•AlloHCT is feasible and may overcome disease refractoriness in this population, but its exact benefit and timing remains to be determined.

Introduction

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) mostly shows an indolent behavior without significant impact on life expectancy in most patients. However, a subgroup of patients faces an aggressive course, characterized by resistance against one or both of today’s standard treatment agent classes, that is, covalent Bruton kinase inhibitors (cBTKi) and B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL2) inhibitors (BCL2i). cBTKi/BCL2i resistance is often associated with adverse genetic aberrations, specifically deletions and/or mutations of the TP53 gene, and a complex karyotype (CK).

Accordingly, current National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines suggest the use of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-engineered T cells (CART) or allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (alloHCT) for patients with disease refractory to both inhibitor classes (double-refractory [DR]),1, 2, 3, 4 and in those who are refractory to 1 inhibitor class coincidental with TP53 abnormalities or CK (single-refractory high-risk genetics [SRHR]).5,6 However, robust evidence proving the poor outlook of patients with DR disease is limited,7,8 and information on the exact prognosis of patients with unfavorable genetics refractory to a single inhibitor class is even poorer.9 Moreover, although registry studies suggest that alloHCT can be effective in patients exposed to cBTKi and/or BCL2i,10, 11, 12 it is still unclear whether that also applies to patients with SRHR and DR features, respectively.

Before this background, the primary aims of this study were to assess the outcome of patients with SRHR CLL compared with those with DR CLL, and to investigate the feasibility and therapeutic potential of alloHCT in these high-risk populations. The results suggest that patients with SRHR CLL and DR CLL, respectively, both have an unfavorable prognosis, which might be improved with alloHCT. It remains to be shown, however, that alloHCT is as effective in patients with SRHR or DR CLL as it is in patients with less advanced disease.

Patients and methods

Patient eligibility and study design

Eligible for this retrospective single-center analysis were all patients who had either a CLL refractory to a cBTKi or a BCL2i and at the same time a proven TP53 abnormality (mutation and/or deletion) or a CK with at least 3 cytogenetic aberrations (SRHR), or who were refractory to both cBTKi and BCL2i (DR). Refractoriness to cBTKi or BCL2i was defined as progression or nonresponse while being on the drug. Candidate patients for this study were identified from an electronic institutional database (DataWareHouse) using the search terms “CLL” plus “venetoclax” plus (“ibrutinib” or “acalabrutinib” or “zanubrutinib” or “pirtobrutinib”) plus “2015 to 2026.” Eligible patients were selected from this pool by screening electronic patient reports for meeting the eligibility criteria. Baseline, treatment, and follow-up data were retrieved from electronic medical records.

The primary end point was event-free survival (EFS) from SRHR stratum entry (defining events relapse/progression, transformation, and death). Secondary end points were OS from stratum entry (SRHR and DR, respectively), and outcomes after alloHCT. SRHR stratum entry was defined as coincidence of newly developed cBTKi or BCL2i refractoriness and first detection of a high risk-defining genetic abnormality, whichever occurred last. DR stratum entry was defined newly developed BCL2i refractoriness after a previous cBTKi refractoriness or vice versa. An exploratory end point was OS from stratum entry and time of transplantation, respectively, stratified by undergoing alloHCT or not. Data analysis was approved by the institutional review board.

AlloHCT was performed according to institutional standards from matched related or unrelated donors using intermediate-dose total body irradiation, fludarabine and antithymocyte globulin for conditioning and a calcineurine inhibitor or short-course methotrexate platform for graft-versus-host disease prophylaxis.13 CART therapy was performed with the third-generation CD19 CART heidagen-lecleucel within a clinical trial.14 One patient was treated with heidagen-lecleucel outside of the trial. Although assignment to alloHCT did not follow a fixed protocol, eligible patients were recommended to undergo transplantation as soon as the SRHR (or DR) criteria were met and a response to the next line of therapy was achieved according to European Society for Blood and Marrow Transplantation and national guidelines.5,15 As long as the CART trial was open, eligible patients were offered participation as first cellular immunotherapy, leaving alloHCT as rescue option.

Statistical analysis

To assess survival probabilities, Kaplan-Meier product-limit estimates were used. OS events were defined as death resulting from any cause. Progression-free survival (PFS) events were defined as CLL relapse/progression or death resulting from any cause. EFS was defined as time from SRHR stratum entry to DR, transformation, or death. Survival curves were compared using log-rank test. Nonrelapse mortality (NRM) and relapse incidence were analyzed using cumulative incidence rates in a competing risk framework. GraphPad Prism software (release 9.5.1; San Diego, CA) was used for calculations and image. Data were analyzed as of 31 March 2026.

Results

Patient cohorts

Using the prespecified search terms, 81 patients were retrieved from the electronic institutional database. Of these, 47 patients met the eligibility criteria and, thus, constituted the total study population. A total of 40 patients entered SRHR, 17 of who’s status evolved to DR during follow-up; 7 additional patients entered the study directly in DR status because high risk genetics were persistently absent or became evident only after achieving double refractoriness, implying that the DR cohort was collectively made up of 24 patients. Survival outcomes

Median EFS of the SRHR cohort was 22 months (Figure 2A). EFS of chemoimmunotherapy-exposed and chemoimmunotherapy-naïve patients was similar (Figure 2B). The 9 patients who converted to SRHR status because of BCL2i resistance and, thus, were rescued with cBTKi, tended to have a longer EFS than the 31 patients who had cBTKi first, but without reaching statistical significance (Figure 2C). The remaining 25 patients did not receive alloHCT for various reasons (ineligibility, patient refusal, donor unavailability), or were still under consideration for alloHCT at the time of data lock. Of 22 patients undergoing alloHCT, 4 patients died of NRM (graft-versus-host disease, n = 2; sepsis, n = 2) and 5 patients due to progressive disease. Taking into account 2 additional patients who experienced disease relapse after alloHCT but could be rescued with CART and donor lymphocyte infusions plus cBTKi, respectively, NRM, relapse incidence, PFS, and OS at 2 years after alloHCT was 19%, 19%, 62%, and 67%, respectively (Figure 3; supplemental Table 2). Median follow-up of survivors was 23 (range, 2-108) months. PFS was numerically superior in patients receiving alloHCT in complete response (CR) and in SRHR status, respectively, without reaching statistical significance in the small sample investigated here (supplemental Figure 3). Of 6 mortality events observed in 11 patients undergoing alloHCT in DR status, 3 were due to early relapse in patients who rapidly went through several lines of therapy without meaningful response to any of them. Of note, none of these 3 patients had a TP53 abnormality. In patients with DR status intended for alloHCT, bridging regimens included cBTKi-BCL2i combinations with each other and/or CD20 antibodies, ncBTKi, and lymphoma-type chemoimmunotherapy. Efficacy was limited; disease status at alloHCT was CR in 1 patient, partial response in 6 patients (54%), and stable disease/progressive disease in 4 patients (36%). On the contrary, patients in the SRHR cohort intended for alloHCT could be effectively salvaged with the alternative drug class, allowing for CR as disease status at transplant in 10 of 11 patients undergoing alloHCT in this stratum, 4 of them with undetectable measurable residual disease. Discussion

Although it is well accepted that patients with DR CLL have a poor prognosis,3,4 precise evidence substantiating and quantifying this assumption is limited. Although there are numerous studies on “double-exposed” CLL without considering the reasons of cBTKi and BCL2i discontinuation or failure,16 only few analyses focus on patients who indeed met the criteria of double refractoriness. Lew et al reported a median OS of only 3.6 months after reaching DR status in 17 heavily pretreated patients.7 In a more recent series from the same group, the median OS of patients who progressed on, or after, a venetoclax-based regimen administered subsequent to previous cBTKi failure was 11.4 months.9 The largest series to date was described by Yoon et al and comprised 30 patients with DR CLL. Patients had received a median of 6 lines of pretreatment before arriving at DR status, and most had adverse genetics.8 Median OS from the DR landmark was 26 months and, thus, comparable with the median OS from the DR landmark of 21 months observed in this study.

Evidence is even more limited for patients meeting SRHR criteria. In 12 evaluable patients from the first Lew study, time to progression to DR status was 23 to 25 months, but not all patients had adverse genetics.7 In the more recent Lew study, 26 patients with TP53 abnormality refractory to cBTKi had a median PFS of ∼18 months on salvage venetoclax-based therapy. With 40 patients meeting SRHR criteria, this study provides, to our knowledge, for the first time, outcome data on a larger sample of this risk category. With 22 and 51 months, respectively, EFS and OS from the SRHR landmark the prognosis of this subset was, indeed, unfavorable, regardless of whether patients were chemoimmunotherapy-exposed before cBTKi/BCL2i treatment or not.

Of note, the EFS of 23 months on venetoclax-based treatment of patients with cBTKi-refractory disease with adverse genetics appears at least comparable with that achieved with the ncBTKi pirtobrutinib in the BRUIN CLL-123 trial in that setting, in which a median PFS of 15.3 months on pirtobrutinib was reported in 59 cBTKi-exposed BCL2i-naïve patients with or without unfavorable genetics.17 This observation thus challenges the rationale for preferring ncBTKi in TP53-altered patients who became refractory to cBTKi.

Abundant in vivo and in vitro data suggest that graft-versus-leukemia effects conferred by alloHCT can induce durable disease control in patients with high-risk CLL.18, 19, 20, 21, 22 Although most of this evidence dates from the pretargeted era, there have been a few studies proving the feasibility and efficacy of alloHCT in CLL also in the era of targeted agents. However, these series largely contained patients who were cBTKi- or BCL2i-exposed rather than refractory to 1 or even both drug classes.10, 11, 12 The reports by Roeker et al and Kim et al comprised 65 and 30 patients, respectively, exposed to cBTKi, venetoclax, or both, and described 2-year PFS rates of ∼70% with only few events thereafter.11,12 Here, we provide, to our knowledge, for the first time, results of allogeneic transplantation performed in patient with true SRHR or DR status, suggesting that alloHCT is feasible and can be effective in these settings. Compared with the aforementioned studies, however, our PFS rates appear a little less favorable, largely due to a number of early events in the cohort of patients receiving alloHCT in DR status. In this context it is worth mentioning the very large proportion of patients with highly CK in our study, as preliminary data suggest a potentially adverse effect of CK on alloHCT outcome.11,12,23

Our CART sample is largely overlapping with the trial population published previously.14 Although its safety features are excellent, it does not seem that Heidagenlecleucel—unlike alloHCT—can result in durable disease eradication in a larger proportion of patients with SRHR or DR CLL. This mirrors experience obtained with other CD19-directed CARTs in this risk cluster.24, 25, 26 Nevertheless, the favorable survival data observed in our small cohort suggest that it might be worthwhile to attempt CART as the much less toxic approach before alloHCT, with the perspective of achieving longer-term disease control directly or with alloHCT as rescue option.

Notwithstanding the limitations of such analyses, our exploratory comparisons could not show a significant benefit of alloHCT over not undergoing transplantation in any setting investigated. In contrast, at no time point was survival of patients who underwent alloHCT inferior to that of patients who did not, and OS curves after alloHCT show a plateau, suggesting that transplantation remains a reasonable option for eligible patients meeting the high-risk criteria studied here. This is supported by anecdotal evidence from previous case series: in the study by Yoon et al, 3 of 7 patients with DR status surviving the 3-year landmark, and 2 of 2 surviving the 5-year landmark, respectively, had undergone alloHCT and/or CART.8 Lew et al described 17 patients with DR CLL. A single patient received alloHCT after bridging with idelalisib-rituximab and remained the only long-term survivor in this series.7 Similarly, in a more recent Australian multicenter study on 32 cBTKi-exposed patients who had a follow-up treatment with venetoclax-rituximab, 4 patients received consolidation with alloHCT. All 4 patients were alive and relapse free at last follow-up, 2 of them belonging to the group of 6 patients surviving beyond the 3-year landmark.9

Collectively, these data show that alloHCT at least does not impair the overall prognosis of patients with SRHR or DR CLL, and has the potential to provide long-term disease control and survival in individual patients of these poor-risk subsets with otherwise limited outlook. Although it is plausible that feasibility and outcomes of alloHCT are compromised in DR compared with SRHR status due to increased disease resistance, higher patient vulnerability, and limited options for bridging and salvage of posttransplant relapse, our data do not provide robust evidence for superiority of performing alloHCT in SRHR status vs deferring it until double refractoriness has occurred. Nevertheless, they strongly suggest further exploration of alloHCT in the SRHR setting.

Apart from the inherent caveats associated with retrospective studies, the limitations of the present analysis consist in the still small sample size and the inevitable bias regarding the exploratory transplant vs no transplant comparisons. Strengths are the thorough workup of treatment refractoriness and genetic background, and the high proportion of patients who underwent alloHCT, allowing, to our knowledge, for the first time, an approach to feasibility and efficacy of alloHCT in patients with SRHR and DR CLL, respectively.

In conclusion, these results demonstrate that the outlook of patients with SRHR CLL is indeed dismal but still better than in patients with DR disease. In both settings, alloHCT is feasible and may overcome clinical and biological disease refractoriness. However, the optimum timing and sequencing of CART and alloHCT, respectively, and the general impact of cellular immunotherapy on the natural course of SRHR/DR CLL remain to be determined. This process has to be continuously aligned with the ongoing advances of targeted and immunotherapies in CLL.27, 28, 29
