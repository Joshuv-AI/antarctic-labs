Faster Algorithms for Shortest Unique or Absent Substrings
Panagiotis Charalampopoulos, Manal Mohamed, Solon P. Pissis, Hilde Verbeek, Wiktor Zuba

Abstract
We revisit two well-known algorithmic problems on strings: computing a shortest unique substring (SUS) and a shortest absent substring (SAS) of a string S S of length n n . Both problems admit folklore 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ) \mathcal{O}(n) -time solutions using the suffix tree of S S . However, for small alphabets, this complexity is not necessarily optimal in the word RAM model, where a string of length n n over alphabet [ 0 , σ ) [0,\sigma) can be stored in 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ σ / log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(n\log\sigma/\log n) space and read in 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ σ / log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(n\log\sigma/\log n) time. We present an 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ σ / log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(n\log\sigma/\sqrt{\log n}) -time algorithm for computing a SUS of S S . This algorithm decomposes the problem according to the length and the period of the sought substring and uses several tools and techniques, such as synchronizing sets, the analysis of runs, and wavelet trees, to reduce the computation of a SUS to a simple geometric problem. Further, we adapt this algorithm and combine it with an efficient construction of de Bruijn sequences in order to obtain an 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ σ / log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(n\log\sigma/\sqrt{\log n}) -time algorithm for computing a SAS of S S .

Shortest unique substrings (SUSs) and shortest absent substrings (SASs) have a wide range of applications in bioinformatics, information retrieval, and data compression. In bioinformatics, SUSs are employed in alignment-free sequence comparison methods

Our results.

We answer this question in the affirmative, improving upon the folklore linear-time solutions for computing a single SUS or SAS in the packed setting.

Theorem 1.1

(SUS)
.

Given a packed string

Our techniques and paper organization.

We obtain

by decomposing the problem based on the

For short substrings, we employ tabulation.
For medium-length aperiodic substrings, we modify an existing technique for longest common substrings

For long aperiodic substrings, we combine sampling via string synchronizing sets

For periodic substrings, we exploit the runs in

We obtain

Other related work.

Kempa and Kociumaka
[
34
]
studied the hardness hierarchy for problems whose fastest known word-RAM algorithms run in
\mathcal{O}(n\sqrt{\log n})
time on inputs of
\Theta(n)
machine words.
This class includes string processing problems such as constructing the BWT
[
31
]
, computing a longest common substring
[
14
]
, and computing LZ77 factorizations
[
33
]
.
For these problems, they showed that the known
\mathcal{O}(n/\sqrt{\log n})
-time algorithms for
binary
strings—equivalently,
\mathcal{O}(n\sqrt{\log n})
-time algorithms when the input size is measured in machine words—are conditionally optimal, with conditional lower bounds established via non-trivial reductions to a variant of dictionary matching.
Computing a SUS (
Theorem

1.1
) or a SAS (
Theorem

1.2
) for binary strings shares the same time complexity.
It remains an interesting open problem whether one can improve upon our algorithms or prove that they are conditionally optimal.
Let us note that proving a conditional lower bound for our problem using the framework of Kempa and Kociumaka
[
34
]
seems challenging. The reductions in said work highlight as the underlying hardness in the studied problems the task of looking for an occurrence of some (sub)string
P
in a string
S
(e.g., as in the longest common substring problem). The problems we study here are of a different flavor: in SUS we look for substrings that do not occur elsewhere in
S
while in SAS we look for strings that do not occur at all in
S
.

Strings.

An

\Sigma

A

.
We refer to each

as a

of
S
.
We consider throughout an
integer

\Sigma=[0,\sigma)
with
\sigma=n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
.
A string

is a

of a string

if we have
P=S[i]\cdots S[i+|P|-1]
for some position

of
S
.
In this case, we say that

at position

of
S

Such an occurrence is called a

of

; we denote it by either
S[i\mathinner{.\,.}i+|P|)
or
S[i\mathinner{.\,.}i+|P|-1]
.
The set of the starting positions of the

of a string

in

is denoted by

.
A

of

is a fragment of the form

, and a

of

is a fragment of the form

.
A substring

of

is called

.
The
reverse
of string
S
, which we denote by

, is defined as
S^{R}=S[n-1]S[n-2]\cdots S[0]
.
The

of two strings

and

Definition 2.1

(Period)
.

An integer
p>0
is a
period
of a string
P
if
P[i]=P[i+p]
, for all
i\in[0,|P|-p)
. The smallest period of
P
is called

of

and is denoted by
\textsf{per}(P)
. A string

is called

if
\textsf{per}(P)\leq|P|/2

Example 1
.

For string
P=\texttt{abaaabaaabaaaba}
, we have
\textsf{per}(P)=4
. Note that
8
is also a period of

. Since
\textsf{per}(P)=4\leq|P|/2=15/2
,

is periodic.

Definition 2.2

(Run)
.

A fragment
F=S[i\mathinner{.\,.}j]
of a string
S
is called a

of
S
if

\textsf{per}(F)\leq|F|/2
, and extending
F
either to the left or to the right (if possible) would result in an increase of its period, that is,

S[i-1]\neq S[i-1+\textsf{per}(F)]
(or

) and
S[j+1]\neq S[j+1-\textsf{per}(F)]
(or

).

Definition 2.3

(Lyndon Root)
.

The

of a run
F
, denoted by
\textsf{Lroot}(F)
, is defined as the lexicographically smallest rotation of the prefix
F[0\mathinner{.\,.}\textsf{per}(F))
of
F
.

Definition 2.4

(Lyndon Representation)
.

The Lyndon representation of a run
F
is a quadruple
\textsf{Lrepr}(F)=(\lambda,e,\alpha,\beta)
such that:

•

\lambda=\textsf{Lroot}(F)
, and

•

F=P\cdot\lambda^{e}\cdot T
, where
P
is a (possibly empty) suffix of
\lambda
with
|P|=\alpha<|\lambda|
, and
T
is a (possibly empty) prefix of
\lambda
with
|T|=\beta<|\lambda|
.

Example 2
.

Let
S=\texttt{\lx@text@underline{abaaabaaabaaaba}b}
. The

F=S[0\mathinner{.\,.}14]=\texttt{abaaabaaabaaaba}
is a run with

. Observe that extending
F
to the right yields the fragment
S[0\mathinner{.\,.}15]=S
, whose period is
14
; hence extending

increases its period.
Moreover, we have
\textsf{Lroot}(F)=\texttt{aaab}

and
\textsf{Lrepr}(F)=(\texttt{aaab},3,2,1)
.

Definition 2.5

(
\tau
-Run)
.

A run

of a string
S
is called a
\tau
-run

\tau>0

|R|\geq 3\tau-1

Lemma 2.1

(Lemma 2.8
[
14
]
)

Let

Compacted tries and suffix trees.

For a set
\mathcal{S}
of strings over
\Sigma
, the

\textsf{TRIE}(\mathcal{S})
is a rooted tree whose nodes are in one-to-one correspondence with the set of the prefixes of the strings in
\mathcal{S}
.
Each edge of
\textsf{TRIE}(\mathcal{S})
is labeled with a letter from

The string
\textsf{str}(v)
represented by a node

is called the

of
\textsf{str}(v)

The order on
\Sigma
induces a corresponding order on the outgoing edges of every node in
\textsf{TRIE}(\mathcal{S})

A node

is

if it has at least two children and

\textsf{str}(v)\in\mathcal{S}

A

for a set

The edges of the compacted trie are labeled by fragments of elements of

rather than single letters.
The

of a node

\textsf{sd}(v)=|\textsf{str}(v)|

v

The compacted trie requires

\mathcal{S}
, as any fragment

for

can be stored in

.

Sketches of linear-time solutions.

Given the suffix tree
\textsf{ST}(S)
of a string
S
of length
n
, both
Shortest Unique Substring
and

can be solved in
\mathcal{O}(n)

Shortest Unique Substring
:
Consider a terminal node

\textsf{ST}(S)

If
v
is not a leaf, then no string that is unique in

occurs at position

of
S

We now consider the case when
v
is a leaf and denote its parent by

The shortest string that is unique in
S
and occurs at position

is

and is of length
\textsf{sd}(u)+1
. By iterating over all leaves of
\textsf{ST}(S)
, we may thus compute all SUSs of

\mathcal{O}(n)
.

Shortest Absent Substring

Consider a node

\textsf{ST}(S)

\sigma

P
that is absent from

\textsf{str}(u)
as its longest proper prefix. However, if

has fewer than
\sigma

has no outgoing edge whose string label starts with

\textsf{str}(u)\cdot c
is absent from
S

By iterating over all nodes of
\textsf{ST}(S)
, we can compute the nodes

with fewer than
\sigma
outgoing edges and minimum string depth, and then output all SASs in time
\mathcal{O}(n+|\textsf{output}|)
.

We decompose the problem into four cases based on the length

(2)

(see

(3)

:

(see

(4)

Periodic case
:
\ell\geq 3\tau
and
p\leq\frac{1}{3}\tau
, for
\tau=\lfloor\frac{1}{15}\log_{\sigma}n\rfloor
or
\tau=\lfloor\frac{1}{3}\log^{4}n\rfloor
(see
Section

3.5
).
Note that when
\tau=0
, this case is obsolete because the period of any string is positive.

In
Section

3.3
, we introduce and solve a simple geometric problem that serves as a subroutine for computing a SUS in both the long aperiodic and the periodic cases.

3.1
Short Case

Lemma 3.1
.

Given an instance of
Shortest Unique Substring
, in
\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)
time,
we can either compute a SUS of
S
of length at most
\ell:=\lfloor\frac{1}{5}\log_{\sigma}n\rfloor
if one exists, or conclude that no SUS of length at most
\ell
exists.

Proof.

We use the standard trick of covering
S
with short overlapping fragments that contain all length-
\ell
fragments of
S
such that the number of distinct strings is strongly sublinear in
n
.
Specifically, we consider the fragment
S_{i}:=S[i\mathinner{.\,.}\min\{i+2\ell,n\})
for each
i\in[0,n-\ell)
that is equivalent to
0
modulo
\ell
.
There are
\mathcal{O}(n/\ell)
such fragments in
S
, and every length-
\ell

fragment of
S
is covered by at least one of them.

Over an alphabet of size
\sigma
, collection
\mathcal{S}:=\{S_{i}\mid i\in[0,n-\ell)\text{ and }i\equiv 0\pmod{\ell}\}
contains
\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{2\ell})=\mathcal{O}(n^{2/5})

distinct
strings. Since
2\ell=\mathcal{O}(\log_{\sigma}n)
, each string in
\mathcal{S}
fits in
\mathcal{O}(1)
machine words.
We lexicographically sort the elements of
\mathcal{S}
by treating them as integers using bucket sort, and then keep at most
two
copies of each distinct string in
\mathcal{S}
.
This takes
\mathcal{O}(n^{2/5}+n/\ell)=

\mathcal{O}(n/\ell)
time.
Let
F_{1}\leq F_{2}\leq\dots\leq F_{q}
, for
q=\mathcal{O}(n^{2/5})
, denote the obtained sorted list.
For each integer
i\in[1,q]
, let
p_{i}
be the left endpoint of an occurrence of
F_{i}
in
S
.

Next, we construct the string
S^{\prime}=\#_{0}F_{1}\#_{1}F_{2}\#_{2}\dots\#_{q-1}F_{q}\#_{q}
of length
|S^{\prime}|=\mathcal{O}(n^{2/5}\ell)
, where the letters
\#_{0},\ldots,\#_{q}\notin~\Sigma
are pairwise distinct.
We map each position
j
of
S^{\prime}
such that
S^{\prime}[j]\in\Sigma
to its original position in
S
.
Formally, any position
j
of
S^{\prime}
such that
j\in(j_{1},j_{2})
,
where
S^{\prime}[j_{1}]=\#_{i-1}
and
S^{\prime}[j_{2}]=\#_{i}
, is mapped to position
p_{i}+(j-(j_{1}+1))
.

Our goal is now to find a shortest substring
P
of
S^{\prime}
that does not contain any letter
\#_{i}
and such that, if
P
occurs at distinct positions
j
and
j^{\prime}
in
S^{\prime}
, then positions
j
and
j^{\prime}
correspond to the same original position in
S
.
To this end, we construct a compacted trie over the suffixes of the strings
F_{i}\#_{i}
.
This can be achieved by building the suffix tree
\textsf{ST}(S^{\prime})
in
\mathcal{O}(n^{2/5}\ell)
time
[
22
]
and postprocessing it in linear time using (batched) weighted ancestor queries (see
[
13
]
).
Each leaf corresponding to a suffix
F_{i}[j\mathinner{.\,.}|F_{i}|)\#_{i}
is labeled with
p_{i}+j
.
Then, for each leaf
u
whose parent
v
does not have string depth
\textsf{sd}(u)-1
, we introduce an artificial explicit node with that string depth along edge
(u,v)
.

Finally, we perform a post-order traversal of
\textsf{ST}(S^{\prime})
to mark all internal nodes
u
all of whose descendant leaves are marked with the same integer, and compute the minimal value of
\textsf{sd}(\textsf{parent}(u))+1
over all such nodes.
If this value is at most
\ell
, we conclude that the SUSs of
S
are of length at most
\ell
, and can return any of them: each SUS corresponds to the path-label of the path from the root to the (possibly implicit) node with string depth
\textsf{sd}(\textsf{parent}(u))+1
in the root-to-
u
path for a marked node
u
.
Otherwise, we conclude that all substrings of
S
of length at most
\ell
have at least two occurrences in
S
.
Said traversal takes time linear in the size of the compacted trie.
Overall, the algorithm thus runs in
\mathcal{O}(n^{2/5}\ell+n/\ell)=\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)
time.

Correctness.

By construction, the postprocessed trie
\textsf{ST}(S^{\prime})
represents all the substrings of
S
of length at most
\ell
(plus some of the substrings of length at most
2\ell
). By keeping at most two copies of each distinct string in
\mathcal{S}
, we ensure that the (non-)uniqueness of substrings is preserved between
S
and
S^{\prime}
. In particular, if a substring
P
of length (at most)
\ell
is unique in
S
, then all the leaf descendants of the node representing
P
in
\textsf{ST}(S^{\prime})
are labeled with the
same position
of its single occurrence in
S
. Therefore, the algorithm needs to find the lowest such node (explicit or implicit) whose leaf descendants all share the same position label. The minimality condition can only be satisfied by a direct child of a branching node
\textsf{parent}(u)
representing a non-unique substring, where
u
itself is an explicit node representing a unique substring.
Finally, by excluding from the output the leaves (that represent unique substrings containing letters
\#_{i}
), and unique substrings whose string depth exceeds
\ell
(since their other occurrence might simply not be represented in
S^{\prime}
), we ensure that the reported substring is indeed a genuine SUS of
S
.
∎

3.2
Medium Aperiodic Case

We look for a SUS with length
\ell\in(\frac{1}{5}\log_{\sigma}n,2^{\sqrt{\log n}})
and period
p>\frac{1}{45}\log_{\sigma}n
.
Our solution for this case builds on
a method for computing a longest common substring in the packed setting
[
14
]
.
This method relies on the
Two String Families LCP
problem, originally introduced in
[
12
]
.
Let us denote the length of the longest common prefix (LCP) of two strings
U
and
V

by
\textsf{LCP}(U,V)
.

Two String Families LCP

Input:
Compacted tries
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{F}_{1})
and
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{F}_{2})
of
\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
,
and two sets
\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{1}\times\mathcal{F}_{2}

with
|\mathcal{P}|,|\mathcal{Q}|,|\mathcal{F}_{1}|,|\mathcal{F}_{2}|\leq N
.

Output:

\max\{\textsf{LCP}(P_{1},Q_{1})+\textsf{LCP}(P_{2},Q_{2}):(P_{1},P_{2})\in\mathcal{P},(Q_{1},Q_{2})\in\mathcal{Q}\}
.

We next define an analogous problem for SUSs:

Shortest Unique String Pair

Input:
Compacted tries
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{F}_{1})
and
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{F}_{2})
of
\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
,
and a (multi)set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{1}\times\mathcal{F}_{2}

with
|\mathcal{S}|,|\mathcal{F}_{1}|,|\mathcal{F}_{2}|\leq~N
.

Output:
Integers
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\geq 0
and a pair
(P_{1},P_{2})\in\mathcal{S}
, such that
(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2})
is minimized and for all
(Q_{1},Q_{2})\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\{(P_{1},P_{2})\}
, either
\textsf{LCP}(P_{1},Q_{1})<\ell_{1}
or
\textsf{LCP}(P_{2},Q_{2})<\ell_{2}
.

We further recall a few definitions from
[
14
]
necessary for stating the needed result from
[
14
]
.

Definition 3.1

(Prefix Family)
.

A set
\mathcal{S}=\{(U_{1},V_{1}),\dots,(U_{N},V_{N})\}
of string pairs is a
prefix family
if there exists a string
Y
such that
U_{i}
is a prefix of
Y
for every
i=1,\ldots,N
.

Definition 3.2

(
(\alpha,\beta)
-Family)
.

A set
\mathcal{S}=\{(U_{1},V_{1}),\dots,(U_{N},V_{N})\}
of string pairs is an
(\alpha,\beta)
-family
if, for all
(U,V)\in\mathcal{S}
,
|U|\leq\alpha
and
|V|\leq\beta
.

For each of these types of families, we design a different algorithm that solves
Shortest Unique String Pair
when
\mathcal{S}
is of said type of family.

3.2.1
Solution for Prefix Families

We consider an instance of
Shortest Unique String Pair
with
\mathcal{S}
being a prefix family
\{(U_{1},V_{1}),\ldots,(U_{N},V_{N})\}
. Since the first components of the elements of
\mathcal{S}
are prefixes of some common string
Y
, we have that
\textsf{LCP}(U_{i},U_{j})=\min\{|U_{i}|,|U_{j}|\}
, for any
i,j\in[1,N]
. We will use this fact to first show a formula for
Shortest Unique String Pair
on a prefix family.

Lemma 3.2
.

Consider an instance of
Shortest Unique String Pair
in which
\mathcal{S}
is a prefix family.
For any integer
\ell_{1}>0
, let
I_{\ell_{1}}=\{i\mid|U_{i}|\geq\ell_{1}\}
. Further let

f(\ell_{1})=\min_{i\in I_{\ell_{1}}}\left(\max_{j\in I_{\ell_{1}}\setminus\{i\}}\textsf{LCP}(V_{i},V_{j})\right),

with
f(\ell_{1})=0
if
|I_{\ell_{1}}|=1
. The pair
(\ell_{1},f(\ell_{1})+1)
minimizing the sum
\ell_{1}+f(\ell_{1})+1
is an optimal solution.

Proof.

We show that the formulation is feasible and that any optimal solution must have a cost at least as large as the one proposed.

Feasibility.

For a fixed
\ell_{1}
, let
i^{*}\in I_{\ell_{1}}
be an index attaining the minimum in
f(\ell_{1})
, and let
\ell_{2}=f(\ell_{1})+1
. We choose the pair
(P_{1},P_{2})=(U_{i^{*}},V_{i^{*}})
as our witness. To satisfy
Shortest Unique String Pair
, every other pair
(Q_{1},Q_{2})=(U_{j},V_{j})\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\{(U_{i^{*}},V_{i^{*}})\}
must be separated. There are two cases for index
j
:

•

Case 1:
j\notin I_{\ell_{1}}
. In a prefix family, all
U
strings are prefixes of some common string. If
|U_{j}|<\ell_{1}\leq|U_{i^{*}}|
, then
U_{j}
is a proper prefix of
U_{i^{*}}
, which implies
\textsf{LCP}(U_{i^{*}},U_{j})=|U_{j}|<\ell_{1}
. Thus, the first condition of
Shortest Unique String Pair
is satisfied.

•

Case 2:
j\in I_{\ell_{1}}
. By the definition of
i^{*}
, we have
\textsf{LCP}(V_{i^{*}},V_{j})\leq\max_{k\in I_{\ell_{1}}\setminus\{i^{*}\}}\textsf{LCP}(V_{i^{*}},V_{k})=f(\ell_{1})
. Since
\ell_{2}=f(\ell_{1})+1
, it follows that
\textsf{LCP}(V_{i^{*}},V_{j})<\ell_{2}
. Thus, the second condition of
Shortest Unique String Pair
is satisfied.

In both cases,
(U_{i^{*}},V_{i^{*}})
is a valid witness for
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})
.

Optimality.

Let
(\ell_{1}^{*},\ell_{2}^{*})
be an optimal solution with witness
(U_{k},V_{k})
. For this witness to be valid, we must have
|U_{k}|\geq\ell_{1}^{*}
. Furthermore, for the solution to be feasible, every other pair
j\in I_{\ell_{1}^{*}}\setminus\{k\}
must satisfy the second condition of
Shortest Unique String Pair
(since they fail the first:
|U_{j}|\geq\ell_{1}^{*}\implies\textsf{LCP}(U_{k},U_{j})\geq\ell_{1}^{*}
).

Therefore, for all
j\in I_{\ell_{1}^{*}}\setminus\{k\}
, we must have
\textsf{LCP}(V_{k},V_{j})\leq\ell_{2}^{*}-1
. This implies that the maximum LCP for this specific
k
is bounded:

\max_{j\in I_{\ell_{1}^{*}}\setminus\{k\}}\textsf{LCP}(V_{k},V_{j})\leq\ell_{2}^{*}-1.

Since
f(\ell_{1}^{*})
is defined as the
minimum
of such maxima over all
i\in I_{\ell_{1}^{*}}
, it follows that
f(\ell_{1}^{*})\leq\ell_{2}^{*}-1\implies\ell_{2}^{*}\geq f(\ell_{1}^{*})+1
. Thus, the cost
\ell_{1}^{*}+\ell_{2}^{*}
is at least
\ell_{1}^{*}+f(\ell_{1}^{*})+1
. Minimizing this over all possible
\ell_{1}
yields the global optimum.
∎

To efficiently implement
Lemma

3.2
, we use the following data structure. For every
(U_{i},V_{i})\in\mathcal{S}
, we define
\mathsf{maxLCP}[i]:=\max_{j\neq i,\,|U_{j}|\geq|U_{i}|}\textsf{LCP}(V_{i},V_{j})
, with
\mathsf{maxLCP}[i]=0
if no such
j
exists. We first show how to compute this array, and then how it is used to solve
Shortest Unique String Pair
.

Fact 1

(Folklore)
.

For any three strings
S_{1}
,
S_{2}
and
S_{3}
with
S_{1}\leq S_{2}\leq S_{3}
in the lexicographical order, we have
\textsf{LCP}(S_{1},S_{3})=\min\{\textsf{LCP}(S_{1},S_{2}),\textsf{LCP}(S_{2},S_{3})\}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

The array
\mathsf{maxLCP}
can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(N)
time.

Proof.

By traversing
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{F}_{2})
, we
obtain the list of
V
strings (the second components of
\mathcal{S}
) in lexicographical order in
\mathcal{O}(N)
total time. For convenience,
let us denote the
sorted
list by

V_{1},\ldots,V_{N}
. In addition to the list, we can also output the LCP values
\textsf{LCP}(V_{1},V_{2}),\ldots,\textsf{LCP}(V_{N-1},V_{N})
in the same traversal.
For finding arbitrary LCP values, we use a data structure for answering range minimum queries over the latter list of
N
LCP values. The data structure is constructed in
\mathcal{O}(N)

time and it can answer queries in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time
[
7
]
.

From
1
, for any triplet
V_{i},V_{j},V_{k}
with
|U_{j}|,|U_{k}|\geq|U_{i}|
and either
V_{i}\leq V_{j}\leq V_{k}
or
V_{i}\geq V_{j}\geq V_{k}
, we have
\textsf{LCP}(V_{i},V_{k})\leq\textsf{LCP}(V_{i},V_{j})
. Repeatedly applying this argument along the lexicographical order shows that, among all strings
V_{j}
with
|U_{j}|\geq|U_{i}|
, the maximum value of
\textsf{LCP}(V_{i},V_{j})
is attained by one of the closest such strings to
V_{i}
in lexicographical order.

We introduce two auxiliary arrays. For each
i\in[1,N]
, we define
\mathsf{prev}[i]:=\max\{j<i\mid|U_{j}|\geq|U_{i}|\}
and
\mathsf{next}[i]:=\min\{j>i\mid|U_{j}|\geq|U_{i}|\}
; note that these values may be undefined for some entries. By the argument from the previous paragraph, we have that
\mathsf{maxLCP}[i]=\max\{\textsf{LCP}(V_{i},V_{\mathsf{prev}[i]}),\textsf{LCP}(V_{i},V_{\mathsf{next}[i]})\}
(where we treat the LCP value with some undefined entry as
0
).

The arrays
\mathsf{prev}
and
\mathsf{next}
can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(N)
time using a well-known algorithm (cf.
[
4
]
). From thereon, we compute the values of
\mathsf{maxLCP}
by taking the maximum LCP of each entry with its corresponding
\mathsf{prev}
and
\mathsf{next}
entries using two
\mathcal{O}(1)
-time range minimum queries. The total time is thus
\mathcal{O}(N)
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

An instance of
Shortest Unique String Pair
where
\mathcal{S}
is a prefix family of size
N
can be solved in
\mathcal{O}(N)
time.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{(U_{1},V_{1}),\dots,(U_{N},V_{N})\}
be a prefix family. By
Lemma

3.2
, it suffices to minimize
\ell_{1}+f(\ell_{1})+1
.

The function
f(\ell_{1})
is non-increasing and changes only when
\ell_{1}
passes some value
|U_{i}|
. Hence, it suffices to consider thresholds
\ell_{1}=|U_{i}|
. We have

f(|U_{i}|)=\min_{k:\ |U_{k}|\geq|U_{i}|}\left(\max_{j:\ |U_{j}|\geq|U_{i}|,\ j\neq k}\textsf{LCP}(V_{k},V_{j})\right).

By definition,
\mathsf{maxLCP}[k]=\max_{j:\ |U_{j}|\geq|U_{k}|,\ j\neq k}\textsf{LCP}(V_{k},V_{j})
upper-bounds the above expression for all
k
, and is tight when
|U_{k}|=|U_{i}|
. Thus,

f(|U_{i}|)=\min_{k:\ |U_{k}|\geq|U_{i}|}\mathsf{maxLCP}[k].

The algorithm proceeds as follows:

(1)

Compute the array
\mathsf{maxLCP}
in
\mathcal{O}(N)
time via
Lemma

3.3
.

(2)

Sort indices
i
with respect to
|U_{i}|
in the non-increasing order, compute the minima

h_{i}=\min_{k:\ |U_{k}|\geq|U_{i}|}\mathsf{maxLCP}[k],

and return
\min_{i}(|U_{i}|+1+h_{i})
.

Both steps take
\mathcal{O}(N)
time, and the statement follows.
∎

j>i
,
\textsf{LCP}(\mathcal{S}[j],\mathcal{S}[i])\leq\textsf{LCP}(\mathcal{S}[i+1],\mathcal{S}[i])=L[i+1]
. Therefore, the greatest LCP value between
\mathcal{S}[i]
and any other string in
\mathcal{S}
is precisely
M[i]=\max\{L[i],L[i+1]\}
. Any prefix of
\mathcal{S}[i]
that is longer than this value (if one exists) is not a prefix of any string in
\mathcal{S}\setminus\{\mathcal{S}[i]\}
. Conversely, any prefix of
\mathcal{S}[i]
of length
M[i]
is guaranteed to be a prefix of at least one neighboring string (the one determining the maximum LCP value). Thus, the length of the shortest unique prefix for string
\mathcal{S}[i]
is given by:

\textsf{SUP}_{i}=\begin{cases}M[i]+1,&\text{if $M[i]<|\mathcal{S}[i]|$}\\
\infty,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The length of the shortest unique prefix for the entire list
\mathcal{S}
is the minimum of these lengths over all indices
i
, that is,
\min_{i\in[0,N)}\left\{\textsf{SUP}_{i}\right\}
. This minimum can be found in
\mathcal{O}(N)
time by first computing all
M[i]
values in
\mathcal{O}(N)
time and then finding the global minimum.
∎

3.2.3
Wrapping Up

We consider separately the cases in which the candidate SUS starts within a
\tau
-run and the case when it does not. In the former case, we apply the algorithm for a prefix family, with entries based on all
\tau
-runs sharing the same structure (see

). In the latter case, the wavelet tree algorithm for an
(\alpha,\beta)

).

Lemma 3.8
.

Given an instance of
Shortest Unique Substring
,
we can compute a SUS
P
of
S
in
\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)
time
if
|P|\in[\frac{1}{5}\log_{\sigma}n,2^{\sqrt{\log n}}]
,

\textsf{per}(P)>\frac{1}{45}\log_{\sigma}n
,
and
\textsf{per}(P[0\mathinner{.\,.}3\lfloor\frac{1}{15}\log_{\sigma}n\rfloor-1])\leq\frac{1}{45}\log_{\sigma}n
.

Proof.

Let
\tau:=\lfloor\frac{1}{15}\log_{\sigma}n\rfloor
. Because
\textsf{per}(P[0\mathinner{.\,.}3\tau-1])\leq\frac{1}{3}\tau
, we know that the SUS starts inside some
\tau
-run, and since the period of the SUS
P
itself is by hypothesis greater, we also know that it extends beyond said
\tau
-run. We thus only have to search for a SUS around the ends of
\tau
-runs.

Step 1: Compute
\tau
-runs.

Using Lemma 2.8 from
[
14
]
,
we compute all
\tau
-runs in
S

in
\mathcal{O}(n/\tau)
total time.

Step 2: Group runs by periodic suffix.

For each run
R
with period
p
, consider its length-
p
suffix
L
. We group all runs with the same suffix
L
into a family
\mathcal{S}_{L}
. Since there are
\mathcal{O}(n/\tau)
runs and at most
\sigma^{\tau/3}=n^{1/45}
distinct length-

suffixes, this step takes
\mathcal{O}(n/\tau)=\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)
time in total.

Step 3: Construct
Shortest Unique String Pair
instances.

For a run
R
starting at position
i
and ending at position
j
, define a string pair

(U,V):=\big((S[i\mathinner{.\,.}j])^{R},\,S[j+1\mathinner{.\,.}j+2^{\sqrt{\log n}}]\big),

and add it to the corresponding family

for the suffix

of

.
The first component

is a prefix of

for some integer

, and thus each family
\mathcal{S}_{L}

Step 4: Solve

for each family.

By Lemma
3.4
,

\mathcal{O}(n/\tau)=\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)
time.

Step 5: Recover the SUS.

The SUS is obtained by taking the minimal-cost solution returned by
Shortest Unique String Pair
for any family (if the first part of the solution is shorter than
3\tau
, then it does not have a periodic prefix – candidate for SUS in the other case is always better).

Analysis.

Correctness follows because the SUS must start within a
\tau
-run and extend beyond it, and all possible runs and their periodic suffixes are considered. All steps—computing
\tau
-runs, grouping by suffix, constructing families, and solving

\mathcal{O}(n/\tau)=\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)

∎

Lemma 3.9
.

Given an instance of
Shortest Unique Substring
,
we can compute a SUS
P
of
S
in
\mathcal{O}(n\log\sigma/\sqrt{\log n})
time
if
|P|\in[\frac{1}{5}\log_{\sigma}n,2^{\sqrt{\log n}}]

and
\textsf{per}(P[0\mathinner{.\,.}3\lfloor\frac{1}{15}\log_{\sigma}n\rfloor-1])>\frac{1}{45}\log_{\sigma}n
.

Proof.

Let
\tau:=\lfloor\frac{1}{15}\log_{\sigma}n\rfloor
,
\beta:=\lfloor 2^{\sqrt{\log n}}\rfloor
, and let
\mathbf{Sync}
be a
\tau

2.1

\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)

For each

\text{pred}(i)=\max(\{j+1\mid j\in\mathbf{Sync},j<i\}\cup\{i-\tau+1,0\})
.
We construct a multiset
\mathcal{S}
of string pairs containing, for each

, the pair
(S[\text{pred}(i)\mathinner{.\,.}i-1]^{R},S[i\mathinner{.\,.}\min\{i+\beta,n\}))

Note that

Let

We construct the compacted trie

of the first components of
\mathcal{S}
in the following way.
Since all first components have length at most
\tau

\mathcal{O}(N+\sigma^{\tau})=\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n+n^{\frac{1}{15}})=\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)
time using bucket sort.
Then, using an LCE data structure on

, which can be constructed in
\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)
time
[
31
]
, we can compute the LCP values between consecutive components; with this information at hand, we can construct

\mathcal{O}(N)

[
30
]
.
The compacted trie

of the second components
can be constructed in the same way after sorting

\mathbf{Sync}
using
[
31
, Theorem 4.3]
in
\mathcal{O}(|\mathbf{Sync}|)=\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)

The set
\mathcal{S}

Correctness.

We first show that solving this

instance yields the desired SUS.
Suppose that there exists a substring
R
of
S
of length

and period
\textsf{per}(R[0\mathinner{.\,.}3\tau-1])>\frac{1}{3}\tau
. Then, by the synchronizing property, there exists some
j\in[0,\tau)
such that every occurrence of
R
has an anchor from
\mathbf{Sync}
at its

-th position.
We can decompose

as
R=R_{1}\cdot R_{2}
, where
|R_{1}|=j
. There is a bijection between occurrences of

and the pairs
(P_{1},P_{2})\in\mathcal{S}
such that

is a prefix of

and
R_{2}
is a prefix of

. If

is unique in

, there is exactly one such pair

. For any other pair
(Q_{1},Q_{2})\in\mathcal{S}

\textsf{LCP}(P_{1},Q_{1})<|R_{1}|
or
\textsf{LCP}(P_{2},Q_{2})<|R_{2}|

Construction and analysis.

We apply
Lemma

to construct a wavelet tree for the first components of

(the list obtained by sorting

according to the lexicographical order of the second components) and to compute the corresponding LCP lists for all nodes. Each node corresponds to a common prefix of length

for a subset of the first components. Within each node, we invoke

to find the shortest unique suffix extension of length

We now analyze the running time of the described algorithm.
Constructing the wavelet tree takes
\mathcal{O}(N(\tau+\log N)/\sqrt{\log N})=\mathcal{O}(n\log\sigma/\sqrt{\log n})

∎

Lemma 3.10
.

Any instance of
Minimum Skyline Point
can be solved in
\mathcal{O}(|P|)
time if the points in
P
are given as a list sorted with respect to one of the two coordinates.

Proof.

We first establish two claims.

Claim 1
.

Suppose that the primary skyline is nonempty and let
(x^{\star},y^{\star})
be a point in the primary skyline with minimal sum of coordinates. Then, either
x^{\star}=0
or there exists a point
(x,y)\in P
with
x=x^{\star}-1
.

Proof.

Assume, toward a contradiction, that the claim is false; that is, that we have
x^{\star}>0
and there is no point
(x,y)\in P
with
x=x^{\star}-1
.
Let
p^{\star}=(x^{\star},y^{\star})
.
By the definition of the primary skyline,
p^{\star}
must be dominated by exactly one point in
P
.
Now consider the point
p_{\text{left}}=(x^{\star}-1,y^{\star})
, which, due to the minimality of the coordinate-wise sum of
p^{\star}
, must be dominated by at least two points in
P
. Therefore, there exists a point
p^{\prime}\in P
that dominates
p_{\text{left}}
and does not dominate
p^{\star}
.
Let
p^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
. Since
p^{\prime}
does not dominate
p^{\star}
, we must have either
x^{\prime}<x^{\star}
or
y^{\prime}<y^{\star}
. We obtain a contradiction in each case:

•

If
x^{\prime}<x^{\star}
, then by our assumption that no point
(x,y)\in P
has
x=x^{\star}-1
, we must have
x^{\prime}\leq x^{\star}-2
. Hence,
x^{\prime}<x^{\star}-1
, and thus
p^{\prime}
does not dominate
p_{\text{left}}
, a contradiction.

•

If
y^{\prime}<y^{\star}
, then
p^{\prime}
clearly does not dominate
p_{\text{left}}
, a contradiction.∎

Claim 2
.

Suppose that the primary skyline is nonempty and let
(x^{\star},y^{\star})
be a point in the primary skyline with minimal sum of coordinates.
Let
P^{\prime}=\{(x,y)\in P\mid x\geq x^{\star}\}
and let
p_{\text{unique}}
be the unique point of
P^{\prime}
that dominates
(x^{\star},y^{\star})
.
If
|P^{\prime}|=1
, we have
y^{\star}=0
.
Else, we have
y^{\star}=y^{\prime}+1
, where
y^{\prime}
is the maximum
y
-coordinate of a point in
P^{\prime}\setminus\{p_{\text{unique}}\}
.

Proof.

By definition,
p^{\star}:=(x^{\star},y^{\star})
is dominated by exactly one point, namely
p_{\text{unique}}
.
Since points are only dominated by points weakly to their right, we have that
p_{\text{unique}}
must be in
P^{\prime}
.
We distinguish between two cases:

Case 1:
|P^{\prime}|=1
.

We have
P^{\prime}=\{p_{\text{unique}}\}
.
If
y^{\star}>0
, then the point
p_{\text{down}}=(x^{\star},y^{\star}-1)
is in
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2}
and the only point of
P
that dominates it is
p_{\text{unique}}
.
Thus,
p_{\text{down}}
lies in the primary skyline, but its coordinate sum
x^{\star}+y^{\star}-1
is smaller than that of
p^{\star}
, contradicting the minimality of
p^{\star}
.
Hence,
y^{\star}=0
.

Case 2:
|P^{\prime}|\geq 2
.

Let
P_{\text{other}}=P^{\prime}\setminus\{p_{\text{unique}}\}
, and let
y^{\prime}
be the maximum
y
-coordinate of a point in
P_{\text{other}}
. Since
p^{\star}
is dominated uniquely by
p_{\text{unique}}
, every point
(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime})\in P_{\text{other}}
satisfies
y^{\prime\prime}<y^{\star}
. Hence,
y^{\prime}<y^{\star}
. Conversely, since
p_{\text{unique}}
dominates
p^{\star}
, its
y
-coordinate is at least
y^{\star}
. Therefore, the point
(x^{\star},y^{\star})
is dominated by exactly one point in
P^{\prime}
if and only if
y^{\prime}<y^{\star}
. By the minimality of
p^{\star}
, we have
y^{\star}=y^{\prime}+1
.
∎

Algorithm.

We assume, without loss of generality, that the points in
P
are sorted by
x
-coordinate in non-decreasing order. We scan
P
from right to left, while maintaining
y_{\max}
and
y^{\prime}
, the two largest
y
-coordinates encountered among processed points (where
y_{\max}\geq y^{\prime}
). We initialize
y_{\max}
and
y^{\prime}
to
-1
, indicating that no processed point has contributed a valid
y
-coordinate yet.

We iterate through
P
by grouping points with the same
x
-coordinate. Let
x_{i}
denote the current
x
-coordinate of the group currently being processed, and let
P_{\text{curr}}=\{(x,y)\in P\mid x=x_{i}\}
be the set of points at this coordinate.
Before processing the points in
P_{\text{curr}}
, we evaluate the candidate
x
-coordinate
x^{\star}=x_{i}+1
. Because we have scanned from right to left, all points
(x,y)\in P
such that
x\geq x^{\star}
have already been processed. Let
P^{\prime}
denote this set of previously processed points. We determine the corresponding
y^{\star}
following
2
:

•

If
|P^{\prime}|=1
, we set
y^{\star}=0
.

•

If
|P^{\prime}|\geq 2
and
y_{\max}>y^{\prime}
, we set
y^{\star}=y^{\prime}+1
.

•

Otherwise (if
|P^{\prime}|=0
or
y_{\max}=y^{\prime}
), the primary skyline does not intersect the set
\{(x^{\star},y)\mid y\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\}
, so we skip this candidate.

After evaluating
y^{\star}
, we update
y_{\max}
and
y^{\prime}
using the

-values in
P_{\text{curr}}
so that they remain the two largest

-coordinates among all processed points.

Finally, after all points in
P
have been processed, we perform a last check for the candidate

using the final values of
y_{\max}
and
y^{\prime}
. The algorithm maintains the candidate
(x^{\star},y^{\star})
that minimizes
x^{\star}+y^{\star}
and returns it as a witness. Each point of
P
is processed exactly once using
\mathcal{O}(1)
simple operations, thus the algorithm runs in
\mathcal{O}(|P|)
time.
∎

Intuition for application to SUS.

In the long aperiodic case (
Section

3.4
) and in the periodic case (
Section

3.5
), we show that a SUS of
S
can be found by solving several instances of the
Minimum Skyline Point
problem. The core idea is to transform the SUS problem into a geometric one. Let us consider one such instance. We first identify a set
\mathcal{S}
of carefully-selected substrings of
S
, each anchored
around a
common fragment

F=S[i\mathinner{.\,.}j]
. Each substring
U\in\mathcal{S}
is then represented by an integer point
(x,y)
, where
x
and
y
are the lengths of the extensions of
U
to the left and the right, respectively, relative to the common fragment
F
.

Example 5
.

Let
S=\texttt{abracadabra}
,
F=S[4\mathinner{.\,.}6]=\texttt{cad}
, and
\mathcal{S}=\{\texttt{bracada},\texttt{cadabra}\}
. The substrings are represented as points by the lengths of their extensions relative to
F
:

bracada

\mapsto(3,1)
(since
bra
is of length 3 and
a
is of length 1), and
cadabra

\mapsto(0,4)
(since the left extension is empty and
abra
is of length 4).

Since strings in
\mathcal{S}
are substrings of
S
anchored around a common fragment, each point maps to one substring of
S
.
Then, a string
U\in\mathcal{S}
is a substring of string
V\in\mathcal{S}
if and only if the point representing
U
is dominated by the point representing
V
. To find a candidate SUS in
\mathcal{S}
, we look for a minimal extension anchored around
F
that is contained within exactly one substring from
\mathcal{S}
. This is equivalent to solving a
Minimum Skyline Point
instance: finding an integer point
(x^{\star},y^{\star})
that lies in the primary skyline of the points representing
\mathcal{S}
that minimizes

x^{\star}+y^{\star}
. The total length of the resulting SUS is then
|F|+x^{\star}+y^{\star}
.

3.4
Long Aperiodic Case

This section addresses the computation of a SUS in the case when

\ell\geq\log^{4}n
and
p>\frac{1}{9}\log^{4}n
.
Let us start with a high-level overview of our solution for this case. We construct a string synchronizing set consisting of
anchor
positions in
S
that enable the identification of identical sufficiently long aperiodic patterns. These anchors are then used to construct two tries whose root-to-leaf paths represent occurrences of these patterns. SUSs are found by locating the shortest paths that occur only once. We achieve this by formulating several instances of the
Minimum Skyline Point
problem based on these tries.

We apply
Theorem

2.1
on
S
with
\tau:=\lfloor\frac{1}{3}\log^{4}n\rfloor
and denote the resulting
\tau
-synchronizing set by
\mathbf{Sync}
. The following proposition
is crucial: if a sufficiently long, aperiodic pattern occurs twice in the string, then every anchor within one occurrence must have a corresponding anchor at the same relative position in the other occurrence. Therefore, to find a unique pattern, we must locate an anchor whose surrounding fragments—those immediately preceding and succeeding it—do not occur around any other anchor and have minimal total length.

Proposition 1

(follows by
Definition

2.8
)
.

Suppose that a substring
X
of
S
with length
|X|=m\geq 3\tau
and period
\textsf{per}(X)>\frac{1}{3}\tau
occurs at distinct positions
i
and
j
in
S
.
Then for all
q\in[0,m-2\tau]
, we have
i+q\in\mathbf{Sync}
if and only if
j+q\in\mathbf{Sync}
.

We construct two tries
T
and
T^{R}
. For every anchor position
i\in\mathbf{Sync}
, we insert
S[i\mathinner{.\,.}n)
into
T
and
S[0\mathinner{.\,.}i)^{R}
into
T^{R}
. In both tries, the leaves are labeled with their corresponding anchor positions. For efficiency, we implement them as
compacted
tries. For any explicit node
v
, let
L(v)
denote the set of leaf labels in the subtree rooted at
v
; recall that
\textsf{str}(v)
denotes the concatenation of the edge labels from the root to
v
and
\textsf{sd}(v)
denotes the string depth of
v
.
Note that these definitions extend naturally to implicit nodes.
If an occurrence of a pattern
P
contains an anchor
i\in\mathbf{Sync}
, then there exist nodes
u
in
T^{R}
and
v
in
T
such that
P=\textsf{str}(u)^{R}\cdot\textsf{str}(v)
and
i\in L(u)\cap L(v)
. Moreover, the size
|L(u)\cap L(v)|
equals the number of occurrences of
P
, provided that
P
is sufficiently long and aperiodic. We formalize our task as follows; for convenience, for the remainder of this section, we assume that the parent of the root node is itself.

Two Trees SUS

Input:
Two rooted trees
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, each with size at most
N
and with leaves uniquely labeled from
[0,N)
, a weight function
w
with range
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
where
w(u)>w(v)
for every strict ancestor
v
of every node
u
, and an integer
k
.

Output:
A pair
(u,v)
of nodes
u
in
T_{1}
and
v
in
T_{2}
minimizing
w(\textsf{parent}(u))+\max(w(\textsf{parent}(v)),k-1)
, subject to
|L(u)\cap L(v)|=1
and
w(v)\geq k
(if they exist).

Lemma 3.11
.

Consider a
Shortest Unique Substring
instance and let
\tau:=\lfloor\frac{1}{3}\log^{4}n\rfloor
. If
S
has a SUS of length
\ell\geq 3\tau
and period
p>\frac{1}{3}\tau
, we can reduce its computation in
\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)
time to an instance of
Two Trees SUS
with
N=\mathcal{O}(n/\tau)
and
k=2\tau
.

Proof.

We begin by constructing a
\tau
-synchronizing set
\mathbf{Sync}
of
S
.
Using this set, we construct two compacted tries
T
and
T^{R}
.
For every anchor
i\in\mathbf{Sync}
, we insert the suffix
S[i\mathinner{.\,.}n)
into
T
and the reversed prefix
S[0\mathinner{.\,.}i)^{R}
into
T^{R}
, labeling the corresponding leaves with the anchor
i
in both tries.
We make all implicit nodes in
T
at string depth
k-1
explicit.
Hence, we have
N=\mathcal{O}(|\mathbf{Sync}|)=\mathcal{O}(n/\tau)=\mathcal{O}(n/\log^{4}n)
.

Any fragment
P
in
S
that contains at least one anchor
i\in\mathbf{Sync}
can be decomposed into
P=\textsf{str}(u)^{R}\cdot\textsf{str}(v)
, where
u
is a node in
T^{R}
and
v
is a node in
T
, both of which can be implicit.
If
P
has length at least
3\tau
and period greater than
\frac{1}{3}\tau
, then by the definition of
\tau
-synchronizing sets, all occurrences of
P
have anchors at the same relative positions. Specifically, any occurrence of
P
starting at position
j
must have
j+q\in\mathbf{Sync}
if and only if the original occurrence at position
i
had an anchor at the same offset (
1
).
Thus, if the node pair
(u,v)
represents the fragment
P
, the number of occurrences of
P
is exactly
|L(u)\cap L(v)|
.
To find a SUS, we must find a pair
(u,v)
of (possibly implicit) nodes such that
|L(u)\cap L(v)|=1
(uniqueness),
\textsf{sd}(v)\geq 2\tau
(to satisfy the anchor offset property), and
\textsf{sd}(u)+\textsf{sd}(v)
is minimized.

To do so, we set the weights of nodes in the two trees as follows.
In both tries, we set the weight of the root node to
0
and assign to each other node
u
a weight
w(u):=\textsf{sd}(u)
. We thus create an instance of
Two Trees SUS
with
k=2\tau
.
If the optimal node pair
(u,v)
satisfies
w(\textsf{parent}(u))+w(\textsf{parent}(v))+2<3\tau
, we conclude that
S
does not have a SUS of length
\ell\geq 3\tau
and period
p>\frac{1}{3}\tau
.
(Note that since we have made all nodes with string depth
k-1
explicit, for any node
v
with
w(v)\geq k
,
v
’s parent has weight at least
k-1
, and hence
\max(w(\textsf{parent}(v)),k-1)=w(\textsf{parent}(v))
.)
Otherwise, we return as a SUS the string
\textsf{str}(u)^{R}[0\mathinner{.\,.}w(\textsf{parent}(u))]\cdot\textsf{str}(v)[0\mathinner{.\,.}w(\textsf{parent}(v))]
—the indices of the
occurrence of this string in
S
can be inferred from the
common leaf label in the subtrees of
u
and
v
.

The time complexity depends on the construction of the
\tau
-synchronizing set, which requires
\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)
time using
Theorem

2.1
, and the construction of the tries.
For the tries, we first construct an LCE data structure over
S
in
\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)
time
[
31
]
supporting
\mathcal{O}(1)
-time LCP queries. We sort the
N
suffixes of
S
in
\mathcal{O}(N\log N)
time using merge sort, performing each comparison with an LCP query and a letter comparison.
Given the sorted list of suffixes and the LCE data structure, the tries can then be constructed in
\mathcal{O}(N)
time
[
30
]
.
The total time for constructing the tries is
\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)+\mathcal{O}(N\log N)\subseteq\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)
.
∎

3.4.1
Solving the
Two Trees SUS
Problem

We solve
Two Trees SUS
as follows.
We first decompose the trees
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
into
heavy paths
.
For each pair of heavy paths, one from
T_{1}
and one from
T_{2}
,
we then construct a
Minimum Skyline Point
instance, which we solve in linear time using
Lemma

3.10
.

Heavy-light decomposition.

We first recall the widely-used heavy-light decomposition
[
41
]
.

Definition 3.6

(Heavy-Light Decomposition
[
41
]
)
.

Consider a rooted tree
\mathcal{T}
.
We obtain a
heavy-light decomposition
of
\mathcal{T}
by marking each edge as either heavy or light as follows. For every internal node of
\mathcal{T}
, the outgoing edge leading to the child with the largest number of descendants is marked as heavy, while all other outgoing edges are marked as light; ties are resolved arbitrarily.
A maximal path of heavy edges is a
heavy path
.

A heavy-light decomposition can be constructed in linear time
[
41
]
. The following fact holds for any heavy-light decomposition:

Fact 2

(Lemma 1
[
41
]
)
.

In a tree with
N
leaves, any root-to-leaf path intersects at most
\log N
heavy paths in the decomposition.

Furthermore, we can construct a
heavy-path tree
that encodes the ancestral relations among all heavy paths and leaves. This is done by contracting every heavy edge, such that all remaining edges are light. In this auxiliary structure, each node corresponds to a contracted heavy path or a leaf. By
2
, the heavy-path tree has height at most
\log N
, which allows for efficient traversal across the original tree structure.

A reduction using pairs of heavy paths.

We first note that any internal node of a tree belongs to exactly one heavy path in its heavy-light decomposition.
We compute the heavy-light decompositions for
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
and consider every pair of heavy paths
h_{1}
from
T_{1}
and
h_{2}
from
T_{2}
, provided that they share at least one leaf label.

For a heavy path
h
, let
L(h)
denote the set of all leaves descending from the root of
h
.
We define the function
d_{h}:L(h)\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, where for some leaf
\ell\in L(h)
,
d_{h}(\ell)
denotes the weight of
\ell
’s lowest ancestor within
h
.
Namely,
d_{h}(\ell)
denotes the maximum weight of a node on
h
that is an ancestor of leaf
\ell
.
With these definitions, we have the following observation:

Observation 3.1
.

Given a heavy path
h
, a node
v
on
h
, and a leaf
\ell
descending from the root of
h
, we have that
\ell\in L(v)
if and only if
w(v)\leq d_{h}(\ell)
.

To solve
Two Trees SUS
, we show that it suffices to solve a
Minimum Skyline Point
instance for every pair of heavy paths
(h_{1},h_{2})
sharing at least one leaf label.
By
2
, each leaf belongs to at most
\log N
sets
L(h)
per tree. We can thus construct each subset
L(h_{1})\cap L(h_{2})
by enumerating, for each leaf label
\ell
, all
\log^{2}N
pairs of heavy paths above it.

Lemma 3.12
.

Any instance of
Two Trees SUS
can be solved in
\mathcal{O}(N\log^{3}N)
time.

Proof.

We construct the heavy-light decompositions of
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
in
\mathcal{O}(N)
time
[
41
]
. This process partitions each tree into a set of disjoint heavy paths.

For every leaf label
\ell\in[0,N)
present in both
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, we identify all pairs of heavy paths
(h_{1},h_{2})
such that
h_{1}
lies on the root-to-leaf path in the first tree and
h_{2}
lies on the root-to-leaf path in the second tree.
Since any root-to-leaf path intersects at most
\mathcal{O}(\log N)
heavy paths (
2
), there are
\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}N)
such pairs for each of the
N
labels.
For each pair, we generate a tuple
(h_{1},h_{2},d_{h_{1}}(\ell),d_{h_{2}}(\ell))
, where
d_{h_{1}}
and
d_{h_{2}}
are retrieved from the weights of the light-edge endpoints.
All tuples are generated in
\mathcal{O}(N\log^{2}N)
time in total and are stored in one list. We sort the list, using the heavy path identifiers
(h_{1},h_{2})
as primary keys and the value
d_{h_{1}}(\ell)
as the secondary key. This ensures that all tuples corresponding to the same pair
(h_{1},h_{2})
appear consecutively, and are then ordered by
d_{h_{1}}(\ell)
. Using merge sort, this step takes
\mathcal{O}(N\log^{2}N\cdot\log(N\log^{2}N))=\mathcal{O}(N\log^{3}N)
time.
Let us denote the sublist corresponding to the pair
(h_{1},h_{2})
of heavy paths by
\mathcal{H}(h_{1},h_{2})
.

Claim 3
.

Consider an instance of
Two Trees SUS
, in which the output nodes are restricted to a given pair of heavy paths
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
from
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
. Given the list
\mathcal{H}(h_{1},h_{2})
, we can reduce this instance in
\mathcal{O}(g)
time to an instance of the
Minimum Skyline Point
problem over a multiset of points of size
\mathcal{O}(g)
, where
g=|\mathcal{H}(h_{1},h_{2})|
.

Proof of claim
.

Recall that in the definition of
Two Trees SUS
, the integer
k
is given as a minimum weight on one of the returned nodes.
Let
\mathcal{L}=L(h_{1})\cap L(h_{2})
. Assume that
\mathcal{L}\neq\emptyset
; otherwise, the instance has no solution.
We wish to find nodes
u\in h_{1}
and
v\in h_{2}
minimizing
w(\textsf{parent}(u))+\max(w(\textsf{parent}(v)),k-1)
such that
|L(u)\cap L(v)|=1
and
w(v)\geq k
.
By
3.1
, this reduces to finding the minimum
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2}
such that exactly one leaf
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
satisfies
d_{h_{1}}(\ell)\geq x_{1}
and
d_{h_{2}}(\ell)\geq x_{2}
.
This is equivalent to solving a
Minimum Skyline Point
on the multiset
P^{\prime}=\{(d_{h_{1}}(\ell),d_{h_{2}}(\ell))\mid\ell\in\mathcal{L}\}\sqcup\{(\infty,k-1),(\infty,k-1)\}
, thus finding a solution
(x^{\star},y^{\star})
with minimal
x^{\star}+y^{\star}
—the extra point that we insert in the multiset twice ensures that if the primary skyline is not empty, then
y^{\star}\geq k
.
Given
\mathcal{H}(h_{1},h_{2})
, we can construct this instance in
\mathcal{O}(g)
time. Finally, to obtain a solution to
Two Trees SUS
, we select the nodes
u
in
h_{1}
and
v
in
h_{2}
satisfying
w(\textsf{parent}(u))+1=x^{\star}
and either
w(\textsf{parent}(v))+1=y^{\star}
or
w(\textsf{parent}(v))+1\leq k=y^{\star}\leq w(v)
.

We apply
3
to each pair of heavy paths that share at least one leaf label, and solve each instance of the
Minimum Skyline Point
problem using
Lemma

3.10
in time linear in the number of points.
Since the total number of tuples across all pairs of heavy paths is
\mathcal{O}(N\log^{2}N)
, the total time required for applications of
3
and
Lemma

3.10
is
\mathcal{O}(N\log^{2}N)
.
We maintain the global minimum value of
w(\textsf{parent}(u))+\max(w(\textsf{parent}(v)),{k-1})
found across all pairs of heavy paths and return a witness pair of nodes as the final solution.
∎

3.4.2
Wrapping Up

The final complexity of the long aperiodic case is determined by the combination of the reduction to the
Two Trees SUS
problem and the subsequent application of the heavy-path-based
Minimum Skyline Point
algorithm.

Lemma 3.13
.

Given an instance of
Shortest Unique Substring
,
we can compute a SUS of
S
in
\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)
time
if it has length
\ell\geq\log^{4}n
and period
p>\frac{1}{9}\log^{4}n
.

Proof.

Using
Lemma

3.11
, we reduce the long aperiodic case to an instance of the
Two Trees SUS
problem. The number of leaf labels (anchors) is
N=\mathcal{O}(|\mathbf{Sync}|)=\mathcal{O}(n/\log^{4}n)
.
As established in the reduction, constructing the synchronizing set, the LCE data structure, and the two compacted tries
T
and
T^{R}
takes

\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)
time.
We then solve the resulting
Two Trees SUS
instance using the algorithm from
Lemma

3.12
. The time required is
\mathcal{O}(N\log^{3}N)
and as
N
is in
\mathcal{O}(n/\log^{4}n)
, we obtain a running time of
\mathcal{O}(n/\log n)
.
Therefore, both the reduction and the solver fit within the target time bound of
\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)
. The algorithm identifies a pair
(u,v)
minimizing
w(\textsf{parent}(u))+\max(w(\textsf{parent}(v)),{k-1})
subject to the uniqueness and length constraints. Since the result of the
Two Trees SUS
problem provides the minimal unique extension for any pattern overlapping an anchor, the resulting substring is a valid SUS for the long aperiodic case.
∎

Periodic Case

In this section, we address the SUS computation in highly periodic substrings of
S
. Specifically, we consider substrings of
S
of medium length with period at most
\frac{1}{45}\log_{\sigma}n
, as well as long substrings of

with period at most
\frac{1}{9}\log^{4}n
. These two cases are handled using
\tau
-runs with
\tau=\lfloor\frac{1}{15}\log_{\sigma}n\rfloor
and
\tau=\lfloor\frac{1}{3}\log^{4}n\rfloor
, respectively.

For the first group of
\tau
-runs (where
\tau=\lfloor\frac{1}{15}\log_{\sigma}n\rfloor
), we employ their standard Lyndon representation (
Definition

2.4
).
These runs can be efficiently computed and grouped by means of
Lemma

2.1
.
Unfortunately,
Lemma

2.1
is not applicable when
\tau=\lfloor\frac{1}{3}\log^{4}n\rfloor
.
To deal with this, we utilize the recently introduced
sparse-Lyndon representation

[
15
]
that allows for efficiently grouping the
\tau
-runs for
\tau=\lfloor\frac{1}{3}\log^{4}n\rfloor
according to their Lyndon roots, but representing via their sparse-Lyndon root.
We formalize this discussion in
Lemma

3.14
.

Lemma 3.14

(Proposition 36
[
15
]
)
.

For any string
S
of length
n
over an integer alphabet
[0,\sigma)
, with
\sigma=n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, all runs in
S
can be computed and grouped by equal Lyndon roots in
\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)
time. For runs with period at most
2\lfloor\frac{1}{18}\log_{\sigma}n\rfloor
, we compute their standard Lyndon representations, while for runs with larger periods we compute their sparse-Lyndon representations.

To streamline the subsequent analysis, we hereafter slightly
abuse terminology
: the terms
\tau
-run, Lyndon root, and Lyndon representation will be used uniformly for the two classes of runs considered in this section. In the context of the second class (
\tau=\lfloor\frac{1}{3}\log^{4}n\rfloor
), these terms implicitly refer to their sparse-Lyndon counterparts.

This unified terminology is justified by the fact that both representations share analogous properties and can be handled identically within our algorithmic framework.
We group all
\tau
-runs by Lyndon root in
\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)
time using
Lemma

3.14
; the only difference is that for some groups, we compute the Lyndon representation of runs, while for others, we compute the sparse-Lyndon representation. For further details and a thorough discussion of sparse-Lyndon representations, we refer the reader to
[
15
]
.

Lemma 3.15
.

Let

be two runs in

(2)

(3)

(4)

4
Reduction of Packed SUS to Binary Alphabets

In this section, we show that the
Shortest Unique Substring
problem over an integer alphabet can be reduced to the binary case in optimal time without any blow-up in the space needed to represent the obtained string.
This reduction allows us to assume that
\sigma=2
for
Shortest Unique Substring
algorithms in the packed setting.

Lemma 4.1
.

Any instance of
Shortest Unique Substring
for a string
S
of length
n
over alphabet
[0,\sigma)
can be reduced to an instance of
Shortest Unique Substring
for a string
S^{\prime}
of length
\mathcal{O}(n\log\sigma)
over a binary alphabet. If both strings are packed, then the reduction works in
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n\log\sigma}{\log n}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n}{\log_{\sigma}n}\right)
time.

Proof.

Let
k:=\lceil\log\sigma\rceil+2
, and let
b(a)
for
a\in[0,\sigma)
be the binary representation of letter
a
padded to length
k-2
. We define the morphism
\pi(a)=0^{k}\cdot 1\cdot b(a)\cdot 1
.
We construct
S^{\prime}
by concatenating
\pi(S[0])\pi(S[1])\cdots\pi(S[n-1])
and appending
0^{k}
.

Correctness.

The use of the
0^{k}
blocks ensures that, for any string
T
,
\pi(T)
can occur only at positions equivalent to
0\bmod{2k}
in
S^{\prime}
. Consequently, a string
T
is unique in
S
if and only if
\pi(T)
is unique in
S^{\prime}
. We now show that a SUS of
S^{\prime}
necessarily corresponds to a SUS of
S
(and lets us retrieve it).

Let
U
be a SUS of
S^{\prime}
.
If
U
is a substring of some fragment
\pi(a)0^{k}
then its uniqueness implies that the letter
a
is unique in
S
, and thus that
a
is a SUS of
S
.
Otherwise,
U
must contain at least one occurrence of
0^{k}
.
Additionally,
U
cannot start (symmetrically end) inside a fragment equal to
0^{k}
as removing the first
0
would yield a shorter unique substring (this letter is fixed by the position modulo
2k
),
contradicting the minimality of
U
.
Thus,
U
must be of the form
x0^{k}U^{\prime}0^{k}y
(or
x0^{k}y
) with
|x|,|y|\in[1,k]
.

U
is a substring of
\pi(T)
, for some unique substring
T
of
S
of length
\ell
with
|0^{k}U^{\prime}|=2k(\ell-2)
—we claim that this
T
is a shortest unique substring of
S
.

To prove this, let
T^{\prime}
be a unique substring of
S
. We easily obtain a unique substring
U^{\prime}=\pi(T^{\prime})[k\mathinner{.\,.}2k|T^{\prime}|)
of
S^{\prime}
(by removing the prefix
0^{k}
from
\pi(T^{\prime})
).
By the minimality of
U
, we have
2k|T^{\prime}|-k=|U^{\prime}|\geq|U|=2k(\ell-2)+k+|x|+|y|\geq 2k(\ell-2)+k+2=2k(\ell-1)-k+2
.
Therefore,
|T^{\prime}|\geq\ell
and hence,
T
is a SUS of
S
.

Complexity.

We have
|S^{\prime}|=2k|S|+k=(2n+1)k=(2n+1)(\lceil\log\sigma\rceil+2)=\mathcal{O}(n\log\sigma)
.
As the reduction proceeds by constructing length-
2k
blocks letter by letter, it can be trivially applied to each machine word in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time (we insert
1\cdot 0^{k}\cdot 1
every
k-2
bits).
∎

We begin by defining an auxiliary problem closely related to

Similar to the solution for

, we solve the

problem by decomposing it into four cases based on the length
\ell
and the period

of the sought substring of
S_{1}
, obtaining the following result.

Theorem 5.1
.

Any instance of
Shortest Exclusive Substring
can be solved in
\mathcal{O}(n\log\sigma/\sqrt{\log n})
time when
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are given in packed representation.

We explain how one can adapt our algorithm for the
Shortest Unique Substring
problem to obtain
Theorem

5.1
in
Section

.
We next present an efficient construction of de Bruijn sequences in the packed setting, which may be of independent interest.

Definition 5.1

(De Bruijn Sequence
[
18
]
)
.

A
de Bruijn sequence
of order
k
over an alphabet
\Sigma
of size
\sigma
is a string of length
\sigma^{k}+k-1
in which every string from
\Sigma^{k}
occurs exactly once.

Lemma 5.1
.

A packed de Bruijn sequence of order
k
over the integer alphabet
[0,\sigma)
for
\sigma\geq 2
can be constructed in
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\sigma^{k}}{k})=\mathcal{O}(\frac{n}{\log_{\sigma}n})
time, where
n
is the length of the sequence. In particular, we can construct its prefix of length
\ell
in
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\ell}{\log_{\sigma}n}+1)
time.

Proof.

A
Lyndon word
is a string that is lexicographically strictly smaller than all of its proper suffixes.
As noted by Fredricksen and Maiorana
[
24
]
, the concatenation of all Lyndon words whose length divides
k
, listed in lexicographical order, forms a de Bruijn sequence.
Duval
[
19
]
provided an algorithm to generate all Lyndon words of length at most
k
in lexicographical order, generating each word by modifying the previous one.

We achieve the stated running time in the packed setting, by observing that
k\leq\lceil\log_{\sigma}n\rceil
, and using
\mathcal{O}(1)
machine words to iterate over all Lyndon words in
[0,\sigma)^{\leq k}
.

In what follows, we describe Duval’s algorithm
[
19
]
without proving its correctness; we only explain how it can be performed efficiently in the packed setting.
We will return a string
S
, initialized as
0
.
We then maintain a length-
k
string
w
initialized as
0^{k}
and a binary string
w^{\prime}
whose
i
th bit is set if and only if
w[i]=\sigma-1
(we update
w^{\prime}
together with
w
).
We repeatedly apply the following steps of the generation loop while
w[0]\neq\sigma-1
:

(1)

Locate
j=\max\{i:w[i]\neq\sigma-1\}
. This is done by finding the rightmost
0
in
w^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time using standard bitwise operations.

(2)

Replace
w[j]
with
w[j]+1
in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time.

(3)

The prefix
w[0\mathinner{.\,.}j]
is now a Lyndon word. If
(j+1)
divides
k
, we append
w[0\mathinner{.\,.}j]
to
S
. This takes
\mathcal{O}(1)
time as the appended string fits into
\mathcal{O}(1)
machine words.

(4)

Replace
w
with the length-
k
prefix of
(w[0\mathinner{.\,.}j])^{\infty}
.
This can be naively performed using
\mathcal{O}(k/(j+1))
operations.
Consider a potential function
\pi(w)=|\{i:w[i]=\sigma-1\}|
, noting that Step
2
increases
\pi(w)
by at most
1
. Conversely, in Step
4
, values
w[i]=\sigma-1
for
i>j
are replaced with
w[i\bmod(j+1)]
. Since
w[0]<\sigma-1
(the algorithm terminates when
w[0]
reaches
\sigma-1
),
\Omega(k/(j+1))
copies of
\sigma-1
are overwritten by setting
w=(w[0\mathinner{.\,.}j])^{\infty}
.
As
\pi(w)\geq 0
at all times, the total number of operations we perform in Step 4 is asymptotically upper bounded by the number of times Step 2 is executed, and hence the amortized running time of Step 4 is
\mathcal{O}(1)
.

After the last iteration, we complete the sequence by appending
0^{k-1}
.
Observe that, for each integer
\ell
there are at most
\frac{\sigma^{\ell}}{\ell}
Lyndon words of length
\ell
, and hence at most
\sum_{\ell\leq k}\frac{\sigma^{\ell}}{\ell}\leq 3\frac{\sigma^{k}}{k}
Lyndon words of length at most
k
. This proves that there are
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\sigma^{k}}{k})
iterations of the loop, and the algorithm takes
\mathcal{O}(\frac{n}{\log_{\sigma}n})
time (where
n=\sigma^{k}+k-1
and
k=\lfloor\log_{\sigma}n\rfloor
) as claimed.
To output a length-
\ell
prefix of this sequence we terminate the algorithm once the length of the output sequence reaches
\ell
, potentially removing up to
k-1
excessive letters from the final Lyndon word. Since
k/\log_{\sigma}n=\mathcal{O}(1)
, the total time complexity is

\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\ell+k}{\log_{\sigma}n}+1\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\ell}{\log_{\sigma}n}+1\right).

∎

Let us fix an instance of the

We first decide if there exists a substring of

\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)

occurs in

S_{2}
contains all the substrings of

We henceforth assume that an exclusive substring exists.

The algorithm presented below is an adaptation of the one presented in

Shortest Unique Substring
problem.
We first define an appropriate variant of the

problem and present an algorithm for it.

Minimum Exclusive Point

Input:
Two sets of points
P_{1},P_{2}
in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\geq 0}
.

Output:
A point
(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\geq 0}
(if one exists) that is dominated by at least one point
p_{1}\in P_{1}
, is not dominated by any point
p_{2}\in P_{2}
, and minimizes
x+y
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Any instance of
Minimum Exclusive Point
can be solved in
\mathcal{O}(|P_{1}|+|P_{2}|)
time, provided that
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are given as lists sorted with respect to one of the two coordinates.

Proof.

We adapt the right-to-left scanning algorithm from
Lemma

3.10
. In the original problem, the goal was to find a point dominated by exactly one point in a set
P
, which required tracking the highest and second-highest
y
-values (
y_{\max}
and
y^{\prime}
). For

, the requirement is to find a point dominated by
at least one
point in
P_{1}
and
no
points in
P_{2}
.

Assume
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are sorted by

-coordinate in non-increasing order. We perform a joint scan from right to left, maintaining
y^{(1)}_{\max}
and
y^{(2)}_{\max}
—the maximum

-coordinates encountered so far in
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
, respectively. Both are initialized to
-1
. For each unique

-coordinate

in
P_{1}\cup P_{2}
, we evaluate the candidate
x^{\star}=x_{i}+1

before
updating the maxima with the points at
x_{i}
. For this

, a valid

must satisfy two conditions:

(1)

y^{\star}\leq y^{(1)}_{\max}
(the point is dominated by

);

(2)

y^{\star}>y^{(2)}_{\max}
(the point is not dominated by
P_{2}
).

Such a
y^{\star}
exists if and only if
y^{(1)}_{\max}>y^{(2)}_{\max}
. To minimize the sum

for the current

, we choose the smallest possible

. We then update
y^{(1)}_{\max}
and
y^{(2)}_{\max}
with all points

and continue the scan. After processing all points, we perform a final check for

.

Since the scan visits each point in
P_{1}\cup P_{2}
once and updates the maxima in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time, the total running time is
\mathcal{O}(|P_{1}|+|P_{2}|)
.

F_{i}
is labeled with
1
if it occurs in
S_{2}
and with
0
if it occurs
exclusively
in
S_{1}
. Using bucket sort, we produce a lexicographically sorted list
F_{1}<F_{2}<\dots<F_{q}
of distinct fragments, each inheriting its label.

Next, we construct the string
S^{\prime}=F_{1}\#_{1}F_{2}\#_{2}\dots\#_{q-1}F_{q}\#_{q}
; where each non-
\#
position inherits the label of its fragment.
Our goal is to find the shortest substring
P
of
S^{\prime}
, not containing any
\#
, such that all occurrences of
P
of
S^{\prime}
correspond to positions labeled
0
. This ensures that
P
appears in
S_{1}
but not in
S_{2}
within the sampled positions.
To achieve this, we build the suffix tree
\textsf{ST}(S^{\prime})
and propagate the labels from the leaves to internal nodes: a leaf is labeled
0
or
1
based on its starting fragment, and an internal node is labeled
1
if any of its children has label
1
, and
0
otherwise.
We prune all branches starting with a
\#
, obtaining a trie of the fragments
F_{1},\dots,F_{q}
, where each prefix is labeled based on whether it occurs solely in
S_{1}
or not.

A shortest exclusive substring of length at most
\ell
, if one exists, is the path label of node
u
with label
0
that minimizes the value
\textsf{sd}(\textsf{parent}(u))+1
(which is at most
\ell
). This string is the prefix of the path label of
u
that is exactly one letter longer than the path label of its
1
-labeled parent. Note that if no node has label
0
, then there is no exclusive substring of length at most
\ell
.

The entire process runs in
\mathcal{O}(n^{2/5}+n/\log_{\sigma}n)=\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)
time, similarly to
Lemma

3.1
: we consider
\mathcal{O}(n/\log_{\sigma}n)
fragments, yielding
\mathcal{O}(n^{2/5})
distinct strings, which are then processed in
\mathcal{O}(n^{2/5}\log_{\sigma}n)
total time.

5.1.2
Medium Aperiodic Case

We first define the following analogous problem of
Shortest Unique String Pair
:

Shortest Exclusive String Pair

Input:
Compacted tries
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{F}_{1})
and
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{F}_{2})
of
\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
,
and two (multi)sets
\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{1}\times\mathcal{F}_{2}

with
|\mathcal{S}_{1}|,|\mathcal{S}_{2}|,|\mathcal{F}_{1}|,|\mathcal{F}_{2}|\leq N
.

Output:
Integers
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\geq 0
and a pair
(P_{1},P_{2})\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
, such that
(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2})
is minimized and for all
(Q_{1},Q_{2})\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
, either
\textsf{LCP}(P_{1},Q_{1})<\ell_{1}
or
\textsf{LCP}(P_{2},Q_{2})<\ell_{2}
.

In other words, we want to find a shortest pair of strings that are prefixes of some pair in
\mathcal{S}_{1}
but not of any pair in
\mathcal{S}_{2}
. For both prefix families and
(\alpha,\beta)
-families, we show how to adapt the algorithms from
Section

3.2
to
Shortest Exclusive String Pair
.

Solution for prefix families.

We consider the case in which
\mathcal{S}_{1}=\{(U_{1},V_{1}),\ldots,(U_{N},V_{N})\}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}=\{(U^{\prime}_{1},V^{\prime}_{1}),\ldots,(U^{\prime}_{N^{\prime}},V^{\prime}_{N^{\prime}})\}
and
\mathcal{S}_{1}\cup\mathcal{S}_{2}
is a prefix family, i.e. all
U_{i}
and
U^{\prime}_{i}
are prefixes of some common string and can thus be represented by their lengths. We first present the analogue of
Lemma

3.2
:

Lemma 5.3
.

Consider an instance of
Shortest Exclusive String Pair
in which
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
are both prefix families. For any integer
\ell_{1}>0
, let
I_{\ell_{1}}=\{i\mid|U_{i}|\geq\ell_{1}\}
and
I^{\prime}_{\ell_{1}}=\{i\mid|U^{\prime}_{i}|\geq\ell_{1}\}
. Further let

f(\ell_{1})=\min_{i\in I_{\ell_{1}}}\left(\max_{j\in I^{\prime}_{\ell_{1}}}\textsf{LCP}(V_{i},V^{\prime}_{j})\right),

with
f(\ell_{1})=0
if
I^{\prime}_{\ell_{1}}=\emptyset
. The pair
(\ell_{1},f(\ell_{1})+1)
minimizing the sum
\ell_{1}+f(\ell_{1})+1
is an optimal solution.

Proof.

The proof borrows heavily from
Lemma

3.2
.

Feasibility.

For a fixed
\ell_{1}
, let
i^{*}\in I_{\ell_{1}}
be the index attaining the minimum in
f(\ell_{1})
and let
\ell_{2}=f(\ell_{1})+1
. We will show that
(U_{i^{*}},V_{i^{*}})
with lengths
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})
is a feasible solution for
Shortest Exclusive String Pair
. For this it must be that every
(U^{\prime}_{j},V^{\prime}_{j})\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
has
\textsf{LCP}(U_{i^{*}},U^{\prime}_{j})<\ell_{1}
or
\textsf{LCP}(V_{i^{*}},V^{\prime}_{j})<\ell_{2}
. There are two cases for each index
j
:

•

Case 1:
j\notin I^{\prime}_{\ell_{1}}
. Then by definition
|U^{\prime}_{j}|<\ell_{1}
, fulfilling the first condition.

•

Case 2:
j\in I^{\prime}_{\ell_{1}}
. Then by definition of
f(\ell_{1})
, we have
\textsf{LCP}(V_{i^{*}},V^{\prime}_{j})\leq\max_{j^{\prime}\in I^{\prime}_{\ell_{1}}}\textsf{LCP}(V_{i^{*}},V^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}})

\leq\ell_{2}-1
, fulfilling the second condition.

Optimality.

Let
(\ell_{1}^{*},\ell_{2}^{*})
be an optimal solution with witness
(U_{i},V_{i})
. Since this solution must be feasible, we have for every
j\in I^{\prime}_{\ell^{*}_{1}}
that
\textsf{LCP}(V_{i},V^{\prime}_{j})\leq\ell^{*}_{2}-1
. Thus we have

\max_{j\in I^{\prime}_{\ell^{*}_{1}}}\textsf{LCP}(V_{i},V^{\prime}_{j})\leq\ell^{*}_{2}-1.

From the definition of
f(\ell^{*}_{1})
it follows that
\ell^{*}_{2}\geq f(\ell^{*}_{1})+1
, which implies that
\ell^{*}_{1}+\ell^{*}_{2}\geq\ell^{*}_{1}+f(\ell^{*}_{1})+1
.
∎

We define
\mathsf{maxLCP}[i]:=\max_{j,|U^{\prime}_{j}|\geq|U_{i}|}\textsf{LCP}(V_{i},V^{\prime}_{j})
for
(U_{i},V_{i})\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
and adapt
Lemma

3.3
to compute these values in
\mathcal{O}(N+N^{\prime})
time using auxiliary arrays
\mathsf{prev}
and
\mathsf{next}
on a sorted list of pairs from both families.

A proof for the following lemma is nearly identical to that of
Lemma

3.4
.

Lemma 5.4
.

An instance of
Shortest Exclusive String Pair
in which
\mathcal{S}_{1}\cup\mathcal{S}_{2}
is a prefix family can be solved in
\mathcal{O}(N+N^{\prime})
time.

Solution for
(\alpha,\beta)
-families.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a lexicographically sorted list of
N
strings, and let
L
be an array of integers, where
L[i]=\textsf{LCP}(\mathcal{S}[i-1],\mathcal{S}[i])
, for all
i\in[1,N)
. Let
f:[0,N)\to\{0,1\}
be a coloring function on the strings of
\mathcal{S}
, with
f(i)=0
for at least one
i
. In
\mathcal{O}(N)
time, we can find the shortest string
P
that is a prefix of some
\mathcal{S}[i]
with
f(i)=0
, such that
P
is not a prefix of any
\mathcal{S}[j]
with
f(j)=1
.

Proof.

For each
i\in[0,N)
with
f(i)=0
, let
L_{p}(i):=\textsf{LCP}(\mathcal{S}[j],\mathcal{S}[i])
where
j
is the largest index
j<i
such that
f(j)=1
. Similarly, let
L_{n}(i):=\textsf{LCP}(\mathcal{S}[k],\mathcal{S}[i])
where
k
is the smallest index
k>i
such that
f(k)=1
. If no such
j
or
k
exists, the corresponding value is
0
. All
L_{p}
and
L_{n}
values can be computed in linear time: if
f(i-1)=0
, then
L_{p}(i)=\min\{L_{p}(i-1),L[i]\}
by
1
; if
f(i-1)=1
, then
L_{p}(i)=L[i]
. The computation of
L_{n}
is analogous, but in the reverse direction.

By
1
, for any
i,j
with
f(i)=0
and
f(j)=1
, we have
\textsf{LCP}(\mathcal{S}[i],\mathcal{S}[j])\leq\max\{L_{p}(i),L_{n}(i)\}
. Hence, any prefix of
\mathcal{S}[i]
longer than this value cannot be shared with any
\mathcal{S}[j]
where
f(j)=1
. Thus, the length of the shortest desired prefix is

\min_{i\in[0,N),f(i)=0}(\max\{L_{p}(i),L_{n}(i)\}+1),

which can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(N)
time.
∎

5.1.3
Long Aperiodic Case

We apply the same technique as in
Lemma

3.13
. First, we construct
\tau
-synchronizing sets for
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, with
\tau:=\lfloor\frac{1}{3}\log^{4}n\rfloor
. Following the original algorithm, we build two compacted tries
T
and
T^{R}
containing the suffixes and prefixes of strings starting or ending at anchor positions. The tries include strings from both
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
; we distinguish them by coloring their leaves based on their source string. Since
n=|S_{1}|+|S_{2}|
, these tries occupy
\mathcal{O}(n\log\sigma/\log^{4}n)
space. The rest of the algorithm proceeds identically to the
Shortest Unique Substring
case, except when mapping pairs of heavy paths to the 2D domain. We use
Minimum Exclusive Point
to identify points dominated by
S_{1}
-leaves that are not dominated by any
S_{2}
-leaves. This ensures that the resulting substrings are exclusive to
S_{1}
.

5.1.4
Periodic Case

We apply the same technique as in
Lemma

3.18
. First, we group all
\tau
-runs from both strings by their Lyndon root. For each distinct root, we have a set of runs from
S_{1}
and a set from
S_{2}
.
For each Lyndon root, we construct a
Minimum Exclusive Point
instance where
P_{1}
represents the periods/length available in
S_{1}
and
P_{2}
represents those in
S_{2}
. Solving these instances allows us to determine the shortest periodic string that appears in
S_{1}
but not in
S_{2}
.
Since our solution for the
Minimum Exclusive Point
(
Lemma

5.2
) matches the asymptotic complexity of
Minimum Skyline Point
(
Lemma

3.10
), the overall time complexity remains identical to that of
Lemma

3.18
.
