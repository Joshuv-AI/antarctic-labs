Biochemistry-based machine learning algorithms in differentiating pleural effusion: current status and perspective
Authors: Wen-Jie Hou, Xu-Lei Hao, Ran-Tong Bao, Li Yan, José M. Porcel, Wen-Qi Zheng, Ya-Nan Xu, Zhi-De Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The differential diagnosis of pleural effusion remains challenging. Microbiological and cytopathological examinations are considered the gold standards; however, they are limited by their low sensitivity, subjectivity, invasiveness and prolonged turnaround times. Pleural fluid and serum biochemical tests offer the advantages of objectivity, short turnaround time, minimal invasiveness and easy accessibility, which can help pulmonologists estimate the risk of the target disease. However, their effectiveness is often suboptimal when they are used alone. Recent advances suggest that machine learning (ML) algorithms can enhance diagnostic accuracy when combined with multiple parameters. Several studies have applied ML approaches based on biochemical tests to diagnose pleural effusion, with preliminary results indicating an improved diagnostic performance. This article reviews the application of such algorithms in the differential diagnosis of pleural effusion, highlights their current limitations and provides recommendations for future research.

Introduction

Pleural effusion is the pathological accumulation of fluid within the pleural cavity and can be caused by more than 60 diseases [1, 2]. In the United States, approximately 1–1.5 million cases of pleural effusion occur annually [3, 4], whereas in the United Kingdom, the number is approximately 0.20–0.25 million cases per year [4, 5]. In China, the annual incidence of pleural effusion is 4684 cases per 1 million hospitalised patients [6]. Common causes of pleural effusion include congestive heart failure, malignancy, pneumonia, tuberculosis, liver cirrhosis, pericardial disease, post-surgical procedures and pulmonary embolism [7]. In China, pneumonia, malignancy, tuberculous pleuritis and congestive heart failure account for >80% of pleural effusion cases [6].

The clinical manifestations of different types of pleural effusions, such as cough, dyspnoea and chest pain, are not specific to any given disease. Therefore, an accurate diagnosis cannot be achieved solely based on the symptoms and signs; further investigations are required. Traditional diagnostic methods for pleural effusion include microbiology, cytology and biochemistry [8]. Generally, diagnostic methods with the advantages of low cost, rapidness and easy accessibility are used as first-line tools to verify the cause of pleural effusion. These methods include effusion cytology and Ziehl–Neelsen or Gram staining. To improve diagnostic efficiency and minimise hospitalisation duration, pulmonologists often employ additional methods, such as effusion cultures, nucleic acid amplification tests [9] and serum and effusion biomarkers. However, these diagnostic methods have limitations. For example, effusion culture is the gold standard for parapneumonic effusion (PPE), but it requires several days to obtain results and its sensitivity is only 56% [10, 11]. Cytopathological examination has 100% specificity for malignant pleural effusion (MPE); however, its sensitivity is only 58.2% [12]. Therefore, a large proportion of patients remain undiagnosed, despite the use of these methods. Under such conditions, histopathological examinations (pleural biopsies) are needed if a patient has a high risk of MPE or tuberculous pleural effusion (TPE). Sometimes, therapeutic trials with anti-tuberculosis drugs, antibiotics and diuretics are needed to determine the cause if a patient has high risk of TPE, PPE or heart failure-related pleural effusion. In this diagnostic sequence, biochemical tests and soluble biomarkers represent first-line, indirect tools aimed at risk stratification, whereas histopathological confirmation obtained through pleural biopsy is a downstream, invasive step, usually reserved for selected cases with a high suspicion of malignancy or when initial investigations are inconclusive. For example, low effusion adenosine deaminase (ADA) levels can be used to exclude TPE in clinical settings with low TPE prevalence, according to the British Thoracic Society (BTS) guidelines [13]. However, the majority of biochemical tests, when used alone, do not have sufficient diagnostic accuracy [14–16]. Therefore, a diagnostic model that integrates multiple biochemical tests is an effective strategy for enhancing diagnostic accuracy [14, 15].

Machine learning (ML) first emerged in the 1980s [17]. As a branch of artificial intelligence, ML analyses data using predefined algorithms to develop trained models [18]. These models can then be applied to new datasets for classification or outcome predictions [19, 20]. In simple clinical terms, ML refers to computer algorithms that learn from large sets of patient data, such as demographic, biochemical or imaging information, to recognise complex patterns and generate diagnostic predictions. When applied to pleural effusion, ML can integrate routinely available variables, including ADA, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and protein levels, to assist clinicians in distinguishing between causes such as tuberculosis, malignancy and heart failure. By identifying relationships that may be difficult to detect using conventional analyses, ML has demonstrated tremendous potential in medical diagnosis [21, 22]. In previous studies, ML has been widely used for the early detection and prognostic assessment of lung cancer [23], the diagnosis of breast cancer [24], risk prediction of type 2 diabetic nephropathy [25], and the prediction of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases [26]. In recent years, there has been an increasing amount of researches on the application of ML in the differential diagnosis of pleural effusions. This article briefly reviews these studies and offers perspectives on the current challenges and future directions.

Literature retrieval and study selection

We searched the PubMed database to identify relevant the studies. The last search was performed on 1 January 2026 and the search algorithms were as follows: (“machine learning”) and (“pleural effusion*” or pleurisy or pleuritis). We included studies that investigated the diagnostic accuracy of serum or effusion biomarker-based ML algorithms, as well as studies with some routine clinical characteristics (e.g., demographics, side of effusion, effusion volume, history, signs and symptoms) in the ML algorithms. However, studies that used only imaging and ML algorithms were excluded. We also manually searched the bibliographic references of the selected studies.

We assessed the quality of the included studies using the following items. First, were the inclusion and exclusion criteria for participant enrolment reasonable? In other words, did the participants represent the patients for whom the ML algorithms were intended to use? Second, did the ML algorithms use reasonable methods for variable selection, model construction and external validation? Third, did ML algorithms have good clinical applications owing to their low cost, easy accessibility and short turnaround time?

ML in the diagnosis of tuberculous pleural effusion

The first study to apply ML for diagnosing TPE was conducted by Neves et al. [27] in 2007. This study developed two logistic regression (LR) models using conventional biochemical indicators (ADA, LDH, lymphocyte percentage and protein levels) and disease duration. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of both models exceeded 0.95 [27]. However, this study did not perform internal or external validation of the LR models [27]. Moreover, LR represents the simplest form of ML, relying on linear assumptions and offering limited flexibility. Subsequent studies on ML-based diagnosis of TPE have employed a variety of models, including random forest (RF) [28], decision tree [29], multilayer perceptron [29], k-nearest neighbours [29], support vector machine (SVM) [30], artificial neural network [31] and light gradient-boosting machine (LGBM) [32]. The parameters used to construct these models included age, sex, disease duration, pleural fluid white blood cell count, ADA, LDH and lymphocyte count. Overall, an AUROC above 0.90 was found in eight of the 14 studies. In addition, the variables selected for the models were easily obtainable and cost-effective. However, these studies have limitations, such as small sample sizes and missing information that are inherent to retrospective designs. Wu et al. [41] applied the large language model ChatGPT-4 for the differential diagnosis of TPE. Like ML, large language models are also a branch of artificial intelligence. ML in diagnosing MPE

Although the specificity of pleural fluid tumour markers for diagnosing MPE can exceed 90%, their sensitivity is generally only approximately 50% [42, 43]. Therefore, combining multiple tumour markers may represent a novel approach to improve diagnostic performance [15, 16]. Conventional combined diagnostic strategies using biochemical markers include serial and parallel tests. Serial testing achieves higher specificity at the expense of sensitivity, whereas parallel testing increases sensitivity at the cost of specificity. However, evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicates that such combined diagnostic approaches do not significantly enhance the overall diagnostic performance of tumour markers [44]. In contrast, ML algorithms are more flexible and have the potential to improve the accuracy of MPE identification.

Several studies have applied ML models to diagnose MPE [38, 45–50], as summarised in table 2. Most of these studies explored the use of ML to integrate multiple pleural fluid or serum tumour markers and biochemical test results. Overall, the ML algorithms demonstrated higher accuracy than individual tumour markers. Notably, one study developed a stacked ensemble (stacking) model based on five ML algorithms. It analysed the associated costs and highlighted that the combination of pleural fluid carbohydrate antigen (CA)19-9, CA15-3 and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) was the most cost-effective and clinically valuable [48].

Among the existing ML models, pleural fluid CEA is the most frequently used variable, followed by LDH and CA125. XGBoost is the most commonly applied ML algorithm and these models generally achieve an AUROC above 0.90, outperforming individual tumour markers. Except for one study [46], the other studies did not perform external validation of their ML models [38, 47, 48, 50]. In the study that included external validation, the performance of the external dataset was suboptimal [46].

There are two inherent limitations in constructing ML models based on tumour markers. First, the measurement of tumour markers has not yet been standardised and the comparability between different assay systems remains limited, which can undermine the reliability of the models and the generalisability of the studies. Second, the cost of tumour marker assays is higher than that of biochemical indicators such as LDH or ADA, resulting in elevated costs for models based on tumour markers.

Porcel et al. [47] developed a decision tree-based ML model using age, body temperature, ADA and LDH, achieving an AUROC of 0.98. This type of model is more favourable, as it is cost-effective and relies on biochemical indicators such as ADA and LDH, which are measured using standardised assays. Even simple combinations of biochemical tests, such as the cancer ratio, can yield clinically useful diagnostic models [51, 52].

Notably, the confirmation of MPE is insufficient for effective disease management. It can only determine the approaches for managing pleural effusion (e.g., pleurodesis and indwelling pleural catheters). To initiate effective anti-cancer treatment, it is essential to ascertain the origin and treatable mutations of primary or metastatic pleural cancers. Some tumour markers can help pulmonologists identify the type of primary cancer [16]. For example, our previous study indicated that high pro-gastrin-releasing peptide levels are indicative of small-cell lung cancer [53]. It would be of great value if further studies could establish ML algorithms with high accuracy, affordability and accessibility to identify a given primary cancer.

ML in diagnosing PPE

The gold standard for diagnosing PPE is effusion culture; however, its sensitivity is less than 60% [2]. Consequently, in clinical practice, the diagnosis of PPE is primarily based on the patient's clinical characteristics, including medical history, blood tests, physical signs, imaging findings and response to antibiotic therapy. Although pleural fluid and serum biomarkers have some diagnostic value in PPE, their performance is suboptimal when used alone [54].

To date, only one study (n=2253) has applied ML algorithms, including LR, SVM, RF and LGBM, to classify pleural effusions of unknown aetiology [55]. Among these, the LGBM model based on 18 features achieved an AUROC of 0.89 for predicting PPE in both the test and external validation sets. Therefore, future efforts should focus on identifying novel diagnostic biomarkers for PPE to provide valuable variables for the construction of ML models.

ML in diagnosing heart failure–related pleural effusion

The diagnosis of heart failure-related pleural effusion primarily relies on the characteristics of the pleural fluid (transudate), the patient's response to diuretic therapy and natriuretic peptide levels in pleural fluid or serum. Notably, pleural fluid N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) has a high diagnostic value for heart failure-related pleural effusion and is recommended by the BTS guidelines [13]. Therefore, no studies have applied ML algorithms to integrate serum or pleural fluid biochemical indicators for the diagnosis of cardiac pleural effusions.

Porcel et al. [56] developed an LR-based scoring system for diagnosing heart failure-related pleural effusion. The selected variables included age, albumin gradient, protein gradient, pleural fluid LDH level and bilateral effusions on chest radiographs. The scoring system (Porcel score) demonstrated a specificity of 95%, an overall diagnostic accuracy of 92% and an AUROC of 0.93. It can accurately identify heart failure-related pleural effusion when natriuretic peptide results are not immediately available. Recently, Porcel et al. [57] developed the BANCA scoring system using data from 2005 patients with pleural effusion. This model incorporated bilateral effusions on chest radiographs, age, pleural fluid NT-proBNP, pleural fluid cholesterol and albumin gradient, achieving an AUROC of 0.96. Given the excellent diagnostic performance of the Porcel and BANCA scoring systems, it is unlikely that ML can further improve the diagnostic accuracy of heart failure-related pleural effusion.

Biomarker-based ML algorithm in undiagnosed pleural effusions: our perspective

As previously discussed, current ML models based on pleural fluid biomarkers have mainly focused on the diagnosis of MPE and TPE. Although the studies differ in the variables included and algorithms used, they consistently indicate that ML can improve diagnostic accuracy. However, it should be emphasised that pleural fluid biomarkers, as well as biomarker-based ML algorithms, provide only a probabilistic estimate of disease and should not be regarded as the diagnostic gold standard for any type of pleural effusion. At present, most studies remain in the early research stage and a substantial gap persists between model development and clinical implementation. Notably, no study has evaluated whether the use of ML algorithms improves patient-centred clinical outcomes. Key questions therefore remain unanswered, including whether ML-based tools can influence treatment decisions, improve outcomes such as survival, quality of life, length of hospital stay or healthcare costs, or be considered sufficient evidence to justify the initiation of disease-specific therapy. Randomised controlled trials will be required to address these issues.

Beyond the gap between research and clinical application, several important limitations of the existing studies should be acknowledged. A first limitation relates to the cost, complexity and generalisability of the reported ML algorithms. An optimal model for pleural effusion should be simple, practical and broadly applicable, relying on a small number of readily available and inexpensive variables, such as ADA, LDH, total protein, glucose or cholesterol, rather than dozens of inputs that hinder reproducibility in routine practice [58]. Highly complex algorithms may achieve excellent performance in the development database but perform poorly when applied to new patients, a problem known as “overfitting”. Although variable selection techniques, such as the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator or Boruta regression, may help mitigate this issue, the final selection of variables should also consider clinical availability, costs and turnaround times. From a practical perspective, a model requiring a week to obtain a key laboratory result is unlikely to be clinically useful, regardless of its theoretical accuracy.

A second limitation concerns the representativeness and size of the study population. Each variable included in the model should be supported by a sufficient number of positive cases to ensure stable and reliable estimates. Moreover, the etiological spectrum of pleural effusion varies substantially across geographic regions [2], with tuberculosis accounting for more than half of all cases in some areas but being rare in others [59]. Consequently, models developed in one population may not perform adequately in different clinical environments. External validation in independent multicentre cohorts is therefore essential to ensure that ML algorithms can be reliably applied across diverse healthcare contexts.

The third limitation relates to the study design. Several methodological pitfalls affect diagnostic accuracy studies in patients with undiagnosed pleural effusions. Thoracoscopy is considered the most accurate diagnostic tool in this setting, despite its invasive nature. However, approximately 10–20% of patients remain without a definitive diagnosis, even after thoracoscopy [60, 61]. In routine practice, these patients require prolonged clinical and radiological follow-up to establish the most likely aetiology. In published studies, however, such cases are commonly excluded from final analyses. Although this exclusion is often unavoidable, it reduces the representativeness of the study population and, consequently, limits the generalisability of the results. Additional challenges arise from the definition of the reference standards. Up to 30% of patients with unilateral pleural effusions have more than one contributing aetiology [62], making the assignment of a single “primary” diagnosis difficult and often heavily dependent on clinical judgment. This inherent diagnostic uncertainty may compromise the reliability and external validity of the reported model performance. Furthermore, some studies have used the clinical response to anti-tuberculosis therapy as a surrogate reference standard for diagnosing tuberculous pleural effusion. This approach is problematic, as treatment response may be influenced by factors such as drug resistance or spontaneous resolution.

Finally, any ML model intended for clinical use should demonstrate that it adds meaningful diagnostic value beyond that provided by established individual biomarkers. For example, in the differential diagnosis of TPE, ADA alone is an excellent discriminator; therefore, an ML model incorporating ADA must show a clear and measurable improvement over ADA alone to justify its clinical adoption.

In summary, the ideal ML model for differentiating pleural effusions should be parsimonious, validated, reproducible, and easily applicable in routine clinical practice. The ultimate goal is not to develop increasingly complex algorithms, but to create intelligent and accessible tools that complement clinical judgment and help transform pleural fluid biomarker data into actionable diagnostic information at the bedside.
