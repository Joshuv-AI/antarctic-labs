ABSTRACT
This report presents a preliminary demonstration of integrated security/safety consequence
modeling to support potential physical security exemption justifications under the new Title 10 Code
of Federal Regulations Part 53 licensing pathway, which allows exemptions if sabotage-induced
radiological consequences do not exceed 25 rem at the site boundary two hours after release without
mitigative actions. A hypothetical heat pipe microreactor subjected to direct sabotage by a 150 lbs.
TNT-equivalent high-explosive device was analyzed using a simplified radionuclide release estimate
based on DOE-HDBK-3010-94, implemented in the MELCOR severe accident analysis code to
model release transport and attenuation through reactor/building compartments, with MELCOR
outputs coupled to MACCS to compute offsite doses and the distance-to-threshold metric D25rem.
Six scenarios were evaluated to examine sensitivities to release pathway/building configuration and
radionuclide form (vapor versus aerosols with different size distributions). Results show a positive
correlation between leak path factor and D25rem and indicate that reactor building
compartmentalization can substantially reduce consequences (a single-story configuration reduced
D25rem by roughly an order of magnitude relative to a cavity-only configuration), while vapor versus
aerosol assumptions had limited impact for the modeled two-story case on the spatial grid used. The
study is intended as an illustrative workflow rather than a design assessment, and key data gaps and
methodological improvements needed to establish technically rigorous guidance for demonstrating
compliance with 10 CFR 53 dose criteria for advanced reactors are highlighted

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission’s new 10 CFR 53 rulemaking enables certain physical
security exemptions for advanced reactors if a licensee can demonstrate that consequences from a
sabotage-induced radiological release would not exceed 25 rem at the site boundary two hours after
release, without relying on mitigative actions. Because formal guidance for demonstrating
compliance with this dose criterion is not yet available, integrated security/safety modeling methods
are needed to support new reactor licensing.
This report provides a preliminary demonstration of an integrated security/safety modeling
approach for a hypothetical heat pipe microreactor subjected to direct sabotage using a 150 lb TNT-
equivalent high-explosive device. A simplified radionuclide release estimate was developed using
DOE-HDBK-3010-94 methods and implemented in the MELCOR severe accident analysis code to
model transport and attenuation within reactor/building compartments. MELCOR outputs were
then used as input to MACCS to compute offsite consequences, reported as D25rem, the distance
from the release point at which dose falls below 25 rem.
A set of six scenarios was evaluated to illustrate sensitivity to release pathway and building
configuration and radionuclide physical form (vapor versus aerosols with different size
distributions). Key observations include:
 • Building source term attenuation correlates with larger D25rem , as expected.
 • Reactor building compartmentalization strongly reduces consequences. Reducing the
 building from two stories to a single story lowered D25rem by roughly an order of magnitude
 compared to a cavity-only configuration, and the cavity-only case approached the
 consequences of a direct-to-environment release.
 • For the baseline two-story reactor building configuration, releasing radionuclides as vapor
 versus aerosols did not materially change LPF or D25rem on the spatial resolution used,
 though the smallest-bin aerosol case slightly increased LPF.
Overall, the preliminary results suggest that the number of compartments/barriers between the
reactor and the environment may be a dominant driver of sabotage consequence metrics such as
D25rem . If supported by higher-fidelity shock physics and radionuclide transport analyses, this has
implications for security design and adversary pathway evaluations, including tradeoffs between
barriers that reduce releases and adversary resource allocation between creating leak paths and direct
sabotage. However, the analysis relies on simplifying assumptions and data-limited representations
of advanced-reactor sabotage response; results are provided as illustrative examples only and should
not be used to draw conclusions about real advanced reactor designs’ ability to meet 10 CFR 53
criteria. Improved technical bases, better sabotage-response data, and more rigorous integrated
modeling are needed to support credible exemption demonstrations.

1. INTRODUCTION
In anticipation of advanced reactor (AR) licensing, the United States Nuclear Regulatory
Commission (NRC) has recently published rulemaking in Title 10 of the Code of Federal
Regulations Part 53 (10 CFR 53) that allows for exemptions to physical security requirements if AR
licensees can demonstrate that consequences from a sabotage-induced radiological source term do
not exceed 25 rem at the site boundary 2 hours after the initial release without mitigative actions [1].
Currently, no guidance exists for demonstrating how the 10 CFR 53 dose criteria can be met. To
facilitate AR licensing, new integrated security/safety modeling approaches are therefore needed.
This report presents a preliminary demonstration of integrated security/safety modeling for direct
sabotage of a hypothetical heat pipe-cooled microreactor (HPR) from a 150 lb. trinitrotoluene
(TNT) equivalent high-explosive device (HED). Using United States Department of Energy (DOE)
guidance from DOE-HDBK-3010-94, “Airborne Release Fractions/Rates and Respirable Fractions
for Nonreactor Nuclear Facilities”, a simplified release scenario was modeled using the MELCOR
severe accident analysis code. Results from MELCOR were used as input to MACCS, a consequence
assessment code, to calculate D25rem i.e., the distance at which the dose from the radioactive plume
released by adversary sabotage decreases below 25 rem.

 NOTE: The purpose of this work was to demonstrate how safety analysis tools could support
 justifying a security exemption under 10 CFR 53 rulemaking. However, because of
 data gaps regarding AR responses to direct sabotage, this work also made
 assumptions that may not reflect realistic reactor conditions or responses following a
 sabotage event. Therefore, all results should be treated as examples only, and the
 authors strongly advise against drawing conclusions about the feasibility of real-world
 advanced reactor designs meeting 10 CFR 53 requirements from this work.

This report is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the MELCOR and MACCS modeling
approach including the DOE-HDBK-3010-94 Five Factor Formula used to estimate the initial
radionuclide (RN) release from an HED attack on the reactor vessel. Section 3 discusses preliminary
results from the MELCOR and MACCS calculations. Finally, Section 4 summarizes high-level
conclusions and highlights key areas for future work to improve the technical rigor of this
preliminary analysis.

2. MODELING APPROACH
The MELCOR and MACCS codes were used to calculate radionuclide transport to the environment
and the resulting dose over distance curves, respectively [2]. The baseline sabotage scenario
examined for this work was direct sabotage of a 5 MW HPR using a 150 lbs charge. The 150 lbs
TNT equivalent charge was chosen as a conservative reference explosive based on subject matter
expert opinion. In alignment with 10 CFR 53, no mitigating actions were modeled.

2.2. MELCOR Modeling
The MELCOR HPR model developed for the MELCOR Advanced Reactor Source Term
Demonstration project was used as the representative microreactor design [1]. The MELCOR HPR
is a 5 MWth heat pipe-cooled reactor. The HPR core mass is 4.57 MTU. At end of life, the HPR
burnup is 1.998 GWd/MTU. The HPR is housed in a below-grade cavity underneath a two-story
reactor building (RB). The reactor cavity and RB floors are connected by a series of flow paths (FPs)
to enable natural circulation. The reactor vessel and RB both were modeled with FPs representing
leak paths to the RB and environment, respectively.

As a lumped parameter control volume (CV) code, MELCOR cannot capture direct sabotage
phenomena like shockwave propagation. Rather than attempting to simulate the physical response
of the HPR vessel to an explosive attack, this work focused on simulating the post-detonation
blowdown following the explosion-induced vessel overpressure. A simplified conservation of mass
and energy approach was used to approximate the vessel overpressure transient:
 • Complete combustion of the 150 lbs. TNT equivalent HED was assumed (i.e., no soot was
 formed). The duration of the HED detonation2 transient was 1 s.

 control function (CF) to match the ambient temperature of the reactor vessel CV

Potentially important phenomena that were not modeled include:
 • Sodium combustion from failed heat pipes: It is likely that direct sabotage to the reactor core
 will fail heat pipes, causing sodium to leak and oxidize. Energy released by sodium
 combustion could drive the release out of the vessel or the reactor building. Given the low
 decay heat of the HPR, additional energy sources from a sodium fire could be impactful.
 • Dose emitting radionuclides in activated cladding, structural materials, or sodium: The
 activity of activated non-fuel materials was assumed to be inconsequential compared to
 irradiated fuel. Not modeling releases of activated structural materials is a normal severe
 accident modeling practice for conventional reactors, although the authors note that severe
 accident modeling practices for advanced reactors are still being developed.
 • Radionuclide release and blowdown timing: The radionuclides and combustion gases were
 concurrently sourced into the reactor vessel. Realistically, there will be a time delay between
 the initial overpressure and the release of fission products where fission products are
 entrained and swept out of the reactor vessel as opposed to being pushed out while they are
 being generated.
 • Heat pipe failure: Compression of heat pipes from the HED shockwave could result in heat
 pipe failure along the heat pipe including in the condenser region (assuming the heat pipe
 working fluid behaves like an ideal gas6). Heat pipe failure in the condenser region would
 create a confinement bypass leak path where radionuclides could transport directly to the
 environment.

2.3. MACCS Modeling
The MACCS consequence analysis code was used to predict D25rem using the release fractions
predicted by MELCOR. Developed by Sandia National Laboratories for the NRC, MACCS is a
consequence modeling tool used to simulate the atmospheric transport and dispersion of
radionuclides released to the environment and their associated consequences [5]. MACCS has

detailed modeling capabilities including simulation of meteorology, protective actions, health effects,

The MACCS HPR consequence model published by Nosek in “MACCS Consequence Analysis
Demonstration Calculations for an Example Heat Pipe Reactor Source Term” was used for this
work [6]. Nosek’s input was based developed to demonstrate HPR consequence modeling using best

(MELCOR Accident Consequence Code System)”. A complete description of Nosek’s input is
available in its companion report. Details of Nosek’s input include:
 • Releases were modeled as a point source.
 • All aerosols were assumed to be in the smallest particle size bin.
 • The Regulatory Guide 1.145 Full Model was used for nearfield modeling.
 • A phantom, non-evacuating cohort was used to evaluate ambient dose. The phantom cohort
 takes no protective actions and receives no shielding. After 2 h, the phantom cohort receives
 perfect shielding so no further dose is accumulated7.
For this analysis, the standard radionuclides recommended for light water reactor applications in
NUREG/CR-7270 shown in Table 1 were used. It should be noted that nuclides shown in Table 1
for LWR applications may not necessarily account for unique isotopic inventories of advanced
reactors that could significantly impact health consequences.
The following changes were made to Nosek’s input:
 • Weather conditions were sampled using stratified sampling with 1-hour intervals (i.e., 24
 samples per day accounting for all weather conditions at a site). Weather conditions were
 sampled from 2025 site data for the Surry Power Station.
 • The Power Model for plume buoyancy was used. The decision to use the Power Model was
 a holdover from earlier versions of this work that did not use MELCOR where values for
 mass release rates and plume densities needed for the Density and Flow Model were not
 available.
 • The source term was modeled as a single plume segment. A linear release over 3600 s was
 assumed based on MELCOR results.
 • The release height was assumed to be 1 m from the ground as it is expected that adversaries
 would breach the reactor building close to the ground (i.e., as opposed to the roof).
 • The spatial grid was redefined to use logarithmic intervals with 0.1 km increments between 0
 to 1 km, 1 km increments from 1 to 10 km, and 10 km increments from 10 to 100 km.

3. MODELING RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The MELCOR and MACCS codes were used to estimate D25rem for estimated direct sabotage source
terms from the HPR. The MACCS results are presented as 95th quantile of peak doses calculated
over each interval of the spatial grid over all weather samples8.

 NOTE: The results shown in this section were produced by simulations with conservative,
 unvalidated assumptions and inputs that may not reflect realistic reactor conditions or
 responses following a sabotage event. The authors advise against drawing conclusions
 about the feasibility of real-world advanced reactor designs meeting 10 CFR 53
 security requirements from these results.

3.1. MELCOR Scenarios
To demonstrate consequence modeling capabilities, a set of scenarios were run with initial
conditions designed to promote RN transport to the environment. The scenarios are listed in

Table 3 with a brief of the modeled initial conditions, LPFs for XE and non-XE RN classes9, and
D25rem. The scenarios modeled were:

 • Scenario 1 (Full release): The RN source term was released directly to the environment with
 no attenuation from the reactor building i.e., LPF equals 1.0. Scenario 1 was modeled for
 comparison purposes only.
 • Scenario 2 (Base scenario): The MELCOR HPR model was run as described in Section 2.2
 (RN sourced in as vapor, 2 story RB above reactor cavity). The pathway for RN to transport
 to the environment includes the reactor cavity and both floors of the RB.
 • Scenario 3 (Cavity-only RB) and Scenario 4 (Single story RB): To investigate how different
 RB configurations could affect LPF, two scenarios were run with a modified RB – Scenario
 3 where the reactor cavity leaks directly to the environment and Scenario 4 where the RB
 was reduced to a single floor above the reactor cavity.
 • Scenario 5 (Smallest-bin aerosol release) and Scenario 6 (Uniform aerosol release): RN released
 from direct sabotage of reactor fuel are expected to be in the form of aerosols. Aerosols are
 less mobile than vapors, meaning an aerosol source term is expected to have a smaller LPF.
 To investigate the effects of different aerosol size distributions, two scenarios were run
 where RN were sourced as aerosols. Scenario 5 assumed direct sabotage produced aerosols
 only in the smallest MELCOR aerosol size bin10, and Scenario 6 assumed aerosols were
 uniformly distributed across all size bins. XE was sourced as an aerosol for both scenarios.

3.2. MACCS Results

 Figure 2. 95th quantile doses over distance for all MELCOR scenarios relative to 25 rem.

Figure 2 shows the 95th quantile peak dose over distance calculated by MACCS for the six
MELCOR scenarios listed in

Table 3. The following results were observed:

 • LPF and D25rem showed a positive correlation. This result was expected as larger RN releases
 should produce higher doses at the same distance.
 • RN attenuation in the RB created a noticeable reduction in D25rem. D25rem for the single-story
 RB model was a factor of 10 lower than the cavity-only model. D25rem for the cavity-only
 scenario was effectively the same as a direct release to the environment. The dose for the
 direct release scenario was slightly higher than the cavity-only scenario which indicates some
 RN attenuation from the reactor cavity.
 • Sourcing RN as vapor or aerosols did not influence LPF for the base 2 story RB
 configuration. Sourcing in RN in the smallest aerosol size bin slightly increased LPF but did
 not produce an observable difference D25rem on the spatial grid used for this work.
Tentatively, the results of the MACCS simulations suggest that the number of compartments
separating the reactor from the environment has the strongest influence on D25rem. If this conclusion
holds, this has multiple implications for security design and adversary pathway analyses. With respect
to security design, increasing the number of barriers or compartments between the reactor and the

environment could require adversaries to use explosives (bounded by the design basis threat) to
create leak pathways which reduces the lbs. of explosives available for direct sabotage. This creates
an optimization problem for pathway analyses where identifying the “worst case scenario” requires
balancing LPF against DR. However, stronger technical bases are needed before definitive
conclusions can be drawn from this type of analysis.

4. CONCLUSIONS AND PATH FORWARD
This report provides a preliminary demonstration of an integrated security/safety modeling
approach for a hypothetical heat pipe microreactor subjected to direct sabotage using a 150 lb TNT-
equivalent high-explosive device. A simplified radionuclide release estimate was developed using
DOE-HDBK-3010-94 methods and implemented in the MELCOR severe accident analysis code to
model transport and attenuation within reactor/building compartments. MELCOR outputs were
then used as input to MACCS to compute offsite consequences, reported as D25rem, the distance
from the release point at which dose falls below 25 rem.
Overall, the preliminary results suggest that the number of compartments/barriers between the
reactor and the environment may be a dominant driver of sabotage consequence metrics such as
D25rem . If supported by higher-fidelity shock physics and radionuclide transport analyses, this has
implications for security design and adversary pathway evaluations, including tradeoffs between
barriers that reduce releases and adversary resource allocation between creating leak paths and direct
sabotage.
As a preliminary demonstration analysis, there are multiple areas where research is needed to build a
defensible technical basis suitable for licensing activities.
 • Shock physics simulations of advanced reactor direct sabotage are needed to estimate DR,
 ARF, and RF. Coupling of shock physics codes with MELCOR may be required to accurate
 simulate the RB response to detonation of an HED.
 • Estimates for LPF could be refined by using to simulate the reactor vessel response
 following direct sabotage. Hot gases expelled from the reactor vessel following detonation of
 an HED are expected to create buoyant internal plumes inside the RB that would more
 readily transport to the environment. Capturing this phenomenon will require coupled
 explosion and computational fluid dynamics simulations.
 • The temperatures produced by HED interactions with fuel may volatilize fission products,
 further increasing the release from sabotage fuel. Integration of fuel particulate temperature
 histories derived from shock physics calculations and fuel performance codes (e.g., BISON)
 may be necessary to accurately predict the total RN release from direct sabotage.
Ideally, experimental validation of direct sabotage simulations will be also performed. However,
given the costs and timeframes associated with large experimental campaigns compared to near-term
advanced reactor licensing timelines, heavy reliance on modeling/simulation to support advanced
reactor licensing may be required.
