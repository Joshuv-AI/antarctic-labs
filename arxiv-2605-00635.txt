Nonlocal Approximation Principle for Entropy Solutions of Scalar Conservation Laws
Alexander Keimer, Lukas Pflug

Abstract
We establish a general nonlocal approximation principle for the entropy solutions of scalar conservation laws on ℝ \mathbb{R} . More precisely, we show that the entropy solution to a nonnegative initial datum can be obtained as a weak-star limit of a corresponding scalar nonlocal conservation law. The flux function of the nonlocal conservation law depends on suitable spatial averages of the density. The proof is based on a reformulation on the Hamilton–Jacobi level: working with the primitives, we identify the limit via the stability properties of viscosity solutions; we then recover the entropy solution using the classical relation between Hamilton–Jacobi equations and scalar conservation laws. We further show that the approximation extends, after a suitable shift, to sign-changing initial data, and we prove a quantitative convergence estimate for convex fluxes in terms of the first moments of the nonlocal kernels. This result makes it possible to define entropy solutions for general fluxes using their nonlocal approximations, which satisfy the requirement for a finite speed of mass propagation, a key feature of hyperbolic conservation laws.

1.1
Theory of Nonlocal Scalar Conservation Laws and the Singular Limit

Given the (local) conservation law in
Eq.

1
, there are several structurally different nonlocal approximations.

The first class
acts directly on the flux:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}q+\partial_{x}\big(\gamma*f(q)\big)=0,\qquad\partial_{t}q+\partial_{x}\big(f(\gamma*q)\big)=0

(4)

where
\ast
denotes spatial convolution and
\gamma
is a nonlocal kernel. These “flux-filtering”approximations were introduced and their singular limits studied in
Coclite et al. 2025
. Since the conservative flux is nonlocal in these approximations, the corresponding solutions generally do not preserve finite-speed propagation of mass, similarly to viscous regularizations.

A second class
is based on a factorization

f(s)=sV(s),s\in\mathbb{R},
whenever possible, with a Lipschitz velocity
V
, leading to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}q+\partial_{x}\big(q\,(\gamma*V(q))\big)=0,\qquad\partial_{t}q+\partial_{x}\big(qV(\gamma*q)\big)=0.

(5)

The first of these equations, which is the subject of this contribution, is obtained by introducing two one-sided kernels into the nonlocal approximation in
Eq.

2
. Here, the convolution regularizes the velocity field, yielding well-posed transport dynamics in the spirit of DiPerna and Lions
DiPerna and Lions 1989
;
Keimer and Pflug 2017
. Moreover, mass is still transported through the local density factor
q
, so the finite-speed propagation of mass is retained.

Early numerical studies on the singular limit can be found in
Amorim et al. 2015
;
Zumbrun 1999
;
Blandin and Goatin 2015
;
Goatin and Scialanga 2016
. For monotone downstream kernels and decreasing velocities, convergence to the local entropy solution was first proven under additional monotonicity assumptions on the initial datum in
Keimer and Pflug 2019
.
For general kernels and velocities, it was shown in
Colombo et al. 2019
that strong
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-convergence may fail and that only weak convergence, or no convergence in certain cases, can be expected. The “positive effect” of adding viscosity was investigated in
Colombo et al. 2019
;
Coclite et al. 2021
;
Colombo et al. 2021
.

Subsequent progress relied on more refined compactness mechanisms:
Bressan and Shen 2020
used a reformulation as a system in a specific setting,
Crippa et al. 2021
obtained convergence under a one-sided Lipschitz condition despite the lack of uniform
\mathcal{TV}
bounds, and
Bressan and Shen 2021
established entropy admissibility for strong
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-limits in the one-sided exponential case. The first general result was obtained in
Coclite et al. 2022
using compactness of the nonlocal quantity rather than of the solutions themselves.

Further extensions have covered more general kernels and compactness estimates
Colombo et al. 2023
;
Colombo et al. 2024
;
Keimer and Pflug 2025
, spatially discontinuous nonlocal laws
Keimer and Pflug 2023
, and balance laws
Chiarello and Keimer 2024
or have focused directly on the solution rather than the nonlocal quantity
De Nitti and Huang 2025
.
The
\mathcal{TV}
assumption on the initial datum was recently removed in
Coclite et al. 2025
via compensated compactness
Tartar 1979
, yielding convergence for merely
\mathcal{L}^{\infty}
-data. Oleĭnik-type estimates
Oleinik 1957
;
Oleĭnik 1963
for such nonlocal approximations were presented in
Coclite et al. 2024
under restrictive assumptions on the data, nonlinear velocity, and kernel.
Regarding numerical schemes that are robust under the singular limit, we refer to
Huang and Du 2024
;
De Nitti and Huang 2025
.

For related problems on nonlocal conservation laws in which the nonlocality is in space and time simultaneously, as well as the related singular limit, we refer to
Du et al. 2023
. For results regarding cases in which the integral operator is of
\mathcal{L}^{p}
type, not
\mathcal{L}^{1}
, and the corresponding singular limit, we refer to
Chiarello et al. 2025
.

A third class
of nonlocal approximations is obtained by starting from the nonconservative form and regularizing the velocity of the underlying transport equation:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}q+\big(\gamma*f^{\prime}(q)\big)\,\partial_{x}q=0,\qquad\partial_{t}q+f^{\prime}(\gamma*q)\,\partial_{x}q=0.

(6)

The convolution yields a Lipschitz velocity field and hence well-posed transport dynamics in the sense of
DiPerna and Lions 1989
;
Keimer and Pflug 2017
;
Coron et al. 2020
. Moreover, uniform
\mathcal{TV}
-bounds imply strong
\mathcal{L}^{1}_{\textnormal{loc}}
-compactness and convergence to a weak solution of the local equation. This singular limit was studied in
Ghoshal et al. 2025
;
Norgard and Mohseni 2008
;
Risebro and Weber 2014
;
Coron et al. 2020
. However, entropy convergence requires strong structural restrictions, in particular, symmetric kernels and quadratic fluxes. Otherwise, even mass conservation may fail in the limit. This further motivates the conservative nonlocal velocity approximation considered in
Eq.

2
.

Among these three classes, the nonlocal conservation laws in
Eq.

5
are the only ones that simultaneously retain mass conservation and finite-speed propagation of mass at the approximate level. In contrast to the flux-filtering approximations in
Eq.

4
, the conservative transport of mass remains localized through the factor
q
; unlike in the nonconservative approximations in
Eq.

6
, mass is preserved. As a generalization of
Eq.

5
,
Eq.

2
is, therefore, particularly well suited for singular-limit approximations that are intended to preserve the hyperbolic character of the original conservation law.

Because
({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

(b)

Convergence of optimizers under uniform convergence:
Let
\Phi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R})
be arbitrary but fixed. Let
(t_{0},x_{0})\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}
be chosen such that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$*$}}}-\Phi
has a local (not necessarily strict) maximum in
(t_{0},x_{0})
.
Let

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}(t,x)\coloneqq\Phi(t,x)+(t-t_{0})^{2}+(x-x_{0})^{2}.

(32)

It holds that
\partial_{1}\tilde{\Phi}(t_{0},x_{0})=\partial_{1}\Phi(t_{0},x_{0})

\partial_{2}\tilde{\Phi}(t_{0},x_{0})=\partial_{2}\Phi(t_{0},x_{0})
, and so
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$*$}}}-\tilde{\Phi}
now has a strict local maximum in
(t_{0},x_{0}).
Defining
\overline{R}=\min\{1,t_{0},T-t_{0}\},
we obtain a sufficiently small neighborhood around
(t_{0},x_{0})
still in
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
so that
\exists R\in(0,\overline{R})
such that

\displaystyle\forall(t,x)\in B_{R}((t_{0},x_{0}))\!\setminus\!\{(t_{0},x_{0})\}\,:\,({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$*$}}}-\tilde{\Phi})(t,x)<({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$*$}}}-\tilde{\Phi})(t_{0},x_{0}).

(33)

Because
(t_{0},x_{0})
is a strict local maximum, for any
r\in(0,R)
, there exists
\kappa_{r}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that

\displaystyle\forall(t,x)\in B_{R}((t_{0},x_{0}))\setminus B_{r}((t_{0},x_{0})):\ ({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$*$}}}-\tilde{\Phi})(t,x)\leq({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$*$}}}-\tilde{\Phi})(t_{0},x_{0})-\kappa_{r}.

Now, choose
I=(x_{0}-1,x_{0}+1)
and, to apply
Eq.

26
, a
K\in\mathbb{N}
large enough that

and
k\geq K
.

Because of the locally uniform convergence of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}
to
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$*$}}}
, we can choose any
r\in(0,R)

large enough that

\displaystyle\|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$*$}}}\|_{L^{\infty}((0,T)\times I)}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{\kappa_{r}}{3}\ \forall k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq K_{r}}.

(35)

Now, we estimate
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}-\tilde{\Phi}
from above in

\displaystyle({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}-\tilde{\Phi})(t,x)

\displaystyle\leq\|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$*$}}}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}((0,T)\times I)}+({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$*$}}}-\tilde{\Phi})(t,x)

\displaystyle\leq({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$*$}}}-\tilde{\Phi})(t_{0},x_{0})-\tfrac{2\kappa_{r}}{3}\ \forall k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq K_{r}},

with the last line implied by

Eq.

35

From below, we obtain

\displaystyle({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}-\tilde{\Phi})(t_{0},x_{0})

\displaystyle\geq-\|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$*$}}}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}((0,T)\times I)}+({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$*$}}}-\tilde{\Phi})(t_{0},x_{0})

(43)

4.1

\Psi(t,y)\coloneqq\Phi(t,x_{0}+y-y_{0})+\tfrac{(x_{0}-y_{0})^{2}}{2\epsilon}

on both sides results in

\displaystyle Q(t,y)-\Psi(t,y)\leq Q^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$+$}}}(t,x_{0}+y-y_{0})-\Phi(t,x_{0}+y-y_{0}).

(76)

By construction,
Q^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$+$}}}-\Phi
is locally maximal in
(t_{0},x_{0})
; thus we have, for
(t,y)
in the vicinity of
(t_{0},y_{0})
,

\displaystyle Q^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$+$}}}(t,x_{0}+y-y_{0})-\Phi(t,x_{0}+y-y_{0})\leq Q^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$+$}}}(t_{0},x_{0})-\Phi(t,x_{0}).

(77)

We then obtain

\displaystyle Q(t,y)-\Psi(t,y)\leq Q^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$+$}}}(t_{0},x_{0})-\Phi(t,x_{0})

\displaystyle=Q(t_{0},y_{0})-\tfrac{(x_{0}-y_{0})^{2}}{2\epsilon}-\Phi(t,x_{0})

(78)

\displaystyle=Q(t_{0},y_{0})-\Psi(t_{0},y_{0})

(79)

and thus
Q-\Psi
has a local maximum at
(t_{0},y_{0})
. Since
Q
is a subsolution, it holds that

\partial_{1}\Psi(t_{0},y_{0})+f(\partial_{2}\Psi(t_{0},y_{0}))\leq 0,

and because the first derivatives of
\Psi(t_{0},y_{0})
and
\Phi(t_{0},x_{0})
coincide, we obtain

\partial_{1}\Phi(t_{0},x_{0})+f(\partial_{2}\Phi(t_{0},x_{0}))\leq 0.

Thus,
Q^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$+$}}}
is also a subsolution. According to
(
Bardi and Capuzzo-Dolcetta 2009
, Prop. 1.9.)
and Rademacher’s theorem,
Q^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$+$}}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{1}Q^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$+$}}}+f\big(\partial_{2}Q^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$+$}}}\big)\leqq 0\ \text{a.e.}

(80)

To obtain the subsolution property of
U^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}
, we make use of the convexity of
f
:

\displaystyle f(\partial_{2}U^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}(t,x))

\displaystyle=f\Big(\big(\partial_{2}Q^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$+$}}}(t,\cdot)*\phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}\big)(x)\Big)\leq\Big(f(\partial_{2}Q^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$+$}}}(t,\cdot))*\phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}\Big)(x)

(81)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}U^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\Big(Q^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$+$}}}(t,\cdot)*\phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}\Big)(x)=\Big(\partial_{t}Q^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$+$}}}(t,\cdot)*\phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}\Big)(x).

(82)

Thus, by convolution of
Eq.

80
with
\phi_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{1}U^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}+f(\partial_{2}U^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}})\leqq 0\ \text{a.e.}

(83)

and consequently—again by
(
Bardi and Capuzzo-Dolcetta 2009
, Prop. 1.9.)
—
U^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}
is a subsolution.

For the claimed inequalities, we obtain from
\partial_{1}V\geq 0
,
\partial_{2}V\leq 0
,

and
Section

5
the following:

(84)

Theorem 5.4

(rate of convergence of the nonlocal approximation)
.

Let
Q^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}
and
Q
be antiderivatives of solutions
q^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}
to the nonlocal conservation law in
Eq.

2
and the entropy solution
q
of
Eq.

1
, respectively.
Then, it holds for sufficiently large
k\in\mathbb{N}
that

\displaystyle\|Q^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}-Q\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})}\leq\mathsf{C}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$2$}}}\big(\mathsf{M}_{{}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$-$}}}}^{{}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}}+\mathsf{M}_{{}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$+$}}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}\big)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}

(87)

with
\mathsf{C}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$2$}}}\coloneqq 2q_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\mathrm{max}$}}}\big(T\big(2+q_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\mathrm{max}$}}}\big)\|\nabla V\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}((q_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\mathrm{min}$}}},q_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\mathrm{max}$}}})^{2})}\big)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
and
\mathsf{M}_{{}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$-$}}}}^{{}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}}
and
\mathsf{M}_{{}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$+$}}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}
as in
Eq.

64
. In particular, it holds for every large enough
k\in\mathbb{N}
that

\displaystyle\|q^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}-q^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$*$}}}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}((0,T);\mathcal{W}^{-1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}))}\leq\mathsf{C}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$2$}}}\,\big(\mathsf{M}_{{}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$-$}}}}^{{}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}}+\mathsf{M}_{{}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$+$}}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}\big)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

(88)

Proof.

To prove the convergence rate, we need to shift the derived
U^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}
and
L^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}
to obtain upper and lower bounds for
Q^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}
. Recall from
Section

5
that
\mathsf{C}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$0$}}}\coloneqq q_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\mathrm{max}$}}}+\tfrac{q_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\mathrm{max}$}}}^{2}}{2}
, and for the sake of simplicity, we define

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}\coloneqq q_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\mathrm{max}$}}}\|\nabla V\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}((q_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\mathrm{min}$}}},q_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\mathrm{max}$}}})^{2})}\tfrac{\mathsf{M}_{{}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$-$}}}}^{{}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}}+\mathsf{M}_{{}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$+$}}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}}{\epsilon}.\quad

We shift

and

as follows:

\displaystyle\underline{U}^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}\coloneqq U^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}-\mathsf{C}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}t-\mathsf{C}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$0$}}}\epsilon,\qquad\overline{L}^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}\coloneqq L^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}+\mathsf{C}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}t+\mathsf{C}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$0$}}}\epsilon.

(89)

By

\displaystyle\leqq 0.

(90)

By the comparison principle, which can be shown by considering the dynamics of

.
Again, by analogous arguments, we obtain

\displaystyle Q-Q^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}

\displaystyle\leq|Q-U^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}|+\mathsf{C}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}t+\mathsf{C}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$0$}}}\epsilon.

(91)

By
Section

5
,
t\in(0,T)
and the definitions of
\mathsf{C}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}^{{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$\epsilon$}}}}
, this becomes

\displaystyle Q-Q^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathsf{C}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$0$}}}\epsilon+T\mathsf{C}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$1$}}}\tfrac{\mathsf{M}_{{}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$-$}}}}^{{}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}}+\mathsf{M}_{{}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$+$}}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}}{\epsilon}.

(92)

Because we obtain a similar result by considering

\displaystyle\|Q-Q^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{\infty}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})}\leq 2\mathsf{C}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$0$}}}\epsilon+T\mathsf{C}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$1$}}}\tfrac{\mathsf{M}_{{}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$-$}}}}^{{}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}}+\mathsf{M}_{{}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$+$}}}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}}{\epsilon}.

By minimizing the right-hand side over

and
\mathsf{C}_{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$1$}}}

in the sense of distributions, we obtain for every

and a.e. in

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}}(q^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}-q^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$*$}}})(t,x)\phi(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg|

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}}(q^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$k$}}}-q^{\scalebox{0.6}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}$*$}}})(t,x)\phi(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg|

\phi
