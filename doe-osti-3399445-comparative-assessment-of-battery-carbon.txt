 1 INTRODUCTION

A “battery passport” is a digital record that provides comprehensive information about an
individual battery across its life cycle. This concept has been introduced by Battery Regulation
(EU) 2023/1542 [1], which applies to batteries for electric vehicles (EVs), industrial batteries
with a capacities greater than 2 kWh, and batteries for light means of transport (LMT) greater
than 2kWh sold in the European Union (EU) market – impacting manufacturers and exporters
across multiple jurisdictions globally. Among its reporting mandates, a key feature of battery
passports is the requirement for a carbon footprint (CF) calculation methodology supported by
enhanced data granularity to enable traceable and verifiable CF results. While no other
jurisdictions have yet adopted formal battery passport requirements like the EU’s, some are
developing CF calculation methods to comply with the EU Battery Regulation or are creating
CF-related regulations that could evolve in a similar direction, reflecting the growing importance
of battery CF guidelines for manufacturers seeking to remain competitive in global markets.

Under the EU Battery Regulation, companies are required to collect and report key data
categories, including general battery information (manufacturers, chemistries, location, date,
weight, capacity, etc.), CF, critical minerals contents, and circularity and resource-efficiency
metrics such as recycled content. The EU Battery Regulation recommends storing this
information in a decentralized digital product passport (DPP) repository for individual battery
packs. The economic operator that places the battery on the EU market is responsible for
collecting, maintaining, and storing the passport data, although this responsibility may transfer as
the battery is reused, refurbished, or recycled.

CF calculations are a cornerstone of CF-related battery regulations and a central component of
battery passports. For example, the EU Battery Regulation mandates a phased approach to
implementing these calculations. First, manufacturers must calculate and declare the CF of their
batteries. Second, these declared values are used to assign batteries to performance classes
(e.g., A, B, C), which are grades of batteries based on their CF levels. Third, the European
Commission (EC) uses the accumulated declarations and class distributions to set a maximum
CF threshold. Batteries exceeding this threshold may face penalties enforced by EU member
states. Several EU entities have contributed to the development of CF calculation methods,
including the Joint Research Centre (JRC), the Advanced Rechargeable & Lithium Battery
Association (RECHARGE), Catena-X, and Battery Passport Consortium (Battery Pass) [2-5].
Beyond the EU, Japan’s Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry (METI) has drafted an
unpublished methodology for automotive battery CF calculations to comply with the EU Battery
Regulation [6]. At the global level, the Global Battery Alliance (GBA) has developed the
Greenhouse Gas (GHG) Rulebook to comply with the EU Battery Regulation and aims to
provide a general framework for CF calculation on a worldwide basis [7].

While these guidelines share common ground in their approach to CF calculations, notable
differences exist. For example, they vary in criteria for determining when to use primary data,
approaches to multi-allocation methods, and the treatment of recycling credits. Understanding
these methodological differences is essential for manufacturers seeking to comply with
regulations across different jurisdictions or to prepare for potential future requirements. Making
this effort is particularly relevant for U.S. battery manufacturers, as the United States does not

 1

yet have a battery CF regulation or standardized methodology. Having a clear understanding of
the commonalities and differences among existing guidelines can help U.S. manufacturers better
assess their competitiveness, as CF calculations are closely linked to supply chain decisions,
manufacturing efficiency, circularity in battery design, and other key factors.

This study compares existing life cycle assessment (LCA) frameworks for CF calculations across
battery passport programs for EV batteries in different regions. These insights are then used to
develop a battery CF calculator. The calculator is designed to include the CF calculation methods
used in several battery passport programs by drawing on data from Argonne National
Laboratory’s Greenhouse gases, Regulated Emissions, and Energy use in Technologies (R&D
GREET) model [8, 9]. It is intended to help U.S. battery manufacturers assess the CF of their
batteries and fulfill requirements across different global battery markets. The CF calculation
methodologies investigated here and incorporated into the calculator include those available
from Europe (EU), the United States (US), Japan (JP), China (CN), South Korea (SK), India
(IN), and Canada (CA). These requirements were selected because they represent the world’s
largest global lithium-ion battery and EV markets and capture the widest diversity of
requirements for battery passports among those we investigated, enabling a robust and globally
relevant comparison.

This study is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews existing battery CF guidelines, regulations,
and plans that are providing the foundation for the comparative analysis. Section 3 describes the
identification of key aspects for comparison. Section 4 describes the life cycle of a lithium-ion
battery to establish the foundation for the subsequent discussion. Section 5 presents the results of
the comparative analysis on LCA frameworks and other essential pillars of the circular economy:
the requirements for traceability, third-party verification, and recycling of critical minerals and
materials (CMMs). Section 6 contains an illustration of a scenario analysis. Finally, Section 7
summarizes the main challenges identified and proposes potential topics for future research.

 2 REVIEW OF BATTERY CF GUIDELINES, REGULATIONS, AND PLANS

Figure 1 presents the battery CF guidelines and relevant regulations reviewed in this study.
These span three major regions: the EU, Asia (China, India, Japan, and South Korea), and
North America (the United States and Canada). In addition, the GBA is developing CF
guidelines intended to support global comparability and alignment with emerging regulatory
frameworks such as the EU Battery Regulation. However, achieving full harmonization remains
challenging due to the uneven pace of regulatory development across regions. Because no
unified battery passport or regulation currently exists across all borders, we investigate each
region in turn. In the following subsections, we examine each region’s battery-related regulatory
plans and battery CF calculation methodologies, comparing them with those of other regions.
At the end of this section, the guidelines that include detailed CF calculation methods are
summarized and selected for the comparative analysis in Section 5.

Figure 1. CF guidelines and relevant regulations and plans in different global regions

2.1 EU BATTERY CF GUIDELINES, REGULATIONS, AND PLANS

The EU is currently leading global efforts to develop a CF calculation framework for its battery
passport under Battery Regulation (EU) 2023/1542. In addition, the EU enforced the Carbon
Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) in 2023, a policy that requires importers to pay a
carbon price on certain goods entering the EU based on the carbon emissions generated during
their production. CBAM is relevant to battery CF calculation because it covers certain battery
supply chain materials – most notably, aluminum [10]. If the CBAM scope expands to include
additional materials, it could further influence battery CF calculations and reporting
requirements. Within this regulatory landscape, several organizations, including the Battery Pass,

 3

the EC’s JRC, RECHARGE, and Catena-X, are each actively involved in developing their own
battery CF calculation guidelines. Nevertheless, the guidelines for calculating battery CF for the
battery passport are not yet officially finalized, even though mandatory deployment of battery
passports in the EU is scheduled for February 2027.

2.2 ASIAN BATTERY CF GUIDELINES, REGULATIONS, AND PLANS

As shown in Figure 1, several Asian countries are actively working to align their battery CF
frameworks with EU Battery Regulation 2023/1542. Among them, Japan is the most advanced,
working closely with the EU to develop its own CF calculation framework. The other three
selected countries – South Korea, China, and India – have outlined plans to implement CF
frameworks that align with the EU regulation. Relevant regulations and plans from each country
were reviewed to understand the direction of the ongoing development.

In Japan, the METI has prepared an unpublished draft methodology for automotive battery CF
calculations that remains under development; this draft was obtained via communication with
contacts at the National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology (AIST) [6].
Additionally, METI has published CF labeling guidelines that provide a voluntary framework for
calculating and disclosing product-level, life cycle GHG emissions. These guidelines are based
on International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 14040/14044 principles and product
category rule-specific methodologies. Although participation is voluntary, METI’s published
guidelines provide a standardized method for quantifying the carbon emissions of battery
products [11].

In South Korea, the Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy (MOTIE), the Ministry of
Environment (MOE), and the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, and Transport (MOLIT) are
planning a battery life cycle tracking system for 2024–2027 to assess battery CF [12].
Additionally, the Environmental Technology and Industry Support Act, enacted by the National
Assembly of the Republic of Korea, requires certain designated enterprises to prepare and
disclose environmental management information, subject to possible government verification.
Battery manufacturers may need to comply with this act if they are classified as environmental
enterprises, apply for green certification, or participate in environmental industry programs [13].

In China, the Ministry of Ecology and Environment (MEE), the National Development and
Reform Commission (NDRC), and the State Administration for Market Regulation (SAMR)
issued an implementation plan in 2024 to establish a national CF management system; lithium-
ion batteries are included among the covered product categories [14]. Following this, the
Ministry of Industry and Information Technology (MIIT) published a notice on carrying out the
CF reporting of automotive power batteries in 2025. Under this directive, EV batteries larger
than 2 kWh sold on the Chinese market must report their CF through the MIIT CF tracking
platform (https://tzjhs.miit-eidc.org.cn/websites/). The reporting requirement will take effect in
2027 [15]. Additionally, China released the national standard GB/T 45565-2025, “Coding
Regulation of Lithium Ion Batteries,” in 2025, which requires a unique nine-digit code for each
battery [16]. This nine-digit code will be linked to data that includes information such as
enterprise type, product type, battery type, and production type.

 4

In India, the Ministry of Road Transportation and Highways (MoRTH) issued a draft guideline
of Battery Pack Addhaar System in December 2025, which includes these high-level
requirements: unique identifier, composition, traceability, CF reporting, and real-time battery
performance and health [17].

2.3 NORTH AMERICAN BATTERY CF GUIDELINES, REGULATIONS,
 AND PLANS

Compared to the EU and Asia, North America appears to lag behind in battery CF regulatory
development. The United States does not have specific battery CF calculation requirements or a
standardized calculation methodology. In addition, the status of battery-related CF regulations
and calculation standards in Canada remains unclear.

In the United States, the Society of Automotive Engineers (SAE) International published J3327,
which is a proposed EV industry standard on battery traceability records; this is relevant to CF
accounting because approaches such as chain of custody (CoC) can be used to track materials
production and recycling, which, in turn, support accounting for credits from recycled content
usage in battery production and end-of-life (EoL) recycling [18]. Other U.S. policies are also
indirectly relevant to battery passports, including the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) provision
that defines tax credits tied to domestic sourcing of critical materials [19]; and the Uyghur
Forced Labor Prevention Act (UFLPA) [20], which drives supply chain due diligence
requirements that intersect with broader battery passport objectives. Additionally, California’s
Climate Corporate Data Accountability Act (SB 253) requires large companies doing business in
California (with more than $1 billion in revenue) to publicly disclose Scope 1, Scope 2, and
eventually Scope 3 GHG emissions, with phased assurance requirements. This act may require
battery manufacturers and other supply chain participants to report GHG emissions if they meet
the revenue threshold. In Canada, Natural Resources Canada is conducting work related to
battery passports; however, progress updates have not yet been shared publicly due to policy
constraints [21].

2.4 GLOBAL BATTERY ALLIANCE’S BATTERY CF CALCULATION

The GBA developed the GHG Rulebook to provide methodological guidance for calculating
battery CF and to support the implementation of the battery passport [7]. While the framework
builds on established standards such as ISO 14040/14044, ISO 14067, the EU Product
Environment Footprint (PEF), and the GHG protocol, achieving global methodological
harmonization remains challenging due to the evolving and regionally differentiated regulatory
landscape for battery CF reporting.

In 2024, the GBA conducted its second and significantly expanded round of voluntary piloting
for the battery passport, with participating cell manufacturers representing approximately 80% of
global EV battery manufacturing capacity. The pilot projects spanned three categories: battery
information; material provenance; and environmental, social, and governance (ESG)
performance [22]. Notably, GHG reporting rates were approximately half the rates of qualitative
ESG reporting, driven by concerns over commercial sensitivity and potential regulatory
implications. The pilots revealed several areas requiring stricter definitions to ensure consistent

 5

and comparable reporting. In particular, the definitions of primary and secondary data were
insufficiently precise – in one extreme case, one consortium classified its data as being entirely
primary while another classified equivalent data as being entirely secondary.

The pilot projects also tested two approaches to calculating the CF of electricity consumption –
the Physically Modelled Approach (PMA) and the Harmonized Market Approach (HMA) (see
detailed definitions of these two methods in Electricity Modeling Session in Section 5.2.2). The
two approaches produced different results for the same sites and imposed a dual reporting
burden, raising questions about whether a single standardized method should be adopted.
Additionally, the GBA’s pilot program prescribes a data integration approach to CF aggregation,
in which each site independently calculates and reports its own emissions data, rather than
relying on a single downstream entity to model the entire supply chain through a conventional
LCA. This approach proved difficult to implement, as it depended on the voluntary participation
and technical capability of every company in the chain. As a result, some consortia reverted to a
reverse data collection approach, in which battery manufacturers were responsible for collecting
information from upstream and midstream entities [23]. These findings suggest that, for the
battery passport to be implementable at scale, more prescriptive rules are needed – including
precise data definitions, a single standardized method for electricity emissions accounting, and
clear protocols for handling data gaps when upstream companies do not participate.

 3 KEY ASPECTS SELECTED FOR COMPARATIVE ANALYSIS

The LCA is a widely used framework for evaluating environmental impacts across the life cycle
of a product. LCAs are mandated under the EU Battery Regulation as the basis for calculating
battery CF. As a result, the key aspects of our comparative analysis were selected based on both
the four-stage LCA framework defined by ISO 14040/14044 and other critical elements
correlated with the broader application of LCA frameworks [24, 25].
According to ISO 14040 and 14044, the standard LCA framework comprises four main phases:
goal and scope definition, life cycle inventory (LCI), life cycle impact assessment (LCIA), and
interpretation [24, 25]. For the purpose of this comparative analysis of different battery passport
programs, we focused on the first two phases: goal and scope definition and LCI development.
As shown in Figure 2, the aspects selected for comparison within goal and scope definition are
scope, functional unit (FU), system boundary, and cut-off rules, while those within LCI are
primary data versus secondary data, primary data collection, energy consumption allocation, data
quality, multi-output allocation, EoL allocation, and electricity modeling.

Figure 2. Overview of data collection methodology and coverage in this study

The subsequent phases – LCIA and interpretation – were excluded from direct comparison for
distinct reasons. The LCIA phase was excluded because all reviewed frameworks employ the
Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) global warming potential (GWP)-100 factor
for CF calculations, offering no meaningful basis for comparison. The interpretation phase was
omitted because its outcomes are inherently case specific, falling outside the scope of a
generalized framework comparison. However, a broader discussion on the limitations of using
annualized CF reporting is provided in Section 5.2.3.

 7

In addition to these core LCA phases, this analysis addressed three other critical aspects. First,
we reviewed the requirements for CF reporting. Second, we examined requirements for reporting
recycling and CMMs to assess their implications for EoL allocation and supply chain
transparency. Third, we compared verification and assurance protocols.

 4 LIFE CYCLE STAGES OF BATTERY SYSTEMS

The life cycle of a lithium-ion battery is illustrated in Figure 3 to establish the foundation for
subsequent discussion. The cradle-to-grave battery life cycle encompasses five main stages:
mining and refining, manufacturing, distribution, operation, and EoL management. The
manufacturing stage is further divided into precursor and component manufacturing, cell
assembly, and finally module and pack assembly.

For the focus of this study, once a battery reaches its EoL and is collected, it can follow several
pathways. It may be reused directly in a second-life application, or it may be sent for
disassembly and recycling to recover valuable materials. The remaining components then go to
final disposal. These EoL processes create circular pathways, allowing recovered materials and
components to be (re)used in the manufacturing of new batteries or in other applications.

Figure 3. Life cycle stages of battery systems

Under the EU Battery Regulation, the following processes are excluded from the system
boundary: (1) manufacturing of equipment used for battery assembly and recycling, as its CF
impact has been deemed negligible; (2) the battery assembly process within the original
equipment manufacturer (OEM) system, as its energy and material consumption are negligible
compared to the manufacturing of OEM components; and (3) the battery operation phase (shown
in Figure 3), as it is not under the direct influence of manufacturers, unless design-stage
decisions by battery manufacturers are shown to have a non-negligible contribution to the CF.

 5 KEY FEATURES OF BATTERY CF FRAMEWORKS

The information presented in this section was gathered through a combination of literature
review and key contact interactions. These two methods are complementary, with stakeholder
insights helping to contextualize and validate findings from the literature. We employed a semi-
structured interview approach, tailoring questions to each contact’s area of expertise. For

of interview questions is provided in Appendix A.

5.1 REGIONAL APPROACHES TO CF GUIDELINE DEVELOPMENT

Based on our literature review and personal communications, we identified a notable pattern in
the development of CF guidelines and regulations. As shown in Table 2, most Western countries
follow a bottom-up approach, with public-private partnerships or industry consortia leading the
effort. This model tends to be highly flexible and decentralized, but can result in a slower, more
fragmented development and implementation process. In contrast, the four Asian countries
employ a top-down structure, with government agencies serving as the primary developers. This
approach is centralized but offers less flexibility for incorporating stakeholder input.
Understanding these distinct development structures can help inform more effective engagement
and collaboration with organizations across different regions.

5.2 COMPARATIVE ASSESSMENT OF EXISTING LCA FRAMEWORKS

As outlined in Section 3 (Figure 2), the comparative analysis is conducted across five aspects:
goal and scope definition, life cycle inventory, CF reporting, recycling and CMMs, and
verification and assurance. Sections 5.2.1 through 5.2.5 present the comparative analyses for
these five aspects.

 10

5.2.1 Comparative Assessment of Goal and Scope Definitions

This section presents the comparative assessment of the goal and scope definitions by
considering four aspects: scope, FU, system boundary, and cut-off rule.

While all reviewed guidelines cover EV batteries, their intended scopes vary. The GBA GHG
Rulebook, aligned with the EU Battery Regulation, applies to EV, industrial (>2 kWh), and LMT
batteries (>2 kWh). The EC JRC Carbon Footprint of Electric Vehicle Batteries (CFB-EV)
framework is more narrowly focused on EV batteries, with separate guidelines for industrial and
LMT batteries. The RECHARGE Product Environmental Footprint Category Rules for High
Specific Energy Rechargeable Batteries for Mobile Applications (PEFCR) applies to
rechargeable cells or batteries across a range of equipment and vehicles. Finally, Catena-X has
the most expansive scope, encompassing the entire automotive supply chain, not just batteries.

The system boundary and FU of an LCA are defined by a framework’s specific goals and vary
among different frameworks. While a full EV battery life cycle encompasses raw material
extraction, manufacturing, distribution, useful life, and EoL as shown in Figure 4, not all
frameworks include every stage (see Section 4 regarding the EU Battery Regulation).

 Figure 4. Comparative assessment of system boundaries

 11

For example, to align with the EU Battery Regulation, both the GBA/Battery Pass GHG
Rulebook and the JRC CFB-EV exclude the battery operation stage and define their FU as
1 kWh of the total energy delivered over the battery’s lifetime. Note that this definition is distinct
from 1 kWh of rated battery capacity typically used in the literature. As specified in the EU
Battery Regulation Annex II, “the total energy is obtained from the number of cycles multiplied
by the amount of delivered energy over each cycle,” meaning that the FU accounts for the
cumulative energy throughout the battery’s entire lifetime rather than its nominal capacity.
Meanwhile, the Catena-X framework, which centers on the automotive supply chain, narrows its
boundary to exclude the battery operation and EoL stages, defining its FU as “1 piece” (a single
battery pack). In addition, the RECHARGE PEFCR framework includes all life cycle stages and
uses the FU of 1 kWh of energy delivered over the battery’s lifetime.

In the goal and scope definition stage, the cut-off rule establishes criteria for excluding inventory
inputs or outputs that contribute minimally to the overall environmental impact. The summary of
these cut-off rules can be found in Table 3. The JRC and METI define the cut-off rule based on
input mass per component, while other guidelines define it based on environmental impacts. The
RECHARGE and Catena-X guidelines recommend a 1% cut-off based on total environmental
impact in a specific category and CF, respectively, while the GBA GHG Rulebook suggests a
3% cut-off based on total GHG emissions. In practice, applying environmental impact–based
cut-offs can be challenging because the environmental contributions of individual processes are
typically unknown before the impacts are calculated, while the purpose of the cut-off is to avoid
modeling processes with negligible contributions.

5.2.2 Comparative Assessment of Life Cycle Inventory

As shown in Figure 2, seven aspects in LCI were compared and discussed among different
frameworks: primary data vs. secondary data, primary data collection, energy consumption
allocation, data quality, multi-output allocation, EoL allocation, and electricity modeling.

Primary data vs. secondary data

A central point of comparison among different regulatory and industry frameworks is the
requirement for primary data – data collected directly from specific processes within the value
chain. In this context, “primary data” and “company-specific data” are considered to be
interchangeable terms.

These frameworks’ data requirements follow three distinct philosophies. The EU Battery
Regulation, JRC, and METI are prescriptive, mandating primary data for a fixed list of critical
components. In contrast, RECHARGE and Catena-X are conditional, requiring the use of
primary data whenever data are available and when data quality meets specified criteria. Finally,
GBA is the most stringent, using a hybrid approach that combines a mandatory component list
with a universal requirement to use primary data whenever available.

The EU Battery Regulation requires company-specific data for all activities related to the
battery’s anode, cathode, electrolyte, separator, and cell casing; however, the scope of “all
activities” is not clearly defined. Sampling data collected from different plants producing the
same battery model is not allowed. The guidelines from the JRC and METI align closely with the
EU Battery Regulation. The JRC defines processes into two categories: mandatory company-
specific processes and non-mandatory company-specific processes. It defines all processes
within the manufacturing life cycle stage as mandatory company-specific processes, effectively
requiring primary data for this entire stage and reinforcing the standards set by the EU Battery
Regulation. Non-company-specific processes are further divided into “most relevant” and “non-
most relevant” processes. For most relevant processes, either primary or secondary data may be
used; for non-most relevant processes, only secondary data are permitted.

The RECHARGE framework requires primary data for all processes under the reporting
company’s operational control. Additionally, RECHARGE defines a “Data Needs Matrix”
method to clarify the use of primary data and secondary data. When the company does not
control specific stages of the value chain, the acceptability of secondary data is determined
through the Data Needs Matrix based on process relevance and data availability. Notably,
however, secondary data are allowed for the processes that need mandatory primary data in
EU Battery Regulation if the company does not have the primary data.

The Catena-X method requires primary data when such data are available and also meet a data
quality standard. Catena-X is more permissive than the EU Battery Regulation because it does
not mandate primary data for specific components or life-cycle stages; instead, it requires
primary data whenever available and enforces transparency through primary data share (PDS)
and data quality rating (DQR) metrics. PDS is the percentage of product CF data derived from
company-specific data rather than secondary datasets, where a higher PDS indicates higher data
quality and greater supply chain specificity. DQR is a metric used to assess the quality of activity

 13

data based on criteria such as technological, temporal, and geographical representativeness,
where a lower DQR indicates higher data quality.

The GBA implements a hybrid model that is arguably the most stringent. It combines a general
rule with a list of specific requirements. Like Catena-X, it mandates the use of primary data
whenever available. However, it goes further by also requiring primary data for a specific list of
critical materials and components: nickel sulfate (or other) and cobalt sulfate (or other), lithium
carbonate and lithium hydroxide, lithium hexafluorophosphate (LiPF 6), lithium metal, natural or
synthetic graphite, silicon, cathode active material precursor, cathode active materials, battery
cells, and battery modules.

These guidelines also share common steps. When primary data are not required, the collection
process of secondary data typically proceeds in one of two ways: either by selecting the most
representative dataset as a direct substitute for a process, or by using multiple datasets to
construct a new LCI based on company-specific data or from the literature. The selection of
these datasets is often guided by specific frameworks. The JRC CFB-EV, GBA, and
RECHARGE PEFCR, for example, establish a hierarchy that prioritizes CFB- or environmental
footprint (EF)-compliant datasets. Figure 5 illustrates the JRC CFB-EV hierarchy for primary
data collection and secondary dataset selection. The Catena-X framework, however, does not
prescribe a fixed hierarchy, instead using a PDS and DQR to govern the selection and application
of secondary data.

Primary data collection

This section examines three key aspects of primary data collection: methods, time frames, and
regulation enforcement on primary data collection.

These guidelines employ two distinct primary data collection methodologies: A single-actor,
reverse-reporting approach, in which the battery manufacturer collects data from its upstream
suppliers; and an upstream data aggregation model, in which each supplier is responsible for
reporting its own data into the system.

Engagements with industry contacts and battery passport developers indicate that larger
companies often have internal IT systems capable of tracking all purchasing records (e.g., bill of
materials from the International Material Data System [IMDS]), which could satisfy company-
specific data requirements. The primary obstacle to utilizing this data is commercial sensitivity
and the subsequent unwillingness to share it publicly. In addition, companies may store data
across multiple systems, and thus consolidating it may require additional effort. Addressing this
data-sharing challenge is fundamental to achieving interoperability within the battery passport
ecosystem. A review of relevant literature suggests three core strategies: implementing role-
based access control and encryption within a trust framework; defining responsibilities via
standardized roles; and using verifiable credentials, logging, and data lineage support to ensure
traceability and accountability [27]. Our contacts at Umicore pointed in particular to the
effectiveness of the upstream data aggregation method. This approach empowers each supply
chain partner to collect data proactively, which eliminates the delays caused by waiting for
requests from downstream entities [28]. However, this approach received mixed feedback in the
GBA’s pilot program, where some consortia found it difficult to implement because not every
company in the supply chain – particularly smaller upstream suppliers – has the technical
capability or resources to independently calculate and store CF data [23].

Our review of LCA frameworks indicates a consensus on using an annual time reference for
primary data collection. All frameworks require the collection of primary data from the most
recent year, whether defined by reporting period, calendar year, or financial year. The use of an
annualized time frame for data collection serves two primary functions: it helps manage
reporting for dynamic operations and addresses confidentiality concerns by allowing companies
to mask precise, real-time manufacturing figures.

The EU Battery Regulation establishes an enforcement framework to ensure compliance with
data disclosure requirements. Failure to provide mandated information – such as CF and
company-specific data – can result in significant consequences, including mandatory corrective
actions, market withdrawal, sales prohibitions, and financial penalties determined by individual
Member States. These enforcement measures are designed to be effective, proportionate, and
dissuasive, promoting consistent compliance across the European Union [29].

Data quality

The quality of both primary and secondary data is critical for CF accuracy. Three main DQR
criteria are defined across all frameworks: technological representativeness, geographical
representativeness, and time-related representativeness. In addition, Catena-X defines two
additional criteria, completeness and reliability, while RECHARGE includes one additional
criterion: precision/uncertainty. Across these frameworks, DQR is typically calculated as the

 16

average of score ratings from the individual criteria, with lower scores indicating higher
data quality.

A CF value reported without an accompanying DQR is of limited utility, as it does not indicate
how accurately the underlying data represents actual conditions. Consequently, companies that
report lower CF values but have high DQR scores (indicating poor data quality) may not have
fully demonstrated genuine emissions reductions.

Multi-output allocation

This study identified significant variation in the requirement for the system
expansion/substitution method in dealing with processes that produce multiple products. We
therefore conclude that for multi-output allocation, methodological specificity is paramount.
Achieving a clear rule is critical to the comparability of different battery packs from different
manufacturers within a framework.

Multi-output allocation arises when a single system generates multiple products. According to
ISO 14044, the preferred hierarchy is: (1) avoid allocation through subdivision or system
expansion; (2) if unavoidable, allocate based on an underlying physical relationship (e.g., mass
or energy content); and (3) allocate using other relationships, such as economic value.

GBA, RECHARGE, and Catena-X follow the ISO hierarchy for multi-output allocation, as
illustrated in Table 5. In contrast, the EU Battery Regulation and the JRC do not allow system
expansion or substitution in CF reporting. Although different frameworks refer to “system
expansion” in their allocation hierarchy, CF results are generally reported for a single FU. In
practice, this approach is commonly implemented through “system substitution.” This method,
where the environmental burden of an avoided process is credited back to the system, allows the
final CF to be attributed to the original FU, aligning with reporting rules. Additionally, for
policy-oriented CF reporting, the substitution method is only viable when the counterfactual
scenarios for co-products are clearly defined and robust for multi-output cases. Notably, the
terms “offset” and “substitution” are often used interchangeably, although offset is not an ISO-
defined LCA terminology. As a result, effective policy-oriented CF reporting requires either a
clear definition of the counterfactual scenario or the avoidance of the substitution method.

The cut-off and substitution approaches have been applied in LCA studies and are in compliance

stating that the end-of-life (EoL) stage shall be modeled using the CFF [33]. Building on this
foundation, the EU Battery Regulation 2023/1542 does not explicitly prescribe a specific EoL
allocation method; however, Annex II on carbon footprint states that the methodology adopted
under Article 7 shall comply with the latest version of the EC PEF method and relevant PEFCRs

Regulation can be interpreted as implicitly aligning its EoL methodology with the CFF.
Similarly, the GBA GHG Rulebook notes that, in the context of the EU Battery Regulation CF
declaration, the CFF will likely be required through reference to the PEF and PEFCR
frameworks, although a final determination has not yet been made.

Within the automotive industry, some OEMs and industry groups appear to have preference for
the CFF methods. For instance, the Japanese Automotive Manufacturers Association (JAMA)
has been keen to utilize the CFF, and JAMA has collected data based on regulated data gathering
that allows them to inform the numerous CFF parameters. Furthermore, the United Nations
Economic Commission for Europe (UNECE) Informal Working Group on Automotive Life
Cycle Assessment (IWG A-LCA) formulation is proposing the CFF method, but they include the
caveat that practitioners may utilize the cut-off method if they deem that approach appropriate
(e.g., for lack of parametric CFF data). It is thus worth highlighting, as is done in the proposed
A-LCA text, that the CFF can be reduced to the cut-off method by setting parameters A and B to
unity, and ED to zero.

These differing characteristics make the selection of a single EoL allocation method for
regulatory purposes necessary within a framework for comparability purposes, as stakeholder
preferences often diverge based on their perspective. Further, although the EU Battery

 20

Regulation (Annex II) and JRC recommended the CFF for its comprehensiveness, its complexity
has created uncertainty regarding its final adoption by the European Commission.

Electricity modeling

Electricity modeling is needed to determine the CI of electricity consumed during a process. It
follows two primary approaches: (1) location-based modeling, which may use the average grid
mix; and (2) market-based modeling, which accounts for renewable energy certificates, supplier-
specific emissions rates, and the resulting residual mix. A third approach – physical-based
modeling – requires hourly matching between electricity generation and site load; however, this
method is currently discussed only within the GBA framework. Robust electricity modeling is
essential to LCA frameworks because electricity is often the largest contributor to CF – such as
for cathode production – and accurate modeling can incentivize manufacturers to adopt low-
carbon electricity sources while preventing double-counting and greenwashing. Frameworks
differ in their data requirements: JRC CFB-EV requires only secondary data even for on-site
generation or supplier-specific electricity, whereas other frameworks, including RECHARGE,
Catena-X, and GBA, require primary data when supplier-specific information is available or
when certain criteria are met.

Figure 6 illustrates the electricity modeling hierarchy across different frameworks. The GBA
Harmonized Market Approach (HMA), RECHARGE, and Catena-X primarily follow a market-
based approach, relying on contractual instruments to ensure the uniqueness of electricity claims.
When contracted electricity with specific attributes is unavailable, these frameworks default to
the residual grid mix – the remaining electricity balance after all contractual claims have been
allocated within a given location. In contrast, the GBA Physically Modelled Approach (PMA)
employs physical-based logic by requiring hourly matching between electricity generation and
site load for asset-specific or contracted electricity. As such, unique claims are not prohibited
under PMA as the consumption grid mix is used as the last option in the hierarchy; however,
electricity modeling is governed by stricter rules that require physical bounding between
electricity generation and demand. Under PMA, market-based claims are accepted only when
physical feasibility is demonstrated; electricity that does not meet these criteria is modeled using
average consumption grid mixes based on geographic location.

Electricity modeling remains the primary barrier to finalizing the CF methodology delegated by
the EC, which has already been delayed by several months [34]. Major concerns have emerged
from countries where heavy reliance on fossil fuels for electricity generation could result in
higher CF for domestically manufactured batteries [35].

5.2.3 Comparative Assessment of CF Reporting

A key finding from the comparative assessment is that all frameworks mandate the reporting of a
single, annualized CF value. While this requirement yields a simple metric (i.e., the single CF
value), it can mask significant “hotspots” from an external point of view. However, an important
internal opportunity actually emerges for the economic operator responsible for calculating the
CF: the operator will have access to the granular data needed to identify these hotspots, thus
positioning them to re-evaluate specific processes or partnerships and drive targeted
improvements in their supply chain.

5.2.4 Comparative Assessment of Recycling and CMMs

First is a discussion on recycling related topics relevant to Battery Passport development. The
information regarding recycling consists of two parts: (1) recycled content used in production,
and (2) recycling efficiency and material recovery at EoL. Next is a discussion of critical
minerals and materials (CMMs) under different jurisdictions. Last, the chain of custody (CoC)
approach is introduced, with an illustration of its interconnection with recycling and CMMs.

 22

Recycled content

Different battery CF guidelines distinguish among three categories of manufacturing-origin
material: internal process reuse/revert, pre-consumer scrap, and post-consumer material ( Figure
7). Internal process reuse/revert refers to manufacturing scrap that is reclaimed within the same
production process and should be treated through production yield or net material input
accounting. Pre-consumer scrap refers to manufacturing scrap that cannot be reclaimed or
reverted within the process that generated it and is sent to recycling. Post-consumer material
refers to end-of-life battery materials recovered after use by end users.

Figure 7. Three main recycling material flow in battery manufacturing

 23

Understanding the definitions of these categories is essential because only eligible secondary
material recovered from pre-consumer and post-consumer streams counts as recycled content
under the major frameworks [1,2,7]. It is noted that the guidelines reviewed here use the terms
“pre-consumer waste” and “post-consumer waste”. While these materials are ultimately
recovered and re-enter production as secondary materials, the CF frameworks use “waste” to
denote their status at the point of diversion. Outside of direct references to the guideline
language, this report uses more neutral terms such as “scraps”, “secondary material”, or
“stream”.

Article 8 of the EU Battery Regulation 2023/1542 requires that EV batteries containing cobalt,
lead, lithium, or nickel in their active materials must be accompanied by documentation
specifying the percentage share of these materials recovered from both battery manufacturing
waste and post-consumer waste, reported for each battery model per year and per manufacturing
plant. The Regulation defines battery manufacturing waste as material rejected during the
manufacturing process that cannot be reused as an integral part of the same process and needs to
be recycled. Reutilized materials, such as rework, regrind, or scrap that can be reclaimed within
the same process, are explicitly excluded from the recycled-content methodology.

The GBA and JRC follow similar logic to the EU Battery Regulation. The GBA defines recycled
content as the proportion of material input to production that has been recycled from a previous
system, reported under two categories: pre-consumer and post-consumer waste. Like Battery
Regulation, the GBA Rulebook defines pre-consumer waste as material diverted from the
manufacturing process that cannot be reclaimed or reverted within the process that generated it,
and it explicitly excludes reutilized materials, such as rework, regrind, or process revert, that are
capable of being reclaimed within the same process. The JRC provides the same indication in the
context of CFF calculation, stating that the CFF should not be applied to materials rejected
during battery manufacturing that are reused as an integral part of the same process, such as run-
around scrap, because these are not considered manufacturing waste; however, the emissions and
process inputs associated with handling such internal scrap must still be accounted for in the
corresponding life-cycle stage.

It is important to note that internal process reuse/revert in battery manufacturing may be more
limited than in other, simpler production processes. While internal process reuse/revert can
occur, it is usually more process-specific than in simpler metal-forming industries such as
steelmaking, where fall-off or run-around scrap from sheet metal stamping can often be directly
remelted. As battery materials move through electrode coating, cell assembly, electrolyte filling,
formation, and module or pack assembly, scrap often becomes composite or contaminated. Once
that happens, the material typically cannot be reclaimed within the same process. Instead, it
becomes pre-consumer scrap that must be sent to a separate recycling process before it can re-
enter production. This more complex recovery pathway is a key reason why the frameworks
reviewed here draw a clear line between process revert and pre-consumer secondary material,
and why only eligible secondary material recovered from pre-consumer or post-consumer
streams qualifies as recycled content. It is also worth noting that several of these frameworks
have not yet finalized their recycled-content definitions or methodologies, meaning the precise
boundaries may continue to evolve.

 24

Beyond the distinction between internal process reuse/revert and pre-consumer scrap, there is
also ongoing discussion about whether pre-consumer scrap itself should be counted as recycled
content. Improving production yield — that is, reducing the amount of scrap generated per unit
of output — directly reduces raw material demand because less input material is needed to
produce the same number of batteries. Recycling production scrap, by contrast, recovers material
that can substitute for virgin input in subsequent production, but it does not change the amount of
scrap generated per unit of output — the production process and its yield remain the same. For
this reason, some analyses do not regard production scrap recycling as increasing the total
availability of raw materials, given that the recovered material originated from within the
production system and was already accounted for as part of the input demand. In contrast, EoL
recycling reintroduces materials that would otherwise be permanently lost from the production
cycle, thereby expanding the available supply and contributing to material circularity. This
fundamental difference in how pre-consumer and post-consumer secondary materials relate to
overall material availability is one reason why the methodological treatment of pre-consumer
scrap in recycled-content accounting continues to be refined across the various frameworks.

Recycling efficiency and recovery of materials

According to Battery Regulation 2023/1542 Annex XII, recycling efficiency is defined as the
ratio of the mass of recycled output fractions produced by the recycling process to the mass of
waste batteries entering the recycling process. The recycling efficiency targets for lithium-ion
batteries are 65% by 2025 and 70% by 2030. Material recovery measures the fraction of a
specific material contained in waste batteries that is recovered in recycled output fractions. The
material recovery targets for cobalt, copper, nickel, and lithium are 90%, 90%, 90%, and 50%,
respectively, by 2027, and 95%, 95%, 95%, and 80%, respectively, by 2031. However, it is
notable that the method for determining how to account for EoL recycling remains unclear.
Specifically, the regulation does not adequately address how to treat recovered materials that are
exported to other countries, processed in facilities outside the original manufacturing region, or
diverted to applications other than EV batteries.

Critical minerals and materials (CMMs)

CMMs and critical raw materials (CRMs) both refer to materials that are essential to key
industries and subject to supply chain risks, but they differ in scope and criteria depending on the
jurisdiction – with CMMs being primarily used in the United States and CRMs in the European
Union. The EU Battery Pass requires that all CRMs present in the battery above a concentration
of 0.1% by weight must be reported in an aggregated manner for the entire battery. This CRM
data should be reported at the battery level or linked to specific data entries indicating the
component in which the material is used, such as the cathode, anode, or electrolyte. However, the
regulation does not specify whether the 0.1% concentration threshold applies to the total battery
mass or to individual component masses, and further clarification is needed.

An additional consideration is the variation in CRM definitions across jurisdictions. For
example, the United States classifies aluminum as a CMM, whereas the European Union does
not [37, 38]. These discrepancies arise because CRM designations in each country are
determined by assessments of economic importance and supply chain vulnerability. Not only

 25

that, but even within the U.S., more than one list of CMM definitions exists (e.g., the
U.S. Department of Energy and U.S. Department of War have unique lists) [39].

Chain of custody (CoC) and the link with CF

CoC frameworks, which focus on mass balance approaches to track materials throughout the
supply chain, play a critical role in monitoring recycled content used in the production, recycling
efficiency and recovery of materials at EoL, and critical material flow. CoC serves as a guardrail
to prevent the double counting of CF credits and ensure transparent reporting by incorporating
location data, mass balance records, and recycling verification. CF calculations should be linked
with CoC data – for example, mass balance information from standards such as SAE J3327: EV
Battery Traceability Record.

5.2.5 Comparative Assessment of Verification and Assurance of Battery
 Passport Reporting

Third-party verification is essential in helping to ensure data credibility. Verification models
such as the EU conformity assessment framework [40], the CEN-CENELEC 1 digital verification
approach using electronically signed data constructs (ESDCs), and the U.S. Customs and Border
Protection (CBP)/Altana digital passport system have been used to verify battery-related data,
including CF, material provenance, and supply chain traceability. The EU approach relies on a
combination of automated and manual (first-, second-, and third-party) assessment, while the
CEN-CENELEC model ensures data integrity through cryptographic signatures [41], and the
CBP/Altana system emphasizes product-level origin verification for trade compliance [42].

However, the verification process can be resource-intensive, as it typically requires pooled
audits; digital monitoring, reporting, and verification (MRV) systems; and standardized assessor
accreditation at scale. However, through communication with the GBA, the verification process
will most likely involve a checklist confirming that the required steps in the CF calculation or
battery passport have been completed rather than a detailed mass balance or measurement-based
review. Additionally, it was indicated that GBA intends to publish detailed criteria for third-party
verification in 2026 [34].

5.3 KEY FINDINGS FROM THE COMPARATIVE ANALYSIS OF BATTERY
 CF FRAMEWORKS

This section summarizes the key findings from the comparative analysis, covering the purpose of
the guidelines, key features, background and foreground data, and types of results. These
findings inform the development of the R&D GREET-based Battery CF calculator, which is
described in detail in Appendix A.

1 CEN = The European Committee for Standardization (CEN); CENELEC = the European Committee for
  Electrotechnical Standardization. Each is a private, international not-for-profit organization
  (https://www.cencenelec.eu/european-standardization/cen-and-cenelec/).

 26

Based on the literature review and key contact interactions, we have identified detailed battery
CF calculation guidelines for the EU, Japan, and the GBA. The JRC, GBA, Battery Pass, and
METI have developed guidelines to comply with EU Battery Regulation 2023/1542.
RECHARGE developed the PEFCR for batteries, and Catena-X developed the CF method for
the automotive supply chain. Given the upcoming enforcement of the EU Battery Regulation, an
increasing trend is for countries to develop their own life-cycle tracking and reporting systems
that comply with the EU Battery Regulation. This trend may expand to other jurisdictions as the
importance of CF reporting grows and the competitiveness of various supply chains becomes
more apparent.

Different guidelines have their own scopes, and as a result, their system boundaries vary. To
comply with the EU Battery Regulation, the JRC, GBA, Battery Pass, and METI define the
boundary from cradle to grave, excluding the battery operation phase. Additionally, if the target
is EV batteries, most guidelines define the FU as 1 kWh of energy provided over the battery’s
life cycle, which differs from the typical FU used in much of the literature.

Data transparency and availability are always key to LCA frameworks. The purpose of
developing battery CF frameworks is to address this challenge, as significant variations in carbon
emissions can occur for the same material across regions. Background data are used to account
for upstream impacts, whereas foreground data typically need to be collected by the user.
Foreground data are equivalent to company-specific data or primary data, which, according to
most guidelines, require companies to input their own information. When to use primary data is
specifically defined in each guideline, with the aim of ensuring that, based on past experience,
the most critical information is collected. However, with technological advancements, primary
data collection criteria may change over time.

Different multi-output and EoL allocation methods are defined across guidelines. For multi-
output allocation, the key is to use a consistent method and transparent assumptions. For EoL
allocation, there are trade-offs among the three methods. Tracking recycled content in production
and recycling efficiency at EoL are important parameters that can be incorporated into EoL
allocation. In addition, CMMs need to be tracked to comply with the EU Battery Regulation.

The required reporting metric under EU Battery Regulation is the total CF per kWh of energy
provided over the battery’s life cycle. In the R&D GREET-based Battery CF Calculator, this
value is calculated using the inputs and features discussed in previous sections. The total CF per
kWh of capacity is also calculated for comparison purposes. In addition, the results include a
breakdown by component. It should be noted that data quality should be presented alongside the
CF value in future reporting. Without the DQR, the CF value is difficult to interpret since users
cannot assess the reliability, representativeness, or uncertainty of the underlying data.

 6 SCENARIO ANALYSIS

Using the R&D GREET-based battery CF calculator, we conducted four scenario analyses to
assess the impact of: (1) recycling technologies and recycled content, (2) renewable energy
integration, (3) advanced manufacturing technologies, and (4) energy efficiency improvements.
The battery operation phase was excluded from all analyses to maintain consistency with the
scope of most reviewed frameworks. Additionally, for simplification and illustration purposes,
the baseline for all different scenarios was defined as NMC622 without any recycling.

6.1 SCENARIO ANALYSIS OF CF FOR DIFFERENT RECYCLING
 TECHNOLOGIES AND RECYCLED CONTENT AMOUNTS

The EU Battery Regulation (2023/1542) establishes minimum recycled content requirements for
key elements in lithium-ion batteries (LIBs), as summarized in Table 7. To examine how these
requirements affect CF variations, we used NMC622 as a representative case study. We applied
the cut-off approach to account for the carbon credits associated with incorporating recycled
content into production. The baseline assumes 0% recycled content, reflecting the limited
volume of LIBs currently reaching EoL recycling. In the scenario analysis, we assume that the
minimum recycled content shares mandated by the regulation will be met by 2031 and 2036,
respectively, and that these targets can be achieved through three recycling methods.

The three primary methods for battery recycling are hydrometallurgical (hydro),
pyrometallurgical (pyro), and direct recycling. For the hydro and pyro processes, recycled
content can be defined on an element-by-element basis, as these methods recover individual
elements or ions at EoL. Direct recycling, however, recovers the cathode as a whole unit, making
element-specific accounting impractical. To address this, we modeled two scenarios for direct
recycling: one in which 10% of the cathode is sourced from direct recycling, and another in
which 20% is sourced from direct recycling.

Figure 8 illustrates the CF variations for NMC622 based on different recycled content levels and
recycling technologies. Under the minimum recycled content requirements for 2031 and 2036,
CF reductions range from 2–4% for hydrometallurgical recycling and from 1–2% for
pyrometallurgical recycling. These reductions are relatively modest for two reasons: first, the
cut-off approach is used to calculate the CF results and the impacts from recycling were counted;
and second, other materials and processes also have significant contributions to overall CF, such
as for graphite production and in the energy used for assembly.

6.2 SCENARIO ANALYSIS OF CF TO RENEWABLE ENERGY INTEGRATION

To evaluate the effect of renewable energy integration on CF, two scenarios were developed
from the baseline case of NMC622 without recycling. The first assumes that 50% of
manufacturing energy is supplied by renewable sources, while the second assumes that all
manufacturing energy is supplied by renewable sources.

As shown in Figure 9, integrating renewable energy into manufacturing has a significant impact
on the overall CF. Replacing 50% of manufacturing electricity with renewable sources reduces
the CF by 14%, while full replacement achieves a 26% reduction.

It is important to note that this analysis applies renewable energy only to the manufacturing
stage. If renewable energy were also integrated into upstream processes, the CF reductions
would be substantially greater. Furthermore, the battery operation phase – excluded from this
scenario analysis - is heavily dependent on grid electricity; incorporating it would likely yield
additional CF reductions as grids transition to energy sources with less CO 2 generation.

However, this scenario analysis is based on simplified assumptions. Real-world implications
would require more comprehensive electricity grid modeling to capture regional and temporal
variations in energy sources.

 7 MAIN CHALLENGES AND POTENTIAL FUTURE RESEARCH TOPICS

As discussed in Section 5, multiple challenges exist in battery CF reporting. Some of them can
potentially be resolved. For example, annualized average CF reporting masks hotspots within the
supply chain, limiting visibility to economic operators who must then determine whether specific
processes or collaborations are viable for improvement. This situation can be avoided by data
disclosure to certain authorities to avoid greenwashing. Additionally, the willingness to share
data remains a concern, although this barrier may be partially mitigated by using annualized
average data that obscures sensitive operational details. The CFF method for EoL allocation is
highly data-intensive and may not be feasible under current U.S. conditions; a cut-off approach
or simplified CFF method may prove more practical for implementation. The definition of
recycled content requires clarification, as production scrap and EoL materials make distinct
contributions to material availability and the circular economy. Additionally, the exclusion of the
battery operation phase warrants further discussion and consideration, as it typically contributes
the largest share of the overall system CF.

In contrast, some of the challenges may be harder to tackle. The electricity modeling
methodology has introduced tension among different regions, as electricity consumption is often
the primary driver of CF and can result in significant variations due to differences in regional
energy sources. In addition, smaller manufacturers face a potential “double penalty”: they not
only lack the economies of scale that reduce per-unit production costs, but their lower production
rates also result in higher CI per unit, placing them at a competitive disadvantage compared to
larger, more established producers.

 APPENDIX A: R&D GREET’S CARBON FOOTPRINT CALCULATOR

A1 R&D GREET-BASED BATTERY CF CALCULATOR

Using insights from the comparative assessment, we designed a battery carbon footprint (CF)
calculator based on Greenhouse gases, Regulated Emissions, and Energy use in Technologies
(R&D GREET) 2025 and its Battery Module and then incorporated the life cycle analysis (LCA)
frameworks of the battery passport programs reviewed in the main section of this report, as
shown in Figure A-1. The calculator consists of eight sections: (1) overall framework and
regions, (2) basic battery information, (3) battery production CF, (4) battery operation CF,
(5) transportation CF, (6) disassembly CF, (7) recycling CF, and (8) total CF and critical raw
materials (CRM) consumption.

In Section 1, users can select LCA guidelines by region and framework. In the CF calculation
sections, users can either select default values from the R&D GREET as foreground data inputs
or enter their own company-specific data. The background data for material and energy

Key Overarching Questions for Stakeholders

 1. What is the status and scope of battery passport and traceability regulations in your
 country?

 2. How are you aligning with or diverging from EU Regulation 2023/1542 (EU only or
 imports to EU)?

 3. How will companies in your country balance the demands of battery passport regulations
 with the cost and time required to implement it? How much is the expected cost per
 battery passport, and do you know who will be responsible for covering that cost?

 4. Are there specific challenges in adapting battery passport regulations to dynamic supply
 chains? For example, the idea of annual average data is conceptually simple — but to
 actually produce those averages, each actor needs to continuously measure and store
 energy and material flows, keep track of ore grades and throughput, and be willing to
 share the data with downstream users. That is a huge lift for mining companies,
 especially those without strong digital systems or with confidentiality concerns.

 2. Are there company-specific vs. national-level efforts on battery greenhouse gas (GHG)

 3. What is the role of third-party verification and how feasible is it at scale? How will you
 make verification useful and practical?
