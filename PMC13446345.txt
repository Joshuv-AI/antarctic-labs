Demethoxycurcumin inhibits breast cancer vascular remodeling through RGS5 to delay the progression of breast cancer
Authors: Jie Chen, Fangling Zhou, Guanqun Wo, Tong Zhao, Yi Wang, Chang Yao, Yanlei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Demethoxycurcumin suppresses breast tumor growth and angiogenesis.•DMC downregulates RGS5 in tumor vasculature and endothelial cells.•RGS5 is a key target mediating DMC’s anti-angiogenic effects.

Introduction

As the third most common malignancy worldwide, breast cancer accounts for millions of new cases annually and results in hundreds of thousands of deaths. In China, it is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women aged 15 to 44, and its mortality rate continues to rise [1]. In 2020, the number of new breast cancer cases reached 2.3 million, representing 11.7% of all newly diagnosed cancer cases, with 685,000 deaths attributed to the disease [2]. Although diagnostic and therapeutic techniques for breast cancer have significantly improved, approximately 34% of patients will ultimately progress to metastatic disease and die from advanced cancer. Therefore, it is crucial to gain a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms of breast cancer and to explore new therapeutic targets.

Breast cancer can be categorized into four intrinsic molecular subtypes: Type A, Type B, HER2-positive, and triple-negative (TNBC). Among these, TNBC is the most challenging subtype in terms of treatment. Metastasis and multidrug resistance are major challenges in breast cancer treatment. The proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of malignant tumors depend on angiogenesis, which facilitates nutrient acquisition from the host environment. This process is crucial for controlling cancer progression [3]. Angiogenesis is a fundamental characteristic of tumors, providing essential oxygen and nutrients necessary for the survival of tumor cells. It supports tumor progression by promoting angiogenesis, playing a critical role in the occurrence and development of solid tumors [4,5]. The vascular structures formed by tumors are abnormal and significantly differ from the normal vascular system. They exhibit heterogeneity in tissue, structure, and function, stemming from an uneven and chaotic vascular network [6]. These abnormalities reflect their pathological characteristics and underscore the need to develop new strategies targeting tumor stromal vascular components to selectively inhibit tumor vascular systems and suppress tumor growth. Inhibiting the abnormal angiogenesis of tumors has become a new strategy for cancer treatment, this therapeutic strategy involves the remodeling of tumor vascular structure and function.

Vascular remodeling (VR) refers to the process by which tumor cells secrete various growth factors (such as VEGF, etc.) to promote the proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis of endothelial cells, leading to the formation of a new vascular network that supplies nutrients and oxygen to the tumor [7]. This remodeling not only involves the formation of new blood vessels but also the restructuring of existing vessels to meet the demands of tumor growth and metastasis.Vascular changes occur to adapt to the demands of blood flow and local metabolism, typically following angiogenesis. This process involves the remodeling of endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and the extracellular matrix of smooth muscle cells to accommodate the flow requirements under physiological or pathological conditions. Vascular remodeling is a regulatory response to the constantly changing environment, ensuring that the vascular structures can effectively support tumor progression and survival.

Currently, the primary treatment modalities for breast cancer include Western medical approaches such as surgery, endocrine therapy, and targeted molecular therapy. First-line drugs for treatment include drugs like Camrelizumab, Trastuzumab, Pertuzumab, Apatinib, CDK4/6 inhibitors, etc. These treatments are aimed at different aspects of cancer progression and are often used in combination to enhance therapeutic efficacy and improve patient outcomes [8,9]. These treatment modalities can significantly reduce the risk of recurrence and mortality in breast cancer patients while extending their survival time. However, the treatment process is often accompanied by a range of side effects, such as upper limb lymphedema, gastrointestinal reactions, endocrine disorders, and the potential for developing drug resistance. These issues can negatively impact the quality of life of breast cancer patients, posing significant challenges to their overall well-being [2,10,11]. Therefore, the search for new drugs and therapeutic targets is urgent. Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has shown significant clinical efficacy in the treatment of breast cancer. Studies have shown that TCM can effectively inhibit the proliferation, metastasis, and invasion of breast cancer cells, as well as address issues related to drug resistance. Additionally, TCM promotes apoptosis and autophagy in breast cancer cells and helps alleviate adverse reactions associated with conventional treatments. This indicates that integrating traditional Chinese medicine with existing therapies may enhance treatment outcomes and improve the quality of life for breast cancer patients [12]. Due to the high safety profile of traditional Chinese medicine and herbal remedies, these approaches have been integrated into breast cancer treatment regimens for some time. This acknowledgment highlights the growing acceptance of alternative therapeutic approaches in conjunction with conventional treatments, emphasize the importance of adopting a more comprehensive approach to cancer care [13].

Turmeric, a medicinal and culinary herb, is widely utilized in traditional Chinese medicine for its abilities to promote qi circulation, invigorate blood flow, and provide anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects. Beyond being an essential herbal remedy, turmeric is also commonly used in cooking as a seasoning and coloring agent, particularly in curry dishes. Research has indicated that active compounds isolated from turmeric rhizomes, such as curcumin and demethoxycurcumin, demonstrate significant anti-inflammatory and anti-tumor activities against various malignant epithelial tumors, including cervical cancer, breast cancer, prostate cancer, and gastrointestinal cancers [[14], [15], [16]]. Curcumin is an edible hepatoprotective component with minimal side effects, it has garnered significant attention due to its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. Demethoxycurcumin, another compound extracted from turmeric rhizomes, belongs to the curcuminoid family. Research has shown that demethoxycurcumin exhibits significant effects in combating liver fibrosis, reducing cirrhosis, and inducing apoptosis in liver cancer cells [17].

Regulator of G-protein signaling 5 (RGS5) has been identified as one of the transcripts expressed by endothelial cells (EC) in the tumor microenvironment. It is a novel biomarker of the cancer vasculature system that is triggered and maintained by the tumor-promoting angiogenic microenvironment. RGS5 is a member of the RGS family, which includes multifunctional GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs) that are known to negatively regulate the signaling of G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs). However, it remains unclear which specific pathways are regulated by RGS5 [18]. Research has indicated that in fibroblasts and smooth muscle cells transfected with RGS5, RGS5 attenuates the signaling capacity triggered by proteins that mediate vascular function, such as angiotensin II (AngII), endothelin-1 (ET-1), and sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) [[19], [20], [21]]. This suggests that RGS5 may be associated with vascular maturation. The RGS5 protein primarily co-localizes with endothelial cells that express platelet/endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (PECAM-1/CD31) [18]. Currently, the role and regulatory mechanisms of RGS5 in angiogenesis remain unclear. Most studies have primarily focused on the regulation of RGS5 in pericyte maturation. However, an increasing number of studies indicate that RGS5 is not only expressed in pericytes but also in vascular endothelial cells and certain tumor cells [22,23]. Furthermore, the level of RGS5 expression in these tumor cells is correlated with patient prognosis [24,25].

Nevertheless, critical research gaps still exist in the current investigations of DMC and RGS5. Existing DMC research largely ignores its modulatory effect on tumor endothelial vascular remodeling. Despite RGS5 being a classic tumor vascular marker, few works verify direct DMC-RGS5 binding or in vivo rescue effects. Herein, the present study was designed to systematically elucidate the molecular mechanism by which DMC restrains pathological vascular remodeling and retards breast cancer progression via targeting RGS5, and to provide experimental evidence for the translational application of DMC as a natural anti-breast cancer agent.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Demethoxycurcumin (DMC) (HY-N0006) was purchased from MCE; Paclitaxel (PTX) (P815862) was obtained from Maclin; Crystal Violet (C8470) was sourced from Solarbio; Matrigel (356234) was purchased from Corning; IL-1β (JL18442), IL-6 (JL20268), IFN-γ (JL10967), and TNF-α (JL10484) were acquired from Jianglai Biology; the apoptosis kit (F6012) was bought from UE; and the TUNEL kit (G1502) was sourced from Seville. The CD31 antibody (15585S) was purchased from Cell Signaling Technology with a dilution of 1:400; RGS5 antibody (immunofluorescence) (11590-1-AP) was obtained from Proteintech with a dilution of 1:200; Ki67 (ab15580) was purchased from Abcam with a dilution of 1:1000. The VEGF antibody (Sc-7269) was sourced from Santa Cruz with a dilution of 1:1000; RGS5 antibody (WB) (ab314670) was acquired from Abcam with a dilution of 1:1000; Caspase-3 antibody (ab184787) was purchased from Abcam with a dilution of 1:2000; Cleaved-caspase 3 antibody (AF7022) was obtained from Affinity with a dilution of 1:1000; Bax antibody (ab32503) was purchased from Abcam with a dilution of 1:1000; Bcl-2 antibody (ab182858) was acquired from Abcam with a dilution of 1:2000; p-NF-κB antibody (ab76302) was purchased from Abcam with a dilution of 1:1000; NF-κB antibody (ab32536) was obtained from Abcam with a dilution of 1:1000; iNOS antibody (13120) was acquired from Cell Signaling Technology with a dilution of 1:1000; GAPDH antibody (5174) was sourced from Cell Signaling Technology with a dilution of 1:5000; Anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) antibody (14708) was purchased from Cell Signaling Technology with a dilution of 1:5000; and Anti-mouse IgG (H+L) antibody (14709) was obtained from Cell Signaling Technology with a dilution of 1:5000.

Experimental animals and grouping

In the Qinglongshan Farm, Jiangning District, Nanjing, we obtained 30 female BALB/c mice, aged 4 to 5 weeks, weighing between 18 and 22 grams (Animal License No.: SYXK (Su) 2018-0049). The mice were housed in the Experimental Animal Center of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine, under strictly controlled environmental conditions, with a temperature of 18 to 22°C, relative humidity of 50 to 60%, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. They had ad libitum access to food and water and were placed in an independent SPF ventilation system with barrier protection. To investigate the effects and potential mechanisms of demethoxycurcumin in a breast cancer mouse model, we designed two experimental groups. The 30 tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into five experimental groups: Control group, Low-dose demethoxycurcumin group (20 mg/kg; L-DMC), Medium-dose demethoxycurcumin group (40 mg/kg; M-DMC), High-dose demethoxycurcumin group (80 mg/kg; H-DMC), and Positive control group (PTX 5 mg/kg, Intraperitoneal injection). This experimental design aims to comprehensively evaluate the effects of varying doses of demethoxycurcumin on breast cancer in mice. Thirty female mice were randomly allocated into five groups (n = 6 per group): control group, AAV-NC group, H-DMC (80 mg/kg) group, AAV-RGS5 group, and AAV-RGS5 + H-DMC (80 mg/kg) group. AAV tail vein injection: injection of 100 μ L, 1 × 10 ¹¹– 2 × 10 ¹² vg/ mouse. By employing genetic intervention (RGS5 overexpression), this study aims to verify whether the anti-tumor effect of demethoxycurcumin depends on the regulation of RGS5, thereby elucidating its molecular mechanism and providing a potential therapeutic target and theoretical basis for anti-angiogenic therapy in breast cancer. This study was approved by the Experimental Ethics Committee of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine (NO. 202512A071) and was conducted in strict accordance with the Guidelines for the Management and Use of Experimental Animals (8th Edition).

Construction of a mouse transplant tumor model

After a one-week adaptation period, the mice underwent tumor modeling. Breast cancer cells (4T1) were collected and adjusted to a cell density of 1 × 108 cells/ml. Following aseptic procedures, the abdomen of the mice was disinfected. A 1 mL syringe was used to puncture the right abdominal side beneath the fourth mammary gland into the fat pad. The skin was gently lifted to inject 0.1 mL of the tumor cell suspension into each mouse. A successful injection was indicated by the formation of a translucent skin wheal, thus establishing the 4T1 breast cancer model. To ensure the viability of the injected cells, the tumor cell suspension was placed in a beaker containing ice throughout the modeling process, which was completed within one hour. The blank group of mice received an injection of an equal volume of 0.9% sodium chloride solution as the 4T1 cells. Mice were monitored daily, and their body weight and tumor volume (length × width²/2) were recorded. When the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm³, drug treatment was initiated. After 14 days of treatment, the mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital, euthanized by cervical dislocation, and the corresponding tissues were collected for subsequent experiments.

HE staining

HE staining was performed to assess the necrosis of the tumor tissue. After embedding in paraffin and sectioning, the brain tissue was stained with 1% toluidine blue solution, followed by staining with HE solution according to standard procedures.

Immunofluorescence staining

Following excision, the tumor tissue samples that were implanted subcutaneously were fixed using paraformaldehyde and then rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). This was followed by antigen retrieval at high temperature. Subsequently, the samples were treated with a solution of fetal bovine serum albumin for sealing. They were then incubated with anti-Ki67 antibody (ab 15580) at 25°C for 1-2 hours. After the incubation period, the samples were washed 1-3 times with PBS, and a fluorescently labeled secondary antibody was introduced and allowed to incubate for one hour. Before sealing the slides, DAPI staining was conducted to highlight the cell nuclei. The outcomes were examined and recorded with a fluorescence microscope, and further quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software (1.8.0_322).

ELISA

Hippocampal concentrations of TNF-α, iNOS, and IL-1β were determined using sandwich ELISA kits following the manufacturer’s protocol, with minor modifications. Samples were assayed in duplicate, and concentrations were calculated from standard curves fitted with a four-parameter logistic model. Data are expressed as pg/mL.

Western blot

After extracting total proteins from tumor tissues of each group, the proteins were quantified. Protein samples were separated using SDS-PAGE and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. Following a 5% non-fat milk blocking step, the membrane was incubated with primary antibodies against Bax, Bcl-2, VEGF, RGS5, Cleaved-caspase3, and Total-caspase3 at 4°C for 12 hours. Afterward, the membrane was incubated with the secondary antibody at 25°C for 2 hours. Protein bands were detected and quantified using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent kit, with GAPDH used as a loading control.

Tubule formation assay

To assess DNA fragmentation, we utilized the TUNEL assay kit-TMR (G1502, Servicebio). PC-12 cells were fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and subsequently treated with cold 70% ethanol. After washing, the cells were incubated with the staining solution at 37°C for 2 hours, followed by DAPI staining. Images were captured using a confocal microscope (Nikon). For staining of frozen sections, similar procedures were followed using DAB for color development and methyl green for counterstaining. All images were acquired using a microscope (Nikon).

Chick embryo allantoic membrane (CAM) experiment

We carefully selected chicken embryos aged 4 to 7 days post-fertilization for experimental incubation. A small opening was made on the side opposite the air cell to expose the chorioallantoic membrane, into which the prepared cell suspension was inoculated. The eggs were returned to the incubator under controlled conditions (37°C, 60% humidity). The drug was subsequently injected into the chorioallantoic membrane, and observations were performed regularly during incubation. After 48 hours, vascular density, total vascular network length, number of branches, and total vascular segments were measured to quantify neovascularization in the Control, L+DMC, M+DMC, and H+DMC groups.

Flow cytometry

For the orthotopic implantation of tumors in nude mice (refer to section 2.3 for specific steps), after the tumors reach a certain size, the nude mice are euthanized by cervical dislocation, and fresh tumor tissues are collected. The tumor tissues are then minced into small pieces as finely as possible and placed in DMEM containing 1 mg/mL Collagenase IV and 10–3 U/L DNase I for digestion for 0.5 to 1 h. After digestion, the single-cell suspension is stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies incubate at 25°C for 20 minutes. The cells are then washed three times with pre-chilled PBS on ice, with each wash lasting 5 minutes, and analyzed using a flow cytometer. Data is analyzed using CytExpert V2.3 and FlowJo X software version 7.6.4.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction(qRT-PCR)

The expression of RGS5 in HUVECs was analyzed using qRT-PCR.

Cells were lysed in SafeLock tubes containing TRIzol, followed by total RNA extraction using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA). Subsequently, one-stranded cDNA was synthesized using the Hifair II SuperMix (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), which involved converting the extracted RNA into cDNA. This process enabled us to obtain high-quality cDNA for subsequent qPCR experiments, laying the groundwork for further gene expression analysis. qRT-PCR was performed on a LightCycler 1.5 system with SYBR Green technology, and relative expression levels were analyzed by the 2^−ΔΔCt method according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-Yl)-2,5-Diphenyltetrazolium Bromide (MTT) Assay

A single-cell suspension was created by combining 4T1 breast cancer cells with HUVECs. The mixture was then diluted to the desired concentration and placed into 96-well plates. Various concentrations of demethoxycurcumin were added to the cells, which were then incubated for a specified duration. After incubation, MTT solution was introduced, followed by DMSO, and the optical density (OD) of each well was assessed using a microplate reader to determine cell viability.

Molecular-docking

Demethoxycurcumin was used as a ligand to evaluate its interaction with human RGS5 based on the docking score (LibDockScore). The 2D SDF file of demethoxycurcumin was downloaded from the PubChem database, and small molecule ligand optimization was performed using Discovery Studio molecular simulation software. The 3D structure PDB file of human RGS5 was obtained from the AlphaFold database and imported into the POCASA website (https://g6altair.sci.hokudai.ac.jp/g6/service/pocasa/) to predict the active pocket using default parameters. The protein PDB file was then imported into Discovery Studio for hydrogen addition and removal of impurities. The predicted active pocket was subsequently imported to determine the docking position. Finally, the optimized small molecule structure was imported, Dock Ligands (LibDock) was selected to perform molecular docking, and the LibDockScore for each ligand–protein complex was recorded.

Transwell assay for cellular migration and invasion

4T1 and HUVEC cells were cultured in 200 µL of medium containing different concentrations of demethoxycurcumin (20, 40, 80 µg/mL). Cells in the lower chamber were maintained in 600 µL of medium containing 10% FBS. Each concentration of demethoxycurcumin was applied to three wells. The cells were incubated at 37°C for 8–10 hours. After incubation, residual cells that did not migrate or invade were gently removed from the membrane surface using a cotton swab. The invasive cells on the membrane were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes, then transferred to methanol and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. The cells were subsequently counted under an inverted microscope. Each experiment was repeated three times.

Statistical analysis

In this experiment, data analysis was conducted utilizing GraphPad Prism 7.0 software, with each experiment repeated three times. Group differences were evaluated using one-way ANOVA, two-way ANOVA, and Dunnett's multiple comparison test. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with statistical significance indicated by * p < 0.05 and ** p < 0.01.

Results

Demethoxycurcumin possesses in vivo anti-tumor activity

Representative images of tumor tissues from each experimental group are shown in Fig. 1B. Compared with the control group, tumors in the demethoxycurcumin-treated groups were markedly reduced in volume in a dose-dependent manner. The tumor volume in the positive drug group was significantly smaller than that in the blank control group. The assessment of tumor incidence indicated that, compared to the control group, the tumor inhibitory rates in the L-DMC group had a p-value less than 0.05, while the M-DMC, H-DMC, and positive control groups all had p-values less than 0.01 (Fig. 1C- D). To assess tumor cell proliferation, Ki67 immunofluorescence staining was performed, which quantitatively measures the proliferation frequency of tumor cells. The tumor sections from the control group showed a high number of Ki67-positive cells, while the treatment groups exhibited a reduction in Ki67-positive cells, particularly in the M-DMC and H-DMC group, demonstrating a significant anti-proliferative effect of demethoxycurcumin (Fig. 1E; F). In the control group, tumor cells were densely arranged, with enlarged, deeply stained nuclei, high proliferative activity, and disorganized tissue structure. In the demethoxycurcumin-treated groups, tumor cells exhibited marked morphological changes, including a reduced nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, looser cellular arrangement, and increased necrotic areas. The positive control group similarly showed pronounced tumor tissue disruption and disordered cell organization. Demethoxycurcumin promotes apoptosis in breast cancer cells

The effects of demethoxycurcumin on tumor cell apoptosis in a breast cancer xenograft model were assessed. Tunel staining, which is commonly used to detect late-stage apoptosis in tumor cells, indicated that the percentage of apoptotic cells in tumor tissues from mice treated with 20 mg/kg demethoxycurcumin (6.5%), 40 mg/kg demethoxycurcumin (9.6%), and 80 mg/kg demethoxycurcumin (14.2%) was significantly higher than that of the control group (2.8%) (Fig. 2A, B). This finding suggests that demethoxycurcumin can induce cell apoptosis. Western blot analysis was conducted to evaluate the expression levels of apoptosis-related proteins, including Bax, Bcl-2, Caspase 3, and Cleaved-Caspase 3, in the tumor tissues (Fig. 2C-F). Compared to the control group, the expression level of the pro-apoptotic protein Bax increased with higher treatment concentrations, while the expression level of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 decreased as the dose increased. Demethoxycurcumin reduces angiogenesis in breast cancer tumors in nude mice

CD31 is a transmembrane glycoprotein mainly expressed in vascular endothelial cells, playing a key role in physiological processes such as cell adhesion, angiogenesis, inflammatory cell migration, and regulation of vascular permeability [26]. RGS5 is a gene whose expression is known to be modulated by hypoxic conditions, which are upregulated in low oxygen environments and participate in physiological processes such as vascular remodeling and endothelial cell apoptosis [27]. Quantitative analysis of microvascular density through co-localization staining of CD31 and RGS5 shows that in untreated tumor tissues, the number of positively co-localized cells for CD31 and RGS5 is the highest, indicating active angiogenesis. After treatment with demethoxycurcumin, the number of positively co-localized cells for CD31 and RGS5 in the tumor tissues decreased, with a trend of dose-dependent decline (*p < 0.05, ⁎⁎p < 0.01), suggesting that demethoxycurcumin inhibits tumor angiogenesis, limiting the tumor’s uptake of oxygen and nutrients from the bloodstream, thereby restricting tumor growth (Fig. 3A, C). The chicken embryo chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) vascular assay model is one of the commonly used animal models for studying angiogenesis and anti-angiogenesis. Treatment with demethoxycurcumin significantly reduced the vascular density in the chicken embryos (Fig. 3B; D; *p<0.05, ⁎⁎p<0.01). This further validates that demethoxycurcumin can inhibit tumor angiogenesis. VEGF is a specific growth factor for endothelial cells that promotes the proliferation, migration, and the generation of new blood vessels. Demethoxycurcumin reduces the invasion, migration, and angiogenic potential of human umbilical vein endothelial cells

To determine the appropriate dosing concentration of demethoxycurcumin for its effects on HUVECs, an MTT assay was performed to assess cell viability after treatment with gradient concentrations of demethoxycurcumin (0.625, 1.25, 2.5, 5, 10, 20, 50 μM) for 24 hours. Demethoxycurcumin at 2.5 μM did not induce significant cytotoxicity; however, concentrations of 5, 10, 20, and 50 μM significantly reduced cell viability, with 20 μM demethoxycurcumin achieving a 50% inhibition rate on HUVEC cells (Fig. 4A, B). Therefore, the low, medium, and high concentrations were determined to be 5, 10, and 20 μM, respectively. Transwell assays were conducted to evaluate the migration and invasion capabilities of HUVECs. The treatment group exhibited reduced cell invasion and migration compared to the control group. Additionally, a tube formation assay was utilized to assess the anti-angiogenic capability of demethoxycurcumin (Fig. 4C; D). Image analysis software was employed for quantitative analysis of the tube length and branching points. Compared to the control group, the groups treated with demethoxycurcumin showed a reduction in both vascular branching and length with increasing drug concentrations (Fig. 4E-G). Demethoxycurcumin inhibits angiogenesis in breast cancer by downregulating RGS5

To explore the functional mechanism of demethoxycurcumin (DMC) on vascular endothelial cells, we first detected DMC-triggered endothelial apoptosis at gradient concentrations. Flow cytometry was used to assess apoptosis after treatment, revealing an increase in apoptotic cells with higher drug concentrations compared to the control group (Fig. 5A, B). To investigate the potential role of RGS5 in inhibiting tumor angiogenesis induced by demethoxycurcumin, we employed immunofluorescence staining to assess the expression and localization of RGS5 within the cells. Immunofluorescence staining showed the localization of RGS5 in control tumors (Fig. 5C; D), RGS5 was primarily localized in the cell membrane and extracellular matrix regions. After drug treatment, the expression of RGS5 was found to decrease. qPCR analysis indicated that the RGS5 mRNA levels in untreated tumors were significantly higher compared to those in demethoxycurcumin-treated samples (Fig. 5E,), with the highest doses of demethoxycurcumin showing the most pronounced inhibition. Compared to the untreated control group, the treated group significantly reduced the expression of the pro-angiogenic tumor marker RGS5 in HUVECs (Fig. 5F; G). The levels of Cleaved-Caspase 3 increased with higher drug concentrations (Fig. 5H; I), indicating an enhanced apoptotic cascade following treatment. The interactions such as the Pi key -alkyl and carbon-hydrogen bonds provide a certain impetus for the binding of human RGS5 No. 119 active pocket with demethoxylcurcumin (Fig. 7A; B; C). CETSA (Cell Thermal Shift Assay) is a method based on detecting the changes in the thermal stability of target proteins after drug treatment. When combined with specific small molecules, the thermal stability of proteins is significantly improved, indicating that it may be an effective drug target. The effect of demethoxylcurcumin on RGS5 was evaluated using CETSA technology. The results show that under the condition of demethoxylcurcumin, the stability of RGS5 at 70°C is significantly improved, while in the control group, the stability of RGS5 at this temperature is poor. Based on these data, we speculate that demethoxylcurcumin may affect its biological function by acting on RGS5 and enhancing the thermal stability of its binding to RGS5, suggesting its potential as a drug target.

Discussion

Nearly 90% of cancer patients die from recurrence and metastasis, with tumor angiogenesis playing a crucial role in tumor growth and spread. Tumor cells secrete a range of pro-angiogenic factors, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and stromal cell-derived factor (SDF-1), which promote the proliferation and migration of endothelial cells in adjacent normal tissues [28]. Angiogenesis is one of the notable features of the occurrence and development of liver cancer. New blood vessels can continuously provide sufficient nutrition and oxygen to liver cancer cells to ensure their continuous survival, rapid proliferation and migration [29]. Based on this pathophysiological characteristic of tumors, anti-angiogenesis has become a key target in anti-cancer treatment. Although clinical drugs (such as camrelizumab and trastuzumab) have shown good efficacy in tumor treatment, the side effects they cause, such as severe gastrointestinal reactions, endocrine disorders and drug resistance, cannot be ignored. Therefore, developing new anti-angiogenic agents with better safety remains an urgent task at present. The natural products of medicinal plants, fungi and microorganisms have shown unique potential as potent and low-toxicity anti-angiogenic compounds.

Demethoxycurcumin(DMC) is one of the main bioactive components in turmeric, and it has been proven to have anti-inflammatory [30], angiogenic inhibitory [31], and anti-tumor activities [32]. This study aims to clarify the mechanism by which demethoxylcurcumin inhibits angiogenesis and metastasis of breast cancer through the novel tumor microenvironment marker RGS5. DMC inhibits the progression of breast cancer by down-regulating RGS5 expression and targeting tumor-associated angiogenesis. Research indicates that angiogenesis induced by cancer is primarily fueled by pro-angiogenic factors, including VEGF, released by cancer cells. VEGF activates endothelial cells, leading to enhanced angiogenesis in the vicinity of the tumor, which in turn facilitates tumor invasion and metastasis [[33], [34], [35]]. We also conducted relevant experiments to evaluate the angiogenic-related protein VEGF and carried out inhibition tests. Our research indicates that DMC significantly inhibits the migration and invasion of breast cancer cells (4T1), and disrupts the formation, migration of blood vessels and the invasion of HUVEC. DMC can also prevent the growth of subcutaneous tumors in nude mice. Immunohistochemical analysis utilizing CD31 and Ki67 markers revealed that DMC decreased tumor proliferation and microvessel density in a manner that depended on the dosage. Our research also found that DMC can down-regulate the expression of RGS5 in breast cancer and vascular endothelial cells, and the trends of in vivo and in vitro experimental results are consistent.

RGS5 is a member of the G protein signal transduction (RGS) superfamily. It negatively regulates GPCRs signal transduction by promoting the GTPase activity of the G protein α subunit [36]. Compared with other family members, RGS5 is highly expressed in the early stage of tumor neovascularization development and maturation, regulating the differentiation and recruitment of perivascular cells and participating in the regulation of vascular maturation [37]. It is also a protein capable of characterizing human tumor endothelial cells and participates in the remodeling of blood vessels during the pathological angiogenesis process triggered by the tumor microenvironment. Endothelial cell walls serve as gateways for cancer cell dissemination. Breast cancer cells can directly induce endothelial cell activation, thereby enhancing vascular permeability [33].

Research found that RGS5 lymphatic endothelial cells enhance the adhesion of breast cancer cells to endothelial cells and facilitate their migration across the endothelium [34], and RGS5 protein coexists with vascular endothelial cells, which can be used as a clinical biomarker. Previous studies have shown that in RIP1-Tag mice, RGS5 mRNA has been proven to be associated with the vascular bed of advanced insulinoma, but not with that of normal/hyperplastic islets [35,38]. The research of Chen X, Higgins J demonstrated that RGS5 mRNA expression is detectable in hepatic sinusoidal endothelial cells of hepatocellular carcinoma, but not in normal or cirrhotic patients. The same is true in the blood vessels of renal cell carcinoma, but it is not expressed in the adjacent kidneys [39,40]. Endothelial cells isolated from ovarian cancer and kidney cancer have a higher expression of RGS5 than those isolated from non-tumor tissues. These findings suggest that RGS5 is a biomarker of cancer-related EC and plays a key role in vascular remodeling during carcinogenesis, indicating its potential as a clinical biomarker [18].

In tumor tissues, the hypoxic microenvironment of the tumor stimulates excessive angiogenesis by increasing various pro-angiogenic factors, including VEGF, PDGF, FGF and angiopoietin [41]. Subsequently, new blood vessels enhance the capacity to transport oxygen and nutrients, further promoting the proliferation of tumor cells.

As tumor cells transition to a more aggressive phenotype, they continue to proliferate, invade, and promote angiogenesis, which in turn enables tumor cells to metastasize through the bloodstream and invade distant tissues [42]. Hypoxia in tumor tissues often promotes angiogenesis, which is conducive to the development and metastasis of tumors; on the other hand, it induces apoptosis and inhibits tumor growth [43]. In this study, we found that a decrease in RGS5 expression would impair endothelial cell function, meaning that in normal endothelial cells, the higher the expression of RGS5, the better. However, in the proliferating endothelial cells caused by the pathological characteristics of tumors, the less the expression of RGS5, the weaker the tubule-forming ability of the endothelial cells. RGS5 regulates apoptosis and proliferation through its interaction with the G protein signaling pathway. Studies have shown that RGS5 inhibits the proliferation of tumor cells by suppressing the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway in the G protein signaling pathway and promoting apoptosis [44]. RGS5 is also involved in the immune regulation of the tumor microenvironment. Juliana Hamzah et al. found that RGS5 regulates the function of immune cells in the tumor microenvironment through interaction with the TGFβ signaling pathway, influencing the immune escape of tumors and the efficacy of immunotherapy [45]. Research indicates that RGS5 plays a crucial role in regulating inflammation-related signaling pathways, which not only contribute to tissue damage and fibrosis but are also involved in vascular remodeling processes in tumors, hypertension, and atherosclerosis.

In conclusion, our research has for the first time clarified the molecular mechanism by which DMC exerts its anti-breast cancer effect by inhibiting the expression of RGS5 in tumor vascular endothelial cells, promoting vascular endothelial cell apoptosis, and regulating vascular remodeling in the tumor microenvironment. However, the current research still has some shortcomings: (1) Insufficient mechanism verification (for example, how RGS5 regulates downstream proteins to increase cell apoptosis; (2) In other cancer mouse models, whether RGS5 can also promote tumor cell apoptosis and inhibit tumor growth. Angiogenesis is closely related to the growth and metastasis of tumors. New tumor blood vessels provide the necessary material basis for the metabolism and metastasis of tumors, becoming an indispensable factor in the occurrence and development of tumors. When conventional chemotherapy is combined with anti-angiogenic or vascular-affecting drugs, the therapeutic effect is also enhanced [46]. This article provides a reference basis for future research on tumor mechanisms and clinical medication, and offers a theoretical foundation and research direction for the application of traditional Chinese medicine in tumor treatment.

Conclusions

Based on our findings, we clarified that demethoxycurcumin (DMC) suppresses breast cancer angiogenesis and progression by directly downregulating RGS5, a specific biomarker of tumor-induced vasculature. Mechanistically, DMC restrains pathological angiogenesis and facilitates breast cancer cell apoptosis to block tumor growth. Conventional clinical anti-angiogenic therapies are hampered by drug resistance and severe side effects; as a low-toxicity food-medicine curcuminoid from turmeric, DMC possesses dual anti-angiogenic and pro-apoptotic activities and serves as a promising adjuvant candidate for breast cancer treatment. Our data also validate RGS5 as a novel pathological biomarker to evaluate tumor angiogenic activity in breast cancer tissues.
