Upper eyelid cicatricial ectropion following herpes zoster ophthalmicus: a rare case with surgical management
Authors: Abdulkadir Calavul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHerpes zoster ophthalmicus (HZO) may lead to ocular and periocular complications through involvement of the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve. Upper eyelid cicatricial ectropion is an uncommon sequela that may cause lagophthalmos, ocular surface exposure, irritation, and aesthetic deformity. This report describes surgical management of post-HZO upper eyelid cicatricial ectropion using a preauricular full-thickness skin graft.Case presentationA 65-year-old woman presented with inability to fully close her left upper eyelid, accompanied by ocular redness, dryness, and burning sensation. She had been diagnosed with HZO two months earlier. Visual acuity was preserved, and no corneal epithelial defect was observed. Despite antiviral therapy and conservative ocular surface lubrication, progressive upper eyelid retraction, eyelash loss, lagophthalmos, and scleral exposure developed. After the active inflammatory phase had resolved and cicatricial retraction persisted, scar release was performed under local anaesthesia. The resulting 1,8 × 0,8 cm anterior lamellar defect was reconstructed with a preauricular full-thickness skin graft. At 8 months, eyelid closure was restored, visible ocular surface exposure was absent, dryness and burning had resolved, and acceptable tissue contour and color match were maintained without wound-site complication.ConclusionsFull-thickness skin grafting may be a practical reconstructive option for selected cases of post-HZO upper eyelid cicatricial ectropion when surgery is timed after resolution of active inflammation and donor-site selection accounts for eyelid color, thickness, and pliability. Broader treatment recommendations require additional cases with objective functional measurements and longer follow-up.

Introduction

Herpes zoster ophthalmicus (HZO) is an infectious and inflammatory condition involving the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve. It may affect the cornea, sclera, conjunctiva, eyelid, and periocular soft tissues, with potential consequences such as chronic ocular irritation, neurotrophic keratopathy, structural eyelid abnormality, and visual morbidity [5, 14, 15, 17, 23]. The clinical burden is greater in elderly patients and in individuals with systemic immunosuppression, although severe periocular sequelae may also occur in immunocompetent patients [2, 19, 30].

In oculoplastic practice, post-HZO eyelid malposition appears to be related to persistent inflammation, anterior lamellar shortening, and periocular fibrosis [1, 29]. Cicatricial ectropion involving the upper eyelid is rarely described compared with lower eyelid disease, and most available reports consist of single cases or small retrospective experiences [7, 9, 20, 31]. Limited comparative data may make surgical planning difficult when the deformity involves upper eyelid anatomy, where thin anterior lamellar skin, orbicularis function, tarsal support, and corneal protection must be considered together [18, 22]. Reports from other surgeons have described different reconstructive options, including postauricular island flap reconstruction, dermofat grafting with an anti-adhesive agent, and full-thickness skin grafting, but standardized treatment criteria remain insufficiently evaluated [7, 9, 25].

The present case describes upper eyelid cicatricial ectropion following HZO that was surgically managed with scar release and a preauricular full-thickness skin graft. The report focuses on operative timing, donor-site selection, technical points of scar release and graft placement, functional recovery, aesthetic outcome, and limitations related to the interpretation of a single case.

Case presentation

Presentation and history

A 65-year-old female patient presented with inability to fully close the left upper eyelid, accompanied by redness, dryness, and burning sensation during the cicatricial phase. Her history revealed painful, itchy, vesicular eruptions on the left upper eyelid and forehead approximately two months earlier. Following evaluation at the dermatology clinic, she was diagnosed with HZO and received oral antiviral treatment with topical therapy. The vesicular lesions regressed; however, progressive eyelid retraction and incomplete eyelid closure persisted despite conservative management. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for scientific publication of the clinical details and accompanying images. Physical examination findings

On examination, prominent scar tissue and fibrosis extended from the left upper eyelid to the forehead. Upper eyelid retraction, eyelash loss, lagophthalmos, and scleral exposure were present. The lagophthalmos defect measured approximately 1.8 × 0.8 cm. Corneal exposure due to lagophthalmos was noted, but visual acuity was preserved and no corneal epithelial defect was observed on clinical examination. Marked ocular dryness and irritation were present. The patient had no systemic disease or immunodeficiency. Conservative approaches, including moisturizing ointment, artificial tears, and massage, did not provide sufficient functional improvement.

Surgical intervention

Surgical correction was performed 10 weeks after the onset of herpes zoster ophthalmicus, once the active vesicular and inflammatory phase had completely resolved and persistent cicatricial retraction continued to impair eyelid closure. Under local anaesthesia, 1 mL of lidocaine (1:100,000 epinephrine) and 2 mL of isotonic solution were injected into the left upper eyelid and preauricular donor site. An incision was made along the retracted scar area, and fibrotic anterior lamellar bands were released until the upper eyelid could be repositioned without visible traction and passive eyelid closure improved (Fig. 6). After release of the contracture, the resulting 1,8 × 0,8 cm defect was reconstructed with a full-thickness skin graft harvested from the preauricular region. The graft was trimmed to fit the defect, conservatively thinned, positioned over the recipient bed, and fixed with fine interrupted sutures (Fig. 7). Postoperative care consisted of antibiotic ointment dressing and ocular surface lubrication as clinically indicated.

Follow-up and results

No wound-site complication, infection, hematoma, graft loss, or recurrent contracture was observed during follow-up. The patient was followed regularly for 8 months. Functional assessment focused on eyelid closure, regression of lagophthalmos, absence of visible corneal exposure, preserved visual acuity, absence of corneal epithelial defect, and relief of exposure-related symptoms. At the 8-month follow-up, complete eyelid closure was achieved, with no clinically detectable lagophthalmos remaining. Visible ocular surface exposure was absent, visual acuity was preserved, no corneal epithelial defect was observed, and the patient reported complete resolution of dryness and burning. Tissue continuity, contour, and color match were satisfactory, and the patient expressed satisfaction with the surgical outcome (Fig. 8).

Discussion

Upper eyelid cicatricial ectropion after HZO may be interpreted as an anterior lamellar shortening process caused by post-inflammatory fibrosis. Similar fibrotic contraction with lagophthalmos has been reported after HZO, including in an immunocompetent child [29]. Orbital myositis and other inflammatory sequelae associated with HZO also support the concept that persistent VZV-related inflammation may leave clinically relevant scar tissue in periocular structures [1, 26]. In the upper eyelid, the reconstructive goal extends beyond coverage of a skin defect. Eyelid mobility, anterior lamellar pliability, tarsal support, orbicularis function, and corneal protection must remain balanced because residual retraction, ptosis, or poor eyelid excursion may compromise both vision-related function and appearance [21].

The timing of surgical repair requires control of active infection and sufficient stabilization of cicatricial tissue. In the present case, grafting was performed after the vesicular lesions and active inflammatory findings had resolved, while persistent lagophthalmos and exposure symptoms continued despite lubrication and massage [28]. Scar release with full-thickness skin grafting is most suitable when the primary problem is anterior lamellar shortage after complete release of restrictive bands [3]. Other reported approaches for upper eyelid cicatricial ectropion include retrograde postauricular island flap reconstruction and dermofat grafting with an anti-adhesive agent [7, 9]. Local or regional flaps may be preferred when adjacent healthy tissue is available and vascularized tissue transfer is needed, whereas full-thickness grafting offers a simpler anterior lamellar replacement when the recipient bed is healthy and the defect is limited [16]. For larger defects, donor-site choice may extend beyond the preauricular region to contralateral upper eyelid, postauricular or retroauricular skin, supraclavicular skin, and inner brachial skin, depending on defect size, skin laxity, color match, thickness, and the need to avoid hair-bearing tissue [4, 13, 25].

Technical success depends on complete release of scar-related traction, careful assessment of the recipient bed, appropriate graft thickness, secure graft fixation, and protection of the ocular surface during healing [8]. In periocular full-thickness skin graft series, favorable eyelid position and color match have been reported, while complications such as partial graft failure, infection, hematoma, graft hypertrophy, graft contraction, hypopigmentation, recurrent ectropion, persistent lagophthalmos, and keratopathy remain clinically relevant [4, 10, 13, 25]. These data mainly derive from lower eyelid or mixed periocular reconstruction cohorts, so direct extrapolation to post-HZO upper eyelid cicatricial ectropion requires caution [27]. The current outcome is compatible with favorable graft integration and should be interpreted as case-level evidence rather than a general success rate for this rare anatomical presentation.

Aesthetic outcome is closely related to donor-site selection, tissue thickness, pigmentation, graft contraction, and scar maturation. The preauricular donor site was selected in the present case because the defect was limited in size and the skin provided acceptable color and texture match for the periocular region [11]. At 8 months, tissue contour and color match were maintained without visible recurrent contracture. Findings related to epithelial thickness, melanocyte activity, and graft pigmentation in the broader literature suggest that long-term aesthetic assessment should include not only graft take but also color stability, surface irregularity, and soft-tissue mobility [6, 12, 24].

Several limitations affect interpretation. The report is limited to one patient and one reconstructive approach. Exact preoperative lagophthalmos distance, standardized ocular surface staining score, and validated patient-reported outcome measures were outside the available clinical dataset. Functional recovery was assessed through clinical eyelid closure, absence of visible corneal exposure, preserved visual acuity, absence of corneal epithelial defect, and symptom resolution. The 8-month follow-up window provides mid-term observation, and longer follow-up is needed to evaluate late graft contraction or pigmentary change. Applicability is most relevant to small anterior lamellar upper eyelid defects after resolved HZO inflammation, with caution for extensive defects, posterior lamellar involvement, active keratitis, or major immunosuppression.

Conclusion

Upper eyelid cicatricial ectropion may develop as a rare periocular sequela of HZO and may lead to persistent lagophthalmos and ocular surface exposure. In selected cases with resolved active inflammation, preserved ocular surface integrity, and limited anterior lamellar shortage, scar release followed by preauricular full-thickness skin grafting may provide functional eyelid closure and acceptable aesthetic alignment. Additional cases with objective measurements and longer follow-up are needed to clarify optimal timing, donor-site selection, complication risk, and anatomical indications.
