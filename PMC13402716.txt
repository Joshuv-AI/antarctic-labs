Targeting IFN-I driven tertiary lymphoid structures halts B cell-mediated bone loss in periodontitis
Authors: Jiawei Lu, Xiao Wu, Zehui Xiong, Jinyi Zhang, Haipeng Yang, Feng Liu, Lijun Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLymphocytes play a central role in adaptive immunity and represent the primary source of RANKL in periodontitis. Understanding their organization and activation within gingiva is therefore essential. This study investigates the role of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs), ectopic lymphoid aggregates that orchestrate local immune responses. Using multiplex immunohistochemistry staining, we found that TLSs form and mature with increasing inflammatory severity. These structures function as hubs for B cell activation and were associated with poorer surgical outcomes. Given the known role of fibroblasts in initiating TLS assembly, we explored the underlying mechanism. Using single-cell RNA sequencing and in vitro assays, we identified that oral pathogens activate the IFN-I–IRF7 axis in gingival fibroblasts, leading to overproduction of IFNβ and driving their differentiation into a CXCL13-producing subtype that facilitates TLS organization. Furthermore, in a mouse periodontitis model, we demonstrated that activated IgD+CD80+ B cells within TLSs promote osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption via RANKL secretion. Therapeutically, inhibition of IFN-I signaling disrupted this pathogenic cascade by suppressing TLS formation and subsequent RANKL production, thereby mitigating periodontitis bone loss. In conclusion, our findings establish TLSs as pathological hubs that coordinate B cell-mediated bone destruction in periodontitis and highlight the IFN-I signaling pathway as a promising target for therapeutic intervention.Subject terms: Periodontitis, Translational research

Introduction

The gingival tissue represents a dynamic interface for host-microbe interactions and active immune regulation. The adaptive immune response, particularly local T and B cell circuits, is central to periodontitis pathogenesis,1 representing both a key driver of tissue damage and a promising therapeutic target.2 In periodontitis, T cell subsets, including Th1, Th17, and cytotoxic T cells, secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines and activate osteoclastogenesis, while B cells contribute not only as antibody producers but also as pro-inflammatory effectors through RANKL-mediated bone resorption.3 Furthermore, emerging evidence implicates autoreactive T and B cells in periodontal tissue destruction via mechanisms such as molecular mimicry and autoantibody production.4 These findings highlight the crucial role of lymphocytes in the pathogenesis of periodontitis. However, the spatial organization of these lymphocytes within the gingival tissue, along with the mechanisms controlling their local activation, remains poorly understood.

Tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) are ectopic lymphoid aggregates that form during chronic inflammation or infection, defined as aggregates containing CD20+ B cells, CD3+CD4+ T cells, and PNAd+ high endothelial venules (HEVs).5 TLSs enable local antigen presentation, T cell activation, B cell maturation, and pro-inflammatory cytokines production.6 The structure and function of TLSs are tissue-specific and may exert both pro-inflammatory and immune-protective effects in different disease states. TLSs support local autoantibody production and exacerbate joint inflammation in rheumatoid arthritis.7 TLS formation driven by stromal cells contributes to tissue remodeling and chronic inflammation in aging kidneys.8 However, TLSs at mucosal sites facilitate local germinal center reactions and protective B cell responses in viral infection.9 Consequently, precision interventions that selectively disrupt TLS formation hold promise for preventing disease progression.10 However, the presence, structural characteristics, and functional roles of TLSs in periodontitis remain unknown.

The assembly of TLSs is typically initiated by stromal cells (such as fibroblasts) under inflammatory stimulation.11,12 In periodontitis, oral pathogens (e.g., Porphyromonas gingivalis [P. gingivalis]) may activate gingival fibroblasts via Toll-like receptors (TLRs),13 triggering the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including the key chemokine CXCL13, which recruits CXCR5+ Tfh and B cells.14 These findings suggest that therapeutically targeting this cascade to block lymphocyte recruitment may disrupt TLS formation and the ensuing tissue-destructive immune response, potentially offering a promising adjunct to conventional periodontal therapy.

In this study, through multidimensional data analysis and functional experiments, we demonstrate for the first time the presence of mature TLSs in periodontitis tissues. Our findings reveal that these structures recruit and activate B cells via the “IFN-I–fibroblast–CXCL13” axis, contributing to bone destruction through RANKL-dependent mechanisms. Contrary to prevailing views, we discovered that the pathogenic potential of TLS-associated B cells in periodontitis is mediated primarily through RANKL secretion rather than antibody production. Collectively, our findings not only reveal immune–bone crosstalk in periodontitis but also provide a rational, precision-medicine framework for TLS-targeted interventions that may prevent progressive tooth loss and reduce systemic inflammatory burden.

Results

Tertiary lymphoid structures were found in periodontitis tissues and were observed to mature with disease progression

While tertiary lymphoid structures have been well-characterized in many chronic inflammatory conditions, their existence and organizational characteristics in periodontitis tissues remain undefined. Using multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) analysis, we evaluated the spatial distribution of TLS-associated immune cells in periodontal tissues from patients with different stages and grades of periodontitis (n = 3), including CD20+ B cells, CD3+ T cells, CD3+CD4+ T helper (Th) cells, as well as the critical lymphoid-organizing chemokine CXCL13, and PNAd+ high endothelial venules (HEVs), which were considered as hallmarks of mature TLSs (Fig. 1a).

The results revealed that the TLS-like lymphoid aggregates in periodontitis were predominantly composed of Th cells and B cells, while CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (CTLs) maintained a dispersed infiltration pattern (Supplementary Fig. S1). Specifically, lymphocyte populations were sparsely present in healthy gingival tissues (Fig. 1a). However, stage III periodontitis exhibited nascent lymphoid aggregates, characterized by focal accumulation of Th cells and B cells (Fig. 1b), but only a few HEVs (PNAd+) were detected, indicating that TLSs were in an immature state. In contrast, in stage IVB (Fig. 1c) and IVC (Fig. 1d) periodontitis, the number of CD4+ Th cells and CXCL13+ cells significantly increased, with more structured distribution and prominent HEVs (PNAd+) surrounding the aggregates, features consistent with those of mature TLSs. Notably, multiple TLSs developed simultaneously in stage IVB periodontitis (Fig. 1c). As the disease progressed to stage IVC, these multiple TLSs fused, leading to extensive lymphocyte accumulation (Fig. 1d).

Taken together, these findings suggested that with increasing severity of periodontitis, the infiltration of CD4+ Th cells and B cells significantly increases (Fig. 1e). The orderly aggregation of lymphocytes in periodontal tissues leads to the formation and maturation of tertiary lymphoid structures, which serve as sites for B cell and CD4+ Th cell interactions.

Tertiary lymphoid structures facilitated B cell maturation and were linked to poor surgical prognosis

To investigate the functional role of tertiary lymphoid structures in periodontitis progression, we then analyzed excised gingival tissues from severe periodontitis patients undergoing periodontal flap surgery, paired with their clinical records and radiological data. IHC staining was used to identify TLSs in periodontitis tissues, and lymphocyte distribution was quantified within TLS and non-TLS regions (Fig. 2a).

Quantitative analysis revealed significantly elevated densities of CD20+, CD3+, and CD4+ cells within TLSs versus whole-section averages (P < 0.001 each, Fig. 2b). TLSs contained greater than 60% of total periodontal B cells, and 36.87% of T cells and 47.14% of Th cells (Fig. 2c). Subsequent correlation analysis with clinical and radiological parameters showed strong positive correlations between intra-TLS B cell density and probing depth (PD, P = 0.004, r = 0.546 2), clinical attachment loss (CAL, P = 0.005, r = 0.535 4), and radiological bone loss (RBL, P = 0.007, r = 0.519 7, Fig. 2d).

Patients were then stratified into TLS(+) and TLS(−) groups for prognostic analysis. We found that TLSs were detected in 28 out of the 45 samples (62.22%) enrolled in the study. As the presence of bleeding on probing (BOP + ) is a well-established clinical indicator of disease progression in periodontitis.15,16 Based on previous studies,15 we define the time to the first postoperative detection of gingival bleeding (BOP + ) as postoperative progression-free survival. As a result, the postoperative progression-free survival was significantly shorter in the TLS(+) group (median: 46 days) than in the TLS(−) group (with over 50% remaining progression-free, P = 0.023, Fig. 2e). TLS subclassification by cellular composition identified three subtypes: T cell-dominant, B cell-dominant, and T/B cell type (Fig. 2f). Notably, most TLSs found in periodontitis tissues were T/B cell type, suggesting close interactions between T and B cells within the periodontal microenvironment that may contribute to the pathogenesis of periodontitis (Fig. 2g).

Collectively, these findings indicated that tertiary lymphoid structures served as focal sites for B cell activation in periodontitis and may contribute to sustained local inflammation, potentially influencing surgical outcomes.

The single-cell atlas of periodontitis tissues revealed the existence and functions of CXCL13+ inflammatory fibroblasts

Stromal cells (typically fibroblasts) are generally considered to initiate the formation of tertiary lymphoid structures.11,12 Given this, we examined single-cell RNA sequencing data from healthy (n = 13) and periodontitis (n = 8) gingival tissues (GSE16424117) to explore fibroblast heterogeneity and their potential contribution to TLS formation. We identified 15 073 fibroblasts (FB, Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). After standard preprocessing, these fibroblasts were classified into six clusters based on gene expression: CXCL13+ FB, POSTN+ FB, BMP4+ FB, APOE+ FB, ITGA4+ FB, and VEGFC+ FB (Fig. 3a, b). Among these clusters, CXCL13+ FB was the most abundant, with significantly increased proportions in the periodontitis tissues (P = 0.049, Fig. 3c). Volcano plots showed high expression of CXCL13 in this cluster (Supplementary Fig. 2c). However, CD21 (CR2), a canonical follicular dendritic cell (FDC) marker, was undetectable (Fig. 3d), suggesting CXCL13+ FB may represent a distinct population from FDCs. To further validate the characterization of CXCL13+ fibroblasts (FBs) in periodontitis tissues, we performed multiplex immunofluorescence staining. Our results show that CXCL13+ cells co-express the fibroblast marker Vimentin, with a subset also positive for α-SMA, and are negative for the epithelial marker pan-cytokeratin (Supplementary Fig. 2d). These findings confirm their mesenchymal origin and support their classification as activated fibroblasts.

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed enrichment of immune defense terms in CXCL13+ FB, including multiple chemokine-mediated pathways (Supplementary Fig. 2e), indicating dual capacity for recruiting lymphocytes and innate immune cells. This supports that CXCL13+ FB are likely key responders to pathogenic stimuli and may undergo phenotypic transformation into lymphoid-organizing fibroblasts under inflammation.11 Additionally, JAK-STAT pathway components (IL6, JAK2, SOD2, IRF8) were enriched (Supplementary Fig. 2e). In contrast, the POSTN+ FB, BMP4+ FB, and APOE+ FB clusters exhibited distinct osteogenic, odontogenic, and tissue-regenerative potential (Supplementary Fig. 2e). Furthermore, CXCL13 mRNA levels were significantly elevated in diseased periodontal tissues (P < 0.001, Fig. 3e). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining confirmed a significantly expansion of the CXCL13+ fibroblast population in the periodontitis group (P = 0.006, Fig. 3f).

We then performed single-cell sequencing analysis of CD4+ T cells and B cells from periodontal tissues and identified their subsets by differential gene expression (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b, d, e). We identified Tfh cells in periodontal tissues that highly expressed CXCL13 and were significantly increased in periodontitis (Supplementary Fig. 3c). CellChat analysis showed enhanced fibroblast-lymphocyte interactions in periodontitis (Supplementary Fig. 3f, g). CXCL signaling emerged as the primary pathway, with CXCL13+ FB acting as the dominant signal source for Th1 (T helper cell type 1), Th17 (T helper cell type 17)/Treg (Regulatory T cells), Tfh (Follicular helper T cells), and Tn (Naïve T cells, Fig. 3g). In periodontitis tissues, signaling toward Th17/Treg and Tfh increased, while signaling toward Tn decreased versus healthy controls (Fig. 3h).

Taken together, our findings show that CXCL13+ fibroblasts in inflamed periodontal tissues exhibit certain phenotypic features of lymphoid tissue fibroblasts, suggesting that they may potentially coordinate TLS assembly in periodontitis.

IRF7-driven overproduction of IFNβ activated gingival fibroblasts into a CXCL13+ inflammatory phenotype

To investigate the mechanisms underlying fibroblast activation during periodontitis, we isolated primary human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs). Cell purity was confirmed by Vimentin expression (Supplementary Fig. 4a). The results showed that LPS treatment significantly upregulated CXCL13 mRNA levels in HGFs but did not affect CCL19 (P = 0.046, Fig. 4a). Given that fibroblasts can be activated to an inflammatory state by distinct cytokines (e.g., type I interferons [IFN-Is],18 IL-17A,19 IL-2220). We further treated HGFs with these cytokines, of which only IFNβ and LPS significantly induced CXCL13 production (P < 0.001 each, Fig. 4b). Since LPS stimulation also triggered IFNβ production in HGFs (Fig. 4c), we hypothesized that the overproduction of IFNβ in periodontitis may be the critical for HGFs activation into the CXCL13+ phenotype. Consistent with this, IFNβ stimulation further increased both CXCL13 and IFNB1 mRNA levels in HGFs (Fig. 4c). We subsequently detected significantly elevated IFNB1 mRNA levels in periodontitis tissues (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Immunofluorescence staining consistently confirmed a significant increase in IFNβ+ fibroblast populations within the periodontitis group (Supplementary Fig. 4c).

To further elucidate the transcriptional regulatory network underlying fibroblast transformation, we performed SCENIC analysis on fibroblast clusters. The results highlighted that key transcription factors (TFs) in the JAK-STAT pathway, including STAT1 and STAT2, exhibit high activity in the CXCL13+ subcluster. This is consistent with previous reports that IFNβ signaling is predominantly mediated through the JAK-STAT signaling pathway. Additionally, IRF7, the primary regulator of type I interferons production, exhibited high activity in CXCL13+ FB, while PRDM1 (Blimp-1), the negative regulator of B cell differentiation,21 was also highly activated in this cluster (Fig. 4d). Subsequently, we found that interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), IFN-I signaling related genes, and transcription factors were highly expressed in fibroblast clusters within the single-cell data (Supplementary Fig. 4d). qPCR analysis showed –that IRF7 and PRDM1 were both transcriptionally activated in response to LPS or IFNβ stimulation (Fig. 4e). In contrast, at the protein level, LPS and IFNβ stimulation led to a significant upregulation of IRF7 but a downregulation of PRDM1 (Fig. 4f, g), revealing a divergence in their post-transcriptional regulation. We propose that the uncoupling of PRDM1 mRNA and protein levels reflects post-transcriptional control, likely mediated by microRNAs known to regulate PRDM1 expression through translational inhibition or mRNA destabilization.22 To assess the activation status of the PRDM1 and IRF7 within periodontal TLSs in situ, thereby better reflecting their relationship with TLSs, we performed immunofluorescence staining in periodontal tissues. The results revealed that fibroblasts within TLSs exhibited substantial nuclear IRF7 activation, whereas PRDM1 activation was more prevalent in healthy controls (Supplementary Fig. 4e, f). These findings suggest that intra-TLS fibroblast activation, particularly IRF7 upregulation, is associated with periodontitis pathogenesis.

To confirm the role of IFN-I signaling, we blocked the IFNAR-1 in LPS-stimulated HGFs using anti-IFNAR1 antibody (Fig. 5a). IFNAR1 inhibition effectively suppressed LPS-induced upregulation of IFNB1 and CXCL13, and IRF7 mRNA, but had minimal effect on PRDM1 (Fig. 5b). Western blot further confirmed reduction of IRF7 activation and IFNβ production in HGFs upon IFN-I signaling inhibition (Fig. 5c). Using siRNA-mediated knockdown in IFNβ-stimulated HGFs (Fig. 5d, Supplementary Fig. 2g, h). We found that IRF7 knockdown reduced both CXCL13 and IFNB1 expression, while PRDM1 knockdown increased CXCL13 without affecting IFNB1 (Fig. 5e). Furthermore, the knockdown of IRF7 significantly attenuated the IFNβ-induced downregulation of PRDM1 at the protein level, supporting a role for IRF7 in regulating PRDM1 expression under IFNβ stimulation (Fig. 5f).

In summary, IRF7 activation under inflammatory conditions promotes IFNβ overproduction and subsequent PRDM1 suppression, driving HGFs toward a CXCL13+ phenotype. These results suggested a self-reinforcing feedforward loop, wherein IFN-I signaling regulated fibroblast reprogramming in periodontitis.

We examined TLS-associated phenotypes in LIP mice. Flow cytometry revealed a substantial increase in peripheral blood Th cell population (CD4+) and their extensive expression of CXCR5 (>80%, Fig. 6b, c, Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). Peripheral Tfh cells (CD4+CXCR5+PD-1+) were significantly elevated on day 8 (P = 0.013) and day 16 (P < 0.001) versus controls (Fig. 6c). Furthermore, gingival Tfh cells tissues increased significantly on day 8 (P = 0.039) but not day 16 (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d). These CXCR5+ Th cells (or Tfh-like cells23) entered the periodontal tissues, responded to CXCL13+ fibroblasts, and formed spatial aggregates (Supplementary Fig. 5e–h).

Germinal center (GC) B cells are crucial for TLS maturation.9 We quantified GC B cells (CD19+Ki67+BCL6+) in the gingiva and submandibular lymph node tissues by flow cytometry. Notably, given that CD19 is widely used as a B‑cell marker for TLS identification in mouse models, we employed CD19 instead of CD20 for this analysis.24 The results showed that gingival GC B cells were significantly higher in LIP mice (P < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 5I, j). Moreover, the proportion of gingival GC B cells exceeded the level in the submandibular lymph nodes (P < 0.001), indicating enhanced TLS-associated GC responses.

We then assessed the number of B cells (CD19+), HEVs (PNAd+), and osteoclasts (TRAP+) in the maxillary sections of LIP mice. Interestingly, the results showed that elevated CD19+ cells aggregated in TLS-like distribution, surrounded by HEVs, with osteoclasts adjacent (Fig. 6d–f). Furthermore, osteoclast numbers were found to be correlated positively with CD19+ cells (P = 0.012) and HEVs (P = 0.016, Fig. 6g). This spatial closeness suggested a potential interaction between the two cell populations.

Taken together, our findings revealed that TLS formation may be associated with bone destruction in periodontitis. We speculated that B cell activation within TLSs may regulate osteoclastogenesis. Therefore, it is necessary to evaluate the activation of B cells during periodontal inflammation.

The CXCL13-CXCR5 axis derived recruitment of B cells and RANKL production by activated B cells are involved in osteoclast formation in periodontitis

We performed flow cytometry on peripheral blood and gingival tissue samples from LIP mice. The results showed that peripheral B cells also widely expressed CXCR5 (Con/LIP Day 08/ LIP Day 16: 89.39%/97.51%/95.55%) and that the proportion of CXCR5+ B cells was significantly increased compared to the control group (P < 0.001, Fig. 7a, b). We then further assessed B cell activation states based on the expression of CD8025,26 and IgD.27–29 The IgD+CD80⁻ B cell subset (likely naïve mature B cells27) dominated among the CXCR5+ B cell subsets (about 60% of CXCR5+ B cells, Fig. 7b). However, the overall peripheral changes were minimal, with only a slight increase in the IgD+CD80+ population by day 16 (Fig. 7b).

Compared to the moderate changes in peripheral blood, the alterations in B cell subsets within the gingival tissues of LIP mice differed markedly. Under resting conditions (control group), only a small number of B cells (mean: 8.56%) were present in periodontal tissues (Fig. 7c). However, in LIP mice, the number of gingival B cells significantly increased on day 8 post-ligation (40.10%, P < 0.001, Fig. 7c). Concurrently, the IgD+CD80+ B cell subset became the predominant B cell subset on day post-ligation (mean percentage of gingival B cells in Con/LIP Day 08/LIP Day 16: 77.30%/77.10%/72.18%, Fig. 7c). Additionally, an IgD⁻CD80+ B cell subset was detected on day 8 post-ligation (Fig. 7d), which may represent a more mature memory B cell population. The proportion of IgD+CD80+ B cell subset on day 8 post-ligation was also significantly higher than that in the control group (mean percentage of gingival live lymphocytes: 40.10% vs 6.69%, P = 0.001, Fig. 7d), suggesting that day 8 might be the most functionally active period for tertiary lymphoid structures in LIP mice.

Further investigation of the gingival tissues from LIP mice revealed that a large number of HEVs were present in the periodontal tissues, accompanied by surrounding B cells. However, on day 16, although HEVs were still abundant, the surrounding B cells gradually decreased (Supplementary Fig. 6a), possibly because they had transformed into more mature memory B cell populations or plasma cell populations (losing CD19 expression).

The positive correlation between number of osteoclasts and B cells in periodontal tissues, observed in our previous results, suggested a potential link between these two cell types. Supporting the observed correlation between B cells and osteoclasts, single-cell analysis and immunofluorescence staining confirmed that B cells express RANKL (TNFSF11) in periodontitis tissues (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Immunofluorescence staining revealed increased RANKL signaling in B cells within human periodontitis tissues and LIP periodontal tissues (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d). Meanwhile, flow cytometry analysis of B cell subsets showed a higher proportion of RANKL+ cells among IgD+CD80+ B cells than among IgD-CD80+ B cells in LIP Day 08 (56.90% vs 0.93% P < 0.001) and LIP Day 16 group (46.23% vs 1.03%, P < 0.001, Fig. 7e, f). We then examined the number of TRAP+ cells in the gingival tissues of healthy individuals and periodontitis patients with/without TLSs. Surprisingly, we found that TRAP+ cells (osteoclast precursors) were mostly clustered near the identified TLSs and were usually on the side of the B-cell zone (Supplementary Fig. 6e, f). Taken together, these results indicated that IgD+CD80+ B cells were the predominate B cell subset in periodontal tissues and played a crucial role in osteoclast induction during periodontal inflammation.

In summary, the results were consistent with our hypothesis that B cells respond to CXCL13-mediated recruitment during periodontitis, gradually activating and maturing within tertiary lymphoid structures in periodontal tissues, and participating in osteoclast formation through RANKL secretion, thereby influencing the progression of periodontitis.

Blocking IFN-Is signaling reduces Tfh recruitment and B cell activation, reducing osteoclast formation in LIP mice

Given the critical role of IFN-I signaling in TLS formation and bone destruction established above, we next tested whether locally blocking IFN-I pathway could be therapeutically beneficial. We administered anti-IFNAR1 to the gingiva of LIP mice (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Blocking IFN-I signaling significantly reduced mRNA levels of Cxcl13 (P = 0.01) and Ifnb1 (P = 0.019) in the gingiva tissues (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Micro-CT analysis confirmed that anti-IFNAR1 treatment significantly alleviated bone destruction on days 8 and 16 post-ligation (P < 0.001 each, Fig. 8a, b). TRAP and Masson staining further confirmed the reduction in alveolar bone loss/collagen destruction and a significant decrease in osteoclast numbers on day 8 (P = 0.015) and day 16 (P < 0.001, Fig. 8c, d), indicating that local IFN-I blockade attenuated periodontitis progression. Furthermore, CXCL13 levels are significantly elevated in the serum sample in the LIP+Isotype group compared to controls but attenuated upon IFNAR1 blockade, indicating that type I interferon signaling contributes to CXCL13 induction in periodontitis (Supplementary Fig. 7c). We also assessed changes in B cell subsets—notably CD80+IgD+ B cells (putative RANKL producers)—following TLS formation. Anti-IFNAR1 treatment reduced total B cell numbers in peripheral blood (52.28% vs 46.40%) and gingiva (32.33% vs 8.40%), suggesting suppressed B cell recruitment (Supplementary Fig. 7e–g). The proportion of gingival CD80+IgD+ B cells significantly decreased (mean percentage of live lymphocytes: 31.80% vs 8.03%, P < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 7h, i). Meanwhile, the numbers of peripheral Th cells (P = 0.022) and Tfh cells (P = 0.015) were also reduced (Supplementary Fig. 7j, k). Meanwhile, immunofluorescence staining results confirmed diminished B cells, PNAd+ areas, CXCL13+ fibroblasts, and CD19+RANKL+ cell numbers upon IFNAR1 blockade (P = 0.011, Fig. 8e, f).

These results indicate that anti-IFNAR1 treatment reduced the activity of TLSs in periodontitis. Specifically, the reduction of CD80+IgD+ B cells led to suppressed RANKL secretion and osteoclast formation in periodontal tissues, supporting its potential as a targeted therapeutic strategy for periodontitis.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that TLSs play a critical role in the development and progression of periodontitis. We found that pathogen-induced differentiation of periodontal fibroblasts into an inflammatory population drives secretion of the chemokine CXCL13, which recruits T and B lymphocytes to infiltrate periodontal tissues, forming structurally and functionally organized TLSs. Spatially organized T follicular helper (Tfh) cells and memory B cells within periodontal TLSs sustain chronic inflammation through RANKL-mediated osteoclastogenesis and cytokine amplification loops. These findings clarify the critical role of TLSs in periodontitis progression and suggest that targeting TLSs could offer a novel therapeutic strategy for periodontitis.

The formation of TLSs typically relies on the “phenotype transformation” of local stromal cells, whose key function of secreting CXCL13 to facilitate the spatial co-localization of CXCR5+ T cells and B cells. Previous studies have shown that in chronic inflammatory contexts such as rheumatoid arthritis, fibroblasts can differentiate into a pro-inflammatory phenotype, such as CXCL13+ fibroblasts, which participate in immune cell recruitment and tissue remodeling through chemokine secretion.30 Our research reveals that activated gingival fibroblasts similarly exhibit this proinflammatory phenotype. Further investigation revealed that this subset may maintain a sustained activation state through an IRF7-dependent, IFNβ self-reinforcing loop. However, we also discovered that gingival fibroblasts comprise multiple functionally distinct subsets, and the regulatory network governing CXCL13 secretion in vitro exhibits significant complexity. Nevertheless, our findings not only expands our understanding of the stromal immune network in periodontitis, but also implies fibroblast phenotype transformation may be an early event in TLS formation, offering new insights into fibroblast heterogeneity in other inflammatory diseases.

Our study also expands the understanding of B cell immune functions. Traditionally, B cells are known for their role in adaptive immunity through antibody production.31,32 However, recent studies highlight that B cells possess multiple antibody-independent functions,33–35 such as the “osteoclastogenic B cells” identified in multiple myeloma that can directly promote osteoclast formation.36 Here, we clarified that the IgD+CD80+ B cells activated by TLSs highly expressed RANKL and may activate osteoclasts through the RANKL–RANK signaling axis, exacerbating periodontal bone destruction. This finding suggests a pathogenic function distinct from conventional B cell roles. However, we have not yet determined whether antigen-specific antibody production accompanies this process, nor can we rule out the role of antibody-dependent functions in TLS-mediated inflammation. Therefore, targeting these B cells or blocking the RANKL signaling may offer new treatments for periodontal bone loss.

Therapeutically, anti-IFNAR1 antibody intervention reversed fibroblast transformation and significantly reduced bone destruction. Given the localized nature of periodontitis, developing targeted therapies like small-molecule inhibitors (such as the JAK inhibitor Baricitinib37) or locally delivered oral gels to block IFN-I signaling holds immense promise. This localized targeting strategy offers a major safety advantage, minimizing the risk of systemic immunosuppression and infection associated with systemic IFN-I blockade,38 making it a highly viable strategy for treating periodontitis.

While this study focuses on the pathogenic role of TLSs in periodontitis, their potential protective functions should not be overlooked. On one hand, the germinal center reactions within TLSs may facilitate the production of pathogen-specific antibodies.39,40 On the other hand, Tfh–B cell interactions could enhance mucosal immune memory. We hypothesize that chronic inflammation in periodontitis may suppress these protective functions. Future studies could apply the spatiotemporal single-cell and spatial omics technologies to further dissect the functional characteristics of TLSs and explore “reprogramming strategies” aimed at developing more precise immune interventions for periodontitis.

We also acknowledge several limitations in the current study. First, the regulatory mechanisms underlying CXCL13 production by human gingival fibroblasts remain incompletely understood and warrant further investigation, especially the transcriptional regulation of PRDM1 and the interaction between IRF7 and PRDM1. Second, TLS characterization in the LIP mouse model was limited by technical challenges, as our use of CD19 and PNAd as key markers does not fully capture the complexity of murine TLS structure. Third, despite the use of IHC staining on serial section of gingival tissues, the identification of TLS in clinical samples may still be subject to false-positive detection; therefore, larger-scale validation studies are needed to confirm our findings. Emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence-assisted histopathological analysis may offer more objective and accurate TLS identification in future investigations.41,42 Addressing these gaps in future research will be critical for advancing our understanding of TLS biology and its therapeutic potential in periodontal disease.

Materials and methods

Study approval

Human gingival tissues for histological staining were obtained from the Department of Periodontics, Stomatological Hospital of Tongji University. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, which was enacted in 1975 and revised in 2000. The study protocol has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Stomatology Hospital Affiliated to Tongji University (Approval No. [2023]-SR-18). All participants provided written informed consent.

The inclusion criteria for patients in this study were: 1. Patients diagnosed with periodontitis Stage III or IV requiring periodontal surgery, or patients with good periodontal health requiring crown lengthening surgery. Among them, the severity of periodontitis and the healthy periodontal status were defined according to the classification of periodontal diseases and conditions43; 2. Subjects without systemic diseases. Exclusion criteria were: 1. Participants who had taken anti-inflammatory drugs or antibiotics in the past 3 months; 2. Former or current smokers. All participants received non-surgical periodontal treatment and surgical treatment from the same experienced periodontist. The clinical information of samples is shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Preoperative (baseline) and postoperative clinical parameters (6 weeks, 3, 6, and 12 months post-surgery) were measured by experienced periodontists. Probing depth and clinical attachment loss were defined as the average values of six sites (mesiobuccal, buccal, distobuccal, mesiolingual/mesiopalatal, lingual/palatal, and distolingual/distopalatal) measured on the teeth within the surgical area. Radiographic bone loss was assessed by CBCT and was calculated by the average percentage of bone loss relative to root length measured at the mesial and distal sites of the teeth within the surgical area.43

Specific pathogen-free C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Leagene Biotechnology. All animal feeding and experimental operations were conducted according to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the ARRIVE guidelines and were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Stomatology Hospital of Tongji University (Approval No. [2023]-DW-27).

All experiments were conducted using 8-week-old mice. All animals were housed in specific pathogen-free conditions. Sample sizes were determined based on the effect size (assumed significance level at 0.05 and assumed power at 90%). The specific sample size of each experiment was provided in the figure legends.

Animal experiment

To establish a mouse ligature-induced periodontitis (LIP) model, sterile 5.0-silk ligatures were placed on the crevice of both sides of the maxillary second molar and knotted on the palatal side for 8 or 16 days. Freshly prepared 109 CFU P. gingivalis in 100 μL 2% (wt/vol) carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC, or CMC alone for controls) was administered every 2 days after ligation. For IFN-I blockade, 1 µg anti-IFNAR1 (Selleck A2121) in 10 µL PBS or isotype control (Selleck A2106) was locally injected into gingival tissues using a microsyringe44 (25-µL syringe with a 33-gauge needle, Hamilton Company), every two days after ligation. The dose was based on a previous study.45

For P. gingivalis preparation, P. gingivalis strain ATCC33277 was cultured anaerobically (85% N₂, 5% CO₂, 10% H₂; 37 °C) in Brain Heart Infusion broth supplemented with 5 g/L yeast extract, 1 mg/L vitamin K1, and 5 mg/L hemin (all from Hope Biotechnology). OD600 of P. gingivalis suspension in culture medium at 0.5 corresponded to mid-log phase and ~1 × 1010 cells/mL. After centrifugation, the pellet was washed 3 times and resuspended with 2% (wt/vol) CMC for the following oral inoculation.

Cell culture

Primary human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs) were isolated and cultured as previously described.46 Briefly, gingival tissues from crown lengthening surgery were washed 3 times, sectioned into 1 mm3 and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (ExCell) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (Gibco). Cells at passage 3–6 were used. For stimulation, HGFs were treated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, InvivoGen) from P. gingivalis, and IFNβ (Biolegend) for 2, 4, 8, 16 and 24 h for qPCR analysis and 24 and 48 h for western blot or ELISA.

Micro-computed tomography Analysis

Mouse maxillae were fixed in 4% PFA (48 h, 4 °C), stabilized in scanning tubes, and scanned (μCT50, SCANCO) in 10 μm resolution under 200 μA, 70 kV. A three-dimensional representative image was reconstructed using SCANCO Medical systems. Alveolar bone loss was quantified by measuring the distance from the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) to the alveolar bone crest (ABC) at five sites on each maxillary second molar.47

H&E, Masson and TRAP staining

Maxillae were decalcified in 10% EDTA (28 days), dehydrated (automated processor, Thermo), and embedded in paraffin. Sections (4 μm) were dewaxed (xylene/ethanol), hydrated, and stained using H&E staining kit (Sangon Biotech), Tartrate-resistant Acid Phosphatase (TRAP) staining Kit (Servicebio), and Masson’s staining kit (Servicebio), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Images were captured using a Nikon microscope (DS-Ri1, Nikon).

Immunohistochemistry and Immunofluorescence staining

The deparaffinized sections were subjected to antigen retrieval, incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide, sealed with 10% serum and 0.1% Triton X-100 and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 2). For immunofluorescence staining, the tissues were processed as above and incubated with donkey anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 or donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 594 (1:400, Invitrogen), followed by nuclear staining with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Images were taken using a Nikon fluorescence microscope (DS-Ri1, Nikon) or scanned using a slide scanner (Zeiss Axioscan 7).

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed with the Opal 7-Color Manual IHC Kit (Akoya Biosciences, USA) according to the protocol recommended by the manufacturer. After deparaffinization and rehydration, sections were subjected to antigen retrieval, blocking, primary antibody incubation, secondary antibody incubation, and fluorescent dye staining. The above steps were repeated until all primary antibodies had been incubated. DAPI was used for nuclear staining. The primary antibodies used in this research were anti-CD20 (1:1 000, Proteintech), anti-CD3 (1:1 000, abcam), anti-CD4 (1:2 000, abcam), anti-CXCL13 (1:400, Proteintech), and anti-PNAd (1:400, Biolegend). Sections were scanned using Zeiss Axioscan 7 slide scanner. For quantitative analysis, five randomly selected positive staining sites in each sample were imaged under high magnification (400×), and the positive cells were counted using ImageJ software (v1.54 d).

Tertiary lymphoid structures detection and classification

Tertiary lymphoid structures were identified by immunohistochemistry on serial paraffin sections of gingival tissues using antibodies against CD20, CD3, CD4, and PNAd. According to the criteria established in a previous similar study,48 TLSs were defined as aggregates containing CD20+ B cells, CD3+CD4+ T cells, and PNAd+ high endothelial venules (HEVs). Samples meeting the above TLS standards were classified as TLS(+), while those without detectable TLS were classified as TLS(-). The density and percentage of CD3+, CD4+, and CD20+ cells were quantified both across the entire tissue section and specifically within TLS areas (delineated by the presence of the above markers) using the “Positive cell detection” and “Fast cell count” functions in QuPath software (v0.5.0).49,50 For tissues containing multiple TLSs, the mean percentage and cell density values of all TLSs were used for analysis. TLS maturity was also classified based on histological features and criteria established in previous studies.48,51,52 Immature TLS were characterized by mixed T- and B-cell zones without clear boundaries and low HEV density, whereas mature TLS exhibited segregated B-cell and T-cell clusters with well-defined boundaries and high HEV density. T and B cell–dominated TLSs were defined as those composed mainly of T and B cells (>75%), respectively, and other TLSs were defined as T/B type TLSs.53

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

CXCL13 levels in HGF supernatants were quantified using Human CXCL13 ELISA kits (MULTI SCIENCES, EK1105) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 100 μL samples were added to antibody-coated 96-well plates and incubated (2 h, room temperature). After adding 100 μL HRP-conjugate reagent (45 min incubation), 100 μL substrate solution was added (20 min, room temperature, dark). Reactions were terminated with 100 μL stop solution, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted using RIPA buffer (Sigma Aldrich) with 1% protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma Aldrich). Lysates were sonicated, centrifuged, and quantified via BCA assay (Beyotime). Proteins (20 μg) were resolved by SDS-PAGE and electroblotted onto PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA (1 h, room temperature), incubated with primary antibodies overnight (4 °C), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1 h, room temperature). Primary antibodies: anti-GAPDH (1:4 000, Proteintech), anti-IFNβ (1:1 000, Proteintech), anti-IRF7 (1:1 000, Santa Cruz), anti-Blimp-1 (1:1 000, CST). Signals were detected using ImageQuant™ 800 (Cytiva) and quantified by densitometry (ImageJ v1.54 d). Results were normalized to GAPDH. Three independent replicates were performed. Antibody details are in Supplementary Table 2.

Quantitative real-time PCR assay and siRNA Transfection

Total RNA was isolated from cells or tissues using TRIzol (Takara). qPCR was performed with the Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeason) on the QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher). Relative quantification used the 2−ΔΔCt method with human GAPDH or mouse Gapdh as endogenous controls. Data were expressed as fold changes. There were three independent replications of the experiment at each time point. Primers are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Transfection was performed using RFectSP V2 (BIOG) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Small interfering RNA (siRNA) was designed and synthesized by RIBOBIO. A negative control and siRNA targeting PRDM1 or IRF7 was used for knockdown experiments. To assess the efficiency of transfection, qPCR was performed 48 h after transfection.

Single cell sequencing analysis

Publicly available scRNA-seq data (GSE164241) were processed using Seurat (v4.0.1).54 Low-quality cells (<200 genes, >5 000 genes, or >15% mitochondrial gene expression) were excluded. Data underwent standard Seurat workflow: normalization, integration (canonical correlation analysis [CCA]), dimensionality reduction, and clustering. Differential gene expression analysis (Wilcoxon rank-sum test) identified genes with |log2FC | ≥ 0.75 and adjusted P < 0.05. Cell type annotation was performed using the combination of automatic annotation (SingleR),55 manual cell annotation with established marker genes, and further verification.

Enrichment analysis and Transcription factor regulating pathway analysis

Differentially expressed genes from FindAllMarkers (with parameters mentioned above) underwent GO enrichment analysis (ClusterProfiler [v4.0.5]). For transcriptional networks, we reconstructed the gene regulatory network following the pipeline of Single-Cell Regulatory Network Inference and Clustering (SCENIC) analysis (v1.3.1).56 Genes not in RcisTarget databases were filtered. v9 cisTarget Human motif database was used to perform cis-regulatory motif analysis. Regulon activity was calculated via runSCENIC_4_aucell_binarize.

Flow cytometry

Preparation of single-cell suspension of peripheral blood, mouse gingiva and submandibular lymph node was performed according to previously described methods.57–59 The prepared single cells were incubated in the dark with fluorescence-labeled antibodies (Supplementary Table 2) for 20 minutes. For intracellular staining of germinal center B cells, single-cell suspension was first stained with anti-CD19. Then, after fixation and permeabilization, cells were further stained with anti-Ki67 and anti-BCL6. Data were acquired by BD FACS Verse TM System (BD Biosciences) and analyzed by FlowJo software (V10.0, TreeStar). Gating strategies can be found in Supplementary Fig. 8.

Statistical analysis

All data analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism (v9.0.1, GraphPad Software) or R (v4.2.1). Progression-free survival time was estimated using the log-rank test to compare between groups. The normality of data distribution was assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. 1. For comparisons between two groups with normally distributed data, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests were used, while for normally distributed data with three or more groups, one-way ANOVA with post-hoc Dunn or Tukey’s multiple comparison tests were performed (indicated in the figure legends). Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. 2. For non-normally distributed data, nonparametric analysis using the Mann-Whitney U test (2-tailed) for two groups or a Kruskal–Wallis test with post-hoc Dunn or Bonferroni multiple comparison tests for 3 groups or more were performed (indicated in the figure legends). Non-normally distributed data were shown as median with 95% confidence interval. Adjusted P values were used and P < 0.05 was considered to reach statistical significance.
