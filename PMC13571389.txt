Retrograde Balloon-Assisted Screening Prevents Guidewire Prolapse During Acute-Angle Distal Cap Puncture in RCA CTO

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) for chronic total occlusion (CTO) has advanced substantially over the past 2 decades, driven by improvements in guidewire technology, dedicated devices, and the development of refined procedural strategies.1 These advances have led to higher procedural success rates and expanded indications for CTO-PCI, even in anatomically complex lesions.2,3 Despite these advances, retrograde CTO-PCI remains technically demanding and is highly dependent on anatomical factors. One of the critical challenges is achieving successful retrograde guidewire wiring into the CTO segment after collateral channel crossing.Take-Home Messages•This report highlights the importance of systematically assessing the retrograde exit angle during PCI for CTO, given its close association with the difficulty of retrograde wiring and overall procedural complexity.•A retrograde balloon-assisted screening approach may serve as an effective bailout strategy to minimize guidewire prolapse and enable more stable and controlled retrograde guidewire manipulation in anatomically challenging steep CTO exit angle.

A retrograde balloon-assisted screening approach may serve as an effective bailout strategy to minimize guidewire prolapse and enable more stable and controlled retrograde guidewire manipulation in anatomically challenging steep CTO exit angle.

Herein, we report a case of a right coronary artery (RCA) CTO in which retrograde wiring via a septal collateral channel was initially unsuccessful because of an extremely steep exit angle. The use of over-the-wire (OTW) balloon-assisted screening, achieved by inflating the balloon just distal to the CTO exit bifurcation, effectively prevented guidewire prolapse. This approach enabled successful retrograde wiring followed by reverse controlled antegrade and retrograde tracking (CART), resulting in successful recanalization of the RCA CTO.

Case Summary

A 70-year-old man was admitted to our hospital for the treatment of decompensated heart failure. Transthoracic echocardiography on admission revealed regional wall motion abnormality of the inferior wall. After improvement of heart failure with optimal medical therapy, coronary angiography was performed to evaluate underlying ischemic heart disease. Coronary angiography demonstrated a CTO of the RCA. Based on myocardial scintigraphy findings demonstrating viable myocardium in the inferior territory, PCI for the RCA CTO was planned.

Procedural Steps

Simultaneous dual coronary angiography clearly demonstrated the CTO segment (Figure 1A, Video 1). Well-developed septal collateral channels suitable for the retrograde approach were identified (Figure 1B), and a primary retrograde approach was selected. The epicardial collateral channels were deemed too small to be used. Because the CTO exit involved a true bifurcation, a primary retrograde approach was considered reasonable to avoid side branch loss. Although the septal collateral channel appeared favorable and easily crossable, a characteristic anatomical feature of this case was the extremely steep angle from the CTO exit toward the main vessel, which was anticipated to make retrograde wiring technically challenging. The septal collateral channel was successfully crossed using a Suoh 03 guidewire (Asahi Intecc), and a Corsair Pro XS microcatheter (Asahi Intecc) was advanced to the retrograde CTO exit easily. Antegrade preparation was subsequently initiated, given that this was the first attempt and the CTO lesion showed no severe calcification. Antegrade guidewire manipulation was attempted using a Miracle Neo 3 guidewire (Asahi Intecc); however, the guidewire advanced only partially and failed to reach the distal CTO segment. Therefore, the strategy was switched to a retrograde approach, as the course of the CTO vessel was unclear and stepping up the antegrade guidewire was considered to carry a high risk of perforation.Figure 1RCA Chronic Total Occlusion(A) Simultaneous coronary angiography in the left anterior oblique cranial view demonstrating the CTO segment. Several retrograde guidewires, including Gladius EX (Asahi Intecc) and Gaia Next 1 (GN1) (Asahi Intecc), were attempted; however, none were able to penetrate the CTO segment. Because of an extremely steep retrograde exit angle (Figure 1A, Video 1), the guidewires repeatedly prolapsed toward the posterolateral branch (PL) (Figure 2A, Video 2). To overcome this challenging situation, we conceived the use of an OTW balloon-assisted screening technique. First, the septal collateral channel was carefully dilated with a 1.2 × 6-mm MINI TREK OTW balloon (Abbott Vascular) at nominal pressure (8 atm). Next, through the dilated septal channel, a 2.0 × 12 mm MINI TREK OTW balloon (Abbott Vascular) and a Zizai microcatheter (Terumo) were delivered. Subsequently, the OTW balloon was then positioned at the entrance of the PL branch, just distal to the CTO exit bifurcation, and inflated to function as a balloon screen (Figure 2C, Video 4). This maneuver effectively suppressed guidewire prolapse and improved retrograde guidewire maneuverability, as illustrated in Figure 2D. Under balloon screening, a GN1 guidewire was successfully advanced into the CTO segment. This step was the key technical maneuver in this case.

Following successful retrograde guidewire advancement, the retrograde guidewire served as a good landmark for antegrade wiring, which allowed more aggressive advancement of the antegrade guidewire. With this reference as a landmark, the antegrade guidewire initially used (Miracle Neo 3) was successfully advanced to an appropriate position for reverse CART. A reverse CART was then performed (Figure 3A, Video 5), during which the retrograde guidewire was stepped up to a Gaia Next 2 (Asahi Intecc), followed by stent implantation, resulting in successful recanalization of the RCA CTO (Figure 3B, Video 6). The procedure was completed without complications, with no injury to the septal collateral channel, and the patient remained clinically stable with a successful procedural outcome.Figure 3Percutaneous Coronary Intervention(A) Reverse controlled antegrade and retrograde tracking was performed. Discussion

Although balloon delivery from the retrograde side has been well described for the original CART technique, the original CART has rarely been performed recently, as the reverse CART approach has become the mainstream.4,5 To the best of our knowledge, there have been no reports describing the simultaneous introduction of a balloon and a microcatheter from the retrograde approach to prevent guidewire prolapse. In the present case, the septal collateral channel was well developed, which allowed simultaneous delivery of both the balloon and microcatheter through the channel. However, to facilitate this technique, predilation of the septal collateral channel with a small-diameter balloon may be necessary. Because there is a potential risk of collateral channel injury associated with balloon dilation, the technique may not be suitable for epicardial collateral channels. Regarding balloon size selection for the balloon screen, choosing a balloon according to the vessel diameter at the CTO exit may help sufficiently prevent guidewire prolapse. In the present case, a 2.0-mm OTW balloon was selected with reference to the coronary angiographic findings of the PL branch vessel diameter. Although it is important to choose a balloon size sufficient to prevent guidewire prolapse, selecting a slightly undersized balloon (approximately a quarter size smaller) may be safer to reduce the risk of vessel injury. In this case, positioning an OTW balloon retrogradely at the CTO exit successfully created a mechanical barrier that suppressed guidewire prolapse and enabled successful retrograde wiring into the CTO segment. Balloon positioning just distal to the CTO exit bifurcation is important to prevent guidewire prolapse, and we consider this to be one of the key technical tips of the present technique.

The concept of balloon-assisted screening itself has been reported in antegrade CTO-PCI.2 For example, in LAD ostial CTO lesions, balloon inflation at the ostium of a large side branch, such as a high lateral branch or the left circumflex artery, is often used to prevent guidewire prolapse and facilitate guidewire advancement into the LAD ostial CTO segment.2,6 The present case represents an adaptation of this established antegrade technique to the retrograde approach. However, careful attention is required to ensure that ischemia of the involved branch does not occur during balloon screening.

Several precautions should be emphasized. In most cases, septal branch dilation with a small balloon is likely required to facilitate delivery of both the balloon for screening and the microcatheter. Although septal channel injury is usually minor and rarely requires intervention, its presence should be carefully assessed. In cases in which septal channel injury is severe enough to compromise hemodynamics, coil embolization may be required, and operators should be well-versed in this technique.7 Therefore, careful evaluation and selection of the septal collateral channel are essential when considering this strategy. The limitation of this technique is that balloon dilatation of the collateral channel is often required, which may limit its applicability to epicardial collateral channels.

Conclusions

In cases with a steep CTO exit angle, retrograde delivery of a balloon for balloon-assisted screening may be an effective strategy to prevent guidewire prolapse and facilitate safer wiring.
