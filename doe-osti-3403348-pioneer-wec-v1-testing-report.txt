ABSTRACT
The “Pioneer WEC” project is tasked with developing a wave energy converter (WEC) to pro-
vide additional electrical power to the CeNtral Surface Mooring (CNSM) within the NSF-funded
Ocean Observatories Initiative (OOI) Pioneer Array. This report summarizes the design, testing,
and deployment of the “Pioneer WEC v1 prototype.” Data from the Pioneer WEC v1 ocean de-
ployment is available online at https://mhkdr.openei.org/ and https://www.osti.gov/
dataexplorer/.

1. INTRODUCTION

The NSF-funded Ocean Observatories Initiative (OOI) [14] Pioneer Array is a set of observation
platforms and sensors currently located at the Southern Mid-Atlantic Bight (MAB), off the coast
of Cape Hatteras, NC [15, 6]. The Pioneer Array MAB deployment provides a platform for col-
lecting data on various scientific topics, including cross-shelf exchange dynamics, Gulf Stream
influences, biochemical cycling, ecosystem responses, and the effects of extreme events like hurri-
canes and freshwater outflows. It also presents opportunities to enhance understanding of hurricane
development and prediction.
Of particular interest to this study, the array contains a number of Coastal Surface Moorings
(CSMs) [13]. Figure 1-1 shows a series of photographs of the CSM system. The CSMs each use
two Superwind SW350 wind turbines (350 W each) and four Kyocera KD-140 solar panels (140 W
each) to provide power for sensing, computation, and telemetry. Energy is stored onboard the buoy
in a bank of absorbent glass mat (AGM) batteries. While the system generally performs to design,
shortages of energy can, at times, require operators to reduce consumption by reducing telemetry,
measurement duty cycling, etc.
OOI provides two sites from which data can be downloaded:
 • OOI Data Explorer: https://dataexplorer.oceanobservatories.org
 • OOI Data Portal (legacy): https://ooinet.oceanobservatories.org
Note that much of the data of interest for this study is considered “engineering” data, which is
generally not of high interest to the oceanographic science community. This engineering data tends
to be hosted on the OOI Data Portal site more than the OOI Data Explorer site.
Energy shortages arise primarily due to lulls in wind speeds and/or solar irradiance as well as
damage to equipment. A scoping study [2] found that just 10-100 W of additional consistent power
generation could potentially resolve these energy shortage challenges. Based on this, a concept
design study was performed [2] and a novel “pitch resonator” wave energy converter (WEC) design
concept was selected for further investigation. The high-level project development timeline (both
past and future) are shown in Table 1-1; reports and papers capturing the work of these project
phases are also listed.
Because one of the goals of this study was to demonstrate marine energy’s potential to power
oceanographic equipment and to serve as a proof of concept, the Pioneer WEC v1 prototype is not
an optimized device. Using the lessons learned from the first deployment, future designs of the
Pioneer WEC will be developed in the coming years, and freely available information and data will
be released to facilitate future development of similar concepts by the marine energy and ocean
observing innovator community. In summary, the Pioneer WEC v1 deployment is intended to:

 2. SYSTEM DESIGN

 The Pioneer WEC v1 prototype was designed based on modeling and empirical testing with the v0 prototype [3]. The v0 prototype was a
 flywheel resonator, with configurable mass distribution. It included the flywheel, magnetic spring, belt drive transmission, and generator.
 Designed for empirical testing of the pitch resonating concept, the v0 prototype was designed for testing configuration modifications and
 basic control but not for compactness or robustness for deployment. A summary of key reference values for v1 is provided in Table 2-1.
 Details of the mechanical system design are provided in Section 2.1; the software and electrical system design details are presented in
 Section 2.2. Additional detailed schematics and design documents are provided in Appendix D and Appendix E.

2.1. Mechanical system design

An illustration of the fundamental mechanical v1 pitch resonator system is shown in Figure 2-2.
An inverted pendulum is supported by a torsional magnetic spring. As the inverted pendulum is
excited by the pitching motion of the buoy, it rocks back and forth, turning a motor/generator and
thus producing electricity. In the v1 prototype, the pendulum and motor/generator are coupled via
a synchronous belt and planetary gearbox. The detailed mechanical design is shown in Figure 2-3.

2.2. Electrical/software system design

The Pioneer WEC v1 prototype uses a TI C2000 LAUNCHXL-F28069M microcontroller to run
time-critical tasks such as motor control and data acquisition. Supervisory control and data logging
is handled by a BeagleBone Black (BBB) single board Linux computer. Figure 2-5 shows a
high-level system electrical diagram. Detailed schematics of the electrical system can be found in
Appendix D. Photographs of the electrical box is shown in Figure 2-6.
The final electrical output of the WEC is provided via the Victron Energy (VE) Buck Boost (BB)
converter. The principal idea behind choosing this part was to use a commercially available and
reliable component as the final interface to the wider WHOI system. The VE Buck Boost charge
controller allows the battery voltage set-point to be freely set. When selecting this set-point, it must
be kept in mind that the WHOI battery bank also interfaces to two other charge controllers: the
solar and wind controllers.
When operating multiple charge controllers on a single battery bank, it is common practice to
provide a small voltage gap between their set-points. This is required to avoid the charge controllers
from interfering with one another. The solar and wind charge controllers are set to approximately
27 V. System integration testing at WHOI in February 2025 included experiments with two different
set-points for the VE Buck Boost: (a) 26.5 V and (b) 26.0 V. Both set-points led to very similar
system behavior, and it was concluded that 26.5 V should be used for the first ocean deployment (a
lower set-point may lead to less power transfer between the WEC system and the battery). Future
testing should explore whether this 26.5 V set-point could be raised even further.

2.2.1. Data and communications

Data and communications within the Pioneer WEC v1 prototype and with other related systems
are illustrated in Figure 2-7. Solid lines in Figure 2-7 show the base system and deployment
configuration while dashed lines show communication channels only present during bench testing.
In its deployment configuration, the WEC produces the following fundamental data streams:

 • Summary data, which is recorded at 1 Hz on the CNSM DCL (Data Concentrator Logger);
 this data is decimated to 0.1 Hz (every 10th line taken) and telemetered to shore on a nightly
 basis. The processing steps used to produce this summary data is shown in Figure C-1.
 Moving averages and root-mean-squares are taken with a window of 1 s.
 • Fault flags, as defined by the logic in Table C-2, are evaluated/set by the c2000 at 5 Hz and
 transmitted to the CNSM DCL at 1 Hz as part of the summary data.
 • High-resolution data sampled at 25 Hz is transmitted from the c2000 to the BBB for storage.
 This data is not readily available during the deployment and can only be accessed by Wi-Fi
 when a ship is in proximity or via TCP/IP over the iridium satellite connection on an ad hoc
 basis subject to the satellite connection plan’s data limits. A description of the fields in this
 high-resolution data is provided in Table C-1.
 • Aggregated data is produced on the BBB at 1Hz to provide a human readable text file.
 For the purpose of this aggregated data, a set of 25 samples is taken, and a field-specific
 operation such as RMS, mean, min, max, first or last sample is taken. For example, power
 values are generally taken as mean, whereas dynamic quantities such as position or velocity
 are aggregated using the RMS. An update rate of 1 Hz allows monitoring of the aggregated
 data file via a live terminal session (25 Hz data would overload a terminal screen).

2.2.2. WEC control

For the v1 prototype’s deployment, a simple resistive damping control was used. While other
control approaches may provide some benefit, based on the goals for the v1 deployment (see
Section 1), a conservative approach was taken in this case. Additionally, modeling of the system
showed that the benefit from optimal control of this system would have been relatively limited by
the physical system design of the v1 prototype.
Feedback control is executed on the c2000 microcontroller. Based on experimental testing and
numerical modeling, the optimal value for proportional damping gain for the feedback controller
was found to be approximately 0.130 As/rad (Ampere of motor current as a function of motor shaft
speed). Note that per the gear ratio between the motor and pendulum (due to the combined effect

2.2.3. State machine

A state machine controls the operation of the c2000. The Simulink Stateflow model for the c2000
state machine is shown in a diagram in Figure C-2. Additionally, to conserve energy, the motor
controller bridge is shut off when the average absolute motor shaft speed1 is less than 50 RPM; the
bridge is re-enabled when the average shaft speed increases above 90 RPM.
The state machine consists of six primary states: Startup, Init, Autonomous Control, Autonomous
Safe Control, External Damping Control, and External Current Control. Startup simply waits a
short duration to allow boot up to complete after power on. Init waits for the encoder, motor driver,
and bus voltage to be in acceptable ranges before allowing transition to one of the control states. It
also ensures there are no present faults and waits until the velocity is low such that the control output
will be small after the transition, avoiding a large jerk of the pendulum. Autonomous Control uses
the default damping gain for the WEC. Autonomous Safe Control uses a different damping gain
that is less power optimal, but produces less average stress on the hardware. Non-critical faults can
trigger transition to this Safe Control mode. In External Damping Control, the target damping gain
is specified by the BBB. In External Current Control, the BBB specifies the target motor current
timeseries.
The fault handler function detects and reports various fault flags described in Table C-2. These
faults can trigger either transitions to Autonomous Safe Control or a return to Init. The BBB
watchdog signal must be present for transitions into and continued operation in external control
states.

3.6. Integration burn-in

Integration of the WEC into the CSM was conducted September 9-23, 2025. During this period, the
housing was finalized and a fit-check with the WEC was performed (see Figure 2-8). Additionally,
the WEC was connected to the CSM for an extended “burn-in” functionality test (see Figure 3-15).
During the burn-in test, the WEC was connected to the CSM DCL using the blue 13-pin production
“data cable” (Ethernet + 12 V + RS232 Rx/Tx) and 2 pin production “export cable,” both 15 ft long.
The WEC was powered using 12 V from the DCL, cycled once daily at 23:59 UTC. During certain
periods of testing (as noted in the test log), the WEC was connected to a Sandia bench top power
supply to create a DC bus. Final integration commissioning tests included:

 • Wavelet experiments in torque control

 47

 Figure 3-15. WEC and CSM equipment during commissioning “burn-in.”

 • Multisine experiments in torque control
 • Ramp experiments in torque control
 • Damping control, with variations in damping
The combination of the above experiments covers the bulk of scenarios expected during ocean
deployment.
Wavelets were run on the motor/generator, as shown in Figure 3-16. Note the torque tracking
deficiency that occurs at approximately 20:27:33 is due to back-EMF voltage levels created by high
motor shaft speeds is expected. The key point of the wavelet experiments was to excite the WEC
in such a way that generates relatively large motions for a limited current (power) input. During
the ocean deployment, this type of forced pendulum excitation can make efficient use of the energy
stored in the DC bus capacitors, allowing in-situ ocean system identification experiments.
Without any external exciting platform (such as LAMP) being available during the system commis-
sioning, power export was tested as follows. First, the relay on the OOI Power System Controller
(PSC) was closed, connecting the WEC system to the WHOI batteries. Next, an external DC power
supply was connected to the WEC DC bus. The voltage on the DC bus was then raised via the DC
power supply to achieve 27.0 V, at which point the C2000 firmware triggers a discharge via the VE
Buck Boost battery charger. The external DC power supply was disconnected as this point. The
discharge cycle then continues until the DC bus reaches 23.0 V. An example experiment showing
this sequence is illustrated in figure 3-17.

4.2. Initial data analysis

While a further analysis will be performed on the full dataset produced during the course of the
six month deployment of the Pioneer WEC v1 prototype, an initial basic analysis is presented
here to illustrate fundamental functionality. This analysis makes use of decimated data (0.1 Hz)
made available each night and full-resolution data (25 Hz) that was downloaded directly from the
CNSM/WEC during periods when the R/V Armstrong was within Wi-Fi range.
Once in the water, the WEC immediately began producing power. Figure 4-4 shows a record of the
DC bus power produced by the WEC during and after the deployment. A full time-history (using
daily averages) for the ocean conditions and the WEC, solar, and wind system power production is
shown in Figure 4-5. Note that the CNSM DC bus potential is also shown in Figure 4-5 and never
falls below 23 V and rarely falls below 25 V during the deployment. From Figure 4-5, we can also
see that the WEC power output is relatively insensitive to the ocean conditions.
The power generation system statistics for the WEC, solar, and wind systems are summarized in
Table 4-1. The solar system outputs a mean of 25 W, but has a median output of 0 W due to the
fact that the solar system is only active during daylight hours. The wind system outputs a mean of
67.7 W, which is an order of magnitude higher than the mean output of the WEC (8.6 W). The WEC
power output is much more consistent than the wind and solar systems, as shown by the coefficient
of variation (CV) in Table 4-1.

5. CONCLUSIONS

The Pioneer WEC v1 prototype has shown initial success during its deployment. The prototype
operates as expected in a real ocean environment and has shown no major mechanical, electrical, or
software faults to-date. The Pioneer WEC v1 prototype produces a mean of 8.6 W of power, which
is in line with the expected 8-10 W based on numerical modeling and simulation of the system.
Based on this, development of the Pioneer WEC v2 prototype is currently underway.
