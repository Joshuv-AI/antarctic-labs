Abstract
Risk assessment instruments, also known as “risk scores,” are widely used in high-stakes decision-making settings such as medicine and the criminal justice system. A risk score predicts the likelihood of an undesired outcome if no intervention is made. Thus, a sufficiently high score is often interpreted as a recommendation to intervene. However, risk scores fail to account for what would happen if a decision-maker does intervene. This failure is problematic because effective decision making requires consideration of both (or multiple) potential outcomes. We propose “triage scores,” which are based on additive counterfactual utilities and include risk scores as a special case. Unlike risk scores, triage scores can incorporate counterfactual outcomes under alternative decisions, enabling decision makers to incorporate a wide range of ethical and practical factors. We illustrate the use of triage scores with an application to our own randomized controlled trial evaluating a pretrial risk score. Our analysis demonstrates that triage scores are able to capture rich utility structures and yield substantively distinct results regarding policy evaluation and learning.



1
Introduction

Today, data-driven algorithms are deeply embedded in decision-making
systems. In high-stakes settings such as medicine and the criminal
justice system, human decision makers frequently rely on
recommendations produced by risk assessment instruments, commonly
referred to as “risk scores”
(
36
;
8
)
.
These scores typically classify the
predicted probability of an undesirable outcome (e.g., illness or
rearrest) under a baseline decision of no intervention. In practice,
individuals with sufficiently high scores are often flagged for
intervention, such as admission to intensive care or the imposition of
cash bail. This workflow implicitly treats prediction under no
intervention as a proxy for prescription. However, identifying
individuals who are “high risk” in the absence of intervention
provides no information about how those same individuals would respond
if an intervention were applied.

This one-sided focus of risk scores is fundamentally
misaligned with the objective of maximizing overall welfare in such
decision-making systems. Effective decision making requires evaluating
what would happen under alternative choices, rather than focusing
solely on outcomes under a single baseline of no intervention. In
pretrial settings, for example, a central question is not simply the
probability that a defendant will be rearrested if assigned no cash bail, but how that probability would change under cash versus no cash (perhaps with differing monitoring and support conditions). In addition, decision makers must weigh the societal
costs of recidivism together with the financial, ethical, and other
costs associated with cash bail and pretrial detention under each
possible course of action. Because risk scores are indexed to
a single baseline decision, they cannot distinguish between
individuals for whom an intervention would meaningfully change
outcomes and those for whom it would not. This failure to consider
counterfactual outcomes also limits our ability to reason about
ethical and practical tradeoffs, such as the regret associated with
unnecessarily detaining an individual who would not have been
rearrested if released.

In this paper, we propose a framework based on “triage scores,” a class of
counterfactual
risk assessment instruments designed to align more closely decision making with the objective of maximizing expected
utility.
Although we refer in this paper to “triage scores,” our focus here is on the development of a counterfactual and evaluative framework for optimizing decision making that incorporates risk from alternative decisions; we do not here actually develop a triage score for any particular setting nor discuss how to do so.
Rather than summarizing risk under a single baseline
potential outcome, triage scores are constructed from counterfactual
utilities that depend on the joint distribution of potential outcomes
under alternative decisions. Our formulation builds on recent advances
in statistical decision theory with counterfactual utilities

(
10
;
6
;
5
;
30
;
9
;
26
, e.g.,)
. By explicitly modeling both the realized
outcome under the chosen decision and the counterfactual outcomes
under alternative decisions, triage scores allow utilities to encode
considerations such as regret from unnecessary detention or failure to
prevent a crime. Under an additive counterfactual utility structure
and the standard assumption of unconfoundedness, we point identify the
expected utility of a decision-making system, enabling systematic
evaluation of existing policies as well as learning of new,
optimal decision rules.

Conceptually, triage scores generalize standard risk scores. In the
simplest binary setting with two decisions (e.g., no cash bail versus cash bail) and a binary outcome (e.g., at least one pretrial rearrest versus no arrest),
conventional risk scores depend solely on the baseline potential
outcome. In contrast, triage scores operate at the level of principal
strata defined by the joint potential outcomes
(
17
)

and assign a utility to each possible decision within a stratum. This
framework enables decision makers to distinguish, for example, among
defendants who would not be rearrested under either no cash bail or
cash bail (
safe
), those for whom cash bail would be
counterproductive (
backlash
), those for whom cash bail would
prevent crime (
preventable
), and those likely to reoffend
regardless of the decision (
hopeless
).

We develop a general statistical framework for evaluating and learning from triage scores using data generated by human decision makers with or
without algorithmic recommendations. We apply this framework to assess
and improve the Public Safety Assessment (PSA), a pretrial risk assessment instrument designed to inform initial release/bail decisions, using data from our own randomized controlled trial

(see
20
, for a related RCT in Wisconsin)
. In
this RCT, algorithmic recommendations were randomly assigned across arrested individuals under a single-blind design, such that defendants were unaware
of their assignment. We also observe the full set of information
available to judges at the time of decision, rendering plausible the assumption that our model of judicial decisions is unconfounded.

Under these assumptions, we establish identification results for the
expected counterfactual utility of three decision-making systems:
human-alone, human-with-AI, and AI-alone. We construct
semiparametric estimators based on augmented inverse probability
weighting to estimate these utilities, adjusting for the full set of
information available to judges at the time of decision. In
particular, we incorporate GenAI-powered inference (GPI) to account
for unstructured confounding information contained in probable-cause
affidavits
(
22
)
. Finally, we show how to estimate an
optimal decision rule under the triage score utility framework by
solving an empirical utility maximization problem over a class of
feasible decision policies.

A short note regarding terminology: for simplicity and brevity, we sometimes refer to a judge’s pretrial decision as ‘release’ or ‘release own recognizance (ROR)’. More accurate phrasing would be ‘no cash bail required for release in this judicial proceeding.’ We clarify this distinction because a judge’s decision not to assign cash bail after a particular arrest does not always mean that the defendant will achieve release. Instead, a defendant might remain incarcerated because the present arrest violated terms of pretrial release on a different set of charges, or because the U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement may want to begin deportation proceedings, or because the defendant requires detoxing or a psychiatric evaluation. Nevertheless, ‘no cash bail required for release in this judicial proceeding’ is cumbersome, so at the risk of some distortion we use the shorthand ‘release’ for brevity.
The key point is that ‘release’ in this setting refers to the judge’s decision, not necessarily what the defendant experiences.

(

2
Empirical Application

We now introduce an empirical application that motivates the proposed
methodology. Our application is based on our own RCT in Utah, which was designed to evaluate the
value of a prominent risk score used in criminal justice system.
Below, we briefly explain the design of this RCT and present a basic
descriptive analysis of data. Finally, we discuss questions based on
this RCT that motivate the development of the triage score utility framework.

2.1
A Randomized Controlled Trial in Utah

This field RCT was part of a series of experiments designed to assess
the effect of providing a risk score called the PSA to judges making bail and pretrial release condition
decisions shortly after arrest.
All sites used similar RCT designs. In Utah, which provided the data for our application here, judges either did or did not receive the PSA when making the first post-arrest decision regarding release, bail, and monitoring conditions. Randomization was by defendant, meaning a defendant remained in either a judge-receives-PSA condition or a judge-does-not-receive-PSA condition for all their arrests.
Primary outcomes included new criminal activity (NCA), denoting a

0
–
1
variable for whether the defendant was arrested or cited for
an incarceration-eligible offense; new violent criminal activity
(NVCA), the same as NCA but for only violent offenses; and failure to
appear (FTA), denoting a
0
–
1
variable for whether the court
issued a bench warrant stemming from a defendant’s missing a required
court date. By definition, NCA, NVCA, and FTA could not occur during
time periods on which the defendant was incarcerated.

The Utah RCT took place in four counties, Davis County, Utah County,
Weber County, and Morgan County. As is true of all field operations,
the Utah sites had its own esoteric features.
First, Utah’s automated systems could produce the PSA only for arrestees who did not have records from other states that the Utah software could not machine read (and translate into PSA inputs), meaning that an unknown but not huge fraction of arrestees were not included in the study population.
Second, Utah’s
pretrial system required a judge to make the first consequential
decision regarding pretrial release and bail, not at a live hearing,
but rather upon a review of paper files only and concurrent with the
judge’s determination of whether probable cause existed for the
arrest. Because there was no live hearing, there were no statements or
arguments from the defendant, from defense counsel, from the
prosecution, or from anyone else. The judge made all decisions based
on files that consisted of the charges, the law enforcement probable
cause (PC) affidavit, whatever online criminal history search the
judge decided to conduct, and the PSA (if the case was randomized to
the PSA-present condition).

The law enforcement PC affidavit was a sworn statement, almost always
from the arresting officer, describing in a paragraph or two the
circumstances of the arrest. The PC affidavit was supposed to
particularize the reasons why the officer believed that the defendant
had committed a criminal offense. Constitutional law (Gerstein
v. Pugh, 420 U.S. 103 (1975)) required a judicial officer to conduct
an independent review shortly after arrest of a law enforcement
officer’s warrantless decision to arrest. Utah judges reviewed the
materials listed above by logging into an online system and indicating
their decisions electronically.

In cooperation with the Utah judiciary, we obtained the PC affidavits
as well as the other materials that judges observed when making their
decisions. Thus, we were able to observe everything that the judge
observed for each decision, rendering plausible the assumption,
described below, of unconfounded decisions conditional on observed
covariate information.

2.2
Public Safety Assessment Instrument (PSA)

The PSA is a set of three integer scores categorizing a defendant’s
risk of FTA, NCA, and NVCA. The FTA and NCA scores run from
1
to

6
, while the NVCA metric takes the form of a
0
–
1
flag. Higher
numbers corresponded to higher risk (according to the PSA). Eight
criminal history factors plus age serve as the PSA’s inputs (see

https://www.advancingpretrial.org/about-the-psa/
). The PSA
scores, which are the same for all jurisdictions, serve as inputs to a
jurisdiction-specific Decision Making Framework (DMF), which
incorporates local circumstances and values to transform the scores
into a recommendation for the judge regarding release, bail, and
monitoring conditions. The output of the PSA-DMF System (we refer in
this paper to the “PSA” for brevity) takes the form of a paper
printout or a computer file that reports the PSA scores, the values of
the nine inputs, the criminal history events that gave rise to those
values, and the DMF recommendation. A philanthropic foundation called
Arnold Ventures funded scientists to construct the PSA, and as of this
writing, dozens of jurisdictions across the United States provide it
to judges to guide initial release decisions.

2.4
Statistical Decision Theory with Counterfactual Utilities

We formalize the evaluation and potential improvement of PSA by
applying the statistical decision theory based on counterfactual
utilities
(
38
;
26
)
. We first
consider the
standard
utilities by specifying a utility for each
decision and its consequence without considering counterfactual
outcomes. In our application, a judge chooses one among the following
four alternative decisions: release on own recognizance (
ror
), low
cash bail (
lcb
), high cash bail (
hcb
), and remand (
rem
).
‘Remand’ here means that the judge decides that the defendant must remain incarcerated without the opportunity to post bail; in Utah,
rem
decisions were uncommon but not so uncommon that we could ignore them.
Our outcome variable of interest is binary, indicating whether or not a
defendant is rearrested for a new criminal activity upon release
(
nca
).
The symbol
\neg
denotes negation; for example,
\neg\textsc{nca}

denotes no
nca
.
Note that an arrestee may not be released immediately due to a

rem
decision or failure to pay cash bail. However, release may
subsequently occur if the initial decision is modified or overturned
at a later court hearing.

This setup implies that we have two possible outcomes under each of
four decisions, requiring the specification of eight utilities. Here,
we provide an example of standard utilities.

Example 1

(Additive standard utilities)
.

We
may specify the utility for the
rem
decision followed by the

nca
outcome as an additive function of two costs, which
negatively contribute to the utility:

\texttt{Utility}(\textsc{rem},\textsc{nca})\ =\ -(\texttt{Cost}_{\textsc{rem}}+\texttt{Cost}_{\textsc{nca}}),

where
\texttt{Cost}_{\textsc{rem}}\geq 0
is the cost associated with the

rem
decision, which may include the cost of detention on both the
public and the defendant, and
\texttt{Cost}_{\textsc{nca}}\geq 0
is the
cost associated with the
nca
outcome, which may include the
societal cost of new criminal activity. One can similarly define the
other seven utilities.

In this paper, we apply the
counterfactual
statistical decision
theory
(
10
;
6
;
30
;
5
;
9
;
26
, e.g.,)
. This framework generalizes the standard
statistical decision theory by incorporating counterfactual outcomes
under alternative decisions when specifying each utility. Thus, the
counterfactual decision theory allows for the notion of “regret” by
comparing the outcome under each decision with counterfactual outcomes
under alternative decisions

(
4
;
28
, e.g.,)
. For example, when
specifying a utility for the
rem
decision, we consider the
counterfactual outcomes under different decisions (e.g.,
ror

decision) as well as the outcome that would be realized under the

rem
decision.

Thus, in our application, while there is only one standard utility
that can be specified for each of the eight decision-outcome pairs,
there are a total of 64
(=2^{6})
counterfactual utilities that can
possibly be specified if one wishes to place no restriction. However,
substantial simplification is required to facilitate interpretation
and practical use. In this paper, we adopt the additive
counterfactual utility framework of
26
, in
which the utility for each decision-outcome pair equals the sum of the
standard utility and separate counterfactual utilities under
alternative decisions.

Example 2

(Additive counterfactual utilities)
.

Consider the utility for the
rem
decision followed by the
nca

outcome. Suppose that the counterfactual outcomes for all three
alternative decisions are identical and are equal to the absence of

nca
event. Then, an additive counterfactual utility is given by,

\texttt{Utility}(\textsc{rem},\textsc{nca})+\widetilde{\texttt{Utility}}(\textsc{ror},\neg\textsc{nca})+\widetilde{\texttt{Utility}}(\textsc{lcb},\neg\textsc{nca})+\widetilde{\texttt{Utility}}(\textsc{hcb},\neg\textsc{nca}),

where the first term is the standard utility whose example is given
in Example
1
, and the other three terms are
counterfactual utilities associated with alternative decisions and
counterfactual outcomes under those decisions.

To operationalize each counterfactual utility, we may use the notion
of regret, which weighs a cost of an alternative decision and another
cost of the counterfactual outcome that would have resulted under this
alternative decision.

\displaystyle\widetilde{\texttt{Utility}}(\textsc{ror},\neg\textsc{nca})

\displaystyle=-\texttt{Regret}^{\textsc{ror}}_{\neg\textsc{nca}}\times(\texttt{Cost}_{\textsc{ror}}+\texttt{Cost}_{\neg\textsc{nca}})

\displaystyle\widetilde{\texttt{Utility}}(\textsc{lcb},\neg\textsc{nca})

\displaystyle=-\textrm{Regret}^{\textsc{lcb}}_{\neg\textsc{nca}}\times(\texttt{Cost}_{\textsc{lcb}}+\texttt{Cost}_{\neg\textsc{nca}})

\displaystyle\widetilde{\texttt{Utility}}(\textsc{hcb},\neg\textsc{nca})

\displaystyle=-\textrm{Regret}^{\textsc{hcb}}_{\neg\textsc{nca}}\times(\texttt{Cost}_{\textsc{hcb}}+\texttt{Cost}_{\neg\textsc{nca}})

where

\texttt{Regret}^{\textsc{ror}}_{\neg\textsc{nca}},\textrm{Regret}^{\textsc{lcb}}_{\neg\textsc{nca}},\textrm{Regret}^{\textsc{hcb}}_{\neg\textsc{nca}}\in[0,1]
are the weights (relative
to the standard utility) given to each counterfactual utility. Here,
a greater regret implies a larger influence of counterfactual outcome
under an alternative decision and its associated counterfactual
outcome. For example, if a judge could have released an arrestee and
achieved the best outcome (no
nca
), then the value of regret, i.e.,

\texttt{Regret}^{\textsc{ror}}_{\neg\textsc{nca}}
, may be greater.

Once the utilities are fully specified, we can statistically evaluate
a different decision-making system by estimating its expected
utilities, which is the average utility across all arrestees in a
target population. The expected utilities enable us to compare the
empirical performance of different decision-making systems. In our
application, such a system includes one in which a judge makes the
decision without help of PSA and the other in which a judge is
provided with PSA. Beyond statistical evaluation, we can also learn
an optimal decision-making rule from the observed data by finding a
decision rule that maximizes the expected utility. In the next two
sections, we will develop these methods. In
Section
5
, we will revisit this RCT and apply our methodology to evaluate the judge’s decision with or without PSA.
We will also derive an optimal decision rule under the triage score utility framework.

3
Triage Score

In this section, we formally develop the triage score framework. Unlike risk
scores, this new counterfactual risk assessment instrument leverages
the full set of potential outcomes. To develop intuition, we begin by
introducing the simplest case with binary decisions and outcomes. We
then generalize our formulation to handle multi-valued decisions and
outcomes.

3.1
Binary Case

We first consider settings with a binary decision
D\in\{0,1\}

and a binary outcome
Y\in\{0,1\}
. In the context similar to that
of our application,
D=0
represents a decision to release an
arrestee on their own recognizance, while
D=1
represents a
decision to impose cash bail. The outcome
Y=1
indicates an
undesirable event, such as a rearrest for new criminal
activity. Lastly, let
Y(d)
denote the potential outcome under
decision
D=d
, for
d\in\{0,1\}
. For example,
Y(1)
represents
the outcome that would be realized if the decision were
D=1
. The
observed and potential outcomes are linked through the relation

Y=DY(1)+(1-D)Y(0)
, which reflects the standard consistency
assumption
(
32
)
. Relaxing this assumption, for instance by allowing for spillover effects, is beyond the scope of this paper.

We now formalize the proposed counterfactual risk assessment
instrument, which we call the “triage score,” within the framework
of statistical decision theory based on counterfactual utilities

(
26
)
. Our approach generalizes existing
counterfactual risk assessment instruments

(
10
;
5
, e.g.,)
by considering
the joint potential outcomes
(Y(0),Y(1))
rather than focusing
solely on the baseline potential outcome
Y(0)
.

In the case of binary decisions and outcomes, the joint potential
outcomes define four principal strata, corresponding to all possible
combinations of
(Y(0),Y(1))=(y_{0},y_{1})

(
17
)
. For
the purpose of exposition, we consider the context of our application
and refer to these four strata as follows:

•

Safe

(Y(0),Y(1))=(0,0)
: a defendant who would
not be rearrested for a new crime under either decision

•

Backlash

(Y(0),Y(1))=(0,1)
: a defendant who
would be rearrested only if cash bail is imposed

•

Preventable

(Y(0),Y(1))=(1,0)
: a defendant who
would be rearrested only if released on their own recognizance

•

Hopeless

(Y(0),Y(1))=(1,1)
: a defendant who would
be rearrested regardless of the decision

To construct the triage score, we assign a utility to each possible
decision within every principal stratum, resulting in a total of eight
utility parameters to specify. However, in this fully general
formulation, the corresponding expected utility is not identifiable
because we do not observe two potential outcomes at the same time. To
address this, we impose an additivity assumption that restricts
counterfactual utilities to be additive in the potential outcomes
without interaction terms.
26
show that this
additivity condition is both necessary and sufficient for the
identification of expected counterfactual utilities under the standard
assumption of unconfoundedness.

the unconfoundedness assumption with additive counterfactual

Once the expected utility is identified, we can evaluate any decision

decision rule that maximizes the expected utility within this
framework. Under additive utilities, this can be done without
identifying the full conditional distribution of principal strata.
Specifically, we estimate the decision rule that maximizes the
expected utility under the decision it recommends within a specified
class of decision rules.

3.3
General Case

We now extend the additive counterfactual utility framework to a more
general setting that allows for multi-valued (i.e., non-binary)
decisions and outcomes. Consider a setting in which the observed
decision
D
and generic decision
D^{\ast}
take on
K_{D}
categories,

D,D^{\ast}\in\mathcal{D}:=\{0,1,\ldots,K_{D}-1\}
, and the
outcome variable takes on
K_{Y}
categories,

Y\in\mathcal{Y}:=\{0,1,\ldots,K_{Y}-1\}
.

This generalization is important both in theory and practice because,
in the binary case, any additive counterfactual utility specification
can be equivalently represented by a standard utility formulation
(though the interpretation may differ). However, in the non-binary
case, the standard utility framework is not sufficiently expressive to
capture additive counterfactual utilities
(
26
)
.
In our empirical application, one could consider a multi-valued decision with

K_{D}=4
;
ror
(
D^{\ast}=0
),
lcb
(
D^{\ast}=1
),
hcb

(
D^{\ast}=2
), and
rem
(
D^{\ast}=3
). Similarly, if we distinguish
two types of rearrest, one for new criminal activity and the other for
new
violent
criminal activity (
nvca
), we have a multi-valued
outcome with
K_{Y}=3
; no rearrest (
Y=0
),
nca
but not
nvca

(
Y=1
), and
nvca
(
Y=2
).

We use
Y(d)
to denote the potential outcome under generic decision

D^{\ast}=d
, for
d\in\{0,1,\ldots,K_{D}-1\}
, and the observed
outcome is equal to
Y=Y(D)
where
D
is the actual decision. Under
this generalized setup, we can define the principal strata as the set
of all possible combinations of potential outcomes,

\bm{Y}=(Y(0),Y(1),\ldots,Y(K_{D}-1))
. We write

\bm{y}=(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{K_{D}-1})
, where

y_{d}\in\mathcal{Y}
for all
d\in\mathcal{D}
, to denote a realization of this
principal stratum. For
example,
(Y(0),Y(1),\ldots,Y(K_{D}-1))=(0,0,\ldots,0)

represents a case in which, regardless of the decision, the potential
outcome is always no new criminal activity.

Depending on the restrictions imposed on the utility function, the
framework may use either the full principal strata defined by the
joint potential outcomes or a coarsening of those strata based on the
baseline potential outcome alone. In this paper, we call the former
the triage score framework, whereas the latter is referred to as the risk score
framework. Under the triage score framework, utilities are assigned
based on both the full vector of potential outcomes and the decision.

Definition 1

(Triage and Risk Score Frameworks)
.

The triage score framework is a statistical decision-theoretic framework based on counterfactual utilities in which the utility of a decision may depend on the full vector of potential outcomes,

\bm{Y}=(Y(0),Y(1),\ldots,Y(K_{D}-1))
, or equivalently on the principal stratum defined by this vector. The risk score framework is a special case, in which utilities depend on the principal stratum only through the baseline potential outcome
Y(d_{0})
, where
d_{0}\in\mathcal{D}
denotes a baseline decision (e.g., release). That is, for each fixed decision
d
, all principal strata sharing the same value of
Y(d_{0})
receive the same utility.

A triage score is a function that summarizes the conditional distribution of the joint potential outcomes given covariates,

s^{\textsc{triage}}:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{Y}^{K_{D}}),

where
s^{\textsc{triage}}_{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})
denotes the predicted probability of principal stratum
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{K_{D}}
for units with covariates
\bm{x}
. The utility function assigns a utility to each possible decision within every principal stratum,

u:\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Y}^{K_{D}}\to\mathbb{R}.

Under the risk score framework, this utility function is restricted so that
u(d,\bm{y})=u(d,\bm{y}^{\prime})
whenever
y_{d_{0}}=y_{d_{0}}^{\prime}
.

A construction of triage scores requires a
total of
K_{Y}^{K_{D}}\times K_{D}
utility parameters as we need to specify a utility for each combination of principal stratum and decision. To identify
the expected utility under the unconfoundedness assumption, which we
formally introduce in the next section, we again consider the additive
counterfactual utilities defined below.

Definition 2

(Additive Counterfactual
Utility)
.

Additive counterfactual utility
function is defined as

u:\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Y}^{K_{D}}\to\mathbb{R}
where for
d\in\mathcal{D}
and
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{K_{D}}
,

u(d,\bm{y})=u_{y_{d}}^{d}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\
d^{\prime}\neq d\end{subarray}}^{K_{D}-1}\tilde{u}_{y_{d^{\prime}}}^{d^{\prime}}.

In words, an additive counterfactual utility function

u\in\mathcal{U}^{\textsc{Add}}
assigns a utility to each decision

d\in\mathcal{D}
within every principal stratum

\bm{y}=(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{K_{D}-1})
, where
y_{d}\in\mathcal{Y}
, and this
utility consists of two components: standard utility
u_{y_{d}}^{d}
and
counterfactual utility
\tilde{u}_{y_{d^{\prime}}}^{d^{\prime}}
. The term

u_{y_{d}}^{d}
is the portion of utility that is realized when the
decision
D^{\ast}=d
is made and the corresponding observed outcome
is

. In contrast,
\tilde{u}_{y_{d^{\prime}}}^{d^{\prime}}

represents the remaining portion of utility that is realized when the
decision
D^{\ast}=d
is made but the counterfactual outcome is

Y(d^{\prime})=y_{d^{\prime}}
under a given alternative decision

d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
and
d^{\prime}\neq d
.

This generalizes the additive counterfactual utility introduced in the
previous section for binary decision and binary outcome to settings
with multi-valued decisions and outcomes. It is possible to further
generalize this by defining the additive counterfactual utilities
conditional on pre-treatment covariates
\bm{X}=\bm{x}
as it is possible
to assign different utility values, depending on individual
characteristics, i.e.,
u:\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Y}^{K_{D}}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
.
To simplify notation and focus on the core idea, we
do not condition on covariates in the utility function throughout this
paper. Nevertheless, all the results presented below can be readily
extended to the cases in which utilities depend on the pre-treatment
covariates.

The additive counterfactual utility in
Definition
2
is a special case of a more general
formulation in
26
, in which we assume a zero
intercept for the utility function within each principal stratum. That
is, the utility for all possible interactions of potential outcomes
that do not depend on the realized decision is assumed to be zero. By
applying the same proof as in Corollary 2 of

26
, it can be shown that the additivity
assumption imposed on the counterfactual utility in
Definition
2
is both necessary and sufficient for
the point identification of the expected utility under the
unconfoundedness assumption. For completeness, we provide the
definition of expected additive counterfactual utility,

\overline{U}(u;D^{\ast})=\mathbb{E}[U(u;D^{\ast}\mid\bm{X})],

where the conditional expected utility of a decision
D^{\ast}
given
the pre-treatment covariates
\bm{X}
is defined as,

\displaystyle U(u;D^{\ast}\mid\bm{X})

\displaystyle:=\

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{y_{0}=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\ldots\sum_{y_{K_{D}-1}=0}^{K_{Y}-1}u(d,\bm{y})\mathbbm{1}\{D^{\ast}=d,Y(0)=y_{0},\ldots Y(K_{D}-1)=y_{K_{D}-1}\}\ \Bigl|\ \bm{X}\right]

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{y_{0}=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\ldots\sum_{y_{K_{D}-1}=0}^{K_{Y}-1}u(d,\bm{y})\Pr(D^{\ast}=d,Y(0)=y_{0},\ldots Y(K_{D}-1)=y_{K_{D}-1}\mid\bm{X}).

Under this additive utility structure, we can define the expected utility of a decision rule
D^{\ast}

under the triage score framework as
\overline{U}(u;D^{\ast})
where
u(d,\bm{y})=u_{y_{d}}^{d}+\sum_{d^{\prime}\neq d}\tilde{u}_{y_{d^{\prime}}}^{d^{\prime}}
is allowed to vary across
\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}

even when
y_{d_{0}}=y_{d_{0}}^{\prime}

\bm{y}=(y_{0},\ldots,y_{K_{D}-1})
and

for all

d\in\mathcal{D}
and all

\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}
with

Accordingly, an optimal decision rule under the triage score framework is defined as

\pi^{\textsc{triage}}\in\mathop{\rm argmax}\limits_{\pi\in\Pi}\overline{U}(u;D^{\pi}),

where
D^{\pi}=\pi(\bm{X})
denotes the decision induced by a policy
\pi
in the policy class
\Pi
. When the policy class is unrestricted so that decisions can be optimized pointwise in
\bm{x}
, the corresponding optimal decision rule under the triage score framework is

\pi^{\textsc{triage}}(\bm{x})\in\mathop{\rm argmax}\limits_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{K_{D}}}u(d,\bm{y})\,s^{\textsc{triage}}_{\bm{y}}(\bm{x}),

where
s^{\textsc{triage}}_{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})
denotes the predicted probability of principal stratum
\bm{y}
given covariates
\bm{x}

\pi^{\textsc{triage}}(\bm{x})\in\mathop{\rm argmax}\limits_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{K_{D}}}\left(u_{y_{d}}^{d}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\
d^{\prime}\neq d\end{subarray}}^{K_{D}-1}\tilde{u}_{y_{d^{\prime}}}^{d^{\prime}}\right)s^{\textsc{triage}}_{\bm{y}}(\bm{x}).

4
Statistical Evaluation and Learning

Given the additive counterfactual utility introduced above, we can now
statistically evaluate the expected utility of any decision rule

D^{\ast}
, including the ones that have not been used in practice, and
learn the optimal decision rule under the triage score utility
framework. In addition, we can
compare the expected utility of a given decision across different
additive utility specifications, allowing a decision-making system to
be evaluated under various sets of utility parameters. In this
section, we develop such statistical evaluation and learning
methodology.

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}u_{y}^{d}\Pr(Y=y\mid D=d,R=r,\bm{X}=\bm{x})\Pr(D^{\ast}=d,R=r\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})

\displaystyle+\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\

The proof of Theorem
1
is given in the
Appendix
B.1
. Theorem
1

shows that the expected utility of the counterfactual risk assessment
system can be point-identified under the stated assumptions. This
identification strategy mirrors Corollary 2 of

26
, which shows that the additivity condition
is both necessary and sufficient for identifying expected
counterfactual utilities under the standard unconfoundedness

for a discussion of how
we adapt this assumption to our setting). In certain cases,

is not required for the identification of

. Specifically,

. In the following, we show how to estimate the

4.2
Evaluating Decisions

We now discuss the estimation strategy using an augmented
inverse propensity weighting (AIPW) estimator. Throughout the rest of
the section, we assume that Assumptions
1
and

2
hold in our study. We begin by defining two nuisance
components:

\displaystyle\text{(i) Decision model }m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{x}):=\Pr(D=d\mid Z=z,R=r,\bm{X}=\bm{x}),

\displaystyle\text{(ii) Outcome model }m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{x}):=\Pr(Y=y\mid Z=z,R=r,D=d,\bm{X}=\bm{x}).

We also define the propensity score under the treatment assignment
z
as:

e(z,\bm{x}):=ze(\bm{x})+(1-z)(1-e(\bm{x}))

where
e(\bm{x}):=\Pr(Z=1\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})
.

We evaluate the expected utility of a generic decision
D^{\ast}
.
By Theorem
1
, the expected utility under a generic
decision rule
D^{\ast}
can be written as

\displaystyle\overline{U}(u;D^{\ast})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}\sum_{z=0}^{1}u_{y}^{d}\Pr(Y=y\mid D=d,R=r,Z=z,\bm{X}=\bm{x})\Pr(D^{\ast}=d,R=r,Z=z\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})

\displaystyle+\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\
d^{\prime}\neq d\end{subarray}}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}\tilde{u}_{y}^{d^{\prime}}\Pr(Y=y\mid D=d^{\prime},R=r,Z=z,\bm{X}=\bm{x})\Pr(D^{\ast}=d,R=r,Z=z\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})\Bigg]

where the summation over
Z
follows from Assumption
1
(b) and consistency.

We propose an AIPW estimator using a two-decision-index
uncentered influence-function term. The first decision index
identifies the potential outcome to be learned from the observed
human decision, while the second decision index identifies the
decision made by the evaluated rule
D^{\ast}
. This distinction is
needed for the counterfactual utility terms, which involve

\Pr(Y=y\mid D=d^{\prime},R=r,Z=z,\bm{X}=\bm{x})
multiplied by

\Pr(D^{\ast}=d,R=r,Z=z\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})
.
In this section, we assume that
D^{\ast}=f(r,\bm{x})
is a deterministic decision rule that is known to the researcher.
The proposed AIPW estimator can be easily extended to accommodate a stochastic decision rule
by introducing an additional nuisance model

m^{D^{\ast}}_{d}(r,\bm{x}):=\Pr(D^{\ast}=d\mid R=r,\bm{X}=\bm{x})
, which may need to be
estimated if it is unknown to the researcher.

Let
O_{i}:=(Y_{i},D_{i},R_{i},Z_{i},\bm{X}_{i})
.

\widehat{\overline{U}(u;D^{\ast})}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}\sum_{z=0}^{1}\Bigg(u_{y}^{d}\widehat{\eta}_{yddrz}(O_{i})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\
d^{\prime}\neq d\end{subarray}}^{K_{D}-1}\tilde{u}_{y}^{d^{\prime}}\widehat{\eta}_{yd^{\prime}drz}(O_{i})\Bigg),

where

\displaystyle\widehat{\eta}_{ykdrz}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X})

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}\{D^{\ast}=d,R=r\}\Bigg\{\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,k,\bm{X})\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})+\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z,D=k\}}{\hat{m}_{k}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}(\mathbbm{1}\{Y=y\}-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,k,\bm{X}))

\displaystyle\hskip 108.405pt+\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,k,\bm{X})(\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z\}-\hat{e}(z,\bm{X}))\Biggl\}.

The following theorem establishes the asymptotic normality of this
AIPW estimator under the rate conditions presented and discussed in
Appendix
A.3
.

Theorem 2

(Asymptotic normality of the AIPW estimator for decision)
.

Suppose that
D^{\ast}=f(R,\bm{X})
is a fixed, known deterministic rule, or that
the stochastic-rule extension is used with a consistently estimated

m_{d}^{D^{\ast}}(r,\bm{x})
. Under
Assumptions
1
,
2
,

3
, and
A.6
,
we have

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\overline{U}(u;D^{\ast})}-\overline{U}(u;D^{\ast}))\xrightarrow{d}N(0,V)

where

V=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\Bigg\{\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}\sum_{z=0}^{1}u_{y}^{d}\eta_{yddrz}(O_{i})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\
d^{\prime}\neq d\end{subarray}}^{K_{D}-1}\tilde{u}_{y}^{d^{\prime}}\eta_{yd^{\prime}drz}(O_{i})-\overline{U}(u;D^{\ast})\Bigg\}^{2}\Bigg].

The proof of Theorem
2
is given in Appendix
B.4
.
Appendices
B.5
and
B.6
provide additional discussion of AIPW estimators for human decisions and AI decisions.

4.3
Optimizing Decisions

Based on this framework, we now discuss how to derive optimal decision
rules that maximize expected utility given a set of utility
parameters. Specifically, consider an additive counterfactual utility

u\in\mathcal{U}^{\textsc{Add}}
. Analogous to
Theorem
1
, the expected utility under a
covariate-dependent policy
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{D}
is identified as follows
under Assumptions
1
and
2
.

\displaystyle\overline{U}(u;\pi)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{y_{0}=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\ldots\sum_{y_{K_{D}-1}=0}^{K_{Y}-1}u(d,\bm{y})\Pr(\pi(\bm{X})=d,Y(0)=y_{0},\ldots Y(K_{D}-1)=y_{K_{D}-1}\mid\bm{X})\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{z=0}^{1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}u_{y}^{d}\mathbbm{1}\{\pi(\bm{X})=d\}\Pr(Y=y\mid D=d,R=r,Z=z,\bm{X}=\bm{x})\Pr(R=r,Z=z\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})

\displaystyle+\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\
d^{\prime}\neq d\end{subarray}}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{z=0}^{1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}\widetilde{u}_{y}^{d^{\prime}}\mathbbm{1}\{\pi(\bm{X})=d\}\Pr(Y=y\mid D=d^{\prime},R=r,Z=z,\bm{X}=\bm{x})\Pr(R=r,Z=z\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})\Bigg].

Thus, we can estimate this optimal policy by solving the following empirical utility maximization problem with AIPW estimator:

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{\bm{u}}\in\mathop{\rm argmax}\limits_{\pi\in\Pi}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{z=0}^{1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}\mathbbm{1}\{\pi(\bm{X}_{i})=d\}

\displaystyle\Big(u_{y}^{d}\widehat{\widetilde{\psi}}_{yrdz}(Y_{i},D_{i},R_{i},Z_{i},\bm{X}_{i})

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\
d^{\prime}\neq d\end{subarray}}^{K_{D}-1}\tilde{u}_{y}^{d^{\prime}}\widehat{\widetilde{\psi}}_{yrd^{\prime}z}(Y_{i},D_{i},R_{i},Z_{i},\bm{X}_{i})\Big)

where
\Pi
is a class of policies specified by the researcher, and
\widehat{\widetilde{\psi}}_{yrdz}
is defined in Appendix
B.6
.

For binary decisions (
\mathcal{D}=\{0,1\}
), this optimization problem can be reformulated as a weighted classification problem.
Define the arrestee-level utility contribution under decision
d
as

\displaystyle\widetilde{U}_{i}(d):=\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{z=0}^{1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}\Big(u_{y}^{d}\widehat{\widetilde{\psi}}_{yrdz,i}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\
d^{\prime}\neq d\end{subarray}}^{K_{D}-1}\tilde{u}_{y}^{d^{\prime}}\widehat{\widetilde{\psi}}_{yrd^{\prime}z,i}\Big).

The empirical utility maximization problem becomes

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{\bm{u}}

\displaystyle\in\mathop{\rm argmax}\limits_{\pi\in\Pi}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\mathbbm{1}\{\pi(\bm{X}_{i})=1\}\widetilde{U}_{i}(1)+\mathbbm{1}\{\pi(\bm{X}_{i})=0\}\widetilde{U}_{i}(0)\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm argmax}\limits_{\pi\in\Pi}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\pi(\bm{X}_{i})U^{\ast}_{i}+\widetilde{U}_{i}(0)\Big],

where
U^{\ast}_{i}:=\widetilde{U}_{i}(1)-\widetilde{U}_{i}(0)
represents the utility gain from assigning cash bail (
d=1
) versus release (
d=0
) for arrestee
i
.
Since
\widetilde{U}_{i}(0)
does not depend on
\pi
, the optimal policy maximizes
\sum_{i}\pi(\bm{X}_{i})U^{\ast}_{i}
, which is equivalent to weighted binary classification where the class label is
\mathbbm{1}\{U^{\ast}_{i}>0\}
and the arrestee weight is
|U^{\ast}_{i}|
.
This reformulation enables the use of standard classification algorithms to learn optimal policies within a given policy class
\Pi
.
For multiclass decisions (
K_{D}>2
), the problem generalizes naturally: finding the optimal policy requires computing
\mathop{\rm argmax}\limits_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\widetilde{U}_{i}(d)
for each case
i
, assigning each arrestee to the decision with highest estimated utility.

For the purpose of our illustration, we further simplify the utility
specification by reducing the number of parameters. First, we assume

final assumption is that when determining costs and regrets, a judge

\frac{c_{\textsc{crime}}^{\textsc{cash}}}{c_{\textsc{crime}}^{\textsc{ror}}}\\

should determine utilities to reflect their own value system and

In contrast, the triage score framework explicitly incorporates

more flexible policy evaluation by accommodating richer and more
tailored utility specifications. The empirical results show that
policy recommendations derived from this framework can differ
substantially from those implied by conventional risk score
approaches.

6
Concluding Remarks

This paper proposes a new framework of triage score that can
accommodate counterfactual utilities while incorporating standard
utilities. Unlike popular risk score approaches that focus on the
baseline potential outcome alone, triage score considers all potential
outcomes, allowing decision makers to choose the best decision among
alternatives. While we applied the proposed methodology to an RCT in
criminal justice, triage score represents a general decision-support
framework that can be applied to a variety of settings.

Future work should consider the real-world application of triage score
framework by directly collaborating with policy makers. Doing so
requires careful elicitation of utility parameters. Moreover, the
proposed framework can be generalized to dynamic settings, where
decisions are made sequentially over time, generating carryover
effects and other temporal dependencies. An ambitious goal is to
develop a dynamic triage score that are dynamically updated as more
decisions are made and additional data become available over time.

A.2.3
Estimation and Implementation

Our implementation of GPI in this paper is designed to estimate the nuisance functions

\displaystyle m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{x})

\displaystyle:=\Pr(D=d\mid Z=z,R=r,\bm{X}=\bm{x}),

\displaystyle m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{x})

\displaystyle:=\Pr(Y=y\mid Z=z,R=r,D=d,\bm{X}=\bm{x}),

where
\bm{X}
includes structured covariates and an estimated affidavit-based deconfounder.

We proceed in three steps.

(1)

Extract internal representations from affidavits.
For each observed affidavit
\bm{S}_{i}
, we obtain an internal representation
\bm{L}_{i}
from an
open-source LLM under deterministic decoding. We then construct a fixed-length representation
(e.g., by pooling token-level hidden states) to use as the input
\bm{L}_{i}
for GPI.

(2)

Estimate a deconfounder and nuisance models.
We parameterize a deconfounder map
f(\bm{L};\lambda)
as a neural network that outputs a

q
-dimensional bottleneck representation. We then estimate
f(\cdot)
jointly with prediction
models for the decision and outcome. In particular, we use DragonNet
(
33
)
to
learn a shared representation and task-specific heads, yielding estimates

\widehat{f}(\bm{L}_{i})
,
\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X}_{i})
, and

\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{X}_{i})
.

(3)

Plug into AIPW evaluation.
Specifically, we plug
\hat{m}_{d}^{D}
and
\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}
into the AIPW estimators defined in
Section
4
. In the RCT, the treatment propensity score is known,

e(z,\bm{x})=0.5
, so we do not estimate
e(\cdot)
.

This procedure allows us to adjust for the rich information contained in PC affidavits while retaining a low-dimensional conditioning set. This supports both the plausibility of Assumption
2
and the practical estimation of the nuisance components required for our semiparametric estimators.

Formally, the validity of this procedure relies on the assumptions stated above together with the consistency of the learned deconfounder. In particular, we assume that the learned representation
\widehat{f}(\bm{L}_{i})
converges to a population-level representation
f(\bm{L}_{i})
such that
\{Y_{i}(d)\}_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\ \!\perp\!\!\!\perp\ \bm{L}_{i}\mid D_{i},Z_{i},R_{i},\bm{X}_{i},f(\bm{L}_{i})
, described in Proposition
A.1
.

In practice, we assume that the DragonNet architecture and training procedure are sufficiently flexible to learn a representation satisfying this condition. The architecture maps the high-dimensional representation
\bm{L}
into a shared representation
f(\bm{L})
, which is then used by both the outcome and decision heads,

\bm{L}\ \longrightarrow\ f(\bm{L})\ \longrightarrow\ \{h_{d}(f(\bm{L})),\,p_{d}(f(\bm{L}))\}_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1},

where
f
denotes the final layer of the shared representation network,
h_{d}
represents the outcome head for the outcome model under decision
d
, and
p_{d}
denotes the decision model.

In the binary decision case, for example, DragonNet jointly optimizes the following objective:

\displaystyle\{\widehat{\lambda},\widehat{\theta}_{0},\widehat{\theta}_{1},\widehat{\phi}\}_{\text{DragonNet}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{\lambda,\theta_{0},\theta_{1},\phi}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl\{Y_{i}-h_{D_{i}}\bigl(f(\bm{L}_{i})\bigr)\Bigr\}^{2}-\alpha\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl[D_{i}\log\bigl(p(f(\bm{L}_{i}))\bigr)+(1-D_{i})\log\bigl(1-p(f(\bm{L}_{i}))\bigr)\Bigr]}_{\text{cross-entropy loss for the decision model}},

where
\lambda,\theta_{0},\theta_{1},
and
\phi
denote the parameters of the networks
f
,
h_{0}
,
h_{1}
, and
p
, respectively.

We assume that these two objectives do not conflict. Formally, let
\mathcal{S}_{Y}=\sigma(\mu_{0}(\bm{L}),\mu_{1}(\bm{L}))
denote the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the outcome mean functions and let
\mathcal{S}_{D}=\sigma(p(\bm{L}))
denote the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the propensity score. We assume that there exists a representation
f
such that
\mathcal{S}_{Y}\vee\mathcal{S}_{D}\subseteq\sigma\bigl(f(\bm{L})\bigr)

Under this assumption, we employ a DragonNet architecture with sufficient width and depth so that the learned representation

can serve as a deconfounder satisfying the required conditional independence.

Remark B.3
.

Under a binary decision setting, or under a multi-valued decision with a restricted form of
additive utility satisfying
\tilde{u}_{y}^{d}=\tilde{u}_{y}
for all

d=0,\ldots,K_{D}-1
(i.e., regret depends only on the counterfactual outcome and not on the
alternative decision), the difference in expected utility between the Human-with-AI and
Human-alone decision rules is identified under Assumption
1
alone.

To see this, recall that

\displaystyle U(u;D(1)\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})-U(u;D(0)\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}u_{y}^{d}\{\Pr(D(1)=d,Y(d)=y\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})-\Pr(D(0)=d,Y(d)=y\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})\}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\

Under Assumption
1
and consistency, the first term is directly
identified from observed data as

\sum_{d,y}u_{y}^{d}\{\Pr(D=d,Y=y\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x},Z=1)-\Pr(D=d,Y=y\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x},Z=0)\}.

Binary decision case.

When
K_{D}=2
, the second term can also be identified using the law of total probability with Assumption
1
(cf. Theorem 1 of
5
). For example,

\displaystyle\Pr(D(1)=0,Y(1)=y\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})-\Pr(D(0)=0,Y(1)=y\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})

Failure for

B.5
Evaluating Human Decision

We evalute the expected utility of the human decision
D(z)
for
z\in\{0,1\}
.
By Theorem
1
, the expected utility can be expressed as

\displaystyle\overline{U}(u;D(z))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}u_{y}^{d}\Pr(Y=y,D=d,R=r\mid Z=z,\bm{X}=\bm{x})

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\
d^{\prime}\neq d\end{subarray}}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}\tilde{u}_{y}^{d^{\prime}}\Pr(Y=y\mid D=d^{\prime},\bm{X}=\bm{x},Z=z,R=r)

\displaystyle\qquad\hskip 100.00015pt\times\Pr(D=d\mid R=r,\bm{X}=\bm{x},Z=z)\Pr(R=r\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})\Bigg].

using the law of total expectation and that
R\!\perp\!\!\!\perp Z\mid\bm{X}
in
Assumption
1
(b). The displayed AIPW terms
below use the deterministic-recommendation specialization

m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})=\mathbbm{1}\{R=r\}
, which holds in our application because

R
is a known function of
\bm{X}
. If
R
is stochastic, the same
construction can instead include the nuisance model

m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X}):=\Pr(R=r\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})
and its corresponding
augmentation term.

With the above expression, we propose an AIPW estimator using the
following two sets of uncentered influence function estimates: one for
the models of conditional expectations of compound outcomes,

\mathbbm{1}\{Y=y,D=d,R=r\}
, and the other for the models of the
product of analogous conditional expectations for counterfactual
utilities,

\widehat{\overline{U}(u;D(z))}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}\Bigg(u_{y}^{d}\widehat{\varphi}_{ydrz}(Y_{i},D_{i},R_{i},Z_{i},\bm{X}_{i})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\
d^{\prime}\neq d\end{subarray}}^{K_{D}-1}\tilde{u}_{y}^{d^{\prime}}\widehat{\widetilde{\varphi}}_{ydd^{\prime}rz}(Y_{i},D_{i},R_{i},Z_{i},\bm{X}_{i})\Bigg).

where

\displaystyle\widehat{\varphi}_{ydrz}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X})=\mathbbm{1}\{R=r\}

\displaystyle\Bigg\{\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{X})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z,D=d\}}{\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})}\Big(\mathbbm{1}\{Y=y\}-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{X})\Big)

\displaystyle\quad+\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{X})\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z\}}{\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})}\Big(\mathbbm{1}\{D=d\}-\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})\Big)\Bigg\},

\displaystyle\widehat{\widetilde{\varphi}}_{ydd^{\prime}rz}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X})=\mathbbm{1}\{R=r\}

\displaystyle\Bigg\{\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}{\hat{m}_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z,D=d^{\prime}\}}{\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})}\Big(\mathbbm{1}\{Y=y\}-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})\Big)

\displaystyle\quad+\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z\}}{\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})}\Big(\mathbbm{1}\{D=d\}-\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})\Big)\Bigg\}.

We now discuss the asymptotic properties of the proposed AIPW estimator.
Assumption
3
bounds the decision probabilities
away from zero for all decision levels in each
(z,r)
stratum. In
particular, it covers both
m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})
and

m_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})
, which appear in the counterfactual
component of the estimator.

Corollary B.3

(Asymptotic normality of the AIPW estimator for human decision)
.

Under Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
, and
A.6
,
for each
z\in\{0,1\}
, we have

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\overline{U}(u;D(z))}-\overline{U}(u;D(z)))\xrightarrow{d}N(0,V_{z})

where

V_{z}=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\Bigg\{\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}\Bigg(u_{y}^{d}\varphi_{ydrz}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\
d^{\prime}\neq d\end{subarray}}^{K_{D}-1}\tilde{u}_{y}^{d^{\prime}}\widetilde{\varphi}_{ydd^{\prime}rz}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X})\Bigg)-\overline{U}(u;D(z))\Bigg\}^{2}\Bigg].

Proof.

We define
\beta_{z}
as

\displaystyle\beta_{z}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}u_{y}^{d}\mathbb{E}[W\mid Z=z,\bm{X}]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\
d^{\prime}\neq d\end{subarray}}^{K_{D}-1}\tilde{u}_{y}^{d^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}[\Pr(Y=y\mid D=d^{\prime},\bm{X}=\bm{x},Z=z,R=r)

\displaystyle\hskip 150.00023pt\times\Pr(D=d\mid R=r,\bm{X}=\bm{x},Z=z)\Pr(R=r\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})]\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}\Bigg(u_{y}^{d}m^{Y}_{y}(z,r,d,\bm{X})m^{D}_{d}(z,r,\bm{X})m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\
d^{\prime}\neq d\end{subarray}}^{K_{D}-1}\tilde{u}_{y}^{d^{\prime}}m^{Y}_{y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})m^{D}_{d}(z,r,\bm{X})m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})\Bigg)\Bigg]

where
W:=\mathbbm{1}\{Y=y,D=d,R=r\}
denote a compound outcome.
We propose AIPW estimators for the two terms,
m^{Y}_{y}(z,r,d,\bm{X})m^{D}_{d}(z,r,\bm{X})m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})

and
m^{Y}_{y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})m^{D}_{d}(z,r,\bm{X})m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})
, separately.

Let the compound outcome model be

m(z,\bm{x}):=\mathbb{E}[W\mid Z=z,\bm{X}=\bm{x}]=m^{R}_{r}(\bm{x})m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{x})m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{x}).

Then, the uncentered influence-function term is given by

\varphi_{ydrz}(W,Z,\bm{X}):=m(z,\bm{X})+\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z\}}{e(z,\bm{X})}(W-m(z,\bm{X})).

Plugging in
W
and
m(z,\bm{x})
, we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{ydrz}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X})=\mathbbm{1}\{R=r\}

\displaystyle\Bigg\{m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{X})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z,D=d\}}{e(z,\bm{X})}(\mathbbm{1}\{Y=y\}-m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{X}))

\displaystyle\quad+m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{X})\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z\}}{e(z,\bm{X})}(\mathbbm{1}\{D=d\}-m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X}))\Bigg\}

where we use
m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})=\mathbbm{1}\{R=r\}
with a slight abuse of notation, since it is a deterministic function of
\bm{X}
.
The AIPW estimator for the first term is given by

\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbb{E}[W\mid Z=z,\bm{X}]]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\varphi}_{ydrz}(Y_{i},D_{i},R_{i},Z_{i},\bm{X}_{i}).

We now examine the remainder bias of this AIPW estimator.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\varphi}_{ydrz}-\varphi_{ydrz}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[m_{r}^{R}(\bm{X})\Bigg\{\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{X})-m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{X})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\Bigg(\frac{e(z,\bm{X})}{\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})}-1\Bigg)m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{X}))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{X})\Bigg(\frac{e(z,\bm{X})}{\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})}-1\Bigg)(m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X}))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{X}))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{X})(m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X}))\Bigg\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[m_{r}^{R}(\bm{X})\Bigg\{\Bigg(\frac{e(z,\bm{X})}{\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})}-1\Bigg)m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{X}))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{X})\Bigg(\frac{e(z,\bm{X})}{\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})}-1\Bigg)(m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X}))\Bigg\}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[m_{r}^{R}(\bm{X})\Bigg\{\frac{e(z,\bm{X})-\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})}{\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})}(m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\bm{X}))\Bigg\}\Bigg]

The absolute bias is bounded by

\lvert\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\varphi}_{ydrz}-\varphi_{ydrz}]\rvert\leq C_{1}(\lVert m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\cdot)-\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\cdot)\rVert_{2}+\lVert m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\cdot)-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d,\cdot)\rVert_{2})\times\lVert e(z,\cdot)-\hat{e}(z,\cdot)\rVert_{2}

By Assumptions
1
(c) and
A.6
, this is
o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
.

Similarly, we can show that the uncentered influence-function term for the second term is given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varphi}_{ydd^{\prime}rz}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X})=\mathbbm{1}\{R=r\}

\displaystyle\Bigg\{m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}{m_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z,D=d^{\prime}\}}{e(z,\bm{X})}(\mathbbm{1}\{Y=y\}-m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))

\displaystyle\quad+m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z\}}{e(z,\bm{X})}(\mathbbm{1}\{D=d\}-m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X}))\Bigg\}

This can be shown by using the product rule illustrated in
24
:

\displaystyle\textsc{IF}(m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X}),m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))

\displaystyle=m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})\textsc{IF}(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))+m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})\textsc{IF}(m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X}))

\displaystyle\quad-m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X}),m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})

Note that the uncentered influence-function term for the outcome model is given by

\displaystyle\textsc{IF}(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))

\displaystyle=m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})+\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z,D=d^{\prime},R=r\}}{\Pr(Z=z,D=d^{\prime},R=r\mid\bm{X})}(\mathbbm{1}\{Y=y\}-m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))

\displaystyle=m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})+\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z,D=d^{\prime},R=r\}}{m_{r}^{R}(\bm{X})m_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})e(z,\bm{X})}(\mathbbm{1}\{Y=y\}-m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))

where the last equality uses Assumption
1
(b).
That of the product of AI model and decision model is given by

\textsc{IF}(m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X}))=m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})+\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z\}}{e(z,\bm{X})}(\mathbbm{1}\{D=d,R=r\}-m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X}))

using the compound outcome
\mathbbm{1}\{D=d,R=r\}
.
Thus, the AIPW estimator for the second term is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbb{E}[\Pr(Y=y\mid D=d^{\prime},\bm{X},Z=z,R=r)\Pr(D=d\mid R=r,\bm{X},Z=z)\Pr(R=r\mid\bm{X})]]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\widetilde{\varphi}}_{ydd^{\prime}rz}(Y_{i},D_{i},R_{i},Z_{i},\bm{X}_{i}).

We now examine the remainder bias in this second AIPW estimator.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\widetilde{\varphi}}_{ydd^{\prime}rz}-\widetilde{\varphi}_{ydd^{\prime}rz}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[m_{r}^{R}(\bm{X})\Bigg\{\underbrace{\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})-m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})}_{T_{1}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\underbrace{\frac{\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}{\hat{m}_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}\Bigg(\frac{e(z,\bm{X})}{\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})}-1\Bigg)m_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))}_{T_{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\underbrace{\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})\Bigg(\frac{e(z,\bm{X})}{\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})}-1\Bigg)(m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X}))}_{T_{3}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\underbrace{\frac{\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}{\hat{m}_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}m_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))}_{T_{4}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\underbrace{\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})(m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X}))}_{T_{5}}\Bigg\}

We first combine
T_{1}
,
T_{4}
and
T_{5}
as

\displaystyle T_{1}+T_{4}+T_{5}

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\frac{\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}{\hat{m}_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}m_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})-m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})\Bigg)(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))

\displaystyle=\Bigg\{\frac{m_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}{\hat{m}_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}(\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})-m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X}))-\frac{m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}{\hat{m}_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}(\hat{m}_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})-m_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X}))\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\qquad\times(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))

Next, combining
T_{2}
and
T_{3}
, we have

\displaystyle T_{2}+T_{3}

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\frac{e(z,\bm{X})}{\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})}-1\Bigg)\Bigg(\frac{\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}{\hat{m}_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}m_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})-m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})\Bigg)(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))

\displaystyle\quad+\Bigg(\frac{e(z,\bm{X})}{\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})}-1\Bigg)(m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))

\displaystyle=\Bigg\{\frac{m_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}{\hat{m}_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}(\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})-m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X}))-\frac{m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}{\hat{m}_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}(\hat{m}_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})-m_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X}))\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\frac{e(z,\bm{X})-\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})}{\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})}(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{e(z,\bm{X})-\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})}{\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})}(m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))

Therefore, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\widetilde{\varphi}}_{ydd^{\prime}rz}-\widetilde{\varphi}_{ydd^{\prime}rz}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[m_{r}^{R}(\bm{X})\Bigg[\Bigg\{\frac{m_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}{\hat{m}_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}(\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})-m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X}))-\frac{m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}{\hat{m}_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}(\hat{m}_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})-m_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X}))\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\qquad\times(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))

\displaystyle\quad+\Bigg\{\frac{m_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}{\hat{m}_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}(\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})-m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X}))-\frac{m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}{\hat{m}_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}(\hat{m}_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})-m_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X}))\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\frac{e(z,\bm{X})-\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})}{\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})}(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{e(z,\bm{X})-\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})}{\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})}(m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))\Bigg]\Bigg]

Thus, the absolute bias is bounded by

\displaystyle\lvert\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\widetilde{\varphi}}_{ydd^{\prime}rz}-\widetilde{\varphi}_{ydd^{\prime}rz}]\rvert\leq C_{2}

\displaystyle(\lVert m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\cdot)-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\cdot)\rVert_{2}\times\lVert m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\cdot)-\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\cdot)\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\lVert m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\cdot)-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\cdot)\rVert_{2}\times\lVert m_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\cdot)-\hat{m}_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\cdot)\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\lVert m_{d}^{D}(z,r,\cdot)-\hat{m}_{d}^{D}(z,r,\cdot)\rVert_{2}\times\lVert e(z,\cdot)-\hat{e}(z,\cdot)\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\lVert m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\cdot)-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\cdot)\rVert_{2}\times\lVert e(z,\cdot)-\hat{e}(z,\cdot)\rVert_{2})

This is
o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
by Assumptions
1
(c),
3
, and
A.6
.

Applying Proposition 2 in
24
,
we can then write

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{z}-\beta_{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}\Bigg(u_{y}^{d}\varphi_{ydrz}(Y_{i},D_{i},R_{i},Z_{i},\bm{X}_{i})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\
d^{\prime}\neq d\end{subarray}}^{K_{D}-1}\tilde{u}_{y}^{d^{\prime}}\widetilde{\varphi}_{ydd^{\prime}rz}(Y_{i},D_{i},R_{i},Z_{i},\bm{X}_{i})\Bigg)

\displaystyle\quad-\beta_{z}+o_{p}(n^{-1/2})

where

\hat{\beta}_{z}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}\Bigg(u_{y}^{d}\widehat{\varphi}_{ydrz}(Y_{i},D_{i},R_{i},Z_{i},\bm{X}_{i})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\
d^{\prime}\neq d\end{subarray}}^{K_{D}-1}\tilde{u}_{y}^{d^{\prime}}\widehat{\widetilde{\varphi}}_{ydd^{\prime}rz}(Y_{i},D_{i},R_{i},Z_{i},\bm{X}_{i})\Bigg).

This leads to the desired result,

\sqrt{n}(\hat{\beta}_{z}-\beta_{z})\xrightarrow{d}N(0,V_{z})

where

V_{z}=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\Bigg\{\sum_{d=0}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}\Bigg(u_{y}^{d}\varphi_{ydrz}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\
d^{\prime}\neq d\end{subarray}}^{K_{D}-1}\tilde{u}_{y}^{d^{\prime}}\widetilde{\varphi}_{ydd^{\prime}rz}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X})\Bigg)-\beta_{z}\Bigg\}^{2}\Bigg].

B.6
Evaluating AI Recommendation

Now, we turn to the expected utility of following the AI recommendation
R
, which is
given by

\displaystyle\overline{U}(u;R)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}\sum_{z=0}^{1}u_{y}^{a(r)}\Pr(Y=y\mid D=a(r),R=r,Z=z,\bm{X}=\bm{x})

\displaystyle\qquad\hskip 100.00015pt\times\Pr(R=r,Z=z\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\
d^{\prime}\neq a(r)\end{subarray}}^{K_{D}-1}\sum_{z=0}^{1}\tilde{u}_{y}^{d^{\prime}}\Pr(Y=y\mid D=d^{\prime},R=r,Z=z,\bm{X}=\bm{x})

\displaystyle\qquad\hskip 100.00015pt\times\Pr(R=r,Z=z\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})\Bigg].

Following the same strategy as the one used for the evaluation of
human decision above, we propose an AIPW estimator using the following
two sets of uncentered influence function estimates: one for

\Pr(Y=y\mid D=a(r),R=r,Z=z,\bm{X}=\bm{x})\Pr(R=r,Z=z\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})
and the other for

\Pr(Y=y\mid D=d^{\prime},R=r,Z=z,\bm{X}=\bm{x})\Pr(R=r,Z=z\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})
.

\widehat{\overline{U}(u;R)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}\sum_{z=0}^{1}\Bigg(u_{y}^{a(r)}\widehat{\psi}_{yrz}(Y_{i},D_{i},R_{i},Z_{i},\bm{X}_{i})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\
d^{\prime}\neq a(r)\end{subarray}}^{K_{D}-1}\tilde{u}_{y}^{d^{\prime}}\widehat{\widetilde{\psi}}_{yrd^{\prime}z}(Y_{i},D_{i},R_{i},Z_{i},\bm{X}_{i})\Bigg),

where

\displaystyle\widehat{\psi}_{yrz}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X})

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}\{R=r\}\Bigg\{\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\bm{X})\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})+\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z,D=a(r)\}}{\hat{m}_{a(r)}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}(\mathbbm{1}\{Y=y\}-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\bm{X}))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\bm{X})(\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z\}-\hat{e}(z,\bm{X}))\Bigg\},

and

\displaystyle\widehat{\widetilde{\psi}}_{yrd^{\prime}z}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X})

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}\{R=r\}\Bigg\{\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})+\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z,D=d^{\prime}\}}{\hat{m}_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}(\mathbbm{1}\{Y=y\}-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})(\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z\}-\hat{e}(z,\bm{X}))\Bigg\}.

The following corollary establishes the asymptotic normality of this AIPW estimator.

Corollary B.4

(Asymptotic normality of the AIPW estimator for AI recommendation)
.

Under Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
, and
A.6
,
we have

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\overline{U}(u;R)}-\overline{U}(u;R))\xrightarrow{d}N(0,V)

where

V=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\Bigg\{\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}\sum_{z=0}^{1}\Bigg(u_{y}^{a(r)}\psi_{yrz}(Y_{i},D_{i},R_{i},Z_{i},\bm{X}_{i})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\
d^{\prime}\neq a(r)\end{subarray}}^{K_{D}-1}\tilde{u}_{y}^{d^{\prime}}\widetilde{\psi}_{yrd^{\prime}z}(Y_{i},D_{i},R_{i},Z_{i},\bm{X}_{i})\Bigg)-\overline{U}(u;R)\Bigg\}^{2}\Bigg].

Proof.

Recall that the uncentered influence-function term for the outcome model is given by

\displaystyle\textsc{IF}(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\bm{X}))

\displaystyle=m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\bm{X})+\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z,D=a(r),R=r\}}{m_{r}^{R}(\bm{X})m_{a(r)}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})e(z,\bm{X})}(\mathbbm{1}\{Y=y\}-m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\bm{X}))

\displaystyle\textsc{IF}(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))

\displaystyle=m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})+\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z,D=d^{\prime},R=r\}}{m_{r}^{R}(\bm{X})m_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})e(z,\bm{X})}(\mathbbm{1}\{Y=y\}-m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))

That of
\Pr(R=r,Z=z\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})
is given by

\textsc{IF}(\Pr(R=r,Z=z\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}))=m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})e(z,\bm{X})+e(z,\bm{X})(\mathbbm{1}\{R=r\}-m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X}))+m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})(\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z\}-e(z,\bm{X}))

where we use that
\Pr(R=r,Z=z\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})=\Pr(R=r\mid Z=z,\bm{X}=\bm{x})e(z,\bm{X})

and
\Pr(R=r\mid Z=z,\bm{X}=\bm{x})=m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})
in our setup.
Let
\psi_{yrz}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X}):=\textsc{IF}(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\bm{X})m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})e(z,\bm{X}))

and
\widetilde{\psi}_{yrd^{\prime}z}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X}):=\textsc{IF}(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})e(z,\bm{X}))
.
Using product rule, the centered influence-function term for the first term is given by

\displaystyle\psi_{yrz}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X})

\displaystyle=\textsc{IF}(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\bm{X}))m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})e(z,\bm{X})+\textsc{IF}(m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})e(z,\bm{X}))m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\bm{X})

\displaystyle\quad-m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\bm{X})m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})e(z,\bm{X})

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}\{R=r\}\Bigg\{m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\bm{X})e(z,\bm{X})+\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z,D=a(r)\}}{m_{a(r)}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}(\mathbbm{1}\{Y=y\}-m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\bm{X}))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\bm{X})(\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z\}-e(z,\bm{X}))\Bigg\}

and similarly

\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}_{yrd^{\prime}z}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X})

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}\{R=r\}\Bigg\{m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})e(z,\bm{X})+\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z,D=d^{\prime}\}}{m_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}(\mathbbm{1}\{Y=y\}-m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})(\mathbbm{1}\{Z=z\}-e(z,\bm{X}))\Bigg\}

where we use
m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})=\mathbbm{1}\{R=r\}
with a slight abuse of notation, since it is a deterministic function of
\bm{X}
.
Thus, we use the following two AIPW estimators for each term:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\Pr(Y=y\mid D=a(r),R=r,Z=z,\bm{X}=\bm{x})\Pr(R=r,Z=z\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\psi}_{yrz}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X})

and

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\Pr(Y=y\mid D=d^{\prime},R=r,Z=z,\bm{X}=\bm{x})\Pr(R=r,Z=z\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\widetilde{\psi}}_{yrd^{\prime}z}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X})

We examine the remainder bias in each term.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\psi}_{yrz}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X})-\psi_{yrz}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})\Bigg\{\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\bm{X})\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})-m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\bm{X})e(z,\bm{X})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\Bigg(\frac{m_{a(r)}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}{\hat{m}_{a(r)}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}-1\Bigg)e(z,\bm{X})(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\bm{X}))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\bm{X})(e(z,\bm{X})-\hat{e}(z,\bm{X}))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+e(z,\bm{X})(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\bm{X}))\Bigg\}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})\Bigg\{\Bigg(\frac{m_{a(r)}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}{\hat{m}_{a(r)}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}-1\Bigg)e(z,\bm{X})(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\bm{X}))\Bigg\}\Bigg]

The absolute bias is bounded by

\lvert\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\psi}_{yrz}-\psi_{yrz}]\rvert\leq C_{1}\lVert m_{a(r)}^{D}(z,r,\cdot)-\hat{m}_{a(r)}^{D}(z,r,\cdot)\rVert_{2}\times\lVert m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\cdot)-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,a(r),\cdot)\rVert_{2}

By Assumptions
3
and
A.6
, this is
o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
.

Lastly, the remainder bias in the second term is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\widetilde{\psi}}_{yrd^{\prime}z}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X})-\widetilde{\psi}_{yrd^{\prime}z}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})\Bigg\{\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})\hat{e}(z,\bm{X})-m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})e(z,\bm{X})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\Bigg(\frac{m_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}{\hat{m}_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}-1\Bigg)e(z,\bm{X})(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})(e(z,\bm{X})-\hat{e}(z,\bm{X}))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+e(z,\bm{X})(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))\Bigg\}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[m^{R}_{r}(\bm{X})\Bigg\{\Bigg(\frac{m_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}{\hat{m}_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\bm{X})}-1\Bigg)e(z,\bm{X})(m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X})-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\bm{X}))\Bigg\}\Bigg]

The absolute bias is bounded by

\lvert\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\widetilde{\psi}}_{yrd^{\prime}z}-\widetilde{\psi}_{yrd^{\prime}z}]\rvert\leq C_{2}\lVert m_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\cdot)-\hat{m}_{d^{\prime}}^{D}(z,r,\cdot)\rVert_{2}\times\lVert m_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\cdot)-\hat{m}_{y}^{Y}(z,r,d^{\prime},\cdot)\rVert_{2}

By Assumptions
3
and
A.6
, this is
o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
.

Applying Proposition 2 in
24
,
we can then write

\displaystyle\widehat{\overline{U}(u;R)}-\overline{U}(u;R)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}\sum_{z=0}^{1}\Bigg(u_{y}^{a(r)}\psi_{yrz}(Y_{i},D_{i},R_{i},Z_{i},\bm{X}_{i})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\
d^{\prime}\neq a(r)\end{subarray}}^{K_{D}-1}\tilde{u}_{y}^{d^{\prime}}\widetilde{\psi}_{yrd^{\prime}z}(Y_{i},D_{i},R_{i},Z_{i},\bm{X}_{i})\Bigg)

\displaystyle\quad-\overline{U}(u;R)+o_{p}(n^{-1/2})

This leads to the desired result,

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\overline{U}(u;R)}-\overline{U}(u;R))\xrightarrow{d}N(0,V)

where

V=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\Bigg\{\sum_{y=0}^{K_{Y}-1}\sum_{r=0}^{K_{R}-1}\sum_{z=0}^{1}\Bigg(u_{y}^{a(r)}\psi_{yrz}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}=0\\
d^{\prime}\neq a(r)\end{subarray}}^{K_{D}-1}\tilde{u}_{y}^{d^{\prime}}\widetilde{\psi}_{yrd^{\prime}z}(Y,D,R,Z,\bm{X})\Bigg)-\overline{U}(u;R)\Bigg\}^{2}\Bigg].

∎
