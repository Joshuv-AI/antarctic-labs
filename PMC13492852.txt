Diet quality in Kazakhstan schools and kindergartens: Implications for national nutrition policy
Authors: Saule K. Tardjibayeva, Aigul A. Abduldayeva, Gulnur N. Doszhanova, Nikolay A. Safonov, Nazira A. Mukhanbetova, Saule A. Iskakova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Nutrition during preschool and school age plays a crucial role in the development of sustainable dietary habits and is a key determinant of child health. In the context of ongoing revisions to national nutrition standards in Kazakhstan, current evidence on dietary quality in organized educational settings is highly relevant. This study aimed to assess the quality of nutrition among preschool and school-aged children in Kazakhstan and to identify potential gaps in the organization of institutional meal provision.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A cross-sectional study was conducted in 2021–2023 among 700 children and adolescents attending kindergartens and schools in various regions of Kazakhstan. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire covering demographic characteristics, health status, and anthropometric measurements. An integrated Diet Quality Index (0–18 points) was developed, incorporating meal regularity and the frequency of consumption of key food groups. Group comparisons (school vs. kindergarten) were performed using the Chi-square test. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 31.0.RESULTS:The median Diet Quality Index score was 12.44 ± 2.31 (interquartile range 12–13), corresponding to 69.1% of the maximum possible value. Overall, dietary quality was satisfactory; however, structural imbalances were identified. Carbohydrate-rich foods—particularly bread, bakery products, and cereals—contributed most substantially to the overall score. Insufficient consumption of vegetables, fruits, and nutrient-dense foods was observed, despite regular meal patterns.CONCLUSION:Standardized meal provision practices ensure relative uniformity across regions; however, overall diet quality remains suboptimal. The findings highlight the need to improve menu composition and support the continued revision of national nutrition standards. Future studies should include detailed nutrient-level analyses and longitudinal assessments of dietary patterns.

Introduction

It should be emphasized that more than half of Kazakhstan’s population consists of persons under the age of 15, indicating that the foundations of national health are established during childhood. This period is critical for the formation of stable dietary, habits and the development of a conscious attitude toward food choices. At the same time, the current preschool and school nutrition system faces several challenges that require comprehensive analysis within an agrifood systems framework. These systems encompass the entire cycle—from food production and distribution to final consumption within organized child nutrition settings—while also addressing issues of sustainability, accessibility, and food quality. The implementation of the government initiative to provide free meals to 1.7 million primary school pupils requires more than $335 million from local budgets, yet the norms and standards applied are reportedly based on outdated approaches.[123]

Analysis of diets implemented in school canteens indicates certain inconsistencies with contemporary recommendations for balanced nutrition. In several cases, menu structures are characterized by a relatively high proportion of energy-dense and highly processed foods, alongside limited inclusion of fresh vegetables and fruits, potentially affecting the overall nutrient profile of the diet. Such dietary patterns may be associated with deficiencies in specific vitamins and minerals, as well as with increasing rates of overweight among schoolchildren. In the long term, these patterns are considered potentially linked to a higher risk of chronic noncommunicable diseases, including type 2 diabetes mellitus and cardiovascular disorders.[45678]

At the time of writing, the draft revised school nutrition standards developed by the Ministry of Health had not yet been implemented and were undergoing professional evaluation. Available expert publications, including materials affiliated with the Kazakh Academy of Nutrition, suggest that certain parameters of the proposed school diets may not fully align with current evidence-based recommendations for child and adolescent nutrition. Reported concerns include possible deviations in macronutrient distribution—particularly regarding carbohydrates and saturated fats—as well as total energy content relative to reference values.[569]

Furthermore, literature indicates that beverages containing added sugars, including packaged commercially packaged fruit juices, continue to be included in the school food environment. Published nutrient analyses suggest that the free sugar content in a substantial proportion of these beverages may be comparable to that of sugar-sweetened soft drinks. Scientific sources also describe the addition of sugar in the preparation of traditional beverages such as compotes, fruit drinks, and tea. Regular high consumption of tea has been discussed in some publications as a factor potentially associated with indicators of iron metabolism, particularly among adolescent girls, although a causal relationship has not been definitively established.[479]

Alternative organizational models, including self-service formats, lunch box systems, and centralized school meal production facilities, are discussed in the literature as potential avenues for further improvement of school nutrition systems.[101112]

Analytical publications report substantial levels of food waste in school canteens. In many educational institutions, a considerable proportion of prepared meals remains uneaten. The literature attributes this phenomenon to organizational features of meal provision and the characteristics of the meals offered, although direct causal relationships have not been definitively established. Expert and industry sources also describe specific eating patterns among primary school pupils, who are often provided with full multicomponent meals during early morning hours. It is suggested that some students may have already eaten prior to attending school or may not experience a strong sense of hunger at that time, which may contribute to increased volumes of uneaten food.[7101314]

Reports from supervisory and monitoring bodies document sanitary and hygienic violations in school canteens, including instances of microbiological noncompliance. These findings indicate the need to further strengthen food safety monitoring and control systems within educational institutions.[213]

Economic analyses of school meal provision note that increased funding does not necessarily translate into improved resource efficiency. The literature describes instances of procurement of low-quality products, violations of storage conditions and shelf-life requirements, as well as limited transparency in supply and oversight processes.[1415]

Several studies address management and resource efficiency issues, indicating that in some cases, technological charts and recipes specify ingredient quantities that are not fully reflected in the final dishes. Limited transparency in financial reporting is also noted, complicating the assessment of the targeted and efficient use of budgetary funds.[16]

Furthermore, a number of studies suggest that school menus are often developed without sufficient consideration of regional culinary traditions and seasonal availability of local products. This may reduce meal acceptability among children and contribute to food waste. Additionally, limited attention is devoted to educational components aimed at developing knowledge and skills related to healthy eating, which may hinder the formation of sustainable dietary habits among schoolchildren.[11171819] In this context, scientific sources emphasize the relevance of implementing educational programs on the fundamentals of nutrition for both students and education professionals.

Additional concerns relate to seasonal product availability, off-season price increases, storage conditions, and physical accessibility of food, particularly during winter periods with extremely low temperatures. Similar systemic challenges were identified in the materials of the FAO technical seminar “Assessing the potential of agrifood systems: an approach to identifying ways to develop healthy school nutrition in Kazakhstan” (3-5 December 2024), conducted within the framework of the TCP/KAZ/3901 project “Contribution to the development and scaling up of healthy nutrition” with the participation of the Ministry of Health of the Republic of Kazakhstan.[81719] The seminar materials highlighted the importance of considering Kazakhstan’s territorial and agroclimatic characteristics in the analysis of school nutrition systems. These include the country’s vast territory, complex logistics chains, limited availability of environmentally sustainable products, sharply continental climate conditions, declining soil fertility, insufficient cooperation with local producers, and limited storage and transport infrastructure.

Despite the existence of formal standards, the actual situation demonstrates significant variability in food quality and compliance with sanitary requirements across regions and schools. School canteens remain a key institutional mechanism for providing balanced meals; however, national monitoring data from the National Center for Healthy Nutrition and UNICEF indicate that approximately one in five students in Kazakhstan reports dissatisfaction with school meal quality. Identified challenges include limited menu variety and palatability, absence of facilities for preparing healthy meals in approximately 20% of schools, inadequate sanitary and technical equipment, shortages of nutritionists and trained kitchen staff, and insufficient consideration of age-specific physiological requirements and food preferences.

Observational data suggest that school menus frequently do not fully comply with national or international dietary recommendations. A study conducted in Kazakhstan in 2021–2023 across approximately 20 schools and kindergartens revealed disparities in adherence to sanitary and technical standards, including insufficient staffing and lack of dietitians, as well as incomplete documentation practices.

Financial aspects also vary considerably: the cost of free hot meals ranges from $0.45 to $1.90 per student. Nevertheless, menu composition often does not meet recommended dietary allowances, with imbalanced distributions of energy and macronutrients. Most schools classify students into only two age groups (6–10 and 11–18 years), whereas a more differentiated structure (6–10, 11–14, 15–18 years) would better reflect physiological needs.

Specialized dietary menus are largely absent. Therapeutic nutrition for children with specific health conditions (e.g., diabetes mellitus, food intolerance, malabsorption syndromes) is typically managed by parents rather than institutional systems. Strengthening collaboration between schools and primary health care providers, including general practitioners, may represent a practical approach in contexts where dietitians are unavailable.

The added value of the present study lies in its comprehensive assessment of school nutrition within the broader agrifood systems context in Kazakhstan. Unlike approaches that focus solely on individual dietary behaviors, this study adopts a multilevel systems perspective, integrating regulatory, organizational, environmental, and outcome-based dimensions of institutional nutrition [Figure 1].

Multilevel conceptual model for determinants of diet quality in schools and kindergarten Kazakhstan

The conceptual model illustrates how national nutrition policy influences the actual quality of children’s diets in schools and kindergartens. National standards and physiological requirements define the composition of menus and dietary structure. These standards are implemented in educational institutions—both urban and rural—where organizational and infrastructural conditions may affect the extent of their practical application.

The degree of compliance with established standards shapes the characteristics of the institutional food environment, including the diversity and structure of meals provided. Ultimately, these conditions are reflected in measurable indicators of children’s diet quality. Thus, the conceptual model enables a systematic examination of the link between regulatory decisions and actual nutritional outcomes.

Objective

To assess the quality of nutrition among school-age and preschool-age children in Kazakhstan and to identify sociodemographic factors associated with higher diet quality within the context of revising national nutrition standards.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted using cluster-based recruitment. Educational institutions were designated by the regional Department of Education. Within each selected institution, all available and eligible children were invited to participate. Questionnaires with incomplete data on key variables were excluded from the final analysis. The study included 700 children attending kindergartens and schools in 5 of the 17 regions of Kazakhstan. The selected regions represented two major cities of national significance (Astana and Almaty), as well as provincial cities and rural settlements. Data were collected from January 2021 to December 2023.

Study participants and sampling

The study population consisted of children enrolled in preschool educational institutions and schools across different regions of the Republic of Kazakhstan. The initial dataset comprised 721 observations. After data cleaning and exclusion of questionnaires with missing values for key variables, 700 participants were included in the final analysis [Figure 2]. Cluster sampling methods were applied [Figure 3]. The sample size was calculated assuming a 5% margin of error and a 95% confidence level; the required minimum sample size was estimated at 700 participants.

Data collection tools and techniques

Data were collected using validated, pretested, and reliable structured questionnaires. The primary instrument was the questionnaire entitled “Study of the Provision of Balanced Nutrition for Children in Preschool and Secondary Educational Institutions,” developed within a scientific and technical project of the Ministry of Science and Higher Education of the Republic of Kazakhstan (2021–2023).

The questionnaire consisted of several sections. Section A collected demographic information, including age, nationality, region of residence, and type of educational institution. Section B gathered general information about parents, including profession, educational level, social status, and the respondent’s relationship to the child. Social status was assessed using a predefined scoring system (0–9 points) in accordance with established scale norms.[14] Section C assessed dietary characteristics, including meal frequency (1–2 times/day, 3–4 times/day, ≥4 times/day), regularity of meals (yes/no/not always), regular breakfast consumption, and frequency of intake of specific food groups (meat and meat products; grain products, including flour-based products and cereals; fruits and vegetables; butter; confectionery products; dairy products). Response options for most items included daily, 1–2 times per week, or rarely/never. Additional items included number of eggs consumed per week, daily fluid intake (200 ml to 1 liter or more), beverage preferences (water, sweetened drinks, juices), and lunch box content (hot food, fruit, nuts, sweets, or no lunch brought from home).

To provide a comprehensive assessment of diet quality, an integrated Nutrition Quality Index was developed to reflect key characteristics of dietary behavior and meal regularity.

The index comprised the following components: regular breakfast consumption; overall meal regularity; consumption of fruits and vegetables; adequate fluid intake; consumption of dairy, meat, and grain products; frequency of confectionery consumption; and use of butter. Each component was scored from 0 to 2 points according to predefined criteria reflecting compliance with recommended dietary practices.[23] The total score ranged from 0 to 18 points, with higher scores indicating better diet quality [Figure 4].

Household income, food security status, and urban-rural infrastructure differences were not included in the analysis, as the objective of the study was to assess diet quality within the framework of institutional nutrition standards rather than to examine socioeconomic disparities. Nevertheless, these external factors may influence dietary quality and should be considered in future research. Data were collected after obtaining official administrative approval and written informed consent from participants.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the current legislation of the Republic of Kazakhstan. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the local ethics committee of the nonprofit joint-stock company Astana Medical University (permit No. 8 dated 25 May 2025). Permission was obtained from the management of schools and kindergartens. Informed consent was obtained from the parents of the participants prior to data collection. The objectives of the study and the methods used were explained to the participants. Participants were assured that their information would remain confidential. In addition, written assent was obtained from participants enrolled in Grade 8 and above.

Statistical analysis

Statistical data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 31.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) Quantitative variables are presented as mean values and interquartile ranges (IQR), while categorical variables are expressed as absolute and relative frequencies. Group comparisons between school-aged and preschool children were conducted using Pearson’s Chi-square test, depending on the type of variable analyzed. Results of the logistic regression analysis are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). The level of statistical significance was set at P < 0.001.

Median diet quality index score was 12 (DQI 11–13). Age showed a tendency to be associated with a higher diet quality index, but statistical significance was not achieved (0.067).

Results

Sample characteristics

The analysis included 700 children for whom complete data were available to calculate the diet quality index. The median age of the participants was 8.9 years (interquartile range [IQR] 5–11). Children attending school were older than preschool children, as expected (P < 0.001).

Descriptive analysis indicated a moderate level of overall diet quality. The mean integrated Diet Quality Index score was 12.44 ± 2.31, corresponding to 69.1% of the maximum possible value, reflecting a moderate degree of adherence to recommended dietary guidelines.

Most participants followed a regular meal pattern, with a mean meal regularity score of 1.38 ± 0.59 and a mean eating frequency of 2.17 ± 0.54, corresponding to three to four meals per day. Fruit and vegetable consumption (1.31 ± 0.61) remained below optimal levels. Intake of meat (1.28 ± 0.59) and cereal products (1.27 ± 0.58) was moderate, as was dairy consumption (1.49 ± 0.63). The frequency of confectionery consumption was relatively high (1.68 ± 0.62). The mean fluid intake score was 2.25 ± 0.98. Regular breakfast consumption was rated at 1.37 ± 0.62, while participation in the school breakfast program was 1.79 ± 0.93.

Correlation analysis revealed a positive association between the Diet Quality Index and fruit and vegetable consumption, as well as adequate fluid intake (P < 0.05). A negative trend was observed between the index and confectionery consumption. One-way ANOVA demonstrated no statistically significant differences in diet quality across regions (P > 0.05), indicating the absence of marked territorial variation.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that regular fruit and vegetable consumption was independently associated with higher diet quality (OR = 3.97; 95% CI 1.66–9.49; P = 0.002). Adequate hydration was also significantly associated with improved diet quality (OR = 5.55; 95% CI 2.05–14.97; P < 0.001). The strongest association was observed for participation in the school breakfast program (OR = 17.96; 95% CI 6.72–48.04; P < 0.001).

Consumption of cereal, meat, dairy products, butter, and confectionery, as well as parental sociodemographic characteristics, did not demonstrate statistically significant independent associations with the integrated Diet Quality Index (P > 0.05).

Overall, the findings indicate that regular fruit and vegetable intake, adequate fluid consumption, and participation in school breakfast programs are the primary determinants of higher diet quality, whereas most structural and sociodemographic variables were not significantly associated with the outcome.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated a moderate level of diet quality among school- and preschool-aged children, with no statistically significant sociodemographic gradients at a stringent significance level. The absence of marked regional or social differences may reflect the institutional characteristics of the Kazakhstani school nutrition system, which operates under unified regulatory standards and methodological guidelines.[2313] National strategic documents, including the National Development Plan of the Republic of Kazakhstan until 2029,[1] emphasize the importance of organized nutrition as a component of state public health policy, potentially contributing to reduced variability across educational institutions.

The observed homogeneity of indicators is consistent with studies highlighting the regulatory nature of food provision in educational institutions in Kazakhstan.[5614] At the same time, national epidemiological monitoring reports on child nutrition indicate the presence of structural imbalances in dietary composition despite standardized supply mechanisms.[47] These findings suggest that institutional standardization may mitigate sociodemographic disparities; however, it does not in itself ensure the achievement of optimal dietary balance.

The most significant predictors of higher diet quality identified in this study were regular fruit and vegetable consumption, adequate hydration, and participation in school breakfast programs. These results are consistent with international evidence demonstrating that improvements in the school food environment and structured meal programs are associated with more favorable nutritional outcomes in children.[1011122021] Systematic reviews also emphasize the importance of educational and behavioral interventions in establishing sustainable healthy eating habits during early life stages.[192223242526]

The absence of statistically significant associations between diet quality and parental socio-demographic characteristics may further support the hypothesis that a centralized system of organized nutrition partially offsets household-level disparities. Similar system-level buffering effects have been described in food systems research, particularly in contexts characterized by standardized procurement procedures and regulated menu planning.[1718]

Although a tendency toward lower diet quality with increasing age was observed, this association did not reach statistical significance. This trend may reflect gradual changes in eating behavior as children grow older and gain greater autonomy in food choices, a pattern also described in studies addressing behavioral aspects of nutrition.[162027]

Overall, the findings underscore the importance of institutional regulatory mechanisms in shaping children’s dietary patterns within school settings. At the same time, there remains a need to further improve menu composition—particularly by increasing the proportion of fruits and vegetables and limiting confectionery products—in accordance with national physiological nutrition standards[3] and broader public health policy objectives.[1]

The analysis allows for a more detailed characterization of the nutritional profile of organized meals in educational institutions. The component structure of the index indicates a predominance of carbohydrate-rich foods—primarily bread, bakery, and cereal products—which contribute most substantially to the overall integral score.

Foods with high biological value—vegetables and fruits, meat and meat products, and milk and dairy products—are represented in the menus of both schools and kindergartens in comparable amounts. However, their combined contribution remains insufficient to offset the structural shift of the diet toward cereal and bakery products, indicating persistent constraints in menu planning despite formal compliance with dietary diversity requirements.

Components reflecting meal patterns, including breakfast regularity and meal frequency, demonstrate stable values in both types of institutions, suggesting adherence to regulatory requirements for meal organization. At the same time, the consistent presence of confectionery products and butter further influences the overall index values.

Overall, the structure of the index highlights the absence of quantitatively significant differences between schools and kindergartens. The key characteristics of dietary patterns appear systemic and are shaped by prevailing approaches to menu planning within the organized child nutrition environment. The integral index values in the surveyed institutions remain below the optimal level. The mean score reached 69.1% of the maximum possible value, which may be characterized as satisfactory but not optimal.

These findings reflect persistent structural features of dietary patterns that are systemic in nature and not associated with sociodemographic differences. The identified nutritional characteristics are likely attributable to the current model of menu planning within the organized child nutrition system.[12356] This underscores the need for further optimization of dietary structures, with greater emphasis on increasing nutrient density while maintaining the principles of standardization and accessibility.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design does not allow causal inferences between diet quality and the analyzed factors. Educational institutions were designated administratively, and the sample was formed using a cluster-based approach with consecutive inclusion of all available children, which limits strict generalizability of the findings to the broader population.

Dietary data were based on parental self-reports, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. The composite Diet Quality Index reflects structural and behavioral characteristics of dietary patterns; however, it does not include quantitative nutrient intake, portion sizes, or biochemical indicators.

Socioeconomic variables (household income, food security status, and infrastructure differences) were not included in the analytical model due to the study’s focus on institutional aspects of nutrition. Nevertheless, these external factors may influence dietary patterns and warrant further investigation in future research. The presence of missing data may have slightly reduced statistical power. The use of a stringent significance threshold (P < 0.001) reduces the risk of type I error but may limit the detection of weaker associations.

Conclusion

Thus, the findings indicate that the current system of free organized nutrition in educational institutions in Kazakhstan ensures relative uniformity in children’s diet quality regardless of region or type of institution, which aligns with the strategic objective of national policy to provide equitable access to balanced nutrition.[12] At the same time, the observed level of the Diet Quality Index (69.1% of the maximum possible value) suggests that target benchmarks for the prevention of diet-related noncommunicable diseases have not yet been fully achieved.[4]

The identified structural imbalances in dietary patterns—primarily a carbohydrate-oriented structure characterized by insufficient consumption of vegetables, fruits, and nutrient-dense foods—indicate the need for menu adjustments and a reassessment of priorities in the organization of institutional nutrition. These findings have practical implications for updating regulatory documents, including the National Nutrition Standards, refining requirements for dietary structure, and developing educational programs aimed at promoting healthy eating behaviors.

Overall, the study provides an empirical foundation for evidence-informed policy decisions aimed at improving the quality of organized nutrition and achieving public health objectives related to child health protection.[1]
