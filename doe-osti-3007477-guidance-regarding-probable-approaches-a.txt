Executive Summary
With the rapid development of advanced reactors and numerous companies requesting pre-application
engagements with the Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC), there is need for the NRC to best prepare
themselves for new challenges presented by advanced technologies. One specific reactor type of interest
is the molten salt reactor (MSR) that uses liquid salt as the fuel for the reactor. Liquid salt fuel reactors
have specific challenges with regards to performing material control and accounting (MC&A). The
challenges result from the fuel form being a continuous fissile material form rather than discrete units
like is found with fuel assemblies in traditional light water reactors (LWRs). The goal of this document is
to provide the NRC with guidance on the different approaches and technologies that may be proposed
by reactor designers to address these problems to prepare staff for regulatory reviews.

The document first introduces several representative designs of liquid-fueled MSRs: the loop type and
the integrated type reactor. A loop type reactor is a MSR that circulates fuel first through a primary loop
with a graphite core where the fissions take place and then the heated salt circulates through an external
secondary loop for cooling before returning to the reactor. The integrated type reactor involves a core
where the cooling loop is integrated into the reactor vessel. The flowsheets for these two types of MSRs
are given a general overview, and the possible key measurement point (KMP) locations within the
flowsheet are identified.

The different potential strategies for an applicant to apply MC&A approaches are presented. First,
relevant reactor MC&A-related regulations are discussed, including unique considerations for MSR
designs. Two different approaches for assessing special nuclear material (SNM) inventory are presented,
one with a single Material Balance Area (MBA) and one with multiple MBAs and how each approach can
be applied to the MSR flowsheet. The multiple MBA approach divides the facility into front end, reactor,
and back end MBAs. Additionally, potential process monitoring (PM) techniques are discussed as well as
methods that integrate advanced containment and surveillance (C/S) and material Continuity of
Knowledge (CoK) in the operations of each plant.

The largest section of the document is a detailed overview of all current and potential measurement
technologies that could be applied for MC&A in an MSR facility. This is subdivided by measurement type
such as Destructive Analysis (DA), Non-Destructive Analysis (NDA), other in situ measurement
techniques, and sampling. Each technology uses a standardized 1-page tech sheet that provides
specifications, advantages, disadvantages, and readiness for deployment for each technology as well as
references. Within the disadvantages, a common theme is the need for adequate sampling of salt during
reactor operation.

Finally, the document combines the information presented in the previous sections to provide example
cases of how different MC&A approaches and technologies may be applied to a given MSR flowsheet.
The first is the case of an integrated type reactor that utilizes a single MBA without PM. The second is
the case of a loop type reactor with multiple MBAs and PM. This is not to act as a recommendation as to
how an MSR designer should design their MC&A system, but to provide a generalized example of
proposed approaches and how the different components could potentially combine.

Section I: Introduction
The development of advanced and small modular reactor (A/SMR) technologies has accelerated
significantly in the past decade in the United States. Over 25 different reactor technologies are currently
in the application or pre-application stage of interaction with the NRC [1]. These technologies cover
concepts from traditional LWRs to advanced concepts such as Pebble Bed and MSRs. Several commercial
vendors are currently developing MSR concepts.

Due to their bulk properties, liquid-fueled MSRs come with known MC&A challenges. Traditional LWRs
utilize fuel in fuel assemblies that allow material to be inventoried as discrete items. Conversely, liquid
fuel MSR utilize fuel that is in a continuous form which presents challenges in assessing the SNM content
as it does not lend itself to item counting.

To meet regulatory requirements, applicants will likely focus on a combination of novel MC&A
approaches and advanced technologies. These approaches may include traditional MC&A related
technologies such as radiation-based NDA and lab-based destructive assay (DA) but also include molten
salt specific applications such as voltammetry and aerosolized salt laser induced breakdown
spectroscopy (LIBS). Novel MC&A approaches could be proposed to ensure confidence in material
accounting, including mass quantifying SNM measurements and associated uncertainties.. This will likely
use a combination of Containment and Surveillance and MC&A measurements over multiple material
balance areas. While not required, some applicants may choose to integrate process monitoring similar
to what would be required for a Category I fuel cycle facility.

This document will make regulators aware of the unique challenges for MSR MC&A as well as the
potential approaches and advanced technologies that may be used by applicants. However, this
document is not intended to recommend any one technology or MC&A approach. Individual applicants
will be responsible for developing their own MC&A strategy based on their unique circumstances.

The document is broken into four different sections. The first focuses on an overview of MSRs from the
context of the overall material flowsheet as well as MC&A-related measurements that can be taken. The
second focuses on the specific different ways MC&A may be applied with regards to the overall MC&A
approach and recordkeeping. The third section details the relevant technologies that may be integrated
for MC&A purposes. The fourth gives example cases as to how the flowsheet, MC&A, and measurement
technologies integrate.

Section II. MSR Flowsheet Options
Before assessing the MC&A approaches and technologies available for MSRs, it’s useful to contextualize
them in terms of their potential flowsheets. The challenges that a proposed MC&A approach will have to
address will be dependent on the system being assessed and its operations and material movement.
Without evaluating flowsheet options, the assessment of technologies and methods will be in a vacuum
and will not best address their applicability to MSRs. Thus, this section looks to detail the primary reactor
arrangement and fuel movement flowsheet options that are of concern when evaluating an MSR.

The primary concern of this document is with two types of reactor designs and how the same general
fuel flowsheet is applied to both. The two types of reactors are a loop type and an integrated reactor
design which are distinguished by whether or not the fuel salt flows out of the primary reactor vessel to
a secondary cooling loop as part of its operations. Either reactor applies the same general flowsheet for
the entire facility. Both of these reactor types and general flowsheet are detailed in section II.A. This
flowsheet is applicable in two different arrangements. The first application of the flowsheet is the case of
an MSR where fueling occurs at startup, and that fuel runs for the operational cycle of the reactor
without any refueling. The second is operations where online refueling occurs to maintain reactivity and
remove fission products. New fuel is added periodically as old fuel is removed and stored outside of the
reactor in a separate storage tank. Both options have their own unique challenges regarding MC&A. In
addition, it must be noted that both options are part of an open fuel cycle, absent of any form of
breeding. The subsequent subsections II.B and II.C will detail these two specific flowsheets and identify
the challenges and where MC&A measurements will be concerned.

In addition to these primary fuel cycle options of interest, there are other proposed fuel cycle options
such as reactors that mimic LWRs in terms of having fuel assemblies and zoned refueling cycles, breeding
reactors with Thorium, and closed cycles that use the intrinsic capabilities of applying pyroprocessing
molten salt fuel. While these options are not the primary concern of this guidance document, section
II.D will provide some general information for robustness with regards to what options may be
considered for these types of applications.

II.A Loop vs. Integrated Designs
A molten salt fueled reactor can be categorized as two subtypes: loop type reactors and integrated
reactors. While both use SNM dissolved in a molten salt, they are differentiated by whether the fuel salt
moves in and out of the primary reactor vessel. In a loop type, the fuel salt flows through the reactor
core through fuel channels where fission occurs, heating the salt [2]. After the flow exits the core, it
subsequently flows through a secondary cooling loop before returning to the reactor to repeat the
process. In an integrated design, the fuel salt is confined entirely to the reactor vessel [3]. The fuel salt
flows through the reactor core where the fission reactions occur, heating the salt. The heated salt is then
cooled in vessel by inactive salt that flows through cooling channels in the reactor vessel. The cooled salt
then flows through a downcomer and back into the inlet of the reactor core. The heated inactive salt is
cooled in an external secondary loop.

In both arrangements, there will be a need to periodically remove gaseous fission products. This could
be performed in one of two ways. The first is a separate off-gas loop that diverts some portion of salt for
treatment in an inert He gas atmosphere. This was the approach previously used by Oak Ridge National

 2

Laboratory for their Molten Salt Reactor Experiment (MSRE). The second is an in-situ removal in the
primary fuel loop using a He bubbling to remove fission products that does not require a separate
treatment loop for the salt [4]. In the case of the former approach, this step would have salt separated
from the reactor core providing an additional location for measuring SNM mass as well as an additional
location for access to salt.

This difference in primary loop designs results in reactor configurations that have their own unique
challenges and risks for safeguards [5]. It would be easier to sample salt for measurements in loop type
reactors, as the secondary loop can be easily accessed. However, the sampling access points would allow
for direct access to fuel salt outside of the core. Integrated designs make the fuel salt from the core more
difficult to access, but it also makes it more challenging to measure the state of the fuel salt for MC&A
purposes as the salt cannot be as easily accessed in a sealed system compared to a sampling loop. Such a
design would likely require the draining of some of the fuel salt from the core which could represent a
significant interruption in operations. Such an approach would require considerations regarding the
timing of the measurement of the physical inventory within the facility’s MC&A program.

Both designs follow a similar basic flowsheet [5]. This general flowsheet is depicted graphically in Figure
1. The facility, like most commercial nuclear power facilities, can be divided colloquially into three
sections: the front end, reactor, and back end. In the front end, fuel is received from the supplier
assuming the molten salt fuel is produced and synthesized at an off-site facility. This fresh fuel is then
stored until it is to be heated and placed within the reactor. The fuel is consumed in the reactor and,
depending on the refueling scheme, is either fully or partially drained to storage tanks. These two
different refueling schemes will be discussed in the subsequent two subsections with attention to the
distinguishing MC&A challenges they possess. The fuel salt in storage will eventually be shipped out of
the facility to final disposal in an open fuel cycle.

Most molten salt reactors currently in licensing and pre-licensing activities with the NRC are of the loop
variety. This includes the Molten Salt Research Reactor (MSRR) and the Natura liquid fuel molten salt
reactor which is a commercial version of the MSRR. Integrated design is proposed by and actively in pre-
licensing activities by Terrestial Energy as the Integral Molten Salt Reactor (IMSR) and Terrapower’s
Molten Chloride Fast Reactor (MCFR) [3, 6].

An additional aspect of reactor design not indicated in the flowsheet is that MSR reactor designs of both
forms will remove salt periodically to remove neutron poisons. This will occur in a separate process
vessel. After removal, the salt will be returned to the reactor for further irradiation. This allows for both
higher burnup and reactivity control.

II.B Once-through Flowsheet with Fuel Additions
One flowsheet option is the use of a once-through cycle with additions of fuel [7, 8, 5]. In this
configuration, the reactor would consist of a single startup fuel mass. This startup mass would be lower
than the total fuel capacity of the reactor and fuel would be added as needed to maintain reactivity as
fissile isotopes are exhausted and neutron poisons build up. At end of lifetime, the fuel would be drained
to a separate storage tank for final disposal.

In this configuration, there would be no regular or continuous online refueling additions and removals.
Fuel to be added requires the use of a storage tank separate from the reactor where it would sit before
additions. For calculating the inventory difference, the beginning inventory would be the reactors’s SNM
content measured before additions are made to the reactor. In a loop type reactor, the fuel salt loop
would possess sampling capabilities for drawing small samples. These samples could be used to
determine the state and condition of the reactor fuel salt and measure SNM quantities. Based on the
results of these samples, additions of fresh fuel could be made. Similar types of sampling of fuel
condition could be performed with integral designs but would be more involved as they would require
draining fuel from the reactor core itself.

An additional concept for fuel monitoring and sampling that could be proposed would be the use of a
holding tank into which a portion of salt could be rerouted from the loop for measurement and analysis
[3]. This has the advantage of providing a greater volume of salt for analysis which would provide a salt
sample more representative of the reactor contents than a small sample from a sampling line. However,
it comes with the disadvantage of being a more easily accessible source of SNM that would have to be
additionally monitored and inventoried as part of an MC&A scheme.

A single once-through cycle is potentially advantageous from an MC&A perspective as its only removal
term in the calculation of the inventory difference is in the case of a separate fission product treatment
loop upon the completion of which the salt is returned to the reactor as an equal addition. SNM
measurements for the material balance would consider the core SNM mass and additions from the fresh
fuel tanks during the reactor’s operation. In addition, a quantitative measurement would have to occur
at end of reactor life to measure spent salt SNM mass. For the purpose of calculating inventory
difference, this measured SNM mass could be used as the ending inventory versus calculated beginning
inventory values over the entire lifetime of the core from burnup calculations. This approach is detailed
further in Section III.C.2. This is advantageous as there would not be substantial removal of fuel from the

 4

core during the operation and with it the bulk salt mass could be tracked. The operational tradeoff of this
advantage is that the reactor core with startup fuel will have a reduced operational lifetime and will have
to undergo full draining of the core or swapping of the core in full. As a result, the operational capacity of
the reactor is diminished.

KMPs for this configuration would likely be the input fuel coming from the fresh fuel feed location, the
reactor in at the sampling portion of the loop, and drain tank. The measurement of the input SNM mass
would likely have the highest confidence as it would be qualified by the manufacturer of the fuel and the
only SNM would be Low Enriched Uranium (LEU) or High-assay Low Enriched Uranium (HALEU).
Measuring the reactor’s SNM content in operation will be challenging in that it will be based on samples
of a highly dynamic composition of fuel salt. The drain tank as a KMP could be challenging if the salt is
contained in a solid bulk form after draining. A solid form makes it more challenging to measure
effectively because of inhomogeneity due to stratification.

II.C Online Refueling (Feed and Bleed)
The other major considered MSR configuration is that of online refueling colloquially known as “feed and
bleed” [6, 9, 10, 5]. Like in a single once-through cycle, the primary reactor system consists of reactor
core with startup fuel composition that facilitates the fissile power generation that the molten salt fuel
flows through. The fuel salt is then cycled through a secondary cooling loop with a heat exchanger for
cooling and returned to the primary loop in the core. Fuel salt removed from the primary and secondary
loops are transferred to drain tanks for processing and disposal.

The primary difference in this cycle is the approach to fuel management. In the feed and bleed
configuration, refueling occurs in line where fuel is added to the reactor periodically like in the once-
through configuration. At the time of addition of fresh fuel, a set amount of spent fuel is removed to the
drain tank instead of retained in the reactor. In doing so, the same core can be utilized for an extended
period of time without having to perform a complete draining of the fuel and/or swap of the core itself.

However, a feed and bleed configuration would however generate additional challenges for MC&A. This
is due to the addition of a removals term to the calculation of the inventory difference. This additional
term could increase the overall uncertainty of the inventory difference calculation. Like the single once-
through cycle with small additions, the KMPs of the feed and bleed configuration would be at the input
fresh fuel feed, from the reactor itself, and from the reactor drain tank. The drain tank would be more
regularly measured due to holding removed fuel in storage during the course of the reactor operation.
Its SNM mass would be factored into every regular inventory difference calculation not just one that
occurs at the end of the operational lifetime of the fuel.

II.D Other Fuel Cycle Options
There are additional fuel cycles that are proposed for Molten Salt Reactors, and it is valuable to be
apprised of all potential options as there could be changes to policies or reactors of the latter type may
in fact be proposed. The following three subsections provide brief overviews of these options.

 5

II.D.1 Traditional Fuel Assemblies
One proposed fuel option for molten salt-based fuels is to contain it in a traditional fuel clad and
hexagonal assemblies [8]. These assemblies would follow similar cycles to current LWRs. They would be
inserted and then rotated during refueling every 18 months with the reactor consisting of multiple zones
of fuel. Compared to a bulk liquid, this would allow the fuel to be counted as individual units in the same
way as ceramic fuel and thus MC&A proposed would likely be analogous with that of current LWR
reactors. As a result, the MC&A requirements would be the same as the current licensed LWR fleet.

II.D.2 Th Breeding
An MSR option that has received considerable attention is the use of an MSR in a breeding orientation
with Th [8]. These options use a mix of U and Th molten salt where the U provides a starting fissile mass
and the Th is bred to produce U-233 as fuel. Th reactors would likely perform in one of two options. The
first is a reactor where the breeding self sustains within the core of the reactor and operates for periods
significantly longer than those of a reactor with refueling cycles (~7 years). The reactor would be started
up with its initial salt mixture and would not be refueled until the end of operations. At end of
operations, the entire core is removed and replaced. The removed core’s fuel salt would be reprocessed
for further use. The second option for Thorium breeding MSRs is a processing loop that is built with the
reactor. During this processing loop, salt with Protactinium-233 is separated from fission products and U-
235/Th salt by the application of counter current exchange. The Protactinium-233 bearing salt is placed
in a decay tank where it decays into U-233 and then is reintroduced into the reactor as fuel.

II.D.3 Closed Fuel Cycle with Reprocessing
The last notable option is a closed fuel cycle that applies reprocessing to the spent MSR fuel salt through
the application of pyroprocessing [8]. Pyroprocessing is a nuclear fuel reprocessing method that applies
electrochemistry to spent nuclear fuel to separate U/TRU products from fission products and rare earths.
Pyroprocessing is advantageous as the medium for electrochemistry is molten salt thus allowing
reprocessing to be applied to molten salt fuel without any additional steps such as fuel disassembly and
dissolution that would be required for the aqueous reprocessing of solid fuels. This can reduce the costs
of reprocessing and is beneficial to countries such as France that have a reprocessing infrastructure in
place.

Section III. MC&A Approaches
Every NRC licensed facility possessing SNM is required to use MC&A measures to prevent or detect loss,
theft, or diversion of the SNM in its possession. These MC&A measures include control and monitoring
to prevent or detect loss when it occurs or soon thereafter; periodic inventories of the SNM to verify the
presence of licensed material; and recordkeeping and reporting requirements. Traditional LWRs, where
the SNM is maintained in item form, perform their inventories by counting fuel assemblies instead of
making discrete measurements of the SNM. Since MSR fuel is in a continuous liquid form, MSR designers
will have to develop and document their MC&A approach as it applies to their facility. This section will go
over the NRC’s MC&A definitions and regulations, how they could apply to an MSR facility, and discuss
potential options that may be presented as part of a licensee’s MC&A program.

III.A NRC MC&A Policy and Terminology
The NRC regulations governing the control and accounting of SNM for certain licensees are found in Title
10 of the Code of Federal Regulations (10 CFR) Part 74, “Material Control and Accounting of Special
Nuclear Material.” The MC&A regulations in Part 74 distinguish between licensees authorized to possess
different types and quantities of SNM, and the requirements are graded based on the category of the
SNM possessed.

The NRC divides SNM into three categories based on their strategic capability to fuel a nuclear explosive
device. A tabulation of these categories is seen in Table 1. These categories are applicable to SNM in
nuclear fuel cycle facilities. Nuclear reactor facilities licensed under 10 CFR Part 50, however, are
excluded from the specific MC&A regulations that fuel cycle facilities are subject to for materials in Table
1. The different categories should be noted as the protection levels associated with different categories
might influence the thinking of applicants when developing their MC&A approach, especially as MSRs
are likely to use HALEU fuel.

NRC facilities licensed under 10 CFR Part 70, “Domestic Licensing of Special Nuclear Material,” and are
authorized to possess greater than 1 effective kg of SNM, are required to submit a Fundamental Nuclear
Material Control (FNMC) plan. The FNMC plan describes how the licensee will control and account for
the SNM in its possession. This includes details of how they intend to measure and account for the SNM
within the facility. An FNMC plan may not be required of MSR applicants, but the content of the FNMC
plan could potentially provide a foundation of the type of information that an applicant may use to
develop its proposed MC&A approach.

To demonstrate how their MC&A program meets the MC&A regulations, applicants will submit a
description of their MC&A program for review. A number of common features will likely be part of an
applicant’s proposed MC&A system:

 • Management Structure: Documented structure of MC&A management at a facility. This includes
 details about who specifically is in charge of the overall MC&A program as well as the
 responsibilities of workers within a facility with regards to the proper application of MC&A.

 • Measurement and Measurement Control: Quantitative measurements of SNM and their related
 process signals that indicate system state. Specific measurements and their integration within an
 MSR system are discussed in Section IV of this document.

 • Physical Inventory: Measurement of SNM inventory with comparison to expected inventory
 based off of reactor burnup calculations. This is described in the discussion of material balances
 below.

 • Item Control: Control of material that is present in countable items such as received fuel
 containers or fuel in long term storage containers. This is described below in this section.

 • Detection and Resolution of Indicators: Either detection of material loss in the form of Inventory
 Difference determined during the physical inventory or indicators that material may have been
 diverted due to disruption of materials compared to what is expected. This is specifically
 important with regards to material that is being irradiated in a system like an MSR and

 maintaining CoK that the material is being subjected to expected operation within the reactor or
 not. Details of these indicators both for inventory and CoK are discussed below.

 • Independent Assessment: Independent assessment of MC&A program to evaluate its

 • Recordkeeping: Records of nuclear material to meet the requirements of 10 CFR 74.19 outlined

In accordance with requirements in 10 CFR 74.19, reactor facilities are required to perform 12 month
inventories and maintain records of such inventories for inspection. In fulfilling MC&A requirements, an

inventory of SNM, in the measured values are evaluated by the calculation of the Inventory Difference
(ID), Active Inventory (AI), and Standard Error of Inventory Difference (SEID). It’s through calculation of
these values that the goals for each category of material are met.

The SEID is calculated as propagation of the variance of the individual measurements. All measurements

Choosing measurements that reduce both these errors will help in providing confidence in ID by

An ID is calculated during a material balance evaluation, performed over a material balance period on
what is known as an Material Balance Area (MBA). The MBA bounds the BI, A, R, and EI to calculate the

ID, AI, and SEID. The calculated metrics for each MBA are then combined to determine the metrics for
the entire facility. The bounds of the MBA are of great importance for a developer’s MC&A approach
and will be discussed further in the next subsections.

In addition to MBAs, another type of internal control area is an Item Control Area (ICA). ICAs are
identifiable physical areas for the storage and control of SNM items. ICAs focus specifically on the
management and accounting of items. ICAs could be used to receive fuel from off-site production
facilities. The fuel would be in self-contained shipping containers that could be treated as items with
unique identifiers and known measured SNM quantities. After fresh fuel salt is removed from its shipping
container to be prepared for reactor use, it cannot be treated as an item because it is no longer
containerized.

Though not required as part of reactor facility licensing, MSR facilities may choose to propose process
monitoring as part of their overall MC&A approach. For fuel cycle facilities that are subject to the
requirements, the following guidelines must be met:

 (a) A statistical test that has at least 95 percent power of detecting an abrupt loss of five formula
 kilograms.

 (b) A quality control test whereby process differences greater than three times the estimated
 standard deviation of the process difference estimator and 25 grams of SSNM are investigated.

 (c) A trend analysis for monitoring and evaluating sequences of material control test results from
 each unit process to determine if they indicate a pattern of losses or gains that are of safeguards
 significance.

These provide some useful context for an MSR applicant who may be interested in implementing a
similar approach at their facility.

In addition to these MC&A measures, additional containment and surveillance (C/S) measures will likely
be implemented in order to provide improved confidence in declared operations. C/S measures could
include: seals and unique identification numbers on received fuel; remote video surveillance of the
reactor and any material access points; or video surveillance, tags, and seals for spent fuel in storage. As
the focus of this paper is on MC&A and its associated technologies, this paper will not go into detail
about C/S technology and deployment. However, it is worthwhile to mention because in the case of an
MSR C/S measures will contribute to maintaining CoK in the facility.

CoK has been defined in safeguards literature as, “[a] system of data or information regarding an item or
activity that is uninterrupted and authentic and provides adequate insight to draw definitive conclusions
that nuclear material is not going to be diverted from peaceful purposes.” This definition is made in
relation to the IAEA, but similar principles could be inferred and applied to NRC domestic safeguards
requirements in 10 CFR 74. Similar principles are already applied with regard category I material subject
to process monitoring where material is monitored to maintain current status of material in process as in
guideline (c) for fuel cycle facility PM. However, CoK does not specifically focus on detecting diversion of
a set quantity of mass like in process monitoring. A proposed strategy for maintaining continuity of
knowledge can provide additional confidence where difficulties with performing a material balance exist
such as is the case with continuous fuel material like in an MSR. The goal would be to provide regular

 11

measurements and sampling of material to ensure that the MSR is operating as declared and as such
helping validate and conclude that the material conclusions from burnup calculations are also accurate.
CoK will be concluded based on both MC&A measurements and C/S. The subsequent sections will touch
on the considerations of CoK that could be integrated into an MC&A approach.

III.B Challenges of Applying MC&A for MSRs
MSRs have unique challenges with regards to meeting the NRC MC&A requirements. Unlike traditional
LWR fuels, MSRs with flowing fuel salt may not be based heavily on item accounting like with LWR fuel
assemblies. As a result, large quantities of SNM will need to be accounted for through quantitative
methods that measure the mass of SNM in kilograms.

The primary challenge is the large amount of mass that needs to be measured within a set material
balance period. The mass of material is so great that even with very low measurement uncertainties, the
SEID can be quite large [11].

To address these issues, a significant amount of US government and private sector R&D has gone into
developing advanced measurement technologies and investigating alternative MC&A approaches that
still meet the requirements of existent regulatory framework. These approaches have been investigated
via extensive modeling and simulation campaigns. These studies have focused on approaches such as
lowering uncertainty by increasing frequency of mass balances, dividing the facility into multiple MBAs,
and assessing trend analysis techniques that could be applied to the MC&A approach of an MSR facility.
These subsequent sections will examine these approaches in detail.

III.C MC&A Methods That Could Be Proposed
To meet the MC&A regulatory requirements, each MSR facility will need to perform SNM physical
inventories and meet the uncertainty and timeliness requirements. To develop their approach to
perform physical inventories, facilities should establish their internal control areas (MBAs and ICAs) as
administrative controls. Properly defined MBAs and ICAs will localize material movement evaluations to
their physical boundaries. Considerations such as material form or changes in content due to irradiation
will inform the selection of these MBA and ICA boundaries. To fulfill the physical inventory requirements,
a facility can approach defining their internal control areas from a few different potential strategies, each
with their own advantages and disadvantages. The first safeguards approach is to designate the entire
facility as a single material balance area. This is resemblant of how safeguards have traditionally been
applied to nuclear facilities. The second approach is subdividing the facility into multiple material
balance areas and performing periodic physical inventories where each is individually required to meet
the regulatory requirements.

In addition to determining material balance areas, applicants may propose to perform more frequent
physical inventories than required by current regulations.

The following subsections will detail each of these with regards to an MSR facility as well as provide
diagrams of the facility flowsheets indicating MBA boundaries.

III.C.1 Single MBA
The most general layout for an MSR facility would be a single MBA as show in Figure 2 [12]. Calculation
of the ID and AI from equation 1 and 2, respectively, would be performed for the single MBA. SNM mass

The first ICA would be where the fresh fuel is received. Fresh nuclear fuel salt would be shipped from a
separate fuel manufacturing facility in bulk containers each with documentation of the mass and
composition of the fuel. These containers would be verified and have seals to ensure that they are not
tampered with. The ICA would have similar administrative controls and physical security as the receiving
area of other commercial facilities based on the category of material.

The other ICA would be at the back end of the facility after the fuel material has been drained from the
reactor. This drained fuel material would need to be measured for composition and mass of SNM. After
these measurements are made on spent fuel material, the fuel salt would be containerized for storage,
cooldown, and future disposal. This would be analogous to on-site storage of fuel in spent fuel casks at
traditional LWR facilities. Like those dry casks, the storage of spent fuel salt in long term containers
would be subject to documentation, controls, surveillance, physical protection, and seals.

Throughout this newly drawn MBA, as shown in Figure 3 , the new material balance would be
performed. For the calculation of ID, the SNM in the fuel would be accounted for as beginning inventory
as it is unpacked out of fuel shipment containers. The fuel drained for removal and storage would be the
ending inventory in the ID calculation.

 The challenge with applying this approach is with regards to the dynamic nature of the reactor material
and how this accounted for. As fuel is consumed, U-235 is burned and the total quantity reduced in the
reactor, and Pu is produced via absorption of neutrons in U-238 and subsequent decay. This changes the
composition of the fuel in real time as the reactor operates. As these changes occur, they affect the
calculation of the ID and AI of the MBA. To address this, the measurement of fuel salt SNM would likely
be performed with increase frequency to ensure that knowledge of the SNM mass in the reactor is
maintained and reduce the SEID. Due to the difficulty in measuring the SNM mass in the reactor, the

 14

determination of the facility’s SNM inventory becomes significantly more challenging. To address the
challenge, a potential licensee may propose a multiple material balance area approach. Multiple
material balance area allows the challenges in tracking fuel inventory to be enveloped within the portion
of the facility where measuring SNM is more difficult and allows the operator to focus on specifically
reducing the SEID of that MBA and thus the SEID of the entire facility.

Even without the use of multiple material balance areas, these challenges can be in part addressed
through application of MC&A measurements in combination with C/S measures as part of maintaining
CoK. MC&A measurements to quantify mass and concentration of the salt can provide confidence that
the change in reactor material is occurring as expected and that the expected material is present within
the salt. Notable digressions between the measured versus expected values of material would trigger
further analysis by the facility to ensure that loss of material has not occurred. MC&A measurements as
part of this CoK verification would maintain verifiable records like the mass measurements taken for the
physical inventory. Additionally, to provide knowledge of system state, these CoK measurements would
have to occur more frequently than only during physical inventories over the associated material balance
periods.

III.C.2 Multiple MBA
A multiple MBA approach divides the facility into multiple MBAs [10]. The expected division would occur
between the front end, reactor, and back end of the facility as seen in Figure 4. Between each of these
sections would be some sort of physical demarcation, e.g. a portal with door, to indicate each MBA. In
addition, applicants may also apply ICAs within the multiple MBAs as seen in Figure 5.

For the front end MBA (MBA#1), the fresh fuel would be received and unpacked from their shipping
containers. For the formulation of the ID calculation, this unpacked fresh fuel would be treated as the
beginning inventory and the fuel transferred to fresh fuel storage as the ending inventory. This MBA
would measure the total mass and enrichment of the fresh fuel salt which would provide a quantification
of the U-235 mass in the MBA. As the beginning inventory and ending inventory is the same in the
formulation of the ID, the uncertainty for the SEID would be attributed to this quantitative
measurement. Assuming the material would only be present in MBA#1 long enough to perform a SNM
mass measurement and materials that are not expected to change composition during the material
balance period, the duration of the material balance period will not have significant effect on the
calculation of the ID or SEID of MBA #1.

material is being accessed only for the purpose of transferring into long term storage containers and
sampling the spent fuel salt for inventory documentation before transferring to the item control area in
final storage containers.

MBA #1 and MBA#3 may propose the use of “just-in-time” approach to minimize the amount of time in
the MBA and with the use of additional containment and surveillance would be able to confirm the
inventory will not have changed during its time in the material balance area without significant resources
attributed to the surveillance of material. This would significantly minimize the active inventory in the
MBA #1 and #3.

The most challenging aspect of performing MC&A in a liquid fueled MSR will be quantifying the mass of
SNM in the MSR reactor. Within a multiple material balance area approach, this is enveloped within MBA
#2. The reactor will contain fuel material on the scale of metric tons in a commercial design and the fuel
composition will be continuously changing. To account for these continuous changes and better detect
significant changes, a designer may propose shortening the material balance periods. By having multiple
material balance areas, the length of this period can be driven by the needs of accurately measuring the
inventory of MBA#2 and calculating its ID and SEID. By shortening the material balance period, there is
an increased ability to make timely conclusions regarding material inventory that needs to be resolved as
it occurs and as materials change within the reactor.

Within MBA#2, measurements of SNM mass would be taken of the fresh salt before it enters the
reactors, on the spent salt after being drained from the reactor, and from the reactor in operation either
from a sampling line within the reactor loop or if salt is periodically removed for treatment of fission
products. In this case, sampling may be from the line dedicated to segregating that salt fuel.

The beginning inventory when calculating the ID of MBA#2 when all SNM is in the reactor is difficult to
define due to the changes in composition with time. The only ID value that is quantitatively measurable
is the ending inventory at the end of the material balance period. The ending inventory would be
subtracted from a beginning inventory that represents the expected SNM composition at the end of the
material balance period. To determine this beginning inventory, burnup codes can calculate the
composition of SNM in the fuel with time as it is consumed in the reactor for the operating parameters
of the reactor. For each material balance period, the calculated burnup for that period will provide an
beginning inventory that is balanced against that measured ending inventory. This approach to
calculating the ID is expounded upon in Shoman et al [11]. This modified form of ID is subject to
uncertainty from both the data for the burnup calculations as well as the uncertainty of the
measurements themselves. Additionally, the results of each quantitative SNM measurement can be used
as inputs for the burnup calculation of the next material balance period.

Like with the single MBA case, MBA#2 will likely rely on regular MC&A measurements to maintain CoK of
the nuclear material in the reactor as this is the portion of the facility flowsheet that is dynamic. These
more regular measurements to quantify SNM would be used to calculate inventory metrics with greater
frequency. The combined C/S and MC&A measurements for CoK would provide confidence where there
is expected uncertainty in the inventory difference calculation due to the known measurement and
uncertainty challenges.

III.D Process Monitoring
As discussed above, PM is not required for production facilities (reactors). However, applicants may
choose to pursue process monitoring as part of their overall MC&A approach. This can be facilitated in
part by the application of more regular inventory difference calculations. As mentioned previously, the
increase in frequency of the calculation of inventory differences can assist the timeliness of detection of
the loss of material. This by itself can assist in meeting condition (a) of a process monitoring schema for
a nuclear facility under 10 CFR Part 74. This would be specifically relevant if it was applied in line with
the timeliness goals (i.e. 7 days) set for SSNM in Cat 1 facilities.

Additionally, the multiple calculations of ID allows for the use of a trend test for detecting material loss.
An ID series’ variance/covariance matrix can be decomposed into what is known as Independent
Transformed MUF and Standard Independent Transformed MUF (SITMUF) [13]. A statistical test known
as a Page’s trend test can be applied to the series of SITMUFs to detect both abrupt and protracted loss.
These tests have been applied to continuous fuel material in real time by the Thermal Oxide
Reprocessing Plant (THORP) in the UK but have yet to be applied to domestic NRC licensed facility at
commercial scale [14]. However, a proposed Page’s test is a strong option for a facility FNMC with
regards to PM being informed by this experience. This trend test would assist in meeting PM condition
(c): A trend analysis for monitoring and evaluating sequences of material control test results from each
unit process to determine if they indicate a pattern of losses or gains that are of safeguards significance.

If the use of PM or some tenets thereof are implemented, the PM approach and its associated MC&A
measurements can also be tied into maintaining CoK along with it. First, the more regular timeliness of
SNM measurements can be integrated concurrently into an CoK approach. Additionally, the trend tests
that apply to the inventory metrics could also apply to the measurements as part of CoK to detect large
digressions from expected material changes in the fuel salt during reactor operations.

III.E Conclusions from Operations Signals
In addition to the technologies for assessing mass movement in an MSR facility, one additional set of
data that could assist in nuclear material control and accounting is the use of operational signals to infer
conclusions about mass movement. These would be process signals that would be already used for
operational and quality purposes. These signals include data streams such as temperature, pressure,
flow rates, and other additional parameters that would need to be monitored and logged by operators to
ensure safe operation of the reactor. All this information can provide insight as to the operating state of
the reactor and provide additional confidence in the MC&A of the reactor operations. Confirming the
operating state of the reactor will also assist in confirming that the parameters of the burnup
calculations are correct. As a result, there is greater confidence in the fidelity of the burnup calculations

 18

with regards to SNM mass changes and the facility’s inventory metrics. The operational signals thus
provide an additional measure for maintaining continuity of knowledge where bulk mass quantification
may have challenges.

Conclusions can be drawn using multivariate approaches. One application is the use of multivariate
techniques such as Principal Component Analysis and Partial Least Squares [15]. These methods have
been applied and investigated as part of quality control procedures to both ensure operations are
occurring within set parameters as well as deriving conclusions on quality attributes. In addition,
methods involving data fusion show promise for these applications as well. Instead of making
conclusions on product quality, these same techniques would be applied to detecting anomalous
behavior of an MSR or its related systems such as refueling and storage.

A significant challenge with applying this method is that it would require extensive data sharing between
the utility and the NRC. The operator may want to maintain operational data as proprietary and not
release it for additional evaluation by the NRC. As a result, such approaches if proposed would require
the negotiation of what streams are to be provided and in what quantities and frequencies. Modeling
and simulation could aid in answering questions as to what data is useful and how much of it would be
required to make operational conclusions.

These approaches and applications will not be discussed further with regards to this document.
However, the NRC should be aware that such approaches may be proposed as part of an applicant’s
MC&A program.

III.F Modeling and Simulation Tools
For the purpose of assessing different MC&A options, an applicant may use modeling and simulation to
assess the effectiveness of their proposed MC&A approach as well as the measurement requirements at
each KMP. The Sandia National Laboratories (SNL), as part of the Materials Protection Accounting and
Control Technologies (MPACT) campaign, has developed numerous open source tools for assessment of
inventory metrics. These two primary tools are the python tool MAPIT and the MATLAB/Simulink tool
F3M [14]. These tools can be used to simulate material flow, change, and performance of inventories
and PM at set time intervals. The inputs are blocks that represent the different processes and uses
Monte Carlo simulations to calculate the relevant inventory metrics and PM quantities and uncertainties.
The specific advantage of this tool is the ability to define the measurement uncertainties required of the
measurement technologies to meet MC&A goals. The models can adjust these uncertainties to provide
the designers with target uncertainties to meet for deployed SNM mass measurement technologies.
Additionally, it can identify KMPs where challenges in meeting MC&A requirements will exist and where
additional emphasis will have to be placed on maintaining continuity of knowledge and containment and
surveillance. The use of these tools would be integrated during the MC&A approach design stage.

The NRC has also recently issued a Technical Letter Report, Modeling and Simulation Supporting Material
Control and Accounting for Advanced Reactors, on this topic [17].

 Description: Analytical technique
 to measure elemental composition

Application: Destructive analysis technique with a typical sample size around 10 ml. High-resolution

 Description: Mass Spectroscopy technique that

Figure 8-LIBS setup (Source: Garrett et al., 2023).

Application: Utilizes a laser to generate plasma on the surface of a material, producing a spectra that can

Specifications: Sample taken of salt, and high energy laser trained on surface of sample. A high energy

cannot be used in tandem to decrease latency.

Name: Scintillators for neutron detection

 Description: Fast and thermal neutron
 detection through scintillation.

Figure 12-The Fast Neutron Collar and discriminating between neutrons and gamma-rays (Sources: Swinhoe et al., 2024 and

Application: Incident ionizing radiation causes excitation of the detector medium which releases
photons that are measured by a photomultiplier tube (PMT). In some scintillators, the capture agent

medium. Detection of fast neutrons prevents high accidentals rate due to small gate widths. Some
materials can be used for dual gamma and neutron detection.

Disadvantages: Some scintillators are typically used in an active interrogation system which requires a
neutron source such as AmLi or DD generator. See sheet on active and passive neutron detection.
Thermal neutron scintillators often rely on lithium enriched in the isotope lithium-6, which has a limited
supply chain. High quality pulse-shape discrimination is necessary. High temperatures can degrade
scintillators.

Readiness for Deployment: The FNCL, an active system using plastic scintillators, is in use by the IAEA for

Name: Neutron coincidence counting and multiplicity counting

 Description: Techniques using the
 timing of neutron detections to
 determine fissile mass.

Figure 13-The Uranium Neutron Coincidence Collar-II with Boiling Water Reactor assembly insert and types of Rossi-α binning
(Sources: Swinhoe, Ensslin, Evans et al, 2024 and Swinhoe, Ensslin, Hutchinson et al., 2024).

Application: Coincidence counting or multiplicity may be used on fresh uranium fuels, especially paired
with active interrogation. Total neutron counting could be used as a confirmatory measurement on
waste streams.

Specifications: Bulk neutron counting, often called singles, does not consider time correlations.
Coincidence counting considers two correlated neutrons, called doubles. Multiplicity counting is a
variation of coincidence counting based on higher-order correlated neutrons, i.e. triples rate. Multiplicity
counting requires fewer assumptions than coincidence counting, but at the expense of longer count
times and the need for higher efficiency detectors. Bulk, coincidence, and multiplicity counting are all
techniques and can be paired with several neutron detector types and with passive or active counting.

Advantages: Can distinguish between fission neutrons and (α,n), although coincidence counting requires
a self-multiplication correction. Multiplicity counting does not require representative physical standards
for calibration.

Disadvantages: High-efficiency detectors as well as high count times may be needed to reach acceptable
uncertainty, especially with passive neutron coincidence or multiplicity counting, or when fuel includes
burnable poisons. Sensitive to matrix effects. Coincidence counting is most useful for well-characterized
materials such as PuO2. Must be combined with other measurements, such as isotopic composition
determined by gamma spectroscopy or other techniques.

 29

Readiness for Deployment: Neutron coincidence counters have been in use for decades. A feasibility
study found that passive and active thermal neutron coincidence counting may be applicable to fresh
fuel in MSRs using large diameter pipes with HALEU fuel, but more research is needed.

Name: Passive and active neutron counting

 Description: Neutron counting technique that can
 be paired with multiple neutron detector types.

Figure 14-Euratom Fast Collar for PWR and BWR assemblies (Source: Swinhoe et al. 2024).

Application: Passive neutron counting is useful in situations where the spontaneous fission rate or the
(ɑ,n) rate are large, such as plutonium or used nuclear fuel. Active neutron counting is particularly useful
for fresh uranium fuel, which has a low spontaneous fission rate, or to lower required counting times.
Active neutron interrogation may also be used in conjunction with gamma detection to determine fissile
mass with fissile species separation (see HPGe tech sheet).

Specifications: Passive counting relies on the natural production of neutrons in a sample through fission
and (ɑ,n) reactions, while active counting induces additional fissions from an external neutron source.
Active interrogation typically uses AmLi sources, although alternative neutron sources are available
including californium-252 and neutron generators (deuterium-deuterium and deuterium-tritium).

Advantages: Active interrogation is useful for materials without sufficiently high neutron production
rates.

 30

Disadvantages: The commonly used neutron source for this techniques, AmLi, introduces uncertainty
due to spectral variations between sources. In a comparison between Cf-25 and AmLi, the AmLi standard
deviation was 5 times than Cf-252

use. Hogue found that an existing active system, Active Well Coincidence Counter (AWCC), would
produce unacceptable uncertainties when measuring uranium fresh fuel in fluoride or chloride molten
salts without additional design modifications, which have not yet been proposed. Devolving fissile
species through detection of delayed gammas from induced fission has only been modeled tested at the
laboratory scale with small amounts of material. Neutron interrogation used to measure delayed gamma
signatures from fission has been modeled and tested at the laboratory scale.

 Description: High-resolution gamma-ray

Figure 17-Two commercial HPGe systems (Sources: Mirion Technologies and ORTECK)

Application: Can be used for both total gamma-ray counting and spectroscopy. Typically used for

Section V. MSR MC&A Application
Having now discussed the topics of MSR flowsheets and configurations, potential MC&A approaches for
MSR, and applicable measurement technologies for MSRs, the next step is to provide examples of
instances where these three topics would intersect in an actual potential application. This sections will
detail the potential proposed MC&A schema for an MSR for two different hypothetical use cases: an
integrated MSR with a single MBA, two ICAs, and no PM, and a loop type MSR with three MBA, two ICAs,
and PM. Both cases will focus on the inventory considerations of the ICAs and MBAs, the measurement
technologies that could be deployed to those locations for those considerations, and how CoK will be
maintained with regards to the operations of the MSR.

These examples are not a recommendation or endorsement of specific MC&A approaches for MSRs.
They are only meant to provide a general overview of the kind of approaches an applicant may propose.

V.A Example #1: An integrated MSR with a single MBA, two ICAs, and no
PM
This scenario, an integrated MSR with a single MBA, two ICAs, and no PM, is represented graphically in
Figure 3.

V.A.1 ICA #1
Fresh fuel in solid form would be received into ICA #1. The fresh fuel would be stored in the shipping
containers in which they were received from fabricators. These containers would be sealed with tamper
proof seals to ensure they were not manipulated in transit and would have associated documents stating
the quantities and enrichment of the received fuel salt. The receivers would need to verify that these
seals are intact and file all necessary documentation for transfer as outlined in Section III.A. Verification
measurements, such as weighing of containers and level measurements, will be part of the receipt
process.

These containers would be stored until they are opened for insertion of the fresh fuel into the reactor.
Any new fuel received at ICA#1 during operation will be for the purpose of additions outside of startup.
The SNM controlled in the area will likely be reduced during this time as the majority of the facility’s
SNM inventory is active in the core, and the amount of additional fuel required is comparatively smaller.
The same measures for securing this additional received fuel will have to follow the same protocols to
ensure its monitoring and containment for application.

At this storage location, the containers would likely be monitored using surveillance equipment such as
cameras that link to the central security, and staff would check seals to ensure that no tampering has
occurred as well as checking documentation to ensure that swapping of inventory documents had not
occurred. Assuming these conditions of containment have been met and the number of received
containers have not been removed apart from approved from internal transfer, there can be assumed to
be no change in quantity of SNM within ICA#1.

In addition to C/S and inventory documentation, additional technology can be deployed to monitor that
the fresh fuel containers have not been manipulated or tampered with. These approaches used in ICA #1
are described in Section V.C.1 Fresh Fuel Receipt.

 48

V.A.2 MBA #1
MBA #1 would encompass all of the reactor facility from the front end to the back end. For the first
formulation of the inventory difference, the reactor’s beginning inventory equal the initial fuel loading
that was transferred from ICA#1. Determining the would involve verifying the bulk mass and enrichment
of material received to determine the total amount of U-235 present and that it matches what was in
the transfer sheets from the fuel manufacturer.

After verification, the salt is then placed in the reactor and the reactor is started up. After startup, the
subsequent calculation of inventory metrics changes. The beginning inventory is the SNM inventory as
calculated by burnup calculations. The ending inventory is the inventory determined by physical
inventory measurements. In addition to being compared to the burnup calculations, these ending
inventory measurements can update the inputs of the burnup calculations to inform the next beginning
inventory calculation as being the starting concentration of the reactor salt for the burnup calculation.
This would require access to the core to attain representative salt as the secondary loop of the reactor is
just for cooling. This could be performed via having a small drain of salt from the reactor by gravity or if
there is a salt cleanup loop separate from the primary and secondary loops there is sampling that could
occur. At either sampling point, the concentration of the salt and thus conclusions about its SNM can be
made. The measurements made at this point are described in Section V.C.2 Fresh Fuel Concentration and
Enrichment Measurement.

With the use of a closed primary loop, a reactor designer may propose to use an approach where CoK is
maintained and verified. With this approach, the assumption is that only salt fuel is added during the
reactor operation and never removed for long term storage. Additionally, any salt removed for fission
product treatment is returned in full to the reactor. Any holdup in the reactor core is accounted for as
the core is not drained but removed at end of lifetime. The bulk mass of the salt can be verified by
ensuring that the salt is never tampered with during its lifetime in the reactor and that the salt removed
for fission product treatment, assuming out of reactor treatment, is fully returned to the core without
interruption. In doing so, this well help bolster confidence in the SNM mass conclusions from the sample
concentration measurements. This CoK can be maintained via mass flow monitoring at all primary loop
penetrations as well as monitoring of elemental and isotopic concentrations. Many different
measurement technologies may be proposed here, and an overview of the most likely flow, NDA, and DA
technologies are described in V.C.3 Reactor System Measurement.

At the end of the operating period, the entire reactor will be shut down and the core removed. This
would end in a final material quantification, likely based on the same concentration measurements
proposed for the reactor system, described in Section V.C.4 Discharge. This represents the last point at
which high precision measurements relying on sampling are possible. Once this is completed the reactor
core will be removed in whole and moved to ICA#2.

V.A.3 ICA #2
The integrated reactor design will likely involve the removal of an entire core with fuel at the end of
operating life. ICA#2 will be where this core is stored before being removed from the facility. Once the

 49

core has been released from the MBA to the ICA the expectation would be that the used core would
remain untampered with during its time in the ICA.To ensure this, the used cores, like the fresh fuel
containers, would be subject to extensive C/S. This would include tamper proof surveillance cameras,
the presence of armed security, and the use of tamper proof seals on access points of the core in
storage. The approach would mimic that of fuel in dry cask storage at commercial nuclear facilities.

Additionally, based off the final quantitative measurements of MBA #1, inventory documentation can be
kept for each core in storage and can be updated based off cooling time and updated accordingly. These
inventory records would then provide the information necessary for transfer of the spent cores to
disposal. These records would be kept until that time after the transfer which they could be archived.

Like ICA #1, in addition to documenting SNM mass and C/S measures, the stored used cores can be
monitored externally by the use of radiation measurements. NDA techniques which do not require
sampling of salt will be deployed at this point, and are described in Section V.C.5 Storage of used Nuclear
Fuel.

V.B Example #2: Loop type MSR with three MBA, two ICAs, and PM
This scenario, a loop type MSR with three MBA, two ICAs, and PM, is represented graphically in Figure 5.

V.B.1 ICA #1
ICA #1 in the loop type MSR would likely differ very little in approach from that of an integrated design as
fuel would be received in a similar manner. Fresh fuel salt would be stored in the shipping containers
with adequate C/S measures and documentation of transfer until ready to be opened for insertion into
the reactor. The primary difference would be the amount of salt received and stored for further
additions. If the plan is to have only one total mass of salt for the loop at a time, loop type reactors
would likely receive a whole loop's worth of salt at the ICA in sequence when salt replacement in the
loop is needed. This would require a greater amount to be tracked in the ICA. If the loop is not to be fully
drained and replaced, but just added in time like an integrated design, the control needs would likely be
similar to that of an integrated design. However, the same approach would follow.

Like with Example #1, additional measurement technology can be deployed to increase confidence in the
material control and are described in Section V.C.1 Fresh Fuel Receipt. A combination of gamma and
neutron measurements not requiring sampling could be used.

V.B.2 MBA #1
MBA #1 covers the section of the facility in which the fuel salt is removed from the container (or
canister?) in which it was received and transferred for insertion into the reactor loop. Within MBA #1,
the primary measurement for MC&A would be the verification of enrichment. Total mass of salt can be
verified through the use of general methods such as scales, but the enrichment will determine the total
mass of U-235. This verification can be made through the use of either NDA or DA methods. These
confirmation measurements will be the primary source of uncertainty in the ID calculation.

 50

MBA #1 can maintain a low active inventory and with it simplify record keeping and ensure that material
is not accessed in an undeclared manner by only keeping the fuel in MBA #1 for a minimum amount of
time. This would likely be done by only moving containers of fuel from ICA #1 to MBA #1 when fuel is
demanded by reactor operations. Once enrichment is confirmed, the fuel is then moved to the reactor
MBA#2. These MBAs can be bounded by the use of locked and monitored portal. If performed in this
manner, the fuel will move smoothly and the ID should be 0 as the beginning and ending inventory are
the same and happen in as close to the same step as possible. Thus, this “just-in-time” approach would
potentially be a proposed method for fuel handling and SNM quantification.

V.B.3 MBA #2
MBA#2 covers the reactor portion of the facility with the reactor loop, salt cleanup loop, and salt
sampling loop. It would be physically separated from MBA#1 by either solid walls with a transfer door or
would be its own reactor containment building with a buffer area between the building for MBA#1 and
MBA#2.

MBA#2 is where the measurement of the SNM mass will require the most forethought. Before the
operation of the reactor during the first mass balance period, for the formulation of the ID, the
beginning inventory will be SNM mass of the fresh salt that was already measured in MBA#1.For this
specific circumstance the beginning inventory of MBA #2’s ID calculation can be the same as the ending
inventory of MBA#1’s ID calculation and would likely require no further measurement to quantify the
mass.

The challenges of measuring the inventory and calculating its metrics begin after reactor operation due
to the continuous nature of the fuel and that its SNM is constantly changing with further irradiation in
the reactor. Thus for future calculations of ID within MBA#2, the beginning inventory would be the fuel
salt SNM mass as calculated by burnup calculations and the ending inventory would be that of the
irradiated salt SNM mass as determined by quantitative measurements of the salt [11]. This ending
inventory SNM mass can be measured via advanced sampling and analysis. These samples would be
collected via a sampling loop that penetrates the secondary cooling loop outside the primary core of the
reactor. This sampling and measurement could be performed through the application of a number of
techniques, described in Section V.C.3 Reactor System Measurement.

Assuming that salt cleanup will be occurring outside the reactor in its own process vessel within MBA#2,
this would also provide a location for the application of SNM quantification related measurements. It
would be beneficial as the turnaround time for SNM quantification measurement could be extended and
there would be a substantial quantity of salt that would allow for additional measurements to be
performed. This could be a useful place to have samplers to remove material for DA or sample loops
required or preferred for techniques including voltammetry,

The frequency of these material balances would likely be performed on a more frequent basis than the
expected once a year that would be performed for a traditional reactor facility or in the other MBAs
identified in this example. This increased frequency would serve both an MC&A purpose as well as an
operational purpose as the constitution of the salt of both fuel and fission products determines its

 51

operations and safety. Within these inventories, the SNM content of the fuel salt tagged for when it was
sampled will be compared to the burnup simulation for that same time of operation. Large digressions
should be noted and investigated in a timely manner in accordance with NRC policy and reported in a
window if unexpected change is noted.

With frequent material balances, PM techniques could be integrated. The use of a Page’s trend test
could be proposed to detect loss. The IDs would be arranged as a time series and would be transformed
via single value decomposition to calculate what is known as Standardized Independent Transformed
(SIT) value. SIT would likely be used for a page’s test as it has demonstrated applicability in modeling and
simulation of fuel cycle facilities for both abrupt and protracted loss of material. Given a more regular
calculation of inventory metrics, the application of process monitoring could provide additional support
to detecting loss. The challenge is that for it to be applicable the length of material balance periods
would have to be substantially short e.g. on the order of weeks. Thus the technologies applied would
need to have a short turn around time on quantifying SNM. Whether DA techniques such as ICP-MS will
be proposed is related to whether the facility design includes an on-site laboratory.

Of the technologies described in Section V.C.3 Reactor System Measurement, many have a measurement
time on the order of seconds to minutes. Other technologies may require minutes to hours depending
on the configuration of the detection system, such as gamma spectroscopy, especially microcalorimetry,
and alpha spectroscopy. Calorimetry also requires hours to thermally equilibrate and may be less likely
to be proposed at this point.

Additionally, as there will be challenges with maintaining a low uncertainty of the aforementioned
material balances, approaches to maintain CoK that the salt or reactor has not been misused can bolster
confidence in operators' declarations for regulators. CoK would be maintained through much more
regular monitoring via a combination of both operational techniques such as monitoring and maintaining
operating temperatures, flows, and pressures, and monitoring other signals using bulk measurement
technologies. If there are a major change in process variables or monitoring variables, it should be able
to detected quickly . This would likely take the form of using basic process control procedures such as
quality charts and multivariable assessments that have been established for industrial process
monitoring in other chemical facilities including mass flow measurements and bulk neutron counting.

V.B.4 MBA#3
MBA #3 divides and constitutes where the fuel salt is drained for final disposal with no intention of
return to the reactor loop. This MBA would be divided by physical barriers from the reactor, likely
located in a separate location below the floor of the reactor to allow for gravity to drain the salt out of
the loop. The salt would be gathered into a container that would both collect the salt and potentially act
as its final disposal container. The container would need to be accessible to quantify the SNM of the fuel
salt removed from the reactor loop. This could be assessed using high precision techniques, especially
DA. Facilities without an on-site laboratory will likely propose to send samples to labs with ICP-MS or
(HR-)ICP-OES capabilities. Other technologies that may be proposed as part of SNM verification at this
point are described in Section V.C.4 Discharge.

For the formulation of MBA #3’s ID, the beginning inventory is the fuel drained from the loop into the
receiving tanks. As the fuel would be drained in bulk, the additions term in the ID during the balance
period will be zero, and the only removal terms will be salt removed. The ending inventory will be the

 52

salt in the same receiving tanks that are then transferred to ICA #2. Assuming no further access to the
salt, the ID of the MBA should be able to be confirmed as close to zero with some uncertainty resulting
from SNM measurements. C/S and monitoring of the fuel salt containers can help to ensure that only
salt sampling interactions occur. .

V.B.5 ICA #2
The final stored drain salt will be stored in ICA #2 until it is removed for final disposal.

Additionally, based off the final quantitative measurements of MBA #3, inventory records can be kept for
each core and can be updated based on cooling time and updated accordingly. These inventory records
would then provide the information necessary for transfer of the spent cores to disposal. These records
would be kept until that time after the transfer which they could be archived.

Like ICA #1, on top of documenting SNM quantities in inventory records and C/S measures, the stored
used cores can be monitored externally by the use of radiation measurements. Sealed salt will be
measured using techniques described in Section V.C.5 Storage of used Nuclear Fuel.

Similar to ICA#2 in the one-MBA model, NDA would be performed primarily using gamma and neutron
measurements. Other techniques could be proposed to augment the radiation measurements, such as
calorimetry, but they should require no sampling or opening of storage containers.

V.C Measurements
The same types of measurements must be conducted regardless of the MC&A schema chosen, but in
which ICA or MBA a particular measurement is taken depends on the proposed schema.

V.C.1 Fresh Fuel Receipt
Measurement of fresh fuel upon receipt may be conducted by combining gamma and neutron detection
in addition to weighing and inventory documentation. At LWRs, fresh fuel receipt is primarily an exercise
in documentation verification and damage inspection because fuel assemblies are in item rather than
bulk form. Any measurements of LWR fuel, such as with handheld gamma detectors, are confirmatory in
nature. For fluid-fueled MSRs, active neutron detection is more likely to be proposed, using a thermal
neutron detector such as helium-3 or boron-lined proportional counter. Passive neutron detection
systems could be deployed, but would require longer count times and/or a higher efficiency detector
system. Similarly, the gamma counting and spectroscopy system chosen will depend on the exact
container, count times, and efficiency targets. Depending on the type of container, acoustic interrogation
to uniquely fingerprint individual filled packages [18] could be considered as part of the C/S system.
Containers need not be unsealed and opened for these measurements.

V.C.2 Fresh Fuel Concentration and Enrichment Measurement
Enrichment is likely to be verified upon unsealing of the fresh fuel containers. This is likely to be
conducted using gamma spectroscopy, particularly measuring the 186 keV peak corresponding to
uranium-235, thermal neutron detection, alpha spectroscopy, or a combination of techniques. Passive
total neutron counting may be adequate for measurement of fresh fuel salts in small pipes or containers

 53

with enrichment levels close to the HALEU limit, 20 wt% U-235, but otherwise coincidence counting
and/or active interrogation are likely needed to obtain reasonable uncertainties [19]. Fast neutron
detection systems including organic scintillators may also be proposed.

Fuel inserted into the reactor system must also be measured, both as a mass removal from fresh fuel
containers and as an insertion into the reactor loop, which will require mass flow measurements as
detailed in the reactor system section. From this point onwards, fuel salt will be in bulk form until the
point at which it is permanently discharged from the reactor system, inventoried, and sealed in a used
fuel container for long term storage.

V.C.3 Reactor System Measurement
V.C.3.i Mass flow
Mass flowmeters will need to measure salt flow through the entire reactor loop, including the off-gas
system, chemical control, drain tank, sampling system, feed system, discharge system, and any other
area or port where fuel salt could be withdrawn. Many different technologies could be proposed as part
of a mass flow measurement system, and likely multiple mass flowmeter designs will be proposed due to
the diversity of systems where mass flow must be accounted for.

Differential flowmeters, like Venturi tubes and variable area meters like rotameters, have a long history
of use in industry, but must still be demonstrated in the relevant molten salt environmental conditions
[20] including high temperature, high radiation, and corrosive chemical species. Mass flow
measurements in MSRE were taken using Venturi tubes and presented challenges to measuring reactor
power [21]that were never fully resolved.

It is likely that non-intrusive flowmeter technologies like electromagnetic flowmeters and ultrasonic
flowmeters will also be proposed, but have a lower precision. Redundancy is important as mass flow
information is a key aspect of CoK.

Mass flow measurements may also be used in conjunction with weighing of containers to verify mass of
fuel salt fed or withdrawn into the reactor loop.

V.C.3.ii Sampling
A sampling system is integral to enabling high precision DA and some NDA measurements. The MSRE at
Oak Ridge had one system for sampling fuel salt and adding new material, but the location and process
of sampling may vary by reactor designer. Salt sampling methods to produce samples around 1 gram will
likely include one or more techniques of salt freezing, suction, or vessel filling [22]. Smaller samples
could be retrieved using a microfluidic sampling device [23]Sampled salt will be used for DA and also for
NDA techniques that cannot be performed in-situ, such as alpha spectrometry [24] and HKED.

V.C.3.iii NDA
In the reactor loop, a wide scope of NDA techniques may be proposed as part of an MC&A program. The
complexity and evolving nature of the fuel salt composition makes measurements complex and mass
flow rates must be combined with elemental and isotopic concentrations to determine fissile mass.

NDA will likely be conducted using a layered approach with several of the technologies discussed in
Section IV. Survey of MC&A Measurement Techniques. Multiple instruments may be integrated with a
single sampler for simultaneous use of several measurement techniques, such as the At-Line Integrated

 54

Analysis System (ALIAS) developed at Argonne [25]. One or more spectroscopic methods including
Raman spectroscopy, UV-Vis-NIR spectroscopy, or LIBS may to be proposed for resolving elemental
concentrations. HKED, or its component parts K-edge Densitometry (KED) and X-ray Fluorescence (XRF)
may also be proposed as part of an integrated sampler-analysis system or as individual instruments. If
microfluidic samplers are proposed for the reactor system, alpha spectrometry may also be used for
determination of isotopic concentrations of nuclear materials [24, 26]

As with other measurements in the facility, gamma and neutron measurements will play an important
role. Bulk neutron counters such as fission chambers or Self Powered Neutron Detectors (SPNDs) will be
used to monitor reactor power and inform burnup calculations. High radiation environments pose a
challenge for monitoring isotopic concentrations using gamma detection. Shielding and/or connection to
a sampler will be required to use high-resolution gamma detectors such as HPGe, microcalorimetry, or
CZT.

The reactor system is also where molten salt specific techniques including electrochemical
sensors/voltammetry and plasma bubble spectroscopy may be deployed. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) in
particular has emerged as the most likely electrochemical technique to be deployed in molten salt
systems, although square wave voltammetry (SWV) and linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) approaches are
also being investigated [27]. Research is ongoing as to whether electrochemical sensors will more likely
be placed in-line exposed to fuel salt flow [28], or as part of an at-line sampling loop.

V.C.3.iv DA
If an on-site lab is proposed for the facility, ICP-MS will likely be used to monitor fuel salt concentration
and fissile species. Samples will be taken from the reactor primary loop, discharged fuel, and systems
such as off-gas treatment. HR-ICP-OES could also be used for this purpose, but the high-resolution
machines capable of resolving isotopes are less widely used than ICP-MS. Without an on-site lab, these
samples must be sent off-site for processing with slower turnaround times.

V.C.3.v Off-gas
Gaseous fission products such as xenon and krypton will form in the fuel salt and must be removed from
the system. The primary concern of an off-gas system is to avoid gas build up in the system and maintain
appropriate radionuclide and hazardous chemical containment, but the system must also include SNM
monitoring and control due to system penetration to the fuel salt. There is substantial variation in the
technologies that could be employed in gas treatment systems to handle the range of gaseous species
produced in a MSR [29]. Reactor designers will have to consider the potential for SNM to become
aerosolized and enter the off-gas system, causing holdup in filters and scrubbers. Volatile uranium and
plutonium fluorides or chlorides, depending on fuel salt composition, will need to be captured as part of
the off-gas system [30]. Access to the off-gas system can be restricted by C/S measures during reactor
operations.

Elemental and isotopic monitoring can provide insight into the presence of SNM holdup in expected
areas and expected absence of SNM in areas such as the discharge stack. Optical spectroscopy including
UV, visible, and/or near-infrared (UV-Vis-NIR) spectroscopy and Raman spectroscopy is likely to be
employed here. Gamma measurements can also provide valuable information, but face a large radiation
background from non-SNM radionuclides that must be considered and addressed. DA techniques used in
the off-gas system could also include LIBS and either ICP-MS or ICP-OES.

 55

V.C.3.vi Drain tank and other fuel salt systems
All areas where fuel salt may be present must be covered by the MC&A program, including areas where
fuel salt will not normally be present but would be in the case of shutdown or emergency, like the drain
tank. Bubblers could be deployed in the drain tank to measure salt level when filled.

V.C.4 Discharge
The volume and composition of salt, including fissile mass, must be measured when discharged from the
reactor into storage container(s). Mass flowmeters can also be used to confirm the volume of salt
removed from the fuel salt loop, which should match the volume filled.

Additionally, this step may also rely heavily on DA techniques that can achieve high precision and process
replicate samples. If the facility includes an on-site lab, samples can be processed with a fast turnaround
time, otherwise sample transportation and shipment time must be considered.

Other neutron measurement techniques will be used with gamma measurements when fissile content
must be measured [31].The physical configuration of the used fuel containers and storage area should be
designed in conjunction with the NDA detector system due to the importance of background and
shielding environment on measurements. Both passive and active neutron techniques are currently used
to characterize LWR spent fuel and could be proposed for the molten salt used fuel stored in
ICA#2. Active neutron techniques that could be modified for MSRs include but are not limited to
differential die away (DDA) [32] and californium interrogation prompt neutron (CIPN) [32]. DGS could be
proposed as another way to leverage an active interrogation source [33] that the facility will already have
for active neutron measurements, although this technique is much less well developed to measure fissile
material of any kind than active neutron, passive neutron, and passive gamma measurements.

Passive neutron techniques include differential die away self-interrogation (DDSI) [34].Neutron
coincidence counting techniques are likely to be employed [35], especially to resolve plutonium [36]
from the neutrons produced from Cm-242 (α,n) reactions . Multiplicity counting is less well developed
even for LWR used nuclear fuel. All of these techniques require additional R&D and special-purpose
detectors will need to be designed with respect to fuel salt composition and storage geometries.

The gamma measurement component could use HPGe, medium resolution detectors such as CZT or
LaBr3, microcalorimetry, or a combination of detectors [37] to analyze burnup and cooling time.
Calorimetry could also be used to measure decay heat of individual containers [38]. Alpha spectrometry
could also resolve isotopic concentrations but would likely require microfluidic sampling [24].

V.C.5 Storage of used Nuclear Fuel
Outside the MBA, only NDA techniques that do not require sampling are likely to be proposed as part of
the MC&A measurement plan, primarily neutron and gamma detection. Continuous neutron and/or
gamma monitoring can play a surveillance role to confirm movement or lack thereof, in addition to C&S
measures.

 56

Total neutron counting could be performed using fission chambers for fuel containers without long
cooling times due to their ability to operate in high neutron fluxes and insensitivity to gamma-rays,
which dominate the total radiation signature.

V.C.6 On-Site Lab
An on-site laboratory is not a necessary facility component, but may be proposed by reactor designers.
Having an on-site laboratory enables much faster analysis of sampled salt, especially when samples or
replicates are measured frequently. The analytical laboratory would house the DA capabilities used by
the reactor, namely ICP-MS or (HR-)ICP-OES.

Section VI. Conclusions
MC&A approaches for liquid fuel MSRs have a number of unique considerations when compared to
traditional LWRs due to fuel design. Unlike LWRs, liquid fuel exists in a continuous bulk form that cannot
be treated as an item once it is removed from its designated container. As a result, an applicant will need
to describe in its application their MC&A program that addresses how SNM will be measured during
operation and how inventories using said measurements will be performed. As part of their MC&A
program, an applicant should define its MBAs and ICAs within the facility, determine which
measurement systems will be used to determine SNM quantities, and how it will be applied to a salt that
is in active use during reactor operation, and what roles C&S, CoK, and potentially PM will play within
their MC&A programs.

This document explored these aspects to provide a robust picture of the potential options being
considered. For the selection of MBAs and ICAs, the authors foresee the use of either a single MBA
approach that covers the facility where it handles SNM or an multiple MBA approach that divides the
facility into three MBAs for the front end, reactor, and back end. This subdivision would be considered
due to the challenges of measuring the SNM mass of the fuel salt in the reactor. Since the composition of
the SNM in the reactor is constantly changing, the reactor will require its own MC&A considerations as
to how the physical inventories are performed at the determined frequency. Though the current fleet of
LWRs perform annual inventories,, an MSR may want to shorten times to both have an quantitative
measure of fissile content of the salt and to ensure that the SNM composition of the fuel salt in the
reactor is in agreement with burnup calculations performed on the reactor. Additionally, the reactor may
be monitored using measurements for CoK to determine that the reactor is continuing to operate in the
expected manner. These online measurements, would be able to detect digressions in operations if they
were to occur and could be made without the need for additional facilities such as an on-site analytical
lab.

As for quantitative measurements of the salt SNM, both readily available measurement technologies and
those in active development have been assessed for MSR applicability. Measurement tools can range
from radiation based NDA and DA techniques to analytical technology applied in an analytical lab to in
situ measurements of the salt using techniques such as voltammetry. One specific challenge identified
with the applications with use of technologies for the quantification of SNM is the need for adequate
sampling capabilities to achieve representative samples from the reactor. Many of the quantitative
measurements are reliant on performing sampling which has only had limited historical applications at
the MSRE and for molten salt related applications at INL.

For CoK measurements, deployed NDA radiation detectors can be used to monitor salt signatures that
will need to be defined by the designers as well as salt holdup. Additionally, with further development of
mass flow monitors, the movement of salt mass in the reactor loop could provide confidence in mass
movement during operations. However, these measurements are not ready for deployment at the time
of writing this document.

In addition to SNM quantification measurements, the facility will likely utilize C&S measures to monitor
and track both fuel canister shipments and drained salt in identified containers. This would be similar in
approach to that used for both fresh and spent fuel assemblies in traditional LWR facilities.
