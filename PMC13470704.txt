Review of Research Advances in Full-Spectrum Fiber Optic Sensing Technologies for CO2 Geological Storage
Authors: Chenglong Zhang, Guobing Wang, Peng Dai, Qian Wu, Jinglong Fu, Chao Zhang, Lei Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fiber optic sensing technologies, owing to their intrinsic safety, immunity to electromagnetic interference, and distributed sensing capabilities, demonstrate unique applications in monitoring CO2 geological storage. However, existing review studies often focus on a single sensor type or specific monitoring scenarios, lacking a systematic classification and scenario adaptation analysis of full-spectrum technologies from point and quasi-distributed to fully distributed sensing. To address this research gap, this paper systematically reviews the principles and performance characteristics of full-spectrum fiber optic sensing technologies, covering core mechanisms of distributed temperature, acoustic, and strain sensing, and establishes a technology-adaptation framework for typical monitoring scenarios including wellbore integrity, reservoir dynamics, caprock faults, and near-surface facilities while integrating the latest advances in machine learning and deep learning for fiber optic data processing. Based on systematic analysis, prioritized short-term and long-term recommendations are proposed, covering multitechnology integration, extreme-environment adaptability, intelligent data processing, and standardization. This work aims to provide theoretical support for the design of monitoring schemes in CCUS projects and to promote the large-scale application of fiber optic sensing technologies in carbon sequestration safety monitoring.

Introduction

In the context of escalating global climate change and the continued promotion of the “dual-carbon” strategy, CO2 geological storage (CGS) serves as a key link within carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) systems for achieving large-scale emissions reduction and long-term carbon fixation, emerging as a critical technology for the development of deep saline formations, depleted hydrocarbon reservoirs, and hard-to-extract coal seams. − In essence, CGS entails the secure injection of captured CO2 into target underground formations, with long-term stabilization achieved via structural trapping, residual saturation, dissolution, and mineralization processes. − However, current monitoring methods such as seismic surveys, well logging, geochemical sampling, and conventional temperature and pressure monitoring still generally suffer from insufficient spatial continuity, limited temporal resolution, weak sensitivity to subtle anomalies, and difficulty operating in high-temperature, high-pressure, high-salinity, and highly corrosive environments, making it challenging to meet the safety monitoring and risk early warning requirements throughout the full lifecycle of CO2 storage. , Based on this, full-spectrum fiber optic sensing technologies, with advantages of continuous, in situ, long-term, and multiscale monitoring, are emerging as an important research direction in CGS monitoring.

Full-spectrum fiber optic sensing technologies, based on optical modulation, scattering, and interference mechanisms, combine notable advantages such as passive intrinsic safety, immunity to electromagnetic interference, corrosion resistance, small size, long transmission distances, and ease of long-term downhole deployment, demonstrating significant application potential in CGS monitoring. Point fiber optic sensors can achieve high-precision measurements of parameters such as temperature, pressure, strain, and CO2 concentration at specific locations; quasi-distributed fiber optic sensing is suitable for multipoint array monitoring of wells, pipelines, and key structural components, whereas fully distributed fiber optic sensing enables continuous spatial profiling along the fiber, providing real-time information on the dynamic evolution of temperature, strain, and vibration fields. Leveraging this monitoring capability from “point” to “line” and from local to global, full-spectrum fiber optic sensing technologies can cover multiple aspects including wellbore integrity evaluation, reservoir plume tracking, caprock and fault stability assessment, surface pipeline monitoring, and near-surface leakage early warning, becoming a critical technological support for enhancing storage safety, precision, and intelligence.

From a technological development perspective, fiber optic sensing in CGS has evolved from single-point measurement to distributed sensing, from single-parameter monitoring to multiparameter coordination, and from basic perception to intelligent interpretation. , Early studies primarily relied on point sensors such as fiber Bragg gratings (FBG), Fabry–Perot (F–P) interferometers, and fluorescent fiber sensors to achieve high-sensitivity, localized monitoring of temperature, pressure, and strain. Subsequently, quasi-distributed solutions based on FBG arrays and multipoint interferometric structures gradually developed, meeting the multipoint sensing requirements of key nodes. In recent years, distributed temperature sensing (DTS), distributed acoustic sensing (DAS), and distributed strain sensing (DSS) technologies, based on Raman, Brillouin, and Rayleigh scattering principles, have rapidly matured, enabling long-distance, continuous profiling and real-time dynamic monitoring, and have further expanded toward hybrid scattering integration, specialty fiber enhancement, multiparameter inversion of temperature, pressure, and acoustic signals, and artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted interpretation, advancing storage monitoring from “single-parameter observation” to “multi-field coupled sensing.”

Numerous researchers have conducted extensive studies on fiber optic sensing for CGS monitoring, ranging from literature reviews and mechanism analyses to engineering applicability assessments. Hadeed et al. conducted a systematic review on the operating principles, structural types, and performance parameters of fiber optic hydrophones, demonstrating their advantages in electromagnetic interference resistance and long-distance multiplexing for offshore CO2 storage monitoring. Li and Zhang performed a review of geophysical monitoring technologies for CGS, analyzing the application of fiber optic, seismic, and well-logging techniques in interlayer strain and plume feature identification. Leven and Barrash studied F–P interferometer-based fiber optic pressure sensors for hydrogeological applications, confirming their high-resolution advantage in confined monitoring spaces. Lu et al. reviewed the application of distributed fiber optic sensing in underground facility monitoring, analyzing its engineering potential with examples including gas storage, geothermal, and surface movement. Jenkins reviewed post-2015 CCS monitoring and verification advances, noting theoretical and practical developments in DAS, pressure monitoring, and marine ecosystem observation, but highlighted the need for more field experiments and validation.

Despite significant progress in integrating fiber optic sensing with CGS monitoring, there remains a lack of systematic consolidation. Existing reviews often focus on a single type of fiber optic sensor or specific monitoring scenarios, lacking a “full-spectrum” perspective on the systematic classification and comparative analysis of point, quasi-distributed, and fully distributed sensors across the full lifecycle of CGS. Moreover, the adaptability of different fiber optic sensing technologies to various monitoring scenarios (e.g., wellbore integrity, reservoir dynamics, caprock faults, near-surface facilities) lacks clear technical guidance, making it difficult to select appropriate solutions in practice. Furthermore, with the increasing application of AI in fiber optic data processing, existing reviews have not fully integrated the latest advances in machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) for signal denoising, feature extraction, multiparameter decoupling, and inversion imaging. Based on this, as illustrated in Figure , this paper systematically reviews the fundamental principles and performance characteristics of full-spectrum fiber optic sensing, establishes an adaptation framework for multiple CGS monitoring scenarios, incorporates the latest AI data processing achievements, analyzes current challenges and future directions, and aims to provide the theoretical support and technical reference for CCUS project monitoring design, promoting large-scale application of fiber optic sensing in carbon storage safety monitoring.

Classification, Principles, and Performance of Full-Spectrum Fiber Optic Sensing Technologies

Point Fiber Optic Sensing

Point fiber optic sensing is a fundamental component of full-spectrum fiber optic technologies, primarily targeting high-precision, localized measurements at critical points such as wellbores, reservoirs, faults, and pipelines. In CGS monitoring, it offers rapid response, high sensitivity, strong electromagnetic interference resistance, and suitability for long-term deployment in harsh environments. Typical examples include fiber FBG sensors, F–P interferometric fiber sensors, and fluorescent fiber chemical sensors, which are, respectively, applied for monitoring temperature, strain, pressure, and CO2 concentration. In FBG sensors, external temperature or strain induces changes in the grating period and effective refractive index, resulting in a shift of the Bragg wavelength, thereby enabling highly sensitive detection of target parameters. The sensing principle is illustrated in Figure . Compared to continuous distributed measurements, point sensing emphasizes precise identification of local anomalies, making it particularly suitable for detailed monitoring of high-risk nodes in storage systems. Consequently, it has been widely applied in early experimental studies and localized engineering monitoring.

Schematic diagram of the sensing principle of FBG sensors. Reprinted with permission from ref Copyright 2024 Wiley.

In the field of point fiber optic sensing, researchers have conducted extensive studies focusing on structural design, mechanical response, and medium identification. You et al. developed a four-cantilever-beam FBG three-component accelerometer, demonstrating through theoretical simulation and experimental validation that it offers a wide operational bandwidth and high sensitivity. Its structure provides a high-precision tool for seismic dynamic monitoring in CGS. da Silva Falcão et al. combined FBG sensors with nuclear magnetic resonance techniques to monitor deformation in calcite-cemented sandstone core injection experiments, revealing structural changes in the rock during CO2 storage. Xu et al. used FBG sensors to monitor phase transitions and fault strain during supercritical CO2 leakage, finding that strain responses within the fault were significantly higher than in surrounding areas. Fan et al. conducted experimental studies on sandstone displacement under varying storage pressures and CO2 phases, confirming that FBG sensors effectively indicate plume migration fronts and reservoir surface strain changes. Kim et al. developed hybrid-matrix composite fiber sensors capable of stable and reversible monitoring of both gaseous and dissolved CO2.

Overall, point fiber optic sensing technology has demonstrated strong parameter identification capability and significant engineering applicability in CGS monitoring. On one hand, FBG and F–P sensors can provide high-precision point measurements of key parameters such as temperature, strain, and pressure, suitable for localized, detailed observation of wellbore integrity, fault activity, and core displacement processes. On the other hand, fluorescent or composite fiber chemical sensors further extend their application to direct CO2 concentration detection. Meanwhile, point sensing also has limitations, such as restricted spatial coverage and the need for multiple deployment points to represent the overall field distribution. Therefore, it is more suitable as a high-precision local monitoring tool in coordination with quasi-distributed and fully distributed fiber sensing. Overall, point fiber optic sensing not only establishes the foundation for fiber monitoring technology in CGS but also provides crucial support for building multiscale, multiparameter, full-lifecycle monitoring systems.

Quasi-Distributed Fiber Optic Sensing

Quasi-distributed fiber optic sensing lies between point and fully distributed sensing. It typically employs FBG arrays, multipoint interferometric arrays, or weak-reflection grating arrays as core structures. By arranging multiple sensing units at predetermined intervals along a single fiber, synchronous monitoring of multiple key locations can be achieved. , Compared with point sensing, which only provides single-point information, quasi-distributed technology offers greater spatial coverage. Compared with fully distributed sensing, it achieves higher single-point accuracy and clearer signal identification, making it particularly suitable for scenarios requiring “multi-point fine monitoring,” such as key wellbore layers, fault zones, pipeline routes, and core surfaces. , Its fundamental principle is to exploit differences in central wavelength, reflection characteristics, or temporal responses among grating units to demodulate and analyze the concatenated signals, thereby achieving multipoint localization and multiparameter identification. In the field of quasi-distributed fiber optic sensing, researchers have conducted extensive studies on CO2 leakage detection, displacement front monitoring, and array optimization. Xu et al. conducted ten experiments on gaseous and liquid CO2 leakage, employing pressure sensors in conjunction with FBG sensors to monitor fault leakage, revealing the “temporary pseudo-sealing” phenomenon in liquid CO2 leakage and its formation mechanism. Sun et al. applied a multichannel FBG sensor array in water/CO2 displacement experiments on porous sandstone, capturing real-time axial and radial strain changes, confirming the technology’s ability to accurately reveal displacement front migration characteristics. Wang et al. developed an FBG array-based pipeline leakage detection method using compressive sensing theory, controlling localization errors of different leak points within 2 m. Saade and Mustapha used an FBG sensing network to monitor longitudinal and circumferential strains in pipelines, integrating support vector machine algorithms to enhance leak detection capability. Deimos et al. combined Raman spectroscopy with hollow-core fiber and other technologies to develop a distributed gas monitoring system integrated with FBG sensors, offering a novel solution for in situ CO2 leakage early warning. Paulsson et al. also developed a fiber-optic vector seismic array system suitable for high-temperature and high-pressure wells, demonstrating the potential of quasi-distributed arrays in dynamic monitoring.

Overall, quasi-distributed fiber optic sensing combines the high precision of point sensing with the multipoint coverage of array monitoring, serving as an effective bridge in CGS monitoring. On one hand, this technology enables high-density, multilocation, and multiparameter synchronous observation within limited channels, particularly suitable for applications requiring fine spatial resolution, such as wellbore integrity assessment, fault leakage detection, core displacement experiments, and pipeline anomaly localization. On the other hand, its system configuration is relatively flexible, allowing further enhancement of monitoring efficiency and detection accuracy through array design, wavelength multiplexing, and intelligent algorithms. However, quasi-distributed sensing points still rely on predeployment, limiting spatial continuity compared with fully distributed technologies. In large-scale engineering applications, issues such as array packaging, long-term stability, and signal demodulation complexity need to be addressed. Therefore, from the perspective of full-lifecycle CGS monitoring, quasi-distributed fiber sensing is better suited as a critical-site enhancement method, working in coordination with point-based and fully distributed technologies to build a multitier monitoring system.

Fully Distributed Fiber Optic Sensing

Distributed Temperature Sensing

Distributed temperature sensing (DTS) is the most mature and widely applied type of fully distributed fiber optic sensing. Typically based on Raman scattering, it analyzes the temperature-dependent intensity response between anti-Stokes and Stokes light in the fiber to achieve real-time continuous temperature profiling along the entire fiber. In CGS monitoring, temperature field variations are often closely related to fluid injection, phase changes, plume migration, wellbore leakage, and cement sheath anomalies. Therefore, DTS can compensate for the sparse and time-limited measurements of conventional temperature logging by providing “continuous measurement lines”. Compared with point-based or quasi-distributed technologies, DTS offers a long measurement range, strong spatial continuity, and suitability for long-term online monitoring, making it especially suitable for full-length observation of slender structures such as wells, reservoirs, and pipelines. As shown in Figure , DTS typically consists of embedded fiber cables, downhole pressure gauges, and surface control systems. It provides continuous temperature information along well depth segments, enabling high-resolution identification of thermal anomalies in the storage system.

Schematic diagram of a distributed temperature sensing system: the wellbore is divided into multiple depth segments to achieve continuous temperature monitoring. Reprinted with permission from ref Copyright 2022 Elsevier.

In the field of DTS research, scholars have conducted numerous studies focusing on wellbore monitoring, leak detection, and interpretation of temperature anomalies. Soliman et al. addressed the insufficient sensitivity of conventional mechanical integrity testing by developing a new DTS-based mathematical formula to estimate nitrogen leakage rates under non-steady-state conditions, validated through computational fluid dynamics modeling. The proposed DTS system schematic demonstrates continuous temperature measurement along segmented well depths, providing a reference for well integrity assessment. Ricard and Pevzner conducted a 13 day field DTS test at the CRC-3 well in Australia, analyzing over 3400 temperature readings, demonstrating that the technique can detect thermal anomalies of 0.7 °C with a spatial resolution of up to 2 m. Lee et al. analyzed DTS data from CO2 monitoring well construction, finding that conventional calibration methods provide optimal results for depth correction and temperature inversion and confirming that DTS can clearly detect thermal anomalies during gravel packing, cementing, and gas-lift operations. Wang et al. applied Raman-scattering-based DTS for microleak detection in pipelines, validating its feasibility in laboratory, pilot-scale, and field environments.

Overall, DTS provides high spatiotemporal resolution for monitoring fluid migration, identifying thermal disturbances, and assessing wellbore integrity in CGSs, serving as a core component of fully distributed fiber optic monitoring systems. On one hand, DTS offers flexible deployment, long monitoring range, and sensitivity to subtle thermal anomalies in wells, reservoirs, and pipelines, effectively supporting leak detection, injection profiling, and operational condition identification. On the other hand, DTS provides continuous temperature profiles with strong interpretive value, facilitating integration with pressure, strain, and numerical simulation data. However, DTS has limitations, including temperature fields influenced by multiple coupled factors, reliance on geological and operational context for anomaly interpretation, and constraints when used alone to interpret complex multifield interactions. Therefore, in full-lifecycle CGS monitoring, DTS is better employed in conjunction with DAS, DSS, and conventional pressure monitoring to enhance comprehensive identification of subsurface fluid behavior and storage risk evolution.

Distributed Acoustic Sensing

Distributed acoustic sensing (DAS) is a key branch of fully distributed fiber optic sensing, primarily based on Rayleigh scattering. By analyzing phase, amplitude, or frequency changes in backscattered light pulses traveling through the fiber, DAS enables continuous detection of vibrations, strain rates, and acoustic disturbances along the entire fiber. , In CGS monitoring, injection-induced plume migration, pressure perturbations, microseismic activity, and interwell wavefield changes often generate identifiable dynamic signals. Thus, DAS provides a novel continuous observation method for reservoir dynamic imaging, interwell seismic monitoring, caprock stability assessment, and pipeline leak early warning. , Its advantages include long monitoring distances, dense spatial sampling, strong electromagnetic interference resistance, and the ability to directly reuse existing wellbore or surface communication fibers. , As shown in Figure , the data acquisition and monitoring workflow illustrates the complete technical chain from downhole vibration collection to real-time surface demodulation and monitoring analysis.

In the area of DAS application research, numerous studies have been conducted. Daley et al. compared DAS with conventional geophones using a modular borehole monitoring system, demonstrating DAS’s strong performance in amplitude, phase, and spatial sampling rate. Grandi et al. used ray-tracing modeling to assess DAS monitoring of CO2 storage in the Goldeneye reservoir, indicating that multiwell DAS-VSP offers cost-effective and high-coverage imaging potential. Isaenkov et al. established a continuous monitoring system combining DAS and permanent sources at the CO2CRC Otway project, demonstrating that deconvolution significantly improves data repeatability. Yurikov et al. further validated the effectiveness of DAS for imaging CO2 plume evolution using multiwell 3D and 4D DAS-VSP data. Pevzner et al. employed multiple DAS arrays in wells along with surface permanent sources to monitor supercritical CO2 injection, identifying changes in strain amplitude and travel-time shifts in the injection zone. Beloborodov et al. tracked velocity anomalies caused by CO2 injection and subsequent migration during a shallow injection test.

Overall, DAS has emerged as one of the most promising fully distributed dynamic sensing technologies for CGS monitoring. Compared with DTS, which primarily reflects thermal anomalies, and DSS, which captures static or quasi-static deformations, DAS excels at detecting injection disturbances, wave propagation, microseismic responses, and leak acoustic signals, making it particularly effective for reservoir dynamic imaging, interwell seismic monitoring, caprock stability assessment, and pipeline anomaly detection. Moreover, compared with conventional geophones, DAS offers significant advantages in deployment efficiency, spatial continuity, and long-term operation; however, its application is limited by coupling conditions, channel localization accuracy, signal-to-noise ratio, large data volumes, and interpretation complexity. Therefore, in engineering practice, DAS is better used in combination with source design, wellbore cable layout, signal processing algorithms, and other monitoring methods to enhance the comprehensive detection of CO2 plume migration, reservoir responses, and potential risk evolution. Overall, DAS not only expands the application boundaries of fiber optic sensing in CGS but also provides critical support for establishing high-spatiotemporal-resolution, full-lifecycle monitoring systems.

Distributed Strain Sensing

Distributed strain sensing (DSS) is a key component of fully distributed fiber optic sensing systems, primarily based on Brillouin scattering, and often implemented using techniques such as Brillouin optical time domain reflectometry (BOTDR) and Brillouin optical time domain analysis (BOTDA) to achieve continuous strain measurement along the entire fiber. , In CGS monitoring, processes such as injection, leakage, caprock deformation, wellbore loading, and reservoir stress redistribution often induce minor strain changes in rock or wellbore structures. Therefore, DSS provides direct mechanical information for wellbore integrity assessment, caprock stability identification, and reservoir dynamic response analysis. , Compared with point-based or quasi-distributed strain measurements, DSS offers long monitoring range, strong spatial continuity, and suitability for long-term online deployment, making it especially suitable for deformation monitoring in elongated or continuous media such as wells, caprocks, and faults. As shown in Figure , fully distributed sensing is based on light scattering effects in the fiber: Raman scattering primarily reflects temperature, Rayleigh scattering mainly responds to vibrations, and Brillouin scattering serves as the core basis for continuous strain inversion, positioning DSS as a critical technology in multifield coupled monitoring.

Schematic of fully distributed fiber optic sensing principles: response mechanisms of Raman, Brillouin, and Rayleigh scattering. Reprinted with permission from ref Copyright 2024 Wiley.

In the area of DSS research, scholars have conducted numerous studies focused on experimental calibration, field validation, and engineering applications. Xue et al. early on established the relationship between Brillouin/Rayleigh frequency shifts and strain through metal plate and sandstone hydrostatic pressure experiments, laying the foundation for subsequent quantitative monitoring. Xue et al. further used a single fiber to continuously measure frequency shifts in sandstone cores under confining and pore pressure in laboratory experiments, demonstrating the feasibility of distributed fibers for continuous strain monitoring in heterogeneous rock samples. Wu et al. integrated Brillouin and Rayleigh techniques to simultaneously monitor temperature and strain during cement hydration, enabling top-of-cement identification and annular anomaly detection. Doonechaly et al. used DTS, DSS, and FBG together to monitor wellbore grouting in an underground laboratory, finding that strain sensing can identify major deformation zones within fault zones.

Overall, DSS provides an important extension in CGS monitoring, shifting focus from detecting thermal anomalies to identifying mechanical responses. Compared with DTS, which focuses on temperature variations, and DAS, which excels at capturing dynamic vibration signals, DSS directly reflects continuous deformation of wellbore, caprock, and reservoir media under fluid perturbations, providing unique advantages for wellbore integrity assessment, caprock stability analysis, fault activity detection, and injection-induced formation response studies. Additionally, DSS can be integrated with DTS, FBG, and conventional pressure monitoring to form a multiparameter temperature–strain–pressure coupled monitoring system, enhancing the interpretation of CO2 plume migration and storage risk evolution. However, DSS in engineering applications still faces challenges such as cross-sensitivity between temperature and strain, complex downhole coupling conditions, stringent calibration requirements, and long-distance signal attenuation. Overall, with improvements in Brillouin sensing precision and multitechnology integration, DSS is expected to play an increasingly central role in full-lifecycle CGS monitoring.

Perspectives and Prospects

From the above analysis, it can be seen that the application of fiber optic sensing in CGS has developed progressively from point-based to quasi-distributed and fully distributed systems, with each technology exhibiting distinct characteristics in terms of monitoring scale, response targets, and applicable scenarios. As shown in Figure , point fiber optic sensing is characterized by high sensitivity, high precision, and strong local specificity, making it suitable for point measurements at critical wellbore nodes, fault-sensitive areas, and core experiments. Quasi-distributed fiber optic sensing achieves multipoint synchronous monitoring through array deployment, providing a balance between precision and spatial coverage, and is suitable for detailed monitoring of critical wellbore layers, pipeline routes, and displacement fronts. Fully distributed fiber optic sensing relies on Raman, Rayleigh, and Brillouin scattering mechanisms to continuously acquire temperature, vibration, and strain information, demonstrating superior holistic perception in reservoir dynamic monitoring, caprock stability assessment, interwell imaging, and long-distance pipeline early warning. Overall, these three technologies are not mutually exclusive but together form a complete technical spectrum, from localized diagnostics to continuous sensing and from single-parameter measurements to multifield coupled monitoring. For complex systems such as CGS, involving wellbore integrity, reservoir migration, caprock safety, and surface risk, a single monitoring technology rarely balances accuracy, spatial coverage, and long-term stability. Hence, establishing a multilevel, multiparameter, and complementary fiber optic monitoring system is key to enhancing storage safety and engineering reliability. Looking forward, the development of fiber optic sensing in CGS will emphasize long-term durability, multiparameter integration, and intelligent interpretation. First, in terms of sensing materials and packaging, it is necessary to improve the durability and stability of fibers and downhole packaging under high-temperature, high-pressure, high-salinity, and CO2-rich environments, reducing attenuation, aging, and coupling failures, thus improving data continuity and reliability at the source. Second, in monitoring modes, future development will focus not on optimizing individual technologies but on coordinated networking across fiber sensing types, using point-based technology for critical node diagnostics, quasi-distributed for key segment monitoring, and fully distributed for continuous sensing from wells to reservoirs, caprocks, and surface facilities, creating a multiscale, three-dimensional monitoring framework. Third, in data processing, as spatiotemporal data volume rapidly increases, traditional empirical interpretation methods are insufficient for fast recognition and quantitative inversion under complex conditions; future approaches should integrate ML, DL, and physics-based modeling to improve anomaly detection, multiparameter decoupling, time-shift imaging, and risk warning accuracy and interpretability. Finally, in engineering applications, it is necessary to establish unified field calibration procedures, performance metrics, and technical standards and enhance deep integration with numerical simulation, history matching, and digital twin platforms, advancing fiber monitoring from “continuous recording” to “intelligent diagnostics” and “decision support”. Overall, with continued advancements in optical fiber materials, demodulation equipment, and intelligent algorithms, full-spectrum fiber optic sensing technologies will play an increasingly central and systematic role in full-lifecycle CO2 storage monitoring.

CO2 Geological Storage Monitoring Scenarios

Wellbore Integrity Monitoring

Wellbore integrity is fundamental to the safe operation of CGS and serves as a critical control link connecting the injection system, the reservoir, and the caprock. − During injection, the casing, cement sheath, casing-cement interface, and cement-formation interface are continuously subjected to temperature changes, pressure cycling, chemical corrosion, and fluid disturbances, which can easily generate microcracks, interface debonding, or localized leakage, thereby creating potential pathways for CO2 migration along the wellbore. , Therefore, the core of wellbore integrity monitoring is not only to detect overt leakage but also to capture early thermal anomalies, strain abnormalities, and dynamic response characteristics, enabling proactive diagnosis of risk evolution. , Figure illustrates typical failure modes of cement sheath integrity, visually demonstrating that wellbore failure often manifests as multi-interface, multimechanism coupled damage.

Schematic of typical failure modes of wellbore cement sheath integrity. Reprinted with permission from ref Copyright 2026 Elsevier.

In the field of wellbore integrity monitoring, researchers have conducted extensive studies from multiple perspectives, including temperature fields, strain fields, and acoustic responses. Zhang et al. integrated DTS with well-reservoir simulations to develop a decision support framework combining leakage detection, interface tracking, and risk warning. Pirrone and Mantegazza used distributed fiber optic temperature data combined with physical modeling to achieve real-time localization of the CO2–brine contact interface in injection wells. Sun et al. employed a hybrid Brillouin–Rayleigh distributed fiber system to quantitatively characterize induced strain around wellbores, confirming that injection rate, pressure, and formation properties significantly control strain magnitude and spatial extent. Raab et al. combined DAS and DTS to monitor the cementing process, finding strong consistency with conventional cement bond log measurements. Su et al. further applied DAS for continuous monitoring of cement hydration and fluid flow, integrating intelligent models to achieve high-precision identification of cement tops and leakage locations.

Overall, fiber optic sensing provides an important technological pathway for wellbore integrity monitoring, shifting from “discrete detection” to “continuous sensing” and from “post-event assessment” to “process diagnostics.” DTS is suitable for detecting thermal anomalies and interface evolution caused by leakage, DSS reflects mechanical responses of formations and cement rings, and DAS excels in capturing cement curing, fluid flow perturbations, and weak leakage signals. The combined application of these three technologies can significantly enhance the comprehensive assessment of casing, cement sheath, and surrounding formation integrity. However, the complex downhole environment, variable sensor coupling conditions, and susceptibility of anomalous signals to temperature–pressure interactions and operational conditions remain major challenges in current engineering practice. Therefore, future wellbore integrity monitoring should emphasize multiparameter joint interpretation, field calibration coupled with numerical modeling, and transition from local failure detection to full-lifecycle risk management.

Reservoir Dynamic Monitoring

Reservoir dynamic monitoring is a core component of the CGS monitoring system, aiming to continuously identify CO2 plume migration paths, pressure front expansion, fluid replacement processes, and changes in reservoir properties during injection. − Compared with wellbore integrity monitoring, which primarily focuses on local structural failures, reservoir dynamic monitoring emphasizes spatiotemporal characterization of postinjection multifield coupled processes, including temperature perturbations, pressure diffusion, wavefield responses, and strain evolution. − Fiber optic sensing technologies, with continuous measurement capability, high spatiotemporal resolution, and suitability for long-term downhole deployment, are well-adapted to this monitoring scenario. − Specifically, DTS can detect thermal anomalies and fluid distribution changes, and DAS can be used for time-lapse vertical seismic profiling (VSP), interwell imaging, and pressure disturbance analysis, while multiparameter integrated monitoring enhances identification of plume boundaries and reservoir heterogeneity. , Figure shows the response characteristics of DAS to reservoir pressure changes and its correspondence with downhole pressure gauge records, demonstrating that fiber optic signals can directly reflect injection-induced reservoir dynamics, providing high-resolution data for reservoir evolution interpretation.

In the field of reservoir dynamic monitoring, researchers have conducted multilevel investigations focusing on CO2 plume imaging, pressure disturbance identification, fluid migration tracking, and reservoir response inversion. Prevedel et al., leveraging the CO2SINK project, established a continuous monitoring system integrating permanent downhole sensors and smart completions, laying the engineering foundation for long-term reservoir observation. In the Ketzin saline aquifer storage pilot, Wuerdemann et al. integrated distributed temperature sensing, resistivity imaging, and seismic monitoring, observing pronounced anisotropic migration of CO2 after injection, with monitoring results aligning well with numerical simulations. In the Aquistore project, Worth et al. integrated 3D seismic surveys, distributed acoustic/temperature sensing, and fluid monitoring techniques, confirming the feasibility of deep saline aquifer storage. Harris et al. further utilized DAS vertical seismic profile data to detect amplitude anomalies associated with CO2 plumes before and after injection, enhancing reservoir response interpretation by combining fluid flow simulations and rock physics modeling. Isaenkov et al. achieved continuous time-lapse monitoring of a 15 kt CO2 injection using permanent sources and a multiwell DAS array, successfully detecting plume signals at the early stage of injection. Glubokovskikh et al. proposed a near-field scattering model based on downhole DAS amplitudes for single-well reservoir change monitoring, indicating that fiber optic sensing is evolving from “anomaly detection” to “quantitative characterization.” Table summarizes representative studies in reservoir dynamic monitoring.

Overall, fiber optic sensing applications in reservoir dynamic monitoring have gradually shifted from simple anomaly detection to multifield coupled characterization and dynamic process interpretation, demonstrating enhanced capabilities from “whether changes occur” to “how and why changes occur.” DTS exhibits stable advantages in injection profiling and thermal anomaly detection, and DAS excels in pressure disturbance sensing, plume imaging, and time-lapse monitoring, while integrated methods combining numerical simulation, history matching, and intelligent algorithms significantly enhance quantitative constraints on reservoir heterogeneity, CO2 saturation, and migration boundaries. However, reservoir dynamic monitoring still faces challenges such as strong geological heterogeneity, complex signal response mechanisms, difficulties in maintaining consistency in time-lapse data, and high complexity in multisource data integration. Therefore, a more rational technical pathway in the future is to focus on the entire reservoir injection process, establishing an integrated monitoring framework centered on continuous fiber optic sensing, supported by numerical simulations and intelligent interpretation, thereby enhancing long-term tracking and prediction of CO2 plume migration, pressure diffusion, and reservoir responses.

Caprock and Fault Monitoring

Caprock and fault monitoring is a critical component in evaluating the safety of CGS, focusing on whether the overlying seal remains intact, whether fault structures are reactivated, and whether CO2 plumes or pressure fronts exhibit anomalous migration along potential weak planes. , Unlike wellbore integrity monitoring, which primarily targets failure of artificial structures, caprock and fault monitoring emphasizes the mechanical stability, fluid containment, and seismic response of natural geological barriers under long-term injection perturbations. As CO2 injection leads to increased pore pressure, redistribution of effective stress, and thermal perturbations, caprocks and faults can exhibit subtle yet continuous strain, thermal anomalies, and microseismic activity, making fiber optic sensing particularly advantageous in this context. In particular, distributed temperature sensing can detect leak-induced thermal anomalies, distributed strain sensing can characterize continuous deformation of caprocks or fault zones, and distributed acoustic sensing is suitable for capturing microseismic events and wavefield changes, thereby providing continuous multiphysical monitoring of storage safety from a “thermal–mechanical–seismic” perspective.

In research on caprock and fault monitoring, scholars have extensively explored seal integrity verification, fault deformation detection, microseismic monitoring, and leak early warning. Liebscher et al., based on continuous pressure–temperature monitoring during the Ketzin demonstration project, confirmed the safety and reliability of the caprock under saline aquifer storage conditions. Nunez-Lopez et al. employed high-density DTS to continuously track interwell flow and overlying layer leakage, showing that combined temperature monitoring with pressure and imaging techniques effectively supports caprock risk assessment. Xue and Hashimoto, using a combined Rayleigh–Brillouin distributed fiber optic method, achieved precise localization and quantitative characterization of target layer deformation, providing a novel approach for caprock and wellbore integrity monitoring. Sun et al. further validated the continuous monitoring capability of injection-induced strain using a hybrid Brillouin–Rayleigh scattering system. Xu et al. experimentally characterized the temperature and strain responses during supercritical CO2 leakage along faults using FBG sensors, finding that internal fault strain was significantly higher than external strain. Li et al. combined time-lapse DAS-VSP, passive microseismic monitoring, and InSAR techniques to identify CO2 migration directions and their impact on regional stability. As shown in Table , current research has established a technological trajectory from continuous thermal anomaly detection to deformation quantification and from microseismic event tracking to multitechnology integrated validation.

Near-Surface Facility Monitoring

Near-surface facility monitoring is a critical component of CGS lifecycle safety, primarily involving continuous surveillance of surface pipelines, wellhead facilities, shallow soil–gas interfaces, and zones of subtle near-surface disturbances. − Compared to reservoir and deep well monitoring, near-surface facilities are more susceptible to temperature fluctuations, background noise, soil moisture variations, and anthropogenic disturbances; however, any abnormal leakage or facility failure can readily trigger environmental hazards and safety incidents. − Therefore, the core objective in this monitoring scenario is the early detection of microleaks, shallow diffusion, abnormal vibrations, and small-scale temperature variations. − Fiber optic sensing technology, with its advantages of long-distance continuous deployment, electromagnetic interference immunity, high sensitivity, and suitability for online monitoring in complex environments, has become a key technical tool for near-surface facility monitoring. − Among these, DAS is suitable for capturing leak-induced noise, vibration propagation, and microseismic responses; DTS can detect localized thermal anomalies caused by leaks; and fiber optic chemical sensors enable in situ continuous measurement of CO2 concentrations. −

In studies on near-surface facility monitoring, researchers have explored buried pipeline leak detection, shallow CO2 release monitoring, soil environmental impact assessment, and near-surface seismic imaging. − Grosswig et al. were among the first to develop a pipeline leak detection system using distributed temperature sensing, demonstrating that the technology can continuously identify slow leaks under both steady-state and transient conditions. Tanimola and Hill further demonstrated that the combined use of DTS and DAS can simultaneously meet the requirements for leak and intrusion detection. In recent years, researchers have focused on applying DAS to monitor microleaks in buried pipelines, revealing how leak aperture, fiber deployment configuration, soil medium, and pressure conditions influence acoustic response characteristics while continuously improving localization accuracy. − Zhou et al. analyzed the temperature variation patterns of high-pressure natural gas leaks from the perspective of Raman scattering-based temperature measurement. Meanwhile, Jechumtalova et al. combined DAS with shallow seismic monitoring to characterize near-surface velocity structures and weak seismic responses of the cap layer, indicating the broad potential of fiber optic technology in near-surface environmental characterization and facility safety monitoring.

Overall, in near-surface facility monitoring scenarios, fiber optic sensing applications have gradually expanded from single-function leak detection to an integrated monitoring approach encompassing leak identification, environmental sensing, structural safety assessment, and shallow-response imaging. DAS excels in monitoring microleaks, vibration propagation, and anomalous events in buried pipelines; DTS offers stable advantages in detecting localized thermal anomalies and slow leaks; and distributed gas concentration sensing provides a complementary method for direct CO2 leak monitoring. The different techniques exhibit clear complementarity: acoustic signals are suitable for rapid detection of dynamic anomalies, temperature signals facilitate identification of persistent leaks, and gas concentration measurements directly support near-surface risk assessment. However, challenges remain, including strong background noise, significant soil heterogeneity, substantial environmental interference, and monitoring thresholds that are strongly influenced by deployment conditions. Therefore, future near-surface facility monitoring should emphasize multimodal fiber optic integration, optimized sensor cable design, and joint interpretation of monitoring data with fluid diffusion models and shallow geophysical methods, thereby enhancing CGS near-surface risk early warning and facility safety assurance.

Perspectives and Prospects

Synthesizing the different monitoring scenarios in this chapter reveals that CGS monitoring requirements exhibit significant hierarchy, coupling, and scenario-specific differences. As illustrated in Figure , wellbore integrity monitoring primarily focuses on failure evolution and local leak detection at the casing–cement–formation interface, requiring sensing technologies with high sensitivity, strong environmental adaptability, and rapid response to early anomalies. Reservoir dynamic monitoring emphasizes continuous characterization of CO2 plume migration, pressure diffusion, and reservoir parameter changes, thereby requiring both large-scale coverage and high spatiotemporal resolution. Caprock and fault monitoring concentrates on the long-term stability of natural barriers and the risk of reactivation of potential pathways, making multiparameter identification using strain, microseismic, and temperature measurements more suitable. Surface pipeline and near-surface leakage monitoring focusses on microleaks, shallow diffusion, and environmental disturbance detection, emphasizing long-distance deployment, real-time alerting, and adaptability to complex environments. Overall, there is no one-to-one correspondence between fiber optic sensing and CGS monitoring scenarios; instead, the underlying logic is that “monitoring objectives determine parameter types, parameter types dictate technology selection, and technology combinations adapt to scenario requirements.” Point, quasi-distributed, and fully distributed technologies have distinct advantages across scenarios, and only through multitechnology integration and multiscale networking can effective continuity be achieved from local diagnostics and key-section tracking to full-domain continuous sensing, ultimately constructing a full-process monitoring system covering wellbores, reservoirs, caprocks, and surface facilities.

Scenario-based fiber optic sensing adaptation framework for CO2 geological storage.

Looking ahead, the development of CGS monitoring scenarios and fiber optic adaptation systems will focus on four aspects. First, monitoring systems will evolve from single-parameter observation to multiphysical coupled sensing, integrating temperature, strain, acoustic, pressure, and gas concentration data to enhance the identification and interpretative reliability of complex anomaly sources. Second, addressing the engineering constraints of different scenarios, fiber deployment, sensor packaging, and downhole coupling processes need further optimization to enhance long-term service performance under high temperature, high pressure, high salinity, and severe disturbance conditions. Third, as DAS, DTS, and DSS data volumes continue to grow, deep integration with numerical simulation, history matching, inversion imaging, and intelligent algorithms is required to transition monitoring results from “anomaly detection” to “mechanistic interpretation” and “risk prediction.” Finally, the scenario adaptation system itself should transition from experience-driven to standardized and systematic approaches, progressively establishing technology selection criteria, performance evaluation metrics, and coordinated monitoring workflows for wellbores, reservoirs, caprocks, pipelines, and near-surface environments. It is foreseeable that future CGS monitoring will no longer be a simple aggregation of individual technical capabilities but will evolve into a comprehensive framework centered on continuous fiber optic sensing, supported by multisource information integration, and aimed at intelligent decision-making, thereby providing a more systematic and reliable technological guarantee for the long-term safety of CGS.

Deep Learning for Fiber Optic Data Processing

As an important branch of ML, DL, with its powerful nonlinear data modeling capability, has become an indispensable tool in fiber optic data processing. The spatiotemporal data acquired by fiber optic sensing systems are often complex and multidimensional, featuring high dynamics and noise interference, and require extraction of valuable information for further analysis and decision-making. Conventional signal processing methods struggle to address these challenges, whereas DL, through multilayer neural network architectures, can automatically identify complex patterns in the data, offering more accurate and reliable processing solutions. Particularly in CGS monitoring, the large volumes of data provided by fiber optic sensing require fast and efficient processing; the application of DL can significantly enhance processing efficiency and accuracy, providing strong technical support for real-time monitoring, anomaly detection, and predictive modeling.

In fiber optic data processing, DL is primarily applied to signal denoising, feature extraction, and anomaly detection. DL can learn higher-level abstract features from large amounts of raw data, making it more precise than traditional algorithms in processing complex subsurface monitoring data. For example, deep neural networks (DNNs) and convolutional neural networks (CNNs) exhibit significant advantages in image data processing, effectively extracting spatiotemporal features from fiber optic signals for anomaly detection and fault diagnosis. In the CO2 storage process, DL can also learn correlations between historical and field data to build efficient models for real-time prediction of CO2 plume evolution or identification of potential leakage points. In summary, DL not only optimizes data processing workflows but also enhances the performance of fiber optic sensing in complex geological environments, providing more intelligent solutions for future monitoring systems.

Existing Challenges and Future Outlook

Fiber optic sensing technology has demonstrated substantial potential for CGS monitoring, but several open scientific and technical problems still restrict its transition from field demonstration to standardized long-term engineering deployment. As summarized in Figure , the current challenges mainly involve multisensor integration, harsh-environment adaptability, high-dimensional data interpretation, and AI-assisted decision-making. These issues are not only engineering constraints but also fundamental barriers to achieving reliable, quantitative, and full-lifecycle monitoring of CO2 storage systems.

Current challenges and future perspectives of fiber optic sensing technology in CO2 geological storage monitoring.

First, the integration of point-based, quasi-distributed, and fully distributed fiber optic sensors remains insufficient. Different sensing technologies are governed by distinct physical mechanisms and are suitable for different monitoring targets. Point sensors provide high-precision local measurements, and quasi-distributed systems enable multinode monitoring, whereas DTS, DAS, and DSS offer continuous spatial information along the fiber. However, a unified framework for coupling these technologies across wellbore integrity, reservoir dynamics, caprock stability, pipeline monitoring, and near-surface leakage detection has not yet been established. As a result, current monitoring schemes often rely on independent sensing modules rather than coordinated multiparameter interpretation, limiting the ability to obtain a comprehensive understanding of CO2 migration and storage risk evolution.

Second, quantitative interpretation under coupled thermal–hydraulic–mechanical–chemical conditions remains challenging. In particular, temperature–strain cross-sensitivity in DSS is a major limitation for accurate deformation inversion. Brillouin-based strain measurements are simultaneously affected by temperature and strain, making it difficult to distinguish mechanical deformation from thermal perturbations during CO2 injection, pressure cycling, and plume migration. Although hybrid Raman–Brillouin systems, collocated DTS/DSS configurations, reference fibers, and physics-constrained inversion models provide possible decoupling strategies, their robustness under heterogeneous geological conditions and long-term field operations still requires systematic validation.

Third, DAS-based monitoring still faces limitations in signal-to-noise ratio and quantitative interpretation. DAS has strong advantages in detecting vibration, acoustic disturbances, microseismic events, and flow-induced signals, but weak leakage-related responses and far-field microseismic signals are often masked by environmental noise, operational interference, and poor fiber-to-formation coupling. The signal quality is strongly influenced by cable type, fiber coating, cement bonding, well completion method, interrogator configuration, and source–receiver geometry. Therefore, improving DAS sensitivity and repeatability remains an important technical barrier, especially for early warning of small-scale leakage, subtle caprock deformation, and low-energy induced seismicity.

Fourth, the long-term stability and durability of downhole fiber optic cables under high-temperature, high-pressure, high-salinity, CO2-rich, and chemically reactive environments remain uncertain. CGS projects require monitoring over years to decades, but fiber coatings, cable armoring, connectors, and bonding materials may degrade under corrosive brine, supercritical CO2 exposure, pressure fluctuations, and thermal cycling. Such degradation can lead to signal attenuation, coupling loss, baseline drift, and sensor failure. Although corrosion-resistant coatings, carbon/polyimide-coated fibers, metal-tube-protected cables, accelerated aging tests, and redundant deployment designs have been proposed, their long-term reliability and failure mechanisms in realistic CGS environments remain insufficiently understood.

Fifth, fiber deployment and coupling reliability are still key obstacles to large-scale application. The monitoring performance of fiber optic systems depends not only on the sensor itself but also on installation quality and mechanical coupling with the target medium. During well completion, cementing, injection, shut-in, and pressure cycling, fiber cables may experience debonding, bending loss, local damage, or changes in coupling conditions. For surface pipelines and near-surface monitoring, burial depth, soil–fiber interaction, temperature fluctuations, and anthropogenic noise further complicate signal interpretation. Therefore, standardized installation protocols, field calibration methods, and lifecycle integrity assessment are needed to reduce uncertainty in monitoring performance.

Finally, data interpretation and intelligent decision-making remain underdeveloped. Fiber optic sensing systems generate large-scale, high-dimensional, and multisource data sets in real time, but traditional processing methods are often insufficient for rapid anomaly identification and risk assessment. ML and DL methods have shown promise in denoising, feature extraction, pattern recognition, and anomaly detection; however, their application in CGS monitoring is constrained by limited labeled data sets, weak model interpretability, site-specific training, and insufficient integration with physical mechanisms. Therefore, developing benchmark data sets, physics-informed ML models, uncertainty-aware interpretation workflows, and standardized performance evaluation protocols remains essential for reliable and transferable fiber optic monitoring.

Beyond technical barriers, field-scale implementation also requires lessons from pilot projects, standardization, and economic assessment. Pilot projects such as CO2CRC Otway, Ketzin, Illinois Basin–Decatur, and Aquistore have shown that permanent or repeat fiber optic deployments can improve injection profiling, DAS-VSP repeatability, thermal anomaly detection, and plume monitoring. Their main successes include dense spatial coverage, continuous monitoring, and lower marginal cost for repeated surveys after permanent installation. However, lessons from these pilots also indicate several limitations, including cable damage or coupling loss during well completion, noisy DAS records, insufficient baseline data, and heavy requirements for data storage and interpretation. From a standardization perspective, CGS fiber optic monitoring should be aligned with ISO 27914/27916 for CGS, relevant API and industry well-integrity guidelines, and fiber-sensor testing or installation guidance from IEC 61757, SEAFOM, and FOSA. Economically, fiber optic systems usually require higher upfront investment than single-point gauges. A downhole monitoring well with permanent fiber and surface interrogator is typically a site-specific capital item on the order of 105 to 106 USD, while surface or pipeline DAS deployment is commonly evaluated at approximately 104 to 4 × 104 USD km–1 when cable installation and system integration are included. Nevertheless, one fiber cable can replace many discrete sensors and support repeated DTS/DAS/DSS measurements, which may reduce lifecycle costs compared with repeated logging, geophone deployment, or 4D seismic surveys.

Overall, the major open problems in fiber optic sensing for CGS monitoring can be summarized as follows: multitechnology integration, temperature–strain decoupling, DAS signal enhancement, long-term cable durability, deployment/coupling reliability, and intelligent interpretation of high-dimensional data. These challenges define the key scientific and technical bottlenecks that must be addressed before fiber optic sensing can become a standardized monitoring technology for full-lifecycle CGS projects. The corresponding actionable recommendations and development pathways are further prioritized in Section 6 according to short-term and long-term implementation horizons.

Conclusions and Recommendations for Future Work

This review systematically summarizes the application of full-spectrum fiber optic sensing technologies in CGS monitoring, covering point-based, quasi-distributed, and fully distributed sensing systems. The sensing principles, performance characteristics, and scenario adaptability of major technologies were analyzed for key monitoring targets, including wellbore integrity, reservoir dynamics, caprock and fault stability, pipeline monitoring, and near-surface leakage detection. Existing studies show that fiber optic sensing provides important advantages for CGS monitoring, such as continuous in situ observation, long-distance deployment, high spatiotemporal resolution, electromagnetic interference resistance, and suitability for harsh downhole environments. Meanwhile, ML and DL offer new opportunities for signal denoising, feature extraction, anomaly detection, and intelligent interpretation of large-scale fiber optic data sets.

To promote the practical application of fiber optic sensing in CGS, future work should be prioritized according to short- and long-term development needs.

Short-term priorities (1–3 years). First, scenario-oriented technology selection guidelines should be developed. Point sensors, quasi-distributed FBG arrays, DTS, DAS, and DSS should be matched with specific monitoring tasks, such as local leakage detection, wellbore integrity assessment, thermal anomaly identification, microseismic monitoring, and caprock deformation analysis. Second, deployment reliability and fiber-to-formation coupling should be improved through standardized installation procedures, field calibration, and baseline drift evaluation. Third, key signal interpretation problems should be addressed, including temperature–strain decoupling in DSS using hybrid Raman–Brillouin systems or colocated DTS/DSS configurations and DAS signal-to-noise ratio enhancement through optimized cable design, improved interrogator settings, and advanced denoising algorithms. Fourth, benchmark data sets and validation protocols should be established to support reliable ML/DL-based data processing and anomaly recognition. Fifth, accelerated aging tests should be conducted to evaluate the durability of fiber coatings, armored cables, connectors, and packaging materials under high-temperature, high-pressure, high-salinity, and CO2-rich conditions.

Long-term priorities (5–10 years). Future research should focus on building integrated and standardized full-lifecycle monitoring systems. Multisensor cooperative networks should be developed by combining point, quasi-distributed, and distributed fiber optic technologies with pressure, seismic, geochemical, and remote-sensing monitoring methods. Long-term downhole fiber systems with corrosion-resistant coatings, redundant cable architectures, and self-diagnostic functions should be advanced for decade-scale operation. In addition, physics-informed ML/DL models and digital-twin platforms should be developed to integrate fiber optic data with reservoir simulation, enabling quantitative risk assessment and predictive decision-making. Finally, unified standards for sensor performance, deployment, calibration, data formats, uncertainty evaluation, and anomaly classification should be established to enhance comparability, regulatory acceptance, and engineering reproducibility.

Overall, fiber optic sensing is expected to become a key technology for safe, intelligent, and full-lifecycle CGS monitoring, provided that deployment reliability, sensor durability, data interpretation, and standardization are systematically improved.
