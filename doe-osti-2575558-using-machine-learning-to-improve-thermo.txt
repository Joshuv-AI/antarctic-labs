Using Machine Learning to Improve Thermostability of MHETase

Steven Mao, Rommel Granja Travez, Ramesh Jha

Abstract:

 Protein engineering is a field which utilizes proteins as tools, which has many useful

applications in medicine, industry, biofuels and more.1 One such protein is MHETase, which is a

protein that plays an important function in the degradation of polyethylene terephthalate (PET)

plastics, which are commonly used in water and soda bottles.2 However, these proteins are

adapted to work in specific conditions, and may not satisfy the desired properties that a new

application would desire, or could be improved. For instance, a more thermostable MHETase

would be more effective in the plastic degradation conditions.3 To make these desired changes,

the primary structure of the protein is mutated, but there are many possible mutations and

positions to mutate to make with the 20 canonical amino acids. Therefore, to narrow down the

possibilities and to make the process of finding a thermostable MHETase variant, we used

sequence design tools that are grounded in machine learning to find mutations that would

improve thermostability of MHETase.4 In particular, we used the tools Protein MPNN and

FireProt to design a more thermostable MHETase enzyme. We then compiled these mutations

into a library and grew these proteins using bacteria colonies, and measured their effectiveness

using a fluorescent protein marker. Thermostable proteins and their marker would fold correctly

and fluorescence would be seen, but if neither folded correctly then there would be no marker

detected. We grew these proteins in bacteria and then intend to use these methods to evaluate

their thermostability.

Introduction:

 1

 Aside from improving the thermostability of MHETase, which is the main focus of this

internship, another focus was to improve the thermostability of another protein family of

endoglucanases, namely Cel9G and Cel9R which can break down cellulose. The optimization of

these proteins through protein engineering could provide a method to improve the breakdown

and decomposition of biomass. Again, protein engineering alters the primary structure, or the

string of amino acids that make up a protein. Once this string is made, it will fold into a specific

shape of the protein which matches its corresponding function. By altering this string, we also

alter how it works and how strong the bonds between different parts of the protein are,

potentially improving the protein function or thermostability. Therefore, this internship will focus

on improving the thermostability of both these two endoglucanases and MHETase. In particular,

I used computational methods to find stronger mutations to improve the thermostability of the

two endoglucanases, while most of the wet work in the internship was spent on the experiments

needed to verify and test the mutations that the machine learning libraries suggested for

MHETase.

 To try and enhance protein activity and stability, it is also important to understand what

the fitness landscape means. Consider that the current effectiveness of a protein is its “height,”

and all of its neighbors are a distance away, and that distance is measured in mutations between

the two variants. By using machine learning, we can find some neighbors of the protein that are

likely to have increased thermostability based on the training data, and then we can test those

neighbors. Then, we take the most effective of those neighbors, find their neighbors, and begin a

greedy uphill walk in order to maximize the efficiency and thermostability of the protein.

Additionally, we can hopefully train an algorithm to identify the commonalities between the

thermostable and extremophile protein, so that way we have a solid understanding of the traits of

 2

the extremely stable proteins, and can use this information to design new proteins or alter

existing ones to have the same levels of thermostability.

Computational Work:

 Most computational work was about finding the most favorable mutations to enhance the

thermostability in the endoglucanases. To first identify mutations, we used the machine learning

tools which are ProteinMPNN and FireProt to generate a library of favorable mutations. Then,

my mentor asked me to develop a ranking of which mutations should be prioritized to test first,

and compare it with his ranking, and the commonly prioritized mutations would be tested later.

To develop this ranking, I first looked at which mutations were most commonly suggested by the

algorithms, the Shannon entropy of a given position, the variation of that position, the number of

flags that the FireProt algorithm gave it, and more. Combining all of these factors, a mutation

that is commonly seen throughout the results that the algorithm returned, and promises to have a

high thermostability based on the computer evaluation, will have a higher score. By isolating the

top 20-30 or so mutations in this way and recording the position and mutation residue, we can

get a picture of what mutations are likely to improve the thermostability of an enzyme and then

test it.

 This computational work took place most of June while I was completing training

courses required by the lab, in order to begin wet work. As said before, ranking the mutations

suggested by FireProt required the development of a score system, and the scores of the

mutations would then be compared to determine the best possible mutations to attempt to

implement. This score is the sum of mutability, variance, and the number of flags that FireProt

assigned to the mutation, where mutability ranks how much/often the wild type residue was

changed, variance is self-explanatory, and the number of flags is a positive indication that the

 3

algorithm assigned to the mutation. Over the following weeks, I developed Python programs

which used the Pandas and NumPy libraries in order to create excel sheets to visualize the

ranking of the proteins, and shared these sheets with my mentor so that he could compare it with

his own rankings. This way, in the future he could determine which ones are most favorable to

test.

 One crucial difference between the way that my mentor and I computed our scores is with

a measure called the FoldX norm which is a value that FireProt gives to a mutation, where a

higher score is less favorable (meaning more unstable) and a lower score is more favorable,

meaning that it is more stable. However, the FoldX score is very variable, as the lowest score

usually goes to around -3 while the highest score can be in the high 30s or 40s. In order to

compensate for these outliers, I used a logarithm formula on the positive scores, in a sense

“compressing” the range of the positive scores, so that the score would become less dependent

on outliers. I believe that it is this difference that made in general my scores to be higher than my

mentor’s. I concluded by writing a program that compared our scores together, as well as which

parts of the scores (mutability, variability) that made our scores different. After troubleshooting

and debugging many of my programs we have a much higher rate of matching mutations at 70%

that are worthy of testing, up from 40% which was the percentage of hits at the start of these

programs. Writing this program returned another set of mutations with different scores, and now

the common mutations that have high scores on both sides can be tested to see their

effectiveness.

 Another focus of these programs is trying to identify the evolutionary pathways that

make an enzyme more thermostable. When making a table with the mutations with the highest

scores, we look at the residue present in Cel9R, which is a thermostable variant of the protein.

mutations compare to the current residue in Cel9R. This way, we can see how the mutations

suggested by FireProt line up with the mutations that were made through evolutionary pressure

to make the proteins in this family more thermostable.

Methods:

 To measure the thermostability of MHETase we use the following process. We first grow

a plasmid containing the MHETase wild type in NEB E. Coli, and then once we have enough

plasmid, we then extract the DNA from the plasmid. We then send this to be sequenced in order

to verify that the DNA we extracted is the correct plasmid backbone. Afterwards, we cut out the

wild MHETase part of the plasmid and replace it with the library which we ordered from TWIST

 6

bioscience. When we successfully transform this into another set of bacteria and grow it, we will

then scrape the colonies and then grow them on a rich medium at a high temperature to isolate

for the selection of thermostable bacteria, grow those, and then evaluate thermostability using the

fluorescent reporter. Again, the glowing reporter will only glow when the MHETase enzyme

itself folds correctly, and this is because proteins in general have their function dependent on

their shape, and even one small misfolding or error in making the amino acids in a protein can

completely alter the shape, thus changing the function and in our case removing the fluorescence

from the fluorescent reporter.

 After this process, we will then purify all our product and then put it through a machine

called a flow cytometer. This is a machine that has a tube that is very slender, so it only allows

one cell, protein, and more to pass through at a time. By shining a light through the current cell

passing through, we can effectively “count” how many times the fluorescent protein successfully

expressed, and therefore how thermostable this variant is. So, using this method, we have a way

to measure the thermostability of the protein.

Wet Lab Work:

 Most of the wet lab work goes through this process, except for giving my mentor a hand

when needed. At the beginning of the wet lab work, I aliquoted the antibiotics that my mentor

and some other colleagues were using, as we were running low, and I made the antibiotic agarose

LB media plates that we would use later too. The reasoning behind these antibacterial media

plates is that only the bacteria that takes in our desired plasmid with the resistance gene will be

able to grow. In this way, we only grow the bacteria that we want to grow, because all others are

killed off by the antibiotics.

 8

 We use this method to grow a plasmid with the wild type MHETase, which we will later

cut out and insert in the library version of MHETase. This library version of MHETase was

generated by ProteinMPNN and bought from TWIST bioscience. We successfully grew the

bacteria that had this plasmid, with a blank control that had no growth (which is a good

indication), and had it sequenced to verify that the plasmid was correct, and the plasmid was the

one that we intended to insert. We grew the bacteria (NEB Turbo) on a LB agar plate with

carbenicillin antibiotic so only our desired plasmid would be there. After this, we used a

restriction digest to successfully cut out the wild type MHETase that was in the plasmid using the

Nhe1 and BstB1 enzymes and added Antarctic Phosphatase to stop the plasmid fragments from

reattaching to each other. This was important as we added in the library through ligation with T4

DNA ligase, as if the plasmid reattached to become a circle again it would be impossible to insert

the library fragment.

library insert that has been digested with the same restriction enzymes will have a much easier

time binding to the backbone. The plan now is to make new digested library, which would be

easier said than done because we have no more of one restriction enzyme, only an older version

which is buffer-incompatible. However, another mentor has found a workaround to this, where

we will use one restriction enzyme to digest, then clean it up using a kit, and then digest with

another enzyme, bypassing the incompatible buffer problem. This workaround does have a big

risk of loss, so I chose an alternative, to borrow Nhe1 enzyme from another lab to use in the time

before our new order of Nhe1 arrives.

Discussion:

 The primary problem that we are facing at this moment is that there is growth on the

control, and that our ligation reaction may not have been done properly. The first time we

attempted to grow the plasmid with the library to test the results, there was very little growth in

the library plates, and we think this could be because during the restriction digest process we

used incompatible buffers, although according to the gel electrophoresis it turned out fine. This

poses a serious question of what went wrong, because then it is very difficult to fix this error in

future experiments. As for this run, because there were so many colonies on the control plate

where there were not supposed to be any colonies at all, we suspect that there may also have

been a problem with the restriction digest that allowed the plasmid to close in on itself. In order

to resolve this issue, we changed the enzymes to the more standard ones that the lab uses.

Additionally, after new library digested insert is made we will start the process again.
