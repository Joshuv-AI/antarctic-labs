Prophylactic mesh reinforcement at emergency midline laparotomy: a systematic review and meta-analysis with pre-specified subgroup analysis of contaminated and dirty abdominal wounds (CDC Class III–IV)
Authors: Ricardo Antonio Gonzalez Jaramillo, Lorena I. Avendaño, Andrea Melissa Briceño González, Brandon Durán Suárez, Jorge Luis Rosales Becerra, Eduardo Jordan Garcia, Carlos Javier Mata Quintero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the efficacy and safety of prophylactic mesh reinforcement (PMR) at the closure of emergency midline laparotomy (EML), with a pre-specified subgroup analysis of contaminated and dirty wounds (CDC class III–IV).MethodsPRISMA 2020-compliant review (PROSPERO CRD420261324766; OSF 10.17605/OSF.IO/ZX2BG; amendments registered before data extraction). PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, Scopus and Web of Science were searched (January 2015–March 2026). RCTs and comparative cohort studies reporting incisional hernia (IH) at ≥ 12 months were eligible; bias was assessed with ROB-2/ROBINS-I. Random-effects meta-analysis used the REML estimator with Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman correction. A pre-specified Borenstein interaction test compared CDC class I–II with III–IV; trial sequential analysis (TSA) and GRADE were applied.ResultsFive studies (4 RCTs; 1 cohort; n = 617) were included. PMR was associated with a reduction in IH (pooled OR 0.221, 95% CI 0.122–0.402 [Knapp–Hartung]; 0.101–0.483 under the conservative truncated variant; I2 = 0%); because control-arm event rates exceeded 10%, this corresponds to an approximate RR of ~ 0.36 (0.28–0.38). The RCT-only analysis was concordant (OR 0.179, 95% CI 0.074–0.435). TSA gave an accrued information size of 617 versus a required ~ 151 (409%), interpreted only as numerical sufficiency, not firm evidence. The CDC interaction was non-significant and underpowered (z = 0.327, p = 0.743). Seroma was significantly increased (k = 4; OR 3.980, 95% CI 1.924–8.234; 1.06–14.94 under the conservative variant), whereas deep SSI was non-significant and underpowered (OR 0.991). GRADE: LOW to VERY LOW.ConclusionPMR at EML closure was associated with a consistent IH reduction across CDC classes; however, contaminated-wound evidence is sparse, the subgroup interaction is exploratory, non-significant and underpowered, and the safety profile — particularly deep SSI — remains uncharacterized. Seroma was significantly increased. Given LOW–VERY LOW certainty, these hypothesis-generating findings should not change practice and support an adequately powered multicentre RCT in CDC class III–IV wounds.Trial registration: PROSPERO CRD420261324766 (registered 23 February 2026); OSF Registrieshttps://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/ZX2BG.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10029-026-03807-3.

Introduction

Incisional hernia (IH) is one of the most common long-term complications after midline laparotomy. Cumulative incidence ranges from 10–30% in unselected surgical populations and exceeds 40% in patients with established risk factors [1, 2]. Emergency surgery is one of the highest-risk settings for IH: rates after emergency midline laparotomy (EML) range from 18 to 54%, reflecting the combined effects of wound contamination, hemodynamic instability, nutritional impairment, and the technical limitations imposed by the emergency setting on fascial closure [3, 4]. Emergency laparotomy is also an independent risk factor for fascial dehiscence, which carries a mortality of up to 20% [5, 6].

Prophylactic mesh reinforcement (PMR) at primary fascial closure is now a well-established strategy for IH prevention in elective high-risk surgery. The five-year follow-up of the PRIMA trial showed IH rates of 24.7–29.8% with PMR versus 53.4% with primary suture [7]. Two large meta-analyses confirmed this benefit: Jairam et al. (k = 12 RCTs; RR 0.35; 95% CI 0.21–0.57) [8] and Aiolfi et al. (k = 14 RCTs; RR 0.38) [9]. On this basis, the updated EHS/AHS guidelines now conditionally recommend PMR in high-risk elective scenarios [2]. The emergency and contaminated-wound setting, however, falls outside current guideline recommendations, representing a fundamental gap in the surgical evidence [2, 10].

Whether PMR can be used safely in contaminated and dirty wounds (CDC class III–IV) is a clinically urgent question. These patients have the highest absolute baseline risk of IH, so the absolute benefit of mesh placement could be greatest precisely in the population where infectious concerns about a prosthetic implant are also greatest. No prior meta-analysis has tested this question with a formal subgroup interaction analysis. The 2023 WSES ECLAPTE guidelines [11] specifically named it as the principal unresolved question in the management of EML closure [10].

Following a prospectively registered protocol (PROSPERO CRD420261324766; OSF 10.17605/OSF.IO/ZX2BG), we performed a PRISMA 2020-compliant systematic review and meta-analysis comparing PMR with suture-only closure after EML (January 2015 to March 2026). The primary IH analysis pooled all wound classes, and a pre-specified interaction test then compared CDC class I–II with class III–IV. One additional eligible RCT (Lima et al., 2020) [12] was included only for 30-day safety outcomes. We expanded the lower bound of the search from the originally registered 2020 to January 2015 (an amendment made before any data extraction), so that the dataset would cover the entire post-STITCH era [13] and capture the full contemporary evidence base.

Methods

Study design and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis were designed, conducted, and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement [14], the Cochrane Handbook v6.3 [15], and AMSTAR-2 [16]. The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261324766; 23 February 2026) and in OSF Registries (10.17605/OSF.IO/ZX2BG), and last amended on 23 March 2026 to (i) pre-specify CDC class III–IV wound contamination as the primary subgroup of interest and (ii) extend the search lower bound from 2020 back to January 2015. Both amendments were made before any data extraction. CONSORT 2010 [17] guided the methodological appraisal of each included RCT. The completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided as Online Resource ESM_2.

In line with PRISMA 2020 transparency requirements, the registered PROSPERO title ("Prophylactic Mesh Reinforcement in High-Risk Midline Laparotomy: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis [2020–Present]") differs from the final manuscript title in three respects: (i) "Emergency" replaces "High-Risk" to describe more precisely the operative setting we studied; (ii) the CDC class III–IV subgroup analysis was pre-specified in the 23 March 2026 amendment, before any data extraction; and (iii) the search lower bound was extended from 2020 back to January 2015 in the same amendment. No data had been extracted at the time of registration or amendment.

To address the single-reviewer limitation noted in earlier versions of this work, title/abstract screening and full-text eligibility assessment were performed independently by RAG-J, BDS, and JLRB; discrepancies were resolved by consensus, with arbitration by CJMQ when required. Data extraction was carried out by RAG-J and verified cell-by-cell against the original published tables by BDS, JLRB, LIA-V, and CJMQ. RAG-J and CJMQ independently reviewed risk-of-bias assessments. All authors read and approved every extracted value and every pooled estimate.

Eligibility criteria (PICO framework)

Population

Adults (≥ 18 years) undergoing emergency or urgent open midline laparotomy for any abdominal indication. Studies confined to hernia repair or to pediatric populations were excluded. Mixed elective/emergency studies were eligible only if emergency-setting data were separately extractable.

Intervention

PMR at the time of primary fascial closure, in any anatomical plane (onlay, retromuscular/sublay, retrorectus, preperitoneal, or intraperitoneal) and with any mesh material (permanent synthetic, absorbable synthetic, biosynthetic, or biologic).

Comparator

Primary fascial closure with suture-only technique, without mesh reinforcement.

Outcomes

Primary: IH at ≥ 12 months (clinical or radiological). Secondary: deep and superficial surgical site infection (SSI; ≤ 30 days), seroma (≤ 30 days), fascial dehiscence, mesh explantation, and 30-day mortality. Studies reporting only short-term outcomes (≤ 90 days) without IH at ≥ 12 months were excluded from the primary analysis but were eligible for the 30-day secondary analyses.

Study designs

RCTs, prospective cohort studies with a concurrent control arm, and retrospective comparative cohort studies. Non-comparative designs and conference abstracts without extractable full-text data were excluded.

Date and language

January 2015 – March 2026, all languages. The 2015 lower bound was selected to coincide with the publication of the STITCH trial [13] and the consequent widespread adoption of the small-bites fascial-closure technique. As noted, this lower bound was amended from the originally registered 2020, before data extraction.

Information sources and search strategy

Electronic searches were conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, Scopus, and Web of Science. ClinicalTrials.gov and the WHO ICTRP were searched for ongoing trials. Reference lists of all included studies and of key systematic reviews were screened by hand. Eight journals were hand-searched for the period 2015–2026: Annals of Surgery, British Journal of Surgery, Hernia, Surgical Endoscopy, JAMA Surgery, Journal of the American College of Surgeons, World Journal of Surgery, and Langenbeck's Archives of Surgery.

PubMed Boolean string (adapted for each database): ("prophylactic mesh" OR "preventive mesh" OR "mesh reinforcement" OR "mesh augmentation") AND ("midline laparotomy" OR "abdominal wall closure" OR "emergency laparotomy") AND ("incisional hernia" OR "hernia prevention") AND (2015:2026[pdat]) AND (humans[MeSH Terms]).

Title and abstract screening was performed independently in Rayyan by RAG-J, BDS, and JLRB. Full-text eligibility was assessed independently by the same reviewers against all pre-specified PICO criteria; disagreements were resolved through discussion and, when needed, arbitrated by CJMQ. Data extraction and verification

A standardized, pre-piloted extraction form was used. RAG-J extracted the data, and BDS, JLRB, LIA-V, and CJMQ verified every value, cell by cell, against the corresponding tables in the original published manuscripts. No values were imputed or estimated. When intention-to-treat (ITT) and per-protocol analyses were both reported, ITT was selected as the primary analysis set. A continuity correction of 0.5 was applied to all cells of studies with zero events in one arm (Mäkäräinen 2025, IH outcome). Lima et al. 2020 [12] reported only the 30-day SSI and seroma analyses; these were excluded from the primary IH analysis because the primary endpoint was fascial dehiscence at 30 days, and IH at ≥ 12 months was not reported. Analysis sets necessarily differed across studies and outcomes (e.g., ITT for Pizza and Ulutas, the 2-year clinically evaluated set for Mäkäräinen, and the as-analysed cohort for Bravo-Salva), reflecting differing follow-up windows and reporting; each extracted cell is documented in ESM_1, and the RCT-only and leave-one-out sensitivity analyses confirmed that this did not materially alter the pooled estimates.

Risk of bias

Risk of bias in RCTs was assessed using the revised Cochrane ROB-2 tool [18], which evaluates five domains: D1 randomization process; D2 deviations from intended interventions; D3 missing outcome data; D4 measurement of the outcome; D5 selection of the reported result. Bravo-Salva 2021 was assessed using ROBINS-I [19] across its bias domains (summarised in Table 2). Lima 2020 was assessed using ROB-2 for the secondary outcomes it included. Assessments were performed independently by RAG-J and CJMQ; disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Statistical analysis

All meta-analyses used random-effects models with the REML estimator. The Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman (HKSJ) variance correction was applied to all confidence intervals, using t-distribution critical values with df = k − 1 [20]. Prediction intervals (PIs) were computed with t(df = k − 2) [21]. Effect estimates are reported as odds ratios (OR), computed with the Woolf log-OR formula; a 0.5 continuity correction was applied to zero-event arms. Heterogeneity was quantified by Cochran's Q and I2, and interpreted with caution at k < 10 [22]. Because the Hartung–Knapp adjustment can produce confidence intervals narrower than the conventional random-effects interval when between-study heterogeneity is low (Q/(k − 1) < 1), all primary and safety estimates were additionally computed with the truncated (modified) Hartung–Knapp variant as a sensitivity analysis, and both intervals are reported.

A pre-specified RCT-only sensitivity analysis (k = 4; excluding Bravo-Salva 2021) was performed for the primary IH outcome. The pre-specified subgroup interaction comparing CDC class I–II with class III–IV was tested with the Borenstein z-test for the difference in subgroup log-ORs (fixed-effect pooling within each subgroup; normal-distribution z for the interaction) [23]; this approach was chosen a priori over the Cochran Q-between test because of its better small-sample behavior at k ≤ 5. A pre-specified exploratory meta-regression of log-OR on follow-up duration is presented in Supplementary Fig. S8. Influence diagnostics included leave-one-out analysis, Cook's-distance approximation, and the Baujat plot (Supplementary Figs. S1–S4). A funnel plot was generated for completeness; formal asymmetry testing was not performed at k < 10 [22].

Trial sequential analysis (TSA) was performed using the method of Wetterslev et al. [24], with α = 0.05 (two-sided), power = 80%, control-arm event rate (CER) = 26%, and relative risk reduction (RRR) = 65%. All analyses were performed in R v4.3.0, using the meta (v6.5–0) and metafor (v4.4–0) packages [Online Resource ESM_1]. The certainty of evidence was assessed with the GRADE approach [25].

Results

Study selection

The study selection process is depicted in Fig. 1 (PRISMA 2020 flow diagram). After deduplication, 892 records were screened by title and abstract. Fifty-one full-text articles were assessed for eligibility; 45 were excluded with reasons documented in Online Resource ESM_2. Lima et al. 2020 [12] met eligibility criteria only for 30-day safety outcomes and were therefore excluded from the primary IH analysis. Five studies [26–30] met all eligibility criteria for the primary IH meta-analysis.

The primary IH analysis included 617 patients (270 mesh, 347 suture). Mesh planes varied: onlay (Bravo-Salva, 2021; Ulutas, 2023; Nageeb, 2021), retrorectus (Mäkäräinen, 2025), and sublay (Pizza, 2021). Four of the five control arms used a 4:1 suture-to-wound-length ratio; Bravo-Salva 2021 used a double-loop PDS suture without the small-bites technique (as explicitly stated in the original publication). Nageeb 2021 was not prospectively registered in any public trial registry and enrolled a substantially younger population (mean age ~ 41 years versus 55–69 years in the other studies). Lima et al. 2020 [12] enrolled patients undergoing emergency laparotomy, mostly for colorectal pathology (77.4%), and contributed only to the secondary outcome analyses.

Trial sequential analysis

TSA was performed with CER = 26%, RRR = 65%, α = 0.05, and power = 80%. The required information size (RIS) was estimated at approximately 151 patients. The accrued information size (AIS = 617; 409% of RIS) exceeded the RIS boundary (Supplementary Fig. S9), suggesting numerical sufficiency for the overall IH effect under the specified parameters. This finding should be interpreted cautiously: the reliability of TSA is limited at k = 5, and the RIS is sensitive to changes in assumptions. With CER = 20% and RRR = 60%, RIS rises to approximately 255 patients, reducing AIS/RIS to 242%. This numerical sufficiency does not, at k = 5, constitute firm evidence and is reported consistently with the LOW–VERY LOW GRADE certainty.

Pre-specified subgroup analysis — CDC class I–II versus III–IV

The pre-specified Borenstein interaction test (fixed-effect pooling within strata; normal z for the interaction) gave z = 0.327, p = 0.743, which is non-significant and indicates no statistically detectable differential effect by wound-contamination class (Supplementary Fig. S5). Both subgroups showed a consistent protective direction: CDC III–IV (k = 3; OR 0.238, 95% CI 0.117–0.486) and CDC I–II (k = 2; OR 0.198, 95% CI 0.083–0.472; subgroup CIs were computed with the pre-specified fixed-effect [inverse-variance] method, consistent with the Borenstein interaction test, rather than with the random-effects HKSJ correction used for the main analyses, which is uninterpretable at df = 1). These analyses are exploratory and severely underpowered. A non-significant interaction does not establish equivalence between contamination classes; it indicates instead that the present evidence is insufficient to distinguish a differential from an equal effect.

Influence diagnostics

Leave-one-out analysis (Supplementary Fig. S4) showed directional consistency across all five iterations (all ORs < 0.25). Bravo-Salva 2021 exhibited the largest positive standardized residual (individual OR 0.288 vs. pooled OR 0.221), pulling the pooled estimate toward the null; its removal produces the RCT-only estimate (OR 0.179). Nageeb 2021 and Ulutas 2023 had the highest Cook's distances (0.215 and 0.183, respectively). The funnel plot is exploratory; formal asymmetry testing was not performed (k < 10 [22]).

Discussion

Summary of findings and place in the literature

This systematic review and meta-analysis of five comparative studies shows that PMR at EML closure is associated with a reduction in incisional hernia (pooled OR 0.221, 95% CI 0.122–0.402; corrected PI 0.111–0.439). The effect is consistent across all five leave-one-out iterations, is preserved in the RCT-only sensitivity analysis (OR 0.179) and remains directionally consistent in both CDC class subgroups (interaction p = 0.743, non-significant and underpowered). TSA indicates numerical sufficiency (accrued information size 617 vs required ≈151; 409% of RIS) under the pre-specified assumptions but does not, at k = 5, constitute firm evidence. The point estimate (RR ≈ 0.36) is in line with the larger elective-surgery meta-analyses [8, 9], which suggests that the emergency context does not materially attenuate the efficacy of PMR. Our findings also align with those of Marcolin et al. [33] (OR 0.18; k = 4 RCTs) and Frassini et al. [34] (RR 0.31–0.44 at 1–4 years; k = 18 mixed designs).

Two methodological features distinguish this analysis from earlier reviews. First, the inclusion of Lima 2020 [12] in the superficial-SSI analysis (k = 4) resolves a spurious significance artefact that had emerged at k = 3 because of near-perfect log-OR homogeneity (anomalously small SE_HK = 0.0244); the corrected k = 4 estimate (OR 1.526, 95% CI 0.717–3.246) is non-significant, in line with the larger literature [8, 9]. Second, the seroma analysis at k = 4 reaches the precision required to detect a statistically significant elevation (OR 3.980, 95% CI 1.924–8.234) that was undetectable at k = 3. Both findings agree with the independently reported estimates of Aiolfi et al. [9] and Jairam et al. [8].

The contaminated-field subgroup

The pre-specified subgroup interaction test (p = 0.743) provides no statistical evidence that PMR efficacy differs between CDC class I–II and III–IV wounds, and both subgroups show a consistent protective direction. This finding challenges the assumption — implicit in current guideline equivocation — that contaminated wounds are a contraindication to PMR. The CDC class III–IV evidence base is, however, limited: three studies (one with ROBINS-I Serious risk of bias, two with premature termination or short follow-up), totaling a mesh-arm n of approximately 132 patients. As an arithmetical illustration only, applying the approximate RR ≈ 0.36 to the control-arm IH rates of Bravo-Salva 2021 (36.6%) and Ulutas 2023 (25.9%) gives estimated absolute risk reductions of approximately 23 and 17 percentage points, corresponding to an NNT of approximately 4 and 6, respectively. These figures are illustrative and should not be interpreted as definitive treatment-effect estimates.

Seroma is the principal safety signal

Seroma emerges as the single most robustly supported clinical safety concern. The k = 4 estimate (OR 3.980, 95% CI 1.924–8.234) agrees with the largest independent meta-analyses [8, 9]. In contaminated fields, the consequences of seroma differ qualitatively from those in elective clean surgery: subcutaneous fluid accumulation around a synthetic mesh in a bacterially colonized wound creates the conditions for biofilm formation, secondary deep infection, and possible explantation. Active seroma prevention should therefore be regarded as a mandatory adjunct: retromuscular rather than onlay positioning to eliminate subcutaneous dead space [35]; meticulous hemostasis; closed-suction drainage; and, in selected cases, negative-pressure wound therapy [34]. (Under the conservative truncated Hartung–Knapp variant this interval widens to 1.06–14.94, so the seroma direction is robust but its precision is estimator-dependent.)

Deep SSI — the critical uncharacterized safety endpoint

Deep SSI in contaminated fields remains essentially uncharacterized. The pooled estimate (OR 0.991; PI 0.184–5.351) spans from roughly fivefold benefit to fivefold harm and is uninformative. With only 12 deep-SSI events across four studies and no contamination-stratified data, the present evidence base provides no meaningful safety profile for this endpoint, which is the principal evidence gap in the field.

Superficial SSI — methodological transparency

The explicit correction of the superficial SSI estimate warrants a brief methodological comment. The k = 3 result arose because three studies with very similar individual log-ORs (0.234, 0.200, 0.149) yielded an anomalously small HKSJ standard error (SE_HK = 0.0244), resulting in apparent statistical significance even though no individual study was significant. Adding Lima 2020 (log-OR = 1.145) introduced between-study variance, raised SE_HK to 0.237, and dissolved the spurious significance. This sequence illustrates the well-known sensitivity of small meta-analyses (k < 5) to the inclusion or exclusion of individual studies, and it highlights the importance of comprehensive evidence synthesis.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings.

First, none of the included primary studies reported within-study CDC-stratified IH data; all subgroup analyses therefore rely on study-level assignment rather than patient-level stratification.

Second, although title/abstract screening, full-text assessment, and data extraction involved multiple reviewers, the primary analyst (RAG-J) performed all statistical computations. The co-authors reviewed but did not independently re-execute these computations. The pooled estimates should be interpreted with this in mind; the full reproducibility dataset (ESM_1) is provided to allow third-party replication.

Third, the most influential CDC class III–IV study (Bravo-Salva, 2021) carries a Serious risk of bias according to ROBINS-I and contributes substantial weight to the primary pooled estimate.

Fourth, PREEMER was terminated at 29.5% of its target enrolment, and the heterogeneity of follow-up duration across studies (12–64 months) limits direct comparability of long-term outcomes.

Fifth, publication bias in the PMR literature is well documented [32], and Nageeb 2021 was not prospectively registered and enrolled a substantially younger population (mean age ~ 41 years) than the other studies.

Sixth, the expanded search (2015–2026) did not identify any additional emergency-laparotomy studies reporting IH at ≥ 12 months. Albendary et al. [36] were excluded because they included studies that did not have IH at ≥ 12 months and used wound failure as a composite endpoint. This reinforces the scarcity of the evidence base.

Seventh, the primary analysis pooled studies that differed in mesh plane (onlay, retrorectus, and sublay) and mesh material (permanent synthetic, partially absorbable, and others); these techniques carry distinct seroma and infection profiles, so the pooled safety estimates should be interpreted as technique-averaged rather than technique-specific, and the small number of studies precluded a formal stratified meta-analysis by mesh plane or material. Eighth, we restricted the synthesis to published, peer-reviewed data and did not contact individual centres or trialists to retrieve unpublished or statistically non-significant datasets; combined with the documented publication bias in this literature [32], this may have biased the pooled effect away from the null.

Finally, TSA numerical sufficiency is sensitive to changes in assumptions and should not be read as establishing definitive evidence in the absence of independent replication.

Conclusions

Prophylactic mesh reinforcement at the closure of emergency midline laparotomy is associated with a statistically significant reduction in incisional hernia (pooled OR 0.221, 95% CI 0.122–0.402; corrected PI 0.111–0.439). The effect is consistent across all leave-one-out iterations and across CDC contamination classes (interaction p = 0.743, non-significant and underpowered). Trial sequential analysis is consistent with numerical sufficiency (accrued information size 617 vs required ≈151; 409% of RIS) under the pre-specified assumptions but, at k = 5, does not by itself constitute firm evidence.

These findings may support the selective, individualized use of PMR in high-baseline-risk contaminated-field patients, weighing the reduction in incisional hernia against the added operative time and technical demands of mesh placement in physiologically compromised emergency patients and the possibility that a retromuscular prosthesis may complicate any future definitive abdominal-wall repair; any absolute-benefit estimate (e.g., NNT ~ 4–6) in CDC class III–IV is illustrative only, given the non-significant, underpowered subgroup. The corrected seroma analysis (k = 4; OR 3.980, 95% CI 1.924–8.234; 1.06–14.94 under the conservative Hartung–Knapp variant) indicates a clinically meaningful seroma signal that demands active prevention through mesh-plane selection (retromuscular positioning to obliterate subcutaneous dead space, where the patient’s physiological condition permits), meticulous hemostasis, and judicious drainage. Superficial SSI is not significantly elevated at k = 4 (OR 1.526, non-significant). Deep SSI in contaminated fields remains entirely uncharacterized and is the principal safety gap. Given the LOW to VERY LOW GRADE certainty throughout, these conclusions are hypothesis-generating rather than practice-defining.

The single highest evidence priority in abdominal-wall surgery is therefore a high-quality, multicentre, randomized controlled trial in CDC class III–IV wounds, with deep SSI and mesh explantation as co-primary safety endpoints, and with adequately powered IH follow-up at ≥ 24 months.
