 Grazing-Angle Estimates for a Crab-Cavity-Driven MCI in the HSR

 Boris Podobedov
 Brookhaven National Laboratory, Upton, NY, 11973, USA
                                   (Dated: September 25, 2026)

 Abstract

A simple MCI scenario based on a fast crab-cavity-driven transverse instability is proposed for
estimating the largest grazing angle at which the stored 275 GeV proton beam can strike a regular
beam-pipe wall in the HSR. Under the deliberately conservative assumptions of failed machine
protection, absent collimation, and an unmitigated instability, the coherent horizontal beam motion
can reach the beam-pipe wall within only a few turns. Using a simplified centroid motion model
together with the HSR lattice functions and piecewise-constant horizontal aperture, a local upper-
envelope estimate gives grazing angles below approximately 5 mrad in the regular FODO arcs and
below 9 mrad outside a region extending 100 m on either side of IP6.

 1. PURPOSE AND ACCIDENT SCENARIO

 The purpose of this note is to define a simple maximum credible incident (MCI) for estimating
the largest grazing angle at which the stored 275 GeV proton beam can strike a regular beam-pipe
wall. The focus is to provide an estimate of the maximum grazing angle around the ring for use as
input to radiation-protection Monte Carlo simulations.
 The following deliberately conservative assumptions are made:

 • the machine-protection system does not abort the beam;

 • the collimators are not inserted;

 • the physical aperture in each constant-cross-section beam-pipe section is represented by its
 local horizontal half-aperture a(s); tapered transition sections are excluded;

 • a fast transverse instability driven by the fundamental mode of a crab RF cavity is allowed
 to develop without stabilization.

 The exclusion of tapered sections is intentional. At a tapered wall, the grazing angle is measured
relative to the local wall orientation, so a sufficiently steep taper can dominate over the milliradian-
scale beam angle considered here. Some presently retained chamber transitions have taper angles
as large as 30◦ . Such regions therefore require a separate treatment and are not included in the
regular-pipe grazing-angle estimate. Importantly, these tapered transitions occupy only a very
small fraction of the total HSR beam-pipe length, and none are present in the regular arc sections.
 For context, several other credible failures that can cause beam loss at store are briefly considered
below. A failure of a dipole or corrector generally changes the magnetic field on a time scale
much longer than the revolution period. The resulting closed-orbit excursion therefore develops
approximately adiabatically, so that first contact with the beam pipe occurs as the orbit gradually
moves onto the aperture and, at least initially, produces a very small grazing angle. Vacuum-related
failures also typically develop on a time scale much longer than a revolution period. A moving
physical obstruction changes the available aperture only slightly from turn to turn, again leading
to a shallow initial interception, while a pressure rise primarily produces distributed beam-gas
losses rather than a localized grazing loss. A pulsed-kicker failure can produce a substantially
larger transverse angle, but normally affects only a fraction of the stored beam. The kicked beam
then executes betatron oscillations around the ring. Since the available HSR kick is not sufficient

 2

to produce immediate interception, the resulting losses are expected to develop over multiple turns
and locations rather than being concentrated in a single regular-pipe region. Taken together, these
failures do not naturally combine rapid development, coherent motion of a large fraction of the
stored beam, and a localized loss at a significant grazing angle.
 By contrast, a strong crab-cavity-driven transverse instability, as studied in Ref. [1], is particularly
important for the present purpose because it combines a very short timescale with coherent
transverse motion of a large fraction of the stored beam. If protection and collimation are absent,
the instability can drive the beam core directly into the beam-pipe wall within only a few turns. If
one aperture restriction dominates the first interception, a large fraction, and potentially nearly
the entire stored beam, can consequently be lost in the corresponding localized region.
 This does not imply that the crab-cavity instability is the most probable machine failure. Rather,
under the assumptions above, it is a strong candidate for a bounding credible incident producing a
localized regular-pipe loss with a significant grazing angle. A conservative local upper-envelope
estimate for this grazing angle is developed below using the lattice beta and alpha functions,
without requiring the detailed betatron phase advance. Incorporating this phase advance could
yield a more restrictive estimate, but would rely on linear-optics phase relations remaining accurate
at the large amplitudes of interest. Since magnet nonlinearities may modify these relations, we
avoid imposing additional phase-dependent restrictions on the possible first-interception locations.

 4. CONCLUSION

 Under the deliberately conservative assumptions of failed machine protection, absent collimation,
and an unmitigated crab-cavity-driven transverse instability, the coherent beam motion can grow
to the beam-pipe wall within only a few turns. This failure is therefore a strong candidate for a
bounding localized beam-loss MCI.
 Within the simplified centroid-only instability model, and assuming a piecewise-constant beam-
pipe aperture, an upper-envelope estimate of the grazing angle was derived around the ring and is
presented in Fig. 2. The bound constrains the coherent amplitude using survival through a regular
arc section on the preceding turn, without requiring the detailed betatron phase advance. The
estimated grazing-angle envelope remains below approximately 5 mrad in the regular FODO arc
sections, where a = 31.5 mm, and below 9 mrad outside the IR6 exclusion region, which extends
100 m on either side of IP6. The overall maximum is about 26.6 mrad and occurs inside IR6.
 We emphasize that the grazing-angle estimates presented here apply only to horizontal beam
motion. In the vertical plane, no corresponding mechanism has presently been identified that would
produce a first interception in the regular arc sections.
 A more restrictive estimate could be obtained by requiring each candidate impact to be a
globally feasible first interception, using the detailed lattice phase advance and upstream aperture
constraints. This may result in a smaller maximum grazing angle and/or exclusion of additional
regions of the vacuum chamber as possible first-hit locations. However, because such a calculation
is more model-dependent at the large amplitudes of interest, it is not pursued here.
 As the estimated grazing angle approaches the milliradian level, further refinement becomes
difficult because beam-pipe alignment errors and closed-orbit offsets and slopes modify the first-
intersection geometry, while finite beam size and divergence broaden the loss location and grazing-
angle distribution. Estimates well below 1 mrad therefore require a more detailed treatment of the
actual beam-pipe geometry, orbit, and finite-emittance beam distribution.
 If further refinement is needed, particle tracking would be the natural next step beyond the
approach presented here.
