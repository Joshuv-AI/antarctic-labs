Disclosing the costs of co-holding liquid assets and high-interest debt has limited impact on behavior
Authors: Rafael M Batista, Ella Mao, Abigail B Sussman, Neale Mahoney, Jessica Min, Michael Haliassos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhy do consumers simultaneously maintain low-yield liquid assets and high-interest revolving debt? This behavior, known as “co-holding,” affects 23% of credit card users in our sample from a major international bank and costs the typical co-holder hundreds of dollars annually in unnecessary interest charges. Our analysis of 38 months of detailed banking records reveals that co-holding is remarkably persistent, with typical co-holders maintaining this behavior for most months observed. Our analysis also reveals that co-holding is not a static financial position: co-holders regularly deposit and withdraw from asset accounts while simultaneously making new credit card purchases. To test whether co-holding could be addressed through information disclosure, we conducted a large-scale field experiment (n=125,328), providing clear information about co-holding behavior and its costs. Customers received targeted messages through their bank’s mobile app, where they could easily transfer money from assets to pay down debt. Despite sufficient power to detect economically small effects, we found no meaningful changes in debt repayment amount, though customers did respond in other ways—making more frequent repayments and paying above required minimums. These results challenge explanations based on limited attention or information gaps and suggest that simple information disclosure, even when carefully designed and delivered through trusted channels, may not effectively address costly financial behaviors.Keywords: household finance, field experiment, information disclosure, consumer debt

Nearly one in four credit card users “co-hold” low-yield liquid assets alongside high-interest debt. By paying >10% interest on their debt while earning <1% on assets, co-holders leave millions of dollars on the table annually. Policymakers and financial institutions often rely on information disclosure to address such costly consumer behaviors. We tested this approach in a preregistered field experiment with 125,328 co-holders at a major international bank, providing clear information about co-holding and its costs. Despite evidence that customers responded to the intervention, we found no meaningful change in debt repayment. These robust null results challenge theories that costly financial behaviors stem from lack of awareness, providing evidence instead for deliberate decision-making that information disclosure alone cannot address.

Introduction

Household debt around the world is at unprecedented levels, with total household debt surpassing national GDP in many countries (1). This trend might suggest that consumers are stretched thin financially. Yet among households carrying high-interest credit card debt, a considerable fraction also maintain low-yield liquid assets (2, 3). This “co-holding” of assets and revolving debt is puzzling because although consumers earn roughly 1% on their liquid assets, they pay upwards of 10% interest on their unpaid debt.

The costs are substantial. The typical co-holder in our sample maintains about AUD$2,000 in both assets and debt, earning AUD$15 annually while paying AUD$245 in interest—a net cost of AUD$230 that could be eliminated if they used their cash to pay down debt. In our field experiment sample alone (over 125,000 individuals), this amounts to nearly AUD$30 million left on the table annually. As we observe, individuals are borrowing money from the same bank where they keep their savings, effectively paying to borrow their own money.

Why do individuals co-hold? Co-holding has been empirically documented for over two decades (4), yet the puzzle remains. Early survey evidence established that cardholders generally understand credit card terms such as APRs and fees (5), leading researchers to propose models where co-holding emerges as purposeful behavior. For instance, consumers may maintain liquid assets as precautionary savings in case credit becomes unavailable (6, 7), or to ensure access to cash for transactions where cards cannot be used (8). These accounts assume consumers make deliberate, informed decisions despite the financial costs.

More recent work examining the psychology underpinning these decisions suggests co-holding may result from systematic mental accounting processes (3, 9, 10). This research proposes that consumers mentally separate their savings from their debt, treating money held in different accounts as nonfungible. Consistent with this view, interventions encouraging savings have been shown to increase checking account balances but leave credit card debt unchanged (3), demonstrating that savings and debt exist in separate mental accounts. This mental segregation may help explain why co-holding persists even when costly, as consumers do not mentally connect their savings with their debt.

Together, this body of work treats co-holding as a deliberate strategy made by consumers who generally understand credit card terms and mentally segregate their savings from their debt. However, these accounts rely on a crucial assumption, namely, that consumers are actively aware of their behavior at the moment of decision. Indeed, a simpler explanation for co-holding is that consumers are unaware of their behavior, either because they fail to attend to their assets and debt simultaneously (11, 12) or because they fail to appreciate how much it is costing them (13–15). Co-holding may arise, for example, if consumers overlook their asset balances when making decisions about debt, and vice versa (16–18), or if they neglect the interest rates (19, 20). Even attentive consumers may fail to appreciate the costs of co-holding because the costs of compounding interest are difficult to calculate (15, 21). We test whether these information gaps drive co-holding by providing consumers with explicit feedback. If the practice stems from oversight rather than a deliberate strategy, clear disclosures about their overlapping balances and associated costs should change how they allocate their funds (3, 20, 22–25). While past empirical work has examined co-holding by promoting savings and measuring its effect on debt (3) or surveying general knowledge of credit terms (5), our intervention directly targets awareness of the co-holding behavior itself. By disclosing this information to consumers we knew to be co-holding, we provide an experimental test of the foundational assumption underlying existing models: that consumers are already aware of their co-holding behavior and its costs at the moment of decision.

Using a unique dataset from the Commonwealth Bank of Australia, we provide novel empirical insights into co-holding behavior through two complementary analyses. First, we examined 38 months of detailed transaction data from over 2,300 customers, revealing that co-holding is both prevalent and persistent. The typical co-holder maintains this behavior for most months in our observation period, often in spells lasting six consecutive months. Moreover, co-holders show substantial month-to-month variation in asset and debt balances, indicating that these accounts are actively used rather than dormant. Second, we tested whether limited attention drives co-holding by conducting the first large-scale randomized field experiment (n=125,328) specifically designed to target co-holding behavior. The experiment provided customers who were co-holding with clear information about their behavior and its costs through the bank’s mobile app where customers could readily act on the information (26). Despite sufficient power to detect economically small effects, we find no meaningful changes in debt repayment amount, though customers in the treated conditions did respond in other ways—making more frequent repayments and paying above required minimums.

These findings have two key implications with distinct theoretical and policy significance. First, limited attention is unlikely to be the primary driver of co-holding, even if we cannot rule it out for some customers. Combined with banking records showing that co-holding is persistent and systematic, these results suggest that co-holding reflects a deliberate decision rather than naive behavior (7, 10, 27). Second, and perhaps more consequentially for policy, information disclosure interventions—even when carefully designed and delivered through trusted channels at actionable moments—are insufficient to change this costly financial behavior. For researchers, this points toward the need for better understanding consumers’ underlying preferences and decision-making processes. For policymakers, it raises important questions about when information-based interventions are likely to be effective and what alternative approaches might be needed to influence this behavior.

Results

We present our findings in two parts. First, we analyze administrative banking data to characterize the prevalence, persistence, and costs of co-holding behavior. Second, we report results from our large-scale field experiment testing whether information disclosure affects debt repayment.

In this study, co-holding refers to maintaining AUD$500 or more in high-interest revolving credit card debt (i.e. interest-incurring portion of balance) while simultaneously holding AUD$500 or more across low-yielding liquid asset accounts (savings plus transaction accounts; see SI Appendix). Co-holders are customers who maintain this pattern for at least three consecutive billing periods—the definition used to select participants for our field experiment.

Analysis of banking data

To understand broader co-holding patterns, we analyzed data from a sample of bank customers who participated in a field survey conducted for another project where they consented to sharing anonymized banking data for this research (N=2,307; 979 co-holders and 1,328 non-co-holders (NCHs); see (9)). This consent enabled access to transaction-level data spanning January 2019 to February 2022 for this subset of customers. We refer to this sample as the “detailed records sample” to distinguish it from the field experiment sample.

Prevalence and persistence

Co-holding affects approximately 23% of credit card users at the bank we partnered with (7% of all customers who primarily bank with this institution).

To track the proportion of months a customer was co-holding, we created an indicator for months where the co-holding amount was at least AUD$500. Most co-holders who participated in our detailed records sample were co-holding most of the time. In fact, co-holding extended well beyond the 3-month window we set to select customers into the sample. Over the 38-month observation period, the median co-holder was co-holding for 23 months (IQR: 13.2–32.0 months). A closer look at the trends suggests that co-holding for these customers came in fits and starts: the median co-holder experienced three distinct co-holding periods (IQR: 2.0–5.0), with each period lasting an average of six consecutive months (IQR: 3.1–14.0 months). In contrast, the median NCH was never observed co-holding (IQR: 0–1.8 months) during the same period.

Economic costs

Co-holding is not only prevalent and persistent; it is also costly. In the months where we observe co-holding (i.e. co-holding amount > AUD$0), the median amount concurrently held in assets and debt was AUD$1,976 (25th–75th percentile: AUD$759–$4,432; M=AUD$3,848; SD=AUD$5,830). A back-of-the-envelope calculation suggests that co-holders could have saved upwards of AUD$230 in interest per year by transferring cash to pay off the revolving debt. Furthermore, over a third of the co-holders had more assets than debt (i.e. “Net Savers”), meaning these customers could eliminate their credit card debt entirely with the money in their asset accounts and still have liquidity left. Extrapolating these costs to all the co-holders at this institution suggests an arbitrage opportunity well over AUD$30 million per year.

Account usage and additional characteristics

A critical question for understanding co-holding is whether it reflects static financial positions—such as maintaining a minimum amount in savings for emergencies or holding old debt from large past purchases—or regular financial decisions. If co-holding were static, we would expect to see relatively stable balances in at least one account type. Instead, our analysis reveals that co-holders actively use both their asset and debt accounts.

Co-holders experience substantial variation in both their asset and debt balances, indicating these accounts are actively used. We see evidence of this in two ways: measuring month-to-month changes in balances and calculating coefficients of variation (CV; (28)). Co-holders show average month-to-month changes of 23.4% in asset balances and 16.9% in credit balances, with average CVs of 0.43 and 0.18, respectively. For comparison, NCHs show an opposite pattern—more variation in credit card balances (Mean Percent Change: 25.4%; Mean CV: 0.80) than in asset balances (Mean Percent Change: 0.3%; Mean CV: 0.27). This volatility indicates that co-holders regularly deposit and withdraw from savings while continuing to use their credit cards for new purchases.

Co-holders also differ from NCHs in other ways. Co-holders maintain higher credit card balances and lower savings balances than NCHs, and they show higher credit utilization rates despite having higher credit limits. Some of this utilization gap is mechanical: many NCHs pay their balances in full each month and therefore carry very low revolving debt by construction. But the gap is also consistent with precautionary liquidity motives (wanting to hold cash on hand for emergencies) and with self-control models in which high utilization is a deliberate strategy to cap one’s capacity to spend. However, our descriptive data cannot adjudicate between these explanations. Co-holders are also more likely to hold personal loans and home loans, which, together with the higher utilization rates, suggests they are more comfortable carrying debt. A supplemental survey conducted online with a different sample of co-holders and NCHs offers further support for these points (see SI Appendix).

Although these patterns reveal that co-holders actively engage with their finances, they leave open the question of whether customers understand the financial implications of their behavior. We therefore conducted a field experiment to test two primary hypotheses: (i) that co-holders are unaware of their behavior, and (ii) that they do not fully appreciate its costs.

Field experiment

If consumers are co-holding simply because they are unaware they are co-holding, we would expect their behavior to change once they receive information about their behavior and what it costs them. To test these hypotheses, we conducted a large-scale field experiment with a larger sample of 125,328 customers whom we observed to be co-holding (124,251 analyzed after minor attrition unrelated to treatment condition; SI Appendix). Customers assigned to treatment conditions received notifications through the bank’s mobile app reminding them they had money available to repay their outstanding credit card balances. The experiment employed a preregistered, three-arm randomized design: a control group that received no notification, a “Notice” group that received a simple reminder about their available assets, and a “Notice + Cost” group that received the same reminder plus an explicit comparison showing the interest cost of AUD$100 in unpaid debt versus expected returns from AUD$100 in assets. Given that we found no differences between the two treatment conditions, we collapse them in our primary analysis. The intervention was delivered at a moment when customers could readily act on the information, appearing when they opened their banking app during 2020 February.a The field experiment was preregistered with the AEA RCT Registry (doi.org/10.1257/rct.5751).

Primary outcome

We find no evidence that providing information about co-holding behavior led to meaningful changes in debt repayment, our preregistered primary outcome of interest. Customers who received notifications paid AUD$19.63 (95% CI [−AUD$7.86, AUD$47.12]), or approximately 1.4%, more on average than those in the control group (Treatment Mean=AUD$1,383; Control Mean=AUD$1,364, p=0.162, Figure 1). This difference was neither statistically significant nor economically meaningful since the average outstanding balance was approximately AUD$6,500. The estimated effect remains essentially unchanged when we adjust for demographics and pretreatment controls (β=AUD$16.78, 95% CI [−AUD$6.68,AUD$40.24]; SI Appendix), suggesting the null effect is robust to observable customer characteristics including age, gender, financial wellbeing scores, and pretreatment saving and borrowing behavior. Notably, the treatment effect remained consistently null when examined by subgroups, including across gender, age, net balance, and the frequency of app logins (SI Appendix). A matched-pairs analysis using customers’ preexperiment financial behaviors and demographic characteristics confirms our primary findings: treated customers showed no greater debt repayment than their matched controls (β=AUD$41.78, SE=AUD$72.48, p=0.56; SI Appendix).

Evidence of engagement

Digital trace data and behavioral outcomes both indicate that, on average, treated customers attended to and engaged with the intervention. The notification card was displayed to approximately 83% of customers assigned to a treatment condition; 35% expanded it to view the full content (42% of those for whom it was displayed); and 5% clicked “Pay Now,” which navigated them directly to a transfer screen (14% of those who expanded the message; see SI Appendix). On behavioral measures, treated customers made more frequent payments (MTrt=3.21) than the control group (MCtl=3.16; Z=4.48, p<0.001) and were significantly more likely to pay amounts above the contracted minimum (PropTrt=83.8%; PropCtl=83.0%; adjusted p<0.001). Because these are intent-to-treat estimates from a randomized design, the nonzero effects on payment behavior are unlikely to arise by chance if the message went entirely unnoticed. The null effect on repayment likewise persists among customers for whom we are more confident of message exposure. For instance, among the 118,708 customers (treated and control) who logged into the app during the experiment month, and who therefore had the opportunity to see the notification, the treatment effect is β=AUD$2.11 (SE=AUD$24.00; SI Appendix). In the matched-pairs analysis described above, which restricts treated customers to those who clicked through to the transfer screen, we again find no difference relative to matched controls.

Secondary outcomes

For most secondary outcomes, the evidence is too limited to rule out a null effect. Treated customers were more likely to repay more than their previous three-month average (PropTrt=0.393 vs. PropCtl=0.382; adjusted p<0.001), but we observed no statistically significant differences in credit card balances, interest charges, savings account balances, or net financial benefit (all adjusted ps>0.05; SI Appendix).

Across outcomes, however, point estimates consistently move in the direction one would expect from an intervention designed to reduce co-holding—repayment amounts increase, debt balances decrease, savings balances decrease. But the effects are small—1–3% relative to the control mean—and for most measures statistically insignificant even in this large sample. This includes interest charges: treated customers made more frequent payments, which should reduce average daily balances and the interest accrued on them, yet the estimated reduction was negligible (Diff: −AUD$0.28; Control Group Mean=AUD$86.53; SI Appendix).

Taken together, these findings show consistent evidence of customer engagement with the intervention alongside small or null effects on the underlying co-holding behavior.

Discussion

Our analysis reveals that co-holding is a prevalent, persistent, and costly behavior exhibited by approximately 23% of credit card users at a major international bank. The median co-holder maintained this pattern for 23 of 38 months observed, holding a median of AUD$1,976 simultaneously in assets and debt, with estimated annual interest costs exceeding AUD$245. Despite these substantial costs, our large-scale field experiment shows that providing clear, actionable information about co-holding and its costs does not meaningfully change debt repayment behavior, our primary outcome. We do, however, observe increased payment frequency and higher likelihood of paying above minimum amounts, suggesting that customers on average engaged with the intervention but ultimately did not meaningfully alter their patterns of co-holding.

Our findings contribute to a growing literature on when and why information interventions succeed or fail (19, 20, 22, 29, 30). While information disclosure can be effective when behaviors result from genuine oversight or complexity (23, 31), our results suggest it may be insufficient to curb co-holding. Even when we explicitly showed customers their behavior and its costs, debt repayment barely changed.

The null result, combined with evidence that, on average, customers noticed and responded to our messages, argues against inattention as the primary driver of co-holding. Customers who simply did not realize they were paying avoidable interest would have reason to act decisively once told, yet we observe only modest shifts in payment frequency and the likelihood of paying above the minimum, with no meaningful change in the total repaid. Though we cannot rule out inattention for the subset of customers who did not engage with the notification, the null effect is robust across multiple outcomes, demographic groups, and analytical approaches, and our design has sufficient statistical power to detect even small effects, indicating that information alone does not meaningfully change this costly behavior in practice. Our banking data and supplemental survey of an independent set of co-holders hint at why. When asked directly whether they were aware they could save money by using their cash to pay down credit card debt, 76% of self-identified co-holders reported that they already knew, and only 7% said they did not (for supplemental survey, see SI Appendix). Even when aware of the trade-off, customers appear to derive enough value from co-holding to outweigh the financial costs. Before asking how to reduce co-holding, institutions should consider whether doing so is always desirable. Several models in economics and finance attempt to rationalize co-holding (6, 8, 32), resting on the assumption that co-holders are aware of their behavior. Our paper provides empirical support for these theoretical accounts. Our findings also suggest that consumers may view the monetary cost of co-holding as a price they pay for something they value, be it a system of mental accounts, preferences over different payment methods, peace of mind, or a sense of responsible spending (3, 9, 10, 33, 34).

This reframing matters for policy. If co-holding reflects an impulsive failure to act on known information, interventions that create friction or automate debt repayment may be warranted. But if it reflects a deliberate strategy, even a costly one, the same interventions risk overriding consumers’ choices, possibly at the expense of outcomes we do not observe in their asset and debt balances. Our supplemental survey evidence is consistent with both accounts: co-holders described a range of motivations, including emergency preparedness and deliberate “bucketing” of funds, yet also reported worse self-control than NCHs (see SI Appendix; also (27)). Disentangling these accounts requires a stronger counterfactual than existing data afford. An ideal design would randomly eliminate co-holding and measure downstream outcomes beyond assets and debt. This remains an open empirical question.

Understanding what drives individuals to co-hold matters for institutions as much as for researchers. Co-holding is clearly profitable for firms in the short run as it generates interest income and incurs little credit risk from consumers who also hold liquid assets. Yet several factors should motivate banks to take up the issue. First, co-holding may be a symptom of financial strain. Customers who carry avoidable financial costs over extended periods may be at greater risk of financial distress and default. In our context, co-holders were nearly twice as likely to be in arrears as NCHs. Second, co-holding invites regulatory scrutiny: regulators in several markets have moved toward requiring that financial products demonstrably serve customers’ interests, and co-holding at scale is a natural candidate for review. But as our results suggest, intervening on the assumption that co-holding is a “mistake” warrants caution. Proactive attention to co-holding allows institutions to shape the terms of any intervention rather than respond to mandated regulation. Third, co-holding surfaces an unmet need: in competitive banking markets, efforts to improve customers’ financial well-being build trust and reduce churn, outcomes that compound over a long-term customer relationship. If co-holding reflects what consumers want rather than what they overlooked, the more durable opportunity may be a product that meets those demands at lower cost. One implication of this is that financial institutions need not choose between consumer well-being and profitability.

For institutions that choose to act, the findings here suggest that information alone is not enough. For instance, rather than disclosing costs, interventions might target the beliefs underlying what individuals value about maintaining liquid assets alongside debt (e.g. (9)). Alternatively, banks could address the structural tension that makes co-holding costly rather than attempting to change individual behavior directly (35, 36)—developing products that allow customers access to their liquid assets while reducing the cost of borrowing. An instructive model already exists in the Australian mortgage market, where “offset accounts” allow borrowers to reduce interest costs while keeping liquid balances accessible. Similar products for revolving credit, such as checking accounts that offset credit card balances at reduced interest rates, could minimize the financial costs of co-holding without requiring consumers to give up access to their liquid assets. Such arrangements would also reduce credit risk to the lender, as customers would effectively collateralize a portion of their outstanding balance.

These findings come with important caveats, particularly regarding the timing of the field experiment. Our experiment ran from 2020 February 17 to March 18, with most messages delivered during the first week. This largely preceded the major COVID-19 disruptions in Australia: while the first case was documented on January 25, the major economic policy responses were not enacted until March 18, the final day of the experiment. That said, we cannot rule out that early pandemic uncertainty affected how individuals responded to our intervention. To the extent the data allow us to assess this, the evidence is reassuring. Consistent null effects across demographic and financial subgroups show no pattern one might expect if COVID-19 had differentially affected treated and control customers (SI Appendix). The detailed banking records analyzed earlier in this paper offer one additional check. Daily spending, asset, and debt flows between 2020 January and March tracked closely with the same window in 2019 and 2021, with meaningful divergence appearing only near the end of March (SI Appendix, Fig. S6).

Beyond the COVID-19 timing, our study shares the external validity considerations common to all field experiments conducted in specific institutional and temporal contexts. For instance, our intervention was conducted at a single financial institution in Australia during a specific time period. We believe the ecological validity of our findings is bolstered by the fact that the Commonwealth Bank of Australia is the country’s largest bank and serves as the main financial institution for more than one-third of Australians. Nevertheless, replication across different markets and economic conditions would help establish whether the patterns reported here hold more broadly. Additionally, our intervention was relatively light-touch, appearing at most twice per customer during a single month. Although our engagement and behavioral measures indicate that the message reached a meaningful share of customers (see Evidence of Engagement), more intensive interventions might yield different outcomes. These findings suggest that interventions that simply give people information are not enough to change costly financial behaviors like co-holding. Effective interventions must, instead, be designed to work with the psychological functions this behavior serves in people’s lives. Understanding these functions—and designing interventions that respect rather than override them—remains a crucial task for behavioral science and financial services.

Methods

Analysis of banking data

We partnered with a major Australian retail bank to analyze de-identified banking records. The banking data analysis used two distinct samples: a representative customer sample to establish the prevalence of co-holding and a smaller sample who met a prespecified set of eligibility criteria and for whom we had detailed transaction-level data, the detailed records sample (SI Appendix).

To establish the prevalence of co-holding, we examined a representative sample of “Main Financial Institution” (MFI, SI Appendix) customers (N=100,000) in January 2020.b Approximately 32,400 were credit card users, including around 7,400 who were co-holding. Summary statistics for this representative sample were calculated by the bank’s analysts and provided to the research team. These statistics provide a sense of how our selective sample compares to the overall banking population: the detailed records sample represents more financially engaged customers, with higher credit limits, account balances, and greater usage of multiple financial products, consistent with the selection criteria (Table 1).

For the descriptive analysis, we obtained direct access to individual-level banking records from 2,307 customers (979 co-holders, 1,328 NCHs) who consented to sharing more granular records when participating in a payment preference survey (see SI Appendix). Eligible customers met a set of key criteria including being the primary holder of a retail or personal credit card, being digitally active on the bank’s smartphone application, having no automatic full-balance repayment arrangements, and not being in financial hardship or arrears (see SI Appendix for complete eligibility criteria and selection process). The consent they provided enabled us to access anonymized data at granular levels including transaction-level records across all account types (checking, savings, and credit cards), daily balances, monthly statement summaries from January 2019 through February 2022, and a demographic snapshot from 2020 January. With these records, we were able to track co-holding persistence over time, quantify associated financial costs, and examine transactions to distinguish active accounts from dormant ones.

We define a co-holder as an individual who simultaneously maintains both revolving credit card debt and liquid assets (checking and savings account balances) across three consecutive billing periods.c Co-holding refers to the act of maintaining both types of balances above specified thresholds. Specifically, customers qualified as co-holding when they maintained minimum revolving credit card balances of AUD$500 and minimum combined liquid asset balances of AUD$500 (SI Appendix).

To track co-holding persistence, we created monthly indicators for periods where the co-holding amount was at least AUD$500. Co-holding amount was calculated as the minimum of revolving debt and liquid asset balances. We measured account activity through month-to-month percent changes in balances and by calculating CV, which compare the standard deviation of balances over the previous 12 months (including the current month) to the mean over the same period (following (28)). These measures enabled us to distinguish between accounts with high transaction volumes versus those that remained relatively static. Economic costs were estimated using back-of-the-envelope calculations that compared the interest costs of revolving credit card debt to the potential returns from liquid asset balances, based on account-specific interest rates.

Field experiment

We conducted a large-scale randomized controlled trial with 125,328 co-holders, whom we observed to be co-holding AUD$500 or more for three consecutive billing periods as of 2020 January. The experiment was designed to test two specific hypotheses: (i) co-holders are unaware that they are co-holding and (ii) co-holders are unaware of the costs of co-holding. The intervention provided salient information about co-holding behavior and its costs at a moment when customers could easily act on this information.

Sample selection

We selected a sample of 125,328 customers based on a set of preregistered criteria. These criteria included, among other things, being 18 years of age or older, being the primary account holder, and being identified by the bank as digitally active (SI Appendix). Customers were excluded if they were in arrears, had a promotional interest rate, or had automatic repayments set to pay off their entire outstanding balance each month. Importantly, each customer had been co-holding AUD$500 or more for three consecutive billing periods as of 2020 January.

Experimental design

The study employed a preregistered, three-arm randomized design. The Control group (n=41,776) received no notification about their co-holding behavior. The Notice group (n=41,776) received an in-app notification reading: “You usually have at least $500 available to pay your credit card debt. Every $100 you pay off could reduce your interest charges.” The Notice+Cost group (n=41,776) received the same initial message with an additional sentence: “…Every $100 you pay off could reduce your interest charges by about $20 per year. Saving $100 only earns about $1 in interest per year…” (see SI Appendix for screenshot).

To ensure balanced treatment groups, we used an anticlustering algorithm (37) that balanced condition assignment on key customer characteristics including gender, age band, customer tenure, credit card type, and loan holdings with the bank.

Implementation

The experiment was implemented from 2020 February 17 to March 18, through the bank’s mobile application. When customers in the treatment groups opened their banking app, they were shown a notification “card” that could be expanded to view the full message. After viewing the message, customers could dismiss the notification, close it, or click “Pay Now” to navigate directly to a money transfer interface. For customers who did not engage with the initial presentation (or dismissed it), the notification reappeared once during a subsequent app login within the experimental period (more details in SI Appendix).

Statistical analysis

We employed an intent-to-treat framework, comparing outcomes across all randomized customers regardless of their engagement with the notifications. We estimated treatment effects using ordinary least squares regression with robust standard errors.
