1. Background
 Sulfur hexafluoride (SF₆) is recognized as the most potent greenhouse gas used in the
power transmission and semiconductor sectors. In the past decade, its global emissions have
increased sharply, largely due to the absence of effective disposal and recovery pathways [1]. This
concern was formally addressed under the Kyoto Protocol, which urged participating nations to
adopt measures to limit SF₆ emissions and mitigate its role in climate change [2]. In recent years,
various abatement strategies have been explored, including non-thermal plasma (NTP)
technologies such as radio frequency, microwave, dielectric barrier discharge, and electron beam
systems[3]. While these methods can decompose SF₆, they also produce hazardous by-products
like sulfur oxyfluorides, sulfur dioxide, hydrofluoric acid, and fluorine gas[4]. Because these
compounds pose both environmental and health risks, replacing traditional disposal practices with
modern, more effective treatment methods has become an urgent priority.
At Los Alamos National Laboratory, SF₆ is used in shock-driven mixing research, including studies
of the Richtmyer–Meshkov instability. These experiments use a heavy-gas containing roughly 60%
SF6, 20% acetone vapor, and 20% air [5], and it is released into the atmosphere in large quantities.
Although there are mature technologies, such as membrane separation and cryogenic distillation,
for separating SF6 from air, they are problematic for direct application when the feed gas contains
high acetone levels ~20 v/v% due to flammability and handling risks. The ultimate goal of this
project is to advance the development and scaling up of an integrated process for eliminating
acetone and purifying SF₆. This process would enable the safe recovery of SF₆, improve its
handling and storage in compressed air tanks, and allow for the effective separation of SF₆ from
air downstream.

2. Executive Summary
 This study focused on the application of activated carbon (AC) adsorption as a pretreatment
stage to achieve high acetone removal efficiency. At the laboratory scale, we developed and
validated the Vaporized Acetone Screening Testbed (VAST) to simulate realistic feed conditions
at a scale of 1/10,000 relative to the full system (real condition: 12,000–25,000 LPM). Using an
optimized artificial feed of 8% acetone vapor at 1–2.5 LPM with a vacuum-dried AC-packed
column (20 g of synthetic Carboxen 563), we generated breakthrough curves to quantify system
performance.
The results showed breakthrough times of 15–35 minutes at 95% removal efficiency, a working
capacity of 332–356 mg/g, and flow-to-mass ratios ranging from 0.05 to 0.125 L/min/g. These
findings provided the design basis for larger-scale processes, including the estimation of AC
requirements (>260 kg at 25,000 LPM) and the expected removal efficiency at scale (95%
efficiency at ~15 min). Together, these results confirm the feasibility of applying AC adsorption
as a scalable pretreatment step for acetone removal in mixed SF₆/air systems.

3. Project Accomplishments
3-1. Establish the Vaporized Acetone Screening Testbed (VAST)

Figure 1. (a) Ventilated oven (with VAST) and the GC–MS used for analysis, (b) VAST installed
inside the oven and schematic of entire system.
 To simulate realistic acetone vapor feed conditions, a laboratory-scale testbed was designed
and constructed. The main components of the system were placed inside a ventilated oven to
provide a controlled thermal environment. A stainless-steel bubbler (operating range: 25–100 °C)
was used to generate acetone vapor, with its temperature continuously monitored by a
thermocouple to ensure stability and reproducibility of the feed concentration. The acetone vapor
was mixed with an inert nitrogen stream and subsequently passed through a packed AC column.
The effluent gas was directly analyzed by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) to
quantify acetone concentrations and assess removal efficiency. To prevent condensation losses
along the transfer lines, all external tubing was wrapped with a thermal insulation cover. This
configuration ensured consistent vapor delivery, minimized sampling artifacts, and enabled
reliable breakthrough testing under controlled conditions.
In order to increase the acetone concentration in the test gas, the oven temperature of the bubbler
was systematically raised during vapor generation. Prior to experimentation, the saturated vapor
pressure of acetone was reviewed to mitigate potential explosion risks associated with unexpected
pressure buildup. At 60 °C, the saturated vapor pressure reached approximately 1.2 bar, slightly
above atmospheric pressure. However, at 100 °C, the pressure increased sharply to over 3.5 bar
(≈50 psi), highlighting the importance of controlled operation. Based on this assessment, vapor
generation experiments were conducted at four representative temperatures: 40 °C, 60 °C, 80 °C,
and 100 °C.

 A commercial acetone balance gas (100 ppm in air) was used to calibrate the GC–MS and
confirm reliable vapor quantification. Tests were conducted at two flow rates (0.3 and 2.5 LPM),
representing the lowest and highest system conditions. Ion counts of acetone fragments and
background gases (H₂O, N₂, O₂) were recorded. At 2.5 LPM (Figure 2a), the normalized acetone
ion ratio stabilized after ~15 minutes despite minor signal drift, establishing 15 minutes as the
standard retention time. Flow-rate dependence (Figure 2b) showed stable acetone signals between
1.0–2.5 LPM, while fluctuations occurred above 3 LPM. Based on these results, all subsequent
experiments were performed within the 1.0–2.5 LPM range to ensure accurate and consistent
measurements.

Oven temperature was used to regulate acetone vaporization under the optimized GC/MS method.
The target concentration was 10–20%, but even at 100 °C the system reached only ~14%, with
column saturation causing significant error. Therefore, 80 °C was selected as the operating
condition, providing a stable ~8% acetone concentration in the feed gas. This value was
consistently applied in all subsequent breakthrough tests.
3-3. Breakthrough Testing of Acetone Adsorption

Figure 3. Breakthrough curves for acetone adsorption on Carboxen 563 (20 g, 8% acetone feed)
at flow rates of 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, and 2.5 LPM.
 Breakthrough experiments were conducted to examine how flow rate influences acetone
removal efficiency in AC column. A feed gas containing 8% acetone was passed through a column
packed with 20 g of Carboxen 563, which was selected as the adsorbent for its high microporosity
and strong affinity toward small volatile organic compounds in the C₃–C₅ range. The breakthrough
curves represent the ratio between the outlet and inlet acetone concentrations (Ct/C₀), where values
near zero indicate effective removal and values approaching one indicate reduced adsorption
capacity. Tests were performed at four flow rates: 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, and 2.5 LPM (Figure 3). As
expected, higher flow rates resulted in earlier breakthrough. At 2.5 LPM, the 95% removal
threshold was reached in under 15 minutes, whereas at 1.0 LPM, breakthrough occurred after more
than 35 minutes.
3-4. Evaluation of Breakthrough Performance
 The breakthrough test results were further analyzed using three performance metrics: (i)
breakthrough time at 95% removal, (ii) working capacity, defined as the amount of acetone
adsorbed per gram of AC up to the breakthrough point, and (iii) the flow-to-mass ratio, which
indicates the flow that can be treated per gram of adsorbent. These values are summarized in Table
2.

As expected, higher flow rates resulted in shorter breakthrough times, shifting both working
capacity and flow-to-mass ratio. Carboxen 563 exhibited strong adsorption performance, with
working capacities exceeding 300 mg/g under the tested laboratory conditions. To ensure stable
performance in larger-scale systems, maintaining an appropriate flow-to-mass ratio will be critical.
If the ratio is too high, the column saturates rapidly and efficiency drops; when held within the
optimal range, adsorption remains effective for longer operating periods. Based on the
breakthrough performance at 95% removal efficiency, the amount of AC required for larger flow
rates was estimated. Using a 15-minute breakthrough time at 2.5 LPM as the baseline, the projected
AC requirement scales up to approximately 200 kg for a flow rate of 25,000 LPM. In practice, a
safety factor must be applied to ensure operational stability and accommodate maintenance
intervals. With a safety factor of 1.3, the required AC could reach ~260 kg. Such quantities present
practical challenges, including larger system size, increased installation and material costs, and
more frequent replacement or regeneration. These considerations highlight the importance of
optimizing both carbon utilization and system design for real-scale applications.

4. Challenges, Issues, Risks, and Opportunities
 o High Acetone Concentration: One of the main challenges encountered was the high
 acetone level (~8%) in the feed gas, which accelerated breakthrough and limited adsorption
 capacity.
 o Proposed Mitigation Strategy: To address this, we considered introducing a cooling step
 before the AC column by applying a water-cooled jacket around the gas line. This step
 would condense a portion of the acetone vapor, reducing the concentration to below 5%
 before reaching the adsorbent.
 o Operational Benefit: With the reduced acetone load, the activated carbon column could
 operate more efficiently, maintaining stable performance and extending breakthrough time.
 o Integration with Membrane Unit: Lowering the acetone concentration at the adsorption
 stage would also prevent overloading of the downstream membrane unit, keeping acetone
 levels ideally below 1% and supporting effective SF₆/air separation.
 o Opportunities: This multi-step approach highlights opportunities for process optimization
 by combining pre-cooling, adsorption, and membrane separation, which may enhance
 stability, reduce material requirements, and improve overall system efficiency.

5. Conclusions
 A laboratory-scale testbed (VAST) was established to simulate acetone vapor feed
conditions and validate GC–MS analysis. Calibration confirmed reliable quantification, with stable
signals achieved after 15 minutes and consistent performance between 1–2.5 LPM. Vapor
concentration was controlled through oven heating, with 80 °C selected as the optimal condition,
providing a stable feed of ~8% acetone. Breakthrough experiments using 20 g of Carboxen 563
showed strong adsorption performance, with breakthrough times ranging from ~15 minutes at 2.5
LPM to >35 minutes at 1.0 LPM. Working capacities exceeded 300 mg/g under laboratory
conditions, and flow-to-mass analysis emphasized the importance of maintaining appropriate
operating ranges. Scaling estimates suggest that processing 25,000 LPM gas would require ~200
kg of activated carbon, or up to ~260 kg with a safety factor. These results demonstrate both the
feasibility and challenges of acetone removal at scale, highlighting the need for optimized
operating conditions and efficient carbon utilization in real systems.
