Tennessee’s growth in 2024 did not outpace its neighbors (N. Carolina, S. Carolina, Georgia, Florida) – but its rapid
population increase, particularly in the eastern and rural parts of the state – has necessitated new developments
and utility connections, creating further strain existing systems and outpacing infrastructure investment.

Residential, industrial, and large load data center facility expansion have exacerbated management backlogs.
Additionally, as newer technologies emerge in larger utility providers, a chasm between urban and rural resilience
grows – creating segmentation across the state.

With new development, industries, and large load additions to the electric grid across the Volunteer state, utilities
must contend with these new connections, management backlogs, and technology gaps – while addressing an
increase in major outages with more frequent extreme weather events, such as Hurricane Helene in 2024. This
issues, in turn, have given way to greater vulnerabilities in adjacent infrastructure – including transportation and
emergency management services, among others.

In TN, the greatest costs for all customers are reported in Davidson (Nashville) & Shelby (Memphis) Counties – the
state’s largest economies. Per customer, greatest costs exist for residents in Clay, Pickett, & Fentress Counties –
and in Stewart (Clarksville) County, where ≅ 50% of the working population is employed. This suggests that rural
and geographically isolated communities disproportionately incur costs during major outage events.

 Vegetation Management – Beyond Trimming & Pruning
Vegetation management plays a critical role in reducing power outage
risks – including fallen trees, wildland fire, and floodwater mitigation.
Effective utility vegetation management should strategically integrate
approaches to address invasive and non-native plant species.

• Invasive vegetation can overwhelm both overhead
 and undergrounded utility assets.

• Invasive shrubs, trees, vines weaken soil stability & tree health.

• Increases risk of outages and utility asset damage as nutrient-
 deprived species become more vulnerable to weather events.

 Critical Infrastructure by Service Area – Emergency Management Implications
FEMA’s Community Lifelines framework was developed to facilitate disaster operations. Lifelines are fundamental
infrastructure services that enable critical societal functions that are essential to the health, safety, and economic
security of our communities. UrbanNET uses graph analysis to calculate the quantity of critical infrastructure “nodes”
located within a given service territory. Examples include facilities such as hospitals, emergency service providers, and
water treatment facilities. Here, we highlight three community lifelines dependent on stable energy systems: food, water,
and shelter; health and medical services; and safety and security.

  While the City of Memphis has the greatest number of critical infrastructure nodes within its utility service area, as a
  region, Middle Tennessee has the greatest volume overall, including the Nashville metro area’s surrounding
  municipalities and electric cooperatives. The higher population density of Middle Tennessee and its strain on
  infrastructure poses a unique challenge for future power outages and emergency management events. Combined with
  vegetation management backlogs, aging populations, and growing transportation bottlenecks around Middle
  Tennessee — recent extreme weather across the region has highlighted an increased need for strategic investment
  into grid resilience for critical customers, supply chain, and services.

  East Tennessee’s rural communities and the greater Knoxville area are on similar paths, with population growth
  constraining critical infrastructure - particularly housing, transportation, and medical services. In the wake of
  Hurricane Helene, decision-making surrounding grid resilience, land-use, and geographic isolation of Appalachia may
  also be considered for investments.

 Overview of Grid Resilience Recommendations
Grid resilience efforts in Tennessee must balance the high costs of grid hardening activities with the cost of impacts to
both residential and non-residential utility customers. Because Tennessee’s infrastructure was designed for a much lower
population, density, and volume of use than its current state, investments to overhaul entire systems may leave other
utilities more vulnerable to increasingly severe outages. Below, we provide an overview of recommendations for each
management category, as well as considerations by state sub-region:
