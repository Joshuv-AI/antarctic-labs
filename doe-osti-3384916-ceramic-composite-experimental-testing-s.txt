 ABSTRACT

Over recent years, ceramic matrix materials such as SiC–SiC and C–C have been gaining interest for use
in fusion reactors, light water reactors (LWRs), and high-temperature reactors (HTRs). These materials
are good candidates to operate in very high temperature and moderate to high radiation environments.

The evaluation of composite materials, in general, is challenging because of variations in precursor
materials, variations in the fabrication process across fabricators, and the wide range of potential fiber
architectures, to name a few. However, the need to evaluate neutron-irradiated properties adds another
layer of complexity, which includes cost, timeline, and specimen size limitations (often associated with
irradiation testing).

A qualification methodology for the use of ceramic composites is provided in the American Society of
Mechanical Engineers (ASME) Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code Section III-5-HHB. The methodology is
supported by ASTM International (ASTM) guides, which provide a pathway to accomplish this effort.
Part of the qualification strategy is for the designer to collect material property data on environmental
conditions representative of its design envelope. These data include irradiation effects.

This report presents an experimental study and test campaign developed to partially address this gap by
providing initial mechanical and physical property data required for design. A variety of different
materials using different manufacturing techniques are considered as part of this campaign. The test plan
suggests performing a screening or partial irradiation study to assist the designer during the material
selection process. The designer can then perform a more comprehensive qualification study if the material
performance is promising.

This work focuses on the status of the specimen preparations (machining of samples), the current test
methods and failure analysis as well as the preparation of irradiation vehicles for the irradiation campaign.
The irradiation will be performed at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) in the High Flux Isotope
Reactor (HFIR) and at Idaho National Laboratory (INL) in the Advanced Test Reactor (ATR).

 1. INTRODUCTION

Ceramic matrix composites (CMCs) are gaining interest for structural applications in high-temperature
and radiation-intensive environments, including advanced fission and fusion energy systems. Their low
density, high-temperature strength retention, corrosion resistance, and non-catastrophic failure behavior
offer clear advantages over monolithic ceramics and many metallic alloys. However, the deployment of
CMCs in nuclear environments is limited by an incomplete understanding of how irradiation alters
constituent phases, fiber/matrix interfaces, and damage-tolerant toughening mechanisms over relevant
dose and temperature ranges. Because these materials derive their performance from a hierarchical
architecture—fibers, interphases, matrix, porosity, and processing-induced defects—radiation-induced
changes at any length scale can propagate to macroscopic property degradation, making targeted
experimental studies essential.

Neutron and ion irradiation can introduce point defects, defect clusters, and transmutation products that
drive swelling, creep, hardening, amorphization, and microcracking in ceramic phases. In fiber-reinforced
systems, irradiation may also modify interphase chemistry and shear strength, altering crack deflection,
fiber pullout, and frictional sliding—mechanisms that underpin the pseudo-ductile response of many
CMCs. At elevated temperatures, the competition between defect production and recovery further
complicates predictions: irradiation can accelerate diffusion and phase evolution while thermal annealing
can partially heal damage, leading to non-monotonic dependencies on dose rate, temperature, and
irradiation spectrum. Additionally, irradiation can change thermal transport and oxidation behavior, which
are critical to component design and safety margins in nuclear service.

An experimental study and test campaign are developed to partially address this gap and to support the
nuclear industry and potential vendors by providing the initial mechanical and physical property data
required for design. The intent for this study is to obtain ASME related CMC material properties to
support designers with relevant data collection and also providing industry with supportive irradiation
data for down selection. In order to do this, testing techniques that accommodate small specimens and
meet ASTM standards (as far possible) are prioritized. However, where this is not possible, new testing
techniques are also being assessed and included. For these cases finite element analysis (FEA) has been
performed and testing is evaluated accordingly. This includes the asymmetric four-point bend test that is
perhaps a more consistent interlaminar shear failure test method compared to the current double notch
shear test prescribed in ASTM C1292 [1]. Lastly mentioned introduced large scatter due to machining
inconsistencies. Another method that is being assessed includes a nonstandard diametrical compression
testing for composites [2]. More on these failure modes are discussed in the pre-irradiation
characterization section of this report.

A fibers investigation is also included as part of the irradiation study. The goal is to understand the
microstructural evolution in carbon fibers with different textures and defect densities in response to
irradiation.

Objectives for this report include a status update on the machining of samples of the received materials,
ongoing testing of the pristine non-irradiated samples, and the current status to encapsulate specimens to
start the first irradiation.

2.1 MATERIALS RECEIVED

Several material vendors were willing to participate in the irradiation program and provided a variety of
their materials to support systematic study. Others provided materials of interest tailored for the high

Some materials emphasized high thermal conductivity with high density C-C with structure that behaves

include different fiber tow sizes (3k vs 12k) utilizing a harness satin weave that are applied in either a 2-
or 3- directional till weave pattern of 1x1 or 2x2 arrangement. C-CAT provided C-C with SiC coatings
and joints materials, while GA supported the study with SiC-SiC variants (Hy-Nicaloy fiber) to establish
a baseline. A random fiber C-C composite was also used for comparison purposes.

In all cases the materials provided are to assess dimensional stability after neutron irradiation, but
different characteristics will be evaluated. The materials are designed for different applications, as some
emphasize high stiffness and/or strength while others are developed for increased thermal performance.

The list is not complete as some suppliers and/or vendors indicated their interest in participating but
requested additional time to prepare materials which will be provided during the latter part of the year.
Currently eight to ten material variants are on hand, and another set (of similar size) is expected to be
received over the next few months.

In addition, eight representative fiber types are included in the study. As with the composites, some of the
fibers have high tensile strength and some have high modulus. Single filament tows are prepared to be
encapsulated in special designed 1” sub-holders the irradiation capsules.

2.2 SAMPLE MACHINING AND MARKING PROTOCOLS

Machining carbon–carbon (C–C) and silicon carbide–silicon carbide (SiC–SiC) composites is challenging
because both materials are heterogeneous, anisotropic, and prone to damage mechanisms that depend
strongly on fiber architecture and interphase design.

C–C is comparatively “softer” than SiC-based systems but is highly abrasive and porous, so conventional
cutting often leads to fiber pullout, matrix tearing, edge fraying, and subsurface delamination; dust
generation is significant and typically requires aggressive extraction and appropriate controls. In contrast,
SiC–SiC is extremely hard and brittle, making it difficult to machine without introducing microcracks or
degrading interfacial regions that govern toughness; as a result, diamond tooling, grinding, ultrasonic
machining, abrasive waterjet cutting, laser-assisted approaches, and electrical discharge machining (only
when a conductive phase is present) are commonly used to limit cutting forces and heat-affected damage.

For both material classes, best practice emphasizes minimizing mechanical and thermal loads, selecting
tooling and parameters based on layup direction, and verifying surface/subsurface integrity via metrology
and non-destructive evaluation, since machining-induced defects can dominate strength, leak-tightness,
and fatigue performance in service.

 2

Defects introduced due to cutting methods can contribute to delamination during irradiation and therefore
it is necessary to pay attention to methods applied.

For first irradiation screening efforts, three primary specimen geometries are selected and used to limit
used capsule volume to maximize the usage of each irradiation capsule. As shown in Figure 2.1 specimen
geometries are limited to bend bars, square and disc types. The thickness is material depended based on
architecture, but varies between ~1.5 – 2.5 mm for the bend bars and square specimens, while disc
thickness is anything between 2 – 5.5 mm.

Figure 2.1. Examples of specimen size and geometries. (From left to right are the bend bar, square and cylindrical
 disc specimens)

Specific cut plans were prepared for each of the materials. For the bend bars, the selected screening
orientation was to align the length of the specimens along the primary fiber direction. The thickness was
aligned with the laminate through thickness direction for both the bend bar and square specimens. The
disc specimens were also cut so that the laminate through thickness directions is aligned with the
thickness of the disc specimen.

All the C-C specimens prepared by ORNL were done with the use of a precise diamond wire saw
(Diamond WireTec DWS.100) to avoid any impurities that could result in entrapment of materials with
high radiological activiation, and they were engraved with a unique ID for pre- and post-irradiation
evaluation.

All specimens were clean and dimensionally inspected according to LAMDA’s standard operating
guideline (SOG), MST-LAMDA-SOG-009, Dimensional Inspection and Bulk Density Guideline for Bars,
Rods, and Disks. The SOG is based on ASTM C559 [3], dimensional inspection of carbon materials.

 3. TESTING AND CHARACTERISATION

Small-specimen mechanical testing provides a practical pathway to quantify strength-related properties
when material availability is limited, geometry is constrained (e.g., irradiated coupons), or conventional
full-size specimens are impractical. In this approach, miniature tensile, flexural, shear, or fracture
specimens are designed to preserve representative stress states while enabling higher throughput and
reduced material volume, and results are interpreted—where applicable—within established ASTM
frameworks (or closely aligned practices) to ensure traceability and comparability. Relevant standards
may include ASTM methods for tensile and flexural testing of composites and ceramics, as well as
specimen preparation and dimensional tolerancing guidance, with careful attention to size effects,
gripping/loading alignment, and surface finish requirements that can disproportionately influence failure
in small geometries. When direct compliance with a standard is not possible due to specimen scale, a
standards-informed protocol combined with validation against larger-specimen data and appropriate
statistical treatment (e.g., Weibull analysis for brittle failure) can still yield defensible estimates of
mechanical strength and enable meaningful comparisons across processing conditions, environments, and
irradiation states.

3.1.3 X-ray Computed Tomography (XCT)

The XCT measurements were performed using a Zeiss Xradia Versa 520 instrument operated at 40 kV,
3W and at 60 kV, 5 W. To maximize the field of view (FOV), a 0.4× scintillator objective was used in
front of the CCD camera. To cover entire sample volumes, stitch scans were employed wherein several
overlapping sections of the samples were scanned and stitched together after reconstruction. For all the
scans, a 1 × 1 binning was used on the detector to improve resolution, resulting in a voxel size of 15.6 µm
for this setup. Data reconstruction was automated using Zeiss software.

Dragonfly PRO v.2025.1 was used for data visualization and quantification. For segmentation of the
pores, solid and exterior sections, deep-learning-aided segmentation was used. The deep-learning model
was trained by the user to recognize regions of interest. The model employed a 2.5D U-Net architecture
for its training with a patch size of 64 and a stride ratio of 0.25 (overlap between the patches) limited to a
maximum of 100 epochs (iterations). Each training employed several frames with sufficient features to
provide input to the model. Automated data augmentation was also employed to create additional training
data.

Select square specimens were used to collect pre- irradiation for baseline XCT analysis. Preliminary
example images are shown in Figure 3.3.

3.2 PRELIMINARY TEST METHOD AND FAILURE MODE ANALYSIS

As mentioned earlier, due to size limitations it is not possible to perform irradiation on standard size
specimens. However, due to code and designer requirements, it is still prudent to understand the material
properties, especially strength and modulus behavior. To accommodate smaller specimens, non-standard
test methods are also being evaluated. FEA were performed for two general classes of test specimens
namely disc compression and bending. These analyses were performed to assess the most likely stress
failure mode. For bending analyses, three types of loading were considered: symmetric three-point,
symmetric four-point (in one-third and one-quarter span distances), and asymmetric four-point (in two pin
distance configurations). Analyses were performed using orthotropic material properties for the vast
majority of cases, with isotropic properties used in several cases for comparison purposes.

For the first order analysis an estimated [0/90] 2D C/C orthotropic material properties were calculated
using the Composite Cylinders Assemblage (CCA) micromechanics approach (to obtain the orthotropic

properties of a unidirectional fiber plus matrix bundle) together with an iso-strain assumption to weight
the bundles equally in the 0° and 90° directions.

flexural modulus for SGL Sigrabond Premium [5] for this material. The resulting properties are shown in

3.2.1 Diametrical disc compression analysis

Analyses of disc compression specimens were performed to assess the likelihood of the specimen failing
in interlaminar normal stress and thereby providing an interlaminar tensile strength. A total of eight
different configurations were analyzed, by considering all combinations of two diameters (6 mm and 12
mm) and the four sets of material properties cited previously (three sets of orthotropic properties and one
set of isotropic properties).

For computational efficiency, a one-eighth symmetry finite element (FE) model was used, as described in
Figure 3.4(a). In this approach, half of each dimension of the specimen is modeled and, appropriate
translational boundary conditions are placed on the symmetry faces (i.e., the X = 0, Y = 0, and Z = 0
faces). The specimen is compressed by applying a downward displacement to the separate rigid plate that
is placed in initial tangential contact with the top surface of the specimen; the plate interacts with the
specimen only by contact.

Results of the one-eighth model were verified by solving one full-model case, illustrated in Figure 3.4(b),
where the entire specimen was modeled and an additional rigid plate was placed at the bottom of the
specimen to react to the compressive load.

 7

 Figure 3.4. One-eighth symmetry FE model (a) and full model (b).

 Comparisons of horizontal and vertical displacement response on the outer face of the specimen for these
two models are shown in Figure 3.5. Both cases use the baseline orthotropic material properties, with the
material thru-thickness direction oriented in the global X-direction. Results are nearly identical for the
two cases. Note that in the full model, vertical displacement ranges from approximately -0.24 mm to -0.12
mm due to the compression of the bottom half of the specimen, whereas vertical displacement in the one-
eighth model ranges from -0.12 mm to 0 mm because the Y = 0 centerline has a UY = 0 boundary
condition applied and the bottom half of the specimen is not explicitly modeled.

 Figure 3.5. Comparison of displacement results for full model and one-eighth model.

Excellent agreement is observed for stress response in the two models, as illustrated in Figure 3.6 where
only the upper one-eighth region of the full model is shown. Specifically, note that there are only
marginal differences in peak axial stress in the two models, which occur at or very close to the contact
region with the rigid load plate (the full model shows 24.1 MPa, while the one-eighth model shows 20.7
MPa). Similarly, only marginal differences are observed in the peak horizontal stress (less than 1%) and
distributions in the two models. The compressive forces in the full model and one-eighth model are
21,194 N and 5,313 N, respectively. The full model therefore requires 4 times the force of the one-eighth
model due to symmetry, as expected, with a factor of 2 resulting from the X = 0 plane and the Z = 0
plane. (Note that the horizontal symmetry plane, Y = 0, does not contribute a factor of 2 because it is
perpendicular to the loading direction). Based on the overall results between the two models, the rest of
the analysis was performed applying the eighth-symmetrical model.

As the disc specimen geometries were partly selected due to material and irradiation capsule
considerations, it was necessary to perform some initial tests and FEA analysis to verify the failure mode.
Analysis on the 6mm (D = 6mm) and the 12mm diameter (D = 6mm) specimens were performed in the
axial or in-plane material stress (where the stress component in the FEA model is denoted as S11) as well
as in the horizontal or thru-thickness material stress (denoted as S33 in the FEA output). Both cases were
evaluated to verify the failure mode.

Based on information provided by Walrath and Adams [6] it is expected that the ratio of thru-thickness
strength to in-plane strength to be significantly less than 1. It is therefore anticipated that the failure will
occur due to thru-thickness stress. Although isotropic materials are not expected for this study, it was
included as part of the analysis for comparison purposes. The failure is determined by evaluating which
failure has the highest peak condition. Analysis contour plots where the lower contour limit was set to 0
MPa (to highlight the contour differences) for the D = 12mm specimen is shown for the axial stress in
Figure 3.7 and the horizontal stress in Figure 3.8.

The general behaviors and trends observed in the these cases are the same as the results for the D = 6 mm

designs would be expected to fail due to horizontal stress.

Special fixtures were fabricated to perform preliminary diametrical compression tests for both the D =
6mm and the D = 12mm specimens. In both cases the specimens are 5.5mm thick. The model verification
tests were performed on the D = 12mm specimens as only limited number of D = 6mm specimens are
available and will be used for irradiation.

 Figure 3.9 shows two 12mm diameter specimens where the thru-thickness direction of the specimens was
oriented axially, but the one condition was oriented in a 0º-ply direction and the other in a 90º-ply
direction. Newton et. al. [2] applied compression in the lateral direction of the fibers between the two
platens, suggesting the thru thickness to be in the horizontal direction when compared to the models
which is different from the specimens observed in the tests. Even though the material orientation is
different from Newton et. al., the tested specimens were observed to fail due to horizontal stress in
testing.

 Figure 3.9. Post-test photos of D = 12 mm with the material thru-thickness direction oriented axially. (i.e. into the

As a basic verification of the FEA, the D = 12 mm case was simulated based on actual material properties
and component stresses were extracted. For completeness, the D = 6 mm case was also simulated. Brief
FEA results for these cases are shown in Figure 3.10. In these results, we see that for the D = 12 mm case,
the peak stress is ~164 MPa in the material in-plane direction and ~6 Mpa in the material thru-thickness
direction. This results in a stress ratio (in-plane to thru-thickness) of ~27, which is greater than the
corresponding strength ratio for any of the materials being considered, implying that failure would be
expected in the in-plane material direction (i.e., horizontal stress or S11 as denoted in the FEA output),
consistent with the test results shown in Figure 3.9. The stress ratio is marginally lower for the D = 6 mm
case, at ~25 (~306 Mpa and ~12 Mpa), but even at the slightly lower value, this specimen, if tested,
would also be expected to fail due to stress in the in-plane material direction (i.e., horizontal stress or S11
as denoted in the FEA output).

Another key finding was that the FEA orienting the material in the non-typical manner (i.e., with the thru-

With the established confidence in the failure mode, the next step will be to verify that the material
properties agree to the conditions provided in the model and then to start the test campaign for the
respective materials on both the D = 12mm and D = 6mm specimens.

3.2.2 Bending specimen analysis

Various failure modes are evaluated for the bend bar specimens to capitalize on the suggested specimen
geometry that fits inside the irradiation capsule. The selected bend bar geometry (24mm in length and
6mm in width), varies in thickness (between 1.5 to 3mm) due to the manufactured material design. These
specimens are typically used for flexure bend testing either applying a 3pt or a 4pt tests. However, prior
analysis has shown that by changing the load pin arrangement, an interlaminar shear (ILS) test (more
valuable for design assessment) can also be applied instead of using the double notch shear specimen
which has critical flaws related to the notch size and geometry that can lead to significant scatter in the
test data and inconsistent strength values, compromising the accuracy of the results.

A total of 20 bending test configurations were simulated with FEA using the properties listed in Table 2.
The general approach used was to gather peak bending stress (i.e., in-plane material direction stress) and
peak interlaminar shear (ILS) stress from each case, calculate the stress ratio, and compare this with
estimated material strengths to predict the anticipated failure mode.

3.3 FIBER TESTING

Fiber tensile testing of individual filaments was conducted using ASTM D3379-75 [7], the standard test
method for tensile properties and young’s modulus for high modulus single filament materials. Under this
standard, a single filament is carefully mounted and subjected to tensile testing to measure its tensile
strength and elongation. Testing is performed using a precision tensile testing machine, where the
filament is gripped and pulled until it breaks, recording the applied force and elongation during the
process.

In ambient conditions, tests are conducted at room temperature to determine the filament’s performance
under normal environmental settings. High temperature testing, on the other hand, involves subjecting the
filament to elevated temperatures using specialized heating chambers or furnaces to assess its tensile
properties at elevated thermal conditions. This dual approach allows for comprehensive evaluation of the
filament’s mechanical behavior both ambient and high temperature environments, ensuring reliable data
for material performance analysis.

The standard filament gauge length is at least 1”, but due to irradiation and space limitations, fiber
filaments as small as ½” gauge length were tested to perform a size effect study comparison. Generally,
carbon and carbon fiber have a low coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) along the fiber axis (between
- 0.6 and – 0.38 α.10-6 / ºC [8]), but some dimensional change associated with the fiber diameter can be
due to uninhibited expansion of the resin. Smaller gauge lengths up to ¼” and at irradiation temperature
are performed for unirradiated condition evaluation.

ASTM standards for tow level testing are best represented by 1” single fiber properties in some systems.
Testing at multiple lengths allows us to build an understanding of mechanical failure modes and
accurately compare with properties tested in rad controlled areas. Baseline size effect testing was

4.1 IRRADIATION VEHICLES AND PREPARATION

For the first irradiation, focus is on the plate materials less than 2.5mm thick. From these small square
specimens and bend bar specimens are prepared, which are prioritized for the first capsules. Other capsule
designs housing cylindrical disc type specimens (CMC2) and mini holders containing fiber whiskers

The first capsules are in process of preparation. Parts have been procured and inspected. Next steps are

 5. CONCLUSION

This report presents the status of an irradiation experiment work package to support CMC qualification
studies. Output from this study will hugely benefit HTR designers and vendors in their demonstrated
interest in C–C and SiC–SiC composites for use in core components.

The test strategy suggests performing a screening study to supply CMC designers with initial and useful
information to support materials decision-making when considering irradiation effects. The proposal
follows the materials qualification intent of ASME III-5 for a variety of materials manufactured using
different fabrication techniques. The end goal will be to evaluate the material’s postirradiation condition.
but currently pre-irradiation examinations are performed on all the specimens that are slated for
irradiation, while test methods are being evaluated for the pre-and post- irradiation campaign. Irradiations
will be performed in HFIR (at ORNL) and in ATR (at INL). The irradiation capsules are making progress
with the first capsule expected to start HFIR irradiation for cycle 517.

Baseline or pre-irradiation testing will be performed during the irradiation periods.
