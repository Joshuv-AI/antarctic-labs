 A Brief History of the LANL Laser Program

 By Lizabeth Johnson, Ph.D.

 WRS-NSRCMS

Introduction

 The invention of the laser in 1960 and subsequent developments led to the application of
lasers in many fields such as science, medicine, and industry. The national defense community
quickly grasped the significance of the laser and applied it to many different programs. An
important application is that of inertial confinement fusion, using lasers to create fusion energy.
This culminated in the achievement of fusion ignition at the National Ignition Facility (NIF) in
2022 (Abu-Shawareb et al. 2022; Abu-Shawareb et al. 2024). In the 1970s and 1980s, Los
Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) engaged in the development of various laser systems,
including some of the most powerful lasers in the world at the time. During that period, LANL
was often in competition with Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL).1 Both
laboratories received significant government funding to develop lasers but pursued different
technological approaches. Although the labs viewed each other as competitors, the work carried
out by staff at the two facilities was necessary to advance the scientific underpinnings of
developing laser concepts and refining the needed technology, both for use in developing and
verifying the functionality of nuclear weapons and as a means of developing an approach to
affordable commercial energy. Although LLNL’s laser program has been discussed in detail
elsewhere (Tarter 2018; Kidder 2006; Nuckolls 1998), this paper provides a brief discussion of
LANL’s laser program, with a particular focus on the technological advances LANL researchers
made and how these laser systems contributed to the evolution of laser technology overall.

General background

 Although the theory behind laser technology was first mooted in the early 20th century, it
wasn’t until 1960 that the first working laser was created by Theodore Maiman at Hughes

 From 1947–1981, the laboratory at Los Alamos was known as Los Alamos Scientific Laboratory or LASL. In
1981, LASL was redesignated as a national laboratory, becoming Los Alamos National Laboratory. The laboratory
at Livermore was initially named the University of California Radiation Laboratory, Livermore Branch in 1952. In
1958, following the death of its founder, E. O. Lawrence, the laboratory was renamed the Lawrence Radiation
Laboratory (LRL). In 1971, the laboratory was again renamed as Lawrence Livermore Laboratory (LLL). Finally, in
1981, it became a national laboratory, as Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL).

 1

Research Laboratories. Maiman’s original laser was solid-state, using a ruby rod, and that
breakthrough engendered scientific activity at many different research facilities over the next few
years. With regard to solid-state lasers, early research was performed with a few different
materials, including the ruby rods used by Maiman. But in 1961, Elias Snitzer at American
Optical developed a neodymium-doped (Nd) glass laser, and over the next decade, other research
facilities adopted Nd glass lasers for their research, including Bell Labs, LLNL, and the
Laboratory for Laser Energetics (LLE) at the University of Rochester. By the mid-1960s, several
types of gas laser were also being used in research. The first CO2 laser was invented by Kumar
Patel at Bell Labs in the early 1960s, and by the mid-1960s, William Bridges at Hughes Research
Laboratories was using krypton and other rare gases for laser research. Krypton lasers had
shorter wavelengths and were more efficient, qualities that attracted military interest. That
interest in turn led some labs to explore excimer lasers, including Avco Everett Research
Laboratory (AERL) and Sandia National Laboratory (SNL) (Hecht 2010b; Webb 2010).

 Interest in laser research at LANL, which began in 1969, grew out of the Lab’s previous
work in nuclear rocket propulsion and fusion research. Two key focal points in LANL’s early
laser program were achieving an efficient fusion process and pursuing commercial power
generation. Early experiments in these two areas led to the creation of a laser program and
division at the Laboratory in 1972 (Boyer 1983a). Over the years, laser research at LANL
evolved from focusing on gas lasers to excimer lasers and, finally, to glass lasers. From its
foundation onward, the laser program at LANL achieved many important milestones in laser
technology.

LANL’s early research program

 In 1968, Keith Boyer was wrapping up research on nuclear rocket propulsion when he
learned about a laser fusion project at the Air Force Weapons Lab at Kirtland Air Force Base in
Albuquerque, New Mexico. The work sparked Boyer’s interest because his earlier research had
been in controlled fusion, and he realized laser fusion could provide a more efficient means of
propulsion. By the following year, he had established a small program involving a 1 J, ps glass
laser that was used for light-target interaction research. Boyer’s staff also began investigating
carbon dioxide (CO2) lasers pumped by electrical discharge that could be used for higher energy
experiments. A third aspect of the program involved chemical lasers for work in photochemistry

 2

and to provide a backup for the nascent CO2 laser research. The chemical laser program was
designed around a hydrogen fluoride (HF) system that could provide data at a wavelength of 2.7
µm; it also cost less to operate than the CO2 laser.

 These early efforts were promising, and in 1971, LANL’s director, Harold Agnew, asked
Boyer to expand the program. Although the program was initially part of the Director’s Office,
by early 1972, it had become complex enough to merit transformation into its own division.
Accordingly, in February of that year, L-Division was established with Keith Boyer as its head.
Over the next few years, LANL’s budget nearly doubled annually, from $700,000 in 1970 to
$10.9 million in 1974. This increase in budget was in contrast to the fact that federal defense
spending declined from 1970 to 1975, reflecting the government’s push for energy research and
development, driven in part by the oil embargo that began in October 1973 (Boyer 1983b; Boyer
1983a; Tartar 2018; Buck 1983; S. Batha, personal communication, January 5, 2026).

The CO2 laser era

 Although Boyer and his staff chose to invest their time and energy in several different
types of lasers, Boyer believed a gas laser would be the best system for generating power. In fact,
Patel’s early experiments with a CO2 laser had demonstrated the lasers could achieve 20%
efficiency and produce up to 200 W of continuous power. Similarly, when the staff at AERL
began experimenting with a CO2 laser system powered by a rocket engine, the system initially
achieved 50 kW (Hecht 2010b; Hecht 2010a). In later years, Boyer stated that his decision to
focus much of the program on CO2 lasers was due to the fact that he believed a large CO2 laser
operating at 3 atm was the only laser that could produce “several hundred kilojoules of energy
delivered in a nanosecond or less to burn pellets with a satisfactory energy gain” and that “an
efficient fusion process might be achieved” with such a laser (Boyer 1983a, p. 18; Boyer 1983b).

 The first CO2 laser system at LANL was a single-beam CO2 laser chain that produced a
few hundred joules. A slightly larger CO2 laser, known as Gemini, was also in use by the mid-
1970s. Gemini was an electron-beam-pumped gas discharge laser with dual beams capable of
achieving 2 kJ with a 1 ns pulse. It was a direct-drive system that was initially used for prototype
studies for a larger system still in the planning stage, but work with Gemini turned to target
experimentation after the system produced the first observable deuterium-tritium (D-T) fusion

 3

neutrons generated by pellet implosion in a CO2 system in 1977 (Boyer 1983a; Boyer 1983b;
Ladish 1979; Schappert 1979).

 Building on the data these early lasers generated, Boyer and his team embarked on the
construction of a larger system. This was Helios, which became operational in 1978. Like
Gemini, it was an electron-beam-pumped gas discharge laser, but it was originally designed as a
six-beam system and later modified to be an eight-beam system. When it was first fired, Helios
produced a 10 kJ, 0.5 ns pulse. Later experiments resulted in Helios producing over 20 TW on
target (Boyer 1983b; Boyer 1983a; Carlson et al., 1981; Ladish, 1979; Schappert, 1979).
According to Boyer, experiments with Helios up to 8 kJ showed “substantial increases in fuel
density and neutron production in ablatively driven pellets. However, they also indicated a hotter
electron component than was previously measured” (Boyer 1983a, p. 19). The issue with hot
electron components led the division staff to explore the use of hohlraum targets, which are
containers that hold the pellet or capsule, absorb the laser light, and then illuminate the pellet or
capsule with x-rays, an approach known as indirect drive. In addition to expanding LANL’s
exploration of laser fusion targets, one of the main goals with the Helios system was to expand
knowledge of laser-plasma interaction, which would indicate whether laser-induced fusion could
be a viable energy source. By 1979, Helios was the world’s most powerful gas laser (Ladish
1979; Perkins 1979).

 By the mid-1970s, the budget for defense projects began to increase once again. By this
time, the Atomic Energy Commission (AEC) had given way to the Energy Research and
Development Administration (ERDA), which came into being in 1975 with a budget of $3.6
billion, some of which was directed to fusion energy research (Tartar 2018; Buck 1982). LANL’s
budget for laser research more than doubled from 1975 to 1981, from $14.5 to $38.9 million.
With this increase in funding, researchers at LANL began planning for the construction of an
even larger CO2 laser system, which they dubbed Antares. Originally designed as a 72-beam, 100
kJ system, Antares was scaled back to a 24-beam system that would be able to deliver 30–40 kJ
in 0.7 ns. The decision to redesign the system was because of data from Helios that indicated
long wavelength light (10 µm) caused the target containing the fusion-reaction fuel to preheat,
which hindered the achievement of fusion. LANL staff expected that the redesigned Antares laser

 5

would allow for the study of energy scaling in target experiments and allow them to perform
higher energy experiments than were possible on the Helios system. This smaller version of
Antares was expected to be operational by late 1983 (S. Batha, personal communication, January
5, 2026; Goldstone et al. 1984; Jansen 1983).

 Experiments performed with the Antares laser in the mid-1980s demonstrated several
advantages of high-powered CO2 lasers, including their high efficiency, reliability, and high
repetition rate. Researchers also developed an understanding of how light intensity and
wavelength influenced target physics. Ultimately, however, the issue of fuel pellets preheating

 8

was a hurdle that could not be overcome with the long wavelength CO2 lasers (Forslund and
Goldstone 1985; GAO 1990). In the words of Boyer, who had by that time retired from LANL,
“The longer wavelengths of both CO2 and glass lasers produced undesirably large hot-electron
components in the absorption process. The resulting self-generated magnetic fields are believed
to reduce the lateral heat conduction that was originally counted on to symmetrize the implosion
of the fuel pellet” (Boyer 1983b, p. 81). Because these issues with CO2 lasers could only be
overcome by constructing a system that would be both impractically large and expensive,
experimentation with the Antares system was halted. Up to that point, Antares represented a
significant investment in terms of research efforts and funding, as the construction and
operations from 1983 to 1985 cost $80 million. At the time, ERDA’s successor agency, the
Department of Energy (DOE), operated on a budget of approximately $12.7 billion annually.
With the end of the Antares program, LANL’s funding for laser research decreased and would
not reach the same levels again (GAO 1990; Fehner and Holl 1994; S. Batha, personal
communication, January 5, 2026).

 During this early period of LANL’s laser program, there was intense competition with
LLNL, which was engaged in similar research and development but was focused on using solid-
state lasers, particularly Nd glass lasers. According to C. Bruce Tarter, LLNL staff chose to work
with solid-state lasers because, although CO2 lasers were more efficient, they created laser
plasma instability (LPI) issues that were difficult to overcome. As to why LLNL staff opted to
work with Nd glass lasers, Tarter indicates that was due to their decision to pursue indirect drive
experimentation, yet another contrast to LANL’s early approach (2018).

 While LANL was scaling up its CO2 lasers, LLNL was undergoing a similar process with
solid-state, Nd glass laser systems. Its initial system was known as the Long Path laser because
two sets of reflecting prisms were placed 68 meters apart (Kidder 2006). The Long Path system
was completed in 1972, and while it typically provided 40–50 J in 10 ns, it could deliver as much
as 400 J (Empowering Light—Historic Accomplishments in Laser Research 2003; Kidder 2006).
Long Path was followed by more complex systems such as Janus, which had single and dual-
beam versions and produced 15–30 J, and Cyclops, a single-beam prototype that produced 100 J.
Both Janus and Cyclops were completed in 1975. While LANL staff were performing
experiments with the 2-beam, 2 kJ Gemini laser, LLNL staff were completing the 2-beam Argus

 9

laser system, which produced 1 kJ (Tarter 2018). Similarly, as LANL staff were building the
8-beam Helios laser, LLNL staff were building the 20-beam Shiva laser. The Shiva laser became
operational in 1977 and delivered 10 kJ in 1 ns the first time it was fired at full power, in contrast
to Helios’s 10 kJ in 0.5 ns result from its initial test. In terms of budget, LLNL’s laser program
had $7 million in funding by 1972, compared to LANL’s $4.5 million weapons research budget
for the same year. However, although LLNL’s nuclear research and development budget was
larger than LANL’s, both were spending roughly 21% of that budget on laser research as of 1974
(Campbell 1998; S. Batha, personal communication, January 5, 2026; MacDougall 1974).

 The rivalry between LANL and LLNL continued into the 1980s. While LANL
researchers were developing the 24-beam Antares laser, LLNL researchers were constructing the
Nova laser system. The first version of the Nova laser was intended to be 20-beam, but it was
scaled back to 10-beam so that some funding could be redirected to study issues related to light
wavelength (Tarter 2018). Just as the issue of hot electrons led LANL staff to end their work with
CO2 lasers, LLNL staff had a similar issue with the Shiva laser and accordingly had to modify
the design of the Nova laser (Tarter 2018; Kruer 1991). At this point, the laser-driven ICF
program was in a crisis moment and very close to failure. Then, work performed by R. S.
Craxton and others at LLE from 1979 to 1980 demonstrated that third harmonic generation could
resolve the problem with long wavelength light preheating fusion capsules, providing the
solution LLNL researchers needed to improve the functionality of the Nova laser (Craxton et al.
2015; Craxton 2025). The Nova laser system was finished in 1984 and was expected to be able to
produce 100 kJ. By 1988, LLNL’s laser program accounted for 25–33% of LLNL’s total budget,
with funding amounting to $275 million in 1989. The Nova laser remained at the center of this
financial investment, and following multiple modifications throughout the 1980s and 1990s, it
continued in use until it was replaced by NIF in the late 1990s (Campbell 1998; Tartar 2018).

The KrF laser era

 The lessons learned from Helios and Antares indicated that LANL staff would need to
work with shorter wavelength lasers if they were to achieve fusion and produce more energy than
was put into the system. As a result, researchers turned their focus to krypton fluoride (KrF)
lasers. LANL staff had used gas-discharge KrF lasers since 1976 as part of the Laboratory’s laser
isotope-separation program. Further work in the late 1970s led to the construction of a KrF laser

 10

that operated at 1 J per pulse, with 500 pulses each second (Jensen, 1985). As a result, staff had
ample experience with KrF lasers when the CO2 laser program was discontinued. With
wavelengths at 0.248 µm, KrF lasers appeared to offer a resolution to the problems that led to the
dismantling of the Antares laser.

 In 1985, LANL researchers began designing a new KrF system named Aurora. Aurora
was a first-generation KrF laser, with amplifiers designed to use electron-beam pumping instead
of gas-discharge. The system consisted of a 0.5 J, 5 ns pulse delivered to the amplifiers in 96
beams, with a total expected output of up to 15 kJ. Aurora was intended to “demonstrate the
production and extraction of laser energy and the optical techniques needed for a KrF fusion
laser” (Jensen 1985, p. 33). When Aurora was first fired late in 1988, its 96-beam pulse train
produced 2.5 kJ and the target chamber received 780 J in 48 beams. When a larger back mirror
was added the following year, the system produced 4 kJ of energy, with the target receiving
1.2 kJ in 36 beams (Rosocha 1989). One of the main objectives of the work with the Aurora laser
system was to position LANL as a primary candidate for the Laboratory Microfusion Facility
(LMF) that was being planned by the DOE (LMF Laboratory Microfusion Capability Study:
Phase 1, summary 1988; Hogan 1989; Harris et al. 1991).

 While LANL researchers were building up their experimental program with Aurora, they
also began work with ultrahigh brightness excimer lasers. There were two of these systems: Los
Alamos Bright Sources I and II (LABS I and II). The primary area of research with these lasers
was to further knowledge regarding how intense coherent ultraviolet radiation affected matter,
including atoms, ions, and plasmas. In addition, LANL staff used the systems to investigate
incoherent x-ray sources and x-ray laser pumping (Jensen et al. 1987). Whereas Aurora produced
power in the range of 1012 W, LABS I could deliver 25 mJ pulses at 3–10 Hz, with pulse lengths
of 0.7 ps, producing an intensity of up to 7 × 1017 W/cm2. Although LABS II had the same basic
design, it had higher brightness and was intended to deliver up to 1 J pulses at 1 Hz, with pulse
lengths of 0.1–0.2 ps, producing an intensity between 1018 to 1020 W/cm2 (Figueira 1989; Jensen
et al. 1987). Following an upgrade, LABS I, operating at the 0.248 µm KrF wavelength, was able
to deliver 35 mJ at 5 Hz (the pulse rate remained 0.7 ps). In contrast, LABS II was operating at

 12

the 0.308 µm xenon chloride (XeCl) wavelength and was able to deliver 250 mJ at 1 Hz, with
pulse lengths of 0.335 ps. LABS II’s intensity now exceeded 1020 W/cm2 (Kyrala et al. 1990).
The intensity of the LABS II system was remarkable for the time and is still rarely achieved even
with modern laser architecture, higher energies, and shorter pulse lengths.

 While the LABS systems continued in use into the mid-1990s, LANL researchers were
experiencing difficulties with the Aurora system. Although the system’s fundamental
wavelength, angular multiplexing, and beam uniformity made it a strong candidate for inertial
confinement fusion (ICF) applications, issues remained with the optics (Zvorykin 2023).
Specifically, coating for the optics had yet to be perfected, which led to a higher incidence of
optical damage. The solution at the time to prevent such damage was to provide a larger area for
the optics, but this increased the cost of operating the system. In addition, the amplifiers of the
Aurora system were also expensive, an issue that researchers believed could be resolved by
scaling the amplifiers to larger sizes. However, larger amplifiers had also demonstrated a lack of
reliability because of “high voltages, high currents, and long pump times required” (Harris et al.
1993, p. 328; Zvorykin 2023). A further issue arose regarding the brightness of the Aurora laser,
which was not sufficient for indirect-drive target illumination without adding larger final optics,
which once again would increase the cost of the system (Harris et al. 1993). In short, Aurora
could achieve the desired efficiency for a commercial energy driver, but it was too expensive. In
a 1988 article, LANL scientists estimated Aurora’s cost to be “several thousand dollars per
joule,” which was “not unexpected or alarming because it is a first-of-a-kind system,” but was
also far beyond the DOE’s cost goal for an LMF of $200 per J (Harris et al. 1988, pp. 167-169).
It was expected that the cost would decrease as more was learned about the system, but the
Aurora facility was nevertheless rejected as a candidate for the LMF.

 Although LANL’s funding for laser research had decreased following the end of the CO2
laser program, the Laboratory still received federal funding to pursue its KrF research. LANL’s
federal funding remained relatively steady throughout this period, from $29.5 million in 1986 to
$31 million in 1990. But after the Aurora laser facility was deemed too expensive to be
considered for the government’s LMF project, LANL’s funding for laser research decreased once
again. In 1991, LANL received only $23.7 million for laser research, and throughout the 1990s,

 13

the funding level remained relatively low in contrast to the previous two decades (S. Batha
personal communication, January 5, 2026).

 Faced with the need to decrease the cost of its KrF system, LANL staff began
constructing a second-generation KrF laser in 1991. This was the Mercury laser. Mercury was
built using many of the components from the Aurora system while also modifying or improving
them as necessary. The number of amplifiers was reduced overall, and the remaining amplifiers
were utilized in a double-pass configuration, leading to energy outputs that were expected to be
similar to those of Aurora. Another issue stemming from the large amplifiers used in Aurora—
the lack of reliability—was addressed by reducing the electrical stress on the system. Beam
quality was also improved by using an all-reflective optical system design, rather than the
partially-refractive design used in the Aurora system (Bigio et al. 1992; Czuchlewski et al. 1992).
The end result was “an 800 J 200 ps single-sided illumination laser,” with 48 beamlets intended
to produce 500 to 1000 J (Younger 1992, p. 76). In addition, reconfigured front-end components
were expected to “generate adjustable pulse lengths from 200 ps to 5 ns,” making the Mercury
system useful for a wide range of ICF experiments, including both direct- and indirect-drive
(Bigio et al. 1992, pp. 2-3). The end goal for LANL researchers was to minimize the cost of the
KrF system while maximizing the effectiveness of the technology.

 However, as the DOE continued to develop its plans for an LMF, the review committee
in charge of determining which technology the LMF would use decided the disadvantages of KrF
systems were not sufficiently overcome and that solid-state lasers promised the most cost-
effective approach to an LMF. Even when the DOE pivoted away from an LMF and toward the
NIF, the decision to primarily fund solid-state research remained in effect. This led LANL to
further reduce its KrF program, except for designing and fabricating targets and providing
assistance to the Naval Research Laboratory (NRL) for the construction of the Nike laser, a KrF
laser that was regarded as a backup for solid-state research (Koonin et al. 1991). As funding for
KrF research diminished, LANL researchers reorganized their laser and ICF research, this time
transitioning to solid-state lasers.

The glass laser era

 Although LANL researchers had primarily focused on CO2 lasers during the 1970s, they
also performed experiments with solid-state, glass lasers. In the early years of the laser division,

 14

one team experimented with a 50 J, ps glass laser that utilized harmonic generation crystals so
that interaction data could be obtained from different wavelengths ranging from 1 to 1/4 µm. In
addition, the team also developed a 500 J glass laser that employed frequency-conversion
crystals that allowed for experimentation from green to ultraviolet light to determine which
wavelength was most efficient for laser fusion (Boyer 1983a; Boyer 1983b).

 When LANL staff turned its focus primarily to glass lasers, they acquired a laser from
KMS, a private laser research company that closed in 1991 after its federal funding was
terminated. The government-owned Chroma laser was transferred to LANL, where it became
known as the Trident laser, a dual-beam system capable of delivering several hundred J with a
smaller beam used for target plasma radiography. The first experiments planned with the Trident
system included “long scale length plasma instability studies, colliding plasma experiments, and
characterization of direct and indirect drive targets” (Watt et al. 1992, p. 1). Over the next
decade, Trident was used for hohlraum experimentation and research on laser plasma instability
(LPI). In the early 2000s, the Trident laser was upgraded. This upgrade included adding a 30 TW
short pulse capability to one of the beams in 2002 and converting another beam to deliver
ultrashort pulses (20–30 J), which allowed for the study of relativistic laser-plasma interactions.
Further upgrades followed, and by 2007, the system had a capacity of 150 J/250 TW with high-
energy long-pulse beams. At that point, Trident was being used for LPI research and for LANL’s
work on High Energy Density Laboratory Plasmas (HEDLP) (Hegelich et al. 2004; Johnson and
Gibson 2004; Fernández et al. 2007).

 In 2008, facing a smaller laser research budget than any since the early 1970s, LANL
began advertising the Trident laser facility to external users. Trident provided potential users with
“high-energy short-pulse laser-matter interactions with powers in excess of 200 TW and energies
greater than 120 J… [with one of the three chambers having] a 10 in. manipulator allowing
OMEGA [LLE’s laser] and National Ignition Facility (NIF) diagnostics to be prototyped and
calibrated” (Batha et al. 2008, p. 1). Within a short time, the Trident laser facility and its staff
were assisting in the research of more than 60 users, the majority of whom were non-LANL, as
well as over 20 institutions, including AWE and foreign universities (Johnson et al. 2009). By
2016, however, Trident needed another upgrade. While the laser research budget had increased
slightly over the preceding eight years, the cost of improving the facility was determined to be
too expensive. In 2017, LANL leadership decided to close the Trident facility.

 Although LANL staff no longer had an onsite laser facility, they continued to perform
experiments and research on a variety of issues at other facilities, such as NIF and LLE’s Omega
laser facility. In particular, LANL staff brought their expertise to bear on hohlraum geometry and
implosion symmetry control to help LLNL staff achieve the long-sought goal of NIF, which was
the production of energy through laser-driven fusion (Woolsey 2022). In the few years since

 16

ignition was achieved at the NIF, LANL staff have continued to contribute to improving
outcomes at the NIF.

Conclusion

 From its beginning in 1969, LANL’s laser program has engaged in cutting-edge research
in various types of lasers. LANL’s early focus on CO2 lasers led to the construction of Helios, the
most powerful gas laser in the world in the late 1970s, with the Antares system set to surpass
Helios when it was completed. Throughout this early period, LANL requested and received
significant federal funding for its groundbreaking projects. When LANL pivoted to focus on KrF
lasers, the staff made significant contributions to KrF laser technology through their work on the
Aurora and Mercury systems. The high-brightness excimer lasers LABS I and II were similar
examples of cutting-edge laser technology. Due in part to the issues that arose with KrF lasers,
the expense of resolving these issues, and declining federal funding, LANL staff transitioned to
work with glass lasers in the mid-1990s. However, that work supported the development of the
NIF, and the research performed by LANL staff with the Trident system made significant
contributions to research into hohlraum geometry and LPI.

 During the 1970s, LANL and LLNL were in close competition, each vying for federal
funding for defense and energy research projects. One aspect was the development of high-
energy and high-power lasers for ICF purposes. LANL focused on developing gas (excimer)
lasers, culminating in the Antares 30–40 kJ CO2 laser. By the early 1980s, both faced the same
hurdle—how to overcome the problem of hot electrons produced by long wavelength light—and
both were forced to redesign their laser systems. When problems with the application of long
wavelength lasers became known, LANL developed high-energy short-wavelength KrF lasers.
When the DOE proposed LMF and, later, NIF, LANL’s rivalry with LLNL continued as both labs
sought to provide the technology and location for the facilities. When LLNL was chosen as the
site for NIF, LANL staff became collaborators to ensure that NIF would succeed in its goal of
achieving fusion.

 This historical overview, while brief, indicates just how significant LANL’s involvement
in the field of laser technology was in the second half of the 20th century. LANL scientists
helped to advance and expand laser science through the research they performed with gas,
excimer, and glass laser systems. That work stands alongside LANL’s other contributions to the

 17

scientific community and further enhances LANL’s well-deserved reputation as a global leader in
science and technology.
