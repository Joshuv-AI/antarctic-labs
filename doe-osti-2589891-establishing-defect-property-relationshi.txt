Project abstract
Studies of new families of two-dimensional nanomaterials (2DNMs) have established that they
possess unique properties that diverge from those of their bulk counterparts. This project aimed to
determine how tolerant 2DNMs are to extreme photon and particle fluxes and to identify the
mechanisms governing their radiation response. Early work indicated that graphene is not as
representative of other 2DNMs as previously assumed; however, the origin of this difference
remained unclear.

To address these knowledge gaps, this project investigated the physical processes occurring at
multiple length scales in transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) and quantified their structural
stability and property evolution under far-from-equilibrium conditions. In-situ and ex-situ ion and
electron irradiations were performed to directly control and monitor defect formation in TMDs.
Complementary density functional theory (DFT) and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were
employed to elucidate the mechanisms of defect generation and evolution.

The outcomes of this work established mechanistic understanding of irradiation-induced defect
formation in 2DNMs, quantified the radiation tolerance of TMDs, and built a fundamental knowledge
base for correlating defect structures with material properties. Collectively, these results provide
new insights into the stability of low-dimensional materials in extreme environments and enable the
predictive design of radiation-tolerant 2DNMs.

Project overview
The DOE Basic Energy Sciences Early Career Award " Establishing defect-property relationships for
2D-nanomaterials" supported the research of principal investigator Assel Aitkaliyeva at the
University of Florida. The overarching objective of this work was to investigate how the low-
dimensional, interface-dominated nature of 2DNMs alters defect production, cascade evolution,
and defect reactions under irradiation. The project focused on TMDs, with molybdenum disulfide
(MoS₂) serving as a model system because it represents the broader class of layered 2DNMs while
exhibiting more complex defect chemistry than graphene. The goal was to determine how irradiation-
induced defects influence material stability and whether such interactions can be harnessed for
defect engineering and self-repair. This early career award supported one postdoctoral scholar, one
PhD student, and two MS students. Fifteen presentations (including four invited talks) and six peer-
reviewed journal publications resulted from this research, with four additional manuscripts currently
in preparation.

Research activities
Goals and hypotheses

The goal of this project was to establish mechanistic links between defect formation, evolution, and
material properties in 2DNMs subjected to irradiation. The research aimed to determine how
dimensionality, interfaces, and substrates influence defect stability and to assess whether radiation
can be harnessed as a tool for controlled synthesis and property modification rather than solely a
degradation mechanism.

The work was organized around two central hypotheses:

 1. Defect-control hypothesis: Defect densities in 2DNMs can be deliberately manipulated
 through ion irradiation to control optical, electronic, and structural properties. If the
 fundamental mechanisms governing defect production and accumulation are understood,
 extended defects can be introduced in a beneficial manner to tune performance.
 2. Self-healing and doping hypothesis: Atoms displaced during irradiation can refill vacancies
 and initiate local self-repair, creating new defect complexes that alter bonding and electronic
 behavior. Such interactions provide an alternative, radiation-assisted route to doping and
 property enhancement.

To test these hypotheses, the project was divided into four interconnected thrusts:

 1. Defect formation and recombination: Identify defect formation and recombination
 mechanisms and the role of sinks in the radiation response of 2DNMs.

 2

 2. Interface and substrate effects: Determine how supporting substrates and interfacial
 coupling influence defect production and annihilation.
 3. Controlled defect engineering: Use irradiation to control defect density and correlate defect
 populations with measurable property changes (tests Hypothesis 1).
 4. Self-healing and radiation-induced doping: Examine the possibility of defect refilling,
 complex formation, and environmental doping during irradiation (tests Hypothesis 2).

Together, these efforts help build a quantitative framework for predicting how 2DNMs respond to and
recover from irradiation, advancing fundamental understanding of defect-mediated phenomena in
materials operating far from equilibrium.

Key findings
This project combined in-situ and ex-situ irradiation experiments with multi-scale modeling and
optical/electron microscopy to establish how 2DNMs form, evolve, and heal defects under
irradiation. The work focused on monolayer and few-layer MoS₂ as a representative TMD, supported
by studies on related 2D/3D heterostructures. All data summarized below were published in peer-
reviewed journals. Rather than restating the full experimental results, this section provides a concise
synthesis of the major findings, with references to the original publications for detailed analysis and
discussion.

Thrust 1: Defect formation and recombination mechanisms

This thrust focused on identifying how defects form, migrate, and annihilate in monolayer (1L) and
few-layer MoS₂ under ion irradiation. Ion irradiations with H⁺, He⁺, and Au²⁺ ions (100 keV - 3 MeV)
were combined with Raman, photoluminescence (PL), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)
analyses to monitor defect generation and recovery. MD and DFT simulations quantified defect
energetics and energy-transfer pathways. Key results:

 • Dominant defect type: Sulfur vacancies (Vₛ) are the primary defects produced under all
 irradiation conditions, consistent with the lower displacement threshold for S compared to
 Mo. Figure 1 shows high angular dark field (HAADF) scanning transmission electron
 microscopy (TEM) micrographs of the defects and Table 1 lists their formation and binding
 energies.
 • Fluence-dependent defect evolution: At low fluences (≤10¹³ cm⁻²), isolated vacancies
 dominate. At intermediate fluences (10¹⁴ - 10¹⁵ cm⁻²), vacancy clusters form; beyond 10¹⁵
 cm⁻², nanopores and local amorphization occur, as shown in Figure 1.
 • Recombination behavior: Partial defect recovery occurs in supported samples, attributed
 to substrate-mediated energy dissipation. In suspended samples, vacancies persist,
 allowing long-lived localized excitons to form.
 • Modeling insight: MD simulations established that at 100 keV-3 MeV, sputtering dominates
 damage formation in substrate-supported monolayers (see Table 2). Theoretical defect
 yields matched XPS-measured S:Mo ratios.

Thrust 1 established a quantitative baseline for how energy, ion species, and substrate coupling
dictate defect formation and annihilation kinetics in 2D systems.

Thrust 2: Interface and substrate effects

This thrust examined how interfaces and supporting substrates affect defect generation, recovery,
and energy dissipation. MoS₂ films were prepared on SiO₂, sapphire, and metallic substrates, and
compared with suspended films. Complementary experiments coupled MoS₂ to spin-crossover
(SCO) crystals to study strain transfer and mechanical actuation. Key results:

 • Substrate influence: Supported MoS₂ displayed delayed amorphization and enhanced
 defect recombination, confirming the substrate’s role as a heat and defect sink.
 • Recoil contributions: At higher fluences and low ion energies (≤100 keV), sputtered
 substrate atoms contribute to additional defect generation in the overlying monolayer, an
 effect absent at MeV energies.
 • Dynamic strain transfer: SCO-based 2D/3D heterostructures demonstrated reversible
 strain coupling of 0.4–0.5%, which produced blue-shifts in Raman and PL peaks and partial
 recovery of irradiated MoS₂ optical behavior.
 • Interface-enabled stability: Substrate-coupled layers exhibited slower vacancy
 coalescence and reduced local strain fields compared to suspended configurations.

Thrust 2 revealed that interfaces are not passive supports but active participants in damage
evolution. Controlled substrate coupling provides a pathway to tailor stability, strain, and recovery in
irradiated 2DNMs.

Thrust 3: Controlled defect engineering

This thrust tested whether defect densities could be deliberately manipulated to tune physical
properties, addressing the Defect-Control Hypothesis (H1). Systematic variation of ion energy and
fluence was used to control defect populations. Optical measurements (Raman, PL) tracked how
changes in defect density translated into measurable property shifts. Key results:

 • Excitonic control: The B/A exciton intensity ratio increased from ~0.03 (pristine) to ~0.52 at
 10¹⁴ cm⁻² fluence, confirming controlled modulation of optical transitions.
 • Vibrational fingerprints: Raman E′ (~382 cm⁻¹) and A′₁ (~407 cm⁻¹) modes shifted
 predictably with fluence, serving as reliable proxies for defect density.
 • Strain and lattice tuning: DFT and experimental data both showed that defect introduction
 leads to compressive strain, tunable via fluence and ion type, as shown in Figure 2.
 • Twist and Moiré engineering: Defect-induced strain fields were used to tune interlayer twist
 angles in MoS₂ homobilayers, producing ordered moiré superlattices (see Figure 3).
 • Mechanical-thermal coupling: Surface-acoustic-wave (SAW) studies revealed phonon
 focusing and elastic anisotropy tied to defect density, yielding a Young’s modulus of 247 ± 9
 GPa, consistent with theory.

Thrust 3 validated Hypothesis 1: defect densities in MoS₂ can be precisely controlled via irradiation,
enabling reproducible tuning of excitonic, vibrational, and mechanical properties.

Thrust 4: Self-healing and radiation-induced doping

This thrust explored whether irradiation could induce self-repair and doping, directly testing the Self-
Healing/Doping Hypothesis (H2). XPS, Raman, and DFT analyses were used to identify vacancy
refilling, impurity adsorption, and oxygen complex formation. Ion fluence and annealing temperature
were varied to map the transition between defect accumulation and recovery regimes. Key results:

 • O₂ adsorption as a healing mechanism: Immediately after irradiation, newly formed sulfur
 vacancies absorbed environmental O₂, forming O₂-S or O-S complexes that compressed the
 lattice and restored local bonding (shown in Figure 4).
 • Optimal self-healing window: O₂ uptake was maximized between 5 × 10¹⁵ and 5 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻²,
 where oxygen occupancy stabilized the structure and partially restored optical response.
 • High-fluence limit: Above 5 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻² (for 100 keV H⁺), the material amorphized and further
 O₂ absorption was suppressed, defining an upper bound for defect-mediated self-repair.
 • Evidence of doping: Oxygen substitution introduced shallow donor states, detectable via
 changes in PL peak positions and XPS Mo⁵⁺ features.
 • Computational validation: DFT and KMC modeling confirmed the energetic favorability of
 O₂ occupation at vacancy sites and provided kinetic barriers consistent with experimental
 uptake rates.

Thrust 4 proved Hypothesis 2: irradiation can drive partial self-repair and environmental doping in
MoS₂. The work established quantitative limits for fluence-dependent healing and clarified the role
of oxygen in stabilizing irradiated 2D lattices.

Together, the four thrusts demonstrate that irradiation can serve as a quantitative, controllable
parameter for designing and repairing 2D materials. The project established:

 • A damage-production framework for substrate-supported MoS₂ (sputtering-dominated at
 100 keV, negligible at 3 MeV).
 • A self-healing and doping mechanism governed by O₂-vacancy interactions.
 • A strain-mediated approach for coupling mechanical and optical tunability in 2D/3D
 heterostructures.
 • A multimodal correlation between defect density, optical response, and structural evolution.

Collectively, these findings bridge atomistic radiation physics and macroscale property control,
forming a predictive foundation for radiation-tolerant and self-healing 2D nanomaterials.
