From Digital to Physical Reservoir Computing: Co-Optimizing Soft Robotic Reservoirs via Dynamics Matching
Nicola Visentin \corresponding, Maximilian Stölzle, Mariano Ramírez Montero, Francesco Braghin, Daniela Rus, Cosimo Della Santina

Abstract
Soft robotic substrates are promising for Physical Reservoir Computing (PRC) because their compliant nonlinear dynamics can provide temporal memory, high-dimensional state transformations, and efficient inference. However, physical reservoirs are often adopted as-is rather than pretrained or co-optimized, potentially limiting soft robotic PRC performance relative to digital reservoirs. We investigate whether a physical reservoir can instead be pretrained against high-performing digital reference dynamics. Our formulation jointly optimizes physical parameters, a diffeomorphic physical–reference state map, and feedforward-feedback control using a differentiable physical model and an acceleration-level equation-error objective that avoids temporal integration. As a proof of concept, we instantiate the formulation with simulated soft robots, a Random Oscillators Network (RON) reference, and parallel multi-start gradient descent. We evaluate the optimized reservoirs on classification (sMNIST and ADIAC) and forecasting (Mackey–Glass and Lorenz96) tasks across four reservoir dimensions. Compared with unoptimized soft robot reservoirs, the optimized reservoirs achieve a mean relative improvement of 33.7 % 33.7\text{\,}\mathrm{\%} across all tasks and datasets, while remaining close to the digital reference. These results demonstrate the feasibility of dynamics-level co-optimization for the simulated soft robotic reservoirs considered here.

Introduction

Reservoir Computing (RC)
(
Nakajima and Fischer 2021
;
Lukoševičius and Jaeger 2009
)
processes temporal data with a fixed nonlinear dynamical system, the
reservoir
, followed by a trainable readout layer. The reservoir expands the input history into a rich state representation, while learning is confined to the readout. As a result, RC avoids backpropagation through the full recurrent dynamics and can be substantially cheaper to train than conventional Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs)
(
Werbos 1990
)
. Physical Reservoir Computing (PRC) extends this idea by replacing the simulated reservoir with a physical substrate
(
Nakajima 2020
;
Dale et al. 2021
)
. Since the reservoir dynamics unfold directly in the physical medium rather than being numerically simulated, PRC can reduce digital computation during inference and is therefore attractive for efficient real-time or embodied processing. Among the many substrates explored for PRC
(
Tanaka et al. 2019
)
, soft robots are especially promising because their compliant, coupled, nonlinear dynamics can naturally provide temporal memory and high-dimensional state transformations
(
Hauser 2021
;
Hauser et al. 2011
)
.

Despite this promise, current PRC systems still lag behind digital reservoirs. In soft robotic PRC, existing demonstrations typically address relatively simple tasks and often use the physical body as-is, whereas digital reservoirs are explicitly selected or tuned for RC
(
Nakajima et al. 2015
;
Horii et al. 2021
)
. Existing reservoir-improvement methods offer limited guidance for this setting: many are tailored to specific digital architectures, rely on hand-tuned heuristics, or require repeated downstream task evaluations
(
Lukoševičius and Jaeger 2009
)
. Likewise, generic soft robot co-design methods
(
Bodily et al. 2017
;
Stölzle et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2023b
)
do not directly resolve the PRC design problem. Optimizing the morphology and control for final task performance would require rolling out each candidate physical reservoir, training the readout, and differentiating through the integrated dynamics of the full PRC pipeline. Replacing this with proxy or surrogate objectives such as expressiveness or memory capacity avoids full task optimization, but makes the design criterion indirect and partly arbitrary. This leaves a clear gap: PRC lacks a tractable way to design physical reservoirs whose dynamics are useful for RC without optimizing through every downstream task.

We address this gap by investigating whether a physical reservoir can be pretrained to approximate a high-performing digital reference reservoir. For differentiable second-order systems, we jointly adapt the physical parameters, a diffeomorphic physical–reference state map, and feedforward-feedback control using an acceleration-level equation-error objective
(
Lee et al. 2024
)
. A differentiable physical model provides gradients without temporal integration. As a proof of concept, we use a physics-inspired Random Oscillators Network (RON)
(
Ceni et al. 2024
)
as reference and simulated Lagrangian soft robots as physical reservoirs, optimized with parallel multi-start gradient descent. After pretraining, the optimized reservoir is inserted into a standard PRC pipeline, and only the readout is trained. The full pipeline remains hybrid: its controller, map, and readout are digital. Fig.
1
provides an overview.

We evaluate this proof of concept in simulation. The contributions are:

1.

We introduce a dynamics-level pretraining perspective in which a physical reservoir is co-optimized against structurally compatible, high-performing digital reference dynamics rather than treated as fixed or selected as-is.

2.

For differentiable second-order systems, we formulate this idea using a digital reference reservoir, a diffeomorphic physical–reference state map, feedforward-feedback controllers co-optimized with physical parameters, and an acceleration-level dynamics-matching objective that avoids temporal rollouts.

3.

We instantiate and evaluate the formulation for simulated soft robotic reservoirs using parallel multi-start gradient descent, providing a proof of concept on classification and forecasting tasks.

2
Related Work

Physical Reservoir Computing

PRC exploits the fact that the reservoir is a fixed nonlinear dynamical system, and thus any physical medium with rich, history-dependent dynamics can replace a software-simulated reservoir
(
Nakajima 2020
;
Dale et al. 2021
)
. This follows the principles of morphological computation
(
Hauser et al. 2014
;
Füchslin et al. 2013
)
, where the body performs part of the computation and reduces digital processing.

Many substrates have been explored for PRC, including photonic systems
(
Van der Sande et al. 2017
)
, nanomaterials
(
Stieg et al. 2014
)
, MEMS
(
Takemura et al. 2023
)
, biological tissue
(
Obien et al. 2015
)
, quantum dynamics
(
Fujii and Nakajima 2016
)
and others surveyed in
(
Tanaka et al. 2019
)
. Soft robots are especially relevant
(
Hauser 2021
)
, since their compliant, coupled nonlinear dynamics can naturally provide the high-dimensional state representations and memory required for RC
(
Hauser et al. 2011
)
.

Real-world soft robot PRC has been demonstrated, for example in
(
Nakajima et al. 2015
;
Horii et al. 2021
)
. Nevertheless, these systems mostly solve simple tasks and still underperform compared to digital reservoirs, which are explicitly tailored for RC. Since designing a physical substrate for reservoir performance is harder than tuning a digital reservoir, the physical reservoir is usually employed as-is, without pretraining or pre-selection.

Reservoir Pretraining

In digital RC, reservoir design usually starts from random architectures with hand-tuned global parameters
(
Lukoševičius and Jaeger 2009
)
. For the well-known Echo State Networks
(
Jaeger 2001
)
, this includes scaling the spectral radius to promote the echo state property
(
Jaeger 2002
)
; more generally, topology, timescales, and architecture-specific parameters can be tuned by search
(
Liebald 2004
;
Jaeger et al. 2007
;
Siewert and Wustlich 2007
;
Ceni et al. 2024
)
. Other approaches optimize task-independent proxy objectives that capture desirable reservoir properties, such as memory, separation, activation entropy, or self-prediction
(
Schrauwen et al. 2008
;
Mayer and Browne 2004
)
. Finally, task-driven adaptation uses labels or downstream performance to tune weights, topology, or global parameters, typically through an outer optimization loop with repeated task evaluations
(
Jiang et al. 2008
;
Ishii et al. 2004
)
.

These methods offer limited guidance for PRC because they are architecture-specific, proxy-based, or require repeated task evaluations. Viewed as teacher-student dynamics distillation
(
Hinton et al. 2015
)
, our method transfers a digital reservoir’s vector field into a physical one without repeated task-level optimization. Related work trains recurrent networks from teacher-generated dynamics (full-FORCE)
(
DePasquale et al. 2018
)
, or mirrors a digital ESN in analog spiking hardware
(
He et al. 2019
)
; we instead co-optimize physical morphology, a state map, and control through offline equation error. Unlike MRAC
(
Landau 1979
)
, this requires neither online adaptation nor the reference at inference.

Physical Reservoir Pretraining

This section formulates the pretraining approach for differentiable second-order physical reservoirs with coordinate spaces compatible with a diffeomorphism. A digital reference reservoir supplies target dynamics, while the physical parameters, state map, and controller are jointly adapted. Sec.
4
presents one simulated soft robot instantiation; Fig.
2
illustrates its pretraining setup.

Figure 2:
Soft robot instantiation of the proposed pretraining formulation. The reference position
\mathbf{y}
, velocity
\mathbf{\dot{y}}
, and input
\mathbf{u}
are mapped into the physical space; the resulting acceleration
\mathbf{\ddot{Q}}
is mapped back to the reference space and compared with the target acceleration
\mathbf{\ddot{y}}
. Dashed lines denote digital components, while solid lines indicate elements to be implemented physically.

3.1
Digital Reference Reservoir

The formulation requires a digital reservoir whose dynamics define the optimization target. The reference should exhibit strong task performance and structural compatibility with the physical substrate, enabling transfer of task-relevant dynamics.

We consider reference dynamics of the second-order form

\mathbf{\ddot{y}}=f_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{\dot{y}},\mathbf{u}),

(1)

where
\mathbf{y}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
are the reference coordinates,
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{\dot{y}})
is the reference state, and
\mathbf{u}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
is the input. The specific instantiation of
f_{\mathrm{ref}}
used here is discussed in Sec.
4.1
.

3.2
Differentiable Physical Reservoir

Let
\mathbf{Q}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{Q}}
denote aggregate physical reservoir coordinates,
\mathbf{T}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{T}}
the actuation, and
\theta_{\mathrm{phys}}
trainable physical parameters. We assume a differentiable second-order model

\mathbf{\ddot{Q}}=f_{\mathrm{phys}}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{\dot{Q}},\mathbf{T};\theta_{\mathrm{phys}}).

(2)

Define the state
\mathbf{Z}=[\mathbf{Q}^{\top},\mathbf{\dot{Q}}^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n_{Q}}
.

Controller

The input
\mathbf{u}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
reaches the physical reservoir through a feedforward term
\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{ff}}(\mathbf{u})
. A feedback term
\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{fb}}(\mathbf{Z})
introduces dynamical coupling and augments reservoir expressiveness. The complete actuation
\mathbf{T}(t)
is

\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{u};\theta_{\mathrm{contr}})=\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{fb}}(\mathbf{Z};\theta_{\mathrm{contr}}^{(\mathrm{fb})})+\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{ff}}(\mathbf{u};\theta_{\mathrm{contr}}^{(\mathrm{ff})}),

(3)

where
\theta_{\mathrm{contr}}=\{\theta_{\mathrm{contr}}^{(\mathrm{fb})},\theta_{\mathrm{contr}}^{(\mathrm{ff})}\}
are learnable parameters.

3.3
Physical–Reference Reservoir Mapping

The digital reference and physical coordinates may differ in meaning and scale. We therefore require
n_{y}=n_{Q}
and introduce a map
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{Q}}
:

\mathbf{Q}=\phi(\mathbf{y};\theta_{\mathrm{map}}),\quad\mathbf{y}=\phi^{-1}(\mathbf{Q};\theta_{\mathrm{map}})=\psi(\mathbf{Q};\theta_{\mathrm{map}}).

(4)

\theta_{\mathrm{map}}
are learnable parameters. The map must be an invertible, second-order smooth diffeomorphism so positions, velocities, and accelerations can be transformed consistently through the Jacobians and Hessians of Eq. (
4
).

More expressive maps can improve dynamics matching, while simpler maps reduce digital computation. Sec.
4.1
specifies the map used in our experiments; further details are provided in the supplementary material.

3.4
Optimization Strategy

Let
\mathbf{y}
denote the digital reference coordinates and
\mathbf{\hat{y}}=\phi^{-1}(\mathbf{Q})
the mapped physical coordinates. Pretraining adapts
\theta=\{\theta_{\mathrm{phys}},\theta_{\mathrm{map}},\theta_{\mathrm{contr}}\}
, namely the physical, map, and controller parameters. The equation-error loss
(
Lee et al. 2024
)
compares accelerations and avoids temporal integration:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\min_{\theta}&\mathcal{L}(\theta),\\[6.0pt]
\text{s.t.}&\theta_{\mathrm{phys}}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{phys}},\quad\theta_{\mathrm{map}}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{map}},\\[4.0pt]
\end{array}

(5)

with loss function
\mathcal{L}
as the Mean Square Error (MSE)

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathrm{MSE}(\mathbf{\ddot{y}},\mathbf{\hat{\ddot{y}}}(\theta))=\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{batch}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{batch}}}\left\|\mathbf{\ddot{y}}^{(i)}-\mathbf{\hat{\ddot{y}}}^{(i)}(\theta)\right\|^{2}.

For samples
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{\dot{y}},\mathbf{u})
, labels
\mathbf{\ddot{y}}
follow from Eq. (
1
), while the mapped physical acceleration
\mathbf{\hat{\ddot{y}}}
—obtained from Eqs. (
2
), (
3
) and (
4
) with its derivatives—is

\begin{gathered}\mathbf{\hat{\ddot{Q}}}=f_{\mathrm{phys}}\bigl(\mathbf{Q}_{\phi},\mathbf{\dot{Q}}_{\phi},\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{Z}_{\phi},\mathbf{u})\bigr),\\
\mathbf{\hat{\ddot{y}}}=\mathrm{D}\psi(\mathbf{Q}_{\phi})[\mathbf{\hat{\ddot{Q}}}]+\mathrm{D}^{2}\psi(\mathbf{Q}_{\phi})[\mathbf{\dot{Q}}_{\phi},\mathbf{\dot{Q}}_{\phi}],\end{gathered}

(6)

where
\mathbf{Q}_{\phi}=\phi(\mathbf{y})
,
\mathbf{\dot{Q}}_{\phi}=\mathrm{D}\phi(\mathbf{y})[\mathbf{\dot{y}}]
, and
\mathbf{Z}_{\phi}=[\mathbf{Q}_{\phi}^{\top},\mathbf{\dot{Q}}_{\phi}^{\top}]^{\top}
. The expressions
\mathrm{D}f(\mathbf{x})[\mathbf{v}]
and
\mathrm{D}^{2}f(\mathbf{x})[\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}]
denote the first and second Fréchet derivatives of
f
at
\mathbf{x}
applied to the indicated directions. The sets
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{phys}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{map}}
enforce physical feasibility and mapping well-posedness.

4
Physical Reservoir Optimization Experiments

This section instantiates the pretraining formulation from Sec.
3
with Lagrangian-inspired digital reference dynamics, soft robot model, map, controller, and optimizer used in our experiments. We then evaluate how closely the optimized physical reservoir matches the target dynamics.

4.1
Optimization Setup

Digital Reference Reservoir

We use the Random Oscillators Network (RON) from
(
Ceni et al. 2024
)
as reference dynamics because it performs well on temporal tasks and, as a network of nonlinearly coupled oscillators, has dynamics compatible with soft robotic systems. A RON consists of
n_{y}
mass-spring-damper elements coupled by a nonlinear forcing term:

\mathbf{\ddot{y}}=-\bm{\gamma}\odot\mathbf{y}-\bm{\varepsilon}\odot\mathbf{\dot{y}}+\tanh(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{V}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{b}).

Here,
\mathbf{y}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
collects the oscillator positions (reservoir coordinates),
\mathbf{u}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
is the input,
\bm{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
and
\bm{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
are stiffness and damping factors, and
\mathbf{W},\mathbf{V},\mathbf{b}
define the nonlinear coupling and input forcing.
\odot
denotes the element-wise product.

We consider four benchmarks involving sequential inputs or time-series prediction: sequential-MNIST (sMNIST), ADIAC, Mackey–Glass and Lorenz96. The original RON configurations in
(
Ceni et al. 2024
)
use relatively large reservoirs, making differentiable soft robot optimization prohibitively expensive. We therefore use four reduced reference dimensions for each task,
n_{y}\in\{6,9,12,15\}
, which keeps the optimization tractable while still testing how the approach scales with reservoir size. This inevitably limits the capability of the model, but our goal is to test whether optimizing the physical reservoir can effectively improve PRC performance, rather than achieving state-of-the-art results.

For each task and reservoir dimension, the remaining RON parameters are selected through task-specific manual tuning following
(
Ceni et al. 2024
)
. This yields 16 pretraining datasets, each obtained by sampling
m=10^{5}
points
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{\dot{y}},\mathbf{u})
and computing the labels
\mathbf{\ddot{y}}
with
f_{\mathrm{ref}}
. Further details on the RON model, task-specific configurations, reduced hidden space dimensionality, and dataset generation are given in the supplementary material.

Soft Robot Model

Each physical reservoir element is a soft robot with generalized coordinates
\mathbf{q}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and Euler–Lagrange dynamics
(
Della Santina et al. 2023
)

\mathbf{M}\mathbf{\ddot{q}}+\mathbf{C}\mathbf{\dot{q}}+\mathbf{D}\mathbf{\dot{q}}+\mathbf{V}=\bm{\tau},

where
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})
,
\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{\dot{q}})
,
\mathbf{D}
, and
\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{q})
denote mass, Coriolis/centrifugal, damping, and potential terms, and
\bm{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the actuation. For
N
robots, let
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn}
and
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn}
stack their coordinates and actuation, and let
\mathbf{\tilde{M}}
,
\mathbf{\tilde{C}}
,
\mathbf{\tilde{D}}
, and
\mathbf{\tilde{V}}
collect the corresponding dynamics. Then

\mathbf{\tilde{M}}\mathbf{\ddot{Q}}+\mathbf{\tilde{C}}\mathbf{\dot{Q}}+\mathbf{\tilde{D}}\mathbf{\dot{Q}}+\mathbf{\tilde{V}}=\mathbf{T},

which instantiates Eq. (
2
) with
\theta_{\mathrm{phys}}=\theta_{\mathrm{rob}}
, the robot physical parameters. We model each robot as a slender continuum arm using the planar Piecewise Constant Strain (PCS) model
(
Renda et al. 2018
)
. Each segment contributes bending, shear, and axial strains, so a robot with
n_{\mathrm{pcs}}
segments has
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{3n_{\mathrm{pcs}}}
. We choose
N
and
n_{\mathrm{pcs}}
such that
3Nn_{\mathrm{pcs}}=n_{y}
. Tab.
1
reports the specific values of
N
and
n_{\mathrm{pcs}}
; the supplementary material gives further model and implementation details.

Controller

We instantiate the feedforward and feedback controllers from Sec.
3.2
as two Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs):

\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{ff}}(\mathbf{u})=\mathrm{MLP}(\mathbf{u};\theta_{\mathrm{contr}}^{\mathrm{(ff)}}),\quad\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{fb}}(\mathbf{Z})=\mathrm{MLP}(\mathbf{Z};\theta_{\mathrm{contr}}^{\mathrm{(fb)}}).

The trainable controller parameters are
\theta_{\mathrm{contr}}=\{\theta_{\mathrm{contr}}^{\mathrm{(ff)}},\theta_{\mathrm{contr}}^{\mathrm{(fb)}}\}
, i.e., the networks’ weights and biases. Both MLPs use two hidden layers of 64 neurons, tanh activations, and a linear output layer.

Physical–Reference Reservoir Mapping

We implement the map
\phi
in Eq. (
4
) as an affine transformation parametrized through a Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)
\mathbf{Q}=\phi(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{c}
. Here,
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{3Nn_{\mathrm{pcs}}\times n_{y}}
,
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{3Nn_{\mathrm{pcs}}}
, and
s_{i}
are the singular values in
\bm{\Sigma}
. If
s_{i}>0
for all
i
and
3Nn_{\mathrm{pcs}}=n_{y}
, then
\phi
satisfies the required mapping properties from Sec.
3.3
, and its inverse is

\mathbf{y}=\phi^{-1}(\mathbf{Q})=\psi(\mathbf{Q})=\mathbf{A}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{c}\right).

This design reduces computation by dropping the second-order derivatives in Eq. (
6
). The learnable mapping parameters
\theta_{\mathrm{map}}
are the offset vector
\mathbf{c}
and the matrix
\mathbf{A}
.

Optimization Algorithm

We solve (
5
) with parallel multi-start gradient descent using Adam
(
Kingma and Ba 2015
)
, cosine learning-rate decay, linear warmup, and gradient clipping. Differentiable soft robot simulators
(
Stölzle et al. 2026b
;
Stölzle et al. 2026a
;
Wang et al. 2023a
)
provide scalable parameter gradients; independent starts enable parallel execution and reduce sensitivity to local minima
(
Antonova et al. 2023
)
.

For this instantiation,
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{phys}}
requires strictly positive geometric and physical parameters
\theta_{\mathrm{phys}}=\theta_{\mathrm{rob}}
. The mapping constraint
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{map}}
enforces
s_{i}>s_{\mathrm{min}}=10^{-4}
to maintain invertibility and conditioning. In all cases, lower bounds are imposed with
\bar{\theta}=\ln(1+\mathrm{e}^{\theta})+\theta_{\mathrm{min}}
.

The controller parameters are initialized with the Glorot/Xavier approach
(
Glorot and Bengio 2010
)
, the mapping is initialized with
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{0}
and a well-conditioned SVD factorization of
\mathbf{A}
, and the robot parameters are sampled from predefined (physical) ranges. Specific initialization values, hardware, software, and hyperparameters are reported in the supplementary material.

For each of the 16 datasets described above, we ran four parallel trials, each initialized with a different random seed.

4.2
Optimization Results

Tab.
1
reports the optimization results (“Full pretraining”) as the mean and standard deviation, across the four random seeds, of the test Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) between the target accelerations
\mathbf{\ddot{y}}
and the physical reservoir accelerations
\mathbf{\hat{\ddot{y}}}
. To isolate the contribution of morphology optimization, we evaluate a baseline (“
\theta_{\mathrm{rob}}
fixed”) reflecting common PRC settings in which the robot morphology is treated as given:
\theta_{\mathrm{rob}}
remains fixed after initialization, while only the mapping and controller parameters,
\theta_{\mathrm{map}}
and
\theta_{\mathrm{contr}}
, are optimized. This tests whether learning the map and controller suffices to reproduce the desired dynamics or whether adapting the physical substrate is beneficial.

The results in Tab.
1
show that fixing
\theta_{\mathrm{rob}}
leads to larger RMSE in all scenarios. Averaging the per-
n_{y}
relative reductions in test RMSE, full pretraining improves over the fixed-morphology baseline by
90.2\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
for sMNIST,
61.6\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
for ADIAC,
77.1\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
for Mackey–Glass, and
86.4\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
for Lorenz96. This indicates that morphology optimization is important for accurately matching the target digital dynamics. The fixed-morphology baseline also exhibits substantially larger standard deviations, showing that its outcome is more sensitive to initialization. In contrast, full pretraining yields low standard deviations across the tested settings, indicating consistent convergence across random seeds.

The test RMSE generally increases with the reservoir dimension
n_{y}
. This is expected, since larger reservoirs induce more complex target dynamics that are harder for the physical reservoir to approximate precisely. Moreover, since the dataset dimension
m
is kept fixed while the phase-space dimension of

6
Conclusions

We presented a pretraining method for soft robotic physical reservoirs that co-optimizes morphology, a physical–reference state map, and a controller to approximate digital reference dynamics. The method uses a dynamics-level equation-error objective, avoiding task-level optimization during pretraining.
Using a Random Oscillators Network (RON)
(
Ceni et al. 2024
)
as the reference model, the proposed approach reduced acceleration-matching errors and the optimized physical reservoirs approximate closely the reference dynamics across the tested datasets. Comparisons with fixed-morphology baselines indicate that morphology optimization is a key contributor to this approximation.
When evaluated in simulated full PRC pipelines on sMNIST, ADIAC, Mackey–Glass, and Lorenz96 tasks, the pretrained reservoirs outperformed unoptimized and partially pretrained physical baselines and retained
90.9\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
of the digital RON reference performance on average.
These preliminary results support dynamics-level co-optimization for low-dimensional soft robotic PRC. For computational tractability, the reference RONs and corresponding physical reservoirs were restricted to
n_{y}\in\{6,9,12,15\}
, which limits absolute task performance relative to state-of-the-art digital RC approaches; scaling the method to higher-dimensional physical reservoirs is therefore a primary direction for future work. Further work should validate the method on hardware and explicitly incorporate unmodeled dynamics, substrate variability, and sensor noise during pretraining and co-optimization to improve robustness to discrepancies between simulated and physical behavior. Another research direction is to characterize the compatibility between digital and physical reservoir vector fields, identifying which target dynamics are reachable by a given class of physical substrates. It will also be important to investigate the trade-off between the complexity of the physical–reference map and controllers and the computational contribution of the body itself, since a highly expressive map or controller could compensate for morphological limitations at the cost of a higher digital overhead.

Appendix A
Physical–Reference Reservoir Mapping

This appendix provides a more detailed discussion about the transformation used to map coordinates between the digital reference space and the physical reservoir space. Let
\mathbf{y}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
denote the digital reference coordinates and
\mathbf{Q}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{Q}}
the physical reservoir coordinates.
The map
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{Q}}
from the reference to the physical reservoir space is defined as

\mathbf{Q}=\phi(\mathbf{y}),

while the inverse transformation is

\mathbf{y}=\phi^{-1}(\mathbf{Q})=\psi(\mathbf{Q}).

To support the proposed optimization strategy, the map must be invertible for consistent transformations between
\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{y}
and sufficiently smooth to transform velocities and accelerations. In practice, this requires a second-order smooth diffeomorphism.

We use Fréchet-derivative notation:
\mathrm{D}\phi(\mathbf{y})
and
\mathrm{D}\psi(\mathbf{Q})
denote first derivatives, interpreted as linear maps, while
\mathrm{D}^{2}\phi(\mathbf{y})
and
\mathrm{D}^{2}\psi(\mathbf{Q})
denote second derivatives, interpreted as symmetric bilinear maps. Then velocities and accelerations transform as

\dot{\mathbf{Q}}=\mathrm{D}\phi(\mathbf{y})[\dot{\mathbf{y}}],\qquad\dot{\mathbf{y}}=\mathrm{D}\psi(\mathbf{Q})[\dot{\mathbf{Q}}],

and

\ddot{\mathbf{Q}}=\mathrm{D}\phi(\mathbf{y})[\ddot{\mathbf{y}}]+\mathrm{D}^{2}\phi(\mathbf{y})[\dot{\mathbf{y}},\dot{\mathbf{y}}],\qquad\displaystyle\ddot{\mathbf{y}}=\mathrm{D}\psi(\mathbf{Q})[\ddot{\mathbf{Q}}]+\mathrm{D}^{2}\psi(\mathbf{Q})[\dot{\mathbf{Q}},\dot{\mathbf{Q}}].

For example,
\mathrm{D}\phi(\mathbf{y}):\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{Q}}
maps a reference velocity to a physical reservoir velocity, while
\mathrm{D}^{2}\phi(\mathbf{y}):\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{Q}}
maps two reference velocities to the corresponding second-order chain-rule correction. In coordinates, for
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
and
i=1,\ldots,n_{Q}
,

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathrm{D}\phi(\mathbf{y})[\mathbf{v}]\bigr)_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{y}}\frac{\partial\phi_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}(\mathbf{y})\,v_{j},

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathrm{D}^{2}\phi(\mathbf{y})[\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}]\bigr)_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{y}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{y}}\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{i}}{\partial y_{j}\partial y_{k}}(\mathbf{y})\,v_{j}w_{k}.

Analogous expressions hold for
\psi
.

The mapping complexity determines how reference coordinates are projected into the physical reservoir space. More expressive transformations (e.g., nonlinear ones) can improve dynamics matching during optimization but require greater computational effort in the digital domain. An overly complex map may therefore shift computation away from the physical substrate, reducing its contribution.

Appendix B
Benchmark Tasks for Physical Reservoir Computing

The proposed Physical Reservoir Computing (PRC) strategy is evaluated on the sMNIST, ADIAC, Mackey–Glass and Lorenz96 benchmark tasks. These tasks were selected because they constitute standard benchmarks for Machine Learning (ML) models operating on temporal sequences. This appendix summarizes the training procedures used for the Reservoir Computing (RC) and PRC systems on each task and provides additional details on the corresponding datasets. In all cases, simulations of the physical reservoir were performed with the Diffrax library
(
Kidger 2021
)
, using an integration step
\delta t=$0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{s}$
and the forward Euler method.

sMNIST (sequential–MNIST)
is a classification task in which the model is required to recognize a handwritten digit from an image presented as a temporal sequence of pixels
(
Le et al. 2015
)
. It is based on the MNIST dataset
(
LeCun et al. 1998
)
, which contains 70000
28\times 28
grayscale images (60000 in the training set and 10000 in the test set) of handwritten digits from 0 to 9. In sMNIST, each image is fed to the reservoir as a sequence of 784 pixels, so the input
u(k)\in\mathbb{R}
is a real number in
[0,\,1]
representing the intensity of the
k
-th pixel. The model is then required to predict the corresponding digit.

Figure 5:
Schematic of how RC is used to perform the sMNIST classification task.

For output layer training in RC (or PRC), the input sequence is passed through the reservoir from
t_{0}=0
to
t_{f}
, and the final reservoir coordinates
\mathbf{y}(t_{f})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
are used as activations. This procedure is repeated for all
m
images in the training set, producing a batched collection of activations
\mathbf{Y}(t_{f})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n_{y}}
. First, a
scaler
function is defined from the mean
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
and standard deviation
\bm{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
of these activations over the
m
samples; it is then applied to scale the activations as

\mathbf{\tilde{y}}_{j}=\mathrm{scaler}(\mathbf{y}_{j}(t_{f}))=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{\sigma})^{-1}\left(\mathbf{y}_{j}(t_{f})-\bm{\mu}\right)\qquad\text{$\forall j=1,...,m$,}

yielding the scaled activation matrix
\mathbf{\tilde{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n_{y}}
.
A
classifier
is subsequently trained on these scaled activations to estimate the probabilities of the 10 classes, corresponding to digits from 0 to 9. In particular, the classifier is implemented as a multiclass logistic regression model

\mathbf{p}=\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{W}_{o}\,\mathbf{\tilde{y}}+\mathbf{b}_{o}),

whose weights
\mathbf{W}_{o}\in\mathbb{R}^{10\times n_{y}}
and biases
\mathbf{b}_{o}\in\mathbb{R}^{10}
are computed by minimizing cross-entropy loss with L2 regularization.

During inference, an input sequence (image) is passed to the reservoir, and the activations
\mathbf{y}(t_{f})
are scaled using the scaler defined during training. The scaled activations
\mathbf{\tilde{y}}
are then provided to the trained classifier, which outputs the predicted probabilities
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{10}
for all classes. The predicted class is obtained by selecting the class with maximum probability. A schematic of this inference process is shown in Fig.
5
.

The performance of the model on this task is evaluated through classification accuracy, computed as the number of correct predictions divided by the total number of images. For computational tractability, the experiments used a training set of 30000 images and a test set of 5000 images extracted from the original MNIST dataset.

ADIAC (Automatic Diatom Identification And Classification)
involves automatic identification of diatoms (unicellular algae) on the basis of images that are converted into temporal sequences
(
Jalba et al. 2004
)
. It is a classification task with 37 classes, and the dataset is composed of 390 train samples and 391 test samples, each consisting of a 176-steps univariate time series
(
Dau et al. 2019
)
. The (physical) reservoir computing system operates on this task exactly as in sMNIST.

Mackey–Glass
is another standard benchmark task, in which the model predicts a time sequence generated by the Mackey–Glass equation
(
Mackey and Glass 1977
)
:

\dot{u}(t)=\frac{\beta\theta^{n}u(t-\tau)}{\theta^{n}+u(t-\tau)^{n}}-\gamma\,u(t).

(8)

This system displays complex behavior for selected parameter values and is commonly used to evaluate the memory capacity of recurrent networks. The dataset is a sequence
\mathbf{u}=[u(0),\;...\;,\;u(N-1)]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
of
N
values from a discrete solution of Eq. (
8
). At time
k
, the model is required to predict the future state of the system after a lag
N_{l}
, namely
u(k+N_{l})
.

During training, an input sequence (i.e., the portion of the dataset from
k=0
to
k=N-N_{l}-1
) is fed to the reservoir, and its response is recorded. The first
N_{w}
steps are discarded as
washout
of the initial transient, and the reservoir coordinates
\mathbf{y}(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
from
k=N_{w}
to
k=N-N_{l}-1
are used as activations. These activations are rescaled as in the sMNIST task and then used to train a linear layer

\hat{u}(k+N_{l})=\mathbf{W}_{o}\;\mathbf{\tilde{y}}(k)+b_{o}\qquad\text{(with $\mathbf{W}_{o}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n_{y}}$, $b_{o}\in\mathbb{R}$)}

using Ridge regression. The corresponding labels, which are compared with the predictions
\hat{u}
, are the portion of the full dataset sequence from
k=N_{w}+N_{l}
to
k=N-1
. A visual schematic of this dataset partition is shown in Fig.
6
.

Figure 6:
Visualization of Mackey–Glass dataset-sequence partition. In this specific example
N=1000,\,N_{w}=200,\,N_{l}=84
.

During inference, the prediction is obtained from the trained output layer, with the activations
\mathbf{\tilde{y}}(k)
computed rescaling
\mathbf{y}(k)
with the scaler defined during training.

The performance of the model on this task is evaluated using the Normalized Root Mean Square Error (NRMSE) between the predicted sequence
\mathbf{\hat{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-N_{w}-N_{l}}
and the target sequence
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{targ}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-N_{w}-N_{l}}
:

\mathrm{NRMSE}(\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{targ}},\mathbf{\hat{u}})=\frac{\mathrm{RMSE}(\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{targ}},\mathbf{\hat{u}})}{\mathrm{RMS}(\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{targ}})}=\frac{\sqrt{\frac{1}{N-N_{w}-N_{l}}\,\lVert\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{targ}}-\mathbf{\hat{u}}\rVert_{2}^{2}}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{N-N_{w}-N_{l}}\,\lVert\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{targ}}\rVert_{2}^{2}}}.

In our experiments we adopted a prediction lag
N_{l}
of 84 steps and
N_{w}=200
washout steps for the initial transient, with one training and one test temporal sequence of
N=2000
steps each.

Lorenz96
is a time-series forecasting task in which, at time
k
, the model is required to predict the future state
\mathbf{u}(k+N_{l})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
of a temporal sequence generated by the multidimensional Lorenz equation
(
Lorenz 1996
)
:

\begin{cases}\dot{u}_{i}=(u_{i+1}-u_{i-2})u_{i-1}-u_{i}+F\\
u_{-1}=u_{n_{u}-1},\;u_{0}=u_{n_{u}},\;u_{n_{u}+1}=u_{1}\end{cases}\qquad(\text{with $i=1,\,...\,,n_{u}$}).

For this task, the model is trained analogously to the Mackey–Glass case, except that the input has dimension
n_{u}>1
. In our experiments,
n_{u}=5
and
F=8
. The evaluation metric is the NRMSE between the target and predicted sequences. A prediction lag
N_{l}
of 25 steps,
N_{w}=200
washout steps, and 128 batched sequences of
N=2225
steps each were used for both training and testing.

Appendix C
Digital Reference Dynamics and Pretraining Dataset Generation

The proposed co-optimization methodology requires appropriate digital reservoir dynamics to serve as the reference. We use the Random Oscillators Network (RON) introduced in
(
Ceni et al. 2024
)
because it performs well on several tasks and is inspired by nonlinearly coupled physical oscillators. Its dynamics are therefore compatible with the soft robot. This appendix introduces the RON and describes the datasets of points
(\mathbf{y},\dot{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{u})
and labels
\ddot{\mathbf{y}}
used for pretraining.

Random Oscillators Network model

The RON is a digital reservoir composed of
n_{y}
mass-spring-damper elements coupled by a nonlinear forcing term. Its continuous-time equations of motion are:

\ddot{\mathbf{y}}=-\bm{\gamma}\odot\mathbf{y}-\bm{\varepsilon}\odot\dot{\mathbf{y}}+\tanh(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{V}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{b}).

(9)

Here,
\mathbf{y}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
collects the oscillator positions (reservoir coordinates),
\mathbf{u}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
is the input vector,
\bm{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
and
\bm{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
collect the stiffness and damping factors, respectively,
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}\times n_{y}}
is the hidden-to-hidden weight matrix,
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}\times n_{u}}
is the input-to-hidden weight matrix,
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
is the bias vector, and
\odot
denotes the element-wise product.
In state-space form, calling
\mathbf{z}=[\mathbf{y},\;\dot{\mathbf{y}}]^{\top}
:

\begin{cases}\dot{\mathbf{z}}_{1}=\mathbf{z}_{2}\\
\dot{\mathbf{z}}_{2}=-\bm{\gamma}\odot\mathbf{z}_{1}-\bm{\varepsilon}\odot\mathbf{z}_{2}+\tanh{(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{z}_{1}+\mathbf{V}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{b})}.\end{cases}

(10)

Eq. (
10
) is then discretized with temporal step
\Delta t
to obtain the discrete-time equations of the digital reservoir, using implicit Euler method for
\mathbf{z}_{1}
equation and explicit Euler method for
\mathbf{z}_{2}
equation:

\begin{cases}\mathbf{z}_{1}(k+1)=\mathbf{z}_{1}(k)+\Delta t\;\mathbf{z}_{2}(k+1)\\
\mathbf{z}_{2}(k+1)=\mathbf{z}_{2}(k)+\Delta t\;\left[-\bm{\gamma}\odot\mathbf{z}_{1}(k)-\bm{\varepsilon}\odot\mathbf{z}_{2}(k)+\tanh{(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{z}_{1}(k)+\mathbf{V}\mathbf{u}(k)+\mathbf{b})}\right].\end{cases}

(11)

The spectral radius of
\mathbf{W}
is denoted by
\rho_{\mathrm{w}}
, and
\nu
denotes the input scaling factor used to rescale
\mathbf{V}
and
\mathbf{b}
at initialization.

Reference RON configuration selection

The properties of a RON reservoir, including memory retention, expressiveness, and timescale, depend on its
configuration
, i.e., the values of its parameters
n_{y},\bm{\gamma},\bm{\varepsilon},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{V},\mathbf{b}
,
\Delta t
and
\nu
. In
(
Ceni et al. 2024
)
, the task-level RON reservoirs adopt
n_{y}=13000
hidden units for sMNIST,
n_{y}=100
for ADIAC,
n_{y}=1000
for Mackey–Glass, and

Appendix D
Planar Piecewise Constant Strain Soft Robot Model

In this work, the soft robots in the physical reservoir are modeled as slender compliant arms and represented using the planar Piecewise Constant Strain (PCS) model
(
Renda et al. 2016
;
Renda et al. 2018
)
. This appendix briefly introduces the model and the assumptions adopted for its implementation.

The PCS is a kinematic model based on a finite-dimensional parametrization
\mathbf{q}
that describes the robot as a backbone curve composed of
n_{\mathrm{pcs}}
segments. Each
i
-th segment has constant (in space) strains (bending
\mathord{\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathcal{K}$}}^{(i)}
[rad/m], axial
\sigma_{\mathrm{ax}}^{(i)}
[–] and shear
\sigma_{\mathrm{sh}}^{(i)}
[–]) and its own physical properties: length
L^{(i)}
[m], damping matrix
\mathbf{D}^{(i)}=\mathrm{diag}([d^{(i)}_{\mathrm{be}},\;d^{(i)}_{\mathrm{ax}},\;d^{(i)}_{\mathrm{sh}}])
[Pa
\cdot
s], radius
r^{(i)}
[m], density
\rho^{(i)}
[kg/m
3
], elastic modulus
E^{(i)}
[Pa], and shear modulus
G^{(i)}
[Pa], with
i=1,\,...\,,n_{\mathrm{pcs}}
. The kinematics of the soft robot can then be expressed as a function of the generalized coordinates
\mathbf{q}=[\mathord{\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathcal{K}$}}^{(1)},\;\sigma_{\mathrm{ax}}^{(1)},\;\sigma^{(1)}_{\mathrm{sh}},\;...\ ,\;\mathord{\scalebox{0.8}{$\mathcal{K}$}}^{(n_{\mathrm{pcs}})},\;\sigma_{\mathrm{ax}}^{(n_{\mathrm{pcs}})},\;\sigma^{(n_{\mathrm{pcs}})}_{\mathrm{sh}}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{3n_{\mathrm{pcs}}}
, while its dynamics are obtained by applying the Euler-Lagrange approach. The definitions of the dynamical matrices and kinematic equations are provided in
(
Renda et al. 2018
)
.

To assess whether the improvements of the fully pretrained reservoir over the other baselines are statistically meaningful rather than an artifact of the particular random seeds, we compare the per-seed benchmark results with a one-sided Mann–Whitney
U
test
(
Mann and Whitney 1947
)
. This non-parametric, rank-based test is well-suited for the small number of samples considered here (four runs per configuration), as it makes no assumptions about the distributions and it is robust to the occasional diverged trial, which only enters as the worst rank. For each task and reservoir dimension

p
-value below 0.05 allows us to reject the null hypothesis that the two sets of per-seed results are drawn from the same distribution. NaN runs are excluded, reducing the corresponding samples to 3, while diverged runs are retained, since the rank-based test is insensitive to their magnitude.

) in 15 out of the 16 configurations, and the stronger

baseline in 11 of the 16. The six non-significant cases almost all involve the partially pretrained baseline at settings where the two distributions partially overlap.

Overall, these tests confirm that the improvement of the fully pretrained reservoir is statistically significant in the large majority of the considered settings (26 of the 32 comparisons). We note that, with four runs per configuration, the smallest attainable one-sided

Appendix G
Digital Inference Cost of Digital and Physical Reservoir Pipelines

Physical Reservoir Computing (PRC) can reduce digital inference cost because the reservoir dynamics evolve directly in a physical substrate rather than being computed through numerical simulation. The proposed architecture nevertheless retains digital feedforward and feedback controllers, together with an affine state map and a task-specific readout. We therefore quantify whether the online cost of these digital components preserves the computational advantage of the physical reservoir relative to the digital Random Oscillators Network (RON).

We compare the two inference pipelines in terms of floating-point operations (FLOPs). For the PRC pipeline, the soft robot dynamics are assumed to evolve physically and therefore incur no digital FLOPs. Accordingly, this analysis does not quantify sensing, actuation, communication, physical energy consumption, or wall-clock latency. We count the evaluation of
\tanh(\mathbf{x})
or
\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{x})
as
n
FLOPs for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Here,
n_{y}
is the reservoir dimension,
n_{u}
is the input dimension,
K
is the number of input time steps,
n_{c}
is the number of classes in classification tasks, and
N_{l}
is the prediction lag in forecasting tasks; the subscripts
cls
and
for
denote classification and forecasting, respectively.

The affine physical-to-reference map can be absorbed into the readout, thereby avoiding a separate online mapping operation. Before this substitution, the task-specific readouts operate on the scaled reservoir variables
\mathbf{\tilde{y}}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{cls}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\mathbf{W}_{o}\;\mathbf{\tilde{y}}(K)+\mathbf{b}_{o}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{c}},

(12)

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{for}}(k+N_{l})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}_{o}\;\mathbf{\tilde{y}}(k)+\mathbf{b}_{o}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}.

(13)

Here,
\mathbf{W}_{o}
and
\mathbf{b}_{o}
denote the trained readout parameters with the appropriate output dimension for each task (Appendix
B
). To express these readouts directly in terms of the physical reservoir coordinates, let

\mathbf{D}_{\sigma}=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{\sigma})^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}\times n_{y}},

where
\bm{\mu}
and
\bm{\sigma}
are the mean and standard deviation used to scale the reference states (Appendix
B
). Substituting the inverse affine map
\mathbf{y}(k)=\mathbf{A}^{-1}(\mathbf{Q}(k)-\mathbf{c})
into the scaling operation gives

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{y}}(k)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{D}_{\sigma}\bigl(\mathbf{y}(k)-\bm{\mu}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{D}_{\sigma}\left[\mathbf{A}^{-1}\bigl(\mathbf{Q}(k)-\mathbf{c}\bigr)-\bm{\mu}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{D}_{\sigma}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{Q}(k)-\mathbf{D}_{\sigma}\left(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{c}+\bm{\mu}\right).

The derivation first replaces the scaled reservoir variables with its definition, then substitutes the inverse map, expands the affine terms, and finally collects the factors that depend on
\mathbf{Q}(k)
and those that remain constant. Applying the trained readout parameters
\mathbf{W}_{o}
and
\mathbf{b}_{o}
therefore yields the task-specific coefficients

\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{W}_{o}\mathbf{D}_{\sigma}\mathbf{A}^{-1},\qquad\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{W}_{o}\mathbf{D}_{\sigma}\left(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{c}+\bm{\mu}\right)-\mathbf{b}_{o}.

For a readout dimension
d
, then
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n_{y}}
is the effective matrix that maps the physical reservoir coordinates
\mathbf{Q}
directly to the readout logits or forecasts. The vector
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the corresponding effective offset, which combines the affine-map translation, the scaling mean, and the trained readout bias. Here,
d=n_{c}
for classification and
d=n_{u}
for forecasting. The two readouts in Eqs. (
12
) and (
13
) can thus be written compactly as

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{cls}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Q}(K)-\mathbf{C}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{c}},

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{for}}(k+N_{l})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Q}(k)-\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}},

for classification and forecasting, respectively.
In the forecasting case, the physical reservoir state at time
k
predicts the target at time
k+N_{l}
. The task-specific coefficients
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{C}
are computed once after readout training, so only the resulting task-specific readout is evaluated online.

Digital cost of reservoir dynamics

•

The RON evaluates the discrete state update in Eq. (
11
), requiring

\mathrm{FLOPs}=K\;(2n_{y}^{2}+9n_{y}+2n_{y}n_{u}).

•

Within the reservoir component of the proposed PRC system, only the feedforward and feedback controllers are evaluated digitally. For the controller architecture used in this work, their cost is

\mathrm{FLOPs}=K\;(385n_{y}+128n_{u}+16640).

(14)

Digital cost of the task-specific readout

After incorporating the affine state map into the coefficients
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{C}
, the RON and PRC pipelines have the same online readout cost:

•

For classification,

\mathrm{FLOPs}=2n_{y}n_{c}+3n_{c}.

•

For forecasting,

\mathrm{FLOPs}=(K-N_{l})\;2n_{y}n_{u}.

Digital cost of the complete inference pipeline

Combining the reservoir and readout contributions gives the following total digital costs:

•

For the RON,

\mathrm{FLOPs}=K\;(2n_{y}^{2}+9n_{y}+2n_{y}n_{u})+2n_{y}n_{c}+3n_{c}\quad\text{for classification};

\mathrm{FLOPs}=K\;(2n_{y}^{2}+9n_{y}+2n_{y}n_{u})+(K-N_{l})\;2n_{y}n_{u}\quad\text{for forecasting}.

•

For the proposed PRC system,

\mathrm{FLOPs}=K\;(385n_{y}+128n_{u}+16640)+2n_{y}n_{c}+3n_{c}\quad\text{for classification};

(15)

\mathrm{FLOPs}=K\;(385n_{y}+128n_{u}+16640)+(K-N_{l})\;2n_{y}n_{u}\quad\text{for forecasting}.

(16)

For
n_{y}\gg n_{u},n_{c}
, the total digital cost of the RON scales as
\mathcal{O}(Kn_{y}^{2})
for both task types. The PRC cost instead scales as
\mathcal{O}(Kn_{y}+n_{y}n_{c})
for classification and
\mathcal{O}(Kn_{y}+(K-N_{l})n_{y}n_{u})
for forecasting. Fig.
8
reports the exact FLOP counts for the four benchmark tasks as functions of
n_{y}
. Because the affine map is incorporated into the readout coefficients, the readout cost is identical for the RON and PRC pipelines. Equating the reservoir-only costs shows that the PRC controllers become less expensive than the digital RON dynamics at approximately
n_{y}\approx 220
for the benchmark input dimensions.

Appendix H
Cross-Task Physical Reservoir Computing Experiments

The main benchmark experiments use task-specific RON configurations (e.g., spectral radius and time step), as specified in Appendix
C
and consistent with established reservoir-tuning practice
(
Jaeger 2002
;
Liebald 2004
;
Jaeger et al. 2007
;
Siewert and Wustlich 2007
;
Ceni et al. 2024
)
. Consequently, both the digital reference dynamics and the physical reservoirs optimized to reproduce them are specialized to individual tasks. This appendix presents additional experiments assessing whether these physical reservoirs can be reused across tasks. The results show that the optimized reservoirs remain effective on multiple tasks without reoptimizing their morphology, with only modest performance degradation in most cases.

This distinction is important for interpreting the proposed approach. A pretrained physical reservoir that remains effective on unseen tasks supports the hypothesis that co-optimization produces a reusable computational substrate with dynamics broadly useful for physical reservoir computing, rather than merely implementing an indirect form of task-specific training. Cross-task reuse also addresses a practical deployment constraint: in PRC, the reservoir is a physical system whose parameters are fixed after fabrication. If pretraining transferred only to its target task, deployment on each new task would require co-optimizing and fabricating a new substrate.

Experimental setup

We restrict these experiments to reservoirs of dimension
n_{y}=6
. In each experiment, a
source
physical reservoir—comprising the soft robots, map, and controllers obtained by co-optimization against a reference RON tuned for a specific
source
task—is combined with a readout layer trained on a different
target
-task dataset.

To address cases in which the source and target input sequences
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
have different dimensions, and the source feedforward (FF) controller therefore cannot be reused, we consider a second scenario in which the FF controller is re-tuned for the target task. Specifically, we fix the soft robots (
\theta_{\mathrm{rob}}
), map (
\theta_{\mathrm{map}}
), and feedback controller (
\theta_{\mathrm{contr}}^{(\mathrm{fb})}
) obtained by co-optimization with the source-task reference RON, and perform a brief auxiliary pretraining stage for only the FF controller (
\theta_{\mathrm{contr}}^{(\mathrm{ff})}
) using the target-task reference RON.

Finally, we evaluate a third scenario in which the map (
\theta_{\mathrm{map}}
) and feedback controller (
\theta_{\mathrm{contr}}^{(\mathrm{fb})}
), in addition to the FF controller, are re-tuned for the target task during auxiliary pretraining.

None of these experiments modifies the soft robots pretrained for the source task. The same physical substrate can therefore be reused across tasks by reprogramming only its digital components.

We evaluate all 12 cross-task source–target combinations among the four tasks considered in this work: sMNIST, ADIAC, Mackey–Glass, and Lorenz96. The target-task input sequence
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
is supplied to the source reservoir using the time-step parameter
\Delta t
of the target-task reservoir. With the source physical reservoir fixed, we conduct the target-task PRC experiments according to the protocol described in Sec. 5 of the paper. Each source–target configuration is evaluated over four runs with different random seeds, and the results are aggregated across runs.

14.8\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
for sMNIST,
27.0\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
for ADIAC, and
10.3\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
for Mackey–Glass. This strategy nevertheless enables source–target pairings with different input dimensions. In particular, the sMNIST, ADIAC, and Mackey–Glass source reservoirs can be evaluated on Lorenz96, with an average performance degradation of
34.4\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
relative to the corresponding Lorenz96 reservoir.

Finally, re-tuning the map and both controllers—that is, all digital but none of the physical components—during the auxiliary target-task pretraining stage (rows labeled “FF + FB + map” in the table) substantially improves performance. The mean performance degradation drops to
2.6\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
for sMNIST,

24.9\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}

One possible explanation for the
24.9\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
performance degradation of Lorenz96 target task could be the choice of the input time step
\Delta t
, which is determined by the target-task reservoir and may be suboptimal for the source-reservoir dynamics. However, adopting the source-reservoir value of
\Delta t
would impose an input sampling rate based solely on the source dynamics, without accounting for relevant characteristics of the target input, such as its duration and frequency content. Future work could therefore tune the input sampling rate according to both the dynamical properties of the source reservoir (e.g., its resonance frequency) and the temporal characteristics of the target input (e.g., its dominant frequency).
