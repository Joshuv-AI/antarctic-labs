EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
 The primary objective of this project is to demonstrate the University of Cincinnati’s direct
air capture (DAC) sorbent technology in a monolithic structure installed in a passive air contactor
(PAC). The modified amine-based sorbent technology provides reduced heat of desorption and
resistance to thermal and oxidative degradations for robust and long-term performances and cost-
effective sorbent management. During the project period, additional amine modifications were
developed and a U.S./PCT patent application was filed. The sorbent technology in a powdered
form was upgraded to a monolithic form in collaboration with BASF. Modified amine-
impregnated sorbent-coated small monolithic structures (modified amine-based monolith hereafter)
showed robust and consistent cyclic performances over a 100-cycle test. The modified amine-
based monolithic structure demonstrated its CO2 working capacity close to its full capacity by
vacuum-assisted Joule heating at ~90 C whereas the unmodified amine-based monolithic
structure requires a higher desorption temperature. This advantage allowed the modified amine-
based monolith to show its CO2 working capacity comparable to the unmodified amine-based
monolith up to 95 C. When the desorption temperature was lowered between 45 C and 70 C
for potential utilization of low-grade heat, the modified amine-based sorbent-coated monolith
showed higher CO2 working capacities and also faster desorption kinetics than the unmodified
amine-based sorbent-coated monolith within the temperature window. Based on the rigorous
performance evaluations, the size of the monolith was scaled up, and its performance was
evaluated in an environmental chamber where temperature, humidity, and wind speed can be
controlled. The monolithic structure released CO2 gas with >90% purity for the benefits of various
downstream CO2 utilization processes.
 This project also studied an optimal PAC design for a scale-up of the DAC system to be
placed under different and practical wind speeds (~1-8 m/s). This was achieved by combining the
fundamental computation fluid dynamics (CFD) with mass transfer and adsorption kinetics. The
model result was used to scale up our DAC system with a PAC for TEA and LCA. The project
advanced the sorbent technology from technology readiness level (TRL) 3 to TRL 4.

Task 2. Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) and CO2 Adsorption Kinetics
Subtask 2.1 Air Flow Model through Monolith in Air Contactor
 Our passive direct air capture (PDAC) system consists of two main sections: (i) a passive
wind collector (PWC) that catches wind at multiple attack angles and tunnels it into a contactor
and (ii) a contactor containing a sorbent-washcoated monolithic structure where accompanying
CO2 molecules are captured. Our objective was to model the wind-driven air flow through the
monolith in our PDAC system. The effects of geometric parameters of the wind catcher and the
monolith configurations on the air velocity and pressure drop in the system were studied for an
optimal design of the PDAC system. A CO2 adsorption kinetic and equilibrium model was coupled
with the CFD model for a single monolith cell channel to describe the CO2 adsorption onto the
sorbent-washcoated layer on the monolith block. The model will serve as a basis for the scale-up
of the PDAC system with a maximum volumetric productivity and a CO2 adsorption efficiency of
DAC system.

Grid Sensitivity Analysis and Optimization of Macro-Environment
 The computational domain where the Navier-Stokes equations are solved was discretized
using an unstructured meshing technique. The domain was separated into three sections – the
macro-environment section simulating wind flow, the wind catcher and upper section of the
contactor, and the monolith. These sections correspond to three graduations of the mesh sizes
employed across the geometry, with the relatively coarse mesh used in the macro-environment and
the finest of the meshing employed in the monolith area. The mesh structure was further refined
to accurately capture the flow characteristics around sharp corners and in the vicinity of walls
where sudden changes in flow directions and steep gradients were expected.
 However, computational time dramatically increased with an increase in mesh resolution
and accompanying nodes at the expense of the solution accuracy. Hence, a grid sensitivity analysis
was performed on the internal velocity profiles to optimize the grid size for subsequent simulations.
This was implemented by comparing the solution sets of the average linear velocity along the
horizontal mid-section of the monolith (see Fig. 2-2) over a range of mesh size distributions
(~20,000 mesh elements to ~200,000 mesh elements). An optimal mesh grid of ~100,000 mesh
elements was selected where there was consistently less than 5% error tolerance

Velocity and pressure distributions
 For an ambient wind speed of 5 m/s, our CFD model shows that the wind catcher can
capture a fraction of wind flow with a maximum velocity of ~1.4 m/s at the entrance of wind
catcher (Fig. 2-3). Then, the air passes through the monolith section where it splits and flows
through individual monolith flow cells (pitch = 2 mm) before leaving the DAC from the bottom.
We find that the maximum air velocity inside the monolith cells is ~2 m/s (at the cell center). The
resulting Reynolds number for the flow through the monolith is ~270. Thus, even though the flow
in the wind catcher section is turbulent, the air flow in the monolith section is laminar due to high
flow resistance derived from millimeter-sized cross-sectional area of the monolith cells (Fig. 2-3).
We also find that for a monolith pitch size of 2 mm, the resulting pressure drop across the monolith
is ~20 Pa (Fig. 2-4). Increasing the pitch size of the monolith can further result in decreasing this
pressure drop.

Effects of external wind speed
 To determine the effects of external wind velocity on internal flow field, we first compared
the air flow in the DAC system with and without the monolith at different external wind speeds
ranging from 1 m/s to 8 m/s. We found that without the monolith, the throughput (i.e., air flow
rate) could linearly increase with wind speed. Upon the introduction of a monolith block with a
3-mm pitch, the air flow rate dropped significantly and non-linearly increased with wind velocity
(Fig. 2-6(a)). The calculated air flow capture efficiency (i.e., the ratio of air flow rate with
monolith to that without monolith) increased from 2% to 14% as the external wind velocity
increases from 1 to 8 m/s, suggesting that higher wind speeds are more conducive to directing a
larger proportion of incoming wind into the PDAC system (Fig. 2-6(b)). This resulted in non-
linear growth in the monolith linear velocity with an increase in external wind speed for virtually
all values of the reactor inlet height and wind catcher inlet height (Fig. 2-6(c, d)).

Subtask 2.2 Determination of CO2 Adsorption Kinetics
 Sorbents in a powdered form were evaluated to determine their adsorption kinetics and
equilibrium under different adsorption conditions. This data will be used to predict CO2 adsorption
with sorbent-washcoated monolith in our DAC system in conjunction with our CFD model. We
used unmodified polyethylenimine (PEI, Mn=600) impregnated onto our porous silica substrate as

                                          Page 7 of 112

a benchmark sorbent and denoted as 50%(wt) PEI/silica. We also compared its adsorption
performances and characteristics with 1, 2-epoxybutane-modified PEI (EB-PEI)-impregnated
sorbent. The modified sorbent was prepared such that the molar ratio of epoxybutane (EB) to
nitrogen content in PEI initially used for the modification was 0.1 and denoted as 0.1 EB-PEI/silica.
Upon impregnation of unmodified PEI and EB-modified PEI onto the silica substrate, the specific
surface areas along with the pore volume dramatically decreased relative to the bare substrates, as
summarized in Table 2-4. However, no noticeable difference in the pore characteristics was
detectable from the sorbent samples in terms of different levels of the modification. The adsorption
study was performed using a surface and pore characterization analyzer (Micromeritics, 3Flex). It
has a U-shaped quartz reactor tube with a 9-mm inside diameter (ID). A furnace was installed in
the system with high precision temperature control. Various inlet CO2 concentrations between 0
ppm and 400 ppm CO2 were obtained by mixing certified 400 ppm CO2 in air. The exit CO2
concentrations were measured using a real-time CO2 analyzer (Quantek Instruments, Model 906)
to obtain breakthrough curves during the adsorption phase.

 Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 2-11, the equilibrium adsorption capacities of the PEI/silica
sorbent consistently increased with temperatures from 0 ℃ (1.06 mmol/g) up to 40 ℃ (1.96
mmol/g) at the inlet concentration of 400 ppm. The equilibrium adsorption capacities increased
with temperatures up to ~35 C and showed a slight decrease at 40 C relative to 35 C over the
entire concentrations range. This is primarily due to the exothermic nature of the adsorption
reaction. The reaction equilibrium prefers low temperature, but the adsorption reaction kinetics
prefers high temperature. The kinetics increases with temperature while the equilibrium prefers
low temperature. Therefore, there is a trade-off between kinetics and equilibrium for exothermic
reactions. The PEI/silica sorbent showed a maximum adsorption capacity at ~35-40 ℃. On the
other hand, the 0.1 EB-PEI/silica sorbent showed a maximum adsorption capacity at slightly lower
temperatures of ~30-35 ℃ at 400 ppm CO2. Since the bonding strength is reduced, the
modification seems to shift the equilibrium of the exothermic adsorption reaction. The
temperature showing a maximum adsorption capacity is reduced from ~35-40 ℃ for the PEI/silica
sorbent to ~30-35 C for the 0.1 EB-PEI/silica sorbent.
 It is interesting to note that the dependence of the adsorption capacities on the inlet CO 2
concentrations with respect to different temperatures is quite different for the two sorbents. The
PEI/silica sorbent was relatively less dependent on inlet CO2 concentrations over the entire
temperatures range. However, the adsorption capacity of the 0.1 EB-PEI/silica sorbent was less
dependent on inlet CO2 concentrations up to 22 C but was significantly dependent above 30 C.
The pore characteristics for both the sorbents are similar (Table 2-4), and thus it is difficult to think
that it is an effect of pore diffusion. Instead, it is presumed that the shifted reaction equilibrium

Task 3. Manufacture of CO2 Sorbent and Long-term Lab-scale Evaluation
Subtask 3.1 Manufacture of CO2 Sorbent
Sorbent Synthesis
Synthesis of support sorbent. In a typical synthesis, the fumed silica and water were mixed at a
weight ratio in a ball milling jar, and then milling balls were added as a milling media.
Subsequently, the milling jar was placed into a ball mill and milled at 80 rpm for a predetermined
period until the slurry forms a uniform phase. Then, a predetermined mass of colloidal silica sol
was added to the resultant slurry mixture. Then, the milling continued until the viscosity of the
slurry was reduced to a predetermined level. The spray-drying was carried out using a spray dryer
with an air-blowing inlet temperature of ~200 °C, and the outlet temperature was kept within the
range of 110 °C to 120 °C. The resultant spray-dried silica particles were calcined in air at 500 ºC
to obtain a mesoporous silica network structure.
Synthesis of epoxide functionalization to PEI. For the conjugation of epoxide chemical (EC) to
PEI, desired amounts of epoxide chemical (EC) and PEI were mixed at a varied molar ratio (i.e.,
a molar ratio of EC to N in PEI used) in a 50 wt.% alcoholic solution. Then, the resultant reaction
mixture was stirred at a room temperature for a predetermined period of time to ensure the
conjugation of EC to PEI.
Synthesis of PEI/support sorbents. The aforementioned modified amine solution was added
onto the silica or alumina substrate by incipient wetness impregnation method. Similarly, the
unmodified amine solution was used to synthesize unmodified PEI-impregnated sorbents. The
PEI loading was varied from 10 wt.% to 50 wt.% relative to the sorbent mass. Following the
impregnation, the synthesized materials were dried overnight at 70 ºC in a vacuum oven to remove
the residual solvent. The obtained sorbent was denoted as xEC-PEI/silica or alumina, where x
stands for a molar ratio of EC to N in PEI.

Characterization
 The amine state distributions after the EC functionalization to unmodified PEI were
determined using 13C NMR spectroscopy (Bruker, AV 400 MHZ) techniques. Prior to the NMR
record, all the unmodified and modified PEI samples were separated from the alcoholic solution
by rotary evaporator and then, a desired quantity of the sample was re-dissolved in CDCl3 solvent.
A FEI XL30 Environmental Scanning Electron Microscope (ESEM) was used to investigate the
surface morphology of synthesized materials. The thermal stability of the sorbents was examined
by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) using a TGA 5500 (TA instrument) between 90 °C to 100 °C
temperature at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under N2 atmosphere. The surface area, pore size
distribution, and pore volume of the sorbents were analyzed by N2 physisorption isotherms
performed at 77 K using a gas adsorption analyzer (Micromeritics, 3Flex). The sample was
degassed at 120 °C under a vacuum for 12 h before the measurement.
CO2 adsorption test
 The CO2 adsorption performances of the synthesized sorbents were evaluated by a cyclic
adsorption-desorption process using TGA (TA Instruments, Discovery TGA 5500) under dry
conditions. 400 ppm CO2 balanced with air was used for the adsorption runs and pure N2 (99.99%)
was used as a purge gas for the degassing and desorption. Approximately 5 mg of previously

CO2 adsorption/desorption under Dry Conditions
 After the structural and surface characterizations of the synthesized unmodified and
modified PEI sorbents, we examined the CO2 adsorption performances using TGA under 400 ppm
CO2 in air at 30 °C for 90 min, and the desorption was performed at 90 °C for 20 min. 10 cycles
of CO2 adsorption/desorption data for the unmodified and modified PEI supported onto silica and
alumina are shown in Figs. 3-10 and 3-11. Although 10 cycles were not long enough to evaluate
the performances of individual sorbents, the modified PEI/silica and alumina sorbents showed a
sign of stability with lower adsorption capacities relative to the unmodified PEI/silica and alumina
sorbents. We currently speculate that this reduced adsorption capacity is attributed to the reduced
bonding strength between modified amine sites and adsorbed CO2 molecules. The most reactive
primary (1°) amine centers, which are more prone to this alkylation mechanism, are converted into
modified secondary (2°) amines, while the secondary amines are converted to tertiary (3°) amines,

                                                                          Page 30 of 112

but with a much lower conversion yield. This could ultimately bring about reduced CO2-amine
interactions in modified PEI relative to the unmodified PEI counterpart.
 Figs. 3-10 (b) and 3-11 (b) show the CO2 adsorption capacities obtained at 30 C after the
1st and 10th cyclic performances at 90 °C desorption temperature. The PEI/silica sorbent exhibits
a maximum CO2 adsorption capacity of 1.75 mmol CO2/g sorbent from the first cycle of the run.
However, after the 10th cycle, there is a 2.28% reduction from the 1st CO2 capacity at the 90 °C
desorption temperature. For the mPEI-1/silica sorbent, the initial CO2 capacity was found to be
slightly lower (~1.5 mmol CO2/g sorbent) than PEI/silica, as shown in Fig. 3-10(b). For the mPEI-
2/silica sorbent, the initial adsorption capacity was 1.2 mmol CO2/g sorbent, which is lower than
the PEI/silica and mPEI-1/silica sorbents. The reduction in the adsorption efficacies for mPEI-1
and mPEI-2/silica after 10 cycles were determined to be 1.31% and 0.82%, respectively. This data
clearly shows that the thermal stability could increase with an increase in the level of the
modification.
 For the sorbent samples supported on alumina, the CO2 working capacity obtained at 30
C for the 1st cycle (Fig. 3-11(b)) of PEI/alumina was 1.13 mmol CO2/g sorbent; after the 10th
cycle, the capacity dropped to 0.88 mmol CO2/g sorbent, with a 22% loss from the 1st CO2 capacity.
This result suggests that the higher capacity loss seems to be higher volatility of PEI than the
sorbent supported onto the silica substrate. The reduction in the CO2 adsorption capacity for mPEI-
1/alumina after the 10th cycle was found to be ~3.6%. From the above results, the silica substrate
could provide a better support for PEI likely due to the narrow pore size distribution and/or higher
surface affinity towards PEI. Overall, as the amount of amine modification increases from mPEI-
1 to mPEI-2, the cyclic stability of the mPEI/silica sorbents increases with a decrease in the CO2
adsorption capacities. In addition, the stability also improves with an increase in the level of the
modification. Thus, we can conclude that the modification has a trade-off between thermal
stability and adsorption capacity for tunable performances.

Subtask 3.2 Long-term Lab-scale Evaluation
 For practical DAC operations, sorbents should show robust and consistent performances.
Therefore, a 100 adsorption/desorption cyclic test was performed for the sorbents. The CO2
adsorption phase was run using 400 ppm CO2 in dry air at 30 °C (as mentioned in the above
experimental section), and the desorption phase was run between 90 °C and 100 °C under N2 flow
for 5 minutes to save total experiment time. The desorption temperatures were varied to examine
the thermal stability of the sorbents. The 100 cyclic performance data for the unmodified PEI/silica
and mPEI-1 and 2/silica sorbents are shown in Fig. 3.12. The adsorption capacity of PEI/silica
after the 1st cycle was found to be 1.81 mmol CO2/g sorbent, which was expected to be higher than
the modified sorbents. However, the capacity was progressively reduced to 1.23 mmol CO2/g
sorbent after 100 cycles at 100 °C for desorption. The unmodified PEI sorbent showed a noticeably
continuous decrease in the adsorption capacity, indicating the loss of PEI over the cycles. On the
contrary, the mPEI-2/silica sorbent showed stable cyclic performance with a small reduction in
adsorption capacities ranging from 1.25 mmol CO2/g sorbent to 1.08 mmol CO2/g sorbent over the
100 cycles at the same desorption temperature of 100 °C. The reduction percentage of CO2
adsorption capacities after 100 cycles for PEI/silica (32%) was found to be 2.3 times higher than
mPEI-2/silica (~14%). When the desorption temperatures were lowered from 100 °C to 90 °C, it
was confirmed that the thermal stability continued to improve the cyclic performances. Despite
the higher CO2 adsorption capacities after the first cycle, PEI/silica did not show a stable cyclic
performance over the 100 cycles, further manifesting the volatile loss and degradation of amine.

 An in-situ FT-IR experiment was performed by exposing all the unmodified and modified

exposure to N2 at 100 °C. There were additional noticeable bands at ~1,650 to 1,670 cm-1 from
PEI/silica, which could not be properly assigned to. This could be a signature of the formation of
some other amine-containing compounds. However, the mPEI-2/silica sorbent did not show any
other extra band for thermal degradation, attesting to its high thermal stability.

Heat of CO2 Desorption
 Desorption energy is one of the key parameters that determine the cost of a DAC
technology. Thus, the desorption energy requirements were investigated using differential
scanning calorimetry (DSC) for the unmodified and modified sorbents. Prior to the heat of

                                                             Page 38 of 112

desorption measurements, the sorbents were saturated with 400 ppm CO2 in dry air in TGA. Then,
the heat of desorption was measured in a N2 flow at a heating rate of 1 ºC/min and by raising the
temperature up to 120 ºC. The PEI/silica sorbent showed a gradual increase in endothermic heat
flow with a peak at ~90 °C followed by a gradual decrease in heat flow (Fig. 3-16). In contrast,
the mPEI-1 and 2/silica sorbents showed a distinct shift in the onset of the endothermic peaks to
lower temperatures. In particular, as the level of the PEI modification with EC increases from
mPEI-1 to mPEI-2, the peak temperature was shifted to a lower temperature, and the heat flow
ended at a lower temperature, indicating that CO2 desorption occurred at lower temperatures. Then,
the heat of CO2 desorption was determined by integrating the area above the thermogram. The
areas above the thermogram clearly show a decrease in the order of PEI/silica > mPEI-1/silica >
mPEI-2/silica. The heat of CO2 desorption and the energy savings relative to PEI/silica are
summarized in Table 3-2. The heat of CO2 desorption for PEI/silica was determined to be -74
kJ/gmol CO2, which is comparable to the values reported in the literature. In contrast, mPEI-1 and
2/silica showed 51.4% and 56.8% less heat of desorption values than PEI/silica. This data also
supports that the EC modification should lower the bonding strength between adsorbed CO2
molecules and modified amine sites.

Performance evaluations of small monolith blocks
 The schematic of the reactor set-up and its images are shown in Fig. 4-3 and Fig. 4-4. The
prepared blocks were wrapped and placed inside a glass reactor with an inner diameter (ID) of 5
cm and length of 1 m. Air supplied from compressed air supply line containing ~(420-480) ppm
CO2 was used for adsorption, and N2 gas was used for CO2 desorption. The CO2 concentrations
in the compressed air supply line were variable in the range because the indoor air was collected
and compressed inside the building. The main objective of this reactor set-up was to determine
the final washcoating formulations of the different monolith blocks to be supplied by BASF. The
flow rates of air and N2 gases were controlled using a mass flow controller to simulate CO2 capture
performances under various flow rates. Online CO2 analyzers were used to monitor and record
the CO2 concentrations at the inlet and outlet of the reactor. A heating tape was used to wrap
around the glass reactor to control the desorption temperature. Two thermocouples were used to
monitor and record the temperatures of the monolith block and N2 gas. Both the CO2 analyzer and
thermocouples were connected to a computer for data acquisition.

CO2 desorption by vacuum-assisted Joule heating
 After the desorption capacity was determined under heated N2 gas flow, the vacuum-
assisted Joule heating was used for the desorption to determine the CO2 working capacities and
purity. Prior to the desorption, a monolith block (3 cm × 3 cm × 15 cm (L), 64 cpsi) was saturated
with compressed air under different humidity and flow rate conditions. The CO2-saturated
monolith block was then wrapped with carbon fiber (TFP, Optiveil 20107A, 34 g/m2). Both ends
of the carbon fiber were sandwiched between a pair of copper busbar with the dimensions of 4 cm
(L) x 2.5 cm (W) x 1 cm (T). A ceramic blanket was layered over carbon fiber to serve as a heat
insulator. Two alligator clips were used to apply the DC voltage to the carbon fiber as shown in
Fig. 4-7. The entire set-up was kept inside a tubular glass reactor. Prior to applying the DC voltage,
the reactor was evacuated using a vacuum pump by keeping the pressure at -(27.7”-28.3”) Hg. A
sampling bag was attached to the outlet of the vacuum pump to collect the gas desorbed from the
block. A schematic diagram and a picture of the desorption set-up is shown in Fig. 4-8 and Fig.
4-9. A DC voltage of 50 V was applied to the carbon fiber using a DC power supply (Kungber).
The block's temperature at the center was continuously monitored using a thermocouple which
was connected to a data logger. The temperature difference in the entire monolith block by Joule
heating was very narrow (ΔT = 3-4 °C). The volume and purity of the desorbed gas were measured
using a CO2 analyzer. The gas flow rate at the outlet of the CO2 analyzer was determined to be
450 mL/min. The unmodified PEI and modified PEI sorbents-coated blocks were denoted as PEI
and mPEI, respectively. Both silica and alumina substrates were used to impregnate PEI and mPEI.
The blocks washcoating the silica or alumina substrate were denoted as mPEI/silica and
mPEI/alumina, respectively.

gas recovered from the block was used as the full CO2 capacity to define the CO2 recovery. The

vacuum relative to the full CO2 capacity determined by taking into account the residual CO2 gas
recovered under the heated N2 flow. The purity of the desorbed CO2 gas was within the range of

recovery percentages increased in the order of PEI < mPEI-1 < mPEI-2. It is most likely as a result
of the reduced bonding strength between adsorbed CO2 molecules and modified amine sites with
an increase in the level of the EC modification. In other words, the amount of CO2 desorbed at
90 °C shows increasing CO2 recovery with an increase in the modification level.

100 cyclic performance evaluations of small monolith blocks
 Based on the above initial desorption data, 100 cyclic performance evaluations were carried
out for the unmodified PEI and mPEI-2 sorbents-coated monolith blocks. For this cyclic study, a
new reactor made of polycarbonate was used (Fig. 4-16). The monolith block was wrapped with
a carbon fiber (Optiveil 20107A, 34 g/m²). A ceramic blanket was layered over the carbon fiber
to prevent the air from flowing outside the block and to introduce the air inside the monolith
channels. Both ends of the carbon fiber were sandwiched between a pair of copper bus bars. The
entire assembly was placed inside the reactor. Two alligator clips were used to connect the bus
bars to the electrical connectors located at the reactor. A thermocouple was inserted into the
second row from the outer wall of the monolith block.
 The reactor was connected to the compressed air line consisting of a mixture of indoor and
outdoor air supplied by the Facilities of the building. Two CO2 analyzers were used to monitor

                                                                                                  Page 58 of 112

and record the inlet and outlet CO2 concentrations of the reactor. The compressed air was
humidified using a bubbler. A humidity sensor (Vaisala, DMT143) was used to measure the water
vapor concentrations in the inlet air and also in the gas during the desorption phase. The
humidified air was introduced to the reactor at ~175 L/min (LPM) and 50% RH at 20 °C. Once
the block was saturated by monitoring the inlet and outlet CO2 concentrations, both inlet and outlet
valves were closed, and a vacuum pressure of >-28” Hg (-29.9” Hg is a full vacuum) was applied
using a vacuum pump. An impinger placed in an ice bath was connected prior to the vacuum pump
to trap most moisture discharged from the reactor. Then, the desorption phase started by turning
on the DC power supply to heat the block using a temperature controller (Step 1 in Fig. 4-15).
CO2 desorption was performed at 90 °C. The gas desorbed from the block was collected in a gas
sampling bag connected to the outlet of the vacuum pump. The purity and volume of the desorbed
CO2 were determined using a mass flow meter (MFM) connected to a CO2 analyzer with a span
of 0%-100% (Step 2 in Fig. 4-15). A schematic diagram and the pictures of the reactor set-up are
shown in Fig. 4-15 and 4-16, respectively.

Heat of desorption
 The desorption energy required to recover CO2 gas from the unmodified PEI- and the
mPEI-2-coated blocks was determined to be 48 kJ/gCO2 and 54 kJ/gCO2, respectively, based on
the full adsorption, the power supplied (constant 50 W) to the blocks, and the desorption time (31
min to reach 90 C) for Joule heating. The desorption energy of the unmodified PEI block was
slightly lower than that of the mPEI-2 block since the CO2 working capacity for the full adsorption
of the unmodified PEI block (1.64 mmol/g sorbent) was higher than that of the mPEI-2 block (1.45
mmol/g sorbent). However, it should also be noted that the time required to saturate the
unmodified PEI block was at least 30 min longer and the capacity decrease (or degradation) for
the unmodified PEI block will be faster than that for the mPEI-2 block over long-term cycles.
These two factors will eventually make a significance in the CO2 separation productivity and the
sorbent replacement cost.

Summary of performance evaluations of small monolith blocks
 From the 100 cyclic evaluations of the small monolith blocks, the key findings can be
summarized as follows: For the monolith blocks saturated at the low humidity condition of 10%-
20% RH, the unmodified PEI block showed less amounts of CO2 desorbed than the mPEI blocks
between 90 C and 100 C. When ~50% RH was used for the saturation, the unmodified PEI block
showed a ~13% higher CO2 working capacity than the mPEI-2 block at 90 C. As the desorption
temperature is further reduced to 80 C, the working capacity difference was reduced to ~3%-8%.
The mPEI block tested under 60%-70% RH showed almost double the amounts of CO2 desorbed
(i.e., adsorption capacities also doubled) relative to the one tested at 10%-20% RH.

Thermal and chemical stability of sorbents-coated onto monolith
 To gain further insight into the thermal and chemical stability of the sorbents in the co-
presence of CO2, H2O and O2, an in-situ FT-IR analysis was performed using an IR spectrometer
(Thermo Fisher Scientific, Nicolet iS50) coupled with a Diffuse Reflectance Infrared Fourier-
Transform (DRIFT) chamber (PIKE Technologies) with a temperature control system. The spectra
resolution and the number of scans were set to 4 cm-1 and 64, respectively, and were recorded in a
range of (400-4,000) cm-1. For this CO2 adsorption/desorption cyclic study, unmodified PEI and
mPEI-2 sorbent samples were collected from the two separate fresh sorbents-coated monolith
blocks. Prior to the analysis, all the sorbents were degassed at 100 °C for 15 min under dry N2
flow in order to remove any residual impurities. Subsequently, to assess the cyclic stability of
each sorbent, a sorbent was saturated under humid air with 60%-70% RH by passing the dry
compressed air through an impinger filled with water at a flow rate of 25 mL/min at 30 C for 30
min. After the sorbent was saturated, desorption was carried out under dry N2 gas at a flow rate
of 25 mL/min at 90 °C for 10 min, followed by the collection of IR spectra over adsorption and
desorption cycles.
 Fig. 4-23 and Fig. 4-24 exhibit a distinct peak at 1,670 cm-1 for unmodified PEI and mPEI-
2 sorbents, whose intensities increased with an increase in the number of desorption cycles. The
characteristic band at 1,670 cm-1 is mostly attributed to the potential formations of imine, amide,
and/or carbonyl (C=O) groups, which exactly overlap each other. Surprisingly, the other assigned
band at 1,601 cm-1 associated with the deformation of primary amine in PEI was not as pronounced
as the band at 1,670 cm-1 from both sorbents. This could potentially explain the low ammonia
formation or the formation of other N-containing products from the MS analysis. It was noticeable
that the peak intensity at 1,670 cm-1 for the unmodified PEI sorbent increased fast with an increase
in the number of desorption cycles relative to that for the mPEI-2 sorbent. This result clearly
indicates that the EC modification significantly increased the thermal and chemical stability of the
sorbent exposed to the humidity and the desorption temperature by suppressing the potential
formations of imine, amide, and/or carbonyl (C=O) groups.

Subtask 4.2 Determination of Cell Size and Length
CFD model for flow inside reactor
 A 3D CFD model was developed to obtain the velocity and pressure profiles of the air flow
through the monolith blocks inside the reactor. The objective of this subtask was to examine the

                                                          Page 68 of 112

velocity and pressure profiles in terms of cell shape and size and dimensions of monolith block for
scale-up. The model was validated by comparing the computed average linear velocities with the
experimentally determined average linear velocities. Both ceramic monolith blocks obtained from
BASF and Daeyoung C&E have a square-shaped cross-section with the same dimensions of 3 cm
× 3 cm. BASF’s monolith block was made of ceramic honeycomb and has a length of 15 cm.
Daeyoung C&E’s monolith block made of stainless steel (SUS 404) has a length of 5 cm. To have
the same length of 15 cm, three of the metallic blocks were connected in series.
 The metallic monolith blocks provided by Daeyoung C&E have triangular-shaped
corrugated channels while the ceramic blocks provided by BASF have square-shaped ones. The
square-shaped channels of the BASF monolith provide a two-way symmetry which allows for only
a quarter-section of the reactor containing the monolith block to be modeled in order to reduce the
computational time. The monolith blocks have square-shaped cross-sections, and the reactor has
a cylindrical shape. In order to introduce the air flow only to the monolith blocks, the space
between the exterior of the monolith blocks and the inner wall of the reactor was blocked using a
ceramic blanket as shown in Fig. 4-5, which is commonly used in catalyst applications. In order
to implement the air flow blockage in the CFD model, a disconnect between the cylindrical reactor
tube and the monolith entry was added to the 3D CAD model as shown in Fig. 4-25. This is

Task 5. Design, Construction, and Start-up of Air Contactor System
Subtask 5.1 Design of Passive Air Contactor
Airflow Dynamics in Closed Environmental Chamber
 Based on the monolith performance data presented in Task 4, we considered different
passive air contactor (PAC) configurations that can be installed in the closed environmental
chamber located at the Recipient’s (University of Cincinnati) site. To model the airflow in the
PAC system inside the closed environmental chamber, we developed a computational fluid
dynamics (CFD) model based on our previously developed wind-driven open flow model. First,
the air flow dynamics in the environmental chamber was modeled and analyzed as shown in Fig.
5-1. In the empty chamber configuration, a significant air flow rate generated by the fan directly
exits through the ceiling vent as shown in Fig. 5-1. This phenomenon can be attributed to the fact
that without any obstruction, the path of the air flow is straightforward. As the air reaches the
walls of the chamber, some of the airflow gets deflected and starts to flow along the chamber's
contours, leading to the formation of circulating patterns or vortices.

 Figure 5-1. Airflow in the empty environmental chamber without PAC.
 Then, the PAC is placed inside the closed environmental chamber, where a fan propels air
that circulates within the chamber. Upon placing the PAC system in the middle of the
environmental chamber, there was a significant change in the air flow dynamics. The central
placement was deliberately chosen to ensure that the flow obstruction created by the PAC was
symmetrical. By placing the PAC at the center, the airflow experiences similar resistance from all
sides, leading to a more uniform flow pattern. This symmetry was found to be crucial in generating
steady-state solutions in our CFD solver, which ensures that the flow characteristics remain
consistent over time. It should be noted that this air flow dynamics would be different than what
the PAC would experience in the outdoor environment. However, it is important to analyze the
flow dynamics occurring in the closed environmental chamber since the PAC performances will
be evaluated inside the chamber.
 The geometric parameters of the wind catcher, along with the external wind velocity, would
determine the amount of air flow to be captured by the PAC system. Our previously developed
CFD simulations on ~500 different PAC configurations indicate that linear velocity inside the
monolith and airflow capture (i.e., air throughput) are most significantly influenced by the reactor

                                          Page 76 of 112

inlet height (Reactor-H) and the windcatcher inlet height (Windcatcher-H). Briefly, the CFD result
suggested that an optimal performance would be obtained when Reactor-H is ~30 cm and
Windcatcher-H is ~50 cm, beyond which diminishing returns were observed in the captured
airflow within the PAC system. Practical constraints can also emerge at larger Windcatcher-H,
including structural instability and airflow reversal due to increased air drag and flow separation.
 Thus, for an optimal airflow inside our bench-scale PAC system, we focused on a Reactor-
H and a Windcatcher-H of 30 cm and 50 cm, respectively. This approach balances the performance
with practical constraints, allowing for adjustments based on site-specific wind conditions. The
complete list of geometric parameters of the wind catcher section used for simulating the bench-
scale PAC system is summarized in Table 5-1.

Subtask 6.2 Performance Evaluations
 After saturating the block in the DAC system, the desorption was performed inside the
system by first pulling a vacuum followed by heating the block with a target temperature of 90 ℃
at the center of the monolith block. After running multiple tests, the polycarbonate plates
surrounding the blocks showed damage by thermal and mechanical stresses and thus the main body
part was upgraded to a combination of aluminum alloy and polycarbonate plates (Fig. 6-1). When
using Joule heating by wrapping the block (15 cm x15 cm x15 cm) with a heating tape, there was
a significant temperature gradient with respect to positions inside the monolith block (e.g., >50 C
difference between the center and outer cells) and thus CO2 recovery was not higher than ~55%.
Therefore, we attempted to increase the recovery in a vacuum oven using an electromagnetic field.
Prior to the heating, the oven chamber was depressurized to a vacuum pressure of -29.5” (inches)
Hg (i.e., 98% vacuum) so that radiative heat transfer from the walls of the oven chamber and
conduction from the oven floor in contact with the block. The poor heat-transfer properties of the
cordierite block still posed a serious challenge for Joule heating-based CO2 desorption.
 To determine the time to reach ~95 C, wireless temperature probes were used to monitor
the temperatures inside the block (Fig. 6-3). Five holes were made inside a mPEI-2/alumina
sorbent-coated block in which wireless temperature probes were placed to measure the internal
temperatures of the block. Two desorption runs were performed. The first run was performed
without initially saturating the block while in the second run, the same block was saturated under

                                                         Page 94 of 112

air at 15 ℃ and 60% RH. The temperature of the oven was set to 95 ℃. The oven temperature
was raised under vacuum, and the block temperatures were monitored over time.

under air at 15 ℃ and 50% RH, the time taking to reach the temperature was longer (Fig. 6-4 (b)).
This difference is deemed to be attributable to the energy used for the desorption of water vapor
and CO2 molecules. However, such a difference was not observed during the Joule heating-based
desorption of unsaturated and saturated small blocks. It is most likely because electromagnetic
radiation is less effective in heat transfer than conduction at this low temperature window.

Additional Study on Desorption Capacities and Kinetics at Low Temperatures
 The poor heat-transfer properties of the ceramic monolithic block overshadowed the heat
of desorption required for unmodified and modified PEI sorbents-coated blocks and also made it
difficult to differentiate the different characteristics of the two sorbents. Therefore, two additional
performance evaluations were carried out in addition to those from the monolithic structure. In
Table 3-2, the heat of desorption values for the modified PEI sorbents in a powdered form showed
>50% reductions relative to those for unmodified PEI sorbents. However, the DSC measurements
do not provide the information on the desorption kinetics. Thus, temperature-programmed
desorption (TPD) experiments were also carried out to investigate the desorption kinetics. In
addition, the working capacities of the small monolithic blocks were also evaluated at the low
temperatures between 45 ℃-70 ℃.

                                                           Page 101 of 112

Temperature-programmed desorption (TPD)
 To study the desorption behavior and kinetics of the sorbents under different desorption
temperatures, CO2/H2O TPD experiments were performed in a custom-made sorbent-bed reactor
as shown in Fig. 6-9. For each test, ~50 mg of the sorbent was loaded into a U-tube reactor. The
reactor was placed in a temperature-controlled bath regulated by a liquid re-circulator (Isotemp
4100 R20, Fisher Scientific). Prior to the adsorption, a sorbent sample was degassed under a 200
ml/min N2 flow at 90 ℃ to remove pre-adsorbed CO2 and H2O. The outlet concentrations of CO2
and H2O were measured using a CO2/H2O analyzer (LI-COR 850). Subsequently, the sorbent-bed
reactor was cooled down to the adsorption temperature of 30 ℃ and the gas was switched to humid
air (~50%-60% RH at 30 ℃) consisting of ~400 ppm CO2. The bed was saturated for 3 hr with
the outlet CO2 concentration reaching up to >95% breakthrough. After the saturation, the air gas
was switched to N2, and the temperature was gradually ramped at ~1.5 ℃/min to a specific
desorption temperature (i.e., 45 ℃, 70 ℃, and 90 ℃). The desorption capacities and rates under
N2 gas sweep were determined from the outlet concentration profiles.

 Figure 6-9. System set-up for CO2 TPD experiments.

 The TPD profiles from the experiments are shown at (a) 45 ℃, (b) 70 ℃, and (c) 90 ℃
desorption temperatures in Fig. 6-10. First, the PEI/silica sorbent exhibited CO2 desorption
profiles highly sensitive to desorption temperatures. As the desorption temperature decreased from
90 ℃ to 45 ℃, the CO2 desorption profiles showed broader desorption envelopes, delayed peak
maxima, and longer desorption time for completion, indicating slower desorption kinetics relative
to its modified counterparts. For example, the time required for complete CO2 desorption reduced
to 38 min, ~55 min, >240 min at 90 ℃, 70 ℃, and 45 ℃ desorption temperatures, respectively. It
should be noted that at 45 ℃, the CO2 desorption rate did not completely reach down to zero even
after 240 min.
 On the other hand, the mPEI sorbent with the three different modification levels showed a
clear trend of faster CO2 desorption. In particular, as the modification level increases, CO2

                                        Page 102 of 112

desorption starts and completes early with a decrease in the area under the profile. This result also
supports our previous hypothesis that the reduced bonding strengths between CO2 molecules
adsorbed onto the modified amine sites can desorb CO2 gas at low desorption temperatures. In
addition, similar desorption profiles were obtainable at 70 ℃ and 90 ℃, suggesting that similar
CO2 desorption performances can be expected between 70 ℃ and 90 ℃. At 45 ℃, the temperature
dependence of the CO2 desorption kinetics became more pronounced: the desorption profile of the
mPEI-3 sorbent looks similar to those at 70 ℃ and 90 ℃ whereas the profiles of the mPEI-1 and
mPEI-2 sorbents showed much slower desorption kinetics with a decrease in the modification level.
These data also supports our hypothesis of the reduced bonding strengths resulting in fast
desorption kinetics particularly at low desorption temperatures.

CO2 Working Capacities of Small Monolith Blocks at Low Temperatures
 The above desorption data for the powdered sorbents were obtained under N2 flow at the
low temperature window. Although the desorption mechanisms are different, it has higher
practical importance to evaluate the desorption characteristics and CO2 working capacities from
monolithic blocks with the sorbents washcoated at the same temperature window. Experiments
were conducted in the same reactor shown in Fig. 4-16.
 For each test, a sorbent-coated block was wrapped in a 25 cm × 15 cm carbon-fiber sheet,
and the ends of the sheet were secured between rolled copper bus bars to establish an electrical
contact. The wrapped block was then covered with a ceramic blanket to prevent a bypass air flow
during adsorption and to reduce heat loss during Joule heating. Electrical connections to the
reactor terminals were made using alligator clips, and thermocouple was used to monitor the real-
time temperature of the monolith block. The prepared assembly was then placed into the reactor.
Air supplied from the Facilities Department was used as the adsorption feed gas. The air stream
was humidified to ~50% RH at 20 °C and delivered to the reactor at a flow rate of ~175 L/min.
The CO2 concentrations at the reactor inlet and outlet were continuously monitored using two

                                                                   Page 105 of 112

online CO2 analyzers (0 ppm to 2,000 ppm, Quantek Inc.). Once a monolith block was saturated
with CO2, the reactor was isolated, and the residual gas was evacuated to -28 inches of Hg using a
vacuum pump. Then, desorption was initiated by applying DC power to induce Joule heating,
raising the block temperature to a desorption temperature (i.e., 45 ℃, 60 ℃, or 70 °C). Then, the
block was held at a target temperature for the predetermined durations of 5 min, 15 min, or 60 min
while the vacuum pump was continuously running. A downstream cold trap was used to condense
the water vapor from the outlet stream, and high-purity CO2 gas was collected in a sampling bag.
The CO2 composition and the quantity of the collected gas were determined using a CO2 analyzer
coupled with a mass flow meter. After the temperature-vacuum desorption used to determine the
CO2 working capacity, further desorption was run to separate the remaining CO2 in the block by
ramping the temperature to 90 ℃ under a 10 L/min of N2 flow.
 Figure 6-12 presents the CO2 working capacities of the blocks containing unmodified and
modified PEI/alumina blocks under different desorption temperatures between 45 ℃-70 ℃ and
vacuum operating durations between 15 min and 60 min. Overall, the working capacities
decreased with a decrease in desorption temperature for all the sorbent blocks. Most importantly,
across the entire desorption temperatures below 70 ℃, the mPEI sorbent-coated blocks showed
higher CO2 working capacities than the PEI sorbent-coated block. These results are different from
the CO2 working capacities obtained between 80 ℃ and 90 ℃ shown in Fig. 4-22. These data
clearly show that there is an inversion of the working capacities at ~70 ℃. In addition, the block
with the unmodified PEI sorbent seems to be more dependent on vacuum operation duration than
that with the mPEI sorbents, suggesting longer vacuum operation most likely attributed to the
higher bonding strength.
 The block with the PEI sorbent showed the average working capacities of (0.58 and 0.84)
mmol CO2/g sorbent at 60 ℃ and 70 ℃, respectively. When the desorption temperature was
lowered to at 45 ℃, it did not show any detectable CO2 working capacity. When the block was
run at 53 ℃ (i.e., the mid-temperature between 45 ℃` and 60 ℃) for 60 min, a negligible CO2
working capacity of 0.01 mmol CO2/g sorbent was detected. On the other hand, the blocks with
the mPEI-1, mPEI-2, and mPEI-3 sorbents showed 1.09, 1.03 and 1.20 mmol CO2/g sorbent at 60 ℃
and (1.40, 1.20 and 1.20) mmol CO2/g sorbent at 70 ℃ respectively. When the blocks in the same
order were evaluated at 45 ℃, the average working capacities were (0.58, 0.57 and 0.80) mmol
CO2/g sorbent, respectively.
 It is interesting to note that the block with the mPEI-1 sorbent shows higher CO2 working
capacities than those with the mPEI-2 and mPEI-3 sorbents at ~60 ℃ to 70 ℃. However, the
mPEI-3 sorbent-coated block showed higher CO2 working capacities than the other two sorbents-
coated blocks below 60 ℃. These findings suggest that low level of amine modification with less
reduced bonding strength results in high CO2 working capacities at ~60 ℃ to 70 ℃. However, at
low desorption temperatures (e.g., below 60 ℃), the less reduced bonding strength does not seem
to allow for the desorption. As the level of the amine modification increases, the bonding strengths
between adsorbed CO2 molecules and modified amine sites become reduced and thus give low
CO2 working capacities at ~60 ℃ to 70 ℃. However, the reduced bonding strengths can benefit
from the low desorption temperatures (i.e., below 60 ℃) which can show high CO2 working
capacities. These results clearly underscore that the modified amine-based sorbent technology has
potential to utilize low desorption temperatures that would also provide higher resistance to
thermal and oxidative degradations.
