 1. SUMMARY

This study measured radiolytic gas generation and pressure buildup in a sealed stainless-steel
system containing alkaline Mo-99 solution with added sodium nitrate as a hydrogen suppressant.
In each of two experiments, approximately 200 Ci of Mo-99 solution was transferred into a
closed experimental vessel inside a hot cell, isolated, and monitored for pressure rise caused by
gas generation during radioactive decay. After pressure buildup, headspace gas samples were
collected and analyzed by mass spectrometry to determine hydrogen and oxygen concentrations.
The purpose was to quantify the magnitude of pressure buildup in a closed system and to
characterize the gas composition produced by radiolysis of the Mo-99 target solution under
representative handling and storage conditions.

The two experiments used similar total Mo-99 activity but differed in solution volume,
headspace volume, and leak integrity. In the first experiment, 197.5 Ci of Mo-99 in 16.92 mL of
solution was loaded into a vessel with a 31.1-mL headspace; a small leak was later identified,
and the measured peak pressure of 46 psig was extrapolated to about 70 psig in the absence of
leakage. The headspace gas from this experiment contained about 21.4% H2 and 63.4% O2, but
the composition was influenced by preferential hydrogen loss through the leak. In the second
experiment, 193 Ci of Mo-99 in 5.41 mL of solution with 0.46 g NaNO3 was loaded into a vessel
with a larger 42.61 mL headspace, and no detectable leak was observed. This experiment
reached a peak pressure of 38 psig, and the measured gas composition was approximately
28.2% H2 and 51.5% O2. Because the second experiment was leak-free, it is considered the more
reliable indicator of the true pressure buildup and intrinsic radiolytic gas composition of the
Mo-99 solution.

Pressure Buildup in Closed Containers

In a sealed vessel containing an irradiated aqueous solution, radiolytically generated gases
initially dissolve in the liquid phase. The dissolved concentration of each gas species increases
until the solution becomes saturated at the prevailing partial pressure. Once the liquid phase
reaches saturation, continued radiolytic gas production leads to the accumulation of a gas phase
in the headspace and a corresponding increase in total system pressure.

The rate of pressure rise in the headspace depends on several coupled factors: the dose rate
(proportional to the activity of the dissolved radioisotope and its decay energy), the G-values for
gas production, the headspace volume, the solution volume, the temperature, and the solubility of
each gas in the solution [6]. For a sealed vessel at constant temperature, the ideal gas law can be
used to relate the pressure contribution from radiolytic gas accumulation in the headspace to the
total moles of gas produced:

gas production. As H2 and O2 accumulate in the headspace and their partial pressures rise, the

                                           H2 + OH• → H2 O + H•

This reaction converts the relatively inert H2 molecule back into a reactive hydrogen atom, which
can then participate in further radical chemistry, including reactions that reform water. Similarly,
dissolved O2 reacts efficiently with hydrated electrons and hydrogen atoms [2]. These reactions
consume the radical precursors that would otherwise form additional H2, and they convert
dissolved O2 into superoxide and hydroperoxyl radicals, which can subsequently recombine to
reform H2O2 and ultimately water. The net effect is that as gas pressures build in a closed
system, the rates of the back-reactions increase, progressively reducing the net rate of gas
production. Eventually, a dynamic steady state is approached in which the rates of radiolytic gas
generation and recombination are balanced, and the pressure asymptotically approaches a plateau
[7, 8].

Effect of Sodium Nitrate on Radiolytic Hydrogen Generation

The addition of sodium nitrate (NaNO3) to irradiated aqueous solutions is a well-established
strategy for suppressing radiolytic hydrogen generation. The nitrate anion (NO3⁻) is an efficient
scavenger of both hydrated electrons and hydrogen atoms. However, nitrate scavenging cannot
eliminate hydrogen production. It is also important to note that while nitrate suppresses hydrogen
generation, oxygen production may be enhanced because the scavenging of reducing radicals
reduces the back-reactions that would otherwise consume H2O2 and O2 [9]. The use of NaNO3 is
therefore a practical mitigation measure that substantially reduces, but does not eliminate, the
risk of flammable hydrogen accumulation in sealed containers of high-activity Mo-99 solutions.

This report presents experimental measurements of pressure buildup and gas composition in
sealed vessels containing approximately 200 Ci of Mo-99 in alkaline solution, with NaNO3
added as a hydrogen suppressant. Two experiments were conducted: the first with 1.44 g of
NaNO3 added to 16.92 mL of product solution, and the second with 0.46 g of NaNO3 added to
5.41 mL of product solution. Pressure was monitored continuously for several days in each
experiment, and representative gas samples were collected for composition analysis.

Experimental Setup Design

Most of the experimental apparatus was constructed from Type 304 or Type 316 stainless steel.
Swagelok ball valves fitted with radiation-tolerant ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene
seats, rather than standard modified PTFE seats, were used throughout the system. Liquid
transfers within the apparatus were performed under vacuum, except for the syringe used to
introduce wash water into the primary shipping container. All syringes had a capacity of 120 mL,
which provided sufficient vacuum for solution transfer.

The primary solution vessel was procured from Swagelok and connected to the remainder of the
system using stainless steel compression fittings. System pressure was monitored using both a
pressure transducer and an analog pressure gauge. Each instrument was calibrated independently,
and the readings were cross-checked to verify measurement accuracy. For overpressure
protection, the system was equipped with a burst disk rated at 450 psi. The burst disk discharge
was routed through a HEPA filter to minimize the potential for airborne radioactive
contamination in the event that liquid droplets were expelled from the product vessel during

 6

pressure relief. The wash bottle had a capacity of 100 mL, sufficient to accommodate the full
volume of wash water contained in the syringe used to rinse the primary shipping container.
Before testing, the experimental setup was leak-tested using a helium mass spectrometer leak
detector. The gas collection vial was evacuated, and the isolation valve (V2) was closed prior to
initiating the experiment.

Gas Analysis

GSD 320 Gas Analyzer (Figure 3). The instrument is a 1‒200 amu quadrupole mass
spectrometer equipped with a secondary electron multiplier detector. It includes a split interface
that allows atmospheric sampling and analysis. The analyzer is connected to a gas sampling and
analysis manifold. A schematic of the manifold is shown in Figure 4, and a photograph is
provided in Figure 5. The manifold consists of 1/4-in. stainless steel tubing with Swagelok®
valves and fittings and is connected to a vacuum pump to enable the introduction and evacuation
of standard and sample gases. System pressure is monitored using an OMEGA
MMA030V10H4C0T3A5CE pressure transducer.

Mo-99 solution

The Mo-99 solution in 0.2M NaOH was obtained from the National Institute for Radioelements,
Belgium. Upon receipt, the solution volume was adjusted as necessary by adding 0.2 M NaOH
and solid NaNO3 to achieve the desired composition. The Mo-99-containing solution was
delivered in the shielded shipping container shown in Figure 6. This shielded container housed
both the secondary and primary shipping containers. First, the shielded container was removed
from the overpack and placed on the transfer cart. The bolts securing the top flange were then
removed, and the flange was lifted off. The carts containing the experimental setup and the
shielded shipping container were subsequently transferred into the hot cell.

Hot Cell Operations

Inside the hot cell, the top flange of the shielded container was removed using telemanipulators.
The secondary shipping container was extracted from the shielded container with a magnetic
lifting tool (Figure 7) and placed in the designated holder on the experimental cart. The cap of
the secondary container was removed using a custom 3D-printed socket, exposing the primary
shipping bottle, which was then transferred to its designated holder. The cap of the primary
bottle was also removed using a custom 3D-printed socket, and the solution transfer tube was
inserted into the bottle.

Valve V3 (Figure 1) was placed in the transfer position, and valves V1, V3, and V4 were

 4. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

4.1 EXPERIMENT 1, USING 200 Ci OF Mo-99

Before Experiment 1, the experimental apparatus was assembled on the transfer cart and leak-
tested using a helium mass spectrometer leak detector operating in direct vacuum mode. No
leaks were detected under vacuum conditions. However, when the system was pressurized to
100 psi, a small pressure leak was identified. Because the leak rate was low and there was
insufficient time to rebuild and requalify the system before the experiment, the decision was
made to proceed with testing.

Approximately 200 Ci of Mo-99 solution (total volume: 16.92 mL) was received for Experiment
1. Prior to the experiment, 1.44 g of NaNO3 was added to the product solution, resulting in a
final headspace volume of 31.1 mL. The entire solution inventory was successfully transferred
from the shipping container to the experimental apparatus. Following the transfer, valves V1,
V3, and V4 (Figure 1) were closed to isolate the product vessel, and pressure data acquisition

cylinder, and the cylinder was removed for subsequent gas composition analysis. The hydrogen
and oxygen concentrations determined for this sample are reported in Table 2. The analytical
uncertainty was estimated to be ±12% for hydrogen and ±10% for oxygen.

4.3 DISCUSSION

A summary of the experimental conditions, measured pressure, and radiolytic gas generation for
the two experiments is presented in Table 4. The slight difference in starting Mo-99 activity is
due to minor delays in delivery of Mo-99 to Argonne; however, the significantly different
radioisotope concentrations for these two experiments should be noted: 11.67 Ci/mL for
Experiment 1 and 35.67 Ci/mL for Experiment 2. Despite these differences, the overall results
are broadly similar. Total gas generation over the full decay of the isotope was 6.25–7.3 mmol.
The variation in gas yield can be attributed primarily to the difference in solution volumes. In the
smaller-volume solution (Experiment 2), a greater fraction of the high-energy beta and gamma
radiation escapes the solution volume, resulting in lower effective energy deposition per unit
activity. Consequently, Experiment 1, with its larger solution volume and correspondingly higher
radiation self-absorption, produced more total radiolytic gas, consistent with its higher leak-
corrected peak pressure.

The significantly lower H2:O2 ratio observed in Experiment 1 (0.338) compared to Experiment 2
(0.548) is attributed to preferential hydrogen loss through a leak identified during Experiment 1.
Because H2 and O2 have very different molecular weights (2 vs. 32 g/mol), they effuse through a
molecular-scale leak at different rates. According to Graham’s law of effusion,

 5. CONCLUSION

In Experiment 2, the lower measured peak pressure (38 psig, versus approximately 70 psig leak-
corrected in Experiment 1) is primarily attributable to its larger headspace volume (42.61 vs.
31.1 mL) and smaller solution volume (5.41 vs. 16.92 mL), both of which reduced the total
amount of gas generated and the pressure increase per mole of gas produced.

The higher O2 fraction and lower H2 fraction observed in Experiment 1 relative to Experiment 2
are consistent with preferential effusive loss of H2 through the leak, rather than with a genuine
difference in radiolytic chemistry. This interpretation is supported by the fact that the solution
compositions, including the NaNO3 concentration (approximately 1.0 M), were essentially
identical in both experiments.

Because Experiment 2 was leak-free, its data provide the more reliable measure of both the
actual pressure buildup and the intrinsic gas composition produced by radiolysis of the Mo-99
target solution under these conditions. Its results, a peak pressure of 38 psig and a headspace
composition of approximately 28% H2 and 52% O2, should therefore be regarded as the more
accurate representation of system behavior and used as the basis for engineering design
calculations and safety assessments.
