Northward expansion of high-stature vegetation reveals net surface-cooling feedbacks in the majority of Canadian Boreal-Tundra ecozones
Authors: Daniel Chukwuemeka Amaogu, Enoch Ofosu, Kevin Bradley Dsouza, Jérôme Pigeon, Lukas U. Arenson, Richard Boudreault, Juan Moreno-Cruz, Yuri Leonenko, Pooneh Maghoul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Climate warming has driven widespread shrub and tree expansion across the Canadian boreal-tundra. This is widely expected to amplify surface warming. However, whether this expectation is supported by observations remains unresolved. Here we show, using satellite observations of vegetation change alongside climate and surface-energy data from 1986 to 2023, that about 70% of central and northern Canadian ecozones exhibit net surface cooling tendencies, driven by interactions between vegetation change and the environment that reduce the energy available to warm the land surface ( −0.003 to −0.009W m−2 yr−1). Cooling is strongest in landscapes transitioning toward mixedwood forests, broadleaf vegetation and treed wetlands, reducing warming by approximately 0.015–0.028°C yr−1 relative to shrub- and conifer-dominated areas. These findings challenge the view that vegetation expansion at high latitudes uniformly drives warming, showing that its effects depend on vegetation type, moisture and environmental context. They further suggest that the continued spread of mixed vegetation communities may moderate surface warming previously associated with conifer-dominated landscapes.

Introduction

Climate change represents an existential threat to human societies and ecosystems, with the IPCC1,2 warning that exceeding 1.5 °C of warming may trigger irreversible Earth system feedbacks including permafrost collapse, ice sheet loss and large scale ecosystem shifts. Recent records confirm this acceleration, as Copernicus Climate Change Service3 reported that both 2023 and 2024 were the warmest years on record, with global mean temperatures surpassing the 1.5 °C threshold across sustained multi-month periods. This outcome highlights the urgency of understanding boreal-Arctic system responses, as the Arctic is warming nearly four times faster than the global average1,2. Permafrost thaw in the Canadian Boreal-Tundra (hereafter CBT; Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1) is among the most significant of these risks. Storing over 1500 Gt of carbon, its destabilization could trigger irreversible feedbacks, amplifying greenhouse gas emissions and undermining global climate targets4–7. Consequently, the evaluation of mitigation strategies in the CBT requires consideration not only of their carbon sequestration potential but also of their effects on surface energy balance and permafrost stability8,9.

In this context, tree expansion within the CBT, including both natural vegetation expansion (‘borealisation’) and afforestation, has become an important focus in discussions of vegetation-climate interactions, given the region’s role as a critical frontier for understanding high-latitude climate-ecosystem feedbacks. However, a number of recent studies that are conceptualized through fire-albedo constraints argue that increasing tree dominance in tundra regions north of the boreal treeline, whether through natural vegetation transitions or assisted ones toward taller, darker evergreen vegetation, can reduce surface albedo and modify ground and turbulent heat fluxes. These changes, in turn, may elevate wildfire risk, accelerate permafrost thaw, and increase carbon emissions9–15.

While these studies, framed within the fire-albedo paradigm and emphasizing positive warming feedback mechanisms, have strongly influenced current interpretations of vegetation-climate feedbacks in the CBT, their synthesis simultaneously raises a critical question regarding whether the hypothesized warming responses are consistently supported by observations across the broader CBT. Importantly, many of these expectations can be traced to earlier coarse-resolution modeling studies which, due to simplified representations of surface and vegetation structure, predicted that forest expansion into tundra would enhance localized warming sufficient to accelerate permafrost thaw8,16,17.

Interestingly, emerging empirical evidence from ecological data and remote sensing (both spectral, e.g., normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI)), and direct vegetation identification from satellite imagery now challenge this view. Over the past four decades, large portions of the CBT have undergone persistent greening, primarily through unmanaged expansion of shrubs, treed wetlands, and mixedwood systems18–25. These transitions toward taller and more structurally complex vegetation modify surface energy fluxes and therefore can provide an empirical basis for understanding how vegetation change influences surface energy dynamics across the CBT. Within this framing, if transitions toward higher stature vegetation inherently and uniformly enhanced surface warming, widespread ongoing greening would be expected to produce detectable regional-scale energy imbalances, including persistent increases in radiative forcing, elevated ground heat fluxes, and thaw-related surface responses. However, such patterns have not been systematically confirmed across the CBT, demonstrating that the biogeophysical consequences of northward high-stature vegetation transitions may be more complex than the prevailing fire-albedo paradigm suggests.

While canopy densification is expected to lower albedo, especially in simulation-based frameworks, several empirical studies highlight concurrent vegetation-type structural mechanisms that can increase albedo (e.g., the dominance of deciduous and relatively high-reflectance vegetation) and enhance surface energy dissipation through processes, such as increased latent heat flux, thermal bridging, snow interception, and sub-canopy shading in high-latitude boreal-tundra ecosystems26–31. Moreover, recent modeling studies have demonstrated that vegetation structure and composition (e.g., shrub vs. conifer, deciduous vs. evergreen) critically influence both the direction and magnitude of surface energy and carbon-cycle feedback11,32–35. This perspective is further supported by Ofosu et al. 32 and Dsouza et al. 36, who, using the Carbon Budget Model of the Canadian Forest Sector (CBM-CFS3) together with remote sensing and climate physics modeling at the western boreal margin, showed that species mixing and ecohydrological conditions determine the balance between albedo-related warming effects and carbon uptake associated with shrub and tree expansion.

Despite these prevailing contrasting modeling and empirical evidence perspectives, a clear observation-constrained assessment of how vegetation structural transitions influence surface energy partitioning across the CBT remains lacking. In particular, it is not yet resolved whether large-scale shifts toward higher stature vegetation states produce consistent surface warming responses, as commonly hypothesized, or whether alternative energy balance adjustments emerge under heterogeneous real-world landscape conditions. Therefore, in this study, we present a multi-decadal observation-constrained assessment of how vegetation structural transitions influence surface energy dynamics across the CBT. By integrating satellite-derived vegetation structure information with meteorological and energy flux records spanning 1986 to 2023, we evaluate whether transitions toward higher stature vegetation states are consistently associated with surface warming or cooling responses, thereby providing an observation-based perspective on the biogeophysical consequences of treed landscape development in the CBT.

Results

NDVI trends and associated vegetation structural transitions

To quantify vegetation structural change across the CBT, we combined Landsat-derived NDVI trends (1986–2023; Supplementary Tables 2–3) with vegetation type transitions from independent land-cover datasets (VLCE2 and GLFC; Methods 4.3–4.6,37–40). NDVI greening and browning trends represented continuous vegetation densification and decline, while land-cover transitions mapped from land-cover datasets captured the redistribution of vegetation types and associated area changes41–43. Agreement between these independent approaches provided a consistency check on the mapped transitions. Widespread northward linear greening and localized exponential greening were observed across CBT ecozones (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Tables 4–618,22,44).

Greening trends were consistent across both disturbed (fire and harvest-affected) and background CBT conditions (i.e., full landscape conditions without explicit disturbance masking), with the highest greening trends observed in the Taiga Shield East and the Taiga and Tundra Cordillera ecozones (0.0013–0.0015 NDVI yr-1) over more than 100,000 km2 (Fig. 1). Localized exponential greening is most evident in the Taiga Cordillera and Southern Arctic, reaching up to 69,933 km2 in the Southern Arctic, indicating rapid vegetation expansion in the latter45. In this study, we quantified the extent of exceedance of vegetation density class thresholds to provide additional context for observed vegetation land cover changes. Using Monte Carlo simulations (Method 4.4), we derived the idealized NDVI trend thresholds required to trigger vegetation density class transitions between 1986 and 2023. This yielded a critical minimum threshold of 0.003 NDVIyr−1 for at least one transition to occur along the trajectory from barren to sparse, moderate, and dense vegetated cover, and ±0.0015 NDVI yr−1 for infilling transitions within adjacent classes (classes 4–5 and 1–2; Supplementary Figs. 4–5 and Supplementary Table 3) over the 38-year study period. Thus, high rates of these threshold exceedances were observed across the greening CBT ecozones, including the Taiga Shield East, Taiga Cordillera, Boreal Shield, Tundra Cordillera, and Southern Arctic. These exceedances are consistent with land cover transitions typical of greening ecological succession (precision ≥ 50%; Table 1), corroborating the observed patterns of widespread vegetation densification and northward expansion across the CBT. Altogether, these changes affected approximately 17–50% of the region, or 1.63 million km2. Latitudinally, the band between 50 and 66°N marked the strongest structural transitions, where shrubs gained about 30,000 km2 as bryoid and herbaceous vegetation contracted, especially near 63–65°N. Mixedwood forests expanded most consistently, adding about 170,000 km2 across the Boreal Shield and Taiga Cordillera and increasing by 6600 km2 (332%) in the Boreal Cordillera. Coniferous forests contracted in the Boreal Shield by about 125,000 km2 (15%) but expanded into the Taiga Plains and Taiga Shield East, offsetting losses at 42–52°N with gains above 60°N. Above 66°N gains were smaller but still positive for taller vegetation types, whereas below 56°N changes remained modest and patchy. In high-fire ecozones, such as the Boreal Shield, Taiga Shield West, and Boreal Cordillera, post-disturbance recovery increasingly favored coniferous and mixedwood stands. Generally, wetland conversion to treed wetlands increased across all ecozones. Taken together, these shifts indicate structural intensification of vegetation, with low-stature types, such as bryoids, herbs, and non-treed wetlands progressively being replaced by shrubs, treed wetlands, and taller forest types, most strongly across 55–66°N44,46.

Climatic gradients in zones of vegetation change

Climatic gradients across the CBT indicate increasingly favorable growth conditions toward northern ecozones47,48 (Supplementary Figs. 8–9 and Supplementary Tables 7–8), consistent with the northward vegetation densification described above. Across 45–70°, temperatures increased at 0.026 °C yr−1 (0.022–0.034; Fig. 4a). Greening in northern ecozones (Southern Arctic, Taiga Plains, and Tundra Cordillera) is primarily associated with temperature (r up to 0.3 in spring-summer), whereas southern regions show stronger precipitation sensitivity (Table 2 and Supplementary Tables 9–13). Latitudinal correlations show NDVI-temperature relationships shifting from negative in southern regions (spring r = −0.23 at 47–48°N) to positive across 51-60°N, peaking near 57–58°N in summer (r = 0.33 and Supplementary Table 11). NDVI-precipitation relationships show the opposite pattern, remaining positive in southern summers (up to r = 0.42) but negative across mid-latitudes (r = −0.39 to −0.27) before becoming weakly positive in the far north (r ≈ 0.09 −0.12). Furthermore, in the central latitude CBT ecozones, major vegetation transitions included shrub expansion (11,600 km2; Taiga Shield West), mixedwood expansion (6600 km2; Boreal Cordillera), and conifer expansion in the Taiga Plains and Boreal Cordillera, with additional broadleaf increases. Despite forests remaining predominantly coniferous (>60%), energy flux trends favored net cooling from spring through fall. This pattern was driven mainly by negative ground storage (G ≈ −0.004 Wm−2 yr−1) and evapotranspiration trends (LE up to −0.004 Wm−2 yr−1), which outweighed smaller positive sensible heat (H) trends (Table 3 and Fig. 6), indicating enhanced turbulent cooling and reduced subsurface energy retention. Although winter trends across the three ecozones generally shifted toward warming, the annual balance converged to net cooling: Boreal Cordillera and Taiga Shield West cooled by about −0.003 Wm−2 yr−1, and Taiga Plains by −0.009. Cooling trends dominated more than half of each ecozone (57–69%), corresponding to reductions of 8.41, 9.41, and 24.5% from 1986 mean annual flux baselines of 1.32, 1.18, and 1.36 Wm−2 (Supplementary Table 16). These results indicate that, unlike the low latitude ecozones, central latitude regions acted as persistent energy buffers, releasing ground heat toward the surface and transferring energy from the surface to the atmosphere across most of their landscapes.

At the high latitude CBT ecozones, where vegetation transitions comprised shrub expansion (14,000 km2; Southern Arctic), mixedwood expansion within the Taiga Cordillera, and increasing dominance of taller vegetation north of 66°N (e.g., Tundra Cordillera), seasonal LE trends indicated strengthening ET cooling (−0.00513 to −0.00009W m−2 yr−1), except for a slight winter ET reduction in the Southern Arctic (0.00014W m−2 yr−1). Mean H was generally positive (0.00139 to 0.00588W m−2 yr−1), except during winter in the Southern Arctic (−0.00161) and Taiga Cordillera (−0.00010). Ground storage (G) was the dominant control. In the Southern Arctic, positive spring and fall storage (0.0009-0.0025) reinforced H and produced seasonal warming despite LE cooling, whereas in both Cordilleras strongly negative G (−0.009) intensified LE losses and offset H gains, sustaining net cooling (Table 3 and Fig. 6). Even in summer, when all ecozones experienced storage losses, shallow G decline in the Southern Arctic (−0.001) allowed positive H to dominate, whereas larger Cordilleran drawdowns maintained cooling. Annually, spatial aggregation (Fig. 6) showed convergence to net cooling of −0.006W m−2 yr−1, corresponding to reductions of 30, 22, and 20.4% from baselines of 0.74, 1.03, and 1.09 Wm−2. Cooling fluxes dominated roughly two-thirds of each ecozone. This contrasts with the central ecozones, where flux trends show modest cooling relative to 1986 baselines, and with the low-latitude ecozones, where two-thirds warmed.

Consistent with these energy partitioning patterns, ecozonal albedo responses (Supplementary Tables 17, 18) provide additional radiative context for surface-energy tendencies across ecozones. To distinguish vegetation-driven responses from those modulated by seasonal snow, and to isolate the radiative contribution of vegetation structure from snow-vegetation interactions, we calculated mean percent decadal albedo changes under snow-free summer and annual conditions (Supplementary Table 17) and compared them with responses across the full seasonal and annual cycle under combined vegetation-snow conditions (Supplementary Table 18). Under snow-free conditions, summer and annual albedo responses are predominantly positive (generally up to 0.8% per decade), indicating that vegetation structural transitions alone do not consistently reduce surface reflectivity. When seasonal snow is included, winter responses remain largely positive, whereas spring responses become more variable, with both positive and negative changes across ecozones (e.g., negative spring albedo in the Taiga Shield East, Taiga Plains, and Boreal Cordillera). Overall, annual albedo responses remain predominantly positive across ecozones under both snow-free and combined vegetation-snow conditions except at the Boreal Shield and Boreal Cordillera. Together, these results show that albedo responses to vegetation structural transitions are not uniformly expressed as surface darkening, but are strongly modulated by snow-vegetation interactions, with the greatest divergence occurring during the spring transition period.

In summary, across the CBT ecozone, our attribution analyses show a consistent vegetation-energy coupling. Ecozone-level Kendall’s τ results (Supplementary Table 19) reveal that vegetation gains are strongly associated with increasingly negative net energy flux trends in nearly all ecozones, indicating consistent surface cooling tendencies. Independent statistical comparisons between greening and stable vegetation areas reinforce this pattern: in all five sampling rounds, greening zones exhibit significantly more negative (cooling) net flux trends, supported simultaneously by Welch’s t-tests and Mann-Whitney U tests (Supplementary Table 20). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that ongoing CBT vegetation gains are not only responding to climate warming but are actively mediating surface-energy pathways, promoting outward flux tendencies that can help protect permafrost.

Discussion

Vegetation transitions across the CBT during the past four decades indicate increasing vegetation expansion and reorganization across northern landscapes. Increasing temperature and precipitation have expanded the environmental envelope for vegetation growth, producing widespread greening signals that coincide with structural land cover transitions across ecozones (Fig. 4). Here, by linking long-term NDVI trajectories with independent land cover observations and surface energy flux analyses, this study provides a pan-CBT assessment connecting vegetation structural change and spatial range shifts with surface energy redistribution across ecozones, extending earlier regional boreal-arctic vegetation expansion studies 25,40,54–59. These vegetation structural and spatial range transitions across the CBT remain spatially heterogeneous, varying differentially across latitude, including pronounced changes north of 50°N due to interacting thermal and hydrological constraints (Tables 2 and Supplementary Tables 9–11). In the Southern Arctic and ecozone portions between 60 and 64°N, warming continues to relax thermal limitations and support shrub expansion25,60, as evidenced by NDVI trends correlating positively with growing season and spring temperature (r = 0.20; spring r = 0.28; Supplementary Tables 9–10), whereas in the Taiga Plains warming-induced moisture stress appears to constrain growth (NDVI-temperature and precipitation correlations of −0.26 and 0.21, respectively)61. The Taiga Shield, in contrast, represents a transitional hydroclimatic regime in which relatively small changes in temperature and precipitation can shift ecosystems between greening and decline24,25,62.

Together, these patterns, including exponential greening that outpaces linear responses (e.g., Southern Arctic, Boreal Cordillera, and Taiga Shield) and net forest gains across ecozones toward mixedwood and wetland-treed systems (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Tables 4–6)18,23,24,40,63–66, indicate that the CBT is currently in a disequilibrium vegetation state under climate warming that is tending, with delayed responses to climate forcing22,23,29,53,67, toward intensified high-stature vegetation range shifts, redistribution, infilling, and a more mixed tree-dominated state. This interpretation contrasts with Rotbarth et al. 23, who reported limited tree expansion at the boreal tundra boundary and attributed it to demographic and environmental constraints. In the present analysis, these constraints manifest not as stasis but as turnover, where cases of tree decline coincide with increases in shrub (24.5%) and mixedwood (239%) systems, consistent with shrubification dynamics50 that indicate active vegetation reorganization rather than absence of change.

As taller vegetation increasingly becomes a dominant feature across the CBT through ongoing natural vegetation transitions, these transitions provide a basis for examining how shifts in vegetation structure are associated with coupled changes in ground-plant-atmosphere energy flux partitioning under current climatic conditions. These observations may also provide broader insight into how future vegetation transitions and northward expansion pathways can influence surface energy redistribution across northern environments49,51,53. It is known, and as also found in our study, that borealisation in the CBT toward shrub- and tree-dominated states, and the associated vegetation-mediated energy fluxes, are accompanied by distinct shifts in surface energy exchange that strongly influence ecological feedbacks in the permafrost-underlain CBT68–71. A central question is whether, and which, vegetation structural changes can generate cooling through energy redistribution that offsets the warming effects commonly associated with reduced reflectivity and increased sensible heat flux in taller canopies.

In this study, mixedwood, broadleaf, and wetland-treed expansions are consistently associated with reduced warming, with annual temperature increases damped to approximately 0.015 °C–0.028 °C yr−1 relative to stronger warming in shrub- and conifer-dominated ecozones. Even during the summer thaw period, when higher temperatures would typically enhance ground heat storage, several ecozones exhibited reduced warming trends of approximately 0.01 °C–0.02 °C yr−1 relative to annual means, and in the majority of ecozones increasing albedo rather than the expected decline72,73. This apparent deviation from the expected albedo decline can be understood in the context of snow-vegetation interactions, but also reflects contrasting radiative responses when vegetation structural effects are evaluated independently from, and in combination with, seasonal snow conditions72,74,75. Comparing snow-free conditions, which isolate vegetation structural effects, with full seasonal conditions shows that vegetation change alone does not consistently produce surface darkening, while the inclusion of snow introduces strong seasonal variability, particularly during winter and the spring transition period, when snowmelt likely exposes underlying vegetation and surface heterogeneity, leading to both positive and negative albedo responses across ecozones8,76. Ecozones characterized by mixedwood expansion and Arctic and Shield shrub-dominated transitions generally retain positive snow-free albedo responses, whereas more negative or variable responses occur where conifer-related transitions remain important, consistent with the dependence of albedo on vegetation structure and snow-canopy interactions74,77.

These patterns suggest that albedo responses to vegetation expansion in the CBT are influenced by both snow-vegetation interactions and ecozone-specific vegetation transition pathways, indicating that vegetation structure modifies surface energy exchange through mechanisms that extend beyond simple canopy darkening. For example, denser and moister canopies can scatter incoming radiation and enhance ET, redistributing energy through latent and turbulent flux pathways rather than ground heat storage76,78. The observed combination of increasing albedo, enhanced latent and ground heat flux export (LE, H, G; Figs. 5, 6, Table 3, and Supplementary Table 16), and reduced summer warming, especially in mixedwood, broadleaf, and wetland-treed systems in this study, supports this mechanism. More broadly, our results show that, in the tree- and shrub-vegetated CBT, surface energy exchange is insufficient when interpreted solely through two-dimensional albedo contrasts, but is more realistically understood when accounting for three-dimensional vegetation structure and its influence on radiation scattering and flux partitioning78.

In line with the above energy dissipation mechanism postulation, the observed vegetation change-induced coupling between flux trends and annual surface temperature showed a supporting and expected pattern across most ecozones. Across ecozones, outward (cooling) flux tendencies are associated with reduced warming tendencies, whereas inward (warming) tendencies correspond to stronger warming (Table 4). Broadleaf and mixedwood expansion strengthens LE and G fluxes, enhancing evaporative and conductive energy export10,35,79, while shrub- and conifer-dominated systems amplify sensible heat flux and reinforce warming75,80,81. The Boreal Shield illustrates how balanced canopy composition can damp warming despite inward baseline fluxes (1.94 ± 0.07 W m−2; Tables 3, 4, and Supplementary Table 16), in contrast to Taiga Shield East, where conifer persistence sustains stronger warming. Similarly, the Hudson Plains and Cordilleras maintain cooling tendencies through wetland-treed and open mixedwood mosaics with high reflectivity and strong latent coupling82–84. These findings confirm that vegetation architecture, more than latitude or background forcing alone, drives spatial heterogeneity in warming across the CBT49,85,86. Mixedwood, broadleaf, and wetland-treed systems promote outward energy redistribution and higher reflectivity, whereas shrubification and conifer densification intensify turbulent coupling and warming5,45,73. In addition, modern thermal observations from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers’ Cold Regions Research and Engineering Laboratory show that vegetated permafrost terrains exhibit reduced ground-heat flux and enhanced surface energy dissipation compared with unvegetated tundra87. The cooling tendencies identified here reflect outward surface energy export and reduced ground heat uptake, contributing to reduced surface warming, yet are not directly equatable to net climate forcing benefit, particularly in wetland-dominated systems transitioning to treed landscapes, where disturbance-driven methane emissions not accounted for in this study may limit the magnitude of the climatic benefit of vegetation change for climate warming. Nevertheless, our results provide a structural and process-based basis for understanding how vegetation pathways influence surface energy balance and thermal responses in northern environments. In this context, the biogeophysical outcomes of both natural and managed shrub and tree expansion pathways in the CBT are likely to vary significantly depending on the resulting vegetation composition. Where mixedwood, broadleaf, and wetland-treed systems dominate, surface cooling tendencies have been observed, whereas conifer-dominated expansion is more consistently associated with warming responses.

It is important to note, however, that this study does not resolve the full climate implications of vegetation structural change across the CBT. In particular, the coupled energy and greenhouse gas consequences, especially methane dynamics, where carbon uptake and emissions may diverge, must be better understood before the observed surface cooling tendencies can be extended to net climate conclusions. Integrating flux tower, chamber, airborne, and remote sensing observations, together with field validation in key transition zones, will be essential to constrain these interactions and assess their broader applicability. Within this study framework, our results show that vegetation structure governs surface energy partitioning and influences reduced surface warming, with implications for permafrost stabilization, indicating that the climatic relevance of afforestation pathways in the CBT may depend on ecozone conditions and vegetation type.

Methods

Description of study area

The CBT analysis extent covers approximately 589 M hectares (or ~6 million km2), across which about 6.54 billion pixels were analyzed. This region covers nine Canadian ecozones, including the Taiga Shield (TS), Taiga Shield West (TSW), Boreal Sheild (BS), Boreal Cordillera (BC) Taiga Cordillera (TC), Taiga Plains (TP), Tundra Cordillera (TUCO) Hudson Plains (HP), and Southern Arctic (SA)88–93. Full disturbance regimes, management histories, and floristic conditions across these regions is in the supplementary materials (Section 2) and by Payette et al. 94, Payette and Delwaide 89, St-Onge and Grandin 91, Hermosilla et al. 88, Rowe 95, and Timoney et al. 96.

NDVI linear and exponential trend slope reclassification

To utilize the observed NDVI trend slopes as an independent validation proxy to check consistency of landcover transition (subsection 4.6), we reclassified the NDVI slope grids to match landcover change or stable areas derived from landcover datasets. Grid-based binning is a standard approach for identifying threshold values across spatial grids. To detect areas where NDVI trends indicate forward (greening), backward (browning), or stable conditions and enable overlay with the 2022 landcover transition raster, we needed to define slope thresholds for NDVI reclassification. In the absence of clearly defined thresholds in the literature, we specified NDVI range thresholds based on theoretical NDVI behavior for four vegetation density classes in our region: barren (−0.01–0.10), sparse (0.10–0.30), moderate (0.30–0.60), and dense (0.60–0.80)100–102. Based on NDVI behavior, we assumed that the barren threshold corresponds to bare ground and wetlands, sparse to lichens, herbs, and shrublands, moderate to patchy evergreen and deciduous vegetation, and dense to fully stocked single or mixed evergreen stands marking the end of succession. We further assumed that in a vegetative successional (greening) or non-recovery (browning) system, the NDVI end-state must lie within either barren or dense thresholds according to its trajectory.

Using these ranges for each class below the dense category, we ran 5000 Monte Carlo simulations to derive a single minimum NDVI slope threshold required for class transition over 38 years (1986–2022) or stability across vegetation density classes consistent with observed landcover transitions. Results showed that an absolute median NDVI slope of at least 0.003 NDVI yr−1 (Supplementary Fig. 2)is required for a full transition between classes. We therefore adopted half of this value as the critical threshold: 0.0015 NDVI yr−1 for linear trends and 0.00132 NDVI yr−1 for exponential trends, representing an intra-class “infilling” threshold: large enough to indicate intra-class dynamics but insufficient for a full class shift. With these thresholds, we formulated five NDVI trend classes corresponding to significant browning and moderate browning (Classes 1–2), statistically stable (Class 3), and moderate and significant greening (Classes 4–5; Supplementary Table 3), which we used to reclassify the NDVI trend slope rasters. The two (i.e., linear and exponential) resulting five-class rasters were then used for cross-consistency checking and validation of landcover transition classes as described in subsection 4.6.

Landcover transition analysis

For landcover transition analysis, we used Canadian annual landcover datasets (1984–2022) from the Virtual Land Cover Engine (VLCE2)94 and the global landcover dataset (1985–2022; GLC_FCS30D, hereafter GLFC V3)98. VCLE2 classes include water, snow/ice, rock/rubble, exposed/barren, bryoids, shrubs, wetland (treed and non-treed), herbs, coniferous, broadleaf, and mixedwood. For our analysis, we extracted the Southern Arctic extent of GLFC V3 and harmonized its subcategories (waterbody, snow/ice, shrubland, bareland, herbs, deciduous forest, evergreen forest, and mixedleaf forest) into corresponding VCLE2 categories to form a unified CBT annual landcover dataset after masking unclassified, water, and built-surface pixels. To establish a stable baseline and reduce spurious year-to-year noise, we applied a pixelwise majority filter to the VCLE2 portion of the 1984–1986 unified landcover dataset and defined the dominant class per pixel as the 1986 baseline. For the GLFC portion, we used the 1985 epoch as the baseline, as it was derived from a multi-year composite that minimized classification errors. Using the harmonized dataset with stabilized baselines, we implemented a pixel-wise FROM–TO (i.e., from baseline class in 1986 to final class in 2022) landcover transition matrix (Supplementary Fig. 5), producing a raster of coded 2022 pixel-wise transition values (full implementation in Supplementary Section 3). From this transition raster, we tabulated geodesic landcover class area changes (1986–2022) and change types (greening, browning, stable, and lateral vegetation shift; Supplementary Fig. 5). For the form changes type, we defined a “meaningful ecological change” as a pixel whose 2022 class matches its 2020 or 2021 state, signifying transition completion, and that has followed a consistent meaningful ecological successional trajectory since its first change within the 37-year series.

Vegetation change and climate trend relationship

To associate the observed vegetation type changes with climatic (temperature and precipitation) conditions, we performed latitudinal gradient and ecozone-level association seasonal and annual analyses. First, we retrieved spring (March–May), summer (June-August), fall (September-November), winter (December-February), and growing season (June–September) monthly minimum and maximum temperatures (°C) and precipitation (mm/month) between 1986 and 2023 from the CRU TS 4.09 dataset103, which had been spatially downscaled from its native 0.5° resolution to approximately 2.5 arc-minutes (km2) using WorldClim v2104. Monthly average temperature was calculated as the mean of minimum and maximum values, after which statistically significant seasonal linear trends (P≤0.05) were derived spatially, pixel-wise, for both climatic variables. We then intersected, pixel-wise, ecozonally and latitudinally, the resulting temperature and precipitation statistically significant seasonal trend rasters one by one with the NDVI-derived statistically significant trend raster (Section 4.3) to estimate their correlations (Spearman, P ≤ 0.05), which we presented in Tables 2, Fig. 4a, b, and Supplementary Table 4.

Attribution of partitioned flux-temperature correlations to vegetation change

We previously applied a series of analytical steps to link climate warming as a primary driver of the vegetation changes reported in this study. First, in subsection 4.6 (results in subsection ??, Table 1), we matched NDVI change with vegetation-type transitions, establishing NDVI trends as a reliable proxy for vegetation gain and loss. Next, in subsection ??, we correlated NDVI trends with long-term temperature and precipitation trends (results in subsection ??Table 2 and Supplementary Table 10–11), implicating climate warming as a primary driver of the observed vegetation changes. To quantify the ecozone-level association between positive NDVI trends (greening) and net energy flux trends, we computed Kendall’s τ using unique, non-overlapping 30,m raster cells from four independent sampling-correlation rounds, considering pixels where NDVI trends exceeded 0 or 0.0015. By default, correlations based on NDVI trends >0 were retained unless higher NDVI thresholds produced stronger and statistically significant negative associations supported by sufficient sample size. These analyses revealed a clear negative monotonic association between positive NDVI trends and net energy flux trends (Supplementary Table 19), indicating surface-cooling tendencies.

We further compared whether stable vegetation areas (no change) differed from greening areas in the magnitude of their net energy flux trends. To do this, we compared net energy flux trends ( W m−2 yr−1) between stable and greening vegetation zones using four sampling-comparison rounds. Each round represents an independent random draw of new, non-overlapping 30m cells from the full study area, stratified into stable (no vegetation transition) and greening (vegetation transition; Supplementary Fig. 5) zones. Net energy flux trend values were compared using Welch’s t-test and the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test, with sample sizes varying across rounds due to the randomized, non-replacement spatial sampling design. Across all rounds, greening areas consistently exhibited more negative (outward) net energy flux trends than stable areas, with statistically significant differences (Supplementary Table 20).
