Early-Stage Oral Squamous Cell Carcinoma in Adolescents and Young Adults Compared with Older Patients Exhibits Distinct Clinicopathological Features
Authors: Sawako Ono, Katsutoshi Hirose, Hotaka Kawai, Tatsuya Abe, Shintaro Sukegawa, Masanori Masui, Chihoko Ikeda, Madoka Isomura, Yuri Tachibana, Junya Ono, Katsumitsu Shimada, Hitoshi Nagatsuka, Hidetaka Yamamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to compare the clinicopathological features of stage I–II oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) between adolescents and young adults (AYAs) and older patients, and to identify prognostic factors in AYA OSCC.MethodsForty-eight AYA patients aged 15–39 years and 69 older patients aged ≥ 65 years with surgically treated stage I–II OSCC were retrospectively analyzed. Histological evaluation, survival analysis, immunohistochemistry for p53, p16, and MTAP, high-risk HPV RNA in situ hybridization, and fluorescence in situ hybridization for CDKN2A and MTAP were performed.ResultsCompared with older patients, OSCCs in AYAs were more frequently located on the tongue (93.7% vs. 47.8%) and more often exhibited a superficial spreading growth pattern (60.4% vs. 31.8%). AYA tumors more frequently showed modified WPOI-1, high tumor budding, and abnormal p53 immunophenotypes (85.4% vs. 68.1%). AYA patients showed significantly better overall and disease-free survival than older patients. Within the AYA group, depth of invasion (DOI) > 5 mm was associated with distant metastasis and poorer overall and disease-free survival. Postoperative lymph node metastasis occurred in 31.2% of AYAs. Univariable analysis suggested that clinical stage II, tumor thickness > 5 mm, DOI > 5 mm, and tumor budding scores 1–2 were associated with postoperative lymph node metastasis. Molecular status, including p53, p16/CDKN2A, and MTAP, did not provide robust prognostic stratification.ConclusionEarly-stage AYA OSCC exhibits distinct clinicopathological features. DOI was the strongest prognostic indicator, whereas clinical stage, tumor thickness, and tumor budding may provide additional prognostic information regarding the risk of late nodal metastasis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12105-026-01944-w.

Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), arising from the oral mucosa, is one of the most common malignant neoplasms in the head and neck region and predominantly affects older adults. Tobacco smoking and alcohol consumption are well-established risk factors, and a subset of cases has also been associated with human papillomavirus (HPV) infection [1, 2].

However, since the 1980s, several countries have reported an increasing incidence of OSCC, particularly oral tongue squamous cell carcinoma, among younger patients, and this trend has persisted in recent decades [3–10]. OSCC in younger patients has attracted increasing attention because many affected individuals lack conventional risk factors such as tobacco smoking and alcohol consumption [8, 9]. Nevertheless, previous studies have used nonuniform definitions of “young” patients, including age cutoffs of ≤ 30, ≤ 40, ≤ 45, or < 50 years, making direct comparisons among studies difficult. In oncology, adolescents and young adults (AYAs) are typically defined as individuals aged 15–39 years, and cancers arising in this age group are increasingly recognized as distinct entities because they often exhibit biological, epidemiological, and clinical characteristics that differ from those observed in pediatric or older adult populations [11–14]. However, studies specifically focusing on OSCC in the AYA population remain limited.

Although AYA patients are often reported to present with stage I–II disease, most prognostic analyses of young-onset OSCC have included patients across all disease stages [15–19]. Consequently, prognostic determinants specific to early-stage OSCC in the AYA population remain unclear. Therefore, identifying robust and clinically applicable prognostic factors for early-stage OSCC in AYAs remains an unmet clinical need.

At the molecular level, OSCC is characterized by a broad spectrum of genetic alterations, with TP53, CDKN2A, FAT1, NOTCH1, and TERT among the most frequently affected genes [17, 20–22]. Among these, TP53 mutations are among the most common driver events in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, including OSCC. Aberrant p53 expression patterns on immunohistochemistry (IHC), which can serve as surrogate markers of TP53 alterations, have been associated with adverse clinical outcomes [23–27]. MTAP, which is located adjacent to CDKN2A on chromosome 9p21, is frequently co-deleted with CDKN2A in various malignancies, including oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma [28–31]. Accordingly, loss of MTAP expression on IHC has been used as a surrogate marker for CDKN2A/MTAP co-deletion and has also been reported to correlate with clinical outcomes [27, 29–31].

In young patients with OSCC, previous studies have reported TP53 and CDKN2A alterations [17, 32, 33]. However, particularly in early-stage disease, immunohistochemical surrogate biomarkers that reliably predict prognosis in this age group have not been established. Therefore, clinically applicable molecular prognostic markers for early-stage OSCC in AYAs remain to be clarified.

Histologically, conventional prognostic factors, including tumor differentiation, tumor thickness, and depth of invasion (DOI), have long been recognized as important predictors of outcome in OSCC [34, 35]. Particularly in early-stage disease, the worst pattern of invasion (WPOI) and tumor budding have been identified as independent prognostic indicators [36, 37]. More recently, modified WPOI criteria and tumor budding-based risk models have been proposed, and their utility for prognostic stratification has been increasingly reported [38, 39]. However, whether these histological risk factors are applicable to early-stage OSCC in AYAs remains insufficiently understood.

Therefore, this multicenter retrospective study was designed around two predefined objectives: (1) to characterize the clinicopathological, histopathological, and molecular features of early-stage OSCC in AYAs compared with those in older patients at the same clinical stage; and (2) to identify prognostic factors in AYA OSCC using histological parameters and immunohistochemical surrogate biomarkers that are applicable to routine diagnostic pathology practice.

Materials and Methods

Patients

According to the American Society of Clinical Oncology guidelines, the AYA group was defined as patients aged 15–39 years, whereas the older group was defined as patients aged ≥ 65 years. We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of 48 patients in the AYA group and 69 patients in the older group who underwent surgery for oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) between April 2000 and March 2020 at the participating hospitals. To investigate prognostic factors in early-stage disease, this study was limited to patients with clinical stage I–II OSCC according to the 8th edition of the Union for International Cancer Control/TNM classification [40].

Clinical information, including age at diagnosis, sex, tumor site, smoking and alcohol history, treatment modality, clinical stage, and follow-up status, was collected from the medical records. Particular attention was paid to systemic conditions and cancer predisposition-related backgrounds potentially associated with AYA-onset OSCC, including Fanconi anemia, dyskeratosis congenita, other inherited cancer predisposition syndromes, immunodeficiency, a history of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, and long-term immunosuppressive therapy. Macroscopic growth patterns were retrospectively classified based on clinical records into three categories: superficial spreading, endophytic, and exophytic types. Superficial spreading lesions were defined as leukoplakia-like lesions with predominant lateral mucosal extension. Endophytic lesions were defined as ulcerative lesions mainly centered in the submucosa, whereas exophytic lesions were defined as outwardly growing mass-forming lesions.

The inclusion criteria were the availability of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor samples and sufficient clinical data. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Okayama University, Okayama, Japan (No. 2306-037).

Histological Evaluation

Following Broders’ criteria, OSCCs were classified as well, moderately, or poorly differentiated [41]. The modified WPOI was assessed according to recently proposed criteria that simplify the original five-tier system into three categories [38]. In lesions classified as modified WPOI-1, cancer cell nests are confined within the main tumor boundary, whereas in those classified as modified WPOI-2 or WPOI-3, some nests extend beyond this boundary. The distinction between modified WPOI-2 and WPOI-3 is based on the distance between tumor satellites: < 1 mm for WPOI-2 and ≥ 1 mm for WPOI-3. Scores of 0, 1, and 2 points were assigned to WPOI-1, WPOI-2, and WPOI-3, respectively, when applying the histological risk model described below.

The tumor budding score, incorporating tumor budding density and cell dissociation, was defined as follows: 0, no single tumor cells and no tumor buds; 1, low-density tumor budding (< 5 buds); and 2, the presence of single tumor cells and/or high-density tumor budding (≥ 5 buds) per × 200 high-power field [38]. The histopathological risk model proposed by Chang et al. was defined by the total score of modified WPOI (0–2 points) and tumor budding score (0–2 points): low risk (0–1 point), intermediate risk (2–3 points), and high risk (4 points) [38]. In addition, according to the model modified by Almangush et al., cases were reclassified into two groups: low risk (0–2 points) and high risk (3–4 points) [39].

Immunohistochemistry and In Situ Hybridization

Serial 4-μm-thick sections cut from FFPE tumor tissues were used for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and IHC. IHC was performed using the BOND-III automated stainer (Leica Biosystems, Mount Waverley, VIC, Australia), and the following primary antibodies: p53 (Pab1801, 1:2000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA), p16 (E6H4, 1:2; Roche, Heidelberg, Germany), and MTAP (42-T, 1:100; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA).

IHC for p53 was evaluated according to the classification system described by Lin et al. [42]. Tumor specimens were categorized into wild-type (p53-conventional), wild-type (p53-HPV), and abnormal expression patterns. The wild-type pattern comprised four subtypes: (1) scattered basal (p53-conventional), defined as scattered p53-positive cells involving < 25% of the basal and/or parabasal layer; (2) patchy basal/parabasal (p53-conventional), characterized by variable weak to focally strong nuclear expression in 25–75% of the basal and/or parabasal layer; (3) mid-epithelial/basal sparing (p53-HPV), characterized by strong mid-epithelial staining with absent or markedly reduced staining in the basal layer; and (4) markedly reduced (null-like)/basal sparing (p53-HPV), characterized by basal sparing with significantly attenuated staining in > 70% of the mid-epithelial layer. The abnormal pattern also included four subtypes: (1) overexpression, basal/parabasal and diffuse, defined as strong and uniform nuclear staining in > 75% of atypical basal and parabasal cells with extension into more than three epithelial layers; (2) overexpression, basal/parabasal only, defined as strong and uniform nuclear p53 staining in > 75% of atypical basal and parabasal cells; (3) null pattern, defined as complete absence of p53 nuclear and cytoplasmic staining, with positive staining observed only in lymphocytes serving as internal controls; and (4) cytoplasmic pattern, defined as weak, moderate, or strong cytoplasmic staining in tumor cells, with or without nuclear staining, for which the presence of both positive and negative internal controls was required.

Positive p16 protein expression (i.e., overexpression) was defined as ≥ 70% of tumor cells showing strong and diffuse nuclear and cytoplasmic staining [43]. For p16-positive cases, in situ hybridization (ISH) was performed to detect HPV RNA using an E6/E7 mRNA probe set (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Newark, CA, USA) targeting 18 high-risk HPV types (16, 18, 26, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 53, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68, 73, and 82) with the RNAscope 2.5 HD reagent kit (Advanced Cell Diagnostics), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Any nuclear or cytoplasmic dot signal was considered positive [44].

Retention of MTAP expression was defined as cytoplasmic expression in tumor cells regardless of nuclear staining. Loss of MTAP expression was defined as the absence of cytoplasmic expression in tumor cells in the presence of positive internal controls, such as inflammatory cells or endothelial cells, as previously described [35].

Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization for CDKN2A and MTAP Deletion

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was performed in cases with MTAP loss. FISH was carried out on 4-μm-thick FFPE tissue sections using the p16/CEN9q Dual Color FISH Probe (Product No. GC002, GSP Lab, Kanagawa, Japan) to evaluate CDKN2A copy number and the MTAP/CEN9q Dual Color FISH Probe (Product No. GC206, GSP Lab) to assess MTAP copy number. Hybridization was performed at 37 °C for 24 h following 10 min of denaturation at 75 °C in a hybridizer (Dako, Glostrup, Denmark). Slides were examined using a BZ-X700 fluorescence microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan). Green and red signals corresponded to FITC-labeled CEN9q and Texas Red-labeled p16 or MTAP, respectively.

FISH signals were evaluated in at least 50 tumor cell nuclei. Cases were defined as having homozygous deletion of CDKN2A when green (CEN9q)-only signal patterns, indicating loss of both red (p16) signals, were observed in > 30% of cells. If this criterion was not met, but loss of one red (p16) signal was observed in > 30% of cells, the case was defined as having hemizygous deletion of CDKN2A. The same criteria were applied for the evaluation of MTAP deletion [35].

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, and continuous variables were compared using the t-test. Percentages were rounded and may not total 100% because of rounding. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause. Disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to the first documented disease progression or relapse. Disease-specific survival (DSS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to death caused by OSCC. Patients who died from causes unrelated to cancer were censored at the time of death for DFS and DSS analyses. The follow-up period was calculated from the date of initial treatment to the date of last clinical follow-up or death. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. In the AYA group, logistic regression analyses were performed to assess the associations of clinicopathological variables with late lymph node metastasis and distant metastasis. Univariable and multivariable models were used to estimate odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1).

Results

Clinical Findings

The clinical findings of the AYA and older patients with OSCC are summarized in Table 1. The AYA group predominantly comprised patients in their 30 s (75.0%), whereas the older group mainly consisted of patients in their 70 s (60.9%). No significant difference in sex distribution was observed between the two groups (p = 0.34). A history of smoking was significantly less frequent in the AYA group than in the older group (33.3% vs 63.8%, p < 0.01). In contrast, no significant difference was observed in the history of alcohol consumption between the two groups (39.5% vs 55.1%, p = 0.20). Among the AYA patients, one had a history of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation for acute myeloid leukemia, and another had systemic lupus erythematosus with antiphospholipid syndrome. Tumor site distribution differed significantly between the two groups (p < 0.01), with tongue tumors being more common in the AYA group (93.7% vs 47.8%), whereas buccal mucosal and gingival tumors were observed only in the older group. Macroscopic growth patterns also differed significantly between the groups (p < 0.01), with superficial spreading lesions predominating in AYA patients and exophytic and endophytic lesions being more frequent in older patients. No multiple lesions were observed in AYA patients. T1 or stage I disease (8th UICC/TNM classification) tended to be more frequent in the AYA group than in the older group (68.7% vs 52.2%), although this difference was not significant (p = 0.08). Treatment modalities differed significantly between the groups (p < 0.01), with the vast majority (97.9%) of with the vast majority (97.9%) of AYA patients undergoing surgery alone without neck dissection, and most (75.4%) older patients receiving surgery with neck dissection. Rates of postoperative local recurrence, delayed lymph node metastasis, and distant metastasis did not differ significantly between the groups; however, the rate of delayed postoperative lymph node metastasis tended to be higher in the AYA group than in the older group (31.2% vs 20.3%). At the point of final follow-up, survival status differed significantly between the groups (p = 0.01): the AYA group had a higher proportion of patients alive without OSCC (89.6% vs 72.5%) and no deaths from other diseases (0% vs 14.5%) than the older group.

Immunoexpression Patterns of p53

Immunohistochemical findings are summarized in Table 3. Three patterns of abnormal p53 expression were observed: (1) overexpression, basal/parabasal and diffuse; (2) overexpression, basal/parabasal only; and (3) null pattern (Fig. 3A, left). No cytoplasmic p53 staining pattern was observed in either group. Discussion

With respect to the first objective, the stage-matched comparison revealed a distinct clinicopathological profile of AYA OSCC. Clinically, AYA tumors were predominantly located on the tongue and more frequently exhibited a superficial spreading growth pattern, which was defined in this study as leukoplakia-like lesions with predominant lateral mucosal extension. This clinical presentation is noteworthy because such lesions may not be immediately suspected to represent invasive carcinoma, particularly in young patients. Therefore, increased awareness of this superficial spreading pattern among dentists and clinicians may facilitate earlier recognition and appropriate biopsy of OSCC in the AYA population.

Leukoplakia-like mucosal changes may also occur in the context of field cancerization, which is characterized by genetically altered mucosa surrounding or distant from the primary tumor [42, 43]. Molecular field changes in OSCC have been associated with TP53 mutations and loss at 9p21, including the CDKN2A locus [44, 45]. In the present study, abnormal p53 expression was significantly more frequent in AYA tumors than in older tumors, suggesting the possible involvement of TP53-related alterations. In contrast, MTAP loss and homozygous deletions of CDKN2A and MTAP were infrequent. Although none of the AYA patients had multiple lesions and clinically evident field cancerization was therefore not strongly supported, the presence of subclinical molecular field changes cannot be excluded, because adjacent non-neoplastic mucosa was not subjected to molecular analysis.

Changes in sexual behavior have been suggested to increase oral HPV infection and contribute to early-onset head and neck cancers [46]. However, high-risk HPV was not detected in any AYA tumors in the present study. Although some AYA patients had been exposed to conventional risk factors, such as tobacco smoking and alcohol consumption, patients without such exposures were also observed. In addition, most AYA patients had no documented history of known inherited cancer predisposition syndromes. OSCC has been reported in young female patients without conventional cancer-related risk factors, suggesting the existence of a distinct subgroup of young patients who develop OSCC in the absence of established etiological factors [47]. Taken together, these findings suggest that mechanisms other than HPV infection and conventional environmental exposures may contribute to OSCC development in a subset of AYA patients. Further studies are needed to clarify the possible involvement of unrecognized genetic susceptibility or as-yet unidentified etiological factors.

Regarding the second objective, the most important finding of the present study was that DOI emerged as the most robust prognostic indicator in early-stage AYA OSCC. Recent studies have suggested that DOI and tumor thickness may have comparable predictive performance, raising the possibility that tumor thickness could serve as a surrogate for DOI, because histological assessment of tumor thickness is usually easier than that of DOI [48]. In contrast, tumor thickness was not significantly associated with prognosis in the present AYA group. Similarly, other pathological parameters, including modified WPOI, tumor budding score, histopathological risk models, and p53 and MTAP expression patterns, did not show clear prognostic associations. OSCC in young patients has been reported to show more aggressive histopathological features, including higher tumor budding [49]. Consistent with this, AYA tumors in the present study showed higher tumor budding scores and were more frequently classified into the high-risk category according to the Almangush risk model than tumors in older patients. Although the high-risk group in this model has been reported to be significantly associated with poor survival [39], the AYA group in the present study showed significantly better OS and DFS than the older group. These findings suggest that conventional histopathological risk models may not fully capture prognosis in early-stage AYA OSCC. Taken together, DOI may provide prognostic information beyond tumor thickness and other histopathological factors in AYA patients with early-stage OSCC and should therefore be prioritized as a histopathological indicator for risk assessment.

Lymph node status is one of the independent prognostic factors in OSCC, and younger patients have been reported to have a higher prevalence of regional metastasis, which may contribute to poorer prognosis [50, 51]. In the present study, univariable analysis showed that clinical stage II, tumor thickness > 5 mm, DOI > 5 mm, and tumor budding scores of 1–2 were associated with late lymph node metastasis in the AYA group. These findings suggest that AYA patients with these risk factors may require closer postoperative surveillance and individualized consideration of additional neck treatment or adjuvant therapy.

This study has several limitations. First, because the number of adverse events, including distant metastasis, was limited in the AYA group, robust multivariable analysis could not be performed, and the results of the univariable analyses should therefore be interpreted with caution. Second, molecular analyses were limited to immunohistochemistry, HPV RNA in situ hybridization, and selected FISH assays; comprehensive genomic profiling was not performed.

In conclusion, early-stage OSCCs in AYAs exhibited distinct clinicopathological and molecular surrogate phenotypes compared with those in older patients. DOI emerged as the most robust prognostic indicator in the AYA group, suggesting that DOI-prioritized, AYA-specific prognostic models may be useful for clinical management. Although most AYA patients in the present study were treated with surgery alone, our findings suggest that postoperative management, including adjuvant treatment to reduce the risk of late lymph node recurrence, may be warranted in selected patients. Clinicopathological risk factors, including clinical stage, tumor thickness, DOI, and tumor budding score, may help guide such decision-making.
