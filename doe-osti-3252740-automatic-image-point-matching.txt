 ABSTRACT
Sparse Image Point Matching (SIPM) is a foundational technology for photo triangulation, structure from
motion (SfM), Simultaneous Location and Mapping (SLAM), and data fusion. The goal of the matching
is to automatically generate sets of image coordinates that identify the same feature across images. Ideally,
the process should be robust to lighting, scale, perspective, and modality changes. The scope of the image-
matching topic in the field of remote sensing (RS) is enormous because of the variety of collection platforms,
modalities, sensor types, applications, and subjects. In this work, we report the history of and assess the state
of the art of visible-spectrum (panchromatic and color) image matching of the Earth’s surface. Work specific
to large-format images (LFI) (e.g., metric aerial cameras and Earth-observing satellites) will be highlighted.
However, the state of the art in this century will mostly be traced through machine vision research and
benchmarks because research specific to LFI is rare.

 1. INTRODUCTION
SIPM is a foundational technology of photo triangulation, structure from motion (SfM), Simultaneous
Location and Mapping (SLAM), and data fusion. Its goal is to produce sets of coordinates that describe the
same feature in multiple images. For best results, the sparse matches need to be accurate, well-distributed
across an image’s two-dimensional (2D) domain, and comprehensive through the stack of images that observe
each feature. To accomplish this, the process should be robust to lighting, scale, perspective, and modality
changes. The scope of the image-matching topic in remote sensing (RS) is enormous because of the variety of
collection platforms, modalities, sensor types, and subjects: image collection platforms include submersibles,
unmanned (uncrewed) aircraft systems (UAS), manned aircraft, and satellites; sensor modalities include
panchromatic, synthetic-aperture radar (SAR), red, green, and blue (RGB), and multi- and hyperspectral;
sensors include frame, panoramic, spherical, push-frame, and line-scanners; and subject matter includes
all the variety on the Earth’s surface, as well as caverns, seabeds, human-made environments, and other
worlds. The principal interest of this work is to assess the state of the art of SIPM of RS images of the Earth’s
surface. However, most of the SIPM research in the past three decades was performed by the machine vision
community on terrestrial imagery. To incorporate that research in this review, we’ll include all SIPM work
in passive, visible-spectrum imaging. Within that scope, there are hundreds of publications and dozens of
noteworthy SIPM algorithms spanning seven decades of research. To ease our discussion of these algorithms,
we begin by introducing the metrics used to compare the algorithms and the three contexts we used to classify
them: historical, pipeline design, and, in the case of deep learning-based matching (DLBM) methods, training
data.

1.1 PERFORMANCE COMPARISONS
There are many ways that authors have assessed and compared the performance of matching algorithms.
Perhaps the most commonly used and least frequently named scale is age. To publish, authors need to
demonstrate in some credible way that they are advancing the state of the art. Hence, they often choose to
compare their work to some earlier algorithm’s performance, which creates a natural bias that assumes newer
is better. We’ll grant that it makes no sense to compare, for example, current work to historical grayscale
matching; however, modern comparisons become less certain among feature matching (FM) and DLBM.
The sheer volume of possible algorithms for comparison makes sampling inevitable. Biases such as ageism
or cherry-picking are hard to avoid in this situation. Thus, it is fair to argue that an author choosing to
compare their methodology to a previous algorithm is something of an endorsement that lends credibility to
the older algorithm. It is equally fair to say that failure to include some credible contenders may indicate
cherry-picking.

Most of the early deep learning (DL) work in SIPM targeted single parts of the pipeline (predominantly,
the descriptor) [78]. Later contributors would report that automation in one part didn’t necessarily lead to
overall improvements [61, 77, 42]. This created a hierarchy among DLBM methods based on which parts(s)
of the pipeline they replaced with deep learning. At times, this hierarchy served as an imperfect surrogate for
performance in choosing algorithms for comparison.
Matching score (also known as precision) is the most common quantitative measure of performance (see [76,
77, 68, 50, 15, 55, 42, 58, 75, 57]). Matching score is the ratio of true positives (TP) to the sum of TP and

Reporting the number of matches or root-mean-square error (RMSE) in pixels was rare (see [75, 65]).
Repeatability is a frequently reported metric that is specific to the detection pipeline stage (see [77, 13, 55,
42]). It refers to a keypoint being repeatably detected regardless of the lighting, perspective, etc. It is typically
expressed as a percentage.
The most comprehensive metrics are based on successful recovery of image poses or homographies (see [13,
15, 58, 58, 65, 57]). Matching score and precision work together to define the matcher’s performance at
a per-point level, but they don’t tell us how useful the matches were. A pair of images with perfect recall
and precision will fail on pose or homography recovery if all of the point matches are badly clustered, too
few in number, or too imprecise. In contrast, the recovery metrics are the direct assessment of the utility
of a given algorithm’s provided matches. Unfortunately, here too, there is a threshold issue with binary
grouping into successful and unsuccessful recoveries that can allow for cherry-picking. Despite this nuance,
we maintain that recovery measurements are the most comprehensive metric and will defer to them when
they are available.

1.2 HISTORICAL CONTEXT
[57] depicted the history of sparse image matching as beginning with matching patches of grayscale values,
transitioning to feature matching (FM), and transitioning again to DLBM. In terms of research interest and
publications, this is a reasonable framing. The implied FM paradigm shift (kicked off by SIFT) was real. In
terms of market implementation, academic acceptance, and emulation, SIFT is unequaled. It is one of the key
technologies that drove automation of SfM and SLAM. However, this historical framing lacks nuance. We’ll
show that each technology remains relevant within its niche and that the paradigm shift away from FM is
nascent.

1.3 PIPELINES
Modeling and discussing SIPM as a processing pipeline is one of SIFT’s most enduring impacts. SIFT’s
original pipeline is shown in Figure 1. It consisted of detection of sparse features of interest, creating a digital
description of each feature (a process that terminates in the describe step but actually starts at detection),
matching of the feature’s digital descriptions to those from another image, and winnowing of the proposed
matches ([40] doesn’t name winnowing as a pipeline step, but does spend most of the matching step discussion
on that topic). All subsequent SIPM research can be framed as proposed improvements to steps within the
SIFT pipeline or variations of it. For ease in comparison and conceptualization, each algorithm’s pipeline
design will be described and the those the depart from SIFT’s will be depicted graphically. Even grayscale
matching, which predates SIFT by decades, will be framed as a primitive, loosely connected pipeline.

1.4 TRAINING DATA
The success of DLBM techniques depends on the quantity and quality of the training data. With few excep-
tions, researchers mined various publicly available benchmark data sets. Training data can be classified by
domains (e.g., indoors, outdoors, or aerial) and then further broken down by subject matter (e.g., architectural,
landscape, etc.). However, we contend the most fundamental categorization is between single-perspective
training (SPT) and multi-perspective training (MPT).
SPT matches images to warped versions of themselves. SPT includes no camera motion and the only
temporal variations are simulated lighting changes and added noise. However, SPT has the advantage of
easy unsupervised training data creation. The training data can be created automatically from any image
by warping the input image using generated homographies to create stacks of images with exact known
relationships. The resulting stack of warped images has infinite known matches through it, and the utility
of sets of point matches can be tested by attempting to recover the known homographies. The solution can
also be tailored to work for specific ranges of rotation, scaling, and perspective by controlling the range of
homographies used to warp the training data. Such solution tailoring is advantageous if the problem scope is
known to be limited [50, 32, 24].
MPT’s advantage is that it includes training data from multiple observation points and may include temporal
variations. It is limited by the available data and imperfect truth. The data availability limitation is in contrast
to SPT that can be generated from any single image with no other support data required. MPT data need

 3

to have known camera models, camera poses, and depth maps. One work used curated sparse matches for
training, but extended to SfM dense matching to avoid limiting the deep learning to only training on points
that previous algorithms handled well [76]. There was a limited use of laser scanning to establish the depth
maps [50], but most relied on dense image matching algorithms in SfM pipelines [77, 50, 15, 55, 42, 58].
MPT’s use of SfM pipelines implies a fundamental limitation: the DL cannot learn to process any images
that previous matching technologies failed to process. This is mitigated, to an unknown degree, by the use of
dense matching to create possible keypoints not included in the SfM pipeline’s sparse matching.
MPT has imperfect truth data because the camera poses are computed quantities with uncertainty, dense
matching is notoriously noisy, and there are temporal variations (e.g., tourists in the image foreground). To
help deal with the dense matching noise, [15] and [42] added additional filtering, and [58] used an active
sensor to stabilize the indoor dense matching. While these efforts are helpful, MPT still suffers from unknown
levels of noise in the associated camera poses and dense matching.
SPT and MPT can both take advantage of standard data augmentation techniques (e.g., adding noise, motion
blur, and brightness adjustments). MPT could also warp image pairs (the same way SPT warps individual
images) to augment their training data, but this was never mentioned.

 2. GRAYSCALE MATCHING
Grayscale matching is done by comparing small patches of pixels to one another. Normalized cross correlation
(NCC) [5] is still a widely used grayscale matching algorithm [71, 78]. The method works by passing a
small pattern image window from a source image over a larger image search window in another image in the
same way that image convolution is done. NCC (the similarity metric) is computed at each location in the
search window. NCC is a measure of how well pixel values in the pattern predict corresponding pixel values
at each location of the search window. The pixel in the search window with the best NCC is the ostensible
match. However, it may be rejected if the value is below a threshold or there are multiple similar NCC peaks.
Subpixel accuracy can be achieved by modeling the local NCC image to find a maximum.
NCC relies on pixel-to-pixel alignment between pattern and search windows, and hence is not robust to
rotation, scale, or non-linear lighting variations. Despite these limitations, NCC has a niche in applications
where data are collected to approximate pixel level alignment or when images can be resampled to approximate
it. The resampling can be done with a priori approximate sensor pose parameters and approximate ranges.
High-altitude photogrammetric mapping and near-nadir Earth-observing satellites are common examples in
the niche where NCC can serve as a fast subpixel matcher. However, the success will depend on the quality
of the support data and the consistency of the lighting.
Least-squares matching (LSM) is a variety of grayscale matching with a different niche, precision. LSM
is cited as having an accuracy between 0.01-0.02 pixels [45]. In practice, we have found that a 2D RMSE
less than 0.1 pixels is expected in photo triangulation adjustments (i.e., bundle adjustments) of nadir images
from metric cameras using LSM. This precision is achieved by iteratively solving to align and match the
pixel values between conjugate image patches. At a minimum, the parameters include one of a variety of
pixel space transformations and illumination corrections [45], but can be extended with multi-view geometric
constraints [22], and even object space modeling [26, 16].
The classic LSM implementation uses a 6-parameter 2D affine transformation to model the spatial deformation
between image patches. An 8-parameter 2D-projective transformation would model the transformation of a
planar subject observed by a frame camera without loss of generality. Hence, the assumption in the model is
that that subject is planar and that the sensors can be approximated as affine cameras over the small local area
used in the LSM (e.g., between 7 and 51 pixels). In practice, this is a reasonable model for many points in
photogrammetry and satellite remote sensing. Helpfully, LSM (like all least-squares operations) includes
estimates of the solution accuracy which can be used to winnow bad matches and in subsequent adjustments
[45].
The primary disadvantages of LSM are speed and the assumption of planar local ground areas (i.e, lack
of generality). LSM is iterative and comparatively slow. Hence, it is not used to find matching points in
a convolution-type search like NCC. Rather, it is relegated to subpixel refinement of previously identified
matches. The planar subject assumption breaks down on range edges which are common in aerial scenes of
human environments are in close range scenes of natural environments. Furthermore, quick convergence
requires good initial values which, like NCC, will depend on images being collected with approximate pixel
level alignment or good a priori support data. LSM continues to be relevant today and, in particular, remains
useful to applications that demand high precision [37].

 3. FEATURE MATCHING
Feature matching abandons matching patches of pixels directly to one another. Instead, numerical descriptions
of the pixel patches are computed and matching is done by comparing the descriptions. This is analogous to
identifying a person based on a bio metric measurements instead of photographs. SIFT was the first widely
successful feature matcher [68]. It was the technology that caused the paradigm shift away from grayscale
matching [75]. It is robust to lighting and perspective shifts [41] and thus works in such a wider variety of
applications that comparison to grayscale matchers (e.g., NCC) is nonsense. The algorithmic development
involved several empirical choices informed by a set of 32 images [41]. Despite that small sample size, SIFT
endures as a respected, versatile, and competitive algorithm decades later. As mentioned, one of SIFT’s
enduring contributions was framing sparse image matching as a processing pipeline (see Figure 1). To better
establish the pipeline context, each of SIFT’s original pipeline steps is briefly explained below. A review of
published modifications and alternatives follows.

3.1 SIFT PIPELINE
3.2 DETECTION
The purpose of the detection phase is to identify the sparse set of image points that will be used for
matching. The original SIFT detected keypoints as local extrema in difference of Gaussians (DoG). In this
context, Gaussians are blurring convolutions that reduce the image’s effective resolution. The original SIFT
recommended that each Gaussian reduce the effective resolution by a factor of one-third. Like a pyramid,
each Gaussian washes out more of the higher-frequency signals but, unlike a pyramid, does not change the
resolution. Thus, local maxima in differences between consecutive Gaussians are pixels where the step from
one sharpness (Gaussian) to the next had the most change. This can be thought of as features coming in and
out of focus at certain effective resolutions.
An early extension to keypoint detection by the original author was to model the local area of the DoG around
the detected keypoint as a quadratic polynomial surface. Within the polynomial model, the local keypoint
could be estimated to a subpixel location as the surface extrema. The surface principal curvatures could also
be analyzed to reject points that were weak extrema (and hence sensitive to noise) and points along linear
edges (that are thus indistinct) [41].
The Gaussian where the feature was detected implies an image scale [40]. Keypoint orientation and description
both depend on this scale (i.e., they are computed within the Gaussian where it was detected). This dependency
is what gives SIFT its scale-invariance [40].

To define the keypoint orientation, the gradients within a window around each keypoint (e.g., 16 x 16 pixels)

multiple descriptions. [41] reported that 15% of points had multiple descriptions.

3.2.2 Description
The keypoint coordinates and scale (from the detection phase), together with the orientation(s), define the
SIFT transform(s). The description(s) are crafted from pixel gradients in the transformed image chip(s)
around the keypoint. The inspiration to use gradients came from work relating mammalian biological vision
and object recognition [41]. There are complex neurons in the mammalian primary vision cortex that respond
to gradient patterns but are insensitive to local shifts [17]. Gradients are also invariant to shifts in brightness
(because they are differences) and, when normalized, become insensitive to scaling [40].
The descriptor algorithm divides the transformed 16 x 16 pixel window around the keypoint into 16 subsections
each measuring 4 x 4 pixels, as shown in Figure 2 by the thick borders. An 8-bin gradient direction histogram
is computed for each subsection. As with the orientation, the directions are weighted by the magnitudes. The
combined 16 x 8 = 128 values (shown as dashed lines on the right half of Figure 2) become the description
of the point. To achieve the biologically inspired insensitivity to local shifts, each sub-region’s histogram
includes gradients from adjacent subsections added with reduced weight [41]. The weight scheme for the dark
subsection in Figure 2 is illustrated by the fading color around it, with lighter tints indicating lower weights.
The resulting 128-value description is scaled to unit length for illumination invariance. Finally, based on
empirical experience, [41] truncated dominant description components to 0.2. The proposed explanation is
that this helps compensate for dramatic lighting changes (e.g., specular reflections and image saturation) by
emphasizing the orientation distribution over a minority of dominant gradient(s).

3.2.3 Matching
The metric for keypoint similarity was defined as the L2 norm of the vector between them, ||di − d j ||2 , where
di is the description of a keypoint in image i, and d j is the description of a keypoint in image j. This was
described as a Euclidean distance, which is an odd expression to apply because the 128 descriptive values are
not coordinates, but it gets the point across. The ostensible match was simply the point the shortest ‘distance’
away [41].

3.2.4 Winnowing
The original author does not expressly call out winnowing as part of the pipeline. However, most of the
matching section was devoted to explaining that the ratio between the similarity of the nearest neighbor
match and the second-nearest neighbor match was an effective means of parsing the good and bad matches.
Specifically, [41] recommended disregarding all matches with a ratio less than 0.8.

SIFT alternatives and modifications roughly map to either a focus on faster matching or better matching. We
will cover each category in turn. The work focused on better matching will be further broken down by the
pipeline stage it addressed.

The spatial weighting of gradients for the histogram principally related to the 4 x 4 section in the third row of
the second column is depicted as orange shading that falls off outside the subsection.

3.3.1 Faster Alternatives
Among the faster matching algorithms, Speeded-Up Robust Features (SURF) may be the best known. It
is conceptually similar to SIFT, in that it is based on measures of image brightness change. However, it
computed these changes using Haar wavelet filters instead of gradients. It also replaced the DoG keypoint
detection with box filters that focused on blob-like features and used reduced-dimension principal component
descriptions [6]. The authors report significant speed and performance improvements compared to SIFT.
However, that claim has not been reliably duplicated [77, 50, 24], and SURF was ignored by several
comparison studies [61, 19, 51, 29].
Among the faster alternatives is series of matching algorithms that used binary descriptors to optimize the
matching time and the memory use. BRIEF [9], BRISK [34], ORB [56], and FREAK [1] are among the most
well-known. A comparison found that SIFT outperformed the binary options but that ORB and BRISK often
sufficed and should be considered for performance gains [24].

3.3.2 Better Performance
The keypoint detection step was identified to be a critical weak point in the pipeline. Repeatability of
detection suffers when lighting changes [79], when the seasons change [59], and in low-texture scenes [67].
An inefficient but effective work-around has been to saturate the image with ‘key-points’ [59, 67, 69, 79, 11,
20, 60]. In the context of remote sensing applications, [39] and [63] proposed less extreme modifications to
get more uniform key-point distributions. SURF’s box filters are also an alternative detection scheme that
worked better in some circumstances [6].
There have been many experiments with different descriptions, including a couple that are RS-specific. [46]
proposed descriptions with more gradient location information. [44] proposed descriptions that were linear
combinations of the standard SIFT descriptors at various scales. [14] proposed domain-size pooling (DSP),
which pools descriptors from different chip sizes. [47], [49], and [30] created descriptors with better affine

 8

invariance. In a remote sensing context, [62] histogram-normalized images prior to using affine invariant
descriptions, and [44] introduced a specialized gradient definition that compensated for non-linear intensity
differences.
The matching step is a function of the similarity metric. The nominal SIFT similarity metric is the L2
norm of vector difference of descriptors. [2] noted that because the descriptions are not coordinates, but
rather histograms, Hellinger distances were a more meaningful similarity metric for comparing them. Their
algorithm has become known as RootSIFT (in which “root” refers to the square root in the Hellinger kernel).
[31] built local geometric consistency in their similarity metric. They accomplished this by grouping points
together based on a Delaunay triangulation and penalizing matching solutions based on how they deformed
the triangles.
The winnowing remains an active area of research. The original author proposed filtering based on the ratio of
the best similarity metric to the second best [41]. That seams to have quickly been replaced or supplemented
by different filtering methods. The most popular of these methods is RAndom SAmple Consensus (RANSAC).
RANSAC proposed to use random subsets of the data to generate solution hypotheses, score each hypothesis
based on how well the whole of the data agreed with it, and repeat until a prescribed level of certainty is
reached that the best solution was found [21]. It has been extensively expanded and modified to increase
robustness and improve the speed [54]. An extension particularly relevant to SIPM is one-to-many RANSAC,
which addressed the issue of repeating texture and structures [70]. [18] proposed a global method that worked
without random sampling; their method worked as effectively as RANSAC in half the time and demonstrated
it in a remote sensing context. [43] compared 9 winnowing algorithms, including the original 1981 RANSAC.
They reported that the original RANSAC had the second best performance with an average F1 = 0.947. The
only better performer was a more modern version of RANSAC with local optimization (average F1 = 0.961
[33]. The best in terms of performance and processing speed was locality preserving matching (LPM), which
solved the robust estimation problem for local neighborhoods in closed form. Their average F1 = 0.939 was
competitive. Their processing time was 18 times faster than the locally optimized RANSAC and 26 times
faster than the original RANSAC. The fastest method tested was Grid-based Motion Statistics (GMS) [8]. It
was 8 times faster than even LPM, but their average F1 = 0.85 was not as competitive.
Efficient RS applications of feature matching require processing images in tiles and parallelization. There are
several reported methods of accomplishing this. [28] applied SIFT in a coarse-to-fine strategy. The coarse
results were used to narrow search regions and allow tiled parallel processing at finer detail. [66] proposed
L2 -SIFT, which used prior knowledge of camera models, camera pose, and ranges to tile directly at fine detail.
[35] and [10] experimented with assuming that sensors could be modeled as affine cameras within the tiles in
order to accelerate performance. It has also been proposed to geometrically correct images (e.g., perform
rough orthographic resampling) prior to matching [27]. In addition to easy tiling and parallelization, this
allows the use of matching algorithms without rotation and scale invariance (e.g., BRIEF [24]). Specialized,
less general matchers, are likely to be faster and more successful [50, 32, 24]. This approach requires
sufficient prior knowledge to do the geometric correction, the correction may be computationally expensive,
and it wouldn’t work well with large changes in range. However, within the niche of satellite images, it is
worth testing.

3.4 FEATURE MATCHING STATE OF THE ART
It is easier to define the top performing algorithms within a limited scope. Our primary interest is Sparse
Image Point Matching (SIPM) satellite images. The defining characteristics of satellite images are the
high altitude, a narrow field of view, and enormous size. The advantages this implies are fairly consistent
ranges from sensor to target, sensor pose estimates, and known a priori sensor models. Those are significant
advantages that allow for direct fine resolution tiling as proposed by [66], and prior geometric correction as

 9

proposed by [27]. Processing satellite images implies the need for robustness to seasonal changes as well as
non-cyclical changes like shifts in land use. Repeating textures and structures in human-made and industrial
environments are also a concern.
Few of the SIFT variants have been subjected to independent verification in any context and none in the
scope of satellite remote sensing images. One comparison paper included a performance test on satellite
data that included SIFT, ORB, and a number of DLBM methods. The authors concluded by recommending
the SIFT2004 for remote sensing applications [29]. That SIFT2004 was still competitive is interesting, but
the study was designed to compare it to DLBM and not to any of the SIFT variants. Another comparison
on close range dense reconstruction also recommends SIFT2004 among feature matches. However, it too
included no SIFT variants [19]. The winnowing research is likely domain-agnostic as it is downstream of the
image dependent detection and description. Hence, LPM is the leading winnowing candidate [43]. However,
LPM may require customization to support satellite sensor models and one-to-many matches. Assuming
the conclusions from tests on architectural image benchmarks are applicable, RootSIFT is likely the best
similarity metric for histogram-based descriptors [2, 61, 15], and DSP-SIFT may be the best descriptor [61].

 4. DEEP LEARNING-BASED MATCHING
Feature matchers use handcrafted operations to detect, describe, match, and winnow Sparse Image Point
Matching (SIPM). The deep learning variants replace some or all of those handcrafted operations. The
premise is that the “manually designed rules can not be thorough” [76], which we take to mean that they
cannot be informed by as much input (training data) or as complex as deep learning-based matching (DLBM).
The deep learning-based matching methods will be presented in chronological order, including a sampling of
the earliest efforts. Presenting them chronologically allows us to see how they have fed off of each other’s
ideas and compared themselves to past work. For each algorithm, we will discuss:
 • The part(s) of the pipeline it replaced with deep learning.
 • The types of training data and how the data were used.
 • The design of the cost functions.
 • How performance was tested and what algorithms it competed with.
Most of the early DL efforts targeted the descriptor [78], and creating DL designs that spanned more of the
pipeline were noteworthy accomplishments. Cost functions are mathematical expressions of the ideals that
deep learning attempts to optimize. For example, the ideal that matching image chips (positive matches)
should have similar descriptions and non-matching (negative) chips should have dissimilar descriptions is
fundamental to any DL that includes keypoint descriptions. Training data are fundamental to deep learning
and will be discussed in the context established in the introduction. Various performance metrics were also
covered in the introduction. The URL of each DLBM approach is given in Table 1.

4.1 MATCHNET, 2015
MatchNet [23] is one the first DLBM methods to span two pipeline steps, specifically description and
matching. The authors used the University of British Columbia Patch Dataset [73] for their training data. The
patches (image chips) in the benchmark data are centered on real interest points matched from SfM dense
stereo-matching pipelines. Hence, the data are MPT (includes training matches from different view points,
but subject to noise). The source images are tourist photos of Yosemite National Park, the Statue of Liberty,
and the Cathédrale Notre-Dame de Paris. The data set consists of about 1.5 million image patches. Their cost
function was based on the ideal that positive chips should have similar descriptions and negative chips should
have dissimilar descriptions. They included 16 negative matches with each positive match. The negative
match selection included confounding samples to build in uniqueness. The authors reported that the negative
match rate (percent of FN plus the percent of FP) was reduced from 28% to 7% compared to SIFT.

 11

4.2 LIFT, 2016
LIFT [77] was the first DLBM methodology to claim to span the detection, orientation, and description
pipeline stages. However, the combined network didn’t converge well, so they actually trained the three
stages separately. The cost for the description training was the standard ideal. The orientation was then
trained to maximize similarity of descriptions of the same point. The detector was trained to shift keypoint
to more distinct nearby locations. Their training data came from two MPT benchmarks: Piccadilly Circus
and the Roman Forum [74]. Both are architectural crowd-sourced photo tourism images. In both cases, the
truth data come from SfM pipelines. The performance metric reported was the MMA. The performance was
compared to two floating point feature matches (SIFT and SURF), two binary feature matchers (ORB and
FREAK), and the DLBM MatchNet. They reported a 15% improvement compared to their closest competitor,
SIFT. MatchNet and FREAK were worse than SIFT by about a factor of 4. The MatchNet comparison shows
a lack of DLBM generality.

4.3 SUPERPOINT, 2017
SuperPoint [13] managed to span detection, orientation, and description, and it optimized them simultaneously.
SuperPoint stands out as having the most unique training data among the DLBM methods. They began training
on synthetic images of geometric primitives they termed quads, tris, cubes, stars, lines, and checkerboards. In
essence, they trained their detector to only find endpoints and intersections of image line segments. They
further trained it to repeatably detect and describe those features in the presence of noise and lighting changes.
As the images were synthetic they could generate them at will and know exactly which points should be
detected (i.e. they had perfect truth). When the network trained exclusively on synthetic data didn’t perform
well on real data, the training data were extended to include the Common Objects in COntext (COCO) images
[38]. COCO images are SPT. They were intended to be used to train object detection, image classification,
and image segmentation. They created training data by homographically warping individual images and used
the successful recovery of the homographies as their performance metric. With this approach they are able to
tailor the amount of rotation and scale invariance they support. They do not quantify how much rotation and
scaling they included in the training, but the example pictures imply it was non-trivial. They reported that
SIFT successfully recovered 5% more homographies than SuperPoint, but SuperPoint recovered 13% more
than LIFT and 78% more than ORB. Note that the self-reported performance improvements of an earlier
DLBM method (LIFT) were contradicted in this independent test.

4.4 LF-NET, 2018
LF-Net [50] also spanned detection, orientation, and description. They used two MPT benchmarks. The
first, ScanNet [12], is an indoor data set of an office environment. It avoided the issues with SfM dense
matching noise by using laser scanners for truth data. The second, YFCC100M [25], is a photo tourism data
set that used off-the-shelf SfM pipelines for truth data. They trained the network to pick points that matched
correctly through feedback from the rest of the pipeline. They also included known orientations and scales of
keypoints derived from image poses and ranges to train the detection and orientation pipeline stages. The
performance metric they reported was the MMA with a 5-pixel error threshold. In order to perform similarly
to SuperPoint, they trained a network without scale and rotation invariance. Without that specialization,
SuperPoint outperformed them by 16%. They reported a factor of 2 improvement in MMA compared to
LIFT, SIFT, and SURF, and factor of 5 compared to ORB. The choice of a large accuracy threshold (5-pixel)
may explain some of the massive improvements (which are outliers among all cited work) compared to
feature matchers. Note that SuperPoint’s excellent performance is contrary to the typical pattern of DLBM
not performing well outside its original context.

 12

4.5 D2-NET, 2019
D2-Net [15] was inspired by work that identified detection as a critical weak point in the SIPM pipeline [79,
59, 67] and that saturating an image with keypoints was a functional work-around [59, 67, 69, 79, 11, 20,
60]. The resulting modified pipeline began with simultaneous description and detection at all pixels (see
Figure 3). The descriptions consist of a collection of detectors that each create a 2D response map. This was
accomplished as a single forward pass through a convolution neural network. Keypoints were detected as
locally distinct features using a soft detection that scored them based on how dominant they were within their
individual detection channel and across all the channels. Description/detection was optionally repeated across
three pyramids and aggregated. The aggregation across pyramids aimed to widen the location’s context,
which sacrifices precision, but aims to handle more difficult images [15].
D2-Net could be described as a hybrid between a handcrafted feature matcher and DLBM because it
functioned well untrained. However, they produced a trained version. The ideal they optimized was that
the describers/detectors should produce similar results for matching points even when there were strong
appearance changes. They used MPT benchmark data set MegaDepth [36] for the training. The benchmark
images were photo tourism of mostly architectural scenes. The truth data were from SfM pipelines, but they
used additional filtering to reduce noise [36, 15].
[15] provided an abundance of performance data. In the first test, they reported MMA as a function of the
pixel threshold from 1 to 10 pixels. The benchmark for this test was HPatches [3], a challenging SPT data set
[42] with a variety of indoor and outdoor images. They tested four versions of their own algorithm (untrained
single-scale, untrained multi-scale, trained single-scale, and trained multi-scale) against RootSIFT, LF-Net,
and SuperPoint. SuperPoint was the best through most of threshold range, but trained versions of D2-Net
outperformed all competitors above the 6.5 pixel threshold. RootSIFT was competitive particularly with pixel
thresholds below 3. LF-Net trailed behind RootSIFT through the whole range. The difference between the
trained and untrained D2-net was negligible for thresholds below 4 pixels, but widened to about 7% at the
10-pixel threshold.
The second test was for image pose recovery. The intent of the test was to determine if the less precise matches
from D2-Net were less useful. This test required MPT benchmarks, and they chose three architectural photo
tourism sets with a variety of cameras and conditions (Madrid Metropolis, Gendarmenmarkt, and Tower of
London) [72, 61]. D2-Net performed competitively with RootSIFT (the only competitor) in this test.
The final test used the MPT Aachen Day-Night benchmark [59]. As the “Day-Night” name implies, this
benchmark had extreme lighting changes. The performance metric was percent of image poses recovered
as a function of accuracy. In this test, D2-Net achieved top performance across the accuracy range. It beat
SuperPoint by 27% and Upright RootSIFT by 38%. The multi-scaled trained version performed the best.

4.6 R2D2, 2019
R2D2 [55] mimics the D2-Net pipeline, but tweaks the cost functions with the aim of restoring the pixel-level
accuracy. To accomplish that goal, they designed cost functions to optimize a differentiable approximation

 13

of the matching accuracy. This was in addition to the ideal that describers/detectors should produce similar
results for matching points, despite changes in perspective, viewpoint, and appearance. Their network also
estimated repeatability and reliability masks to aid the matching. Repeatability was defined as an estimate
of the probability that a keypoint can be accurately matched. It had the affect of downweighting repeating
textures. For training data, they used two MPT benchmarks, “Oxford and Paris” [53] and Aachen Day-Night
[59]. Both were architectural and included extreme lighting changes. The truth data for both started with
SfM pipelines, but additional pairwise processing was done to reduce noise. They repeated the D2-Net
performance test with HPatches [3]. The results for D2-Net, SuperPoint, LF-Net, RootSIFT were similar to
what D2-Net published. R2D2 was competitive at low accuracy thresholds (less than 2.5 pixels), was about
10% better than the nearest competitor (SuperPoint) for thresholds between 2.5 and 8 pixels, and matched
D2-Net for thresholds between 8 and 10 pixels.

4.7 ASLFEAT, 2020
ASLFeat [42] used D2-Net’s pipeline and network design as their starting point, but they were able to apply
deep learning to the entire pipeline from description through winnowing (see Figure 3). They incorporated
the winnowing into their cost functions by requiring that sets of local correspondences should conform to a
2D projective transformation (this constraint is lossless if the object space is locally planar and the image
can be considered a pinhole camera). Including the local geometric constraint would allow the matcher
to disambiguate similar features (i.e., solve the one-to-many question). As a companion capability, they
abandoned the common practice of including the most-confounding negative match in the training data (i.e.,
‘triplet matching’ [4, 48]). They observed that, in the case of repeating features, triplet matching actually
prevents the network from converging. In addition, the cost functions were also optimized to make descriptor
more locally distinct. They used one SPT data set [64] and one MPT data set [52]. The SPT data set was
comprised of architectural images from internet photo collections. The MPT consisted of series of images of
vehicles. The MPT truth data came from SfM pipelines with some additional visibility filtering.
ASLFeat compared their algorithm to R2D2, D2-Net, SuperPoint, LF-Net, and SIFT. They first repeated the
HPatches [3] test from D2-Net and R2D2. Their results appear to exactly confirm those published before,
with the addition of ASLFeat taking the top spot by 3-5% over R2D2 (note that these tests are SPT data,
and thus ASLFeat’s local 2D projective constraint is lossless). The second series of tests came from one
MPT benchmark [7], but the results were reported separately by image type category: indoor SLAM, vehicle
navigation, wide-baseline, and photo tourism. The performance metric was the percent of successful pose
recoveries [42]. The results are given in Table 2. ASLFeat outperformed each algorithm in each test case. The
closest competition was in the vehicle navigation image set test, in which ASLFeat beat SIFT by 1%. In the
photo tourism category, ASLFeat beat the closest competitor R2D2 by 27%. R2D2 was the most consistently
competitive, at 18% behind ASLFeat. LF-Net was the least competitive, at an average of 66% behind ASL.

4.8 SUPERGLUE, 2020
SuperGlue [58] is the followup to SuperPoint; it applied deep learning to the two parts of the pipeline
that SuperGlue did not cover (matching and winnowing). Thus, SuperPoint and SuperGlue together are a
complete matching pipeline. They included winnowing in the matching the same way that ASLFeat did, via
local 2D projective constraints. This addresses the one-to-many issue with repetitive features and allowed
them to constrain their matching network to the ideal that a keypoint have exactly one or zero matches.
They used the SPT data set “Oxford and Paris” [53] and the MPT data set ScanNet [12]. As mentioned
previously, ScanNet was unique in its use of laser depth capture to reduce dense matching noise. SuperGlue
performance comparisons didn’t include any of their competitor’s complete pipelines. Instead, they feed
SuperPoint descriptors as input into the competitor’s final two stages (matching and winnowing). Hence, they
demonstrated only that their novel matcher, notably custom-trained to operate on SuperPoint input, was the

4.9 LOFTR, 2021
LoFTR uses the same pipeline as D2-Net, R2D2, and ASLFeat, all of which begin with description. Like
ASLFeat, LoFTR’s DL network spanned the whole pipeline from description to winnowing. Their algorithm
included a coarse-to-fine progression. They used similar local geometric constraints to winnow. Their training
data were unspecified, and their comparison to other algorithms was incomplete. They report significant
improvement compared to D2-Net and R2D2, but skip the most direct competitors (ASLFeat and SuperGlue).

4.10 MD-NET, 2022
MD-Net [57] was built from R2D2 design and, like R2D2, trained only the description and detection pipeline
stages. They trained multiple detectors separately to detect different groups of points. Point matching was
later done among the individual groups. This presorting into groups made the matching stage faster. The
stated purpose was to show that competitive networks could be trained with only SPT data. They used the
data set “Oxford and Paris” [53] with homographic warp and data augmentation. They then tested in one
SPT benchmark (HPatches [3]) and two MPT benchmarks (Aachen Day-Night [59] and Image Matching
Benchmark). The performance metric was percent recovered, either of the homographies or of the image
poses. MD-Net’s percent improvements are given in Table 3. In summary, MD-Net was competitive with
R2D2 and ASLFeat, and it beat Upright-SIFT by an average of 11%. Note that Upright-SIFT was the best

4.11 DEEP LEARNING STATE OF THE ART
Remote sensing applications haven’t made the shift from using feature matching to using DLBM [29]. None
of the DLBM algorithms was designed or trained on RS data, and a comparison that included two of the
recent DLBM methods (SuperGlue [58] and ASLFeat [42]) concluded by recommending the classic SIFT
feature matcher [29]. A comparison outside of remote sensing concluded that SIFT was better than LIFT
[61]. Another comparison found that LF-Net might be an improvement, but was slow, and the authors
recommended Root. However, there is some agreement that SuperGlue was useful in RS applications when
feature matchers failed [78, 29].
There are few comparisons of DLBM to non-DLBM in RS applications. As a supplement, we will argue
for the credibility of the different deep learning-based matching algorithms. DLBM techniques that have
shown some consistent performance in independent tests are deemed more credible, due to many of the
DLBM methods suffering from inconsistency. For instance, MatchNet reported a dramatic improvement in
false matches compared to SIFT [23], but performed worse in an independent comparison [77]. Similarly
LIFT’s initial promise was contradicted by SuperPoint [13] and LF-Net [50]. LF-Net in turn was cast in
similar doubt by D2-Net [15], R2D2 [55], and ASLFeat [42]. In contrast, SuperPoint reported only modest
improvements over SIFT, but had its reputation propped up by the LF-Net, D2-Net, R2D2, and ASLFeat
performance reports. D2-Net was emulated by R2D2, ASLFeat, and MD-Net [57], and two of them (R2D2
and ASLFeat) duplicated D2-Net’s reported performance metrics. [75] also tested D2-Net on four pairs of
difficult remote sensing images. The results were impressive, if anecdotal. R2D2 and ASLFeat’s performance
was checked by MD-Net. Finally, as noted above, SuperGlue was endorsed for limited-use RS applications
[78, 29]. Hence, SuperPoint, D2-Net, R2D2, SuperGlue, and ASLFeat appear credible. SuperPoint heads the
list for under-promising and over-delivering – although it didn’t perform well in the RS comparison [29].
ASLFeat is the only full pipeline network, but it’s new and has only one independent verification. D2-Net
stands out for its pipeline design (that was emulated by R2D2, ASLFeat, and MD-Net), verified performance,
and impressive anecdotes in RS.

 5. CONCLUSION
RootSIFT and DSP-SIFT are the best candidates for the SIPM state of the art among handcrafted matchers.
The state of the art is not well established among DLBM approaches, but we’ve identified five that are
credible contenders: SuperPoint, D2-Net, R2D2, SuperGlue, and ASLFeat. Within the scope of satellite
image matching, there is little comparison work, and the work that has been done uses algorithms that
were not designed or trained for the use case. Fundamental among the open questions is whether it is
advantageous to do prior geometric correction and hence minimize the need for scale and rotation invariance,
as recommended by [27] and supported by [50, 32, 24]. This question has been ignored because the majority
of SIPM work since NCC in the 1970s has tended toward superior invariance in rotation and scale. This is
analogous to ignoring an available dimensionality reduction and may have a significant performance impact,
whether using feature matching or deep learning-based matching.
The geometric constraints built into SuperGlue [58] and ASLFeat [42] that allow them to deal with repeating
features are a fascinating development, and the anecdotal results they showed were impressive. However,
they are not lossless. With advances in the winnowing algorithms [43, 18], it may be better to leave the
winnowing out of the matcher.
[29] observed that DLBM techniques performed competitively on aerial RS images despite being trained on
architectural and landscape images. This invites the question: How well would deep learning-based matching
methods perform with training data designed specifically for our remote sensing images?
