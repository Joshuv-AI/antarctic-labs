Dissecting Alzheimer's proteomic landscape through NULISA profiling of brain cell‐specific extracellular vesicles
Authors: Ashish Kumar, Mitu Sharma, Yixin Su, Sangeeta Singh, Jordan E Tanley, Ramon Casanova, Fang‐Chi Hsu, Suzanne Craft, Michelle M Mielke, Timothy M Hughes, Gagan Deep
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONBlood‐based biomarkers are essential for early detection, monitoring, and therapeutic development in Alzheimer's disease (AD) and related dementia (ADRD), but current assays lack brain cell specificity and sensitivity to low‐abundant proteins.METHODWe isolated the following brain cell‐derived small extracellular vesicles (sEV) from the plasma of individuals with normal cognition (CN), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), or ADRD: neurons (NDE), astrocytes (ADE), microglia (MDE), oligodendrocytes (ODE), pericytes (PDE), and endothelial cells (EDE). Using NULISAseq, we profiled 122 proteins, spanning AD pathology, neurodegeneration, and neuroinflammation.RESULTSsEV proteomes showed distinct brain cell‐type‐specific signatures. MCI exhibited early dysregulation of neuroprotective, inflammatory, and vascular markers in NDE, MDE, and ODE. ADRD displayed broader alteration tau, amyloid, neuroinflammation, vascular dysfunction, and synaptic loss across multiple sEV populations.DISCUSSIONCombining NULISAseq with brain cell‐derived plasma sEV enables the detection of multicellular molecular changes in ADRD, supporting their use as a minimally invasive platform for biomarker discovery.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, brain cell‐derived sEV, liquid biopsy, NULISA, small extracellular vesicles

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) affects approximately 6.9 million Americans aged 65 and older. 1 It is a progressive, irreversible, age‐related neurodegenerative disorder marked by extracellular amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles of hyperphosphorylated tau. AD also involves neuronal and synaptic loss, chronic neuroinflammation, vascular changes, and region‐specific brain atrophy. 2 , 3 Revised diagnostic criteria define AD biologically, independent of clinical symptoms, based on amyloid and tau markers and co‐pathologies such as α‐synucleinopathy, transactive response DNA‐binding protein 43 (TDP‐43), and cardiovascular disease. 4 , 5 , 6 While US Food and Drug Administration‐approved blood tests exist, clinical diagnosis still relies heavily on clinical evaluation, increasing misdiagnosis risk, especially atypical or among patients with early‐onset disease or mild cognitive impairment (MCI). 7 , 8 Thus, minimally invasive, cost‐effective blood‐based biomarkers for early screening and longitudinal monitoring are urgently needed.

Challenges in AD blood‐based biomarker discovery include poor brain cell specificity and reduced sensitivity due to peripheral dilution and degradation of target proteins. 9 Traditional untargeted plasma proteomics often yield inconsistent results because high‐abundance plasma proteins (e.g., albumin, immunoglobulins, fibronectin) mask brain‐specific signals. The 22 most abundant proteins, including albumin (55% to 60%), immunoglobulins (10% to 20%), transferrin, apolipoproteins, and complement proteins, represent ∼99% of the total protein mass in human plasma. 10 Moreover, the transport of macromolecules across the blood‐brain barrier (BBB) is tightly regulated, resulting in low levels of brain cell‐specific proteins in plasma. In addition, the glymphatic system, a perivascular clearance pathway facilitating cerebrospinal fluid–interstitial fluid exchange, also contributes to the removal of these proteins from the brain, and dysfunction in this pathway may further limit their appearance in plasma. 11 , 12 Additional challenges include protein stability, peripheral uptake, and secretion by non‐neuronal cells, reducing overall brain specificity. Identifying proteins released by specific brain cells and leveraging ultrasensitive technologies are critical to overcome‐ these limitations. These limitations highlight two important needs: (1) sample sources that better reflect the pathologies in the cells of the central nervous system (CNS) that are affected by the disease and (2) ultrasensitive, multiparametric platforms for AD biomarker profiling.

Small extracellular vesicles (sEV) derived from CNS cells have emerged as promising candidates to improve diagnostic accuracy in AD. 13 These are membrane‐bound nano‐sized vesicles (<200 nm in diameter) secreted by all cells, including brain cells, encapsulate proteins, RNAs, and metabolites, protecting them from degradation. 13 Their cargo mirrors the physiological and pathological state of parent cells, making them a valuable source of disease‐specific biomarkers. Over the last decade, extensive research has shown that neuron‐derived sEV (NDE) isolated from plasma carry AD‐related proteins and demonstrated diagnostic and prognostic value. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Our recent work has extended this approach to sEV originating from other CNS cell types, such as astrocyte‐derived sEV (ADE), microglia‐derived sEV (MDE), oligodendrocyte‐derived sEV (ODE), pericyte‐derived sEV (PDE), and endothelial cell‐derived sEV (EDE). We demonstrated the feasibility of isolating six brain cell‐specific sEV subtypes from the same plasma samples and profiled their miRNA content. These miRNAs varied by cognitive status, correlated with cortical thickness on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and provided mechanistic insights into early AD changes. 18 Profiling biomarkers in brain cell‐specific sEV could improve understanding of neuronal, glial and vascular states during AD progression, and yield more specific blood‐based biomarkers for AD.

Advances in proteomics have introduced ultrasensitive, multiplexed assays, such as Olink, SomaScan, and Nucleic acid‐Linked Immuno‐Sandwich Assay (NULISA). NULISA, an automated platform, quantifies hundreds of low‐abundance proteins across a 12‐log dynamic range with attomolar sensitivity. Recent studies using NULISA for AD and related dementias (ADRD) have improved detection of both classical and novel biomarkers of neurodegeneration, inflammation, and immune activation. 19 , 20 In our work, we have employed the NULISAseq CNS Disease Panel to measure 122 CNS‐related proteins in plasma, total sEV, and six brain cell‐specific sEV subtypes (NDE, ADE, MDE, ODE, PDE, and EDE) from cognitively normal (CN), MCI, and ADRD individuals. By integrating ultrasensitive proteomics with multiple brain cell‐specific sEV profiling, we aim to overcome major limitations in blood‐based biomarker development for ADRD.

METHODS

Study participants

The plasma samples from individuals with normal cognition (n = 10), MCI (n = 10), or ADRD (n = 10) were collected as a part of the Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) MIND‐A visit between 2019 and 2021. The demographic information of the participants is presented in Table S1. Briefly, participants completed blood draw, detailed cognitive testing with the Uniform Data Set version 3, physical examination and function, and genetic testing (APOE ε4), provided a medication inventory, responded to health and lifestyle questionnaires, and provided information for proxy/informant interviews. 21 All available clinical data were used in the phenotypic adjudication of cognitive status for CN, MCI, and ADRD with subtyping for AD. Following adjudication, plasma samples were selected from 10 participants from each cognitive group for biomarker assays.

Isolation of total sEV and brain cell‐derived sEV subtypes from plasma

Total sEV and brain cell‐derived sEV subtypes were isolated from plasma using the methodology recently published by us. 18 Briefly, plasma samples from all participants were processed using our modified precipitation method to isolate total sEV. 18 , 22 For the isolation of six different brain cell‐derived sEV, 1000 µg of total sEV protein served as the standardized input for immunocapture, ensuring equivalent capture conditions across all participants and groups. Total sEV were incubated overnight at 4°C with continuous mixing with 8 µg of specific biotin‐labeled antibodies: L1CAM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog No.: 13‐1919‐82), GLAST (Miltenyi Biotec, Auburn, CA, USA, Catalog No.: ACSA‐1‐Biotin), TMEM119 (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA, Catalog No.: 853302), PDGFRα (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog No.: A15732), PDGFRβ (BioLegend, Catalog No.: 323604), and CD31 (BioLegend, Catalog No.: 102503). TMEM119 antibody was labeled with biotin using FluoReporter Mini‐Biotin‐XX protein Labelling Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog No.: F6347). Further, 40 µL of streptavidin‐coated magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog No.: 88817) were washed once with 400 µL of washing buffer (0.2 µm filtered tris buffered saline + 0.1% Tween‐20) and resuspended in 100 µL of resuspension buffer (0.2 µm filtered phosphate‐buffered saline [PBS] + 1% bovine serum albumin) before adding to the sEV‐antibody suspension for 1 h of incubation at room temperature with continuous mixing. The beads were then washed three times with wash buffer. For the elution of a specific sEV subpopulation, beads were resuspended in 200 µL of gentle Ag/Ab elution buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog No.: 21027, pH 6.6) for 10 min. Beads were magnetized and removed, and eluted sEV were transferred to a new tube containing 10% v/v 1 M Tris (pH = 9) to neutralize the pH of the elution buffer. Brain cell‐derived sEV were lysed using 10X RIPA buffer on ice for 30 min with intermittent vortexing and then stored at −80°C.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

The size and concentration of sEV were measured by NTA using Nanosight NS300 (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK). The instrument was primed using PBS, pH 7.4, and the temperature was maintained at 25°C. Five measurements (30 s each) were obtained for each sample, and their average was plotted as a representation of size distribution and concentration.

NULISAseq‐based targeted protein profiling

Plasma, total sEV, NDE, ADE, MDE, ODE, PDE, and EDE from individuals who were CN or were diagnosed with MCI or ADRD (n = 10 per group) were analyzed across three plates with the NULISAseq CNS Disease Panel for multiplexed detection of 122 proteins using an Alamar ARGO HT Platform. Each plate contained three sample controls for intra‐plate coefficient of variation (CV) assessments, three inter‐plate controls for data normalization, and four negative controls for limit of detection (LOD) estimation. Next, 10 µL of plasma or 25 µL of sEV lysate per sample was used for the analysis. Based on a previous pilot analysis using a few similar samples, MAPT (microtubule‐associated protein tau), Aβ38, and Aβ40 assays were tuned up to accommodate much lower concentrations of these analytes in sEV samples compared to plasma samples. In addition, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), neurofilament light chain (NfL), and neurofilament heavy chain (NfH) assays were tuned down to accommodate extremely high levels of these three analytes in sEV samples compared to plasma samples. All steps of the NULISAseq workflow, starting with immunocomplex formation with DNA‐barcoded capture and detection antibodies, capturing and washing the immunocomplexes on paramagnetic oligo‐dT beads, releasing the immunocomplexes into a low‐salt buffer, and subsequently performing a second capture step with streptavidin beads, were performed on the automated Alamar ARGO HT platform as described previously. 23 Finally, the proximal ends of the DNA strands on each immunocomplex were ligated to generate a DNA reporter molecule containing both target‐specific and sample‐specific barcodes. DNA reporter molecules were pooled and amplified by polymerase chain reaction, purified, and sequenced on Illumina NextSeq 2000. Sequencing data were processed using the NULISAseq algorithm. The sample (SMI)‐ and target (TMI)‐specific barcodes were quantified, and up to two mismatching bases or one indel and one mismatch were allowed.

Data analysis

For data analysis, normalization of the raw read counts was carried out to remove potential unwanted technical variation that may be introduced during data collection. First, internal control (IC)‐based normalization was done by dividing the target counts for each sample well by that well's IC counts. Second, inter‐plate control (IPC) normalization was then carried out by dividing IC‐normalized counts by target‐specific medians of the IPC (pooled plasma) sample replicates on that plate. Finally, data were rescaled by adding 1 to the normalized count and log2‐transformed to derive NULISA protein quantification (NPQ) units and form a more normal distribution for subsequent statistical analyses. NPQ is on a logarithmic scale that can quantify multiplicative differences (fold change) between samples or groups.

All statistical analyses were performed using the NULISAseqR package and SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Differentially expressed targets were identified using a linear regression model embedded in the NULISAseqR package with disease status (ADRD, MCI, and CN) as predictor and NPQ as output. Regression coefficient estimates and their 95% confidence intervals (lower and upper confidence bounds) for all targets comparing the ADRD versus CN and MCI versus CN groups are presented in Table S2. Volcano plots (−log10 p value vs log2 fold change) were generated using results from the linear regression models. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the expression profiles of the differentially expressed targets to reduce dimensionality and identify major sources of variance in the dataset. Heatmaps illustrated the abundance levels of targets with significant univariate associations between CN versus MCI, CN versus ADRD, and MCI versus ADRD. To generate the clustered heatmaps, NPQ values for each protein were transformed into z‐scores. Proteins and samples were clustered using agglomerative hierarchical clustering with a Euclidean distance metric and complete method. As this was a pilot study, analyses was focused on estimation rather than on formal hypothesis testing of between‑group differences. Accordingly, multiple‑comparison correction was not performed, and nominal p values were used to prioritize candidate targets rather than to support definitive confirmatory inference.

RESULTS

Isolation and characterization of brain cell‐derived sEV subtypes

To examine cell type‐specific molecular changes across the AD clinical spectrum, along with plasma and total sEV, we isolated six brain cell‐derived sEV from CN, MCI, and ADRD individuals and profiled 122 proteins using the NULISAseq CNS disease panel (Figure S1). NTA confirmed sEV enrichment with a mean size <200 nm (Figure S2A). Notably, MDE exhibited a significantly larger size in MCI (Figure S2A) but lower concentration (Figure S2B). ODE were significantly larger in both MCI and ADRD compared to CN (Figure S2A), while NDE concentration was significantly reduced in ADRD versus CN (Figure S2B).

NULISAseq assay: detectability, variability, and controls

Protein detectability on NULISAseq varied by sample type. Plasma showed the highest detectability (96.7%), followed by total sEV (77.1%). Brain cell‐derived sEV exhibited relatively lower and variable detectability with 25.4% in NDE, 24.6% in ADE, 25.4% in MDE, 27.0% in ODE, 37.7% in PDE, and 33.6% in EDE (Figure S3A). This likely reflects cell‐specific protein loading rather than technical limitations, as each subtype displayed a distinct profile. Plasma and total sEV, representing systemic secretomes, showed higher detectability, as expected. Further, the assay reproducibility was robust, with intra‐plate and inter‐plate CVs of 12.1% and 14.2%, respectively (Figure S3B and 3C).

NULISAseq analysis of plasma

Differential protein expression in plasma across clinical groups is shown via volcano plots, PCA, and heatmaps (Figure 1). CN versus MCI revealed limited changes (Figure 1A, upper panel), with upregulation of C‐C motif chemokine ligand 13 and 17 (CCL13 and CCL17) and downregulation of neurofilament heavy chain (NEFH). PCA showed moderate separation between CN and MCI (PC1 = 56.52%, PC2 = 31.15%) (Figure 1A, middle panel). CN versus ADRD exhibited broader alterations (Figure 1B, upper panel), including Aβ40, Aβ38, phosphorylated tau 231 (pTau‐231), presenilin‐1 (PSEN1), α‐synuclein (SNCA), superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1), vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), neurofilament light chain protein (NEFL), neuronal pentraxin‐2 (NPTX2), and C‐reactive protein (CRP). Interestingly, the significantly low level of pro‐inflammatory microglial mitogen colony‐stimulating factor‐2 (CSF2) was observed in ADRD. PCA indicated partial separation between CN and ADRD (PC1: 46.09%, PC2: 15.22%) (Figure 1B, middle panel). MCI versus ADRD comparison showed increased expression of several proteins, including interleukin‐1β (βB), serum amyloid A1 (SAA1), S100 calcium‐binding protein A12 (S100A12), and NPTX1 (Figure 1C, upper panel). PCA confirmed MCI versus ADRD group separation (PC1 = 46.39%, PC2 = 27.27%) (Figure 1C, middle panel). Heatmaps comparing various groups and illustrating the relative expression of differential altered proteins are shown in Figure 1A–C, lower panels. Additionally, data of all significantly altered proteins are shown as box plots in Figure S4.

NULISAseq analysis of total sEV

Next, total sEV were isolated from all the plasma samples, and NULISAseq was performed. CN versus MCI revealed only an increased expression of MAPT (tau) (Figure S5A, upper panel), a hallmark protein associated with neurofibrillary pathology and neurodegeneration. 24 Given the minimal proteomic difference between these two groups, PCA was not performed. CN versus ADRD showed upregulation of pTau‐217, pTau‐181, transactive response DNA‐binding protein 43 kDa (TARDBP or TDP‐43), insulin‐like growth factor‐1 (IGF1), and GFAP and downregulation of C‐C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), tyrosine 3‐monooxygenase/tryptophan 5‐monooxygenase activation protein zeta (YWHAZ), S100B, and corticotropin‐releasing hormone (CRH) (Figure S5B, upper panel). PCA demonstrated separation between CN and ADRD (PC1 = 36.32%; PC2 = 17.05%) (Figure S5B, middle panel). MCI versus ADRD revealed reduced levels of neuroprotective and synaptic proteins, including synaptosomal‐associated protein 25 (SNAP25), glutamic‐oxaloacetic transaminase 1 (GOT1), CCL26, YWHAZ, and S100B in ADRD, while APOE was significantly elevated (Figure S5C, upper panel), consistent with its central role in amyloid metabolism and AD risk. 25 PCA revealed partial separation between ADRD and MCI (PC1 = 56.58% and PC2 = 16.59%) (Figure S5C, middle panel). Heatmaps comparing various groups and illustrating the relative expression of differential altered proteins are shown in Figure S5B‐5C, lower panels. Additionally, the data of all significantly altered proteins are shown as box plots in Figure S6.

NULISAseq analysis of NDE

In NDE isolated from plasma, no protein was upregulated in MCI compared to CN (Figure 2A, upper panel). However, several proteins, including placental growth factor (PGF), guanosine diphosphate dissociation inhibitor 1 (GDI1), phosphoglycerate kinase 1 (PGK1), interleukin 15 (IL15), and PDZ and LIM domain 5 (PDLIM5) were significantly downregulated in MCI. PCA revealed a partial separation between MCI and CN (PC1 = 51.28%, PC2 = 20.75%) (Figure 2A, middle panel). In CN versus ADRD, NDE exhibited increased levels of inflammatory cytokines (IL4, IL7, Il13, IL17A, IL33), neurodegeneration‐related proteins (such as MAPT, CCL11), phosphorylated tau (pTau‐217, pTau‐181, and pTau‐231), and synaptic regulators (PDLIM5, arginase [AGRN]), while PGF, PGK1, GDI1, and YWHAZ were reduced (Figure 2B, upper panel). PCA demonstrated clear separation between ADRD and CN (PC1 = 58.26%, PC2: 14.96%) (Figure 2A, middle panel). MCI versus ADRD revealed notable upregulation of total and phosphorylated tau (MAPT, pTau‐181, pTau‐217, pTau‐231), Aβ1‐42, and IL family cytokines (IL4, IL7A, IL33, IL1B), while expression of a few proteins (NGF, IL1B, GOT1, and NEFH) was reduced. PCA revealed partial separation between MCI and ADRD (PC1: 72.76%, PC2 = 6.96%) (Figure 2C, middle panel). Heatmaps comparing various groups and illustrating the relative expression of differential altered proteins are shown in Figure 2A–C, lower panels. NULISAseq analysis of MDE

MDE showed increased pro‐inflammatory markers C‐X‐C motif chemokine ligand 1 (CXCL1), CCL13, and CRP, while key regulators of neuroprotection and synaptic function, such as TEK receptor tyrosine kinase (TEK), PGF, surfactant protein D (SFTPD), NPTX2, YWHAZ, and kinase insert domain receptor (KDR), were reduced in MCI compared to CN (Figure 4A, upper panel). PCA showed excellent separation of MCI from CN group (PC1: 43.3%, PC2: 16.44%) (Figure 4A, middle panel). CN versus ADRD revealed elevated inflammation and immune activation markers such as CRP, metalloproteinase inhibitor 3 (TIMP3), and CCL17, whereas proteins such as parkinsonism associated deglycase (PARK7), chitotriosidase‐1 (CHIT1), and YWHAZ were reduced (Figure 4B, upper panel). PCA suggested partial separation of ADRD from CN (PC1: 37.69%, PC2: 18.62%) (Figure 4B, middle panel). MCI versus ADRD showed upregulation of PGF, POSTN, CCL17, ubiquitin carboxyl‐terminal hydrolase isozyme L1 (UCHL1), and IL10, supporting enhanced inflammatory and phagocytic responses in AD, while the levels of CXCL1 and CCL26 were downregulated (Figure 4C, upper panel). PCA showed clear separation of ADRD from MCI (PC1: 58.54% and PC2: 9.84%) (Figure 4C, middle panel). NULISAseq analysis of ODE

In ODE, the cytokine IL17A, brain abundant membrane attached signal protein 1 (BASP1), REST, and pTau‐181 were elevated in MCI compared to CN, whereas IL16, VEGFA, and malate dehydrogenate 1 (MDH1) were reduced (Figure 5A, upper panel). PCA did not show a clear separation of MCI from CN (PC1: 64.98%, PC2: 15.74%) (Figure 5A, middle panel). In CN versus ADRD, ODE exhibited broad downregulation of AD‐related proteins, including major Aβ forms (Aβ42, Aβ40, Aβ38), tau (MAPT, pTau‐217, pTau‐181, pTau‐231), multiple cytokines (Il10, IL12p70, Il13, Il15, IL1B, IL2, IL33, IL4, IL5, and IL7), PSEN1, and APOE (Figure 5B, upper panel). Conversely, NEFH, triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2), and ficolin‐2 (FCN2) were elevated in ADRD (Figure 5B, upper panel). PCA showed limited separation between CN and ADRD (PC1: 73.5%, PC2: 6.09%) (Figure 5B, middle panel). MCI versus ADRD revealed robust downregulation of a wide panel of neurodegeneration‐related proteins, including Aβ38, Aβ40, Aβ42, and total and phosphorylated tau (MAPT, pTau217, pTau‐231, pTau‐181), along with PSEN1, oligo‐SNCA, NEFL, and multiple cytokines (Figure 5C, upper panel). In contrast, inflammatory and apoptotic proteins such as IL16, IGF1, NPTX2, UCHL1, and VSNL1 were upregulated in ADRD (Figure 5C, upper panel). NULISAseq analysis of PDE

PDE showed no significant changes between CN and MCI, though subtle dysregulation trends were noted (Figure 6A). CN versus ADRD revealed reduced vascular markers such as VEGFA, nerve growth factor (VGF), and AGRN and increased TIMP3 and intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM1) (Figure 6B, upper panel). PCA indicated strong separation between these groups (PC1:37.07% PC2:16.9%) (Figure 6B, middle panel). Comparative analysis of traditional AD biomarkers across plasma, total sEV, and brain cell‐derived sEV subtypes

We analyzed the relative levels of traditional AD biomarkers, including Aβ42, Aβ40, Aβ38, MAPT, pTau‐181, pTau‐217, and pTau‐231, across plasma, total sEV, NDE, ADE, MDE, ODE, PDE, and EDE. Overall, significantly elevated levels of Aβ40, Aβ38, and pTau‐231 were observed in plasma from ADRD versus CN. In total sEV, only pTau‐181 and pTau‐217 showed elevated levels in ADRD while other markers did not show significant alteration. However, an increase in MAPT level was observed in MCI compared to CN. In contrast, NDE exhibited significantly increased levels of MAPT, pTau‐181, pTau‐217, and pTau‐231 in ADRD compared to CN and MCI, while Aβ42 was elevated only in ADRD compared to MCI. ADE showed significantly increased levels of all these markers between CN and ADRD comparison, while the levels of MAPT, pTau‐181, pTau‐217, and pTau‐231 showed increased levels between MCI and ADRD. Notably, ODE displayed significantly reduced levels of all these markers in ADRD compared to CN and MCI (Figure 8), though the level of pTau‐181 was found to be increased in MCI compared to CN. DISCUSSION

This study provides a comprehensive targeted proteomic analysis of plasma, total sEV, and brain cell‐derived sEV subtypes across the AD clinical spectrum using the highly sensitive NULISAseq platform. Our findings reveal distinct, cell type‐specific protein signatures that reflect compartmentalized pathophysiological processes. Notably, at the MCI stage, NDE and ADE showed changes in neurodegenerative and neurovascular biomarkers associated with AD pathogenesis, whereas MDE and ODE indicated inflammation and white matter involvement. Interestingly, sEV derived from vascular cells (PDE and EDE) suggested inflammation and barrier dysfunction. These differential patterns underscore the potential of sEV‐based liquid biopsy for detecting early and holistic pathophysiological changes in different brain cells during AD progression.

A recent study using NULISAseq reported GFAP and S100A12 as significantly altered proteins in AD plasma. 28 Also, the analysis of ATN markers using Single Molecule Array (Simoa)‐based assays, validated using Olink in the plasma of AD individuals demonstrated a lower plasma Aβ42/40 ratio and a higher plasma NfL level, while plasma tau levels remain unchanged. 29 A 2023 systematic review and meta‐analysis by Sahrai et al. on Simoa‐based assays for serum samples and a large prospective study by Mattsson et al. reported higher plasma NEFL levels in MCI. 30 , 31 Consistent with these reports, we observed increased Aβ38, Aβ40, pTau‐231, NEFL, NPTX2, and α‐synuclein in ADRD. Moreover, the elevated proteins, such as IL1B, SAA1, S100A12, and NPTX1, between MCI and ADRD indicate peripheral immune activation associated with disease advancement. 20 , 32 However, plasma proteins reflect systemic secretome and lack brain specificity.

There are limited studies analyzing the expression of proteins in plasma and sEV concurrently. We observed a high degree of concordance in terms of proteins target detectability between plasma and total sEV. The total sEV exhibited higher levels of MAPT at MCI stage, as reported earlier, 33 although the levels of other typical AD pathological proteins were not found to be altered. Interestingly, a consistent downregulation of YWHAZ and S100B was observed in ADRD, indicative of synaptic dysfunction, neuronal hyperexcitability, and stress signaling impairment. 34 , 35 The decline in SNAP25 and GOT1 in MCI versus ADRD suggested a decline in synaptic vesicle trafficking and mitochondrial function. 36 Conversely, elevated APOE in ADRD, in the total sEV fraction, reaffirmed its central role in modulating Aβ metabolism and contributing to disease risk. 25 Increased pTau‐217, pTau‐181, and GFAP in total sEV from ADRD further support their biomarker potential. Despite these promising findings, sEV proteins, like plasma proteins, may not be exclusively brain derived.

This is the first study to present a comprehensive proteome of sEV derived from six brain cell types, revealing novel molecular players at early and late stages of ADRD. Reduced levels of IL15, GDI1, PGK1, PGF, and PDLIM5 in NDE from MCI individuals are associated with disruptions in immune signaling, synaptic function, energy metabolism, and vascular integrity at the MCI stage. 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 Consistent downregulation of PGF, PGK1, GDI1, and NEFL replicated plasma findings. Importantly, NDE showed the most pronounced upregulation of canonical AD markers, including pTau‐181, pTau‐217, pTau‐231, and MAPT in ADRD versus both MCI and CN, highlighting progressive tauopathy and positioning NDE as highly specific carriers of tau pathology. 41 , 42 Elevated pro‐inflammatory cytokines (IL4, IL17α, IL33, and IL1B) in NDE from ADRD indicate neuronal contribution to the inflammatory milieu via sEV release. The concomitant reduction of neurotrophic markers like NGF and NEFH across MCI and ADRD points to impaired neurotrophic support and axonal damage, 43 , 44 reinforcing neurodegenerative progression.

In ADE, higher VEGFA and RUVBL2 at MCI suggested astrocytic responses to vascular and oxidative stress at this stage. 45 , 46 Consistent with a prior report, GFAP was significantly lower in ADE from ADRD. 47 Similar to NDE, ADE exhibited a marked increase in pTau‐181, pTau‐217, pTau‐231, and MAPT across disease stages, implicating astrocytes in tau propagation. 48 Additionally, elevated Aβ (Aβ38, Aβ40, and Aβ42) and cytokines in ADE may promote astrocytic pro‐inflammatory activation, 49 while downregulation of VEGFA and S100B in ADRD points to astrocyte‐mediated neuroinflammation and BBB dysfunction. 50 , 51 These shifts underscore ADE as indicators of glial activation and early neurovascular uncoupling in AD.

MDE showed more pronounced protein changes in MCI than ADRD, suggesting that inflammation‐related microglial alterations may be more prominent at the MCI stage. However, longitudinal studies are required to further establish temporal changes. Reduced YWHAZ in MCI may impair the ability of microglia to clear Aβ, promoting plaque accumulation, which may exacerbate neuroinflammation and neuronal damage. This hypothesis is further supported by observed higher levels of TIMP3 in MDE from ADRD, as this protein plays an indirect role in regulating Aβ production and the pathophysiology of Aβ deposition in brains. 52 , 53 Moreover, reduced levels of NPTX2 in MDE at MCI indicate a hyperphagocytic activity of microglia, leading to synaptic loss and neurodegeneration. 54 In MDE, comparison between MCI and ADRD showed increased expression of inflammatory and phagocytic markers such as CCL17, POSTN, FGF2, GFAP, and PGF, indicative of a pro‐inflammatory microglial phenotype. 55 , 56 , 57 , 58 The observed decrease in CXCL1 and CCL26 highlights a potential dysregulation in chemokine gradients, which may impair microglial surveillance and response capabilities. 59 , 60 These findings indicate a possible phenotypic shift of microglia from surveillance to a chronically activated state, a hallmark of AD‐associated neuroinflammation. Notably, CRP expression in MDE strongly discriminated MCI from CN, emphasizing systemic and local inflammation as major contributors to early AD. 61 , 62

Higher IL17α, BASP1, and REST levels in ODE from MCI individuals indicate early oligodendrocyte involvement in differentiation, myelination, and immune signaling. 63 , 64 Notably, pTau‐181 elevation in ODE in MCI align with prior mouse studies linking oligodendroglial tauopathy to white matter degeneration. 65 , 66 Additionally, downregulation of VEGFA, IL16, and MDH1 further suggests metabolic stress, while widespread downregulation of neurodegeneration‐linked proteins reflects progressive oligodendrocyte dysfunction and impaired axonal support with cognitive decline. Increased pro‐inflammatory and apoptotic proteins, such as IL16, IGF1, and VSNL1, suggest oligodendrocytic stress response and its role in demyelination and white matter degeneration in AD. In ADRD, ODE showed consistent downregulation of Aβ42, PSEN1, MAPT, and pTau‐181, indicating early myelin and axonal injury, along with higher FCN2, NEFH, and TREM2, indicating accelerated myelin degeneration and active oligodendrocytic response in late‐stage pathology. 67 , 68

Endothelial cells and pericytes, along with astrocytes, form the BBB, which is critical in maintaining brain homeostasis. Both PDE and EDE exhibited only subtle protein changes at the MCI stage (except a marginal CALB2 increase in EDE), suggesting largely preserved BBB integrity. Further, elevated VEGFA and oxidative stress markers in ADE in MCI may signal early astrocytic involvement in BBB. 69 Further, in ADRD, decreased levels of GDF15, VEGFA, VGF, and SFRP1 in PDE suggest impaired angiogenic response leading to compromised BBB and neuroinflammation. 70 , 71 Higher inflammatory cytokines levels in ADRD may drive adhesion molecule expression (ICAM1, annexin A5, and CD63), 72 reflecting the transition from a functional BBB to a pro‐inflammatory, permeable state. However, markers used for PDE and EDE isolation are not exclusive to brain pericytes or endothelial cells, warranting further validation.

These findings highlight the complexity of sEV‐mediated signaling during cognitive decline. Each sEV subtype provided unique insights into parent cell status, revealing coordinated deterioration across neuronal, glial, and vascular compartments as AD progresses. These observed changes support a model in which inflammation, tau pathology, synaptic loss, and neurovascular breakdown advance in parallel. Early changes in NDE, MDE, and ODE, highly indicative of immune signaling activation, leading to dysfunctional synaptic and myelination pathways, underscore the utility of brain cell‐derived sEV for disease staging, early detection, and mechanistic insight. Prominent alterations in NDE and ADE proteins in ADRD, especially for classical AD biomarkers, may allow for sensitive and cell‐specific patient stratification. Moreover, strong involvement of MDE, ODE, and EDE underscores their role as active participants in disease propagation, consistent with emerging views of AD as a system‐level disorder affecting neurons, glia, and vasculature. 73 , 74 These findings reinforce the notion that MCI is not merely a prodromal stage of AD but a distinct phase marked by specific molecular changes. 75 Also, these findings align with the multifactorial nature of AD, involving not only amyloid and tau pathology but also neuroinflammation, vascular dysfunction, and synaptic loss. The cell type specificity of these changes provide insights into the compartmentalized progression of the disease and offers a framework for targeted therapeutic interventions.

This foundational work has limitations that may inform the context of these findings. First, this study was designed to optimize and validate methods to measure CNS biomarkers within various brain cell‐derived sEV; therefore, it was not powered to detect small differences or perform adjustments for multiple comparisons beyond nominal thresholds. Second, these participants represent cognitive phenotypes and not etiologic diagnosis. Furter studies incorporating ATN biomarker confirmation and stratification by biomarker status, as well as larger sample sizes, will be required to further evaluate these findings. Third, the cross‐sectional design of the study precludes inference about temporal order of molecular events. The observed differences between CN, MCI, and ADRD groups reflect inter‐individual variation at a single time point, which may not replicate as intra‐individual disease progression, and will require longitudinal validation. Lastly, although the surface markers used for sEV isolation are not exclusively CNS specific, prior work supports their use.

Taken together, our findings demonstrate that NULISAseq combined with brain cell‐derived sEV captures dynamic, cell‐specific proteomic changes, offering a minimally invasive window into evolving AD pathology and a foundation for future biomarker refinement.
