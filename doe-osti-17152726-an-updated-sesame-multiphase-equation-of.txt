 Abstract

 This report details the updated Nickle (Ni) multiphase tabular sesame equation
 of state (EOS), alongside shock data along the principal Hugoniot to ¿ 1 TPa pres-
 sures on the Sandia National Laboratory’s Z Pulse Power Facility. Ab initio density
 functional theory calculations are performed to provide constraining data for the Ni
 EOS, alongside references experimental data. Uncertainty quantification is performed
 by the Markov Chain Monte Carlo method and produces EOS parameter posterior
 distributions. The updated tabular sesame Ni EOS has been assigned material ID
 3105.

1 Introduction
Nickel is an elemental metal with a wide range of uses in modern technology. As such, an
accurate description of its equation of state (EOS) is required for hydrodynamic simulations
of systems that include nickel components. Nickel is also found in massive quantities in
the Earth’s core and rocky space objects such as meteors. An accurate EOS model is thus
necessary for geological impact models for planetary defense and planetary formation [1].
The previous tabular Sesame EOS with material ID 83103 was developed by Travis Sjostrom
in 2022 [2]. Since then, an abundance of new high-pressure shock data has been produced
and published. Of note are the detailed shock and x-ray diffraction experiments by Pereira
et al. [3]. The investigators found that under shock, the face-centered cubic (fcc) structure of
nickel is stable to 322 GPa. Then, nickel undergoes shock melting from 322 GPa to around
500 GPa, with a mixture of liquid and fcc being measured at 486 GPa. Sesame EOS 83108
describes shock melting at 258 GPa, which is too low in light of these recent findings.

 1

In this report, we detail recent shock compression experiments performed on pure nickel at
the Z Pulse Power Facility up to TPa conditions and the subsequently revised nickel Sesame
EOS. New data allow further refinement of EOS parameters to more accurately reflect the
higher shock melt onset pressure recently measured. In addition, we use a more refined
Tartarus average-atom model for the thermal electronic portion of the EOS [4, 5]. This new
iteration of the nickel Sesame EOS, given material ID 3105, presents a high-fidelity model
fit for hydrodynamic simulations of nickel at extreme conditions.
Establishing starting thermodynamic parameters for pure nickel at ambient conditions is
required to begin EOS construction. At ambient conditions, nickel crystals have an fcc
crystal structure with a density ρref of ∼8.9 g/cm3 [6]. Nickel is ferromagnetic with a strong
magnetic dipole per atom of 0.6 Bohr magnetons at room temperature. Nickel undergoes a
second-order phase transition to a paramagnetic phase at 630 K. The measured adiabatic
bulk modulus KS of nickel ranges between 178 and 192 GPa at ambient conditions [7].
The measured coefficient of volumetric thermal expansion αV sits around 3.9×10−5 K−1 at
ambient conditions [8]. Finally, the isobaric specific heat capacity (constant pressure) cp is
measured at 4.5×10−4 MJ/kg [9, 10]. Using the Gruneisen relation, the resulting Gruneisen
parameter γ = (αKS )/(cp ρref ) is estimated to be between 1.80 and 1.85 at ambient conditions.

2 Experimental Details
Shock compression and release experiments were performed on the Sandia National Labo-
ratory Z pulse power machine, colloquially called the Z machine [11]. On the Z machine,
aluminum flyer plates can be accelerated to 40 km/s [12], and this capability is used in this
study to explore shock states in nickel from 0.3 TPa to ¿ 1.0 TPa. Both a coaxial geometry
(for impact velocities less than 25 km/s) and a 2-sided stripline geometry (for impact ve-
locities ¿ 25 km/s) are utilized in this work. The Al flyer plate was shocklessly accelerated
toward a high-purity Ni sample with a thickness of 250-500 µm and a density of 8.9 g/cm−3 .
Two push-pull velocity interferometer systems for any reflector (VISAR [13, 14]), with a
frequency-doubled Nd:YAG laser operating at 532 nm and dual velocity-per-fringe (VPF)
capabilities, were used to measure the velocity of the flyer plate from initial rest to impact.
Three separate VISAR signals were recorded per sample and window. Since nickel is opaque
to VISAR light, impact time was determined from fiducial signals from transparent window
material, in this case quartz, stacked on the back end of the nickel sample. Shock breakout
was observed at the back surface of the opaque sample through the quartz window. The
VISAR fiducial measurements and thicknesses of layers were used to compute the shock ve-
locity in the nickel sample. An additional, thick quartz witness window was used to monitor
the shock wave, allowing the necessary corrections to the shock velocity measurements for
the acceleration of the setup. The impact velocity (particle velocity), the shock velocity,
and the initial density of the samples supply the required information to compute the shock-
state density and pressure from the Rankine-Hugoniot relations [15]. To do this, a Monte
Carlo impedance-matching analysis was used [16, 17]. The Monte Carlo calculations provide
uncertainty quantification and credibility intervals for the shock velocity in the sample and
subsequent thermodynamic state variables computed along the shock Hugoniot of Ni and

3 Density Functional Theory Calculations
The cold compression pressure and energy of fcc nickel were computed by density functional
theory using the QuantumESPRESSO software package [19]. A stringent, fully relativis-
tic optimized norm-conserving Vanderbilt (ONCV) pseudopotential from the Pseudo-Dojo
database was used [20, 21]. This pseudopotential leaves 18 electrons per atom to be explic-
itly solved for, allowing us to calculate the compressive response up to 2.5x ambient density
without risking pseudo-core overlap. A 4-atom conventional fcc cell was used with a spin-
polarized formulation. Atomic magnetic moments were initialized to 0.6 Bohr magnetons
and allowed to optimize. At maximum density, a 26×26×26 grid of k-points was required
to converge total energies, charge densities, and Kohn-Sham eigenvalues. Across all densi-
ties, a 140 Ry plane-wave energy cutoff was sufficient to converge the total energy per atom
to within 5 meV. Internal energy and pressure were computed on the subsequent grid of
densities.
Ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations were also performed in the Quantum
Espresso package at high temperatures to estimate the liquid isotherms and Hugoniot. To
optimize computational efficiency, a PAW (projector augmented wave) pseudopotential was
used, and the maximum compression was limited to 2x. The plane-wave energy cutoff was
reduced to 80 Ry. The Γ-point was used for Brillouin zone sampling. AIMD simulations
were run on 32-atom cells on a temperature and density grid spanning T = 10000, 20000,
30000, and 40000 K, and ρ = 15, 16, 17, and 18 g/cm−3 . The ion temperature was controlled
by an Anderson thermostat.
The electronic thermal EOS was determined by average-atom Dirac-Kohn-Sham DFT as im-
plemented in the Tartarus model [5]. In this way, relativistic effects are implicitly included.
Helmholtz free energy, internal energy, and pressure were computed on wide-ranging temper-
ature and density grids. The cold curve contribution was extrapolated via Padé continuation
and subtracted from the table to isolate the thermal electronic equation of state. PCHIP
interpolation was used to fill in points where TARTARUS calculations failed to converge.
The thermal electronic EOS was then exported and loaded into OpenSesame for full EOS
generation.

5 Optimization and Uncertainty Quantification
An initial version of the new nickel EOS was fit by hand for both the solid and liquid phases.
However, once reasonable parameters were determined, the parameter posteriors of ρ0 , K0 ,
K0′ , Θref , γref , and γ ′ of the solid phase were sampled using Markov chain Monte Carlo as
implemented in the OpenSesame code [32, 33, 30]. Flat priors were determined from the
initial expert fit and experimental minimum and maximum values. The standard deviations
of the data sets were also sampled. The parameter posteriors produced (Fig. 1) remain close

 4

to, but not identical to, the parameters chosen for our final EOS. Cold density, bulk modulus,
and the pressure derivative of the bulk modulus are well sampled by MCMC. The thermal
ionic EOS parameter posterior distributions for Debye temperature, Gruneisen parameter,
and the derivative of the Gruneisen parameter do not encompass our final choices for these
parameters but remain close. This discrepancy is likely due to data bias. An abundance of
data is used for cold compression (DAC experiments and DFT calculations), more than is
used for thermal expansion. Further, the magnetic EOS parameters are not optimized but
contribute heavily to the heat capacity, which may bias the sampling for the thermal ionic
parameters.

6 Experimental Results
Results from the Z-shots are shown in Table ??. The four highest-pressure shots all lie
above the melt completion pressure projected by Pereira et al.. To interrogate this, we plot
the us vs. up data along with data for fcc available from the literature [34, 35, 36] in Fig.
2e. The Z-shot at a up of 3.499 km/s and a pressure of 308.2 GPa falls cleanly on the
linear trend set by the lower-particle-velocity experiments. The four highest points are not
well represented by this same linear relation and instead are better fit by a different linear
relation. Considering these results in conjunction with previous studies, we hypothesize that
the four highest-pressure Z-shots are measuring the shock Hugoniot in the liquid phase. The
intermediate Z-shot at a up of 4.029 km/s and a pressure of 381.9 GPa seems to fall between
these two lines and thus could represent a mixed-phase point along the shock Hugoniot,
which would be in agreement with the findings of Pereira et al..

7 Equation of State
Final nickel EOS parameter choices are presented in Table ?? for the solid and liquid phases.
Note that the final values selected for an optimal fit to all thermodynamic curves do not
match the means of the sampled posteriors above. Selected thermodynamic curves for the
updated nickel EOS are shown in Fig. 2 (a)-(f). Fig. 2 (a) shows the ambient-pressure
density isobar from 0 K through melt. The ambient-pressure melt temperature in our EOS
model is 1730 K, in excellent agreement with the observed ambient melt temperature [37].
The specific volume change at ambient melt is 6.01×10−3 cm3 /g. This EOS also exhibits a
volumetric coefficient of thermal expansion of 4.0×10−5 K−3 , in good agreement with exper-
iments [37]. Fig. 2 (b) shows the specific heat at constant pressure (isobaric) as a function
of temperature. Typical Debye-like character is exhibited up to around room temperature.
Contributions from the magnetic EOS take over in the range 300-700 K, where nickel tran-
sitions from ferromagnetic to paramagnetic at a Curie temperature of 630 K, exhibiting a
characteristic divergence in this response function. Above 700 K, our EOS slightly under-
estimates the experimental measurements [38]. While the Tartarus electronic thermal EOS

Moving away from isobaric data, we examine the room-temperature isothermal compres-
sive response in Fig. 2 (d). The EOS exhibits excellent agreement with available room-
temperature diamond anvil cell data and matches the high-density compression response
from the DFT calculations (note that these are 0 K calculations) up to 1.5 TPa and 2.5x
compression. We conclude that a Rose-Vinet model is sufficient to capture the cold com-
pression EOS of nickel [39, 40, 1]. Beyond 2.5x compression, we smoothly continue the cold
curve along the cold Thomas-Fermi-Dirac compression model.
Our updated EOS shows excellent agreement with historical shock results and the new Z
machine shots. Examining the shock velocity us as a function of particle velocity up , shown
in Fig. 2 (e), we can see that the behavior separates into two segmented linear regimes.
Below a up of 3.7 km/s, what is predicted to be the solid shock response follows a linear
trend starting from a bulk sound velocity c0 of 4.6 km/s. Combined with the ambient density,
this leads to a bulk modulus of 188 GPa. This sound velocity is in good agreement with
the LASL shock compendium, which reports a value of 4.53 km/s [34]. However, its starting
density of 8.875 g/cm3 is slightly lower than the accepted modern non-porous values. Above
a particle velocity up of 3.7, a transition is seen to a different linear trend, which our EOS
predicts corresponds to the liquid phase. Fig. 2 (f) shows the shock Hugoniot in pressure-
density space, where two separate trends are also observed. The solid phase persists up to

a pressure of 320 GPa, in excellent agreement with the recent results of Pereira et al., who
observed stable fcc up to 322 GPa [3]. From 320 GPa to 460 GPa, the updated EOS remains
in a mixed state, then continues into the pure liquid phase. Excellent agreement is seen with
the Z machine shock data in all regimes.
Next, we examine the melt curve in our EOS model. The melt curve shows good agreement
with experimental values spanning pressures between 0 and 110 GPa [41, 42, 43, 44]. Between
0 and 410 GPa, the melt curve is in good agreement with ab initio molecular dynamics
simulations by Zhao et al. [45]. The shock Hugoniot in pressure-temperature space is also
shown over the melt curve. Specific vertical lines mark where Pereira et al. observe fcc and
mixed liquid+fcc, and where they extrapolate melt completion [3]. While we agree with
their observed onset of shock melt at 320 GPa, our model exhibits shock melt completion at
a pressure below that at which they still observe a solid-liquid mixture.
Finally, we consider the vapor dome of nickel from the updated EOS. We find a liquid-vapor
dome critical point at a density of 2 g/cm3 , in agreement with more recent theoretical results
and an improvement in critical density over the prior EOS [46]. However, the new critical
temperature is reduced to 8000 K from the old value of 9850 K. The new value is below both
the simulated value of 9470 K and the experimental value of 9100 ± 200 K, whereas the
old value was closer to both [47]. Our critical pressure remains essentially the same as that
of the prior Ni EOS at 0.65 GPa, whereas theory and experiments still estimate a critical
pressure closer to 1 GPa.

8 Conclusions
This iteration of the nickel EOS contains an improved description of shock melt as guided by
experiments on the Z machine and the recent work by Pereira et al. [3]. The shock Hugoniot
data determined by the Z shots suggest separate solid and liquid response regions, which
agree well with other recent findings. Points along the liquid shock Hugoniot interpolated
from AIMD simulations also agree extremely well with the Z shots. The updated EOS rep-
resents the Hugoniot in these two regions well. While the prior Ni EOS utilized a Lindeman
melt model, we explicitly determine the melt curve by a Gibbs free energy intersection along
the boundary. The prior EOS construction utilized particle swarm optimization, whereas in
this case MCMC parameter sampling was used to determine credible parameter distributions
given the data sets presented in this study. There remains room for further investigation. The
temperature-dependent bulk modulus must be reconciled with available data, even though
the reference value of the updated Ni EOS is in good agreement. Finally, the discrepancy
between the MCMC-optimized parameter averages and the final chosen parameter values
represents a final use of expert judgment to fit this EOS. Examination of the data sets for
inter-set thermodynamic consistency would be a valuable next step in aligning MCMC pa-
rameter averages with the final chosen parameters. EOS generation was performed with the
LANL-based OpenSesame code. The new Ni EOS has been given a material ID of 3105.
