Arthroscopic‐Assisted Combined Superior Capsular Reconstruction and Lower Trapezius Transfer Using Dual Achilles Allografts for Irreparable Posterosuperior Rotator Cuff Tears
Authors: Woo‐Sung Do, Yong‐Min Chun, Sung‐Jae Kim, Joon‐Ryul Lim, Tae‐Hwan Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIrreparable posterosuperior rotator cuff tears remain challenging, particularly in young, active patients. Superior capsular reconstruction restores static stability but is limited by graft failure and reduced external rotation, especially with infraspinatus fatty infiltration. Lower trapezius transfer restores dynamic force coupling but lacks static support. This Technical Note describes an arthroscopic‐assisted mini‐open technique combining superior capsular reconstruction with lower trapezius transfer using dual Achilles allografts. The graft's thickness provides durable, superior coverage, whereas a mini‐open anterior portal extension simplifies graft passage and suture management. This combined approach integrates static and dynamic stabilization to improve stability and range of motion and to reduce subacromial contact pressures in patients with irreparable posterosuperior rotator cuff tears.

Irreparable posterosuperior rotator cuff tears (PSRCTs) remain a difficult clinical problem, particularly in young, active patients without advanced glenohumeral arthritis. Superior capsular reconstruction (SCR) provides static superior stability and helps prevent humeral head migration, but it carries notable limitations. 1 Graft failure rates are high with thinner dermal allografts, which are prone to elongation and thinning, 2 and SCR's ability to resist superior translation decreases at higher abduction angles. 3 Moreover, SCR does not substantially improve external rotation compared with lower trapezius transfer (LTT), 4 and outcomes are further compromised by severe infraspinatus fatty infiltration, which disrupts transverse force coupling and increases the risk of graft failure.

LTT offers a dynamic solution by restoring anteroposterior force coupling with a line of pull similar to that of the infraspinatus. It effectively reduces superior humeral head translation across abduction angles, but it does not fully replace the static stabilizing role of the superior capsule. 3 , 5

This Technical Note describes an arthroscopic‐assisted mini‐open approach that combines SCR with LTT using a robust Achilles allograft. The Achilles graft, with its greater thickness and strength, provides reliable superior coverage and minimizes elongation compared with dermal allografts, while avoiding donor‐site morbidity of autografts. Additionally, the mini‐open anterior portal extension facilitates efficient graft passage and minimizes suture entanglement, common challenges in arthroscopic SCR. By integrating static and dynamic stabilization, this combined technique aims to enhance glenohumeral stability, restore range of motion, and decrease subacromial contact pressures, offering a comprehensive biomechanical solution for complex PSRCTs.

Arthroscopic Procedures

Patient Positioning and Portal Placement

With the patient placed in the beach‐chair position using an arm holder (Spider, Smith & Nephew), a standard posterior viewing portal is established, and diagnostic arthroscopy is performed. Standard anterior and lateral working portals are then created. If a subscapularis tear is identified, a traction suture is placed for subsequent repair during the mini‐open procedure. The arthroscope is switched to the lateral viewing portal for subsequent procedures. The anterior and Neviaser portals are created to allow glenoid anchor insertion.

Glenoid Preparation and Insertion of Glenoid Anchors

Debridement and preparation of the glenoid beneath the remnant capsule are performed using a shaver. Through the anterior portal, a suture anchor (AlphaVent, Stryker, Greenwood Village, CO) is inserted at the 11 o'clock position (left shoulder orientation) (Figure 1, Video 1). Using the Neviaser portal, another anchor is inserted at the 1 o'clock position. During this step, a retriever is used to elevate the capsule to facilitate anchor placement. Once the anchor is inserted, the suture is immediately retrieved through the lateral portal.

Insertion of the glenoid anchor at the 1 o'clock position through the Neviaser portal, with a retriever inserted via the lateral working portal used to elevate the remnant tissue and retrieve the sutures after anchor placement (B).

Achilles Tendon Preparation for LTT

The Achilles tendon is prepared using 2 high‐strength sutures (FiberWire, Arthrex, Naples, FL) in a Krackow fashion (Figure 2). An additional suture is placed at the opposite end of the tendon, and it is tied to a tensioning device and pretensioned with 6 pounds of load for more than 10 minutes. 6

Achilles Tendon Preparation for SCR

The Achilles tendon is folded longitudinally, bringing the proximal and distal parts together (Figure 3). One side is secured with interrupted sutures, and the other end is folded to create a rectangular configuration and is fixed with mattress sutures. From the edge of the graft, 5 mm is marked to indicate the width of the glenoid footprint.

LTT Preparation

A 4 cm transverse skin incision is created just lateral to the medial border of the scapular spine and about 1 cm inferior to it (Figure 4). Dissection is carried out to release the tendon from the surrounding fascial tissue as well as from its attachment on the inferior aspect of the scapular spine.

Exposure of the lower trapezius tendon in the beach‐chair position. A 4‐cm transverse skin incision was made just lateral and approximately 1 cm inferior to the medial border of the scapular spine (A). The lower trapezius tendon was mobilized (B).

Graft Passage

A small window is created in the superficial infraspinatus fascia to facilitate graft passage. Under visualization from the posterolateral portal, a suture is advanced through the fascial opening using a tonsil forcep (Figure 5). The suture is then retrieved through the lateral portal and used as a shuttle relay to pass the graft.

Passage of the tendon graft through the fascial opening in the beach‐chair position. (A) An arthroscopic view from the lateral viewing portal (asterisk) showing retrieval of a shuttle suture through the lateral working portal (arrowhead) (A). Tendon graft is passed using the shuttle suture (B).

Mini‐Open Procedures

Approach

A mini‐open incision is made along the anterior portal following the deltopectoral interval to access the subacromial space (Figure 6). All sutures are then retrieved through this incision. If a concomitant subscapularis tear is present, it is repaired using an open technique through the same approach.

Mini‐open approach for retrieval of glenoid anchor sutures and Achilles tendon graft. A mini‐open incision was made along the anterior portal following the deltopectoral interval (A). All suture limbs from the anterior and posterior glenoid anchors and the Achilles tendon graft were retrieved through this incision (B).

Graft Fixation at the Greater Tuberosity for LTT

The greater tuberosity is prepared using a curette. A single medial anchor (AlphaVent, Stryker, Greenwood Village, CO) is inserted at the posteromedial aspect of the greater tuberosity, and the graft is secured with mattress sutures (Figure 7). The tied strands are then secured to the anterior aspect of the greater tuberosity (GT) lateral wall with a knotless anchor (PopLok, CONMED, Largo, FL, or SwiveLock, Arthrex, Naples, FL), completing the fixation using a transosseous equivalent technique.

Fixation of the Achilles tendon graft for LTT using the medial and lateral GT anchors. The Achilles tendon graft for LTT was fixed using a medial GT anchor (A). The tied strands were then secured to the anterior aspect of the GT lateral wall with a knotless anchor, thereby completing the fixation with a transosseous equivalent technique (B). (GT, greater tuberosity; LTT, lower trapezius transfer.)

Graft Fixation at the Greater Tuberosity for SCR

A medial anchor is placed at the anteromedial footprint of the GT, just anterior to the LT tendon. Sutures from the anterior and posterior glenoid anchors are passed through the medial edge of the prepared SCR graft, approximately 5 mm from the margin (Figure 8). The graft is then advanced with a knot pusher, and these glenoid sutures are tied to secure the medial fixation. The sutures from the GT medial anchor are subsequently passed through the lateral portion of the graft for fixation to the GT. After confirming and trimming the graft length, the lateral side is secured to the GT lateral wall with a knotless anchor in a transosseous equivalent fashion.

Fixation of the SCR and LTT grafts in the beach‐chair position. (A) The arrowhead indicates the anterior glenoid anchor suture limbs, and the asterisk indicates the posterior glenoid anchor suture limbs, which were passed through the medial edge of the prepared SCR graft approximately 5 mm from its margin. (B) The arrowhead marks the Achilles tendon graft used for SCR, and the asterisk marks the Achilles tendon graft used for LTT, both firmly fixed in position. (LTT, lower trapezius transfer; SCR, superior capsular reconstruction.)

Graft Anastomosis

With the arm positioned in abduction and full external rotation, the graft is secured extra‐articularly. It is passed through the prepared LT segment, folded back, and sutured both onto itself and to the LT using high‐strength sutures. The connection between the Achilles tendon and the lower trapezius tendon is reinforced with a Pulvertaft weave technique.

DISCUSSION

Irreparable PSRCTs in young, active patients remain a challenging condition, as joint‐preserving options are generally preferred over reverse total shoulder arthroplasty. Conventional strategies, including debridement and partial repair, often fail to restore biomechanics and are associated with persistent pain and unsatisfactory outcomes. Therefore, we describe a combined SCR and LTT technique, with pearls and pitfalls summarized in Table 2 and advantages and limitations in Table 3.

Isolated posterior cuff atrophy, which represents the most appropriate indication for LTT, is uncommon, with most patients showing posterosuperior involvement. In these cases, isolated LTT can restore external rotation but may not adequately reestablish the superior fulcrum, leading to persistent superior migration. Similarly, SCR with acellular dermal matrix has been used to restore superior stability but carries the risk of graft thinning over time, potentially compromising long‐term outcomes.

To overcome these limitations, we employ an Achilles tendon allograft to create a thick, durable SCR graft while simultaneously preparing a second Achilles strip for LTT. This approach allows restoration of both superior stability and dynamic external rotation with robust tissue quality. Moreover, by slightly extending the anterior portal and performing a mini‐open step, graft passage and fixation are simplified, reducing suture tangling, shortening operative time, and lowering technical demands compared with a fully arthroscopic SCR.

Nevertheless, limitations exist, including graft availability and a lack of established long‐term results. Future comparative studies are warranted to clarify whether this combined approach provides superior outcomes over isolated procedures.

In conclusion, the combined SCR and LTT technique offers a joint‐preserving, biomechanically synergistic solution for irreparable PSRCTs in young, active patients, with the potential for more reliable restoration of shoulder function.
