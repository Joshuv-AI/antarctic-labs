  E2C Technical Assistance Opportunities

In-Depth Partnerships ~3 years
Multiyear partnership comprising teams (local governments, community-based organizations, and electric utilities) that work
alongside national laboratory staff to apply robust modeling and analysis tools and simulate hardware in conditions that match
communities' unique contexts to evaluate potential scenarios and strategies before full technology deployment.
Supports nine communities currently.

Peer-Learning Cohorts ~6 months
A total of 6 months of regular convenings where cohorts of up to 15 participants exchange strategies and best practices, learn in
a collaborative environment, and workshop plans and strategies to overcome challenges relating to a similar energy
topic. Eligibility varies by cohort topic.
Supports about 100 communities per year.

Expert Match ~3 months
Short-term, no-cost technical assistance for communities seeking to answer near-term energy questions. Eligible entities include
local governments, utilities, community-based organizations, schools, and nonprofits.
Supports about 200 communities per year. Applications are accepted all year on a rolling basis.

 Caveats
Topic Caveat

 This analysis uses a variety of assumptions regarding the cost of technologies, future loads, and future energy tariffs. These
Model inputs
 assumptions may differ from reality. Additional financial analysis may be required to more accurately model costs and benefits.

 The modeled battery dispatch strategy assumes the system can perfectly predict future electric loads and can thus be considered
Perfect foresight
 an upper bound on performance.
 These initial findings should be considered preliminary and can serve as the basis for further viability analyses of solar and
Preliminary analysis
 storage.
Electricity system upgrade The analysis does not include cost estimates for upgrading the electrical system for islanded operation during an outage (e.g.,
costs for resilience critical load panel, microgrid controller). Actual upgrade needs and costs are site specific and require additional analysis.
 Future load changes (e.g., energy efficiency upgrades or electric vehicle charging stations) were not considered in this analysis.
Building load
 Future load growth may impact requirements for system sizing and resilience design.
Incentives Incentives and eligibility for incentives may change in the future.

 The project may incur costs for potential upgrades to the utility distribution system, which the utility may pass on to the project
Interconnection upgrade costs developers. These costs were not included in this analysis because of uncertainty and potential variability in these costs, which
 could be estimated later in the project development process.

Renewable generation The estimated power output from the solar PV and wind system is based on historical resource data and represented as typical
resource data meteorological year data. Actual power output may differ.

 The techno-economic model assumes all capital costs are paid upfront. Costs associated with project financing (such as interest
Project financing
 on loans) is not included in this analysis.

 consumption. Operation is limited to July and August between 12 p.m. and 6 p .m. to provide a lower
 upfront capital cost resilience solution that does not rely on an emergency backup generator. The
 minimum required PV and battery sizes to survive the outage are reported.

Results

 Scenarios Evaluated
• The following scenarios were evaluated separately for the Greenville Grocery Store and the Greenville
 Elementary School based on priorities discussed with the site:
 o Business-as-usual: The site purchases all electricity from the grid.
 o Cost-optimal: PV, wind turbines, and battery energy storage are optimally sized to minimize the site’s total
 life cycle costs over a 25-year analysis period.
 o On-site energy generation: PV, wind turbines, and battery energy storage are optimally sized to meet 20%,
 40%, 60%, 80%, and 100% of the site’s annual electric load with on-site generation, subject to space
 availability. Rather than generating energy on-site to match load at every time step, the site may buy
 electricity from the grid during periods of low generation and offset these purchases by exporting excess
 generation during other periods. As a result, grid purchases may still occur even in the 100% scenario.
 o Resilience: PV, wind turbines, battery energy storage, and backup diesel generators are sized to meet 4-
 hour, 12-hour, 24-hour, 72-hour, and 168-hour outages in three critical load scenarios.
 ─ If a diesel generator is selected, the minimum size is set to the maximum critical load.
 ─ When a PV system is sized independently to pair with a diesel generator, a 30-minute battery with an
 inverter capacity at the maximum critical load is added to support tandem PV and generator
 operation and prevent excessive wear on the generator.

Conclusion

 Conclusion and Next
 Steps
 • Based on the analysis assumptions, only PV is cost-effective to reduce electricity costs at the Greenville Grocery Store
 and Elementary School over a 25-year analysis period.
 o A 54.3-kWdc rooftop PV system is cost-optimal at the grocery store, serving 14% of annual load with on-site
 generation. A 651.6-kWdc ground-mount PV system is cost-optimal at the elementary school, serving 100% of the
 annual load.
 • Meeting the modeled 4-hour to 1-week outages with PV, wind, and battery storage alone is not cost-effective. Instead, in
 the 50% and 20% critical load scenarios, the presented solution includes a diesel generator sized to the maximum
 critical load and adds a 30-minute battery with matching inverter capacity to support tandem PV-generator operation.
 o This configuration results in negative NPV at the grocery store across all scenarios. At the elementary school, NPV
 is positive when ITC is available for PV and remains positive at 20% critical load even without the ITC.
 o To avoid incurring battery costs, the site may consider relying solely on diesel generation during grid outages.
 However, generator reliability decreases as outage duration increases, and generators require regular testing
 and maintenance and a reliable fuel supply during emergencies. An estimate of generator O&M costs are
 included in this analysis, but fuel costs are not as they depend on the frequency and duration of actual outages.
 o This analysis does not include any avoided outage expenses and community resilience benefits, which could offset
 the modeled costs.
• An additional resilience scenario was evaluated to size technologies to support a limited critical load (primarily phone
 charging and space cooling) without relying on emergency backup generators and fuel resupply during extended outages.
 Although this approach significantly reduced upfront capital costs, it remained NPV-negative over the analysis period.

 Resilience Modeling

 Inputs Assumptions

 Critical load scenarios 50% of typical load, 20% of typical load, bottom-up modeling based on devices.

 Technologies are optimally sized to meet 4-hour, 12-hour, 24-hour, and 72-hour
 outages with at least a 90% probability of survival, regardless of when the outage
 occurs during the year. When a PV system is sized independently to pair with a diesel
 generator, a 30-minute battery with an inverter capacity at the maximum critical
 load is added to support tandem PV and generator operation and prevent excessive
 Modeled outage scenarios
 wear on the generator.

 A scenario for a 1 outage (168 hours) is also evaluated. However, as outage duration
 increases, generator reliability declines, reducing the probability of survival at 168
 hours to approximately 85%.
